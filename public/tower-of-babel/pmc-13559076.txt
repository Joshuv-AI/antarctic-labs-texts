The hypoxic burden algorithm: an integrated index for assessing intermittent hypoxia in pediatric obstructive sleep apnea—a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Lingling Zhong, Fanyu Mu, Uul Gegeen, Junmei Zhang, Liang Li, Wei Wang, Guangping Liu, Bei Shen, Shuang Li, Hualei Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPediatric obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) involves recurrent upper-airway obstruction during sleep, which leads to intermittent hypoxia (IH) and contributes to neurocognitive, cardiovascular, and metabolic complications. Polysomnography (PSG) and the Obstructive Apnea-Hypopnea Index (OAHI) are commonly used for diagnosis; however, conventional nocturnal hypoxic metrics such as lowest oxygen saturation (LSpO2), mean oxygen saturation (MSpO2), total time with oxygen saturation below 90% (T90), and oxygen desaturation index ≥3% (ODI3) fail to capture the cumulative burden. Hypoxic burden (HB) has therefore gained attention as an integrated measure that reflects the frequency, depth, and duration of desaturation events; however, its use and clinical value in pediatric OSA remain uncertain. This study aims to evaluate the clinical value of HB in diagnosing pediatric OSA and compare its diagnostic performance with conventional nocturnal hypoxic metrics.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of PSG data from 115 children with suspected OSA at Tianjin Children’s Hospital between December 2020 and November 2025. Data were analyzed using internally developed Matlab software. The differential performance of five nocturnal IH parameters (LSpO2, MSpO2, T90, ODI3, and HB) was compared across four groups: normal, mild, moderate, and severe OSA. All data were manually scored according to the AASM Scoring Manual (version 2.6).ResultsThe cohort (72 males, 43 females; mean age 6.44±2.86 years) was distributed into normal (n=15), mild (n=63), moderate (n=23), and severe OSA (n=14) groups based on OAHI. Significant differences were observed among severity groups for ODI3, T90, and HB. Correlation analysis showed LSpO2 and MSpO2 had weak-to-moderate negative correlations with OAHI (r=−0.490 and r=−0.543, respectively). ODI3 (r=0.788) and T90 (r=0.700) showed moderate positive correlations. Importantly, HB demonstrated a strong correlation with OAHI (r=0.801). ROC curve analysis indicated weak diagnostic capability for LSpO2 (AUC =0.689) and MSpO2 (AUC =0.614). ODI3 (AUC =0.857) and T90 (AUC =0.701) showed moderate value, while HB (AUC =0.888) demonstrated the best diagnostic performance.ConclusionsWe successfully developed an HB algorithm, demonstrating its significant advantage in pediatric OSA diagnosis. Compared to traditional indices that reflect only a single dimension of oxygen desaturation, HB integrates the frequency, depth, and duration of hypoxia. This provides a robust, objective alternative for the early screening and clinical assessment of children with OSA.

Key findings

• The study developed a hypoxic burden (HB) algorithm using raw polysomnography data. HB showed the strongest correlation with the Obstructive Apnea-Hypopnea Index (r=0.801) and the highest diagnostic accuracy (area under the curve =0.888), outperforming conventional hypoxic indexes.

What is known, and what is new?

• Traditional indices such as lowest oxygen saturation, mean oxygen saturation, time with oxygen saturation below 90%, and oxygen desaturation index ≥3% describe only one aspect of desaturation and fail to reflect the total HB in pediatric obstructive sleep apnea (OSA).

• The HB algorithm integrates frequency, depth, and duration of desaturation, providing a more comprehensive measure of intermittent hypoxia and superior diagnostic performance.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• HB offers a physiologically meaningful index for assessing pediatric OSA severity. Incorporating HB into clinical evaluation could improve diagnostic precision and enable earlier identification of children with significant HB.

Introduction

Pediatric obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a sleep-related breathing disorder characterized by the recurrent collapse or narrowing of the upper airway during sleep, leading to airflow limitation or complete cessation (Figure 1A,1B). The repeated collapse and obstruction of the upper airway induce a cascade of pathophysiological changes in the body (1), including intermittent hypoxia (IH), hypercapnia, and sleep structure fragmentation. Due to its high prevalence and severe short- and long-term complications, pediatric OSA is receiving increasing attention from both parents and society (2).

The chronic disruption caused by OSA is responsible for a wide range of developmental and systemic complications. The effects of OSA on the developing brain are particularly concerning; clinical evidence strongly links pediatric OSA to significant neurocognitive impairment, including deficits in executive function, attention, and working memory, often manifesting as behavioral problems such as hyperactivity and aggression (3). Further, OSA imposes a significant cardiovascular dysfunction burden (4). Recurrent drops in oxygen saturation (SpO2) and subsequent surges of sympathetic nervous system activity lead to endothelial dysfunction (5), systemic inflammation, and hypertension, increasing the long-term risk of cardiovascular disease, even in children (6).

Metabolically, OSA has been associated with insulin resistance and altered glucose metabolism (7). These severe end-organ consequences underscore the need for precise, quantifiable measures that accurately reflect the total physiological burden imposed by the disorder, rather than relying solely on event frequency.

Currently, polysomnography (PSG) is considered the gold standard for diagnosing pediatric OSA. PSG monitoring is used to calculate the Obstructive Apnea-Hypopnea Index (OAHI) and the lowest oxygen saturation (LSpO2) during sleep, which are used to determine the severity of OSA and the degree of hypoxia, respectively (8,9).

However, OAHI quantifies the frequency of obstructive apneas and hypopneas per hour of sleep, and thus fails to capture the actual duration or severity of IH. Similarly, LSpO2 describes the maximum drop in SpO2 experienced by the child throughout the entire night, but does not objectively reflect the frequency or duration of desaturation events. Thus, LSpO2 cannot fully and accurately represent the true severity of IH in affected children. Consequently, a marked dissociation between OAHI and LSpO2 levels is commonly observed (10).

The limitations of OAHI and LSpO2 have promoted researchers to investigate alternative metrics that incorporate the temporal dimension of hypoxemia. However, these currently available nocturnal hypoxic metrics often lack the sensitivity required to identify children at risk for OSA-related complications. This lack of sensitivity arises because these traditional indices evaluate hypoxia in isolation—for example, OAHI and oxygen desaturation index (ODI) solely count the frequency of events, while LSpO2 merely records the maximum depth of oxygen drops. Two such parameters frequently used are the ODI and the time with oxygen saturation below 90% (T90). ODI quantifies the frequency of significant drops (typically ≥3%) in SpO2 (11), offering a slight improvement over OAHI by focusing on a physiological consequence rather than on airflow cessation alone (12). T90 provides a measure of cumulative duration, quantifying the total time the child spends in a potentially hazardous hypoxic state. T90 has shown promising correlations with long-term complications; however, both ODI (a frequency measure) and T90 (a duration measure) remain unidimensional. Because they strictly measure either frequency or time, they cannot fully capture the overall physiological stress imposed on a child. Neither metric integrates the cumulative severity of the oxygen deficit across all three critical components: frequency, duration, and depth of desaturation (13).

IH is a primary pathophysiological mechanism by which pediatric OSA leads to neurocognitive, cardiovascular, and metabolic complications (14,15). This cyclical hypoxia-reoxygenation process, driven by periodic upper airway collapse, can occur tens to hundreds of times nightly (16).

While low-dose IH may activate protective mechanisms (17). the high-frequency, moderate-to-severe IH typical of pediatric OSA triggers oxidative stress and pathological signaling pathways, driving cellular damage (18,19).

Therefore, IH in OSA is a powerful pathological stimulus, acting as the “common pathway” linking nocturnal respiratory events to multi-system daytime dysfunction. Consequently, a physiologically driven method for measuring OSA severity could better promote and improve the personalized management of pediatric OSA. Although several parameters can quantify the hypoxic burden (HB) to some extent, it remains important to explore more comprehensive metrics that can overcome the limitations of single-dimensional measures [e.g., OAHI, LSpO2, mean oxygen saturation (MSpO2), T90, and oxygen desaturation index ≥3% (ODI3)] and more fully capture the overall HB experienced by patients (Figure 1C).

The “burden” is quantified by calculating the area under the SpO2 curve when saturation drops below a critical physiological threshold (Figure 1D). This area under the curve (AUC) approach mathematically captures the combined effect of how low the SpO2 drops (depth) and for how long it stays low (duration), effectively measuring the cumulative oxygen deficit that drives end-organ damage. Such a mathematically integrated metric provides a superior representation of the total physiological stress than simple event counts or total time measures. This innovative approach seeks to provide a singular, robust index that more closely reflects the underlying pathophysiology of OSA and is thus more predictive of adverse clinical outcomes (20).

Thus, this study aimed to: (I) explore the differences in IH parameters among children with varying degrees of OSA severity; and (II) develop a HB algorithm using raw PSG data to identify a precise metric for assessing IH severity in pediatric OSA. We hypothesized that HB, as an integrated metric incorporating frequency, depth, and duration of desaturation, would demonstrate stronger correlation with OAHI and superior diagnostic performance than traditional hypoxic indexes. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-0660/rc).

Methods

Participants

A retrospective analysis of the medical records of 115 children who underwent full-night PSG monitoring at Tianjin Children’s Hospital (Tianjin University Children’s Hospital) between December 2020 and November 2025 was conducted. Physical examination included the use of the 5-point Brodsky Tonsil Grading Scale and the 4-point Adenoid Grading Scale (21,22). No formal sample size calculation was performed; the sample size was determined by the number of eligible children who underwent PSG during the study period.

All patients were selected based on rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria. Patients were included in the study if they were aged ≥2 and <14 years, had a total PSG recording time ≥7 hours, had a sleep efficiency ≥70%, and presented with habitual snoring and/or other clinical symptoms such as apnea or mouth breathing during sleep. Patients were excluded from the study if they had congenital upper respiratory tract anatomical abnormalities, central nervous system diseases, or any relevant comorbid conditions (e.g., Down syndrome, neuromuscular diseases, metabolic diseases, pulmonary diseases, or sickle cell disease). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Children’s Hospital (Tianjin University Children’s Hospital) (No. 2022-SYYJCYJ-001). This retrospective study used de-identified clinical data and was exempted from informed consent.

Study methods

PSG

This, retrospective, single‑center cohort study was based on routinely collected PSG data. All children underwent full-night PSG monitoring using the Somte-PSG system (Compumedics, Australia) in a dedicated sleep monitoring laboratory at Tianjin Children’s Hospital (Tianjin University Children’s Hospital). The recorded parameters included: electroencephalogram (EEG) (F4/A1, C4/A1, O2/A1, F3/A2, C3/A2, and O1/A2), electrocardiogram, electrooculogram, electromyogram, thoracoabdominal respiratory effort, SpO2, pressure and thermal airflow, snoring, body position, and leg movements. All PSG recordings were acquired using the same Somte-PSG system under standardized laboratory conditions. The same PSG equipment and identical acquisition settings were used for all participants. The measurement procedures for SpO2, airflow, respiratory effort, and EEG were consistent across all groups. No changes in equipment or scoring protocol occurred during the study period.

The raw data were then manually scored and interpreted by experienced sleep physicians in accordance with the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) Scoring Manual (version 2.6) (23). All PSG recordings were manually scored by two experienced pediatric sleep physicians who were strictly blinded to both the patients’ clinical information and each other’s scoring results. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion to reach a consensus. Apnea, hypopnea, and desaturation were defined according to the AASM Scoring Manual (version 2.6).

Potential sources of bias included selection bias due to the single‑center retrospective design, information bias related to manual PSG scoring, and confounding by age, body mass index (BMI), and tonsillar hypertrophy. Standardized PSG scoring procedures and predefined inclusion criteria were used to minimize information bias; however, residual confounding could not be excluded.

Diagnostic criteria

According to the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Pediatric OSA, published by the Chinese Medical Association in 2020, patients were stratified based on their OAHI. OAHI is calculated as the total number of obstructive apneas and hypopneas per hour of sleep. Under the guidelines, OSA severity was classified as normal (OAHI ≤1 event/hour), mild (1< OAHI ≤5 events/hour), moderate (5< OAHI ≤10 events/hour), and severe (OAHI >10 events/hour) (8).

The manually scored data were exported from the PSG software as comma-separated variable (.csv) files and subsequently analyzed using Matlab software.

Given the reliance on SpO2 recordings for subsequent calculations, the exported raw SpO2 signals, which were acquired at a sampling frequency of 32 Hz, underwent rigorous preprocessing. First, to eliminate invalid signal periods, all non-numerical values and SpO2 values below 40% were classified as measurement errors attributable to factors such as improper probe placement or device initialization. These erroneous data points were subsequently excluded from the analysis. Second, to address temporary sensor disconnections frequently resulting from pediatric patient movement during sleep, an adaptive, time-interval-based segmentation algorithm was implemented. Specifically, if the time gap between two consecutive valid data points exceeded 1.5 times the normal sampling period—a duration greater than 1.5/32 seconds—the gap was explicitly identified as a sensor disconnection event. Consequently, the signal was partitioned into independent, continuous valid segments. These preprocessing procedures effectively mitigated major artifacts associated with device detachment and prevented severe integration errors during the calculation of desaturation areas across data discontinuities.

Statistical analysis

All variables were checked for missingness; no missing data were present, and no imputation was required. Because the primary aim was to compare hypoxic indexes rather than estimate causal effects, multivariable adjustment was not performed. Because the primary aim was to compare hypoxic parameters rather than estimate causal effects, multivariable adjustment was not performed. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on patients’ OSA severity (normal, mild, moderate, and severe).

All data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 25.0). Normally distributed continuous data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation, while categorical data were presented as frequencies. Comparisons between groups for categorical data were performed using the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. For normally distributed quantitative data, analysis of variance was used to compare differences across multiple groups. Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess the correlation between normally distributed variables. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Diagnostic value of different hypoxic indexes in predicting pediatric OSA

Figure 4 displays the ROC curves for the five hypoxic indexes. The analysis showed that LSpO2 [AUC =0.689; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.520–0.857] and MSpO2 (AUC =0.614; 95% CI: 0.475–0.754) had weak diagnostic capability for pediatric OSA. In contrast, T90 (AUC =0.701; 95% CI: 0.557–0.845) demonstrated moderate diagnostic value, while ODI3 (AUC =0.857; 95% CI: 0.772–0.942) and the HB metric (AUC =0.888; 95% CI: 0.809–0.968) demonstrated comparable and excellent diagnostic efficacy. Notably, while ODI3 exhibited a higher sensitivity (90.0% vs. 76.0%), HB demonstrated a superior specificity (93.3% vs. 66.7%) (Table 5). In summary, LSpO2 and MSpO2 were weak diagnostic predictors, T90 showed moderate diagnostic value, and both ODI3 and HB demonstrated robust and complementary clinical utility.

Discussion

OAHI is currently the primary metric used worldwide for diagnosing and grading the severity of pediatric OSA. IH is a key hallmark of OSA and has been widely used as an experimental model in pathophysiological studies of the disorder (27,28). With the increasing adoption of portable sleep monitoring, IH-related parameters have become a popular area of research due to their low cost, minimal technical requirements, and ease of acquisition. In this context, we developed an HB algorithm based on raw PSG data. We analyzed the clinical characteristics of children with and without OSA and, for the first time, investigated the clinical utility of five nocturnal IH-related parameters—LSpO2, MSpO2, T90, ODI3, and HB—in assessing pediatric OSA.

In our study, statistically significant differences in age and BMI were observed among the different OSA severity groups. Further analysis of tonsil size demonstrated a statistically significant difference only between the mild and severe groups. Conversely, no statistically significant differences were observed in gender, duration of medical history, or adenoid size across groups. These findings align with previous research. Mitchell et al. (29) identified race, obesity, and age (specifically 5.0–9.9 years) as factors related to OSA severity in a study of more than 450 children with OSA. Other studies have also reported a weak correlation between tonsil size and pediatric OSA severity (3).

While pediatric OSA can occur at any age, it is most common between 2–8 years of age, a period associated with significant development of peripheral lymphoid tissue (e.g., the adenoids and tonsils) around the upper airway (30). Obesity has also emerged as one of the most important risk factors for pediatric OSA. The direct interaction between obesity and OSA has been explained by fat deposition in the upper airway structures, leading to airway narrowing and subsequent pharyngeal collapse during the night (31,32). Consequently, physical examination results alone may not be sufficient or effective for accurately detecting pediatric OSA or determining its severity.

As a frequency parameter that assesses respiratory events, OAHI fails to fully capture the depth and duration of these events, making it suboptimal for evaluating clinical symptoms and prognosis (33). Consequently, hypoxic indexes, which are more direct markers of oxygen deprivation, have been increasingly used to supplement OAHI. MSpO2 is frequently reported in sleep studies and reflects the average level of oxygen impairment during sleep; it is often used alongside LSpO2 to independently determine the severity of OSA. Our results confirm this relationship, showing that OAHI is moderately negatively correlated with MSpO2 (r=−0.543, P<0.01).

Further, LSpO2 is the most widely used parameter. In China, LSpO2 is used alongside OAHI to determine the presence and severity stratification of OSA (8). However, a dissociation between OAHI and LSpO2 is commonly observed clinically, whereby some children with a low OAHI may still exhibit markedly reduced LSpO2 (10,34). In line with this observation, we found that OAHI was only weakly negatively correlated with LSpO2 (r=−0.490, P<0.01). Moreover, LSpO2 demonstrated weak diagnostic capability for pediatric OSA (AUC =0.689; 95% CI: 0.520–0.857). Similarly, Ahn et al. (35) found that the absolute tongue volume correlated more strongly with LSpO2 during sleep than with OAHI severity. Therefore, given that LSpO2 reflects only the maximum degree of desaturation during sleep and can be influenced by other factors, reliance on this single index alone is likely insufficient for accurately assessing the severity of hypoxia in pediatric OSA.

Furthermore, it is important to consider that respiratory events often terminate via cortical arousals rather than severe oxygen desaturations. Children with a lower arousal threshold may exhibit frequent respiratory-related arousals, prematurely terminating apneas or hypopneas before significant oxygen drops occur. This physiological mechanism can result in smaller oxygen desaturations despite a clinically significant frequency of respiratory events, thereby explaining the frequently observed discrepancies between OAHI and nocturnal hypoxia indices. Consequently, while OAHI primarily captures the fragmentation of sleep architecture driven by frequent arousals, the HB serves as a highly specific and complementary metric for quantifying the actual end-organ hypoxic stress. Together, these multidimensional indices could provide a more comprehensive clinical picture of pediatric OSA.

T90 objectively describes the duration and severity of hypoxia throughout the entire sleep period. We found that T90 differed significantly among children with varying degrees of OSA severity, showed a moderate positive correlation with OAHI (r=0.700, P<0.05). These findings are consistent with those of Li et al. (36), who reported that T90 showed a stronger correlation with OAHI compared with ODI, LSpO2, and MSpO2. Further, T90 is considered an important predictor of long-term complications (36).

ODI is also a useful parameter reflecting the degree of hypoxia. We found that the ODI3 value was significantly higher in the severe group than in the mild and moderate OSA groups. ODI3 showed a moderate positive correlation with OAHI (r=0.788, P<0.01) and had an AUC of 0.857 (95% CI: 0.772–0.942), demonstrating moderate value in diagnosing pediatric OSA. However, similar to the limitations of OAHI, ODI primarily measures only the frequency of hypoxic events (37). This highlights the need for a metric capable of capturing all dimensions of OSA-related desaturation, including frequency, duration, and depth, to better quantify nocturnal hypoxia.

Furthermore, it is important to acknowledge the intrinsic dependency between OAHI and ODI3, as hypopneas accompanied by oxygen desaturations directly contribute to the calculation of both indices. Consequently, the strong correlation observed between ODI3 and OAHI is somewhat anticipated and may offer limited additional clinical information. As a future direction, reporting the proportion of hypopneas associated with desaturations versus those terminated by cortical arousals would be highly meaningful. Patients with a higher proportion of desaturation-linked hypopneas are expected to exhibit a stronger correlation between OAHI and all hypoxic indices. Conversely, in children who predominantly experience hypopneas terminated by arousals—resulting in a lower proportion of severe desaturations—a dissociation between OAHI and traditional metrics like ODI3 may occur. It is precisely in this specific subset of patients that the HB metric might demonstrate its unique complementary and discriminatory value by more comprehensively capturing the overall physiological stress.

HB is defined as the ratio of the total area enclosed by the oxygen desaturation curve below a specific SpO2 baseline to the total sleep time (38). This metric has garnered attention because it integrates three key variables related to OSA-associated hypoxia: frequency, amplitude, and duration of desaturation events. Currently, there is no universal consensus on the algorithms used to calculate HB, primarily due to a lack of standardization regarding the baseline value for defining hypoxic injury. For example, Chen et al. (26) used 90% SpO2 as the baseline for evaluating OSA-related hypoxic injury, while other studies have defined the baseline as the maximum SpO2 within the 100 seconds preceding the end of the desaturation event (39). Currently, commercial software packages such as Sleepware G from Respironics (USA) and Cidelec from Angers (France) incorporate HB calculation. Additionally, Biologix (Brazil) provides HB information based on oxygen saturation and pulse rate; however, it is limited to adult populations and does not offer complete polysomnography data. This highlights the critical need for developing an integrated HB algorithm specifically based on complete PSG for pediatric OSA.

Our internally developed HB algorithm focuses on desaturation events below the physiologically hazardous 90% threshold and their duration, based on the rationale that these are more closely linked to the adverse IH outcomes in pediatric OSA. While the critical threshold of arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) or SpO2 that reliably indicates tissue hypoxic injury cannot be definitively established, SpO2 values above 90% are generally not considered hypoxic. According to the characteristics of the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve, an SpO2 of 90% corresponds to a PaO2 of 60 mmHg, and mild SpO2 reductions (>90%) do not significantly affect cellular physiological function, especially since increased arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2) can actually favor oxygen dissociation.

Consistent with this physiological basis, we found that HB was strongly correlated with OAHI (r=0.8010, P<0.01). Further, the ROC curve analysis indicated that HB exhibited an AUC of 0.888 (95% CI: 0.809–0.968), demonstrating comparable diagnostic efficacy to ODI3 (AUC =0.857). Rather than viewing HB as absolutely superior, our results suggest it serves as a robust physiological complement: while ODI3 provides high sensitivity in detecting event frequency, HB offers high specificity (93.3%) by capturing the multi-dimensional depth of hypoxia.

This study had several limitations. First, it was a retrospective analysis, and inherent design constraints may introduce selection bias and information bias, which should be considered when interpreting the results. Second, our study was conducted at a single tertiary pediatric center and only included children aged 2 years and older, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings to broader community populations, younger children, and different ethnic groups. Although pediatric sleep laboratories typically evaluate patients up to 18 years of age, adolescents older than 14 years were excluded from the current study. This specific age range was selected because it aligns with the period of significant development of peripheral lymphoid tissues around the upper airway, such as the adenoids and tonsils. In adolescents older than 14 years, these lymphoid tissues undergo physiological atrophy, and the etiology of OSA progressively shifts toward adult patterns. Including older adolescents would therefore introduce substantial population heterogeneity. Third, the relatively small sample size may affect the representativeness and statistical power of the results. Fourth, factors such as obesity, sleep stages, and other potential confounders may have influenced data quality and interpretation, but were not fully controlled in this study. Most importantly, the calculation of HB in this study used 90% SpO2 as the baseline for assessing OSA-related hypoxic injury, without incorporating respiratory signals. As a result, oxygen desaturation events caused by central apneas and hypopneas may have been included. Future research should adopt a prospective design, expand the sample size to establish diagnostic contingency tables using the optimal cut-off values proposed here, and compare the correlation between the method proposed by Azarbarzin et al. (12). and our findings to further validate and extend our results.

Conclusions

By analyzing the characteristics of nocturnal IH-related parameters in children with and without OSA, this study found that the use of a single HB index offers significant advantages in diagnosing pediatric OSA. Although larger sample sizes and prospective clinical trials are needed to further validate its utility, HB reflects IH events induced by OSA across multiple dimensions, and its calculation relies solely on SpO2 data. Owing to its simplicity and ease of application, HB shows promise as a low-cost tool for large-scale OSA screening in resource-limited settings or in populations intolerant to conventional testing.
