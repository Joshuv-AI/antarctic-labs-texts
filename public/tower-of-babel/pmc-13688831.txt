AI-integrated nanomaterial-based biosensors for biomedical diagnostics: engineering design, signal processing, and clinical translation
Authors: Chandrashekar Thalluri, Sarad Pawar Naik Bukke, Jharna Medhi, Mallikarjun Vasam, Ananda Kumar Chettupalli, Neha Gupta, Koushik Narayan Sarma, Milon Selvam Dennison, Bukke Pasunath Naik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNanobiosensor platforms based on nanomaterials have the potential to change the future of biomedical diagnostics with highly sensitive detection as well as quick responses and various functionalities. This review article will systematically outline the design principles, signal transduction mechanisms, and the biomedical use of nanobiosensors with a strong focus on including AI and wearable technologies to achieve these benefits. Examples of nanomaterials (organic and inorganic, as well as hybrid), including metallic nanoparticles, graphene derivatives, quantum dots, and polymer nanocomposites, allow nanobiosensors to provide increased biosensor performance using mechanisms such as electrochemical transduction, surface plasmon resonance, Förster resonance energy transfer, and surface enhanced Raman scattering. These mechanisms make it possible to detect biomarkers with incredible sensitivity for many different types of diseases, such as all types of infection, cancer, and metabolic disorders. Furthermore, when nanobiosensors are combined with AI and IoT applications, they provide a means of processing data in real time, utilizing predictive analytics and monitoring health based on individualized care. While significant progress has been made over the past decade, barriers to translation remain, including scaling up manufacturing capacity, achieving consistent, reproducible results, validating effectiveness in clinical settings, and obtaining FDA approval for use. This review article will compare various types of biosensing platforms and their respective strengths and limitations; discuss the current challenges faced in developing these platforms; and define future areas of focus for research.Keywords: Nanobiosensors, Nanomaterials, Biomedical diagnostics, Signal transduction mechanisms, Artificial intelligence integration, Point-of-care monitoring

Introduction

Biosensor systems play an important role in biomedical diagnostics, environmental monitoring, and as a fundamental aspect of any medical management strategy for an individual's health [1, 2]. These systems are able to measure biological process as a quantitative signal allowing for rapid and specific detection of many analytes (e.g., protein, nucleic acid, metabolite, and biological agents) [3]. The typical biosensor consists of three primary components, such as a biorecognition element (primarily an enzyme, antibody, adenine/uridine or nucleic acid), a device which converts biological response to a measurable electrical signal, and a reactor system to amplify and analyze the output signal and provide it back into the measuring device.

Conventional biosensors, while they are designed to meet specific applications, do not provide numerous inherent disadvantages, such as lack of sensitivity, unstable under physiological conditions, long response times, and low multiplexing capability [4]. Additionally, though traditional sensors can be miniaturized and integrated into portable or implantable devices, a lack of materials suitable for this application with biocompatibility, stability, and at the same time efficient signal transduction has made this task difficult.

Due to the integration of nanotechnology with biosensing platforms over the past two decades has changed biosensing as a whole, it has also brought together biological recognition and an electronic/optical detection method [5, 6]. Nanomaterial’s including (but not limited to) metal and metal oxide (Au, Ag, Pt, ZnO, TiO₂), carbon nanostructures (graphene, CNTs, fullerenes), quantum dots (QDs), polymeric and organic to inorganic hybrid nanocomposites all provide excellent physicochemical properties. These nanomaterials provide an increased surface to volume ratio allowing for increased bioreceptor loading, and tunable optical, electrical, and catalytic properties allowing for higher sensitivity and faster response kinetics in comparison with traditional bio sensing methods. Amplification of weak bio recognition events through signal transduction mechanisms (electron transfer enhancement, surface plasmon resonance (SPR), fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET), surface enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) is one of the significant advantages of nanomaterials. These signal transduction mechanisms significantly increase the limit of detection, allowing for ultra-sensitive quantitation of biomarkers in the picomolar to femtomolar range [7, 8].

Nanomaterials can also enable the creation of small, multifunctional biosensing platforms for minimally invasive clinical diagnostics at home (point-of-care testing), continuous monitoring of patients’ movements with wearable devices, and implantable biosystems that can monitor multiple parameters [9]. By converging nanotechnology, materials science, and bioengineering, there has been an emergence of next generation, microfluidics-based lab on a chip/biosensor systems that can perform high volume screening and acquire real-time information about assay results [10, 11].

The development of new biosensors based on nanomaterial technology emphasizes the growing use of artificial intelligence and machine learning to further augment these devices [12]. AI algorithms will allow for increased analytical accuracy, automation of the identification of disease patterns, and continuous interpretation of the large, complex data sets generated by many of these sensors [13]. The development of this integrated biosensor technology represents a considerable advancement toward personalized medicine, providing physicians and their patients with biosensor systems that can not only detect and identify disease, but also provide continuing information that would assist both the clinician and patient to predict the rate of disease progression and response to an existing condition, therapeutic, or treatment in real-time [14].

Recent developments in the interdisciplinary field of research show how nanomaterial-based biosensors could revolutionize healthcare diagnostics, environmental monitoring, food safety, and biomedical research [15, 16]. In this review article, we will discuss how these technologies have been developed and their fundamental mechanisms of action, as well as the prospect of applying them to future diagnostic applications using artificial intelligence assisted biosensing platforms and integrated diagnostic/therapeutic systems (theranostics). We believe that the combination of these two technology developments is what will drive the next generation of intelligent and dynamic healthcare technology solutions [17].

Classification and design of nanomaterial-based biosensors

Nano-enabled biosensors work by enhancing the biosensor's signal amplification, sensitivity, and selectivity through the use of nanomaterial-based transducers to transfer an electric signal from the bioreceptor to the electrode and to amplify that signal to a higher sensitivity level than could be achieved with traditional methods. Nano-enabled biosensors can be grouped into three main categories based on their chemical compositions/properties and therefore applications: organic nanomaterials, inorganic nanomaterials, and hybrid or composite nanomaterials [18].

Organic nanomaterials

These nanoparticles (e.g., polystyrene, polyaniline, polyethylene glycol, chitosan), liposomes, dendrimers, micelles, or carbon dots, are becoming more popular in biosensing applications because they are biocompatible, can be easily modified chemically, and can be easily functionalized with biological molecules. Typical examples of organic nanomaterials in electrochemical biosensors are polymer nanoparticles (e.g., polyaniline, polypyrrole and polyethylene glycol), polyaniline based and polypyrrole-based electrochemical biosensors are used as a stable matrix to immobilize enzymes and facilitate rapid electron transfer between the bioreceptors and electrodes [19]. For instance, glucose biosensors based on polyaniline chitosan composites demonstrate enhanced sensitivity due to the synergistic effect of the polymer’s conductivity and chitosan’s biocompatibility, allowing for efficient conversion of glucose to gluconic acid and H₂O₂, which is then electrochemically detected. Dendrimers, such as poly amidoamine (PAMAM), are highly branched macromolecules that offer abundant terminal groups for bioconjugation, increasing the surface loading of enzymes or antibodies. PAMAM modified electrodes have been successfully used for sensitive detection of cancer biomarkers like CA-125, achieving lower detection limits than conventional electrodes. Liposomes and micelles, mimicking biological membranes, are effective carriers for signal reporters or enzymes, and have been applied in immunosensors for cardiac troponin I, where liposome encapsulated fluorescent dyes are released upon antigen antibody interaction. Carbon dots, or carbon quantum dots, with strong fluorescence, high photostability, and low cytotoxicity, are widely utilized in fluorescence and chemiluminescence biosensors. For example, nitrogen doped carbon dots functionalized with folic acid have enabled selective detection of folate receptor positive cancer cells through fluorescence enhancement [20].

Inorganic nanomaterials

The use of inorganic nanomaterials, primarily metallic nanoparticles, metal oxide materials and semiconductors is essential for developing high-performing biosensors due to the superior electrical conducting abilities of metals, the ability to catalyze reactions, mechanical strength, and optical tunability. Metallic nanoparticles (metal nanocrystals) such as gold, silver, platinum, and palladium have been found to offer enormous surface area, high levels of efficient electron transfer and excellent levels of compatibility with biological molecules. Gold nanoparticles that are functionalized with antibodies have become an essential part of many surface plasmon resonance (SPR) based biosensors as a means of supporting the ability of sensors to monitor in real time the interaction between a particular antigen and an antibody. The use of silver nanoparticles in SERS biosensors as plasmonic enhancement of signal amplification to support trace detection of DNA and protein. Platinum nanoparticles act as catalyst amplifiers in a glucose sensor, thereby improving the electrochemical oxidation of glucose at low over potentials [21]. The metal oxide nanomaterial’s (including ZnO, TiO₂, Fe₃O₄, SnO₂ and CuO) have high iso-electric points and their own unique redox activity, which supports the immobilization and signaling transduction. ZnO nanorods connected to electrodes offer a reliable and fast method for detecting urea in urea biosensors constructed with urease. The utilization of magnetic Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles affords an advantage in the ability to separate and enrich nucleic acids before the detection process using magnetic separation, therefore, enhancing the electrochemical sensitivity of those nucleic acids. The presence of titanium dioxide (TiO₂) nanostructures enables a much higher rate of photocatalysis than conventional methods which allow their use as photoelectrochemical biosensors with the benefit of label-free detection illuminated by ultraviolet light (UV). Semiconductor quantum dots such as CdSe, CdTe and ZnS have recently attracted great interest in the areas of optical and multiplexed biosensing. A quantum dot-based energy transfer (FRET) biosensor designed from cadmium selenide/zinc sulfide (CdSe/ZnS), exhibits the capability of feto-molar detection limits (fM) for HIV-1 DNA samples. This sensitivity is attributed to exceptionally high energy transfer rates from quantum dots to dye acceptors [22].

Hybrid and composite nanomaterials

These are composed of organic and inorganic components, which create a multifunctional structure that exhibits improved stability, conductivity, and chemical reliability [23]. For example, metal carbon composite materials such as graphene/gold nanoparticle hybrid materials provide a high surface area with a good electron mobility of graphene and the ability to bind to and tunnel electrons through gold nanoparticles, making graphene/gold nanoparticle hybrid materials excellent electrochemical immunosensors when detecting prostate specific antigen (PSA). In the same way, polymer quantum dot hybrid materials such as PMMA/CdTe composite materials have a greater photoluminescent efficiency and biocompatibility, and therefore provide an improved basis for fluorescence-based cancer biomarker detection. Also, polymers metal oxides hybrid materials such as chitosan/ZnO nanocomposite materials enhance enzyme immobilization and inhibit enzyme leaching, resulting in longer lasting lactate biosensors [24]. The combination of graphene and polymers, such as PEDOT and other composites, will improve the electrical properties while still being flexible enough to work as an electrochemical biosensor worn for measuring glucose in sweat continuously [25]. The combination of multiple elements producing hybrid materials results in increased sensitivity, specificity, stability, and the ability to sense multiple methods, making these materials ideal choices for developing new generations of biosensors that can be used in wearable and implantable devices as well as integrated with artificial intelligence.

Nanomaterials biosensors need to be carefully designed based upon many critical aspects. First, nanoparticle size, shape, and surface area affect surface area (and thus) the kinetics of electron transfer [26, 27]. Second, this is enhanced by utilizing thiols, carboxylic acid, or silane to chemically modify the surface for the development of biosensors that immobilize bioreceptors while maintaining their bioactivity. Third, increased stability and biocompatibility can be achieved through coating materials, e.g., poly(methyl methacrylate), silica, polyvinylpyrrolidone. Fourth, the nanomaterial used must match the mechanism of signal amplification being used (e.g., plasmonics, electrochemistry, fluorescence) to achieve the highest possible performance of the biosensor. Finally, issues related to reusable and scalable biosensors for use in clinical settings and commercial markets must also be considered.

Organic and inorganic nanostructures have unique properties that make them ideal for a variety of applications. Organic nanostructures are characterized by their flexibility and biocompatibility, whereas inorganic nanostructures possess strong electrical and optical properties, allowing them to be extremely sensitive to changes in their environment [28]. Hybrid systems combine these characteristics of organic and inorganic nanomaterials to create multifunctional, high-performance devices. The selection and engineering of these nanomaterials will support the development of the next generation of intelligent, real time, personalized nanobiosensors. Comparative analysis of nanomaterials, sensing mechanisms, and analytical performance in nanobiosensors

Fundamental mechanisms governing nanobiosensing processes

Nanomaterial biosensor technology is based on multiple biological sensing processes that create measurable signal outputs when certain biological interactions occur [43, 44]. The unique physicochemical properties of nanomaterials (e.g., increased surface area, controllable conductivity, optical reactivity, catalytic capability) result in increased signal production and improved rate and sensitivity of signal detection. Nanobiosensing utilizes various mechanistic (i.e., electrochemical, optical/plasmonic, colorimetric/chemiluminescent) methods, each of which has distinct benefits for use in pharmaceutical or clinical testing situations [45].

Biosensors

The use of electrochemical methods is among the most popular types because they are very sensitive and simple to make and can be used with small, portable devices [46]. Nanomaterials like gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), platinum nanoparticles, graphene, and carbon nanotubes are often used to build electrodes that allow for faster transfer of electrons between the sensor and the sample, more area on which the biomolecules attach, and greater ability for amplifying signals produced by the reaction [47]. For example, a sensor made with an AuNP-coated electrode coated with glucose oxidase has been used to create an electrochemical glucose sensor that is highly sensitive and has quick response time with limits of detection down to the micromolar level. Additionally, graphene-based immunosensors present a strong platform for detecting cancer biomarkers PSA and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), with improved electrochemical response because of graphene's exceptional conductivity and high surface area. Also, electrochemical nanobiosensors have been utilized to quickly and accurately detect pathogens like HIV-1 and SARS-CoV-2, which allows for immediate point-of-care diagnosis in the pharmaceutical and clinical industry [48].

Optical and plasmonic biosensors

The advantage of how nanomaterials interact with light and allow sensitive, label-free detection [49, 50]. One of these biosensor methods is SPR, which uses the fact that conduction electrons in metallic nanostructures (most often made from gold or silver) will resonate in response to incoming light. SPR biosensors allow real-time monitoring of molecular binding events without the need for labeling. For example, AuNP-based SPR sensors have been used to detect drug protein interactions and antibody antigen binding in pharmaceutical research, facilitating drug screening and therapeutic monitoring. SERS takes advantage of plasmonic nanostructures to amplify Raman signals by several orders of magnitude [51, 52]. SERS-based biosensors employing silver or gold nanostructures have demonstrated ultra-sensitive detection of pharmaceutical compounds, such as antibiotics (e.g., kanamycin and ampicillin), and biomarkers at trace concentrations. FRET mechanisms, often utilizing quantum dots or dye functionalized nanoparticles, enable multiplexed detection in drug development studies. For instance, CdSe quantum dot-based FRET sensors have been used for real-time monitoring of enzyme activity and high-throughput screening of pharmaceutical inhibitors [53, 54].

Colorimetric and chemiluminescent biosensors

These represent another important class, where nanomaterials act as enzyme mimetics (nanozymes) to catalyze reactions that produce measurable color or light signals [55]. Metal nanoparticles, such as AuNPs, Fe₃O₄, and cerium oxide (CeO₂) nanoparticles, exhibit peroxidase like activity and are commonly used for rapid and visual detection of clinically relevant analytes. For example, AuNP-based colorimetric sensors have been developed for the visual detection of glucose in diabetic patients samples, enabling simple and inexpensive point-of-care testing. Nanozymes based on Fe₃O₄ have been used as hydrogen peroxide and oxidative stress markers in the evaluation of drug formulations, CeO₂ nanoparticles have been employed to monitor the superoxide levels produced by pharmaceuticals which are used in the evaluation of drug-induced oxidative stress [56]. These types of colorimetric and chemiluminescent approaches offer a relatively simple yet effective method for the detection of these oxidative stress markers with high sensitivity from a broad range of reactive oxygen species, which is beneficial in remote environments as well as when performing rapid evaluation testing of pharmaceutical compounds without additional requirement for sophisticated instrumentation.

To summarize, multiple types of mechanisms (e.g., electrochemical mechanisms, optical/plasmonic mechanisms, colorimetric/chemiluminescent mechanisms) can work together in nanobiosensing as complementary strategies, where each mechanism utilizes the unique physical properties of certain nanomaterials to improve one or more of the following characteristics: sensitivity, selectivity, and speed [57]. The integration of these different types of mechanisms has enabled the development of pharmaceutical and clinical devices that can be used for rapidly monitoring drugs, for detecting biomarkers, for screening therapeutics, and for diagnosing diseases. Current research is still focused on how to improve these products with respect to reproducibility of results, the ability to multiplex, and the ability to operate in a real-time manner [58]. As such, nanomaterial-based biosensors are becoming increasingly important tools in the fields of modern pharmaceutical science and precision medicine. Mechanistic pathways of nanomaterial-based biosensing and pharmaceutical applications

Nanomaterial-based platforms for diagnosis

Nanomaterials have transformed the way we conduct diagnostics by providing improved sensitivity, a fast response time, and multiple uses for diagnostic platforms [60, 61]. Nanomaterials integrated into electrochemical, optical, wearable, and theranostic biosensors are now used in both pharmaceutical development and clinical practice; therefore, they provide a method for the early diagnosis of disease, rapid physiological monitoring, and a tailored approach to therapeutic interventions.

Electrochemical biosensors

The main application area of electromechanical biosensors is currently in the diagnostics field, primarily due to their cost-effectiveness, high sensitivity, and ability to be miniaturized (e.g., printed as circuits using the electropolymerization technique). The primary method used to increase the performance of electrochemical sensors is to combine carbon based nanostructures (e.g., graphene, nanotubes, reduced graphene oxide, etc.) and metallic nanoparticles (e.g., AuNPs, Pt, Pd) [62]. The combination of these materials substantially increases the kinetics of their electron transfer, increases the surface area available for immobilizing the biosensor target molecule (the bioreceptor) to enhance the sensitivity of electrochemical sensors in detecting numerous clinically significant biomarkers, and provides improved detection limits (usually measured in nM) for these biomarkers. An example of this is the use of gold nanoparticles in combination with graphene for creating a sensitive biosensor to detect glucose levels in individuals diagnosed with diabetes. Glucose biosensors that incorporate graphene and AuNPs have exhibited very rapid response times (usually in the tens of seconds) and detection limits of approximately nM (nanomolar range). Another instance is DNA-based electrochemical biosensors created using carbon nanotubes and AuNPs that were utilized in pharmaceutical applications to identify genetic mutations related to the pathogenesis of cancer. These biosensors allow medical professionals to diagnose and treat cancer in the early stages and to tailor potential therapies to individual patients. Electrochemical immunosensors targeting specific cancer biomarkers PSA and CEA have been developed, yielding a greater analytical performance than would be expected from the combined use of these nanomaterials with their biorecognition properties because of the synergistic interplay of nanomaterials and their biomolecular recognition properties [63].

Optical biosensors

The use the distinct light-responsive features of nanomaterials to enable multiplexed, real-time, and label-free detection [64, 65]. Nano-sized quantum dots (QDs), gold nanorods, and various types of metallic nanostructures have been widely used for their tunable optical properties and photostability. For example, when conjugated with antibodies and/or aptamers, QDs provide the ability to do simultaneous detection of various biomarkers in pharmaceutical testing, including enzyme assays or drug–protein interactions [66]. Surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy, optical sensors made with either silver or gold nanostructures have provided very sensitive measurements of antibiotics and metabolite levels, which is critical to maintaining product quality during drug manufacture and supporting pharmacokinetic studies on drug development [67].

Biometric data collection through wearables and implants

It is an emerging area of healthcare technology that utilizes flexible and bio compatibly produced nanomaterials. Examples of such types of products include graphene-based textiles, nanowires and hydrogel composites designed for monitoring physiological parameters through sweat, saliva and interstitial fluids [68]. Graphene polymer flexible patches have been create for monitoring diabetic patient's glucose levels, providing real-time information and data sent to their mobile phones or medical devices. Additionally, lactate sensors made using nanowires are available for use with wearable patches, for monitoring athlete’s metabolic status and critically ill patient metabolic response. Hydrogel nanocomposite based implantable devices are also able to monitor the continuous changes in the pH, ionic concentration and drug metabolite levels of interstitial fluids, which provide precise pharmacokinetic profiling [69], and therefore enable more appropriate and personalized therapy options.

Theranostics

The use of the combination of nanobiosensing with a treatment to allow for simultaneous detection and treatment of a disease [56]. The platforms that are based on nanomaterials have been designed to deliver drugs or to provide a form of photothermal therapy once a biomarker has been identified. For instance, gold nanoshells can be utilized in a theranostic platform to both identify tumor biomarkers (using SPR or fluorescence) and to then perform the targeted photothermal ablation (destruction) of cancer cells when exposed to near IR light [70]. Some other examples include, the ability of magnetic nanoparticles to capture circulating tumor cells, allow imaging, and control release of chemotherapy agents [71]. Together, these systems exhibit the merging of diagnosis and therapy, allowing for the best possible treatment outcome and also limiting the overall drug side effects to the patient, by providing real-time information and optimizing the therapeutic effect of pharmaceutical drugs [72].

In summary, nanomaterial-based diagnostic platforms [73] (i.e., electrochemical, optical, wearable devices, adjunctive therapy) present an opportunity for a paradigm shift in the pharmaceutical industry. Not only do they provide extremely sensitive methods for detecting biomarkers, but they also allow for continuous monitoring of the body physiologically and for tailoring medications to specific patients, thus creating the path for the next generation of precision medicine. The careful selection of nanomaterials and the design of the sensor play an important role in achieving optimal analytical performance, biocompatibility, and clinical translation. AI-enhanced nanobiosensing and data analytics

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML)

The diagnostics are evolving into something new by providing an ability to quickly and accurately comprehend complex and high-dimensional datasets via nanomaterial-based biosensors [87]. Many nanobiosensors generate enormous volumes of data due to multiplexed detection, optical imaging, or real-time continuous monitoring. Traditional methods for analyzing these types of data can sometimes be limited by issues such as poor signal quality (i.e., high levels of noise), high variability, or significant overlap among different spectral features that reduce sensitivity/reliability, etc. AI approaches (e.g., deep learning, CNNs, SVMs, etc.) offer solutions to these problems through improved signal processing capabilities, reduced susceptibility to noise, and the creation of predictive analytics based on historical information [89].

Surface-enhanced Raman scattering biosensors are a strong application of AI for nanobiosensing in the field of cancer diagnostics. SERS biosensors use a surface enhanced Raman scattering effect to create unique spectral signatures (fingerprints) for a variety of biomolecules, including proteins, nucleic acids, and small metabolites. One of the challenges in using SERS for biomarker identification is that these spectral signatures are highly complex and can be greatly affected by experimental error. However, AI is capable of classifying chemical structures associated with cancer from SERS spectra, even at very low concentrations [90, 91]. For instance, deep learning based on convolutional neural networks (CNNs) can be used to classify SERS spectra derived from blood samples, making it possible to detect ovarian and breast cancers at an early stage with an accuracy rate of more than 90%.

Nanobiosensors utilize electrochemical as well as optical modalities [92, 93]; therefore, signal fluctuations caused by environmental influences and/or degradation over time will affect their use. Fortunately, artificial intelligence can assist with the analysis of data collected by the nanobiosensors. Through the use of machine learning models, pattern recognition, drift compensation, and enhancement solutions can be applied to improve the quantitative measurement output from sensors such as glucose, lactate, and metabolites of drug substances. In addition to development of better quality quantitative outputs, the ability to rapidly screen large numbers of therapeutic candidates is enhanced, while decreasing error rates associated with the screening process through the use of nanomaterial-based fluorescent and FRET sensors, thereby facilitating drug development and research on drugs.

Internet of things (IoT)

The incorporation of AI-driven analytical approaches into nanobiosensors enhances their ability to process biologicalsignals, recognize complex patterns, and generate predictive diagnostic insights. Furthermore, wearable andimplantable sensor technologies enable real-time data transfer to cloud platforms, allowing AI algorithms to analyzetrends, detect deviations, and forecast health outcomes [94, 95].

Furthermore, AI helps to bring together information from multiple biosensor modalities (example, electrochemical, optical, and wearable biosensors), which ultimately enhances diagnostic dependability [96]. In oncology, integrated solutions provide a means through which both SERS-based identification of tumor markers and electrochemical evaluation of drug metabolites can work together to give clinicians a real-time overview of patient treatment results through AI analysis.

To sum up, we can say that AI-enhanced nanobiosensing is a new generation of pharmaceutical and clinical diagnostics that will allow for the creation of more accurate and real-time detection methods of disease, as well as development of predictive models and personalized medicinal healthcare [97]. This will be made possible by combining multiple advanced technologies utilizing nanotechnology, artificial intelligence (AI) software algorithms, and IoT connected platforms.

Most next generation biosensors will now provide intelligent, automatic and highly reliable diagnostic solutions for all types of applications, from point-of-care testing through precision medicine to remote monitoring of patients’ health. Biomedical applications of nanomaterial-based biosensors

Biosensors based on nanomaterials have been created as influential new technologies for the area of biomedical diagnostics, allowing the fast, selective, and highly sensitive identification of pathological markers, microbial pathogens, and other metabolic indicators [102, 103]. Nanomaterial characteristics such as large surface area, adjustable optical/electrical abilities, and compatibility with biological systems, along with catalytic ability, make these sensors capable of addressing many of the issues associated with standard diagnostic tests. Applications for biosensors using nanomaterials have also greatly increased and now include identifying and diagnosing disease caused by infectious agents, identifying and diagnosing cancer, monitoring patients with heart disease or other metabolic conditions, as well as the developing field of theranostics [104].

Infectious disease detection

Detection of viral and bacterial pathogens has greatly benefited from nanomaterial-based biosensors due to their rapid response, high sensitivity, and capability for miniaturization.

Viral pathogens

These gold nanoparticle (AuNP)-based colorimetric biosensors have been extensively applied for rapid detection of SARS-CoV-2 antigens, producing visible color changes within minutes [105]. Graphene-based field effect transistor (FET) sensors conjugated with specific antibodies have achieved ultra-sensitive detection of viral RNA, including influenza A, zika virus, and SARS-CoV-2, reaching femtomolar sensitivity. Quantum dot (QD)-based fluorescence sensors have been used for multiplex detection of viral nucleic acids, enabling simultaneous monitoring of multiple viral strains in complex biological samples [106].

Bacterial pathogens

These are magnetic nanoparticle assisted electrochemical biosensors can selectively capture and detect Escherichia coli, Salmonella, and Listeria monocytogenes by recognizing bacterial DNA, antigens [107], or outer membrane proteins. Silver nanoparticle-based SERS sensors allow the rapid identification of bacterial pathogens, including multidrug-resistant strains, with high sensitivity. Liposome encapsulated nanomaterials have also been used for amplified colorimetric detection of Mycobacterium tuberculosis, providing rapid, low-cost point-of-care diagnostics for resource-limited settings [108].

Cancer diagnosis

Nanomaterials enhance the signal amplification efficiency of biosensors, enabling rapid readouts and multiplexing capabilities and high-dimensional physical structure flexibility in various biosensor assays [93]. These advantages significantly improve the applicability of biosensors in cancer diagnosis, clinical microbiology, pharmaceutical assays for evaluating antimicrobial efficacy, and pandemic-response strategies.

Gold nanoparticle-based sensors

AuNP-based devices, in conjunction with antibodies, provide a unique platform, these devices utilize a hybrid FET configuration, which allows for high-performance methods to assess clinically relevant protein biomarkers [109], many commercially available AuNP detectors are capable of detecting picomolar protein levels, thus making them ideal candidates for screening for early stage cancers.

Graphene and graphene oxide platforms

Antibodies or aptamers used in conjunction with graphene [110, 111] or rGO-based FET devices are effective for detecting breast, lung, and ovarian cancers; however, there is substantial additional data demonstrating the improved sensitivity of these devices as compared to traditional methods.

SERS-based multiplex detection

The increased sensitivity of silver or gold nanostructures found in SERS sensors allows for the detection of multiple cancer biomarkers in serum or plasma at the same time as they produce enhanced Raman signals [112]. Multiplex SERS immunosensors allow for the identification of PSA, CEA, and AFP at the same time in a single sample, thereby enabling complete biomarker profiling.

Quantum dot-based fluorescence sensors

QD biosensors were designed to allow for the high-throughput identification of cancer-related nucleic acids and proteins, enabling early identification and ongoing monitoring of the response to therapy in order to personalize cancer treatment [113, 114]. The rapid, non-invasive, and highly sensitive nature of these nanomaterial tools provides significant advantages over more traditional ELISA and PCR techniques in diagnostic settings.

Cardiovascular and metabolic disorders

As a result of the use of nanomaterial-based biosensors, real-time cardiovascular and metabolic biomarker detection has improved tremendously [115], enabling earlier diagnosis and ongoing monitoring of cardiovascular/metabolic biomarkers, allowing personalized response interventions.

Glucose monitoring

The continuous and accurate readings of glucose levels in diabetic patients are achieved through continuous glucose monitoring systems that utilize electrochemical sensors built from graphene, carbon nanotubes, or AuNP-modified electrodes [116]. These systems provide patients with a wearable graphene-based patch that continuously tracks glucose levels in either sweat or interstitial fluid (the liquid between cells). The patch is linked to a mobile health platform, enabling remote monitoring of glucose levels.

Cholesterol and lipid profiling

Using AuNPs and PtNP-modified electrochemical electrodes, total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, and triglyceride levels can be detected at extremely sensitive limits, allowing for the assessment of cardiovascular risk and monitoring of therapies.

Cardiac biomarkers

Nanomaterial-enhanced electrochemical immunosensors can detect both troponin I and T in early diagnoses of acute myocardial infarction at the picogram per milliliter level. Multi-analyte sensors can detect multiple analytes (e.g., glucose, troponin, and cholesterol) simultaneously to develop comprehensive metabolic and cardiac profiles, allowing for individualized patient care.

Wearable biosensors

Through the integration of bleeding edge nanotechnology into flexible hydrogel and nanocomposite sensors, providers can track lactate, pH, electrolytes, and a variety of other metabolic markers from a variety of bodily fluids (i.e., sweat, saliva, and interstitial fluids). These innovative devices are quickly becoming standard tools for remote patient monitoring, sports medicine, and intensive care unit use, enabling earlier intervention and greater feedback on therapeutic response for the patient and provider.

Nanotechnology-based sensors provide an improved way of registering metabolic and cardiovascular monitoring, which improves both diagnosis sensitivity and specificity through lowering sample volume, response time, and complex instrumentation. These technologies are thus well suited for point-of-care and personalized medicine methods [117, 118].

To summarize, nanomaterial-based biosensor devices hold tremendous promise across many fields of biomedicine for rapid and multiplexed detection of viruses and bacteria, thus improving control of infections and mitigating responses to pandemics. In addition, they possess higher sensitivity for the detection of cancer biomarkers leading to improved patient outcomes with early diagnosis, therapy monitoring, and personalized therapy development. Finally, continuous and accurate measurement of cardiovascular and metabolic biomarkers will promote better health decision-making and increase the ability of healthcare professionals to deliver their patients actively managed and personalized healthcare. Challenges and future directions

Nanomaterial biosensors have an amazing potential to change the future of diagnostics for different types of infectious diseases and for cancer and metabolic disorders [119]. While many advances have been made in the use of nanomaterials for biosensors, a number of significant issues continue to prevent the widespread use of nanomaterials for clinical applications. A primary problem is the issue of scalability; many of the nanomaterials used in biosensors are produced via a lab-based process, which is difficult to replicate consistently on an industrial scale. This limits both reproducibility on a batch-to-batch basis and also affects performance of nanobiosensors [120]. Small differences in size or surface modification of the nanomaterial can lead to significant differences in electrochemical and optical performance, making nanobiosensors less reliable for clinical use. Furthermore, incorporating complex nanomaterial systems into standardized, commercially available devices presents another challenge, because fabrication typically requires several separate steps, uses specialized equipment, and requires the user to deal with harsh chemicals.

The second major issue associated with the commercialization of nanobiosensors is their clinical validation and regulatory approval. Many sensors have demonstrated excellent levels of sensitivity and the ability to detect multiple analytes at the same time when tested in the lab, but the performance of these sensors when tested with real biological samples (e.g., blood, saliva, and interstitial fluid) can be negatively impacted by nonspecific binding, matrix interference, and unfavorable stability under physiological conditions. The absence of standardized procedures for fabrication, testing, and validating nanobiosensors makes it difficult to compare the diagnostic performances of different nanobiosensors across different studies and delays the adoption of nanobiosensors for clinical applications [121].

In the coming years, multiple strategic directions appear positioned to address the issues noted above. The emergence of biodegradable and self-powered nanobiosensors has attracted much interest, especially in the context of their use in implantable or wearable applications. For example, through the use of biodegradable polymer–nanomaterial composites, temporary monitoring of specific in vivo biomarkers can be accomplished, followed by complete degradation of these composites once no longer needed. Utilizing energy harvesting mechanisms, such as piezoelectric and triboelectric nanogenerators, enables continuous self-monitoring of biomarker levels without the need for an external power source. Another major transition is the incorporation of artificial intelligence (AI) and wearable electronics into a product offering. AI-driven analytics can be used to analyze large amounts of information generated from multiplexed biosensors by reducing signal noise, while also creating future predictions for biomarkers being monitored. The continuous monitoring requirements for glucose, lactate, and pH levels can be satisfied with the use of graphene sheet-based textiles and hydrogel nanocomposite materials wearable devices. By connecting these wearable devices to an AI platform, predictive capabilities to identify when a person is at risk for hyperglycemia or hypoglycemia can be afforded to diabetic people, allowing for timely treatment. Additionally, green synthesis techniques for nanomaterials are quickly emerging as an important trend for sustainable and eco-friendly biosensor manufacturing [122, 123]; for example, biosynthesis of gold and silver nanoparticles with plant extract mediators, rather than toxic chemical reagents, allows for sustainable, low energy biosensor production and creates biocompatible nanomaterials, suitable for application in clinical settings.

Critical comparison and translational perspectives

A critical comparison of nanomaterial-based biosensors highlights key differences in performance, scalability, and clinical applicability. Electrochemical biosensors offer advantages such as portability, low cost, and rapid response, making them ideal for point-of-care use. In contrast, optical platforms like SPR and SERS provide ultra-high sensitivity and multiplexing capability but are limited by complex instrumentation and environmental susceptibility. Wearable and implantable biosensors enable continuous monitoring; however, challenges related to stability, biocompatibility, and signal drift persist. Although artificial intelligence enhances data analysis and predictive diagnostics, issues such as data standardization, model validation, and regulatory approval hinder clinical translation. Additionally, variability in nanomaterial synthesis affects reproducibility. Future efforts should focus on scalable fabrication, improved material stability, and standardized validation, alongside integration of biodegradable systems and robust AI to accelerate clinical adoption and advance precision medicine.

In summary, the advancement of diagnostic capabilities with the use of nanomaterial-based biosensors is significant; however, meeting the challenges of scalability, reproducibility, clinical validation, and sustainability are critical to their future success. Future studies should include research that considers the use of biodegradable materials, a self-powered wearable device, the integration of artificial intelligence, the use of green synthesis methods, and the establishment of standardization protocols for upcoming nanomaterial-based biosensors to be fully effective in precision medicine, real-time monitoring, and global health. Challenges and future directions in nanomaterial-based biosensors

Conclusion

Nanomaterial-based biosensors and diagnostic tests have revolutionized healthcare today because they provide more accurate, sensitive, specific, and fast methods for disease detection. They take advantage of the unique properties of organic, inorganic, and hybrid nanomaterials to create a platform that can detect multiple targets simultaneously, in real-time, and at very low concentrations (for example, HIV), using many different types of biomarkers pathogen, cancer-associated protein, and metabolic indicators, to name just a few. When these platforms are integrated with artificial intelligence (AI) and wearable electronics, they also offer opportunities for predictive analytics, personalized monitoring, and remote healthcare applications. There are challenges with scalability, reproducibility, and clinical application; however, advancements in biodegradable materials, sustainable production methods, and standardized manufacturing protocols are setting the stage for wider acceptance of nanomaterial-based biosensors. In order to maximize the potential of nanomaterial-based biosensors and achieve the next generation of patient-focused diagnostic systems that connect the gap between laboratory research and actual clinical applications, partnerships among material scientists, engineers, healthcare professionals, and data scientists are essential.
