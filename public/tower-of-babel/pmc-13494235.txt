Effects of ivermectin on the activation and desensitization of the human GABAA receptor
Authors: Spencer R Pierce, Allison L Germann, Ka Tak Tsoi, Gustav Akk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIvermectin (IVM) is a widely used antiparasitic drug that acts through allosteric activation and potentiation of the glutamate-gated chloride channel present in neurons and muscle cells of helminths and insects. In mammals, IVM activates several members of the Cys-loop ion channel family, including the GABAA receptor. Although the centrally located GABAA receptors are protected against the actions of IVM by the transporter protein P-glycoprotein 1 present in the blood-brain barrier, many peripheral GABAA receptors are exposed to blood-borne IVM. Here, we have investigated the activating and modulatory effects of IVM on α5β3γ2L and α1β3γ2L GABAA receptors expressed in Xenopus oocytes. We show that IVM is a potent and efficacious agonist of the α5β3γ2L receptor with an EC50 of 0.06 μM and a maximal probability of being in the active state >0.3. IVM, unlike typical agonists of the GABAA receptor, does not desensitize the receptor even in 20-minute-long applications. Receptors desensitized by high concentrations of GABA are reactivated upon exposure to IVM. Coapplication of IVM with GABA or the allosteric agonist, etomidate, manifests as simple additivity of individual responses rather than potentiation. The application of the allosteric agonist, pentobarbital, is without effect or reduces the response to IVM. Overall, GABAA receptor activation by IVM exhibits a number of unique properties not encountered with commonly used or studied GABAergic drugs.Significance StatementPeripheral GABAA receptors are accessible to the widely used antiparasitic agent ivermectin (IVM). This study shows that IVM activates the α5β3γ2L and α1β3γ2L subtypes at therapeutic concentrations. Responses to IVM are not potentiated by the transmitter GABA or the allosteric agonists etomidate or pentobarbital. Receptors activated by IVM do not exhibit desensitization, while receptors desensitized by GABA are reactivated upon exposure to IVM.Key words: GABAA receptor, Ivermectin, Activation, Desensitization, Anesthetics

Introduction

Ivermectin (IVM) (22,23-dihydroavermectin B1) is an antiparasitic drug originally derived through chemical modification of the mixture of avermectins found in the soil bacterium Streptomyces avermitilis.1,2 The mechanism of action of IVM in helminths and insects is allosteric activation and potentiation of the glutamate-gated chloride channel (GluCl) that hyperpolarizes the neuronal and muscle cells, resulting in paralysis and, eventually, death of the target organism. IVM is considered safe in mammals. Although it acts on a number of receptors of the Cys-loop ion channel family, including the α7 nicotinic receptor,3 glycine receptor,4 and the GABAA receptor,5 active removal of IVM by the transporter protein P-glycoprotein 1 ensures that its levels remain low in the brain.6

The GABAA receptor is also expressed in the periphery, where its activation influences a number of physiological processes.7 For example, autocrine GABA signaling regulates insulin release through activation of ternary α1/2β3γ2 receptors in pancreatic β-cells.8 Functional GABAA receptors have been found in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, where they modify cytokine secretion.9 Various subunits of the GABAA receptor are also expressed in the kidney,10 liver,11 and the reproductive system,12,13 although their physiological role and clinical significance are not fully clear. Lastly, functional GABAA receptors, possibly including the α5β3γ2 subtype, are found in dorsal root ganglia and trigeminal ganglia.14, 15, 16 These neurons are not protected by the blood-brain barrier, thereby allowing access to systemically administered drugs, including orally ingested IVM.

Here, we have investigated the activating and modulatory effects of IVM on recombinant α5β3γ2L and α1β3γ2L GABAA receptors. We were motivated by inconsistencies in published data regarding the ability of IVM to act as a potentiator of the GABAA receptor,17,18 and the recent interest IVM has received due to its potential anticancer properties.19,20 We show that IVM potently activates GABAA receptors without inducing desensitization, while receptors desensitized in the presence of high concentrations of GABA are reactivated when IVM is coapplied with GABA. Interestingly, IVM does not show potentiation in coapplication with GABA or the allosteric agonists, etomidate (Eto) and pentobarbital (PEB). The effects of IVM are qualitatively similar in α5β3γ2L and α1β3γ2L subtypes.

Materials and methods

The studies were conducted on recombinant human α5β3γ2L, α1β3γ2L, α5β3, and α1β3 GABAA receptors. The Genbank accession numbers are NM_001165037.2 (α5), NM_000806.5 (α1), NM-000814.5 (β3) and NM_198904.2 (γ2L). The receptors were expressed in Xenopus oocytes, purchased from Xenopus 1. The oocytes were injected with 3.5 ng of cRNA, in the ratio of 1:1:5 (α5 or α1:β3:γ2L) or 5:1 (α5 or α1:β3) per oocyte. The excess amount of γ2L subunit cRNA was used to minimize the expression of binary αβ receptors, while the excess amount of the α subunit cRNA in binary receptors was intended to minimize the expression of homomeric β3 receptors. The oocytes were maintained at 16 °C for 1–2 days before conducting electrophysiological recordings. All experiments were conducted at room temperature. Further details on oocyte handling, injections, and receptor expression can be found in a previous report.21

The electrophysiological recordings were done using a standard 2-electrode voltage clamp. Bath and drug solutions were gravity-applied (flow rate: ∼5 mL/min) from a glass bottle or 30-mL glass syringes through Teflon tubing (ID: 1.07 mm). Solutions were switched manually using 4-port bulkhead switching valves (model: V-101D) and medium-pressure 6-port bulkhead valves (model: V-341) purchased from IDEX Health & Science. Currents were amplified with OC-725C (Warner Instruments) or Axopatch 200B (Molecular Devices) amplifiers, digitized with Digidata 1200 or 1322A digitizers (Molecular Devices), and stored on a PC hard drive using Clampex software (pClamp, Molecular Devices).

Receptor activity was recorded in gap-free or in episodic stimulation mode with 1-second-long sweeps. The traces were low-pass filtered at 30–50 Hz and sampled at 10–20 ms sampling intervals. In the gap-free mode (“current protocol”), the holding potential was –60 mV. In episodic stimulation (“conductance protocol”), the holding potential was –60 mV for the first 150 ms of a sweep, followed by –70 mV for 700 ms, and –60 mV for 150 ms. Conductance was calculated from the difference in current amplitudes between the –70 mV and the –60 mV holding potentials as g = ΔI/ΔV. Additional technical details on recording conductance responses are available in our recent publication.22 Episodic stimulation was mainly employed in long-duration recordings to avoid the influence of changes in ionic gradients and the electrochemical driving force.

The bath solution was ND96 (96 mM NaCl, 2 mM KCl, 1.8 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, 5 mM HEPES) adjusted to pH 7.4 with NaOH. The pipette solution was 3 M KCl. Working solutions containing the chemical agonist (GABA, IVM, Eto, propofol, and PEB) or the antagonist picrotoxin (PTX) were made in ND96. HEPES, GABA, IVM (CAS number: 70288-86-7; >90% ivermectin B1A), propofol, PEB, and most salts were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Eto was purchased from TRC Canada. MgCl2 was obtained from VWR Chemical.

The stock solution of GABA was made in ND96 at 500 mM and stored at –20 °C. The stock solution of IVM was made in DMSO at 20 mM and stored at 4 °C. The stock solution of Eto was made in DMSO at 200 mM and stored at –20 °C. The stock solution of propofol was made in DMSO at 200 mM and stored at room temperature. The stock solution of PEB was made in ND96 at 1–2 mM and stored at room temperature. The stock solutions were diluted with ND96 to working concentrations on the day of the experiment.

Receptor activity was quantified through its probability of being in the active state (PA). PA was calculated by comparing the amplitude of the test current or conductance response to the current or conductance responses to 1 mM GABA + 50 μM propofol and 200 μM PTX. The combination of GABA and propofol was expected to generate a peak response with PA indistinguishable from 1.21 PTX was assumed to inhibit all receptors and generate a PA of 0.23

The concentration-response relationships were fitted with the Hill equation, yielding low- and high-concentration asymptotes, and the EC50 and Hill coefficient of a curve. Results

Functional properties of the α5β3γ2L GABAA receptor

We commenced with basic characterization of the function of the α5β3γ2L receptor. To estimate the level of constitutive activity, we compared current responses to the combination of 1 mM GABA + 50 μM propofol, which was expected to generate a peak PA of 1, and 200 μM PTX, which was expected to inhibit all receptors to reach a PA of 0. The constitutive PA, calculated as IPTX/(IPTX – IGABA+propofol), was 0.0046 ± 0.0029 (n = 8). Geometric means and SD for this and other activation parameters are given in Supplemental Table 1. A sample current trace is shown in Fig. 1A.

The GABA concentration-response relationship was measured by exposing the oocytes to 0.03–100 μM GABA. The responses at each concentration of GABA were converted from units of current to units of PA through normalization to the peak response to 1 mM GABA + 50 μM propofol tested on the same cell and assuming a constitutive PA of 0.0046. Fitting the GABA concentration-response data to the Hill equation yielded a low-concentration PA asymptote of 0.013 ± 0.005 (n = 5), a high-concentration PA asymptote (maximal PA,GABA) of 0.94 ± 0.03, an EC50 of 4.4 ± 1.6 μM, and a Hill coefficient of 1.48 ± 0.13. The concentration-response data are summarized in Fig. 1B. The estimated EC50 is similar to the previously reported value of 6 μM for α5β3γ2L receptors expressed in mouse L929 fibroblast cells.29

Further analysis was done by fitting the GABA concentration-response data with the MWC state equation (eq. 1). The value of L [ = (1 – PA,const)/PA,const] was constrained to 400 based on the estimated PA,const (above), and the number of transmitter binding sites was fixed at 2. The fitted KC,GABA was 14.3 ± 3.7 μM, and cGABA was 0.013 ± 0.003. Thus, the binding of 2 GABA molecules provides –5.0 ± 0.1 kcal/mol of gating energy to stabilize the active state (eq. 2) in the α5β3γ2L receptor.

IVM activates the α5β3γ2L GABAA receptor

IVM directly activates the α5β3γ2L receptor. Sample current responses to 0.03 and 0.3 μM IVM are shown in Fig. 2A. The concentration-response relationship was measured by exposing oocytes to 0.01–1 μM IVM. Due to the essentially irreversible activation by IVM, the recordings were done on separate oocytes, so that each oocyte was subjected to a single application of a single concentration of IVM. For normalization purposes, each oocyte was initially tested with 100 μM GABA (assumed PA of 0.92; Fig. 1B). The IVM concentration-response data are shown in Fig. 2B. With PA,min constrained to 0.0046 (constitutive PA, above), fitting the Hill equation to the mean PA data yielded the maximal PA,IVM of 0.32 ± 0.01 (best-fit parameter ± standard error of the fit), an EC50 of 0.065 ± 0.005 μM, and a Hill coefficient of 1.49 ± 0.13 (n = 5–7 at each concentration).

The PA of the response to IVM was calculated through normalization to the peak response to 100 μM GABA (PA = 0.92) in the same oocyte. Previous work on the related α1β2γ2S subtype has reported a lack of receptor desensitization during prolonged exposure to IVM.17 Here, we measured desensitization of the α5β3γ2L receptor in the presence of IVM. To minimize the influence of a shift in internal Cl– concentration, the experiments were conducted using the conductance protocol (see Materials and Methods22). Twenty-minute-long exposure to 0.1 or 1 μM IVM (n = 5 for each) indicated no measurable desensitization (Fig. 3A). We then tested desensitization of the α5β3γ2L receptor activated by 1 mM GABA in the absence or presence of 1 μM IVM. Exposure to GABA generated a desensitizing response. The ratio of residuals at the end of 4-–6-minute recordings to peak amplitudes was 0.59 ± 0.13 (n = 13). When IVM was coapplied with GABA, the responses exhibited reduced desensitization with the residual to peak ratio of 0.88 ± 0.07 (n = 7). The ratios are statistically significantly different (P = .000009, 2-sample t test). Sample traces are shown in Fig. 3B.

(B) Conductance responses to 1 mM GABA applied alone (left) or in the presence of 1 μM IVM (right). The graph shows the ratios of residual to peak conductances (mean ± SD) in ∼6-minute recordings. The dashed line at residual/peak = 1 indicates a lack of desensitization. (C) Conductance responses to 1 μM IVM applied during the steady-state response to 1 mM GABA applied alone (left) or in the presence of 1 μM of the neurosteroid, 3α5βP (right). The graph shows the potentiating effect of 1 μM IVM (mean ± SD), calculated as the ratio of peak response to GABA or GABA + 3α5βP in the presence of IVM to the residual response to GABA or GABA + 3α5βP before the application of IVM. The dashed line at the effect of IVM = 1 indicates no change in steady-state response in the presence of IVM.

It was hypothesized earlier that the lack of desensitization in the α1β2γ2S in the presence of GABA + IVM stems from IVM driving the return of receptors from the desensitized to the active state.17 To test this hypothesis, we measured the effect of 1 μM IVM on the steady-state response to 1 mM GABA. We reasoned that if IVM acts solely to increase the PA of the response, then its effect is minimal (∼5%) due to the relatively high PA at saturating GABA (Fig. 1B). A larger potentiating effect would support the idea that IVM promotes reactivation of desensitized receptors. In 7 cells, the application of 1 μM IVM increased the steady-state response to 1 mM GABA to 137% ± 26% of the control, reaching 78% ± 7% of the peak response to 1 mM GABA in the same oocyte (Fig. 3C). From this, we infer that IVM acts, at least in part, by promoting transition from desensitized to active state.

Next, we conducted an analogous experiment in the presence of the neurosteroid 3α-hydroxy-5β-pregnan-20-one (3α5βP). Coapplication of an allosteric potentiator enhances the PA,1 mM GABA to ∼1.21 This would preclude the standard PA-based potentiation and, therefore, any remaining potentiation of the steady-state response would reflect reactivation of desensitized receptors. In the first, the control experiment, we measured the potentiating effect of 3α5βP on receptors activated by GABA. Coapplication of 1 μM 3α5βP increased the steady-state response to 1 mM GABA to 105% ± 3% (n = 5) of the control (not shown). The inverse of this, 0.95 ± 0.03, is in good agreement with the estimated maximal PA in the presence of GABA (Fig. 1). We then measured the effect of IVM on the response to GABA + 3α5βP. In 6 cells, coapplication of 1 μM IVM increased the steady-state response to 1 mM GABA + 1 μM 3α5βP to 170% ± 94% of the control (Fig. 3C). The peak response to the triple combination of GABA + 3α5βP + IVM reached 85% ± 11% of the initial peak response to GABA + 3α5βP (ie, peak PA of 1). We infer that IVM promotes the desensitized to active transition in the α5β3γ2L GABAA receptor.

Lack of energetic additivity between IVM and orthosteric or allosteric agonists in the α5β3γ2L GABAA receptor

Coapplication of agonists with distinct binding sites and mechanisms of action results in energetic additivity that manifests as apparent synergistic potentiation.30 IVM is an allosteric agonist of the GABAA and the related GluCl receptor with binding sites in the transmembrane domain at the intersubunit interfaces.18,31 Accordingly, IVM may be expected to potentiate the response to the orthosteric agonist GABA in a manner predictable by the coagonist model.

To test this hypothesis, we compared responses to low GABA in the absence and presence of 0.1 μM IVM. To minimize technical errors resulting from cell-to-cell variability, both responses were measured in the same set of oocytes. The assumption was that increased background activity upon exposure to IVM will result in enhanced response to GABA compared to GABA applied alone (according to eq. 1 with modified L), and that the calculated ΔGGABA (eqs. 3 and 4) are similar in the absence and presence of IVM. Tests in 5 oocytes yielded a mean PA in the presence of 0.04 μM GABA of 0.03 ± 0.01. Exposure to 0.1 μM IVM generated a response with a PA of 0.26 ± 0.06, while coapplication of GABA with IVM increased the response PA to 0.32 ± 0.06. The ΔGGABA were –1.01 ± 0.19 kcal/mol and –0.17 ± 0.07 kcal/mol in the absence and presence of IVM (P = .0013, paired t test). The mean effect size is –0.84 (95% CI [–1.14 to –0.55]) kcal/mol. A sample conductance trace is given in Fig. 4A.

To provide an idea of the magnitude of this difference, we calculated the expected PA,GABA assuming a constant ΔGGABA. With a ΔGGABA of –1.01 kcal/mol (as estimated from the direct activation experiment), the PA in the presence of IVM + GABA is expected to be 0.66 (instead of the observed 0.32). And with a ΔGGABA of –0.17 kcal/mol (as estimated from the potentiation experiment), the PA for the direct activating response to GABA is expected to be 0.006 (instead of the observed 0.03). We infer that GABA and IVM do not act energetically additively. We note that the lack of IVM-potentiation of currents generated by low GABA has been previously reported for the α1β2γ2S GABAA receptor.17

Analogous experiments were done by combining 0.1 μM IVM with low concentrations of Eto or PEB (Fig. 4, B and C). The application of 2 μM Eto elicited a response with peak PA of 0.02 ± 0.01 (n = 6; ΔGEto = –0.93 ± 0.20 kcal/mol). Coapplication of Eto with IVM increased the PA from 0.27 ± 0.12 to 0.30 ± 0.14, with ΔGEto increased to –0.08 ± 0.03 kcal/mol (P = .0002; paired t test). The mean effect size is –0.85 (95% CI [–1.08 to –0.61]) kcal/mol.

Exposure to PEB directly activated the receptor (PA = 0.02 ± 0.01, n = 5; ΔGPEB = –0.87 ± 0.21 kcal/mol). PEB applied on the background of activity elicited by IVM (PA = 0.25 ± 0.11), however, reduced, instead of increasing, activity (PA,IVM+PEB = 0.24 ± 0.11; ΔGPEB = 0.04 ± 0.02 kcal/mol; P = .0006, paired t test). The mean effect size is –0.91 (95% CI [–1.17 to –0.65]) kcal/mol.

In sum, in all agonist combinations, the test agonist (GABA, Eto, or PEB) made a smaller energetic contribution when it was coapplied with IVM compared to when it was applied in the absence of IVM.

As a positive control, we measured responses to 3 μM Eto in the absence and presence of 0.05 μM GABA (Fig. 4D). The application of Eto alone generated a response with PA of 0.03 ± 0.02 (n = 6). In the presence of GABA (PA = 0.02 ± 0.01), the PA of the response to Eto was 0.21 ± 0.25. From eqs. 3 and 4, we calculate that Eto exhibits a slightly lower, rather than higher, efficacy in the absence (–0.82 ± 0.53 kcal/mol) vs presence of GABA (–1.21 ± 0.66 kcal/mol; P = .009, paired t test).

We have previously reported that the energetic contributions by propofol and the steroid etiocholanolone are reduced (ie, ΔGagonist values are more positive) when the background PA is increased.32 Given the differences in PA,background in the presence of 0.05 μM GABA (0.02) in the control experiment and the PA,background in the presence of 0.1 μM IVM (0.25–0.27), we considered the possibility that the more positive ΔGagonist in the presence of IVM is due to higher PA.background rather than the indication of lack of independence.

To test this possibility, we retested the GABA-IVM pair at 0.01 μM IVM. The application of 0.05 μM GABA elicited a peak response with a PA of 0.02 ± 0.01 (n = 5). In the presence of 0.01 μM IVM (PA = 0.04 ± 0.01), GABA generated a response with PA of 0.08 ± 0.03. The calculated (eqs. (3), (4)) ΔGGABA were –0.95 ± 0.17 kcal/mol in the absence of IVM, and –0.47 ± 0.09 kcal/mol (P = .006, paired t test) in the presence of IVM (Fig. 4E). Hence, the lack of energetic additivity for the GABA-IVM pair is observed at low and high PA,background.

New insights into the activation and modulation of the α1β3γ2L GABAA receptor by IVM

Prior work has shown that IVM efficaciously activates the α1β2γ2S GABAA receptor.17,18 Peak activation is, however, not followed by desensitization.17 We have assumed that the α1β2γ2S and α1β3γ2L subtypes have largely identical functional and pharmacological properties. Here, we have followed up on some aspects of previous studies.

In the first experiment, we confirmed (the lack of) receptor desensitization in the presence of IVM. The receptors were activated by 1 mM GABA in the absence (not shown) or presence of 1 μM IVM (Fig. 5A). The ratios of the residual response at the end of 4–6-minute application to peak response were 0.57 ± 0.13 (n = 10) in the absence of IVM, and 1.02 ± 0.12 (n = 7) in the presence of IVM (P = .000003, 2-sample t test).

Next, we measured the effect of IVM on steady-state activity in the presence of saturating GABA. The receptors were activated by 1 mM GABA. Upon reaching the steady-state response, the oocyte was exposed to 1 mM GABA + 1 μM IVM. In 7 cells, coapplication of IVM enhanced the steady-state response to 163% ± 43% of the control. This resulted in the development of a nondesensitizing response with the amplitude near (91% ± 7%) that of the initial peak response to 1 mM GABA. A sample trace is shown in Fig. 5B. In the α1β3γ2L receptor, the maximal PA,GABA is 0.9,27,33 thereby limiting any PA-based potentiation to ∼10%. Based on previous single-channel work,17 we have discarded the possibility that IVM increases single-channel conductance. An analogous experiment was conducted testing the potentiating effect of IVM on the steady-state conductance in the presence of 1 mM GABA + 1 μM 3α5βP (Fig. 5C). In a control experiment, 1 μM 3α5βP increased the steady-state response to 1 mM GABA to 114% ± 5% (n = 5) of the control. This is in agreement with the expected potentiation assuming a PA,1 mM GABA of 0.9. The application of 1 μM IVM increased the steady-state response to 1 mM GABA + 1 μM 3α5βP to 120% ± 7% (n = 8) of the control. We interpret the findings as IVM promoting the desensitized to active transition in the presence of saturating GABA.

We also investigated energetic additivity between IVM and GABA, Eto, or PEB (Fig. 5, D–F). In the presence of 0.1 μM IVM, the application of 0.7 μM GABA increased the PA from 0.23 ± 0.17 (n = 6) to 0.39 ± 0.15 (ΔGGABA = –0.55 ± 0.25 kcal/mol). In the same oocytes, the PA of the response to 0.7 μM GABA alone was 0.02 ± 0.02 (ΔGGABA = –2.80 ± 0.47 kcal/mol; P = .0003, paired t test). The mean effect size is –2.25 (95% CI [–2.88 to –1.62]) kcal/mol. Accordingly, GABA and IVM do not act energetically additively in the α1β3γ2L receptor.

Analogous experiments were conducted by combining IVM with Eto or PEB. The application of 2 μM Eto potentiated the response to 0.1 μM IVM from a PA of 0.47 ± 0.19 to 0.53 ± 0.18 (n = 5; ΔGEto = –0.17 ± 0.05 kcal/mol). In the absence of IVM, the PA in the presence of 2 μM Eto was 0.06 ± 0.03 (ΔGEto = –3.64 ± 0.32 kcal/mol; P = .00002, paired t test). The mean effect size is –3.47 (95% CI [–3.86 to –3.08]) kcal/mol.

The PA of the response to 20 μM PEB was 0.03 ± 0.03 (n = 5; ΔGPEB = –2.90 ± 0.66 kcal/mol). On average, PEB was without effect when applied in the presence of IVM. The PA of the response to 0.1 μM IVM was 0.51 ± 0.10 (n = 5), and the PA of the response to IVM + PEB was 0.51 ± 0.12. The calculated ΔGPEB was 0.01 ± 0.06 kcal/mol in the potentiation experiment (P = .0006, paired t test). The mean effect size is –2.91 (95% CI [–3.72 to –2.09]) kcal/mol.

Energetic additivity for the GABA-Eto and GABA-PEB pairs in the α1β3γ2L receptor has been shown in prior work.34,35 Here, we did not conduct additional control experiments on energetic additivity.

Discussion

IVM is widely used as an antiparasitic in human and veterinary medicine. Its beneficial effect stems from (over)activation of the GluCl channel, present in nematodes, arthropods, and a number of insects. In mammals that do not express GluCl, IVM can directly activate or modulate the function of several Cys-loop receptor channels. These include the GABAA receptor, glycine receptor, and the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor.3, 4, 5 IVM is generally excluded from the brain by the transporter protein P-glycoprotein 1 that is expressed at the blood-brain barrier. Brain penetration is, however, increased in P-glycoprotein knockouts or loss-of-function mutants, or when the transporter protein is pharmacologically inhibited by high levels of IVM or some other clinical drugs.6,36, 37, 38, 39

The GABAA receptor is also expressed in the periphery, where it regulates neuronal activity in the trigeminal and dorsal root ganglia, insulin release in pancreatic β-cells, and cytokine release in T cells.7,40 These receptors may be susceptible to systemically administered IVM. Peripheral GABAA receptors are also exposed to orally or intravenously administered GABAergic drugs, including sedative-anesthetics such as Eto and barbiturates, whose actions may modulate responses to IVM in the periphery. Here, we have tested the ability of IVM to directly activate the GABAA receptor and to modulate the activity generated by the transmitter, GABA, or the allosteric agonists, Eto and PEB.

We report 3 main findings. The first is that IVM directly activates the α5β3γ2L and α1β3γ2L subtypes of the human GABAA receptor. Activation is potent, with EC50 at 0.06 μM in the α5β3γ2L receptor, and nearly irreversible. Previous estimates for IVM EC50s in mammals range from 2 to 7 μM in the α1β1-3γ2 subtypes.17,41,42 The underlying reason for the difference in apparent affinities is not clear. Although the higher constitutive PA in α5β3γ2L vs α1β3γ2L is expected to left-shift the agonist concentration-response relationship, it is also expected to increase the maximal PA for IVM.43,44 The maximal response to IVM in α5β3γ2L (Fig. 2B) is, however, comparable or even lower than that in α1β1-3γ2 subtypes.17,42 We also note that the irreversible action of IVM and our approach of a single application of a single concentration of IVM on individual cells may complicate direct comparison across studies. Using mutated α1β2γ2L and binary α1β2 GABAA receptors, Estrada-Mondragon and Lynch18 proposed that drug interactions with the γ2(+)-β2(–) interface mediate the irreversible effect, while the α1(+)-β2(–) interface mediates the reversible action of IVM. In our hands, IVM-activated α5β3 and α1β3 receptors exhibited both reversible (23% ± 10% and 38% ± 14%, respectively) and irreversible (77% and 62%, respectively) components in a 10-minute wash (Supplemental Fig. 1). One possible reason for this discrepancy is a difference or variability in stoichiometries of the binary receptors, for example, of an α(+)-α(–) interface in our experiments mediating the irreversible effect of IVM. In GluCl, the IVM EC50 estimates range from low nanomolar to 0.4 μM,45,46 while its irreversible action presumably underlies the lethal effect of the drug in invertebrates.

The second main finding pertains to the effect of IVM on GABAA receptor desensitization. In receptors exposed to long applications of saturating GABA, IVM promotes transition from desensitized to active states. When IVM is coapplied with saturating GABA, it prevents the development of desensitization. Further, despite the relatively high PA of the direct activating response to saturating IVM (0.3 in α5β3γ2L activated by 1 μM IVM), no desensitization was observed in recordings lasting up to 20 minutes. Irreversible activation by IVM has been reported previously for the α1β2γ2 subtype.17,18 We are not aware of other agonists or modulators of the GABAA receptor that abolish desensitization in such a manner. We note, however, that in the related α7 nicotinic receptor, type II positive allosteric modulators (eg, PNU-120596) prevent desensitization in the presence of acetylcholine and reactivate desensitized receptors through interactions with or exclusion of membrane cholesterol from its binding site in the receptor.47, 48, 49

And third, GABAA receptor activation by IVM is not energetically additive with activation by the transmitter GABA or the allosteric agonists, Eto or PEB. Energetic additivity for an agonist pair is typically observed when the compounds bind to distinct, nonoverlapping sites and utilize independent mechanisms of action. It manifests as apparent synergistic potentiation. The lack of energetic additivity between GABA and IVM was particularly unexpected given the physical separation between transmitter binding sites in the extracellular domain and the IVM binding sites in the transmembrane domain in Cys-loop receptors.18,31 Energetic additivity has been previously demonstrated for a number of combinations of GABA with allosteric agonists, including Eto and PEB, that act through transmembrane binding sites.35,50 The physical basis for the lack of energetic additivity for the GABA-IVM pair is unclear, but, functionally, it entails impairment of the link between the open activation gate and the state of the orthosteric site so that the orthosteric site remains in the low-affinity state despite the receptor being in the conducting state.

PEB weakly reduced or was without effect on the response to IVM (Fig. 4C; Fig. 5F). Both compounds are expected to bind to α(+)-β(–) and γ(+)-β(–) interfaces.18,51,52 Thus, one possible explanation for these observations lies in the competition for overlapping binding sites by PEB and IVM, where relative occupation of the sites by the compounds and their gating efficacies determine the direction and magnitude of the modulatory effect.53 We consider it unlikely that channel block by PEB at these concentrations masks its potentiating effect.35

A previous study employing mutations to the anesthetic binding sites in the transmembrane domain revealed sensitivity to substitutions at nonbinding interfaces.54 For example, the γ2(S301W) and α1(A291W) mutations (TM3-36′ residues at γ2(+) and α1(+) interfaces, respectively) strongly reduced potentiation by Eto, whose binding sites are at the β(+)-α(–) interfaces. Analogous crosstalk between anesthetic binding sites at different intersubunit interfaces may contribute to the lack of energetic additivity observed for the Eto-IVM pair.

Experiments duplicated on the α5β3γ2L and α1β3γ2L receptors produced qualitatively similar results in all tests. Further, IVM showed slow activation and lack of desensitization in α5β3 and α1β3 binary receptors (Supplemental Fig. 1). Previous studies employing the α1β1γ2 and α1β2γ2S subtypes, as well as native hippocampal GABAA receptors, have also indicated consistency in several key findings, such as potent direct activation and lack of desensitization in the presence of IVM.5,17,41 We infer that the actions of IVM are generally not GABAA receptor subtype-specific.

Given the relative dearth of information on the α5β3γ2L GABAA receptor, we also conducted basic analysis of its activation properties. Compared to the α1β2/3γ2L subtypes, the receptor exhibits a ∼30-fold higher constitutive PA. It is slightly more sensitive to the transmitter with ∼5-fold lower EC50, but with similar maximal PA.

The plasma concentration of IVM can reach up to 0.3 μM depending on the dose and simultaneous food intake.55,56 IVM is metabolized in the liver, predominantly by the CYP3A4 isoform of cytochrome P450 enzymes.57 Accordingly, CYP3A4 inhibitors, eg, the antibiotic clarithromycin and the antihypertensive verapamil, may be expected to further raise IVM concentration in the blood. Overall, we propose that peripheral GABAA receptors are sensitive to IVM at concentrations reached during oral administration. The magnitude of activity of IVM-activated α5β3γ2L and α1β3γ2L receptors (PA ∼0.2–0.5 in the presence of 0.1 μM IVM) is several-fold higher than that of these receptors activated by ambient GABA. In cells expressing GABAA receptors, IVM elicits a nondesensitizing Cl– conductance that is likely to modify internal Cl– concentration, cell input resistance, and the resting membrane potential, and has specific physiological effects (eg, insulin or cytokine release, nociception) associated with the cell type.
