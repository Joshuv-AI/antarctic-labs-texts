A new modic changes classification and its impact on posterior lumbar interbody fusion
Authors: Xiong Zhang, Xiaoxia Qiao, Xiaozhe Zhou, Linfeng Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrent research on Modic Changes (MCs) in Posterior Lumbar Interbody Fusion (PLIF) has predominantly focused on types 1, 2, and 3; however, findings remain inconsistent. To address this gap, we introduce a novel classification system based on endplate sclerosis status (sclerotic vs. non-sclerotic) to better evaluate the influence of MCs on PLIF outcomes.MethodsPatients were divided into Group A (normal endplates), Group B (MCs without endplate sclerosis), and Group C (MCs with endplate sclerosis), with fifty patients in each group. VAS and ODI scores were recorded preoperatively, postoperatively, and during follow-up to assess clinical outcomes. At least one year after surgery, CT scans were performed to evaluate postoperative cage subsidence and bony fusion.ResultsThe postoperative cage subsidence rate recorded were 4% for Group A, 16% for Group B, and 2% for Group C, with a statistically significant difference (P < 0.05). The postoperative bony fusion rate were 98% for Group A, 96% for Group B, and 80% for Group C, with a statistically significant difference (P < 0.05). At the final follow-up, the Visual Analog Scale(VAS) and Oswestry Disability Index(ODI) scores in Group C were significantly higher than those in Groups A and B (P < 0.05), indicating worse clinical outcomes.ConclusionMCs without endplate sclerosis increase the risk of cage subsidence after PLIF surgery but do not effect clinical outcomes. MCs with endplate sclerosis are associated with a reduced bony fusion rate following PLIF surgery, leading to worse long-term clinical outcomes.Keywords: PLIF, Modic changes, Endplate sclerosis, Bony fusion, Cage subsidence

Introduction

MCs refer to bone marrow and endplate lesions adjacent to degenerative lumbar intervertebral discs, characterized by abnormal signals in the endplate and underlying bone as observed through MRI [1, 2]. Importantly, these changes are not associated with bone marrow malignancy, sepsis, or seropositive rheumatic disorders [3]. The overall prevalence of MCs is approximately 5.8%, with higher rate observed in middle-aged and older adults exhibiting degenerative spinal changes. Additionally, MCs are more common among patients receiving chronic glucocorticoid therapy and those with poor posture [4, 5]. The endplate is the main pathway for nutrient delivery to the intervertebral disc, so MCs accelerates disc degenerationby disrupting this process [6], leading to the progression of lumbar degenerative diseases. PLIF is a mature surgical technique often used in clinical treatment.

There is ongoing controversy in the literature regarding the effect of MCs on cage subsidence and bony fusion in PLIF surgery [7–9]. Recently, some scholars have suggested that type 3 MCs can prevent cage subsidence in lumbar interbody fusion surgery [10, 11]. Compared with type 3 MCs, patients with type 1 and type 2 MCs have a higher fusion rate after lumbar fusion surgery (type 3 MCs both have endplate sclerosis).Therefore, we propose that endplate sclerosis is a critical factor influencing both cage subsidence and bony fusion outcomes in PLIF patients with MCs.

However, recent research indicates that endplate sclerosis is not exclusive to type 3 MCs, but can also occur in all MC types, including types 1, 2, 3, and mixed forms [12]. Therefore, the traditional type 1, 2 and 3 MCs classification is no longer applicable. In light of these findings, We will reorganize the groups based on endplate sclerosis or non-sclerosis: normal (without MCs), degeneration (MCs without endplate sclerosis), and sclerosis (MCs with endplate sclerosis). This study aims to investigate the effect of endplate sclerosis or non-sclerosis of MCs on cage subsidence and bony fusion in PLIF.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

We conducted this study in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study’s protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third Hospital of Hebei Medical University. Informed consent was waived.

Patient population

This retrospective matched study included 1151 patients, with or without MCs, who underwent PLIF with cage implantation at our hospital between January 2020 and December 2022. All patients received preoperative lumbar spine CT and MRI examinations. The study was approved by the institutional review board, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Patients were selected based on specific inclusion and exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Age ≥ 18 years; (2) single-segment PLIF; (3) Voluntary acceptance of surgery with signed informed consent. The exclusion criteria included: (1) Diagnosis of spinal tumors, spinal tuberculosis or spinal infection; (2) History of prior lumbar spine surgery; (3) Severe osteoporosis (T-score ≤ −3.5); (4) Psychiatric disorders or depression leading to non-compliance with treatment; (5) Incomplete follow-up data.

Of the 1151 patients initially screened 652 were excluded for various reasons: 4 less than 18 years; 118 who underwent multisegmental surgery; 390 were unable to follow up due to personal reasons, lacking follow-up or incomplete imaging; 6 due to a diagnosis of spinal tumors, spinal infections, or spinal tuberculosis; 63 with a history of prior lumbar spine surgery; and 71 with severe osteoporosis (T-score ≤ −3.5).

A total of 499 patients were included in the study. They were evaluated for MCs using MRI scans and assessed for endplate sclerosis through sagittal reconstruction CT images. The Hounsfield Unit(HU) ratio (HU value for an affected endplate/HU value for a normal endplate, HU value: CT value) can be used to assess cases where the evaluation of endplate sclerosis is controversial [10]. Patients were categorized into three groups: Group A (without MCs, 365 patients), Group B (MCs without endplate sclerosis, 76 patients), and Group C (MCs with endplate sclerosis, 58 patients). To minimize selection bias, 1:1 matching was performed based on age, gender, surgical grade, diagnosis, duration of surgery, and intraoperative bleeding. The caliper was set to 0.2 times the standard deviation of the propensity score (specific value: 0.05). After matching, a total of 150 patients were included in the final analysis, with 50 patients in each group (Fig. 1).

Data collection

Patient characteristics, including age, sex, Body Mass Index (BMI), hypertension, diabetes, spondylolisthesis, Lumbar lordosis(LL). We measured Vertebral Bone Quality(VBQ) and Endplate Bone Quality(EBQ) using lumbar T1 weighted MRI. We calculated the novel VBQ score for each patient by dividing the median signal intensity of L1-4 vertebrae by the signal intensity of Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF) [13] (Formula 1; Fig. 2).

Representative image of ROI used to calculate the VBQ score and EBQ score

For EBQ measurement, the area of interest was the subchondral bone 3 mm above and below the operating level, where the average signal intensity of the two endplates was divided by the signal intensity of the cerebrospinal fluid space at the L3 level [14] (Formula 2; Fig. 2).

VAS and ODI scores were collected preoperatively, postoperatively, and at follow-up. Preoperative lumbar X-rays, CT scans, and MRI were employed to diagnose LDD and assess endplate status. At least one year following surgery, CT scans were conducted to assess cage subsidence and bony fusion.

Outcome measures

Cage subsidence was measured using the Marchi criteria [15]: Grade 0 indicates a loss of 0–24% of postoperative disc height; Grade I represents a loss of 25–49%; Grade II signifies a loss of 50–74%; and Grade III reflects a loss of 75–100%. Grades 0 and I were considered low-grade while Grades II and III were considered high-grade subsidence.

Bony fusion was evaluated using the Brantigan and Steffee criteria [16]: Grade I denotes significant imaging pseudarthrosis; Grade II indicates probable radiographic pseudarthrosis; Grade III reflects uncertain imaging status; Grade IV represents probable radiographic fusion; and Grade V confirms radiographic fusion. Fusion was classified as Grades IV or V, while Grades I to III indicated non-fusion.

Binary logistic regression was employed to identify factors associated with cage subsidence and bony fusion in patients undergoing PLIF.

All surgical procedures were performed under general anesthesia by one of two senior spine surgeons. Two orthopedic surgeons independently evaluated MCs, endplate sclerosis, bony fusion, and cage subsidence to assess interobserver reliability. To evaluate intraobserver reliability, one of the surgeons repeated the radiological measurements one week later. The Intraclass Correlation Coefficients (ICCs) for intraobserver and interobserver reliability were found to be 0.896 and 0.904, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 27.0. Descriptive data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, mean with a 95% confidence interval (CI), and as frequencies and percentages, as appropriate. For normally distributed variables, one-way ANOVA was followed by Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons. For non-normally distributed variables, the Kruskal-Wallis test was followed by Dunn’s test. A binary logistic regression model was implemented to assess risk factors, incorporating age, sex, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, spondylolisthesis, LL, VBQ, and EBQ as predictors. Results

Patient demographics

A total of 150 patients participated in this study, consisting of 53 males and 97 females, with a mean age of 52.60 years (range: 22–78 years). Among the cohort, 38 patients (25%) were diagnosed with spondylolisthesis, 56 patients (37%) had hypertension, and 38 patients (25%) were identified as having diabetes mellitus. The patients were categorized into three groups: Group A (without MCs), Group B (MCs without endplate sclerosis), and Group C (MCs with endplate sclerosis), with each group comprising 50 cases. Analysis of baseline data indicated that there were no statistically significant differences among the three groups regarding age, sex, BMI, spondylolisthesis, LL, hypertension, or diabetes (Table 1).

VAS and ODI in three groups

There were no significant differences in VAS and ODI scores among the three groups before and after the operation. The mean final follow-up VAS scores were 1.58 ± 0.84 for Group A, 1.64 ± 0.80 for Group B, and 3.14 ± 1.01 for Group C. Similarly, the mean follow-up ODI scores were 13.40 ± 3.15 for Group A, 13.52 ± 2.58 for Group B, and 20.06 ± 3.18 for Group C, with a statistically significant difference observed (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5; Table 4). At the final follow-up, VAS and ODI scores in Group C were significantly higher than those in Groups A and B, with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) (Fig. 6; Table 5).

Risk factors

A binary logistic regression model was employed to analyze various risk factors, including age, sex, BMI, hypertension, spondylolisthesis, LL, VBQ, and EBQ. The analysis demonstrated that EBQ is an independent risk factor for postoperative cage subsidence in patients undergoing PLIF (OR, 0.260; 95% CI: 0.068–0.993; P = 0.049). Furthermore, EBQ was also identified as an independent risk factor for postoperative bony fusion (OR, 0.078; 95% CI: 0.016–0.376; P = 0.002) (Tables 6 and 7).

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to investigate the impact of endplate sclerosis or non-sclerosis of MCs on PLIF, specifically focusing on cage subsidence and bony fusion. Considering the limitations of previous classification systems, we propose a novel grouping method based on the condition of the endplates: normal (without MCs), degeneration (MCs without endplate sclerosis), and sclerosis (MCs with endplate sclerosis). The results indicated that postoperative cage subsidence rate were significantly higher in patients within the endplate degeneration group compared to those in the normal and endplate sclerosis groups. Conversely, the postoperative bony fusion rate in the endplate sclerosis group was lower than those in both the normal and endplate degeneration groups, leading to worse long-term clinical outcomes according to VAS and ODI scores. Additionally, binary logistic regression analysis revealed that EBQ was an independent risk factor for both postoperative cage subsidence and bony fusion in patients undergoing PLIF.

Higher cage subsidence rate in MCs without endplate sclerosis group

Cage subsidence is primarily attributed to axial compression loads at the interface between the cage and the endplate [17]. Various risk factors for cage subsidence have been reported in the literature [18, 19]. Liu et al. suggested that subsidence is not directly related to symptom recurrence or imaging fusion, as a robust pedicle screw and rod system is employed to stabilize the posterior elements, supplemented by additional bone grafting around the cage [20]. However, many researchers contend that the subsidence of the interbody fusion device significantly affects postoperative outcomes [21, 22].

In our study, we found that MCs without endplate sclerosis increased the incidence of cage subsidence; however, the final follow-up VAS and ODI scores indicated that it does not significantly affect postoperative outcomes. MCs without endplate sclerosis results in a reduction of endplate bone mass, creating a substantial density difference between the cage and the endplate. When subjected to longitudinal loading, this density difference heightens the likelihood of stress collapse in the endplate. Furthermore, MCs contributes to paraspinal muscle atrophy and decreased bone density, which ultimately leads to reduced lumbar stability and screw strength, thereby elevating cage subsidence rate [23, 24]. In contrast, MCs with endplate sclerosis may impart greater mechanical properties to the endplates, consequently reducing the likelihood of cage subsidence [10].

Therefore, preventive measures should be implemented. Overaggressive decortication of the endplates should be avoided in patients with endplate degeneration, and excessive manipulation may disrupt blood supply and increase the risk of subsidence [25]. Additionally, the use of wide and large cages can offer superior segmental stability in biomechanical testing [26]. Preoperatively, careful measurement of the disc height at the operated segment is essential, and the appropriate type of cage should be selected intraoperatively.

Lower bony fusion rate in MCs with endplate sclerosis group

Kuisma et al. suggested that MCs with endplate sclerosis may represent a regenerative process involving new bone formation in the bone marrow [27]. They speculated that the reactive sclerosis of MCs observed on CT scans reflects bone healing. In contrast, Li et al. argued that endplate sclerosis reflects densely mineralized bone within the vertebral body rather than a true regenerative process [28]. They noted that such mineralization may restrict blood flow to the vertebral-graft interface, resulting in delayed or failed bony fusion, making endplate sclerosis a critical factor influencing fusion outcomes in patients undergoing PLIF.Yang et al. further proposed that the reduced short-term bony fusion rate in cases with endplate sclerosis is primarily due to inflammatory factors and impaired blood supply to the fusion area [29]. The production of inflammatory mediators, such as IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α, inhibits the osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells by activating the NF-κB pathway or Notch receptors, potentially leading to trabecular bone formation between the graft and vertebral bone [30, 31].

Our findings align with these observations, confirming that MCs with endplate sclerosis reduces the bony fusion rate, causing adverse clinical outcomes according to the final follow-up VAS and ODI scores. Therefore, preoperative CT should be routinely performed to identify endplate sclerosis and guide the surgical plan. When encountering endplates sclerosis, the hardened tissue can be carefully scraped until uniform blood leakage is observed, or bone grafting in these areas can be avoided. In cases of severe endplate sclerosis, increasing the amount of bone graft through bilateral decompression grafting or performing posterior posterolateral bony fusion may help prevent complications associated with postoperative fusion failure [28]. Additionally, using a larger cage can increase the contact surface between the cage and the endplate, promoting bony fusion.

EBQ

Binary logistic regression analysis identified higher EBQ scores as an independent risk factor for postoperative cage subsidence in patients undergoing PLIF, consistent with previous studies [14]. Additionally, we found that lower EBQ scores is an independent risk factor for bony fusion in patients undergoing PLIF; however, further research is needed to explore this relationship in greater detail.

Conclusion

MCs without endplate sclerosis increase the risk of cage subsidence rate after PLIF surgery but do not affect clinical outcomes. MCs with endplate sclerosis can reduce the bony fusion rate after PLIF surgery, resulting in poorer long-term clinical outcomes, as indicated by VAS and ODI scores. In addition, EBQ is an independent risk factor for both cage subsidence and bony fusion in PLIF patients.
