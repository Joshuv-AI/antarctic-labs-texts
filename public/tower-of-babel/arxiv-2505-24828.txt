2.
Introduction

We consider a general version of the Fermi-Pasta-Ulam-Tsingou (FPUT) lattice with long range interactions. Specifically:

(1)

\ddot{u}_{j}=\sum_{m\geq 1}\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(u_{j+m}-u_{j})-\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(u_{j}-u_{j-m}).

Here
j\in{\bf{Z}}
,
t\in{\bf{R}}
,
u_{j}(t)\in{\bf{R}}
and
\Phi_{m}:{\bf{R}}\to{\bf{R}}
. The usual interpretation is that these are the equations of motion for a mechanical system consisting of infinitely many particles, indexed by
j

j^{th}
particle at time
t

u_{j}(t)
and

\Phi_{m}(r)
gives the
potential energy associated with the interaction between a particle and the particles
m
spots down the line, in either direction. In this setting
(
1

is simply Newton’s law. There are multiple other interpretations, ranging from power transmission and molecular dynamics to automotive traffic, but here we stick with the usual mechanical point of view.

Our goal in this paper, and its sequel, is to understand the existence of traveling wave solutions to systems of the form (
1
).

The lattice (

Assumption 1

There exists

Of course

2.1.
The dispersion relation, phase speed and classification thereof

Linearizing (
1
) about the equilibrium
r_{*}j
gives

(4)

\ddot{u}_{j}=\sum_{m\geq 1}\alpha_{m}(u_{j+m}-2u_{j}+u_{j-m}).

Plugging the plane wave
Ansatz

u_{j}(t)=e^{i(kj-\omega t)}
into (
4
) and some routine algebra/trigonometry results in
the dispersion relation

(5)

\omega^{2}=\theta(k):=\sum_{m\geq 1}4\alpha_{m}\sin^{2}(mk/2).

We also define

(6)

\lambda(k):={\theta(k)\over k^{2}}=\sum_{m\geq 1}\alpha_{m}m^{2}\sinc^{2}(mk/2),

along with

(7)

c_{0}^{2}:={\lambda(0)}=\sum_{m\geq 1}\alpha_{m}m^{2}.

\sqrt{\lambda(k)}
is the phase speed and
c_{0}
is variously referred to as the “speed of sound” or the “critical speed.”

We make a few observations about
\theta(k)
: (a) it is
2\pi
-periodic in the wavenumber
k
, (b) it is even in
k
, and (c)
\theta(0)=0
.
Indeed, given that we have not specified the coefficients
\alpha_{m}
at this stage, we see something rather surprising:
\theta(k)
can be
any
function meeting (a), (b) and (c). We codify this:

Lemma 1
.

Let
\theta(k)
be a piecewise continuously differentiable, even,
2\pi
-periodic function which vanishes at
k=0
. Then there are coefficients
\alpha_{m}
so that the linear dispersion relation for (
1
) is given by
\omega^{2}=\theta(k)
.

Proof.

The hypotheses on
\theta(k)
imply that it is equal to its Fourier cosine series. That is to say
\theta(k)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}b_{m}\cos(mk)
for some coefficients
b_{m}
. The condition that
\theta(0)=0
tells us that
b_{0}=-\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}b_{m}
which in turn tells us that
\theta(k)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}b_{m}(-1+\cos(mk))
. Letting
\alpha_{m}=-b_{m}/2
along with the half angle formula finishes the proof.
∎

We will not make direct use of Lemma
1
in what follows, but that there is so much flexibility in
\theta(k)
means
that it is often easier to work
in terms of it and

as opposed to conditions on the coefficients

.

2.2.
The traveling wave equation and long wave limit

We make the traveling wave
Ansatz
:

(8)

u(j,t)=r_{*}j+\varepsilon U_{\varepsilon}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad x:=\varepsilon(j-c_{\varepsilon}t).

Following
[
4
]
, we have incorporated the “long wave scaling” here, made manifest by the presence of the parameter
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
. Ultimately
we will use
\varepsilon
as a bifurcation parameter.
Substitution of (
8
) into (
1
) and a bit of effort demonstrate that

W_{\varepsilon}(x):=U_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(x)
and the wave-speed
c_{\varepsilon}
solve the “nonlinear eigenvalue” equation

(9)

\varepsilon^{2}c_{\varepsilon}^{2}W_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{m\geq 1}m{\mathcal{A}}_{\varepsilon m}[\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(r_{*}m+m\varepsilon^{2}{\mathcal{A}}_{\varepsilon m}W_{\varepsilon})-\varsigma_{m}].

The maps
{\mathcal{A}}_{\varepsilon m}
(as in
[
4
]
) are instances of the averaging operators

{\mathcal{A}}_{h}F(x):={1\over h}\int_{x-h/2}^{x+h/2}F(y)dy.

The derivation of (
9
) is carried out in the proof of Lemma 2 in
[
4
]
for the finite range problem and it carries over to this case with essentially no extra work; we omit the details.
Additionally at this stage we assume that
c_{\varepsilon}
has the form

(10)

c^{2}_{\varepsilon}=c_{0}^{2}-{1\over 2}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)\varepsilon^{2}.

The reason for the unusual prefactor on the
\varepsilon^{2}
term will become clear below; see Remark
1
.

Using the expansion (
2
) in (
9
) converts it to

(11)

{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}W_{\varepsilon}={\mathcal{Q}}_{\varepsilon}(W_{\varepsilon},W_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon^{2}{\mathcal{P}}_{\varepsilon}(W_{\varepsilon})

where

\begin{split}{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}&:=\varepsilon^{-2}\left(c_{0}^{2}-{1\over 2}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)\varepsilon^{2}-\Lambda_{\varepsilon}\right),\\
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}&:={\sum_{m\geq 1}\alpha_{m}m^{2}{\mathcal{A}}^{2}_{\varepsilon m}},\\
{\mathcal{Q}}_{\varepsilon}(V,W)&:=\sum_{m\geq 1}\beta_{m}m^{3}{\mathcal{A}}_{\varepsilon m}[({\mathcal{A}}_{\varepsilon m}V)({\mathcal{A}}_{\varepsilon m}W)],\\
{\mathcal{P}}_{\varepsilon}(W)&:={1\over\varepsilon^{6}}\sum_{m\geq 1}m{\mathcal{A}}_{\varepsilon m}[\Psi_{m}^{\prime}(m\varepsilon^{2}{\mathcal{A}}_{\varepsilon m}W)].\end{split}

Note that all of the mappings defined above respect parity in that they map even functions to even functions and so on.

In
[
4
]
it is shown that taking the Fourier transform of
{\mathcal{A}}_{h}F(x)
gives

\widehat{{\mathcal{A}}_{h}F}(k)=\sinc(hk/2)\widehat{F}(k)

and so if we take the Fourier transform of
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}F
we get

\widehat{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}F}(k)=\lambda(\varepsilon k)\widehat{F}(k)

where
\lambda
is as in (
6
). This calculation demonstrates the central role that
\lambda
plays in this business.
If we (formally) expand
\lambda(\varepsilon k)
about
k=0
, use
c_{0}^{2}=\lambda(0)
and recall that
\lambda
is even we see that

\widehat{{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}W_{\varepsilon}}(k)=\varepsilon^{-2}(c_{0}^{2}-{1\over 2}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)\varepsilon^{2}-\lambda(\varepsilon k))\widehat{W}_{\varepsilon}=\left(-{1\over 2}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)(1+k^{2})+{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{2}k^{4})\right)\widehat{W}_{\varepsilon}(k).

The usual identification of the operator
\partial_{x}^{2}
with the Fourier multiplier
-k^{2}
motivates setting

(12)

{\mathcal{B}}_{0}:=-{1\over 2}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)(1-\partial_{x}^{2}).

We will provide precise estimates on
{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}-{\mathcal{B}}_{0}
below.
Moreover, in
[
4
]
it is shown that
{\mathcal{A}}_{h}
converges strongly to the identity on
L^{2}
, which leads us to putting

(13)

{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}(V,W):=bVW\text{ where }b:=\left(\sum_{m\geq 1}\beta_{m}m^{3}\right).

Again, precise estimates on
{\mathcal{Q}}_{\varepsilon}-{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
are forthcoming. Similarly, the estimates for
\Psi^{\prime}
in (
2
) indicate that
{\mathcal{P}}_{\varepsilon}
is formally
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
with respect to
\varepsilon
.

Thus, if we put
\varepsilon=0
in (
11
) in accordance with (
12
) and (
13
), we arrive at

(14)

-{1\over 2}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)\left(W_{0}-W_{0}^{\prime\prime}\right)=bW_{0}^{2}.

This equation is well-known to have a unique even nontrivial homoclinic solution
given by

(15)

W_{0}(x):=-{3\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)\over 4b}\sech^{2}\left({x\over 2}\right).

Obviously this is nonsense if
b=0
and that is why we assume it is not in Assumption
2
.
Note that
W_{0}
is smooth and exponentially decaying and so, for all

,
\|W_{0}\|_{H^{s}}<\infty
.

Remark 1
.

If, at (
10
), we put
c_{\varepsilon}^{2}=c_{0}^{2}+\mu\varepsilon^{2}
and repeat the above computation, at (

) we would
have the equation

The rest of the paper is dedicated to determining what happens to

The keen-eyed reader will note that terms in

contain high order derivatives which in turn raise the grim specter of singular perturbation.

Core estimates

3.1.
Estimates of
{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}

The linear operator
{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}
is the central operator in this work and in particular we need to understand its behavior as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
.

Our main estimates are contained in the following:

Proposition 2
.

Suppose that (
1
) is Type I.
For all
s\in{\bf{R}}
and
\varepsilon>0
,
{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}
is a bounded and invertible map from

to itself. Moreover,
there exists
C_{\mathcal{B}}>0
and
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{B}}>0
so that the following estimates hold for all

and

(16)

\|{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}F\|_{H^{s}}\leq C_{\mathcal{B}}\varepsilon^{-2}\|F\|_{H^{s}},

(17)

\|{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}F\|_{H^{s}}\leq C_{\mathcal{B}}\|F\|_{H^{s}},

(18)

\|({\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}-{\mathcal{B}}_{0})F\|_{H^{s}}\leq C_{\mathcal{B}}\varepsilon^{\sigma}\|F\|_{H^{s+\sigma}}

and

(19)

\|({\mathcal{B}}^{-1}_{\varepsilon}-{\mathcal{B}}^{-1}_{0})F\|_{H^{s}}\leq C_{\mathcal{B}}\varepsilon^{\sigma}\|F\|_{H^{s}}.

Remark 2
.

The most important estimate in the above is (

Proof.

Recall that

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}F}(k)=\varepsilon^{-2}\left(c_{0}^{2}-{1\over 2}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)\varepsilon^{2}-\lambda(\varepsilon k)\right)\widehat{F}(k).

Let

Type I conditions tell us

and
\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)<0
. Thus the multiplier for

satisfies

{1\over 2}|\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)|\leq\varepsilon^{-2}\left(c_{0}^{2}-{1\over 2}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)\varepsilon^{2}-\lambda(\varepsilon k)\right)\leq(c_{0}^{2}-\underline{\lambda})\varepsilon^{-2}+{1\over 2}|\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)|.

And so the usual tools for estimating Fourier multiplier operators

3

\begin{split}\left|{\varepsilon^{2}\over\lambda(0)-{1\over 2}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)\varepsilon^{2}-\lambda(\varepsilon k)}+{2\over\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)\left(1+k^{2}\right)}\right|=&{2\over|\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)|}{|T_{2}(\varepsilon k)|\over|-{1\over 2}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)\varepsilon^{2}-T_{1}(\varepsilon k)|\left(1+k^{2}\right)}\end{split}

\leq&C\sup_{|k|\leq k_{*}/\varepsilon}{|\varepsilon k|^{\sigma+2}\over(-{1\over 2}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)\varepsilon^{2}+\mu_{*}\varepsilon^{2}k^{2})(1+k^{2})}\\

On the other hand, we have

\begin{split}&\sup_{|k|\geq k_{*}/\varepsilon}\left|{\varepsilon^{2}\over\lambda(0)-{1\over 2}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)\varepsilon^{2}-\lambda(\varepsilon k)}+{2\over\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)\left(1+k^{2}\right)}\right|\\
\leq&\sup_{|k|\geq k_{*}/\varepsilon}\left|{\varepsilon^{2}\over\lambda(0)-{1\over 2}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)\varepsilon^{2}-\lambda(\varepsilon k)}\right|+\sup_{|k|\geq k_{*}/\varepsilon}\left|{2\over\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)\left(1+k^{2}\right)}\right|\end{split}

Conditions (ii) and (iv) imply that, for any

That is to say, the denominator in the first term on the right is bounded from zero uniformly.
Thus the first term is

We conclude

(23)

\begin{split}&\sup_{|k|\in{\bf{R}}}\left|{\varepsilon^{2}\over\lambda(0)-{1\over 2}\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)\varepsilon^{2}-\lambda(\varepsilon k)}+{2\over\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)\left(1+k^{2}\right)}\right|\leq C\varepsilon^{\sigma}.\end{split}

This implies (
19
), and we are done.

∎

3.2.
Estimates of
{\mathcal{A}}_{h}

The averaging operators
{\mathcal{A}}_{h}
play an important role and we record some key estimates:

Theorem 3
.

There exists
C_{\mathcal{A}}>0
so that the following hold for all
h>0
,
s\in{\bf{R}}

(24)

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{A}}_{h}F\|_{H^{s}}\leq\|F\|_{H^{s}},

(25)

\|{\mathcal{A}}_{h}F\|_{H^{s+1}}\leq C_{\mathcal{A}}(1+h^{-1})\|F\|_{H^{s}},

(26)

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{A}}_{h}F-F\|_{H^{s}}\leq C_{\mathcal{A}}h^{2}\|F^{\prime\prime}\|_{H^{s}},

(27)

Proof.

For
H^{0}=L^{2}
the details can be found in

4
]
, but the case for general

is more or less no different and so we largely omit
the details. We do provide a slightly different take on the proof of (

We have
\displaystyle{\mathfrak{F}}[{\mathcal{A}}_{h}F-F](k)=(\sinc(hk/2)-1)\widehat{F}(k).

The function

has a zero of order two at

and this, with some Calculus trickery,
implies that there is a constant

so that

via

\displaystyle\widehat{\Theta_{h}F}(k)={\sinc(hk/2)-1\over(hk)^{2}}\widehat{F}(k),

we have, for all

Some algebra on the Fourier side shows us that

3.3.

{\mathcal{Q}}_{\varepsilon}

For the bilinear term

Proposition 4

Suppose Assumption

s\geq 1

For (

4

4

By which we mean the famous estimate

and its best friend

to see that

For (

4.
Solitary waves

Let
\lambda(k)
be Type I and take Assumptions
1
and
2

as given.
We prove the existence of solitary wave solutions, which is to say a nontrivial solution of (

We begin at (

Routine computations show that

From Proposition
2

Now we claim that

(44)

\|{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}{\mathcal{Q}}_{\varepsilon}(W_{0},V)-{\mathcal{B}}_{0}^{-1}{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}(W_{0},V)\|_{H^{1}}\leq C\varepsilon^{\sigma}\|V\|_{H^{1}}.

And so we see that

]

5.1.

Theorem
6
recaptures the bulk of the main result for the finite range problem as studied
in
[
4
]
(their Corollary 14). In addition to an assumption on the smoothness of the potentials

similar to our Assumption
1
, they have three major conditions:

(a)

and
\gamma_{m}
are zero except
at finitely many choices for

(b)

the coefficients

are always non-negative and at least one is positive,

(c)

Condition (a) immediately implies the convergences in Assumption
2
and (c) is exactly

. The conditions also imply their lattice is Type I. First of all,

for all

and we know immediately that

for all

and so
we have condition (i). Likewise, since

and so we have (ii).

Next, since
\sinc(k)
is
C^{\infty}
on
{\bf{R}}
,
condition (a) tells us that
\lambda(k)
is
C^{\infty}
on
{\bf{R}}
, as it is just a finite sum of
\sinc
s.
The two estimates in (iii) are then just easy consequences of Taylor’s theorem. The second holds with

\sigma=2
. A small note here is that
\lambda^{\prime}(0)=\lambda^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)=0
because
\lambda(k)
is even.

As for (iv), look at

\lambda(0)-\lambda(k)=\sum_{m\geq 1}\alpha_{m}(1-\sinc^{2}(mk/2)).

If
k\neq 0
then
1-\sinc^{2}(mk/2)>0
for all
m\geq 1
. Thus, since all the terms in the last sum are positive, the sum itself is positive for all
k
.
And we have
\lambda(0)>\lambda(k)
if
k\neq 0
. And since
\lambda(k)\to 0
as
|k|\to\infty
and is continuous, we can conclude
\sup_{|k|\geq k_{*}}\lambda(k)<\lambda(0)
for any non-zero
k_{*}
.

So the conditions they impose on their lattices easily fulfill all the assumptions needed in Theorem
6
and our result applies with
\sigma=2
.
Their condition (b) does allow them to prove that their solitary wave is positive and unimodal.

5.2.
NNN Lattices

We now consider next nearest neighbor lattices as presented in
[
16
]
or
[
15
]
. Those problems (after some elementary
changes of variables and renaming constants) correspond to setting

\Phi^{\prime}_{1}(r)=r+\beta_{1}r^{2}+\Psi_{1}^{\prime}(r),\quad\Phi^{\prime}_{2}(r)=gr+\beta_{2}r^{2}+\Psi_{2}^{\prime}(r)\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi^{\prime}_{m}(r)=0\text{ when $m>2$}.

The functions
\Psi^{\prime}_{1}
and
\Psi^{\prime}_{2}
are assumed smooth and enjoy the cubic type estimates in (
3
). That is to say, their lattices pass Assumption
1
(with
r_{*}=0
).
The constant
g
is what we call
\alpha_{2}
;
g
is the name in both
[
16
]
and
[
15
]
and so we use it here for consistency. The convergences in Assumption
2
are met because all the coefficients are zero after
m=2
.
We need
b\neq 0
to hold, which here means that
\beta_{1}\neq-8\beta_{2}
. In
[
16
]
, they specify
\beta_{1}\neq 0
(which they call “
a
”) and
\beta_{2}=0
, so our condition encompasses theirs. In
[
15
]
they require
0<\beta_{2}<\beta_{1}/2
(their equation (7), where they use
\alpha
where we use
\beta
). Again,
our condition encompasses theirs.

This lattice is Type I when
g>-1/16
. Here is a quick explanation. We have

\lambda(k)=\sinc^{2}(k/2)+4g\sinc^{2}(k)\quad\text{and}\quad c_{0}^{2}=1+4g.

This is clearly bounded below and we have (i).
Then we compute
\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)=-{1\over 6}-{8g\over 3}
which tells us that

\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)<0

when
g>-1/16
. As in the previous section, the smoothness of
\lambda(k)

and Taylor’s theorem gives the estimates in (iii) with
\sigma=2
.
More differential Calculus can be used get condition (iv) for
g>-1/16
.

And so we can conclude the existence of solitary waves in NNN lattices as in (
5.2
) so long as
g>-1/16

and
\beta_{1}\neq-8\beta_{2}
. In particular we have the results of Theorem
6
with
\sigma=2
.
This is what is found in
[
16
]
, though the calculations there are not fully rigorous. In
[
15
]
the authors study the case when
g\in(-1/4,-1/16)
. It turns out that this case is an example of what we call Type II and that’s a story for another time.

5.3.
Calogero-Moser

Now we show that certain generalized Calogero-Moser lattices (as studied in
[
6
,
5
]
) meet the assumptions
of Theorem
6
and thus establish the existence of solitary waves. We recall that this lattice corresponds to putting
\Phi_{m}(r)=1/r^{a}

where
a>1
is a parameter. Our particular interest is when
a>3
.
In (
8
) we put, for simplicity,
r_{*}=1
.

The first thing is to compute
\alpha_{m}
,
\beta_{m}
and
\gamma_{m}
.
We have

\alpha_{m}=\Phi^{\prime\prime}_{m}(m)=a(a+1)m^{-a-2}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta_{m}=\Phi^{\prime\prime\prime}_{m}(m)/2=-{1\over 2}{a(a+1)(a+2)}m^{-a-3}.

It doesn’t take too much effort to show that
\gamma_{m}=a(a+1)(a+2)(a+3)m^{-a-4}
works in (
3
).
Which is to say we have Assumption
1
and the estimates in (
3
).
Since
a>3
we see that the convergences in Assumption
2
are met as well. And since
\beta_{m}<0
for all
m
it follows that
b\neq 0
, as we want.

So we need to confirm that the lattice is Type I. First of all, since
\alpha_{m}>0
for all
m
, we have
\lambda(k)\geq 0
and so (i) is easily confirmed.
Indeed this also implies that
\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)<0
so we have (ii).

Establishing (iii) is complicated as
\theta(k)
is not a
C^{\infty}
function. Indeed, its regularity depends
on
a
. First note that
\theta(k)=\theta_{a}(k)
where

\theta_{a}(k):=4a(a+1)\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}{1\over m^{a+2}}\sin^{2}(mk/2)=2a(a+1)\left(\sum_{m\geq 1}{1\over m^{a+2}}-\sum_{m\geq 1}{1\over m^{a+2}}\cos(mk)\right).

We claim that
\theta_{a}(k)
is

•

C^{4,a-3}
on
{\bf{R}}
when
a\in(3,4]
,

•

C^{5,a-4}
on
{\bf{R}}
when
a\in(4,5)
,

•

C^{5,1-\delta}
on
{\bf{R}}
, for all
\delta>0
, when
a=5
,

•

C^{5,1}
on
{\bf{R}}
when
a>5
.

Most of this claim is a consequence of this following,
which is Theorem 4.2 in
[
7
]
:

Theorem 7
.

Suppose that
f(k)=\sum_{m\in{\bf{Z}}}f_{m}e^{imk}
and, for some
r\in{\bf N}
and
q\in(0,1)
,

\sup_{m\in{\bf{Z}}}|f_{m}||m|^{r+q}<\infty
. Then
f\in C^{r-1,q}
on
{\bf{R}}
.

All the statements in the claim come directly from this, except the case when
a=4
. But if you are persistent in doing lots of integrals you can find that

\theta_{4}(k)={2\over 9}\pi^{4}k^{2}-{5\over 18}\pi^{2}k^{4}+{1\over 6}\pi|k|k^{4}-{1\over 36}k^{6}

on
k\in(-\pi,\pi]
and is the
2\pi
-periodic extension of the above elsewhere. And that function is easily checked to be
C^{4,1}
on
{\bf{R}}
.
Note also that if
a>5
, Theorem
7
implies that
\theta_{a}(k)
is smoother than
C^{5,1}
. That extra regularity does not translate into any particularly interesting extra features of the solution and so we simply lump all cases with
a>5
into the one class.

Next put

\eta_{a}(k)=\theta_{a}(k)-{1\over 2}\theta_{a}^{\prime\prime}(0)k^{2}-{1\over 24}\theta_{a}^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}(0)k^{4}.

So then

\lambda(k)=\lambda_{a}(k):={\eta_{a}(k)\over k^{2}}+{1\over 2}\theta_{a}^{\prime\prime}(0)+{1\over 24}\theta_{a}^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}(0)k^{2}

and

\lambda_{a}^{\prime\prime}(k)={\eta_{a}^{\prime\prime}(k)\over k^{2}}-{4\eta_{a}^{\prime}(k)\over k^{2}}+{6\eta_{a}(k)\over k^{4}}+{1\over 12}\theta_{a}^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}(0).

If
a\in(3,4]
then
\theta_{a}(k)\in C^{4,a-3}
and

(47)

\eta_{a}(k)=\int_{0}^{k}\int_{0}^{k_{1}}\int_{0}^{k_{2}}\int_{0}^{k_{3}}\theta_{a}^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}(k_{4})dk_{4}dk_{3}dk_{2}dk_{1}.

Since we know
\theta_{a}^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}(k)
is in
C^{0,a-3}
we have

|\eta_{a}(k)|\leq C\left|\int_{0}^{k}\int_{0}^{k_{1}}\int_{0}^{k_{2}}\int_{0}^{k_{3}}|k_{4}|^{a-3}dk_{4}dk_{3}dk_{2}dk_{1}\right|\leq C|k|^{a+1}.

Similarly one can show (by differentiating the formula for
\eta
) that

|\eta^{\prime}_{a}(k)|\leq C|k|^{a}\quad\text{and}\quad|\eta_{a}^{\prime\prime}(k)|\leq C|k|^{a-1}.

So we conclude
that

(48)

|\lambda_{a}^{\prime\prime}(k)-{1\over 12}\theta_{a}^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}(0)|\leq C|k|^{a-3}.

Note that we see from this calculation that
\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)=\theta^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}(0)/12
.
Furthermore, if
a\in(4,5)
all of the above can be repeated with one more integral and one more derivative in
(
47
) and we wind up with (
48
) unchanged. Likewise, if
a>5
you wind up once again at (
48
)
but the right hand side is
Ck^{2}
. And for
a=5
you have
C|k|^{2-\delta}
for any
\delta>0
.

Next, for
a\in(3,5)
if we use the FTOC again, followed by the previous estimate:

(49)

\begin{split}\left|\lambda_{a}(k)-\lambda_{a}(0)-{1\over 2}\lambda_{a}^{\prime\prime}(0)k^{2}\right|&=\left|\int_{0}^{k}\int_{0}^{y}[\lambda_{a}^{\prime\prime}(z)-\lambda_{a}^{\prime\prime}(0)]dzdy\right|\\
&\leq C\left|\int_{0}^{k}\int_{0}^{y}|z|^{a-3}dzdy\right|\\
&\leq C|k|^{a-1}.\end{split}

Thus the second estimate in (iii) holds
with
\sigma=a-3
, provided
a\in(3,5)
. If
a>5
one has the same but with
\sigma=2
. And
for
a=5
you can take
\sigma=2-\delta
, where
\delta>0
is arbitrary.

Then note that by taking a small enough value of
k_{*}
, the first estimate in (iii) is a byproduct of the second estimate and (ii). So now we have all of (iii). As for (iv), recall the
\alpha_{m}>0
which, as we saw in Section
5.1
, implies

\lambda(k)<\lambda(0)
for all
k
. As in that section, the continuity of
\lambda(k)
and its convergence to zero implies (iv).
Thus we have all the hypotheses necessary to deploy Theorem
6
, with
\sigma
taken as in the previous paragraph. That is we have:

Corollary 8
.

Let
\Phi_{m}(r)=\Phi(r)=1/r^{a}
and
r_{*}=1
. For all
a>3
there exist
\varepsilon_{a}>0
,
\kappa_{a}>0

and
\sigma_{a}>0
so that the following hold for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. There is a unique function
V_{\varepsilon}\in E^{3}
with

\|V_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}}\leq\kappa_{a}
so that

c_{\varepsilon}^{2}=a(a+1)\zeta(a)+{a(a+1)\over 12}\zeta(a-2)\varepsilon^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad W_{\varepsilon}(x)=-{\zeta(a-2)\over 4(a+2)\zeta(a)}\sech^{2}(x)+\varepsilon^{\sigma_{a}}V_{\varepsilon}(x)

solve (
9
). If
a\neq 5
then
\sigma_{a}=\min\{a-3,2\}
. If
a=5
, then

\sigma_{a}=2-\delta
for any
\delta>0
.

Remark 3
.

In the above
\displaystyle\zeta(a):=\sum_{m\geq 1}m^{-a}
is the famous zeta-function.
The profile
W_{\varepsilon}(x)
is smooth because
1/r^{a}
is smooth. Additionally, one can show that
W_{\varepsilon}(x)
is negative
for all
x
. The argument is exactly the same as the one which is used in
[
5
]
to establish the positivity
of traveling waves in the case
a\in(4/3,3)
and so we leave it out. Lastly, we note that the case
a=3
remains open; formal estimates from
[
6
]
indicate that there should be a KdV-like solitary wave solution. But the method presented here is insufficient in its present form to establish this.
