Integrated analyses reveal a potential role for PFKFB4 in M2 macrophage polarization and hepatocellular carcinoma progression
Authors: Siying Wang, Qinqiu Zhang, Tian Li, Zibo Xu, Xuyu Chen, Chen chen Wei, Zhaoxia Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the third most lethal malignant tumor worldwide, PFKFB4 has emerged as a key metabolic enzyme with critical roles in cancer progression and immune regulation. However, its precise role in hepatocellular carcinoma remains unclear.MethodsIn this study, we performed integrated analyses, including single-cell transcriptomics, bioinformatics, enrichment analysis, in vitro functional assays, and in vivo validation, to explore the role of PFKFB4 in HCC progression and its influence on macrophage polarization.ResultsSingle-cell transcriptomic analysis identified elevated PFKFB4 expression in macrophages, correlating significantly with M2 polarization markers. Functional assays demonstrated that knockdown of PFKFB4 suppressed HCC cell proliferation, migration, and promoted apoptosis, while its overexpression exhibited the opposite effects. Mechanistically, inhibition of PFKFB4 reduced activation of the MEK/ERK signaling pathway, suggesting this pathway as a potential mediator. Additionally, Co-culture experiments revealed that knockdown of PFKFB4 in tumor cells led to a reduced M2 polarization of macrophages, suggesting its role in immune modulation.ConclusionCollectively, our findings indicate that PFKFB4 drives hepatocellular carcinoma progression through activation of the MEK/ERK signaling pathway and, in parallel, influences tumor progression via tumor cell–mediated modulation of M2 macrophage polarization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-026-04326-y.

Introduction

Cancer has become the predominant cause of early mortality globally in the 21st century [1]. Projections indicate that the global incidence of all cancers may double by 2070 relative to 2020 levels [2]. Among all malignancies, liver hepatocellular carcinoma ranks as the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with approximately 906,000 new cases and 830,000 deaths annually [3]. Despite significant advances in understanding cancer pathogenesis and developing therapeutic strategies, cancer-related mortality continues to rise [4]. In recent years, the expansion of publicly accessible online resources has greatly facilitated the availability of genomic data. A comprehensive analysis of genes that play key roles in cancer initiation and progression may offer critical theoretical insights into the underlying mechanisms of tumor development.

Glycolysis is a fundamental metabolic pathway in human cells that generates key substrates essential for diverse biosynthetic and energy-producing processes. An increasing body of evidence suggests that glycolysis constitutes the central metabolic pathway in tumor cells, with its byproduct, lactate, also implicated in modulating responses to cancer immunotherapy [5, 6]. Phosphofructo-2-kinase (PFK-2) is a critical regulator of glycolysis, playing a key role in modulating glycolytic flux in eukaryotic cells [6]. PFK-2 is abundantly expressed in the mammalian liver and exists in four isoforms, collectively known as the 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase family (PFKFB1–4). Among them, the PFKFB4 gene, encoding phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase 4, is located on chromosome 3p21.31, comprises at least 14 exons, and has been well-characterized in humans [7, 8]. In the present study, researchers demonstrated that PFKFB4 dynamically regulates the levels of fructose-2,6-bisphosphate (F26P), an allosteric activator of phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK1), a rate-limiting enzyme in the glycolytic pathway [9, 10]. PFKFB4, which is upregulated under hypoxic conditions, is essential for the growth and survival of various cancer cell lines. Given that many cancers exhibit markedly increased glucose uptake, and several targeted agents have been shown to suppress tumor progression by inhibiting glucose metabolism, PFKFB4 kinase inhibitors are considered promising candidates for the development of novel anticancer therapies [11, 12].

The tumor immune microenvironment plays a crucial role in the initiation, progression, and metastasis of hepatocellular carcinoma [13]. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are predominantly characterized by an alternatively activated (M2-like) phenotype and play a pivotal role in promoting cancer progression and facilitating immune evasion within the tumor microenvironment [14, 15]. TAMs represent one of the most abundant immune cell populations within the tumor microenvironment (TME) and persist throughout all stages of liver cancer progression. Consequently, targeting TAMs has emerged as a promising strategy in the development of cancer immunotherapies [16].

Here, we performed a comprehensively analysis of the PFKFB4 gene using datasets from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), integrating single-cell RNA sequencing analysis, immune cell infiltration analysis, gene expression and survival analysis, co-expression analysis, gene-set enrichment analysis. These bioinformatics approaches were further combined with experimental validation to systematically evaluate the role of PFKFB4 in hepatocellular carcinoma progression and its association with the tumor immune microenvironment.

Methods

Case collection

Ten pairs of hepatocellular carcinoma and adjacent non-tumorous liver tissues were collected from patients diagnosed with hepatocellular carcinoma and undergoing surgical resection at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. All tissue samples were histopathologically diagnosed by the Department of Pathology and were accompanied by detailed clinicopathological data. Fresh tissue samples were immediately preserved in liquid nitrogen until further use. For histological analysis, tissues were fixed, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and subjected to immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence assays.

Single-cell clustering and cell type identification

This study utilized the GSE202642 dataset, which contains single-cell RNA sequencing data from liver cancer patients, including 7 liver cancer samples and 4 normal control samples. The dataset is available through the GEO and can be accessed via the GEO platform. Initially, raw data underwent preprocessing, including the removal of low-quality cells and genes. Quality control was performed using the R package SeuraV5, with filtering criteria including a gene count between 500 and 5000 per cell, a mitochondrial gene expression ratio below 20%, and a minimum gene expression threshold. Then, the data were normalized and standardized to mitigate technical noise. Subsequently, principal component analysis was applied for dimensionality reduction, and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was used for further visualization of cellular heterogeneity. Based on the Principal Component Analysis (PCA) results, clustering analysis was performed to identify distinct cell populations, with cell type identification further validated by known marker genes.

CytoTRACE analysis

To investigate the differentiation process and identify the differentiation origin of the cell populations, we performed pseudotime analysis using the Cytotrace2 tool. Cytotrace2 is a method designed to estimate the differentiation potential of individual cells based on their gene expression profiles. This approach allows us to quantify the “differentiation trajectory” of cells and pinpoint the most likely origin of differentiation. By applying Cytotrace to our dataset, we assessed the trajectory of cell differentiation across different populations, identifying the starting points (roots) of differentiation within the population. This analysis enabled us to infer the developmental pathways and hierarchical relationships between the different cell types.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis

To investigate the key genes involved in the transition of macrophages, we performed trajectory analysis using the Monocle3 R package. First, macrophage populations were identified from the scRNA-seq dataset based on known marker genes. Cells corresponding to M0, M1, and M2 macrophages were isolated for further analysis. We then applied Monocle2 to infer the pseudotime trajectory of macrophage differentiation, focusing on the M0-to-M2 transition. The algorithm constructs a continuous trajectory by ordering cells along a pseudo-temporal axis, reflecting their progression through different differentiation stages. Key genes along the pseudotime trajectory were identified by comparing gene expression at different stages of macrophage polarization. Genes showing significant changes in expression over pseudotime were considered potential key regulators of macrophage polarization.

Gene expression analysis

The PFKFB4 gene expression data for 33 types of tumors were extracted from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) datasets. The R package “BiomaRt (version 2.44.4)” was used to transfer gene ID. The ggplot2 (version 3.3.3) package of R (version 3.6.3) was used for the expression differential analysis.

Survival prognosis analysis

To evaluate the prognostic significance of gene PFKFB4, we utilized the GEPIA2.0 web platform, which provides survival and expression data for over thirty cancer types [17]. The ‘Survival Map’ module was used to visualize the prognostic relevance of PFKFB4, with hazard ratios calculated using the Cox proportional hazards model. The cut-off values for high and low expression groups were both set at the 50%, and the time axis was standardized in months. Subsequently, we downloaded the overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) datasets for PFKFB4 to generate the survival plots shown below.

Genetic alteration and methylation analysis

The cBioPortal platform (https://www.cbioportal.org) was used to investigate the genetic alterations and mutation sites of PFKFB4 across various tumor types. The distribution of these alterations was quantified by calculating their respective frequencies. At the same time, we performed a waterfall plot analysis of the top 20 mutant genes in the PKFFB4 low and high expression groups through the “maftools” software package [18].

The MEXPRESS online tool collected TCGA data, thus providing a convenient interface to illustrate the relationship between PFKFB4 expression and methylation sites [19].

Immune infiltration analysis

To further investigate the potential role of PFKFB4 in the six aforementioned immune cell types, we employed public analysis platforms including Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER) and Sangerbox, integrating multiple computational algorithms such as EPIC, QUANTISEQ, and MCP-COUNTER. Specifically, we utilized the TIMER algorithm to generate scatter plots depicting immune cell infiltration, thereby assessing the differential expression of PFKFB4 across various immune cell types [20].

Further, data from The Human Protein Atlas and single-cell Liver Cancer Atlas were used for principal component analysis (PCA). After reducing the dimension of the dataset, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) dimensionality reduction algorithm was applied to make samples show separated clusters and distributions in low dimensional space [21].

Analysis of PFKFB4 expression and clinical relevance

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated using the ‘pROC’ (v1.17.0.1) and ‘ggplot2’ (v3.3.3) packages in R (v3.6.3). The ‘rms’ (v6.2.0) and ‘survival’ (v3.2.10) packages were used to construct a nomogram model. Gene expression data were obtained from the TCGA database and subjected to log2 transformation. To further investigate the potential role of PFKFB4 in tumor proliferation, we analyzed its association with classical proliferation markers [22]. Next, we calculated the relationship between PFKFB4 gene and these markers via TCGA database.

Enrichment analysis of PFKFB4 related gene

The protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of PFKFB4 was constructed using the STRING database (Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins) [23]. with ‘Homo sapiens’ selected as the reference species. Over fifty PFKFB4-related interactions were identified and analyzed. For visualization, the network was imported into Cytoscape software (v3.8.2) (http://www.cytoscape.org) [24].

The GEPIA2.0 platform was used to retrieve the top 200 genes co-expressed with PFKFB4. Correlation analyses were subsequently performed via the TIMER database to assess immune relevance. Functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, were conducted using the R packages ‘clusterProfiler’ (v3.14.3), ‘ggplot2’ (v3.3.3), and ‘GOplot’ (v1.0.2) in R (v3.6.3). A false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of < 0.05 was applied to determine statistical significance [25].

LinkedOmics (https://www.linkedomics.org/) is a publicly accessible database that integrates multi-omics data across 33 kinds of TCGA cancer types and cohorts from the Clinical Proteomics Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) [26]. In this study, LinkedOmics was utilized to identify genes differentially expressed in association with PFKFB4 in the TCGA liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC). Pearson correlation analysis was performed to evaluate gene expression associations, and the results were visualized using volcano plots and heatmaps.

Cell culture, cell transfection, and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Human hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines (Huh-4 and Huh-7), the murine hepatoma cell line Hepa1-6, and the normal human liver cell line THLE2 were obtained from laboratory stocks. Cells were cultured in either DMEM or RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ and 90% relative humidity. Culture media were refreshed every 2–3 days.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was performed to assess gene expression levels across different hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines. Cells were harvested and lysed using TRIzol reagent for total RNA extraction. We extracted the corresponding RNA and reverse transcribed into cDNA. The primers were displayed in Supplementary table.

Plasmid vectors and small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) were transfected into hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines using Lipofectamine 2000 and incubated for 48 hours prior to RNA extraction for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis. Three independent PFKFB4-targeting siRNAs (si-PFKFB4 #1, #2, and #3) and a negative control siRNA (si-NC) were utilized. Total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript III First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit for qPCR (Vazyme, China). qRT-PCR was conducted with SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. PFKFB4 expression was quantified across independent experiments, and relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH serving as the internal control. The target sequences used for gene knockdown were: si2-PFKFB4: 5’-GACGUGGUCAAGACCUACAAATT − 3’ and si3-PFKFB4: 5’-CCUGUGGCAUAUGGUUGUAAATT-3’. The shRNA plasmid sequence used in the animal model was as follows: 5’- CCAGAGTCGCATCGTTTATTA − 3’. A similar experimental procedure was also used for gene overexpression experiments.

Functional assays for cellular phenotyping

First, Huh-4 (2000 cells/well) and Huh-7 (2000 cells/well) cells planted into 96 well plates after 48 h of transfection. Incubated at 37 °C for 6 h, cell viability was measured at 450 nm after 0 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h and 96 h, using a kit (APEBIO, K1018, China). Secondly, cells were seeded evenly in a 24-well plate at a density of 100,000 cells per well 48 h post-transfection and incubated for 24 h. Fresh washing buffer was prepared, and EdU reagent was added to the wells at a 500:1 ratio with 10% complete culture medium. After incubation for 2–4 h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and washed with the washing buffer. Permeabilization solution (Beyo, P0097) was then added and incubated for 10 min. Subsequently, click buffer and Hoechst staining solution were sequentially added, followed by thorough washing (BeyoClick, C0085). Finally, fluorescence microscopy was used to capture images of the transfected cells. For colony formation assays, transfected cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1,000 cells per well. Following incubation for approximately 7–14 days, cell colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. The resulting colonies were subsequently counted and imaged.

For migration assays, hepatocellular carcinoma cells were resuspended and seeded into the upper chambers of transwell inserts containing medium supplemented with 1% FBS. Complete medium with 10% FBS was added to the lower chambers to serve as a chemoattractant. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, migrated cells on the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet for 20–30 min. For the wound healing assay: cells were evenly seeded into a 6-well plate and cultured until reaching 80–100% confluence. A sterile pipette was used to create straight scratch in each well, generating cell-free gap, and the scratch area was marked. After washing the wells with PBS, the medium was replaced with culture medium containing 1–2% serum. Images of the wound area were captured under a microscope at 0 h, 24 h, and 48 h.

Apoptosis was assessed using an apoptosis detection kit (Yeasen, 40304ES60). The culture medium from the 6-well plate was collected into a 5 mL flow cytometry tube, while adherent cells were detached by enzymatic digestion, washed, and subsequently combined into the same tube. The cells were centrifuged. The pellet was resuspended in PBS, followed by another centrifugation step. The resulting pellet was then resuspended in 400 µL of binding buffer, and 5 µL of Annexin V-Alexa Fluor 647 along with 10 µL of propidium iodide (PI) were added. After incubation in the dark for 15 min, apoptosis was analyzed using a flow cytometer.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) assay

For the immunohistochemistry part, tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. The sections were baked at 55–60 °C to melt the paraffin, followed by deparaffinization with xylene, rehydration through a graded ethanol series, and washing. Antigen retrieval was performed using 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at temperatures at 95 °C. After washing, sections were incubated with 3% H₂O₂ at room temperature for 10 min, followed by blocking.

Primary antibodies were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 30 min. Detection was performed using DAB staining, and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Finally, the sections were dehydrated, mounted, and observed under a microscope.

For the immunofluorescence part, tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by incubation with 0.1% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 10 min. After washing with PBS three times, the samples were incubated with 5% BSA at room temperature for 30 min to block non-specific binding. The primary antibody, diluted appropriately, was incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by washing with PBS. The samples were then incubated with the secondary antibody for 1 h in the dark and washed again with PBS. DAPI (1:1000) was used for nuclear staining, and the samples were incubated in the dark before washing. Finally, the slides were mounted and observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Isolation and induction of bone marrow-derived macrophages, co-culture, and flow cytometry analysis

Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were sacrificed following immersion in 75% ethanol for disinfection. The femur and tibia were isolated, and the bone marrow was flushed out with 10 mL PBS after cutting off the epiphyses. The cell suspension was passed through a 70 μm cell strainer, centrifuged, and treated with red blood cell lysis buffer. After washing with PBS and counting, cells were resuspended in 10 mL of pre-warmed culture medium supplemented with M-CSF (2 µL/mL, PeproTech) to induce macrophage differentiation. The culture medium was refreshed periodically.

The macrophages were seeded in the lower chamber of a 6-well plate at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells/well. Tumor cells from either the control or treatment groups were seeded in the upper 0.4 μm transwell inserts at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells/well. After 24–48 h of co-culture, cells were collected for further flow cytometry analysis.

For gene knockdown, BMDMs were transduced with lentiviral shRNA targeting PFKFB4. Transduction was performed in the presence of polybrene (8 ug/mL), followed by medium replacement after 24 h.

Protein extraction and western blot

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, R0020) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, P1045). Cells were lysed on ice for 30 min with intermittent vortexing, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Western blotting was performed to assess protein expression. Briefly, protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA), membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature with gentle shaking. β-Tubulin was used as the control. Antibodies against ERK, MEK, phospho-ERK (P-ERK), and phospho-MEK (P-MEK) were purchased from Proteintech. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system.

Lactate measurement

Lactate concentrations were determined using a lactic acid content assay kit (Applygen, E2063) following the manufacturer’s protocol. For extracellular lactate analysis, conditioned media were collected, centrifuged to remove debris, diluted 1:4 in assay buffer, and subjected to enzymatic reaction. For intracellular lactate measurement, cell lysates were prepared in extraction buffer and cleared by centrifugation. Samples and standards were incubated with reaction mixture at 37 °C for 20 min, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Lactate concentrations were calculated from a standard curve.

Establishment of subcutaneous tumor model in mice

After culturing Hepa1-6 cells with stable PFKFB4 knockdown and control cells, the cells were harvested and counted to prepare a cell suspension at a concentration of 4 × 10⁴ cells/µl. A total of 50 µl of each cell type was subcutaneously injected into 10 male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old), which were randomly assigned to groups. Tumor volume was measured every four days and calculated using the formula: V = 0.5 × length × width². On day 28, mice were euthanized, and subcutaneous tumors were excised for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was carried out using R software (version 4.3.1) and GraphPad Prism (version 9). All in vitro experiments were conducted in triplicate. Statistical analyses were performed using Student’s t-test for normally distributed data, and non-parametric tests were applied to data with non-Gaussian distributions. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (p < 0.05, p < 0.01, *p < 0.001).

Results

Gene expression analysis

To investigate the transcriptional profile of PFKFB4 across various cancer types, RNA-seq data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) were analyzed. PFKFB4 mRNA expression was found to be significantly elevated in 24 out of 33 tumor types for which complete data were available (Fig. 1A). Notably, high expression levels were observed in liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), brain lower-grade glioma (LGG), glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA), cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC), cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma (OV), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), rectum adenocarcinoma (READ), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), sarcoma (SARC), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma (PCPG), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC), and uterine carcinosarcoma (UCS), with statistical significance (P < 0.05). In the TCGA database, LIHC represents hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). These results showed that PFKFB4 are highly expressed in various cancer species relative to adjacent normal samples at mRNA expression levels.

Survival prognosis analysis

As PFKFB4 expression were significantly upregulated in most cancer types, it is necessary to probe their prognostic values. The log-rank test confirmed that the expression of PFKFB4 was notably associated with poor prognosis of overall survival (OS) for cancers of LIHC(p = 0.0069), CESC (p = 0.029), KICH(p = 0.034), KIRP (p = 0.00081), MESO(P = 0.034) (Fig. 1B). On the other hand, disease-free survival (DFS) analysis indicated that the upregulated PFKFB4 was significant associated with poor prognosis of LIHC (p = 0.028), KICH (p = 0.045), SKCM (p = 0.0046), THCA (p = 0.019) (Fig. 1C). In sum, high level of PFKFB4 was correlated with poor prognosis in many cancers, especially in LIHC and KICH.

Genetic alteration and methylation analysis

Next, we evaluated the mutation status of PFKFB4 in various cancer types. We identified PFKFB4 gene missense mutations, amplification and deep deletion in 1% of hepatocellular carcinoma patients from the cBioPortal database (Figure S1A). We further found that PFKFB4 was mutated in multiple tumors (Figure S1C). The alteration rate of PFKFB4 gene is the highest in patients with “amplification” as the primary type of LIHC. In addition, we obtained the alternative splicing information of LIHC from TCGA SpliceSeq database (Figure S1B). Importantly, we divided the LIHC sample data into PFKFB4 low expression group and PFKFB4 high expression group, and analyzed the differences between these two groups of gene sets. The top 20 mutant genes in each group were plotted as waterfall plots (Figure S1D). The top three mutated genes in PFKFB4 high expression group were PCLO, CSMD3 and XIRP2, with mutation frequencies of 18.3%, 13.6% and 13.1%, respectively. The mutated genes in the low PFKFB4 expression group were FGA, CFAP46, and NOS1, with mutation frequencies of both 11.2% respectively.

We analyzed DNA methylation sites of PFKFB4 in LIHC using the MEXPRESS database, including CpG dinucleotide and CpG island (Figure S2A). The analysis found that PFKFB4 was positively correlated with six methylation sites, including cg10107836, cg14592491, cg16986746, cg16902559, cg26029248, cg19348001 (P < 0.05). PFKFB4 was also negatively correlated with 13 methylation sites, including cg14564247, cg01022780, cg21141812, cg10319474, cg17226356, cg22065514, cg25845890, cg10179004, cg14699903, cg05125838, cg12259140, cg20732160, cg10135708 (P < 0.05) (Figure S2B).

Comprehensive Single-cell landscape of liver cancer

We analyzed a single-cell RNA sequencing dataset of liver cancer, comprising four normal tissue samples and seven liver cancer tissue samples, to construct a comprehensive cellular atlas (Fig. 2A). Following rigorous quality control and data filtration, a total of 73,193 high-quality single cells were retained for downstream analysis. To minimize batch effects, the Harmony integration algorithm was applied, followed by graph-based clustering and marker-based annotation to classify distinct cell populations. Clustering analysis identified several major cell types, including endothelial cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), natural killer (NK) cells, and T cells (Fig. 2B). Epithelial cells were defined by the expression of Epithelial Cell Adhesion Molecule (EPCAM), Aldehyde Dehydrogenase 1 Family Member A1 (ALDH1A1), and Albumin (ALB), while fibroblasts were identified based on Actin Alpha 2 (ACTA2) and Collagen Type I Alpha 2 Chain (COL1A2) expression. Myeloid-Derived Suppressor Cells (MDSCs) were defined by the expression of Integrin Subunit Alpha M (ITGAM) and CD33, NK cells by Fibroblast Growth Factor Binding Protein 2 (FGFBP2) and Fc Gamma Receptor IIIa (FCGR3A), B cells by Membrane Spanning 4-Domains A1 (MS4A1) and CD79A, T cells by CD3E and CD3D, and macrophages by CD68, CD14, and CD163. Endothelial cells were marked by Platelet and Endothelial Cell Adhesion Molecule 1 (PECAM1) and Von Willebrand Factor (VWF). The expression of these marker genes confirmed the accurate classification of each cell population (Fig. 2C). Next as a complement, we analyzed the immune data from several GEO datasets via websites and found that macrophages were highly expressed in them, especially in the GSE140228 dataset. Meanwhile, we adopted t-SNE technique to visualize the ten subclasses, and show their distribution and dissimilarity in LIHC. The following cell types were mentioned: B-cells, CD4 + Tconv cells, CD8 + Tex cells, DC cells, ICD cells, Mast cells, Mono/Macro cells, NK cells, Plasma cells and Tprolif cells (Figure S3A). Notably, PFKFB4 was expressed across all identified cell types, with the highest expression observed in macrophages (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, its expression was significantly upregulated in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues, suggesting a potential role for PFKFB4 in liver cancer progression (Fig. 2E).

Identification and characterization of macrophage subpopulations

To investigate the role of macrophages in the tumor microenvironment, we isolated macrophages from both tumor and normal tissue samples for further analysis (Fig. 3A). The UMAP visualization demonstrated the distribution of macrophages from tumor and normal samples, revealing distinct clustering patterns (Fig. 3B). Meanwhile, studies have also shown that the polarization of macrophages from M1(anti-tumor macrophages) to M2 (pro-tumor macrophages) contribute to immune escape [27, 28]. Macrophages in malignant tumors, mainly the M2 subtype, promote tumor progression [29]. Indeed, we found that markers CD163, IL10, VEGFA, VSIG4 and MS4A4A of M2 were all positively correlated with PFKFB4 expression (Figure S3B) [30, 31].

To facilitate the classification of macrophage subpopulations, we examined the expression of key marker genes associated with M1 and M2 macrophages, allowing for the initial identification of these subsets (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, differentiation analysis using Cytotrace was performed to determine the differentiation trajectory of macrophages. The results indicated a clear differentiation hierarchy, with the least differentiated population identified as M0 macrophages (Fig. 3D). By integrating marker-based identification with differentiation trajectory analysis, we classified macrophages into three subpopulations: M0, M1, and M2 (Fig. 3E). To further explore the differentiation dynamics, we conducted pseudotime trajectory analysis, which revealed the progressive differentiation of M0 macrophages into M1 and M2 subsets (Fig. 3F). Notably, during the transition from M0 to M2 macrophages, the expression of PFKFB4 progressively increased, highlighting its crucial role in M2 macrophage polarization (Fig. 3G). The above findings illustrated that PFKFB4 may be involved in the regulation of macrophage M2 polarization.

Immune infiltration analysis

Immune cells play pivotal roles in regulating tumor development [32]. To investigate the involvement of PFKFB4 in the tumor immune microenvironment across various cancer types particularly hepatocellular carcinoma—we conducted a detailed analysis of its immunoregulatory potential. Specifically, we assessed the correlations between PFKFB4 expression and the infiltration levels of six immune cell types: CD4⁺ T cells, CD8⁺ T cells, B cells, dendritic cells, macrophages, and neutrophils. PFKFB4 expression was found to be positively correlated with immune cell infiltration in multiple cancers, with notably strong associations observed in liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), and thyroid carcinoma (THCA) (r > 0, P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A). Notably, using an alternative algorithm within the TIMER platform, we further confirmed that PFKFB4 expression in liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) was positively correlated with the infiltration levels of all six immune cell types (r > 0, P < 0.05) (Fig. 4B). The positive correlation between LIHC and PFKRB4 immune infiltration is consistent across two algorithms.

Analysis of PFKFB4 expression and clinical relevance

Moreover, various biological processes such as proliferation and metastasis are critically involved in cancer initiation and progression. We specifically investigated the potential association between PFKFB4 and key epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers, including CDH2 (N-cadherin), FN1 (fibronectin 1), TWIST1 (Twist family BHLH transcription factor 1), and VIM (vimentin). The results demonstrated that PFKFB4 expression was positively correlated with the expression of these EMT markers in LIHC, THCA, LUAD, and KIRP (r > 0, P < 0.05) (Fig. 5A). In addition, we examined the relationship between PFKFB4 expression and classical proliferation markers, including marker of proliferation Ki-67 (MKI67) and proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA). Our analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between PFKFB4 and these proliferation markers across multiple cancer types, including LIHC, UCEC, THCA, STAD, SKCM, SARC, PRAD, PCPG, OV, MESO, LUSC, LUAD, LGG, KIRP, KICH, ESCA, CHOL, BRCA, BLCA, and ACC (r > 0, P < 0.05) (Fig. 5B). These findings suggest that PFKFB4 may play a critical role in promoting proliferation and metastasis across multiple cancer types, particularly in liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC). Collectively, these results reinforce the potential clinical relevance of PFKFB4 as a biomarker and therapeutic target.

To assess the clinical diagnostic performance of PFKFB4, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis for LIHC was conducted using expression data from the TCGA dataset (Figure S5A). AUC values between 0.8 and 0.9 are generally considered indicative of high diagnostic performance, while values between 0.7 and 0.8 reflect fair performance. In our analysis, the area under the curve (AUC) for PFKFB4 in diagnosing LIHC was 0.875, suggesting strong discriminatory ability. However, the AUC values declined over time, indicating a reduction in predictive accuracy for long-term survival. One-, three-, and five-year survival probabilities were further evaluated as indicators of clinical response to medical intervention. Additionally, a nomogram model was constructed using the R package to predict individual patient survival outcomes (Figure S5B). To enhance clarity, we generated a table summarizing the correlations between PFKFB4 expression and various clinicopathological characteristics using data from the TCGA database. High PFKFB4 expression was found to be significantly associated with tumor stage, histological grade, patient weight, and overall clinical stage (Supplementary Table).

Enrichment analysis of PFKFB4

To further explore the biological significance of PFKFB4 in LIHC, the co-expression profiles of PFKFB4 in the LIHC were analyzed by LinkedOmics database. As shown in Fig. 6A, a total of 7,458 genes were significantly negatively correlated with PFKFB4, while 12,465 genes exhibited significant positive correlations (FDR < 0.05). Heatmaps were generated to display the top 50 genes most strongly associated with PFKFB4 expression in both the positive and negative directions (Fig. 6A). We further identified 50 proteins predicted to interact with PFKFB4 using the STRING database and constructed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network using Cytoscape software (Fig. 6B). The visualized network revealed strong associations between PFKFB4 and several key metabolic enzymes, including HKDC1 (hexokinase domain-containing protein 1), HK1 (hexokinase-1), HK2 (hexokinase-2), HK3 (hexokinase-3), and H6PD (hexose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase). To further explore the potential biological functions of PFKFB4, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were conducted based on the top 200 PFKFB4-correlated genes (Fig. 6C). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that PFKFB4 was most significantly associated with the cell cycle, HIF-1 signaling pathway, and human T-cell leukemia virus 1 infection. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was categorized into three domains: cellular component (CC), biological process (BP), and molecular function (MF). A visual representation of the most enriched GO terms was generated. BP analysis highlighted enrichment in processes such as mitotic nuclear division, organelle fission, and nuclear division. MF terms were predominantly associated with microtubule binding, tubulin binding, and microtubule motor activity. CC enrichment included the spindle, condensed chromosome, centromeric region, and chromosome centromeric region. These findings suggest potential molecular mechanisms through which PFKFB4 may contribute to cancer pathogenesis.

Cellular functional assays following PFKFB4 knockdown and overexpression

Initially, the expression level of PFKFB4 was assessed in human hepatocellular carcinoma tissues and adjacent non-tumorous tissues by immunohistochemical staining (Fig. 7A). Subsequently, to investigate the cellular function of PFKFB4 in LIHC, we examined the mRNA expression levels of PFKFB4 in normal liver cell THLE2 and LIHC cell lines Huh-4 and Huh-7 by qRT–PCR. We found that PFKFB4 was significantly upregulated in the LIHC cell lines (Fig. 7B). Two siRNAs targeting PFKFB4 were used for the knockdown, and the most efficient siRNA (PFKFB4 -3#) was selected for the next experiments (Fig. 7C). We subsequently investigated the effect of PFKFB4 knockdown on cell proliferation by CCK-8, EdU and colony formation assays. The results show that the downregulation of PFKFB4 significantly reduced the proliferation viability of Huh-4 and Huh-7 cells and the number of colonies formed by the cells (Fig. 7D-F). Furthermore, the transwell assay was also performed, and the result showed that the migration of Huh-4 and Huh-7 cells were significantly suppressed by PFKFB4 silencing (Fig. 7G). Wound healing was also remarkedly suppressed by PFKFB4 silencing in LIHC cell lines (Fig. 7H). Flow cytometry analysis also revealed that silencing of PFKFB4 expression promoted cell apoptosis (Fig. 7I). Meanwhile, we also investigated the effects of PFKFB4 overexpression on cellular functions by plasmid-based transfection. First, we validated the expression level of PFKFB4 following transfection with the PFKFB4 overexpression plasmid. (Fig. 8A). Secondly, cell proliferation was assessed using CCK-8, EdU incorporation, and colony formation assays, which revealed that cells in the PFKFB4-overexpression group exhibited enhanced proliferative capacity compared to the empty vector control group (Fig. 8B-D). Transwell and wound healing assays were performed to evaluate cell migration, while flow cytometry analysis indicated that PFKFB4 overexpression suppressed cell apoptosis (Fig. 8E-G). In the animal experiments, control and PFKFB4-knockdown Hepa1-6 murine hepatocellular carcinoma cells were subcutaneously implanted into mice. The results demonstrated that PFKFB4 knockdown significantly inhibited the growth of subcutaneous tumors (Fig. 9A-C). These findings suggest that PFKFB4 functions as an oncogene and promotes the progression of hepatocellular cancer cells.

PFKFB4 modulates hepatocellular carcinoma progression via the MEK/ERK pathway

Mechanistically, enrichment analysis revealed that the genes associated with PFKFB4 expression were significantly involved in multiple oncogenic pathways, including cell cycle regulation, glycolysis and various classic cancer-related pathways. These processes are known to be closely associated with the MEK/ERK signaling cascade [33–36], a key regulator of cellular proliferation, metabolism, and survival. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that PFKFB4 may modulate HCC development through the ERK/ERK pathway. To investigate the downstream targets of PFKFB4, we performed Western blot analysis. Upon PFKFB4 knockdown in Huh-4 and Huh-7 cells, the total protein levels of MEK and ERK remained unchanged compared with the control group. However, the levels of phosphorylated MEK and ERK were markedly reduced in the si-PFKFB4 group, indicating that silencing PFKFB4 suppresses the activation of the MEK/ERK signaling pathway (Fig. 9D). Taken together, PFKFB4 downregulation may inhibits the activation of the MEK/ERK signaling pathway, thereby suppressing the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma.

PFKFB4 regulates M2 polarization of macrophages in hepatocellular carcinoma

Based on pathway enrichment analysis, several biological processes associated with PFKFB4 expression were found to be potentially linked to M2 polarization of macrophages, such as the HIF-1 signaling pathway, p53 signaling pathway and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis [37–41]. In human hepatocellular carcinoma tissues and adjacent non-tumor tissues, multiplex immunohistochemistry was performed using antibodies against CD206 and PFKFB4 to identify M2 macrophages, with CD206-positive cells serving as a marker of M2 polarization. The results showed that tissues with high PFKFB4 expression exhibited a greater abundance of M2-polarized macrophages, suggesting a positive association between PFKFB4 expression and M2 macrophage infiltration (Fig. 10A). To further investigate the role of PFKFB4 in modulating macrophage polarization, we co-cultured PFKFB4-knockdown and control Hepa1-6 hepatocellular carcinoma cells with mouse bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that knockdown of PFKFB4 in cancer cells significantly reduced the proportion of M2-polarized macrophages in the co-culture system (Fig. 10B), qPCR analysis revealed that BMDMs co-cultured with gene-knockdown tumor cells exhibited significantly reduced expression of the M2 markers CD206, IL-10 and Arg1 compared with the control group (Fig. 10C, Figure S6). Suggesting that PFKFB4 in tumor cells may promotes M2 macrophage polarization through tumor–macrophage crosstalk. To determine whether macrophage polarization is directly regulated by PFKFB4 in a cell-intrinsic manner, we knockdown PFKFB4 in primary BMDMs and assessed CD206 expression by flow cytometry. The absence of a measurable difference between PFKFB4-knockdown group and NC group BMDMs suggests that PFKFB4 is not a dominant intrinsic regulator of M2 polarization under same experimental conditions (Figure S7). To explore the metabolic basis underlying PFKFB4-mediated tumor–macrophage crosstalk, we next quantified extracellular lactate levels in Huh-4 cells following PFKFB4 knockdown. As expected, silencing PFKFB4 significantly reduced lactate production in the culture medium compared with controls (Fig. 10D). These findings suggest that PFKFB4 may facilitate M2 macrophage polarization, at least in part, by enhancing lactate secretion from tumor cells.

Discussion

Liver hepatocellular carcinoma is one of the deadliest malignancies in the world, with a poor prognosis [42, 43]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore potential biomarkers for early diagnosis and treatment of LIHC. Glycolysis is a fundamental metabolic process in human cells, and numerous studies have demonstrated that glycolysis-related genes play critical roles in the regulation of malignant tumor progression [44–46]. In this study, we elucidated the previously unclear role of PFKFB4 in hepatocellular carcinoma progression and explored its association with immune regulation, with a particular focus on macrophage polarization. (Fig. 11). Through integrated single-cell transcriptomics, bioinformatics analyses, and extensive functional assays, our results clearly demonstrated that PFKFB4 expression is significantly associated with HCC malignancy and the polarization of macrophages toward an M2 phenotype.

Schematic model illustrating the role of PFKFB4 in tumor progression

We found in this study that PFKFB4 is an effective biomarker for predicting proliferation, migration and prognosis in HCC patients. Accumulating evidences suggest that PFKFB4 expressed in many kinds of cancer high and promotes cancer progression that affect prognosis, such as breast cancer, bladder cancer and colon adenocarcinoma [47–49].

Notably, our single-cell sequencing analysis revealed a distinct enrichment of PFKFB4 expression in macrophages, especially in those undergoing M2 polarization. This finding is consistent with recent studies highlighting the importance of metabolic enzymes in shaping the tumor immune landscape [50, 51], further reinforcing the concept that metabolic reprogramming in TAMs contributes significantly to immunosuppression and tumor progression. Previous studies have also found that the expression of PFKFB4 was associated with immune cell infiltration of macrophages and other immune cells in COAD patients [47]. Multiple studies have reported that the MEK/ERK signaling pathway is also involved in the regulation of M2 macrophage polarization [52, 53]. Many observations have also highlighted the role of immune checkpoints in cancer immunotherapy, which has revolutionized the paradigm of cancer treatment [54]. Compared to previous studies mainly focused on tumor cell-intrinsic roles of PFKFB4, our research expanded the understanding of its extrinsic functions within the tumor microenvironment, thus providing a more comprehensive view of its role in cancer biology.

We also summarized multiple interacting proteins through a PPI network analysis. Furthermore, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses predicted the potential involvement of PFKFB4 in various important biological processes. These results suggested the possible molecular mechanism of PFKFB4 in cancer pathogenesis.

Accumulating evidence indicates that multiple oncogenic regulators contribute to shaping macrophage polarization in hepatocellular carcinoma. Among these regulators, activation of Yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1) has been shown to promote M2-like macrophage polarization. Conversely, inhibition of YAP1 may suppress hepatocellular carcinoma progression by reprogramming macrophages toward an M1-like, pro-inflammatory phenotype [55]. To interrogate the mechanism by which tumor cell PFKFB4 influences macrophage polarization, we employed a transwell co-culture system. Knockdown of PFKFB4 in HCC cells led to a marked decrease of M2-associated genes (CD206, Arg1 and IL-10) in co-cultured BMDMs. In conjunction with the observation that macrophage-intrinsic PFKFB4 perturbation did not significantly alter CD206 expression, our data suggest that PFKFB4 functions primarily within tumor cells to remodel the microenvironmental cues that instruct macrophage polarization, providing a mechanistic basis for the observed tumor–immune interaction. In the present study, we observed that knockdown of PFKFB4 significantly reduced lactate levels in the culture supernatant. As lactate accumulation has been implicated in shaping an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, the decrease in lactate production following PFKFB4 suppression may contribute to alleviating macrophage M2-like polarization and restoring antitumor immune responses.

However, even though our research is based on several databases, there were still some inevitable limitations. Although we provided substantial evidence linking PFKFB4 to M2 polarization and MEK/ERK pathway, direct mechanistic confirmation remains incomplete. Additionally, the complexity of the immune microenvironment in vivo cannot be completely replicated in vitro, potentially limiting the generalizability of our co-culture observations. Secondly, our clinical data might be affected by sampling errors due to the limited sample size. To overcome these limitations, future studies should incorporate conditional knockout mouse models specifically targeting PFKFB4 in macrophages or tumor cells separately. Moreover, due to the different specific role of PFKFB4 in various cancers, it needs to be confirmed in more extensive clinical cases. Admittedly, this is the deficiency of our study, and we will further improve it in the follow-up research plan.

This study provides valuable insights into the intersection of metabolism, signaling pathways, and immune regulation in cancer, suggesting that metabolic enzymes like PFKFB4 might be potential therapeutic targets.

Conclusion

Collectively, our findings indicate that PFKFB4 drives hepatocellular carcinoma progression through activation of the MEK/ERK signaling pathway and, in parallel, influences tumor progression via tumor cell–mediated modulation of M2 macrophage polarization. PFKFB4 could function as an oncogene and would serve as a biomarker for hepatocellular carcinoma, sharing new insights into precision medicine.
