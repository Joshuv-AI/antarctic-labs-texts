TRIM16 mediates YAP1 K63-linked ubiquitination to alleviate sepsis-induced acute liver injury through YAP/Nrf2 axis in mice
Authors: Kaina Zhang, Mengqing Liu, Yeqing Zheng, Zuozhong Gao, Ziwei Dong, Mengyao You, Yang Guo, Fanfan Liang, Zhenghang Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sepsis-induced liver injury is associated with high morbidity and mortality, yet its underlying mechanisms remain elucidated. In this study, we observed a significant reduction in the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM16 in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-injured hepatocytes; however, its functional role and regulatory mechanisms were unclear. Using in vitro LPS-induced hepatocyte injury and in vivo cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced septic liver injury models, we investigated TRIM16’s effects via small interfering RNA and AAV9-overexpression plasmids in hepatocytes. Results demonstrated that TRIM16 overexpression rescued LPS-induced suppression of hepatocyte viability, reduced cellular dysfunction (ALT, AST), and significantly suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokine expression (TNF-α, IL-6) and oxidative stress (SOD, MDA). Conversely, TRIM16 silencing exacerbated hepatocyte damage and dysfunction. In septic mice, TRIM16 overexpression alleviated liver injury and fibrosis by inhibiting oxidative stress and inflammatory responses. Mechanistically, TRIM16 directly bound to and stabilized YAP1 through K63-linked ubiquitination, facilitating its nuclear translocation. This process enhanced Nrf2 activation and subsequent expression of antioxidant genes. Collectively, our findings reveal that TRIM16 protects against septic liver injury by mitigating oxidative stress and inflammation via the YAP1/Nrf2 pathway, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for sepsis-induced acute liver injury.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-025-01517-4.

Introduction

Sepsis, a life-threatening systemic inflammatory response to infection, frequently leads to multi-organ dysfunction, with acute liver injury (ALI) significantly worsening clinical outcomes [1, 2]. The liver, central to metabolic and immune homeostasis, is highly susceptible to septic insults due to exposure to gut-derived toxins and inflammatory mediators. Despite advances in understanding sepsis pathophysiology, the molecular mechanisms driving hepatic dysfunction during sepsis remain incompletely elucidated. Current evidence implicates excessive oxidative stress and dysregulated inflammatory response as pivotal contributors to septic liver damage [3]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) overproduction overwhelms antioxidant defenses, damaging hepatocytes and promoting mitochondrial dysfunction, while dysregulated cytokine release (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) perpetuates tissue injury and fibrosis [4, 5]. Thus, identifying pathways that simultaneously mitigate oxidative stress and inflammation could offer novel therapeutic strategies for sepsis-induced liver injury [6, 7].

E3 ubiquitin ligases, critical regulators of protein stability and function through post-translational modifications, have emerged as key players in cellular stress responses [8]. Tripartite motif-containing protein 16 (TRIM16), a member of the TRIM family, has recently gained attention for its roles in autophagy, antioxidant defense, and immune response. For instance, TRIM16 is upregulated in nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, where its overexpression plays protective effects by promoting the degradation of phospho-TAK1 [9]. Similarly, our recent work demonstrated that doxorubicin-induced TRIM16 upregulation exerts an intrinsic protection against cardiotoxicity by suppressing oxidative stress and inflammation [10]. However, the role of TRIM16 in sepsis-induced acute liver injury remains unexplored. Intriguingly, our preliminary data revealed a marked reduction in TRIM16 expression in hepatocytes exposed to lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a hallmark pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP) in sepsis. This observation prompted us to hypothesize that TRIM16 depletion during sepsis may exacerbate liver injury by impairing cellular defenses against oxidative and inflammatory insults.

The Hippo signaling effector Yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1) has been implicated in tissue repair and regeneration, with recent studies highlighting its role in modulating oxidative stress and inflammation. YAP1 activation promotes nuclear translocation, where it interacts with transcription factors, such as TEAD1 to drive pro-survival gene expression. However, YAP1 is highly unstable under stress conditions, and its regulatory mechanisms in sepsis-induced liver injury are poorly defined. In LPS-treated hepatocytes, we found that TRIM16 overexpression dramatically elevated YAP1 protein levels, while silencing TRIM16 reduced them. As an E3 ligase, TRIM16 catalyzes both K63-linked ubiquitination to regulate protein stabilization and signaling, and K48-linked ubiquitination to direct substrates for proteasomal degradation. Notably, novel evidence has demonstrated that the E3 ligase TRAF6 stabilizes YAP1 by K63 polyubiquitination, promoting YAP1/TFCP2 signaling in melanoma [11]. However, whether TRIM16 interacts with YAP1 to regulate its stability and activity during sepsis, thereby influencing Nrf2-mediated antioxidant responses, remains unknown.

In this study, we investigate the role of TRIM16 in sepsis-induced liver injury and elucidate its molecular mechanisms. We demonstrate that TRIM16 binds to and stabilizes YAP1 through K63-linked ubiquitination, facilitating its nuclear translocation and subsequent activation of Nrf2-driven antioxidant defenses. Our findings uncover TRIM16 as a critical regulator of oxidative stress and inflammation in sepsis-induced liver injury, positioning it as a promising therapeutic target for this life-threatening condition.

Materials and methods

Animal experiment

Six-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were acquired from the Laboratorial Animal Center of Xi’an Jiaotong University and routinely fed and housed as previously described [11].

Firstly, AAV9-TRIM16 (1.7 × 1012 vg/ml, 200 µL/mouse) and negative control supplied by Hanheng Biotechnology (Hanheng Biotech, Shanghai, China) were injected to mice via tail vein in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, the mice were routinely housed for 4 weeks, and then subjected to cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) as described in previous studies [12]. Mice were briefly anaesthetized using 1.5–2% isoflurane via inhalation. The cecum was ligated at a point midway between the ileocecal valve and the bottom of cecum, and punctured three times with a 27G needle before being repositioned within the abdominal cavity. Sham-operated mice underwent the same surgical procedure, with the exception of cecal ligation and puncture. Following the surgery, 1 ml of 0.9% saline was administered to compensate for fluid loss. Mice were randomly assigned to the following groups for administration via tail vein injection: (a) the negative control adeno-associated virus 9 (NC) group was injected with negative AAV9, (b) the CLP group received negative AAV9, (c) the AAV9-TRIM16 group received AAV9-TRIM16, (d) the AAV9-TRIM16 combined with CLP group was injected with AAV9-TRIM16. After 48 h of modeling, the mice were anesthetized and their livers were subsequently excised for histomorphology and assessment of liver function. The blood samples and liver tissues were meticulously collected for western blotting analysis of the associated proteins.

Cell culture and treatment

The human normal liver cells (WRL68, CL-48; L02, HL-7702) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), and the rat liver cell line BRL3A, obtained from Cell Bank of Shanghai Institute of Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, were maintained in high-glucose DMEM or MEM medium (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with FBS (Gibco, Grand Island, NY), as well as penicillin and streptomycin (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) at 37 °C in a humidified chamber with 5% CO2 and 95% air. The cells were starved in serum-free medium for 12 h, and then were treated with different interventions, including the following groups: (i) CON, the cells were maintained in serum-free medium with the vehicle; (ii) LPS, the cells were treated with 0.1, 1, 5, 10, 20 µg/mL LPS (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 24 h and 1 µg/mL LPS for 3, 6, 12, 24, 48 h; (iii) siTRIM16, The cells with TRIM16 knockdown were transfected with siRNA-TRIM16 (GenePharma, Shanghai, China) (80 pmol/mL) and TransIT-X2 (Mirus, Madison, USA) for 24 h, followed by culturing in fresh DMEM medium; (iv) siTRIM16 + LPS, the cells were treated with siRNA-TRIM16 for 24 h followed by treatment with 1 µg/mL LPS for 24 h; (v) OE-TRIM16, the cells were exposed with the overexpressing TRIM16 cDNA plasmids (GenePharma, Shanghai, China) (1.2 µg/mL) for 48 h and then the medium was changed; (vi) OE-TRIM16 + LPS, the cells pretreated with TRIM16 cDNA plasmids for 48 h followed by treatment with 1 µg/mL LPS for 24 h; (vii) siTRIM16 + LPS + Verteporfin, Following 48-hour transfection with TRIM16-targeting siRNA, cells were pretreated with verteporfin (VP, 1 µM; MCE, USA), a YAP inhibitor, for 1 h prior to LPS stimulation; (viii) OE-TRIM16 + LPS + VP, when transfected with TRIM16 cDNA plasmids for 24 h, the cells were exposed to 1 µM VP for 1 h prior to treatment with LPS; viv) siYAP1 + OE-TRIM16, Following 24-hour transfection with YAP1-targeting siRNA, cells pretreated with TRIM16 cDNA plasmids for 48 h; vv) siYAP1 + OE-TRIM16 + LPS, Following 24-hour transfection with YAP1-targeting siRNA, cells pretreated with TRIM16 cDNA plasmids for 48 h followed by treatment with 1 µg/mL LPS for 24 h.

Primary hepatocytes were isolated from the livers of the WT mice at age of 8-weeks as previously described [12]. Isolated hepatocytes were cultured in high-glucose DMEM medium and allowed to grow for 12 h, then starved in serum-free medium for 12 h, following by treatment with or without LPS (1 µg/mL) for 24 h.

Histomorphology and masson’s trichrome staining

Individual liver lobes were washed with physiological saline followed by fixing with 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in paraffin and sliced into sections. Hematoxylin-Eosin and Masson’s trichrome staining were performed on the sections as previously described [13]. Then, we observed the hepatic sections under a digital microscope (IX53; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and analyzed these using ImageJ 1.44p software.

Immunofluorescence staining

The liver cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min following the multiple interventions, and then permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 5 min. After washed 3 times with PBS, the cells were blocked in 5% BSA for 30 min and subsequently incubated with TRIM16 antibody (1:100, #54441, Signalway antibody), Nrf2 antibody (1:50, YM8624, Immunoway) and/or YAP1 antibody (1:50, 13584-1-AP, 66900-1-Ig, Proteintech) overnight at 4 °C. Secondary antibodies conjugated with Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Fluor 594 (1:50, S0006, Affinity) and/or Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Fluor 488 (1:50, S0018, Affinity) were incubated at room temperature for 1.5 h. Afterwards the nuclei were stained with DAPI staining solution for 10 min in the dark. Images were acquired with a fluorescence microscope.

Cell proliferation assay

After several treatments above, we used the CCK-8 assay kit (C0041, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) to measure the cell viability following the manufacturer’s instructions. For each measurement, 10 µL of CCK-8 detection solution was added, and then the cells were incubated at 37℃ for 2 h. Finally, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader to calculate cell viability.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Cell culture supernatant of the liver cells and serum of mice collected from blood samples were centrifugated at 4 °C, 4000 rpm for 20 min and the supernatant was acquired. The enzymatic content of ALT and AST were quantitatively assessed using commercial enzymatic assay kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The commercially available kits (Ruixin Biotech, Quanzhou, China) were utilized.

Oxidative stress detection

The liver tissue was sectioned, homogenized, and centrifuged at 16,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain the supernatant. The cultured cells were collected, sonicated at 4 °C, centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was obtained. Then we detected the activities of CAT (catalase), SOD (superoxide dismutase), MDA (malondialdehyde) and GSH (glutathione) according to the manufacturer’s instructions using commercial enzymatic assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). Specifically, the levels of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) released from liver cells were measured using the cell culture supernatant.

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from the liver cells or liver tissues using Trizol reagent (Genstar, China). cDNAs were then synthesized using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Genstar, China). The primers utilized in this study are listed in Table 1. PCR amplifications were performed using the 2 × RealStar Green Fast Mixture (Genstar, China) on the CFX Connect™ Real-Time System (Bio-RAD). The relative expression levels of mRNA were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blotting analysis

Protein extraction and western blotting analysis were performed as previously described [14]. The proteins of hepatic tissues and liver cells were extracted using cell lysis buffer for Western and IP (P0013, Beyotime, China), supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Germany). The protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay kit (Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein (20–40 µg) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, MA, USA). The primary antibodies, including anti-TRIM16 (1:1000, #54441, Signalway antibody), anti-YAP1 (1:1000, 13584-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-phospho-YAP1(Ser127) (1:1000, AF3328, Affinity), anti-Nrf2 (1:1000, 16396-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-ub (1:750, 10201-2-AP, Proteintech), anti-k48 ub (1:1000, A3606, ABclonal), anti-k63 ub (1:1000, CY6579, Abways), and anti-Beta actin (1:5000, 66009-1-Ig, Proteintech) were incubated overnight at 4 °C. Secondary antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase, including Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (1:5000, AB0102, Abways), Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (1:5000, AB0101, Abways) or IPKine™ HRP, Mouse Anti-Rabbit IgG LCS (1:2000, A25022, Proteintech) was incubated for 1.5 h. Beta actin was used as a protein loading control. The blots were developed using ECL reagent SuperKine™ West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Abbkin, China) and visualized by gel imaging system (SYNGENE, G: BOX). Protein expression levels were quantified by using Image J.

Immunoprecipitation (IP)

The cells or hepatic tissues were lysed as described above. One mg of protein sample was incubated with YAP1 Rabbit antibody (2 µg, 13584-1-AP, Proteintech) at 4 °C overnight. Afterwards, the mixture was incubated with Protein A Agarose (Fast Flow for IP, P2051, Beyotime) for 2 h at 4 °C. After pelleting the agarose beads and resuspending them in sample buffer, the mixture was subjected to boiling before being loaded onto a 10% SDS-PAGE gel. The associated protein contents were subsequently analyzed using western blotting.

TUNEL staining

TUNEL staining was performed to assess cell death in liver tissues using the Elabscience® One-step TUNEL In Situ Apoptosis Kit (E-CK-A320), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Paraffin-embedded liver sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series to distilled water. The sections were then treated with proteinase K for 15 min at 37 °C and washed with PBS. After incubation with the TUNEL reaction mixture at 37 °C for 1 h, cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. TUNEL-positive cells were visualized and imaged using a Nikon A1 confocal microscope (Nikon, New York, NY, USA) equipped with a ×63 oil immersion objective.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (San Diego, CA, USA). The significance of differences was assessed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test and unpaired Student’s t-test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The protein expression of TRIM16 was downregulated in LPS-injured hepatocytes

Initially, cultured liver cells were exposed to a range of LPS concentrations, from 0.1 µg/mL to 20 µg/mL, or treated with 1 µg/mL LPS for 3 to 48 h. The results demonstrated that cell viability decreased markedly in a concentration- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 1A-a), while the protein expression of TRIM16 was significantly reduced in a similar pattern (Fig. 1B-a). Similar alterations in TRIM16 expression were identified in the L02, BRL-3 A, and mouse primary hepatocytes (Fig. 1A, B). Furthermore, the immunostaining images showed that TRIM16 proteins are distributed in both cytoplasm and nucleus of hepatocytes and were remarkably reduced upon exposure to 1 µg/mL LPS for 24 h (Fig. 2C).

TRIM16 upregulation inhibited LPS-induced hepatocyte oxidative stress and inflammatory response

Oxidative stress and inflammatory response play a critical role in septic live injury; we next determined the function of TRIM16 in LPS-induced hepatocellular injury. In hepatocytes, immunostaining analysis demonstrated that TRIM16 protein levels were markedly increased following transfection with OE-TRIM16 plasmids, which also reversed the LPS-induced reduction (Fig. 2A). TRIM16 overexpression effectively suppressed LPS-triggered cell death and attenuated the elevated ALT and AST levels released from hepatocytes (Fig. 2C), indicating that TRIM16 plays a protective role against LPS-induced hepatocytes injury. Furthermore, our findings revealed that OE-TRIM16 remarkably enhanced antioxidant defense and attenuated inflammatory responses in LPS-treated hepatocytes. This was supported by increased SOD activity, decreased MDA contents and downregulated mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6 in LPS-treated hepatocytes (Fig. 2D). Conversely, silencing TRIM16 exacerbated LPS-induced cytotoxicity, as evidenced by reduced hepatocyte viability and antioxidant activity as well as elevated cytokine levels (Fig. 2B–D). Collectively, these results suggest that the upregulation of TRIM16 confers a protection against LPS-induced oxidative stress and inflammatory damage, thereby mitigating LPS-mediated hepatocellular injury.

YAP1/Nrf2 protein expression was downregulated in LPS-induced hepatocyte injury

It has been demonstrated that YAP1 nuclear translocation promotes Nrf2-mediated antioxidant defenses and suppresses inflammatory cascades [15], we hypothesized that LPS-induced hepatocyte injury may involve dysregulation of the YAP1/Nrf2 axis. To evaluate this mechanism, we performed comprehensive immunoblotting analyses in three distinct hepatocyte models exposed to LPS challenge. Our data revealed that LPS treatment induced significant concentration-dependent reductions in the protein expression of YAP1 and Nrf2 (Fig. 3A–C). Conversely, the protein expression of p-YAP1(Ser127), an inactivated form predominantly localized in the cytoplasm, was markedly elevated in LPS-injured hepatocytes (Fig. 3A–C). Furthermore, immunofluorescence analysis corroborated these findings by revealing LPS-induced nuclear depletion of YAP1(Fig. 3D).

TRIM16 mitigated LPS-induced hepatocyte injury by YAP1/Nrf2 pathways

Given our previous finding that TRIM16 activated YAP1/Nrf2 signaling axis in cardiomyocytes [10], we investigated its regulatory effects on this pathway in LPS-challenged hepatocytes. Immunoblot assay revealed that TRIM16 overexpression rescued LPS-induced reduction of YAP1, TEAD1, and Nrf2 protein expression, while concurrently decreasing p-YAP1(Ser127) in hepatocytes (Fig. 4A, B). Conversely, TRIM16 knockdown potentiated the LPS-induced alterations in these proteins. Consistent with these observations, immunofluorescence assay revealed that TRIM16 silencing caused more pronounced nuclear YAP1 depletion than LPS treatment alone, whereas TRIM16 overexpression restored nuclear YAP1 localization (Fig. 4C, D), suggesting TRIM16 facilitates YAP1 nuclear retention under LPS stress.

To delineate the functional role of the TRIM16/YAP1 axis in hepatocyte injury, we employed the YAP1 inhibitor verteporfin. CCK8 assay showed that verteporfin treatment significantly reduced cell viability in LPS-challenged hepatocytes (Fig. 5A). Western blots analysis confirmed verteporfin-mediated suppression of YAP1 and downstream Nrf2 expression in LPS-treated cells (Fig. 5B). Notably, verteporfin abrogated the elevated effect of OE-TRIM16-induced upregulation of YAP1 and Nrf2 proteins, while amplifying the inhibitory effects of TRIM16 knockdown on these proteins (Fig. 5C). Crucially, co-treatment with verteporfin completely reversed the protective effects of TRIM16 overexpression against LPS-induced cytotoxicity, as evidenced by restored LDH release and suppressed SOD and enhanced MDA activity (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, knockdown of YAP1 abrogated the TRIM16-mediated increase in Nrf2 mRNA (Fig. 5E) and protein expression. Immunofluorescence (Fig. 5F) and western blot analyses (Fig. 5G) confirmed that TRIM16 overexpression failed to reverse the LPS-induced decrease in Nrf2 levels when YAP1 was depleted. Collectively, these findings establish TRIM16 as a critical regulator of LPS-induced hepatocyte injury through YAP1/Nrf2-dependent mechanisms.

TRIM16 stimulates K63-linked ubiquitination of YAP1 and stabilizes its function

Capitalizing on TRIM16’s established role as an E3 ubiquitin ligase mediating K63-linked ubiquitination for substrate stabilization [16], we hypothesized that whether TRIM16 stimulate K63-linked ubiquitination of YAP1 and stabilizes its function. To investigate this mechanism, we first analyzed the mRNA expression of YAP1 and TRIM16 in hepatocytes subjected to different interventions and found that YAP1 mRNA level was not significantly changed in LPS-treated hepatocytes especially both in overexpression and knockdown of TRIM16, as shown in Fig. 6A, suggesting a posttranscriptional regulation of YAP1 processing. Next, immunoprecipitation analysis showed that OE-TRIM16 partially restored the interaction between TRIM16 and YAP1 in LPS-stimulated hepatocytes. LPS reduced the K63-linked, but not K48-linked, ubiquitination of YAP1, and this reduction was moderately reversed upon TRIM16 overexpression (Fig. 6B). Additionally, confocal microscopy quantification showed TRIM16 overexpression increased nuclear YAP1 intensity and enhanced TRIM16-YAP1 co-localization in Fig. 6C. Overall, these findings suggest that TRIM16 may potentially enhance the nuclear localization of YAP1 in cardiomyocytes subjected to LPS insult by facilitating YAP1 stabilization.

TRIM16 overexpression arrested liver dysfunction and inflammation induced by CLP in mice

To further investigate its therapeutic potential in systemic sepsis, we systematically evaluated the protective role of TRIM16 overexpression in CLP-induced acute liver injury murine models. Initial validation confirmed successful establishment of sepsis-associated hepatic dysfunction, as evidenced by characteristic histopathological alterations in CLP-operated mice compared to sham controls. Quantitative histomorphometric analysis of HE and MASSON-stained sections revealed profound structural derangements including disrupted hepatic architecture, extensive inflammatory infiltration, and progressive fibrotic alterations (Fig. 7A, B). Notably, AAV9-mediated TRIM16 overexpression significantly ameliorated these pathological changes, restoring hepatocyte organization while reducing both inflammatory cell accumulation and collagen deposition. The functional relevance of these morphological improvements was corroborated by biochemical analyses. TRIM16 overexpression conferred substantial protection against CLP-induced elevations in serum transaminases, with ALT and AST levels reduced compared to wild-type CLP mice (Fig. 7C). Concomitant enhancement of antioxidant defenses was observed through significant upregulation of hepatic GSH, CAT activity, and SOD levels in AAV9-TRIM16 treated animals, indicating effective mitigation of sepsis-associated oxidative stress. Mechanistically, TRIM16 overexpression demonstrated potent anti-inflammatory properties in the septic hepatic microenvironment. RT-qPCR analysis revealed dramatic suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, with IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 mRNA levels reduced compared to CLP controls (Fig. 7D). Additionally, TUNEL staining revealed that the number of apoptotic cells in the liver tissue was markedly increased in the CLP group compared with the control group, whereas TRIM16 overexpression significantly reduced hepatocyte apoptosis (Fig. 7E). This coordinated downregulation of both oxidative stress markers and inflammatory mediators suggests that TRIM16 exerts its hepatoprotective effects through dual modulation of redox homeostasis and innate immune responses. Collectively, our findings establish TRIM16 as a critical regulator of hepatic pathophysiology in sepsis, with therapeutic overexpression effectively attenuating the triad of histopathological damage, oxidative injury, and cytokine storm characteristic of sepsis-induced liver dysfunction.

TRIM16 overexpression protected against CLP-induced liver injury through YAP/Nrf2 axis

To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying TRIM16-mediated hepatoprotection, we systematically investigated its regulatory effects on the YAP1/Nrf2 signaling axis in CLP-induced hepatic pathophysiology. Immunoblots and RT-qPCR analysis revealed dramatic overexpression of TRIM16 mRNA and proteins in liver tissues of AAV9-loaded mice (Fig. 8A). Quantitative immunoblotting revealed significant downregulation of both YAP1 and Nrf2 protein expression in CLP-challenged mice, which was reversed by AAV9-mediated TRIM16 overexpression (Fig. 8B). Immunoprecipitation revealed that YAP1-TRIM16 interaction was reduced in CLP-treated mice compared to the sham group. TRIM16 overexpression partially restored this interaction, indicating a compensatory role of TRIM16 in maintaining YAP1 stability under septic conditions (Fig. 8C). Ubiquitination profiling further characterized the post-translational regulatory mechanism. TRIM16 overexpression specifically enhanced K63-linked polyubiquitination of YAP1 without affecting K48-linked modifications, indicating a non-degradative ubiquitination pattern that potentially modulates YAP1 subcellular localization or transcriptional activity (Fig. 8C). This molecular stabilization was functionally associated with restored Nrf2-mediated antioxidant responses, as evidenced by coordinated upregulation of Nrf2 downstream targets in TRIM16-overexpressing livers. These findings establish a novel mechanistic paradigm wherein TRIM16 exerts dual regulatory functions: (1) As a scaffolding protein that physically bridges YAP1 to the ubiquitination machinery, and (2) As an E3 ubiquitin ligase that catalyzes K63-specific ubiquitination to stabilize YAP1-Nrf2 signaling axis. The resultant enhancement of Nrf2-mediated cytoprotective programs provides a molecular basis for the observed attenuation of oxidative stress and inflammatory damage. Conclusively, our data position TRIM16 as a master regulator of the YAP1/Nrf2 pathway in sepsis-associated liver injury, with therapeutic targeting of this axis representing a promising strategy for mitigating hepatic dysfunction in critical care settings.

Discussion

In this study, we provide evidence that TRIM16 alleviates sepsis-induced acute liver dysfunction by restoring oxidative stress and inflammatory responses via the YAP1/Nrf2 axis. We reveal a previously unrecognized mechanism whereby TRIM16 stabilizes YAP1 through K63-linked ubiquitination, facilitating its nuclear localization and subsequent upregulation of Nrf2 expression. Notably, LPS-challenged hepatocytes exhibited marked TRIM16 downregulation, creating a pathological deficit in YAP1/Nrf2 signaling. TRIM16 overexpression attenuated hepatic oxidative stress and inflammation in septic mice, while TRIM16 knockdown exacerbated LPS-induced hepatocyte injury by destabilizing the YAP1/Nrf2 pathway. These findings identify TRIM16 as a novel regulator of septic liver injury and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target for this condition.

Emerging evidence highlights the critical role of ubiquitination in modulating protein stability and activity during liver injury [17]. Sepsis-induced liver dysfunction involves multifaceted mechanisms, including oxidative stress, inflammation, and subsequent hepatocyte damage [18]. The ubiquitin-proteasomal system, which regulates diverse protein functions, has been implicated in septic liver injury, where aberrant proteasomal degradation contributes to pathological progression. For instance, hepatocyte-specific CD36 knockout alleviated LPS-induced liver injury and autophagosome-lysosome fusion defects in mice by modulating UBQLN1-mediated proteasomal degradation of autophagic SNARE proteins [19]. As an E3 ubiquitin ligase, TRIM16 regulates protein stabilization and function via its B box domain. TRIM family proteins are known regulators of cellular processes such as innate immunity, autophagy, and tumorigenesis [20, 21]; however, the functional significance of TRIM16 in sepsis-induced liver dysfunction remains poorly understood. These findings not only establish TRIM16 as a novel regulator of hepatic homeostasis during systemic inflammation but also suggest its potential as a therapeutic target for sepsis-induced liver injury.

Targeting oxidative stress and inflammatory cascades becomes a central focus in sepsis-associated acute liver injury research. Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), a master regulator of cellular antioxidant defense and inflammation modulation, represents a promising therapeutic hub [22, 23]. Emerging evidence demonstrates TRIM16’s capacity to stabilize and activate Nrf2 through dual mechanisms: disrupting the KEAP1-Nrf2 inhibitory complex and modulating the Src-Prdx1 redox signaling axis, as established in cardiomyocytes [24, 25]. Notably, our earlier work revealed TRIM16-mediated ubiquitination of phosphorylated TAK1 (p-TAK1) under doxorubicin stress, implicating a potential crosstalk between the TAK1-YAP-Nrf2 axis and TRIM16 activity in cardiac contexts [10]. This aligns with independent findings showing TRIM16-dependent K48-linked ubiquitination and degradation of p-TAK1 in lipid-stressed hepatocytes [9], underscoring its conserved regulatory role across stress contexts. However, TRIM16’s functional significance in sepsis-induced hepatic injury and its mechanistic relationship with YAP/Nrf2 signaling remained unexplored. Our current investigation bridges these gaps through systematic in vivo and in vitro approaches. In CLP-induced septic mice, AAV9-mediated hepatic TRIM16 overexpression robustly restored YAP1 and Nrf2 protein levels, counteracting their sepsis-associated depletion. The studies in vitro demonstrated that LPS-induced suppression of YAP1/Nrf2 expression in hepatocytes was exacerbated by TRIM16 knockdown but reversed by TRIM16 overexpression. Crucially, pharmacological inhibition of YAP1 using verteporfin abolished TRIM16-mediated hepatoprotection, establishing functional causality. These findings collectively delineate a novel mechanistic axis wherein TRIM16 preserves YAP1/Nrf2 signaling integrity to mitigate septic liver injury.

YAP, a central Hippo signaling effector, critically regulates hepatic homeostasis and stress adaptation, with its dysregulation implicated in diverse liver pathologies [26]. Emerging evidence positions the YAP-Nrf2 axis as a pivotal redox regulatory mechanism, exemplified by YAP-driven Nrf2 activation sustaining antioxidant defenses in malignancies [27]. Hepatic-specific Yap1 ablation precipitates hemorrhagic necrosis, oxidative stress exacerbation, and inflammatory cell infiltration through disrupted connective tissue growth factor (Ctgf) signaling [28]. Macrophage-specific deletion of Dvl2 exacerbates ischemia-reperfusion injury by impairing the YAP-HSF1 regulatory axis [29], collectively underscoring YAP’s multifaceted role in hepatic stress adaptation. Our findings extend this paradigm by elucidating TRIM16 -mediated YAP1 stabilization as a critical sepsis adaptation mechanism. We demonstrate that TRIM16 overexpression rescues sepsis-induced YAP1 depletion in hepatocytes through direct binding and K63-linked polyubiquitination, a post-translational modification that ensures YAP1 nuclear retention and functional integrity. Crucially, immunoprecipitation assays revealed that sepsis disrupts endogenous YAP1-TRIM16 interactions, a pathology reversible by TRIM16 supplementation. This mechanism parallels TRIM16’s established role in stabilizing NFE2L2/Nrf2 via K63-linked ubiquitination [30], but uniquely converges on YAP1 to amplify Nrf2 transcriptional programs. Crucially, TRIM16-mediated YAP1 stabilization sustains Nrf2 activation, creating a coordinated defense against sepsis-induced oxidative and inflammatory insults.

Conclusion

In summary, this study elucidates a novel hepatoprotective paradigm wherein TRIM16 protects against sepsis-induced acute liver injury through coordinated regulation of the YAP1/Nrf2 axis by stabilizing YAP1 and facilitating its nuclear localization. The TRIM16-YAP1-Nrf2 axis represents a pharmacologically tractable target, offering new avenues for developing precision therapies against sepsis-associated hepatic dysfunction. Future investigations should explore TRIM16’s structural determinants of E3 ligase specificity and its potential cross-regulation with other Hippo pathway components.
