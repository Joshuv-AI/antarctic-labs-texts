Vestibular schwannoma clinical prognostic factors and nomogram construction using SEER database
Authors: Yuyu Wei, Jialong Tian, Xiaojun Pang, Huai Chen, Xuhong Jin, Zibin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to delineate the long-term overall survival (OS) landscape and identify factors associated with OS in a large, population-based cohort of patients diagnosed with vestibular schwannoma (VS).Materials and methodsClinical, demographic, and treatment data for VS patients from the SEER database (2000–2019) were extracted and randomly divided into training and validation cohorts. Cohort comparability was assessed using Chi-square, Fisher’s exact, and Mann-Whitney U tests. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify factors associated with OS. Nomograms for predicting 3-, 5-, and 10-year OS were constructed. Model performance was evaluated using the concordance index (C-index), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsFrom the initial pool of 28,693 VS patients in the SEER database, 20,235 were included in the analysis. Among these, 14,167 patients (70.01%) were allocated to the training cohort, and 6,068 patients (29.99%) to the validation cohort. Multivariate Cox regression identified age (hazard ratio [HR] = 12.895; 95% confidence interval [CI] 8.347–19.809; p < 0.001), sex (HR = 0.761; 95% CI 0.683–0.849; p < 0.001), race (HR = 0.214; 95% CI 0.089–0.515; p < 0.001), tumor size (HR = 3.024; 95% CI 2.464–3.712; p < 0.001), primary site surgery (HR = 0.409; 95% CI 0.300–0.557; p < 0.001), and radiation therapy (HR = 0.812; 95% CI 0.710–0.929; p = 0.002) as independent prognostic factors. Nomograms based on these variables demonstrated robust predictive capability, with a C-index of 0.737 ± 0.065 for the training cohort and an AUC of 0.759, 0.766, and 0.790 for predicting 3-, 5-, and 10-year OS, respectively. In the validation cohort, the model yielded a C-index of 0.763 ± 0.053 and AUCs of 0.757, 0.765, and 0.760 for the corresponding timeframes. Other factors, such as year of diagnosis, reporting source, surgical-radiation sequence, laterality, and chemotherapy, did not exhibit significant associations with OS.ConclusionThis analysis of over 20,000 VS patients provides a comprehensive overview of long-term survival in this patient population. We identified age, sex, race, tumor size, and treatment modality (surgery and radiotherapy) as factors associated with OS. The developed nomograms offer a tool for visualizing the combined impact of these demographic and clinical factors on long-term survival, which may contribute to comprehensive patient assessment and counseling at the time of diagnosis.Keywords: Vestibular schwannoma, SEER database, Nomogram, Overall survival, Population study, Prognostic factors

Introduction

Vestibular Schwannoma (VS) was first described by Sandifort in 1777. It is a benign tumor that arises from Schwann cells of the vestibular nerve (eighth cranial nerve) within the internal auditory canal. Typically, it extends into the cerebellopontine angle and represents a classic form of vestibular nerve sheath tumor [1]. The annual incidence of VS ranges from approximately 3.0 to 5.2 per 100,000, with a slightly lower incidence in males than in females. VS primarily affect middle-aged adults, with the average age of onset ranging between 55 and 65 years [2]. They account for approximately 6% to 10% of all intracranial tumors and 80% to 90% of those located in the cerebellopontine angle, making them among the most common benign intracranial tumors in this region. Studies suggest that ionizing radiation, allergic diseases, and noise exposure may be associated with the development of VSs [3]; however, the exact pathogenesis remains unclear [4].

Common clinical symptoms include hearing loss, tinnitus, vertigo, and ataxia, with unilateral hearing loss (in 90% of cases) and tinnitus (in over 60% of cases) being the most frequent manifestations. As the tumor enlarges, it may cause expansion of the internal auditory canal, compress the brainstem, medulla, and cerebellum, and affect cranial nerves and blood vessels in the posterior fossa [5]. In severe cases, these complications can be life-threatening. Although VSs progress more slowly than malignant tumors, the period from initial tumor development to symptom onset and diagnosis can span several years to decades. Current treatment options for VSs include conservative management, surgical resection, and radiation therapy, providing patients with a range of therapeutic options [6]. Given its benign nature, overall survival (OS) is not a primary endpoint for evaluating specific VS treatments. However, a diagnosis of VS places a patient within a healthcare system, and the tumor’s management occurs in the context of the patient’s overall health and lifespan. Large-scale, population-level data on the long-term survival of VS patients and the factors associated with it remain limited.

The SEER database, or the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program, is maintained by the U.S. National Cancer Institute (NCI) and aggregates data on cancer incidence, survival rates, and treatment outcomes across the United States, thereby supporting cancer research and epidemiological studies [7]. The SEER database provides a unique opportunity to address this knowledge gap. Therefore, this study leverages the SEER database to analyze a cohort of nearly 30,000 VS patients. Our objective is not to propose OS as a metric for treatment efficacy, but to describe the long-term survival profile of this population and to develop a model that synthesizes the complex interplay of demographic and clinical factors associated with OS. This macroscopic perspective may provide valuable context for patient counseling and risk stratification at the population level.

Materials and methods

Data extraction

Data were obtained using SEER*Stat software (version 8.4.3) from the SEER Research Plus Data, 17 Registries, November 2021 Subset (2000–2019). This subset includes clinical, pathological, treatment, and follow-up information for 28,693 patients diagnosed with VSs (VS).

Inclusion criteria comprised patients with ICD-O-3 histology codes 9560/0 (Neurilemoma, NOS), 9560/1 (Neurilemomatosis), or 9560/3 (Malignant Neurilemoma).

Exclusion criteria included: [1] patients with non-VS tumors; [2] survival times less than one month or unknown; [3] tumors located outside of the primary site; and [4] insufficient treatment data or reliance on death certificates or autopsy reports. Data retrieved covered patient age, sex, race, year of diagnosis, reporting source, tumor size, surgical-radiation sequence, laterality, primary site surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, and OS (OS). Endpoint

OS (OS), defined as the duration from diagnosis to death or last follow-up, served as the primary endpoint.

As shown in Fig. 1; Table 1, the data were split into training and validation cohorts in a 7:3 ratio (n = 20,235). The training cohort consisted of 14,167 patients (70.01%), while the validation cohort had 6,068 patients (29.99%). In summary, rather than simply excluding cases with missing values, treating “unknown” as a distinct category represents one of the most widely accepted and methodologically sound approaches, particularly when working with clinical variables that frequently contain unreported data. Its simplicity and ability to preserve dataset integrity have made it a preferred strategy in many studies. Accordingly, our nomogram incorporates an “Unknown” option for relevant variables, enabling clinicians to generate predictions even when tumor size or race is unspecified. This approach enhances the practical utility of the model in real-world clinical settings and aligns with the common methodology employed in numerous SEER-based nomogram studies [8].

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (version 26.0, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 4.1.2, Vienna, Austria). Variables with a p-value < 0.10 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate analysis. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed p-value < 0.05 [9].

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 20,235 patients diagnosed with VS were included in the analysis based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Among them, 14,167 patients (70.01%) were assigned to the training cohort, and 6,068 patients (29.99%) were placed in the validation cohort (Fig. 1). The median survival times for the overall group, training cohort, and validation cohort were 77 months (Interquartile Range [IQR], 36–127), 78 months (IQR, 36–127), and 76 months (IQR, 36–125), respectively (Table 1).

Survival factors in the training cohort

Log-rank tests were used to create survival curves for various clinical factors, including age (p < 0.0001), gender (p < 0.0001), race (p < 0.0001), tumor size (p = 0.0045), primary site surgery (p < 0.0001), and radiation (p = 0.082) (Fig. 2). Univariate analysis with the Cox proportional hazards model identified clinical factors with p-values < 0.10, which were subsequently included in the multivariate analysis. As presented in Table 2, age, sex, race, tumor size, primary site surgery, and radiation were found to significantly impact OS (OS) in multivariate Cox regression. However, year of diagnosis, reporting source, surgical-radiation sequence, laterality, and chemotherapy did not show significant associations with OS (Table 2).

The analysis revealed that better OS was associated with patients younger than 49 years, female, non-Black, with tumors ≤ 1 cm, who underwent appropriate surgical method or received radiation. Conversely, poorer outcomes may be associated with older age, male gender, Black race, tumors > 3 cm, absence of surgery, and no radiation treatment (Fig. 3).

Nomogram construction and internal validation

After identifying six independent prognostic factors, nomograms were constructed to predict 3-, 5-, and 10-year survival for the training cohort. The C-index for the training cohort was 0.733 ± 0.065, with the model’s area under the curve (AUC) for 3-year, 5-year, and 10-year survival being 0.759, 0.766, and 0.790, respectively (Fig. 4). Calibration curves indicated that the predicted probabilities for 3-, 5-, and 10-year survival closely matched observed survival outcomes (Fig. 5). Decision curve analysis (DCA) for 5- and 10-year survival demonstrated the clinical relevance of the model (Fig. 6).

The nomogram was then applied to the validation cohort, which yielded a C-index of 0.763 ± 0.053. The AUCs for the 3-, 5-, and 10-year survival ROC curves were 0.757, 0.765, and 0.760, respectively (Fig. 4). Calibration curves for the validation cohort also exhibited good agreement with observed survival data (Fig. 5). DCA for 5- and 10-year survival in the validation cohort confirmed the model’s clinical applicability (Fig. 6).

Discussion

VS has been recognized for over two centuries. Most cases are benign and progress slowly. Advances in audiometry, CT, and MRI have enabled earlier detection, facilitating timely intervention. While initial management emphasized gross total resection, contemporary strategies prioritize functional preservation.

The SEER database, maintained by the National Cancer Institute, is a large-scale clinical oncology registry encompassing cancer diagnosis, treatment, and survival data from approximately 30% of the U.S. population [10]. Its rigorously documented baseline characteristics and OS follow-up data are particularly robust. Leveraging these resources, we extracted clinical, therapeutic, and survival information from nearly 30,000 patients diagnosed with VS between 2000 and 2019. To our knowledge, this constitutes one of the largest cohort studies to date examining OS in VS patients. Our analysis highlights the prognostic significance of age, sex, race, tumor size, surgical intervention, and radiotherapy. We constructed survival curves for each variable and validated the resulting model using the C-index, ROC analysis, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA).

VS is a benign tumor associated with prolonged OS, often approaching general population life expectancy. Treatment goals therefore focus on functional preservation—including hearing and facial nerve function—tumor control, and quality of life maintenance, rather than survival extension. As demonstrated by Mistarz et al., patients aged 70 years or older with VS exhibit survival rates comparable to those of an age-matched background population [11], underscoring the critical role of age in determining OS. Our findings strongly align with the established biology of VS. As consistently reported, and as demonstrated in our analysis, patient age is the dominant factor associated with OS, reflecting the indolent nature of the tumor.

The association of larger tumor size with OS is plausible, as giant schwannomas can, albeit rarely, lead to fatal complications from brainstem compression or hydrocephalus. The associations observed for sex, race, and treatment modalities require careful interpretation. They likely reflect a complex amalgamation of biological differences, socioeconomic factors, access to care, and underlying patient comorbidities—variables not fully captured in the SEER database.

Tumor size is a well-established prognostic factor. Small tumors (< 3 cm) are associated with excellent outcomes under any management strategy (observation, radiosurgery, or microsurgery), with risks primarily related to treatment-associated complications such as hearing loss or facial weakness rather than mortality. In contrast, large tumors (≥ 3 cm), particularly giant schwannomas (>4 cm), often cause brainstem compression, hydrocephalus, and elevated intracranial pressure. These present considerable surgical challenges and may adversely affect survival [12]. Preoperative tumor size correlates strongly with postoperative facial nerve function, as demonstrated by Orin Bloch et al. [13]. Our results align with these observations, showing increasing hazard ratios with larger tumor size. Patients with tumors >3 cm have significantly worse prognosis than those with tumors < 1 cm.

Regarding surgical approach selection, Shaheryar F. Ansari et al. reviewed 35 studies involving 5064 patients and concluded that the retrosigmoid approach (RS) is associated with lower rates of postoperative facial nerve dysfunction compared to the translabyrinthine approach (TL) for tumors >1.5 cm [14]. Most VS grow slowly or remain stable for years, with minimal impact on survival. However, rapidly growing tumors may cause severe neurological symptoms and require aggressive intervention. Tumors extending to the ventral brainstem, jugular foramen, or tentorium cerebelli are associated with higher surgical risks and potentially reduced OS due to challenges in achieving gross-total resection [15].

Three main treatment modalities are available: observation, surgery, and radiotherapy [16]. Active surveillance with clinical and radiological monitoring is suitable for minimally symptomatic patients with small tumors, advanced age, or significant comorbidities [17–19]. Carmen Ruiz-García and Luis Lassaletta recommend observation for patients aged 65–70 years with Koos grade I–II tumors, transitioning to active treatment if significant growth occurs [20]. Malene Kirchmann et al. reported that 63% of 156 observed VS showed no significant growth over 10 years [21]. Most growth occurs within 3–5 years after diagnosis, typically at a rate of 1–2 mm per year [22]. Long-term monitoring remains essential, as Robert J. Macielak et al. found that approximately 4% of tumors exhibited delayed growth after five years of stability [23].

Microsurgical resection remains the primary treatment for large, symptomatic, or growing tumors. Extent of resection influences OS [24], and younger patients with larger tumors often require more aggressive management. Common surgical approaches include the middle fossa approach (for hearing preservation in medium-sized tumors), the retrosigmoid approach (providing broad cerebellopontine angle exposure), and the translabyrinthine approach (typically for larger tumors or those in patients with poor hearing) [14, 19]. Michael A. Cohen emphasizes that anatomical variability of the internal auditory canal may affect exposure of the facial and cochleovestibular nerves, influencing functional outcomes [25]. Surgical approach depends on tumor size, hearing status, location, and patient age and health [26–28]. Although gross-total resection (GTR) is ideal, intraoperative judgment regarding functional preservation remains challenging, and final outcomes are often determined during follow-up [29].

Nickalus R. Khan reviewed 420 surgical cases from North American institutions (1998–2021) and reported GTR/near-total resection (NTR) rates of 82%, with recurrence in 10 patients. Residual tumor was most commonly attributed to adhesions at the facial nerve–tumor interface [19]. Turki Elarjani et al. noted higher recurrence rates after NTR than GTR, supporting the latter as the preferred strategy when safe and feasible [19].

Our analysis identified surgery as an independent prognostic factor. In selected clinical contexts, surgical resection (including partial, GTR, or STR) may be associated with improved OS compared to observation. However, given the benign natural history of VS and the lack of granular recurrence and functional data in SEER, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. Contemporary management prioritizes functional preservation and quality of life, advocating for maximal safe resection rather than radical excision regardless of neurological risk [30].

Radiotherapy—including stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) [31], Gamma Knife [32], linear accelerator-based systems (e.g., CyberKnife, Novalis), proton beam therapy [33], and fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy [34, 35]—serves as an alternative for small tumors or patients unsuitable for surgery. It is less effective for larger tumors [16] and carries risks of inflammation, fibrosis, and vascular injury, which may complicate subsequent surgery and impair cranial nerve function [36].

We confirmed radiotherapy as an independent prognostic factor and found no significant effect of treatment sequencing (i.e., preoperative vs. postoperative) on OS. However, from a surgical perspective, preoperative radiation may increase adhesion or tissue rigidity, complicating resection. Both primary and adjuvant radiotherapy are accepted in clinical practice. Patients undergoing radiation require lifelong imaging surveillance, currently recommended annually [37, 38]. Chemotherapy did not demonstrate a significant effect on OS in our cohort. The choice among observation, surgery, and radiotherapy necessitates careful clinical judgment.

Although most published studies emphasize functional outcomes such hearing and facial nerve preservation, the integration of multifactorial influences on OS has received less attention, and large-scale prognostic models remain scarce. This population-based study of over 20,000 VS patients provides a detailed overview of the long-term survival landscape associated with this benign intracranial tumor. We developed and validated nomograms that integrate demographic and clinical factors—specifically age, sex, race, tumor size, surgical intervention, and radiotherapy—to visualize their combined association with OS. We fully acknowledge that the primary goals of VS management are functional preservation, tumor control, and quality of life maintenance, not survival extension. The clinical value of our model lies not in guiding specific therapeutic choices (e.g., surgical approach), but in offering a broader prognostic context.

Limitations of the study

Limitations of this study are as follows: [1] The absence of data on functional outcomes (hearing, facial nerve function, quality of life) in the SEER database significantly restricts the model’s utility in guiding specific therapeutic decisions, which rightly prioritize these functional endpoints [2]. Confounding by NF2 Status: The SEER database does not allow for the accurate identification or exclusion of patients with neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2). As NF2 is associated with a different prognosis and tumor behavior, its inclusion introduces an uncontrolled confounding factor [3]. Data Granularity: The use of “unknown” categories for variables like tumor size and race, while a practical necessity for retaining statistical power, limits the biological interpretability of the hazard ratios for these groups [4]. Generalizability: As the study is based on a U.S. population, the applicability of the nomograms to other healthcare systems and ethnic populations requires external validation. Despite these limitations, the principal strength of this study is its scale and population-based nature, which provides a unique “big picture” view of survival in VS that is difficult to obtain from single-institution studies. The model performs robustly in predicting OS, confirming that the identified factors collectively provide a statistically sound representation of survival in this cohort.

Conclusion

Through the analysis of a large, population-based cohort, this study delineates the factors associated with long-term overall survival in patients with VS. The developed nomograms provide a reliable tool for visualizing the combined impact of demographic and clinical factors on 3-, 5-, and 10-year OS. We emphasize that this model is not intended to guide specific therapeutic interventions for the tumor itself, but rather to serve as a supplementary tool for providing a comprehensive prognostic overview during initial patient counseling and for stratifying long-term risk in population health studies.
