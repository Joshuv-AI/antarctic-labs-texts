Hidden Momentum and the Absence of the Gravitational Spin Hall Effect in a Uniform Field
Andrzej Czarnecki *, Ting Gao *

Abstract
We re-examine the recent claim that a Dirac particle freely falling in a uniform gravitational field exhibits a spin-dependent transverse deflection (gravitational spin Hall effect). Using a circulating mass model, we show that hidden momentum arises in uniform fields when an object carries angular momentum. On the quantum side, we analyze the Dirac Hamiltonian in a uniform potential, construct its Foldy–Wouthuysen form, and evaluate the Heisenberg evolution of spin-polarized Gaussian packets. The state used previously, with ⟨ p ⟩ = 0 \langle p\rangle=0 , is not at rest: because canonical and kinetic momenta differ, the packet carries a spin-dependent hidden momentum from t = 0 t=0 . Imposing ⟨ x ⁡ ( 0 ) ⟩ = ⟨ v ⁡ ( 0 ) ⟩ = 0 \langle x(0)\rangle=\langle v(0)\rangle=0 requires a compensating spin-dependent ⟨ p ⁡ ( 0 ) ⟩ \langle p(0)\rangle ; with this preparation ⟨ x ⁡ ( t ) ⟩ = 0 \langle x(t)\rangle=0 to leading order in the gravitational acceleration g g . Generalizing, an exact Foldy–Wouthuysen transformation (linear in g g but to all orders in 1 / c 1/c ) shows that spin-dependent transverse motion begins no earlier than at O ⁡ ( g 2 ) O(g^{2}) for a broad class of wave packets. We conclude that a uniform field does not produce a gravitational spin Hall effect at linear order; the previously reported drift stems from inconsistent initial states and misinterpreting canonical momentum.

called the gravitational spin Hall effect (SHE)—with analogues in
optics
Onoda et al. 2004
;

, whose counterpart,
“spinoptics” in curved spacetime, is still under active debate
Frolov 2024
;
Frolov and Shoom 2024
;
Andersson and Oancea 2023
;

2
Summary of Wang’s Study of the Gravitational Spin Hall Effect

The analysis presented in Ref.
Wang 2024
considers a state that, at the initial time

t=0
, is described by a Gaussian wave packet,

\psi\left({\bm{x}},t=0\right)=\frac{1}{\left(a^{2}\pi\right)^{3/4}}\exp\left(-\frac{{\bm{x}}^{2}}{2a^{2}}\right),

(1)

where
a
is a constant characterizing the spatial extension of the
initial packet. The complete wave function also contains a Dirac spinor
factor describing polarization along the
\pm y
-axis; it remains
constant during free fall, so we omit it here.

The time evolution of the wave packet is governed by the Dirac Hamiltonian
with a linear potential term
V\left(z\right)=-mgz
describing the
interaction with the gravitational field. This Hamiltonian is unitarily
transformed using the Foldy–Wouthuysen
transformation
Foldy and Wouthuysen 1950
;
Foldy 2006
.
It is found that at time
t>0
the expectation values of the
x

and
z
coordinates are nonzero. For the
z
coordinate,
this is, of course, as expected for free fall,

\left\langle z\left(t\right)\right\rangle_{\pm}=\frac{gt^{2}}{2},

(2)

where the subscript on the LHS refers to the
y
-polarization state.
However, the
x
coordinate is also found to develop a nonzero,
polarization-dependent expectation value:

\left\langle x\left(t\right)\right\rangle_{\pm}=\mp\frac{\hbar gt}{4mc^{2}}.

(3)

We use SI units rather than setting
\hbar=c=1
to emphasize the
order of quantum and relativistic effects.

The nonzero expectation value of a horizontal deflection is interpreted
as a gravitational spin Hall effect, previously established only in
non-uniform fields, such as those experienced by a particle flying by a
star
Liberman and Zel’dovich 1992
;
Frolov 2024
;
Frolov and Shoom 2024
;
Andersson and Oancea 2023
;
Yamamoto 2018
;
Bliokh et al. 2015
;
Noh et al. 2021
.
An astonishing feature of Equation (
3
) is
that the expectation value grows linearly with time. In other words,
the
x
-component of velocity seems to be independent of time; it is nonzero
even at
t=0
when the wave packet is released. This contradicts
the graphic in Ref.
Wang 2024
. A motion with a
constant horizontal velocity corresponds to a projectile trajectory depicted
in Figure
1
a. However, for a phenomenon to
be interpreted as a Hall effect, the particle should develop the transverse
component of velocity from zero. The canonical momentum operator has a zero expectation value in the
initial wave packet described by Equation (
1
). However, canonical and kinetic momenta differ in the presence of a gravitational field. As we demonstrate below, that packet does have a nonvanishing velocity.

3
Hidden Momentum

In this section, we revisit a classical toy model that demonstrates that an object with angular momentum can have “hidden momentum” in a gravitational field, which does not vanish even when there is no overall motion of the system.

3.1
A Simple Model

The notion of hidden momentum first appeared in a 1967 paper
by Shockley and James
Shockley and James 1967
in the context of electromagnetism.
A concise discussion and a list of key references up to 2012 can be
found in
Griffiths 2012
.

A simple model of a non-electromagnetic system containing hidden momentum
can be constructed, following an electric model described in
Babson et al. 2009
.
Consider a set of masses
m
that circulate without friction in a pipe
bent into a rectangular shape, shown in Figure
2
. In
the upper horizontal section,
n
masses progress from right to left
with speed
v
; they accelerate in the left vertical section due
to the gravitational field;
N
of them move with speed
V>v
in
the lower horizontal section (conservation of the current imposes

NV=nv
); and they finally slow down in the right vertical section. In
the nonrelativistic limit, the total mechanical momentum of the circulating
masses vanishes because the larger speed in the lower section is compensated
for by the proportionally smaller number of masses. The net momentum of
the masses arises due to the difference in relativistic factors
\gamma

corresponding to
v
and
V
; we denote this effect by a subscript
sr for special relativity,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{sr}}p_{x}

\displaystyle=N\gamma_{V}mV-n\gamma_{v}mv

(4)

\displaystyle=N\left(\gamma_{V}mV-\frac{V}{v}\gamma_{v}mv\right)

(5)

\displaystyle\simeq NmV\frac{V^{2}-v^{2}}{2c^{2}}.

(6)

If the length of the vertical sides is
h
, we have

V^{2}-v^{2}=2gh,

(7)

and

\Delta_{\text{sr}}p_{x}=NmV\frac{gh}{c^{2}}.

(8)

Figure 2:
Masses circulating in a frictionless pipe bent into a rectangle. The
rectangle is placed in the constant gravitational field near the Earth.

3.2
Effect of Gravitational Time Dilation

In Equation (
4
), we used just the special-relativistic factors

\gamma
, for example,
\gamma_{v}=1/\sqrt{1-v^{2}/c^{2}}
. Accounting
for the gravitational redshift corrects them by an altitude-dependent
amount,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}/c^{2}}}\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left(1+\frac{gz}{c^{2}}\right)^{2}-v^{2}/c^{2}}}\simeq 1-\frac{gz}{c^{2}}+\frac{v^{2}}{2c^{2}}.

(9)

Taking the center of the circuit as the reference point
z=0
, such
that the upper horizontal section is at
z=h/2
, we get a redshift
correction to the
x
-momentum,

\Delta_{\text{rs}}p_{x}=N\left(\frac{gh}{2c^{2}}mV+\frac{V}{v}\frac{gh}{2c^{2}}mv\right)=N\frac{gh}{c^{2}}mV.

(10)

This equals the
p_{x}
found in Equation (
8
), so it doubles
that effect.

3.3
Angular Momentum in the Pipe

The masses in the horizontal sections carry angular momentum relative
to the center of the frame, pointing out of the figure:

L_{h}=m\frac{h}{2}\left(V+\frac{V}{v}v\right)N=mhVN.

(11)

The masses in vertical sections have speed
u=\left(v+V\right)/2
on
average. Their number is
N\cdot V/u\cdot h/b
, where
b
is the
length of a horizontal section, so they contribute to the angular momentum

L_{v}=2\cdot\frac{b}{2}\cdot mu\frac{NVh}{ub}=mNVh=L_{h}.

(12)

Thus, the total mechanical momentum
{\bm{p}}
,
\left|{\bm{p}}\right|=\Delta_{\text{sr}}p_{x}+\Delta_{\text{rs}}p_{x}
,
can be written in terms of the angular momentum as

{\bm{p}}_{\text{mech}}=\frac{{\bm{L}}\times{\bm{g}}}{c^{2}},

(13)

where
\left|{\bm{L}}\right|=L_{v}+L_{h}=2mNVh
and points out of the figure.

4
Gravitational SHE in the Uniform Gravitational Field Revisited

In Section
2
, we see that the motion of the Dirac particle in the uniform gravitational field derived in
Wang 2024
leads to unphysical consequences. To understand the origin of this problem, we evaluate the evolution of various operators for the wave packet considered in
Wang 2024
. Focusing on the upper-left block of the Hamiltonian which describes positive energy states, the FW-transformed Hamiltonian can be written as

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}}=Vmc^{2}+\frac{\bm{p}\cdot\left(V\bm{p}\right)}{2m}+\frac{g\hbar}{4mc^{2}}\left(\sigma_{1}p_{2}-\sigma_{2}p_{1}\right),\quad V=1-\frac{gz}{c^{2}}.

(14)

The wave function considered in
Wang 2024
is a Gaussian wave packet with the spinor polarized along the positive or negative
y
-direction

\psi_{\pm}(\bm{x},0)=\phi(\bm{x},0)\chi_{\pm},

(15)

\phi(\bm{x},0)=\frac{1}{\left(a^{2}\pi\right)^{3/4}}e^{-\frac{\bm{x}^{2}}{2a^{2}}},

(16)

\chi_{+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
i\end{pmatrix},\quad\chi_{-}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}i\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

(17)

The expectation value of an operator
\mathcal{O}
at a given time is then

\langle\mathcal{O}(t)\rangle=\langle\psi(\bm{x},0)|U^{-1}\mathcal{O}U|\psi(\bm{x},0)\rangle,

(18)

where
U=\mathrm{Exp}\left(-i\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}}t/\hbar\right)
is the time evolution operator. Using the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff (BCH) expansion,
U^{-1}\mathcal{O}U
can be written as

U^{-1}\mathcal{O}U=\mathcal{O}+i\frac{t}{\hbar}\left[\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}},\mathcal{O}\right]-\frac{t^{2}}{2\hbar^{2}}\left[\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}},\left[\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}},\mathcal{O}\right]\right]+\cdots.

(19)

By evaluating
\langle x\rangle
, the author of
Wang 2024
concluded that the wave packet is moving along the
x
-direction in a spin-dependent way. However, if one evaluates the momentum,

\left[\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}},p_{1}\right]=0,

(20)

it leads to
p_{1}(t)=0
and suggests the wave packet is not moving along the
x
-direction, in direct conflict with Equation (
3
). This apparent contradiction is resolved by noting that the momentum in the Hamiltonian refers to the canonical momentum, which does not always reflect the true motion of the particle. Indeed, applying the classical Hamilton’s equations to the Hamiltonian in Equation (
14
) gives

m\frac{dx}{dt}=m\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}}}{\partial p_{1}}=Vp_{1}-\frac{g\hbar}{4c^{2}}\sigma_{2}.

(21)

Therefore, the conditions for
v_{1}=0
and
p_{1}=0
are different for the Hamiltonian under consideration. For a particle to initially stay at rest, it has to have a nonzero, spin-dependent initial momentum. This classical observation carries over to quantum mechanics by noting that

\frac{d}{dt}\langle x(t)\rangle=\langle\psi(\bm{x},t)|\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}},x\right]|\psi(\bm{x},t)\rangle=\langle\psi(\bm{x},t)|\left(\frac{Vp_{1}}{m}-\frac{g\hbar}{4mc^{2}}\sigma_{2}\right)|\psi(\bm{x},t)\rangle.

(22)

Contrary to the usual expectation, this suggests that the Gaussian wave packet in
Equation (
16
),
which is usually believed to be motionless, is moving in the simultaneous presence of spin and a gravitational field. Moreover, under the condition
\langle\bm{p}(0)\rangle=0
, different orientations of the spin give different initial velocities. To determine whether there is a spin Hall effect, it is important to start from the correct initial conditions. Given that the spin Hall effect refers to the deflection of the trajectory in the presence of spin, it is appropriate to choose
\langle\bm{x}(0)\rangle=0
and
\langle\bm{v}(0)\rangle=0
as the starting point to discuss the trajectory. For this reason, instead of using the wave functions in Equation (
15
), we consider the wave functions

\Psi_{\pm}(\bm{x},0)=\Phi_{\pm}(\bm{x},0)\chi_{\pm},

(23)

\Phi_{\pm}(\bm{x},0)=\frac{1}{\left(a^{2}\pi\right)^{3/4}}e^{-\frac{\bm{x}^{2}}{2a^{2}}+ik_{\pm}x/\hbar},

(24)

where
k_{\pm}=\pm\frac{g\hbar}{4c^{2}}
, which introduces a spin-dependent initial momentum to enforce the initial condition
\langle\bm{v}(0)\rangle=0
. Indeed, as one can explicitly check,

\langle\bm{v}(0)\rangle_{+}=\langle\bm{v}(0)\rangle_{-}=0.

(25)

The trajectory of the particle is then determined by evaluating
\langle\bm{x}(t)\rangle
with the help of Equation (
19
). The motion along the
y
-direction trivially vanishes at leading order in
g
, and the motion along the
z
-direction gives the usual free-fall motion. For the
x
-direction, we get

\left[\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}},x\right]=\left(\frac{Vp_{1}}{m}-\frac{g\hbar}{4mc^{2}}\sigma_{2}\right),

(26)

whose expectation value for the wave function in Equation (
23
) vanishes exactly as required by the initial conditions. Taking another commutator with
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}}
introduces an extra power of
g
; therefore, we conclude that there is no gravitational spin Hall effect for the motion described by the Hamiltonian in Equation (
14
).

Before concluding this section, we note that there is some ambiguity in the interpretation of
x
as the position operator of the particle; however, as discussed at the end of Section
5
, this conclusion is not affected.

5
Absence of Gravitational SHE in a Uniform Gravitational Field: All Orders in
1/c

In this section, we generalize the result in Section
4
to linear order in
g
but to all orders in
1/c
and for a wide class of wave packets. For this purpose, we start from the Dirac Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}=\beta mc^{2}V+V\bm{\alpha}\cdot\bm{p}c-\frac{i\hbar c}{2}\bm{\alpha}\cdot\bm{\nabla}V,

(27)

where
\beta=\gamma^{0}
and
\alpha^{i}=\gamma^{0}\gamma^{i}
. As noted in
Obukhov 2001
, this Hamiltonian allows for an exact FW transformation
Eriksen and Kolsrud 1960
;
Nikitin 1998
. Following the discussion in
Eriksen and Kolsrud 1960
, this is achieved by a two-step unitary transformation

U=U_{2}U_{1},\quad U_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(1+\gamma^{0}J\right),\quad U_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(1+\gamma_{0}\epsilon^{\prime}\right),

(28)

where
J
is a unitary operator that anticommutes with both
\mathcal{H}
and
\gamma^{0}
:

JJ^{\dagger}=J^{\dagger}J=1,\quad J\mathcal{H}=-\mathcal{H}J,\quad J\gamma^{0}=-\gamma^{0}J.

(29)

A Hamiltonian in general contains both odd (i.e., anticommuting with
\gamma^{0}
) and even pieces (i.e., commuting with
\gamma^{0}
):

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{odd}}+\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{even}},\quad\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{odd}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{H}-\gamma^{0}\mathcal{H}\gamma^{0}\right),\quad\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{even}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{H}+\gamma^{0}\mathcal{H}\gamma^{0}\right).

(30)

The effect of
U_{1}
is to transform this Hamiltonian to a new one that contains only odd terms,

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=U_{1}\mathcal{H}U_{1}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{odd}}-J\gamma^{0}\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{even}},\quad\gamma^{0}\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=-\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\gamma^{0}.

(31)

The second transformation is then defined in terms of this new Hamiltonian:

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}}=U_{2}\mathcal{H}^{\prime}U_{2}^{\dagger},\quad\epsilon^{\prime}=\mathcal{H}^{\prime}/\sqrt{(H^{\prime})^{2}},

(32)

and this transformation converts the odd Hamiltonian into an even one:

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}}=\gamma^{0}\sqrt{(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})^{2}}.

(33)

The key challenge for constructing the exact FW transformation is to find the operator
J
that satisfies the requirements in Equation (
29
). For Hamiltonian (
27
) under consideration, this is possible with

J=i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{5},

(34)

where
\gamma^{5}=i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{1}\gamma^{2}\gamma^{3}
. We therefore find

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=V\gamma^{0}\bm{\gamma}\cdot\bm{p}c-imc^{2}V\gamma^{0}\gamma^{5}-\frac{i\hbar c}{2}\gamma^{0}\bm{\gamma}\cdot\bm{\nabla}V,

(35)

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}}=\gamma^{0}\sqrt{m^{2}c^{4}V^{2}+Vp^{2}c^{2}V+\frac{\hbar^{2}c^{2}}{2}V\Delta V-\frac{\hbar^{2}c^{2}}{4}\phi^{2}+V\hbar c^{2}\bm{\Sigma}\cdot\left(\bm{\phi}\times\bm{p}\right)+m\hbar c^{3}V\bm{\phi}\cdot\bm{\gamma}\gamma^{5}},

(36)

where

\bm{\phi}=\bm{\nabla}V,\quad\bm{\Sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\sigma}&0\\
0&\bm{\sigma}\end{pmatrix}.

(37)

Focusing on the upper-left block of the Hamiltonian and taking the uniform gravitational potential
V=1-gz/c^{2}
, this Hamiltonian simplifies:

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}}=\sqrt{m^{2}c^{4}V^{2}+Vp^{2}c^{2}V-gV\hbar\bm{\sigma}\cdot\left(\hat{\bm{z}}\times\bm{p}+mc\hat{\bm{z}}\right)-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4c^{2}}g^{2}}.

(38)

To understand the behavior of this Hamiltonian, we take the dominant term
m^{2}c^{4}
outside the square root and expand in the relativistic and gravitational corrections:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}}

\displaystyle=mc^{2}\sqrt{1+\xi},

(39)

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle=V^{2}-1+\frac{1}{m^{2}c^{2}}Vp^{2}V-\frac{1}{m^{2}c^{4}}gV\hbar\bm{\sigma}\cdot\left(\hat{\bm{z}}\times\bm{p}+mc\hat{\bm{z}}\right)-\left(\frac{\hbar g}{2mc^{3}}\right)^{2}.

(40)

Expanding the Hamiltonian for small
\xi
generates powers
\xi^{n}
, which in general are difficult to calculate. However, if one focuses on terms up to linear order in
g
(under the assumptions
gz/c^{2}\ll 1
and
g\hbar/(mc^{3})\ll 1
), it is possible to proceed relatively easily. In that case,
\xi
reduces to

\xi=\frac{1}{m^{2}c^{2}}Vp^{2}V-\frac{2gz}{c^{2}}-\frac{1}{m^{2}c^{4}}g\hbar\bm{\sigma}\cdot\left(\hat{\bm{z}}\times\bm{p}+mc\hat{\bm{z}}\right),

(41)

and after dropping all terms of higher order in
g
,

\displaystyle\xi^{n}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(mc)^{2n}}\left(Vp^{2}V\right)^{n}-\frac{1}{(mc)^{2(n-1)}}\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}p^{2l}\frac{2gz}{c^{2}}p^{2(n-1-l)}

\displaystyle-\frac{ng\hbar}{(mc)^{2n}c^{2}}p^{2(n-1)}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\left(\hat{\bm{z}}\times\bm{p}+mc\hat{\bm{z}}\right)

(42)

Using the relation

p^{2l}zp^{2m}=zp^{2(l+m)}-2i\hbar lp_{3}p^{2(l+m-1)},

(43)

the result above simplifies to

\displaystyle\xi^{n}=

\displaystyle\frac{p^{2n}}{(mc)^{2n}}-\frac{2ngz}{c^{2}}\left[\frac{p^{2n}}{(mc)^{2n}}+\frac{p^{2(n-1)}}{(mc)^{2(n-1)}}\right]

(44)

\displaystyle+\frac{2ni\hbar gp_{3}}{m^{2}c^{4}}\left[n\frac{p^{2(n-1)}}{(mc)^{2(n-1)}}+(n-1)\frac{p^{2(n-2)}}{(mc)^{2(n-2)}}\right]-\frac{ng\hbar\bm{\sigma}\cdot\left(\hat{\bm{z}}\times\bm{p}+mc\hat{\bm{z}}\right)}{m^{2}c^{4}}\frac{p^{2(n-1)}}{(mc)^{2(n-1)}}.

Then, the Hamiltonian can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}}=

\displaystyle mc^{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\frac{p^{2n}}{(mc)^{2n}}+b_{n}\frac{gz}{c^{2}}\frac{p^{2n}}{(mc)^{2n}}+c_{n}\frac{i\hbar gp_{3}}{m^{2}c^{4}}\frac{p^{2n}}{(mc)^{2n}}

\displaystyle+d_{n}\frac{g\hbar\bm{\sigma}\cdot\left(\hat{\bm{z}}\times\bm{p}+mc\hat{\bm{z}}\right)}{m^{2}c^{4}}\frac{p^{2n}}{(mc)^{2n}},

(45)

where

\displaystyle a_{n}=\frac{(-1)^{n-1}(2n)!}{4^{n}(n!)^{2}(2n-1)},\quad b_{n}=-2na_{n}-2(n+1)a_{n+1},

(46)

\displaystyle c_{n}=2(n+1)^{2}a_{n+1}+2(n+1)(n+2)a_{n+2},\quad d_{n}=-(n+1)a_{n+1}.

We now show that the Hamiltonian in Equation (
45
) does not lead to the spin Hall effect up to linear order in
g
. For this purpose, we evaluate
\langle x(t)\rangle
under the initial conditions
\langle x(0)\rangle=0
and
\frac{d}{dt}\langle x(t)\rangle|_{t=0}=\langle\psi(\bm{x},0)|i\left[\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}},x\right]|\psi(\bm{x},0)\rangle/\hbar=0
. The commutator
\left[\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}},x\right]
gives

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}},x\right]=mc^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\left(-2ni\hbar p_{1}\right)\left(a_{n}\frac{p^{2(n-1)}}{(mc)^{2n}}+b_{n}\frac{gz}{c^{2}}\frac{p^{2(n-1)}}{(mc)^{2n}}+c_{n}\frac{i\hbar gp_{3}}{m^{2}c^{4}}\frac{p^{2(n-1)}}{(mc)^{2n}}\right.

(47)

\displaystyle\left.+d_{n}\frac{g\hbar\bm{\sigma}\cdot\left(\hat{\bm{z}}\times\bm{p}+mc\hat{\bm{z}}\right)}{m^{2}c^{4}}\frac{p^{2(n-1)}}{(mc)^{2n}}\right)-mc^{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{n}\frac{ig\hbar^{2}\sigma_{y}}{m^{2}c^{4}}\frac{p^{2n}}{(mc)^{2n}}.

When taking more commutators with
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}}
, we note that the spin-dependent, non-commuting terms involve either
g\left[p_{3},gz\right]
or
g^{2}\left[\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}\right]
; they are all of order
O(g^{2})
or higher. Using Equation (
19
), we get

\langle x(t)\rangle_{\mathrm{spin-dependent}}\sim O(g^{2}),

(48)

as long as the wave function does not introduce extra
1/g
behavior. A similar argument leads to
\langle y(t)\rangle_{\mathrm{spin-dependent}}\sim O(g^{2})
, and
\left[\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}},z\right]
does not contain the spin. Therefore, all spin-dependent motion necessarily starts at order
O(g^{2})
or higher. We note that this result is consistent with the semiclassical analysis of the vector model of spin
Ramírez and Deriglazov 2017
.

In the above analysis, we show that there is no gravitational spin Hall effect in a uniform gravitational field up to linear order in
g
for the Hamiltonian in Equation (
45
). Before closing the discussion, we comment on the applicability of this conclusion in other representations. If one takes the first few terms in Equation (
45
), the result is

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW,LO}}=mc^{2}\left(1-\frac{gz}{c^{2}}\right)+\frac{p^{2}}{2m}-\frac{g\hbar\bm{\sigma}\cdot\left(\hat{\bm{z}}\times\bm{p}+mc\hat{\bm{z}}\right)}{2mc^{2}}.

(49)

Comparing with Equation (
14
), the form of the spin-dependent interaction is different. As noted in
Obukhov 2001
, this Hamiltonian is related to the one in Equation (
14
) by a unitary transformation, which changes not only the form of the Hamiltonian but also the position and spin operators. Indeed, it is a nontrivial task to interpret the meaning of these operators in a specific representation
Foldy and Wouthuysen 1950
;
Newton and Wigner 1949
;
Fradkin and Good Jr. 1961
;
Silenko and Teryaev 2005
. A more recent discussion of this issue as well as a potential framework for the analysis can be found in
Deriglazov and Guzmán Ramírez 2017
. However, our argument for the vanishing of the gravitational spin Hall effect to leading order in
g
essentially relies only on three pieces of information:

1.

The expectation value of commutator
\left[\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}},\bm{x}\right]
vanishes as a result of the requirement that the particle start from rest.

2.

In the
g\to 0
limit, the Hamiltonian and the wave function reduce to those for free particles.

3.

The spin-dependent interaction is independent of the position of the particle, which is true for the uniform gravitational field.

Items 2 and 3 together guarantee that the spin-dependent part in
\left[\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}},\left[\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{FW}},\bm{x}\right]\right]
(and higher-order commutators) starts at order
g^{2}
. Therefore, although we consider the Hamiltonian in
Obukhov 2001
, the same conclusion applies to a much wider class of representations, for example, the Hamiltonian in
Silenko and Teryaev 2005
, as well.

6
Summary

We revisited the proposed gravitational spin Hall effect in a

uniform
field and tracked both classical and quantum sources of
apparent transverse motion. Classically, the circulating mass model shows that the
hidden relativistic mechanical momentum

(Equations (
4
)–(
8
) and (
10
))
can be written as

{\bm{p}}_{\mathrm{mech}}={\bm{L}}\times{\bm{g}}/c^{2}

(Equation (
13
)). Quantum mechanically, starting from the FW
Hamiltonian in a linear potential, Equation (
14
), we noted that the
canonical transverse momentum is conserved,

[H_{\mathrm{FW}},p_{1}]=0

(Equation (
20
)),
while the velocity operator
contains a spin term,
m\,\dot{x}=Vp_{1}-\tfrac{g\hbar}{4c^{2}}\sigma_{2}

(Equation (
21
))
, implying
d\langle x\rangle/dt=\langle Vp_{1}/m-\tfrac{g\hbar}{4mc^{2}}\sigma_{2}\rangle
(Equation (
22
)). Therefore, a
packet with
\langle p(0)\rangle=0
is
not
at rest. Preparing
truly at-rest states requires a spin-dependent phase with
k_{\pm}=\pm g\hbar/4c^{2}
in the initial wave functions (Equations (
23
)–(
25
)), which enforces

\langle[H_{\mathrm{FW}},x]\rangle=0
at
t=0
(Equation (
26
)) and
eliminates any
O(g)
transverse drift. Generalizing, we used the
exact FW transformation (Equations (
27
)–(
33
)) and its specialization to

V=1-gz/c^{2}

(Equation (
38
))
to derive the all-orders-in-
1/c
,
linear-in-
g
Hamiltonian (Equation (
45
)). The commutator structure then
shows that spin-dependent transverse motion does not arise at orders
lower than
g^{2}
(Equations (
47
) and (
48
)). While the leading-order Hamiltonian

H_{\mathrm{FW,LO}}
(Equation (
49
)) is related to
Equation (
14
) by a unitary transformation,
the absence of a uniform-field gravitational SHE at
O(g)
is
representation-independent. Our result shows consistency with the expectation from the equivalence principle. The previously reported drift
\langle x(t)\rangle_{\pm}=\mp\hbar gt/(4mc^{2})
(Equation (
3
)) is traced to
inconsistent initial conditions and conflicting canonical and kinetic
momenta.
