Protocol for detecting protein recruitment to clathrin structures in cultured cells
Authors: Laura A. Orofiamma, Costin N. Antonescu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryClathrin-mediated endocytosis internalizes key cell-surface receptors and relies on coordinated recruitment of adaptors and accessory proteins to clathrin structures. Here, we present a protocol for quantifying protein recruitment to diffraction-limited clathrin structures at the plasma membrane. We describe steps for the inducible expression of tagged proteins, total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy, the analysis of proteins within clathrin structures, UltraID-based proximity-labeling, and western blotting detection of proteins in clathrin structures. This protocol is applicable to the detection of any protein within clathrin or related structures.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Orofiamma et al.1

Before you begin

Understanding the molecular mechanism and regulation of clathrin-mediated endocytosis relies in part on the detection and quantification of various key proteins to clathrin-coated pits at the plasma membrane. For additional context about the cell physiology of clathrin-mediated endocytosis and clathrin structures, please refer to Orofiamma et al.1

Accurate quantification of the recruitment of proteins within clathrin structures remains challenging due to the heterogeneity of clathrin structures. Different subsets of clathrin structures have distinct protein compositions and functions. In addition, protein recruitment can dynamically change across temporally distinct phases of the CME process.2,3,4 Together, this heterogeneity makes it important to resolve not only the mean recruitment of specific proteins across all clathrin structures, but also their recruitment to individual clathrin structures or subsets of clathrin structures identified by specific protein markers.1,5,6,7,8 Microscopy-based methods that rely on co-localization measurement strategies such as Manders’ or Pearson’s coefficients involve establishing correlation between fluorescence intensities of two channels across all pixels in an image, which has inherent limitations including those imposed by uneven background signal across an image that can convolute co-localization detection. These methods are also challenging to use to observe the co-localization of two proteins specifically within the context of clathrin structures. To address this gap, the protocol presented here combines TIRF microscopy with a custom Gaussian-based object detection algorithm that identifies individual diffraction-limited clathrin structures and measures protein recruitment at their precise locations.9 By restricting imaging to the plasma membrane, TIRF microscopy further minimizes cytosolic background and enables reliable determination of proteins that are present, enriched, or depleted at defined clathrin structures at the plasma membrane.10 The structures can be further characterized based on the presence or absence of up to two additional proteins detected at clathrin sites, allowing measurement of the recruitment of specific proteins to distinct subsets of clathrin structures at the plasma membrane.1,5,6,7,8

Because imaging-based readouts alone may not always be able to fully resolve protein proximity or interaction, this imaging workflow is complemented with a biochemical approach that leverages a highly efficient biotin ligase such as UltraID that is able to achieve biotinylation of proteins in the vicinity of clathrin-localized proteins in a timeframe roughly comparable to the lifetime of individual clathrin structures. Rapid proximity labeling (10 minutes) followed by streptavidin pulldown and immunoblot detection provides complementary evidence of the proximity and may confirm the recruitment of a novel protein to other established clathrin structure-associated proteins.

The protocol below describes the specific steps for detecting proteins within clathrin structures in ARPE-19 cells, as performed in Orofiamma et al.,1 where we examined how AMPK activation influences the recruitment of the Arf6 and Dab2 to plasma membrane clathrin structures. Although optimized for ARPE-19 cells, this workflow can be adapted for other adherent cell types to quantify protein abundance at clathrin structures or other plasma membrane localized structures.

Innovation

This protocol combines controlled inducible protein expression that approximates endogenous levels of protein expression by western blotting to limit artificial localization that can occur by overexpression of exogenous proteins, high-resolution TIRF microscopy that avoids interference from intracellular protein pools, and an image analysis pipeline to measure the recruitment of multiple proteins to clathrin-coated structures with improved precision compared to more commonly used colocalization analysis methods.

This workflow includes a custom MATLAB detection analysis pipeline that identifies clathrin-coated pits by fitting a Gaussian model that approximates the point spread function for each wavelength, developed by Aguet et al.9 By first using this approach to identify cell surface clathrin objects based on a fiduciary marker of clathrin structures such as clathrin or the adaptor AP2, this method allows for the determination of the exact position of individual clathrin structures. This can then be followed by the measurement of fluorescence intensities from additional channels at those same coordinates defined by the primary fiduciary channel. For all channels, intensity is taken as the amplitude of the Gaussian model that approximates the point spread function of each fluorescence channel at the position of the clathrin object. This is advantageous as it allows protein enrichment within each clathrin structure to be measured relative to the fluorescence intensity in the immediate surrounding region of that structure, rather than relying on a single background intensity value applied uniformly across all structures in an image, as described in Aguet et al.9 In practice, this measures the level of protein enrichment or depletion at the level of individual clathrin structures relative to localized background with high accuracy. Since determination of protein localization is done at the level of individual clathrin objects rather than globally across the entire image, this strategy allows analysis of individual clathrin structures or grouping of clathrin structures into subpopulations based on their protein composition. The resulting dataset includes clathrin structure counts, and object intensities of each fluorescence channel at clathrin structure detection sites.

To support the imaging results, the protocol also integrates proximity labeling using UltraID, the smallest and fastest biotin ligase currently available.11 UltraID, when fused to a protein of interest, allows for rapid (10 minute) biotin labeling of proteins within ∼10 nm of the biotin ligase domain. Although proximity biotinylation can favor proteins with longer residence times in the vicinity of the bait protein, the rapid labeling kinetics of UltraID improve the detection of more transient or dynamic interactions relative to earlier biotin ligases. While proximity biotinylating is often used in conjunction with mass spectrometry to discover new candidate interacting proteins, this approach can also be used with candidate-based approaches to test the localization of specific proteins in the vicinity of clathrin objects or clathrin-associated proteins. To this end, this protocol leverages proximity biotinylation of candidate clathrin structure interacting proteins, coupled to streptavidin pulldown and western blotting to independently monitor the proximity between proteins of interest and known clathrin-associated proteins (such as AP2).

Together, these tools create a combined imaging-biochemistry workflow that captures protein recruitment to clathrin structures.

Timing: 2 days

Expected outcomes

This protocol is designed to assess how proteins of interest associate with clathrin structures at the plasma membrane using two complementary approaches: high-resolution TIRF imaging paired with custom MATLAB-based detection, quantification, and sorting of clathrin structures by protein composition, and proximity-dependent labeling with UltraID.

With the optimized imaging conditions presented here, TIRF microscopy should produce clear, high-contrast images in which clathrin puncta (and other plasma membrane proteins) appear as distinct diffraction-limited spots across the ventral surface of the cell (Figure 1A). Examples of selection of image ROI (as in step #13) are shown in Figure 1B, and examples of sub-optimal and optimal TIRF illumination depth conditions are shown in Figure 1C. After processing these images through the MATLAB analysis pipeline described here, users can expect quantitative readouts of protein abundance within clathrin structures. This analysis workflow was implemented using conventional TIRF microscopy, which provides high temporal resolution and compatibility with live-cell imaging while minimizing phototoxicity and photobleaching. Although deconvolution and super-resolution approaches can provide improved spatial resolution and more detailed structural information about clathrin assemblies, these methods often require longer acquisition times, increased imaging complexity, specialized instrumentation, or higher illumination intensities that may limit throughput and live-cell compatibility. In contrast, the Gaussian-based detection approach used here enables robust and reproducible identification and quantification of diffraction-limited clathrin structures in both fixed and live-cell imaging datasets. Limitations

This protocol has some inherent limitations that may affect reproducibility or experimental sensitivity. TIRF microscopy, as with all forms of microscopy, is limited by the optical resolution of the microscope and the selection of representative ROIs. Differences in microscope calibration, laser intensity, or detector sensitivity may introduce variability. This MATLAB-based analysis is optimized for punctate clathrin structures and other cell surface-associated proteins that are punctate in structure within a specific range of intensities and sizes, and therefore may not be applicable to all plasma membrane structures.

The success of the UltraID pulldown depends strongly on the quality of the doxycycline-inducible stable cell lines generated, including the UltraID expression and activity. Additionally, variations in the biotin concentration, labeling time, and cell density can alter the biotin labeling efficiency.

As with all antibody-based techniques, there is variation in the specificity of antibody binding for a protein of interest, and it is best to use only validated antibodies for these experiments.

Finally, this protocol is optimized for fixed-cell imaging and proximity labeling coupled to streptavidin pulldown. As such, this is not directly transferrable to live-cell imaging or high-throughput screens without additional optimization.

Problem 2: Low signal-to-noise TIRF images (related to step 12)

The TIRF penetration depth may be too shallow to adequately excite fluorophores within clathrin puncta. This will result in images where clathrin objects are difficult to resolve, even using the computational image analysis method. This may also be caused by insufficient laser power or short exposure times that can further reduce the contrast between true signal and background fluorescence. Importantly, optimal microscope acquisition conditions are not fixed and may vary depending on multiple factors, including the specific microscope setup, laser configuration, detector sensitivity, and labeling efficiency of the fluorophore. Alternatively, the TIRF penetration depth may be too deep, leading to unwanted illumination of intracellular or cytosolic structures (see Figure 1C for examples). The latter may result in regions such as the nucleus being clearly visible as regions that are emitting lower intensity fluorescent signals (e.g., of clathrin), since deeper illumination of the cell will result in fluorescence emitted by intracellular clathrin (e.g., on the TGN). While deeper penetration depth of the TIRF illumination will appear to provide strong signal-to-noise, the signal derived specifically from plasma membrane clathrin structures will be convoluted by that from intracellular clathrin structures, which are distinct in terms of protein composition and function.

Potential solution

•Optimize the TIRF penetration depth. While this will be unique to individual microscopes, it may be optimal to start with a higher excitation incident angle that results in a shallower TIRF illumination. If signal-to-noise is low such that clathrin structures are not clearly evident, then the excitation incident angle can be reduced (while still being held above the critical incident angle required for TIRF) until clathrin structures show sufficient signal. While doing so, it is important to ensure that the distribution of clathrin puncta is approximately uniform across the surface of the cell; noting that a clear outline of the nucleus that is relatively lacking in clathrin fluorescent signals suggests that the TIRF penetration is too deep into the sample (see Figure 1C). Problem 3: MATLAB analysis errors (related to steps 13–19)

Errors during MATLAB processing often arise from missing or improperly linked analysis scripts, incorrect file paths, or incompatible image dimensions. Folder structures that contain spaces, special characters, or nested directories can prevent the pipeline from locating required files. Inconsistent ROI sizes across channels from the same cell or image can also cause the script to produce an error.

Potential solution

•Use the “Set Path” feature in MATLAB to ensure all analysis packages are correctly loaded and stored in an easily accessible location (e.g., Desktop or Documents), rather than buried within nested folders.•Confirm that the Level 1 root folder is also placed directly in an accessible directory. Also verify that no folder names across Levels 1-4 contain spaces or special characters.•Ensure that all images within each Level 4 folder corresponding to a single Level 3 ROI have identical ROI dimensions (pixel x pixel), as mismatched sizes will prevent successful analysis.

Problem 5: Low band resolution of biotinylated proteins (related to step 39)

Poor band resolution may result from insufficient expression of the UltraID fusion protein, leading to weak or diffuse biotinylation patterns. Additionally, high viscosity lysates that result from residual genomic DNA can impair protein separation during SDS-PAGE which produce smeared or poorly resolved bands.

Potential solution
