A novel peptide-compound conjugate alleviates endotoxin-induced inflammation via NF-κB/MAPK modulation
Authors: Youngsic Eom, Min Jeong Jeon, Jeong Hwa Lee, Soyoung Jang, Sang-Cheol Han, Mi-Young Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractThe anti-inflammatory ability of a novel peptide–compound conjugate, 2IP5MP-CW, synthesized by fusing 2-isopropyl-5-methylphenoxy acetic acid with cysteine and tryptophan, was investigated in this study. 2IP5MP-CW markedly inhibited the growth of Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus and exhibited potent antioxidative activity by suppressing reactive oxygen species production and mitigating oxidative DNA damage induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS). In RAW 264.7 macrophages, 2IP5MP-CW significantly reduced LPS-induced TNF-α production and suppressed nitric oxide (NO) generation as well as the expression of inflammation-related proteins iNOS and COX-2. The compound also attenuated the phosphorylation of ERK, JNK, NF-κB p65, IKKβ, and IκBα, thereby blocking the nuclear translocation of phosphorylated NF-κB p65. In an LPS-induced mouse model of pulmonary inflammation, 2IP5MP-CW treatment reduced the phosphorylation of ERK, JNK, NF-κB p65, IKKβ, and IκBα in LPS-challenged lung tissue. Furthermore, it markedly attenuated TNF-α production and downregulated iNOS and COX-2 expression. Histological examination further confirmed that 2IP5MP-CW alleviated inflammatory cell infiltration and tissue injury. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that 2IP5MP-CW exerts potent anti-bacterial, anti-oxidative, and anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing LPS-induced TNF-α and NF-κB/MAPK signaling, highlighting its therapeutic potential against LPS-mediated inflammatory lung injury.Key Messages A novel peptide–compound conjugate, 2IP5MP-CW, was designed and synthesized.2IP5MP-CW exhibited in vitro and in vivo anti-bacterial activity against E. coli and S. aureus.2IP5MP-CW showed anti-oxidative activity by suppressing ROS production.2IP5MP-CW demonstrated anti-inflammatory activity in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells.2IP5MP-CW exhibited anti-inflammatory efficacy in a pulmonary inflammation mouse modelSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00109-025-02608-y.Keywords: 2-Isopropyl-5 Methylphenoxyacetyl cysteine and tryptophan (2IP5MP CW), Inflammation, Anti Bacterial activity, Anti Oxidative activity, Anti Inflammatory activity

Introduction

Inflammatory responses can be initiated by various injury agents, including lipopolysaccharides (LPS) [1, 2]. LPS, the outermost part of cell envelope of Gram-negative bacteria, binds to the receptor complex of Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/myeloid differentiation factor-2 (MD-2), driving the activation and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines within the framework of the innate immune response against bacterial infection [3–5]. Inflammation is controlled at the molecular level by major signaling pathways, such as nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) pathway and the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, along with Janus kinase (JAK)-signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) pathway and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [6], vital in both initiation and modulation of inflammatory processes. The MAPK pathway is a vital signal transduction pathway that regulates the cellular responses to various stimuli, comprising three primary kinase cascades: ERK, JNK, and p38 MAPK. Activated by extracellular stimuli, such as the oxygen radical-induced oxidative stress, these cascades play a significant physiological role in cell division, survival, differentiation, apoptosis, and inflammation [7–9]. The NF-κB pathway is another main regulator of the immune response to infection, triggered by a range of stimuli, including bacterial or viral antigens, inflammatory cytokines, and stress signals. Once activated, NF-κB moves into the nucleus, and interacts with the promoter section of target DNA, modulating the expression of numerous genes associated with inflammatory response, immune reaction, cell growth, and programmed cell death. Its key role in the innate immunity and the control of pro-inflammatory cytokine generation designates the NF-κB pathway as an important target for developing anti-inflammatory drugs [10–12].

To date, various classes of anti-inflammatory agents—including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), biopharmaceuticals, natural products, and peptide-based molecules—have been developed. However, despite these advances, highly effective agents with minimal side effects are still needed. Conventional NSAIDs primarily exert transient effects by inhibiting the cyclooxygenase (COX) pathway [13], but long-term use is associated with gastrointestinal toxicity [14]. Biologics, although effective in targeting specific inflammatory cytokines, are limited by high cost and increased infection risk due to immunosuppression [15].

With recent progress in synthetic and molecular design strategies, peptides have emerged as promising therapeutic leads because of their high specificity, biocompatibility, and tunable structures [16]. In particular, peptide–small molecule conjugates have been developed to combine the advantages of both components—improving efficacy, safety, and pharmacological stability—thus representing the next generation of peptide therapeutics [17, 18]. These conjugates have demonstrated enhanced anti-inflammatory activity compared with either peptides or small molecules alone. Therefore, the development of multifunctional conjugates with potent and broad anti-inflammatory effects while minimizing side effects remains an important goal.

In this study, 2-isopropyl-5-methylphenol (IPMP), a natural monoterpenoid found in Thymus vulgaris, was conjugated to cysteine (Cys) and tryptophan (Trp) dipeptides. IPMP possesses antimicrobial properties. Cys and Trp, which are potent antioxidants, were attached to IPMP to enhance its pharmacological activity against bacterial inflammation with improved efficacy [19, 20].

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) act as key mediators of tissue damage during acute bacterial infections and inflammation. Excessive ROS production not only promotes bacterial proliferation but also amplifies inflammatory signaling. Therefore, targeting ROS generation and its downstream effects represents a promising strategy for controlling inflammation associated with bacterial infection.

In this study, a novel multifunctional peptide–compound conjugate, 2IP5MP-CW, was synthesized and shown to possess anti-bacterial, anti-oxidative, and anti-inflammatory activities. Unlike many conventional anti-inflammatory agents, which primarily target COX enzymes or glucocorticoid receptors, 2IP5MP-CW simultaneously modulates both the NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways, providing a broader mechanism of action against inflammatory responses. This dual-action mechanism enables more comprehensive suppression of inflammation compared to single-pathway therapeutics and suggests that 2IP5MP-CW may overcome some limitations of existing treatments. We evaluated the therapeutic effects and mechanistic basis of 2IP5MP-CW in vitro and in vivo, offering a novel approach for managing LPS-induced lung inflammation and potentially other inflammatory conditions.

Materials and methods

2IP5MP-CW

The preparation of 2-isopropyl-5-methylphenoxyacetyl cysteine and tryptophan (2IP5MP-CW) was accomplished via solid-phase peptide synthesis technique. For peptide synthesis, Fmoc-Trp(Boc)-OH was introduced into the 2-CTC (2-chlorotrityl) resin. Following deprotecting the Fmoc group using 20% piperidine in DMF, subsequent reactions were performed sequentially with Fmoc-Cys(Trt)-OH and thymoxy acetic acid to form a peptide with protecting group. The protecting group was removed using 5% H2O in trifluoroacetic acid solution to obtain the desired peptide. The resulting peptide was purified using preparative liquid chromatography (Prep LC). The purified peptide was subsequently concentrated under reduced pressure, followed by freeze-drying was performed to acquire the final solid product. Supplementary synthesis methods and NMR data for 2IP5MP-OH (C-NMR) and 2IP5MP-OH (H-NMR) are available online.

Cell culture

The RAW 264.7 cells, originating from murine macrophages, were provided by the Korean Cell Line Bank (Seoul, Korea). RAW 264.7 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium enriched with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum and 100 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin. The cell cultures were sustained at 37 °C in a humidified setting with 5% CO2 and 95% air [21, 22].

Bacterial culture and anti-bacterial activity determination

Escherichia coli (Gram-negative) and Staphylococcus aureus (Gram-positive) were cultured in Luria–Bertani (LB) broth at 37 °C under aerobic conditions [23]. For in vitro assays, bacterial suspensions were adjusted to 1.5 × 105 CFU/mL (McFarland standard) and treated with various concentrations of 2IP5MP-CW for 18 h. Bacterial growth was determined spectrophotometrically at 600 nm.

In vivo antibacterial efficacy was assessed using a mouse lung infection model (n = 5 per group). Mice were pretreated with 2IP5MP-CW (20 mg/kg) or ampicillin (AMP, 20 mg/kg) before intraperitoneal infection with E. coli or S. aureus (5 × 10⁸ CFU/kg). Six hours post-infection, lungs were collected, homogenized [24, 25], and plated on LB agar for enumeration of colony-forming units (CFU). Bacterial burden was expressed as CFU per lung. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (SCH25-0062).

Oxygen radical absorbance capacity (ORAC) assay

The antioxidant capacity of 2IP5MP-CW was determined using the ORAC method. Samples were mixed with fluorescein and 2,2′-azobis(2-amidinopropane) dihydrochloride (AAPH) in a microplate, and fluorescence decay was recorded every 2 min for 1 h at 37 °C. Trolox served as a standard reference antioxidant. The antioxidant capacity was expressed as micromoles of Trolox equivalents (μM TE/L) based on the area under the fluorescence decay curve (AUC) relative to the Trolox calibration curve [26].

Comet assay for DNA damage assessment

The RAW 264.7 cells were integrated into low-melting-point agarose on microscope slides and lysed to form nucleoids containing supercoiled DNA. Following lysis, the slides were positioned in an electrophoresis unit, and the DNA was permitted to unwind in an alkaline buffer. Electrophoresis was performed to separate damaged DNA from undamaged cells, leading to a comet-like appearance under a fluorescence microscope. The severity of DNA damage was evaluated through assessing the tail length and percentage of DNA contained within the tail. The olive tail moment, tail DNA percentage, and tail length, combining the amount of DNA in the tail with its migration distance, were calculated to evaluate the extent of DNA damage more comprehensively [27].

Measurement of cellular ROS

RAW 264.7 cells (5 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in 12-well black plates and incubated for 24 h. Cells were then co-treated with LPS (1 μg/mL) and 2IP5MP-CW (25–50 μM) for 18 h, followed by staining with 20 μM 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFDA) for 45 min. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a plate reader (Synergy HTX; BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA) at excitation/emission wavelengths of 560/630 nm. Cellular ROS levels were expressed relative to the control group. Representative fluorescence images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope (IX73; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) [28].

Nitric oxide (NO) assay

NO production in RAW 264.7 macrophages was measured using the Griess reaction. Cells were stimulated with LPS (1 μg/mL) and treated with 2IP5MP-CW (25–50 μM) for 18 h at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. Supernatants (100 μL) were mixed with Griess reagent (0.2% naphthyl-ethylenediamine dihydrochloride and 0.2% sulfanilamide in 10% H₃PO₄), and absorbance was measured at 570 nm to quantify nitrite levels. The assay evaluated the inhibitory effect of 2IP5MP-CW on LPS-induced NO synthesis [29].

Immunofluorescence staining

RAW 264.7 cells were seeded on poly-L-lysine–coated coverslips and incubated for 24 h. Cells were then treated with 2IP5MP-CW in medium containing 1% penicillin–streptomycin for 6 h, while control cells were exposed to LPS alone. After treatment, cells were fixed with 4% formalin and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS. Non-specific binding was blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin for 1 h. Cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-phospho-NF-κB p65 primary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) and subsequently with goat anti-rabbit IgG Texas Red conjugate (1:500, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA) for 3 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Invitrogen, USA), and images were captured using a confocal microscope (LSM 710; Carl Zeiss, Germany) [30].

TNF-α determination

We quantified TNF-α levels in both cell culture supernatants and mouse serum using commercially available ELISA kits (Mouse TNF-alpha DuoSet ELISA, R&D Systems, USA). For in vitro analysis, RAW 264.7 macrophages were stimulated with LPS (1 µg/mL), and the culture medium was collected for TNF-α measurement following the kit protocol [31]. For in vivo TNF-α assessment, blood samples were obtained from mice (n = 5 per group) 2 h after LPS injection, and serum TNF-α concentrations were determined using the mouse TNF-α ELISA kit in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines [32]. All samples and standards were analyzed in triplicate to ensure assay reliability.

Western blotting

RAW 264.7 macrophages were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator for 24 h. The cells were treated with 50 µM 2IP5MP-CW or 20 µM TPCA-1 and 1 µg/mL LPS for 6 h [33].

Proteins separated by 10% SDS-PAGE were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using standard wet-transfer conditions. Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA for 2 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against phosphorylated and non-phosphorylated forms of JNK, ERK1/2, IKKβ, IκBα, and NF-κB p65 (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). After washing, membranes were incubated for 1 h with HRP-conjugated mouse or rabbit secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology) and visualized using an ECL detection reagent (Westar Supernova, Cyanagen, Bologna, Italy) with a Chemidoc imaging system (Lugen Sci Co., Ltd., Korea). Signals were detected and quantified to determine protein expression levels [34].

Inflammatory lung injury model and analysis

LPS-induced pulmonary inflammation was evaluated in BALB/c mice following the modified method of Mo et al. [35]. Mice received an intraperitoneal injection of LPS (10 mg/kg), and 2IP5MP-CW was administered intraperitoneally at 10 or 20 mg/kg 1 h before LPS challenge. Six hours after LPS injection, animals were sacrificed and lung tissues were collected for western blot and histological (H&E) analyses to assess inflammation-related protein expression and tissue injury. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (SCH24-0039).

Histological analysis

The specimens were immersed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution overnight at 4 °C before undergoing dehydration using an ascending series of ethanol and xylene. The specimens were embedded in paraffin using standard paraffin embedding methods. Tissue sections were cut to a thickness of 2–3 mm, placed in individual cassettes, and processed for 13 h using an STP120 Spin Tissue Processor. The sections were then sliced to a thickness of approximately 3–4 µm using a Finesse ME Microtome (Thermo Shandon, Pittsburgh, PA, USA) and mounted onto slides. After deparaffinization and rehydration, H&E staining was carried out on the sections to investigate the morphological changes [36].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 20) (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The values for each group are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), paired with Scheffe’s post hoc test were utilized to compare the effects by various treatments of the materials. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

In vitro and in vivo anti-bacterial activity of 2IP5MP-CW

The antibacterial effects of 2IP5MP-CW were evaluated against Escherichia coli (Gram-negative) and Staphylococcus aureus (Gram-positive), two representative bacterial species. As shown in Fig. 2a, 2IP5MP-CW inhibited bacterial growth in a dose-dependent manner, with the highest inhibition observed at 500 µM—approximately 57% for E. coli and 60% for S. aureus.

In vivo antibacterial activity was further assessed using mouse lung infection models. As shown in Fig. 2b, no colonies were detected in the lung homogenates of uninfected control animals. In contrast, E. coli- and S. aureus-infected mice exhibited average bacterial loads of 1471 CFU/lung and 1786 CFU/lung, respectively. Treatment with 2IP5MP-CW (20 mg/kg) markedly reduced bacterial counts to 755 CFU/lung (E. coli, 49% inhibition) and 250 CFU/lung (S. aureus, 86% inhibition). For comparison, ampicillin (20 mg/kg) decreased bacterial loads to 565 CFU/lung (E. coli, 69%) and 192 CFU/lung (S. aureus, 90%). Although slightly less potent than ampicillin, 2IP5MP-CW demonstrated marked in vivo antibacterial activity, particularly against Gram-positive bacteria.

Anti-oxidative activity of 2IP5MP-CW

The antioxidative activity of 2IP5MP-CW was evaluated using an ORAC assay, which quantifies oxygen radical scavenging in μM Trolox equivalents per liter (μM TE/L). As shown in Fig. 2c, the compound demonstrated dose-dependent antiradical activity. At the highest concentration of 1250 μM, the compound achieved an ORAC value of 101 μM TE/L, which progressively decreased with lower concentrations, reaching 9 μM TE/L at 39 μM. The intracellular ROS levels were measured using DCF-DA to confirm the antioxidant efficacy of 2IP5MP-CW (Fig. 2d). These results indicated that the compound effectively mitigated in vitro LPS-induced ROS production. LPS treatment also significantly increased the ROS levels, which were notably reduced by 2IP5MP-CW application as shown in the fluorescent image (Fig. 2e). In addition, the results of the comet assay demonstrate the protective effects of 2IP5MP-CW against oxidative DNA damage (Fig. 2f). This assay assessed DNA fragmentation using tail DNA content, olive tail moment, and tail length as indicators. The cells were subjected to oxidative stress induced by 200 μg/mL LPS. However, treatment with 2IP5MP-CW at concentrations of 25 μM and 50 μM significantly reduced all parameters of DNA damage, indicating a strong protective effect against LPS-induced oxidative DNA damage.

In vitro anti-inflammatory activity of 2IP5MP-CW

The inhibitory effects of 2IP5MP-CW on NO production were evaluated using Griess reagent. The findings indicated that LPS treatment significantly increased NO production by approximately 5.6-fold compared with that in the control. However, 2IP5MP-CW at varying concentrations of 25, 37.5, and 50 μM induced a concentration-dependent reduction in NO production (Fig. 3a). These results suggest that 2IP5MP-CW exhibits anti-inflammatory properties by effectively suppressing NO production in RAW 264.7 cells exposed to the pro-inflammatory agent LPS. In particular, 2IP5MP-CW at 25 and 37.5 μM reduced NO production by approximately 37% and 67%, respectively, in contrast to that in the LPS-stimulated cells. Western blot analysis and the corresponding densitometry quantification demonstrated that 2IP5MP-CW led to a dose-dependent reduction of iNOS and COX-2 protein expression (Fig. 3b). Treatment with 2IP5MP-CW at concentrations of 25, 37.5 and 50 µM significantly lowered the levels of these enzymes in comparison to those in the LPS-induced control group, suggesting the compound’s prominent anti-inflammatory effects. iNOS and COX-2 are critical players in mediating inflammation, with iNOS catalyzing the production of NO, a key inflammatory mediator, and COX-2 contributing to the synthesis of proinflammatory prostaglandins [37, 38].

Inhibition of NF-κB signaling protein expression by 2IP5MP-CW

2IP5MP-CW suppressed the phosphorylation of IKKβ, NF-κB p65, and IκBα in RAW 264.7 cells activated by LPS (Fig. 4a). These inhibitory effects were concentration-dependent, demonstrating its ability to disrupt the activation of the NF-κB pathway, a key regulator of inflammation.

To verify that this inhibition occurs via the IKKβ–NF-κB axis, TPCA-1, a selective IKKβ inhibitor, was used as a positive control. As expected, TPCA-1 markedly decreased the phosphorylation of IKKβ, IκBα, and NF-κB p65, which had been elevated by LPS stimulation. Notably, 2IP5MP-CW exhibited a similar inhibitory pattern to TPCA-1, suggesting that it may act through a comparable mechanism involving suppression of the IKKβ-mediated NF-κB signaling cascade. The results obtained from TPCA-1 treatment are presented in the Supplementary Data (Supplementary Fig. 2).

To further explore its role on nuclear translocation of NF-κB, RAW 264.7 cells underwent LPS treatment to elicit inflammation, after which 2IP5MP-CW was administered. Confocal microscopy showed that phosphorylated NF-κB (p-NF-κB) was relocated to the nucleus in the group treated solely with LPS. On the other hand, nuclear translocation was noticeably diminished in cells treated with 2IP5MP-CW (Fig. 4b).

Inhibition of the expression of MAPK signaling protein by 2IP5MP-CW

As shown in Fig. 5, 2 IP5MP-CW lessened the LPS-induced activation of the MAPK signaling pathway, as evidenced by lower levels of ERK1/2 and JNK phosphorylation. This modulation of the MAPK pathway indicates that 2IP5MP-CW contributes to inflammation control at the molecular signaling level [39, 40]. The compound demonstrated prominent attenuation in these proteins, indicating that its anti-inflammatory effects could be, in part, driven by the reduction of the MAPK signaling pathway.

In vivo anti-inflammatory activity of 2IP5MP-CW in mouse lung tissue

Following LPS induction, 2IP5MP-CW led to a notable decrease in LPS-triggered iNOS and COX-2 expression in the mouse lung tissues. The administration of 10 and 20 mg/kg 2IP5MP-CW led to a significant reduction in both iNOS and COX-2 levels, highlighting the anti-inflammatory potential of this molecule in vivo (Fig. 6a). Additionally, the anti-inflammatory effects of 2IP5MP-CW in mouse lung tissue were further supported by a decline in the phosphorylation levels of IKKβ, NF-κB p65, and IκBα expression challenged by LPS (Fig. 6b). Treatment with 2IP5MP-CW also caused a marked reduction in the phosphorylation of ERK1/2 and JNK in lung tissues, indicating its efficacy in mitigating MAPK-mediated inflammatory responses in vivo (Fig. 6c), further emphasizing its prospects as an in vivo anti-inflammatory agent.

TNF-α determination in vitro and in vivo

The inhibitory effects of 2IP5MP-CW and TPCA-1 on TNF-α levels in LPS-induced inflammation were first compared in RAW 264.7 macrophages. LPS stimulation (1 µg/mL) markedly increased TNF-α production, whereas 2IP5MP-CW treatment reduced TNF-α levels in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 7a). Specifically, 25, 37.5, and 50 µM 2IP5MP-CW decreased TNF-α secretion by 12.1%, 23.9%, and 53.8%, respectively, relative to the LPS group. The IKKβ inhibitor TPCA-1 further suppressed TNF-α by 71.1%, confirming the involvement of the IKKβ–NF-κB axis. Notably, the inhibitory trend observed with 2IP5MP-CW was similar to that of TPCA-1, suggesting that 2IP5MP-CW may exert its anti-inflammatory effects through a comparable mechanism involving blockade of the IKKβ-mediated NF-κB signaling pathway.

The in vivo anti-inflammatory activity of 2IP5MP-CW was next evaluated by measuring serum TNF-α levels 2 h after LPS injection (Fig. 7b). Administration of 2IP5MP-CW (10 or 20 mg/kg) reduced serum TNF-α by 20.4% and 30.1%, respectively, whereas dexamethasone (DEX, 5 mg/kg) decreased it by 43.9%. Although less potent than DEX, 2IP5MP-CW still exhibited a clear and dose-dependent suppression of TNF-α production, supporting its systemic anti-inflammatory activity through inhibition of the IKKβ–NF-κB pathway.

Histological analysis of LPS-stimulated mouse lung tissue after 2IP5MP-CW treatment

Histological analysis of the lung tissues from LPS-challenged mice treated with 2IP5MP-CW revealed reduced alveolar hemorrhage, edema, bronchial wall thickening, and leukocyte infiltration compared with those of the untreated LPS group (Fig. 8). These results provide morphological evidence supporting the anti-inflammatory efficacy of 2IP5MP-CW in an in vivo model of acute lung inflammation [41, 42]. Overall, 2IP5MP-CW could play a therapeutic role in addressing LPS-induced inflammatory lung injury through its combined antibacterial, antioxidative, and anti-inflammatory properties.

Discussion

In this study, an IPMP-conjugated cysteine–tryptophan compound, designated as 2IP5MP-CW, was synthesized for the first time and demonstrated a broad spectrum of anti-bacterial, anti-oxidative, and anti-inflammatory activities. This multifunctional profile highlights its potential as a novel therapeutic scaffold for inflammatory disorders associated with bacterial infection. The compound displayed dose-dependent anti-oxidative capacity in multiple assays (ORAC, fluorescence, and comet), underscoring its ability to mitigate oxidative stress—a pivotal upstream event in inflammation-mediated tissue injury.

In macrophage-based models, 2IP5MP-CW markedly inhibited LPS-induced nitric oxide production and attenuated the phosphorylation of key regulatory proteins in the NF-κB and MAPK pathways, including IKKβ, IκBα, and NF-κB p65. These findings indicate that 2IP5MP-CW suppresses inflammatory signaling at the transcriptional level, thereby preventing the downstream expression of pro-inflammatory mediators.

2IP5MP-CW markedly inhibited LPS-induced NO production, and its anti-inflammatory activity was significantly enhanced by conjugation of CW to 2IP5MP. At 25 μM, 2IP5MP-CW showed a substantially stronger inhibitory effect on NO production than 2IP5MP alone, whereas 30 μM 2IP5MP exhibited minimal activity in a previous report [43]. These findings suggest that conjugation with CW confers synergistic efficacy within the hybrid scaffold, thereby amplifying the anti-inflammatory potency of 2IP5MP [44].

Mechanistically, 2IP5MP-CW modulates key inflammatory signaling cascades activated by LPS. In both RAW 264.7 macrophages and mouse lung tissues, 2IP5MP-CW reduced the phosphorylation of IKKβ, IκBα, and NF-κB p65, thereby preventing NF-κB activation and nuclear translocation [45, 46]. In parallel, the compound also attenuated MAPK-associated inflammatory signaling, further contributing to the suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokine production.

Because both NF-κB and MAPK pathways are major upstream regulators of TNF-α transcription and secretion, their concurrent inhibition by 2IP5MP-CW provides a mechanistic basis for the observed reduction in TNF-α levels. These findings indicate that the broad anti-inflammatory activity of 2IP5MP-CW arises from simultaneous modulation of the NF-κB and MAPK pathways, which also underlies its antioxidant and tissue-protective effects through mitigation of ROS-induced oxidative stress.

Beyond its cellular effects, the compound also demonstrated notable in vivo anti-bacterial efficacy. In mouse infection models, treatment with 2IP5MP-CW (20 mg/kg) significantly reduced bacterial loads following intraperitoneal challenge with either Escherichia coli or Staphylococcus aureus. The inhibitory effects reached 49% and 86% for the respective bacterial strains, demonstrating substantial anti-bacterial activity in vivo. Although the efficacy was somewhat lower than that of the antibiotic control (ampicillin, 20 mg/kg), 2IP5MP-CW exhibited consistent anti-bacterial effects against both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria. These findings, combined with the previously validated in vitro antibacterial data, demonstrate that 2IP5MP-CW exerts potent anti-inflammatory and anti-bacterial effects—two key components of its broader triple-action profile.

Histological evaluation of LPS-challenged mouse lungs further confirmed that 2IP5MP-CW attenuated inflammatory cell infiltration and preserved alveolar structure. These findings suggest that the combined modulation of oxidative, inflammatory, and bacterial stress contributes to the overall tissue-protective outcome.

Although the LPS model provides a convenient means of evaluating acute inflammatory responses, it represents a simplified single-stimulus system derived from Gram-negative bacterial components [47]. Consequently, it may not fully replicate the complex, polymicrobial interactions observed in clinical sepsis or mixed-pathogen infections. To overcome this limitation, future studies will employ co-stimulation with LPS and peptidoglycan, as well as dual-infection models using both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, to further validate the generalizability of 2IP5MP-CW under more physiologically relevant conditions.

From a translational standpoint, this integrated mechanism, encompassing anti-bacterial, anti-oxidative, and anti-inflammatory activities, highlights the clinical potential of 2IP5MP-CW as a multifunctional therapeutic candidate. In sepsis or acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), treatment typically requires a combination of antibiotics, corticosteroids, and antioxidants to simultaneously control infection, inflammation, and oxidative injury [48, 49]. Such polypharmacy inevitably increases the risk of drug–drug interactions and cumulative toxicity. By contrast, the multi-target activity of 2IP5MP-CW may reduce therapeutic complexity and enhance safety, offering a more integrated strategy for managing systemic inflammation.

To contextualize its therapeutic relevance, the efficacy of 2IP5MP-CW was compared with that of dexamethasone (DEX), a representative corticosteroid used clinically for inflammation control. Although 2IP5MP-CW did not reach the same magnitude of TNF-α suppression observed with DEX, it exhibited a clear dose-dependent anti-inflammatory response and maintained excellent tolerability in vivo. These findings suggest that, rather than competing with steroids in potency, 2IP5MP-CW may serve as a beneficial and mechanistically distinct anti-inflammatory candidate, offering reduced toxicity and broader redox and anti-bacterial coverage.

In summary, 2IP5MP-CW integrates multi-modal anti-bacterial, anti-oxidative, and anti-inflammatory properties within a single chemical framework. Through concurrent modulation of NF-κB/MAPK signaling and suppression of bacterial burden, it exerts protective effects against inflammation-induced tissue injury. While further validation in clinically relevant infection or systemic inflammation models is warranted, these findings highlight the potential of 2IP5MP-CW as a promising multi-target therapeutic scaffold for infection-associated inflammatory conditions.
