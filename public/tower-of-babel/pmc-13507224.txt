Modulation of grape must vitamin content by oenological treatments affects Saccharomyces cerevisiae fermentation performance and wine volatile composition
Authors: Fabien Garces, Jonathan Picart, Matteo Bosaro, Emilie Riviere, Christophe Morge, Hervé Alexandre, Chloé Roullier-Gall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study explores the impact of oenological treatments with fining agents or yeast-based nutrients on the vitamin content of grape musts, and thus on the fermentative performance of Saccharomyces cerevisiae yeasts and the volatile composition of wine. Products such as bentonites and oenological carbons were shown to deplete several B-group vitamins from the must, while yeast derivatives were able to release significant amounts. Bentonite was used to modulate the vitamin B1 (thiamine) content of a real must and different yeast inoculation procedures were evaluated: active dry yeast and cells precultivated in order to modulate their physiological state and intracellular B1 pool. A significant impact of initial thiamine on fermentative activity was observed, while the effect of inoculation seemed to be linked to the adaptation of yeasts to liquid medium rather than intracellular B1. Thiamine availability was linked to an overall increase in volatile compound production. B1 addition to a bentonite treated must restored the volatile profile of the untreated control, suggesting that thiamine depletion is a major factor underlying the effects of bentonite treatment on wine volatile composition. These results allowed to identify the mechanisms by which fining agents can modify wine characteristics while confirming thiamine’s importance in vinification processes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56764-3.

Introduction

Wine is a complex matrix resulting from the alcoholic fermentation of grape must by Saccharomyces cerevisiae yeasts. The conversion of sugars into ethanol and CO2 is the main effect of alcoholic fermentation, but a broad range of other compounds are synthesized by yeasts as byproducts of its metabolism, and some of those secondary metabolites play a role in the aromatic and sensory characteristics of the final product1. Low nutrient availability during the course of alcoholic fermentation may affect S. cerevisiae and lead to sluggish or stuck fermentation2, and impact the volatile profile of wine3. Over the last few decades, yeast nitrogen nutrition in ammonium and amino acids forms (referred as Yeast Assimilable Nitrogen, YAN) has been studied in depth, and shown to strongly impact the course of alcoholic fermentation by impacting fermentation kinetics, cell growth, and volatile compound production4. More recently, other nutrients such as lipids and vitamins have stirred greater interest. Lipids play a role in cell membrane fluidity, and therefore in yeast stress resistance capabilities, and their availability in the fermentation medium can impact the formation of volatile metabolites5, especially for ethyl esters which are fatty acid derivatives6. Regarding vitamins, in particular the hydrosoluble compounds of the B-group vitamins, their importance relies on their role as cofactors or their precursors for numerous enzymatic activities in yeast metabolism7. Saccharomyces cerevisiae is able to perform de novo biosynthesis of most B vitamins, with the exception of biotin for which prototrophy seems limited to specific strains8. However, de novo biosynthesis can be an energy intensive process for yeasts, and uptake from the extracellular environment is generally preferred, with efficient transport systems being expressed by yeasts to achieve the harvest of vitamin compounds7. In an oenological context, vitamin availability is indeed deemed essential in the case of several compounds, such as vitamin B1 (thiamine) for fermentative activity, B7 (biotin) for cell growth and B5 (pantothenic acid) in both processes9. The implication of the availability of vitamins B1 and B7 in volatile aromatic compound production was also demonstrated in synthetic must10. The vitamin content of grape musts is therefore an important parameter to take into account in oenology. The initial vitamin content of grape musts can be influenced by several factors such as geographical origin, cultivar or vintage11, or by ripeness levels12. However, musts may be treated before the start of alcoholic fermentation for clarification, colour adjustment, and chemical or biological stabilization. Such treatments were shown to impair the normal course of wine fermentation when used without consideration, because of nutrient depletion13. On the other hand, yeast derived products may also be used to ensure successful fermentation by releasing nutrients in the medium14. Assessing the impact of those oenological treatments on vitamin availability is therefore of utmost importance for efficacious use in the industry. Most previous studies on this subject in the oenological context were performed in already fermented wine, until a recent study15 which, for the first, demonstrated time an impact of 6 fining agents and yeast derivatives on several vitamin compounds in a grape must matrix. The present work aims to provide a precise quantification of the effect of a wider collection of 26 fining agents and yeast derivatives on the vitamin content of grape musts, in order to assess their potential in triggering or compensating vitamin deficiencies. It also explores the mechanisms at work in the treatments’ impacts on must fermentability and final wine volatile composition.

Materials and methods

Grape musts, oenological products and yeast strain

Grenache noir rosé must and Grenache blanc white must from the Languedoc region of France, harvested in 2024, were used in this study. They were frozen and stored at − 20 °C before utilization. The oenological characteristics of the musts used are specified in Supplementary Table 1. The 26 oenological additives used (Supplementary Table 2), included both commercial products and raw materials from different categories, such as fining agents (oenological carbons, bentonites, PVPP, pea proteins) or yeast-derivatives. One S. cerevisiae strain was selected for this study among several available commercial yeast strains in active dry yeast form (ADY). This strain was chosen for its apparent higher sensitivity to B1 depletion in a synthetic medium as stated in Evers et al.9, where it is referred as strain B. Musts, products and strains were all supplied by Sofralab SAS (Magenta, France).

Assays of oenological products

All the assays were carried out in 50 mL tubes containing 50 mL of rosé grape must, stored in a cold chamber set at 4 °C for 48 h. The 26 available products were tested at the maximum recommended or authorized doses according to the producers’ instructions, or recommendations from the OIV (International Organisation of Vine and Wine) or IFV (Institut Français de la Vigne et du Vin) depending on the product category (Supplementary Table 2). They were prepared and added according to the producers’ recommendations. An untreated control was included in the same conditions. All the assays were triplicated. After 48 h, the samples were centrifuged at 6800g for 10 min and supernatants were frozen at − 20 °C until analysed.

LC–MS/MS analysis of 5 B-group vitamins

The analysis of vitamins B1 (thiamine), B2 (riboflavin), B3 (2 vitamers: nicotinic acid and nicotinamide), B5 (pantothenic acid), and B6 (2 vitamers: pyridoxine and pyridoxal) was performed at Italiana Biotecnologie (Montebello Vicentino, Veneto region, Italy). It was performed on an LC–MS/MS system consisting of a Waters Acquity UPLC H-Class Plus LC system and a Waters Xevo TQS-Micro mass spectrometer. Grape must samples were diluted in solvent A, with the addition of 22 µM Methotrexate as an internal standard. Separation was performed on an Acquity UPLC HSS T3 1.8 µm, 2.1 × 100 mm column, at 40 °C. The following gradient elution was used: 1% solvent B in solvent A from 0 to 0.5 min; from 1 to 5% solvent B in solvent A from 0.5 to 1.5 min; from 5 to 55% solvent B in solvent A from 1.5 to 3 min; from 55 to 1% solvent B in solvent A from 3 to 3.1 min and 1% solvent B in solvent A until 4 min (solvent A: 10 µM ammonium formate + 0.1% formic acid in H2O; solvent B: 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). The injection volume was set at 2 µL and the flow rate at 0.6 mL/min. Detection and quantification were performed by positive Electrospray Ionization (ESI+) followed by acquisition in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. Quantification was based on peak area and standard curves.

RP-HPLC-Fluo analysis of 3 vitamin B1 vitamers

The analysis of 3 vitamin B1 vitamers (thiamine and its phosphate esters) was performed on an Agilent 1260 Infinity II LC system (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, United States of America). It consists of a quaternary pump (Reference Agilent G7111B), a Diode Array Detector (G7115A), an autosampler (G7129A), and a fluorescence detector (G7121B). Data were collected and analysed using OpenLab CDS software (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Stock standard solutions for each vitamer were prepared in 25 mL ultrapure water. Individual vitamer standards were prepared as 1/100 dilutions of the stock solutions. A series of ten standards containing all vitamers in different concentrations expected in grape musts were prepared from the same stock solutions and used as external calibration solutions. All the injected samples and standards underwent precolumn derivatization using the thiochrome method described by Bontemps et al.16 and Liddicoat et al.17. Briefly, the samples were derivatized in alkaline conditions by a potassium ferricyanide solution (1% w/v in 15% w/v NaOH), to oxidize thiamine and its esters into highly fluorescent thiochromes. This process was automated using the sample preparation mode on the LC system. Chromatographic separation was performed on a Zorbax SB-Aq 4.6 × 250 mm, 5 µm column (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA), protected by a Zorbax SB-Aq 4.6 × 12.5 mm, 5 µm guard column (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA), at 25 °C. The following gradient elution was used: 20% solvent B in solvent A from 0 to 1 min; from 20 to 60% solvent B in solvent A from 1 to 5 min; 60% solvent B in solvent A from 5 to 8 min; from 60 to 20% solvent B in solvent A from 8 to 10 min and 20% solvent B in solvent A until 20 min (solvent A: potassium phosphate buffer 25 mM at pH = 8, filtered through 0.22 µm pores before use; solvent B: 100% methanol). The injection volume was set to 13 µL. The flow rate was maintained at 0.8 mL/min during the 20 min of elution. The fluorescence detector was set for the detection of thiochrome and its esters (λex = 370 nm; λem = 425 nm). Chromatography grade vitamin B1 standards [thiamine hydrochloride ≥ 99% (T4625), thiamine monophosphate chloride dehydrate ≥ 99% (T8637) and thiamine pyrophosphate ≥ 95% (C8754)] were purchased from Sigma (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). The chemicals necessary for aqueous mobile phase preparation and the thiamine derivatization process [potassium phosphate monobasic ≥ 99% (795488), potassium phosphate dibasic ≥ 98% (P8281) and potassium ferricyanide 99% (702587)] were also purchased from Sigma. HPLC grade methanol ≥ 99.9% (34860) was purchased from Honeywell Riedel-de Haën (Charlotte, NC, USA).

Must treatments

The results of the product assays experiment allowed to choose a treatment to modulate the vitamin content of a natural grape must, with a particular focus on vitamin B1. A white grape must was selected and measured at 170 µg/L of free thiamine equivalent with the method described in “RP-HPLC-Fluo analysis of 3 vitamin B1 vitamers” section, this must is therefore referred as our Control. A 100 g/hL bentonite treatment was applied to a portion of this must to create a deficiency, lowering its B1 content to 50 µg/L, creating our Treated condition. Some of the Treated must was then supplemented with thiamine hydrochloride (Reference Sigma T4625) to raise B1 levels back to the content of the Control must, as a mean to differentiate between the effects of thiamine depletion compared to other unknown compounds removed by bentonite treatment. This third condition is therefore referred as Treated + B1.

Yeast inoculation procedures

Three different inoculation conditions were used: direct use of Active dry yeasts (ADY), yeast cells precultured in thiamine rich media, and cells precultured in low thiamine conditions. ADY were resuspended in 10× their mass of 37 °C mineral water, and allowed to rehydrate for 20 min before inoculation. For precultivated cells, we based our protocols on the one described by Labuschagne et al.18. Briefly, rehydrated ADY were diluted at 0.3% (v/v) in 10 mL of preculture medium which was then incubated for 24 h at 30 °C with agitation at 100 rpm. After 24 h, each 10 mL preculture was added to 90 mL of fresh medium for another 24 h incubation under the same conditions. Two preculture media were used: a classical Yeast Nitrogen Base (YNB) medium, which has a high vitamin B1 content (detailed composition in Supplementary Table 3) and a modified YNB medium with a lower thiamine content (divided by 8 compared to classical YNB), that we called thiamine starving medium (TSM). All the fermentations were carried out in 150 mL Erlenmeyers covered with cotton wool and aluminium foil, containing 100 mL of grape must. Inoculation was performed at 106 viable cells per mL. For the ADY, the correct volume of rehydrated yeasts was added directly. For the two preculture conditions, an extra washing step was performed: the correct volume was sampled for each preculture and then centrifuged for 10 min at 2500g. The supernatant was discarded and the cells were resuspended in 5 mL of sterile physiological water before another round of centrifugation under the same conditions. The supernatant was discarded and the resulting pellets were used for inoculation.

Yeast intracellular B1 content

Precultures in YNB and TSM, and ADY suspensions were performed in triplicate. The necessary volume to obtain 107 cells was then transferred to centrifuge tubes, and centrifuged at 6800g for 5 min. Cells were washed twice with physiological water, then resuspended in 0.4 mL of 0.1 M HCl. The suspensions were transferred to lysis tubes containing 80 mg of 500 µm glass beads and mechanically lysed in a Precellys® 24 Touch Homogenizer (Bertin Technologies, Montigny-le-Bretonneux, France). The samples were subjected to 4 agitation cycles of 30 s at 6500 rpm, with 1 min in an ice bath between each cycle to avoid any thiamine degradation due to overheating. The tubes were then centrifuged at 6800g for 5 min, and the supernatants were frozen until analysis with the RP-HPLC-Fluo method described in “RP-HPLC-Fluo analysis of 3 vitamin B1 vitamers” section. to account for total vitamin B1 content, including free thiamine, thiamine monophosphate and thiamine pyrophosphate.

Cell growth monitoring

Viable cell count was monitored by flow cytometry, based on the procedure described by Bordet et al.19. In brief, 5(6)-carboxyfluorescein diacetate (cFDA) was used as a fluorochrome to mark viable cells. 1 µL of cFDA at 1500 µM in acetone was added to 100 µL of yeast samples diluted in McIlvaine buffer (100 mM citric acid and 200 mM Na2HPO4 at pH 4). The samples were incubated in the dark for 10 min before analysis on a BD Accuri C6 Plus flow cytometer (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA).

Fermentation assays

Fermentations were carried out under 9 different experimental conditions, corresponding to combinations of the three must conditions (Control, Treated and Treated + B1) and the three inoculation methods (ADY, YNB and YSM). See Supplementary Table 4. Each of those 9 experimental conditions was performed in triplicate. The experiment was conducted at 22 °C without agitation. CO2 production was monitored by daily weight loss measurements as described by Seguinot et al.20. This allowed calculating the cumulative CO2 production and the fermentation rate over time. Samples were taken daily for cell growth monitoring as described in 2.4.4 to calculate population parameters, and for sugars (glucose and fructose) and ethanol monitoring on an OenoFOSS FTIR (Fourier-transform infrared) spectrometer (FOSS Analytical, Hillerød, Denmark). Fermentation was considered complete when residual sugar content was under 2 g/L. The remaining volume of each fermenter was then centrifuged for 10 min at 2500g and supernatant was frozen until analysed.

Volatile compounds analyses

All analyses on volatile aromatic compounds were performed at Laboratoire Océania (Vallet, Pays de la Loire, France). Esters were analysed by Headspace-SPME-GC/MS on an Agilent 7890 gas chromatographer combined with a LECO Pegasus BT ToF mass spectrometer. Quantification was based on peak area and standard curves. Higher alcohols were analysed by GC/FID on a Varian® GC/FID system, consisting of a CP-8400 autosampler, CP-1177 injector, and a 3900 Gas Chromatograph with a Flame Ionization Detector (FID). Data were acquired and processed on Galaxie software, version 1.7.4.5. The system was fitted with a ZB-WAX capillary column (60 m × 0.25 mm internal diameter × 0.25 µm film thickness) from Phenomenex (Torrance, CA, USA). Quantification was based on peak area and standard curves, with 4-Methyl-2-pentanol being added during sample preparation as an internal standard for normalization. OAV (odour activity values) for each compound were calculated by dividing the concentrations by their olfactory perception thresholds found in the literature.

Statistical analysis

All statistical tests, PCA (Principal Component Analysis), figures and results tables were performed with RStudio software (Posit PBC, Boston, MA, USA) with R version 4.4.0. For all statistical tests, a p value < 0.05 was used to determine significance. Data from the oenological treatment assays were processed with a 1-way ANOVA (analysis of variance) followed by a post-hoc Dunnett test for comparison between each treatment versus control. For intracellular B1 assessment, a 1-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test for pairwise comparison between the 3 different inoculation conditions was performed. The fermentation assays, according to a 3 × 3 experimental design, were treated through a 2-way ANOVA to verify the importance of potential interactions between the must and inoculation factors in explaining the observed variance. This was followed by Tukey’s test for pairwise comparison between the levels of each main factor, which are the primary focus of this study.

Results and discussion

In this work our objectives were to assess the impact of a wide variety of pre-fermentative treatments on nutrient availability with a focus on B-group vitamins, and study the impact of vitamin deficiencies in a natural grape must matrix on fermentability and volatile compound production by S. cerevisiae yeasts.

Effect of oenological treatments on vitamins

Among the 26 treatments tested at maximal doses (Supplementary Table 2) in a rosé grape must, 16 led to significant changes compared to the control for at least one of the 7 tested vitamers (Fig. 1). In most cases the impactful treatments had significant effects on several vitamin compounds simultaneously. Products belonging to the same categories generally showed similar effects, with vitamin depletion observed for several fining agents, while yeast derivatives released vitamins into the must (one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s test vs. control, p < 0.05). Bentonites

Bentonite (constituted mainly of montmorillonite, a type of clay), is a commonly used mineral fining treatment in oenology used to improve the protein stability of wine. Regarding vitamins, we demonstrated that all 3 tested bentonites can severely deplete the must of vitamins when used at 100 g/hL (Fig. 1). Vitamin B1 was the most affected compound (up to − 92% for bentonite no. 1 compared to the control). Regarding the other vitamins, all the bentonites had significant effects on B6 vitamers (up to − 71% and − 72% for pyridoxal and pyridoxine, respectively), and on vitamin B2 (up to − 38%). Interestingly, only bentonite no. 3 showed a significant impact on nicotinamide, a B3 vitamer, while none of the 3 products had effects on vitamin B5. The effect of bentonite on several vitamin compounds can be explained by its mode of action. Its surface negative electric charge21 allows the adsorption of positively charged molecules, including proteins and many other compounds. This depletion effect of bentonite was already demonstrated in oenological context for phenolic and volatile aromatic compounds22. Regarding vitamin compounds, vitamin B2 depletion from white and red wine through bentonite treatment was also previously demonstrated23. Otherwise, the effect of bentonite or various clays on other vitamin compounds in oenological context has been little studied up to now. Only Jaulmes et al.24 demonstrated a vitamin-depleting effect of bentonite on wine (Reported by Ournac25). On grape must, a recent study by Ramousse et al.15 showed a depleting effect of one bentonite on several B vitamins in must. Recent research on other fields, such as animal nutrition26, also demonstrated the water-soluble vitamin binding properties of bentonite.

Oenological carbons

Oenological carbons (or charcoals) are plant-based activated carbons commonly used in oenology to correct alterations or colour. We found that carbons used at 100 g/hL had a significant depleting effect on vitamins in grape must (Fig. 1). They presented similar trends to bentonite for B1 (up to − 89%) and B6 (up to − 66%). A stronger effect on vitamin B3 (up to − 60%) was found for most of the tested products. The most striking effect was found for riboflavin, with an almost complete depletion for half of the tested carbons. Interestingly, carbons nos. 1, 2 and 3 had smaller effects on most compounds compared to the others. As for bentonites, vitamin B5 was unaffected by all the tested carbons. These effects can be explained by the mode of action of this fining agent: activated carbon retains various compounds through adsorption within its porous structure27 in a non-specific way. In oenology, those properties can be used for must or wine decontamination, to remove mycotoxins such as Ochratoxin A28 or unwanted volatile compounds causing off-flavours29. Carbons are also used to prevent white wine browning, by removing polyphenols from the matrix30. The use of carbons for riboflavin removal in finished wine was already described23, showing similar results to our study on grape must, especially the stronger effect of activated carbons compared to bentonites. Ramousse et al.15 also reported similar trends for one carbon tested on two grape musts.

Yeast derivatives

Contrary to bentonites and oenological carbons, we showed that yeast-derived compounds can release significant amounts of vitamin compounds (Fig. 1), with more heterogeneous effects depending on the product compared to fining agents. In particular, B2 and B3 were released in significant amounts by more than half of the tested products (up to + 151% for riboflavin, + 464% for nicotinic acid and + 43% for nicotinamide), while significant B1 enrichment (+ 48%) was observed only with yeast derivative no. 7, a partially autolyzed yeast extract. These enrichments are consistent with those reported previously for an inactivated dried yeast addition15. The differences between products could be linked to several factors such as strains, cultivation conditions and industrial manufacturing processes such as pH, temperature or autolysis time. In particular, a publication by Tao et al.31 reported that yeast autolysis processes can lead to more nutrient degradation compared to purely mechanical processes, including vitamins. Although enrichment in most vitamins can be seen as positive for vinification processes, the increase in riboflavin can be a point of concern, since its presence in excessive concentration in the final wine can lead to the well-known light-struck taste (LST), a well described wine off-flavour32. An increase in the occurrence of LST is described for riboflavin content in wine exceeding 50–75 µg/L33, which is close to the maximal addition found in the present experiment for yeast derivative no. 5, with 49.4 µg/L of vitamin B2 being released in the must. As shown by Canito et al.34, wine yeasts still release significant amounts of riboflavin by the end of alcoholic fermentation even when the vitamin is added before inoculation, which confirms that the addition from yeast derivatives reported in the present study could increase LST risk.

Fermentability assessments

Vitamin B1 (thiamine) was among the most impacted compounds during our oenological treatment assays, with further testing demonstrating a depletion effect even for lower bentonite doses (Supplementary Fig. 1). This vitamin has been long used as a fermentation activator in oenology and is currently the only B vitamin authorized as an additive by OIV (International Organisation of Vine and Wine) regulations35. Recent studies on synthetic must showed its essential nature to ensure good fermentative performances alongside vitamins B5 and B79. The strong depletion we noted for treatments with fining agents, especially bentonites, can lead to adverse effects on the alcoholic fermentation of wine. The final concentrations in the treated samples were as low as 50 µg/L of residual thiamine, below the commonly described S. cerevisiae deficiency threshold of 125 µg/L for this essential vitamin18. Such deficiencies in vitamin B1 can lead to a reduction of fermentative activity and modify wine’s volatile composition9,10. However, this has rarely been demonstrated in real grape must because of the difficulty of procuring large quantities of naturally depleted grape must as a basis for experimentation. Therefore, the recent results on the effects of fining treatments on this vitamin opened new perspectives for experimental investigation on a real grape must matrix. Fermentation trials of bentonite and carbon treated musts with known B1 content have already been performed, with surprisingly low impacts on fermentability and a higher impact on volatile compounds production15. The low impact on fermentability in this previous study was attributed to the low sugar content of the musts used, intended for low-ethanol base wine for sparkling wine production, and therefore potentially easier to ferment for yeasts compared to a more typical grape must. This highlighted the necessity of further investigations on musts with higher potential alcohol by volume (ABV), which is the scope of the present study.

Inoculation procedures and intracellular B1 content

In addition to several must conditions depending on must treatment and thiamine addition, we wanted to compare the effect of different inoculation procedures on the course of alcoholic fermentation and final wine characteristics. We hypothesized that the use of active dry yeast (ADY) or cells precultured in low or high B1 content could lead to different physiological states and modulate the intracellular thiamine pool at inoculation, potentially compensating B1 deficiencies in the must. To assess the impact of our inoculation procedures on intracellular B1, yeast lysates from each condition were analysed for all 3 B1 vitamers using the RP-HPLC-Fluo method described in “RP-HPLC-Fluo analysis of 3 vitamin B1 vitamers” section, in order to account for all the B1 nutritional forms potentially available. Each condition presented a significantly different B1 intracellular content (Supplementary Fig. 2), the lowest for Thiamine Starving Medium (TSM) at 26 µg of free thiamine equivalent in 1010 cells and the highest for Yeast Nitrogen Base (YNB) medium at 168 µg. ADY were an intermediate level between the two precultures at 51 µg. These results confirm the strong capabilities of S. cerevisiae to accumulate available thiamine from its environment, as already described in several previous publications18,36.

Fermentation kinetics and yeast growth

To investigate the impact of vitamin B1 deficiency on must fermentability and volatile compound production, three different grape must conditions were used. First, a natural grape must measured at 170 µg/L of B1 was selected, henceforth referred to as Control must. To induce vitamin B1 deficiency, a portion of the Control must was treated with bentonite to reach 50 µg/L of B1, resulting in the Treated condition. Since bentonite does not selectively remove only vitamins, and to verify that the observed effects were specifically related to vitamin B1 deficiency, the results were compared to a third condition, obtained from the Treated must supplemented with vitamin B1 to reach the same concentration as the Control must, henceforth referred to as the Treated + B1 condition. Results of the 2-way ANOVA showed significant impacts of both must and inoculation factors as well as their interaction (Supplementary Table 5). Although statistically significant, the interaction effects explained a smaller proportion of variance than the main factors, and were therefore not the primary focus of interpretation.

The must factor had a significant impact on both the maximal fermentation rate and the maximal cell population, while the maximal specific growth rate and generation time were not affected (Table 1). The vitamin B1 deficiency induced by bentonite treatment resulted in a significant 12% decrease in Rmax. This decrease appears to be largely associated with vitamin B1 deficiency, as Rmax was similar to the Control in the must supplemented with vitamin B1. It is interesting to note that supplementation with vitamin B1 in the bentonite-treated must did not restore the maximum population observed in the Control must. This suggests that bentonite depletes the medium of other nutrients that affect the maximum population with a 32% decrease in the Treated must compared to the Control must. Yeast assimilable nitrogen (YAN) does not seem to be affected by bentonite treatment according to the literature37 and our own findings (Supplementary Table 1). However, negative impacts have been reported for other nutrients such as lipids15, which could contribute to this decrease in population. The effect of thiamine availability primarily on fermentative activity is consistent with previous findings made on synthetic must9, which showed no effects on population parameters. The effect of bentonite treatment on the maximal population could, however, be dependent on the must matrix used, since Ramousse et al.15 reported no significant change for the maximal population in two bentonite-treated musts despite the measured nutrient loss. Regarding the effect of the inoculation factor, the most striking effect was found for the maximal cell population, with increases of 39% and 27% compared to ADY for TSM and YNB, respectively (Table 2), suggesting the maximal population reached during the alcoholic fermentation can be affected by the adaptation of yeast cells to liquid culture conditions before inoculation rather than by the B1 intracellular pool. The maximal specific growth rate and the generation time derived from it seem to be the only parameters that could be linked with intracellular thiamine, with non-significant differences between TSM and ADY (low and medium intracellular B1, respectively), while YNB shows a reduced generation time value, under 5 h. While significant impacts on the fermentation could be observed and linked to our analysed factors, they were surprisingly mild even in the most thiamine depleted conditions. Even with the slight differences in kinetics and population determined, no sluggish fermentations were observed during this experiment. All the conditions reached dryness (< 2 g/L residual sugars), and obtained wines from the 9 conditions had similar oenological characteristics, with mean ethanol contents ranging from 12.65 ± 0.49 to 13.26 ± 0.10%, total acidity from 3.84 to 4.47 g/L of H2SO4 equivalent, and pH from 3.14 to 3.23 (Supplementary Table 6). Ramousse et al.15 observed similar trends, formulating the hypothesis that the low impact of bentonite treatment could be explained by the easily fermentable matrixes used (grape must with potential ABV under 10%). The relatively mild effects of the bentonite induced B1 deficiency we observed on a grape must with higher sugar content (potential ABV over 13%) suggest that additional mechanisms should be considered. It is possible that S. cerevisiae de novo biosynthesis pathway for thiamine is sufficient to compensate for deficiencies in the specific strain used, although it is generally deemed to be a highly energy-intensive process for the cells7. The genetic and physiological determinants underlying thiamine biosynthesis in the strain used in this study are not yet specifically characterized which constitute a limitation of the present work. Future work on yeast gene expression and strain comparison could help clarify the contribution of vitamin biosynthesis on the responses to vitamin limitations.

Overall impact on volatile compounds

GC/FID and HS-SPME-GC/MS were used to quantify 13 compounds, specifically 5 higher alcohols, 5 ethyl esters and 3 acetate esters. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the complete dataset as an exploratory analysis (Fig. 2). The first two dimensions explained 64.6% of the variation, and the addition of the third dimension brought the total to 82.5% of explained variance (Supplementary Fig. 3). The 3 levels of the must factor were represented on the PCA biplot by colour and 95% confidence ellipses, while the inoculation factor was represented by dot shape. The ellipses allow visualising a clear separation between the Treated must on one side, versus the Control and the Treated + B1 musts on the other side. This clustering pattern suggests that thiamine availability strongly contributes to the differences in volatile composition observed following bentonite treatment. 2-way ANOVA showed a significant impact on nine compounds for the must factor and eight for the inoculation factor. Four compounds were significantly affected by the interaction between factors (Supplementary Table 7). The most striking interaction occurred with ethyl-2-methylpropanoate, with differential effects of ADY versus preculture depending on the must condition (Supplementary Fig. 4), with the precultures resulting in higher concentrations compared to ADY in Control must, while final concentrations were similar for all inoculation methods in the Treated + B1 must. However, the variance explained by interactions was generally smaller than the contribution of the main factors, which will be discussed further on. It should nevertheless be noted that the small-scale semi-open fermentation design used in this study may have introduced some variability in oxygen exposure compared to industrial vinification conditions, which can influence yeast metabolism and volatile compound production. However, all conditions were conducted using the same experimental setup, allowing relative comparisons between treatments.

Impact of must factor

High thiamine availability was associated with an overall increase in higher alcohol and ester content, with similar profiles between the Control must and the Treated + B1 must for most of the 9 impacted compounds (Fig. 3). The only exceptions were 2-methyl-1-propanol (also known as isobutanol) and ethyl-2-methylpropanoate which presented significantly higher values in the bentonite treated must with low B1 content. The positive impact of thiamine availability on higher alcohol production can be linked to its role in decarboxylation of α-keto acids towards their aldehydes in the Ehrlich pathway, which then led to higher alcohol synthesis38. The effect of thiamine on acetate esters is a logical continuation since these esters are the result of the condensation of higher alcohols with acetyl-CoA. For ethyl esters, the significant impacts we found are consistent with reported results obtained from synthetic must experiments10, but the exact mechanisms remain unclear since thiamine does not intervene directly in the associated pathways. Most impacted compounds were measured above their odour perception thresholds (Table 3). The higher alcohols detected can be mainly associated with alcoholic or fusel descriptors, with the exception of 2-phenylethanol which is associated with floral, rose like aromas39. This alcohol was among the most impacted compounds, with an odour activity value (OAV) of 2.54 in the thiamine depleted condition compared to 3.88 in the untreated Control must and 3.77 in the Treated + B1 must (Supplementary Table 8), corresponding to a roughly 50% increase in high B1 must compared to the Treated must. Its acetate ester, 2-phenylethyl-acetate, which is also described as having rose like aromas40, was under the perception threshold in the Treated must (OAV < 1), but above it in the Control and Treated + B1 musts. Impacted ethyl esters, associated with fruity and sweet aromas41,42, were positively or negatively impacted by B1 availability depending on the compound, suggesting a complex and indirect effect of thiamine on their synthesis pathways, with a possible role of unknown impacts of the bentonite treatment. Impact of inoculation factor

Globally, ADY were associated with lower concentrations of six impacted volatiles (out of eight) compared to the preculture conditions (Fig. 4). For the two other compounds, 2-phenylethyl acetate was present in higher concentrations in the ADY condition for all musts and ethyl-octanoate presented different effects of inoculation procedures depending on the must matrix. No effect could be linked to the intracellular B1 content of the inoculated yeasts, the adaptation to liquid medium seeming to be the main factor influencing the results. As for the must factor, most compounds impacted by the different inoculation procedures were measured above their odour perception thresholds (Table 4). One of the most important effects was found for ethyl-2-methylbutanoate, which is described as having fruity aromas, such as apple or strawberry41. This ester had an OAV of 1.45 in ADY condition, versus 1.93 (+ 33%) and 1.75 (+ 21%) for TSM and YNB, respectively (Supplementary Table 9). These results can be compared to those of Bordet et al.46, these authors reported an increase in general ester content when using ADY compared to precultures, and no impact on higher alcohols except for one of the tested strains. This seems contradictory to our results but it should be noted that differences in preculture composition, the must matrixes used and the yeast strains chosen can explain these different impacts, underlining the multifactorial aspect of volatile profile determinants. If we focus on two specific compounds that were analysed in both studies, 2-phenylethyl-acetate and ethyl-octanoate, we can see that 2-phenylethyl-acetate presents similar trends to ours between ADY and precultures for 2 of the 4 strains tested by Bordet et al.46. For ethyl-octanoate, we found similar results to the previous study only in the Treated must with 50 µg/L of thiamine. This comparison suggests that the impact of preculture methods on the wine volatile profile should be taken into account with other factors in mind, such as yeast strain and the culture medium or grape must matrixes used, particularly their nutrient levels. Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that treating grape musts with oenological fining products strongly depleted their vitamin content. In parallel, we demonstrated that yeast derivatives could compensate for the vitamin deficiencies induced by bentonite or oenological charcoal treatments. Further experimentation on the impact of bentonite on the fermentability of a natural grape must with typical sugar content (potential ABV at 13.23%) showed significant yet smaller than expected impacts that were linked to vitamin B1 availability for fermentation kinetics, possibly due to an efficient biosynthesis pathway for thiamine in the yeast strain used, while other compounds removed by bentonite treatment had an impact on cell growth parameters. The most striking effect was found for the volatile composition of the finished wines, which was clearly linked to thiamine availability. Since most of the impacted compounds were detected above their olfactive perception thresholds, the observed changes could lead to perceptible modifications of the sensory profile of the wines, which may be a subject for further investigation. Thus, the major impact of thiamine availability was observed on volatile composition rather than on fermentation completion. Overall, this study provides new insights on the impact of pre-fermentative treatments on nutrient availability, which could be useful for the informed use of such treatments by the wine industry. It also confirms the importance of vitamins for the production of volatile aromatic compounds during wine fermentation and suggests that the vitamin status of musts could be a new parameter to consider for fermentation control.
