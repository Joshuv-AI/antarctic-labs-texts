Effect of pulsed ultrasound pretreatment on enzymatic hydrolysis and bio-functional properties of luffa cylindrica seed protein hydrolysates
Authors: Hoda Shahiri Tabarestani, Mohammad Mansour Samie, Alireza Sadeghi Mahoonak, Ali Moayedi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPulsed ultrasound pretreatment was investigated as a strategy to enhance the enzymatic hydrolysis and bio–functional properties of Luffa cylindrica seed protein concentrate. The influence of ultrasound power (100–200 W) and treatment time (5–25 min) on the degree of hydrolysis (DH), structural characteristics, functional properties, and antioxidant activity of Alcalase–generated hydrolysates was evaluated. Ultrasound pretreatment resulted in higher DH values compared with conventional enzymatic hydrolysis (8.13% after 4 h). Ultrasound–assisted treatments followed by only 2 h of hydrolysis produced DH values of 9.6–35.3%, with the maximum obtained at 200 W for 25 min, suggesting that ultrasonic pretreatment may accelerate proteolysis while reducing processing time. SDS–PAGE indicated progressive degradation of native proteins, including reduced intensity of high–molecular–weight bands (>45 kDa) and the appearance of peptides below 15 kDa. FTIR analysis suggested ultrasound–induced conformational changes that may facilitate enzyme accessibility. Moderate treatment (200 W, 5 min) was associated with the highest emulsifying activity (33.3 m2 g−1) and foam capacity, whereas prolonged treatment (200 W, 25 min) corresponded with improved foam stability and antioxidant activity. Ultrasound treatment also resulted in more negative zeta potentials (up to − 20.6 mV), which may contribute to improved solubility and dispersion stability. Overall, pulsed ultrasound appears to be a promising approach for modifying the structural and functional properties of L. cylindrica protein hydrolysates for potential food applications.Keywords: Luffa cylindrica, Ultrasound pretreatment, Enzymatic hydrolysis, Antioxidant activity, Emulsifying activity, FTIR

Introduction

The global food system faces the dual challenge of meeting a growing demand for protein while minimizing environmental impact. This has intensified interest in underutilized agricultural by-products as sustainable sources of functional ingredients. Luffa cylindrica, commonly known as luffa or sponge gourd, is one such source. Primarily cultivated for its fibrous fruit used as a natural sponge, its seeds are a nutritionally rich but largely discarded by-product. Each mature fruit yields seeds containing significant protein (approximately 27%), fat, and carbohydrates, along with various secondary metabolites [1]. Despite this valuable nutritional profile, luffa seeds are typically discarded during sponge production [2], representing a loss of potential resources for the food and nutraceutical industries.

The high protein content of luffa seeds positions them as a promising substrate for generating bioactive peptides—specific protein fragments with health-promoting properties. These peptides can exhibit antioxidant, antihypertensive, and antidiabetic activities, offering significant potential for preventing lifestyle-related diseases. For instance, research on Luffa cylindrica seed protein hydrolysates have already demonstrated in vitro inhibitory effects against angiotensin-1-converting enzyme (ACE), α-amylase, and α-glucosidase [3]. While conventional enzymatic hydrolysis is the primary method for producing such peptides, it is constrained by limitations including long reaction times, strict pH and temperature requirements, and inefficient enzyme-substrate interactions, which can lead to enzyme deactivation [4], [5]. To date, no studies have attempted to overcome these challenges using advanced processing technologies specifically tailored to luffa seed proteins, further underscoring the lack of systematic research on this underutilized substrate.

To overcome these limitations, ultrasound technology has emerged as a highly effective green processing aid. Through cavitation, thermal, and mechanical effects, ultrasound disrupts compact protein structures, reduces aggregate size, and increases the surface area accessible to enzymes, thereby enhancing hydrolysis efficiency and bioactivity [4], [6]. This approach has been extensively validated across a remarkably diverse spectrum of protein sources, with its versatility more recently demonstrated for a wide array of underutilized and non-conventional protein matrices. These include grain legumes and pseudocereals such as fava bean, chickpea, and quinoa; oilseeds like camelina, pumpkin, and sea buckthorn; and agro-industrial by-products including proso millet bran and brewer's spent grains[7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]. Furthermore, the technique has proven effective for more novel sources, including insect, mushroom, and various marine proteins (e.g., loach, oyster, tuna), as well as algal proteins like Arthrospira platensis and Porphyra yezoensis, where it has been shown to enhance both structural and bio-functional properties [13], [14], [15]. The collective evidence demonstrates that ultrasound-assisted enzymatic hydrolysis consistently improves hydrolysis kinetics and enhances the functional and biological properties of the resulting hydrolysates [16], [17], [18]. These improvements are attributed to well-characterized structural modifications, including protein unfolding, increased surface hydrophobicity, and alterations in secondary structure (reduced α-helix and increased β-sheet content), which collectively facilitate enzyme-substrate interactions [19], [20]. Beyond these structural and functional modifications, ultrasound also shows promise for modulating protein allergenicity, digestibility, and specific bioactivity profiles, further expanding its potential for developing tailored ingredients for specialized applications [21], [22].

Despite this extensive body of work, no studies have evaluated the structural, functional, or bioactive consequences of ultrasound-assisted enzymatic hydrolysis in luffa seed proteins, representing a clear and significant gap in the valorization of this promising but neglected by-product. Furthermore, while continuous ultrasound modes are common, pulsed ultrasound protocols offer potential advantages in energy efficiency and process control, which may produce distinct effects on protein structure and hydrolysis kinetics [23]. Yet, the influence of pulsed ultrasound on luffa seed protein and the resulting implications for enzyme accessibility, peptide release, and functional performance has never been investigated. Given the nutritional significance of this underutilized by-product and the demonstrated potential of UAE, there is a clear rationale for systematically investigating pulsed ultrasound pretreatment to enhance the value of luffa seed protein concentrate (LPC). This positions the present work as a foundational study establishing the first mechanistic and functional insights into ultrasound-enabled valorization of luffa seed proteins.

Therefore, this study investigates the effects of pulsed ultrasound pretreatment on the enzymatic hydrolysis of LPC using Alcalase. The specific objectives are to evaluate how ultrasound exposure time (5, 15, and 25 min) and power (100 and 200 W) influence the degree of hydrolysis, antioxidant activity (assessed via DPPH radical scavenging, iron chelation, and total antioxidant capacity assays), functional properties (solubility, foaming and emulsifying capacity and stability), molecular weight distribution, and structural changes in LPC using Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR), and zeta potential. By establishing a clear structure–property relationship for ultrasound-treated LPC, this research aims to contribute to the development of efficient, scalable processes for transforming an agricultural waste stream into high-value functional ingredients, aligning with the principles of green chemistry and a circular economy. In doing so, this work provides the first comprehensive framework for understanding of luffa seed protein—laying essential groundwork for future valorization strategies.

Material and methods

Materials

Luffa cylindrica (luffa) seeds were obtained from local agricultural farms in Northern Iran. The seeds were manually cleaned to remove foreign matter, damaged seeds, and other impurities prior to processing. All chemicals and reagents used in this study were of analytical grade. Chloroform, methanol, hydrochloric acid (HCl), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sodium phosphate monobasic, sodium phosphate dibasic, trichloroacetic acid (TCA), bovine serum albumin (BSA), Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, copper sulfate, sodium potassium tartrate, 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), ferrozine, iron(II) chloride (FeCl2), sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), sulfuric acid (H2SO4), sodium phosphate (Na3PO4), and ammonium molybdate ((NH4)2MoO4) were procured from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Sunflower oil was purchased from a local supermarket in Gorgan, Iran. The enzyme Alcalase® (protease from Bacillus licheniformis, activity 2.972 U/mL) was obtained from Novozymes (Denmark). A wide molecular weight marker kit was obtained from ExcelBand™ (Solon, Ohio, USA). Deionized water was used for the preparation of all solutions throughout the experiments.

Preparation of luffa seed protein concentrate (LPC)

Defatting of luffa seed meal

Luffa seeds were subjected to cold pressing using a screw oil press (Model D 90, Barad Engineering Group, Iran) to extract the oil. The resulting press cake (seed meal) was collected and further ground into a fine powder using an electric grain mill (ROM Products, China). The ground material was sieved through an 80-mesh laboratory sieve to ensure uniform particle size. The sieved meal was defatted using a chloroform–methanol mixture (3:1, v/v) at a solid-to-solvent ratio of 1:4 (w/v). The suspension was continuously stirred for 8 h at room temperature using an electric stirrer to facilitate efficient lipid extraction. The resulting slurry was filtered through a glass sinter to separate the defatted meal from the solvent. The residual solvent was allowed to evaporate overnight at room temperature in a fume hood. The defatted meal was then dried in a hot air oven (Model ULE 500, Memmert, Germany) at 30 °C for 1 h to remove any remaining moisture. After drying, the sample was re-ground to a fine powder and stored in airtight containers at 4 °C until further use for protein extraction.

Protein extraction

Loofa protein concentrate (LPC) was prepared using the pH-shift (alkaline extraction-isoelectric precipitation) method described by Arise et al. [3], with minor modifications. Protein extraction was carried out by dispersing the defatted seed powder in 0.1 M NaOH (pH 12) at a ratio of 1:10 (w/v). The suspension was stirred continuously for 1 h using a magnetic stirrer to facilitate protein solubilization. The resulting slurry was then centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C (Fan Taraz Tech, Iran), and the supernatant containing soluble proteins was collected. The extraction procedure was repeated once on the residual pellet under the same conditions to maximize protein recovery, and the supernatants from both extraction cycles were pooled. Protein precipitation was achieved by adjusting the pH of the combined extract to 4.0 (the isoelectric point of luffa protein) using 1 M HCl (CLEAN P 500, Taiwan). The precipitated protein was recovered by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The protein pellet was washed twice with distilled water to remove residual salts and impurities, and subsequently neutralized to pH 7.0 using 0.1 M NaOH. The resulting LPC was freeze-dried (Model FDB-5503, Operon, South Korea) at −50 °C for 48 h, and the dried concentrate was stored in sealed containers at 4 °C until further analysis and hydrolysis experiments. The protein, fat, and ash contents of the LPC were determined according to AOAC [24] standard methods No. 925.1 (N × 6.25), 923.03, and 920.85, respectively. The mean values were found to be 56.43%, 2.8%, and 1.3%, respectively.

Preparation of luffa protein hydrolysates

Enzymatic hydrolysis (E-LPH)

Enzymatic hydrolysis of LPC was performed according to the method of Arise et al. [3], with minor modifications. Lyophilized LPC was dissolved in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 8.0) to achieve a final protein concentration of 5% (w/v). The solution was stirred for 30 min at room temperature to ensure complete dispersion. Alcalase® 2.4 L was added at an enzyme-to-substrate ratio of 1:100 (w/w, enzyme protein/substrate protein). The hydrolysis reaction was carried out in a temperature-controlled water bath at 55 °C for 4 h under continuous agitation to ensure uniform enzyme distribution and substrate contact. The enzymatic reaction was terminated by heating the mixture in a boiling water bath (Model WB 14, Memmert, Germany) at 100 °C for 15 min to inactivate the protease. After cooling to room temperature, the resulting hydrolysate was centrifuged at 4000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C (Model Z36HK, HERMLE, England) to separate soluble peptides from insoluble, non-hydrolyzed residues. The supernatant, designated as enzymatic luffa protein hydrolysate (E-LPH), was collected, freeze-dried, and stored in airtight containers at 4 °C for subsequent analyses.

Ultrasound-assisted enzymatic hydrolysis (US + E-LPH)

For ultrasound-assisted enzymatic hydrolysis, LPC was first dissolved in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 8.0) at a concentration of 5% (w/v). The protein solution was subjected to pulsed ultrasound pretreatment using an ultrasonic processor (Model UP 200H, Hielscher Ultrasonics, Germany) equipped with a titanium sonotrode (S14, diameter 14 mm). The system operated at a fixed frequency of 20 kHz with a maximum power output of 200 W. Ultrasound was applied in pulse mode (10 s on, 5 s off) to minimize excessive heat generation and sample overheating. The sonotrode was immersed to a depth of approximately 1.5 cm in the sample solution, and the treatment was conducted in a glass beaker (50 mL) surrounded by an cold-water bath to maintain the temperature below 30 °C. A real-time temperature profile for the most intensive sonication condition (nominal 200 W, 25 min) is provided in Fig. S1, showing that the temperature remained between 25 and 29.5 °C throughout the treatment, with a maximum of 29.5 °C recorded at the end of the process. For all other conditions, temperature increases were even smaller. To verify that the enhanced effects were specifically caused by ultrasound cavitation and not by temperature, a control was conducted using heat treatment alone at 30 °C for identical time periods (5, 15, and 25 min) in the absence of ultrasound. No notable increase in hydrolysis degree was detected relative to untreated controls (Fig. S2). Sonication was performed at two amplitude levels (50% and 100%), corresponding to approximate power outputs of 100 W and 200 W, respectively. The corresponding spatial-average acoustic intensities were calculated as 27.1 W/cm2 (nominal 100 W) and 55.7 W/cm2 (nominal 200 W). For each power level, three different exposure times (5, 15, and 25 min) were applied, resulting in a total of six pretreatment conditions (100 W-5 min, 100 W-15 min, 100 W-25 min, 200 W-5 min, 200 W-15 min, and 200 W-25 min). Immediately following ultrasound pretreatment, enzymatic hydrolysis was initiated by adding Alcalase® 2.4 L at an enzyme-to-substrate ratio of 1:100 (w/w). The hydrolysis reaction was conducted at 55 °C for 2 h under continuous agitation. The reaction time was reduced from 4 h (conventional method) to 2 h for ultrasound-pretreated samples based on preliminary experiments indicating accelerated hydrolysis kinetics. To determine the optimal hydrolysis duration, preliminary kinetic experiments were conducted with ultrasound-pretreated samples (200 W, 25 min) and conventional samples (no pretreatment) over a time range of 0.5 to 5.5 h. The results (Fig. S3) indicated that the US-pretreated samples reached a plateau in DH after approximately 2 h, whereas the conventional samples required 4 h to reach their (lower) plateau. Based on this kinetic evidence, a 2-hour hydrolysis time was selected for all ultrasound-pretreated samples in the main experimental design to capture the accelerated kinetics without unnecessary processing time. The reaction was terminated, and the hydrolysate was processed following the same procedure described in Section 2.3.1 (heat inactivation at 100 °C for 15 min, centrifugation at 4000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C). The resulting product was designated as ultrasound-assisted enzymatic luffa protein hydrolysate (US + E-LPH), freeze-dried, and stored at 4 °C for further analyses.

Determination of degree of hydrolysis (DH)

The degree of hydrolysis (DH) was determined using the trichloroacetic acid (TCA) solubility method as described by Peričin et al. [25]. This method quantifies the proportion of protein fragments soluble in 10% TCA, which corresponds to small peptides and amino acids released during hydrolysis. Briefly, 4 mL of a 5% (w/v) protein hydrolysate solution was mixed with an equal volume (4 mL) of 20% (w/v) TCA, yielding a final TCA concentration of 10% (w/v). The mixture was allowed to stand at 4 °C for 30 min to facilitate complete precipitation of non-hydrolyzed and partially hydrolyzed large proteins. The mixture was subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to separate the TCA-soluble fraction (supernatant) from the TCA-insoluble fraction (precipitate). The protein contents of both the supernatant (TCA-soluble protein fraction) and the original hydrolysate (total protein content) were determined using the Lowry method [26], with bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard. A non-hydrolyzed blank (protein sample subjected to the same conditions without enzyme addition) was included to subtract baseline TCA-soluble components. The Lowry method was chosen over the Bicinchoninic acid protein assay (BCA) method because residual TCA in the supernatant lowers the pH required for optimal BCA color development, and preliminary tests confirmed that BCA gave inconsistent peptide recovery. In contrast, the Lowry method proved more tolerant to trace TCA with careful pH adjustment and offers higher sensitivity for dilute TCA-soluble fractions. The degree of hydrolysis was calculated according to Eq. (1).

Protein solubility

Protein solubility of LPC and hydrolysates (E-LPH and US + E-LPH) was determined according to the method described by Yang et al. [23]. Sample solutions (1%, w/v) were prepared in distilled water, and the pH was adjusted to the desired values (pH 7.0). The solutions were stirred magnetically for 30 min at room temperature to reach equilibrium, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The protein content of the resulting supernatants (m1) was quantified using the Lowry method [26], with bovine serum albumin used as the standard. A calibration curve was constructed using BSA standards (0–200 µg/mL), and protein concentrations were calculated using the linear regression equation Y = 0.0201x + 0.0015 (R2 = 0.98). Total protein content (m) of the original sample solutions was determined using the Kjeldahl method (AOAC Method No. 925.1). Protein solubility was calculated using Eq. (2).

Emulsifying and foaming properties

Emulsifying properties were evaluated following the procedure described by Pi et al. [27]. Oil-in-water emulsions were prepared by mixing 15 mL of sunflower oil with 45 mL of a 1% (w/v) protein sample dispersion (LPC, E-LPH, or US + E-LPH) adjusted to pH 7.0. The mixture was homogenized using a high-speed homogenizer (Heidolph Instruments, Schwabach, Germany) equipped with a 10 mm dispersing tool at 10,000 rpm for 2 min at room temperature to form a stable emulsion. Immediately after homogenization (0 min) and after 10 min of standing, 50 µL of the emulsion was carefully withdrawn from the bottom of the container using a micropipette. The aliquot was immediately diluted with 5 mL of 0.1% (w/v) sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) solution and mixed thoroughly by vortexing. The absorbance of the diluted samples was measured at 500 nm using a UV–Visible spectrophotometer (Model 7615, Jenway, England) against a blank of 0.1% SDS solution. The DPPH free radical scavenging activity

The free radical scavenging activity of LPC, E-LPH, and US + E-LPH against DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) was determined according to the method of Kanbargi et al. [29]. Sample solutions were prepared at a concentration of 5% (w/v) in distilled water. Briefly, 200 µL of sample solution was mixed with 600 µL of freshly prepared 0.15 mM DPPH solution in methanol. The mixture was vortexed and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance was then measured at 517 nm using a UV–Visible spectrophotometer (Model 7615, Jenway, England). The control (blank) consisted of 200 µL distilled water and 600 µL DPPH solution prepared under the same conditions to match the solvent composition of the sample reaction mixtures. Fe2+ chelating activity

The iron chelating activity of LPC, E-LPH, and US + E-LPH was determined following the method of Kanbargi et al. [29], which measures the ability of samples to compete with ferrozine for Fe2+ ions. Sample solutions were prepared at a concentration of 5% (w/v) in distilled water. An aliquot of 200 µL of sample solution was mixed with 600 µL of distilled water and 10 µL of 2 mM FeCl2 solution. The reaction was initiated by adding 20 µL of 5 mM ferrozine solution, followed by vortexing. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 10 min to allow formation of the ferrozine-Fe2+ complex. The absorbance was measured at 562 nm using a UV–Visible spectrophotometer (Model 7615, Jenway, England). A control was prepared in the same manner using distilled water instead of the sample. Total antioxidant capacity (TAC)

Total antioxidant capacity (TAC) was determined according to the phosphomolybdenum method of Raza et al. [30], based on the reduction of Mo(VI) to Mo(V) by antioxidant compounds and subsequent formation of a green phosphate/Mo(V) complex at acidic pH. Higher absorbance values correspond to greater antioxidant capacity. Briefly, 0.1 mL of sample solution (5%, w/v) was mixed with 1 mL of reagent solution composed of 0.6 M sulfuric acid (H2SO4), 28 mM sodium phosphate (Na3PO4), and 4 mM ammonium molybdate ((NH4)2MoO4). The mixture was vortexed and incubated in a boiling water bath at 95 °C for 90 min. After cooling to room temperature, the absorbance was measured at 695 nm using a UV–Visible spectrophotometer (Model 7615, Jenway, England) against a blank containing 0.1 mL of distilled water and 1 mL of reagent solution. Results were expressed as absorbance units at 695 nm.

SDS-PAGE

SDS-PAGE was performed following the method of Sambrook and Russell [31] with minor modifications, using a vertical electrophoresis system (Junny, China). Samples of luffa protein concentrate (LPC) and its hydrolysates were mixed with 5X Laemmli loading buffer (containing 2.5 mg/mL bromophenol blue, 50% (v/v) glycerol, 10% (w/v) SDS, and 0.25 M Tris-HCl, pH 6.8) supplemented with 1% (v/v) β-mercaptoethanol. The mixtures were heated at 95 °C for 5 min and immediately cooled on ice. Aliquots of 20 µL (approximately 2 mg/mL protein) and 5 µL of a molecular weight marker were loaded onto a gel consisting of a 4% stacking gel and a 15% resolving gel. Electrophoresis was carried out at a constant voltage of 200 V for 4 h, until the dye front reached the bottom of the gel. Following electrophoresis, gels were stained for 2 h with 0.1% (w/v) Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 dissolved in a methanol:glacial acetic acid:water solution (4:1:5, v/v/v) and subsequently destained for 2 h with a methanol:glacial acetic acid:water solution (2:1:7, v/v/v) until protein bands became clearly visible. The destained gels were scanned at 600 dpi (Epson Perfection, Japan) and preserved in sealed plastic pouches at 4 °C for documentation.

Zeta potential measurements

Zeta potential was measured using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano–ZS90 analyzer at 25 °C. Samples were prepared as dispersions at a concentration of 20 mg/mL in Milli–Q water and adjusted to pH 7. Measurements were taken after a pre–equilibration period of five minutes.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy of LPC and its hydrolysates powder was performed using an Agilent Cary 600 spectrometer (USA). Spectra were recorded in transmittance mode over the range of 4000–400 cm−1 at room temperature. Protein secondary structures were evaluated by deconvolution of the FTIR Amide I region (1700–1600 cm−1). Spectra were baseline–corrected and normalized prior to analysis. Peak positions were identified from second–derivative spectra, followed by Gaussian curve fitting to resolve the Amide I band into individual components. Sub–bands were assigned as follows: α–helix (1658–1650 cm−1), β–sheet (1640–1610 and 1695–1680 cm−1), β–turn (1680–1660 cm−1), and random coil (1650–1640 cm−1). The relative content of each secondary structure was calculated from the area percentage of its corresponding sub–band within the total Amide I region.

Statistical analysis

This study employed a completely randomized design (CRD) with a 2 × 3 factorial arrangement to investigate the effects of sonication power (100 and 200 W) and time (5, 15, and 25 min) on the characteristics of luffa protein hydrolysate. All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3) using independently prepared hydrolysate batches, and the results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data were analyzed using ANOVA, and significant differences among treatment means were compared using Tukey's HSD test (p < 0.05) in Minitab (Version 22). All graphs were prepared using Microsoft Excel version. 2016, and error bars represent the SD of triplicate measurements.

Results and discussion

Effect of ultrasound-assisted enzymatic hyrolysis on degree of hydrolysis

The results confirm that pulsed ultrasound pretreatment significantly enhances the enzymatic hydrolysis of Luffa cylindrica protein concentrate (LPC). Conventional enzymatic hydrolysis for 4 h (E–LPH) resulted in a degree of hydrolysis (DH) of 8.13%, whereas ultrasound–pretreated samples followed by only 2 h of hydrolysis (US + E–LPH) achieved significantly higher DH values (p < 0.05), ranging from 9.6% to 35.3% (Fig. 1). This demonstrates that ultrasound pretreatment can substantially accelerate hydrolysis while reducing processing time, consistent with previous reports on ultrasound–assisted enzymatic hydrolysis of plant proteins [4], [6].

Effect of ultrasonic pretreatment on the enzymatic hydrolysis of luffa protein concentrate. The degree of hydrolysis (DH) was determined for samples treated with enzyme only (control) and for those subjected to ultrasonic pretreatment at 100 W or 200 W for 5, 15, and 25 min prior to enzymatic hydrolysis.

Hydrolysis efficiency increased with ultrasonic power, indicating a clear dependence on energy input. At a constant pretreatment time of 25 min, increasing the power from 100 W to 200 W raised the DH from 14.2% to 35.3%. At 200 W, extending sonication from 5 to 25 min further enhanced hydrolysis (23.1–35.3%), reflecting cumulative cavitational effects that disrupt protein aggregates and expose additional enzymatic cleavage sites. In contrast, at 100 W, extending treatment time produced no significant improvement, indicating that insufficient ultrasonic intensity cannot effectively modify protein structure [32], [13]. Supporting this, FTIR analysis (Table 2) confirmed that ultrasound pretreatment altered the secondary structure of LPC proteins. Several ultrasound–treated samples, particularly at 200 W, showed increased α–helix content along with variations in β–sheet and β–turn structures and a general reduction in random coil content. These structural rearrangements suggest ultrasound–induced disaggregation and reorganization of protein conformations, which may facilitate enzymatic accessibility to peptide bonds.

Under optimized conditions (200 W, 25 min), the maximum DH reached 35.3%, comparable to values reported for ultrasound–assisted hydrolysis of other plant proteins such as rapeseed, sunflower, and walnut [33], [34], [35]. These findings demonstrate that L. cylindrica seed protein is a promising substrate for ultrasound–assisted enzymatic hydrolysis and highlight the potential of this underutilized agricultural by–product as a source of value–added protein hydrolysates.

Effect of ultrasound-assisted enzymatic hyrolysis on protein solubility

Protein solubility is a key functional property governing the applicability of hydrolysates in food and pharmaceutical systems. Ultrasound pretreatment significantly modulated the solubility of luffa protein hydrolysates in a parameter–dependent manner (Fig. 2), despite high baseline solubility in untreated LPC (92.8%) and conventional E–LPH (95.7%), attributable to the intrinsic hydrophilicity of the protein matrix and the generation of smaller, more hydrophilic peptides during enzymatic cleavage[3], [29]. Notably, improvements in solubility were closely associated with both the degree of hydrolysis (Fig. 1) and changes in zeta potential (Fig. 6). Treatments at 200 W produced the highest DH values and correspondingly more negative zeta potentials (–11.87 to –17.2 mV), indicating enhanced electrostatic repulsion and dispersion stability, which collectively contributed to superior solubility [17], [19], [36], [11]. In contrast, the lower power treatment (100 W) exhibited a time–dependent pattern in which short sonication (5 min) generated a highly negative zeta potential (–20.59 mV) and elevated solubility, whereas prolonged exposure (15–25 min) reduced the magnitude of surface charge (–10.5 to –6.85 mV), coinciding with lower solubility and suggesting aggregation due to insufficient electrostatic stabilization. This non–linear behavior supports the proposed unfolding–reaggregation mechanism reported for several plant proteins [37], [38], [8]. These findings demonstrate that ultrasound–induced structural unfolding enhances DH, while the resulting surface charge characteristics (zeta potential) govern colloidal stability and solubility, confirming that optimal ultrasonic energy input simultaneously maximizes hydrolysis efficiency and dispersion stability.

Effect of ultrasound-assisted enzymatic hyrolysis on emulsifying properties

Ultrasound-assisted enzymatic hydrolysis significantly improved the emulsifying properties of luffa protein compared with untreated concentrate (LPC) and enzyme-only hydrolysate (E-LPH). Both LPC and E-LPH exhibited low emulsifying activity (Fig. 3A; EAI ≈ 15.3–16.4 m2/g), confirming the limited intrinsic emulsifying capacity previously reported for luffa protein [3]. In contrast, the combined ultrasound–enzyme treatment significantly increased EAI (p < 0.05), consistent with findings for egg yolk, whey, and pumpkin seed proteins following ultrasound processing [39], [36], [11].

Emulsion activity index (A) and emulsion stability index (B) of luffa protein hydrolysates: untreated protein concentrate (LPC), enzymatically hydrolyzed LPC (enzyme control), and following ultrasonic pretreatment (100 W or 200 W) for 5, 15, and 25 min before enzymatic hydrolysis.

Ultrasonic power strongly influenced emulsifying performance. Samples treated at 200 W showed significantly higher EAI values than those treated at 100 W at all sonication times (p < 0.05). This improvement was likely associated with ultrasound-induced structural modifications, including partial unfolding and disruption of protein aggregates, which may enhance exposure of hydrophobic groups and adsorption at the oil–water interface [17], [19]. At 200 W, the highest EAI was observed after 5 min (33.3 m2/g), whereas at 100 W the maximum value (24.1 m2/g) occurred after 15–25 min. These results agree with previous studies showing that controlled ultrasound intensity and treatment duration can improve emulsifying properties [38], [20].

The emulsion stability index (ESI) showed a similar trend (Fig. 3B). At 200 W, ESI reached its maximum at 5 min, suggesting that moderate structural modification favored interfacial stabilization [16], [40]. In contrast, prolonged sonication (15–25 min) reduced ESI, possibly due to excessive structural disruption or aggregation, as reported for walnut and zein proteins [33], [41]. At 100 W, ESI remained comparatively low across all treatment times, indicating that the applied energy was insufficient to induce structural changes associated with improved emulsifying behavior [42], [43]. The lower solubility observed at 100 W–15 min (Fig. 2) coincided with reduced emulsifying performance, suggesting aggregation effects that limited protein dispersion and interfacial interactions.

Comparison with DH results (Fig. 1) showed that the highest DH value (200 W, 25 min) did not correspond to the greatest EAI or ESI, indicating that extensive hydrolysis alone did not improve emulsifying performance under the studied conditions. Similar observations have been reported for chickpea, oyster, okara, and collagen proteins [44], [45], [46], [47]. The superior emulsifying properties observed at 200 W–5 min suggest that moderate hydrolysis combined with limited structural modification was more favorable for emulsion formation and stabilization. However, additional analyses, including peptide profiling, droplet size distribution, and emulsion microstructure characterization, are needed to confirm these structure–function relationships.

Effect of ultrasound-assisted enzymatic hyrolysis on foaming properties

Ultrasound-assisted enzymatic hydrolysis significantly affected the foaming properties of luffa protein (p < 0.05), with foam capacity and foam stability showing different responses to treatment. Native luffa protein exhibited low foam capacity, and enzymatic hydrolysis alone resulted in only modest improvement (Fig. 4A), consistent with previous observations [3]). In contrast, the combined ultrasound–enzyme treatment markedly increased foam capacity in a power- and time-dependent manner. The highest foam capacity was observed at 200 W–5 min, where moderate DH and improved solubility (Fig. 2) likely enhanced protein dispersion and adsorption at the air–water interface. Similar ultrasound-induced improvements in foaming properties have been reported for egg white, whey, and pumpkin seed proteins [36], [11].

Foam capacity (A) and foam stability (B) of luffa protein: untreated protein concentrate (LPC), enzymatically hydrolyzed LPC (enzyme control), and following ultrasonic pretreatment (100 W or 200 W) for 5, 15, and 25 min before enzymatic hydrolysis.

Prolonged sonication at 200 W (15–25 min) further increased DH (Fig. 1), indicating more extensive proteolysis. Although increased surface charge can enhance dispersion, excessive hydrolysis may generate smaller peptides with limited ability to stabilize interfacial films, leading to reduced foam capacity. Similar behavior has been reported for walnut and zein proteins [33], [41]. In contrast, foam stability was highest at 200 W–25 min (Fig. 4B), possibly due to structural modifications and increased electrostatic repulsion that improved interfacial film stability, consistent with reports on soy, rice, and mung bean proteins [16], [40], [23], [48], [37]. Overall, optimal foaming performance appears to depend on the combined effects of hydrolysis degree, structural modification, surface charge, and solubility rather than the extent of hydrolysis alone. However, additional analyses, such as peptide characterization and interfacial microstructure evaluation, are needed to confirm these structure–function relationships.

Effect of ultrasound-assisted enzymatic hyrolysis on antioxidant activity

Enzymatic hydrolysis significantly enhanced the antioxidant properties of luffa protein, while ultrasound-assisted treatment further intensified these effects (Table 1). The native protein concentrate exhibited low intrinsic antioxidant activity, characterized by weak total antioxidant capacity, low DPPH radical scavenging activity (RSA), high IC50 values, and limited metal-chelating ability. This may be attributed to the restricted accessibility of antioxidant amino acid residues within the native protein structure [3].

Enzymatic hydrolysis alone significantly improved total antioxidant capacity and metal-chelating activity (p < 0.05), likely due to increased exposure of electron-donating and metal-binding residues [29], [28]. However, the increase in DPPH RSA remained limited, suggesting that hydrolysis alone was insufficient to maximize radical scavenging activity, consistent with previous reports on lupin and sunflower proteins [18], [34].

Ultrasound-assisted enzymatic treatments produced more pronounced improvements in a power- and time-dependent manner (p < 0.05). At 100 W, antioxidant activity gradually increased from 5 to 25 min, possibly due to the progressive release of antioxidant peptide fractions, as reported for mung bean and rice proteins [37], [23]. At 200 W, all antioxidant parameters increased markedly, with maximum activity observed after 25 min. This treatment yielded the highest radical scavenging and metal-chelating activities and the lowest IC50 value, which may be attributed to intensified cavitation effects promoting structural disruption and enhanced enzymatic hydrolysis. Similar findings have been reported for walnut and chickpea proteins [33], [44].

Ultrasonic cavitation can disrupt protein aggregates and unfold tertiary structures, thereby exposing buried residues and improving enzyme accessibility to cleavage sites [4], [6]. These structural modifications likely contributed to the generation of low-molecular-weight peptides associated with radical scavenging and metal-chelating activities [47], [7]. Ultrasound-induced fragmentation may also alter peptide composition and structural features involved in electron donation and metal coordination [49], [45]. However, because peptide sequencing, fractionation, and structure–activity analyses were not performed, the specific peptides and mechanisms underlying the observed antioxidant activity remain to be elucidated.

The observed power-dependent enhancement agrees with previous studies demonstrating that higher ultrasound intensities promote the release of antioxidant peptides from various protein sources [39], [46]. Similarly, the time-dependent increase in activity may reflect cumulative cavitation effects during treatment [15], [41]. The inverse relationship between IC50 and ultrasonic intensity further suggests that stronger ultrasonic treatment generated hydrolysates with greater radical scavenging potential, consistent with findings reported for pulse proteins and brewer’s spent grain [8], [42].

Molecular weight distribution analysis by SDS-PAGE

The molecular weight distribution of proteins in luffa protein concentrate (LPC) and its hydrolysates was examined by SDS-PAGE under reducing conditions (Fig. 5). Untreated LPC (Lane LPC) displayed a broad range of polypeptides, with prominent bands near 98, 72, 54, 43, 34, 22, and 15 kDa. Based on prior characterization of luffa seed proteins, the bands at 54 and 34 kDa may represent the basic and acidic subunits of the major 11S globulin fraction, while the 22 kDa band may correspond to a 2S albumin [1], [2].

SDS-PAGE analysis of luffa protein concentrate (LPC) and its hydrolysates. Lanes show untreated LPC, conventional enzymatic hydrolysis (E-LPH), and ultrasound-assisted enzymatic hydrolysis (US + E-LPH) at 100 W (L1–L3: 5, 15, 25 min) and 200 W (L4–L6: 5, 15, 25 min). Molecular weight markers (kDa) are indicated.

In contrast, the conventional enzymatic hydrolysate (Lane E-LPH) showed marked structural alteration. High molecular weight bands (>45 kDa) characteristic of native LPC were substantially reduced, confirming extensive proteolytic cleavage. This was accompanied by a strong increase in staining intensity below 15 kDa, indicating accumulation of short peptides [25], [28], which was consistent with the measured DH value.

All ultrasound-assisted hydrolysates (Lanes L1–L6) exhibited electrophoretic patterns similar to the conventional hydrolysate, with complete loss of bands above 45 kDa and most protein material concentrated below 15 kDa. Subtle differences in band intensity within this low molecular weight region were evident across treatments. The sample treated at 200 W for 25 min (L6) showed the most intense staining in the very low molecular weight range, suggesting more extensive proteolysis under higher-intensity sonication. These variations may result from differences in hydrolysis extent and peptide bond cleavage patterns generated by specific ultrasound parameters.

Mechanistically, the enhanced hydrolysis observed at higher ultrasound intensity may be attributed to acoustic cavitation, which produces shear forces capable of disrupting aggregates, decreasing particle size, and increasing enzyme-accessible surface area [20], [43]. This disruption could promote protein unfolding and exposure of buried hydrophobic regions and cleavage sites for Alcalase action [50]. The progressive increase in low molecular weight peptides at 200 W may suggest cumulative cavitational effects that continuously expose additional cleavage sites during prolonged sonication [37]. Conversely, the lack of clear time-dependent changes at 100 W suggests that low-intensity sonication may not induce sufficient structural modification to substantially enhance hydrolysis [32], [13].

Zeta potential

The zeta potential of the enzyme-only Luffa cylindrica protein hydrolysate (E-LPH) was − 15.4 mV, indicating a net negative surface charge at the measurement pH. This suggests that enzymatic hydrolysis exposed more anionic than cationic groups. In contrast, the untreated protein concentrate (LPC) exhibited a positive charge (+11.4 mV), indicating that hydrolysis altered the surface charge characteristics of the protein (Fig. 6). Ultrasound-assisted enzymatic hydrolysis further modified the surface charge, with effects depending on ultrasonic power and treatment time. At 100 W, the most negative zeta potential (−20.59 mV) was observed after 5 min, while longer treatments produced fewer negative values (15 min: −10.5 mV; 25 min: −6.85 mV). At 200 W, the zeta potential shifted from slightly positive (+8.6 mV) at 5 min to strongly negative values at longer durations (15 min: −11.87 mV; 25 min: −17.2 mV).

These results are consistent with previous studies showing that ultrasound-assisted enzymatic hydrolysis often leads to more negative zeta potentials compared with conventional hydrolysis. Such changes are generally attributed to cavitation-induced structural modifications, including protein unfolding and disruption of aggregates, which can expose buried hydrophilic and anionic residues and increase surface charge density [14], [51], [16], [37].

The observed power- and time-dependent behavior also agrees with earlier reports indicating that ultrasound parameters require careful optimization. The decrease in negative charge magnitude during prolonged treatment at 100 W may reflect over-processing effects such as peptide reaggregation or modification of charged groups, which could reduce electrostatic repulsion [23], [17]. In contrast, the initial positive value at 200 W–5 min may indicate limited exposure time for structural unfolding, while longer treatment likely promoted greater exposure of anionic groups, resulting in more negative zeta potential values [35], [20].

In general, higher absolute zeta potential values are associated with stronger electrostatic repulsion and potentially improved colloidal stability. Accordingly, samples such as US 5 min/100 W (−20.59 mV) and US 25 min/200 W (−17.2 mV) may exhibit enhanced dispersion or interfacial stability. Similar relationships between increased negative surface charge and improved functional properties have been reported for ultrasound-modified protein hydrolysates [14], [16], [18], [10]. However, because droplet size distribution, emulsion microstructure, and peptide composition were not analyzed in the present study, direct correlations between zeta potential and functional properties remain speculative and require further investigation.

Structural analysis by FTIR spectroscopy

FTIR spectroscopy (Fig. 7) combined with Amide I deconvolution (Table 2) showed that ultrasound-assisted enzymatic hydrolysis (US + E LPH) induced intensity-dependent modifications in the secondary structure of luffa protein while preserving the characteristic backbone functional groups detectable by FTIR. These changes are likely associated with acoustic cavitation, which can disrupt stabilizing non-covalent interactions and expose previously buried protein regions [14], [16].

FTIR spectra of untreated luffa protein concentrate (LPC), enzymatic hydrolysis (E-LPH), and ultrasound-assisted enzymatic hydrolysis (US + E-LPH) at 100 W (exposure times: 5, 15, 25 min) and 200 W (exposure times: 5, 15, 25 min).

Compared with untreated luffa protein concentrate (LPC), enzymatic hydrolysis alone (E-LPH) reduced random coil content from 20.6% to 7.7% while increasing α-helix content from 23.98% to 33.17%, indicating a shift toward more ordered conformations (Table 2). Ultrasound-assisted hydrolysis further altered these structural features. Most US + E LPH treatments maintained relatively low random coil content (8–14%) while increasing α-helix and β-turn structures, reaching maxima of 36.53% and 34.71%, respectively. These findings suggest that ultrasound promoted disruption and subsequent rearrangement of hydrogen-bonded structures, consistent with previous reports on ultrasound-induced modification of protein secondary motifs [43], [52].

β-sheet content exhibited a power-dependent response. Moderate ultrasound treatment (100 W, 5 min) increased β-sheet structures to 33.11%, whereas high-power treatment (200 W, 5 min) reduced them to 24.08%, possibly reflecting cavitation-induced restructuring of hydrogen-bonded β-sheet domains [9].

The FTIR spectra supported these quantitative observations. Enhanced O–H/N–H stretching bands (3500–3200 cm−1) suggested increased hydrogen bonding and exposure of polar groups, while changes in the C–H stretching region (2920 and 2850 cm−1) indicated rearrangement of hydrophobic domains. Shifts in the Amide I and II regions were consistent with changes in α-helix and β-sheet balance identified by deconvolution analysis. Modifications within the 1200–900 cm−1 region further suggested altered protein–matrix interactions, particularly under prolonged or high-intensity ultrasound treatment.

Overall, ultrasound appeared to accelerate structural rearrangement during enzymatic hydrolysis, with 200 W treatments producing comparable structural modifications within shorter processing times than 100 W treatments. In contrast, enzymatic hydrolysis without ultrasound caused comparatively smaller structural changes, suggesting that ultrasound enhanced enzyme accessibility and promoted conformational reorganization. Although these structural alterations may influence physicochemical and interfacial properties, direct relationships with functional performance or bioactivity cannot be conclusively established without complementary analyses such as particle size distribution, emulsifying characterization, or peptide profiling. Further investigation is therefore required to clarify the functional significance of the observed FTIR changes.

Conclusion

Pulsed ultrasound pretreatment was shown to be an effective strategy for enhancing the enzymatic hydrolysis of Luffa cylindrica seed protein concentrate. Ultrasound increased the degree of hydrolysis within shorter processing times, while higher power and longer treatment durations promoted greater peptide fragmentation, as confirmed by SDS-PAGE analysis. FTIR spectra and zeta potential measurements further indicated ultrasound-induced changes in protein secondary structure and surface charge, which may have influenced enzyme accessibility and dispersion behavior. These structural modifications were accompanied by changes in the functional properties of the resulting hydrolysates. Moderate ultrasound conditions favored improved emulsifying properties and foam capacity, whereas more intensive treatments enhanced foam stability and antioxidant activity. Overall, the findings demonstrate that ultrasound processing conditions can modulate the structural and functional characteristics of luffa protein hydrolysates and may offer a promising approach for tailoring protein ingredients for food applications. However, the peptide sequences responsible for the observed antioxidant activity were not identified. Therefore, further studies involving peptide fractionation and LC–MS/MS analysis are needed to clarify structure–activity relationships and identify bioactive components. In addition, evaluation of process scalability and hydrolysate performance in real food systems would support future industrial application.
