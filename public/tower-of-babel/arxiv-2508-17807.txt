PoRe: Position-Reweighted Visual Token Pruning for Vision Language Models
Kai Zhao 1, Wubang Yuan 1, Alex Lingyu Hung 2, Dan Zeng 1,🖂

Abstract
Vision-Language Models (VLMs) typically process a significantly larger number of visual tokens compared to text tokens due to the inherent redundancy in visual signals. Visual token pruning is a promising direction to reduce the computational cost of VLMs by eliminating redundant visual tokens. The text-visual attention score is widely-adopted criteria for visual token pruning as it reflects the relevance of visual tokens to the text input. However, many sequence models exhibit a recency bias, where tokens appearing later in the sequence exert a disproportionately large influence on the model’s output. In VLMs, this bias manifests as inflated attention scores for tokens corresponding to the lower regions of the image, leading to suboptimal pruning that disproportionately retains tokens from the image bottom. In this paper, we present an extremely simple yet effective approach to alleviate the recency bias in visual token pruning. We propose a straightforward reweighting mechanism that adjusts the attention scores of visual tokens according to their spatial positions in the image. Our method, termed Position-reweighted Visual Token Pruning ( PoRe ), is a plug-and-play solution that can be seamlessly incorporated into existing visual token pruning frameworks without any changes to the model architecture or extra training. Extensive experiments on LVLMs demonstrate that our method improves the performance of visual token pruning with minimal computational overhead. Our code is available at https://github.com/intcomp/PoRe .

Introduction

The recent impressive success of Large Language Models (LLMs)
has led to the development of Vision-Language Models (VLMs)
that extend their reasoning capabilities to
multimodal tasks.
These VLMs
typically include a vision encoder (
\eg
CLIP) that processes visual inputs into
a compact sequence of visual tokens
and
a language model (
\eg
LLaMA)
for answer generation.
To enable the language model to process visual input,
the visual and language modalities are typically aligned using an alignment module,
which may range from a simple linear layer to a multi-layer perceptron
or a more complex query-based network.

Visual signals are typically more redundant than text signals,
resulting in a significantly larger number of visual tokens compared to text tokens.
VLMs often process significantly more visual tokens than text tokens,
leading to high computational costs,
especially with high-resolution images or videos.
Visual token pruning is a promising direction to reduce the computational cost of VLMs
by eliminating redundant visual tokens.

FastV
[
4
]
is an early work that introduces visual token pruning
based on text–visual attention scores.
Specifically, it computes the attention between the final text token and each visual token,
and discards those with the lowest scores. Several subsequent methods
[
6
,
11
,
25
]

follow this paradigm,
employing similar attention-based criteria to prune visual tokens.

Although the text–visual attention score is an intuitive metric for assessing the importance of visual tokens,
a recent study
[
24
]
revealed that it is biased toward later visual tokens in the input sequence
due to the recency bias of sequence models.
As shown in
fig.

1
(a),
the averaged attention scores across visual tokens exhibit a strong bias toward
the bottom of the image.
To address this, the authors proposed a purely vision-based pruning approach that estimates token importance
solely from the visual input. However, in vision–language tasks,
the relevance of visual information is inherently dependent on the text input,
and such vision-only pruning can lead to substantial performance
degradation—particularly when language prompts focus on background regions or other less salient areas of the image.

In this paper, we adopt the text–visual attention paradigm but introduce a simple yet effective remedy to mitigate the recency bias in attention scores
Specifically, we propose a position-aware reweighting strategy that adjusts each visual token’s attention score according to its spatial location in the image.
We begin by computing the average text–visual attention scores
over a large dataset to characterize the recency bias pattern.
Based on this pattern, we derive reweighting factors,
which are then applied to adjust individual token scores prior to pruning.

Our method,
Position-reweighted Visual Token Pruning (
PoRe
),
is a plug-and-play technique
that can be easily integrated into existing visual token pruning methods
that use text–visual attention scores as pruning criteria,
without requiring any modifications to the model architecture nor
additional training.

Related Work

Token pruning for VLMs seeks to remove redundant vision tokens while preserving task accuracy.
Before VLM-specific pruning, token reduction and length compression were explored in NLP.
Dai et al.
[
5
]
compress hidden sequences to trade length for depth efficiently.
Huang et al.
[
9
]
select core token subsets via coreset theory to lower complexity.
Nawrot et al.
[
13
]
pool tokens dynamically to adapt sequence length on the fly.
Rae et al.
[
14
]
compress long-range memories to keep context affordable.

Building on these ideas, VLM works reveal substantial redundancy in early visual tokens.
Chen et al.
[
4
]
propose FastV to rank attention and prune at shallow layers, yielding strong speedups with minor loss.
Moving from fixed to input-aware selection, training-free methods score tokens using task signals.
Zhang et al.
[
26
]
present SparseVLM, a text-guided per-layer ranking that adapts ratios and recycles dropped tokens into compact summaries.
Sun et al.
[
16
]
exploit cross-attention with language queries to prune up to 90% of tokens by mid-layers while preserving performance.
Wang et al.
[
17
]
show visual-encoder [CLS] attention is a strong training-free prior and ensemble it across layers for robust pruning.
To curb distributional drift, calibration based schemes align pre and post pruning signals.
Ye et al.
[
21
]
fit a tiny calibration objective to match attentions,
enabling fast recipes without retraining.
Depthaware strategies then reduce tokens progressively rather than in one shot.
Xing et al.
[
18
]
propose PyramidDrop,
pruning more in shallow blocks and less later for stable accuracy–efficiency trade-offs.
For streaming video,
Zhuang et al.
[
28
]
trim tokens over space and time to maintain decoding speed.
To align kept tokens with user intent,
instruction aware pruning targets query-relevant regions.
Huang et al.
[
7
]
roll out cross-attention and apply group-wise pruning that remains effective even with a frozen vision encoder.
To tailor budgets per input and layer, adaptive policies set ratios dynamically.
Ye et al.
[
22
]
introduce ATP-LLaVA with a light policy and spatial augmentation that preserves structure under aggressive pruning.

Beyond text–visual attention, diversified signals help avoid near-duplicate tokens.
Zhang et al.
[
23
]
rely on visual cues, selecting salient tokens and removing similar ones for steadier accuracy at high pruning.
Alvar et al.
[
1
]
maximize diversity of kept tokens to preserve complementary evidence.
Zhang et al.
[
24
]
maximize conditional diversity with a DPP conditioned on the instruction.
Graph-based viewpoints further explain what to keep and why.
Jiang et al.
[
10
]
show both foreground and background matter and propagate importance on a token graph to select balanced subsets training-free.
Systems-aware designs ensure compatibility with modern kernels and caching.
Yang et al.
[
19
]
optimize a once-per-prefill objective compatible with FlashAttention and KV cache, shrinking memory by reducing cached keys and values.
High-resolution inputs motivate early budgeting to avoid OOM.
Arif et al.
[
2
]
allocate per-partition budgets with [CLS] attention and drop tokens before the LLM at 4K inputs.

External guidance can also steer selection under tight budgets.
Zhao et al.
[
27
]
use a small VLM’s global attention to guide pruning and enable early exit, keeping accuracy with as low as 9% tokens.
Finally, some methods automate policy search or maintain sparsity throughout decoding.
Liang et al.
[
12
]
search for generalizable pruning policies from few samples under structural-risk minimization.
Huang et al.
[
8
]
sparsify vision–language context dynamically so efficiency benefits persist beyond prefilling.

The literature progresses from early attention-ranked pruning toward instruction-,
calibration-, graph-,
diversity- and systems-aware criteria,
with adaptive and systems-ready designs that remain training-free and KV/FlashAttention compatible.
These advances set the stage for methods that explicitly correct positional biases and reweight token importance in a plug-and-play manner.

3
Position-Reweighted Pruning

In this section, we introduce our method for visual token pruning.
We first introduce the text-visual attention criteria for visual token pruning,
and then demonstrate the recency bias in attention scores.
Finally, we present our position-aware reweighting strategy to mitigate this bias.

3.1
Text-Visual Attention for Visual Token Pruning

We follow FastV
[
4
]
and refer to the attention that visual tokens receive
from the final text token as the last attention,
or text-visual attention.
We use
A\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
to denote the attention between the last text token, denoted as
t_{L}
,
and the set of
N
visual tokens
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
Specifically, let
Q_{t_{L}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the query vector of
t_{L}
, and
K_{v_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the key vector of visual token
v_{i}
. The attention score
A_{i}
for
v_{i}
is computed as

A_{i}=\frac{\exp\left(Q_{t_{L}}^{\top}K_{v_{i}}/\sqrt{d}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\left(Q_{t_{L}}^{\top}K_{v_{j}}/\sqrt{d}\right)},\quad i=1,\dots,N.

(1)

The attention scores are averaged across multiple self-attention heads to obtain a single attention score for each visual token. Thus,
A=[A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{N}]^{\top}
represents the final normalized attention distribution from
t_{L}
over all visual tokens.

The attention score can serve as a criterion for token pruning,
as visual tokens receiving lower attention are less relevant to the language input and therefore contribute less to the final output.

3.2
Position-Reweighted Attention Scores

Recent studies show that attention scores in VLMs exhibit a recency bias
[
23
]
,
where later visual tokens receive higher scores, potentially causing important earlier tokens to be overlooked during pruning.
To illustrate this phenomenon,
we plot the average attention score over token index in
fig.

2
(a).
As shown, there is a significant upward trend, indicating that tokens appearing later in the sequence receive progressively more attention.

To decouple content-agnostic recency bias
and the content-dependent attention score,
we decompose the original attention scores into two components:

A_{i}=P_{i}*\hat{A}_{i},

(2)

where
P\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
represents the content-agnostic
recency bias,
and
\hat{A}_{i}
is the desired attention score that
depends solely on the image content.

The recency bias term
P_{i}

is obtained by averaging the attention scores of each visual token across the dataset.
Formally, let
A_{i}^{(m)}
be the attention score of the
i
-th visual token in the
m
-th sample.
The averaged attention
\overline{A}_{i}
is computed as

\overline{A}_{i}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}A_{i}^{(n)},

(3)

where
M
is the total number of samples in the dataset.

(a) illustrates the averaged attention scores at different image positions.

The averaged attention
\overline{A}
is noisy,
so we fit it with a parametric exponential function using least-squares curve fitting.
The fitted exponential function is then taken as the final recency bias.
Notably, the exponential form naturally models the exponential decay of memory.

Finally, we use the recency bias to reweight the total attention
A_{i}
in order to derive the position-reweighted (
PoRe
) attention:

\hat{A}_{i}=A_{i}/P_{i}
.

Experiments

Our preliminary results demonstrate that
PoRe
significantly improves the FastV baseline, as shown in
table

1
. Across all pruning ratios and benchmarks, our position-reweighted pruning consistently outperforms FastV, especially at aggressive pruning ratios (e.g.,
75\%
and
50\%
), where the margin is most pronounced.

When retaining 128 tokens (
77.8\%
), our method achieves higher accuracy on key datasets such as VQA
{}^{\text{V2}}
, GQA, and SQA
{}^{\text{IMG}}
, with comparable computational cost. Overall, position-reweighting effectively mitigates recency bias, preserves informative tokens, and delivers robust accuracy improvements without model changes or retraining.

Conclusion

We introduced a position reweighting mechanism
to compensate for the recency bias in attention-based visual token pruning.
Our preliminary results demonstrate the effectiveness of our method
and highlight the importance of addressing recency bias in visual token pruning.
More comprehensive experiments and analyses will be provided in the forthcoming update.
