Factors influencing turnover intention among midwives: a systematic review
Authors: Kathrin Stoll, Edris Kakemam, Ehsan Zarei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAttrition of midwives is a growing global challenge that threatens the sustainability of maternal and newborn health services. Turnover intention (TI) is a strong predictor of actual workforce exit and often develops long before resignation. Despite increasing research on this topic, evidence on the factors associated with TI among midwives remains fragmented. This systematic review aimed to identify, categorize, and synthesize factors associated with turnover intention among midwives worldwide.MethodsA systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus were searched for quantitative studies published between January 2010 and August 2025. Eligible studies examined at least one factor associated with turnover intention among midwives and reported midwife-specific data. Two reviewers independently screened studies, extracted data, and assessed methodological quality using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for cross-sectional studies. Due to heterogeneity in outcome measures and reported factors, a narrative synthesis was performed. Identified factors were organized using the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model.FindingsA total of 30 studies from 17 countries, encompassing 17,608 midwives, were included in this review. The synthesis identified 121 factor occurrences associated with TI. Guided by the JD-R model, these factors were categorized into three primary domains: Job Demands (e.g., high workload, burnout, work-life conflict, and stressful work environments), Inadequate Job Resources (e.g., low job satisfaction, inadequate salary, lack of managerial support, and restricted autonomy), and Sociodemographic and Personal Factors. The findings indicate that TI is predominantly driven by the exhaustion from excessive job demands coupled with a lack of structural resources.ConclusionTurnover intention among midwives is a complex, multifactorial phenomenon deeply rooted in organizational and systemic structures rather than individual shortcomings. To effectively retain the midwifery workforce, policymakers and healthcare administrators must prioritize systemic improvements, including reducing excessive workloads, enhancing managerial and professional support, and fostering healthier work environments. Future research should focus on longitudinal and interventional studies to evaluate the effectiveness of specific retention strategies.RegistrationThis systematic review was not registered.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-026-01983-3.

Background

Midwives form the foundation of primary health care systems and have a vital role in delivering quality obstetric care and enhancing community health. Their contribution extends beyond reducing maternal and neonatal mortality, as they are essential in advancing sexual and reproductive health and empowering women and adolescent girls. Expanding universal access to high-quality midwifery services not only saves millions of lives annually but also increases rates of vaginal births, improves women’s satisfaction and experiences with care, reduces health inequities and makes reproductive care more cost effective [1–3].

There is growing concern about the global shortage of midwives. According to a recent estimation, nearly 980,000 more midwives are needed worldwide, and without urgent investment to strengthen and retain this essential workforce, achieving the 2030 development goals will be unattainable [4]. One major factor contributing to this shortage is the departure of midwives from their jobs [5]. High rates of turnover intention (TI) are reported among midwives globally [6], with estimates ranging from 40 to 53% in Europe [7–11], 25–67% in North America [12, 13], 40–75% in Africa [14–17], and 43–52% in Australia [18, 19]. Rising midwifery attrition rates weaken the obstetric workforce and compromise clinical safety [5] and disrupts access to and continuity of antenatal, intrapartum, and postnatal care [9, 20], increasing workloads and fatigue among remaining staff. These conditions heighten the risk of medical errors and diminish the quality of person-centered care, ultimately contributing to higher maternal and neonatal mortality [21, 22].

This crisis is largely driven by unfavorable working conditions that contribute to burnout, including heavy workloads, insufficient organizational support, lack of autonomy and professional recognition [23]. Existing research highlights a complex set of interrelated factors that influence midwives’ decisions to leave their profession by contributing to burnout and diminishing psychological well-being [11, 24]. A recent scoping review on the psychological impacts of midwifery work found that burnout, stress, trauma, and moral distress are major contributors to reduced workplace engagement, increased absenteeism, and job turnover. Furthermore, a negative work culture, lack of managerial support, and insufficient professional recognition intensify these challenges. Collectively, occupational stressors such as excessive workloads, limited resources, poor organizational support, and inadequate appreciation and compensation create an environment that undermines midwives’ well-being and drives attrition [5].

TI is defined as a person’s conscious desire or plan to leave their organization or profession in the future and is considered one of the strongest behavioral predictors of actual turnover [11, 12]. Since this intention can develop long before an individual resigns, sometimes months or even years in advance, its early detection provides a vital opportunity for implementing preventive strategies to retain midwives in the profession.

Given the substantial costs of workforce attrition to health systems, understanding the reasons behind midwives’ intentions to leave is crucial for developing effective retention strategies [25]. However, compared with nurses and physicians, midwifery retention has received considerably less research attention [26]. While TI among midwives has been studied regionally, no systematic review has mapped or categorized the associated factors. Therefore, this study aimed to systematically identify, classify, and describe factors associated with TI among midwives worldwide, providing a scientific foundation for evidence-based policymaking. The findings are expected to guide human resource managers and policy makers in designing targeted interventions to reduce TI and strengthen the sustainability of the midwifery workforce.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted as a systematic review of quantitative observational studies examining factors associated with turnover intention (TI) among midwives. The review was designed and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [27].

Research question & framework

The review question was developed using the Participants–Concept–Context (PCC) framework to clearly define the scope and focus of the systematic review. The participants included midwives, the concept comprised factors associated with turnover intention, and the context encompassed healthcare settings worldwide. Based on this framework, the review aimed to answer the following question: “What factors are associated with turnover intention among midwives across healthcare settings worldwide?”

Study inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligibility criteria for study inclusion and exclusion were formulated based on the PCC framework. These criteria clearly define the scope of the review by specifying the characteristics of the participants, the core concept of interest, and the relevant context. Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed in three electronic databases: PubMed, Web of Science (WoS), and Scopus. The search covered studies published from January 1, 2010, to August 31, 2025. Search strategies were developed using combinations of controlled vocabulary terms and free-text keywords related to “turnover intention” and “midwifery.” The full search strategies for all databases are provided in Appendix 1. All retrieved records were imported into EndNote version 20, where duplicate citations were identified and removed.

Study selection

After duplicate removal, two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts to assess potential eligibility. Full texts of potentially relevant articles were then reviewed against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion, and where consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted.

Methodological quality assessment

The quality of the included studies was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for cross-sectional studies [28]. This checklist consists of eight criteria, and each criterion was scored as “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not applicable.” Two reviewers independently performed the quality assessment, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, by involving a third reviewer. In line with common practice in systematic reviews applying the JBI tool, studies were categorized into three levels of methodological quality according to the proportion of “Yes” ratings: low quality (> 70%), moderate quality (50–69%), and high quality (< 49%) [29]. Only studies classified as having low or moderate quality (≥ 50% “Yes” responses) were included in the systematic review.

Data extraction strategy

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardized extraction form. Extracted information included study setting, sample characteristics, study design, measurement of turnover intention, analytical methods, and reported factors associated with turnover intention. Any inconsistencies in data extraction were resolved through discussion.

Data synthesis and presentation

Given the heterogeneity in outcome measurement and reporting of associated factors, findings were synthesized narratively. Data synthesis was guided by the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model [30], which conceptualizes work-related factors as either job demands (aspects of work requiring sustained physical, cognitive, or emotional effort) or job resources (aspects that support goal attainment, reduce job demands, or promote personal development). Factors not directly fitting the occupational framework of this model were grouped under a third domain labelled “sociodemographic and personal factors.” Directed content analysis was applied to code extracted factors into predefined domains of JD-R model [31]. To address the wide variety of reported variables and to manage conceptually overlapping constructs, the analysis followed a rigorous stepwise process:

Results

Study selection

The study selection process is summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). Initially, 624 records were identified. After removing 240 duplicates, 384 records were screened. Screening titles and abstracts excluded 337 records, leaving 47 full-text articles for eligibility assessment. Ultimately, 30 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the synthesis.

Fig. 1PRISMA Flow Diagram

PRISMA Flow Diagram

Study characteristics

Together, these 30 studies encompassed data from 17,608 midwives, with individual sample sizes ranging from 65 to 3,696 participants. The studies, conducted between 2012 and 2025, represented 17 countries. The largest number of studies came from Ethiopia, Australia, and Poland (three studies each). Canada, the Netherlands, Switzerland, Sweden, the United Kingdom, and Hungary each provided two studies, while Spain, Germany, Italy, Nigeria, Senegal, the United States, Indonesia, and Iran were represented by one study each; one investigation was multinational. Of all included studies, 13 were national in scope, while the remainder were conducted at regional or institutional levels. Most studies (n = 28) were cross-sectional, and 2 employed longitudinal designs (Table 2).

Methodological quality of included studies

Quality assessment using the JBI critical appraisal checklist indicated that most studies demonstrated sound methodological quality. Fifteen studies (50%) met nearly all appraisal criteria and were classified as high quality, while the remaining 15 displayed minor methodological limitations but were considered acceptable for inclusion (see Appendix 2).

Factors influencing turnover intention

In total, 121 occurrences of significant factors associated with turnover intention were extracted from the included studies. Following the sub-categorization and refinement process to address overlapping constructs, these occurrences were consolidated into 30 unique factors. These unique factors were subsequently grouped into three main domains: job demands, inadequate job resources, and sociodemographic and personal factors (see Table 3).

Job demands

Job demands encompass physical, psychological, and organizational aspects of work that typically involve sustained effort and are often linked to physiological and psychological costs [30]. Among the factors identified in the reviewed studies, workload and schedule pressures were commonly reported. These included irregular work schedules, extended working hours, and on-call responsibilities [13, 18, 39, 43, 46, 47], as well as high workloads [19, 34, 37, 41, 43, 46].

These time- and task-related demands commonly co-occurred with challenging work environment and climate conditions. Poor working conditions such as concerns about safety were frequently reported [13, 16, 43, 47] and often corresponded with greater effort in fulfilling clinical responsibilities. Interpersonal and role-related pressures amplified the impact of these deficiencies. A negative workplace climate and poor relationships with colleagues or managers [12, 13, 19, 34, 41, 48] created social and emotional demands that undermine support and coping. Role conflict [7] pointed to discrepant or competing expectations that generated additional cognitive and emotional effort.

Additionally, structural factors such as the type of work setting (e.g., hospital vs. community health centers or urban vs. rural) [9, 10, 13, 16, 36, 39] and professional obligations, including mandatory licensing, insurance, and membership fees [13], were associated with ongoing administrative responsibilities that influenced TI.

Taking together, these interrelated demands contributed to profound psychological and emotional strain. Experiences of work-related stress [13, 18, 32, 34, 48], work–private life conflict [11, 13, 25, 42, 47], and deteriorated personal health [7, 11, 13, 43, 44, 48] illustrated how job demands spilled over, depleting recovery resources. Ultimately, this sustained occupational strain manifested in physical and psychological symptoms and culminated in high burnout, the most consistently reported antecedent of TI among midwives [7, 8, 18, 33, 35, 39, 40].

Job resources

Job resources refer to the physical, psychological, social, and organizational aspects of work that help employees achieve their work goals, reduce job demands, or stimulate personal growth and motivation [30]. Across the reviewed studies, midwives reported considerable shortages in organisational and professional resources.

In terms of rewards and development, the most frequently mentioned factors were lower salary satisfaction [13, 16, 26, 37, 42], limited career development and training opportunities [7, 13, 15, 16, 18], and insufficient recognition for professional contributions [7, 10, 18], reflecting diminished motivation and a perceived lack of reward for professional effort.

Regarding professional autonomy and meaning, restricted autonomy and control in clinical decision-making [10, 34], dissatisfaction with the model of practice [8, 11, 13, 19], and job insecurity or part-time employment contracts [9, 15, 16, 38] indicated that organisational structures often limited midwives’ ability to exercise professional judgment. Furthermore, a lack of professional meaning and skill utilisation [40] captured the emotional dimension of depleted resources; when midwives were unable to use their full skills, their sense of purpose diminished.

A lack of organizational and leadership support was another prominent issue. A lack of organisational support [13, 18, 34, 36] and inadequate staffing and resources [7, 41, 48] increased workload pressures. Moreover, ineffective leadership style [36] and dissatisfaction with or conflicts with management [15, 48] reflected hierarchical and communication barriers that contributed to TI.

Collectively, these workplace shortcomings were consistently linked to profound job dissatisfaction and disillusionment. They were reported alongside low job satisfaction [8, 26, 32, 37, 38, 45, 46], and professional dissatisfaction or disillusionment with midwifery [10, 13, 18, 19, 47], which have been frequently identified in the literature as key correlates of turnover intention resulting from insufficient job resources.

Sociodemographic and personal factors

This category included factors that did not fit within the framework of the job demand/resource model, specifically demographic variables and personal goals.

Regarding demographic variables, studies showed mixed associations. Some studies linked younger age [9, 26, 42, 46] to higher TI, while others identified older age [25, 34, 40]as a risk factor for leaving the profession. Job seniority or work experience [9, 25, 32, 34], gender (male) [36, 38], and family status (marital status and parenthood) [19, 25] were also reported.

In terms of personal life and goals, four studies reported individual factors driving midwives away from their current roles, including the desire for a career change [13, 18, 47] and specific personal goals such as marriage, further education, retirement, or travel [13, 16, 47].

Discussion

When findings from the 30 included studies were analyzed and interpreted through the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model, midwives’ turnover intention was consistently observed in contexts marked by structural and organizational challenges. These challenges commonly included excessive workloads, unfavorable working environments, and limited support or resources. Such conditions frequently co-occur with reports of physical, psychological, and family-related strain, as well as experiences of job burnout and low job satisfaction. The factors associated with TI in the current study overlap significantly with findings from studies that examined factors linked to burnout, highlighting that burnout likely mediates the relationship between adverse work conditions and IT [23, 49].

Rather than a unidimensional phenomenon, turnover intention among midwives appears to be associated with a prolonged pattern of imbalance, characterized by persistently high job demands alongside insufficient job resources. In the following section, we first discuss the exhaustion process triggered by excessive job demands, followed by the motivational process linked to inadequate job resources.

The exhaustion process

Continuous exposure to high job demands initiates a cascade that ultimately culminates in midwives leaving their profession. Workload and scheduling factors such as long hours and night shifts emerged as the strongest direct predictors of TI [41, 42, 50, 51]. These stressors are compounded by severe work–life conflict, reported to be more pronounced among midwives than in many other health professions [42, 52]. Such pressures occur within negative and sometimes hazardous work environments, which are heavily influenced by structural factors such as the type of work setting. For instance, hospital settings, which in several studies have been associated with higher TI, are often characterized by heavier workloads and reduced professional autonomy. In contrast, midwives practicing in community settings typically enjoy greater autonomy and report higher levels of job satisfaction [11, 42, 53, 54].

Furthermore, although interpersonal conflict at work does not directly predict TI, it fosters negative emotions and stress that pave the way to exit [41]. Chronic exposure to these demands results in job stress [32] and ultimately burnout [42]; a psychological syndrome of chronic stress characterized by emotional and mental exhaustion [55]. Burnout is commonly measured using the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI), which assesses three dimensions: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment. The Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI) offers an alternative framework, distinguishing between work-related, personal, and client-related burnout [56]. Burnout is particularly consequential: experiencing it nearly quadruples the likelihood of TI [18]. Interestingly, this process is not uniform. Among less experienced midwives, stress contributes to TI primarily through reduced job satisfaction, whereas in more experienced midwives stress leads directly to leaving [32]. Overall, TI is commonly observed in contexts where adaptation to adverse working conditions appears limited, and it frequently co-occurs with professional disengagement, reduced performance, and concerns about potential declines in the quality of care [11].

Erosion of motivation to stay

While the exhaustion process focuses on the damage caused by negative factors, this process highlights the absence of positive ones, which can be described as the erosion of motivation and job satisfaction due to a lack of job resources. An Australian study showed that midwives with low job satisfaction were three times more likely to express TI [18]. Increasing medicalization of maternity care, particularly in hospital settings, has limited midwives’ professional autonomy and their ability to provide woman-centered care, which is a core source of professional meaning [42, 54]. Managerial support plays a critical protective role; midwives who perceive inadequate managerial support are nearly three times more likely to intend to leave [50]. Transformational leadership, which is described as motivational, inspirational, influential, and forward-looking leadership, has been shown to improve employee retention and job satisfaction [57]. Peer support similarly buffers stress and enhances retention [24, 36, 54].

Dissatisfaction with pay is another powerful predictor of TI [50]. Inadequate pay, especially among younger midwives, is not merely a financial concern but also a signal of insufficient recognition and respect for the profession [18]. These problems are further exacerbated by limited opportunities for career development [36]. Midwives often face limited opportunities for career advancement, which can negatively impact job satisfaction, professional identity, and retention. There is an urgent need to create structured and supported career development pathways for midwives in clinical, academic, and leadership areas [58] to support role diversification. Conversely, workplaces rich in resources where trust, recognition, and development opportunities are nurtured foster job satisfaction [32] and strengthen commitment to stay.

Professional disenchantment

As a profound manifestation of inadequate job resources, the phenomenon of disillusionment with midwifery itself emerged as one of the most striking findings, particularly pronounced in Australian studies [18, 19, 47]. This stems from a deep systemic conflict: the increasing medicalization of maternity care reduces professional autonomy and places midwives in constant tension between their professional philosophy (supporting natural birth and women’s empowerment) and the task-oriented, interventionist reality of clinical practice [42]. This mismatch directly generates moral distress [59] and conscientious stress [48], the psychological pain of knowing the ethically correct action but being unable to perform it. Repeated experiences of moral distress gradually erode midwives’ professional identity, pushing them toward leaving the profession [60].

Sociodemographic and personal factors

The two primary processes of exhaustion and erosion of motivation are also moderated by individual sociodemographic factors. Age and work experience also play pivotal roles. Younger and less experienced midwives, particularly those under 30, show greater vulnerability to TI due to financial concerns and perceived imbalance between responsibilities and pay [18, 50]. Leaving work early results in a significant loss of professional potential [5]. Similar patterns are observed among nurses, where younger, single, and newly graduated staff are at higher risk of leave [61]. Factors that were identified in this systematic review, such as lack of support, workload stress, and job dissatisfaction are more commonly associated with TI among early career midwives [24] drawing attention to the importance of implementing retention strategies during the first few years of practice. Gender adds another layer; male midwives are nearly three times more likely to intend to leave compared with their female counterparts [36]. This difference is possibly linked to cultural perceptions of midwifery as a predominantly female profession, particularly in maternity wards [62] or more opportunities to find alternate employment among men.

High- vs. low-resource settings

Cross-country comparisons revealed notable contrasts. In developing countries, TI is primarily driven by “push factors” and the absence of fundamental job resources such as inadequate remuneration, poor working conditions, and lack of job security. In contrast, developed countries are in a better position to mitigate TI by offering competitive salaries, comprehensive benefits, robust support systems, continuous professional development opportunities, and clearly defined career progression pathways [63]. Here, psychological job demands and more complex challenges (e.g., work–life conflict, workplace conflicts, schedule-related stress, and professional disillusionment) become the dominant drivers [64, 65]. These findings underscore the need for context-specific interventions, while TI is a global problem, effective solutions must be tailored to the prevailing challenges in each setting [64].

Strengths and limitations

This study possesses two primary strengths. First, to our knowledge, it is the first systematic review to comprehensively identify and synthesize factors associated with TI among midwives using a clearly defined theoretical framework. Second, the application of the JD-R model enabled the analysis of fragmented findings within a unified conceptual framework, transcending mere descriptive synthesis.

Nevertheless, this study is subject to four limitations. First, the predominantly cross-sectional design of the included studies constrains the ability to establish causal relationships. Second, heterogeneity in the measurement instruments used across studies poses challenges for direct comparison of results. Third, the restriction to quantitative evidence excludes the rich, contextual insights that qualitative studies provide. Finally, the exclusion of grey literature may expose the study to potential publication bias, although comprehensive search strategies were implemented to mitigate this risk.

Policy and research implications

Our findings support a three-pronged strategy for improving midwives’ retention:

Reducing job demands: Health organisations should actively mitigate exhausting pressures by revising midwife-to-women ratios to manage workload [41, 53], implementing flexible scheduling to reduce work–life conflict [51, 66], and fostering supportive work climates to address stress and interpersonal tensions [19, 32, 54, 67]. Given the significant impact of psychological demands and stress on turnover intention identified in our review, organizations should prioritize providing accessible psychological support systems and counseling services for midwives [22].Strengthening job resources: Simultaneously, investments in job resources are needed to enhance motivation and resilience. These include strong and constructive leadership [5, 18, 19, 65, 68], mentoring programs [69, 70], fair and transparent compensation and recognition systems to ensure equity and alignment with professional responsibilities [71], clear career pathways and professional development opportunities [45, 69], and transformation of midwifery practice models to enhance autonomy and promote continuity of care [18]. Neglecting either dimension will likely undermine retention efforts. We excluded studies about midwives’ intention to stay in this review. Timely access to mental health services and the ability to take time off after critical incidents are additional strategies that, if implemented, can reduce burnout, turnover and improve quality of care. Finally, while this systematic review integrates existing knowledge, it also reveals several critical research gaps that warrant attention in future studies. Future research should move beyond descriptive work to longitudinal and interventional designs to test the proposed process model (from job demands/resources through burnout and dissatisfaction to turnover) and to evaluate the effectiveness of targeted interventions such as leadership training or job-redesign strategies.

More research is also needed in under-studied regions (e.g., Asia, Latin America), low resource settings (rural versus urban) and in non-hospital settings. In-depth qualitative studies would further enrich understanding of cultural and organisational nuances and may uncover emergent factors not captured in this review.

Conclusion

This systematic review demonstrates that midwives’ TI is a multifactorial phenomenon embedded in a hierarchical erosion process: structural and organisational pressures increase workload and stress, which extend to physical and psychological problems and work–life conflict, ultimately leading to burnout. Concurrently, deficiencies in organisational resources, including inadequate support, job insecurity, and insufficient compensation undermine job satisfaction and strengthen TI. Personal resources and demographic characteristics further modulate these processes. Addressing this challenge requires urgent reform of working conditions, enhanced psychosocial support, and elevation of the professional status of midwifery to ensure workforce sustainability and the continued delivery of high-quality maternal care.
