A systematic review and meta-analysis of treatment-induced hypertension risks from androgen receptor pathway inhibitors in randomized controlled trials for prostate cancer
Authors: Qianqian Gong, Feng Ge, Tiejiang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAndrogen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs) have revolutionized prostate cancer treatment, but their cardiovascular safety profile requires comprehensive evaluation. This meta-analysis aims to examine the association between ARPI use and hypertension risk in prostate cancer patients.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, and ClinicalTrials.gov for randomized controlled trials comparing ARPIs with control. Pooled risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using random-effects models for all-grade and grade ≥ 3 hypertension. Subgroup analyses were performed by individual ARPI agents and combination therapies.ResultsTwenty-two studies (derived from 21 distinct randomized controlled trials) involving 22,420 patients were included for analysis. ARPIs significantly increased the risk of all-grade hypertension (RR 1.82, 95% CI 1.51–2.21, p < 0.001). Combination therapy with abiraterone plus enzalutamide showed the highest risk (RR 3.46, 95% CI 2.96–4.05), followed by enzalutamide monotherapy (RR 2.20, 95% CI 1.67–2.91) and abiraterone monotherapy (RR 1.46, 95% CI 1.20–1.78). For grade ≥ 3 hypertension, ARPIs significantly increased risk (RR 2.17, 95% CI 1.68–2.81, p < 0.001) with similar subgroup patterns. Darolutamide and apalutamide demonstrated relatively favorable cardiovascular safety profile among monotherapies.ConclusionARPI treatment significantly increases hypertension risk in prostate cancer patients, with substantial variability among specific agents and regimens. Combination therapy poses the highest risk, while darolutamide demonstrated a lower risk profile in this indirect comparison. These findings support routine blood pressure monitoring and aggressive management in patients receiving ARPI therapy, particularly those with pre-existing cardiovascular risk factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16284-9.

Introduction

Prostate cancer treatment has undergone a paradigm shift with the introduction of androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs), including abiraterone, enzalutamide, apalutamide, and darolutamide [1, 2]. These agents have demonstrated significant survival benefits across the disease spectrum, from metastatic hormone-sensitive to castration-resistant prostate cancer [3, 4]. However, as treatment durations extend and patient populations broaden, understanding the complete safety profile of these medications becomes increasingly important [5, 6].

Among the various adverse effects associated with ARPIs, cardiovascular toxicities represent a particularly significant concern [7]. Hypertension has emerged as one of the most common cardiovascular adverse events, with potential implications for patient safety, treatment adherence, and overall cardiovascular health [8, 9]. The pathophysiology of ARPI-associated hypertension is complex and may involve multiple mechanisms, including mineralocorticoid excess with abiraterone, direct vascular effects, and alterations in endothelial function [10].

The clinical significance of treatment-induced hypertension extends beyond mere numerical blood pressure elevations. Uncontrolled hypertension can lead to serious complications including cardiovascular events, cerebrovascular accidents, renal impairment, and treatment discontinuation [11]. For prostate cancer patients, who are typically older adults with multiple comorbidities, the development or exacerbation of hypertension can significantly impact quality of life and overall prognosis [12, 13].

While individual clinical trials have reported hypertension as an adverse event, the magnitude of risk varies considerably across studies and different ARPI agents [14–16]. A comprehensive synthesis of available evidence is necessary to quantify this risk accurately and inform clinical decision-making. Furthermore, understanding the differential risk profiles among various ARPIs and combination regimens can guide drug selection and monitoring strategies, particularly for patients with pre-existing cardiovascular conditions.

While major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) represent critical hard endpoints in cardio-oncology safety assessment, the present meta-analysis focuses specifically on hypertension for several reasons. First, hypertension is the most frequently reported cardiovascular adverse event across ARPI trials, offering a more robust dataset for quantitative synthesis. Second, variability in MACE definitions and reporting practices across trials limits the feasibility of a reliable pooled analysis. Third, hypertension serves as an early, modifiable risk factor that, if adequately managed, may mitigate subsequent cardiovascular complications. Therefore, elucidating the hypertensive risk profile of ARPIs provides immediately actionable insights for clinical monitoring and preventive care. Our systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to quantitatively synthesize evidence from randomized controlled trials to determine the association between ARPI use and hypertension risk in prostate cancer patients, with particular emphasis on differential effects among specific agents and the clinical implications of these findings.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

We conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [17]. A comprehensive literature search was performed across PubMed, Web of Science, and ClinicalTrials.gov. from inception through December 2025. The search strategy incorporated relevant keywords and medical subject headings related to "prostate cancer," "androgen receptor pathway inhibitors," "abiraterone," "enzalutamide," "apalutamide," "darolutamide," "hypertension," "blood pressure," and "randomized controlled trials."

Eligibility criteria included: (1) phase 2 or 3 randomized controlled trials; (2) enrollment of adult men with prostate cancer; (3) comparison of an ARPI (abiraterone, enzalutamide, apalutamide, or darolutamide) or their combinations with control (placebo or standard care); (4) reported specific data on all-grade and/or grade ≥ 3 hypertension incidence according to standardized criteria. We excluded non-randomized studies, phase 1 trials, reviews, meta-analyses, and studies without relevant hypertension data.

It should be noted that all included trials evaluated ARPIs in combination with androgen deprivation therapy (ADT). The intervention groups received ADT plus one ARPI agent (referred to as “ARPI monotherapy” in this analysis) or ADT plus combination ARPI therapy, while control groups received ADT alone or ADT plus placebo. The term “monotherapy” thus denotes the use of a single ARPI agent in addition to ADT, not ARPI used without ADT.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two investigators independently screened all identified records according to predefined selection criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus or third investigator. Using a standardized data extraction form, we collected information on study characteristics, patient demographics, intervention details, and hypertension outcomes.

Methodological quality was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2) [18], evaluating five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of reported results. Studies were categorized as having "low risk," "some concerns," or "high risk" of bias for each domain.

Statistical analysis

We calculated risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for hypertension incidence between ARPI and control groups. Primary outcomes were pooled RRs for all-grade hypertension and grade ≥ 3 hypertension. Given expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity, we employed random-effects models using the DerSimonian and Laird method for all meta-analyses.

Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, with values of 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Between-study variance was estimated using tau-squared (τ2). Subgroup analyses were performed based on specific ARPI agents and combination therapies. Tests for subgroup differences examined whether effect sizes differed significantly across subgroups. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger's test. A p value < 0.05 indicates significant statistical risk of publication bias. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.2.1 with the 'meta' and ‘metafor’ packages.

Our systematic search identified 11,167 records from 3 databases. After removing duplicates, we screened 5,698 records by title and abstract. Of these, 426 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 22 studies (representing 21 distinct clinical trials) [14–16, 19–37] involving 22,420 patients met inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Risk of bias assessment

Methodological quality assessment using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool is presented in Supplemental Table 1. Most studies demonstrated low risk of bias in randomization process, missing outcome data, selection of reported results, and overall risk of bias. Some concerns were noted in several studies, primarily in the domains of deviations from intended interventions and outcome measurement, mainly due to open-label designs in some trials.

3.5. Sensitivity analysis and heterogeneity exploration.

To explore the robustness of the pooled results and the sources of substantial heterogeneity, we conducted leave-one-out sensitivity analyses for both primary outcomes. For all-grade hypertension, omitting any single study did not meaningfully alter the pooled estimate, with the RR remaining highly stable between 1.75 and 1.89 (p < 0.001) (Supplemental Fig. 1). Similarly, the sensitivity analysis for grade ≥ 3 hypertension confirmed the stability of the pooled RR, which ranged from 1.97 to 2.26 (p < 0.001) (Supplemental Fig. 2). To explore the substantial heterogeneity, we performed an exploratory analysis omitting the entire combination therapy (abiraterone plus enzalutamide) subgroup. This targeted exclusion meaningfully reduced the I2 from 87.4% to 77.5% for all-grade, and from 68.7% to 32.5% for grade ≥ 3 hypertension, quantitatively identifying the intensified treatment regimen as a primary driver of the observed heterogeneity.

Publication bias

Funnel plots for all-grade (Supplemental Fig. 3) and grade ≥ 3 (Supplemental Fig. 4) hypertension showed generally symmetrical distributions, suggesting low likelihood of substantial publication bias. Egger's test results were non-significant for both outcomes, with p-values of 0.86 for all-grade hypertension and 0.10 for grade ≥ 3 hypertension, supporting the absence of significant publication bias.

Discussion

This comprehensive meta-analysis included 21 RCTs (reported across 22 publications) involving over 20,000 patients, making it the largest sample size study to date that covers all four types of ARPI and has the most comprehensive disease stages (from nmHSPC to mCRPC) for quantitative assessment of ARPI-related hypertension risks. Although the association between ARPI and hypertension has been reported before, this study still provides additional incremental evidence: for the first time, it evaluates the exploratory subgroup differences among four ARPI monotherapies and combination regimens within the same framework, providing a quantitative basis for drug selection for high-risk patients; it provides additional insights of ineffective combination regimens, revealing the upper limit of cardiovascular costs from dual androgen pathway inhibition, serving as a preliminary reference for future safety assessment of combination therapies; and it proposes mechanism-oriented differentiated blood pressure management strategies, making this study have both risk identification and clinical practice guidance value.

The significantly elevated hypertension risk across all ARPIs underscores the importance of this adverse effect as a class effect. However, the extraordinary variability between treatment regimens is clinically remarkable. The point estimates of hypertension risk varied by treatment regimen. Notably, the combination of abiraterone plus enzalutamide (a strategy not endorsed in current guidelines due to a lack of demonstrated survival benefit) served as a critical benchmark, demonstrating an approximately 3.5-fold increased risk. While this precludes its clinical use, this finding serves as a critical scientific benchmark. It demonstrates the profound additive cardiovascular stress achievable through dual potent androgen pathway inhibition, underscoring the importance of rigorous cardio-oncology assessment in the development of any future combination therapies targeting this axis. In contrast, darolutamide monotherapy was associated with the most favorable profile (less than twofold increased risk), while enzalutamide and abiraterone monotherapies occupied intermediate positions (approximately 2.4-fold and 1.7-fold increases, respectively). This wide spectrum underscores the significant variability in the cardiovascular safety profiles of different ARPIs and argues against considering hypertension risk as a uniform class effect.

The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying ARPI-associated hypertension likely differ between agents. For abiraterone, hypertension is primarily attributed to mineralocorticoid excess due to CYP17A1 inhibition, leading to sodium retention, volume expansion, and subsequent blood pressure elevation [38]. This mechanism explains the efficacy of concomitant corticosteroid administration in mitigating hypertension risk with abiraterone. For enzalutamide and apalutamide, the mechanisms are less clear but may involve direct effects on vascular function, endothelial regulation, and possibly interactions with other blood pressure control systems [39]. Darolutamide's distinct chemical structure and limited penetration into certain tissues may contribute to its more favorable cardiovascular profile [40, 41].

An important nuance in interpreting the risk profile of abiraterone is the universal co-administration of prednisone (or prednisolone) in clinical trials, typically at 5–10 mg daily, to prevent mineralocorticoid excess. While this corticosteroid is essential for the drug’s safety profile, it may itself influence blood pressure. Our analysis compared abiraterone + prednisone against placebo + prednisone, thereby estimating the incremental risk attributable to abiraterone over a constant prednisone background. Consequently, the reported risk ratio for abiraterone (RR 1.74 for all-grade hypertension) should be interpreted as the net effect of the abiraterone-prednisone combination relative to prednisone alone, rather than the effect of abiraterone in isolation. This contextualizes its risk between enzalutamide (no routine steroid) and the lower-risk darolutamide.

Another layer of complexity arises from the varying therapeutic backbones across trials. Notably, in studies like ENZAMET and ARASENS, the experimental and control arms both included docetaxel chemotherapy in addition to ADT (with or without an ARPI). Our analysis indicates that the significant increase in hypertension risk associated with ARPIs persisted even within this more intensive treatment context. This suggests that the hypertensive effect of ARPIs is a robust phenomenon, manifesting independently of, or additively to, the cardiovascular profile of concomitant cytotoxic therapy. It reinforces the message that vigilance for hypertension is necessary across all treatment intensities involving ARPIs, and that the risk is primarily driven by the ARPI component rather than being entirely attenuated or explained by the chemotherapy backbone.

While the pooled analysis demonstrates a robust association between ARPIs and hypertension, the substantial statistical heterogeneity (I2 up to 87.4%) warrants careful interpretation. Beyond the specific ARPI regimen, several clinical variables contribute to the observed heterogeneity. First, the included trials encompass a broad continuum of disease stages, ranging from nmHSPC to mCRPC. Patients across this spectrum inherently possess differing baseline cardiovascular risk profiles, ages, and cumulative exposures to prior therapies, all of which can influence their susceptibility to treatment-induced hypertension [42]. Second, variations in therapeutic backbones introduce additional clinical complexity. For instance, in trials such as ARASENS and PEACE-1, patients in both the control and experimental arms received concurrent docetaxel chemotherapy. Although our subgroup analyses indicate that the hypertensive effect of ARPIs persists independently of cytotoxic therapy, the physiological stress and concomitant medications (such as prophylactic steroids) associated with different background regimens may still cause fluctuating hypertension incidence rates across studies [43]. Third, the median follow-up duration across the included trials varied extensively. Because treatment-induced hypertension can be a cumulative adverse event, trials with prolonged observation periods are naturally poised to capture a higher incidence of delayed hypertensive events, further contributing to the variability of the pooled estimates [44].

The clinical implications of these findings are substantial. First, pretreatment cardiovascular risk assessment should be routine before initiating ARPI therapy, with particular attention to existing hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and other risk factors [45]. Second, blood pressure monitoring should be performed regularly during treatment, with frequency tailored to the specific ARPI agent and patient's baseline risk. Third, aggressive blood pressure management should be implemented when hypertension develops, with consideration of appropriate antihypertensive agents based on the suspected mechanism.

For patients developing significant hypertension on ARPI therapy, several management strategies can be considered [46, 47]. For abiraterone-associated hypertension, ensuring adequate corticosteroid coverage and using mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists may be particularly effective. For enzalutamide and apalutamide, conventional antihypertensive agents may be appropriate. In severe cases, dose modification, treatment interruption, or switching to an alternative ARPI with a better cardiovascular profile may be necessary.

The differential hypertension risk among ARPIs should inform treatment selection, particularly for patients with pre-existing cardiovascular conditions or multiple risk factors. For high-risk patients, in light of its relatively lower risk ratio in this analysis, darolutamide warrants consideration for patients with high cardiovascular risk, and this observation requires validation through direct head-to-head trials. Conversely, for patients requiring combination therapy for disease control, proactive hypertension management should be anticipated and implemented.

Our study has several limitations. First, since the analysis was not an individual patient data (IPD) meta-analysis, we could not perform stratified analyses based on key baseline characteristics such as pre-existing cardiovascular disease history. This limits our ability to identify which patient subgroups (e.g., those with vs. without prior hypertension or heart disease) are at the highest risk for ARPI-induced hypertension, which is critical for personalized risk mitigation strategies. Furthermore, our subgroup analyses comparing different ARPI agents are strictly exploratory and based on indirect evidence across separate trials. Because we did not perform a formal network meta-analysis (NMA), and given the absence of head-to-head randomized data among these agents, these findings should not be interpreted as a definitive 'risk hierarchy.' Differences in patient baselines and trial designs preclude making strong comparative claims regarding the superiority or safety of one specific agent over another. Therefore, the varying risk estimates observed among the monotherapies should be treated as hypothesis-generating. Second, although all included trials utilized the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) [48] to grade adverse events, there is inherent variability across trials regarding the specific assessment methods, monitoring frequencies, and reporting thresholds for hypertension. Adverse event reporting in oncological trials typically relies on routine, intermittent in-clinic blood pressure measurements rather than standardized 24-h ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) or strict home-monitoring protocols. Furthermore, differences in the frequency of vital sign assessments across trial protocols, as well as subjective variability in distinguishing transient blood pressure spikes from sustained hypertension, may lead to inconsistent reporting. This methodological heterogeneity introduces potential observation bias, which could affect the comparability of hypertension incidence rates across different studies and limit the precision of our pooled estimates. Consequently, our findings should be interpreted as pragmatic, trial-based estimates rather than exact epidemiological incidences. Third, the limited number of studies for some ARPIs, particularly apalutamide and combination therapies, resulted in wider confidence intervals for some estimates. Fourth, most included patients were clinical trial participants who may not fully represent real-world populations with more comorbidities and concomitant medications. Fifth, it is important to acknowledge that our analysis focused on hypertension rather than composite cardiovascular endpoints such as MACE. While hypertension represents a common and clinically manageable toxicity, MACE outcomes—including myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiovascular death—carry greater prognostic significance. Future studies should prioritize standardized collection and reporting of MACE to better characterize the long-term cardiovascular safety of these agents. In the interim, our findings on hypertension risk provide a pragmatic basis for enhanced monitoring and early intervention in patients receiving ARPI therapy.

Several important research gaps remain. Future studies should investigate the long-term cardiovascular outcomes associated with ARPI-induced hypertension, identify patient factors predicting increased vulnerability, develop optimal management algorithms, and evaluate the impact of different antihypertensive strategies on both blood pressure control and cancer outcomes. Additionally, prospective comparative studies directly evaluating cardiovascular safety among different ARPIs would provide more definitive guidance for clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis demonstrates that ARPI therapy significantly increases hypertension risk in prostate cancer patients, with substantial variability among different agents and regimens. Based on our indirect comparison, combination therapy was associated with the highest risk of hypertension, whereas darolutamide showed a relatively lower risk profile among the evaluated monotherapies. Our results should inform shared decision-making, with treatment selection individualized based on the patient's cardiovascular risk profile and tolerance for potential adverse effects. Implementation of routine blood pressure monitoring and aggressive management strategies is essential for prostate cancer patients receiving ARPIs, particularly those receiving high-risk regimens or with pre-existing cardiovascular conditions. Future head-to-head studies are needed to provide more definitive evidence for clinical drug selection.
