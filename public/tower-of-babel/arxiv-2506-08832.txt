Evidence for a sign change of the ISW effect in the very recent universe: hot voids and cold overdensities at z < 0.03 z<0.03
Frode K. Hansen, Diego Garcia Lambas, Andrés N. Ruiz, Facundo Toscano, Luis A. Pereyra

Abstract
We find that CMB photons passing through local voids ( z < 0.03 z<0.03 ) are hotter than expected at the 2.7 − 3.6 ​ σ 2.7-3.6\sigma level. Combined with earlier findings showing a > 5 ​ σ >5\sigma cooling of CMB photons in galactic filaments in the same redshift range, we now have possible evidence for a negative Integrated Sachs-Wolfe (ISW) effect in the very recent universe. In addition to having opposite sign, the observed amplitude is an order of magnitude larger than the predicted Rees-Sciama and ISW effects for the nearby universe. An altered growth of gravitational potentials at very low redshift, as predicted by some dark energy and modified gravity models, could give rise to the observed sign change. We discuss the results in light of the latest Data Release 2 results of the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) showing evidence for dynamical dark energy. When removing the CMB quadrupole, we find the temperatures measured in voids to a large degree uncorrelated with the temperature measured in galaxies and the observed mean difference of 41 ​ μ 41\mu K between void and galaxy temperatures is about 6.5 ​ σ 6.5\sigma larger than found in simulations.



1
Introduction

In
Luparello et al. 2023
;
Hansen et al. 2023
;
Garcia Lambas et al. 2024
;
Cruz et al. 2024
;
Hansen et al. 2025
;
Toscano et al. 2024
, substantial evidence for an unexpected cooling of CMB photons passing through galactic halos in nearby (
z<0.02
) massive galactic filaments was shown (this is now also confirmed at the
>3\sigma
level for
0.02<z<0.04
in Dominguez Feldmann et al. 2025, in preparation). Looking particularly at late type spiral galaxies, the temperature of the CMB fluctuations was shown to be
27\mu
K lower than the mean, more than
5\sigma
below expected temperature fluctuations in simulations. In
Hansen et al. 2025
, we speculated on a possible explanation in terms of an interaction of CMB photons with dark matter. Here we look at an alternative explanation in terms of changing gravitational potentials.

In the well-known Rees-Sciama (RS) effect
(
Rees & Sciama 1968
)
, photons passing through the changing gravitational well of an overdensity during non-linear growth of matter perturbations, gain energy in entering the well, but loose more energy exiting, due to the well growing as the photons pass. A cooling of these photons is therefore expected, but the predicted RS-effect is more than an order of magnitude smaller than what we observe
(
Maturi et al. 2007
;
Cai et al. 2010
)
. A similar, but opposite effect, the Integrated Sachs-Wolfe (ISW) effect
(
Sachs & Wolfe 1967
)
, takes place when dark energy causes accelerated expansion of the universe and therefore causing an opposite change in the potentials, giving rise to heating of CMB photons in overdensities and cooling in underdense regions. The latter effect has been detected at higher redshifts
z\gtrsim 0.1
through cross-correlating CMB maps with tracers of large scale structure (see
Krolewskia & Ferraroc 2025
;
Dong et al. 2023
for recent estimates and an extensive list of references).

At the redshifts were we have observed cooling of CMB photons in galactic halos at the order of
20-30\mu
K, the expected amplitude of the RS-effect (about
1\mu
K) is far too small to explain the observations. Furthermore, as shown in
Cai et al. 2010
, the ISW effect is expected to be dominating over the RS effect for structures in the nearby universe (at the
2-4\mu
K level). One would therefore expect a heating and not a cooling of photons passing through nearby potential wells. The latest results of the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI)
(
DESI Collaboration 2025
;
Lodha et al. 2025
)
have shown evidence for dynamical dark energy and an unexpected behaviour of the dark energy equation of state during the recent history of the universe. The equation of state and behavior of dark energy at redshifts lower than considered by DESI is therefore unknown. Here we speculate if this may open the possibility for a negative ISW-effect where the growth of the gravitational potentials are amplified during the recent (
z\lesssim 0.1
) universe, giving rise to cooling of CMB photons passing through gravitational potentials. As we will discuss later, some theories for dark energy predict ISW effects which change sign
(
Hlozek et al. 2015
;
Renk et al. 2017
)
.

The hypothesis of a possible negative ISW effect in the very recent universe could be supported by the fact that the observed cooling appears weaker in elliptical galaxies residing in clusters of galaxies (
Luparello et al. 2023
, H2025) which are virialised at an earlier stage. If this hypothesis is true, we should also see the opposite effect, the heating of CMB photons passing through local voids. In this paper, we will test the hypothesis, identifying all low redshift voids (
z<0.03
) and compare the mean Planck CMB temperature in the areas of these voids to the corresponding temperature in simulations.

2
Data and Method

Our main aim in this paper is to measure the mean CMB temperature in the areas of the sky corresponding to the projected voids of the nearby universe. We use CMB intensity maps taken by the Planck satellite
1
1

1

http://pla.esac.esa.int/pla
. Both data from the Public Release 3 (PR3)
(
Planck Collaboration, I 2020
)
as well as the recent Planck Public Release 4 (PR4)
(
Planck Collaboration, intermediate results LVII 2020
)
will be used. The Planck data were cleaned by 4 different foreground methods as described in
Planck Collaboration, IV 2020
. In general the temperature maps for all 4 foreground methods agree very well, and in H2025 we made a thorough study of the differences on the CMB cooling in galactic halos, showing only tiny differences. For this reason, we will here focus on two methods, SMICA for PR3 as 10 000 simulated maps for this methods were made available, and SEVEM for PR4 since this was the only method providing a substantial number of simulations (600) for that release. We use the Planck common mask created for PR3
(
Planck Collaboration, IV 2020
)
to mask possible galactic residuals, leaving
78\%
of the sky available for analysis.

For identifying voids, we use the 2MASS Redshift Survey (2MRS)
2
2

2

http://tdc-www.harvard.edu/2mrs/
redshift catalogue
(
Huchra et al. 2012
)
. We aim to study the voids within a similar redshift range,
z=[0,0.02]
as in H2025, but in order to include more distant voids overlapping with this range, and to increase the statistics, we use all voids in the range
z=[0,0.03]
. The voids were identified with the public void finder
Sparkling
3
3

3

https://gitlab.com/andresruiz/Sparkling

(
Ruiz et al. 2015
;
Ruiz et al. 2019
)
using as tracers all 2MRS galaxies with absolute
K
-band magnitudes
M_{K}<-23.93
, which constitute a volume limited sample at
z=0.03
assuming
h=0.674
. Briefly, voids are identified as spherical regions with an integrated density contrast satisfying
\Delta(R_{\rm void})<-0.9
, being
R_{\rm void}
the void radius, which means that voids contains only the
10\%
of the density of tracers. The algorithm performs an iterative re-centering procedure to guarantee that void centers are located as close as possible to the local minima of density field, and also a cleaning procedure retaining the largest non-overlapping voids while removing the smallest ones.

We identified a total number of 193 voids in this redshift range. The number of voids is clearly much smaller than the number of galaxies, giving considerably poorer statistics in this case. We further expect an RS-like effect to be much stronger for larger voids (in the same way as we found that larger galaxies give rise to stronger cooling
(
Luparello et al. 2023
;
Hansen et al. 2023
)
). We will therefore make two samples where we look at the voids with a void radius larger than the median size of the sample (
R_{\mathrm{void}}>14
Mpc) and the 1/4 largest voids of the sample (
R_{\mathrm{void}}>16.5
Mpc). We will only consider voids where the mean density contrast out to 2-3 void radii,
\Delta_{23}
(defined as the maximum value of the integrated density contrast between 2 and 3 void radii), is negative (in the following, we will call these ’open voids’), meaning that the voids are surrounded by an underdense region and not by a shell of matter. We know that the latter voids may be shrinking in contrast to the open voids which will normally be expanding during non-linear growth of perturbations
(
Sheth & van de Weygaert 2004
;
Ceccarelli et al. 2013
;
Paz et al. 2013
)
. The total number of open voids is 78.

The angular radius of these voids vary between
\sim 6^{\circ}
to about
\sim 30^{\circ}
with a median of
\sim 10^{\circ}
. Using the full angular extension of these voids to calculate the mean void temperature, a substantial part of the sky would be used and most projected void areas will overlap with neighbouring voids. We need to reduce the radius we use for measuring the void temperature, making a tradeoff between not covering too much area at the same time maintaining sufficient statistics. Our main results will be produced using the mean void temperatures within half the void radius which reduces overlap to a minimum (all results are estimated using this radius unless otherwise stated), but we will also quote results for 1/4 and 3/4 void radius. We use the term ’void temperature’ to denote these mean temperatures of the CMB fluctuations taken over the voids.

Depending on the identification criteria used, voids can have different shapes
(
Colberg et al. 2008
;
Paillas et al. 2018
)
, but on average are well described as spherical volumes
(
Sheth & van de Weygaert 2004
;
Demchenko et al. 2016
;
Wojtak et al. 2016
)
. Our finder algorithm assumes voids as regions with spherical shape, therefore we apply a symmetric wavelet transform, the Spherical Mexican Hat Wavelets
(
Martínez-González et al. 2002
, SMHW)
, to the CMB fluctuation map in order to amplify spherical structures on the sky. Our main results will be based on the wavelet transformed map with a wavelet scale of
4^{\circ}
corresponding to an angular scale of
\sim 10^{\circ}

(
Vielva et al. 2004
)
which agrees with the median angular extension of the voids. All results are based on this wavelet scale, unless otherwise stated. We will also test our results with slightly smaller or larger scales and with the original temperature map.

A main worry when measuring the void temperature in Planck data where we already know that the CMB temperature around galaxies is much colder than expected, is a possible anti-correlation between the temperature measured in voids and the temperature measured around galaxies. Voids are generally in a part of the sky where there are few galaxies and cold galaxy temperatures could therefore increase the possibility of hot void temperatures. In the results, we will show that such an anti-correlation does indeed exist in simulated maps with a quadrupole present. Removing the quadrupole, the correlation is reduced to
2-4\%
depending on the void sample used. We will therefore always remove the quadrupole estimated outside the Planck common mask from Planck data and all simulated CMB maps. The quadrupole is estimated inverting the coupling matrix of the
a_{\ell m}
coefficients as described in detail in H2025.

For tests where we use the mean temperature around galactic halos, we will, unless otherwise stated, use the sample described in H2025 based on large, late type spiral galaxies in the redshift range
0.004<z<0.02
in the three areas with the most massive nearby cosmic filaments described in detail in H2025. In the following, we use the term ’galaxy temperature’ to denote the mean temperature of the CMB fluctuations taken over these galactic halos. Note that for all estimates of galaxy temperature, we always remove the first five multipoles of the CMB map to reduce signal to noise as detailed in H2025.

We have shown that the CMB fluctuations around galaxies are colder than expected and the CMB temperature around voids are hotter than expected. In Figure

A Gaussian fit to the temperature differences is drawn as a red line in the figure and shows that the distribution is close to Gaussian but slightly skewed. As an approximate measure of significance, assuming a nearly Gaussian distribution, the temperature difference in the data is

4
Discussion and Conclusion

Based on the findings of H2025 where CMB temperatures are significantly colder around nearby galactic halos than expected in the standard
\Lambda
CDM model, we put forward the hypothesis that this could be caused by recent changes in the gravitational potential evolution, giving rise to a negative Integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect. Such a hypothesis predicts that not only are the CMB photons passing through overdensities cooled, but CMB photons passing through underdensities would be heated. We test the hypothesis by measuring the mean temperature of the CMB temperature as measured by the Planck satellite in all nearby voids. We have shown that the mean CMB temperature in these voids is indeed larger than expected at the
2.7-3.6\sigma
level. The larger the void radius, the larger the detection. The difference is more significant in SMHW wavelet coefficients, something which is also expected given the spherical shape of voids. We also estimate the temperature difference between the mean temperature around galactic halos and the mean temperature in voids and find a temperature difference which is
\sim 6.5\sigma
larger in the data as compared to 10 000 simulated maps, assuming a Gaussian distribution of temperature differences in simulations.

In the introduction, we already mentioned our worry of a possible expected correlation between galaxy temperatures and void temperatures. Due to the large scale fluctuations of the CMB, a simulation with cold galaxy temperatures could have a higher probability of having hot void temperatures. For testing correlations and consistencies, we will in the following focus on these following cases:

•

For the voids: (A) our main result for 1/2 void radius and
R_{\mathrm{void}}>16.5
Mpc and (B) the largest detection with 3/4 void radius for all voids with
\Delta_{23}<0
. In both cases results are based on the wavelet transformed map.

•

For the galaxies: (C) our main result of H2025 with large, late type spiral galaxies in massive cosmic filaments within
0.004<z<0.0.02
, and (D) all large, late type spiral galaxies within
0.004<z<0.02
.

We will first test correlations between the mean temperature of (1) galaxy sample C which is the sample giving the largest detection in H2025 and (2) the two void samples A and B which show the largest detections in table
1
. Not removing the quadrupole from the maps before estimating the void temperature, we find a
\sim 40\%
anti-correlation between void and galaxy temperatures in simulated maps. Removing the quadrupole, we find a
1.9\%
anti-correlation between case C and A (our main result) and a
3.2\%
anti-correlation between case C and B (the most significant result). These correlations are far too small to explain the excess void temperatures.

To further test whether the observed hot void temperatures could be a result of an anti-correlation with cold galaxy temperatures, we have also chosen to look at the 100 simulations (out of 10 000) with the coldest temperature in the galaxy regions and the 100 simulations with the hottest temperature in the galaxy regions. For these two sets of 100 simulations, we have plotted the histograms of their corresponding void temperatures shown in Figure
4
. If a correlation exists between extreme galaxy temperatures and void temperatures, one would expect to see a significant difference between these two histograms.

For this test, we did not use case C which is the main detection of H2025. The reason for this is that for this case, none of the 10 000 simulations have galaxy temperatures as cold as the real Planck data. To ensure that the hot void temperatures are not a result of extremely cold galaxy temperatures, we need a case where some simulations show colder galaxy temperatures than the data, while the data still have significantly cold temperatures. This is the case for sample D which has been used for selecting the two sets of 100 simulations used in the histograms. Although in the right histogram, three of the simulations with cold galaxy temperatures also show slightly hot void temperatures, the void temperatures are far from the ones observed in the data. Moreover for the largest voids in the left histogram, a simulation with hot galaxy temperatures is also the one with the highest void temperature. The two distributions of void temperatures taken from the simulations with the
1\%
coldest and the
1\%
hottest galaxy temperatures agree to a large extent. We find no evidence for an explanation of hot void temperatures based on correlations with cold galaxy temperatures. The two findings appear independent.

We have found that CMB photons passing through voids are much hotter than expected whereas CMB photons passing through the overdensities in the galactic halos are much colder than in simulations. This is the opposite of the predicted ISW effect in the local universe
(
Cai et al. 2010
)
due to a cosmological constant, but consistent with what one would observe if an unknown mechanism makes the gravitational potentials in the recent universe (
z\lesssim 0.1
) increase, causing a negative ISW effect. At high redshifts (
z\gtrsim 0.1
) the decreasing potentials giving rise to the positive ISW effect consistent with a cosmological constant has been detected at the
3-4\sigma
level and in
Francis & Peacock 2010
the correlation between the integrated projected mass density from 2MASS in the redshift range
z=[0,0.1]
using photometric redshifts and WMAP CMB data was found to be within the expectation for the ISW effect at large scales. However, recent results from DESI
(
DESI Collaboration 2025
;
Lodha et al. 2025
)
show evidence for dynamical dark energy and possible phantom crossing at higher
z
. Note also
Kovács et al. 2022
where a negative ISW effect is claimed also for
z>1.5
.

The DESI results seem to indicate that the properties and evolution of the dark energy may be poorly understood. Could unknown properties of dark energy give rise to strongly increasing gravitational potentials at small redshifts? An example of a theory which predicts such a behaviour is given in
Hlozek et al. 2015
. In that work, ultra light axions play the role of dark energy at early times and behave like dark matter in recent times thereby increasing the potentials and giving rise to the ISW effect changing sign as shown in their Figure 5. Also in theories of Galilean gravity such a sign change can occur
(
Barreira et al. 2014
;
Renk et al. 2017
)
. Further work is necessary to test whether these theories or similar mechanism are fully consistent with the observed signal and other cosmological observations.

The amplitude of the ISW effect at larger redshift is normally calculated by cross-correlating the maps of large scale sctructure, often represented as maps of galaxy number density, with the CMB fluctuations, assuming linear evolution of density perturbations. Due to the proximity of structures at
z<0.03
, such a cross-correlation would require detailed knowledge of the density field at non-linear scales since individual galaxies and voids here occupy a substantial number of pixels, often spanning several degrees on the sky. A more accurate comparison with the expected ISW effect is therefore deferred to future study as it requires calculating the ISW effect using a detailed model of the nearby density field.
