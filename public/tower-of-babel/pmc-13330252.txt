Depression, anxiety and stress among nursing students: a latent profile analysis
Authors: Li Zeng, Jiayi Zhou, Yuan Zhang, Zhongqing Yuan, Fengxue Yang, Xiuying Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNursing students experience high prevalence of depression, anxiety, and stress, but traditional variable‑centered approaches may overlook distinct subgroups. This study aimed to identify homogeneous profiles of affective distress and examine their related factors.MethodsNursing students (n = 1184) from five medical colleges in Sichuan Province, China were recruited from March 3 to May 30, 2025. Participants were asked to complete questionnaires regarding depression, anxiety and stress, social emotional competency and alexithymia. Latent profile analysis (LPA) was used to identify depression, anxiety, and stress profiles. Chi-square tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to examine differences in characteristics. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to explore related factors.ResultsThe prevalence of depression, anxiety, and stress among nursing students were 38.52%, 46.12%, and 22.47%, respectively. LPA revealed three distinct profiles: “severe affective distress” (11.06%), “moderate affective distress” (35.14%), and “mild affective distress” (53.80%). Related factors for depression, anxiety, and stress included social emotional competency, alexithymia, gender, parenting style, monthly family income, professional identity, satisfaction with campus life, adverse childhood experiences, and campus bullying.ConclusionsTo our knowledge, limited research has applied LPA to examine depression, anxiety, and stress among nursing students. This study identified three distinct latent profiles, revealing significant heterogeneity influenced by individual, family, academic, traumatic characteristics, while social emotional competency and alexithymia. The identified associated factors, including modifiable characteristics such as social emotional competency, alexithymia, campus environment, and bullying, may inform future targeted interventions.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Nursing students, Depression, Anxiety, Stress, Latent profile analysis

Introduction

Depression, anxiety, and stress are prevalent among most populations worldwide and frequently co-occur, particularly among nursing students [1, 2]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 17 studies involving 13,247 nursing students indicated that approximately 52% experienced depression, 32% experienced anxiety, and 30% experienced stress [3]. Depression manifests through enduring negative affect, diminished pleasure in activities, and somatic alterations including appetite shifts and sleep disruptions. In contrast, anxiety involves persistent apprehension toward future events, whereas stress entails a sustained state of physiological hyperarousal accompanied and a tendency to become easily overwhelmed [1, 4]. The pervasive adverse effects of depression, anxiety, and stress impact multiple facets of nursing students’ academic and personal lives, including academic performance, career sustainability, and quality of life [5].

Nursing students experience disproportionately high levels of depression, anxiety, and stress, due to their exposure to various stressors stemming from lifestyle, familial, academic, and traumatic events [5–7]. Multiple studies confirmed that experiencing poor sleep, having an unbalanced diet, and maintaining a sedentary lifestyle were associated with a higher prevalence of depression, anxiety, and stress [6, 8]. Additionally, research indicated that positive parenting styles were negatively correlated with symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress, while greater family affluence was linked to lower stress levels [5, 9]. In addition, medical students with higher and lower academic burdens were more likely to experience moderate to high levels of depression and anxiety, while students with strong professional identity and high satisfaction with campus life were less likely to experience psychological problems [10–11]. Furthermore, adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) (such as abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction) and exposure to multiple forms of campus bullying are linked to elevated health risks, including depression and anxiety [7, 12]. Additionally, depression, anxiety, and stress exhibit significant associations with social emotional competency and alexithymia [13–14].

Background

Social emotional competency

Based on the Tripartite Model of Self-Construal, Chen et al. [15] constructed a theoretical model of college students’ social emotional competency comprising four dimensions: relationship with oneself, relationship with others, relationship with the collective, making responsible decisions. This model integrates the social emotional competency framework proposed by the Collaborative for Academic, Social and Emotional Learning (CASEL) with the Chinese perspective emphasizing the three relational dimensions of “self-others-collective” [16–17]. Research indicated that nursing students’ ability to identify, express, and regulate their own emotions enabled them to better navigate college challenges, while also positively influencing their stress management, emotional development, and mental health [18–19].

Alexithymia

Alexithymia is a personality trait characterized by difficulties in identifying and describing feelings, a limited ability to distinguish physiological arousal from feelings, and the use of an externally oriented cognitive style focused on external events rather than inner psychological experiences [14]. Alexithymia is considered a vulnerability factor influencing numerous psychopathologies and emotional-behavioral problems, potentially impairing an individual’s stress management capacity significantly by hindering emotional awareness, expression, and regulation [20]. Nursing students with alexithymia exhibited heightened vulnerability to stress-related exhaustion and were at higher risk for anxiety and depression, due to maladaptive coping strategies and impaired emotional self-management [14].

Latent profile analysis

Most research on depression, anxiety and stress among nursing students has focused on assessing prevalence and related factors using variable-centered approaches [2, 21–22], which may mask heterogeneous subgroups with distinct symptom profiles. Latent profile analysis (LPA) is a person-centered approach that delves into data to classify individuals from a heterogeneous population into homogeneous subgroups, as well as clarification of the proportion of each subgroup, to help further understand individual distribution characteristics [23]. Therefore, it is necessary to apply latent profile analysis to identify previously unobserved subgroups of depression, anxiety and stress among nursing students, thereby providing a more nuanced foundation for prevention.

Study aim

To the best of our knowledge, limited research has applied LPA to examine depression, anxiety, and stress among nursing students. Therefore, this study aimed to: (a) assess depression, anxiety and stress in nursing students; (b) identify distinct categories of depression, anxiety and stress among nursing students; and (c) describe factors associated with high depression, anxiety and stress among nursing students, including sociodemographic information, social emotional competency, and alexithymia.

Methods

Design and data collection

A cross-sectional study design utilizing convenience sampling was employed. The questionnaire was created and distributed via an online survey platform (www.wjx.cn). Permission to conduct the study was obtained from the college administrators. Prior to formal questionnaire distribution, all volunteers (including counselors, faculty members, and student representatives) received standardized training. Before distribution, volunteers explained the relevant concepts, study purpose, significance, and emphasized questionnaire completion guidelines. Participation was voluntary, based on informed consent, and questionnaires were submitted anonymously. To ensure data quality, only one submission per IP address was permitted. The sample size was calculated using the formula: N = Z1−a/22p (1-p)/d2, where Z1−a/2 = 1.96 (at 5% type 1 error p < 0.05), d (allowable error) was set at 0.05, and p was estimated at 30.9%-43.3% based on previous similar research [21]. Based on this formula, the required sample size ranged from 328 to 377. Accounting for potential sampling errors and invalid responses, a total of 1200 questionnaires were distributed. 1184 valid questionnaires were returned, yielding a valid response rate of 98.67%.

Participants

This study was conducted between March 3 and May 30, 2025 across five medical colleges in Sichuan Province, China. A total of 1184 nursing students were enrolled. The inclusion criteria for participants were: (a) full-time nursing students; (b) able to comprehend and complete the questionnaires; and (c) informed consent and voluntarily participated in the study. Exclusion criteria were: (a) history of psychiatric disorders or related conditions; (b) on leave or withdrew from school; and (c) unavailable on campus during the survey period.

Measures

Sociodemographic information questionnaire

The sociodemographic information questionnaire was utilized to collect data across five domains: individual characteristics, lifestyle, family background, academic situation, and traumatic events. Individual characteristics were assessed in terms of age and gender. Lifestyle were measured by sleep hours per day, regular diet (defined as consuming three meals on time without binge eating), and regular exercise (defined as engaging in weekly physical activities such as running, yoga, or jumping rope). Family background was evaluated based on parenting style and monthly family income. Parenting style was classified into three categories: authoritarian (characterized by strictness and demands for unconditional obedience), permissive (characterized by leniency and excessive indulgence and freedom), and authoritative (characterized by democratic parenting with clear rules and adequate support). Academic situation was examined through class rank, academic pressure, professional identity (the degree of recognition of the nursing profession), and satisfaction with campus life (the level of satisfaction with the campus environment, atmosphere, and overall life). Traumatic events were operationalized as adverse childhood experiences and campus bullying.

The Chinese version of the depression, anxiety, stress scale-21

The original version of Depression, Anxiety, Stress Scale-21 was developed by Lovibond and Lovibond [24] to assess individuals’ affective distress. Gong et al. [25] investigated the validity and reliability of the Chinese version of the Depression, Anxiety, Stress Scale-21 among college students. This scale comprises three subscales: depression, anxiety, and stress. Each subscale consists of 7 items, for a total of 21 items. Participants rated each item on a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from “did not apply to me at all” (scored 0) to “applied to me very much” (scored 3). The score for each subscale is calculated by summing the scores of its respective 7 items and then multiplying the sum by two. Thus, each subscale score ranges from 0 to 42. The severity thresholds for each subscale are defined as follows: depression: 0–9 = normal; 10–13 = mild; 14–20 = moderate; 21–27 = severe; 28–42 = extremely severe. anxiety: 0–7 = normal; 8–9 = mild; 10–14 = moderate; 15–19 = severe; 20–42 = extremely severe. stress: 0–14 = normal; 15–18 = mild; 19–25 = moderate; 26–33 = severe; 34–42 = extremely severe. Scores meeting or exceeding the following thresholds indicate the presence of respective negative emotional state: depression ≥ 10, anxiety ≥ 8, stress ≥ 15. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.922 for the depression subscale, 0.906 for the anxiety subscale, and 0.916 for the stress subscale.

The Chinese college students’ social emotional competency scale

The Chinese College Students’ Social Emotional Competency Scale, developed by Chen et al. [15], is a psychometric instrument designed primarily to assess the social emotional competency of college students. The scale comprises four dimensions: relationship with oneself (6 items), relationship with others (5 items), relationship with the collective (8 items), making responsible decisions (7 items), for a total of 26 items. Participants respond to each item using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” (scored 1) to “strongly agree” (scored 5). The total scale score ranges from 26 to 130, with higher self-reported scores indicating a higher level of developed social emotional competency. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale was 0.978.

The Chinese version of the toronto alexithymia scale

The original version of the Toronto Alexithymia Scale was developed by Bagby et al. [26] to assess individuals’ level of alexithymia. Yi et al. [27] investigated the validity and reliability of the Chinese version of the Toronto Alexithymia Scale among college students. The scale comprises three dimensions: difficulty identifying feelings (7 items), difficulty describing feelings (5 items), and externally oriented thinking (8 items), for a total of 20 items. Participants respond to each item using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” (scored 1) to “strongly agree” (scored 5). The total scale score ranges from 20 to 100. Scores are interpreted as follows: 20–50 indicates non-alexithymia, 51–60 indicates borderline alexithymia, and ≥ 61 indicates alexithymia [27]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale was 0.954.

Data analysis

LPA was performed using Mplus version 8.3. The 21 individual items of the Depression, Anxiety, Stress Scale scores served as the observed indicators, and models specifying 1 to 5 latent profiles were estimated sequentially. Model fit was evaluated using three types of indices: (1) Information Criteria: Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (aBIC). Lower values on these indices indicate better model fit [28]. (2) Classification Criteria: Entropy value, ranging from 0 to 1. Higher entropy values indicate greater classification precision; an entropy value ≥ 0.8 is generally considered a good classification of the model [29]. (3) Likelihood Ratio Test Criteria: Lo-Mendell-Rubin (LMR) and Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT). A statistically significant result (p < 0.05) for these tests indicates that the current profile model performed better than the previous one [23]. In a word, model selection was based on a combination of statistical fit indices (lower AIC, BIC, aBIC), entropy ≥ 0.8, significant LMR and BLRT (p < 0.05), as well as the interpretability and parsimony of the profiles.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0. Sociodemographic characteristics of the nursing students were described using frequencies and percentages. Differences in group characteristics across different latent profiles were examined using chi-square tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). All variables that were theoretically relevant were subsequently entered into multivariate logistic regression analyses to assess their influence on different latent profiles. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance level of 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sichuan Nursing Vocational College (No. 2024011) and strictly adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to questionnaire distribution, all participants were fully informed of the research purpose, relevant concepts, and significance, and were clearly notified that the questionnaire was submitted anonymously. Participation was contingent upon confirmed informed consent.

Results

Common method bias test

Common method bias was assessed using Harman’s single-factor test. Six factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, among which the first common factor accounted for 23.02% of the variance, which is below the critical threshold of 40%, confirming the absence of substantial common method bias in the data of this study. Although Harman’s single‑factor test has limitations, it is widely used as a conservative initial diagnostic. As a complementary check, we examined the bivariate correlations among the main study variables, and none exceeded 0.70, suggesting that common method bias is unlikely to substantially distort the findings.

Participant characteristics

Of the 1184 participants, 9.54% were males and 90.46% were females, with an average age of 19.38 ± 1.11 years. Over half of the nursing students slept for < 7 h per day (51.69%), while 61.15% maintained a regular diet and 73.31% lacked regular exercise. Additionally, 34.97% of nursing students reported adverse childhood experiences, and 6.50% experienced campus bullying (Table 1).

Latent profiles of depression, anxiety and stress

Among 1184 nursing students, the prevalence of depression, anxiety, and stress were 38.52% (n = 456), 46.12% (n = 546), and 22.47% (n = 266), respectively (Fig. 1). Based on depression, anxiety and stress scores as indicators, five latent profile models were fitted sequentially, beginning with a one-class latent profile model and incrementally increasing the number of classes. The fit statistics for each model are presented in Table 2. Although the AIC, BIC, and aBIC continued to decrease as more profiles were added, the LMR test became non‑significant at the 5‑profile solution (p = 0.146), indicating that a fifth class did not significantly improve model fit. We further examined the 4‑profile solutions: the 4‑profile solution split the “moderate affective distress” subgroup into two nearly identical subgroups that differed only marginally in symptom intensity, without yielding a clinically meaningful new phenotype. In contrast, the 3‑profile model had the highest entropy (0.982), large class sizes (11.06%, 35.14%, 53.80%), and excellent average latent class probabilities (98.9%‑99.8%). Moreover, a three‑level severity structure (mild, moderate, severe) is theoretically coherent and widely recognized in clinical psychology research [30]. Therefore, based on statistical fit, interpretability, class stability, and theoretical coherence, the 3‑profile solution was selected as the most parsimonious and interpretable model.

As shown in Fig. 2, latent profile analysis yielded three distinct affective distress. The first, encompassing 11.06% (n = 131) of respondents, exhibited markedly elevated depression, anxiety and stress and was designated the “severe affective distress” subgroup. The second, representing 35.14% (n = 416) of the sample, displayed moderate elevations across all items and was labelled the “moderate affective distress” subgroup. The third, comprising 53.80% (n = 637) of participants, demonstrated uniformly low depression, anxiety and stress and was thus classified as the “mild affective distress” subgroup.

Factors associated with depression, anxiety and stress

Tolerance (TOL) and variance inflation factor (VIF) were used to assess multicollinearity among independent variables in the regression model. The results indicated TOL values exceeding 0.1 (range: 0.850–0.970) and VIF values below 10 (range: 1.031–1.176), demonstrating no evidence of multicollinearity. Related factors of depression, anxiety and stress among nursing students are presented in Table 4, including social emotional competency, alexithymia, gender, sleep hours per day, regular diet, parenting style, monthly family income (RMB), professional identity, satisfaction with campus life, adverse childhood experiences, and campus bullying. In terms of the magnitude of association, campus bullying showed the largest odds ratio (OR) among categorical variables (OR = 5.466 for “severe affective distress”; OR = 3.412 for “moderate affective distress”). Alexithymia, as a continuous variable, demonstrated a highly significant and consistent association with both “severe affective distress” (OR = 1.198) and “moderate affective distress” (OR = 1.491).

Discussion

This study found that the prevalence rates of depression, anxiety, and stress among nursing students were 38.52%, 46.12%, and 22.47%, respectively. These rates are consistent with previous research findings and significantly higher than the overall prevalence rates reported among non-medical students in China [31]. Multiple studies indicated that nursing students frequently encountered diverse stressors in their academic and daily lives, contributing to emotional distress including depression, anxiety, and stress [2, 22]. The high prevalence of depression, anxiety, and stress may adversely affect nursing students’ academic performance and future clinical practice [5]. Therefore, nursing educators should prioritize nursing students’ mental health and actively promote reforms in nursing education to safeguard the stability of the future nursing workforce.

The analysis revealed three distinct latent profiles: “severe affective distress” subgroup (11.06%), “moderate affective distress” subgroup (35.14%), “mild affective distress” subgroup (53.80%). Although the Depression, Anxiety, Stress Scale provides severity cutoffs, LPA empirically classifies individuals based on the joint pattern of depression, anxiety, and stress scores. This approach revealed the natural distribution of severity profiles in our sample and allowed us to identify factors differentially associated with moderate versus severe affective distress, and these insights are not obtainable from applying separate cutoffs or using total scores.

Regarding individual characteristics, gender differences were observed in depression, anxiety, and stress among nursing students. Compared to females, males were more likely to be classified into the “severe affective distress” subgroup, which contrasts with previous research [5]. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in research methodologies and sample sources, necessitating further investigation.

In terms of family background, nursing students from families with a permissive parenting type demonstrated significantly higher odds of being classified into the “moderate affective distress” subgroup compared to those with an authoritative parenting type. Furthermore, regarding monthly family income (RMB), nursing students from families earning ≤ 3000 showed significantly greater odds of being belonging to the “severe affective distress” subgroup compared to those from families earning > 6000. The permissive parenting style, characterized by high warmth and low control, is prone to leading children to feel overly indulged while deficient self-regulation, manifesting in increased behavioral problems, and elevate the risk of depression, anxiety, and stress [32]. Additional research demonstrated that financial stress was associated with repetitive negative thinking, thus, nursing students from lower-income families exhibit higher levels of anxiety and stress [11]. These findings suggest that implementing targeted scholarship programs for nursing students from low-income backgrounds may alleviate their financial pressure. Meanwhile, protecting the mental health of these students may require parental involvement.

In terms of academic situation, nursing students with a moderate and strong professional identity demonstrated a higher probability of belonging to the “mild affective distress” subgroup compared to those with a weak professional identity. Similarly, nursing students reporting high satisfaction with campus life showed a greater likelihood of classification into the “mild affective distress” subgroup than those reporting low satisfaction with campus life. Nursing students’ professional identity potentially influences their mental health outcomes. Specifically, individuals with a strong professional identity exhibit lower levels of depression, anxiety, and stress [33]. Moreover, a supportive campus environment may foster a level playing field, thereby enhancing nursing students’ opportunities for academic success while also promoting their ability to engage in adaptive coping with emotional challenges [11, 34]. Therefore, nurse educators may enhance nursing students’ sense of professional mission and potentially improve their mental health by implementing professional identity strengthening programs (such as nursing role model narrative workshops) and establishing peer support initiatives (such as senior students guiding junior students).

Regarding traumatic events, nursing students with a history of adverse childhood experiences or campus bullying were significantly more likely to be classified into either the “severe affective distress” subgroup or the “moderate affective distress” subgroup compared to those without such experiences. This finding aligns with previous research [7, 12]. Adverse childhood experiences exert far-reaching detrimental effects on an individual’s health and well-being across the lifespan, manifesting as depression, anxiety, and stress, and potentially leading to behavioral problems [35]. Notably, campus bullying strongly associated with “moderate affective distress” and “severe affective distress” subgroups. This association may operate through persistent self‑blame, social withdrawal, and distorted cognitive appraisals, which can amplify emotional distress over time [7, 11]. Besides, experiences of campus bullying can foster distorted attributional patterns, potentially leading individuals into a vicious cycle of emotions [7]. These findings suggest that administrators may not only focus on the psychological well-being of nursing students, but may also need to proactively screen for their traumatic experiences and provide necessary mental health support.

It is noteworthy that nursing students’ social emotional competency was associated with “mild affective distress”, whereas alexithymia was associated with “moderate affective distress” and “severe affective distress”. Previous research confirmed that enhancing social emotional competency improved students’ adaptability and well-being, thereby helping prevent issues such as social exclusion and depression later in life [35, 36]. Besides, mindfulness-based social-emotional learning has been shown to be effective in alleviating depression, stress, and anxiety in nursing students, which contributes to modifying negative behaviors and automatic thoughts [37]. Conversely, alexithymia impairs the ability to identify, describe, and regulate emotions, creating a vulnerability to cumulative negative affect and maladaptive coping, thereby increasing the risk of severe distress [14]. These findings support the explanation of correlations between variables, but should still be confirmed in future longitudinal or experimental studies. If the causal relationship is further confirmed, nursing educators and administrators could consider integrating and developing curricula focused on social-emotional learning and emotion identification to enhance nursing students’ emotion regulation abilities and improve their mental health.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the generalizability of the findings may be limited due to the restricted geographical scope of participant recruitment. Future multi-center studies with broader geographic coverage are needed to examine the effect of regional context. Secondly, the outcome measures were based on self-report, which may potentially introduce bias. Additionally, as a cross-sectional study, it is difficult to infer causal relationships. Future research should employ longitudinal, multi-center designs with larger sample sizes to comprehensively explore how different characteristics influence depression, anxiety, and stress among nursing students.

Conclusions

This study employed latent profile analysis to identify three distinct affective distress profiles among nursing students: “severe affective distress” (11.06%), “moderate affective distress” (35.14%), and “mild affective distress” (53.80%). Latent profiles were associated with social emotional competency, alexithymia, gender, parenting style, monthly family income, professional identity, satisfaction with campus life, adverse childhood experiences, and campus bullying. Based on these findings, future research could explore interventions targeting modifiable factors. For example, social‑emotional learning programs may enhance social emotional competency; emotion recognition and expression training could address alexithymia; improving campus environment and professional identity through mentorship and career support might increase satisfaction; and anti‑bullying campaigns combined with trauma‑informed care for adverse childhood experiences could reduce psychological distress. Given the cross‑sectional design, causal inferences cannot be drawn, and these suggested directions require validation in longitudinal or interventional studies.
