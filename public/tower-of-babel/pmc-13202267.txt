Transcript-independent quantitative analysis of RNA cap structures by LC-MS/MS
Authors: Ryan Kilburn, Rachel C Gomez, Steven Evans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIncorporation of the 5′ cap into RNA is a critical quality attribute that requires accurate assessment for therapeutic application. Existing analytical approaches often rely on transcript-specific probes or sequence-dependent workflows, which limit throughput and platform-level applicability. Here, we describe a quantitative, transcript-independent LC-MS/MS assay for RNA cap incorporation designed to support RNA therapeutic process development and quality control, using CleanCap-AG as a representative co-transcriptional capping chemistry. The workflow combines nuclease P1 digestion with external calibration using authentic cap analog standards, enabling quantification of capped (CC-A) and uncapped (ATP) 5′ species while correcting for residual unincorporated reagents through paired digested and undigested analyses. The assay supports a 0.01–20 pmol calibration range using commercially available standards and a 10 min LC-MS/MS acquisition, facilitating high analytical throughput. Analytical characterization demonstrated excellent linearity (r2 > 0.995), accuracy (84%–102% recovery), and precision consistent with accepted bioanalytical criteria (RSD <15% at 1 and 10 pmol). Application to multiple RNA transcripts confirmed efficient digestion and accurate cap quantification independent of sequence context. This method provides a rigorously characterized, sequence-agnostic analytical platform for RNA cap analysis compatible with chemical and potentially enzymatic capping strategies, enabling scalable process development, lot comparability assessment, and manufacturing quality control.Keywords: RNA, mRNA, RNA capping, LC-MS/MS, nuclease P1, quantitative analysis, external calibration, critical quality attribute, manufacturing quality control

Introduction

Messenger RNA (mRNA) therapeutics have emerged as a transformative platform for vaccines, protein replacement, and personalized genetic medicines.1 As these products advance toward clinical application, analytical characterization becomes central to assessing critical quality attributes (CQAs) that govern safety, efficacy, and regulatory acceptance.2,3 One of the most important CQAs is the 5′ cap structure, which regulates mRNA stability, translation initiation, ribosome engagement, and innate immune impact.4,5 Caps vary in methylation state, including G-cap (GpppN), Cap-0 (m7GpppN), and Cap-1 (m7GpppNm), and these modifications strongly influence therapeutic potency and immunogenicity.6,7 In higher eukaryotes, Cap-2 structures (m7GpppNmNm) are also prevalent and further enhance self-/non-self-discrimination. Cap methylation modulates innate immune recognition, as incompletely methylated or uncapped RNA can activate antiviral response pathways and immune sensors.5 Co-transcriptional cap analogs such as CleanCap are therefore designed to efficiently generate Cap-1 structures, improving translation while minimizing immune activation. Accurate, quantitative assessment of cap incorporation is therefore essential for mRNA manufacturing and process optimization.3

Capping of synthetic RNA is achieved using either post-transcriptional enzymatic strategies or co-transcriptional incorporation of synthetic cap analogs during transcription initiation. Enzymatic capping systems, such as Faustovirus capping enzyme or the vaccinia capping enzyme complex,8,9,10 exploit viral capping mechanisms through a sequential triphosphatase, guanylyltransferase, methyltransferase reaction cascade. When coupled with vaccinia cap 2′-O-MTase, these systems generate Cap-1 RNA.11,12 Although enzymatic capping offers flexibility and scalability, incomplete catalytic turnover can produce heterogeneous mixtures of capped intermediates,13,14 requiring robust analytical methods to verify cap identity and abundance.

Co-transcriptional capping, including ARCA (anti-reverse cap analog) reagents and CleanCap analogs, offers high efficiency and simpler reaction setups in a one-pot reaction.15,16 These dinucleotide cap analogs consist of a 7-methylguanosine linked via a 5′-5′ triphosphate bridge to a modified initiating nucleotide. In this approach, ARCA and CleanCap-GG compete with GTP during transcription initiation, whereas CleanCap-AG (CC-AG) and CleanCap-AU (CC-AU) compete with ATP, resulting in transcripts that are either capped or uncapped.17 However, this strategy requires specific 5′ template sequences and can involve higher reagent costs.15 Importantly, both enzymatic and chemical approaches share the same analytical challenge, the accurate quantification of capped versus uncapped RNA across diverse sequence contexts.

Several analytical strategies have been developed to characterize mRNA cap structures, each with distinct advantages and limitations. Probe-based methods remain one of the most widely applied approaches for cap quantification.18 In these assays, a sequence-specific DNA or chimeric probe anneals to the 5′ end of the transcript, enabling cleavage with enzymes such as RNase H at the DNA-RNA hybrid. These DNA-RNA cleavage enzymes tend to result in multiple oligomeric species as the specific cut site is not exact.19,20 The resulting short 5′ fragment, containing either cap or triphosphate at the 5′ end, is purified via an affinity tag on the probe and analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), or polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis.10,20,21 This strategy is sensitive and effective but requires custom probe design and optimization for each RNA construct, making it challenging for high throughput or platform level analytics.19

Alternative approaches use enzymatic digestion strategies that bypass the need for sequence specific probes. RNase T1, which cleaves 3′ of guanosines, produces defined fragments analyzable by LC-MS.22 These fragments vary from transcript to transcript due to sequence differences and often require building data analysis platforms to analyze the resulting LC-MS data.23 When paired with stable isotope-labeled (SIL) internal standards, RNase T1 digestion enables quantitative evaluation of capped versus uncapped RNA.24 However, SIL-RNAs are costly, sequence-dependent, and not widely accessible.

Another widely used enzyme is nuclease P1 (NP1), which digests RNA into nucleotide monophosphates while preserving the intact 5′ terminal structure, either the triphosphate or capped dinucleotide.3,25 NP1-based LC-MS workflows such as CapQuant and CAP-MAP have been used for qualitative or semi-quantitative analysis of cap methylation patterns.12,25,26 However, current applications generally lack quantification, relying on signal intensity comparisons, or require specialized isotope-labeled standards not available for many cap analogs.24,27 One study provided initial evidence that NP1 workflows have the capability of quantifying RNA cap incorporation but lacked the characterization necessary for adoption into current biomanufacturing workflows.28 Existing NP1 workflows are not characterized to analyze the necessary components needed to determine RNA cap incorporation with the quantitative rigor required for regulatory and manufacturing settings.

Collectively, current probe-based, RNase T1, and NP1 approaches have established important analytical foundations for cap characterization. Probe-based strategies have proven to be highly effective at quantifying cap incorporation but their dependence on transcript sequence and high development burden per RNA construct is a serious limitation in the context of increasing throughput and applicability of analysis.18,19,20 RNase T1 can process and digest RNA independent of sequence and is a valuable tool for sequence confirmation and cap quantification;24 however, this analytical methodology still needs to be customized based on the digestion patterns of specific RNA sequences. While an NP1 method has been developed to differentiate cap methylation,12 and initial RNA cap quantification,28 more work needs to be done to characterize these workflows for biomanufacturing settings. Additionally, no published NP1-based method integrates quantification of capped versus uncapped 5′ species and corrects for residual reagents using paired digested/undigested analyses. These gaps highlight the need for a universal, quantitative, and transcript agnostic method to determine cap incorporation with manufacturing and regulatory goals in mind.

In this study, we present a sequence-independent LC-MS/MS workflow that combines NP1 digestion with external calibration using authentic cap analog standards. By enzymatically liberating only the 5′ nucleotide or capped dinucleotide, and by independently quantifying digested and undigested fractions, the method enables accurate and reproducible measurement of capped and uncapped RNA without requiring probe design, isotope-labeled controls, or transcript-specific reagents such as DNAzymes. We analytically characterized the assay across multiple RNA transcripts, assessed analytical performance metrics including linearity, accuracy, and precision, and demonstrated applicability to therapeutically relevant RNA. This approach provides a scalable, standardized analytical framework that addresses many limitations of existing cap-analysis strategies and is suitable for research, development, and quality control workflows across the expanding landscape of RNA therapeutics.

Results

Overview of workflow

An LC-MS/MS workflow was established to quantify RNA 5′-cap structures independent of transcript sequence using external calibration and NP1 digestion. The method quantifies capped-RNA as the CleanCap-A (CC-A) dinucleotide species (hereafter referred to as CC-A) and uncapped RNA as ATP following enzymatic digestion.

Accurate quantification required paired analysis of NP1-digested and undigested samples. NP1-digested samples report capped (CC-A) and uncapped (ATP) RNA species, while undigested samples quantify residual unincorporated cap analog or ATP reagents. NP1 digestion of cap analog standards (CC-AG or CC-AU) generated products chemically equivalent to those derived from capped-RNA digestion (Figure S1). Subtraction of undigested signal from digested measurements corrected for residual reagent carryover.

Relative cap incorporation was calculated as the proportion of CC-Acorrected relative to the total detected signal (CC-Acorrected and ATPcorrected), where corrected values represent molar amounts obtained after blank subtraction and correction using the corresponding undigested control (Equation 1). This workflow enabled quantitative determination of RNA capping efficiency while accounting for unincorporated reagent contamination through paired digested and undigested analysis.

Characterizing cleancap analogs

Information-dependent acquisition in negative ion mode was used to identify precursor and product ions for CC-AG, CC-AU, CC-A, and ATP. To reflect experimental conditions, analytes were analyzed in mixtures containing either CC-AG or CC-AU with ATP and subjected to NP1 digestion or no digest controls. Ions corresponding to theoretical m/z values were observed for all analytes. Precursor ions were identified at m/z 1144.3 (CC-AG), 1105.3 (CC-AU), 799.3 (CC-A), and 506.1 (ATP) (Table 1).

Optimization of LC-MS/MS parameters

The LC and MS/MS parameters were optimized to enable simultaneous detection of intact cap analogs, digested cap products, and ATP under identical analytical conditions. Chromatographic separation was achieved using a monolithic C18 column with dibutylammonium acetate and acetonitrile mobile phases. A 5 min gradient followed by column washing and re-equilibration produced a total run time of 10 min per injection (Table S1).

A multiple reaction monitoring method was developed using optimized LC and MS/MS parameters (Table 1,S1, and S2). Overlay of chromatograms from CC-AG standards, capped-RNA digests, and uncapped RNA digests demonstrated co-elution of corresponding analytes with no observable interferences (Figures 2A–2D). Comparable chromatographic behavior was observed for CC-AU standards (Figure S2B). Although RNA samples capped with CC-AU were not available for analysis in this study, CC-AU standards were included to support method development and demonstrate analytical applicability to alternative cap chemistries.

Analyte-specific characterization

Assay sensitivity was determined using water blanks (n = 10) to establish baseline noise for each analyte of interest. The limits of detection (LOD) and limits of quantitation (LOQ) were defined as 3× and 10× the noise level, respectively. Using CC-AG standards, the LOD/LOQ values were 0.04/0.14 pmol for ATP and 0.03/0.11 pmol for CC-A. CC-AU standards observed higher LOD/LOQ values of 0.33/1.1 pmol for ATP and 0.24/0.79 pmol for CC-A (Table 2). Digested CC-AG standards containing 0.1 pmol of total material generated signals clearly distinguishable from noise (Figure 2D).

Potential matrix effects were evaluated by comparing ratios of qualitative-to-quantitative MRM (multi reaction monitoring) transitions between external standards and processed hemagglutinin (HA) RNA samples. ATP analysis utilized the ion identified at 159 m/z as the quantitative ion, while 134 and 273 m/z ions were chosen as the qualifiers (Figures 1A, 1B, and S2A). These ratios averaged 0.14 (0.01) and 0.07 (0.01) for the standard and 0.14 (0.01) and 0.07 (0.01) for the RNA sample, respectively (Figure 2E). Data are reported as mean (SD), where SD represents the standard deviation of replicate measurements. The same process was conducted for CC-A analytes with the 422 m/z ion representing the quantitative ion and 287 and 438 being chosen as the qualifier ions (Figures 1C, 1D, and S2C). The 422/287 ion pair were observed at 0.12 (0.01) and 0.12 (0.00), while the 422/438 pair was observed at 0.17 (0.03) and 0.16 (0.01) for standards and samples, respectively (Figure 2E). The CC-AG analyte was characterized by the 767 m/z quantifier ion and the qualifier ions were identified as 865 and 961 (Figures 1E, 1F, and S2B) resulting in ratios for standards being 0.16 (0.06) and 0.16 (0.05), while samples-maintained ratios of 0.14 (0.09) and 0.14 (0.03) (Figure 2E). The ion ratios differed by less than 20% between standards and samples for all analytes (Figure 2E), indicate comparable ionization behavior across matrices.

External calibration curves were generated over a range of 0.01–20 pmol. Both digested and undigested standards exhibited coefficients of determination exceeding 0.995 for all CC-AG and CC-AU analytes (Figures 3A and 3B). Accuracy (trueness) of CC-AG and CC-AU standards was verified by comparing measured versus expected concentrations of calibration standards, which all showed strong correlation (r2 > 0.999) (Figures 3C and 3D).

Quantitative characterization

Quantitative performance was evaluated using ATP and CC-A, for both CC-AG and CC-AU standard sets, as representative analytes of uncapped and capped RNA, respectively. Accuracy (trueness) was assessed as percent recovery (measured versus expected analyte amount), while precision was evaluated as relative standard deviation (RSD) across replicate measurements. Intra-assay precision was determined from replicate injections (n = 3) of the same prepared sample. Inter-assay precision was evaluated across independently prepared samples generated from a common stock solution on three separate days, with each preparation analyzed in triplicate. Measurements were conducted at 10 and 1 pmol input levels. At 10 pmol, recoveries for all standards ranged between 90% and 110% (Table 2). At 1 pmol, CC-AG-based ATP exhibited a recovery of 93%, where recovery is defined as the ratio of measured to expected analyte amount expressed as a percentage, while the remaining analytes showed recoveries ranging from 84% to 116%. CC-AG-derived CC-A had an observed recovery of 84% of the expected value, while CC-AU-based ATP and CC-A exceeded 100% recovery at 112% and 115%, respectively (Table 2).

Inter-assay precision, evaluated across independently prepared samples on three separate days, was below 3% RSD for CC-AG analytes at 10 pmol and ranged from 7% to 8% for CC-AU analytes. All analytes exhibited RSD values below 15% at 1 pmol. Intra-assay precision, assessed as injection to injection variability, was less than 15% for all analytes except CC-AU-derived ATP and CC-A at 1 pmol, which remained below 20% (Table 2).

Application to RNA samples

Digestion efficiency of NP1 was evaluated using multiple RNA transcripts incubated with 0, 5, 10, or 20 units of NP1 enzyme. Undigested controls retained intact RNA bands, whereas all other treatments resulted in efficient degradation by denaturing 1% agarose gel electrophoresis (Figure 4A). In addition, denaturing Tris-borate-EDTA (TBE)-urea gel analysis did not reveal detectable accumulation of low-molecular-weight RNA species (Figure S5).

To assess quantitative performance on therapeutically relevant RNA, capped and uncapped HA transcripts were mixed at defined ratios. The contrived RNA samples were mixed at 100%-, 75%-, 50%-, 25%-, and 0%-capped RNA and the samples exhibited cap incorporation, as defined by relative abundance of CC-A, at 98.1 (0.6), 72.9 (0.5), 50.0 (1.4), 25.0 (1.0), and 0.0 (0.0), respectively (Figure 4B). Data are reported as mean (SD), where SD represents the standard deviation of replicate measurements. In this analysis, CC-A and ATP signals are normalized such that their combined abundance represents 100% of detected analytes, with CC-A corresponding to capped RNA and ATP representing the uncapped fraction. The coefficient of determination among these spike-in amounts showed strong correlation (r2 = 0.9994) (Figure 4B).

The method was further applied to a panel of diverse RNA transcripts representing therapeutic use cases. The eGFP transcript was purchased from TriLink Biotechnologies and is reported by the manufacturer to exhibit >95% cap incorporation based on internal quality control. Analysis using the LC-MS/MS workflow described here yielded a cap incorporation of 98.4% (±0.6%) (Figure 4C), consistent with the reported value. The HA transcript had an observed capping efficiency of 99.1% (0.5%). FLuc samples 1 and 2, as well as Cas9 samples 1 and 2 represent independently transcribed RNAs using the same template for the specified sets of RNA. The FLuc transcripts yielded cap incorporations of 95.7% (1.4%) and 94.3% (1.9%), respectively, while the Cas9 RNAs had capping efficiencies of 96.6% (1.2%) and 97.4% (1.1) (Figure 4C). Replicate transcripts generated from identical templates exhibited comparable capping efficiencies (Figure 4C).

To illustrate how this correction strategy is applied in practice, representative datasets are shown for LiCl-purified HA RNA, LiCl-purified Cas9 RNA with an additional silica-based spin purification step, and eGFP RNA obtained from TriLink and subjected to a silica-based spin column polish (Figure 4D). Absolute molar amounts of capped (CC-A) and uncapped (ATP) species were quantified in both NP1-digested and undigested samples. The undigested measurements reflect residual unincorporated reagents, which were subtracted from the corresponding digested values to generate corrected analyte amounts. This correction resulted in a shift in calculated cap incorporation from 91.9% (uncorrected) to 98.1% (corrected), demonstrating the importance of accounting for residual species in accurate quantification.

Discussion

As RNA therapeutics continue to expand, there is an increasing need for analytical methods that can reliably quantify CQAs across diverse RNA constructs. Among these, 5′ cap incorporation is essential for RNA stability, translational efficiency, and immunogenicity control.5,15 Here, we describe the development and analytical characterization of an LC-MS/MS workflow that enables quantitative, sequence-agnostic determination of RNA capping efficiency using NP1 digestion and external calibration with authentic cap analog standards.

To contextualize this approach, we compared key features of representative RNA cap analysis methods, including probe-based, ribozyme-based, NP1-based, and multi-attribute LC-MS approaches (Table 3). This comparison highlights differences in transcript dependence, quantitation strategy, and suitability for CQA monitoring and lot comparability in manufacturing settings. As summarized in Table 3, many existing methods provide sensitive detection of RNA cap structures but rely on transcript-specific probes, sequence-dependent cleavage, or relative signal comparisons. These constraints limit platform-level deployment and complicate comparability across RNA constructs. In contrast, the workflow described here integrates NP1 digestion with external calibration and paired digested/undigested correction, enabling absolute quantification of capped and uncapped species within a unified analytical framework.

A central challenge in RNA cap quantification is distinguishing incorporated cap structures from residual reagents that may co-purify with RNA. NP1 digestion addresses this by degrading RNA into nucleotide monophosphates while preserving the 5′ end as either ATP (uncapped RNA) or a capped dinucleotide species (CC-A), generating a defined and sequence-independent molecular signature suitable for targeted-LC-MS/MS analysis (Figures 1A–1F). Processing both RNA samples and cap analog standards under identical digestion conditions ensures chemical equivalence between analytes and calibrants, supporting accurate quantitation (Figure 2E).

A key feature of this workflow is the inclusion of a no digest control analyzed in parallel with NP1-digested samples. This design enables correction for unincorporated ATP or cap analogs that persist following purification. Subtraction of undigested signal from digested measurements mitigates reagent carryover and prevents systematic bias in capping efficiency estimates (Figure 4D). This two-condition strategy extends prior NP1-based approaches by explicitly accounting for residual reagents and supports reliable CQA monitoring and lot-to-lot comparability across RNA transcripts.12,28

Quantitation was achieved using external calibration curves generated from authentic ATP and cap analog standards subjected to the same enzymatic treatment as experimental samples. Because ionization efficiency varies among nucleotides and cap analogs, analyte specific calibration curves were required to ensure quantitative accuracy (Figures 3A and 3B). Measured versus expected standard amounts showed strong correlation across the calibration range (Figures 3C and 3D), and qualitative-to-quantitative ion ratios were consistent between standards and RNA samples (Figure 2E), indicating comparable fragmentation behavior. Agreement within ±20% supports consistent analyte response with minimal matrix effects under the conditions tested. Higher LOD and LOQ observed for CC-AU relative to CC-AG likely reflect reduced ionization efficiency and lower signal intensity under current conditions; sensitivity may be improved through transition optimization or increased injection volume. As an external benchmark, analysis of commercially available eGFP mRNA (TriLink Biotechnologies), reported to exhibit >95% cap incorporation, yielded 98.4% (±0.6%) using this workflow (Figure 4C), supporting quantitative accuracy. Collectively, these validation metrics demonstrate robust and reproducible quantification of RNA capping efficiency without reliance on sequence-specific reagents.

By restricting analysis to mono-, di-, and tri-nucleotide species within a limited mass range, this approach avoids challenges associated with intact mRNA mass spectrometry, including charge-state complexity and spectral deconvolution.23 Within the broader context of RNA therapeutic development, cap incorporation represents one of several CQAs, alongside RNA integrity, double-stranded RNA content, sequence fidelity, and impurity profiles.29 The method described here complements existing analytical approaches by providing a targeted and quantitative readout of 5′ cap incorporation that is compatible with manufacturing and QC workflows.

From a practical perspective, the LC-MS/MS workflow balances analytical rigor with throughput, with a runtime of approximately 10 min per sample and no requirement for sequence-specific reagents. Although implementation requires mass spectrometry instrumentation, the use of commercially available standards and straightforward sample preparation supports feasibility in both process development and quality control settings.

Application of the method to multiple RNA transcripts demonstrated consistent and reproducible quantification of capping efficiency across diverse sequence contexts (Figure 4C). The RNA samples analyzed in this study were generated using T7-based in vitro transcription (IVT), either through in-house production workflows or obtained from commercial sources (e.g., TriLink Biotechnologies). Variability in transcription conditions, capping efficiency, and purification strategies can influence the distribution of 5′-terminal species and should be considered when interpreting cap incorporation measurements. The ability to apply a single, sequence-agnostic analytical workflow across distinct RNA constructs eliminates the need for transcript-specific method development and improves efficiency during RNA optimization and scale-up. Additionally, this approach enables analysis without disclosure of proprietary sequence information, facilitating external testing and cross-site quality control.

Stable isotope-labeled internal standards represent the gold standard for LC-MS/MS quantification.24,30 However, their limited commercial availability for RNA cap analogs constrains routine implementation. In this context, external calibration using authentic standards provides a practical and accessible alternative for quantitative cap analysis.

Despite these advantages, several limitations should be considered. The current implementation has been analytically characterized for CleanCap chemistry and quantification of ATP and CC-A as representative uncapped and capped species. Additional 5′-terminal species may be present depending on transcription conditions, including GTP-, GDP-, and ADP-derived species, as well as 5′ diphosphate RNA arising from mis-initiation or incomplete capping. These species are not explicitly quantified and may contribute to overestimation of capped RNA when relying solely on ATP and CC-A measurements. Although the paired digested/undigested correction mitigates bias from residual reagents, it does not account for alternative 5′-terminal RNA species, highlighting a limitation of sequence-agnostic, digestion-based approaches and the potential need for complementary analytical methods.

Abortive transcripts generated during IVT represent an additional potential source of bias, as short RNA species would yield 5′-terminal products indistinguishable from those derived from full-length RNA following NP1 digestion. In this study, denaturing TBE-urea gel analysis did not indicate accumulation of low molecular weight RNA species (Figure S5), suggesting minimal contribution under the conditions tested. However, this effect may be transcript- and process-dependent and should be evaluated in other systems.

Extension of this approach to additional cap analogs and enzymatic capping systems will require corresponding reference materials, extension of analyte ion panels, and possibly further development of LC-MS/MS conditions. Overlapping signals from enzymatic reagents or partially capped intermediates may necessitate additional cleanup strategies and careful implementation of the paired digested design.

Future work should focus on expanding analyte coverage to include additional nucleotide species and intermediates, optimizing sample preparation workflows, and integrating stable isotope-labeled standards to further improve quantitative accuracy and support comprehensive characterization of RNA 5′-end heterogeneity across diverse capping strategies and transcription conditions.

In summary, this study presents a scalable, sequence-agnostic LC-MS/MS workflow for quantitative assessment of RNA cap incorporation. By integrating NP1 digestion, paired no digest controls, and external calibration, this approach establishes a robust analytical foundation for RNA therapeutic process development and manufacturing quality control.

Materials and methods

Reagents and standards

The cap analogs CC-AG (TriLink Biotechnologies, San Diego, CA, USA; Cat. No. N-7113) and CC-AU (TriLink Biotechnologies, San Diego, CA, USA; Cat. No. N-7114) as well as eGFP mRNA (TriLink Biotechnologies, San Diego, CA; USA, Cat. No. L-7601) were obtained from TriLink Biotechnologies. HiScribe IVT kits (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA; Cat. No. E2040), NP1 (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA; Cat. No. M0660S), and Monarch RNA Cleanup kits (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA; Cat. No. T2040) were purchased from New England Biolabs. LC-MS/MS grade solvents included water, acetonitrile (Honeywell B&J, Charlotte, NC, USA) and dibutylammonium acetate (TCI Scientific, Portland, OR, USA). All reagents were used without further purification.

RNA synthesis and purification

RNA was synthesized using either a proprietary continuous flow IVT system or a conventional batch IVT format, both employing T7 RNA polymerase. In all cases, transcription reactions utilized DNA templates containing a T7 promoter sequence (5′-TAATACGACTCACTATA-3′).

Co-transcriptional capping was achieved through incorporation of CleanCap analogs (e.g., CleanCap-AG) during transcription initiation, following manufacturer-recommended conditions. Transcription reactions included standard ribonucleotide triphosphates (rNTPs), buffer components, and enzyme concentrations typical for T7-based IVT systems. DNA templates were designed to support efficient transcription initiation and cap analog incorporation.

For continuous flow IVT, reagents were preincubated and transcription was initiated by addition of 5 mM rNTPs. The reaction was maintained at 37°C under continuous flow conditions, and the outflow RNA was subsequently treated with DNase I to remove template DNA. While the continuous flow system is proprietary, the reaction chemistry and transcription principles are consistent with established T7 IVT methodologies.

Following DNase I treatment, RNA was purified by addition of an equal volume of 7.5 M LiCl solution (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Samples were incubated at −20°C from 30 min to overnight before centrifugation at 15,000× g for 30 min. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was washed twice with 75% ethanol using 15 min spins at 15,000× g. After air drying, pellets were resuspended in RNase-free water.

The LiCl precipitated RNA was further purified using a Monarch Spin RNA Cleanup Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA; Cat. No. T2040) according to the manufacturer’s instructions with a final elution volume of 50 μL. No additional desalting was performed prior to LC-MS/MS other than the Monarch cleanup. RNA concentration was determined by UV absorbance at 260 nm using a NanoPhotometer (Implen, Westlake Village, CA, USA).

NP1 digestion

Purified RNA was split into two treatments and incubated with or without NP1 (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA; Cat. No. M0660S) to digest RNA and liberate 5′-terminal nucleotides for analysis. This ensured that the only variable between paired samples was NP1 activity. Unless stated otherwise, digestion conditions used 10 units NP1 per 10 pmol RNA (i.e., 1 U/pmol) in a 25 μL reaction incubated at 37°C for 60 min, followed by heat inactivation at 70°C for 10 min. Efficient degradation of RNA was assessed by denaturing gel electrophoresis, as indicated by the absence of intact RNA bands.

Denaturing RNA gels were prepared with 1% agarose and 17% (v/v) formaldehyde in MOPS buffer (20 mM MOPS pH 7.0, 5 mM sodium acetate, 1 mM EDTA). The gels were stained with GelRed stain (Biotium, Fremont, CA, USA). Samples were added 1:1 into 2× RNA loading dye (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and heated at 70°C for 5 min before crashing on ice for 2 min. Samples were loaded into denaturing agarose gel and subject to 100 V for 30 min. The RNA bands were visualized using UV transillumination.

To generate external calibration standards, ATP was incubated with either CC-AG or CC-AU under identical conditions (with and without NP1) to produce digested (CC-A and ATP) and undigested (capped trinucleotide and ATP) standard sets. These samples were used to prepare the calibration curve for quantification of capped RNA.

Liquid chromatography

Chromatographic separation was performed using a 1260 Infinity II HPLC system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) operated at a flow rate of 800 μL/min for an Onyx Monolithic C18 100 × 3.0 mm ID column (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA; Cat. No. CH0-8158) or 1,500 μL/min for an Onyx Monolithic C18 100 × 4.6 mm ID column (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA; Cat. No. CH0-7643). The mobile phases were (1) 10 mM dibutylammonium acetate in LC-MS grade water and (2) LC-MS grade acetonitrile. Onyx Monolithic C18 columns (100 × 3 or 4.6 mm) (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) were used for analyte separation. The gradient was programmed as follows: 2.5%–20% B (0–5 min), 20%–100% B (5–7 min), 100% B (7–8 min), and re-equilibration at 2.5% B (8–10 min). The total runtime per sample was 10 min.

Mass spectrometry

Detection was performed on a QTRAP 4500 mass spectrometer equipped with a Turbo V electrospray ionization source (Sciex, Marlborough, MA, USA). Negative ion mode was used for all acquisitions with the following source and compound-dependent parameters: CUR (curtain gas) = 55, CAD (collision activated dissociation) = high, TEM (temperature) = 650°C–700°C, GS1 (source gas 1) = 35, GS2 (source gas 2) = 35, DP (declustering potential) = −60, EP (entrance potential) = −10, CE (collision energy) = −60, and CXP (collision cell exit potential) = −7.

The information-dependent acquisition (IDA) method wizard available using Sciex Analyst software (Sciex, Marlborough, MA, USA; v.1.8.3) was used to create an IDA method with the intention of identifying ideal transitions for respective cap analytes. The method was 5 min long and operated under continuous flow, direct inject conditions at 7 μL/min. MS parameters were used as mentioned previously and the enhanced mass scanning scan range was 400–1,200 Da. Enhanced product ion method scanned from 50 to 1,200 Da at a scan rate of 10,000 Da/s. Only ions that exceeded 6,000 cps were collected and former target ions were excluded for 15 s and after more than two occurrences.

Multiple reaction monitoring was employed to detect cap analytes, including ATP, CC-A, and CC-AG or CC-AU. Transitions were selected based on observations from IDA analysis and optimized to achieve maximum signal and consistent retention time across all analytes. Final optimized parameters may differ slightly depending on instrument configuration.

Quantification and data processing

Digested and no digest standards were serially diluted 10-fold in LC-MS/MS grade water to generate concentrations of 1, 0.1, 0.01, and 0.001 μM for calibration curve generation. Each concentration was injected at volumes of 5–20 μL to establish a 10-point external calibration range (0.01–20 pmol). Samples were prepared in LC-compatible autosampler vials (Microsolv Technology, Leland, NC, USA) and injected at a typical load of 10 pmol per run.

IDA results were analyzed utilizing Sciex OS Explorer software (Sciex, Marlborough, MA, USA; v.2.1.0). Acquisition results files were integrated into the software and corresponding precursor ions were identified based on theoretical m/z ratio. Product ions of precursors were structurally confirmed using the software by assigning the respective analytes chemical structure to the acquired spectrum. Chemical structures were either acquired from ChemSpider (Royal Society of Chemistry; https://www.chemspider.com) or created using MolView (https://molview.org). Product ions were associated with respective chemical structures and key transitions were identified based on intensity and uniqueness to the original structure.

Data acquisition and integration were conducted using Analyst software (Sciex, Marlborough, MA, USA; v.1.8.3). Peak integration was performed automatically and manually reviewed; no smoothing was applied, and baseline was set using the software default algorithm. Quantitative data were exported as text files, processed in Microsoft Excel, and fitted using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad, Boston, MA, USA; v.10.6.1).

Calibration curves were generated by linear regression (r2 > 0.995 for all analytes), and analyte amounts for each sample were calculated using the slope and intercept for each analyte. Background signal was corrected by subtracting bracketed blank injection preceding each calibration or sample set and subtracted from measured peak areas. Quantitative acceptance criteria were applied to ensure data quality and consistency across calibration and sample analyses. Acceptance criteria included (1) qualifier to quantifier ion ratios within ±20% of the mean ratio observed in calibration standards, consistent with accepted LC-MS/MS practices for confirming analyte identity and minimizing matrix-dependent effects; (2) back-calculated calibration concentrations within ±15% of nominal values for concentrations at or above the lower LOQ and within ±20% near the LOQ; and (3) replicate precision with RSD < 15%, unless otherwise stated.
