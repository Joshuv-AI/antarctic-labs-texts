Fibrous foods system from lignocellulosic biomass
Authors: Haitao Yu, Jianchun Jiang, Hao Xu, Jingcong Xie, Kaili Zhang, Ying Xu, Kui Wang
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

With continuous global population growth, intensifying constraints on arable land resources, and frequent occurrence of extreme weather events, traditional food systems are increasingly strained by resource limitations, environmental pressures, and rising production costs, with implications for public health. In parallel, the accelerating global aging trend and the expanding prevalence of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and hyperglycemia are driving a rapid shift from merely “satisfying basic needs” to “promoting health.” In 2023, the United Nations emphasized the importance of agricultural development, food security, and nutrition for global sustainable development, while the African Union’s Agenda 2063 also proposed improving the productivity of modern agriculture and the added value of agricultural products to ensure food and nutrition security. In recent years, the General Office of the State Council has explicitly called for adopting a “broad food perspective” and building a diversified food supply system, harnessing food resources from forests, and deriving calories and protein from plants. In 2025, China’s annual production of forest foods is approximately 240 million tons. Furthermore, emphasis has been placed on expanding both direct and indirect food sources, tapping into novel food resources, and ensuring an effective supply of diverse food products. Securing future food supplies therefore hinges not merely on increasing production but on transitioning toward a food system that is more sustainable, efficient, and health-oriented.1,2

In this letter, we propose a strategy for the steerable depolymerization and fractional utilization of lignocellulosic biomass to prepare fibrous foods (Figure 1A). Fibrous foods products primarily refer to functional products (such as monosaccharides, oligosaccharides, proteins, and dietary fibers [DFs]) prepared from lignocellulosic biomass through green biorefining technologies. The main chemical components of lignocellulosic biomass include 40%–50% cellulose, 25%–35% hemicellulose, and 15%–30% lignin,3 and it is currently used primarily in energy, materials, and chemicals. Due to its rich carbohydrate composition, it holds tremendous potential for use in the production of fibrous foods and functional nutritional products.4 The health benefits of fibrous foods are largely attributed to their rich composition of DFs, prebiotic oligosaccharides, and fermentable proteins. These naturally derived components have been shown to regulate glucose absorption in the digestive tract, improve gut microbiota, promote cholesterol metabolism, and significantly control postprandial blood glucose, blood pressure, and inflammatory responses.5 Numerous studies have demonstrated the considerable potential of functional fibrous foods in nutritional regulation and disease prevention among elderly populations with chronic diseases.6Figure 1Strategies for preparing fibrous foods from lignocellulosic biomass(A) The fractional utilization of lignocellulosic biomass to prepare fibrous foods and the functional regulation effects.(B–E) Enzymatic hydrolysis (B and C), solid-state fermentation (D), and hydrothermal pretreatment extraction (E) strategy for producing monosaccharides, oligosaccharides with prebiotic function, proteins, and dietary fibers.

Pretreatment technology is a critical step in the conversion of lignocellulosic biomass into fibrous foods. The main objectives are to break down the complex cell wall structure of lignocellulose, improve the accessibility of cellulose and hemicellulose, and preserve natural functional components wherever possible. Currently, lignocellulosic biomass pretreatment technologies primarily include physical methods (mechanical disintegration, steam explosion), chemical methods (acid-alkali pretreatment, organic solvents, ionic liquids, and deep eutectic solvents), biological methods, and their combined techniques. Although these technologies have been widely applied in the production of biofuels and bio-based chemicals, their direct application in food production is still hindered by issues related to food-grade safety, chemical residues, energy consumption, and cost. For example, solvent residues or byproduct formation hinder the development of food-grade products. Therefore, future pretreatment technologies for the production of fibrous foods should focus on food-grade standards, environmental friendliness, and sustainability.

Monosaccharides

Monosaccharides are defined as the most elementary carbohydrate units. Glucose is the most direct energy source: it can accelerate muscle glycogen synthesis during periods of hunger, strenuous exercise, intense mental work, or high metabolic states, helping to alleviate fatigue. Xylose is recognized for its low calorific value and prebiotic properties, which have been demonstrated to stimulate the proliferation of beneficial intestinal bacteria. In contrast, arabinose has been demonstrated to exhibit physiological functions, including regulating blood sugar levels, providing antioxidant benefits, and enhancing intestinal flora (Figure 1B). At present, industrial production of edible glucose mainly relies on starch via enzymatic or hydrolytic conversion. Compared with grain-based raw materials, lignocellulosic biomass offers significant advantages, including widespread availability, low cost, and independence from competition with the human food supply, making it a promising non-grain source for functional sugars. Biorefinery technologies have enabled efficient conversion of lignocellulosic biomass into sugar platform compounds7; corn cobs and stover, rich in holocellulose, are preferred raw materials. In major agricultural provinces such as Shandong, Henan, and Heilongjiang, enterprises have achieved large-scale production of glucose, xylose, and arabinose from agricultural residues. For instance, the Anhui Fengyuan Group converts 3 tons of corn stover into 1 ton of mixed sugars. Developing fibrous foods based on monosaccharides supports food security and sustainable biomanufacturing. Developing fibrous foods based on monosaccharides provides new energy and nutritional sources and alleviates pressure on traditional food systems, offering a promising pathway toward food security and sustainable development.

Oligosaccharides

Lignocellulosic biomass also has been shown to have considerable potential for the production of functional oligosaccharides, including common types such as xylo-oligosaccharides (XOSs), manno-oligosaccharides (MOSs), fructo-oligosaccharides (FOSs), and cello-oligosaccharides (COSs).8 These oligosaccharides exhibit poor absorption rates in the intestine and can selectively stimulate the growth of beneficial bacteria in the colon, increasing the number of probiotics, thereby optimizing the intestinal flora and playing a significant prebiotic role. These oligosaccharides also reduce levels of fatty acids, phosphates, cholesterol, and triglycerides in the intestine. Similarly, most oligosaccharides are produced by enzymatic or acid hydrolysis (under strictly controlled reaction conditions) of agricultural residues, primarily crop straw and processing byproducts such as corn cobs and corn stover (Figure 1C). Enzymatic hydrolysis is the preferred method for commercial production due to its milder reaction conditions, higher product specificity, and reduced byproduct formation. In the process of enzymatic hydrolysis, the glycosidic bonds between sugar units in cellulose or hemicellulose are cleaved, resulting in the formation of oligosaccharides and monosaccharides with a wide range of polymerization potential. The preparation of oligosaccharides from lignocellulosic biomass as fibrous foods can meet human needs for metabolic health and nutritional regulation, representing a sustainable economic model.

Proteins

Lignocellulosic biomass is rich in carbohydrates, and microbial fermentation can produce abundant proteins, meeting both human protein demands in the food and supplement industries and animal feed needs. The production of proteins from lignocellulosic biomass involves two main steps. First, the complex structure of lignocellulosic biomass is decomposed through chemical pretreatment or enzymatic hydrolysis to release fermentable sugars, such as glucose, xylose, and arabinose, which serve as carbon sources for microbial growth. Subsequently, specific microbial strains, such as bacteria, yeast, or filamentous fungi, are employed in solid-state fermentation to convert these fermentable sugars into mycoproteins (Figure 1D). For example, solid-state fermentation using Yarrowia lipolytica for 15 days can convert corn stover into proteins, achieving a protein content of 16.23%. Mycoproteins have been demonstrated to possess a rich nutritional profile, and their consumption has been steadily increasing worldwide. For instance, mycoproteins can help regulate human digestive processes and improve blood cholesterol levels. Compared with a chicken-protein-based diet, mycoprotein consumption significantly reduces overall energy intake.9 Therefore, producing proteins from lignocellulosic biomass has emerged as a promising strategy to address global food security challenges while simultaneously contributing to human health improvement.

DFs

DFs are an important component of fibrous foods, mainly composed of non-starch polysaccharides and related plant-derived components that cannot be digested and absorbed by the human intestine. Based on solubility, they can be divided into soluble DFs (SDFs) and insoluble DFs (IDFs) (Figure 1E). Lignocellulosic biomass, rich in carbohydrate polymers, is an important non-grain resource for preparing DFs. It is possible to achieve efficient extraction and functional modification of DFs by selectively removing lignin and modulating the structure of cellulose and hemicellulose through pretreatment technologies. The extracted SDFs mainly consists of soluble hemicellulose, pectin, and some oligosaccharides, exhibiting good water- and oil-holding capacity, viscosity, and fermentation properties. It can act as a prebiotic to promote the growth of beneficial bacteria such as bifidobacteria and lactobacillus, regulate the composition and metabolic activity of the intestinal flora, and promote the production of beneficial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids, thereby improving glucose and lipid metabolism and reducing the risk of chronic diseases such as obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease. IDF has been demonstrated to possess high water absorption and swelling properties, which have been shown to increase stool volume, promote intestinal motility, and maintain intestinal health. Both have a synergistic effect in maintaining metabolic homeostasis in the human body.10 Overall, transforming lignocellulosic biomass into DFs represents an efficient pathway to achieve high-value utilization of non-grain resources and promote a circular economy.

Conclusion and prospects

Lignocellulosic biomass, as an abundant, widely available, and renewable non-grain resource, provides the novel materials foundation for the development of functional sugars and other fibrous foods. In the future, the development of fibrous foods should gradually shift from single-product preparation to a cascade utilization model, which can be implemented step by step according to the stability and added value of different components. For example, priority can be given to recovering nutrients such as proteins, then selectively releasing monosaccharides and functional oligosaccharides, and finally preparing the remaining fiber into DFs products, to achieve the synergistic utilization of nutrients and functional components and promote the circular economy and provide a feasible pathway for establishing a non-grain fibrous foods system.

Lignocellulosic biomass still faces several key challenges in the production of fibrous foods, such as the diverse impurities and complex structures of raw materials, which lead to low conversion rates, and the generation of byproducts during pretreatment, which complicates separation processes. The future development of fibrous foods should focus on breakthroughs in food-grade pretreatment technologies, giving priority to green processes such as hydrothermal pretreatment, subcritical water, food-grade organic acids, and bio-enzyme technologies; strengthening control over potential risk substances such as byproducts; and establishing a comprehensive food safety system covering raw materials, pretreatment, separation and purification, and product evaluation. Simultaneously, life cycle assessment and techno-economic analysis should be combined to systematically evaluate environmental sustainability and economic feasibility, aiming to achieve a balance between efficient conversion, nutrient retention, and food safety.

Current research primarily focuses on the preparation of these products and their gut-regulatory functions rather than the targeted development of functional products; consequently, preparing reconstituted products by high-temperature extrusion molding of DFs, functional oligosaccharides, and starch; developing edible products such as low-glycemic-index functional rice or noodles; and analyzing their textural properties, flavor characteristics, and consumer acceptance is a key direction for future research in fibrous foods. Moreover, in-depth studies of the physiological effects of fibrous foods are essential to achieve a cross-scale connection from basic science to health applications.
