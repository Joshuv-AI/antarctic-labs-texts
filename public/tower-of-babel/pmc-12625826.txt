Motor learning principles reported in stroke trials of upper limb task-oriented training: a scoping review
Authors: Subramanian Durairaj, Sanjukta Sardesai, John M Solomon, Mindy F Levin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveUpper limb task-oriented training (UL-TOT) is a complex intervention in which practice conditions related to motor learning principles are applied to enhance upper limb motor recovery after stroke. The Template for Intervention Description and Replication guidelines suggest that detailed reporting of a complex intervention is essential in published studies. Therefore, this review aimed to determine the extent to which practice conditions related to motor learning principles were reported in UL-TOT stroke clinical trials.MethodsA comprehensive search was done using appropriate keywords in PubMed, CINAHL, Web of Science, Scopus and Cochrane databases from 2000 to 2024. Two reviewers independently conducted title screening, abstract screening and full-text evaluation based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. A third reviewer resolved the conflicts between the two reviewers during the screening process. Finally, the articles that fulfilled the criteria were included for data extraction.Results23 802 studies were retrieved, and 166 studies were retained. Practice conditions such as practice variability (98%), dosage (97%) and movement complexity (96%) were reported more frequently, task selection for practice (75%), challenging and progressive task practice (76%) were reported frequently, practice order (57%), practice distribution (51%), feedback type (44%) and timing (44%) were reported occasionally. Feedback frequency (37%) was reported rarely.ConclusionsPractice conditions such as practice variability, dosage, movement complexity, task selection, challenging and progressive task practice were reported consistently, while practice distribution, order and feedback were reported inconsistently. Developing a standard checklist for practice conditions related to motor learning principles can improve detailed reporting of practice conditions in future UL-TOT stroke clinical trials. This can help researchers replicate and reliably implement the intervention in specific populations and build on and create more effective interventions.Keywords: Stroke, Review, REHABILITATION MEDICINE, Exercise

Introduction

Task-oriented training (TOT) is a therapeutic approach in which the individual actively engages in practising salient, context-specific, goal-oriented functional tasks repetitively guided by extrinsic feedback.1 2 TOT is a fundamental approach applied in various ways,3 including unilateral or bilateral (symmetrical or asymmetrical) arm training (BAT),4 motor relearning programme (MRP)5 and constraint-induced movement therapy (CIMT).6 TOT can also be delivered through technological approaches such as virtual reality (VR) and robotic systems. TOT is based on the dynamical systems model of motor control,7 the ecological theory of motor learning8 and follows the principles of neural plasticity.9 Several stroke rehabilitation practice guidelines recommend TOT for rehabilitating upper limb function after a stroke.10 11

Practice guidelines also recommend that practice conditions related to motor learning principles be applied during upper limb TOT (UL-TOT) to enhance motor learning. Practice structure and extrinsic feedback are the two practice conditions that are essential for motor learning.2 Practice structure includes variables such as practice distribution, practice variability, practice order, dosage, task selection for practice, movement complexity, challenging and progressive task practice. Extrinsic feedback includes variables such as feedback type, timing and frequency.2 12

The Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) guidelines suggest that detailed reporting of a complex intervention is essential in published studies.13 14 A well-structured and well-executed TOT clinical trial that adopts practice conditions related to the principles of motor learning may be of little use if these practice conditions are not reported in detail.

Few reviews have identified the motor learning principles used in the neurorehabilitation literature and examined their effects on neural plasticity and motor learning.12 15 16 Such studies could support clinicians and researchers in implementing these practice conditions during UL-TOT to enhance motor recovery. However, it remains unclear whether these practice conditions have been reported in detail in existing UL-TOT trials.

A previous review systematically investigated whether exercise training principles were reported in randomised controlled trials (RCTs) involving aerobic training and strength training in individuals with subacute and chronic stroke.17 However, to date, no studies have evaluated the extent to which the practice conditions related to motor learning principles are reported in UL-TOT, which is a complex intervention that incorporates both motor learning principles and exercise training principles.

Under-reporting of practice conditions based on motor learning principles in UL-TOT stroke trials makes it difficult for other researchers to replicate the study and for clinicians to apply the intervention effectively and achieve the desired level of benefit. Therefore, the primary objective of this scoping review was to investigate whether, and to what extent, practice conditions related to motor learning principles have been reported in stroke clinical trials involving UL-TOT. This review: (1) may inform clinicians and researchers about the extent to which the practice conditions are reported in stroke trials involving UL-TOT; (2) may help determine whether observed outcomes reflect the true efficacy of TOT or are influenced by methodological limitations in its implementation and (3) can help standardise reporting practices and improve the overall quality of evidence in stroke rehabilitation research.

Methodology

This scoping review was conducted based on the methodological framework provided in the Joanna Briggs Manual of Evidence Synthesis guidelines18 and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension guidelines for Scoping Review (PRISMA-ScR).19 The PRISMA-ScR checklist is provided in online supplemental table 6.

Search strategy

Search terms and keywords were developed based on population (stroke, cerebrovascular accident), intervention (task-oriented training, task-specific training, bilateral arm training, motor relearning programme, constraint-induced movement therapy) and area of interest (upper limb, upper extremity). Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms were taken from PubMed, Cochrane and CINAHL databases and alternative keywords from the published studies. A search strategy was formulated using the MeSH terms and keywords, and a comprehensive search was performed in PubMed, CINAHL, Web of Science, Scopus and Cochrane databases between January 2000 and May 2020. The search was again updated in the five databases using the same MeSH terms and keywords from June 2020 to June 2024. The details of search terms, keywords and MeSH terms used are shown in online supplemental table 1, and the formulation of the search strategy is in online supplemental table 2.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion

Studies were included if they (1) analysed the effect of TOT on people in acute, subacute or chronic stage of stroke who were above the age of 18 years; (2) compared one type of TOT with other types of TOT or with conventional therapy; (3) delivered the intervention face to face with a therapist/participant ratio of 1:1; (4) were RCTs, non-RCTs, cross-over, case series, case studies or single-group pre–post studies and (5) were published between January 2000 and June 2024 in the English language.

Exclusion

Studies were excluded if they delivered (1) technology-based TOT such as VR or robotics; (2) strength training techniques without TOT; (3) neurophysiological approaches (Rood, Bobath or Proprioceptive Neuromuscular Facilitation), mirror therapy, mental practice, pharmacological or surgical interventions with or without TOT or were prospective cohort studies.

Selection of studies

Studies retrieved from the search strategy were exported to EndNote V.X5 software (Thomson Reuters Corporation, Canada). After removing duplicates, the remaining studies were imported into Rayyan software.20 Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, two reviewers (SD and SS) independently reviewed the titles and chose the studies for abstract review. The same reviewers independently reviewed the abstracts, and the studies selected by both reviewers were submitted to full-text review. Conflicts between the reviewers during title, abstract and full-text screening were resolved by a third reviewer (JMS). The decision taken by the third reviewer was considered the final decision. After resolving the conflicts in the full-text review, the studies were included for data extraction.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were involved in the design or conduct of this study.

Data collection process

Data extraction was done by two independent reviewers (SD and SS) from all included studies using a standardised data extraction form. Any conflicts in the extracted data were resolved by the third reviewer (JMS). The included studies used standard definitions to extract the data (practice conditions related to motor learning principles). The details of the standard definitions used to extract the data from the included studies are shown in online supplemental table 3. The reference studies cited for the detailed treatment protocol in the included studies were also screened for data extraction.

Data synthesis

Categorisation of practice conditions related to motor learning principles reported in UL-TOT stroke trials

Each practice condition, such as practice distribution, practice variability, practice order, dosage, task selection, movement complexity, challenging and progressive task practice, feedback type, timing and frequency, was categorised as (1) Reported—if the practice condition was reported, (2) Not reported—if the practice condition was not reported or (3) Unclear or inconsistently reported—if the reviewer was unable to make a clear decision.

Data extracted from the studies were numerically coded and analysed in EZE (easy R) software, and the results were reported descriptively in the form of percentages. Percentages of practice conditions were categorised on 5-point Likert scale as: very rarely (1%–20%), rarely (21%–40%), occasionally (41%–60%), frequently (61%–80%) and more frequently (81%–100%).

Results

Study selection

The systematic search in 5 databases identified 23 802 studies. After duplicate removal, 11 911 studies were included for the title and abstract screening. 11 589 studies that failed to fulfil the inclusion criteria during the title and abstract screening were excluded. The full texts of the remaining 322 studies were retrieved and screened for the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and 156 studies were excluded. Finally, 166 studies were included for data extraction and analysis. The PRISMA flow chart reports the details of the selection process of studies (figure 1).

Study characteristics

Of the 166 included studies, 65 were RCTs, 46 were non-RCTs, 27 were single-group pre–post studies, 14 were case studies, 4 were cross-over trials, 9 were case series and 1 used an A-B-A study design. In terms of stroke stage, the majority of studies (51% or 85 studies) were conducted in individuals with chronic stroke, 22% (37 studies) in those with subacute stroke, 21% (35 studies) in individuals with mixed stages of stroke, 4% (6 studies) in individuals with acute stroke and in 2% (3 studies), the stage of stroke was unclear. Regarding the study setting, 81% (135 studies) were conducted in hospitals, clinics or rehabilitation centres, 5% (8 studies) in home settings, 4% (7 studies) in both clinical and home settings, and in 10% (16 studies) of the studies, the setting was unclear.

Practice conditions related to motor learning principles reported in UL-TOT stroke clinical trials

In the 166 included studies, practice structure variables, such as practice variability, dosage and movement complexity, were reported more frequently (81%–100%), and task selection for practice, challenging and progressive task practice was reported frequently (61%–80%). However, practice order, practice distribution, feedback type and timing were reported occasionally (41%–60%) and feedback frequency was reported rarely (21%–40%). The details and percentage of practice conditions reported, not reported and unclear in the individual UL-TOT stroke clinical trials are shown in online supplemental table 4 and figure 2.

Percentages of practice conditions related to motor learning principles reported, not reported and unclear in the included studies. The green colour represents the percentage of practice conditions reported, the red colour represents the percentage of practice conditions not reported and the yellow colour represents the percentage of practice conditions reported that were unclear.

In studies that reported practice order schedules (57%), 34% of the studies delivered blocked practice, 11% random practice and 55% delivered both blocked and random practice. In ‘practice distribution schedule’ reported studies (51%), massed practice was delivered in most of the reported studies (61%) compared with distributed practice (39%). In studies that reported feedback type (44%), knowledge of performance (KP) was reported in 13%, knowledge of results (KR) in 65%, and both KP and KR in 22%. Feedback information was given as motivation in 27%, about error correction in 6% and motivation and error correction in 11% of the studies. In ‘feedback timing’ reported studies (44%), 92% of the studies reported that feedback was delivered at the end of the trial or session, 3% during the trial, and 5% both during and after the trial completion. Regarding feedback frequency (37%), 2% reported average feedback, 7% reported faded feedback, 8% reported summary feedback, 10% reported relative frequency feedback, 47% reported bandwidth feedback and 26% reported mixed feedback (including summary, average and bandwidth feedback).

In studies that reported task selection for practice (75%), 37% reported that tasks were selected by a therapist, 10% by a participant, and 53% by both participant and therapist. Among studies that reported practice variability (98%), 98% of the studies delivered variable practice and 2% delivered constant practice. Among studies that reported movement complexity (96%), 46% reported whole task practice and 54% reported part progressed to whole task practice. Among studies that reported challenging and progressive task practice (76%), 94% reported challenging and progressive task practice and 6% reported non-challenging and non-progressive task practice.

In studies that reported the dosage (97%), the amount of practice quantified as duration was 80%, the number of repetitions was 4%, and the number of repetitions and duration was 16%. The median dosage in the form of the amount of practice, frequency and duration reported in various TOT techniques, such as unilateral or BAT trials, was approximately 1 hour and 1 session per day, 4 sessions per week for 5 weeks; BAT (symmetric) trials were 1 hour and 1 session per day, 5 sessions per week for 4 weeks; MRP trials were 2 hours and 1 session per day, 5 sessions per week for 4 weeks; and CIMT trials were 3 hours and 1 session per day, 5 sessions per week for 2 weeks.

Discussion

This scoping review evaluated the practice conditions related to motor learning principles reported in UL-TOT stroke clinical trials, excluding those involving technology-based TOT such as VR and robotic systems. We included trials that delivered TOT manually, as this approach is low-cost and easy to implement in various settings, including clinics and homes, making it globally accessible. In contrast, technology-based TOT approaches are limited by high costs, particularly in resource-limited settings. Therefore, reviewing the practice conditions reported in manually delivered TOT is valuable for clinicians, as it is more widely used than technology-based TOT. Additionally, technology-based TOT methods use preprogrammed tasks incorporated with motor learning principles and provide automatic data tracking for easy reporting across participants. Combining data from technology-based TOT studies with those from manually delivered TOT studies may introduce bias, potentially overstating the extent to which motor learning principles are reported in manually implemented TOT.

The results of this review showed that practice conditions such as practice variability, dosage, movement complexity, challenging and progressive task practice, as well as task selection for practice, were consistently reported. However, a few essential practice conditions (practice distribution, practice order and feedback) that have an association with large postintervention and follow-up effect sizes were only occasionally reported.

The practice distribution schedule applied during TOT improves both performance and retention. Available literature suggests that distributed practice enhances performance and retention. In contrast, massed practice improves retention.21 Although the exact mechanism behind the effects of distributed practice on performance is unclear, it is hypothesised that these effects may be due to the alleviation of the ‘repetition suppression effect’ and fatigue-like mechanisms known as ‘reactive inhibition’.22 Massed and distributed practice improves retention through synaptic plasticity and synaptic and system consolidation.22 In 51% of the studies, the practice distribution schedule was reported either as ‘massed’, ‘distributed’, or ‘intensive’ practice or in terms of ‘active training time and rest period’. In 32% of the studies, neither ‘massed’ nor ‘distributed’, ‘intensive’ practice, nor ‘active training time and rest period’ was specified. In 17% of the studies, the practice distribution schedule was unclearly reported as ‘break time was given to reduce fatigue’. A few studies reported practice time for each task or the number of repetitions without active and rest time, active training periods without rest time and referencing treatment protocols in brief with technique manuals.

Tasks can be practised in either blocked, serial or random order. Variable task practice delivered in random or serial order can enhance retention and transfer compared with task practice in a constant or blocked order.23 24 In studies that reported blocked order (34%), each task was practised repetitively in sets or minutes. In studies that reported random order (11%), tasks were randomly selected and practised, or participants reached random targets. In studies that reported blocked and random order (55%), it was unclear whether within-task variability was applied in 35% of studies and in which order it was delivered. In studies that did not report the practice order (19%), 22% of studies reported the tasks that were practised along with within-task variability, 66% of studies reported the tasks that were practised without within-task variability, and in 12% of studies, neither tasks practised nor within-task variability were reported. In 24% of studies that unclearly reported the practice order, 31% of studies reported the tasks that were practised along with within-task variability, 44% of studies reported the tasks that were practised without within-task variability, 20% of studies reported neither tasks practised nor within-task variability, and in 5% of studies, the task practised was reported in general along with within-task variability.

In TOT, feedback can be provided as KP or KR. Evidence suggests that KP can improve movement quality and motor performance during skill acquisition and retention. KR can improve motor performance during skill acquisition and movement quality during retention.25 Feedback can be delivered either during an ongoing action (concurrent), or after the task or session has been completed (terminal). The frequency of feedback can be continuous, faded, bandwidth, summary or average.2 26 27 Although there is a lack of evidence for the timing and frequency of feedback, concurrent and faded feedback can promote better motor learning.28 In studies that reported feedback type (44%), KP was provided about the missing movement components and KR about movement trajectory and time, number of repetitions or successful repetitions. KP was provided either during or at the end of each trial, and KR was given after the completion of each trial or at the end of the set of trials or sessions. In studies that reported the frequency of feedback (37%), summary feedback was reported in the form of a percentage (20% KR), faded feedback as ‘reduced feedback’, and bandwidth feedback as ‘feedback is given when compensatory movements were observed or when small improvements in task performance were observed’, relative frequency feedback as ‘feedback about the number of successful repetitions after each trial’, average feedback as ‘bar diagrams on a computer screen after each block and after 4 blocks of training’. In studies that unclearly reported the type (25%), timing (25%) and frequency (29%) of feedback, it was reported in general as feedback that was given without mentioning the type, timing and frequency. In studies that did not report feedback type (31%), timing (31%) and frequency (34%), neither feedback type nor timing and frequency were mentioned.

In TOT, a task can be selected by participants, a therapist or both participants and therapist. Task practice based on the participant’s goals, interests and needs can motivate them to participate in task practice actively. It could be perceived as a meaningful practice in facilitating the recovery process.28,30 In 10% of the studies, participants were asked to select their preferred daily tasks, or the Canadian Occupational Performance Measure was used to identify the tasks desired by participants, which were then practised. In 37% of the studies, standardised tasks designed by the therapist were practised. In 53% of the studies, based on the participant’s preference and specific joint movement deficits, the participant and therapist selected a subset of tasks from a developed battery for practice. In 12% of the studies, individual tasks were practised, and within-task variability was reported, but it was unclear whether the therapist or the participant selected the task. Studies that did not report the task selection (13%) reported the individual tasks that were practised along with techniques used, such as ‘MRP’ or CIMT, without reporting the shaping.

Task practice during TOT can be delivered either as constant or variable practice. Evidence suggests that practice variability within a task is highly associated with large postintervention effect sizes.16 23 24 In studies that delivered constant task practice (2%), single standardised task practice was delivered. In 98% of the studies that reported variable practice, 85% reported practice variability in individual tasks, and 15% reported practice variability in general as the practice of ‘functional activities, meaningful tasks, reaching activities and activities of daily living’.

Task practice can be delivered as whole or part practice, or part practice progressing to whole task practice based on the individual’s motor abilities. In 46% of the studies, the tasks were practised in their entirety. In 54% of the studies, daily tasks were broken down into functional components, each practised repetitively. Subsequently, each mastered component was combined into a functional sequence to perform the whole task.

The challenge point framework suggests that an optimal challenge point exists, where learning is maximised without a decline in performance, and this point changes with the improvement in an individual’s motor and cognitive abilities.31 Evidence supports that challenging and progressive task practice increases corticomotor excitability and promotes motor skill learning compared with non-challenging and non-progressive task practice.32 33

Challenging and progressive task practice was reported in 76% of the studies. Conversely, 13% of studies did not report on the use of challenging and progressive task practice, either in general or in detail. In 11% of the studies, the type of task practised and within-task variability were reported, but it remained unclear whether the task practice was challenging and progressive.

Among the studies that reported challenging and progressive task practice (76%), 6% of studies implemented non-challenging and non-progressive task practice, while 94% delivered challenging and progressive task practice. In studies that implemented challenging and progressive task practice (94%), 59% provided detailed descriptions of the parameters used for progression, such as speed, distance, height, number of repetitions, weight, object size and task complexity. However, in 41% of the studies, challenging and progressive task practice or shaping was mentioned in general without specifying the parameters used for progression.

In UL-TOT, dosage parameters are expressed as the amount, frequency and duration of practice. Evidence suggests a moderate dose-response relationship with the outcome.34 In 82% of the studies, the amount of practice was reported as time scheduled for therapy without reporting the active practice and rest times. Studies that reported dosage as time scheduled for treatment can challenge readers to estimate how much time the participants actively used their arms during a session because numerous factors, such as the individual’s arm and hand impairment level, practice distribution schedule, task complexity and fatigue, can affect the amount of practice in a session. Frequency was primarily expressed in the number of sessions per day or number of days per week and the duration over which the intervention was delivered, mainly as the number of weeks.

Although available evidence suggests no association between the number of practice conditions used in UL-TOT and post-intervention effect size, each practice condition benefits motor learning and recovery when applied during UL-TOT.12 16 17 As practice distribution is associated with a significant postintervention effect size, while practice order and feedback are linked to both a significant post-intervention effect size and a high follow-up effect size, the results of our review suggest that these essential practice conditions that positively affect skill acquisition and retention should be consistently reported in future studies. A possible reason for the inconsistent reporting of essential motor learning principles could be that researchers are uncertain about which practice conditions need to be reported, as numerous practice condition variables are reported in neurorehabilitation literature and TOT stroke clinical trials.12 16 17

Conclusions

This scoping review showed that despite some practice conditions related to motor learning principles being reported consistently in TOT stroke clinical trials, essential practice conditions such as practice distribution, practice order and feedback were only occasionally reported. Although the TIDieR guideline is sufficient for reporting interventions,35 UL-TOT requires more details about practice conditions to ensure appropriate replication of the intervention in clinical settings. Knowledge of these details can provide insight into the active ingredients in complex interventions like TOT and enable researchers to modify and optimise the TOT interventions in future trials. Therefore, developing a standard checklist specific to the practice conditions related to motor learning principles may improve the quality and consistency of reporting in UL-TOT clinical trials. A preliminary version of such a checklist is provided in online supplemental table 5.
