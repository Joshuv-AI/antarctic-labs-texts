Diacerein alleviates endometrial fibrosis in an intrauterine adhesion model via ferroptosis inhibition
Authors: Cong Shi, Jianhua Sun, Jumin Niu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntrauterine adhesion (IUA) is a significant contributor to uterine infertility, primarily characterized by endometrial fibrosis. Although diacerein exhibits anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic properties, its therapeutic potential in IUA remains unclear. This study investigated the protective effects of diacerein in an IUA rat model induced by mechanical injury. Histological analysis revealed that diacerein treatment alleviated endometrial damage, and immunohistochemical staining confirmed the restoration of CK19 and CK18 expression, indicating improved epithelial integrity and regeneration. Diacerein mitigated endometrial fibrosis by inhibiting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), as evidenced by increased E-cadherin and decreased N-cadherin expression, likely via suppression of TGFβ/SMAD2 signaling. Diacerein exerted anti-inflammatory effects in IUA rats. Notably, diacerein inhibited ferroptosis by reducing lipid peroxidation, limiting Fe2+ accumulation, and modulating ferroptosis-related proteins, including ACSL4, SLC7A11, GPX4, and FTH1. The protective effects of diacerein were mirrored by ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 treatment but reversed by ferroptosis inducer Erastin, confirming that diacerein alleviates endometrial fibrosis in IUA rats through ferroptosis suppression. Mechanistically, diacerein modulated the NRF2/HMGB1 signaling pathway, restoring NRF2 and HO1 levels while downregulating HMGB1 expression. Collectively, these findings suggest that diacerein effectively attenuates ferroptosis-mediated fibrosis in IUA, highlighting ferroptosis inhibition as a promising therapeutic strategy for IUA management.Keywords: intrauterine adhesion, diacerein, endometrial fibrosis, ferroptosis, NRF2/HMGB1 pathway

Introduction

Intrauterine adhesion (IUA) arises from impaired endometrial repair following mechanical trauma or infection, ultimately resulting in oligomenorrhea, amenorrhea, recurrent pregnancy loss, and secondary infertility [1, 2]. The hallmark of IUA is endometrial fibrosis, characterized by excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition that replaces hormonally responsive endometrial tissue. Consequently, the endometrial cavity becomes obstructed by avascular, non-functional fibrotic synechiae bundles [2]. Persistent inflammation plays a crucial role in driving fibrotic progression, further exacerbating tissue dysfunction [3, 4]. At present, hysteroscopic transcervical resection of adhesion (TCRA) remains the mainstay treatment for intrauterine adhesions (IUA), and is frequently combined with hormonal therapy and non-degradable stents as postoperative adjuvant measures. However, this approach has significant drawbacks, including a high recurrence rate, limited efficacy in preserving fertility, and challenges in endometrial regeneration due to the unique anatomical constraints of the uterus [5]. These limitations have prompted researchers to explore more effective therapeutic strategies.

Cell death plays a crucial role in the onset and development of fibrosis in various organ systems [6]. Among the newly identified types of regulated cell death, ferroptosis has recently garnered considerable interest due to its implication in fibrotic disorders. Excessive iron deposition and lipid peroxidation have been shown to promote fibrogenesis, whereas inhibitors of ferroptosis exert promising anti-fibrotic effects [7]. Previous study has identified ferroptosis as a key contributor to endometrial fibrosis in IUA [8]. In endometrial tissues of IUA patients, ferroptosis is markedly increased, particularly in endometrial epithelial cells, as indicated by increased expression of the lipid peroxidation marker 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal (4-HNE) and reduced expression of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), a key ferroptosis suppressor. This ferroptotic activity facilitates epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in endometrial epithelial cells, driving the secretion of pro-fibrotic mediators such as transforming growth factor β (TGFβ) and platelet-derived growth factor BB (PDGF-BB). These cytokines subsequently induce the differentiation of endometrial stromal cells into myofibroblasts, culminating in fibrotic tissue formation. Notably, pharmacological inhibition of ferroptosis has been shown to mitigate endometrial fibrosis in IUA animal models [9, 10], emphasizing its potential as a therapeutic target for IUA-associated fibrosis.

Diacerein, an anthraquinone derivative, functions as an interleukin-1 beta inhibitor with notable anti-inflammatory properties and has been recognized as an effective treatment for osteoarthritis [11, 12]. Beyond its role in osteoarthritis management, diacerein exhibits antioxidant, anti-apoptotic, antidiabetic, and antineoplastic properties, demonstrating therapeutic potential in various conditions, including kidney injury, diabetes mellitus, cancer, testicular damage, and lung injury [13,14,15,16,17]. In gynecological disorders, diacerein has been shown to alleviate endometrial hyperplasia and polycystic ovary syndrome by reducing inflammatory responses and oxidative stress [18,19,20], highlighting its potential as a treatment for reproductive health conditions. However, the therapeutic potential of diacerein in IUA remains unexplored, and the underlying mechanisms have yet to be elucidated.

The purpose of this study was to investigate the potential of diacerein as a therapeutic agent for IUA by alleviating endometrial fibrosis. Additionally, we explored the involvement of ferroptosis in this process to better understand the underlying mechanisms.

Materials and Methods

IUA rat model and diacerein treatment

Specific pathogen free (SPF) female Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (8–10 weeks old, 180–220 g) were used to establish IUA models via mechanical injury. The rats were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Animal License No.: SCXK (Liao) 2025-0001, Benxi, China) and kept in SPF-grade animal rooms at temperature of 22 ± 2°C, humidity of 50 ± 5%, with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, free access to standard chow and water. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the China Medical University (KT20251387) and conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH).

In the first part of the study, rats were randomly assigned to five groups (n=6 per group): sham group, sham+diacerein-H (DIA-H) group, IUA group, IUA+diacerein-L (DIA-L) group, and IUA+DIA-H group. In the IUA group, rats were anesthetized with isoflurane inhalation (3% for induction and 2% for maintenance), and their lower abdominal fur was shaved. A longitudinal incision (approximately 20 mm) was made, and a 16-gauge needle was inserted and rotated to abrade the entire endometrial lining of both uterine horns until the walls appeared rough and pale, while ensuring the integrity of the uterine serosa. The uterus was then rinsed with sterile saline, and the incisions were sutured. In the diacerein-treated groups, following IUA induction, rats received either 25 mg/kg/day (low dose) or 50 mg/kg/day (high dose) of diacerein (DIA, dissolved in 0.1% carboxymethylcellulose sodium (CMC-Na) solution, CAS No: 13739-02-1; Aladdin regents Co., Ltd.) via oral gavage once a day. The sham group underwent laparotomy without additional intervention. After 35 days of treatment, the animals were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium at a dose of 200 mg/kg, and blood samples and uterine tissues were collected immediately after euthanasia. The blood samples were centrifuged at 1,200 r/min for 10 min at 4°C to obtain serum, and uterine tissues were partially fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 24 h for histopathological analysis, with the remaining uterine tissues were frozen in liquid nitrogen for molecular detection.

In the second part of the study, rats were randomly allocated into five groups (n=6 per group): sham group, IUA group, IUA+Fer-1 group, IUA+DIA-H group, and IUA+DIA-H+Erastin group. Rats in the IUA+Fer-1 group received intraperitoneal injection of ferrostatin-1 (5 mg/kg, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) every other day. In the IUA+DIA-H+Erastin group, diacerein (50 mg/kg/day) was administered via gavage daily, while erastin (20 mg/kg, MedChemExpress) was given through intraperitoneal injection every other day.

Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining

Fixed uterine tissues were dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, cleared with xylene, and embedded in paraffin at 60°C. The paraffin-embedded tissues were then sectioned into 5-µm slices. To prepare the sections for staining, they were first deparaffinized in xylene and subsequently rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Hematoxylin (H8070, Solarbio Science & Technology, Beijing, China) staining was performed for 5 min, followed by differentiation in 1% hydrochloric acid alcohol for 3 s. Staining with eosin (G1100, Solarbio Science & Technology) was carried out for 3 min. The sections were then dehydrated again through graded ethanol concentrations and cleared with xylene. Finally, they were mounted using neutral resin. Microscopic examination was conducted at 40× magnification to assess endometrial morphology. Endometrial thickness was measured, while the areas of the endometrium and endometrial lumen (mm2) were calculated. Additionally, the number of endometrial glands was counted.

Immunostaining

Immunohistochemical staining was performed to assess the expression of CK19, CK18, Collagen I, Collagen III, PTGS2, and HMGB1. Following deparaffinization and rehydration, the tissue sections underwent antigen retrieval and were blocked with goat serum. Subsequently, the slides were incubated with primary antibodies, including anti-CK19 (ab220193, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-CK18 (ab133263, Abcam), anti-Collagen I (ab270993, Abcam), anti-Collagen III (ab6310, Abcam), anti-PTGS2 (ab179800, Abcam), and anti-HMGB1 (#6893, Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA). After primary antibody incubation, the sections were treated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse or anti-rabbit IgG (31430, 31460, ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA). The immunoreactivity was visualized using diaminobenzidine (DAB; DA1010, Solarbio Science & Technology), and the nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Microscopic images were captured at 40× magnification using an OLYMPUS microscope (OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan).

To evaluate EMT in uterine tissues, immunofluorescence staining was conducted using diluted primary antibodies: anti-E-cadherin (ab231303, Abcam) and anti-N-cadherin (#14215, Cell Signaling Technology) antibodies overnight at 4°C. Then, the sections were incubated with goat anti-mouse IgG H&L conjugated to Alexa Fluor 594 (ab150116, Abcam) at room temperature for 1 h. Following nuclear counterstaining with DAPI, fluorescence images were acquired under an OLYMPUS microscope at 200× magnification.

Masson’s trichrome staining

To evaluate collagen deposition in uterine tissues, paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated prior to staining. Masson’s Trichrome Stain Kit (G1340, Solarbio Science & Technology) was used for staining, followed by a rinse with 0.2% acetic acid solution. The sections were then differentiated with 1% phosphotungstic acid and counterstained with aniline blue. After a soak in 0.2% acetic acid solution, the samples were dehydrated, treated with xylene for clearing, and mounted using neutral resin. Microscopic images were captured at 40× and 200× magnification using an OLYMPUS microscope.

Western blot analysis

Proteins were extracted from rat uterine tissues using the RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (P1045, Beyotime Biotechnology) were added to the lysis buffer at a ratio of 1:50 (v/v) before use. The protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0011, Beyotime Biotechnology). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg per sample) were separated via SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, MA, USA). After blocking with 5% nonfat milk, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against TGFβ (ab215715, Abcam), phospho-SMAD2 (Ser465/467) (#3108, Cell Signaling Technology), ACSL4 (#38493, Cell Signaling Technology), GPX4 (#59735, Cell Signaling Technology), SLC7A11 (ab307601, Abcam), FTH1 (ab75973, Abcam), NRF2 (#20733, Cell Signaling Technology), HO1 (#43966, Cell Signaling Technology), HMGB1 (#6893, Cell Signaling Technology), HOXA10 (#58891, Cell Signaling Technology), and LIF (sc-515931, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, USA). Following overnight incubation, the membranes were washed three times with TBST and then probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (31430, 31460, ThermoFisher Scientific). Protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system (P0018, Beyotime Biotechnology). The band intensities of western blotting were quantified using ImageJ software. The relative protein expression level was calculated as the ratio of target protein intensity to GAPDH.

ELISA

The levels of TNF, IL1B, and IL6 in serum were determined by commercial reagent kits (H052-1, H002-1, H007-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Quantitative RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from homogenized uterine tissues using TRIzol reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from the isolated RNA using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-PCR) was performed on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA) with PowerTrack SYBR Green Master Mix (ThermoFisher Scientific). The specific primer sequences used were as follows: Tnf, Forward: 5′-TGTGCCTCAGCCTCTTCTCATT-3′, Reverse: 5′-TTGTCACTCGAGTTTTGAGAAGATG-3′; Il1b, Forward: 5′-CTACCTATGTCTTGCCCGTGG-3′, Reverse: 5′-GGATTTTGTCGTTGCTTGTCTC-3′; Il6, Forward: 5′-GGATATAACCAGGAAATTTGCC-3′, Reverse: 5′-GTATTGCTCTGAATGACTCTGG-3′; and Gapdh, Forward: 5′-AACGGCACAGTCAAGGCTGA-3′, Reverse: 5′-ACGCCAGTAGACTCCACGACAT-3′. Gene expression levels were normalized to Gapdh, which served as the internal reference gene. Relative expression changes were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCT method.

Determination of MDA, GSH, and Fe2+

Uterine tissues were homogenized, and the resulting supernatants were collected for biochemical analysis. The concentrations of malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione (GSH), and ferrous ion (Fe2+) were quantified using commercial reagent kits (A003-1, A006-2, A039-2, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism software. Data were presented as the mean ± SD. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Diacerein promotes endometrial regeneration in IUA rats

To assess the therapeutic impact of diacerein on endometrial histopathological alterations, we established an IUA rat model using mechanical injury. Representative uterine tissue images are shown in Fig. 1A. The IUA group exhibited notable morphological abnormalities, including partial obstruction of the uterine cavity due to adhesion formation, which was alleviated following diacerein treatment. H&E staining revealed severe endometrial damage, characterized by a narrowed lumen, atrophic columnar epithelium, a reduced number of endometrial glands, and diminished endometrial thickness. Treatment with diacerein effectively mitigated these pathological changes, leading to increased endometrial thickness, gland count, endometrial area, and lumen expansion in IUA rats (Fig. 1B). To further evaluate endometrial integrity, we performed immunohistochemical staining for CK19, a cytoskeletal protein crucial for maintaining epithelial structure. In the sham group, CK19 exhibited strong cytoplasmic staining in endometrial epithelial cells, whereas its expression was markedly reduced in IUA rats. However, diacerein treatment restored CK19 expression (Fig. 1C). Additionally, endometrial epithelial regeneration was assessed by examining CK18 expression, which was primarily localized in the cytoplasm of epithelial cells and endometrial glands. Compared with the sham group, CK18 expression was lower in the model group, but diacerein treatment enhanced CK18 protein level (Fig. 1D). These findings suggested that diacerein promotes endometrial regeneration in IUA rats.

(B) H&E staining of uterine sections showing endometrial morphologic structures across groups (n=6). Scale bar=500/200 µm. (C, D) Immunohistochemical staining for CK19 and CK18 in rat uterine tissues after diacerein treatment (n=6). Scale bar=100 µm. *P<0.05, **P<0.01 vs. the indicated group.

Diacerein mitigates EMT-mediated fibrotic progression in IUA rats by inhibiting TGFβ/SMAD2 signaling

Masson’s trichrome staining revealed dense collagen fiber accumulation in the endometrial stroma of IUA rats, confirming the presence of endometrial fibrosis. However, treatment with diacerein reduced collagen deposition in the affected tissues (Fig. 2A). Immunohistochemical analysis further demonstrated that the expression of fibrosis markers Collagen I and Collagen III was significantly higher in the uteri of the model group compared to the sham group. Notably, diacerein administration led to a reduction in their expression levels (Fig. 2B). Additionally, we examined the expression of TGFβ and its downstream mediator SMAD2. Western blot analysis and immumohistochemical staining revealed a significant increase in TGFβ and phosphorylated SMAD2 levels in the uteri of the model group, whereas diacerein treatment effectively suppressed their expression (Fig. 2E). To further investigate the effect of diacerein on EMT in vivo, we assessed E-cadherin and N-cadherin expression using immunofluorescence staining. Compared with the sham group, the IUA model group exhibited a decline in E-cadherin-positive cells and an increase in N-cadherin-positive cells, indicating EMT induction. However, diacerein treatment reversed these changes, restoring E-cadherin expression while reducing N-cadherin expression (Figs. 3A and B). Overall, these findings suggested that diacerein alleviates EMT-driven fibrotic progression in IUA rats by inhibiting the TGFβ/SMAD2 signaling pathway.

Scale bar=500/200 µm. (B) Immunohistochemical analysis of collagen I, collagen III, TGF-β and p-SMAD2 expression in uterine tissues (n=6). Scale bar=100 µm. (C) Western blot analysis of TGFβ and phosphorylated SMAD2 (p-SMAD2) (n=3). *P<0.05, **P<0.01 vs. the indicated group.

Diacerein attenuates EMT in IUA rats. Immunofluorescence staining of (A) E-cadherin and (B) N-cadherin, showing EMT induction in IUA rats and its reversal following diacerein administration (n=6). Scale bar=50 µm.

Diacerein reduces inflammatory responses in IUA rats

Inflammation plays a pivotal role in the progression of IUA, contributing to endometrial injury and fibrosis. In line with this, our findings revealed a marked increase in the levels of proinflammatory cytokines, including Il1b, Tnf and Il6, in the uterine tissues of IUA rats (Fig. 4A), along with elevated serum concentrations of these cytokines (Fig. 4B). Notably, diacerein administration attenuated this inflammatory response in both uterine tissues and serum compared with the IUA group (Figs. 4A and B). These results indicated that diacerein exerts anti-inflammatory effects in IUA rats.

Diacerein reduces inflammatory responses in IUA rats. (A) The mRNA levels of inflammatory cytokines (Il1b, Il6, and Tnf) in uterine tissues detected by RT-PCR (n=6). (B) Serum concentrations of IL1B, IL6, and TNF in the IUA model and diacerein-treated rats were assessed using ELISA (n=6). Data are presented as mean ± SD. **P<0.01 vs. the indicated group.

Diacerein attenuates ferroptosis in uterine tissues of IUA rats

To explore the potential mechanisms by which diacerein influences endometrial fibrosis following IUA, we assessed its impact on ferroptosis in IUA rats. The IUA group exhibited higher level of MDA and a reduction in GSH level in uterine tissues. In contrast, diacerein treatment reversed these changes by lowering MDA level and restoring GSH level (Figs. 5A and B). Additionally, IUA modeling led to an elevation in Fe2+ content in the uterine tissues, but diacerein administration reduced Fe2+ content (Fig. 5C). Immunohistochemical analysis of the ferroptosis marker PTGS2 showed increased expression in the uterine tissues of IUA rats, whereas diacerein treatment resulted in a decrease in PTGS2 level (Fig. 5D). Western blot analysis further demonstrated elevated ACSL4 expression, alongside reduced levels of SLC7A11, GPX4, and FTH1 proteins in the IUA group compared to the sham group. Notably, diacerein treatment counteracted these ferroptosis-related protein changes in IUA rats (Fig. 5E). Taken together, these findings indicated that diacerein mitigates ferroptosis in the uterine tissues of IUA rats.

(C) Fe2+ content in uterine tissues of the IUA model and diacerein-treated rats (n=6). (D) Representative Immunohistochemical staining of PTGS2 in in uterine tissues (n=6). Scale bar=100 µm. (E) Western blot analysis was performed to measure the expression of ferroptosis-related proteins, including ACSL4, SLC7A11, GPX4, and FTH1 (n=3). Diacerein ameliorated endometrial fibrosis of IUA rats through ferroptosis suppression

To further elucidate the role of diacerein in ferroptosis-mediated endometrial fibrosis, we employed Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) to repress ferroptosis in IUA rats and Erastin to induce ferroptosis in diacerein-treated IUA rats. Intraperitoneal injection of Fer-1effectively suppressed ferroptosis, as reflected by lower MDA level, elevated GSH level, and diminished PTGS2 expression compared to the IUA group (Figs. 6A–C). Moreover, Fer-1 administration led to reduced endometrial injury, decreased collagen accumulation, and alleviated endometrial fibrosis, as confirmed by H&E staining, Masson’s trichrome staining, and immunohistochemical analysis of collagen I and collagen III (Figs. 6D–F). Diacerein exerted similar effects to Fer-1, mitigating ferroptosis and endometrial fibrosis in IUA rats (Figs. 5A–F). In contrast, Erastin treatment reversed the ferroptosis-inhibitory effects of diacerein, as evidenced by increased MDA level, reduced GSH level, and elevated ROS production (Figs. 6A–C). Furthermore, diacerein-treated IUA rats receiving Erastin exhibited exacerbated endometrial damage, increased collagen deposition, and aggravated endometrial fibrosis compared to those treated with diacerein alone (Figs. 6D–F). Collectively, these findings indicated that the antifibrotic effects of diacerein in IUA rats are, at least in part, mediated through ferroptosis suppression.

Diacerein exerts regulatory effects on the NRF2/HMGB1 signaling pathway in IUA rats

To investigate the involvement of the NRF2/HMGB1 signaling pathway in IUA and its modulation by diacerein, we evaluated the expression levels of NRF2 and HMGB1 in uterine tissues from IUA rats. Western blot analysis revealed a reduction in NRF2 and HO1 protein levels following IUA induction compared to the sham group, whereas diacerein treatment effectively restored their expression (Fig. 7A). Conversely, the Hmgb1 mRNA and HMGB1 protein levels were upregulated in IUA rats relative to sham-operated controls, but this increase was reduced by diacerein administration (Figs. 7B and C). Immunohistochemical staining further demonstrated that HMGB1 exhibited strong nuclear positivity along with cytoplasmic expression in IUA rats, while diacerein treatment reduced its staining intensity (Fig. 7D). Taken together, these findings indicated that diacerein exerts regulatory effects on the NRF2/HMGB1 signaling pathway in IUA rats.

Discussion

This study is the first to highlight diacerein as a potential treatment for IUA, showcasing its ability to reduce endometrial fibrosis, inhibit ferroptosis, suppress inflammation in IUA rats. These protective effects may be associated with its regulation of the NRF2/HO1/HMGB1 signaling pathway.

EMT is a fundamental biological process in which epithelial cells acquire mesenchymal traits, contributing significantly to fibrosis across various organs [21, 22]. In IUA, EMT in endometrial cells is believed to play a role in endometrial fibrosis, culminating in endometrial scar healing [23,24,25]. TGFβ functions as a primary inducer driving EMT during the development of tissue fibrosis [26]. By activating downstream signaling molecules such as SMAD2, SMAD3, SMAD4, and SMAD7, TGFβ facilitates epithelial cell transition into mesenchymal-like cells, thereby stimulating myofibroblast activation, promoting ECM accumulation, and inhibiting matrix degradation. These pathological changes ultimately lead to tissue fibrosis and scarring. Elevated TGFβ expression has been extensively documented in both human and animal models of IUA, with its levels closely associated with the severity of adhesion formation [27]. TGFβ is widely employed to induce EMT in endometrial epithelial cells, providing a model for exploring potential therapeutic strategies to mitigate fibrosis in IUA [28,29,30]. In our study, we observed a decrease in E-cadherin expression accompanied by an increase in N-cadherin level in the uterine tissues of IUA rats, indicative of EMT activation. Additionally, TGFβ/SMAD2 signaling was activated in the IUA model, coinciding with EMT progression. Of note, diacerein treatment significantly attenuated EMT-related phenotypic changes and suppressed the activation of the TGFβ/SMAD2 signaling pathway in endometrial tissue. These findings suggest that diacerein’s antifibrotic effects may be attributed to its ability to counteract TGFβ/SMAD2-driven EMT, offering potential therapeutic value for IUA management.

Ferroptosis arises from the uncontrolled peroxidation of membrane lipids due to disrupted redox homeostasis. GPX4, a key antioxidant enzyme, prevents lipid peroxidation by utilizing GSH as a cofactor [31]. SLC7A11 is crucial for maintaining cellular redox balance by regulating intracellular GSH level, thereby protecting against oxidative stress and ferroptosis [32]. In contrast, ACSL4 promotes ferroptosis by facilitating the esterification of CoA into polyunsaturated fatty acids, such as arachidonic acid, which accelerates phospholipid peroxidation [33]. Ferritin plays a protective role by sequestering iron ions and limiting the labile iron pool, thereby counteracting ferroptotic cell death [34]. Consistent with previous studies [8, 9], our results showed that GPX4, SLC7A11, and FTH1 were downregulated, whereas ACSL4 was upregulated in the uterine tissues of IUA rats, supporting the involvement of ferroptosis in IUA progression. Notably, diacerein treatment reversed these ferroptosis-associated alterations. To investigate whether the antifibrotic effects of diacerein are mediated via ferroptosis inhibition, we administered Erastin, a classic ferroptosis inducer, to IUA rats that had received diacerein treatment. Erastin restored ferroptosis and led to exacerbated endometrial fibrosis, reinforcing the notion that diacerein mitigates fibrosis in IUA by inhibiting ferroptosis.

Recent research indicates that the endometrium in IUA patients remains in a state of persistent inflammatory activation [3], which markedly contrasts with the transient inflammatory response observed during normal cyclic endometrial shedding. Study has demonstrated that inflammation and fibrosis coexist in endometrial tissues of IUA patients, with a positive correlation between inflammatory cytokine level and fibrotic marker level. Using animal models to track the temporal dynamics of inflammation and fibrosis and assess fibrosis severity, investigators have further demonstrated that inflammation precedes fibrosis and that persistent inflammatory activity accelerates fibrotic progression [3]. In this study, our findings revealed that diacerein administration reduced inflammatory factor levels in both endometrial tissues and serum of IUA mice, indicating that its protective effect against IUA may be attributed to the suppression of pro-inflammatory factor release following endometrial injury. Consistently, diacerein’s antifibrotic effects in liver and myocardial tissues are linked to its anti-inflammatory properties, further supporting its role in mitigating fibrosis through inflammation reduction [35, 36].

High-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) is a non-histone chromosomal protein that plays a critical role in inflammation and fibrosis [37]. Functioning as a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) molecule, HMGB1 is passively released by ferroptotic cells, and its inhibition has been proposed as a potential therapeutic approach for ferroptosis-related disorders [38]. Additionally, as a proinflammatory cytokine, HMGB1 is actively secreted by immune-inflammatory cells, where it binds to surface receptors such as RAGE and TLRs, initiating inflammatory cascades [39, 40]. Beyond its involvement in inflammation, HMGB1 contributes to EMT and fibrotic progression through activation of the TGFβ pathway [41, 42]. Notably, the NRF2/HO1 signaling pathway has been reported to suppress HMGB1 expression and release, thereby alleviating HMGB1-driven inflammation and fibrosis [42,43,44]. Building upon previous research, our study revealed an increase in HMGB1 expression in the uterine tissues of the IUA group compared with the sham group, accompanied by a decline in NRF2 and HO1 levels. Diacerein treatment effectively suppressed HMGB1 expression while enhancing NRF2 and HO1 expression, indicating that its anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic effects in IUA may be attributed to the regulation of the NRF2/HO1/HMGB1 signaling pathway.

In conclusion, diacerein effectively ameliorates IUA via suppressing ferroptosis, mitigating inflammatory responses, and inhibiting EMT-mediated endometrial fibrosis. These findings highlight the promising therapeutic potential of diacerein against ferroptosis-associated endometrial fibrosis. Moreover, our study sheds novel light on the mechanism underlying diacerein-facilitated regeneration of the damaged endometrium, providing a prospective therapeutic strategy for the maintenance of female fertility.
