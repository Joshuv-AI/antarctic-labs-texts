Black-hole evaporation for cosmological observers
T. L. Campos, C. Molina, J. A. S. Lima

Abstract
This work investigates the evaporation of black holes immersed in a de Sitter environment, using the Vaidya-de Sitter spacetime to model the process. The role of cosmological observers is highlighted in the development and Hayward thermodynamics for non stationary geometries is employed in the description of the compact objects. Results of the proposed dynamical model are compared with the usual description based on stationary geometries, with specific results for primordial black holes (PBHs). The timescale of evaporation is shown to depend significantly on the choice of cosmological observer, and may differ substantially from predictions based on stationary models in later times. Deviations are also shown with respect to the standard assertion that there is a fixed initial mass just below 10 15 ​ g ∼ 10 − 18 ​ M ⊙ 10^{15}\,\text{g}\sim 10^{-18}M_{\odot} for the PBHs which are ending their evaporation process today.



I
Introduction

Fundamentally, black holes are dynamical objects, either due to the accretion of matter that crosses their event horizon or because of the semiclassical phenomenon of Hawking radiation. Thus, black holes are never actually static, although frequently modeled by stationary geometries when their mass variation is slow.
In addition, the black-hole background can be itself dynamical. For instance, considering a primordial black hole (PBH) formed during the radiation dominated era, it will evolve during the expansion of the Universe, eventually reaching the period when the cosmological constant dominates.

Underlying this proposal is the fact that the dynamics of a PBH should assume neither a static nor a pure de Sitter description of the Universe. These two scenarios are considered to be the extremes of a spectrum of possible PBH models based on FLRW cosmology. To see how the usual Eq. (
1
) might fail, we propose to explore the other side of the spectrum, where the expansion rate is maximized. The discrepancies between the models are expected to become more pronounced in the future, when the de Sitter description should be more appropriate.
Furthermore, cosmological observers are privileged, in the sense that the evaporation of PBHs, as seen from Earth, can be effectively modeled by the framework established by these observers.

Our approach is explored and compared with the conventional static framework, challenging previously established results. Anticipating some key findings of the present work, we demonstrate that the timescales of black-hole evaporation can exhibit significant dependence on the choice of a cosmological observer. Notably, we identify scenarios in which the evaporation is decelerated, with some black holes never completely evaporating when the process is measured using a cosmological time.

II
Cosmological observers in Vaidya-de Sitter

Vaidya-de Sitter spacetime describes an asymptotically de Sitter and dynamical black hole, taking into account the backreaction of photons being accreted or emitted radially. Its line element, in outgoing Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates, is given by

ds^{2}=-\bigg[1-\frac{2GM(u)}{c^{2}r}-\frac{r^{2}}{a^{2}}\bigg]c^{2}du^{2}-2c\,dudr+r^{2}d\Omega^{2}\,,

(2)

in which
{a=\sqrt{\nicefrac{{3}}{{\Lambda}}}}
with
\Lambda
a positive cosmological constant.

The dynamics of a black hole due to its evaporation can be described by the mass function
M
of the retarded time coordinate
u

(
Hiscock 1981b
)
. On the other hand, considering cosmological black holes, such as primordial ones, it is important to be able to measure the evolution of their mass with respect to a cosmic time.
This means that it is necessary to compare the Eddington-Finkelstein time coordinate with the cosmological time, which is measured by observers that are comoving with the expansion of the Universe.

However, these observers are typically defined in homogeneous and isotropic spacetimes, and the presence of a black hole causes a perturbation on this scenario. Our procedure is to first determine a set of cosmological observers in pure de Sitter space, and extend their world lines to Vaidya-de Sitter.

In de Sitter, cosmological observers can be extracted from comoving coordinates,

\displaystyle\begin{split}ds^{2}=-c^{2}d\tau^{2}+a^{2}\cosh^{2}\frac{c\tau}{a}\left(d\chi^{2}+\sin^{2}d\Omega^{2}\right)\,,\end{split}

(3)

which privilege a set of geodesics characterized by
\chi
,
\theta
and
\phi
constant, with
\chi
ranging from
0
to
\pi
.
These are the world lines of the observers under our consideration, which follow the spacetime expansion.

In outgoing Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates for de Sitter, the line element is adapted to the emitted radial null geodesics and assumes the form of Eq. (
2
) with a vanishing
M
. We have found the transformation from comoving to outgoing Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates, valid for
0<\chi<\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}}\,
, to be

u^{\prime}=\,\begin{cases}\,\text{arctanh}\bigg(\frac{\tanh\tau^{\prime}}{\cos\chi}\bigg)-\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{1+r^{\prime}}{1-r^{\prime}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{1+\sin\chi}{1-\sin\chi}\right)\,,\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{for }0<r^{\prime}<1\,,\\
\,\text{arctanh}\bigg(\frac{\cos\chi}{\tanh\tau^{\prime}}\bigg)-\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{r^{\prime}+1}{r^{\prime}-1}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{1+\sin\chi}{1-\sin\chi}\right)\,,\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{for }1<r^{\prime}<\infty\,,\end{cases}

(4)

where

r=a\cosh\frac{c\tau}{a}\sin\chi\,,

(5)

and dimensionless retarded time (
u^{\prime}
), cosmological time (
\tau^{\prime}
) and radial position (
r^{\prime}
) are defined:

u^{\prime}\equiv\frac{cu}{a}\,,\,\,\,\tau^{\prime}\equiv\frac{c\tau}{a}\,,\,\,\,r^{\prime}\equiv\frac{r}{a}\,.

(6)

Eq. (
4
) was set so that
u^{\prime}=0
when
\tau^{\prime}=0\,
. Alternatively, instead of labeling a cosmological observer by constant values of
\chi
, it is convenient to label them by
r^{\prime}_{0}
, where
r^{\prime}_{0}
is the value of the dimensionless radius
r^{\prime}
when
\tau^{\prime}=0\,
. That is,
r^{\prime}_{0}
gives the initial position of the observer relative to the cosmological horizon:

r^{\prime}_{0}\equiv a\sin\chi\,,\hskip 14.22636pt0<r^{\prime}_{0}<1\,.

(7)

For each value of
r_{0}^{\prime}
,

\alpha:\tau\mapsto\big[u(\tau),r(\tau),\theta_{0},\phi_{0}\big]\,,

(8)

defines a curve parameterized by the cosmological time in de Sitter, showing that cosmological observers recede exponentially fast from the origin of the coordinate system. At this point, we extend this interpretation from pure de Sitter to Vaidya-de Sitter. This means that the world lines characterizing cosmological observers in the presence of the black hole will be defined by the curves of Eq. (
8
) targeted at the Vaidya-de Sitter spacetime.

As the observer, labeled now by
r^{\prime}_{0}
, moves away from the black hole, the time coordinate
u
deviates from the cosmological proper time
\tau

For such cases, measuring the dynamics of the black hole with respect to
u
cannot be correctly compared to the evolution of the background geometry unless the transformation between these two time coordinates is performed, which requires choosing a specific cosmological observer.
This point is significant, for instance, when the evaporation of black holes is measured in time scales comparable to the age of the Universe, such as for PBHs.

III
Evaporation model

For the characterization of the evaporation process, the black-hole temperature must be considered.
Since Vaidya-de Sitter is a dynamical and non-asymptotically flat geometry, the canonical method for studying thermal emission from black holes, based directly on Quantum Field Theory
(
Hawking 1975
;
Birrell and Davies 1994
)
, is not easily implemented. An alternative approach is to treat Hawking radiation as a tunneling phenomenon
(
Parich et al. 2000
)
.

This formalism is implemented in Hayward’s generalized thermodynamics
(
Hayward 1994
;
Hayward 1996
;
Hayward 1998
)
. In this framework, the black-hole boundary is defined as a future outer trapping horizon (FOTH).
And the temperature
T
associated to the black hole is proportional to the geometric surface gravity
\kappa_{G}
of the FOTH
(
Hayward 2009
)
,

T(u)=\frac{\hbar}{k\,c}\,\frac{\kappa_{G}(u)}{2\pi}\,.

(9)

For Vaidya-de Sitter geometry, the geometric surface gravity is given by

\kappa_{G}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{r}\bigg[1-\frac{2GM(u)}{c^{2}r}-\frac{r^{2}}{a^{2}}\bigg]_{\text{\tiny FOTH}}\,.

(10)

The mass loss of the black hole, due to Hawking radiation, is a quasi-local phenomenon in the vicinity of the horizon. As a result, we expect the Schwarzschild term
\nicefrac{{2GM}}{{c^{2}r}}
in the metric to dominate the geometry in this region. In fact, for reasonable values of the black-hole mass (even for supermassive ones), the last term can be neglected at first order due to the extremely small value of the cosmological constant. A similar analysis leads to the position of the FOTH being well approximated by the (dynamical) Schwarzschild radius,
\nicefrac{{2GM(u)}}{{c^{2}}}
. Hence Eq. (
9
) will have the well-known form of the temperature of a Vaidya black hole
(
Piesnack and Kassner 2022
)
.

In combination with the Stefan-Boltzmann law
(
Piesnack and Kassner 2022
;
Carballo et al. 2018
)
, the mass variation of the Vaidya-de Sitter black hole is well approximated by

\frac{dM}{du}=-\frac{\hbar c^{4}}{15360\pi G^{2}}\frac{1}{M(u)^{2}}\,.

(11)

After solving this differential equation, and transforming to the cosmological time via (
4
), the mass function
M
in terms of the dimensionless cosmological time
\tau^{\prime}
is determined,

\displaystyle\begin{split}M(\tau^{\prime},r^{\prime}_{0},M_{0})=\begin{cases}\bigg\{M_{0}^{3}-\frac{\hbar c^{4}}{5120\pi G^{2}}\frac{a}{c}\bigg[\text{arctanh}\bigg(\frac{\tanh\tau^{\prime}}{\cos\chi}\bigg)-\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{1+r^{\prime}}{1-r^{\prime}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{1+\sin\chi}{1-\sin\chi}\right)\bigg]\bigg\}^{\frac{1}{3}}\,,\hskip 8.5359pt\textrm{for }0<r^{\prime}<1\,,\\
\\
\bigg\{M_{0}^{3}-\frac{\hbar c^{4}}{5120\pi G^{2}}\frac{a}{c}\bigg[\text{arctanh}\bigg(\frac{\cos\chi}{\tanh\tau^{\prime}}\bigg)-\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{r^{\prime}+1}{r^{\prime}-1}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{1+\sin\chi}{1-\sin\chi}\right)\bigg]\bigg\}^{\frac{1}{3}}\,,\hskip 8.5359pt\textrm{for }1<r^{\prime}<\infty\,.\end{cases}\end{split}

(12)

It should be stressed that the main distinction between the evaporation rate in expanding and stationary universes is not due to the direct interference of the cosmological constant in the temperature of the black hole, but its influence on the trajectory of observers. In Schwarzschild, a distant static observer has a proper time which is
u
in good approximation, and then describes the evaporation process with
M(u)
. This results in Eq. (
1
). In contrast, in a rapidly expanding spacetime,
\tau
provides a more accurate description for cosmological observers, and we argue that Eq. (
12
) should be used instead.

IV
Analyzing the evaporation model

To track the rate of evaporation, the parameter space of our model requires specifying a cosmological observer, defined by
r^{\prime}_{0}
, and an initial mass
M_{0}
. We have systematically explored the parameter space of the model, and we will present a selection of representative results. Our goal is to highlight the main differences between our model and the usual stationary description applied to PBHs
(
Carr et al. 2021
;
Carr and Kuhnel 2020
;
Masina 2020
;
Carr et al. 2016
)
.

At this point, it is important to outline some key differences between these approaches. In several works, the time coordinate in Eq. (
1
) is treated as a cosmological time.
In other words, the evaporation of black holes derived by Hawking
(
Hawking 1975
)
in the context of a Schwarzschild spacetime is applied to the primordial ones, with a typical time frame given by Eq. (
1
).
However, our results indicate that this framework, applied in scenarios where the dynamical nature of the Universe cannot be overlooked, can lead to non-standard evaporation times and potentially incorrect conclusions.

In Figure

, we consider the evolution of
M
according to our proposed evaporation model summarized in Eq. (
12

It can be checked that the two lightest black holes in this figure show an evaporation rate similar (numerically and in functional form) to the traditional static model, which relies on the typical Schwarzschild time and follows
{(M^{3}-M_{0}^{3})\propto t}
. On the other hand, the heavier black hole exhibits a completely new behavior for the rate of evaporation.
Thus, a key finding is that black holes in static and dynamical backgrounds can evaporate with significantly different profiles, depending on their initial mass and the considered observer. This result has important implications when considering PBHs from the perspective of observers on Earth.

\lim_{\tau^{\prime}\rightarrow\infty}u^{\prime}=\text{arctanh}\big(\cos\chi\big)+\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{1+\sin\chi}{1-\sin\chi}\right)=\textrm{finite}\,.

Our results also indicate that the rate of evaporation of a black hole, measured with respect to a cosmological time, can be very sensitive to its initial mass. Even in the same order of magnitude, for example at

This phenomenon has no counterpart in the usual analysis considering Schwarzschild spacetime.

One may ask which black holes eventually evaporate completely, which is an observer-dependent phenomenon. Considering the family of cosmological observers analyzed in the present work, we evaluate the following limit:

\lim_{\tau^{\prime}\rightarrow\infty}M(\tau^{\prime})\,\,\text{for some specific observer $r^{\prime}_{0}$}\,.

(16)

This provides the final mass of the black hole in the infinite future as a function of its initial mass.

V
Final Remarks

In this work, we analyze the evaporation of black holes immersed in a de Sitter environment, comparing our results with those from the usual static frameworks, with special attention to PBHs. In this description, the Vaidya-de Sitter spacetime is used to model a black hole in a scenario of accelerated expansion of the Universe.
The role of observers is emphasized in the present development.
Since the considered framework is dynamical, Hayward’s approach to black-hole thermodynamics is applied.
The results obtained are suggestive, indicating that the PBH evaporation occurs at different rates than expected when measured by cosmological observers.

In several studies examining the evaporation of PBHs, the phenomenon is analyzed without considering the effects of a positive cosmological constant. In such cases, the Schwarzschild solution is typically employed as the framework for describing the evaporation process.
However, if the spacetime is dynamical and not asymptotically-flat, the preferable observers must be redefined.
We focus on a class of observers that are (asymptotically) comoving with the expansion of the Universe. For these observers, we have shown that the rate of evaporation of a black hole can be highly distinct when compared to the Schwarzschild counterpart.

In the conventional descriptions, primordial black holes that reach the end of their lifetime at the current age of the Universe are those with an initial mass just below
10^{15}\,\text{g}\sim 10^{-18}M_{\odot}
.
Our work predicts deviations to this result for a wide set of observers who measure the cosmological time, leading to primordial black holes evaporating earlier or later than expected.
Naturally, these results must be taken with a grain of salt since a more accurate setting for the dynamics of a PBH should be neither static nor de Sitter, but somewhere in between.
Notwithstanding, our model provides a preliminary estimate of the deviation that can be expected when the spacetime expansion is taken into consideration.

As the Universe ages and its expansion rate accelerates, differences between the usually used static evaporation model and our dynamic description become more pronounced.
As the cosmological constant becomes dominant, the expected phenomenology asymptotically approaches the description presented in this work.
The most remarkable new result is the fact that, with respect to the cosmological time, some black holes will never fully evaporate from the point of view of observers on Earth.
