Satisfaction and Treatment Burden Among Early Adopters of Long-Acting Injectable Cabotegravir-Rilpivirine With and Without Viremia in Three US Ryan White Clinics
Authors: Katerina A Christopoulos, Alex Beckert, Caleb McCracken, Kaylin Dance, Sophie Plotkin, Lauren F Collins, Moira C McNulty, Matthew D Hickey, Mallory O Johnson, Aaloke Mody, Xavier A Erguera, Kimberly A Koester, Geoffroy Liegeon, Jorge Salazar, Jose Gutierrez, Janet Grochowski, Samantha E Dilworth, Torsten B Neilands, Elizabeth T Montgomery, Jonathan A Colasanti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmong underserved people with HIV with a median of one year of long-acting cabotegravir-rilpivirine use, including those starting with viremia due to adherence challenges, we found higher satisfaction and lower treatment burden compared to daily oral ART, although nearly half preferred another dosing modality and/or a longer dosing interval.Keywords: adherence challenges, cabotegravir-rilpivirine, long-acting antiretroviral therapy, patient-reported outcomes, satisfaction

Long-acting injectable cabotegravir/rilpivirine (LA-CAB/RPV), an intramuscular injection given by a health care provider every four or every eight weeks, can replace daily oral antiretroviral therapy (ART) for people with HIV-1 infection (PWH). Little is known about patient-reported outcomes of LA-CAB/RPV in real-world settings in the U.S., particularly those who initiate LA-CAB/RPV with viremia due to adherence challenges, who were excluded from registrational clinical trials. One analysis at a public clinic in Chicago [1] of PWH initiating LA-CAB/RPV with VS found high satisfaction but used an abbreviated version of the HIV Treatment Satisfaction Questionnaire [2], making it difficult to fully compare findings to clinical trials. The industry-sponsored BEYOND study, conducted in 27 U.S. clinics, reported high satisfaction; however, only 9% had viremia at LA-CAB/RPV initiation [3], and it is unclear if satisfaction differed for this group. We sought to add to the literature by investigating satisfaction and treatment burden with LA-CAB/RPV, including among PWH who initiated with viremia due to adherence challenges. We also assessed preferences for delivery locations, interest in other long-acting dosing intervals and modalities, and the impact of LA-CAB/RPV on the patient-provider relationship.

METHODS

As part of a multi-method study evaluating the implementation of LA-CAB/RPV at three Ryan White-funded academic HIV clinics, Ward 86 in San Francisco, the Ponce de Leon Center at Grady in Atlanta, and the University of Chicago (the “MODERN ART” study), we conducted a cross-sectional survey of the treatment experience of LA-CAB/RPV. Inclusion criteria were English or Spanish-speaking, current receipt of LA-CAB/RPV, and ≥3 injection timepoints (in order to allow time to acclimate to injections). We excluded those who had discontinued LA-CAB/RPV because at the time there were few PWH in this category; thus, we chose to explore their experiences qualitatively [4]. The survey had the following sections: (1) the 12-item status and change versions of the HIV Treatment Satisfaction Questionnaire [2, 5, 6], in which participants rated current satisfaction with LA-CAB/RPV as well as satisfaction thinking back to when they were on oral ART; (2) an adapted version of the Treatment Burden Questionnaire [7], for which we also asked participants to compare their current experience to when they were on oral ART; (3) intention to persist with LA-CAB/RPV; (4) reasons for preferring LA-CAB/RPV; (5) preferred injection location (responses not mutually exclusive); and (6) interest in other LA-ART modalities and dose intervals. (Supplementary File 1) We also included a question with a free-text response: “In a few words, can you tell us the impact being on Cabenuva has had on your relationship with your HIV provider? If there has been no impact, simply type “na.”

Participants were recruited through clinic providers and staff or directly if they had consented to be recontacted in prior studies of the principal investigators. After providing written informed consent, participants self-administered web-based surveys in English or Spanish under the guidance of a research coordinator. Surveys occurred either in person or via video conference in private rooms in or adjacent to the clinic. Participants reported socio-demographics, and research coordinators abstracted clinical characteristics via chart review. Participants were reimbursed $40.

We summarized data using descriptive statistics. Chi-squared and Wilcoxon tests compared quantitative responses by VS status at LA-CAB/RPV initiation (HIV-1 RNA <50 copies/mL vs ≥50 copies/mL); there were no significant differences, thus we present results for the overall sample. Multivariable logistic regression investigated characteristics of PWH who experienced a reduction in effort to remember ART treatment, with difficulty dichotomized as extremely/very/moderately difficult versus a little bit/not at all difficult. We conducted a content analysis of free-text responses on the impact of LA-CAB/RPV on the patient-provider relationship by VS status [8].

This study was approved by the University of California, San Francisco, Institutional Review Board (IRB), which served as a single-site IRB.

RESULTS

From 9/2023 to 7/2024, we surveyed 206 PWH (30% of whom initiated LA-CAB/RPV with viremia; 70% who initiated with VS), with 56% of respondents from Ward 86, 24% from the Ponce Center, and 20% from the University of Chicago (Supplementary Table 1A). The median number of injection administrations was 9 (IQR 5–14), and the median time on injections was 373 days (IQR 164–569 days), with 67% on every 8-week dosing (72% of those with VS at initiation; 56% of those with viremia at initiation). Median age was 46 (range 19–74, IQR 35–56), and 23% were women, 47% Black, 21% Latine, 46% struggling to survive financially/barely paying bills, 36% with housing instability, and 22% with illicit opioid or stimulant use in the past 30 days.

The mean treatment satisfaction score was 61.5 out of a maximum of 66 (standard deviation 5.3) and the mean change in satisfaction with LA-CAB/RPV compared to oral ART was +28.9 (scale range from −33 being very dissatisfied to 0 being no change to +33 being very satisfied). While 43% endorsed pain as a problem at the first injection, only 11% did for their most recent injection. Mental well-being was perceived as better on LA-CAB/RPV as compared to oral ART for 74%. Participants reported improvements in worry about HIV status disclosure (13% expressed current worry on LA-CAB/RPV, compared to 67% reporting worry when reflecting on their experience of daily oral ART) and treatment as a reminder of HIV status (8% on LA-CAB/RPV, compared to 52% when reflecting on daily oral ART). While 35% reported that both oral ART and LA-CAB/RPV were “not at all” or “a little difficult” to remember, 62% who had found oral ART “extremely,” “very,” or “moderately” difficult to remember found LA-CAB/RPV “not at all” or “a little bit difficult,” with significantly reduced effort (Supplementary Table 1B) among those with viremia at initiation, unstable housing/homelessness, and stimulant/opioid use in unadjusted analyses and unstable housing/homelessness (AOR 2.33, 95% CI 1.18, 4.60) and substance use (AOR 2.42, 95% CI 1.13, 5.19) remaining significant in adjusted analyses.

Regarding confidence in persisting with LA-CAB/RPV, 97% were either moderately, very, or extremely confident they would persist. No participant saw themselves going back to daily oral pills. Not having to remember to take oral ART was the most common reason for preferring LA-CAB/RPV (Figure 1A). Only 4% of participants found HIV clinic visits more of a problem with LA-CAB/RPV and nearly all (91%) endorsed the HIV clinic as a preferred location for injections, while some were open to other location options (Figure 1B). When queried about preference for hypothetical drug delivery platforms, nearly half of respondents preferred a different LA modality: patch (20%), subcutaneous injection (14%), implant (8%), slow-release oral pill (3%), and intravenous infusion (2%). Most (60%) preferred a dosing interval of six months (22%) or one year (38%), with less popular dosing intervals including every 8 weeks (16%), every 3 months (14%), every 4 weeks (9%), and every week (1%).

Free text responses revealed that most respondents reported positive impacts on relationships with their doctors and other members of their care team, eg, behavioral health specialist and program manager; only two respondents noted no change. These effects appeared more pronounced for participants who initiated LA-CAB/RPV with viremia. As one respondent stated, “There have been way more ‘high five’ moments with Cabenuva. I’m undetectable now and I wasn’t before.” These participants also described increased and more consistent clinic attendance. Several noted being able to concentrate more on other issues in provider visits; as one participant stated, “We focus on other lab tests to check my overall health versus spending a significant amount of time discussing the importance of regimen adherence.”

DISCUSSION

In a broad, multi-site sample of PWH, including those with financial/housing instability and substance use, who had a median of one year of LA-CAB/RPV use, we found high levels of treatment satisfaction and decreased treatment burden as compared to daily oral ART, consistent with clinical trials [9–11]. Results were similar for participants initiating LA-CAB/RPV with and without viremia; however, the decrease in effort to remember to take ART medication was particularly salient for those experiencing homelessness/housing instability and/or substance use. In addition, those initiating LA-CAB/RPV with viremia whose virus became undetectable noted improved relationships with providers and the ability to focus on other health issues.

A preference for clinic receipt of injections was expressed by >90% of respondents. Our qualitative research has found that strengthened and trusting relationships with care teams and positive feedback from providers help explain this preference [4, 12]. Taken together, these findings demonstrate how LA-CAB/RPV can lighten the “work” of being a patient [13]. by reducing the cognitive and emotional labor of daily pill-taking, decreasing concerns about inadvertent disclosure, and allowing some clinic visits to shift from adherence counseling toward broader health needs.

Despite high satisfaction and respondents reporting that injection pain improved over time, nearly half of respondents expressed a hypothetical preference for a different dosing modality and a longer dosing interval. Given new options in the LA-ART pipeline, including every six-month options such as subcutaneous lenacapavir and intravenous broadly neutralizing antibodies [14], continued assessment of delivery preferences is important to optimize successful implementation.

Limitations to this analysis include a cross-sectional design; recall bias is a possibility. Only a small number of PWH had discontinued LA-CAB/RPV at the time of survey recruitment, so we did not sample them. We subsequently explored reasons for discontinuation at one study site (San Francisco) and found that pain and other treatment-associated side effects were the most common reasons [15].

In summary, users of LA-CAB/RPV in Ryan White-funded care settings reported improved psychosocial experiences of HIV care and treatment, especially PWH with viremia at initiation and/or socio-structural barriers to engagement such as homelessness/housing instability and substance use. Inquiry into satisfaction and treatment burden should continue as more PWH initiate LA-CAB/RPV and time on injections increases.
