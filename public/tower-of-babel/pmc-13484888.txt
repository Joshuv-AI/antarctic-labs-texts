Systemic Therapy for High-Grade Olfactory Neuroblastoma: From Molecular Lineages to Targeted and Immune Strategies
Authors: Bin He, Lin Ling Li, Yu Tan, Ke Wei Zhao, Hua Li, Ling Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvanced olfactory neuroblastoma (ONB) remains a therapeutic challenge because evidence for systemic treatment is derived largely from small retrospective series and extrapolation from related neuroendocrine malignancies. This narrative review brings together diagnostic pathology, multi-omics classification, contemporary guideline-based management, and emerging systemic approaches for high-grade, recurrent, or metastatic ONB. Neural and Basal molecular programs provide a useful biological framework, but they are not validated tools for treatment selection. Delta-like ligand 3 (DLL3)-directed therapy and epigenetic–immune combinations are supported mainly by lineage-based or preclinical evidence, whereas somatostatin receptor (SSTR)-directed strategies and immune checkpoint blockade have generated only limited ONB-specific clinical data. All remain investigational. The first prospective immunotherapy trial in recurrent or metastatic ONB did not meet its objective-response endpoint, although prolonged disease stabilization was observed in a subset of patients. We therefore outline a practical approach that preserves established multidisciplinary care while using molecular and immune profiling to clarify diagnosis and guide clinical-trial referral. Routine biomarker-directed systemic therapy will require prospective multicenter validation using standardized assays and predefined biomarkers.

Introduction

Olfactory neuroblastoma (ONB) is a rare malignant neoplasm of the olfactory neuroepithelium that accounts for approximately 2–6% of sinonasal malignancies.1 Its estimated incidence is approximately 0.4 cases per million population per year.1,2 The rarity of ONB, its biological heterogeneity, and its prolonged relapse pattern make randomized trials difficult. Consequently, most clinical evidence comes from retrospective institutional series, population-based analyses, and multicenter cohorts.3–5

For locally extensive disease, platinum-based induction chemotherapy can produce radiographic responses and facilitate subsequent local treatment in selected patients, although durable benefit remains uncertain.6 Advances in endoscopic skull-base surgery and conformal radiotherapy have broadened local-treatment options, but high-grade or anatomically advanced ONB remains at risk of local and distant recurrence.5,7 When distant metastases develop, prognosis is poor, and the optimal systemic treatment strategy remains uncertain.8

To address these limitations, translational research has moved beyond morphology alone toward molecular stratification. Integrated multi-omics studies have defined lineage-associated states.8 Immune studies have identified heterogeneous programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression and lineage-associated immune features.9,10 Recent multi-omics reviews have summarized candidate signaling pathways and emerging targets (Table 1).11,12 International multicenter work also illustrates the value of collaborative evidence generation in this rare disease.13 These classifications remain research constructs: platforms, subtype definitions, and clinically actionable cutoffs have not been harmonized. Several proposed therapies are extrapolated from small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) or other neuroendocrine tumors rather than supported by direct ONB trials. Accordingly, this review connects emerging molecular observations with current clinical practice while explicitly separating established ONB-specific evidence from biologically plausible but investigational strategies. We summarize the limits of conventional systemic therapy, examine Neural and Basal biology, assess candidate biomarkers, and propose a trial-oriented workflow rather than a replacement for guideline-based multidisciplinary management.

Literature Search and Selection Strategy

This manuscript is a narrative review. PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched for English-language publications and trial records from January 2000, with the searches last updated on July 21, 2026. Search terms included “olfactory neuroblastoma,” “esthesioneuroblastoma,” “molecular subtype,” “multi-omics,” “pathology,” “surgery,” “radiotherapy,” “systemic therapy,” “chemotherapy,” “DLL3,” “somatostatin receptor,” “EZH2,” “immunotherapy,” “biomarker,” and “clinical trial.” Reference lists of relevant reviews and primary studies were also screened. The search was designed to provide a clinically oriented synthesis and was not registered as a systematic-review protocol.

We prioritized prospective trials, multicenter cohorts, translational multi-omics studies, and contemporary reviews or guidelines. Small retrospective series and case reports were retained when they addressed rare clinical scenarios or emerging therapies not represented in larger studies. Because this was a narrative review, duplicate independent study screening and formal risk-of-bias assessment were not undertaken. Evidence was interpreted according to its directness for ONB, study design, cohort size, assay validity, and reliance on extrapolation from other tumor types.

Pathologic and Molecular Framework for Therapeutic Stratification

Hierarchical Differential Diagnosis and Molecular Exclusion

ONB belongs to the differential diagnosis of poorly differentiated sinonasal tumors. The principal mimics include sinonasal neuroendocrine carcinoma (SNEC), poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma, sinonasal undifferentiated carcinoma (SNUC), NUT carcinoma, SWI/SNF-deficient sinonasal carcinoma, and Ewing sarcoma family tumors.21 Accurate classification therefore requires the integration of morphology, immunophenotype, and, in selected cases, molecular findings rather than reliance on any single diagnostic marker.

Morphologically, lobular growth, neurofibrillary stroma, rosette formation, and an S100-positive sustentacular network support a diagnosis of ONB, although these characteristic features may be inconspicuous or absent in high-grade tumors. Synaptophysin, chromogranin A, and INSM1 confirm neuroendocrine differentiation but are not specific for ONB, because their expression may also be observed in other sinonasal neuroendocrine malignancies.7,21

The extent of epithelial differentiation provides an additional basis for distinction. ONB is generally cytokeratin-negative or shows only focal cytokeratin expression, whereas diffuse cytokeratin positivity favors SNEC, SNUC, or poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma. In this setting, interpretation together with epithelial and squamous-lineage markers can help resolve the substantial morphological overlap among these entities.22,23

Molecularly defined sinonasal malignancies should also be considered when the morphology and immunophenotype are atypical for conventional ONB. Nuclear NUT expression or demonstration of a NUTM1 rearrangement supports NUT carcinoma, whereas loss of INI1 (SMARCB1) or SMARCA4 expression identifies the corresponding SWI/SNF-deficient carcinoma. IDH2 hotspot mutations, particularly at R172, are strongly associated with SNUC, although this finding should be interpreted in conjunction with the morphological and broader molecular context rather than as an isolated exclusion criterion for ONB.14 When a round-cell sarcoma remains within the differential diagnosis, lineage-specific molecular testing is necessary because neuroendocrine-marker expression alone cannot reliably establish tumor lineage.

For diagnostically unresolved tumors, expert head and neck pathology review is particularly important. DNA methylation profiling may facilitate classification when morphology, immunohistochemistry, and targeted molecular findings remain discordant.15 Establishing the correct pathological entity should precede molecular subtyping of ONB and any subsequent consideration of biomarker-directed therapeutic strategies.

Genomic and Transcriptomic Heterogeneity

After diagnosis is established, molecular studies reveal considerable heterogeneity within ONB. Most tumors have a low tumor mutational burden (TMB).24 Recurrent alterations and copy-number changes vary among cohorts, and FGFR3 amplification has been reported in a subset.25,26 A recent multi-omics synthesis by Demir et al highlighted recurrent perturbations involving WNT/β-catenin, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, chromatin-remodeling, and DNA-repair pathways, while emphasizing that proposed targets still require functional and clinical validation in ONB.11 A 2026 systematic review of 24 omics studies reached similar conclusions and emphasized the limited functional validation of proposed biomarkers and therapeutic targets.12 A recent AACR Project GENIE analysis expanded the available genomic dataset and reported recurrent alterations involving TP53, FRK, NOTCH3, SMARCA4, RET, and CTCF, although their value for treatment selection remains unproven.20 These observations do not establish benefit from matched treatment.

Transcriptomic and epigenomic studies have described Neural and Basal molecular programs with different lineage and immune features.16 Single-cell analysis expanded this binary model to Neural, Basal, and Mesenchymal malignant states.17 Complementary lineage studies linked ONB to globose basal cells and normal olfactory stem or progenitor trajectories.27,28 These models overlap but are not identical and may capture cell-state plasticity within a tumor rather than fixed clinical subtypes.

Taken together, these observations indicate that ONB heterogeneity is organized not only by genomic alterations but also by lineage-associated cellular states and their interactions with the tumor microenvironment. Neural, Basal, and Mesenchymal programs may coexist within the same tumor and reflect cellular plasticity.17,18 Among these states, the Basal program is of particular translational interest because of its progenitor-like phenotype, epigenetic characteristics, and immune contexture.16,17 These biological features are examined in detail in the following section.

The Basal Molecular Program: Biology, Immune Microenvironment, and Therapeutic Vulnerabilities

The Basal program represents a distinct biological state rather than simply a loss of neural differentiation. In the original multi-omics cohort, Basal tumors were less differentiated and showed more necrosis, mitotic activity, and Ki-67 labeling than Neural tumors. Classe et al identified an IDH2 R172-mutant-enriched subgroup that comprised approximately 35% of Basal-classified tumors and was characterized by an epigenetic CpG island methylator phenotype; the same cohort also showed progenitor-like transcriptional features and greater lymphocytic infiltration in Basal tumors.16 Molecular evidence subsequently linked ONB to globose basal cells.27 Single-cell and experimental models placed Basal and Mesenchymal malignant states along olfactory epithelial developmental trajectories and demonstrated substantial state heterogeneity.17,18 Experimental lineage tracing indicates that plasticity can occur, although its extent and clinical relevance in human ONB remain uncertain.18

Because the original Basal classification was associated with greater lymphocytic infiltration, subsequent studies have examined whether this feature represents a functionally immune-active phenotype.16 The available evidence indicates a more heterogeneous immune contexture than the term “immune-enriched” alone would suggest. In a deconvolution analysis of 18 Basal and Neural tumors, M2 macrophages were the largest immune-cell population overall. Activated memory CD4 T cells were increased in Basal tumors, whereas resting dendritic cells were more frequent in Neural tumors; PD-L1 expression and overall CD20, CD4, and CD8 cell densities did not differ significantly between the subtypes.19 A separate single-cell study identified heterogeneous T-cell functional states and abundant immunosuppressive tumor-associated macrophages across ONB samples.17 Thus, increased immune-cell infiltration in Basal ONB may coexist with suppressive myeloid populations and nonuniform T-cell activation states. Basal ONB should therefore not be assumed to be uniformly immunologically active or intrinsically sensitive to checkpoint blockade on the basis of immune-cell abundance alone.

From a translational perspective, the IDH2 R172-mutant-enriched methylator subgroup provides a rationale for molecularly stratified preclinical and early-phase investigations of mutant-IDH or epigenetic approaches, provided that IDH2-mutant SNUC is rigorously excluded through integrated pathological and molecular assessment.14,16 The coexistence of activated memory CD4 T cells, M2-like macrophages, and heterogeneous T-cell states also suggests that future immunotherapy studies should evaluate both lymphoid activation and myeloid-mediated suppression rather than infer checkpoint-inhibitor sensitivity solely from lymphocytic infiltration.17,19

The coexistence of Basal and Mesenchymal malignant states further raises the possibility of cell-state plasticity.17,18 However, current evidence does not establish whether systemic treatment induces transitions between these states or whether such transitions contribute to acquired therapeutic resistance. Addressing these questions will require paired pretreatment and progression specimens evaluated using longitudinal single-cell or spatial profiling. Accordingly, Basal classification currently provides a biologically grounded framework for biomarker development and prospective trial stratification, whereas its value for selecting routine systemic treatment remains to be demonstrated.

The most important difference lies beneath the shared transcriptional programs. SCLC is strongly associated with tobacco exposure and typically carries a high mutation burden with near-universal disruption of TP53 and RB1.25 ONB has no comparable environmental mutagenic exposure, and targeted genomic studies show a generally low TMB with a different and less consistent set of driver alterations.24 This contrast weakens any assumption that ONB will reproduce the neoantigen landscape or immunotherapy sensitivity of SCLC.

The comparison is therefore most useful at the level of lineage biology. Shared NEUROD1- and POU2F3-like states, intratumoral heterogeneity, and cell-state plasticity can guide biomarker studies and trials of lineage-directed targets.18,30 They do not establish genomic equivalence or equal drug sensitivity. SCLC-derived strategies should be tested in biomarker-defined ONB cohorts with ONB-specific efficacy endpoints rather than adopted as routine treatment.

Current Management of ONB and the Remaining Therapeutic Gap

Current management remains anchored in stage, resectability, Hyams grade, disease distribution, and multidisciplinary assessment. The ESMO–EURACAN guideline and the NCCN framework for paranasal sinus malignancies prioritize histology, stage, resectability, surgical margins, and risk-adapted radiotherapy when defining local treatment; systemic therapy is individualized for high-grade, unresectable, recurrent, or metastatic presentations.35,36 Molecular findings can be incorporated after diagnostic confirmation to identify relevant clinical trials and selected tumor-agnostic options.

Surgical Resection: Endoscopic Advancements and Biological Limitations

Surgical resection aiming for negative margins remains the foundation of curative treatment for resectable ONB. A systematic review found comparable disease-control outcomes between endoscopic and open approaches in selected patients, but the comparison was limited by retrospective selection and stage imbalance.37 Nationwide data show increasing use of endoscopic surgery in contemporary practice.38 Endoscopic endonasal resection is widely used for appropriately selected tumors, whereas open or combined approaches remain necessary for disease with extensive lateral, orbital, frontal-sinus, or parenchymal involvement.39

The operative approach should be selected according to tumor extent and the ability to obtain oncologically appropriate margins rather than by a presumption that one access route is universally superior. Even after complete resection, high-grade disease remains associated with local and distant relapse, underscoring the importance of adjuvant treatment and long-term surveillance.5,7

Radiotherapy: Conformal Delivery and Locoregional Control

Postoperative radiotherapy (PORT) is commonly incorporated for locally advanced disease, high-grade or poorly differentiated histology, positive or close margins, perineural involvement, nodal disease, or residual tumor; omission may be discussed only in carefully selected low-grade, early-stage tumors after complete resection.35,40 No prospective dose-comparison study has defined an optimal prescription. Retrospective ONB series commonly use approximately 54–60 Gy to the postoperative bed after complete resection and escalate toward 66 Gy for positive margins or suspected residual disease. Dose and target volume are individualized according to margin status, skull-base extension, and optic and cerebral organ-at-risk constraints.40

Conformal techniques such as intensity-modulated radiotherapy and proton therapy can improve dose shaping near the optic apparatus and brain. Reported disease-control and toxicity outcomes are encouraging, but comparative evidence remains retrospective and susceptible to selection bias.40,41

Advanced radiation techniques primarily improve dose distribution and may reduce late neuro-ophthalmic exposure; a survival advantage over other appropriately delivered modalities has not been demonstrated.40,41 Elective neck irradiation remains individualized according to stage, grade, and estimated nodal risk. Multicenter data and the REFCOR consensus support consideration in selected higher-risk disease rather than routine use for every patient.31,42 Radiotherapy remains a locoregional treatment, and patients with biologically aggressive ONB may still develop distant relapse.5

Systemic Chemotherapy: Stage-Specific Roles and Evidence Limitations

Systemic chemotherapy remains the least standardized component of ONB management. To accurately assess its clinical utility, conventional chemotherapy must be evaluated across distinct treatment phases.

Neoadjuvant Chemotherapy and Chemoradiotherapy

For locally advanced ONB, particularly when orbital or intracranial extension makes complete upfront resection unlikely, induction platinum-based chemotherapy may be considered within a multidisciplinary plan. Retrospective series report radiographic responses in selected patients, but there is no randomized evidence that induction therapy improves overall survival.6,42

Definitive chemoradiotherapy may be considered for unresectable disease or when surgery is contraindicated, usually with a platinum-based regimen in specialist multidisciplinary care. However, the evidence is limited to small retrospective series and extrapolation from other high-grade neuroendocrine malignancies, and surgery followed by PORT remains preferred when complete resection is feasible.7,35,42

Adjuvant Chemotherapy

Postoperative chemotherapy is not routinely indicated after complete resection. The ESMO–EURACAN guideline states that postoperative concurrent chemoradiotherapy with a platinum agent may be considered for high-grade and/or poorly differentiated sinonasal tumors [IV, C], a category that includes Hyams grade III/IV ONB.35 The REFCOR consensus similarly retains surgery followed by adjuvant radiotherapy as standard care and allows chemotherapy to be considered in high-grade or unresectable ONB.42 These recommendations concern selected high-risk settings and do not establish routine sequential chemotherapy after PORT.

ONB-specific retrospective evidence is conflicting. In a Mayo Clinic series of 12 patients with completely resected Hyams grade III/IV, Kadish C tumors, the six patients who received mainly cisplatin–etoposide chemotherapy had a longer median time to relapse, but the cohort was too small to define a survival benefit or a standard indication.43 In a later 38-patient comparison, adding platinum-based adjuvant chemotherapy to surgery and radiotherapy did not improve overall or recurrence-free survival.44 Accordingly, concurrent platinum with postoperative radiotherapy may be considered for selected high-grade or poorly differentiated cases after multidisciplinary review. Additional sequential chemotherapy should be reserved for exceptional circumstances, such as continuation of a clearly responsive induction strategy or treatment of residual or metastatic disease.

Chemotherapy for Recurrent or Metastatic Disease

For recurrent or metastatic ONB, cytotoxic chemotherapy remains an option when rapid disease control is required, but the evidence is derived from small retrospective series and heterogeneous regimens.45 Responses may be temporary, and long-term salvage cohorts document repeated local and distant relapse despite multimodality treatment.46 Treatment selection should account for disease tempo, symptoms, prior therapy, available local salvage, patient fitness, and access to a clinical trial.

Targeted Therapy: Exploring Lineage Vulnerabilities and Radioligand Therapy

DLL3 is a biologically plausible lineage-directed target because high-grade Neural ONB can share neuroendocrine programs with SCLC.18,52 Tarlatamab has demonstrated activity in previously treated SCLC, but no mature ONB-specific efficacy data are available.32 Accordingly, DLL3 testing may support enrollment in a DLL3-directed clinical trial but does not currently justify routine off-label tarlatamab use in ONB.

Somatostatin receptor (SSTR) expression and functional imaging provide a direct means of confirming target availability. In a 404-patient multicenter study, 82.4% of tested tumors expressed SSTR2, and three patients with metastatic disease treated with SSTR2-targeted peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT) achieved stable disease.31 A two-center retrospective series of seven heavily pretreated patients reported four partial responses and two cases of stable disease, with a median progression-free survival of 17 months; selection bias and treatment heterogeneity limit interpretation.49 A subsequent case report further documented the feasibility of PRRT in recurrent ONB.53 A recruiting phase I trial of [212Pb]VMT-α-NET (NCT06479811) includes eligible SSTR-positive tumors, including ONB, but had not reported ONB-specific efficacy results as of July 21, 2026.50 A 2025 conference abstract describing 13 patients treated with 177Lu-DOTATATE reported preliminary radiographic activity, but full peer-reviewed results were not yet available.51 SSTR2 expression therefore supports receptor imaging, dosimetry, and trial selection, but it is not yet a prospectively validated predictor of treatment benefit in ONB.

Immunotherapy: From Retrospective Signals to Prospective Dual-Blockade

Evidence for immune checkpoint inhibition in ONB remains limited. Tissue studies show spatially heterogeneous PD-L1 expression and lymphocyte distribution.9 Single-cell and deconvolution studies identify heterogeneous T-cell states together with suppressive macrophage and myeloid populations.17,19 High-grade tumors may also show reduced major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) expression and low HLA-DR, CXCL9, and CXCL10 expression associated with stromal rather than intratumoral T-cell localization.33 These findings describe potential immune-evasion mechanisms but do not constitute validated treatment-selection biomarkers. A bicenter retrospective series of 11 patients with recurrent/metastatic ONB included six treated with nivolumab or pembrolizumab. Among these six patients, the objective response rate was 33.3% (one complete and one partial response) and the disease-control rate was 66.7% (two additional patients with stable disease); median progression-free survival was 7.8 months.47 The very small treated sample and retrospective selection preclude firm efficacy estimates.

The phase II BARON study (NCT05012098) prospectively evaluated bintrafusp alfa in 11 patients with recurrent/metastatic ONB. No objective responses were observed, although prolonged stable disease occurred in a small subset.48 The trial therefore supports the feasibility of prospective ONB studies but does not establish dual PD-L1/transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) blockade as effective therapy.

No biomarker has been prospectively validated to predict immunotherapy response in ONB. PD-L1 staining is spatially heterogeneous and lacks an ONB-specific scoring threshold. Importantly, in the six-patient anti-PD-1 series, both objective responders had PD-L1-negative tumors, and TMB or microsatellite instability (MSI) status showed no apparent association with benefit; one evaluable responder had a TMB of 0 mutations/Mb.47 These observations are too sparse to establish biomarker independence, but they argue against using PD-L1 negativity or low TMB alone to exclude patients from prospective trials. ONB is usually TMB-low, although exceptional tumor-agnostic alterations—such as MSI-high/mismatch-repair deficiency, TMB-high status, NTRK or RET fusions, BRAF V600E, and actionable FGFR alterations—may justify testing in advanced disease under ESMO–EURACAN guidance.24,35

Candidate tissue biomarkers should capture immune function rather than cell counts alone. Reduced tumor-cell MHC-I expression, low HLA-DR/CXCL9/CXCL10 expression, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and tumor-associated macrophages are potential correlative measures of immune suppression.33 Neural, Basal, and Mesenchymal-like states may contextualize these findings, but subtype definitions and assays remain unharmonized, and no prospective study has linked a lineage state to checkpoint-inhibitor outcome.16,17 Prospective immunotherapy studies should prespecify assay platform, specimen site and timing, cutoffs, and treatment hypotheses, and should integrate paired tissue and circulating biomarkers with clinical outcomes.

Combination Strategies: Overcoming Immune Exclusion via Epigenetic Remodeling

Epigenetic–immune combinations are mechanistically attractive but remain preclinical or hypothesis-generating in ONB. EZH2 expression has been associated with genomic and immune features but does not itself establish sensitivity to an EZH2 inhibitor; experimental work in other cancers links polycomb activity to reduced antigen presentation.10,34 However, no ONB-specific study has shown that an EZH2 inhibitor restores antigen presentation or improves checkpoint-inhibitor efficacy, and no dedicated clinical trial has established benefit. These combinations should therefore be presented as testable translational strategies rather than treatment recommendations.

No ONB-specific study has established a clinically meaningful radiation–immune interaction or abscopal effect. Any prospective radioimmunotherapy study should prespecify radiation dose, target volume, sequencing, and toxicity monitoring. This is particularly important in previously irradiated skull-base fields: a multi-institutional sinonasal reirradiation study documented substantial severe toxicity, and a separate sinonasal cohort identified reirradiation and skull-base involvement as risk factors for cerebral radiation necrosis.54,55

Combination studies should incorporate predefined stopping rules and careful assessment of neurological, endocrine, vascular, and optic toxicity. Until prospective data are available, potentially overlapping toxicities argue against routine untested combinations outside specialist multidisciplinary care or a clinical trial.

Integrating Targeted Therapies with Chemotherapy and Practical Precision Workflows

Platinum-based chemotherapy remains a pragmatic option for rapid cytoreduction in selected patients with high-burden or symptomatic disease, but responses are often not durable. Molecular findings may support trial eligibility or an individualized discussion after standard options have been considered; they should not automatically displace therapies with greater ONB-specific clinical experience.

A practical workflow should begin with expert head and neck pathology review and exclusion of molecularly defined mimics, followed by anatomical staging, assessment of resectability and metastatic burden, and multidisciplinary selection of established treatment options. Only after these steps should research-level Neural/Basal classification and biomarkers such as DLL3, SSTR2, PD-L1, immune infiltration, TMB, EZH2, and potentially actionable genomic alterations be used to support clinical-trial screening. International Collaboration and Prospective Evidence Generation

Although ONB evidence generation is geographically distributed, it remains fragmented across retrospective clinical cohorts, molecular datasets, and small prospective studies. A French–US collaboration established the original Neural/Basal multi-omics classification,16 whereas North American investigators subsequently developed experimental lineage models and completed the first prospective immunotherapy trial specifically for recurrent or metastatic ONB.18,48 A 12-institution transatlantic cohort assembled clinical data from 404 patients and incorporated SSTR2 expression, receptor imaging, and PRRT findings in selected subsets.31 Additional large-scale evidence has been generated through North American multicenter cohorts,5 a Japanese nationwide analysis,38 Chinese genomic and single-cell studies,17,26 and a Korean multicenter consortium.13 Earlier Australia–US multicenter work also demonstrated the feasibility of obtaining long-term cross-national outcome data in this rare disease.56

Collectively, these studies demonstrate the feasibility of collaborative ONB research but do not yet constitute a harmonized international evidence platform. Future prospective networks should incorporate central pathology review, standardized staging and Hyams grading, prespecified molecular and immune assays, common response and toxicity criteria, and longitudinal collection of tissue, blood, and imaging data. Basket trials should prespecify an ONB stratum and report ONB-specific enrollment, efficacy, and safety outcomes rather than subsuming these patients within broader neuroendocrine-tumor cohorts.

Limitations

This narrative review has several limitations. It did not use a preregistered protocol, duplicate independent study selection, or formal risk-of-bias assessment; therefore, selection bias cannot be excluded. ONB is rare, and most available evidence comes from small retrospective cohorts. Prospective clinical evidence remains limited. In addition, several therapeutic strategies are extrapolated from SCLC or other neuroendocrine tumors.

Conclusion

Surgery and radiotherapy remain the mainstays of treatment for localized ONB, whereas systemic therapy for advanced disease continues to rely on limited and heterogeneous evidence. Molecular studies have refined the biological classification of ONB and identified candidate therapeutic vulnerabilities, but they have not yet yielded validated treatment-selection tools. DLL3- and SSTR-directed approaches, immune checkpoint blockade, EZH2 inhibition, and epigenetic–immune or radiation–immune combinations therefore remain investigational. At present, molecular profiling is most useful for diagnostic clarification and clinical-trial matching within established multidisciplinary care. Further progress will depend on prospective multicenter studies using harmonized molecular classification, predefined biomarkers, and ONB-specific efficacy and safety endpoints.
