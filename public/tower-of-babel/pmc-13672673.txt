Diagnostic Accuracy of PID‐5‐BF for Screening Personality Disorders: A Cross‐Sectional Study in Clinical and Nonclinical Samples
Authors: Béla Birkás, András Norbert Zsidó, Sára Gergely, Regina Tóth, Júlia Andróczi, Tibor Cece Kiss, András Láng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe DSM‐5 Alternative Model for Personality Disorders introduced a dimensional approach to personality pathology, with maladaptive traits assessed by the Personality Inventory for DSM‐5 Brief Form (PID‐5‐BF). Despite extensive validation, evidence on its diagnostic accuracy and empirically derived cutoff scores remains limited. This study aims to compare PID‐5‐BF trait levels and profiles across individuals with personality disorders (PDs), clinical participants without PDs, and nonclinical participants and to evaluate its diagnostic accuracy and screening cutoffs. This cross‐sectional study included 1168 Hungarian participants: 867 nonclinical, 237 clinical without PD, and 64 with PD. Group differences were examined using one‐way ANOVAs, whereas domain profiles were evaluated with mixed‐design repeated‐measures ANOVA. Diagnostic accuracy was assessed using ROC analyses adjusted for age, sex, and education, and optimal cutoffs were determined using Youden's index. Participants with PD showed the highest pathological trait scores, followed by clinical participants without PD and nonclinical participants. The largest group effects emerged for disinhibition, total score, and detachment. For distinguishing PD from nonclinical participants, the adjusted AUC for the PID‐5‐BF total score was 0.849, with an optimal cutoff of ≥ 1.32. Domain‐specific cutoffs ranged from 0.60 to 1.60 (median = 1.20). Negative predictive values were high (mean NPV = 0.934), whereas positive predictive values were modest (mean PPV = 0.327). The PID‐5‐BF may be useful as a brief screening measure for identifying individuals requiring further personality assessment. However, its limited positive predictive value and weaker discrimination between PD and other clinical conditions preclude its use as a stand‐alone diagnostic instrument.Keywords: cutoff scores, diagnostic accuracy, personality disorders, PID‐5‐BF, ROC analysis, screening instrument

Background

The diagnosis and treatment of personality disorders (PDs) constitute a prevalent mental health issue affecting more than 7% of the global population (Winsper et al. 2020). The repercussions of PDs on the quality of life for affected individuals, their significant others, and the broader social environment have been extensively documented (Huang et al. 2009; Volkert et al. 2018; Winsper et al. 2020). PDs are even associated with increased mortality as a recent meta‐analysis of longitudinal studies has shown (Komasi et al. 2025). Among individuals with PDS, a significant elevation in all‐cause mortality was found (SMR = 4.16), with an especially pronounced increase in mortality from unnatural causes (SMR = 14.27) (Komasi et al. 2025). These findings further underscore the importance of feasible clinical strategies for the early identification of clinically significant personality pathology. In an effort to address the limitations of the categorical approach to PDs, the Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (AMPD; Skodol et al. 2011) was introduced in Section III of the DSM‐5 (American Psychiatric Association [APA] 2000), a dimensional approach to PDs, a departure from the prevailing categorical framework (Birkhölzer et al. 2021; Zimmermann et al. 2023). In the AMPD, Criterion A of PDs was reconceptualized as level of personality functioning, whereas Criterion B was replaced by different configurations of pathological personality traits.

Personality trait configurations for Criterion B can be composed of five domains: negative affectivity, detachment, antagonism, disinhibition, and psychoticism (Krueger and Hobbs 2020; Zimmermann et al. 2019). These five traits are regarded as the maladaptive counterparts of the widely accepted and supported five‐factor personality model dimensions (Krueger et al. 2012). Consistent with the conceptualization of maladaptive traits as extreme or dysfunctional variants of general personality dimensions, negative affectivity shows a strong positive association with neuroticism, whereas detachment, antagonism, and disinhibition are inversely associated with extraversion, agreeableness, and conscientiousness, respectively (Griffin and Samuel 2014; Suzuki et al. 2015; Watson et al. 2013). The relationship between psychoticism and openness appears to be less consistent (Chmielewski et al. 2014). As the extrapolation of normal personality traits into pathological extremes, maladaptive personality traits demonstrate analogous sex differences and age trends as their normal trait counterparts (Vergauwe et al. 2023).

The presence of maladaptive personality traits in AMPD is operationalized in the Personality Inventory for DSM‐5 (PID‐5; Krueger et al. 2012) and its shortened versions, such as the 25‐item Brief Form (PID‐5‐BF; Anderson et al. 2018). The latter was developed to enable rapid screening while preserving psychometric robustness and the integrity of the full PID‐5 assessment (Fossati et al. 2017). A substantial body of research has demonstrated the structural and predictive validity of the PID‐5‐BF to be comparable to that of the original 220‐item PID‐5. These findings suggest that the brief version is adequate for identifying potential personality pathology (Bach et al. 2016; Debast et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2021). The original and abbreviated versions of this inventory have been adapted to several languages and cultures, thus demonstrating its universal reliability and validity as a measure of pathological personality traits in community samples (Birkás et al. 2018; Coelho et al. 2022; Zatti et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2021). Although the AMPD dimensions align with traditional PD categories and offer reliable utility, only a few studies have examined the diagnostic accuracy of PID‐5 measures in defining score thresholds for subclinical and pathological trait configurations (Bach et al. 2016; Griffin and Samuel 2014; Krueger et al. 2012; Miller et al. 2022; Suzuki et al. 2015; Watson et al. 2013; Wright et al. 2012). The available evidence nevertheless suggests that clinically informative thresholds can be derived for different versions of the PID‐5, although such thresholds may vary according to instrument version, population, and clinical reference standard. Using the original 220‐item PID‐5 in Iranian clinical and nonclinical samples, scores above approximately 1.5 across domains and facets were found to be indicative of clinical status (Komasi et al. 2024). Similarly, in a Brazilian psychiatric inpatient sample, PID‐5 traits differentiated psychiatric patients from normative participants, and the resulting diagnostic indices were characterized by relatively high negative predictive values (NPVs) but comparatively low positive predictive values (PPVs) (Lugo et al. 2018). More recently, the PID5BF + M has also demonstrated the ability to distinguish clinical from community participants in an Iranian sample (Komasi et al. 2025a). Together with normative approaches that define pathological elevations relative to population distributions (e.g., Miller et al. 2022), these studies illustrate both the potential clinical utility of quantitative thresholds and the need for population‐ and instrument‐specific validation. This evidence is particularly relevant for the PID‐5‐BF because its brevity makes it suitable for initial screening in settings in which administration of the full PID‐5 may not be feasible. However, empirically established cutoff values specifically for the 25‐item PID‐5‐BF remain limited.

Consequently, a conspicuous discrepancy exists in the diagnostic calibration of the PID‐5‐BF. The implementation of empirically derived cutoff scores for the PID‐5‐BF has the potential to enhance the efficiency and accuracy of screening for elevated pathological personality traits. Moreover, the implementation of cutoff scores aligns with established clinical assessment methodologies, thereby enhancing the interpretability of the measure. In conclusion, PID‐5‐BF as a screening instrument can support clinical decision‐making processes concerning further diagnostic procedures or treatment planning. In addressing this gap, the present study endeavored to define empirically validated cutoff scores for the instrument in question and to explore its diagnostic performance on a clinical and nonclinical sample.

Objective

Considering the need for further evaluation of the clinical utility of the PID‐5‐BF, the present study had two primary aims.

Research Aim 1. We examined whether individuals with PDs, clinical participants with symptom disorders but without PDs, and nonclinical participants differed in both the overall level and the domain‐specific profile of pathological personality traits. We expected participants with PDs to show the highest levels of pathological personality traits, followed by clinical participants without PDs, whereas the nonclinical group was expected to show the lowest scores. We further examined whether the magnitude of these group differences varied across the five PID‐5‐BF domains.

Research Aim 2. We evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of the PID‐5‐BF total and domain scores for distinguishing the three groups and derived cutoff scores intended to support the use of the PID‐5‐BF as an initial screening instrument.

Method

Procedure

The study was approved by the Scientific and Research Ethics Committee of the Hungarian Medical Research Council (reference no. IV/9827‐1/2021/EKU), and all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. To obtain data for the nonclinical subsample (N = 867), subjects with relative mental well‐being were selected from the Hungarian adaptation study of PID‐5‐BF (Birkás et al. 2018). Eligibility for the nonclinical group required relative or complete agreement with the statement “I consider myself mentally healthy,” rated on a 4‐point Likert scale. This item was used as an indicator of self‐perceived mental well‐being rather than as a diagnostic assessment of the absence of mental disorder (Ahmad et al. 2014; McAlpine et al. 2018; Stubbs and Achat 2023).

The clinical subsample (N = 301) comprised data collected from psychiatric outpatient units and psychotherapy inpatient units in Budapest using convenient sampling. Patients diagnosed with mental disorders were selected for inclusion in the study. Participants with neurodevelopmental, psychotic, and mood disorders with psychotic features were excluded from the study. Clinical diagnoses were established according to the ICD‐10 criteria as part of routine clinical assessment by professionals (e.g., psychiatrists and/or licensed clinical psychologists). Data collection and diagnostic review were conducted by co‐authors with clinical psychologists degree. On this basis, the clinical sample was divided into participants with a PD, with or without comorbid symptom disorders (PD group), and participants with symptom disorders but without a PD diagnosis (non‐PD clinical group).

The nonclinical subsample's data were collected online using Google Forms, whereas the clinical sample's data were collected using a paper–pencil format. The data were collected anonymously. Participation in the study was voluntary, with the option to withdraw at any time without facing any negative consequences. No form of compensation or reward was provided in exchange for participation. In all cases, participants provided informed consent.

Sample

A total of 1168 individuals (854 women and 314 men) participated in the study. The mean age of the subjects was M = 30.47 years (SD = 11.07; Md = 27). A comparison of demographic characteristics was conducted across subsamples (see Table 1). A subsequent chi‐squared test revealed that sexes were equally distributed across the subsamples. There was a significant age difference between the subsamples exhibiting a modest to medium effect size (partial η 2 = 0.043). According to the Bonferroni‐corrected post hoc tests, participants in the non‐PD subsample were significantly older than those in the nonclinical or PD subsamples. No statistically significant age difference was identified between the nonclinical and PD subsamples. A subsequent χ 2‐test of the distribution of educational levels across subsamples revealed that the clinical subsample, particularly the non‐PD subsample, exhibited a significantly higher level of education compared to the nonclinical subsample.

In the clinical subsample, the PD subsample comprised 64 patients. Specifically, 43.1% of the sample received an unspecified or mixed PD diagnosis, whereas 50.8% received a specific A or B cluster PD diagnosis. The remaining 6.1% of the sample were diagnosed with a specific C cluster PD. The non‐PD subsample included 237 patients. The most prevalent primary diagnoses in this subsample were from the categories of “anxiety, dissociative, stress‐related, somatoform and other nonpsychotic mental disorders” (F40–F48; 58.3%) and “mood (affective) disorders” (F30–F39; 38.3%) groups of mental disorders.

Measures

In addition to the reporting of demographic data (age, sex, and level of education), all participants completed the Hungarian version of the PID‐5‐BF (Birkás et al. 2018). The PID‐5‐BF is a brief self‐report questionnaire comprising 25 items that assess maladaptive personality traits in accordance with the AMPD. The assessment of pathological personality domains is conducted using a set of five items for each domain (refer to Table 2 for illustrative examples). The items were rated using a 4‐point Likert scale, ranging from 0, indicating completely untrue to 3, indicating completely true. The domain and total scores were calculated by averaging the scores of individual responses. Higher scores on each scale are indicative of the presence of more pronounced pathological personality traits. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach's α. Consistent with commonly used psychometric guidelines, α ≥ 0.70 was considered indicative of acceptable internal consistency, whereas values between 0.60 and 0.69 were considered marginal and interpreted cautiously, particularly given that each PID‐5‐BF domain is represented by only five items (Tavakol and Dennick 2011). The PID‐5‐BF total score showed good internal consistency (α = 0.855), whereas disinhibition (α = 0.722) and psychoticism (α = 0.755) demonstrated acceptable internal consistency. Detachment (α = 0.687) and negative affectivity (α = 0.696) were slightly below the conventional 0.70 threshold, whereas antagonism showed the lowest internal consistency (α = 0.616). Consequently, findings involving these latter domain scores, particularly Antagonism, should be interpreted with caution.

Statistical Analytical Plan

Data analyses and data visualizations were performed using jamovi (The jamovi project 2024) and the pROC package (Robin et al. 2011) in R (v4.1.2; R Core Team 2021). Potential demographic differences between groups were first evaluated using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for age and chi‐square tests for sex and educational attainment. To examine between‐group differences in the overall level of pathological personality traits, separate one‐way ANOVAs were conducted for the PID‐5‐BF total score and each of the five domain scores. Significant omnibus effects were followed by Tukey‐adjusted pairwise comparisons. Partial η 2 was calculated as the effect‐size estimate for omnibus ANOVA effects, and Cohen's d was calculated for pairwise group comparisons.

To examine whether the profile of pathological trait scores differed between groups, a mixed‐design repeated‐measures ANOVA was conducted. PID‐5‐BF domain (negative affectivity, detachment, antagonism, disinhibition, and psychoticism) served as the within‐subject factor, and clinical group (nonclinical, non‐PD clinical, and PD) served as the between‐subject factor. Of primary interest was the domain × group interaction, indicating whether the relative pattern of domain scores differed across groups. Thus, the repeated‐measures component reflected multiple trait‐domain scores obtained from each participant rather than longitudinal measurements.

The diagnostic accuracy of PID‐5‐BF was evaluated through the implementation of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The data set was then randomly segmented into two distinct sets: a training set comprising 70% of the total data and a test set constituting the remaining 30%. The training set was utilized to calibrate the model and calculate cutoff scores. ROC curve analyses were adjusted for sex, age, and education level to control for the potential confounding effects of these variables (for details, see Section 3.2). In the context of ROC curve analysis, the measurement of diagnostic accuracy is achieved through the calculation of the area under the ROC curve (AUC). The AUC is a quantitative metric that ranges from 0 to 1, with 1 representing a test with perfect performance (Faraggi and Reiser 2002). It is widely accepted that diagnostic tests should have an AUC greater than 0.5, with those demonstrating an AUC greater than 0.75 being considered to be of significant clinical utility (Chapman et al. 2016; Jutzeler et al. 2017). The test set was subsequently utilized to predict probabilities.

The ROC analysis delineates the sensitivity (i.e., true positives) and specificity (i.e., true negatives) at each scale value. The determination of a cutoff score is predicated on a perpetual trade‐off between sensitivity and specificity. Ideally, the cutoff value should optimize both sensitivity and specificity (Fan et al. 2006). The determination of the cutoff values was informed by Youden's index (Youden 1950), which is defined as J = maxc {Sensitivity(c) + Specificity(c) − 1}. The cutoff value with the highest Youden index value was deemed optimal. The statistical results are presented in tables rather than in the text to facilitate the interpretation of the results.

Results

In accordance with our initial research objective, the three subsamples (PD, non‐PD, and sine morbo, respectively) differed significantly in the PID‐5‐BF total score and in all five domain scores (Table 3). The magnitude of the omnibus group effects varied considerably across outcomes. The largest effect was observed for disinhibition (partial η 2 = 0.136), followed by the PID‐5‐BF total score (partial η 2 = 0.123), detachment (partial η 2 = 0.118), and negative affectivity (partial η 2 = 0.090). In contrast, group differences in psychoticism and antagonism were substantially smaller (partial η 2 = 0.013 for both). For the total score, disinhibition, detachment, and negative affectivity, all three groups differed significantly in the expected direction: the nonclinical group showed the lowest scores, the non‐PD clinical group showed intermediate scores, and the PD group showed the highest scores. For psychoticism and antagonism, the nonclinical and non‐PD clinical groups did not differ significantly, whereas participants with PDs scored significantly higher than both groups. Thus, the hypothesis of progressively elevated pathological traits across the three groups was supported for the total score and three of the five domains but not uniformly across all domains.

In view of the divergent effect sizes observed for the subsamples across the various PID‐5‐BF scales and in accordance with our second research aim, we employed repeated‐measures ANOVA to ascertain whether patterns of pathological personality traits—and not merely the intensity of their presence—differed across the three subsamples. The descriptives for the subsamples are presented in Table 3 and visualized in Figure 1. The findings of the analysis indicated a significant interaction between the scores on different scales and subsamples (F (8,4660) = 24.717; p < 0.001; partial η 2 = 0.041). Consequently, the differences between the subsamples in different personality trait domains are not equivalent in their magnitude. In particular, disinhibition and detachment exhibited the most pronounced differences between the groups, whereas antagonism and psychoticism contributed to a lesser extent to group discrimination, as evidenced by the effect sizes and between‐group contrast. In this model, the main effect of “group” was significant (F (2,1166) = 82.00, p < 0.001, partial η 2 = 0.123).

In order to test the diagnostic accuracy of the PID‐5‐BF total score and scales and to determine cutoff scores, ROC curve analyses were performed using the sample split method. A random sample of 70% of the data set was utilized to determine the cutoff values that optimally discriminated between the subsamples. The remaining 30% of the data set was then utilized to evaluate the performance of these cutoff scores. The determination of cutoff scores was performed with adjustment for sex, age, and education level. 1 As shown in Table 4, the findings indicate that the PID‐5‐BF total score, disinhibition, and detachment scores demonstrate a high degree of accuracy in differentiating between the nonclinical and non‐PD subsamples, as well as the nonclinical and PD subsamples. The AUC values for these metrics exceed 0.75, indicating their potential for reliable discrimination. The negative affectivity variable demonstrated the capacity to accurately differentiate between nonclinical and PD subsamples. The psychoticism and antagonism scales were not able to successfully differentiate between nonclinical and any of the clinical subsamples. For the clinically most relevant contrast between the nonclinical and PD groups, the corrected AUC for the PID‐5‐BF total score was 0.849 and the optimal cutoff was ≥ 1.32. As indicated by the prevalence‐adjusted performance indices (i.e., PPVs and NPVs), it was observed that all cutoff scores were more successful in avoiding false negative results (average NPV = 0.934) compared to false positive results (average PPV = 0.327). Consequently, only a small number of individuals were identified as having a PD if that was not the case. Concurrently, a considerable number of individuals evaded identification as having a symptom disorder or a PD, despite the existence of such disorders. The scale with the highest performance was PID‐5‐BF total score, which demonstrated particular efficacy in differentiating between individuals with a healthy mental state (nonclinical subsample) and those with a PD (subsample).

Discussion

The present study examined pathological personality traits across nonclinical participants, clinical patients without PDs, and patients with PDs and evaluated the diagnostic performance of the PID‐5‐BF across these groups. Three findings appear to be particularly important. First, the total level of pathological personality traits followed the expected gradient, with the lowest scores in the nonclinical group, intermediate scores among clinical participants without PDs, and the highest scores among participants with PDs. Second, this gradient was not uniform across domains: Disinhibition and detachment showed considerably stronger between‐group differentiation than psychoticism and antagonism. Third, the PID‐5‐BF performed considerably better in distinguishing PD patients from nonclinical participants than in differentiating PDs from other psychiatric disorders. This pattern supports the potential utility of the measure for broad screening of personality pathology but also indicates important limits to its diagnostic specificity.

The progressively increasing total scores across the three groups are consistent with the dimensional conceptualization underlying the AMPD. Maladaptive personality traits are not assumed to be exclusively present among individuals with categorical PD diagnoses; rather, they vary continuously and are also associated with broader psychopathology. Accordingly, the intermediate scores of the non‐PD clinical group are theoretically meaningful rather than representing measurement error. Previous studies have similarly demonstrated elevated PID‐5 traits across heterogeneous psychiatric populations and substantial associations between maladaptive traits and psychopathology beyond formally diagnosed PDs (e.g., Anderson et al. 2018; Lugo et al. 2018; Pires et al. 2021).

The particularly pronounced group differences in disinhibition and detachment may also help explain why these domains showed relatively favorable diagnostic performance in the ROC analyses. Disinhibition captures tendencies toward impulsivity, irresponsibility, and difficulties in regulating behavior, whereas detachment reflects withdrawal, restricted interpersonal engagement, and reduced capacity for positive emotional involvement. These features may become particularly pronounced in clinically significant personality pathology and may therefore contribute to discrimination between PD and nonclinical participants. In contrast, antagonism and psychoticism yielded much smaller group effects. Importantly, this should not be interpreted as evidence that these domains are clinically unimportant. Their relevance may be more specific to particular PD configurations or subgroups and may therefore be obscured when heterogeneous PD diagnoses are combined into a single group. This issue is especially pertinent in the present sample because a large proportion of patients received mixed or unspecified PD diagnoses.

The cutoff findings should be interpreted within the emerging literature on clinically meaningful thresholds for PID‐5 measures. Former findings related to the 220‐item PID‐5 in Iranian clinical and nonclinical samples showed that scores above approximately 1.5 across domains and facets were indicative of clinical status (Komasi et al. 2024). These values are not directly comparable with the present PID‐5‐BF total score cutoff of 1.32 because the studies differed in instrument length, scoring unit, clinical criterion, sample composition, and cultural context. Nevertheless, both findings support the broader premise that quantitatively interpretable thresholds can facilitate the clinical use of dimensional personality measures. Similarly, in another study, discrimination of psychiatric patients from normative participants using the original PID‐5 was reported while also observing a pattern of high negative but relatively low PPVs (Lugo et al. 2018). Recent findings for the PID5BF + M further indicate that brief maladaptive trait measures can discriminate clinical from community samples (Komasi et al. 2025b). Taken together, these studies suggest that brief PID‐5 measures may have considerable value for screening while also indicating that cutoff scores should not be assumed to generalize unchanged across instruments, populations, or healthcare contexts.

The discrepancy between the high NPVs and comparatively low PPVs is clinically important. In the present study, the average NPV across cutoff analyses was 0.934, whereas the average PPV was only 0.327. Thus, scores below the proposed thresholds were relatively informative for identifying individuals unlikely to belong to the relevant clinical group, whereas scores above the threshold were substantially less definitive evidence of a PD. This pattern is consistent with the role of the PID‐5‐BF as a screening rather than diagnostic measure. A positive screen should therefore be understood as an indication for more comprehensive assessment of personality functioning and personality pathology rather than as confirmation of a PD diagnosis. This distinction is particularly important because predictive values are influenced by disorder prevalence and may therefore vary considerably across clinical settings.

The scores of the present nonclinical sample may also be considered in relation to the representative PID‐5 normative data reporting mean domain scores of 0.92 for negative affectivity, 0.84 for detachment, 0.71 for antagonism, 0.66 for disinhibition, and 0.63 for psychoticism (Miller et al. 2022). Corresponding means in the present Hungarian nonclinical sample were 1.10, 0.78, 0.54, 0.81, and 0.88, respectively. Thus, relative to these reference values, the present nonclinical participants showed somewhat higher negative affectivity, disinhibition, and psychoticism and somewhat lower antagonism and detachment. These comparisons should remain descriptive because Miller et al. (2022) used the original PID‐5 rather than the 25‐item PID‐5‐BF, and the samples also differ in demographic and cultural characteristics. Nevertheless, the observed variation illustrates why universal raw‐score thresholds should be treated cautiously. Cultural differences in the expression, interpretation, and endorsement of maladaptive personality characteristics, together with differences in sampling and healthcare context, may affect both score distributions and optimal clinical thresholds. Cross‐cultural replication is therefore particularly important before the present Hungarian cutoff values are applied outside comparable settings.

Domain‐level findings should also be interpreted in light of the internal consistency estimates observed in the present sample. Although the PID‐5‐BF total score showed good reliability and disinhibition and psychoticism exceeded the conventional 0.70 threshold, detachment (α = 0.687), negative affectivity (α = 0.696), and particularly antagonism (α = 0.616) showed lower internal consistency. The brevity of the PID‐5‐BF necessarily limits the number of indicators available for each domain, which can reduce coefficient alpha; nevertheless, lower reliability increases measurement error and may attenuate associations with external criteria. Consequently, domain‐specific cutoff estimates—especially for antagonism—should be regarded as preliminary and should not be used as stand‐alone clinical thresholds without further replication.

Strengths and Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, the PD group was relatively small (N = 64). This limitation becomes particularly important in the training–test split used for the diagnostic accuracy analyses because fewer than 20 participants with PD were available in the test subset. Consequently, estimates of diagnostic accuracy and optimal cutoff scores may be unstable, and the observed performance may be overestimated or sample specific. Independent replication in substantially larger PD samples is therefore required.

Second, the nonclinical group cannot be regarded as a diagnostically confirmed healthy or sine morbo sample. Classification was based on participants' responses to a single self‐report item (“I consider myself mentally healthy”). Although single‐item measures of self‐rated mental health have shown associations with broader indices of psychological well‐being, this item is not a validated diagnostic screening instrument and cannot exclude the presence of a current or previous mental disorder. Some individuals with unrecognized, untreated, or undisclosed psychopathology may therefore have been included in the nonclinical group. This could have reduced group differences and influenced the estimated cutoff scores. Accordingly, we use the term “nonclinical” rather than “healthy” or “sine morbo” when referring to this group.

Third, the sample was not nationally representative. Differences in age and educational attainment were present across groups, and although the ROC analyses statistically adjusted for age, sex, and education, statistical adjustment cannot substitute for representative sampling. Future research using representative Hungarian samples would allow the development of more robust national normative data and would facilitate investigation of demographic variation in PID‐5‐BF scores.

Fourth, the diagnostic heterogeneity within the PD group—particularly the high proportion of mixed or unspecified PD diagnoses—may also have reduced the apparent discriminative performance of domains that are more strongly associated with specific PD configurations.

Fifth, the internal consistency of detachment, negative affectivity, and particularly antagonism was below the conventional 0.70 threshold. Although this may partly reflect the small number of items within each PID‐5‐BF domain, it introduces additional measurement error and requires caution when interpreting domain‐specific group differences and cutoff values.

Finally, the present analyses primarily adopted a variable‐oriented approach. The AMPD conceptualizes personality pathology in terms of configurations of maladaptive traits rather than isolated domain elevations. Future studies using latent profile analysis, cluster analysis, or related person‐oriented approaches may therefore provide additional information about clinically meaningful patterns of PID‐5‐BF scores.

Conclusion

The present findings support the PID‐5‐BF primarily as a brief screening instrument, rather than as an independent diagnostic test for PD. Its brevity and straightforward scoring make it feasible for settings in which a rapid initial assessment of maladaptive personality traits is needed. Among the examined indices, the total score showed the most consistent screening performance. A total score of ≥ 1.32 may therefore be considered a preliminary indicator that more comprehensive assessment of personality pathology is warranted when distinguishing individuals with PD from nonclinical individuals.

Importantly, this cutoff should not be interpreted as a diagnostic boundary. The comparatively low PPVs observed in the present study indicate that a substantial proportion of individuals exceeding a threshold will not necessarily have a PD. Conversely, the high NPVs suggest that the PID‐5‐BF may be more useful for ruling out substantial personality pathology than for confirming a diagnosis. Positive screening results should therefore be followed by comprehensive assessment of personality functioning, maladaptive trait configuration, clinical history, and, where appropriate, structured diagnostic evaluation.

Domain scores may provide useful supplementary information about the likely configuration of maladaptive traits, but their interpretation should be more cautious than that of the total score because diagnostic performance and reliability differed substantially across domains. In particular, the present domain‐specific cutoff estimates should be regarded as provisional rather than as stand‐alone clinical decision rules (Bach et al. 2015; Pires et al. 2021; Anderson et al. 2018; American Psychiatric Association (APA) 2000; Miller et al. 2022; Bastiaens et al. 2021; Rossi et al. 2021).
