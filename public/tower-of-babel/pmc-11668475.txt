Incidental diagnosis of subcutaneous filariasis with an unusual presentation in a non-endemic area—Need to worry?
Authors: Shivanee Sharma, Pooja S. Kala, Avneesh Malviya, Anjali Lakhani, Naveen Chandra
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report an interesting case of Bancroftian filariasis diagnosed on fine needle aspiration cytology in a young female hailing from a non-endemic hilly area of India, who presented with a subcutaneous swelling on the left forearm. This is an unusual extra-lymphatic site to harbor filarial infestation and the cytology smears showed adult worms, eggs, and microfilariae of Wuchereria bancrofti.

Introduction

Filariasis and its complications exist as a major public health problem in subtropical and tropical countries including India.[12] Many Indian states are endemic for filariasis excluding a few states, e.g., Uttarakhand.[3] The common causative agents of filariasis are Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia malayi, and Loa loa. The infections are usually asymptomatic but can be symptomatic with acute to chronic presentation and can even sometimes be fatal.[45]

The conventional method for diagnosing this disease is the demonstration of microfilaria in the peripheral blood smear. Other methods to diagnose filariasis, like antigen testing, molecular assays, and PCR are expensive and not routinely available in most laboratories.[6] There have been reports of diagnosing this disease by demonstrating microfilaria in other parts of the body and body fluids by cytopathological examination.[7] Cytopathology is an underutilized but easy, cheap, and widely available modality to diagnose filariasis in a country with a high disease burden like India. This is also useful when there is an unusual presentation, especially sans a demonstration of the parasite or eosinophilia in the peripheral blood smear.[58]

We report a case of a young female hailing from a non-endemic hilly area, with the presence of adult gravid worm, eggs, and microfilaria of W. bancrofti in cytopathology smears prepared from an aspirate taken from left mid-arm swelling. The patient did not have a history of travel or contact history with the endemic zone.

Case History

A 21-year-old homemaker from a low socio-economic status, residing in Dehradun, Uttarakhand, presented in the medicine outpatient department. She noticed a swelling in her left forearm two weeks back which was gradually increasing in size. She visited the out-patient department in Government Doon Medical college, Dehradun, India. There were no other related symptoms. The patient did not travel to any endemic area for filariasis in the past 1 year. On inspection, an ill-defined subcutaneous swelling, measuring 2 × 2 cm, was seen medially below the left cubital fossa [Figure 1]. The swelling was soft and non-tender on palpation, and the overlying skin was normal. A clinical diagnosis of lipoma was made based on local examination before sending the patient for fine needle aspiration (FNA) to confirm.

Clinical picture of the patient’s left forearm subcutaneous swelling

FNA was performed from the swelling. FNA yielded a few drops of greyish-white material. Air-dried and wet smears were prepared from the aspirate. The smears were then stained with May-Grunwald Giemsa (MGG) and Papanicolaou (PAP) stains.

Microscopic examination of smears showed >100 microfilariae per slide along with two types of adult worms [Figures 2 and 3]. The bulkier and longer gravid female adult worms, contained and were delivering embryonated eggs, simultaneously. The other adult worm was thinner and did not contain any eggs. This later worm was possibly a male adult worm. The microfilariae present in the smear were sheathed, and there was an absence of nuclei at the tip of the tails. Multiple embryonated eggs and coiled microfilariae were also noted. The background was dirty and showed a fair number of mixed inflammatory cells predominantly consisting of eosinophils along with macrophages, plasma cells, and lymphocytes. An occasional ill-defined granuloma along with multinucleate giant cells were also present in the smears. Notably, a polymorphous population of lymphoid cells was not seen.

The stages of the Wuchereria bancrofti (x400). (a) Embryonated eggs in clusters adherent to their mother (MGG stain). (b) Numerous microfilariae, >100 in number (MGG stain). (c) Embryonated eggs and a multinucleate giant cell in a dense inflammatory background (PAP stain). (d) Sheathed microfilaria of W. bancrofti (MGG stain)

Based on the aforementioned cytological findings, the microfilariae were identified as the larval forms of Wuchereria bancrofti, and a final diagnosis of filariasis was rendered, which affected an extra-lymphatic site. A complete blood count (CBC) and peripheral blood smear examination were also performed. The CBC parameters were within normal limits, and the peripheral smear was negative for any haemoparasite. No peripheral blood leukocytosis or eosinophilia was noted.

Discussion

Filariasis and its consequences is a major public health problem in India that poses a significant socio-economic challenge as well. As per the National Center for Vector-borne Disease Control, the Indian states of Andhra Pradesh, Assam, Bihar, Chhattisgarh, Goa, Jharkhand, Karnataka, Gujarat, Kerala, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Orissa, Tamil Nadu, Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal, Pondicherry, Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Daman and Diu, Dadra and Nagar Haveli, and Lakshadweep are endemic to filariasis. Notably, Uttarakhand (where our center is situated and the index case hails from) is not enlisted.[9]

Taxonomically, the filarial worms are classified as nematodes (roundworms) belonging to the superfamily Filarioidea and family Onchocercidae.[10] The clinical infection in filariasis is broadly of two types, i.e., a) skin and subcutaneous tissue with the most commonly reported causative organisms being Onchocerca volvulus and Loa loa, and b) lymphatic filariasis where the most commonly reported organisms are Wuchereria bancrofti and Brugia malayi.[11] The mosquitoes serve as intermediate hosts, the main species being Culex in urban/semi-urban areas and Anopheles in rural areas. Humans are the definitive hosts where the mosquitoes introduce third-stage larval forms, developing into adults in the lymphatics and releasing microfilariae that travel along the lymphatic and blood channels to get ingested by mosquitoes and continue their development into further larval forms.[12] The incidence of filariasis in India has been predominated by two out of the eight species of filarial worms, viz. Wuchereria bancrofti and Brugia malayi. The clinical presentation of W. bancrofti in humans has been reported to vary from asymptomatic microfilaremia, and acute lymphangitis to chronic lymphangitis with lymphedema. The demonstration of filarial worms at any stage in the peripheral blood smear is considered the gold standard despite the availability of various other methods.

The filarial worms are distinguished by the presence/absence of sheath and the nuclei in the tail.[8] The contribution of cytology in diagnosing parasitic infestation in India is relatively less acknowledged and is usually an incidental finding.[5] The unusual sites noted in medical literature for Bancroftian filariasis diagnosed on cytology smears were the genital region (epididymis, testes, vulva, and cervix), breast, thyroid, bone marrow, retroperitoneum, body fluid (urine), and effusion cytology (pleural fluid) and seldom from subcutaneous/soft tissues.[571314]

The present case was of subcutaneous extra-lymphatic Bancroftian filariasis from a non-endemic area, provisionally diagnosed as a lipoma over the left arm and sent for FNA. All the stages of development of the worm were noted—the adult male and female worms, embryonated eggs, and the microfilariae.

The presence of an adult gravid worm on cytology has been rare, and even more so in subcutaneous swelling as in our index case.[4] The female and male W. bancrofti worms measure 80–100 × 0.25 mm and 40 × 0.1 mm, respectively.[5] The sheathed microfilariae of W. bancrofti are differentiated from other sheathed larval forms of Brugia malayi and Loa loa by the absence of nuclei at the tip of the tail. The microfilariae of Brugia malayi have two distinct nuclei at the tip of the tail while those of Loa loa have nuclei extending up to the tip of the tail.

The first case of Bancroftian filariasis in Uttarakhand was reported in 2009 by Thapliyal et al.[3] which was followed by a handful of other reports. The sites reported were the spermatic cord, testes, and arm.[6815] Subcutaneous swellings showing various stages have been reported by various authors in endemic areas, whereas such a finding has not been reported frequently in a non-endemic region.[11131617]

The increased number of cases of filariasis in the non-endemic regions could be because of climate change, and migration of population which affects the demography of the area.[3] In India, the elimination of filariasis was pledged in the National Health Policy (2002) and was expected to be completed by the year 2015. The deadline was later extended to 2021.[18] The occurrence of filariasis in non-endemic areas, with such a high parasitic burden (as in the present case) today raises an alarm for better control of the vector, quicker diagnosis with a high index of suspicion, and early initiation of treatment helping in the elimination of this disease.

Filariasis may remain asymptomatic for a long time, with atypical presentations as well. India is still lagging in eliminating it. All cytopathological smears must be viewed with due diligence, considering parasitic infestations as possible differential since it is inexpensive but invaluable in diagnosing asymptomatic, atypical presentations, in the absence of parasitemia/eosinophilia, thus aiding in early interventions in non-endemic regions.
