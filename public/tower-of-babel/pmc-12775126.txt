Deep learning-based high dynamic range 3D reconstruction
Authors: Yifan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThree-dimensional (3D) reconstruction based on fringe projection profilometry (FPP) is a crucial technique for capturing surface topography in high-precision industrial manufacturing. However, overexposure phenomenon frequently occurs in captured images due to variations in object reflectance and lighting conditions, leading to reduced 3D reconstruction accuracy. This represents the most challenging issue in high dynamic range (HDR) environments. To this end, I propose a deep learning-based fringe image restoration method. It utilizes the derivative networks of U-Net to restore saturated fringes, enabling subsequent 3D reconstruction. This method significantly enhances reconstruction accuracy without requiring additional hardware or capturing multiple extra image sets for prediction. I further systematically compared the performance of three network architectures—U-Net, Res-U-Net, and SE-U-Net—in the fringe repair task, revealing their respective capabilities through quantitative experimental analysis. Comparative experiments show that all three networks in this paper can effectively repair saturated fringes, with SE-U-Net exhibiting superior performance in restoring missing regions. This study not only validates the effectiveness of deep learning for repairing saturated fringe images in HDR scenes, but also provides guidance for selecting network models in grating fringe restoration.Keywords: Three-dimensional, Fringe projection profilometry, Overexposure phenomenon, Deep learning, U-NetSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Optics and photonics

Introduction

Three-dimensional (3D) imaging techniques1 achieve 3D reconstruction by comprehensively capturing the spatial geometric information of objects—including their depth, shape, and spatial structure. They have been widely applied in fields such as medical diagnosis, industrial inspection, and reverse engineering2. Typically, 3D imaging techniques are categorized into contact measurement3 and non-contact measurement with the latter primarily relying on optical methods. Among these, structured light scanning technology4, owing to its high accuracy and resolution, has become a focus of both research and practical applications.

As a significant branch of structured light scanning5, Fringe Projection Profilometry (FPP) projects a set of known structured fringe images onto the object’s6. A camera captures the deformed fringe images, which undergo phase demodulation, phase unwrapping, and 2D-3D using calibration parameters7. The most common methods for phase demonstration include transform profilometry based on single frame fringe projection, such as Fourier transform profilometry (FTP)8 and wavelet transform profilometry (WTP)9, and phase-shifting profilometry (PSP)10,11 based on multi frame fringe projection. The phase demodulation accuracy of FTP and WTP is low, suitable for simple continuous shapes, and sensitive to noise. PSP has high precision and robustness, but requires three or more fringes. FTP, WTP, and PSP algorithms do not obtain continuous phases, but are folded into wrapped phases from -π to π, requiring further phase unwrapping.

Phase unwrapping methods are generally categorized into Temporal Phase Unwrapping (TPU)12,13 and Spatial Phase Unwrapping (SPU)14,15. TPU is often combined with Phase-shifting Profilometry (PSP)16 to achieve high-precision, pixel-by-pixel phase extraction. In contrast, SPU typically employs Fourier transforms to extract phase information17,18, relying on phase values from adjacent pixels for unwrapping, which makes it unsuitable for isolated or complex objects. Furthermore, in practical measurements, SPU is susceptible to factors such as noise, abrupt changes, and loss of detail in shadowed regions, resulting in low reconstruction accuracy.

In addition, the diversity of surface colors and textures on objects leads to varying reflectivity. Constrained by the camera’s grayscale range (0–255 for 8-bit cameras), signals from highly reflective areas often exceed the dynamic range of the camera. This causes fringe images to saturate, resulting in the loss of surface information in corresponding regions and triggering significant measurement errors or even erroneous results. The most commonly used method for 3D measurement of highly reflective surfaces is typically referred to as high dynamic range (HDR) technology19. Achieving accurate 3D shape measurement has remained a key challenge and difficulty in HDR 3D reconstruction.

Currently, multiple high dynamic range (HDR) measurement methods exist. For instance, spraying powder onto object surfaces to reduce reflectivity converts specular reflection into diffuse reflection20, but uneven spraying can compromise measurement accuracy. Additionally, researchers have proposed alternative solutions that typically require additional hardware or the projection of more images. These include adding extra cameras21,22, polarizers23, or employing multi-exposure techniques24,25, all of which significantly increase system complexity and time consumption.

Furthermore, Cui et al.26 introduced an adaptive HDR method based on temporal superposition, which determines optimal parameters for specific scenarios and predicts phase accuracy. Xu et al.27 proposed adaptive fringe projection, where adaptive fringe images are generated through speckle image matching. While this reduces redundancy in multiple exposures, it still requires additional images. Ma et al.28 proposed a bit-decomposition projection method that suppresses HDR interference through differential processing, and adjusts the merged image to an 8-bit grayscale fringe image via binary difference decomposition. However, this method is highly sensitive to projector defocusing, limiting its accuracy under significant defocus or depth variations.

Subsequently, deep learning29, a data-driven machine learning technique, has achieved transformative success in fields such as image segmentation, computational imaging, and optical metrology30. In recent years, it has demonstrated potential in wrapped phase computation31,32, fringe noise removal33, missing fringe restoration34–36, and enhance fringe modulation37. Wang et al.34 designed a self-supervised network (SMEF) that requires merging images with different exposures during the testing phase to eliminate abnormally exposed regions, necessitating additional image acquisition for the prediction process. Yang et al.35 introduced a low-modulation region detection module based on U-Net, combining enhancement modules, global fringe distribution, and detail intensity to predict saturated and dark region information. However, this method relies on simulated fringe training and does not fully account for disturbances such as noise and ambient light in actual measurements. Zhang et al.36 utilized U-Net combined with fringe restoration to achieve saturated image restoration across multiple systems, but did not address derivative networks or comparative analysis of multiple network performances.

To address this, this paper proposes a deep learning-based restoration method for HDR images. Three network models: U-Net, Res-U-Net, and SE-U-Net38, are introduced to predict normal fringe images. This method requires only the images captured at different exposure times as input and output for network training. Subsequently, the obtained normal fringe images undergo TPU via the tri-frequency heterodyne algorithm to obtain the final phase information. Experimental results demonstrate that all three networks can predict high-quality images that are visually indistinguishable from the ground truth, enabling accurate 3D reconstruction. Among them, SE-U-Net performs best in restoring missing fringe information.

The grayscale range of an 8-bit camera typically spans from 0 to 255. In practical measurements, objects with varying surface reflectivities are often encountered. Differences in reflective properties can significantly impact the quality of fringe images. Deep neural network architecture

This study employs U-Net and its two enhanced variants, Residual U-Net (Res-U-Net) and Squeeze-and-Excitation U-Net (SE-U-Net), for the task of fringe image restoration. U-Net is a classical encoder-decoder architecture initially developed for medical image segmentation. Its skip-connections mechanism effectively preserves detailed image information, which is crucial for restoring texture in fringe patterns. Res-U-Net incorporates residual connections to mitigate the vanishing gradient problem in deep networks, thereby enhancing the capability to reconstruct continuous fringe information in saturated regions. SE-U-Net introduces a channel attention mechanism that adaptively recalibrates feature responses by emphasizing informative channels, making it particularly suitable for handling complex illumination variations caused by differing surface reflectivities.

The core architectures of the three networks are illustrated in Fig. 2. Figure 2a and b depict the encoder and decoder structures of U-Net, respectively. The encoder progressively extracts features and reduces spatial resolution via convolutional and pooling layers, while the decoder restores spatial resolution through upsampling and skip connections. Figure 2c and d show the encoder and decoder of Res-U-Net, which introduces residual connections atop the U-Net backbone. Figure 2e and b present the encoder and decoder of SE-U-Net. The encoder enhances the structure in Fig. 2a by integrating SE modules. The Squeeze operation compresses features via global average pooling, and the Excitation operation generates channel-wise weights through a two-layer fully connected network, ultimately performing per-channel feature recalibration through weighted scaling.

The proposed method

Specifically, I compare three models—U-Net, Res-U-Net, and SE-U-Net—to evaluate their performance in restoring fringe images. By training these networks, I can predict clear, normal images from input saturated images, enabling subsequent phase extraction and 3D reconstruction. For data-driven deep learning algorithms, the process is divided into two distinct phases: training and testing. During the training phase, the quality of the training data is critical to network performance; hence, the fidelity of the ground truth images directly determines the quality of the predicted outputs. To achieve accurate restoration of missing fringe information, I captured fringe patterns under different exposure conditions. Specifically, saturated fringe images acquired with a 60-ms exposure time served as the network input, while unsaturated fringe images captured with a 10-ms exposure time were used as the output (i.e., ground truth labels). However, for certain objects, saturated regions persisted even with reduced exposure times. In such cases, a developer spray was applied to obtain more accurate unsaturated images as ground truth. This approach effectively mitigates saturation artifacts, ensuring high-quality image prediction and reliable subsequent reconstruction results. In the dataset, only approximately 10% of the highly reflective metallic objects required the use of contrast enhancement spray to acquire ground truth images. Experiment and verification

To validate the effectiveness and accuracy of the proposed method for the 3D shape measurement of highly reflective objects, I construct a FPP system. The experimental setup consists of a digital projector (DLP LightCraft 4500, resolution 1140 × 912 pixels) and an industrial camera (MER-131-210U3M, resolution 640 × 480 pixels). The experiment projects four-step PSP fringe images with three distinct fringe frequencies (high-frequency 64, medium-frequency 56, and low-frequency 49) onto the measured surface, employing a tri-frequency heterodyne TPU for phase unwrapping.

To train the neural network models, I construct datasets by capturing saturated and normal images at varying exposure times. To maximize the network’s generalization capability, different objects (plaster models, masks, and building blocks) are randomly combined and rotated to various angles. At each frequency, 140 datasets of different scenes are established, comprising a training set (100 scenes), a validation set (20 scenes), and a testing set (20 scenes). Each scene contains 12 saturated fringe images and 12 normal fringe images. Representative samples are shown in Fig. 4.

Figure 4a shows the saturated four-step PSP fringe image used as input, while Fig. 4b shows the corresponding label images. Figure 4c shows the intensity curve for a specific cross-section, it can be observed that in the saturated images, most fringe intensities exceed 255, indicating loss of fringe information. In contrast, the fringe intensities in the ground truth image remain below 255, preserving complete information.

The network model was constructed using the PyTorch framework, and all training and testing procedures were conducted on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX A4000 GPU. The training set was used to update the model weights, while the validation set was utilized for real-time performance monitoring, serving as the basis for hyperparameter tuning and selecting the best-performing model.

These models were trained using a mini-batch gradient descent strategy with a batch size of 2. Convolutional kernel parameters were initialized using the Kaiming Normal method. The Mean Squared Error (MSE) was used as the loss function to quantify the discrepancy between the predicted and ground truth images and to guide the parameter updates via gradient descent. The Mean Absolute Error (MAE) was adopted as an evaluation metric to independently assess the models’ generalization performance on unseen data.

The Adam optimizer was used with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10⁻4. A reduction learning rate scheduler was employed, which monitored the training MSE and reduced the learning rate by a factor of 0.5 if the loss failed to decrease by more than 0.001 over 20 consecutive epochs. To stabilize the training, gradient clipping was applied to constrain the norm of the gradients to a maximum value of 10. The training process ran for 300 epochs. Throughout the training, model checkpoints were saved. The checkpoint that achieved the minimum MAE on the validation set was selected as the final model. Conclusion

3D reconstruction of highly reflective objects in HDR images has long been a challenging task. While traditional HDR methods can handle saturation fringes, they often rely on additional hardware or multiple images, increasing both cost and complexity. In recent years, the widespread application of deep learning in image processing has introduced novel approaches to this problem.

The proposed deep learning-based HDR image restoration framework effectively recovers missing information within saturated fringes and converts it into unsaturated images suitable for normal 3D reconstruction—all without requiring additional hardware support or extra image acquisition during the prediction phase. Through systematic introduction and performance comparison of U-Net, Res-U-Net, and SE-U-Net, experiments demonstrate that all three models exhibit strong restoration capabilities, with SE-U-Net achieving superior overall performance.

The core contribution of this study lies not in the first application of U-Net for fringe restoration, but in the systematic comparison of the performance of three network architectures. Through quantitative evaluation metrics—including PSNR, SSIM, and several composite performance indicators—this research provides a detailed analysis of the performance differences among the networks. It not only validates the feasibility and robustness of deep learning methods but also provides a practical solution and theoretical foundation for achieving high-accuracy and high-efficiency 3D reconstruction in real industrial scenarios.

However, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, deep learning models require substantial amounts of high-quality paired data for training, and acquiring large-scale datasets with precise ground-truth measurements is often time-consuming and labor-intensive. Second, while the trained model performs effectively within the current measurement system, its generalizability to other optical setups remains limited. These limitations suggest valuable directions for future research, such as exploring self-supervised learning paradigms and domain adaptation methods to improve data efficiency and cross-system robustness.
