A home and community-based multicomponent physical activity intervention program for Chinese older adults: a development and feasibility study
Authors: Nan Hua, Chengfan Qin, Ange Wang, Yichen Yang, Xinxia Zhang, Qin Zhang, Jing Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInsufficient multicomponent physical activity is prevalent in older adults. In China, a series of supportive policies to promote physical activity (PA) has been implemented. However, feasible PA interventions to promote multicomponent PA among community-dwelling older adults are limited.ObjectiveWe aimed to develop a multicomponent PA intervention program for community-dwelling older adults and assess the feasibility.MethodsThe multicomponent PA intervention was developed by a multidisciplinary working group based on Social Cognitive Theory, the Transtheoretical Model, and behavior change techniques. A three-month feasibility study was conducted in a community in Hangzhou, China. Feasibility was tested by Bowen’s framework for assessing the acceptability, demand, implementation, practicality, adaptation, integration, expansion, and limited efficacy of the PA intervention program. Demographic and clinical information, group exercise attendance, experience questionnaires, physical measurements, and self-reported exercise records were collected.ResultsThe developed PA intervention combined face-to-face group exercise in a community center and home-based exercise assisted by a WeChat applet or exercise manuals, with a weekly goal of engaging in multicomponent exercise at least three days per week recommended by PA guidelines for older adults. A total of 20 participants were recruited for the feasibility study. Overall, 19 participants (95%) completed the intervention in the feasibility study (average age 77 (SD 9) years), with 12 participants using the WeChat applet and 7 using the exercise manuals for home-based exercises. The intervention satisfied all of Bowen’s feasibility criteria. In the third month, 93% of the WeChat applet group and 79% of the non-WeChat applet group adhered to weekly exercise goals. After three months of intervention, statistically significant improvements were observed in physical activity level (Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly score + 33.6, 95% CI 23.5to 43.6), grip strength (mean difference + 1.4 kg, 95% CI 0.1 to 2.7) and chair stand test (mean difference − 3.5 s, 95% CI -4.9 to -2.2).ConclusionHigh exercise adherence was observed throughout the three-month intervention. The theory-based blended PA intervention program was quite feasible in real-world settings, serving as a novel model for promoting multicomponent PA engagement among community-dwelling older adults in China. A future large-scale randomized controlled trial is needed to test the long-term effectiveness and scalability of the PA intervention program.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06633-x.Keywords: Physical activity, Older adults, Community- and home-based, Blended interventions, Multicomponent exercise, Feasibility

Introduction

Physical inactivity remains prevalent globally, with the prevalence being highest among older adults than other age groups [1, 2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) and many other PA guidelines recommend that older adults should engage in at least 150 min of moderate aerobic physical activity per week [3, 4]. More importantly, older adults should engage in multicomponent physical activity that emphasizes functional balance and strength training three or more days a week to maintain functional capacity [5]. However, many older adults failed to achieve the recommended PA level, and participation in multicomponent PA was particularly low [6], revealing a pronounced evidence-practice gap.

In China, a series of supportive policies creates favorable conditions for implementing PA interventions. To address the chronic disease burden associated with population aging, China has introduced a series of health promotion policies aimed at encouraging healthier lifestyles among citizens, such as “Healthy China 2030” [7] and “National Fitness Program (2021–2025) [8]”. The government is actively building an age-friendly environment, expanding the infrastructure and human resources for community exercise service construction, improving exercise venues and facilities, and training instructors for the public exercise education and guidance [8–10]. Despite the increasing policy efforts to promote population-level physical activity, the evidence on theory-driven, contextually appropriate PA interventions designed specifically for improving multicomponent PA levels for community-dwelling older adults remains limited in China.

Applying behavior change theories and behavior change techniques (BCTs) is essential for developing PA interventions. Behavior change theories provide a basis for understanding the mechanisms and determinants of behavior change [11]. For example, the Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) and the Transtheoretical Model (TTM) are widely used effective theories for promoting PA [12]. The TTM conceptualizes behavior change as a multi-stage process, with tailored strategies required at each stage to facilitate progress [13]. The SCT highlights key targets such as self-efficacy, observational learning, self-regulation, and environmental facilitation for behavior change [14, 15]. BCTs, which are active components and specific strategies of an intervention designed to change behavior, serve as tools to translate theoretical constructs into practical strategies [16]. Integrating behavior change theories with appropriate BCTs is therefore important for developing effective interventions to promote PA among older adults [12].

In addition to the theoretical basis, the mode of delivery is also vital to intervention success [17]. Blended interventions [18], which combine mHealth with traditional face-to-face approaches, may be more suitable for older adults than single-format approaches, given their wide variation in digital skills and technology acceptance [19, 20]. Nevertheless, few studies have applied blended interventions for multicomponent PA promotion of older adults. To advance health equity in the older populations, the optimal and viable combination of mHealth with face-to-face interventions that incorporate BCTs for promoting PA warrants further investigation [18, 21]. With the increasing trend of population aging, integrating public resources to develop a multicomponent PA promotion program that is equitable, effective, and scalable for community-dwelling older adults in real-world settings holds significant societal value and provides implications for health promotion policy making.

To better address the evidence-practice gap in multicomponent physical activity among older adults, by integrating government-supported public resources, the present study aimed to: (1) develop a theory-based blended multicomponent PA intervention for community-dwelling older adults based on the SCT, TTM, and BCTs; and (2) assess its feasibility for promoting the initiation of multicomponent PA participation among older adults. This study serves as the basis for a future randomized controlled trial on a larger scale.

Method

The study comprises two research components: (1) the development of a PA intervention program targeting improving multicomponent PA levels of community-dwelling older adults; (2) a three-month study conducted in a local community to assess the implementation feasibility of the developed PA intervention program in the real-world setting using Bowen’s feasibility framework [22].

The development process of the PA intervention program

The development process was guided by a multidisciplinary working group, consisting of geriatric clinicians and rehabilitation exercise specialists, and was carried out in four steps:

Step 1: design a set of multicomponent exercise movements tailored for older adults and assess the safety and learnability

A set of multicomponent exercise movements was developed based on PA guidelines for older adults [3–5] and research evidence, including Vivifrail and Otago programs [23, 24], which have demonstrated benefits in functional capacity and balance. The exercise intensity was individualized based on the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion scale [25] reported by older adults. The final multicomponent exercise consists of 19 movements and walking, including aerobic, strength, balance, and flexibility training (Table S1). This corresponds to approximately 40–50 min of structured multicomponent exercise supplemented by 30 min of dedicated outdoor walking.

We then assessed the safety and learnability of the designed exercise movements using a convenience sample of hospitalized older adults. Conducting the evaluation in a clinician-supervised clinical environment enables the research group to identify potential risks and refine the exercise movement prior to its broader implementation in practice at community and home settings. Clinicians participating in the Geriatric Medicine Clinician Training Program at the Department of Geriatrics at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University were recruited for the assessment. The research group provided training for these clinicians on how to guide exercise for older adults, and then they delivered exercise coaching sessions on the designed multicomponent exercise for older inpatients at the ward. Clinicians were asked to answer the questions assessing the time required for older patients to learn the exercises and the reasons that the movements could be performed at home by older adults. (Supplementary File). Additionally, clinicians were required to report any adverse events during exercise sessions. Each clinician provided their written consent.

Step 2: determine behavior change strategies for promoting adherence to multicomponent exercise

The SCT [14] and TTM [13] provided the theoretical basis for the intervention program, and BCTs offered practical tools to determine behavior change strategies. We divided the physical activity promotion process into different stages, and stage-matched behavior change strategies were discussed. The discussion was informed by evidence from previous behavioral interventions and the practical experience of our multidisciplinary team. The selection of BCTs and the determination of specific behavior change strategies were guided by the APEASE criteria (Affordability, Practicability, Effectiveness and Cost-effectiveness, Acceptability, Safety, and Equity) [26], which we have predefined (Supplementary file).

Step 3: determine the mode of intervention delivery and develop an exercise-assistance WeChat applet

The working group discussed the intervention delivery modes and frequency, including face-to-face exercise sessions in community centers and home-based individual exercise supported by a WeChat applet. To accommodate older adults with varying levels of digital literacy, we determined an adapted blended intervention: participants proficient with smartphones used the WeChat applet, while those with difficulties in using smartphones were provided with printed exercise manuals for home-based exercise. The behavior change strategies delivered through each mode were also determined.

The development of the exercise-assistance WeChat applet was collaborated with the software college of Zhejiang University. Behavior change strategies were incorporated into the exercise-assistance applet to facilitate home-based exercise to help increase adherence to exercise.

Feasibility testing of the multicomponent PA intervention program in the real-world setting

A three-month, single-arm feasibility study was conducted from October 27, 2023, to February 4, 2024, in a local community in Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China. This was a feasibility study with an exploratory design aimed at assessing the implementation feasibility of the intervention program in a real-world setting. The feasibility was assessed according to Bowen’s feasibility framework, including eight dimensions: acceptability, demand, implementation, practicality, adaptation, integration, expansion, and limited efficacy.

Participants

A target of 20 participants was determined based on the capacity of the available community exercise area. A sample of residents from the Xiaoying community in Hangzhou, China, was recruited. The recruitment process was facilitated by the community staff. E-posters, telephone calls, and face-to-face introductions of the program were used to recruit residents aged ≥ 60 years. People who were interested in the program were invited to attend a face-to-face screening session. The inclusion criteria were older adults aged ≥ 60 years who were willing to participate in the program and did not meet the WHO-recommended amount of multicomponent physical activity. We conducted a survey (Supplementary file) to assess whether PA levels meet the WHO guideline recommendations [3] at screening. Older adults were asked whether they had participated in moderate-intensity aerobic activity (including walking), vigorous-intensity aerobic activity, strength training, balance training, and stretching exercises in the past week, as well as the number of days and duration for each activity. Older adults who did not engage in at least 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week (or an equivalent amount) and multicomponent exercise on at least three days per week were considered not to meet the WHO recommendations. The exclusion criteria were older adults who were unable to walk 100 m without assistance from others. Eligible participants were asked to complete baseline questionnaires and physical function testing. Each participant provided their written consent.

Intervention

The weekly exercise goal set for older adults was to complete the multicomponent exercise at least three times per week, with a minimum of 150 min of walking per week. Group exercise sessions were held twice a week at the Xiaoying community center, supervised by an exercise instructor. The exercise instructor received training sessions before the intervention, which emphasized familiarization with the multicomponent exercise and the WeChat applet, the core principles of behavior change theories, the application of BCTs, and implementation details for pre-, during-, and post-group exercise sessions. After training, exercise instructors were required to pass a performance assessment by the research team to ensure intervention fidelity before delivering the intervention. In the first two weeks, the group exercise sessions primarily focused on health education on exercise and exercise movement teaching. All older adults received an exercise manual and exercise equipment (two resistance bands and a pair of dumbbells) to encourage exercise at home at least once a week. From the third week onwards, older adults who had smartphones were introduced to using the WeChat applet to assist with home-based exercise. Older adults can follow exercise videos within the applet, record their completion status by clicking a ‘Complete’ button after finishing each exercise at home, and manually input their daily walking duration. Exercise instructors emphasized to older adults the importance of recording their exercise truthfully and promptly after each exercise to improve the accuracy of exercise records. Additionally, older adults were taught to use the WeChat applet for goal setting, exercise planning, and viewing the completion status of planned exercises and feedback provided by the exercise instructor. Exercise instructors monitored older adults’ exercise completion status at least once per week. As monitoring is an important strategy in our intervention, they should review exercise completion via the WeChat applet at the start of each week and provide tailored encouragement or suggestions. This weekly monitoring frequency is aligned with the exercise goals, ensuring consistency between goal-setting and monitoring. Older adults without smartphones can follow an exercise manual. The adherence management of older adults without smartphones mainly relied on face-to-face communication. The PA intervention combined group exercise twice a week at the community center and individual exercise at home once a week throughout the study period. Given the three-month intervention period, strategies aimed at maintaining long-term PA behavior were not implemented.

Measurements

At baseline, data was collected on demographic characteristics (age, sex, education level, marital status, living status, current smoking status, current drinking status, physical activity level, and proficiency with smartphone), health conditions (BMI, number of chronic diseases, number of medications, and history of falls in the last year), and physical function (grip strength and chair stand test). Physical activity level was measured by the Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly, a low-cost instrument with quick administration time that has been validated against physical activity data measured by portable accelerometers [27, 28]. A higher score indicates a greater level of physical activity [29].

The definition and feasibility-related outcomes of each area in Bowen’s feasibility framework were described in Table 1. Feasibility-related outcomes consisted of the participants’ perceptions of the PA intervention program, exercise adherence, and improvement of health outcomes.

SUS scale System Usability Scale

Likert scale questions were used to assess the participants’ perceptions of the PA intervention program. For example, satisfaction with the PA intervention program was assessed using a five-point Likert scale by asking if they found it ‘very satisfied’, ‘somewhat satisfied’, ‘neither’, ‘somewhat dissatisfied’, or ‘very dissatisfied’. The details of these survey questions were outlined in the supplementary file. The system usability scale was used to measure the user experience of the exercise-assistance WeChat applet. A product scoring of at least 70 could be considered ‘acceptable’ [30]

The evaluation of adherence to exercise included the attendance records of group exercise sessions, questionnaires, and self-reported exercise records from the WeChat applet. Participants who did not use the WeChat applet were asked if they had followed the whole exercise movements at home (‘totally followed’, ‘partly followed’, or ‘seldom followed’), the exercise frequency, the number of days they walked per week, and the duration of walking by questionnaires. All participants were asked whether they had adopted the WeChat applet or the exercise manual to aid with home-based exercise. The data on exercise adherence was collected at two months and three months.

The improvement of health outcomes was assessed by measuring changes in grip strength, chair stand test, physical activity scale for elderly score (PASE), self-reported general health improvement, as well as improvement in sleep and mood at three months. Grip strength and the chair stand test are important indicators of physical function, reflecting upper and lower limb muscle strength. Both are associated with frailty, fall risk, and the ability of daily living in older adults [31, 32]. The increase of 1 kg in grip strength was associated with a 3.2% reduction in all-cause mortality risk [33], and an absolute change of 0.5 to 4.7 s in the chair stand test has been reported as clinically meaningful [34]. The assessment of self-reported general health, sleep, and mood improvement was based on a four-point Likert scale as ‘greatly improved’, ‘slightly improved’, ‘not sure’, and ‘not improved’.

Statistical analysis

This study used a per-protocol analysis approach, including only participants who completed the follow-up assessments (n = 19). Descriptive statistics were reported as the mean with standard deviation (SD) for continuous data and count with percentage (%) for categorical data. A paired t-test was used to assess the differences between pre- and post-intervention in grip strength, chair stand test, and physical activity scale for elderly score. Stata software (Version 16) was used for statistical analysis.

Results

Safety and learnability of the designed multicomponent exercise movements for older adults

A total of 27 clinicians were recruited for the assessment of the multicomponent exercise movements for older adults. Almost 80% of the clinicians were from the geriatrics department, rehabilitation medicine department, or general internal medicine department (Table S2). A total of 37 hospitalized older adults (mean age 79.9 years (SD 10.4); 51.4% male) performed the set of multicomponent exercise movements under the guidance of clinicians. These older adults had poor physical function, with nearly half having low grip strength (45.9%), and the average time for the chair stand test was 13.6s (SD 6.7) (Table S3).

Overall, 78% of the clinicians reported that the older adults they supervised were able to follow the whole exercise movements after three or fewer sessions (Figure S1A). The most frequently reported reasons that the multicomponent exercise movements could be performed at home by older adults were “simple exercise movements” (25, 93%), “small required exercise space” (25, 93%) (Figure S1B). No clinicians reported any adverse events during exercise among hospitalized patients.

Behavior change strategies of the intervention program

The PA intervention program consisted of three stages: the getting started stage, the PA participation stage, and the maintaining PA stage. The key BCTs and behavior change strategies for each stage were displayed in Fig. 1. In the “getting started” stage, the goal was to reshape older adults’ knowledge and attitudes towards exercise to build self-efficacy and to provide a supportive environment for exercise. In the “PA participation” stage, the focus shifted to improving older adults’ ability to self-regulate exercise adherence by helping them develop skills in goal setting, action planning, and self-monitoring. The main strategies during the long-term maintenance phase include regular physical function assessments and sustained social support. Physical assessments help older adults recognize their progress and enhance self-efficacy, while continued support through WeChat, phone calls from exercise instructors, or participation in group exercise provides social connection to increase adherence.

Selection of BCTs

According to SCT, behavior change can be achieved by enhancing self-efficacy through (1) mastery experiences, (2) social modeling, and (3) persuasion; strengthening self-regulation through (1) self-monitoring, (2) goal setting, (3) feedback, and (4) social support; and promoting observational learning and environmental facilitation [14] (Fig. 1). Based on these theoretical pathways, we identified relevant behavior change techniques (BCTs) from the 96-item BCT Taxonomy (v1) [35]. Commonly used and effective BCTs in previous PA interventions, including problem solving, demonstration of the behavior, action planning, and instruction on how to perform the behavior, were also considered [36, 37]. The final BCTs were chosen based on consensus within the multidisciplinary team, following the predefined APEASE criteria. The selected behavior change techniques were shown in Fig. 1.

Determination of behavior change strategies

The behavior change strategies for each selected BCT were further determined through multidisciplinary discussion based on the definition of each BCT [35] and with the guidance of APEASE criteria. For example, goal setting is setting or agreeing on a goal defined in terms of the behavior to be achieved [35]. In our program, aligned with the WHO guidelines, we encourage older adults to set a goal of engaging in multicomponent exercise three times per week. The behavior change strategies corresponding to the selected BCT are presented in Fig. 1.

Intervention delivery modes

The intervention delivery modes of our PA program were blended, combining face-to-face group exercise sessions in community centers and applet-assisted or manual-assisted individual exercise at home. The theory-based WeChat applet designed to assist the intervention delivery was shown in Fig. 2. The content of the exercise manual included health education information on the benefits of exercise, information about multicomponent exercise, recommended exercise amount and intensity for older adults, as well as illustrated demonstrations of exercise movements through descriptive text and photographs (Figure S2).

For older adults, the applet’s primary functions included setting weekly goals and plans, learning and exercising with instruction videos, monitoring self-performance, and receiving feedback from exercise instructors. For exercise instructors, the applet’s main function was to monitor the exercise completion status of older adults and to provide feedback from them

The overview of the blended PA intervention program was displayed in Fig. 3. The frequency ratio of mHealth-based interventions and face-to-face interventions changed over the intervention period. During the “getting started” and “PA participation” stages, face-to-face group sessions in community centers were the primary delivery mode of intervention, supplemented by applet-assisted or manual-assisted individual exercise at home. In the “maintaining PA” stage, the primary delivery mode shifted entirely to applet-assisted or manual-assisted individual exercise, although older adults could still access community centers for group exercise.

Implementation feasibility of the intervention program according to bowen’s framework

Twenty-three participants were screened for eligibility for the feasibility test study, of whom three participants (13%) were excluded for reasons including age below 60 years (n = 1) or declining participation in group exercise sessions over three months (n = 2). Twenty participants were recruited in the feasibility study. The average age of all participants was 77.0 years (SD 8.7) (Table 2). Thirteen participants used the WeChat applet to assist with home-based exercise (WeChat applet group). Seven participants used an exercise manual to assist with home-based exercise (non-WeChat applet group).

Overall, our PA intervention program for promoting multicomponent PA among older adults in community- and home-based settings was feasible in all areas of focus in Bowen’s model (Fig. 4).

The results of the feasibility testing of our blend multicomponent physical activity intervention program according to Bowen’s Framework in eight dimensions

Acceptability

The participants had positive perceptions about the PA intervention program. All participants reported being ‘very satisfied’ (95%) and ‘somewhat satisfied’ (5%) with the program and felt it was ‘very appropriate’ (63%) or ‘somewhat appropriate’ (37%) for them. The exercise was deemed as ‘very easy’ (42%) or ‘somewhat easy’ (47%) by the participants, and 74% reported the exercise manual being ‘very clear’ (53%) or ‘somewhat clear’ (21%) to understand (Figure S3). The WeChat applet was considered acceptable by participants, as indicated by the average system usability scale score of 84.6 (SD 13.7).

Demand

During the three-month intervention period, older adults showed high adherence to multicomponent exercise. The adherence to walking for ≥ 150 mins per week after group sessions among all participants was 71% at one month, 82% at two months, and 80% at three months. Taking exercises at the community center and exercises at home together, the average weekly exercise adherence of participants in the WeChat applet group was high and stable over three months (87% at one month, 94% at two months, and 93% at three months). In the non-WeChat applet group, participants had high attendance for group exercise sessions (80% at one month, 70% at two months, and 79% at three months), while less than half (43%) ‘totally followed’ the entire exercise movements each time at home. All participants found the implementation of this PA promotion program for older adults to be ‘very necessary’ and 95% intended to continue exercising with the current PA intervention program (Figure S3).

Implementation

The implementation of the intervention proceeded as planned and the resources required to implement the intervention were low. The group exercise sessions were held as scheduled, except for two cancellations on holidays. Only one exercise instructor was needed for health education, exercise coaching during group exercise sessions, and monitoring home-based exercise via the WeChat applet. A20m² room (1m² per person) with a screen for playing exercise videos was used for group exercises that were freely provided by the community. The total cost for exercise equipment (two dumbbells and two elastic bands per person) and an exercise manual was 21 yuan per person.

Practicality

The implementation of the program was considered practicable. There were 79% of the participants reported that they could arrange at least three days a week to complete these exercise movements in their leisure time. The majority of participants (79%) reported that they were able to complete the entire exercise, and 90% expressed confidence in maintaining long-term exercise (Figure S3). After using the applet three to five times, 67% of participants reported they were able to operate the applet. Within a three-month intervention, only one participant (5%) dropped out. There were no adverse events during the intervention period.

Adaption

There were no major adaptations made to the PA intervention program during implementation. There were some suggestions on improving the exercise-assistance WeChat applet and the exercise manual received from participants. Participants suggested that the applet would be more helpful if the font size and buttons were enlarged. They also recommended shortening the instruction time in exercise videos uploaded on the WeChat applet when they got familiar with the exercise. In the non-WeChat applet group, older adults with low literacy levels found it challenging to comprehend the text in the exercise manual. It was suggested that the exercise manual should include more illustrative photos or provide posters.

Integration

The PA intervention program fit with the existing infrastructure at the community center. No system changes were required to integrate the PA intervention program into the community.

Expansion

The low cost of the PA intervention program made it feasible for expansion. The community center did not have to pay any costs except for providing a space for exercise. The Chinese government has set a goal to develop age-friendly communities in China [38]. Providing suitable exercise areas for older adults is the focus of building age-friendly communities. The increasing exercise area for older adults in the future would strongly support our PA intervention programs to be implemented in communities.

Limited efficacy

Statistically significant improvements were observed in grip strength (+ 1.4 kg, 95% CI 0.1 kg to 2.7 kg), chair stand test (−3.5 s, 95% CI −4.9 s to −2.2 s), and physical activity level (+ 33.6, 95% CI 23.5 to 43.6) after a three-month intervention (Table 3). The majority of participants reported that their general health, sleep quality, and mood had improved as a result of engaging in the program. All of the participants reported feeling either ‘greatly’ (26%) or ‘slightly improved’ (74%) in general health, and ‘greatly’ (74%) or ‘slightly improved’ (26%) in mood status, and 63% of participants reported improvement in sleep quality (Fig. 5).

Discussion

We developed a blended community- and home-based multicomponent physical activity intervention program for community-dwelling older Chinese adults. Feasibility testing showed that this PA intervention was viable for promoting multicomponent physical activity participation recommended by guidelines for older adults and was well integrated into the real-world settings under the construction of an age-friendly environment in China. Our study bridged the gap between the evidence-based benefits of multicomponent physical activity and practical implementation, establishing a model for promoting PA in daily settings that was tailored to China’s aging population.

Our study pioneered a multifaceted approach to enhance multicomponent physical activity engagement among community-dwelling older adults, combining both community and home settings. Supportive policies created a favorable environment for implementing and scaling this program. Nowadays in China, most adults and older adults have access to public exercise area within a 15-minute walk [39] and the number of social sports instructors has reached 3.2 million [9] to provide scientific instruction for the public. Our blended multicomponent PA intervention integrated existing social resources and required minimal additional inputs, making it feasible and scalable to promote exercise participation among older adults. In addition to exercise participation, significant improvements in physical function were observed after intervention with minimal resources, suggesting the promise of the PA intervention program for improving functional capacity among older adults in community and home settings.

Our feasibility study indicated that the blended physical activity intervention program designed based on behavior change theories and BCTs was feasible to promote multicomponent exercise engagement in older adults. The findings were consistent with previous app-based interventions [40–42] showing the potential of mHealth to promote exercise participation. The relatively high adherence in the WeChat applet group was observed, which may be partly due to the development of self-management skills facilitated by the WeChat applet [43]. In contrast, despite that home-based exercise adherence among the non-WeChat applet group was lower, this group showed high adherence to face-to-face group exercise sessions, which might be due to peer interaction, professional support, and social bonds among older adults [44–46].

Our study provided implications for practice, policy-making, and the future large-scale randomized controlled trial (RCT). The findings suggest that integrating basic public services to provide exercise facilities and exercise guidance for older adults at the community level is an acceptable and feasible approach to promoting physical activity. Meanwhile, both face-to-face and mHealth approaches have their unique advantages and cater to different audience groups; thus, they could both be applied to benefit a broader population when delivering intervention [47]. At the policy level, continuous support should be maintained for community-based exercise services, including the provision of venues, personnel training, and infrastructure [9, 48], so as to enhance physical activity and health levels among the older population and ultimately achieve healthy aging. Additionally, a future large-scale RCT that includes a more diverse demographic population is necessary to evaluate the long-term effectiveness, cost-effectiveness, and sustainability of the blended physical activity intervention program in promoting multicomponent physical activity levels and improving health outcomes.

There were several limitations that need to be acknowledged: (1) The preliminary safety assessment of the designed multicomponent exercise movement was conducted among hospitalized older adults, who may not fully represent community-dwelling older adults. However, if the exercise movements are safe, easy to learn, and can be performed at home for those with poorer function, it is likely reasonable for community-dwelling older adults to perform them. The feasibility study further confirmed its appropriateness and safety for community-dwelling older adults. (2) The majority of participants included in the feasibility study were female. A lower recruitment ratio of male participants was a common issue in health intervention studies [49], and future research needs to intentionally recruit male participants to improve generalizability in male populations. (3) Physical activity was assessed through self-reported measures, including the PASE questionnaire and home-based exercise records within the WeChat applet. PASE is a validated scale with significant correlation to accelerometer-based measurements [27, 28]. In addition to the self-reported app-based exercise records, attendance at group exercise sessions also contributed to tracking exercise participation. To enhance data accuracy, participants were encouraged to record their home-based exercise promptly and accurately; however, some reporting errors (e.g., forgetting to record) were unavoidable. (4) The pre-post-study design without a control group was less rigorous than randomized controlled trials (RCTs). However, previous data show that participation in strength training among older adults in China is low (7.1% in males and 3.1% in females) [6], which is consistent with our screening results. The improved multicomponent exercise participation observed after a 3-month intervention indicated a promising effective behavioral shift in this context. (5) The feasibility study was conducted in a single community, which may limit the generalizability. (6) The intervention period was three months and only the feasibility of the initial two stages of the PA program was assessed. These limitations would be addressed in a subsequent large-scale and long-term RCT to cover a broader demographic and to test the long-term effectiveness and feasibility of the PA intervention program.

Conclusion

Our theory-based PA intervention program combined face-to-face group exercise sessions in community centers with home-based exercises assisted by the WeChat applet or exercise manuals, was quite feasible to promote PA among older adults. Attitudes towards the physical activity intervention program were positive among older adults. High and stable exercise adherence was observed throughout the three-month intervention, with improvements observed in physical function. This successful initiative serves as a model for promoting multicomponent physical activity engagement among community-dwelling older adults in China. In future, a large-scale randomized controlled trial is needed to assess the long-term effectiveness, scalability across diverse communities and regions, and cost-effectiveness of the program.
