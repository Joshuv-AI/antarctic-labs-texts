A Bifunctional UiO‐66 Platform Enabled by Schiff‐Base Engineering for CO2 Conversion and Epoxide–Anhydride Copolymerization
Authors: Chen‐Yen Tsai, Shih‐Yun Chou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn this study, we report a series of bifunctional UiO‐66‐based metal–organic framework (MOF) catalysts, functionalized with Schiff‐base ligands and coordinated with divalent metal ions (M = Zn, Cu, Ni), for the sustainable transformation of epoxides via ring‐opening copolymerization (ROCOP) with anhydrides and coupling with CO2. The rational integration of Lewis acidic metal centers and tunable porosity within the UiO‐66 framework enables enhanced catalytic activity and selectivity. These catalysts achieve high conversions and excellent selectivity toward polyesters and cyclic carbonates under mild conditions (80°C for CO2 coupling; 110°C for ROCOP), with minimal by‐products. Importantly, the catalysts exhibit robust recyclability, maintaining structural integrity and performance over multiple cycles. This work demonstrates a versatile and durable MOF platform for green polymerization and CO2 valorization, aligning with principles of sustainable catalysis and circular carbon utilization.Keywords: bifunctional catalyst, bifunctional, catalysis, copolymer, materials science, polyester, polymerization, selectivity

Introduction

Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) are crystalline porous materials with well‐defined, atomically precise, and tunable structures, making them an ideal platform for designing catalytic sites and exploring structure–activity relationships [1, 2, 3, 4]. As heterogeneous catalysts, MOFs offer distinct advantages, such as adjustable porosity and active sites, enabling efficient catalysis and precise control over reaction pathways. Additionally, their heterogeneous nature enhances recyclability and allows for easy separation from reaction products [5]. The diverse structures and pore volumes of MOFs further support multi‐metal cascade catalysis, improving reaction efficiency and reusability. These exceptional properties position MOFs as a versatile and promising platform for achieving highly efficient and selective chemical transformations [6]. The range of transformations catalyzed by MOFs is extensive, including condensation reactions [7], photocatalytic CO2 reduction [8], carbon dioxide fixation reactions [9, 10, 11], and other reactions [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. However, there has been limited exploration of MOF catalysis in polymerization reactions [19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25], and even less research on ring‐opening copolymerization (ROCOP) reactions [26, 27]. Moreover, most investigations have been restricted to single‐metal MOFs, leaving the potential of multi‐metallic MOFs largely unexplored. Recently, the incorporation of secondary metal ions into MOF frameworks has emerged as an effective strategy for enhancing catalytic, electronic, and luminescent properties [28]. Two distinct metal ions can be incorporated within the same structure to form bimetallic MOFs, which have been reported to modulate physicochemical properties and, in some cases, improve performance in specific applications such as electrocatalysis [29]. Compared to their single‐metal counterparts, bimetallic MOFs exhibit broader applications, particularly in gas sensing, separation and storage, drug delivery, and catalysis [30]. Similar studies have demonstrated that bimetallic MOFs exhibit superior catalytic activity compared to monometallic MOFs when employed as heterogeneous catalysts [31, 32, 33].

The ROCOP of cyclic anhydrides and epoxides is gaining attention as a promising alternative due to its ability to proceed under mild conditions with low energy input while yielding high‐molecular‐weight polyesters. Moreover, the diverse selection of anhydrides and epoxides enables the synthesis of a wide range of polyesters with varying structures and properties. While MOFs have shown great promise in a variety of catalytic transformations, their application in polymerization and copolymerization reactions has only recently gained broader attention, and many aspects remain to be fully understood. Polymerization reactions, which are crucial for producing a wide range of materials, could benefit significantly from the tunable nature of MOFs. Their well‐defined structures and active sites may provide opportunities with high specificity, in terms of compatibility of functional groups, and selectivity, since pure isomerism can be achieved [25]. Therefore, the ability to incorporate different metals or ligands into the framework opens new avenues for designing bi‐metal MOFs capable of facilitating various polymerization pathways.

In this study, we introduce a series of bi‐metal‐UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐M(II) (M = Zn, Cu, Ni) frameworks as heterogeneous catalysts for the ring‐opening copolymerization (ROCOP) of epoxides with cyclic anhydrides, as well as the cycloaddition reaction of CO2. UiO‐66 was selected owing to its exceptional thermal and chemical stability, which is advantageous under the relatively harsh conditions typically required for these catalytic transformations. While the microporous nature of UiO‐66 may impose certain diffusion limitations compared to mesoporous MOFs, its structural rigidity and high connectivity (12‐connected Zr6 nodes) provide a robust platform for heterogeneous catalysis. Moreover, the presence of –NH2 functional groups facilitates post‐synthetic Schiff base modification and subsequent coordination with metal centers, allowing the introduction of catalytically active sites. Although the accessibility of Zr‐based Lewis acidic sites is more restricted than in some lower‐connected frameworks (e.g., PCN‐222 or PCN‐224), our design compensates through the introduction of well‐defined, accessible M(II) active centers. We hypothesize that the resulting UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐M(II) systems can function as efficient and recyclable green catalysts, enabling the selective production of cyclic carbonates and aliphatic polyesters under mild to moderate conditions.

Results and Discussion

In this work, the Zr‐based MOF, UiO‐66‐NH2, was selected as the carrier because it possesses the amine functional group on the ligands that can be connected to salicylaldehyde. UiO‐66‐NH2 was synthesized using a previously reported method [34, 35]. Subsequently, the amine groups underwent a Schiff‐base reaction with the aldehyde [36, 37], prepared with a ligand, resulting in the formation of a distinctive UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base MOF. This MOF was further coordinated with zinc, copper, and nickel ions, yielding the desired [UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐M(II)] (M = Zn, Cu, and Ni) catalyst, as illustrated in Scheme 1. The Powder X‐ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns (Figure S1) reveal that the crystallinity of the catalysts is well preserved following post modification, exhibiting no significant deviation from that of pristine UiO‐66‐NH2 [7]. The Fourier‐transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐M(II) (M = Zn, Cu, and Ni) closely resemble that of UiO‐66‐NH2, with characteristic peaks at 1592 and 1672 cm−1, corresponding to the stretching vibrations of C═C and C═O, respectively. A broad band centered around 3361 cm−1 is attributed to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of NH2 groups, which overlap with the O−H stretching vibration. Additionally, the C─N and Zr─O stretching modes of the MOF are observed at 1229 and 766 cm−1, respectively, within the weak fingerprint region of the spectra (Figure S2).

Synthetic procedure for UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐M(II) (M = Zn, Cu, and Ni).

The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images show that the synthesized particles exhibit a spherical morphology. Energy‐dispersive X‐ray (EDX) analysis reveals the presence of zirconium (Zr) and oxygen (O) in the porous Zr‐MOF catalyst, along with carbon (C) and nitrogen (N). Furthermore, the successful synthesis of [UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐M(II)] (M = Zn, Ni, Cu) is verified by the detection of Zn, Ni, and Cu species. These findings indicate the successful incorporation of metal species into the [UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐M(II)] materials (Figure 1a–f). High‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR‐TEM) images combined with elemental mapping were employed to examine the spatial distribution of Zn species within the UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base framework. As shown in Figure 1g, Zr, N, C, and Zn elements are homogeneously distributed throughout the MOF particles, with no obvious Zn‐rich domains or phase segregation observed.

As illustrated in Figure 2, the Zn, Cu, and Ni K‐edge X‐ray absorption near‐edge structure (XANES) spectra confirm the presence of Zn, Cu, and Ni in [UiO‐66@Schiff‐Base‐M(II)] (M = Zn, Ni, Cu) and further indicate that the metal sites adopt a +2‐oxidation state. X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were conducted to analyze the chemical composition and chemical status of the elements of the as‐prepared [UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐M(II)] (M = Zn, Ni, Cu). As shown in Figure 3a, the survey XPS spectra show the coexistence of C, N, O, Zr, and the corresponding metal elements. The deconvoluted high‐resolution XPS spectra are shown in Figure 3b–h, including the C 1s, O 1s, N 1s, Zr 3d, Zn 2p, Ni 2p, and Cu 2p regions. The fitted peak assignments are labeled in the spectra, providing clearer information on the oxidation states and bonding environments of the UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐M(II) catalysts. In particular, the deconvoluted metal‐region spectra show characteristic peaks at 1020.33 and 1043.64 eV for Zn 2p [38], 931.12 and 951.31 eV for Cu 2p [39], and 854.54 and 872.43 eV for Ni 2p [40], supporting the incorporation of the corresponding divalent metal species. These results align with previously reported metal signals for immobilized bi‐metal‐based complexes. The chemical compositions of Zn, Cu, and Ni were analyzed using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP‐OES, Table S1). The Zn, Cu, and Ni loading contents in the catalysts were estimated to be Nc = 0.42, 0.35, and 0.32, respectively. The N2 adsorption and desorption isotherms for UiO‐66‐NH2, UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base, and UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐M(II) (M = Zn, Cu, and Ni) were recorded at 77 K, as presented in Figure S3. The type I isotherm indicates the microporous nature of the pristine MOF. The Brunauer‐Emmett‐Teller (BET) surface area ranged from 912 to 438 m2/g, with a pore size distribution between 8 and 15 Å. Upon incorporating an additional metal, both the BET surface area and pore volume further decreased (Table S2). This reduction in surface area and pore size suggests that metal coordination occurs on the internal surfaces of the pores. The pore sizes of the functionalized MOFs ranged from 8.8 to 15.2 Å, which aligns with the microporous characteristics observed in the N2 adsorption isotherms. From the characterization results above, we have successfully synthesized a series of UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐M(II) (M = Zn, Cu, and Ni). In the next step, we will explore the catalytic performance of these bimetallic MOFs and compare them with monometallic MOFs to assess the differences.

Catalytic Cycloaddition of CO2 and Epoxides

To elucidate the factors influencing variations in catalytic activity, further characterization of the three catalysts was performed. The initial exploration of the CO2 coupling reaction was conducted using 50 mg of catalyst, 35 mmol of propylene oxide (PO), an initial CO2 pressure of 300 psi, a reaction time of 8 h, tetrabutylammonium bromide (TBAB) as a co‐catalyst, and a temperature of 80°C, as shown in Table 1. From the experimental results, it can be concluded that under identical conditions, UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐Zn(II) exhibits excellent catalytic activity, achieving a conversion rate of 81% and a turnover frequency(TOF) of up to 303 h−1. The product selectivity was evaluated by 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis (Figure S10). No obvious signals corresponding to byproducts were observed, indicating the selective formation of the corresponding cyclic carbonate products under the optimized reaction conditions. To further confirm the reproducibility of the CO2 coupling reaction, the catalytic experiment using UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐Zn(II) was independently repeated three times under identical reaction conditions, and the results are summarized in Table S6. The comparable conversion and TOF values obtained from the repeated experiments confirm the good reproducibility of the catalytic performance. The PXRD results indicate that these catalysts maintain the crystallinity of their lattice structure after the CO2 coupling reaction (Figure S4). To investigate the catalytic activity, we doubled the amount of PO monomers to 70 mmol, resulting in an increase in TOF to 479. Additionally, we extended the reaction time to 24 h, while the conversion rate remained at 80%. These results indicate that the reaction time and substrate loading influence the catalytic performance and support the selection of the optimized CO2 coupling conditions. These results demonstrate that UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐Zn(II) is an excellent catalyst. In the CO2 coupling reaction, the co‐catalyst plays a crucial role. In this study, we selected four different co‐catalysts for the reaction: TBAB, tetrabutylammonium chloride (TBAC), tetrabutylammonium iodide (TBAI), and 4‐dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP). As shown in Table 2, the reaction does not proceed without the addition of a co‐catalyst. Furthermore, DMAP does not react in this system. In contrast, TBAB exhibits the best catalytic performance. Increasing the TBAB loading from 0.1 to 1.0 mmol gradually improved the catalytic performance, with the conversion increasing from 51% to 80% and the TOF increasing from 127 to 200. We also tested the stability of the UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐Zn(II) catalyst. After 10 reaction cycles, the XPS and PXRD results indicated that the catalyst was not degraded (Figures S5 and S6a), and its catalytic performance remained stable at 75%–80% (Figure S6b). These findings demonstrate that UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐Zn(II) possesses good reusability and stability.

To further evaluate the substrate scope of the catalytic system, several epoxides with different structural features were investigated under the optimized reaction conditions. As summarized in Table S3, UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐Zn(II) exhibited good catalytic activity toward various terminal epoxides, affording the corresponding cyclic carbonates with satisfactory conversions and TOF values. In contrast, the relatively lower activity observed for cyclohexene oxide (CHO) may be attributed to the increased steric hindrance and reduced ring strain associated with the cyclic epoxide structure. (Figures S11–S14) Based on the cooperative catalytic behavior observed in this system and related literature reports (Scheme S4), a catalytic pathway for the cycloaddition of CO2 with epoxides is proposed. Since the present results do not allow us to unambiguously distinguish the exact contribution of each individual metal site, the proposed mechanism is illustrated using a generic Lewis acidic metal site to represent a possible substrate‐activation center in the UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐M(II) catalyst. The epoxide is first activated through coordination with the Lewis acidic metal site, which increases the polarization of the C–O bond and facilitates ring‐opening. TBAB then provides the nucleophilic Br− species, which attacks the less hindered carbon atom of the activated epoxide to form an alkoxide intermediate. This intermediate then undergoes CO2 insertion to generate a carbonate intermediate, followed by cyclization to produce the corresponding cyclic carbonate and regenerate the catalytic site. The cooperative effect between the Lewis acidic metal site and the bromide ion is believed to play an important role in promoting the cycloaddition reaction.

Ring‐Opening Copolymerization of Epoxides and Cyclic Anhydrides

The second reaction we explored was the ring‐opening copolymerization of CHO with phthalic anhydride (PA). First, we evaluated the catalytic activity of UiO‐66‐NH2, UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base, and UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐M(II) (M = Zn, Cu, Ni) under reaction conditions involving CHO (25 mmol), PA (25 mmol), and toluene (1.0 mL), with the addition of the chain transfer agent 9‐anthracenemethanol (9‐AnOH), at 110°C for 24 h (Table 3). The experimental results indicate that UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐Zn(II) exhibits the highest catalytic activity. To further determine the suitable catalyst loading for ROCOP, the amount of UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐Zn(II) was varied from 25 to 100 mg under otherwise identical reaction conditions, as shown in Table 3, entries 3, 6, and 7. Among the tested catalyst loadings, 50 mg gave the best catalytic performance, indicating that an appropriate catalyst amount is important for achieving efficient CHO/PA copolymerization. Additionally, the selectivity toward the ester linkage exceeds 99% (Figure S15), indicating highly selective alternating copolymerization and comparing favorably with reported MOF‐based ROCOP systems [26]. To further confirm the reproducibility of the catalytic performance, the CHO/PA copolymerization catalyzed by UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐Zn(II) was independently repeated three times under identical reaction conditions, and the results are summarized in Table S7. The comparable PA and CHO conversions, together with the consistently high ester ratio of >99:<1, confirm the reproducibility of the highly selective polyester formation. These findings demonstrate the great potential of UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐Zn(II) for copolymerization reactions. Next, we explored different chain transfer agents, including benzoic acid, DMAP, TBAC, TBAB, and TBAI. All the tested additives maintained excellent ester linkage selectivity. Among them, 9‐AnOH gave the best catalytic performance under the standard 24 h reaction conditions, with the conversion reaching up to 90%. Matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionization time‐of‐flight (MALDI‐TOF) analysis was performed on the sample from Table 4, entry 4. The spectrum exhibited a series of peaks at regular intervals, each separated by approximately 246 Da, which corresponds to the repeating unit of the CHO/PA copolymer. This finding further confirms that CHO/PA copolymerization follows an alternating pattern and that chain propagation is exclusively initiated by the DMAP group (Figure S7). We also attempted the copolymerization reactions five times. The experimental results indicate that UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐Zn(II) retains its crystalline structure and catalytic activity, demonstrating good stability during the copolymerization reaction (Figure S8). Notably, the elemental distribution remains essentially unchanged after catalytic reactions (Figure S9), indicating that the Zn(II) species are stably immobilized within the framework during catalysis. The absence of Zn aggregation or particle formation strongly suggests that Zn(II) does not exist as external Zn‐based nanoparticles but is highly dispersed within the MOF matrix. While elemental mapping does not provide atomic‐level site identification, the observed homogeneity at the nanocrystal scale, together with the preserved distribution after catalysis, supports the structural integrity and robustness of the Schiff‐base‐coordinated Zn(II) centers under reaction conditions.

To further evaluate the substrate generality of the catalytic system, the copolymerization of a diverse set of epoxides with anhydrides was systematically examined, including 1,2‐epoxyhexane (HO), styrene oxide (SO), cyclopentene oxide (CPO), 4‐vinyl‐1‐cyclohexene 1,2‐epoxide (VCHO), epichlorohydrin (ECH), and propylene oxide (PO). These epoxides vary in steric bulk, electronic properties, and functional group substitution, thereby providing a rigorous assessment of catalyst tolerance toward structurally distinct monomers. All reactions were conducted under identical conditions to those employed for phthalic anhydride (PA) or diglycolic anhydride (DGA), ensuring direct comparability of catalytic performance. As summarized in Table 5, UiO‐66–Schiff‐base–Zn(II) effectively promotes the copolymerization across a broad range of epoxide–anhydride combinations. Moderate to high monomer conversions were obtained depending on substrate structure, with consistently an ester–linkage selectivity of>99% as determined by 1H‐NMR analysis (Figures S16–S29). Notably, both linear and cyclic epoxides, as well as functionalized substrates such as VCHO and ECH, were well tolerated, indicating that the catalytic sites remain accessible and active despite variations in steric and electronic demand. The observed substrate compatibility can be attributed to the cooperative effect between the Lewis acidic Zn(II) centers and the spatial confinement provided by the UiO‐66 framework. The immobilized catalytic species likely benefit from a well‐defined coordination environment that stabilizes reactive intermediates while suppressing side reactions, thereby maintaining high chemoselectivity. Moreover, the preservation of catalytic activity across structurally diverse monomers highlights the robustness of the MOF architecture under polymerization conditions. Collectively, these results demonstrate that UiO‐66–Schiff‐base–Zn(II) serves as a versatile heterogeneous platform for epoxide–anhydride copolymerization, combining broad substrate scope, high selectivity, and structural stability. Such characteristics further support the potential of MOF‐confined molecular catalysts in sustainable polymer synthesis and CO2 valorization. Based on the cooperative catalytic behavior of the UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐M(II)/9‐AnOH system, a plausible mechanism for the ring‐opening copolymerization of CHO and PA is proposed (Scheme S5). Similarly, the Lewis acidic metal site shown in the proposed mechanism represents a possible substrate‐activation center in the catalyst, rather than assigning the catalytic role to one specific metal center. Initially, the epoxide oxygen atom of CHO coordinates with the Lewis acidic metal site, thereby facilitating epoxide activation toward ring‐opening. In the presence of 9‐AnOH as the initiator, the activated epoxide undergoes nucleophilic attack to generate a metal‐alkoxide intermediate. Subsequently, PA inserts into the metal‐alkoxide species to form a metal‐carboxylate intermediate, followed by successive alternating insertions of CHO and PA to afford the corresponding polyester product. The cooperative interaction between the Lewis acidic metal site and the alcohol initiator is considered essential for promoting efficient copolymerization and high ester selectivity. The comparison among UiO‐66‐NH2, UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base, and UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐M(II) indicates that Schiff‐base functionalization and subsequent metal incorporation affect the catalytic behavior of the UiO‐66‐based catalysts. However, the catalytic activity does not simply increase for all metalated samples. Among the UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐M(II) catalysts, the Zn(II)‐containing system consistently exhibited the highest catalytic activity in both CO2 cycloaddition and epoxide–anhydride copolymerization reactions. Interestingly, this trend does not directly correlate with the BET surface area or pore volume, since the Cu(II) and Ni(II) analogues possess comparable or even higher porosity but lower catalytic activity. Instead, the superior catalytic performance of the Zn(II) system is likely associated with its higher metal‐site density, as reflected by the larger Nc value obtained from ICP‐OES analysis, together with the suitable Lewis acidic character of Zn(II) for substrate activation. These results suggest that the catalytic performance is governed by a combination of metal identity, metal‐site density, and accessibility of the coordinated Lewis acidic sites, rather than by porosity alone.

The catalytic results indicate that the introduction of coordinated metal centers after Schiff‐base functionalization significantly enhances the catalytic activity for both CO2 cycloaddition and epoxide–anhydride copolymerization reactions. In particular, the UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐Zn(II) catalyst consistently exhibited the highest catalytic activity among the UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐M(II) systems, suggesting that both the intrinsic nature and density of the coordinated metal sites influence the catalytic performance. As summarized in Tables S4 and S5, various MOF‐based catalytic systems have been reported for CO2 cycloaddition with epoxides and epoxides–anhydride copolymerization under different reaction conditions. Although several reported catalysts exhibit excellent activity for CO2 cycloaddition under relatively mild conditions, most of these systems are mainly designed for a single catalytic transformation. In contrast, the present UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐M(II) system provides a tunable Schiff‐base coordination environment that enables bifunctional catalytic applicability toward both CO2 cycloaddition and epoxide–anhydride copolymerization within the same heterogeneous framework. Notably, UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐Zn(II) efficiently promotes CHO/PA copolymerization with an ester linkage selectivity higher than 99%, indicating highly selective alternating polyester formation. Together with its recyclability and retained crystallinity after repeated catalytic cycles, this bifunctional and highly selective catalytic behavior highlights the advantage of the Schiff‐base‐engineered UiO‐66 platform compared with many previously reported MOF‐based catalytic systems.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated the catalytic performance and stability of UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐Zn(II) in CO2 coupling and ring‐opening copolymerization reactions. The experimental results demonstrate that UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐Zn(II) exhibits excellent catalytic activity in the CO2 coupling reaction, achieving a high conversion rate of 81% and a TOF of up to 303 under optimized conditions. The catalyst maintains its crystallinity and stability after multiple reaction cycles, as confirmed by PXRD and XPS analyses. Furthermore, the presence of a co‐catalyst is essential for the CO2 coupling reaction, with TBAB showing the best catalytic performance. In the ring‐opening copolymerization of CHO with PA, UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐Zn(II) displayed the highest catalytic activity and exceptional selectivity toward the ester linkage (>99%). The catalyst also demonstrated good stability and reusability over multiple reaction cycles. MALDI‐TOF analysis confirmed the alternating copolymerization pattern, initiated exclusively by the DMAP group. Expanding the substrate scope, UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐Zn(II) effectively catalyzed the copolymerization of various epoxides with anhydrides while maintaining excellent selectivity. Overall, these findings highlight UiO‐66‐Schiff‐Base‐Zn(II) as a highly efficient and stable catalyst for CO2 utilization and copolymerization reactions, demonstrating its potential for sustainable polymer synthesis.
