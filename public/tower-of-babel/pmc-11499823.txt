How do parents experience video triage when seeking care for their acute ill children? A qualitative study in a Danish out-of-hours primary care setting
Authors: Mette Amalie Nebsbjerg, Katrine Bjørnshave Bomholt, Morten Bondo Christensen, Linda Huibers, Elisabeth Assing Hvidt
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe objective is to explore parents’ experiences with telephone contacts to out-of-hours primary care (OOH-PC) that include use of one-way video (video triage).DesignA qualitative interview study using Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis approach.SettingOOH-PC in the Central Denmark Region.ParticipantsA convenience sampling strategy was used to include parents of young children (aged 1–5 years), who received video triage in OOH-PC: nine women and one man aged 31–42 years. Data were collected from 26 April to 23 May 2023.ResultsThree main themes were developed, each containing two subthemes: Theme 1. Video triage accelerates diagnostic clarity and prompt action, enhancing feeling of safety: Parents experienced that video triage provided faster diagnostic clarification than telephone triage. The visual assessment in video triage reduced uncertainties of describing symptoms and increased the parents’ feeling of safety. Theme 2. Video triage changed the communicative and relational behaviour: Parents found video triage impersonal as it focused on the physical examination. The conversation before activation of video was found important, as it established a feeling of trust and reassurance. Theme 3. Experiences of presence and involvement in video triage: Parents found their active involvement in video triage challenging, as it required divided attention and shared responsibility.ConclusionsOur study indicates the importance of paying attention to the changes in communication and consultation processes afforded by the implementation of one-way video triage. One-way video triage is experienced as a valuable tool for examination, yet it is also perceived to impair the building of trust in the patient–physician relationship.

Background

In recent years, telephone triage in out-of-hours primary care (OOH-PC) has been expanded with the implementation of video use (video triage).14 Prior to this, triage professionals based their clinical decision-making solely on patient information provided verbally over the telephone. The possibility to visually assess patients through video is likely to enhance the experiences of safety and efficiency for both triage professionals and patients.

A few studies have investigated professionals’ experiences with video triage in an OOH-PC setting. Payne and Clarke3 conducted a focus group study among general practitioners (GPs) in British OOH-PC, which showed that the GPs used video triage to support their decision-making as to whether further assessment was needed or self-care advice was sufficiently safe. Gren et al5 performed a questionnaire study with Danish triage nurses who used video triage for contacts concerning children. They found that video triage (compared with telephone triage) improved the triage nurses’ assessment of the child and offered increased reassurance regarding the treatment plan.

Only limited knowledge exists regarding the experiences of patients and/or their next of kin with video triage in an OOH-PC setting.1 5 As many contacts to OOH-PC concern children,6 7 including calls involving video triage, knowledge is needed about parents’ experiences with video triage in OOH-PC. Children usually present with mild and self-limiting symptoms in OOH-PC, and these symptoms are rarely perceived as urgent from a medical point of view.7 8 The most important reason for parents to contact OOH-PC on behalf of their ill children is a need for reassurance.5 9 In the Danish questionnaire study by Gren et al,5 parents receiving video triage reported higher satisfaction and higher feeling of safety compared with parents receiving telephone triage. However, we need to identify which factors influence parents’ experiences with video triage in OOH-PC.

The aim of this study was to explore parents’ experiences with video triage in OOH-PC by investigating the three following research questions: What are parents’ experiences with telephone and video triage in OOH-PC? How do parents experience the communication processes in video triage? How do parents manage involvement tied to video triage?

Method

Design

We conducted a qualitative, interpretative interview study with the aim of exploring the subjective first-person experiences and meanings of participants, adhering to a subjective ontology and interpretive epistemology. The authors of this paper have different disciplinary backgrounds: four are medical doctors, including the first author, and one is a sociologist with expertise in qualitative research methods. The study was conducted in accordance with the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research checklist (online supplemental appendix 1).

Participants

The data corpus used in the analysis of this article contains data from 10 semistructured interviews with parents of young children (aged 1–5 years), who received a video triage after contacting OOH-PC in the Central Denmark Region due to a health problem in their child. Parents of young children were selected because video triage is most often used for these contacts.10 Participants included nine women and one man, aged 31–42 years (online supplemental appendix 2).

Setting

In Denmark, most healthcare services are publicly funded and free of charge for all residents. The healthcare system is centrally regulated. However, most services, including OOH-PC services, are provided by the local governments of the five Danish regions. OOH-PC services are open on weekdays from 16:00 to 8:00 and during weekends and holidays. GPs are obligated to cover shifts in their regional OOH-PC service. GPs in the OOH-PC service perform triage either without the use of video (telephone triage) or with the use of video (video triage). The GPs also decide on the triage outcome: self-care, referral to face-to-face consultation (clinic consultation or home visit) or referral to hospital.11 Based on their own experiences, the GPs decide themselves whether the reason for the encounter is suitable for video triage. If the GP finds a contact suitable for video triage, a video link is sent as a text message to the caller’s smartphone. When the patient accepts and activates the video link, the GP can see the patient, but the patient cannot see the GP. This one-way video solution was enforced due to the absence of cameras on the computers in the OOH-PC services and the urgency imposed by the COVID-19 pandemic. GPs are generally very satisfied with the one-way solution, and the adoption of this solution is still ongoing (personal communication).

Recruitment

Our initial ambition was to engage one of the coauthors (MBC), being a GP and performing video triage in the OOH-PC service himself, in recruiting participants for the study employing purposeful sampling.12 This, however, only resulted in recruitment of one participant. Due to pragmatic reasons (time constraints), we had to change our recruitment strategy into convenience sampling; seven participants were recruited through announcements posted by the first author (MAN) on social media, such as Facebook and LinkedIn. Two participants were recruited through a snowball sampling method, which was facilitated by referrals from study participants or acquaintances of the first author. The first author knew of the informants beforehand but did not have a close or personal relationship with them; one participant taught together with her several years ago, another resided in the same village as her, and the third was known through a mutual acquaintance. All participants had had at least one video triage in OOH-PC concerning their children, and 7 out of 10 had more than one (online supplemental file 2).

Patient and public involvement

None.

Data collection

The interviews were conducted from 26 April to 23 May 2023 by the first author, either through online (via Teams or Zoom) (n=6) or in-person attendance in the participant’s home (n=4).13 The participants could choose between an online or in-person interview. For the online interviews, both the interviewer and participant kept their cameras on. The duration of the online interviews and the in-person interviews was the same. On completion of the online interviews, the quality of the personal interaction was not judged to be of lower quality compared with the in-person interview situations. The interviews were conducted in Danish. The authors developed an interview guide, which was based on previous findings within this area3 5 and clinical knowledge from one of the authors, who works as a GP in OOH-PC. The interview guide consisted of closed-ended questions, such as ‘Were you the only adult present with your child during video triage?’, and open-ended questions, such as ‘What did you expect would happen when you contacted the OOH-PC service?’ and ‘How would you describe the communication between yourself and the GP when video triage was used?’

The interview guide was tested by the first author in a pilot interview before initiating the data collection. This was done to assess the clarity and comprehensibility of the questions and to refine the interview technique. Subsequently, interview questions were revised and refined to generate new and better formulations. Each interview began with a brief introduction to the study objectives and an explanation of how the interview was planned to proceed. Participants were instructed to focus on their most recent video triage in OOH-PC concerning their children aged 1–5 years. Experiences with video triage relating to older children or oneself and experiences with video consultations in daytime general practice served as a further reference for participants during the interview. The interviews lasted 40–60 min, were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim by the first author (n=2) and a professional transcriber (n=8). The interviews were not translated into English. Quotes cited in the article were translated by the first author. NVivo software, V.13, was used to assist in the analytic process of coding and thematising. Data collection and analysis were conducted sequentially. However, preliminary analytical observations, thoughts and ideas were noted down continuously during data generation and taken up in the data analysis phase.

Data analysis

The interviews were analysed according to Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis strategy14 15 considered to be an easy to use qualitative analytic method for identifying, analysing and reporting themes (patterns) within a qualitative data, independent of theoretical and epistemological position. The first author read all the interviews and generated codes. An open and inductive approach was used in the coding process. Based on the codes, a list of 32 initial themes was developed. These initial themes revolved around broader topics, such as participants’ experiences with encounters in the healthcare system, including OOH-PC services, and the emotions experienced by the parents when their child was ill or had been injured. Additionally, specific themes delved into experiences with video triage, including aspects such as the technical setup and communication with the GP. The initial themes were discussed by all authors and condensed into three main themes: ‘video triage accelerates diagnostic clarity and prompt action, enhancing feeling of safety’ (theme 1), ‘video triage changed the communicative and relational behaviour’ (theme 2) and ‘experiences of presence and involvement in video triage’ (theme 3). This condensation process was guided by the study objectives and existing literature in the field, as this article aimed to contribute with novel insights to the existing knowledge base. To increase rigour and trustworthiness, ongoing researcher reflexivity regarding overall presuppositions in relation to the topic at hand and to analytic interpretations was a focus point all through the analytic process.

Results

The results are presented through three main themes and six subthemes (online supplemental appendix 3).

Theme 1: Video triage accelerates diagnostic clarity and prompt action, enhancing feeling of safety

Video triage provides quicker clarification

In general, parents found it challenging to travel with their ill child to the OOH-PC service for a clinic consultation; their child was tired and feeling unwell, in addition to the common experience of long waiting times in crowded waiting rooms. As one parent explained:

Twenty minutes in the car with my son, who had a temperature of 41 °C and felt unwell… and also just because… you could say… it was an infection in a wound, it wasn’t… I mean, it wasn’t like he was about to die, you could say, right? So, it was a relatively minor thing. So, I thought it was fine that we could just handle it on video [video triage]. (P6, female, 36 years)

Most parents reported that many of their previous OOH-PC clinic consultations concerning their children ended with reassurance and advice. Similar outcomes were experienced after video triage, so video saved them a visit to the OOH-PC clinic. Based on these experiences, the parents believed that video triage led to faster diagnostic clarification and prompt action taken towards illness management, which enhanced their feeling of reassurance since they did not have to be worried for a long time.

Parents with a child needing further examination after video triage appreciated the direct referral to the hospital instead of first having an OOH-PC clinic consultation. They believed that this direct referral would not have been granted if the triage had been performed through telephone. So, based on their previous experiences with OOH-PC clinic consultations, most parents were convinced that video triage enhanced the effectiveness of the OOH-PC system by benefitting patients, GPs and ‘the system’ altogether.

Visual assessment increases parents’ feeling of safety

The parents found it challenging to verbally describe visual aspects of a symptom, such as describing a rash or their child’s breathing, in telephone triage. Therefore, the possibility of visual assessment provided by video triage added an extra dimension, which increased the parents’ feeling of safety compared with telephone triage. As one parent said:

I think it [video triage] adds that extra… dimension… and it also provides a sense of peace of mind, you know… because… well, I don’t think that just a phone contact [telephone triage]… well, I’m not sure I would have felt safe with that. (P4, male, 35 years)

In addition, most parents explained that they had called the OOH-PC service due to worries about their child’s well-being. They described how they expressed their worries, and how the GPs used the visual assessment to address these worries; the GPs described what they observed and explained whether or not it was perceived as a source of concern, which increased the parents’ feeling of safety. For example, a parent explained:

I found him [the GP] skilled and very explanatory about how… he says, ‘Well, chickenpox looks like this at this stage, and that’s completely okay’. Ehm… ‘That’s what you can expect when it’s been scratched and then inflammation occurs’. Ehm… So, I really thought it was good guidance and he also told me, ‘You should keep an eye on this’. And he prepared me, so to speak, by saying, ‘This is what you should keep an eye on yourself.’ (P6, female, 36 years)

In summary, drawing on their previous OOH-PC contacts, the parents found that video triage was beneficial as it contributed to faster clarification and higher feeling of safety, which ultimately helped reduce the duration of parental worry.

Theme 2: Video triage changes the communicative and relational behaviour

Video triage challenges relationship building

In general, video triage constituted a new experience for the parents. They noted that the communication behaviour in video triage affected the experiences of relational quality of the contact. When the video link was activated, a shift in the communication process took place; the GP’s initial focus on establishing rapport with the parent and gathering information about the child’s condition changed into a targeted execution of the physical examination. The parents described how the GP was focused on examining a single body part rather than being attentive to the whole body/person, and the GP guided parents to zoom in on the problem area itself rather than show the child in the whole figure. The GP made no attempts to establish a relation to the child (eg, by using social talk, such as saying hi or engaging in conversation) before, during or after the physical examination. The parents noticed that this behaviour was different from what they had experienced in clinic consultations. The parents also mentioned that the visual communication was a one-way model in video triage, as they could not see the GP. Consequently, one-way video triage was experienced to imply a more impersonal contact. Some parents mentioned that they missed the GPs’ body language, and one parent expressed that not being able to see the GP made her feel less cared for. In line with this point, a parent described the perceived impersonal contact with the OOH-PC service in the following way:

Well, when you call the OOH-PC service [….] you don't even know the first name of the person you talk to. Uh… so you've called a system. And when you can't even see the system, uh… it doesn't exactly encourage that feeling of having a relation. (P10, female, 32 years)

Since the use of video triage was perceived by the parents to be intended mainly for the physical examination, some of the parents found it awkward and beyond the purpose to continue using the camera in the postexamination phase. One parent explained how she intentionally pointed the camera downwards to the floor when the examination of her child had finished, since she was not the object of examination. It seemed wrong to continue filming her child, as the GP had already observed everything needed. Some parents mentioned that it would have been more sensible to stop the video connection after the examination and complete the conversation without the use of video.

Conversation prior to video triage is important

Due to the above communicative constraints afforded by video triage, the parents found it important to initiate the communication process without the use of video. They described that their feeling of trust was established in the telephone conversation prior to activation of video triage. In addition, the parents experienced that they communicated on more equal emotional terms without video. A parent experienced that the conversation with the GP prior to video triage provided a safe space and prepared her for turning on the video:

Yeah, I just feel like it’s more reassuring that you kind of, uh… get to talk and sort of say, now, uh… Because it could also be that, if it was at night, and you were breastfeeding without clothes on or… well, you know, it could be somewhat embarrassing, uh… on video [video triage]. (P3, female, 32 years)

The parents emphasised that the video solution had to be two-way for video triage to serve as a tool for building trust and rapport, as this would allow them to see the GP and thereby facilitate a more personal and symmetrical communication.

Theme 3: Experiences of presence and involvement in video triage

Divided attention

The parents described how the physical examination done in video triage demanded divided attention. While listening to the guidance from the GP, they also had to do the actual examination, to ensure good conditions for auditory and visual technologies and to make their sick child cooperate. As one parent explained:

And then they [GPs] say, ‘Well, I can offer a video consultation [video triage].’ So, I focus on turning on the video, and then I must make sure to film the right spot – and film properly – so the doctor can see what we're talking about. So, I can feel that my focus… also to fully hear what they're saying sometimes… it becomes a bit more challenging because I must also concentrate on the filming. (P6, female, 36 years)

In addition, some parents explained how they had to cooperate with their partner to get through the examination, and how they would have had difficulties in managing the examination if one parent had been the only adult at home.

Shared responsibility

Some parents explained that having to do the physical examination themselves with guidance from the GP was experienced as a source of uncertainty. They were uncertain whether they understood the guidance correctly, while being in a worried state. One parent described how feeling nervous and stressed affected the ability to understand and adhere to the GP’s guidance:

How stressed one is when making a call, right? I mean, it’s also crucial for how one can receive information. If you're very stressed, it’s not certain that you hear what’s being said, no matter how well it’s expressed. (P5, female, 40 years)

Some parents also highlighted the belief that conducting the physical examination is a core competence of medical professionals. Thus, relying on the parents to do the examination implied a new shared responsibility between parents and GPs. One parent explained that this new shared responsibility required the GPs to be prepared for (and comfortable with) handing over this responsibility to the parents, thus letting go of some of their control:

So, it’s about whether this [video triage] is more for the parents' sake, or if the GP is also okay with it in terms of professionalism. Whether they are believed to be able to encompass it; that someone else is doing what their hands would normally do. Yeah, so it was just a thought [that I had] afterwards about whether the GP felt like being professional. Not that I didn't think he [GP] was [professional]. But it’s an entirely different way of working as a doctor, I suppose. (P1, female, 34 years)

Discussion

Summary of results

In consideration of the challenging past visits to the OOH-PC clinic with their children and the difficulties of describing visual symptoms in telephone triage, the parents found video triage efficient and to increase their feeling of safety. The parents experienced that one-way video triage inhibited relationship building since it was used only for the physical examination. Therefore, the telephone conversation prior to activation of the video link was perceived as important because it established a feeling of trust. Video triage required parent involvement, and the parents experienced a high degree of shared responsibility, which was perceived as a source of uncertainty by some parents.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first interview study to explore parents’ experiences with video triage in an OOH-PC setting. The study was conducted by an interdisciplinary team of researchers, including GPs (MAN, KBB, MBC and LH), primarily quantitative researchers, and a social scientist and qualitative researcher investigating digital health and patient–physician communication (EAH). This nurtured dialogues about monodisciplinary and methodological assumptions (such as the role of ‘bias’ in qualitative research) and taken-for-granted understandings about relational dynamics within the physician–patient relationship. An additional strength was the data deriving from contacts to OOH-PC, which represented different reasons for encounter, severity levels and triage outcomes; this ensured diversity of experience among the participants.

Our study also has some limitations. By using convenience sampling, our opportunity to collect data building on maximal variation and homogeneity was reduced. Thus, we acknowledge that our study might capture a narrow segment of the population, which may not include diverse perspectives or experiences. Related to this limitation, and since all participants were living in or near a big city, the sample lacks representation from people living in rural areas, who might represent a population with lower educational levels and lower IT literacy.16 17 In line with other studies on unscheduled paediatric healthcare, most participants in our study were women,18 which could potentially have impacted the results, as mothers may have different perspectives than fathers. Moreover, we acknowledge that convenience sampling involves a high degree of bias. Thus, the convenience sampling strategy employed in this study has likely impacted the findings in the following ways: participants may have wanted to participate because of a particular attitude towards the OOH-PC services and/or technology use (eg, being positively biased towards technology use in healthcare delivery). Moreover, participants known to the first author beforehand may have wanted to participate based on social desirability. However, as noted, all participants were not close to the first author and/or at least a step removed (a friend’s friend).

Comparison to other studies

One of the main findings from this study is that parents perceived one-way video triage as a barrier to building rapport, as it was only used as a tool for physical examination (eg, examination of chickenpox, a red eye or a rash). In daytime general practice, the video technology used in video consultations affords two-way communication between the GP and the patient, which enacts a sense of mutual social presence, even though the consultations are technologically mediated.19 This is particularly pronounced among those who are already familiar with the GP from prior in-person visits. Other studies have shown that video consultations in daytime general practice are most often used for encounters with no need for a physical examination, such as administrative purposes and follow-up consultations.20 21 In addition, patients generally do not find two-way video consultations in daytime general practice suitable for physical examinations.19 This variation identified between daytime general practice and OOH-PC may stem from various sources, including differences in the GP–patient relationship and reasons for encounter (with the contacts to OOH-PC concerning acute illness). Additionally, video consultations are frequently compared with in-clinic consultations in daytime general practice, whereas video triage in OOH-PC is often evaluated in comparison to telephone triage.3 22 This variance may play a role in the observed differences in outcomes between the two settings, as video can be seen as a downgrade in daytime general practice and an upgrade in OOH-PC.

Parents found one-way video impersonal, which impaired the building of trust in the relationship between patient and GP. Other studies have indicated that GPs experience relational limitations during two-way video consultations in daytime general practice, such as lack of non-verbal communication cues and of showing emotions.21 23 24 In our study, the parents highlighted that the GPs did not try to establish a relation to their child in video triage, which contrasted their experiences with GPs in face-to-face clinic consultations. This could be explained by their need to build trust. An interview study found that a main reason for GPs to establish a relationship with a child is to gain sufficient trust to be able to conduct the physical examination.25 Since the physical examination during video triage in OOH-PC is conducted by the parents, the GPs may think that establishing a relationship to the child is less important and not worth the invested time.

The parents had to take an active role in examining their child during video triage. This finding is in line with a study conducted in daytime general practice, which also found a high degree of patient involvement during video consultations.26 Patient involvement changes the established roles and responsibilities in the doctor–patient relationship. This so-called invisible work has been found to be linked to the implementation and management of new digital health technologies.27 Our study emphasised the importance of acknowledging that invisible work, such as changed roles, responsibilities and required skills, is needed to make video triage a well-functioning technological system in OOH-PC. For example, we found that the GPs needed to guide the parents throughout the physical examination for the parents to feel capable and secure during the examination. Another example is that the parents found it important that the GPs articulated what they observed during the visual assessment. This has also been shown to be important during video consultations in daytime general practice.19 For the parents to feel safe, the GPs must create intimacy at distance by listening and being attentive.

Implications for clinicians and policymakers

Our study indicates the importance of paying attention to the changes in communication and consultation processes afforded by the implementation of video triage. One-way video triage is experienced as a valuable tool for examination, yet it is also perceived to impair the building of trust and relationship. To prevent such impairment, our study suggests that GPs should be advised to turn off the camera in the post-examination phase or to consider using two-way video triage. This study focuses on the parents’ perspective, but interview studies are also needed on the GPs’ perspectives on one-way video triage. Furthermore, future studies should investigate the changes in communication and behaviour afforded by one-way video triage, as well as investigate whether parents have encountered different experiences had the video triage been two-way. The aim of this study was to explore experiences with video triage for children. Future studies should explore experiences with video triage among diverse patient populations, as this might contribute to differences in results, for example, regarding the salient issue of parents’ feelings of safety afforded by video triage.

Conclusion

Video triage of children increased the parents’ feeling of safety and provided quicker clarification compared with telephone triage. Video triage also changed the communicative and relational behaviour and required parent involvement. Our study highlights the importance of attending to and further investigating the changes in communication and behaviour that are afforded by video triage to ensure a well-functioning technological system.
