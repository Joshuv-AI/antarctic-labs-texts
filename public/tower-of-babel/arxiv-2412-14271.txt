Dissipative Phase Transition in the Two-Photon Dicke Model
Aanal Jayesh Shah, Peter Kirton, Simone Felicetti, Hadiseh Alaeian

Abstract
We explore the dissipative phase transition of the two-photon Dicke model, a topic that has garnered significant attention recently. Our analysis reveals that while single-photon loss does not stabilize the intrinsic instability in the model, the inclusion of two-photon loss restores stability, leading to the emergence of superradiant states which coexist with the normal vacuum states. Using a second-order cumulant expansion for the photons, we derive an analytical description of the system in the thermodynamic limit which agrees well with the exact calculation results. Additionally, we present the Wigner function for the system, shedding light on the breaking of the Z 4 Z_{4} -symmetry inherent in the model. These findings offer valuable insights into stabilization mechanisms in open quantum systems and pave the way for exploring complex nonlinear dynamics in two-photon Dicke models.

The model –

We consider an ensemble of two-level quantum emitters coupled to a single-mode cavity, as depicted in Fig.
1
(a). The dynamics can be described via the following Hamiltonian (
\hbar=1
)

~H=\omega_{c}a^{\dagger}a+\frac{\omega_{a}}{2}J_{z}+\frac{\lambda}{N}J_{x}(a^{\dagger 2}+a^{2})\,,

(1)

where
\omega_{c}
and
\omega_{a}
are the resonance frequencies of the cavity and the atom, respectively.
\lambda
is the atom-light coupling strength, and
N
is the total number of spins, also called the system size here.
J_{x,y,z}
are collective spin operators that follow the usual commutation relation of
[J_{x},J_{y}]=2iJ_{z}
. We note that
1/N
used in this work allows us to make the equations of motion
N
-independent, which can then be solved in the TD limit (cf. the Supplemental Material (SM) for more information).

\omega_{a}=\omega_{c}=1
and
\kappa_{1}=0.4
, and the green vertical dashed lines in panels (a)-(d) indicate the onset of the phase transition predicted by the analytical model.

As can be inferred from Eq. (
1
), the Hamiltonian has a
Z_{4}
symmetry (cf. Fig.
1
(b)) which is distinct from the standard Dicke model with a
Z_{2}
symmetry, as pictorially depicted in Fig.
1
(c). In addition, the two-photon Dicke Hamiltonian is invariant under the generalized parity operator of
\Pi
=
e^{i\pi\left(a^{\dagger}a/2+J_{z}\right)}
. This operator has four eigenvalues
\pm 1
and
\pm i
, so the Hamiltonian can be divided into four block-diagonal subspaces.

Moreover,
[H,J^{2}]=0
, with
J^{2}=J_{x}^{2}+J_{y}^{2}+J_{z}^{2}=N(N+1)
, which means that the total spin is a conserved quantity of the dynamics. There are only
N+1
such states, since the spin ranges from -
N
to
N
, so the size of the spin Hilbert space scales linearly with the system size. As detailed in the SM, the Heisenberg equations of motion for the photon or spin operators are
N
-independent via the following scaling

~J_{x,y,z}\rightarrow\frac{J_{x,y,z}}{N}~,~a\rightarrow\frac{a}{\sqrt{N}}\,.

(2)

When the dynamics of the open quantum system are Markovian, the evolution of the density operator,
\rho
, is determined by the following Lindblad master equation

~\dot{\rho}=-i[H,\rho]+\sum_{n}\mathcal{D}[L_{n}]\rho\,,

(3)

where the dissipation related to the jump operator
L_{n}
is read as

~\mathcal{D}[L_{n}]\rho=L_{n}\rho L_{n}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}(L_{n}^{\dagger}L_{n}\rho+\rho L_{n}^{\dagger}L_{n})\,.

(4)

In this work, we include the loss mechanisms which correspond to photons leaking out of the cavity with

L_{1}=\sqrt{\kappa_{1}}a~,~L_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa_{2}}{N}}a^{2}\,,

(5)

where
\kappa_{1(2)}
corresponds to the one (two)-photon loss rate and again the factor of
N
is chosen to obtain
N
independent MF equations in the TD limit. Since these jump operators do not commute with the generalized parity,
\Pi
, this
Z_{4}
symmetry becomes a weak symmetry of the dynamics
[
73
]
. On the other hand, the total spin
J^{2}
is still a good quantum number since the jumps are limited to only operators which affect the photons, rendering it a strong symmetry of the dynamics.

Conclusion –
Here we presented exact solutions of a driven-dissipative two-photon Dicke model. Through detailed analyses, we showed that the inherent instability of the Hamiltonian cannot be stabilized via one-photon cavity loss, but adding even a small amount of the two-photon loss can stabilize the model for all system sizes. Further, through the second-order cumulant approximations, we derived an analytical model that describes the thermodynamic phases of the system as well as the finite-size system effects. Finally, calculations of the Wigner function highlighted the emergence of superradiant phases beyond the critical coupling and highlighted how the
Z_{4}
-symmetry of the model can be seen.
For future work, we propose more detailed studies to describe the nature of the phase transition and calculate its critical properties. In addition, one can study the robustness of different phases to spin decay and dephasing

[

Equation of motion for one-photon loss

For the two-photon Dicke model subject to the one-photon loss, the Heisenberg equations of motion for the cavity field and collective spin operators have the following form

\displaystyle\langle\dot{a^{2}}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left(\kappa_{1}+2i\omega_{c}\right)\langle a^{2}\rangle-\frac{4i\lambda}{N}\langle J_{x}n\rangle\,,

(1)

\displaystyle{\langle\dot{a^{\dagger^{2}}}\rangle}

\displaystyle=-\left(\kappa_{1}-2i\omega_{c}\right)\langle a^{\dagger^{2}}\rangle+\frac{4i\lambda}{N}\langle J_{x}n\rangle\,,

(2)

\displaystyle\langle\dot{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\kappa_{1}\langle n\rangle+\frac{2i\lambda}{N}\left(\langle J_{x}a^{2}\rangle-\langle J_{x}a^{\dagger^{2}}\rangle\right)\,,

(3)

\displaystyle\langle\dot{J_{x}}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\omega_{a}}{N}\langle J_{y}\rangle\,,

(4)

\displaystyle\langle\dot{J}_{y}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{2\omega_{a}}{N}\langle J_{x}\rangle-\frac{2\lambda}{N}\left(\langle J_{z}a^{2}\rangle+\langle J_{z}a^{\dagger^{2}}\rangle\right)\,,

(5)

\displaystyle\langle\dot{J}_{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{2\lambda}{N}\left(\langle J_{y}a^{2}\rangle+\langle{J_{y}}a^{\dagger^{2}}\rangle\right)\,,

(6)

where
n=a^{\dagger}a
is the photon number operator.

Throughout this work, we used the MF approximation to separate spin-photon correlations as

\langle\hat{O}_{\textrm{spin}}\hat{O}_{\textrm{photon}}\rangle=\langle\hat{O}_{\textrm{spin}}\rangle\langle\hat{O}_{\textrm{photon}}\rangle\,.

(7)

As mentioned in the main text, one can arrive at a set of
N
-independent equations through the scaling introduced in Eq. (
2
). The scaled and spin-photon separated equations in the TD limit read as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\braket{X}

\displaystyle=-\kappa_{1}\braket{X}-2i\omega_{c}\braket{Y}\,,

(8)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\braket{Y}

\displaystyle=-\kappa_{1}\braket{Y}-2i\omega_{c}\braket{X}-i8\lambda\braket{J_{x}}\braket{n}\,,

(9)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\braket{n}

\displaystyle=-\kappa_{1}\braket{n}+i2\lambda\braket{J_{x}}\braket{Y}\,,

(10)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\braket{J_{x}}

\displaystyle=-2\omega_{a}\braket{J_{y}}\,,

(11)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\braket{J_{y}}

\displaystyle=2\omega_{a}\braket{J_{x}}-2\lambda\braket{J_{z}}\braket{X}\,,

(12)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\braket{J_{z}}

\displaystyle=2\lambda\braket{J_{y}}\braket{X}\,,

(13)

where
X=a^{2}+a^{\dagger^{2}}
and
Y=a^{2}-a^{\dagger^{2}}
.

By solving for a steady state (ss), two different phases can be identified, where the phase transition occurs at the critical coupling
\lambda_{c}
as

\lambda_{c}=\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\kappa^{2}+4\omega_{c}^{2}}\,.

(14)

1) The normal phase for
\lambda\leq\lambda_{c}
, where all spins are in the ground state and there is no macroscopic occupation of the cavity.

\langle X\rangle_{ss}=\langle Y\rangle_{ss}=\langle n\rangle_{ss}=\langle J_{x}\rangle_{ss}=\langle J_{y}\rangle_{ss}=0
and
\langle J_{z}\rangle_{ss}=-1
.

2) The superradiant phase for
\lambda\geq\lambda_{c}
, where the spins are polarized, and there is a non-zero cavity occupation.

\langle X\rangle_{ss}=\langle Y\rangle_{ss}=\langle n\rangle_{ss}=\langle J_{x}\rangle_{ss}=\langle J_{z}\rangle_{ss}\neq 0
and
\langle J_{y}\rangle_{ss}=0
.

Since there is no spin-related jump operators, the total spin is a conserved quantity, i.e. the strong symmetry, as

\langle J_{x}\rangle_{ss}^{2}+\langle J_{y}\rangle_{ss}^{2}+\langle J_{z}\rangle_{ss}^{2}=1

(15)

After the PT,

, therefore

\langle J_{x}\rangle_{ss}^{2}+\langle J_{z}\rangle_{ss}^{2}=1\,.

(16)

The explicit solutions for the SP read as follows

\displaystyle\langle J_{x}\rangle_{ss}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{\lambda_{c}}{\lambda}\,,

(17)

\displaystyle\langle J_{y}\rangle_{ss}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(18)

\displaystyle\langle J_{z}\rangle_{ss}

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{1-\frac{\lambda_{c}^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}}\,,

(19)

\displaystyle\langle n\rangle_{ss}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\omega_{a}}{\omega_{c}}\frac{\langle J_{x}\rangle_{ss}^{2}}{\langle J_{z}\rangle_{ss}}\,,

(20)

\displaystyle\langle X\rangle_{ss}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{a}}{\lambda}\frac{\langle J_{x}\rangle_{ss}}{\langle J_{z}\rangle_{ss}}\,,

(21)

\displaystyle\langle Y\rangle_{ss}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\kappa_{1}}{2i\omega_{c}}\langle X\rangle_{ss}\,,

I.1
Linear Stability Analysis

To check the stability of the steady-state solutions obtained in the previous section, we perform a linear stability analysis.

Let
\eta(t)=x(t)-x^{*}
be a small perturbation around
x^{*}
, a fixed point of the EoM. Inserting this ansatz back into the scaled EoM and linearizing them around the fixed point by retaining only linear terms in perturbation, i.e. O(
\eta
) we arrive at

\dot{\eta}(t)=\mathcal{B}(x^{*})\eta\,.

(23)

where
\mathcal{B}(x^{*})
, aka the Bogoliubov matrix, is formally the small perturbation matrix around the fixed point
x^{*}
. If the spectrum of this matrix is always within the left-half of the complex plane, then the solution is stable, and otherwise it is unstable.

For the normal state, the Bogoliubov matrix
\mathcal{B}
is read as

\mathcal{B}=\begin{bmatrix}-\kappa_{1}&-2i\omega_{c}&0&0&0&0\\
-2i\omega_{c}&-\kappa_{1}&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&-\kappa_{1}&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&-2\omega_{a}&0\\
2\lambda&0&0&2\omega_{a}&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&0&0\\
\end{bmatrix}

(24)

with the following eigenvalues

-\kappa_{1}\pm 2i\omega_{c},-\kappa_{1},\pm 2i\omega_{a},0\,.

(25)

which implies that the normal state is always stable.

For the superradiant state the stability matrix reads as

\mathcal{B}=\begin{bmatrix}-\kappa_{1}&-2i\omega_{c}&0&0&0&0\\
-2i\omega_{c}&-\kappa_{1}&-8i\lambda\langle J_{x}\rangle_{ss}&-8i\lambda\langle n\rangle_{ss}&0&0\\
0&2i\lambda\langle J_{x}\rangle_{ss}&-\kappa_{1}&2i\lambda\langle Y\rangle_{ss}&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&-2\omega_{a}&0\\
-2\lambda\langle J_{z}\rangle_{ss}&0&0&2\omega_{a}&0&-2\lambda\langle X\rangle_{ss}\\
2\lambda\langle J_{y}\rangle_{ss}&0&0&0&2\lambda\langle X\rangle_{ss}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}

(26)

The eigenvalues for the superradiant phase can be numerically determined for different steady-state values. As shown in Fig.
5
there are always eigenvalues with positive real parts, which signal the instability, as highlighted by the gray region in panel (b).

Equations of motion for one- and two-photon losses

The inclusion of the two-photon loss modifies the equations of motion as follows

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle a\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\kappa_{1}}{2}+i\omega_{c}\right)\langle a\rangle-2i\frac{\lambda}{N}\langle J_{x}\rangle\langle a^{\dagger}\rangle-\frac{\kappa_{2}}{N}\langle a^{\dagger}a^{2}\rangle\,,

III
Quantum trajectory

As mentioned in the main text, for larger
N
, obtaining the full solution by ED is not feasible, so we used the stochastic QT approach instead. To randomize enough the state vector at
t=0
to capture subtle features especially close to the phase transition point, we randomly draw a state from a density matrix at infinite temperature, i.e., all possible states have equal probability of being selected.

If
\ket{\psi_{i}}
is the
i^{\textrm{th}}
trajectory, one can determine the density matrix
\hat{\rho}
as

\hat{\rho}=\sum_{i}^{N_{T}}\frac{\ket{\psi_{i}}\bra{\psi_{i}}}{N_{T}}\,,

(53)

where
N_{T}
is the number of trajectories.

To calculate photonic properties such as the probability of Fock state occupation as well as the Wigner function, we calculated the reduced photonic density matrix as
\hat{\rho}_{\textrm{ph}}=\textrm{Tr}_{s}(\hat{\rho})
, where
\textrm{Tr}_{s}
represents the partial trace operator over all spin degrees of freedom.

The convergence of the results was confirmed by not having any change after increasing the simulation time and the number of trajectories
N_{T}
. Figure
7
shows the variation of the average photon number vs.
\lambda
for
N=15
when the number of trajectories increased from 500 to 3000, denoted by the light blue and dark blue curves, respectively. The analytical results are shown in black to highlight the good agreement between QT and the analytic results as the number of trajectories increases. We attribute the remained discrepancy between the dark blue and black lines close to the phase transition to computational limitations.
