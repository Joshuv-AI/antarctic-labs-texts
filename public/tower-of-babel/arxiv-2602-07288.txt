On the Necessity of Two-Stage Estimation for Learning Dynamical Systems under Both Noise and Node-Wise Attacks
Jihun Kim, Javad Lavaei

Abstract
The least-squares estimator has achieved considerable success in learning linear dynamical systems from a single trajectory of length T T . While it attains an optimal error of 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / T ) \mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T}) under independent zero-mean noise, it lacks robustness and is particularly susceptible to adversarial corruption. In this paper, we consider the identification of a networked system in which every node is subject to both noise and adversarial attacks. We assume that every node is independently corrupted with probability smaller than 0.5 0.5 at each time, placing the overall system under almost-persistent local attack. We first show that no convex one-stage estimator can achieve a consistent estimate as T T grows under both noise and attacks. This motivates the development of a two-stage estimation method applied across nodes. In Stage I, we leverage the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm estimator and derive an estimation error bound proportional to the noise level σ w \sigma_{w} . This bound is subsequently used to detect and filter out attacks, producing a clean dataset for each node, to which we apply the least-squares estimator in Stage II. The resulting estimation error is on the order 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / T ) \mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T}) plus the product of σ w \sigma_{w} and the number of misclassifications. In the event of perfect separability between attack and non-attack data—which occurs when injected attacks are sufficiently large relative to the noise scale—our two-stage estimator is consistent for the true system.

Introduction

Control systems are generally subject to exogenous factors; in particular, noise and attacks.
Noise
is generally a factor that does not directly corrupt the system, yet is unavoidable: it persists within the system states as a white stochastic disturbance despite the nominal stability of the underlying process.
Its sources include fundamental environmental physics
(
Bentley 2005
)
, unmodeled internal dynamics due to device imperfections
(
Van Der Ziel and Chenette 1978
)
, and digital processing or quantization errors
(
Widrow and Kollár 2008
)
. For this reason, it is reasonable to model process noise as an independent, zero-mean (or even symmetric) disturbance that does not intentionally corrupt the system. On the other hand,
attacks
are intentional manipulations that can be severe enough to mislead states of the system: they may be adaptively designed based on the full information history available at each time and even injected concurrently across nodes
(
Pasqualetti et al. 2013
)
. Attacks need not be modeled as zero-mean, since attacks are often chosen by an adversary to bias the system; we therefore refer to them as adversarial attacks.

While systems are subject to both noise and attacks, detecting attacks is of primary interest, since they are the factors that can adversely affect the system. Indeed, the system remains recoverable only when the occurrence of attacks is at most 50%; if the system is consistently contaminated in the same direction with at least 50%, it becomes impossible to distinguish between the “good” and “bad” parts of the data. This concept has been formalized by the result that the number of correctable errors is at most half of all states
(
Fawzi et al. 2014
)
.
Building on this principle, attack detection schemes have been widely developed, especially for cyber-physical systems where attacks occur infrequently but can be maliciously large
(
Zhang et al. 2015
)
. Notably,
Pajic et al. 2017
considered systems affected by both attacks and bounded noise, aiming to detect attacks in the presence of noise.
However, the analyses in aforementioned works assume that the system dynamics is known a priori and focus on reconstructing the original states from noisy or attacked measurements. In practice, having access to the system dynamics is overly optimistic and often unrealistic.

As modern systems grow increasingly complex, entirely or partially unknown system dynamics has motivated the development of
system identification
methods. Given only a single trajectory of length
T
, the primary challenge to estimate the underlying system dynamics arise from the temporal correlation among states.
One of the earliest approaches was the classical least-squares method (see Chapter II,
Koopmans 1950
), which achieves an estimation error of
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})
as
T\to\infty
. This idea was revisited in recent works
(
Simchowitz et al. 2018
;
Sarkar and Rakhlin 2019
)
, which established non-asymptotic guarantees, showing that an error of
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})
is indeed optimal and attained by the least-squares once
T
exceeds a finite threshold. However, these approaches are limited to the case of zero-mean independent factors; in other words, they are applicable only in the presence of noise.
To address this limitation, non-smooth convex estimators have been studied from a non-asymptotic perspective, such as the
\ell_{2}
-norm or the
\ell_{1}
-norm estimator
(
Yalcin et al. 2024
;
Kim and Lavaei 2025
)
. These estimators, however, now primarily account for adversarial attacks but do not incorporate stochastic noise. To the best of our knowledge, system identification using a single trajectory under the simultaneous presence of noise and attacks has received limited attention. One notable exception is
Simchowitz et al. 2019
, which introduces known control inputs with zero mean at each time
t
and models the attack as being designed based on the information history up to time
t-1
; however, the attack cannot use the information of the current state, as opposed to a standard definition of adversarial attacks.

In this paper, we study a linear dynamical system subject to both noise and attacks. The noise is persistent, independent, and zero-mean, whereas the attacks are modeled by an adversary who leverages the full information history, including the current state at each time
t
.
In particular, we consider
networked systems
consisting of a node associated with each scalar state
(
Olfati-Saber et al. 2007
)
. The adversary targets the subsystem corresponding to each node independently with a probability smaller than 0.5, which results in the overall system being subject to
almost-persistent local attack
.
Our goal is to detect such attacks and subsequently obtain an accurate system estimate by designing and analyzing a two-stage estimator.
Our contribution is three-fold:

1.

We first show that any convex one-stage estimators (
e.g.
, least-squares or
\ell_{1}
-norm estimators) fail to identify the true system in the presence of both persistent noise and (possibly infrequent) adversarial attacks. In particular, if attack times at each node occur with positive probability and noise is symmetric,
an adversary can always construct attacks that prevent convergence to the true system, regardless of how long the observed trajectory length grows.

2.

Motivated by the fact that one-stage estimators fail in the presence of both noise and attacks, we propose a two-stage estimator that first applies the
\ell_{1}
-norm estimator to filter potential attacks out and subsequently leverages the least-squares estimator for system identification. Since attacks occur in a node-wise manner, the procedure is applied independently across nodes, enabling node-level attack detection.

3.

We show that the estimation bound obtained from the two-stage estimator depends on the noise level and the number of misclassifications. In other words, the estimation performance improves when the separability between clean and corrupted data instances is high,
i.e.
, attacks are sufficiently large compared to the noise scale to be reliably distinguished from clean data. Indeed, perfect separability provides a consistent estimate of the true system.

Problem Formulation

Consider a discrete linear dynamical system given as:

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=\bar{A}x_{t}+w_{t}+v_{t},\quad t=0,\dots,T-1,

3
Analysis of One-Stage Estimation

Given a single trajectory
(x_{0},\dots,x_{T})
, we consider a general system identification method for the linear model (
1
) to solve the optimization problem, where a convex loss function is applied to the prediction residual
x_{t+1}-Ax_{t}
. The examples include the representative least-squares estimator
(
Simchowitz et al. 2018
;
Sarkar and Rakhlin 2019
)
which is applicable for non-asymptotic analysis in the presence of independent zero-mean noise, and robust alternatives such as the
\ell_{2}
-norm or the
\ell_{1}
-norm estimator
(
Yalcin et al. 2024
;
Kim and Lavaei 2025
)
, which are designed for settings with infrequent adversarial attacks.

\displaystyle\min_{A}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|x_{t+1}-Ax_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\min_{A}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|x_{t+1}-Ax_{t}\|_{1}.

(3)

Moreover, sparse identification approaches include to constrain the norm of the matrix governing the system
(
Brunton et al. 2016
)
.
Given that
\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2}
,
\|\cdot\|_{1}
, and many others are convex, coercive, and even, we consider a general standard convex optimization framework used in system identification.

Definition 4

(Standard Convex Optimization for System Identification)
.

Consider a function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

1.

\phi
is convex; i.e.,
\phi((1-\lambda)x+\lambda y)\leq(1-\lambda)\phi(x)+\lambda\phi(y)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
.

2.

\phi
is coercive; i.e.,
\lim_{\|x\|_{2}\to\infty}\phi(x)=\infty
.

3.

\phi
is even; i.e.,
\phi(x)=\phi(-x)
.

Then, a standard framework for system identification is to minimize the aggregate loss of
\phi(x_{t+1}-Ax_{t})
subject to a constraint on
A
, or equivalently,

\min_{A}~~\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\phi(x_{t+1}-Ax_{t})\quad\text{s.t.}~~~\|A\|\leq R,

(4)

where
R\in(0,\infty]
and
\|\cdot\|
denotes an arbitrary matrix norm.

The optimization problem (
4
) is natural in the sense that any estimator should try to minimize the distance between
x_{t+1}
and
Ax_{t}
, with the belief that
Ax_{t}
is the best representation of
x_{t+1}
in average. Also,
\phi
is often selected to be convex for tractability; to be coercive to penalize a large difference between
x_{t+1}
and
Ax_{t}
heavily; to be even to ensure fairness between the positive and negative differences.
The estimators given in (
3
) fall within this class of optimization framework for a choice of
R=\infty
. We now demonstrate that this general framework fails to recover the true system under the combined effect of adversarial attacks and stochastic symmetric noise; in particular, the resulting estimator does not converge to the true system dynamics. Before presenting the main theorem, the following lemma provides the intuition for how an adversary can construct an attack to mislead the estimator.

Lemma 5
.

Given a function
\phi
that falls under Definition
4
,
define a constant

\displaystyle m_{\phi}:=\liminf_{\|x\|_{2}\to\infty}\frac{\phi(x)-\phi(0)}{\|x\|_{2}}.

Assume that for any symmetric sub-Gaussian variable
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbb{E}_{w}[|\phi(w)|)]<\infty
holds. Fix such a
w
and define
\phi_{w}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
to be
\phi_{w}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{w}[\phi(x+w)]
. Then, three properties hold:

1.

\phi_{w}(x)
is convex.

2.

m_{\phi}=\liminf_{\|x\|_{2}\to\infty}\frac{\phi(x)-\phi(0)}{\|x\|_{2}}=\liminf_{\|x\|_{2}\to\infty}\frac{\phi_{w}(x)-\phi_{w}(0)}{\|x\|_{2}}.

3.

m_{\phi}>0
.

The proof of the lemma relies on the convexity and coercivity of
\phi
. Proof details are provided in Appendix
A
.
The attacker strategically leverages the finite constant
m_{\phi}
, established for each
\phi
, to design an attack that biases the estimator defined in terms of
\phi
. We now present the main theorem on the failure of one-stage estimators. For analytical simplicity, we establish the result assuming bounded noise; however, the analysis extends to sub-Gaussian noise as detailed in Appendix
B.2
.

Theorem 6
.

Suppose that
w_{t}
follows a bounded symmetric and absolutely continuous distribution. Let
A_{T}
be a minimizer to (
4
).
Given an attack probability
p>0
, there exists a sequence of attacks
\{v_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, satisfying Assumptions
2
and
3
, such that
A_{T}
does not converge to
\bar{A}
almost surely.

Proof Sketch.

To show the existence of system dynamics
\bar{A}
that the estimator fails to recover, we study the case where
\bar{A}=0
.
Given the dynamics (
1
), the estimator (
4
) can be rewritten as

\min_{A}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\phi(-A(w_{t-1}+v_{t-1})+w_{t}+v_{t})\quad\text{s.t.}~~~\|A\|\leq R,

(5)

where we define
g_{t}(A):=\phi(-A(w_{t-1}+v_{t-1})+w_{t}+v_{t})
with
x_{0}=w_{-1}+v_{-1}
.
Since a convex
\phi
is differentiable almost everywhere and
w_{t}
is absolutely continuous and independent of
v_{t}
, the subdifferential
\partial g_{t}(A)
evaluated at
\bar{A}=0
is almost surely the unique matrix

\displaystyle\nabla g_{t}(0)=-\nabla\phi(w_{t}+v_{t})\cdot(w_{t-1}+v_{t-1})^{T}.

(6)

Accordingly, we design an attack that produces
\mathbb{E}[\partial g_{t}(0)]=\mathbb{E}[\nabla g_{t}(0)]\neq 0
.
We restrict the injection of the attack to time instances where a full attack is allowed across all nodes (this happens with probability
p^{n}>0
). For those times, we define the attack signal
v_{t}
as follows:
v_{t}=\bar{c}x_{t}/\|x_{t}\|_{2}
when
t
is odd and
v_{t}=0
when
t
is even, where
\bar{c}>0
is a finite constant determined by
\phi
to satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{\phi_{w_{t}}(\bar{c}x/\|x\|_{2})-\phi_{w_{t}}(0)}{\bar{c}}\geq\frac{m_{\phi}}{2}

(7)

for all
x\neq 0
, which is guaranteed to exist when chosen to be sufficiently large, due to Lemma
5
.
Under this attack design, we derive that
\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}(\nabla g_{t}(0))]<0
, by leveraging Lemma
3
, the convexity and evenness of
\phi
, and the symmetry of
w_{t}
. Our attack design has 1-dependence (dependence restricted to consecutive samples), and thus it is sufficient to invoke the Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN) from
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}g_{t}(0)
to
\mathbb{E}[\nabla g_{t}(0)]
.

Now, let
A_{T}
be a minimizer to (
5
). Suppose for the contradiction that
A_{T}\to 0
as
T\to\infty
. Since the sequence
\{A_{T}\}_{T\geq 0}
is bounded, one can construct the compact set
\mathcal{A}
that includes
\bar{A}
and
\{A_{T}\}_{T\geq 0}
. Then, due to the SLLN and the uniform convergence on compact sets of convex functions (Theorem 10.8,
Rockafellar 1970
), we can establish

\sup_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}g_{t}(A)-\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(A)]\right|\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{as}\quad T\to\infty.

It follows that
A_{T}\to 0
implies that
0
is a minimizer to
\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(A)]
subject to
A\in\mathcal{A}
. Since Slater’s condition holds, Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions yield
0\in\partial\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(0)]
. Since
g_{t}
is convex and finite, we have
\partial\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(0)]=\mathbb{E}[\partial g_{t}(0)]=\mathbb{E}[\nabla g_{t}(0)]

(
Rockafellar 1968
)
.
However, our attack design incurs
\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}(\nabla g_{t}(0))]<0
, which yields a contradiction, implying that
A_{T}
does not converge to
\bar{A}=0
.

Full proof details are provided in Appendix
B.1
. Note that the assumption of bounded noise ensures that the expectation
\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(x)]
exists. Appendix
B.2
uses alternative analysis to handle cases where
\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(x)]
is not defined.
\blacksquare

The limitations of one-stage estimators highlighted in Theorem
6
necessitates a two-stage estimation framework. The following sections detail this method and provide a rigorous analysis of the estimation error.

4
Design of Two-Stage Estimation

In this section, we propose a two-stage estimation method whose objective is to detect large attacks and discard the corresponding time instances to obtain clean data. Once clean data are obtained, we leverage a classical least-squares to produce an accurate estimate of the true system.
Our method is still challenged by attacks whose magnitude is comparable to or smaller than the noise level. Such attacks are referred to as
stealthy
and are inherently intractable to detect, since small attacks are information-theoretically indistinguishable from noise. Accordingly, we focus on scenarios in which most attacks are sufficiently large to be detected and removed by the proposed filtering procedure. We note that this regime cannot be addressed within a one-stage framework, since the proof of Theorem
6
constructs attacks of sufficiently large magnitude to mislead any one-stage estimator (see the sufficiently large
\bar{c}
given in (
7
)). This demonstrates that our two-stage estimation framework outperforms one-stage estimators. We present our framework in Algorithm
1
.

Two-Stage Estimation using
\ell_{1}
-norm estimator and the least-squares

A trajectory of length
T:(x_{0},\dots,x_{T})
. A detection threshold parameters

(

\displaystyle\min_{a}f_{T}^{(i)}(a):=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}|x_{t+1}^{(i)}-a^{T}x_{t}|

\mathring{a}_{i}\in\argmin_{a}f_{T}^{(i)}(a)

(

(9)

(

) Solve
n
least-squares optimization problems to find the point estimate

\displaystyle\min_{a}L_{T}^{(i)}(a):=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}(x_{t+1}^{(i)}-a^{T}x_{t})^{2}

(10)

and let
\hat{a}_{i}\in\argmin_{a}L_{T}^{(i)}(a)
for all
i=1,\dots,n.

Output:

Stage I :
\mathring{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathring{a}_{i}^{T}\end{bmatrix}_{i=1}^{n}
, Stage II :
\hat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{a}_{i}^{T}\end{bmatrix}_{i=1}^{n}
, with each vector stacked as rows.

4.1
Algorithm Description

The algorithm consists of two stages,
Stage I
and
Stage II
. In Stage I, our goal is to detect large attacks. To this end, we seek an estimate of the true system
\bar{A}
that is sufficiently accurate in the presence of both noise and attacks. Since no one-stage estimator can produce an arbitrarily accurate estimate of
\bar{A}
, we can at best obtain an estimate with a small constant error. This estimate can then be leveraged to classify data along the trajectory into “clean” and “corrupted” instances, which is feasible when
\bar{A}
is estimated with adequate accuracy. For this stage, we adopt a row-wise
\ell_{1}
-norm estimator, as given in (
8
), which produces

\mathring{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathring{a}_{i}^{T}\end{bmatrix}_{i=1}^{n}

with index
i
corresponding to each node.

Before proceeding to Stage II, we use the estimate
\mathring{a}_{i}^{T}
for each node
i
to filter out data points suspected of being attacked. Specifically, we compare the residual

|x_{t+1}^{(i)}-\mathring{a}_{i}^{T}x_{t}|=|(\bar{a}_{i}-\mathring{a}_{i})^{T}x_{t}+w_{t}^{(i)}+v_{t}^{(i)}|

against the threshold
\beta_{1}\|x_{t}\|+\beta_{2}
, where
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
are tuning parameters.
Given that
\mathring{a}_{i}
is sufficiently close to
\bar{a}_{i}
,
we expect the residual to be small when
v_{t}^{(i)}=0
and detectably large enough when
v_{t}^{(i)}\neq 0
. Based on this criterion, we collect clean data for each node
i
from the original time indices
\{0,\dots,T-1\}
by retaining those instances whose residuals fall below the threshold.

In Stage II, we obtain an estimate of the true dynamics
\bar{A}
using the collection of (potentially) clean data for each node. Assuming that this dataset is perfectly clean,
i.e.
, contains only noise and no attacks, the least-squares estimator guarantees the optimal estimation error bound of
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})
.

Remark 7
.

Note that we adopt row-wise
\ell_{1}
-norm estimators instead of the full-matrix
\ell_{1}
-norm estimator for two reasons. First, it decomposes
\|\bar{A}-\mathring{A}\|
into
n
separate estimation errors, allowing us to analyze each node independently. In particular,
\|\bar{a}_{i}-\mathring{a}_{i}\|
can be individually bounded, whereas an upper bound on
\|\bar{A}-\mathring{A}\|
does not provide separate bounds for each row. Second, row-wise estimation improves numerical efficiency. Both the full and row-wise estimators reduce to linear optimization problems, whose computational complexity scales cubically with the number of variables. The full estimator involves
n^{2}
variables, leading to
\mathcal{O}(n^{6})
complexity, while solving
n
separate row-wise estimators, each with
n
variables, incurs only
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\cdot n)=\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
complexity.

Remark 8
.

In Stage II, we apply least-squares to the filtered dataset, assuming all data are clean, i.e.,
w_{t}^{(i)}+v_{t}^{(i)}=w_{t}^{(i)}
. In practice, the filtering procedure inevitably produces misclassifications unless attacked and non-attacked data are perfectly separable. False negatives—attacked data that remain in the dataset—occur when
|w_{t}^{(i)}+v_{t}^{(i)}|
is not large (or bounded by some
W
), introducing a bias of at most
W
. False positives—clean data removed from the dataset due to unusually large
|w_{t}^{(i)}|
—also induce bias by truncating a tail of the noise distribution. Both types of errors directly or indirectly bias the filtered dataset, degrading the performance of the least-squares estimator.

Algorithm
1
requires selecting parameters
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
, whose optimal values are often unknown in advance. This necessitates an alternative filtering procedure instead of (
9
). The new procedure first discards time instances with extremely small or large norms, and then ranks the ratios
\frac{|x_{t+1}^{(i)}-\mathring{a}_{i}^{T}x_{t}|}{\|x_{t}\|_{2}}
to select the smallest ones. A suitable cutoff can be determined from observing the distributions of state norms or residual-to-state ratios; for instance, it is easy to determine when most attacks are large relative to noise. Details of this method are provided in Appendix
C
.

5
Analysis of Two-Stage Estimation

In this section, we present the main theorems for the two-stage estimation. We first bound the estimation error in Stage I, then characterize how large attacks must be to be detected by the filtering procedure, and finally derive the estimation error bound of the least-squares estimator in Stage II.

5.1
Analysis of Stage I

To derive estimation error bounds for the row-wise
\ell_{1}
-norm estimators, we first consider a noise-aware system in which the full noise sequence
\{w_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is available to the estimator, and subsequently extend the results to the general setting where the noise is unobserved. While prior works
(
Yalcin et al. 2024
;
Kim and Lavaei 2025
)
impose temporal sparsity assumptions, which requires the attack at each time
t
to be either nonzero or entirely zero, we significantly relax this constraint in Assumption
3
to adapt to
networked systems
. Under our node-wise attack assumption, at least one node is attacked at each time with probability
1-(1-p)^{n}\approx 1
; effectively, the overall network is under almost-persistent local attack. Despite allowing different adversarial strategies across nodes, the row-wise
\ell_{1}
-norm estimator can still exactly recover the noise-aware system, in the sense that a positive normalized gap exists between the objective value at the true system
\bar{A}
and that at any other
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

Consider a noise-aware system
x_{t+1}=\bar{A}x_{t}+w_{t}+v_{t}

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{T}^{(i)}(a_{i}):=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}|\tilde{x}_{t+1}^{(i)}-a_{i}^{T}x_{t}|=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}|(\bar{a}_{i}-a_{i})^{T}x_{t}+v_{t}^{(i)}|

Remark 13
.

The conservative choice of

and

in Theorem

5.3
Analysis of Stage II

In this section, we derive an estimation error bound for the least-squares estimator applied to the filtered dataset
\mathcal{T}_{i}
for each
i

e.g.
, attacks
\gg
noise), the filtered dataset contains only clean data with zero-mean noise, without discarding any tail samples, allowing the least-squares estimator to achieve the optimal estimation error that converges to zero as
|\mathcal{T}_{i}|\to\infty

We now formally present the main theorem, which is analyzed under the same parameter choices as in Theorem

Theorem 14

Suppose Assumptions

Remark 15
.

The first error term in (

. In contrast, since

, the second term remains constant, proportional to the noise level
\sigma_{w}
times the number of misclassifications. This implies that the estimation error diminishes with

for perfectly separable data. Since misclassifications are driven by attack magnitudes beyond the estimator’s control, the focus must be shifted to reducing
\sigma_{w}

However, a reduction in noise often leads to a decrease in the excitation parameter

(see Remark

,
where

is an independent zero-mean Gaussian control input. The

w_{t}
, which enables to identify
\bar{A}
via two-stage estimation by treating
x_{t+1}-u_{t}
as the next state.
This approach yields an estimation error that decreases proportionally as the noise parameter
\sigma_{w}
is reduced.

7
Conclusion

This paper investigates system identification for networked linear dynamical systems from a single trajectory of length
T
under persistent noise and node-wise probabilistic adversarial attacks. We first prove that any convex one-stage estimator fails to achieve an estimation error that converges to zero as
T\to\infty
; this fundamental limitation necessitates a two-stage estimation approach. Our proposed method leverages row-wise
\ell_{1}
-norm estimators in the first stage to achieve a small, constant estimation error. This initial estimate serves to filter out time instances dominated by large attacks while retaining those with manageable noise levels. Using this filtered data, the least-squares method yields an estimation error comprising a term that diminishes with
T
and a constant term proportional to the product of the noise level and the number of misclassifications. This work provides the first theoretical guarantees demonstrating that a two-stage estimation method is essential for modern real-world systems simultaneously affected by both stochastic noise and adversarial attacks.

g_{t}(A):=\phi(-A(w_{t-1}+v_{t-1})+w_{t}+v_{t})
with
x_{0}=w_{-1}+v_{-1}
.
Since a convex
\phi
is differentiable almost everywhere and
w_{t}
is absolutely continuous, while independent of
v_{t}
, the subdifferential
\partial\phi(w_{t}+v_{t})
is essentially
\{\nabla\phi(w_{t}+v_{t})\}
almost surely. Accordingly,
the only subgradient of
g_{t}(A)
evaluated at
A=0
is given as

\displaystyle\nabla g_{t}(0)=-\nabla\phi(w_{t}+v_{t})\cdot(w_{t-1}+v_{t-1})^{T}.

(21)

Following Assumption
3
,
\xi_{t}^{i}=1
for all
i=1,\dots,n
with probability
p^{n}
. In these time instances,
v_{t}
is allowed to be arbitrary sub-Gaussian along all its components, so we consider choosing
v_{t}=\bar{c}x_{t}/\|x_{t}\|_{2}
for some constant
\bar{c}>0
if
t
is odd and
v_{t}=0
if
t
is even (we defer the value of
\bar{c}
to the next paragraph). At the rest of time instances, we let
v_{t}=0
.
Under this design of attacks, there are two cases to analyze
\nabla g_{t}(0)
. Define the time set
\mathcal{T}=\{t\in\{0,\dots,T-1\}:t\text{~is odd and ~}\xi_{t}^{i}=1,~\forall i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\}
.

\begin{split}\nabla g_{t}(0)=\begin{cases}-\nabla\phi(w_{t}+\bar{c}w_{t-1}/\|w_{t-1}\|_{2})\cdot w_{t-1}^{T}&if $t\in\mathcal{T}$,\\
-\nabla\phi(w_{t})\cdot(w_{t-1}+v_{t-1})^{T}&if $t\notin\mathcal{T}$.\end{cases}\end{split}

(22)

We determine the value of
\bar{c}
. We select a sufficiently large
\bar{c}
to satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{\phi_{w_{t}}(\bar{c}x/\|x\|_{2})-\phi_{w_{t}}(0)}{\bar{c}}\geq\frac{m_{\phi}}{2}

(23)

for all
x\neq 0
, which is indeed possible due to Lemma
5
.

We are now ready to prove the theorem. Due to
w_{t}
being bounded, we have
\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla g_{t}(0)\|]<\infty
which incurs a well-defined expectation and can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}(\nabla g_{t}(0))]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}(\nabla g_{t}(0))~|~\mathbb{I}\{\mathcal{T}\}]]

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{n}}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[-\nabla\phi(w_{t}+\bar{c}w_{t-1}/\|w_{t-1}\|_{2})^{T}w_{t-1}]+\Bigr(1-\frac{p^{n}}{2}\Bigr)\cdot\mathbb{E}[-\nabla\phi(w_{t})^{T}(w_{t-1}+v_{t-1})]

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{n}}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[-\nabla\phi(w_{t}+\bar{c}w_{t-1}/\|w_{t-1}\|_{2})^{T}w_{t-1}~|~\bm{\sigma}\{w_{t-1}\}]]+\Bigr(1-\frac{p^{n}}{2}\Bigr)\cdot\mathbb{E}[-\nabla\phi(w_{t})^{T}]\mathbb{E}[w_{t-1}+v_{t-1}]

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{n}}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[-\nabla\mathbb{E}[\phi(w_{t}+\bar{c}w_{t-1}/\|w_{t-1}\|_{2})^{T}~|~\bm{\sigma}\{w_{t-1}\}]\cdot w_{t-1}]+0

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{n}}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[-\nabla\phi_{w_{t}}(\bar{c}w_{t-1}/\|w_{t-1}\|_{2})^{T}\cdot w_{t-1}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p^{n}}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[-\biggr(\frac{\phi_{w_{t}}(\bar{c}w_{t-1}/\|w_{t-1}\|_{2})-\phi_{w_{t}}(0)}{\bar{c}}\|w_{t-1}\|_{2}\biggr)\right]\leq-\frac{p^{n}}{2}\cdot\frac{m_{\phi}}{2}\mathbb{E}[\|w_{t-1}\|_{2}]<0,

(24)

where the third equality comes from

\mathbb{E}[\nabla\phi(w_{t})]=0
since a function
\phi(x)=\phi(-x)
implies
\nabla\phi(x)=-\nabla\phi(-x)
at all differentiable points and
w_{t}
is symmetric. The second last inequality comes from
\phi_{w_{t}}
being convex, and the last inequality is due to the choice of
\bar{c}
given in (
23
). The last strict inequality is from Lemma
3
(applying
Z=I/\sqrt{n}
) and
x_{t}=w_{t-1}
when
t\in\mathcal{T}
.

Now, by strong law of large numbers, we can fix
A
and say that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}g_{t}(A)\longrightarrow\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(A)]\quad\text{as}\quad T\to\infty,

since
\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(A)]
is properly defined due to the boundedness of
w_{t}
. This is indeed possible since the design of
v_{t}
makes both
\{g_{t}(A):t\text{~ is odd}\}
and
\{g_{t}(A):t\text{~ is even}\}
an independent sequence.

Now, Let
A_{T}
be a minimizer to (
20
). The existence of a minimizer is clear due to the convexity and (almost sure) coercivity of the objective in
A
.
Suppose for the contradiction that
A_{T}\to 0
as
T\to\infty
.
Then, we appeal to the uniform convergence on compact sets of convex functions (Theorem 10.8,
Rockafellar 1970
), which holds when a sequence of finite convex functions converges pointwise to a finite limit on a dense subset of a relatively open convex domain.
Since
A_{T}\to 0
, the sequence is bounded. Thus, regardless of whether
R
in (
20
) is finite, there exists
0<\tilde{R}<\infty
such that the compact set
\mathcal{A}=\{A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:\|A\|\leq\tilde{R}\}
contains
\bar{A}
and
\{A_{T}\}_{T\geq 0}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}g_{t}(A)-\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(A)]\right|\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{as}\quad T\to\infty.

Denote
\epsilon_{T}
by
\sup_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}g_{t}(A)-\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(A)]\right|
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(A_{T})]\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}g_{t}(A_{T})+\epsilon_{T}\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}g_{t}(A)+\epsilon_{T}\leq\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(A)]+2\epsilon_{T},\quad\forall A\in\mathcal{A},

where the first and last inequality is due to the definition of
\epsilon_{T}
and the second inequality is due to an optimality of
A_{T}
. Since
\epsilon_{T}\to 0
as
T\to\infty
due to uniform convergence, we know that
A_{T}\to 0
implies that
0
is a minimizer to
\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(A)]
subject to
A\in\mathcal{A}
. Since Slater’s condition holds for the constraint
A\in\mathcal{A}
, Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) necessary conditions for
\mu\geq 0
imply that

\displaystyle 0\in\partial_{A}(\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(A)]+\mu\|A\|^{2})|_{A=0}=\partial_{A}\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(0)]+2\cdot 0\cdot\{A\mid\|A\|_{*}\leq 1\}=\partial_{A}\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(0)],

where
\|\cdot\|_{*}
is the dual norm (Theorem 23.5 (d),
Rockafellar 1970
).
Since
g_{t}
is convex and finite, we have
\partial\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(0)]=\mathbb{E}[\partial g_{t}(0)]

(
Rockafellar 1968
)
. However, we established in (
24
) that
\mathbb{E}[\partial g_{t}(0)]=\mathbb{E}[\nabla g_{t}(0)]<0
almost surely. This yields a contradiction, implying that
A_{T}
does not converge to
0
.
∎

B.2
Extensions to Infinite Expectation Structure

Similar to Section
B.1
, we show that a minimizer cannot converge to
0
when the true system is
\bar{A}=0
. Consider
g_{t}(A)
in (
20
). Section
B.1
established that if the expectation
\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(A)]
exists for all points in a neighborhood of the origin, and
\mathbb{E}[\nabla g_{t}(0)]
exists, then
0
cannot be a minimizer. We now address the scenario where at least one of these expectations may not exist. Since
\phi
is nonnegative, the function
g_{t}(A)
is also nonnegative. Consequently, if the expectation
\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(A)]
is undefined, it must diverge to infinity. Also, recall that the form of
g_{t}(A)
is a function of sub-Gaussian variables due to Assumption
2
.
We will first show that if at least one aforementioned expectation is infinite in a neighborhood of
0
, then considering a light sub-Gaussian tail, it means that the function
\phi
grows super-exponentially;
i.e.
,
\lim\inf_{\|x\|_{2}\to\infty}\frac{\log\phi(x)}{\|x\|_{2}^{2}}\geq\alpha>0
.

We take the same time set
\mathcal{T}
from (
22
), and design
v_{t}=\bar{c}w_{t-1}
whenever
t\in\mathcal{T}
;
i.e.
,

\begin{split}g_{t}(A)=\begin{cases}\phi(-Aw_{t-1}+w_{t}+\bar{c}w_{t-1})&if $t\in\mathcal{T}$,\\
\phi(-Aw_{t-1}+w_{t})&if $t\notin\mathcal{T},t-1\notin\mathcal{T}$,\\
\phi(-A(w_{t-1}+\bar{c}w_{t-2})+w_{t})&if $t\notin\mathcal{T},t-1\in\mathcal{T}$.\end{cases}\end{split}

(25)

We consider a scenario where
w_{t}
are independent and operate on comparable scales.
The value
\bar{c}>2
is chosen sufficiently large to ensure that the super-exponential growth of
\phi
is realized with positive probability (infinitely often); specifically, we assume the existence of constants
\alpha,q>0
such that
\mathbb{P}(\phi(w_{t}+\bar{c}w_{t-1})\geq\alpha\exp(\|w_{t}+\bar{c}w_{t-1}\|_{2}^{2}))\geq q
.

Consequently, for sufficiently large
T
, the empirical average at
A=0
, given by
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}g_{t}(0)
, is dominated by the time instances in
\mathcal{T}
(the terms
\phi(w_{t}+\bar{c}w_{t-1})
), as the remaining terms involve only small noise
\phi(w_{t})
. Now, consider a small perturbation
A=\epsilon I
where
0<\epsilon<\min\{\frac{1}{4},R\}
. The evaluation of
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}g_{t}(\epsilon I)
is determined by the trade-off between two effects: the reduction in loss for
t\in\mathcal{T}
(where the input magnitude drops roughly from
\bar{c}
to
\bar{c}-\epsilon
), and the penalty for
t-1\in\mathcal{T}
(where the input magnitude increases from
0
to
\epsilon\bar{c}
).

Since
\phi
is convex, even, and grows super-exponentially, the reduction exponentially outweighs the penalty, provided
\bar{c}
is sufficiently large. This implies that the function value decreases at
A=\epsilon I
relative to
A=0
for all sufficiently large
T
. This completes the analysis.

Alternative Filtering Procedure for Algorithm

In this section, we present an alternative method for the filtering procedure. Consider that

\displaystyle\frac{|x_{t+1}^{(i)}-\mathring{a}_{i}^{T}x_{t}|}{\|x_{t}\|_{2}}=\frac{|(\bar{a}_{i}-\mathring{a}_{i})^{T}x_{t}+w_{t}^{(i)}+v_{t}^{(i)}|}{\|x_{t}\|_{2}}\leq\|\bar{a}_{i}-\mathring{a}_{i}\|_{2}+\frac{|w_{t}^{(i)}|}{\|x_{t}\|_{2}}+\frac{|v_{t}^{(i)}|}{\|x_{t}\|_{2}}.

(26)

since the upper bound in (

Algorithm 2

Two-Stage Estimation with the Ranking Subroutine

Input:
A trajectory of length
T:(x_{0},\dots,x_{T})
. Acceptance probabilities
0\leq q_{1},q_{2}\leq 1
.
\textcolor

blue// Replace Line

in Algorithm

with the following.

(
Filtering i
) Let the values
y_{t}=\|x_{t}\|

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\{t\in\{0,\dots,T-1\}:y_{(\lceil 1-\frac{q_{1}T}{2}\rceil)}\leq y_{t}\leq y_{(\lfloor 1+\frac{q_{1}T}{2}\rfloor)}\}.

Appendix D
Proof of Theorem
9

The proof is adapted from
Kim and Lavaei 2025
. We first start with establishing a lower bound on the difference of
\tilde{f}_{T}^{(i)}
at
a_{i}
and
\bar{a}_{i}
.

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{T}^{(i)}(a_{i})-\tilde{f}_{T}^{(i)}(\bar{a}_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}|(\bar{a}_{i}-a_{i})^{T}x_{t}+v_{t}^{(i)}|-\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}|v_{t}^{(i)}|

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=0,\\
v_{t}^{(i)}=0\end{subarray}}^{T-1}|(\bar{a}_{i}-a_{i})^{T}x_{t}|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=0,\\
v_{t}^{(i)}\neq 0\end{subarray}}^{T-1}(\bar{a}_{i}-a_{i})^{T}x_{t}\cdot\mathrm{sgn}(v_{t}^{(i)}),

where the last term stems from

\displaystyle|(\bar{a}_{i}-a_{i})^{T}x_{t}+v_{t}^{(i)}|\geq|v_{t}^{(i)}|+(\bar{a}_{i}-a_{i})^{T}x_{t}\cdot\mathrm{sgn}(v_{t}^{(i)})

due to the gradient inequality for a convex function
|\cdot|
and considering that a subgradient of
|\cdot|
is a sign function, denoted by
\mathrm{sgn}(\cdot)
. Letting
u=\frac{\bar{a}_{i}-a_{i}}{\|\bar{a}_{i}-a_{i}\|_{2}}
, we attain

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{T}^{(i)}(a_{i})-\tilde{f}_{T}^{(i)}(\bar{a}_{i})\geq\|a_{i}-\bar{a}_{i}\|_{2}\cdot\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=0,\\
v_{t}^{(i)}=0\end{subarray}}^{T-1}|u^{T}x_{t}|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=0,\\
v_{t}^{(i)}\neq 0\end{subarray}}^{T-1}u^{T}x_{t}\cdot\mathrm{sgn}(v_{t}^{(i)})\right].

(27)

Thus, it suffices to study for such a lower bound for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\|u\|_{2}=1
.

Moreover, an additional lower bound can be established. Assumption
3
is in fact a special case of node-wise sign-symmetric attacks. It specifies that the event
{v_{t}^{(i)}=0}
occurs with probability
1-p
, while
{v_{t}^{(i)}\neq 0}
occurs with probability
p
. Since
|u^{T}x_{t}|\geq u^{T}x_{t}\cdot\mathrm{sgn}(v_{t}^{(i)})
regardless of the value of
v_{t}^{(i)}
, the probability mass assigned to
{v_{t}^{(i)}\neq 0}
can be shifted to a sign-symmetric distribution without decreasing the right-hand side of (
27

p
allows one to construct sign-symmetric attacks by balancing the existing distribution of
{\mathrm{sgn}(v_{t}^{(i)}):v_{t}^{(i)}\neq 0}

{-\mathrm{sgn}(v_{t}^{(i)}):v_{t}^{(i)}\neq 0}
.
A similar argument appears in Theorem 3 of
Kim and Lavaei 2025
. Hence, we may assume without loss of generality that
\mathbb{P}(v_{t}^{(i)}=0)=1-2p
, while sign-symmetric attacks occur with probability
2p
.

We now provide a lower bound on
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}|u^{T}x_{t}|

\|u\|_{2}=1.

Lemma 16
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
4

Lemma 25
.

Consider a zero-mean sub-Gaussian variable
w
with
\|w\|_{\psi_{2}}=\sigma_{w}
. Then, for a constant
\alpha>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[w\cdot\mathbb{I}\{w\leq\alpha\sigma_{w}\}]\geq-\sigma_{w}\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[w\cdot\mathbb{I}\{w\geq-\alpha\sigma_{w}\}]\leq\sigma_{w}\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\right),

where
\mathbb{I}\{\cdot\}
is an indicator function.

Proof.

We apply integration by parts to
\mathbb{E}[w\cdot\mathbb{I}\{w>s\}]
for
s\geq 0
to derive

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[w\cdot\mathbb{I}\{w>s\}]

\displaystyle=s\mathbb{P}(w>s)+\int_{s}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(w>x)dx.

For the first term, since
\mathbb{P}(w>s)\leq\exp\bigr(-\frac{s^{2}}{2\sigma_{w}^{2}}\bigr)
for all
s\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle s\mathbb{P}(w>s)\leq s\exp\left(-\frac{s^{2}}{2\sigma_{w}^{2}}\right).

For the second term, we have

\int_{s}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(w>x)dx\leq\frac{\sigma_{w}^{2}}{s}\exp\left(-\frac{s^{2}}{2\sigma_{w}^{2}}\right)

due to the standard upper bound for the Gaussian tail integral (Mill’s Ratio type bound) stating that
\int_{x}^{\infty}e^{-t^{2}/2}dt<\frac{1}{x}e^{-x^{2}/2}
holds for
x>0
. Substituting
s=\alpha\sigma_{w}
yields that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[w\cdot\mathbb{I}\{w>\alpha\sigma_{w}\}]\leq\alpha\sigma_{w}\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\right)+\frac{\sigma_{w}}{\alpha}\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\right).

The fact that
\mathbb{E}[w\cdot\mathbb{I}\{w>\alpha\sigma_{w}\}]+\mathbb{E}[w\cdot\mathbb{I}\{w\leq\alpha\sigma_{w}\}]=\mathbb{E}[w]=0
completes the proof of the first statement. The expectation on the opposite-side tail can be similarly derived.
∎

\mathring{a}_{i}
