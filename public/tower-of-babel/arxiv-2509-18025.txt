Deep learning as the disciplined construction of tame objects
Gilles Bareilles 1 *, Allen Gehret 1,2 *, Johannes Aspman 1, Jana Lepšová 1, Jakub Mareček 1

Abstract
. One can see deep-learning models as compositions of functions within the so-called tame geometry. In this expository note, we give an overview of some topics at the interface of tame geometry (also known as o-minimality ), optimization theory , and deep learning theory and practice. To do so, we gradually introduce the concepts and tools used to build convergence guarantees for stochastic gradient descent in a general nonsmooth nonconvex, but tame, setting. This illustrates some ways in which tame geometry is a natural mathematical framework for the study of AI systems, especially within Deep Learning.

Introduction

1.1.
Context: Frameworks for studying AI systems and Deep Learning

The rapid evolution of Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems has allowed these systems to be applied across many fields, including high-risk applications such as credit-scoring
[
bhatoreMachineLearningTechniques2020
]
, recidivism forecasting
[

]

[

While these advances promise social benefits, they also raise pressing concerns regarding the reliability, interpretability, fairness, and safety of systems that are both novel and deployed at scale.
Consequently, there is a growing consensus among scholars, policymakers, and industry stakeholders for the establishment of robust regulatory frameworks and standardized evaluation protocols to ensure a responsible and trustworthy AI deployment
[
dilhacMontrealDeclarationResponsible2018

To that end, a number of road-maps have been suggested in order to align AI systems with human preferences; see e.g.,
[

hoangFabuleuxChantierRendre2021
]

This prompts the question: what is a good theoretical framework to provide guarantees on current AI systems, or more precisely, on Deep Learning objects?
Let us be specific: by “good framework”, we mean a framework that is both
realistic
, in that it encompasses relevant applications, such as current AI systems, and
prolific

One such widespread framework is convex analysis and close variants
[

,
hiriart-urrutyFundamentalsConvexAnalysis2001

[

,

]
.
Yet, these theories reach their limits with deep learning theory, where objects typically do not satisfy convexity, or close variants at every point.

1.2.
A realistic framework

Definability in o-minimal structures covers nearly all functions and problems occurring in Deep Learning theory and practice.
It does so by providing
composability guarantees

will remain definable.

The list of

To quote

…the class [of definable sets and functions] is closed with respect to practically all operations used in variational analysis…

In less technical terms, definability is preserved by almost all common operations in optimization, such as composition of functions, (partial) minimization, construction of dual objects, (sub)differentiation,

This feature follows directly from the first-order logic background of such structures, as discussed in

One notable exception is that solutions to differential equations may not remain definable in some o-minimal structure.

Note that many of the popular theoretical frameworks used to analyze Deep Learning objects fail to account for feedforward ReLU networks, arguably the simplest networks.
Indeed, the vast majority of approaches developed to study optimization schemes require the functions to be either differentiable with a (globally) Lipschitz gradient, or to feature some form of convexity such as strong convexity, convexity, weak convexity, prox-regularity, or at the very least Clarke regularity, in order of decreasing strength; see e.g.,

Yet, all these assumptions fail for the function

This function can be seen as a ReLU Network with depth one; it reveals the nature of the difficulty of nonsmooth nonconvex optimization.
These nonsmooth nonconvex features are also present in higher-dimensional and more complex networks; yet they are (nearly) always definable in some o-minimal structure.

Note that many theoretical frameworks come with
composability guarantees
.
One popular such framework is convexity theory; for instance, adding two convex functions, or taking the indicator function of a convex set always maintains convexity, while subtracting two convex functions does not.
Here again, building functions and sets from convex objects and according to a list of prescribed composition rules, in a “disciplined way”, is useful.
It provides objects that are convex,
and which are in a form suitable for optimization; see the disciplined convex programming line of work
[

]

1.3.
A prolific framework

We argued above that many of the popular theoretical frameworks are not broad enough to account for practical functions such as ReLU-based networks.
We argue here that they are also too broad, in that they often include ill-behaved functions and sets, which don’t actually appear in practice.
Crucially, this limits their ability to allow for general, wide-ranging, results.
We thus want to restrict our mathematical universe to rule out pathological objects.

Let us first give some examples of pathological objects.
In topology, one can think about sets whose frontier have the same (or higher!) dimension, functions that exhibit infinitely many changes in monotonicity in a bounded set.
See
3.4
,
3.37
,
3.36
and
3.35
.
In optimization theory, there are (two-dimensional) smooth convex coercive functions with curious behavior: failure of convergence of steepest descent with exact search, of central paths, or of Newton’s flow, but also infinite length of Tikhonov path, or failure of the smooth Kurdyka–Łojasiewicz inequality
[
bolteCuriositiesCounterexamplesSmooth2022
]
.
Also, optimization over trigonometric functions is not decidable, even in the box-constrained case
[
liberti2019undecidability
, 3.5.5]
.

In contrast with this, definability in o-minimal structures restrains the world of functions and sets to well-behaved, pathology-free objects.
As such, definable functions and sets enjoy remarkable properties.
The concepts that characterize a set being small in
\mathbb{R}
, including finiteness, countability, nowhere dense, meager, or zero Lebesgue measure become equivalent (
3.5
).
There exists a definable choice lemma, which provides a constructive version of the
Axiom of Choice (
3.6
).
A definable curve admits one-sided limits everywhere; this limit is also definable (
Lemma

3.21
).
There is a unique notion of dimension of a set (
3.32
), and the frontiers of definable sets have lower dimensions than the sets (
3.34
).
These results build up to various stratification theorems: any definable set partitions into “smooth” definable subsets (manifolds), which fit “nicely” together; a parallel result holds for function (
Theorem

3.39
).

Stratification results proved particularly prolific in optimization theory.
They allowed to characterize how generalized derivatives behave on generic nonsmooth nonconvex functions
[
bolte2007clarke
]
, among other results.
Eventually, this approach allowed to prove the convergence of the Stochastic Subgradient Method (SSM), also known as Stochastic Gradient Descent, on nearly any function encountered in the training of DNNs
[
davis2020stochastic
]
.
We discuss this result and its proof ideas in
Section

4
.
Focusing on tame functions and sets allowed to obtain the first theory of Automatic Differentiation (AD) on nonsmooth functions, along with a convergence proof of AD-based SSM
[
bolte2020mathematical
,
bolteConservativeSetValued2021
]
; see
Section

for details.

1.4.
Content and outline

In this document, we seek to provide a unified exposition of concepts at the interface of
o-minimality
,
optimization theory
, and
deep learning
theory and practice.
As a motivating case-study, we expose the material that builds towards the convergence proof of the Stochastic Subgradient Method.
The ideas presented in this document are not new.
Rather, we have strived to provide proofs and arguments that are up-to-date, concise, and help develop intuition, based on material from three distinct fields, scattered across many articles.
As such, we hope that this document, along with the many articles it references, will help people to learn and use this theoretical framework.

Section

3

2.1.
Semialgebraic sets

In

, one studies sets defined by polynomial equalities and inequalities over real numbers, in contrast to working over complex numbers as in

We begin with the notion of

Definition 2.2
.

[

A

\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a set of the form

(2.2)

\bigcup_{i=1}^{s}\bigcap_{j=1}^{r_{i}}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f_{i,j}(x)\diamond_{i,j}0\}

where
f_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
and
\diamond_{i,j}\in\{<,=\}

and
j=1,\ldots,r_{i}

One can show that the complement of a semialgebraic set is also semialgebraic, and thus the semialgebraic sets are precisely the boolean combinations of polynomial equalities and inequalities.

Definition

2.2

Lemma 2.3

(o-minimality property)

[

Every semialgebraic subset of
\mathbb{R}
is a finite union of intervals and points.

2.2.
Semialgebraic functions

Having introduced semialgebraic sets, we now turn to semialgebraic functions. Given a function
f:A\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we say that
f
is
semialgebraic
if its graph
\Gamma(f)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
is a semialgebraic set. The following two lemmas enable us
to easily recognize semialgebraic functions.

Lemma 2.8

(Composability)
.

The composition of semialgebraic functions is semialgebraic.

Proof.

For simplicity, consider semialgebraic functions
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. By definition, there exist semialgebraic subsets
\varphi(x,y)
and
\psi(x,y)
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
\Gamma(f)=\varphi(x,y)
and
\Gamma(g)=\psi(x,y)
. By shifting variables in the definition of
g
, we may consider
\Gamma(g)
as defined by a semialgebraic set
\psi(y,z)
. Now, if we consider both
\Gamma(f)
and
\Gamma(g)
as subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
involving all three variables
x,y,z
, we see by the
2.4
that:

(2.5)

\Gamma(g\circ f)\ =\ \pi_{x,z}(\varphi(x,y)\cap\psi(y,z))

is also semialgebraic. Indeed, viewing this expression in “logical form” it describes the set:

(2.6)

\theta(x,z)\ :=\ \exists y\ (f(x)=y\ \text{and}\ g(y)=z),

which is just the definition of function composition.
∎

Lemma 2.9

(Definition by case distinction)
.

Suppose the functions
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and the set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
are semialgebraic. Then the following function is also semialgebraic:

(2.7)

h\ :\ \mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},\quad x\ \mapsto\ h(x)\ :=\ \begin{cases}f(x),&\text{if $x\in X$};\\
g(x),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

The proof is left as an exercise for the reader.
∎

(Semialgebraic asymptotics)

Let

Lemma 2.12
.

The real exponential function
\exp:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

Proof.

This follows from the observation that
Lemma

characterizes the available asymptotic behaviour of semialgebraic functions, and the asymptotics of

is not among them.
∎

The need to extend the semialgebraic framework so that the exponential function

Tarski’s Problem (approximately)
.

Develop versions of results from semialgebraic geometry in a setting that also includes the real exponential function

2.3.
Moment-SOS

It is well-known that finding global minimizers of nonconvex functions is in general extremely difficult (NP-hard).
Surprisingly, for certain optimization problem exclusively made of polynomials, finding global minimizers becomes less difficult.
Here, we take a quick detour from the main exposition on o-minimal structures, and provide a glimpse of the
Moment / Sum Of Squares method
, as one of the major success stories in the realm of
semialgebraic optimization
.
For details and references, we point the reader to
[
magronSparsePolynomialOptimization2023
,
Lasserre_2015
]
.

Consider the nonconvex optimization problem

(2.8)

f^{\star}=\min_{x\in X}f(x),\qquad\text{ where }X=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:g_{1}(x)\geq 0,\dots,g_{m}(x)\geq 0\},

and
f
,
g_{1}
, …,
g_{l}
are real polynomials over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Problem (
2.8
) can be cast as a linear problem over the infinite-dimensional space of probability measures:

(2.9)

f_{\infty}=\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(X)_{+}}\left\{\int_{X}f(x)d\mu(x),\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\int_{X}d\mu=1\right\},

where
\mathcal{M}(X)_{+}
denotes the cone of finite Borel measures supported on
X
.
To see that
f_{\infty}=f^{\star}
, note first that a Dirac mass placed at a global minimizer of (
2.8
) is feasible for (
2.9
), which implies
f_{\infty}\leq f^{\star}
.
Second, note that there holds
\int_{X}f(x)d\mu(x)\geq\int_{X}f^{\star}d\mu(x)=f^{\star}
for any finite Borel measure of unit mass, which implies
f_{\infty}\geq f^{\star}
.

Going from formulation (
2.8
) to (
2.9
) thus replaces the (difficult) nonconvexity of
f
and
X
by the (easier) linearity of the objective of (
2.9
) and convexity of its feasible set.
This is done at the cost of introducing a new difficulty: (
2.9
) is an infinite-dimensional problem, difficult to manipulate numerically.

To address this difficulty, one may consider an increasing sequence of subspaces
\mathcal{M}(X)_{d}
that converges to
\mathcal{M}(X)
.
The most basic choice consists in taking
\mathcal{M}(X)_{d}
as the set of measures which have moments of order higher than
2d
equal to zero.
We thus obtain a sequence of optimization problems:

(2.10)

f_{d}=\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(X)_{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)d\mu(x).

In theory, the following theoretical guarantee, first obtained by Lasserre
[
lasserreGlobalOptimizationPolynomials2001
]
holds: if
X
is a bounded set,
3
3

3

The result holds more generally under the Archimedean condition on
X
: there exists
N>0
such that the polynomial
N-\|x\|_{2}^{2}
can be written as
\sigma_{0}+\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}g_{i}
where
\sigma_{i}
are sum-of-square polynomials.

f_{d}
is a nondecreasing sequence that converges to
f^{\star}
.
In addition, the convergence is
generically finite
, that is, for almost every problem of the form (
2.8
), there exists a
d^{\star}
such that
f_{d^{\star}}=f^{\star}
.
Finally, the problems (
2.10
) are convex conic (semidefinite) programs; they can thus be solved by off-the-shelf polynomial-time algorithms, such as Interior Point Methods.

However, here as well there is no free lunch: the problem (
2.10
) has
\binom{n+d}{n}=\mathcal{O}(d^{n})
variables which, in practice, only allows to solve (
2.10
) for
d=1
, and sometimes
d=2
, for industrial problem.
Exploiting sparsity in the polynomials and constraints of problem (
2.8
) helps in solving larger instances
[
magronSparsePolynomialOptimization2023
]
.
Finite convergence may occur for surprisingly low values of
d
, such as
d=1
or
2
for Optimal Power Flow problems
[
wangCertifyingGlobalOptimality2022
]
.
Another relevant topic is recovering minimizers
x^{\star}
of problem (
2.8
) from (approximate) solutions to problems (
2.10
).

3.
O-minimality and tame geometry

The arrival of
o-minimality
and
tame geometry
in 1984 can be attributed to three sources:

•

First, van den Dries
[
vandenDries84Remarks
]
introduced
structures of finite type
as a framework for attacking a version of Tarski’s problem. The main idea, as we will see below, is to consider an axiomatic setting which assumes an analogue of the
2.4
and in which the o-minimality property (
Lemma

2.3
) holds.

•

Second, Pillay and Steinhorn
[
PillaySteinhorn1984
]
recognized the analogy with
strongly minimal structures
, coined the term

, and developed much of the basic model-theoretic properties in a series of three papers
[
PillaySteinhorn86I
]
,
[
KnightPillaySteinhorn86II
]
(with Knight), and
[
PillaySteinhorn88III
]
.

•

Later, it was realized that o-minimality is a good framework for Grothendieck’s desired
tame topology
from
Esquisse d’un Programme

[
Grothendieck84
]
; see
[
Rolin08_Establishing
]
for commentary.

In this section, we will give a brief introduction to o-minimality, describe some main examples of o-minimal structures, and present some of the key properties which make an o-minimal framework conducive to
convergence guarantees
.

The standard reference for o-minimality is
[
driesTameTopologyOminimal1998
]
; other references include the introductions
[
coste2000introduction
,
MillerFields12
]
, the surveys
[
van1999minimal
]
,
[
rolin2011survey
]
, and the paper
[
van1996geometric
]
which serves as an introduction for geometers.

3.1.
o-minimal structures

The definition of
o-minimal structure
can be viewed as a generalization of the definition of
semialgebraic set
.

Definition 3.1
.

A
structure
on
\mathbb{R}
is a sequence
\mathcal{R}=(\mathcal{R}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
such that for each
n\geq 0
:

(S1)

\mathcal{R}_{n}
is a boolean algebra of subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
;

(S2)

if
A\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
, then
\mathbb{R}\times A
and
A\times\mathbb{R}
belong to
\mathcal{R}_{n+1}
;

(S3)

\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{1}=x_{n}\}\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
;

(S4)

if
A\in\mathcal{R}_{n+1}
, then
\pi(A)\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
, where
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the projection map on the first
n
coordinates.

A structure
\mathcal{R}
on
\mathbb{R}
is
o-minimal
if it satisfies:

(O1)

\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x<y\}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}
;

(O2)

the sets in
\mathcal{R}_{1}
are exactly the finite unions of intervals and points.

Finally, to simplify our discussion, we shall assume all structures satisfy:

(S5)

\{a\}\in\mathcal{R}_{1}
for every
a\in\mathbb{R}
;

(S6)

\mathcal{R}_{3}
contains the graphs of addition and multiplication
+,\cdot:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Here are some comments and conventions concerning the definition:

•

We say a set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
definable
(in the structure
\mathcal{R}
) if
X\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
.

•

We say a function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is
definable
if its graph is
a definable set.

•

We can view Axiom (S4) as imposing an analogue of Tarski-Seidenberg to hold for the definable sets.

•

Axioms (S1)-(S4) secretly say that the definable sets of a structure are precisely those sets which are definable via some
first-order
\mathcal{L}
-formula
\varphi(x)
where
\mathcal{L}
is some
first-order language
; for more on this connection to
first-order logic
, see
[
ADAMTT
, Appendix B]
.

•

Axiom (S5) says that all
parameters
are automatically definable.

•

Axiom (S6) together with (S5) imposes the condition that the semialgebraic sets are definable in every o-minimal structure; one can also consider smaller o-minimal structures – such as “
(\mathbb{R};<)
” and “
(\mathbb{R};<,+)
” – although these are less immediately relevant for the purposes of training DNNs.

•

Axiom (O1) ensures that the ordering is definable; this implies that every interval is guaranteed to be definable, thanks to (S5). In fact, in the presence of (S6) the axiom (O1) is redundant in the sense that we can definably recover the ordering from the field structure via:
x<y\Leftrightarrow\exists z(z\neq 0\ \&\ z^{2}=y-x)
(we won’t use this fact).

•

Axiom (O2) imposes an analogue of
Lemma

2.3
to hold for the definable sets. Note that this is the only axiom which imposes a restriction on the definable sets.

•

By default, in this document the adjective
tame
when applied to some set or function in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
means
definable in some o-minimal structure on
\mathbb{R}
.

The quintessential example of an o-minimal structure is the semialgebraic setting of
Section

2
.

Example 3.2

(
\mathbb{R}_{\operatorname{alg}}
: the semialgebraic sets)

For each
n\geq 0
, let
\mathcal{S}_{n}
denote the collection of all semialgebraic subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
; see

2.2

We note that

extends to the more general setting of functions definable in any structure.

In any structure, the composition of definable functions is definable.

Proof.

The same as the proof of

2.8

Remark 3.15
.

One might wonder if in general the amalgamation of any two o-minimal structures is always o-minimal. The answer is “no”: Le Gal
[

]
constructed an o-minimal structure
\mathbb{R}_{h}
such that
\mathbb{R}_{\operatorname{an},h}
is not o-minimal. In particular, there is no “largest” o-minimal structure. However, by Zorn’s Lemma, every o-minimal structure is contained inside of a (not necessarily unique) maximal o-minimal structure.

One consequence of this regarding our convention of the adjective

is that the following statement is technically incorrect:

(3.3)

The composition of tame functions is tame.

Instead, it should be understood that one actually means the following:

(3.4)

The composition of tame functions definable in the same o-minimal structure is tame.

This subtlety will not affect us as the tame functions we consider in this document (the activation and loss functions) are in fact definable in a common o-minimal structure; see
Remark

3.19
.

3.2.5.
\mathbb{R}_{\operatorname{Pfaff}}
(the Pfaffian closure of
\mathbb{R}_{\operatorname{alg}}
)

It is natural to wonder whether an o-minimal structure is closed under solutions to differential equations, where the coefficients of said equation are definable in the given structure. In general, the answer is “no” since e.g. the function
\sin(x):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a solution to the initial value problem
y^{\prime\prime}+y=0
and
y(0)=0
.

In contrast, the following theorem by Speissegger shows that for certain differential equations – the so-called
Pfaffian equations
– the solutions always exist in some (possibly larger) o-minimal structure called the
Pfaffian closure

\operatorname{Pfaff}(\mathcal{R})
of
\mathcal{R}
.

Theorem 3.16

(Pfaffian closure)
.

[

]

Let
\mathcal{R}
be an o-minimal structure. Then there is an o-minimal expansion
\operatorname{Pfaff}(\mathcal{R})
of
\mathcal{R}
which is closed under solutions to Pfaffian equations in the following strong sense (where “definable” refers to definable in
\operatorname{Pfaff}(\mathcal{R})
). If
U
is a definable and connected open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\omega=a_{1}dx_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}dx_{n}
is a
1

U

a_{i}:U\to\mathbb{R}
of class

, and
L\subseteq U
is a Rolle leaf of
\omega=0

L
is also definable.

For our purposes, it suffices to know the following fact about the Pfaffian closure:

Corollary 3.17
.

[

Suppose

is an open interval,

\operatorname{Pfaff}(\mathcal{R})

\operatorname{Pfaff}(\mathcal{R})
.

Let
\mathbb{R}_{\operatorname{Pfaff}}

(2)

(3)

(1) The function

Thus the compositional inverse
\exp:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is also definable in
\mathbb{R}_{\operatorname{Pfaff}}
.

(2) It follows from (1) that the function
(2/\sqrt{\pi})\exp(-t^{2}):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is also definable in
\mathbb{R}_{\operatorname{Pfaff}}
. Applying

3.17
again shows that the error function

defined by:

(3.6)

\operatorname{erf}(x)\ =\ \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x}\exp(-t^{2})dt

is also definable in
\mathbb{R}_{\operatorname{Pfaff}}
.

(3) The definability of
\arctan
follows likewise using the formula:

(3.7)

\arctan(x)\ =\ \int_{0}^{x}\frac{1}{t^{2}+1}dt

The final claim now follows since

and
\arctan
are definable in
\mathbb{R}_{\operatorname{Pfaff}}
by (2) and (3), and all other functions presented in

are already definable in

; see

3.12
.
∎

Remark 3.19

3.3.
Tame asymptotics

For the rest of
Section

3
, we fix an o-minimal structure
\mathcal{R}
, so “definable” means “definable in
\mathcal{R}
”.

In this subsection, we shall see that an o-minimality framework gives rise to a world of “tame asymptotics” and thus is a setting which is conducive to “convergence guarantees” of various kinds. The following easy statement expresses a “logical asymptotics”, i.e., any definable “property”
D
is either eventually true or eventually false:

Local o-minimality 3.20
.

Suppose
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is definable and fix
a\in\mathbb{R}
. Then there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that either:

(1)

(a,a+\varepsilon)\subseteq D
, or

(2)

(a,a+\varepsilon)\cap D=\varnothing
.

A similar statement holds to the left of
a
, as well as at
\pm\infty
.

The power and appeal of
local o-minimality
is that we can use our imagination when choosing the definable set
D
to obtain useful consequences. For example:

Lemma 3.21

(Existence of one-sided limits)
.

Suppose
f:(a,b)\to\mathbb{R}
is definable. Then for each
c\in[a,b)
the limit
\lim_{t\downarrow c}f(t)
exists in
\mathbb{R}_{\pm\infty}
. Moreover, the following is also definable:

(3.10)

[a,b)\to\textstyle\mathbb{R}_{\pm\infty},\quad c\ \mapsto\ \lim_{t\downarrow c}f(t).

Proof sketch.

Suppose towards a contradiction there exists
\ell\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\liminf_{t\downarrow c}f(t)<\ell<\limsup_{t\downarrow c}f(t)
. Then the definable set
D:=\{t\in(c,b):f(t)\geq\ell\}
contradicts

3.20
.
∎

As an application, we have the following result of Graña Drummond and Peterzil
[
DrummondPeterzil02
]
which Ioffe
[
Ioffe08
]
refers to as “the first study in which o-minimality was directly applied to optimization”.

Example 3.22

(Convergence of central path)
.

Graña Drummond and Peterzil observed in
[
DrummondPeterzil02
]
that under certain natural assumptions, if a semi-definite programming problem is definable in
\mathbb{R}_{\operatorname{an},\exp}
, then the central path trajectory always converges. Here we briefly recount a more general version as formulated in
[
Ioffe08
]
.
Assume that
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is compact and
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times U\to\mathbb{R}
are such that
f
and each
g(\cdot,u)
are convex continuous.
The optimization problem reads:

(3.11)

\min_{x\in X}f(x)\quad\text{such that $g(x,u)\leq 0$, for every $u\in U$}.

A common strategy is to introduce a so-called
barrier function

\beta:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, where
\beta
is finite for strictly feasible
x
and equal to
\infty
otherwise, and to study the auxiliary problem with a parameter
\mu>0
:

(3.12)

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)+\mu\beta(x).

Let
x(\mu)
denote a solution to the auxiliary problem (called the
central path
); this might not exist or be uniquely defined.
Under this setup, the main result follows easily from
Lemma

3.21
:

•

Suppose
f,g,\beta
are definable in an o-minimal structure. If
x(\mu)
is well-defined and bounded for sufficiently small
\mu>0
, then the central path
x(\mu)
converges as
\mu\downarrow 0
.

A similar result was also proved in
[
Halicka02
]
in the semialgebraic setting using the so-called
curve selection lemma
, a version of
3.6
. See
[
DrummondPeterzil02
,
Halicka02
]
for a further discussion of the history of this problem.

We also have a set-valued version of
Lemma

3.21
:

Lemma 3.23

(Existence of one-sided Painlevé-Kuratowski limits)
.

Suppose
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is definable, viewed as a definable family
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
of definable sets
X_{t}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
t\in\mathbb{R}
). Then the one-sided Painlevéve-Kuratowski limit (cf.
[
rockafellar2009variational
, 4]
) of this family exists and is definable:

(3.13)

\textstyle\liminf_{t\downarrow 0}X_{t}\ =\ \limsup_{t\downarrow 0}X_{t}.

As an application, two notions of
tangent cone
(cf.
[
rockafellar2009variational
, 6A]
) always coincide in the tame setting:

Lemma 3.24

(Tangent cone)
.

If
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is definable, then
Q
is geometrically derivable, i.e., the tangent cone
T_{Q}(x)
of
Q
at a point
x\in Q
equals the derivable tangent cone
T_{Q}^{\operatorname{der}}(x)
, i.e.,

(3.14)

\textstyle\limsup_{t\downarrow 0}t^{-1}(Q-x)\ =\ \liminf_{t\downarrow 0}t^{-1}(Q-x).

Higher-order versions of this fact are true as well, for instance, every definable set is
parabolically derivable
in the sense of
[
rockafellar2009variational
, Definition 13.11]
. For more on coincidence of tangent cones in the tame setting see
[
KurdykaLeGalNguyen18
]
.

Thus in general we should be on the lookout for any properties of the form
\liminf_{t\downarrow 0}X_{t}=\limsup_{t\downarrow 0}X_{t}
for some
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, as these are guaranteed to hold in the tame setting.

We now turn our attention to the nature of 1-variable definable functions.

Monotonicity Theorem 3.25
.

[
driesTameTopologyOminimal1998
, 3.1.2]

If
f:(a,b)\to\mathbb{R}
is definable, then there exist points
a=a_{0}<a_{1}<\cdots<a_{k}<a_{k+1}=b
such that each restriction
f|_{(a_{i},a_{i+1})}
is continuous, and either constant or strictly monotone.

In fact, we can achieve an arbitrarily high finite degree of smoothness:

Smooth Monotonicity Theorem 3.26
.

Given
r\geq 1
, if
f:(a,b)\to\mathbb{R}
is definable, then there exist points
a=a_{0}<a_{1}<\cdots<a_{k}<a_{k+1}=b
in
(a,b)
such that each restriction
f|_{(a_{i},a_{i+1})}
is
C^{r}
-smooth, and either constant or strictly monotone.

In order to quickly prove the
3.26
, we recall the classical Lebesgue’s Theorem (1904) from real analysis:

If
f:(a,b)\to\mathbb{R}
is monotone, then
f
is differentiable a.e. on
(a,b)
.

Proof.

Let
r=1
. By the
3.25
, we can assume
f
is monotone.
Since the points of (non)differentiability are definable (exercise!), and measure zero by Lebesgue’s Theorem, we have from the
3.5
that there are only finitely many points of non-differentiability. The case
r\geq 2
follows by induction.
∎

Here is a more subtle property. Recall from freshman calculus that
l’Hopital’s rule
gives a one-direction implication, under some
indeterminate form
assumption. The reverse implication does not hold in general in the “naive” direction, as the following example shows:

(3.15)

\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{t^{2}\sin t^{-1}}{t}\ =\ 0\quad\text{however}\quad\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{(t^{2}\sin t^{-1})^{\prime}}{t^{\prime}}\ =\ \lim_{t\downarrow 0}(2t\sin t^{-1}-t^{-1})\quad\text{does not exist}

In the tame setting we have:

Lemma 3.27

(Reverse l’Hopital’s rule)
.

Suppose
\phi,\psi:(0,\varepsilon)\to\mathbb{R}
are definable with
\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\phi(t)=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\psi(t)=0
, and assume
\psi^{\prime}(t)>0
as
t\downarrow 0
. Then for
\ell\in\mathbb{R}
we have:

(3.16)

\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{\phi(t)}{\psi(t)}\ =\ \ell\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{\phi^{\prime}(t)}{\psi^{\prime}(t)}\ =\ \ell.

Remark about
Lemma

3.27
.

The version here is from
Tame functions are semismooth
(2009)
[
bolte2009tame
, Lemma 1]
by Bolte, Daniilidis, and Lewis, and was the crucial observation needed to prove their main theorem that all definable locally lipschitz functions are semismooth
[
bolte2009tame
, Theorem 1]
; see also
Remark

3.29
below.

The history of
Lemma

3.27
and its various disguised forms dates back already to the connection between o-minimality and so-called
Hardy fields
, e.g.,
[
Miller94
,
van1994elementary
]
(1994), see also
[
MillerFields12
, §3]
. The Hardy field
H(\mathcal{R})
of
\mathcal{R}
is the ordered differential field of germs at
+\infty
of
1
-variable definable functions
\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. Hardy fields are important invariants of o-minimal structures as they capture all possible asymptotic behavior of 1-variable definable functions.

More recently, Aschenbrenner, van den Dries and van der Hoeven have introduced an abstract setting of so-called
H
-fields
designed to capture the first-order properties of Hardy fields.
Their work
Asymptotic differential algebra and model theory of transseries
(2017)
[
ADAMTT
]
culminates in an analogue of the
2.4
in a certain natural framework for asymptotic algebraic differential equations over
H
-fields (in the same way semialgebraic geometry is a natural framework for polynomial equations over
\mathbb{R}
).

In the theory of
H
-fields,
Lemma

3.27
shows up as e.g.,
[
ADAMTT
, 10.1.4]
. This usage in the
H
-field setting (which includes the Hardy field setting, and thus the o-minimal setting) can be traced back to at least as early as 1979 in

On the value group of a differential valuation

[
rosenlicht1979value
]
by Rosenlicht.
∎

As already alluded to, in theory one can characterize the asymptotics which might occur in an o-minimal structure. The simplest version of this is:

Definition 3.28
.

[
MillerFields12
]

We say that
\mathcal{R}
is
polynomially bounded
if for every definable
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that eventually
|f(t)|\leq t^{N}
as
t\to+\infty
. This is equivalent
[
MillerFields12
, Theorem 1.2]
to the seemingly stronger property: for every eventually nonzero definable
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, there exists an exponent
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and
C\in\mathbb{R}
such that:

(3.17)

f(t)\ \sim\ Ct^{\gamma}\quad\text{as $t\to+\infty$}.

We define the
field of exponents
of
\mathcal{R}
to be the set of all
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
such that the function
t^{\gamma}:(0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is definable in
\mathcal{R}
; it can be checked that this is in fact a subfield of
\mathbb{R}
.

The structures
\mathbb{R}_{\operatorname{alg}}
and
\mathbb{R}_{\operatorname{an}}
are polynomially bounded with field of exponents
\mathbb{Q}
(see
[
vandenDries86Ran
]
for
\mathbb{R}_{\operatorname{an}}
). The structure
\mathbb{R}_{\operatorname{alg}}^{\mathbb{R}}
mentioned in (2) of
Remark

3.19
is polynomially bounded with field of exponents
\mathbb{R}
. Any structure containing the exponential function, for instance
\mathbb{R}_{\exp}
,
\mathbb{R}_{\operatorname{an},\exp}
, and
\mathbb{R}_{\operatorname{Pfaff}}
, will not be polynomially bounded and automatically have field of exponents
\mathbb{R}
. To characterize the asymptotics of o-minimal structures which are not polynomially bounded, one can use
Hardy fields
and
transseries
as demonstrated in
[
DMM97LEPS
]
.

Remark 3.29
.

The property
polynomially bounded
has useful practical consequences in optimization. We mention two of them here:

(1)

In
Tame functions are semismooth

[
bolte2009tame
, Theorem 2]
the authors essentially show that in a polynomially bounded setting, the nonsmooth Newton’s method converges superlinearly for definable locally lipschitz functions with an error at the
k
th step of
O(2^{-(1+\gamma)^{k}})
for some positive parameter
\gamma>0
coming from the field of exponents.

(2)

In
Section

4
below, we consider
qualitative
properties of the convergence of the subgradient method; we do not address the
rate
of convergence.
To analyze the rate, one often uses the so-called
Polyak-Łojasiewicz-Kurdyka (PLK) inequalities
(also known as
KL inequalities
); in this case, a polynomially bounded setting similarly can yield an exponent which can imply the superlinear convergence of a descent algorithm.
See
[
bentoConvergenceDescentOptimization2025
]
for an example of how this is done.

Remarkably, the exponential function is unavoidable in an o-minimal structure which is not polynomially bounded:

Exponential dichotomy 3.30
.

[
Miller94
]

Exactly one of the following is true:

(1)

\mathcal{R}
is polynomially bounded, or

(2)

the exponential function
\exp:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is definable in
\mathcal{R}
.

It is unknown if one can achieve growth rates faster than every iterated exponential in an o-minimal structure, i.e., every known o-minimal structure is
exponentially bounded
:

Open Question 3.31
.

Does there exist an o-minimal structure which defines a
transexponential
function? I.e., a function
E:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for every
n\geq 1
, eventually
E(t)\geq\exp_{n}(t)
as
t\to+\infty
, where
\exp_{1}:=\exp
and
\exp_{n+1}:=\exp\circ\exp_{n}
.

Progress has been made on the
Hardy field
fragment of this question in Padgett’s thesis
[
padgett2022sublogarithmic
]
. The existence of transexponential Hardy fields was shown by Boshernitzan in (1986)
[
boshernitzan1986hardy
]
. Recently, Aschenbrenner, van den Dries, and van der Hoeven proved the following remarkable result: all maximal Hardy fields (which are necessarily transexponential
[
boshernitzan1986hardy
]
) have the same first-order theory as differential fields, and moreover assuming the Continuum Hypothesis (CH) they are all isomorphic
[
aschenbrenner2025maximalhardyfields
]
.

3.4.
Dimension and stratifications

We now turn our attention to properties of definable sets of higher dimension, which commonly arise in deep learning.

The first statement partially extends the
3.5
into higher dimension and states that a coherent value of “dimension” can be assigned to every definable set.
We use the fiber notation; see

Dimension Theorem 3.32
.

Suppose
\mathcal{R}
is an o-minimal structure. There exists a unique function:

(3.18)

\dim\ :\ \{\text{definable sets}\}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{-\infty\}

such that:

(D1)

(a)

\dim(S)=-\infty\Leftrightarrow S=\varnothing
for definable
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
;

(b)

\dim(\{a\})=0
for all
a\in\mathbb{R}
;

(c)

\dim(\mathbb{R})=1
;

(D2)

\dim(S_{1}\cup S_{2})=\max\{\dim(S_{1}),\dim(S_{2})\}
for definable
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
;

(D3)

\dim
is preserved under permutation of coordinates;

(D4)

if
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is definable and we set
B_{i}\coloneqq\{a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\dim(S_{a})=i\}
for
i=0,1
, then
B_{i}
is definable and

(3.19)

\dim\big(\{(a,b)\in S:a\in B_{i}\}\big)\ =\ i+\dim(B_{i}),\quad\text{for $i=0,1$.}

Example 3.33
.

If
\mathcal{R}=(\mathbb{R};<,+,\cdot)

from

2.7

Remark 3.42

4.
Convergence of Subgradient Method

The previous section provided convergence guarantees at the elementary level of definable curves, including the existence of one-sided limits of definable curves, and the convergence of central path methods in optimization.
In this section, we illustrate how the properties of definable objects yield convergence guarantees through the example of Stochastic Subgradient Method (SSM), also known as Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) (in the case where the function is differentiable).
We provide in this section an exposition of known results, notably those in Davis
et al

[
davis2020stochastic
]
, and Bolte
et al

[
bolte2007clarke
]
.

In
Section

4.1
, we recall some notions of variational analysis, useful when dealing with nonsmooth objects.
In
Section

4.2
, we discuss a continuous time version of SSM, as a first step towards the discrete setting.
In
Section

4.3
, we move to the setting of interest, where the iteration is discrete.
Finally, we provide in
Section

4.4
complementary remarks that connect the discrete setting with implemented methods.

4.1.
Preliminaries from variational analysis

We first recall the notion of Clarke subdifferential and provide some context on its relevence in nonsmooth nonconvex optimization.
Then, we complete the notion of manifold, introduced in
Section

1.5
, with tangent and normal spaces, tangent metric, and Riemannian gradient.

The notion of gradient, valid and useful for differentiable functions, is well extended to the nondifferentiable setting by that of Clarke subdifferential.
This notion is applicable when the points where the function is not differentiable have measure zero.
Interestingly, this holds true as soon as the function is locally Lipschitz – this is Rademacher’s theorem
[
nekvindaSimpleProofRademacher1988
]
, and also by the stronger and independent stratification statement
Theorem

3.39
.

Definition 4.1
.

Suppose
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a locally Lipschitz function. The
Clarke subdifferential

\partial^{c}f
is the following set-valued map:

(4.1)

\partial^{c}f\ :\ \mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad x\ \mapsto\ \partial^{c}f(x)\ :=\ \operatorname{conv}\{\lim_{i\to\infty}\nabla f(x):\text{$x_{i}\to x$, $x_{i}\in\operatorname{Diff}(f)$}\},

where
\operatorname{Diff}(f)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the set of points
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
where
f
is (Fréchet) differentiable; see
Definition

. An element of the Clarke subdifferential is
called a
subgradient
.

The Clarke subdifferential captures (most of) the first-order geometry of a function.
In particular, it allows to formulate a necessary condition on the local minimizers of a function, known as Fermat’s rule generalized
[
rockafellar2009variational
, Th. 10.1]
.
Except in specific cases (e.g.,
Section

2.3
),
finding global, or even local minimizers of a general nonsmooth nonconvex function is too difficult.
In what follows, we thus settle for finding Clarke critical points.

Proposition 4.2

(Fermat’s rule)
.

If
\bar{x}
is a local minimizer of
f
, then
0\in\partial^{c}f(\bar{x})
.

We summarize below some useful properties of the Clarke subdifferential.

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a locally Lipschitz function and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

(1)

if
f
is definable in an o-minimal structure, then
\partial^{c}f
is also definable in that structure;

(2)

\partial^{c}f(x)
is convex, compact, and nonempty;

(3)

\partial^{c}f(x)
is upper semicontinuous, i.e., for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\eta>0
such that

(4.2)

\partial^{c}f(y)\subset\partial^{c}f(x)+{B}(0,\varepsilon),\quad\text{ for any }y\in{B}(x,\eta);

(4)

if
0\not\in\partial^{c}f(x)
, then there exists
\eta>0
such that

0\not\in\partial^{c}f(y)
for any
y\in{B}(x,\eta)
.

Proof.

Part i) is left as an exercice.
Part ii) is proved in
[
clarkeOptimizationNonsmoothAnalysis1990
, Proposition 2.1.2(a)]
, using Theorem 2.5.1
[
clarkeOptimizationNonsmoothAnalysis1990
]
to connect
Definition

4.1
to Clarke’s subdifferential definition
[
clarkeOptimizationNonsmoothAnalysis1990
, p. 27]
.
Part iii) is proved in
[
clarkeOptimizationNonsmoothAnalysis1990
, Proposition 2.1.5(d)]
.
Part iv) is an application of part ii):
since
0\not\in\partial^{c}f(x)
and
\partial^{c}f(x)
is a closed set, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
0\not\in\partial^{c}f(x)+{B}(0,\varepsilon)
.
By part iii), there exists
\eta>0
such that
\partial^{c}f(y)\subset\partial^{c}f(x)+{B}(0,\varepsilon)
for all
y\in{B}(x,\eta)
.
Hence,
0\not\in\partial^{c}f(y)
for all
y\in{B}(x,\eta)
.
∎

Consider a
d
-dimensional
C^{p}
-manifold
M
included in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and a point
x\in M
.
The

at

.
This is a

The

in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We denote the orthogonal projection onto

by
\proj_{T_{M}(x)}(\cdot)
.
Finally, we equip each tangent and normal space by the (restriction of the) scalar product of the ambient euclidean space

, thus making the manifold Riemannian.

Consider a
d
-dimensional
C^{p}
-manifold
M
included in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a point
x\in M
, and a function
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
.
The

of

\operatorname{D}f(x):T_{M}(x)\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
\operatorname{D}f(x)[\eta]:=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}f\circ c(t)\right|_{t=0}
when that limit exists, where

.
When

is differentiable relative to

, and call

.

is continuously differentiable, or

, on

is differentiable relative at

When

is definable, then so are

We distinguish between the euclidean gradient

We choose not to include

Moreover, if there exists a function

.

4.2.
Descent of subgradient curves in continuous time

In this section, we consider a definable locally Lipschitz function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and an absolutely continuous
curve
x:[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

(
\text{SSM}^{\text{cont}}
)

x^{\prime}(t)\ \in\ -\partial^{c}f(x(t)),\quad\text{for a.e. $t\geq 0$}.

Note that, while
f
and
\partial^{c}
are definable,

is definable;
thus the following exposition does not assume

to be definable.
We present the statement and proof ideas of Davis, Drusvyatskiy, Kakade and Lee
[
davis2020stochastic
]
, along with earlier results of Bolte, Daniilidis, Lewis and Shiota
[
bolte2007clarke
]
.
The purpose of this section is to show that

decreases along trajectories of
Eq.

.
Specifically, we aim to prove the following result.

Proposition 4.4

(Subgradient descent)
.

Suppose
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a definable locally Lipschitz function and
x:[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an absolutely continuous curve satisfying
Eq.

SSM
cont

.
Then,

(4.3)

f(x(t))\ =\ f(x(0))-\int_{0}^{t}\operatorname{dist}^{2}(0,\partial^{c}f(x(\tau)))d\tau.

In particular,
f\circ x
is non-increasing.

The following is a stratification result for functions; it extends the basic
Lemmas

2.5
and
3.39
.
This result provides the existence of a partition of the space into
C^{1}
-manifolds, over which the function behaves smoothly.
In addition, any Clarke subgradient decomposes as the as a sum of two perpendicular vectors: some normal vector and the Riemannian gradient.
This was first proved by Bolte
et al

[

, Proposition 4]
.

Projection formula 4.5
.

Suppose
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a definable locally Lipschitz function. Then there exists a definable
C^{1}
-stratification
\mathfrak{S}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that for each stratum
M\in\mathfrak{S}
, we have that
f|_{M}
is
C^{1}
and

(4.4)

\partial^{c}f(x)\subset\nabla_{R}f(x)+N_{M}(x),\quad\text{ for every }x\in M.

Proof.

This result follows from
[
bolte2007clarke
, Proposition 4]
;
[

,

]
also provide similar statements.
First,

is a definable function, thus it admits a Whitney stratification

[

; see also

.
Second,

is locally Lipschitz continuous, thus it is lower semicontinuous.
Consequently, the conclusion of
[

is equivalent to the claim.
∎

When

Consider the setting of

If there exists a vector

Let

, and

We thus have that

, and

∎

(a)

\nabla_{R}f(x)\in\partial^{c}f(x)

Chain rule 4.7
.

Suppose

4.3.
Stochastic Subgradient Method converges on tame functions: a simplified, deterministic, exposition

We note that the convergence of SSM on general definable functions is a surprisingly recent result (2020, in Davis
et al

[
davis2020stochastic
]
), and it fundamentally depends on properties guaranteed by o-minimality.
We outline the general ideas of the proof, with particular emphasis on the aspects where o-minimality plays a critical role.
For the sake of clarity and length, we focus here on a noiseless full-batch setting, and discuss extensions to noisy and minibatch settings in the next section.

Setting the stage, we consider finding a Clarke critical point of a given function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, that is a point
\bar{x}
such that
0\in\partial^{c}f(\bar{x})
.
To that end, we consider using the Stochastic Subgradient Method, defined as:

(

Our main application case is the training of DNNs, which involves, for example, the minimization problem
Eq.

1.2
.
For simplicity, we consider an idealized setting where

is an element of the Clarke subdifferential of a full-batch of data.
This simplified setting suffices to discuss the features of o-minimality.
Extensions to small-batch settings and Automatic Differentiation are discussed in

and

.

The convergence result is given below
[

, Corollary 5.9]
.

Proposition 4.8
.

Suppose

4.4.
The broader picture around Stochastic Subgradient Method

We presented above a convergence proof for a simplified version of Stochastic Subgradient Method, described in

.
Here, we discuss how the gap between this simplified setting and practice can be bridged.

4.4.1.
On the need to account for nonsmoothness

Rademacher’s theorem states that, if a function
f
is Lispchitz continuous, then the set of points where it is nonsmooth has measure zero.
Therefore optimization algorithms are unlikely to encounter such points–especially so when the iteration features noise, as is the case of
Eq.

SSM
discrete

.
One might then be led to give credit to the following statement, or a variation of it.

Since with probability one, the function is differentiable at iterates, everything happens as if the function was differentiable, and thus smooth convergence arguments apply directly.

Unfortunately, things are not so simple.
Indeed, methods for smooth optimization have vastly different behaviors on nonsmooth functions, as we now illustrate.

Section

4.4.1
displays the behavior of three optimization methods on a particular nonsmooth function.
Overall, the behavior of the methods is driven by the nonsmoothness of the function (its stratification), and particularly so by
M^{\star}
, the stratum that contains the minimizer.
Stochastic Subgradient Method incurs strong oscillations perpendicular to
M^{\star}
.
The BFGS method, a state-of-the-art method for minimizing
C^{1}
functions with superlinear convergence speed, incurs oscillations and ultimately converges linearly at best on nonsmooth functions
[
lewisNonsmoothOptimizationQuasiNewton2013
]
.
The proximal gradient method can be applied only to a restricted class of nonsmooth functions, including that of
Section

4.4.1

[
beckFirstOrderMethodsOptimization2017
]
.
Contrary to SSM and BFGS, it generates iterates that are attracted to nonsmooth points – the cartesian axes here.
Even more, the iterates belong to
M^{\star}
after a finite number of iterations; this can be used to accelerate the proximal gradient method
[
