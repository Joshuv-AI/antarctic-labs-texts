Deciduous Teeth Extraction Practice and Associated Factors Among Under Five‐Years‐Old Children in the Gambella Region, South West, Ethiopia: A Mixed Study
Authors: Takele Shilu Demewoz, Gizachew Worku Dagnew, Zemenu Shiferaw Yadita
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsDeciduous teeth extraction (DTE) is a harmful traditional practice often performed by traditional healers. This practice can lead to both acute and chronic complications that significantly impact children's health. Despite national strategies and initiatives, DTE remains prevalent in Ethiopia, especially in the Gambella region. However, there is limited information on its prevalence and determinants in this area.MethodBoth qualitative and quantitative studies were conducted. A community‐based cross‐sectional study involved 803 mothers with children under five in the Gambella region. Multistage simple random sampling was employed, and data were collected using a pretested structured questionnaire and in‐depth interviews. Data entry was performed using Epi Data version 4.6, and the analysis was conducted in SPSS version 25. Both bivariable and multivariable binary logistic regression analyses were used to identify factors associated with deciduous tooth extraction (DTE). Odds ratios were calculated with a 95% confidence interval, and a p‐value of less than 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.ResultThe prevalence of Deciduous teeth extraction practice among < 5 years was 48.3% (95% CI: 0.44–0.51). Mothers lack of awareness (AOR = 2.08; 95 CI:1.21–3.58), mothers lack of information on scientific solutions of teething symptoms (AOR = 2.08; 95% CI: 1.21–3.60), mother's positive attitude towards milk tooth extraction (AOR = 10.06; 95%CI: 6.36–15.91), being ill during teething eruption (AOR = 8.70; 95% CI: 5.56–13.62) and Community DTE tradition (AOR = 2.06; 95% CI: 1.34–3.15) were significantly associated. The qualitative findings also found that culture, misconception about DTE, peer and social pressure as factors.ConclusionsDeciduous teeth extraction practice in the Gambella region poses a significant public health concern. Lack of awareness regarding the consequences and scientific solutions for teething symptoms, mothers' attitudes, illness perceptions during teething, and community traditions were identified factors. Hence, comprehensive awareness campaigns involving all stakeholders should be emphasized. Health care providers should play a crucial role to educate both mothers and traditional healers.Keywords: deciduous teeth extraction, Ethiopia, gambella, under five children

Introduction

Deciduous teeth extraction (DTE) is one of the most common harmful traditional practices performed by traditional healers without anesthesia and unsterile materials [1]. DTE practice is a procedure in which early teeth or un‐erupted deciduous tooth follicles of a child are pulled out after an incision is made on the gum [2]. The reason to conduct milk tooth extraction is thought to be a cause of vomiting, diarrhea, fever, and stunted growth in infants [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Instruments such as razor blades and wire are used by traditional healers, traditional birth attendants, and children's parents [3]. The tooth that is most likely to be extracted is deciduous, and it is almost always bilateral and lower [7]. It is referred to locally as Ebino, nylon teeth, tooth bud extraction, deciduous tooth extraction, deciduous canine tooth extraction, and oral mutilation by various cultures [8, 9].

Deciduous teeth extraction practice is immediate and long term complication. Immediate complication includes excessive bleeding, anemia, septicemia, HIV/AIDS, hepatitis, meningitis, tetanus and injury of the tongue [5]. Most immediate complications are fatal which lead to shock and even death [10]. Long effects impacts include the absence of permanent teeth growth, wrong (deformed) teeth, and hyperplastic and displacement teeth [5, 8, 10].

Measures against the practice of DTE educating people about the complications of milk teeth extraction focusing the danger of blood borne infections like HIVAIDS [5, 10]. According to UN sustainable developments goals, Target 5.3 outlines strategies to eliminate harmful practices, including child, early, and forced marriage, as well as female genital mutilation [11]. Eight East & Central Africa countries including Central African republic, Uganda, Rwanda, Kenya and others, have strategies to end infants oral mutulatition within 10 years [12].

Globally, deciduous teeth extractions less common in industrialized countries. However, it has been detected in dental examinations of children of African migrants in Israel and Sweden [9]. Child deciduous teeth extraction is a widespread and dangerous traditional practice affecting 25 million children in East Africa [13]. This due to the fact that those east Africa countries have low socioeconomic condition, deep‐rooted cultural attitudes and poor quality health care systems [14, 15]. Deciduous teeth extraction practices have a significant impact on children's health. A study revealed that false tooth extraction contributes up to 18% of child mortality in Uganda [10].

In Ethiopia where traditional practices are performed in more than 80% of the population and this contributed by religious leaders, government bodies and the victim people themselves. Traditional Practices are very resistant to change [5]. According to the National Baseline Survey on HTPs carried out in 1997, Deciduous teeth extraction is practiced in more than 80.2% of the general population [5]. MTE is the most common bad traditional practice in Ethiopia that ranges 31.7% to over 80% overall prevalence [4, 16, 17]. The main reason of practicing this bad traditional practice were misconception about the causes of diarrhea [16, 18]. Age of the child, maternal occupation, paternal education, Residence, positive attitude, intentions towards MTE and others were found to be contributing factors for MTE practice [6, 7, 12, 17].

Even though there are different strategies, policies, and initiative towards prevention harmful traditional practices in Ethiopia, Deciduous teeth extraction continue to be a common traditional practice in the Gambella region. There is paucity of evidence, due to the fact that majorities of the studies focused in the urban areas and most of them are institutional based studies. To develop appropriate, locally sensitive and effective preventive measures for DTE practices, the extent of the problem and the contributing factors should be investigated. Therefore, this study was aimed at assessing DTE, to identify associated factors among under than 5 years old children in the Abobo district, Gambella regional state of Ethiopia.

Methods

Study Design, Setting and Period

This study was conducted in Abobo Distric of Gambella regional state, South West Ethiopia. The district has 17 rural and 2 urban kebeles (the smallest administrative unit) with a total population of 27,788. There were 3751 under five children in the district. A Community‐based cross‐sectional study design triangulated with phenomenological qualitative study design was conducted from March 20th to April 20th, 2023 (A mixed study).

Population

The source population were all under 5‐year‐old children along with their mothers resides in Abobo district of Gambella regional state, Ethiopia. While the study population were all under 5‐year‐old children along with their mothers resides in the randomly selected kebels of Abobo district, Gambella regional state, Ethiopia. The study units were all randomly selected under 5‐year‐old children along with their mothers resides in the randomly selected kebels of Abobo district, Gambella regional state, Ethiopia.

Inclusion Criteria

All under‐5‐year‐old children whose mothers (legal guardians) had lived in the study area for at least 6 months.

Exclusion Criteria

Mothers or caregivers who were critical ill and unable to communicate were excluded from the study.

Sample Size Determination

Both single and double population proportion sample size determination techniques were employed in the study. The double population proportion technique involved considering different significant factors using Epi Info version 7 software. Meanwhile, the single population proportion formula was utilized by incorporating the prevalence of DTE from a previous study. Upon comparison of the two approaches, it was determined that the largest sample size was obtained based on the single population proportion formula. Therefore, the final sample size was estimated using the single population proportion formula, assuming a 95% confidence interval, reliability coefficient (Z a/2 = 1.96), a 5% margin of error, a design effect of 2, a nonresponse rate of 10%, and a prevalence of DTE practice of 58.68% [7] resulting in a final sample size of 818.

Sampling Procedure

The study employed a multistage sampling technique to select participants from the target population. Out of the 17 rural and 2 urban kebeles within the district, 6 rural and 1 urban kebele were chosen using a lottery method. The total number of eligible children under 5 years old in each selected kebele was obtained from health extension workers and kebele administrative authorities, and the sample size was proportionally allocated to these selected kebeles. Subsequently, a simple random sampling technique utilizing computer‐generated random numbers was employed to select the study participants, ensuring a comprehensive representation of both rural and urban areas in the Gambella region.

Study Variable

•The independent variables include; Socio‐demographic characteristics, Health service related factors, Environmental/community related factor, Peer pressure, Mothers ‘information and attitudes.

Data Collection Tool and Procedure

For the quantitative aspect, a structured questionnaire was developed based on existing literature [1, 8, 9, 12, 15, 16, 19]. The questionnaire underwent translation from English to Amharic (the local language) and back to English to ensure consistency. It covered socio‐demographic details, environmental factors, health service‐related aspects, mother's information on milk teeth extraction, and attitudes towards the practice. In the qualitative segment, an in‐depth interview guide was prepared in English, translated into Amharic, and discussions conducted in local languages. Mobile recorders were employed for data collection after obtaining participants' permission, with open‐ended questions posed in quiet settings on a one‐to‐one basis. The guide included both topical and probing inquiries. Seven diploma nurses and two nurses holding a bachelor degree served as data collectors and supervisors, respectively. Pretesting of the questionnaire was carried out on 5% of the sample size to ensure data quality, while both data collectors and supervisors underwent 2 days of training. Supervisors and principal investigators oversaw the data collection process closely, and collected data were thoroughly reviewed for completeness before entry.

Measurement

Deciduous teeth extraction practice: It is the procedure of pulling out the early teeth of index children. It measured by asking the mother by preparing yes or no questionnaire.

A positive attitude (favorable) towards Deciduous teeth extraction: means when they scored greater than 50% among six attitude questions. It has five Likert‐scale questions; strongly agree score five, agree scores four, not sure scores three, disagree scores two and strongly disagree scores one [7].

A negative attitude (unfavorable) towards Deciduous teeth extraction: means when they scored less than 50% among six attitude questions. It has five Likert‐scale questions; strongly agree score five, agree scores four, not sure scores three, disagree scores two and strongly disagree scores one.

Mothers who had information about scientific solutions mean when the mothers answers one from the following ‐gently rubbing the affected gum by cleaned figure, by applying anesthetic cream, use of counter pain mediation (pediatric preparation of ibuprofen), tether to bite on (rubber like rings), wet clean cloth with cold water and let the child to bite on it [20].

Data Processing and Analysis

The data underwent rigorous checks for completeness, consistency, and appropriate coding before being inputted into EpiData software version 4.6 and subsequently exported to SPSS version 25 for further analysis. A comprehensive descriptive statistical analysis was conducted, encompassing the computation of frequencies, percentages, and means, with results presented through tables, graphs, and charts for clarity. Identification of factors influencing the study variables was achieved through binary logistic regression, commencing with bivariable analysis followed by multivariable regression. Variables with a significance level below 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression, where adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals were computed, considering p < 0.05 as statistically significant. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer and Lemeshow goodness of fit test, yielding a p = 0.442, while checks for normality and chi‐square assumptions were also conducted. Additionally, multi‐collinearity was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (0.62) and tolerance tests. For the qualitative component, a thematic analysis was performed manually, involving the transcription of audio recordings into Amharic text, translation into English, followed by coding, data reduction, and interpretation.

Ethical Approval and Consent to Participate

A written ethical approval letter (Protocol Number: 693/2023, Assigned No: 003) was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Bahir Dar University, College of Medicine and Health Sciences, on March 6, 2023. Additionally, a supporting letter was secured from the Gambella Regional Health Bureau. Written consent was asked from each study participant before data collection. For those participants who were unable to read the consent, a verbal explanation was given to them and their consent was recorded by the data collectors. They were informed about the objective of the study, the confidentiality of their data, and the right to refuse participation. Names and other personal identification of participants were not included throughout the study process and in the final report to ensure anonymity.

Results

Socio‐Demographic Characteristics

A total of 803 mothers/caregivers, along with their under‐5‐year‐old children, participated in the study, yielding a response rate of 98.2%. The mean age of the mothers/caregivers was 27.77 years ± 6.04 (standard deviation), while the mean age of the children was 20.83 months + 14 (Standard deviation). Approximately half of the mothers (49.6%) fell into the age group of 20–29 years, and nearly half (42.8%) of them had children under 12 months old. The majority of mothers identified as Protestant (78.5%) and were married (95%), with the largest proportion (80.6%) being housewives. Moreover, a substantial portion of mothers (65.3%) had no formal education, similar to fathers (62.6%), the majority of whom worked as farmers (69%) and resided in rural areas (71.1%). Additionally, the study found that 71.1% of respondents reported a monthly income of less than 2500 Ethiopian birr (Table 1).

Mothers' Information and Attitudes Towards Deciduous Teeth Extraction

Among the total participants' mothers, 335 (41.7%) had information about the consequences of DTE practice. Additionally, from the overall respondents, 273 (34%) possessed knowledge about scientific solutions for DTE practice. The majority (72.9%) acquired this information from health workers, while 18 (6.5%) obtained it from close relatives, 28 (10.3%) from friends, and 28 (10.3%) from mass media (Table 3). Additionally, among the study participants, 517 (64.4%) mothers exhibited a positive attitude toward milk teeth extraction. Furthermore, 163 (20.3%) agreed that milk teeth extraction practice should continue, while 216 (26.9%) believed that failure to extract milk teeth could result in a child's death. Moreover, 165 (20.5%) supported milk teeth extraction, while 189 (23.5%) considered it harmful. Furthermore, out of 803 respondents, 259 (32.3%) believed that milk teeth extraction is an effective treatment for childhood illnesses like diarrhea and vomiting (Table 4).

Prevalence of Deciduous Teeth Extraction Practice on Under 5 Years Old Children

The prevalence of deciduous teeth extraction practice among children under 5 years old in the Abobo district was 48.3% (95% CI: 0.448, 0.517), with the most common period for extraction being between 5 and 8 months, accounting for 70.4% of cases. One‐third of the decisions regarding deciduous teeth extraction (34.3%) were made jointly by the child's father and mother. Among the total children who underwent deciduous teeth extraction, 372 (95.8%) procedures were conducted by traditional healers, with approximately half (46%) of the mothers learning about the practice from friends. Moreover, around two‐thirds (73.3%) of the materials used for deciduous teeth extraction were “wosfie” (a small metal rod with sharp tip). Additionally, peer pressure was reported by nearly three‐quarters (71.3%) of mothers to practice milk teeth extraction. The primary reason cited for extracting deciduous teeth, as reported by 78.3% of cases, was the development of diarrhea in the child. Furthermore, of the total children who underwent deciduous teeth extraction, 54% of the mothers expressed an intention to continue the practice in the future (Table 5).

Factors Associated With DTE

On bivariable binary logistic regression analysis, several factors including the education levels of the mother and father, the father's occupation, household income, distance from health facilities, the child being ill during teething eruption, availability of transport, availability of TV or radio, lack of information on the consequences of milk teeth extraction, lack of information on scientific solutions for teething symptoms, milk teeth extraction (DTE) being a tradition in the community, and mothers' positive attitudes towards DTE were considered as candidate variables for multivariable binary logistic regression if their p‐values were less than 0.25. Upon conducting multivariable logistic regression analysis, it was found that mothers who lacked information on the consequences of milk teeth extraction, lacked information on scientific solutions for teething symptoms, had children who were ill during teething eruption, lived in communities where DTE was a tradition, and exhibited positive attitudes towards DTE showed statistically significant associations with DTE practice among children under 5 years old at a p‐value less than 0.05.

Mothers who lacked information on the consequences of deciduous teeth extraction were twice as likely to extract their child's deciduous teeth compared to those who had such information (AOR = 2.082; 95% CI: 1.211–3.580). Similarly, mothers who lacked information about scientific solutions were also twice as likely to extract their child's deciduous teeth compared to those who had information (AOR = 2.089; 95% CI: 1.211–3.602). Furthermore, mothers with positive attitudes towards deciduous teeth extraction were 10 times more likely to extract their child's deciduous teeth compared to those with negative attitudes (AOR = 10.065; 95% CI: 6.363–15.919).

Children who were ill during teething eruption were nearly nine times more likely to have their deciduous teeth extracted compared to those who were not ill during teething eruption (AOR = 8.703; 95% CI: 5.560–13.625). Similarly, there was a significant association observed between MTE practice and MTE being a tradition in the community; children whose mothers lived in communities where MTE was a tradition were twice as likely to have their deciduous teeth extracted compared to those whose mothers lived in communities where MTE was not a tradition (AOR = 2.062; 95% CI: 1.349–3.150) (Table 6).

Qualitative Study Result

Socio Demographic Characteristics of Qualitative Study Participants

In a qualitative study, a total of 8 participants were involved in in‐depth interviews. Regarding gender, half of the respondents were female. Among these participants, four were government employees, two were farmers, and two were housewives. In terms of age, half of the participants, 4 were between 30 and 39 years old, while 2 were between 20 and 29 years old, and the remaining were above 40 years old. Considering education level, four had higher education, two were unable to read or write, one had primary education, and the rest had secondary education. Ethnically, half of the participants, four were Ayauk, two were Kembata, one was Hidiya, and the rest were Wolayta.

Community's Beliefs and Cultural Factors Towards Deciduous Teeth Extraction

The majority of participants in the qualitative study expressed the belief that deciduous teeth extraction cannot be cured by modern medical treatments. For instance, a 39‐year‐old teacher stated, “The societies don't think that teething can be cured by medical treatment” (II‐2). Similarly, another participant, a 32‐year‐old health extension worker, explained, “My point of view is that because it cannot be cured medically by treatment, my baby teeth were extracted” (II‐7).

The findings indicate that deciduous teeth extraction (MTE) practice is deeply entrenched in the cultural traditions of the community. For instance, a 40‐year‐old father expressed, “Extraction of deciduous teeth of the infants is cultural. Our great fathers, our mothers have been doing this deciduous teething. We will do this while hearing from them” (II‐1). Similarly, a 45‐year‐old traditional healer stated, “Extracting of deciduous teeth is culturally common. We are learned from our ancestors” (II‐5). These statements underscore the generational transmission and acceptance of DTE practice as a cultural norm, passed down through families and upheld as part of the community's heritage. Pressure were facilitated factors for deciduous teeth extraction practice on less than 5 years old children. For instance, one participant's in in depth interview stated that: “For reasons that prompt the extraction of deciduous teeth due to pressure from our friends and it learnt from our friends.” (32 years old health extension workers in II‐7.

Community Knowledge Towards Deciduous Teeth Extraction Practice

The qualitative findings highlight a notable lack of information regarding deciduous teeth extraction (MTE) practice among children under 5 years old. For example, a 39‐year‐old male participant expressed, “Still, the communities are extracting the deciduous tooth. There is due to a lack of awareness in this area” (II‐2). Similarly, a 32‐year‐old health extension worker stated, “We have no knowledge of what caused the deciduous tooth's extraction” (II‐7). Additionally, participants emphasized the absence of scientific solutions for teething symptoms. One participant mentioned, “Baby teeth cannot be cured with medical treatment. As a society, there must be a scientifically proven solution to stop it” (II‐2). These perspectives underscore the need for increased awareness and access to evidence‐based information regarding alternatives to traditional MTE practices for addressing teething symptoms in young children.

Community's Attitudes Towards Milk Teeth Extraction Practice

The majority of study participants in the qualitative study expressed a positive attitude towards milk teeth extraction among children under 5 years old in their community. For instance, a 39‐year‐old mother who is a housewife stated, “My opinion is that having baby teeth extraction is important. If the baby is not deciduous teethextracted and the fever does not stop. When milk teeth come out, my child becomes healthy immediately” (II‐3). Similarly, a 39‐year‐old male teacher shared, “My opinion about the extraction of milk teeth is very important. After I extracted the baby deciduous teeth, she became healthy. This deciduous teething extraction should continue” (II‐2). These statements reflect a belief among participants that deciduous teeth extraction is beneficial for alleviating health issues in children and should be continued as a practice in their community.

Participants Response on the Magnitude of MTE

Participants in the qualitative study overwhelmingly indicated that DTE is a common practice among children under 5 years old in their community. One participant, a 40‐year‐old father, stated, “Extraction of deciduous tooth is common for this area. It is being widely implemented” (II‐1). Similarly, a 29‐year‐old health extension worker mentioned, “The community deciduous tooth extraction practice among under 5 years old mostly occurs in rural areas” (II‐8).

Communities Reasons for Milk Teeth Extraction

Regarding the reasons for deciduous teeth extraction, almost all participants believed that children in the communities were affected by diarrhea, vomiting, fevers, and poor growth. For example, a 39‐year‐old mother who is a housewife explained, “The root cause of diarrhea is dirty teeth. If this dirty tooth doesn't come out, the child will become thinner, and its growth will be stunted” (II‐3). Another mother, aged 25, shared, “The reason for my baby teeth removal was my daughter having diarrhea. After this, my daughter became thinner. Then I went to a traditional healer and had it pulled” (II‐4). Additionally, a 37‐year‐old traditional healer stated, “The reasons for deciduous teeth extraction are due to dirty teeth. This is teeth with white patches in the gum. When a dirty tooth is retained, it produces a worm. This dirty tooth can cause green diarrhea and vomiting” (II‐6). These responses highlight the community's perception that deciduous teeth extraction is necessary to address health issues such as diarrhea and vomiting caused by retained milk teeth.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to assess the prevalence of (DTE and associated factors among children under 5 years old in the Abobo district, South West Ethiopia. The prevalence of DTE practice among children under 5 years old in Abobo district was found to be 48.3% (95% CI: 0.448, 0.517), a figure consistent with a baseline survey study conducted in North Gondar (48.1%) [21]. However, it was lower than studies conducted in Alle Special Woreda (58.5%) [7], Dembia district (82.6%) [17], Gondar University (81.9%) [6], Tanzania (60%) [22], Uganda 90.3% [23, 24] and in Kenya (78%) [25]. The disparity with studies conducted in Ethiopia and Tanzania may be attributed to differences in customs, culture, time, and community awareness levels. On the other hand, the observed difference with studies conducted in Gondar University, Uganda, and Kenya might be because those studies focused on urban residences, whereas this study included participants from both rural and urban areas.

Conversely, the prevalence of DTE practice in this study was higher than studies conducted in Debre Tabor (11.1%) [19], South Achefer (31.7%) [3], Axum (12.5%) [26], Mpumudde Uganda (28.8%) [27], Kenyatta National Hospital (14.9%) [28], and Kajiado district in Kenya (24.5%) [1], Sudan (10.6%) [29], Israel (12.5%) [30], and among East African origin Sweden residents (21%) [5]. The observed differences could be attributed to variations in participants' residences (rural vs. urban), culture, education level, studied population, community awareness, and government commitment. These findings were supported by qualitative data, where the majority of participants affirmed that DTE was widely practiced among children under 5 years old in the Abobo district.

Mothers who lacked information on the consequences of milk teeth extraction were found to be two times more likely to extract their child's milk teeth than those who had such information. This finding was consistent with studies conducted in South Achefer and Debre Tabor town [3, 19] suggesting similarities in socio‐demographic characteristics of participants such as education status of caregivers and occupation of the mother. Lack of awareness emerged as a significant reason for milk teeth extraction in the community, as indicated by qualitative study participants.

Similarly, mothers who lacked information about scientific solutions were also found to be two times more likely to extract their child's milk teeth than those who had such information. This finding was consistent with a study conducted in South Achefer [3] suggesting similarities in socio‐demographic characteristics of participants. Qualitative data further supported this, with many participants indicating a belief that there was no medically proven treatment for milk teeth extraction, leading them to resort to traditional healers.

Moreover, mothers with a positive attitude towards milk teeth extraction were found to be ten times more likely to extract their child's milk teeth than those with a negative attitude. This finding was consistent with a study conducted in Alle Special Woreda [7] was supported by qualitative data indicating that favorable attitudes were a key factor driving MTE practice among children under 5 years old in the district.

Additionally, children who were ill during teething eruption were 8.7 times more likely to have their milk teeth extracted than those who were not ill during teething eruption. This may be attributed to the painful gums experienced during teething, leading to chewing and sucking behaviors that can cause bacterial infections, diarrhea, and subsequent milk teeth extraction [2].

Furthermore, children whose mothers lived in communities where DTE was a tradition were found to be two times more likely to have their milk teeth extracted than those whose mothers lived in communities where DTE was not a tradition. This finding was supported by qualitative data, with participants highlighting the cultural significance of DTE and societal pressure to conform to traditional practices. The primary reason cited for milk teeth extraction in both quantitative and qualitative data was diarrhea, indicating a common misconception linking milk teeth to childhood illnesses [3].

From the total milk teeth extracted in children, 78.3% were attributed to diarrhea, consistent with similar studies conducted at Gondar University Hospital 68.7% [6], in Dembia 84.5% [17], in Tanzania 83.5% [31], and Easter Sudan 88.5% [32]. Qualitative analysis revealed that participants believed milk tooth extraction stemmed from children experiencing diarrhea, vomiting, fevers, and poor growth, echoing findings from a study in Uganda where traditional healers associated extracting deciduous canines with curing childhood ailments [33]. Misconceptions and myths surrounding diarrhea were evident within the community [6, 29, 32, 34]. Furthermore, qualitative findings highlighted that child health status and societal pressure were facilitating factors for milk teeth extraction in children under five in the Abobo district, aligning with research conducted in Alle special woreda [7].

In conclusion, this study sheds light on the prevalence and factors associated with milk teeth extraction among children under 5 years old in the Abobo district. It underscores the need for comprehensive education programs to raise awareness about the consequences of DTE and to promote evidence‐based approaches for managing teething symptoms. Efforts to address misconceptions and cultural beliefs surrounding DTE are crucial for ensuring the well‐being of children in the community.

Strength and Limitation of the Study

Strength of the Study

Mixed community‐based cross‐sectional study design: This approach allows for the collection of data from a diverse range of participants, providing a comprehensive understanding of the issue at hand. It enhances the generalizability of findings to the broader population.

Large sample size: A large sample size increases the statistical power of the study, enhancing the reliability and precision of estimates. It reduces the likelihood of random error and increases the confidence in the study's conclusions.

Limitations of the Study

Social desirability bias: Participants may have provided responses that they perceived as socially acceptable rather than reflecting their true beliefs or behaviors. This could lead to an underestimation or overestimation of certain factors related to milk teeth extraction practices.

Recall Bias

Participants may have difficulty accurately recalling past events or experiences, leading to inaccuracies in the data collected. This could impact the validity of the findings, particularly regarding the prevalence of milk teeth extraction practices.

Potential Underestimation of Prevalence

The study acknowledges that it might have underestimated the prevalence of milk tooth extraction practices. This could be due to various factors such as cultural taboos or reluctance to disclose certain practices.

Conclusions and Recommendations

The prevalence of DTE among children under 5 years old in the Abobo district of the Gambella Region poses a significant health challenge, indicating a need for targeted interventions. Factors contributing to this practice, including maternal lack of information, entrenched cultural traditions, and misconceptions, highlight the complexity of addressing DTE effectively.

To tackle this issue, health departments and offices should implement tailored strategies for disseminating health information, focusing on both rural and urban communities. Strengthening facility‐based health education and establishing health clubs could serve as effective platforms for combating harmful practices and promoting evidence‐based solutions. Continuous awareness creation among communities, mothers, and traditional healers is paramount. Health care providers must utilize diverse channels, such as mass media and local meetings, to disseminate accurate information and dispel misconceptions surrounding teething symptoms and DTE. Collaborative efforts involving women's and children's affairs, health care providers, traditional healers, and social institutions are essential to influence perceptions and change attitudes towards milk teeth extraction.

Nongovernmental organizations can play a vital role in advocacy, mass mobilization, and facilitating review meetings to comprehensively address the issue. Furthermore, future research focusing on older children can provide updated insights into the prevalence and factors influencing milk tooth extraction practices, thereby informing more targeted interventions and policies.
