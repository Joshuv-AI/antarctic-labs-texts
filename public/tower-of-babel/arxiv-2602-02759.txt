Near-Universal Multiplicative Updates for Nonnegative Einsum Factorization
John Hood, Aaron Schein

Abstract
Despite the ubiquity of multiway data across scientific domains, there are few user-friendly tools that fit tailored nonnegative tensor factorizations. Researchers may use gradient-based automatic differentiation (which often struggles in nonnegative settings), choose between a limited set of methods with mature implementations, or implement their own model from scratch. As an alternative, we introduce NNEinFact, an einsum-based multiplicative update algorithm that fits any nonnegative tensor factorization expressible as a tensor contraction by minimizing one of many user-specified loss functions (including the ( � , � ) (\alpha,\beta) -divergence). To use NNEinFact, the researcher simply specifies their model with a string. NNEinFact converges to a stationary point of the loss, supports missing data, and fits to tensors with hundreds of millions of entries in seconds. Empirically, NNEinFact fits custom models which outperform standard ones in heldout prediction tasks on real-world tensor data by over 37 % 37\% and attains less than half the test loss of gradient-based methods while converging up to 90 times faster.

Introduction

Matrix and tensor factorization models serve as fundamental tools for extracting latent structure from high-dimensional multi-way data
(
33
;
14
)
. These techniques impose structural constraints—such as low-rank factorizations or sparsity—to compress complex datasets while preserving their essential characteristics. Nonnegative variants of these factorizations
(
16
;
11
)
have proven particularly valuable in scientific applications due to their interpretable, parts-based representations, leading to routine use for exploratory and descriptive data analysis.

A researcher’s choice of factorization model crucially impacts interpretability, ability to recover underlying latent structure, and fit to the data
(
30
;
33
)
. Yet scientists face a critical gap: tailored factorizations—essential for capturing domain-specific structure—remain inaccessible to most researchers lacking considerable technical skills. Beyond a handful of algorithms with mature implementations, inference algorithms are typically tailored to individual models, implemented from scratch, scale poorly to large datasets, or require substantial implementation effort and programming ability. General methods based on automatic differentiation typically don’t work well in practice, suffering from slow convergence, sensitivity to hyperparameter selection, among other problems
(
46
)
, making it difficult and time-consuming to explore novel models.

Contributions.
The rest of this paper introduces NNEinFact as a general tool for tailored nonnegative tensor factorization. We make the following contributions:

1.

NNEinFact.
A simple, general-purpose nonnegative tensor factorization method built around three calls to the einsum operation that fits a wide family of models under a general set of loss functions.

2.

Flexible modeling.
Switching between loss functions involves changing one of two parameters, and specifying a tailored tensor decomposition model is done with a string — allowing the user to efficiently build, fit and refine models
(
8
;
6
)
.

3.

Theoretical guarantees.
We use a majorization-minimization framework to prove NNEinFact’s convergence to a stationary point of the objective.

4.

Empirical performance.
NNEinFact quickly estimates custom tensor decompositions, outperforming gradient-based automatic differentiation (the only practical alternative for estimating such models). These custom models attain substantially lower heldout loss than more common modeling choices.

5.

Interpretable representations.
In a rideshare pickup case study, we show how NNEinFact efficiently extracts interpretable spatiotemporal structure.

Together, these contributions offer a fresh perspective on modern nonnegative tensor factorization, serving as a foundation for future research in scalable, interpretable, and statistically principled scientific modeling.

Reformulating Generalized Tensor Factorization

as Einsum Factorization

We consider the
M
-mode tensor
{Y}\in\mdmathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}I_{1}}\times\dots\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}I_{M}}}
(where mode

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}I_{m}}}
) with nonnegative entries
{y_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}i_{1},\dots,i_{M}}}}
, writing
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}=({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}i_{1}},\dots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}i_{M}})
. The task of nonnegative tensor decomposition is to approximate

with
\widehat{{Y}}
such that

for all

\widehat{{Y}}
as the
tensor contraction
over
K

modes, where the
k^{\text{th}}
contracted mode has dimension
{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}R_{k}}

{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathbf{r}}}=({\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}r_{1}},\dots,{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}r_{K}})
to denote these additional indices, this is simply
\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}={\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathbf{r}}}}\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathbf{r}}}

We parameterize
\widehat{{Y}}
using
L

\Theta^{{(1)}},\Theta^{{(2)}},\dots,\Theta^{{(L)}}
. For each
\Theta^{{(\ell)}}
, its modes may be partitioned into

{Y}
and

modes which are not shared by
{Y}
. We use
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}_{\ell}}
to index the observed indices of
\Theta^{{(\ell)}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathbf{r}_{\ell}}
to index its contracted indices. The family of decompositions we consider, referred to as

(

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}={\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathbf{r}}}}\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathbf{r}}},\quad{\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathbf{r}}}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\theta^{{(\ell)}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\textbf{i}_{\ell}},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathbf{r}}_{\ell}}}}.

Expression (
2.1
) is compactly written using

. Under einsum notation, indices which appear on the only left (and not the right) are summed over; the indices on the right specify the observed indices. Conveniently,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}_{1}{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathbf{r}}_{1}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}_{2}}{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathbf{r}}_{2}},\dots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}_{L}}{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathbf{r}}_{L}}\rightarrow{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{M}}

(2.2)

corresponds to the einsum notation for (
2.1
) and is compactly expressed as a string in Python. We denote (
2.2
) by
model_str
. Given parameters

\displaystyle{\widehat{{Y}}\leftarrow\texttt{einsum}(\texttt{model\_str},\{\Theta^{{(\ell)}}\}_{\ell=1}^{L})}

efficiently computes
\widehat{{Y}}
. This family includes the canonical choices of Tucker
(

)
, CP
(
25
)
, and tensor-train
(
38
)

Example 1: Tucker.

The Tucker decomposition of multi-rank
({\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}R_{1}},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}R_{2}},\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}R_{M}})

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}=\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}r_{1}}=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}R_{1}}}\ldots\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}r_{M}}=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}R_{M}}}\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathbf{r}}},\quad\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathbf{r}}}=\theta_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathbf{r}}}\prod_{m=1}^{M}\theta_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}i_{m}},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}r_{m}}}^{{(m)}}.

(2.4)

It consists of factor matrices

Example 2: CP.

The rank-
{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}R}

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}=\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}r}=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}R}}\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}r}},\quad\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}r}}=\prod_{m=1}^{M}\theta^{{(m)}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}i_{m}},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}r}}

The tensor-train decomposition, with

4
Theoretical Guarantees

We use a majorization-minimization (MM) framework to establish
Algorithm

1
’s convergence to a stationary point of the loss
\mathcal{L}
. Given a function to minimize (
\mathcal{L}
), MM offers a principled approach to optimization by iteratively constructing tight upper bounds, or surrogate functions,
{Q}
, that
majorize

\mathcal{L}
and minimizing them. Iteratively majorizing and minimizing yields a convergent algorithm that monotonically decreases
\mathcal{L}

Related Work

An extensive literature develops algorithms tailored to specific nonnegative
tensor decompositions and loss functions. Mature implementations exist for CP
and Tucker
(
30
;
31
;
41
;
16
;
42
;
11
;
54
)
,
with the MATLAB Tensor Toolbox
(
3
)
, Python’s Tensorly
library
(
34
)
, and R’s nnTensor package
(
48
)
providing accessible open-source code. These libraries exclusively implement CP and Tucker. Alternative factorizations require either developing specialized
algorithms for each model or relying on generic optimization methods that suffer
from slow convergence and hyperparameter sensitivity
(
5
;
46
)
, creating a significant barrier to exploring domain-specific tensor models.

Researchers have begun to exploit the general form of (
2.1
) to develop flexible tensor decomposition methods.
53
introduce (
2.1
) as
probabilistic latent tensor factorization
and derive multiplicative updates for the Euclidean distance.
52
extended this framework to
generalized tensor factorization
under the
\beta
-divergence family,
deriving heuristic multiplicative updates based on the connection between
exponential families and Bregman divergences. However, their updates lack formal
convergence guarantees—they do not prove monotonic descent or convergence to
a stationary point. Their approach is limited to
\beta
-divergence and lacks a scalable or accessible implementation. NNEinFact
advances this work by: (i) providing a rigorous majorization-minimization-based
proof of monotonicity and convergence, (ii) introducing the decomposability framework (
3.3
) that vastly expands the family of applicable loss functions, and (iii) centering the einsum function as a general and easily accessible tool amenable to GPU acceleration.

Recent work develops tensor methods for
discrete density estimation
under
particular loss functions.
20
provides an
\alpha
-divergence minimization framework for mixtures of CP, Tucker, and tensor-train.

21
view normalized nonnegative tensors as discrete
distributions and minimize KL divergence for decompositions under (
2.1
), deriving specific algorithms for CP, Tucker, and tensor-train. While more general, these approaches still develop specialized algorithms for particular model-loss combinations.

Beyond squared Euclidean distance and KL divergence, various f-divergences and Bregman
divergences
(
44
;
9
)
have been shown to handle sparse and noisy tensor data well. The
\alpha
-divergence (an f-divergence)
and
\beta
-divergence (a Bregman divergence) are robust to missing values,
outliers, and model misspecification.
15
and
19
use the MM framework to develop multiplicative update algorithms for these divergences under NMF, CP and Tucker. Both divergences are special cases of the
(\alpha,\beta)
-divergence family, for which
13
proposed multiplicative updates in the NMF setting. The decomposability property (
3.3
) unifies and extends these prior results to arbitrary einsum factorizations and novel loss functions.

6
Empirical Evaluation

Our experiments compare model structures and optimization algorithms across many loss functions. First, we compare NNEinFact to gradient-based automatic differentiation, demonstrating superior model fit at a fraction of the cost. Then, we demonstrate how problem-tailored tensor decompositions achieve superior data fit compared to standard methods in three real-world settings. Through a case study on Uber pickup data in New York City, we show how custom models recover interpretable spatiotemporal structure using a very small number of parameters.

The first class captures weekday morning commutes across Manhattan. The second shows weekday afternoon commutes originating in Midtown Manhattan, the city’s primary central business district. 7
Conclusion

NNEinFact is most valuable when exploring
custom factorizations beyond CP and Tucker, using loss functions other than
least-squares, or rapidly prototyping tensor models. Like most nonnegative algorithms, NNEinFact would likely benefit from multiple random initializations. For standard CP or Tucker with least-squares where specialized algorithms like hierarchical alternating least squares exist, such implementations may offer empirical advantages.

NNEinFact provides a foundation for modern tensor methods as multiway data becomes increasingly prevalent. The connection between (
\alpha
,
\beta
)-divergences and probabilistic models motivates principled approaches to scientific modeling. The computational efficiency of einsum-based updates, combined with rapidly improving hardware, may enable greater scaling. Beyond nonnegative tensor decomposition, we expect NNEinFact to offer value in many areas of machine learning where tensor decomposition methods are becoming increasingly relevant, such as probabilistic circuits
(

)
and tensor-based regression
(
36
)
.

\tilde{\theta}^{{(\ell)}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\ell}},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathbf{r}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\ell}}}
yields the multiplicative update. If the multiplicative update lies outside of
[\varepsilon,\infty)
, the minimum is attained at
\theta_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\ell}},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathbf{r}}_{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\ell}}}^{{(\ell)}}=\varepsilon
due to the convexity of
Q
.

Proof of convergence to a stationary point.
We draw from the work of
43
, Theorem 2, which establishes convergence to stationary points for a class of algorithms referred to as
Block Successive Upper-bound Minimization Algorithms
.
Algorithm

1
is one of these. In particular,

•

Q
is quasi-convex in
\Theta^{{(\ell)}}
.

•

Q(\Theta^{{(\ell)}}\mid\tilde{\Theta}^{{(\ell)}})
has a unique minimum in
\Theta^{{(\ell)}}
.

•

Q(\Theta^{{(\ell)}}\mid\Theta^{{(\ell)}})=\mathcal{L}(\Theta^{{(\ell)}})
for all
\Theta^{{(\ell)}}\geq\varepsilon
.

•

Q(\Theta^{{(\ell)}}\mid\tilde{\Theta}^{{(\ell)}})\geq\mathcal{L}(\Theta^{{(\ell)}})
for all
\Theta^{{(\ell)}},\tilde{\Theta}^{{(\ell)}}\geq\varepsilon
.

•

\nabla_{\Theta^{{(\ell)}}}Q(\Theta^{{(\ell)}}\mid\tilde{\Theta}^{{(\ell)}})=\nabla_{\Theta^{{(\ell)}}}\mathcal{L}(\Theta^{{(\ell)}})
at
\tilde{\Theta}^{{(\ell)}}=\Theta^{{(\ell)}}
for all
\Theta^{{(\ell)}}\geq\varepsilon
.

•

Q(\Theta^{{(\ell^{\prime})}}\mid\tilde{\Theta}^{{(\ell^{\prime})}})
is continuous in
\{\Theta^{{(\ell)}}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
for all
\Theta^{{(\ell)}}\geq\varepsilon,\ell\in[L]
.

The convexity of
Q(\Theta^{{(\ell)}}\mid\tilde{\Theta}^{{(\ell)}})
in
\Theta^{{(\ell)}}
implies that
Q
is quasi-convex
[
23
]
. The closed-form expression for the minimum of
Q(\Theta^{{(\ell)}}\mid\tilde{\Theta}^{{(\ell)}})
implies uniqueness, while the third and fourth statements are the surrogate property. A quick evaluation of (
A.17
) at
\tilde{\Theta}^{{(\ell)}}=\Theta^{{(\ell)}}
implies tangency and the continuity of
Q
follows from construction and the continuity of
\mathcal{L}
.

Since these statements hold, Theorem 2 of
43
applies: every limit point is a stationary point of objective
3.1
.

Appendix B
Algorithmic Details

All experiments were run on one GPU and all algorithms were implemented in Pytorch.

Stopping criterion.
We use a variety of stopping criterion to evaluate convergence, including increasing validation loss for
5
consecutive iterations, a decrease in the training loss of less than
10^{-6}
, or 5,000 iterations of training.

B.1
Divergences

We implemented the
(\alpha,\beta)
-divergence
[
12
]
setting of
Algorithm

1
, a family of divergences parameterized by
\alpha,\beta\in\mdmathbb{R}
. The
(\alpha,\beta)
-divergence is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\alpha,\beta}(x,y)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\alpha\beta}\!\left[\tfrac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}x^{\alpha+\beta}+\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}y^{\alpha+\beta}-x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}\right]&\alpha,\beta,\alpha+\beta\neq 0\\[11.99998pt]
\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\left[y^{\alpha}-x^{\alpha}+\alpha{x^{\alpha}}\log\!\tfrac{x}{y}\right]&\beta=0,\ \alpha\neq 0\\[10.00002pt]
\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\left[\tfrac{x^{\alpha}}{y^{\alpha}}-1+\alpha\log\tfrac{y}{x}\right]&\alpha=-\beta\neq 0\\[10.00002pt]
\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\beta^{2}}\left[\beta y^{\beta}\log\tfrac{y}{x}-y^{\beta}+x^{\beta}\right]&\alpha=0,\ \beta\neq 0\\[10.00002pt]
\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\log x-\log y\right)^{2}&\alpha=\beta=0\end{cases}

(B.1)

Special cases include the
\alpha
-divergence
[
2
]
, for
\alpha+\beta=1
and the
\beta
-divergence
[
4
]
for
\alpha=1
. Included are the KL divergence
(\alpha=1
,
\beta=0)
, reverse KL
(\alpha=0,\beta=1)
, squared Euclidean distance
(\alpha=1,\beta=1)
, Itakura-Saito divergence
(\alpha=1,\beta=-1)
, as well as the
squared Hellinger distance
(\alpha=\beta=0.5)
, Neyman
\chi^{2}

(\alpha=-1,\beta=2)
and Pearson
\chi^{2}
divergences
(\alpha=2,\beta=-1)
.

When
\alpha\neq 0
,
a(x,y)=x^{\alpha}y^{\beta-1}
and
b(x,y)=y^{\alpha+\beta-1}
. When
\alpha=0
,
\beta=1
,
a(x,y)=\log(x/y)
and
b(x,y)=1
. Otherwise, when
\alpha=0
and
\beta\neq 1
, we could not derive a decomposition satisfying the property (
3.3
).

Under the
(\alpha,\beta)
-divergence,
g(\lambda)
is defined by:

\displaystyle g(\lambda)=\begin{cases}\lambda^{1-\beta}&1/\alpha-\beta/\alpha>1\\
\lambda^{\alpha+\beta-1}&1/\alpha-\beta/\alpha<0\\
\log(\lambda)&\alpha=0,\beta=1\\
\lambda^{\alpha}&0\leq 1/\alpha-\beta/\alpha\leq 1\end{cases}

(B.2)

Maximum likelihood under the negative binomial.
The negative binomial random variable
X
with mean
y
and dispersion parameter
\phi
has probability mass function

\displaystyle p(x\mid y,\phi)=\frac{\Gamma(x+\phi)}{\Gamma(\phi)\Gamma(x+1)}\left(\frac{\phi}{\phi+y}\right)^{\phi}\left(\frac{y}{\phi+y}\right)^{x}.

(B.3)

The terms in the negative log-likelihood proportional to
y
are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(x,y)=(\phi+x)\log(\phi+y)-x\log(y),

(B.4)

which decomposes into convex and concave parts

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{vex}}(x,y)=-x\log(y),\quad\mathcal{L}^{\text{cave}}(x,y)=(\phi+x)\log(\phi+y).

(B.5)

Then

\displaystyle\partial_{y}\mathcal{L}^{\text{vex}}(x,\lambda y)+\partial_{y}\mathcal{L}^{\text{cave}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=-\lambda^{-1}\frac{x}{y}+\frac{\phi+x}{\phi+y}

(B.6)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-1}\left(\lambda\frac{\phi+x}{\phi+y}-\frac{x}{y}\right).

(B.7)

Here,
c(\lambda)=\lambda^{-1}
,
g(\lambda)=\lambda
,
a(x,y)=\frac{x}{y}
and
b(x,y)=\frac{\phi+x}{\phi+y}
. Under the negative binomial likelihood, where each
{y_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}\sim\text{NegBinom}(\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}},\phi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}})}
. Then for fixed
\phi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}
, the multiplicative update that minimizes the negative log-likelihood is

\displaystyle\Theta^{{(\ell)}}\leftarrow\max\left(\varepsilon,\Theta^{{(\ell)}}\odot\left(\frac{\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}\left[\nabla_{\Theta^{{(\ell)}}}\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}\right]\frac{y_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}}{\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}}}{\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}\left[\nabla_{\Theta^{{(\ell)}}}\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}\right]\frac{y_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}+\phi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}}{\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}+\phi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}}}\right)\right).

(B.8)

The geometric distribution arises when
\phi=1
.

Maximum likelihood under the Bernoulli.
The Bernoulli random variable
X\sim\text{Bern}(p)
has probability mass function

\displaystyle p(x\mid p)=p^{x}(1-p)^{1-x}

(B.9)

for
x\in\{0,1\}
. Reparameterizing using the odds ratio
\mu:=\frac{p}{1-p}
, the negative log likelihood simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(x,\mu)=\log(1+\mu)-x\log(\mu)

(B.10)

which decomposes into convex and concave parts

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{vex}}(x,\mu)=-x\log(\mu),\quad\mathcal{L}^{\text{cave}}(x,\mu)=\log(1+\mu).

(B.11)

Then

\displaystyle\partial_{y}\mathcal{L}^{\text{vex}}(x,\lambda y)+\partial_{y}\mathcal{L}^{\text{cave}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=-\lambda^{-1}\frac{x}{y}+\frac{1}{1+y}

(B.12)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-1}\left(\lambda\frac{1}{1+y}-\frac{x}{y}\right).

(B.13)

Here,
c(\lambda)=\lambda^{-1}
,
g(\lambda)=\lambda
,
a(x,y)=\frac{x}{y}
and
b(x,y)=\frac{1}{1+y}
. Under odds estimation using
Equation

2.1
, the multiplicative update that minimizes the negative log-likelihood is

\displaystyle\Theta^{{(\ell)}}\leftarrow\max\left(\varepsilon,\Theta^{{(\ell)}}\odot\left(\frac{\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}\left[\nabla_{\Theta^{{(\ell)}}}\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}\right]\frac{y_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}}{\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}}}{\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}\left[\nabla_{\Theta^{{(\ell)}}}\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}\right]\frac{1}{\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}+1}}\right)\right).

(B.14)

This framework extends to the binomial setting where
y_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}\sim\text{Binomial}(n_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}},p_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}})
. For known number of trials
n_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}
, the corresponding update is

\displaystyle\Theta^{{(\ell)}}\leftarrow\max\left(\varepsilon,\Theta^{{(\ell)}}\odot\left(\frac{\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}\left[\nabla_{\Theta^{{(\ell)}}}\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}\right]\frac{y_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}}{\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}}}{\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}\left[\nabla_{\Theta^{{(\ell)}}}\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}\right]\frac{n_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}}{\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}+1}}\right)\right).

(B.15)

In each of these settings,
\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}
is the estimated odds
\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}:=\frac{\hat{p}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}}{1-\hat{p}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}}
.

Jensen-Shannon divergence.
The Jensen-Shannon divergence is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}x[\log(x)-\log(\tfrac{x+y}{2})]+\tfrac{1}{2}y[\log(y)-\log(\tfrac{x+y}{2})]

(B.16)

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{x+y}{2}\log(\tfrac{x+y}{2})+\tfrac{1}{2}[x\log(x)+y\log(y)].

(B.17)

It has convex-concave decomposition

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{vex}}(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{2}[x\log(x)+y\log(y)],\quad\mathcal{L}^{\text{cave}}(x,y)=-\tfrac{x+y}{2}\log(\tfrac{x+y}{2}).

(B.18)

Then

\displaystyle\partial_{y}\mathcal{L}^{\text{vex}}(x,\lambda y)+\partial_{y}\mathcal{L}^{\text{cave}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\log(\lambda)+\log(y)-\log(\tfrac{x+y}{2})-1)

(B.19)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}[\log(\lambda)-\log(\tfrac{x+y}{2y})].

(B.20)

Then
a(x,y)=\log(\frac{x+y}{2y})
,
b(x,y)=1
,
g(\lambda)=\log(\lambda)
, and
c(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}
. The multiplicative update is

\displaystyle\Theta^{{(\ell)}}\leftarrow\max\left(\varepsilon,\Theta^{{(\ell)}}\odot\exp\left(\frac{\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}\left[\nabla_{\Theta^{{(\ell)}}}\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}\right]\log(\frac{y_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}+\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}}{2\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}})}{\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}\left[\nabla_{\Theta^{{(\ell)}}}\hat{y}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{i}}}\right]}\right)\right).

(B.21)
