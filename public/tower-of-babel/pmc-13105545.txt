Associations between blood cell-based inflammatory indices and their changes and mortality in patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement: a retrospective investigation with prospective multicenter cohort validation study
Authors: Weiya Li, Hongde Li, Shiqin Peng, Yuan Feng, Yong Peng, Zhengang Zhao, Zhongkai Zhu, Chengxiang Song, Yijun Yao, Yue Yin, Tianyuan Xiong, Mao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Chronic inflammation is a driver of cardiovascular diseases, including valvular calcification. Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and neutrophil percentage-to-albumin ratio (NPAR) are promising markers of systemic inflammation. However, research on the predictive value of dynamic changes in these indices for clinical outcomes in patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) remains limited.Materials and methods:This study employed a retrospective single-center cohort (2012–2023, n = 1629) with validation in a prospective multicenter cohort (2023–2024, n = 443). The primary endpoint was all-cause mortality. All participants were stratified into tertiles based on pre- and post-TAVR values of NLR, PLR, and NPAR. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models and restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were used to assess associations of pre-TAVR, post-TAVR, and dynamic changes in these three composite inflammatory markers with mortality. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and time-dependent Harrell’s C-index were applied to evaluate the time-varying predictive value of these markers for mortality.Results:Multivariable-adjusted Cox regression models showed that the highest tertile of inflammatory markers (both pre- and post-TAVR) was associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality than the lowest tertile (pre-TAVR: NLR, aHR = 1.61, P = 0.032; PLR, aHR = 1.88, P = 0.003; NPAR, aHR = 1.69, P = 0.027). Multivariable-adjusted Cox regression and ROC analysis showed that post-TAVR inflammatory markers demonstrated a greater predictive value for mortality than pre-TAVR values. Time-dependent Harrell’s C-index analysis showed high and time-constant predictive values of the pre- and post-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR for mortality. An increase in post-TAVR inflammatory markers from baseline was associated with a sharp rise in mortality risk. Replicate analyses in the multicenter prospective cohort validated the robustness of the findings from our single-center analysis.Conclusions:Post-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR exhibit superior predictive value for mortality compared to pre-TAVR values, with NPAR being the most powerful predictor. Measurement of these three blood cell-based composite inflammatory markers enables precise risk stratification of TAVR patients. Further interventional studies targeting these markers, especially NPAR, are warranted.

Background

Aortic stenosis (AS) is the most common valvular heart disease worldwide, and patients with severe AS have a rapidly progressive course and an extremely poor prognosis[1,2]. Current evidence indicates that transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) is an appropriate option for AS patients across all surgical risk stratifications, and it will increasingly be applied to low-risk and younger patients[3,4]. With the annual increases in TAVR procedures[5,6], risk assessment in AS populations to support clinical decision-making is crucial, while knowledge regarding pre- and post-TAVR evaluation to improve long-term outcomes remains limited. To date, research on serological markers for predicting the prognosis of AS patients undergoing TAVR remains scarce[7].

Chronic inflammation has been established as a driver of cardiovascular diseases, including atherosclerosis and valvular calcification[8]. Over the past decade, inflammation-related indices such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) have been widely investigated in cardiovascular diseases[9,10]. Recent studies have shown that preoperative NLR and PLR are associated with poor outcomes in patients following TAVR[8,9,11]. Neutrophil percentage-to-albumin ratio (NPAR), a novel inflammatory biomarker, is considered a marker that better reflects inflammatory status and disease prognosis than NLR and PLR[12–14]. However, its relationship with long-term outcomes of patients after TAVR remains unclear. TAVR presents a dual inflammatory paradox: while relieving aortic stenosis may exert anti-inflammatory effects, the procedure itself-via implant devices, biomaterials, and surgical stress can perpetuate or exacerbate systemic chronic inflammation and immune responses[15]. The complex interplay of post-TAVR inflammation and immunity, which may influence long-term outcomes, necessitates the clarification of how inflammatory markers and their temporal changes impact patient prognosis. The relationship between post-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR indices and their changes from baseline and long-term mortality is limited, which is of critical importance in determining whether interventions targeting post-TAVR inflammatory status could improve patient outcomes.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the association of noninvasive, cost-effective, widely available, and easily accessible hematological inflammation markers – including NLR, PLR, and NPAR (pre-procedure, post-procedure, and their changes) – with long-term all-cause mortality in AS patients undergoing TAVR, using a retrospective sample from a high-volume center. Methods

Study design and population

This ambispective study consists of two parts, a retrospective study from a high-volume single center (West China Hospital, Sichuan University, China), and a prospective multicenter cohort (China Aortic Valve Transcatheter Replacement Registry II [CARRY II] cohort) validation. For the retrospective cohort, we consecutively collected patients with severe AS who underwent TAVR at West China Hospital from April 2012 to August 2023, and this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital. For the multicenter prospective CARRY II registry (ChiCTR2200066949) study, we collected patients with severe AS who underwent TAVR across 13 large-scale teaching hospitals in China from January 2023 to October 2024, and the study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of each participating center. The study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from each participating patient. All patients were selected for TAVR at their respective centers following discussion by a multidisciplinary heart team-comprising cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, echocardiographers, computed tomography (CT) imaging specialists. The multidisciplinary heart team selected prosthetic valve type, size, and vascular access based on a comprehensive evaluation of cardiac CT and echocardiography.

From 1825 patients in the single-center retrospective cohort, 112 were excluded due to lost follow-up data and 84 due to missing data for baseline NLR, PLR, and NPAR, leaving 1629 patients for analyzing the association between pre-TAVR NLR, PLR, NPAR, and mortality. Subsequently, 317 additional patients were excluded (due to missing data for post-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR, or follow-up duration < 6 months), resulting in 1052 patients for evaluating post-TAVR NLR, PLR, NPAR, and all-cause mortality (Fig. 1A). In the CARRY II cohort, 503 patients were enrolled. After excluding 60 patients due to missing data precluding calculation of baseline NLR, PLR, and NPAR, 443 patients remained for validating the association between pre-TAVR NLR, PLR, NPAR, and all-cause mortality. Subsequently, 116 additional patients were excluded (60 for missing data on post-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR, 3 with infection and 2 died within 1 month post-TAVR), leaving 327 patients for validating post-TAVR NLR, PLR, NPAR, and all-cause mortality (Fig. 1B). Follow-up and endpoint

All patients were followed up at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after TAVR, followed by annual follow-up evaluations thereafter. In this study, all-cause mortality was defined as the primary endpoint.

NLR, PLR, and NPAR assessment

For the single-center cohort, fasting blood samples were collected from all participants before TAVR to perform complete blood count and biochemical analyses, aiming to obtain data on neutrophil count, neutrophil percentage, platelet, lymphocyte count, and serum albumin level. Five-part complete blood cell counts were primarily performed using the Sysmex XN-9000 analyzer (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan), while biochemical parameters were measured by professional laboratory technicians using the Roche Cobas c 702 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany). For the multicenter prospective registry study, blood sample laboratory analyses were conducted at a certified local laboratory in each participating center, using automated analyzers and quality control procedures recommended by the manufacturers. None of the patients had obvious signs of infection before TAVR.

NLR was calculated as the ratio of total neutrophil count to lymphocyte count, PLR as the ratio of total platelet count to lymphocyte count, and NPAR was determined by dividing the neutrophil percentage by the serum albumin concentration (g/dl). For the single-center cohort, based on hematological indices collected at 1, 3, and 6 months after TAVR, the mean values of neutrophil count, neutrophil percentage, lymphocyte count, and serum albumin level were calculated to derive NLR, PLR, and NPAR post-TAVR. For the prospective multicenter prospective cohort, laboratory test data were collected at 1 month after TAVR and were used to calculate NLR, PLR, and NPAR post-TAVR.

Other covariates collection and definitions

For the single-center cohort, clinical data were primarily obtained from a prospectively established database and supplemented with information from the hospital’s inpatient electronic medical record system. For the multicenter prospective CARRY II registry, data were collected using standardized electronic case report forms, with uniform definitions applied across all sites; these data also underwent independent verification and quality checks by two trained researchers. Baseline data including demographic characteristics, laboratory tests, comorbidities, echocardiographic parameters, and procedural details. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kilograms) divided by height (meters) squared, and the Society of Thoracic Surgeons Predicted Risk of Mortality (STS-PROM) score for each patient was computed by our cardiologists. All comorbidities were defined according to ICD codes. Creatinine clearance was calculated using the Cockcroft-Gault formula[17].

Statistical analyses

Patients were divided into survival and deceased groups based on their survival status, with baseline characteristics described accordingly. Additionally, patients were stratified into tertiles according to NLR, PLR, and NPAR values, followed by subsequent survival analyses. Missing data for all variables in this study were no more than 3% for the single-center cohort and 5% for the multicenter prospective cohort, and multiple imputation was performed using the “mice” R package for all missing baseline data.

Chi-square test and linear-by-linear association analyses were used to examine differences in procedural complications among the pre-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR tertiles. Cumulative event rates of all-cause mortality among NLR, PLR, and NPAR tertiles were calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Simple and multivariable Cox regression models were performed to evaluate the associations between pre- and post-TAVR NLR, PLR, NPAR tertiles and all-cause mortality, with hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) reported. All regression models satisfied the proportional hazards assumption. Three Cox regression models were established: model 1 was unadjusted, model 2 was adjusted for age and sex. Candidate covariates for multivariable analysis included variables with a P-value <0.05 in univariate Cox regression analysis and those clinically considered to potentially affect mortality. Therefore, for the single-center cohort, model 3 was adjusted for all candidate covariates, including age, sex, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, prior myocardial infarction, cerebral vascular disease, chronic kidney disease, dialysis, atrial fibrillation, creatinine clearance, triglyceride, fasting plasma glucose, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, STS-PROM score, New York Heart Association (NYHA) class, statins usage, aspirin usage, P2Y12 inhibitor usage, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), aortic valve peak velocity, aortic valve mean gradient, left ventricular diastolic diameter, moderate-to-severe mitral regurgitation, moderate-to-severe tricuspid regurgitation, emergency operation, and moderate-to-severe paravalvular leak (PVL). Similarly, for the multicenter prospective cohort, model 3 was adjusted for all candidate covariates, including age, sex, atrial fibrillation, serum creatinine, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and STS-PROM score. Collinearity diagnosis was performed for all covariates in the fully adjusted Model 3 to ensure the absence of collinearity among covariates.

Additionally, we performed restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis with adjustment for all covariates in Cox regression model 3 to further explore the dose-response relationships between pre-TAVR, post-TAVR, and post-TAVR change-from-baseline values of NLR, PLR, and NPAR with all-cause mortality. Three knots were set at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the distribution, and optimal cutoff values for predicting all-cause mortality were derived from the RCS curves.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to determine the predictive value of pre- and post-TAVR, and post-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR for the endpoint event. Time-dependent Harrell’s C-index were used to evaluate the annual predictive value of these indices for all-cause mortality over time in the single-center cohort. The calculation of C-index implemented 1000 iterations of bootstrap resampling (R package “boot”). Finally, we examined whether there were differences in post-TAVR changes from baseline in NLR, PLR, and NPAR between the survival and deceased groups to identify the most critical indices for management and intervention. Subgroup analyses based on sex, age (≤/ >) 75 years, and LVEF (≤/ > 50%) were also performed.

To assess the robustness of the results, the following sensitivity analyses were conducted: (1) a landmark analysis excluding patients who died within 30 days because these patients may be severely ill and potentially have confounding high pre-TAVR inflammatory markers and high mortality; (2) separate assessments of the associations between the three hematological inflammatory markers (measured at 1 and 6 months post-TAVR, respectively) and mortality; (3) an additional 1-year landmark analysis evaluating the association between the average values of hematological inflammatory markers (measured at 1, 3, and 6 months post-TAVR) and mortality; and (4) analyses of the association between pre- and post-TAVR hematological inflammatory markers and mortality after excluding samples with any missing variables.

All the analyses were performed using R version 4.1.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) statistical software. A two-sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Associations between pre-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR and TAVR procedural complications

For the retrospective single-center cohort, in the evaluation of the correlation between pre-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR and all procedural complications including stroke, annulus rupture, conversion to surgery, ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, atrial fibrillation, bleeding, vascular complication, new permanent pacemaker, and aortic dissection, no significant trend was observed in any complication across the tertile groups of NLR, PLR, and NPAR (Supplemental Digital Content Table S4–S6, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G388; all P-values for the trend >0.05).

Associations between pre- and post-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR and all-cause mortality

In the single-center study, the highest tertile groups of NLR, PLR, and NPAR had the highest 4-year all-cause mortality both pre- and post-TAVR (Fig. 2A–F, all log-rank test P-values are <0.0001). In multicenter prospective study, patients in pre-TAVR NLR and NPAR tertile 3 had significantly higher all-cause mortality (Fig. 3A and C, for pre-TAVR NLR: P = 0.009; for pre-TAVR NPAR: P < 0.0001), while those in tertile 3 of pre-TAVR PLR had the highest all-cause mortality with marginal statistical significance (Fig. 3B, P = 0.053). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis also demonstrated significant differences in all-cause mortality incidence, with higher rates observed in the high post-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR tertiles (Fig. 2D–F, for post-TAVR NLR: P = 0.002; for post-TAVR PLR: P = 0.008; for pre-TAVR NPAR: P < 0.0001). Supplemental Digital Content S7, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G388, shows the results of univariate Cox regression analysis of all baseline variables for all-cause mortality in the single-center study. A total of 28 covariates that were statistically significant in the univariate Cox regression analysis or clinically considered relevant to all-cause mortality were included in multivariate Cox regression Model 3 as the final analysis results. Similarly, in the multicenter prospective study, six variables that were statistically significant in the univariate Cox regression analysis or clinically relevant to clinical outcomes were included in the final Cox Regression Model 3 (Supplemental Digital Content Table S8, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G388). The predictive value of pre- and post-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR for all-cause mortality

As shown in Supplemental Digital Content Figure S1A–D, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G388, pre-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR had certain predictive values for all-cause mortality. In the single-center cohort, the area under the ROC curve (AUC) of NLR, PLR, and NPAR were 0.62 (95% CI: 0.57–0.67, P < 0.001), 0.60 (95% CI: 0.55–0.65, P < 0.001) and 0.64 (95% CI: 0.59–0.69, P < 0.001), respectively. In the multicenter prospective study, the AUC of pre-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR were 0.69, 0.63, and 0.74. Additionally, in both the single-center study and the multicenter prospective cohort, post-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR demonstrated excellent predictive ability for all-cause mortality (Supplemental Digital Content Figure S1B, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G388 for single-center cohort: NLR, AUC = 0.79; PLR, AUC = 0.68; NPAR, AUC = 0.81; Supplemental Digital Content Figure S1D, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G388 multicenter prospective cohort: NLR, AUC = 0.79; PLR, AUC = 0.71; NPAR, AUC = 0.84; all P-values <0.001).

As shown in Supplemental Digital Content Figure S2A–C, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G388 in the single-center cohort, at annual follow-up time points after TAVR, pre-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR exhibited a good predictive value for all-cause mortality (C-indices at 1, 2, 3, and 4 years for NLR were 0.81, 0.79, 0.79, and 0.79; for PLR were 0.80, 0.78, 0.79, and 0.79; for NPAR were 0.81, 0.79, 0.79, and 0.80). Post-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR demonstrated excellent predictive ability for all-cause mortality over time (Supplemental Digital Content S2D–F, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G388 indices at 1, 2, 3, and 4 years for NLR were 0.87, 0.88, 0.90, and 0.89; for PLR were 0.86, 0.87, 0.89, and 0.89; for NPAR were 0.87, 0.90, 0.89, and 0.88), with their predictive power exceeding that of pre-TAVR values.

RCS analysis of pre- and post-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR for all-cause mortality

In the single-center cohort, fully multivariate adjusted RCS analysis showed that pre- (Fig. 4A: P-value for non-linearity = 0.019) and post-TAVR (Fig. 4D: P-value for non-linearity = 0.006) NLR had a nonlinear positive correlation with all-cause mortality, while pre- and post-TAVR PLR and NPAR showed a positive linear correlation with all-cause mortality (Fig. 4B, C, E, and F: all P-values for non-linearity >0.05). Associations between NLR, PLR, and NPAR changes and all-cause mortality

Multivariable-adjusted RCS analysis was used to evaluate the association between post-TAVR changes from baseline in NLR, PLR, and NPAR and all-cause mortality. In the single-center cohort, as depicted in Figure 5A–C, the optimal cutoff values for changes in NLR, PLR, and NPAR in predicting all-cause mortality were 0.78, −4.67, and −0.45, respectively (Fig. 5A–C). In the multicenter prospective cohort, the optimal cutoff values for changes in NLR, PLR, and NPAR in predicting all-cause mortality were 0.21, −4.63, and −0.13 (Figure 5D–F). Overall, the optimal cut-off values were all around 0; an increase in post-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR from baseline (with a change of >0) was associated with a sharp rise in the corresponding mortality rate. Furthermore, we evaluated differences in changes of NLR, PLR, and NPAR between the survival and deceased groups. As shown in Supplemental Digital Content Figure S3, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G388 changes in PLR were consistent between the two groups, with both showing a downward trend after TAVR (Supplemental Digital Content Figure S3A and C, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G388 deceased vs survival group, − 4.46 vs −12.85, P = 0.051). Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses showed that the association between PLR and mortality appeared stronger in male patients, those aged ≤75 years, and those with LVEF ≥50% (Supplemental Digital Content Table S9, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G388). A series of sensitivity analyses further confirmed the robustness of the results. First, among patients who survived more than 30 days after TAVR, the all-cause mortality rate remained significantly higher in the tertile 3 groups of pre-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR than in the tertile 1 groups (Supplemental Digital Content Figure S4, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G388 NLR: P = 0.009; PLR: P < 0.001; NPAR: P < 0.001). Furthermore, as shown in Supplemental Digital Content Table S10, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G388, analyses of the associations between NLR, PLR, and NPAR at 1 month and 6 months post-TAVR and all-cause mortality, respectively, yielded results consistent with the primary findings. Additionally, after excluding patients with endpoints occurring within 1 year of discharge, a significant association between NLR, PLR, and NPAR measured within 6 months post-TAVR and all-cause mortality remained observable (Supplemental Digital Content Figure S5, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G388). Finally, after excluding any samples with missing data, the tertile 3 groups of NLR, PLR, and NPAR (both pre- and post-TAVR) had significantly higher all-cause mortality rates compared with the tertile 1 groups (Supplemental Digital Content Table S11, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G388).

Discussion

The main findings of our study are as follows: (1) Both pre- and post-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR showed high predictive value for all-cause mortality in patients who underwent TAVR for AS. The highest tertile groups of these indices had a significantly higher mortality risk compared with the lowest tertile groups, and post-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR exhibited stronger predictive power than pre-TAVR values. (2) Among the three indices, NPAR consistently demonstrated the highest predictive value for all-cause mortality. (3) The optimal cutoff values for pre- and post-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR in predicting all-cause mortality were determined. (4) An increase in post-TAVR NLR, PLR, and NPAR from baseline was associated with a sharp rise in all-cause mortality.

Currently, predictive tools for adverse outcomes in patients after TAVR remain limited, particularly in terms of hematological markers. In patients with severe AS, circulating blood cells are exposed to high shear stress as they pass through the aortic valve. This repetitive shear stress injury, occurring approximately 1500 times daily, affects inflammatory and immune responses involving platelets, lymphocytes, and other related cells[15,18–20]. While TAVR resolves stenosis and mitigates this response, several factors-including bioprosthetic valve implantation, intraoperative hypotension leading to organ hypoperfusion, valve deployment, disruption of the native valve, hemodynamic changes, antigenicity of the bioprosthesis, and vascular injury-are likely to further trigger systemic inflammatory and immune responses involving blood cells[21,22]. Thus, exploring the dynamic changes of markers reflecting systemic inflammation postoperatively is crucial for risk stratification in patients undergoing TAVR.

NLR, PLR, and NPAR are emerging biomarkers of inflammatory status with substantial potential[23,24]. These indices can be derived from standard pre-TAVR blood tests and offer advantages of simplicity, rapidity, cost-effectiveness, and noninvasiveness. Neutrophils play a pivotal role in both acute and chronic inflammatory responses[25]. Platelets are also critical in vascular injury and inflammation: they produce cytokines and chemokines as mediators of vascular inflammation[26], and release anti-inflammatory factors, pro-inflammatory factors, and angiogenic factors into the systemic circulation to participate in systemic inflammatory responses[27]. Lymphocyte suppression is associated with persistent inflammatory states[28], a decrease in lymphocyte count has been shown to be closely related to poor prognosis in patients with atherosclerotic coronary artery disease[29]. Additionally, low serum albumin levels serve also as markers of inflammatory severity and susceptibility to infectious complications[30]. While NLR, PLR, and NPAR have been linked to the severity of coronary artery disease and calcific aortic stenosis[23,31], research on the association between postoperative inflammatory status and adverse outcomes in TAVR remains limited.

Available studies indicate that elevated baseline inflammatory markers in TAVR patients were associated with increased mortality, readmission, and other adverse outcomes[8,32–34]. For example, Hangyu Liu, Charl Khalil, Antonio Totaro, and colleagues have found that higher NLR before TAVR correlated with short-term adverse events[10,33,34]. Baseline PLR was found to demonstrate significant predictive value for all-cause mortality in TAVR patients with concomitant tumors[8]. No studies have explored the correlation between NPAR and its changes with adverse outcomes after TAVR. Additionally, previous studies have shown that NPAR outperforms NLR and albumin alone in predicting certain adverse outcomes[35]. In our study, we used the mean values of NLR, PLR, and NPAR at 1, 3, and 6 months after TAVR as postoperative observation indicators. This approach avoids the influence of procedural stress on short-term blood indices and more stably reflects the postoperative systemic inflammatory status.

Among the markers in our study, the NPAR – particularly its post-TAVR value – exhibited the greatest potential for mortality prediction. Neutrophils are well-known as one of the cellular effectors playing a key role in mediating inflammatory responses[36]. Hypoalbuminemia, a robust prognostic indicator, primarily serves as a marker of malnutrition and inflammation[37]. Hypoalbuminemia-induced inflammatory state can lead to increased expression of vascular endothelial growth factor and enhanced vascular permeability, which has been proven to potentially increase the risk of infection and heart failure[30]. Additionally, this inflammatory state has also been shown to interfere with the appropriate homeostatic response after surgery, resulting in low immunity and poor prognosis[38]. Albumin has been incorporated into nearly all nutritional and prognostic assessment scales for elderly or critically ill patients, and its strong correlation with mortality has been well-established. Notably, candidates for TAVR are typically critically ill elderly individuals with multiple comorbidities. This may explain, in part, why the NPAR exists excellent prognostic performance for outcomes in the TAVR population. The calculation of NPAR exacerbates the discrepancy between these two inflammatory indices, potentially making it more sensitive to the degree of inflammation[14].

Furthermore, we determined the optimal cutoff values for NLR, PLR, and NPAR to predict mortality. During post-TAVR follow-up monitoring, patients with NLR, PLR, and NPAR exceeding 3.4, 103.3, and 14.6, respectively, showed a higher risk of death. Additionally, regardless of baseline NLR, PLR, and NPAR levels, an increase in post-TAVR inflammatory indices compared to baseline was associated with a significant rise in mortality-a phenomenon warranting greater attention. Based on these findings, we analyzed differences in inflammatory marker changes between deceased and surviving TAVR patients. We found that NPAR increased in the deceased group while decreasing in the surviving group, suggesting that interventions lowing NPAR may confer the greatest benefit to TAVR patients. Our study indicates that evaluating underlying systemic inflammation using simple blood biomarkers could stratify patients likely to survive after TAVR, enabling effective health monitoring. This may prompt more aggressive follow-up strategies. Further research is crucial to determine whether interventions to improve post-TAVR inflammatory status-particularly targeting NPAR (such as inflammation control and nutritional support)-can reduce long-term mortality. In addition, implantation of prosthetic can cause mechanical stretching injury to the aortic wall, which may lead to sustained vascular endothelial cell disruption, local platelet activation, and neutrophil infiltration. In our study, the vast majority of patients received domestic self-expanding valves. Compared with balloon-expandable valves, these self-expanding valves exhibit weaker radial support force, which may result in a less severe inflammatory state[39]. Therefore, our conclusions will require further validation in TAVR populations treated with balloon-expandable valves in future studies.

The current study has several potential limitations. First, this study used a design of single-center retrospective cohort exploration plus prospective multicenter validation, and adjusted for as many outcome-influencing covariates as possible. However, unmeasured confounding factors (e.g., valve-associated thrombosis, tumor progression) may still affect the findings. Second, the relatively low rate of cardiac mortality precluded a comprehensive evaluation of the association between inflammatory markers and cardiac death. Third, variables potentially influencing outcomes-such as frailty and dietary habits-could not be assessed. Additionally, the use of immunosuppressants may affect systemic inflammatory status, but relevant data were uncollectible. Finally, low mean BMI in our study may limit applicability to high-BMI TAVR populations.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates that blood cell-based composite inflammatory markers exhibit good predictive value for mortality, particularly post-TAVR inflammatory markers. An increase in post-TAVR inflammatory markers relative to baseline indicates a sharp rise in mortality risk. This study enables precise risk stratification of post-TAVR patients using simple blood biomarkers, thereby advancing clinical management strategies and optimizing patient care pathways. Interventional trials targeting inflammatory status are essential, especially those focusing on the NPAR.
