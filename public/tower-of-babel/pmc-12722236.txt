Metabolomics and sensomics reveal non-volatile flavor dynamics in summer–autumn steamed green tea flakes processing
Authors: Zixin Ni, Hongjing Pan, Yiqin Chen, Jiayi Chen, Wangjing Wu, Wei Chen, Yuefei Wang, Jihong Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Summer-autumn steamed green tea flakes have attracted attention as a cost-effective extended product of traditional spring shaded Tencha, but their metabolic and sensory transformations during processing remain unexplored. This study employed non-targeted metabolomics and sensomics approaches, including quantitative descriptive analysis, electronic tongue evaluation, and weighted gene co-expression network analysis—to systematically investigate the dynamic non-volatile metabolic transformations and their associations with sensory attributes. A total of 228 metabolites were significantly correlated with sensory traits. Results showed a decline in green characteristics, while seaweed-like notes, umami intensity, and overall preference were enhanced, particularly during the second oven-drying stage. Flavonoid glycosides underwent selective degradation under moist-heat conditions, and the concurrent decrease in tea polyphenols—particularly EGCG—collectively contributed to the reduction in astringency, while the accumulation of trehalose, arachidonic acid, eicosapentaenoic acid, and 17α,20α-dihydroxypregn-4-en-3-one potentially enhanced flavor balance and seaweed-like characteristics. These findings provide a biochemical basis for optimizing Tencha production using summer-autumn tea materials.

Introduction

Tea is one of the most widely consumed beverages in the world, valued for its diverse flavors, rich cultural heritage, and numerous health benefits1. Among various tea types, Tencha is a unique steamed green tea characterized by its seaweed-like flavor, umami taste, and vibrant color, which has traditionally been produced from high-quality spring tea cultivated under shading conditions2. Tencha has served both as a direct tea product and as the raw material for matcha production3. However, recent shifts in tea production and consumer demand from traditional tea products to diversified and refined tea products have spurred interest in alternative raw materials4. Notably, summer-autumn teas, which accounted for over 60% of the total tea production5, have shown considerable promise when processed using cutting techniques that do not require strict appearance standards. These summer-autumn tea materials were innovatively processed via large-scale mechanized Tencha production lines to produce steamed green tea flakes that could be direct brewed, packaged into tea bags, or ground into powders for incorporation as functional ingredients in various foods such as cakes, noodles, and ice creams6. This approach adhered to the traditional Tencha processing, except for the use of summer-autumn tea materials—a modification that is significant in the current market context.

Despite its growing market presence, the quality dynamics of summer-autumn steamed green tea flakes during processing remain underexplored. Tea processing plays a crucial role in shaping sensory properties and chemical composition, which define the unique attributes of different tea types and influence final product quality7. Investigating the metabolic transformations and flavor development during processing is critical for improving production techniques and enhancing taste and functional properties. In tea flavor research, metabolomics approaches and sensomics techniques have been increasingly employed to quantitatively assess flavor compounds and their chemical basis. Quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA) enables evaluators to quantatively assess the sensory attributes of tea products by descriptive terms8. The electronic tongue (E-tongue), which mimics human taste perception through sensors, could provide the sensory intensity data, minimizing errors caused by subjective perception9. Besides, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) could uncover biochemical networks that associated with specific sensory characteristics10. Integrating QDA, E-tongue, and WGCNA thus enables a comprehensive assessment of tea flavor by linking chemical composition with human perception and identifying key metabolites underlying sensory attributes. This combined approach provides a holistic understanding of flavor formation during tea processing and has been successfully applied to investigate the quality characteristics of green tea11, white tea12, yellow tea13, black tea14, oolong tea15, and dark tea16.

This study aimed to elucidate the non-volatile flavor dynamics and chemical transformations during the processing of industrial-scale summer-autumn steamed green tea flakes. By employing non-targeted metabolomics and sensomics approaches such as QDA and E-tongue assessments, we identified and quantified the major metabolites that contribute to tea taste and quality. Additionally, WGCNA was applied to explore the relationships between metabolite modules and sensory traits. The findings provided novel insights into the feasibility of utilizing summer-autumn tea materials via Tencha production method.

Results and discussion

The taste attributes during processing

To understand the changes in taste properties during the processing of summer-autumn steamed green tea flakes, QDA and E-tongue were employed (Fig. 1). QDA was aimed to evaluate the taste properties from a sensory perception perspective, allowing for a more intuitive and quantifiable assessment. Meanwhile, the E-tongue utilized sensors to present the characteristics of various tastes in a digital format. In the QDA experiment, three traditional taste keywords were identified: umami, sweet, and bitter. Additionally, two texture-related keywords, astringent and thickness, two descriptive keywords, green and seaweed-like, and one preference keyword were derived.The five sensors of the E-tongue corresponded to sourness, saltiness, umami, sweetness, and bitterness.

In terms of traditional taste characteristics, the umami score decreased during the SF stage, followed by an increase as processing continued, which was consistent with the results from the E-tongue. For sweetness, there were minimal differences across the samples, with QDA scores ranging from 3.375 to 4 and E-tongue scores between 5.9 and 6.9 except FL (2.3). The QDA results for bitterness indicated that both FL and SF had low bitterness, while ODS showed a significant increase compared to ODF, which subsequently slightly decreased in DL. Interestingly, the E-tongue detected the highest level of bitterness in SF, and it also responded to both sourness and saltiness, neither of which were present in any of the samples in the QDA. This suggests that the E-tongue does not always align with the actual perceived taste and cannot fully substitute for human sensory characteristics.

The mismatch between QDA and E-tongue results reflects the inherent differences between instrumental and sensory evaluation. The E-tongue, widely used in the food industry for rapid and objective taste assessment, provides high sensitivity to specific taste-active compounds. Its detection thresholds for certain tastants, such as sodium ions (saltiness) or hydrogen ions (sourness), are generally lower than human perception thresholds17. However, the E-tongue performs static measurements in aqueous solutions and thus cannot capture the dynamic changes that occur during oral processing. For example, the “sweet aftertaste” of tea usually appears after the initial bitterness and astringency18, associated with compounds such as theophylline and rutin19. As the E-tongue sensors cannot fully replicate the complexity of human perception, the results should be interpreted together with sensory data to obtain a more comprehensive and reliable understanding of tea flavor.

For texture-related descriptions, the scores for thickness and astringency showed similar trends. FL and SF had lower scores, with a significant increase in ODF, followed by a second rise in ODS, and a slight decrease in DL, where the scores were similar to ODS. Among the two descriptive keywords, “green” exhibited the greatest variation in scores, decreasing from 8.125 in FL to 6 in SF, further dropping to 3.875 in ODF, then to 2.5 in ODS, and finally to 2 in DL. The most notable change occurred between SF and ODF. The “seaweed-like” flavor, a characteristic of matcha20, increased significantly during processing. It was barely perceptible in FL and SF, with scores of 0.5 and 0.875, respectively, but rose to 2 in ODF. The largest increase occurred between ODF and ODS, where the score raised from 2 to 4.875, and then slightly decreased in DL to 4.75. This indicates that the seaweed-like characteristic gradually developed during the later stages of processing.

Regarding preference, FL, SF, and ODF all had relatively low scores of 1.625, 1.625, and 2.5, respectively, while ODS and DL had higher preference scores of 5.125 and 6, respectively. This highlights the importance of properly processing. Bitterness and astringency are also important factors contributing to the quality of tea, playing a crucial role in the formation of the tea’s flavor profile21. In this research, although FL and SF exhibited lower bitterness and astringency, and slightly higher sweetness, the low thickness and strong green characteristics resulted in a lack of the typical tea flavor profile, leading to lower preference scores. This suggests that the overall quality of the tea infusion cannot be judged solely by individual taste dimensions, but requires a comprehensive analysis of multiple factors.

Main quality components analysis during processing

Dramatic changes in the content of key quality components were observed during the processing of summer-autumn steamed green tea flakes (Fig. 2), particularly after the SF procedure. Both free amino acids and soluble proteins exhibited similar trends throughout the processing of summer-autumn steamed green tea flakes. After SF, significant reductions in both free amino acids and soluble protein were observed (P < 0.05). Specifically, the content of free amino acids decreased from 2.53% to 2.23%, while the soluble protein content decreased from 4.37% to 3.76%, indicating the protein denaturation and amino acid degradation under moist heat. In contrast, the soluble sugar content displayed an inverse trend relative to free amino acids and soluble protein. It increased significantly by 23.7%, rising from 3.8% in FL to 4.7% in SF, indicating that polysaccharide hydrolysis and the accumulation of soluble sugars such as glucose and sucrose occurred during the steaming process, which may have contributed to the enhancement of sweetness. This observation was consistent with findings of Yu et al.22. However, the content decreased during ODF and remained relatively stable through to the final stage, likely due to the occurrence of Maillard reactions at the higher temperatures in the initial oven-drying phase, which consumed reducing sugars, while the subsequent decline in temperature slowed the reaction rate.

Although amino acids and soluble compounds typically undergo Maillard reactions at high temperatures, our results revealed no reduction in the content of amino acids during the oven-drying processes, with content varying from 2.23% to 2.72%. Previous studies indicated that the content of free amino acids and soluble sugars in steamed green tea was higher than that in fried green tea23, suggesting that the Maillard reaction might be more subdued in steamed green tea.

Water extract, which represents all soluble substances in water, exhibited a downward trend throughout the processing. Caffeine, a contributor to bitter taste24, decreased after steam-fixing and then increased in the later stages.

Tea polyphenols, which contribute a strong astringent taste to the flavor profile11, showed a decreasing trend during processing. The content of tea polyphenols decreased from 14.74% in FL to 12.71% in DL. Catechins, major tea polyphenol compounds known for their strong health benefits25, showed varied trends during processing (Fig. 2F–O). All ester type catechins (EGCG, GCG, ECG, and CG) experienced a significant decrease after SF (P < 0.05) and gradually increased during two oven-drying stages. Among them, EGCG is the most potent and predominant bioactive catechin26 and is also responsible for the stronger bitter and astringent tastes. Its content in fresh leaves was the highest (6.53%), decreasing to 5.86% in DL (P < 0.05). In contrast, the non-ester type catechins, EGC and EC, increased in content after SF. The content of EC dropped by 45.17% in the final DL stage. No significant differences were detected in the content of GC throughout the processing.

Overall, compared with FL, the levels of tea polyphenols, water extract, C, and EGCG—originally present at relatively high abundance—were significantly reduced, which likely contributed to the decrease in astringency. Meanwhile, several compounds that declined sharply after SF including free amino acids, soluble protein, CAF, GA, GCG, ECG, and CG, exhibited partial recovery during the oven-drying stages, potentially promoting a more balanced and integrated taste profile.

Metabolite profiles during processing

Figure 3B showed the PCA score plot of the samples distributed by processing phases, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 55.15% and 25.93% of the total variance, respectively. Specifically, FL samples were located on the lower right of the first principal component, SF samples in the upper middle, ODF samples on the central left, and ODS and DL samples on the lower left. This distribution indicated significant metabolic differences among FL, SF, ODF, and the later processing stages, whereas ODS and DL samples exhibited similar metabolic profiles. Therefore, we conducted further analysis for differential metabolites of the samples.

To further investigate these metabolic differences, partial least squares discrimination analysis (PLS-DA) was conducted. The PLS-DA model explained 92.8% of the variation in X (R²X) and 75% of the variation in Y (R²Y), with a predictive ability of Q² = 0.694, based on seven-fold segment cross-validation, indicating good fit and reliable predictive performance. The permutation test with 200 random permutations (Fig. 3D) confirmed the validity of the model, showing no evidence of overfitting, with the R² and Q² intercepts on the Y-axis being less than 0.05 and 0.5, respectively. The PLS-DA score scatter plot (Supplementary Fig. 1) demonstrated clear separation of the samples from different processing stages, consistent with the PCA findings. Hierarchical cluster analysis (Fig. 3C) further supported this trend, showing progressively refined groupings as processing proceeded.

To evaluate the contribution of specific metabolites to different processing stages, 463 differential metabolites were identified based on variable importance in projection (VIP) values > 1 and P < 0.05. These included 109 terpenoids, 102 flavonoids, 57 lipids and lipid-like molecules, 43 organoheterocyclic compounds, 26 steroids and steroid derivatives, 24 phenolic acids and derivatives, 20 carbohydrates and derivatives, 19 organic oxygen compounds, 14 amino acids and derivatives, 9 classified as others, 7 benzenoids, 7 coumarins and derivatives, 7 organic acids and derivatives, 5 nucleotides and derivatives, 4 lignans and derivatives, 3 quinones, 3 indoles and derivatives, 2 alkaloids and derivatives, 1 vitamin, and 1 tannin.

WGCNA analysis of metabolites associated with taste characteristics

To explore the trends in metabolite changes and their associations with taste, WGCNA was applied (Fig. 4A and B). The metabolites were categorized into six modules, containing 87 compounds in the blue module, 26 in the red module, 78 in the brown module, 73 in the yellow module, 46 in the green module, and 104 in the turquoise module. It was found that the red, brown, yellow, and turquoise modules showed significant correlations with sensory attributes (p < 0.05) with correlation coefficients |r | >0.6. Specifically, the red module was significantly correlated with preference (r = 0.71). The brown module was significantly positively correlated with bitterness (r = 0.71), seaweed-like taste (r = 0.75), thickness (r = 0.71), and preference (r = 0.67), while negatively correlated with green (r = −0.6). The yellow module was significantly positively correlated with umami (r = 0.74), bitterness (r = 0.76), astringency (r = 0.66), seaweed-like taste (r = 0.91), thickness (r = 0.85), and preference (r = 0.92), while negatively correlated with green (r = −0.77). The turquoise module showed a significant negative correlation with green (r = −0.69).

The Fig. 4C showed a total of 281 metabolites from the four modules (yellow, turquoise, red, and brown) that were significantly related to taste characteristics. Amino acids and their derivatives. Along with polypeptides, amino acids and derivatives are often associated with distinct taste characteristics. They can be categorized into sweet, bitter, sour, complex flavor, and tasteless amino acids14. After SF, a significant decrease was observed in the content of L-gamma-glutamyl-beta-phenyl-beta-L-alanine. However, tetrahydrodipicolinate, gamma-L-glutamyl-L-pipecolic acid, L-dopachrome, and hypoglycin B were observed to increase during processing. Tetrahydrodipicolinate, a derivative of aspartic acid, was found to accumulate during the ODS and DL stages. It has also been reported that tetrahydrodipicolinate increases in garlic subjected to heat treatment27. However, its specific taste characteristics have yet to be documented. In this study, it was significantly positively correlated with umami, seaweed-like, thickness, and preference.Gamma-L-glutamyl-L-pipecolic acid showed a marked increase during the early stages of processing (P < 0.01) and reached its peak in the final ODS and DL stages. Previous studies have reported that glutamylation could reduce the bitterness of protein hydrolysates and enhance their kokumi, umami, and salty tastes28.

Benzenoids. Zingerone has been considered as a pungent substance typically derived from ginger29,30. Zingerone showed positive correlations with umami (0.73), bitter (0.67), seaweed (0.85), thickness (0.78), and preference (0.91), accumulating during later stages of summer-autumn steamed green tea flakes processing. Its content in ODS was 10.63 times higher than in FL, ultimately reaching the highest level in DL, where it was 17.24 times that of FL. In ginger, zingerone also accumulates by cooking or heating, resulting from the transformation of gingerol30.

Carbohydrates and derivatives. All differential carbohydrates and their derivatives that were correlated with taste increased during the processing of summer-autumn steamed green tea flakes. The content of trehalose, for instance, rose significantly during the later stages, reaching a level in ODS that was 5.30 times higher than in FL. Trehalose has been reported to modulate food flavors by suppressing bitterness, enhancing sourness, and providing a milder sweetness while prolonging sweetness retention31. It is also speculated that trehalose may play a similar role in balancing the taste.

Flavonoids. Flavonoids, which are typically associated with bitterness and astringency, also contribute to the color of tea infusions32,33. Among the 50 differential flavonoids identified, 21 were flavonoid glycosides, 10 flavanols, 8 biflavonoids and polyflavonoids, 7 isoflavones, and 4 others. Most of these flavonoids (37) were found to be abundant in FL but decreased significantly after the SF stage, showing positive correlations with green taste intensity (r = 0.7 ~ 0.8). Compounds such as assamicain A, 6”-caffeoylhyperi, epigallocatechin-(4beta → 8)-epicatechin 3-O-gallate, and prodelphinidin A2 3’-gallate could barely detected in the SF stage, suggesting substantial transformation under moist-heat conditions. In DL tea products, flavonoids like trifolirhizin, catechin 3’,4’-diglucoside, 2’-O-methylcajanone, and pinobanksin 5-[galactosyl-(1 → 4)-glucoside] were significantly enriched and positively correlated with preference (r = 0.66 ~ 0.72). During the baking process, the transformation and decomposition of flavonol glycosides could reduce bitterness and astringency, contributing to the desirable flavor of tea infusions34. Specifically, the methylation of phenolic hydroxyl groups in flavanone glycosides has been shown to decrease bitterness35. In this study, most differential flavonoids decreased rapidly during the SF stage, while some initially increased before declining, and a few accumulated in DL. These results suggest that bitterness and astringency were mitigated through processing, enhancing the sensory qualities of the tea.

Lipids and lipid-like molecules. Lipids and lipid-like molecules were found to participate in the formation of tea flavor profiles. Some contribute directly to taste perception, while others serve as precursors for numerous volatile compounds generated during tea processing36. Among the differential lipids identified, approximately two-thirds accumulated during the later stages of processing. The content of (2’E,4’Z,7’Z,8E)-Colnelenic acid decreased dramatically after the spreading (SF) stage, possibly due to oxidative degradation under moist heat conditions, producing alcohols, aldehydes, and ketones37. Arachidonic acid has been shown to enhance flavor intensity, total taste intensity, umami, and aftertaste in broiler meat38. Its level dropped during SF stage but increased during the oven-drying process. The organic acid pimelic acid accumulated in the final drying (DL) stage, potentially contributing a slight acidic taste39,40. Costunolide, a compound known to exhibit a bitter taste41, showed a positive correlation with bitterness (r = 0.81) in this study. Its content was low during the FL to ODF stages but accumulated during the ODS and DL stages. The content of 9,12,13-TriHOME, another compound associated with bitterness42,43, increased progressively after the SF stage. The continued degradation and oxidation of linoleic acid during heating may have contributed to the sustained increase in TriHOME content, which contributed to the bitterness of the tea.

Nucleotides. Nucleotides are recognized as a major class of umami substances, alongside amino acids and umami peptides 44. In this study, 5’-methylthioadenosine accumulated during the oven-drying stages, with its content in ODS reaching 3.51 times higher than that in FL. In bottled beer, its content was reported to be higher at 37 °C than at 4 °C, presumably due to protein degradation, which released methionine that was subsequently converted into ethylene. In the presence of copper, ascorbate, and phosphate, 5’-methylthioadenosine was formed as a by-product45. Furthermore, molecular docking analysis suggested that 5’-methylthioadenosine might bind to the taste receptor Otop1, potentially contributing to sour taste46. However, in this study, 5’-methylthioadenosine exhibited positive correlations with umami (r = 0.72), bitterness (r = 0.77), astringency (r = 0.67), seaweed-like taste (r = 0.88), thickness (r = 0.85), and preference (r = 0.9). Its precise taste characteristics and role in flavor perception warrant further investigation.

Organic oxygen compounds. Rishitin accumulated significantly during the ODS stage, while it was barely detectable in FL. It showed positive correlations with bitterness (r = 0.63), seaweed-like taste (r = 0.61), and thickness (r = 0.6). The content of 1-hydroxyacorenone was highest in DL, reaching 50.17 times that of FL, and it showed a positive correlation with preference (r = 0.7).

Organoheterocyclic compounds. The content of 2-(1-pentenyl)furan in DL was 3.38 times higher than that in FL. This compound is known for its grassy, beany, and green flavor47. In our study, it demonstrated positive correlations with seaweed-like taste (r = 0.67) and preference (r = 0.62). Artabsin, which exhibits a bitter taste48, was found to be 12.65 times more abundant in DL than in FL. Additionally, mascaroside in DL was 2.01 times higher than in FL. This compound also possesses a bitter taste49, consistent with our finding that it showed a positive correlation with bitterness (r = 0.75).

Others. The content of eugenyl benzoate in ODS was found to be 11.21 times higher than in FL, and it exhibited positive correlations with bitterness (r = 0.65), seaweed-like taste (r = 0.64), thickness (r = 0.7), and preference (r = 0.63). Derivatives of eugenyl benzoate have been reported to act as inhibitors of colorectal cancer50. Dimethyl sulfoxide, a sulfur-containing compound, showed a rapid decline after SF, with its content decreasing to only 19.80% of that in FL. However, during the subsequent ODF and ODS processes, its concentration steadily increased, reaching 3.97 times that of FL in ODS. Notably, dimethyl sulfoxide is a thermal oxidation product of dimethyl sulfide, and has been reported to be relatively nontoxic and odorless51. In contrast, dimethyl sulfide is an important aroma-contributing compound in various foods. It has been identified as a major contributor to the aroma of lager-style beers52, cooked vegetables53, and truffles54. At appropriate concentrations, it imparts a canned corn flavor, and in clams and oysters, it is considered a character-impact compound of stewed clam aroma55. Additionally, it imparts a seaweed-like aroma in cooked soybeans56. In the present study, dimethyl sulfoxide was positively correlated with seaweed-like taste (r = 0.81). Given that the summer-autumn steamed green tea flakes also exhibits a seaweed-like flavor, further research is needed to elucidate the formation and transformation mechanisms of dimethyl sulfide and dimethyl sulfoxide during processing.

Phenolic acids and their derivatives. Phenolic acids and their derivatives are carboxyl and hydroxyl aromatic compounds57, and these compounds contribute to the bitter and astringent characteristics of tea flavor58. In the present study, nine differential phenolic acids identified were classified into four cinnamic acids and derivatives, two benzoic acids and derivatives, and three quinic acids and derivatives. Gallic acid (GA), associated with astringency and bitterness59,60, was abundant in FL, with its content showing a downward and upward trend during processing. This trend for GA was consistent with the changes observed in the main quality components analysis. 1,3-Dicaffeoylquinic acid and 1,5-dicaffeoylquinic acid exhibited similar trends, accumulating during ODS. Dicaffeoylquinic acids are potential precursors of a coffee-like bitter taste61. In the present study, both 1,3-Dicaffeoylquinic acid and 1,5-dicaffeoylquinic acid showed positive correlations with bitterness. Furthermore, EGCG has been shown to prevent the degradation of dicaffeoylquinic acids62. Ferulic acid, with a low taste threshold of 90 ppm, imparts sour, bitter, and astringent characteristics63,64. It accumulated in DL, with its content being 15.32 times higher than in FL. Caftaric acid, a cinnamic acid, was found to be 11.49 times more concentrated in DL than in FL. Some studies have linked caftaric acid to the bitter taste in wine65, while Gawel et al.66 suggested that it does not increase bitterness in wine; rather, it was found to reduce burning and drying sensations, thus improving the texture of the wine. In our study, caftaric acid showed no significant correlations with bitter or astringent. Piperenol A, which has a vanillin- or cherry-like fragrance67, accumulated during the drying process. Its content in ODF, ODS, and DL was 1.99, 4.27, and 2.87 times that of FL, respectively.

Steroids and derives compounds. 17α,20α-dihydroxypregn-4-en-3-one was detected with its content in ODS and DL being 15.97-fold and 17.80-fold higher than in FL, respectively. This compound has been widely recognized as an “aquatic odor” marker68, in our study, it had positive correlations with seaweed-like taste (r = 0.89) and preference (r = 0.91), suggesting a potential contribution to the fresh flavor of summer-autumn steamed green tea flakes. Cyclocalamin was not detected in FL or SF but was first observed in the ODF stage, with significant enrichment observed in ODS and DL stages. As Cyclocalamin has been previously detected in citrus limonoids69,70, it was speculated to possess a citrus-like fruity flavor.

Tannin. The content of theasinensin B was found to be abundant in FL; however, it rapidly decreased during the SF stage, reaching only 0.29% of the fresh leaf content. By the later stages of ODF, ODS, and DL, it became nearly undetectable. As a dimer of EGCG and EGC71, it is presumed to decompose during the heat treatment process.

Terpenoids. Terpenoids have been reported to contribute significantly to the flavor of tea, particularly as terpene glycosides, which can be hydrolyzed into volatiles that impart green, floral, and fruity aromas72,73. 4-(2,6,6-trimethyl-1-cyclohexen-1-yl)-2-butanone, characterized by earthy, woody, and amber-like notes74, showed increased accumulation during the ODS and DL stages. It also demonstrated positive correlations with seaweed-like taste (r = 0.8), thickness (r = 0.76), and preference (r = 0.77). Glycyrrhetinic acid, which is 250 times sweeter than sucrose75, also exhibited significant accumulation during the ODS and DL stages, enhancing the potential sweetness of the tea products. Notably, several triterpenoids commonly associated with bitterness76,77, such as lucidenic acids and ganoderic acids, displayed significant changes during tea processing. Ganoderic acid Mi, ganoderic acid Mj, lucidenic acid N, and ganoderic acid Mf were most abundant in fresh leaves but decreased rapidly after the SF stage. They all showed positive correlations with green (r = 0.69 ~ 0.84).

Overall, this study provides insights into the dynamic changes of non-volatile metabolites during the processing of summer-autumn steamed green tea flakes, highlighting the pivotal role of the ODS stage in enhancing characteristic flavor development. These findings provide a biochemical basis for optimizing Tencha production line using summer-autumn tea, supporting its broader application for tea and food processing.

Methods

Tea materials

Fresh tea leaves (FL) of Camellia sinensis with one bud and two leaves were hand-plucked in July 2023 from Meitan, Guizhou, China. Following plucking, the leaves were processed according to traditional Tencha production procedures. Initially, the fresh leaves underwent steam fixation (SF) at 150 °C, after which they were allowed to cool to ambient temperature. This was followed by a two-stage oven-drying process: the first stage involved oven-drying at 165 °C for 15 min (ODF), and the second stage involved oven-drying at 120 °C for 20 min (ODS). After drying, the leaves and stems were separated, and the final drying step was carried out for 8 min, reducing the water content of the dry leaves (DL) to less than 5%. All samples were then frozen using liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for further analysis and utilization.

Quantitative descriptive analysis of samples

A total of 1.5 g of tea samples were steeped in 150 mL of boiling water for 2 min, and the infusion was then strained into a 200 mL ceramic evaluation bowl for sensory evaluation. An expert panel of eight trained tea evaluators (six females and two males, aged 24–30 years) conducted the assessment. Before scoring, the panel jointly defined the key taste and texture descriptors to be evaluated. Using the QDA method, they then rated the intensity of each descriptor. The sensory attributes assessed were umami, sweetness, bitterness, astringency, thickness, greenness, seaweed-like character, and overall preference. Each attribute was scored on a 0–10 scale, where 0 represented no perceptible intensity, 5 represented moderate intensity, and 10 represented strong intensity. Prior to evaluation, panelists were calibrated using standard reference solutions set at an intensity level of 5: 4.0 g/L sucrose for sweetness, 0.015 g/L quinine for bitterness, 0.79 g/L monosodium glutamate for umami, 0.75 g/L epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) for astringency, and 0.3 g/L seaweed extract for the seaweed-like note. Finally, a radar chart was generated to illustrate the sensory profile of summer-autumn steamed green tea flakes throughout processing procedures. Each sample was evaluated in triplicate on separate sessions and the order of samples was randomized for panelists to ensure reliability. All panelists consented to participate in this survey, and the protocol was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Zhejiang University’s College of Biosystems Engineering and Food Science (approval code: 2025-5).

E-tongue evaluation

The E-tongue evaluation was performed by using Astree II e-tongue system (Alpha M.S.O, Toulouse, France). Seven sensors including AHS (serial number 73406-09005671-90A), PKS (serial number 74115-11001104-92A), CTS (serial number 74321-11001173-92C), NMS (serial number 74208-1100119-92B), CPS (serial number 74511-11001206-92D), ANS (serial number 74602-11002535-92E), and SCS (serial number 74406-09006275-90A) were used. Among these, AHS, CTS, NMS, AND, and SCS were used for sourness, saltiness, umami, sweetness, and bitterness, respectively. Tea sample infusions were prepared using the same method as in the QDA. For each test, the total test time was 120 s, with data being collected every second and a cleaning time of 10 s between tests. To ensure the accuracy of collected data, each sample was analyzed in triplicate.

Determination of soluble sugar, soluble protein, and free amino acids, caffeine, gallic acid, and catechins

The content of soluble sugar, soluble protein, and free amino acids were determined according to Ni et al.78. A total of 0.3 g sample of tea powder was extracted with 45 mL boiling water for 45 min in a 100 °C water bath. After centrifugation, the supernatant was diluted to 50 mL to obtain the sample solution79.

The soluble sugar content was determined using the anthrone-sulfuric acid colorimetric method 80. To the beginning, 8 mL of 2 mg/mL anthrone-sulfuric acid solution was added to 1 mL of the sample solution in an ice bath, immediately shaken, and then boiled in a 100 °C water bath for 7 min. The mixture was promptly cooled in an ice bath for 10 min, and the soluble sugar content was calculated based on the standard curve at 620 nm.

Soluble protein content was measured using the Coomassie Brilliant Blue method. To 0.1 mL of the sample solution, 3 mL of Coomassie Brilliant Blue solution was added. After 6 min, the absorbance was measured at 595 nm, with the blank serving as a control. The protein content was calculated based on a standard curve constructed using bovine serum albumin.

The free amino acids were quantified using the ninhydrin colorimetry method based on the national standard GB/T 8314–2013. To 1 mL of the sample solution, 0.5 mL of pH 8.04 phosphate buffer and 0.5 mL of 2% ninhydrin solution were added. The mixture was heated in a boiling water bath for 15 min, then cooled and diluted to 25 mL. After 10 min, absorbance was measured at 570 nm. The amino acid content was determined from the standard curve.

The analytical procedure of determining the contents of caffeine (CAF), gallic acid (GA), and catechins (GC, EGC, C, EC, EGCG, GCG, ECG, and CG) was performed according to the method previously established by our group81,82. Briefly, 0.2 g of ground tea powder was extracted twice with 70% (v/v) methanol at 70 °C for 10 min, and the combined extracts were diluted to 10 mL. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane before HPLC analysis. Separation was performed on a C18 column using a binary gradient of mobile phase A (3% acetonitrile, 0.5% acetic acid in water) and mobile phase B (30% acetonitrile, 0.5% acetic acid in water). The flow rate was 1.0 mL/min, detection wavelength 280 nm, and column temperature 25 °C. Standard compounds were obtained from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.

Metabolite extraction

For metabolite extraction, 100 mg of tea sample was placed in a 2 mL centrifuge tube along with a 6 mm grinding bead. Then, 800 μL of an extraction solution composed ofmethanol and water (4:1, v:v) containing internal standard (0.02 mg/mL of L-2-chlorophenylalanine) was added. The mixture was homogenized using the Wonbio-96c frozen tissue grinder (Shanghai Wanbo Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) at −10 °C for 6 min at 50 Hz, followed by low-temperature ultrasonic extraction at 5 °C for 30 min (40 kHz). The samples were then incubated at −20 °C for 30 min, after which they were centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was carefully collected and transferred to an injection vial for LC-MS/MS analysis. Four biological replicates were performed for each sample.

UHPLC-MS/MS analysis

The sample supernatant was analyzed using a Thermo UHPLC-Q Exactive system equipped with an ACQUITY BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.7 μm) from Waters (USA). The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water: acetonitrile (2:98, v/v) (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B). The gradient elution was set as follows: 0–0.5 min, 2% B; 0.5–7.5 min, B increased from 2% to 35%; 7.5–13 min, B increased from 35% to 95%; 13–14.4 min, 95% B; 14.4–14.5 min, B decreased from 95% to 2%; 14.5–16 min, 2% B. The flow rate was maintained at 0.40 mL/min, with a column temperature of 40 °C. The injection volume was 3 μL.

The UPLC system was coupled to a Thermo UHPLC-Q Exactive Mass Spectrometer, equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operating in both positive and negative ionization modes. The source temperature was set to 400°C, with sheath and auxiliary gas flow rates of 40 arb and 10 arb, respectively. The ion-spray voltage floating (ISVF) was set to −2800 V for negative mode and 3500 V for positive mode. The normalized collision energy for MS/MS was set at 20–40–60 V. Full MS resolution was set at 70,000, while MS/MS resolution was set at 17,500. Data were acquired using Data Dependent Acquisition (DDA) mode, with detection conducted across a mass range of 70–1050 m/z.

LC/MS raw data analysis

LC/MS raw data preprocessing was carried out using Progenesis QI software (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA), and a three-dimensional data matrix in CSV format was exported. This matrix contained sample information, metabolite names, and mass spectral response intensities. Internal standard peaks and known false positives (such as noise, column bleed, and derivatized reagent peaks) were excluded from the matrix. The data were then cleaned by removing redundant peaks and pooling relevant data. Metabolites were identified through a search in a self-constructed plant-specific metabolite database (MJDBPM).

The processed data were analyzed using online platform Majorbio Choud (cloud.majorbio.com). Metabolic features detected in at least 80% of samples were retained. For samples where metabolite levels fell below the quantitation threshold, minimum values were imputed. Each metabolic feature was normalized using the sum normalization method to correct for sample preparation and instrument variation. Additionally, variables with a relative standard deviation (RSD) greater than 30% in quality control (QC) samples were excluded.

Data processing and statistical analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was performed using SIMCA 14.1. Oneway analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to evaluate the differences among groups using SPSS (26.0) with Tukey’s method. WGCNA analysis was using Metware Cloud (https://cloud.metware.cn). Graphs were generated using Excel and GraphPad Prism 6.0. The correlation network diagram was generated using Cytoscape 3.10.3. A 95% confidence interval (P < 0.05) was considered statistically significant.
