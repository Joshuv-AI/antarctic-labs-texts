Vaccinology in the twenty-first century revisited
Authors: Alan D T Barrett, Richard E Rupp, Richard W Titball, Veronika von Messling
Published: 2026

Ten years ago, npj Vaccines was launched with an intentionally broad view of vaccinology. A decade later, that breadth has proven prescient.

The journal’s launch editorial in 2016 stated: “Vaccinology is defined as the science of vaccines, and historically includes basic science, immunogens, the host immune response, delivery strategies and technologies, manufacturing, and clinical evaluation. More recently, the science has expanded further to include the safety, regulatory, ethical and economic considerations of vaccine development and utilization. Veterinary vaccines are equally important in the field of vaccinology for their contribution not only to animal health but also to the security of the food supply for humans. Although traditionally vaccinology has focused on infectious diseases, as we move forward in the twenty-first century vaccines will also potentially make significant contributions to the control of non-infectious diseases such as cancers, neurodegenerative diseases and addictions”1.

Over the past decade, npj Vaccines has contributed to this broad vision of vaccinology by publishing work across the full spectrum of vaccine research and development.

There have been major advances in the science of vaccinology, most notably in platform technologies, including mRNA technology2, viral vectors3, proteins4, and glycoconjugates5. There have also been important advances in liponanoparticle (LNP) delivery systems not only for SARS-CoV-2 vaccines6 but also for vaccines against other pathogens such as respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)7. This progress has been recognized, in part, by the journal publishing a collection on LNP-delivered vaccines8. In addition, novel vaccine immunogens have been developed using state-of-the-art techniques, such as artificial intelligence and machine learning, which offer great promise in vaccine development9,10. Similarly, there has been substantial progress in adjuvants to enhance the immune response of vaccines11, understanding the role of the microbiome in immunization12, and vaccines for older adults in the setting of immunosenescence13.

In 2016, we commented that “we owe a huge debt of gratitude to the pioneering vaccinologists of the 20th century for their success in developing vaccines for many of the life-threatening childhood diseases”1 and that has continued in the twenty-first century with vaccines for many diseases, including malaria14,15, monkeypox/mpox16, dengue17, Ebola18,19, and RSV20.

In addition, significant progress has been made in HIV and influenza vaccine development, including broadly neutralizing antibodies21,22 and mRNA-based vaccines23,24. With the adoption of the One Health perspective, the veterinary vaccine space has expanded from protection of livestock from infectious diseases to ensure food production25,26 to food safety27, and even prevention of zoonoses28. Finally, vaccine approaches are increasingly explored as immunotherapeutics, be it as vaccines for non-infectious diseases such as addictions29–31, Alzheimer’s disease32, allergies33, and cancers34,35, as therapeutic vaccines for chronic infectious diseases36, or to combat antimicrobial resistance37.

However, there are some areas where vaccines are needed, or improved vaccines, including fungi38, tuberculosis39, the ESKAPE pathogens including Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa40,41, group A Streptococcus42, noroviruses43, and parasites beyond malaria44.

Arguably the greatest success of twenty-first century vaccinology to date was the development of vaccines to prevent COVID-19, the illness caused by SARS-CoV-2, with candidate vaccines progressing from discovery to Emergency Use Authorization (EUA) in less than 12 months45, whereas vaccine development has taken decades. COVID-19 provided a remarkable demonstration of how physicians, scientists, regulators, manufacturers, and public health officials can work together to address an urgent global public health threat. Indeed, 64 COVID-19 vaccines have been licensed or authorized for emergency use, utilizing a variety of platform technologies46. A number of these have been published in the journal47–53. In addition, the pandemic highlighted important issues related to equitable vaccine access across populations and regions54, as well as vaccine hesitancy, including concerns about the safety of vaccines developed in such a short period of time55,56. Safety is a critical aspect of any vaccine, and COVID-19 vaccines underwent extensive evaluation for both efficacy and safety50,57.

Alongside these scientific achievements, we have seen significant advances in regulatory science58–61. However, the success of COVID-19 vaccine development has created the expectation that scientists can now generate vaccines on demand. This is not the case. Vaccine development remains limited by scientific, financial, manufacturing, resource, and personnel constraints.

npj Vaccines has established itself as a top-ranking journal in the field of vaccinology publishing high-quality papers in across the field. It is part of the Nature Research portfolio and published in partnership with the University of Texas Medical Branch, Sealy Institute for Vaccine Sciences. The journal remains dedicated to highlighting the most important scientific advances in vaccine research and development. Given the public health importance of vaccines, in addition to publishing high-quality original research, npj Vaccines will also publish reviews, perspectives, commentaries, editorials and correspondence from readers, to provide the most current information and developments for those interested in vaccines.

We look forward to your contributions and continuing to publish high-impact papers in the field of vaccinology.
