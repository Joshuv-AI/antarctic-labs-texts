Trans-Glasso: A Transfer Learning Approach to Precision Matrix Estimation
Boxin Zhao, Cong Ma, Mladen Kolar

Abstract
Precision matrix estimation is essential in various fields; yet, it is challenging when samples for the target study are limited. Transfer learning can enhance estimation accuracy by leveraging data from related source studies. We propose Trans-Glasso, a two-step transfer learning method for precision matrix estimation. First, we obtain initial estimators using a multi-task learning objective that captures both shared and unique features across studies. Then, we refine these estimators through differential network estimation to adjust for structural differences between the target and source precision matrices. Under the assumption that most entries of the target precision matrix are shared with those of the source matrices, we derive non-asymptotic error bounds and show that Trans-Glasso achieves minimax optimality under certain conditions. Extensive simulations demonstrate Trans-Glasso ​​’s superior performance compared to baseline methods, particularly in small-sample settings. We further validate Trans-Glasso in applications to gene networks across brain tissues and protein networks for various cancer subtypes, showcasing its effectiveness in biological contexts. Additionally, we derive the minimax optimal rate for differential network estimation, representing the first such guarantee in this area. The Python implementation of Trans-Glasso, along with code to reproduce all experiments in this paper, is publicly available at https://github.com/boxinz17/transglasso-experiments .



1
Introduction

Estimating the precision matrix, i.e., the inverse covariance matrix, is a fundamental task in statistical analysis and has broad applications, including in portfolio optimization, speech recognition, and genomics
(
3
)
.
The precision matrix is closely tied to Gaussian graphical models: estimating the support of the precision matrix corresponds to uncovering the network structure of conditional dependencies between multivariate normal variables
(
3
)
.
However, estimating a precision matrix accurately is often challenging when the sample size is small compared to the dimension—a typical scenario in high-dimensional settings.

In many applications, the target study has a limited sample size, while data from related studies may be available.
Transfer learning
(
30
)
provides a promising approach in these scenarios by leveraging information from related source studies to improve estimation accuracy in the target study.
For example, in gene expression studies across different tissue types, sample sizes may be small for specific tissues, but data from related tissues can help improve estimates
(
4
)
.
Similarly, protein network studies for different cancer subtypes can benefit from transfer learning, as leveraging data from related subtypes can enhance estimation for a particular subtype with limited data
(
34
)
.

A critical aspect of transfer learning is establishing similarity between the target and source tasks. Here, we assume that most entries of the target precision matrix are shared with those of the source matrices, with only a few differences. Based on this assumption, we propose Trans-Glasso, a novel two-step transfer learning method for precision matrix estimation. First, we obtain initial estimators through a multi-task learning objective that captures shared and unique dependencies across datasets. Second, we refine these estimators using differential network estimation to adjust for differences between the target and source matrices
(
14
;
11
)
.

We provide a theoretical analysis of Trans-Glasso, deriving non-asymptotic error bounds and establishing that the method achieves minimax optimality in a wide range of parameter settings. Through extensive simulations, we demonstrate that Trans-Glasso outperforms several baseline methods, particularly in scenarios where the target sample size is small. We also apply Trans-Glasso to gene networks across brain tissues and protein networks for various cancer subtypes, showing its practical effectiveness in biological applications. Additionally, as a byproduct of our analysis, we derive the minimax optimal rate for differential network estimation—to our knowledge, the first such guarantee in this area.

Related Work

Precision matrix estimation.

Estimation of sparse precision matrices in a single study is well studied. Common methods include penalized M-estimator
(
46
;
11
;
38
;
19
;
8
)
and constrained
L_{1}

(

2

(
13
;
9
;
51
)
. See
(
40
)
for a survey. While related to transfer learning, multi-task learning aims to estimate parameters of all studies, whereas transfer learning focuses solely on the target study.

Transfer learning.

Transfer learning has a long history
(
30
)
. Recently, interest in transfer learning for statistical problems has grown.
Li et al. (21)
;
He et al. (14)
studied high-dimensional linear regression. The fused regularizer in
(
14
)
is similar to our multi-task objective; however,
He et al. (14)
focuses on linear regression, while we focus on precision matrix estimation, leading to distinct technical analyses.
Li et al. (23)
;
Tian and Feng (39)
studied high-dimensional generalized linear regression.

Cai and Wei (5)
studied nonparametric classification and
Cai et al. (4)
investigated multi-armed bandit problems.

Lin and Reimherr (24)
;
Cai et al. (8)
studied transfer learning for functional data analysis.

Kpotufe and Martinet (17)
;
Pathak et al. (33)
;
Ma et al. (29)
;
Ge et al. (12)
;
Pathak and Ma (32)
addressed covariate shift—a special form of transfer learning—under different statistical models.

The most relevant work to this paper is
(
4
)
, which also studied transfer learning for precision matrix estimation. The key difference is the similarity assumption.
(
4
)
assumes that the divergence matrices between the target and source precision matrices are sparse. We assume that most entries of the target precision matrix are shared across source precision matrices, with few different entries. Although the assumption in
(
4
)
is motivated by the KL divergence between Gaussian distributions, ours is a structural assumption, making it applicable beyond Gaussian data and easier to interpret. Consequently, our method differs significantly from that of
(
4
)

Differential Network Estimation.

Our approach leverages differential network estimation techniques, which aim to directly estimate the difference between two precision matrices without the need to estimate the individual ones
(

;
11
;
27
;
28
)

Fazayeli and Banerjee (10)
explored this concept in the context of Ising models. Additionally,
Zhao et al. (12)
;
Zhao et al. (13)
extended differential network estimation methods to functional data, while
Tugnait (42)
broadened its application to multi-attribute data.

2
Problem Setup

Imagine that we observe
n_{0}
i.i.d. samples
\{\mathbf{x}^{(0)}_{i}\}^{n_{0}}_{i=1}\coloneqq\mathcal{D}_{0}
from a sub-Gaussian target distribution
\mathcal{P}_{0}
. Each sample
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is assumed to have zero mean and covariance matrix
\Sigma^{(0)}
.
Our goal is to estimate the target precision matrix
\Omega^{(0)}=(\Sigma^{(0)})^{-1}
. Additionally, we have access to
K
sub-Gaussian source distributions
\{\mathcal{P}_{k}\}^{K}_{k=1}
, each with
n_{k}
i.i.d. samples
\{\mathbf{x}^{(k)}_{i}\}^{n_{k}}_{i=1}\coloneqq\mathcal{D}_{k}
. For
1\leq k\leq K
, each
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
also has zero mean, covariance matrix
\Sigma^{(k)}
, and corresponding precision matrix
\Omega^{(k)}=(\Sigma^{(k)})^{-1}
. The goal is to leverage samples from both the target and source distributions to accurately estimate
\Omega^{(0)}
.

To facilitate transfer learning, we assume structural similarity between the target and source precision matrices, whereby most entries in
\Omega^{(0)}
are shared with those in
\Omega^{(k)}
, with relatively few differences. This assumption enables us to efficiently utilize source samples to enhance the estimation of the target precision matrix.
Formally, we characterize the relationship between the target and source precision matrices by the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

For each
0\leq k\leq K
, there exists a shared component
\Omega^{\star}
and a unique component
\Gamma^{(k)\star}
with disjoint supports such that

\Omega^{(k)}=\Omega^{\star}+\Gamma^{(k)\star},

(1)

where
|\Omega^{\star}|_{0}\leq s
,
|\Gamma^{(k)\star}|_{0}\leq h
, and
|\Gamma^{(k)\star}|_{1}\leq M_{\Gamma}
. The parameters
s
and
h
satisfy
s\gg h
, indicating that the majority of the structure is shared, while unique components are minimal.

See Figure
1
for a visual illustration of Assumption
1
.
Assumption
1
is inspired by the assumptions widely used in the differential network estimation literature
(
14
)
.
To see this connection, for each
1\leq k\leq K
, define
\Psi^{(k)}=\Omega^{(k)}-\Omega^{(0)}
to be the differential network between the target
\Omega^{(0)}
and the source
\Omega^{(k)}
. Two immediate implications of Assumption
1
are

\left|\Psi^{(k)}\right|_{0}\leq 2h,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left|\Psi^{(k)}\right|_{1}\leq 2M_{\Gamma},\quad\text{for all }1\leq k\leq K.

This exactly matches Condition 1 in the paper
(
14
)
.

We also note that the structural similarity in Assumption 1 is conceptually related to the heterogeneity assumption in
(
1
)
, which models the discrepancy between proxy and true predictive tasks as a sparse bias; both frameworks exploit the idea that differences across tasks are concentrated in a small set of coordinates, thereby enabling efficient transfer.

In addition, Assumption
1
is naturally interpretable within Gaussian graphical models. Suppose we have an undirected graphical model
G=(V,E)
where nodes represent variables and edges represent conditional dependencies. In this model, an edge exists between nodes
i
and
j
if and only if
\Omega^{(k)}_{ij}\neq 0

(
3
)
. Under Assumption
1
, the target precision matrix
\Omega^{(0)}
and the source matrices
\left\{\Omega^{(k)}\right\}
share a large subset of edges, with only a small number of unique edges in each source, corresponding to sparse deviations
\Psi^{(k)}
.

Figure 1
:
Illustration of Assumption
1
. The target precision matrix,
\Omega^{(0)}
, is shown alongside two source precision matrices,
\Omega^{(1)}
and
\Omega^{(2)}
. Black crosses represent the shared entries across the matrices, while colored shapes indicate individual, unique entries.

Finally, we compare Assumption
1
with the similarity assumption based on divergence measures adopted in
(
4
)
. Specifically,
Li et al. (4)
assume that the divergence matrix
\Upsilon^{(k)}=\Omega^{(0)}\Sigma^{(k)}-I_{d}
is sparse, a condition motivated by controlling the KL divergence between two multivariate Gaussian distributions. In contrast, our structural assumption does not rely on Gaussian-specific properties and naturally extends beyond Gaussian graphical models, providing a more intuitive interpretation of shared structure. We emphasize that we do not claim Trans-Glasso consistently outperforms Trans-CLIME, the method proposed in
(
4
)
, and we leave a detailed theoretical comparison to future work. Further discussion is provided in Appendix
A
.

3
Trans-Glasso Algorithm

In this section, we introduce Trans-Glasso, a transfer learning method for precision matrix estimation. It comprises two steps: (1) initializing precision matrix estimators via a
multi-task
learning problem and (2) refining them with differential network estimation to account for structural differences between target and source matrices.

3.1
Initialization via Multi-Task Learning

To leverage shared structure across the target and source matrices, we begin by jointly estimating precision matrices for both the target and source distributions. Based on Assumption
1
, we employ a multi-task variant of the graphical lasso estimator
(
11
)
, which we refer to as Trans-MT-Glasso (
Trans
fer
M
ulti-
T
ask
G
raphical
lasso
).

Let
\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{n_{k}}\sum^{n_{k}}_{i=1}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}_{i}\mathbf{x}^{(k)\top}_{i}
denote the sample covariance matrix for
0\leq k\leq K
. We define
\Theta\coloneqq(\Omega,\left\{\Gamma^{(k)}\right\}^{K}_{k=0})
for the shared component and the sparse unique components. The Trans-MT-Glasso objective is then given by

\widehat{\Theta}=\left(\widehat{\Omega},\{\widehat{\Gamma}^{(k)}\}^{K}_{k=0}\right)\in\,\,\arg\min_{\Theta\in\mathcal{C}\left(M_{\mathrm{op}}\right)}\left\{\mathcal{L}\left(\Theta\right)+\lambda_{\text{M}}\Phi\left(\Theta\right)\right\},

(2)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\Theta\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum^{K}_{k=0}\alpha_{k}\left\{\left\langle\Omega+\Gamma^{(k)},\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}\right\rangle-\log\det\left(\Omega+\Gamma^{(k)}\right)\right\},

(3)

\displaystyle\Phi\left(\Theta\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left|\Omega\right|_{1}+\sum^{K}_{k=0}\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}\,\left|\Gamma^{(k)}\right|_{1},\qquad\text{and}

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\left(M_{\mathrm{op}}\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{\Theta=\left(\Omega,\left\{\Gamma^{(k)}\right\}^{K}_{k=0}\right)\,:\,\Omega+\Gamma^{(k)}\succ 0\ \text{and}\ \max_{0\leq k\leq K}\left\|\Omega+\Gamma^{(k)}\right\|_{2}\leq M_{\mathrm{op}}\right\}.

(5)

Here,
\alpha_{k}=n_{k}/N
with
N=\sum^{K}_{k=0}n_{k}
controls the contribution of each source,
\lambda_{\text{M}}>0
is a regularization parameter, and
M_{\mathrm{op}}>0
is a predefined constant. The constraint
\left\|\Omega+\Gamma^{(k)}\right\|_{2}\leq M_{\mathrm{op}}
is primarily included to facilitate theoretical analysis.
While we theoretically need to set
M_{\mathrm{op}}
to be a sufficiently large constant, as detailed in a later section, in practice, we simply set
M_{\mathrm{op}}=\infty
to effectively remove this constraint.

The first term of (
2
) measures parameter fitness with observed data, while the second term
\Phi(\Theta)
promotes sparsity in both the shared and individual components.
The sparsity penalization level for
\Gamma^{(k)}
is proportional to
\sqrt{n_{k}/N}
, a factor that is crucial to balance contributions from the target and sources. See Section
5.1
for details.

In the end, we construct the initial estimators of
\Omega^{(k)}
as
\widecheck{\Omega}^{(k)}=\widehat{\Omega}+\widehat{\Gamma}^{(k)}
for
0\leq k\leq K
.

3.2
Refinement via Differential Network Estimation

To further enhance accuracy, we refine these initial estimators by estimating the differential networks
\Psi^{(k)}=\Omega^{(k)}-\Omega^{(0)}
, which capture structural differences between each source and the target. This refinement step adjusts for potential biases in the initial estimates.

Estimating
\Psi^{(k)}
has been extensively studied in the differential network estimation literature
(
14
;
11
)
, with a variety of good estimators. For instance,

Yuan et al. (11)
proposed estimation by solving

\widehat{\Psi}^{(k)}\in\,\,\arg\min_{\Psi}L_{D}\left(\Psi;\widehat{\Sigma}^{(0)},\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}\right)+\lambda^{(k)}_{\Psi}\left|\Psi\right|_{1},

(6)

where
\lambda^{(k)}_{\Psi}
is a tuning parameter, and

L_{D}\left(\Psi;\widehat{\Sigma}^{(0)},\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}\right)=\frac{1}{4}\left(\left\langle\widehat{\Sigma}^{(0)}\Psi,\Psi\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}\right\rangle+\left\langle\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}\Psi,\Psi\widehat{\Sigma}^{(0)}\right\rangle\right)-\left\langle\Psi,\widehat{\Sigma}^{(0)}-\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}\right\rangle.

For our purpose, any reasonable differential network estimator can correct for the bias. Thus, we treat differential network estimation as a black-box algorithm, outputting
\widehat{\Psi}^{(k)}
.

With the initial estimator
\widecheck{\Omega}^{(k)}
and refined differential network estimators
\widehat{\Psi}^{(k)}
, we construct the final transfer learning estimator for
\Omega^{(0)}
as

\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}=\sum^{K}_{k=0}\alpha_{k}\left(\widecheck{\Omega}^{(k)}-\widehat{\Psi}^{(k)}\right),

(7)

where
\widehat{\Psi}^{(0)}=0
by definition. The final estimator Trans-Glasso (
Trans
fer learning
G
raphical
lasso
) integrates both shared information and source-specific refinements, yielding a transfer learning approach that leverages structural similarities across datasets for improved precision matrix estimation.
Compared to
(
4
)
, our method is more sample efficient as it does not require sample splitting between the steps.

4
Implementation in Practice

In this section, we provide practical guidelines for implementing Trans-Glasso, including optimization techniques, hyperparameter selection, and a method for identifying the informative set when not all sources are useful.

4.1
Optimization Algorithms

To implement Trans-Glasso, we first solve the Trans-MT-Glasso objective from Equation (
2
) to obtain initial estimates for
\Omega^{\star}
and
\Gamma^{(k)\star}
. This is a constrained optimization problem that can be efficiently solved using the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM)
(
1
)
.
In this section, we slightly abuse notation by using superscripts to denote the iteration round and subscripts to represent the population.

Define

X=\begin{bmatrix}\Omega_{0}\\
\Omega_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\Omega_{K}\end{bmatrix},\ Y=\begin{bmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma_{0}\\
\Gamma_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\Gamma_{K}\end{bmatrix},\ \text{and}\ B=\begin{bmatrix}I_{d}&I_{d}&0&\cdots&0\\
I_{d}&0&I_{d}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
I_{d}&0&0&\cdots&I_{d}\end{bmatrix},

where
X\in\mathbb{R}^{(K+1)d\times d},Y\in\mathbb{R}^{(K+2)d\times d},B\in\mathbb{R}^{(K+1)d\times(K+2)d}
.
With this notation, the objective in Equation (
2
) can be reformulated as

\displaystyle\text{\text{minimize}}\quad f(X)+g(Y),\qquad\text{subject to}\,\,X=BY,

where

\displaystyle f(X)

\displaystyle=\sum^{K}_{k=0}f_{k}(\Omega_{k}),\quad f_{k}(\Omega_{k})=\alpha_{k}\left\{-\log\det\left(\Omega_{k}\right)+\left\langle\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)},\Omega_{k}\right\rangle\right\}+\mathbb{I}\left(\Omega_{k}\succ 0\right),\quad 0\leq k\leq K,

\displaystyle g(Y)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\text{M}}\left|\Omega\right|_{1}+\lambda_{\text{M}}\sum^{K}_{k=0}\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}\left|\Gamma_{k}\right|_{1},

where
\mathbb{I}\left(\Omega\succ 0\right)=0
if
\Omega\succ 0
and
\mathbb{I}\left(\Omega\succ 0\right)=\infty
otherwise.

The augmented Lagrangian for this problem is:

L_{\rho}(X,Y,Z)=f(X)+g(Y)+\rho\left\langle Z,X-BY\right\rangle+\frac{\rho}{2}\left\|X-BY\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}},

where
Z^{\top}=[Z^{\top}_{0},Z^{\top}_{1},\ldots,Z^{\top}_{K}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(K+1)d}

is a dual variable and
\rho
is a penalty parameter.

After initializing
Y^{(0)}
and
Z^{(0)}
such that
\Omega^{(0)}
,
\Gamma^{(0)}_{k}
, and
Z^{(0)}_{k}
are symmetric, ADMM iteratively updates:

\displaystyle X^{(t)}=\arg\min_{X}L_{\rho}\left(X,Y^{(t-1)},Z^{(t-1)}\right),

(8)

\displaystyle Y^{(t)}\in\arg\min_{Y}L_{\rho}\left(X^{(t)},Y,Z^{(t-1)}\right),

(9)

\displaystyle Z^{(t)}=Z^{(t-1)}+\rho(X^{(t)}-BY^{(t)}).

(10)

Note that (
10
) is equivalent to
Z^{(t)}_{k}=Z^{(t-1)}_{k}+\rho\left(\Omega^{(t)}_{k}-\Omega^{(t)}-\Gamma^{(t)}_{k}\right)
for
0\leq k\leq K
.
See Appendix
L
for detailed steps and stopping criteria.

To refine the initial estimators, we solve the D-Trace loss objective (
6
) for differential network estimation.
Here, we use a different proximal gradient descent algorithm
(
31
)
following
(
13
;
12
)
.

For simplicity, let
L_{D}(\Psi)\coloneqq L_{D}\left(\Psi\,;\,\widehat{\Sigma}^{(0)},\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}\right)
for the chosen
k
. In iteration
t
, we update
\Psi^{(t-1)}
by solving

\Psi^{(t)}=\arg\min_{\Psi}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left\|\Psi-\left(\Psi^{(t-1)}-\eta\nabla L_{D}\left(\Psi^{(t-1)}\right)\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}+\eta\cdot\lambda^{(k)}_{\Psi}\left|\Psi\right|_{1}\right\},

(11)

where
\eta
is a user-specified step size. Note that
\nabla L_{D}(\cdot)
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
\|\widehat{\Sigma}^{(0)}\otimes\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}\|_{2}=\|\widehat{\Sigma}^{(0)}\|_{2}\|\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}\|_{2}
. Thus, for
0<\eta\leq\|\widehat{\Sigma}^{(0)}\|^{-1}_{2}\|\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}\|^{-1}_{2}
, the proximal gradient method converges
(
2
)
. The update in (
11
) has a closed-form solution:

\Psi^{(t)}_{jl}=\left[\left|A^{(t-1)}_{jl}\right|-\lambda^{(k)}_{\Psi}\eta\right]_{+}\cdot A^{(t-1)}_{jl}\,/\,\left|A^{(t-1)}_{jl}\right|,\qquad 1\leq j,l\leq d,

(12)

where
A^{(t-1)}=\Psi^{(t-1)}-\eta\nabla L_{D}(\Psi^{(t-1)})
and
x_{+}=\max\{0,x\}
for any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Details on the optimization algorithms, including stopping criteria and descriptions, are in Appendix
L
.

4.2
Hyperparameter Selection

This section covers the selection of hyperparameters, specifically
\lambda_{\text{M}}
in Trans-MT-Glasso (
2
) and
\lambda^{(k)}_{\Psi}
in D-Trace loss (
6
).

We choose
\lambda^{(k)}_{\Psi}
for
k\in[K]
to minimize the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) of D-Trace loss:

\text{BIC}^{(k)}_{\Psi}=\left(n_{0}+n_{k}\right)\left\|\frac{1}{2}\left(\widehat{\Sigma}^{(0)}\widehat{\Psi}^{(k)}\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}+\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}\widehat{\Psi}^{(k)}\widehat{\Sigma}^{(0)}\right)-\widehat{\Sigma}^{(0)}+\widehat{\Sigma}^{(k)}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}+\log\left(n_{0}+n_{k}\right)\cdot\left|\widehat{\Psi}^{(k)}\right|_{0},

(13)

following
(
11
)
. After selecting
\lambda^{(k)}_{\Psi}
and obtaining
\widehat{\Psi}^{(k)}
for all
k\in[K]
,
\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}
depends on
\lambda_{\text{M}}
. Recall that
N=\sum^{K}_{k=0}n_{k}
.
We choose
\lambda_{\text{M}}
to minimize the BIC of Trans-Glasso, defined as

\text{BIC}_{\mathrm{Trans}}=N\cdot\left[\left\langle\widehat{\Sigma}^{(0)}\,,\,\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}\right\rangle-\log\det\left(\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}\right)\right]+\log N\cdot\left|\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}\right|_{0}.

(14)

4.3
Identifying the Informative Set

In practice, it is not necessarily true that all source distributions are structurally similar to the target. We propose a data-driven method to estimate the informative set
\mathcal{A}\subseteq[K]
.

We obtain differential network estimations
\widehat{\Psi}^{(k)}
for all
k\in[K]
, with the hyperparameter
\lambda^{(k)}_{\Psi}
chosen by minimizing the BIC criterion in (
13
). We then rank sources according to the sparsity level of
\widehat{\Psi}^{(k)}
. Let
R_{k}
be the rank of the source
k
. For any
1\leq k_{1},k_{2}\leq K
,
|\widehat{\Psi}^{(k_{1})}|_{0}\leq|\widehat{\Psi}^{(k_{2})}|_{0}
implies
R_{k_{1}}\leq R_{k_{2}}
. After ranking sources, we input samples into Trans-Glasso and determine the number of sources based on the cross-validation (CV) error. For
K_{\mathrm{chosen}}=0,1,\ldots,K
, we select sources
k
with
R_{k}\leq K_{\mathrm{chosen}}
. When
K_{\mathrm{chosen}}=0
, we obtain
\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}
from graphical lasso
(
11
)
using the target data alone. We then compute the CV error of
K_{\mathrm{chosen}}
by the following procedure:

(i)

We randomly split the
target samples
into
M
-fold.

(ii)

For
m=1,\ldots,M
, we select the
m
-th fold as the validation set,
\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}
, and the rest as the training set. We input the training set and chosen source samples into Trans-Glasso to obtain
\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}
. We compute the CV error for the
m
-th fold as

\text{CV}_{m}=\frac{1}{2d}\left\{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}}\text{tr}\left(\mathbf{x}^{(0)}_{i}\mathbf{x}^{(0)\top}_{i}\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}\right)-\log\det\left(\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}\right)\right\}+\frac{1}{2}\log\pi,

(15)

and define
\text{CV}(K_{\mathrm{chosen}})=\frac{1}{M}\sum^{M}_{m=1}\text{CV}_{m}
.

We set the estimated informative set as
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}=\{k\in[K]:R_{k}\leq K^{\star}_{\text{chosen}}\}
, where
K^{\star}_{\text{chosen}}=\arg\min_{k=0,1,\ldots,K}\text{CV}(k)
. Source samples from
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
are used to estimate
\Omega^{(0)}
. If
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}=\emptyset
, we obtain
\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}
via graphical lasso on target samples. We note that sample splitting is not necessary between estimating
\mathcal{A}
and
\Omega^{(0)}
. This procedure is called Trans-Glasso-CV. We evaluate its empirical performance in Section
6
and defer the study of its theoretical properties to future research. In the next section, we establish the theoretical results under the assumption that
\mathcal{A}=[K]
.

5
Theoretical Analysis

In this section, we establish theoretical guarantees for the Trans-Glasso algorithm. We begin by analyzing the initial estimation step using Trans-MT-Glasso, followed by an error bound for the complete Trans-Glasso estimator. Finally, we derive a minimax lower bound, demonstrating that Trans-Glasso is minimax optimal in a wide range of parameter regimes.

To simplify the theoretical statements, we assume the following condition throughout this section.

Assumption 2
.

Assume that

M_{\Sigma}\coloneqq\max_{0\leq k\leq K}\left|\Sigma^{(k)}\right|_{\infty}=O(1)\qquad\text{and}\qquad M_{\Omega}\coloneqq\max_{0\leq k\leq K}\left\|\Omega^{(k)}\right\|_{2}=O(1).

(16)

5.1
Analysis of Trans-MT-Glasso

We first provide error bounds for the initial multi-task estimation step, Trans-MT-Glasso. This method estimates both the shared precision matrix component
\Omega^{\star}
and the deviation matrices
\Gamma^{(k)\star}
based on the structural similarity outlined in Assumption
1
.

The following theorem provides a high probability upper bound on the Frobenius norm error for the Trans-MT-Glasso estimator. Recall that
N=\sum^{K}_{k=0}n_{k}
and let
\widebar{n}=N/(K+1)
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
2
hold.
Fix a failure probability
\delta\in(0,1]
.
Suppose that the local sample size is large enough so that

\min_{0\leq k\leq K}n_{k}\geq 2\log(2\left(K+2\right)d^{2}/\delta).

(17)

Set
M_{\mathrm{op}}\geq M_{\Omega}
and the penalty parameter
\lambda_{\text{M}}
such that

\lambda_{\text{M}}\geq 160M_{\Sigma}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2\left(K+2\right)d^{2}/\delta)}{2N}}.

(18)

Then with probability at least
1-\delta
, the estimator satisfies

\sum^{K}_{k=0}\alpha_{k}\|\widecheck{\Omega}^{(k)}-\Omega^{(k)}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\frac{18\left(s+(K+1)h\right)\lambda^{2}_{\text{M}}}{\kappa^{2}},

(19)

where
\kappa=\left(2M_{\Omega}+M_{\mathrm{op}}\right)^{-2}
.

Note that the loss function defined in (
3
) is not strongly convex with respect to the Euclidean norm.
This prevents us from obtaining error guarantee for individual precision matrix.
Nevertheless, we make a key observation that the loss function exhibits strong convexity with respect to the weighted norm used in (
19
).
See Appendix
G
for a detailed proof.

When we choose
\lambda_{M}\asymp\sqrt{(\log d)/N}
, the rate shown in Theorem
1
consists of two parts. The first part, which is of the order
(s\log d)/{N}
, refers to the estimation error of the shared component.
In words, Trans-Glasso uses all the samples to estimate the shared component.
The second part, of the order
(Kh\log d)/{N}
, relates to the estimation error of the individual components, i.e., on average, there are
N/K
samples to estimate each individual component.

5.2
Analysis of Trans-Glasso

After the initial estimates are obtained via Trans-MT-Glasso, the differential network estimation step refines these estimates by isolating the deviations
\Psi^{(k)}
. This yields the final Trans-Glasso estimator
\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}
.

As discussed in Section
3
, any differential network estimator can be used in Step 2 for refinement. The differential network estimates
\widehat{\Psi}^{(k)}
are treated as the result of a black-box algorithm, obeying

\left\|\widehat{\Psi}^{(k)}-\Psi^{(k)}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\lesssim g^{(k)}_{\mathrm{F}}(n_{0},n_{k},d,h,M_{\Gamma},\delta)\coloneqq g^{(k)}_{\mathrm{F}}

(20)

simultaneously for all
k=1,\ldots,K
with probability at least
1-\delta
.
We now establish a non-asymptotic error bound for this estimator, which combines the initial estimation error with the error from differential network estimation.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}
be the Trans-Glasso estimator obtained in Equation (
7
). Under the same conditions as in Theorem
1
, and assuming Equation (
20
) holds for the differential network estimators,
with probability at least
1-2\delta
, one has

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}-\Omega^{(0)}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\lesssim\left(\frac{s}{N}+\frac{h}{\widebar{n}}\right)\log(2\left(K+2\right)d^{2}/\delta)+\sum^{K}_{k=0}\alpha_{k}g^{(k)}_{\mathrm{F}}.

The error rates depend on differential network estimators’ performance. Next, we provide specific error rates using the D-Trace loss estimator.

5.2.1
A differential network estimator: D-Trace loss minimization

We characterize
g^{(k)}_{\mathrm{F}}
in the case when the D-Trace loss estimator (
6
) is used. We use the tighter D-Trace loss estimator analysis from
(
12
;
13
;
42
)
to obtain the following lemma, which is derived directly from Corollary
3
in Appendix
H
.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
-
2
hold. Assume that

\min_{0\leq k\leq K}n_{k}\gg h^{2}\log\left(2(K+1)d^{2}/\delta\right).

Set

\lambda^{(k)}_{\Psi}=C\sqrt{\frac{\log\left(2(K+1)d^{2}/\delta\right)}{\min\{n_{k},n_{0}\}}}\quad\text{for all }k\in[K]

for some large constant
C>0
. Then

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{\Psi}^{(k)}-\Psi^{(k)}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\lesssim\sqrt{h}M_{\Gamma}\sqrt{\frac{\log\left(2(K+1)d^{2}/\delta\right)}{\min\{n_{k},n_{0}\}}}

holds simultaneously for all
k\in[K]
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

By Lemma
1
, we have

\displaystyle g^{(k)}_{\mathrm{F}}=\sqrt{h}M_{\Gamma}\sqrt{\frac{\log\left(2(K+1)d^{2}/\delta\right)}{\min\{n_{k},n_{0}\}}}

for the D-Trace loss estimator. Plugging the above results into Corollary
1
, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 2
.

Let
\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}
be obtained by Trans-Glasso (
7
) with the D-Trace loss estimator used in Step 2. Assuming the assumptions in Theorem
1
and Lemma
1
hold.
For a given
\delta\in(0,1]
, letting

\lambda_{\text{M}}\asymp\sqrt{\frac{\log(2\left(K+2\right)d^{2}/\delta)}{N}},\quad\lambda^{(k)}_{\Psi}\asymp M_{\Gamma}\sqrt{\frac{\log\left(2(K+1)d^{2}/\delta\right)}{\min\{n_{k},n_{0}\}}}\quad\text{ for all }k\in[K],

we have

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}-\Omega^{(0)}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\sqrt{\frac{s}{N}}+(1+M_{\Gamma})\sqrt{\frac{h}{\widebar{n}}}+M_{\Gamma}\sqrt{\frac{h}{n_{0}}}\right)\sqrt{\log(2\left(K+2\right)d^{2}/\delta)}

with probability at least
1-2\delta
.

The estimation error consists of three parts: shared component estimation, individual component estimation, and differential network estimation.
If
\widebar{n}\geq n_{0}
and
M_{\Gamma}
is bounded by a universal constant, the error scales as
\sqrt{\frac{s\log d}{N}}+\sqrt{\frac{h\log d}{n_{0}}}
.
Note that the rate obtained when using only target samples is on the order of
\sqrt{\frac{s\log d}{n_{0}}}+\sqrt{\frac{h\log d}{n_{0}}}
.
Thus, when
s\gg h
and
s/N\ll h/n_{0}
, transfer learning can significantly reduce the error rate.
In Appendix
N
, we also derive an upper bound for the expected error, which follows the same order.

5.3
Minimax Lower Bounds and Optimality

To evaluate the theoretical performance of Trans-Glasso, we derive the minimax lower bound for estimating the target precision matrix
\Omega^{(0)}
over the parameter space defined by Assumptions
1
-
2
.
More precisely, we define the relevant parameter space:

\mathcal{G}\left(s,h\right)\coloneqq\left\{\left\{\Omega^{(k)}\right\}^{K}_{k=0}:\,\Omega^{(k)}\succ 0,\,\Omega^{(k)}=\Omega^{\star}+\Gamma^{(k)\star},\,\text{supp}\left(\Omega^{\star}\right)\cap\text{supp}\left(\Gamma^{(k)\star}\right)=\emptyset\ \forall k,\right.\\
\left.\left|\Omega^{\star}\right|_{0}\leq s,\,\max_{0\leq k\leq K}\left|\Gamma^{(k)\star}\right|_{0}\leq h,\,\max_{0\leq k\leq K}\left|\Gamma^{(k)\star}\right|_{1}\leq M_{\Gamma},\,\max_{0\leq k\leq K}\left\|\Omega^{(k)}\right\|_{2}\leq M_{\Omega},\,\max_{0\leq k\leq K}\left|\Sigma^{(k)}\right|_{\infty}\leq M_{\Sigma}\right\},

(21)

where
M_{\Gamma}>0
,
M_{\Omega},M_{\Sigma}>1
are universal constants.

Intuitively, the performance limit of estimating the target precision matrix is dictated by the information-theoretic lower bounds of estimating two parts, namely the shared component and the individual component. This motivates us to develop lower bounds for estimating these two parts.

Lower bound for estimating shared component.

The following lemma provides the minimax lower bound for estimating the shared component when all the distributions are the same.

Lemma 2
.

Assume that we have
n
i.i.d. samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
from
N(0,\Omega^{-1})
, where

\Omega\in\mathcal{G}_{1}=\left\{\Omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}\,:\,\Omega\succ 0,\,\left|\Omega\right|_{0}\leq s,\,0<c_{1}\leq\gamma_{\min}(\Omega)\leq\gamma_{\max}(\Omega)\leq c_{2}<\infty\right\},

and
c_{1},c_{2}
are universal constants.
In addition, assume that
s\geq d\geq c^{\prime}n^{\beta}
for some universal constants
\beta>1
and
c^{\prime}>0
, and

\lceil s/d\rceil=o\left(\frac{n}{\left(\log d\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right).

We then have

\inf_{\widehat{\Omega}}\sup_{\Omega\in\mathcal{G}_{1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{\Omega}-\Omega\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\right]\gtrsim\frac{s\log d}{n}.

The proof is based on Theorem 6.1 in
(
2
)
. See
Appendix
I
for more details.
By Lemma
2
, it is easy to see that the squared Frobenius error of estimating the shared component is lower bounded by
{s\log d}/{N}
where
N
is the total number of samples.

Lower bound for estimating the individual components.

We also provide a lower bound for estimating the individual component. When
\Omega^{(k)}=I_{d}
for all
1\leq k\leq K
and
\Omega^{(0)}=I_{d}+\Delta
with
\textrm{diag}(\Delta)=0
, the source samples are not helpful to estimate
\Omega^{(0)}
at all. Thus, the minimax lower bound for estimating
\Delta
provides a valid minimax lower bound for the transfer learning problem.

Lemma 3
.

Assume that we have
n
i.i.d. samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
from
N(0,\Omega^{-1})
, where

\displaystyle\Omega\in\mathcal{G}_{2}=

\displaystyle\left\{\Omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}:\,\Omega\succ 0,\,\Omega=I_{d}+\Delta,\,\Delta_{jj}=0\text{ for all }1\leq j\leq d,\right.

(22)

\displaystyle\left.\left|\Delta\right|_{0}\leq h,\,\left|\Delta\right|_{1}\leq C_{\Gamma},\,0<c_{1}\leq\gamma_{\min}(\Omega)\leq\gamma_{\max}(\Omega)\leq c_{2}<\infty\right\},

where
C_{\Gamma}>0
,
c_{1}<1
and
c_{2}>1
are constants.
In addition, assume that

d\geq 4h,\,h\log d\geq 8\log 3,\,\frac{h\log d}{n}\leq\min\left\{2,8(1-c_{1})^{2},8(1-c_{2})^{2}\right\},\,h\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n}}\leq 4C_{\Gamma}.

(23)

We then have

\inf_{\widehat{\Omega}}\sup_{\Omega\in\mathcal{G}_{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{\Omega}-\Omega\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\right]\gtrsim\frac{h\log d}{n}.

See Appendix
J
for the proof, which
relies on a novel construction of the packing set of the parameter space and on the celebrated Fano’s method
(
10
, Section 15.3)
.

It is worth noting that Lemma
3
also provides a minimax lower bound for estimating the differential network
\Omega_{X}-\Omega_{Y}
for two precision matrices
\Omega_{X}
and
\Omega_{Y}
when the
L_{1}
-norm of the differential network is bounded. To our knowledge, this is also the first lower bound for differential network estimation
(
14
;
11
)
. As a result, we can derive the first minimax optimal rate for differential network estimation.
See Appendix
M
for a more detailed discussion.

Combining pieces together.

Combining Lemma
2
and Lemma
3
, we have the following lower bound for estimating the target precision matrix in this transfer learning setup.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that we have
n_{k}
i.i.d. samples from a sub-Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{P}_{k}
with zero mean and precision matrix
\Omega^{(k)}
for all
0\leq k\leq K
. Besides, assume that
s\geq d\geq c^{\prime}N^{\beta}
for some universal constants
\beta>1
,
c^{\prime}>0
and

\lceil s/d\rceil=o\left(\frac{N}{\left(\log d\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right).

In addition, assume that

d\geq 4h,\,h\log d\geq 8\log 3,\,h\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n_{0}}}\leq 4M_{\Gamma},\frac{h\log d}{n_{0}}\leq\min\left\{2,\,8\left(1-M_{\Omega}\right)^{2},\,8\left(1-\frac{1}{M_{\Sigma}}\right)^{2}\right\},

where
M_{\Gamma}>0,M_{\Omega},M_{\Sigma}>1
are universal constants defined in (
21
).
We then have

\inf_{\widehat{\Omega}}\sup_{\{\Omega^{(k)}\}^{K}_{k=0}\in\mathcal{G}(s,h)}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{\Omega}-\Omega^{(0)}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\right]\gtrsim\frac{s\log d}{N}+\frac{h\log d}{n_{0}}\,.

Theorem
2
demonstrates that Trans-Glasso achieves minimax optimality for the parameter space specified in (
21
) when
\widebar{n}\geq n_{0}
. The obtained minimax optimal rate is reasonable given
N
samples for estimating the shared component with
s
non-zero entries and
n_{0}
samples for estimating the individual component with
h
non-zero entries. Furthermore, from a practical viewpoint, the rate suggests that the target sample size only needs to be sufficiently large in relation to the sparsity level
h
of the individual component. In contrast, if we only have target samples, the target sample size needs to be large enough to match the sparsity level
s+h
of the entire precision matrix, which can be significantly larger.

6.1
Data Generation Models

We generate data from three distinct models to assess the flexibility of Trans-Glasso.
Each model starts with a shared component, followed by individual components. The final precision matrices are made positive definite by adding a diagonal matrix. Specifically, each model is set as follows.

•

h

\lceil h/2\rceil
entries

•

Model II:

8
Conclusion

We introduce Trans-Glasso, a novel transfer learning approach for precision matrix estimation that mitigates small target sample limitations by leveraging related source data. Trans-Glasso follows a two-step process: initial estimation via multi-task learning, followed by refinement through differential network estimation. This method achieves minimax optimality across various parameter regimes. Extensive simulations show that Trans-Glasso consistently outperforms baselines, demonstrating robustness and adaptability, especially in high-dimensional settings with limited target samples.

Future directions include extending Trans-Glasso to other graphical models, such as Gaussian copula
(
25
)
, transelliptical
(
26
)
, functional
(
35
;
41
;
49
)
, and Ising models
(
18
)
, as well as developing inferential methods within the transfer learning framework.

Appendix A
Comparison of Similarity Assumptions

In this section, we provide a more detailed comparison between our Assumption
1
and the similarity assumption used in
(
4
)
. Let

\Upsilon^{(k)}=(\Omega^{(0)}-\Omega^{(k)})\Sigma^{(k)}=-\Psi^{(k)}\Sigma^{(k)},

or equivalently,
\Psi^{(k)}=-\Upsilon^{(k)}\Omega^{(k)}
.
Li et al. (4)
assume that
\Upsilon^{(k)}
is column-wise sparse in the
L_{p}
-norm. While our Assumption
1
and the assumption in
(
4
)
do not imply each other and are generally not directly comparable, our assumption may be more suitable in certain applications.

First, whereas
\Upsilon^{(k)}
is motivated by controlling the KL divergence between Gaussian distributions, our Assumption
1
is purely structural and applicable to general distributions. Second, our assumption offers a natural interpretation in Gaussian graphical models, unlike the divergence-based similarity assumption in
(
4
)
. Finally, a technical advantage of our framework lies in its flexibility when establishing sparsity relationships. While sparsity in
\Psi^{(k)}
does not necessarily imply sparsity in
\Upsilon^{(k)}
, and vice versa, additional structural assumptions on
\Sigma^{(k)}
or
\Omega^{(k)}
can relate the two. Specifically, we have

\left|\Psi^{(k)}\right|_{0}\leq\sum_{i,j}\mathds{1}\left\{\sum^{d}_{l=1}\mathds{1}\left\{\Upsilon^{(k)}_{il}\neq 0\right\}\mathds{1}\left\{\Omega^{(k)}_{lj}\neq 0\right\}\geq 1\right\}.

If both
\Upsilon^{(k)}
and
\Omega^{(k)}
are sparse, then
\Psi^{(k)}
is also sparse. Conversely, if both
\Psi^{(k)}
and
\Sigma^{(k)}
are sparse, then
\Upsilon^{(k)}
must be sparse. A key distinction is that sparsity in
\Upsilon^{(k)}
implies sparsity in
\Psi^{(k)}
only when
\Omega^{(k)}
is sparse, whereas sparsity in
\Psi^{(k)}
implies sparsity in
\Upsilon^{(k)}
only when
\Sigma^{(k)}
is sparse. In graphical models, sparsity assumptions on the precision matrix
\Omega^{(k)}
are far more common than on the covariance matrix
\Sigma^{(k)}
, making sparsity in
\Psi^{(k)}
generally a weaker and more practical assumption than sparsity in
\Upsilon^{(k)}
.

Although a comprehensive theoretical comparison between our Assumption
1
and the similarity assumption in
(
4
)
is challenging, we provide some empirical insights in the following discussion. We begin by introducing the divergence distance used in
(
4
)
to quantify the difference between
\Omega^{(0)}
and
\{\Omega^{(k)}\}^{K}_{k=1}
, defined as

h_{\mathcal{D}}\coloneqq\max_{1\leq k\leq K}\left\{\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\left\|\Upsilon^{(k)}_{j,\cdot}\right\|_{p}+\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\left\|\Upsilon^{(k)}_{\cdot,j}\right\|_{p}\right\},

where
\Upsilon^{(k)}_{j,\cdot}
denotes the
j
-th row of
\Upsilon^{(k)}
and
\Upsilon^{(k)}_{\cdot,j}
denotes its
j
-th column. Here,
\|\cdot\|_{p}
is the
L_{p}
vector norm, for which
Li et al. (4)
establish theoretical guarantees when
p\in[0,1]
.

Lemma 4
.

For any two matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, we have

|AB|_{\infty}\leq\min\left\{\|A\|_{\infty}|B|_{\infty},\|B\|_{1}|A|_{\infty}\right\}.

(24)

Proof.

For
1\leq j\leq d
, denote by
B_{:j}
the
j
-th column of
B
.
We then have

\displaystyle|AB|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\|AB_{:j}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\|A\|_{\infty}\|B_{:j}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|A\|_{\infty}\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\|B_{:j}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|A\|_{\infty}|B|_{\infty}.

The other claim follows from the facts that
|AB|_{\infty}=|B^{\top}A^{\top}|_{\infty}
, and
\|B^{\top}\|_{\infty}=\|B\|_{1}
.
∎

\|\tilde{\theta}^{0}\|_{2}<\frac{1}{4}

We have

\left\|\theta^{j}\right\|_{2}=\left\|\tilde{\theta}^{j}\right\|_{2}\geq\left\|\tilde{\theta}^{j}-\tilde{\theta}^{0}\right\|_{2}-\left\|\tilde{\theta}^{0}\right\|_{2}>\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}=\frac{1}{4}\quad\text{for }1\leq j\leq M,

where the last inequality follows from (

Combining Case 1 and Case 2, we have proved that

Lemma 10
.

Let
m\geq 2
be an even integer and set
d=2m
. Let
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
be such that
\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq 1/4
and define

\Omega=\begin{bmatrix}I_{m}&B\\
B^{\top}&I_{m}\end{bmatrix}.

Then
\Omega\succ 0
and

D_{\text{KL}}\left(N\left(0,\Omega^{-1}\right)\|N\left(0,I_{d}\right)\right)\leq\frac{16}{15}\left\|B\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}.

Proof.

We begin with proving
\Omega\succ 0
.
By Weyl’s inequality, we know that

\displaystyle\gamma_{\min}\left(\Omega\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\left\|\begin{bmatrix}0&B\\
B^{\top}&0\end{bmatrix}\right\|\geq 1-\left\|\begin{bmatrix}0&B\\
B^{\top}&0\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle=1-\sqrt{2}\left\|B\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

As a result, we have
\Omega\succ 0
.

Now we move on to the second claim regarding the KL divergence. Recall that

D_{\text{KL}}\left(N\left(0,\Omega^{-1}\right)\|N\left(0,I_{d}\right)\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\log\det\left(\Omega\right)-d+\text{tr}\left(\Omega^{-1}\right)\right],

(26)

which motivates us to compute
\log\det\left(\Omega\right)
and
\text{tr}\left(\Omega^{-1}\right)
.
Let
B=UDV^{\top}
be the singular value decomposition of
B
with
U,D,V\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
,
U^{\top}U=UU^{\top}=I_{m}
,
V^{\top}V=VV^{\top}=I_{m}
, and
D=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m})
. We have the following identities.

\displaystyle\log\det\left(\Omega\right)

\displaystyle=\sum^{m}_{i=1}\log\left(1-\lambda^{2}_{i}\right).

(27a)

\displaystyle\text{tr}\left(\Omega^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum^{m}_{i=1}\frac{2}{1-\lambda^{2}_{i}}.

(27b)

Combining (
26
), (
27a
), and (
27b
), we obtain

\displaystyle D_{\text{KL}}\left(N\left(0,\Omega^{-1}\right)\|N\left(0,I_{d}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\sum^{m}_{i=1}\log\left(1-\lambda^{2}_{i}\right)+\sum^{m}_{i=1}\frac{2}{1-\lambda^{2}_{i}}-d\right]

\displaystyle=\sum^{m}_{i=1}\left[\frac{\log\left(1-\lambda^{2}_{i}\right)}{2}+\frac{1}{1-\lambda^{2}_{i}}-1\right].

Since
\lambda^{2}_{j}\leq\sum^{d}_{i=1}\lambda^{2}_{i}=\|B\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\leq{1}/{16}
, we have
-\lambda^{2}_{j}\geq-{1}/{16}
. Also note that
\log(1+x)\leq x
for all
x\geq-1
. We thus have

\displaystyle D_{\text{KL}}\left(N\left(0,\Omega^{-1}\right)\|N\left(0,I_{d}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum^{m}_{i=1}\left[\frac{-\lambda^{2}_{i}}{2}+\frac{1}{1-\lambda^{2}_{i}}-1\right]

\displaystyle=\sum^{m}_{i=1}\frac{\lambda^{2}_{i}+\lambda^{4}_{i}}{2\left(1-\lambda^{2}_{i}\right)}\leq\sum^{m}_{i=1}\frac{\lambda^{2}_{i}}{1-\lambda^{2}_{i}}\leq\frac{16}{15}\sum^{m}_{i=1}\lambda^{2}_{i}=\frac{16}{15}\left\|B\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}.

This completes the proof.

Proof of Equation (
27a
).

Using Section 9.1.2 of
Petersen et al. (7)
, we have

\det\left(\Omega\right)=\det\left(I_{m}-B^{\top}B\right)
.
Since

I_{m}-B^{\top}B=I_{m}-VD^{2}V^{\top}=V\left(I_{m}-D^{2}\right)V^{\top},

(28)

we have
\det\left(\Omega\right)=\det\left(I_{m}-D^{2}\right)=\prod^{m}_{i=1}\left(1-\lambda^{2}_{i}\right)
and hence

\log\det\left(\Omega\right)=\sum^{m}_{i=1}\log\left(1-\lambda^{2}_{i}\right).

Proof of Equation (
27b
).

Using Section 9.1.3 of
Petersen et al. (7)
, we have

\Omega^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{m}&B\\
B^{\top}&I_{m}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\left(I_{m}-BB^{\top}\right)^{-1}&-B\left(I_{m}-B^{\top}B\right)^{-1}\\
-\left(I_{m}-B^{\top}B\right)^{-1}B^{\top}&\left(I_{m}-B^{\top}B\right)^{-1}\end{bmatrix},

which implies

\text{tr}\left(\Omega^{-1}\right)=\text{tr}\left\{\left(I_{m}-BB^{\top}\right)^{-1}\right\}+\text{tr}\left\{\left(I_{m}-B^{\top}B\right)^{-1}\right\}.

(29)

It follows from (

\left(I_{m}-B^{\top}B\right)^{-1}=V\left(I_{m}-D^{2}\right)^{-1}V^{\top}

(30)

G.1
Additional Notation

We first introduce some additional notation that will be helpful in the proof.
We define the Hilbert space for the parameters

\mathbb{H}\coloneqq\left\{\Theta=\left(\Omega,\left\{\Gamma^{(k)}\right\}^{K}_{k=0}\right):\,\Omega,\Gamma^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\text{ for all }0\leq k\leq K\right\},

with the associated inner product

\left\langle\Theta,\Theta^{\prime}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\left\langle\Theta,\Theta^{\prime}\right\rangle\coloneqq\left\langle\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}\right\rangle+\sum^{K}_{k=0}\left\langle\Gamma^{(k)},\Gamma^{(k)\prime}\right\rangle.

The space
\mathbb{H}
endowed with the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is indeed a Hilbert space.
Correspondingly, we have

\left\|\Theta\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}=\left\|\Omega\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}+\sum^{K}_{k=0}\left\|\Gamma^{(k)}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}.

We also need the dual norm of
\Phi(\cdot)
, which is defined as

\Phi^{\star}\left(\Theta\right)\coloneqq\sup_{\Theta^{\prime}:\Phi(\Theta^{\prime})\leq 1}\left\langle\Theta,\Theta^{\prime}\right\rangle.

(31)

Recall that
\Theta^{\star}=(\Omega^{\star},\{\Gamma^{(k)\star}\}^{K}_{k=0})
is the true parameter.
Let
\mathcal{S}_{\Omega}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\Gamma^{(k)}}
be the supports of
\Omega^{\star}
and
\Gamma^{(k)\star}
for all
0\leq k\leq K
, respectively.
Under Assumption
1
, the true parameter
\Theta^{\star}
lies in the following subspace of
\mathbb{H}
:

\mathbb{M}\coloneqq\left\{\Theta=\left(\Omega,\left\{\Gamma^{(k)}\right\}^{K}_{k=0}\right):\,\textrm{supp}(\Omega)\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\Omega},\,\textrm{supp}\left[\Gamma^{(k)}\right]\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\Gamma^{(k)}}\text{ for all }0\leq k\leq K\right\}.

(32)

The orthogonal complement of
\mathbb{M}
is given by

\mathbb{M}^{\bot}\coloneqq\left\{\Theta=\left(\Omega,\left\{\Gamma^{(k)}\right\}^{K}_{k=0}\right):\,\textrm{supp}(\Omega)\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\Omega}^{c},\,\textrm{supp}\left[\Gamma^{(k)}\right]\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\Gamma^{(k)}}^{c}\text{ for all }0\leq k\leq K\right\}.

(33)

Clearly, for any
\Theta\in\mathbb{M}
and
\Theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{M}^{\bot}
, we have that
\langle\Theta,\Theta^{\prime}\rangle=0
.

We also need to define the projection of a parameter onto a subspace. For any matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and any subspace
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, we define

\left[B\right]_{\mathcal{F}}=\arg\min_{\tilde{B}\in\mathcal{F}}\left\|\tilde{B}-B\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}.

Similarly, for any
\Theta\in\mathbb{H}
and any subspace
\mathbb{F}\subseteq\mathbb{H}
, we define

\left[\Theta\right]_{\mathbb{F}}=\arg\min_{\tilde{B}\in\mathbb{F}}\left\|\tilde{B}-B\right\|_{\mathbb{H}}.

In addition, for
S\subseteq[d]\times[d]
, we define

\mathcal{M}(S)\coloneqq\left\{B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}:\textrm{supp}(B)\subseteq S\right\}.

For any
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, we define
\left[B\right]_{S}\coloneqq\left[B\right]_{\mathcal{M}(S)}
.
This way, for any
\Theta=(\Omega,\{\Gamma^{(k)}\}^{K}_{k=0})\in\mathbb{H}
, we have

\left[\Theta\right]_{\mathbb{M}}=\left(\left[\Omega\right]_{\mathcal{S}_{\Omega}},\left\{\left[\Gamma^{(k)}\right]_{\mathcal{S}_{\Gamma^{(k)}}}\right\}^{K}_{k=0}\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left[\Theta\right]_{\mathbb{M}^{\bot}}=\left(\left[\Omega\right]_{\mathcal{S}_{\Omega}^{c}},\left\{\left[\Gamma^{(k)}\right]_{\mathcal{S}_{\Gamma^{(k)}}^{c}}\right\}^{K}_{k=0}\right).

Finally, we define our metric on the estimation error. For
\Theta=(\Omega,\{\Gamma^{(k)}\}^{K}_{k=0})\in\mathbb{H}
, let

H(\Theta)\coloneqq\sum^{K}_{k=0}\alpha_{k}\left\|\Omega+\Gamma^{(k)}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}.

(34)

We define

\widehat{\Delta}\coloneqq\widehat{\Theta}-\Theta^{\star}=\left(\widehat{\Omega}-\Omega^{\star},\left\{\widehat{\Gamma}^{(k)}-\Gamma^{(k)\star}\right\}^{K}_{k=0}\right),

where
\widehat{\Theta}
is the Trans-Glasso estimator (
2

H(\widehat{\Delta})=\sum^{K}_{k=0}\alpha_{k}\left\|\widehat{\Omega}-\Omega^{\star}+\widehat{\Gamma}^{(k)}-\Gamma^{(k)\star}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}=\sum^{K}_{k=0}\alpha_{k}\left\|\widecheck{\Omega}^{(k)}-\Omega^{(k)}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}.

Appendix M
Minimax Optimal Rate for Differential Network Estimation

Differential network estimation aims to estimate the difference between two precision matrices without first estimating the individual precision matrices. Although existing studies focus on providing upper bounds for this problem
(
14
;
11
;
13
)
, there is no known matching lower bound, making the minimax optimal rate an open question.
As a byproduct of our analysis, in this section, we provide a minimax optimal rate for differential network estimation problem under certain conditions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first minimax optimal guarantee towards this direction.

We start by formulating the problem setup.
Note that we reintroduce some notation used in this section to make it self-contained, and one should not confuse it with the notation used in the other parts of the paper.
Suppose that we have
n_{X}
i.i.d. samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n_{X}}\sim N(0,\Omega^{-1}_{X})
and
n_{Y}
i.i.d. samples
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n_{Y}}\sim N(0,\Omega^{-1}_{Y})
. Let
\Delta\coloneqq\Omega_{Y}-\Omega_{X}
be the differential network between
\Omega_{X}
and
\Omega_{Y}
. Our goal is to use samples from two populations to estimate
\Delta
. In addition, we assume that
\Omega_{X}
and
\Omega_{Y}
belong to the following parameter space.

\left(\Omega_{X},\Omega_{Y}\right)\in\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\left\{\Omega_{X},\Omega_{Y}\in\mathbb{S}^{d\times d}\,:\,c_{1}\leq\gamma_{\min}(\Omega_{X})\leq\gamma_{\max}(\Omega_{X})\leq c_{2},\right.\\
\left.c_{1}\leq\gamma_{\min}(\Omega_{Y})\leq\gamma_{\max}(\Omega_{Y})\leq c_{2},\,\left|\Delta\right|_{0}\leq h,\,\left|\Delta\right|_{1}\leq C_{\Gamma}\right\},

(59)

where
c_{1},c_{2},C_{\Gamma}>0
are positive universal constants. The parameter space defined in (
59
) requires that the smallest eigenvalues and the largest eigenvalues of
\Omega_{X}
and
\Omega_{Y}
are lower bounded and upper bounded, respectively.
Besides, we also require that the sparsity level of the differential network is bounded by
h
, and the
L_{1}
norm of the differential network is bounded by universal constant.

We first present the minimax lower bound for the above estimation problem. We state the result in the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Assume that

d\geq 4h,\,h\log d\geq 8\log 3,\,\frac{h\log d}{n}\leq\min\left\{2,8(1-c_{1})^{2},8(1-c_{2})^{2}\right\},\,h\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n}}\leq 4C_{\Gamma}.

(60)

We then have

\min_{\widehat{\Delta}}\sup_{(\Omega_{X},\Omega_{Y})\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{\Delta}-\Delta\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\right]\gtrsim\frac{h\log d}{\min\left\{n_{X},n_{Y}\right\}}

Proof.

Let
\Omega_{X}=I_{d}
, note that

\left(I_{d},\Omega_{Y}\right)\in\mathcal{M}\Leftarrow\Omega_{Y}\in\mathcal{G}_{2},

where
\mathcal{G}_{2}
is defined in (
22
). Now that samples from population
X
are useless for estimating
\Omega_{Y}
, and we can only rely on samples from population
Y
to estimate
\Omega_{Y}
. By Lemma
3
, we then have

\min_{\widehat{\Delta}}\sup_{(\Omega_{X},\Omega_{Y})\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{\Delta}-\Delta\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\right]\geq\min_{\widehat{\Omega}_{Y}}\sup_{\Omega_{Y}\in\mathcal{G}_{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{\Omega}_{Y}-\Omega_{Y}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\right]\gtrsim\frac{h\log d}{n_{Y}}.

Similarly, we can show that

\min_{\widehat{\Delta}}\sup_{(\Omega_{X},\Omega_{Y})\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{\Delta}-\Delta\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\right]\gtrsim\frac{h\log d}{n_{X}}.

Combine the above two inequalities, we have the final result.
∎

Next, we derive the matching upper bound. Let
\widehat{\Delta}
be the D-Trace loss estimator defined in (
6
), and
\check{\Delta}(\tau)
be the truncated version of
\widehat{\Delta}
as defined in (
61
).
Following directly from Theorem

Combine Theorem

and Theorem

Theorem 5

Assume that the conditions of Theorem

and Theorem

\min_{\widehat{\Delta}}\sup_{(\Omega_{X},\Omega_{Y})\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{\Delta}-\Delta\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\right]\asymp\frac{h\log d}{\min\left\{n_{X},n_{Y}\right\}}.

Appendix P
Graph Recovery Guarantee for Gaussian Graphical Models

While this paper primarily focuses on precision matrix estimation, it is also of great interest to explore the implications of Trans-Glasso for graph recovery in Gaussian graphical models (GGMs), given the close relationship between the precision matrix and the underlying graph structure. In this section, we introduce a graph estimation procedure based on thresholding the output of Trans-Glasso, establish a theoretical guarantee for its accuracy, and demonstrate its advantage over graph estimators that rely solely on target data.

Let
E^{(0)}=\left[E^{(0)}_{jl}\right]_{1\leq j,l\leq d}\in\{0,1\}^{d\times d}
denote the adjacency matrix of the undirected Gaussian graphical model for the target data. Specifically, for any
j\neq l
, we set
E^{(0)}_{jl}=1
if nodes
j
and
l
are connected, and
E^{(0)}_{jl}=0
otherwise. By definition,
E^{(0)}_{jj}=0
for all
1\leq j\leq d
. Given the well-established connection between GGMs and the precision matrix
(
3
)
, we have, for any
j\neq l
,

E^{(0)}_{jl}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\Omega^{(0)}_{jl}\neq 0,\\
0&\text{if }\Omega^{(0)}_{jl}=0.\end{cases}

We propose a graph estimator based on thresholding the output of Trans-Glasso,
\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}
. Specifically, let
\widehat{E}^{(0)}=\left[\widehat{E}^{(0)}_{jl}\right]_{1\leq j,l\leq d}\in\{0,1\}^{d\times d}
denote the estimated adjacency matrix. We set
\widehat{E}^{(0)}_{jj}=0
for all
1\leq j\leq d
, and for any
j\neq l
, define

\widehat{E}^{(0)}_{jl}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }|\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}_{jl}|>\epsilon_{n},\\
0&\text{if }|\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}_{jl}|\leq\epsilon_{n},\end{cases}

where
\epsilon_{n}
is a thresholding parameter.

We measure the graph estimation error using the Hamming distance between
E^{(0)}
and
\widehat{E}^{(0)}
, defined as

\text{Err}\left(\widehat{E}^{(0)}\right)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{1\leq j,l\leq d}\mathds{1}\left\{\widehat{E}^{(0)}_{jl}\neq E^{(0)}_{jl}\right\}.

Additionally, let
\omega_{\min}=\min_{(j,l):E^{(0)}_{jl}=1}\left|\Omega^{(0)}_{jl}\right|>0
denote the minimum signal strength in the graphical model. The following deterministic bound then holds for
\text{Err}\left(\widehat{E}^{(0)}\right)
.

Proposition 1
.

If
0<\epsilon_{n}\leq\frac{1}{2}\omega_{\min}
, then

\text{Err}\left(\widehat{E}^{(0)}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2\epsilon^{2}_{n}}\left\|\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}-\Omega^{(0)}\right\|^{2}_{\text{F}}.

Moreover, if the right-hand side is smaller than
1
, perfect graph recovery is achieved, i.e.,
\widehat{E}^{(0)}=E^{(0)}
.

Proof.

Let

S_{\mathrm{I}}=\left\{(j,l)\,:\,E^{(0)}_{jl}=0,\,\widehat{E}^{(0)}_{jl}=1\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad S_{\mathrm{II}}=\left\{(j,l)\,:\,E^{(0)}_{jl}=1,\,\widehat{E}^{(0)}_{jl}=0\right\}

denote the sets of type-I error and type-II error. We then have

\text{Err}\left(\widehat{E}^{(0)}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{1\leq j,l\leq d}\mathds{1}\left\{\widehat{E}^{(0)}_{jl}\neq E_{jl}\right\}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left|S_{\mathrm{I}}\right|+\left|S_{\mathrm{II}}\right|\right).

Note that for any
(j,l)\in S_{\mathrm{I}}
, we have

\left|\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}_{jl}-\Omega^{(0)}_{jl}\right|=\left|\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}_{jl}\right|>\epsilon_{n}.

(69)

Besides, for any
(j,l)\in S_{\mathrm{II}}
, we have

\left|\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}_{jl}-\Omega^{(0)}_{jl}\right|\geq\left|\Omega^{(0)}_{jl}\right|-\left|\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}_{jl}\right|\geq\left|\Omega^{(0)}_{jl}\right|-\epsilon_{n}\geq\omega_{\min}-\epsilon_{n}\geq\epsilon_{n},

(70)

where the last inequality follows from the assumption that
\epsilon_{n}\leq\frac{1}{2}\omega_{\min}
.

Combining (
69
) and (
70
), we have

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}-\Omega^{(0)}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{(j,l)\in S_{\mathrm{I}}}\left(\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}_{jl}-\Omega^{(0)}_{jl}\right)^{2}+\sum_{(j,l)\in S_{\mathrm{II}}}\left(\widehat{\Omega}^{(0)}_{jl}-\Omega^{(0)}_{jl}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\epsilon^{2}_{n}\left(\left|S_{\mathrm{I}}\right|+\left|S_{\mathrm{II}}\right|\right)

\displaystyle=2\epsilon^{2}_{n}\cdot\text{Err}\left(\widehat{E}^{(0)}\right),

which then implies the final result.
∎
