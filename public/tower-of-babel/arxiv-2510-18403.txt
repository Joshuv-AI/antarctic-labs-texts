Crystalline Motion of discrete interfaces in the Blume-Emery-Griffiths Model
M. Cicalese, G. Fusco, G. Savaré

Abstract
. We study the discrete-to-continuum evolution of a lattice system consisting of two immiscible phases labelled by − 1 -1 and + 1 +1 in presence of a surfactant phase labelled by 0 0 . The system’s energy is described by the classical Blume-Emery-Griffith model on the lattice ε ​ ℤ 2 \varepsilon{\mathbb{Z}}^{2} , and its continuum evolution is obtained as ε → 0 \varepsilon\to 0 through a minimizing-movements scheme with a time step proportional to ε \varepsilon . The dissipation functional we choose contains two contributions: a standard Almgren-Taylor-Wang type term penalizing the distance between successive configurations of the + 1 +1 phase, and a term penalizing the variation of the surfactant mass and modeling surfactant evaporation. The latter term depends on a scaling parameter γ > 0 \gamma>0 , which determines whether the surfactant mass is conserved at each time step. We focus on the case in which the initial configuration consists of a single crystal of phase 1 1 completely wetted by the surfactant. For γ > 2 \gamma>2 the surfactant can loose mass and the evolution reduces to the crystalline mean curvature flow of an Ising-type model, while for γ < 2 \gamma<2 the conservation of the surfactant mass leads to a more complex evolution characterized by stronger non-uniqueness and partial pinning.



Keywords: Blume-Emery-Griffith surfactant model, discrete-to-continuum, minimizing movements, crystalline curvature flow

AMS subject classifications: 53E10, 49J45, 74A50, 82B24, 82C24

Introduction

In statistical mechanics and materials science, lattice models are essential for understanding energy-driven physical systems, particularly in their capacity to model phase transitions and microstructure formation. Beyond their descriptive power, they serve as a crucial rigorous framework for the validation and derivation of asymptotic mesoscopic and continuum phenomenological models which can be obtained as the lattice spacing vanishes. A central technique in the description of the asymptotic behaviour of low energy states and in particular ground states of lattice models is the
\Gamma
-convergence. The
\Gamma
-limit defines a rigorous variational coarse-graining and the whole process goes under the name of discrete-to-continuum passage. The class of those discrete systems that give rise to an asymptotic continuum energy model favoring phase separation has been extensively studied in the static framework over the past 20 years (for a comprehensive study the reader is referred to the book
[
3
]
and its references). This class includes classical ferromagnetic Ising-type lattice spin systems. These consist of interacting particles occupying the nodes of (a subset of) a lattice with each particle labelled by a value from a finite set (in the classical Ising case, particles can take only two possible values). Each of these values defines a spin phase which tends to be connected to save energy, and, as a result, the entire system can be described in terms of a finite partition into connected subsets of the lattice (the phases) interacting via an appropriate perimeter functional. Within this description the asymptotic continuum energy is a (possibly anisotropic) perimeter-type energy of a partition of sets of finite perimeter. Finding the phase transition energy of the coarse grained system is therefore equivalent to solving a classical minimal partitioning problem in the calculus of variations.

The relation between the perimeter functional and the Ising system, discussed above in the static setting, extends to the dynamic setting. The appearance of interfacial energies as an approximation of spin energies in fact suggests to consider a continuum description of discrete evolution problems in terms of geometric motions of interfaces. For the ferromagnetic Ising model, where the spin variable takes only two values, the continuum description involves two homogeneous phases separated by an interface with crystalline perimeter energy (see
[
2
]
). In
[
10
]
, Braides, Gelli, and Novaga introduced a continuum geometric evolution of these phases by combining the Almgren-Taylor-Wang time-discrete minimizing-movements scheme
[
6
]
(see also
[
28
]
) with a coarse-graining procedure. This pioneering result revealed the emergence of an evolution characterized by new and intriguing phenomena arising from the possible interaction between the two approximation parameters - namely, the lattice and time scales. This line of research has since been further developed by several authors, who have shown that the presence in the model of anisotropies or additional scales, such as in the case of periodic inclusions or oscillating forcing terms, can give rise to additional new and nontrivial features in the resulting motion (see
[
11
,
13
,
14
,
29
,
32
]
and
[
12
]
for an introduction to the subject).

In this paper, following the variational approach outlined above, we begin the analysis of the discrete Blume–Emery–Griffiths (BEG) model introduced in
[
8
]
(see also
[
21
]
and refer to the book
[
31
]
for an introduction to the physics of surfactants in interfacial phenomena). This model describes a three-phase Ising-type system, representing two immiscible phases (for instance, water and oil) in the presence of a third phase acting as a surface-active agent (surfactant). The surfactant, by adsorbing onto the interfaces between the two immiscible phases, can significantly modify the interfacial energy and, consequently, the morphology and evolution of the system.

In the discrete setting, a rigorous variational coarse-graining of this class of surfactant models— including the BEG energy—was obtained in
[
4
]
through
\Gamma
-convergence (see also
[
25
,
26
]
for related, though less rigorous, studies on ground-state behavior). Earlier continuum descriptions of surfactant effects in phase separation problems were developed in
[
22
]
(see also
[
18
,
19
]
for the vectorial and non-local version of the problem) and later extended in
[
1
]
. Building on these results, we now turn to the study of the evolution problem associated with the BEG model, and in particular to its dependence on the conservation (or loss) of surfactant mass during the motion. This effect is captured through a time-discrete minimizing-movements scheme of the type introduced in
[
10
]
, suitably modified by a dissipation potential that incorporates a parameter controlling the degree of surfactant conservation.

From a physical perspective, numerous experimental and theoretical studies have shown that surfactants can profoundly alter the dynamics of interfaces in immiscible fluids. The accumulation of surfactant at the contact line may generate Marangoni stresses that stabilize the interface and induce pinning, whereas surfactant evaporation or redistribution can promote depinning and motion
[
20
,
23
,
24
,
27
,
33
,
34
]
. These competing mechanisms—pinning through surfactant accumulation and depinning through surfactant loss—have a direct analogue in our model, where they correspond to two distinct regimes determined by the value of the control parameter. For small values of this parameter, the surfactant mass is effectively conserved, leading to pinned crystalline evolutions; for large values, evaporation dominates and the motion reduces to crystalline mean curvature flow. The ultimate goal of this paper is to provide a first rigorous description of these phenomena by constructing an explicit example of a single-crystal evolution that qualitatively changes depending on whether surfactant evaporation is allowed or not.

We further notice that many technical difficulties in our problem, and in primis the careful analysis needed to prove the connectedness of the phases along the flow, come from the fact that it involves three (and not only two) phases. Even in the continuum framework, where there is no lattice scale, the study of network evolutions arising as the boundaries of partitions with more than two phases is still not completely understood, despite the many interesting contributions obtained in the last years (see
[
30
]
and the references therein). In the discrete setting, rigorous results on the evolution by minimizing movements of multi-phase interfacial systems are quite scarce. A first example was obtained by the first author in
[
9
]
(see also
[
12
]
and the references therein). The scarcity of rigorous results in the lattice case can be attributed to the intrinsic complexity of the problem: minimizers may be highly non-unique and may develop nontrivial lattice-scale oscillations, usually referred to as microstructures. We end this first part of the introduction by mentioning that, if the aim of the discrete-to-continuum evolution scheme is that of approximating the crystalline mean curvature flow, then other algorithms can be used in order to avoid microstructure formation, hence pinning phenomena or spatial drift (see e.g.,
[
16
]
)

The Blume-Emery-Griffith surfactant model

Given a bounded open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with Lipschitz boundary, on the square lattice
\Omega_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\cap\Omega
we consider the set
{\mathcal{A}}_{\varepsilon}=\{u:\Omega_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow\{\pm 1,0\}\}
of those functions whose values on the nodes of the lattice correspond to particles of water and oil for
u=+1
and
u=-1
, respectively or of surfactant for
u=0
. For a given configuration of particles, the energy of the system is given by

(1.1)

\mathcal{E}^{latt}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\sum_{n.n.}\varepsilon^{2}(-u(p)u(q)+k(u(p)u(q))^{2})

where
n.n.
stands for nearest neighbors and it means that the sum is performed over those
p,q\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
such that
|p-q|=\varepsilon
. Here
k>0
is a constant which represents the quotient between bi-quadratic and quadratic exchange interaction strengths. In
[
4
]
it was shown that, as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, upon identifying arrays
\{u(p)\},p\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
with their piecewise-constant interpolations on the cell of the lattice, the
\Gamma
-limit (with respect to the
L^{1}
-convergence) of
\mathcal{E}^{latt}_{\varepsilon}
is finite on
L^{1}(\Omega;[-1,1])
and it is given by the constant value
2|\Omega|(-1+k)\wedge 0
. Such a value is achieved by one of the three uniform states, namely
u=1
,
u=-1
and
u=0
. As a consequence, on choosing
k<1
one can select the uniform states
u=\pm 1
to be ground states. In the spirit of
development by
\Gamma
-convergence
(see
[
15
]
for a more general treatment of this topic) one can refer the energy to its minimum
m_{\varepsilon}:=\sum_{n.n.}\varepsilon^{2}(k-1)
and further scale the functionals by
\varepsilon
and obtain the new family of functionals

(1.2)

\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(u):=\frac{\mathcal{E}^{latt}_{\varepsilon}(u)-m_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}=\sum_{n.n.}\varepsilon(1-u(p)u(q)-k(1-(u(p)u(q))^{2})).

Observe that in (
1.2
) the interaction energy of two neighboring particles taking both the value
+1
(or
-1
) is zero, while the interaction of a surfactant particle
0
with all the other particles costs the positive value
(1-k)
. For this reason it is also said that the BEG functional describes a
repulsive surfactant model
. At this scaling, a simple computation shows that, on choosing
\frac{1}{3}<k<1
, interposing a particle in the phase
0
between two particles with opposite phases decreases the energy. Hence one can expect that the phase
0
acts as a surfactant phase in this range of values of
k
. This educated guess is confirmed by computing the
\Gamma
-limit as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
(with respect to the
L^{1}
convergence) of the energies
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}
and proving that it is given by the interfacial-type energy

(1.3)

\mathcal{E}(u)=\int_{S(u)}\psi(\nu_{u})\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}

where
u\in BV(\Omega;\{\pm 1\}),\psi(\nu)=(1-k)(3|\nu_{1}|\vee|\nu_{2}|+|\nu_{1}|\wedge|\nu_{2}|)
denotes the anisotropic surface tension,
S(u)
is the jump set of
u
, namely the interface between the phases
\{u=1\}
and
\{u=-1\}
and
\nu_{u}
is the measure theoretic inner normal to
S(u)
.

Time-discrete evolution of the Blume-Emery-Griffith surfactant model

In our work we are interested in energy-driven motions deriving from the functional
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}
. We follow the approach by Braides, Gelli, Novaga
[
10
]
and Braides, Solci
[
12
]
, who coupled the minimizing movements approach a la Almgren, Taylor and Wang (
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
) with a discrete-to-continuous analysis. In our setting the minimizing movements scheme can be described as follows. Given a lattice spacing
\varepsilon>0
, we first introduce a time step
\tau=\tau(\varepsilon)
and an initial datum
u^{\varepsilon}_{0}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\varepsilon}
. Then, we define a discrete motion by successive minimizations: given the spin configuration at the
j
-th time step
u^{\varepsilon}_{j}
we obtain the next configuration
u^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}
as

(1.4)

u^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}\in\text{ argmin}\Bigl\{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(u^{\varepsilon}_{j+1})+\frac{1}{\tau}\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon,\gamma}(u^{\varepsilon}_{j+1},u^{\varepsilon}_{j})\Bigl\},

where
\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon,\gamma}
is a dissipation term which penalizes configurations
u^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}
which are distant from
u^{\varepsilon}_{j}
. In the present case it is given as the sum of two contributions
\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon,\gamma}\coloneqq\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}^{1}+\varepsilon^{\gamma}\mathcal{D}^{0}_{\varepsilon}
. The first one (see (
2.7
)) penalizes large variations between
\{u^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}=1\}
and
\{u^{\varepsilon}_{j}=1\}
, and coincides with the dissipation introduced in
[
10
]
. The second term, instead, penalizes large variations of the total amount of surfactant, i.e.
\#\{u^{\varepsilon}_{j}=0\}
, see (
2.8
). The parameter
\gamma>0
acts as a scaling factor for the second contribution, thereby tuning its influence in the overall dissipation. As it will be further stressed in the following of this introduction, the parameter
\gamma
has a critical role in the conservation of the surfactant mass during the discrete evolution and it is here introduced to account for the influence of the presence (or absence) of surfactant evaporation on the geometry of the evolving phases. We observe that, without this second contribution, i.e. if the amount of surfactant is free to change at every step, then the minimizing movements we considered would coincide with the minimizing movements originated by functional (
1.3
) and already analyzed in
[
12
, Section 4.1.5]
.
We then associate to every function
u^{\varepsilon}_{j}
the set
A_{j}^{\varepsilon}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
given by the union of all the squares of side length
\varepsilon
centered at points
p
where
u^{\varepsilon}_{j}(p)=1
(see (
2.3
)). We focus on the case in which lattice and time scale interact most, that is
\tau(\varepsilon)=\zeta\varepsilon
for some
\zeta>0
, and we choose a single crystal initial datum
u^{\varepsilon}_{0}
, i.e., such that
A^{\varepsilon}_{0}\cap\varepsilon{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
is the discretization of an octagon (the Wulff shape of the functional (
1.3
)). Then for every
t\geq 0
we define the set function
A^{\varepsilon}(t):=A^{\varepsilon}_{\lfloor t/\tau\rfloor}
. We prove that
A^{\varepsilon}(t)
converges uniformly locally in time to a limit flow
A(t)
, and we prove several results which describe how
A(t)
depends on the parameter
\gamma
.

2
we present the setting of the problem and we introduce some notation and definition. In Section
3
we show that the set
A^{\varepsilon}_{j}
is connected as long as its vertical and horizontal diameter are sufficiently large and we prove that at each time step
j
,
A^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}\subset A^{\varepsilon}_{j}
. Then, in the following sections, we discuss two qualitatively different motions corresponding to
\gamma>2
and
\gamma<2
. In Section

we deal with the case
\gamma>2
. We denote by
I^{\varepsilon}_{j}:=\{u^{\varepsilon}_{j}=1\}
and
Z^{\varepsilon}_{j}:=\{u^{\varepsilon}_{j}=0\}

and for a set
J\subset\varepsilon{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
we denote by
\partial^{+}J\subset\varepsilon{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
the set of points not belonging to
J
which are adjacent to a point of
J
.
With this notation, we prove that at every time step
j
the variation of surfactant, i.e.

\frac{\varepsilon^{\gamma}}{\tau}|\#Z^{\varepsilon}_{j}-\#Z^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}|

gives a negligible contribution to the dissipation, and that the minimizing set

is a discrete octagon completely surrounded by surfactant, i.e.

\varepsilon^{2}\#Z^{\varepsilon}_{0}\to 0

First, we prove that at each time step the amount of surfactant remains constant and the minimizer set
I_{j}^{\varepsilon}
is surrounded by surfactant. For a set

we denote by

we prove that the energy of

is given by

\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(u^{\varepsilon}_{j})=2\varepsilon(1-k)\#Z^{\varepsilon}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}(1-k)\Bigl(Per(Z^{\varepsilon}_{j}\cup I^{\varepsilon}_{j})+Per(I_{j}^{\varepsilon})\Bigl).

Recalling that
\#Z^{\varepsilon}_{j}
is constant with respect to
j
at
\varepsilon
fixed, we can explain how the minimization scheme works by simplifying the problem and considering in place of

the energy

(1.5)

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(u^{\varepsilon}_{j}):=Per(Z^{\varepsilon}_{j}\cup I^{\varepsilon}_{j})+Per(I_{j}^{\varepsilon})=Per(R_{Z^{\varepsilon}_{j}\cup I^{\varepsilon}_{j}})+Per(R_{I^{\varepsilon}_{j}}).

I^{\varepsilon}_{j}
is a discrete octagon having at least one of its sloped sides sufficiently long with respect to the scale parameter
\varepsilon
(for the sake of simplicity in this introduction we can think of such a length to be of order

is still an octagon with long sloped sides. However, differently from the situation analysed in Section
4

to time step

(see Proposition

We give an heuristic explanation of the pinning. Using that there exists a long sloped side of
I^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}
, and recalling that
\varepsilon^{2}\#Z^{\varepsilon}_{0}\to 0
, one can show that the minimizer
u^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}
satisfies
Z^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}\cup I^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}\subset R_{I^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}\cup\partial^{+}I^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}}.
Since we already know that
I^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}
is surrounded by surfactant, it follows that
Per(R_{Z^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}\cup I^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}})=Per(R_{I^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}\cup\partial^{+}I^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}})

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(u^{\varepsilon}_{j+1})=\Bigl(Per(R_{I^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}})+8\varepsilon\Bigl)+Per(R_{I^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}})=8\varepsilon+2Per(R_{I^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}}).

In particular the energy depends only on the perimeter of the minimizing set
I^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}
, and not on the set

. As a consequence of that and of the a priori estimate on the Hausdorff distance between consecutive optimal shapes (see Proposition

\tilde{I}^{\varepsilon}_{j+1}
are pinned with respect to the sloped sides of

. Finally from by the definition of

we have that

We can summarize the results described above by saying that they match the main features of surfactant-induced-pinning phenomena as those observed in physical systems involving surfactant-mediated interface motion. In this context of evaporating or surfactant-laden droplets, the removal or redistribution of surfactant can indeed lead either to smooth motion of the contact line or to its partial arrest (see, e.g.,
[
7
]
). In other words, in phase separation phenomena in presence of surfactant, mass conservation or slow exchange of surfactant may hinder domain evolution and induce metastable configurations. These observations are consistent with the two limiting regimes captured by our discrete model: for
\gamma>2
the surfactant effectively evaporates and the crystal evolves by crystalline mean curvature flow, whereas for
\gamma<2
the conservation of surfactant mass produces constrained, partially pinned motions.

Formulation of the problem

Notation

We denote by
{\mathbb{N}}
the sets of natural numbers starting from
1
. We denote by
\varepsilon{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
the square lattice with lattice spacing
\varepsilon>0
. We will call the points of
\varepsilon{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
with the letters
p,q
. When dealing with more than two points we name them as
p^{i}
, and we denote by
p^{i}=(p^{i}_{1},p^{i}_{2})
their components. We write
p\preccurlyeq q
to say that
p_{1}\leq q_{1}
and
p_{2}\leq q_{2}
. The symmetric difference of two sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is denoted by
A\triangle B
, their Hausdorff distance by
d_{\mathcal{H}}(A,B)
. For
i=1,2
we denote by
e_{i}
the standard basis of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Given
p\in\varepsilon{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
we define
\mathcal{N}(p):=\{p\pm\varepsilon e_{i}:\>i=1,2\}
so that, in particular,
\#\mathcal{N}(p)=4
for every
p\in\varepsilon{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
. For
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
\lfloor x\rfloor
and
\lceil x\rceil
the floor and ceiling of
x
, respectively. When no ambiguity arises, we write
[x]
to denote the closest integer to
x
. In the proofs we will often use the symbol

to denote a generic constant, whose value may change form line to line.

Setting of the problem

On the scaled square lattice
\varepsilon{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
we consider the set

{\mathcal{A}}_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\Bigl\{u\colon\varepsilon{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\to\{\pm 1,0\}\text{ such that }\#\{p\in\varepsilon{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}:u(p)=0\}<+\infty\Bigl\}

and the family of energies
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}:{\mathcal{A}}_{\varepsilon}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
defined as follows

(2.1)

\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\sum_{n.n.}\varepsilon(1-u(p)u(q)-k(1-(u(p)u(q))^{2})),

where

First stage of the minimizing movements: pinning of the diagonals.

Remark 5.5
.

In this remark we give an outline of the following of this section.

The minimizing movements can be divided in two qualitatively different stages. In the first one the minimizer is an octagon at each step, having at least a diagonal side of length bigger than
2\sqrt{2}\varepsilon\sqrt{C_{\varepsilon}}
. During this stage the diagonal sides having positive length remain pinned, while the parallel sides move inwards without changing their orientation. We computed the sides displacements in Proposition
5.8
. In this paragraph, before stating this Proposition, we introduce two Lemmas.
In the first one
5.6
we discuss the optimal placement of the surfactant set
Z_{j}
around
I_{j},
and we introduce a peculiarity of the case
\gamma<2,
i.e. that the surfactant placement is not uniquely determined, not even when
I_{j}
is known (in particular it is no longer true that
Z_{j}=\partial^{+}I_{j}
for every
j
). We deal with this problem as follows. First, we observe that only the cardinality of
Z_{j}
(and not its geometrical shape) plays a role in the minimization problem
\argmin\{\mathcal{F}^{\tau,\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,u_{j})\}
, in particular the set
I_{j+1}
depends only on
I_{j}
and on
\#Z_{j}=C_{\varepsilon}
. Second, since we require that
\varepsilon^{2}C_{\varepsilon}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we have that the surfactant does not appear in the limit flow. Therefore, since our main concern is to describe the limit flow of the phase
1
(which, as just mentioned, depends only on the sets
I_{j}
), in the construction of the minimizing movement
(u^{\varepsilon}_{j})_{j}
, we will only describe the shape of
I_{j}
, and we give only an approximate description of
Z_{j}
. This description is the content of Lemma
5.6
.

The second preliminary result of this paragraph is the technical Lemma
5.7
, which contains some preliminary remarks concerning the geometry of the set
I_{j+1},
and the distance of its boundary from the boundary of
I_{j}
.

The second stage of the motion is completely different, and will be analyzed in the next paragraph. During this stage, the minimizer
I_{j}
is an
\varepsilon
-quasi-rectangle at each time step, and in this case the sides displacements are given in Proposition
5.13
. However, in this second stage a precise description of the motion is more difficult. Therefore, we studied the minimizing movements of a special class of minimizers (the “good minimizers”, see Definition
5.11
).

In the final Theorem
5.14
we computed the limit flow, which follows immediately from the results of Propositions
5.8
and
5.13
.

In the following Lemma we show a possible way to arrange the surfactant set of a minimizer
u_{j+1}
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\gamma<2
. Let
u_{j},\ u_{j+1}\in\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}
be such that
u_{j}
satisfies assumption (
H
) of Subsection

, and

is a minimizer for
\mathcal{F}^{\tau,\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,u_{j})
