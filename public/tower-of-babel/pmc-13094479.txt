The analysis of moisture migration and distribution during nut drying process based on ODT-CNN by LF-NMR
Authors: Yang Yi, Ke Yang, Shan Zeng, Long Wang, Bing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding moisture distribution in nuts is crucial for optimizing the drying process, a complex challenge since these internal changes are not externally visible. Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) can address this by providing both T2 relaxation signals to quantify moisture state and magnetic resonance (MR) images to visualize its distribution. However, methods that rely on a single data type are inherently limited: T2 signals provide quantitative detail but lack spatial context, while MR images provide spatial context but lack quantitative detail on water mobility. This trade-off leads to an incomplete and inaccurate assessment of the true dryness state. To overcome this issue, the One-dimension Transformer Convolutional Neural Network (ODT-CNN) is proposed, which a deep learning model designed to synergistically fuse these complementary data sources. The model features two specialized branches: a Transformer encoder captures long-range dependencies in the 1D T2 relaxation signals, while a CNN extracts hierarchical spatial features from the 2D MR images. The information from both branches is then integrated to achieve a holistic assessment. This fusion methodology achieved a state-of-the-art accuracy of 97.41%, significantly outperforming single-modality approaches. Furthermore, a water activity evaluation index based on the least squares method was proposed to assess the dryness of nuts also. This study highlights the potential of LF-NMR technology for analyzing water loss and distribution during nut drying, providing a reliable method and theoretical basis for accurately assessing nut dryness.Keywords: LF-NMR, Nuts, Moisture distribution, Non-destructive testing, Deep learning

Introduction

Nuts are rich in unsaturated fatty acids, flavonoids, vitamins and a variety of essential nutrients for the human body. They have significant medicinal and health benefits and are widely loved by consumers. The proper drying process is crucial in the production of nut, as it effectively removes moisture, inhibiting the growth and reproduction of microorganisms, preventing spoilage, and prolonging storage time, while also preserving nutrients and enhancing flavor (Chen et al., 2021; Turan, 2018). However, insufficient drying will shorten storage time and cause economic losses, while excessive drying can destroy nutrients and result in unnecessary waste. Therefore, the degree of drying significantly impacts the storage duration and edible value of nut, and monitoring and controlling this process is essential.

Currently, various detection technologies are increasingly applied to analyze drying degree of agricultural products. For instance, Sun et al. used near-infrared hyperspectral images (HSI-NIR) to assess the drying process of melons under different pretreatment methods. Lin et al. employed hyperspectral imaging systems (HIS) to monitor the drying process of vegetables in a microwave vacuum. Amir et al. combined a near-infrared spectrometer with multivariate analysis to evaluate the ultrasonic drying process. Onu et al. utilized adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference systems (ANFIS), artificial neural networks (ANN), and response surface methodology (RSM) to detect the drying process of potatoes (Li et al., 2024; Lin and Sun, 2022; Malvandi et al., 2022; Netto et al., 2021; Onu et al., 2020; Pan et al., 2024). These studies have shown that detecting moisture changes is a crucial indicator for evaluating the state of dryness.

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) technologies have great advantages in analyzing changes in moisture content and distribution due to their highly sensitive monitoring of hydrogen nuclei in moisture atoms. With strong penetration, diverse chemical composition analysis, simple operation, and low detection cost, these technologies have become powerful tools for examining the state, fluidity, and distribution of moisture in various foods (He et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024). The T2 relaxation signals obtained through the Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG) sequence provides detailed information about the state of moisture in the food matrix and its binding properties. Concurrently, magnetic resonance (MR) images visualizes the spatial distribution of moisture and its visual changes during storage and processing stages. Previous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of LF-NMR in identifying food adulteration, monitoring moisture during fruit processing, and determining the degree of fruit decay (Cheng et al., 2020; Qiao et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2018; Zhu et al., 2021; Musse et al., 2009). However, these studies often overlook the complex, non-linear relationships between moisture content, its spatial distribution, and the resulting tissue structure changes. Traditional image analysis, which relies on handcrafted feature extraction, can capture global changes but often fails to interpret the subtle, localized patterns of moisture loss and tissue shrinkage that signify the transition between drying stages. Similarly, while traditional machine learning models can handle T2 relaxed data, they struggle to combine it with the high-dimensional spatial information provided by magnetic resonance imaging, such as spatial correlations, textural patterns, and morphological features.

Therefore, to address the above-mentioned limitations, the objectives of this study are as follows: (1) To utilize LF-NMR to monitor the drying process of nuts and acquire T2 relaxation data and MR images, followed by a systematic evaluation of the limitations of single-source approaches for moisture activity characterization; (2) To establish a moisture activity evaluation framework by introducing a classification criterion based on the inflection characteristics of moisture content and shrinkage, thereby enabling the identification of transitions between constant-rate and falling-rate drying stages and providing a physically interpretable basis for moisture activity characterization; (3) The information fusion network ODT-CNN is proposed that integrates T2 relaxation data and MR images to improve the accuracy of moisture activity identification and compensate for the deficiencies of single-source approaches. Overall, this study provides a new perspective for moisture activity evaluation and offers a data-driven approach to support drying process analysis.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

Fresh chestnuts were harvested from commercial orchards in Luotian District, Hubei Province, China. To ensure uniform physiological maturity and consistent initial moisture content, harvesting was strictly conducted during the second week of November 2023, corresponding to the peak maturity period. Following collection, samples were promptly transported to the laboratory under temperature-controlled conditions to minimize moisture loss and prevent thermal stress. These chestnuts were promptly transported to the laboratory for subsequent experiments. About 120 chestnuts without obvious damage or contamination and with a weight difference within ±5g were randomly selected for testing. In order to obtain chestnut samples of different dryness, fresh chestnuts were stored in an incubator at 25 °C and a constant temperature drying oven with a humidity of 40-45% to simulate the natural drying process during storage at room temperature according to the method outlined by Pino-Hernández (Donis-González et al., 2012; Pino-Hernandez et al., 2022; Pino-Hernández et al., 2021). To characterize the changes at discrete stages of drying, samples were temporarily removed from the incubator every two days over a two-week period for LF-NMR analysis. This offline measurement approach provided a series of static snapshots representing the progressive stages of dryness. After each measurement, the samples were returned to the incubator to continue the drying process. The selected chestnut samples retained their outer shells during the drying process, and no obvious external defects, such as mold or cracks, were observed on the shells.

T2 relaxation signals and MR images measurements

Measurements of T2 relaxation signals and MR images were performed using an NMI20 series MesoMR12-060H-HTHP NMR imaging analyzer (Niumag Analytical Instrument Corp., Shanghai, China). The instrument, equipped with a 0.5T superconducting magnet and a 60 mm inner-diameter probe, has been extensively utilized in food research (Cong et al., 2024; Ezeanaka et al., 2019; Ribeiro et al., 2014). For each measurement session, the instrument was first preheated to a stable internal temperature of 32 °C to ensure signal consistency. Following preheating, an automated calibration procedure was performed using a standard rapeseed phantom to optimize the instrument's parameters. This standard phantom was used solely for instrument calibration and is not part of the experimental chestnut samples. After the successful calibration, the phantom was removed, and a single chestnut sample was placed in the center of the probe's detection area. The CPMG sequence was then executed to acquire the T2 transverse relaxation data. From this, the relaxation times (T21, T22, T23) and their corresponding signal amplitudes (A21, A22, A23) were calculated through an inversion process. This process aimed to convert the qualitative image data into a set of numerical descriptors suitable for analysis by machine learning models. The grayscale intensity data from each MR image served as the basis for this feature calculation. Following the methodology of Qiao (Qiao et al., 2019), a suite of eight statistical features was computed from the grayscale values to quantify different aspects of the image. Mean represents the average pixel intensity and indicates the overall brightness. Variance describes the extent to which pixel values deviate from the mean, signifying global contrast. Inertia measures the local contrast complexity and textural heterogeneity. Average Gradient captures the structural sharpness and edge information of tissue shrinkage. Mode identifies the most frequently occurring gray value, representing the dominant signal component. Chestnut dryness detection

Flowchart of chestnut dryness assessment

T2 relaxation signals and MR images measurements were conducted on chestnut samples to gather features into their internal composition. T2 relaxation signals measurements provide crucial information on the relaxation time T2 and relaxation signal amplitude A2 of chestnuts, derived from relaxation spectrum analysis during the attenuation process. The relaxation time T2 serves as an indicator of the moisture type within the sample, discernible through distinct peaks in the relaxation spectrum. These peaks offer insights into the presence of bound water, immobilized water, and free water in the sample, based on their appearance times. Additionally, the signal amplitude A2 correlates with the content of various moisture types present in the sample, with higher signal amplitudes indicating greater moisture content (Guan et al., 2023; Qiao et al., 2019). MR imaging scans capture images of the drying process, providing visualization of moisture distribution within the sample. These images offer insights into the chestnut's interior, particularly highlighting the shrinkage process caused by drying. Notably, although T2 mapping can theoretically be constructed using Equation (2), the low signal-to-noise ratio in low-field (0.5T) environments makes pixel-wise T2 fitting inaccurate for complex biological matrices (Does et al., 2019). Instead, this study utilizes PDWI for spatial visualization and CPMG for quantitative relaxation.

Utilizing the distinctive characteristics of T2 relaxation information and MR images, chestnut samples can be defined as moisture active or moisture inactive based on least squares analysis. The moisture active state corresponds to the constant-rate drying stage, characterized by high concentrations of free water and immobilized water. Conversely, the moisture inactive state signifies the falling-rate drying stage and the onset of storage stability (Almeida et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2023), where free water is largely removed, leaving primarily bound water and tightly associated immobilized water (Iji et al., 2025; Lo et al., 2020; Meenu et al., 2021; Xue et al., 2009). The ODT-CNN dryness evaluation network is proposed to fuse T2 relaxation signals with spatial features extracted from MR images, thereby enabling a robust assessment of nut dryness. The performance of the ODT-CNN framework is evaluated using classification accuracy, defined as the proportion of correctly identified moisture active or inactive samples relative to the total number of samples in the test set. Specifically, the model is designed to predict the Moisture Activity Status, a binary classification that distinguishes between the active dehydration phase and the stable moisture phase. By leveraging the complementary information from both data modalities, the network achieves accurate classification and serves as a reliable decision-support tool for identifying optimal drying endpoints in industrial nut processing, where internal transformations are not externally observable. ODT-CNN: A fusion architecture

To effectively fuse the fundamentally different T2 relaxation and MR image data, a standard, single-branch neural network is unsuitable. Therefore, the One-dimension Transformer Convolutional Neural Network (ODT-CNN) is designed, a architecture with two specialized branches tailored to the unique characteristics of each data type, as depicted in Fig. 2.

(1)One Dimension Transformer

One Dimension Transformer

ODT-CNN model structure diagram.

T2 relaxation signals data typically consists of time series data with A2 and T2 values, which exhibit time dependence and sequence relationships (Kiselev and Novikov, 2018; Matviychuk et al., 2019). There can be long-term dependencies between distant data points in the sequence, meaning that earlier data points may influence the current point. Additionally, the relationship between A2 and T2 values, as different feature dimensions, must be considered simultaneously. To build an effective encoding model for this type of data, a Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) encoder is used. The Transformer's self-attention mechanism effectively captures long-distance dependencies in the sequence, and compared to traditional RNN models like LSTM and GRU (Ni et al., 2023; Sherstinsky, 2020; Zhao et al., 2017), it performs better with long sequences as it can directly calculate global dependencies. Furthermore, the Transformer can comprehensively consider the relationships between A2 and T2 values throughout the entire sequence, thereby more effectively extracting and fusing features.

The Transformer encoder primarily relies on self-attention networks (SAN) to encode the input sequence into a latent representation space. For a sequence data x={x1,…,xl} of length l, it is initially mapped into embedding vectors. These vectors are then augmented with positional encodings to create the input representation, denoted as Ix. Subsequently, the input representation Ix is multiplied by query matrix Qx, key matrix Kx, and value matrix Vx to derive corresponding Q, K, and V matrices. As illustrated in the structure diagram on Fig. 2, T2 relaxation signals are segmented into relaxation times and corresponding relaxation signal amplitudes. Each pair, consisting of a relaxation time and its corresponding signal amplitude, is treated as a data set and fed into the linear projection of the couple data block for initial encoding. This block, composed of multiple linear layers, encodes each data pair into a single token and adjusts the token's size to be compatible with the Transformer Encoder.

Following initial encoding, these tokens are passed into the Transformer Encoder for further encoding and feature extraction. The architecture of the Transformer Encoder is built from N Transformer Encoder blocks. Each block includes two Layer Normalization modules that normalize the data to facilitate the efficient computation of attention scores, thereby accelerating model training and enhancing generalization capabilities. A Feedforward Neural Network (FNN) within each block performs linear transformations on the vectors at each position, augmenting the model's ability to represent data. Additionally, a Multi-Head Attention module leverages multiple attention heads to conduct related operations in parallel, enhancing computational efficiency and capturing dependencies across different positions in the input sequence. (2)Convolutional Neural Network

Convolutional Neural Network

MR images can visually display the complex texture, shape, and contours of the internal slices of the nut, exhibiting strong spatial correlation with adjacent pixels being highly correlated. As drying progresses, these images reveal subtle but significant changes, such as tissue shrinkage, the formation of cracks, and heterogeneous moisture distribution, which are difficult to quantify with simple metrics. Traditional feature extraction methods provide a global summary but are insensitive to the localized textural and morphological features that are critical for distinguishing between a “Moisture Active” and “Moisture Inactive” state. To address this, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) were chosen for feature extraction from MR images. The strength of CNNs lies in their hierarchical learning ability. Their unique local receptive field mechanism allows them to automatically learn and identify low-level features like edges and textures in the initial layers, and then progressively combine these into more complex, abstract features in deeper layers. Furthermore, the inherent translation invariance of CNNs is crucial for this application, as it allows the network to accurately identify these key features regardless of minor variations in the chestnut's position or orientation within the MR image. This capability is essential for building a robust model that overcomes the limitations of manual feature engineering and can reliably interpret the complex visual data associated with the drying process.

In recent years, the development of high-performance CNN has surged, with notable examples including GoogLeNet (Szegedy et al., 2015, 2016), VGGnet (Simonyan and Zisserman, 2015), ResNet (He et al., 2016), and ResNeXt (Xie et al., 2017). These models have achieved impressive classification results in the ImageNet competition, a benchmark in image classification tasks. Motivated by these advancements, our experimental approach for analyzing chestnut samples involved employing multiple CNNs architectures to perform feature extraction on MR images. The models selected for this purpose included VGG16, Inception V3, ResNet34, ResNet50, ResNet101, ResNeXt50, and ResNeXt101. After an extensive comparison and assessment of various performance metrics across these models, ResNet50 was chosen as the optimal feature extraction network for MR images of chestnut samples. The decision was based on its superior balance of accuracy and computational efficiency, making it well-suited for our application. Details of the performance indicator comparisons and the rationale behind selecting ResNet50 are provided in the subsequent experimental section of this paper.

(3)MLP Head

MLP Head

The innovation of the ODT-CNN lies in its ability to synergize these two streams of information. The high-level feature vectors extracted from both the ODT and CNN branches are concatenated, creating a single, comprehensive representation that encapsulates both the quantitative moisture state and its spatial distribution. Based on the work of Alexey Dosovitskiy and others (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021), our model incorporates a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) Head positioned at the end of the architecture. During the training phase, the MLP Head comprises multiple fully connected layers followed by a softmax layer. However, during the testing phase, it is simplified to include only a single fully connected layer and a softmax layer. The primary function of the MLP Head is to integrate the output features extracted by the ODT and the CNN, transforming them into a more compact representation. This amalgamation of features enhances the model's ability to perform the final classification task effectively.

Machine learning models and hyperparameter

To comprehensively evaluate the discriminative capability of T2 relaxation features, five representative classification algorithms are employed, including Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Back Propagation Neural Network (BPNN) (LaValley, 2008; Nalepa and Kawulok, 2019; Rigatti, 2017; Velthoen et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2015). Specifically, LR models the posterior probability distribution through a multinomial logistic function, thereby providing a linear decision boundary in the feature space. To ensure convergence, the LR model is configured with the Limited-memory Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (L-BFGS) optimizer, a multinomial loss function, and a maximum of 100 iterations. In contrast, SVM constructs an optimal separating hyperplane by maximizing the margin between classes, thereby enhancing generalization performance. To effectively capture nonlinear relationships, the SVM employs a radial basis function (RBF) kernel with a regularization parameter of C=1.0 and a kernel coefficient of γ≈0.071. This configuration helps prevent overfitting on the relatively small-scale dataset.

For ensemble learning approaches, RF and XGBoost are implemented to address the high variance and nonlinear interactions. RF, as a bagging-based ensemble method, aggregates multiple decision trees to reduce variance and improve robustness against noise (Demir and Şahin, 2022; Rigatti, 2017; Velthoen et al., 2023). The RF model is constructed using 100 decision trees, with a maximum tree depth limited to 10 and a minimum of 2 samples required to split an internal node. These constraints control model complexity, preventing individual trees from overgrowing while maintaining sufficient representational capacity. Similarly, XGBoost extends the gradient boosting framework by introducing second-order optimization and regularization, enabling efficient learning of complex nonlinear relationships while controlling overfitting. The XGBoost model is configured with 100 boosting iterations, a maximum tree depth of 6, and a learning rate of 0.01, which together balance learning efficiency and model stability.

Furthermore, BPNN is employed as a deep learning baseline, utilizing multilayer nonlinear transformations and gradient-based backpropagation to approximate complex mappings between input features and output labels. A fully connected neural network architecture is implemented, consisting of an input layer with 14 neurons, a hidden layer with 50 neurons, and an output layer corresponding to the target categories. The hyperbolic tangent (tanh) function is used as the activation function to introduce nonlinearity. Model parameters are optimized using the Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam) algorithm with a learning rate of 0.01, which ensures stable convergence and mitigates oscillatory behavior during training. A cross-entropy loss function is adopted in conjunction with mini-batch gradient descent using a batch size of 64. The network is trained for 100 epochs.

Results and discussion

Moisture activity division by moisture content and shrinkage

The degree of drying in food is often determined by monitoring its moisture activity throughout the drying process (Fathi et al., 2022; Lertworasirikul and Tipsuwan, 2008). In nuts, moisture activity is defined as a proposed evaluation index to dynamically assess the degree of dryness, which is typically closely linked to moisture content and internal shrinkage. Internal shrinkage refers to the volumetric reduction and atrophy of the internal tissue as moisture is lost from the cellular matrix. To investigate this relationship, an experiment was conducted using 102 chestnut samples with their hulls intact. Specially, the shrinkage data was derived from the area reduction of the internal nut tissue within the MR images, and these values were correlated with the moisture content (Lufu et al., 2020; Rani and Tripathy, 2020). As shown in Fig. 3a, the x-axis represents the moisture content, the y-axis represents the internal shrinkage, and each scatter point corresponds to a sample. In order to effectively propose the moisture activity evaluation metric, a fitting curve is used to map the data distribution patterns. Given the data's distribution characteristics, a logarithmic function, y=alogb⁡(x)+c, is chosen for fitting. Using the least squares method, the optimal fitting parameters a, b, and c are determined for all data points. The resulting fitted curve is depicted in Fig. 3a.

(a) Fitting curve of chestnut sample moisture content and shrinkage data using least squares method; (b) First derivative curve and threshold of fitting curve; (c) Second derivative curve and threshold of fitting curve; (d) Scatter plot of chestnut data classified according to the selected threshold.

Following curve fitting, the first and second-order derivatives of the fitted curve were calculated to identify the inflection point, where the rate of change of the first-order derivative alters significantly. Specifically, the inflection point was defined as the first point on the first-order derivative curve where the difference between the derivatives of two neighboring data points is less than or equal to 0.03% (Cruz-Tirado et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2023). This point marks a significant shift in the data's growth rate, holding both physical and statistical significance, as shown in Fig. 3b and c. Based on this criterion, the process was categorized into two distinct stages: the “moisture active” phase, which precedes the threshold and represents the constant-rate drying period characterized by rapid moisture migration; and the “moisture inactive” phase, which follows the threshold, signifying the falling-rate period and the onset of structural and moisture stability, illustrated in Fig. 3d. This definition method, rooted in the inflection point, not only accurately captures the data's intrinsic structure and characteristics but also provides more precise and meaningful criteria for subsequent analysis and applications (Almeida et al., 2025).

T2 relaxation information analysis

Analysis of T2 relaxation curves

The T2 transverse relaxation time as a critical data in T2 relaxation information reflects the mobility of molecular substances. An analysis of the transverse relaxation time inversion spectra from 200 chestnut samples revealed significant differences in the mobility between lipid and other macromolecules compared to water molecules. Specifically, lipid macromolecules, which are present in high amounts, exhibited reduced and more stable mobility. In contrast, water molecules, which are less abundant, showed higher mobility. Fig. 4 shows the variation trends of ten representative transverse relaxation spectra from the sample set and the transverse relaxation spectrum of the same chestnut sample over 16 days. Notably, the amplitudes corresponding to the three principal relaxation peaks are clearly identified and annotated in the figure, facilitating interpretation of the underlying moisture state transitions.

(a) Transverse relaxation spectra of 10 chestnut samples collected for the first time; (b) Variation trend of the transverse relaxation spectrum of the same chestnut sample over 16 days.

Observing the T2 inversion spectrum of chestnut in Fig. 4, we can find that it is characterized by three distinct peaks: T21, T22, and T23. The leftmost peak, T21 (0.01 ms to 10 ms), corresponds to bound water located primarily in the plant cell walls, occupying small voids and strong water-binding sites which limit its mobility. The middle peak, T22 (10 ms to 100 ms), represents immobilized water associated with macromolecules such as carbohydrates, showing the largest signal amplitude due to the chestnut's high content of starch, lipids, and other macromolecules. The rightmost peak, T23 (100 ms to 1000 ms), reflects free water found in the large voids and structural cellulose-rich regions of the plant cell, representing the most mobile water fraction. The free water predominantly found in the vesicles of plant cells, exhibits the highest mobility but the smallest signal amplitude. By analyzing the changes in T2 (T21, T22, T23) and their corresponding amplitudes A2 (A21, A22, A23), it is possible to assess the moisture activity and dryness in chestnuts, since the moisture content significantly influences fruit quality.

After 16 days of continuous observation, the storage process of chestnuts revealed variable changes in the amplitude values (A2) over time. Fig. 5a illustrates a general downward trend in A2, with a notably stable decline rate. A22 experienced the most significant and rapid decrease, followed by A21, suggesting that some immobilized water, which has relatively higher mobility than bound water and easier to lose. Conversely, A23 displayed the smallest and slowest decline, attributed to its lower overall content, as depicted in Fig. 5c. Changes in the proportions of A21, A22, and A23 within A2 were also noted. According to Fig. 5d, the proportion of A21 continuously increased, while the proportions of A22 and A23 decreased, indicating that the primary losses during storage were immobilized and free water. Bound water, which is relatively stable due to its binding to biological macromolecules and cell structures, exhibited minimal loss. Fluctuations in T2 also varied during storage, as shown in Fig. 5b. T21 remained mostly unchanged, indicating stable bound water conditions. In contrast, T22 showed a continuous and accelerating decrease, suggesting a decreasing association of water with surrounding structures. During the first 12 days, T23 fluctuates between 500 ms and 800 ms, which is closely related to environmental factors such as temperature and humidity. After 14 days, T23 is no longer detectable, leaving only the T21 and T22 peaks visible in the T2 spectrum. This disappearance suggests that this fraction of moisture has either been lost or its concentration has fallen below the detection threshold. Based on the above analysis, the relaxation characteristics of chestnuts changed significantly during the natural drying process during storage. These changes can thus serve as reliable indicators for assessing the moisture activity and dryness in chestnuts.

Detection of T2 relaxation information

A total of 540 samples are included in this study, of which 420 samples are assigned to the training set, 60 samples to the validation set, and the remaining 60 samples to the test set. The distribution of samples between the two categories, “Moisture Active” and “Moisture Inactive,” is approximately balanced across all subsets to avoid classification bias. The input features consist of relaxation times (T21, T22, T23), amplitudes (A21, A22, A23), and their corresponding amplitude occupation rates (R21, R22, R23). These features are used to train multiple models, including LR, SVM, RF, XGBoost, and BPNN, with their respective hyperparameter configurations described in Section 2.4.3. To ensure consistency and eliminate scale-related bias, all input variables are normalized to the range of (0, 1), and the output labels are encoded as 1 for “Moisture Active” and 2 for “Moisture Inactive.” All experiments in this study are conducted based on these three subsets, where the training set is used for model fitting, the validation set for hyperparameter tuning, and the test set for the final performance evaluation. For clarity, the “training accuracy” reported in the tables corresponds to the performance evaluated on the validation set, while the “validation accuracy” refers to the performance obtained on the test set.

As shown in Table 2, the five machine learning models achieved test accuracies ranging from 68.55% to 81.87%. The best-performing model, XGBoost, reached 81.87%. This approach of applying machine learning algorithms to LF-NMR relaxation data is consistent with methodologies used in other food science applications, such as assessing the water content in apple slices (Guan et al., 2023) or detecting decay in blueberries (Qiao et al., 2019). However, a notable finding in results is the significant performance gap between classes, which is further elucidated by the performance heatmap in Fig. 6a, which reveals a persistent diagonal band of reduced accuracy along the decision boundary. This discrepancy suggests that while T2 relaxation data is effective at characterizing the relatively stable water states outside the threshold, it lacks the detailed structural information needed to resolve ambiguity. This limitation highlights the necessity of incorporating a different data modality that can capture these spatial features, thereby providing a more complete picture of the nut's internal state.

MR images analysis

Analysis of MR images

MR imaging technology offers a non-destructive means to visually analyze the interior of samples in a quick and safe manner. In this study, MR images was used to monitor the internal atrophy process of chestnuts stored at room temperature over several days. This method allows for more precise assessments of the moisture activity and dryness of the chestnuts. Fig. 7 illustrates the changes in the internal structure of the chestnuts over the course of 16 days, with observations on days 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, and 16. The first and second rows display MR images and corresponding pseudo-color images of vertical sections of the chestnut samples, respectively. The third and fourth rows show the same for horizontal sections of the chestnuts. Fig. 7 has a spatial resolution of 0.3125 mm/pixel, and the right side shows its color bar, which is used to indicate the signal strength of the magnetic resonance image. In the grayscale images, a higher gray value corresponds to a lighter shade and a stronger signal, while a lower gray value indicates a darker shade and a weaker signal. In the pseudo-color images, a stronger signal is represented by a redder color, and a weaker signal by a bluer color. This visual representation helps in understanding the progression of atrophy and dryness within the chestnuts as storage and drying time increases.

Vertical (a) and horizontal (b) slice magnetic resonance images and corresponding pseudo-color images of chestnuts stored for 16 consecutive days.

As depicted in Fig. 7, chestnuts undergo a gradual atrophy process during storage, with the shrinking area expanding over time. The images captured at 0 and 2 days display a fresh, normal chestnut appearance, with a full interior and uniform moisture distribution, evidenced by a stronger signal closer to the center indicating higher moisture content. By days 4 and 6, the chestnuts exhibit signs of being sightly drying, with visible moisture loss leading to a slight shrinking of the peripheral outline. However, distinguishing them from fresh chestnuts with the naked eye alone difficult. From day 8 onwards, the chestnuts display slight atrophy, with noticeable dark areas on several sections, indicating the destruction of biological tissues caused by the atrophy during drying process. These atrophy areas experience significant moisture loss. Images captured on days 10, 12, 14, and 16 show further expansion of the atrophy areas, indicating worsening atrophy. Additionally, due to substantial moisture loss, the middle of the chestnuts continues to shrink towards the upper and lower ends, resulting in visible cracks. Ultimately, the chestnuts lose moisture to the extent that MR image signal capture becomes difficult. Notably, the outer shell of the chestnuts does not visibly manifest the change, making it challenging to determine their atrophy and drying status through external observation alone.

ODT-CNN model for evaluation of moisture activity

The preceding experiments established that single-modality methods face complementary limitations: T2-based models struggle with late-stage drying samples, while MR-based models are less effective for early-stage drying ones. This provides a clear rationale for a fusion approach. The ODT-CNN model was designed specifically for this purpose, integrating both data types to overcome these individual weaknesses and provide a more robust evaluation.

The initial validation of this fusion strategy involved training the ODT-CNN models with MR images and processed T2 relaxation features. The ODT module is configured based on a standard Transformer encoder architecture, consisting of 2 encoder layers with 4 attention heads, a hidden embedding dimension of 64, and a feedforward network dimension of 128. A dropout rate of 0.1 is applied to enhance generalization, and layer normalization is employed to stabilize training. The model is optimized using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001. The CNN encoder follows the same parameter settings as described in Section 3.3.3 to ensure consistency across experiments. In addition, the MLP used for feature fusion and classification consists of two fully connected layers with 128 and 64 neurons, respectively, and employs the ReLU activation function to introduce nonlinearity. These parameters are selected to balance model capacity and computational efficiency, considering the relatively small dataset size. As shown in Table 5, this approach yielded a significant performance boost over all single-modality methods. The top-performing model, ResNeXt101+ODT, achieved an impressive overall accuracy of 94.19%. This result confirms that the fusion of quantitative T2 features and spatial MR image features provides a more comprehensive data representation, leading to a superior classification outcome. The remarkable success of this design is visualized in Fig. 6d. The performance heatmap for the ODT-CNN is uniformly high across the entire data space, demonstrating that it has successfully overcome the weaknesses observed in the other models. By synergistically fusing the two data streams, the model resolves the vertical ambiguity of the T2-only model and the horizontal ambiguity of the image-only model, maintaining exceptional accuracy even along the difficult diagonal classification threshold.

Conclusion

In this study, a fusion framework integrating LF-NMR T2 relaxation data and MR images is developed to characterize moisture activity during the drying process of chestnuts. The results indicate that models based solely on T2 relaxation data or MR images are insufficient to fully describe the complexity of internal moisture dynamics. In contrast, the proposed ODT-CNN model, which integrates quantitative T2 relaxation features with MR image information, achieves improved classification performance, reaching an accuracy of 97.41% on the test set, thereby demonstrating the advantage of information fusion. The proposed framework is better interpreted as a decision-support tool for moisture activity identification rather than a precise measurement method. In practical applications, it may assist in determining suitable drying endpoints and provide support for optimizing drying strategies in nut processing. However, as the study is conducted on a single type of nut under controlled experimental conditions, further validation across different materials and industrial environments is necessary. Overall, this work demonstrates the potential of combining LF-NMR data and MR images with machine learning methods for supporting drying process analysis in a data-driven manner.
