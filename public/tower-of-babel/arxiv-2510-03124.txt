Pointed Hopf algebras revisited, with a view from tensor categories Thanks: The work was partially supported by CONICET and Secyt (UNC)
Iván Angiono

Abstract
. Hopf algebras appear in connection with various problems in Pure Mathematics and Theoretical Physics, mainly through their categoriesof representations, which are examples of tensor categories. In recent years, there have been major advances in the classification of finite-dimensional Hopf algebras over the complex numbers, especially when restricted to the pointed case. In this survey, we aim to review the main results in this direction, stating the classification theorems recently obtained and the problems still open, and describing the tools needed to solve the problem by means of the so-called Lifting Method of Andruskiewitsch and Schneider. We will highlight a more categorical point of view related to these classification results, which offers for a better description of the so-called liftings up to involving cocycle deformations, and which in turn allows certain problems to be reduced to the associated graded pointed Hopf algebras. We will emphasise this point of view with applications to other contexts related to the aforementioned pointed Hopf algebras, such as finite pointed tensor categories, Hopf algebras that do not satisfy the Chevalley property and their finite-dimensional Nichols algebras, and finally module categories over the categories of representations of pointed Hopf algebras.

1.
Introduction.

A Hopf algebra
H
is an associative algebra with unit which at the same time admits a structure of coalgebra, compatible with the algebra structure, and an antipode
\mathcal{S}:H\to H
(an endomorphism of antialgebras, satisfying appropriate identities). The coalgebra structure provides a structure of
monoidal category
on
\operatorname{mod}{H}
, the category of finite-dimensional left
H
-modules, and the antipode makes it
rigid
: that is, the dual vector space is also a representation, whose action is given using
\mathcal{S}
.
Dually, the category
\operatorname{comod}{H}
of finite-dimensional
H
-comodules has the same properties. See e.g.
[
48
]
.

In addition, both categories are
\Bbbk
-linear and abelian. Putting altogether, both
\operatorname{mod}{H}
and
\operatorname{comod}{H}
are tensor categories. Moreover, the forgetfut functors to
\operatorname{vec}_{\Bbbk}
(the category of finite-dimensional vector spaces) are a tensor functor. Reciprocally, any tensor category
\mathcal{C}
with a fiber functor to
\operatorname{vec}_{\Bbbk}
can be reconstructed as
\mathcal{C}\simeq\operatorname{comod}{H}
for
H
a Hopf algebra, and when the category is finite, we can also identify
\mathcal{C}
with the category of
H^{*}
-comodules
[
27
]
. And although not all tensor categories come from Hopf algebras (as far as they cannot admit a fiber functor), they bring prominent examples. As well, many applications of Hopf algebras come trought tensor categories. Thus, providing new examples of Hopf algebras is a key step towards the applications and problems on Hopf algebras themselves and those on tensor categories.

From now on, we fix an algebraically closed field
\Bbbk
of characteristic zero.
Classical results establish that commutative Hopf algebras are related with affine group schemes by means of the algebras of functions while cocommutative Hopf algebras come from groups acting on Lie algebras, due to a celebrated result by Cartier-Kostant-Milnor-Moore. Prominent examples of non commutative and non cocommutative Hopf algebras are given by Drinfeld and Jimbo quantum groups
[
25
,
41
]
. Later, Lusztig considered integral forms close to these quantum groups, divided powers versions and finite-dimensional Frobenius-Lusztig kernels, in connection with his studies of representation theory of algebraic groups in positive characteristic
[
44
]
.

All these versions of quantum groups are
pointed
Hopf algebras. We recall that a coalgebra is called pointed if any simple subcoalgebra is one-dimensional, and is the dual notion of a basic algebra. Looking at a Hopf algebra
H
, it means that the coradical
H_{0}
(the sum of all simple subcoalgebras) becomes the group algebra of the set
G(H)
of group-like elements once we take into account the product. At the level of tensor categories, it means that
\operatorname{comod}{H}
is pointed: every simple object
X
is invertible. Since the late nineties the classification of pointed Hopf algebras and the exploration of connections with other topics were intensively considered. The objective of this paper is to recall the main points about the classification, stating those subfamilies already classified as well as the open questions and connections with other problems. Indeed, Section
2
contains the relevant definitions and a description of the aforementioned Lifting Method, with an emphasis on describing Nichols algebras. In addition, it reviews the solutions obtained on the classification of finite-dimensional Nichols algebras over finite groups, which is the first step of the Method, solutions to the generation in degree one problem, and finally the results already obtained on liftings together with their relation to cocycle deformations, which led to the strongest results on the classification of pointed Hopf algebras.

In Section
3
we will describe three problems related to pointed Hopf algebras, mainly using the mentioned categorical point of view. First, the natural generalisation in the context of tensor categories, which is the classification of finite pointed tensor categories. We will see that, in the case of abelian groups, there have been significant advances, and that in some sense, the classification reduces to problems of Hopf algebras, or even more so, their associated Nichols algebras. Next, we will describe examples that fall outside the scope of the previous LIfting Method, corresponding to Hopf algebras generated by the coradical, which properly contain it: We will use the previous classification to give basic Hopf algebras, and also to classify finite-dimensional Nichols algebras over these basic Hopf algebras. Finally, we will provide some tools that may be useful for describing all module categories over the tensor category of representations of a pointed Hopf algebra as before.

2.
On the classification of finite-dimensional pointed Hopf algebras.

Next we recall some invariants related with Hopf algebras needed to introduce the main strategy for the classification of pointed Hopf algebras, the so-called Lifting Method; we refer to
[
48
]
for unexplained notation on this topic. Next, we will present the known solutions to the steps of the Lifting Method, with emphasis on the results on finite-dimensional Nichols algebras over finite groups, and finally we will state different classification results for pointed Hopf algebras, obtained by following the steps of this method, and open questions.

2.1.
The Lifting method.

Recall that the coradical
C_{0}
of a coalgebra
C
is the sum of all simple subcoalgebras. Starting on
C_{0}
we can define recursively the terms
(C_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of the coradical filtration as follows:

\displaystyle C_{n}

\displaystyle=C_{0}\wedge C_{n-1}=\Delta^{-1}(C_{0}\otimes C+C\otimes C_{n-1}),

\displaystyle n\geq 1.

This is an exhaustive coalgebra filtration, i.e.

\displaystyle\Delta(C_{n})

\displaystyle\subseteq\sum_{j=0}^{n}C_{j}\otimes C_{n-j}\text{ for all }n\geq 0,

and

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\cup_{n\geq 0}C_{n}.

The associated graded vector space
\operatorname{gr}{C}\coloneqq\oplus_{n\geq 0}C_{n}/C_{n-1}
, where
C_{-1}=0
, is a coradically graded coalgebra: it means that the coradical filtration of
\operatorname{gr}{C}
is the filtration associated to the graduation.

We say that
c\in C
is a group-like element if
\Delta(c)=c\otimes c
, and denote by
G(C)
the set of all group-like elements of
C
. These elements are in correspondence with one-dimensional subcoalgebras, so
\Bbbk G(C)\subseteq C_{0}
. In case that
\Bbbk G(C)=C_{0}
we say that
C
is
pointed
.

When we consider a Hopf algebra
H
, the coradical filtration
(H_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is not always a Hopf algebra filtration. Indeed, it is so if and only if
H_{0}
is a Hopf subalgebra. We will come back to the case in which the coradical is not a subalgebra in §
3.2
. But for the rest of this subsection
we assume that
H_{0}
is a Hopf subalgebra
: This is the case when
H
is pointed, since
G(H)
is a group (with the multiplication of
H
) and
\Bbbk G(H)=H_{0}
.

Under this assumption
\operatorname{gr}{H}
is a coradically graded Hopf algebra,
H_{0}
is a cosemisimple Hopf algebra and the inclusion
\iota:H_{0}\hookrightarrow\operatorname{gr}{H}
and the projection
\pi:\operatorname{gr}{H}\to H_{0}
are Hopf algebra maps such that
\pi\circ\iota=\operatorname{id}_{H_{0}}
. Due to Radford-Majid Theorem
[
48
]
,
\operatorname{gr}{H}
can be reconstructed as
\operatorname{gr}{H}\simeq R\#H_{0}
, where
R\coloneqq\operatorname{gr}{H}^{\operatorname{co}\pi}
is the subalgebra of left coinvariants, which is a coradically graded connected Hopf algebra in the category
{}^{H_{0}}_{H_{0}}\mathcal{YD}
of left
H_{0}
-Yetter Drinfeld modules with the restriction of the multiplication, a suitable coproduct
\underline{\Delta}:R\to R\otimes R
in
{}^{H_{0}}_{H_{0}}\mathcal{YD}
and the grading
R=\oplus_{n\geq 0}R^{n}
,
R^{n}\coloneqq R\cap\operatorname{gr}{H}^{n}
. The object
R^{1}\in{}^{H_{0}}_{H_{0}}\mathcal{YD}
is called the
infinitesimal braiding
, the algebra
R\in{}^{H_{0}}_{H_{0}}\mathcal{YD}
is the
diagram
and the subalgebra
\mathcal{B}(R^{1})\coloneqq\Bbbk\langle R^{1}\rangle
generated by
R^{1}
is the associated
Nichols algebra
: We will come back to this last object in §
2.2
. If
H
is finite-dimensional, then
R
, and a fortiori
\mathcal{B}(R^{1})
, are so.

2.1.1.
Yetter-Drinfeld modules.

Before introducing Lifting Method, we recall the definition and properties of the category
{}^{K}_{K}\mathcal{YD}
, for
K
a Hopf algebra with bijective antipode:

•

The objects
V
of
{}^{K}_{K}\mathcal{YD}
are left
K
-modules and
K
-comodules, whose action and coaction satisfy that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\delta(h\cdot v)

\displaystyle=h_{(1)}v_{(-1)}\mathcal{S}(h_{(3)})\otimes h_{(2)}\cdot v_{(0)},

for every

\displaystyle h\in K,\,v\in V.

The maps are simultaneously
K
-linear and
K
-colinear.

•

The tensor product of two Yetter-Drinfeld modules is a Yetter-Drinfeld module, as well as the dual, with the usual structure of modules and comodules on the tensor product and the dual. Thus,
{}^{K}_{K}\mathcal{YD}
is a monoidal category, and its subcategory of finite-dimensional objects is a tensor category. Moreover, it is braided, with braiding
c_{V,W}:V\otimes W\to W\otimes V
given by

(2.2)

\displaystyle c_{V,W}(v\otimes w)

\displaystyle=v_{(-1)}\cdot w\otimes v_{(0)}

for every

\displaystyle v\in V,\,w\in W.

This allows to consider Hopf algebras in
{}^{K}_{K}\mathcal{YD}
.

•

If
K
is semisimple and finite-dimensional, then the category
{}^{K}_{K}\mathcal{YD}
is so, as we assume
\operatorname{char}\Bbbk=0
. In particular, this happens when
K=\Bbbk G
, where
G
is a finite group.

Example 2.1
.

Let
G
be a group. A Yetter-Drinfeld module
V\in{}^{\Bbbk G}_{\Bbbk G}\mathcal{YD}
is a
G
-graded vector space
V=\oplus_{g\in G}V_{g}
, which is simultaneously a
G
-module, and such that
h\cdot V_{g}=V_{hgh^{-1}}
for all
g,h\in G
. When
G
is finite, as said above, the category is semisimple, and simple Yetter-Drinfeld modules are parametrized by pairs
(\mathcal{O},\rho)
, where
\mathcal{O}
is a conjugacy class of
G
and
\rho:G^{h}\to\operatorname{Aut}(U)
is an irreducible representation of
G^{h}
, the centralizer of an element
h\in\mathcal{O}
. The corresponding object
M(\mathcal{O},\rho)\in{}^{\Bbbk G}_{\Bbbk G}\mathcal{YD}
is explicitly described as follows. As
G
-module,

\displaystyle M(\mathcal{O},\rho)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Ind}^{G}_{G^{h}}\rho=\Bbbk G\otimes_{\Bbbk G^{h}}U.

Let
\{h_{1}=h,h_{2}\cdots,h_{m}\}
be an enumeration of
\mathcal{O}
, and for each
i=1,\cdots,m
, let
z_{i}\in G
be such that
h_{i}=z_{i}hz_{i}^{-1}
, with the convention
z_{1}=e
.
Hence,
M(\mathcal{O},\rho)=\oplus_{i=1}^{m}\Bbbk z_{i}\otimes U
and the action is as follows:

\displaystyle g\cdot(z_{i}\otimes W)

\displaystyle=z_{j}\oplus\rho(g^{\prime})(w),

where

\displaystyle gz_{i}=z_{j}g^{\prime},

for each

\displaystyle g\in G,\,1\leq i\leq m,\,w\in W.

For the action
\delta:M(\mathcal{O},\rho)\to\Bbbk G\otimes M(\mathcal{O},\rho)
we simply have:

\displaystyle\delta(z_{i}\otimes w)

\displaystyle=h_{i}\otimes(z_{i}\otimes w),

for each

\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq m,\,w\in W.

2.1.2.
The method.

In accordance with what has already been described, we have collected some invariants such as the coradical
H_{0}
, the diagram
R
, the infinitesimal braiding
R^{1}
and the Nichols algebra
\mathcal{B}(R^{1})
. The Lifting Method, originally defined by Andruskiewitsch and Schneider in
[
14
]
, proposes to address the classification of Hopf algebras with a fixed finite-dimensional coradical
K
(a cosemisimple Hopf algebra, which is also semisimple as
\operatorname{char}\Bbbk=0
) by reversing the steps we took above. In effect, we seek to solve the following problems:

LM1)

Find all
V\in{}^{K}_{K}\mathcal{YD}
such that
\dim\mathcal{B}(V)<\infty
.

LM2)

For each
V\in{}^{K}_{K}\mathcal{YD}
as in the previuos step, determine all coradically graded connected Hopf algebras
R=\oplus_{n\geq 0}R^{n}
such that
R^{1}=V
.

Related with this step there is a famous conjecture made by Andruskiewitsch and Schneider
[
15
]
:

Conjecture 2.2
.

Assume that
K=\Bbbk G
, where
G
is a finite group. If
R=\oplus_{n\geq 0}R^{n}\in{}^{K}_{K}\mathcal{YD}
is a coradically graded connected Hopf algebra such that
\dim R<\infty
, then
R
is generated as an algebra by
R^{1}
; in other words,
R=\mathcal{B}(R^{1})
.

Notice that
R
is generated by
R^{1}
if and only if
R\#K
is generated as an algebra by
R^{1}
and
K
, where
R^{1}
, respectively
K
, is identified with
R^{1}\#1
, respectively
K
, as a subspace of
R\#K
.

LM3)

For each
R
as in
LM2)
, find all Hopf algebras
H
such that
\operatorname{gr}{H}\simeq R\#K
.

There is a related problem, which was the key step to solve
LM2)
and
LM3)
for the known cases, as we will discuss later in §
2.3
and
2.4
:

LM2’)

For each
V\in{}^{K}_{K}\mathcal{YD}
such that
\dim\mathcal{B}(V)<\infty
, give a presentation by generators and relations of
\mathcal{B}(V)
.

In the remaining part of this section we will present the known solutions for these steps when
K=\Bbbk G
, with
G
finite; that is, we will present the state of art of the classification of finite-dimensional pointed Hopf algebras.

2.1.3.
Cocycle deformations.

Now we give properties of cocycle deformations of Hopf algebras that would be useful later. Recall that a
2
-cocycle on
H
is a convolution invertible linear map
\sigma:H\otimes H\to\Bbbk
such that

\displaystyle\sigma(x,1)

\displaystyle=\sigma(1,x)=\epsilon(x),

\displaystyle\sigma(x_{(1)},y_{(2)})\sigma(x_{(2)}y_{(2)},z)

\displaystyle=\sigma(y_{(1)},z_{(1)})\sigma(x,y_{(2)}z_{(2)})

for all
x,y,z\in H
.
Given a
2
-cocycle
\sigma
, set
\cdot_{\sigma}:H\otimes H\to H
as the map

\displaystyle x\cdot_{\sigma}y

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sigma(x_{(1)},y_{(1)})x_{(2)}y_{(2)}\sigma^{-1}(x_{(3)},y_{(3)}),

\displaystyle x,y

\displaystyle\in H.

This map defines an new associative product on
H
, such that
(H,\cdot_{\sigma},1,\Delta,\epsilon,\mathcal{S}_{\sigma})
is a Hopf algebra, for an appropriate antipode
\mathcal{S}_{\sigma}
.
The importance of this notion lies in the fact that the categories of comodules of two Hopf algebras
H
and
H^{\prime}
are tensor equivalent if and only if there exists a
2
-cocycle
\sigma
on
H
such that
H^{\prime}\simeq H_{\sigma}
.

As the coalgebra structure of
H
and
H_{\sigma}
is the same, the coradical filtration coincides. In particular, if
H
is pointed, then
H_{\sigma}
is so. In
[
5
]
some mild conditions were given on
\sigma
in order to show that the associated graded Hopf algebras of
H
and
H_{\sigma}
coincide. This points towards the idea of constructing all liftings as in
LM3)
using
2
-cocycles or, equivalently, the so called Hopf-Galois objects. The same work sets out a strategy for this purpose.

2.2.
Nichols algebras.

Let
K
be any Hopf algebra, not necessarily semisimple.
As mentioned before, a
Nichols algebra
associated to a Yetter-Drinfeld module
V\in{}^{K}_{K}\mathcal{YD}
is a graded Hopf algebra
\mathcal{B}(V)=\oplus_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{B}^{n}(V)
in
{}^{K}_{K}\mathcal{YD}
, which is connected, i.e.
\mathcal{B}^{0}(V)=\Bbbk 1
, coradically graded and is generated by
\mathcal{B}^{1}(V)\simeq V
. These conditions are equivalent to the following

1
1

1

An element
x
in a Hopf algebra
\mathcal{B}
is
primitive
if
\underline{\Delta}(x)=x\otimes 1+1\otimes x
, and
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})
denotes the set of all primitive elements of
\mathcal{B}
.

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{0}(V)

\displaystyle=\Bbbk 1,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{1}(V)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B}(V))\simeq V,

\displaystyle\text{and }\mathcal{B}^{1}(V)\text{ generates }\mathcal{B}(V)\text{ as algebra.}

One can check that an algebra
\mathcal{B}\in{}^{K}_{K}\mathcal{YD}
with the properties above is unique. In fact, another way to describe
\mathcal{B}(V)
is as a quotient
\mathcal{B}(V)=T(V)/\mathcal{I}(V)
, where
\mathcal{I}(V)
is the maximal graded Hopf ideal of the tensor algebra
T(V)
generated by elements of degree
\geq 2
(i.e. the sum of all Hopf ideals with this property).

In addition, the structure of
\mathcal{B}(V)
depends really on the braiding map
c_{V,V}:V\otimes V\to V\otimes V
. Indeed, one can define the Nichols algebra
\mathcal{B}(V)
for each braided vector space
(V,c)
. We refer to
[
1
]
and the references therein for more information about Nichols algebras and the unexplained definitions.

A solution to
LM1)
for a fixed semisimple Hopf algebra
K
involves the description of the defining ideal
\mathcal{I}(V)
, or a more appropriate inviariant controlling the dimension of
\mathcal{B}(V)
. Also, an inclusion
W\hookrightarrow V\in{}^{K}_{K}\mathcal{YD}
induces an inclusion
\mathcal{B}(W)\subseteq\mathcal{B}(V)
, due to the universal property of Nichols algebras, so we usually argue recursively to solve the referred step of the Lifting Method. Next we discuss the case
K=\Bbbk G
, for
G
a finite group.

2.2.1.
The Weyl groupoid.

Next we introduce useful invariants to determine when a Nichols algebra is finite-dimensional, and in such case, to get the dimension. They are the Weyl groupoud and the associated root system. We refer mainly to
[
35
]
, but most of the results included here can be also found in
[
11
]
.

Fix
\theta\in\mathbb{N}
. Set
\mathbb{I}=\{1,\cdots,\theta\}
and
\mathcal{F}^{K}_{\theta}
as the collection of all direct sums
\oplus_{i\in\mathbb{I}}V_{i}\in{}^{K}_{K}\mathcal{YD}
, where
\dim V_{i}<\infty
. Given
V=\oplus_{i\in\mathbb{I}}V_{i}\in\mathcal{F}^{H}_{\theta}
, the Nichols algebra
\mathcal{B}(V)
is
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\theta}
-graded:
\mathcal{B}(V)=\oplus_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\theta}}\mathcal{B}(V)_{\alpha}
, where
V_{i}=\mathcal{B}(V)_{\alpha_{i}}
.

For the rest of this subsection we assume that
V_{j}\in{}^{K}_{K}\mathcal{YD}
is simple for all
j\neq i
.

Let
i\in\mathbb{I}
. We say that
V
is
i
-finite
if for all
j\neq i
there exists
m\geq 1
such that
(\operatorname{ad}V_{i})^{m}V_{j}=0
in
\mathcal{B}(V)
. If so, then we set:

(2.4)

\displaystyle c_{ij}^{V}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}2,&j=i,\\
-\max\{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}|(\operatorname{ad}V_{i})^{n}V_{j}\neq 0\},&i\neq j;\end{cases}

\displaystyle\rho_{i}(V)_{j}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}V_{i}^{*},&j=i,\\
(\operatorname{ad}V_{i})^{-c_{ij}^{V}}V_{j},&i\neq j.\end{cases}

Notice that
\rho_{i}(V)\in\mathcal{F}^{H}_{\theta}
: it is called the
i
-reflection
of
V
. Also,
c_{ij}^{V}=-\max\{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}|\mathcal{B}(V)_{n\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}}\neq 0\}
.

Remark 2.3
.

[
35
, Corollary 13.4.3 & Theorem 13.3.9]

For all
j\neq i
,
\rho_{i}(V)_{j}\in{}^{K}_{K}\mathcal{YD}
is simple. In addition, there is a close relation between the Nichols algebras
\mathcal{B}(V)
and
\mathcal{B}(\rho_{i}(V))
.

Assume that
V
is
i
-finite for all
i
. Then we set
C^{V}\coloneqq(a_{ij}^{V})\in\mathbb{Z}^{\theta\times\theta}
, which is a generalized Cartan matrix
[
35
, Lemma 13.4.4]
, and define the symmetries
s_{i}^{V}:\mathbb{Z}^{\theta}\to\mathbb{Z}^{\theta}
,

s_{i}^{V}(\alpha_{j})=\alpha_{j}-c_{ij}^{V}\alpha_{i}
for all
j\in\mathbb{I}
. If
C^{V}
is decomposable, i.e. there exist
\{1,\cdots,\theta\}=I_{1}\cup I_{2}
, where
I_{1}\cap I_{2}=\emptyset
and
c_{ij}^{V}=0
for all

and

such that

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(V)

\displaystyle\simeq\mathcal{B}(U_{1})\otimes\mathcal{B}(U_{2}),

where

\displaystyle U_{\ell}\coloneqq\oplus_{j\in I_{\ell}}V_{j},\,\ell=1,2.

We refer to
[
10
]
for a proof. Thus, in order to study finite-dimensional examples, it is enough to just consider the case in which
C^{V}
is indecomposable. If so we say that
V
, or either
\mathcal{B}(V)
as well, is
braided indecomposable
.

We say that
V

admits all reflections
if
V
is
i
-finite for all
i
, so
\rho_{i}(V)
is well defined for all
i
, and recursively

\rho_{i_{k}}\cdots\rho_{i_{1}}(V)
is
i
-finite for all
i,i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}\in\mathbb{I}
and all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

In this case, we can attach to

a semi-Cartan graph
\mathcal{G}(V)=\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{I},\mathcal{X},\rho,(A^{W})_{W\in\mathcal{X}})
, where
\mathcal{X}
is the subset of all

obtained by applying
\rho_{i}
’s to

, up to isomorphism, and

is defined as above.

Following
[
35
, Definition 13.6.3]
, the
Weyl groupoid

\mathcal{W}(V)
of
V
is the subgroupoid of
\mathcal{X}\times\operatorname{GL}\left(\mathbb{Z}^{\theta}\right)\times\mathcal{X}
generated by
\sigma_{i}^{W}\coloneqq(W,s_{i}^{W},\rho_{i}(W))
, with
W\in\mathcal{X}
,

. Thus, every element of
\mathcal{W}(V)
is of the form

(W,\omega,W^{\prime})
, where

2.2.3.
Nichols algebras over non abelian groups.

Now assume that
G
is a non-abelian group. As said in Example

is the conjugacy class of an element

One might think that the simplest case to determine when a Nichols algebra is finite-dimensional is when the Yetter Drinfeld modules module is simple, and it is the first to be tackled, thinking on a recursive argument on the number of simple summands. This is not what happens here. Determining when a Nichols algebra
\mathcal{B}(M(\mathcal{O},\rho))
is finite-dimensional is an open question, while the case where there are two or more summands, even still difficult, has been already solved: the role of the Weyl groupoid is fundamental in controlling the dimension of the Nichols algebra, as we will explain in the next paragraphs.

As the structure of the Nichols algebra depends on the underlying braided vector space, as we already mentioned, we can see braided vector spaces associated to
racks
and cocycles
[
10
]
. We recall that a rack is a pair
(X,\rhd)
, where
X
is a set and
\rhd:X\times X\to X
is a self distributive function such that
\varphi_{x}\coloneqq x\rhd-:X\to X
is bijective for all
x\in X
. A
2
-cocycle for a rack
(X,\rhd)
and a finite-dimensional vector space
W
is a function
\mathfrak{q}:X\times X\to GL(W)
such that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}_{x,y\rhd z}\mathfrak{q}_{y,z}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{q}_{x\rhd y,x\rhd z}\mathfrak{q}_{x,z}

for all

\displaystyle x,y,z\in X.

Given a
2
-cocycle
\mathfrak{q}
, we get an structure of braided vector space on
V=\Bbbk X\otimes W
:

\displaystyle c\left((x\otimes w)\otimes(x^{\prime}\otimes w^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=(x\rhd x^{\prime}\otimes\mathfrak{q}_{x,x^{\prime}}(w^{\prime}))\otimes(x\otimes w),

for all

\displaystyle x,x^{\prime}\in X,\,w,w^{\prime}\in W

Prominent examples of racks and
2
-cocycles come from a conjugacy class of a fixed group and a representation of the centralizer of a fixed element of this conjugacy class. In the case when the conjugacy class is finite, we obtain a finite-dimensional braided vector space, and this is the right context to deal with simple Yetter-Drinfeld modules over non abelian groups.

It is expected that most of the Nichols algebras over a non-abelian group are infinite-dimensional. In that sense, one says that a group
G

collapses
if every Nichols algebra over
G
is infinite dimensional. An effective way to determine that a group collapses is by looking at appropriate subracks of all conjugacy classes of the group. This was made for many simple groups, involving a detailed knowledge of the structure and in some case computations with GAP, see e.g.
[
6
,
9
]
for groups of Lie type and sporadic groups, respectively.

A different and promising approach appeared recently in
[
33
,
12
]
, where reduction to positive characteristic was explored to determine that most conjugacy classes collapse, for example for groups of odd order.

2.3.
Pointed Hopf algebras over abelian groups.

Due to Theorem

A first answer to this problem was given in
[

is not divisible by 2, 3, 5, 7. Under this restriction, all possible matrices

\lambda_{\beta}
for each root

\lambda_{\beta}=0

\chi_{\beta}^{N}\neq\epsilon

.

Later on it was proved in
[

]
that all liftings in
[
15
]
are cocycle deformations of the associated coradically graded Hopf algebra.

A key step in solving the general case (without restrictions on the order of the group) was to observe that many relations can eventually be deformed, which makes a recursive deformation process harder to describe explicitly, and that it is necessary to involve the cocycle deformations beforehand, rather than a posteriori as in the aforementioned case. 3.
Applications to other classification problems.

We would like to describe some other classification problems which are related with the classification of pointed Hopf algebras.

3.1.
Pointed tensor categories.

Next we consider a categorical version of our main problem, more precisely the classification of finite pointed tensor categories; we refer to
[
27
]
for unexplained notions.

Recall that a tensor category
\mathcal{C}
is
pointed
if every simple object is invertible, i.e. has Frobenius-Perron dimension one. Prominent examples of pointed categories are those of comodules over pointed Hopf algebras, but to get all pointed tensor categories we need to consider a generalization of the notion of Hopf algebras
H
. A coquasi-Hopf algebra
H
is a coalgebra provided with a unit, a multiplication
m:H\otimes H\to H
which is associative up to an element
\omega:H\otimes H\otimes H\to\Bbbk
, and a antipode, which is a triple
(\mathcal{S},\alpha,\beta)
, where
\mathcal{S}:H\to H
is a linear map, and
\alpha,\beta\in H^{*}
are elements satisfying certain compatibility equations. The category
\operatorname{comod}{H}
of finite-dimensional
H
-comodules is a tensor category with associativity given by
\omega
, unit given by
\epsilon
and dual objects determined by the antipode, and admits a quasi-tensor functor to
\operatorname{vec}_{\Bbbk}
.

An example of a coquasi-Hopf algebra is given by a finite group
G
and a normalized 3-cocycle
\omega
on
G
, i.e. a function
\omega:G\times G\times G\to\Bbbk^{\times}
such that

\displaystyle\omega(gh,k,\ell)\omega(g,h,k\ell)

\displaystyle=\omega(g,h,k)\omega(g,hk,\ell)\omega(h,k,\ell),

\displaystyle\omega(g,1,h)

\displaystyle=1,

for all
g,h,k,\ell\in G
. Here
H=\Bbbk^{\omega}G
with the usual coalgebra and algebra structure but the associativity is given by
\omega
, and in addition

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(g)

\displaystyle=g^{-1},

\displaystyle\alpha(g)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\beta(g)

\displaystyle=\omega(g,g^{-1},g)^{-1},

for all

\displaystyle g\in G.

The category
\operatorname{comod}{H}
is tensor equivalent to the category
\operatorname{vec}_{G}^{\omega}
of
G
-graded vector spaces, where the associativity isomorphism is given by
\omega
.

Given a pointed coquasi-Hopf algebra
H
, the graded coquasi-Hopf algebra
\operatorname{gr}{H}=\oplus_{n\geq 0}H^{(n)}
associated to the coradical filtration is a coradically graded coquasi-Hopf algebra, and
H^{(0)}\simeq\Bbbk^{\omega}G
, where
G
is the group of group-like elements, and
\omega
is a normalized 3-cocycle. One may follow an addapted version of the Lifting Method, and try to classify first all finite-dimensional coradically graded pointed coquasi-Hopf algebras, then ask for the corresponding generation in degree one problem, and finally compute all the liftings. Related with the second step, in
[
27
]
it was conjectured that any finite pointed tensor category is tensor generated by elements of length
\leq 2
, which is a generalization of Andruskiewitsch-Schneider conjecture to the level of tensor categories, and means that every finite-dimensional coradically graded coquasi-Hopf algebra is generated by elements in degree 0 and 1.

The classification of finite pointed tensor categories started in
[
26
]
, after a couple of works by the same authors dealing with the first steps of the addapted Lifting Method. They solve the case in which the group of simple objects form a cyclic group of prime order
p
. That is,
G=\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
and the strategy is based on the following facts:

(1)

Take any finite-dimensional pointed coquasi-Hopf algebra
H
.
3
3

3

In
[
26
]
the authors works really with the dual notion, the so-called quasi-Hopf algebras.
Then
H^{(0)}=\Bbbk^{\omega}\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, and the 3-cocycle
\omega
is
trivializable
over
\mathbb{Z}/p^{2}\mathbb{Z}
: it means that the pullback
\pi^{*}\omega
under the canonical projection
\pi:\mathbb{Z}/p^{2}\mathbb{Z}\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
is a 3-cocycle over
\mathbb{Z}/p^{2}\mathbb{Z}
cohomologous to the trivial cocycle.

(2)

There exists an action on
\operatorname{mod}{H}
by
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
such that the equivariantization of
\operatorname{mod}{H}
by
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
gives a coquasi-Hopf algebra
\widetilde{H}
with coradical
\Bbbk^{\pi^{*}\omega}\mathbb{Z}/p^{2}\mathbb{Z}
. Thus
\widetilde{H}
is, up to cocycle deformation, isomorphic to a pointed Hopf algebra with coradical
\Bbbk\mathbb{Z}/p^{2}\mathbb{Z}
.

(3)

Using facts from the classification of pointed Hopf algebras
[
14
]
, the authors were able to prove that

\mod{\widetilde{H}}\simeq\mod{\widehat{H}}
for a coradically graded Hopf algebra with coradical
\Bbbk\mathbb{Z}/p^{2}\mathbb{Z}
.

(4)

Using this fact, there exists a positive answer to the conjecture of generation in degree one for finite pointed tensor categories.

(5)

Following this path back, the authors calculated all coquasi-Hopf algebras obtained by de-equivariantization of coradically graded Hopf algebras over
\mathbb{Z}/p^{2}\mathbb{Z}
, thus providing a list of coradically graded pointed co-quasi-Hopf algebras of the form
\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{q}}\#\Bbbk^{\omega}\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, which classify all finite pointed tensor categories with group of invertible elements
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
up to tensor equivalence.

Following the same strategy, the case in which the group is cyclic (of any order coprime with
210
) was developed in
[
21
]
. A key point is that, again, any 3-cocycle over
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
is trivializable, thus one relates liftings of coquasi-Hopf algebras with de-equivariantizations of Hopf algebras. In a few not fully precise words, the equivariantization process (coming back from coquasi-Hopf to Hopf algebras) and the cocycle deformation process of Hopf algebras
commute
. Therefore, any pointed tensor category with cyclic group is, up to tensor equivalence, the category of comodules over a coradically graded Hopf algebra obtained by de-equivariantization of a Hopf algebra with coradical
\mathbb{Z}/n^{2}\mathbb{Z}
.

As a generalization of this idea, the de-equivariantization was used in
[
17
]
to classify finite-dimensional coradically graded coquasi-Hopf algebras with coradical
\Bbbk^{\omega}G
, where
G
is abelian and
\omega
is trivializable. The final answer relies on the classification of coradically graded pointed tensor algebras.

Even more, finite-dimensional coradically graded coquasi-Hopf algebras with abelian coradical were classified in
[

3.2.
Hopf algebras without Chevalley property.

The Lifting Method depicted in §
2.1
has an strog assumption on the Hopf algebra structure: The Hopf algebra must satisfy the
Chevalley property
, i.e. the coradical is a Hopf subalgebra. In some cases this assumption does not hold and we need to consider a generalization of the Lifting Method
[
7
]
. To this end, Andruskiewitsch and Cuadra introduced a new coalgebra filtration
\{H_{[n]}\}_{n\geq 0}
, called the
standard filtration
. Let
H
be a Hopf algebra with coradical
H_{0}
. The
Hopf coradical
of
H
is the subalgebra
H_{[0]}
generated by
H_{0}
. The next terms are defined recursively:

\displaystyle H_{[n+1]}

\displaystyle\coloneqq H_{[0]}\wedge H_{[n]}=\Delta^{-1}\left(H_{[0]}\otimes H+H\otimes H_{[n]}\right),

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\geq 0.

As proved in
[
7
]
,
K=H_{[0]}
is a Hopf subalgebra with coradical
H_{0}
, and
\{H_{[n]}\}_{n\geq 0}
is a Hopf algebra filtration. Also,
K
is generated as an algebra by the coradical. Starting on these facts, in
[
7
]
the authors propose a generalized Lifting method associated to the standard filtration. Thus, the starting point is to fix a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra
K
generated by the coradical and determine all finite-dimensional Nichols algebras in
{}^{K}_{K}\mathcal{YD}
.

As said in
[
7
, Question I]
, a first step is to determine all Hopf algebras
K
generated by a fixed cosemisimple coalgebra
C
. In particular, we may have
K=C
, and
K
is semisimple, as we have fixed an algebraically closed field
\Bbbk
of characteristic zero. As the classification of such Hopf algebras is widely open, we cannot attack this question in a broad sense, so in principle we would like to get families of Hopf algebras generated by the coradical.

Example 3.2
.

Fix
m,n\geq 2
. The generalized Taft algebra
T_{m,n}
is the algebra generated by
g,x
with relations

(3.1)

\displaystyle g^{mn}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle gx

\displaystyle=\xi\,xg,

\displaystyle x^{n}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle\text{ a primitive root of unity of order }n.

It is a Hopf algebra with coproduct determined by
\Delta(g)=g\otimes g
,
\Delta(x)=x\otimes 1+g\otimes x
, and of dimension
mn^{2}
, since the set
\{g^{a}x^{b}|0\leq a<mn,\,0\leq b<n\}
is a basis of
T_{m,n}
. In addition,
T_{m,n}
is pointed, with
G(T_{m,n})\simeq\mathbb{Z}/mn\mathbb{Z}
, coradically graded and the infinitesimal braiding is one-dimensional.

The Hopf algebra
T_{m,n}
has, up to isomorphism, a unique non-trivial lifting
R_{m,n}
, called the Radford algebra: The structure is essentially the same, up to change the last relation in (
3.1
) by
x^{n}=1-g^{n}
.

The dual Hopf algebra
R_{m,n}^{*}
is generated by its coradical, as proved in
[
8
]
.

Following this example, we can look for Hopf algebras generated by the coradical within those already classified. More precisely.

Question 3.3
.

Determine for which parameters
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
as in Theorem
2.17
, respectively
2.19
, the Hopf algebra
\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})^{*}
is generated by the coradical.

Indeed, Example
3.2
corresponds to the case in which the infinitesimal braiding is one dimensional. Up to finishing the classification of finite-dimensional pointed Hopf algebras, a full answer for the question below will provide the classification of all basic Hopf algebras generated by the coradical.
Notice that the answer will fully depend on the structure as a Hopf algebra.

On the other hand, an asnwer to the next question of the generalized Lifting Method, that is the determination of all finite dimensional Nichols algebras in
{}^{\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})^{*}}_{\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})^{*}}\mathcal{YD}
for
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
as in Question
3.3
, can be obtained from a categorical point of view.
As explained in
[
3
]
, when
G
is abelian, we can take advantage of the following chain of equivalences of braided tensor categories:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}:{}^{\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})^{*}}_{\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})^{*}}\mathcal{YD}

\displaystyle\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}{}^{\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}_{\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}\mathcal{YD}\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}{}^{H}_{H}\mathcal{YD}\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}\operatorname{mod}{D(H)}

where
H=\operatorname{gr}{\mathcal{H}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
, and
D(H)
denotes the Drinfeld double of
H
, a certain Hopf algebra with underlying vector space
H\otimes H^{*}\simeq\mathcal{B}(V)\otimes\Bbbk(G\times\widehat{G})\otimes\mathcal{B}(V^{*})
. Here we combine the cocycle deformation obtained in Theorem
2.17
for the second equivalence, together with classical equivalences which hold for any finite-dimensional Hopf algebra. The Hopf algebra
D(H)
has a triangular decomposition, and any simple module is of the shape
L(\lambda)
, where
\lambda\in\widehat{G\times\widehat{G}}=\widehat{G}\times G
and
L(\lambda)
is the simple quotient of the Verma module
M(\lambda)
, the induced module from
D(H)^{\geq}=\mathcal{B}(V)\otimes\Bbbk(G\times\widehat{G})
. In this case:

Theorem 3.4
.

[
3
, Theorems 1.1 & 1.2]

Let
G
be a finite abelian group,
V\in{}^{\Bbbk G}_{\Bbbk G}\mathcal{YD}
such that
\dim\mathcal{B}(V)<\infty
, and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
as in Theorem
2.17
. Let
\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
be the associated lifting of
\mathcal{B}(V)\#\Bbbk G
.

i)

Let
W\in{}^{\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})^{*}}_{\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})^{*}}\mathcal{YD}
. If
\dim\mathcal{B}(W)<\infty
, then
W
is semisimple.

ii)

Let
W\in{}^{\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})^{*}}_{\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})^{*}}\mathcal{YD}
be semisimple, so
\mathcal{G}(W)=\oplus_{i=1}^{n}L(\lambda_{i})
for some
\lambda_{i}\in\widehat{G}\times G
. Then
\dim\mathcal{B}(W)<\infty
if and only if
\dim\mathcal{B}(V\oplus\lambda_{1}\oplus\cdots\lambda_{n})<\infty
, where
\lambda_{i}\in{}^{\Bbbk G}_{\Bbbk G}\mathcal{YD}
means the corresponding one-dimensional Yetter-Drinfeld module.

This result gives a classification of all finite-dimensional Nichols algebras in
{}^{\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})^{*}}_{\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})^{*}}\mathcal{YD}
, which in turn give a classification of all finite-dimensional Nichols algebras of finite-dimensional basic Hopf algebras over abelian groups, see
[

: There exists a bosonization inside the braided tensor category

inducing a decomposition

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(V\oplus\lambda_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\lambda_{n})

\displaystyle\simeq\mathcal{B}(L(\lambda_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus L(\lambda_{n}))\#\mathcal{B}(V).

A particular case was explicitly computed in
[

A natural question is if these results can be extended to basic Hopf algebras over non-abelian groups.

3.3.
Module categories over Hopf algebras.

Recall that a
\mathcal{C}
-module category over a tensor category
\mathcal{C}
is a 4-uple
(\mathcal{M},\otimes,m,\ell)
, where
\mathcal{M}
is an abelian category and
\otimes:\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
is a bifunctor, and

\displaystyle m_{X,Y,M}

\displaystyle:(X\otimes Y)\otimes M\to X\otimes(Y\otimes M),

\displaystyle\ell_{M}

\displaystyle:\mathbf{1}\otimes M\to M,

\displaystyle X,Y\in\mathcal{C},

\displaystyle\,M\in\mathcal{M},

where
\mathbf{1}\in\mathcal{C}
is the unit,
are natural isomorfisms satisfying the following identities:

\displaystyle(\operatorname{id}_{X}\otimes m_{Y,Z,M})m_{X,Y\otimes Z,M}(a_{X,Y,Z}\otimes\operatorname{id}_{M})

\displaystyle=m_{X,Y,Z\otimes M}m_{X\otimes Y,Z,M},

\displaystyle(\operatorname{id}_{X}\otimes\ell_{M})m_{X,\mathbf{1},Y}

\displaystyle=r_{X}\otimes\operatorname{id}_{M},

for all
X,Y,Z\in\mathcal{C}
and
M\in\mathcal{M}
. Here,
r_{X}:X\otimes\mathbf{1}\to X
is the natural isomorfism in
\mathcal{C}
.
For example, any tensor category
\mathcal{C}
is a module category over itself.

Let
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{N}
be
\mathcal{C}
-module categories.

•

We say that
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
are equivalent if there exist
\mathcal{C}
-module
functors
F:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
and
G:\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{M}
such that the compositions
F\circ G
and
G\circ F
are naturally isomorphic to the corresponding identities.

•

The direct sum of these categories is the
\Bbbk
-linear category
\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
with the canonical
\mathcal{C}
-structure. A
\mathcal{C}
-module category
\mathcal{N}
is
indecomposable
if
\mathcal{N}
is not equivalent to a direct sum of two non trivial module categories.

•

\mathcal{M}
is
exact
if it is finite and for every projective object
P\in\mathcal{C}
and every
M\in\mathcal{M}
,
P\otimes M
is projective in
\mathcal{M}
.

As any exact module category decomposes as the direct sum of finite exact indecomposable modules categories,
it suffices to classify exact indecomposable module categories in order to get all exact module categories.

Let
H

\mathcal{C}=\operatorname{mod}{H}
following
[
13

(A,m,u)

\operatorname{comod}{H}

An
H

H

H

H

Let

H

Explicitly, given an

\displaystyle a\cdot(x\otimes m)

This defines a functor

Next we recall some results from

As stated in loc.cit., examples of exact indecomposable module categories are provided either when

According with
[

V

\mathcal{B}(V)
is
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\theta}
-graded as stated previously and all
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\theta}
-graded coideal subalgebras of
\mathcal{B}(V)
are classified by morphisms of the Weyl groupoid
\mathcal{W}(V)
ending in
V
; even more, there exists a partial order on this set and the correspondence gives an isomorphism of partially ordered sets. On morphisms ending in
V
, we say that
w_{1}\leq w_{2}
if there exists a reduced expression of
w_{2}
such that
w_{1}
is the beginning of
w_{2}
; the partial order

is called the Duflo order as it generalizes the corresponding notion for Weyl groups, and as in this case, it can be read from the root system. Indeed, if we fix a reduced expression

, the set of positive roots in
\varDelta_{V}^{+}
changing to negative roots up to apply

is given by
\{\alpha_{i_{1}},s_{i_{1}}^{V}(\alpha_{i_{2}}),\cdots,s_{i_{1}}^{V}\cdots s_{i_{\ell-1}}(\alpha_{i_{\ell}})\}

\mathcal{W}_{V}
is given by the order on the subsets of positive roots by means of convex orders on
\varDelta_{V}^{+}
, see
[

Thus, combining
[

]
and

Invoking again a categorical point of view and Theorem
2.17

is categorical Morita equivalent to
\operatorname{comod}{L}

[

, which in turn coincides with
\operatorname{comod}{H}
for

, hence there exists a bijective correspondence between module categories of
\operatorname{mod}{L}
and
\operatorname{mod}{H}
