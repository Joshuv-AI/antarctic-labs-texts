Comparing the Existence of Golden Proportion Using Maxillary Anterior Teeth Dimensions in Primary and Permanent Dentition: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: Riya A Pursnani, Ashish Saxena, Ajay Parihar, Shikhar P Chauhan, Neha Verma, Gururamchandran Pushparekha
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAims and backgroundThe golden proportion is based on the premise that there is a link between natural beauty and mathematics. The study aimed to analyze the mesiodistal width of maxillary anterior teeth in primary and permanent dentition to determine whether a golden proportion exists among them.Materials and methodsSixty subjects were randomly selected in accordance with inclusion and exclusion criteria. Participants were divided into two groups, each comprising 30 children with primary dentition between 3 and 6 years old, and 30 children with permanent dentition between 12 and 13 years old. Intraoral photographs were taken using a smartphone (in-built sensor: Sony IMX890, focal length: 24 mm, aperture: f/1.8) and a cheek retractor, with the head upright and the occlusal plane parallel to the floor. The distance between the participant and the camera was fixed at 4 cm. The digital photographic images were transferred to a computer, and the perceived mesiodistal width was measured for anterior teeth using Adobe Photoshop CS (version 8) software.ResultsThe ratio of lateral incisor to central incisor was not found to differ significantly between permanent and primary dentition (p-value > 0.05). However, the ratio of canine to lateral incisor was significantly greater in primary dentition compared to permanent dentition (p-value < 0.05).ConclusionThe study revealed that the golden proportion was inconsistent in terms of relative tooth width in primary dentition.Clinical significanceThe golden proportion existing in permanent dentition serves as a guide for restoring severely mutilated anterior teeth, so it is important to assess whether the golden proportion exists in primary dentition.How to cite this articlePursnani RA, Saxena A, Parihar A, et al. Introduction

Beauty is a pleasurable experience perceived through our senses, shaped by our connections, influenced by our life philosophies, and intuitively sensed. Since the dawn of time, people have searched for the essence of beauty. Everyone places a lot of value on facial attractiveness because it significantly affects self-image.1 Achieving a balanced proportion in the restoration or replacement of teeth is a key responsibility in the field of cosmetic dentistry. To achieve the best results in dental care, the dentist must analyze the proportions and shapes of the teeth. The visual appeal of a cosmetic restoration can be impacted by various factors that define a beautiful smile, such as the extent of gum exposure, the placement of the midline, the architecture of the gums, the proportions of the clinical crown, and the positioning of the teeth.2 The anterior teeth of the maxilla have a big impact on cosmetic dentistry since their facial features are so prominent when you smile.

The golden ratio is rooted in the notion that there is a link between mathematics and the beauty observed in nature. Tatarkiewicz in 1991 believed that the human form had mathematical proportions that could be replicated through artistic means. He valued the finer points, particularly on the face, as the essence of beauty.3 The Greeks created some of the most stunning and opulent temples, as well as the most exquisite statues, using mathematical standards or norms as their foundation. The golden ratio philosophy guided the construction of these marvels.

Huntley (1985) asserts that the use of this ratio in Greek writings is most certain. The famous Greek sculptor Phidias's initials, phi (Φ), which stand for the golden ratio, were utilized as a guide. The golden number, often known as the phi number, is 1.61803.3 The ratio between these two components is 0.618, which explains the ratio of 1 to 1.618. Fibonacci later expressed this value in mathematics, where the value of a word equals the addition of the preceding two terms (for instance, 1 + 1 = 2; 1 + 2 = 3; 2 + 3 = 5; and so on).4 The Greek philosopher Pythagoras is mentioned in some of the first references to this ratio. He saw patterns and combinations in nature that could only be explained by the study of numbers.

Various academic fields have embraced the golden ratio, with dentistry being one of them. Through Lombardi (1973), esthetic dentistry made its debut in the 1970s. Ricketts (1982) gained significant notoriety in the field of facial analysis by comparing a few attractiveness indices to the golden ratio. Additionally, Preston (1993) merits commendation for his work on the golden ratio within esthetic dentistry. He assessed the different aspects of a smile and determined that the ratio is only relevant between the incisors.

In his 1978 work, Levin advocated for the use of the golden ratio theory to relate the increasingly wider anterior teeth as observed from the labial front. When looking straight at them, the central incisor should be in “golden proportion” to the lateral incisor, while the width of the lateral incisor should also correspond in “golden proportion” to that of the canine.

The occurrence of the golden ratio with respect to permanent dentition among different population groups has been the subject of numerous research studies; however, information on primary dentition is less clear. The present study investigates the presence of the golden ratio in the deciduous anterior maxillary teeth of children aged 3–6 and compares it with that found in children aged 12–13, aiming to explore any potential correlation between the two.

Materials and Methods

Design

Observational, cross-sectional, institutional-based study.

Procedure

The research was carried out at the Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry Department, with data being collected over the duration of 6 months.

Following approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee, data for the study were obtained by taking intraoral pictures using a smartphone (in-built sensor: Sony IMX 890, focal length: 24 mm, aperture: f/1.8) utilizing a cheek retractor to enhance the visibility of the maxillary anterior teeth (Figs 1A and B). Informed consent was secured from the parents and guardians of the participants prior to their involvement.

Source

Data were collected from the outpatient department at the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry.

Method of Selection

Participants were selected using the convenience sampling method.

Data Measurement

A total of 60 participants were selected for the study, which defined the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Two groups were formed from the participants, each consisting of 30 children: one group included those aged 3–6 years with a primary set of teeth, while the other group comprised children aged 12–13 years with a permanent set of teeth.

After uploading the digital photographs to the computer, the program's zoom feature (set to 50%) allowed for the visible delineation of the teeth with mesial and distal borders.

The mesiodistal width of the incisors and canines was measured using the feature that helps in horizontal measurement in Adobe Photoshop CS 8 (Figs 2A and B). The widths of the central incisors were used as divisors to calculate the perceived widths of the maxillary lateral incisors, whereas the widths of the canines were divided by those of the maxillary lateral incisors.

The resulting values were then compared to the standard golden proportion, that is, 0.61–0.63, for both permanent and primary anterior teeth.

Bias

To avoid any observation error, the measured proportion was compared with the standard golden proportion by an investigator who was trained and calibrated by an expert.

Study Size

The calculation of the sample size was performed using G*Power 3.1.9.7 software, revealing that at least 52 samples were mandatory. Thus, it was proposed to include 60 participants in the study.

Results

The study included a total of 30 subjects with permanent dentition and 30 subjects with primary dentition.

The median age of children with permanent dentition was 13.0 (12.0–13.0) years, and that of children with primary dentition was 5.0 (4.0–6.0) years.

Among the children with permanent dentition, the number of females was greater than the number of males (63.3 vs 36.7%), whereas among the children with primary dentition, the number of males was greater than the number of females (60.0 vs 40.0%).

The median width of the central incisor in permanent dentition and primary dentition was 5.0 (5.0–5.5) mm and 4.0 (3.5–4.5) mm, respectively. The median width of the lateral incisor in permanent dentition and primary dentition was 3.5 (3.0–3.5) mm and 2.5 (2.0–3.0) mm, respectively. The median width of the canine in permanent dentition was 2.3 (2.0–2.5) mm, and in primary dentition, it was 2.5 (2.0–3.0) mm.

The ratio of the lateral to central incisor in permanent and primary dentition was 0.6 (0.6–0.7) and 0.6 (0.6–0.7), respectively (Fig. 3). The ratio of the canine to lateral incisor in permanent and primary dentition was 0.6 (0.6–0.7) and 0.9 (0.8–1.05), respectively (Fig. 3). The lateral incisor to central incisor ratio did not differ significantly across permanent and primary dentition (p-value > 0.05). Conversely, the canine to lateral incisor ratio was significantly greater in primary dentition compared to that in permanent dentition (p-value < 0.05). On comparing the frequency of the existence of the golden proportion, a nonsignificant difference was seen between permanent and primary dentition (p-value > 0.05) (Table 1).

The analysis revealed that the deviation of the canine to lateral incisor ratio from the golden proportion was –0.009 (–0.018–0.082) in permanent dentition and 0.282 (0.182–0.432) in primary dentition. Discussion

The golden ratio, often cited in dental esthetics, is used to create proportionate relationships between the widths of anterior teeth. By comparing the apparent widths of teeth like central incisors and canines, dentists may apply the ratio to create balanced smiles. However, this mathematical concept is not universally embraced, as dental esthetics depend on many factors, including personal taste and facial harmony. Therefore, the golden ratio serves more as a guideline than a fixed rule.

Gerard Ciche, in his thoughtful explanation of the role of the golden proportion in esthetic dentistry, remarked, “Esthetic dentistry is a blend of precise measurements and artistic intuition. The geometric aspect can be structured and taught, but the artistic sensitivity and perception differ from person to person, honed through sculpting and hands-on experience.”5

Dr Ciche highlighted the importance of blending both science and art to achieve esthetically pleasing results in dentistry. The golden ratio is one such example of a scientific concept that can be applied as a helpful tool during the restorative process, guiding the creation of balanced and harmonious dental esthetics. However, it is just one component, and artistic insight is equally essential to produce truly beautiful outcomes.

Lombardi and Levin applied the golden ratio concept to the maxillary anterior teeth.6,7 However, other researchers have pointed out that this proportion is not always seen in clinical cases, where variations are common.8 A study analyzing plaster casts of natural teeth found that only 17% followed the golden ratio. This leads to the question: if most teeth don't conform to this rule, what ratio is typical for the majority of people?9 While the golden proportion serves as a useful guide for esthetic judgment, ratios between 0.6 and 0.8 are typically considered esthetically pleasing.

Maxillary anterior teeth are considered fundamental to the esthetic perception of dentofacial beauty.10 For pediatric dentists, restoring heavily decayed primary anterior teeth is a complex task.11 The golden percentage can be applied in cases such as the restoration of peg lateral incisors, and precision tools are available to assist in designing and evaluating proportional outcomes.5

This study found that the average width ratio of the maxillary lateral incisor to central incisor was 0.62 across both primary and permanent dentition. For the maxillary canine to lateral incisor, the ratio was 0.67 in permanent dentition and 0.93 in primary dentition. The lateral incisor to central incisor ratio did not significantly differ between dentitions (p-value > 0.05), but the canine to lateral incisor ratio was significantly greater in primary dentition (p-value < 0.05).

The 2008 study by Murthy et al. found that the golden proportion was observed in only 14–25% of participants regarding the visible widths of maxillary anterior teeth in their natural state.12 In this study, the widths of the six maxillary anterior teeth, as viewed from the front, were represented by the central incisor at 22%, the lateral incisor at 15%, and the canine at 13%.

The research by Chander et al. in 2012 revealed no significant statistical differences in the golden proportion ratios when analyzed by gender. The mean ratio between maxillary and mandibular teeth ranged from 73 to 84%. Ratios of 1.2 and 1.3 were observed more often than the golden ratio of 1.618, which occurred in merely 1% of the samples.13

Mamidi et al. completed a similar analysis in 2020, determining that the average measured width ratio of the maxillary lateral incisor to the central incisor was 0.80.14 The study revealed the average perceived ratio between the maxillary lateral incisor and the central incisor was 0.75, while the ratio between the maxillary canine and the lateral incisor was recorded at 1.34. Additionally, the width ratio between the maxillary canine and lateral incisor was recorded at 0.92. The recorded ratio comparing width-to-height of the maxillary central incisor was 1.14, while the perceived ratio comparing width-to-height was noted as 1.09. The study did not find any statistically significant differences between male and female participants.

In a 2019 research effort by Jain et al., the focus was on evaluating the differences in mathematical proportions of maxillary anterior teeth widths compared to the golden proportion among patients with narrow, average, and wide smiles. Results revealed significant deviations from the golden proportion across all smile types concerning the proportions of the lateral incisor and canine.15 The most pronounced variations were found in the wide smile group, followed by the narrow smile group. In contrast, individuals with average smiles displayed the least deviation from the golden ratio.

In 2019, Aldegheishem et al. conducted a study aimed at elucidating the presence of the golden proportion in the widths of maxillary anterior teeth among Saudi males and females with permanent dentition. The findings unequivocally indicated the nonexistence of the golden ratio, with the notable exception of tooth number 12 in male subjects, which recorded a mean of 0.83, a standard deviation of 0.09, and a p-value of 0.144 (p > 0.05).16 Furthermore, a concurrent study by Sandeep et al. in 2015 revealed that the golden proportion was not detectable between the perceived mesiodistal widths of the maxillary central and lateral incisors, nor between those of the lateral incisors and canines.17

In 2022, a systematic review by Ahmed et al. determined that 62% of golden proportions were not found in the successive widths of maxillary anterior teeth for the majority of populations across the various regions studied.18 The mean proportion values for central incisor-to-lateral incisor (CI-to-LI) and canine-to-lateral incisor (Ca-to-LI) were recorded as 54.526 ± 14.684 and 63.975 ± 13.121 on the right side, with corresponding values of 55.024 ± 13.072 and 63.975 ± 13.121 on the left side.

In 2021, Abdallah et al. conducted a study aimed at evaluating the prevalence of the golden standard (GS) and golden proportion in the maxillary anterior teeth of the Saudi population in Makkah. The results indicated that 43% of GS ratios, 14% of golden proportion ratios for canines/laterals, and 34% of golden proportion ratios for laterals/centrals fell in the acceptable range.19 There were no significant differences in GS ratios between any genders (p = 0.512) or among different racial groups (p = 0.137); however, significant differences were detected based on facial shape (p = 0.001). Regarding canines/laterals golden proportion ratios, significant differences were detected between genders (p = 0.000), across racial groups (p = 0.000), and according to facial shapes (p = 0.001). Regarding the golden proportion ratios of laterals/centrals, no significant differences were found between genders (p = 0.216), but notable variations were identified across races (p = 0.000) and facial shapes (p = 0.007).

A 2017 study by Al-Sheakli focused on evaluating the golden proportion of maxillary anterior teeth across various facial morphology types and identifying potential gender differences within a sample of individuals with class I normal occlusion. The findings demonstrated a significant gender disparity in facial measurements, while dental measurements showed no gender-based differences. The study also indicated that the golden proportion was not present across different facial types and that there was no significant difference related to facial morphology. Thus, the golden proportion was found to be unrelated to gender and facial form.20

Conclusion

Findings from the research showed a lack of consistency in the golden proportion concerning the relative tooth widths in primary dentition. Although the golden proportion serves as a valuable reference for esthetic dental treatment planning, the statistical relationship between the mesiodistal widths of primary anterior teeth was found to be less significant.

Clinical Significance

The golden proportion in permanent dentition serves as a guide for restoring severely mutilated anterior teeth, making it essential to assess whether the golden proportion exists in primary dentition.
