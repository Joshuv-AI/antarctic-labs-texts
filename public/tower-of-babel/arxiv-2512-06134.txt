Physics-Informed Neural Koopman Machine for Interpretable Longitudinal Personalized Alzheimer’s Disease Forecasting
Georgi Hrusanov, Duy-Thanh Vu, Duy-Cat Can, Sophie Tascedda, Margaret Ryan, Julien Bodelet, Katarzyna Koscielska, Carsten Magnus, Oliver Y. Chén, for the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative

Abstract
Early forecasting of individual cognitive decline in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is central to disease evaluation and management. Despite advances, it is as of yet challenging for existing methodological frameworks to integrate multimodal data for longitudinal personalized forecasting while maintaining interpretability. To address this gap, we present the Neural Koopman Machine (NKM), a new machine learning architecture inspired by dynamical systems and attention mechanisms, designed to forecast multiple cognitive scores simultaneously using multimodal genetic, neuroimaging, proteomic, and demographic data. NKM integrates analytical ( α \alpha ) and biological ( β \beta ) knowledge to guide feature grouping and control the hierarchical attention mechanisms to extract relevant patterns. By implementing Fusion Group-Aware Hierarchical Attention within the Koopman operator framework, NKM transforms complex nonlinear trajectories into interpretable linear representations. To demonstrate NKM’s efficacy, we applied it to study the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) dataset. Our results suggest that NKM consistently outperforms both traditional machine learning methods and deep learning models in forecasting trajectories of cognitive decline. Specifically, NKM (1) forecasts changes of multiple cognitive scores simultaneously, (2) quantifies differential biomarker contributions to predicting distinctive cognitive scores, and (3) identifies brain regions most predictive of cognitive deterioration. Together, NKM advances personalized, interpretable forecasting of future cognitive decline in AD using past multimodal data through an explainable, explicit system and reveals potential multimodal biological underpinnings of AD progression.

Koopman Operator Theory (KOT) in Machine Learning

Deep Learning for Disease Progression

A powerful way to model complex temporal dynamics in longitudinal clinical data is to use deep learning. Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs), show promise for neurodegenerative disease prediction

II-D

Attention Mechanisms in Biomedical Applications

Through selective weighting, attention mechanisms advance sequence modeling

III-C

Preliminaries of the Koopman Operator Theory (KOT)

KOT enables the linear representation of nonlinear systems by lifting dynamics to a higher-dimensional space. For a nonlinear system
{\bm{x}}_{t+1}=f({\bm{x}}_{t})
, the Koopman operator
\mathcal{K}
acts on observable functions:
(\mathcal{K}g)({\bm{x}})=g(f({\bm{x}}))
. Specifically, we approximate this using
d_{z}
observables
\bm{\Phi}({\bm{x}})=[\phi_{1}({\bm{x}}),\ldots,\phi_{d_{z}}({\bm{x}})]^{\top}
to obtain a lifted representation
{\bm{z}}_{t}=\bm{\Phi}({\bm{x}}_{t})
, where dynamics become linear:

{\bm{z}}_{t+1}\approx\mathbf{K}{\bm{z}}_{t},

(2)

with
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}\times d_{z}}
approximating the infinite-dimensional operator
\mathcal{K}
, where
d_{z}
is the latent dimension.
Eq.

2
, therefore, enables linear analysis of complex nonlinear disease dynamics (in autonomous cases; we extend to controlled dynamics below).

III-D

Our Contributions to Improving the KOT

We formulate the longitudinal many-to-many forecasting problem as follows: one seeks to predict future disease-related cognitive scores
{\bm{y}}_{t+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
(e.g., MMSE, CDRSB, and ADAS13) at time
t+1
using observed multimodal biomarkers
{\bm{x}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{44}
at three time points up to time
t
, where the multimodal biomarkers consist of 44 features, including one genetic feature (APOE4), three CSF features (Amyloid-beta (A
\beta
), t-tau, and p-tau protein), three PET imaging features (FDG, PIB, and AV45), 18 MRI imaging features (17 Schaefer networks assembled from 202 cortical thickness regions and Intracranial Volume (ICV)), and demographic information (two numeric and 17 categorical features).

Our work introduces three contributions to advance KOT for future disease forecasting using past multimodal data.

Contribution 1: Simultaneous Learning of Observables and Koopman Dynamics.
Unlike classical Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (EDMD), which requires predefined observables, we jointly learn both the lifting function
\bm{\Phi}(\cdot)
and the Koopman matrix
\mathbf{K}
in an end-to-end fashion. Specifically, we parameterize:

{\bm{z}}_{t}=\bm{\Phi}({\bm{x}}_{t};{\bm{\theta}}_{\text{enc}}),\qquad{\bm{z}}_{t+1}=\mathbf{K}{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\text{ref}}+{\bm{c}}_{t},

(3)

where
\bm{\Phi}(\cdot;{\bm{\theta}}_{\text{enc}})
,
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}\times d_{z}}
,
{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\text{ref}}
(refined latent representation via residual blocks) and
{\bm{c}}_{t}
are a neural encoder with parameters
{\bm{\theta}}_{\text{enc}}
, the learnable Koopman matrix, the temporally refined latent representation, and an attention-derived control vector, respectively (see
Sec.

III-G
).

Contribution 2: Biologically-Informed Feature Architecture.
We decompose the input feature space into biologically meaningful modalities
{\bm{x}}_{t}=[{\bm{x}}_{t}^{(g)}:g\in{\mathbb{G}}]
, where
{\mathbb{G}}=\{\text{genetic, CSF, PET, MRI, demographics}\}
. Each modality is processed by dedicated, modality-specific encoders before fusion. This approach preserves modality-specific biological structures and enables cross-modal interactions in the lifted space;
\beta
-knowledge guides the aggregation (e.g., Schaefer regions to 17 networks).

Contribution 3: Hierarchical Attention-Based
Control
.

We extend the autonomous Koopman dynamics to incorporate
personalized control via hierarchical attention that processes
temporal windows of refined latent states
[{\bm{z}}_{t-w+1}^{\text{ref}},\ldots,{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\text{ref}}]
,
where
w
is the window size. The control vector
{\bm{c}}_{t}
adapts to
relevant biomarker patterns and subject-specific longitudinal trajectories

III-E

Optimizing the Multimodal Longitudinal Forecasting Problem: Technical Overview

The complete pipeline connects input biomarkers to cognitive predictions via:

{\bm{x}}_{t}\xrightarrow{\bm{\Phi}}{\bm{z}}_{t}\xrightarrow{\text{refine}}{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\text{ref}}\xrightarrow{\mathbf{K},{\bm{c}}_{t}}{\bm{z}}_{t+1}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t+1}
,
where
\mathcal{D}:=\mathcal{D}(\cdot;{\bm{\theta}}_{\text{dec}})
is a neural decoder with parameters

{\bm{\theta}}_{\text{dec}}
. Our joint optimization objective combines two complementary loss terms:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}+\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{koop}}+\mathcal{R}_{\text{spec}},

(4a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\mathcal{D}({\bm{z}}_{t+1}^{\text{ref},(i)};{\bm{\theta}}_{\text{dec}})

\displaystyle\quad-{\bm{y}}_{t+1}^{(i)}\|_{2}^{2},

(4b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{koop}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N(T-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\|{\bm{z}}_{t+1}^{\text{ref},(i)}

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbf{K}{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\text{ref},(i)}-{\bm{c}}_{\text{control}}^{(i)}\|_{2}^{2},

(4c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\text{spec}}

\displaystyle=\eta\big(\max\{0,\|\mathbf{K}\|_{2}^{2}-\rho^{2}\}\big)^{2}.

(4d)

Here,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}
enforces accurate prediction of cognitive scores across the
N

\mathcal{L}_{\text{koop}}

\mathcal{R}_{\text{spec}}

III-H

Multimodal Feature Encoding Using
\beta
knowledge

The
\beta
knowledge guides the NKM to first partition features based on modalities and then process them using a modality-specific encoder, thereby preserving biological interpretability, maintaining meaningful representations, and enabling group-level attention reasoning in later stages. At each timestep
t
, NKM decomposes the input
{\bm{x}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{25}
(or
{\bm{x}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{44}
when including demographic information) into feature groups: genetics, CSF, PET, functional brain networks (Schaefer’s 17 networks), and, when available, demographics.
A modality-specific encoder then processes each modality
g\in{\mathbb{G}}
as follows:

{\bm{z}}_{t}^{(g)}=\mathcal{E}^{(g)}\big({\bm{x}}_{t}^{(g)}\big),\quad{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\text{enc}}=\texttt{Fusion}\!\left(\left[{\bm{z}}_{t}^{(g)}:g\in{\mathbb{G}}\right]\right).

(5)

For each modality
g
,
{\bm{z}}_{t}^{(g)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{g}}
, and the fusion operation (with projection) yields
{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\text{enc}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}}
. These encoders learn data-driven approximations of the Koopman observables
\bm{\Phi}({\bm{x}}_{t})
. Here, we consider the core biological features as
\mathbb{R}^{25}
(including 17 Schaefer’s networks, three PET features, three CSF features, one APOE4 genetic feature, and one intracranial volume (ICV) feature); the full input vector, including demographic information, is
\mathbb{R}^{44}
for comprehensive modeling.

III-I

Temporal Representation

Subsequently, the NKM refines the encoded latent vectors over a temporal window
\{{\bm{z}}_{t-w+1}^{\text{enc}},\dots,{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\text{enc}}\}
:
{\bm{z}}_{\tau}^{\text{ref}}={\bm{z}}_{\tau}^{\text{enc}}+\texttt{ResBlock}({\bm{z}}_{\tau}^{\text{enc}}),\quad\forall\tau\in\{t-w+1,\dots,t\}
.
This residual transformation, consisting of five blocks, enhances temporal structure while preserving the original signals, thereby improving the modeling of disease trajectories.

III-J

Hierarchical Attention Control Using
\alpha
knowledge

To adaptively guide latent evolution based on historical multimodal biomarkers, NKM employs a hierarchical attention mechanism that encodes both time- and modality-specific context. Formally, given the refined latent window
[{\bm{z}}_{t-w+1}^{\text{ref}},\dots,{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\text{ref}}]
and modality-specific embeddings
\{{\bm{z}}_{t}^{(g)}\}_{g\in{\mathbb{G}}}
, the attention module computes a pair of weighted summaries:

{\bm{c}}_{t}^{\text{time}}=\sum_{i=0}^{w-1}\alpha_{i}\,{\bm{z}}_{t-i}^{\text{ref}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\bm{c}}_{t}^{\text{feat}}=\sum_{g\in{\mathbb{G}}}\beta_{g}\,{\bm{z}}_{t}^{(g)},

(6)

where

\alpha_{i}=\frac{\exp\!\big(\frac{{\bm{q}}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{k}}_{t-i}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\big)}{\sum_{j=0}^{w-1}\exp\!\big(\frac{{\bm{q}}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{k}}_{t-j}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\big)}

and

\beta_{g}=\frac{\exp\!\big(\frac{{\bm{v}}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{u}}_{g}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\big)}{\sum_{g^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{G}}}\exp\!\big(\frac{{\bm{v}}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{u}}_{g^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\big)}

are attention weights computed using scaled dot-product attention, with query
{\bm{q}}_{t}
, keys
{\bm{k}}_{\tau}={\bm{W}}_{k}{\bm{z}}_{\tau}^{\text{ref}}
(similarly for
{\bm{u}}_{g}
), and scaling factor
\sqrt{d_{k}}
for temporal attention, and feature query
{\bm{v}}_{t}
and keys
{\bm{u}}_{g}
for modality attention. This formulation weights recent visits more heavily while allowing data-driven weighting of both temporal and modality-specific information.

A learned gating function then fuses time- and feature-specific control signals, namely
{\bm{c}}_{t}^{\text{time}}
and
{\bm{c}}_{t}^{\text{feat}}
, into a unified control vector
{\bm{c}}_{t}
:

{\bm{c}}_{t}=g\cdot{\bm{c}}_{t}^{\text{feat}}+(1-g)\cdot{\bm{c}}_{t}^{\text{time}},\qquad g=\sigma\!\big([{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\text{ref}};{\bm{c}}_{t}^{\text{time}}]{\bm{W}}_{g}+{\bm{b}}_{g}\big),

(7)

where
\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigmoid function,
{\bm{W}}_{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d_{z}+d_{z})\times d_{z}}
, and
{\bm{b}}_{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}}
. The concatenation allows the gating mechanism to consider both the current refined state and the temporal summary, ensuring a balanced fusion. This attention-derived control signal
{\bm{c}}_{t}
adaptively steers the Koopman state transition, enabling personalized modeling of the disease-related cognitive score dynamics and highlighting critical visits and key biomarkers for each individual.

III-K

Koopman State Transition

To evolve the latent state, we use a learned Koopman matrix
\mathbf{K}
with attention-based control:

{\bm{z}}_{t+1}=\mathbf{K}{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\text{ref}}+{\bm{c}}_{t},

(8)

where
{\bm{z}}_{t}
denotes the refined latent representation after temporal processing. This formulation decomposes disease dynamics into two complementary components: the Koopman matrix
\mathbf{K}
models short-term temporal dependencies and variations around the mean trajectory, while the control vector
\mathbf{c}_{t}
encodes long-term disease progression and personalized deviations specific to each patient’s clinical profile. The spectral constraint
\|\mathbf{K}\|_{2}<1
ensures stability of the temporal component without limiting the model’s ability to capture progressive cognitive decline through
\mathbf{c}_{t}
.

The matrix
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}\times d_{z}}
approximates the infinite-dimensional Koopman operator
\mathcal{K}
, modeling global cognitive dynamics, while
{\bm{c}}_{t}
captures personalized deviations via the attention-derived control vector. This formulation generalizes the autonomous relation
{\bm{z}}_{t+1}=\mathbf{K}{\bm{z}}_{t}
by incorporating patient-specific dynamics. Unlike classical EDMD, we jointly learn both the observables
\bm{\Phi}
(via neural encoders) and the Koopman matrix
\mathbf{K}
in an end-to-end manner, enabling adaptation to cognitive decline trajectories.

III-L

Forecasting Future Disease Outcome

Finally, we predict multiple future cognitive scores from the evolved latent state using a simple regression head:

\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t+1}=\mathcal{D}({\bm{z}}_{t+1}).

(9)

The decoder simultaneously predicts MMSE, CDRSB, and ADAS13, allowing the NKM to capture shared structure across cognitive landscapes without having to train three different neural networks.

III-M

Training and Optimization

We train the NKM end-to-end using the composite loss in
Eq.

4
, augmented with spectral regularization to ensure stable long-term predictions:

\mathcal{R}_{\text{spectral}}=\bigl(\max\{0,\,\|\mathbf{K}\|_{2}^{2}-\rho^{2}\}\bigr)^{2},

(10)

where
\rho\in(0,1)
is a target upper bound for the spectral norm of
\mathbf{K}
. We optimize the objective using AdamW with early stopping based on validation loss, and select the hyperparameters
\lambda
and
\gamma
via cross-validation.

Stability and accuracy of Koopman predictions
. The above regularization ensures that
\|\mathbf{K}\|_{2}<1
, providing a theoretical guarantee of stable out-of-sample predictions. The three loss components act complementarily:
\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}

IV-A

Data and Experiment Setting

To evaluate NKM’s efficacy, we applied it to analyze data from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI)
[
47
]
. Specifically, we aimed to forecast multiple AD-related cognitive scores using historical multimodal data.

The multimodal data include 44 features per time step: 18 MRI features measuring Intracranial Volume (ICV) and cortical thickness of 202 brain regions organized by the Schaefer’s 17-network parcellation (i.e., 17 cortical thickness features plus ICV), PET imaging (FDG, PIB, and AV-45), genetic (APOE4), CSF (Amyloid-beta (A
\beta
), t-tau, and p-tau protein), and demographics (two numeric and 17 categorical features). The longitudinal outcomes consist of three cognitive impairment measures, including Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Clinical Dementia Rating - Sum of Boxes (CDRSB), and ADAS-Cog13 (ADAS13) scores.

For sequence modeling, we considered subjects with at least four time points and excluded subjects with fewer than four time points. This results in final sequences from 949 patients. For each subject, we constructed
temporal windows of width three
from consecutive visits (see
Sec.

VIII-D4
for a discussion on window sizes).
Once the NKM model is trained, for each temporal window we use features from the first three time points as input to predict the outcomes at the fourth time point, and the observed outcomes from the fourth time point are used to evaluate forecasting performance
(see
Fig.

2
).

To ensure that no data leakage occurred during training and evaluation, we employed a subject-stratified five-fold cross-validation procedure. To handle missing values, we used k-nearest neighbors (KNN) imputation within each fold and standardized all features using parameters estimated exclusively from the training data, again to avoid leakage.
To evaluate the forecasting performance, we used Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlation coefficients as well as mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean square error (RMSE) between the predicted and observed scores at the next visit, averaged across the three cognitive scores. We trained each model for up to 300 epochs with a batch size of 64; convergence, however, typically occurred earlier due to the early stopping criteria in the training pipeline. IV-C

Unveiling Disease-Progression Dynamics in the Koopman Latent Space

The brain can be regarded as a dynamical system. One of the goals of the paper is to characterize the brain’s dynamical properties as it begins to develop cognitive impairment and Alzheimer’s disease. Therefore, it is important to unveil the disease trajectory over time.

NKM uncovers the disease dynamics in its latent space. More specifically, the latent Koopman representations not only encode distinctly clustered features unique to each group (CN vs. MCI vs. AD), but also depict transition dynamics between groups (e.g., transitions from MCI to AD) (see
Fig.

3

\rightarrow
CN, MCI
\rightarrow
MCI, and AD
\rightarrow
AD) and conversion trajectories (CN
\rightarrow
MCI and MCI
\rightarrow

Fig.

3
). Subjects entering a CN state whose directed pathways also fall in the CN state are likely to maintain normal cognitive function. Subjects entering an AD state whose directed pathways also fall in the AD state are likely to have AD. Subjects entering areas around the early conversion pathway and whose trajectories are along this pathway are likely to begin developing mild cognitive impairment. Subjects entering areas around the disease progression pathway and whose trajectories are along this pathway are likely to convert into AD or MCI due to AD. Further, the latent Koopman states not only uncover the disease dynamics but also appear to be associated with brain volume changes. Particularly, the Koopman representations most associated with hippocampal and ventricular volumes help reveal how brain volume changes may give rise to disease transitions (see the right panel of
Fig.

3

IV-E

Biological and Neuroanatomical Substrates of Longitudinal Cognitive Decline via Interpretability Analysis

Here, we examine the importance and consistency of the biomarkers identified by NKM for longitudinal personalized AD forecasting.
First, NKM uncovers brain signatures respectively predictive of three cognitive scores over time (see
Fig.

2
). Our results indicate that longitudinal AD score prediction involves distributed cortical regions, including frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital areas. Consistently, regions within the default mode network (DMN), visual periphery, limbic system, and temporal–parietal areas were identified as key neural biomarkers across all three scores. These findings not only align with evidence that widespread cortical changes in AD are associated with global cognitive decline
[
49
,
50
]
, but also highlight that the patterns captured by these distributed changes provide valuable information for forecasting AD progression over time. The limbic regions appear informative for predicting both MMSE and CDRSB scores, consistent with functional decline and limbic vulnerability in early AD
[
51
]
. In contrast, the DMN
[
52
,
53
]
, particularly
DefaultA

[
54
]
,
consisting of parts of BA39 and BA10, is especially predictive of the ADAS13 score over time. This both aligns with previous findings on the association between BA39
[
55
]
, BA10
[
56
]
, and AD, and underscores its relevance for forecasting longidutinal cognitive decline.

Second, NKM identified APOE4 as a useful AD biomarker. While AD is recognized as a polygenic disease
[
57
]
, the APOE4 allele remains the most well-established genetic risk factor for late-onset AD
[
5
]
. Third, our results suggest that CSF features, particularly p-tau, are useful for predicting longitudinal MMSE, CDRSB, and ADAS13 scores, consistent with the central role of tau pathology in AD progression
[
59

. Fourth, PET images, particularly FDG-PET, provide predictive value for AD forecasting, as FDG-PET measures glucose metabolism in the brain, which is typically reduced in AD

Although the relationship between ICV and AD progression is not yet fully established, individuals with larger brains are thought to have greater “brain reserve”, potentially allowing them to tolerate more pathology before exhibiting cognitive symptoms

. Finally, we find that the biomarkers with both high feature importance scores and selection stability (i.e., consistently ranked among the top 10 features across 50 runs) are largely MRI-derived. This is likely attributable to the substantially greater availability of longitudinal MRI data in our dataset (each subject having at least three MRI scans), which enables the model to capture temporal trajectories of disease progression reflected in the repeatedly measured scans.
In contrast, the availability of repeated measurements varies significantly by modality. While ICV data is available longitudinally for nearly all subjects (923 of 949), repeated measurements are sparse for

V
Discussion

The structural pipeline of the NKM model that we presented in this paper, and the division of
\alpha
- and
\beta
-knowledge, provides us with a robust machine learning framework for longitudinal assessment of multiple disease-related outcomes using multimodal data.

The modular design of the NKM allows the model to be run on datasets that do not include all modalities considered in the current study or other studies – a common challenge in AD research and datasets. Another design decision that invites discussion is using a one-hot encoder rather than a categorical embedding for demographic features. This is a common trade-off in machine learning
[
62
]
. Within the NKM, a one-hot encoding is used so that these categorical groups are fed into dedicated Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) within the Feature-Aware Encoder we proposed. One-hot encoding offers interpretability and simplicity, treating demographic categories as mutually orthogonal without assuming relationships. Since demographic data typically have low cardinality, this is a suitable mathematical procedure. Hence, we avoid mathematical overparameterization, and, consequently, the integration
of demographics with the models’ group-aware design is more seamless. This design choice offers two key advantages: (1) it maintains demographic feature independence without introducing representational bias, and (2) it ensures direct compatibility with the Koopman operator’s state-space formulation. The alternative would be to use the categorical embeddings that learn dense vectors per category. This may capture more of the semantic similarities for better in-model interaction, and, within our use case, this may improve the gating mechanism, as the signals coming from the categories will be more prominent/obscure. However, given the low cardinality of our demographic data, categorical embeddings pose the risk of overfitting and training overhead, while reducing the interpretability of the model architecture
[
63
,
64
]
. Therefore, we use one-hot encoding to handle demographic information,
which lacks complex semantics, and rely on the encoder’s MLPs to provide equivalent compression.

VI
Final remarks

Our work has a few limitations. First, due partly to the data acquisition, we considered regularly sampled time series. Future work should evaluate the efficacy of NKM on irregularly sampled data. Second, our main focus here is to identify interpretable multimodal biomarkers predictive of future disease progression; independent work should evaluate whether these biomarkers are causally linked to AD progression. Third, while one can apply NKM to study other diseases, the trained architectures from AD data cannot yet be directly transferred to study datasets of other diseases. Future work can explore the potential of NKM for forecasting other disease trajectories. Lastly, recent studies have suggested promising blood-based biomarkers (e.g., p-tau181/217, neurofilament light chain (NfL), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP))
[
65
]
; while not included in our study, they offer new avenues to improve NKM by incorporating additional biomarkers.

Duality with Perron-Frobenius Operator

The Koopman operator
\mathcal{K}
is dual to the Perron-Frobenius operator
\mathcal{P}
, which propagates probability densities:

(\mathcal{P}\rho)({\bm{y}})=\int_{\{{\bm{x}}:{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}})={\bm{y}}\}}\rho({\bm{x}})\left|\det\left(\frac{\partial{\bm{f}}}{\partial{\bm{x}}}\right)^{-1}\right|d{\bm{x}},

where
\rho
is a probability density function.

The duality relation is:

\int_{{\mathbb{M}}}g({\bm{y}})(\mathcal{P}\rho)({\bm{y}})d{\bm{y}}=\int_{{\mathbb{M}}}(\mathcal{K}g)({\bm{x}})\rho({\bm{x}})d{\bm{x}},

for all observables
g
and densities
\rho
.

This duality connects our Koopman framework with probabilistic models of dynamical systems.

VIII-C
1
Finite-Dimensional Koopman Approximation

Consider the nonlinear dynamical system:

{\bm{x}}_{t+1}\;=\;f({\bm{x}}_{t}),\qquad{\bm{x}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{M}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
f:{\mathbb{M}}\to{\mathbb{M}}
is a nonlinear map on the state manifold
{\mathbb{M}}
.
The Koopman operator
\mathcal{K}
is the composition operator:

(\mathcal{K}g)({\bm{x}})\;=\;g\bigl(f({\bm{x}})\bigr),\qquad g\in{\mathbb{F}},

acting on a suitable function space
{\mathbb{F}}
of observables.

Let a finite set of observables be collected in

\bm{\Phi}:{\mathbb{M}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}}
:

\bm{\Phi}({\bm{x}})\;=\;\begin{bmatrix}\phi_{1}({\bm{x}})\\[-2.0pt]
\phi_{2}({\bm{x}})\\[-4.0pt]
\vdots\\[-4.0pt]
\phi_{d_{z}}({\bm{x}})\end{bmatrix},\qquad{\bm{z}}_{t}\;=\;\bm{\Phi}({\bm{x}}_{t}).

We approximate the infinite-dimensional Koopman operator by a matrix
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}\times d_{z}}
:

\bm{\Phi}\!\bigl(f({\bm{x}}_{t})\bigr)\;=\;(\mathcal{K}\bm{\Phi})({\bm{x}}_{t})\;\approx\;\mathbf{K}\,\bm{\Phi}({\bm{x}}_{t}).

(22)

Component-wise this reads:

(\mathcal{K}\phi_{j})({\bm{x}})\;\approx\;\sum_{i=1}^{d_{z}}K_{ij}\,\phi_{i}({\bm{x}}).

Approximation quality.

With
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathbb{F}}}
a norm on
{\mathbb{F}}
, define

\varepsilon_{j}\;=\;\min_{\{a_{ij}\}}\Bigl\|\mathcal{K}\phi_{j}\;-\!\sum_{i=1}^{d_{z}}a_{ij}\phi_{i}\Bigr\|_{{\mathbb{F}}}.

For tighter bounds on attractors like disease states (e.g.,
{\mathbb{A}}\subset{\mathbb{M}}
), we use a uniform (pointwise Euclidean) bound:

\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{A}}}\bigl\|\mathcal{K}\phi_{j}({\bm{x}})-\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d_{z}}a_{ij}\phi_{i}({\bm{x}})\bigr\|_{2},

implicitly making the error estimate more restrictive.

VIII-C
2
Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (EDMD)

Given snapshots
\{({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{y}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}

with
{\bm{y}}_{i}=f({\bm{x}}_{i})
, EDMD solves:

\min_{\mathbf{K}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl\|\bm{\Phi}({\bm{y}}_{i})-\mathbf{K}\,\bm{\Phi}({\bm{x}}_{i})\bigr\|_{2}^{2}.

Denote:

\displaystyle{\bm{X}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Phi}({\bm{x}}_{1})^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{\Phi}({\bm{x}}_{m})^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d_{z}},

(23)

\displaystyle{\bm{Y}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Phi}({\bm{y}}_{1})^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{\Phi}({\bm{y}}_{m})^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d_{z}}.

Then:

\mathbf{K}={\bm{Y}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{X}})^{\dagger},

(24)

where
†
is the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse. In practice,
{\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}
is often rank-deficient due to limited sample sizes or colinearities in the data, requiring careful regularization.

Empirical
Gramians

Define:

{\bm{G}}:=\tfrac{1}{m}\,{\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{X}},\qquad{\bm{A}}:=\tfrac{1}{m}\,{\bm{Y}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}.

With these
forward

Gramians
,
Eq.

24
becomes:

\mathbf{K}\;=\;{\bm{A}}\,{\bm{G}}^{\dagger}.

(25)

Regularized variant

For numerical stability,

\mathbf{K}={\bm{A}}\bigl({\bm{G}}+\alpha{\bm{I}}\bigr)^{-1},

unchanged by the above correction.

The remaining discussion on the joint learning of

(\bm{\Phi},\mathbf{K})
and on regularization carries through verbatim.

VIII-C
3
Neural Parameterization of Observables and Koopman Matrix

Our neural framework learns both the observable functions
\bm{\Phi}
and the Koopman matrix
\mathbf{K}
simultaneously.

Encoder Architecture

The SiLU (Sigmoid Linear Unit) activation function, also known as the swish function, is defined as
\mathrm{SiLU}(x)=x\cdot\sigma(x)
where
\sigma
is the sigmoid function. This activation has been shown to outperform ReLU in deep networks due to its smooth, non-monotonic nature.

We implement
\bm{\Phi}
as a feature-aware encoder followed by a shared integration network:

\displaystyle{\bm{h}}_{t}^{(g)}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{SiLU}\bigl(\mathrm{LayerNorm}({\bm{W}}_{g}\,{\bm{x}}_{t}^{(g)}+{\bm{b}}_{g})\bigr),\quad g=1,\dots,G,

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Phi}({\bm{x}}_{t};{\bm{\theta}}_{\text{enc}})=\mathrm{SiLU}\Bigl({\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{int}}\bigl[{\bm{h}}_{t}^{(1)},\dots,{\bm{h}}_{t}^{(G)}\bigr]+{\bm{b}}_{\mathrm{int}}\Bigr),

where
{\bm{x}}_{t}^{(g)}
are disjoint feature groups (projected to uniform
d_{z}
after fusion), and LayerNorm is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{LayerNorm}({\bm{u}})

\displaystyle=\bm{\gamma}\odot\frac{{\bm{u}}-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+\epsilon}}+\bm{\beta},

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{i},\qquad\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}(u_{i}-\mu)^{2}.

with learnable parameters
\bm{\gamma},\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a small constant
\epsilon>0
for numerical stability.

The feature-aware encoding allows our model to capture domain-specific patterns in different input modalities, while the integration network combines these representations into a unified Koopman embedding.

Koopman Matrix Learning

While the classical EDMD approach computes
\mathbf{K}
analytically using
Eq.

24
, we learn
\mathbf{K}
by directly minimizing the Koopman prediction loss (defined in
Sec.

VIII-C6

). This approach allows joint optimization of both
\bm{\Phi}
and
\mathbf{K}
.

For enhanced stability and interpretability, we can parameterize
\mathbf{K}
using its eigendecomposition:

\mathbf{K}={\bm{V}}{\bm{\Lambda}}{\bm{V}}^{-1},

where
{\bm{V}}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{z}\times d_{z}}
contains the eigenvectors and
{\bm{\Lambda}}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{d_{z}})
contains the eigenvalues. By directly parameterizing and learning
\{\lambda_{i}\}
and
{\bm{V}}
, we can impose constraints on the eigenvalues to ensure stability (e.g.,
|\lambda_{i}|<1
) while maintaining the expressivity of the model.

VIII-C
4
Koopman Matrix Initialization and Optimization

We now provide details for the initialization and optimization processes of the Koopman matrix
\mathbf{K}
, which is central to our Neural Koopman Machine framework. These processes ensure efficient training dynamics and numerical stability while maintaining the theoretical properties necessary for accurate dynamical system modeling.

Structured Initialization of the Koopman Matrix

The convergence characteristics and learning dynamics of our model are significantly influenced by the initialization of the Koopman matrix
\mathbf{K}
. We initialize
\mathbf{K}
as a perturbation of the identity matrix:

\mathbf{K}_{0}={\bm{I}}+{\bm{B}}_{0},

(26)

where
{\bm{B}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}\times d_{z}}
is a small random perturbation with entries drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\text{init}}^{2})
, and
\sigma_{\text{init}}\ll 1
is a small scalar (typically
\sigma_{\text{init}}=10^{-2}
).

This initialization is theoretically justified: (1) the identity mapping assumes approximate constancy between time steps, suitable for high-frequency sampling; (2) eigenvalues near 1 allow learning stable/marginally stable modes; (3) it facilitates gradient flow.

To ensure
\|\mathbf{K}_{0}\|_{2}<1
, we first compute the perturbation

\mathbf{K}_{0}^{\text{init}}=\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{B}_{0},

where
\mathbf{B}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}\times d_{z}}
has entries drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\text{init}}^{2})
with
\sigma_{\text{init}}\ll 1
. We then project via SVD:

\mathbf{K}_{0}^{\text{init}}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\top},

and clip the singular values:
\tilde{\sigma}_{i}=\min(\sigma_{i},\rho_{\text{init}})
where
\rho_{\text{init}}<1
(typically 0.99), yielding the final initialized matrix:

\mathbf{K}_{0}=\mathbf{U}\tilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathbf{V}^{\top}.

Optimization Dynamics of the Koopman Matrix

The Koopman matrix
\mathbf{K}
evolves during training according to the gradients of our composite loss function.

•

Gradient-Based Updates. Our composite loss includes a Koopman prediction term (
Eq.

4
):

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Koopman}}=\frac{1}{N(T-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\bigl\|{\bm{z}}_{t+1}^{\prime\,(i)}-\mathbf{K}\,{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime\,(i)}-{\bm{c}}_{\mathrm{control}}^{(i)}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}.

The gradient of this loss with respect to
\mathbf{K}
is:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{K}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Koopman}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\lambda}{N(T-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\bigl(\mathbf{K}\,{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime\,(i)}

(27)

\displaystyle+{\bm{c}}_{\mathrm{control}}^{(i)}-{\bm{z}}_{t+1}^{\prime\,(i)}\bigr)\,{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime\,(i)\top}.

(28)

This gradient can be rewritten using empirical covariance matrices. Define:

\hat{{\bm{C}}}_{zz}=\frac{1}{N(T-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime\,(i)}{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime\,(i)\top},

\hat{{\bm{C}}}_{z^{\prime}z}=\frac{1}{N(T-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}{\bm{z}}_{t+1}^{\prime\,(i)}{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime\,(i)\top},

\hat{{\bm{C}}}_{cz}=\frac{1}{N(T-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}{\bm{c}}_{\mathrm{control}}^{(i)}{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime\,(i)\top}.

Then
Eq.

27
becomes:

\nabla_{\mathbf{K}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Koopman}}=2\lambda\left(\mathbf{K}\hat{{\bm{C}}}_{zz}+\hat{{\bm{C}}}_{cz}-\hat{{\bm{C}}}_{z^{\prime}z}\right).

For gradient descent with learning rate
\eta
, the update rule for
\mathbf{K}
at iteration
j
is:

\mathbf{K}_{j+1}=\mathbf{K}_{j}-\eta\,\nabla_{\mathbf{K}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Koopman}}.

•

Fixed-Point Analysis. At convergence, assuming
\hat{{\bm{C}}}_{zz}
is invertible, the gradient vanishes when:

\mathbf{K}^{*}=(\hat{{\bm{C}}}_{z^{\prime}z}-\hat{{\bm{C}}}_{cz})\hat{{\bm{C}}}_{zz}^{-1}.

This is analogous to the closed-form EDMD solution in
Eq.

25
, but incorporates the effect of the control term. This demonstrates the connection between our gradient-based optimization and classical EDMD, with the control term acting as a correction to the cross-covariance matrix.

VIII-C
5
Temporal and Feature-Group Attention Mechanisms

For non-autonomous systems or systems with complex temporal dependencies, we incorporate a control mechanism based on temporal self-attention, extended to feature groups as in
III-I
and
III-J
.

Given the sequence of refined latent states

{\bm{Z}}^{\prime}=[{\bm{z}}^{\prime}_{1},\dots,{\bm{z}}^{\prime}_{T}]\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d_{z}}
,
we compute query, key, and value projections (single-head for alignment with the main text):

\displaystyle{\bm{Q}}

\displaystyle={\bm{Z}}^{\prime}\,{\bm{W}}^{Q},\quad{\bm{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d_{k}},

\displaystyle{\bm{K}}^{\text{att}}

\displaystyle={\bm{Z}}^{\prime}\,{\bm{W}}^{K},\quad{\bm{K}}^{\text{att}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d_{k}},

\displaystyle{\bm{V}}

\displaystyle={\bm{Z}}^{\prime}\,{\bm{W}}^{V},\quad{\bm{V}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d_{v}},

where
{\bm{W}}^{Q},{\bm{W}}^{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}\times d_{k}}
and
{\bm{W}}^{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}\times d_{v}}
are learnable weight matrices.

The attention scores are computed as:

{\bm{A}}=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{{\bm{Q}}\,({\bm{K}}^{\text{att}})^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T},

and the output is:

\mathrm{Attention}({\bm{Q}},{\bm{K}}^{\text{att}},{\bm{V}})={\bm{A}}\,{\bm{V}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d_{v}}.

At the final timestep
T
, we compute a group-specific context vector:

{\bm{c}}_{\mathrm{group}}={\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{grp}}\,{\bm{z}}_{T}^{\text{ref}}+{\bm{b}}_{\mathrm{grp}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}},

where
{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{grp}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}\times d_{z}}
,
{\bm{b}}_{\mathrm{grp}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}
, and
d_{\text{model}}=d_{z}
.

We then form a gating vector (ensuring consistent dims via projection if needed):

{\bm{g}}=\sigma\!\left({\bm{W}}_{g}\,[{\bm{z}}_{T}^{\text{ref}};\mathrm{Attention}[T,:]]+{\bm{b}}_{g}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}},

where
\sigma
is the sigmoid activation function.

The final control vector is obtained by fusing the temporal and group-specific contexts:

{\bm{c}}_{\mathrm{control}}={\bm{g}}\odot{\bm{c}}_{\mathrm{group}}+(1-{\bm{g}})\odot\mathrm{Attention}[T,:]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}},

where
\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication.

Our controlled latent dynamics thus become:

{\bm{z}}_{t+1}^{\prime}=\mathbf{K}\,{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime}+{\bm{c}}_{\mathrm{control}}.

VIII-C
6
Composite Loss Function and Gradients

We train our model end-to-end by minimizing a composite loss function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl\|\mathcal{D}({\bm{z}}_{t+1}^{(i)};{\bm{\theta}}_{\text{dec}})-{\bm{y}}_{t+1}^{(i)}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}}

\displaystyle+\lambda\,\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{N(T-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\bigl\|{\bm{z}}_{t+1}^{(i)}-\mathbf{K}{\bm{z}}_{t}^{(i)}-{\bm{c}}_{\mathrm{control}}^{(i)}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{koop}}}\!.

where
\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t+1}^{(i)}=\mathcal{D}({\bm{z}}_{t+1}^{\text{ref},(i)};{\bm{\theta}}_{\text{dec}})
is the predicted next outcome,
\mathcal{D}
is a decoder network, and
\lambda>0
balances the two loss terms.

The gradient of the Koopman loss with respect to
\mathbf{K}
is:

\begin{split}\nabla_{\mathbf{K}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Koopman}}=\frac{2\lambda}{N(T-1)}\\
\times\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}(\mathbf{K}{\bm{z}}_{t}^{(i)}+{\bm{c}}^{(i)}-{\bm{z}}_{t+1}^{(i)})\,{\bm{z}}_{t}^{(i)\top}.\end{split}

VIII-C
7
Spectral Regularization and Stability

As shown in
Eq.

21
, the stability of multi-step predictions depends critically
on the spectral properties of the Koopman matrix
\mathbf{K}
. To encourage
\|\mathbf{K}\|_{2}<1

(i.e., all eigenvalues lie within the unit circle), we introduce a spectral regularization term:

\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{K})=\eta\,\bigl(\|\mathbf{K}\|_{2}^{2}-\rho^{2}\bigr)_{+}^{2},

where
\eta>0
is a regularization coefficient,
\rho<1
is a target upper bound for the
spectral norm, and
(x)_{+}=\max(0,x)
activates the penalty only when
\|\mathbf{K}\|_{2}>\rho
.

To approximate
\|\mathbf{K}\|_{2}
efficiently during training, we use power iteration with

p
iterations (typically
p\in\{5,10\}
). Starting with a random unit vector
\mathbf{v}_{0}
,
we iteratively compute:

\mathbf{v}_{i+1}=\frac{\mathbf{K}\mathbf{v}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{K}\mathbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}},\quad i=0,1,\ldots,p-1,

and approximate the spectral norm via the Rayleigh quotient:

\|\mathbf{K}\|_{2}\approx\|\mathbf{K}\mathbf{v}_{p}\|_{2}.

This approximation converges exponentially fast to the largest singular value when the
spectral gap
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{K})-\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{K})
is large.

The total loss with spectral regularization becomes:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}+\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{koop}}+\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{K}).

During inference, we optionally apply hard projection
\mathbf{K}\leftarrow\mathbf{K}/\max(1,\|\mathbf{K}\|_{2}/\rho)

to ensure strict spectral bounds for long-horizon forecasting.

Window Sizes

In sequential modeling, the window size selection is a crucial design factor that greatly impacts how well disease dynamics are captured. We chose a window size of three consecutive visits for our Neural Koopman Machine applied to AD progression, based on theoretical considerations from dynamical systems theory and clinical insights.

According to the ADNI protocol, the progression of AD shows significant changes over 12 to 18 months, or two to three semi-annual visits
[
47
]
. This timeframe achieves the best possible balance between capturing long-term trajectories that accurately reflect the progression of the disease and short-term fluctuations that could be noise
[
69
]
.

Koopman operator theory for nonlinear dynamical systems benefits from having enough embedding dimensions to reconstruct the system attractor. For cognitive decline, which follows nonlinear trajectories with possible bifurcations at transition points, a minimum dimension of 3 captures these dynamics, while the window size remains computationally tractable and suitable for phase space reconstruction in nonlinear systems.

Our choice is further supported by data retention considerations. With a window size of three (meaning four time points to allow making one prediction based on three past visits), we had an acceptable dropout rate of about 30%, but increasing the window size to four or five raised the data retention rate to
\sim
50%, limiting statistical power and possibly introducing selection bias toward patients with long follow-up periods.

VIII-E
3
Cognitive Assessments

Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE):
A questionnaire used to measure cognitive impairment, assessing orientation, attention, memory, language, and visuospatial skills. Scores range from 0–30: a higher MMSE score suggests better cognitive function and a lower score may suggest the presence of cognitive impairment or dementia.

Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDRSB):
A clinical assessment tool that evaluates six cognitive and functional domains: memory, orientation, judgment and problem solving, community affairs, home and hobbies, and personal care. Each domain is rated 0–3, and scores are summed (total range: 0–18), with higher scores indicating greater impairment.

Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale–Cognitive Subscale 13 (ADAS13):
A comprehensive cognitive battery consisting of 13 tasks assessing: 1. Spoken language ability, 2. Comprehension of spoken language, 3. Recall of test instructions, 4. Wordfinding difficulty in spontaneous speech, 5. Following commands, 6. Naming objects and fingers, 7. Constructional praxis, 8. Ideational praxis, 9. Orientation, 10. Word-recall task, 11. Word recognition task, 12. Delayed Word Recall, and 13. Number Cancellation Word recognition task. Scores are summed and range from 0–85, with higher scores indicating greater cognitive impairment.

VIII-E
4
Technical Abbreviations

Koopman Operator Theory (KOT):
A mathematical framework, originally introduced by Bernard Koopman, that represents nonlinear dynamical systems via an infinite-dimensional linear operator acting on observables, enabling linear analysis of the system dynamics in a lifted functional space.

Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (EDMD):
A data-driven method for approximating the Koopman operator by lifting the system into a finite-dimensional subspace spanned by selected basis functions. EDMD generalizes standard Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD), enabling the analysis of nonlinear dynamical systems via a linear representation in the lifted space.

Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD):
A method for characterizing the dynamics of complex systems by decomposing spatiotemporal data into spatial modes and corresponding temporal coefficients that capture coherent structures evolving over time.

Reverse Time Attention Model (RETAIN):
A neural network architecture that employs reverse-time attention mechanisms to make predictions on sequential healthcare data while providing interpretable feature-level explanations.
