Post-traumatic superior mesenteric artery syndrome following lumbar spine stabilization: A case report and literature review
Authors: Imen Ben Ismail, Khadija Yaich, Marwen Sghaier, Saber Rebii, Hakim Zenaidi, Ayoub Zoghlami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuperior Mesenteric Artery Syndrome (SMAS) is a rare cause of proximal intestinal obstruction resulting from compression of the third portion of the duodenum between the aorta and the superior mesenteric artery. It is most frequently reported in patients with rapid weight loss, catabolic states, or after elective spinal deformity surgery. SMAS following post-traumatic spinal stabilization is extremely uncommon and under-recognized.Case presentationWe report the case of a 17-year-old male with no significant past medical history who sustained a lumbar burst fracture (L3) after a domestic fall from a height of three stories. He underwent percutaneous pedicle screw fixation with an initially uneventful postoperative course. On postoperative day 11, he developed persistent postprandial vomiting and epigastric pain. Physical examination revealed dehydration and epigastric tenderness without peritoneal signs. Laboratory studies demonstrated mild hyponatremia and inflammatory response, with preserved renal function. Abdominal computed tomography revealed marked dilation of the stomach and proximal duodenum with a markedly reduced aortomesenteric distance (3 mm), consistent with SMAS, without evidence of intra-abdominal effusion or obstruction. A conservative management approach was implemented, including nasogastric decompression, intravenous fluid resuscitation, urinary catheterization, and close clinical monitoring. The patient's symptoms progressively resolved, and he resumed full oral intake within one week, without the need for surgical intervention.ConclusionThis case highlights that SMAS can occur even after post-traumatic spinal stabilization in otherwise healthy adolescents. Awareness of this rare complication is essential for early recognition and timely intervention. Conservative management, when instituted promptly, can be highly effective and may prevent unnecessary surgical procedures. Spine surgeons and perioperative teams should consider SMAS in the differential diagnosis of postoperative vomiting and feeding intolerance, even in post-traumatic contexts.Keywords: Superior mesenteric artery syndrome, Spine surgery, Lumbar fracture, Duodenal obstruction, Conservative management

Introduction

Superior Mesenteric Artery Syndrome (SMA syndrome), also known as “Wilkie's syndrome”, is a rare but potentially serious cause of high gastrointestinal obstruction characterized by extrinsic compression of the third portion of the duodenum between the abdominal aorta and the overlying Superior Mesenteric Artery (SMA) [1].

In physiologic conditions, retroperitoneal and mesenteric fat tissue normally cushions the duodenum and maintains an aorto-mesenteric angle (and distance) sufficient to prevent compression [2].

Any condition leading to marked loss of that fat pad such as rapid weight loss, catabolic states, prolonged immobilization, or spinal deformity surgery can drastically narrow the aorto-mesenteric angle, reduce the aorto-mesenteric distance, and predispose to duodenal compression [3].

While classically described in the context of severe cachexia (e.g. in anorexia nervosa, malnutrition, burns, or malignancy), SMA syndrome has also been reported in patients with spinal cord injury, prolonged immobilization, or after spinal surgery especially corrective procedures for scoliosis [4].

Indeed, but few authors have described SMA syndrome as a complication following spinal trauma or spine stabilization surgery, rendering this etiology uncommon and likely under-recognized [5].

The clinical presentation of SMA syndrome may be subtle, often mimicking more common postoperative or posttraumatic gastrointestinal disturbances (nausea, vomiting, delayed gastric emptying) [6].

Consequently, diagnosis is frequently delayed which may worsen nutritional and metabolic derangements, prolong hospital stay, and increase morbidity. Cross-sectional imaging, especially contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) with sagittal reconstructions, proves to be a highly valuable diagnostic tool, allowing accurate measurement of aorto-mesenteric angle and distance, and assessment of duodenal compression [7].

Given the rarity of post-traumatic or post-spine-surgery SMA syndrome, each case can provide important insights into its pathophysiology, risk factors, diagnostic pitfalls, and management strategies. Here, we present the case of a young male who developed acute SMA syndrome eleven days after percutaneous pedicle screw fixation for a lumbar burst fracture an etiologic scenario scarcely documented in the literature. Through a review of the literature, we discuss potential mechanisms, the role of spinal trauma and surgery, and emphasize the need for heightened clinical suspicion in similar contexts.

Case presentation

A 17-year-old male with no previous medical history was admitted to the orthopedic department after a domestic fall from approximately 12 m. On admission, he was hemodynamically stable and neurologically intact. Whole-body computed tomography revealed a burst fracture of the L3 vertebral body with posterior wall retropulsion, resulting in about 75% narrowing of the spinal canal. No intracanal bone fragment was identified. A fracture of the left transverse process of L3 was also noted.

The patient underwent percutaneous pedicle screw fixation (Fig. 1). The immediate postoperative course was uneventful, and he was discharged a few days later in good general condition.

Postoperative anteroposterior and lateral radiographs of the lumbar spine showing percutaneous pedicle screw fixation at L2–L4 for stabilization of the L3 burst fracture. The vertebral alignment is restored, and hardware is in satisfactory position.

Eleven days after surgery, he presented again with postprandial vomiting and epigastric pain that had been evolving for four days. He denied fever, diarrhea, or abdominal distension. On examination, he appeared fatigued and clinically dehydrated, with periorbital darkening and reduced skin turgor. His temperature was normal, blood pressure 110/50 mmHg, and heart rate 102 beats per minute. The abdomen was soft and non-distended, with localized tenderness in the epigastrium but no guarding or rebound tenderness. The operative site was clean, without signs of infection.

Laboratory investigations revealed elevated urea (30 mmol/L) and creatinine (102 μmol/L, clearance 92 mL/min), an inflammatory response with CRP at 143 mg/L and leukocytosis of 22,000/mm3, mild hyponatremia (Na 121 mmol/L) and hypochloremia (Cl 84 mmol/L). Potassium was normal (3.7 mmol/L). Hemoglobin level was 11.5 g/dL, platelets 119,800/mm3, and serum lipase within the normal range (60 IU/L).

Abdominal CT scan showed marked gastric and duodenal distension with fluid and air content. The aortomesenteric distance measured only 3 mm on sagittal reconstruction (Fig. 2), confirming the diagnosis of superior mesenteric artery (SMA) syndrome. No intra-abdominal effusion, pneumoperitoneum, or vascular obstruction was detected.

Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT scan showing marked gastric and proximal duodenal distension. Axial view confirming duodenal compression between the aorta and the SMA (two yellow arrows). (For interpretation of the references to colour in this figure legend, the reader is referred to the web version of this article.)

The patient was managed conservatively with nasogastric decompression, aggressive intravenous hydration, and urinary catheterization. His condition gradually improved, with progressive resolution of vomiting and normalization of oral intake within one week.

Discussion

Superior Mesenteric Artery Syndrome (SMAS) also known as “Wilkie syndrome” is a rare but well-recognized cause of upper gastrointestinal obstruction, resulting from extrinsic compression of the third portion of the duodenum between the abdominal aorta posteriorly and the Superior Mesenteric Artery (SMA) anteriorly. The pathophysiology typically involves a decrease in the aortomesenteric angle and/or distance, often due to loss of the cushioning mesenteric and retroperitoneal fat pad [8].

Although SMAS classically arises in the context of rapid weight loss, catabolic states (e.g. burns, malignancy), prolonged immobilization, or external compression (e.g. body cast), it has also been described following spinal surgery for deformity correction [9]. In the setting of spinal deformity correction particularly for adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) factors thought to predispose include low body mass index (BMI), rapid changes in spinal alignment (lengthening of the spinal column), and consequent alteration of the spatial relationship between spine, aorta, SMA, and duodenum [10].

In fact, a recent systematic review focusing on SMAS after correction of spinal deformity (AIS) identified 61 patients across 29 studies [8]. The mean BMI among these patients was 16.5 ± 2.9 kg/m2 consistent with a lean habitus, and most were adolescents. The interval between surgery and onset of symptoms was highly variable: although many developed symptoms early (within days to weeks), others presented months to years later. The lack of a strict temporal pattern underscores how unpredictable SMAS can be in postoperative spinal patients. Our case differs from the majority of the published cases in two important ways. First, our patient did not undergo corrective surgery for a spinal deformity, but percutaneous stabilization of a post-traumatic lumbar fracture. Second, he presented on postoperative day 11, which is within the early postoperative window but in a context not usually considered high-risk for SMAS. There are few reports in the literature of SMAS developing after post-traumatic spine surgery making our observation particularly valuable.

The occurrence of SMAS in a post-traumatic context may reflect a combination of factors: immobilization, relative anorexia or reduced oral intake post trauma/surgery, loss of mesenteric fat, and anatomic alterations induced by spinal stabilization that modify the spatial relationships between the aorta, SMA, and duodenum. The report from Bouali et al. [7] describes a similar post-traumatic spine surgery patient who developed SMAS early (postoperative day 3) though in their case conservative management failed and a gastrojejunostomy was eventually required [11]. This underscores that SMAS in post-traumatic spine surgery may not always follow the benign course seen in many post-deformity correction cases.

From a clinical standpoint, SMAS often presents with non-specific symptoms such as postprandial vomiting, epigastric pain or fullness, nausea, and poor tolerance to oral intake. On examination, the abdomen may be soft, non-distended, with little to no peritoneal signs making it easily misinterpretable as postoperative ileus, delayed gastric emptying, medication intolerance, or other common postoperative complications [12]. In our patient, the soft abdomen without guarding or rigidity, absence of fever, and isolated epigastric tenderness fit this pattern. Diagnosis requires a high index of suspicion, especially in spine-surgery patients with unexplained vomiting or feeding intolerance. Cross-sectional imaging contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) with sagittal reconstructions is particularly helpful; it allows measurement of the aortomesenteric distance/angle and visualization of duodenal dilation and obstruction [13]. In our case, the aortomesenteric distance measured only 3 mm clearly supportive of SMAS.

Regarding management, initial conservative treatment remains the standard of care: gastric decompression (nasogastric tube), fluid and electrolyte correction, nutritional support (enteral or parenteral), and careful monitoring [14]. In series of SMAS after spinal deformity surgery, conservative measures were successful in many patients, particularly when diagnosis was made early [8]. However, failure of conservative therapy or clinical deterioration may prompt surgical intervention (e.g., duodenojejunostomy or gastrojejunostomy) to bypass the obstruction [15]. In our patient, the favorable clinical response resolution of vomiting, restoration of oral intake within a week supports the efficiency of conservative management when promptly instituted. This case adds to the limited but growing body of evidence that SMAS can occur after post-traumatic spinal stabilization, not only after elective deformity correction. It raises several important practical messages for clinicians orthopedic surgeons, trauma surgeons, anesthesiologists, intensivists, and gastroenterologists: Maintain a high index of suspicion for SMAS in any spine-surgery patient (traumatic or elective) who develops unexplained vomiting, epigastric pain, or feeding intolerance even in the absence of classical risk factors (e.g., low BMI, scoliosis). Consider early abdominal imaging with sagittal CT reconstruction if clinical picture is suspicious. Initiate conservative therapy promptly (gastric decompression, fluid/electrolyte correction, nutritional support), since early management may avoid serious complications (gastric perforation, aspiration, prolonged morbidity) and eliminate the need for surgical bypass. Monitor nutritional status and weight closely in postoperative spine patients, especially adolescents or young adults, and ensure adequate caloric intake to preserve mesenteric fat. Conclusion

Although SMAS is uncommon after spinal surgery, and even more rarely reported in the post-traumatic stabilization context, it remains a real and potentially serious complication. Our case highlights that timely recognition and conservative management can lead to full recovery. Spine and trauma surgeons should remain aware of this entity and include SMAS in the differential diagnosis of postoperative vomiting or feeding intolerance regardless of the nature of spinal surgery (elective vs traumatic) or presence of classical risk factors.
