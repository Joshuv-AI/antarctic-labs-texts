Bidirectional reallocations of sitting time, physical activity, and sleep in relation to risk of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular disease: An analysis of the Prospective Urban Rural Epidemiology (PURE)-China cohort study
Authors: Yilin Huang, Jun Hao, Bo Hu, Yang Wang, Lap Ah Tse, Weida Liu, Qiujing Cai, Biyan Wang, Zhiguang Liu, Tong Bu, Sumathy Rangarajan, Scott A. Lear, Wei Li, PURE-China Investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•A J-shaped association between daily sitting time and risks of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular disease was found in the PURE-China cohort, with increased risks at both low and high sitting levels.•Reallocating 30 min of sitting to physical activity in individuals sitting ≥4 h/day linked to 3–4%% lower composite outcome risk and 6%–7% lower all-cause mortality.•A “sitting paradox” was observed in those with low sedentary time (<4 h/day): reallocating 30 min of physical activity or prolonged sleep to sitting was linked to a 4%–6% lower risk of the composite outcome and a 4%–10% lower risk of all-cause mortality.

Introduction

Public health guidelines recommend at least 150 min of moderate or 75 min of vigorous physical activity per week, encouraging people to “sit less, move more” to achieve a number of health benefits.1, 2, 3 Numerous large-scale cohort studies have demonstrated a linear association between prolonged sedentary time and increased risks of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality.4, 5, 6, 7, 8 However, some studies have found that low levels of sitting may not always result in the greatest risk reduction. Analyses conducted in predominantly high-income cohorts—such as the UK Biobank—reported the second quartile of sitting time (8.2–9.4 h/day) to confer the lowest risk.9 Additionally, several Chinese multicenter studies have reported a J-shaped relationship between sitting time and all-cause mortality, cancer, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease (CVD), although the elevated risks at the lowest levels of sitting have not consistently reached statistical significance.4,10, 11, 12 However, this may be partly explained by urban-rural differences as additional studies conducted in suburban or rural populations reported a linear association.5,13 Thus, it remains uncertain whether reducing sedentary time yields consistent benefits across diverse populations.

Data from the Prospective Urban Rural Epidemiology (PURE) study highlight substantial global disparities in daily sitting time.14 In North America and Europe, only one third of participants reported sitting less than 4 h per day, compared to over 50% in China and many African countries. These findings align with the 2012 Lancet Physical Activity Series, which reported a lower prevalence of sitting <4 h/day in Europe (35.9%) than in Southeast Asia (76.2%).15 Previous research has introduced the concept of the “physical activity paradox”, whereby very high levels of occupational physical activity may not be health-protective and may even be harmful, potentially due to prolonged periods of activity without sufficient recovery.16 In such low-sitting and high-activity settings, the general recommendation to “sit less, move more” may not be applicable.

Because physical activity, sedentary behavior, and sleep share the 24-h day, increasing one behavior necessarily reduces time available for others. These behaviors should therefore not be examined in isolation. Replacing sedentary time with moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA), walking, or sleep is associated with reduced risks of premature mortality and cardiometabolic outcomes.7,17, 18, 19 For example, a pooled analysis of over 1 million adults found that engaging in 60–75 min of moderate-intensity activity per day could offset the excess mortality associated with prolonged sitting.17 Data from the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) indicated that replacing just 30 min of sitting per day with MVPA was linked to a 50% reduction in mortality risk.20 Compositional data analyses further suggest that replacing sedentary time with any intensity of physical activity is generally beneficial.21 However, these findings are mostly based on populations with high sedentary time from high-income countries. It remains unclear whether similar reallocation benefits apply to different populations with lower sitting time.

In China, only 2 cohort studies have explored the health impact of replacing sedentary time with physical activity or sleep, and both focused solely on cardiovascular outcomes, rather than all-cause mortality.11,18 In addition, available evidence has generally not considered bidirectional time reallocations, specific activity domains (e.g., leisure vs. non-leisure physical activity), or heterogeneity by occupational physical activity. Therefore, this study aimed to assess whether reallocating sedentary time to physical activity or sleep is associated with reductions in all-cause mortality and CVD in a large Chinese cohort. We also explored whether modest increases in sitting time among individuals with low sedentary behavior could provide survival or cardiovascular benefits, reflecting a potential “sitting paradox” in line with the physical activity paradox. We hypothesized a J-shaped association between sitting time and health outcomes, with the lowest risk at moderate sitting levels. Accordingly, reductions in sitting would be beneficial primarily above a certain daily threshold, whereas in those with very low sitting time a modest increase in sitting might be associated with lower risk, potentially reflecting improved recovery from high physical demands.

Methods

Study design and participants

For the present analysis, we utilized data from the PURE-China cohort, adopting a study design, sampling strategy, and follow-up approach consistent with previously published PURE research.22 In brief, adults aged 35–70 years were enrolled between January 1, 2005 and December 31, 2009 from 115 urban and rural communities across 12 provinces in China. A follow-up survey has been conducted every 3 years since baseline. The data for the present study were updated to August 31, 2021. Based on national guidelines for coordinated regional development at the time of participant enrollment, provinces were categorized into eastern (Beijing, Jiangsu, Liaoning, and Shandong), central (Shanxi and Jiangxi), and western regions (Shaanxi, Sichuan, Yunnan, Inner Mongolia, Qinghai, and Xinjiang) according to their socioeconomic development levels. We excluded participants with missing key covariates (age or sex), missing or implausible/extreme exposure values, prevalent CVD at baseline, or no available follow-up information.

The PURE study was a global cohort study approved on both ethical and scientific grounds by the McMaster University Research Ethics Board (Approval No. 03-206, dated July 7, 2003). The PURE-China component received additional approval from the Ethics Committees of Fuwai Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Approval No. 2020-1313), and the Beijing Hypertension League Institute. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. We followed the STROBE checklist to ensure rigor in our analysis.

Exposures

Information on 1-week sitting time and total physical activity was collected using the long-form International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), a standardized interviewer-administered instrument that typically requires approximately 10–15 min to complete and has been validated in multiple countries, including China.23, 24, 25 Details of the long-form IPAQ and corresponding metabolic equivalent task (MET) values are provided in Supplementary Materials. The total daily sitting hours were assessed using 2 questions in IPAQ: “During the last 7 days, how much time did you usually spend sitting on a usual (1) weekday and (2) weekend day?” Daily sitting time was calculated as the average sitting time per day ((5 × weekday hour + 2 × weekend hour)/7). According to previous literature,14,17 daily sitting time was initially categorized into 4 groups: <4, 4–<6, 6–<8, and ≥8 h/day in China, we further subdivided this category into <2 h/day and 2–<4 h/day to enhance granularity at the lower end of exposure. Total physical activity was calculated as the total time spent in working, transportation, and housework activities (collectively defined as non-recreational physical activity), as well as in recreational activities, expressed in minutes and converted to MET-min per week using activity-specific MET values.26 Participants were categorized into low (<600 MET-min/week), moderate (600–3000 MET-min/week), and high (>3000 MET-min/week) levels, corresponding to <150, 150–750, and >750 min/week of moderate physical activity, respectively.26,27 MVPA was defined as the total time spent in moderate- and vigorous-intensity activities in ≥10-min bouts. Night sleep duration was recorded on the standardized questionnaire by asking: “During your longest or nocturnal sleep period, what time do you normally go to bed and wake up (24-h format)”; it was then calculated as the average nocturnal sleep duration and expressed in h/day.28

Covariates are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. These included age; sex; urban or rural residence; smoking status; alcohol use; waist-to-hip ratio; the INTERHEART risk score (a composite measure of cardiovascular risk factor burden incorporating age, sex, smoking status, diabetes, hypertension, family history of heart disease, waist-to-hip ratio, psychosocial factors, diet, and physical activity);29 body mass index (BMI); presence of hypertension, diabetes, and baseline chronic diseases (including hepatitis or jaundice, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, tuberculosis, malaria, cancer and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV); physical impairments (whether having difficulty grasping, walking, bending, reading, seeing people, speaking/hearing, or using walking aids); and symptoms of depression.30 We adopted a validated integrated socioeconomic status (SES) indicator developed by latent class analysis (PROC LCA in SAS)31 combining education, occupation, and household wealth index.32 SES was categorized into 3 levels; the latent class membership probabilities are shown in Supplementary Table 2.

Outcome

The primary outcome was the composite of CVD events (defined as cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, stroke, and heart failure) and all-cause mortality. Secondary outcomes consisted of each individual component of the primary composite outcome. Outcomes were collected using standardized case-report forms and adjudicated centrally in Beijing by a trained clinical event committee (CEC) using established definitions described previously.33 All adjudicated events that occurred up to August 31, 2021 were included.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized overall and by categories of sitting time. Event rates were expressed per 1000 person-years. Missing covariate data were imputed 5 times using the PROC MI procedure in SAS software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and estimates were combined with Rubin’s rules. Continuous variables were imputed using regression methods, binary or ordinal variables with logistic regression, and nominal variables with the discriminant function method. Covariates were selected a priori based on prior evidence and biological plausibility.12 Associations between daily sitting time and clinical outcomes were assessed using Cox proportional hazards frailty models with a random intercept for community-level clustering. Proportional hazards assumptions were verified using log–log plots. Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, urban/rural residence, and a random intercept to account for community-level clustering. Model 2 additionally included socioeconomic status, lifestyle factors, physical activity, family history of coronary heart disease (CHD), depression, physical impairments, and baseline chronic diseases, and was considered the primary model for interpretation. Model 3 further adjusted for potential mediators (hypertension, diabetes, BMI, and hyperlipidemia) and was treated as a sensitivity analysis. Restricted cubic splines (4 knots at the 5th, 25th, 75th, and 95th percentiles) were used to examine dose–response relationships.34

Subgroup analyses were conducted based on the characteristics of this study and previous literature,14 including age (<50 vs. ≥50 years), sex (female or male), urban or rural residence, socioeconomic status (low, moderate, or high), education level (none/primary or unknown, secondary, trade, or college/university), occupation (professionals or managers, skilled workers, unskilled workers, or homemakers), household wealth index (low, moderate, high), obesity (BMI <28 kg/m² vs. ≥28 kg/m²),35 hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, depression, physical impairments, baseline chronic diseases, abdominal obesity, and the INTERHEART risk score (below vs. at or above the median). Interaction effects between sitting time and each subgroup variable were formally tested by including cross-product terms in the models. Stratified analyses were performed within each physical activity subgroup, using the 2–<4 h/day sitting time category as the reference. Joint analyses further classified participants according to combined levels of sitting time and physical activity, with the group reporting 2–<4 h/day of sitting and high physical activity serving as the reference category. Sensitivity analyses sequentially excluded participants with any physical impairments, baseline chronic diseases, events occurring within the first 2 years, or reported sitting time of 0 h/day. Associations of sitting time with outcomes were also examined separately for weekdays and weekends. For CVD outcomes, we additionally evaluated competing risks using Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models.36

Isotemporal substitution and partition models estimated the effects of reallocating time between sitting, physical activity, and sleep, including subgroup analyses by occupation (manual or non-manual work).37 Isotemporal models were based on the self-reported total time calculated as the sum of sitting, physical activity, and sleep, consistent with prior isotemporal substitution studies.14,38, 39, 40 Effects were scaled to a 30-min reallocation and reported as hazard ratios (HRs). For example, when estimating the effect of sitting time reallocation, the model included total time, physical activity, and sleep but excluded sitting time; thus, the HRs for physical activity and sleep represent the estimated hazard associated with the replacement of 30 min of sitting with these behaviors. The same modeling principle was applied to other reallocation scenarios. Further details of the isotemporal substitution and partition models are provided in Supplementary Materials. Sleep duration was modeled piecewise with a knot at 8 h based on spline results (Supplementary Fig. 1). Although isotemporal substitution is conceptually bidirectional, estimated effects may not be symmetric when exposure–response relationships are nonlinear, because substitutions are evaluated at different exposure levels along the risk curve. Statistical significance was defined as two-sided p < 0.05. Analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.) and R 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

A total of 47,931 participants were recruited for the PURE-China study. After excluding participants with missing age or sex (n = 3), missing or extreme exposure data (n = 2166), prevalent CVD at baseline (n = 3917), or no follow-up data (n = 112), a total of 41,733 participants were included in the final analyses. The participant selection process is shown in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Baseline characteristics

The mean age of participants was 50.6 ± 9.7 years (mean ± SD), and 17,367 (41.6%) were male. The median sitting time at baseline was 3.0 h/day (interquartile range (IQR): 1.7–4.6 h/day); the distribution of sitting time was right-skewed, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 3. Sitting time was longer among individuals with higher SES, increasing from a median of 2.3 h/day in the low SES group to 4.0 h/day in the high SES group. Participants with shorter sitting time were more likely to reside in rural areas, attain lower levels of education, hold fewer professional or managerial occupations, and belong to lower household wealth tertiles. In contrast, total physical activity, MVPA, walking, and non-recreational activities (working, transportation, and housework) decreased progressively with higher sitting time (Table 2). Association of sitting time and cardiovascular disease and death

As shown in Fig. 1, restricted cubic splines revealed J-shaped nonlinear associations of sitting time with the composite outcome and all-cause mortality, with the lowest risks at ∼4 h/day. For major CVD, the association was also nonlinear but without a clear J-shaped pattern (all p < 0.001). Restricted cubic spline analyses for other outcomes are presented in Supplementary Fig. 4. In multivariable-adjusted models, a J-shaped relationship was observed for most outcomes, with increased risks at both low (<2 h/day) and high (≥6 h/day) levels of sitting time compared to the reference group (2–<4 h/day). Isotemporal substitution effects of sitting time, physical activity, and sleep

Fig. 3 illustrates the isotemporal substitution effects (per 30 min) of reallocating time between sitting and various physical activities, stratified by sitting duration, in both directions. Among participants sitting ≥4 h/day, substituting sitting with MVPA, nonrecreational, or working activity was associated with a 3%–4% lower risk of the composite outcome (MVPA: HR = 0.97, 95%CI: 0.95–0.99, p = 0.006; nonrecreational: HR = 0.97, 95%CI: 0.95–0.99, p = 0.009; working: HR = 0.96, 95%CI: 0.94–0.99, p = 0.002). For all-cause mortality, replacing sitting with physical activity—excluding recreational activity—was significantly associated with a 6%–7% risk reduction. Conversely, among participants sitting <4 h/day, replacing various physical activity domains with sitting was associated with a 4%–6% lower risk of the composite outcome. For example, replacing working physical activity with sitting yielded an HR of 0.96 (95%CI: 0.94–0.98, p < 0.001). Notably, replacing prolonged sleep (≥8 h/day) with sitting was similarly associated with a decreased risk (HR = 0.94, 95%CI: 0.90–0.97, p < 0.001). Overall, isotemporal substitution patterns differed by baseline sitting duration: reallocating sitting to physical activity was associated with lower risk in the ≥4 h/day group, whereas reallocating activity or prolonged sleep to sitting was associated with lower risk in the <4 h/day group. Although effect sizes were modest at the 30-min scale, the directionality of substitution effects was consistent and biologically plausible. Patterns for CVD mortality were similar to those observed for the composite outcome, whereas few statistically significant associations were detected for myocardial infarction, stroke, or heart failure (Supplementary Tables 8 and 9). Discussion

In this large prospective cohort study of 41,733 participants from 12 provinces across western, central, and eastern China, we found a J-shaped dose–response association between sitting time and the risks of major CVD and all-cause mortality with the lowest risk observed at approximately 4 h of sitting per day. Isotemporal substitution models supported these findings. Among individuals sitting ≥4 h/day, reallocating sitting time to physical activity, particularly MVPA or work-related activity, was associated with a lower risk of the composite outcome and all-cause mortality. Conversely, among individuals sitting <4 h/day, replacing physical activity or prolonged sleep (≥8 h/day) with sitting time was also associated with lower risks of both all-cause mortality and cardiovascular outcomes.

Our finding that the observed positive association between prolonged sitting and increased health risks aligns with previous studies, particularly for sitting durations ≥8 h/day.8,17,41,42 Interestingly, our study also found that very low sitting time (<2 h/day) was associated with an elevated risk of all-cause mortality, although the association with major CVD was not statistically significant. These findings challenge the conventional view that “less sitting is always better” and align with the concept of a “sitting paradox”. Similar J- or U-shaped associations have been reported previously.41,43,44 For example, a pooled analysis of nearly 100,000 participants from 3 Chinese cohorts also found a non-linear association between sitting time and all-cause mortality, using <6 h/day as the reference.4 Similarly, findings from the Southern Community Cohort Study indicated that Black participants in the second sitting quartile had slightly lower cardiovascular mortality than those in the lowest quartile, whereas White participants showed a more linear trend.45

Among individuals with very low sedentary exposure (<4 h/day), reallocating time from physical activity or prolonged sleep to sitting was unexpectedly associated with lower mortality risk. To our knowledge, this is the first evidence suggesting that increasing sitting time—when substituted for activity or prolonged sleep—may provide benefits in populations with extremely low baseline levels of sitting, especially in participants with manual work. In our cohort, over 60% of individuals sitting less than 2 h per day were employed in physically demanding occupations (e.g., agriculture, construction), compared with only ∼23% of those sitting ≥8 h/day, indicating that very low sitting clustered among individuals with physically demanding jobs. This pattern echoes the so-called “physical activity paradox”, whereby high levels of work-related physical activity do not always confer the same health advantages as leisure-time activity and may even increase cardiovascular risk, particularly in necessity-based contexts.46,47 Supporting this view, prior studies, including analyses from the China Kadoorie Biobank, have shown that the health effects of physically demanding occupations vary by education and socioeconomic status, with greater work-related activity sometimes associated with higher cardiovascular risk, while leisure-time activity remains protective.48,49 Recent work has distinguished “necessity-based” activity (occupational or transport-related, common in low- and middle-income countries) from “choice-based” activity (recreational exercise). Individuals in necessity-based contexts may already exceed World Health Organization activity recommendations, often at the cost of limited recovery opportunities.50, 51, 52 For such groups, modest increases in sitting—when they replace periods of heavy physical exertion—may allow essential physiological recovery and reduce short-term risk. We therefore propose a “sitting paradox” to capture this context-dependent phenomenon, while emphasizing the need for further longitudinal and mechanistic studies to distinguish recovery-oriented sitting from passive sedentary leisure and to clarify the long-term health implications of each. Importantly, these findings should not be interpreted as advocating increased sitting time in the general population, but rather as suggesting that, in certain subgroups with very low baseline sitting and high occupational physical activity, adequate rest—including sitting—may be beneficial.

We further found that reallocating sitting time to physical activity was significantly associated with lower all-cause mortality among individuals with long sitting time. This finding is consistent with prior studies, including 2 large Chinese cohorts18,53 and international evidence,19,46 which have consistently demonstrated the cardiovascular benefits of replacing sedentary time with physical activity. These findings are particularly relevant given the global shift toward more sedentary lifestyles, driven by increased prevalence of desk-based occupations and prolonged screen time. Together, these results support recommendations from the World Health Organization’s 2020 global guidelines and the Healthy China 2030 Action Plan on sedentary behavior, both of which emphasize reducing sitting time and increasing physical activity. They also reinforce the message that physical activity levels above current recommendations can help offset the health risks associated with prolonged sitting, and that sedentary individuals may benefit substantially from replacing sitting time with active behaviors.3,54

Subgroup analysis in our study revealed a more evident increase in CVD risk among low-sitting individuals in urban and manual worker populations compared to rural and non-manual worker populations. This is consistent with findings from 2 other studies conducted exclusively in rural and peri‑urban Chinese populations, which found linear associations between sitting time and CVD risk.5,13 One explanation is that urban individuals with very low sitting time are often female skilled workers or those with low or moderate socioeconomic status, whose occupations involve prolonged physical labor without sufficient opportunities for rest. In such contexts, sitting may function less as a discretionary sedentary behavior and more as a necessary form of rest. Additionally, not all sedentary activities are inherently harmful. Behaviors such as reading or social engagement may offer cognitive and emotional benefits.55 Moreover, low sitting time does not necessarily exclude prolonged standing, which has similarly been associated with adverse health outcomes.56 These findings suggest that in subpopulations with high occupational physical demands—particularly urban manual laborers—sitting may play a restorative role and should not be viewed uniformly as a risk factor. This highlights the importance of more nuanced, context-specific public health guidance on sedentary behavior.

This study has several notable strengths. Our data were derived from a substudy of the international PURE cohort, which features high follow-up rates and rigorous measurement of baseline variables, as well as standardized adjudication of clinical outcomes. We employed a comprehensive analytic approach that included both conventional association models and isotemporal substitution analyses. Importantly, we considered bidirectional time reallocations among sitting time, physical activity, and sleep, and their respective associations with cardiovascular outcomes and all-cause mortality. To our knowledge, this is the first study to apply threshold-based stratification of sedentary time within isotemporal substitution models, providing a more nuanced understanding of its health implications, especially for low- and middle-income countries.

However, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, measurement error in self-reported IPAQ variables is inevitable. Although IPAQ underestimates total sedentary time by ∼2.5 h compared with accelerometry, it may better capture modifiable, context-specific sitting behaviors (e.g., desk work, commuting, social leisure), which are most relevant for targeting behavior change. Device-based measures are more objective but costly and may miss such contextual information.57 Future studies combining device-based and self-reported data may improve accuracy and behavioral insight. Second, exposures—including physical activity, sleep, and sitting time—were only measured at baseline and may have changed over time, leading to exposure misclassification and potential attenuation of associations. Third, residual confounding remains possible, particularly in individuals with very low physical activity or prolonged sitting, where underlying health conditions may influence both behavior and mortality risk. Despite excluding those with major chronic diseases at baseline and adjusting for multiple confounders, reverse causation cannot be entirely excluded. In the ≥8 h/day sitting category, the smaller sample size also resulted in reduced precision for risk estimates. Finally, the health impact analysis is based on modeled substitution scenarios that may not fully reflect real-world behavior change or feasibility. These results should therefore be viewed as hypothetical effects under idealized assumptions rather than direct causal estimates.

Conclusion

This study highlights the context-dependent health effects of sitting, introducing the concept of the “sitting paradox”. Public health guidance should move beyond the simple message that “less sitting is always better” and instead consider all types of activity throughout the day, encouraging reduced sitting for sedentary individuals while recognizing that moderate sitting may be beneficial for those with physically demanding routines. Model-based substitution analyses provide a framework for future interventions aimed at reallocating time between sitting, physical activity, and sleep to reduce premature mortality and cardiovascular risk.
