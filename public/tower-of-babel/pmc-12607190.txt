Integrated genomic and structural dissection of melanism in Indian leopard (Panthera pardus fusca)
Authors: Avijit Ghosh, Sachin Kumar, Paresh Porob, Mukesh Thakur, Malay Shukla, Satish Kumar, Sweta Nidhi, Abhishek Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMelanism in wild felids represents an intriguing evolutionary trait, potentially advantageous under specific ecological conditions, but the genetic basis of this phenotype remains unclear in many populations. Here, we explored melanism in the Indian leopard (Panthera pardus fusca) by integrating whole-genome sequencing with evolutionary and structural analyses.ResultsWe identified a single-point mutation (p.C117F) in the agouti signalling protein (ASIP) gene. Evolutionary analyses indicated signs of positive selection acting on the ASIP locus, while codon usage patterns revealed no impairment in translational efficiency. Computational predictions (SIFT, PROVEAN) classified the mutation as functionally damaging. Structural modelling combined with molecular dynamics simulations revealed significant disruptions in the stability, helical content, and receptor-binding affinity of the mutated ASIP protein compared to the wild type. The mutation notably diminished ASIP’s inhibitory interaction with melanocortin-1 receptor (MC1R), which is crucial for melanogenesis regulation. Machine-learning models differentiated between mutant and wild-type protein structures by analysing molecular features.ConclusionsOur results support the conclusion that the p.C117F mutation significantly contributes to melanism in Indian leopards, shedding new light on this trait’s molecular underpinnings and evolutionary history. This study identifies the p.C117F mutation as a key driver of melanism in leopards of the Indian subcontinent, providing mechanistic insight into the evolution of complex phenotypes in non-model species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12227-z.Keywords: Melanism, Leopard, ASIP gene, Molecular dynamics, Protein interaction, In-silico protein modelling, Genomics, Machine learning

Background

Melanism, a prominent and visually distinct biological phenomenon characterized by the increased deposition of the dark pigment melanin, results in individuals with an entirely dark or black coat colour [1]. This unique trait has evolved independently across various taxa, prominently observed among mammals such as felids, canids, rodents, and primates [1]. Within the felid family, melanism is especially notable in species such as jaguars (Panthera onca), leopards (Panthera pardus), and even tigers (Panthera tigris), wherein individuals exhibiting this phenotype are commonly referred to as ‘Black Panthers’ [2–4]. In leopards, melanism has been recorded within specific geographical regions, suggesting possible adaptive significance related to ecological factors like habitat and climate conditions [5]. Notably, melanistic leopards exhibit a distribution bias, being predominantly observed in densely forested regions in Southeast Asia, including the biodiversity-rich Western Ghats of India and the wet-zone forests of Sri Lanka [6–9]. Despite widespread interest, comprehensive molecular characterization of melanism in Indian leopards remains limited, highlighting a significant gap in our understanding of the evolutionary mechanisms driving this trait.

At the molecular level, melanism generally arises from alterations in the melanocortin signalling pathway, particularly in the melanocortin 1 receptor (MC1R) and Agouti Signalling Protein (ASIP) genes [2, 5]. ASIP encodes a paracrine antagonist of MC1R that regulates the balance between eumelanin (black/brown) and pheomelanin (yellow/red) production [3, 10]. Mutations in either ASIP or MC1R can enhance melanocortin signalling, promoting increased eumelanin synthesis and the manifestation of melanism. In felids, melanism often arises through loss-of-function mutations in ASIP or gain-of-function mutations in MC1R. In leopards, melanism has been linked to a premature stop-gain mutation (c.333 C >A; p.C111*) [2], while a more recent study identified a missense variant (c.353 C >A; p.C113F) in Sri Lankan leopards [11]. Both variants localize to the cysteine-rich domain of exon 4, which is critical for MC1R binding. On the other hand, melanism is predominantly caused by a specific mutation in MC1R in jaguars, significantly enhancing receptor activity [5]. In canids, for instance, melanism is controlled by the K locus, corresponding to a mutation in the β-defensin (CBD103) gene, which interacts epistatically with MC1R to promote eumelanin production [12]. Further illustrating the complexity of melanism, a recent study by Sagar et al. (2021) [13] demonstrated novel genetic evidence for melanistic striping in tigers from Odisha, India, pinpointing mutations in the Transmembrane Aminopeptidase Q (Taqpep) gene. Such studies underscore the intricate nature of melanistic phenotypes and emphasize the necessity of integrated genomic and proteomic approaches to unravel their genetic basis. Despite these insights, melanism’s molecular drivers and functional consequences in Indian leopards have not been comprehensively characterized. Notably, the population of melanistic leopards from the Western Ghats of India, an ecologically distinct and biodiversity-rich region, remains largely unexplored from a genetic and evolutionary point. Detailed investigation of these leopards is crucial for understanding melanism’s molecular basis and elucidating its adaptive significance, evolutionary trajectory, and potential conservation implications.

To address this significant gap, we conducted whole-genome sequencing of a melanistic leopard from Goa, situated in the Western Ghats of India. Our genomic analyses identified a critical nonsynonymous mutation (p.C117F) within the ASIP gene, previously associated with melanism in Sri Lankan leopard populations [11]. Subsequent proteomic and computational modelling confirmed the functional implications of this mutation, demonstrating its structural and functional effects on protein stability and receptor interaction. Additionally, we utilized machine learning (ML) techniques to classify and validate this molecular alteration using several features. This comprehensive study provides valuable insights into melanism’s genetic architecture, functional implications, and evolutionary context in Indian leopards. By integrating genomics, proteomics, and computational methodologies, our research significantly advances the understanding of melanism as an adaptive evolutionary phenomenon, contributing critically to broader conservation genetics and evolutionary biology frameworks (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Sample collection and DNA extraction

Blood sample from a melanistic black leopard (Panthera pardus fusca) in Goa, Western Ghats, was generously provided by the Goa Forest Department for research purposes. It was venturing into a human settlement of Balli village in South Goa. The Forest Department rescued the animal on 1 st April 2023 to the Bondla Wildlife Sanctuary, Goa, India (15.44028 N, 74.10639 E) (Fig. 2A and B) followed by health examination and the sample collection. The blood sample was collected in an EDTA tube and stored on ice in a transport box during transportation to the laboratory at the Centre for Wildlife Forensics, National Forensic Sciences University, Goa, India. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, genomic DNA was isolated using a QIAGEN DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany).

A Map showing the sampling location of this study, along with other presence locations of melanistic and non-melanistic leopards within its distribution range. B Photograph of the captured individual of this study on the weighing machine before release

Whole genome sequencing and consensus sequence extraction

High-throughput sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using 150 bp paired-end reads. Libraries were constructed using the Illumina TruSeq DNA PCR-Free Library Preparation Kit following the manufacturer’s protocols. The sequencing generated a coverage of approximately 30x depth. Six sequences of P. pardus were downloaded from the ENA database (Table S1) to represent a broad geographical range across the leopard’s distribution and to include both wild-type and known melanistic genotypes from different subspecies. Raw sequencing data were processed and quality-checked using FastQC [14] and trimmed using fastp [15]. The cleaned reads were aligned to the leopard reference genome (Panthera pardus) (GenBank accession number: GCA_024362965.1) using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA-MEM) [16]. After removing duplicate reads, consensus sequences of melanism-related genes (ASIP, MC1R, DCT, EGLN1, OCA2, PCLB4, PMEL and TYRP1) were extracted by their coordinates in the reference genome using the command ‘samtools consensus --min-MQ 30 --min-BQ 20 --min-depth 3’ [17]. As the MC1R gene was not annotated in the reference genome of the P. pardus, we mapped the MC1R gene sequence from the Felis catus genome (GenBank accession number: GCA_018350175.1) with the reference genome of P. pardus to extract the coordinates of the MC1R ortholog of the P. pardus using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA-MEM) [16].

Molecular evolutionary analysis

To assess the selection pressures acting on the ASIP gene, codon-aligned nucleotide sequences from 7 wild type and two melanistic P. pardus individuals, including the melanistic leopard, were analyzed using the SLAC (Single-Likelihood Ancestor Counting) method implemented on the Datamonkey web server (www.datamonkey.org) [18]. Alignment was performed at the codon level to preserve reading frame integrity. SLAC estimates nonsynonymous (dN) and synonymous (dS) substitution rates at each codon site across a phylogenetic tree inferred under the GTR nucleotide substitution model. Statistical significance was assessed at a p-value threshold of 0.1. Sites with dN >dS and statistically significant p-values were considered to be under positive (diversifying) selection, while sites with dN < dS were considered under purifying selection.

Codon usage bias analysis

Codon usage bias in the ASIP gene was analyzed based on 13 codon-aligned full-length coding sequences using MEGA X [19]. The standard genetic code was used to calculate the frequency of each codon, and Relative Synonymous Codon Usage (RSCU) values were computed [20]. An RSCU value >1 indicates preferential usage, while values < 1 denote rare codons. Particular attention was paid to codon usage at and around the identified mutation site. Average codon frequencies were calculated across all sequences.

Variant effect prediction

Two computational tools were used to assess the functional impact of the identified p.C117F mutation in the ASIP gene: SIFT v4 (Sorting Intolerant From Tolerant) and PROVEAN v1.1.5 (Protein Variation Effect Analyzer) [21, 22]. The wild-type ASIP protein sequence was submitted along with the specified mutation to each server using default parameters. SIFT predicts the effect of amino acid substitutions based on sequence homology and evolutionary conservation, while PROVEAN evaluates the impact of protein variation based on sequence-based alignment scores. Mutations with a SIFT score below 0.05 or a PROVEAN score of ≤ −2.5 were considered deleterious.

Modelling, docking and molecular dynamics simulation

Three-dimensional structural models of wild-type and mutant ASIP proteins and the MC1R protein were constructed using AlphaFold2 [23]. The predicted 3D structures of wild-type and mutant ASIP proteins were validated using PROCHECK v.3.5.4 [24], which analyzes stereochemical quality by assessing Ramachandran plot statistics and backbone geometry. Only models with more than 90% residues in the favoured regions were accepted. For evaluating secondary structural composition, the STRIDE algorithm [25] was employed. Protein-protein docking between ASIP and MC1R proteins was performed using HADDOCK v2.4 [26]. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the wild and mutant ASIP proteins were carried out using GROMACS 2023.3 [27]. Each protein model was placed in a dodecahedral box with a minimum margin of 1.0 nm from the protein surface and solvated using the TIP3P water model. The AMBER99SB-ILDN force field was employed for protein topology generation. System neutrality was achieved by adding counterions, and physiological salt conditions (0.15 M NaCl) were maintained. Energy minimization was conducted using the steepest descent algorithm until convergence (maximum force < 1000 kJ/mol/nm). Equilibration was carried out in two steps: (i) NVT ensemble for 100 ps using the velocity-rescaling thermostat at 300 K, and (ii) NPT ensemble for 100 ps using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat at 1 bar. The MD simulations were then run for 100 ns with a 2-fs time step under periodic boundary conditions. The LINCS algorithm was applied to constrain all bonds involving hydrogen atoms. Long-range electrostatics were treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method with a cutoff of 1.2 nm for both Coulomb and van der Waals interactions. Trajectories were analyzed for root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), principal component analysis (PCA), hydrogen bond analysis and secondary structural analysis.

MM-GBSA binding free energy calculation

Binding free energy calculations were conducted on docked complexes of ASIP and MC1R using the HADDOCK v2.4 web server employing the Molecular Mechanics Generalized Born Surface Area (MM-GBSA) method [28]. Single snapshot binding free energy estimates were obtained for both wild-type and mutant complexes to evaluate the impact of the identified mutation on binding affinity and stability.

Interaction analysis using ligPlot+

Protein-ligand and protein-protein interactions between ASIP and MC1R were further analyzed using LigPlot + v2.2.4 [29]. Docked complexes generated from HADDOCK were used as input in PDB format. LigPlot + was employed to visualize and identify hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts at the binding interface of wild-type and mutant ASIP–MC1R complexes. The software was run using default settings, and the resulting interaction maps were manually curated to highlight key differences in contact residues between the two complexes.

Protein stability prediction and evolutionary conservation analysis

Protein stability changes due to the identified mutation were predicted using DUET, integrating mCSM and SDM methodologies [30]. Evolutionary conservation of the mutated residue was analyzed using the ConSurf server, highlighting the conservation profile of amino acids in ASIP across multiple species [31].

Machine learning analysis

Machine learning (ML) analyses were performed using structural and dynamic features extracted from MD simulations of wild-type and mutant ASIP proteins. The dataset consisted of 7 features × 200 data points (features included RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration, SASA, secondary structure content, hydrogen bond counts, and PCA eigenvalues). To minimize the risk of overfitting, we implemented 5-fold cross-validation. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC metrics. Among the models tested (Random Forest, Logistic Regression, and XGBoost) [32, 33], Random Forest consistently yielded the best performance, with an accuracy of 92.5%, precision of 91.8%, recall of 93.2%, F1-score of 92.4%, and ROC-AUC of 0.96. Logistic Regression achieved an accuracy of 84.1% and ROC-AUC of 0.88, while XGBoost performed moderately with an accuracy of 80.0% and ROC-AUC of 0.83.

Gene interaction and pathway analysis

GeneMANIA was utilized to assess potential interactions and pathways involving ASIP, MC1R, and other related genes. This helped elucidate the broader molecular interaction landscape and potential functional implications of identified mutations [34].

Results

Identification of ASIP mutation in melanistic leopard

Whole genome sequencing of the melanistic leopard from Goa, India, achieved approximately 30x coverage, facilitating a comprehensive variant analysis against the reference leopard genome (Panthera pardus; GCA_001857705.1). A critical nonsynonymous single-nucleotide variant (SNV) was identified in the ASIP gene (c.350G >T), resulting in a missense mutation from cysteine to phenylalanine (c.350G >T; p.C117F) in exon 4 of the ASIP gene (Table 1; Fig S1). This mutation is homologous to the one previously identified in melanistic leopards from Sri Lanka (C113F) [11], as the amino substitution (C >F) is located in the 6th Cysteine residue of the Cysteine-rich domain of exon 4 of the ASIP gene.

Selection pressure analysis

Selection pressure analysis of the ASIP gene revealed evidence of pervasive positive selection at p.C117F(p < 0.1), as determined by the SLAC method. No sites exhibited significant purifying selection. This finding suggests that portions of the ASIP gene, including potentially functionally critical residues, have undergone adaptive evolutionary changes. Identifying positive selection in the coding region supports the hypothesis that melanism in leopards may have arisen through adaptive molecular evolution rather than being a consequence of neutral drift. These results reinforce the functional and evolutionary significance of the p.C117F mutation identified in the melanistic Indian leopard (Table 1).

Codon usage bias in ASIP gene

Codon usage analysis of 13 ASIP coding sequences from Panthera pardus revealed significant bias at specific synonymous sites. The mutation site at codon 117 involves a change from cysteine (encoded by UGC, RSCU = 2.00) to phenylalanine (encoded by UUC, RSCU = 2.00), both of which are the most preferred codons for their respective amino acids within the ASIP gene (Table S2). This suggests the amino acid substitution occurred without imposing a codon-level translational disadvantage. The retention of high RSCU at the mutation site supports the hypothesis that the melanistic mutation is selectively neutral or adaptive at the translational level.

Prediction of functional impact

Variant effect prediction analysis revealed that the p.C117F substitution in the ASIP protein is likely deleterious to its function. The mutation yielded a SIFT score of 0.000, indicating a highly conserved and functionally important residue, and a PROVEAN score of −9.422, far below the threshold for neutrality. These results provide strong computational support that the mutation impairs ASIP function, aligning with structural, dynamic, and interaction analyses. Collectively, these findings strengthen the hypothesis that this mutation contributes to the melanistic phenotype observed in the Indian black panther.

Protein structure and stability analysis

Structural modelling using AlphaFold2 revealed significant structural differences between wild-type and mutant ASIP proteins (Fig. 3A and B). The substitution (p.C117F) notably altered protein conformation near the critical binding domain, potentially influencing the ASIP-MC1R interaction. Ramachandran plot analysis via PROCHECK indicated high stereochemical quality in both wild-type and mutant ASIP structures, with 93.2% and 91.5% of residues in favoured regions, respectively (Fig S2). The STRIDE analysis revealed a modest reduction in α-helical content in the mutant ASIP compared to the wild-type, indicating conformational instability post-mutation (Table S3).

A Predicted 3D structure of wild-type ASIP. B Mutant ASIP (C117F) structure. C ConSurf conservation map highlighting the highly conserved cysteine-rich domain

Comparative molecular dynamics simulations (100 ns) were conducted for wild-type and mutant ASIP proteins to investigate the structural consequences of the p.C117F mutation. The mutant ASIP protein exhibited a higher average RMSD (~ 3.1 nm) and greater residue fluctuations (RMSF > 2.0 nm at several sites), indicating increased structural instability relative to the wild-type (RMSD ~ 2.4 nm) (Fig. 4). The Rg was also elevated in the mutant, reflecting reduced compactness and a more flexible conformation. A reduction in α-helical content and a consistent increase in solvent-accessible surface area in the mutant suggests a partial loss of structural integrity (Fig. 5; Table 2). Hydrogen bond analysis showed a mild decline in intramolecular H-bonds in the mutant, potentially contributing to conformational flexibility. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) further revealed greater conformational sampling in the mutant protein (Fig. 5; Table 3). These results suggest that the p.C117F substitution significantly alters the structural and dynamic landscape of ASIP, potentially impairing its stability and interaction efficiency (Fig. 4).

Protein-Protein interaction and MM-GBSA analysis

Docking studies using HADDOCK 2.4 predicted a notable difference in the binding mode of the ASIP-MC1R complexes between wild and mutant forms (Fig S3). The wild-type complex displayed stronger and more stable interactions, with MM-GBSA binding free energy estimated at − 9.9 kcal/mol, compared to − 9.5 kcal/mol for the mutant complex (Table 4). This difference indicates reduced binding affinity due to the mutation, supporting a hypothesis of decreased functional interaction between ASIP and MC1R in melanistic individuals (Fig. 6; Table 5).

Interaction interface analysis

Detailed interaction mapping using LigPlot + revealed distinct binding profiles for the wild-type and mutant ASIP–MC1R complexes. In the wild-type complex, hydrogen bonds were observed between ASIP residues Phe122, Arg123, and Lys104 with MC1R residues Cys199, Gly178, Gly177, and Thr176. Hydrophobic interactions were extensive, involving residues such as Phe121, Leu21, and Cys117 on ASIP. In contrast, the mutant complex showed a marked reduction in hydrogen bonding, limited to Lys104 and Pro106 of ASIP interacting with Gly177 of MC1R (Fig. 6; Table S4). Although hydrophobic contacts were still present in the mutant, the altered interaction landscape suggests compromised binding affinity, consistent with MM-GBSA energy estimates and reduced structural stability.

Protein stability and evolutionary conservation analysis

Protein stability analysis using DUET indicated that the mutation significantly destabilizes the ASIP protein, with a predicted ΔΔG of − 0.679 kcal/mol. Additionally, evolutionary conservation analysis using the ConSurf server revealed that residue Cys117 is highly conserved and structurally important across diverse mammalian species, further emphasizing the potential functional impact of its substitution (Fig. 3).

Machine learning analysis

Machine learning analyses clearly distinguish between wild-type and mutant proteins. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) distinctly separated the two forms based on structural and dynamic features (Fig. 7A). Random Forest and XGBoost classifiers identified the radius of gyration, helicity, and RMSF as crucial discriminative features, reinforcing the biological significance of the observed mutation in influencing structural and functional properties (Fig. 7B; Table S5 & S6).

Gene interaction and pathway analysis

GeneMANIA analysis identified ASIP and MC1R as central components of the melanocortin signalling pathway, interacting with genes involved in pigmentation and melanogenesis pathways (Fig S4). This comprehensive interaction network analysis supported the hypothesis that mutations in ASIP significantly impact melanogenesis, potentially driving melanistic phenotypes observed in the studied leopard population. Collectively, these findings provide robust genetic, structural, functional, and computational evidence supporting the role of the identified ASIP mutation in melanism in Indian leopards. The data underscore the importance of this mutation in altering protein stability, interaction dynamics, and evolutionary significance.

Discussion

Melanism in felids presents an intriguing evolutionary trait shaped by genetic, ecological, and selective pressures. In this study, we explored the molecular underpinnings of melanism in the Indian black panther (Panthera pardus fusca), focusing on a missense mutation p.C117F in the ASIP gene. Through a combination of genomic, structural, evolutionary, and computational analyses, our findings provide a comprehensive perspective on how a single-point mutation may drive a visible and evolutionarily relevant phenotype.

Identifying the p.C117F substitution through whole genome sequencing in P. p. fusca and its previous documentation in melanistic Sri Lankan leopards (P. p. kotiya) raises the question of a shared ancestry of melanism in both subspecies. This suggests that the mutation predates the geographical isolation of these populations and was retained through positive selection. Such an interpretation aligns with previous population histories and geographic isolation patterns between South India and Sri Lanka. The discovery of p.C117F adds to a growing body of evidence implicating exon 4 of ASIP in melanism across leopard populations. Prior studies identified a nonsense variant (p.C111*) and a missense variant (p.C113F, homologous to p.C117F of this study) within the same domain [2, 11]. A mutation in the same domain (c.C384G; p.C128W) also causes melanism in Asian golden cat (Pardofelis temminckii) [2]. These observations suggest that any Cysteine substitution in Cysteine-rich domain can lead to a melanistic phenotype even in different species and subspecies. The recurrent emergence of function-impairing mutations in this exon suggests its functional sensitivity and possible adaptive importance in specific ecological contexts. Given the clustering of mutations within a ~ 20 amino acid stretch of the cysteine-rich domain, evolutionary constraint and positive selection may jointly shape this locus in melanistic leopards.

A codon under positive selection in the ASIP gene, as identified by the SLAC method, further supports the hypothesis that this mutation may confer an adaptive advantage. The role of melanism in camouflage within dense forest habitats or thermoregulation in tropical climates might contribute to its persistence. Importantly, codon usage bias analysis indicated that the codons encoding cysteine and phenylalanine at position 117 were most frequently used, suggesting that the mutation did not disrupt translational efficiency. This absence of codon-level disadvantage could have facilitated the fixation or maintenance of the mutation in specific leopard lineages. While evolutionary models support the selection and retention of the mutation, structural analyses helped clarify its impact at the protein level. In silico prediction tools, including SIFT and PROVEAN, classified the mutation as deleterious, potentially impairing the normal function of the ASIP protein. The mutated residue lies within a highly conserved region, as shown by ConSurf analysis, highlighting its functional significance. DUET further predicted a decrease in protein stability due to the mutation, a feature that often correlates with functional compromise.

Molecular docking analysis between ASIP and MC1R revealed reduced binding affinity in the mutant complex, as assessed by MM-GBSA calculations. Since ASIP acts as an inverse agonist of MC1R, reduced binding may result in disinhibition of the receptor and increased eumelanin production, phenotypically observed as melanism. These predictions were reinforced by 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations, where the mutant ASIP protein showed increased RMSD, RMSF, and SASA values compared to the wild-type, suggesting greater conformational flexibility, decreased compactness, and greater dynamic variation. Secondary structure analysis indicated a reduction in α-helical content and alteration in hydrogen bonding networks in the mutant, further supporting a change in structural integrity. The simulation trajectories’ Principal Component Analysis (PCA) demonstrated a broader conformational landscape for the mutant, with the eigenvalue spectrum confirming greater dynamic variation. These changes imply that the mutation has systemic effects on protein behaviour, not just localized disruption.

Using machine learning models, including Random Forest and XGBoost, added a quantitative layer to this analysis. By training on structural and energetic features, these models not only classified the mutant and wild-type forms with high accuracy but also identified critical parameters such as radius of gyration and secondary structure composition as primary discriminators. This integrative approach enhances the objectivity and reproducibility of the findings. Gene interaction and pathway analyses using GeneMANIA contextualized ASIP and MC1R within a broader melanogenesis framework, connecting them with known pigmentation genes such as TYR and DCT. These network-level insights suggest that the mutation’s effects are not isolated but may propagate across the pigmentation regulatory network.

This study demonstrates how a single point mutation can influence molecular evolution, protein structure and function, and ultimately, phenotype. The p.C117F substitution in ASIP appears to be a pivotal mutation underlying melanism in leopards of the Indian subcontinent and Sri Lanka. By combining traditional genomics with advanced structural biology and AI-driven analytics, we provide a model for dissecting complex traits in non-model, conservation-relevant species. Future studies involving broader sampling and functional assays could further validate this mutation’s mechanistic implications and adaptive significance. Though the causative mutation of the melanism is the same as that found in the melanistic leopard of Sri Lanka, there may be a few other mutations responsible for melanism among all the subspecies of P. pardus. Further, this study involves in-silico prediction of protein interaction of melanin regulation pathway that require confirmation through in-vitro testing. Therefore, studying several melanistic individuals across species and geographic region may show the bigger picture of the mechanism of melanin synthesis and regulation in animal kingdom.

Conclusion

This study provides critical insights into the genetic, structural, and evolutionary mechanisms underlying melanism in Indian leopards (Panthera pardus). Through comprehensive genomic sequencing, we identified a significant nonsynonymous mutation (p.C117F) in the ASIP gene, shared by melanistic leopards from geographically distinct populations. Structural modelling, molecular dynamics simulations, and advanced computational analyses substantially impacted protein stability and functional interactions, reducing the affinity between ASIP and MC1R proteins. This mutation’s evolutionary conservation and destabilizing nature highlight its functional importance and potential adaptive significance. These findings substantially advance our understanding of melanism, emphasising its role as a complex adaptive trait driven by localised ecological pressures. Future research should focus on broader population-level studies, ecological assessments, and identification of additional molecular factors influencing melanistic phenotypes, thereby contributing to wildlife conservation strategies and evolutionary biology frameworks.
