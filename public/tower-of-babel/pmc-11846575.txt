Total Metacarpal Reconstruction with Fascia-Wrapped Fibular Graft Following Ewing Tumor Resection in a Growing Patient
Authors: Samer Abdel Al, Muhamad A Al-Qawasmi, Muntaser Abu Shokor, Hamza M Khalaf, Mohammad A Alattar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractEwing tumor, a rare bone tumor, exceptionally affects the hand. We report a unique case of this tumor in the fourth metacarpal of a young patient. Reconstructing a digit after complete metacarpal removal because of sarcoma resection is a complex challenge, especially in growing individuals. We employed a novel technique involving a nonvascularized fibular autograft wrapped in fascia and intricate soft tissue reconstruction for proximal phalanx growth. The patient’s function remained satisfactory for more than 2-year follow-up.Key words: Case report, Ewing tumor, Fibula graft, Metacarpals, Reconstructive surgery, Wide local excision

Ewing sarcoma is the second most common primary malignant bone tumor in children, accounting for 3% of all pediatric malignancies and 10% of all primary malignant bone tumors. It is mainly diagnosed in the second decade of life, although 20%–30% of cases are diagnosed in the first decade.1

Radiographic evaluation commonly reveals a large destructive lesion in the diaphysis or metaphysis characterized by an ill-defined, invasive, moth-eaten pattern. Diagnosis is confirmed through a biopsy showing monotonous small round blue cells with prominent nuclei.

This tumor is recognized for its aggressive nature, demanding swift and comprehensive medical interventions, often involving a combination of surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy. Despite these therapeutic efforts, approximately 30%–40% of cases experience metastases or local recurrence.2

Surgical resection stands as a primary therapeutic approach, encompassing either limb salvage or amputation. In recent years, there has been an exploration into the use of fibular bone grafts for postoncologic reconstruction of long bones.3, 4, 5

Reconstruction following resection becomes challenging in pediatric cases given the need to factor in ongoing growth and joint functionality. Moreover, addressing the resection and reconstruction of the short bones of the hands poses heightened complexities, as evidenced by various case reports.6

This case report highlights a viable reconstructive option for an extremely rare case of Ewing sarcoma involving the hand.

Case Report

The patient is a 12-year-old left-handed girl with non-metastatic Ewing tumor of the left fourth metacarpal. She received six cycles treatment per interval compressed protocol for Ewing tumor. The initial plan was radical resection of the involved metacarpal by ray amputation of the fourth digit, but the family came to our center to explore other options. The patient’s parents provided informed consent and was conducted following the World Medical Association's Code of Ethics (Declaration of Helsinki).

On physical examination, she had been having a palpable, painful swelling on the dorsum of the hand, associated with limited fourth metacarpophalangeal joint range of motion.

The reviewed histopathology had features consistent with Ewing sarcoma. Fluorescent in situ hybridization results for EWSR1 gene rearrangement is positive in 70% of the interphases.

The magnetic resonance imaging scan of the upper extremity showed a treatment effect with interval regression of the extraosseous soft tissue component of the fourth metacarpal, measuring 1.9 cm (AP) × 2 (LR) cm compared with 2.7 × 2.4 cm in the initial scans.

The computed tomography scan of the hand with contrast showed a destructive mass involving the fourth metacarpal, focally involving the lateral aspect of the left fourth metacarpal base.

The case was discussed at the Sarcoma Multidisciplinary Clinic, and the plan was to salvage the digit following radical resection of the fourth metacarpal.

Intraoperative procedure

A curvilinear longitudinal incision was used over the dorsal aspect of the involved bone, raising both radial and ulnar flaps. We continued dissection until we reached the extensor digitorum tendons, which were retracted and preserved to expose the tumor mass (Fig. 1).

Intraoperative findings of the bone mass involving the fourth metacarpal.

Furthermore, meticulous dissection started distally from the metacarpophalangeal joint where the sagittal band was preserved by pending the joint and retracting it more distally to expose the extensor expansion and joint capsule, which were opened and tagged for later repair. Dissection continued proximally to the carpometacarpal joint where both volar and dorsal ligaments were cut to expose the joint capsule. We performed an en-bloc resection of the fourth metacarpal while preserving most of the lumbrical. Most of the interosseous muscles, other than the dorsal and the volar muscle attachments, were left on the tumor as our margin. In addition, we were able to preserve the metacarpal neurovascular bundle, along with flexor tendons, palmar plate, and the maximum length possible for the slings of the deep transverse metacarpal ligament to allow later reconstruction to provide stability of the new head. The resected specimen consisted of the metacarpal measuring 5 × 2.5 × 2 cm (Fig. 2).

The fourth metacarpal mass after wide local excision shows its dimension.

The left nonvascularized fibular autograft was then harvested using a lateral approach. A 6 cm section of bone was harvested accompanied by its lateral muscle compartment fascia. Consequently, we trimmed the graft edges distally in a circular fashion as the new head to articulate with the proximal phalanx and in a transverse fashion proximally to allow arthrodesis with the carpal (Fig. 3).

Harvested fibula autograft and its lateral fascia after trimming.

Accordingly, multiple holes were drilled in the fibula to anchor the wrapped facia and hold it in place. A retrograde partially threaded 2.5 mm (0.098 in) K-wire was inserted in the site of the resected fourth metacarpal and fixed to the Hamate bone under the C-arm to act as a scaffold for our graft. Finally, the new fascia-wrapped nonvascularized fibular autograft was slid over the K-wire, and the protruding distal part of the K-wire was cut (Fig 4).

C-arm shows the intraoperative steps for fixation of the harvested fibula graft and fixation.

Moreover, with a complex soft tissue repair of the lumbrical, the interosseous muscles, the palmar plate, the fourth metacarpal lateral band, and the slings of both sides of the deep transverse metacarpal ligaments were sutured to the fibular graft fascia, along with the repair of extensor fascia and retinaculum, to ensure extra stability for the graft. An ulnar gutter back slab was applied after skin closure and dressing and kept for 6 weeks.

Postoperative findings

The histopathological report showed in the gross description that it consists of a metacarpal 5 × 2.5 × 2 cm. The patient had a 2.5 cm osseous tumor with no lymphovascular invasion. The treatment effect resulted 88% necrosis. All surgical resection margins were tumor-free, indicating complete removal of the lesion. The microscopic description showed histologic type as Ewing sarcoma.

Rehabilitation and adjuvant chemotherapy

The patient underwent physical and hand therapy after surgery and gradually regained her preoperative functional status. A follow-up x-ray at 12 weeks showed a healed bone graft without any complications. The patient completed adjuvant chemotherapy but did not receive radiotherapy because of the risk of secondary cancers and potential complications for the hand joints and graft.

Follow up

The 24-month follow up and routine surveillance imaging showed no evidence of recurrence or metastatic disease. The patient was satisfied and pleased with the appearance and functionality of the hand (Fig. 5, Video E1, available online on the Journal’s website at https://www.jhsgo.org).

Hand function after 2-year follow-up.

The x-ray revealed successful bone graft healing without evidence of recurrence or infection. However, volar subluxation of the fourth metacarpophalangeal joint has been noted, attributed to the growth of the proximal phalanx of the ring finger. The patient currently exhibits no complaints. If symptoms were to arise, a decision would be made to remove the K-wire and arthrodesis (Fig. 6).

X-ray for left hand after 2 years follow up.

Discussion

Ewing tumor of the hand metacarpal is a rare and aggressive malignancy, presenting unique challenges in diagnosis and treatment because of its complex anatomy and functional demands of the hand.7,8 Although the overall incidence of Ewing tumor is low, it represents the second most common primary malignant bone tumor in childhood and adolescence. However, its occurrence in the hand is exceptionally rare. In a review of the Intergroup Ewing Sarcoma Study, 2 cases of 1,351 involved the hands.9 The most frequent location in the hand is the long-finger metacarpal. In a review by Baccari et al,10 4 of 15 cases in the hand affected the thumb. The hand is affected in less than 0.2% of all Ewing tumors.10

Given the overlapping symptoms of Ewing sarcoma and infections, a definitive diagnosis requires a combination of clinical, radiographic, and histological evaluations. Morphologically, Ewing tumor has a similar appearance to other small round blue cell tumors such as small-cell osteosarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, primitive neuroectodermal tumor, and other related tumors, such as Ewing tumor, Ewing-like sarcoma, and B-core sarcoma.

Although some authors recommend amputations for Ewing tumors of the hand, still, limb salvage procedures can be a viable option. However, preservation of hand functionality can be difficult owing to the need to achieve wide excision with negative margins. This case is an example of functional reconstruction with a long follow-up period.10

Optimal treatment of upper-extremity bone tumors requires a careful balance of local tumor control and preservation of hand function. Ewing tumor presenting in the fourth metacarpal is exceedingly rare. The use of a triangular-shaped cortical fibula bone autograft following resection of Ewing sarcoma of the fourth metacarpal can provide disease-free survival, a stable finger, restored carpometacarpal joint, acceptable metacarpophalangeal joint function, and a cosmetic result. At the 24-month follow-up, the patient was satisfied with the appearance and functionality of the hand.

This case highlights the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in treating rare hand malignancies. The use of a fascia-wrapped nonvascularized fibula autograft for metacarpal reconstruction provides a promising option for preserving hand function and achieving positive outcomes. Further research is necessary to gain a better understanding of the long-term benefits and potential risks associated with this reconstructive technique.
