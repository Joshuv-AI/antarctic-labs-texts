Use of LLMs in preparing accessible scientific papers
Allison Doami, Christine James, Dan Lu, Lia Prins, Annette Torrence, Boris Veytsman

Abstract
Making scientific papers accessible may require reprocessing old papers to create output compliant with accessibility standards. An important step there is to convert the visual formatting to the logical one. In this report we describe our attempt at zero shot conversion of arXiv papers. Our results are mixed: while it is possible to do conversion, the reliability is not too good. We discuss alternative approaches to this problem.

Introduction

Science becoming a mass occupation has been a revolutionary
transformation for the society. The further acceleration of
scientific progress might be achieved by the tapping of the pool of
talents from the previously underrepresented populations: women,
minorities, disabled people. Thus making science accessible for all
is not just the matter of equity. It is important for all of us.

This task has both social and technical dimensions. It is not enough
just to attract these populations to science. To make contributions
of the disabled people possible, we need to solve the technical
problems of making work in science available for them. The importance
of this task is being more and more recognized

.

This is especially true with respect to people with low or no vision.
Scientific output consists of mostly papers and books: textual
information with images, tables etc. We need to make them available
for assistive technologies like screen readers, Braille terminals,
etc. There are standards for accessible Web pages

4

the logical information to the reader. A sighted person knows that a
bold-faced line probably is a section header, a centered line in the
beginning of a paper is probably a title, a line under it probably has
the information about authors, etc. All these devices are described
as
visual formatting.
As the term itself suggests, this
formatting is intended for visual consumption. It is more difficult
for assistive devices like screen readers to express the meaning for
the users. The recommendations of EBU
[
3

insist
on the logical formatting:

Do not create manually individual headings by making them bold and
by increasing the font size. Instead, create a style with this
layout and apply it to every heading in your document. This will
allow blind and partially sighted people to navigate easily through
the document, using the heading list, generated by the screen
reader. The advantages for you as an author are that it is easy to
modify the style instead of all individual headings and that styles
allow the automatic updating of your table of contents.

Incidentally, logical formatting as opposed to visual formatting helps not
just the people with low vision, but also another category of non-sighted
consumers of texts: machines. Logically formatted papers are much
more “machine-readable” and allow better analysis by various
automatic tools, which is important for classification and
summarization of knowledge. This is an example of the well known
“curb-cut effect”: the changes made for the benefit of disabled
people often have unintended positive effects for the society in

2.3
The third attempt: let’s try metadata and external knowledge

While guessing paper logical structure may be a very difficult task,
the determination of paper metadata might be a simpler one. By
metadata we mean title, authors, affiliations and abstract. Visual
formatting of these entities may include centered text, large font,
bold font, and—for affiliations—markers like

We also added some knowledge base to the things ChatGPT considers,

This example highlights the importance of data integrity within the body of the file. As you can see, ChatGPT chose to update sections (ineffectively) without specific instructions to do so. We included explicit instructions to avoid editing the body of the paper in later iterations of the prompt, which was usually effective, but not reliably so.

We also tried a simplistic approach where we supplied a paper that manually verified to be accessible and asked ChatGPT to update another file to be similarly accessible. In this case, ChatGPT replaced the author of the paper formatted with visual formatting with the author from the accessibly formatted paper. Additional training data and more explicit prompts might help this type of approach be feasible in future efforts.

In these and numerous other cases, ChatGPT tended to create non-compilable files, with senseless commands like

\verb|\def\giorno{15/6/98\end{abstract}|.

Only after several rounds of dialog ChatGPT was able to produce satisfactory result.

3
Discussion

In our experiments zero-shot transformation of text was possible.
A good picture of the challenges faced can be inferred from a
single ChatGPT prompt we created
(see Appendix
A
). Each phrase of this prompt was added
with intention to address specific issues encountered during
experimentation on different papers with variable visual formatting.

The ways of visual formatting of the elements were quite
different and numerous: authors employed font changes, size changes, centering,
etc. The most difficult task is the parsing of affiliations: authors
often have several affiliations, which are mapped to the authors in a
“many-to-many” relation. In some cases, the affiliations may be expressed
in tables, inserted within the front matter text, or, more commonly, expressed
with footnote-like markers, which are quite different from paper to paper.

As a result, the transformation was not reliable enough to get good
results without human intervention and supervision. In one experiment,
a single ChatGPT prompt was able to successfully transform 7 of 10 visually
formatted papers. In another example, the LLM changed the authors of the paper
to make it more “accessible” by transferring an author from a logically formatted
paper given to ChatGPT as an example into the transformed paper. This shows
that ChatGPT has difficulty distinguishing between formatting and content.
Note that even when we asked ChatGPT to change only the metadata,
it still occasionally changed the body of the article. At times during our
experiments, ChatGPT would ignore instructions in the prompt or,
more dangerously, state in the prompt response that it followed the instructions
when it had actually inappropriately made those edits to the transformed file.
In many cases ChatGPT created non-compilable papers that generated
L
a
T
e
X
errors.

It should also be noted that ChatGPT was able to learn how to create compilable
and logically formatted
L
a
T
e
X
when provided with multiple, iterative instructive
prompts. It’s possible that a custom ChatGPT bot trained on a large quantity of
representative data might be able to overcome some of the limitations encountered
during these experiments.

For bulk conversion the biggest task is data validation, especially
since there could be many edge cases. Validation of any transformed
text is extremely important due to the generative nature of AI
technology that powers ChatGPT and other LLMs. In addition to “honest
mistakes” when identifying and transforming visual to logical
formatting (e.g. incorrectly mapping an author to another author’s
affiliation), generative AI may “hallucinate” and insert completely
unrelated content anywhere within the paper. Previous work shows that
hallucinations are very common when generating bibliographic citations
for
\acronym
LLM-generated academic papers referencing real academic papers. A
significant percentages of citations generated by ChatGPT in these
experiments were completely fabricated
[
19
]
, which is
not surprising since
\acronym
LLMs are language-processing rather than
information processing tools.

This exploratory research had a number of
factors that helped us avoid complications that LLMs can present.
Focusing on metadata (material before

\section{Introduction}
[…]
\maketitle
, or the end of
the abstract) allowed us to circumvent a limitation of the modern LLMs:
the short window for reading. Another mitigating factor was the fact
that the authors usually have a mostly uniform order of metadata
presentation: a title is first, followed by the author(s), then
affiliations and an abstract with optional keywords. A
systematic approach to the parsing of metadata may include the use of the
arXiv API that provides ground truth for titles, authors and
abstracts. Note that our experience shows that the affiliation metadata in arXiv
API is less complete than the affiliation in the papers themselves.

An alternative approach to the use of ChatGPT might be the deployment
of a specially trained model to recognize visual formatting and
convert it to logical formatting. An approach to develop this model
might be based on the ideas discussed in
[
17
,
18
]
:

1.

Take a large database of logically formatted papers.

2.

Use a script to convert it to visually formatted manuscript.

3.

Train an algorithm to convert it back.

4
Conclusions

The zero-shot approach to convert visual to logical formatting gives
tantalizingly good results, which unfortunately are not acceptable
without human supervision. A specially trained model or a custom GPT trained on a more extensive and diverse data set might be a better solution for the problem.

Appendix A
The final prompt

Below is the final prompt developed in our experiments

You are a latex expert. Your task is to make latex files readable to
blind people. Blind people would use a screen reader to read
papers. Inaccessible latex files will use visual formatting (text
properties) to indicate logical properties of a document that would
be specifically identified using specific key words in accessible
latex. For example, an inaccessible latex file may use bold,
italics, centering, whitespace, and other text properties to format
a document title. To make this file more accessible, replace an
inaccessible title with visual formatting and no title command with
an accessible title command. The title in an inaccessible latex
paper will often be formatted with center, bold, italics or similar
and is near the beginning of the document. If the title is already
inside a title command, it is ok to retain visual formatting like
bold and italics. Some title commands include a short version in
square brackets and a longer version in curly braces. This is
accessible, but is not required to be accessible. Can you make this
file accessible? Do not change anything in this file other than the
title. The author and affiliation are not part of the title. After
you make changes, check the resulting file to make sure that it has
been updated correctly.

Accessible latex files should use the “
\author{}
” and
“
\affiliati
on{}
” commands. After the title is correctly
formatted, identify the authors (one or more names of people who
wrote the paper) and their affiliations (the organizations, such as
a university, where the authors are affiliated). These accessible
latex commands, “
\author{}
” and “
\affiliation{}
”
should replace visual formatting such as bold, italics, centering,
whitespace, and other text properties to format the authors and
their affiliations. In an accessible file, each author or
affiliation should be specified with a separate author or
affiliation command. A “
\thanks
” command is also an
accessible way to express affiliation, though not preferred. Each
affiliation should be placed immediately below the affiliated author
and should replace any previous text used to indicate
affiliations. Affiliations can span multiple lines in an
inaccessible latex file and should be combined into a single command
affiliation to be accessible. Sometimes authors and affiliations may
have no visual markup at all. In those cases, they are usually in
close proximity to the title. Look for symbols or numbers that map
authors to affiliations as a clue.

Some author commands include a short version in square brackets and a longer version in curly braces. This is accessible, but is not required to be accessible. It’s also acceptable to include visual formatting inside names, since sometimes people have special characters like “
\H{o}
” and “
\’{a}
” inside their name.

Subscript or superscript may be helpful in cases with multiple authors
and multiple affiliations. These subscripts or superscripts should be
removed after using them to find the correct affiliations for the
authors. After you make these changes, check the names of the authors
to make sure that they do not include any subscript or superscript
text or symbols.

Accessible latex files should also have a maketitle command placed
after all front matter. The maketitle should never include extra
characters such as “
}
”. Make sure that the maketitle is placed after
the title, affiliation, author and similar commands nearby. Sometimes
a latex file may include a def command that contains a maketitle
command. If it does, then this new defined version of maketitle can be
used in accessible files instead of the normal maketitle. The
maketitle command is usually placed immediately after the front
matter, but may be placed later in the file before the first
section. After you make changes, check the resulting file to make sure
that it has been updated correctly.

Identify the abstract within the latex file. Remember that an abstract
is one or more paragraphs that describe the paper and usually is
placed right after the author and affiliation information and before
any sections that might exist. It may also be inside a titlepage and
would end when the titlepage ends or when a new section begins. An
accessible latex file will place the abstract between begin and end
abstract commands. Inaccessible files may use visual formatting to
label the abstract. The abstract of an accessible paper will be placed
below the rest of the front matter.
