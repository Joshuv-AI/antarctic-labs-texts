The Effect of Adding Terminalia chebula Powder to Broilers Subjected to Salmonella spp. on Performance, Immunity, Lipid Profile and Histology
Authors: Mohanad Falhi Hamood, Raghad Ali Abdaljaleel, Akhil Mosleem Alsadwi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe current study evaluated the effects of dietary Terminalia chebula powder (TC) supplementation on the performance, immune response, some lipid profile and histological changes in broilers challenged with Salmonella spp.MethodsA total of 160 Rose 308 newly hatched broiler chicks were randomly distributed to four treatments in a 2 × 2 factorial arrangement, with TC supplementation (0 or 750 mg/kg/diet) and Salmonella challenge (challenged or unchallenged) as the main factors. The four treatments were as follows: G1, basal diet with no TC and no challenge; G2, basal diet supplemented with 750 mg/kg diet TC and no challenge; G3, basal diet with no TC and with challenge (1 × 108 CFU/mL administered orally at Day 10); and G4, basal diet supplemented with 750 mg/kg diet TC and challenged. The study estimated body weight (BW), feed intake (FI), serum total cholesterol and triglyceride, infectious bursal disease (IBD) vaccine antibody titres and histological changes in the liver, spleen and duodenum.ResultsThe challenge with Salmonella significantly reduced BW, FI and IBD antibody titres (p < 0.001) throughout the study; however, serum cholesterol increased and triglyceride levels decreased (p < 0.001). Birds in the G3 group (challenged, no TC) showed the most severe reductions in performance and histological damage, including liver degeneration, splenic atrophy and villous atrophy with necrosis in the intestine. On the contrary, supplementation with 750 mg/kg TC (G2 and G4) significantly improved BW, FI and lipid profiles (p < 0.001), and mitigated the negative effects of the Salmonella challenge, mainly in the intestine and liver. Birds in the G4 group (challenged and supplemented with TC) exhibited moderate improvements in liver integrity, spleen congestion and intestinal villous length compared to the non‐supplemented, challenged group.ConclusionThese results suggested that supplementation with TC powder at 750 mg/kg with and without challenge significantly enhance growth performance, immune response and serum lipid profile. Adding TC appears to offer protective effects by alleviated the detrimental impact of Salmonella spp., making it a valuable dietary additive for improving broiler health under pathogenic stress.

Introduction

The contamination of poultry feed or carcass with bacteria can cause a significant risk to both poultry and consumer health. However, the excess use of antibiotics can cause antibiotic‐resistant bacteria in animals’ and it may transfer to human (Sivanantham et al. 2023). Different parts of plants or whole plant are used to produce medicines that are called botanical medicine or phytobiotic, which use for therapeutic purpose. Phytobiotic can play essential roles in production of animals from diseases by using different mechanisms (Rehman et al. 2025). They use to improve performance of animals by decreasing microbial population of gastrointestinal tract (Rafeeq et al. 2022). They also use them as antimicrobial by increasing the intestinal mucus secretion, which lead to reduce bacterial adhesion to intestinal mucosa and reduce pathogenic load (Krauze 2021).

Terminalia chebula (TC) is one of those phytobiotic plants, which have numerous activities such as, antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory due to its diverse components. It is wildly separated in South Asia and India (Sivanantham et al. 2023; Poudel et al. 2023).

Salmonella is one of prevalent pathogenic bacteria that are commonly found in poultry. It can survive without oxygen. It causes foodborne diseases globally by infecting the gastrointestinal tract and causes nausea, diarrhoea and cramps in humans (Jung et al. 2022). Salmonella spp. can be transmitted horizontally from animals to humans by the consumption of contaminated products especially meat and eggs in poultry (Hugas and Beloeil 2014).

There is a scarce literature evaluating the effect of TC on broilers. A study was conducted by Cheng et al. (2024), concluded that adding TC extract with different concentrations to broiler diet cause a rise in body weight (BW), decrease in feed‐to‐gain ratio with improving antioxidant capacity and intestinal morphology in 56‐day experiment. So, according to what was stated previously the TC powder or extract may promote the performance and sustain the health of broilers. Additionally, there is a limited research showed who TC worked with the presence of Salmonella spp. challenge. For that reason, the goal of the current research was to study the effect of adding TC powder to broiler diet on some performance parameters and study the antimicrobial effects of the powder on reducing the bacterial infection by Salmonella spp.

Materials and Methods

Birds and Management

A total of 160‐day‐old broiler chicks (Rose 308) of straight‐run were obtained from a commercial hatchery and transported to the poultry research farm. The study was approved with an approved number (P.G./9 dated 23 February 2025), the housing environment was fully controlled. The broilers were reared in wire mesh wood shaving‐floor pens measuring 2 × 2.5 m for 35 days with continuous lighting. For the first 3 days, the temperature was initially set at 33°C, followed by a weekly reduction of 2°C to 3°C until it stabilized at 22–24°C, which was maintained for the rest of the experiment. Feed and water were provided ad libitum throughout the study. Vaccination for Newcastle disease and infectious bronchitis diseases (ND, Volac, Germany) (IB, Volac, Germany) were given at Day 9 and 18 of the study, whereas infectious bursal disease (IBD) vaccination was given at Day 14 (Boehringer Ingelheim‐HQ Germany). All vaccinations were given via drinking water. The birds were observed on a daily basis to assess overall health, as well as to monitor temperature, lighting, water supply, feed availability and any unexpected occurrences within the rearing environment.

Plant Materials

The dried fruits of TC were obtained from a local market in Baghdad province, Iraq. It is a product of Sanaa Zesty (India). The fruits were officially authenticated by the Iraqi National Herbarium, Directorate of Seed Testing and Certification, Ministry of Agriculture, Iraq, with an authentication number 436 in 20/02/2025. The TC powder was prepared by grinding the dry fruit by using coffee grinder (Mr. Coffee, sunbeam Products Inc., Boca Raton, FL). The powder was then added to chicken feed.

Dietary Treatments

Birds were randomly distributed into 4 dietary treatments with 2 replicates for each treatment, 20 birds in each replicate in a completely randomized design.

Birds were fed a commercial corn‐soybean basal diet formulated on the basis of NRC (1994) in a two‐phase feeding programme starter (Days 0–21) and finisher (Days 22–35) (Table 1). In each phase, dietary treatments consisted of G1 that fed basal diet only with no TC powder and no challenge (TC‐0, No Challenge), G2 fed basal diet + 750 mg/kg diet of TC powder with no challenge (TC‐750, No Challenge), G3 fed basal diet with no TC powder and challenged with Salmonella spp. (TC‐0, challenged), and G4 fed basal diet with 750 mg/kg diet of TC powder and challenged with Salmonella spp. (TC‐750, challenged).

Salmonella spp. Challenge

A Salmonella spp. isolate previously recovered from infected chickens in Baghdad, Iraq, was used as the challenge organism. The isolate was first cultured on Salmonella‐Shigella agar (Difco, USA) then serially diluted to obtain a final concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/mL. The concentration of the bacterial suspension was confirmed by serial dilution and plate counting on selective medium before administration. At 10 days of age, the isolate was freshly prepared before challenge, and birds in the challenged groups were orally inoculated with 1 mL/bird of the prepared suspension.

Performance

Both BW and feed intake (FI) were recorded in g per pen on Days 0, 7, 14, 21, 28 and 35. The pen was the experimental unit for the performance parameters for the study. The total birds in each pen were weighted at the beginning and end of each week in order to calculate average bird weight per pen, whereas, FI measured was by calculating total feed offered and remaining unconsumed feed at the beginning and end of each week to find the average FI per bird per pen.

Blood Samples and Serum Preparation

At the end of the study (35 days), blood samples were taken randomly from three birds for each replicate (six birds for each treatment). Blood samples were obtained from wing vein by disposable syringe to sample tubes that do not contain anticoagulant. The serum was separated from the blood by using centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10–15 at 4°C.

Determination of Total Cholesterol

The total serum cholesterol was measured by using standard diagnostic reagent from (Human Company, Wiesbaden, Germany) by applying enzymatic colorimetric method (CHOD‐PAP). A set of test tubes were prepared as follows: Reagent blank was made by adding 1000 µL of cholesterol reagent to 10 µL distilled water. Standard tube prepared by mixing 1000 µL of cholesterol reagent with 10 µL standard solution. Additionally, the broiler sample was prepared by pipetting 10 µL of serum in tube and mixed with 1000 µL of cholesterol reagent. All the tubes mixed gently then incubated at 37°C for 5 min, later than the absorbance was evaluated at 500 nm by using a spectrophotometer (Spectronic Genesis 5, USA). The results were calculated in mg/dL as follows: Totalcholesterolmg/dL=Absorbanceofsample/Absorbanceofstandard×concentrationofstandard

Determination of Triglycerides

Triglycerides in the blood of the broiler chickens were measured by using diagnostic reagent from (Human Company, Wiesbaden, Germany) by enzymatic colorimetric method (GPO‐PAP). The evaluating method depends on the enzymatic hydrolysis of triglyceride, which ends with a colour reaction. The manufactured company supplies ready to use reagent kits for triglyceride. A set of three tubes were prepared first one was reagent, which consider as blank, it contain 1000 µL triglyceride. Other tube is the standard; prepared by mixing 1000 µL of triglyceride reagent with 10 µL standard solutions. Final one was made of 1000 µL triglyceride reagent mixed with 10 µL serum samples. Tubes were prepared and incubated for 5 min at 37°C. The absorbance was read at 505 nm (Spectronic Genesis 5, USA), and the results were calculated as follows: Triglyceridesconcentrationmg/dL=Absorbancesample/Absorbancestandard×concentrationofstandard

Determination of IBD Vaccine Antibody Titre

ELISA technique was used to evaluate IBD antibody titre by using (ProFlock) kit manufactured by (Synbiotics Corporation, USA). Blood samples were collected from wing vein from three birds from each replicate (six birds for each treatment). The titre was evaluated at Days 15 and 35 of the experiment. First, diluted blood samples and controls were prepared in dilution buffer. Then, samples were transferred to an antigen‐coated micro plate and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Followed by multistep washing cycle by adding horseradish peroxidase conjugated and incubated for another 30 min. Later, the micro plate was washed again and treated with substrate for 15 min, followed by adding stop solution. Finally, the ELISA micro plate reader was used to measure optical density values (OD) in a wavelength 405–450 nm in 15–30 min from stopping the reaction. The titre values were automatically calculated by commercial (ProFlock) software based on Sample‐ to‐Positive equation.

Histopathological Examination

On Day 35, three birds/treatment were randomly selected, euthanized and slaughtered. Tissue samples of liver (right lob), spleen (whole) and duodenum (distal loop) were collected. The collected tissues then fixed in 10% buffered formalin for 48 h. Tissue sections of 0.5 cm thickness were prepared and placed in plastic cassettes and subjected to dehydration using an automated tissue processor (Histo‐Line ATP700, Italy). The dehydrated tissues were embedded in paraffin using a tissue embedding system (HESTION TEC2800‐C, China). The paraffin‐embedded tissues were later trimmed and sectioned into 4–5 µm slices using a semi‐automatic microtome (Histo‐Line MRS3500, Italy). These sections were carefully placed in a water bath (FALC BI, Italy), then mounted on glass slides using a hot plate (Hysh11, Korea). Staining was performed using haematoxylin and eosin (DakoCytomation, Denmark). The prepared slides were analysed under a light microscope (Olympus, Japan) at 40× and 10× magnifications (Suvarna et al. 2019).

Statistical Analysis

Data were analysed using two‐way ANOVA for a completely randomized design in a 2 × 2 factorial arrangement, with TC supplementation (0 or 750 mg/kg diet) and Salmonella spp. challenge (challenged or non‐challenged) as the main effects, including their interaction. All data were analysed separately at each time point, and pen was considered the experimental unit. Analyses were performed using the Standard Least Squares procedure of the JMP Pro 16.0.0 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). When significant effects were detected, means were separated using Tukey's honest significant difference (HSD) test at p ≤ 0.05.

Histological Findings

Broilers in the G1 (TC‐0, No Challenge), which were fed a basal diet with no TC supplementation and no Salmonella challenge, exhibited normal histological structures in the liver, spleen and intestine (Figure 1A–C).

(A–C) G1 (basal diet, no challenge): Normal histology in the liver, spleen and intestine, indicating healthy tissue structure. (D–F) G2 (basal diet + TC, no challenge): mild changes in the liver and spleen (perivascular inflammation, lymphoid hyperplasia), with generally healthy intestinal villi but some submucosal gland distention. (G–I) G3 (basal diet + Salmonella challenge): Severe histological damage in all organs, including liver degeneration, lymphoid atrophy in the spleen and villous atrophy with necrosis in the intestine. (J–L) G4 (basal diet + TC + Salmonella challenge): moderate improvements in liver and intestinal health compared to the G3 group, with increased villous length and reduced severity of liver and spleen damage.

Additionally, broilers in G2 that were received (TC750 mg/kg, no challenged) had a moderate histological changes, which were observed in the liver, spleen and intestine. Liver sections revealed perivascular heterophilic aggregation, indicating mild inflammatory changes around blood vessels, with little effect on adjacent hepatic cells (Figure 1D). The spleen tissue showed moderate congestion of the splenic reticular tissue, along with lymphoid hyperplasia, indicating increased lymphocyte activity. Additionally, sign of apoptosis was noticed, which characterized by empty, clear spaces in some splenic follicles (Figure 1E). The duodenal tissue was distinguished by long, villi were well‐developed and also cystic distention of the sub‐mucosal glands was visible (Figure 1F). This shows mild to moderate sub‐mucosal changes even with overall villous health. Moreover, G3 birds that feed (TC‐0 mg/kg, challenged) observed a significant histological damage across all three organs. The liver exhibited moderate to severe degeneration, with a marked loss of parenchymal structure and individualization of hepatocytes, indicating substantial liver damage (Figure 1G). Lymphoid atrophy with destructive lesions affecting the lymphoid follicles and severe congestion of the red pulp was showed in the spleen (Figure 1H). These modifications in the spleen reflect a breakdown of immune functionality. The duodenal tissue showed sloughing of the villous epithelium, along with cellular infiltration and evidence of villous atrophy. Superficial necrosis was also detected, showing severe intestinal damage as a result of the Salmonella infection (Figure 1I).

In contract, for G4 (TC‐750 mg/kg, challenged) the histological findings were intermediate between the (TC‐750, non‐challenged) and (TC‐0, challenged) groups. The liver showed multiple infiltrations of mononuclear cells (MNCs) around blood vessels, with congestion of sub‐capsular sinusoids (Figure 1J). These findings indicate inflammatory changes, but with less severe degeneration compared to G3. The spleen showed sub‐capsular infiltration of MNCs and fibromuscular hypertrophy of the splenic blood vessels (Figure 1K), suggesting moderate immune activation and fibrosis. The tissue of intestine showed a marked increase in villous length, with moderate distention of lymphatic loops and infiltration of MNCs (Figure 1L). This indicates an overall improvement in intestinal integrity compared to the G3 group, although some mild inflammatory changes were still present.

Discussion

The current study aimed to evaluate the effect of dietary TC supplementation on the growth performance, immune response and histological health of broilers challenged with Salmonella spp. The results of the current study highlight the potential benefits of TC in moderating the negative effects of Salmonella infection, offering promising implications for poultry health management under pathogenic stress conditions.

Growth Performance and FI

The challenge with Salmonella spp. significantly impaired broiler growth, as it showed by reducing the BW and FI of the challenged groups. These findings are consistent with earlier reports showed that Salmonella spp. infection compromises nutrient absorption and growth in birds, due to metabolic disturbance, which may cause intestinal damage and immune system activation (Shaji et al. 2023). In the current study, birds in the G3 (challenged, no TC) experienced the most pronounced reductions in the BW and FI, probably due to severe intestinal villous atrophy and liver damaged that present in the histological analysis.

Interestingly, the supplementation of 750 mg/kg TC significantly improved BW and FI in both unchallenged (G2) and Salmonella‐challenged (G4) broilers. Both G2 and G4 revealed higher growth rates compared to their no‐supplemented counterparts, who suggested that TC improves nutrient utilization and may have growth‐promoting properties. Consisting with our findings Cheng et al. (2024) revealed that TC extract significantly enhanced growth performance by increasing final BW and average daily intake, while reducing the feed‐to‐gain ratio in broilers. In addition, a research conducted by Xu et al. (2022) reported that adding oregano essential oil to drinking water of commercial yellow chicken breeders inhibits the infection with Salmonella Gallinarum and Salmonella Pullorum. Moreover, phytobiotic have compounds like eugenol and trans‐cinnamaldehyde that cause decrease in Salmonella enterica serovar Enteritidis growth and reduce caecal colonization after 10 days of challenge in broiler birds (Kollanoor‐Johny et al. 2012).

Adding TC, which is phytobiotic to broilers diet improves health and promote growth of birds due to the present of bioactive compounds such as tannins and flavonoids, which are known with their antioxidant, antimicrobial and anti‐inflammatory function. These compounds help in reducing the negative impacts of oxidative stress and inflammation related with Salmonella spp. infection in order to support better nutrient absorption and growth (Arain et al. 2022).

Additionally, the presence of tannins in the TC can condense with proteins by hydrogen and hydrophobic bonding to create stable tannin‐protein complexes, which lead to intestinal astringency and maintain intestinal health (Engström et al. 2016).

Serum Cholesterol and Triglycerides

The results showed that the Salmonella challenge significantly increased serum cholesterol levels while decreasing triglyceride levels in broilers. This lipid dysregulation is a common feature of inflammatory stress and bacterial infection. This could lead to altered lipid metabolism and affect liver function (Mezes et al. 1997). In this study, G3, which was the challenged group without TC supplementation exhibited the highest cholesterol levels, consistence with the observed histopathological damage in the liver. However, TC supplementation significantly reduced serum cholesterol levels and increased triglyceride concentrations in both challenged and unchallenged birds. This line up with Cheng et al. (2024), who observed that TC extract supplementation reduced the malondialdehyde levels in plasma, indicative of reduced lipid peroxidation and improved antioxidant capacity. TC acts as potent antioxidant, capable of improving liver function and reducing oxidative stress. Additionally, herbal extract contain minerals, vitamins, phosphate, lecithin and choline that interfere with biosynthesis of cholesterol in liver and lead to dissolution of cholesterol and fatty acid (Hafeez et al. 2020).

TC may modulate the metabolism of lipid potentially by its antioxidant and hepatoprotective effect (Hameed et al. 2019). The cholesterol levels reduction may cause an improvement in liver health and reduced the inflammation, whereas the increase in triglycerides could occur due to high inclusion rate of TC or the form how it was given. As it was mentioned previously TC contains high concentration of tannins, which is antioxidant that helps in improving gut health. However, a tremendous amount from tannins can cause liver stress and damage that leads to lose the ability of liver to metabolize fats, which cause release of triglycerides to the blood (Cheng et al. 2024).

Immune Response and IBD Vaccine Titre

The Salmonella challenge significantly reduced IBD vaccine titres in challenged broilers that indicated a compromised immune response. This result aligns with previous research, which showed that Salmonella spp. infections can suppress the immune system and reduce vaccine efficacy, due to the systemic inflammation and lymphoid tissue damaged that caused by infection (Chappell et al. 2009).

In the present study, the G3 group displayed the lowest IBD titres with sever histological damage to the spleen, including lymphoid atrophy and splenic tissue destruction. TC supplementation significantly improved IBD titres especially in G4 group that exhibited a stronger immune response than the non‐supplemented, challenged birds. This finding suggested that TC may improve immune function, potentially by its bioactive compounds that possess anti‐inflammatory and properties (Sultan et al. 2023). By reducing oxidative stress and inflammation, TC may help in preserving lymphoid tissue integrity and developing the immune system's ability to respond to vaccine. The immune system enhancement that was recorded in the current study is supported by the findings from Cheng et al. (2024), where TC extract improved immune markers by increasing the levels of immunoglobulins (IgM and IgG) and reduced pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐6 and IL‐1β. The histological examination of immune organs in this study exposed that TC supplementation reduced hepatic damage and spleen congestion in challenged birds, which is consistent with immune boosting and the anti‐inflammatory effects of TC extract as reported by Cheng et al. (2024). Tian et al. (2022) reported that condenses supplementation of tannin increased the immunity in black sheep exposed to cadmium poisoning by increasing the concentrations of IgM, IgG and IgA. Additionally, the presence of active components of TC, like phenolic acid and flavonoids, may increase Ig levels and improve function of immune system (Wu et al. 2022).

Histological Changes

The histological analysis exposed substantial tissue damage in the duodenum, spleen and liver of Salmonella‐challenged broilers, particularly in G3 group. Marked degeneration and hepatocyte individualization was shown in the liver; however, the spleen exhibited lymphoid atrophy and sever congestion. In addition, the intestinal tissue revealed villous atrophy and epithelial sloughing, which resulted in reduced nutrient absorption capacity and overall intestinal health. These pathological changes are typically due to Salmonella infections that could induce systemic inflammation and damage to gastrointestinal and immune organs (Foley et al. 2013).

In contrast, broilers in the G4 group (challenged, supplemented with TC) exhibited less severe histopathological lesions, with improvements in liver integrity, spleen architecture and intestinal villous length. This protective effect could be attributed to the anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant properties of TC, which likely helped reduce tissue damage and promote recovery from Salmonella‐induced oxidative stress. These findings are agreed with previous studies showing that TC can protect against liver damage and intestinal inflammation in other animal models (Jayesh et al. 2019). The improvement in gut morphology, with the increased villus height (VH) and VH‐to‐crypt depth ratio (VH/CD) observed in TC‐supplemented birds, also mirrors the findings of (Cheng et al. 2024), who stated that TC extract improved intestinal health by improving intestinal villi and increasing the ratio of VH/CD in multiple intestinal sections. As stated previously that TC is rich with tannins, an early research conducted by Jing et al. (2022) reported that adding tannins to the diet of broilers increases VH/CD and decreases CD. A digestion and absorption surface increase when VH/CD increases (Ding et al. 2022). This research found an improvement in intestinal VH, which suggested that TC may enhance gut morphology in order to have better nutrient absorption and overall growth performance in broilers.

Conclusion

In conclusion, dietary supplementation with TC powder at 750 mg/kg with and without challenge significantly improved BW, FI and serum lipid profile. The supplementation of TC also mitigated the negative effects of Salmonella challenge, mainly in the intestine and liver. Dietary addition of TC may also improve intestinal morphology by improving digestion and absorption surface of the gut. These findings suggest that TC may be a useful phytogenic feed additive for supporting broiler health under enteric challenge conditions. Future research should be conducted to explore the optimal dosage and long‐term effects of TC supplementation in broilers, as well as its potential interactions with other feed additives and vaccination programmes.
