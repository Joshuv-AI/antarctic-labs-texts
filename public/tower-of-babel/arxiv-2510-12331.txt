Convergence to a non-explicit steady state in non-factorized kinetic Fokker-Planck equations with (very) weak velocity confinements
Émeric Bouin, Luca Ziviani

Abstract
. In this article, we prove some convergence results for kinetic Fokker-Planck equations with strong space confinement but fat-tailed local equilibria and non-explicit global steady states. We extend the results of [ 7 ] to a wider class of fat-tailed local equilibria, with rates of convergence in a large class of weighted 𝖫 1 \mathsf{L}^{1} spaces. We complement our results with numerical simulations to investigate the shape of the non-explicit steady state.



1.
Introduction

This work is devoted to the kinetic Fokker-Planck equation,

(1.1)

\partial_{t}f=\mathcal{L}f:=-v\cdot{\nabla_{x}}f+{\nabla_{x}}V\cdot{\nabla_{v}}f+{\nabla_{v}}\cdot\left(\mathcal{M}{\nabla_{v}}\left(\frac{f}{\mathcal{M}}\right)\right)

where the unknown function
f=f(t,x,v)
depends on time
t\in[0,\infty)
, position
x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
and velocity
v\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. We consider external potentials
V\colon{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
of the form

(1.2)

V=\frac{\lfloor\cdot\rceil^{\alpha}}{\alpha}

with
\alpha>1
, where we denote
\lfloor\cdot\rceil=\sqrt{1+\lvert\cdot\rvert^{2}}
. The local equilibrium may take one of the following forms. Either

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=c_{\beta}^{-1}e^{-\frac{\lfloor\cdot\rceil^{\beta}}{\beta}},

with
\beta>0
and
c_{\beta}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\left(-\frac{\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}}{\beta}\right)\,\mathrm{d}v
, or

(1.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=d_{\gamma}^{-1}\lfloor\cdot\rceil^{-d-\gamma}

with
\gamma>1
and
d_{\gamma}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lfloor v\rceil^{-d-\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}v
. The evolution equation is complemented with an initial datum
f_{0}\in\mathsf{L}^{1}
. The equation is mass conservative, that is,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{0}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v,\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

Equation (
1.1
) is usually used to describe the evolution in time of the density function of particles under the influence of an external macroscopic potential
V
. It is a very suitable physical model for gases, plasmas, or stellar systems, but it has also numerous applications in biology, chemistry, and material science. The operator
\mathcal{L}
in (
1.1
) can be split into
\mathcal{L}=\mathsf{L}-\mathsf{T}
, where
\mathsf{T}
is a transport operator and
\mathsf{L}
is a collision operator. The transport operator
\mathsf{T}
is

\mathsf{T}f=v\cdot{\nabla_{x}}f-{\nabla_{x}}V\cdot{\nabla_{v}}f,

and corresponds to the Lie derivative of
f
along the Hamiltonian vector field associated with the energy

(1.5)

E(x,v)=\frac{\lvert v\rvert^{2}}{2}+V(x).

The collision operator
\mathsf{L}
is the Fokker-Planck operator,

\mathsf{L}f={\nabla_{v}}\cdot\left(\mathcal{M}{\nabla_{v}}\left(\frac{f}{\mathcal{M}}\right)\right)=\Delta_{v}f-{\nabla_{v}}\cdot\left(\frac{{\nabla_{v}}\mathcal{M}}{\mathcal{M}}f\right)

Observe that our assumptions on
\mathcal{M}
((
1.3
) or (
1.4
)) lead to power law drifts, that is

\frac{{\nabla_{v}}\mathcal{M}}{\mathcal{M}}=-\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta-2}v

if
\mathcal{M}
is given by (
1.3
), and

\frac{{\nabla_{v}}\mathcal{M}}{\mathcal{M}}=-(d+\gamma)\lfloor v\rceil^{-2}v

if
\mathcal{M}
is given by (
1.4
).

In the case
\beta=2
, equation (
1.1
) turns out to be the classical kinetic Fokker-Planck equation, with an explicit global Gibbs state,

G=Z^{-1}\exp(-E)

for some normalisation constant
Z>0
. This equation has been extensively studied and various hypocoercivity methods have been used
[
17
,
16
,
20
,
31
,
34
,
32
,
46
]
to show exponential convergence to the steady state in weighted
\mathsf{L}^{2}
norms. Still with
\beta=2
, in
[
6
]
, the author proves convergence for weaker potential (
1.2
) with
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
For other results with weak confinement we refer the interested reader to
[
2
,
18
]
.

On the other hand, when the local equilibrium
\mathcal{M}
is not a Gaussian, the transport operator and the collision operator do not share the same kernel. This fact makes the existence of a stationary state
G
less trivial, indeed such function must solve the equation
\mathsf{T}G=\mathsf{L}G
where both sides of the equation are not zero. It is therefore necessary to use non-explicit methods, like the Harris theorem, to show the existence of such an equilibrium.

For further use, we define the formal dual operator
\mathcal{L}^{*}
by

(1.6)

\mathcal{L}^{*}m:=v\cdot{\nabla_{x}}m-{\nabla_{x}}V\cdot{\nabla_{v}}m+\Delta_{v}m+\frac{{\nabla_{v}}\mathcal{M}}{\mathcal{M}}\cdot{\nabla_{v}}m

For a positive weight function
m\colon{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
\mathsf{L}^{1}(m)
the functional space defined by the norm

\lVert f\rVert_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(m)}:=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lvert f(x,v)\rvert m(x,v)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v.

The main results of this paper are existence and uniqueness of a steady state
G
to (
1.1
) and convergence of solutions towards
G
. The first main result concerns the kinetic Fokker-Planck equation when
\mathcal{M}
has thin tails as in (
1.3
).

Theorem 1.1
.

When
\mathcal{M}
is given by (
1.3
) with
\beta>0
, there exists a positive normalised steady state
G\in\mathsf{L}^{1}\left(\exp({\delta E^{\frac{\min\{\beta,2\}}{2}}})\right)
with
\delta>0
small enough.

Moreover, let
f
be a solution to (
1.1
), with initial data
f_{0}
. Then, for every
\theta\in(0,\min(1,\frac{\beta}{2})]
, there exists
\lambda>0
such that for any
f_{0}\in\mathsf{L}^{1}(e^{\delta E^{\theta}})
,

\left\|f-\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{0}\right)G\right\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}}\lesssim e^{-\lambda t^{\theta}}\left\|f_{0}-\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{0}\right)G\right\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(e^{\delta E^{\theta}})}.

This theorem extends the results of
[
7
]
, where only
\beta\geq 2
is covered. We provide the analogous result for the missing range of parameters
0<\beta<2
, and observe a slower decay rate. Notice that, for
\beta\geq 2
, we recover the exponential decay, as expected. Our proof can actually cover the result of
[
7
]
.

For fat-tailed local equilibrium (
1.4
), we have the following result.

Theorem 1.2
.

When
\mathcal{M}
is given by (
1.4
) with
\gamma>1
, there exists a positive normalised steady state
G\in\mathsf{L}^{1}(E^{\frac{\gamma}{2}})
. Moreover, for any
f
, solution to (
1.1
) with initial data
f_{0}\in\mathsf{L}^{1}(E^{k})
, and for any
k\in(1,1+\tfrac{\gamma}{2}]
we have,

\left\|f-\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{0}\right)G\right\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\langle t\rangle^{k}}\left\|f_{0}-\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{0}\right)G\right\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(E^{k})}.

Actually, the latter convergence rate result is also correct when
\mathcal{M}
is sub-exponential, and for any
k
. We shall mention it as a side result.

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
f
be a solution to (
1.1
), with initial data
f_{0}\in\mathsf{L}^{1}(E^{k})
when
\mathcal{M}
is given by (
1.3
) with
\beta>0
. For every
k>1
fixed, we have

(1.7)

\left\|f-\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{0}\right)G\right\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\langle t\rangle^{k}}\left\|f_{0}-\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{0}\right)G\right\|_{\mathsf{L}^{1}(E^{k})}.

It is worth noticing that

1.1
and
1.2
are true even if the potential
V
behaves like
\tfrac{\lfloor\cdot\rceil^{\alpha}}{\alpha}
, that is if

V\asymp\lfloor\cdot\rceil^{\alpha}

and

x\cdot{\nabla_{x}}V\asymp\lvert x\rvert^{2}\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha-2}

for all
x\in B_{R}^{c}
, where
B_{R}
denotes the ball centered at the origin with radius
R>0
. Regarding the variable
v
one could also lighten the exact
\tfrac{{\nabla_{v}}\mathcal{M}}{\mathcal{M}}=-\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta-2}v
in the same flavour at the expense of heavier computations below.

Let us describe qualitatively the results of the main theorems. A possible heuristic explanation of
1.1
is that the transport operator is pushing towards a distribution which can be written as a function of the energy
E
, while the collision operator is pushing towards a distribution who decays in
v
like
e^{-\frac{\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}}{\beta}}
. For this reason, both tendencies might find a compromise on a steady state whose profile should resemble

G\approx e^{-\delta E^{\frac{\beta}{2}}}\sim e^{-\frac{\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}}{\beta}}

for
\lvert v\rvert\to\infty
. In fact we find that the steady state is in
\mathsf{L}^{1}\left(\exp({\delta E^{\frac{\beta}{2}}})\right)
.

The same idea applies to
1.2
, but this time the collision operator is pushing towards a polynomially decaying function. We then expect that the steady state might resemble a function of the energy which a similar decay in the variable
v
, that is

G\approx E^{-\frac{d+\gamma}{2}}\sim\lfloor v\rceil^{-d-\gamma}

for
\lvert v\rvert\to\infty
. In fact, we found that the steady state belongs to
\mathsf{L}^{1}(E^{\frac{\gamma}{2}})
, namely, it should at least have energy moments of order
\frac{\gamma}{2}
. This implies in particular that
G
has at most moments in
v
up to the order
\gamma
, exactly as
\mathcal{M}
.

This work can be considered as an extension of
[
7
]
, where the kinetic Fokker-Planck equation (
1.1
) with local equilibrium (
1.3
) with
\beta\geq 2
is considered. Cao first proved the existence of a unique steady state and convergence in weighted
\mathsf{L}^{1}
norms by using the Harris theorem. In a second step, this result is extended to
\mathsf{L}^{p}
spaces thanks to regularising properties of the semigroup
S_{\mathcal{L}}
and extension arguments. In the particular cases where the steady state does not have an explicit expression, Lyapunov functions play an important role, because they allow to deduce the existence of stationary states and a suitable weighted
\mathsf{L}^{1}
space where we can find them. However, finding sharp Lyapunov functions turns out to be particularly hard, especially for weaker confinements in velocity. We aim to complete the picture left open by
[
7
]
by providing Lyapunov functions for fatter tailed
\mathcal{M}
given in (
1.3
) for
0<\beta<2
and (
1.4
) for
\gamma>1
. This is one of the most important content of the present paper.

We shall stress that the condition
\alpha>1
is unavoidable in our computations: our main concern is the strength of the velocity drift. Few results are actually known when
\alpha\leq 1
: only the very special case
\beta=2
is discussed in
[
6
]
and things are there based on the fact that the steady state
G
is known and explicit. The origin of the difficulty in other situations is that weak Lyapunov functions are not known at the moment, and results in this direction are the aim of a subsequent work. Some results when the collision operator is of scattering type are given in
[
9
]
, but do not cover Fokker-Planck operators.

The main tool we will use is the Harris theorem, which is nowadays very popular to prove the existence of a stationary measure for stochastic semigroups and convergence toward it. The first result on the stability of Markov processes goes back to Doeblin
[
15
]
, who showed exponential convergence to a stationary measure assuming that the transition probabilities possess a uniform lower bound. In
[
30
]
, some sufficient conditions for the existence of a stationary measure are discussed and, in the last few years, these results have been further developed. In particular, we mention
[
29
,
19
,
42
,
41
,
11
,
28
]
for such theorems, which are now known as Harris-type results. In this work, we need the sub-geometric version of it, which we will recall in
Appendix

A
for self-consistency. For more information about the sub-geometric Harris theorem, see
[
18
,
28
,
11
]
. In the last decades, the Harris theorem has been successfully applied to many kinetic equations: such as the kinetic Fokker-Planck equation
[
7
]
, the homogeneous Fokker-Planck equation
[
36
]
, the linear Boltzmann equation
[
9
,
22
]
and the run-and-tumble equation in biology
[
5
,
24
,
23
]
. See also the review
[
47
]
. Two main ingredients are needed for the Harris theorem: a Lyapunov condition and a positivity condition. In the case of Fokker-Planck equations, the Lyapunov condition can be verified directly using the adjoint operator
\mathcal{L}^{*}
, on the other hand, the positivity property correspond to prove a certain Harnack inequality.

Harnack inequalities for kinetic Fokker-Planck equation were first proved in
[
25
]
by a
non-constructive argument, later was revised by
[
27
]
with a new constructive De Giorgi approach based on ideas of
[
43
]
. See also
[
26
]
for another constructive proof by the Moser-Kružkov approach. As the name suggests, these methods are adaptations form the De Giorgi argument
[
13
,
14
]
for proving Hölder regularity for elliptic equations. Moreover, Harnack inequalities have also been obtained for the long-range Boltzmann equation in
[
35
]
and with applications to the Landau equation
[
25
]
.

It is worth mentioning that a very similar model to (
1.1
) has been studied in
[
4
]
, replacing the transport operator
\mathsf{T}
with another Hamiltonian operator more compatible with the collision operator. This greatly simplifies the problem as the steady state is explicit, so no Harris type arguments are needed. The authors managed to perform a
\mathsf{L}^{2}
-hypocoercivity method type method, taking its foundations in
[
17
]
. As in the present paper, slower convergence rates were obtained due to the heavy tails of
\mathcal{M}
.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. In
Section

2
, we provide many weak Lyapunov functions, first for the case (
1.3
) and then for (
1.4
). In
Section

3
, we prove the positivity condition required by the Harris theorem. Unlike in
[
7
]
, we do not show a regularisation result of the type
\mathsf{L}^{1}\to\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
about the semigroup
S_{\mathcal{L}}
, but we use most recent results on Harnack inequalities as in
[
8
]
. Finally, in
Section

4
we prove the main results. We end the paper by recalling the Harris theorem in
Appendix

A
.

2.
Weak Lyapunov functions

In this section, we seek weight functions
m\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[1,\infty)
verifying the so-called Lyapunov condition, that is

\mathcal{L}^{*}m\leq C\mathds{1}_{B_{R}}-\phi(m),

where
C,\,R>0
and
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a concave function such that
\lim_{\lvert(x,v)\rvert\to\infty}m(x,v)=\infty
. We provide below a family of such weights, in both cases
\mathcal{M}
defined as in (
1.3
) and in (
1.4
). These functions are crucial to apply the Harris theorem of
Appendix

A
.

Let
H\colon{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
be the function defined by

(2.1)

H(x,v)=E^{\ell}+\epsilon\,\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{A}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{B}}\,x\cdot v,

for some
\ell>1
,
\epsilon>0
and real constants
A\in\mathbb{R}
and
B\in(0,1)
to be adapted later on.

2.1.
Preliminaries

To make the following calculations clearer, we introduce a useful lemma with technical estimates.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any
\ell>1
, we have the following

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}(E^{\ell})\leq\left[2(\ell-1)+d+v\cdot\frac{{\nabla_{v}}\mathcal{M}}{\mathcal{M}}\right]\ell E^{\ell-1}

and, for any
A\in\mathbb{R}
and
B\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\frac{\lfloor v\rceil^{B}}{\lfloor x\rceil^{A}}\mathcal{L}^{*}\left(\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{A}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{B}}(x\cdot v)\right)+(1-B)\lvert x\rvert^{2}\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha-2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lvert v\rvert^{2}+\lfloor v\rceil^{(\beta-1)_{+}}\lvert x\rvert

for every
x,v\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. Whenever
\frac{(A+1)_{+}}{\alpha}+\frac{1-B}{2}\leq\ell
, there exist
\epsilon
small enough such that

(2.2)

H\asymp E^{\ell}.

We have,

\displaystyle\left|\nabla_{v}H\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim H^{2-\frac{2+\beta}{2\ell}}\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}+H^{\frac{2(A+1)}{\ell\alpha}}.

Finally, for any
\Phi\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
,

(2.3)

\mathcal{L}^{*}(\Phi(H))=\Phi^{\prime}(H)\mathcal{L}^{*}(H)+\Phi^{\prime\prime}(H)\lvert{\nabla_{v}}H\rvert^{2}.

Proof of
Lemma

2.1
.

Step 1.
We first prove the bound (
2.2
). Thanks to the Young inequality, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{A}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{B}}\left\lvert x\cdot v\right\rvert\leq\lfloor x\rceil^{1+A}\lfloor v\rceil^{1-B}\lesssim E^{\frac{(1+A)_{+}}{\alpha}}E^{\frac{1-B}{2}}\lesssim E^{\ell}

whenever,
\frac{(A+1)_{+}}{\alpha}+\frac{1-B}{2}\leq\ell
. Therefore, provided that
\epsilon>0
is small enough, we conclude that the bound (
2.2
) is satisfied.

We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}(E^{\ell})

\displaystyle=\left[(\ell-1)\frac{\lvert v\rvert^{2}}{E}+d+v\cdot\frac{{\nabla_{v}}\mathcal{M}}{\mathcal{M}}\right]\ell E^{\ell-1}

\displaystyle\leq\left[2(\ell-1)+d+v\cdot\frac{{\nabla_{v}}\mathcal{M}}{\mathcal{M}}\right]\ell E^{\ell-1}

Moreover

\displaystyle v\cdot{\nabla_{x}}\left(\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{A}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{B}}x\cdot v\right)

\displaystyle=v\cdot\left(\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{A}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{B}}v+A\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{A-2}(x\cdot v)x}{\lfloor v\rceil^{B}}\right)=\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{A}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{B}}\left(|v|^{2}+A\frac{(x\cdot v)^{2}}{\lfloor x\rceil^{2}}\right)

Interchanging the roles of
A
and
B
, we get similarly,

\displaystyle x\cdot{\nabla_{v}}\left(\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{A}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{B}}x\cdot v\right)

\displaystyle=x\cdot\left(\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{A}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{B}}x-B\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{A}(x\cdot v)\,v}{\lfloor v\rceil^{B+2}}\right)=\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{A}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{B}}\left(|x|^{2}-B\frac{(x\cdot v)^{2}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{2}}\right)

Now,

\displaystyle\Delta_{v}\left(\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{A}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{B}}x\cdot v\right)

\displaystyle=-B\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{A}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{B}}(x\cdot v)\left[\frac{d+2}{\lfloor v\rceil^{2}}-(B+2)\frac{\lvert v\rvert^{2}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{4}}\right].

Gathering all terms,

\displaystyle\frac{\lfloor v\rceil^{B}}{\lfloor x\rceil^{A}}\mathcal{L}^{*}\left(\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{A}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{B}}(x\cdot v)\right)

\displaystyle=|v|^{2}+A\frac{(x\cdot v)^{2}}{\lfloor x\rceil^{2}}-\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha-2}\left(|x|^{2}-B\frac{(x\cdot v)^{2}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-B(x\cdot v)\left[\frac{d+2}{\lfloor v\rceil^{2}}-(B+2)\frac{\lvert v\rvert^{2}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{4}}\right]+\left(x-B\frac{(x\cdot v)}{\lfloor v\rceil^{2}}v\right)\cdot\frac{{\nabla_{v}}\mathcal{M}}{\mathcal{M}}

We bound each term by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\lvert v\rvert^{2}+A\frac{(x\cdot v)^{2}}{\lfloor x\rceil^{2}}\leq(1+\lvert A\rvert)\lvert v\rvert^{2}\lesssim\lvert v\rvert^{2},

-\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha-2}\left(|x|^{2}-B\frac{(x\cdot v)^{2}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{2}}\right)\leq-(1-B)\,\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha-2}|x|^{2}

B(x\cdot v)\left[\frac{d+2}{\lfloor v\rceil^{2}}-(B+2)\frac{\lvert v\rvert^{2}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{4}}\right]\leq\lvert B\rvert(\lvert B\rvert+d+4)\lvert x\rvert\lesssim\lvert x\rvert

\left(x-B\frac{(x\cdot v)}{\lfloor v\rceil^{2}}v\right)\cdot\frac{{\nabla_{v}}\mathcal{M}}{\mathcal{M}}=(1+\lvert B\rvert)\lvert x\rvert\left\lvert\frac{{\nabla_{v}}\mathcal{M}}{\mathcal{M}}\right\rvert\lesssim\lvert x\rvert\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta-1}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\lfloor v\rceil^{B}}{\lfloor x\rceil^{A}}\mathcal{L}^{*}\left(\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{A}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{B}}(x\cdot v)\right)+(1-B)\lvert x\rvert^{2}\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha-2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lvert v\rvert^{2}+\lfloor v\rceil^{(\beta-1)_{+}}\lvert x\rvert.

Now, since

\displaystyle\nabla_{v}H=\ell E^{\ell-1}v+\epsilon\left(\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{A}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{B}}\,x-B\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{A}(x\cdot v)}{\lfloor v\rceil^{B+2}}\,v\right),

we have,

\displaystyle\left|\nabla_{v}H\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim E^{2(\ell-1)}|v|^{2}+\left(\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{A+1}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{B}}\right)^{2}\lesssim E^{2\ell-2}\lfloor v\rceil^{2-\beta}\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}+E^{\frac{2(A+1)}{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\lesssim E^{2\ell-2}E^{1-\frac{\beta}{2}}\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}+H^{\frac{2(A+1)}{\ell\alpha}}\lesssim H^{2-\frac{2+\beta}{2\ell}}\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}+H^{\frac{2(A+1)}{\ell\alpha}}

as we wanted. Finally, for any
\Phi\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, we have

\displaystyle{\nabla_{x}}(\Phi(H))

\displaystyle=\Phi^{\prime}(H){\nabla_{x}}H

\displaystyle{\nabla_{v}}(\Phi(H))

\displaystyle=\Phi^{\prime}(H){\nabla_{v}}H

and

\Delta_{v}(\Phi(H))=\Phi^{\prime\prime}(H)\lvert{\nabla_{v}}H\rvert^{2}+\Phi^{\prime}(H)\Delta_{v}H,

so (
2.3
) follows.

∎

2.2.
Sub-Gaussian local equilibrium.

The purpose of this section is to prove the following proposition, that provides a Lyapunov function with sub-exponential growth at infinity.

Proposition 2.2
.

Assume that the local equilibrium is given by (
1.3
). Define the function
H\colon{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
through,

(2.4)

H:(x,v)\mapsto E^{2}+\epsilon\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha a}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{1-b}}x\cdot v,

where
\epsilon>0
and
a,b\in(0,1)
be explicit parameters given later in the proof. Then,
H\asymp E^{2}
, and

(2.5)

\mathcal{L}^{*}H\lesssim C\mathds{1}_{B_{R}}-H^{\frac{1}{2}}\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}.

and for every
\theta\in(0,\min(1,\tfrac{\beta}{2})]
, there exists
\delta>0
small enough such that the function
m=\exp(\delta H^{\frac{\theta}{2}})
satisfies the Lyapunov condition

(2.6)

\mathcal{L}^{*}m\lesssim C\mathds{1}_{\lvert v\rvert\leq V,\,\lvert x\rvert\leq X}-m\,(\ln m)^{-\tfrac{1-\theta}{\theta}}.

for some constants
C,\,V,\,X>0
large enough.

Proof of
2.2
.

Observe importantly that
Lemma

2.1
ensures that
H\asymp E^{2}
for well chosen
\epsilon
since
a+\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{b}{2}\leq 2
when
a
and
b
are small enough.

Step 1.
We first prove (
2.5
). Thanks to
Lemma

2.1
with
A=a\alpha
,
B=1-b
,
\ell=2
and by substituting
\tfrac{{\nabla_{v}}\mathcal{M}}{\mathcal{M}}=-\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta-2}v
, we have a constant
C>0
such that,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}H=\mathcal{L}^{*}(E^{2})+\epsilon\mathcal{L}^{*}\left(\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha a}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{1-b}}x\cdot v\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left[2+d-\lvert v\rvert^{2}\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta-2}\right]E+\epsilon\lfloor v\rceil^{b-1}\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha a}\left[C\lvert v\rvert^{2}-b\lvert x\rvert^{2}\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha-2}+C\lfloor v\rceil^{(\beta-1)_{+}}\lvert x\rvert\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left[\mathds{1}_{|v|\leq V}-\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}\right]E-b\epsilon\lvert x\rvert^{2}\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha-2}\lfloor v\rceil^{b-1}\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha a}+C\epsilon\lfloor v\rceil^{b-1}E^{a}\left[\lfloor v\rceil^{2}+\lfloor v\rceil^{(\beta-1)_{+}}E^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right],

for some
V>0
large enough. Observe that,

\displaystyle\lfloor v\rceil^{b-1}E^{a}\left[\lfloor v\rceil^{2}+\lfloor v\rceil^{(\beta-1)_{+}}E^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right]

\displaystyle=\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}E\lfloor v\rceil^{b-\beta-1}E^{a-1}\left[\lfloor v\rceil^{2}+\lfloor v\rceil^{(\beta-1)_{+}}E^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right]

\displaystyle=\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}E\left[\lfloor v\rceil^{b-\beta+1}E^{a-1}+\lfloor v\rceil^{b-\beta-1+(\beta-1)_{+}}E^{a+\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}E\left[\lfloor v\rceil^{b-\beta+2a-1}+\lfloor v\rceil^{b-2}E^{a+\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}\right]\lesssim\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}E,

since the conditions
b+2a\leq\beta+1
,
b\leq 2
and
a\leq 1-\frac{1}{\alpha}
are satisfied for
a,b>0
small enough. Notice that a choice for
a>0
is possible if and only if
\alpha>1
. Last, observe that

\displaystyle\left[\mathds{1}_{|v|\leq V}-\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}\right]E

\displaystyle-b\epsilon\lvert x\rvert^{2}\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha-2}\lfloor v\rceil^{b-1}\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha a}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left[\mathds{1}_{|v|\leq V}-\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}\right]E-\epsilon\lvert x\rvert^{2}\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha-2}\lfloor v\rceil^{b-1}\mathds{1}_{|v|\leq V}\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha a}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left[\mathds{1}_{|v|\leq V}-\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}\right]E-\epsilon\lvert x\rvert^{2}\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha-2}\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha a}\mathds{1}_{|x|\geq X}\mathds{1}_{|v|\leq V}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathds{1}_{|v|\leq V}\mathds{1}_{|x|\leq X}-\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}E,

when
X
is large enough. The conclusion is obtained after taking
\epsilon
small.

Step 2.
We deduce (
2.6
). Let us now consider the function
m=\Phi\left(H\right):=\exp(\delta H^{\frac{\theta}{2}})
,
\theta\in\left(0,\min\left(1,\tfrac{\beta}{2}\right)\right]
and
\delta>0
. Recalling
Lemma

2.1
, we have

\mathcal{L}^{*}m=\Phi^{\prime}(H)\mathcal{L}^{*}(H)+\Phi^{\prime\prime}(H)\lvert{\nabla_{v}}H\rvert^{2}.

After direct computations,

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(H)=\frac{\delta\theta}{2}H^{\frac{\theta}{2}-1}\Phi(H)=\frac{\delta\theta}{2}H^{\frac{\theta}{2}-1}m

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime\prime}(H)=\frac{\delta\theta}{2}H^{\frac{\theta}{2}-1}\Phi^{\prime}(H)+\frac{\delta\theta}{2}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}-1\right)H^{\frac{\theta}{2}-2}\Phi(H)\leq\frac{\delta\theta}{2}H^{\frac{\theta}{2}-1}\Phi^{\prime}(H)

the latter since
\frac{\theta}{2}-1\leq 0
. We end up with,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}m

\displaystyle=\Phi^{\prime}(H)\mathcal{L}^{*}(H)+\Phi^{\prime\prime}(H)\lvert{\nabla_{v}}H\rvert^{2}\leq\Phi^{\prime}(H)\left(\mathcal{L}^{*}(H)+\frac{\delta\theta}{2}H^{\frac{\theta}{2}-1}\lvert{\nabla_{v}}H\rvert^{2}\right).

Recalling (
2.5
) to estimate the first and
Lemma

2.1
to estimate the last contribution of the above parenthesis, one gets

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}m

\displaystyle\lesssim\Phi^{\prime}(H)\left(C\mathds{1}_{B_{R}}-H^{\frac{1}{2}}\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}+\frac{\delta\theta}{2}H^{\frac{\theta}{2}-1}\left(H^{2-\frac{2+\beta}{4}}\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}+H^{\frac{\alpha a+1}{\alpha}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\Phi^{\prime}(H)\left(C\mathds{1}_{B_{R}}-H^{\frac{1}{2}}\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}\left(1-\frac{\delta\theta}{2}\left(H^{\frac{\theta}{2}-\frac{\beta}{4}}+\lfloor v\rceil^{-\beta}H^{a+\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{\theta}{2}-\frac{3}{2}}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\Phi^{\prime}(H)\left(C\mathds{1}_{B_{R}}-H^{\frac{1}{2}}\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}\right)

taking

sufficiently small, since
\frac{\theta}{2}-\frac{\beta}{4}\leq 0
,
\theta\leq 1
and

. Finally,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}m

\displaystyle\lesssim\Phi^{\prime}(H)\left(C\mathds{1}_{B_{R}}-H^{\frac{1}{2}}\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim H^{\frac{\theta}{2}-1}\left(C\mathds{1}_{B_{R}}-H^{\frac{1}{2}}\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}\right)m\lesssim C\mathds{1}_{B_{R}}-\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}H^{\frac{\theta-1}{2}}m.

As a conclusion, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}m

\displaystyle\lesssim C\mathds{1}_{B_{R}}-H^{\frac{\theta-1}{2}}\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}m\lesssim C\mathds{1}_{B_{R}}-m(H^{\frac{\theta}{2}})^{\frac{1-\theta}{\theta}}\leq C\mathds{1}_{B_{R}}-m\,(\ln m)^{-\frac{1-\theta}{\theta}},

which concludes the proof.
∎

2.3.
Fat-tailed local equilibrium

Proposition 2.3
.

Assume that the local equilibrium is given by (

) with
\gamma>1
. Let

for some

and

(2.8)

H\asymp E^{\ell}

and

(2.9)

\mathcal{L}^{*}H\lesssim C\mathds{1}_{\lvert v\rvert\leq V,\,\lvert x\rvert\leq X}-H^{1-\frac{1}{\ell}}.

for some constants
C,\,V,\,X>0
large enough. For every

, the function

satisfies the Lyapunov condition

(2.10)

\mathcal{L}^{*}m\lesssim C\mathds{1}_{\lvert v\rvert\leq V,\,\lvert x\rvert\leq X}-m^{1-\tfrac{1}{k}},

for some constants
C,\,V,\,X>0
large enough.

Proof of
2.3
.

Lemma

2.1
ensures that
H\asymp E^{\ell}
for
\epsilon
sufficiently small since

\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}+\ell-2+a\right)_{+}+\frac{b}{2}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}+\ell-2+a+\frac{b}{2}\leq\ell,

when
a
and
b
are small enough.

By substituting
\tfrac{{\nabla_{v}}\mathcal{M}}{\mathcal{M}}=-(d+\gamma)\lfloor v\rceil^{-2}v
and recalling
Lemma

2.1
, we have a constant
C>0
such that,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}H

\displaystyle\leq\left[2(\ell-1)+d-(d+\gamma)\lvert v\rvert^{2}\lfloor v\rceil^{-2}\right]\ell E^{\ell-1}+\epsilon\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha(\ell-2+a)}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{1-b}}\left(-b\lvert x\rvert^{2}\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha-2}+C\lvert v\rvert^{2}+C\lvert x\rvert\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left((d+\gamma)\lfloor v\rceil^{-2}-\left[\gamma-2(\ell-1)\right]\right)\ell E^{\ell-1}+\epsilon\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha(\ell-2+a)}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{1-b}}\left(-b\lvert x\rvert^{2}\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha-2}+C\lvert v\rvert^{2}+C\lvert x\rvert\right).

Since

, define

Observe that

\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha(\ell-2+a)}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{1-b}}\lvert v\rvert^{2}\leq\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha(\ell-2+a)}\lfloor v\rceil^{1+b}\leq E^{(\ell-2+a)_{+}}E^{\frac{1+b}{2}}\leq E^{\ell-1},

when either

or

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}H

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(C\mathds{1}_{|v|\leq V}-\frac{c}{2}\right)E^{\ell-1}+\epsilon\mathds{1}_{|v|\leq V}\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha(\ell-2)+\alpha a}}{\lfloor v\rceil^{1-b}}\left(-b\lvert x\rvert^{2}\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha-2}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(C\mathds{1}_{|v|\leq V}-\frac{c}{2}\right)E^{\ell-1}-\epsilon\mathds{1}_{|v|\leq V}\frac{\lfloor x\rceil^{\alpha(\ell-1)+\alpha a}}{\lfloor V\rceil^{1-b}}\mathds{1}_{|x|\geq X}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(C\mathds{1}_{|v|\leq V}-\frac{c}{2}\right)E^{\ell-1}-\epsilon\mathds{1}_{|v|\leq V}\frac{E^{\ell-1}\lfloor X\rceil^{\alpha a}}{\lfloor V\rceil^{1-b}}\mathds{1}_{|x|\geq X},

for

large enough, from which one concludes

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}H\lesssim C\mathds{1}_{|v|\leq V}\mathds{1}_{|x|\leq X}-E^{\ell-1},

that is exactly (
2.9
) thanks to the equivalence (
2.8
).

Let

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}m

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{\ell}H^{\frac{k}{\ell}-1}\Big(\mathcal{L}^{*}H+\Big(\tfrac{k}{\ell}-1\Big)\frac{\lvert{\nabla_{v}}H\rvert^{2}}{H}\Big)\lesssim H^{\frac{k}{\ell}-1}\Big(C\mathds{1}_{\lvert v\rvert\leq V,\,\lvert x\rvert\leq X}-H^{1-\tfrac{1}{\ell}}\lfloor v\rceil^{\beta}\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim C\mathds{1}_{\lvert v\rvert\leq V,\,\lvert x\rvert\leq X}-H^{\frac{k-1}{\ell}}\lesssim C\mathds{1}_{\lvert v\rvert\leq V,\,\lvert x\rvert\leq X}-m^{\frac{k-1}{k}}.

3.
About the positivity condition

In this section, we prove the positivity condition needed to apply the Harris theorem. We follow the idea of
[
8
]
, which gets the positivity condition as a direct consequence of the Harnack inequality. We denote by
S_{\mathcal{L}}
the semigroup associated to (
1.1
).

Proposition 3.1
.

For any
0<T_{0}<T_{1}
and compact set
\mathcal{C}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}
, there holds

(3.1)

\sup_{\mathcal{C}}S_{\mathcal{L}}(T_{0})f_{0}\leq C\inf_{\mathcal{C}}S_{\mathcal{L}}(T_{1})f_{0},

for some constant
C=C(T_{0},T_{1},\mathcal{C})>0
.

The proof is based on a local version of the Harnack inequality and an iteration argument in order to obtain (
3.1
). The local Harnack inequality has been obtained in
[
27
]
by De Giorgi methods, see also
[
25
]
for previous results,
[
3
, Theorem 5.1]
for seminal considerations in this topic, together with the Harnack inequality proved by Lanconelli and Polidoro in
[
39
, Theorem 5.1]
. The iteration argument is taken from
[
40
]
, and is an adaptation of the ideas from
[
1
]
. We recall the main result of
[
27
]
. Consider the following partial differential equation

(3.2)

\partial_{t}g=\mathfrak{M}g:=-v\cdot{\nabla_{x}}g+\Delta_{v}g+b\cdot{\nabla_{v}}g,\qquad t\in\mathbb{R},x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},v\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
b=b(x,v)
is a measurable and locally bounded vector field. The class of equations (
3.2
) is invariant under the set of transformations

\mathcal{T}_{\eta,z_{0}}\colon(t,x,v)\mapsto(t_{0}+\eta^{2}t,x_{0}+\eta^{3}x+\eta^{2}tv_{0},v_{0}+\eta v),

where
r>0
and
z=(t_{0},x_{0},v_{0})\in\mathbb{R}\times{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. For this reason, we define for
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\times{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}
and
r>0
the set

Q_{r}(z_{0}):=\Big\{-r^{2}<t-t_{0}\leq 0,\lvert x-x_{0}-(t-t_{0})v_{0}\rvert<r^{3},\lvert v-v_{0}\rvert<r\Big\}

and observe that
\mathcal{T}_{\eta,z_{0}}(Q_{r}(0,0,0))=Q_{\eta r}(z_{0})
. The following theorem is taken from
[
27
, Thm. 5 & Prop. 12]
but it is rewritten as in
[
8
]
using the invariant transformations and the local boundedness of

.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
z_{0}=(t_{0},x_{0},v_{0})
with
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
(x_{0},v_{0})\in\mathcal{C}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}
a compact subset. We have the following two results.

(i)

There is
\zeta>0
such that any non-negative weak (super-)solution
g
to (
3.2
) in
Q_{1}
satisfies

\lVert g\rVert_{\mathsf{L}^{\zeta}(\widetilde{Q}_{r_{0}}^{-}(z_{0}))}\lesssim\inf_{Q_{r_{0}}(z_{0})}g

where

and
\widetilde{Q}^{-}_{r_{0}}(z_{0}):=\mathcal{T}_{1,z_{0}}(Q_{r_{0}}(-\tau,0,0))=Q_{r_{0}}(z_{0}-(\tau,\tau v_{0},0))

(ii)

Let

be a non-negative weak sub-solution to (

\lVert h\rVert_{\mathsf{L}^{\infty}(Q_{r}(z_{0}))}\lesssim C\lVert h\rVert_{\mathsf{L}^{\zeta}(Q_{R}(z_{0}))}

with

.

3.1
.

Step 1: Local Harnack inequality
.
We prove that for any

\partial_{t}f=-v\cdot{\nabla_{x}}f+{\nabla_{x}}V\cdot{\nabla_{v}}f+\Delta_{v}f-\frac{{\nabla_{v}}\mathcal{M}}{\mathcal{M}}\cdot{\nabla_{v}}f-{\nabla_{v}}\cdot\left(\frac{{\nabla_{v}}\mathcal{M}}{\mathcal{M}}\right)f,

and for any

(3.4)

\sup_{\widetilde{Q}^{-}_{r_{0}/2}(z_{0})}f\lesssim\inf_{Q_{r_{0}}(z_{0})}f,

. Notice that for
\mathcal{M}
as in (
1.3
) or as in (

\left\lVert{\nabla_{v}}\cdot\left(\frac{{\nabla_{v}}\mathcal{M}}{\mathcal{M}}\right)\right\rVert_{\mathsf{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{C})}=:\lambda<\infty.

We define

\displaystyle\partial_{t}g=\mathfrak{M}g+\left(\lambda-{\nabla_{v}}\cdot\left(\frac{{\nabla_{v}}\mathcal{M}}{\mathcal{M}}\right)\right)g\geq\mathfrak{M}g,

(i)

\lVert g\rVert_{\mathsf{L}^{\zeta}(\widetilde{Q}_{r_{0}}^{-}(z_{0}))}\lesssim\inf_{Q_{r_{0}}(z_{0})}g

which, minimizing

and maximizing it over

\lVert f\rVert_{\mathsf{L}^{\zeta}(\widetilde{Q}^{-}_{r_{0}}(z_{0}))}\lesssim\inf_{Q_{r_{0}}(z_{0})}f.

On the other hand, let us define

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h=\mathfrak{M}h-\left(\lambda+{\nabla_{v}}\cdot\left(\frac{{\nabla_{v}}\mathcal{M}}{\mathcal{M}}\right)\right)h\leq\mathfrak{M}h\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{1+2d}

)
. Once the local Harnack inequality (

.

For any
M>0
and for any

, there exists a nonzero measure

such that

S_{\mathcal{L}}(T_{1})f_{0}\geq\nu\int_{\mathcal{C}}S_{\mathcal{L}}(T_{0})f_{0},

,

.

3.3
.

We know that for any

and any compact set

We deduce that

\displaystyle S_{\mathcal{L}}(T_{1})f_{0}\geq\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{C}}\inf_{\mathcal{C}}S_{\mathcal{L}}(T_{1})f_{0}\geq\frac{\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{C}}}{C}(\sup_{\mathcal{C}}S_{\mathcal{L}}(T_{0})f_{0})\geq\frac{\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{C}}}{C\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert}\int_{\mathcal{C}}S_{\mathcal{L}}(T_{0})f_{0}.

5.
Numerical results

This section is devoted to numerical simulations of the kinetic Fokker-Planck equation (
1.1
). In particular, we are interested in describing the shape of the steady state for some choices of parameter
\alpha
and
\beta
in the strip
(\alpha,\beta)\in[1,\infty)\times(0,2)
.

5.1.
Description of the algorithm

The simulations in this work are obtained using the finite volume numerical scheme presented in
[
44
]
and the code has been written in Python
3
3

3

The code is available at
https://github.com/luca-ziviani/KineticFokkerPlanck_Python
. For simplicity, we restrict ourselves to the one dimensional case
d=1
. We considered a rectangular domain
(x,v)\in[-L,L]\times[-V,V]
, with
L,V>0
large enough, and we divided the intervals into cells with a uniform subdivision of size
\Delta x=\tfrac{2L}{N_{x}}
and
\Delta v=\tfrac{2V}{N_{v}}
, where
N_{x}
and
N_{v}
are even positive numbers. The coordinates of the cell centres are
x_{n}=-L+(n+\tfrac{1}{2})\Delta x
for
n=0,\dots,N_{x}-1
and similarly
v_{m}=-V+(m+\tfrac{1}{2})\Delta v
for
m=0,\dots,N_{v}-1
. The volume element is

\omega_{n,m}=[x_{n}-\tfrac{\Delta x}{2},x_{n}+\tfrac{\Delta x}{2}]\times[v_{m}-\tfrac{\Delta v}{2},v_{m}+\tfrac{\Delta v}{2}],

whose volume is
\lvert\omega_{n,m}\rvert=\Delta x\Delta v
. Concerning the time coordinate, we fix
T>0
large and partition the interval
[0,T]
in
N_{t}>1
sub-intervals of length
\Delta t=\tfrac{T}{N_{t}}
, so we have the points
t_{k}=k\Delta t
, for
k=0,\dots,N_{t}
. We approximate a function
f=f(t,x,v)
on points
(x_{n},v_{m})
at time
t_{k}
by the average

f_{n,m}^{k}=\frac{1}{\lvert\omega_{n,m}\rvert}\int_{\omega_{n,m}}f(t_{k},x,v)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v.

The main idea of a finite volume scheme is to compute the flux through each side of every element volume
\omega_{n,m}
and use it to compute
\{f_{n,m}^{k+1}\}_{n,m}
from the previous state
\{f_{n,m}^{k}\}_{n,m}
. In
[
44
]

v\cdot{\nabla_{x}}f
, the Kurganov-Tadmor flux
[
37
]
is implemented, for the remaining differential operator in the velocity variable a generalised Chang-Cooper
[
12
]

\{-L,L\}\times[-V,V]
and zero-flux on the sides
[-L,L]\times\{-V,V\}
. Finally, the two types of flux are put together by a Strang splitting, and the evolution in time is performed by a Runge-Kutta method of order 2.

Since we are trying to simulate an unbounded domain
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
as a very large rectangle
[-L,L]\times[-V,V]
, we expect to see some perturbation of the solution for points near the boundary. Nevertheless, we hope to see our results confirmed at least for points relatively close to the origin.

5.3.
Level plots of the steady state

In this section we aim to compare steady state
G
of the kinetic Fokker-Planck equation and the function
\widetilde{G}(x,v)=\exp(-\delta E^{\frac{\beta}{2}})
suggested by
1.1
. We considered the final state

of the numerical simulation as an approximation of
G

At first, we would like to check whether the approximated steady state

resemble a function of the type
(x,v)\mapsto\Gamma(E(x,v))
for a certain profile
\Gamma\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
. To verify this, it is sufficient to check if all the points of the type
(E(x,v),f(T,x,v))
with
(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
fall approximately on the same curve of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(i.e. on a parametric curve in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
of the form
s\mapsto(s,\Gamma(s)))
. In
Figure

5.3
we consider
\alpha=1.5
and
\beta=0.5
. In the left picture, we show a scatter plot of all points of the type
(E(x,v),f(T,x,v))
for all
(x,v)
of the discretized domain
[-L,L]\times[-V,V]
. We observe that these points (in black) tend to gather around the same curve. Furthermore, by plotting the points of the type

5.3

and
