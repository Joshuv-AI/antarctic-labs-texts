Multidimensional MRI radiomics-based model predicts recurrence risk in triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Zirui Wang, Teng Ma, Yifan Li, Xinyi Sun, Yongmei Wang, Yan Mao, Tianyi Ma, Xu Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patients with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) often have a poor prognosis. Existing staging systems cannot accurately assess patients’ recurrence risk, due to the tumor’s significant heterogeneity. Given the lack of a reliable prognostic assessment method, this study aimed to propose a combined multimodal prognostic model. We systematically integrated the clinicopathological indicators with multi-scale radiomics features in a large-scale TNBC cohort (n = 700). Remarkably, “intratumoral habitat heterogeneity analysis” and “peritumoral microenvironment characteristics” were innovatively incorporated in our model to reflect the potential biological behavior of individual tumors. The best consistency index (C-index) illuminated the superior prognostic capabilities of the combined model. Time-dependent ROC analysis demonstrated robust AUC at the 1-, 3-, and 5-year disease-free survival (DFS). Overall, our predictive model could provide a more comprehensive description of the biological aggressiveness of TNBC, overcoming the limitations of traditional staging systems in individualized prognosis assessment and better assisting doctors in developing personalized treatment and follow-up plans.

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) accounts for 10-20% of all breast cancer cases and is among the most aggressive subtypes, with a high risk of metastasis1–3. Patients with TNBC do not benefit from endocrine or targeted therapies because of the lack of ER, PR, and HER2 expression and show strong resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy; therefore, recurrent TNBC usually progresses rapidly4,5. Accurate assessment of recurrence risk is therefore critical for guiding treatment and prognostic monitoring.

Currently, TNBC recurrence risk assessment primarily relies on the clinical and pathological staging systems6. However, due to the pronounced heterogeneity of TNBC, staging frameworks such as the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) are unable to identify tumor heterogeneity and the specific outcomes it leads to, and are unable to accurately assess the recurrence risk of patients7–11. This underscores the urgent need for a new prognostic assessment method that can address tumor heterogeneity in TNBC.

Radiomics is a high-throughput data mining and analysis method based on medical images, which extracts multiple quantitative features extracted from imaging data and analyzed them using artificial intelligence techniques12,13. It has been widely applied in cancer diagnosis, prognosis prediction, and treatment response assessment14. Habitat analysis is an emerging approach that identifies different tumor regions or cell subpopulations through the clustering of image features, which can extract image features of different tumor subregions individually and then perform a more accurate integrated analysis of information from multiple dimensions15,16. In addition to tumor regions that reflect intrinsic tumor phenotype, peritumor region also contains valuable information related to tumor development and metastasis17,18. For example, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of peritumor can reveal lymphocyte infiltration, peritumor lymphatic infiltration, and lymphangiogenesis, which are also important biomarkers4,19,20.

The extraction of high-dimensional quantitative features from the MRI of TNBC patients offers a promising strategy for TNBC recurrence risk assessment. The study aimed to develop a recurrence risk prediction model for TNBC patients based on habitat-derived MRI features, the radiomics features of the tumor and its surrounding areas, and combined with clinicopathology data. This collection includes 14 form features, 360 first-order features, and a wide variety of textural characteristics. We first conducted a repeatability analysis on the extracted features. Based on 30 randomly selected cases, we calculated the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for each feature extracted by two observers. Features with ICC > 0.85 were retained for subsequent analysis. Features selection

We first performed correlation filtering on 1834 features (Pearson correlation coefficient r > 0.9), removing highly collinear features from the initial 1834 radiomics features extracted from habitat subregions, retaining 1125 features. Subsequently, univariate Cox regression analysis (p < 0.05) was performed to evaluate the association of each of the 1125 features with progression-free survival. Features with statistically significant associations were retained, ultimately yielding 64 features. Finally, L1-regularized Cox regression was applied to these 64 features for refinement and coefficient shrinkage. This process yielded 34 habitat radiomics features (λ = 0.019), 9 peri1mm radiomics features (λ = 0.114), and 16 traditional radiomics features (λ = 0.018) for the final model construction. The Supplementary Figs. 3-5 illustrates the distribution of these feature categories graphically, providing a visual summary of their prevalence across the dataset.

Model establishment

In the training cohort, the combined multimodal model had the highest consistency index (C-index) of 0.887 (95% CI: 0.859-0.915), demonstrating prognostic performance. It was superior to the habitat model alone (C-index: 0.809 (95% CI: 0.774-0.844)), the radiomics model (C-index: 0.759 (95% CI: 0.721-0.797)), and the clinical model (C-index: 0.677 (95% CI: 0.636-0.718)). Similarly, the same results were obtained in the validation cohort, with the combined model also showing the highest C-index of 0.818 (95% CI: 0.766-0.870), confirming its generalizability. Discussion

Patients with TNBC often have poor prognosis. The TNM staging system is often used to assess patient prognosis and guide treatment choices. However, significant tumor heterogeneity among TNBC patients, and patients with the same TNM staging may have significantly different prognoses, and existing clinical staging systems or prognostic models do not accurately reflect the biological behavior of tumors23–25. In this study, the performance of the pure clinical model based on the AJCC 8th edition staging system was suboptimal, with a C-index of 0.677 in the training cohort and 0.702 in the validation cohort. To address this challenge, we developed and validated an innovative prediction model based on multidimensional MRI features that can effectively stratify the risk of recurrence in patients with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). The combined model, which integrates tumor habitat features, peritumoral radiomics features, and key clinical pathological indicators, demonstrated significantly better predictive performance than any single-dimensional model, showing excellent performance and good generalization ability in both the training (C-index: 0.887) and validation (C-index: 0.818) sets. This result strongly proves that through multidimensional data integration, we can more comprehensively depict the biological aggressiveness of TNBC, thereby overcoming the limitations of traditional TNM staging in individualized prognostic evaluation.

A particularly noteworthy and inspiring finding is that the radiomics model based solely on the peritumoral area (especially the 1 mm range) (Peri1mm Model) demonstrated superior predictive ability (training set/validation set C-index: 0.835/0.815) that is even better than the traditional radiomics model that only focuses on the tumor itself (Radiomics Model, C-index: 0.759/0.626). This phenomenon strongly suggests that the invasion and metastasis potential of a tumor is not only determined by its internal characteristics, but its “dialogue” interface with the host microenvironment, that is, the peritumoral area, may play a more critical role. The radiomics features of the peritumoral area can reflect key biological processes such as tumor angiogenesis, lymphatic infiltration, and infiltration of immune cells (such as lymphocytes)18. Our model quantifies microscopic changes that are invisible to the naked eye, proving that the microenvironmental state at the front of the tumor invasion is a crucial factor in determining the prognosis of patients with TNBC.

Previous studies have confirmed the potential of radiomics in predicting DFS or treatment response in breast cancer26–29. However, our study is the first to systematically integrate the three dimensions of “intratumoral habitat heterogeneity analysis”, “peritumoral microenvironment characteristics, and “ clinicopathological indicators” in a large-scale TNBC cohort (N = 700) to construct a prognostic model. We demonstrated the superiority of this multidimensional combined modeling strategy through a rigorous head-to-head comparison, which is an important advancement. Subsequently, the constructed nomogram provided clinicians with an intuitive and easy-to-use individualized risk assessment tool. For newly diagnosed TNBC patients, doctors can use this model to calculate the specific recurrence risk at one, three, and five years. For patients with a score of “high risk,” clinical consideration may be given to upgrading adjuvant therapy or recommending them to join new drug clinical trials; while for “low-risk” patients, it may support us to make more prudent treatment decisions, thereby avoiding overtreatment and its toxic side effects, and truly achieving precise and individualized management of TNBC.

Our study differs from previous research in several respects. First, prior studies have largely been limited to single intratumoral features or simple peritumoral regions. This study is the first to systematically integrate intratumoral habitat heterogeneity, the peritumoral microenvironment, and clinical-pathological indicators. This multimodal framework of “intratumoral heterogeneity, external microenvironment, and clinical phenotype” aims to provide a more comprehensive characterization of the biological essence of high recurrence risk in TNBC. Second, we conducted a careful comparison across multiple models, demonstrating that comprehensive habitat-based modeling outperforms models relying solely on imaging and pathological data, as well as clinical predictive models. Third, by innovatively incorporating HER2 status and tumor status into the clinical data, we explored their prognostic value in TNBC.

This study had some limitations. First, as a retrospective single-center study, the exclusion of patients with non-standardized treatment regimens and those presenting with distant metastases at initial diagnosis resulted in a more standardized treatment approach among the enrolled cohort. Consequently, our findings may overestimate survival rates under current standard treatments. We plan to conduct prospective multicenter studies for validation to ensure external generalizability. Second, immunotherapy represents a major advancement in the treatment of TNBC, and the patients in the study were treated before the data from the Keynote522 study30; therefore, this group of patients had not yet been routinely treated with immunotherapy, which may have affected the survival data. Finally, the current model depends on manual segmentation by radiologists, which limits the efficiency of its clinical application. The development of automated and standardized segmentation tools will be essential for broader clinical promotion and future application.

Methods

Study design

We combined conventional radiomics with habitat analysis-based radiomics to extract a compressive set of features from tumor region of interest (ROI) and their internal subregions. Specific feature screening approaches were used to eliminate redundancy and retain variables with significant prognostic value. These features were then used to construct a recurrence risk model. Based on the median recurrence risk score, the patients were divided into high- and low-risk groups, and differences in survival were assessed. In addition, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis over time was performed to assess the discriminative performance of the model at the 1-, 3-, and 5-year Disease-free-survival (DFS). The workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Patients and clinical data collection

We retrospectively collected clinical, pathological, and imaging data from patients with breast cancer treated at the Qingdao University Affiliated Hospital between August 2012 and August 2020. The inclusion criteria were :(1) pathologically confirmed TNBC (hormone receptor (HR)-negative and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative); (2) underwent breast surgery treatment; (3) Complete dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (DCE-MRI) within 2 weeks prior to surgery; (4) receive follow-up treatment according to appropriate guidelines after surgery; and (5) having complete clinical and pathologic information and 36 months or more of follow-up data. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) presence of other malignant tumors, (2) evidence of distant metastasis at initial diagnosis (pathological biopsy or PET/CT demonstrating distant metastasis), (3) diagnosis of non-primary BC, and (4) occult breast cancer.

Clinical and pathological data included age at diagnosis, menopausal status, Tstage, Nstage, pathology type, histologic grade, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), HER2 status, Ki67 expression, surgical method, and survival results. A 15% expression criterion was used to divide TILs into groups with high and low expression levels21. HER2-low was defined as an HER2 score of 1 or 2 according to immunohistochemistry with an amplified in situ hybridization assay, and HER2-0 was defined as an HER2 score of 0 according to the same evaluation22. A 20% cutoff was used to categorize Ki-67 into high and low expression groups. In accordance with the, T and N staging were determined according to the based on 8th edition of the AJCC TNM classification postoperative pathological findings11. DFS was defined as the interval between the completion of the primary procedure and occurrence of metastases and recurrence. Interval between primary surgery conclusion and onset of metastasis and recurrence. Recurrence and metastasis were diagnosed based on histologic evidence or evidence from adjuvant ultrasonography, cerebral magnetic resonance imaging, computed tomography (CT), radionuclide bone scan, or positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET)/CT. se In August 2024, the last follow-up was conducted. Follow-up information was collected through text messages, phone calls, and outpatient reviews. This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University (approval number: QYFY-WZLL-30243). Informed consent was obtained from all subjects.

Image acquisition and post-processing

All MRI examinations were completed within 2 weeks before the start of surgery, and the MRI configuration parameters are described in the Supplementary Table 3. All the patients were placed in the prone position. Imaging sequences included T2-weighted images (T2WI), and dynamic enhancement scans (DCE). The contrast agent Gd-DTPA (0.2 mmol/kg, flow rate 2.0 ml/S) was injected after plain scanning. The first enhancement scan was performed within 60 s, and then the enhancement scans were performed at 90, 180, 270, and 360 time points after injection. Dynamic-enhancement scanning images were acquired.

Images from the first-time node in the patient’s DCE-MRI were selected and resampled to obtain uniform voxel spacing (1 × 1 × 1 mm³); N4 bias field correction was performed using the Python package SimpleITK (v2.0.2); Bilinear interpolation was applied to the imaging data, while nearest-neighbor interpolation was used for label resampling to preserve boundary integrity.

Two radiologists with more than five years of breast MRI diagnostic experience used ITK-SNAP (v4.0.2) to manually segment the processed images individually, outlining the primary tumor region layer-by-layer while avoiding necrotic and liquefied areas of the tumor, enabling the acquisition of a comprehensive ROI (Fig. 5A. Peritumoral region expansion

To examine the effect of the Peritumoral Region on the predictive power of the model, we extended the peritumoral zone around the tumor by 1, 3, and 5 mm. With the help of the mask padding tools on the OnekeyAI platform, this enlargement was carried out utilizing a radial dilation approach. The dilatation of the peritumoral areas is shown in Fig. 5C.

Habitat subregion generation

We used a habitat analysis technique that included unsupervised clustering and superpixel segmentation to describe the intratumoral variability. One hundred spatially coherent superpixels were created from each tumor area using the Simple Linear Iterative Clustering (SLIC) method, which clusters pixels based on their color similarity and spatial proximity in the image space. The compactness parameter was empirically set to 10 based on preliminary visual inspections to ensure optimal adherence to internal tumor boundaries without over-fragmentation. To capture the spatial organization, texture, and intensity-related properties indicative of the tumor microenvironment, 19 radiomic features—including shape descriptors, textural features, and first-order statistical attributes— were retrieved from each superpixel.

Based on their radiomic similarity, these characteristics were used as inputs for k-means clustering, which divides the superpixels into distinct subregions by iteratively updating centroids to minimize the within-cluster sum of squares. A biologically realistic depiction of the internal heterogeneity of the tumor was made possible by merging the superpixels within the same cluster to create spatially connected habitat zones. Using this technique, we identified unique phenotypic niches inside the tumor that might be associated with diverse biological activities. To ensure maximum intracluster compactness and intercluster separability, the Calinski-Harabasz (CH) index was evaluated over a range of cluster counts (3–10) to establish the ideal number of clusters. Scikit-learn (v1.0.2) was used to implement all the processes. Figure 5D illustrates the representative habitat areas.

Radiomics feature extraction

Each ROI and its derived habitat area underwent its own radiomic feature extraction process. Before calculating the features, we conducted several image modifications, such as wavelet decomposition and Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) filtering, to improve the robustness and variety of the derived features. Shape descriptors, which quantify geometric qualities; first-order statistics, which represent voxel intensity distributions; and texture matrices, including GLCM, GLRLM, GLSZM, and NGTDM, which record spatial and structural relationships, are among the feature categories.

A K-nearest neighbor (KNN)-based approach was utilized to guarantee full labeling and maintain spatial coherence for voxels that were not assigned to any cluster during habitat formation. To ensure repeatability and scientific rigor, the PyRadiomics software (v3.0.1) was used to extract all features in accordance with the Imaging Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI) guidelines. Further details are provided in the Supplementary Fig. 6.

Radiomics feature selection

A multistep feature selection pipeline was used to reduce overfitting and preserve the most important variables for survival modeling. Extremely collinear characteristics (threshold > 0.9) were removed using Pearson’s correlation analysis. Characteristics with the highest prognostic importance (determined by the univariate Cox p-value) were maintained within each connected group. In this process, biological diversity was maintained, although dimensionality decreased. Using univariate Cox proportional hazards analysis, each attribute was evaluated separately for its relationship with DFS. For additional modeling, features with statistically significant relationships (p < 0.05) were retained. L1-regularized LASSO-Cox regression, which combines variable selection and coefficient shrinkage, was used to improve the final features. A parsimonious yet robust feature set was obtained by using 10-fold cross-validation to determine the ideal regularization value (λ) in order to minimize the partial likelihood deviation.

Prediction model establishment

Using the selected characteristics, prognostic models based on Cox proportional hazards regression were created. After a thorough feature selection procedure, the features obtained from the tumor ROI were used to build the Radiomics Model. After a thorough feature selection procedure, features taken solely from the created peritumoral ROI were used to develop the perix mm, where x represents the tumoral margin. To capture intratumoral heterogeneity, the Habitat Model included radiomic characteristics obtained from spatially clustered subregions. Univariate and multivariate Cox analyses were conducted on the clinical variables to determine the independent predictive factors for the Clinical Model. Ultimately, the Habitat Signature was combined with a few clinical indicators from multivariate analysis in the Combined Model, which produced a thorough framework for risk assessment and was shown as a nomogram for personalized disease-free survival prediction.

Patients were categorized into high- and low-risk groups according to the optimal cut-off point set by the median risk score. Kaplan-Meier survival curves and the log-rank test were used to evaluate differences in survival across risk groups. Time-dependent ROC curves were constructed to assess model discrimination at the three- and 5-year follow-up intervals.

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous data. For data that were regularly distributed, parametric comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test; in other cases, the Mann-Whitney U test was used. Table 1 summarizes baseline characteristics. The proportional hazards (PH) assumption of the Cox regression models was validated using Schoenfeld residual tests and visual inspection of residual plots. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Python (v3.7.12), Onekey (v5.4.27), Scikit-learn (v1.0.2) and R (v4.5.3) were used for all statistical analyses. The deep learning processes were performed on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU using PyTorch (v1.8.1) and MONAI (v0.8.1).
