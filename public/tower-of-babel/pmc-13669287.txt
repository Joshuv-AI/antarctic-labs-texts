Targeting P2Y14R inhibits proliferation of cervical cancer cells and organoids and activates cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling pathway
Authors: Rui Li, Hai-Yun Xiong, Shi-Yu Chen, Yong Tang, Wen-Bin Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purinergic signaling has been widely recognized as a critical regulator of tumor progression, yet the role of P2Y14R in cervical cancer remains unclear. This study used the P2Y14R antagonist PPTN in in vitro cell experiments; the results showed that it significantly inhibited cell proliferation and increased LDH release. The anti-tumor effect of P2Y14R antagonism was further validated using a cervical cancer organoid model. Mechanistically, PPTN treatment elevated intracellular cAMP levels, enhanced the phosphorylation of PKA and CREB, and thereby activated the cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling axis. Moreover, PPTN intervention reduced Ki67 expression in cervical cancer organoids without affecting CK13 expression, suggesting that the anti-tumor effect of PPTN may not mediated through regulation of differentiation. Collectively, these findings identify P2Y14R as a novel regulator of the cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling pathway in cervical cancer cells, providing a promising therapeutic target for cervical cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11302-026-10192-0.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer among women worldwide and represents one of the most frequent malignancies affecting female longevity, particularly in developing countries [1]. Each year, over 500,000 women are newly diagnosed with cervical cancer globally, and more than 300,000 women die from this disease [2]. The development of cervical cancer is mainly attributed to human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, which is associated with over 95% of cases [3]. Among the various HPV subtypes, HPV-16 and HPV-18 are the predominant pathogenic subtypes, accounting for at least 70% of all cervical cancer cases [3]. The clinical prognosis of cervical cancer is highly dependent on early screening and diagnosis, with a five-year survival rate exceeding 90% for patients diagnosed at an early stage. However, once tumor recurrence or distant metastasis occurs, patient outcomes deteriorate significantly, accompanied by a substantial reduction in survival rate [2]. Therefore, identifying promising therapeutic targets or potential novel drugs for the treatment of cervical cancer remains a major challenge.

In recent years, accumulating evidence has indicated that purinergic signaling has emerged as a promising therapeutic target in various diseases [4]. Purinergic receptors are expressed in tumor cells of diverse origins, tumor-infiltrating inflammatory cells, and supporting stromal cells, enabling them to regulate tumor growth through multiple mechanisms [5, 6]. G protein-coupled P2Y receptors are an important subtype of purinergic receptors. They do not exert a single regulatory effect in tumors, but present distinct dual effects of oncogenesis and tumor suppression [7]. Studies on breast and prostate cancers have illustrated that P2Y receptors enhance the invasive and metastatic capacity of tumor cells by regulating cell migration and chemotaxis, and they also participate in the construction of the metastatic microenvironment in breast cancer [8, 9]. On the contrary, other investigations have demonstrated that activation of P2Y1R or P2Y2R inhibits the proliferation of nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells and induces apoptosis in human colon cancer cells [10, 11]. Human cervical cancer cells have been widely utilized to investigate the role of purinergic signaling in tumors [12]. Activation of P2Y1R promotes the proliferation of HeLa cells [13], while UTP and ATP activate P2Y2R to enhance cervical cancer cell proliferation [14]. P2Y6R is associated with the regulation of HeLa cell migration [12].

P2Y14R is extensively involved in various inflammatory responses and multisystem pathological processes [15]. Activation of P2Y14R reduces IL-6 production and inhibits the proliferation of glioma GL261 cells [16]. Upon activation, P2Y14R couples to the Gi protein, which inhibits the adenylate cyclase (AC) pathway and subsequently downregulates intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) levels, thereby triggering corresponding biological effects [17]. Therefore, pharmacological antagonism of P2Y14R may theoretically increase intracellular cAMP levels. The primary downstream effector of cAMP is protein kinase A (PKA), which, upon activation, phosphorylates various substrate proteins, including the transcription factor CREB [18]. The cAMP/PKA/CREB axis plays a complex and context-dependent role in cancer, promoting either cell survival or growth arrest depending on the cellular environment and signaling dynamics [19]. However, the role of P2Y14R in the initiation and progression of cervical cancer remains unclear, and further investigation is urgently needed.

Results

PPTN inhibits the proliferation of HeLa cells, SiHa cells and cervical cancer organoids in vitro

The P2Y14 receptor is clearly expressed in cervical cancer tissue, HeLa and SiHa cells, and cervical cancer organoids (supplementary Fig. 1A-B). To investigate whether P2Y14R participates in the onset and progression of cervical cancer, HeLa and SiHa cells were incubated with the P2Y14R antagonist PPTN for 6, 12 and 24 h. CCK-8 cell proliferation assays demonstrated that PPTN exerted an inhibitory effect on the growth of HeLa and SiHa cells at all detected time points (Fig. 1A). The IC50 values of PPTN were subsequently quantified: in HeLa cells, the values were 22.72 μM, 14.66 μM and 6.869 μM at 6 h, 12 h and 24 h, respectively; in SiHa cells, the values were 20.71 μM, 19.71 μM and 10.66 μM. We selected the 1/2 IC50 value for each cell type at 24 h as the intervention concentration and rounded the calculated results to the nearest whole number. Therefore, the actual PPTN concentrations used to treat HeLa and SiHa cells were 3 μM, and 5 μM, respectively. Colony formation assays verified that PPTN significantly impaired the colony-forming capacity of HeLa and SiHa cells (Fig. 1B). We next treated cervical cancer organoids with PPTN for 72 h. Results showed obvious apoptosis in organoids after 72 h of treatment. Organoid viability assays confirmed that PPTN greatly inhibited organoid growth (Fig. 1C), and the IC50 of PPTN against cervical cancer organoids was determined. For subsequent organoid experiments, half of this IC50 value was used, rounded to the nearest whole number, resulting in 17 μM PPTN. LDH release assays demonstrated that PPTN disrupted the membrane integrity of HeLa cells, SiHa cells and cervical cancer organoids. Notably, the maximal cytotoxic effect in HeLa and SiHa cells occurred at 6 h post-treatment (Fig. 1D). Immunofluorescence staining was performed to detect the expression of Ki67 and CK13 in organoids. The results showed that PPTN treatment decreased the fluorescence intensity of Ki67, whereas no obvious change was observed in CK13 expression (Fig. 1E). We found that PPTN significantly increased intracellular cAMP concentrations in both the cellular and organoid models (Fig. 2A). Discussion

In this study we find that treatment with the P2Y14R antagonist PPTN was associated with activation of the cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling axis and reduced proliferation of HeLa cells, SiHa cells, and cervical cancer organoids.

Among the purinergic receptors, P2Y14R has been predominantly studied in inflammatory diseases. A previous study demonstrated that in ulcerative colitis, P2Y14R protects intestinal epithelial cells from necroptosis through the PKA/CREB/RIPK1 signaling axis [20]. However, the role of P2Y14R in cervical cancer remains poorly understood.

The role of cAMP in cancer cells is complex and context-dependent [21]. For instance, in glioblastoma, cAMP inhibits cell growth by upregulating p21 and p27 and activating the PKA/Epac1-Rap1 signaling axis [22, 23]; whereas in some contexts, cAMP may also promote tumor progression [18]. In our study, treatment with the P2Y14R antagonist PPTN increased intracellular cAMP levels, accompanied by enhanced phosphorylation of PKA and CREB. Depending on the cellular context and the downstream target genes involved, CREB can either promote or suppress tumorigenesis [24]. In our experiment, inhibition of P2Y14R led to activation of the cAMP/PKA/CREB axis, which may direct the transcriptional program of CREB toward growth-inhibitory target genes.

This study adopted a cervical cancer organoid model for in vitro experiments. Compared with conventional two-dimensional monolayer cell culture, organoids can better retain the heterogeneity, cellular structure and biological characteristics of primary tumors.

Ki67 is a well-established marker of cellular proliferation that is predominantly expressed during the active phases of the cell cycle but is absent or markedly reduced in quiescent cells [24]. CK13 is primarily expressed in differentiated non-keratinized squamous epithelium, including the suprabasal layers of the normal ectocervical epithelium, and is associated with the squamous differentiation phenotype in cervical lesions and cervical cancer organoids [25]. A reduction in Ki67 accompanied by increased CK13 expression may suggest that inhibition of proliferation is associated with enhanced squamous differentiation, whereas increased Ki67 together with reduced CK13 expression may reflect active proliferation accompanied by attenuation of the squamous differentiation phenotype. In the present study, PPTN significantly reduced Ki67 fluorescence intensity without significantly altering CK13 expression, suggesting that PPTN primarily reduced the proliferative activity of cervical cancer organoids without producing a detectable change in the CK13-associated squamous differentiation phenotype. Combined assessment of proliferation- and differentiation-related markers may allow a more comprehensive interpretation of the biological response to treatment in organoid models. However, CK13 alone cannot fully characterize the overall differentiation state of cervical cancer organoids, and additional differentiation markers and functional analyses are required to confirm whether PPTN affects organoid differentiation.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, only pharmacological intervention with PPTN was used to inhibit P2Y14R activity. These findings provide preliminary pharmacological evidence that P2Y14R antagonism may contribute to the antiproliferative effects of PPTN in cervical cancer cells and organoids. Genetic validation is required to confirm the receptor-specific role of P2Y14R. Further experiments including P2Y14R knockdown and overexpression are required to further clarify the biological function of P2Y14R in cervical cancer. Secondly, all anti-tumor results were obtained from in vitro cell and organoid models. In vivo animal experiments are needed to verify whether P2Y14R blockade can hinder tumor growth in living animals. Thirdly, PPTN only works at micromolar concentrations, which limits its further clinical application. Accordingly, future research will screen novel small-molecule compounds targeting P2Y14R to develop new therapeutic drugs for cervical cancer.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that PPTN, a P2Y14R antagonist, inhibits the proliferation of HeLa (HPV18+) and SiHa (HPV16+) cervical cancer cells as well as cervical cancer organoids. PPTN treatment elevates intracellular cAMP levels, enhances the phosphorylation of PKA and CREB, and thereby activates the cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling axis. These findings indicate that P2Y14R is involved in the anti-tumor effects of PPTN in cervical cancer cells of two distinct HPV genotypes and in cervical cancer organoids.
