Chemicals of Concern in Chemical Hair Straightening Products: A Scoping Review
Authors: Emily Weaver, Marissa Chan, Lariah Edwards, Ami R Zota
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose of reviewChemical hair straighteners may contribute to adverse health outcomes, particularly among women of color, due to their higher use of these products. However, there is limited research on which chemical ingredients and contaminants may be the most putative agents. We conducted a scoping review to summarize the existing exposure assessment literature on chemicals of concern in two popular types of chemical hair straighteners (chemical relaxers and keratin treatments) and provide recommendations for future research.Recent findingsTwenty-one studies and reports used one or more of the following methods to identify chemicals of concern: product testing, ingredient label analysis, biomonitoring, and air monitoring. Multiple chemicals were characterized in these products including formaldehyde, formaldehyde releasing preservatives (FRPs), volatile organic compounds, fragrance ingredients, metals, and endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) (e.g., phthalates, phenols, and parabens). Chemical composition varied by product type, with keratin treatments more frequently containing formaldehyde or FRPs and relaxers containing EDCs and fragrance ingredients. In addition to assessing potential exposures for consumers, several studies highlighted the potential for higher exposures in salon workers.SummaryMultiple chemicals of concern, including endocrine active and carcinogenic chemicals, were identified in these products, and composition varied by product type. Future research should use new approaches, such as exposomics, to understand the full extent of environmental exposures among consumers and salon workers. Community-informed interventions are also imperative for reducing chemical exposures of concern and related health outcomes. Introduction

The global hair care market is an important driver of environmental health; it was valued at approximately 93.9 billion USD in 2024 and is projected to reach 128.7 billion USD by 2034 [1]. Chemical hair straighteners have come under scrutiny by the scientific, legal, and regulatory communities due to the growing body of literature linking their use to a range of adverse health effects including impaired fertility, increased risks of uterine fibroids and increased risk of hormone-related cancers (i.e., breast, uterine, and ovarian cancers) [2–5]. Hair relaxers and keratin treatments, such as Brazilian blowouts, are two popular types of chemical straighteners that straighten or smooth curly hair through different chemical processes [6, 7]. Specifically, relaxers (alkaline-based) are a multi-step process using various hydroxides to straighten hair by breaking disulfide bonds in the hair cortex [6]. In comparison, keratin treatments (acid-based) straighten hair through hydrolyzing keratin to produce a waterproof film [7]. These products can be found both in salons as services provided by professionals, as well as purchased for use at home from grocery, pharmacy, and beauty supply stores. Supplemental Table S1 compares the two chemical straightening methods in detail.

These products are a potential source of exposure to a wide range of environmental chemicals including endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs)—synthetic or natural substances that can interfere with the body’s hormone system (e.g., phthalates and parabens), carcinogens (e.g., formaldehyde), and heavy metals (e.g., lead) [8–11]. Chemicals may be intentionally added for functional use, such as acting as a preservative or maintaining fragrance, or unintentionally introduced as a byproduct or contaminant of the production and/or packaging process [8, 12, 13]. Consumers and salon workers may be exposed to chemicals of concern in chemical straighteners through inhalation, dermal contact, and ingestion. Fumes, such as formaldehyde, may be emitted while performing straightening treatments (specifically, through applying heat via blow drying), which can be directly inhaled [14]. Additionally, chemicals in these products can be absorbed through the skin and scalp [15, 16]. When misused, relaxers can cause scalp burns [7, 17] due to their alkaline nature, which may lead to greater dermal uptake of chemicals of concern. Chemicals may also be accidentally ingested due to residues left on the hands after product application.

Chemical straighteners have been a central focus of beauty justice, which is both an emerging area of research that recognizes racial and ethnic inequalities in exposure to potentially toxic chemicals in personal care products as an environmental justice concern and an area of advocacy focused on equitable access to safer products to address exposure and health inequities [9, 18, 19]. Relaxers are predominantly used by Black women in the United States (U.S.), with usage reported to start in childhood/adolescence and continuing into adulthood as often as every six weeks [20–23]. While U.S. Black women use keratin treatments [23], these treatments are also reported among Latina women across various countries such as Brazil and among Asian women such as in Saudi Arabia [9, 24–26]. However, since studies often group both relaxers and keratin treatments as “chemical hair straighteners”, demographic differences in use patterns across the two different styling treatments are under-reported. Salon workers, who are often women of color of reproductive age, face substantial occupational health risks as they perform these services multiple times a day and often work in poorly ventilated spaces [27].

Hair styling and product use patterns, including use of chemical straighteners, have significant social, cultural, political, and historical meaning [28]. This is particularly true for women of color whose hair has historically been subject to extreme scrutiny driven by racism (i.e., structural, interpersonal, internalized) and the resulting Eurocentric beauty standards [29, 30]. In the U.S., centuries of laws regulating how women of color wear their hair have been used as a tool to facilitate racism, particularly against Black women [31]. For example, as an effort to exercise control and mark racial difference, Louisiana’s Tignon laws in the 18th century required free Black women to cover their hair with a cloth if their hair could not lie flat [32]. From the 16th to 19th century in several Latin American countries, phenotypes such as hair texture and skin tone were used to differentiate groups in a racialized, hierarchical Casta system and to determine socioeconomic standing [33]. More recently, some schools and workplaces across the U.S. have enforced dress codes that penalize certain natural and protective hair styles typically worn by Black people, such as locs or braids [34].

Historically, hair straightening became popularized in part in response to racism, notably hair-based discrimination. In the early 1900s, Sarah Breedlove, known professionally as Madame CJ Walker, popularized a line of products for Black women that spread across the U.S. and eventually globally [35]. This line included a pomade that when used with a hot comb could straighten hair temporarily. Many Black women in the U.S. began to straighten their hair to seek better social and professional treatment; the message that straight hair would translate to increased opportunities and wealth was amplified by professional organizations, such as the National Association of Colored Women [36]. As the demand for straight hair gained popularity throughout the 1900s, early formulas of relaxers came onto the market. This shift meant that women could save time straightening their hair, as relaxed hair could come into contact with water without completely re-curling. Relaxer use in the U.S. has shifted in popularity since its introduction to the market, driven partly by social and political movements such as the civil rights and Black power movements [37]. Relaxers are available for use in salons and over-the-counter for use at home. For both of these applications, relaxers are available in lye-based formulations (e.g., contain sodium or potassium hydroxide) and no-lye formulations (e.g., contain calcium, guanidine, or lithium hydroxide) [17]. While sales have largely declined over time (most recently attributed to the growth of the natural hair movement; sales of relaxers decreased around 26% between 2008 and 2013), the market is projected to grow as relaxers have regained popularity among consumers in the US [38, 39]. In contrast, keratin treatments are a comparatively new hair styling method. It was first introduced in Brazil in the early 2000s and quickly gaining popularity globally [40]. Market studies have described how keratin treatments are increasing in popularity, particularly among US consumers under the age of 35 [41]. Although the popularity of hair straightening has risen and fallen over time, racialized beauty standards remain; an analysis of beauty perceptions and norms in New York City published in 2023 reported that participants believed that straight hair and lighter skin were associated with attributes including beauty and professionalism [23]. While prior epidemiological studies have been critical for understanding potential health risks associated with product use [3–5, 42–44], there has been a lack of research characterizing specific chemicals of concern in these products that may contribute to the observed epidemiologic associations and broader health inequities. This scoping review aims to summarize existing data on exposure to chemicals of concern in chemical hair straighteners including intentionally added ingredients and environmental contaminants; to examine the full scope of the literature, we drew from both academic, peer-reviewed sources as well as reports and white papers published by government agencies and non-governmental organizations (NGO). Additionally, we will identify potential gaps in the literature and suggest recommendations to inform future research and interventions.

Methods

We relied on the PRISMA extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-SCR) to develop our search and review practices (see Supplemental Table 2). We searched Google Scholar, PubMed, and Web of Science for relevant peer-reviewed articles from up to June 2025. To capture the range of products intended to chemically straighten or smooth textured hair, we included studies on keratin treatments, “Brazilian Blowouts,” or relaxers. We used the following search terms referencing exposure assessment (“analysis,” “biomonitoring,” “targeted analysis,” or “non-targeted analysis”), as well as at least one term that specified chemical straighteners (“relaxer,” “chemical straightener,” “Brazilian Blowout,” “keratin treatment,” “hair product,” or “hair products”). Our search was limited to studies published in English in or after 1995. We also conducted a broad internet search for white papers from government organizations and non-profits in June 2025. We employed two different approaches to identify relevant white papers. We completed a Google search using the same search terms and reviewed the first ten pages of Google search for relevant papers. Additionally, we conducted a more targeted search for white papers by searching the websites of Breast Cancer Prevention Partners, Environmental Working Group, and Women’s Voices for the Earth. These US-based organizations have a long history of engaging in research on and advocacy around chemicals in personal care products. In the results we will refer to peer-reviewed articles by the author’s name (e.g., Peteffi et al.), in comparison, we will refer to the white papers as either the governmental/non-governmental organization’s name or NGO.

All titles and abstracts of retrieved peer-reviewed studies were screened in Microsoft Excel based on pre-specified inclusion/exclusion criteria. Studies not eliminated in the title and abstract screening were screened through a full-text review. Our search for white papers yielded only a few reports; thus, we completed a full-text review of all these results. Included papers must have met the following criteria: (1) written in English, (2) discussed chemical hair straightening exposure assessment (including product testing, biomonitoring, air monitoring, or label analysis of chemical straighteners), (3) reported the ingredients or contaminants in chemical straighteners separately from other hair or personal care products, and (4) included a clear description of the methods used by researchers.

All papers were screened by at least two study co-authors and included based on the above criteria. In cases where both co-authors disagreed on whether to include a paper, a third co-author would read the abstract. If it was unclear from the abstract whether chemical straighteners were analyzed or reported separately from other hair products, the paper was included for full-text review. We identified 961 papers from our search (952 academic and 9 white papers) (Fig. 1). We excluded 911 papers during the screening process and identified 41 peer-reviewed papers for a full-text review. A total of 21 papers, 14 peer-reviewed and seven white papers, were included in our scoping review based on their meeting of our eligibility criteria. From each paper, we extracted the following information into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet: the type of chemical straightener studied, study location, chemicals of interest investigated and detected, and key results related to chemicals of concern in chemical straighteners. For biomonitoring papers, we also collected information about study design and study population. Additional details regarding air sampling methods were collected for air monitoring papers.

PRISMA-ScR flowchart showing the article identification, screening, and inclusion/exclusion process for peer-reviewed articles and white papers

While functionally similar in straightening hair, relaxers and keratin treatments have several differences, including chemical composition (Supplemental Figure S1). Based on the data extracted, we summarized the literature based on the three groups of chemical straighteners (keratin treatments, relaxers, and non-specific straightener) and three exposure assessment methods (product testing/ingredient review, biomonitoring, air monitoring). We then compared the results of studies within each chemical straightener and exposure assessment grouping. We further divided the results by the chemical or chemical classes discussed in each paper. Some of the included studies such as Barbosa et al. and Peteffi et al. employed multiple exposure assessment methods, tested multiple types of products, and/or characterized a range of chemicals of concern. Therefore, those studies are discussed in multiple sections. Importantly, one California-based paper attempted to examine the product labels for both relaxers and keratin treatments after the passing of a state law requiring cosmetics to disclose ingredients, but was unable to report results on keratin treatments as the company who made the product did not comply with the legal requirement [45]. Thus, this analysis is discussed in the relaxer section but not the keratin treatment section of the results.

Results

Keratin Treatments

Intentionally Added Ingredients and Environmental Contaminants

Six studies evaluated chemicals of concern in keratin treatments [45–50]. There was some variation in the analytic methods employed by study, but most used high performance liquid chromatography alone or in combination with other methods.

Formaldehyde and Formaldehyde-Releasing Preservatives

Five studies quantified the presence of formaldehyde or formaldehyde-releasing preservatives (FRPs) in keratin treatments, with several of them finding that the majority of products contained formaldehyde (Table 1) [46–50]. For example, Pierce et al. tested four keratin treatments used at US-based salons and found that three of them contained formaldehyde concentrations ranging from 3 to 11.5% [46]. Several products also contained concentrations of chemicals that may degrade to formaldehyde under heat ranging from 0.3% to 0.4%. Notably, several studies found detectable levels of formaldehyde in both salon and drugstore keratin treatments labeled as formaldehyde-free. For example, Maneli et al. tested seven keratin treatments sold directly to consumers in South Africa and found that formaldehyde levels were between 0.17 and 1.4% including in five products labeled as formaldehyde-free [50]. The Oregon Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) report tested 105 samples from 12 unique keratin smoothing and Brazilian blowout products used in 54 different salons [48]. Testing found that products contained on average 7.3 to 8.8% formaldehyde, even in two products that were either labeled as formaldehyde-free or did not list formaldehyde or FRPs as an ingredient on their label.

Biomonitoring Studies in Salon Workers

Two studies reported concentrations of chemicals in urine of salon workers before and after performing keratin treatments (Table 2) [47, 51]. These papers evaluated the presence of phthalates and formaldehyde exposure in salon workers through biomonitoring or measuring chemical metabolite concentrations in urine, which is a common way to characterize recent exposure.

Formaldehyde

Barbosa et al. analyzed urine samples from various salon workers, including hairdressers, hairdresser assistants, receptionists, manicurists, and managers based in Brazil; the authors grouped workers based on their work responsibilities and formaldehyde exposure levels [47]. Salon workers (n = 26) who directly handled keratin treatment products had higher post-shift urinary formic acid (a metabolite of formaldehyde) compared to their pre-shift concentrations (19.93 mg/L vs. 22.44 mg/L). (p = 0.194)

Phthalates

Boyle et al. leveraged a study cohort consisting of salon workers (n = 23) and a control group of office workers (n = 17) to evaluate post-shift phthalate metabolites in salon workers who provided chemical hair straightening services (pre-shift samples were not taken) [51]. The authors found that select phthalate metabolites were higher in post-shift urine samples in hairdressers performing keratin treatments that day (4.4% of workers) compared to those who did not perform the service that day (95.7% of workers), particularly monoethyl phthalate (MEP), mono(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (MEHP), and mono-isobutyl phthalate (MiBP) (MEP: 243.6 ng/mL vs. 158.4 ng/mL, MEHP: 1.7 ng/mL vs. 0.4 ng/mL, and MiBP: 3.6 ng/mL vs. 2.6 ng/mL); however these comparisons were based on a small sample. Furthermore, the authors found that MEP was also higher in hairdressers who reported performing keratin treatments in general (60.9%) compared to those who did not offer these services (39.1%) (MEP: 181.4 ng/mL vs. 134.6 ng/mL).

Salon Air Monitoring from Keratin Treatments

Six papers or reports used ambient air monitoring to identify chemicals of concern from keratin treatments [46–49, 52, 53]. Most studies investigated the presence of formaldehyde or FRPs, although two studies examined other environmental exposures of concern, such as particulate matter (PM) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs).

Formaldehyde and FRPs

Five studies evaluated the extent to which formaldehyde was released into the air during keratin treatments and reported detectable levels of airborne formaldehyde [46–49, 52]. For example, Pierce et al. evaluated occupational exposure to formaldehyde and FRPs in keratin treatments at different points in the treatment process (i.e., product application, blow drying, flat ironing) [46]. Air samples were collected in four locations characterizing different proximities to a keratin treatment, including in the “breathing zone” of the client and stylist and area samples throughout the salon (bystander sample) such as at a nearby salon chair or in the reception area. Study authors tested the air during the application of four keratin treatment products and found that airborne levels of formaldehyde ranged from 0.02 to 1.19 ppm throughout different zones of the salon, with higher concentrations measured closer to the client and stylist for three of the treatments. Reports from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and Oregon OSHA detailed requests by salon owners/workers who were concerned about their health connected to application of keratin treatments. In response to these requests, exposure assessments were carried out at salons measuring airborne formaldehyde concentrations during keratin treatments (n = 7 salon for OSHA report and n = 1 salons for CDC report) [48, 49]. Internationally, one study conducted in Brazil (Barbosa et al.) used passive air samplers throughout the salon at breathing height of workers to measure formaldehyde emissions during keratin treatment services [47]. The authors found a high positive correlation between formaldehyde present in products and airborne formaldehyde emissions. (p < 0.001)

Other Air Emissions Data: Particulate Matter (PM) and Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs)

Two studies investigated the environmental chemicals emitted during keratin treatment services [49, 53]. Kaikiti et al. measured the emissions of PM 2.5, 4, 10, and VOCs at five different salons in Cyprus [53]. They found that keratin treatments were significantly associated with higher airborne volume of various PM and VOC emissions, which were detected in nearly all samples (frequency of detection at least 60% of duration each sample), including alkanes, aromatics, and terpenes. As a note, while the sources of PM from chemical straightening treatments have not been identified, studies measuring airborne pollutants in salons serving Black and Latina clientele hypothesize that products may be one source [54]. Additionally, the CDC report characterizing formaldehyde also tested for other VOCs, acetone, and methyl ethyl ketone, in their air samples, but those were not detected [49].

Key Conclusions from the Literature on Keratin Treatments

Of the eight papers that investigated chemicals of concern in keratin treatments, most detected formaldehyde and FRPs. Many of these papers tested products to characterize the chemicals in them or measured air emissions to determine the chemicals released during their use. Even products labeled as formaldehyde-free had detectable formaldehyde levels. More studies are needed to understand other chemicals of concern that salon workers and consumers are exposed to from keratin treatments.

Chemical Hair Relaxers

Intentionally Added Ingredients and Environmental Contaminants

Research from both academia and white papers has begun identifying potentially harmful ingredients in chemical hair relaxers. Specifically, nine studies identified a variety of chemicals of concern including, EDCs, formaldehyde and FRPs, and metals in hair relaxers through product testing or ingredient label review (Table 1) [10, 45, 55–61].

Fragrance Ingredients or Ingredients Associated with Fragrance

The most commonly reported ingredients of concern were fragrance ingredients (including linalool, limonene, and hexyl cinnamal) [62] or chemicals associated with fragrance (such as diethyl phthalate (DEP), a solvent that is used to hold fragrance) [63]; these were identified in five studies [10, 45, 55, 57, 59]. One study from Irungu et al. assessed chemicals of concern through ingredient label review in hair relaxers available in two counties in Kenya [57]. They reported that almost half of the 27 chemicals of concern identified were fragrance chemicals. Additionally, all relaxers contained at least one fragrance chemical. Furthermore, Helm et al. examined products commonly used by Black women in New York City for endocrine-disrupting or asthma-associated chemicals and quantified DEP, linalool, and limonene in three relaxers [10]. Two NGO white papers also identified fragrance or fragrance ingredients in relaxers [45, 55].

Other Ingredients and Contaminants of Concern (e.g., Formaldehyde, FRPs, Lead)

Okeke et al. investigated the ingredient labels of the best-selling relaxers sold online in five major retailers for allergens [59]. Across 41 relaxers, the most common allergens were propylene glycol, cetyl steryl alcohol, and fragrance. Formaldehyde and FRPs (i.e., diazolidinyl urea, DMDM hydantoin, and phenoxyethanol) were also identified. However, the FRPs had a lower significance and prevalence of allergens (Significance-Prevalence Index Number (SPIN) ranging from three to 31) compared to other ingredients such as fragrance (SPIN 515). Additionally, the Kenya-based study from Irungu et al. (described above) also reported formaldehyde on the label of relaxers [57]. In comparison, a US-based analysis from the Breast Cancer Prevention Partner’s Campaign for Safe Cosmetics found no detectable levels of formaldehyde in three hair relaxers marketed to children, but did detect 1,4 dioxane, a possible carcinogen [56]. EWG published two reports in 2016 and 2025 quantifying chemicals of concern listed on different hair product ingredient labels, including relaxers [60, 61]. The 2025 EWG report found that relaxers commonly contained hazardous ingredients, with 78% of products containing caustic ingredients (sodium or calcium hydroxide) and 25.9% containing FRPs.

Interestingly, a few analyses identified that relaxer kits marketed to children contained other chemicals of concern [10, 55, 56]. For example, the product testing analysis of hair products commonly used by Black women from Helm et al. reported that among all the products tested, the two relaxer kits marketed to children had the highest concentrations of five EDCs and/or asthma-associated chemicals regulated by California Proposition 65 or prohibited by the European Union (i.e., o-phenylphenol, DEP, nonylphenol, Bisphenol A, and diethanolamine) [10]. Many of these chemicals of concern are phenols and are used in personal care products for a variety of functions including as preservatives, emulsifiers, and to hold fragrance. Only one Nigeria-based study from Iwegbue et al. examined metals contamination [58]. The authors reported that relaxers had higher concentrations of lead and iron compared to other hair products.

Biomonitoring Studies of Salon Workers

One occupational study among the US-based salon worker population that used office workers as the control examined exposure to environmental chemicals of concern (i.e., phthalates and VOCs) from relaxers using targeted and untargeted approaches to biomonitoring [51]. Boyle et al. reported that certain phthalate metabolites varied by hair relaxing services [51]. Specifically, stylists that performed hair relaxing services on the day of urine collection (n = 19) had higher concentrations of MEP, mono-(2-ethyl-5-carboxypentyl) (MECPP) phthalate, sum of DEHP metabolites, and mono- (2-ethyl-5-oxohexyl) phthalate (MEOHP) compared to those who did not (n = 4).

Key Conclusions from the Literature on Relaxers

All of the existing research by academic and NGOs identified chemicals of concern in chemical hair relaxers. Specifically, chemicals associated with fragrance and EDCs (notably, phthalates) were the most common groups of chemicals identified in hair relaxers. We need to further analyze relaxers for understudied chemicals and contaminants of concern (e.g., FRPs and metals).

Most of the studies focused on relaxers used product testing or ingredient review to identify chemicals of concern. Of these, four were academic (based in the US, Kenya, and Nigeria) and five were reports from US NGOs. Most studies only examined a small number of relaxers and used a targeted analytic approach, thus, the range of products and resulting chemicals of concern may not be fully evaluated or highly generalizable (Tables 1 and 2).

Non-Specific Hair Straightening Products

Four studies characterized exposure to chemicals of concern (i.e., phthalates, phenols, VOCs, and formaldehyde) in straighteners that were not specifically identified as a relaxer, keratin treatment, or other chemical straighteners (such as a Japanese heat straightener that uses ammonium thioglycolate) [27, 64–66].

We identified three occupational studies among different salon worker populations. Newmeyer et al. compared urinary concentrations of chemicals of concern in hairdressers and office workers among the US-based salon worker population previously discussed [64]. Higher median concentrations of 24 chemicals were identified among hairdressers compared to office workers using a suspect screening method, including, certain parabens (methyl, ethyl, and propyl) and phthalates (dibutyl and dihexyl). In the same cohort, Louis et al. reported that median concentrations of almost all of the VOC biomarkers (26 out of the 28) were higher among hairdressers working in Black salons as compared to hairdressers working in Dominican salons [66]—highlighting how product use patterns among different communities can have implications for occupational and consumer health. Notably, 1-PMA (a biomarker of 1-bromopropane, a solvent with neurotoxic properties) was significantly higher among stylists who performed chemical straighteners or relaxers compared to those who did not (median concentrations straightener use and no straightener use: 17.8 ng/mL and 2.74 ng/mL, p = 0.04). Interestingly, higher concentrations of certain biomarkers were also reported among hair stylists wearing gloves during chemical treatments compared to stylists who did not wear gloves. Peteffi et al. used a combination of methods (i.e., product testing, biomonitoring, and air monitoring) to examine formaldehyde exposure from use of “hair straightening products” among salon workers in Brazil [27]. They reported significant differences in pre- and post-shift urinary formaldehyde concentrations. Notably, the salon that used the straightener with the highest concentration of formaldehyde (5.9%) was also reported to have the highest mean difference between pre- and post-shift urinary concentrations of formic acid (17.24 mg/L ± 12.91). Additionally, that same salon reported the highest ambient concentrations of formaldehyde (0.16 ppm).

One study from Schildroth et al. reported on the association between hair straightener use and urinary concentrations of a variety of phthalate metabolites and phenols among the SELF cohort, which consists of Black women in the Detroit, Michigan area [65]. High frequency of relaxer use (classified as six or more times in the past year) was associated with higher concentrations of EDCs including, 2,5-dichlorophenol, ethyl paraben, monobenzyl phthalate (MBzP), mono-2-ethyl-5-carboxypentyl terephthalate (MECPTP) and mono-2-ethyl-5-hydroxyhexyl terephthalate (MEHHTP). While the authors of this analysis did not distinguish between straightener types in their main analysis, the most common type of hair straightener reported in the analytic population was lye-based hair relaxer use.

Key Conclusions from the Literature on Non-Specific Straightening Products

A few studies have used the broad classification of hair straightening products, which may include relaxers, keratin treatments, or others. Three of those were occupational; thus, this highlights the need for more precise exposure assessment methods in order to link chemical exposure to specific services performed by salon workers. Furthermore, only one study, analysis in the SELF cohort, was among a consumer population.

Discussion

In this scoping review, we identified 21 peer-reviewed studies, NGO reports, and government reports that quantified or identified chemicals of concern in two types of chemical hair straighteners (i.e., relaxers and keratin treatments). The majority of studies were based in the US, and a few were from South American or African countries. The chemical classes or groups identified among all studies were formaldehyde, FRPs, EDCs (such as phthalates, phenols or parabens), fragrance ingredients, metals, and VOCs. Specifically, the most frequently identified chemicals of concern were formaldehyde and FRPs in keratin treatments, compared to EDCs and fragrance ingredients in hair relaxers. Product testing was the most common method of characterizing exposure. Numerous studies were among salon workers who performed keratin treatments and consistently reported formaldehyde or FRPs in these products through several exposure assessment methods.

While a number of recent papers have highlighted the adverse health outcomes associated with chemical straightening products [2–5, 42, 44], most of the studies used crude methods to characterize exposure to chemical mixtures from hair product use. Exposure assessment has been described as a general limitation of the subfield of epidemiology focused on health effects of personal care product use [67]. Identifying chemicals of concern in these products can help researchers better understand the biologic plausibility underlying observed associations between chemical straightener use and adverse health outcomes. Furthermore, this research may inform future research, consumer and business interventions, consumer-facing education, and policies surrounding chemicals of concern in chemical hair straighteners. Below, we discuss gaps in this body of literature, as well as give recommendations for future research.

Strengths and Limitations of Existing Research

The studies we reviewed had some notable strengths. Many studies employed more than one exposure assessment method (i.e., air monitoring during product use and targeted product testing), which allowed us to make comparisons across studies as well as validate inferences across exposure assessment methodology (e.g., comparison of air monitoring data with product ingredient data). Furthermore, a few studies (such as Newmeyer et al.) employed an untargeted exposure assessment approach. Since chemical hair straighteners are a combination of ingredients that may be transformed via heat [68], untargeted approaches are critical as they can identify the full range of chemicals of concern that targeted assessment methods can miss. Lastly, many of these studies were occupational, which is important when considering that salon workers are more highly exposed to chemicals of concern in hair products due to the numerous salon services provided over a full eight hour or longer workday.

There were also multiple limitations across this body of work. Most studies were conducted in specific populations defined by geographic region and analyzed the most commonly used products via a targeted approach. Thus, there may be limited generalizability in terms of these results. Specifically, the full range of chemical straighteners and resulting chemicals of concern in these products may not be reported. The cross-sectional surveys used to assess chemical straightener use in some of the biomonitoring studies investigating associations between products used and biomarkers of chemical exposures are limited. For example, keratin straighteners, relaxers, and other chemical treatments such as perms are commonly combined into one category. However, each product type has a different function and chemical composition. Furthermore, these surveys were often not specific to chemical straightener type, so granular product use data are missing. For example, some surveys only asked respondents about ever use of these chemical hair treatments or use within a certain time frame (e.g., use in the past 12 months). Finally, limited studies examined chemicals of concern and populations at risk of exposure specifically to chemical relaxers. Formaldehyde and FRPs are ingredients of concern primarily in keratin treatments—and have been highlighted in the media by the recent FDA ruling to ban these ingredients in chemical straighteners [69]. However, few analyses examined formaldehyde and FRPs in relaxers. Additionally, none of the studies conducted air monitoring in salons solely or predominantly providing relaxer services. In general, we did not assess strength of evidence or the quality of any of the included studies.

Recommendations for Future Research

Building on these strengths, limitations, and the conclusions from our review, we propose four key recommendations for expanding research on hair relaxers, keratin treatments, and the chemicals of concern in them.

Recommendation #1

Implement an exposomics framework to characterize all exposures from chemical hair straighteners in an untargeted and comprehensive manner. To date, most studies of hair product exposures, including several in this review, employ a targeted approach selecting environmental chemicals and/or metabolites based on pre-existing knowledge. We recommend that future studies include modern exposome analyses, or exposomics, in their study design. Specifically, high-resolution mass spectrometry could allow for an expansive and agnostic assessment of exogenous chemicals and their by-products, as well as their related endogenous metabolites in different biological matrices [70]. This analysis of urine or other biological samples taken from individuals after a chemical straightener treatment could advance our understanding of the chemical exposures associated with using these products. Furthermore, wrist bands are increasingly used as passive personal samplers to measure a range of chemical exposures [71]. This lower cost sampler is less burdensome for participants than biological sampling and is reported to be an effective tool to assess exposure to a wide range of environmental chemicals [71].These findings could help clarify the biologic plausibility of observed epidemiologic associations with chemical straighteners. Furthermore, exposomics in combination with other high-resolution methods (e.g., metabolomics) could be used to understand both the short- and long-term health consequences of using these products.

Recommendation #2

Generate empirical data characterizing chemical byproducts formed via heat activation and chemical mixtures produced during the chemical straightener treatment process. There are multiple steps in the relaxer and keratin treatment process, including the use of several hair products and heat styling tools such as hair dryers, flat irons, hot combs, and curling irons (Supplemental Table S1). We recommend that future studies investigate and characterize the chemicals and byproducts formed as a result of mixing hair products and/or heat activation. Studies have already reported elevated volatile and semivolatile organic compounds and nanoparticles in indoor air due to heat-based hair styling after applying multiple hair products during a realistic hair care treatment [68, 72]. Future work could simulate the hair straightening process in a controlled environment to characterize the chemicals produced at each step (e.g., applying various hair products, use of heat styling tools). In addition to characterizing byproducts, the findings could also identify the most harmful products or steps of the treatment process, which may be critical for future interventions.

Recommendation #3

Develop and test community-informed multi-level intervention studies in collaboration with community partners to inform decision-making around chemical straightener use among diverse populations. Prior studies indicate that interventions that integrate community knowledge and socio-cultural context are more effective in fostering positive health behavior changes, particularly among minoritized populations [73–75]. There is a lack of effectiveness and intervention research focused on reducing the use of and increasing informed decision-making about chemical straighteners. We recommend that intervention studies focus on developing effective strategies for reducing chemical straightener use in diverse populations. These studies should include women of color, frequent users of chemical straighteners. Additionally, these studies should include men and individuals with other gender identities who may also use these products but are often excluded from studies on hair product use. Research examining Black men’s perceptions of Black women’s hair product use has begun to explore how other genders perceive beauty standards and product use patterns [76]. However, researchers have yet to examine differences in chemical hair straightener use patterns by gender. Studies aiming to specifically examine salon workers and the use of PPE will also be critical in developing occupational interventions. Finally, these studies should be equally led by community groups or organizations with strong ties to communities who use these products and/or are involved in advocacy around chemicals in beauty and personal care products. Adding community knowledge and social and cultural information is critical, as the decision to stop using these products can be significant and life-changing for many individuals. As previously discussed, there is a long, complex history associated with straight hair that needs to be recognized when developing interventions. Finally, these interventions can extend beyond individual change and target multiple key actors to create long-term, systemic change. For example, an educational based intervention can reduce exposure in the salon environment (e.g., better ventilation and use of PPE during chemical straightening treatments), while also highlight safer alternatives for community members (e.g., silk press- a less chemically intensive, heat based straightening method). These collaborations can also ensure that study findings are disseminated beyond peer-reviewed publications. This would ensure that the research is more accessible to communities most affected and potentially translated for use towards policy change.

Recommendation #4

Incorporate information about ingredient composition into characterization of exposure to hair straightening products in epidemiologic studies. To date, most studies rely on surveys that ask respondents to recall past use of chemical straighteners which may be subject to recall bias. Additionally, many of the prior epidemiologic studies lacked information on type and brand of hair straighteners used by participants even though studies that directly measured the chemical content of hair straighteners demonstrated substantial variation in the chemical profiles of hair relaxers and other hair products across different brands [10, 67]. We recommend that future researchers consider more advanced exposure assessment techniques in their studies to provide more robust exposure information. For example, our team in a recent study assessing personal care product use among women of color had participants document product use in real time using a smartphone app [9]. This method effectively created a product inventory that included product information, such as brand names and ingredients, and captured images of products. All product use entries by participants were also date and time stamped. Such information is useful for accurately capturing frequency of product use, which is critical to understanding exposure-response relationships as well as the short- and long-term health consequences of chemical straightener use. While all research teams may not have the resources to develop a study-specific smartphone app for data collection, researchers can also partner with organizations such as Clearya, an app that helps identify toxic ingredients in their products, to leverage this kind of technology and collect more specific exposure data for their research studies [9].

Conclusion

Recent research has highlighted how chemical straightener use may be associated with a range of adverse health outcomes across the life course. Identifying the ingredients of concern in these products is critical to begin to understand the mechanisms through which straightener use may impact health. This research may also inform multi-level interventions (e.g., consumer, retailer, neighborhood) and policies focused on chemicals of concern in straighteners. This review calls for future environmental health research that utilizes more advanced exposure assessment methods such as untargeted metabolomics and mixtures approach, to more accurately capture problematic environmental exposures from hair straighteners for both consumers and salon workers. Lastly, in addition to better characterizing the problem, we importantly need to turn towards identifying solutions. We recommend the development of meaningful community-based collaborations to design culturally relevant and sustainable interventions. Importantly, upstream interventions, including those targeting structural racism and market-based strategies, are needed to reduce environmental exposure and health inequities experienced by communities at risk.
