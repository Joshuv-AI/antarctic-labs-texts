Random matrices acting on sets: Independent columns
Yaniv Plan, Roman Vershynin

Abstract
We study random matrices with independent subgaussian columns. Assuming each column has a fixed Euclidean norm, we establish conditions under which such matrices act as near-isometries when restricted to a given subset of their domain. We show that, with high probability, the maximum distortion caused by such a matrix is proportional to the Gaussian complexity of the subset, scaled by the subgaussian norm of the matrix columns. This linear dependence on the subgaussian norm is a new phenomenon, as random matrices with independent rows or independent entries typically exhibit superlinear dependence. As a consequence, normalizing the columns of random sparse matrices leads to stronger embedding guarantees.

Introduction

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be a random matrix and
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
a fixed set. We study the question:
Under what circumstances is
A
well conditioned when restricted to
T

More technically, we study the quantity

\delta\coloneqq\sup_{x\in T}\Big\lvert\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{2}-\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\Big\rvert.

Observe that the
isometry constant

\delta
is the smallest constant such that

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}-\delta\leq\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}+\delta\qquad\mbox{for all }x\in T.

If
\delta
is small,
A
embeds
T
into
\mathbb{R}^{m}
without significantly distorting Euclidean norms. If
T
is replaced by the Minkowski difference
T-T

T
, so we can
think of
A
as a
near isometry
on
T
. Random matrices that are near isometries are useful in a variety of applications, including compressed sensing
[
12
,
11
]

[
14
]
, and randomized sketching
[
27

]
. The subset
T
of interest varies by application. When
T
belongs to the sphere and
\delta<1

A
is a random subspace that must not intersect
T
, and thus the study of
\delta
also has interesting implications regarding the behaviour of random subspaces
[
13

.

If

is a random Gaussian matrix, the size of

Definition 1

(The Gaussian width and Gaussian complexity of a set)
.

The Gaussian width
w(T)
and the Gaussian complexity
\gamma(T)
of a set
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
are defined as follows:

w(T)\coloneqq\E\sup_{x\in T}\langle g,x\rangle,\quad\gamma(T)\coloneqq\E\sup_{x\in T}\lvert\langle g,x\rangle\rvert

where
g
is a standard normal random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e.
g\sim N(0,I_{n})
.

These two quantities are identical for symmetric sets, and in general we have
w(T)+\mathrm{rad}(T)\asymp\gamma(T)
, where
\mathrm{rad}(T):=\sup_{x\in T}\lVert x\rVert_{2}
. For more properties of Gaussian width and complexity, see
[
26
]
.

To extend our discussion to more general random matrix ensembles, recall that the
subgaussian norm
of a random variable
Z
is defined as

\left\lVert Z\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}:=\inf\left\{t>0:\E\exp(Z^{2}/t^{2})\leq 2\right\}

and

is called

\left\lVert Z\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}<\infty
. Further, the subgaussian norm of a random vector

is defined as

\lVert V\rVert_{\psi_{2}}:=\sup_{x\in S^{n-1}}\lVert\langle V,x\rangle\rVert_{\psi_{2}}

(1)

i.e. it is the largest subgaussian norm of one-dimensional marginals of

[
26
]

Matrices with subgaussian entries (or rows or columns) represent a notable generalization beyond the Gaussian case. In particular, this generalization includes discrete and/or sparse random matrices, which can be used to accelerate computations
[
16
]
and are essential in applications like group testing
[

]
and wireless network activity detection
[

Theorem 2

(Corollary 1.2 in
[

.

Let

be an
m\times n

\E\sup_{x\in T}\Big\lvert\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{2}-\sqrt{m}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\Big\rvert\leq CK\sqrt{\log K}\,\gamma(T).

Moreover, for any

\sup_{x\in T}\Big\lvert\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{2}-\sqrt{m}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\Big\rvert\leq CK\sqrt{\log K}\left[w(T)+u\cdot\mathrm{rad}(T)\right].

Theorem 3

Let

\E\sup_{x\in T}\Big\lvert\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{2}-\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\Big\rvert\leq CK\gamma(T).

Moreover, for any

\sup_{x\in T}\Big\lvert\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{2}-\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\Big\rvert\leq CK\left[w(T)+u\cdot\mathrm{rad}(T)\right].

Corollary 4
.

Let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix whose columns
A_{i}
are independent, mean zero, subgaussian random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying
\left\lVert A_{i}\right\rVert_{2}=\lambda
almost surely and
\left\lVert A_{i}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq K
. Then, for any subset
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\E\sup_{x\in T}\Big\lvert\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{2}-\lambda\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\Big\rvert\leq CK\gamma(T).

Moreover, for any
u\geq 0
, with probability at least
1-3e^{-u^{2}}
, we have

\sup_{x\in T}\Big\lvert\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{2}-\lambda\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\Big\rvert\leq CK\left[w(T)+u\cdot\mathrm{rad}(T)\right].

Remark 5

(Why is column normalization needed?)
.

In contrast to the previous result (Corollary
2
), here we require a hard normalization of columns:

\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{2}=\lambda\quad\text{a.s. for all }i.

(2)

One cannot remove this assumption even for
n=1
, or replace it with a weaker assumption of isotropy as in Theorem
2
. This is because a mean-zero isotropic subgaussian random vector
A_{1}
might vanish with probability
1/2
. For example, if
\delta\sim\mbox{Bern}(1/2)
and
X
is (independently) chosen uniformly from
\sqrt{m}S^{m-1}
, then
\delta X
is a subgaussian vector with
O(1)
subgaussian norm. If the columns of
A
are i.i.d. copies of
\delta X
and
T=\{e_{1}\}
, the first canonical basis vector, then

\sup_{x\in T}\Big\lvert\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{2}-\lambda\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\Big\rvert=|\delta\sqrt{m}-\lambda|\geq\sqrt{m}/2

with probability
1/2
regardless of the value of
\lambda
. In other words, both the probabilistic bound and bound in expectation become substantially dependent on
m
without the normalization assumption on the columns.

Remark 6

(Column normalization improves the dependence on the subgaussian norm)
.

The hard normalization of columns has an interesting beneficial effect on the dependence on the subgaussian norm. In the previous result (Theorem
2
) the dependence is
O(K\sqrt{\log K})
, and this dependence is optimal even for matrices with all independent entries
[
13
]
. But as soon as we normalize the columns as in Corollary
4
, the dependence becomes linear, i.e.
O(K)
. We see in Section
2
that this small improvement leads to completely different asymptotic behaviour for the embedding guarantees of sparse matrices. We also observe in Remark
12
that this improvement only occurs for matrices with independent columns of a fixed norm, not for matrices with independent rows of a fixed norm.

Remark 7

(Comparison to
[
4
]
)
.

We now compare to the results in
[
4
]
. Therein, the authors require the columns of
A
to satisfy: 1) a “thin-shell condition”, i.e., to have concentrated norms, and 2) a moment condition on the marginals. This allows a broad class of distributions, including subgaussian. We specialize their results to the setting of Corollary
4
with
\lambda=\sqrt{m}
, and also assume that a) the columns of
A
are isotropic, b)
T\subset S^{n-1}
, and c)
\gamma(T)\geq\sqrt{\log n}
to allow a simpler comparison. In this case, the main result in
[
4
]
(Theorem 1.5), yields the following. For
u\geq 1
, with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c\gamma(T)^{2})-n^{-u}
,

\sup_{x\in T}\Big\lvert\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert^{2}_{2}-m\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\Big\rvert\leq C(K,u)\cdot\sqrt{m}\left[\gamma(T)(1+\gamma(T)/\sqrt{m})\right]\log(en/\gamma^{2}(T)).

Note that this is a uniform bound on the squared difference
\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert^{2}_{2}-m\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}_{2}=(\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert^{2}-\sqrt{m}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2})(\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{2}+\sqrt{m}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2})
thus explaining the extra factor of
\sqrt{m}
. Further, in typical cases of interest
\gamma(T)<\sqrt{m}
. Thus, it is natural to compare
C(K,\mu)\gamma(T)\log(en/\gamma^{2}(T))
to the error bound given in Corollary
4
, which takes the form
CK\gamma(T)
. In other words, by concentrating on the subgaussian case, we are able to improve on the results in
[
4
]
by giving the
explicit and optimal linear dependence on the subgaussian norm
K
, and also removing the
\log(en/\gamma(T)^{2})
factor — thereby showing that there is no dependence on the ambient dimension.

1.1
Notation

We use
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}
for the Euclidean norm of vectors, and
S^{n-1}
for the set of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with Euclidean norm
1
. We say that
f\lesssim g
if there exists an absolute constant
C
such that
f\leq Cg
everywhere. The symbols
C,c
generally refer to absolute constants which may vary from instance to instance. We say
f\asymp g

2
An example: sparse matrices

Sparse random matrices are a central object of study in the context of sparse Johnson Lindenstrauss embeddings
[
1
]
and sparse subspace embeddings
[
7
]
. These give a method to facilitate computation by embedding data into a lower dimension near isometrically via a sparse (and thus low complexity) transform. The central question is:
How should we choose the distribution of
A
to (a) minimize the number of nonzero entries and (b) ensure it behaves like a near-isometry?

An interesting phenomenon has been observed (and understood) in this field: Choosing
A
with i.i.d. entries limits its ability to near-isometrically embed sparse vectors
[
20
,
3
]
. However, choosing the columns from a distribution with exactly
s
non-zero entries per column can give a significant improvement. The literature in this area generally considers embeddings of singletons, finite sets, or subspaces, with the notable exception in
[
5
]
, discussed further below. We extend the understanding of this phenomenon to arbitrary bounded sets in the following.
We begin with an illustrative example to give an intuition for this point.

2.1
Approximate vs. exact sparsity

Consider two different models to generate a sparse
m\times n
matrix with (about)
s
nonzero entries per column. In the first, “approximate” model, we sample each entry independently from the
\Ber(s/m)
distribution. In the second, “exact” model, we put
s
ones uniformly at random in each column of the matrix. Although individual entries in both models have the same
\Ber(s/m)
distribution, it turns out that these two models have diverging behaviour as
m
increases. In the first model, the number of nonzero entries in a column converges to a
\mbox{Poisson}(s)
distribution. Thus, the column norms will concentrate around
\sqrt{s}
, but their deviations will not decrease to zero as
m
increases. Equivalently, the matrix will randomly distort the norm of a standard basis vector, and
that distortion will never decrease to zero
. In contrast, in the second model, the number of nonzero entries per column is exactly
s
and so the column norms are exactly
\sqrt{s}
. Further, as
m
increases, the columns become more and more likely to have disjoint support and thus become orthogonal. Thus, the resulting matrix (multiplied by
1/\sqrt{s}
for normalization) asymptotically becomes
an exact isometry
with probability approaching
1
as
m
increases.

The following two results make these observations quantitative and general.

Corollary 8

(Approximately
s
-sparse columns)
.

Let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix whose entries are independent random variables which take the value
1
or
-1
with probability
\frac{s}{2m}
each, and
0
with probability
1-\frac{s}{m}
. Then, for any subset
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\E\sup_{x\in T}\Big\lvert\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{2}-\sqrt{s}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\Big\rvert\leq C\gamma(T).

Moreover, this bound is optimal in general. For any integers
1\leq s\leq m/2
and
n\geq 1
there exists a non-empty subset
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\E\sup_{x\in T}\Big\lvert\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{2}-\sqrt{s}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\Big\rvert\geq c\gamma(T).

Note that the right-hand side does not decrease when
m
grows.

Proof.

Let
A_{i}
denote the columns of
A
. It is not hard to check (see Lemma
26
) that

\left\lVert A_{i}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log(2/p)}}\quad\text{where }p=\frac{s}{m}.

If we normalize
A_{i}
by setting
Z_{i}=A_{i}/\sqrt{p}
, then all coordinates of
Z_{i}
are independent random variables with mean zero and variance
1
. Hence
Z_{i}
are independent and isotropic random vectors with subgaussian norms bounded by
K=1/\sqrt{p\log(2/p)}
. Thus, applying Theorem
2
for the matrix
A/\sqrt{p}
, we obtain

\E\sup_{x\in T}\Big\lvert\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{2}-\sqrt{m}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\Big\rvert\lesssim K\sqrt{\log K}\,\gamma(T)\lesssim\frac{\gamma(T)}{\sqrt{p}}.

Multiply by
\sqrt{p}
on both sides to obtain the first part of the theorem.

To prove the second part, choose
T=\{e_{1}\}
, the first vector of the standard basis in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

\E\sup_{x\in T}\Big\lvert\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{2}-\sqrt{s}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\Big\rvert=\E\left\lvert\sqrt{Z}-\sqrt{s}\right\rvert\quad\text{(where $Z\sim\Binom(m,p)$)}.

Since
\gamma(T)\asymp 1
, it suffices to prove that
\E\left\lvert\sqrt{Z}-\sqrt{s}\right\rvert\gtrsim 1
. A quick computation

\E(Z-s)^{4}\lesssim m\E X_{1}^{4}+m^{2}(\E X_{1}^{2})^{2}\lesssim mp+m^{2}p^{2}\lesssim s^{2}

Then using Paley-Zygmund inequality for

\mathbb{P}\left\{\lvert Z-s\rvert\geq\frac{\sqrt{s}}{2}\right\}=\mathbb{P}\left\{(Z-s)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\E(Z-s)^{2}\right\}>c.

Combining this with (

Corollary 9

(Exactly
s
-sparse columns)
.

Let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix whose columns are independent random vectors drawn uniformly from the set of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
that have all
0,-1,1
entries and exactly
s
nonzero entries. Then, for any subset
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\E\sup_{x\in T}\Big\lvert\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{2}-\sqrt{s}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\Big\rvert\leq\frac{C\gamma(T)}{\sqrt{\log(2m/s)}}.

Moreover, this bound is optimal in general. For any integers
1\leq s\leq m
there exists
n=n(m,s)
and a non-empty subset
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\E\sup_{x\in T}\Big\lvert\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{2}-\sqrt{s}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\Big\rvert\geq\frac{c\gamma(T)}{\sqrt{\log(2m/s)}}.

Note that the right-hand side decreases when
m
grows.

Proof.

It is not hard to check (see Lemma
26
) that the columns
A_{i}
of
A
satisfy

\left\lVert A_{i}\right\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{s}\text{ a.s.}\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lVert A_{i}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log(2m/s)}}.

Apply Corollary
4
to obtain the first part of the theorem.

To prove the second part, choose

T=\left\{e_{1}-e_{2},\,e_{1}-e_{3},\,\ldots,e_{1}-e_{n}\right\}

where
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
is the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{Ax=0\text{ for some }x\in T\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left\{A_{1}=A_{i}\text{ for some }i=2,\ldots,n\right\}

\displaystyle=1-\left(1-\mathbb{P}\left\{A_{1}=A_{2}\right\}\right)^{n-1}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{A_{1}=A_{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{s}\binom{m}{s}}

\displaystyle\geq\Big(\frac{2em}{s}\Big)^{-s}\quad\text{(by a standard bound \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{hdp-book}{}{}, Exercise~0.0.5]})}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{n}\quad\text{(if we choose) }n=\left\lceil\Big(\frac{2em}{s}\Big)^{3s}\right\rceil.

Then

\mathbb{P}\left\{Ax=0\text{ for some }x\in T\right\}\geq 1-\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{n-1}\geq c.

When
Ax=0
for some
x\in T
, we have
\Big\lvert\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{2}-\sqrt{s}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\Big\rvert=\sqrt{2s}
. It follows that

\E\sup_{x\in T}\Big\lvert\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{2}-\sqrt{s}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\Big\rvert\geq c\sqrt{2s}.

(4)

On the other hand, denoting by
g_{i}
independent
N(0,1)
random variables, we have

\gamma(T)=\E\max_{i=2,\ldots,n}\lvert g_{1}-g_{i}\rvert\lesssim\sqrt{\log n}\lesssim\sqrt{s\log(2m/s)}.

(5)

where the first inequality follows from the standard bound on the maximum of subgaussian random variables
[
26
, Exercise 2.5.10]
, and the second inequality follows by our choice of
n
. Comparing (
4
) and (
5
), we complete the proof.
∎

Remark 10

(Diverging asymptotic behaviour)
.

As shown in Corollaries
8
and
9
, in general as
m\rightarrow\infty
the isometry constant for sparse matrices with i.i.d. entries remains lower bounded, whereas the isometry constant for sparse matrices with exactly
s
non-zeros per column decreases to 0.

Remark 11

(Alternative treatments of isometry constants of sparse matrices)
.

We point out three different treatments of the isometry constants of sparse matrices depending on the structure of
T
:

1.

There is a rich understanding of the isometric properties of sparse matrices in the literature on sparse Johnson Lindenstrauss embeddings (see e.g.,
[
16
]
) when
T
is a singleton (or a finite set).

2.

When
T
is a
d
-dimensional subspace, again there is a thorough understanding in the literature on sparse (oblivious) subspace embeddings. Here it is shown that one only needs
m=O(d)
to have an isometry constant lower than 1, even if the sparsity is polylogarithmic (see e.g.,
[
6
]
) – a result which does not follow from Corollary
9
.

3.

We are aware of one paper,
[
5
]
, which addresses the isometric properties of sparse matrices for arbitrary subsets of the sphere.
The main theorem in
[
5
]
controls the isometry constant by a new quantity, which is akin to a sparsified gaussian complexity. We don’t repeat that theorem here because the conditions are relatively complex, but note that it recovers the results on sparse subspace embeddings (up to log factors) as a special case. Thus, the machinery in
[
5
]
allows tighter control of the isometry constant than Corollary
9
for some sets and parameter regimes. However, the results in
[
5
]
do not imply that the restricted isometry constant converges to 0 as
m\rightarrow\infty
and thus do not imply the diverging asymptotic behaviour of “approximately sparse” and “exactly sparse” matrices shown in our work and discussed in the remark above. Further, we note that our work does not require
T
to be a subset of the sphere, and is oblivious to every property of
T
aside from its gaussian complexity.

Remark 12

(Exactly
s
-sparse rows do not give the same benefit)
.

The reader may be wondering whether the improvements gained by choosing the columns from a distribution with exactly
s
non-zeros could alternatively be gained by choosing the rows from such a distribution. In fact, this is not the case. For instance, suppose
A
is a square
m\times m
matrix whose rows are independently drawn from the set of vectors that have all
0,-1,1
entries and exactly
s
nonzero entries. Then, the first column (or any other) of
A
has entries which are independent random variables which take the value
1
or
-1
with probability
\frac{s}{2m}
each, and
0
with probability
1-\frac{s}{m}
. The lower bound in Corollary
8
then applies, since it is realized by the set
T=\{e_{1}\}
which isolates the first column.

3
Normalizing the columns of an i.i.d. matrix

We now consider random matrices with independent, symmetric, subgaussian entries. We show that normalizing the columns improves the dependence of the isometry constant on the subgaussian norm. We assume the entries have variance 1 which implies that the rows are isotropic, thus fitting the models in
[

. This causes the columns to concentrate around
\sqrt{m}
and so we normalize them onto
\sqrt{m}S^{n-1}
to more easily compare to the results in

F

Let

be an

Remark 14

(Effect of normalizing columns)
.

Observe that the rows of

4
Proof of Theorem
3

As in our work
[
19
]
, whose exposition can be found in
[
26
, Section 9.1]
, Theorem
3
follows automatically from Talagrand’s majorizing measure theorem and the following result:

Theorem 17

(Subgaussian increments)
.

Let
A
be a random matrix satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
3
. Then the random process

Z_{x}:=\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}-\lVert x\rVert_{2}

has subgaussian increments, namely

\lVert Z_{x}-Z_{y}\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq CK\lVert x-y\rVert_{2}\quad\text{for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

To see how this leads to Theorem
3
, let us show how to combine Theorem
17
with the specialized version of Talagrand’s comparison inequality below. This result is from
[
19
]
; for more general versions, see
[
26
, Section 8]
.

Theorem 18

(Comparison inequality)
.

Let
(Z_{x})_{x\in T}
be a random process on a bounded set
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Assume that the process has subgaussian increments, that is there exists
M\geq 0
such that

\|Z_{x}-Z_{y}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq M\|x-y\|_{2}\;\text{ for all }\;x,y\in T.

Then

\E\sup_{x,y\in T}|Z_{x}-Z_{y}|\leq CM\,\E\sup_{x\in T}\langle g,x\rangle,

where
g\sim N(0,I_{n})
. Moreover, for any
u\geq 1
, the event

\sup_{x,y\in T}|Z_{x}-Z_{y}|\leq CM\left(\E\sup_{x\in T}\langle g,x\rangle+u\cdot\mathrm{diam}(T)\right)

holds with probability at least
1-e^{-u^{2}}
. Here
\mathrm{diam}(T):=\sup_{x,y\in T}\|x-y\|_{2}
.

The first part of Theorem
18
can be found in
[
24
, Theorem 2.4.12]
and the second part can be found in
[
10
, Theorem 3.2]
. See also
[
26
, Theorem 8.5.5]
.

Theorem
3
then follows by observing that for any
x_{0}\in T
,

\sup_{x\in T}\lvert Z_{x}\rvert\leq\lvert Z_{x_{0}}\rvert+\sup_{x\in T}\lvert Z_{x}-Z_{x_{0}}\rvert\leq\lvert Z_{x_{0}}\rvert+\sup_{x,y\in T}\lvert Z_{x}-Z_{y}\rvert.

The second summand is controlled by Theorems
18
and
17
. The first summand is a single subgaussian random variable. Indeed, by Proposition
19
below,
\lVert Z_{x_{0}}\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim K\lVert x_{0}\rVert_{2}\leq K\mathrm{rad}(T)
. It is relatively straightforward to complete the proof of Theorem
3
, see
[
13
, Section 4]
for the full detailed argument.

We now build the proof of Theorem
17
, starting with two special cases.

4.1
Unit vector
x
, zero vector
y

Let us first prove a partial case of Theorem
17
for
\lVert x\rVert_{2}=1
and
y=0
.

Proposition 19

(Concentration of norm)
.

Let
A
be a random matrix satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
3
. Then for any
x\in S^{n-1}
we have

\big\lVert\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{2}-1\big\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim K.

To prove this result, let us first note that
Ax
is a subgaussian random vector:

Lemma 20

(Sum of independent subgaussians)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent, mean zero, subgaussian random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Then

\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\leq C\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert X_{i}\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}.

In particular, under the assumptions of Theorem
3
, for any unit vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

\lVert Ax\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq CK.

Proof of Lemma
20
.

The first part is an easy consequence of Definition (
1
) and
[
26
, Proposition 2.6.1]
. The second part follows if we expand
Ax=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}A_{i}
and apply the first part to
X_{i}=x_{i}A_{i}
.
∎

We now proceed with a moment generating function (MGF) bound.
For any fixed
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, consider the function

\Phi_{\lambda}(z)\coloneqq\exp(\lambda z).

(8)

Then the MGF of a random variable
Z
can be expressed as
\E\Phi_{\lambda}(Z)
.

Lemma 21
.

Let
A
and
x
be as in Proposition
19
. Set
S:=\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}^{2}-1
. Then

\E\Phi_{\lambda}(S)\leq\exp(C\lambda^{2}K^{2})\quad\mbox{for}\quad\lvert\lambda\rvert\leq\frac{c}{K^{2}}.

Proof.

Expanding the norm, we get

\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}^{2}=\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}A_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i\neq j}x_{i}x_{j}\langle A_{i},A_{j}\rangle.

By assumption we have
\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{2}=1
and
\lVert x\rVert_{2}=1
. Thus,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}=1

an so

S=\sum_{i\neq j}x_{i}x_{j}\langle A_{i},A_{j}\rangle.

(9)

Since
\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{2}=1
by assumption, using Cauchy-Schwarz we can see that
S
is a bounded random variable, so
\Phi_{\lambda}(S)
is finite for all
\lambda
. Let’s now get a quantitative bound.

Fix any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and let
A^{\prime}
be an independent copy of
A
. Then

\displaystyle\E\Phi_{\lambda}(S)

\displaystyle=\E\Phi_{\lambda}\left(\sum_{i\neq j}x_{i}x_{j}\langle A_{i},A_{j}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\leq\E\Phi_{\lambda}\left(4\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}\langle A_{i},A^{\prime}_{j}\rangle\right)\quad\text{(decoupling \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{hdp-book}{}{}, Exercise~6.1.4]})}

\displaystyle=\E\Phi_{\lambda}\left(4\langle Ax,A^{\prime}x\rangle\right).

Let us now condition on
A
. Recall, by Lemma
20
,
\lVert A^{\prime}x\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq CK
.
Then the random variable
\langle Ax,A^{\prime}x\rangle
is (conditionally) subgaussian, and its conditional subgaussian norm is bounded by
CK\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}
, see
[
26
, Definition 3.4.1]
. Thus

\displaystyle\E\Phi_{\lambda}\left(4\langle Ax,A^{\prime}x\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\E_{A}\E_{A^{\prime}}\exp(4\lambda\langle Ax,A^{\prime}x\rangle)

\displaystyle\leq\E_{A}\exp(C\lambda^{2}K^{2}\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}^{2})\quad\text{(by Equation~\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{hdp-book}{}{}, (2.16)]})}.

We have shown that

\E\Phi_{\lambda}(S)\leq\E\exp(C\lambda^{2}K^{2}\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}^{2}).

Recall that
S:=\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}^{2}-1
. We now replace
\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}^{2}
with
S+1
in the equation above to yield a recursive equation for the moment generating function of
S
. This gives

\E\Phi_{\lambda}(S)\leq\exp(C\lambda^{2}K^{2})\cdot\E\exp(C\lambda^{2}K^{2}S)\quad\text{for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

(10)

Now assume that

0\leq\lambda\leq 1/(2CK^{2}).

Then
0\leq C\lambda K^{2}\leq 1/2
, and Jensen inequality gives

\E\exp(C\lambda^{2}K^{2}S)=\E\big[\exp(\lambda S)^{C\lambda K^{2}}\big]\leq\left[\E\exp(\lambda S)\right]^{C\lambda K^{2}}=[\E\Phi_{\lambda}(S)]^{C\lambda K^{2}}.

Insert this into Equation
10
and rearrange the terms to get

\E\Phi_{\lambda}(S)\leq\exp(C\lambda^{2}K^{2})^{1/(1-C\lambda K^{2})}.

Since
1-C\lambda K^{2}\geq 1/2
by assumption, we conclude that

\E\Phi_{\lambda}(S)\leq\exp(2C\lambda^{2}K^{2})\quad\text{for all }0\leq\lambda\leq 1/(2CK^{2}),

(11)

as desired.

Finally, let us lift the nonnegativity assumption and simply assume that

\lvert\lambda\rvert\leq 1/(2CK^{2}).

We have

\displaystyle\E\exp(C\lambda^{2}K^{2}S)

\displaystyle=\E\Phi_{\mu}(S)\quad\text{(where $\mu=C\lambda^{2}K^{2}$)}

\displaystyle\leq\exp(2C\mu^{2}K^{2})\quad\text{(using \eqref{eq: Phi bound for positive}, since $0\leq\mu\leq 1/(2CK^{2})$)}

\displaystyle\leq\exp(2C\lambda^{2}K^{2})\quad\text{(since $\mu\leq\lvert\lambda\rvert)$}.

Substitute this into (
10
) to get

\E\Phi_{\lambda}(S)\leq\exp(3C\lambda^{2}K^{2})\quad\text{for all }\lvert\lambda\rvert\leq 1/(2CK^{2}).

The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 22

(Why normalized columns give linear dependence on
K
)
.

We can now point out the first place where the assumption of normalized columns allows linear dependence on
K
in our main result. In the proof above,

S=\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}^{2}-1=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}(\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}-1)+\sum_{i\neq j}x_{i}x_{j}\langle A_{i},A_{j}\rangle.

Since the columns of
A
have norm 1, the first term disappears, leaving just a linear combination of the inner products of different columns (see Equation (
9
)). After decoupling, this gives the neat recursive equation (
10
) for
S
which ultimately allows a tight control of its tail. If we had not assumed normalized columns then the variations in the norms of
A_{i}
would have necessarily not allowed this (as follows from
[
13
, Proposition 4.5]
).

The following lemma converts the bound on the moment generating function of
S
into a tail bound.

Lemma 23
.

Let
S
be as in Lemma
21
. Then for any
t\geq 0

\mathbb{P}\left\{\lvert S\rvert\geq t\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{C}{K^{2}}\left(t^{2}\wedge t\right)\right).

Proof.

Let
0\leq\lambda\leq c/K^{2}
, as in Lemma
21
. Then

\mathbb{P}\left\{S\geq t\right\}=\mathbb{P}\left\{e^{\lambda S}\geq e^{\lambda t}\right\}\leq e^{-\lambda t}\E\Phi_{\lambda}(S)\leq\exp(-\lambda t+C\lambda^{2}K^{2}),

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
21
.

The optimal choice of
\lambda
subject to the constraint
\lambda\leq c/K^{2}
is

\lambda=t/(2CK^{2})\wedge c/K^{2}
. With this choice, we obviously have
\lambda\leq t/(2CK^{2})
, which is equivalent to

C\lambda^{2}K^{2}\leq\lambda t/2
. Thus,

\mathbb{P}\left\{S\geq t\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\leq\exp(-\lambda t+C\lambda^{2}K^{2})\leq\exp(-\lambda t/2)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{C^{\prime}}{K^{2}}\left(t^{2}\wedge t\right)\right).

\mathbb{P}\left\{-S\geq t\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}
may be bounded similarly, and the the proof is completed via a union bound.
∎

Proof of Proposition
19
.

By Lemma
23
, for
t\geq 0

\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\lvert\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}^{2}-1\right\rvert\geq t\right\}\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{C}{K^{2}}\left(t^{2}\wedge t\right)\right).

Now we use the following elementary observation, which holds for all
z,\delta\geq 0
:

\lvert z-1\rvert\geq\delta\quad\text{implies}\quad\lvert z^{2}-1\rvert\geq\delta\vee\delta^{2}.

We obtain

\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\lvert\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}-1\right\rvert\geq\delta\right\}\leq\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\lvert\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}^{2}-1\right\rvert\geq\delta\vee\delta^{2}\right\}\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{C\delta^{2}}{K^{2}}\right),

since if
t=\delta\vee\delta^{2}
then
t^{2}\wedge t=\delta^{2}
. The proof is complete.
∎

4.2
Vectors on the sphere, squared process

We now show that
\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}^{2}-\lVert Ay\rVert_{2}^{2}
has a mixed tail bound for
x,y\in S^{n-1}
. We will then convert that into a subgaussian tail bound for
Z_{x}-Z_{y}
in the next section.

Lemma 24

(Squared process)
.

Let
A
be a random matrix satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
3
.
Fix any pair of vectors
x,y\in S^{n-1}
.
Then the random variable

\Delta\coloneqq\frac{\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}^{2}-\lVert Ay\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\lVert x-y\rVert_{2}}

satisfies

\mathbb{P}\left\{\lvert\Delta\rvert\geq t\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{C}{K^{2}}\left(t^{2}\wedge t\right)\right).

Proof.

Set

u:=x+y,\quad v:=x-y.

Then

\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}^{2}-\lVert Ay\rVert_{2}^{2}=\langle Au,Av\rangle=\sum_{i}\langle A_{i},A_{i}\rangle u_{i}v_{i}+\sum_{i\neq j}\langle A_{i},A_{j}\rangle u_{i}v_{j}=:I+II.

Observe that
u_{i}v_{i}=x_{i}^{2}-y_{i}^{2}
and
\langle A_{i},A_{i}\rangle=\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}=1
. Thus

I=\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}-y_{i}^{2}=\lVert x\rVert_{2}^{2}-\lVert y\rVert_{2}^{2}=1-1=0.

Now, set
\hat{v}=v/\lVert v\rVert_{2}
so

\Delta=\frac{\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}^{2}-\lVert Ay\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\lVert v\rVert_{2}}=\sum_{i\neq j}\langle A_{i},A_{j}\rangle u_{i}\hat{v}_{i}.

Using decoupling (
[
26
, Exercise 6.1.4]
) as in the proof of Lemma
21
, we let
A^{\prime}
be an independent copy of
A
and get

\E\Phi_{\lambda}(\Delta)\leq\E\Phi_{\lambda}\left[4\langle Au,A^{\prime}\hat{v}\rangle\right].

As in the proof of Lemma
21
, taking the conditional expectation with respect to
A^{\prime}
gives

\E\Phi_{\lambda}(\Delta)\leq\E\exp(C\lambda^{2}K^{2}\lVert Au\rVert_{2}^{2}).

Observe that
\lVert u\rVert_{2}=\lVert x+y\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert x\rVert_{2}+\lVert y\rVert_{2}=2
and assume
\lvert\lambda\rvert\leq 1/(CK^{2})
. Then
C\lambda^{2}K^{2}\leq\lvert\lambda\rvert
and by Lemma
21

\E\exp(C\lambda^{2}K^{2}\lVert Au\rVert^{2})\leq\exp(C\lambda^{2}K^{2}).

Thus,

\E\Phi_{\lambda}(\Delta)\leq\exp(C\lambda^{2}K^{2})\qquad\mbox{for}\quad\lvert\lambda\rvert\leq\frac{c}{K^{2}}.

To finish, observe that this is exactly the same bound on the MGF that we had previously for
S=\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}^{2}-1
(as in Lemma
21
). Thus, it can be converted into the same tail bound as in Lemma
23
, but with
S
replaced by
\Delta
, as desired.
∎

4.3
Vectors on the sphere, original process

We now show that the random process
Z_{x}=\lvert\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}-1\rvert
has subgaussian increments for vectors on the sphere, i.e., we show that

\lVert Z_{x}-Z_{y}\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq CK\lVert x-y\rVert_{2}

when
\lVert x\rVert_{2}=\lVert y\rVert_{2}=1
.

This follows from the following lemma, together with the observation that

\lvert Z_{x}-Z_{y}\rvert=\lvert\lvert\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}-1\rvert-\lvert\lVert Ay\rVert_{2}-1\rvert\rvert\leq\lvert\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}-\lVert Ay\rVert_{2}\rvert.

Lemma 25
.

Fix any pair of vectors
x,y\in S^{n-1}
. Then

\left\lVert\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}-\lVert Ay\rVert_{2}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq CK\lVert x-y\rVert_{2}.

Proof.

This proof is essentially the same as the proof of
[
19
, Lemma 5.4]
. We repeat it here for convenience.

Assume
x\neq y
(as otherwise the result clearly holds) and set

Q:=\frac{\lvert\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}-\lVert Ay\rVert_{2}\rvert}{\lVert x-y\rVert_{2}}.

We wish to show that

\mathbb{P}\left\{Q\geq t\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\leq C\exp\left(-C\frac{t^{2}}{K^{2}}\right)\quad\mbox{for }t\geq 0.

(12)

We show this separately for small
t
and large
t
. Set
v=x-y
and
\hat{v}=v/\lVert v\rVert_{2}
.

Case 1:

t\geq 2
.

By the triangle inequality,

\lvert\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}-\lVert Ay\rVert_{2}\rvert\leq\lVert A(x-y)\rVert_{2}=\lVert Av\rVert_{2}
. Thus,

Q\leq\lVert A\hat{v}\rVert_{2}.

Then,

\mathbb{P}\left\{Q\geq t\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\leq\mathbb{P}\left\{\lVert A\hat{v}\rVert_{2}\geq t\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}=\mathbb{P}\left\{\lVert A\hat{v}\rVert_{2}-1\geq t-1\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\leq\mathbb{P}\left\{\lVert A\hat{v}\rVert_{2}-1\geq t/2\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}

since
t\geq 2
. Recall that
\lVert\lVert A\hat{v}\rVert_{2}-1\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim K
by Proposition
19
and so

\mathbb{P}\left\{\lVert A\hat{v}\rVert_{2}-1\geq t/2\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\leq 2\exp\left(-C\frac{t^{2}}{K^{2}}\right)

as desired.

Case 2:

t<2
.
Observe that

\frac{\lvert\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}-\lVert Ay\rVert_{2}\rvert}{\lVert x-y\rVert_{2}}=\frac{\lvert\Delta\rvert}{\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}+\lVert Ay\rVert_{2}}\leq\frac{\lvert\Delta\rvert}{\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}}

with
\Delta
defined as in Lemma
24
.

Define the events
F=\{\lvert\Delta\rvert\geq t/2\}
and
G=\{\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}\leq 1/2\}
so that

\left\{\frac{\lvert\Delta\rvert}{\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}}\geq t\right\}\subset F\cup G.

By the union bound

\mathbb{P}\left\{\frac{\lvert\Delta\rvert}{\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}}\geq t\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\leq\mathbb{P}(F)+\mathbb{P}(G).

By Lemma
24
,
\mathbb{P}(F)\leq 2\exp(-Ct^{2}/K^{2})
and by Proposition
19
,

\mathbb{P}(G)=\mathbb{P}\left\{\lVert Ax\rVert_{2}-1\leq-\frac{1}{2}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{C}{K^{2}}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{Ct^{2}}{K^{2}}\right)

since
t/2\leq 1
(we have absorbed two factors of 4 into the numeric constant
C
).

Thus,

\mathbb{P}(F)+\mathbb{P}(G)\leq 4\exp\left(-C\frac{t^{2}}{K^{2}}\right)

giving the desired bound on
\mathbb{P}\left\{Q\geq t\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}
.
∎

4.4
Arbitrary vectors, original process

We now show that

\lVert Z_{x}-Z_{y}\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim K\lVert x-y\rVert_{2}

(13)

for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, thus completing the proof of Theorem
17
. Again, we present this for convenience, since the argument is already made in
[
19
]
(see also
[
26
, Section 9.1.4]
).

Without loss of generality, assume
\lVert x\rVert_{2}\geq\lVert y\rVert_{2}
. Assume
y\neq 0
as otherwise Equation (
13
) is already shown by Proposition
19
. By a simple rescaling argument, we may assume
\lVert y\rVert_{2}=1
. Let
\hat{x}=x/\lVert x\rVert
, i.e., the projection onto the sphere (or the convex hull of the sphere). Since projection onto a convex set is contractive, and by definition of projection, we have

\lVert\hat{x}-y\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert x-y\rVert_{2}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\lVert x-\hat{x}\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert x-y\rVert_{2}.

(14)

By the triangle inequality,
\lVert Z_{x}-Z_{y}\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq\lVert Z_{x}-Z_{\hat{x}}\rVert_{\psi_{2}}+\lVert Z_{\hat{x}}-Z_{y}\rVert_{\psi_{2}}.
We now use the results from the previous sections to show that each term is bounded by
CK\lVert x-y\rVert_{2}
, thus completing the proof. Indeed, by linearity,
Z_{x}=\lVert x\rVert_{2}\cdot Z_{\hat{x}}
. Further,
\lVert x\rVert_{2}-1=\lVert x-\hat{x}\rVert_{2}
and so

\lVert Z_{x}-Z_{\hat{x}}\rVert_{\psi_{2}}=\lVert x-\hat{x}\rVert_{2}\cdot\lVert\lVert A\hat{x}\rVert_{2}-1\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq CK\lVert x-\hat{x}\rVert_{2}\leq CK\lVert x-y\rVert_{2}.

The first inequality follows by Proposition
19
and the second by Equation (
14
).

Next, by Lemma
25
followed by Equation (
14
),

\lVert Z_{\hat{x}}-Z_{y}\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq CK\lVert\hat{x}-y\rVert_{2}\leq CK\lVert x-y\rVert_{2}.

The proof is finished.

5
Proofs of the material in Sections
2
,
3

We begin with a lemma controlling the subgaussian norm of the columns of “approximately sparse” and “exactly sparse” matrices, as referenced in Sections
2
,
3
.

Lemma 26
.

Let
s\leq m
be a positive integer and consider random vectors
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The vector
X
is drawn uniformly from the set of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with that have all
0,1,-1
entries and exactly
s
non-zero entries. The entries of
Y
are independent and take the value
1
or
-1
with probability
\frac{s}{2m}
each, and
0
with probability
1-\frac{s}{m}
. Then,

\lVert X\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{\log(1+m/s)}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\lVert Y\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{\log(1+m/s)}}.

(15)

Proof.

It is not hard to show that by definition,
\lVert Y_{i}\rVert_{\psi_{2}}=1/\sqrt{\log(1+m/s)}
. The bound on
\lVert Y\rVert_{\psi_{2}}
then follows from
[
26
, 2.6.1]
and Definition (
1
).

We now bound the moments of the marginals of
X
by the moments of the marginals of
Y
, thus controlling
\lVert X\rVert_{\psi_{2}}
by
\lVert Y\rVert_{\psi_{2}}
. Below, given an event
F
and a random variable
Z
, we use the notation

\lVert Z|F\rVert_{\psi_{2}}:=\inf\{t>0:\E[\exp(Z^{2}/t^{2})|F]\}

i.e., it is the subgaussian norm of
Z
conditioned on
F
.

Let
u\in S^{n-1}
and let
G
be the event
G:=\{\lVert Y\rVert_{0}\geq s\}
where we use
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{0}
for the number of nonzero entries of a vector. Since
s
is the median of
\lVert Y\rVert_{0}
, we have
\mathbb{P}\left\{G\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\geq 1/2
(see
[
15
]
). Let
p\geq 1
and observe that

\E\lvert\langle Y,u\rangle\rvert^{p}\geq\E\left[\lvert\langle Y,u\rangle\rvert^{p}\Big|G\right]\cdot\mathbb{P}\left\{G\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\geq\E\left[\lvert\langle Y,u\rangle\rvert^{p}\Big|\lVert Y\rVert_{0}=s\right]\cdot\frac{1}{2}=\E\lvert\langle X,u\rangle\rvert^{p}\cdot\frac{1}{2}.

The last equality follows since conditioned on
\lVert Y\rVert_{0}=s
,
Y
has the same distribution as
X
. We have shown that

\E\lvert\langle X,u\rangle\rvert^{p}\leq 2\E\lvert\langle Y,u\rangle\rvert^{p}.

It follows from the moment-based characterization of the subgaussian norm
[
26
, Equation 2.15]
that
\lVert X\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq C\lVert Y\rVert_{\psi_{2}}
as desired.
∎

5.1
Proof of Corollary
13

Our strategy is to condition on the event
F
that the column norms of
A
are lower bounded, show that conditionally on this event
\tilde{A}
has independent, mean-zero, subgaussian columns, and then apply Corollary
4
.

The norm of a vector with independent subgaussian entries is well concentrated. Indeed, by
[
13
, Theorem 4.1]

the columns of
A
satisfy,

\lVert\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{2}-\sqrt{m}\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim K\sqrt{\log K}\qquad i=1,2,\ldots,n

and thus,

\mathbb{P}\left\{\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{m}}{2}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}=\mathbb{P}\left\{\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{2}-\sqrt{m}\leq-\frac{\sqrt{m}}{2}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\leq 2\exp\left(-C\frac{m}{K^{2}\log K}\right).

Then, by the union bound, the event

F:=\left\{\min_{i=1,2,\ldots,n}\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{2}\geq\frac{\sqrt{m}}{2}\right\}

satisfies

\mathbb{P}\left\{F\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\geq 1-2n\exp\left(-C\frac{m}{K^{2}\log K}\right)=1-2\exp\left(\log(n)-C\frac{m}{K^{2}\log K}\right).

By assumption,
m\geq CK^{2}\log(K)\cdot\log(n)
, and so the
\log(n)
term is insignificant, only changing the constant:

\mathbb{P}\left\{F\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\geq 1-2\exp\left(-C\frac{m}{K^{2}\log K}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}.

Now condition on the event
F
. Clearly, the columns of
A
are (conditionally) non-zero. Further,

\lVert\tilde{A_{i}}|F\rVert_{\psi_{2}}=\left\|\frac{\sqrt{m}}{\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{2}}A_{i}\bigg|F\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq 2\lVert A_{i}|F\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq C\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{\psi_{2}}.

(16)

The first inequality follows because
\sqrt{m}/\lVert A_{i}\rVert\leq 2
(conditionally). The second inequality follows because
conditioning on an event with probability at least
0.5
cannot increase the subgaussian norm of
A_{i}
by more than a constant factor. For instance, this follows by checking the moment condition in
[
26
, Proposition 2.5.2]
.

Thus, by
[
26
, Proposition 2.6.1]
,

\lVert\tilde{A_{i}}|F\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq C\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq CK.

Further, since the entries of
A_{i}
are symmetric, and thus the signs of those entries are independent from their magnitudes, the conditional entries of
\tilde{A_{i}}
are still symmetric. It follows that
\E[\tilde{A_{i}}|F]=0
. Summarizing, conditional on
F
,
\tilde{A}
has the following properties

\lVert\tilde{A_{i}}\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{m},\quad\lVert\tilde{A_{i}}|F\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq CK,\quad\E[\tilde{A_{i}}|F]=0.

Clearly, the columns of
\tilde{A}
remain independent conditional on
F
and so we are in the setting of Corollary
4
with
\lambda=\sqrt{m}
. The proposition follows.

Remark 27

(Replacing
m\geq CK^{2}\log(K)\cdot\log(n)
with
m\geq CK^{2}\log(K)
)
.

In Equation (
16
), conditioning on
F
is equivalent to conditioning on the event that
\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{2}\geq m/\sqrt{2}
(by independence). Thus, we only need
\mathbb{P}\left\{\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{2}\geq m/\sqrt{2}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\geq 1/2
for Equation (
16
) to hold. This only requires
m\geq CK^{2}\log(K)
, rather than
m\geq CK^{2}\log(K)\cdot\log(n)
(since we may remove the union bound involved in controlling
\mathbb{P}(F)
). Thus Corollary
13
holds with the milder assumption on
m
, but without a lower bound on
\mathbb{P}(F)
. Similar arguments allow us to take the mild assumption
s\geq 1
in Corollary
15
at the expense of losing the lower bound on
\mathbb{P}(F)
.

6
Conclusion

We have shown that random matrices with normalized independent columns have similar behaviour that of random matrices with independent rows, except with better dependence on the subguassian norm. We conclude with an open question: Can the assumption of normalized columns be removed? This question is made concrete with the following conjecture.

Conjecture 6.28
.

Let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix whose columns
A_{i}
are independent, mean zero, subgaussian random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
\left\lVert A_{i}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq K
. Then, for any subset
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\E\sup_{x\in T}\Big\lvert\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{2}-\left\lVert Dx\right\rVert_{2}\Big\rvert\leq C(K)\gamma(T)

where
D
is the diagonal matrix with
D_{i,i}=\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{2}
and
C(K)
depends only on
K
.
