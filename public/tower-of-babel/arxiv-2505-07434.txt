Efficient Lifting of Discrete Logarithms Modulo Prime Powers
Giovanni Viglietta, Yasuyuki Kachi

Abstract
We present a deterministic algorithm that, given a prime p p and a solution x ∈ ℤ x\in\mathbb{Z} to the discrete logarithm problem a x ≡ b ( mod p ) a^{x}\equiv b\pmod{p} with p ∤ a p\nmid a , efficiently lifts it to a solution modulo p k p^{k} , i.e., a x ≡ b ( mod p k ) a^{x}\equiv b\pmod{p^{k}} , for any fixed k ≥ 1 k\geq 1 . The algorithm performs k ⁡ ( ⌈ log 2 ⁡ p ⌉ + 2 ) + O ⁡ ( log ⁡ p ) k(\lceil\log_{2}p\rceil+2)+O(\log p) multiplications modulo p k p^{k} in the worst case, improving upon prior lifting methods by at least a factor of 8.

Overview.
The algorithm assumes that
a^{z}\equiv b\pmod{p}
and returns
x
such that
a^{x}\equiv b\pmod{p^{k}}
(or reports that no such

exists), provided that
p\nmid a
.
1
1

1

The case

is of little interest, as the minimum solution

can easily be found without knowledge of

, either via an exhaustive search, since necessarily
0\leq x\leq k
, or by solving the linear equation
\nu_{p}(a)x=\nu_{p}(b)
, after ruling out some trivial cases. The details are left to the reader.

The final solution

is expressed in the form
x=(p-1)y+z
(Line 39), where

is given, and

is computed incrementally, one digit in base

at a time. Specifically, the algorithm computes a sequence
(y_{j})_{j\geq 0}
such that
y_{j}<p^{j}
, that is,
y_{j}
has
j
digits in base

. In particular,
y_{0}=0
(Line 7) and
y_{j+1}=y_{j}+d_{j}p^{j}
with
0\leq d_{j}<p
(Line 28). That is,
y_{j+1}
is obtained by appending a new digit

to the left of the base-

Accordingly, we can define

and

(Lines 6, 29–35). As we will prove in

3
, if the digit

is chosen appropriately (Lines 25 and 27), then

is a “better approximation” of

than

, in some sense. Indeed, we can immediately verify that

and therefore

and

coincide at least on their first digit in base

. Then, as the computation progresses, each new

matches

on one additional digit. More specifically, if we define

(Lines 13–18), then

Thus, by the time

, the numbers

and
b
are equal modulo

, and

is the final solution.

Thus, the first digit of

Proof of Correctness

In this section we set out to justify all the claims we made in
Section

2
about the correctness of our algorithm. We first introduce three elementary lemmas. Note that
Lemma

1
is a well-known fact in number theory, but its proof has been included for self-containment.

Lemma 1
.

Let
p
be a prime,
j\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, and
x\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
p\nmid x
. Then

\ord_{p^{j+1}}(x)\in\left\{\ord_{p^{j}}(x),\ p\cdot\ord_{p^{j}}(x)\right\}

Proof.

Let
d_{j}=\ord_{p^{j}}(x)
for
j\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. By definition of
d_{j+1}
, we have

x^{d_{j+1}}\equiv 1\pmod{p^{j+1}}

and in particular

x^{d_{j+1}}\equiv 1\pmod{p^{j}}

which implies that
d_{j}\mid d_{j+1}
. Now, since

x^{d_{j}}\equiv 1\pmod{p^{j}}

there exists an integer
m
such that
x^{d_{j}}=1+mp^{j}
. Thus,

x^{pd_{j}}=\left(x^{d_{j}}\right)^{p}=\left(1+mp^{j}\right)^{p}=\sum_{i=0}^{p}{p\choose i}m^{i}p^{ij}=1+{p\choose 1}mp^{j}+np^{2j}=1+mp^{j+1}+np^{2j}

for some integer
n
. Since
j\geq 1
, the last term in the previous sum vanishes modulo
p^{j+1}
. Hence,

x^{pd_{j}}\equiv 1+mp^{j+1}+np^{2j}\equiv 1\pmod{p^{j+1}}

We conclude that
d_{j}\mid d_{j+1}\mid pd_{j}
, and by the primality of
p
either
d_{j+1}=d_{j}
or
d_{j+1}=pd_{j}
.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Let
p
be an odd prime,
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that
1\leq r\leq\nu_{p}(x-1)
. Then, for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
,

x^{p^{j}}\equiv 1+hp^{r+j}\pmod{p^{r+j+1}}

(1)

where
h=\frac{x-1}{p^{r}}\mod p
.

Proof.

We will prove
Equation

1
by induction on
j\geq 0
. For
j=0
,
Equation

1
becomes

x\equiv 1+hp^{r}\pmod{p^{r+1}}

which holds by definition of
h
. Assuming
Equation

1
to hold for some
j\geq 0
, let us prove it for
j+1
in lieu of
j
. By assumption,
x^{p^{j}}=1+hp^{r+j}+mp^{r+j+1}
for some integer
m
. Thus,

x^{p^{j+1}}=\left(1+hp^{r+j}+mp^{r+j+1}\right)^{p}=\left(1+p^{r+j}(h+mp)\right)^{p}=\sum_{i=0}^{p}{p\choose i}p^{i(r+j)}(h+mp)^{i}

(2)

Observe that the term
{p\choose i}p^{i(r+j)}
is a multiple of
p^{r+j+2}
for all
1<i<p
. Indeed,

{p\choose i}=\frac{p(p-1)\cdots(p-i+1)}{i(i-1)\cdots 2\cdot 1}

is a multiple of
p
, because the prime factor
p
in the numerator is not canceled by any factor in the denominator, since
i<p
. Therefore,

\nu_{p}\left({p\choose i}p^{i(r+j)}\right)=i(r+j)+1\geq 2(r+j)+1\geq r+j+2

which holds because
r\geq 1
and
j\geq 0
. Moreover, for
i=p
, since
p
is an odd prime, we have

\nu_{p}\left({p\choose p}p^{p(r+j)}\right)=p(r+j)\geq 3(r+j)\geq r+j+2

Thus, every term of the summation in
Equation

2
vanishes modulo
p^{r+j+2}
except possibly for
i=0
and
i=1
. It follows that
Equation

1
is verified for
j+1
:

x^{p^{j+1}}\equiv 1+{p\choose 1}p^{r+j}(h+mp)\equiv 1+hp^{r+j+1}+mp^{r+j+2}\equiv 1+hp^{r+j+1}\pmod{p^{r+j+2}}

which concludes our proof by induction.
∎

.

and

x^{2^{j+1}}=1+h2^{r+j}+m2^{r+j+1}

. Thus

is odd, implying that

and hence

We conclude that

∎

We are finally ready to prove that our algorithm is correct.

Theorem 4
.

The algorithm in
Listing

is correct. That is, if

is a prime,
p\nmid a
,

, and

Proof.

We can immediately rule out the uninteresting special case

, and the algorithm correctly returns

in this case. So, let

and let us assume that

is an odd prime; the special case

will be discussed later. A solution

can always be expressed in the form

with

and

, by Euclidean division. We will first prove that returning

in Line 22 is correct.

Since

, by Fermat’s Little Theorem we have

. Thus,

a^{x}\equiv a^{(p-1)y+n}\equiv\left(a^{p-1}\right)^{y}a^{n}\equiv 1^{y}a^{n}\equiv a^{n}\pmod{p}

Since
a^{z}\equiv b\pmod{p}
by assumption, and

, we must have

n\equiv z\pmod{\ord_{p}(a)}

Define

and
h=\frac{a^{p-1}-1}{p^{r}}
, as in
Listing

1
. Due to Fermat’s Little Theorem, and because

, we have

as well. Also, by definition of
\nu_{p}
,

is an integer. Since

, we have
\ord_{p^{r}}(a)\mid p-1
, and therefore
\ord_{p^{r}}(a)\leq p-1
.

Lemma

1
with

and

implies that
\ord_{p^{r}}(a)=\ord_{p}(a)p^{i}
with
0\leq i<r
. Since
\ord_{p^{r}}(a)\leq p-1
, we must have

and hence
\ord_{p^{r}}(a)=\ord_{p}(a)
. Therefore,

and so

. Now, if
x
is a solution to
a^{x}\equiv b\pmod{p^{r}}
, then necessarily

b\equiv a^{x}\equiv a^{(p-1)y+n}\equiv\left(a^{p-1}\right)^{y}a^{n}\equiv a^{n}\equiv a^{z}\pmod{p^{r}}

So, if
a^{z}\not\equiv b\pmod{p^{r}}
, then there is no solution to the equation modulo
p^{r}
, and therefore no solution modulo
p^{k}
, because

by construction. We conclude that returning
None
in Line 22, after checking that

(Line 20) is correct, as there is no solution

in this case.

We will now prove that, if the condition
a^{z}\equiv b\pmod{p^{r}}
holds (and so the algorithm does not return

), then there is a unique integer

, with

, that satisfies the equation

. Moreover, we will show that the

digits of

in base

are correctly computed in Lines 25, 27, and 28 of the algorithm, and this will conclude the proof of correctness for any odd prime

.

We will prove our claim by induction. Assume that the first

digits of

have been correctly computed, i.e., we have
y_{j}<p^{j}
such that

There are

possible choices for the next digit of

; choosing a particular digit

, with

, gives rise to

and

Applying
Lemma

2
with

(recall that

), we obtain

a^{(p-1)p^{j}}\equiv 1+hp^{r+j}\pmod{p^{r+j+1}}

Thus, we can compute

, for
0\leq d<p-1
, as follows:

\displaystyle c^{(d+1)}_{j+1}-c^{(d)}_{j+1}

\displaystyle=a^{(p-1)\left((d+1)p^{j}+y_{j}\right)+z}-a^{(p-1)\left(dp^{j}+y_{j}\right)+z}

\displaystyle=a^{(p-1)\left(dp^{j}+y_{j}\right)+z}\left(a^{(p-1)p^{j}}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\left(a^{dp^{j}}\right)^{p-1}a^{(p-1)y_{j}+z}\left(a^{(p-1)p^{j}}-1\right)

\displaystyle\equiv(1+m_{1}p)(b+m_{2}p)hp^{r+j}

\displaystyle\equiv hbp^{r+j}\pmod{p^{r+j+1}}

for some integers

and

. In other words, incrementing the

th digit of

Now, in order to match the

. Moreover, recall that

Therefore, it is correct to consider the two options

Otherwise, if the correct

, we compute the

To set up a proof by induction, assume that

for some

, since

\displaystyle c^{(1)}_{j+1}-c^{(0)}_{j+1}

\displaystyle=a^{2^{j+1}+y_{j}}-a^{y_{j}}

For some integers

∎

4
Optimizations and Performance Analysis

Measuring complexity.
We will analyze the complexity of our algorithm in terms of the number of multiplications modulo
p^{k}
it performs in the worst case, as a function of the parameters
p
and
k
.

This is justified by the fact that additions and subtractions are relatively low-cost operations in standard multitape Turing machines, requiring only
O(n)
steps, where
n
is the number of digits of the operands.

By contrast, there is an abundance of multiplication algorithms, ranging from the schoolbook long multiplication algorithm in
O(n^{2})
steps to the Harvey–Hoeven algorithm in
O(n\log n)
steps. Asymptotically faster multiplication algorithms usually introduce greater constant factors, making them advantageous only for computation with very large numbers. On the other hand, the modular exponentiation
a^{j}\modulo m
is typically performed by repeated squaring, which takes roughly
2\lceil\log_{2}j\rceil
multiplications modulo
m
.

Moreover, we will assume that large integers are internally stored in base
p
(or a power of
p
). This makes divisions and multiplications by powers of
p
essentially free operations, which is particularly convenient for our algorithm. Indeed, the only integer division or modulo operation by a non-power of
p
found in our algorithm is in Line 3 of
Listing

1
: a division by the relatively small number
p-1
, which is a non-essential normalization step and can optionally be removed without compromising the algorithm’s correctness.

For the above reasons, we will measure the complexity of our algorithm by counting the number of multiplications modulo
p^{k}
it performs in the worst case. As we will see, after several optimizations, this number will turn out to be
k(\lceil\log_{2}p\rceil+2)+O(\log p)
.

Performance of Bach’s algorithm.
We will describe the discrete logarithm lifting technique by Bach
[
3
]
using the notation of
Section

2
. That is, we are given
a^{z}\equiv b\pmod{p}
with
p\nmid a
and seek
x
such that
a^{x}\equiv b\pmod{p^{k}}.
For
p\neq 2
, Bach’s algorithm first computes
\theta(a)
and
\theta(b)
, where

\theta(x)=\frac{x^{(p-1)p^{k-1}}-1}{p^{k}}\mod p^{k-1}

(4)

Then the algorithm solves the linear equation
\theta(a)y\equiv\theta(b)\pmod{p^{k-1}}
and, if
y
exists, it returns

x=\left((z-y)p^{k-1}+y\right)\mod{p^{(p-1)p^{k-1}}}

The numerator of the fraction in
Equation

4
has to be computed modulo
p^{2k-1}
. Once
\theta(a)
and
\theta(b)
are known, the rest of the algorithm is relatively simple, and its running time negligible.

Thus, the bottleneck of Bach’s algorithm is computing the function
\theta
twice, which essentially involves two exponentiations modulo
p^{2k-1}
, each of which takes

2\lceil\log_{2}(p-1)p^{k-1}\rceil\approx 2k\log_{2}p

multiplications modulo
p^{2k-1}
. The complexity of Bach’s algorithm is therefore roughly

2\cdot 2k\log_{2}p\cdot M\left(\log_{p}p^{2k-1}\right)=4k\log_{2}p\cdot M(2k-1)

(5)

where
M(n)
is the running time of the chosen multiplication algorithm when applied to operands of
n
digits in base
p
.

We remark that this is not only the worst-case running time, but the running time in all cases, because Bach’s algorithm starts out by computing
\theta(a)
and
\theta(b)
and then decides whether the problem has a solution or not based on the linear equation in
y
, which involves
\theta(a)
and
\theta(b)
.

Performance of
Listing

1
.
We now study the performance of our algorithm as defined in
Listing

1
. We will later develop some optimizations that further reduce its running time by a constant factor.

Up to the main loop of Line 26, the only non-negligible operations are the three exponentiations modulo
p^{k}
(or modulo

) with exponent at most
p
required to compute
c_{0}
,
e_{0}
, and

. Indeed, recall that if large numbers are represented in base

and

takes a negligible time. This whole part takes

.

As for the main loop, we do at most

We did not justify this part in

Conclusion

We have proposed an efficient discrete logarithm lifting algorithm, i.e., an algorithm for solving the modular equation
a^{x}\equiv b\pmod{p^{k}}
when an integer
z
such that
a^{z}\equiv b\pmod{p}
is given, and
a
is not a multiple of the prime
p
. Of course the equation has either no solutions or infinitely many solutions, since

.

When a solution exists, our algorithm expresses it as
x=(p-1)y+z
, where
y\geq 0
is smallest possible. This number
x
is guaranteed to be the minimum non-negative solution to the modular equation, provided that
\ord_{p}(a)=p-1
. Otherwise, if
\ord_{p}(a)
is known, we can use it in lieu of
p-1
in Lines 3, 13, and 39 of
Listing

1
and in Line 41 of
Listing

3
. As a result, the returned value

satisfies
0\leq x<\ord_{p^{k}}(a)
, and is therefore the minimum non-negative solution (this easily follows from our analysis of the algorithm in
Theorem

4
).

If large integers are represented modulo
p
(or powers of
p

k(\lceil\log_{2}p\rceil+2)+O(\log p)

multiplications modulo
p^{k}
in the worst case (typically much fewer operations are needed, especially when the solution
x
is small or does not exist). This is an improvement by at least a factor of 8 on a previous algorithm by Bach
[
3
]
.

The bottleneck of our algorithm is, by far, the call to
pow_dual

Listing

3
. Future optimization efforts should focus on improving this step.
