Dust coagulation assisted by streaming instability in protoplanetary disks
Ryosuke T. Tominaga, Hidekazu Tanaka

Abstract
The streaming instability is a promising mechanism for planetesimal formation. The instability can rapidly form dense clumps that collapse self-gravitationally, which is efficient for large dust grains with the Stokes number on the order of 0.1. However, dust growth models predict that collisional fragmentation prevents dust grains from growing to such sizes. We perform local simulations of the streaming instability and measure characteristic collision velocities and collision rates of dust grains based on their trajectories in moderate clumping. The collision velocities are on the order of 0.1 percent of the sound speed or lower, implying that dust grains can overcome the fragmentation barrier via the clumping. We also find that the collision rates are appreciably high regardless of the low collision velocities. Corresponding timescales are on the order of ten Keplerian periods or shorter, suggesting that dust grains can overcome the drift barrier as well. This streaming-instability-assisted (SI-assisted) coagulation greatly relaxes the conditions for planetesimal formation as recently implied.

I
Introduction

Formation of planetesimals from dust grains is one of the key steps in plant formation. There are some obstacles known to prevent dust coagulation in protoplanetary disks. One of the major obstacles is the radial drift of dust toward the central star
[
87
,
85
, e.g.,]
, which is caused by different orbital velocities of dust and gas. While dust grains tend to move at Keplerian velocities, gas moves at sub-Keplerian velocities because of a pressure gradient force in the radial direction. Because of the headwind of gas, the dust grains lose angular momentum and move inward. The maximum drift speed is on the order of 0.01 au/yr in protoplanetary disks. Such fast drifting motion prevents dust grains from growing larger before they reach the central star
[
13
, e.g.,]
.

Another obstacle is collisional fragmentation
[
10
, e.g.,]
. Experiments and numerical simulations suggested critical velocities beyond which dust grains are disrupted
[
11
,
12
,
83
, e.g.,]
. Critical velocities depend on material properties, including chemical composition and monomer size of dust aggregates
[
18
,
22
, e.g.,]
. According to
Wada et al. [83]
, the critical velocity for water ice aggregates with 0.1
\mu\mathrm{m}
-sized monomers is
\sim 50\;\mathrm{m/s}
, which is comparable to the maximum drift speed. Recent studies have shown that
\mathrm{CO}_{2}
ice is much less sticky than water ice
[
52
,
53
]
. They suggested that the critical velocity for
\mathrm{CO}_{2}
ice is about 10 times lower than that for water ice. Thus, fragmentation may prevent dust coagulation even in a cold outer region when dust is covered by
\mathrm{CO}_{2}
ice mantles
[
58
,
55
, see also]
.

One of the promising mechanisms to circumvent the obstacles is hydrodynamic instabilities
[e.g.,
71
,
43
, and references therein]
. Among them, the streaming instability (SI) is the leading and extensively studied mechanism
[
92
, e.g.,]
. The SI is driven by aerodynamic interaction between drifting dust and gas
[
72
,
73
,
49
, e.g.,]
and can increase local dust densities by orders of magnitude
[
37
,
4
, e.g.,]
. Numerical simulations of the SI have shown that the local dust density can exceed the Roche density, meaning that resulting dust clumps gravitationally collapse into planetesimals
[
35
,
38
,
70
,
61
,
1
, e.g.,]
. In contrast to the gravitational instability
[
60
,
68
]
, the SI does not require self-gravity and is thus expected to operate at various stages of the disk evolution in terms of dust mass.

Conditions for planetesimal formation via the SI have been investigated in previous studies
[
14
,
89
,
44
,
47
]
. They performed vertically stratified simulations for multiple parameter sets and derived critical dust-to-gas ratios as a function of the Stokes number,
\mathrm{St}
, which is the dimensionless stopping time of dust.
Carrera et al. [14]
found the critical dust-to-gas surface density ratio to be
\simeq 0.02
for
\mathrm{St}\simeq 0.1
and larger for smaller
\mathrm{St}
. Although the critical values have been updated in the subsequent studies, they also show that the critical dust-to-gas ratio decreases as
\mathrm{St}
increases to
\mathrm{St}\sim 0.1

[
89
,
44
,
46
]
.
Lim et al. [47]
simulated the SI with forced gas turbulence in stratified simulations. They found that the condition is well described in terms of midplane dust-to-gas ratios, and the critical value is
\simeq 1
for
\mathrm{St}=0.1
and a few times higher for
\mathrm{St}=0.01
(see Figure 4 therein). The critical values are similar among the runs with different strength of the turbulence. These studies indicate that the SI can form planetesimals if dust grains grow to the size of
\mathrm{St}\sim 0.01-0.1
. The required
\mathrm{St}
may be even smaller since
Yang et al. [89]
and
Li & Youdin [44]
observed strong clumping in runs with
\mathrm{St}=10^{-3}
.

The amount of concentrated dust grains via the SI depends on
\mathrm{St}
.
Johansen & Youdin [37]
showed cumulative dust density distributions obtained from their unstratified simulations (see their Figure 11). They showed that more dust grains reside in overdense regions for
\mathrm{St}=1
than for
\mathrm{St}=0.1
. According to the stratified simulations with multiple dust species
[
4
]
, the mass fraction of dust grains in overdense regions seems largest for
\mathrm{St}\sim 10^{-0.5}
. The concentration of smaller dust grains (
\mathrm{St}<0.1
) is limited, and the mass fraction of dust grains in dense regions can be an order of magnitude smaller than for
\mathrm{St}\sim 0.1-1
. Recent high-resolution simulations with
\mathrm{St}=0.01
also showed that the dust densities exceed the Roche density only at limited grid cells
[
46
]
.

Besides, the previous numerical simulations have shown that it takes long time for the dust density to reach the Roche density when
\mathrm{St}
is small. According to
Li & Youdin [44]
, duration time of this pre-clumping phase can be a few hundreds of the Keplerian periods for
\mathrm{St}\sim 10^{-2}
unless dust-to-gas surface density ratios are initially high enough. This would be problematic for planetesimal formation in local regions such as a gas bump.
Carrera & Simon [15]
simulated the SI in a gas bump and showed that the clumping with
\mathrm{St}\simeq 0.01
is so slow that the dust densities do not exceed the Roche density before the dust crosses the bump
[
17
, see also]
.

These studies suggest that planetesimal formation via the SI requires sufficiently large
\mathrm{St}
(e.g., 0.1-0.3) from a viewpoint of planetesimal formation efficiency. The required dust size seems comparable to the drift-limited size
[
13
, e.g., see]
. The dust growth model predicts much smaller
\mathrm{St}
when the critical velocity is lower than
\sim 10\;\mathrm{m/s}
and fragmentation hinders dust growth
[
24
, e.g., see]
. As mentioned above, dust grains will experience such disruptive collisions even in a cold region because of the low critical velocity for
\mathrm{CO}_{2}
ice. Therefore, an additional mechanism to circumvent the fragmentation barrier is necessary to trigger the strong clumping and planetesimal formation via the SI.

The dust growth beyond the fragmentation barrier potentially occurs during moderate clumping due to the SI.
Klahr & Schreiber [39]
suggested that large-scale gas turbulence is ineffective in stirring dust grains once they are locally concentrated. In such a case, the SI-driven turbulent motion will be the dominant source of collision velocities. Previous studies of the SI showed that the collision velocities can be so low that dust grains avoid disruptive collisions in overdense regions
[
36
,
7
,
4
]
. According to
Schreiber & Klahr [66]
, velocity dispersion of dust grains in the SI turbulence decreases with increasing dust density
[
4
, see also]
. Based on these studies, we estimated coagulation timescales in our previous study and suggested that dust grains have enough time to grow before the dust densities exceed the Roche density via the SI
[
79
]
. The above process is thus a promising way to bridge the gap: collision velocities start decreasing once the SI develops, which allows dust grains to grow beyond the fragmentation barrier.

In this work, we perform numerical simulations of the SI to measure coagulation rates more precisely. Our measurement takes into account time variations in dust densities and velocities along their trajectories in the SI turbulence. We demonstrate that the SI-assisted coagulation greatly relaxes the conditions for planetesimal formation.

We note that
Ho et al. [30]
performed numerical simulations incorporating both the SI and dust growth. They also imply that the dust growth during the SI can relax the conditions for planetesimal formation since the Stokes number is enhanced. In their study, they simply adopt a constant strength of turbulence to mimic the SI-induced collisions. In contrast, we focus on revealing collision properties of the SI-assisted coagulation, based on which we develop a simple model to show relaxed conditions. Therefore, this study and
Ho et al. [30]
are complementary to each other.

This paper is organized as follows. We describe basic equations and numerical methods in Section
II
and show the results of the measurement of coagulation rates as well as characteristic dust densities and collision velocities. We discuss implications to planetesimal formation and validities of our simulations in Section
IV
. We conclude in Section
V
.

II
Method

We use the Athena code
[
76
,
75
,
5
]
to simulate the SI in an unmagnetized isothermal gas. Athena is a grid-based code and includes a super-particle module developed by
Bai & Stone [5]
for modeling a dusty gas system. The super-particle module allows us to measure physical properties of (sub-)grid-scale dust collisions (collision velocities and rates), which is in contrast to fully grid-based codes based on two-fluid approximation of dusty gas disks.

We adopt the local shearing coordinates
(x,\;y,\;z)=(r-R,\;R(\phi-\Omega t),z)
, where
(r,\;\phi,\;z)
denotes the cylindrical coordinates,
R
denotes radial distance from the central star of mass
M_{\ast}
, and
\Omega
is the Keplerian angular velocity
[
28
]
. We use the cornered transport upwind (CTU) integrator
[
19
]
and the HLLC Rieman solver with the piecewise parabolic reconstruction to calculate numerical fluxes
[
20
,
76
, see]
. We assume an axisymmetric disk and perform two-dimensional simulations. In this work, we omit the vertical stellar gravity. The following subsections describe basic equations, numerical settings and diagnostics to estimate coagulation rates in detail.

II.1
Basic equations

The equations for the gas in the local shearing coordinates are

\frac{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{g}}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\left(\rho_{\mathrm{g}}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\right)=0,

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{g}}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle+\nabla\cdot\left(\rho_{\mathrm{g}}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\mbox{\boldmath$u$}+P\mbox{\boldmath$I$}\right)

\displaystyle=\rho_{\mathrm{g}}\left[2\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\times\Omega\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{z}+3\Omega^{2}x\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{x}+\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{d}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{g}}}\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}-\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}{t_{\mathrm{stop}}}\right].

(2)

In the above equations,
\rho_{\mathrm{g}}
and
u
denote the gas density and velocities in the local frame, and
P
denotes the gas pressure. We use the isothermal equation of state,
P=\rho_{\mathrm{g}}c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}
, where
c_{\mathrm{s}}
is the isothermal sound speed. Unit vectors in each direction are denoted by
\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{x},\;\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{y}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{z}
. The final term on the right hand side of Equation (
2
) represents the backreaction from dust to gas. The backreaction depends on the dust density
\rho_{\mathrm{d}}
and the stopping time
t_{\mathrm{stop}}
.

The super-particle module of Athena utilizes the following equation of motion
[
5
]
:

\frac{d\mbox{\boldmath$v$}_{i}}{dt}=-2\eta v_{\mathrm{K}}\Omega\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{x}+2\mbox{\boldmath$v$}_{i}\times\Omega\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{z}+3\Omega^{2}x_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{x}-\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}{t_{\mathrm{stop}}},

(3)

where the subscript
i
represents a label of a super-particle. As described in
Bai & Stone [5]
, the orbital motion of super-particles is forced to be super-Keplerian by a constant force term,
-2\eta v_{\mathrm{K}}\Omega\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{x}
, which mimics the effect of the radially outward pressure gradient in the gas disk. The coefficient
\eta v_{\mathrm{K}}
represents the velocity difference from the Keplerian velocity
v_{\mathrm{K}}
that the gas should have in a dust-free case, where

\eta\equiv-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{c_{\mathrm{s}}}{v_{\mathrm{K}}}\right)^{2}\frac{d\ln\rho_{\mathrm{g}}}{d\ln r}.

(4)

In this work, we assume
\eta v_{\mathrm{K}}=0.05c_{\mathrm{s}}
. We refer readers to
Bai & Stone [5]
for more details of this approach.

We employ the triangular-shaped cloud (TSC) interpolation scheme to calculate the drag force on super-particles. This scheme is also used to calculate the backreaction from super-particles to gas at grid points.

II.2
Numerical settings

Our study is concerned with efficiency of dust coagulation especially for small dust grains whose Stokes number is much less than unity. Since the spatial scale of the SI is shorter for smaller
\mathrm{St}
, we set the radial and vertical extents (
L_{x},\;L_{z}
) of the domain to be
0.01H
in this work. We fix the domain size in a parameter study where
\mathrm{St}
ranges from 0.01 to 1. We consider this setting reasonable although the most unstable wavelength is much larger for
\mathrm{St}=1
. This is because such large dust grains should sediment in a disk, and the dust scale height
H_{\mathrm{d}}
can be on the order of
10^{-2}H

[
25
,
93
]
:

H_{\mathrm{d}}=\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\mathrm{St}}}H\sim 10^{-2}H\left(\frac{\alpha}{10^{-4}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{\mathrm{St}}{1}\right)^{-1/2},

(5)

where
\alpha
is the dimensionless measure of turbulence strength
[
69
]
. Recent ALMA observations suggest weak turbulence of
\alpha\sim 10^{-5}-10^{-3}

[
26
,
27
,
59
,
81
]
, which motivates us to use
10^{-4}
as the reference value in Equation (
5
). Modes whose length scale is longer than
H_{\mathrm{d}}
would not develop, and thus we expect that adopting the small domain size for runs with
\mathrm{St}=1
would be valid. We adopt the periodic boundary condition in all directions
[
6
, e.g.,]
, which seems valid if the domain size is smaller than the dust scale height.

The number of cells is
N_{x}\times N_{z}=256\times 256
, and the total number of super-particles is
9N_{x}N_{z}
.
Bai & Stone [5]
suggested that one particle per cell on average is sufficient for convergence. Our simulations use the larger number of particles to estimate their velocity dispersion as in
Schreiber & Klahr [66]
. The Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy number was fixed to 0.5.

Initial conditions are the same as those in previous studies
[
5
,
66
,
8
, e.g.,]
. The initial gas density
\rho_{\mathrm{g},0}
is uniform. The super-particles are randomly distributed, and the resulting density fluctuations are seed perturbations. We initialize the radial and azimuthal velocities using the equilibrium drift velocities with the uniform dust-to-gas ratio
\epsilon\equiv\rho_{\mathrm{d},0}/\rho_{\mathrm{g},0}

[
54
]
. Initial vertical velocities of dust and gas are set to be zero.

We set
\rho_{\mathrm{g},0}=c_{\mathrm{s}}=\Omega=1
in code units. Parameters in this study are the Stokes number
\mathrm{St}\equiv t_{\mathrm{stop}}\Omega
and the initial dust-to-gas ratio
\epsilon
. Table
1
summarizes parameter sets explored in this study and times (
t_{\mathrm{s}},\;t_{\mathrm{e}}
) between which we measure coagulation rates.

II.3
Diagnostics

Figure 1:
Top three panels show dust density distributions at
t=t_{\mathrm{s}}
for the mr1St1, mr1st3, and mr1st10 runs. Bottom panels show histograms of the time-averaged coagulation rates
\overline{t_{\mathrm{coag},i}^{-1}}
(see Equation (
11
)). The histograms show single-peak profiles, with which we can estimate the typical evolutionary timescales of the systems.

Collision probability depends on dust density and collision velocity, which vary spatially and temporally in the SI-driven turbulence. In this study, we take into account their variations along particle trajectories and derive statistically-averaged collision rates. The collision rates are estimated for equal-mass collisions since we assume single-species dust in each run.

A collision rate at the
i
th particle position is given by

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{coll},i}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{d},i}4\pi a^{2}\Delta v_{i}}{m},

(6)

where
a
and
m
denote the size and the mass of a dust grain, and
\Delta v_{i}
denotes the collision velocity. We assume dust grains in the Epstein regime to relate
\mathrm{St}
and the dust size:

\mathrm{St}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{8}}\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{int}}a}{\rho_{\mathrm{g}}c_{\mathrm{s}}}\Omega,

(7)

where
\rho_{\mathrm{int}}\equiv 3m/4\pi a^{3}
is the internal density. We define the coagulation timescale
t_{\mathrm{coag},i}
as the size-doubling timescale,

t_{\mathrm{coag},i}^{-1}=\frac{1}{a}\frac{da}{dt}=\frac{t_{\mathrm{coll},i}^{-1}}{3},

(8)

where we assume perfect sticking
[
79
, cf.]
. Using the above equations, we obtain

t_{\mathrm{coag},i}^{-1}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{8}}\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{d},i}\Delta v_{i}}{\rho_{\mathrm{g}}c_{\mathrm{s}}\mathrm{St}}\Omega.

(9)

Since the gas motion induced by the SI is almost incompressible
[
91
,
37
, e.g., see]
,
\rho_{\mathrm{g}}
in the above equations is assumed to be the unperturbed gas density
\rho_{\mathrm{g},0}
.

Collision velocities are different between particle pairs. In this study, we assume that the collision velocity for the
i
th particle is represented by velocity dispersion of super-particles in a cell where the
i
th particle is located. The velocity dispersion
\sigma(t,x_{i},z_{i})
is given by

\sigma(t,x_{i},z_{i})^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{p},i}}\sum^{n_{\mathrm{p},i}}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$v$}^{\prime}_{i}-\left<\mbox{\boldmath$v$}_{i}^{\prime}\right>_{\mathrm{cell}}\right)^{2},

(10)

where
n_{\mathrm{p},i}
represents the number of particles that are located in the same cell as the
i
th particle,
\mbox{\boldmath$v$}^{\prime}_{i}
is the velocity relative to the Keplerian velocity, and
\left<\mbox{\boldmath$v$}^{\prime}_{i}\right>_{\mathrm{cell}}
is the average velocity of these particles.

To obtain average coagulation rates, we first integrate
t_{\mathrm{coag},i}^{-1}
and estimate the number of collisions that the
i
th particle experiences for
t_{\mathrm{s}}\leq t\leq t_{\mathrm{e}}
. The time-averaged coagulation rate for the
i
th particle is given by

\overline{t_{\mathrm{coag},i}^{-1}}\equiv\frac{1}{\Delta T_{\mathrm{s,e}}}\int^{t_{\mathrm{e}}}_{t_{\mathrm{s}}}t_{\mathrm{coag},i}^{-1}dt^{\prime},

(11)

where
\Delta T_{\mathrm{s,e}}\equiv t_{\mathrm{e}}-t_{\mathrm{s}}
. We output particle data every
0.5\Omega^{-1}
and use them for the time integration. We take the average of the coagulation rates for all super-particles:

t_{\mathrm{coag}}^{-1}\equiv\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{p}}}\sum_{i}\overline{t_{\mathrm{coag},i}^{-1}}.

(12)

This gives the evolutionary timescale of the system due to coagulation.

We also calculate
t_{\mathrm{coag}}^{-1}
-weighted (coagulation-rate-weighted) average values of the dust density and the velocity dispersion:

\overline{\rho_{\mathrm{d},i}}\equiv\frac{\int^{t_{\mathrm{e}}}_{t_{\mathrm{s}}}t_{\mathrm{coag},i}^{-1}\rho_{\mathrm{d}}dt^{\prime}}{\Delta T_{\mathrm{s,e}}\overline{t_{\mathrm{coag},i}^{-1}}},

(13)

\overline{\sigma_{i}}\equiv\frac{\int^{t_{\mathrm{e}}}_{t_{\mathrm{s}}}t_{\mathrm{coag},i}^{-1}\sigma dt^{\prime}}{\Delta T_{\mathrm{s,e}}\overline{t_{\mathrm{coag},i}^{-1}}}.

(14)

Their ensemble average is given as follows:

\left<\rho_{\mathrm{d}}\right>\equiv\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{p}}}\sum_{i}\overline{\rho_{\mathrm{d},i}},

(15)

\left<\sigma\right>\equiv\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{p}}}\sum_{i}\overline{\sigma_{i}}.

(16)

These represent characteristic dust density and velocity dispersion for the SI-assisted coagulation.

Results

IV

IV.1
SI-assisted coagulation in a gas bump

Previous studies investigated the SI in a gas bump where dust grains are enriched
[
77
,
3
,
16
, e.g.,]
.
Carrera et al. [17]
showed that the SI with
\mathrm{St}\simeq 0.01
cannot form planetesimals because dust grains cross the bump before the dust density exceeds the Roche density. In this case, we should compare the coagulation timescale with the crossing timescale. If we assume the radial scale of the bump to be
\sim H
, the crossing timescale is on the order of
t_{\mathrm{drift}}H/r\sim 5\times 10^{-2}t_{\mathrm{drift}}
for
\eta v_{\mathrm{K}}=0.05c_{\mathrm{s}}
. According to Figure
5
, the coagulation timescale is shorter than
\sim 4\times 10^{-3}t_{\mathrm{drift}}
for
\mathrm{St}=0.01
. This indicates that the coagulation timescale is also shorter than the crossing time. Although a spatial variation in
\eta v_{\mathrm{K}}/c_{\mathrm{s}}
is not taken into account here, we expect that such small dust grains will grow during the moderate clumping, which would enable planetesimal formation in the bump.

IV.2
Relaxing the conditions for planetesimal formation

We have shown that dust grains can grow efficiently during the clumping. As suggested by
Ho et al. [30]
, such dust growth relaxes the conditions for planetesimal formation. Here, we develop a simple model of dust evolution and quantitatively show how much the SI-assisted coagulation relaxes the conditions.

Our primary focus is on dust growth beyond the barriers enabled by the SI. We thus first suppose that the dust growth is limited initially (i.e., before the onset of the SI), and dust grains of
\mathrm{St}=\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{0}}
are vertically distributed in a disk. We consider a range of
\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{0}}\geq 10^{-2}
in this work. For
\mathrm{St}<\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{0}}
, coagulation may proceed faster than the SI because linear growth timescales of the SI are long for small dust while timescales of turbulence-induced coagulation without the clumping are independent from
\mathrm{St}

[
13
, e.g.,]
. In this case, the dust growth to the size of
\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{0}}
promotes the SI, which one may call “coagulation-assisted SI". The SI-assisted coagulation concerned in this work occurs after this phase, which we discuss below.

We also suppose that, once the SI operates, the concentrated dust grains are shielded from external gas turbulence
[
39
]
, and their collision velocities are determined by
\left<\sigma\right>
. The SI-assisted coagulation permits further dust growth since the collisions velocities are sufficiently low (
\lesssim 10^{-3}c_{\mathrm{s}}
, see the right panel of Figure
3
). The assumption of sticking collisions will be valid at least in an outer region beyond the water snow line, where critical velocities seem higher than a few cm/s as mentioned in Section
I
.

It is expected that the SI develops in a relatively denser region around the midplane
[e.g., see
43
, and references therein]
. We thus introduce a critical value of
\epsilon
for the SI-assisted coagulation (
\epsilon_{\mathrm{c,gro}}
). We call a region of
\epsilon\geq\epsilon_{\mathrm{c,gro}}
the SI-active zone (Figure
6
). To evaluate an amount of dust grains that can grow, we assume the Gaussian distribution of the dust density. The half width of the SI-active zone
z_{\mathrm{c}}
is then given as follows:

z_{c}=H_{\mathrm{d}}\sqrt{2\ln\left(\frac{\epsilon_{\mathrm{mid},0}}{\epsilon_{\mathrm{c,gro}}}\right)},

(24)

where the gas density is assumed to be uniform for
|z|\leq z_{c}
. The dust surface density of the SI-active zone
\Delta\Sigma_{\mathrm{d}}
is

\Delta\Sigma_{\mathrm{d}}=\Sigma_{\mathrm{d}}\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{z_{c}}{\sqrt{2}H_{\mathrm{d}}}\right),

(25)

where
\Sigma_{\mathrm{d}}
is the total surface density, and
\mathrm{erf}(z)
is the error function.

The dust grains in the SI-active zone settle toward the midplane as they grow. This increases the midplane dust-to-gas ratio and trigger planetesimal formation once the dust-to-gas ratio becomes sufficiently high (
\epsilon_{\mathrm{mid}}\geq\epsilon_{\mathrm{c,plts}}
). An amount of massive clumps and thus planetesimals also depends on
\mathrm{St}
. There would be a critical Stokes number
\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{c,plts}}
, beyond which the formation rate of planetesimals dominates the coagulation rate. In the present model, we simply adopt
\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{c,plts}}=0.3
and assume that dust grains grow up to this size. This is motivated by the previous numerical simulations that showed the highest clumping efficiency at
\mathrm{St}\simeq 0.1-1

[
4
, e.g., see]
.

If all dust grains in a disk grow and settle (i.e.,
\epsilon_{\mathrm{c,gro}}\to 0
), the midplane dust-to-gas ratio increases by a factor of
\sqrt{\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{c,plts}}/\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{0}}}
(see Equation (
5
)):

\epsilon_{\mathrm{mid}}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{mid},0}\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{c,plts}}}{\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{0}}}},

(26)

where
\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{0}}
and
\epsilon_{\mathrm{mid},0}
denote the Stokes number and the midplane dust-to-gas ratio before the onset of the SI, respectively. In this case, the critical value of
\epsilon_{\mathrm{mid},0}
for planetesimal formation is obtained from
\epsilon_{\mathrm{mid}}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{c,plts}}
, or

\epsilon_{\mathrm{mid},0}\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{c,plts}}}{\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{0}}}}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{c,plts}}.

(27)

Since dust grains can grow only in the SI-active zone, we should replace
\epsilon_{\mathrm{mid},0}
with
\epsilon_{\mathrm{mid},0}\Delta\Sigma_{\mathrm{d}}/\Sigma_{\mathrm{d}}
in Equation (
27
). The midplane dust-to-gas ratio after the SI-assisted coagulation is given by

\epsilon_{\mathrm{mid}}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{mid},0}\frac{\Delta\Sigma_{\mathrm{d}}}{\Sigma_{\mathrm{d}}}\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{c,plts}}}{\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{0}}}}.

(28)

We again note that we assume the Gaussian distribution for the grown dust with the scale height of
H_{\mathrm{d}}\sqrt{\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{0}}/\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{c,plts}}}
.
2
2

2

We admit that this assumption is not necessarily valid. The settling timescale
\sim(\mathrm{St}\Omega)^{-1}
can be comparable to the coagulation rate shown in Figure
2
. We will address this issue in future work.
Using Equations (
24
) and (
25

\epsilon_{\mathrm{mid},0}

\epsilon_{\mathrm{mid},0}\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{c,plts}}}{\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{0}}}}\mathrm{erf}\left(\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{\epsilon_{\mathrm{mid},0}}{\epsilon_{\mathrm{c,gro}}}\right)}\right)=\epsilon_{\mathrm{c,plts}}.

(29)

\epsilon_{\mathrm{mid},0}>\epsilon_{\mathrm{c,gro}}
.

IV.4
Bouncing barrier

In addition to the radial drift and fragmentation barriers, the bouncing barrier is another obstacle to prevent dust growth
[
95
,
21
, e.g.,]
. The process has been investigated from laboratory experiments
[
42
,
86
,
40
,
65
, e.g.,]
and numerical simulations
[
84
,
64
,
67
,
2
,
57
, e.g.,]
. The previous studies showed that relatively compact aggregates can rebound for the collision velocity lower than
1\;\mathrm{m/s}
. This indicates that the bouncing can limit dust growth before the fragmentation takes place.

When the bouncing barrier keeps
\mathrm{St}
small, the SI becomes less efficient than often expected, while it can hold dust grains in disks for
\sim 1\mathrm{Myr}

[
21
]
; otherwise they will be depleted quickly
[
13
,
9
, e.g.,]
. If the bouncing barrier limits
\mathrm{St}
to be less than
10^{-2}
, dust grains would be too tightly coupled to form a large amount of dense clumps via the SI. Overcoming the bouncing barrier is challenging, but necessary to induce such strong clumping with the help of dust coagulation for planetesimal formation. In future work, we should investigate the efficiency of the SI-assisted coagulation in the presence of the bouncing barrier.

IV.5
Caveats and Outlooks

In this section, we describe several caveats and limitations regarding the present simulations.

IV.5.1
Unequal-mass collisions and size distributions

Throughout this work, we have explored the coagulation rates due to equal-mass collisions. As shown in Figure
1
, the coagulation rates have a certain degree of dispersion in a single run. This means that the SI-assisted coagulation would broaden a size distribution even when dust grains are initially monodisperse. As the size distribution becomes wider, collisions due to differential drift motion would contribute to the dust growth, which is not taken into account in the present work.

We roughly estimate relative importance of the unequal-mass collisions with respect to the equal-mass collisions. If the unequal-mass collisions of dust with
\mathrm{St}=\mathrm{St}_{1}
and
\mathrm{St}_{2}
(
\mathrm{St}_{2}<\mathrm{St}_{1}<1
) are driven by the radial drift motion, the collision velocity
\Delta v(\mathrm{St}_{1},\mathrm{St}_{2})
is roughly given by
2(\mathrm{St}_{1}-\mathrm{St}_{2})\eta v_{\mathrm{K}}
. Here, we neglect the factor originating from the back reaction from dust to gas, and thus the actual collision velocity is lower. If the mass densities of both species are comparable, the relative importance of the unequal-mass collisions can be measured by ratios of the cross sections and the collision velocities:
5
5

5

We examine the relative importance in terms of mass flux to a dust of
\mathrm{St}=\mathrm{St}_{1}
through collisions rather than in terms of collision probability. The former depends on
\rho_{\mathrm{d}}
while the latter depends on the number density, which is typically assumed to be larger for smaller dust grains.

f\equiv\frac{\pi(a_{1}+a_{2})^{2}\Delta v(\mathrm{St}_{1},\mathrm{St}_{2})}{4\pi a_{1}^{2}\Delta v(\mathrm{St}_{1},\mathrm{St}_{1})},

(30)

where
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
denote the dust sizes corresponding to the Stokes number of
\mathrm{St}_{1}
and
\mathrm{St}_{2}
, respectively. Based on Figure
3
, we can roughly approximate
\Delta v(\mathrm{St}_{1},\mathrm{St}_{1})
as
\Delta v(\mathrm{St}_{1},\mathrm{St}_{1})\simeq\Delta v_{\mathrm{tur}}(\alpha=10^{-5})
. Assuming
\mathrm{St}_{1}\gg\mathrm{St}_{2}
in Equation (
30
), we have

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{4}\frac{2\mathrm{St}_{1}\eta v_{\mathrm{K}}}{\Delta v_{\mathrm{tur}}(\alpha=10^{-5})}

\displaystyle\sim 0.5\left(\frac{\mathrm{St}_{1}}{0.01}\right)^{0.5}\left(\frac{\eta v_{\mathrm{K}}/c_{\mathrm{s}}}{0.05}\right).

(31)

The factor
f
should be smaller as we overestimate
\Delta v(\mathrm{St}_{1},\mathrm{St}_{2})
in overdense regions by a factor of
\sim(1+\epsilon)

[
78
, e.g., see]
. This indicates that growth due to the equal-mass collisions dominates that due to the unequal-mass collisions even if the mass densities are comparable between the two species
(\mathrm{St}=\mathrm{St}_{1},\mathrm{St}_{2})
.

Recent unstratified simulations with multiple dust species showed that dust segregation occurs through the SI-induced turbulence, and large dust grains tend to be abundant in overdense regions
[
90
,
50
]
.
Matthijsse et al. [50]
showed that the densest regions of different
\mathrm{St}
are spatially separated and that local size distributions can have a single peak. Such segregation will promote the growth of large dust grains through equal-mass collisions. Therefore, we expect that unequal-mass collisions might affect insignificantly the evolution of mass-dominating dust grains once the SI-assisted coagulation operates.

Bai & Stone [4]
showed that the differential drift velocity fits the median collision velocities in most of the runs. However, it is still unclear whether or not such high velocity collisions dominate the dust growth. For example, they mentioned that high-speed collisions take place outside dust clumps, suggesting that the median collision velocity might overestimate characteristic collision velocities for the dust growth. In future work, we will investigate collision velocities and coagulation rates due to unequal-mass collisions in more detail.

It should be noted that broadening the size distribution may change the efficiency of the SI and thus the equal-mass collision rates. It has been recently highlighted that the SI itself is affected by size distributions.
Krapp et al. [41]
showed that the SI with multiple dust species can be significantly slow
[
51
,
94
, see also]
.
Zhu & Yang [94]
showed that the largest dust does not necessarily determine the efficiency of the SI, and smaller dust grains can appreciably change growth rates. Their contribution to the instability is not straightforward since they can change the dominant mode. It will be worth investigating how efficiently small dust grains are swept up regardless of their limited contribution to the growth of mass-dominating large dust grains.

IV.5.2
Stratified disks and three-dimensional simulations

The present study is based on the unstratified simulations. The previous studies based on stratified simulations show that dust grains are accumulated into azimuthally elongated filaments
[
37
,
88
,
45
,
62
, e.g.,]
. It was also shown that small dust grains are stirred up by large dust grains that drive clumping around the midplane
[
4
,
63
, e.g.,]
. Those radial and vertical structures will be important to investigate where and how efficiently the SI-assisted coagulation takes place.

Besides, vertical shear of the azimuthal velocity in the stratified disk leads to another type of instability called vertically shearing streaming instability (VSSI)
[
33
,
48
]
.
Lin [48]
found that VSSI dominates over the classic SI in stratified disks. VSSI induces turbulence and stirs dust grains on radial scales of
10^{-3}H
, which is typically smaller than the radial scale of the SI. Thus, VSSI can be another driving source of dust collisions.

Finally, the present study is based on two-dimensional simulations. The clumping efficiency can be different in three-dimensional simulations. For example, the maximum density can be lower in three-dimensional simulations
[
37
,
4
, e.g.,]
. Thus, our analysis might overestimate the coagulation rates. We will address those points in future work.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we perform unstratified local simulations of the SI and examine how fast dust grains can grow during the moderate clumping. To evaluate the coagulation rates as precisely as possible, we utilize the super-particle trajectories to take into account temporal variation in dust densities and velocities. Our main findings are summarized as follows:

1.

The average coagulation rates are
\sim 10^{-2}\Omega-10^{-1}\Omega
in most of the runs and maximized at
\mathrm{St}\simeq 0.03-0.1
for a given initial dust-to-gas ratio. The coagulation-rate-weighted average of dust densities is larger than the initial dust density by a factor of a few to ten, meaning that the density enhancement indeed assists the coagulation.

2.

The weighted-average velocity dispersion is on the order of
10^{-3}c_{\mathrm{s}}
at the highest. Thus, the SI-assisted coagulation cannot be prevented by the fragmentation barrier as suggested in the previous studies
[
36
,
7
,
4
, see also]
.

3.

The coagulation timescales are orders of magnitude shorter than the drifting timescale. Therefore, the radial drift will not limit the SI-assisted coagulation. For
\mathrm{St}\lesssim 0.1
, the SI-assisted coagulation will produce larger dust grains in the so-called pre-clumping phase.

4.

The SI-assisted coagulation appreciably relaxes the conditions for planetesimal formation because (1) it increases
\mathrm{St}
and (2) the midplane dust-to-gas ratio
\epsilon_{\mathrm{mid}}
is also enhanced by subsequent settling
[
30
, see also]
. According to our simple model, the critical value of the dust-to-gas ratio is
\simeq 0.4
for
\mathrm{St}_{\mathrm{0}}=0.01
, which is only one-tenth of the critical value in the previous study (Figure
7
).

5.

Our model also suggests that planetesimal formation can be initiated only by moderate clumping that increases the local dust-to-gas ratio from
\epsilon\leq 1
to several (see Figure
3
). The SI-assisted coagulation and settling realize states where the so-called strong clumping occurs and amplifies the dust density by orders of magnitude.

Our simulations do not treat external gas turbulence, which may enhance collision velocities. However, once the SI develops, such larger-scale turbulence is expected to be inefficient in stirring dust grains
[
39
]
. Therefore, dust grains will safely grow through the SI-assisted coagulation. Numerical simulations incorporating both the SI and the coagulation is necessary to reveal dust evolution toward planetesimals in more detail
[
30
, see]
. Especially, to incorporate the low-velocity collisions driven by the SI is vital since otherwise fragmentation will limit the dust growth regardless of the density enhancement.
