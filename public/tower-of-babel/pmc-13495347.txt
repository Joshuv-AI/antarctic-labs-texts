Real-world outcomes of chemoimmunotherapy in advanced biliary tract cancer: a multicenter study with a propensity-matched historical cohort
Authors: L.G. Da Fonseca, C.S. Freire, R.A.V. Guedes, M.C. Lyra, M.I.F.M. Braguiroli, M.B. Siqueira, A. de Mendonça Palladino, H.L. Najman, D.R. Da Rocha Filho, M.C. Gifoni, G.L.S. Pereira, M.C.A. Teixeira, R. Paraná, P.M. Hoff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBiliary tract cancer (BTC) comprises a heterogeneous group of malignancies, often diagnosed at advanced stages. Phase III trials have shown that adding immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) to gemcitabine and cisplatin improves survival, supporting their use as first-line therapy; however, real-world evidence remains limited.Materials and methodsWe conducted a multicenter observational study including patients with advanced BTC treated with ICI-based regimens plus chemotherapy. Clinical data were retrospectively collected. Overall survival (OS) was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and prognostic factors were evaluated with multivariate Cox regression. A historical control cohort without ICI exposure was analyzed using propensity score matching.ResultsA total of 152 patients received ICI-based therapy (median age 66.5 years, 57.9% men, and 68.4% metastatic). The most common primary sites were intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (52.6%) and gallbladder carcinoma (23.7%). Durvalumab plus gemcitabine/cisplatin was used in 88.1% of cases. After a median follow-up of 8.3 months, the median OS was 15.5 months (95% confidence interval 12.3-21.5), and the response rate was 38.8%. Maintenance therapy was given to 37.5%, mainly as ICI monotherapy. Independent adverse prognostic factors included Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of >0 [hazard ratio (HR) 3.91], neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio of ≥3 (HR 1.98), and vascular invasion (HR 1.83). Ten (6.6%) grade ≥2 immune-related adverse events were reported. A propensity score-matched analysis showed improved survival with ICI versus the historical cohort without ICI (HR 0.61, P = 0.025).ConclusionIn this real-world cohort, ICI plus chemotherapy achieved outcomes comparable with pivotal trials, supporting its effectiveness in broader populations and highlighting the need for improved access.

Introduction

Biliary tract cancer (BTC) comprises a heterogeneous group of malignancies arising from the gallbladder and bile ducts. Collectively, they are characterized by an aggressive clinical course, with most diagnoses occurring at advanced stages.1,2 Managing BTC is complicated by impaired performance status (PS), biliary obstruction, infectious complications, and treatment-related toxicities.1,2 Since 2010, cisplatin plus gemcitabine has become the backbone systemic treatment for BTC, demonstrating improved survival compared with gemcitabine.3

After more than a decade, recent clinical trials have highlighted the role of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). The addition of durvalumab to gemcitabine plus cisplatin has improved progression-free survival and overall survival (OS) compared with chemotherapy alone.4,5 Similarly, pembrolizumab has improved OS, leading to regulatory approvals and widespread adoption of these regimens.6 However, the clinical benefit is heterogeneous across subgroups, with risks of adverse events and financial toxicity.

Real-world evidence increasingly supports decision making outside of clinical trials. Since 2022, data on ICI in BTC have emerged, particularly from Europe, Asia, and China.7, 8, 9, 10, 11 However, the epidemiology of BTC varies across regions, with particularities regarding comorbidities, presentation, and ethnicity in Latin America.12 In this study, we describe characteristics and outcomes of patients receiving ICI in a multicenter Brazilian cohort, compared with a historical cohort treated before ICI incorporation.

Materials and methods

Study design and eligibility criteria

We conducted an observational multicenter study within a Brazilian oncology network (Oncologia D’Or). The study focused on patients with BTC who received at least one dose of ICI as part of routine clinical care. The included population was selected from medical records from December 2018 to July 2025.

The centers receive patients from other health care services (primary or secondary units or private physicians) or through self-referral. Patients with BTC are managed by multidisciplinary teams. Whenever there is an indication for systemic treatment—whether due to advanced-stage disease or contraindication to surgery or locoregional therapy—the patient is evaluated by clinical oncologists. Participating centers are part of the Brazilian complementary health system, which serves ∼25% of the population. Public and complementary systems have separate sets of covered medications and are regulated by different sectors within the Ministry of Health. Immunotherapy for BTC is incorporated only into the latter.13

Inclusion criteria included patients aged at least 18 years with a histological diagnosis of BTC, including intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA), distal cholangiocarcinoma (dCCA), perihilar cholangiocarcinoma (pCCA), gallbladder carcinoma, and ampullary carcinoma with immunohistochemical staining consistent with pancreatobiliary subtype, and with diseases not amenable to curative therapies. Patients with insufficient data were excluded. A historical cohort of patients with BTC consecutively treated at the same centers before the regulatory approval of ICI was also analyzed.

The study protocol complied with international ethical standards. The study was approved by the local ethics committee (Hospital e Maternidade São Luiz—HMSL, ethical report: 7.782.318; amendment 1; 7 May 2024), and informed consent was waived. Study data were recorded in a web-based electronic case report (REDCap; Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN) hosted by the Instituto D’Or. Data were last updated on 1 August 2025. The study was reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines and to the European Society for Medical Oncology Guidance for Reporting Oncology real-world evidence.14

Data collection

Clinical data from both the ICI and historical control cohorts were retrospectively collected from medical records by two investigators (L.G.D.F. and C.S.F.) using standardized case report forms. The remaining coauthors were consulted whenever necessary for data clarification. Variables included demographics, comorbidities, laboratory values, and site of origin. Disease characteristics were evaluated through imaging and serum carbohydrate antigen [(CA) 19-9] and carcinoembryonic antigen values. PS was assessed using the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) classification.15 Missing data were infrequent and were handled through complete-case analysis without imputation.

Treatment administration

Patients received treatment from the assistant physicians and were administered according to established protocols. Durvalumab plus gemcitabine and cisplatin were administered at the following doses: durvalumab 1500 mg on day 1 of each cycle, plus gemcitabine 1000 mg/m2 and cisplatin 25 mg/m2 on days 1 and 8 of each cycle (every 3 weeks). Chemotherapy was administered for eight cycles, followed by maintenance durvalumab every 4 weeks until progression or limiting toxicity.4 Pembrolizumab plus gemcitabine and cisplatin were administered as follows: pembrolizumab 200 mg on day 1, plus gemcitabine 1000 mg/m2 and cisplatin 25 mg/m2 on days 1 and 8 of each cycle (every 3 weeks).6 The decision on maintenance after cycle 8, as well as the treatment schedule and dosing, could be adapted according to the physician’s discretion and depended on multiple factors, including progression during the chemoimmunotherapy phase, clinical deterioration, and treatment-free interval strategies. Other backbone chemotherapies could be used, such as carboplatin plus gemcitabine, oxaliplatin plus gemcitabine, or gemcitabine alone.16,17 Alternative regimens were included because the study aimed to reflect real-world practice, particularly in patients considered unfit for cisplatin or requiring adapted regimens due to renal dysfunction, frailty, or toxicity concerns. Clinical monitoring and assessments were carried out according to the local practice. Treatment was continued until disease progression, loss of clinical benefit, or limiting toxicity. According to the local protocol, locally advanced BTC is managed with upfront systemic treatment, and locoregional therapies, including intra-arterial, radiation, and surgical therapies, are considered exceptional only after a response assessment and multidisciplinary evaluation.

Radiological response to treatment was assessed per RECIST criteria v1.1.18 Images were carried out as part of periodic restaging and analyzed locally. RECIST v1.1 assessments were based on imaging reviews carried out locally at participating institutions. Treatment-related adverse events were registered according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events v5.0.19 Only adverse events that were considered related to the ICI were described.

Statistical analysis

The primary endpoint was OS. Secondary endpoints included objective response rate, treatment patterns, safety, prognostic factor analyses, and exploratory comparisons with a historical cohort.

We used descriptive statistics to summarize demographic characteristics, including frequencies, medians, and interquartile ranges (IQRs). OS curves were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Survival was defined as the time between the first ICI treatment and death. Cox regression analyses were carried out to identify prognostic variables and to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The multivariable model was based on variables identified as statistically significant in the univariate analysis. All relevant baseline characteristics were included as follows: primary site, disease, previous treatment, disease status, ECOG PS, vascular invasion, ascites, albumin (< or ≥3.5 g/dl), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) (< or ≥3), bilirubin (< or ≥1.5), and CA 19-9 (< or ≥37 U/ml). ECOG PS, vascular invasion, NLR, and serum albumin were statistically significant in the univariate analysis (P < 0.05) and were included in the multivariable model. Response rate was analyzed separately as a time-dependent variable, adjusted for the independent variables.

A propensity score was estimated using logistic regression, including ECOG PS and chemotherapy backbone. These variables were selected because they represented clinically relevant factors with minimal missingness across cohorts. ECOG PS was independently associated with prognosis in the multivariable analysis, while chemotherapy backbone represented a potential source of imbalance between groups, given the established superiority of platinum doublets over single agents.3 Given the moderate sample size and number of events, a propensity score model was preferred to reduce overfitting and instability. Patients were matched 1:1 using nearest-neighbor matching with replacement and a caliper of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score. Inverse probability weighting was evaluated, but matching was chosen due to concerns about extreme weights and limited cohort overlap. It was also regarded as a more clinically interpretable method. Robust variance estimation was applied to account for the nonindependence introduced by replacement matching.

Given visual evidence of violation of the proportional hazards assumption, the restricted mean survival time (RMST) was used as a complementary measure. RMST was estimated up to a prespecified truncation time of 40 months, corresponding to the area under the Kaplan–Meier survival curves within this time horizon. All statistical analyses were carried out using STATA 17.0 (Stata, TX). Forest plots were generated using Google Colab (https://colab.research.google.com).

Treatment characteristics and outcomes

After a median follow-up of 8.3 months (IQR 3.7-14.9), 84 patients (55.2%) died, whereas 63 (41.4%) were alive (18 under treatment, and 5 were lost to follow-up). The median duration of ICI treatment was 4.4 months (IQR 2.4-8.2). Durvalumab was the most used ICI (n = 145; 95.4%), followed by pembrolizumab (n = 6; 3.9%) and nivolumab (n = 1; 0.7%). The most common backbone chemotherapy was cisplatin plus gemcitabine (90.1%), with a median of six cycles. Maintenance was applied in 57 (37.5%) patients after chemotherapy de-escalation, while 36 patients received a treatment-free interval after treatment de-escalation. Maintenance strategies included ICI monotherapy (n = 46) or gemcitabine plus ICI (n = 11). Table 2 provides details on the treatment characteristics and duration. The overall response rate was 38.8%, including 55 patients with partial responses and 4 with complete responses (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100749). A significant association was found between the best response and type of maintenance (P = 0.001). Maintenance was less frequent among patients with stable disease (50.0%) than among those with partial response (72.1%). For patients with a partial response, maintenance with immunotherapy alone was more common than with immunotherapy plus gemcitabine (56.5% versus 19.6%), while 23.9% received a treatment-free interval. Similarly, for patients with stable disease, maintenance with immunotherapy was more common than with gemcitabine plus immunotherapy (43.7% versus 6.3%), while 50% received a treatment-free interval. The safety profile was favorable, with 10 (6.6%) cases of ICI-related adverse events of grade ≥2 (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100749). Treatment discontinuation due to toxicity occurred in six patients (3.9%).

There was no significant difference in OS by tumor site. The median OS was 22.9 months (95% CI 8.0-not available [NA]) for gallbladder carcinoma; 15.6 months (95% CI 12.2-21.6) for iCCA; 13.7 months (95% CI 9.4-NA) for dCCA; 11.6 months (95% CI 2.2-NA) for pCCA; and 5.9 months (95% CI 1.1-NA) for ampullary tumors (P = 0.683) (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100749). Radiological response is a postbaseline variable and is reported as an exploratory descriptive analysis. Patients with a partial response had a median OS of 27.5 months (95% CI 20.9-NA), while those with stable disease had a median OS of 14.3 months (95% CI 11.6-18.0) and those with progressive disease had a median OS of 5.7 months (95% CI 2.2-8.6). Low response rates were observed in patients with vascular invasion (28.1%), NLR ≥ 3 (19.3%), and low albumin (25.0%) (Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100749).

Historical control group: characteristics and outcomes

Data from 94 patients consecutively treated over the 3 years preceding local approval of ICI (2018-2021) were collected. The baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. There were no differences in baseline characteristics between the historical and the ICI cohorts after adjusting for multiple comparisons using an independent-samples (unpaired) t-test for continuous variables and a two-sided Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. The median age of the historical cohort was 63.8 years (IQR 53.5-74.3; t-test P = 0.361), and ECOG PS was 0-1 in 89.3% (P = 0.653). The main sites were intrahepatic (n = 56, 59.6%), followed by perihilar (n = 13, 13.8%, P = 0.093). The presentation was locally advanced (n = 37, 39.4%) or metastatic (n = 57, 60.6%, P = 0.234). The most used chemotherapy regimen was gemcitabine plus cisplatin (n = 70, 74.5). Second-line therapies were received by 64 patients (68.1%), mainly oxaliplatin plus 5-fluorouracil (FOLFOX) (n = 36, 38.3%) and irinotecan plus 5-fluorouracil (FOLFIRI) (n = 11, 11.7%).

After a median follow-up of 11.3 months (IQR 7.3-21.3), the median OS in the historical group was 14.5 months (95% CI 9.8-17.6). A Cox regression showed no difference in survival between the historical and ICI cohorts (HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.57-1.09, P = 0.13). The Kaplan–Meier curves show an initial crossing of the curves and a subsequent separation, with a plateau at 35-40 months in favor of the ICI group (Figure 2). Discussion

This study provides real-world evidence on the use of chemoimmunotherapy in patients with advanced BTC, corroborating the outcomes in the pivotal trials.4,6 Among 152 patients, we observed a median OS of 15.5 months, which aligns with the 12.8- to 15.8-month range reported in clinical and real-world studies worldwide.9, 10, 11,20

Notably, our cohort comprised a more heterogeneous population than those typically enrolled in clinical trials. For instance, 12.6% of patients had an ECOG PS of ≥2, and comorbidities such as autoimmune disorders were included. Besides, we observed a notable prevalence of diabetes and overweight, while a low prevalence of cirrhosis. A previous Brazilian study suggested an association of metabolic disorders and cholangiocarcinoma.21 On the contrary, an analysis of 109 studies from different regions revealed that 10.2% of patients with cholangiocarcinoma had cirrhosis.22 This suggests distinct regional risk factors.

Latin America is underrepresented in BTC trials, although the local incidence is high.23 In the TOPAZ-1 trial, only Argentina (n = 22) and Chile (n = 9) were represented.4 In the KEYNOTE-966, the inclusion of patients from Latin America was not detailed, although an unbalanced representation of Asia was observed (45%).6 Both trials were statistically positive, leading to global regulatory approval, including Latin American countries. While these regimens are associated with modest benefits, regional evidence is needed to confirm their applicability.

The median OS and response rate were equivalent across primary sites and disease extent, although we acknowledge a higher proportion of locally advanced tumors (versus metastatic) than in the pivotal clinical trial.4,6 Multivariate analysis confirmed that ECOG PS of >0, NLR of ≥3, and vascular invasion were negative prognostic factors. Vascular invasion is a surrogate of local aggressiveness and frequently induces complications such as portal hypertension and strictures of adjacent structures, such as the bile ducts.24 Studies suggest that NLR may predict outcomes in liver tumors.25,26 NLR reflects the balance between neutrophils, which promote inflammation and cancer growth, and lymphocytes, which drive antitumor immunity. It is suggested that an inverse relationship exists between NLR and CD8+-cell infiltration.27, 28, 29 In BTC, however, the link between systemic and local immunity remains unclear.

In patients with initial disease control, the pivotal trials were designed to deliver a maximum of eight cycles of chemotherapy; however, the benefit of maintenance beyond eight cycles remains uncertain. The TOPAZ-1 trial was intended for an ICI-only maintenance,4 while the KEYNOTE-966 included gemcitabine with ICI.6 We observed that maintenance strategies are used in over half of patients who completed chemoimmunotherapy, and the response to initial treatment may influence the decision to use maintenance therapy. This trend varies across reports favoring less-intensive maintenance for responders and prospective studies are warranted to confirm the optimal strategy.

We found a favorable safety profile, showing discontinuations due to toxicity in 6%. The incidence of immune-related adverse events was low, although it is potentially underestimated due to limited follow-up or underreporting.

In addition to descriptive outcomes, we compared the effectiveness of chemoimmunotherapy versus a historical cohort treated before the local approval of ICI. After propensity score matching, a statistically significant difference in OS was observed. Visual inspection of Kaplan–Meier curves revealed a late separation, with a survival plateau emerging beyond 35 months, accounting for 25%-30% of patients in the ICI cohort. Although the between-group difference in RMST up to 40 months did not reach conventional statistical significance, the numerically longer RMST observed in the ICI cohort suggests a potential late survival advantage consistent with the durable benefit pattern reported with immunotherapy across solid tumors.

This study has limitations inherent to its retrospective design and the context of a segmented health care system, including potential selection and reporting biases, as well as variability in imaging assessments and follow-up intervals. Twenty-seven patients were initially excluded for insufficient data. We found no clear evidence of systematic exclusion of patients with more aggressive disease; however, selection bias cannot be ruled out. Molecular testing was not uniformly carried out, reflecting access barriers, and programmed death-ligand 1 expression was not consistently available.

One important limitation of this study is the relatively short follow-up in comparison with the observed OS. This reflects the recent incorporation of ICI into clinical practice and the consequent limited duration of available observation. Therefore, the estimated long-term survival outcomes should be interpreted with caution, as additional follow-up may modify the magnitude of the observed benefit. Furthermore, the current follow-up duration limits a more comprehensive assessment of late recurrences, long-term disease control, and delayed toxicities. Despite these limitations, the study provides relevant real-world evidence regarding the early effectiveness and safety in a population that is frequently underrepresented in clinical trials.

An additional limitation inherent to historical comparisons relates to the temporal separation between the historical and ICI cohorts. Because patients in the historical cohort were treated predominantly during earlier years of the study period, differences in outcomes may have been influenced not only by ICI exposure but also by evolving supportive care practices, availability of molecular profiling, and increased access to subsequent lines of therapy during more recent years. Furthermore, follow-up duration differed between cohorts, and a higher proportion of censored observations in the ICI cohort may have introduced informative censoring and affected long-term estimates. Although propensity score matching was carried out to reduce baseline imbalances, residual confounding related to calendar-time effects cannot be fully excluded and should be considered when interpreting the comparative results.

Despite these limitations, this study is among the first analyses of immunotherapy in BTC conducted in Latin America, offering insights from a health care system with unique structural and access challenges. Only a small proportion of patients in our region have access to ICI due to the financial burden and barriers to early diagnosis. The findings support the need to discuss measures to reduce financial toxicity and to explore biomarkers to identify patients who are more likely to benefit.

Conclusion

Our real-world cohort supports the efficacy and safety of combining chemoimmunotherapy in BTC, with survival outcomes comparable to those in pivotal trials. Prognostic factors, including ECOG PS, NLR, and vascular invasion, were associated with worse outcomes, underscoring the need for individualized approaches. Low-resource adaptations of ICI-based regimens remain a critical next step in optimizing care across regions.
