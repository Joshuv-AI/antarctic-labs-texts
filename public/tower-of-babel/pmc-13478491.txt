Validation of the ultra short term heart rate variability measures under changing psychophysiological state of the healthy participants at rest
Authors: Karina Maciejewska, Cezary Zając, Klaudia Duch, Maciej Giza, Aleksandra Masternak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeart rate variability (HRV) is the variability of the time intervals between successive heartbeats. The guidelines recommend at least a 5-min ECG recording to establish HRV measures reliably. However, new medical and engineering applications and dynamic environmental conditions enforce using shorter recordings (ustHRV). There are, however, no up-to-date guidelines, and many studies using ustHRV lack methodological rigor. Thus, we investigated which statistical and spectral ustHRV measures may be reliably assessed from shorter ECG lengths compared to the benchmark (5-min) recording. In addition, we investigated how changes in the psychophysiological state of the participants due to mental fatigue and drowsiness, and the difference in the sensory processing, impact the agreement of ustHRV surrogates with the reference signal. The correlation analysis, intra- and inter-group statistical tests, trend analysis, and Bland-Altman plots showed that the statistical measures and HF power from the recordings of as short as 30 s and the other frequency measures of 1 min are good surrogates of the stHRV when the participants are rested and vigilant. Longer recordings may be needed with increasing mental fatigue or drowsiness. This work lays the path to the novel applications of the ustHRV analysis in dynamic environments and factors changing humans’ psychophysiological state.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55797-y.Keywords: Heart rate variability (HRV), Ultra-short-term HRV (ustHRV), Validation, Mental fatigue, Energy dietary supplementationSubject terms: Electrocardiography - EKG, Peripheral nervous system, Cardiovascular biology, Health care, Biomedical engineering

Introduction

Heart rate variability (HRV) is the variability of the time intervals between successive heartbeats, measured using the time intervals between R waves in the electrocardiogram (ECG). HRV is commonly interpreted as reflecting the balance between the sympathetic and parasympathetic branches of the autonomic nervous system (ANS)1. The gold standard for the HRV analysis is to use an ECG recording of at least 5 min to collect enough data for accurate interpretations, as recommended by the guidelines established by the Task Force of the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and the North American Society of Pacing and Electrophysiology (NASPE) in 19962. While 24-hour recordings are often used for long-term HRV analysis, 5-min segments are considered sufficient for short-term HRV (stHRV) analysis in controlled settings. The 5-min recording time allows for avoiding challenges such as non-stationarity and reduced patient compliance that are present during longer ones.

HRV in clinical applications is usually used as a predictor of the risk or mortality in cardiovascular patients, to improve early diagnosis, or to monitor recovery after heart failure or autonomic dysfunctions1,3–5. However, HRV, as a representation of the ANS activity controlled by the central nervous system (CNS), is also used as a peripheral biomedical signal that helps understand the functioning of the human brain and body to study state-dependent changes in autonomic activity2,4. These changes are studied in health (stress and relaxation regulation, meditation, sleep, emotional processing, cognitive processes such as attention and memory, or cognitive load) and pathology (e.g., neurological disorders, anxiety, depression)2,4–11. In such applications, the participants execute tasks during which psychophysiological signals are tracked in short intervals to study how the brain and body (including the heart) respond to the stimuli during the experimental session. Therefore, forcing the participants to be at rest for a minimum of 5 min doesn’t allow for investigating those dynamic cognitive processes that require the nervous system to respond to incoming stimuli on narrow time intervals (often several seconds). In such applications, HRV is typically interpreted at the group level rather than as a diagnostic biomarker at the individual level.

In addition, recent developments in biomedical technology (such as wearable sensors, smart clothing, and wireless devices) and the use of HRV in ambulatory and dynamic settings (during exercise, motion, driving, rehabilitation, postural changes, human-technology interaction (HTI), medical decision support systems), supported by machine learning (ML) algorithms, broaden the usefulness of HRV in more ecologically valid settings, thus further forcing the need to shorten ECG recording time.

Therefore, in recent years, there has been a growing interest in using shorter ECG recordings to assess HRV due to the increasing demand for non-invasive, real-time, and practical tools in clinical, research, and consumer settings3–5.

These ultra-short (term) HRV (ustHRV or usHRV) analyses conducted over periods of less than 5 min (or even only within several seconds) have gained popularity due to their ease of use and convenience5,12–15. However, these measurements face critical challenges that can impact their reliability. Burma et al.13 addressed concerns about the reliability and susceptibility of such short ECG recording durations to transient physiological changes, such as respiratory rate and blood pressure changes, which may distort HRV measurements. Kim16 and Bernardes12 emphasized that longer measurement times are required for accurate analysis of HRV under dynamic conditions. Yet, the standard guidelines don’t rule out using durations shorter than 5 min for specific applications, but point out that they must be validated against the gold standard of 5 min. Careful validation and appropriate metric selection are essential to mitigate these challenges.

Thus, the aim of this study was to evaluate how ECG recording duration affects the agreement between the ustHRV estimates and the standard 5-min reference.

Already 10 years ago, the researchers highlighted that only some metrics, such as RMSSD, are reliably repeatable in ultrashort contexts, while others (e.g., SDNN, LF, HF) lacked consistent validation17. Even though, there are still no guidelines on ustHRV, and many works presenting ustHRV outcomes lack scientific rigor and do not adequately assess criterion validity4,5. Only several studies, primarily reviews, have attempted to set directions for using the ustHRV. Pecchia et al.4 and Shaffer et al.5 independently reviewed the literature on the ustHRV to find the best research recommendations. Among the 29 papers that fulfilled the inclusion criteria in 4, only one used a rigorous method, while the others used unsatisfactory techniques due to incorrect use of statistical tests. As a result of this review, the authors proposed an algorithm that would allow for a reliable assessment of the validity of ustHRV. The proposed algorithm consists of the following four steps: (1) correlations with benchmark feature (i.e., HRV measures calculated from a 5-min ECG recording); (2) bias assessment, i.e., a visual analysis, such as Bland-Altman plots, used to check for bias in the means or medians of HRV features between the two measures; (3) information preservation, i.e., statistical tests to assess whether the surrogate features significantly differ between the conditions at various time scales; (4) consistency of trends, i.e., the trends in HRV features should be consistent across different time lengths. According to Shaffer et al.5, who analyzed 28 papers on ustHRV, its application to medicine and performance assessment still requires advances in several important areas, including the identification of the minimum ustHRV periods required to appropriately estimate short-term HRV characteristics and demonstration that ustHRV metrics alone can predict real-world health or performance outcomes.

Therefore, the rationale of this paper was to investigate which HRV parameters can be reliably assessed when an ultra-short-term ECG signal is recorded, and what is the minimal time interval that allows for this. We selected a subset of widely used linear HRV indices representing time- and frequency-domain physiology. Thus, we measured both statistical (RMSSD, SDSD, and pNN50%) and spectral (frequency) (high-frequency power, low-frequency power, and three relative measures) HRV parameters. The aim was not to compare HRV feature families but to assess the effects of signal duration on the established metrics. In addition to a standard measurement taken when the participants were vigilant and relatively rested, we additionally evaluated how agreement changes during a prolonged experimental session, as well as the effect of a single-dose energy boost dietary supplement in EO (eyes-open) and EC (eyes-closed) conditions on the agreement of the ustHRV measures with the reference one (benchmark). The reason for this study design was to evaluate the robustness of ustHRV estimates relative to the standard reference under changing experimental conditions, including progressing mental fatigue and drowsiness. The EC and EO conditions allowed us to manipulate the sensory processing load further. Finally, acute energy boost supplementation, which we have previously shown to reduce the effect of mental fatigue on the CNS in a resting state18 and during a cognitive task19, acted as an additional factor that may change the agreement of the HRV measures among different ECG recording lengths. These manipulations were used as conditions to test the robustness of measurement agreement, not to establish HRV as a biomarker of psychophysiological state.

Based on the2, the hypotheses of this work are: (1) HRV parameters related to the higher frequencies may stabilize over shorter recording durations, (2) since statistical measures correspond to HF power, these measures should also be reliably assessed from the ustHRV recordings. Therefore, we expect the HF, RMSSD, SDSD, and pNN50% may show higher agreement for recordings of at least 1-min. Shorter ECG recordings are to be evaluated, since there are no well-grounded works using such short time intervals.

This study is methodological in nature and focuses on measurement validity rather than physiological or clinical interpretation of HRV. However, the physiological interpretation of the HRV presupposes measurement stability. Therefore, validating the temporal limits of the HRV estimation is a prerequisite for meaningful physiological inference. Thus, the significance of this study is two-fold. First, we add to the limited literature on ustHRV validation that maintains a scientific regime. Moreover, our experimental design allowed us to validate the ustHRV measures not only in one condition. Comparing the results that included all the aforementioned factors used in our experiment, we got a better insight into whether (and how) each of the ustHRV measures behaved under the experimental settings, which changed the psychophysiological state of the participants at rest. Thus, the strength and novelty of this work lie in its being one of the most comprehensive systematic assessments of recording-duration sensitivity across numerous standard HRV indices under changing experimental states. To our knowledge, this study provides one of the first systematic mappings of minimum reliable ECG recording durations for these indices across such conditions. This is important, as most previous studies focused on one recording, active physical activity, or special populations (such as athletes). Such a narrow study design focuses on psychophysiological mechanisms limited to one specific condition, which doesn’t allow for generalization to the population at large and/or activities unrelated to physical effort. Thus, our findings can provide more information on whether the studied ustHRV measures can be reliably used in cardiovascular, neurocognitive, neurological, or neuroengineering applications. This work has a special significance in medical applications.

Methods

Participants

Forty-seven healthy, physically active students or university alums (26.1 ± 4.6 years old) were recruited (27 women). The sample size was established based on the previous literature4. The inclusion criteria were right-handedness, moderate caffeine usage, normal or corrected to normal vision, and no history of neurological, psychological, or cardiovascular disorders. The participants abstained from consuming alcohol, caffeine, intoxicants, energizing beverages, or other such substances for at least 12 h before the study. Moderate caffeine consumption was defined as not more than 400 mg/d, according to Health Canada, the U. S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA, 2012), the European Food Safety Authority (2015), and the U.S. Dietary Guidelines for Americans (USDHHS & USDA, 2015)20. The caffeine usage by our participants was assessed based on their answers in the questionnaire. The study was conducted with each subject’s understanding and written consent, according to the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association, Declaration of Helsinki, and approved by the Committee of Ethics of the University of Silesia in Katowice on scientific studies conducted on humans (number 2/2018).

Procedure

This work presents the analysis of HRV based on the ECG recorded in our previous study. The original study aimed to investigate the influence of the energy dietary supplement with a small amount of caffeine on the functioning of the CNS and ANS. For details of the study design, see 19 and 18. The study group received 55 mg of caffeine, including caffeine extract, guarana, yerba mate, cocoa powder, vitamin B, and other vitamins and minerals as a single effervescent tablet. The placebo group received a single effervescent tablet, matched for its content of the remaining ingredients. To maintain the real-world application of such supplementation, a single tablet was used for each participant (as recommended by the manufacturer). The groups didn’t differ in the participants’ height or body mass. ECG signal was collected in three runs: before the supplementation (run 1), 30 min after the supplementation (run 2), and 90 min after the supplementation (run 3), as the peak plasma concentration of caffeine is reached in 30–120 min after the intake21–23.

Seven ECG recordings were taken in each run, of which five were task-related (visual and auditory oddball tasks, motor reaction-time task, Stroop task, and mental arithmetic task), and two were 5-minute resting states (EO and EC conditions). Only resting-state conditions were used in the present study. During each run, participants were seated in a comfortable chair in a dimmed room. During the resting-state EO condition, participants were instructed to fixate on a white cross on a black computer screen. Participants were asked to minimize muscle tension and avoid large movements during EO and EC conditions. The experiment started at the same time of the day (2 pm ± 2 h) to avoid possible differences in the circadian rhythm, which were reported to influence human cognition24. Figure 1 presents the study design.

The study design: the first ECG measurement was taken before the supplementation (run 1), the second one − 30 min after the supplementation (run 2), and the third one – 90 min after the supplementation. The supplementation was the energy boost dietary supplement in the study group and the placebo in the placebo group. Each resting state measurement was taken twice – with eyes open (EO), and eyes closed (EC).

ECG acquisition

Each participant’s preparation lasted around 30 min due to the simultaneous EEG recording. During preparation, participants rested in a sitting position. This guaranteed normalizing the physiological activity that might confound the data, such as breathing rate, blood pressure, and sweating. ECG signal was recorded using Einthoven Lead II channel, with the standard electrode placement: right wrist and left foot for the active electrodes and right foot for the ground, using 2000 Hz sampling rate and 0.5–35 Hz FIR band-pass filter with Blackman window. Standard Ag/AgCl gel-based electrodes were used, with OneStep Clear Gel (ANT Neuro) inserted into the electrodes to provide better contact between the skin and the electrodes. The impedance was kept below 10 kΩ. Participants were allowed to breathe and blink freely during the recordings, but were asked to avoid intentional deep breaths. The MP36R system with AcqKnowledge 4.2 software (Biopac Systems Inc., USA) was used for data acquisition and offline pre-processing.

ECG pre-processing

Participants’ preparation, ECG data acquisition, pre-processing, and analysis strictly followed the standard and updated guidelines and recommendations11,25–30. The signal pre-processing pipeline is presented in Fig. 2. If the ECG signal was longer than 5 min, it was trimmed at the beginning and end so that precisely 5 min of recording was left. We ensured that there were only whole cycles in the recording after trimming, i.e., no cycle was cut in between. Then, if the signal baseline was not around 0 mV, it was corrected by adding an appropriate value to each data point so that the isoelectric line between the heart cycles was around 0 mV. At that time, the first signal quality check was done by visually inspecting the recording. Heart rate (HR) was then calculated, plotted, and checked if the values were between the physiological limits (~ 60–90 BPM, second quality check). If there were any abnormalities in the ECG signal at that point, they were corrected by using the ‘connect endpoints’ function, which interpolated the chosen fragment of a signal with a straight line. We ensured that the R peaks were untouched as they were used for the HRV analysis. If there was an artifact on the remaining peaks in the signal, the calculations of the ECG complexes from that cycle were excluded from the following analysis. Next, R peak maxima were located, classified, and labeled (QRS marker). A third quality check was done at that step to ensure all the normal cycles had been labeled correctly.

HRV analysis

Before the HRV analysis, a quality check of the raw tachogram (i.e., the plot of the consecutive RR intervals along the number of beats) from the whole recordings (i.e., 5-min) was performed (fourth quality check). First, we identified any interval that differed from the average of 10 preceding intervals by more than 20% (i.e., higher than 120% or lower than 80%, as was done in31). Since the guidelines on the HRV analysis do not recommend using automatic tools for ectopic beat correction11,25–30, this step was used only to identify the suspicious beats. If such an ectopic beat was identified, it was located on the raw ECG signal and handled manually. If the interval was ectopic (e.g., a missing beat or missing QRS label), it was removed and interpolated according to32. After handling all the ectopic beats, the raw tachogram was plotted again to ensure no artifacts remained, since even one ectopic beat may disrupt the HRV analysis33.

Then, a multi-epoch HRV analysis was performed. First, we extracted 10-s, 30-s, 1-min, 2-min, 3-min, 4-min, and 5-min time intervals (epochs) from each ECG recording. 10-s to 4-min data segments served as the ECG lengths to validate the candidates for the ustHRV surrogates, and a 5-min recording served as the reference (benchmark), i.e., stHRV. The HRV analyses were performed on each data length. Statistical (RMSSD, SDSD, and pNN50%) and frequency (LF power, HF power, nLF power, nHF power, and LF/HF ratio) HRV measures were calculated according to the international guidelines11,25–30. Due to the questionable reliability of the very low frequency band (VLF) in short-term recordings and the recommendation not to analyze this parameter from recordings shorter than 5 min, we did not include VLF in our analyses2,29,34.

The events (i.e., the QRS markers) were used to locate the R waves for the spectral HRV analysis, with the ‘QRS peak’ as the event type. RR intervals were then re-sampled to a continuous sampling rate to extract frequency information. 8 Hz cubic-spline interpolation was used to generate the continuous time-domain representation of the RR intervals. The frequency information was extracted from the RR intervals and analyzed to calculate the standard ratios. Welch periodogram was used to generate the power spectrum density (PSD) using a Hamming window. The remaining parameters were: 1024 points Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) width, 50% overlap, linear regression detrending applied for each segment independently before the FFT computation, and automatic window size L (calculated as L = n/4.5, where n was the number of samples in the data length). The power in the low-frequency (LF, 0.04 to 0.15 Hz), the power in the high-frequency range (HF, 0.15 to 0.4 Hz), normalized LF power (nLF, calculated as LF/(LF + HF)), normalized HF power (nHF, calculated as HF/(LF + HF)), and LF/HF ratio were calculated. HRV analysis was performed using AcqKnowledge 5.0 software (Biopac Systems Inc., USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Statistica 14.1. Calculated statistical and frequency HRV measures from each participant from the placebo and study groups, all runs, all studied lengths, and both studied conditions (EC and EO) were subject to statistical analysis.

Because this work requires a strict criterion for including the participants in the analysis (i.e., the participant must have a clean, artifact-free ECG signal of at least 5 min for each run, for at least one condition), 38 participants (20 women) were subject to the next steps of data analysis. At the next step, two more participants were excluded due to an incidental finding (one person) and data disruption (one person). Next, after computing HRV measures for each participant, we applied a criterion of ±2SD from the group mean to identify participants whose HRV measures were highly atypical. This step reduced the influence of extreme between-subject variability that could skew group-level statistics. Such a criterion helps ensure that the group-level analyses reflect typical physiological patterns and improve statistical robustness. This is especially important for the physiological measures, such as HRV, as they are known to be sensitive to individual differences in the autonomic function, lifestyle, and health status. The number of outliers (single data points) removed was 5.38% (SD = 3.00) and 5.99% (SD = 3.27) on average across all measures and lengths for EC and EO conditions, respectively. The highest number of outliers was observed for pNN50% for 10-s signals for both conditions (20% and 24%, respectively). This is logical, as this measure is calculated based on the number of pairs of successive RR intervals that differ by more than 50 ms. Within only 10 s, there are only a few RR intervals, which makes this parameter much more prone to deviation from the mean than other HRV measures. The possible impact of this situation is discussed further in the text. All other measures had a similar number of outliers removed. The final number of participants included in the analysis of each HRV measure for each condition, group, length, and run is presented in Table S1.

The normal distribution was tested with the Shapiro-Wilk test, homogeneity of variance was measured with Levene’s test, and sphericity was measured with Mauchly’s test. The original data was not normally distributed, so we log-transformed it (using natural logarithm with base Euler). It is assumed that the transformed distribution is not normal, and it is recommended that the parametric tests be used if the data is log-transformed4,35. However, since our log-transformed data were generally normally distributed in the EC condition but not in the EO condition, we performed both parametric and nonparametric statistics to ensure the results were consistent and didn’t depend on the statistical tests. The results of the Shapiro-Wilk test are presented in Table 1. The results of Levene’s and Mauchly’s tests are gathered in Tables S2 and S3 in the Supplementary Materials, for the EC and EO conditions, respectively. Pearson’s correlation coefficient and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient were used to analyze the correlations. To compare the values of the studied HRV measures among the lengths of the extracted ECG recordings, rmANOVA and Friedman tests with the repeated variable LENGTH (10 s, 30 s, 1 min, 2 min, 3 min, 4 min, 5 min) were used. Bonferroni correction was used to control for the multiple comparison problem (MCP). To evaluate the impact of the experimental setting (time throughout the experimental session, represented by three runs, and the energy boost supplementation effect, described as the placebo and study groups) on the agreement of the HRV measures between the studied lengths of the ECG signal, rmANOVA with the repeated variable RUN (run 1, run 2, run 3), and categorical factors: GROUP (placebo, study) and LENGTH was performed. Nonparametric Friedman and Kruskal-Wallis tests were performed to test the RUN and LENGTH variables, respectively. In addition, Bland-Altman plots were created to visualize the agreement and bias of the HRV measures from run 1 (before the supplementation) between each of the studied lengths of the ECG signal with the reference length (5 min). The level of statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Such an extensive analysis design allowed us for a comprehensive and reliable validation of the ustHRV surrogate measures compared to the benchmark short-term HRV at rest and under experimental conditions, as recommended in4. In case of more than one condition, these recommendations emphasize the need for a 4-step statistical procedure: correlation with benchmark measure, visual investigation of bias using Bland-Altman plots, statistical tests among the conditions, and the inter-group trend analysis across time scales to observe the trends.

Correlations

The correlation results were analyzed separately for run 1, with both groups collapsed, and for all runs, with the data split into the placebo and study groups. The first case represented a standard recording at rest. On the other hand, looking at how the correlations changed throughout the experimental session in the placebo and study groups allowed us to observe the possible impact of the setting used in this experiment (time and supplementation) on the correlations between the surrogates and the reference length.

Thus, first, we performed a correlation analysis of each measure between each signal length and the 5-min signal length, taken before the supplementation for both groups collapsed (Table 1). As recommended in4, we set up a threshold (R = 0.7) for which the values were considered correlated.

First, the results from the parametric and nonparametric tests were congruent. The frequency measures related to LF power (LF, nLF, nHF, and LF/HF) correlated with the reference signal length only when the signal was at least 1-min long. However, HF power correlated starting from 30-s. Similar correlations were observed for all statistical measures.

This agrees with the nature of these measures. The lower edge of the LF power was 0.04 Hz, so the time interval of one cycle was 25 s, and one cycle is not enough to reliably assess the signal’s power. On the other hand, the lower edge of the HF power was 0.15 Hz, which gave 6.(6) s for one cycle. Thus, in the 30-s time window, there were approximately 4.5 cycles, which is above the accepted limit of 3 cycles30. On the contrary, the statistical measures are based on the number of consecutive RR intervals. Since the average interval is about 700–900 ms, it gives 33 to 43 RR intervals, a reasonable number of data points to calculate the statistical measures.

To better visualize how these correlations changed with the time of the experimental session and whether they differed between the groups, we plotted the shortest signal lengths for which the measures correlated with the reference signal for each run and group. The results of this analysis of the trends of their changes for statistical (Fig. S1A) and frequency (Fig. S1B) measures are presented in the Supplementary Materials.

However, looking only at correlations is insufficient to assess the agreement between the surrogate and the reference measures. Therefore, in the next step, we compared the HRV measures among the lengths of the recordings.

rmANOVA revealed: (1) the main effect of RUN for RMSSD, SDSD, pNN50%, and HF power, (2) the main effect of LENGTH for HF power, LF power, nHF power, nLF power, and LF/HF ratio, and (3) the main effect of GROUP for LF power. Discussion

At rest

The correlations from both conditions suggest that when ECG is measured at rest with no experimental setting that might influence the mental state of the subjects, statistical measures and HF power correlate well with the reference signal when the ECG recording is at least 30-s long. LF power correlated for the signals of at least 1 min in the EC and 30 s–1 min (depending on the tests used) in the EO condition. 1 min was needed in the EC and 2 min in the EO condition for the relative frequency measures (1–2 min for LF/HF ratio, depending on the test) (Table 1). We would also like to note the need to cautiously interpret the LF/HF ratio. This measure is commonly used as a sympathovagal balance; however, there is a critique of this approach (please see37 for reference). The statistical measures from run 1 didn’t differ substantially among the lengths. HF power was comparable with the reference length starting from the 30-s recordings and the other frequency measures starting from 1-min signals in both conditions (Figs. 3 and 6). The Bland-Altman plots revealed a bias close to 0 for the statistical measures in both conditions, even in the 30-s recordings. It was also reasonably close to 0 for HF power from the 30-s recordings and close to 0 for the longer ones. For the remaining frequency measures, it was close to 0 only when the signal was at least 1-min long (Figs. 5 and 8).

Thus, our results show that when ECG is recorded at rest in reasonably short experimental sessions, when the participants are still alert, a 30-s recording is enough to analyze statistical HRV measures reliably. This result is consistent with the recent findings by38 and39, who found SDNN and RMSSD reliable in recordings as short as 30 s. Orini et al.14 found that even a 15-s signal agreed well with the 6-min one, but since this length is between the ones we investigated, it is hard to compare the results directly. A few other studies presented different results that suggested an acceptable agreement in 1-min recordings for SDNN or as short as 10 s for RMSSD3,13,40. These studies, however, suffer from methodological limitations (small sample sizes, the benchmark recording of less than 5 min, inappropriate statistical methods, or no balance in terms of sex of the participants) or differed in the protocol design (standing position, not including a 30-s length in the experiment). Similar inconsistencies were observed for pNN50, which has been shown in these works to agree well with as little as 20 s40 through 1 min3, up to even above 4 min13. One point must be addressed in relation to our results for the pNN50% measure. As shown in the Methods section, the value of this measure calculated from the 10-s recordings had a bigger deviation from the mean than pNN50% from the longer recordings (due to a small number of RR intervals in such a short recording), which may result in decreased statistical power. This might be the reason we didn’t find differences in pNN50% between this length and the other ones. However, since we performed a carefully designed set of statistical tests, based on the literature recommendation, the final outcome showed that only recordings not shorter than 30 s allowed us for a reliable pNN50% assessment. Therefore, this issue did not impact our overall conclusions. In addition, it emphasizes the need for a well-designed analysis procedure composed of several tests to be able to reliably validate surrogate measures, such as ustHRV.

The discrepancies observed in the previous works are probably related to the limitations of the studies mentioned above, but also to differences in the experimental conditions, which influenced the psychophysiological state of the participants. As we show in this work, this may impact the agreement, even if the ECG is recorded at rest (i.e., without the physical activity). Some HRV features, such as mean NN intervals, have been shown to retain reliability with recording windows as short as 30 s12. However, not all measures are stable over ultrashort periods during cognitively demanding tasks or under cognitive stress. Recently, the need for further validation to establish the robustness of ustHRV in different arousal levels has also been emphasized41.

Regarding the frequency measures, Orini et al. (Orini et al., 2023) reported that the agreement between ultra-short (15-s) and short-term (6-min) HRV indices was moderate for HF power. Our results show that 10-s recordings didn’t, but 30-s ones corresponded well with the stHRV, complementing these findings. Wehler et al. (Wehler et al., 2021) reported that HF was reliable and retained considerable differences between control and definite cardiac autonomic neuropathy (dCAN) from ECGs down to 10 s. However, this study employed a different study design, as the authors verified the assessment of dCAN using ustHRV, comparing 11 patients with 76 healthy controls. However, the most prevalent group differences in that study were found for LF longer than 60 s. Interestingly, our results (and a few previous studies that complied with the methodological regime) showed that older recommendations, e.g., at least 250-s recording time for LF analysis4, are unnecessary when the participants rest physically. Most importantly, however, our results show that the studies that claim the reliability of the HRV measures related to lower frequencies, calculated from the ECG recordings of as short as 10–20 s, should be treated cautiously.

At rest with the changing psychophysiological state

The correlations of the statistical measures and HF power barely changed throughout the session in the EO condition. In contrast, the threshold of R = 0.7 increased in the EC condition in run 2 in the placebo group for the statistical measures (to 3 min for RMSSD and SDSD) and in both groups for HF power (up to 3 min in the placebo and 2 min in the study group). The correlations of the LF, nHF, nLF, and LF/HF ratio also changed, with the biggest change for the nHF and nLF in run 2 in the study group in the EO condition (up to 4 min) (Figs. S1 and S2).

Time passing through the experimental session (i.e., from run 1 to run 3) didn’t change the comparisons among the lengths for the pNN50%, HF, LF, nHF, and LF/HF. SDSD from 10-s recordings became considerably lower than the longer ones starting from run 2 in both conditions. nLF from the 1-min signal became considerably lower than longer recordings in run 3 in the EO condition (Figs. 3 and 6).

In longer experimental sessions (evaluated by us as the three runs where the signals were collected along the 3-h experiment) as well as when stimulatory substances with a small amount of caffeine are being studied (evaluated by us as the placebo and study groups) (Figs. 4 and 7), no LENGTH factor (either main or interactions) was found for the statistical measures in either condition, whereas it was confirmed for the frequency measures with the same post-hoc differences as in the previous analysis. In addition, for the LF power in the EO condition, the LENGTHxGROUP effect was also observed.

The trend analysis based on the rmANOVA analysis with the repeated variable RUN and the categorical factor GROUP performed separately for each length showed that the effects observed for the benchmark length were mapped into the length of at least 30 s for the EC condition. In the EO condition, these results were ambiguous.

This analysis shows that the ustHRV measures from the 30-s recordings in the EC condition reproduced the condition-dependent changes observed in the standard stHRV metrics, suggesting that their sensitivity to autonomic modulation is preserved at shorter recording lengths. However, this does not constitute direct physiological validation of the ANS activity.

Comparing the shortest signal lengths for which the measures correlated with the reference signal (Fig. S2) showed that the agreement of the statistical measures and the HF power from 30-s and longer signals in the EO condition did not change. However, in the EC condition, even these measures agreed only when the signal was longer (up to 3 min in the placebo group, 30 min after supplementation) (Fig. S1). Since these effects were more pronounced in the placebo than in the study group, this suggests that it is due to the increasing drowsiness and mental fatigue, which were impeded after energy boost supplementation, as shown in our previous work for the resting state EEG in18, and in42 where we found subtle effects of supplementation on the HF power in the placebo group. However, direct physiological effects cannot be ruled out. On the other hand, LF power from at least a 2-min recording agreed with the reference signal later throughout the experimental session. The agreement of the normalized measures was disturbed, with some measures needing even 4 min in some cases. Our findings agree well with the nature of the studied measures and the electrophysiological processes they represent. Since the heart rate tends to decrease during long experimental sessions, which is related to increasing mental fatigue and drowsiness, it is understandable that the HRV measures related to the slower frequency should be more prone to possible alterations. In addition, the normalized measures depend on both the high- and low-frequency components, so finding less stable effects for these measures is sensible.

Interestingly, the pattern of these changes in the agreement between the surrogate and the reference signal shows an opposite effect between HRV measures representing short-term variations and those representing long-term variations, and the EO and EC conditions. The agreement with the reference signal for the statistical measures and the HF power worsened more in the placebo than in the study group in the EC condition. On the other hand, for the LF-related measures, this disagreement was more pronounced in the EO condition (especially in the study group for the normalized measures). The possible explanation may be related to the mechanism of heart rate regulation by the ANS. Having their eyes closed, the participants used fewer attentional resources, and the perceptual/sensory processing was lower than when their eyes were open. Therefore, the measures related to short-term variations (statistical and HF power) may be disrupted more easily in the EC condition through the parasympathetic branch (which contributes 80% to the heart rate at rest and slows the heart rate through acetylcholine release) if no energy boost is supplied. This is in line with the recent findings that the parasympathetic nervous system acts as a relaxation system and tends to be activated under increasing mental fatigue43. On the other hand, other measures depend on both branches, so the impact of the changes in the autonomic control of the heart rate on the ustHRV agreement is more ambiguous.

To sum up, our study shows that as long as the ECG is recorded at rest in vigilant participants, 30 s is enough to analyze HRV using statistical or high-frequency measures. However, when the environment might induce or deepen mental fatigue or drowsiness, e.g., when the experimental session lasts longer, the psychophysiological state of the participants changes. Therefore, very short ECG recordings may not represent the HRV well in these conditions. The effect of the supplementation used in the study group, which acts as an energy boost, on how the HRV measures from the surrogate lengths agreed with the reference signal supports this interpretation. Similarly, the psychoactive state of the human organism differs between the EC and EO conditions due to differences in perceptual processing, which may influence the balance of the autonomic branches. Importantly, even though the aim of this work was to compare the reliability of the ustHRV parameters measured under different experimental conditions, and not to establish these measures as biomarkers of an individual’s psychophysiological state, the experimental manipulations applied in this study (rest, fatigue, drowsiness) are known to be associated with changes in the autonomic regulation. Therefore, they provide a relevant framework for assessing the robustness of the ustHRV measures under varying physiological conditions. Moreover, some of the ustHRV measures not only agreed with the reference 5-min HRV metrics, but also reproduced their condition-dependent patterns across the experimental session. This suggests that the sensitivity of the established HRV indices to autonomic regulation may be preserved at shorter recording lengths under the investigated conditions. However, we emphasize that this does not constitute direct physiological validation of the ANS activity, which would require targeted experimental interventions. Rather, our findings support the use of ustHRV as a practical surrogate of the standard stHRV metrics, whose physiological interpretation is grounded in the existing literature.

These promising results lay the path to the novel applications of HRV analysis in dynamic and ambulatory environments and factors changing human psychophysiological state. However, they also indicate that different lengths of the ECG recordings may be needed in various conditions, even though they are recorded when the participants are seated.

Our findings have several potential practical applications. First of all, in the medical applications, they suggest the minimal time needed to record the ECG signal for the reliable HRV analysis, especially when the patients are not fully rested, e.g., after waiting to be admitted to the hospital. The results also allow for setting up the minimal recording time for the ambulatory settings. Moreover, they support the researchers who wish to use HRV measures in dynamic conditions or to capture event-related reactions of the ANS (e.g., HTI, cognitive neuroscience, driving, rehabilitation, postural studies) in designing their experiments. Finally, all applications related to the implementation of the neuroengineering solutions, such as wearable sensors and smart clothing, may benefit from knowing whether the HRV measures may be reliable derived from ultra-short ECG recordings.

Crucially, this is one of only a few works that validate the ustHRV and maintain a high methodological regime. Some other works have focused on the active participants during or after physical exercises or on special populations, such as athletes. Whereas, to our best knowledge, this is the first work presenting such an investigation for the participants at rest, but with a changing psychophysiological state. Even though the resting state (as it represents the default, idle activity of the nervous system) and experimental manipulation aiming at inducing mental fatigue (which is the state where people seek the energy boost) were our intention, our results should not be interpreted as the overall ustHRV validation where other conditions are applied, especially in dynamic setups. Therefore, we will further exploit the validity of the ustHRV measures in task-related experiments focused on event-related analysis, i.e., investigating the heart’s response to stimuli from the environment and increased cognitive load. Another line of research that we have already initiated and will further investigate is analyzing biomedical signals (including ECG) in mobile participants, which allows for capturing changes in the neurovascular activity in real-life settings.

The present study should be interpreted as a methodological validation of an ECG recording duration for the established HRV metrics, rather than a clinical or physiological model of autonomic function.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated which HRV measures may be reliably assessed when an ultra-short-term ECG signal is recorded, compared to the 5-min benchmark recording. Moreover, we investigated how changes in the psychophysiological state of the participants due to mental fatigue and drowsiness, energy dietary supplementation, and the difference between the EO and EC conditions impact the agreement of the ustHRV surrogates with the reference signal. Our results suggest that the statistical HRV measures and HF power from the recordings of as short as 30 s, and the other frequency measures of 1 min are good surrogates of the stHRV when the participants are at rest and vigilant. However, experimental setting that increases mental fatigue or reduces perceptual processing requires longer recordings. This work lays the path to the novel applications of the ustHRV analysis in dynamic environments and factors changing humans’ psychophysiological state. Crucially, this is one of only a few works that validate the ustHRV and maintain a high methodological regime. Notably, to our best knowledge, this is the first work presenting such an investigation at rest but with the changing psychophysiological state of the participants. However, our results should not be interpreted as the overall ustHRV validation where other conditions are applied, especially in dynamic setups. Therefore, we will further exploit the validity of the ustHRV measures in task-related experiments focused on event-related analysis, as well as in mobile participants, which allows for capturing changes in the neurovascular activity in real-life settings.

Study strengths and limitations

Our results show that it is possible to obtain reliable measures of the HRV using shorter ECG recordings than the standard 5-min length. However, to avoid overinterpretation, we would like to point out that our data were measured from young, healthy adults, in specific conditions (resting state, lightly varied psychophysiological states). Thus, the effects may not be the same in patients or other populations, even under the same experimental conditions. However, we believe that our work may pave the way for future studies that will extend our results. We would also point out that our sample size, even though it is typical in the neurophysiological studies using biomedical signals, is worth increasing in future studies, especially when other populations are investigated, for better generalizability.

The strength of the study is the fact that the majority of HRV studies do not involve energy-boosting supplements. However, since the usage of such products has strongly increased in recent years, their effects should not be overlooked. Therefore, our study provides a starting point for such studies. Moreover, our results are relevant and important for resting baseline conditions, which can benefit from reduced recording lengths, which in turn decreases the overall study length and subject fatigue, resulting in fewer confounds in investigating the effects of a variety of manipulations.

Another strength is the fact that the supplementation that we used as one of the manipulations was not caffeine itself, but a multi-ingredient dietary supplement that contained a low dose of caffeine and other stimulatory ingredients. Such a manipulation was designed to investigate the potential impact of such products, taking advantage of the interactions between the ingredients, which were described in our previous works18,42. However, this does not allow us to generalize our results to all supplements, especially those containing only caffeine, as they may lack the effects related to these interactions.

Finally, a carefully designed set of complementary tests, created based on the literature recommendations, allowed us to reliably assess the validity of ustHRV measures in the studied conditions.
