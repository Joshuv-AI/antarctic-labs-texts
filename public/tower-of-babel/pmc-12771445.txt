Phosphorus-Promoted VS2 Nanosheets: Unlocking Defect Engineering and Interlayer Modulation for Enhanced Hydrogen Evolution Reaction
Authors: Arunkumar Prabhakaran Shyma, Raja Sellappan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
VS2-based catalyst materials have been relatively underexplored for the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) due to their limited active sites and lower conductivity compared to other transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs), despite being cost-effective and stable in alkaline conditions. In this work, we present phosphorus (P)-assisted interlayer expansion and defect engineering of VS2 nanosheets to significantly enhance their catalytic performance for the HER by improving their intrinsic electronic conductivity. A simple one-pot hydrothermal method is utilized to synthesize P-doped VS2 nanosheets with a defect-rich, interlayer-expanded structure. Phosphorus doping leads to electron redistribution in the VS2 system due to differences in the electronegativity. The phosphorus dopant serves as a bridge, helping to more evenly balance the charge gradient between the V and S atoms. The material demonstrates remarkable kinetic activity markers as well as boosted performance toward HER. The synergistically doped P1-VS2 achieved an overpotential of 191 mV at a current density of 10 mA cm–2 in a 1 M KOH medium. The catalyst also delivers a low Tafel value of 65.91 mV/dec. Doping of P into the VS2 lattice changes its physiochemical properties due to the difference in electronegativity between S and P atoms. The intercalated P played a pivotal role in establishing more active sites, which could potentially enhance the HER performance.

Introduction

Since the concern over fossil fuel emissions, there has been an increase in demand for clean energy production and utilization. , In this regard, hydrogen (H2) produced from renewable energy is regarded as one of the potential candidates over the existing energy storage and conversion because of its high energy density, zero carbon footprint, and feedstock for different industrial applications. Electrochemical water splitting is a promising approach for producing H2 from water using renewable electricity and is considered as a matured technology with the potential to scale up production. Electrochemical water splitting proceeds with two main reactions: hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and oxygen evolution reaction (OER). , A high overpotential is always desirable for water electrolysis since the hydrogen adsorption kinetics in an aqueous medium is sluggish in nature. A noble electrocatalyst such as platinum (Pt) and its alloys are considered forefront HER catalysts, but their high cost and limited availability hinder their practical usage. Identifying earth-abundant and non-noble catalysts is crucial for the proper establishment of a water electrolyzer over the existing energy storage and conversion methods. The proposed HER electrocatalyst material should lower the overpotential for hydrogen adsorption, thereby minimizing the energy utilization.

Transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs) such as MoS2, WS2, NiS2, CoS2, and their selenide counterpart have attracted considerable amount of attention as electrocatalysts for HER. TMDCs have shown its charm in terms of electrocatalytic activity as well as their intriguing properties such as tunable band gap and fast carrier dynamics. The cost-effectiveness of TMDCs compared to Pt-based alloys also makes them a potential alternative for HER. In this regard, VS2 is considered as a practically viable candidate for HER owing to its structural similarities with other established TMDCs such as MoS2 and NiS2. The metallic nature of VS2 favors to expedite the HER rate by fast electron dynamics. Theoretical studies on VS2 confirms the possibility of the same to act as a potential HER electrocatalyst due to its sheet morphology and abundant active sites. It has been found recently that heteroatom doping on TMDCs can effectively modify their electronic structure, thereby enhancing their catalytic activity. The major role of a dopant is to alter the structural and electronic characteristics of the catalyst facilitating significant improvement on the hydrogen adsorption or desorption process. We have opted phosphorus (P) as the dopant candidate for VS2 to explore its electrochemical characteristics toward HER. According to recent reports, P has shown its supremacy by enhancing the structural as well as the electronic properties of CoS2 and MoS2 toward HER. , The intercalated P could possibly create additional active sites as well as defects in the parent material for enhanced electron kinetics. , In this work, we have attempted to dope a VS2 nanosheet with P to assess its electrochemical activity toward hydrogen adsorption and desorption energy. Doping of P into the VS2 lattice changes its physiochemical properties because of the difference in electronegativity between S and P atoms. This shift in intrinsic characteristics of the material can play a vital role in surface catalysis and its kinetics. This work provides a promising yet novel route to develop an electrocatalyst through a facile P intercalation into VS2 which results in defect modulations as well as interlayer expansion for better electrocatalytic activity via boosted electron dynamics.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Ammonium metavanadate (NH4VO3), thioacetamide (CH3CSNH2), and sodium hypophosphite (NaH2PO2·2H2O) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich and used directly without further purification.

Synthesis

A facile hydrothermal method was employed to prepare VS2 and P-doped VS2 nanosheets. In a typical reaction, 10 mmol of CH3CSNH2 was dissolved in 70 mL of distilled water followed by the addition of 2 mmol of NH4VO3. The resulting suspension was kept stirring for 40 min at room temperature. The suspension was then transferred to a 100 mL Teflon lined autoclave and maintained at a temperature of 180 °C for 20 h. The autoclave was naturally cooled to an ambient temperature. Following the reaction, the material was extracted by centrifugation using DI water and ethanol. The collected sample was dried in a hot air oven at 60 °C for 10 h. Various molar ratios were carried out in between NaH2PO2·2H2O/CH3CSNH2 to prepare P-doped VS2 nanosheets. Three P-doping ratios were implemented such as low, moderate, and high corresponding to a molar ratio of 0.05, 0.1, and 0.2, respectively. The prepared samples were named VS2 (Pristine), P-VS2 (5% P), P1-VS2 (10% P), and P2-VS2 (20% P), respectively. All of the chemical reagents were procured from Sigma-Aldrich and used without further purification. Synthesis scheme for phosphorus-promoted VS2 nanosheets is shown in Figure S1 in the Supporting Information.

Material Characterizations

X-ray diffraction (XRD) data for all of the prepared samples were obtained using a Malvern PANalytical ‘X′Pert PRO instrument with a Cu Kα (λ = 0.15406 nm) radiation source. Field emission scanning microscopy (FESEM, Thermo Fisher FEI Quanta 250 FEG) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM, FEI-Tecnai G2 20 Twin) images were utilized to probe the morphologies of the prepared samples. Raman spectrographs were procured using a Horriba Raman spectrometer. The elemental composition and oxidation states of the prepared samples were thoroughly characterized by using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (Nexsa G2 Surface Analysis System, Thermo Fisher). Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (EPR) spectra were recorded on a JEOL JES-FA200 spectrometer operating in the band frequency range of 8.75–9.65 GHz.

Electrochemical Characterizations

All electrochemical characterization experiments were conducted by using a CHI660E electrochemical workstation in a 1 M KOH solution at room temperature. The catalyst preparation process involved dispersing 5 mg of the material in 250 μL of water, 245 μL of ethanol, and 5 μL of a 5 wt % Nafion solution, followed by ultrasonication for 30 min to form a homogeneous catalyst ink. This ink was then drop-cast onto a glassy carbon electrode (GCE) with an area of 0.07 cm2. Electrochemical measurements were performed in a 1 M KOH aqueous solution, utilizing the GCE as the working electrode (WE), while a Hg/HgO electrode served as the reference electrode and a platinum foil as the counter electrode. Linear scan voltammetry (LSV) was carried out at a scan rate of 2 mV/s within the potential range of 0 to −900 mV. Additionally, Chronopotentiometry was conducted at a current density of 10 mA cm–2 to evaluate the electrocatalyst’s stability. The potentials of the electrode were changed to the RHE scale by using eq as follows: 1 ERHE=EHg/HgO+0.098V+(0.059×pH)

All measurements were conducted without applying the iR compensation. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was carried out at a potential of 0.1 V versus Hg/HgO, covering a frequency range of 0.5–105 Hz. The electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) of the synthesized catalysts was determined by analyzing the capacitive current associated with double-layer charging at various scan rates during cyclic voltammetry (CV). The double-layer capacitance (C dl) was derived from CV curves recorded in the non-Faradaic potential region. It was calculated by plotting ΔJ = J a – J c at 0 V (vs Hg/HgO) as a function of the scan rate, where C dl corresponds to half of the linear slope. Here, J a and J c represent the anodic and cathodic currents, respectively.

Computational Details

The geometry optimization and electronic energy computation have been performed using Density Functional Theory (DFT) employing basic principles in the Quantum Espresso 7.1 software program. A generalized gradient approximation was used to determine the exchange correlations with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional and was used for investigating the interactions between the valence electrons and core electrons. The semiempirical dispersion-corrected DFT = D3 approach established by Grimme has been employed to account for van der Waals interactions. To achieve the required level of accuracy, the Brillouin zone has been sampled by using a 1 × 1 × 1 k-point grid. All the atoms were permitted to reach the fully relaxed total energy of convergence threshold using 1 × 10–5 a.u. in the geometry optimization, and the cutoff energy was 33 Ry. The force acting on the atoms was less than 0.01 Ry/a.u.

The impact of defect engineering as well as the interlayer expansion on the electrocatalytic activity of P-doped VS2 nanosheets toward HER was investigated using a three-electrode configuration in 1 M KOH as an electrolyte. The overpotentials needed to achieve a geometrical current density of 10 mA cm–2 were acquired by performing LSV for the prepared catalysts as shown in Figure a. The observed electrocatalytic trend of the prepared catalyst toward HER is as follows: Pt/C > P1-VS2> P-VS2> VS2> P2-VS2. An overpotential of 127, 191, 233, 257, and 321 mV was delivered by Pt/C, P1-VS2, P-VS2, VS2, and P2-VS2, respectively. Notably, the sample P1-VS2 delivers the lowest overpotential among all P-doped VS2 nanosheets, which opens a new avenue for better HER activity by moderate P doping in the VS2 nanosheet. The higher feeding ratio of P into the VS2 crystal lattice tends to retard the catalytic activity toward HER. The ruptures in the layers as seen in Figure d and diminishing of XRD crystalline peaks for higher P feeding ratio (P2-VS2) might be responsible for the reduced catalytic activity. According to previous reports, P doping in TMDCs plays a significant role in triggering active sites by creating defects and controlling surface electronic state to enhance intrinsic electrical conductivity. − The enlarged interlayer spacing of VS2 as a result of P doping could increase the density of exposed active sites and alter the electronic configuration to optimize the hydrogen adsorption free energy, which can further improve the activity toward HER.

The reaction kinetics and intrinsic catalytic properties of the prepared materials were further investigated by analyzing Tafel slopes as depicted in Figure b. Tafel plots were obtained from the HER polarization curves. The Tafel slope of the benchmark material (Pt/C) is found to be 40 mV dec–1. The Tafel slopes of the prepared catalysts, namely, VS2, P-VS2, P1-VS2, and P2-VS2, were found to be 97.28, 88.76, 65.91, and 105.96 mV dec–1, respectively. The lower Tafel slope value delivered by P1-VS2 compared to other candidates shed light into its fast charge transfer kinetics and improved intrinsic conductivity toward HER, which initiates faster hydrogen adsorption. The Tafel slope can also be used as a tool to analyze the rate-determining step toward HER. The electrochemical HER originates and proceeds mainly via three steps and eventually water molecules are reduced to H2. − The Volmer adsorption process is the first step where the water molecule interacts with an electron to generate a hydrogen atom (H*), which is adsorbed to the catalyst surface (M) according to eq . 2 H2O+M+e−→M−H*+OH−

The next rate-determining step has two pathways of H2 generation, i.e., the Heyrovsky step (eq ) and Tafel step (eq ). 3 H2O+e−+M−H*→H2+OH−+M 4 2M−H*→H2+2M

The rate-determining step for the P1-VS2 catalyst could be Volmer–Heyrovsky as per the obtained Tafel slope value (65.91 mV dec–1).

The sample P1-VS2 has delivered a remarkable stability of 20 h of operational time at a current density of 10 mA cm–2, which suggests its potential practical viability as shown in Figure a. Furthermore, Figure b shows the LSV of P1-VS2 before and after the stability test, indicating the catalyst’s durability in alkaline media with minimum hysteresis. The turnover frequency (TOF) is a kinetic activity metric for the HER, analogous to Tafel values. The TOF values indicate how quickly hydrogen is formed or the material surface is catalyzed for hydrogen evolution. The TOF values calculated for the catalysts are 0.035, 0.051, 0.155, and 0.009 s–1 for VS2, P-VS2, P1-VS2, and P2-VS2, respectively. The sample P1-VS2 delivered the highest TOF among the prepared catalysts, which could be ascribed to its larger electrochemical surface area (ESCA). The ESCA values of the prepared catalysts were found to be 1.75, 5.5, 31.75, and 2.5 cm –2 for VS2, P-VS2, P1-VS2, and P2-VS2, respectively. The roughness factor (RF) of the prepared catalysts was calculated and turned out to be 25, 78.57, 453.57, and 35.71 for VS2, P-VS2, P1-VS2, and P2-VS2, respectively. The RF can be interpreted as the ratio of geometrical area and ECSA, which is related to the intrinsic reaction kinetics of the electrocatalyst. All calculation details and comparison of the electrochemical activity of all of the synthesized electrocatalysts (Table S1) can be found in the Supporting Information. The enhanced catalytic activity and kinetics of hydrothermal P1-VS2 nanosheets are thought to stem from the synergetic benefits of increased interlayer spacing and defect-rich structure. On the other hand, the defects created on the basal planes are projected to increase the availability of active edge sites, hence significantly improving electrocatalytic performance. Moreover, the increased interlayer spacing can provide favorable structural and electrical modulations for the catalyst to deliver higher hydrogen adsorption kinetics. Doping phosphorus into VS2 alters the chemical bonding due to the difference in electronegativity between the P and S atoms. The surface charge density of doped VS2 can be redistributed through physicochemical interactions, affecting the surface catalysis or kinetic activity of the parent material. However, further increasing the doping gradient of P into VS2 (P2-VS2) hinders the active sites and distracts the sheet morphology of VS2 as evident from ECSA values as well as FESEM images (Figure d).

Double layer capacitance (C dl) is a significant parameter for a catalyst when it comes to catalytic activity. It acts as a promoter for active sites since ECSA is proportional to it. The C dl value of the prepared catalysts was probed by employing CV at various scan rates (Figure S3 in the Supporting Information) and are depicted in Figure a. The sample P1-VS2 delivered the highest C dl value among the prepared catalysts, which shows its ability to act as a potential candidate for water splitting. The high C dl of the sample P1-VS2 might be attributed to the high ECSA as well as the RF since the C dl value is proportional to ECSA. The reaction kinetics of the prepared materials were further probed by employing Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). Figure b shows the Nyquist plot of materials at an overpotential of 0.1 V. The sample P1-VS2 exhibits the smaller charge transfer resistance (R ct) value of 76.38 Ω compared to other catalysts (VS2, 214 Ω; P-VS2, 117.31 Ω; and P2-VS2, 184.58 Ω) which shed light into the synergistic role played by P atoms in VS2 to promote hydrogen adsorption energy for alkaline water splitting. The minimal R ct value signifies the higher faradaic efficiency as well as rapid electron transfer kinetics. The moderate doping of P might be promoted by the intrinsic conductivity of the VS2 nanosheet by altering the charge carrier dynamics favoring electrochemical activity toward HER.

DFT calculations were performed in order to gain insights on the fundamental principle behind the catalytic behavior of the prepared catalysts. Phosphorus doping was represented by one, two, and three phosphorus atoms, corresponding to low, medium, and high levels of doping. Figure a1–a4 shows the constructed supercell of VS2, P-VS2, P1-VS2, and P2-VS2, respectively. The predicted density of states (DOS) of the prepared catalysts is depicted in Figure b1–b4. The valence band is formed by the filled d and p states of V, S, and P, respectively. The conduction band, on the other hand, is composed of the unoccupied d and p states of V, S, and P. The dotted vertical line at 0 eV corresponds to the Fermi energy level. The P doping introduces additional acceptor states near the valence band, which increases the DOS close to the Fermi level. It can be seen that the gradual shift in DOS near the Fermi level of the P-doped sample such as P-VS2, P1-VS2, and P2-VS2 compared to pristine VS2. The samples P1-VS2 and P2-VS2 maintain similar DOS near the Fermi level, which signifies P1-VS2 as an optimized candidate for electrocatalytic water splitting. This effectively raises the number of available charge carriers, enhancing the overall conductivity of the material.

With increased electron concentration due to P doping, the Fermi level shifts downward toward the valence band. This shift indicates a change in the electronic properties of P-doped VS2, promoting a more favorable environment for charge conduction. Enhanced conductivity due to P doping directly influences the electrocatalytic performance of VS2. The improved charge transfer allows for more efficient reaction kinetics as charge carriers can more readily participate in the electrocatalytic process, leading to faster reaction rates. P doping not only modifies the electronic structure by increasing DOS near the Fermi level but also enhances the material’s conductivity, which is vital for efficient electron transfer in electrocatalytic reactions. Figure c1–c4 represents the predicted eigenstates of prepared catalysts. It can be noted that P1-VS2 possesses an increment in the intensity compared to the other candidates. A higher eigenstate intensity signifies a greater likelihood of locating an electron in that specific molecular orbital. Within the framework of molecular orbital theory, this implies that the electron density in that area is more concentrated. At some adsorption sites, there is an increase in the electron density when the eigenstate intensity of orbitals on the material’s surface is high. Better adsorption may be facilitated by stronger contacts between the hydrogen atoms or molecules and the surface as a result of this increased electron density. High eigenstate intensity can help hydrogen adsorb and dissolve more readily on surfaces, which is frequently a need for catalytic activity. Figure d shows the computed free energy profiles of the prepared catalysts. For the spontaneous HER, the free energy of hydrogen adsorption is nearly 0 eV. It could be observed that P1-VS2 delivers an adsorption free energy of 0.58 eV that outperforms the other prepared catalysts. This suggests that among the prepared catalysts, P1-VS2 can maintain a desirable balance and enough adsorption to promote the Volmer step. Figure e shows the EPR spectra of the pristine and P1-VS2 samples. The EPR spectra exhibit a clear signal at a g-factor of approximately 2.005. This value is widely attributed to localized unpaired electrons trapped at sulfur vacancy sites in TMDCs. The pristine VS2 shows a weak EPR signal, indicating a low native sulfur vacancy concentration. In contrast, the P-doped VS2 sample shows a significantly more intense EPR signal, suggesting an increase in the density of paramagnetic sulfur vacancies due to doping.

It is significant to note that only the appropriate dopant loading could result in higher catalytic efficacy. Higher dopant concentrations would cause aggregation, secondary phase formation, and other problems that would impair the material’s conducting behavior and electrocatalytic effectiveness. Lower dopant concentrations might not provide the anticipated HER activity. Hereby, we have demonstrated an optimal P doping into the VS2 lattice (P1-VS2) toward better hydrogen evolution catalysis. The recently reported HER performance of vanadium-based and interlayer expanded MX2 catalysts is displayed in Table S2 in the Supporting Information. To make these electrocatalysts successful for real-time applications, issues with scalability and cost-effectiveness must be resolved while regulating the dopant concentration at the ideal level during the shift from lab scale to bulk.

Conclusion

In this work, we successfully synthesized the nonmetal element of P-doped VS2 nanosheets with increased interlayer spacing. The P1-VS2 nanosheets displayed a Tafel slope of 65.91 mV dec–1 and delivered a low overpotential of 191 mV at a benchmark current density of 10 mA cm–2, showing exceptional HER activity as well as excellent stability that is superior to that of pristine, low and high P-doped VS2 samples. The enhanced catalytic activity and robust stability of the prepared material likely originated from the synergy of expanded interlayer spacing as well as defects that are generated as a result of P doping. On the other hand, this synergy effect resulted in exposing more active sites in P1-VS2, which enabled structural as well as electronic modulations in favor of HER. This study also revealed that the doping of P into the VS2 lattice not only created ample active sites but also improved the intrinsic conductivity of the VS2 nanosheets. Further, the increased interlayer distance has facilitated rapid proton/electron adsorption, enabling faster hydrogen release. Phosphorus doping led to electron redistribution around the V and S atoms due to differences in electronegativity. The phosphorus dopant served as a bridge, helping to more evenly balance the charge gradient between the V and S atoms. The experimental findings were consistent with the obtained theoretical results. Our findings suggest a novel avenue to enhance the VS2 catalysts via P-doping. This strategy can be expected to be achieved with other transition metal chalcogenides, as well.
