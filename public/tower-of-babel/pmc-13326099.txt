Building rehabilitation care capacity for older adults living in long-term care through family/care partner and physical therapist engagement: a scoping review
Authors: Alexander J. Stephen, Denise M. Connelly, Nicole A. Guitar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOlder adults living in long-term care homes often experience impairment in mobility and function requiring physical therapy rehabilitation. Maintaining residents’ physical function can promote quality of life, reduce pain and skin breakdown, and support the provision of personal care. Family/care partners notably contribute to resident well-being. Understanding engagement between physical therapists and family/care partners is essential to advancing resident rehabilitation and health outcomes, particularly as staffing capacity, time, and access to rehabilitation services become increasingly constrained. This review aimed to synthesise and assess the quality of the literature describing engagement between physical therapists and family/care partners of older adults in long-term care.MethodsA scoping review was conducted following Arksey and O’Malley’s framework and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews checklist. Six databases and grey literature were searched without date restrictions. Papers were eligible for inclusion if they: (1) described engagement between physical therapists and family/care partners as reported by any participant group; (2) were conducted in a long-term care home or equivalent setting; (3) focused on physical rehabilitation for older adults; and (4) were available in English. Any study design was eligible. Data were analysed thematically and methodological quality was assessed. ResultsOf 3443 papers and records screened, nine studies were included. Most (n=8) were qualitative and conducted in high-income countries and all were rated as “good” quality. Four themes surfaced describing engagement between physical therapists and family/care partners in long-term care: (1) physical therapists support greater family/care partner involvement in rehabilitation; (2) opportunities to enhance structured communication between physical therapists and family/care partners; (3) misalignment of family/care partner expectations with physical therapy service capacity; and (4) enhancing role clarity and value of family/care partners in rehabilitation.ConclusionEngagement between physical therapists and family/care partners in long-term care homes is underdeveloped despite recognition of its potential benefits. Clearer role definitions, structured communication, and supportive organisational policies are needed to enhance collaboration, reduce caregiver burden, and support manageable physical therapist workloads. Strengthening these partnerships could improve rehabilitation processes, resident health outcomes, and sustainability of long-term care services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07591-8.

Background

Residents of long-term care (LTC) homes commonly present with multiple physical and cognitive impairments that require ongoing support. In this review, a ‘LTC home’ refers to a permanent residential setting where individuals receive nursing care, such as nursing homes and skilled nursing facilities [1, 2]. Most residents are ‘older adults’ (i.e., people ≥ 65 years of age); with over half being 80 years of age or older [3]. Their complex and interrelated care needs are supported by multidisciplinary teams that typically include nursing, physical therapy (also referred to as physiotherapy), occupational therapy, speech language pathologists, recreation therapy, medicine, pharmacy, and personal support workers or nursing aids [4–6].

Physical therapy in LTC

Physical therapists play a central role in LTC homes by managing pain, mobility limitations, and the secondary effects of chronic illness [7–9]. Individualised and goal-directed physical exercise and rehabilitation programs, often designed in coordination with occupational therapists and speech language pathologists, have been shown to improve: (1) static and dynamic balance; (2) sit-to-stand ability; (3) muscle strength; (4) activities of daily living; and (5) cognitive function in older adults [10–15]. Physical therapy treatments also contribute to an overall reduction in disability, fall-related injuries, and hospital-related costs [16].

Despite the benefits, use of physical therapy services in LTC is low. Estimates suggest that between 64 and 89% of Canadian LTC residents, and 33–90% of residents internationally, do not access physical therapy services even when available [17, 18]. Funding and delivery models may explain this variation in use. For example, in the Netherlands, government funding supports widespread access in LTC resulting in a physical therapy usage rate for residents at greater than 60% [19, 20]. In contrast, Canadian LTC homes often outsource physical therapy to private contractors, leading to variability in service delivery [21]. When contracted service thresholds are exceeded, physical therapy rehabilitation activities may be delegated to non-physical therapy staff (e.g., nurses, personal support workers) [22]. However, LTC staff already manage wide-ranging responsibilities including health monitoring, medication administration, daily living assistance, and emotional support under chronic short-staffing conditions [23]. In fact, at least 58 000 additional nurses and personal support workers have been estimated to be needed in Ontario LTC homes by the year 2029 [24–27]. Delegation of physical rehabilitation activities to non-physical therapy staff also requires appropriate training, competence, and confidence, and must adhere to jurisdiction-specific legislation, professional standards, and organisational policies, further limiting the feasibility of this approach.

The Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic further disrupted physical rehabilitation in LTC. Infection-control measures, staffing restrictions, and workforce redeployment significantly reduced resident access to physical therapy services, with lasting implications for resident outcomes and system-level costs [28]. Collectively, these challenges underscore a pressing need to explore alternative approaches to maintain LTC resident rehabilitation services.

Family/care partners

Alongside health professionals, family caregivers, also known as family/care partners, play a critical role supporting resident well-being. A ‘family/care partner’ is defined as a person (e.g., a spouse, an adult relative, or friend of the care recipient) who provides unpaid care to a resident in LTC, and possesses a ‘caregiver identity,’ as described by Montgomery & Kosloski 2012, that is distinct from traditional family or friendly ties [29]. The contributions of family/care partners to resident care, such as providing companionship, assisting with activities of daily living, organising medical appointments, advocating for services to promote resident well-being, helping LTC staff manage behavioural expressions, have been found to reduce the incidence of hospitalisation, promote participation in daily activities, and preserve resident quality of life [30–33]. The COVID-19 pandemic underscored the importance of family care/partners when visitor restrictions prevented them from entering LTC homes, amid heightened burnout and job dissatisfaction reports among LTC staff [33, 34]. COVID-19 therefore, solidified family/care partners as a valuable and “invisible workforce” within LTC homes [34, 35].

Many family/care partners report feeling unrecognised and have a perception that they are treated as ‘peripheral’ members of the formal care team despite their self-reported desire to be involved in the care team [36]. A recent report suggested that the prevailing institutional culture in LTC frequently positions family/care partners as visitors rather than collaborators, leading to tokenistic rather than meaningful contributions to resident care [37]. Limited access to resident health records, difficulty understanding medical jargon of healthcare providers, reduced availability to LTC staff as a result of personal and professional obligations, cultural disparities, and lack of trust have been reported to hinder effective care coordination between staff and family/care partners [36, 38]. While interventions to improve staff–family interaction have shown promise (e.g., virtual care planning [39]), sustained integration of family/care partners into resident care teams remains a challenge across many healthcare settings [40]. Moreover, caregiver burden, a multidimensional concept describing the physical, emotional, social, and financial strain experienced by individuals who provide care to someone with chronic illness or disability [41], continues to be a significant concern in LTC settings [42]. It is unclear whether these challenges also apply to the dyad of a physical therapist and family/care partner.

Family/care partner-physical therapist engagement

An enhanced understanding of the physical therapist-family/care partner relationship could inform policies and practices on how to meaningfully involve family/care partners in the physical rehabilitation of LTC residents to reduce the burden on LTC staff while assisting the family/care partners in fulfilling their desire of involvement. Given that family/care partners often perceive opportunities for resident physical activity and rehabilitation as insufficient [43], engaging them alongside physical therapists may help increase the amount, and quality, of rehabilitation provided to residents in LTC. This involvement could support the overextended LTC staff, and reduce secondary impairments from chronic disease, thereby supporting quality of care for residents in LTC. In this review, ‘engagement’ is defined as collaboration and communication (e.g., oral, written, or behavioural) that directly supports the planning, delivery, or monitoring of physical rehabilitation for older adult LTC residents. Physical therapy benefits for older adult residents and family/care partner contributions are well documented separately, but understanding the intersection of these topics is limited.

The purpose of this scoping review was to summarise and assess the quality of the literature describing engagement of physical therapists with family/care partners in provision of rehabilitation for older adults living in LTC homes. The aims of this study were to: (1) describe the scope, diversity, and nature of engagement; (2) identify gaps and opportunities for further study; and (3) generate insights to inform practice, policy, and future research to enhance the overall quality of rehabilitation care provided in LTC homes.

Methods

A scoping review methodology was selected given the exploratory nature of the research question and study aims, the anticipated heterogeneity in study designs and outcomes, and the intent to identify gaps in the existing literature. The study protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework on July 21, 2025. Arksey & O’Malley’s framework for scoping reviews was used [44]: (1) determining the research question; (2) searching relevant databases to identify potential articles; (3) screening all abstracts/titles for relevance; (4) removing duplicates and searching the reference lists of relevant articles for any additional articles; (5) completing a full text review of eligible articles to determine inclusion; and (6) extracting all relevant data from selected articles. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist was used to guide reporting (see Appendix A) [45].

Research question

The following research question was asked:What is known about physical therapist-family/care partner engagement specific to physical rehabilitation for older adults in LTC?

What is known about physical therapist-family/care partner engagement specific to physical rehabilitation for older adults in LTC?

Search strategy

The search strategy was developed in consultation with a Research and Instructional University Librarian at Western University and was conducted on July 28, 2025. No limit was applied to the year of publication when the search was conducted. Computer-aided searches using database-specific search terms and syntax were completed in the following databases: MEDLINE (Ovid), PsychInfo (Ovid), CINAHL, Embase, Scopus, and the Nursing & Allied Health ProQuest collection. The search terms used were variations of: Physical Therapists OR Physiotherapists OR Physical Therapy Assistants OR Physiotherapy Assistants AND long-term care OR long-term care homes OR nursing homes OR homes for the aged OR long-term care facilities OR Assisted Living Facilities AND family OR caregivers OR friends OR adult children OR visitors to patients OR carers OR child of impaired parents OR family/care partners. All search terms were used as keywords in the databases that permitted keyword searches in addition to each database-specific subject heading. A forward and backward citation search of all papers selected for data extraction was completed using ‘citationchaser’ software [46].

A grey literature search was conducted using: (1) Google as an online search engine; (2) the Canadian Institute for Health Information external database; and (3) OpenAI Chat GPT artificial intelligence (AI) software [47]. Two phrases were entered into the search engine and database separately (‘physical therapist and family caregiver engagement in long-term care homes’; ‘family caregiver and physical therapist interaction in nursing homes’). The following prompt was entered into the AI software (‘provide a list of up to 50 non–peer-reviewed literature sources, such as reports, organisational publications, guidelines, dissertations, or grey literature, that discuss physical therapist and family engagement in long-term care homes’).

Evidence screening and selection

Papers were selected based on the following inclusion criteria: the (1) paper described engagement, as defined in the study, between physical therapists and family/care partners, as reported by any participant group (e.g., physical therapists, residents, LTC staff, family/care partners); (2) setting was a LTC home or equivalent; (3) focus of the paper was physical rehabilitation for older adults; and (4) papers were available in English. Any type of study design was eligible for inclusion. Studies were excluded if they were available solely as published abstracts without an accompanying full report.

All studies from the database and citation searches were uploaded into Covidence review management software (© 2025 Covidence) where duplicate records were removed. Two authors (AJS & DMC) independently screened the titles and abstracts yielded by the database search against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Studies deemed potentially eligible then underwent full-text review to confirm inclusion. For the grey literature search, the titles of the first 10 pages of results generated by the Google searches were screened. When a title suggested potential relevance, the full text of the source was retrieved and assessed against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. All results from the grey literature database were screened in a similar way and, if potentially eligible, reviewed in full text. All sources identified through the AI software were independently verified by cross-referencing titles and details using Google. In cases where the two authors provided conflicting screening decisions (e.g., one ‘include’ and one ‘exclude’), disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Reasons for paper exclusion during the full text review were recorded and reported in a PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1).

Data extraction, synthesis, and presentation

Data from included papers were extracted by two authors (AJS and DMC) using an a priori author-designed data extraction form which did not require revision during the review process (Appendix B). The data extraction form elucidated specific details from the included papers about the: study participants; study aim; study methods; key findings; and implications relevant to the aim of the current study. Extracted data was compiled in a summary table (Table 1). Review of the compiled summary table was completed with additions and modification of content between all three authors. The verified data were synthesised into research themes using an iterative, open coding process and accompanied by a narrative that connected the themes to the purpose of the study [48].

Quality appraisal

The quality assessment tool for disparate data by Hawker et al. was used to assess the quality of the included studies [58]. The tool consists of nine subscales: abstract and title, introduction and aims, method and data, sampling, data analysis, ethics and bias, results, transferability /generalisability, and implications and usefulness of the study. The items in each subscale were rated on a 4-point ordinal scale described as good [4], fair [3], poor [2], and very poor [1]. Each article; therefore, could receive a total score within a range of 9–36. A score of 28–36 is associated with good quality, 20–27 for fair, 10–20 with poor quality, and less than 10 for very poor quality. Quality ratings were independently assigned by two reviewers (AJS, DMC) and subsequently compared. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion until final ratings were assigned to each paper.

Results

Study selection

The database, citation and grey literature searches identified 3443 records eligible for inclusion after duplicates were removed. A total of 79 published white literature sources and 29 records from the grey literature search were reviewed in full text. Of these, nine white literature sources and zero grey literature sources met the inclusion criteria.

Study descriptions

The number of participants per study ranged from 10 to 114 [51, 57]. Six studies included care staff and management from the LTC home, six included physical therapists, three included LTC residents, and three included family/care partners. Four papers included information regarding participant gender, and three included ages of participants [49, 50, 54, 55]. Eight studies used qualitative research methods, and one study used mixed methods [51]. Three studies were conducted in the Netherlands, two in Canada, one in the United Kingdom, one in Japan, one in Norway, and one in Hong Kong. Table 1 contains a description of the included studies.

Two studies described types of nursing care and rehabilitation as they pertained to stroke rehabilitation and pain management for residents living in LTC [52, 53]. One study described the perceptions of the role of physical therapists who treat residents with dementia [57] and another described their daily practices and preferences while working in LTC homes [56]. van Dijk et al. aimed to explore the usage of the Powerless in Daily Living (PDL) care program to improve resident care and function [51]. Boer et al. explored the perceptions, needs and preferences for physical therapy care of LTC residents according to family/care partners [49]. Ohura et al. focused on the process of care goal setting for LTC residents by healthcare providers [50]. Lai et al. implemented an educational program and committee to determine its effectiveness at reducing restraint use in a private LTC home [54]. Trollebø et al. described facilitators and barriers to physical activity in LTC homes from the perspectives of healthcare professionals and family/care partners [55].

Themes surrounding family/care partner-physical therapist engagement

Engagement between physical therapists and family/care partners specific to LTC homes was synthesised into four interrelated themes: [1] physical therapists support greater family/care partner involvement in rehabilitation (n = 5); [2] enhancing role clarity and value of family/care partners in rehabilitation (n = 6); [3] opportunities to enhance structured communication between physical therapist and family/care partners (n = 8); and 4) misalignment of family/care partner expectations with the scope and role of physical therapy (n = 6). Figure 2 presents a proposed conceptual model illustrating these relationships, depicting a directional progression from physical therapist support for family/care partner involvement in rehabilitation, to role clarity and recognition of family/care partner value, to structured physical therapist-family/care partner communication, and ultimately to expectation alignment. While presented linearly, these themes are mutually reinforcing with clearer expectations regarding the scope and role of physical therapy in LTC feeding back to further enhance role clarity and the value that family/care partners can contribute to the physical rehabilitation of residents. This model represents an interpretive synthesis of the findings and is intended to provide a conceptual understanding of how these factors may interact, rather than a definitive or empirically tested framework.

Physical therapists support greater family/care partner involvement in rehabilitation

Physical therapists, family/care partners, and LTC staff described family/care partner input into goal setting, care planning, and rehabilitation activities as beneficial to residents. For example, family/care partners were described as “invaluable” for supporting pain assessment and advocating for timely intervention [53] and in Lai et al. they were referred to as essential and “an integral member of care” (p.8) [54]. Ohura et al. reported that the meaningful inclusion of family/care partners in the physical rehabilitation process fostered trust and gratitude among all involved parties, which strengthened care team motivation and awareness of resident progress [50]. One participant explained: “Although interacting with care providers and families of residents is difficult, when it goes well, for example, when I feel close to the family and when the families express gratitude, I feel that I become more aware of the level of achieving goals.” (p.15) [50]. Similarly, McCarroll et al. emphasised the importance of family involvement in supporting residents with dementia. One physical therapist noted: “We have to work with our other colleagues and our other carers and our relatives because otherwise the individual just doesn’t remember” (p.285) [57].

Enhancing role clarity and value of family/care partners in rehabilitation

Family/care partners described the role of physical therapists in LTC as limited or absent. Some reported being poorly informed about the role or activities of the physical therapist in the LTC home and “unaware that physical therapy was provided at all” (p.169) [49]. One participant explained: “I don’t see any physical therapists. Maybe there’s someone walking around in uniform, but I don’t know what their function is. I don’t know whether he or she belongs to the nursing staff or to physical therapy.” (p. 170) [49]. Ohura et al. noted that care goals were primarily shaped by residents’ conditions and prognosis; family/care partners were described as an “associated factor” rather than active participants in goal setting [50]. Feelings of exclusion arose in other contexts as well including a family/care partner who felt “like the fifth wheel on the wagon” (p.6) [55], and during transitions in stroke care [52].

Uncertainty about who is responsible for planning, delivering, and supporting resident physical rehabilitation was common. Family/care partners described not knowing how to best support residents in collaboration with physical therapists. One participant in Trollebø et al. said: “There must be proposals from them [physical therapists] because I have heard ‘Please do something’, but we don’t know what to do?” (p.6) [55]. Others admitted that they had little understanding of what physical therapists did in LTC, making it difficult for them to define their own role: “I don’t have enough understanding about physical therapy, because I do not know what they [the physical therapists] do.” (p.170) [49]. Many requested clear guidance and education from physical therapists, such as instruction on safe transfers and use of mobility equipment [49]. Stolee et al. cautioned that family/care partners might unintentionally over-assist residents, fostering dependence rather than independence, and highlighted the importance of proper training [52].

Physical therapists expressed mixed views regarding the role family/care partners should play in rehabilitation. Uncertainty centred around whether the provision of physical rehabilitation/activity outside of formal sessions should remain their responsibility or be delegated to family/care partners [56]. Sterke et al. described physical therapists encouraging family/care partners to supervise walking, or exercise, and providing advice on safe transfers and assistive devices [56]. However, Boer et al. noted that physical therapists were sometimes uncertain about whether their suggestions and recommendations were followed consistently and safely by family/care partners [49].

Opportunities to enhance structured communication between physical therapists and family/care partners

Physical therapist-family/care partner collaboration during care planning and treatment often depended on professional initiative, particularly in the absence of structured processes. In Trollebø et al. a family/care partner stated: “It must be signalled from the nursing home, where they want us to contribute.” (p.6) [55]. In Lai et al., family involvement in restraint reduction and safe mobility occurred when the Restraint Review Committee (which included a physical therapist) explicitly approached them [54]. In the PDL survey, institutes reported involving family/care partners when staff (including physical therapists) requested information about the resident (78–100%), sought consent to use PDL (61–67%), provided information (86–88%), or included them in care planning (42–54%) [51]. Sterke et al. described physical therapists initiating contact with family/care partners at resident admission and following up to determine whether a request for therapy support existed [56].

Information flow about rehabilitation goals, frequency, and progress was often described as insufficient or inconsistent. In Fox et al., both family/care partners and physical therapists identified this as a barrier with links to delayed pain management and untreated symptoms among residents [53]. Family/care partners interviewed by Boer et al. reported being unaware of whether residents received physical therapy on certain days and described “little to no communication with the physical therapist of the nursing home” (p.170) [49]. Another family/care partner echoed this, stating: “I don’t have prejudice against the physical therapists. In my experience they [the physical therapists] are all clear and empathic. But at the moment I do miss communication.” (p.170) [49]. In some instances, the perceived lack of updates was so severe that family/care partners removed residents from LTC to seek therapy at home [52].

Family/care partners expressed a desire for more structured ways to receive updates from physical therapists. In Boer et al., they requested opportunities to meet with physical therapists at regular care-plan meetings, and information in accessible and non-technical language [49]. Written updates were also suggested: “If I could read updates, for example a monthly update, in the digital patient file, that would be sufficient for me” (p.170) [49]. Physical therapists echoed the need for dialogue with family/care partners. For example, one noted the value of “having a meeting point, being present, being able to talk about patients together” (p.6) [55]. Ohura et al. reported that regular information sharing was viewed as critical for aligning goals; however, systematic processes to correspond with family/care partners specific to rehabilitation were rarely established [50]. When such processes were introduced, outcomes improved. For example, family/care partners involved in the PDL program reported greater satisfaction with resident care and more meaningful dialogue with physical therapists [51].

Misalignment of family expectations with the scope and role of physical therapy

Family/care partners often expected more from physical therapists than what was typically provided. This included calls for stronger leadership in care planning, as reflected by one participant in Boer et al.: “I would like direct involvement of the physical therapist, so that the physical therapist can discuss his observation immediately within the multidisciplinary team” (p.171) [49]. Another family/care partner expected physical therapists to be more visible within the LTC home: “In my opinion, a physical therapist should be present at every care-plan meeting” (p.170) [49].

At times, family/care partner expectations regarding plans and ongoing treatment conflicted with physical therapist recommendations. In Lai et al., some family/care partners resisted restraint reduction strategies recommended by physical therapists because they prioritised safety and fall prevention over resident mobility goals [54]. Others resisted taking on responsibilities themselves, expecting physical therapists to assume all aspects of resident rehabilitation [50]. Some family/care partners described their involvement as “unstable” and “unreliable,” which appeared to reflect the voluntary and non-obligatory nature of their participation [55]. One participant stated:: “I don’t think that family caregivers should have any designated duty towards the nursing home. You should visit your husband when you want to, not when you have to.” (p.6) [55].

Physical therapists noted that some family/care partners held expectations that exceeded what was feasible within the context of resident care. One physical therapist explained: “Relatives expect us to keep practicing endlessly, but physiotherapy trajectories always have a beginning and an end. At some point, it is no longer physiotherapy but support with daily activities” (p.6) [56]. Another added: “Sometimes families think that we will keep walking with their father or mother every day. But that is not realistic — that belongs with the nursing staff, not physiotherapy” (p.7) [56]. When some physical therapists did not meet family/care partner expectations, they were left feeling discouraged: “You kind of just have one of those moments where you are like oh my goodness this is really, really difficult; this is a horrible thing that the family and the patient are going through and it’s quite hard for, well it is for me, to see someone like that and actually I can’t help and I’m sorry I can’t help; that can be quite difficult” (p.285) [57].

Discussion

The purpose of this scoping review was to synthesise, summarise and assess the quality of the literature describing engagement of physical therapists with family/care partners in provision of rehabilitation for older adults in LTC homes. A comprehensive search of six databases and grey literature sources yielded nine papers meeting inclusion criteria, highlighting the relatively underdeveloped nature of this area of research. Four independent yet interrelated themes surfaced from the included papers: [1] physical therapists support greater family/care partner involvement in rehabilitation; [2] opportunities to enhance structured communication between physical therapists and family/care partners; 3) misalignment of family/care partner expectations with the scope and role of physical therapy; and 4) enhancing role clarity and value of family/care partners in rehabilitation. Addressing these gaps could enable family/care partners to play a more meaningful role, alongside physical therapists, in supporting rehabilitation for LTC residents.

Strengthening family/care partner involvement

A notable finding of this review is a disconnect between the perceived importance of physical therapist-family/care partner engagement according to physical therapists and family/care partners, and the realities of daily practice. For the most part, physical therapists working in LTC welcome and value assistance from family/care partners. These findings align with broader evidence demonstrating family/care partner substantial contributions to resident care [35] and further indicate that these contributions are recognised within LTC physical therapy practice. Yet, many family/care partners report feeling excluded or marginalised in their interactions with physical therapists, reinforcing trends outlined in the introduction that point to persistent gaps in inclusion despite their recognised contributions [36, 37]. Collectively, these findings suggest that existing resources, programs, and policies intended to support family/care partner involvement in physical rehabilitation are insufficient, inconsistently implemented, or absent. At the clinical level, physical therapists should continue formal and informal communication with family/care partners clearly acknowledging the value of their contributions and identifying practical opportunities for involvement in resident rehabilitation. This may take the form of brief, opportunistic conversations during visits, inclusion in goal-setting and care planning, hands-on demonstration of exercises, and clear, individualised guidance on how family/care partners can safely support movement during care provision interactions.

At the organisational level, LTC management must support these care contributions by reducing barriers to engagement. Physical therapists who operate independently from LTC home teams may face greater challenges initiating and sustaining collaboration with family/care partners, as in-person or routine proximate interactions within daily workflows, communication structures, and interdisciplinary care processes limit opportunities to establish relationships and engage family/care partners consistently. Emerging evidence suggests that structured, multi-point engagement strategies may be effective. For example, a recent pilot program demonstrated that introducing engagement opportunities prior to admission, during admission, and at family conferences was perceived by both staff and family/care partners as beneficial [59]. Family/care partners already participating in the program were also identified as potential peer resources for new families [59]. Formal recognition strategies, such as role clarification, structured training, or incentives (e.g., awards or tax benefits), may further support sustained involvement. These approaches must be implemented with caution, as family/care partners already experience considerable emotional and logistical strain; efforts to enhance engagement should aim to support, rather than add to, their caregiving burden.

Enhancing communication between physical therapists and family/care partners

Effective communication between staff working within LTC homes is a well-documented precursor to constructive relationships, person-centred care, and informed decision-making aimed at preserving and improving resident health and well-being [60–62]. The need for improved communication, a main theme from this study, is not exclusive to the family/care partner-physical therapist dyad in LTC. For example, a recent scoping review of informal caregivers of people with dementia in LTC also identified communication shortcomings between family care/partners with staff as a main theme [63]. Similarly, Whitlatch described how care teams comprised of a triad formed by a family member, resident, and staff member within LTC have regularly reported a lack of communication and a high rate of misunderstandings about a resident’s diagnosis, prognosis, care decision-making and plan of care follow-up [64]. The communication challenges described as a theme in this scoping review addressing family/care partners and physical therapists mirror findings from across LTC settings [65], suggesting that these issues reflect broader limitations in how collaboration is structured and supported among these care providers.

The establishment of an evidence-based pragmatic framework to promote and guide communication between physical therapists and family/care partners may facilitate better treatment outcomes for LTC residents. Such a framework would enable family/care partners and physical therapists to more easily report health status changes that inform rehabilitation programming and follow-up and provide contextual knowledge about a resident’s behaviour or personality that strengthens therapeutic relationships. A recent synthesis of common staff-family communication methods in LTC homes suggests that future frameworks should incorporate staff professional development and education, technology training and support, and easy accessibility of information for family/care partners [65]. To be effective, frameworks must also be flexible enough to accommodate the realities of family life including limited time, competing responsibilities, and other practical barriers, so that even busy family/care partners can engage meaningfully in supporting resident rehabilitation.

Defining roles and expectations in LTC rehabilitation

Both family/care partners and physical therapists described uncertainty regarding their respective roles in supporting physical rehabilitation for LTC residents. This aligns with prior research documenting a broad lack of clarity regarding what family care/partners can or should do to support residents in LTC [66]. This review found that some family/care partners are willing to assume responsibilities traditionally held by physical therapists - a trend reported previously [67] - and that some believe they should not have any designated role(s) in rehabilitation. Further, there is limited understanding of what family/care partners currently do in practice to support resident rehabilitation and whether such tasks align with physical therapy best practices. It is equally unclear what roles family/care partners feel comfortable assuming in the context of resident rehabilitation and where they prefer to defer to professional expertise. In the absence of this knowledge, physical therapists may underestimate family willingness or capability, assuming family/care partners cannot or will not contribute when in fact many are both willing and able. This gap in understanding represents a key barrier to effective partnership and engagement.

To address this gap, clear, evidence-based guidelines should be developed collaboratively by physical therapists, family/care partners, and LTC administrators to delineate possible family roles in rehabilitation. For example, beyond assisting with daily activities, family/care partners could be trained by physical therapists and assistants to guide residents through safe joint mobility exercises for pain relief. In some jurisdictions, such as Ontario, Canada, physical therapists may even delegate certain controlled acts (e.g., wound care, oxygen administration, and tracheal suctioning) to family/care partners under appropriate supervision [68]. Any such guidelines must be flexible to reflect limits in family/care partner capacity. Such limits might include time, geographical restrictions, competing responsibilities, financial constraints, or their own physical and/or cognitive impairments. With clear communication and collaboration, adequate supervision and instruction, and well-defined roles, family care/partners could contribute more directly and confidently to rehabilitation, improving resident health outcomes, supporting patient-centred care, and sustaining LTC rehabilitation services.

Limitations

There are some methodological factors which may have influenced the results of this review. Limiting the literature to English-only content resulted in the exclusion of at least three known papers written in German, Japanese and Korean that may have provided relevant information regarding the review’s aim. Most included studies originated from high-income countries which may limit the applicability of findings to lower-resource LTC contexts with different staffing models and cultural expectations. Further, the findings from studies completed in high income countries may not be representative of all payment and reimbursement models, as considerable variability exists across jurisdictions in funding structures and care delivery approaches. The use of a proximity-based search strategy may have contributed to the low yield of included studies (n = 9), as relevant studies discussing these groups within the same paper but in separate sections may have been excluded. Lastly, we did not consult with key stakeholders (e.g., family/care partners, administrators or LTC staff physical therapists), regarding the assembly of our data extraction form, nor did we consult with them during our data analysis, which may have provided us with additional interpretations of the findings from this review [69].

Future research

The limited studies describing family/care partner-physical therapist engagement within LTC homes highlights the need for deeper exploration into how this relationship shapes resident outcomes and rehabilitation care delivery. Notably, most included studies examined physical therapists and family/care partners separately, with very few directly exploring their interactions while working with the same resident. Further research exploring what is needed from both family/care partners and physical therapists to foster partnerships in provision of rehabilitation is needed. Specifically, research investigating how physical therapists can best engage with family/care-partners from the perspective of the family/ care partners will support initiatives to collaborate in LTC (i.e., identifying what is realistic to decrease their burden as a caregiver while achieving their personal desires to assist their loved one(s)). Clarifying the specific actions and methods family/care partners use to support resident rehabilitation and examining how these contributions align with established physical therapy practices would help define and strengthen their potential roles. Further, a review including other contributors to physical activity and rehabilitation, such as kinesiologists, personal trainers, occupational therapists, speech language pathologists, personal support workers and nurses, would be beneficial to understand engagement of family/care partners with LTC staff towards building rehabilitation capacity for the well-being of older adults living in LTC homes.

Conclusion

This review establishes a foundational understanding of engagement between physical therapists and family/care partners in LTC homes as it relates to rehabilitation. While family/care partner contributions are valued, engagement remains inconsistent, expectations are misaligned, and roles are poorly defined. These findings underscore that this area is underexplored and point to the urgent need for future strategic research and policy attention. Engagement between physical therapists and family/care partners represents an underutilised pathway for potentially building capacity in LTC rehabilitation; however, the current evidence primarily characterises the barriers and nature of this relationship rather than demonstrating measurable increases in care capacity. As such, this review provides a starting point for advancing a new research agenda focused on building practical, evidence-informed strategies to support meaningful and sustainable family–physical therapist partnerships in rehabilitation for older adults living in LTC.
