STING deficiency does not rescue short telomere-mediated aging phenotypes and longevity in TERC- or TERT-telomerase deficient mice
Authors: Rosa M Marión, José Carlos González, Juana M Flores, Elena Piñeiro-Yáñez, Rosa Serrano, Maria A Blasco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway plays a key role in responding to viral genomes and endogenous DNA byproducts by inducing inflammatory pathways. Its roles in senescence and in the response to telomere shortening have also recently been proposed. To address its role in age-related pathologies and decreased longevity associated with short telomeres, we generated double-mutant mice deficient for STING and either the telomerase RNA component (TERC) or the telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) component. In both telomerase-deficient mouse cohorts, STING deficiency did not rescue any major phenotypes, including decreased body weight, infertility, multiple degenerative pathologies and, importantly, the progressively decreased maximum and median lifespan of increasing generations of Terc- or Tert-deficient mice. These findings indicate that STING does not mediate aging phenotypes associated with short telomeres in mammals. Furthermore, they have potential relevance for therapeutic strategies based on STING inhibition in short-telomere-associated age-related diseases in mammalian organisms.Keywords: telomeres, telomerase, aging, inflammation, stimulator of interferon genes, STING

Introduction

Telomeres protect chromosome ends from being recognized as DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), preventing persistent DNA damage response (DDR) activation, end-to-end chromosome fusions, and other telomeric aberrations1,2 which lead to genomic instability. A telomerase component, telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), elongates telomeres de novo by copying an associated RNA component known as the telomerase RNA component (TERC).3 Although active at pluripotency stages, telomerase is silenced in most tissues after birth, leading to telomere shortening with age due to increasing rounds of cell division4,5,6,7 to sustain growth, tissue repair, and organismal homeostasis. Once the telomeres reach a critically short length, they limit tissue regeneration by impairing stem cell mobilization and inducing cell arrest or apoptosis.8 Telomere “uncapping” triggers persistent DDR activation involving different DNA damage signaling and repair pathways,9,10,11 ultimately causing chromosomal rearrangements including extrachromosomal DNA and micronuclei, which can be detected by the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) innate immunity DNA sensor pathway.12

The cGAS-STING pathway is an ancient mechanism activated by cytosolic double-stranded DNA (dsDNA).13 cGAS detects viral and endogenous cytoplasmic DNA.13 However, excessive cGAS-STING signaling can promote chronic inflammation and diseases.14 Upon dsDNA binding, cGAS generates the secondary messenger cGAMP,15 which activates STING.16 Downstream signaling induces nuclear translocation of IRF3 and nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB),17,18 inducing the transcription of type I interferons (IFNs), interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), inflammatory cytokines, and other targets.19 Overall, activation of the cGAS-STING axis contributes to sterile inflammation and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP).20

In addition to infection-related cytosolic DNA of microbial origins, cGAS is also activated by various forms of endogenous self-DNA that accumulates upon defective DNA replication, repair and mitosis, mitochondrial damage or dysfunction, cell death, tumoral cells, reduced cytoplasmic nucleases, de-repression of transposable elements, or defects in autophagy.21

Recent studies have proposed that cGAS-STING promotes senescence and inflammatory regulation in senescent cells. cGAS-STING has been shown to be required for cellular senescence induced by DNA damage, oxidative stress, or oncogene activation20,22 and to contribute to age-related chronic inflammation through SASP following senescence-associated events, such as loss of nuclear integrity,23 downregulation of DNA-clearing enzymes,24 de-repression of endogenous retroviral elements,25,26 or mitochondrial dysfunction.27,28 Indeed, STING inhibition in aged mice reduces chronic inflammation and preserves healthy lifespan.27 Recently, a non-canonical cGAS-STING pathway was shown to drive organismal aging in the progeria mouse model.29 These findings have supported STING as a therapeutic target for progeroid and age-related diseases. However, numerous studies have described a central role of STING in maintaining organismal homeostasis, including its requirement for intestinal homeostasis and inflammation control,30,31,32,33 as well as its role in metabolism and bone homeostasis.34,35,36 Additionally, microglial STING protects mice from neurological decline associated with age.37 These studies underscore that using STING antagonists for aging and disease may have detrimental effects.

In the context of cancer, genomic instability as a consequence of either tumorigenesis or cancer treatments also generates abnormal dsDNA fragments that can activate a cGAS-STING response.38 In fact, mouse studies have provided evidence supporting the role of STING in initiating anti-cancer immune responses, leading to the use of STING stimulation as cancer therapy.39

Several reports have suggested that telomeres may be a major player in the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway. In particular, the so-called extrachromosomal telomeric repeats (ECTRs) released into the cytoplasm can be recognized by cGAS.40,41 ECTRs can be generated by t-loop excision or by homology recombination at telomeres, resulting in free circular configurated structures,42,43,44 which are normally present in cells that maintain telomeres by recombination or alternative lengthening of telomeres (ALT) cells.45 In addition, the cGAS-STING pathway is found muted in ALT-proficient tumor cells, thereby potentially evading the ECTR-induced antiproliferative effect and enabling ALT development.41

In addition, critically short or dysfunctional telomeres are known to lead to several of the scenarios that activate cGAS-STING.12,46 Thus, cGAS-STING activation has been shown upon induction of telomere dysfunction in response to telomeric DSBs,47 treatment with the telomere targeting drug (6-thio-dG),48 or uncapping telomeres by TRF2 depletion.47,49 Finally, cGAS activation is also associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and mtDNA leakage, which have been associated with short telomeres and aging in telomerase-deficient mice46 or aged mice.50

Together, these findings raise the mechanistic hypothesis that cGAS-STING mediates phenotypes caused by short or dysfunctional telomeres, thereby influencing cancer and aging. Here, we tested whether the aging-related pathologies of telomerase-deficient mice are mediated by cGAS-STING activation. The only report that has addressed this question was recently published.51 The authors claim that the cGAS-STING axis is responsible for the aging phenotypes of telomerase-deficient zebrafish, as STING deficiency in Tert-deficient zebrafish shows rescue of SASP, fertility, body weight, and, more importantly, lifespan.

Here, we report that none of these findings are valid in the context of mammalian organisms, such as mice. We generated two independent telomerase-deficient mouse colonies lacking either the TERC or the TERT in a STING-proficient or STING-deficient background. In both cohorts, STING deficiency failed to rescue body weight, a plethora of degenerative phenotypes associated with short telomeres, or mouse longevity. Beyond indicating that the role of STING in mediating telomere phenotypes is not conserved in mammals, these findings could be relevant when considering therapeutic strategies based on STING inhibition for age-related diseases associated with short or dysfunctional telomeres.

Results

Absence of STING does not rescue decreased survival in Terc-deficient mice

To address the role of STING in mediating short telomere-induced aging phenotypes, mice deficient for the telomerase RNA component or Terc, referred to as Terc−/− mice, were crossed with mice carrying an allele of Sting (also referred to as Sting1 and Tmem173) harboring a missense mutation in exon 6, which results in an isoleucine-to-asparagine change in amino acid 199 and prevents protein production.52 Hereafter, and in accordance with its commercial designation, we will refer to this Sting-mutant allele as Stinggt/gt. Then, we intercrossed heterozygous Terc+/− mice with Stinggt/gt mice to generate first-generation double-mutant G1 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt mice, which were then intercrossed until fourth-generation (G4) Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt mice.

First, we measured telomere length in peripheral blood leukocytes from single-mutant and double-mutant mice at 45 weeks of age. As expected, single G3 Terc-deficient mice, referred to as G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+, both males and females, had significantly shorter telomeres than wild-type mice (Figure 1A). STING deficiency per se in Terc+/+/Stinggt/gt mice did not result in shorter telomeres in either males or females (Figure 1A). Accordingly, STING deficiency did not significantly modify telomere length in the context of G3 telomerase-deficient Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt mice, indicating that STING does not affect the telomere length.

As telomerase deficiency leads to smaller mouse size with increasing mouse generations, we next determined body weight in the different mouse cohorts. As expected, third- (G3) and fourth (G4)-generation telomerase-deficient G3–G4 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ mice, both males and females, showed a marked decrease in body weight (Figure 1B). Male mice mutant for STING only but wild-type for Terc, referred to as Terc+/+/Stinggt/gt mice, showed a decreased body weight compared with wild-type control males (Figure 1B). Interestingly, STING deficiency did not rescue the low weight phenotype in double-mutant G3–G4 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt mice (Figure 1B), demonstrating that STING does not play a role in the dwarf phenotype associated with short telomeres in Terc-deficient mice.

One of the most notable phenotypes of telomerase-deficient mice is reduced viability, which aggravates with increasing mouse generations.53,54 As expected, telomerase-deficient G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ mice, both males and females, showed a marked decrease in survival compared with wild-type controls, which was further aggravated in the G4 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ mice (Figure 1C). Female mice mutant for STING but wild-type for telomerase, referred to as Terc+/+/Stinggt/gt mice, showed reduced survival, although many mice from the Terc+/+ groups were still alive (Figure 1C). Importantly, STING deficiency did not rescue the decreased longevity in double-mutant G3–G4 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt mice, demonstrating that STING does not play a major role in mediating reduced longevity due to Terc deficiency.

Absence of STING does not rescue degenerative phenotypes in Terc-deficient mice

To further study the role of STING in degenerative pathologies associated with telomerase deficiency, we performed a comprehensive phenotypic analysis of the different mouse cohorts sacrificed at 45 weeks of age.

Peripheral blood leukocyte composition showed a decrease in lymphocyte numbers and an increase in neutrophils in G3 Terc-deficient mice, phenotypes that were not rescued by STING deficiency (Figure S1). We also observed an increase in red blood cells and hemoglobin levels associated with STING deficiency but independent of telomerase deficiency and telomere length in males (Figure S1). In summary, we did not see improvement in TERC-deficiency-associated blood counts following STING abrogation, suggesting that STING does not mediate these phenotypes.

Next, dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) analyses confirmed reduced fat content in both male and female single G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ telomerase-deficient mice that was not rescued by STING deficiency in G3 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt double-mutant mice (Figures S2A and S2B), consistent with the observed body weight (Figure S2C). We observed a decreased bone mineral density in female single G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ telomerase-deficient mice that was not rescued by STING deficiency in G3 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt double-mutant mice (Figure S2B). STING deficiency in the context of wild-type Terc in Terc+/+/Stinggt/gt did not show any alterations in these parameters. Finally, we did not see any significant changes in brain weight at this age in any of the phenotypes (Figure S2C).

Next, we performed a full histopathological analysis of different tissues. Due to the important role of STING in innate immunity in the liver,55 we analyzed the liver in the different mouse cohorts. We did not see any impact of STING deficiency on the severity of different liver degenerative pathologies including liver steatosis in the context of the G3 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt mice (Figures S3A and S3B). We next assessed liver inflammation across the different mouse cohorts. Telomerase deficiency has been reported to directly alter the macrophage compartment and contribute to a chronic senescent and pro-inflammatory milieu.56,57,58 In particular, short telomeres in Terc-deficient mice impair macrophage mitochondrial integrity, promoting inflammasome hyperactivation and leading to inflammatory macrophages.57 We, therefore, used F480 to assess macrophage-associated inflammation in our mouse cohorts. Interestingly, telomerase-deficient G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ males and females showed increased inflammation in the liver, as determined by the immunohistochemical analysis with the macrophage marker F480 (Figure S3C). STING deficiency in the double-mutant G3 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt mice, however, did not rescue the increased inflammation associated with Terc deficiency (Figure S3C).

Skin phenotypes, such as thinning of the skin layers and loss of subcutaneous fat, have been associated with telomerase deficiency and short telomeres.8 These pathologies include epidermal and piloannexal atrophies. We have classified these skin pathologies as severe, medium, mild, or healthy (see STAR Methods for details). Both male and female G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ mice showed an increased skin degenerative pathologies compared with wild-type mice; however, STING deficiency in the double-mutant G3 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt mice did not rescue these skin pathologies (Figures 2A and 2B). Instead, we observed a trend toward an increased incidence of these pathologies in the absence of STING (Figure 2B).

Interestingly, both male and female G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ telomerase-deficient mice showed a thinning of the subcutaneous fat layer, which again was not rescued by STING deficiency in the double-mutant G3 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt mice (Figure 2C).

Depletion of seminiferous tubules and reduced testis size are characteristic phenotypes associated with Terc deficiency in mice.59 As expected, we found increased degenerative pathologies in the testis of G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ telomerase-deficient mice (Figure S4A). These pathologies included hypospermatogenesis, maturation arrest, and Sertoli cell-only syndrome (for severity grading, see STAR Methods). Again, these degenerative pathologies were not rescued by STING deficiency in the double-mutant G3 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt mice (Figure S4A).

Absence of STING does not rescue loss of spleen architecture and loss of cellularity in the spleens of Terc-deficient mice

A hallmark phenotype of Terc-deficient mice is the loss of the spleen architecture and spleen cell populations.60,61 Thus, we performed a full histopathological analysis of the spleen of the different mouse cohorts. Degenerative alterations, including loss of splenic architecture and cellular imbalance, were classified as severe, mild, or absent (healthy) according to their severity (STAR Methods). As expected, late-generation G3 male and female Terc−/−/Sting+/+ mice showed more severe spleen pathology (Figure S4B) which was not rescued by STING deficiency in the double-mutant G3 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt mice. Next, we studied different spleen cell populations by performing immunohistochemistry with the B220/CD45RR marker of the B cell lineage (magenta in Figure 2D) and with the CD3 marker of the T cell lineage (teal in Figure 2D) in the red pulp (RP) region of the spleen in both males and females. We found a significant decrease in the B cell lineage in G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ telomerase-deficient males and females that again was not rescued by STING deficiency in the double-mutant G3 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt mice (Figure 2E).

Absence of STING does not rescue loss of bone marrow architecture of Terc-deficient mice

Bone marrow aplasia is linked to critically short telomeres and telomerase deficiency in humans and mice.59,62 We stained bone marrow with myeloperoxidase (MPO), a marker of the granulocytic myeloid lineage (Figure S5A) and TER119, a marker of erythroid lineage (Figure S5A). Quantification of the staining showed a significant reduction of the TER119-positive cells in G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+-deficient mice (Figure S5B), which was not rescued by STING deficiency in the G3 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt double-mutant mice (Figure S5B).

Absence of STING does not rescue small and large intestinal atrophy of Terc-deficient mice

A hallmark phenotype of Terc-deficient mice is the presence of degenerative pathologies in the small and large intestines.54,63 Thus, we performed a full histopathological analysis of the small and large intestines of the different mouse cohorts. Degenerative pathologies included shortening of villi, epithelial and glandular atrophy, inflammatory reaction, areas in the mucosa with few glands, glandular cystic hyperplasia, ulcerated areas, and glandular hyperplasia that narrows the intestinal lumen, and these were classified as severe, medium, mild, and healthy according to their severity (STAR Methods). As expected, late-generation G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ mice showed increased number and severity of small and large intestinal pathologies (Figure 3A; Figure S6A). STING deficiency in double-mutant G3 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt mice, however, did not rescue the severity of the pathologies (Figure 3A; Figure S6A).

Short telomeres result in increased DNA damage in the small intestine.64 Thus, we determined DNA damage in the different mouse cohorts in the small and large intestines, using the DNA damage marker γH2AX. As expected, late-generation G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ mice showed an increased number of cells positive for the DNA damage marker γH2AX (Figure 3B; Figure S6B). STING deficiency in double-mutant G3 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt mice, however, did not rescue the abundance of γH2AX-positive cells (Figure 3B; Figure S6B).

We determined inflammation in the small and large intestines in the different mouse cohorts, as telomerase deficiency and short telomeres are associated with increased inflammation.54 To this end, we used F480 as an inflammation marker. Late-generation G3 Terc/-/Sting+/+ mice showed an increased percentage of areas positive for F480 compared with the wild-type or single STING-mutant controls, indicating the presence of inflammation in both the small and large intestines (Figure 3C; Figure S6C). STING deficiency in double-mutant G3 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt mice, however, did not rescue F480 positivity (Figure 3C; Figure S6C).

Finally, we measured proliferation in the small intestine by using the proliferation marker Ki67. As expected, late-generation G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ mice showed fewer Ki67-positive cells (Figure 3D). STING deficiency in double-mutant G3 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt mice, however, did not rescue the defect in proliferation (Figure 3D).

Gene expression pattern in the small intestine of Terc-deficient mice upon STING depletion

To determine whether genome-wide expression programs induced by short telomeres are dependent on STING, we directly analyzed the cellular transcriptome by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) (STAR Methods) in the small intestine of Terc+/+/Sting+/+, Terc+/+/Stinggt/gt, G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+, and G3 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt mouse cohorts (3–5 animals per genotype). First, we examined the significantly differentially expressed genes (DEG) between the different genotypes. When comparing Terc+/+/Sting+/+ and Terc+/+/Stinggt/gt small intestines, no significant DEGs were identified, indicating that STING deficiency in the context of telomerase-proficient mice does not impact global gene expression in the small intestine.

To determine whether short telomere-associated gene expression changes depend on STING, we compared G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ with Terc+/+/Sting+/+ wild-type controls (Figure 4A, left) and G3Terc−/−/Sting gt/gt with Terc+/+/Sting gt/gt mice (Figure 4A, right). EnrichR analysis of upregulated genes showed similar significantly enriched WikiPathways in both G3 telomerase-deficient cohorts, independent of STING (Figure 4A). These included p53 signaling and inflammatory pathways, previously known to be upregulated in late-generation Terc-deficient mice,12,65,66 indicating that the absence of STING does not globally alter the gene expression programs activated by Terc deficiency. Consistently, no significant DEGs were found between G3 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt and G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ mice, indicating that the short telomere-induced transcriptional programs in Terc-null mice are largely independent of cGAS-STING.

Consistent with this, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ to Terc+/+/Sting+/+ and G3 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt to Terc+/+/Stinggt/gt comparisons showed similar expression of hallmark inflammatory response genes, among others, regardless of STING (Figure S7A), indicating that global induction of the inflammatory response derived from the presence of short telomeres does not require activation of the STING pathway. However, GSEA of G3 Terc−/−Stinggt/gt to G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ comparison revealed a reduced expression of viral defense genes in telomerase-null mice lacking STING (Figure S7B), consistent with the role of the cGAS-STING axis in this response.67 Thus, although cGAS-STING contributes to inflammatory viral-response genes in Terc-null mice, the global inflammatory response to short telomeres is preserved without STING.

p53 pathway is activated in the absence of STING in Terc-deficient mice

STING has been reported to be required for p53 pathway activation and senescence caused by short telomeres in telomerase-null zebrafish.51 Interestingly, our small intestine transcriptome analysis revealed that the p53 pathway was significantly enriched in both G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ and G3 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt cohorts, regardless of STING (Figure 4A). GSEA confirmed similar expression of the p53 pathway genes in G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ to Terc+/+/Sting+/+ and G3 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt to Terc+/+/Stinggt/gt comparisons, independent of STING (Figure S7C). To further confirm the activation of the p53 pathway in Terc-deficient mice, we measured p53 levels in the small intestine. We observed increased levels of p53 in G3 Terc-deficient mice compared with wild-type mice, independent of the presence of STING (Figure 4B). As a readout of p53 pathway activation, we measured p21 and found similarly increased p21-positive cells in G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ and G3 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt mice (Figure 4C). Thus, unlike in zebrafish, STING is not required for p53 expression or pathway activation in response to short telomeres in mice.

STING is required for cellular senescence due to short telomeres in telomerase-null zebrafish.51 To assess senescence, we measured p16 in the small intestine. p16 was not detected in wild-type or Terc+/+/Stinggt/gt mice (Figure 4D), whereas both G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ and G3 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt mice showed similar p16 expression patterns, with most animals showing patchy and mild expression, while some mice showed strong patchy expression (Figure 4D). These results indicated that STING is not required for short telomere-induced senescence in mice.

STING pathway is not acutely activated in Terc-deficient mice

We asked whether STING signaling is not activated by short telomeres in Terc-null mice or, alternatively, is activated but dispensable for pathology. We first measured STING pathway components in the small intestine and found no increased expression of TBK1, pTBK1, IRF3, or pIRF3 in G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ mice (Figure 4E), suggesting that the STING pathway is not acutely activated. We then measured the expression of selected ISGs by qPCR. In agreement with RNA-seq data (Figure S7B), some ISGs genes were induced in telomerase-null mice in a STING-dependent manner (Figure S7D). However, most canonical STING-responsive genes were not induced in G3 Terc−/−/Sting+/+ mice (Figure S7D), indicating that there is not a robust STING-dependent transcriptional response to short telomeres. Altogether, these results indicated that STING is not acutely activated in Terc-null mice.

Absence of STING does not rescue decreased survival in TERT-deficient mice

As some reports suggest differential roles of TERC and TERT in certain non-canonical cellular functions such as gene expression, mitochondrial protection, and inflammation68,69,70 we next verified a potential role of STING in mediating short telomere-induced aging phenotypes in mice deficient for TERT. To this end, Tert−/− mice71 were crossed with Stinggt/gt-mutant mice. Then, we intercrossed heterozygous Tert +/− mice with Stinggt/gt mice to generate first-generation double-mutant G1 Tert−/−/Stinggt/gt mice, which were then intercrossed until G4 Tert−/−/Stinggt/gt mice.

First, we determined body weight in the different mouse cohorts. As expected, G4 telomerase-deficient male and female Tert−/−/Sting+/+ mice showed a marked decrease in body weight (Figure 5A). Interestingly, STING deficiency did not rescue the low body weight phenotype of double-mutant G4 Tert−/−/Stinggt/gt mice (Figure 5A), demonstrating that STING does not play a role in the dwarf phenotype associated with short telomeres in Tert-deficient mice.

Next, we studied mouse viability by following the different mouse cohorts throughout their lifespans until the humane endpoint. As expected, G4 male and female Tert−/−/Sting+/+ mice showed a marked decrease in survival compared with wild-type controls (Figure 5B). Importantly, STING deficiency did not rescue the reduced longevity of G4 Tert-deficient mice in double-mutant G4 Tert−/−/Stinggt/gt mice, demonstrating that STING does not play a major role in mediating decreased longevity owing to TERT deficiency.

Absence of STING does not rescue small and large intestinal atrophy in TERT-deficient mice

As intestinal atrophy is a major phenotype of Tert-deficient mice, we performed a full histopathological analysis of the small and large intestines from the different mouse cohorts. Degenerative pathologies included the shortening of villi, epithelial and glandular atrophy, inflammatory reaction, areas in the mucosa with few glands, glandular cystic hyperplasia, ulcerated areas, and glandular hyperplasia that narrows the intestinal lumen, and these were classified as severe, mild, and healthy according to their severity (STAR Methods). As expected, late-generation G4 Tert−/−/Sting+/+ mice showed an increased number and severity of intestinal pathologies in the small and large intestines (Figures 6A and 6B). STING deficiency in double-mutant G4 Tert−/−/Stinggt/gt mice, however, did not rescue the severity of the pathologies (Figures 6A and 6B).

Next, we determined DNA damage in both the small and large intestines by using the DNA damage marker γH2AX. Late-generation Tert−/−/Sting+/+ mice showed an increased number of γH2AX-positive cells compared with the Tert wild-type controls both in the small and large intestines (Figures 6C and 6D). Again, STING deficiency did not rescue the presence of DNA damage.

Finally, we determined cell proliferation in the large intestine by using the Ki67 marker. Late-generation G4 Tert−/−/Sting+/+ showed a tendency toward lower proliferation compared with Tert wild-type controls (Figure 6E) that was not rescued in G4 Tert−/−/Stinggt/gt mice.

p53 pathway activation is independent of STING in TERT-deficient mice

Deletion of STING abolishes the expression of p53 and cell senescence due to short telomeres in telomerase-null zebrafish.51 To determine whether the activation of p53 pathway in the TERT-deficient mice is dependent on STING, we measured p53 expression in the small intestine of our mouse cohorts (Figure 7A). We found that the p53 levels in G4 Tert−/−/Stinggt/gt mice were similar to those in G4 Tert−/−/Sting+/+ mice, indicating that the deletion of STING does not alter p53 expression in Tert-null mice, which is in accordance with our results in the Terc-null mouse model (Figure 4B).

To confirm p53-pathway activation in response to short telomeres in the Tert-deficient mice, we measured p21 in the small intestine in our mouse cohorts. We found that the p21 levels were increased in both G4 Tert−/−/Stinggt/gt and G4 Tert−/−/Sting+/+ mice compared with wild-type mice (Figure 7B), indicating that short telomeres in the Tert-deficient mice activate the p53 pathway independent of STING. Next, we measured p16 in the small intestine in our mouse cohorts. We observed no p16 expression in any of the mouse cohorts, regardless of STING (Figure 7C). These results indicated that p53 pathway activation in TERT-deficient mice is independent of STING.

STING pathway is not activated in TERT-deficient mice

Finally, we determined whether the STING pathway is activated in Tert-deficient mice. We first measured STING pathway components in the small intestine of our mouse cohorts. As in the Terc-null model, we observed no increased expression of TBK1, pTBK1, IRF3, or pIRF3 in G4 Tert−/−/Sting+/+ mice compared with wild-type animals (Figures 7D and 7E). Then, we measured the expression of some ISGs by qPCR and observed no increased expression in G4 Tert−/−/Sting+/+ mice compared with wild-type animals (Figure 7F). Thus, these results indicated that the STING pathway is not activated in response to short telomeres in Tert-deficient mice.

Discussion

Recent findings have indicated an important role of the cGAS-STING pathway in mediating aging and some of the major degenerative pathologies associated with telomerase deficiency in zebrafish.51 Given the fact that telomere shortening is considered among the primary hallmarks of aging,72,73 a role for cGAS/STING in mediating short telomere phenotypes could have substantial implications for the development of novel therapeutic strategies for age-related diseases, as well as for the telomere syndromes, which include a plethora of degenerative conditions associated with extreme telomere shortening owing to mutations in genes involved in telomere maintenance, such as telomerase, or telomere protection, such as several of the telomere-binding proteins known as shelterins.74

Here, we addressed the role of the mammalian STING by generating different generations of mice deficient for STING and for either the TERC or the TERT component. The study of STING deficiency in both the Terc and Tert-deficient mouse models is relevant because several studies have suggested potential roles of TERC and TERT that are independent of the telomerase activity. The results reported here demonstrate that STING deficiency does not rescue degenerative phenotypes or reduced longevity associated with short telomeres in either Terc-deficient mice or Tert-deficient mice.

The observation of similar, widely overlapping phenotypes across our Terc- and Tert-null genetic models, linked only by the loss of telomerase function and presence of short telomeres, indicates that telomere shortening accounts for the observed effects. Specific differences found between models, such as expression of p16 in the Terc-null but not in the Tert-null mice, could be explained by the severity of the phenotypes in the different models, which are generally more severe in the case of Terc-null mice, or by the non-canonical functions of the telomerase components. In any case, our results with both telomerase-deficient mouse cohorts show that STING deficiency does not rescue any of the major phenotypes associated with short telomeres, including lifespan.

Interestingly, in our models, STING deficiency phenotypes, in some cases, showed worsening, both in the single STING mutant and in the telomerase-null context. These effects were not general but, sometimes, sex and organ specific, in accordance with reports describing context- and sex-dependent STING effects in different tissues.34,35 These results align with a growing body of work highlighting homeostatic roles of basal STING signaling.30,31,32,33,34,35,36,37 Thus, in our mouse models, STING ablation in a telomerase-null context did not prevent degeneration but even might have aggravated it instead by removing a homeostatic layer that may contribute to physiological support. Further work is needed to elucidate the organismal effect of STING depletion.

Our findings contrast with those of Şerifoğlu et al. (2025), where cGAS-STING was proposed as the primary transducer of telomere-originated damage into tissue decline. Instead, our data support a more layered model: although short telomeres in mice may trigger innate immune sensors and some interferon/inflammatory signatures, cGAS-STING is not the principal driver of telomere-dependent phenotypes in vivo. One possible explanation is the differential reliance on innate immunity. Zebrafish depend more heavily on innate immunity, while adaptive immunity matures later75 and is less robust, making the impact of alterations in sensors such as STING on chronic inflammation and tissue aging stronger. In mice, adaptive immune cells and cytokine feedback loops may buffer or redirect the response, reducing the effect of STING suppression on lifespan.

It is well established that short telomeres trigger a robust DDR through ATM-ATR, activating and stabilizing the p53-p21 axis, which halts proliferation of damaged cells and can induce senescence and inflammation.76,77,78,79 In zebrafish with short telomeres, STING appears to be required for p53-p21 axis stabilization.51 By contrast, in our short-telomere mouse models, the inflammatory transcriptional response is largely preserved, and the p53-p21 axis remains active without STING, indicating that the inflammatory and short-telomere-induced degenerative program proceeds through the p53-p21 axis in an STING-independent manner.

Our results provide insights into the role of STING in aging and in short-telomere-associated age-related diseases in mammalian organisms, with potential relevance for therapeutic strategies. Although our work focuses on the specific context of chronic STING deficiency in mice, the detrimental effects observed upon STING loss suggest that prolonged systemic STING inhibition may require careful and context-specific evaluation in mammalian settings, particularly where telomeres are short, as it may impair tissue organization or even reduce survival.

Limitations of the study

While our study establishes that deleterious phenotypes associated with short telomeres in telomerase-null mice are independent of STING, several caveats warrant consideration. First, the Tert-null model is less extensively characterized than the Terc-null model. Thus, although the survival, pathological, and mechanistic data obtained so far fully support the conclusions for this model, a comprehensive characterization of tissue pathologies remains to be performed. In addition, mechanistic studies were performed only in males in both telomerase-null models. Furthermore, histopathological changes and their severity were assessed by a specialized pathologist, but quantitative measurements could further strengthen the conclusions. Finally, whether these findings generalize to other mammalian organisms remains to be determined.

Experimental model and study participants details

Mice

All mice were generated and maintained at the Spanish National Cancer Research Center (CNIO) under specific pathogen-free conditions in accordance with the recommendation of the Federation of European Laboratory Animal Science Associations. All experiments and animal procedures were approved by our Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and by the Ethics Committee for Research and Animal Welfare (CEIyBA) (PROEX 338-0-21).

Telomerase deficient mice in a C57BL/6 background, Terc−/− mice80 and Tert−/− mice71 were bred with mixed-background Goldenticket (Gt) mice kindly provided by Dr. Óscar Fernández Capetillo at CNIO. The Goldenticket (Gt) mouse has a chemically-induced (ENU) mutant allele of Sting (also referred to as Sting1 and Tmem173) harboring a missense mutation in exon 6, which results in an isoleucine-to-asparagine change in amino acid 199 and prevents protein production.52 Heterozygous Terc+/− mice were crossed to Stinggt/gt mice to generate first-generation G1 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt mice, which were then intercrossed until fourth generation G4 Terc−/−/Stinggt/gt. Similarly, heterozygous Tert+/− mice were crossed to Stinggt/gt mice to generate first-generation G1 Tert−/−/Stinggt/gt mice, which were then intercrossed until fourth generation G4 Tert−/−/Stinggt/gt.

After establishing colonies for all indicated genotypes and generations, animals were assigned to two cohorts for each sex serving distinct aims. A time-matched cohort (5 animals per genotype) was used for cross-sectional comparisons of tissue phenotypes at a common developmental stage; the animals were sacrificed at 45 weeks for Terc mice cohorts and at 35 weeks for Tert mouse cohorts. To examine body composition, dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) was performed at an age of 45 weeks for the Terc mouse cohorts and 35 weeks for Tert mouse cohorts. The remaining animals were included in an aging cohort for the longitudinal survival and weight studies, in which mice were followed until their natural death or the humane endpoint. Initial tissue phenotyping was performed in both male and female mice. After confirming that STING deficiency did not rescue survival in either sex and that tissue phenotypes were largely similar between sexes, subsequent analyses were limited to male mice to optimize time and resource use.

Method details

Telomere quantitative fluorescence in situ hybridization (QFISH)

Whole blood (120–150 μL) was collected from mice of all genotypes. Red blood cells were lysed (Erythrocyte Lysis Buffer, Qiagen), and leukocytes were plated at an appropriate cell density in duplicate into clear-bottom, black-walled 96-well plates pre-coated with 0.001% poly-L-lysine (30 min). Plates were incubated at 37°C for 2 h, fixed in methanol/acetic acid (3:1, v/v; 2 × 10 min), and stored overnight at −20°C. High-throughput (HT)-QFISH was performed as described.81 Confocal images were acquired on an Opera High-Content Screening system (PerkinElmer) Telomere length was quantified as the mean intensity of individual telomere spots.

Western blot

Whole cell extracts were prepared using RIPA buffer. Protein concentration was determined using the Bio-Rad DC Protein Assay (Bio-Rad). Up to 40 μg of protein per extract were separated by electrophoresis on NuPAGE 4–12% Bis-Tris gels. After protein transfer onto 0.45 μm nitrocellulose membranes (Amersham), the membranes were incubated with the indicated antibodies. Antibody binding was exposed after incubation with a secondary antibody coupled to fluorescent dyes and detected using an Odyssey DLx Imager. Primary antibodies: anti-GAPDH (14C10 Clone, Cell Signaling Technology, #2118), anti-p53 (Pab 240 ab26, Abcam), anti-TBK1 (Cell Signaling Technology, #3013, anti-phospho-TBK1 (D52C2 Clone, Cell Signaling Technology, #5483), anti-IRF3 (D83B9 Clone, Cell Signaling Technology, #4302), anti-phospho-IRF3 (Ser396) (4D4G Clone, Cell Signaling Technology, #4947, and anti-β-Actin (C4 Clone, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-47778).

Immunohistochemistry analysis

After mice sacrifice, organs were fixed in 4% formaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin-embedded tissue sections at a thickness of 5–7 μm were subjected to immunohistochemical staining. Tissue sections were processed with 10 mM sodium citrate (pH 6.5) cooked under pressure for 2 min. Slides were washed in water, then in Buffer TBS-0.5% Tween 20, blocked with peroxidase, washed with TBS-0.5% Tween 20 and blocked with fetal bovine serum. Primary antibodies were: anti-phospho-Histone 2AX (ser139) (JBW301, Millipore, #05–636), anti-Ki-67 (D3B5 Clone, Cell Signaling Technology, #9129), anti-myeloperoxidase (MPO, Dako, A0398), anti-TER-119/Erythroid cells (Ly76) (BD Biosciences, #550565), anti-CD45RR/B220 (BD Biosciences, #557390), anti-CD3e (2GV6 Clone, Roche, #790–4341), anti-p21 (291H/B5) (Monoclonal Antibodies Core Unit at CNIO), anti-p16 (p1633B) (Monoclonal Antibodies Core Unit at CNIO), and anti-F4/80 (Bethyl, A700-209). All primary antibodies were incubated following manufacturer instructions. Slides were then incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated with peroxidase from DAKO. Following incubation with the secondary antibodies, positive cells were visualized using 3,3-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride plus (DAB+) as a chromogen. Counterstaining was performed with nuclear hematoxylin. Images were captured with an Olympus AX70 microscope.

Pathological grading

Small intestine, large intestine, spleen, liver, skin, and testicle paraffin-embedded sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin for pathological examination, grading and classification. Pathological grading of hematoxylin and eosin sections was blindly conducted by a specialized pathologist using different criteria depending on the tissue, either in a classification with three or four grades, ranging from healthy to mild affection, moderate affection, and severe affection.

Skin pathology was assessed as follows: mild affection: presence of small focal areas of epidermal and piloannexal atrophy; medium affection: presence of multifocal areas of epidermal and piloannexal atrophy; severe affection: presence of multifocal-extensive areas of epidermal and piloannexal atrophy.

Testis pathology was assessed as follows: mild affection: presence of hypo spermatogenesis of all cell types (Sertoli cells, spermatogonia, spermatocytes and few spermatozoa) can be seen with a low level of cellularity within the seminiferous tubules; medium affection: presence of maturation arrest (numerous spermatogonia, few spermatocytes and no spermatozoa); and severe affection: presence of Sertoli only syndrome (total absence of germ cells, mild basement membrane thickening and an increased number of interstitial Leydig cells).

Spleen pathology was assessed as follows: mild affection: presence of mild or focal loss of the spleen architecture and cellular dysregulation; severe affection: severe multifocal and diffuse loss of the spleen architecture and cellular imbalance.

Liver pathology was assessed by checking steatosis and inflammation. Steatosis was classified as follows: mild affection: 5–33% of hepatocytes affected; medium affection: 33–66% of hepatocytes affected; and severe affection: >66% of hepatocytes affected. Liver inflammation was classified as follows: mild affection: <2 inflammation foci; medium affection: 2–4 inflammation foci; and severe affection: >4 inflammation foci (all of the previous at 10× amplification in a 2,12 mm2 field).

Small intestine pathology was assessed as follows: mild affection: presence of areas with shortening of villi due to epithelial and glandular atrophy, and mild diffuse inflammatory reaction; medium affection: presence of multifocal areas in the mucosa with few glands or cystic regenerative epithelial and glandular cystic hyperplasia, focal ulcers and moderate diffuse inflammatory reaction; and severe affection: presence of extensive ulcerated areas and marked diffuse transmural inflammation or regenerative glandular hyperplasia that narrows the intestinal lumen.

Large intestine pathology was assessed as follows: mild affection: presence of areas with epithelial and glandular atrophy and slight diffuse inflammatory reaction; medium affection: presence of multifocal mucosal areas with sparse glands and others with epithelial and glandular regenerative cystic hyperplasia, focal ulcers and moderate diffuse inflammatory reaction; and severe affection: presence of extensive ulcerated areas and marked diffuse transmural inflammation or regenerative glandular hyperplasia that narrows the intestinal lumen.

Image analysis

The analysis of the stained and digitized immunohistochemistry sections was performed using QuPath (version 0.5.1-×64). In each image, regardless of the type of tissue or staining, the stain vectors were estimated through automatic detection. Subsequently, the region corresponding to the whole tissue was delineated with an annotation generated by applying a pixel classifier thresholder adjusted according to the average channel intensities of the image. This procedure was carried out at high to very high resolution, with the application of a Gaussian prefilter to improve detection accuracy.

In a second stage, depending on the intrinsic characteristics of the tissue, the nature of the staining (total cellular, nuclear, or cytoplasmic), and the quality of digitalization, quantification was conducted using two different analytical approaches, which can be interpreted according to the axes of the corresponding graphical representations. The first approach consisted of simply generating an annotation corresponding to the positive staining, whose area was normalized relative to the total analyzed area. The second approach was based on segmentation of the nuclei and/or cells of the tissue. For this purpose, either the Cellpose extension (version 0.9.6) or the StarDist extension (version 0.5.0) was employed, depending on the case. Based on the resulting segmentation, an object classifier was developed to distinguish between the different positive staining present in each preparation.

RNA-seq

Total RNA from tissue was extracted with the RNeasy kit (QIAGEN) and used for 3′mRNA-seq analysis. RNAs were converted into sequencing libraries using the “QuantSeq 3‘ mRNA-Seq V2 Library Prep Kit (FWD) for Illumina” (Lexogen, Cat.No. 191). Directional cDNA libraries containing UMIs were obtained and sequenced in a single read format on an Illumina NovaSeq X instrument. Read alignment to the mouse reference genome was performed with STAR and counts were obtained with featureCounts. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (version 1.30.1). Differentially expressed genes were identified based on an adjusted p-value threshold of 0.05. Functional analysis was performed with Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) (version 4.3.2). The input matrix for GSEA consisted of normalized values derived from the differential expression analysis. Gene sets from MSigDB v2024.1.Mm were used, including curated gene sets of canonical pathways, gene ontology gene sets, chemical and genetic perturbations gene sets, and hallmark gene sets. Only gene sets exhibiting significant enrichment levels (FDR q-value <0.25) were considered. RNA-seq data have been deposited at GEO as GSE309674 and are publicly available as of the date of publication.

Quantitative PCR

Samples from small intestine of five mice from each genotype were used for quantitative PCR. Total RNA from cells was extracted as described above (see RNA-seq). Reverse transcription was performed using the iSCRIPT cDNA Advance synthesis kit (BIO-RAD) according to manufactures protocols. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using Go-Taq qPCR master mix (Promega) according to the manufacturer's protocol. All values were obtained in triplicates. Primers used were: Isg15-Forward AAGTCCACGAAGACCAGTTCTG, Isg15-Reverse AGGCGCAAATGCTTGATCAC, Ifit1-Forward TGTTTGCATGGCTGAAGTGC, Ifit1-Reverse GGTCTTGTTGTTCAGTGCCTTC, Ifit3-Forward ATGAGCGCTATTGCAACCTC, Ifit3-Reverse ACCTCTTCCTTCTCGATTGACC, Ifi35-Forward AAATTCCAGCAAGCCCCTCA, Ifi35-Reverse TGAAGGCACAACTGTCAGGG, Ifi44-Forward TTATGTCTCAGAGTGGCAGCTG, Ifi44-Reverse ACCGTCAAGCTCCGAGATAATC, Irf7-Forward CTCCTGAGCGCAGCCTTG, Irf7-Reverse GTTCTTACTGCTGGGGCCAT, Ifitm1-Forward AGGAAGATGGTGGGTGATACG, Ifitm1-Reverse AGCTGATGTTCAGGCACTTG, Cxcl11-Forward AAAAGTCACGTGCACACTCC, Cxcl11-Reverse GGTCCGGATTGCAGTAACAATG, Cxcl10-Forward TGGCTAGTCCTAATTGCCCTTG, Cxcl10-Reverse TTGTCTCAGGACCATGGCTTG, Ifitm3-Forward GGTGGGTGATGTGACTGGAG, Ifitm3-Reverse AGTGTGAAGGTTTTGAGCGT, Ccl5-Forward TTTGTCACTCGAAGGAACCG and Ccl5-Reverse AGCAAGCAATGACAGGGAAG.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Survival data were analyzed by Kaplan Meier survival curves, and comparisons were performed by Mantel–Cox/log rank test.

All statistical analysis were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.1.1. Statistical significance was determined by ordinary one-way ANOVA with Šídák’s multiple comparisons test correction. Data are presented in graphs as mean ± SEM. p values of less than 0.05 were considered significant and marked as: ns, not significant; ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
