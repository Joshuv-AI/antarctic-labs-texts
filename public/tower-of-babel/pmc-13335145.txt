Longitudinal blood transcriptome profiling reveals immune dynamics in sepsis
Authors: Jinha Hwang, Jae Hyun Cha, Sejoong Ahn, Jong-Hak Park, Sua Kim, Minsuk Jung, Yunsang Choi, Hyeri Seok, Won Suk Choi, Myung-Hyun Nam, Dae Won Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSepsis is a life-threatening condition caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, with significant biological heterogeneity. Transcriptomic profiling offers insights into immune-related gene expression patterns that may inform patient stratification. This study aimed to identify transcriptomic signatures of sepsis or septic shock.MethodEleven adult patients who presented to the emergency department with sepsis or septic shock were enrolled at a tertiary hospital in Korea. Serial whole-blood samples were collected on days 1, 3, and 7. RNA sequencing was performed using Illumina paired-end protocols. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on normalized transcriptomic data to derive a gene signature. Enrichment scores were calculated using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis and validated in external bulk and single-cell RNA-seq datasets.ResultsPCA identified first principal component (PC1) as the major axis of variation, separating samples by sepsis severity and timepoint. Clustering of the top 50 PC1 contributing genes identified three transcriptomic subgroups with distinct expression patterns. Enrichment scores for this gene set increased with severity and declined over time. In multiple external sepsis cohorts, enrichment scores showed consistent trends and were significantly associated with 28-day mortality. Functional analysis revealed predominant neutrophil-associated pathways, as further supported by external single-cell RNA-seq validation.ConclusionsWe identified a neutrophil-driven transcriptomic signature associated with sepsis severity and temporal immune dynamics. These findings suggest that longitudinal transcriptomic profiling may provide additional insight into the evolving host immune response during sepsis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13416-1.

Background

Sepsis is defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction due to a dysregulated host response to infection [1]. Sepsis is one of the leading causes of mortality, disability and healthcare expenditures worldwide [2]. This global health threat affects approximately 48.9 million people annually and is responsible for an estimated 11 to 14 million sepsis-related deaths each year [2, 3]. Prompt recognition and early treatment are essential to preventing progression to shock, multi-organ failure, disability, and death.

Recent approaches to sepsis treatment remain fundamentally supportive and fail to target the underlying dysregulated immune response. Although numerous randomized controlled trials have been conducted to address this therapeutic gap, most have failed to demonstrate clinical benefit [4, 5]. The trials examining various medications such as steroids and recombinant activated protein C have not reliably reduced mortality or improved organ function [5–7]. In addition, the reliance on targeting therapies against pro- or anti-inflammatory pathways have failed to address the complex interplay of inflammatory responses in sepsis [5, 8, 9]. The primary reason for these failures is the lack of robust phenotypic stratification, leading to diluted efficacy signals and conflicting results. Improving therapeutic precision requires understanding sepsis heterogeneity at the molecular and clinical levels.

In this context, transcriptomic analysis can reveal gene expression patterns associated with specific physiological or pathological conditions of sepsis, thereby explaining clinical variability and facilitating the identification of biomarkers and therapeutic targets [10, 11]. Previous transcriptomic studies have identified molecular subphenotypes (also known as endotypes) associated with distinct immune responses in sepsis [12–14]. The GAinS cohort identified two sepsis response signatures (SRS1 and SRS2), in which the SRS1 endotype was associated with immunosuppression and increased mortality [12]. Similarly, the MARS study classified patients with sepsis into four genomic endotypes (MARS1–4), in which the MARS1 endotype was characterized by decreased expression of genes involved in innate and adaptive immunity and was associated with higher mortality [13]. In pediatric septic shock, gene expression profiling has also revealed subclasses characterized by differential immune signaling and prognostic outcomes [14]. Furthermore, Balch et al. demonstrated that immunological endotypes in critically ill patients can change over time, reflecting dynamic alterations in host immune responses during the course of illness [15]. However, most transcriptomic studies in sepsis have relied on single time-point measurements, which may not fully capture these temporal dynamics. Stratifying patients based on transcriptomic profiles may improve risk stratification and facilitate the design of precision clinical trials by accounting for the biological heterogeneity of sepsis [16–18].

In this study, we performed longitudinal transcriptomic analysis of patients with sepsis or septic shock using serially collected whole-blood samples. The aim was to identify gene expression profiles that distinguish patients with sepsis or septic shock from non-septic controls and evaluate their association with clinical outcomes.

Methods

Patient enrollment

This was a single-center retrospective study using prospectively collected data and blood samples from adult patients over 18 years of age with a quick sepsis-related organ failure assessment (qSOFA) score of 2 or higher who had visited the emergency department (ED) at the Korea University Ansan Hospital from December 2020 to July 2021 and who agreed to participate in the study. Next, we set another inclusion criterion as an increase in the SOFA score by ≥ 2 points in the ED, regardless of the presence of current infection. Consequently, all study participants had a qSOFA score of ≥ 2 points and an increase in the SOFA score by ≥ 2 points. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria have been previously reported [19]. Then we categorized eligible participants into the following three groups according to the presence of infection and sepsis severity: non-infectious organ failure, sepsis, and septic shock. Clinical diagnosis of sepsis or septic shock was defined by the 2016 SCCM/ESICM International Sepsis Definitions Conference (Sepsis-3) [1].

The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of the Korea University Ansan Hospital (2024AS0030). Written informed consent was obtained from study participant or legal representative if not available.

Human blood collection, sample preparation and total RNA sequencing

Blood samples for RNA analysis were drawn in PAXgene tubes™ (Becton-Dickinson, Breda, the Netherlands) within 24 hours after ED visit (HD#1), hospital day 3 (HD#3), and hospital day 7 (HD#7) from 11 patients and 4 healthy volunteers.

Total RNA was extracted with the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN), quantified by Quant-iT RiboGreen (Invitrogen), and assessed on a TapeStation RNA ScreenTape (Agilent); only samples with RIN > 7.0 were used. For each sample, 0.5 µg RNA was processed with the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Globin kit (Illumina): rRNA depletion, fragmentation, first-strand cDNA synthesis with SuperScript II and random primers, and second-strand synthesis with DNA Polymerase I/RNase H in the presence of dUTP. Libraries underwent end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, and PCR enrichment. Final libraries were quantified by KAPA qPCR (KAPA/Roche) and size-checked on a TapeStation D1000 ScreenTape. Indexed libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq X Plus (Illumina) to generate 2 × 100 bp paired-end reads (Macrogen, Seoul, Korea).

Transcriptomic data processing and analysis

Trimmomatic v0.38 was used to remove adapter sequences and trim bases with poor base quality. Sequencing quality was assessed before and after trimming; all samples achieved Q30 > 96%, mapping rate > 97%, with total reads ranging from approximately 100 to 132 million per sample (Supplementary Table S1). The trimmed reads were aligned to the Homo sapiens (hg38) using HISAT v2.1.0 [20] and aligned data were sorted and indexed using SAMtools v 1.9. Transcripts were assembled and quantified using StringTie v2.1.3b [21] to obtain raw read count and transcripts per million (TPM). Raw gene-level read counts were processed using the DESeq2 package (v1.38) [22]. Genes with fewer than 10 total counts across all samples were removed and variance-stabilizing transformation (VST) was applied to normalized the count data. Principal component analysis (PCA) on the top 500 most variable genes assessed global transcriptional differences across samples. Genes contributing most strongly to the first principal component (PC1) were extracted based on loading values for downstream interpretation. The PC1-contributing genes were subsequently analyzed using g:Profiler [23] to identify enriched biological pathways and Gene Ontology (GO) terms. To evaluate the gene expression signatures associated with the top 50 genes contributing to PC1, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was performed using the Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) package [24].

Sepsis dataset

To validate the transcriptomic signature, six publicly available transcriptomic datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) to analyze gene expression patterns in sepsis patients. These included one RNA-Seq dataset (GSE154918) [25] and five gene expression microarray datasets (GSE13904, E-MTAB-4421, E-MTAB-4451, E-MTAB-5273, and E-MTAB-5274) [12, 26, 27].

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis

Single-cell RNA-seq data from the OMIX005600 dataset [28] were processed using Seurat (v4) [29]. Cells with <200 or >2,500 detected genes or >10% mitochondrial gene content were excluded. Gene features detected in fewer than 0.1% of total cells were filtered out. All samples were merged into a single object, log-normalized, and scaled after identifying highly variable genes. Dimensionality reduction was performed using PCA, followed by batch correction with Harmony [30] based on sample identity. The corrected embeddings were used for UMAP visualization and clustering (resolution = 0.5).

Cell type annotation was performed by examining canonical marker gene expression across Seurat-defined clusters. Average expression profiles of canonical markers for each cluster were visualized as a heatmap (Supplementary Figure 3A), and the specificity of marker expression was further validated using a DotPlot of marker genes from the original dataset (Hong et al., iScience 2022) across annotated cell types (Supplementary Figure 3B), confirming cluster-specific expression patterns consistent with the original study. For functional scoring, AUCell [31] and Seurat’s AddModuleScore function were applied to assess gene set enrichment score at the single-cell level. The PC1 signature score was calculated using AUCell, and the scores of gene sets related to neutrophil maturation and biological functions curated by Xie et al. [32] were calculated using the AddModuleScore function to compare the functional states between PC1-high and PC1-low neutrophils.

To investigate the temporal and severity-dependent changes in the transcriptomic landscape of sepsis, we conducted PCA using RNA-seq data from sepsis, septic shock, and healthy controls sampled on days 1, 3, and 7. PCA revealed a clear and progressive separation along the PC1 axis, with control samples tightly clustered on one end and septic shock samples distributed at the opposite extreme (Fig. 1A). The density plot along PC1 further confirmed a gradual shift in transcriptomic profiles from day 1 to day 7 in septic patients, indicating a temporal evolution in gene expression that correlates with disease progression (Supplementary Figure 1A and B). This highlights the robustness of PC1 in capturing the primary axis of transcriptomic variation associated with sepsis severity and temporal evolution. We next selected the top 50 genes with the highest absolute PC1 loadings as a focused and interpretable gene set representing the dominant transcriptomic variation captured by PC1. Hierarchical clustering of these genes was performed to investigate the gene expression patterns underlying the PC1 axis. This clustering resulted in three distinct groups (Groups A, B, and C) with characteristic patterns of gene expression (Fig. 1B). Group A predominantly comprised day 1 septic shock samples with uniformly high gene expression, whereas Group C included control and late-phase samples with consistently low gene expression. These findings suggest that PC1-based clustering effectively captures transcriptomic changes associated with sepsis progression and severity.

Expression patterns of representative PC1-contributing genes were visualized to illustrate their temporal and group-specific dynamics (Fig. 1C; Supplementary Fig. 2). ARG1, IL1R2, FCER1A, and ITGA7 were significantly upregulated in septic shock, with the largest differences at early timepoints (days 1 and 3). ARG1, a regulator of inflammatory tone and myeloid function, showed markedly higher expression in early shock, and IL1R2, FCER1A, and ITGA7 exhibited similar early upregulation, consistent with activation of neutrophils and other innate immune cells.

To further understand the functional pathways associated with the PC1 signature, we performed enrichment analysis on the top 50 PC1 genes. The most significantly enriched terms included neutrophil degranulation (Reactome) and cellular components related to secretory granule, specific granule, tertiary granule, and secretory vesicle (Gene Ontology Cellular Component) (Fig. 1D). Together, these findings indicate that the PC1 signature reflects neutrophil-driven activation and degranulation, capturing dysregulated innate immunity.

External validation confirms prognostic utility of the PC1 signature

The clinical and prognostic relevance of the top 50 PC1-contributing genes was evaluated using ssGSEA across three cohorts with serial whole-blood transcriptomic data—the Ansan cohort, GSE13904, and GSE154918. Enrichment scores were compared across clinical groups (control, sepsis, and septic shock) and available timepoints (day 1, 3, or 7, depending on cohort) (Fig. 2A). Across all three cohorts, enrichment scores were elevated in sepsis and septic shock patients compared to controls, particularly at the earliest timepoint. Enrichment scores tended to decline over time within each clinical group, reflecting the progressive resolution of the sepsis-associated transcriptomic response. We also observed significantly higher PC1 enrichment scores in septic shock than in sepsis across all timepoints. Furthermore, analysis of four independent cohorts with available 28-day mortality data (E-MTAB-4421, E-MTAB-4451, E-MTAB-5273, E-MTAB-5274) revealed that non-survivors had significantly higher enrichment scores than survivors in three out of four datasets (Fig. 2B). These findings suggest that the PC1 signature is a reliable biomarker of both immune dysregulation and 28-day mortality risk.

Neutrophils drive the PC1-associated transcriptomic program and exhibit enhanced functional activation

To investigate the cellular origin of the PC1 signature, we analyzed single-cell RNA-seq data from sepsis patients. Unsupervised UMAP projection revealed distinct transcriptional clusters corresponding to major immune cell types (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Figure 3A). Cell type annotations were further supported by DotPlot analysis of marker genes across cell types (Supplementary Figure 3B), confirming cluster-specific expression patterns. Neutrophils constituted the majority of cells, accounting for 72.5% of the total population, followed by monocytes (13.1%) and smaller fractions of T cells and dendritic cells, indicating a myeloid-dominant profile in early sepsis (Fig. 3B). To investigate temporal changes in immune composition, we compared cell type proportions across days 1, 3, and 5. Among the identified immune populations, only B cells and CD8+ T cells exhibited a statistically significant increase over time, while the relative proportions of the other immune cells did not show significant temporal variation (Fig. 3C). We next evaluated the temporal dynamics of PC1 signature in single cell dataset. Using AUCell, we identified PC1 signature–high cells across the single-cell dataset. These cells were primarily localized to the neutrophil cluster and were most abundant on day 1 (Fig. 3D). Notably, 90.46% of PC1 high cells belonged to the neutrophil lineage, further confirming the neutrophil-specific nature of this transcriptional program (Supplementary Figure 3C). The frequency of PC1 high cells markedly declined by day 5, with a statistically significant reduction in PC1 high neutrophil fractions between day 1 and subsequent timepoints (p = 0.0028 for day 3; p = 0.0013 for day 5). These findings indicate that the PC1 signature captures temporally regulated neutrophil activation during the early phase of sepsis.

To further explore the functional state of PC1-high cells, we performed pathway scoring across neutrophil-related gene sets. PC1-high neutrophils exhibited significantly elevated scores in pathways related to neutrophil maturation, and neutrophil activation compared to PC1-low neutrophils (Fig. 3E). This was accompanied by increased scores for metabolic pathways such as glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, indicating that PC1-high neutrophils represent a metabolically active, hyperinflammatory subpopulation that plays a critical role in early sepsis responses.

Discussion

Using serial whole-blood RNA-seq from sepsis and septic shock patients, PCA identified PC1 as the dominant axis of transcriptomic variation. Based on PC1 loadings, we selected 50 key genes to define the transcriptomic signature. Importantly, the PC1 loadings were used solely for gene selection in the discovery cohort (Ansan, n = 11) and not as a scoring metric; enrichment scores were independently calculated using ssGSEA in six external cohorts that were not involved in signature derivation, thereby ensuring a clear separation between discovery and validation. The signature tracked disease severity, varied dynamically over time, and showed prognostic relevance across multiple external datasets. In particular, the signature was significantly associated with 28-day mortality in three out of four independent cohorts, representing a clinically distinct endpoint that cannot be explained by a tautological relationship with severity alone. The progressive temporal decline of enrichment scores across serial timepoints further reflects a dynamic immune trajectory rather than a static severity measure, providing additional independent evidence of biological relevance. Functional annotation and external single-cell RNA-seq indicated enrichment of neutrophil programs, supporting its biological and clinical relevance.

In sepsis, transcriptomic analysis has been used to uncover underlying biological heterogeneity and to classify patients into distinct endotypes [33–35]. Previous studies, including cohorts from the United Kingdom and the Netherlands, have demonstrated the prognostic and therapeutic significance of such classifications [12, 13]. In our study, we identified three transcriptomic subgroups (A, B, and C) based on the expression patterns of PC1-contributing genes. We further evaluated enrichment scores based on these genes, which demonstrated potential as a continuous metric for subgrouping. Both approaches revealed differences in disease severity and temporal progression, highlighting their potential biological and clinical relevance.

Prior transcriptomic studies have consistently identified neutrophil-enriched profiles, reflecting early hyperinflammatory responses [36, 37]. Sweeney et al. and Baghela et al. reported neutrophil-dominant endotypes associated with upregulated inflammatory signatures and poor outcomes [10, 38, 39]. In our study, functional annotation and external single-cell RNA-seq validation confirmed neutrophil-associated expression patterns, reinforcing their central role in the transcriptomic response to sepsis. Moreover, progressive temporal declines in signature expression were consistently observed across datasets, reflecting a shift from early hyperinflammation to immune resolution or suppression. Beyond the general concept of neutrophilia, our single-cell analysis further characterized the signature-associated neutrophil subpopulation at the functional level. PC1-high neutrophils exhibited significantly elevated activity in neutrophil maturation, specific granule release, glycolysis, and oxidative phosphorylation compared to PC1-low neutrophils, defining a metabolically active, hyperinflammatory subpopulation and providing mechanistic insight into the transcriptomic heterogeneity within the neutrophil compartment during sepsis.

In our PC1-contributing genes, several genes are associated with adaptive immune responses, such as JCHAIN and IL18RAP which are established markers of B cells and T cells, respectively. Although these adaptive markers represent relatively minor component of the signature, their presence suggests that our transcriptomic signature may capture early transcriptional signals of adaptive immune activation. Taken together, these findings suggest that longitudinal transcriptomic profiling effectively captures the overall dynamics of the host immune response in sepsis, from early hyperinflammation to later immune recovery.

A key strength of this study is the integration of serial transcriptomic data into the signature derivation process. Unlike previous studies that defined endotypes at a single timepoint and subsequently evaluated their temporal behavior, our approach incorporated longitudinal gene expression profiles [14, 40, 41]. As a result, the derived signature encodes the temporal trajectory of the host immune response, thereby enhancing its biological interpretability and potential clinical relevance. From a clinical perspective, longitudinal transcriptomic profiling may offer advantages over single-time-point measurements in sepsis. Because the host immune response evolves throughout the course of illness, repeated transcriptomic measurements may provide additional information for patient stratification and disease monitoring. With continued advances in molecular diagnostic technologies, transcriptomic assays may become increasingly feasible in clinical settings and may complement conventional biomarkers in sepsis management. Accordingly, future studies should focus on dynamic, time-resolved approaches rather than relying solely on single-time-point classifications.

This is the first study to perform longitudinal transcriptomic profiling of sepsis patients in Korea. Although numerous transcriptomic studies of sepsis have been conducted globally, Korean data remain limited [12, 13, 42]. A previous transcriptomic study of 133 Korean patients proposed diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers but lacked external validation and serial sampling [43]. Our study addresses these limitations and contributes to the global applicability of sepsis transcriptomes across diverse populations.

Our study has several limitations. The sample size was relatively small, limiting the statistical power to assess associations with clinical outcomes and treatment response. Prospective validation in larger, well-characterized cohorts is needed to confirm the clinical utility of the signature and to evaluate its potential role in guiding therapeutic strategies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our longitudinal transcriptomic profiling revealed a neutrophil-driven gene signature associated with sepsis severity and temporal immune dynamics. The PC1-derived transcriptomic signature showed consistent associations with disease severity and mortality across multiple external cohorts, supporting its biological relevance. These findings suggest that longitudinal transcriptomic analysis may provide additional insight into the evolving host immune response during sepsis. However, the relatively small sample size of the discovery cohort limits the generalizability of our findings, and further validation in larger prospective cohorts will be necessary to establish the clinical utility of this approach.
