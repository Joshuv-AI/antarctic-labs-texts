Longitudinal echocardiographic systolic changes in dogs receiving doxorubicin-based chemotherapy
Authors: Gustavo Cavinato Herrera, Matheus Matioli Mantovani, Jacqueline Ribeiro de Castro, Andresa dos Santos Veras, Marcelo José Barbosa Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDoxorubicin is widely used in veterinary oncology but carries a risk of cumulative cardiotoxicity. Serial echocardiographic evaluation is commonly used to monitor dogs receiving potentially cardiotoxic chemotherapy; however, evidence remains limited regarding which indices best capture early longitudinal systolic changes. Conventional monitoring relies largely on fractional shortening and M-mode estimated ejection fraction, which are based on geometric assumptions and may have limited sensitivity for subtle systolic change. This study evaluated whether Simpson-derived left ventricular ejection fraction better captures longitudinal echocardiographic systolic changes than conventional indices in dogs receiving doxorubicin-based chemotherapy. Twenty-nine client-owned dogs were prospectively enrolled, including 23 females and 6 males, with a median age of 10.0 years (IQR, 7.0–11.0) and median body weight of 13.2 kg (IQR, 7.0–21.6). Twelve dogs received doxorubicin as monotherapy and 17 received combination protocols. Echocardiography was performed before the first cycle (D0) and after three consecutive cycles (D1, D2, D3).ResultsSimpson-derived left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) showed a progressive numerical decline from 72.0 ± 7.3% at baseline to 66.5 ± 7.7% at D3; however, no individual time-point estimate reached statistical significance in the mixed-effects model (D1, p = 0.183; D2, p = 0.618; D3, p = 0.206). M-mode estimated LVEF and fractional shortening did not show comparable directional patterns or significant longitudinal changes. A decline greater than 10% points in Simpson-derived LVEF was identified in 9 of 29 dogs (31.0%), all while absolute values remained within conventional reference limits. Six of these dogs were not identified by either conventional index.ConclusionsSerial, baseline-referenced Simpson-derived LVEF identified more longitudinal echocardiographic systolic changes than conventional indices in this cohort. These findings suggest that Simpson-derived LVEF may provide complementary information for tracking baseline-referenced systolic changes in dogs receiving doxorubicin. However, these changes should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of myocardial injury, and their clinical relevance requires confirmation in larger prospective studies.

Background

Doxorubicin, an anthracycline antibiotic widely used in veterinary oncology either as monotherapy or in combination with other chemotherapeutic agents, is considered a cornerstone of treatment for several canine neoplasms, including lymphoma, osteosarcoma, and carcinomas [1, 2]. Despite its antineoplastic efficacy, doxorubicin carries a well-documented risk of cumulative, dose-dependent cardiotoxicity that may result in progressive left ventricular systolic dysfunction and, in severe cases, heart failure [3–5]. The proposed mechanisms are multifactorial and include oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, topoisomerase IIβ-mediated DNA damage, and cardiomyocyte injury, ultimately contributing to impaired myocardial contractile function [5]. The incidence of clinical cardiotoxicity in dogs receiving doxorubicin has been reported at approximately 4% in the general population, rising to 15.4% in breeds predisposed to dilated cardiomyopathy [1]. In this context, serial echocardiographic assessment is recommended for monitoring left ventricular function in dogs receiving potentially cardiotoxic chemotherapy [4, 5].

Cardiac monitoring in dogs receiving doxorubicin has traditionally relied on fractional shortening and M-mode estimated ejection fraction (Teicholz method) as primary indices of left ventricular systolic function [1, 4, 6]. Both parameters are derived from one-dimensional linear measurements that assume a fixed ellipsoidal ventricular geometry, an assumption that may not hold across the wide range of body sizes, conformations, and structural changes encountered in the canine population [7–9]. In contrast, Simpson-derived left ventricular ejection fraction is obtained by volumetric disc summation from apical views and does not depend on geometric assumptions about ventricular shape [7]. The superiority of this volumetric approach over M-mode derived estimates has been demonstrated in the early detection of systolic changes in Doberman Pinschers with dilated cardiomyopathy [8]. Furthermore, in a retrospective study of 494 dogs receiving doxorubicin, a meaningful decline in fractional shortening was consistently detected only after five or more cumulative doses, at which point irreversible myocardial injury had likely already occurred in some animals [1], suggesting that conventional linear indices may detect cardiotoxic deterioration at a relatively late stage.

A further limitation of conventional monitoring is its reliance on reference-based interpretation alone, that is, on whether a given value falls below an established lower limit of normality. Clinically relevant reductions in systolic function may occur while absolute values remain within the reference range. In human cardio-oncology, a reduction of 10% points or greater in left ventricular ejection fraction relative to the individual baseline is considered a cardiotoxicity-compatible change, even when the final value does not fall below the lower limit of normality [5, 9]. This criterion was developed and validated in human patients and has not been formally validated in dogs; its application in the present study is therefore operational and exploratory [10]. Current monitoring guidelines for human oncology patients recommend the biplane Simpson method as the echocardiographic standard for ejection fraction assessment when three-dimensional echocardiography is unavailable, in preference over M-mode linear methods [5, 10, 11]. Serial, individualized, baseline-referenced assessment may therefore identify cardiotoxicity-compatible changes that would be missed by purely cross-sectional interpretation, regardless of the method employed.

Although conventional echocardiographic indices have been used to monitor dogs receiving doxorubicin, current evidence does not clearly establish whether Simpson-derived left ventricular ejection fraction better captures longitudinal systolic changes than M-mode estimated ejection fraction or fractional shortening, or whether baseline-referenced declines may occur while absolute values remain within conventional reference limits [1, 4, 5, 7]. The present study aimed to evaluate whether Simpson-derived left ventricular ejection fraction, M-mode estimated ejection fraction, and fractional shortening identify longitudinal echocardiographic systolic changes in dogs treated with doxorubicin in this cohort, and to determine the proportion of dogs with a decline greater than 10% points in Simpson-derived ejection fraction relative to baseline, even when values remained within conventional reference limits. We hypothesized that Simpson-derived left ventricular ejection fraction would identify a greater number of dogs with echocardiographic systolic changes than M-mode estimated ejection fraction and fractional shortening, and that declines greater than 10% points from baseline could be detected in some animals even when absolute values remained within the reference range.

Methods

Study design and animal selection

This was a prospective, longitudinal observational study conducted in accordance with the ethical principles governing animal experimentation and approved by the Ethics Committee for the Use of Animals of the Federal University of Uberlândia (CEUA/UFU), under protocol number 054/20. Written owner consent was obtained before enrollment.

Dogs referred to the Oncology Clinic and Surgery Department (SECCON) of the Veterinary Hospital of the Federal University of Uberlândia (HV-UFU) and affiliated private veterinary oncology clinics in Uberlândia, Minas Gerais, Brazil, were eligible if they had a confirmed oncologic diagnosis and an indication for doxorubicin-based chemotherapy. Dogs were classified according to the chemotherapy protocol prescribed by the attending oncologist: doxorubicin as monotherapy (Doxo group) or doxorubicin combined with other chemotherapeutic agents (Combination group). Treatment classification therefore reflected routine clinical management rather than investigator-assigned allocation.

Dogs were excluded if they had congenital heart disease, a breed phenotype consistent with dilated cardiomyopathy, or a primary cardiac neoplasm at baseline echocardiographic evaluation. Dogs with myxomatous mitral valve disease were eligible provided they were classified as ACVIM stage B1 at baseline, that is, with structural valvular changes but without cardiac remodeling and without echocardiographic evidence of systolic or diastolic dysfunction [12].

Doxorubicin protocol and follow-up

Doxorubicin hydrochloride (Fauldoxo®, Libbs Farmacêutica, Brazil) was administered intravenously at a dose of 30 mg/m² per session, according to commonly used veterinary oncology protocols [1]. The interval between sessions varied according to the chemotherapy protocol prescribed by the attending oncologist but was generally approximately 30 days between cycles. Cumulative dose was not standardized across animals and depended on the number of cycles completed during routine clinical care. Echocardiographic assessments were performed before the first cycle (D0, baseline) and after each subsequent cycle (D1, D2, and D3). This variability in treatment schedule reflected the observational design and routine clinical setting of the study.

Echocardiographic examination

All dogs were positioned in lateral recumbency and manually restrained without sedation or anesthesia. Echocardiographic examinations were performed using two-dimensional, M-mode, pulsed-wave Doppler, continuous-wave Doppler, color flow mapping, and tissue Doppler imaging, in accordance with recommendations for canine transthoracic echocardiography [13], with modifications proposed by Boon [14]. All examinations were performed by a single experienced operator using a GE Logiq F6 ultrasound system (General Electric Healthcare) equipped with electronic sector transducers with frequencies ranging from 3 to 7 MHz, with simultaneous electrocardiographic monitoring.

From the right parasternal short-axis view, aortic and left atrial diameters were measured in two-dimensional mode at the level of the cardiac base, and the LA: Ao ratio was calculated [15]. From the same acoustic window, M-mode measurements were obtained at the level of the chordae tendineae. Left ventricular internal dimensions at end-diastole (LVIDd) and end-systole (LVIDs) were measured and used to calculate fractional shortening (FS) as FS (%) = [(LVIDd − LVIDs) / LVIDd] × 100 [16]. M-mode estimated ejection fraction was calculated from LVIDd and LVIDs using the Teicholz formula [6]. Left ventricular dimensions were normalized to body weight using the equations of Cornell et al. [17]. Simpson-derived left ventricular ejection fraction was obtained from the apical four-chamber view using the method of discs, with manual tracing of the endocardial border at end-diastole and end-systole. Left ventricular end-systolic volume was indexed to body weight (iLVESV, mL/kg) according to Wess et al. [7]. The mean of three consecutive measurements was used for all echocardiographic variables.

Transmitral E and A wave velocities were obtained from the apical four-chamber view, and the E/A ratio was calculated. Isovolumic relaxation time was measured from the apical five-chamber view. Pulsed-wave tissue Doppler imaging was performed at the lateral mitral annulus to obtain Em and Am velocities, and the E/Em ratio was calculated [18].

Outcome variables

The primary continuous outcomes were the absolute longitudinal changes in Simpson-derived LVEF (ΔLVEF_Simpson), M-mode estimated ejection fraction (ΔLVEF_Mmode), and fractional shortening (ΔFS), each calculated relative to the individual baseline value at each post-baseline assessment. Delta values were defined as follows: ΔLVEF_Simpson = LVEF_Simpson at Dn − LVEF_Simpson at D0; ΔLVEF_Mmode = LVEF_Mmode at Dn − LVEF_Mmode at D0; ΔFS = FS at Dn − FS at D0, where Dn corresponds to each post-baseline time point (D1, D2, and D3). These changes were expressed as absolute differences in percentage points. Because the outcomes were already defined as change from baseline, baseline itself was not included as an outcome observation in the longitudinal delta models.

An additional binary outcome was defined as a decline greater than 10% points in LVEF_Simpson relative to individual baseline, irrespective of whether the absolute value at follow-up remained within conventional reference limits [5]. This threshold was applied at each follow-up assessment and cumulatively to identify dogs with at least one such event.

Complementary exploratory outcomes included longitudinal changes in normalized left ventricular end-systolic diameter and indexed left ventricular end-systolic volume. These variables were analyzed descriptively and were not primary endpoints.

Statistical analysis

Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of residual plots. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), according to distribution. Categorical variables are presented as absolute and relative frequencies. Baseline characteristics between treatment groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and descriptive comparison for categorical variables.

For the longitudinal analysis of echocardiographic systolic variables, linear mixed-effects models were fitted separately for each primary outcome: ΔLVEF_Simpson, ΔLVEF_Mmode, and ΔFS. Delta values were calculated as the value at each post-baseline time point minus the individual baseline value. Therefore, baseline was not included as an outcome observation in these models. Fixed effects included time point (D1, D2, and D3), and a random intercept for each dog was included to account for within-subject correlation over repeated measurements. Time was treated as a categorical variable.

The main analysis included all dogs exposed to doxorubicin, regardless of whether doxorubicin was administered as monotherapy or in combination with other chemotherapeutic agents. Mixed-effects models were used to account for repeated measurements within dogs and to allow inclusion of all available post-baseline observations despite incomplete follow-up. Model assumptions were evaluated by inspection of residual normality, homoscedasticity, and autocorrelation.

Results from the mixed-effects models were expressed as estimated changes, 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p-values. An exploratory subgroup analysis was performed to assess whether the longitudinal trajectory of ΔLVEF_Simpson, ΔLVEF_Mmode, and ΔFS differed between dogs receiving doxorubicin alone and those receiving doxorubicin in combination, using a time-by-group interaction term. Complementary exploratory analyses were performed for normalized left ventricular end-systolic diameter and indexed left ventricular end-systolic volume.

Inter- and intraobserver variability were assessed for left ventricular volumetric measurements using the coefficient of variation, calculated as CV = 100 × (standard deviation / mean of the measurements). Four dogs were randomly selected for this analysis. Left ventricular volume measurements were repeated by the same observer on different days to assess intraobserver variability and were independently performed by two observers to assess interobserver variability.

The frequency of declines greater than 10% points in Simpson-derived LVEF relative to individual baseline was calculated at each post-baseline time point and cumulatively across follow-up. The same threshold was also descriptively applied to M-mode estimated LVEF and fractional shortening to compare the number of assessments and dogs identified by each method. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05. All analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.3; R Core Team).

Results

Study population

Thirty-four dogs were initially enrolled. Five dogs were excluded from the final analysis: four died because of progression of the underlying neoplastic disease after completing only two chemotherapy sessions, and one dog was lost to follow-up because treatment was discontinued. Therefore, a total of 29 client-owned dogs undergoing doxorubicin-based chemotherapy at the Veterinary Hospital of the Federal University of Uberlândia (HV-UFU) and affiliated private oncology clinics were included in the study. Twelve dogs (41.4%) received doxorubicin as a single agent (Doxo group), and 17 (58.6%) received doxorubicin in combination with other chemotherapeutic agents (Combination group). The combination protocols included vincristine (n = 5), cyclophosphamide (n = 5), and carboplatin (n = 7). The doxorubicin dose was not adjusted according to the concomitant cytostatic agent and was maintained at 30 mg/m² per session in all dogs.

The study population was predominantly female (23/29, 79.3%) and mixed breed (13/29, 44.8%). Other breeds represented included Yorkshire Terrier (n = 3), Pug (n = 2), Pitbull (n = 2), Maltese (n = 2), Poodle (n = 2), Shih Tzu (n = 2), Border Collie (n = 1), Golden Retriever (n = 1), and Chow Chow (n = 1). Median age was 10.0 years (IQR: 7.0 to 11.0), and median body weight was 13.2 kg (IQR: 7.0 to 21.6 kg). No statistically significant differences in age or body weight were observed between treatment subgroups (Table 1).

Longitudinal changes in systolic echocardiographic variables

Simpson-derived left ventricular ejection fraction showed a progressive numerical decline over follow-up (Fig. 1). Mean values decreased from 72.0 ± 7.3% at baseline to 70.1 ± 6.9% at D1, 68.3 ± 8.6% at D2, and 66.5 ± 7.7% at D3, corresponding to an overall mean reduction of approximately 5.5% points by the end of follow-up. Median delta values were − 1.89 pp at D1, − 3.46 pp at D2, and − 3.00 pp at D3. In the linear mixed-effects model, estimated changes were directionally negative at all post-baseline time points, although none reached statistical significance individually (Table 3).

Indexed left ventricular end-systolic volume showed a progressive numerical increase from 0.52 ± 0.19 mL/kg at baseline to 0.55 ± 0.25 mL/kg at D1, 0.58 ± 0.28 mL/kg at D2, and 0.67 ± 0.38 mL/kg at D3. The estimated effect at D3 was + 27.18% (95% CI: −1.81 to 56.17; p = 0.065), a result that did not reach statistical significance but was directionally consistent with the decline observed in Simpson-derived left ventricular ejection fraction.

Fractional shortening did not show a consistent decline during follow-up. Mean values were 39.7 ± 9.4% at D0, 41.0 ± 6.1% at D1, 40.4 ± 5.0% at D2, and 41.0 ± 5.4% at D3. Median delta values were + 0.00 pp at D1, − 2.38 pp at D2, and + 1.83 pp at D3. Mixed-effects model estimates were small and non-significant at all time points.

Normalized left ventricular end-systolic diameter also showed no significant longitudinal change. Mean values were 0.79 ± 0.14 at D0, 0.81 ± 0.11 at D1, 0.80 ± 0.13 at D2, and 0.77 ± 0.13 at D3, with no significant effects identified in the mixed-effects model.

Overall, Simpson-derived left ventricular ejection fraction showed a progressive downward numerical trend across follow-up time points, with all mixed-effects model estimates in the negative direction, whereas M-mode estimated left ventricular ejection fraction, fractional shortening, and normalized left ventricular end-systolic diameter did not show comparable directional patterns. No individual time-point estimate reached statistical significance. The progressive increase in indexed left ventricular end-systolic volume was directionally consistent with the decline observed in Simpson-derived left ventricular ejection fraction.

Exploratory subgroup analyses were performed to assess whether longitudinal changes in systolic echocardiographic indices differed between dogs receiving doxorubicin alone and those receiving doxorubicin in combination protocols. No significant time-by-group interactions were identified for Simpson-derived LVEF, M-mode estimated LVEF, or fractional shortening (all interaction p-values > 0.22), indicating that the observed longitudinal patterns did not differ significantly between treatment subgroups.

Frequency of decline greater than 10% points

Across all post-baseline observations, a decline greater than 10% points in Simpson-derived LVEF relative to individual baseline was observed in 13 of 71 assessments (18.3%). In contrast, the same criterion applied to M-mode estimated LVEF identified 7 of 71 assessments (9.9%), and to fractional shortening identified 5 of 71 assessments (7.0%). The frequency of Simpson-derived LVEF decline greater than 10 pp increased progressively over time, from 10.3% at D1 to 20.8% at D2 and 27.8% at D3. No comparable temporal pattern was observed for M-mode estimated LVEF (10.3%, 8.3%, and 11.1%) or for fractional shortening (6.9%, 8.3%, and 5.6%) (Fig. 2).

At the individual-dog level, at least one decline greater than 10% points in Simpson-derived LVEF was identified in 9 of 29 dogs (31.0%). All such declines occurred while absolute Simpson-derived LVEF values remained within conventional reference limits. The same threshold identified 5 dogs (17.2%) by M-mode estimated LVEF and 4 dogs (13.8%) by fractional shortening. Among the 9 dogs identified by Simpson-derived LVEF, 6 were not identified by either of the conventional indices.

Discussion

The main finding of this study was that Simpson-derived LVEF showed a more consistent baseline-referenced downward numerical pattern than M-mode estimated LVEF or fractional shortening in dogs receiving doxorubicin-based chemotherapy. In addition, declines greater than 10% points from individual baseline were identified more frequently by Simpson-derived LVEF, and all such declines occurred while absolute values remained within conventional reference limits. These findings do not establish myocardial injury or diagnostic superiority for doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity; however, they suggest that volumetric assessment may provide complementary information for tracking longitudinal systolic changes in this setting.

The divergence between Simpson-derived LVEF and the conventional indices is consistent with known methodological differences between these approaches [7, 8, 19]. Fractional shortening and M-mode estimated ejection fraction are derived from one-dimensional linear measurements and depend on geometric assumptions about ventricular shape that may not be valid across the morphological diversity of the canine population [7–9]. By contrast, Simpson-derived LVEF is based on volumetric disc summation from apical views and does not rely on assumed ventricular geometry [7]. This distinction is clinically relevant because geometric assumptions may reduce the ability of linear indices to detect subtle systolic changes in dogs undergoing remodeling or exhibiting marked variation in thoracic conformation and body size. In the present study, this methodological difference was reflected in the longitudinal data: Simpson-derived LVEF showed a coherent downward numerical pattern, whereas M-mode estimated LVEF fluctuated around baseline and fractional shortening did not show a consistent decline.

These findings are broadly consistent with previous veterinary studies. Gallay-Lepoutre et al. [4] found that conventional Doppler echocardiographic indices, including fractional shortening, did not identify significant longitudinal changes in dogs receiving doxorubicin despite concurrent evidence of biochemical myocardial stress. Similarly, Hallman et al. [1], in a retrospective study of 494 dogs receiving doxorubicin, reported that a clinically meaningful decline in fractional shortening was consistently detected only after five or more cumulative doses, when myocardial injury may already have been advanced. Taken together, these studies suggest that conventional linear indices may detect cardiotoxic deterioration relatively late during treatment.

All dogs with declines greater than 10% points in Simpson-derived LVEF remained within conventional reference limits at the time of the observed change. Therefore, these dogs would not have been identified as having reduced systolic function if interpretation had been based only on absolute reference intervals. This finding supports the use of serial, baseline-referenced assessment, while also highlighting the uncertainty of interpreting such changes in the absence of validated canine thresholds. In the present study, the > 10%-point threshold was used only as an exploratory, baseline-referenced criterion informed by human cardio-oncology literature [5, 10]. This threshold has not been validated in dogs and should not be interpreted as a diagnostic criterion for canine cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction. Because the final LVEF values remained within conventional reference limits, the clinical and biological significance of these changes remains uncertain.

The progressive numerical increase in indexed left ventricular end-systolic volume, although exploratory and not statistically significant at the conventional threshold, was directionally consistent with the decline observed in Simpson-derived LVEF. This concordance between a volumetric functional index and a complementary volumetric structural variable supports the biological plausibility of the observed pattern. By contrast, normalized left ventricular end-systolic diameter did not show a comparable trajectory, further supporting the possibility that volumetric measures may be more informative than linear measurements for detecting early systolic change in this setting.

Because no independent gold standard for systolic dysfunction or myocardial injury was available, the higher number of dogs identified by Simpson-derived LVEF cannot be interpreted as evidence of diagnostic superiority. The present study was designed to assess longitudinal echocardiographic changes from individual baseline, not to determine the accuracy of each method for detecting doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity. Within this framework, Simpson-derived LVEF appeared to capture changes from baseline more frequently than the conventional indices examined.

Simpson-derived LVEF should also be interpreted in light of its physiological limitations. Like other ejection fraction-based indices, it is influenced by loading conditions and may vary with hydration status, heart rate, afterload, and chemotherapy-associated clinical or hemodynamic changes [5, 10, 11]. Thus, the observed longitudinal reductions should not be interpreted in isolation as definitive evidence of myocardial injury, but rather as echocardiographic systolic changes that warrant further investigation in studies incorporating independent markers of myocardial damage [1, 4, 20].

Measurement variability is an important consideration in serial echocardiographic studies, particularly when longitudinal changes are interpreted relative to individual baseline values. In the present study, intra- and interobserver variability for left ventricular volumetric measurements was low, indicating good repeatability and reproducibility of the Simpson-derived measurements. This finding is consistent with the favorable variability reported by Wess et al. [7] for Simpson-derived volumetric assessment in dogs. Although random variability cannot be entirely excluded, the favorable variability profile observed in the present study supports the reliability of the volumetric measurements and suggests that the longitudinal patterns identified are unlikely to be explained solely by measurement noise. Confirmation in larger cohorts and across different operators and clinical settings remains warranted.

More advanced echocardiographic techniques, such as speckle-tracking-derived global longitudinal strain, may detect myocardial dysfunction even earlier than ejection fraction-based measures. In human cardio-oncology, these methods have demonstrated value for identifying subclinical dysfunction before overt decline in ejection fraction [5, 10, 11, 16]. In veterinary medicine, Wolf et al. [21] reported subclinical systolic abnormalities in dogs with lymphoma using speckle tracking that were not captured by conventional indices. Because strain analysis was not available in the present study, future investigations combining Simpson-derived LVEF with myocardial deformation imaging may help define the most sensitive echocardiographic strategy for early identification of doxorubicin-associated systolic change in dogs.

The present study has several limitations. The modest sample size limited statistical power and reduced the precision of longitudinal estimates. The population was clinically heterogeneous with respect to tumor type, chemotherapy protocol, and treatment interval, which may have introduced variability in cardiac response. Although longitudinal comparisons between treatment groups were performed, subgroup comparisons were exploratory and underpowered because treatment classification reflected routine clinical management and subgroup sizes were small. No robust non-doxorubicin control group was available; therefore, the contribution of neoplasia-related factors, systemic illness, or treatment-associated effects other than doxorubicin cannot be fully excluded.

In addition, no independent reference standard for myocardial injury or clinically validated cardiotoxicity endpoint was available. Cardiac biomarkers, particularly cardiac troponin I, were not measured, and strain analysis or advanced cardiac imaging was not performed. Their concurrent assessment would have provided complementary evidence of myocardial injury and strengthened the interpretation of the observed echocardiographic changes [1, 20]. Although inter- and intraobserver variability for left ventricular volumetric measurements were low in the present study, reproducibility should be confirmed in larger cohorts and across different operators and clinical settings.

Future prospective studies with larger cohorts, more homogeneous treatment protocols, non-doxorubicin control groups, cardiac biomarkers, myocardial deformation imaging, and clinically validated endpoints are warranted to confirm these findings and to define clinically meaningful thresholds for longitudinal systolic change in dogs receiving potentially cardiotoxic chemotherapy.

Conclusions

In this cohort of dogs receiving doxorubicin-based chemotherapy, Simpson-derived left ventricular ejection fraction captured more baseline-referenced longitudinal echocardiographic systolic changes than M-mode estimated ejection fraction and fractional shortening. Declines greater than 10% points from individual baseline occurred in 9 of 29 dogs (31.0%), and all remained within conventional reference limits. These observations suggest that Simpson-derived LVEF may provide complementary information for serial echocardiographic monitoring in dogs receiving doxorubicin. However, they should not be interpreted as proof of true doxorubicin-induced myocardial injury or diagnostic superiority for cardiotoxicity detection. Further studies are needed to confirm these findings and clarify their clinical relevance.
