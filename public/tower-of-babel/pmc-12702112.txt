Scale-dependent drivers of ecosystem service supply-flow-demand couplings in the Shanxi Yellow River Basin, China
Authors: Shurong Wu, Ruobin Li, Yuying Yan, Ziqiang Du, Zhitao Wu, Keming Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryIntegrating ecosystem service (ES) supply-flow-demand relationships across multiple scales is crucial for effective ecological management. Using the Shanxi Yellow River Basin as a case study, we evaluated six ESs at the grid, watershed, and county scales in 2020. By applying redundancy analysis and extreme gradient boosting, we identified key drivers and simulated ES flows. Results show: (1) overall ES supply meets demand, with most ESs clustering in the northwest and southeast, while crop production dominates central lowlands; (2) supply-demand mismatches exhibit scale-dependent effects, showing fine-grained details at grid level but amplified heterogeneity at broader scales; (3) while main drivers (temperature, precipitation, population density, GDP) remain constant across scales, their influence intensity varies markedly; (4) ES flows display distinct directions, with recreation and crops moving urban-to-rural, while other ESs oppositely. This framework offers a transferable approach for watershed-scale sustainability planning in regions facing ecological-economic trade-offs.Subject areas: Earth sciences, environmental science, environmental issues

Introduction

Over the past five decades, human-induced impacts on ecosystems have intensified, becoming more widespread and pronounced than at any other time in history.1,2 These disruptions threaten the sustainable development of interconnected natural and socioeconomic systems and are projected to have even broader implications in the future. Ecosystem services (ESs)—the benefits that humans obtain directly or indirectly from natural ecosystems—are widely recognized as a crucial framework for linking human well-being and environmental systems in resource management.3 Recent studies on ES have emphasized conceptual development, indicator classification,4,5 quantification, valuation,6 and analyses of trade-offs and synergies.7,8 Increasing global attention has been directed toward understanding the complex relationship between ES supply and human demand,9 with this gap particularly evident in regions where tensions between natural resource availability and socioeconomic development are acute.10 Such imbalances jeopardize sustainable social-ecological development. A spatial mismatch between ES supply and demand (ESSD) arises across regions due to unequal natural resource endowments and uneven socioeconomic development.11 Consequently, the ESs available within a region are often not effectively utilized to meet the needs of its population.12

Researchers have increasingly focused on ecosystem service flows (ESFs) to more comprehensively characterize mismatches among the processes underlying ES supply, demand, and spatial transmission.13,14 ESFs describe the spatial linkages between service-providing areas (SPAs) and service-beneficiary areas (SBAs).15 By clarifying the processes of ES provision, flow, and utilization, ESFs can guide more effective ecosystem management and decision-making.16 Strengthening the understanding of ESFs is also critical for advancing spatial management, enhancing ES provision, and examining how different combinations of natural, social, and ecological drivers influence ES dynamics and shift supply-demand balances. Distinct ESSD scenarios can generate positive or negative responses depending on social-ecological factors. These responses may vary in magnitude across spatial dimensions, potentially displaying nonlinear characteristics and threshold effects that could accelerate ES degradation. Building on prior findings, the effective management of social-ecological systems may require targeted spatial interventions tailored to specific drivers. Such interventions address spatial variability in ESSD and provide insights for mitigating conflicts between social development and environmental sustainability.

The quantification of the ES supply has primarily relied on energy analysis, monetary valuation, and material quality evaluation methods. Material quantity evaluation methods include InVEST, RUSLE, SWAT, and ARIES.17 Among these, InVEST is the most widely applied and has been successfully implemented in numerous ecological management cases.18 For ES demand assessment, commonly employed methods include matrix models, ecological footprint analysis, public participation techniques, monetary approaches, and socioeconomic indicators integrating population, land use, nightlight, and GDP intensity. Widely used indicators such as the supply-demand ratio (ESDR) and coupling coordination models19 are applied to elucidate ESSD relationships. Some researchers have also incorporated the distance-decay concept from geography into gravity models, modifying the formulas to more accurately measure the strength and extent of ESFs across regions and spatial scales. The most advanced models in this domain include the breakpoint and field strength models.20,21 Analytical techniques such as geographical detectors, redundancy analysis, geographically weighted regressions, and structural equation modeling22 are commonly used to explore ES drivers. More recently, advances in machine learning have further reshaped approaches to ES driver analysis.

Substantial progress has been made in studying ES supply-flow-demand (SFD). However, several limitations persist: (1) although scholars have mainly quantified multiple ESSDs at a single scale, such as continental, national, basin, urban, or urban agglomeration scales,23 a key challenge lies in aligning the scale of ES supply with the scale of demand.24 Most existing studies rely on static assessments, overlooking temporal dynamics and cross-scale interactions that define real-world ES delivery and utilization. (2) In addition, most prior research on ESSD balance has depended on the ESDR, neglecting the trade-offs and synergies that shape ES interactions.25 Integrating trade-off and synergy mechanisms into ESSD assessments is essential for addressing diverse regional needs, optimizing resource allocation, and supporting effective ESFs. (3) Current research on the dynamics of ESFs across multiple scales remains largely conceptual, with limited quantitative analysis. A particularly evident gap exists in spatially explicit simulation and the study of scale-related mechanisms. Further empirical investigations are needed to refine the understanding and measurement of ESFs.26 and (4) Finally, although interest in the social-ecological factors influencing ESSD balance is growing, few studies have systematically evaluated the driving mechanisms behind ESSD mismatch patterns. The formation of ESSD mismatches is a complex process involving natural, social, and ecological factors. Examining how these factors interact and influence ESSD is crucial for developing effective ecosystem management strategies.

To address these gaps, this study employs the Shanxi Yellow River Basin (SYRB) as a case study to examine the matching relationships between ESSD at grid, watershed, and county scales, simulate ESFs, and generate insights for ES management. The SYRB (Figure 1), where tensions between development and protection are especially pronounced, exhibits marked spatial imbalances in ESSD.27 This occurs largely because most areas in the basin are ecologically sensitive, whereas industrialization and urbanization are concentrated within limited plains. In addition, the scale of ES demand does not align with the scale of management institutions, placing the basin at risk of ecological overload. Accurately estimating ESSD and clarifying the direction and intensity of ESFs at different scales are therefore essential. Assessing the driving mechanisms of ESSD matching is also pivotal for sustainable regional development and critical for optimizing coordinated resource utilization while promoting harmonious human-nature relationships. The research objectives are as follows: (1) examine scale-dependent mismatches in ESSD; (2) incorporate trade-off and synergy analyses into ESSD assessments; (3) quantify ESFs using an enhanced gravity model; and (4) apply interpretable machine learning to unravel the nonlinear and interactive effects of social-ecological drivers on ESSD patterns. Through this approach, we aim to provide a more robust, dynamic, and policy-relevant framework to support spatial planning and ecological governance in regional social-ecological systems.

Schematic diagram of the SYRB

This study proposes an innovative “three-scale cascade” analytical framework that systematically addresses the cross-scale matching challenge in ES assessment by integrating a nested assessment system with spatially explicit modeling techniques. Methodologically, the framework first establishes a multi-scale, linked assessment system: on the supply side, 1 km grids serve as the basic calculation unit and are aggregated upward to the watershed and county levels, while on the demand side, population-weighted dasymetric mapping is applied to accurately align statistical data with assessment units. For ESF simulation, differentiated transmission paths are designed for different service types: soil conservation (SC) and water yield (WY) rely on hydrological corridors, outdoor recreation (OR) and crop production (CP) are based on transportation networks, and an improved gravity model is applied at the watershed and county levels to quantify cross-scale ESFs. The innovative contributions of this framework are reflected in three dimensions: (1) Theoretical Advancement: introduces a cross-scale SFD cascade framework that integrates ESSD matching with ESF dynamics, enabling hierarchical governance; (2) Empirical Innovation: reveals scale-specific ES trade-off-synergy regimes in the SYRB, showing how biophysical-socioeconomic couplings produce governance-relevant mismatches; and (3) Methodological Breakthrough: develops a scalable analytical pipeline that combines multi-source data, machine learning, and redundancy analysis (RDA) to disentangle multi-scale driver hierarchies, offering transferable tools for global river basins.

Results

Construction of the interaction network of ecosystem service supply and demand at multiple scales

Mapping the ecosystem service supply and demand

In terms of ES supply (Figure 2), the average supply values of habitat quality (HQ), SC, OR, WY, carbon sequestration (CS), and CP in the SYRB were 0.62 (dimensionless), 104.34 t/hm2, 0.24 km2/km2, 136.65 mm, 121.05 t/hm2, and 3.21 t/hm2, respectively.

The spatial distribution of ESSD in the SYRB

At the grid scale, HQ, SC, OR, WY, and CS exhibited particularly high values in the northwest and southeast of the SYRB (Taihang Mountains and Lüliang Mountains). In contrast, low values were widely distributed across the central basin and southwestern plain. Meanwhile, CP was the main service in the central basin and southwestern plain of the SYRB. (2) At the watershed scale, the HQ, SC, OR, WY, and CS values were concentrated in watersheds II, III, and VI, whereas lower values were mainly found in watersheds IV and V, where the CP values were relatively higher. (3) At the county scale, high values of HQ, SC, OR, WY, and CS were distributed in the northeastern counties of Lüliang City and Xinzhou City, the southeastern counties of Changzhi City, and the eastern counties of Jincheng City, which are located in the Taihang and Lüliang Mountains, boasting abundant forest resources and remarkable ecological strengths, such as habitat maintenance and climate regulation.

In terms of ES demand, the average demand values of HQ, SC, OR, WY, CS, and CP in the SYRB were 0.45 (dimensionless), 14.53 t/hm2, 0.14 km2/km2, 50.22 mm, 37.57 t/hm2, and 3.84 t/hm2, respectively.

At the grid scale, demand for HQ, OR, WY, CS, and CP was particularly high in the central and southwestern plains of the SYRB, where cities are concentrated and economies are developed. In contrast, SC demand was strongest in the mountainous areas of the northwest and southeast. (2) At the watershed scale, demand for HQ, OR, WY, CS, and CP was relatively high in watersheds IV and V, largely because of high population concentrations in these regions. In contrast, demand was relatively low in watersheds II, III, and VI, with the notable exception of SC, which exhibited high demand therein owing to significant soil erosion risks. (3) At the county scale, high demand for HQ, OR, WY, CS, and CP was concentrated in Taiyuan, Jinzhong, Changzhi, Linfen, and Yuncheng—areas that have experienced rapid economic development and high population density. High-demand areas for SC were located in the mountainous county towns of Lüliang City and Xinzhou City, where soil erosion is severe.

This study revealed significant similarities in ESSD spatial patterns across different scales. At all scales, both supply and demand for SC were high in the northwestern and southwestern regions of the SYRB, with no evident conflicts. This pattern is largely due to the steep slopes of the Taihang and Lüliang Mountains, which are highly susceptible to soil erosion under heavy rainfall and complex terrain conditions.9 However, vegetation in these mountainous areas effectively controls erosion through canopy structures and root systems that stabilize the soil.28 In mountainous regions, HQ, OR, WY, and CS were more prominent, whereas CP dominated in the plains. Forests, as key habitats for diverse species, also act as major carbon sinks. They conserve water, regulate the hydrological cycle, and provide extensive green spaces. Except for SC, the demand intensity of other ES decreased radially from the urban core outward, showing a strong correlation with GDP and population density.

Matching of ecosystem service supply and demand

In the SYRB, the mean ESDR was consistent across scales and ranked in descending order as follows: SC (0.07) > HQ (0.03) > CS (0.02) > WY (0.01) > OR (0.00) > CP (−0.03). With the exception of CP, all ES types achieved self-sufficiency in the SYRB. Specifically, OR and WY were the most balanced in terms of ESDR, whereas SC and CP showed the greatest imbalance.

The ESDR at the grid, watershed, and county scales has similar mean values but different ranges and spatial variability due to scale-dependent characteristics. The grid scale can capture the heterogeneity of ESSD at the local level (especially the large differences in soil supply and demand capacities between mountainous areas and river valleys in SC, resulting in a wide range of values). The watershed scale integrated complete hydrological units, and the complementary nature of upstream and downstream ESDR (upstream supply offsetting downstream demand) narrows the range of values. The county scale, with its administrative boundaries, led to higher ESDR in counties containing complete forests, while counties spanning urban and rural areas have lower overall values due to the high demand in built-up areas.

The mismatch between ESSD exhibited distinct spatial scale effects (Figure 3A). At the grid scale, surplus areas of HQ, SC, OR, WY, and CS were located in the northwest and southeast of the SYRB, where local demand was met. However, CP showed deficits in these same areas. At the watershed scale (Figure 3B), surplus areas of HQ, SC, OR, WY, and CS were primarily concentrated in ecological zones (forests, grasslands, and wetlands) within Watersheds II, III, and VI. In contrast, deficits were concentrated in population-dense regions of Watersheds IV and V, particularly in urban centers. Although deficit areas expanded overall at this scale, spatial complementarity between upstream supply and downstream demand helped maintain balance in ESSD. At the county scale (Figure 3C), remote mountainous counties largely achieved self-sufficiency, but mismatches persisted in densely populated and developed counties and their surrounding areas. Deficits in HQ, SC, OR, WY, and CS were predominantly concentrated in the central regions of Taiyuan, Linfen, and Yuncheng.

Overall, as the scale shifted from the grid to the watershed and then to the county, the distribution of surplus and deficit areas became more clearly differentiated. Surplus areas were primarily concentrated in the Taihang and Lüliang Mountains, while deficit areas were increasingly concentrated in major urban centers. The watershed scale tended to amplify spatial mismatches, whereas the county scale allowed for the precise identification of cities and counties with ES deficits.

In general, ESSD mismatches were more concentrated at the watershed and county scales but more dispersed at the grid scale, which aligns with the findings of Yin et al.9 Multi-scale assessment can capture spatial heterogeneity of ESSD mismatches at the grid scale, providing a strong reference for the scientific and targeted management of different ES. At the same time, it reveals spatial aggregation at the watershed and county scales, reflecting the supply of key ES. It is important to note that ESSD matching reflects the alignment between regional natural resources and social demand. However, institutional boundaries, social contexts, and ecological scales are often inconsistent, creating disconnections in natural resource management at specific administrative levels and scales.

Measuring the correlation between ecosystem service supply, demand, and ecosystem service supply-demand ratio

Correlation analyses of ES supply, demand, and ESDR were conducted at three spatial scales, revealing trade-off and synergistic relationships between six ES types. The correlations between ES pairs varied as the scale shifted from the grid to the watershed and county levels.

In terms of ES supply (Columns A and B in Figure 4), all ES pairs passed the significance test (p < 0.05) at the grid scale. The strongest synergy occurred between HQ and CS, whereas the most pronounced trade-off was observed between HQ and CP. Among the 8 ES pairs with trade-off effects (HQ-CP, SC-WY, SC-OR, SC-CP, OR-WY, OR-CP, WY-CP, and CS-CP), synergy was observed in 7 pairs (HQ-SC, HQ-OR, HQ-WY, HQ-CS, SC-OR, SC-CS, and WY-CS). At the watershed scale, only 8 ES pairs passed the significance test (p < 0.05), showing more intense and differentiated trade-offs and synergies. Directional changes in these relationships were relatively minor. Only the relationships between WY and SC, and between WY and OR, shifted from synergy to a trade-off as the scale changed. The synergy between HQ and SC was the strongest, and the trade-off between HQ and CP was the most significant.

At the county scale, trade-offs and synergies between most ES pairs continued to intensify, with relatively high absolute values of correlation coefficients. 12 ES pairs passed the significance test (p < 0.05), with the strongest synergy observed between HQ and CS and the most pronounced trade-off between HQ and CP. Apart from ES pairs that did not pass the statistical significance test, the trade-off and synergy patterns were consistent with those identified at the grid scale. Across all three scales, ES pairs that consistently exhibited synergies included HQ-SC, HQ-OR, HQ-WY, HQ-CS, SC-OR, SC-CS, and WY-CS. The correlation coefficients between CP and the other 5 ESs decreased across the three scales, either consistently or with fluctuations, but overall, the trade-off effect was amplified.

Almost all ES demand pairs passed the significance test (p < 0.05). Consistent trade-offs and synergistic relationships were observed across all three scales, reflecting the strong link between ES demand and human well-being (Columns C and D in Figure 4). The strongest trade-offs occurred between SC and OR, WY, CS, and CP, whereas the remaining ES pairs exhibited synergistic relationships. Among these, the strongest synergy was observed between HQ and CP.

Regarding ESDR (Columns E and F in Figure 4), the overall direction of the trade-off and synergies was consistent across the three scales, although ESSD relationships and scale effects influenced the degree of correlation. At the grid scale, 12 ES pairs were significant (p < 0.05). HQ exhibited trade-offs with SC, WY, CS, and CP, whereas CP showed trade-offs with SC and OR. The remaining ES pairs exhibited synergies, with the strongest trade-off occurring between HQ and CP—exactly the opposite of what was observed for ES demand. At the watershed scale, only six ES pairs were significantly correlated. Among them, CP showed trade-offs with HQ and OR, whereas OR exhibited synergies with HQ and WY, and SC exhibited synergies with WY and CS. At the county scale, both trade-offs and synergies were magnified. Trade-offs mainly occurred between HQ, OR, WY, and CP. The surpluses of HQ, OR, and WY were opposed to the surplus of CP, suggesting that social-ecological constraints limit the possibility of simultaneous oversupply of all ESs in the same area. Focusing excessively on maximizing the CP surpluses may therefore pose risks by undermining the supply-demand balance of HQ, OR, and WY. Overall, synergies between ES pairs were generally more favorable than trade-offs. As the scale shifted from the grid to the watershed and county scales, the intensity of the trade-offs and synergies increased, and in some cases, surpluses or deficits were also amplified.

Simulation of ecosystem service flows at multiple scales

The directions and intensities of ESFs were quantified by spatially matching the ESSD to identify SPAs, SBAs, and multi-scale ESF patterns. At the grid scale (Figure 5A), the numbers of flows for HQ, SC, OR, WY, CS, and CP were 75, 89, 70, 89, 90, and 70, respectively. HQ and CS exhibited omnidirectional flows, moving from areas of high supply to those of high demand. Although HQ itself does not involve direct spatial transfer, its ecological benefits can extend to wider regions through mechanisms such as species migration, seed dispersal, and the diffusion of ecological processes.29 These mechanisms produce effects analogous to those of other ESFs. CS primarily diffuses and flows through atmospheric and hydrological cycles, thereby contributing to air purification and climate regulation. In contrast, SC and WY were unidirectional services dominated by natural pathways flowing downstream along rivers and tributaries under the influence of gravity. OR and CP, by contrast, followed human-made transportation networks, including major railways, highways, and waterways. Notably, OR flowed from demand areas to supply areas, requiring individuals to actively approach supply locations to experience them. Except for OR and CP, the remaining ES flows moved from high-supply regions in the northwest and southeast toward the central urban areas in the southwest and the middle of SYRB.

At the watershed scale (Figure 5B), the numbers of flows for HQ, SC, OR, WY, CS, and CP were 117, 133, 107, 135, 75, and 92, respectively. ESFs occurred not only within but also between sub-watersheds (Figure 5D). The direction and magnitude of flows varied across different ES. HQ, WY, and CS primarily flowed from watersheds I, II, III, and VI toward watersheds IV and V. SC also flowed from watersheds I, II, and III toward watersheds IV and V. Residents in watersheds IV and V actively moved toward watersheds I and III to obtain OR services. CP primarily flowed from suburban areas into demand centers, with watersheds IV and V serving as major food suppliers for watersheds I, II, III, and VI. Except for OR and CP services, the flow directions of the remaining ES were consistently from watersheds I, II, III, and VI toward watersheds IV and V.

At the county scale (Figure 5C), HQ, SC, OR, WY, CS, and CP flows were 128, 146, 139, 102, 97, and 78, respectively. HQ, WY, and CS in Linfen and Yuncheng were primarily supplied by suburban areas. In addition to the abundant internal flows within the counties and cities themselves, smaller contributions originated from Xinzhou, Lüliang, Changzhi, and Jincheng (Figure 5E). OR flows mainly occurred across cities, with residents of Taiyuan, Linfen, and Yuncheng actively moving to Xinzhou, Lüliang, and Jincheng for leisure and entertainment. By contrast, CP exhibited relatively low flow density because limited surpluses remained after meeting the demands of the source areas themselves.

Classified according to the mobility of supply and demand subjects, the ESFs of HQ, SC, WY, and CS were supply-mobile service flows, whereas those of OR and CP were use-mobile service flows. Consequently, OR required users to actively obtain services through transportation or other means.15 At the grid scale, ESFs were closer to their original and undisturbed flow paths. At the watershed and county scales, however, the division of SPAs and SBAs meant that ESFs did not capture actual flow trajectories but instead reflected simplified spatial transfer mechanisms between regions. Multi-scale analysis revealed that ESFs generally flowed toward areas with population agglomeration and economic development. This pattern was most evident at the county scale, where the more developed cities displayed greater ESF density.

Identification of social-ecological drivers at multiple scales

Using RDA and the interpretable machine learning model XGBoost, this study revealed the correlations between ESSD characteristics at different scales (grid, watershed, and county) in 2020 and their social-ecological drivers. Drivers included annual mean temperature (Tem), precipitation (Pre), actual evapotranspiration (ET), digital elevation model (DEM), slope, normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), percentage of clay in topsoil (Tclay), percentage of sand in topsoil (Tsand), population density (POP), gross domestic product (GDP), and nighttime light (NTL).

As shown in Figure 6A, at the grid scale, natural and social factors jointly influenced the ESSD. Climatic factors (Tem, Pre, and ET), biophysical factors (NDVI and DEM), and economic factors (POP and GDP) affected the distribution pattern of ESSD by influencing HQ, SC, and CP. At the watershed scale, the influence of natural factors was relatively prominent. Precipitation, elevation, and soil factors mainly affected ESSD distribution patterns by intervening in HQ and SC. At the county scale, socioeconomic factors (GDP and POP) were more prominent, influencing the supply-demand distribution patterns of ES by affecting HQ and CP. Across the three scales, temperature and precipitation positively affected ES surpluses, whereas elevation and slope may increase ES deficits. Socioeconomic factors (GDP and POP) were positively correlated with CP but negatively correlated with all other ES. The relationships between drivers and ESSD became clearer as the scale changes. The supply and demand of CP were more closely related to socioeconomic factors (GDP and POP), and the strength of this correlation increased with a change in scale. However, the influence of these factors was limited.

The dominant drivers varied across scales (Figures 6B and 6D). Natural ecological factors were influential at all scales, accounting for the largest proportion (58.8%) at the watershed scale. Socioeconomic drivers (POP, GDP, and NTL) were negatively correlated with ESDR across all scales and exerted the greatest influence at the county scale, where they accounted for 47.4%. At the grid scale, the proportions of the different influencing factors were more evenly distributed. The predictive performance of machine learning models was evaluated and compared across three scales: grid, watershed, and county. As shown in Figure 6C, the county scale demonstrated the most stable performance, whereas the watershed scale achieved the highest precision.

Overall, the ESSD of the SYRB depended on the interaction between biophysical ecosystem supply and human demand at specific spatiotemporal scales, shaped by both natural and socioeconomic contexts. At the grid scale, the drivers of mismatches were complex and diverse, reflecting the heterogeneity of land-use types. Ecological deficit areas at this scale were typically contiguous zones of construction land rather than fragmented patches. In contrast, at the watershed and county scales, the drivers influencing mismatches were more uniform. Regions often shared similar natural ecological or socioeconomic characteristics, such as resource endowments, population distribution, and governance models.

Discussion

Scale effects of ecosystem service supply and demand

Most ESSD assessments are conducted at a single scale. However, this approach often fails to fully capture the complexity of the phenomenon, leaving decision-makers without a stable foundation for action. The mismatch of ESSD is closely related to the mismatches among the supply, process, and management scales. The choice of assessment scale strongly influences both the scope and the outcomes of analysis. Nevertheless, empirical studies on the scale effects of ESSD and their implications for ecosystem management strategies remain relatively limited.23

This study highlights the complexity of ESSD mismatches in the SYRB, which stem from both natural constraints and fragmented governance systems. ESs such as SC and CS follow ecological boundaries such as watersheds and forests, yet are managed within administrative boundaries such as provinces and cities. This misalignment is particularly evident in the governance of the SYRB.30 The cities pursue divergent objectives, creating conflicts between upstream conservation and downstream development. Such fragmentation undermines ecosystem integrity and corroborates previous research on governance challenges in the SYRB.27 Furthermore, the region’s role as a national energy base exacerbates policy conflicts. For instance, coal production plans directly contradict ecosystem protection goals. Moreover, the prevailing emphasis on economic development weakens local efforts to safeguard ecological resources. There is also a lack of effective mechanisms to encourage cooperation across administrative regions.31 Downstream areas often benefit from upstream services such as WY without compensating the upstream providers, undermining local efforts to pursue sustainable management.32 Although a payment for ecosystem services (PESs) scheme has been proposed, its implementation remains limited due to insufficient funding and unclear responsibility allocation.33 In addition, local governments lack practical tools for cross-regional ESSD assessment, and fragmented data management systems hinder comprehensive ecological flow monitoring,34 Together, these challenges exacerbate the ESSD scale mismatch.

ESSD assessments conducted at different scales may yield divergent results. Figure 7 clearly illustrates the challenge of scale mismatch. Management levels operate across a continuum that begins with the ecosystem scale and extends to the social system scale, navigating between the management of natural resources and the regulation of ES supply. This framework highlights the spatial misalignment between SPAs and SBAs, which represents a fundamental cause of potential conflicts in ES applications.35 This study revealed the spatial scale effects of ESSD mismatches. Beyond influences from patches, local areas, regions, countries, and the global scale at the landscape pattern level, ES dynamics are also shaped by finer divisions under the urban-planning scale, including sites, neighborhoods, blocks, urban areas, and cities. Addressing scaling issues is therefore essential when integrating ES into policy and planning processes.

Multi-scale conceptual framework

Adopting a multi-scale approach that aligns ecological, social, and governance scales with ecosystem management—while considering individual levels and integrating cross-scale characteristics—can generate insights that single-scale assessments often overlook. Administrative scales (e.g., townships, counties, cities, and provinces) provide practical platforms for policy implementation at corresponding governance levels. In contrast, assessments at the landscape and microscale levels enable more precise ecological restoration and conservation efforts. Such integration offers a more robust foundation for ES policy development and implementation.

This study aimed to integrate relevant characteristics across environmental, historical, social, and cultural dimensions through multi-scale evaluations. Accordingly, it sought to provide scientific strategies tailored to different scales for ecological management. In recent years, substantial experience has been gained in assessing ES supply at the landscape scale, where natural ecological factors play a dominant role (Figure 6D). However, when multiple stakeholders are involved or when ecological compensation is considered, the administrative scale is often more effective for fostering cooperation and decision-making. To address this, the present study developed the multi-scale SFD framework to generate targeted management strategies for decision-makers. Practical experience indicates that scientific hierarchical management informed by ESSD relationships and scale-specific characteristics offers the most effective pathway for achieving ecological protection and sustainable development goals.

Insights into ecosystem service flows

Importance of ecosystem service flows

A mere understanding of ESSD within a region is insufficient for effective policy implementation. Exploring the cascade process of SFD is crucial to maintaining and enhancing human well-being, which is the key to integrating SPAs and SBAs.36 Deficit regions, characterized by ES demand exceeding supply, inevitably seek ES from other areas to sustain essential societal functions. Consequently, ESF analysis aids in balancing regional ESSD while promoting ecological flows. It also supports policymakers in identifying the potential and extent of interregional ecological collaboration, thereby providing a foundation for ecological compensation. Although current studies simulate the spatial flow processes of ES, the threshold for translating these findings into practice is relatively high. Therefore, simpler, more operational, and generalizable ESF models are urgently needed.

This study applied different methodologies to simulate multi-scale ESFs. At the grid scale, ESFs were quantified using a simplified path-transmission-mechanism modeling, which emphasized the directional characteristics of transmission media, such as the atmosphere, rivers, organisms, soil, and human activities.37 At the watershed and county scales, the breakpoint formula was employed to determine the direction and magnitude of ESFs between SPAs and SBAs. Natural geographical features and spatial accessibility were integrated to optimize ESFs simulation, for example, by refining SC and WY flows according to upstream-downstream relationships within the sub-watersheds.

This integrated approach addresses the limitations of existing models by combining process-driven simulations with spatial optimization frameworks (Figure 8), thereby providing actionable insights for cross-regional ecological management. The study shows that although the direction, velocity, volume, and spatial characteristics of ESFs vary across scales, overall spatial consistency emerges. For example, ESFs at all scales indicate that, with the exception of CP, ES predominantly flows from the suburban areas of the Taihang and Lüliang Mountains toward central urban areas, basins, and plains, as well as from less-developed counties to core cities. Although the ESF pathways were simplified by considering distance, the directional characteristics aligned with the flow patterns documented in other regions.38 Furthermore, economically developed core cities received a disproportionately high influx of ES. At the county scale, ESF variations exhibited stronger urban concentration effects; however, these patterns did not consistently appear with the same direction or magnitude at the grid or watershed scales. Large-scale ESF analyses often overlook the influence of hierarchical landscape structures, ecosystems, and non-linear interactions among ecological patches.

Management strategies

To optimize the spatial mismatch of ESFs from the Taihang and Lüliang Mountains to downstream urban and plain areas, this study proposes an integrated ecological governance framework that combines ecological compensation (EC) mechanisms with multi-scale spatial planning strategies. This framework is structured around three key components:

EC policies should be differentiated according to the characteristics of ESFs. For widely diffused ESs such as HQ and CS, a regional EC mechanism should be established based on the spatial range of atmospheric and hydrological transport. Beneficiary cities (e.g., Linfen and Yuncheng) should provide cross-city EC to upstream areas and ecological functional zones, including the Lüliang and Taihang Mountains. The compensation standards may be quantified according to ESF volumes and regional GDP.39 For pathway-dependent services such as WY and SC, watershed-level longitudinal EC should be strengthened. Downstream water-use regions (e.g., watersheds IV and V) should serve as the main compensators, providing EC funds to upstream water source areas and key SC zones (e.g., watersheds I, II, and III). These funds should support ecological projects such as constructing water source conservation forests, restoring wetlands, and maintaining terraced fields. The EC amounts may be calculated according to runoff regulation and SC benefits.

A multi-scale ecological infrastructure network should be established to strengthen ESF connectivity. At the grid scale, natural flow pathways must be protected, and key ecological nodes (e.g., river corridors and habitat stepping stones) should be identified and restored to maintain the natural diffusion channels of services such as HQ and CS. At the watershed scale, ESFs between sub-watersheds should be coordinated. Interception wetlands and filter strips should be developed in critical convergence areas (e.g., watersheds IV and V) to enhance ecosystem service capture capacity and reduce reliance on external regions. At the county scale, land-use spatial layouts should be optimized, and the dominant ES functions of each county should be clarified. Ecological supply counties (e.g., Xinzhou and Lüliang) should focus on strengthening WY, CS, and SC capacities, whereas urban agglomeration counties (e.g., Taiyuan and Linfen) should strictly regulate development intensity and minimize excessive dependence on surrounding ESs.

Coordinated regulation of both supply and demand sides should be implemented to reduce ESSD mismatches. On the supply side, key ecological corridors—including water conservation zones and wildlife migration passages—should be identified and incorporated into territorial spatial planning, building upon existing protected areas in Taihang and Lüliang Mountains. On the demand side, core cities should rigorously enforce the three-line spatial control system, which includes urban development boundaries, ecological protection redlines, and permanent basic farmland,21 while formulating differentiated land use policies tailored to the requirements of specific ESs to ensure balanced utilization and sustainable management.

Framework establishes cross-regional compensation mechanisms tailored to different ESF types, clarifies EC standards and payment pathways according to the characteristics of specific ecosystem services, and integrates ecological infrastructure and spatial control measures across grid, watershed, and county scales. By combining EC with territorial spatial planning, the framework promotes synergy between supply enhancement and demand regulation, thereby reinforcing regional ecological security and advancing sustainable development capacity.

Driving factors of ecosystem service supply and demand mismatches

A comprehensive understanding of how social-ecological factors influence ESSD is essential for achieving and sustaining resilient social-ecological systems. Within the SYRB, diverse social-ecological drivers significantly affected the six types of ES and their complex interrelationships. As the scale shifts from the grid to the watershed and then to the county scale, the relative contribution of drivers to ESSD alterations increases. Nevertheless, the direction of most driver-ESSD relationships remains consistent across scales. At the grid scale, the necessity and rationality of ecosystem management can be clearly verified. ES supply depends on natural, biophysical, and social factors, although natural drivers remain dominant. At the watershed scale, ESSD is primarily influenced by physical geographical conditions and ecological changes. For example, NDVI is a positive factor that enhances ES surpluses. Increasing vegetation cover improves plant biomass and soil carbon storage in the soil, thereby facilitating more effective ecological management.40

The supply of ES is primarily positively driven by natural factors such as NDVI, Pre, Tem, and DEM, while human activities, represented by NTL, GDP, and POP, suppress the supply by encroaching on ecological spaces (Figure S1). Therefore, in this study, services such as HQ, SC, OR, WY, and CS are mainly distributed in the Taihang and Lüliang Mountains, which have higher terrain elevation and extensive vegetation cover. Demand, on the other hand, is mainly dominated by socio-economic factors (POP, GDP, and NTL), with limited influence from natural conditions. As a result, high demand values are mainly concentrated in areas with high population density and developed economies. The spatial mismatch of ESSD is essentially a demand-led process. The aggregation of human activities in space and the uneven consumption of resources lead to demand far exceeding the local ecological supply capacity, thus forming an imbalance characterized by shortages. Notably, high levels of GDP and POP tend to intensify human encroachment on croplands, forests, and grasslands, thereby diminishing key supply ESs such as WY and regulating ESs such as SC and CS, and strengthening their trade-offs with CP. Furthermore, the relationships of a given ES with HQ and those with OR or CS often involve similar dominant factors, likely due to strong spatial synergies among these ESs. Similarly, comparable drivers tend to govern interactions involving SC and WY. It is also observed that correlations between CP and other ESs are frequently dominated by socioeconomic factors and generally exhibit trade-off relationships (Figure S2).

Additionally, soil exerts only a minor influence on ES (Column A in Figure 9), likely because vegetation in the study area is more strongly constrained by topography than by soil conditions.41 At the county scale, socioeconomic factors play a more prominent role. For example, human activities are significantly negatively correlated with HQ, SC, OR, WY, and CS, thereby contributing to ES deficits. Rapid socioeconomic and urban expansion has inevitably transformed suburban land into construction land, intensifying conflicts between economic development and ecological protection, thereby affecting human benefits from nature. However, the impact of human activity is not universally negative. Accelerated social development has also enhanced CP, which shows a significant upward trend over time. Regarding the interactions of drivers (Column B in Figure 9), the combined influence of topographic and socioeconomic factors exerts the strongest effect on the ES. For example, topographic variables (e.g., DEM and slope) strongly shape ESSD patterns, while socioeconomic pressures introduce potential risks to ecosystem stability when both natural and human drivers are considered together.42

Limitations of the study

This study undertakes a multi-scale investigation into ESSD mismatches based on multiple models (Figure 10), their underlying driving forces, and the associated spatial flow patterns. The findings provide valuable insights to support decision-makers in designing spatially targeted strategies that promote the sustainable development of social-ecological systems. However, several limitations should be acknowledged to contextualize the results and guide future research.

Methods for calculating ESSD in the SYRB

The present study has several limitations. First, it focuses exclusively on the representative ESSD status in 2020, thereby overlooking temporal variations and the complex dynamics of ESSD mismatches over time. Second, although the study assumes that ESFs at the watershed and county scales follow the proximity principle—where flows decrease with distance—it does not incorporate anthropogenic influences or other complexities that may introduce biases. For example, OR service flows are likely shaped more by human-driven factors such as information dissemination, cultural contexts, policy frameworks, and infrastructure, which may operate through scale-dependent mechanisms. Third, the simplified treatment of ESFs may affect the precision of management strategies. For instance, relying on static ESDR values may overestimate the supply capacity of isolated areas. Likewise, ignoring the dynamic complexities of supply and demand flows—such as seasonal variability and diverse transmission pathways—can result in the overestimation of local supply capacity or underestimation of cross-regional dependence.43 Finally, the failure to quantify spillover effects of services may create imbalances in rights and responsibilities between “conservation” and “beneficiary” regions. This imbalance is often evident in upstream river basin areas, which bear the costs of conservation, while downstream areas benefit without compensation.44

ESs are shaped by the complex interactions among diverse environmental spatial variables, inherently exhibiting spatial heterogeneity. Identifying regions where ES supply adequately meets local demand, particularly those with the highest ES surplus capacity, is essential for maintaining a balanced regional supply and demand. Future management strategies should prioritize a systematic spatial assessment of these supply-demand areas before implementing interventions. Only after establishing this fundamental understanding can scientifically grounded compensation strategies be developed. These strategies should function as effective and appropriately calibrated incentive mechanisms to safeguard crucial ES hotspots across relevant spatial scales.

Method details

Study area

The SYRB, located between 34°33′-40°18′N and 110°11′-113°37′E (Figure 1), encompasses 11 prefecture-level cities and 91 counties, including county-level cities and municipal districts. Covering an area of 97,000 km2, it represents approximately 62% of Shanxi Province’s total area. The SYRB is rich in coal, natural gas, and mineral resources, playing a pivotal role in supporting national industries. Guided by China’s strategies for environmental preservation and high-quality development, the basin has become a priority area for future sustainable development. Its unique geographic setting and resource endowments present significant challenges in balancing ecological protection with economic growth, making it an ideal case for examining the complex interrelationships among ES supply, demand, and spatial distribution.

The SYRB experiences a warm-temperate continental monsoon climate, with hot, humid summers contrasting sharply with cold, dry winters. Geographically, it is dominated by the Taihang and Lüliang Mountains, with the Fen River Valley situated between them, forming a characteristic mountainous loess plateau. The region’s soil is predominantly loose and sandy, making it highly vulnerable to erosion. Intense precipitation accelerates this erosion, thereby exacerbating land degradation. Reflecting the hydrological and geomorphological features of the Yellow River Basin, the SYRB can be divided into six sub-basins: Area I represents the basin of the Yellow River tributary; Area II encompasses the Sanchuan River Basin; Area III covers the Xinshui River Basin; Area IV corresponds to the Sushui River Basin; Area V comprises the Fen River Basin; and Area VI includes the Qin River Basin. The ecosystem in this region is extremely fragile and unstable, facing persistent ecological threats such as soil erosion, salinization, and desertification. In recent years, rapid industrial development has further intensified conflicts between anthropogenic activities and the natural environment, thereby heightening ecological risks. Consequently, the SYRB has been designated a key ecological conservation and monitoring zone.

Methods

Estimating the ESSD relationship

To determine the ESSD indicators (encompassing both the supply and demand of ecosystem services), three criteria were applied. First, the relevance of each ES to the natural, geographical, and socioeconomic characteristics of the SYRB was evaluated. Second, the selection reflected the representation of diverse ES categories within the Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (2005) classification framework.45 Third, the availability and reliability of data for quantifying the selected ES indicators were assessed.

Based on these criteria, six ESSD indicators were selected for assessment. Two provisioning services were included: CP and WY. CP is fundamental to national livelihoods, directly shaping food security, economic stability, and human well-being. WY, estimated using the InVEST model, is particularly critical in this water-scarce region for maintaining ecological balance and supporting long-term sustainability. Two regulating services were evaluated: SC and CS. Soil erosion is especially severe in the middle and upper reaches of the SYRB, making SC a key ecological restoration indicator, here quantified using the RUSLE model. CS was selected in alignment with China’s “dual-carbon” goals, which emphasize both energy transition and climate change mitigation, underscoring the role of ecosystems in carbon storage and climate regulation. For supporting services, HQ was chosen as a proxy for biodiversity, highlighting the integration of biodiversity conservation into ES management.46,47 Finally, one cultural service, OR, was included because it provides accessible green spaces that enhance residents’ quality of life through recreation and leisure opportunities. Regarding the assessment of ES demand, for OR, the demand was set at 60 m2 per capita based on the overlay method for green spaces and referenced from relevant studies.12 For WY demand, it was calculated by multiplying the per capita water demand of 208.5 m3/person (from the Shanxi Statistical Yearbook 2020) by the population density. For CS demand, it was derived by multiplying the per capita carbon emission of 15.6 tons/person (based on Shanxi Province data from 2020) by the population density. For CP, the supply was estimated using the NDVI ratio method as referenced from existing studies.9 The demand was calculated by multiplying the per capita crop consumption of 159.3 kg/person (from the Shanxi Statistical Yearbook 2020) by the population density.

We selected 2020 as the baseline year owing to policy requirements, data completeness, and its overall representativeness. The Chinese government formally designated 2020 as the baseline for the Yellow River Basin ecological protection plan, thereby aligning this study with national management objectives.50 Three spatial scales were applied 1 km2 grid, watershed, and county—to correspond with different levels of administrative governance of natural capital and ES within the SYRB. The grid scale, representing the local level, enables detailed and fine-grained ecosystem management, providing decision-makers with nuanced information essential for precise ecological interventions. The watershed scale, representing the landscape level, maintains ecosystem integrity and connectivity, as hydrologically defined sub-watersheds capture the interactions and flows across the entire basin.51 Finally, the county scale was selected as the fundamental planning and management unit. At this level, ecological protection and high-quality development are strongly influenced by county-level policies, which directly determine the utilization and conservation of local ES.

Based on the frequency distribution of the study area’s ESDR—where more than 80% of the values were concentrated within the range [-0.1, 0.1]—an innovative unequal-interval grading method was adopted to highlight critical management thresholds.23 Specifically, the ESDR was divided into five functional intervals: deficit area (ESDR < −0.1), mild deficit area (−0.1 ≤ ESDR < −0.05), balance area (ESDR = 0 ± 0.05), mild surplus area (0.05 < ESDR ≤0.1), and surplus area (ESDR >0.1). This grading scheme, grounded in the observed distribution of ESDR, applies unequal-interval division, with −0.1 and 0.1 selected as key thresholds. These correspond to the safe lower limit and development upper limit of ES supply capacity, thereby ensuring both the statistical robustness of each interval and the precise identification of priority areas for differentiated management.

A combination of correlation and network analyses was employed to elucidate the complex trade-offs and synergies between ESSD. Prior to correlation analysis, all variables were standardized to remove the influence of differences in magnitude. Spearman correlation analysis was then applied to the ESSD relationships of the six ecosystem service types using the corrplot package in R. The absolute value of the correlation coefficient reflects the strength of the trade-offs and synergies, with positive values indicating synergistic relationships and negative values denoting trade-offs. Based on the Spearman correlation matrix, a multi-scale, unidirectional, undirected, and weighted ES network was subsequently constructed using the igraph package in R.

Quantifying the ESFs of SYRB

This study quantified ESFs at three spatial scales: grid, watershed, and county. Three types of ESFs were identified—in situ, omnidirectional, and directional—used to qualitatively describe the spatial movement of ES from SPAs to SBAs.5 Specifically: (1) In situ ES refers to services that are directly utilized at the location of generation, where supply and demand occur within the same spatial unit without requiring transmission through flow paths or media. (2) Omnidirectional ES refers to services without a fixed direction or medium, benefiting the entire region. A surplus of omnidirectional ES in one area can offset deficits elsewhere. (3) Directional ES are further classified into nature-path-dominant and human-path-based types. Under natural conditions, nature-path-dominant ES are transmitted to distant regions through ecological conduits such as river systems, propelled by intrinsic forces like gravity.54 In contrast,human-path ES are transmitted to beneficiary areas via anthropogenic conduits, including transportation networks, and are driven primarily by unmet social demand.

At the grid scale, the flow directions of omnidirectional ES (HQ and CS) were determined by the ESDR with ESFs moving from areas of higher ratios toward those with lower ratios.55 Hydrological connectivity and elevation differences governed the flows of nature-path-dominant services (SC and WY). In contrast, the flows of human-path-dominant services (OR and CP) were shaped by transportation networks.

At the watershed and county scales, the breakpoint formula was applied to estimate the distance or spatial extent of ESFs,56,57 while the field-strength formula was used to calculate their intensity.56 These methods were chosen in light of the significant spatial migration and distance-decay characteristics of ESs.58 To reduce disparities in ESFs across scales—which could undermine comparability of results and policy relevance—flow threshold filtering was applied by eliminating minor ESFs (i.e., flow paths contributing less than 1% of the total flux). Furthermore, the nature-path-dominant ESFs of WY and SC were refined by incorporating upstream–downstream relationships among sub-watersheds, and reverse flows were removed to preserve the integrity of principal ecological processes.

Notably, this study did not attempt to precisely identify actual ESFs, as such an endeavor was beyond both the research objectives and the constraints of available data. Instead, the analysis delineates overarching patterns of ESFs that function as ecological linkages across multiple spatial dimensions. Although numerous ESFs exist at various scales, only flows from surplus to deficit areas were selected—specifically, the flows from SPAs to SBAs. ESFs in areas where supply and demand were balanced were excluded from the simulation. Finally, this study focused exclusively on internal service flows within the SYRB and did not account for potential spatial spillover effects.59

Assessing social-ecological drivers of ESSD mismatches

Identifying the drivers of the ESSD relationship is crucial for ecological governance and the promotion of sustainable environmental progress. The variance inflation factor was used to test multicollinearity among the drivers, ensuring that all values remained below the acceptable threshold (VIF <10) across the three spatial scales. Ultimately, three categories of indicators were retained—natural ecological, biophysical, and socioeconomic factors—represented by 11 social-ecological drivers. This selection reflects a compromise between minimizing correlation and ensuring sufficient data availability at the required scales.

Redundancy analysis (RDA), implemented with the vegan package in R, was applied across multiple spatial scales to examine the relationships between ES and social-ecological drivers and to determine how much variation in ESSD these drivers could explain. Subsequently, the explainable machine-learning model XGBoost was applied to rank the relative importance of these drivers at different scales.

Quantification and statistical analysis

In this study, we used ArcGIS 10.5 for data analysis. The zonal statistics function was used to calculate the supply and demand situation of ESs at different scales. The driving factors were evaluated using R software and machine learning. The ESFs were simulated using the breakpoint and field strength models.
