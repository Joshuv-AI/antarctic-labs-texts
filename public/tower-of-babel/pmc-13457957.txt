Sensitive determination of breast cancer biomarker, CA 15-3, using a label free electrochemical aptasensor based on palladium nanoparticles decorated carbon nanofibers
Authors: Maryam Nayeri, Seyed Morteza Seifati, Navid Nasirizadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study introduces an electrochemical aptasensor for the sensitive determination of the breast cancer biomarker, carbohydrate antigen 15–3 (CA 15-3). The aptasensor was fabricated by modifying a glassy carbon electrode (GCE) with carbon nanofibers (CNFs) and electrodepositing palladium nanoparticles (PdNPs) on it. CNFs provide a large surface area and active sites for the subsequent electrodeposition of PdNPs. Moreover, PdNPs provide binding sites for attachment of aptamers through coordinate bonds between Pd and amino groups. The fabrication process was monitored using cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). The assay is based on the difference in charge transfer resistance (Rct) before and after CA 15-3 binding. Using the EIS technique, a linear concentration range of 1.0 × 10–2 – 200.0 U mL⁻1 and a detection limit of 7.8 × 10–3 U mL⁻1 were obtained for CA 15-3. Furthermore, its clinical utility was confirmed through successful analysis of human serum samples, with results correlating well with the standard ELISA method.Keywords: CA 15-3 biomarker, Electrochemical aptasensor, Breast cancer, Carbon nanofibers, Palladium nanoparticlesSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biomarkers, Cancer, Chemistry, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Breast cancer remains a major public health challenge worldwide, with recent statistics highlighting its significant global impact1,2. In 2022, an estimated 2.3 million new cases of breast cancer in women were reported, along with approximately 670,000 related deaths. Projections indicate that by 2050, annual incidence may rise to 3.2 million cases and 1.1 million deaths, with a disproportionately higher burden in countries with lower Human Development Index (HDI) rankings3. Traditional diagnostic methods, including mammography, ultrasound, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), remain the cornerstone of breast cancer diagnosis. However, these techniques have limitations, such as high costs, limited accessibility, potential radiation exposure, and reduced sensitivity in detecting early-stage cancers, particularly in women with dense breast tissue4. Cancer biomarkers are essential tools for both screening and monitoring treatment response. Among them, Carbohydrate Antigen 15–3 (CA 15-3, a member of the mucin–1 (MUC–1) glycoprotein family, is the most commonly used serum biomarker for breast cancer. It is particulary valuable for detecting metastatic disease and for ongoing follow-up of patients diagnosed with breast cancer5.

CA 15-3 levels in blood serum can be measured to monitor disease progression and detect breast cancer recurrence. Elevated CA 15-3 concentrations may indicate aggressive adenocarcinomas, including ovarian, pancreatic, gastric, or liver cancers. The typical reference range for CA 15-3 in blood is 30 U/mL6,7. The primary techniques for measuring the CA 15-3 biomarker are based on immunoassays, predominantly enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) tests8,9. Other immunoassay methods have also been reported, differing mainly in their signal transduction approaches, such as chemiluminescence10, electrochemiluminescence11, fluorescence12, surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) and electrochemical detection methods13. Although varoius methods exist for detecting cancer biomarkers, these methods are often complex, time-consuming, costly, and require specialized laboratory equipment. Therefore, there is a need for rapid and accurate methods to identify cancer biomarkers. Researchers are exploring the development of affordable devices that can precisely measure biomarkers with higher sensitivity and shorter assay times14,15.

In comparison to other expensive and complex diagnostic techniques, electrochemical biosensors have emerged as a cutting-edge platform. This claim is due to their simple and cost-effective design, excellent sensitivity and selectivity, scalability, potential for miniaturization, and high speed in detecting biomolecules and biomarkers16,17. Single-stranded oligonucleotide aptamers (DNA or RNA), obtained through an in vitro selection process (SELEX), are recognized as highly valuable recognition elements in biosensors. These aptamers offer numerous advantages, including highly specific binding affinity, exceptional selectivity, and the ability to be functionalized with a wide range of molecules (such as thiol, amine, carboxyl, and biotin). Furthermore, these molecules can be conjugated with nanomaterials18–20, are cost-effective, and demonstrate remarkable stability even at ambient temperature21.

The performance of an aptasensor is directly influenced by the modification of the electrode surface, as this modification determines both the sensor’s sensitivity and the biological activity of the aptamer. Consequently, the use of nanomaterials as a design strategy enables the development of high-performance aptamer-based sensing platforms. Various nanomaterials have been employed for this purpose. Carbon-based nanomaterials, such as graphene22, carbon nanotubes23, carbon nanospheres24, carbon nanofibers25, and carbon quantum dots26, have recieved significant attention for electrode surface modification.

CNFs are a class of carbon nanomaterials that have attracted significant interest for practical applications. With their unique properties, carbon nanofibers can enhance electron transfer and improve the rate of electron transport27. CNFs have emerged as highly promising materials in electrochemistry. These nanofibers are produced from polymeric precursor fibers through a carbonization process. Their high carbon content (> 95%) imparts properties such as corrosion resistance, a large surface area, and excellent electrical conductivity28. CNFs are hollow cylindrical structures with diameters ranging from 50 to 500 nm and lengths extending to several tens of microns. Their high aspect ratios (length-to-diameter ratio > 100) enable uniform alignment on the surfaces where they are immobilized. Furthermore, they have been shown to provide a stable platform for robust biosensor applications29.

Precious metal nanoparticles can be utilized as catalysts or dopants to modify surfaces or in conjunction with other nanomaterials to enhance the sensing performance of electrochemical sensors. Furthermore, the loading position, chemical state, particle count, and dispersion uniformity of the metal nanoparticles significantly influence the performance of the biosensors30. Furthermore, carbon nanofibers offer a high specific surface area, providing an ideal platform for the uniform distribution and strong adhesion of metal nanoparticles. PdNPs have unique properties such large surface area, excellent biocompatibility, superior electrocatalytic activity, and high electrical conductivity29. This have been widely used in various applications such as hydrogen storage/sensing31, fuel cells32, and sensors33. Notably, PdNPs exhibit higher oxidative activity compared to gold and platinum nanoparticles34.

In this study, a simple and label-free electrochemical aptasensor was developed for the determination of CA 15-3. CNFs decorated with PdNPs were used as the substrate. The large surface area and high conductivity of CNFs make them suitable substrates for attaching PdNPs and increasing the sensitivity of the aptasensor. The CA 15-3 specific aptamer binds to PdNPs through a coordinate bond between Pd and the amino groups of the aptamer. Using the EIS method, the calibration plot and detection limit for CA 15-3 were determined. The proposed aptasensor provides a simple and label-free approach for the quantitative measurement of CA 15-3 in clinical samples. The ability to measure very low concentrations of the breast cancer biomarker with high sensitivity and good selectivity are additional advantages of this electrochemical aptasensor. By choosing suitable aptamers, this sensing platform holds promise for the determination of a broad range of cancer biomarkers.

According to our literature review, the use of aptamers for the detection of CA15-3 is reported in few papers35,36, whereas the majority of published work has relied on antibody-based assays. The adoption of aptamers as an innovative recognition element for this biomarker offers substantial advantages. On the other hand, the reported studies uses complex and expensive detection procedures, as the measurable electrochemical signal is generated by electroactive labels that are covalently linked to secondary reporting biomolecules. In contrast, the present study utilizes a simple, label-free aptamer interface integrated with a highly conductive PdNPs–CNF network, a nanostructured platform that has not been previously applied to measure CA15-3.

Experimental

Materials

Palladium chloride (PdCl2), ammonium chloride (NH₄Cl), bovine serum albumin (BSA), human serum albumin (HSA), insulin (INS), uric acid (UA), glucose (Glu), cholesterol (Chol), potassium ferricyanide (K3[Fe(CN)6]), potassium ferrocyanide (K4[Fe(CN)6]), phosphoric acid (H3PO4), and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) were obtained from Merck. Carbon nanofibers (CNFs) was prepared from US Research Nanomaterials, Inc. (USA). CA 15-3, CEA and CA 125 ELISA kits were purchased from Monobind Inc. (USA). The CA 15-3 specific aptamer were prepared from Bioneer Co. (South Korea), respectively. The aptamer sequence was 5’–NH2–C6–GCAGTTGATCCTTTGGATACCCTGG –3'. The amino-modified aptamer was dissolved in nuclease-free water and subsequently diluted in phosphate buffered solution (PBS, pH 7.4, 0.1 M).

Apparatus and methods

Electrochemical data were acquired using an Autolab PGSTAT 30 potentiostat/galvanostat in conjunction with the General Purpose Electrochemical System (GPES) and the Frequency Response Analyzer (FRA) software. A three-electrode cell including a modified glassy carbon working electrode, Pt counter electrode and Ag/AgCl reference electrode was employed. The pH of solutions were adjusted with a Metrohm 827 pH/mV meter. Morphology of nanomaterials were characterized field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, TESCAN MIRA4) operating at 15.0 kV. EIS measurements were performed in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) with 5.0 mM [Fe(CN)6]3/4- and 0.1 M KCl at 250 mV (amplitude: 5 mV; frequency range: 0.1 Hz – 100 kHz). Cyclic voltammetry was also recorded in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) with 5.0 mM [Fe(CN)6]3-/4- and 0.1 M KCl from -0.25 V to 0.7 V with scan rate 100 mV s–1.

Fabrication of the CA 15-3 aptasensor

The fabrication of the aptasensor began with polishing a GCE using alumina powder, followed by thorough rinsing with double-distilled water. The GCE was then modified by drop-casting 5.0 µL of a CNFs suspension (1.0 mg mL–1 in water) onto its surface. To prepare PdNPs on the electrode surface, the CNFs-modified GCE (CNFs/GCE) was immersed in a plating bath containing 1.0 mM PdCl2 and 1.0 M NH4Cl at pH 6.0. A constant potential of -0.25 V was then applied for 60 s to electrodeposit the PdNPs, resulting in the formation of PdNPs/CNFs/GCE37. CNF provides a large surface area and active sites for the subsequent electrodeposition of PdNPs. In addition, PdNPs provide binding sites for attachment of aptamers through coordinate bonds between Pd and amino groups38. Accordingly, the effective surface areas of the BGCE, CNFs/GCE and PdNPs/CNFs/GCE were estimated 0.031, 0.049 and 0.061 cm2, respectively, from the cyclic voltammograms of 1.0 mM K3[Fe(CN)6] solution at various scan rates39. Thus, decorating the electrode surface with PdNPs/CNFs creates a significantly increased active area, enabling more efficient immobilization of the aptamer. In the next step, 5.0 µL of CA 15-3 specific aptamer was applied to the electrode surface and incubated in a humid environment at room temperature for 4 h. The CA 15-3 aptamer can bind to the PdNPs/CNFs/GCE through the interaction between Pd and the -NH2 group of the aptamer. To remove unbound aptamers, the Apt/PdNPs/CNFs/GCE was rinsed with phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). After rinsing the modified electrode, 5.0 µL of a 1.0% BSA solution was applied and incubated for 30 min in a humid chamber to block non-specific binding sites. Subsequently, 5.0 µL of different concentrations of CA 15-3 were incubated on the electrode for 45 min to form the final aptasensor (CA15-3/BSA/Apt/PdNPs/CNFs/GCE). The analytical signal was obtained from the difference in the charge transfer resistance (Rct) of the Apt/PdNPs/CNF/GCE before and after CA 15-3 incubation. Preparation of human serum samples

Human serum samples (a healthy human serum sample and two patient samples) were obtained from a clinical laboratory to validate the aptasensor. The CA15-3 levels of the blood serum samples had been measured in the clinical laboratory using the ELISA method. Each sample was diluted twofold with phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.0). Analytical accuracy and the potential for matrix effects were also investigated. Specifically, the diluted samples were fortified with varying concentrations of standard solutions of CA 15-3. Recovery rates were then determined by comparing the concentration measured by the aptasensor with the theoretically spiked concentration.

Results and discussion

Characterization of CNFs and PdNPs/CNFs/GCE

The morphology and surface architecture of CNFs were investigated using FESEM. As shown in Fig. 1A, the SEM micrographs reveal a three-dimensional network of interconnected nanofibers. The individual fibers have a smooth surface texture and form a spaghetti-like, non-woven mat structure with random orientation. This intricate fibrous network provide a large surface area and facilitate efficient electron transport which makes it an ideal scaffold for subsequent functionalization with nanoparticles. The elemental composition of the CNFs was confirmed by EDS analysis. The survey spectrum from the sample, shown in Fig. 1B, is dominated by a strong carbon peak, confirming the CNFs matrix as the primary material. After electrodeposition, SEM analysis was again used to characterize the distribution and morphology of the PdNPs on the CNFs scaffold. FESEM did not reveal distinct, discrete PdNPs on the surface of the CNFs, suggesting a possible sub-nanometer or highly dispersed deposition (Fig. 1C). However, the presence of palladium was unequivocally confirmed by EDS. The EDS spectrum showed distinct peaks corresponding to the Pd-L emission line, confirming the successful electrodeposition of PdNPs (Fig. 1D). The absence of clearly observable nanoparticles in SEM micrographs, along with a positive EDS signal, strongly suggests that the deposited palladium is present as an ultra-fine coating or as nanoparticles below the resolution limit of the SEM instrument.

FESEM (A, C) and EDS (B, D) CNFs and PdNPs/CNFs/GCE, respectively.

Tracking the fabrication steps of the aptasensor

The stepwise fabrication of the aptasensor was monitored using CV and EIS. Nyquist plots and cyclic voltammograms of the bare GCE (BGCE), CNFs/GCE, PdNPs/CNFs/GCE, Apt/PdNPs/CNFs/GCE, BSA/Apt/PdNPs/CNFs/GCE, and CA15-3/Apt/PdNPs/CNFs/GCE in phosphate buffer solution (0.1 M, pH 7.0) with 5.0 mM [Fe(CN)6]3-/4- and 0.1 M KCl were recorded. The Nyquist plots are shown in Fig. 2A. In the Nyquist plots, the Rct corresponds to the diameter of the semicircle, while the linear segment represents a diffusion-controlled process40. The EIS Nyquist plots (Fig. 2A) demonstrate the successful stepwise fabrication of the aptasensor. A small semicircle for the BGCE indicates low Rct (~ 1270 Ω). Modification with CNFs further decreases the Rct (~ 310 Ω), due to the increased surface area and high conductivity of the CNFs. After electrodeposition of PdNPs on the CNFs/GCE, the surface area also increases, resulting in a further decrease in the diameter of the semicircle and Rct (~ 190 Ω). Upon immobilization of the aptamer, the semicircle diameter and Rct progressively increase (~ 580 Ω). This is attributed to the introduction of insulating layers and electrostatic repulsion from the negatively charged aptamer backbone against the [Fe(CN)6]3/4- probe. By blocking the electrode surface by BSA, the semicircle diameter and Rct increase too (~ 660 Ω). Finally, incubation with 10.0 U mL–1 causes a substantial increase in Rct (~ 1160 Ω), confirming target binding. This is due to the formation of an aptamer–CA 15-3 complex that further impedes electron transfer.

(A) Nyquist plots (B) and cyclic voltammograms of BGCE, CNFs/GCE, PdNPs/CNFs/GCE, Apt/PdNPs/CNFs/GCE, BSA/Apt/PdNPs/CNFs/GCE, and CA15-3/BSA/Apt/PdNPs/CNFs/GCE in a phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.0) containing 5.0 mM [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− and 0.1 M KCl. CA 15-3 concentration: 1.0 U mL–1.

CV was also used to monitor the aptasensor fabrication. Cyclic voltammograms of the aptasensor at different modification steps are shown in Fig. 2B. As can be seen, the oxidation and reduction peak currents of the [Fe(CN)6]3-/4- probe increase at the CNFs/GCE compared to the BGCE, confirming successful modification and enhanced conductivity. Subsequent modification with PdNPs increases the anodic and cathodic peak current. A decrease in the current and a shift in the peak potential occur after immobilization of the CA 15-3 aptamer, indicating its covalent attachment to the electrode surface. Finally, incubation with CA 15-3 resulted in a more pronounced current decrease and potential shift, confirming successful target capture. The consistent data obtained from both CV and EIS corroborate the effective construction of the aptasensor.

Optimization of experimental parameters

The important experimental parameters of the aptasensor were carefully optimized to maximize its response toward CA 15-3. The factors such as the aptamer concentration, the concentration of CNF, the immobilization time of aptamer, and CA 15-3 incubation time were investigated. Optimization was performed by monitoring the Rct values. The signal output was quantified as the change in Rct of Apt/PdNPs/CNFs/GCE before (Rct1) and after (Rct2) binding with CA 15-3, (ΔRct = Rct2 – Rct1).

To optimize the CNF concentration on the aptasensor response, various concentrations of CNF in water (0.3 mg mL–1, 0.6 mg mL–1, 1.0 mg mL–1 and 1.5 mg mL–1) were prepared. The prepared suspensions was used to modify the GCE. Then, the PdNPs was electrodeposited on the modified electrodes. CA 15-3 aptamer (10.0 µM) was then immobilized on the modified electrodes. The electrode surface was then blocked with BSA and incubated with 10.0 U ml–1 CA 15-3. The Nyquist plots were recorded and ΔRct values were calculated. The results are shown in Fig. 3A. As can be seen, ΔRct increased with increasing CNF concentration up to 1.0 mg mL–1 and then slightly decreased. As the CNF concentration increased to 1.0 mg mL–1, the response increased due to the larger surface area. Beyond this concentration, the response decreased slightly because of CNF agglomeration and an increase in the background current. Therefore, mg mL–1 was chosen as the optimal CNF concentration. To optimize the aptamer immobilization concentration, the aptasensor response was investigated as a function of the concentration of the CA 15-3 specific aptamer. The GCE was modified with CNFs and PdNPs. CA 15-3 aptamer at different concentrations (5.0–15.0 µM) were then immobilized for 3 h in a humid environment. After blocking with BSA and incubating with 10.0 U ml–1 CA 15-3, the Nyquist plots were recorded and ΔRct were calculated. The results (Fig. 3A) demonstrate that ΔRct increases up to a concentration of 10.0 µM. The reason is that up to 10.0 µM, the aptamer has low non-specific adsorption and a consistently favorable orientation. Consequently, 10.0 µM was chosen as the optimal aptamer concentration. To determine the optimal aptamer immobilization time, the PdNPs/CNFs/GCE was incubated with a 10.0 µM aptamer solution for periods ranging from 2 to 6 h. After BSA blocking and incubation with CA 15-3 (10.0 U mL–1), Nyquist plots were recorded. The resulting signal (ΔRct, Fig. 3B) increased up to a 4 h incubation, indicating maximal coverage of the available active sites. Based on these results, a 4 h immobilization period was considered optimal. The incubation time for CA 15-3 binding was optimized by exposing the fabricated aptasensor to 10.0 U mL–1 CA 15-3 for 15 to 75 min. The analytical signal (ΔRct) increased over time, plateauing after 45 min (Fig. 3C), indicating completion of the aptamer-CA 15-3 binding event. An incubation time of 45 min was therefore selected for all subsequent assays.

Analytical performance of the aptasensor

The quantitative detection of CA 15-3 was performed using the EIS method under optimized conditions. Figure 4A presents the Nyquist plots of the Apt/PdNPs/CNFs/GCE after incubation with different concentrations of CA 15-3. A clear trend of increasing Rct with higher biomarker concentrations was observed, which is attributed to the insulating effect of CA 15-3 on the electrode surface. The impedance data were fitted using the equivalent circuit model shown in the inset of Fig. 4A. The analytical signal, defined as ΔRct = Rct2 – Rct1, exhibited a linear relationship with the logarithm of CA 15-3 concentration across a wide linear range from 1.0 × 10–2 to 200.0 U mL–1 (Fig. 4B). The limit of detection (LOD) was calculated using the equation ylod = ybl + 3sbl41 and the calibration plot, where ybl and sbl are the mean response and standard deviation of the blank, respectively. Based on this, LOD was determined to be 7.8 × 10–3 U mL–1. As summarized in Table 1, the analytical performance of the proposed aptasensor for CA 15-3, including its wide linear range and low detection limit, is competitive with other reported methods, demonstrating its potential for sensitive and reliable for early detection of breast cancer.

The proposed label-free electrochemical aptasensor based on PdNPs–CNFs introduces a distinct material and sensing strategy that differs substantially from previously reported CA 15-3 biosensors. Earlier studies relied predominantly on antibody-based immunosensors, sandwich-type architectures, redox-labeled aptamers, magnetic-bead amplification, polymer films, or optical/electrochemiluminescent methodologies, often involving complex fabrication and labeling steps (Table 1). In contrast, the present work employs a simple, label-free aptamer interface supported by a highly conductive PdNPs–CNF network, a nanostructure not used in earlier CA 15-3 platforms. This hybrid provides superior electron-transfer kinetics, increased surface area, and enhanced stability. As a result, the sensor achieves higher sensitivity, improved reproducibility, and a more cost-effective fabrication route, establishing a clear advancement over existing CA 15-3 detection strategies.

Evaluation of the selectivity, reproducibility, repeatability, and stability of the aptasensor

A critical parameter in aptasensor design is the specific recognition of the target analyte in the presence of potential interferents. To assess this, the selectivity of the developed aptasensor was evaluated by measuring the ΔRct response to its target, CA 15-3 at 1.0 U mL–1, and to several interfering species in serum, including CEA, CA 125, HSA, INS, UA, Chol, and Glu. These interferents were chosen due to their prevalence in blood serum and potential for cross-reactivity. Accordingly, electrochemical signals of the aptasensor were recorded when the target, interfering species, and their mixture were incubated on BSA/Apt/PdNPs/CNFs/GCE. As shown in Fig. 5, the ΔRct response generated by CA 15-3 was significantly greater than the signals produced by any of the interfering species. The considerable difference in signal response confirms that the aptasensor possesses the excellent selectivity for reliable quantification of CA 15-3 without significant interference from other serum components.

The selectivity of the aptasensor evaluated by recording the signal after incubating HSA (40.0 mg mL–1), CA 125 (100.0 U mL–1), CEA (50.0 ng mL–1), Ins (0.01 mU mL–1), UA (0.5 mg mL–1), Glu (0.8 mg mL–1), Chol (0.5 mg mL–1), CA 15-3 (1.0 U mL–1), and mixture of these. Error bars represent the RSD of three independent experiments.

The reproducibility of the aptasensor was evaluated by preparing four independent sensors and measuring their response to 1.0 U mL–1 CA 15-3. The resulting signals showed a relative standard deviation (RSD) of 4.9%. The repeatability of the aptasensor was evaluated by performing multiple consecutive measurements (n = 5) under identical conditions using the same electrode. To evaluate the repeatability of the aptasensor, five consecutive assays of a 1.0 U mL–1 CA 15-3 standard were performed on a single aptasensor. The resulting signals demonstrated a relative standard deviation (RSD) of 3.8%. Furthermore, stability was assessed over seven days, with 96.1% of the initial response retained when stored at 4 °C. These results demonstrate the robust reproducibility, repeatability, and appropriate stability of the proposed aptasensor.

Analysis of CA 15-3 in real samples

CA 15-3 is a critical biomarker for breast cancer detection and monitoring disease progression. Accurate quantification of CA 15-3 in human serum is therefore of significant clinical importance. Although the proposed aptasensor demonstrates promising performance in this initial study, its transition to clinical use requires further validation with real samples. To assess the practical utility of the developed aptasensor, it was used to analyze three human serum samples. The samples were as described in the experimental section. The prepared samples were incubated on the aptasensor surface. The ΔRct values were used to quantify CA 15-3 in human serum samples. The results showed average concentrations of 8.9 ± 0.3 U mL–1, 75.1 ± 2.2 U mL–1, and 117.4 ± 2.2 U mL–1 for these samples. These values show excellent agreement with the reference ELISA method, which reported 9.4 U mL–1, 70.7 U mL–1, and 123.5 U mL–1, respectively. The experimental t-values (texp) for CA 15-3 in human serum samples were calculated to be 2.7, 2.4 and 1.9, respectively. These values are lower than the critical t-value at the 95% confidence level and 2 degrees of freedom (tcrit = 4.3). The t-test indicated that there was no evidence of any systematic difference between the results obtained by the two methods. In addition, the accuracy of the aptasensor was verified through a spiking experiment in diluted human serum. The method showed satisfactory recovery rates ranging from 95.1% to 104.1% (Table 2), confirming its reliability in a complex matrix. These results demonstrate that the proposed aptasensor is a highly promising and reliable tool for the clinical determination of CA 15-3.

Conclusion

A simple and label free electrochemical aptasensor was developed for the detection of the breast cancer biomarker CA 15-3 based on PdNPs electrodeposited on CNFs modified GCE. CNFs and PdNPs provide a large surface area and active sites for the attachment of aptamers. The EIS method showed a wide linear concentration range from 1.0 × 10–2 to 200.0 U mL–1 and low LOD of 7.8 × 10–3 U mL–1 for CA 15-3. Moreover, the proposed aptasensor showed acceptable selectivity, reproducibility, repeatability, and stability for measuring CA 15-3. Importantly, the biosensor performed reliably in human serum samples, yielding results consistent with the standard ELISA method.
