Large language models in implant dentistry: a scoping review of applications, performance, and limitations
Authors: Hyun-Jun Kong, Yu-Lee Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis scoping review evaluates the current state of generative artificial intelligence (AI) in implant dentistry, focusing on the performance, clinical applications, and inherent limitations of large language models (LLMs) in both clinical and educational settings.MethodsA scoping review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA-ScR guidelines. A comprehensive search across four electronic databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase) was performed for literature published through December 2025. Eighteen eligible studies were analyzed to assess model architectures, specific task performance, and comparative proficiency against human experts.ResultsThe included studies, all published in 2024 or 2025, were classified as examining patient interaction (n = 9), medical knowledge (n = 7), or diagnostic assessment (n = 2). The analysis revealed a distinct evolution in performance with the advent of advanced reasoning models (e.g., ChatGPT-o1, DeepSeek-R1), which occasionally surpassed licensed dentists in certification examinations. Comparative assessments between medical-specific models and general-purpose LLMs yielded divergent outcomes, indicating that domain specialization does not inherently guarantee superior clinical accuracy against state-of-the-art generalist architectures. Nevertheless, reliability remains a concern; despite the integration of retrieval-augmented generation, hallucinations persist—especially in systematic search tasks—and the inability of text-based models to interpret diagnostic imaging continues to limit their clinical autonomy.ConclusionsAlthough generative AI has attained expert-level proficiency in theoretical knowledge retrieval, it currently serves best as an adjunctive support system, rather than a replacement for clinical judgment. Given the persistent risks of hallucination and the lack of visual processing capabilities, strict professional oversight is mandatory. Future research must prioritize the development of multimodal models and validate clinical outcomes through randomized trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08747-z.

Background

Dental implants are currently the established standard for restoring both function and aesthetics in edentulous patients [1, 2]. In recent years, the field has transitioned toward digital implantology. Modern workflows now routinely integrate intraoral scanning, cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), and computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) technologies [3, 4]. However, this digital shift has created an unexpected challenge: a massive increase in the data volume requiring management. Implant planning is inherently difficult. It demands the careful balancing of various diagnostic factors, from bone density assessment to final prosthetic contouring. This complex decision-making process puts a heavy cognitive load on clinicians and requires deep specialized expertise [5]. Beyond the surgery itself, maintaining precise documentation and providing clear patient education further add to the daily clinical workload [6].

To address these demands, artificial intelligence (AI) has gained significant momentum in dental research [7]. So far, computer vision has dominated the field. Convolutional neural networks now read radiographs and optical scans with accuracy comparable to human experts [8]. These deep learning tools excel at spotting pathologies, identifying implant systems, and even drafting prosthetic designs [9–11]. However, vision-centric models face a clear boundary. They process pixels, not language. They cannot synthesize a patient’s medical history, reason through a complicated treatment plan, or manage the nuances of patient communication. Handling the linguistic and cognitive side of implantology requires advanced natural language processing [12].

The arrival of the transformer architecture and large language models (LLMs) represents a major turning point [13]. Platforms like OpenAI’s GPT and Google’s Gemini do more than just process vast datasets; they exhibit genuine reasoning capabilities [14]. In general medicine, these tools already show their value. They pass rigorous exams like the USMLE, organize electronic health records, and improve patient communication [15–17]. Recent evidence suggests LLMs can function as active decision support systems. They can quickly query medical literature to propose differential diagnoses and support evidence-based choices in real-time [18].

Dental research has only recently begun to explore these generative tools. Early investigations focused mainly on testing chatbots against licensing exams or creating basic educational materials [19, 20]. However, implant dentistry is a unique discipline defined by strict surgical protocols and complex material science. To date, there has been no systematic effort to map how LLMs fit into this specific workflow. While general reviews of AI in prosthodontics exist, few focus exclusively on the implant therapy process. Therefore, this scoping review aims to address this gap. The goal is to chart the current landscape of LLMs in implantology, evaluating their efficacy in diagnosis and planning while critically addressing flaws like AI hallucinations and inherent bias.

Methods

Study design

This scoping review adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) statement [21]. Although a formal protocol was not prospectively registered in a public database, the protocol was developed a priori to comprehensively characterize the existing body of literature about the application, utility, and performance of LLMs within the specific domain of dental implantology.

Eligibility criteria

The Population, Concept, and Context (PCC) framework guided the eligibility criteria for this review [22].

Population

Dental professionals, undergraduate and postgraduate students, and simulated patient scenarios designed to model individuals undergoing implant therapy.

Concept

The application of generative AI. Specifically, the focus remained on large language models capable of natural language processing (e.g., the GPT series, Gemini, and LLaMA). The screening process strictly excluded research focused entirely on computer vision algorithms (such as convolutional neural networks) that lacked a text-generation component.

Context

The entire dental implant workflow. This covers everything from preoperative diagnostics and surgical planning to patient consultations and education.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible literature included English-language original research articles, technical reports, and proof-of-concept studies. The search carried no publication date restrictions. To maintain rigorous data quality, the review omitted secondary literature. This meant leaving out general reviews, editorials, and conference abstracts that lacked detailed methodologies.

Information sources and search strategy

The literature search targeted four major databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase. The strategy relied on Boolean operators. It linked generative AI terminology directly with implant dentistry keywords.

The exact Boolean string designed for the primary database (PubMed) appears below. The research team then adapted this core syntax to fit the specific search interfaces of the remaining databases:("Large Language Models" OR "LLM" OR "Generative AI" OR "ChatGPT" OR "GPT-4" OR "Gemini" OR "BERT" OR "Natural Language Processing") AND ("Dental Implants" OR "Implant Dentistry" OR "Oral Implant" OR "Osseointegration" OR "Bone Augmentation").

The search captured all records published up to December 2025. Finally, a manual check of the reference lists from all included studies helped identify any hidden gray literature or extra sources.

Selection of sources

Upon the retrieval of citations, duplicate records were systematically eliminated, and the screening process was managed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). The selection process was executed in two sequential phases by two independent reviewers (H.J.K and Y.L.K) to ensure objectivity.

Title and abstract screening

Initial records were evaluated for relevance and alignment with the established PCC framework.

Full-text assessment

Potentially eligible articles underwent a comprehensive full-text review to verify strict adherence to the inclusion and exclusion protocols.

Any discordance between reviewers regarding study eligibility was resolved exclusively through thorough discussion until a consensus was reached.

Data charting process

Data extraction was executed using a pre-calibrated, standardized form developed in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). To mitigate bias and ensure data fidelity, two reviewers independently performed the extraction process, capturing specific variables deemed critical to the study objectives.

The following data items were extracted.

Study characteristics

Authorship, publication year, geographic origin, and the specific methodological design used.

AI Model details

Technical details about the generative AI architecture tested (e.g., GPT-4, Gemini, LLaMA) and the input prompts or datasets used.

Key findings

Primary outcome measures, particularly statistical performance metrics (e.g., accuracy, sensitivity) and qualitative conclusions about model efficacy.

Application domain

The operational scope of the model within the implant workflow, categorized as diagnostic assessment, medical knowledge, or patient interaction.

Critical appraisal

In line with the PRISMA-ScR guidelines [21], a formal assessment of methodological quality or risk of bias was not undertaken for the included studies. This approach aligns with the distinct purpose of a scoping review, which aims to broadly map the thematic landscape and characteristics of emerging literature, rather than to synthesize quantitative effect sizes or evaluate the evidentiary weight of individual findings.

Synthesis of results

A descriptive narrative synthesis was used to analyze the extracted data. Results

Selection of included studies

The initial electronic search across four databases yielded 203 citations (PubMed: 47, Web of Science: 39, Scopus: 56, Embase: 61). Following the removal of duplicates, 66 unique records were retained for preliminary screening. The subsequent review of titles and abstracts resulted in the exclusion of 37 records, primarily due to topic irrelevance (n = 23), use of non-generative AI models (n = 7), or classification as non-primary literature, such as reviews (n = 7).

Twenty-nine articles advanced to the full-text assessment phase. In this stage, a further 11 studies were deemed ineligible. The detailed examinations revealed that three studies used non-generative architectures despite ambiguous titles. An additional eight were excluded due to insufficient specificity to implant dentistry; they predominantly addressed broader scopes within general medicine (n = 1), oral and maxillofacial surgery (n = 5), or periodontology (n = 2).

General characteristics of the included studies

The eighteen included studies [23–40] were all published in 2024 or 2025, underscoring the rapid and recent proliferation of generative AI research in the dental sector. In terms of geographic distribution, Türkiye contributed the highest volume of research (n = 6) [24, 29, 30, 33, 35, 37]. Most of the remaining cohort represents a global cross-section of single-center studies from the USA [28, 39], China [23], Saudi Arabia [25], Iran [26], Brazil [27], Vietnam [31], Singapore [34], and Greece [36]. Cross-border academic collaboration was also evident, with three studies conducted through multinational partnerships across Europe, North America, and Asia [32, 38, 40].

Regarding the model architecture, ChatGPT (OpenAI) served as the benchmark platform and was featured in every study included in this review. The evaluated iterations ranged from legacy versions (GPT-3.5) to advanced reasoning models (GPT-4o, GPT-o1, and GPT-o3-mini). Competing general-purpose LLMs also had a significant presence, with Google Gemini (Pro, Flash, Advanced) analyzed in nine studies [23–25, 27, 28, 31, 35, 36, 38] and Microsoft Copilot in seven [24, 25, 27–29, 31, 38]. Recent investigations have further expanded the evaluative scope to include emerging competitors such as DeepSeek-R1 [23, 27, 35] and Claude 3.5 Sonnet [27, 35], alongside medical-specific systems such as OpenEvidence [26, 28, 33], ScholarQA [26], and MedGebra [36].

Consistent with the classification framework established for this review, the studies were stratified into three primary domains: patient interaction (n = 9) [24, 25, 29, 30, 33–37], medical knowledge (n = 7) [23, 26, 31, 32, 38–40], and diagnostic assessment (n = 2) [27, 28]. Patient interaction

The most extensive subset of this review (n = 9) investigated the efficacy of generative AI in facilitating patient communication and delivering educational content about dental implant therapy [24, 25, 29, 30, 33–37]. The primary objective across these studies was to evaluate the models' capacity to generate accurate, readable, and empathetic responses to frequently asked questions sourced from clinical scenarios and online forums.

General-purpose LLMs, particularly early iterations of the GPT series, established a baseline of utility for translating complex clinical concepts into accessible language. Investigations by Yagcı et al. [24] and Tahani et al. [25] confirmed that ChatGPT-3.5, Gemini, and Copilot chatbots provided responses with acceptable accuracy. However, consensus across the studies emphasized that such tools function best as supplementary resources, rather than autonomous advisors, due to occasional inconsistencies. Similarly, Güzelce et al. [29] and Çoban et al. [30] validated the utility of ChatGPT in explaining edentulism treatments, though the latter study cautioned against potential commercial bias in recommendations for specific implant manufacturers.

A distinct performance hierarchy emerged with the integration of advanced "reasoning" architectures. Tuzlalı et al. [35] conducted a comparative analysis of five contemporary models and reported that ChatGPT-o1, designed for chain-of-thought processing, achieved superior scores in both accuracy and clarity, closely trailed by DeepSeek-R1. The reasoning-enhanced models demonstrated a significant proficiency gap over earlier iterations and competitor engines such as Perplexity Pro, particularly in handling nuanced patient inquiries.

The comparison between medically fine-tuned models and generalist LLMs yielded divergent outcomes. Akpınar et al. [33] reported that MediSearch, a model optimized for medical queries, outperformed ChatGPT-4.0 in addressing technical questions about the "All-on-Four" protocol. However, Kofos et al. [36] observed a paradoxical trend in which the general-purpose ChatGPT-4.0 surpassed the specialized MedGebra model, specifically in terms of clarity and user-friendliness.

Beyond model architecture, methodological variables such as prompt engineering and evaluator expertise significantly influenced the reported outcomes. Tay et al. [34] identified a trade-off in prompt design: whereas contextualized prompts successfully improved the inclusion of scientific citations, they inadvertently degraded the readability of the responses, compared with simple zero-shot prompts. Furthermore, Terzi et al. [37] highlighted a critical discrepancy in expert consensus; oral and maxillofacial surgeons consistently assigned lower quality scores to AI responses than general practitioners, suggesting that specialists apply stricter clinical standards when evaluating AI-generated advice.

Medical knowledge

Seven studies investigated the proficiency of AI models in processing professional medical knowledge, passing certification examinations, and conducting academic research [23, 26, 31, 32, 38–40].

In the domain of standardized assessment, advanced LLMs have demonstrated the ability to rival, and occasionally surpass, human expertise. Revilla-León et al. [39] reported that ChatGPT-4.0 not only secured a passing score on the EAO certification exam but also significantly outperformed licensed dentists. Similarly, Salem et al. [40] used the ITI certification exam to highlight a generational leap in performance: whereas legacy models (GPT-3.5) struggled, GPT-4.0 achieved proficiency levels comparable to human professionals. Both studies established a consensus that current state-of-the-art models have effectively cleared the threshold of expert theoretical knowledge in text-based evaluations.

However, performance was not uniform across all architectures; the specific model type emerged as a decisive variable. Nguyen et al. [31] identified a trade-off between speed and accuracy, demonstrating that "deep-reasoning" models (e.g., OpenAI o3, Gemini 1.5 Pro) achieved superior accuracy (up to 83.1%) on complex oral implantology questions, compared with lightweight variants such as GPT-4o or Gemini Flash, albeit with higher latency. Similarly, Wu et al. [23] highlighted the rapid maturation of reasoning capabilities, noting that Gemini 2.0 Flash exhibited the most comprehensive ability to handle complex case analyses.

Economic accessibility also correlated with clinical reliability. Koidou et al. [38] provided evidence that the subscription-based Gemini Advanced significantly outperformed its free counterpart (Gemini Basic) in addressing clinical queries about peri-implant disease. This finding suggests a critical caveat for practitioners: reliance on free, basic-tier models could compromise the quality of clinical decision support.

Despite those outcomes in knowledge retrieval, the application of AI in rigorous academic research remains fraught with challenges. Hooshiar et al. [26] evaluated the ScholarQA and OpenEvidence retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems. Although they successfully mitigated the rate of hallucinations (fabricated citations), compared with standard LLMs, they failed to completely eliminate interpretive errors. Furthermore, Bencze et al. [32] exposed the limitations of AI in autonomous systematic reviewing. Their assessment of ChatGPT-4.1’s Deep Research function revealed a critical imbalance: although the tool exhibited high specificity (98%), it suffered from poor sensitivity (47.8%) and continued to hallucinate non-existent references, indicating that current AI tools are not yet viable substitutes for manual systematic search and selection processes.

Diagnostic assessment

A targeted subset of the literature (n = 2) focused on the direct integration of LLMs into clinical workflows, specifically evaluating their utility in diagnostic reasoning and automating operative documentation [27, 28].

In the realm of diagnostic proficiency, Amador Barbosa et al. [27] conducted a comprehensive evaluation of diverse architectures—ChatGPT-4o, ChatGPT-o1, Claude, Copilot, and DeepSeek—using simulated case scenarios for peri-implant mucositis and peri-implantitis. The analysis showed that ChatGPT-4o exhibited the highest diagnostic accuracy and consistency among the tested models. Conversely, Microsoft Copilot demonstrated significant variability and low reliability. These findings underscore a critical caveat for practitioners: not all AI is created equal, and specific model selection is paramount when using these tools for clinical decision support.

To address the administrative burden of clinical practice, Kazim et al. [28] assessed the capacity of ChatGPT, Gemini, and OpenEvidence to generate automated operative dictations for surgical and restorative procedures. Their investigation revealed that all tested platforms, when guided by structured prompts, were able to produce clinically accurate and comprehensive notes. Notably, even minimum specific prompts yielded clinically viable outputs, though the quality and detail significantly improved with prompt structure. This elucidates a key operational insight: for administrative automation, the prompt engineering proficiency of the clinician is as determinant of output quality as the underlying AI architecture itself.

Discussion

Comparative proficiency: the gap between theoretical knowledge and clinical competence

The evidence synthesized in this review points to a fundamental disruption in the traditional hierarchy of medical knowledge acquisition. Recent iterations of LLMs have demonstrated the capacity to not merely rival but occasionally surpass licensed professionals in standardized assessments. The investigation by Revilla-León et al. [39] marks a watershed moment. They documented that ChatGPT-4.0 not only cleared the threshold for the EAO certification exam but also statistically outperformed the human cohort. Corroborating that trajectory, Salem et al. [40] observed a generational leap in performance: although legacy models (GPT-3.5) faltered, GPT-4.0 achieved parity with licensed dentists on the ITI certification exam. These findings signal a notable progression. In the domain of theoretical retrieval and text-based logic, generative AI has effectively bridged the gap with human expertise.

This rapid ascent in proficiency is not solely a function of expanded datasets. It is largely attributable to architectural evolutions in "reasoning" capabilities. As highlighted by Nguyen et al. [31] and Wu et al. [23], the integration of chain-of-thought processing allows contemporary models (e.g., OpenAI o3, Gemini 2.0 Flash) to deconstruct complex clinical scenarios step-by-step, rather than relying on stochastic next-word prediction. This methodological shift explains the models' newfound success in complex case analyses where earlier iterations consistently failed.

Crucially, however, proficiency in standardized testing must not be conflated with clinical competence. Board examinations operate within a controlled, text-based environment. In contrast, clinical practice demands the management of dynamic variables, visual ambiguity, and patient-specific idiosyncrasies that text-only models cannot perceive. This limitation is underscored by Terzi et al. [37], who noted that oral and maxillofacial surgeons consistently assigned lower quality ratings to AI responses than general practitioners. This discrepancy suggests that although AI possesses exhaustive explicit knowledge, it currently lacks the tacit knowledge and surgical intuition that characterize specialist-level decision-making. Consequently, AI has established itself as a formidable theoretical savant. Nevertheless, it remains an adjunctive tool requiring strict oversight, rather than a replacement for autonomous clinicians.

The evolution of models: from general chatbots to reasoning and specialized AI

The chronological synthesis of the included studies delineates a distinct technological inflection point. Earlier investigations using ChatGPT-3.5 frequently cited verbose or generic outputs as a primary limitation [25]. However, the deployment of reasoning models in late 2024 and 2025 has fundamentally redefined the performance benchmark. Tuzlalı et al. [35] and Wu et al. [23] demonstrated that architectures such as ChatGPT-o1 and DeepSeek-R1 significantly outperformed their predecessors by leveraging inference-time computing. This process allows the model to simulate a chain-of-thought before finalizing an output. This shift is particularly salient for implant dentistry, where clinical decision-making requires multi-step deductive logic rather than mere stochastic information retrieval.

A critical finding of this review is the debate regarding model specialization. The prevailing hypothesis suggests that AI models fine-tuned exclusively on medical datasets should inherently supersede their general-purpose counterparts. Akpınar et al. [33] provided evidence supporting this, noting that the medically tuned MediSearch offered superior technical details about the All-on-Four protocol. However, this was not a universal constant. Contradictory evidence presented by Kofos et al. [36] revealed that the generalist ChatGPT-4o paradoxically outperformed MedGebra, a specialized medical AI, in terms of clarity and utility. This suggests that domain specialization does not strictly guarantee superiority. Often, the massive parameter scale and advanced reasoning infrastructure of top-tier foundation models outweigh the benefits of smaller, domain-specific training. Moreover, as noted by Hooshiar et al. [26], even RAG systems (e.g., ScholarQA) designed to ground answers in literature are not immune to interpretative errors. They frequently lag behind the raw reasoning power of advanced generalist models.

Finally, the performance disparity between free and paid tiers has profound clinical implications. Koidou et al. [38] established a clear gradient of reliability within the Google ecosystem, with Gemini Advanced significantly surpassing its basic iteration. This observation emphasizes a practical guideline for the dental community. The intelligence required for safe clinical or educational application is currently economically gated. Consequently, reliance on free, legacy versions of AI constitutes a risk. Investment in advanced, subscription-based models is not merely an option but a prerequisite for reliable performance.

Clinical reliability and the challenge of hallucination

Despite the demonstrated prowess of LLMs in reasoning and standardized testing, their deployment in real-world clinical and research settings presents a complex dichotomy. In the domain of patient interaction, the ability of AI to democratize information access is well-supported [24, 25]. However, a critical discordance in quality perception remains. Terzi et al. [37] identified an inverse correlation between evaluator expertise and perceived AI performance. Oral and maxillofacial surgeons consistently assigned lower quality ratings to AI-generated responses than general practitioners. This disparity implies that AI outputs might suffice for generalist-level inquiries. Yet, they currently lack the requisite clinical nuance, depth, and safety considerations demanded by specialists managing complex surgical pathologies.

The most formidable impediment to professional integration is the phenomenon of hallucination, or plausible confabulation, in which models generate factually incorrect information with high confidence. This risk is particularly acute in the realm of academic rigor. Bencze et al. [32] exposed significant epistemic limitations in the deep research capabilities of current models. They noted that these platforms frequently fabricated citations and achieved a sensitivity of only 47.8% in systematic search tasks. Such findings categorically disqualify current AI tools from functioning as autonomous researchers, as they fail to capture the breadth of literature required for systematic evidence synthesis. Furthermore, reliance on RAG is not the answer. Hooshiar et al. [26] observed that systems such as OpenEvidence reduced the frequency but did not eliminate fabricated references. This proves that access to data does not equate to understanding.

Given the documented vulnerabilities [26, 32, 37], this synthesis of current evidence dictates a mandatory operational standard: human-in-the-loop workflow. The consensus across the included literature is that AI functions most effectively as a high-capacity adjunctive engine. However, the final validation of clinical decisions and academic citations must remain the exclusive purview of human experts to mitigate the risks of inaccuracies and ensure patient safety [24, 25]. To safely integrate these technologies, clinicians should currently restrict LLM use to 'low-risk, high-reward' tasks. Practical applications include drafting patient education materials, streamlining clinical documentation, and utilizing models for preliminary decision support, such as summarizing clinical guidelines. Strict adherence to the 'Human-in-the-Loop' principle is paramount. Clinicians must rigorously verify all AI-generated content, anonymize patient data, and deploy these tools strictly as administrative adjuncts, never as autonomous diagnostic authorities.

The integration of generative AI also introduces critical privacy and medicolegal safety challenges. Because public LLMs may retain query data for future training, inputting sensitive patient information risks violating stringent data protection regulations. Therefore, clinicians must strictly de-identify all clinical data prior to AI processing. Furthermore, the "black box" nature of these algorithms complicates clinical accountability. In cases where AI-augmented decisions lead to adverse outcomes, legal liability remains ambiguous. Until privacy-compliant, enterprise-grade AI systems and robust legal frameworks are established, the clinician bears the ultimate ethical and legal responsibility for all AI-assisted interventions.

Limitations and future directions

Although this review provides a comprehensive synthesis of the current landscape, several inherent limitations merit consideration. First, the existing literature exhibits a marked geographic and linguistic homogeneity. A disproportionate volume of the included studies originated from a single nation (Türkiye, n = 6) [24, 29, 30, 33, 35, 37], and evaluations were predominantly conducted within an Anglocentric framework. This concentration restricts the cross-cultural generalizability of the findings, leaving a critical gap in understanding how these models function across diverse linguistic syntaxes and clinical guidelines globally.

Second, from a methodological perspective, although a formal risk-of-bias assessment is not mandated for scoping reviews, a critical evaluation of the current evidence base reveals notable limitations in study quality. There is significant variability in study designs and evaluation metrics, which complicates direct, standardized comparisons between different AI platforms. Furthermore, the current body of evidence relies almost exclusively on cross-sectional designs and in silico simulations using standardized datasets or fictional vignettes. Although such benchmarking studies are a necessary first step, they often lack ecological validity—failing to emulate the stochastic, high-pressure, and noisy reality of clinical environments. To date, there is a significant lack of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) designed to measure the direct effects of AI-assisted decision-making on tangible endpoints such as surgical success rates or long-term patient prognoses.

Third, there is a fundamental discrepancy between the text-centeredness of current LLM and the requirements of implant procedures. As Salem et al. [40] rightly argued, the inability of text-only models to interpret essential diagnostic imagery—such as periapical radiographs, CBCT volumes, and intraoral scans—is a significant operational bottleneck. Considering these points, AI in implantology, which requires histology and anatomical observation, is bound to have limitations.

Therefore, the direction of future research must shift to two areas.. First, priority should be assigned to the validation of multimodal LLMs that can seamlessly integrate pixel-based visual data with textual clinical histories, thereby mirroring the multimodal diagnostic process of human clinicians. Simultaneously, the field must transition from static, exam-based evaluations to prospective RCTs. Future investigations should quantify the practical utility of these tools in chairside settings, specifically measuring metrics of workflow efficiency, patient comprehension, and post-operative clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

The synthesis of evidence in this scoping review underscores a rapid advancement in the application of Generative AI within dental implantology. While current state-of-the-art models have demonstrated significant theoretical capabilities in standardized evaluations, these results should be interpreted with caution, given that performance varies significantly by platform and is based primarily on computer simulation evaluations. Furthermore, the transition to autonomous clinical application remains heavily impeded by fundamental limitations. The persistent risk of hallucination, coupled with the inherent sensory blindness of text-based models to essential diagnostic imagery, currently precludes independent clinical reliance. Therefore, these technologies should not replace human expertise but rather establish themselves as useful auxiliary tools that complement clinical decision-making. Human intervention must remain an essential standard to ensure patient safety and accuracy in implant treatment.
