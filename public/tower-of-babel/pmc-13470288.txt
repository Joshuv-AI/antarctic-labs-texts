Experiences of Identity-Related Vigilance in Surgical Trainees
Authors: Jamila K. Picart, Lauren A. Szczygiel, Marquise D. Singleterry, Alyssa A. Pradarelli, Hope T. Jackson, Gurjit Sandhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionHow do surgical trainees experience identity-related vigilance in the learning environment?FindingsIn this qualitative study with 20 general surgery trainees, several major themes were identified: trainees experienced vigilance as a recurring negative cycle that leads to feelings of burnout and thoughts of attrition, including perceiving threats of increased identity-related scrutiny, recalibration self-presentation to avoid judgment, and checking the environment for safety or risk. However, opportunities exist to interrupt this cycle through community-building, psychological safety, and fostering belonging.MeaningThe findings of this study suggest that vigilance represents a mechanism by which identity-based mistreatment can produce symptoms of burnout and thoughts of attrition.

Introduction

Vigilance, the subsequent state of heightened awareness and ongoing monitoring for bias or mistreatment arising from prior direct or indirect experiences of identity-based harm,1 is increasingly recognized in academic learning environments, including medicine. In higher education, vigilance has been associated with poor mental health and thoughts of attrition.2,3,4,5,6 In undergraduate medical and dental students, vigilance has also been linked to reduced confidence in clinical readiness.7,8,9 However, there have been minimal studies of identity-related vigilance in graduate medical training.

Surgical trainees from racial and ethnic minoritized backgrounds, women, and lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, intersex, asexual, and other sexual and gender minority (LGBTQIA+) individuals face disproportionately high rates of mistreatment during training, including identity-based mistreatment.10,11,12,13,14 Surgical residents with these social identities have also been noted to experience higher rates of burnout, career dissatisfaction, and attrition.15,16,17 While existing studies of surgical trainee identity-based mistreatment have focused on the discrete mistreatment events and immediate reactions,12,13 they do not provide insight into the subacute and longitudinal processes that may follow mistreatment. A better understanding of how surgical trainees experience the resultant state of vigilance after mistreatment may be critical to addressing identity-based disparities in burnout and attrition. Accordingly, this study was designed to explore how surgical trainees experience vigilance in the learning environment, the consequences of vigilance for learning and for the desire to remain in a surgical career, and how they mitigate its potential negative impacts.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This exploratory qualitative study was deemed exempt from review by the University of Michigan institutional review board (IRBMED HUM No. 00267016). All participants provided verbal informed consent prior to participation, including consent to record, to transcribe the interviews, and to publish deidentified quotes. This study adheres to the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) reporting guideline.

Our analytic approach was informed by a social constructivist perspective, which conceptualizes race, gender, and other identities as produced through social structures rather than as inherent biological attributes.18 This epistemological stance prioritizes participants’ subjective interpretations of their experiences as central to understanding complex social phenomena within training environments19 and informed our interview questions, data analysis, and theme development.

Participants were recruited using site-mediated, purposive convenience sampling. Residency programs with which the research team had previous collaboration were asked to distribute study invitation emails to their residents and fellows. All current surgical residents and fellows were eligible for inclusion. Trainees self-selected into the study by responding to the research team directly. Additional recruitment occurred through messaging at national surgery conferences. Each participant received $25 compensation. Recruitment occurred between May to October 2025. The final sample was considered adequate based on information power.20 With a focused study aim, the highly specific and rich data collected, which were coded concurrently with recruitment, yielded sufficient information to address the research question.

Data Collection

A semistructured interview guide was developed through an iterative review and pilot-testing process. It was refined throughout data collection to enable deeper exploration during interviews, consistent with established qualitative research practices (interview guide in eMethods in Supplement 1). The guide covered how vigilance may be experienced in the surgical learning environment, including the extent to which trainees felt scrutinized by others, how they perceived their identities factoring into this, and how this affected their behaviors. Interview guide development was led by J.K.P., with a study member (L.A.S.) assisting and the senior author (G.S.) providing final feedback. Demographic information, including age, year in residency training, program type, gender identity, sexual orientation, race and/or ethnicity, and disadvantaged socioeconomic background, was collected via a Qualtrics survey completed by the participant. Interviews were conducted using secure teleconferencing software by a single PhD-level social scientist with expertise in qualitative interviewing (L.A.S.). The mean (SD) interview duration was 34 (8) minutes. All interviews were audio recorded, transcribed, and deidentified.

Data Analysis

We uploaded interview transcripts into MAXQDA 2024 software (VERBI Software) for storage and analysis. Our iterative, inductive analytic approach was informed by the framework approach to thematic analysis.21 Development of the coding framework was led by L.A.S. with study members (J.K.P. and M.D.S.) assisting and the senior author (G.S.) providing final feedback. Three authors (J.K.P., M.D.S., and A.P.) are current or prior general surgery residents, and 2 authors (L.A.S. and G.S.) are qualitative researchers. All transcripts were read multiple times to generate inductive codes. Codes were applied to subsequent sets of interviews until we reached coding saturation, the point at which no new codes were generated.22 The final codebook was applied to all interview transcripts. After coding all transcripts, we reviewed the coded content, identified themes, and synthesized data across themes.

We employed several strategies to enhance the trustworthiness and rigor of this qualitative study. Analysis was conducted iteratively by a multidisciplinary research team. Our team consists of 5 Black and Brown cisgender women (2 surgical residents, 1 surgical faculty, and 2 PhD scientists) within a department of surgery, a field in which we are underrepresented. Navigating these professional environments ourselves, we bring familiarity and deep investment in this topic, which may have encouraged participants’ trust. We recognize the risk of confirmatory bias inherent to this closeness. To address this, we engaged in reflexive team discussions and attended to discrepancies. We present this work as researchers, both professionally and personally invested in advancing equity within surgical training. Credibility was further supported through member checking, in which participants reviewed brief summaries of preliminary themes and provided feedback on whether they were consistent with their experiences.

Results

We interviewed 20 surgical trainees across 7 training sites; 18 participants completed demographic surveys, and 2 did not respond after multiple reminders. Nine trainees (45.0%) identified as women and 9 (45.0%) as men; 16 (80.0%) identified as heterosexual or straight, 2 (10.0%) as homosexual, gay or lesbian, or queer; 5 (25.0%) identified as Asian, 6 (30.0%) as Black or African American, 1 (5.0%) as Middle Eastern or North African, 1 (5.0%) as multiple race, and 6 (30.0%) as White (Table 1).

Four Major Themes Identified by Participants Experiencing Vigilance

Identification of Perceived Threat of Being Under Increased Scrutiny

Interviewees described a range of interactions that triggered feelings of vigilance, most often subtle or ambiguous moments rather than overt discrimination. These included microaggressions such as comments about an accent, or seemingly innocuous inquiries that nevertheless heightened awareness of being different in some way or being “other” than their peers. For example, 1 participant, an international medical graduate, described small talk in which he was asked about where he trained, a topic unrelated to their current conversation. This made the trainee feel “very defensive” and compelled him to “defend the quality” of his training (participant 20). These kinds of interactions foregrounded aspects of trainees’ social identities and prompted hypervigilance around anticipating future discrimination, bias, or harsher judgment compared with their peers.

Although our sample included surgical trainees from both minoritized and majority backgrounds, their interpretations of scrutiny diverged. All trainees described feeling under the microscope as a routine element of surgical training. However, trainees from a majority background tended to attribute criticism to the culture of surgical training and as something “not meant to hurt you…even if it’s very blunt” (participant 07). In contrast, trainees from minoritized backgrounds—especially those with prior experiences of discrimination—were more likely to perceive additional scrutiny, beyond what is typical in surgical training, as related to their identity. They described “assessing [surroundings] for psychological safety” at all times (participant 9). Several participants viewed this mindset as a protective strategy, characterizing vigilance as necessary to prevent further harm since they felt more at risk of mistreatment. One participant linked their vigilance to receiving vague, potentially biased formal feedback. They explained, “I was like, am I crazy?…It’s these words, right? These, like, ‘inefficiency,’ or not ‘fitting in with our culture,’ and ‘professionalism concerns,’ and all of that is how they get you, right?” (participant 10).

Recalibration of Self-Presentation to Prevent Future Negative Judgment

Once feelings of vigilance were triggered—often when an aspect of their identity was made salient— interviewees described feeling compelled to modify their speech, behavior, or appearance to prevent anticipated negative treatment. One participant explained, “I’m suddenly alert that maybe I need to present myself as more professional and authoritative, otherwise [patients] won’t view me as the doctor who they need to listen to” (participant 02). Trainees emphasized that this recalibration was not a single event, but a continual process of monitoring and making micro-adjustments.

Participants described this state as highly stressful. Many recounted moments of immediately second-guessing their actions and replaying interactions to scrutinize whether they had said or done something that invited bias or undermined their professional reputations. One trainee described this constant recalibration as interfering with their ability to be authentic, explaining, “I’m always wondering—should I be a little more confident or a little less confident?” (participant 13). This mindset often extended into off hours, with participants describing perseverating over past interactions, rehearsing conversations, or strategizing changes they could make to protect themselves from poor treatment. One trainee reflected, “Going home, I would think, OK, what am I going to do differently tomorrow? How can I make the situation better for myself?” (participant 3).

Checking the Environment for Safety and Risk

In addition to monitoring and adjusting their own behavior, participants described closely monitoring others and their broader training environments. This external monitoring involved watching how attendings, peers, patients, and team members responded to them, including interpreting tone, body language, and patterns of interaction. Trainees described “checking” others’ reactions to how they were presenting themselves and continually updating their sense of fit. As a trainee reflected, “It felt like I was paranoid. It felt like I was reading into situations all the time, much more than I should have been” (participant 19).

Importantly, this monitoring was not simply an effort to ensure others’ approval or to be liked, but rather an attempt to ensure their identity, and how it is being perceived within the environment, was not contributing to negative interactions. Because the reasons for negative or distant interactions were rarely explicit, trainees described difficulty determining whether such moments reflected routine interpersonal variation, situational factors, or potential bias. As a result, external cues seldom provided relief, and external monitoring persisted. One trainee explained what this lack of closure felt like: “And you’re like…were you just having a bad day yesterday? What’s up? What is going on? I thought I was crazy” (participant 8). Many trainees expressed fatigue from the need to consistently monitor how their identity may contribute to how they are perceived.

Interrupting the Cycle

Trainees described the cycle of vigilance as ultimately unsustainable given the cumulative emotional and cognitive effort required to remain “always on,” continuously monitoring both themselves and their environments. Over time, participants described reaching a point where the ongoing demands of vigilance could no longer be maintained in the same way. Interviewees described 2 primary pathways through which the cycle of vigilance was interrupted: disengagement from training through burnout or attrition, or temporary relief achieved through community, belonging, and support.

Burnout and Attrition

For some trainees, the prolonged demands of vigilance led to emotional or cognitive disengagement from training. Participants described feeling depleted, detached, or less invested in their work because of the sustained effort required to anticipate and manage potential bias. As 1 trainee reflected, “Is it actually worth it to like do this extra however much energy a day to address all these like microaggressions.…And for a while I was like, maybe it’s not. Maybe I should quit and I would be happier” (participant 12). In some cases, trainees described withdrawing emotionally as a protective response; in others, participants described thoughts of leaving their training programs or the profession altogether.

Temporary Relief Through Community and Belonging

Trainees also described moments of temporary relief from vigilance when they experienced a sense of community, belonging, or shared understanding with peers, mentors, or affinity groups. These relationships provided spaces where trainees felt less scrutinized, less responsible for monitoring how they were being perceived, allowing them to “process [feelings of vigilance] and just not letting it get super under my skin on a day-to-day basis” (participant 3). Participants described these connections as buffering the impact of vigilance by offering validation, psychological safety, and opportunities to temporarily lower their guard. However, this relief was often situational and time limited, rather than a permanent solution.

Discussion

The purpose of this qualitative study was to better understand whether trainees experience vigilance in the surgical learning environment, bridging the gap from reports of mistreatment to subacute and long-term effects. Our results showed that trainees made to feel othered in relation to some aspect of their identity experience vigilance as a recurring negative cycle, including perceiving threats of increased scrutiny in the workplace, adjusting self-presentation to avoid negative judgments, and monitoring the environment for safety and risk. Furthermore, this experience stood in stark contrast to the expected attention and critique that all trainees participating in this study, identity-agnostic, experienced as surgical trainees. Our study also highlighted opportunities, such as intentional community building, creating psychologically safe spaces, and enhancing belonging to interrupt the negative cycle of vigilance.

Previous studies have identified surgical trainee well-being as a critical concern, not simply for the learners themselves but for the sustainability of the health care system.23,24,25 Surgical trainee well-being is essential to patient safety, learning and professional development, and workforce sustainability.23,25,26,27 Burnout and attrition flourish when well-being is threatened. Identity-based mistreatment is a well-known, preventable threat to surgical trainees’ well-being.13 Our study builds on prior research quantifying the frequency of identity-based mistreatment to examine how these events affect a trainee’s day-to-day experiences in the learning environment, including increased feelings of burnout and thoughts of voluntary attrition. To our knowledge, prior studies found identity-based mistreatment to be associated with thoughts of burnout, but had limited explanatory insight into the social processes contributing to the relationship. Our study demonstrates that experiences of vigilance may play a role in how identity-based mistreatment contributes to reduced career satisfaction and thoughts of attrition among trainees.

One of the most critical findings of our study is the distinction between attention expected during residency training and the attention that elicits experiences of vigilance. Surgical trainees are constantly evaluated in operative skills, bedside manner, medical knowledge, professionalism, and more.28 Importantly, educational attention aims to help trainees achieve competency through objective feedback on their performance. Even if feedback is critical, if it is focused solely on the trainee’s performance, it is not linked to experiences of vigilance. Instead, vigilance was often associated with experiences of scrutiny related to the trainee’s identity or with vague feedback that was not contextualized in relation to their performance. Trainees who have been made to feel other than their peers are more likely to report feeling vigilant. Feedback should not reference aspects of a trainee’s identity. In cases of vague feedback, although it is unlikely that the faculty intended harm, harm can be avoided by providing a clear description of the discrete performance being evaluated.29

This study is noteworthy as the first, to our knowledge, to examine experiences of vigilance in graduate medical education (GME). General surgery training, and GME in general, is high stakes, requiring trainees to learn while caring for vulnerable patients, constantly facing the fear of failure, increasing responsibility, and ongoing assessments. These aspects of medical education impose a significant cognitive load on trainees. Recent focus has centered on improving the learning environment to reduce unnecessary cognitive load.30,31 Vigilance imposes an additional cognitive and emotional burden that has yet to be addressed in research. Although it affects a subgroup of trainees, the diversion of attention away from learning and patient care to avoid unwanted identity-related attention is critical. Future research should investigate whether similar findings are observed in other specialties and assess their impact across the graduate medical education learner population.

While further research explores the extent of identity-related vigilance in GME, institutions can begin addressing it. Institutions can begin locally, by fostering a learning environment where mistakes are viewed as part of training rather than reflections of identity.32 Furthermore, institutions can support cross-specialty GME events that build camaraderie outside of work.33 Regionally and nationally, programs should support trainee involvement in professional organizations that cultivate inclusive networks and affirm othered identities.

Strengths and Limitations

Using qualitative methodology, we explored identity-related vigilance which has been minimally described in graduate medical education. To our knowledge, vigilance has not been described in a combined learning and working environment, such as a surgical residency. Finally, we conducted thorough quality checks, including consensus coding, established thematic sufficiency, and conducted member checking to ensure the accuracy of our results.

This study should be interpreted in light of its limitations. First, trainees with more profound experiences of vigilance may have been more likely to participate in our study. However, through member checking, we mitigated bias and ensured that the experiences presented resonated with all participants. Second, while it is an important area for future study, we cannot be sure that the findings presented here represent the experiences of other graduate medical education learners.

Conclusions

In this qualitative study, we examined how surgical trainees experienced vigilance, a heightened state of awareness and continuous monitoring for bias or mistreatment stemming from previous identity-based harm. Our findings suggest that identity-based harm can lead to ongoing negative consequences, including feelings of burnout and thoughts of attrition. To promote surgeon well-being and a sustainable workforce, the surgical community needs to intentionally focus on reducing incidents of identity-based mistreatment and creating community spaces to mitigate the impacts of vigilance within our community.
