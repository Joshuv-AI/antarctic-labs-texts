Are we back to normal? Evaluating the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on referrals to a child protection service in a tertiary care centre in Vienna, Austria
Authors: Chryssa Grylli, Sophie Klomfar, Johanna Brandlechner, Laura Schaller, Katharina Weimann, Anastasia Pantazidou, Eva Mora-Theuer, Christoph Krall, Catherine Quantin, Susanne Greber-Platzer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReports worldwide on suspected child maltreatment (CM) during and after the COVID-19 pandemic have been inconsistent. As no study has yet systematically examined whether structural disruptions to child protection systems have left measurable and lasting traces in referral patterns beyond the acute crisis phase, this study investigated the pandemic’s impact on suspected CM referrals to the Forensic Examination Center for Children and Adolescents (FOKUS) in Vienna, Austria, and whether enduring shifts in CM presentation and referral dynamics have occurred.MethodsRetrospective study using patient administrative data of suspected CM cases referred to FOKUS between 16 March 2019 and 15 March 2023, covering the periods before, during and after the pandemic.ResultsA total of 401 patients were referred to FOKUS during the study period. During the COVID-19 period, suspected physical abuse cases declined by 55.3% (p=0.029), while sexual abuse cases remained stable. Post-pandemic reports of suspected physical abuse returned to pre-pandemic levels, whereas suspected sexual abuse cases increased (p=0.013). Suspected sexual abuse cases showed a progressive increase in median age over time (p=0.025). Psychological support was more frequently required during the pandemic (63.5% vs 43.8% pre-pandemic, p=0.019), with elevated need continuing into the post-pandemic period (59.8%, p=0.018).ConclusionThe ongoing rise in suspected sexual abuse cases accompanied by different age victim profiles and increased need of psychological support following the pandemic indicates that a return to pre-pandemic norms is unlikely and the notion of ‘back to normal’ seems an oversimplification.Keywords: Child Abuse, COVID-19, Adolescent Health, Child Health, Psychology

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic and its associated mitigation measures have profoundly altered everyday family life worldwide. School closures, social distancing mandates and economic disruption created conditions widely recognised as risk factors for child maltreatment (CM), including increased parental stress, financial hardship, social isolation and reduced access to support services.1 2 At the same time, the very measures intended to contain the virus simultaneously dismantled many of the systems responsible for detecting and reporting CM. The closure of schools and kindergartens removed one of the most critical points of contact between children and mandated reporters, raising serious concerns among paediatricians, teachers, psychologists and child protection organisations about the visibility of maltreatment during lockdown periods.3

International evidence has suggested a paradoxical pattern: while risk factors for CM increased during the pandemic, reports show contradictory patterns of suspected CM cases incidence during this period.4 5 The German Society for Child Protection in Medicine, among others, explicitly warned that pre-existing family vulnerabilities were exacerbated by pandemic-related stressors, and that the reduction in face-to-face contacts with professionals created a dangerous blind spot in child protection systems.2 Critically, the consequences of these disruptions were not expected to resolve with the lifting of restrictions, but rather to persist and potentially manifest in altered patterns of CM reporting in the years following the pandemic.6

In Austria, pandemic mitigation measures were implemented in waves of varying intensity. Hard lockdowns with complete closure of schools and kindergartens took place for most of the period between March 2020 and February 2021, before restrictions were substantially reduced in 2021 and most educational and social services resumed normal operations, except for a 3-week closure in April 2021.7 These recurring school and kindergarten closures are particularly relevant for CM surveillance, as educational institutions represent a primary gateway through which suspected maltreatment is identified and referred to child protection services (CPS).8

The psychological impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on children and adolescents has been well documented, with studies reporting significant increases in anxiety, depressive symptoms and reduced quality of life.9 In Austria, these consequences were particularly pronounced among youth from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, for whom financial hardship and reduced access to support services compounded pandemic-related stress.10 Mental health professionals reported a marked increase in adolescents seeking emergency psychiatric care for non-suicidal self-injury driven by disrupted daily structure, restricted social support and limited access to professional help during lockdown.11 To our knowledge, no study has yet systematically examined the compounded experience of children simultaneously exposed to CM and post-pandemic-related stressors, nor whether the structural disruptions to child protection systems have left measurable and lasting traces in referral patterns beyond the acute phase of the crisis.

Despite growing international concern, there remains a significant gap in the literature regarding the longitudinal impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on suspected CM referrals to paediatric health centres, particularly studies that simultaneously examine multiple types of maltreatment, track changes across distinct pandemic phases and extend follow-up into the post-pandemic period. This challenge is further compounded in Austria by the absence of a centralised national register with uniform documentation for CM cases, which makes the systematic extraction and comparison of child protection data across institutions and time periods particularly difficult.12 Most existing studies either focus on one single CM type or do not differentiate between CM types or limit their time windows,5 13 leaving open the question of whether pandemic-related disruptions to child protection systems have had lasting effects beyond the acute phase of the crisis.

The Forensic Examination Center for Children and Adolescents (FOKUS) is a tertiary CPS at the Department of Pediatrics and Adolescent Medicine of the Medical University Hospital, Vienna, Austria. The multidisciplinary team consists of paediatricians, clinical psychologists, forensic physicians, a social worker and research assistants. FOKUS offers support to child protection teams in Vienna, with a focus on timely investigations and clarifications in cases of suspected CM.12 14 Its central position and uninterrupted availability throughout the COVID-19 pandemic make FOKUS uniquely suited to capture longitudinal trends in CM referrals across the pre-pandemic, during pandemic and post-pandemic periods.

The primary aim of this study was therefore to evaluate the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on suspected CM cases referred to FOKUS in Vienna, comparing the number and characteristics of referrals across the pre-pandemic, pandemic and post-pandemic periods. Secondary objectives included assessing the urgency and extent of psychological support required by affected children as well as analysing the frequency and characteristics of specific maltreatment types, namely physical and sexual abuse. Particular attention was given to whether pre-pandemic patterns have been restored or whether the pandemic has produced enduring shifts in CM presentation and referral dynamics.

Methods

All suspected CM cases reported to FOKUS during a 4-year time period, from 16 March 2019 to 15 March 2023, were included in this retrospective study.

Database, patient inclusion and variables

Data were derived from the Department of Pediatrics and Adolescent Medicine of the Medical University of Vienna, using the secure Clinical Information System. Patients and the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

All patients were under 18 years of age at the time of FOKUS enrolment. Suspected CM cases comprised all index patients (ie, the child through whom a suspected case was initially reported to FOKUS) and, where applicable, their siblings or other minors residing in the same household.

Data collection encompassed all types of CM documented at FOKUS, including physical, sexual and psychological abuse and neglect, as well as less frequently occurring forms such as female genital mutilation, fabricated or induced illness and cases classified as unclear; the latter referring to patients who exhibited signs suggestive of post-traumatic stress in the absence of evident physical indicators of maltreatment. Cases were included based on suspected maltreatment at the time of referral. Although multiple forms of suspected CM could coexist within a single case, analyses were based exclusively on the primary suspected maltreatment documented at the time of referral. Demographic information was collected for all referred individuals, including age and gender (male, female or other) of suspected CM cases, as well as siblings of referred patients, with the study population encompassing all individuals between 0 and 18 years of age.

The referral pathways through which cases reached FOKUS were also documented. FOKUS operates as a tertiary CPS whose expertise can be requested by the hospital-based multidisciplinary CPS of all Vienna-based hospitals for support in the assessment of complex suspected CM cases, whether treated as inpatients or outpatients. These child protection teams are alerted through various channels, including direct clinical observation during patient contact as well as through referrals from social services (with schools and kindergartens reporting through this channel), police, out-of-home care and self-referrals to the emergency department by patients themselves or their parents, guardians or relatives.12

Psychological support and interventions are provided within FOKUS when deemed necessary. The need for such support is generally determined by medical professionals based on their clinical assessment. The interventions consist of collecting patients’ medical and psychosocial histories, conducting clinical psychological explorations, providing supportive counselling as well as interventions to promote stabilisation and furthermore carrying out clinical psychological diagnostics. Involvement of FOKUS psychologists is particularly considered when the suspected incident remains unclear, for instance, in cases of inconsistency between the reported history and the presenting injury or when signs of distress are observed in the child, adolescent or their caregivers during the medical evaluation.

Time frames

The data collection period covers 4 years (16 March 2019 to 15 March 2023). Each time frame covered 1 year and always started on 16 March due to the start of the first lockdown on 16 March 2020. Four study groups were created: pre-COVID (16 March 2019 to 15 March 2020); COVID-1 (16 March 2020 to 15 March 2021); COVID-2 (16 March 2021 to 15 March 2022); and post-COVID (16 March 2022 to 15 March 2023).

Data analysis

Patient data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.26 and R statistical software V.3.5.0. P values <0.05 are considered statistically significant.

Suspected CM cases were retrieved and analysed chronologically and according to the type of suspected maltreatment reported. Descriptive statistics were chosen to present patient details and describe the study groups. Continuous variables were compared among the study groups using linear regression, χ2 test, Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test. Proportions were compared among the study groups using χ2 tests. Poisson regression analysis was performed to analyse the effect of time on reported suspected CM cases.

Inferential statistics were performed initially on total numbers of suspected CM cases followed by separate analysis according to type of maltreatment suspected. Suspected physical and sexual abuse cases were further analysed, while for the remaining types of suspected CM, independent inferential statistical analysis was not feasible due to the small numbers reported (table 1).

Referral systems

CPS (32.7%) and healthcare professionals (33.9%) were the two largest referral sources across the entire study period, followed by self-presentations (19.5%), police (9.5%) and out-of-home care settings (3.5%), as shown in online supplemental table 1. CPS referrals showed by far the largest absolute decrease during COVID-1 compared with pre-COVID (n=48–16=32; −67%) disproportionate to the overall reduction in total referrals during this period (112–63=49; −43.8%); for details see online supplemental table 2. CPS was also the dominant referral source specifically for suspected physical abuse (53.5% across the study period), whereas police referrals predominated suspected sexual abuse (20.7%).

Relative proportions of different suspected maltreatment types (physical abuse vs sexual abuse vs all other) of referrals remained stable across all periods for both CPS and police referrals. Suspected physical abuse referrals constituted the vast majority of CPS referrals throughout the entire period, with values ranging from 69.25% to 75%, while suspected sexual abuse referrals constituted 76.9–88.9% of police referrals (see table 4).

During COVID-1, we observed an abrupt reduction in visits to the outpatient emergency unit of the Department of Pediatrics and Adolescent Medicine, with a drop of 83.6% compared with pre-COVID period (n=15 963 cases during pre-COVID and n=2610 in COVID-1). In contrast, the relative frequency of FOKUS cases per 1000 paediatric emergency cases has increased from seven cases per 1000 in the pre-COVID period to 24 cases per 1000 in the COVID-1 period.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated suspected CM cases referred to FOKUS across four distinct time periods—pre-pandemic (pre-COVID), during the pandemic (COVID-1 and COVID-2) and post-pandemic (post-COVID)—with the aim of assessing changes in overall referral numbers and differences in the type of maltreatment, referral pathways and the need for psychological support. Our findings reveal a complex and multifaceted picture that resists simple interpretation and underscores the importance of analysing different types of suspected CM separately while also considering the functioning of referral systems during the pandemic.

Overall case numbers and the paradox of declining referrals

A significant decrease in total suspected CM cases was observed during the first pandemic period (COVID-1) compared with the pre-pandemic period (pre-COVID), with numbers gradually returning to pre-pandemic levels during the second pandemic period (COVID-2) and the post-pandemic period (post-COVID). This decline should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of reduced CM incidence. Reductions in emergency department visits and hospitalisations during the COVID-1 pandemic have been reported worldwide (eg, ref 15), a pattern mirrored at our own paediatric department, where overall emergency visits fell by 83.6% during this period. Despite this reduction in absolute numbers, the relative frequency of suspected CM cases among total emergency presentations increased. This pattern was similarly reported in French hospitals, where the relative frequency of young children hospitalised due to physical abuse rose even as absolute numbers remained stable.16 Similarly, studies conducted across Europe during the pandemic indicate that despite a reduction in overall referral numbers, the severity and complexity of cases were more pronounced compared with the pre-pandemic period.17 18 Studies drawing on administrative data have broadly reported decreases in suspected CM referrals during the pandemic, while cross-sectional studies have more frequently reported increases,19 a heterogeneity of trends also observed across European countries.5 It is important to note that the decline in referrals during COVID-1 was disproportionately driven by CPS, which showed the largest absolute decrease in any referral pathway, while other pathways remained comparatively unaffected. This could be explained by the closure of schools and kindergartens during lockdowns, which normally would have detected and referred suspected cases to CPS, and it points to the underfunction of this particular detection channel. These findings highlight the critical importance of distinguishing between reduced visibility of CM and reduced incidence and point to the need for a more granular analysis by maltreatment type and referral pathway.

Physical abuse: a surveillance artefact

Suspected physical abuse cases declined significantly during both COVID-1 and COVID-2 periods, only returning to pre-pandemic levels in the post-pandemic period. These findings are consistent with the chronology of lockdown measures in Austria, with the most severe restrictions concentrated in COVID-1 and a gradual normalisation of social services, schools and kindergartens during COVID-2. Crucially, our data demonstrate that social services account for over 50% of physical abuse referrals to FOKUS. The closure and reduced functioning of these institutions during the pandemic therefore likely reduced the primary detection pathways for physical abuse, suggesting that the observed decline reflects a decrease in surveillance rather than a genuine reduction in CM. Similar decreases in physical abuse referrals have been reported in the USA.20 Studies from the USA and UK reporting increases in physical abuse during the first lockdown21–23 are not necessarily contradictory: these studies focused predominantly on infants and young children under 2 years of age suspected of abusive head trauma, a subgroup unlikely to be affected by school and kindergarten closures, and therefore not comparable to our broader age range.

Sexual abuse: a different pattern requiring a different explanation

As expected, females represented the vast majority of victims in suspected sexual abuse cases, a gender distribution well documented in the literature.24 25 The higher proportion of females in total CM cases can largely be attributed to the substantial contribution of suspected sexual abuse referrals.

In contrast to physical abuse, suspected sexual abuse cases did not decline during the pandemic period and showed a progressive and significant increase in both suspected cases and average victim age, reaching significantly higher levels by the post-pandemic period. This divergent pattern could be linked to the distinct referral pathways for sexual abuse: unlike physical abuse, the majority of sexual abuse cases reaching FOKUS were referred via police or through self-referrals to the emergency department, with social services accounting for 12.9% of referrals. These channels remained largely functional during lockdown, explaining the relative stability of sexual abuse referrals during the pandemic itself. These findings are corroborated by similar results from the USA, where total rates of CM and neglect as well as physical abuse alone were comparable before and after school closures due to COVID-19, while sexual abuse rates increased following school closures.26 Consistent with our findings, a recent study from Spain reported an increase in suspected child and adolescent sexual violence after the pandemic, with adolescents being particularly affected and more frequently exposed to non-cohabiting aggressors.27

The post-pandemic increase, however, warrants closer examination. Several converging factors may contribute to this trend. During lockdown, adolescents were deprived of the social contacts necessary for healthy development, while simultaneously experiencing a marked rise in screen time and online activity. Data from the UK indicate an increase of up to 17% in online sexual crimes against children during the pandemic,28 and European Union Agency for Law Enforcement Cooperation (EUROPOL) reported a rise in the production and sharing of child sexual abuse material online during this period.29 The pandemic-related surge in pornography consumption among adolescents, driven by social isolation, boredom and the need to manage negative emotions, has raised concerns about its potential role in shaping sexual attitudes, including the normalisation of coercive behaviours and gender-based power imbalances.30 31 Discussions with colleagues and child protection professionals across Austria and other European countries further point to a growing pattern of sexual violence in which perpetrators are frequently minors, who are recent acquaintances or peers of the victim; a trend that warrants systematic investigation. Additionally, the growing societal visibility of sexual violence through campaigns such as MeToo may have lowered barriers to disclosure, contributing to increased reporting.32 Taken together, these factors suggest that the post-pandemic rise in suspected sexual abuse cases and the concurrent shift towards older victims may reflect both genuinely altered risk landscapes and improved reporting.

Psychological support: an indicator of increasing complexity

The need for psychological support among referred CM cases increased significantly during the pandemic and remained elevated in the post-pandemic period, with no return to pre-pandemic levels observed. This persistent elevation is particularly notable and may serve as an indicator of the increasing complexity and severity of cases reaching FOKUS. It is worth noting that during the COVID-2 period, no significant increase in psychological support was recorded compared with pre-pandemic levels, likely reflecting a shortage of available psychologists during this phase rather than a genuine reduction in need; this is a limitation that should be considered when interpreting these figures.

The sustained elevated need for psychological support observed in our data and persisting well beyond the acute pandemic phase is unlikely to reflect a single cause, but rather an accumulation of interacting stressors. Pandemic-related disruptions have left lasting psychological traces in the general child and adolescent population. Children’s mental health remained significantly below pre-pandemic levels as late as autumn 2024.33 For children additionally exposed to CM, this already impaired baseline was likely further compounded by the psychological burden of maltreatment itself, a double vulnerability with consequences that extend well beyond the pandemic period. Well-established protective factors against the psychological sequelae of trauma, such as social contact and peer support,25 were precisely the resources most severely curtailed by lockdown measures, removing a key buffer for an already vulnerable group. These findings underscore the importance of a holistic and sustained approach to CM case management that explicitly accounts for the long-term psychological consequences of compounded adversity.34

Synthesis and implications

These results have direct implications for child protection policy: the continued functioning of schools, kindergartens and social services as detection and referral mechanisms must be explicitly safeguarded in future public health emergencies. The sustained increase in the need for psychological support further suggests that CM response services should anticipate long-term mental health demands beyond acute crisis periods. Furthermore, the ongoing rise in suspected sexual abuse cases, coupled with shifting victim age profiles and the recently documented absence of national clinical guidelines for the management of child sexual abuse in many European countries,35 underscores the urgent need for targeted research, prevention strategies and clinical protocols tailored to this evolving landscape.

Limitations

Due to the tertiary setting of this study, the range, prevalence, severity and complexity of CM cases may differ from those managed at the local level, where less complex cases may be handled exclusively by social services without requiring specialised involvement. Furthermore, cases included in this study are referrals at a local level and may not reflect the actual trends on referrals and case characteristics at a national or international level.

Strengths

Cases were evaluated by a trained and experienced multidisciplinary child protection team, including physicians and psychologists, applying standardised definitions and protocols for the diagnosis and evaluation of suspected CM throughout the study period.

Future research

Future research should prioritise elucidating the drivers of the post-pandemic increase in suspected sexual abuse, with particular attention to the characteristics of perpetrators and victims, their relationship and the context in which abuse occurs. The role of pandemic-related changes in adolescent online behaviour, including pornography consumption and its potential influence on sexual attitudes, boundary setting and peer relationships, represents a compelling and underexplored avenue for investigation. Analysis of referral pathways revealed interesting trends that warrant further investigation. We are currently extending the study period to evaluate longer term changes in referral pathways and their implications for future detection and prevention strategies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have presented evidence that changes in suspected CM cases during COVID-19 are multifactorial in nature and cannot be interpreted without careful consideration of maltreatment type and the functioning of referral systems. The ongoing rise in suspected sexual abuse cases, accompanied by shifting victim age profiles and a persistently elevated need for psychological support, indicates that a return to pre-pandemic norms is unlikely and that the notion of ‘back to normal’ remains an oversimplification.
