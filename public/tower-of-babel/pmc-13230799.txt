A machine-learning virtual source model for nanoscale transistors
Authors: Qimao Yang, Jing Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neural networks (NNs) are powerful tools for modeling transistor characteristics from data, yet purely data-driven models are data-hungry, can yield unphysical results, and often fail to generalize. We present an integrated NN–virtual source (NN–VS) model that combines the physical rigor of the virtual source framework with the adaptive learning of neural networks. This end-to-end trainable hybrid model achieves higher accuracy, stronger data efficiency, and superior extrapolation power compared to pure NN models, while eliminating the manual parameter extraction required in the conventional VS models. Importantly, the NN–VS model demonstrates robust performance in data-limited scenarios. The model accurately captures experimental nanoscale transistor characteristics, including two-dimensional (2D) semiconductor field-effect transistors (FETs) and silicon FinFETs. The application of the model is illustrated in compute-in-memory circuit simulations with ferroelectric FETs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47672-7.

Introduction

As transistor channel lengths scale down to the nanometer regime and a wide variety of new channel materials are being explored, accurate and efficient transistor models that can describe a variety of nanotransistors are valuable to semiconductor design. The virtual source (VS) model1 is a semi-empirical model based on transistor device characteristics in both subthreshold and above-threshold regimes. It has been successfully applied to a variety of nanotransistors, including silicon MOSFETs1, carbon nanotube FETs2, graphene FETs3, and GaN MOSFETs4. The model, however, requires manual and device-specific parameter extraction. Its parameters need to be re-extracted from device to device, which makes the process labor-intensive and operator-dependent.

Prior works have explored various data-driven approaches for semiconductor device simulation. Machine learning (ML)-based device models have recently been developed to accelerate the design loop. For instance, neural networks have been employed to directly map device geometries to current-voltage (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I\text {-}V$$\end{document}) characteristics5,6, serving as high-speed surrogates for TCAD simulations. More advanced architectures have also been proposed to capture complex device behaviors, such as variability and degradation7–9. These pure ML approaches demonstrate promising accuracy and speed. However, purely ML-based models can suffer from significant limitations, particularly the risk of making unphysical predictions such as non-zero source-drain current when the applied drain voltage is zero. In addition, enhancing model accuracy requires a large volume of training data, which increases the burden of precise data acquisition.

To overcome these challenges, hybrid models have been introduced. These models express the current as the product of a factor predicted by a neural network and a current predicted by a well-established transistor model, such as the Berkeley Short-channel IGFET Model (BSIM)10, a ballistic transistor model11, to improve the model performance or simplified compact equations12 to predict the drain current. The approach improves the model accuracy, but the BSIM model has hundreds of model parameters which requires significant domain knowledge and effort to extract. The ballistic transistor model involves a self-consistent loop that lowers the model speed. Recent advancements have also introduced inverse modeling frameworks for automated parameter extraction13 and meta-learning approaches for fast model adaptation14. While these methods significantly accelerate the modeling process compared to traditional TCAD or manual extraction, a critical limitation of these approaches is their reliance on prior data availability for the specific device under test. For instance, parameter extraction frameworks require full \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I\text {-}V$$\end{document} curves to function13, and adaptive transfer learning models require a subset of target device measurements for fine-tuning14.

In this work, we develop a hybrid model that combines neural networks with the virtual-source approach, leveraging their strengths while overcoming the limitations for modeling a variety of nanotransistors. The NN component of the integrated model takes the device structural, material, and bias parameters as input, and produces intermediate parameters, which are the model parameters of a simplified VS model component. Notably, the device parameters branch is designed to treat physical parameters as a permutation-invariant set, inspired by the fact that structural/material parameters define the device collectively rather than in ordered tuples. This design enhances both data efficiency and generalization. The VS model component subsequently predicts device characteristics. The integrated NN–VS model framework is fully end-to-end trainable, effectively eliminating the need for manual parameter extraction while embedding physical insights from the VS formalism. Approach

The integration of neural networks into a widely applicable transistor model offers a new approach to take advantage of both the strength of device physics in the transistor model and the strength of interfacing with device data of the NNs. Here, we carefully design the integrated computational model through a “divide-and-conquer” approach to accelerate and automate model parameter extraction, extend the model applicability, and improve model accuracy. While the original VS model requires manual parameter extraction for each device and pure NN models miss the device physics information, our goal is to develop an accurate, interpretable, and simple model that can be applied across multiple devices. Analytical transistor theory

The analytical transistor model is based on the virtual source (VS) theory. The VS model can describe transistor characteristics ranging from drift-diffusion and velocity saturation to quasi-ballistic and ballistic transport regimes15,16. As described extensively in references15,16, it captures characteristics in different transport regimes by assigning model parameters with proper dependence on the transistor’s physical and bias conditions. By combining the VS model with a neural network (NN) in an integrated framework, the NN component provides the flexibility to capture parameter dependencies and account for non-ideal effects such as temperature.

The analytical transistor model component in the integrated model framework takes the output from neural networks and outputs the device characteristics. It is designed to satisfy the following requirements: First, as a step in the overall computational graph that links the device structural, material, and bias parameters to device characteristics, the analytical model needs to be differentiable. Second, the model needs to capture the empirical device characteristics, to alleviate training difficulty and data demand by integrating the domain knowledge of transistor physics. Neural network design

The neural network component, as depicted in Fig. 1, is designed with a dual-branch architecture. This structure is physically motivated to disentangle the influence of a transistor’s static, intrinsic properties from its dynamic operational state under bias.

The first branch, the device branch, processes intrinsic device parameters such as gate insulator thickness (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{ins}$$\end{document}), gate length (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_g$$\end{document}), and dielectric constant (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon _r$$\end{document}). These parameters define the fundamental physical structure of the transistor. To capture the fact that device parameters form an unordered set of heterogeneous physical quantities rather than an ordered vector, the device branch employs a shared-MLP with learnable type embeddings, followed by permutation-invariant pooling. Each parameter (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{ins}, L_g, \epsilon _r$$\end{document}) is tokenized with its type embedding and passed through the same MLP, enforcing a universal mapping conditioned on the parameter type. The outputs are aggregated via mean pooling to form a compact device representation that is invariant to input order and more data-efficient than treating parameters as a simple concatenated vector. This representation is then directly used to determine parameters that are largely governed by the device’s structure, such as the subthreshold swing factor n, the injection velocity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upsilon _0$$\end{document}, the saturation voltage \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_{DSAT}$$\end{document}, and the fitting parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}.

The second, parallel bias branch, processes the external operational voltages, primarily the gate voltage (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_G$$\end{document}) and drain voltage (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_D$$\end{document}). It is designed to capture the transistor’s general response to electrical stimuli.

A key design choice is late fusion via a modulation layer17, where intrinsic device properties (device branch) modulate the response to external biases (bias branch). Concretely, given the bias-branch activations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{h}$$\end{document}, the fusion applies a feature-wise affine transform:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{Fusion}(\textbf{h}\mid \boldsymbol{\rho },\boldsymbol{\omega }) = \boldsymbol{\rho }\odot \textbf{h} + \boldsymbol{\omega }, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\odot$$\end{document} denotes element-wise multiplication and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\rho },\boldsymbol{\omega }$$\end{document} are feature-wise scale and shift vectors predicted by the device branch. This implements the physical notion that device structure scales and shifts the bias response before the analytical VS stage.

Finally, the modulated feature vector is used to predict only the threshold voltage \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_t$$\end{document}. This is because \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_t$$\end{document} is uniquely dependent on both the intrinsic device structure and the applied biases (e.g., through effects like DIBL). Therefore, it is most accurately predicted from the fused representation that combines information from both domains.

It is worth highlighting that this physics-aware disentanglement enables high accuracy using only lightweight, shallow MLPs for each component, avoiding the need for over-parameterized black-box models. The specific dimensions (typically 2-3 layers), along with the hyperparameter optimization methodology (grid search on validation set), are provided in Supplementary Information.

Data generation and model training

Data generation: To illustrate the application of the integrated model to nanoscale transistors, we apply it to both nanotransistors based on two-dimensional (2D) semiconductors and silicon FinFETs.

The 2D MOSFET has a double gated structure similar to those in Ref.18. The channel material is assumed to be monolayer MoS\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}. The device \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I\text {-}V$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q\text {-}V$$\end{document} characteristics of the 2D FET are obtained from numerical device simulations based on self-consistent solutions to the non-equilibrium Green’s function (NEGF) quantum transport equation and the electrostatic Poisson equation. Phonon scattering is treated using the self-consistent Born approximation in the NEGF simulation18,19. The simulation calculates the intrinsic device characteristics without parasitic contact resistance, and has been previously applied to assess 2D FETs near the scaling limits18.

Furthermore, to illustrate that the integrated NN–VS model is general and can be applied to other device data, we also apply it to the device data of the silicon FinFET. The FinFET device data is generated by the Berkeley short-channel IGFET model for common multi-gate transistors (BSIM-CMG) model20.

Baseline NN model for comparison

To rigorously evaluate the performance, accuracy, and data efficiency of the proposed physics-informed NN–VS model, we established a baseline model for comparison. The baseline model was trained and evaluated on the exact same dataset generated by the NEGF and BSIM-CMG simulations described in the Data Generation section.

The baseline model is a state-of-the-art multi-layer perceptron (MLP) model21, an architecture that has been successfully applied to transistor modeling8,9,22,23.

Specifically, following the methodology in Ref.21, to enhance numerical stability across subthreshold and strong inversion regions, the baseline predicts a transformed variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_1 = \ln (I_D/V_D)$$\end{document}, such that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_D = V_D \cdot e^{y_1}$$\end{document}. Furthermore, the training is guided by a composite physics-aware loss function that includes penalties not only for the current error but also for its first and second-order derivatives (conductance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_m, g_d$$\end{document} and their derivatives \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g'_m, g'_d$$\end{document}). This ensures that the MLP learns smooth, physically plausible characteristics. The MLP employed consists of 4 hidden layers, each with 256 neurons.

All other model training settings, including the optimizer parameters of the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001, a batch size of 128, the training data split, and the training duration of 500 epochs were kept identical to those used for the NN–VS model to ensure a fair comparison.

Training by dataset with multiple devices and interpretability of intermediate model parameters

In the original virtual source model, the parameters are extracted for an individual device, and it needs to be re-parameterized again if the device’s geometric or material parameters change1. By harvesting the ML capabilities in the NN–VS model, the model is end-to-end trainable by ML techniques based on the data encompassing a set of devices with varying structural parameters, such as gate insulator thickness and gate length. The NN learns a generalized relationship across multiple devices with varying device parameters, which automates and removes human bias in parameter extraction. This learned relationship maps device structural and material parameters to the intermediate physical parameters, which are the inputs to the VS model component for an entire class of devices. Analyzing this input-output relationship embedded within the trained NN can therefore yield broader physical insights than single-device extraction methods.

The intermediate parameters of the proposed NN–VS model, which serve as the output of the NN component and simultaneously as input to the analytical model, as shown in Fig. 1, can offer valuable insights into device characteristics. As an example, the NN part of the hybrid model predicts n, which is the ratio between the subthreshold swing to its ideal limit, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n=SS/SS_0$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$SS_0\approx 60\,\mathrm {mV/dec}$$\end{document} at room temperature. SS values are calculated from this model-predicted n. Figure 5 compares this derived SS against NEGF-extracted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$SS_{Data}$$\end{document}, demonstrating high predictive accuracy across variations in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_g$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{ins}$$\end{document}, and two dielectric constants, which confirms the accurate learning of n.

This capability allows prediction of the physically meaningful VS model parameters directly from the device structural and material parameters by using the trained NN component. It also offers the flexibility to optimize the design of the device characteristics, such as the subthreshold swing, by using the NN model component.

Model application to circuit simulation

To demonstrate the application of the NN–VS source model in circuit simulations, we simulated a crossbar array (CBA) circuit for analog compute-in-memory (CiM) multiply-accumulate (MAC) computing functionalities, as shown in Fig. 9. The MAC computation is crucial for implementing neuromorphic computing systems, as well as for solving linear algebra computation problems26. The device component in the CBA is a ferroelectric FET (FeFET)26,27. The device can be modeled as a ferroelectric capacitor in series with the gate electrode of a MOSFET, following Ref.28, as illustrated in Fig. 9a and b. The ferroelectric layer is modeled with a thickness of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{FE}=10\,\textrm{nm}$$\end{document} and a relative dielectric constant of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon _{r,FE}=25$$\end{document}. The transistor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I\text {-}V$$\end{document} characteristics are computed by the proposed NN–VS model. The internal node voltage \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_{MOS}$$\end{document}, as shown in Fig. 9b, is computed by solving the charge conservation relation at the internal node as described in detail in Supplementary Information. We focus on demonstrating the use of the transistor model in circuit-level applications. The FeFET devices operate in read mode without polarization switching, and the polarization states are held at fixed, pre-programmed levels to represent matrix elements in multiply–accumulate (MAC) operation.

Based on the model, we simulate a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$4\times 4$$\end{document} FeFET CBA circuit as shown in Fig. 9c. We focus on the read operation in which the ferroelectric polarization values are fixed. The MAC operation is performed by using a scheme as demonstrated experimentally in Ref.26. Each multiplier can take four values. The ferroelectric polarization encodes 4 different polarization values, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{'0'},\,P_{'1'},\,P_{'2'},\,P_{'3'}$$\end{document}, which control the threshold voltage and result in 4 approximately equally spaced current values at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_G=V_D=V_{DD}$$\end{document}, as illustrated in Fig. 9d. To perform the matrix-vector multiplication, the matrix elements are stored into the ferroelectric states of the FeFETs in the CBA, and the vector is encoded into the width of the applied voltage pulse in each row. The MAC computation result is read out as the voltages on the load capacitors of the columns of the CBA.

As shown in Fig. 10, the transient circuit simulation is performed by a time-stepping scheme that numerically solves the charge conservation equation to update the internal gate potential at each step, and it outputs time-dependent current and voltage values. Because the load capacitor collects the current flow through all transistors in a column, its final charge is determined by the sum of the product of the pulse width and the average current through a FeFET over all rows. The results show that the transient circuit characteristics, including both the stepwise current response and the gradual capacitor voltage accumulation, can be accurately reproduced by the NN–VS model. Across all input pulse combinations, the NN–VS predictions closely track the reference device-level NEGF data in both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{out}(t)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_{out}(t)$$\end{document}, demonstrating the model’s fidelity in capturing temporal charge integration effects and current modulation induced by FeFET polarization states.

Conclusion

In this work, we introduced the NN–virtual source (NN–VS) model, a physics-informed framework that integrates the analytical rigor of the virtual source model with the adaptability of neural networks. By embedding physical constraints into a data-driven architecture, the NN–VS model achieves both high accuracy and strong generalization, even under sparse training conditions. This end-to-end trainable framework not only eliminates manual parameter extraction but also extends seamlessly across technologies, from 2D FETs to silicon FinFETs, and to circuit-level applications. The model accurately describes experimental device characteristics of industrial silicon FETs and emerging 2D-semiconductor-based FETs. These results highlight a scalable path toward next-generation device models that are accurate, data-efficient, and physically interpretable.
