Robust MPC for Large-scale Linear Systems
Georg Schildbach

Abstract
State-of-the-art approaches of Robust Model Predictive Control (MPC) are restricted to linear systems of relatively small scale, i.e., with no more than about 5 states. The main reason is the computational burden of determining a robust positively invariant (RPI) set, whose complexity suffers from the curse of dimensionality. The recently proposed approach of Deadbeat Robust Model Predictive Control (DRMPC) is the first that does not rely on an RPI set. Yet it comes with the full set of essential system theoretic guarantees. DRMPC is hence a viable option, in particular, for large-scale systems. This paper introduces a detailed design procedure for DRMPC. It is shown that the optimal control problem generated for DRMPC has exactly the same computational complexity as Nominal MPC. A numerical study validates its applicability to randomly generated large-scale linear systems of various dimensions.



I
INTRODUCTION

Originating from process industry, for systems with slow dynamics
[
1
]
, Model Predictive Control (MPC) has spread to applications with sampling times in the millisecond range, and even below
[
2
]
. However, MPC remains attractive for the control of large-scale systems, such as chemical plants
[
3
]
, building climate control
[
4
]
, energy networks
[
5
]
, and supply chains
[
6
]
.

In most practical applications, an exact model of the system is not available. Therefore, a certain degree of robustness to model uncertainty or disturbances is of great importance. The theory of Robust MPC has advanced significantly over the past decades, with powerful and highly researched frameworks being readily available
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
. However, all existing methods of Robust MPC are limited to small-scale systems. The main reason is that all of them require the (offline) computation of robust positively invariant sets, including the most popular approaches, Tube MPC
[
8
]
and Robust MPC with Affine Disturbance Feedback
[
9
]
. As a result, these conventional approaches may work for systems with state dimensions up to 5, as a rule of thumb.

Commonly explored techniques to circumvent this limitations include model reduction
[
4
]
and distributed MPC
[
3
]
. However, model reduction is not always applicable and leads to additional conservatism, because part of the system dynamics are modeled as disturbances. Distributed MPC comes with an increased computational burden, even if it is distributed, and also leads additional conservatism.

This paper leverages the recently proposed approach of Deadbeat Robust Model Predictive Control (DRMPC)
[
10
,
11
]
for large-scale systems. Roughly speaking, the target is systems with state dimensions of 5 or higher. A precise definition of what ‘large-scale’ means in this context, and which systems fall within the scope of DRMPC, is given in the paper. The paper also provides a detailed description of all steps required for the practical design of the DRMPC, along with a summary of essential system theoretic guarantees. A numerical study validates the applicability of DRMPC to randomly generated large-scale systems, showing that it goes far beyond the capabilities of conventional Robust MPC approaches.

PROBLEM STATEMENT

III-A

Control System

Starting point is a (large-scale) discrete-time, linear-time invariant control system with additive disturbances

x_{k+1}=Ax_{k}+Bu_{k}+d_{k}\;.

(1)

Here
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{0+}
denotes the time step,
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are the state and the input and

is the disturbance at time step

, respectively. System (
1
) comes with a given initial condition
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Assumption 1

(
control system
)

(a) The state of the system
x_{k}
can be measured in each step
k
. (b) The system matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and input matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
form a controllable pair.

The control objective is to regulate the state of system (
1
) to the origin, while respecting state and input constraints:

x_{k}\in\mathbb{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\;,\kern 5.0ptu_{k}\in\mathbb{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}\quad\forall\;k\in\mathbb{Z}_{0+}\;.

(2)

The additive disturbance is also contained in a given disturbance set:

d_{k}\in\mathbb{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\quad\forall\;k\in\mathbb{Z}_{0+}\;.

(3)

Assumption 2

(
constraint sets
)

(a) The input and state constraint sets
\mathbb{U}
and
\mathbb{X}
are polyhedrons containing the origin. (b) The disturbance set
\mathbb{D}
is a polytope containing the origin.

III-B

Large-Scale System

The theory of this paper holds for
all
linear systems (
1
). However, the specific focus of this paper is on
large-scale
systems. This section provides a more precise mathematical characterization of what ‘large-scale’ means in the context or Robust MPC; i.e., what is assumed to be computationally possible and what is prohibitive. It is assumed that a desired prediction horizon
N\geq n
has already been selected.

Assumption 3

(
Nominal MPC
)

(a) The sets
\mathbb{U}
,
\mathbb{X}
,
\mathbb{D}
are given, and stored, in H-representation. (b) The sets
\mathbb{U}
and
\mathbb{X}
are such that the finite-horizon optimal control problem (FHOCP) corresponding to
Nominal MPC
, i.e., without any disturbances, is computationally feasible (in real time).

The H-representations of
\mathbb{U}
and
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{D}
are denoted as
G^{(\mathrm{u})}
,
h^{(\mathrm{u})}
and
G^{(\mathrm{x})}
,
h^{(\mathrm{x})}
and
G^{(\mathrm{d})}
,
h^{(\mathrm{d})}
, respectively. The conversion of an H-representation to its corresponding V-representation, also called
vertex enumeration
, is known to be computationally hard. In fact, the complexity of vertex enumeration is usually polynomial in the input and output size, and exponential only in some special (degenerate) cases
[
12
]
. The input and output size, i.e., the combination of H-representation and V-representation of a polyhedron, however, generally grows exponentially with the dimension, even in simple cases (e.g., a hyper-cube).

Assumption 4

(
Minkowski sum,
optional
)

(a) Vertex enumeration for the sets
\mathbb{U}
,
\mathbb{X}
,
\mathbb{D}
is computationally prohibitive. (b) Computing the Minkowski sum of any two of these sets, or their linear transformations, is intractable.

Assumption 5

(
Pontryagin difference
)

It is possible to perform linear transformations and Pontryagin differences of all relevant sets.

To exemplify Assumption
5
, consider a
linear transformation
of the set
\mathbb{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
by a matrix
K\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. The set
K\mathbb{D}
is a polyhedron in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, characterized by

K\mathbb{D}=\Bigl\{Kd\;\Big|\;d\in\mathbb{D}\Bigr\}\;.

(4)

Proposition 6

Let
K\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be any linear transformation matrix. The Pontryagin difference
\mathbb{U}\ominus K\mathbb{D}
is given by

u\in\mathbb{U}\ominus K\mathbb{D}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad G^{(\mathrm{u})}u\leq h^{(\mathrm{u})}-s

(5a)

where
s
is a vector whose elements satisfy

\hskip 128.0374pts_{[i]}\triangleq\max_{d\in\mathbb{D}}G^{(\mathrm{u})}_{[i,\cdot]}Kd\;.

(5b)

Proof.

Observe the following equivalence:

\displaystyle\mathbb{U}\ominus K\mathbb{D}

\displaystyle=\Bigl\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\;\Big|\;u+Kd\in\mathbb{U}\;\;\forall\;d\in\mathbb{D}\Bigr\}

\displaystyle=\Bigl\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\;\Big|\;G^{(\mathrm{u})}\bigl(u+Kd\bigr)\leq h^{(\mathrm{u})}\;\;\forall\;d\in\mathbb{D}\Bigr\}

\displaystyle=\Bigl\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\;\Big|\;G^{(\mathrm{u})}u\leq h^{(\mathrm{u})}-G^{(\mathrm{u})}Kd\;\;\forall\;d\in\mathbb{D}\Bigr\}\;.

The result follows immediately from the last line.
∎

The maximization on the right-hand side of (
5a
) represents a Linear Program (LP). Hence, computing the Pontryagin difference in Proposition
6
amounts to solving a finite number of LPs. In this exemplary case, the number of LPs corresponds to the size of the H-representation of
\mathbb{U}
; each LP has
n
decision variables and as many linear constraints as the H-representation of
\mathbb{D}
. The complexity of the resulting polyhedron
\mathbb{U}\ominus K\mathbb{D}
is
exactly
the same as that of the original polyhedron
\mathbb{U}
, as only the right-hand side of the H-representation is modified.

Almost all Robust MPC approaches (except the one used in this paper) require the pre-computation of robust positively invariant sets
[
8
,
9
]
. Their definition
[
13
]
is based on a
pre-stabilization
of system (
1
):

u_{k}=Kx_{k}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x_{k+1}=\bigl(A+BK)x_{k}+d_{k}\;,

(6)

where
K\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is a stabilizing
feedback gain
, which exists by virtue of Assumption
1
.

Definition 7

((robust) positively invariant set)

(a) A set
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{X}
is called a
positively invariant set
(PI set) of system (
1
) if for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
it holds that
Kx\in\mathbb{U}
and
(A+BK)x\in\mathcal{X}
. (b) It is called a
robust positively invariant set
(RPI set) if, additionally,
(A+BK)x+d\in\mathcal{X}
for all
d\in\mathbb{D}
.

The complexity of computing RPI sets can be easy, in particular cases, but it is generally very hard
[
7
,
14
,
15
]
. It depends on whether a maximal, minimal, or any type of RPI set is required or acceptable; and it increases quickly with the system dimensions (‘curse of dimensionality’). Practical experience indicates that a computation is almost always intractable for systems with state dimension
n\geq 5
. In this paper, simply the following is assumed.

Assumption 8

(
robust positively invariant set,
optional
)

The computation of an RPI set for system (
1
) is prohibitive.

IV
DEADBEAT ROBUST MODEL PREDICTIVE CONTROL

IV-A

Deadbeat Prestabilization

A slightly modified version of the original DRMPC approach
[
10
]
is used in this paper. It is based on a
deadbeat (disturbance feedback) policy
for pre-stabilization, instead of
deadbeat input sequences

[
11
]
. For the following derivation, consider system (
1
), starting from an initial condition
x_{0}=d_{0}
that corresponds to a single disturbance
d_{0}\in\mathbb{D}
.

Definition 9

(
deadbeat horizon
)

The
deadbeat horizon

M

[
10
]
is the smallest number such that the matrix
\mathcal{P}_{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times nm}
,

\mathcal{P}_{M}\triangleq\Bigl[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c}A^{M-1}B&A^{M-2}B_{0}&\;\cdots&AB&B\end{array}\Bigr]\;,

(7)

has full rank
n
.

By virtue of Assumption
1
(b),
M
is well defined and satisfies
M\leq n
, because
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is the
controllability matrix
of (
1
). This implies the following result.

Lemma 10

(
deadbeat disturbance feedback policy
)

There exists a
deadbeat (disturbance feedback) policy
, consisting of
M
linear feedback gains
K_{0},K_{1},\dots,K_{M-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with

\bar{u}_{0}=K_{0}d_{0}\;,\;\bar{u}_{1}=K_{1}d_{0}\;,\;\dots\;,\;\bar{u}_{M-1}=K_{M-1}d_{0}\;,

(8)

such that
x_{M}=0
for any initial condition
x_{0}=d_{0}
.

Proof.

First, observe that the first
M
states of the nominal system—i.e., system (
1
) without any disturbances—can be expressed as

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{1}

\displaystyle=Ad_{0}+Bu_{0}\;,

(9a)

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{2}

\displaystyle=A^{2}d_{0}+ABu_{0}+Bu_{1}\;,

(9b)

\displaystyle\cdots

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{M-1}

\displaystyle=A^{M-1}d_{0}+A^{M-2}Bu_{0}+\dots+Bu_{M-2}\;,

(9c)

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{M}

\displaystyle=A^{M}d_{0}+A^{M-1}Bu_{0}+\dots+Bu_{M-1}\;.

(9d)

Since the matrix
\mathcal{P}_{M}
in (
7
) has full rank, there exists a sequence of deadbeat inputs
\bar{u}_{0},\bar{u}_{1},\dots,\bar{u}_{M-1}
that force
\bar{x}_{M}=0
. Using
\bar{x}_{M}\overset{!}{=}0
, (
9
d) becomes

-A^{M}d_{0}\overset{!}{=}A^{M-1}B\bar{u}_{0}+A^{M-2}B\bar{u}_{1}+\dots+B\bar{u}_{M-1}\;.

(10)

To verify that the deadbeat policy is indeed well-defined, substitute (
8
) into (
10
) to obtain

-A^{M}d_{0}=\\
\Bigl[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c}A^{M-1}BK_{0}&A^{M-2}BK_{1}&\dots&BK_{M-1}\end{array}\Bigr]d_{0}\;.

(11)

Since the deadbeat policy must work for all
d_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the matrices multiplying
d_{0}
in (
11
) on both sides must be equal, thus

-A^{M}=\\
\Bigl[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c}A^{M-1}B&A^{M-2}B&\dots&B\end{array}\Bigr]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}K_{0}\\
\hline\cr K_{1}\\
\hline\cr\dots\\
\hline\cr K_{M-2}\\
\hline\cr K_{M-1}\end{array}\right]\;.

(12)

This can be expressed as a linear equation system to solve for the linear feeback gains
K_{0},K_{1},\dots,K_{M-1}
:

-\mathrm{vec}\left(A^{M}\right)=\\
\left[\hskip-5.69046pt\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\mathcal{P}_{M}&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&\mathcal{P}_{M}&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\mathcal{P}_{M}&0\\
0&0&\cdots&0&\mathcal{P}_{M}\end{array}\hskip-5.69046pt\right]\mathrm{vec}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{c}K_{0}\\
\hline\cr K_{1}\\
\hline\cr\dots\\
\hline\cr\hskip-5.69046ptK_{M-2}\\
\hline\cr\hskip-5.69046ptK_{M-1}\end{array}\right]\right)\;.

(13)

The claim follows from the fact that
\mathcal{P}_{M}
has full rank
n
, according to Definition
9
, and hence the matrix on the right-hand side of (
13
) has full rank
Mn
.
∎

Remark 11

Alternatively, it is possible to calculate a
time-invariant
disturbance feedback gain
K\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
by pole placement. Placing all
n
poles at
0
by an appropriate choice of
K
renders the matrix
(A+BK)
nilpotent; hence the deadbeat property follows. However, this only works for the choice of
M=n
.

Note that Lemma
10
not only proves the existence of the deadbeat policy. It also provides a constructive approach for computing the corresponding feedback gains
K_{0},K_{1},\dots,K_{M-1}
, namely by solving the linear equation system (
13
). In fact, (
13
) can be decomposed into
n
independent linear equation systems, each for one the
n
collective columns of the feedback gains
K_{0},K_{1},\dots,K_{M-1}
. This poses no computational problem, even for large scale systems, and can be performed offline.

Remark 12

(a) If the deadbeat policy is not unique, then some additional cost function (e.g., least squares) has to be defined when solving for
K_{0},K_{1},\dots,K_{M-1}
, subject to the equality constraint (
13
).
(b) The deadbeat horizon
M
may be deliberately chosen as a higher number than the smallest number for which
\mathcal{P}_{M}
has full rank
n
. This opens (additional) degrees of freedom in the choice of the deadbeat policy.
(c) The condition
N\geq n
for the choice of the prediction horizon, as stated above, may be relaxed to

.

IV-B

Deadbeat Robust Model Predictive Control

For the setup of the Finite-time Optimal Control Problem (FTOCP), it is assumed that a sequence of deadbeat feedback gains

have been pre-computed offline, as described in Section

Similar to
[

, the setup of the DRMPC problem is described only for step

, as the generalization to subsequent steps

is straightforward. Based on Lemma

, the following affine disturbance feedback policy should be applied in order to account for the deadbeat of each disturbance over the prediction horizon:

\displaystyle u_{0}

\displaystyle=u_{0|0}\;,

(14a)

\displaystyle u_{1}

\displaystyle=u_{1|0}+K_{0}d_{0}\;,

(14b)

\displaystyle u_{2}

\displaystyle=u_{2|0}+K_{1}d_{0}+K_{0}d_{1}\;,

(14c)

\displaystyle u_{3}

\displaystyle=u_{3|0}+K_{2}d_{0}+K_{1}d_{1}+K_{0}d_{2}\;,

(14d)

\displaystyle\;\;\vdots

\displaystyle u_{M}

\displaystyle=u_{M|0}+\,\,K_{M-1}d_{0}+\ldots+K_{0}d_{M-1}\;,

(14e)

up to the deadbeat horizon, and beyond that

u_{j}=u_{j|0}+\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}K_{i}d_{j-i-1}\qquad\forall\;M\leq j\leq N-1\;.

(14f)

The new notation

is used for the nominal control input in step

, as planned in step

, and

for the corresponding predicted nominal state, where

. This scheme suggests the following tightening of the input constraints for the nominal control inputs:

\displaystyle u_{0|0}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{U}\;,

(15a)

\displaystyle u_{1|0}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{U}\ominus K_{0}\mathbb{D}\;,

(15b)

\displaystyle u_{2|0}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{U}\ominus K_{0}\mathbb{D}\ominus K_{1}\mathbb{D}\;,

\displaystyle\triangleq A+BK_{0}\;,

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}

\displaystyle\triangleq A^{2}+ABK_{0}+BK_{1}\;,

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}

\displaystyle x_{2|0}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{X}\ominus\mathbb{D}\ominus\Phi_{0}\mathbb{D}\ominus\Phi_{1}\mathbb{D}\ominus\ldots\ominus\Phi_{M-2}\mathbb{D}\;,

\displaystyle\quad x_{0|0}=x_{0}\;,

\displaystyle\quad\eqref{Equ:TightenedIC}\;,\quad\eqref{Equ:TightenedSC}\;,

\displaystyle\quad x_{N|0}=0\;.

Remark 13

The DRMPC problem (

IV-C

System Theoretic Guarantees

Under the DRMPC regime, in each time step

the first element of the optimal input sequence from (

, is applied to the system.

Theorem 14

(

VI
CONCLUSION

DRMPC shows a competitive performance for small-scale systems, compared to other state-of-the-art Robust MPC approaches. Moreover, DRMPC pushes the boundaries of Robust MPC to systems of large scale, i.e., with several hundred states and inputs. It has exactly the same (online) computational complexity as a Nominal MPC approach. The computational complexity of the (offline) setup of DRMPC is reduced because it does not require the computation of an RPI set, which suffers from the curse of dimensionality. This has been validated by a small numerical study.
