Growing Avoiders from the Right: An Operator-Theoretic Approach
Reza Rastegar

Abstract
Marcus and Tardos [ 12 ] proved the Stanley–Wilf conjecture by reducing pattern avoidance to an extremal problem on 0 0 – 1 1 matrices. We give a parallel proof for classical permutation patterns that stays entirely in the “grow from the right” world of enumerative combinatorics. A v v -avoiding permutation is built by right insertion; at each step we keep a pruned family of locations of ( k − 1 ) (k{-}1) -partial occurrences of v v (the frontier ), each carrying its forbidden rank interval. The insertion step then induces a nonnegative transfer operator on a doubly weighted ℓ ∞ \ell^{\infty} space. A quadratic penalty in the length makes this operator bounded, and a Neumann-series argument on a natural separable predual yields analyticity of the growth series, hence finite exponential growth for Av ⁡ ( v ) \mathrm{Av}(v) . The formulation is completely internal—we never pass to 0 0 – 1 1 matrices—and it cleanly separates the pattern-dependent combinatorics of the frontier from a purely operator-theoretic core. In particular, we obtain an abstract “right-insertion/transfer-operator” theorem: any system whose frontier grows at most linearly and whose transfer operator satisfies a uniform quadratic length bound has an analytic growth series.



Keywords.
permutation patterns, pattern avoidance, Stanley–Wilf conjecture, transfer operator, quasi-compact operator, frontier method, right-to-left growth, analytic combinatorics

Introduction

Permutation patterns form one of the central testing grounds for modern enumerative and structural combinatorics; see, for instance, the foundational work of Simion–Schmidt, Bóna, Kitaev, and Vatter
[
14
,
3
,
11
,
15
]
.
The starting point of this paper is the now-classical fact that for every fixed permutation pattern
v
the number

|\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v)|=|\{\pi\in S_{n}:\pi\text{ avoids }v\}|

grows at most exponentially fast in
n
. This is the content of the Stanley–Wilf conjecture, posed independently by Stanley and Wilf in the early 1980s and settled by Marcus and Tardos
[
12
]
– see also Theorem
4.7
. Their proof recasts pattern avoidance as an extremal problem on
0
–
1
matrices, proves a strengthened form of the Füredi–Hajnal conjecture, and then reads off exponential growth. Because the argument is short, elementary, and sharp enough to imply all then-known special cases (e.g.
[
14
,
1
,
3
]
), it has become the standard reference; see Vatter’s survey
[
15
]
for a modern account and for the role of Marcus–Tardos in the structural theory of permutation classes.

Our aim is not to improve on the Marcus–Tardos bound, nor to bypass matrix methods for their own sake, but to show that the same exponential-growth phenomenon
for classical patterns
can be obtained inside a framework that is already familiar in enumerative combinatorics and in dynamical systems: “grow the object from a boundary, keep a bounded amount of local data, and let a transfer operator act on a weighted function space.” Variants of this philosophy occur in the transfer-matrix method for walks on strips
[
7
, Ch. 4]
, in column-by-column growth of polyominoes and related lattice objects (see e.g.
[
4
,
5
]
), and in the use of Ruelle–Perron–Frobenius operators to encode symbolic extensions of dynamical systems
[
2
,
10
]
. In all those settings one ends up with a linear operator
T
on a Banach space of functions on the state space, and the exponential growth rate of the combinatorial objects is the spectral radius of
T
.

What we show here is that classical permutation pattern avoidance admits exactly such a formulation. We build a permutation by repeatedly appending a rightmost position and choosing its value by rank (the usual right-to-left reconstruction); to ensure that the new letter does not complete an occurrence of
v
, we keep at each step a pruned family of
locations of
(k{-}1)
-partial occurrences
of
v
, which we call the
frontier
. Each such partial occurrence determines a contiguous interval of ranks for the new letter that would extend it to a full
k
-pattern at the next step, so the frontier implicitly encodes the corresponding forbidden-rank intervals. (Shorter partial occurrences of
v
play no direct role in the next step: they must first grow to length
k{-}1
before they can become dangerous, so tracking them would only inflate the state without adding information.) Two mild obstacles appear:

•

for every active
(k{-}1)
-partial occurrence of
v
, the frontier must remember
where
it sits in the current permutation and which ranks would extend it, so the state is not just the permutation but the permutation
plus
this pruned family of partial occurrences (or, equivalently, the associated forbidden-rank intervals);

•

the number of legal ranks grows linearly with the length, so a single weight in the length is not enough to make the transfer operator bounded.

We deal with the first point by making the frontier explicit at the level of
(k{-}1)
-partial occurrences, computing from each of them the interval of ranks that would complete it, and discarding those whose completion interval is empty (they can never become dangerous at the very next step). We deal with the second by placing our operator on a doubly weighted
\ell^{\infty}
space in which we penalize both the frontier size and
quadratically
the length. With these choices the insertion operator becomes bounded on a doubly weighted
\ell^{\infty}
space. This suffices, via a dual Neumann-series argument, to obtain analyticity of the counting series in a disc and hence the Stanley–Wilf finiteness conclusion for classical patterns (Theorem
4.7
). As an additional structural result (which we record but do not use for the finiteness statement itself), a standard core/tail decomposition of Ionescu Tulcea–Marinescu
[
9
]
, in the streamlined form of Hennion
[
8
]
, shows that the operator is in fact a finite-rank perturbation of a contraction and therefore quasi-compact.

This reformulation has two concrete advantages.

First, it separates the
combinatorial
task from the
analytic
one. On the combinatorial side we must specify how, under right insertion, we keep track of the “dangerous”
(k{-}1)
-partial occurrences of the pattern. For classical patterns this comes down to defining the frontier and proving that it behaves monotonically under legal insertions (Lemma
2.9
), in the sense that existing partial occurrences (and their associated forbidden-rank intervals) are transported but not destroyed. Once this is in place, the rest of the argument—choice of weights, duality, and the use of a Neumann series on the Banach dual—is pattern-independent.

Second, the operator viewpoint packages the Stanley–Wilf finiteness statement into a reusable template: grow the permutation by right insertion, choose a complexity measure on decorated states, and apply general transfer-operator machinery to read off exponential growth from spectral data. This makes transparent which ingredients are genuinely pattern-specific (the organization of the frontier) and which are universal (the weighted
\ell^{\infty}
framework and the dual Neumann-series argument). It also suggests how one might try to extend the method to certain “right-visible” generalizations of classical patterns, where all constraints triggered by adding a new rightmost entry are already visible at the moment of insertion; we return briefly to this perspective in the concluding remarks.

Beyond the mere existence of a finite exponential growth rate, one would like to understand the
precise
asymptotic behaviour of
|\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v)|
, for instance whether it always admits an expansion of the form

|\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v)|\;\sim\;c_{v}\,n^{\alpha_{v}}\,\rho_{v}^{n}

for some constants
c_{v}>0
,
\alpha_{v}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\rho_{v}>0
. Our framework does not address this question. In special cases, such as the monotone pattern
12\cdots k
, sharp asymptotics of this shape are known by completely different methods (via the representation theory of the symmetric group and the RSK correspondence; see Regev
[
13
]
). From the transfer-operator viewpoint, such refinements would require a much more delicate analysis of the peripheral spectrum and the singularities of the resolvent for
T_{v,1}
or its adjoint, and we do not pursue this direction here.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
sets up the right-insertion picture for classical pattern avoidance, defines the frontier as a pruned family of
(k{-}1)
-partial occurrences together with their forbidden-rank intervals, and makes its monotonicity under legal insertions fully explicit, including an explicit “frontier blocks what it should block” lemma. Section
3
introduces the two-parameter penalty and proves that the insertion operator is bounded even at
z=1
after shrinking the penalty. Section
4
develops a separable predual and a dual transfer-operator point of view from which we read off an analytic counting series; along the way we also record the standard core/tail decomposition and quasi-compactness of the operator, although these structural results are not needed for Theorem
4.7
. We conclude in Section
5
with remarks on how far the “right-visible” condition might be pushed beyond classical patterns, and how this operator-theoretic viewpoint sits with the broader theory of permutation classes
[
15
]
.

2
Permutation pattern avoidance

In this section we formalize the right-insertion viewpoint for classical permutations avoiding a fixed pattern. The idea is to view a growing permutation as a state, and the admissible ways of inserting a new value as legal extensions, so that our transfer-operator machinery can be applied directly to the pattern-avoidance problem.

More concretely, we will build permutations one value at a time by inserting a new entry at the right end, with an insertion rule that is in bijection with the Lehmer-code encoding of permutations. Pattern-avoidance will then be enforced by restricting to those insertion choices that keep the forbidden pattern out; the complexity function will be chosen so that it captures the amount of “forbidden-pattern structure” already present in the growing permutation. This viewpoint allows us to rewrite the avoidance problem as the study of a suitable transfer operator on a state space of decorated permutations.

2.1
Right insertion, its formal definition, and the Lehmer code

Among the many possible ways to grow a permutation (left insertion, insertion into arbitrary positions, inflation operations,
etc.
), right insertion with rank parameters turns out to be particularly convenient: it is compatible with the factorial number system, it produces a simple product structure on the space of insertion codes, and it fits directly into our operator framework via the map
\Phi
and the local extension sets
A(x)
. We therefore fix a canonical right-insertion scheme and recall its connection to the classical Lehmer code.

Fix a classical pattern
v\in S_{k}
with
k\geq 2
. We build a permutation
\pi
by
right insertion
:

Definition 2.1

(Right insertion)
.

Let
\pi\in S_{n}
and let
r\in\{1,\dots,n+1\}
. Define
\mathrm{ins}(\pi,r)\in S_{n+1}
to be the permutation obtained by

1.

appending a new rightmost position at index
n+1
;

2.

giving it value
r
;

3.

and increasing by
1
every existing value of
\pi
that is
\geq r
.

It is a standard fact that this right-insertion encoding is
bijective
. We omit the proof and refer the reader interested in learning more about encodings of permutations to, for instance,
[
3
]
.

Lemma 2.2

(Right insertion and Lehmer code)
.

For every
n\geq 0
, the map

(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})\in\prod_{j=1}^{n}\{1,\dots,j\}\longmapsto\mathrm{ins}(\cdots\mathrm{ins}(\mathrm{ins}(\varnothing,r_{1}),r_{2})\cdots,r_{n})

is a bijection from
\prod_{j=1}^{n}\{1,\dots,j\}
onto
S_{n}
. In particular, there are
n!
such insertion sequences of length
n
, and each
\pi\in S_{n}
is produced by
exactly one
of them.

2.2
Frontiers for classical patterns

To enforce
v
-avoidance during right insertion, we must block exactly those
ranks whose insertion would
complete
an occurrence of
v
. A key
simplification is that only
partial occurrences of length
k-1
can be
completed to a full
k
-pattern. Shorter prefixes play no role at the next
step: they may later grow into
(k-1)
-prefixes after further insertions, but
they cannot become dangerous immediately, and tracking them would artificially
inflate the frontier without providing any additional safety.

We therefore adopt the following compressed, location-based definition of the frontier.

Definition 2.3

(Frontier for a classical pattern)
.

Let
v\in S_{k}
with
k\geq 2
, and let
\pi\in S_{n}
avoid
v
.

A
(k{-}1)
-partial occurrence
of
v
in
\pi
is a choice of indices

i_{1}<\dots<i_{k-1}

such that
(\pi_{i_{1}},\dots,\pi_{i_{k-1}})
is order-isomorphic
to the prefix
(v_{1},\dots,v_{k-1})
.

For such a
(k{-}1)
-partial occurrence, define its
forbidden rank set

J_{\pi}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})\subseteq\{1,\dots,n+1\}

by

J_{\pi}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})\;:=\;\bigl\{r\in\{1,\dots,n+1\}:\pi^{\prime}=\mathrm{ins}(\pi,r)\text{ satisfies }(\pi^{\prime}_{i_{1}},\dots,\pi^{\prime}_{i_{k-1}},\pi^{\prime}_{n+1})\sim(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})\bigr\},

i.e.
J_{\pi}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})
is the set of ranks
r
for which inserting
r
uses the new rightmost position to complete a full copy of
v
.
As we will see below (Lemma
2.5
), each such set
J_{\pi}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})

is in fact an interval of consecutive ranks. We allow this set to be empty.

The
frontier
of
\pi
is the set

\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi)\;:=\;\bigl\{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}):i_{1}<\dots<i_{k-1}\text{ is a $(k{-}1)$-partial occurrence of $v$ in $\pi$ and }J_{\pi}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})\neq\varnothing\bigr\}.

Thus
\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi)
is a finite family of locations of
(k{-}1)
-partial occurrences; each element of the frontier carries, implicitly, its forbidden rank-interval
J_{\pi}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})
. The union

\mathrm{Forb}_{v}(\pi)\;:=\;\bigcup_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})\in\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi)}J_{\pi}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})

is the set of
forbidden ranks
, and we write

A_{v}(\pi)\;:=\;\{1,\dots,n+1\}\setminus\mathrm{Forb}_{v}(\pi)

for the set of
frontier-legal ranks
.

The following lemma records finiteness and the basic soundness of the
construction.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
v\in S_{k}
and
\pi\in S_{n}
avoid
v
. Then:

1.

\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi)
is finite;

2.

if
r\notin\mathrm{Forb}_{v}(\pi)
, then
\mathrm{ins}(\pi,r)\in\mathrm{Av}(v)
.

Proof.

(1) There are at most
\binom{n}{k-1}
choices of
(k{-}1)
-tuples

i_{1}<\dots<i_{k-1}
, hence only finitely many
(k{-}1)
-partial occurrences and therefore only finitely many frontier elements.

(2) By construction, if
r\in\mathrm{Forb}_{v}(\pi)
then there is a

(k{-}1)
-partial occurrence
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})
whose forbidden rank set
J_{\pi}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})
contains
r
, and
inserting
r
completes a copy of
v
using the new rightmost position.
Thus
\mathrm{ins}(\pi,r)\notin\mathrm{Av}(v)
whenever

r\in\mathrm{Forb}_{v}(\pi)
.

It remains to prove the converse: if
\mathrm{ins}(\pi,r)
contains a copy
of
v
, then necessarily
r\in\mathrm{Forb}_{v}(\pi)
.

Let
\pi^{\prime}=\mathrm{ins}(\pi,r)
and suppose
\pi^{\prime}
contains
v
. Then there
exist indices

j_{1}<\dots<j_{k}

such that
(\pi^{\prime}_{j_{1}},\dots,\pi^{\prime}_{j_{k}})\sim v
.
If
j_{k}\neq n+1
, then all
j_{\ell}\leq n
, so the same
k
-tuple of
positions already existed in
\pi
. Since right insertion preserves the
relative order among the old entries, we would have

(\pi_{j_{1}},\dots,\pi_{j_{k}})\sim v
, contradicting
\pi\in\mathrm{Av}(v)
.

Hence
j_{k}=n+1
and
j_{1}<\dots<j_{k-1}\leq n
. Again, because the insertion

\mathrm{ins}(\pi,r)
only increases some of the old values but does not
change their relative order, the subsequence

(\pi_{j_{1}},\dots,\pi_{j_{k-1}})
is order-isomorphic to the prefix

(v_{1},\dots,v_{k-1})
. Thus
(j_{1},\dots,j_{k-1})
is a
(k{-}1)
-partial
occurrence of
v
in
\pi
.

By Definition
2.3
, the forbidden rank set

J_{\pi}(j_{1},\dots,j_{k-1})

consists exactly of those
r
for which inserting at rank
r
completes a copy of
v
using the new rightmost position. Since
our chosen
r
does precisely that, we have

r\in J_{\pi}(j_{1},\dots,j_{k-1})\subseteq\mathrm{Forb}_{v}(\pi).

Therefore, if
r\notin\mathrm{Forb}_{v}(\pi)
, then
\mathrm{ins}(\pi,r)

cannot contain an occurrence of
v
, i.e.
\mathrm{ins}(\pi,r)\in\mathrm{Av}(v)
.
∎

We now justify the claim that each forbidden rank set is an interval of
consecutive integers.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
v\in S_{k}
and
\pi\in\mathrm{Av}(v)
, and fix a
(k{-}1)
-partial occurrence

i_{1}<\dots<i_{k-1}
of
v
in
\pi
. Let

J:=J_{\pi}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})

be its forbidden rank set as in Definition
2.3
. Then
J
is an (integer) interval: there
exist integers
1\leq a\leq b\leq n+1
such that

J\;=\;\{a,a+1,\dots,b\}.

Proof.

Write
x_{\ell}:=\pi_{i_{\ell}}
for
\ell=1,\dots,k-1
. For each

r\in\{1,\dots,n+1\}
, the right insertion
\pi^{\prime}=\mathrm{ins}(\pi,r)

leaves the relative order among the old entries
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1})

unchanged, and replaces the new point by the value
\pi^{\prime}_{n+1}=r
.
Thus the pattern of

(\pi^{\prime}_{i_{1}},\dots,\pi^{\prime}_{i_{k-1}},\pi^{\prime}_{n+1})\;=\;(\pi^{\prime}_{i_{1}},\dots,\pi^{\prime}_{i_{k-1}},r)

is determined entirely by the relative order of
r
with each
x_{\ell}
.

Fix
\ell\in\{1,\dots,k-1\}
, and consider the comparison between the new
point and
x_{\ell}
in
\pi^{\prime}
. After insertion we have

\pi^{\prime}_{i_{\ell}}\;=\;\begin{cases}x_{\ell}+1,&\text{if }x_{\ell}\geq r,\\
x_{\ell},&\text{if }x_{\ell}<r.\end{cases}

A direct check shows:

•

r<\pi^{\prime}_{i_{\ell}}
(new point below
x_{\ell}
) if and only if

r\leq x_{\ell}
;

•

r>\pi^{\prime}_{i_{\ell}}
(new point above
x_{\ell}
) if and only if

r\geq x_{\ell}+1
.

Thus, for each
\ell
, the relation “new below
x_{\ell}
” or
“new above
x_{\ell}
” imposes a
single-sided
constraint on
r
of
the form
r\leq x_{\ell}
or
r\geq x_{\ell}+1
.

Now compare with the pattern
v
. For each
\ell
we require that the order
relation between
v_{k}
and
v_{\ell}
matches that between
r
and

\pi^{\prime}_{i_{\ell}}
:

•

If
v_{k}<v_{\ell}
, then we need
r<\pi^{\prime}_{i_{\ell}}
, i.e. the constraint
r\leq x_{\ell}
.

•

If
v_{k}>v_{\ell}
, then we need
r>\pi^{\prime}_{i_{\ell}}
, i.e. the constraint
r\geq x_{\ell}+1
.

Therefore
r\in J
if and only if it satisfies simultaneously all such
constraints, one for each
\ell
. Equivalently,

J\;=\;\bigl\{r\in\{1,\dots,n+1\}:r\leq x_{\ell}\text{ whenever }v_{k}<v_{\ell},\;r\geq x_{\ell}+1\text{ whenever }v_{k}>v_{\ell}\bigr\}.

Let

a\;:=\;\max_{\{\ell:\,v_{k}>v_{\ell}\}}(x_{\ell}+1),\qquad b\;:=\;\min_{\{\ell:\,v_{k}<v_{\ell}\}}x_{\ell},

with the usual conventions that the maximum over an empty set is
1
and
the minimum over an empty set is
n+1
. Then the above description shows
that

J\;=\;\{r\in\{1,\dots,n+1\}:a\leq r\leq b\}.

If
a>b
, this interval is empty (and then the partial occurrence contributes
nothing to the frontier); otherwise it is precisely the integer interval

\{a,a+1,\dots,b\}
. This proves the claim.
∎

2.3
State space and closure under legal insertions

We now package the permutation and its frontier into a single object. This will be the concrete state space on which our transfer operator will act.

Definition 2.6

(State space for
\mathrm{Av}(v)
)
.

Let
v\in S_{k}
be fixed. The
state space

\mathsf{X}_{v}
consists of all pairs

x\;=\;(\pi,\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi)),

where
\pi
is a
v
-avoiding permutation and
\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi)
is the frontier of
\pi
in the sense of Definition
2.3
.

For such a state
x=(\pi,\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi))
we define:

•

the
frontier size

m(x)\;:=\;|\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi)|,

•

the
length

s(x)\;:=\;|\pi|.

The admissible right-insertions from
x
are the ranks that are not blocked by the frontier:

A_{v}(x)\;:=\;\{1,\dots,s(x)+1\}\setminus\mathrm{Forb}_{v}(\pi),

where
\mathrm{Forb}_{v}(\pi)
is the union of the forbidden rank-intervals
J_{\pi}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})
over
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})\in\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi)
.
For
r\in A_{v}(x)
we write

\Phi_{v}(x,r)

for the new state obtained by inserting
r
on the right of
\pi
(using Definition
2.1
) and recomputing the frontier of the resulting permutation.

Thus, informally, a state remembers both “where we are” in the class
\mathrm{Av}(v)
(the permutation
\pi
itself) and the current collection of dangerous
(k{-}1)
-partial occurrences (its frontier). The set
A_{v}(x)
plays the role of the extension set.

The key structural property we
do
use is that legal insertions stay inside the state space; this is just a restatement of Lemma
2.4
(2) in the present notation.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
v\in S_{k}
and let
x=(\pi,\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi))\in\mathsf{X}_{v}
. For every
r\in A_{v}(x)
the permutation

\pi^{\prime}:=\mathrm{ins}(\pi,r)

avoids
v
, and the recomputed frontier
\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi^{\prime})
makes
(\pi^{\prime},\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi^{\prime}))
an element of
\mathsf{X}_{v}
.

Proof.

By definition of
A_{v}(x)
we have

r\notin\mathrm{Forb}_{v}(\pi).

Lemma
2.4
(2) says precisely that for such an
r
the right insertion
\pi^{\prime}=\mathrm{ins}(\pi,r)
still avoids
v
. By Lemma
2.4
(1), the newly computed frontier
\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi^{\prime})
is finite, so
(\pi^{\prime},\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi^{\prime}))
again belongs to
\mathsf{X}_{v}
.
∎

Remark 2.8
.

In principle one could allow states from which no legal extension is possible, i.e. states
x
with
A_{v}(x)=\varnothing
. In the concrete classical-avoidance setting, we can prove that this does not happen: every
v
-avoiding state admits at least one legal right insertion. However, our analytic arguments will only use Lemma
2.7
, i.e. that
if
we take a legal rank, we stay inside
\mathrm{Av}(v)
.

Finally, observe that the number of available ranks is always bounded by the length:

|A_{v}(x)|\;\leq\;s(x)+1,

simply because there are at most
s(x)+1
positions in which a new value can be inserted on the right.

In the classical pattern setting we will also need a simple but useful structural property of the frontier: under a
legal
right insertion, the existing
(k{-}1)
-partial occurrences in the frontier never disappear, and their forbidden rank-intervals are transported by the rank-bumping map. Informally, when we insert a new value of rank
r
, every old value
<r
stays put and every old value
\geq r
is bumped up by
1
, so the relative order among all old entries is preserved and the interval of dangerous ranks attached to each frontier element is simply shifted. The next lemma makes this precise.

Lemma 2.9

(Monotonicity of the frontier under legal insertions)
.

Let
v\in S_{k}
with
k\geq 2
, and let
x=(\pi,\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi))\in\mathsf{X}_{v}
with
\pi\in S_{n}
. For
r\in A_{v}(x)
define the
rank-bumping map

\varphi_{r}:\{1,\dots,n+1\}\longrightarrow\{1,\dots,n+2\}\setminus\{r\},\qquad\varphi_{r}(q)\;:=\;\begin{cases}q,&q<r,\\
q+1,&q\geq r.\end{cases}

Let
\pi^{\prime}:=\mathrm{ins}(\pi,r)
and
x^{\prime}:=(\pi^{\prime},\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi^{\prime}))
. Then for every
(k{-}1)
-tuple

p=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})\in\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi)

we have:

1.

p
is still a
(k{-}1)
-partial occurrence of
v
in
\pi^{\prime}
;

2.

its new forbidden rank set is given by

J_{\pi^{\prime}}(p)\;=\;\varphi_{r}\bigl(J_{\pi}(p)\bigr);

in particular
J_{\pi^{\prime}}(p)\neq\varnothing
whenever
J_{\pi}(p)\neq\varnothing
.

Consequently
\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi)\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi^{\prime})
and

m(x^{\prime})=|\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi^{\prime})|\;\geq\;|\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi)|=m(x).

Proof.

Fix
p=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})\in\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi)
and write
x_{\ell}:=\pi_{i_{\ell}}
for
\ell=1,\dots,k-1
. Set
\pi^{\prime}:=\mathrm{ins}(\pi,r)
.

By definition of right insertion we have

\pi^{\prime}_{i_{\ell}}=\varphi_{r}(\pi_{i_{\ell}})=\varphi_{r}(x_{\ell})\qquad(1\leq\ell\leq k-1),

and
\varphi_{r}
is strictly increasing as a function of the rank. Thus

(\pi^{\prime}_{i_{1}},\dots,\pi^{\prime}_{i_{k-1}})=(\varphi_{r}(x_{1}),\dots,\varphi_{r}(x_{k-1}))

is order-isomorphic to
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1})
and hence to
(v_{1},\dots,v_{k-1})
. Therefore
p
remains a
(k{-}1)
-partial occurrence of
v
in
\pi^{\prime}
.

Let
q\in\{1,\dots,n+1\}
and set
s:=\varphi_{r}(q)\in\{1,\dots,n+2\}
. There are two relevant permutations:

\tau:=\mathrm{ins}(\pi,q)\in S_{n+1},\qquad\tau^{\prime}:=\mathrm{ins}(\pi^{\prime},s)\in S_{n+2}.

By construction,

\tau_{n+1}=q,\qquad\tau^{\prime}_{n+2}=s,

and for
j\leq n
,

\tau_{j}=\begin{cases}\pi_{j},&\pi_{j}<q,\\
\pi_{j}+1,&\pi_{j}\geq q,\end{cases}\qquad\tau^{\prime}_{j}=\begin{cases}\pi^{\prime}_{j},&\pi^{\prime}_{j}<s,\\
\pi^{\prime}_{j}+1,&\pi^{\prime}_{j}\geq s.\end{cases}

On the level of
ranks
we can write the two
k
-tuples we care about as

(\pi_{i_{1}},\dots,\pi_{i_{k-1}},q)\quad\text{and}\quad(\pi^{\prime}_{i_{1}},\dots,\pi^{\prime}_{i_{k-1}},s)=(\varphi_{r}(\pi_{i_{1}}),\dots,\varphi_{r}(\pi_{i_{k-1}}),\varphi_{r}(q)).

Since
\varphi_{r}
is strictly increasing, these two
k
-tuples are order-isomorphic. It follows that

(\tau_{i_{1}},\dots,\tau_{i_{k-1}},\tau_{n+1})\sim(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(\tau^{\prime}_{i_{1}},\dots,\tau^{\prime}_{i_{k-1}},\tau^{\prime}_{n+2})\sim(v_{1},\dots,v_{k}).

In other words,

q\in J_{\pi}(p)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad s=\varphi_{r}(q)\in J_{\pi^{\prime}}(p).

Thus

J_{\pi^{\prime}}(p)=\{\varphi_{r}(q):q\in J_{\pi}(p)\}=\varphi_{r}\bigl(J_{\pi}(p)\bigr),

proving (2). Since
\varphi_{r}
is injective,
J_{\pi}(p)\neq\varnothing
implies
J_{\pi^{\prime}}(p)\neq\varnothing
.

Because
p\in\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi)
means exactly that
J_{\pi}(p)\neq\varnothing
, we deduce that
p
also belongs to
\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi^{\prime})
. Thus
\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi)\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi^{\prime})
, and therefore

m(x^{\prime})=|\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi^{\prime})|\;\geq\;|\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi)|=m(x).

∎

3
A two-parameter penalty and boundedness

We now face the only genuinely nontrivial analytic obstacle. In our concrete permutation setting we obtain

|A_{v}(x)|\;\leq\;s(x)+1,

so the number of admissible insertions grows at most linearly with the length. A single penalty in the frontier size
m(x)
cannot offset this linear growth uniformly in
s(x)
, so we also penalize large lengths, using a quadratic weight in
s(x)
.

Definition 3.1

(Two-parameter Banach space)
.

Let
0<\theta<1
and
0<\kappa<1
. Define

\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}:=\left\{f:\mathsf{X}_{v}\to\mathbb{C}:\|f\|_{\theta,\kappa}:=\sup_{x\in\mathsf{X}_{v}}\theta^{-m(x)}\kappa^{-s(x)^{2}}|f(x)|<\infty\right\}.

Thus
\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}
is a weighted
\ell^{\infty}
space on the state space
\mathsf{X}_{v}
, where the value of
f
at a state
x
is discounted by a factor

\theta^{m(x)}\kappa^{s(x)^{2}}.

The parameter
\theta
penalizes large frontiers, while
\kappa
penalizes large lengths, and the quadratic exponent
s(x)^{2}
is chosen so that it dominates the linear growth in
|A_{v}(x)|
.

The transfer operator is defined by the same formula as before, now restricted to the concrete state space:

(T_{v,z}f)(x):=\sum_{r\in A_{v}(x)}z\,f(\Phi_{v}(x,r)),

where
A_{v}(x)
and
\Phi_{v}(x,r)
are as in Definition
2.6
.

Lemma 3.2

(Boundedness of
T_{v,z}
)
.

Let
v
be fixed and
0<\theta<1
. Then there exist
0<\kappa<1
and
r_{0}>0
such that, for all
|z|\leq r_{0}
, the operator
T_{v,z}
is bounded on
\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}
. Moreover, by shrinking
\kappa
if necessary we may assume
r_{0}\geq 1
, so in particular
T_{v,1}
is bounded on
\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}
and
x\in\mathsf{X}_{v}
be arbitrary. Write

m:=m(x),\qquad s:=s(x),

for the frontier size and the length at
x
.

For any legal rank
r\in A_{v}(x)
the new state is
\Phi_{v}(x,r)=(\pi^{\prime},\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi^{\prime}))
. By Lemma
2.9
the frontier size is monotone:

m\bigl(\Phi_{v}(x,r)\bigr)\geq m,

and by construction the length increases by exactly one,

s\bigl(\Phi_{v}(x,r)\bigr)=s+1.

Using the definition of the norm on
\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}
, this implies

|f(\Phi_{v}(x,r))|\;\leq\;\|f\|_{\theta,\kappa}\,\theta^{m(\Phi_{v}(x,r))}\,\kappa^{s(\Phi_{v}(x,r))^{2}}\;\leq\;\|f\|_{\theta,\kappa}\,\theta^{m}\,\kappa^{(s+1)^{2}}.

From a state
x
of length
s
there are at most
s+1
legal ranks (indeed
|A_{v}(x)|\leq s+1
). Summing over
r\in A_{v}(x)
gives

|(T_{v,z}f)(x)|\;=\;\Bigl|\sum_{r\in A_{v}(x)}z\,f(\Phi_{v}(x,r))\Bigr|\;\leq\;|z|\sum_{r\in A_{v}(x)}|f(\Phi_{v}(x,r))|\;\leq\;|z|(s+1)\|f\|_{\theta,\kappa}\,\theta^{m}\,\kappa^{(s+1)^{2}}.

Divide both sides by
\theta^{m}\kappa^{s^{2}}
to compare with the norm:

\theta^{-m}\kappa^{-s^{2}}|(T_{v,z}f)(x)|\;\leq\;|z|(s+1)\,\|f\|_{\theta,\kappa}\,\kappa^{(s+1)^{2}-s^{2}}\;=\;|z|(s+1)\,\|f\|_{\theta,\kappa}\,\kappa^{2s+1}.

The right-hand side depends on
f
only through its norm and depends on
x
only through
s=s(x)
.

Fix any
\kappa\in(0,1)
. For
s\to\infty
we have
\kappa^{2s}\to 0
exponentially fast, while
(s+1)
grows only linearly. Thus

M(\kappa):=\sup_{s\geq 0}(s+1)\kappa^{2s+1}<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad M(\kappa)\xrightarrow[\kappa\to 0]{}0.

Hence we can choose
\kappa
sufficiently small that

M(\kappa)\leq 1.

For such a choice, the previous estimate yields, for all
x\in\mathsf{X}_{v}
,

\theta^{-m(x)}\kappa^{-s(x)^{2}}|(T_{v,1}f)(x)|\;\leq\;M(\kappa)\,\|f\|_{\theta,\kappa}\;\leq\;\|f\|_{\theta,\kappa}.

Taking the supremum over
x
shows that

\|T_{v,1}f\|_{\theta,\kappa}\leq\|f\|_{\theta,\kappa},

so
T_{v,1}
is bounded on
\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}
.

For general
z
we merely track the factor
|z|
:

\theta^{-m(x)}\kappa^{-s(x)^{2}}|(T_{v,z}f)(x)|\;\leq\;|z|\,M(\kappa)\,\|f\|_{\theta,\kappa}.

Taking the supremum over
x
gives

\|T_{v,z}f\|_{\theta,\kappa}\;\leq\;|z|\,M(\kappa)\,\|f\|_{\theta,\kappa}.

Define

r_{0}:=\frac{1}{M(\kappa)}.

Our choice of
\kappa
above ensures
M(\kappa)\leq 1
, hence
r_{0}\geq 1
. For every
|z|\leq r_{0}
we then have

\|T_{v,z}f\|_{\theta,\kappa}\leq\|f\|_{\theta,\kappa},

so
T_{v,z}
is bounded on
\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}
for all such
z
, and in particular for
z=1
.
∎

This is the central analytic estimate. The quadratic dependence on
s(x)
in the weight was chosen so that the factor
(s+1)\kappa^{2s+1}
remains uniformly bounded (and can be made arbitrarily small by shrinking
\kappa
), thereby compensating for the linear growth of the number of legal ranks
|A_{v}(x)|
.

4
Quasi-compactness and the counting series

We now prove, in this operator language, the analogue of the Marcus–Tardos theorem: the ordinary generating function of
\mathrm{Av}(v)
has positive radius of convergence, hence the exponential growth rate is finite. The structure of the proof is the standard one for transfer operators: we split the space into a finite part where everything is complicated but finite-dimensional, and a tail where the operator is a contraction; we then pass to a separable predual where the natural Dirac mass lives, and invoke a Neumann series for the dual operator. Only the boundedness of
T_{v,1}
and the dual
\ell^{1}
framework are actually needed for the enumeration theorem (Theorem
4.7
); the core/tail estimates and quasi-compactness recorded below are included as a structural refinement and for later use in the abstract right-visible setting.

4.1
Finite core and tail

Fix an integer
C\geq 0
. Partition the state space
\mathsf{X}_{v}
according to the length:

\mathsf{X}_{v}^{\leq C}:=\{x\in\mathsf{X}_{v}:s(x)\leq C\},\qquad\mathsf{X}_{v}^{>C}:=\mathsf{X}_{v}\setminus\mathsf{X}_{v}^{\leq C}.

There are only finitely many permutations of length
\leq C
, and for each such permutation there are only finitely many possible frontiers (a frontier is obtained by selecting
(k{-}1)
-partial occurrences with nonempty forbidden intervals among finitely many candidates). Hence
\mathsf{X}_{v}^{\leq C}
is a finite set.

Define the projection
P_{C}:\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}\to\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}
by

(P_{C}f)(x):=\begin{cases}f(x),&x\in\mathsf{X}_{v}^{\leq C},\\
0,&x\in\mathsf{X}_{v}^{>C}.\end{cases}

Define also the tail projection
P_{C}^{>}:\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}\to\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}
by

(P_{C}^{>}f)(x):=\begin{cases}0,&x\in\mathsf{X}_{v}^{\leq C},\\
f(x),&x\in\mathsf{X}_{v}^{>C}.\end{cases}

Thus
P_{C}^{>}=I-P_{C}
, and we refer to
P_{C}
and
P_{C}^{>}
as the core and tail projections.

Thus
P_{C}f
is supported on a finite set, and
P_{C}
is the projection onto the finitely supported functions living on states of length at most
C
.

With this notation we decompose the transfer operator as

T_{v,z}=T_{v,z}P_{C}+T_{v,z}P_{C}^{>}.

The first summand has finite rank (its range is contained in the finite-dimensional space of functions supported on
\mathsf{X}_{v}^{\leq C}
), while the second summand is the “tail” part, which only sees states of large length.

4.2
Tail contraction

The next lemma says that, if
C
is large enough, then the tail part is a strict contraction in the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\theta,\kappa}
.

Lemma 4.1

(Tail contraction)
.

Let
T_{v,z}
be as in Lemma
3.2
(in particular
T_{v,1}
is bounded). Then there exists
C
such that the tail–tail block

L_{C}:=P_{C}^{>}T_{v,1}P_{C}^{>}

satisfies

\|L_{C}\|_{\theta,\kappa}<1.

The same is true for all
|z|\leq 1
, with
L_{C}
replaced by
P_{C}^{>}T_{v,z}P_{C}^{>}
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}
with
\|f\|_{\theta,\kappa}\leq 1
, and define
g:=P_{C}^{>}f
. Then
g(x)=0
whenever
s(x)\leq C
, and
\|g\|_{\theta,\kappa}\leq 1
.

Fix
x\in\mathsf{X}_{v}
. If
x\in\mathsf{X}_{v}^{\leq C}
, then

(L_{C}f)(x)=(P_{C}^{>}T_{v,1}P_{C}^{>}f)(x)=0

by definition of
P_{C}^{>}
on the range. Thus the only nontrivial estimates concern
x\in\mathsf{X}_{v}^{>C}
.

For such an
x
set
s:=s(x)
and
m:=m(x)
. Since
x
is in the tail,
s\geq C+1
. Using the same estimate as in the proof of Lemma
3.2
, but with
g
in place of
f
, we obtain

\theta^{-m}\kappa^{-s^{2}}|(T_{v,1}g)(x)|\;\leq\;(s+1)\,\kappa^{2s+1}\,\|g\|_{\theta,\kappa}\;\leq\;(s+1)\,\kappa^{2s+1}.

Since
L_{C}f=P_{C}^{>}T_{v,1}g
and
x
is already in the tail, we have
(L_{C}f)(x)=(T_{v,1}g)(x)
, so the same bound holds for
L_{C}f
:

\theta^{-m(x)}\kappa^{-s(x)^{2}}|(L_{C}f)(x)|\;\leq\;(s(x)+1)\,\kappa^{2s(x)+1}.

As
s\to\infty
, the factor
(s+1)\kappa^{2s+1}
tends to
0
. Hence we can choose
C
so large that

(s+1)\kappa^{2s+1}\leq\frac{1}{2}\qquad\text{whenever }s\geq C+1.

For such a choice of
C
we have, for all
x\in\mathsf{X}_{v}
,

\theta^{-m(x)}\kappa^{-s(x)^{2}}|(L_{C}f)(x)|\leq\frac{1}{2},

hence

\|L_{C}f\|_{\theta,\kappa}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Taking the supremum over all
f
with
\|f\|_{\theta,\kappa}\leq 1
shows that
\|L_{C}\|_{\theta,\kappa}\leq 1/2<1
.

The same argument applies verbatim to
L_{C,z}:=P_{C}^{>}T_{v,z}P_{C}^{>}
for
|z|\leq 1
, yielding

\|L_{C,z}\|_{\theta,\kappa}\leq|z|\,\sup_{s\geq C+1}(s+1)\kappa^{2s+1}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

∎

Remark 4.2
.

Lemma
4.1
is used only to obtain quasi-compactness (Proposition
4.3
) and part (a) of the abstract right-visible theorem (Theorem
4.10
). The Stanley–Wilf finiteness result in Theorem
4.7
relies solely on the boundedness statement in Lemma
3.2
together with the dual Neumann-series argument in Section
4.4
.

4.3
Quasi-compactness

We can now state the quasi-compactness of
T_{v,z}
in our setting.

Proposition 4.3

(Quasi-compactness of
T_{v,z}
)
.

Fix
v
and
|z|\leq 1
, and let
C
be as in Lemma
4.1
. Define

L_{C,z}:=P_{C}^{>}T_{v,z}P_{C}^{>},\qquad K_{C,z}:=T_{v,z}-L_{C,z}.

Then
K_{C,z}
has finite rank and
L_{C,z}
is a strict contraction on
\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}
. In particular
T_{v,z}
is quasi-compact: its essential spectral radius is bounded by
\|L_{C,z}\|_{\theta,\kappa}<1
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.1
,
L_{C,z}
is a strict contraction on
\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}
for
|z|\leq 1
.

It remains to show that
K_{C,z}
has finite rank. Using
P_{C}+P_{C}^{>}=I
on both domain and range, we can expand

T_{v,z}=T_{v,z}(P_{C}+P_{C}^{>})=T_{v,z}P_{C}+T_{v,z}P_{C}^{>},

and further decompose

T_{v,z}P_{C}^{>}=(P_{C}+P_{C}^{>})T_{v,z}P_{C}^{>}=P_{C}T_{v,z}P_{C}^{>}+P_{C}^{>}T_{v,z}P_{C}^{>}.

Thus

T_{v,z}=\underbrace{T_{v,z}P_{C}+P_{C}T_{v,z}P_{C}^{>}}_{=:K_{C,z}}\;+\;\underbrace{P_{C}^{>}T_{v,z}P_{C}^{>}}_{=:L_{C,z}}.

The range of
T_{v,z}P_{C}
is supported on states of length at most
C+1
: starting from a core state of length
\leq C
, one legal insertion produces length
\leq C+1
, and there are only finitely many such states. Hence
T_{v,z}P_{C}
has finite rank.

The range of
P_{C}T_{v,z}P_{C}^{>}
is supported on
\mathsf{X}_{v}^{\leq C}
by construction, so this operator also has finite rank. Therefore
K_{C,z}=T_{v,z}P_{C}+P_{C}T_{v,z}P_{C}^{>}
is a finite-rank operator.

We have thus written
T_{v,z}=K_{C,z}+L_{C,z}
with
K_{C,z}
finite rank and
L_{C,z}
a strict contraction. By the standard theory of Ionescu Tulcea–Marinescu
[
9
]
and Hennion
[
8
]
, this implies that
T_{v,z}
is quasi-compact, and its essential spectral radius is bounded above by
\|L_{C,z}\|_{\theta,\kappa}<1
.
∎

4.4
Reading off the counting series via a separable predual

We now assemble the three ingredients developed so far:

(a)

the
combinatorial
part: states are
v
-avoiding permutations together with their frontiers (Definition
2.6
); legal insertions always stay in the state space (Lemma
2.7
); and the frontier is monotone under legal insertions (Lemma
2.9
);

(b)

the
analytic
part: with the two-parameter weight

\|f\|_{\theta,\kappa}=\sup_{x\in\mathsf{X}_{v}}\theta^{-m(x)}\kappa^{-s(x)^{2}}|f(x)|,

the transfer operator

(T_{v,z}f)(x):=\sum_{r\in A_{v}(x)}z\,f(\Phi_{v}(x,r))

is bounded on
\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}
for
|z|\leq 1
(Lemma
3.2
);

(c)

the
core/tail
decomposition: for a large cutoff
C
we have

T_{v,1}=K_{C,1}+L_{C,1},

where
K_{C,1}
has finite rank (its range is supported on states of length at most
C+1
) and the tail–tail block
L_{C,1}:=P_{C}^{>}T_{v,1}P_{C}^{>}
has operator norm
<1
(Lemma
4.1
, Proposition
4.3
).

Point (c) is exactly the hypothesis of Ionescu Tulcea–Marinescu and Hennion: an operator of the form

\text{(finite rank)}\;+\;\text{(strict contraction)}

is quasi-compact, and this is what Proposition
4.3
records. In concrete terms, all the “non-decaying” behaviour of
T_{v,1}
lives in a
finite-dimensional
part (the core), while the infinite tail is uniformly contracting. Thus whatever we do with
T_{v,1}
can be reduced to: control finitely many modes explicitly, and let the tail be absorbed by the contraction. For the Stanley–Wilf enumeration step we will only need boundedness and the induced action on a convenient dual, but quasi-compactness provides useful structural information for later refinements.

The only remaining issue is that the Banach dual of a weighted
\ell^{\infty}
on a countable set is too large and nonseparable to be convenient for counting. We therefore shrink the primal space slightly to enforce decay along the tail.

Define

\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}:=\left\{f\in\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}:\theta^{-m(x)}\kappa^{-s(x)^{2}}|f(x)|\xrightarrow[s(x)\to\infty]{}0\right\}.

This is the weighted analogue of the usual
c_{0}
-space: functions whose weighted values tend to zero along states of diverging length. It is a closed subspace of
\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}
and hence a Banach space in its own right.

Lemma 4.4
.

The space
\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}
is invariant under
T_{v,1}
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}
and set

g(y):=\theta^{-m(y)}\kappa^{-s(y)^{2}}\,|f(y)|.

By definition
g(y)\to 0
as
s(y)\to\infty
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Then there exists
S\geq 0
such that

g(y)\leq\varepsilon\qquad\text{whenever }s(y)\geq S.

Let
x\in\mathsf{X}_{v}
with
s(x)=s
. Using the estimate from Lemma
3.2
with
z=1
and the fact that any child
y=\Phi_{v}(x,r)
has length
s(y)=s(x)+1
, we obtain

\theta^{-m(x)}\kappa^{-s(x)^{2}}\,|(T_{v,1}f)(x)|\leq(s(x)+1)\kappa^{2s(x)+1}\,\max_{r\in A_{v}(x)}g(\Phi_{v}(x,r)).

If
s(x)\geq S
, then all children satisfy
s(\Phi_{v}(x,r))\geq S+1
, so
\max_{r}g(\Phi_{v}(x,r))\leq\varepsilon
, and therefore

\theta^{-m(x)}\kappa^{-s(x)^{2}}\,|(T_{v,1}f)(x)|\leq\varepsilon\,(s(x)+1)\kappa^{2s(x)+1}.

As
s(x)\to\infty
, the factor
(s(x)+1)\kappa^{2s(x)+1}
tends to
0
, so the right-hand side tends to
0
as well. This shows that

\theta^{-m(x)}\kappa^{-s(x)^{2}}\,|(T_{v,1}f)(x)|\xrightarrow[s(x)\to\infty]{}0,

i.e.
T_{v,1}f\in\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}
.
∎

The core/tail decomposition from Lemma
4.1
clearly restricts to
\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}
: the projection
P_{C}
has finite rank and maps
\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}
into itself, and the tail
T_{v,1}(I-P_{C})
acts on
\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}
with the same operator norm
<1
. Thus
T_{v,1}
is quasi-compact on
\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}
as well, with the same “finite-dimensional core + contracting tail” structure; but for the enumeration theorem we will only need that
T_{v,1}
is bounded on
\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}
.

On the countable state space
\mathsf{X}_{v}
the continuous dual of
\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}

can be identified with a
weighted

\ell^{1}
space. More precisely, put

w(x):=\theta^{m(x)}\kappa^{s(x)^{2}},\qquad x\in\mathsf{X}_{v}.

Then we have a canonical isometric identification

\bigl(\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}\bigr)^{\ast}\;\cong\;\ell^{1}\bigl(\mathsf{X}_{v},w\bigr):=\left\{\mu:\mathsf{X}_{v}\to\mathbb{C}:\|\mu\|_{1,w}:=\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}_{v}}|\mu(x)|\,w(x)<\infty\right\},

(1)

via the pairing

\displaystyle\langle\mu,f\rangle=\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}_{v}}\mu(x)\,f(x),\qquad\mu\in\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w),\ f\in\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}.

(2)

The series is absolutely convergent and satisfies

|\langle\mu,f\rangle|\leq\|\mu\|_{1,w}\,\|f\|_{\theta,\kappa}.

This is just the standard duality
(c_{0}(\mathsf{X}_{v}))^{\ast}\simeq\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v})

transported through the weighted isometry

f\longmapsto\bigl(\theta^{-m(x)}\kappa^{-s(x)^{2}}f(x)\bigr)_{x\in\mathsf{X}_{v}}.

In particular, the Dirac mass at the empty state
x_{\varnothing}

(the state corresponding to the empty permutation with trivial frontier)
belongs to this dual and has norm
1
, since
w(x_{\varnothing})=\theta^{0}\kappa^{0}=1
.

Using the identification (
1
), every continuous linear functional
on
\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}
is given by a unique
\mu\in\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w)
via the above
pairing. For a bounded linear operator

T\colon\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}\longrightarrow\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}

we define its (Banach) dual operator

T^{\ast}\colon\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w)\longrightarrow\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w)

by the duality relation

\langle T^{\ast}\mu,f\rangle\;=\;\langle\mu,Tf\rangle\qquad\text{for all }\mu\in\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w),\ f\in\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}.

By the general theory of Banach adjoints (the dual operator on the continuous dual)

T^{\ast}
is bounded and satisfies

\|T^{\ast}\|_{\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w)\to\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w)}=\|T\|_{\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}\to\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}};

see, for example, any standard functional analysis text for the duality

(c_{0})^{\ast}\simeq\ell^{1}
and the norm identity for the Banach adjoint.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
T_{v,1}\colon\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}\to\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}

be the transfer operator at
z=1
. Its dual operator

T_{v,1}^{\ast}\colon\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w)\longrightarrow\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w)

is the unique bounded operator satisfying

\langle T_{v,1}^{\ast}\mu,f\rangle\;=\;\langle\mu,T_{v,1}f\rangle\qquad\text{for all }\mu\in\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w),\ f\in\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}.

By construction
T_{v,1}^{\ast}
is bounded and

\|T_{v,1}^{\ast}\|_{\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w)\to\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w)}=\|T_{v,1}\|_{\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}\to\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}}<\infty.

To spell out the action of
T_{v,1}^{\ast}
on coordinates, fix
\mu\in\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w)

and
f\in\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}
. By definition of the dual operator and of

T_{v,1}
we have

\displaystyle\langle T_{v,1}^{\ast}\mu,f\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mu,T_{v,1}f\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}_{v}}\mu(x)\,(T_{v,1}f)(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}_{v}}\mu(x)\sum_{r\in A_{v}(x)}f\bigl(\Phi_{v}(x,r)\bigr).

The double series is absolutely convergent (by the norm bounds used to show that

T_{v,1}
is bounded), so we may rearrange the order of summation and group terms
according to
y=\Phi_{v}(x,r)
:

\displaystyle\langle T_{v,1}^{\ast}\mu,f\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathsf{X}_{v}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathsf{X}_{v},\,r\in A_{v}(x)\\
\Phi_{v}(x,r)=y\end{subarray}}\mu(x)\right)f(y).

Under the identification (
1
), this means that
T_{v,1}^{\ast}\mu

corresponds to the element
\nu\in\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w)
given by

\nu(y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathsf{X}_{v},\,r\in A_{v}(x)\\
\Phi_{v}(x,r)=y\end{subarray}}\mu(x),\qquad y\in\mathsf{X}_{v},

that is,

\displaystyle(T_{v,1}^{\ast}\mu)(y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathsf{X}_{v},\,r\in A_{v}(x)\\
\Phi_{v}(x,r)=y\end{subarray}}\mu(x).

(3)

In particular,
T_{v,1}^{\ast}
is the natural “push-forward along one legal insertion
step” on measures.

Iterating the action of
T_{v,1}^{\ast}
starting from the Dirac mass at the empty state, we obtain the following combinatorial identification.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
v
be fixed and let
x_{\varnothing}
be the state corresponding to the empty
permutation with its (trivial) frontier. Then, for every
n\geq 0
,

(T_{v,1}^{\ast})^{n}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}(\mathbf{1})\;=\;|\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v)|,

where
\mathbf{1}
denotes the everywhere-
1
function on
\mathsf{X}_{v}
, so that

(T_{v,1}^{\ast})^{n}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}(\mathbf{1})=\sum_{y\in\mathsf{X}_{v}}(T_{v,1}^{\ast})^{n}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}(y).

Proof.

For
n\geq 1
and states
x,y\in\mathsf{X}_{v}
, define a
legal insertion history of
length
n
from
x
to
y
to be a sequence of ranks

(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})

such that, writing inductively

x_{0}:=x,\qquad x_{j}:=\Phi_{v}(x_{j-1},r_{j})\quad(1\leq j\leq n),

we have
r_{j}\in A_{v}(x_{j-1})
for all
j
and
x_{n}=y
. We denote the set of all
such histories by

\mathcal{H}_{n}(x\to y)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{H}_{n}(x):=\bigcup_{y\in\mathsf{X}_{v}}\mathcal{H}_{n}(x\to y).

We claim that for each
y\in\mathsf{X}_{v}
,

(T_{v,1}^{\ast})^{n}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}(y)\;=\;|\mathcal{H}_{n}(x_{\varnothing}\to y)|.

(4)

We prove this by induction on
n
. For
n=0
,
(T_{v,1}^{\ast})^{0}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}=\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{0}(x_{\varnothing}\to y)
consists of the empty
history if
y=x_{\varnothing}
and is empty otherwise, so (
4
)
holds.

Assume (
4
) holds for some
n\geq 0
. Using the coordinate
formula (
3
) we obtain

\displaystyle(T_{v,1}^{\ast})^{n+1}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}(y)

\displaystyle=T_{v,1}^{\ast}\bigl((T_{v,1}^{\ast})^{n}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}\bigr)(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathsf{X}_{v},\,r\in A_{v}(x)\\
\Phi_{v}(x,r)=y\end{subarray}}(T_{v,1}^{\ast})^{n}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}(x).

By the induction hypothesis, each summand
(T_{v,1}^{\ast})^{n}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}(x)

is the number of legal histories of length
n
from
x_{\varnothing}
to
x
.
Each pair
(x,r)
with
\Phi_{v}(x,r)=y
corresponds to extending such a history
by one legal step
r
from
x
to
y
, and every legal history of length
n+1

from
x_{\varnothing}
to
y
arises in exactly one way from such an extension.
Thus the sum is exactly
|\mathcal{H}_{n+1}(x_{\varnothing}\to y)|
, proving
(
4
) for
n+1
.

Next we show legal histories are in bijection with
\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v)
.
Fix
n\geq 1
and a legal history

(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})\in\mathcal{H}_{n}(x_{\varnothing}),

and write the corresponding state sequence as

x_{0}=x_{\varnothing},\;x_{1},\dots,x_{n},\quad\text{with }x_{j}=(\pi^{(j)},\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi^{(j)})).

(a)
Every legal history produces a
v
-avoiding permutation of length
n
:

By Lemma
2.7
, each
\pi^{(j)}
avoids
v
, so the endpoint

x_{n}
encodes a
v
-avoiding permutation
\pi^{(n)}
of length
n
.

(b)
Distinct legal histories give distinct permutations:

The underlying right-insertion encoding

(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})\longmapsto\mathrm{ins}\bigl(\cdots\mathrm{ins}(\mathrm{ins}(\varnothing,r_{1}),r_{2})\cdots,r_{n}\bigr)

is injective on
all
sequences
(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})\in\prod_{j=1}^{n}\{1,\dots,j\}

by Lemma
2.2
. Restricting to those sequences that form legal histories
does not change this: two distinct legal histories cannot produce the same permutation.
Thus the map

\mathcal{H}_{n}(x_{\varnothing})\longrightarrow\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v),\qquad(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})\longmapsto\pi^{(n)}

is injective.

(c)
Every
v
-avoiding permutation arises from a legal history:

Conversely, let
\pi\in\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v)
be arbitrary. By Lemma
2.2
, there is a
unique sequence
(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})\in\prod_{j=1}^{n}\{1,\dots,j\}
such that

\pi=\mathrm{ins}\bigl(\cdots\mathrm{ins}(\mathrm{ins}(\varnothing,r_{1}),r_{2})\cdots,r_{n}\bigr).

For
j=0,1,\dots,n
let
\pi^{(j)}
be the permutation obtained after
j
insertions;
thus
\pi^{(0)}=\varnothing
and
\pi^{(n)}=\pi
. Inserting on the right does not
destroy existing occurrences of
v
: it only bumps values of existing entries.
Hence, if some intermediate
\pi^{(j)}
contained
v
, then so would the final

\pi^{(n)}
, contradicting
\pi\in\mathrm{Av}(v)
. Thus each
\pi^{(j)}
avoids
v
.

For each
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
we have

\pi^{(j)}=\mathrm{ins}(\pi^{(j-1)},r_{j}).

Since
\pi^{(j)}
avoids
v
, inserting
r_{j}
into
\pi^{(j-1)}
did not create any
occurrence of
v
using the new rightmost point. By construction of the frontier
(Definition
2.3
), this means that
r_{j}
does
not
lie in any
of the forbidden intervals
J_{\pi^{(j-1)}}(p)
attached to frontier elements

p\in\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi^{(j-1)})
, i.e.

r_{j}\in A_{v}\bigl(\pi^{(j-1)},\mathcal{F}_{v}(\pi^{(j-1)})\bigr).

In other words,
(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})
satisfies the legality condition at every step,
so it belongs to
\mathcal{H}_{n}(x_{\varnothing})
.

Thus every
\pi\in\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v)
arises as the endpoint of a unique legal history
starting from
x_{\varnothing}
, and we obtain a bijection

\mathcal{H}_{n}(x_{\varnothing})\;\longleftrightarrow\;\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v).

Since the sets
\mathcal{H}_{n}(x_{\varnothing}\to y)
partition
\mathcal{H}_{n}(x_{\varnothing})

according to the endpoint
y
, we have

\sum_{y\in\mathsf{X}_{v}}|\mathcal{H}_{n}(x_{\varnothing}\to y)|=|\mathcal{H}_{n}(x_{\varnothing})|=|\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v)|.

(5)

We now complete the proof. By definition of the duality pairing (
2
) and of the
constant function
\mathbf{1}
, we have

(T_{v,1}^{\ast})^{n}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}(\mathbf{1}):=\big\langle(T_{v,1}^{\ast})^{n}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}},\,\mathbf{1}\big\rangle=\sum_{y\in\mathsf{X}_{v}}(T_{v,1}^{\ast})^{n}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}(y)\,\mathbf{1}(y)=\sum_{y\in\mathsf{X}_{v}}(T_{v,1}^{\ast})^{n}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}(y).

Using (
4
) and (
5
) this becomes

(T_{v,1}^{\ast})^{n}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}(\mathbf{1})=\sum_{y\in\mathsf{X}_{v}}|\mathcal{H}_{n}(x_{\varnothing}\to y)|=|\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v)|,

as claimed.
∎

We can now state the main consequence.

Theorem 4.7
.

For every fixed classical pattern
v
there exists
R_{v}>0
such that

F_{v}(z):=\sum_{n\geq 0}|\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v)|\,z^{n}

is analytic for
|z|<R_{v}
. Equivalently, the exponential growth rate

\limsup_{n\to\infty}|\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v)|^{1/n}

is finite.

Proof.

Fix the transfer operator at
z=1
and write

T_{v}:=T_{v,1}.

From now on, the complex variable
z
denotes the argument of the generating
function
F_{v}(z)
and is unrelated to the weight parameter in
T_{v,z}
.

Since
T_{v}
is bounded on
\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}^{0}
, its dual operator

T_{v}^{\ast}
is bounded on
\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w)
, with

M:=\|T_{v}^{\ast}\|_{\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w)\to\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w)}<\infty.

For
|z|<1/M
the Neumann series for bounded operators,

(I-zT_{v}^{\ast})^{-1}=\sum_{n\geq 0}z^{n}(T_{v}^{\ast})^{n},

converges in operator norm on
\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w)
and depends analytically on
z
.
In particular, for each fixed
\mu\in\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w)
, the vector-valued series

\sum_{n\geq 0}z^{n}(T_{v}^{\ast})^{n}\mu

converges in
\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w)
for
|z|<1/M
and defines an analytic

\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w)
-valued function of
z
.

Now apply this with
\mu=\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}
. By
Lemma
4.6
,

|\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v)|=(T_{v}^{\ast})^{n}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}(\mathbf{1})

for all
n\geq 0
, where

(T_{v}^{\ast})^{n}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}(\mathbf{1}):=\big\langle(T_{v}^{\ast})^{n}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}},\,\mathbf{1}\big\rangle=\sum_{y\in\mathsf{X}_{v}}(T_{v}^{\ast})^{n}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}(y).

Thus

F_{v}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}|\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v)|\,z^{n}=\sum_{n\geq 0}z^{n}(T_{v}^{\ast})^{n}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}(\mathbf{1}).

For each
n
, the measure
(T_{v}^{\ast})^{n}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}
is supported on
states of length
n
(hence on a finite set), so

(T_{v}^{\ast})^{n}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}(\mathbf{1})
is a well-defined scalar.
Moreover, the scalar series above is obtained by composing the analytic

\ell^{1}(\mathsf{X}_{v},w)
-valued function

z\longmapsto\sum_{n\geq 0}z^{n}(T_{v}^{\ast})^{n}\delta_{x_{\varnothing}}

with the continuous linear functional

\nu\longmapsto\langle\nu,\mathbf{1}\rangle.

Therefore
F_{v}(z)
is analytic on the disc
|z|<1/M
.

Let
R_{v}
be the radius of convergence of
F_{v}(z)
. We have shown that

R_{v}\geq 1/M>0
, so
R_{v}>0
and the exponential growth rate

\limsup_{n\to\infty}|\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v)|^{1/n}=R_{v}^{-1}

is finite, as claimed.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

Theorem
4.7
only asserts that the ordinary generating function
F_{v}(z)
has positive radius of convergence, hence that
|\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v)|
grows at most exponentially fast. Our argument does
not
identify the exponential growth constant with the spectral radius of
T_{v,1}^{\ast}
in any precise way, nor does it attempt to determine the subexponential factor in the asymptotics of
|\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v)|
.

For special patterns, much sharper results are known by other methods. For instance, Regev’s representation-theoretic analysis of the monotone pattern
12\cdots k
shows that

|\mathrm{Av}_{n}(12\cdots k)|\;\sim\;c_{k}\,n^{\alpha_{k}}\,\rho_{k}^{n}

for explicit constants
c_{k}>0
,
\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\rho_{k}=(k-1)^{2}
; see
[
13
]
. From the operator-theoretic point of view, such results would correspond to a very detailed understanding of the peripheral spectrum of
T_{v,1}^{\ast}
and of the singularity structure of the resolvent
(I-zT_{v,1}^{\ast})^{-1}
near
|z|=1/\rho_{v}
. Developing this finer spectral theory lies well beyond the scope of the present paper, and we restrict ourselves here to the finiteness of the exponential growth rate.

4.5
An abstract right-visible growth theorem

The argument in Sections
2
–
4
only uses the following structural features of the classical pattern-avoidance state space. It is therefore convenient to record an abstract version of the result.

Definition 4.9

(Right-visible growth system)
.

A
right-visible growth system
consists of:

•

a countable state space
X
with a distinguished
root

x_{\varnothing}\in X
;

•

functions
s,m:X\to\mathbb{N}
, called the
length
and the
complexity
;

•

for each
x\in X
a finite set
A(x)
of
admissible extensions
, and a map

\Phi:\{(x,r):x\in X,\ r\in A(x)\}\longrightarrow X

such that
\Phi(x,r)
is the state obtained from
x
by one legal right-extension with parameter
r
.

We assume the following hypotheses.

(RV1)

(
Finite width at fixed length
) For each
L\in\mathbb{N}
there are only finitely many
x\in X
with
s(x)\leq L
.

(RV2)

(
Unit length growth
) For every
x\in X
and
r\in A(x)
we have

s(\Phi(x,r))=s(x)+1.

(RV3)

(
Monotone complexity
) For every
x\in X
and
r\in A(x)
we have

m(\Phi(x,r))\geq m(x).

(RV4)

(
At most linear branching
) There exist constants
C_{0},C_{1}\geq 0
such that

|A(x)|\leq C_{0}+C_{1}\,s(x)\qquad\text{for all }x\in X.

A
legal history of length
n
starting from
x_{\varnothing}
is a sequence
(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})
with

x_{0}:=x_{\varnothing},\qquad x_{j}:=\Phi(x_{j-1},r_{j}),\quad r_{j}\in A(x_{j-1})\text{ for all }j.

We denote by
a_{n}
the number of legal histories of length
n
starting from
x_{\varnothing}
.

Given such a system, we define, for parameters
0<\theta<1
and
0<\kappa<1
, the weighted
\ell^{\infty}
space

\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}:=\Bigl\{f:X\to\mathbb{C}:\|f\|_{\theta,\kappa}:=\sup_{x\in X}\theta^{-m(x)}\kappa^{-s(x)^{2}}|f(x)|<\infty\Bigr\},

and for
z\in\mathbb{C}
the transfer operator

(T_{z}f)(x):=z\sum_{r\in A(x)}f\bigl(\Phi(x,r)\bigr).

The proof of Lemma
3.2
goes through verbatim under (RV1)–(RV4), with
s(x)+1
replaced by the more general linear bound
C_{0}+C_{1}s(x)
in (RV4). Similarly, the core/tail decomposition in Section
4
only uses (RV1) and (RV2), and the duality argument in Section
4.4
uses only the fact that
X
is countable and that
s
increases by one at each step.

We therefore obtain the following abstract version of Theorem
4.7
.

Theorem 4.10

(Abstract right-visible growth)
.

Let
(X,s,m,A,\Phi)
be a right-visible growth system in the sense of Definition
4.9
. Then there exist parameters
0<\theta<1
,
0<\kappa<1
, and
R>0
such that:

(a)

For all
|z|\leq 1
the operator
T_{z}
is bounded and quasi-compact on
\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}
.

(b)

Writing
F(z):=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}
, the generating function
F
is analytic for
|z|<R
.

In particular, the exponential growth rate

\limsup_{n\to\infty}a_{n}^{1/n}=R^{-1}

is finite.

Proof sketch.

Fix
0<\theta<1
. The proof of Lemma
3.2
, with
|A(x)|
bounded linearly in
s(x)
by (RV4), shows that there exists
0<\kappa<1
such that
T_{1}
is bounded on
\mathcal{B}_{\theta,\kappa}
. The proof of Lemma
4.1
then shows that for a suitable cutoff
C
we can decompose

T_{1}=K_{C,1}+L_{C,1},

with
K_{C,1}
finite rank and
L_{C,1}
a strict contraction. This yields quasi-compactness of
T_{1}
and of
T_{z}
for
|z|\leq 1
exactly as in Proposition
4.3
. The duality and counting arguments in Section
4.4
carry over without change, giving analyticity of
F
in a disc and hence finiteness of the exponential growth rate.
∎

Remark 4.11
.

As in the concrete classical case, the quasi-compactness conclusion in part (a) is a structural add-on. For the mere finiteness of the exponential growth rate in part (b) one can work only with boundedness of
T_{1}
and the dual Neumann-series argument, ignoring the core/tail decomposition if desired.

Corollary 4.12

(Operator-theoretic Stanley–Wilf for classical patterns)
.

For each classical pattern
v\in S_{k}
, the right-insertion system
(X_{v},s,m,A_{v},\Phi_{v})
constructed in Section
2
is a right-visible growth system. In particular, Theorem
4.10
applies to the sequence
a_{n}=|\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v)|
and yields Theorem
4.7
.

Proof.

The state space
X_{v}
is countable, the length
s
is the permutation length, and
m
is the frontier size. Hypotheses (RV2) and (RV3) are exactly the unit-length and monotonicity properties recorded in Lemma
2.7
and Lemma
2.9
; (RV1) holds because there are only finitely many permutations (and frontiers) of a given length; and (RV4) holds with
C_{0}=1
and
C_{1}=1
, because there are at most
s(x)+1
admissible ranks from a state of length
s(x)
. Thus
(X_{v},s,m,A_{v},\Phi_{v})
is a right-visible growth system, and
a_{n}=|\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v)|
is exactly the number of legal histories of length
n
from the empty state
x_{\varnothing}
.
∎

The abstract theorem covers many standard transfer-matrix situations. For instance, if
X
encodes walks in a fixed-width strip with a finite step set, then
m
can be taken constant and
A(x)
consists of the finitely many legal next steps; (RV1)–(RV4) are automatic and Theorem
4.10
recovers the classical fact that the counting series has positive radius of convergence. More sophisticated examples include column-convex polyominoes grown column by column, where
m
records a finite boundary profile. We do not pursue these examples here, but the point is that the combinatorial input is entirely contained in the specification of
(X,s,m,A,\Phi)
, while the analytic conclusion is uniform.

5
Concluding remarks

We have recast the Stanley–Wilf finiteness statement for
classical
permutation patterns in an operator-theoretic language. From this point of view, the proof naturally splits into two conceptually distinct parts. The
pattern-dependent
step is entirely combinatorial: one must specify a suitable state space of decorated permutations, define the frontier as a pruned collection of
(k{-}1)
-partial occurrences (and their forbidden-rank intervals), and verify that it behaves well under right insertion (monotonicity of the frontier and the fact that it blocks exactly the bad ranks). The
pattern-independent
step is analytic: once such a description is available, a suitable two-parameter weighting makes the transfer operator bounded on a doubly weighted
\ell^{\infty}
space, and a dual Neumann-series argument then yields analyticity of the associated counting series. If one also performs the standard core/tail decomposition, one moreover obtains quasi-compactness of the transfer operator as a structural refinement.

Viewed this way, classical patterns are examples of what one might call
right-visible
permutation classes: when we grow the permutation by appending a rightmost entry, every constraint that could possibly be triggered by that step is already visible at the moment of insertion. The transfer-operator proof shows that, for such classes, once one has a well-behaved frontier (monotone under legal insertions and producing only a controlled amount of new local data per step), the analytic machinery can be deployed in a largely black-box fashion.

Several natural directions remain open and lie mostly on the combinatorial side:

•

It would be interesting to identify other classical avoidance problems that admit a genuinely right-visible frontier description. Even within classical patterns, one could ask whether certain families (for instance, those with additional symmetries or structural restrictions) give rise to frontiers with particularly simple dynamics under right insertion, and whether this can be exploited to sharpen asymptotics beyond mere exponential growth.

•

More speculatively, one can ask to what extent the frontier picture extends to generalized patterns (vincular, bivincular, or mesh patterns) for which the constraints remain local to a bounded “window” at the right. Examples include right-anchored vincular patterns or mesh patterns whose shaded cells lie in a finite right strip. For these classes the combinatorial side of the story—defining a frontier of partial occurrences and proving its monotonicity—appears to go through with only modest modifications, but known results on generalized and consecutive patterns (see, for instance,
[
6
]
and the discussion in
[
11
]
) show that one cannot expect a Stanley–Wilf type exponential bound in full generality. In the operator language, this obstruction would manifest as a failure of the key operator-norm estimates at
z=1
.

•

Finally, even in the purely classical setting, it would be natural to ask whether the operator framework can be refined to produce information stronger than mere finiteness of the exponential growth rate. For example, one could hope to obtain pure exponential asymptotics or detailed spectral information on the transfer operator, in the spirit of analytic combinatorics and Ruelle–Perron–Frobenius theory
[
7
,
2
]
. This would require a more delicate analysis of the peripheral spectrum and of the associated eigenvectors on the separable predual, but the basic quasi-compactness structure is already in place.

A natural next step would be to go beyond the mere finiteness of the exponential growth rate and to seek sharp asymptotics for
|\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v)|
. In the monotone case
v=12\cdots k
, Regev
[
13
]
showed that
|\mathrm{Av}_{n}(v)|
admits a precise asymptotic of the form
c_{k}\,n^{\alpha_{k}}\,\rho_{k}^{n}
by exploiting the RSK correspondence and detailed information on irreducible characters of
S_{n}
. It is tempting to ask whether our transfer-operator framework can be refined to recover such asymptotics for broader families of patterns. From the present point of view, this would amount to a careful study of the peripheral spectrum of
T_{v,1}
(or
T_{v,1}^{\ast}
) and of the singularity type of the resolvent at its spectral radius, a problem that appears substantially harder than the quasi-compactness and analyticity questions treated in this paper.
