Mask-associated dry eye disease following short-term facemask wear with or without nasal bridge taping among health workers at a Nigerian tertiary care facility: a cross-over randomized clinical trial
Authors: Kosisochukwu Perpetual Amaefuna, Regina Enubi Morgan, Charles Obu Bekibele
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMask-Associated Dry Eye disease (MADE) has been linked to facemask wear predominantly because of the upward flow of expired air towards the eyes. Taping the rim of the mask to the nasal bridge has been proposed to reduce MADE. This study compared dry eye disease rates following short-term face mask wear with or without taping the nasal bridge among health workers in National Hospital Abuja.MethodsThis was a cross-over randomized clinical trial conducted between February and July 2024 at National Hospital Abuja. It involved 77 health workers recruited according to their work environments (Open = 38 and Restricted = 39) using a stratified sampling method. Participants completed a modified MADE Questionnaire to assess MADE risk factors and symptoms. Dry eye symptom scores, Schirmer’s test values, Tear Break-Up Time (TBUT), and Oxford ocular surface staining scores were measured before and after six hours of facemask wear, with and without nasal bridge taping, on two separate days. Participants were randomized into group A or B corresponding to facemask wear without taping or with taping on the 1st day. MADE was defined as presence of dry eye symptoms plus an abnormal TBUT, Schirmer’s or Oxford corneal staining score post-facemask wear. Descriptive statistics, paired T-test, ANOVA, binary logistic regression and crossover analysis were used to analyze data using SPSS version 26, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 32.40 ± 9.14 years, with a male to female ratio of 1:2.21. There was a statistically significant difference in all outcome variables pre and post facemask wear with and without taping at the nasal bridge (p < 0.05). TBUT and Oxford scores showed greater improvement post-taped mask wear when compared with mask wear without taping (7.40 ± 2.24 s vs. 5.63 ± 1.82 s; and 0.23 ± 0.43 vs. 0.51 ± 0.70; p < 0.001). MADE developed in 14.3% of participants after facemask wear without taping, versus 3.9% with taping. Working in open environments was identified as a predictor of MADE in the non-taping group (OR: 0.111, p = 0.019). Crossover analysis showed significant carry-over effects in outcome variables.ConclusionsShort-term facemask wear can result in dry eye disease and taping facemasks at the nasal bridge can reduce these effects.Trial registrationRetrospectively registered with the Pan African Clinical Trial Registry (https://pactr.samrc.ac.za/) on August 1, 2025 PACTR202508721722676.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-026-04997-1.

Background

Facemask wear has been documented to increase the upward spread of air to the eyes which wrap around the ocular surface and induce dry eye and several ocular surface modifications which have been collectively termed ‘Mask Associated Dry Eye’ (MADE) [1–5]. The term was first used by an American ophthalmologist D.E White in June 2020 [4]. MADE was defined as a condition in which dry eye symptoms were present sometimes or worsened after wearing a facemask [6]. Dry eye disease can significantly reduce quality of life and is a common finding in ophthalmology clinics worldwide with prevalence ranging from 5 to 50% based on symptoms alone and up to 75% if based on clinical signs [7].

Health workers routinely wear facemasks at work, and this is even more so after the COVID-19 pandemic. Facemask wear has been documented to increase the upward flow of expired air to the eyes, especially in ill-fitted masks, which wrap around the ocular surface causing increased tear evaporation and precipitant ocular surface inflammation through lipid layer disruption or by maintaining a water vapor gradient [1]. Other mechanisms identified include: hypercapnia from increased carbon dioxide in expired air leading to increased corneal inflammation and hyperesthesia [1, 8]; increased ocular surface temperature and evaporation from warmer expired air [1]; and bacterial hypercolonization with resultant increased bacterial lipase activity and altered tear film stability [1]. The prevalence of MADE from previous studies ranged from 18.3% to 70% among face mask wearers [6, 9], with an incidence of 7.9% in a Chinese survey [10]. The risk factors for MADE include prolonged facemask wear, prolonged visual display terminal and reading time, previous or existing dry eye disease, wearing of ill-fitted masks, advanced age, female sex, higher educational status, and occupations requiring prolonged or constant face mask wear [1, 9–11]. Although prolonged face mask wear has been identified as a major risk factor for developing MADE [12–14], few studies assessing the effect of facemask wear for short periods of less than 8 h also found increased dry eye clinical features following face mask wear [15–17]. Reported clinical features of MADE include redness, burning sensation, tearing, feeling of dryness, itching, pain, increased ocular surface staining, reduced Tear Break-Up Time (TBUT), and Schirmer’s test values [6, 10, 18].

A 19-item questionnaire specific to MADE, termed MADE-Q which assessed the risk factors for MADE and the frequency of dry eye symptoms was developed by Boccardo and modified by Fan et al. [6, 10] The daily frequency of symptoms was recorded as never (0 times per day), sometimes (1–4 times per day), or often (> 4 times per day). All the symptoms were grouped into a single question to facilitate dissemination of the survey.

Studies have shown that proper taping of face masks led to reduced dry eye symptomatology and improved ocular surface parameters [19–21]. Improper taping of masks however can lead to a pull on the lower lid causing mechanical ectropion, lagophthalmos, reduced blinking, increased tear evaporation, and exposure keratopathy [1]. Despite routine use of facemasks by health workers in Nigeria, there are currently no local studies to the best of our knowledge on MADE in Nigeria. This is particularly important considering the peculiarities of the differences in environmental, and phenotypical make-up of Africans compared to other races. The objective of this study is to compare dry eye disease rates following short-term face mask wear with or without taping the nasal bridge among health workers and examine the associated factors contributing to MADE.

Materials and methods

The study was carried out at National Hospital Abuja-Nigeria between February 2024 to July 2024. It was a cross-over randomized clinical trial which investigated the development of dry eye disease following short-term face mask wear with or without taping the nasal bridge among health workers in National Hospital Abuja. Participants served as their own controls in the study.

Sample size was determined using the statistical formula for cross-over trials assuming a 20% change in TBUT post-intervention from a similar study [21], a statistical power of 80%, 5% type 1 error rate (α), and 10% non-response rate. A design effect of 3 was applied following the use of a stratified sampling technique.

A stratified sampling method was used to recruit participants to reduce differences in the work environment, and a total of 77 health workers, 38 participants from an open environment and 39 participants from a restricted environment concluded the research.

The exclusion criteria were recent diagnosis of dry eye disease within 6 months, presence of co-existing eyelid anomalies, use of preservative containing eye drops within the last 3 months, prior eye surgeries, contact lens wear, diabetes mellitus, thyroid eye disease, hypertension, pregnancy, use of hormone replacement therapy, prior facial radiation therapy, routine prolonged facemask wearers, respiratory diseases precluding compliance to facemask wear, non-compliance or poor compliance to facemask wear during the study duration, known allergy to the mask tape/face mask/fluorescein dye, and presence of high refractive errors (> 6 dioptres of myopia or hyperopia).

The symptom section of the Mask-Associated Dry Eye Disease Questionnaire (MADE-Q) [6, 10] was modified for this study to improve objectivity as the original questionnaire had no standardized scoring system for dry eye symptoms. The number of symptoms was increased to 8 as opposed to the 5 symptoms used in the original MADE-Q and the questions about the symptoms were assessed on an individual basis as opposed to grouping the symptoms as a single question in the original MADE-Q. The full modified MADE-Q used in this study is attached as a supplementary file named Modified MADE-Q. The risk factors included: daily visual display terminal time, outdoor time, reading time and facemask wear duration. The frequency ranged from rarely (0–4 h daily) to often (> 8 h daily). The dry eye symptom score was derived from a total of 8 symptoms which were scored from 0 (never) to 2(often) with a total score of 0–16. 0–1 = Normal; 2–4 = Mild dry eye disease; 5–9 = Moderate dry eye disease; 10 and above= severe dry eye disease.

All participants had a pre-intervention assessment which comprised of dry eye symptom scoring, measurement of visual acuity, Tear break-up time, Schirmer’s test and oxford corneal staining score. A computer-generated, randomly arranged table of 77 numbers was used to randomly separate the participants into 2 groups: A or B. Each number with its corresponding group was printed on a small piece of paper, folded, and placed in a bag. Each eligible participant picked one number from the pool, unfolded it, and read out the number and group to the researcher. This became their assigned study number and group. Group A represented wearing a facemask without taping at the nasal bridge on study day 1, while Group B represented wearing a facemask with taping at the nasal bridge on study day 1.

All participants wore a disposable 3-ply surgical facemask (UNIGLOVES PROFILE-TYPE IIR) with elastic ear loops under scrutiny of the researcher, to ensure a proper fit. Taping of the upper edge of the face mask to the nasal bridge was done using a 3.5inches X 0.5 inches Mask Tape (Gya Labs) applied to the inner rim of the mask and proper fit was ensured by the researcher before the participants left the eye clinic. They were also taught how to adjust and re-apply the mask tape, and the blink response of the participants was assessed after the application of the mask tape to ensure that the normal lower lid position was not affected by the mask tape. The facemask was worn for 6 h and participants were monitored every 2 h either physically or via phone calls to ensure compliance. Participants returned to the eye clinic after 6 h and a post-intervention ocular assessment was done to determine the dry eye symptom score, TBUT, Schirmer’s and Oxford grading scores. On the second study day, participants converged to the clinic, were given a facemask, and crossed over to taped/non-taped mask wear depending on their allocated group on day 1. Group A participants wore the mask with taping at the nasal bridge while group B participants wore the mask without taping at the nasal bridge for 6 h respectively. A post-intervention assessment was done after 6-hours of facemask wear as in day 1. A 24-hour washout period was allowed between the 2 study days. All pre-intervention assessments were done between 8 and 9 am and the post-intervention assessments between 2 and 3 pm. The average humidity during the study period was 68% while the temperature was 26.1 °C.

Data analysis was done using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26 (IBM SPSSS Statistics, Chicago, Illinois, USA). Data from both eyes were collected but the mean values between the two eyes was used for analysis. Mean and standard deviation were used for continuous variables like age, dry eye symptom scores, TBUT, Schirmer’s and Oxford corneal staining scores, while frequency and proportions were used for categorical variables like sex, risk factors for MADE, occupation and symptom frequency. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The paired sample t-test was used to compare the dry eye symptom scores, TBUT, Oxford corneal staining and Schirmer’s test scores pre- and post-facemask wear with and without taping. A repeated measure Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was also used to compare the differences between the outcome variables at baseline, pre-taping and post-taping. Binary Logistics Regression analysis was done to identify the socio-demographic predictors of Mask-Associated Dry Eye Disease following facemask wear. Cross-over specific analysis was also done. MADE was defined as presence of dry eye symptoms plus an abnormal TBUT, Schirmer’s or Oxford corneal staining score post-facemask wear.

The age range of participants in the study was 20–54 years, with a mean age of 32.40 ± 9.14 years. There were more females than males in the study with a male-to-female ratio of 1:2.21. Discussion

The mean age of participants was similar to the mean age of health workers in other studies on Mask-Associated Dry eye disease (MADE) [13, 21–23]. Similarly, a study on dry eye disease in Nigeria also had predominantly younger persons aged less than 40 years in their study [24]. This was however in contrast to the findings of Tatti et al. [5] and Maidawa et al. [25], who had a higher mean age of 44.56 ± 14.87 and 44.75 years ± 14.38 respectively. The relatively younger age of participants is likely because younger persons were more likely to be free of chronic ocular and systemic diseases which were major exclusion criteria from study participation compared to older persons. In addition, health workers in the hospital population comprise of people less than 65 years of age, as this is the new official retirement age for skilled clinical health workers in Nigeria [26].

Our study had more females compared to males, which is similar to other studies [5, 22, 23]. The higher number of female participants may be due to better health-seeking behaviors in females, similar to the findings of Atuanya et al. [27], hence more willingness to volunteer for participation and eye examination compared to males.

Similar to our study, other studies on MADE reported higher dry eye symptoms and corneal staining scores, and reduced TBUT and Schirmer’s test scores following facemask wear without taping [5, 16, 19]. These similarities is likely due to the proposed mechanism of MADE linked to upward leakage of exhaled air toward the ocular surface during mask wear, with subsequent acceleration of tear film evaporation, destabilization and eventual ocular surface irritation and inflammation [1]. As found in our study, taping of facemask also significantly improved the TBUT and Oxford staining scores in other studies [19–21]. This likely reflects the effectiveness of mask taping in limiting upward airflow from exhaled breath toward the eyes through improved sealing of the facemask around the nose and cheeks, hence a reduction in tear film evaporation and improved ocular surface integrity. However, the absence of a significant difference in dry eye symptom scores and Schirmer’s test values between taped and non-taped mask wear in our study is similar to the findings of Nair et al. who evaluated health workers wearing masks during an 8 h shift [21]. Askoy et al. [19] and Itokawa et al. [20] had a significant reduction in dry eye symptom scores using the OSDI following taping of masks. This disparity is likely because of the shorter duration of mask wear in our study compared to that of Askoy et al. where the facemask was worn with taping for 15 days [19].

Currently, there is no standardized criteria for diagnosing MADE and like Itokawa et al.’s [20] study, we defined MADE as presence of dry eye symptoms and an abnormal tear homeostatic test. In our study, 14.3% of participants developed MADE following facemask wear without taping. This is higher than the incidence of 7.9% recorded in a Chinese survey [10]. Other cross-sectional surveys reported a prevalence of 10.9% to 30.7% [6, 9, 20, 28, 29]. Although we found a lower incidence of MADE following mask taping, the reduction was not statistically significant. Another study also found less cases of MADE following taping of masks [20]. The reduction of MADE following taping of masks is presumably because of the restriction of upward air flow towards the eyes by the tape, hence reducing ocular surface evaporation and dryness.

Working in an open environment was found to be a significant predictor of MADE following facemask wear without taping at the nasal bridge (OR = 0.111, P = 0.019). This is similar to the findings from Echieh et al.’s (2021) study, who reported that cleaners working outdoors had a higher prevalence of dry eye disease due to more exposure to environmental factors such as dust, sunlight and smoke exposure than indoor cleaners working in more restricted environments, although this was not statistically significant [30]. Chlasta-Twardzik et al. (2021), also found that work environment significantly influenced the development of dry eye disease [31]. This is in contrast to the findings in Onwubiko et al.’s (2016) study on dry eye disease who found no significant association between outdoor/ indoor work environments on development of dry eye disease (OR 1.57, p = 0.289) [32]. This disparity may be due to a difference in the study design, unexplored occupation-dependent dry eye disease predisposing factors, and the diverse occupation of participants in their study.

The significant carry-over effect from baseline found in all outcome variables suggests that that the 24-hour wash-out period used in this study was likely insufficient. Future crossover studies evaluating MADE may benefit from the use of a longer washout period to minimize residual ocular surface effects and reduce the likelihood of carryover bias.

This study is limited by a short wash-out period, the absence of a strict control of environmental conditions, and lack of constant video surveillance to monitor facemask wear during the study due to the peculiar work schedule of health workers. Although phone call and random physical checks were done, the absence of round-the-clock video surveillance may lead to a Hawthorne effect which may lead to symptom under-reporting.

Conclusion

Short-term facemask wear for 6 h can result in dry eye disease especially in individuals working in an open environment. Applying a tape to the upper edge of the mask can reduce the upward flow of air towards the eyes and lead to mask-associated dry eye. Health workers and other routine mask wearers are encouraged to wear a mask tape and properly fit the mask to prevent ocular surface disturbances. Further studies involving a larger population with a strict control of environmental conditions are necessary to develop standardized diagnostic criteria for MADE.
