A Response to: Letter to the Editor regarding “Efficacy of Multifocal Soft Contact Lenses in Reducing Myopia Progression Among Taiwanese Schoolchildren: A Randomized Paired-Eye Clinical Trial”
Authors: Yao-Lin Liu, Tzu-Hsun Tsai
Published: 2024

We appreciate the author's interest and their valuable comments regarding our article ”Efficacy of Multifocal Soft Contact Lenses in Reducing Myopia Progression Among Taiwanese Schoolchildren: A Randomized Paired-Eye Clinical Trial” [1, 2]. Our study utilized a paired-eye design, which we believe is a reasonable and valid approach for this clinical trial. In a paired-eye design, the treatment effect is determined by comparing the treated eye to the control eye within the same subject, thereby minimizing inter-subject variability and potential confounding factors. This design is advantageous in controlling for individual biological and environmental differences, ensuring that each subject serves as their own control. While recent myopia control trials have predominantly involved randomization by subjects, paired-eye and crossover designs have also been employed in previous studies evaluating the myopia control effects of soft contact lenses [3, 4]. We believe that this approach remains both appropriate and feasible for our study.

Regarding the calculation of the percentage of myopia progression reduction, we determined the difference in progression between the treatment eye and the control eye for each subject, and then averaged these differences across all participants. This method is reasonable because it allows for a direct comparison between each treatment eye and its paired control eye. This approach aligns with our primary outcome measurement, which used paired t tests for statistical inference within the paired-eye design. However, we acknowledge that this calculation method can influence the resulting numbers. For example, if the treatment eye shows 0 D progression while the control eye shows 0.25 D progression in the same participant, the percentage reduction for this participant would be calculated as 100%. As Brennan et al. [5] highlighted, using percentage reduction in progression can be misleading and should be interpreted with caution. Nonetheless, we maintain that our method is appropriate within the context of our study design.

Concerning the clinically meaningful reduction of myopia progression, we agree with the International Myopia Institute's report, which states that there is no established minimum percentage reduction considered clinically meaningful [6]. The threshold for what constitutes a meaningful reduction can vary depending on factors such as the duration of treatment, sample population, and study design considerations. We believe that any reduction in myopia progression, as long as a causal effect is evident, is clinically significant, as even a small reduction can be beneficial by decreasing the risk of ocular morbidities in children later in life [7].
