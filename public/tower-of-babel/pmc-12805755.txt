Autistic eye contact? A hermeneutic phenomenological multicenter study of the similarities and differences in eye-contact experiences between adults with and without autism
Authors: Jos Boer, Nynke Boonstra, Linda Kronenberg, Sonja Kuipers, Richard Vuijk, Bram Sizoo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Eye contact is one of the most important forms of interpersonal communication. Nonetheless, research has shown that there is no gold standard for how eye contact should occur. Atypicalities in eye contact are one of the core features of autism spectrum disorder (ASD), but there is still no consensus on what constitutes atypical eye contact in ASD. The current research explores both the breadth and depth of experiences with eye contact in adults with and without ASD. We used a hermeneutic phenomenological multicenter design in which 15 adults with ASD and 15 adults without ASD were interviewed. Analyses using Multisite Qualitative Analysis (MSQA) and the PRICE model for saturation identified four themes: opinion on eye contact, experience of eye contact, approach toward eye contact, and needs regarding eye contact. Adults with and without ASD appeared to have overlapping and distinct experiences. This study provides the first insights into similarities and differences in experiences with eye contact in adults with and without ASD. The results provide guidance for future research and for the development of interventions to reduce problems arising from eye contact in ASD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11689-025-09663-z.

Eye contact is considered one of the most important forms of interpersonal communication [1]. Eyes reveal a person’s mental state and a variety of complex social and emotional information [2]. Eye contact provides the function of synchronization between interaction partners [3] and signals involvement in an interaction [4]. Moreover, eye contact activates pro-social behavior [5]; leads to greater insight into other people’s identities [6]; improves various forms of self-awareness [7]; and has been shown to improve several of the other’s social-cognitive functions, such as face memory, joint attention, compassion, and empathy [8–10].

The importance of eye contact can also be seen in the attention paid to the eyes during interactions. Studies in the 1980s [11, 12] showed that 55% of attention goes to the nonverbal, 38% to the tone of voice, and only 7% to the words that are being spoken, something that became known as the Mehrabian 55-38-7 rule. Within the nonverbal, 71% of attention goes to the face, of which 45% goes to the eyes [13]. These studies suggest that during an interaction, two and a half times as much importance is attached to the eyes than to spoken words.

While eye contact is highly defining of human relationships, research shows that there seems to be no gold standard for how eye contact should be made or interpreted by the interaction partner [14]. Nevertheless, one of the core features of ASD is abnormalities in eye contact, according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders [15]. This suggests a dichotomous image that eye contact would be atypical in individuals with ASD and normal in neurotypical individuals. Yet, eye contact appears to be partly determined by characteristics, such as age, gender, culture, and personality [14]. Atypicalities in eye contact have also been noted in other mental disorders, such as schizophrenia [16], post-traumatic stress disorder [17], and social anxiety disorder [18]. Additionally, there has been discussion about when eye contact occurs: when the gaze is directed within 38 degrees to the left or right of the other person’s eyes [19], when looking between the eyes [20], or when a mutual gaze lasts no longer than three to four seconds [21]. The above raises the question of what constitutes atypical eye contact in ASD.

In recent years, various empirical research has been conducted on eye contact in ASD. `A decreased tendency in children to look at and follow an adult’s eyes is considered to be one of the earliest indicators of an impending ASD diagnosis [22, 23]. People with ASD also seemed to have no preference for a direct or averted gaze [24], remember a direct gaze less well [25], and give no priority to eyes over other facial areas [26, 27]. People with ASD are thought to have difficulty understanding social-communicative eye information [28], experience reduced social attention [29], and avoid eye-contact action [30, 31]. Special features regarding brain activity during eye-contact in people with ASD were also seen, such as abnormal amygdala activity [32]. Atypical eye contact in ASD is reported to lead to significant barriers and challenges during social interactions [33].

Various models have been developed to explain what this atypical eye contact in ASD entails: the hyperarousal/gaze-aversion theory [34], the hypo-arousal/social motivation theory [35, 36], the mindblindness theory [37] and the fast-track modulator theory [38]. However, there is no consensus on how autistic eye contact should be understood.

Qualitative research could contribute to greater understanding, but qualitative studies of eye contact in adults with ASD are very scarce. A study by Trevisan et al. [39] provided meaningful insights, although it involved people with self-declared ASD and their classification was not officially verified. Garvey et al. [40] are possibly the first to interview adults with ASD about eye-contact experiences. New insights into awareness, phenomenology, and strategies concerning making eye contact in adults with ASD have been found [40]. In adults without ASD, multiple studies have explored their experience of eye contact. For example, Argyle and Dean [41] examined how variations in eye contact influences feelings of intimacy and social distance. Similarly, Kleinke’s comprehensive review [42] summarizes decades of research on typical adults’ subjective reactions to gaze, including reports of anxiety, comfort levels, and cultural norms related to eye contact. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no research in which adults without ASD were asked about their views on eye contact. To date, there are no studies comparing views on eye-contact between adults with and without ASD.

Further research into similarities and differences between adults with and without autism regarding eye contact could lead to greater knowledge and understanding of the characteristics of eye contact in adults with ASD compared to people without ASD. This knowledge and understanding would, for example, help in the development of interventions to reduce complaints that people with ASD experience regarding eye contact [39], despite the finding in a previous literature review showing that eye contact varies considerably, both in adults with and without ASD [14].

The present study aims to examine the subjective views and experiences of adults with ASD compared to adults without ASD in relation to eye-contact. Factors such as views about the phenomenon, problems because of eye contact, development in eye contact, interpretations of others, and more are explored.

Methods

To gain insight into the subjective eye-contact views and experiences of adults with ASD compared to adults without ASD, a hermeneutic phenomenological design using Multisite Qualitative Analysis (MSQA) [43] and the PRICE model for saturation during data analysis [44] was used. MSQA is an analytical method for multisite qualitative studies that enables the highest qualitative accuracy and the gathering of rich context-specific insights in lived experiences deriving from the data, which maximizes applicability. This iterative analysis method comprises separate within-site data analysis followed by a between-site analysis and, finally, a second within-site analysis [43, 45]. For a graphical representation of MSQA, see Fig. 1. Methodologically, the PRICE model helps achieve saturation throughout the thematic analysis process by following the steps of perspectivation, recapitulation, integration, crystallization, and edification [44]. We chose semi-structured interviews for data collection and analysis because adults with ASD attach importance to, on the one hand, having the space to share their experiences in their own way but, on the other hand, need frameworks to know what is asked of them [46]. A hermeneutic phenomenological approach was chosen as a flexible and practical way to understand the meaning of lived experiences. This approach, grounded in philosophical concepts developed by Heidegger and Stambaugh [47] and Gadamer [48], combines the perspective of phenomenological research which focuses on the essence of the phenomenon of experiences, and the perspective of hermeneutic research which focuses on the interpretation of these experiences.

Multisite design was chosen in response to criticism that in research, single-site findings may limit the relevance, transferability, and reliability for other settings [49, 50].

We used the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies (COREQ) checklist [51] because it provides the explicit and comprehensive reporting of qualitative research. For the checklist, see Appendix 1. The Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) checklist [52] was also used. It aims to improve the transparency of all aspects of qualitative research by providing clear standards for reporting qualitative research.

We developed the research design in collaboration with three adults with ASD and three adults without ASD to ensure that relevant information from the perspective of the target groups was included.

Researcher characteristics and reflexivity

The participants were recruited, and the interviews were conducted by JB (male, the first author of this study, and a nursing specialist in psychiatry at a specialist center for developmental disorders). JB attended a course on Atlas.ti and qualitative research methods. The participants did not know the researcher other than for the research purpose, and they knew that the interview was being conducted for research purposes. The research team comprised experienced researchers in qualitative studies who have published numerous qualitative research articles. To prevent a lack of reflexivity [53] on the part of JB, member checks were used in addition to considering the perspectives of other members of the research team and of the second reviewer [54].

Context

The study was conducted in the Netherlands. The participants with ASD comprised adults in outpatient treatment at one of three specialized centers for ASD: Dimence Group in Deventer, SARR Rotterdam, or Leo Kannerhuis Amsterdam. Four of the 15 adult with autism were being treated for their autism, whereas the remaining 11 were treated for a combination of autism and mental health co-morbidity (see Table 2). Participants without autism reported they had never been diagnosed with autism or any other psychiatric disorder. The researchers did not confirm this by assessing the absence of autistic symptoms in the control group. The participants were asked to choose whether the interview was conducted online (Microsoft Teams) or at a physical location that was convenient for them. This appeared to be their home, the centers where they were receiving treatment, or online via Microsoft Teams. There were no systematic differences between autistic and non-autistic participants in the location of interviews. The participants were given the opportunity to ask questions or take a break at any time during the interview.

Sampling strategy and units of study

The participants were selected by stratified sampling. Single random sampling was used for the group of adults with ASD, and clustered sampling was used for the site of adults without ASD. To determine whether no further sampling was necessary to achieve saturation, guidelines for sample size in qualitative research [55, 56] and the PRICE model for saturation during data analysis were used [44].

Adults without ASD were recruited by messages on an intranet via secondary vocational schools, colleges, universities, and churches, based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. If they were interested in participating, the researcher sent them detailed research information by secure email, after which they were given time to consider their intention to participate and to ask the researcher for further clarification about the study. After they signed the consent form, the researcher sent them an invitation by secure email to conduct the interview.

For adults with ASD, the researcher approached practitioners from the three specialized centers for ASD. Practitioners assessed which of their clients were eligible for the study based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria given in Table 1. For adults with ASD, the researcher approached practitioners from the three specialized centers for ASD. For all three centers, cognitive function in or above the normal intelligence range is a condition for acceptance into their treatment programs. Practitioners assessed which of their clients were eligible for the study based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria given in Table 1. For the purpose of matching, we used educational attainment level of participants in both groups. When eligible, the practitioner discussed with their clients their willingness to participate based on the detailed research information. Where necessary, clients were given time to think about their participation and the opportunity to ask additional questions of their practitioner. After receiving the signed consent form, the researcher securely sent an invitation to the clients to conduct the interview.

Data-collection instruments and technologies

The interviews were conducted from June to September 2024.

The interviews were conducted using a topic list (see Fig. 2), which was developed in co-design with three adults with autism and three adults without autism, who provided lay feedback to the research team and were not otherwise involved in the study. The topic list was pilot-tested with 2 participants. Data from the interviews conducted during the pilot test were not included in the final analysis. Based on the pilot test, no adjustments in the topic list appeared to be necessary.

Fig. 2Topic list interviews

Topic list interviews

During the research, no repeat interviews were conducted. The participants were informed prior to participation that the interview would be audio recorded. Field notes were made by JB after every interview, and they were used to assist in the analysis of the transcribed audio recordings. The mean length of the interviews was 45 min, ranging from 30 to 54 min. The transcripts were randomly returned for feedback to one participant with and one participant without ASD. No comment or correction was recommended.

Data processing

Communication with and about the participants occurred via secure email. Audio recordings of the interviews were executed on a Samsung A54 telephone. The interviews were securely and anonymously transcribed verbatim at a transcription company to Microsoft 365 MSO (version 2402). During data analysis, the software program Atlas.ti (version 24.2.1.32227) was used for the coding process, and Microsoft 365 MSO (version 2402) was used for theme creation. The paper data, including the signed consent forms, were kept in a locked safe in a locked room of the organization from which the research was conducted. The digital data was linked to a number that only the researchers had access to. Finally, digital data was stored on a secure hard drive of the organization from which the research was conducted.

Data analysis

Two authors (JB and SK) independently performed the steps within the MSQA method using the PRICE model. Within each site, JB and SK consulted on findings after five interviews, after which the remaining 10 interviews within each site were analyzed. Findings were then discussed with the entire research team for each site. A member check according to McKim [57] was then randomly conducted on the findings among participants with and without ASD, from which no changes emerged.

Techniques to enhance trustworthiness

Representativeness was promoted by the multicenter design [43] and variation in demographic characteristics within and between sites. The credibility, reliability and authenticity of the data were enhanced because the interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim. Furthermore, credibility was increased because the topic list and research design were developed in co-design with adults with ASD. Additionally, the credibility of the data was guaranteed through member checking, in which participants with and without ASD checked the data analysis for accuracy. Credibility and reflexivity during data analysis were also guaranteed by using a peer research group, which was involved in all steps of the research process and with a second reviewer, who was an independent person from outside the peer group. To increase the verifiability of the analysis, the memos for meaning were included in the analysis. To increase authenticity, the interviews were conducted in a place where the participants felt as comfortable as possible. Credibility was increased because data collection was only completed when data saturation was reached based on the sample size [56] and data-analysis method [44]. Consistency between researcher and reviewer was promoted by the fact that both worked according to the same protocol of the MSQA method and PRICE model. Additionally, due to the iterative process, regular discussions occurred between the two to reach consensus.

Theme 1: opinion on eye contact

This theme describes the views of adults with and without ASD regarding aspects of eye contact. The results relate to eye contact as a phenomenon, the functions of eye contact, and autistic eye contact.

Subtheme 1 – Eye contact as a phenomenon

Most of the participants with ASD saw eye contact as looking at each other, while some of the participants with ASD defined eye contact as contacting each other through the eyes.

Participant with ASD (4) – “That you look at each other. That you focus on someone’s eyes.”

Participant with ASD (4) – “That you look at each other. That you focus on someone’s eyes.”

Meanwhile, most of the participants without ASD defined eye contact as making contact through the eyes.

Participant without ASD (1) – “I would say that contact means that you experience some kind of connection, which may sound a bit soft. But you look at someone and you think: hey, apart from speaking to each other, we also have some kind of contact through our eyes.”

Participant without ASD (1) – “I would say that contact means that you experience some kind of connection, which may sound a bit soft. But you look at someone and you think: hey, apart from speaking to each other, we also have some kind of contact through our eyes.”

The participants with ASD were divided about what they thought of eye contact as a phenomenon. Some believed that eye contact can be useful because someone’s eyes provide information about that person. Yet, most find that they prefer another part of the body as a source of information, such as the mouth, or they indicated that they do not need eye contact in social contact.

Participant with ASD (6) – “I find that particularly uncomfortable. But hey, if that’s what it has to be, then it has to be done. But that gives me more of a negative added value to the conversation than a positive added value to the conversation. So I don’t feel any connection or anything, mainly discomfort.”

Participant with ASD (6) – “I find that particularly uncomfortable. But hey, if that’s what it has to be, then it has to be done. But that gives me more of a negative added value to the conversation than a positive added value to the conversation. So I don’t feel any connection or anything, mainly discomfort.”

This differs from the participants without ASD, who almost all find eye contact of added value for themselves. In particular, they mentioned that it provides an extra layer of communication.

Participant without ASD (5) – “But I think it helps to look at: hey, is this message kind of, maybe sincere. So it might also say something about intention. In any case, it adds an extra layer of communication. And sometimes that gets confusing. But if you do it right, it has an affirmative effect.”

Participant without ASD (5) – “But I think it helps to look at: hey, is this message kind of, maybe sincere. So it might also say something about intention. In any case, it adds an extra layer of communication. And sometimes that gets confusing. But if you do it right, it has an affirmative effect.”

Subtheme 2 – Functions of eye contact

Most of the participants with ASD believed the function of eye contact is to demonstrate attention to the other person, to connect with the other person through the eyes, and to signal decency.

Participant with ASD (9) – “That I, that someone knows: oh, she is listening to me, because I would like people to know that I am listening.”

Participant with ASD (9) – “That I, that someone knows: oh, she is listening to me, because I would like people to know that I am listening.”

The participants without ASD believed eye contact has the special function of making a connection with the other person. They also thought that eye contact is a form of synchronization between interaction partners, in particular that eye contact can be used to see whether conversation partners are on the same page, a function that is not mentioned by the participants with ASD.

Participant without ASD (10) – “I think that, yes, that can sometimes really shape the conversation a bit or that you actually look at each other for a moment and say, we see, we see, we have the same, our reaction to this.”

Participant without ASD (10) – “I think that, yes, that can sometimes really shape the conversation a bit or that you actually look at each other for a moment and say, we see, we see, we have the same, our reaction to this.”

Subtheme 3 – Autistic eye contact

Just under half of the participants with ASD find eye contact atypical if it is too long or too short, although they could not indicate when it is too long or too short. The other participants with ASD had individual views on what atypical eye contact would be. This is similar to how the participants without ASD described atypical eye contact.

Participant with ASD (4) – “And I think, if I take a guideline, it is somewhere that you should not continuously stare at someone, but also not continuously look away. That once in a while, you focus on someone’s eye again for a moment and then you look in all directions again. That it is a natural conversation.”

Participant with ASD (4) – “And I think, if I take a guideline, it is somewhere that you should not continuously stare at someone, but also not continuously look away. That once in a while, you focus on someone’s eye again for a moment and then you look in all directions again. That it is a natural conversation.”

Almost every participant with ASD was convinced that there is such a thing as autistic eye contact, although different characteristics were suggested for this individually. The most common belief was that autistic eye contact is characterized by making less eye contact or that people with ASD do not understand eye contact.

Participant with ASD (9) – “You notice that a little more, that’s different. You quickly realize that when someone makes little eye contact, you know: oh, he might have autism.”

Participant with ASD (9) – “You notice that a little more, that’s different. You quickly realize that when someone makes little eye contact, you know: oh, he might have autism.”

The participants without ASD were more cautious about what autistic eye contact would be, and about half said they had no opinion about this. The other participants without ASD provided individual characteristics, such as that people with ASD look away more, make less eye contact, or have a rational approach to eye contact.

Participant without ASD (10) – “Well, I don’t know, but my feeling is that it doesn’t really exist. So I don’t think you can quickly tell from someone’s eye contact whether he or she has autism, to put it the other way around.”

Participant without ASD (10) – “Well, I don’t know, but my feeling is that it doesn’t really exist. So I don’t think you can quickly tell from someone’s eye contact whether he or she has autism, to put it the other way around.”

Theme 2: experience of eye contact

This theme describes how eye contact is experienced by adults with and without ASD. The results relate to consciousness and eye contact, experiences across a life course, and experiencing eye contact with acquaintances and strangers.

Subtheme 1 – Consciousness and eye contact

Almost all the participants with ASD had experienced eye contact consciously since childhood until the present.

Participant with ASD (11) – “I’m not counting seconds, but it remains a bit of an emotional issue for me: now it feels unpleasant, so I have to kind of look away. These are mainly the moments that I choose, so when someone asked me a question, I looked at that person to let them know: I am listening to your question. Then I thought: when I’m talking, it’s easier for me to look away, because people often look away when they’re thinking, for example. I thought: I’m thinking about my answer, so I can look away. Then looking back at that person at the moment I was giving an important part of my answer, to know: this is important.”

Participant with ASD (11) – “I’m not counting seconds, but it remains a bit of an emotional issue for me: now it feels unpleasant, so I have to kind of look away. These are mainly the moments that I choose, so when someone asked me a question, I looked at that person to let them know: I am listening to your question. Then I thought: when I’m talking, it’s easier for me to look away, because people often look away when they’re thinking, for example. I thought: I’m thinking about my answer, so I can look away. Then looking back at that person at the moment I was giving an important part of my answer, to know: this is important.”

This contrasts with all the participants without ASD, who had never experienced eye contact consciously until the present, with the exception of situations in which they intentionally wanted to induce a reaction in the other person, such as in front of the class or during a meeting.

Participant without ASD (2) – “But if you have a formal interview, as I said with the job application for example, then I am consciously working. I think this is also due to the training, that they all teach different techniques for this. So, for example, also mirroring, also the way you sit. So if you’re having a conversation and someone is leaning back, you better do the same. Then you match the vibe a bit.”

Participant without ASD (2) – “But if you have a formal interview, as I said with the job application for example, then I am consciously working. I think this is also due to the training, that they all teach different techniques for this. So, for example, also mirroring, also the way you sit. So if you’re having a conversation and someone is leaning back, you better do the same. Then you match the vibe a bit.”

Subtheme 2 – Experiences across a life course

Several participants with ASD experienced eye contact with adults as threatening in their youth and therefore avoided eye contact. Others became aware of their eye contact because parents reminded them that they needed to make more eye contact.

Participant with ASD (2) – “I think it gave some sort of threatening feeling perhaps. Not that I thought: that person is going to eat me now. A parent like that would give you back: he won’t eat you. He’s not going to kill you. And you knew that. Then you think: yes. But then it felt…You really feel like you’re being stared at. And that was really a kind of menacing feeling. Don’t know.”

Participant with ASD (2) – “I think it gave some sort of threatening feeling perhaps. Not that I thought: that person is going to eat me now. A parent like that would give you back: he won’t eat you. He’s not going to kill you. And you knew that. Then you think: yes. But then it felt…You really feel like you’re being stared at. And that was really a kind of menacing feeling. Don’t know.”

Several participants without ASD also experienced eye contact with adults as threatening in their youth but did not avoid eye contact in response. The other participants without ASD could not clearly remember their experiences of eye contact in childhood.

Participant without ASD (10) – “No, I don’t really remember that. Perhaps at most it is sometimes said, look at me when I talk to you, so to speak, more in the parenting role. Maybe from my parents’ point of view, but not that I really think: that really comes to the fore.”

Participant without ASD (10) – “No, I don’t really remember that. Perhaps at most it is sometimes said, look at me when I talk to you, so to speak, more in the parenting role. Maybe from my parents’ point of view, but not that I really think: that really comes to the fore.”

In adulthood, almost all the participants with ASD experience eye contact as intense and uncomfortable. This is mainly because they feel that they are being penetrated through their eyes, and their vulnerabilities, such as ASD, are then seen. Consequently, they feel that they are being judged on these vulnerabilities.

Participant with ASD (14) – “The worst thing imaginable, what you just said, is that people can look inside you, that vulnerable thing, that very penetrating thing, that particular thing. And it is also that sometimes, if someone keeps looking for a long time or looks in a certain way, I start to think even more about what someone means and that I can become even more insecure in a conversation.”

Participant with ASD (14) – “The worst thing imaginable, what you just said, is that people can look inside you, that vulnerable thing, that very penetrating thing, that particular thing. And it is also that sometimes, if someone keeps looking for a long time or looks in a certain way, I start to think even more about what someone means and that I can become even more insecure in a conversation.”

This discomfort contrasts with the participants without ASD, who have experienced eye contact in adulthood as neutral to positive. They mainly experience eye contact as something that adds value to interactions. Some also feel that they are being invaded, but they do not experience this as unpleasant or judgmental.

Participant without ASD (15) – “Yes, I like it sometimes. Then people also know about_ I always like that, that people can still read from you, even if you can’t express it with words, for example, that people can see from your face, or from your eyes for that matter, that it touches me or that I am shocked or that I have to think about it for a moment. That I can show it this way, while I may still be looking for the words I want to convey about it.”

Participant without ASD (15) – “Yes, I like it sometimes. Then people also know about_ I always like that, that people can still read from you, even if you can’t express it with words, for example, that people can see from your face, or from your eyes for that matter, that it touches me or that I am shocked or that I have to think about it for a moment. That I can show it this way, while I may still be looking for the words I want to convey about it.”

Subtheme 3 – Experiencing eye contact with acquaintances and strangers

Some of the participants with ASD find eye contact with acquaintances more pleasant than with strangers. This is because they have a greater sense of what eye contact means from acquaintances, can decide for themselves how to make eye contact with acquaintances, and feel that acquaintances do not judge them based on their eye contact.

Participant with ASD (9) – “If I know people very well, then I know that I don’t have to look at them all the time. That person knows me a little, so that’s different. But when I really meet new people, I really start the conversation: oh yes, you really have to look at people, don’t look away for too long, make sure you appear polite, because eye contact is polite, then you listen to the person, then the person feels heard. So I always try to take that into account.”

Participant with ASD (9) – “If I know people very well, then I know that I don’t have to look at them all the time. That person knows me a little, so that’s different. But when I really meet new people, I really start the conversation: oh yes, you really have to look at people, don’t look away for too long, make sure you appear polite, because eye contact is polite, then you listen to the person, then the person feels heard. So I always try to take that into account.”

Of the participants without ASD, about half find eye contact with acquaintances as pleasant as with strangers. The other half find eye contact with acquaintances more pleasant than with strangers because it is familiar to them, and they can easily see from the other person’s eyes how that person is feeling mentally.

Participant without ASD (12) – “And then it is much easier to read someone, because you know someone well. And then you can very easily, I think, look at someone and say: this is how he is now, or that is how he feels now. I think it’s much easier to see that from someone you know well, because you’ve seen all those emotions in someone before.”

Participant without ASD (12) – “And then it is much easier to read someone, because you know someone well. And then you can very easily, I think, look at someone and say: this is how he is now, or that is how he feels now. I think it’s much easier to see that from someone you know well, because you’ve seen all those emotions in someone before.”

Theme 3 - Approach toward eye contact

This theme describes how adults with and without ASD have managed eye contact in their lives. The results relate to development in eye contact, the management of eye contact across a life course, and the interpretation of other people’s eye contact.

Subtheme 1 – Development in eye contact

Most of the participants with ASD have rationally learned to make and interpret eye contact themselves or sometimes with the help of others.

Participant with ASD (7) – “Yes, just like a lot of social things. Then it’s just noticing that people are doing that and then just imitating it, and just doing that as much as possible.”

Participant with ASD (7) – “Yes, just like a lot of social things. Then it’s just noticing that people are doing that and then just imitating it, and just doing that as much as possible.”

Most of the participants without ASD did not learn eye contact but rather developed it in a natural, unconscious way.

Participant without ASD (2) – “I don’t know if I have a direct memory of that. I think that came automatically. If you hear that a number of times you think: I have to do that, apparently that is part of it and you will then automatically do it. And at some point it gets stuck in the subconscious.”

Participant without ASD (2) – “I don’t know if I have a direct memory of that. I think that came automatically. If you hear that a number of times you think: I have to do that, apparently that is part of it and you will then automatically do it. And at some point it gets stuck in the subconscious.”

Subtheme 2 – Managing eye contact across a life course

As children, just under half of the participants with ASD were told by parents that they should make more eye contact with others. For just under half of the participants with ASD, eye contact was not discussed in their youth.

Participant with ASD (6) – “Yes, I heard that as a child: looking at a little more, but I think I also notice: hey, but I do need the face to read emotions, but not specifically the eyes.”

Participant with ASD (6) – “Yes, I heard that as a child: looking at a little more, but I think I also notice: hey, but I do need the face to read emotions, but not specifically the eyes.”

In the participants without ASD, virtually no one was taught how to make eye contact during childhood. However, most were raised to believe that making eye contact is a sign of decency.

Participant without ASD (4) – “I can’t remember a time when I didn’t do it. But it’s just like when someone, when someone important comes along, a pastor comes along and says: well, shake your hand, look at you and when someone asks a question, you just look at them and answer politely.”

Participant without ASD (4) – “I can’t remember a time when I didn’t do it. But it’s just like when someone, when someone important comes along, a pastor comes along and says: well, shake your hand, look at you and when someone asks a question, you just look at them and answer politely.”

In adulthood, almost every participant with ASD had thought about how they were managing their eye contact during an interaction and what they should do at that moment. Yet, the vast majority indicated that they consciously avert their gaze during interactions because eye contact feels unpleasant, or it allows them to concentrate better on what is being said. An overwhelming majority also said that the way they make eye contact is by copying the other person’s eye behavior so that the other person will find their eye contact as normal as possible. Additionally, an overwhelming majority said they camouflage their difficulty with handling eye contact because they want to appear normal.

Participant with ASD (11) – “With people I know less well, it is important, especially with people who do not know that I am autistic, it is important to pretend that I am not autistic, I think, and to pretend that I can be just like them and therefore can indeed have eye contact at the right time.”

Participant with ASD (11) – “With people I know less well, it is important, especially with people who do not know that I am autistic, it is important to pretend that I am not autistic, I think, and to pretend that I can be just like them and therefore can indeed have eye contact at the right time.”

Almost all the participants without ASD have no plan for how to handle eye contact in adulthood. However, they sometimes consciously use eye contact to reinforce their message to others. Several participants without ASD also sometimes look away during interactions to think better about the conversation.

Participant without ASD (7) – “No, that’s why I know, I can’t formulate it very well. No, I don’t consciously think about that.”

Subtheme 3 – Interpretation of other people’s eye contact

Almost all of the participants with ASD find it difficult to understand another person’s eye contact, specifically reading what that person feels or means from their eyes.

Participant with ASD (1) – “I find that very difficult. I can try, but I have always had some difficulty - which is also typical - with reading facial expressions. Ultimately, that is also a matter of practice. I eventually learned some of it, but I prefer the voice alone.”

Participant with ASD (1) – “I find that very difficult. I can try, but I have always had some difficulty - which is also typical - with reading facial expressions. Ultimately, that is also a matter of practice. I eventually learned some of it, but I prefer the voice alone.”

Theme 4 - Needs regarding eye contact

This theme describes the future wishes of adults with and without ASD regarding eye contact. The results relate to problems due to eye contact, wishes to optimize eye contact, and intervention to reduce symptoms in ASD arising from eye contact.

Subtheme 1 – Problems due to eye contact

In their opinion, almost all of the participants with ASD have not experienced any significant problems with their eye contact, although they have been told by others that they are difficult to read or that others experience a penetrating gaze from them. One participant with ASD indicated that he cannot have an office job because eye contact is expected, which the participant cannot meet.

Participant with ASD (11) – “I’ve never worked full-time in an office. While I was studying, you only have a few hours of contact hours, but those are not really contact hours, because you are in a large lecture hall with people. I also consciously choose to do that and I certainly will not do that with my future job, not choose a life where I have to have eye contact with people every day, because it takes so much extra energy that I would rather put into actually doing my work.”

Participant with ASD (11) – “I’ve never worked full-time in an office. While I was studying, you only have a few hours of contact hours, but those are not really contact hours, because you are in a large lecture hall with people. I also consciously choose to do that and I certainly will not do that with my future job, not choose a life where I have to have eye contact with people every day, because it takes so much extra energy that I would rather put into actually doing my work.”

1 participant without ASD also indicated that he had been told others have found him difficult to read. Nonetheless, all other participants without ASD indicated that they have never experienced any problems with their eye contact.

Participant without ASD (15) – “No, I don’t think so. In any case, I have never received any criticism for that.”

Participant without ASD (15) – “No, I don’t think so. In any case, I have never received any criticism for that.”

Subtheme 2 – Wishes to optimize eye contact

About half of the participants with ASD were satisfied with their eye contact and had no desire to optimize it. The other participants expressed various wishes for optimization: some wanted to make eye contact more easily, some to determine for themselves how eye contact is made, and others to receive guidance on how eye contact should be made.

Participant with ASD (3) – “No, I don’t have the idea. I think it’s fine this way. I don’t think it would accomplish anything.”

Participant with ASD (3) – “No, I don’t have the idea. I think it’s fine this way. I don’t think it would accomplish anything.”

In the participants without ASD, virtually no one wished to optimize their eye contact. Most of the participants without ASD also indicated that they do not want people with ASD to adjust their eye contact to others. The participants without ASD especially wished that society would allow everyone to make eye contact in whatever manner suited them without judgement, in the spirit of diversity and inclusivity.

Participant without ASD (6) – “In some ways we are very inclusive, but we all have to meet a certain standard in this performance society. That’s actually very strange, I think. While we are all wonderfully different, and that is something very cool.”

Participant without ASD (6) – “In some ways we are very inclusive, but we all have to meet a certain standard in this performance society. That’s actually very strange, I think. While we are all wonderfully different, and that is something very cool.”

Subtheme 3 – Intervention to reduce symptoms in ASD arising from eye contact

About half of the participants with ASD indicated that they would have less difficulty with eye contact if society would allow them to make eye contact in their own way without judgement. According to these participants, this would require society to be informed about how eye contact is conducted differently among people. Other participants with ASD indicated that training in eye contact could be useful for them, and some indicated that they would benefit from psychoeducation about eye contact.

Participant with ASD (5) – “Yes, society has also dealt with shaking hands very differently due to corona. It would be so nice if something similar could be done with eye contact. That everyone decides together: everyone can think of their own way of making eye contact and we don’t make a point of that.”

Participant with ASD (5) – “Yes, society has also dealt with shaking hands very differently due to corona. It would be so nice if something similar could be done with eye contact. That everyone decides together: everyone can think of their own way of making eye contact and we don’t make a point of that.”

About half of the participants without ASD agreed with the participants with ASD who would like society to be informed that eye contact occurs differently and that people, such as those with ASD, can decide for themselves how they make eye contact without being judged for it. Additionally, the participants without ASD advocated that people with ASD who have difficulty with eye contact discuss this with others so that others can understand their difficulty and consider it.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate eye-contact views and experiences in adults with and without ASD using the MSQA method in combination with the PRICE model. Four between-site overarching themes emerged from the data: opinion on eye contact, experience of eye contact, approach toward eye contact, and needs regarding eye contact. For participants with and without ASD, eye contact could be defined as follows: interaction partners looking at each other or interaction partners contacting each other through their eyes. Eye contact was seen as a way of connecting that provides a deeper layer than the common definition of eye contact as a direct gaze exchanged between two people during an interaction [58]. However, both groups had different views on the phenomenon of eye contact. Generally, the participants with ASD found eye contact less useful, didn’t not consider eye contact as an added value, and preferred other body parts for nonverbal communication. Our results are consistent with research showing that people with ASD rely primarily on information from the mouth [59] and with the hypo-arousal/social motivation theory [35, 36], which suggests that people with ASD do not prioritize social stimuli such as the eyes. The participants without ASD believed that eye contact is valuable, partly because of the extra information it provides about the other person and the interaction. This is consistent with previous research in adults without ASD that found eye contact has an important role in establishing meaningful rapport [60]. Participants with and without ASD also thought differently about the function of eye contact. The participants with ASD considered eye contact as a tool to make a statement, while those without ASD believed eye contact is a means of demonstrating mutual connection. The latter is consistent with earlier research showing that, for people without ASD, eye contact leads to greater empathy [61], more involvement [4], and affection [62, 63]. Participants with and without ASD had a similar view on atypical eye contact. Both had experienced atypical eye contact, which was too long or too short, although neither could indicate when eye contact was an inappropriate duration. The fact that both groups could not indicate the duration of eye contact in this situation is consistent with previous research [14] describing how there appears to be no gold standard for eye contact and that variations are influenced by a variety of factors. Most of the participants with ASD were convinced that there is such a thing as autistic eye contact, and they provided different views on how eye contact in those with ASD differs from those without ASD, such as making less eye contact and having no understanding of eye contact. This outcome is consistent with research showing that people with ASD experience difficulties in reading information from the eyes [39, 64]. In contrast, the participants without ASD were more hesitant about whether there is such a thing as autistic eye contact.

A significant difference between participants with and without ASD was that the participants with ASD had experienced eye contact consciously for as long as they could remember, and the participants without ASD had always done this unconsciously. Research by Garvey et al. [40] also shows that eye contact in ASD is often a conscious process. Regarding experiences of eye contact across a lifespan, the participants with ASD were reminded in their youth to make eye contact more often than the participants without ASD. This was because the former looked away too much due to experienced intrusion or, for example, did not realize that they had to look at the other person. The uneasy feeling of being looked at is also described in research by Trevisan et al. [39], which reports that people with ASD relate this to violation, fear of conveying private information, and intimacy. In their youth, the eye contact of the participants without ASD was not much corrected. Rather, these participants were taught that eye contact is a sign of decency, in addition to other social rules. In adulthood, the participants with ASD also experienced eye contact differently than the participants without ASD: mainly negatively. The participants with ASD felt that eyes speak the truth and that the other person penetrated them during eye contact. Consequently, their vulnerabilities, including their ASD, could be seen, and they were assessed accordingly. This is consistent with the hyperarousal/gaze-aversion theory [34, 65], where eyes are seen as a negative valence. This contrasts with the participants without ASD, who mainly experience eye contact as adding value to interactions. Some also experienced eye contact as enabling others to see who they are and what they think and feel inside, but they had no problem with this. Furthermore, the participants with ASD experienced eye contact as more pleasant with familiar people than with strangers. This is because with familiar people, the meaning of eye contact was clearer, and familiar people gave them more freedom in how they wanted to make eye contact. Research by Garvey et al. [40] also describes how people with ASD experience eye contact with familiar people as more comfortable than with strangers. The participants without ASD generally found eye contact with familiar people more enjoyable than with strangers, although more of the participants without ASD also enjoyed eye contact with strangers because eye contact could then contribute to strengthening the relationship. This has been demonstrated in studies by, for example, Burgoon et al. [4], MacDonald [60], and Söderberg et al. [66].

Another significant difference between participants with and without ASD was that the participants with ASD had learned eye contact rationally by observing and copying others or by being given tips. Research by Garvey et al. [40] also describes how people with ASD became aware of their eye contact through feedback or through the process of increased awareness. This contrasts with the participants without ASD, where eye contact, in their opinion, had developed in a natural and unconscious manner. Although the participants with ASD had practiced eye contact since childhood, this has not made it easy for them today. In contrast, most of the participants with ASD still didn’t not know how to handle eye contact. They generally found it difficult to understand how their own eye contact appeared to others, what the social rules for eye contact were, and what meaning to attribute to the eye use of others. This is consistent with previous studies showing that people with ASD demonstrate significant impairment in recognizing complex mental states from the eyes [67, 68] and with the mindblindness theory, which assumes that people with ASD have difficulty understanding the mental states of others [69]. The participants with ASD nevertheless wanted to prevent the other person from seeing these struggles, and thus possible signs of ASD. Consequently, they attempted to camouflage their struggles by copying the other person’s eye contact. The camouflage used by autistic adults during social interactions has been confirmed in previous research, such as Berardin et al. [70], Cook et al. [71], Garvey et al. [40], and Van der Putten et al. [72]. The participants without ASD had hardly thought about eye contact in their lives. They had experienced no difficulty in understanding how it should operate, and they had no plan for how to manage it. They sensed what others mean by eye contact, could read from other people’s eyes how that person is feeling mentally, and sensed how they were perceived by others. This is consistent with research by Schmidtmann et al. [73], which shows that people without ASD can recognize very subtle differences of mental states when judging the eye region.

Although literature, such as Howlin and Yates [33], describes how eye contact in ASD leads to a variety of problems, the participants with ASD indicated that they had not encountered any significant issues during their lives. They were satisfied with their eye contact and mainly had the idea that their eye contact was a problem for the other person because that person would think that eye contact should be conducted in a certain way based on social norms. As the participants with ASD regularly deviated from these expectations, they understanded that this could feel uncomfortable for the interaction partner. The participants with ASD wished that everyone would be free to decide how they use eye contact without being judged. For them, this would require providing society with information about how eye contact is used differently and for society to understand those differences. Additionally, some of the participants with ASD would like guidance on how to handle eye contact. This finding relates to the research of Dalton et al. [74], Kylliäinen & Hietanen [75] and Kliemann et al. [76] in which it has been found that lower exposure to eye contact during critical developmental periods influences reactivity to gaze later in life. This influence on arousal could partly explain why some adults with ASD have the desire for receiving guidance in managing it. The finding also relates to studies, such as those by Garvey et al. [40] and Trevisan et al. [39], in which is reported that adults with ASD feel pressure to comply with social norms regarding eye contact and would like to have more freedom to make eye contact in their own way. The participants without ASD had not encountered any significant problems in their lives because of eye contact and felt no need to change their own eye contact. Nonetheless, these participants did have wishes regarding eye contact in people with ASD. Just like the participants with ASD, they argued that society should be more open-minded about eye contact in the context of diversity and inclusivity. They believed this could be achieved by educating society on how eye contact is conducted differently. In their opinion, this could also be achieved by talking to people with ASD about eye contact to facilitate greater understanding and acceptance.

The current research has strengths and limitations. A strength is that the interviews were conducted by a researcher with expertise in ASD, which made it possible to fully understand the opinions and experiences of the participants. Furthermore, with the limited amount of qualitative research in the area of ASD, this study provides greater representativeness and generalizability than comparable studies to date due to its sample size, use of various demographic characteristics of participants, and multi-site approach. Another strength is that the participants had a level of self-insight, leading to a high degree of depth and complexity in the assessment.

The limitations include that many adults with ASD have been provided with information about eye contact in their lives, which may have influenced their answers during the interviews. Conversely, the answers of the participants without ASD were only briefly considered because they had never thought about the topic of eye contact until the interview and therefore had to formulate answers on the spot. Another limitation is that children and adults with low intelligence were not included, whereas our group of autistic individuals may have been enriched for mental health co-morbidity; both issues may reduce the representability of this study for the full population of autistic people. Furthermore, we used educational attainment as a proxy for cognitive function, whereas the correlation between both variables may be less evident in autistic individuals [77]. Further, as we did not assess autistic symptoms in the control group, we cannot exclude that (some of the) controls did have undiagnosed or sub threshold autism themselves; the study may have attracted individuals who were concerned about having autism themselves, with the risk of volunteer bias.

Previous research has shown that several demographic factors influence how eye contact is made and experienced. Future research could therefore explore the extent to which these factors influence the eye-contact experiences of people with and without ASD. Another consideration for future research is to investigate the experience of eye contact in people with low intelligence and other psychiatric disorders as there is evidence that peculiarities of eye contact also occur in these groups. Additionally, the results provide guidance for the future development of interventions to reduce problems arising from eye contact in ASD.
