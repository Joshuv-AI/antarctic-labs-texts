METS-IR and SII as mediators in the association between smoking and depressive symptoms: insights from NHANES (2005–2018)
Authors: Yanyu Zhou, Jinbiao Zhuang, Qitao Bian, Zhiqi Xu, Yujun Chen, Heng Yang, Gongxian Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepression is associated with smoking and inflammation. However, whether inflammation and metabolic insulin resistance mediate the relationship between smoking and depression remains unclear.MethodsWe analyzed 15,391 participants from the 2005–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), representing approximately 92,321,194 individuals in the USA. Data on depressive symptoms (assessed by the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 [PHQ-9]), smoking (assessed via smoking questionnaire), and Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII) and metabolic insulin resistance score (METS-IR) were evaluated. Additionally, the effect of smoking on all-cause mortality among individuals with depressive symptoms was assessed.ResultsWeighted logistic regression analysis showed that current smoking was significantly associated with a higher risk of depressive symptoms (OR = 3.02, 95% CI: 2.46–3.69, P < 0.001), with consistent findings across subgroups. Weighted generalized linear regression indicated that smoking upregulates SII and METS-IR levels. Specifically, current smokers had SII and METS-IR levels 86.1 (95% CI: 67.5–104.8) and 0.01 (95% CI: 0.005–0.02) units higher than never-smokers, respectively. Restricted cubic spline models demonstrated nonlinear dose-response relationships between SII, METS-IR, and depressive symptom severity (P for nonlinear < 0.05). SII and METS-IR mediated 0.69% and 0.86% of the relationship between smoking and depressive symptoms, respectively. Lastly, smoking appeared to increase all-cause mortality in individuals with depressive symptoms.ConclusionsThe positive association between smoking and depressive symptoms is partly mediated by SII and METS-IR. Smoking cessation may alleviate depressive symptoms and improve survival in individuals with depressive symptoms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07114-6.Keywords: Depressive symptoms, Smoking, METS-IR, SII, Mediation analysis

Introduction

Smoking and depression are highly co-occurring phenomena [1, 2]. Studies have shown that people with depression exhibit higher rates of lifetime smoking and current smoking, which encompasses both active smoking and passive exposure to secondhand smoke. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) [3], globally, more than 8 million people die prematurely each year due to active smoking or secondhand smoke exposure. Additionally, over 300 million people suffer from depression worldwide, with its prevalence increasing annually, placing an enormous burden on the global healthcare system. Some large-scale cross-sectional studies have shown that tobacco exposure is closely related to a high risk of depression [4, 5].

The etiology of depression is the result of multifactorial interactions, including biological, psychological, and socio-environmental aspects, with chronic inflammation and immune system heterogeneity recognized as contributing factors in the pathological processes underlying depression. On one hand, smoking has been shown to exacerbate mood disorders by disrupting the function of key brain neurotransmitters, such as 5-hydroxytryptamine and dopamine [6]. On the other hand, smoking contributes to the development of depression by inducing chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, which serve as a pathological basis for mood disorders and further exacerbate their severity [7].

Although the association between smoking and depression has been extensively studied, the mediating mechanisms underlying this relationship remain poorly understood. Research has shown that smoking elevates inflammation levels, induces insulin resistance, and disrupts metabolic homeostasis, which may collectively contribute to the pathogenesis of depression [7]. This connection may serve as an important bridge linking smoking with depression through the systemic immune- inflammation index (SII) and the metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR), which are widely regarded as reliable indicators of inflammation levels and the degree of insulin resistance, respectively [8, 9]. In addition, the mediating effects of these factors in the association between smoking and depression remain unclear and warrant further investigation. Given that the smoking rate among patients with depression is significantly higher than that of the general population, and many patients often resort to smoking to relieve stress and anxiety, this seemingly “self-treatment” behavior may actually bring more serious health risks. Smoking behavior not only directly affects the physical health of patients with depression but may also impact their mental health, thereby increasing the risk of death.

Therefore, utilizing a large, nationally representative sample from the 2005–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), this study aims to explore the following: the relationship between smoking, depressive symptoms, and mediating factors (SII and METS-IR); the mediating effects of SII and METS-IR on the association between smoking and depression; and, finally, to investigate the impact of smoking on survival outcomes in populations with depressive symptoms.

Methods

Study population

NHANES is designed to assess the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the United States. The survey employs a multistage probability sampling methodology and combines questionnaires, physical examinations, and laboratory tests to obtain a nationally representative sample. The NHANES protocol was reviewed and approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board, with approval obtained semiannually between full proposal reviews. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participants were screened for inclusion based on the following eligibility criteria: (1) inclusion of 42,143 participants aged ≥ 18 years from 2005 to 2018; (2) exclusion of individuals with missing values in the inflammation indicator SII (n = 3,842); (3) exclusion of those with incomplete responses to the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) (n = 3,553); (4) exclusion of individuals with missing values for METS-IR (n = 18,749); (5) exclusion of participants with missing smoking data (n = 608). After applying these criteria, a final sample of 15,391 survey respondents was included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of sample selection process

Depressive symptoms

The 9-item PHQ-9 was used to assess depressive symptoms. The PHQ-9 is widely recognized as a valid and reliable instrument for screening and diagnosing depression in diverse populations. The total PHQ-9 score ranges from 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating greater severity of depressive symptoms. When compared with clinical interview-based diagnostic criteria, a PHQ-9 score of ≥ 10 demonstrates a sensitivity of 85% and a specificity of 89% for identifying major depressive disorder (MDD) [10, 11].

METS-IR and SII

To measure insulin resistance, this study utilized the METS-IR, which has been demonstrated as an effective alternative to traditional methods. The formula for METS-IR is as follows: METS-IR = Ln[(2 × fasting glucose (mg/dL) + fasting triglycerides (mg/dL)) × body mass index (kg/m²)]/[Ln(high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (mg/dL))]. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m²) [9]. The level of inflammation was assessed using the SII, which is calculated using the formula: SII = (P × N)/L, where P, N, and L represent the blood cell counts of peripheral platelets, neutrophils, and lymphocytes, respectively [12].

Smoking

Smoking data was obtained from two questions within the SMQ_D questionnaire folder: SMQ020 (“These next questions are about cigarette smoking and other tobacco use. (Have you/Has SP) smoked at least 100 cigarettes in (your/his/her) entire life?“) and SMQ040 (“(Do you/Does SP) now smoke cigarettes?“). The variable for smoking status was categorized into three groups: ‘non-smoking’,'former smoking’ and ‘current smoking’. Participants who answered ‘No’ to the first question (SMQ020) were defined as non-smokers. Participants who answered ‘Yes’ to SMQ020 and ‘Not at all’ to SMQ040 were categorized as former smokers. Conversely, those who answered ‘Yes’ to SMQ020 and either ‘Every day’ or ‘Some days’ to SMQ040 were classified as current smokers.

Survival data

The primary data include survival time and status. This study utilized the NHANES public mortality data file up to December 31, 2019, which was linked to the National Death Index (NDI) using probabilistic matching to determine the mortality status of the follow-up population. All-cause mortality was defined as death from any cause; survival time was defined as the number of months from the interview date to the date of death, or for individuals who did not experience the event, the number of months from the interview date to December 31, 2019, which was considered the follow-up period. The survival time of individuals who were lost to follow-up was treated as “censored data,” with the default assumption being that they survived until the last follow-up time.

Covariates

This study selected several covariates for inclusion in the analysis based on established or observed plausible and relevant biological relationships, and in reference to the relevant literature [13–17]. Covariates included age, gender, race (Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Other Race), education level (< high school, completed high school, > high school), the family poverty income ratio (poverty: PIR < 1; non-poverty: PIR ≥ 1), marital status (married/living with a partner, never married/widowed/divorced/separated), alcohol status (yes/no), physical activity (inactive, moderate, vigorous, or both moderate and vigorous), hypertension (yes/no), diabetes (yes/no), and comorbid conditions (yes/no). Alcohol status was determined using data from two 24-hour dietary recalls. Participants were classified as alcohol users if they reported alcohol consumption in at least one of the 24-hour dietary recalls. A person is considered to have hypertension if any of the following conditions is met: a survey questionnaire asks: “Has a doctor ever told you that you have hypertension?” If the answer is “yes,” the person is considered to have hypertension; Systolic and diastolic blood pressures are measured in a resting state for four consecutive days. If the systolic blood pressure is greater than 140 mmHg or the diastolic blood pressure is greater than 90 mmHg on any of these days, the person is considered to have hypertension; The person is currently undergoing antihypertensive drug therapy. A person is considered to have diabetes if any of the following conditions is met: a survey questionnaire asks: “Has a doctor ever told you that you have diabetes?” If the answer is “yes,” the person is considered to have diabetes; The person is currently undergoing treatment with insulin or hypoglycemic drugs; Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level is ≥ 6.5%; Fasting blood glucose level is ≥ 126 mg/dL; 2-hour postprandial blood glucose level is ≥ 200 mg/dL. Participants were classified as having comorbid conditions if they reported at least one of the following medical conditions: kidney failure, kidney stones, heart failure, stroke, hepatopathy, or cancer. To handle missing data, the missForest R package was used. This random forest-based imputation technique is highly computationally efficient for high-dimensional datasets, accommodating both categorical and continuous predictors [18].

Statistical analysis

The samples from NHANES were obtained through a complex, multi-stage probability sampling design, and each individual was assigned a unique sample weight. In our data analyses, both WTSAF2YR and WTMEC2YR sample weights were considered, selecting the smaller value when both were available. For the baseline characteristics of the study population, continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were presented as number (percent). The independent t-test and chi-square test were performed to compare baseline characteristics, assessing differences in continuous and categorical variables, respectively, between participants with and without depressive symptoms.

This study employed weighted multiple logistic regression to analyze the relationship between smoking and depressive symptoms. METS-IR and SII were divided into four groups based on quartiles. Confounding factors were adjusted in the regression models as follows: Model 1 was an unadjusted model; Model 2 adjusted for age, gender, race, and marital status; and Model 3 adjusted for all covariates, including age, gender, race, marital status, BMI, education level, PIR, alcohol status, physical activity, hypertension, diabetes and comorbid. Subsequently, we selected a cutoff value of PHQ-9 total score > 4 and conducted logistic regression analysis again. The results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs).

Weighted linear regression was then used to explore the relationships between METS-IR, SII, and smoking, with METS-IR and SII treated as continuous variables. The regression models included Model 1 (unadjusted) and Model 2 (adjusted for all covariates). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were applied to investigate the potential dose-response relationships between METS-IR, SII, and the severity of depressive symptoms, with the median value used as the reference. In these models, METS-IR and SII were treated as continuous variables, and depressive symptom was assessed using the PHQ-9 total score. Additionally, we conducted a two-stage linear regression analysis by identifying the inflection points of the curves (either the lowest points or the points where the slope changes most significantly). Five knots were selected to capture the potential non-linear relationship (5%, 27.5%, 50%, 72.5%, 95%).

To determine whether METS-IR (M1) or SII (M2) mediated the effect of smoking (X) on depressive symptoms (Y), a mediation analysis was conducted under the prerequisites of a “statistically significant association between X and M” and a “statistically significant association between M and Y.” The mediation analysis utilized 1,000 bootstrap samples to ensure robust estimates of indirect effects. Additionally, this study excluded individuals using antidepressants, anti-infective drugs, and immunomodulators for sensitivity analysis. Finally, we conducted supplementary analyses examining the mediating effects of METS-IR and SII when treated as categorical variables.

Finally, we analyzed the impact of smoking on all-cause mortality risk among individuals with depressive symptoms using the Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and Cox proportional hazards regression model. In addition, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by excluding participants with comorbid conditions.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 15,391 participants were recruited for this study, representing approximately 92,321,194 individuals in the USA, and Table 1 presented the baseline characteristics of the participants. The population was categorized into two groups: Non-depressive symptoms and Depressive symptoms. The mean age of all participants was 49.2 years, with 48.8% being male. The ratio of participants with depressive symptoms compared to those without depressive symptoms was approximately 11:1. Among the total population, 20.0% were current smokers, 24.5% were former smokers, and 55.4% were never smokers. There were also statistically significant differences (P < 0.001) between the two groups in terms of education level, PIR, race, marital status, alcohol consumption, smoking status, physical activity, BMI, comorbidities, diabetes, and hypertension. However, no significant differences were observed between the two groups for age and physical activity (P > 0.05).

In the subgroup analysis, the interaction P-values for all subgroups were greater than 0.05, suggesting that the results were stable across different populations. The results indicate that smoking is significantly associated with depressive symptoms in all models. In Model 3, the OR is 2.21 (95% CI: 1.95, 2.51, P < 0.001).

The relationship between smoking and the indicators METS-IR and SII

The results of the weighted generalized linear regression (Table 3), adjusting for all relevant covariates, showed a significant positive association between smoking and SII. Specifically, for former smokers, the β coefficient was 31.3 (95% CI: 14.9, 47.6; P < 0.001), and for current smokers, the β coefficient was 86.1 (95% CI: 67.5, 104.8; P < 0.001). Similar findings were observed for METS-IR, with a β coefficient of 0.02 (95% CI: 0.01, 0.03; P < 0.001) for former smokers and 0.01 (95% CI: 0.005, 0.02; P < 0.001) for current smokers. The trend tests for all models were less than 0.05, indicating that current smokers had higher SII levels compared to past smokers, while their METS-IR levels were relatively lower.

Nonlinear association between METS-IR and SII and depressive symptoms

The METS-IR and SII were found to exhibit nonlinear relationships with the severity of depression symptoms (Fig. 2), where a higher PHQ-9 total score indicates more severe depression. For both METS-IR and SII, the overall association was statistically significant (P overall < 0.01) with evidence of nonlinearity (P for nonlinearity < 0.05). The inflection point for METS-IR was determined to be 2.0. When METS-IR ≤ 2.0 (Table 4), the severity of depression symptoms was positively correlated with METS-IR (β = 1.53 (1.46,1.61), P < 0.001). However, when METS-IR > 2.0, the association between METS-IR and the severity of depression symptoms weakened (β = 0.14 (0.13, 0.142), P < 0.001), as the slope became flatter, indicating a potential plateau effect in the relationship. Similarly, the inflection point for SII was 330.7. When SII ≤ 330.7, the two variables were negatively correlated, whereas when SII > 330.7, they were positively correlated. This pattern reflects an overall “U-shaped” association between SII and depression severity.

Mediating effect of METS-IR and SII on smoking and depressive symptoms

According to the above analysis, the prerequisites for the mediation analysis were met. Although the mediating variable, when treated as a categorical variable, was not significantly associated with depressive symptoms in the logistic regression analysis, the RCS curves indicated a correlation between the mediating variable and the severity of depressive symptoms. After adjusting for all covariates, mediating effects were observed for both SII and METS-IR (Fig. 3). Specifically, SII mediated 0.69% of the total effect (indirect effect = 5.15 × 10⁻⁴, P = 0.028; direct effect = 7.38 × 10⁻², P < 0.001), while METS-IR mediated 0.86% of the total effect (indirect effect = 6.34 × 10⁻⁴, P = 0.002; direct effect = 7.34 × 10⁻², P < 0.001). The mediation analysis with METS-IR and SII as categorical variables is presented in Figure S2. The mediation effects of METS-IR and SII account for 1.3% and 1.7%, respectively. Finally, after excluding individuals who had used antidepressants, anti-infective drugs, and immunomodulatory agents, this study conducted the mediation analysis again, and the results are shown in Figure S3. The mediation effects of METS-IR and SII accounted for 0.63% and 0.67%, respectively.

The impact of smoking on all-cause mortality in individuals with depression

The follow-up data for these populations were extracted from the NHANES database. After applying the final screening criteria, a total of 1,297 individuals with depressive symptoms and complete follow-up data were included in the analysis (Fig. S4). From the KM survival curves, it was observed that current smokers exhibited a significantly lower survival rate compared to non-smokers with depression (P = 0.0209), while no significant difference in survival was found between former smokers and non-smokers (P = 0.2166). After adjusting for all covariates, similar results were observed: current smokers demonstrated a higher risk of all-cause mortality compared to never smokers with depression (HR = 2.42, 95% CI: 1.62–3.61; P < 0.001) (Fig. S5a). Finally, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding individuals with comorbidities, and the results were presented in Fig. S5b.

Discussion

The current study observed a close association between smoking and severe depressive symptoms, with SII and METS-IR potentially playing a minor mediating role in this relationship. Furthermore, it was found that current smokers are at a substantially higher risk of depressive symptoms compared to past smokers. Importantly, In the MDD population (PHQ-9 total score ≥ 10), current smokers exhibited a higher risk of all-cause mortality compared to never smokers and former smokers.

As mentioned previously, TSE includes both active and passive inhalation of tobacco smoke, and previous studies have assessed the degree of TSE using blood cotinine levels [4, 5] which found that the severity of depressive symptoms was positively correlated with blood cotinine levels and that this relationship could be mediated by the SII. In the present study, we aimed to specifically investigate the effect of active smoking on the development of depressive symptoms. Additionally, building on previous research, we analyzed whether the METS-IR, a surrogate indicator of insulin resistance, mediated the development of depressive symptoms along with the SII, an indicator reflecting the level of inflammation. The study results indicate that, although the mediating effect is statistically significant, its proportion is relatively low. First, the cross-sectional design makes it challenging to capture the long-term cumulative effects of inflammation. The impact of smoking on the body may take several years to become evident through this pathway. Second, the inflammatory and metabolic pathways likely need to interact with other pathways to produce significant effects. This mediation analysis is based on traditional statistical models, and analyzing any single pathway in isolation may reveal only a relatively small contribution. This lower proportion can be regarded as the lower limit of the pathway’s contribution. Despite the low mediating proportion shown in the study, it still holds some guiding value for the prevention of depression. For smokers, regularly monitoring insulin resistance and inflammation levels can transform these subtle effects into early warning signals. The extent to which treatments targeting inflammation and insulin resistance can alleviate depressive symptoms still needs to be further verified through clinical practice.

The results of this study show that compared to former smokers, current smokers have higher risks of depression, as well as higher levels of SII, which may be attributed to several factors. First, previous research has indicated that the blood cotinine levels of current smokers are significantly higher than those of former smokers and never smokers, reflecting a higher degree of TSE among current smokers [19]. Second, former smokers and never smokers are primarily affected by exposure to secondhand smoke, which includes sidestream smoke produced by burning cigarettes and mainstream smoke exhaled by smokers. Compared to the mainstream smoke inhaled by active smokers, sidestream smoke contains higher concentrations of fine inhalable particles and toxic gases [20, 21]. Studies have shown that short-term exposure to secondhand smoke can rapidly increase inflammatory levels in the body, potentially reaching levels similar to those of active smokers [22, 23]. Other research has also found that despite lower blood cotinine concentrations, non-smokers exposed to high levels of secondhand smoke exhibit levels of most cardiovascular risk biomarkers similar to those of smokers [19]. However, this seems inconsistent with the findings of this study. This discrepancy may be due to the limitations of the NHANES database, which lacks data on the smoking frequency of smokers, the extent of secondhand smoke exposure among former and never smokers, and the duration of smoking cessation. These missing data limit our ability to further analyze these factors.

Smoking and depression are highly comorbid, which suggests a complex and multifactorial relationship. This association may arise from multiple mechanisms. At the biological level, nicotine causes lasting changes in cholinergic and monoaminergic systems [24, 25]. Additionally, smoking can alter the regulation of the stress response system, both within and outside the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. This dysregulation can heighten smokers’ sensitivity to external stressors, which may, in turn, contribute to the development or exacerbation of depressive symptoms [26]. Therefore, smoking and depression are mutually influential and may create a vicious cycle, further exacerbating the psychological burden on individuals with depression. Furthermore, prolonged exposure to tobacco has been shown to elevate inflammation levels and release carcinogens and oxidants, which can induce damage through inflammatory signaling pathways, ultimately contributing to tissue damage and disease progression [27, 28]. Beyond these biological mechanisms, psychosocial factors also play a significant role. For instance, people suffering from depressive symptoms may actively smoke as a coping mechanism to alleviate psychological distress [24]. Evidence from other studies also suggests that smoking contributes to depression. For instance, quitting smoking has been shown to alleviate symptoms of depression and anxiety [29]. Besides active smoking, exposure to secondhand smoke poses significant risks to both physical and mental health. Evidence from a study examining the impact of secondhand smoke on adolescent mental health suggests that such exposure may significantly increase the risk of stress, depression, and suicidal behavior in adolescents [30]. What’s more, exposure to secondhand smoke has been shown to significantly elevate stress levels in both workplace and domestic environments, affecting both smokers and non-smokers alike [31]. The results of the current study also indicated that current smokers were more affected by smoking compared to those who had quit. This may be attributed to the fact that smoking does not significantly alter the body’s physiological status in the short term.

This study demonstrates a significant association between smoking and depression. However, due to the cross-sectional nature of the NHANES study, we cannot completely rule out the possibility of reverse causality. Specifically, depressed individuals may exhibit increased smoking behavior due to: (1) using smoking to alleviate psychological stress; (2) impaired self-regulation capacity making smoking cessation more difficult; and (3) depression-related unhealthy lifestyles potentially leading to metabolic disorders (e.g., diabetes, hypertension) that affect METS-IR and SII levels. If such reverse causality exists, the current findings may primarily reflect the promoting effect of depressive symptoms on smoking behavior. To establish causal relationships, future longitudinal cohort studies are needed to accurately determine the temporal sequence among smoking (exposure), METS-IR/SII (mediators), and depressive symptoms (outcome).

Inflammation is strongly associated with the development of depression, and SII serves not only as an important mediator but also as an independent risk factor for the onset and progression of depression [4, 8]. At the molecular level, the level of neutrophil-related genes is upregulated in depressed patients [32], which further illustrates the important influence of SII on depression. Furthermore, insulin resistance may also serve as an indicator of the body’s capacity to regulate and combat inflammation [33, 34]. Patients with insulin resistance may impair the anti-inflammatory effects of insulin, leading to an increase in inflammatory markers in the blood, such as TNF-alpha, interleukin-6 (IL-6), and CRP. These inflammatory factors can disrupt neurotransmitter function in the brain and contribute to neuroinflammation. Given the understanding of these biological processes, it is also important to consider the broader systemic effects. Persistent inflammation may result in endothelial dysfunction, which is associated with a variety of disorders, including cardiovascular disease and metabolic abnormalities [35, 36], such as vascular depression and dementia. In addition to METS-IR, several other indicators, such as TyG and TyG-BMI, can reflect the degree of insulin resistance. A large-scale cross-sectional study examining the association between TyG and depression demonstrated that TyG exhibits a significant linear dose-response relationship with depression [37]. Certain studies have indirectly demonstrated the impact of inflammation on depressive symptoms [4, 38, 39]. Lower dietary inflammation, as measured by the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII), mitigates the effects of tobacco exposure on depression. Its role as a representative marker of dietary inflammatory status highlights the contribution of inflammation to the development of depression. In summary, smoking promotes the onset and progression of depression by elevating systemic inflammation levels, disrupting metabolic processes, and facilitating the transfer of inflammatory factors from the periphery to the central nervous system via the blood-brain barrier.

This study, employing a nonlinear model analysis, has uncovered a significant U-shaped relationship between SII and the depressive symptoms. Notably, when SII levels are excessively low, depressive symptoms tend to worsen. This phenomenon may be attributed to several factors: First, platelets are not only clotting factors but also crucial carriers of neurotransmitters such as 5-HT. Low platelet levels reduce the transport of 5-HT to the brain, affecting synaptic plasticity, thereby exacerbating depressive symptoms [40–42]. Therefore, the data show a correlation between decreased SII and depression. Second, certain diseases, like aplastic anemia and myelodysplastic syndromes [43, 44], can lower SII levels and may themselves induce depressive symptoms in patients, resulting in a statistically observable correlation between SII and depression. Additionally, anti-inflammatory drugs, which have shown promising efficacy as adjunctive treatments for depression [45], and certain antidepressants with anti-inflammatory properties, such as fluoxetine and mirtazapine, may influence SII levels [46]. Regarding METS-IR, the relationship with depressive symptoms plateaus once it exceeds a certain threshold. This may be due to the following reasons: First, when METS-IR reaches a certain level, the body triggers compensatory secretion of adiponectin, which activates the AMPK signaling pathway to counteract the adverse effects of insulin resistance on the nervous system. Studies have shown that adiponectin levels are negatively correlated with depression risk. Second, there is a nonlinear association between the severity of insulin resistance and blood-brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction [47]. In the early stages of metabolic disorder, BBB permeability significantly increases, allowing neurotoxic substances such as free fatty acids and interleukin-6 (IL-6) to enter the central nervous system [48]. However, when metabolic damage reaches a critical threshold, BBB permeability decreases, likely due to astrocyte remodeling and the activation of antioxidant pathways [47]. In summary, the body employs various compensatory mechanisms to mitigate the impact of insulin resistance on depressive symptoms, leading to the observed plateau.

Subgroup analyses in the current study indicate that this association remains consistent across diverse population groups. Furthermore, the all-cause mortality rate among individuals with depression who smoke was found to be more than twice as high as that of non-smokers. This elevated mortality risk may be attributed to smoking’s contribution not only to depression but also to other severe conditions, including stroke, kidney failure, and cardiovascular disease [49, 50]. Even though individuals with these disorders were excluded from the sensitivity analyses, the all-cause mortality rate among smokers was still more than three times higher. A plausible explanation is that smoking may increase levels of corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), both of which are closely linked to mood regulation and behavioral change [51, 52]. It also causes chronic alterations in heart rate, blood pressure, and respiratory rate, which exacerbate depressive symptoms and subsequently increase the risk of death [53]. Although smoking may provide temporary relief from anxiety, quitting is ultimately the better long-term strategy. For individuals with depressive symptoms, a scientifically informed approach to smoking cessation, combined with support in adopting a healthier lifestyle, can significantly enhance both their physical and mental health, ultimately leading to an improved overall quality of life. Based on the existing evidence, we speculate that the impact of smoking on health and mortality risk may be realized through two pathways. On the one hand, smoking directly exerts a negative influence on physical health by increasing the risk of comorbidities. On the other hand, smoking may indirectly increase mortality risk by exacerbating depressive symptoms and reducing treatment adherence. While this study has explored the associations among smoking, the mediating substances, and depressive symptoms, the mediating effect of depressive symptoms on the relationship between smoking behavior and mortality risk remains to be further verified.

Based on the findings of this study, we recommend the monitoring of METS-IR and SII during smoking cessation interventions to optimize intervention outcomes. For example, in smoking cessation clinics, participants’ METS-IR and SII levels could be regularly assessed (e.g., at baseline, 3 months, and 6 months) and combined with psychological evaluations (such as the PHQ-9 scale) to analyze their associations with depressive symptoms. This approach not only helps better manage smoking cessation interventions in clinical practice but also enables longitudinal cohort studies to clarify whether changes in METS-IR and SII following smoking cessation mediate improvements in depressive symptoms.

The current study has several strengths. First, the NHANES database was utilized, and sample weights were incorporated to ensure that the findings are nationally representative. Second, missing covariates were imputed, which significantly reduced errors caused by missing data. Lastly, a detailed comparison of risk differences between current smokers and those who had quit smoking was performed, providing a deeper analytical perspective. However, the study also has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes the ability to draw causal conclusions and there may be unadjusted confounding factors, such as unmeasured smoking-related details (e.g., smoking frequency, duration of cessation), degree of secondhand smoke exposure, occupational stress, family stress, material resource conditions, and social relationships. Second, both smoking behavior and PHQ-9 scores were self-reported via questionnaires, potentially introducing information bias. Finally, given the “U-shaped” and “plateau” relationships observed in the RCS curves, which may affect the results obtained from mediation analysis based on linear assumptions. In addition, given the relatively small proportion of the mediation effect, its clinical significance should be interpreted with caution. Future research is needed to validate the extent to which these two pathways can improve depressive symptoms.

Conclusions

This study suggests an association between smoking and more severe depressive symptoms, with slightly potential mediation through SII and METS-IR pathways, which may hold potential for novel therapeutic approaches in the treatment of depression. In addition, current smokers, compared to former smokers, show a closer correlation with depression and higher blood SII levels. This research underscores the importance of encouraging smoking cessation as part of the management strategy for patients with depression. Timely cessation may alleviate symptoms and improve survival in depressed populations, offering significant health benefits. Lastly, future cohort studies should focus on the effects of dynamic changes in blood SII and insulin resistance within this pathway.
