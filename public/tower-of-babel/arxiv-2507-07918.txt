Strong time-periodic solutions to a multilayered fluid-structure interaction problem
Felix Brandt, Claudiu Mîndrilă, Arnab Roy

Abstract
. This article studies a nonlinearly coupled, time-periodic fluid-structure interaction (FSI) problem involving the Navier-Stokes equations for an incompressible, viscous fluid coupled with a multilayered elastic structure consisting of a thin damped shell and a thick viscoelastic component. The main objective is to establish the existence of a strong time-periodic solution. To address the full nonlinear problem, we employ a fixed point argument, which relies on a detailed analysis of the linearized system together with appropriate nonlinear estimates. The linearized problem is analyzed by employing the Arendt-Bu theorem on maximal periodic L p \mathrm{L}^{p} -regularity, which requires several new analytical ingredients including a careful treatment of the thick structural layer, a refined lifting procedure, a decoupling strategy to establish ℛ \mathcal{R} -sectoriality of the associated FSI operator and a careful spectral analysis. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first result proving the existence of strong time-periodic solutions for a multilayered fluid-structure interaction problem .

1.
Introduction

Fluid-structure interaction (FSI) problems with dynamic interfaces have become a prominent subject of research, driven by both theoretical challenges and practical applications.
A natural classification arises based on the position of the structure relative to the fluid:
Some models describe structures immersed in the fluid, while others consider structures attached to the boundary of the fluid domain.
Thorough reviews of analytical developments and practical applications in FSI theory can be found in the excellent surveys by Kaltenbacher et al.
[
29
]
and Čanić
[
9
]
and the references cited therein.

The present paper establishes for the first time the existence of a strong time-periodic solution to a
multilayered
FSI problem subject to time-periodic forcing terms.
In fact, we consider the nonlinearly coupled interaction problem of a viscous, incompressible Newtonian fluid with a multilayered structure consisting of a thin plate-type and a thick (visco-)elastic layer.
Here the term
nonlinear coupling
means that the fluid-structure interface is not fixed, but it is evolving in time, giving rise to a moving boundary problem.
The model will be introduced in detail in
Section 2
and can be found in (
2.17
).
The motivation for studying multilayered FSI problems is strongly rooted in biomedical applications, where many physiological structures, such as blood vessels, are composed of multiple elastic and viscoelastic layers.
We refer here for example to plaque growth in arteries, which is a key factor in atherosclerosis and whose modeling and numerical simulations can e. g. be found in
[
24
,
52
]
.

Previous studies in the context of boundary coupled FSI problems primarily focused on the analysis of the initial value (Cauchy) problem, and are mainly restricted to the situation of a single, thin structure attached to the fluid boundary.
First results on the existence of weak solutions to incompressible fluids coupled with elastic plates are due to Chambolle et al.
[
13
]
and Grandmont
[
25
]
.
The analysis was extended to models involving linear elastic
[
36
]
and nonlinear
[
49
]
Koiter shells.
A constructive approach to the existence of weak solutions to incompressible fluid-linear elastic shell systems via the Arbitrary Lagrangian-Eulerian (ALE) method was developed by Muha and Čanić
[
47
]
.
More recently, the same authors extended in
[
48
]
the scope of FSI models to incorporate multilayered structures, featuring the coupling of a thin shell with an additional thick elastic layer, and laying the groundwork for more realistic biomechanical and engineering applications.
The model from
[
48
]
is the basis for the present work.

Concerning
strong
solutions to FSI problems with elastic structures located at the fluid boundary, a first local-in-time well-posedness result for fluid-structure systems involving viscoelastic beams and small initial data was established in the foundational work
[
3
]
.
Remarkably, Grandmont and Hillairet
[
26
]
proved the global-in-time strong well-posedness of such FSI problems in a 2D fluid-1D beam setting for large data.
Strong solutions to FSI problems involving nonlinear elastic Koiter shells were studied in
[
14
,
39
]
, while approaches to FSI problems of incompressible fluids and damped (thin) structures via
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-theory have been carried out by Denk and Saal
[
18
]
and Maity and Takahashi
[
41
]
.
Let us emphasize that none of these works addresses the situation of a multilayered structure.

More recently, attention has also turned to FSI problems of greater complexity, including the case of compressible or heat-conducting fluids coupled with beams and shells, see e. g.
[
8
,
40
,
38
]
.
In
[
33
]
, Kuan, Čanić and Muha studied a multilayered FSI problem, where the thick layer is assumed to be poroelastic.

The study of
time-periodic
problems is a classical topic in mathematical fluid mechanics.
We refer here for instance to the pioneering articles by Serrin
[
55
]
, Judovič
[
27
]
and Prodi
[

as well as the works of Kozono and Nakao

On the other hand, time-periodicity plays a central role in modeling phenomena where cyclic behavior is inherent, especially in biomedical applications.
For example, the rhythmic nature of blood flow, governed by the heart’s repetitive pumping, naturally leads to models with periodic forcing.
However, despite their relevance, FSI problems in the time-periodic setting remain underexplored.
Several recent studies have taken important steps toward overcoming the associated analytical difficulties.
In the case of incompressible fluids interacting with (single) elastic structures, results on strong solutions have been obtained by Casanova
[
11
]
as well as Celik and Kyed
[
12
]
.
The existence of weak solutions to FSI problems in time-periodic settings has been rigorously investigated in
[
44
,
45
]
and
[
43

, where the latter work addresses a multilayered FSI problem.
For time-periodic solutions to FSI problems involving different fluid models, such as compressible ones, and structural configurations, we also refer to
[
28
,
51
,
32
]
.
Time-periodic weak solutions to parabolic-hyperbolic systems have also been investigated in
[
46
]
, while time-periodic fluid-
rigid body

[
6

.

In the following, we describe the strategy of the proof of the strong time-periodic result.
Due to the nonlinear coupling and the resulting time-dependent fluid domain, we employ a change of variables that acts locally in space to reduce the problem to the situation of a fixed fluid domain problem, but with terms that are non-local in time.
We also rewrite the main result in this reference configuration.

In order to tackle the full, nonlinear, time-periodic problem, we use a fixed point argument, which in turns requires a good understanding of the linearized problem combined with suitable nonlinear estimates.
Our approach relies on the
Arendt-Bu theorem

[
2
]
based on operator-valued discrete Fourier multipliers.
Roughly speaking, the latter theorem characterizes the strong solvability of the linearized
time-periodic problem
in terms of maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity of the
initial value problem
and a spectral condition on the semigroup, or, equivalently, its generator, see also
Section 4
.
For further background on operator theory and maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity, we also refer to the monographs
[
1
,
16
,
54
]
or the book chapter
[
35
]
.

With regard to the Arendt-Bu theorem, it is a major task to establish the maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity of the initial value problem.
In fact, we first show the
boundedness of the
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
-calculus
of the associated damped wave operator matrix in
Subsection 4.2
, which especially implies its maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity.

For handling the kinematic coupling conditions on equality of velocities and displacements as made precise below in (
2.7
) and (
2.8
), we then use a lifting procedure, see
Subsection 4.3
and
Subsection 4.3
.
Inspired by
[
41
]
in the case of a single thin structure, see also the works
[
42
,
21
,
7
]
in the context of fluid-rigid body interaction problems, invoking the so-called
added mass operator
, and employing the above lifting procedure, we then reformulate the linearized problem in terms of a
multilayered fluid-structure operator

A_{\mathrm{mfs}}
in
Subsection 4.4
and (
4.27
).
The strongly coupled nature of the present multilayered FSI problem leads to a highly
non-diagonal domain
.
Thus, to establish the maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity, or, equivalently, the
\mathcal{R}
-sectoriality of
A_{\mathrm{mfs}}
thanks to the celebrated result by Weis
[
59
]
, we use a decoupling approach inspired by
[
5
]
.
More precisely, building upon the aforementioned lifting procedure, we invoke a similarity transform that leads to an operator with diagonal domain, but of a more complicated shape.
Together with a suitable decomposition of the decoupled operator matrix and perturbation arguments, this yields the maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity of the multilayered fluid-structure operator
A_{\mathrm{mfs}}
in
Subsection 4.4
.

For the linear theory, it then remains to verify the invertibility of
A_{\mathrm{mfs}}
.
Due to the damping in the thick layer, the spectrum
\sigma(A_{\mathrm{mfs}})
does
not
only consist of eigenvalues, so it is insufficient to merely study the associated eigenvalue problem.
Instead, we use another decoupling argument and successively unveil the shape of the essential spectrum.
Joint with the control of the point spectrum, this leads to the invertibility of
A_{\mathrm{mfs}}
in
Subsection 4.5
, and culminates in the maximal periodic regularity, see
Theorem 4.16
.

Finally, we derive the nonlinear estimates, where a particular focal point is the handling of the terms emerging from the change of variables to the fixed domain.
Note that the nonlinear estimates required in the periodic setting differ from those typically used in initial value problems.
The fixed point argument is then completed upon combining the maximal periodic regularity with the nonlinear estimates.

Let us elaborate on the main contributions and methodological advances of this article.
This work appears to be the first to establish
the existence of strong solutions for a multilayered FSI problem
.
The presence of the thick structural layer introduces substantial analytical challenges, requiring significant extensions beyond existing FSI theory.
A key technical contribution is the bounded
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
-calculus of the operator matrix associated with the damped wave system (see
Subsection 4.2
), which, to the best of our knowledge, is new and may be of independent interest. The presence of the thick layer also necessitates a further step in the lifting procedure, and the resulting multilayered fluid-structure operator is considerably more complex than in the case of a single thin layer as in
[
41
]
.
In particular, to tackle the strongly coupled nature of the present multilayered problem, a more intricate decoupling approach tailored to the functional analytic setting of the damped wave problem is required. As far as we are aware, this is the first time such a technique has been employed in the context of boundary-coupled FSI problems with elastic structures, though it may prove useful in a broader class of FSI models.
Furthermore, the damping in the thick structural layer renders the spectral analysis substantially more delicate. In particular, the determination of the essential spectrum requires a careful decomposition of the decoupled operator matrix.

This article has the following outline.
In
Section 2
, we introduce the time-periodic multilayered FSI problem and present the main result on the existence of a strong time-periodic solution for small periodic forces.

Section 3
is dedicated to the transformation to a fixed fluid domain.
The central
Section 4
discusses the linear theory for the time-periodic problem, including the damped wave problem, the reformulation in operator form, and the maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity and invertibility of the resulting operator matrix.
In
Section 5
, we establish the nonlinear estimates and complete the proof of the main result.

2.
The multilayered FSI problem and main result

In this section, we first introduce the multilayered FSI problem and then state the main result on the existence of a strong time-periodic solution to this problem under the action of small time-periodic forces.

We consider the 3D viscous, incompressible Navier-Stokes equations, for the fluid.
Due to the nonlinear coupling, the interface separating the fluid and the thick structural layer is generally not fixed, but it can move when time changes.
As a result, the fluid domain
\Omega_{\mathrm{f}}(t)
depends on the plate displacement, i. e., it is of the form
\Omega_{\mathrm{f}}(\eta(t))
, for
\eta
representing the displacement of the plate.
For simplicity, we will usually write
\Omega_{\mathrm{f}}(t)
in the sequel.
As we investigate the time-periodic problem, for a fixed time period
T>0
, we study the multilayered FSI problem on a time interval of the form
(0,T)
.
For the fluid velocity
u^{\mathcal{F}}
and pressure
\pi^{\mathcal{F}}
on the moving domain, the resulting fluid subproblem reads as

(2.1)

\partial_{t}u^{\mathcal{F}}+(u^{\mathcal{F}}\cdot\nabla)u^{\mathcal{F}}=\mdiv\sigma_{\mathrm{f}}(u^{\mathcal{F}},\pi^{\mathcal{F}})\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt\mdiv u^{\mathcal{F}}=0,\kern 5.0pt\text{for}\kern 5.0ptt\in(0,T),\kern 5.0ptx\in\Omega_{\mathrm{f}}(t),

where

(2.2)

\sigma_{\mathrm{f}}(u^{\mathcal{F}},\pi^{\mathcal{F}})=2\mu_{\mathrm{f}}\mathbb{D}(u^{\mathcal{F}})-\pi^{\mathcal{F}}\Id_{3},\kern 5.0pt\text{with}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{D}(u^{\mathcal{F}})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\nabla u^{\mathcal{F}}+(\nabla u^{\mathcal{F}})^{\top}\bigr),

represents the Cauchy stress tensor for some fluid viscosity parameter
\mu_{\mathrm{f}}>0
.
For simplicity, and as it does not affect the analysis, we will assume that
\mu_{\mathrm{f}}\equiv 1
throughout this paper.

Concerning the geometry of the problem, we suppose that the boundary of the (moving) fluid domain consists of a moving part
\Gamma_{\eta}(t)
, representing the interface with the thin structure, as well as a rigid part
\Gamma_{0}
.
More precisely, we assume that the domain of the thin structure
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a smooth subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and this in turn implies that
\Gamma_{\eta}(t)=\{(s,\eta(s)):s\in\omega\}
.
It follows that
\Gamma_{0}=\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{f}}(t)\setminus\Gamma_{\eta}(t)
.

For the plate, we assume that its motion is governed by a damped plate equation in
\omega
.
After rescaling by the (thin) structure thickness and density, and for
f_{\mathrm{p}}
denoting the force density acting on the structure and to be specified in the sequel, the linearly (visco-)elastic Koiter shell model is given by

\partial_{tt}\eta+C_{0}\eta-C_{1}\Delta_{s}\eta+C_{2}\Delta_{s}^{2}\eta+D_{0}\partial_{t}\eta-D_{1}\partial_{t}\Delta_{s}\eta+D_{2}\partial_{t}\Delta_{s}^{2}\eta=f_{\mathrm{p}},\kern 5.0pt\text{for}\kern 5.0ptt\in(0,T),\kern 5.0pts\in\omega.

Here
C_{i}\geq 0
and
D_{i}\geq 0
are the (rescaled) material constants corresponding to structural elasticity and viscosity, respectively, and the subscript
s
indicates that the respective differential operators act with respect to
\omega
, i. e., these are horizontal operators.
In the sequel, we will take into account the situation of
C_{2}=D_{1}=1
and
C_{0}=C_{1}=D_{0}=D_{2}=0
, i. e.,

(2.3)

\partial_{tt}\eta+\Delta_{s}^{2}\eta-\partial_{t}\Delta_{s}\eta=f_{\mathrm{p}},\kern 5.0pt\text{for}\kern 5.0ptt\in(0,T),\kern 5.0pts\in\omega.

Moreover, we suppose that the deformed plate does not have contact with the other part of the fluid domain, so
\Gamma_{0}\cap\Gamma_{\eta}(t)=\emptyset
for all
t\in(0,T)
.

Finally, for the thick layer, we invoke the equations of linear (visco-)elasticity.
In this case,
d
denotes the structural displacement of the thick (visco-)elastic layer, and for Lamé coefficients
\mu_{\mathrm{s}}
,
\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}>0
and a viscosity parameter
\delta>0
, the Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor takes the shape

(2.4)

\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}(d,\partial_{t}d)=\mu_{\mathrm{s}}\bigl((\nabla d+(\nabla d)^{\top})+\delta(\nabla\partial_{t}d+(\nabla\partial_{t}d)^{\top})\bigr)+\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}(\mdiv d+\delta\mdiv\partial_{t}d)\Id_{3}.

In the following, in order to ensure strong ellipticity of the associated Lamé operator, we assume that

(2.5)

\mu_{\mathrm{s}}>0\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt\mu_{\mathrm{s}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}>0.

The structural problem is already defined in the Lagrangian framework, and we denote the associated domain by
\Omega_{\mathrm{s}}
.
By the Lagrangian formulation, this domain does not depend on time or the displacement of the plate.
The boundary of
\Omega_{\mathrm{s}}
consists of two parts, namely the flat interface
\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}
as introduced above and the remaining part
\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{s}}\setminus\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}
.
The equations of linear (visco-)elasticity read as

(2.6)

\partial_{tt}d=\mdiv\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}(d,\partial_{t}d),\kern 5.0pt\text{for}\kern 5.0ptt\in(0,T),\kern 5.0ptx\in\Omega_{\mathrm{s}}.

The interaction problem is complemented by coupling and boundary conditions.
The coupling conditions consist of kinematic and dynamic ones.
Concerning the former, we assume that there is no slip between the fluid and the thin structure or between the thin and the thick structural layer.
For the unit vector
\mathrm{e}_{3}=(0,0,1)^{\top}
, the continuity of velocity reads as

(2.7)

u^{\mathcal{F}}(t,s,\eta(t,s))=\partial_{t}\eta(t,s)\mathrm{e}_{3},\kern 5.0pt\text{for}\kern 5.0ptt\in(0,T),\kern 5.0pts\in\omega.

For
x=(s,z)
, the continuity of displacement between the thick and the thin structure translates as

(2.8)

d(t,x)=\eta(t,s)\mathrm{e}_{3},\kern 5.0pt\text{for}\kern 5.0ptt\in(0,T),\kern 5.0ptx\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}.

The dynamic coupling condition describes the balance of forces at the fluid-structure interface
\Gamma_{\eta}(t)
, and it enters in the shape of the forcing term
f_{\mathrm{p}}
in (
2.3
), with forces on the plate exerted by the fluid and the thick structure.
With the outer unit normal vector

n_{\Gamma_{\eta}(t)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{s}\eta|^{2}}}\binom{-\nabla_{s}\eta}{1}

to
\Gamma_{\eta}(t)
, and with the Cauchy stress tensor
\sigma_{\mathrm{f}}
from (
2.2
) and the Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor
\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}
from (
2.4
), the dynamic coupling conditions can be rephrased as

(2.9)

f_{\mathrm{p}}=-\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{s}\eta|^{2}}\left.\left(\sigma_{\mathrm{f}}(u^{\mathcal{F}},\pi^{\mathcal{F}})n_{\Gamma_{\eta}(t)}\right)\right|_{\Gamma_{\eta}(t)}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{3}+\left.(\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}(d,\partial_{t}d)\mathrm{e}_{3})\right|_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{3}.

We now prescribe the boundary conditions for the fluid velocity
u^{\mathcal{F}}
at the rigid part
\Gamma_{0}
of the fluid boundary, for the plate at the boundary
\partial\omega
of its domain
\omega
and for the displacement
d
of the thick structure at the non-interface part
\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{s}}\setminus\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}
of the boundary of the thick layer.
Here
u^{\mathcal{F}}
and
d
satisfy homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, while the plate is clamped, i. e.,

(2.10)

\displaystyle u^{\mathcal{F}}(t,x)

\displaystyle=0,\kern 5.0pt\text{for}\kern 5.0ptt\in(0,T),\kern 5.0ptx\in\Gamma_{0},\kern 5.0pt\eta=\nabla_{s}\eta\cdot n_{\omega}=0,\kern 5.0pt\text{for}\kern 5.0ptt\in(0,T),\kern 5.0pts\in\partial\omega,\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle d(t,x)

\displaystyle=0,\kern 5.0pt\text{for}\kern 5.0ptt\in(0,T),\kern 5.0ptx\in\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{s}}\setminus\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}},

where
n_{\omega}
represents the unit exterior normal vector to
\partial\omega
.
Finally, the time-periodicity condition is

(2.11)

\displaystyle u^{\mathcal{F}}(0,\cdot)

\displaystyle=u^{\mathcal{F}}(T,\cdot),\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0pt\Omega_{\mathrm{f}}(\eta(0)),\kern 5.0pt\eta(0,\cdot)=\eta(T,\cdot)\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt\partial_{t}\eta(0,\cdot)=\partial_{t}\eta(T,\cdot),\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0pt\omega,

\displaystyle d(0,\cdot)

\displaystyle=d(T,\cdot)\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt\partial_{t}d(0,\cdot)=\partial_{t}d(T,\cdot),\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0pt\Omega_{\mathrm{s}}.

Moreover, we consider suitable forcing terms
f\colon(0,T)\times\Omega_{\mathrm{f}}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
in the fluid equation,
g\colon(0,T)\times\omega\to\mathbb{R}
in the plate equation and
h\colon(0,T)\times\Omega_{\mathrm{s}}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
in the equations of linear (visco-)elasticity.

For the sake of a more compact representation, we summarize the interaction problem consisting of the respective fluid, plate and linearly (visco-)elastic subproblem (
2.1
), (
2.3
) and (
2.6
) as well as the coupling conditions (
2.7
)–(
2.9
), the boundary conditions (
2.10
) and the time-periodicity conditions (
2.11
).
At this stage, let us also remark that we take into account the volume constraint

(2.12)

\int_{\omega}\eta\,\mathrm{d}s=0

in the following.
Thus, we introduce the space
\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{m}}^{q}(\omega)
of functions with average zero in
\mathrm{L}^{q}(\omega)
, so

(2.13)

\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{m}}^{q}(\omega)\coloneqq\left\{f\in\mathrm{L}^{q}(\omega):\int_{\omega}f\,\mathrm{d}s=0\right\}.

We define
\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{m}}^{q}(\Omega_{\mathrm{f}})
likewise.
In addition, we invoke the orthogonal projection
P_{\mathrm{m}}\colon\mathrm{L}^{q}(\omega)\to\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{m}}^{q}(\omega)
onto the space of functions with average zero.
It is given by

(2.14)

P_{\mathrm{m}}f\coloneqq f-\frac{1}{|\omega|}\int_{\omega}f\,\mathrm{d}s,\kern 5.0pt\text{for}\kern 5.0ptf\in\mathrm{L}^{q}(\omega).

We then apply this projection to the plate equation (
2.3
), leading to the equations

(2.15)

\displaystyle\partial_{tt}\eta+P_{\mathrm{m}}\Delta_{s}^{2}\eta-\Delta_{s}\partial_{t}\eta

\displaystyle=-P_{\mathrm{m}}\left(\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{s}\eta|^{2}}\left.\left(\sigma_{\mathrm{f}}(u^{\mathcal{F}},\pi^{\mathcal{F}})n_{\Gamma_{\eta}(t)}\right)\right|_{\Gamma_{\eta}(t)}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{3}\right)

\displaystyle+P_{\mathrm{m}}\left(\left.(\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}(d,\partial_{t}d)\mathrm{e}_{3})\right|_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{3}\right)

and

(2.16)

\displaystyle\int_{\omega}\pi^{\mathcal{F}}(t,s,\eta(t,s))\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{\omega}\Delta_{s}^{2}\eta(t,s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{\omega}\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{s}\eta|^{2}}\left.\left(2\mathbb{D}(u^{\mathcal{F}})n_{\Gamma_{\eta}(t)}\right)\right|_{\Gamma_{\eta}(t)}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{3}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle-\int_{\omega}\left.(\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}(d,\partial_{t}d)\mathrm{e}_{3})\right|_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{3}\,\mathrm{d}s.

This allows us to determine the pressure precisely, not only up to a constant.
As a consequence, we only keep (
2.15
) together with the volume constraint (
2.12
), so we solve the pressure up to a constant.
Finally, we employ (
2.16
) to determine the constant in the pressure.

The resulting problem, with (
2.15
) and (
2.12
) replacing (
2.3
), for
f_{\eta}\coloneqq f\circ X_{\eta}
for a diffeomorphism
X_{\eta}\colon(0,T)\times\Omega_{\mathrm{f}}\to(0,T)\times\Omega_{\mathrm{f}}(t)
made precise in
Section 3
, and with
\mathcal{J}_{\eta}\coloneqq\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{s}\eta|^{2}}
, reads as

(2.17)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}u^{\mathcal{F}}+(u^{\mathcal{F}}\cdot\nabla)u^{\mathcal{F}}&=\mdiv\sigma_{\mathrm{f}}(u^{\mathcal{F}},\pi^{\mathcal{F}})+f_{\eta},\kern 5.0pt\mdiv u^{\mathcal{F}}=0,&&t\in(0,T),\kern 5.0ptx\in\Omega_{\mathrm{f}}(t),\\
\partial_{tt}\eta+P_{\mathrm{m}}\Delta_{s}^{2}\eta-\partial_{t}\Delta_{s}\eta&=-P_{\mathrm{m}}\left(\mathcal{J}_{\eta}\left.\left(\sigma_{\mathrm{f}}(u^{\mathcal{F}},\pi^{\mathcal{F}})n_{\Gamma_{\eta}(t)}\right)\right|_{\Gamma_{\eta}(t)}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{3}\right)\\
&\qquad+P_{\mathrm{m}}\left(\left.(\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}(d,\partial_{t}d)\mathrm{e}_{3})\right|_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{3}\right)+P_{\mathrm{m}}g,&&t\in(0,T),\kern 5.0pts\in\omega,\\
\int_{\omega}\eta(t,s)\,\mathrm{d}s&=0,&&t\in(0,T),\\
\partial_{tt}d&=\mdiv\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}(d,\partial_{t}d)+h,&&t\in(0,T),\kern 5.0ptx\in\Omega_{\mathrm{s}},\\
u^{\mathcal{F}}(t,s,\eta(t,s))&=\partial_{t}\eta(t,s)\mathrm{e}_{3},&&t\in(0,T),\kern 5.0pts\in\omega,\\
d(t,x)&=\eta(t,s)\mathrm{e}_{3},&&t\in(0,T),\kern 5.0ptx\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}},\\
u^{\mathcal{F}}(t,x)&=0,&&t\in(0,T),\kern 5.0ptx\in\Gamma_{0},\\
d(t,x)&=0,&&t\in(0,T),\kern 5.0ptx\in\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{s}}\setminus\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}},\\
\eta=\nabla_{s}\eta\cdot n_{\omega}&=0,&&t\in(0,T),\kern 5.0pts\in\partial\omega,\\
u^{\mathcal{F}}(0,\cdot)&=u^{\mathcal{F}}(T,\cdot),&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0pt\Omega_{\mathrm{f}}(\eta(0)),\\
\eta(0,\cdot)&=\eta(T,\cdot)\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt\partial_{t}\eta(0,\cdot)=\partial_{t}\eta(T,\cdot),&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0pt\omega,\\

4.
Analysis of the linearized problem

The present section is of central importance.
In fact, its aim is to establish the so-called
maximal periodic
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity
via the Arendt-Bu theorem
[
2
]
.
For a generator
A\colon\mathrm{X}_{1}\subset\mathrm{X}_{0}\to\mathrm{X}_{0}
of a
\mathrm{C}_{0}
-semigroup on
\mathrm{X}_{0}
, the latter result provides a characterization of the well-posedness of the inhomogeneous periodic abstract Cauchy problem

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} w^{\prime}(t)-Aw(t)&=f(t),\quad t\in(0,2\pi),\\
w(0)&=w(2\pi),\end{aligned}\right.

in terms of the well-posedness of the associated
initial value problem
and a spectral condition on the
\mathrm{C}_{0}
-semigroup.
More precisely, setting
\mathbb{F}\coloneqq\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,2\pi;\mathrm{X}_{0})
and
\mathbb{E}\coloneqq\mathrm{W}^{1,p}(0,2\pi;\mathrm{X}_{0})\cap\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,2\pi;\mathrm{X}_{1})
, we recall that maximal periodic
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity means that for every
f\in\mathbb{F}
, there exists a unique solution
w\in\mathbb{E}
to (
4.1
).
Furthermore, the closed graph theorem then implies the existence of a constant
C>0
such that

\|w\|_{\mathbb{E}}\leq C\cdot\|f\|_{\mathbb{F}}.

The Arendt-Bu theorem is given as follows.

Proposition 4.1

(
[
2
, Thm. 5.1]
)
.

Consider a Banach space
\mathrm{X}_{0}
, and let
A\colon\mathrm{X}_{1}\to\mathrm{X}_{0}
be the generator of a
\mathrm{C}_{0}
-semigroup on
\mathrm{X}_{0}
.
Then
A
has maximal periodic
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity if and only if
A
has maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity, and the semigroup satisfies
1\in\rho(\mathrm{e}^{2\pi A})
.
Maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity means that for every
f\in\mathbb{F}
, there is a unique solution
w\in\mathbb{E}
to the abstract Cauchy problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} w^{\prime}(t)-Aw(t)&=f(t),\kern 5.0ptt\in(0,2\pi),\\
w(0)&=0.\end{aligned}\right.

A simple rescaling argument reveals that the Arendt-Bu theorem is also valid for general time intervals
(0,T)
.
On the other hand, the spectral mapping theorem for generators of analytic semigroups, see e. g.
[
20
, Cor. IV.3.12]
, and the fact that the maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity already implies the generation of an analytic semigroup yield that the spectral condition
1\in\rho(\mathrm{e}^{2\pi A})
is equivalent with
0\in\rho(A)
.

This section is dedicated to showing that the linearized problem that corresponds to the transformed problem (
3.4
) lies within the scope of the Arendt-Bu theorem as recalled in
Section 4
.
In fact, setting
\eta_{1}\coloneqq\eta
,
\eta_{2}\coloneqq\partial_{t}\eta
,
d_{1}\coloneqq d
and
d_{2}\coloneqq\partial_{t}d
in order to rewrite the linearized problem as a first order problem, we find that the linearized problem under consideration is given by

(4.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}u-\mdiv\sigma_{\mathrm{f}}(u,\pi)&=f,\kern 5.0pt\mdiv u=0,&&t\in(0,T),\kern 5.0pty\in\Omega_{\mathrm{f}},\\
\partial_{t}\eta_{1}&=\eta_{2},&&t\in(0,T),\kern 5.0pts\in\omega,\\
\partial_{t}\eta_{2}+P_{\mathrm{m}}\Delta_{s}^{2}\eta_{1}-\Delta_{s}\eta_{2}&=-P_{\mathrm{m}}\left(\left.\sigma_{\mathrm{f}}(u,\pi)\right|_{\Gamma}\mathrm{e}_{3}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{3}\right)\\
&\qquad+P_{\mathrm{m}}\left(\left.(\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}(d_{1},d_{2})\mathrm{e}_{3})\right|_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{3}\right)+P_{\mathrm{m}}g,&&t\in(0,T),\kern 5.0pts\in\omega,\\
\int_{\omega}\eta_{1}(t,s)\,\mathrm{d}s&=0,&&t\in(0,T),\\

Proof.

The first assertion of (c) immediately follows from (b) upon recalling

[

-regularity in terms of

-boundedness of the resolvents.

[

Hence, it remains to show (a) and (b).
In fact, we will first prove that

Conceptually, our proof is inspired by the considerations in

[

-calculus here.
At this stage, note that perturbation theory for the latter concept is significantly more involved than for sectoriality.

As an auxiliary tool, we introduce the operator matrix
B_{\mathrm{s}}\colon\mathrm{D}(B_{\mathrm{s}})\subset\mathrm{X}_{0}^{\mathrm{s}}\to\mathrm{X}_{0}^{\mathrm{s}}
, defined by

B_{\mathrm{s}}\coloneqq A_{\mathrm{s}}-\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\

Moreover, we invoke the isomorphism

as well as its inverse given by

S=\begin{pmatrix}\Id&0\\
\delta^{-1}&\Id\end{pmatrix}\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0ptS^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\Id&0\\
-\delta^{-1}&\Id\end{pmatrix},

respectively.
We then introduce the operator matrix
C_{\mathrm{s}}\colon\mathrm{D}(C_{\mathrm{s}})\subset\mathrm{X}_{0}^{\mathrm{s}}\to\mathrm{X}_{0}^{\mathrm{s}}
taking the shape

C_{\mathrm{s}}=SB_{\mathrm{s}}S^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}-\delta^{-1}\Id&\Id\\
0&\delta L_{0}\end{pmatrix},\kern 5.0pt\text{where}\kern 5.0pt\mathrm{D}(C_{\mathrm{s}})=S\mathrm{D}(B_{\mathrm{s}})=\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega_{\mathrm{s}})^{3}\times\left(\mathrm{W}^{2,q}(\Omega)^{3}\cap\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega_{\mathrm{s}})^{3}\right).

Next, we invoke the diagonal part
\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{s}}\colon\mathrm{D}(\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{s}})\subset\mathrm{X}_{0}^{\mathrm{s}}\to\mathrm{X}_{0}^{\mathrm{s}}
of

, i. e.,

(4.10)

\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{s}}\coloneqq\diag(-\delta^{-1}\Id,\delta L_{0}),\kern 5.0pt\text{with}\kern 5.0pt\mathrm{D}(\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{s}})=\mathrm{D}(C_{\mathrm{s}}).

From
Subsection 4.2
, we immediately infer that
-\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{s}}\in\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(\mathrm{X}_{0}^{\mathrm{s}})
with

.
The next goal is to transfer this property from
\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{s}}
to
C_{\mathrm{s}}
.
To this end, observe that
C_{\mathrm{s}}=\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{s}}+\tilde{P}_{\mathrm{s}}
, where

\tilde{P}_{\mathrm{s}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\Id\\

For this purpose, let
(d_{1},d_{2})\in\mathrm{D}(\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{s}})
.
Making use of (
4.9
) as well as

, which is implied by

, together with the shape of

, we find that

-C_{\mathrm{s}}
is bounded with respect to a fractional power of
-\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{s}}
.
It then follows from
[
35
, Prop. 13.1]
that there exists
\mu\geq 0
such that
-C_{\mathrm{s}}+\mu=-\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{s}}-\tilde{P}_{\mathrm{s}}+\mu\in\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(\mathrm{X}_{0}^{\mathrm{s}})
with

.

is possible, since

is invertible.
Indeed, by

\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
-calculus is preserved under isomorphisms, see e. g.
[

-B_{\mathrm{s}}\in\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(\mathrm{X}_{0}^{\mathrm{s}})
with

.
Next, note that
A_{\mathrm{s}}=B_{\mathrm{s}}+P_{\mathrm{s}}

Let now

as well as

is the unique solution to the stationary Stokes problem with inhomogeneous boundary conditions (

Next, we elaborate on the handling of the pressure by considering (weak) Neumann problems.

\displaystyle+\left\|\frac{1}{\det\left(\nabla X\right)}\left[\left(u\cdot\nabla\right)u\right]_{\alpha}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega_{\mathrm{f}}))}+\left\|\left[b\left(\partial_{t}a\right)\left(X\right)u\right]_{\alpha}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega_{\mathrm{f}}))}

\displaystyle+\left\|\left[\left(\nabla u\right)\left(\partial_{t}Y\right)\left(X\right)\right]_{\alpha}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega_{\mathrm{f}}))}\leq CR^{2}.

Concatenating the previous estimates, we deduce that
\|F(u,\pi,\eta_{1},\eta_{2})\|_{\mathbb{E}_{0}^{u}}\leq CR^{2}
for
C>0
.

The second part of the proof is dedicated to the estimate of the other nonlinear term
G(u,\pi,\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
.
Here, for the first term
\partial_{s_{i}}\eta_{1}[\frac{\partial a_{ik}}{\partial x_{3}}\left(X\right)+\frac{\partial a_{3k}}{\partial x_{i}}\left(X\right)]u_{k}
, we employ
Subsection 5.1
(a) to estimate the first factor in
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\omega))
by
CR
, estimate the factor in the middle by
CR
upon observing that (
5.1
) also holds when the norm is replaced by
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{L}^{3p}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{3q}(\omega))}
, since the terms appearing in the estimate in
Subsection 5.1
(d) are functions defined on
\omega
, and use
Subsection 5.1
(e) to estimate
u_{k}
, which is defined on a part of the fluid boundary, in
\mathrm{L}^{\nicefrac{{3p}}{{2}}}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{\nicefrac{{3q}}{{2}}}(\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{f}}))
by
CR
.
Thus, from Hölder’s inequality, it follows that

\left\|\partial_{s_{i}}\eta_{1}\left[\frac{\partial a_{ik}}{\partial x_{3}}\left(X\right)+\frac{\partial a_{3k}}{\partial x_{i}}\left(X\right)\right]u_{k}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{q}(\omega))}\leq CR^{3}.

Likewise, we get
\|2\frac{\partial a_{3k}}{\partial x_{3}}\left(X\right)u_{k}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{q}(\omega))}\leq CR^{2}
.
For
\partial_{s_{i}}\eta[a_{ik}\left(X\right)\frac{\partial Y_{l}}{\partial x_{3}}\left(X\right)+a_{3k}\left(X\right)\frac{\partial Y_{l}}{\partial x_{i}}\left(X\right)]\frac{\partial u_{k}}{\partial y_{l}}
, we use the same argument as before to estimate the first factor by
CR
, and we employ
Subsection 5.1
(c) to bound the sum in the middle.
By virtue of
Subsection 5.1
(e), which enables an estimate of
\nabla u
in
\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{q}(\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{f}}))
by
CR
, we derive from Hölder’s inequality that

\left\|\partial_{s_{i}}\eta\left[a_{ik}\left(X\right)\frac{\partial Y_{l}}{\partial x_{3}}\left(X\right)+a_{3k}\left(X\right)\frac{\partial Y_{l}}{\partial x_{i}}\left(X\right)\right]\frac{\partial u_{k}}{\partial y_{l}}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{q}(\omega))}\leq CR^{2}.

For the last term, we use
Subsection 5.1
(a) and (c) to get
\bigl\|a_{3k}(X)\frac{\partial Y_{l}}{\partial x_{3}}(X)-\delta_{3,k}\delta_{3,l}\bigr\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\omega))}\leq CR
.
Thus, by the same estimate of
\nabla u
as for the former term, joint with Hölder’s inequality, it follows that

\left\|\left(a_{3k}\left(X\right)\frac{\partial Y_{l}}{\partial x_{3}}\left(X\right)-\delta_{3,k}\delta_{3,l}\right)\frac{\partial u_{k}}{\partial y_{l}}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{q}(\omega))}\leq CR^{2}.

Putting together the estimates of the four terms, we conclude that
G(u,\pi,\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
can be estimated in
\mathbb{E}_{0}^{\eta}=\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{q}(\omega))
by
CR^{2}
, completing the proof of the proposition.
∎
