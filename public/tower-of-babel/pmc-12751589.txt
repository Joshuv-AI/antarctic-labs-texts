Detection of rifampin-resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis using CRISPR/Cas14a-enabled molecular techniques
Authors: Gengshan Wu, Yifan Ren, Yuanning Wang, Yong Zhao, Yutong Wu, Hong Sun, Zhaogang Sun, Ruibai Wang, Zongmin Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe emergence of drug-resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) strains highlights the urgent need for precise and timely diagnostic methods to prevent prolonged and complex treatment regimens. This study aims to develops a CRISPR/Cas14a-based assay for accurate identification of MTB and rifampin-resistant MTB (RR-MTB) strains.MethodThe 16 S rDNA sequence and rifampin resistance-determining region (RRDR) of rpoB gene were chosen for the detection of MTB and RR-MTB, respectively. Several sgRNAs were designed for each target and evaluated for their performance. The platform was then systematic optimized by adjusting the concentrations of different components, followed by the evaluation of its sensitivity for the detection of MTB and RR-MTB. The system’s efficacy was further validated through a double-blind test on 16 clinical MTB isolates, and the results were compared with genomic sequencing.ResultsThrough a meticulous screening process, we identified optimal single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) capable of distinguishing MTB from nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) and the eight predominant mutation types associated with rifampin resistance. Our refined CRISPR/Cas14a platform demonstrated a remarkable sensitivity, with a limit of detection (LOD) of 200 copies/µL for MTB and 2 copies/µL for RR-MTB, respectively. This platform demonstrated a 100% accuracy rate in identification of RR-MTB using clinical MTB isolates.ConclusionsThe CRISPR/Cas14a-based platform we developed exhibited superior performance for the detection of MTB and RR-MTB, with significant implications for the diagnosis and management of tuberculosis, particularly in regions with high prevalence of drug-resistant strains.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12190-w.Keywords: CRISPR/Cas14a, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Rifampin resistance, Nucleic acid detection, Single nucleotide variant

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), a communicable disease mainly caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB), remains a major threat to public health. Current standard TB chemotherapy requires a 6-month-long administration with a combination regimen including four first-line drugs rifampicin (RIF), isoniazid (INH), pyrazinamide and ethambutol [1]. However, the emergence of multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) and extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB) renders the treatment more complex, costly and time-intensive [2], in which resistance to RIF is of greatest concern. Drug resistance in MTB is mainly due to mutations in its chromosomal genes [3]. Taken RIF-resistant MTB (RR-MTB) as an example, more than 96% of RR-MTB harbor clustering in an 81-base-pair RIF resistance-determining region (RRDR) of rpoB gene. Numerous mutation types within RRDR have been identified, among which mutations in 531, 526 and 516 codons (corresponding to Escherichia coli RNAP codons) are the most prevalent. Their frequencies in the Chinese mainland are shown in Table S1. In addition, more than 90% of RR-MTB also show resistance to INH; therefore, RIF resistance is considered as a valuable surrogate marker for MDR-TB [4].

The accurate and rapid identification of TB and drug-resistant TB (DR-TB) is a prerequisite for appropriate and effective treatment. Over the years, nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) have been widely applied for the detection of MTB through amplifying distinct genomic targets, among which the insertion element IS6110 remains probably the most frequently used detection target [5, 6]. Nevertheless, it is important to note that certain isolates of MTB lack the IS6110 element, which can result in potential false negative results [7, 8]. By contrast, 16S rDNA sequence is highly conserved among Mycobacterium genus with only several nucleotide changes in the variable region and therefore serves as an alternative target for specific detection of MTB [9].

Recently, a number of innovative nucleic acids detection methods based on clustered regularly interspaced short palindrome repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR-associated (Cas) protein system have emerged as promising techniques [10, 11], such as Cas12a-based DETECTR [12] and Cas13a-based SHERLOCK [13]. Cas14a, the most recently identified and smallest member of the Cas protein family, also possesses collateral nuclease activity, which is triggered upon binding to and cleaving target single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) substrates. This process is directed by a composite of trans-activating crRNA (tracrRNA) and crRNA, which can be fused into a single-guide RNA (sgRNA).

Unique to Cas14a is its independence from the protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sequence for substrate recognition, a characteristic that distinguishes it from other Cas effector proteins. Furthermore, Cas14a exhibits enhanced capability in discrimination of the single-nucleotide mutation compared to Cas12a, showing low tolerance for mismatches between crRNA and the internal seed sequence of ssDNA targets [14]. The unique attributes of Cas14a make it highly suitable for the high-fidelity detection of any targeted ssDNA, without the constraint of PAM sites.

Here, we have developed a CRISPR/Cas14a-based detection platform for the identification of MTB and RR-MTB. This was achieved by using sgRNAs that selectively target the 16 S rDNA sequence for MTB detection and RRDR of rpoB gene for the identification of the RIF resistance (Fig. 1). Following a meticulous screening process, we successfully identified the optimal sgRNAs that can accurately distinguish MTB from NTM and discern the eight predominant mutation types associated with RIF resistance, which are prevalent in the Chinese mainland. The CRISPR/Cas14a-based approach we developed is highly effective in distinguishing MTB strains from closely related NTM, as well as in identifying the eight predominant mutation types within RRDR in MTB strains prevalent in China. Furthermore, this platform has exhibited high accuracy in the double-blinded detection of clinical MTB isolates.

Schematic diagram of the CRISPR/Cas14a-based fluorescence detection platform. The ssDNA target generated through asymmetric PCR is subjected to Cas14a detection. The guidance of a complementary sgRNA to the target sequence activates both the specific nucleases and collateral nucleases of Cas14a, leading to the cleavage of FQ reporter and emission of a robust fluorescent signal. In theory, when a single-point mismatch exists between the ssDNA substrate and a specific sgRNA, the collateral activity of Cas14a will not be activated or in much less extent and consequently, no fluorescent signal will be detected

Methods

Purification of Cas14a protein

The pLBH531_MBP-Cas14a1 plasmid (Addgene, plasmid number 112500) was transformed into BL21(DE3). The Cas14a protein was purified as previously reported [15]. In brief, bacterial cells containing pLBH531_MBP-Cas14a1 were grown in LB at 37°C until OD600nm of approximately 0.6. Protein expression was induced by adding 0.5 mM of isopropyl b-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) followed by incubation at 150 rpm in an 16°C incubator for 12 to 14 h. Proteins were then purified by nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid-agarose (Ni-NTA) affinity chromatography and desalted using PD-10 desalting columns (GE Healthcare, Pittsburgh, PA, USA). After 1.5 h TEV (NewEngland Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, United States) cleavage at 30°C, the his-MBP-tag proteins were removed by Ni-NTA agarose, and the purified proteins were analyzed by electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). The concentrations of proteins were determined with BCA protein assay kit (23227, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and preserved at -80°C.

Design and preparation of sgRNA

sgRNA design

The designed sgRNAs consist of a full length of 222 nucleotides (nt), which includes a 202-nt constant region known as tracrRNA, and a 20-nt variable region referred to as crRNA. The crRNA sequence is determined by the specific target substrates (Table S2). For the detection of MTB, a set of 5 crRNAs were designed. These crRNAs introduce tiling mismatches started from the 9th to the 13th nucleotide across the target sequence of the 16 S rDNA gene in M. shinjukuense, while they are all fully complementary to the 16 S rDNA sequence of MTB. For the detection of RR-MTB, 2 or 8 crRNAs were designed for each mutation type by tiling one-nucleotide mismatches across the target sequence of rpoB gene, while these crRNAs were designed to be fully complementary to the respective substrates containing diverse single-point mutations.

Construction of the template for sgRNA synthesis

The sequences for the T7 promoter and tracrRNA were inserted into the pSMART plasmid, positioned directly upstream of two BbsI restriction sites. For each designed crRNA, a pair of complementary 24-nt oligonucleotides with BbsI cohesive ends were synthesized and cloned into the pSMART plasmid using the Golden Gate assembly method [16]. The template for synthesis of sgRNA via transcription was generated by PCR amplification. The PCR reaction mixture was comprised of 3µL of template DNA, 2µL of forward primer (10µM), 2µL of reverse primer (10µM) and 43µLof reaction buffer. The sequences of the crRNAs and PCR primers used in this study are listed in Table S2.

Synthesis of sgRNAs

The sgRNAs were synthesized in vitro using the TranscriptAid T7 High Yield Transcription Kit (#K0441, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 1 µg of DNA template, prepared in the previous step, was combined with 8µL of NTP mix, 2µL of TranscriptAid enzyme mix and 4µL of 5×TranscriptAid reaction buffer. The mixture was then brought to a final volume of 20µL with DEPC-treated water and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Following incubation, the reaction products were diluted with DEPC-treated water, mixed with the RNA loading dye and analyzed by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel to validate the quality of the synthesized sgRNAs.

Generation of SsDNA substrates

The ssDNA substrates used in this study were either chemically synthesized or produced via asymmetric PCR using genomic DNA or plasmids as templates. In the case of asymmetric PCR, the reaction mixture contained 5µL of 10×reaction buffer, 3µL of template, 5µM forward primer and 50µM reverse primer, and DEPC-treated water was added to bring the total volume to 50µL. The amplification was performed using T100™ Thermal Cycler (BIO-RAD, Hercules, CA, United States) with the following program: an initial denaturation at 98°C for 2 min, followed by 50 cycles of 98°C for 10s, 58°C for 10s, and 72°C for 10s, with a final extension at 72°C for 2 min. The sequences of the chemically synthesized ssDNA oligonucleotides and the primers for asymmetric PCR are listed in Table S2.

CRISPR/Cas14a detection system

The Cas14a detection mixture comprised 5µL of CutSmart Buffer (New England Biolabs, United States), 500nM of Cas14a, 250nM of sgRNA, 1µM of reporter probe, and either 100nM of synthesized ssDNA oligonucleotides or 5µL of the asymmetric PCR products. DEPC-treated water was added to bring the total volume to 50µL. The reaction was performed at 45°C for the indicated duration with fluorescence measurements taken at 1-min intervals (λex = 490 nm; λem = 520 nm) using a SpectraMax instrument (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, United States).

Template DNA Preparation

For specificity assay

To assess the specificity of the detection method, genomic DNA from 9 species of pathogenic NTM was employed, including M. phlei, M. fortuitum, M. abscessus, M. chelonae, M. gordonae, M. scrofulaceum, M. asiaticum, M. kansasii, and M. avium [17]. For this assay, 5µL of genomic DNA at 0.5ng/µL was added to asymmetric PCR reaction mixture mentioned above, using the primers 16S-F/R to amplify their respective 16S rDNA sequences.

For sensitivity assay

Genomic DNA extracted from an MTB (strain number DTB193, Table S3) or RR-MTB (strain number T2, Table S3) [18] was serially diluted to concentrations of 1, 10− 1, 10− 2, 10− 3, 10− 4, 10− 5 and 10− 6ng/µL. Subsequently, 3µL of each serial dilution of genomic DNA was used as template for asymmetric PCR amplification using the primers 16S-F/R or RRDR-F/R to generate the ssDNA substrates.

For the detection of clinical MTB isolates

16 clinical isolates of MTB were selected from a panel of MTB strains with various anti-TB drug resistant mutations, which are maintained by the Tuberculosis Department of National Institute for Communicable Disease Control and Prevention at the Chinese Centre for Disease Control and Prevention. The detection of these strains was performed double-blindly. Genomic DNA from the 16 MTB strains was extracted using QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Cat. No. 51306, QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The extracted DNA was then diluted to 1ng/µL and used as a template for asymmetric PCR amplification. The DNA samples were also subjected to rpoB gene amplification, followed by sequencing to determine the mutation profiles in the RRDR of rpoB gene in the selected MTB strains.

Results

Development of a CRISPR/Cas14a-based platform for the specific detection of MTBC

As 16S rDNA sequence is highly conserved among all species within Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC), it was selected as the target for the detection of MTBC. Firstly, 16S rDNA sequences of closely related strains within Mycobacterium genus was retrieved from the NCBI database by BLAST analysis using the 16S rDNA sequence of the MTB reference strain H37Rv as the query sequence. Among these, M. shinjukuense, a member of NTM, exhibited the highest homology to H37Rv in the 16S rDNA sequence. This finding aligns with previous phylogenomic and comparative genomic studies of genus Mycobacterium [19]. Through sequence alignment, a 20-bp sequence within the 16S rDNA gene was identified as the target sequence for detection, which is shared by all members of MTBC but differs by three bases from the corresponding sequence of M. shinjukuense. The 20-bp conserved sequence was used for crRNA design and a 150-bp ssDNA fragment harboring this 20-bp target was synthesized and named as MTBC-16S. The counterpart fragment from the M. shinjukuense 16S rDNA gene was also synthesized and named as M. shinjukuense-16S (Fig. 2a).

Cas14a is noted for its high sensitivity to mismatches located near the center of the target ssDNA substrate that is complementary to the sgRNA. Nonetheless, the precise position of mismatches where Cas14a exhibits the least tolerance is not consistent and varies with different substrates [14]. Therefore, we conducted a series experiments aimed at identifying the optimal sgRNA that allows Cas14a to differentiate with high specificity between target and non-target ssDNA. We introduced 5 distinct sgRNAs into the Cas14a detection system to assess their performance in identifying the presence of MTBC-16S or M. shinjukuense-16S. Upon introduction of MTBC-16S as substrate, the application of MTBC-sgRNA12 yielded the most pronounced fluorescent signals among the 5 tested sgRNAs. Conversely, when M. shinjukuense-16S was used as the substrate, the fluorescent signals for all 5 sgRNAs were nearly undetectable (Fig. 2b-c). These results suggest that MTBC-sgRNA12 can be used in Cas14a-based detection platform for discriminating MTBC from the closely related M. shinjukuense.

Development of CRISPR/Cas14a-based platform for RR-MTB detection

To detect 531 C➜T mutation within RRDR of rpoB gene, 8 different sgRNAs were designed by tiling one-nucleotide mismatches from the 9th to 16th position, across the target sequence of ssDNA substrate containing the wild-type RRDR of rpoB. A substrate named 531-1 M, which carried the specific point mutation (C➜T), was synthesized to serve as a perfectly matched ssDNA control (Table S2). Thus, each of the 8 sgRNAs exhibits complete complement with the 531-1 M substrate, while introducing a mismatch with the WT substrate at one position among the 9th to 16th positions of the corresponding crRNAs (Fig. 3a). CRISPR/Cas14a detection systems utilizing 8 different sgRNAs were individually prepared and the WT or 531-1 M substrate was added into the detection system to evaluate the performance of sgRNAs in discriminating WT from 531 to 1 M. As shown in Fig. 3b, the use of 531-sgRNA12 generated the most pronounced difference in fluorescence intensity between WT and 531-1 M ssDNA substrates. These results indicate that 531-sgRNA12 is the most effective one for CRISPR/Cas14a system to discriminate the 531 C➜T mutation in the RRDR of the rpoB gene.

Similarly, we conducted a screening process to identify the optimal sgRNAs for detecting 526 C➜G and 516 A➜T mutations within the RRDR of the rpoB gene. Our findings showed that the most effective sgRNAs for these mutations were 526-1-sgRNA11 and 516-1-sgRNA12, respectively (Fig. 3c-d). Based on these results, we inferred that while the exact position of the mismatch to which Cas14a is most sensitive can vary depending on the substrate, mismatches at the 11th or 12th position often offer the highest potential for the accurate discrimination of single nucleotide mutations. Therefore, in the subsequent screening experiments for the remaining 5 mutation types in RRDR, we limited our design to only two sgRNAs, each with a mismatch at the 11th or 12th position of the substrates. Using this strategy, we successfully acquired sgRNAs that can effectively discriminate the respective single-point mutation types (Fig. S2).

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that CRISPR/Cas14a-based systems tailored for RR-MTB detection have been successfully established, which are able to identify the 8 most prevalent mutation types within the RRDR of the rpoB gene.

Optimization of CRISPR/Cas14a-based detection platform

To enhance the performance of the detection platform, we conducted a systematic optimization of the various components within the system. We began by evaluating the purified Cas14a (Fig. S1) and the 531-sgRNA12 at different concentrations to ascertain the optimal conditions that would produce the highest fluorescent signal intensity in response to the synthetic ssDNA 531-1 M substrate in the sample set. The results showed that the highest fluorescence intensity was obtained with a Cas14a concentration of 500nM and an sgRNA concentration of 250nM, maintaining a ratio of Cas14a to sgRNA at 2:1 (Fig. S3a-b).

To assess the impact of reporter design on fluorescence intensity, we tested FQ reporters containing either 12 or 21 poly-T sequences to evaluate which one produces a stronger signal. Contrary to previous studies that have noted Cas14a’s collateral nuclease activity favoring longer ssDNA substrates [14], our data indicated that the 12nt-reporter yielded a higher fluorescence intensity and achieved the peak fluorescent signal more rapidly than the 21nt-repoter (Fig. S3c). These results contradicted a previous report [20], and we speculate that the discrepancy may be attributed to the use of different buffers in the two detection systems. Next, we assessed the impact of different buffers on the fluorescence intensity generated by a positive sample when employing our Cas14a detection method (Fig. S3d). Considering factors such as availability and detection performance, we selected CutSmart Buffer (NEB) as the optimal reaction buffer.

Finally, we optimized conditions for asymmetric PCR to efficiently generate ssDNA substrates from the unknown samples. As shown in Fig. S3e-f, the use of a 5µM forward primer in a 1:10 ratio relative to the reverse primer yielded the highest fluorescent signal among all the tested conditions for asymmetric PCR amplification. Therefore, this optimized condition was adopted for the subsequent experiments.

Specificity and sensitivity of the optimized CRISPR/Cas14a-based platform for detecting MTBC and RR-MTB

To evaluate the specificity of the optimized CRISPR/Cas14a-based platform, ssDNA targets from 16 S rDNA of MTB and 9 species of NTM were amplified by asymmetric PCR using the corresponding genomic DNA as templates. The resulting amplicons were then analyzed using CRISPR/Cas14a detection platform. As shown in Fig. 4a, the fluorescence intensity for MTB was very high, while the other 9 NTM samples exhibited signals levels comparable to the negative control of M. shinjukuense. These results indicate that the optimized CRISPR/Cas14a-based platform exhibits high specificity for MTBC, effectively distinguishing it from NTM.

To determine the LOD for the optimized CRISPR/Cas14a-based platform in identifying MTBC, genomic DNA extracted from MTB strain DTB193 was serially diluted by 10-fold and used as templates to generate ssDNA substrates through the optimized asymmetric PCR protocol. A 200-bp fragment of the 16 S rDNA sequence from M. shinjukuense was cloned into pUC19 to generate the pUC19-Mshi-Frag plasmid, which served as the negative control. The platform exhibited a marked increase fluorescent signals compared to the negative control when the concentrations of the genomic DNA reached as low as 10− 3ng/µL (Fig. 4b left panel). Thus, the LOD for detecting MTBC using this platform was determined to be 10− 3ng/µL, which is equivalent to 200 copies/µL. To determine the LOD for RR-MTB, an optimized CRISPR/Cas14a-based detection platform in conjunction with 531-sgRNA12, was used for the detection of RR-MTB strain T2 with 531 C➜T mutation in the rpoB gene. A plasmid pUC19-WT-Frag, which contains the RRDR sequences of the wild-type rpoB gene, served as the negative control. The detection platform could easily discriminate RR-MTB from the negative control at a genomic DNA concentration as low as 10−5ng/µL (Fig. 4b right panel). Meanwhile, the presence of the negative control at a high concentration of 1 ng/µL did not elicit any cross-reactivity. Therefore, the LOD of our optimized CRISPR/Cas14a-based detection platform for RR-MTB was determined to be 10−5ng/µL, which is equivalent to 2 copies/µL.

Application of CRISPR/Cas14a-based platform for identifying RIF resistance in clinically isolated MTB strains

To evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of the CRISPR/Cas14a platform for the detection of RIF resistance in MTB strains, a panel of 16 clinical MTB isolates (including rifampicin-resistant, MDR, and XDR strains) was selected from the mycobacterial strain library at the China CDC. These strains had been characterized by second- or third-generation genome sequencing and phenotypic drug susceptibility testing. The rpoB gene of these strains was amplified and sequenced (Fig. S5). A double-blind approach was employed during CRISPR/Cas14a testing to ensure unbiased results. ssDNA substrates, representing the RRDR sequences of each isolate, were generated by asymmetric PCR and subsequently subjected to CRISPR/Cas14a-based RR-MTB detection using a panel of 8 optimized sgRNAs (Fig. 3, Fig. S2). A total of 128 individual detection assays, comprising 16Samples tested with 8 sgRNAs, were conducted to detect RIF resistance in the clinical MTB isolates. The threshold for distinguishing positive from negative outcomes was set at twice the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the negative controls. The results revealed that out of the 128 assays, 15 yielded positive results, with 14 out of the 16 isolates harboring at least one mutation within their RRDR sequences (Fig. 5a). Specifically, one strain (number 16) carries two mutation sites, while two strains (number 3 and 12) exhibit no mutations within their RRDRs (Fig. 5a, Fig. S4a-h). These observations were consistent with the mutation profiles identified through genomic sequencing of the RRDR of rpoB gene of the MTB isolates (Table S3). As illustrated in Fig. 5b-c, the CRISPR/Cas14a-based detection platform demonstrated a 100% accuracy rate for both positive and negative detections, suggesting its high sensitivity and specificity in identifying RR-MTB. These results indicate that this innovative detection platform holds promise for clinical application.

Discussion

It was reported that about 7.5 million people were newly diagnosed with TB in 2022, ranking it among the leading infectious causes of death worldwide. Additionally, an estimated 3.1 million cases remained undiagnosed or were not reported to national authorities [21]. To achieve the goal of WHO’s “Ending TB by 2030”, the development of precise TB diagnostic methods to enhance detection rates is crucial. Recently, molecular techniques, particularly NAATs, have gained prominence for their high accuracy and rapidness [22]. In addition to conventional molecular tests, CRISPR-based platforms leveraging the collateral nuclease activity of Cas effector proteins have made significant advance [23]. Over the past five years, more than ten platforms utilizing Cas9, Cas12a, or Cas13a have been developed for TB diagnosis and the screening of drug-resistant strains [24–34]. Regarding Cas14a-based detection system, only one relevant paper has been published during the peer review of this article, describing a method for the rapid detection of drug-resistant MTB from sputum samples [35].

This study marks our initial effort to develop a CRISPR/Cas14a-based platform for TB diagnosis and RR-MTB screening by leveraging the collateral activity of Cas14a. We conducted a screening process to identify the optimal sgRNA capable of distinguishing MTB from NTM and 8 sgRNAs targeting the eight most prevalent mutation types within RRDR of the rpoB gene, which are responsible for RIF resistance. Through optimization of the reaction components, our platform achieved a LOD of 200 copies/µL for MTB and 2 copies/µL for RR-MTB detection, respectively. This platform also exhibited superior performance in a double-blind test in detection of 16 clinically MTB isolates, further validating its potential diagnostic utility.

Our findings reveal that while the precise position of mismatches where Cas14a exhibits the least tolerance is not consistent and varies with different substrates, mismatches at the 11th or 12th position generally offer the greatest accuracy in distinguishing single nucleotide mutations. Currently, the eight optimized sgRNAs used in our platform are estimated to cover approximately 90% of RR-MTB, leaving about 10% of RIF-MTB strains remaining undetectable. Depending on the specific requirements of detection, the coverage of our CRISPR/Cas14a-based detection platform for RR-MTB can be further expanded by incorporating additional sgRNAs for the mutation types not covered by the current set of sgRNAs. Cas14a’s low tolerance for single-base mismatches, coupled with its ability to recognize substrates independent of PAM sites, provides substantial flexibility in target selection, underscoring the versatility of our CRISPR/Cas14a-based platform. Apart from RIF, the resistance of MTB towards other drugs such as INH and streptomycin is also predominantly caused by single nucleotide variants [36–38]. Therefore, our platform is readily adaptable for the screening of these drug-resistant MTB strains through the design of appropriate sgRNAs. Our current method has several limitations. First, we have not yet compared its detection performance against clinically standard methods using clinical samples. Second, the workflow requires a nucleic acid pre-amplification step that depends on a thermal cycler, followed by a separate detection step. Developing a one-pot assay that integrates isothermal amplification with the CRISPR/Cas14a detection system would minimize equipment dependency, making it more suitable for future point-of-care testing (POCT). Third, the assay is limited by its single-target design. A future multiplex format, capable of simultaneously detecting different mutations by integrating multiple optimized sgRNAs, would significantly improve diagnostic throughput of RR-MTB. This platform could be further extended to screen for multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) by incorporating sgRNAs targeting a broader panel of drug-resistance genes.

Conclusions

We developed a highly sensitive and specific nucleic acid detection platform that leverages the high single-base discrimination and PAM-independent nature of Cas14a to diagnose MTB and RR-MTB, providing an innovative and alternative diagnostic approach. While the current format has limitations for POCT and multiplex detection applications, this method establishes a foundation for the development of diagnostic tools targeting single-nucleotide mutations, such as those conferring antibiotic resistance or causing genetic disorders.
