Spatiotemporal evolution and determinants analysis of health resource supply-demand coupling coordination in China
Authors: Yuxiao Wang, Jingru Zhang, Xinyue Zhang, Tinghui Lian, Lu He, Jun Xie, Jiantao Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe imbalance between supply and demand in China’s healthcare sector has become increasingly pronounced. This study aims to quantitatively assess the coupling and coordination status of health resource supply and demand in China, reveal its determinants, with the goal of narrowing regional disparities and improving the overall coordination level. By analyzing the spatiotemporal evolution of health resource allocation from 2002 to 2021, we seek to provide evidence-based policy recommendations for addressing the imbalance in China’s healthcare sector.MethodsUsing provincial panel data from 2002 to 2021, we constructed a comprehensive evaluation index system for the health resource supply-demand system. The entropy weight method and the Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution were employed to quantify the comprehensive levels of supply and demand. The coupling coordination degree (CCD) and its types were assessed using a coupling coordination model. Finally, the geographically and temporally weighted regression (GTWR) model and Geodetector model was used to analyze the spatiotemporal evolution and determinants of CCD in 31 provinces of China from 2002 to 2021.ResultsFrom 2002 to 2021, the CCD of health resource supply-demand in China improved significantly, increasing from 0.400 to 0.804, reaching a good coupling coordination level. Although the regional coefficient of variation (CV) of CCD decreased from 0.225 to 0.206, regional heterogeneity persisted, with the eastern region exhibiting the highest coordination, followed by the central, western, and northeastern regions. Analysis using Geodetector and GTWR models identified key spatiotemporal determinants. Notably, education level emerged as the core factor explaining the spatial heterogeneity (q = 0.608), and its interaction with urbanization (q = 0.885) significantly amplified these spatial differences. Population density, government support, and transportation accessibility were also identified as important influencing factors.ConclusionsThe current healthcare supply-demand conflict in China is characterized by insufficient supply, low quality, and an irrational structure and layout. Although coupling coordination has improved under recent reforms and policies, challenges such as poor coordination degrees and significant regional disparities persist. The Chinese government should strengthen supply-side healthcare reforms to address regional disparities and enhance the coupling coordination of health resource supply and demand.Keywords: Health resource equity, Coupling coordination, Spatial disparity, GTWR, Determinants

Introduction

Health resources are a general term for the human, material, and financial resources available for the provision of healthcare services under certain socioeconomic conditions [1]. “Institutions, beds, personnel, equipment and funds” are the five elements of health resources, which were declared in the “Decision on Health Reform and Development” document of the Communist Party of Central Committee and The State Council in 1997 [2]. With population growth, economic development, urban expansion, and an accelerated aging process, the demand for healthcare services has increased dramatically, while the supply of health resources has lagged behind rapid growth [3, 4]. Therefore, in the context of the increasingly prominent contradiction between the supply and demand of health resources, a quantitative assessment of the supply-demand capacity, clarification of the matching status, and alleviation of the supply-demand contradiction have attracted the attention of health economics, health management, geography, and other related disciplines.

The effective and equitable allocation of health resources to ensure quality healthcare is accessible and affordable is an important topic in health resource research. Currently, studies on the supply and demand of health resources mainly focus on three perspectives: equity of resources, accessibility of services, and efficiency of resource allocation. First, in the study of resource allocation equity, researchers often use economic indicators such as the Lorenz curve [5], Gini coefficient [6], and Thiel index [7] to analyze the distribution of health resources among different regions and populations to assess resource allocation equity. Second, in healthcare accessibility research, researchers typically use the two-step forward catchment area (2SFCA) model in geography [8–10] to analyze the accessibility of healthcare facilities. In addition, studies have analyzed the spatial differences and effects of health resource allocation using spatial autoregressive [11], spatial Durbin [12], and spatial error models [13]. Third, in research on resource allocation efficiency, researchers commonly use data envelopment analysis [14–16], the Malmquist productivity index [17, 18], the super-efficient SBM model [19] and other methods to assess the efficiency of the use of health resource inputs, outputs, and output effectiveness. In addition, the construction of an evaluation index system has become a mainstream model for many scholars to conduct a comprehensive assessment of the level of healthcare service provision. Li ZG et al. measured the supply level of Chinese medicine medical services in China through a Delphi constructed indicator system [20].

A growing body of international literature addresses the core challenge of dynamically matching health resource supply with population health demand recently. Health workforce planning in countries such as the United Kingdom and Germany is increasingly based on predictive modeling [21, 22]. Australia enhances the precision of supply-demand alignment by integrating data on the geographical distribution of general practitioners with projections of population demographic changes and disease burden [23]. In contrast, the governance of supply-demand imbalances in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in Africa and Southeast Asia, primarily focuses on addressing fundamental resource scarcity. Relevant research emphasizes supply-oriented resource allocation models, such as community health workers [24] and digital healthcare services [25].

In the study of influencing factors of medical resource allocation, Yueming Xi et al. used binary logistic regression model to explore residents’ evaluation of medical resource allocation and its determinants [26]. Lei Yuan et al. used the geo-detector method to reveal the key factors affecting the distribution of high-quality medical resources in China [27]. Jing Gong et al. used Tobit model to estimate the influencing factors of health resource allocation efficiency, and found that the efficiency of medical resource allocation was affected by residents’ average annual income, population density and education level [28].

Although the aforementioned studies have made valuable contributions to the examination of the supply and demand for health resources from the perspectives of equity, accessibility, and efficiency, their scope remains limited: (1) Current research generally adopts a supply-oriented analysis framework, which primarily focuses on measuring the scale of resource supply. This approach, however, neglects demand-side considerations; specifically, it often fails to incorporate key variables such as Patient affordability and is therefore limited in reflecting the actual efficiency of resource allocation. (2) The traditional method mainly uses local regression to analyze the influencing factors, ignoring the non-stationary changes in the spatial and temporal evolution of regional medical resources.

To address this gap, we employed the Coupling Coordination Degree (CCD) model to systematically examine the spatio-temporal evolution of health resource supply-demand matching across China and its 31 provinces. Furthermore, Geographically and Temporally Weighted Regression (GTWR) and Geodetector was applied to identify the main influencing factors of health resource allocation.

The CCD model is an effective and comprehensive evaluation tool for assessing the degree of balanced development of a region as a whole, reflecting the degree of interconnectedness and coordinated development between two or more systems [29]. Because the model is simple and easy to calculate, and the results are intuitive, it is widely used in empirical research on coupling coordination development among many systems, such as ecosystems [30], energy [31], urbanization [32], and demographics [33], in different regions. In the health resource supply-demand system, the balanced layout is not a simple averaging of the quantity but rather a coordination of supply-demand within each region. The GTWR model is an extension of the Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) model, incorporating a time dimension [34]. This allows for the analysis of influencing factors across both spatial and temporal dimensions. Although GTWR has been widely applied in studies of spatial distributions of influencing factors, its application in the field of medical resource allocation remains limited.

In summary, the primary goals of this study were to (1) construct a comprehensive evaluation indicator system for the supply and demand of health resources; (2) analyze the spatio-temporal characteristics of the coupling coordination of supply-demand; and (3) identify the main influencing factors of health resource allocation dynamics across different developmental periods. The research findings may provide evidence-based policy recommendations for imbalances in China’s healthcare sector.

Methods

Index system construction

Constructing an evaluation index system is more comprehensive compared to simply selecting a single input or output indicator for empirical analysis [35]. The assessment results are not only more reliable but also better reflect objective reality. Therefore, drawing on existing research [36, 37], this study follows principles such as comparability and objectivity, and seeks to expand and refine the current index system from both supply and demand perspectives, thereby enhancing the comprehensiveness and rationality of the constructed evaluation index system.

In our framework (Fig. 1), the supply system is a composite index comprising multiple indicators that reflect not only the quantity of resources (e.g., beds per 1000 population) but also their quality (e.g., daily average outpatient visits per doctor, as a higher volume generally implies stronger service delivery). In the construction of demand system, our primary focus was on assessing the alignment between the actual supply and actual utilization of medical resources. Accordingly, demand-side indicators are selected to reflect economic behaviors and service consumption that have already taken place. Furthermore, the selection of indicators strictly adheres to the core objective of the research, which is to “analyze the coordination of supply and demand of regional health resources”. For instance, since secondary and tertiary hospitals are the main providers of medical services in China [38], we select “hospital density” to quantify the supply capacity of “medical facilities”. As show in Table 1, we constructed a comprehensive evaluation indicator system, including secondary and tertiary indicators. Descriptive statistics of the indicators are presented in Appendix 1.

Data sources

All data were obtained from the Statistical Information Center of the National Health and Health Commission of China (http://www.nhc.gov.cn/mohwsbwstjxxzx/new_index.shtml).

We obtained study data for 31 provinces and cities in China (excluding Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan) from 2002 to 2021 years of statistical information provided by the center. Data gaps were only found for the indicator “Health Expenditure as a Share of GDP” in years 2002 to 2006, the overall missing rate is 24.22%. Given the minimal proportion of missing and the clear time-series nature of the data, we employed linear interpolation, a standard and widely accepted method in the academic literature, to impute the missing data [39].

Estimate annual average percentage change

Joinpoint 5.1.0 regression model [45] was used to estimate the average percentage change (APC), annual average percentage change (AAPC), and 95% confidence interval (CI) in health resource supply, demand, and CCD from 2002 to 2021. After the trend of the time change was segmented, the researcher could clearly see the rising, fast-rising, flat, falling, and fast-falling segments. In turn, the background and reasons for the segmentation points can be analyzed to provide a basis for future medical policy formulation and resource allocation.

Geographically and temporally weighted regression model

Traditional ordinary least squares (OLS) regression assumes spatial homogeneity, which constrains its capacity to capture spatial heterogeneity. In contrast: GWR addresses spatial non-stationarity by calibrating local regression equations across spatial units, revealing scale-dependent spatial variations between drivers and outcomes [46]. GTWR extends GWR by incorporating temporal dimensions, simultaneously modeling spatiotemporal non-stationarity in regression coefficients [47, 48]. The GTWR model was implemented using the dedicated GTWR add-in for ArcGIS. The selection of the optimal spatial bandwidth was automatically performed based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AICc), and the local regression estimation employed the Gaussian kernel function [49]. Following the iterative model optimization, the resulting optimal spatial bandwidth was determined to be 0.119069. To further confirm the model’s stability and robustness, we also evaluated the R2 value to ensure the reasonableness and reliability of the results.

To further explore the interaction mechanism of the determinants and combined impact on health resources, we introduced the Geodetector model to conduct a bivariate interaction analysis. This approach enables a quantitative assessment of synergistic or antagonistic relationships between variables [50]. Analysis was conducted using the Geodetector Software in Excel.

The temporal change of supply, demand and CCD

As shown in Fig. 3, the joinpoint regression model analysis shows that from 2002 to 2021, the temporal change trend of supply and demand shows three distinct phases: “rapid rise,” “steady development,” and “sudden decline.” The period of rapid increase in supply is 2002–2009, with an APC of 34.128% (P < 0.05); 2010–2019 is the period of stable development, during which the growth rate slows down, with an APC of 6.862% (P < 0.05); the period of 2020–2021 supply exhibits a statistically significant downward trend, with an APC of -7.266% (P < 0.05). With the mutual impact of supply and demand, the temporal changes in the CCD also showed three clear stages, similar to supply. From 2002 to 2008, CCD proliferated with an APC of 8.256% (P < 0.05), from 2009 to 2019, CCD developed steadily with an APC of 3.221% (P < 0.05); and from 2020 to 2021, CCD showed a decreasing trend with an APC of -3.670% (P > 0.05).

Determinants analysis

According to GTWR regression results (Fig. 8), the impact of various factors on medical resource supply-demand coupling coordination degree (CCD) exhibits significant spatiotemporal heterogeneity, with regression coefficients demonstrating robust statistical significance.

Spatial distribution of GTWR model’s regression coefficient

Population density exerts a steadily intensifying positive influence on medical resource CCD, characterized by a northwest-high to southeast-low coefficient gradient. During 2002–2010, high positive-impact clusters concentrated in northwestern and southwestern regions (e.g., Xinjiang, Qinghai, Guangdong). By 2014–2021, these high-value zones expanded eastward toward central and eastern regions, while low-value areas predominated along the eastern coast. The contradiction between supply and demand shows the characteristics of gradient transfer with population flow.

Urbanization rate demonstrates predominantly positive yet spatially fluctuating impacts. In 2002, coefficients clustered in low-positive zones. The 2006–2010 period saw high-value clusters expanding toward East and South China, evolving into eastern coastal concentrations during 2014–2021, with low-value zones persisting in western and central mountainous areas.

Educational attainment maintains primarily positive spatial influence. High-impact clusters initially centered in East and North China (2002), later expanding southward to central regions and educationally developed urban peripheries (2006–2010). By 2014–2021, these consolidated in eastern/central core cities, while low-value zones shifted westward.

Government support consistently exhibits positive spatial effects. High-impact clusters emerged in North/Northwest China (2002–2006), subsequently expanding toward central/eastern regions (2006–2014), and ultimately extending to eastern/southern cities (2014–2021).

Transportation accessibility has demonstrated a significant positive influence, exhibiting an “axial development” pattern. Cities located along major transportation corridors, such as the Beijing-Guangzhou and Longhai lines, have formed high-value clusters characterized by deep red coloration on spatial distribution maps. Notably, the development of the high-speed rail network since 2014 has substantially enhanced transportation accessibility in China’s central and western regions.

Discussion

In this study, we established a comprehensive evaluation index system to assess the health resources supply-demand. Using a comprehensive evaluation method, we quantified the CCD and categorized the coupling types of health resource supply-demand across China from 2002 to 2021. Subsequently, we applied the joinpoint model to identify the three developmental stages of health resource supply-demand dynamics. Finally, we employed the GTWR and Geodetector model to explore the determinants influencing the evolution of CCD in health resource allocation.

Three-stage changes in supply

From 2002 to 2021, the comprehensive evaluation of the health resource supply and demand showed an upward trend. The development of supply has experienced three stages: “rapid rise” (APC = 34.128%, 2002–2009), “steady development” (APC = 6.862%, 2009–2019), and “sudden decline” (APC=-7.266%, 2020–2021). From 2012 to 2019, the demand increased rapidly (APC = 7.171%). As the government is the main supplier of health resources in China, and public hospitals are the main carriers of supply and demand for all types of health resources, the development of health resource supply-demand is intimately related to reforms in healthcare. During the period of 2002–2009, the government successively promulgated the Decision on Further Strengthening Rural Health Work (Zhongfa [2002] No. 13) [54], the Plan for the Construction and Development of the Rural Health Service System (Weigui Caifa [2006] No. 340) [55], and the Guiding Opinions on the Development of Urban Community Health Services (Guofa [2006] No. 10) [56], which were aimed to strongly promote the construction of rural and urban community health care. China places great emphasis on the systematic integration and rational allocation of healthcare resources in the development of its healthcare service system. Since the launch of the new round of healthcare reform in 2009, the country has made concerted efforts to build a vertically integrated healthcare delivery system covering the entire life course, through comprehensive measures in policy guidance, resource distribution, information interoperability, and payment system reform. Compared to the UK’s general practitioner “gatekeeper” model, China’s approach places stronger emphasis on policy-driven institutional restructuring and targeted resource allocation. This is particularly evident in substantial investments in infrastructure, human resource development, and information technology, which have significantly strengthened the service capacity of primary care institutions [57]. The top-down integration model allows for a more rapid and coordinated response to national-level strategic objectives. From an international comparative perspective, the Chinese experience not only carries significant implications domestically but also provides a potentially valuable reference for global health governance. However, the 2020 public health emergency epidemic caused a global shock to health systems [58]. Particularly during the pandemic, China adopted tight “social prevention and control” measures [59], which contributed well to controlling the spread of the pandemic [60] but also limited the non-essential medical demands of residents [61, 62]. This is likely an important reason for the “sudden drop” in supply and demand between 2020 and 2021, and to some extent, it also affected the continuous release of the effectiveness of medical reform.

Spatial distribution characteristics of CCD

The analysis of the coupling coordination degree revealed that the supply-demand coordination of health resources in China exhibited a positive development trend from 2002 to 2021, increasing from 0.400 to 0.804 (AAPC = 3.732%, P >0.05), reaching a state of good coupling coordination by 2021. However, the overall coordination level remained moderate, and imbalances between supply and demand were still evident. From a spatial perspective, the degree of coordination in the supply-demand coupling of medical resources varied significantly across regions, displaying a stepwise decreasing pattern: eastern region >central region >western region >northeastern region. These findings are consistent with those of previous related studies [63]. Such gradients are not merely statistical observations but reflect the enduring impacts of national regional development policies and historical socioeconomic transformations. For instance, since the implementation of the Northeast Revitalization Strategy in the early 21st century, although some industrial cities have undergone economic restructuring, persistent population outflows have significantly altered healthcare demand, particularly amid aging populations and the rising prevalence of chronic diseases [64]. This demographic shift has exacerbated the mismatch between healthcare service supply and population needs, thereby constraining the coordination and operational efficiency of regional healthcare systems. In contrast, the “Rise of Central China” strategy and the “Western Development” policy have not only promoted regional economic and social development but also significantly increased investment in medical resources in underdeveloped areas of central and western China, including infrastructure construction, recruitment of medical personnel, and improvements in information technology [65, 66]. This also confirms the result in the hot spot analysis that “cold spot areas (such as western regions like Guangxi and Sichuan) disappeared in 2021”.

Underlying mechanisms of the determinants

The results of determinants Analysis based on GTWR model show that population density, urban rate, education level, government support and transportation accessibility are the main driving factors of CCD. High-population density areas have a stronger driving effect on the coupling of medical resource supply and demand, especially in the central and western regions. This phenomenon reflects two underlying mechanisms: first, central urban agglomerations enhance resource allocation efficiency through scale effects [67]; second, regions such as Xizang have amplified the influence of population factors due to policy preferences, such as targeted construction assistance. In contrast, the driving effect of traditionally densely populated areas in eastern China has weakened, suggesting that simple population growth may lead to diminishing marginal returns. The urbanization rate impacts CCD through two distinct mechanisms: first, urban expansion increases the population with access to medical facilities; second, the elevated demand for medical services in urban areas intensifies the pressure on available healthcare resources. In particular, the rapid urbanization of emerging urban agglomerations in China’s central and western regions has significantly challenged the carrying capacity of local medical infrastructure. The substantial improvement in educational level has significantly enhanced the alignment of CCD. A highly educated population not only drives greater demand for high-quality healthcare services but also contributes skilled professionals to the medical system. The government plays a direct role in influencing medical resource supply through fiscal inputs. The government investment coefficient in western regions such as Xizang and Inner Mongolia is notably high, reflecting the state’s strong commitment to supporting border areas via transfer payments and targeted policies. Due to geographical constraints, government investment has become a crucial mechanism for these specific regions to address deficiencies in healthcare provision. Transportation accessibility primarily influences the CCD pattern by altering the accessibility of medical resources. Results by Gecdetector model identifies education level as the core driver (q = 0.608) of the spatial heterogeneity in health resource allocation. It influences the system in two key ways: improving health literacy to drive higher-quality demand [68], and supporting a skilled healthcare workforce [69]. Importantly, the interaction detection analysis reveals that the strongest interaction occurs between education level and urbanization rate promoting talent agglomeration [70] and reinforcing urban areas as hubs for both demand and supply, creating a virtuous cycle that amplifies its dominant role.

Health resource allocation optimization strategy

The spatio-temporal dynamic analysis based on the GTWR model reveals significant spatial heterogeneity in the influencing factors and their effects on health resource distribution across different regions. This provides a scientific basis for formulating region-specific policy recommendations. (1) Promote the integration of health resources in eastern core city clusters. In economically developed regions like the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei, Yangtze River Delta, and Pearl River Delta areas, health departments should take the lead, in coordination with medical insurance bureau and medical treatment combination, to foster vertical integration between tertiary hospitals and primary care institutions. The sinking of high-quality medical resources should be achieved through multi-site physician practices, telemedicine, and digital platforms to form an efficient and coordinated healthcare service network. (2) Accelerate the establishment of regional medical centers in central areas. In order to address the uneven distribution of medical resources in central and rapidly urbanizing regions, provincial health departments, in collaboration with local governments, finance bureaus, and public hospitals, should expedite the development of regional medical centers. These centers are encouraged to partner with higher medical education institutions to create “medical-education synergy bases,” thereby enhancing the quality of talent cultivation through clinical teaching and ensuring a stable supply of healthcare professionals for the region. (3) Strengthen the foundational guarantees in western regions characterized by relatively weak infrastructure. In less-developed western areas, it is imperative for the government to spearhead initiatives aimed at reinforcing basic support systems by enhancing the fiscal transfer payment mechanism to facilitate healthcare infrastructure development. Furthermore, the establishment of transportation networks should be closely aligned with the strategic placement of regional medical centers. Additionally, a nationwide telemedicine network ought to be progressively developed to mitigate the physical barriers of time and space in accessing medical care, thereby improving the overall capacity of regional healthcare services. (4) Dynamically align resource allocation with population migration Trends. In regions with population outflow (e.g., northeastern region), resource efficiency should be improved through the consolidation of underutilized medical institutions. In contrast, for areas experiencing population inflow, health and urban planning must integrate population forecasting models to proactively guide the expansion and optimization of medical resources, ensuring that healthcare development keeps pace with demographic changes. (5) Finally, policy guidance and supervision mechanisms should be reinforced through the establishment of a comprehensive evaluation system and the promotion of inter-regional collaborative support programs to facilitate the balanced development of medical services across the country.

Limitations

There may exist unobserved variables, such as the floating population ratio, was not explored. The spatial distribution of migrant population is often highly heterogeneous and seasonally variable, making it challenging to capture its short-term impacts on local medical service demands under the current static framework. Future research, ideally leveraging long-term, high-frequency survey data, is needed to explore these effects in greater detail. Furthermore, future studies could employ more advanced, multi-system models [71] to deepen the understanding of the fundamental supply-demand relationship established in this paper.

Conclusions

The current conflict between supply and demand in China’s healthcare field is becoming increasingly prominent. The main aspect of the conflict lies in the irrationality of supply, especially the insufficient amount of health resources, low quality, and irrational structures and layouts. Although the coupling coordination of supply and demand for health resources has improved under reforms and policies, issues of poor degree and large regional disparities remain. Therefore, the Chinese government should be more firmly committed to supply-side healthcare reforms to narrow regional disparities in supply and improve the level of coupled coordination of supply and demand for health resources.
