Exploration of ZnO-Chitosan Nanoparticles to Enhance Defense Gene Expression in Rice against Brown Planthopper
Authors: Panatda Jannoey, Duangdao Channei, Kittisak Buddhachat, Thipkesorn Khantawet, Tanattha Prachakchit, Waranya Thiphkam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the effects of noncomposite and composite forms of ZnO-chitosan nanoparticles (ZnO-Chi-NPs) on the activation of defense-related genes against brown planthopper (BPH) infestation in rice seedlings. Composites of ZnO-Chi-NPs were prepared by a wet chemical process by combining commercial ZnO powder with chitosan pellets. Field emission scanning electron microscopy analysis revealed that the ZnO-Chi-NPs were uniformly distributed, with particle sizes ranging from 30 to 150 nm, consistent with a specific surface area of 4.97 m2/g obtained from the BET analysis. The synthesized composite ZnO-Chi-NPs did not promote rice growth, resulting in lower Zn accumulation in rice seedlings, and showed reduced activation of defense-related gene expression. In contrast, the noncomposite form of 10 ppm chitosan and 50 ppm ZnO exhibited higher seedling height and root length compared with those treated with the composite formulation. In addition, the noncomposite treatment induced higher expression levels of PR genes (PR2 and PR3) as well as genes involved in jasmonic acid and salicylic acid biosynthesis pathways, including allene oxide synthase and phenylalanine ammonia-lyase genes. Rice seedlings treated with the noncomposite formulation also demonstrated enhanced tolerance to BPH infestation. Furthermore, higher Zn accumulation was detected in rice seedlings grown under the noncomposite ZnO and chitosan treatment. Rice grown with ZnO-Chi-NP composites exhibited higher Zn accumulation at later growth stages, which may result in a slower Zn2+ release. These findings suggest that the noncomposite formulation of ZnO and chitosan more effectively activates both SA- and JA-mediated signaling pathways, resulting in the upregulation of PR2 and PR3 defense genes in response to BPH infestation. It is suggested that ZnO and chitosan nanoparticles could be developed as nanofertilizers or integrated with traditional fertilizers to enhance rice resistance against BPH infestation.

Introduction

In recent years, nanofertilizers containing nanoparticles (NPs) have been increasingly applied in agriculture. These nanofertilizers enhance plant resistance, reduce the incidence of diseases, and stimulate photosynthetic activity. NPs demonstrate rapid absorption by roots because of their high surface area-to-volume ratio and significant potential for improving agricultural productivity, providing a valuable alternative to conventional fertilizers. By contrast, conventional fertilizers exhibited low nutrient-use efficiency due to leaching, leading to economic losses and diminished soil fertility. −

Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs) and chitosan nanoparticles (Chi-NPs) are well-recognized nanomaterials due to their biodegradability, biocompatibility, nontoxicity, low allergenicity, and wide use in agriculture. − They have been extensively applied in agriculture, causing positive effects on plant growth. These nanoparticles have been reported to enhance rice seedling development and photosynthetic efficiency, as well as to promote stem elongation, stimulate root growth, and improve seed germination. − They readily penetrate plant tissues through roots, aerial parts, wounds, and root junctions, with low molecular weight. Chi-NPs demonstrate high performance bioavailability, extended half-life, and larger surface-area-to-volume ratio, enhancing nutrient uptake and contributing to increased productivity. −

In addition, ZnO-NPs have been reported to enhance photosynthesis, nutrient uptake, enzymatic activity, and defense-related gene expression, while also influencing carbon and nitrogen metabolism, regulating phytohormones and secondary metabolite pathways, and reinforcing the antioxidant defense system. ,, Similarly, Chi-NPs activate plant defense mechanisms by inducing the activity of key defense-related enzymes, including catalase, polyphenol oxidase, phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), and peroxidase.

Foliar application of ZnO-NPs in tobacco has been shown to elevate the levels of salicylic acid (SA) and abscisic acid (ABA) while upregulating key defense-related genes such as PAL, PR-1, CHS, and POD genes, thereby reducing viral and mitigating plant disease severity. Likewise, foliar application of Chi-NPs has been reported to significantly enhance soybean yield compared with conventional chitosan treatments, highlighting their potential as effective nanoformulations for sustainable crop improvement. , Metagenomic analyses of the rice microbiome following Chi-NP foliar application revealed a reduction in pathogenic genera such as Magnaporthe while promoting the abundance of beneficial microbes such as Thiobacillus, Nitrospira, Azonexus, Agarivorans, and Bradyrhizobium in the phyllosphere and Sphingomicrobium and Nocardioides in the rhizosphere.

Both NPs are also well recognized for their inhibitory effects against multiple phytopathogens in rice. ZnO-NPs showed inhibited Magnaporthe oryzae (rice blast), Xanthomonas oryzae pv oryzae (bacterial leaf blight), Rhizoctonia solani (sheath blight), Bipolaris oryzae (brown leaf spot), and Sphaerulina oryzina (narrow brown leaf spot). − Similarly, Chi-NPs exhibit strong antimicrobial activity against major rice pathogens such as Pyricularia grisea and X. oryzae, which cause blast and blight diseases, respectively. ,,

ZnO-NPs have been demonstrated to alleviate the adverse effects of salinity stress during the germination stage in various rice genotypes, thereby enhancing seedling establishment under abiotic stress conditions. Likewise, seed priming with Chi-NPs significantly improves the germination rate and seedling vigor and enhances biochemical and antioxidant defense responses under salinity stress conditions. ZnO-NPs improve the tolerance of Pleioblastus pygmaeus to heavy metals such as arsenic and mercury by activating antioxidant defense mechanisms and minimizing metal accumulation and translocation within plant tissues. In addition, ZnO-NPs have been reported to enhance plant defense responses and reduce larval damage caused by Tomato Tuta absoluta, Tobacco mosaic virus, and T. absoluta infestation. ,

Based on the individual beneficial effects of ZnO-NPs and Chi-NPs, this study also incorporates the advantages of both nanomaterials by synthesizing ZnO-Chi-NPs in a composite form. Composite ZnO-Chi-NPs were applied to rice seedlings to investigate rice interactions with the brown planthopper (BPH) compared to the noncomposite form.

BPH, a piercing-sucking insect pest, causes severe yield losses in rice production. However, no studies have reported evaluation of noncomposite and composite forms of ZnO-Chi-NPs for the management of BPH.

The molecular mechanisms underlying the defense responses of rice treated with these nanomaterials against BPH infestation were investigated through the analysis of defense-related gene expression, particularly pathogenesis-related (PR) genes and signaling molecule biosynthesis genes. Both groups of genes represent an essential key to the plant immune network, acting as crucial mediators in defense activation.

Among the defense-related genes, this study focused on PR genes, including PR1a, PR2, PR3, PR6, and PR15 genes. These PR genes were selected as representative markers of plant defense responses under stress conditions, whereas otherPR genes are more specifically associated with antibacterial, antifungal, antinematode, and antivirus defense mechanisms.

The PR1 gene plays an important role in plant responses to both biotic and abiotic stress, including drought and fungal infection. Overexpression of PtPR1a in Arabidopsis thaliana significantly enhanced resistance to Pseudomonas syringae, while PR1a overexpression in poplar increased resistance to Septotis populiperda. PR2 encodes β-1,3-glucanase, an enzyme that hydrolyzes β-O-glycosidic bonds of β-glucans in plant cell walls, contributing to cell wall loosening. β-1,3-Glucanases can function synergistically with chitinases to degrade cell walls. This mechanism may explain the cell wall leakage observed in rice during BPH infestation in this study. The PR3 gene encodes Chitinase, which is specifically responsive to chitosan supplementation in the rice growth medium and is one of the most extensively studied. The PR6 gene encodes protease inhibitor proteins that suppress digestion in the insect larval midgut by inhibiting gut proteases. The induction of PR6 gene expression by ZnO and chitosan may indicate that these nanoparticles can disrupt the digestive system of BPH, offering a potential alternative to chemical insecticides. The PR15 gene encodes oxalate oxidase, an enzyme involved in maintaining oxidative stress balance in plant cells and was used in this study as an indicator of oxidative damage in rice treated with ZnO and chitosan. PR15 gene expression is typically induced by reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation following pathogen attack.

Moreover, PR genes are known to be activated by different signaling molecules, including SA, jasmonic acid (JA), and ethylene. Therefore, this study also investigated key genes of JA signaling-related biosynthesis genes (allene oxide synthase (AOS) and LOX), Auxin responsive gene (AXR), and SA biosynthesis (PAL and isochorismate synthase (ICS)), whereas the ACO gene is an important gene associated with ethylene biosynthesis, respectively. These genes were analyzed comparatively to determine which signaling pathways were activated.

The findings highlight the potential of ZnO-NPs and Chi-NPs of noncomposite and composite form against BPH and may serve as bioinsecticides for controlling BPH infestations.

Materials and Methods

Materials

All chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) was obtained from Loba Chemie (India), while sodium tripolyphosphate (TPP) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Acetic acid was procured from a commercial supplier. Chitosan (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. 900344; DD 99%, Mw ∼ 100 kDa) and commercial ZnO powder (Sigma-Aldrich; maximum particle size ∼45 μm) were used as received. All reagents were employed without further purification.

Preparation of Chi-NPs

A solution was prepared by dispersing 1.00 g of commercial chitosan pellets in 1% (v/v) acetic acid solution. The mixture was sonicated for 30 min at room temperature prior to the gradual addition of 10.00 mL of sodium TPP cross-linking solution, which was introduced dropwise. The addition of TPP induced ionic interactions between the negatively charged phosphate groups of TPP and the positively charged amino groups of chitosan, thereby promoting the nanoparticle formation through ionotropic gelation.

Subsequently, the solution was subjected to ultrasonic treatment for 30 min at a power of 150 W and a frequency of 40 kHz using an ultrasonic bath, followed by standing for 3 h in a 60 °C water bath. The resulting product was obtained as a viscous gel solution.

Preparation of Composite ZnO-Chitosan Nanoparticles (ZnO-Chi-NPs)

The synthesis of composited ZnO-Chi-NPs was carried out with the incorporation of ZnO powder. Specifically, ZnO powder was mixed with chitosan pellets in a 1% v/v acetic acid solution. The resulting mixture was subjected to sonication for 30 min at a power of 150 W and a frequency of 40 kHz using an ultrasonic bath to ensure homogeneity. ZnO reacts with acetic acid to form soluble zinc acetate [Zn­(CH3COO)2], thereby releasing Zn2+ ions into the solution, as represented by the following equation: ZnO+2CH3COOH→Zn(CH3COO)2+H2O

The Zn2+ ions act as the metal centers of a coordination complex, with chitosan serving as the ligand through interactions between its –NH2 and –OH functional groups and the Zn2+ ions, as illustrated in Figure . An increase in the solution pH facilitates the formation of a more stable complex between chitosan and ZnO nanoparticles. ,

Schematic representation of the complexation reaction between the functional groups of chitosan and Zn2+ ions derived from ZnO nanoparticles.

A 1 M NaOH solution was gradually added dropwise under magnetic stirring, until the pH reached 10 (15.00 mL in this study). Subsequently, 10.00 mL of TPP cross-linking solution was introduced, and the mixture was further sonicated for 30 min at room temperature. The addition of TPP facilitated cross-linking between chitosan chains, leading to the formation of a stronger and more stable polymeric network, which enhanced the dispersion and immobilization of ZnO nanoparticles within the chitosan matrix.

The suspension was subsequently aged for 3 h in a water bath at 60 °C. The resulting white precipitate was recovered by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 10 min, washed several times with deionized water, and dried at 80 °C for 12 h.

Material Characterization

All materials, including commercial ZnO, Chi-NPs, and ZnO-Chi-NPs, were fully characterized. The morphological and physicochemical properties of the synthesized nanoparticles were evaluated by using various analytical techniques.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained to determine the crystalline nature and phase compositions (Philips analytical diffractometer) with Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (PerkinElmer). Particle size was measured using a Zetasizer Nano-ZS-90 instrument (Malvern Instruments). The morphology and sizes of the synthesized nanomaterials were identified by a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at an acceleration voltage of 10 kV, as well as energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) and elemental mapping modes. The surface areas of the samples were analyzed by a multipoint Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method using nitrogen adsorption/desorption isotherm measurements (Autosorb-1-MP Quantachrome Instruments).

Plant Material and Growth Conditions

Dehulled rice seeds were surface-sterilized using 15% (v/v) Clorox for 10 min twice, followed by a 10% Clorox treatment for 10 min. The seeds were then soaked in sterile water and gently shaken at 170 rpm for 5 min, with three replications. The sterilized seeds were placed in Petri dishes with covered lids and then transferred to the sterilized MS medium agar. Prior experiments were conducted to evaluate the toxicity of ZnO nanoparticles and Chi-NPs separately on rice seedlings, as presented in Tables S1 and S2. The optimum concentrations of ZnO, Chi-NPs, and ZnO-Chi-NPs with different treatments as shown in Table were used in this study.

The rice cultures of all treatments were cultured in an aseptic room under a photon flux density of 150 μmol m–2s–1, a 12-h light/dark cycle, 55–60% relative humidity, and 25 ± 2 °C for 6 weeks. Afterward, the rice seedlings were transferred from aseptic glass-bottom containers to pots and placed in a greenhouse for 7 days for the BPH infestation.

Evaluation of Rice Damage Severity Level Caused by BPH

The phenotypic evaluation of rice seedling resistance to BPH under different treatments was conducted as follows. Rice seedlings initially grown under aseptic conditions in glass bottles were transferred to pots and maintained in cages for 7 days to allow for acclimatization and growth. Subsequently, third-instar BPH nymphs were individually introduced into the rice plants at an infestation ratio of 8:1 (BPH nymphs/rice stem). The damage level score was taken when about 90% of the plants of the susceptible check (treatment 1; control) died for 14 days after infestation. Rice damage was monitored and evaluated according to the International Rice Research Institute standard rating scale for BPH infestation as follows: 0 = no visible damage (HR); 1 = partial yellowing of the first leaf (R); 2 = yellowing of the first leaf (R); 3 = partial yellowing of the first and second leaves (MR); 4 = yellowing of the first and second leaves (MR); 5 = pronounced yellowing with slight stunting (MS); 7 = wilting with severe stunting (S); and 9 = all test plants dead (HS). The damage scores were further classified as HR = highly resistant, R = resistant, MR = moderately resistant, MS = moderately susceptible, S = susceptible, and HS = highly susceptible. Rice plants from each treatment with BPH infestation were collected separately for RNA extraction. The damage scores were integrated with defense-related gene expression data to evaluate and support the effectiveness of each treatment against the BPH infestation.

Determination of Zinc Content in Rice Plants

Zinc (Zn) content in rice plant tissues and agar medium was determined according to AOAC Official Method 999.10, with slight modifications for plant samples. Briefly, rice plants with different treatment were sampling, thoroughly rinsed with deionized water to remove agar and oven-dried at 70 °C until a constant weight was obtained. The dried samples were ground into a fine powder using a grinder.

A total of 0.50 g of dried rice plant powder and agar medium was separately weighed into a microwave digestion vessel. The sample was digested with concentrated nitric acid by using a microwave digestion system. After digestion, the clear solution was cooled to room temperature, filtered, and diluted with deionized water. The zinc concentration in the digested solution was then quantified using high-resolution continuum source atomic absorption spectrophotometry (Analytik Jena, ContrAA800D, Germany). Calibration was performed by using Zn standard solutions, and reagent blanks were included throughout the analytical procedure. The Zn concentration was calculated based on the dry weight of the plant material and expressed as mg kg–1 dry weight. All analyses were performed in triplicate.

The percentage of zinc release from agar of each treatment was calculated using the following equation: % zinc release = C0−CtC0×100 .

C0: initial concentration of Zn at 0 days in the agar medium. C0 = initial concentration of Zn at 0 days in the agar medium. Ct = concentration of Zn at specific days in the agar medium.

Defense Response Gene Quantitative Real-Time PCR

Selected defense genes of PR1a (SCP-like extracellular protein), PR2 (β-glucanase), PR3 (Chitinase), PR6 (protease inhibitor), and PR15 (oxalate oxidase) were used. Signaling biosynthesis-related genes such as C 4 H (phenylpropanoid pathway-related cinnamate-4-hydroxylase), LOX (lipoxygenase), AOS, AXR (auxin-responsive protein), and ACO (ACC oxidase) were also used. ROS-responsive genes such as SOD (superoxide dismutase), TRX (thioredoxin I), and GST (glutathione transferase) were determined quantitatively by quantitative real-time PCR with specific forward and reverse primers. RNA was extracted from the 2 weeks rice seedlings that grew in different treatments according to the protocol of E.Z.N.A. Plant RNA Kit (Omega, USA). The purity and quantity of the RNAs were evaluated using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Agarose gel electrophoresis (1%) was used to determine the integrity of the isolated RNA. DNase I (Thermo Scientific, Lithuania) was applied to remove genomic DNA. cDNA was synthesized based on the instructions of the Maxime TM RT PreMix with the oligo-dT-15 primer kit (Intron Biotechnology, Korea).

The thermal cycling CFX-connect Real Time machine (Biorad, USA) was used for assays with a 20 μL reaction mixture containing 5 μL of cDNA template, 10 μL of HOT FIREPol Eva green SYBR Green (Solis biodyne, Biorad, USA), 1 μL of each 10 μM primer (forward or reverse primer), and 3 μL of water. The amplification procedure included an initial denaturation step of 95 °C for 12 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, a dedicated annealing temperature for each gene for 30 s and a final extension at 72 °C for 20 s.

The relative gene expression levels were calculated with the 2–ΔΔCt formula, where the control condition (Treatment 1) was used as the calibrator. Triosephosphate isomerase (Ti) gene was used as an internal reference gene. Three replicates of both biological and technical samples were analyzed for gene expression. Statistical analysis using SPSS software version 17 at p ≤ 0.05 was performed for mean comparisons.

Results and Discussion

XRD Analysis

The diffraction pattern of chitosan shown in Figure a exhibited a characteristic broad peak at 2θ ≈ 20.38°, which is typically indicative of the semicrystalline nature of chitosan. This peak arises from the carbon backbone of the polymer and is consistent with previously reported findings. The diffraction pattern in Figure b displayed distinct peaks at 2θ values of 31.79°, 34.45°, 36.28°, 47.53°, 56.60°, 62.84°, 66.41°, 67.91°, 69.07°, 72.56°, and 76.89°, corresponding to the (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), (103), (200), (112), (201), (004), and (202) crystallographic planes, respectively. No additional characteristic peaks were detected, confirming the purity of the synthesized ZnO. These results indicate that the obtained material was ZnO with a hexagonal wurtzite structure, in good agreement with the JCPDS card No. 36-1451. The reference data report hexagonal cell parameters of a = b = 3.249 Å and c = 5.206 Å. In Figure c, the characterization of ZnO-Chi-NPs revealed major peaks corresponding to both ZnO and chitosan. The intensity of peaks in the XRD pattern of the ZnO-Chi-NPs composite decreased compared to that in the XRD pattern of pure ZnO and chitosan, attributed to the coordination reaction between Zn2+ and chitosan functional groups.

XRD patterns of (a) chitosan, (b) ZnO, and (c) ZnO-Chi-NPs.

In addition, the decrease in the ZnO peak intensity may also indicate changes in the structural order of the composite, such as reduced crystallinity, peak broadening, and partial amorphization induced by the incorporation of chitosan into the ZnO crystal framework. These combined effects further confirm the formation of ZnO-Chi NPs with altered structural characteristics.

The average crystallite sizes of ZnO, chitosan, and the ZnO/chitosan nanocomposite were calculated using the Debye–Scherrer equation, as follows: D=kλβcos⁡θ where D represents the crystallite size (nm), k is the Scherrer constant (typically 0.94), λ is the wavelength of the X-ray radiation (Cu Kα = 0.1541 nm), β is the full width at half-maximum (FWHM, in radians) of the most intense diffraction peak, and θ is the corresponding Bragg angle.

The calculated average crystallite sizes were 2.08 nm for chitosan and 87.33 nm for ZnO, as summarized in Table . Following nanocomposite formation, the crystallite size of ZnO decreased to 8.91 nm, while that of chitosan remained at 3.10 nm. The reduction in the ZnO crystallite size is attributed to the coordination of chitosan molecules with ZnO nanoparticles, which introduces steric hindrance and restricts further crystal growth, thereby preventing the formation of larger crystallites.

The composite ZnO-Chi-NPs exhibited a larger surface area and pore volume than the individual components, accompanied by a notable reduction in pore diameter compared with pure ZnO and chitosan. The higher surface area, despite the smaller pore size, can be attributed to the presence of numerous fine pores that collectively contribute a greater total surface area per unit mass, a characteristic feature of nanoparticle-based materials.

FESEM-EDS Analysis

The FESEM images and EDS elemental analyses of Chi-NPs, ZnO, and composite ZnO-Chi-NPs samples are presented in Figure . The FESEM image in Figure a shows uniformly distributed, spherical chitosan NPs with sizes ranging from 20 to 70 nm. The inset illustrates the morphology of a chitosan pellet prior to nanoscale processing, characterized by a smooth surface and a sheetlike structure.

FESEM images (50,000×) and corresponding (a,d) EDS spectra of Chi-NPs, (b,e) ZnO, and (c,f) composite ZnO-Chi-NPs.

ZnO (Figure b) exhibited uniform hexagonal morphologies with slight agglomeration. The particles displayed well-defined hexagonal facets, with mean diameters of 100–200 nm and lengths of 150–500 nm. The average crystallite size of ZnO, estimated from the XRD data using the Scherrer equation, was 87.33 nm, which is smaller than the particle size observed by FESEM. This difference is expected because the crystallite size determined by XRD represents the average size of coherently diffracting crystalline domains, whereas FESEM measures the overall dimensions of the particles. Therefore, a single particle observed by FESEM may consist of several crystallites (i.e., a polycrystalline particle), resulting in a particle size larger than the corresponding crystallite size. In addition, slight particle agglomeration during the synthesis and drying processes may further contribute to the larger particle dimensions observed by FESEM. Similar discrepancies between crystallite size and particle size were determined by XRD.

Consequently, the values obtained from the two analytical techniques are not identical. The composite ZnO-Chi-NP surface (Figure c) exhibited a uniformly mixed morphology, distinct from that of pristine ZnO. The nanocomposite consisted of clustered structures with rough surfaces, with particle sizes ranging from 30 to 150 nm.

The surface roughness and small particle size of the nanocomposite contributed to enhanced surface adsorption. , This observation is consistent with the higher surface area of ZnO, as confirmed by BET N2 adsorption–desorption analysis. Similar results were reported by Jayasuriya et al. (2013) who developed novel composite ZnO-Chi-NPs films for biomedical applications. They observed that the incorporation of ZnO NPs increased surface roughness, which was associated with an enhanced surface area of the films. This increase in roughness was suggested to facilitate improved cell attachment by modifying the chitosan film surface.

EDS at a magnification of 50,000× was employed to investigate the elemental composition of chi-NPs, ZnO NPs, and the composite ZnO-Chi-NPs, as shown in Figure d–f. The EDS spectrum of Chi-NPs (Figure d) revealed the presence of C, O, and N, with weight percentages of 57.8%, 31.8%, and 10.5%, respectively. Analysis of ZnO-NPs (Figure e) showed only Zn (75.6%) and O (24.4%), with no other impurities detected. A minor peak at 2.15 keV was attributed to metallic Au, originating from the gold coating applied during SEM preparation to minimize charging effects. The EDS spectrum of composite ZnO-Chi-NPs (Figure f) displayed peaks corresponding to Zn and O, confirming the incorporation of ZnO-NPs. Additional peaks of C and N were attributed to the amine (−NH2) groups of chitosan, further validating the successful attachment of ZnO-NPs to the chitosan matrix. Additional elemental mapping of the composite ZnO-Chi-NPs (Figure ) revealed distinct color contrasts corresponding to Zn, O, C, and N, confirming their uniform distribution throughout the sample. This homogeneous distribution demonstrates the successful functionalization of ZnO with chitosan and the uniform dispersion of both components within the composite.

FTIR Analysis

FTIR analysis was performed to investigate the changes in functional groups during the synthesis of the nanocomposites. The FTIR spectra of ZnO, Chi-NPs, and ZnO-Chi-NPs are presented in Figure . In Figure a, the characteristic absorption band of ZnO was observed at 697.78 cm–1, corresponding to the Zn–O vibration, while the broad band centered at 3419 cm–1 was assigned to the O–H stretching vibrations of surface hydroxyl groups and physically adsorbed water molecules on the ZnO surface.

FTIR spectra of (a) pure ZnO, (b) pure chitosan, and (c) composite ZnO-Chi-NPs.

The FTIR spectrum of chitosan (Figure b) exhibited absorption bands between 3289.47 and 3836.48 cm–1, associated with the stretching vibrations of −OH and −NH groups. In addition, the band at 2869.64 cm–1 was assigned to the asymmetric stretching vibration of the −CH group.

In addition, the FTIR spectrum of chitosan showed a characteristic amide absorption band at 1590.21 cm–1, while the peaks at 1416.81 cm–1 and 1373 cm–1 were attributed to C–N stretching vibrations of the amine group.

Figure c shows the main differences in the ZnO-Chi-NPs FTIR spectrum compared to pure chitosan. The broad peak at 3353.83 cm–1 in the chitosan spectrum corresponded to stretching vibrations of the hydroxyl, amide, and amine groups. This peak shifted to lower wavenumbers after combination with ZnO (3360.74 cm–1), suggesting an interaction between these groups and ZnO. The FTIR spectrum of the composite ZnO-Chi-NPs showed bands corresponding to NH2 groups at 1593.04 cm–1 and 3360.74 to 3835.75 cm–1, exhibiting broadening and a decrease in intensity.

The peak at 1025.55 cm–1, associated with C–O stretching in the chitosan spectrum, shifted to a higher wavenumber of 1032.57 cm–1 in the nanocomposite spectrum, implying the immobilization of ZnO into chitosan. The peak at 1416.81 cm–1 in the pure chitosan spectrum, attributed to C–N stretching, shifted to 1415.11 cm–1 in the nanocomposite, suggesting the strong attachment between ZnO and the amide groups of chitosan molecules.

Size Distribution Analysis

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) was measured by a Malvern Zetasizer Nano-ZS instrument to analyze the key characteristic parameters of the nanosuspensions, specifically the particle size distribution and zeta potential. The results displayed in Figure illustrate the size distribution profiles of ZnO-NPs, Chi-NPs, and ZnO-Chi-NPs.

For Chi-NPs, the size was determined by the peak intensity at 295 nm, yielding a PDI value of 0.393 (Figure a) and suggesting the formation of nanoparticles. In Figure b, ZnO exhibited an average particle size of 335 nm and a ζ-potential of +32.5 mV. Conversely, the composite ZnO-Chi-NPs exhibited larger particle sizes (420 nm) and a lower ζ-potential (+8.23 mV) compared to the single ZnO-NPs, as illustrated in Figure c. The decrease in the ζ-potential or surface charge parameter of ZnO-NPs after composite formation with chitosan was attributed to the interaction between chitosan functional groups and Zn2+ ions from the ZnO-NPs, along with the –NH2 and –OH groups of chitosan.

These results concur with Castiglione et al. (2023) reported that a lower concentration of ZnO (10 ppm) significantly promoted root length compared with the control, while a higher concentration of ZnO (100 ppm) resulted in a decrease in root length. Boonyanitipong et al. (2011) also found that high concentrations of ZnO (100–1000 ppm) suppressed root elongation and decreased the number of roots, while a low concentration (10 ppm) enhanced root length after 7 days under sterile conditions.

Li et al. (2016) suggested that excessive levels of zinc and other metals in the growing medium disrupt root apex homeostasis by causing an imbalance in ROS, leading to oxidative damage. Zinc functions as a cofactor and an activator of key enzymes and transcription factors. Fluctuations in zinc concentration influence various metabolic pathways including hormone regulation and auxin biosynthesis. Wang et al. (2021) demonstrated that low concentrations of zinc enhanced auxin levels, promoting root elongation in rice, whereas higher concentrations had the opposite effect.

Moreover, several studies have confirmed that plant root growth can be stimulated by low concentrations of chitosan, whereas higher concentrations suppress root development of Arabidopsis, , chili, and grapevine.

Previous studies have also reported that high concentrations of ZnO-NPs can inhibit plant growth, biomass accumulation, and photosynthesis. For example, elevated ZnO-NP levels negatively affected phosphorus uptake in rice, caused chloroplast damage, and reduced photosynthetic efficiency in barley. Moreover, Srivastav et al. (2021) found toxicity symptoms in wheat at higher ZnO-NP concentrations (150–200 mg L–1), which were attributed to zinc-induced disruptions in homeostasis and indirect effects on the uptake and interactions of other essential elements. Previous studies suggested that elevated levels of nanoparticles interfere with plant signaling pathways. For example, JA, a key signaling molecule involved in the activation of PR genes, was suppressed in the presence of higher nanoparticle concentrations. However, the inhibitory effect of high concentrations of chitosan on plant growth was not observed in soil or field experiments but was evident in hydroponic or nutrient-based systems.

These results may be related to differences in Zn2+ release from the growth medium into rice seedlings between the composite ZnO-Chi-NPs and the noncomposite forms. Higher zinc accumulation in rice seedlings may induce oxidative stress, leading to ROS generation and subsequent activation of defense-related genes.

The limited release of Zn2+ ions from the composite form may result in a lower activation of gene expression. The chitosan matrix acts as a diffusion barrier; it also acts as a chelating polymer, binding Zn2+ through amine (−NH2) and hydroxyl (−OH) groups (Figures and c) which may delay Zn2+ ion liberation into the surrounding medium. This hypothesis is consistent with the results shown in Figure a; the composite formulations (treatments 4 and 6) released a lower percentage of Zn from the agar medium than the noncomposite formulations (treatments 3 and 5) during the first 2 weeks of rice seedling growth. Zn release was not detected in treatments 1 and 2 because these treatments did not contain ZnO.

Moreover, the zinc content in rice seedlings grown under the composite forms (treatments 4 and 6) was lower than that observed in the noncomposite forms (treatments 2 and 5) (Figure b). The zinc content in rice grown in 50 ppm of noncomposite ZnO (treatment 3) was significantly higher than that observed in rice treated with 50 ppm of ZnO combined with chitosan (treatment 4) during the first 2 weeks (Figure ). A similar result was observed at the 100 ppm ZnO concentration, where rice treated with 100 ppm of noncomposite ZnO (treatment 5) exhibited higher zinc accumulation than rice treated with the composite 100 ppm ZnO-chitosan formulation during the early two-week period. This result indicated that noncomposite treatments release higher zinc at early weeks of rice growing, to supplement rice seedling, subsequently inducing activation of gene expression. The higher zinc content in rice seedlings may promote higher gene expression due to increased ROS generation induced by zinc exposure.

The PR15 gene was monitored to investigate ROS-related responses in rice seedlings following treatment with ZnO and chitosan (Figure m). The PR15 gene encodes oxalate oxidase enzyme, which eliminates ROS by catalyzing the oxygen-dependent degradation of oxalate, producing 1 mol of H2O2 and 2 mol of CO2. In the early stage, oxalate does not directly eliminate ROS but instead acts as a substrate to produce H2O2, which functions as a defense signaling molecule in plants. In addition, PR15 is classified as a germin-like protein and has been reported to exhibit superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, thereby contributing to the oxidative stress responses.

Treatment 2 and Treatment 5 exhibited higher expression of the PR15 gene compared with the other treatments and the control. These results suggest that high-level H2O2, which is the product of oxalate oxidase, was found in the noncomposite forms of both treatments and then elevated activating defense-related gene expression. Previous results showed that rice seed priming and foliar application with H2O2 improved yield components. The tiller numbers, number of panicles, number of filled grains, filled grain weight, and harvest index were improved and activated multiple stress tolerance mechanisms. This resulted in, related to the physiological data as shown in Figure , seedling height under treatment 2 was slightly greater than that of the other treatments, although it was not significantly different from the control group.

The expression profiles of other ROS-responsive genes are presented in Figure . Higher expression levels of SOD and POX genes were also observed in treatments 2 and 5. In contrast, treatments 4 and 6, which involved the composite formulations, exhibited downregulation of oxidative stress-related genes, including SOD, POX, GST, and TRX genes. These findings suggest that the noncomposite forms in treatments 2 and 5 contained higher Zn levels, which may have induced higher ROS accumulation and subsequently activated defense-related genes, particularly PR genes, during the early stages of plant growth.

Among the PR genes, PR2 and PR3 showed the highest levels of induction, particularly in response to treatment 5 (Figure b,c,f), while PR6 and PR15 were significantly moderately upregulated (Figure d,e). PR1a exhibited a lower level of almost gene expression compared to the other genes (Figure a–f).

The PR2 gene encodes 1,3-β-glucanase, an enzyme that hydrolyzes the β-O-glycosidic bonds in callose, a β-1,3-glucan polymer with β-1,6 branches present in plant cell walls. 3-β-Glucanase reactions release glucan oligosaccharide fragments which act as elicitors to trigger immune responses in rice seedlings, particularly against M. oryzae. In addition to its role in defense signaling, the PR2 gene is also involved in the synthesis, remodeling, and turnover of cell wall components, serving as a structural barrier during pathogen attacks, as reported in Arabidopsis. Oide et al. (2013) also demonstrated that the PR2 gene plays a role in callose deposition, particularly when ABA treatment against Leptosphaeria maculans and P. syringae. PR2 knockout mutants showed impaired callose deposition, suggesting that PR2 may act as a modulator of callose deposition and cell wall remodeling.

The PR3 gene, which encodes the Chitinase enzyme, was significantly upregulated by up to 4.93-fold, particularly in treatment 5 compared to the other treatment (Figure c,f). Chitinase is a chitin-degrading enzyme that enhances plant resistance against insects and fungal pathogens by targeting chitin-containing structures of plant cell walls and insect exoskeletons. Overexpression of Chitinase in A. thaliana improved resistance to Plasmodiophora brassicae, increased host survival and seed yield, and upregulated immune-related gene expression, contributing to broad-spectrum disease resistance. Suwanchaikasem et al. (2023) reported that colloidal chitosan significantly enhanced defense responses in Cannabis sativa roots by increasing the total Chitinase activity and the expression of PR genes. Wang et al. (2021) found that the treatment of rice seedlings with long-chain chito-oligosaccharides led to the upregulation of the PR2 and PR3 genes, further supporting the role of these enzymes in plant immune responses. It may exert a synergistic effect on enhancing rice protection against BPH in this study.

However, the results showed that the PR1a, PR6, and PR15 genes were not significantly upregulated following ZnO-NP and Chi-NP application compared to the other genes (Figure ). The highest expression levels observed were 1.32-fold (PR1a), 2.27-fold (PR6), and 2.98-fold (PR15), indicating that these genes did not play a major role in the defense response of rice treated with ZnO and Chi-NPs.

The PR1a gene plays a critical role in plant immunity activation. The overexpression of the PR1a gene in A. thaliana significantly enhanced resistance to P. syringae and improved resistance to S. populiperda. The limited activation of the PR1a gene in this study suggested that ZnO and chitosan treatments did not trigger an immune response via the PR1a gene.

The PR6 gene encodes protease inhibitors that are typically involved in defense against herbivores by inhibiting digestive enzymes in the insect gut. The lower significant upregulation of PR6 in rice plants treated with ZnO NPs and Chi NPs. This suggested that both nanoparticles did not accumulate sufficient levels of protease inhibitors to effectively impair insect digestion against BPH.

In addition, signaling molecules, including JA, SA, ethylene, and auxin, are important components of plant defense-response mechanisms. When ZnO and chitosan induce ROS production, particularly H2O2, generated H2O2 acts as a signaling molecule that subsequently triggers phytohormone signaling pathways. These signaling molecules further activate the expression of defense-related genes in the plants.

This study found that the SA-related biosynthetic genes such as PAL and ICS showed notable activation (Figure g,h), in combination with the JA-related genes AOS and lipoxygenase (LOX) and 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid synthase (ACC) of ET-related gene (Figure j–l). In contrast, auxin-responsive genes (AXR) were downregulated in almost all treatments when compared to the control (Figure m).

Among these, PAL, ICS, and AOS exhibited the highest expression levels, at 6.01-, 4.00-, and 4.58-fold increases, respectively, particularly in treatments 2 and 5 (Figure g,h,j), suggesting that synergistic activation of SA- and JA-mediated signaling pathways enhanced defense responses in rice after treatment with ZnO and Chi-NPs. Conversely, the AXR gene, associated with auxin signaling, was downregulated across all treatments (Figure m), indicating a possible antagonistic interaction between auxin signaling and the induced defense responses triggered by nanoparticle treatments.

Moreover, the upregulation of the PAL and C4H genes (Figure g) observed in this study may activate the lignin-biosynthesis pathway. Lignin strengthens plant cell walls and plays a critical role in physical defense against pathogens. The upregulation of PAL activity observed in tea leaves and rice cell suspension cultures treated with Chi-NPs, acting as nanoelicitors, led to increased accumulation of phenolics and flavonoids. , Similarly, ZnO-NPs enhanced PAL activity, boosted defense-related enzyme activities, and increased lignin content in cotton, thereby improving resistance against Verticillium wilt. Zhu et al. (2025) reported that ZnO-NP treatment in cotton increased lignin content and modulated hormone levels, including elevated auxin, SA, JA, and melatonin while reducing ethylene levels.

Yang et al. (2022) reported that OsPAL1 and OsPAL6 genes were plant defense response marker genes that play crucial roles in activating defense mechanisms. Upregulation of PR genes, key transcription factors, and PAL genes in rice leads to the enrichment of defense-related pathways, which collectively contribute to resistance against rice sheath blight caused by R. solani infection. Therefore, the activation of the PAL gene observed in this study may contribute to the induction of lignin biosynthesis through the SA and JA signaling pathways against rice pathogens such as BPH. These results were consistent with Figure , which showed that rice grown under treatments 2 and 5 exhibited higher tolerance to BPH infestation compared to the other treatments.

PR Genes and Signaling Molecule-Related Gene Biosynthesis after BPH Infestation

The rice seedling–BPH interaction in this study was conducted to determine the expression of defense-related genes specifically associated with BPH infestation. After BPH attack, the expression levels of PR genes, including PR1a, PR2, PR3, and PR15, were upregulated compared with those in seedlings without BPH infestation (Figure a–d). These results suggest that rice seedlings activate PR genes in response to BPH attack, particularly the PR2 gene.

The PR2 gene showed the highest expression after BPH attack (Figure b) with a fold increase of 11.27, followed by PR15 (5.8-fold), PR1a (2.49-fold), and PR3 (1.2-fold), respectively. Rice seedling treatment with Treatment 5 (100 ppm of ZnO-NPs) still had the highest PR2 gene expression at 11.27-fold compared to other treatments, respectively (Figure b).

The PAL, ICS, C4H, AOS, and LOX genes related to signaling biosynthesis pathways were activated following BPH infestation across all treatments (Figure e–i), with the exception of ACC and AXR (Figure j,k), which showed no significant expression changes. The most significantly increased expression levels after BPH infestation were observed in AOS (3.10-fold), PAL (2.27-fold), ICS (1.9-fold), and LOX (1.27-fold) compared to rice without BPH infestation. These findings suggested that the JA and SA signaling pathways were more strongly activated than the ethylene- and auxin-responsive pathways in rice treated with ZnO and Chi-NPs following BPH attack.

Moreover, the enhanced BPH tolerance observed in rice grown under treatment 5 (Figure ) may be associated with the higher expression of the PR2 gene, potentially resulting from the synergistic effects of JA and SA signaling pathways against BPH infestation.

Previous reports found that SA and JA are key hormonal signaling pathways involved in plant resistance, with the JA pathway playing a particularly vital role in defense responses against herbivorous insects. JA regulates plant defense mechanisms and contributes to plant growth and development. Deng et al. (2022) demonstrated that increased expression of OsAOS and OsPAL in rice after infestation by the rice leaf-folder (Cnaphalocrocis medinalis) reflected elevated levels of JA and SA accumulation. Interestingly, silencing of OsAOS did not compromise rice resistance to BPH, suggesting that distinct herbivores trigger specific and independent signaling cascades. Herbivore infestation elicits differentiated defense responses in rice, with the JA and SA pathways playing pivotal roles in mediating resistance.

The other previous studies which supported the findings of this research demonstrated that infestation by gravid BPH females systemically activated also JA- and SA-dependent defense responses in rice. These defense responses include the accumulation of endogenous metabolites such as proteinase inhibitors, callose, secondary metabolites (e.g., terpenes, alkaloids, and flavonoids), and volatile organic compounds, all of which contribute to enhanced resistance in rice. Infestation by Chilo suppressalis in rice triggers the upregulation of JA-responsive genes associated with the biosynthesis of proteinase inhibitors, volatiles, and other defense-related compounds. By contrast, ET signaling was negatively regulated during BPH infestation. Ma et al. (2019) reported that BPH attack leads to the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of OsEIL1, a key ethylene-responsive transcription factor, thereby suppressing ET signaling during herbivore stress.

However, Ali et al. (2018) found that biotrophic pathogen attack also activates the SA pathway, which promotes transcription of NPR1 (nonexpressor of pathogenesis-related gene 1). NPR1 subsequently induces SA-responsive genes such as PR1 and PR2, leading to their accumulation and contributing to systemic acquired resistance.

These findings support the role of the noncomposite forms of ZnO and Chi-NPs in enhancing biochemical defenses against BPH through activation of JA- and SA-mediated signaling pathways and PR genes compared with the composite forms.

Conclusions

Our results demonstrated that newly synthesized composite ZnO-Chi-NPs did not promote rice growth and lower activate defense gene expression compared to the noncomposite form. The release of zinc from the composite form into rice seedlings was limited in the early stages of rice growth. However, the advantage of the synthesized composite exhibiting higher zinc accumulation was observed at later growth stages.

The noncomposite forms of ZnO more effectively released zinc into rice tissues, thereby promoting rice growth and inducing higher expression of defense-related genes compared with the composite forms.

When comparing the noncomposite treatments of ZnO and Chi-NPs, the low concentration of Chi-NPs (treatment 2) promoted rice growth, moderately induced defense-related gene expression, and improved rice seedling survival under BPH infestation. In contrast, the high concentration of ZnO (treatment 5) did not significantly promote rice growth compared with the control but markedly enhanced defense-related gene expression and increased rice tolerance to BPH attack. These findings suggest that ZnO treatments induced stronger defense-related gene expression both before and after the BPH infestation.

The proposed mechanism identified in this study suggests that the noncomposite forms of ZnO and chitosan can penetrate rice tissues and subsequently trigger ROS production, as evidenced by the upregulation of PR15 gene and other oxidative stress-related genes. The elevated ROS levels may contribute to the activation of JA and SA signaling pathways through the enhanced expression of AOS, PAL, and ICS genes against BPH. These signaling pathways subsequently stimulate the expression of defense-related genes, particularly PR2 and PR3. The encoded enzymes, β-1,3-glucanase (PR2) and Chitinase (PR3), may contribute to plant immune responses by promoting pattern-triggered immunity as shown in Figure . The oligosaccharides generated from their enzymatic activities may further function as microbe-associated molecular patterns, which are recognized by pattern recognition receptors on the plant cell surface. This study highlights the potential application of noncomposite ZnO and Chi-NPs as effective biopesticides for enhancing rice defense responses against BPH.
