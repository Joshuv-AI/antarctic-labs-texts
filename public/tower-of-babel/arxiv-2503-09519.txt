Simple and accurate approximations to the Riemann zeta function
Alexey Kuznetsov

Abstract
We develop approximations to the Riemann zeta function that enable high-precision computation in the critical strip and in other vertical strips. These approximations combine the main sum in the Riemann-Siegel formula with a simple approximation to the remainder term involving only elementary functions and certain precomputed coefficients obtained by Gaussian quadrature. We also derive approximations for the derivative of the Riemann zeta function and present extensive numerical evidence demonstrating the accuracy of these approximations.



Keywords
: Riemann zeta function, Riemann-Siegel formula, Gaussian quadrature, high-precision

algorithm

Introduction and main results

There exist many methods for computing the Riemann zeta function. One of the simplest is the Euler-Maclaurin summation method

[
3
,
13
,
26
]
, which is easy to implement, provides rigorous error bounds, and allows for high-precision computation of
\zeta(s)
. However, a major drawback of this method is that it requires summing
O(|s|)
terms, making it impractical for large values of
s
. Another highly efficient algorithm, developed by Borwein
[
4
]
, also has rigorous error bounds and enables high-precision computation of
\zeta(s)
when
\textnormal{Im}(s)
is not large.

For values of
s
with large
\textnormal{Im}(s)
, the preferred method is the Riemann-Siegel formula and its various extensions. The original Riemann-Siegel formula
[
3
,
6
,
26
,
27
]
was developed for computing
\zeta(s)
on the critical line
\textnormal{Re}(s)=1/2
. It consists of a main sum of
N_{t}:=\lfloor\sqrt{t/(2\pi)}\rfloor
terms (here and throughout this paper we denote
s=\sigma+{\textnormal{i}}t
and assume that
t>0
) and a remainder term, which can be expressed in terms of certain integrals or expanded as an asymptotic series. Keeping the first
j
terms of this asymptotic series results in an error of
O(t^{-\frac{1}{4}-\frac{j}{2}})
(see
[
6
]
for rigorous and effective upper bounds for these errors). Considerable work has been devoted to extending and improving the Riemann-Siegel formula. Odlyzko and Schönhage
[
22
]
introduced a fast algorithm for evaluating
\zeta(s)
at multiple points, which is particularly useful for locating the zeros of the Riemann zeta function on the critical line. Hiary

[
10
]
developed an algorithm that reduces the complexity of evaluating
\zeta(1/2+{\textnormal{i}}t)
at a single point to
O(t^{4/13+o(1)})
, compared to the standard Riemann-Siegel formula’s complexity of
O(t^{1/2})
. A simpler version of Hiary’s algorithm
[
10
]
achieves complexity of
O(t^{1/3+o(1)})
with minimal memory requirements.
Another algorithm due to Hiary
[
11
]
produces Riemann-Siegel-type approximations (requiring
O(t^{1/2+o(1)})
terms) by starting from the Euler-Maclaurin formula.
Smoothed versions of the Riemann-Siegel formula have been derived in
[
2
,
16
,
24
,
26
]

and a Riemann-Siegel-type formula for computing
\zeta(s)
for general values of
s
(not necessarily on the critical line) was stated in
[
22
]
, with error estimates developed in
[
5
]
.

In this paper, we construct approximations to
\zeta(s)
that are valid in arbitrary vertical strips, including the critical strip, for large values of
\textnormal{Im}(s)
. Our method is conceptually similar to the approach of Galway
[
7
]
, whose algorithm computes
\zeta(s)
by numerically evaluating the integrals that appear in the error term of the Riemann-Siegel formula. Galway’s approximations can yield highly accurate results, but their implementation requires careful tuning, as they depend on three parameters
\{z_{1},z_{2},M\}
that must be appropriately chosen to achieve the best accuracy. In contrast, our approximations depend on a single integer parameter
p
along with certain precomputed complex coefficients
\{\omega_{p,j}\}_{0\leq j\leq p}
and
\{\lambda_{p,j}\}_{1\leq j\leq p}
. Our approximations allow high-precision computation of
\zeta(s)
in the critical strip, and near it, for values of
|\textnormal{Im}(s)|>100
. High-precision computations of the Riemann zeta function are not merely of theoretical interest; they can lead to significant mathematical discoveries. For example, computations of the nontrivial zeros of
\zeta(s)
to high precision were a crucial ingredient in the disproof of Mertens conjecture

[
23
]
.

Given the numbers
\{\omega_{p,j}\}_{0\leq j\leq p}
and
\{\lambda_{p,j}\}_{1\leq j\leq p}
, we define

{\mathcal{I}}_{M,p}(s):=\omega_{p,0}M^{-s}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}\omega_{p,j}\Big[e^{-2\pi M\lambda_{p,j}}\big(M+{\textnormal{i}}\lambda_{p,j}\big)^{-s}+e^{2\pi M\lambda_{p,j}}\big(M-{\textnormal{i}}\lambda_{p,j}\big)^{-s}\Big],

(1)

where
M>0
and
s\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
For a function
f:{\mathbb{C}}\mapsto{\mathbb{C}}
we denote

\bar{f}(s):={\overline{f(\bar{s})}}.

(2)

Clearly, if
f
is an entire function of
s
, then so is
\bar{f}
. For
p\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
N\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
we introduce

F(s;N,p):=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}n^{-s}+\chi(s)\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}n^{s-1}-\frac{(-1)^{N}}{2}\Big[{\mathcal{I}}_{N+\frac{1}{2},p}(s)+\chi(s)\overline{{\mathcal{I}}}_{N+\frac{1}{2},p}(1-s)\Big],

(3)

where
\overline{{\mathcal{I}}}_{N+\frac{1}{2},p}(\cdot)
is defined via (
2
) and

\chi(s):=\frac{(2\pi)^{s}}{2\cos(\pi s/2)\Gamma(s)}.

(4)

Our approximations to the Riemann zeta function are given by

\zeta_{p}(s):=F(s;N_{t},p),

(5)

where
N_{t}=\lfloor\sqrt{t/(2\pi)}\rfloor
.

These approximations
\zeta_{p}(s)
are very easy to evaluate. They require only the precomputed values of
\{\omega_{p,j}\}_{0\leq j\leq p}
and
\{\lambda_{p,j}\}_{1\leq j\leq p}
, along with elementary functions such as the exponential function and the logarithm function, as well as the Gamma function (which is needed to compute
\chi(s)
). The Gamma function can be efficiently computed to high precision via the Stirling series
[
14
]
. A MATLAB implementation for computing
\zeta_{8}(s)
is provided in Appendix B.
The main question now is: how closely do these functions
\zeta_{p}(s)
approximate
\zeta(s)
? While we do not offer any rigorous error bounds, we aim to demonstrate the accuracy of these approximations through extensive numerical experiments.

To test the accuracy of these approximations in a vertical strip
a\leq\textnormal{Re}(s)\leq b
, we aim to compute

\max\limits_{a\leq\sigma\leq b}\big|\zeta_{p}(\sigma+{\textnormal{i}}t)-\zeta(\sigma+{\textnormal{i}}t)\big|

and plot this quantity as a function of
t
. Since computing the exact maximum is impractical, we approximate it by evaluating the function at one hundred points
\sigma_{k}
equally spaced on the interval
[a,b]
. Thus, we define

\Delta_{p}(t;a,b):=\max\limits_{0\leq k\leq 100}\big|\zeta_{p}(\sigma_{k}+{\textnormal{i}}t)-\zeta(\sigma_{k}+{\textnormal{i}}t)\big|,\;{\textnormal{ where }}\;\sigma_{k}=a+(b-a)k/100.

The choice of
100
points in our definition of
\Delta_{p}
is arbitrary and not essential. Using
50
or
200
points would produce nearly identical numerical results. When focusing on the critical strip, we simplify the notation and write
\Delta_{p}(t):=\Delta_{p}(t;0,1)
.

To compute
\Delta_{p}(t;a,b)
, we require benchmark values of
\zeta(s)
computed to sufficiently high precision. These were obtained using Galway’s method
[
7
]
(see Section
2

[
1

arbitrary-precision package.

2
Deriving the approximations and computing
\omega_{p,j}
and
\lambda_{p,j}

We begin with the following result: for every integer
N\geq 0
and
s\in{\mathbb{C}}

\zeta(s)=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}n^{-s}+\chi(s)\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}n^{s-1}-\frac{(-1)^{N}}{2}\Big[{\mathcal{I}}_{N+\frac{1}{2}}(s)+\chi(s)\overline{{\mathcal{I}}}_{N+\frac{1}{2}}(1-s)\Big],

(6)

where

{\mathcal{I}}_{M}(s):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{e^{-\pi x^{2}-2\pi M\theta x}}{\cosh(\pi\theta x)}\Big(M+\frac{x}{\theta}\Big)^{-s}{\textnormal{d}}x,

(7)

and
\theta:=\exp(-\pi{\textnormal{i}}/4)
.
The function
\overline{{\mathcal{I}}}_{M}
is defined via (
2
).
The above result is equivalent to formulas (1.1), (1.3) and (1.4) in
[
7
]
, after a change of variable of integration

z=N+1/2+\theta x
in
[
7
]
[equation (1.1)]. It can also be derived from formulas (1.1) and (3.2) in

[
5
]
.

Formula (
6
) was used by Galway
[
7
]
to derive a numerical quadrature algorithm for computing
\zeta(s)
. We used a special case of Galway’s approximations to compute the benchmark values of
\zeta(s)
and
\zeta^{\prime}(s)
. We approximate
{\mathcal{I}}_{M}(s)
by

{\mathcal{I}}_{M}(s;h):=h\sum\limits_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\frac{e^{-\pi(kh)^{2}-2\pi M\theta kh}}{\cosh(\pi\theta kh)}\Big(M+\frac{kh}{\theta}\Big)^{-s},

(8)

where
h>0
. Note that for
M\geq 1/2
, the integrand in (
7
) is analytic in a strip
|\textnormal{Im}(x)|<\pi/\sqrt{2}
and decays very rapidly as
|x|\to\infty
.
According to Lemma 2.1 and estimates (2.2) and (2.3) in
[
7
]
, for any
s\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
M\geq 1/2
and
c\in(0,\pi/\sqrt{2})
we have

{\mathcal{I}}_{M}(s;h)-{\mathcal{I}}_{M}(s)=O\big(\exp(-c/h)\big),\;\;\;h\to 0^{+}.

(9)

This result also follows from
[
28
]
[Theorem 5.1]. The implied constant in the big-O term depends on
s
,
M
and
c
.

We define for
N\geq 0

G(s;N,h):=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}n^{-s}+\chi(s)\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}n^{s-1}-\frac{(-1)^{N}}{2}\Big[{\mathcal{I}}_{N+\frac{1}{2}}(s;h)+\chi(s)\overline{{\mathcal{I}}}_{N+\frac{1}{2}}(1-s;h)\Big].

Formula (
9
) implies that for any non-negative integer
N
we have
G(s;N,h)\to\zeta(s)
as
h\to 0^{+}
, so that we have an infinite family of approximations to
\zeta(s)
, allowing us to choose (for every
s
) the one that is easiest to compute. It is well known
[
7
,
27
]
that the optimal choice is
N=N_{t}=\lfloor\sqrt{t/(2\pi)}\rfloor
(recall that
s=\sigma+{\textnormal{i}}t
and
t>0
). This choice ensures that the saddle point of the integrand in (
7
) is as close as possible to the real line, so that the resulting integral is easy to evaluate numerically.
We define
\zeta(s;h)=G(s;N_{t},h)
. This is how we compute the benchmark values of
\zeta(s)
to high precision. Galway
[
7
]
developed more general (and more efficient) approximations, but this simpler version suffices for our purposes. To implement
\zeta(s;h)
, we used Stirling’s series algorithm for computing the Gamma function
[
14
]
and truncated the infinite sum in (
8
) once the terms became smaller than
10^{-d}
, where
d
is the working precision. To illustrate the accuracy of this approximation, we conducted the following experiment. We downloaded the value of the 30th zero
1/2+{\textnormal{i}}\gamma_{30}
of the Riemann zeta function on the critical line, which was computed to 1000 decimal digits in
[
21
]
, and we evaluated
\zeta(1/2+{\textnormal{i}}\gamma_{30};h)
for a decreasing sequence of
h
. The results, shown in Figure
7
, demonstrate that the values of
\zeta(1/2+{\textnormal{i}}\gamma_{30};h)
decrease precisely at the rate
\exp(-\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\times\frac{1}{h})
, which is consistent with (
9
).

We now explain the ideas behind our approximations and the method used to compute the coefficients
\omega_{p,j}
and
\lambda_{p,j}
. Formula (
6
) shows that we can compute
\zeta(s)
once we have an effective way to evaluate
{\mathcal{I}}_{M}(s)
.
We rewrite (
7
) in the form

{\mathcal{I}}_{M}(s):=M^{-s}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{g(x;s)}\times\frac{e^{-\pi x^{2}}}{\cosh(\pi\theta x)}{\textnormal{d}}x=M^{-s}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{g(x;s)}\eta({\textnormal{d}}x),

(10)

where we denoted

g(x)=g(x;s):=-2M\pi\theta x-s\ln\Big(1+\frac{x}{M\theta}\Big)\;\;\;\;{\textnormal{ and }}\;\;\;\;\eta({\textnormal{d}}x):=\frac{e^{-\pi x^{2}}}{\cosh(\pi\theta x)}{\textnormal{d}}x.

We consider the complex numbers
\omega_{p,j}
and
x_{p,j}=\lambda_{p,j}/\theta
as the weights and nodes of a quadrature
with respect to the complex-valued measure
\eta({\textnormal{d}}x)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f(x)\eta({\textnormal{d}}x)\approx\omega_{p,0}f(0)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}\omega_{p,j}\Big[f(x_{p,j})+f(-x_{p,j})\Big].

(11)

A classical way to determine the coefficients
\omega_{p,j}
and
x_{p,j}
is to require that (
11
) holds exactly for a chosen set of functions
f_{k}(x)
.
For example, the Gaussian quadrature method requires (
11
) to be exact for all polynomials of degree
\leq 4p+1
. In fact, this was the first approach we explored, and while it produces decent results, the method we introduce next provides better accuracy and is somewhat easier to implement.

For the remainder of this section, we assume that
s
lies within a fixed vertical strip
a\leq\sigma\leq b
and we set
M=N_{t}+1/2
, where
s=\sigma+{\textnormal{i}}t
,
t>0
and
N_{t}=\lfloor\sqrt{t/(2\pi)}\rfloor
. We now examine the asymptotic behavior of the integrand in (
10
) as
t\to+\infty
.
Expanding
g(x;s)
in a Taylor series around
x=0
, we obtain
for
x\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
|x|<M

g(x;s)=\Big(-\frac{{\textnormal{i}}s}{2\pi M}-M\Big)2\pi\theta x+\frac{1}{2}\frac{s}{M^{2}\theta^{2}}x^{2}+\sum\limits_{k\geq 3}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k}\frac{s}{M^{k}\theta^{k}}x^{k}.

(12)

The first coefficient in this expansion simplifies to

-\frac{{\textnormal{i}}s}{2\pi M}-M=\frac{t-{\textnormal{i}}\sigma}{2\pi(N_{t}+\frac{1}{2})}-N_{t}-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{t}{2\pi N_{t}}-N_{t}-1+O(t^{-1/2}),

as
t\to+\infty
.
Defining

B(t):=\frac{t}{2\pi N_{t}}-N_{t}-1.

and using the inequality
N_{t}\leq\sqrt{t/(2\pi)}<N_{t}+1
, we obtain the bound

-1\leq B(t)\leq 1+O(t^{-1/2}).

(13)

The second coefficient in (
12
) simplifies to

\frac{1}{2}\frac{s}{M^{2}\theta^{2}}=-\frac{t}{2(N_{t}+\frac{1}{2})^{2}}+O(t^{-1})=-\pi+O(t^{-1/2}).

When
t
is large enough, we have
|s|<2t
and
M=N_{t}+1/2>\sqrt{t}/3
(since
\sqrt{2\pi}<3
). Therefore, for
t
large enough,
the sum in (
12
) can be estimated as follows

\Big|\sum\limits_{k\geq 3}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k}\frac{s}{M^{k}\theta^{k}}x^{k}\Big|<2t\sum\limits_{k\geq 3}\Big(3|x|/\sqrt{t}\Big)^{k}=\frac{54|x|^{3}}{\sqrt{t}-3|x|},

(14)

for
x\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
|x|<\sqrt{t}/3
.
Thus, we conclude that

g(x;s)=-\pi x^{2}+2\pi\theta B(t)x+O(t^{-1/2}),

(15)

as
t\to+\infty
, uniformly in
x
on compact subsets of
{\mathbb{C}}
.

For
t
large, the integral in (
10
) is therefore approximately equal to

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{-\pi x^{2}+2\pi\theta B(t)x}\eta({\textnormal{d}}x),

where
B(t)
typically lies in the interval
[-1,1]
, though occasionally it may slightly exceed one (see Figure

).

.

To determine the quadrature coefficients
\{\omega_{p,j}\}_{0\leq j\leq p}
and

\{x_{p,j}\}_{1\leq j\leq p}
, we require that (
11
) holds exactly for a set of
4p+2
functions

f_{k}(x)=e^{-\pi x^{2}+2\pi\theta y_{p,k}x},\;\;\;k=0,1,\dots,4p+1,

where the points
\{y_{p,k}\}_{0\leq k\leq 4p+1}
are equally spaced in the interval
[-1,1]
:

y_{p,k}:=-1+\frac{2k}{4p+1}.

Thus, we arrive at the following problem: we seek
2p+1
complex numbers
\{\omega_{p,j}\}_{0\leq j\leq p}
and

\{x_{p,j}\}_{1\leq j\leq p}
such that

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{e^{-2\pi x^{2}+2\pi\theta y_{p,k}x}}{\cosh(\pi\theta x)}{\textnormal{d}}x=\omega_{p,0}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}\omega_{p,j}e^{-\pi x_{p,j}^{2}}\Big[e^{2\pi\theta y_{p,k}x_{p,j}}+e^{-2\pi\theta y_{p,k}x_{p,j}}\Big],

(16)

for all
k=0,1,\dots,4p+1
.

To simplify the above problem, we note that the integral on the left-hand side of (
16
) is one of the Mordell integrals
[
20
]
and can be computed explicitly. Using formula (7) in
[
15
]
(or transforming the integral in
[
6
]
[Theorem 4.1.1])
we evaluate

H(y):=\int\limits_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{e^{-2\pi x^{2}+2\pi\theta xy}}{\cosh(\pi\theta x)}{\textnormal{d}}x=\frac{1}{\cos(\pi y)}\Big[\sqrt{2}\cos(\pi y/2)e^{-\frac{\pi{\textnormal{i}}}{8}(4y^{2}+1)}-e^{-\frac{\pi{\textnormal{i}}}{4}}\Big].

(17)

Note that
H(y)
is an entire function of
y
.
We denote
\mu_{p,k}:=H(y_{p,k})

and introduce new variables
\{u_{j},z_{j}\}_{-p\leq j\leq p}
, defined as follows:
u_{0}=\omega_{p,0}
,
z_{0}=1
and for
j=1,2,\dots,p

u_{j}:=\omega_{p,j}e^{-\pi x_{p,j}^{2}-2\pi\theta x_{p,j}},\;\;\;u_{-j}:=\omega_{p,j}e^{-\pi x_{p,j}^{2}+2\pi\theta x_{p,j}},\;\;\;z_{j}:=e^{2\pi\theta\frac{2}{4p+1}x_{p,j}},\;\;\;z_{-j}:=1/z_{j}.

Note that
u_{j}
and
z_{j}
also depend on
p
, but we suppress the dependence on
p
to simplify notation.
In these new variables, the system of equations (
16
) simplifies to

\sum\limits_{j=-p}^{p}u_{j}z_{j}^{k}=\mu_{p,k},\;\;\;k=0,1,\dots,4p+1.

(18)

Our task is now to determine the values of
\{u_{j},z_{j}\}_{-p\leq j\leq p}
satisfying
z_{0}=1
,
z_{-j}=1/z_{j}
and
u_{-j}=z_{j}^{4p+1}u_{j}
for
j=1,2,\dots,p
, which solve the system of equations (
18
).

The solution to the above system of equations is obtained using classical methods of Gaussian quadrature
[
9
,
17
,
19
,
25
]
. Let
{\mathscr{P}}_{n}
be the space of polynomials with complex coefficients of degree not exceeding
n
. Define a linear functional
L
acting on
{\mathscr{P}}_{4p+1}
via

L[x^{k}]=\mu_{p,k},\;\;\;k=0,1,\dots,4p+1.

The system (
18
) is equivalent to the condition

L[Q]=\sum\limits_{j=-p}^{p}u_{j}Q(z_{j})\;\;{\textnormal{ for all }}\;Q\in{\mathscr{P}}_{4p+1}.

(19)

Thus, we can recognize
z_{j}
and
u_{j}
as the nodes and weights of the Gaussian quadrature for the linear functional
L
.
We find these nodes and weights via orthogonal polynomials with respect to the linear functional
L
(see
[
25
, Definition 1.1]
). We denote
m:=2p+1
, and we aim to find polynomials
\{P_{m,n}\}_{0\leq n\leq m}
satisfying
{\textnormal{deg}}(P_{m,n})=n
and the orthogonality condition: for
1\leq n\leq m

L[P_{m,n}Q]=0\;{\textnormal{ for all }}\;Q\in{\mathscr{P}}_{n-1}.

We will suppress the dependence on
m
in what follows and will write simply
P_{n}=P_{m,n}
.

It is known (see
[
25
, Theorem 3.2]
) that these orthogonal polynomials exist if and only if
L
is
quasi-definite
on
{{\mathscr{P}}}_{m}
. The latter condition means that certain Hankel determinants involving the coefficients
\mu_{p,k}
are non-zero (see formula (2.1) and Definition 3.1 in
[
25
]
). Assuming that the orthogonal polynomials
P_{n}
exist, they can be computed via the three-term recurrence relation

P_{n+1}(x)=(x-a_{n})P_{n}(x)-b_{n}P_{n-1}(x),\;\;\;n=0,1,\dots m-1,

(20)

with initial conditions
P_{-1}(x)\equiv 0
,
P_{0}(x)\equiv 1
. The recurrence coefficients are given by

a_{n}=\frac{L[xP_{n}^{2}]}{L[P_{n}^{2}]},\;\;\;b_{n}=\frac{L[P_{n}^{2}]}{L[P_{n-1}^{2}]}.

The above formulas can be found in equations (3.1) and (3.2) in
[
25
]
or in
[
18
, Theorem 2.9]
.

Before we proceed, we establish the following result:

Proposition 1
.

Assume that there exist polynomials
\{P_{n}\}_{0\leq n\leq m}
that are orthogonal with respect to the linear functional
L
. Then
the polynomial
P_{m}
must satisfy

P_{m}(x)=-x^{m}P_{m}(1/x)
for all
x\neq 0
.

Proof.

We begin by stating a preliminary result about determinants. Let
B=\{b_{i,j}\}_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
be an
n\times n
matrix. Following
[
8
]
, we denote by
B^{\tau}
the transpose of
B
with respect to the anti-diagonal. In other words,
B^{\tau}=\{\tilde{b}_{i,j}\}_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
where
\tilde{b}_{i,j}=b_{n+1-j,n+1-i}
for
1\leq i,j\leq n
.
It is true that

{\textnormal{det}}(B)={\textnormal{det}}(B^{\tau}).

(21)

This follows from the factorization
B^{\tau}={\textrm{J}}B^{T}{\textrm{J}}
(see
[
8
]
), where
J
is an
n\times n
exchange matrix, defined via
{\textrm{J}}_{i,j}=1
if
i+j=n+1
and
{\textrm{J}}_{i,j}=0
otherwise.

Now we can prove Proposition
1
. Since
H(y)
is an even function (see (
17
)), the moments satisfy
\mu_{p,k}=\mu_{p,4p+1-k}
for
0\leq k\leq 4p+1
. Using this fact and the well-known representation of orthogonal polynomials as determinants (see formula (2.2) in
[
18
]
), we obtain

P_{m}(x)=C\times{\textnormal{det}}\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{0}&\mu_{1}&\mu_{2}&\cdots&\mu_{2p-1}&\mu_{2p}&\mu_{2p}\\
\mu_{1}&\mu_{2}&\mu_{3}&\cdots&\mu_{2p}&\mu_{2p}&\mu_{2p-1}\\
\mu_{2}&\mu_{3}&\mu_{4}&\cdots&\mu_{2p}&\mu_{2p-1}&\mu_{2p-2}\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
\mu_{2p-1}&\mu_{2p}&\mu_{2p}&\cdots&\mu_{3}&\mu_{2}&\mu_{1}\\
\mu_{2p}&\mu_{2p}&\mu_{2p-1}&\cdots&\mu_{2}&\mu_{1}&\mu_{0}\\
1&x&x^{2}&\cdots&x^{2p-1}&x^{2p}&x^{2p+1}\end{bmatrix}

(22)

for some constant
C
. In the above formula, we used the simplified notation
\mu_{k}=\mu_{p,k}
.
For
k=0,1,\dots,m
we denote by
A_{k}
the submatrix obtained from the matrix in (
22
) by removing the last row and the
(k+1)
-st column. Performing Laplace expansion along the last row, we obtain

P_{m}(x)=C\sum\limits_{k=0}^{m}(-1)^{k}{\textnormal{det}}(A_{k})x^{k}.

It is straightforward to verify that
A_{k}=A_{m-k}^{\tau}
. The desired result follows from (
21
).

Next, we assume that we have been able to compute orthogonal polynomials

\{P_{n}\}_{0\leq n\leq m}
via the three-term recurrence relation
(
20
), and we assume further that all roots
\{z_{j}\}_{-p\leq j\leq p}
of the polynomial
P_{m}
are simple. While this would be guaranteed if the moments
\mu_{p,k}
were moments of a positive measure (which is not the case here), in all our computations we found that the roots of
P_{m}
were indeed simple. Proposition
1
implies that one of the roots must be equal to
1
and the other roots come in pairs
z_{j}
and
1/z_{j}
. This allows us to order the roots
\{z_{j}\}_{-p\leq j\leq p}
in increasing order of their absolute values (that is,
|z_{j}|\leq|z_{j+1}|
) and in such a way that
z_{-j}=1/z_{j}
and
z_{0}=1
.
The weights of the Gaussian quadrature are computed using
[
17
, formula (3)]
:

u_{j}=\frac{L[P_{m-1}^{2}]}{P_{m-1}(z_{j})P_{m}^{\prime}(z_{j})},\;\;\;-p\leq j\leq p.

(23)

Note that
P_{m}^{\prime}(z_{j})\neq 0
due to our assumption that the roots are simple. Additionally,
P_{m-1}(z_{j})\neq 0
, since otherwise, the three-term recurrence (
20
) would imply that
P_{n}(z_{j})=0
for all
0\leq n\leq m
, which is impossible (recall that
P_{0}(z)\equiv 1
).

We claim that the weights and nodes of this Gaussian quadrature satisfy the identity
u_{-j}=z_{j}^{4p+1}u_{j}
. This follows from (
18
) and the symmetry relation
\mu_{p,k}=\mu_{p,4p+1-k}
. Indeed, after we have found
z_{j}
and established that
z_{-j}=1/z_{j}
, we can interpret (
18
) as a system of linear equations in the
2p+1
unknowns
\{u_{j}\}_{-p\leq j\leq p}
. This system has a unique solution, since its coefficient matrix is the Vandermonde matrix constructed from distinct numbers
\{z_{j}\}_{-p\leq j\leq p}
. By changing indices
k\to 4p+1-k
and
j\mapsto-j
in (
18
), and using the identities
\mu_{p,k}=\mu_{p,4p+1-k}
and
1/z_{j}=z_{-j}
, we conclude that the numbers

\{u_{-j}z_{j}^{-4p-1}\}_{-p\leq j\leq p}
also solve the same system of equations. By the uniqueness of the solution, it follows that
u_{-j}=u_{j}z_{j}^{4p+1}
for all
-p\leq j\leq p
.

After we have found the nodes
\{z_{j}\}_{-p\leq j\leq p}
and the weights
\{u_{j}\}_{-p\leq j\leq p}
of Gaussian quadrature (
19
), we compute
\omega_{p,j}
and
x_{p,j}
from (
24
). We set
\omega_{p,0}=u_{0}
and

x_{p,j}=\frac{4p+1}{4\pi\theta}\log(z_{j}),\;\;\;\omega_{p,j}=u_{j}e^{\pi x_{p,j}^{2}+2\pi\theta x_{p,j}},\;\;\;j=1,2,\dots,p.

(24)

By construction, these numbers
\{\omega_{p,j}\}_{0\leq j\leq p}
and

\{x_{p,j}\}_{1\leq j\leq p}
satisfy the system of equations (
16
).

With the coefficients
\{\omega_{p,j}\}_{0\leq j\leq p}
and

\{x_{p,j}\}_{1\leq j\leq p}
computed, we can now use (
11
) and approximate

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{M}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{e^{-\pi x^{2}-2\pi M\theta x}}{\cosh(\pi\theta x)}\Big(M+\frac{x}{\theta}\Big)^{-s}{\textnormal{d}}x

\displaystyle\approx\omega_{p,0}M^{-s}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}\omega_{p,j}\Big[e^{-2\pi M\theta x_{p,j}}\Big(M+\frac{x_{p,j}}{\theta}\Big)^{-s}+e^{2\pi M\theta x_{p,j}}\Big(M-\frac{x_{p,j}}{\theta}\Big)^{-s}\Big].

We recognize that the right-hand side is precisely
{\mathcal{I}}_{M,p}(s)
defined in (

) (recall that we denoted
\theta x_{p,j}=\lambda_{p,j}
).

It remains to explain the pattern in the error

\zeta_{p}(1/2+{\textnormal{i}}t)-\zeta(1/2+{\textnormal{i}}t)
that we observed in
Figure

. Define the function

H_{p}(y):=\omega_{p,0}+2\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}\omega_{p,j}e^{-\pi{\textnormal{i}}\lambda_{p,j}^{2}}\cosh(2\pi\lambda_{p,j}y).

(25)

The system of equations (
16
) is equivalent to

H_{p}(y_{p,k})=H(y_{p,k}),\;\;\;k=0,1,\dots,4p+1,

(26)

where we recall that
y_{p,k}=-1+2k/(4p+1)
and
H(y)
is defined in (
17
).
We remark here that equations (
26
) are particularly useful: they allowed us to verify the accuracy of the computed values of
\{\omega_{p,j}\}_{0\leq j\leq p}
and
\{\lambda_{p,j}\}_{1\leq j\leq p}
.
To proceed, we interpret
H_{p}(y)
as an approximation to the Mordell integral
H(y)
: this approximation is exact at
4p+2
equally spaced points on
[-1,1]
. In Figure

, we plot the error
|H_{p}(y)-H(y)|
of this approximation for

and

. These graphs closely resemble those in Figure

, which display the error

We rewrite (

) in an equivalent form:

{\mathcal{I}}_{M}(s)=M^{-s}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{e^{-2\pi x^{2}+2\pi\theta B(t)x}}{\cosh(\pi\theta x)}{\textnormal{d}}x+M^{-s}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{e^{-2\pi x^{2}+2\pi\theta B(t)x}}{\cosh(\pi\theta x)}\times\Big[e^{\pi x^{2}-2\pi\theta B(t)x+g(x;s)}-1\Big]{\textnormal{d}}x.

According to (

), the term inside the square brackets converges to zero as

.
By the Dominated Convergence Theorem, the second integral on the right-hand side is

as

. Thus we obtain

{\mathcal{I}}_{M}(s)=M^{-s}H(B(t))+o(M^{-s}),\;\;\;t\to+\infty.

\displaystyle M^{s}{\mathcal{I}}_{M,p}(s)

Comparison with the Riemann-Siegel formula

The Riemann-Siegel approximation to
\zeta(s)
has long been the preferred way to compute

on and near the critical line

when

is large

In this section, we compare our approximations
\zeta_{p}(s)
with the Riemann-Siegel approximations on the critical line. Following

we denote

\vartheta(t):=\textnormal{Im}\big[\ln\Gamma(1/4+{\textnormal{i}}t/2)\big]-t\ln(\pi)/2,

to

where

and

\displaystyle C_{0}(z)

\displaystyle=F(z):=\frac{\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}(z^{2}+3/4))}{\cos(\pi z)},

(30)

\displaystyle C_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{F^{(3)}(z)}{2^{2}\cdot 3\pi^{2}},\;\;\;C_{2}(z)=\frac{F^{(6)}(z)}{2^{5}\cdot 3^{2}\pi^{4}}+\frac{F^{(2)}(z)}{2^{4}\pi^{2}}.

where the rational numbers

are computed recursively (see formula (3) in

]

(32)

\displaystyle|R_{3}(t)|<0.031\,t^{-9/4},\;\;\;|R_{4}(t)|<0.017\,t^{-11/4},\;\;\;|R_{5}(t)|<0.061\,t^{-13/4},

with the bounds for
K\leq 4
being optimal.

F^{(j)}(z)
will have
\cos(\pi z)^{j}
in the denominator, which makes it hard to compute the values of
F^{(j)}(z)
when
z
is close to
\pm 1/2
. The standard solution to this problem is not to use the explicit formulas for
C_{n}(z)
for
n\geq 1
and instead to use Taylor or Chebyshev expansions. The coefficients of Taylor and Chebyshev expansions of
C_{n}(z)
(given with a precision of 50 decimal digits) can be found in Tables IV and V in
[
6
]
.

In Figure
10
we plot the errors of the Riemann-Siegel approximations
\zeta^{\textnormal{RS}}_{K}(t)
for
K\in\{1,3,5\}
, the corresponding upper bounds (
32
) (gray curves) and the error of our approximation
\zeta_{7}(1/2+{\textnormal{i}}t)
(red curve). We see that our approximation
\zeta_{7}
is better than the Riemann-Siegel approximation
\zeta^{\textnormal{RS}}_{5}
in the range
10^{2}\leq t\leq 10^{3}
, and it is better for most values of
t
in the range
10^{3}\leq t\leq 10^{4}
. For very large values of
t
, the Riemann-Siegel approximation
\zeta^{\textnormal{RS}}_{5}
will be more accurate than
\zeta_{7}
, as it has a faster rate of asymptotic decay. Indeed, while
R_{5}(t)
decays as
O(t^{-13/4})
, formula (
29
) implies that the error
|\zeta_{7}(1/2+{\textnormal{i}}t)-\zeta(1/2+{\textnormal{i}}t)|
decays as
O(t^{-1/4})
.

At the same time, our approximation
\zeta_{7}
is considerably easier to implement than
\zeta^{\textnormal{RS}}_{5}
. Computing
\zeta_{7}
requires only
15
precomputed complex coefficients
\{\omega_{7,j}\}_{0\leq j\leq 7}
and
\{\lambda_{7,j}\}_{1\leq j\leq 7}
(these coefficients can be downloaded
here
). On the other hand, computing
\zeta^{\textnormal{RS}}_{5}
to full double precision requires around 100 coefficients of the Taylor series expansion (20 for each
C_{n}(z)
term for
n=1,2,\dots,5
), or around 65 coefficients of the Chebyshev expansion (13 for each
C_{n}(z)
term for
n=1,2,\dots,5
) (see Tables IV and V in
[
6
]
). When computing
\zeta^{\textnormal{RS}}_{5}
in full quadruple precision, we will need even more: namely, about 170 Taylor or 120 Chebyshev coefficients. Thus, when comparing our approximations
\zeta_{p}
with the Riemann-Siegel approximations
\zeta^{\textnormal{RS}}_{K}
that achieve similar accuracy, our approximations have the advantage of requiring fewer precomputed coefficients and being easier to implement. The disadvantage is that currently there are no rigorous error bounds for
\zeta_{p}(s)-\zeta(s)
, but we hope this is a temporary problem and that efficient and rigorous error bounds will be obtained in the near future.

4
Computing
\zeta(s)
in the entire complex plane

Figure 11:
The algorithms used to compute
\zeta(s)
in the region
\textnormal{Im}(s)\geq 0
and
\textnormal{Re}(s)\geq 1/2
.

The Riemann zeta function is an important and well-studied special function, so one would expect that most programming languages would have available routines for computing
\zeta(s)
for arbitrary complex values of
s
. While a number of such routines are available (for example, MATLAB and Maple implementations), they tend to be rather slow and inefficient. In this section, we explain how our approximations
\zeta_{p}(s)
can be used together with the direct summation method and Euler-Maclaurin approximations to compute
\zeta(s)
for arbitrary complex values of
s
.

We note first that we only need to focus on computing
\zeta(s)
in the region
\textnormal{Re}(s)\geq 1/2
and
\textnormal{Im}(s)\geq 0
, as the functional equation
\zeta(s)=\chi(s)\zeta(1-s)
and the symmetry relation
\zeta(s)=\overline{\zeta(\overline{s})}
allow one to compute
\zeta(s)
for every
s\in{\mathbb{C}}
. In the region

\textnormal{Re}(s)\geq 1/2
and
\textnormal{Im}(s)\geq 0
we will switch between three different methods for computing
\zeta(s)
, depending on which method is more efficient for the given value of
s
.

The first method is based on direct summation. We assume that
\textnormal{Re}(s)\geq C_{1}>1
and write

\zeta(s)=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}n^{-s}+{\mathcal{E}}_{1}(N,s),

(33)

where the error
{\mathcal{E}}_{1}(N,s)
is bounded by:

|{\mathcal{E}}_{1}(N,s)|\leq\sum\limits_{n>N}n^{-\textnormal{Re}(s)}<\int_{N}^{\infty}x^{-C_{1}}{\textnormal{d}}x=\frac{1}{C_{1}-1}N^{1-C_{1}}.

For example, setting
C_{1}=8
we find that the direct summation with
N=150
terms computes
\zeta(s)
in the half-plane
\textnormal{Re}(s)\geq 8
with error
|{\mathcal{E}}_{1}(150,s)|<8.5\times 10^{-17}
, thus achieving full double precision.

The second method is the well-known Euler-Maclaurin summation formula, see
[
3
, Section 3]
and
[
26
, Section 2.2.5]
, which has the form

\zeta(s)=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N-1}n^{-s}+\frac{1}{2}N^{-s}+\frac{N^{1-s}}{s-1}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\frac{B_{2k}}{(2k)!}N^{1-s-2k}\prod\limits_{j=0}^{2k-2}(s+j)+{\mathcal{E}}_{2}(s,N,K),

(34)

As was explained in
[
26
]
, the error term satisfies
|{\mathcal{E}}_{2}(s,N,K)|<10^{-d}
if
\textnormal{Re}(s)\geq 1/2
and

2\pi N\geq 10|s+2K-2|,\;\;\;2K-1>d+\frac{1}{2}\log_{10}|s+2K-1|.

Thus,
in the region
1/2\leq\textnormal{Re}(s)\leq C_{1}
and
0\leq\textnormal{Im}(s)\leq C_{2}
(this is the dark gray region in Figure
11
) we can use the Euler-Maclaurin summation method to compute
\zeta(s)
to any desired accuracy
\epsilon
in
O_{\epsilon}(|s|)
arithmetic operations.

The third method for computing
\zeta(s)
is our approximation
\zeta_{p}(s)
, which will be used in the region
1/2\leq\textnormal{Re}(s)\leq C_{1}
and
\textnormal{Im}(s)\geq C_{2}
(the light gray region in Figure
11
).

To make the above algorithm efficient, one needs to choose the values of
p
,
C_{1}
, and
C_{2}
carefully.
For example, if we take
C_{2}
very large,
then our numerical results in Section
1
indicate that we can use a smaller value of
p
, so that
\zeta_{p}(s)
still gives an approximation to
\zeta(s)
with the desired accuracy. The downside is that for some values of
s
with
\textnormal{Im}(s)
large we will have to use the Euler-Maclaurin method, and this will require more computation time. Similar considerations apply to choosing
C_{1}
: increasing
C_{1}
will require fewer terms in the direct summation method but may also require increasing
p
and require more terms in the Euler-Maclaurin method. Thus, the parameters
p
,
C_{1}
, and
C_{2}

should be chosen to balance the desired accuracy against computational cost.

We implemented this algorithm for computing
\zeta(s)
and
\zeta^{\prime}(s)
for general complex values of
s
in MATLAB and Python (in double precision) and in Fortran (in quadruple precision). This code can be downloaded at

GitHub
or from the author’s
webpage
.

For the double-precision MATLAB and Python implementations, we used
p=8
,
C_{1}=5
and
C_{2}=200
. The resulting values of
\zeta(s)
have typical relative errors of order

10^{-13}
for
|\textnormal{Im}(s)|\leq 100
,
10^{-12}
for
|\textnormal{Im}(s)|\leq 10^{3}
and
10^{-11}
for
|\textnormal{Im}(s)|\leq 10^{4}
.
This loss of precision for large values of
\textnormal{Im}(s)
is consistent with what we observed in the example at the end of Section
1
(see Figure
6
). At the same time, our MATLAB version of
\zeta(s)
is faster than the MATLAB built-in function
zeta(s)
by a factor of hundreds or thousands (depending on the range of
|\textnormal{Im}(s)|
).

For the Fortran implementations we used
p=30
and
C_{2}=400
. We did all calculations in quadruple precision and for every
s
in the region
0\leq\textnormal{Im}(s)\leq 400
, we used either Euler-Maclaurin summation or direct summation, depending on which of these has smaller computational complexity for that particular value of
s
. Similarly, in the region
\textnormal{Im}(s)>400
we switched between the
\zeta_{30}(s)
approximation and direct summation, depending on which has smaller computational complexity. After testing this implementation of
\zeta(s)
, we found that it had typical relative errors of order
10^{-31}
in the region
|\textnormal{Im}(s)|<100
,
10^{-30}
for
|\textnormal{Im}(s)|<10^{3}
, and
10^{-29}
for
|\textnormal{Im}(s)|<10^{4}
.
