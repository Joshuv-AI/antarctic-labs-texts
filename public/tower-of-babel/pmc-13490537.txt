Environmental detection of amoxicillin using a molecularly imprinted graphene oxide-polyethylenimine electrochemical sensor
Authors: Tomasz Nazim, Agata Smułka, Tadeusz Ossowski, Michał Cegłowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Amoxicillin (AMX), a widely used β-lactam antibiotic, is frequently detected in surface waters due to its high consumption and limited metabolic degradation. Its environmental presence contributes to the spread of antibiotic resistance and poses risks to aquatic ecosystems and public health, highlighting the need for efficient monitoring tools. In this work, we report the development of a novel, low-cost, and highly selective electrochemical sensor for the indirect detection of amoxicillin, based on a glassy carbon electrode (GCE) modified with graphene oxide (GO) functionalized by a molecularly imprinted polyethyleneimine polymer (GO-MIP). The GO-PEI composite was synthesized via epoxide ring-opening chemistry and imprinted with AMX to generate selective recognition cavities. The sensor’s performance was characterized using cyclic voltammetry (CV), differential pulse voltammetry (DPV), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). The proposed GO-MIP sensor exhibited high sensitivity and selectivity for AMX, with a linear detection range from 10 nM to 1 mM, with a limit of detection (LOD) of 0.52 µM in standard solutions and 5.06 µM in environmental water samples. The proposed sensor represents the first application of a molecularly imprinted polyethyleneimine-functionalized graphene oxide composite for AMX detection. It combines low cost, ease of fabrication, and high selectivity, making it a promising proof of concept platform for environmental monitoring of antibiotic contaminants.

Introduction

Amoxicillin (AMX) is a β-lactam antibiotic widely used for treating a range of bacterial infections caused by Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria1, 2, 3. Its popularity stems from its broad-spectrum efficacy, relatively low toxicity, and affordability, making it a crucial drug in both human and veterinary medicine4, 5. Amoxicillin undergoes minimal metabolism in the human body and is primarily excreted unchanged in urine6. Moreover, amoxicillin residues are also found in animal-derived products, such as honey7 and milk8. The potential risks associated with such residues highlight the pressing need for reliable methods to detect and quantify AMX in diverse matrices3, 9, 10, 11.

Various methods are employed to detect amoxicillin in biological and environmental samples, such as high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)12, 13, liquid chromatographic/tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)14, 15, and immunoassays (ELISA)16, 17. They often involve lengthy sample preparation, specialized equipment, and relatively high operational costs18. According to the European Commission regulation, the maximum residue limit for amoxicillin in food products of animal origin, excluding milk, has been established at 50 ppb. For milk, a stricter limit of 4 ppb has been set19.

Various approaches to the electrochemical detection of amoxicillin include both unmodified electrodes (such as glassy carbon, graphite, stainless steel, or screen-printed electrodes) and chemically modified electrodes9. Unmodified electrodes often exhibit low sensitivity or fail to produce a well-defined analytical signal for AMX20, 21. Consequently, a variety of surface modifications have been developed, employing carbon-based materials (e.g., carbon nanotubes22, graphene oxide23, or carbon black24, metal nanoparticles (e.g., gold25, copper26, silver27, and polymeric layers28, 29.

A popular approach to enhance the selectivity of electrochemical sensors is to use molecularly imprinted polymers (MIPs). MIPs are fabricated in the presence of a target molecule (template), which guides the formation of recognition sites tailored to the template’s size, shape, and functional groups. MIP-based sensors have demonstrated considerable success in detecting a variety of compounds, including antibiotics: sulfonamides, tetracyclines, β-lactams30, 31, 32, 33, amino acids34 and oxytocic drugs35. Several studies have specifically reported MIP-based sensors for amoxicillin detection, showcasing improved analytical performance and robustness compared to their non-imprinted counterparts36, 37, 38.

In this research, we propose a selective sensor for amoxicillin detection, distinguished by its low cost, rapid measurement, and straightforward design. We develop further the concept of employing polyethyleneimine as a polymeric backbone for the creation of MIPs40. To evaluate its analytical performance, we utilized various electrochemical methods, including cyclic voltammetry (CV), differential pulse voltammetry (DPV), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). Overall, this approach offers a promising and efficient route for amoxicillin detection in an aqueous environment. Currently, similar solutions based on graphene oxide and MIPs for detecting AMX have been described in the literature41, 42. However, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first proposal for the fabrication of an electrochemical sensor with molecular imprinting based on graphene oxide and polyethyleneimine. Our study presents a novel, low-cost, and preliminary electrochemical sensor for the indirect detection of amoxicillin in the presence of [Fe(CN)6]³⁻/⁴⁻ ions as a redox probe, employing a molecularly imprinted material derived from graphene oxide modified with molecularly imprinted polyethylenimine. This design is based on the high reactivity of GO surfaces, the strong affinity of PEI for molecular binding, and the selectivity imparted by the MIP technique.

Materials and chemicals

The following chemicals were obtained and used in the research without undergoing any additional purification steps: graphite powder (synthetic, 7–11 micron, Thermo scientific), sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 96%, Chempur, pure p.a.), orthophosphoric acid (H3PO4, 85% Chempur, pure p.a.), methanol (≥ 99.9%, Sigma Aldrich, HPLC grade), acetonitrile (≥ 99.9%, Sigma Aldrich, HPLC grade), methyl p-toluenesulfonate (MeOTs, 97%, Acros Organics), 2-ethyl-2-oxazoline (EtOx, 99+%, Thermo Scientific), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 35–38%, Chempur, pure p.a.), potassium permanganate (KMnO4, Chempur pure p.a.), 1,4-butanediol diglycidyl ether (BDE) (> 93%, TCI Chemicals), amoxicillin (96%, Thermo Scientific), ciprofloxacin (≥ 98%, Sigma Aldrich), paraffin oil (Stanlab, pure p.a.), methanol (≥ 99.7%, Chempur, pure p.a.), potassium chloride (KCl, Stanlab, pure p.a.), potassium hexacyanoferrate(III) (K3[Fe(CN)6], 98%, Acros Organics, pure p.a.), potassium hexacyanoferrate(II) (K4[Fe(CN)6], 98%, Acros Organics, pure p.a.). All solutions were prepared from deionized water from Hydrolab Poland.

Instrumentation

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were collected with a Quanta 250 FEG (FEI) scanning electron microscope. The FTIR measurements were performed using Nicolet iS5 (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) spectrophotometer equipped with a SPECAC Golden Gate, diamond ATR unit with a resolution of 2 cm− 1. Size Exclusion Chromatography (SEC) measurements were performed, using HPLC Ultimate 3000 system (Dionex, USA), equipped with a TSKgel G5000PWXL-CP 300 × 7.8 mm, 10 μm column. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was performed using a UHV Specs System (Germany) equipped with a hemispherical electron energy analyzer. The spectra were acquired under ultrahigh vacuum conditions (1 × 10− 9 mbar) with 1486.6 eV Al-Kα radiation. Pass energies of 20 eV and 60 eV were applied for detailed and survey measurements, respectively. Peak fitting was carried out using the CasaXPS (version 2.3.16Dev52, https://www.casaxps.com) software, employing Shirley background subtraction and Gaussian-Lorentzian peak shape. The spectra processing was performed using the OriginPro 2022 (version 9.9.0.225, https://www.originlab.com) software. For the electrochemical measurements, a Methron Autolab potentiostat/galvanostat, M204, with FRA32M electrochemical impedance spectroscopy module and Nova 2.15 operating system was used.

Experimental

Synthesis

Graphene oxide synthesis

Graphene oxide was obtained using a slightly modified procedure described by Tour43. To a mixture of 1 g of graphite, 120 mL of H2SO4, and 13 mL of H3PO4, 3 g of KMnO4 was slowly introduced under continuous stirring. The mixture was left to react overnight. After 24 h, the temperature of the mixture was increased to 50 °C and it was sonicated for 30 min. Subsequently, the mixture was transferred to an ice bath, where 250 mL of H2O was slowly added while maintaining vigorous stirring. After waiting for 30 min, 30% hydrogen peroxide solution was added until the color changed completely to yellow-green. The material was purified by centrifugation, initially with 10% hydrochloric acid, followed by alternating washes with water and methanol until a pH of 4 was reached. The solvent was then evaporated in a vacuum dryer. The resulting GO was used in subsequent stages of synthesis.

Preparation of PEI

The synthesis of linear PEI was conducted using the method proposed by O. Sedláček44. The resulting polymer exhibited a dispersity of Đ = 1.07 and Mn = 630 (determined by SEC).

Preparation of PEI-functionalized GO

The reaction of PEI with GO was based on epoxide chemistry, where the polymer was incorporated through the ring-opening of epoxide groups present in the GO structure after the oxidation process. This approach aligns with literature data on the functionalization of GO39. 700 mg of GO was dispersed with ultrasonic assistance in 100 mL of water. The pH of the dispersion was then adjusted to 7.8 using a 0.1 M NaOH solution and the dispersion was then added dropwise, with continuous stirring, to 10 g of PEI dissolved in 50 mL of methanol. The temperature of the mixture was maintained at 40 °C. After the entire dispersion was added, the mixture was left to react overnight while maintaining a temperature of 40 °C and continuous stirring. The material was then subjected to dialysis in a mixture of 50% water and 50% methanol, followed by multiple centrifugations in methanol. After evaporation, the mass of the remaining material (GO-PEI) amounted to 1184 mg.

GO-MIP synthesis

A total of 1 g of GO-PEI was dispersed in 50 mL of methanol under ultrasonic assistance. To this dispersion, 84 mg of AMX, previously dissolved in 30 mL of methanol, was added. After 30 min, 0.84 mL of 1,4-butanediol diglycidyl ether was introduced dropwise under continuous stirring. The reaction mixture was maintained at 40 °C for 24 h in an open flask to allow solvent evaporation. The resulting black powder was washed multiple times with methanol and subsequently ground using a mortar and pestle. The material then underwent dialysis in methanol, with several solvent exchanges. Each dialysis step lasted 24 h and was repeated until no detectable amoxicillin remained in the dialysis solution. The final product was referred to as GO-MIP. GO-NIP was obtained under identical conditions, except for the addition of amoxicillin as the template.

Preparation of sensor

The synthesized GO-MIP and GO-NIP, high-purity paraffin oil and toluene were used to prepare the paste used to modify the surface of the GCE electrode. The paste was prepared by sequentially mixing GO-MIP, paraffin oil, and toluene by grinding in a mortar and pestle until a homogeneous consistency was achieved. The optimal GO-MIP to oil to methanol ratio of 1:4:50 (w/w) was used. GO-NIP paste was prepared following the same procedure. The resulting pastes were then used to modify the surface of GCE electrodes. A 3 mm diameter glassy carbon electrode (GCE) was polished with 0.3 mm Al2O3 powder, then washed with deionized water and dried in a stream of nitrogen. 10 µL of the previously prepared paste was applied to the thus prepared surface using an automatic pipette and allowed to air dry for about 30 min.

Electrochemical measurements

All electrochemical measurements were carried out in a three-electrode system, with a modified GCE electrode as the working electrode, Ag/AgCl (0.1 M KCl) as the reference electrode and a platinum wire as the counter-electrode. CV, DPV and EIS were performed to evaluate the effect of the modification on the electrode. The scan rate for CV was 100 mV/s, and the measurements were recorded in the potential range between − 0.8 V and 1.4 V. DPV measurements conditions were: scan rate of 10 mV/s, step potential of 0.005 V, modulation amplitude of 0.025 V, modulation time of 0.05 s and interval time of 0.5 s, polarization range of −0.8 V to 1.4 V. EIS tests were conducted under open-circuit potential conditions with a voltage perturbation amplitude of 10 mV and for a frequency range of 100,000 to 0.1 Hz, 10 points per decade of frequency. Electrode control measurements were carried out with 1 mM K3[Fe(CN)6] in 0.1 M KCl.

Sampling

Water samples were taken in the city of Poznań, from the Warta River. They were stored in a sterilized plastic bottle, in a refrigerator at 4 °C. Prior to analysis, the water was allowed to reach room temperature and then was filtered using a syringe filter to remove suspended solids.

Result and discussion

Physicochemical characterization

PEI was introduced onto the surface of GO through a reaction based on epoxide ring-opening chemistry, enabling the covalent attachment of PEI to the GO surface. The linear structure of PEI ensured uniform reactivity and accessibility of its amine groups, promoting efficient functionalization. Subsequently, the PEI-modified GO was cross-linked using 1,4-butanediol diglycidyl ether in the presence of amoxicillin as the template molecule. Specifically, the imprinting step occurs during this cross-linking stage. The template (AMX) first forms a complex with the flexible PEI backbone through non-covalent interactions. The subsequent addition of the cross-linking agent locks this spatial arrangement into a rigid 3D network. This process ensured the formation of molecularly imprinted sites specific to amoxicillin within the polymer network. The introduced functional groups are capable of forming strong interactions with the template, enhancing the selective recognition properties of the final material.

Morphology

The SEM images of the analyzed samples are depicted in Fig. 1. In the case of GO (Fig. 1a and b), the images show large, well-defined flakes with a characteristic layered structure. These display smooth edges, typical of graphite-based materials. In contrast, the samples subjected to crosslinking, such as GO-MIP (AMX unloaded) (Fig. 1h), GO-MIP (AMX loaded) (Fig. 1f), and GO-NIP (Fig. 1j), exhibit much smaller particles. The flakes appear fragmented, with a more irregular distribution of particle sizes and shapes. The GO-PEI (Fig. 1d) in reference to GO shows a marked increase in surface roughness. The addition of PEI likely disrupts the regular stacking of the sheets, resulting in more irregular and textured surface morphology. A comparison between GO-MIP (AMX unloaded), GO-MIP (AMX loaded), and GO-NIP shows no visible morphological differences. The surfaces and edges of the sheets remain comparable across the samples, indicating that neither the molecular imprinting process nor the loading of amoxicillin disrupts the characteristic layered structure of GO.

FT-IR spectroscopy

The FT-IR spectra (Fig. 2) of GO-PEI, GO-MIP (AMX loaded), GO-MIP (AMX unloaded), and GO-NIP display characteristic signals reflecting the structural modifications introduced during synthesis. In the spectrum of GO-PEI, a broad band around 3300 cm− 1, corresponding to O-H and N-H stretching vibrations, confirms the presence of hydroxyl and amine groups. Peaks at 2940 cm− 1 and 2828 cm− 1 are associated with C-H stretching vibrations, characteristic of alkyl chains within the PEI structure. A band at 1660 cm− 1 is attributed to N-H bending vibrations and possibly residual C = O groups from graphene oxide. Additionally, a peak at 1080 cm− 1 corresponds to C-O stretching, indicating oxygen functionalities on graphene oxide. In the case of GO-MIP (AMX loaded), additional bands at 1774 cm− 1 and 1580 cm− 1 emerge, reflecting the incorporation of amoxicillin. The signals observed in the spectrum for the GO-MIP (AMX loaded) sample are among the most intense for pure amoxicillin, confirming the presence of the antibiotic in the sample45. The spectrum of GO-MIP (AMX unloaded), following template removal, closely resembles that of GO-NIP, with the disappearance of bands associated with the presence of amoxicillin.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)

XPS analysis was performed to trace the changes in bonding characteristics and elemental composition of the samples during the modification process, providing evidence of successful functionalization and structural transformation. The spectrum of pristine GO (Fig. 3a) displays characteristic peaks corresponding to carbon (C 1 s) and oxygen (O 1 s), with an atomic C: O ratio of 2.08, confirming the presence of oxygen-rich functional groups. The deconvolution of the C 1 s peak (Fig. 3b) further identifies contributions from sp2-hybridized carbon (C = C), sp3-hybridized carbon (C–C), and carbon bound to oxygen, consistent with the structure of graphene oxide. Upon modification with PEI, significant changes are observed in the XPS spectra (Fig. 4a). The appearance of an N 1 s peak confirms the successful introduction of nitrogen-containing functional groups. The atomic ratio of oxygen to nitrogen to carbon was determined to be 1:1.05:4.62 (O: N:C). Following the introduction of PEI, the deconvolution of the C 1 s spectrum (Fig. 4b) reveals a decrease in the ratio of the C–O and C–N peaks relative to the C–C, C = C peak, indicating a possible reduction of oxygen-containing groups. The XPS spectrum of GO-MIP (AMX unloaded) (Fig. 5a) shows a reduction in the intensity of the N 1 s peak, which is attributed to the crosslinking reaction involving a non-nitrogenous crosslinking agent. The elemental ratio of oxygen to nitrogen to carbon (O: N:C) is 1:0.53:4.72. The deconvolution of the C 1 s peak (Fig. 5b) reveals that contributions from C–N bonds are retained, though their relative intensities are altered, indicating successful crosslinking.

Electrochemical sensing

Electrochemical characteristics of the received electrodes

In order to characterize the electrodes obtained by introducing GO-polymer paste on the GC surface, CV and EIS measurements were performed for the solution 0.1 M KCl containing 1 mM [Fe(CN)6]3−/4−. CV for GO-MIP and GO-NIP paste-modified electrodes show a redox process with peak separation of ΔE = 212 mV and ΔE = 198 mV (Fig. 6a and b). A decrease in peak currents is also observed, possibly due to the polymer’s chemical structure attached to the surface. High repeatability of measurements allows us to assume that the access to the surface of the active electrode does not change, and that the observed changes are related solely to the presence of the imprinted GO-PEI.

Selectivity study of the proposed GCE-GO-MIP sensor

Surface modification with the imprinted polymer GO-MIP was performed to increase the selectivity of electrode recognition toward amoxicillin (AMX). CV and DPV characterization of a 1 mM solution of AMX in 0.1 M KCl was conducted on unmodified GCE, modified with GO-MIP (GCE-GO-MIP) and GO-NIP (GCE-GO-NIP). A characteristic current peak in the region of 0.95 V was obtained on each of the analyzed electrodes (Fig. 7a). The current response in terms of peak area was highest for GCE, while the polymer-modified electrodes showed lower values. The current response of GCE-GO-MIP was slightly higher than GCE-GO-NIP in both CV and DPV measurements, suggesting a stronger affinity towards AMX. The observed effects may be due to the presence of molecular cavities in MIPs into which the antibiotic molecule fits, generating a weaker current response. In this study, GO-NIP was used only as a reference point for the quality of the analyzed material and as evidence of the presence of an imprint in GO-MIP. The low reactivity of GO-NIP with AMOX prevents a full calibration and characterization, as well as a quantitative comparison of the performance of the proposed materials.

In a similar potential range, the current response is also shown by another antibiotic, ciprofloxacin, for which the current response of the same electrodes was investigated (Fig. 7b). The CV measurement showed that the highest current response was shown by the pure GCE electrode, a small peak was also observed for GCE-GO-NIP. DPV measurement gave a decent response for GCE, whereas GCE-GO-NIP had the highest current value. GCE-GO-MIP exhibited a double current peak, possibly due to the deposition of different-sized molecules compared to AMX on the electrode surface. Therefore, the CV signal may be greater because it is a component of these two peaks, which are not observed for the other electrodes analyzed. The GCE-GO-MIP contains binding sites for AMX, so the demonstrated sensor in the presence of other compounds may show higher current responses. The presence of a signal from ciprofloxacin occurs because of its partial binding to the polymer structure. The results indicate the selective properties of GCE-GO-MIP towards AMX. The low current responses of GCE-GO-MIP are probably related to the penetration of AMX into the molecular cavities, hindering access to the active electrode surface. Another important factor to consider when analyzing the above results is the plausible deposition of the antibiotic on the surfaces of the resulting electrodes, occurring due to potential changes during the measurement. The observed current effects may be a component of all of the issues discussed above, and based on them, it can be concluded that the GCE-GO-MIP sensor is preferred for AMX detection.

With the above results, it can be said that the selectivity of the analyzed material towards AMX. The antibiotic fits into the cavity present in the polymer, generating a weaker electrochemical signal. Therefore, it was decided to conduct further studies by a non-indirect method, using a hexacyanoferrate standard.

Electrochemical detection of amoxicillin in a standard system

Due to the high affinity of the molecular cavities of the printed polymers for the antibiotic AMX, the conditions for the determination had to be adjusted. AMX penetrates the cavity, reducing access to the electrode surface, resulting in a lower current response. This effect is closely dependent on the AMX concentrations used. Therefore, the resulting GCE-GO-MIP-modified electrodes were used for AMX concentration detection by performing an indirect determination. Due to the complexity of the observed effects, direct detection would result in reduced sensor sensitivity.

Aqueous solutions of AMX were prepared, with increasing concentrations from 10 nM to 1 mM, in which the prepared sensors were incubated for 15 min. CV and EIS measurements were then performed in a 0.1 M KCl solution containing 1 mM [Fe(CN)6]3−/4−. The results obtained are shown in Fig. 8.

The linearity range of the measurement was evaluated by linear regression analysis of the dependence of the logarithm of concentration and Rct value. The equation of the curve and the coefficient of determination R2 were established.

Fig. 8(a) CV and (b) EIS and linear regression obtained for the GCE-GO-MIP sensor versus incubated concentration of amoxicillin, measured by the indirect method in the presence of 0.1 M KCl and 1 mM [Fe(CN)6]3-/4-.

(a) CV and (b) EIS and linear regression obtained for the GCE-GO-MIP sensor versus incubated concentration of amoxicillin, measured by the indirect method in the presence of 0.1 M KCl and 1 mM [Fe(CN)6]3-/4-.

CV measurements (Fig. 8a) were conducted for AMX concentrations ranging from 1 µM to 1 mM. The current response for the electrode incubated at a concentration of 1 mM was lower than that for the GCE standard but approximately 15 µA higher than the blank GCE-GO-MIP sensor measurement. A decrease in the current response value was observed, dependent on the AMX concentration at which the sensor was incubated, was observed. To achieve more accurate detection of interfacial events caused by charge transfer, a different concentration range (10 nM to 1 mM) was selected for EIS measurements. As a result of sensor incubation, changes in charge transfer resistance were observed (Fig. 8b). For the 1 mM sample, the resistance was slightly higher (4654 Ω) than for the GCE-GO-MIP blank (3999 Ω). The Rct value increases to 5355 Ω for a concentration of 0.1 mM. EIS measurements for successively increasing concentrations generate a signal with increased Rct values, reaching 1308 Ω. The obtained EIS results directly correlate with CV results, where an increase in resistance values is observed as the CV current response decreases.

The ΔRct value for the electrodes after incubation in the corresponding AMX concentration was calculated. A linear regression was then determined from the dependence of ΔRct on the logarithm of AMX concentration (Fig. 8b)46, 47. The results suggest the formation of two equilibria and linear regression equations. The equation of the straight line for the range of lower concentrations was: ΔRct = 1.677 log C(AMX) + 2.541 × 10− 6 at R2 = 0.96.

The limit of detection (LOD) value represents the smallest amount of a test substance that can be distinguished from its absence. The LOD value was determined from the equation LOD = 3.3(Sy/S) in which Sy is the standard deviation of the linear coefficient of the analytical curve obtained, and S is the slope of the calibration curve (analytical sensitivity). The data obtained made it possible to calculate the LOD value for AMX, which for the GCE-GO-MIP electrode was 0.52µM. The obtained result is comparable to those reported in studies20 and48.

The observed initial increase in charge transfer resistance may be related to the presence of cavities that allow the molecules reach the electrode surface. As the AMX concentration increases, the charge transfer mechanism changes, as the interaction of the antibiotic with the binding sites in the modified electrodes can induce changes in the polymer structure. An excess of AMX increases the resistance to electron transfer through the redox probe, resulting in a lower current signal obtained in the CV. It can be concluded that indirect recognition involves tight binding of AMX to imprinted cavities present in the polymer. Upon saturation, excess AMX rebinds to the surface, resulting in higher sensor sensitivity than would be achievable through direct recognition.

Electrochemical detection of amoxicillin in environmental samples

The sensitivity of the GCE-GO-MIP sensor was tested in river water samples by adding known amounts of AMX and in the presence of a base electrolyte (0.1 M KCl). A base electrolyte (0.1 M KCl) was added to the river water samples in a 1:1 ratio, and AMX was introduced in the appropriate amount. The solutions were prepared such that the AMX concentrations were progressively higher, corresponding to the reference concentrations. A GCE-GO-MIP electrode was introduced into the prepared solution; the electrode was left in the solution for 15 min, after which measurements were taken. The process was repeated for each concentration.

(a) CV; (b) EIS and linear regression obtained for the GCE-GO-MIP sensor depending on the incubated concentration of amoxicillin in environmental samples, measured by indirect method in the presence of 0.1 M KCl and 1 mM [Fe(CN)6]3−/4−.

Indirect concentration detection studies using CV and EIS were performed (Fig. 9). CV results obtained for a concentration range of 0.026 µM to 1.67 mM show decreasing peak currents as the incubated AMX concentration increases. The observed current responses are significantly lower than those obtained for a pure GCE electrode in [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− solution. The obviated effect can probably be attributed to adsorption of environmental elements on the sensor surface, which mask the electron transfer process. In addition, both AMX and the analytes present in the environmental water may penetrate the cavities of the imprinted polymer. EIS studies carried out for a concentration range of 76.7 nM to 0.17 mM confirm the sensor behavior trend obtained by the CV method. The Rct values measured for the incubated electrodes increase as the concentrations analyzed increase. The pattern of resistance values and the decrease in peak CV currents can be a direct result of a change in the charge transfer mechanism. For a standard sample containing only AMX, charge transfer could occur through GO-MIP, while, as a result of surface saturation due to the presence of multiple environmental analytes, transfer can occur through the entire sensor surface. This explains the observed changes, where the intensity is influenced by the adsorption of various elements on the surface, hindering electron transfer from the outset compared to the standard determination.

Nonetheless, the results of the obtained ΔRct values for the electrodes incubated in environmental water made it possible to plot the dependence of ΔRct on the logarithm of AMX concentration (Fig. 9b). By performing a linear regression, a linear equation was obtained: ΔRct = 6.28 log C(AMX) + 6.86 × 10− 5 with R2 = 0.97. With this, LOD = 5.06 µM was determined. It is interesting to note that the LOD value obtained from the analysis of environmental samples is higher than that of the AMX standard determination attempt.

The obtained LOD value for the environmental samples can be attributed to the matrix effect. Unlike the simple aqueous solutions used for calibration, environmental samples contain additional components, such as natural organic matter. It is hypothesized that these components adsorb onto the electrode surface, which reduces the number of available sites for the electrode process and causes the current responses to be noticeably lower than in reference analyses. One should also take into account the fact, the change of the proposed electron transfer mechanism in different samples can also affect the obtained LOD values. The results obtained may suggest a potential sensory application of the proposed matrix directly in environmental samples, taking into account the limitations of the method. During the analysis of real water samples, deviations from the linear calibration range were observed. This phenomenon is most likely due to the presence of matrix components in complex samples, which may affect the electrochemical reaction, especially at relatively high potentials (> 1.0 V), where competing oxidation processes may occur.

Conclusions

In this study, we successfully developed an electrochemical sensor for the detection of AMX, based on GCE modified with graphene oxide functionalized by molecularly imprinted polyethyleneimine. The sensor was designed to utilize the specific recognition cavities created during the molecular imprinting process for selective AMX binding. EIS and CV revealed that the presence of AMX caused a concentration-dependent increase in charge transfer resistance and a corresponding decrease in current response. The formation of specific molecular cavities within the GO-MIP material was evidenced by the differences in electrochemical response between the imprinted GO-MIP and non-imprinted GO-NIP electrodes. The GCE-GO-MIP sensor exhibited increased charge transfer resistance and reduced current signals upon AMX binding, confirming the presence of recognition sites that hinder electron transfer when occupied by the target analyte. In standard aqueous solutions, the sensor exhibited a linear detection range from 10 nM to 1 mM, with a LOD of 0.52 µM and a correlation coefficient R² = 0.96. This study should be regarded as a proof-of-concept study demonstrating the usefulness of modified GCE with a molecular imprint for the detection of antibiotics in environmental samples. Any analytical validation will be the subject of future research. The comparison of responses toward amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin revealed the sensor’s selectivity, as AMX induced a more defined and consistent electrochemical signal. Future work may focus on extending the sensor’s applicability to other antibiotics or optimizing it for real-time, in situ environmental monitoring.
