Predictive analysis of health-related quality of life trajectories in older patients with chronic pain based on explainable machine learning models
Authors: Xiaoang Zhang, Weichen Liu, Yaqing Hu, Daying Zhang, Yuping Liao, Ziqi Wu, Meijuan Huang, Jianmei Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealth-related quality of life (HRQoL) is a vital indicator of evaluating care outcomes and prognosis, yet little is understood about its developmental trajectories in older patients with chronic pain. This study aimed to identify latent HRQoL trajectories and their predictors, and to develop explainable machine learning models for predicting HRQoL deterioration.MethodsThis prospective cohort study assessed 608 older patients with chronic pain at admission and at 1, 3, and 6 months post-admission, collecting data on HRQoL, general characteristics, pain level, activities of daily living (ADL), depression, and perceived social support. Growth mixture modeling was applied to identify trajectories of physical and mental HRQoL. Predictors were selected using LASSO regression and SVM-RFE. Nine explainable machine learning models were developed for both components, and SHAP interpreted the outputs. An HRQoL decision-support dashboard was developed to facilitate potential clinical application.ResultsThree physical HRQoL trajectories were identified: Stable High, Decline and Low Stability, alongside two mental HRQoL trajectories: Improvement and Decline. Key predictors included education level, pain duration, pain level, ADL, depression, and perceived social support, with ADL and pain level being the most influential for physical and mental HRQoL, respectively.ConclusionsThis dual-trajectory study identified five distinct HRQoL patterns in older patients with chronic pain, elucidating key predictors via explainable machine learning. The proposed HRQoL decision-support dashboard may provide an interpretable tool to support understanding of predictive relationships and assist healthcare professionals in HRQoL assessment.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

China’s aging population is one of the largest and most rapidly expanding globally, representing a significant demographic shift. Recent estimates indicate that over 250 million individuals in China are aged 60 or older, a number projected to grow substantially in the coming decades due to increasing life expectancy and declining birth rates [1]. This demographic shift strains healthcare systems, particularly in managing chronic pain, which affects 35–50% of older adults [2]. This pervasive condition not only compromises functional independence in older adults but also escalates healthcare costs, posing significant challenges to both individuals and society [3]. Consequently, enhancing the health and well-being of older adults with chronic pain is a critical public health priority, essential for promoting healthy aging.

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) is a multidimensional construct encompassing physical, psychological, and social domains, reflecting patients’ perceptions of how their condition impacts their overall well-being [4]. It is a key indicator for evaluating care strategies effectiveness and predicting outcomes [5]. Older adults with chronic pain experience significantly reduced HRQoL compared to the general older population, often exhibiting negative emotions and altered social roles, leading to increased caregiving dependency and healthcare resource challenges [5]. Therefore, for healthcare professionals, a thorough focus on the HRQoL is essential for tailoring precise interventions to improve health outcomes in this population.

Comprehensive assessment is the first step in prioritizing and addressing HRQoL in clinical and research settings. However, a key limitation is that, although many healthcare providers and researchers actively evaluate HRQoL, these assessments are often confined to single time points, typically at admission or discharge [6, 7]. Given that HRQoL is a dynamic and evolving indicator, this approach results in a limited understanding of the longitudinal trajectories and heterogeneity of HRQoL among older adults with chronic pain throughout the continuum from inpatient treatment to post-discharge care, delaying recognition of deterioration and hindering timely intervention. Conversely, early identification of risk factors for declining HRQoL trajectories would not only enable healthcare providers to discern HRQoL trends through streamlined key assessments, thereby conserving medical resources and time, but also facilitate targeted, proactive preventive strategies before adverse outcomes arise [8]. In light of these considerations, a comprehensive understanding of HRQoL trajectories and their predictors in this population is essential for improving patient prognosis.

Although numerous studies have examined the potential risk factors for HRQoL trajectories in older patients with conditions such as esophageal and breast cancer, including age, body mass index, educational level, and disease duration [9, 10], their relevance to older patients with chronic pain remains unclear. Moreover, the risk factors for HRQoL identified through cross-sectional surveys in this population still require validation. In addition, most studies have employed traditional methods, such as logistic regression models, to identify risk factors and predict HRQoL trajectory [9]. However, the predictive accuracy and understanding of the complex interrelationships between variables are still limited.

Explainable machine learning models represent a cutting-edge approach in artificial intelligence, offering healthcare professionals enhanced HRQoL trajectory prediction. These models leverage tools such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), an intuitive method for interpreting and visualizing model outputs, to provide clear insights into the complex interactions among factors influencing HRQoL [11]. Unlike traditional statistical methods, these models capture non-linear relationships and uncover nuanced information that conventional approaches may overlook or fail to integrate [12]. For example, healthcare professionals using these models can intuitively visualize how factors such as pain and depression interact to shape the unique HRQoL trajectory for individual patients. It can also provide healthcare professionals with an overarching analysis of which risk factors hold the greatest predictive value across the patient population. The combined precision and comprehensiveness of the disease insights provided by these models can significantly assist healthcare professionals in making more proactive, individualized care decisions. While these models have proven useful in predicting HRQoL in populations such as breast cancer and arthritis patients [11, 13], their application to older patients with chronic pain remains underexplored.

Therefore, this study aims to identify the HRQoL trajectories in older adults with chronic pain and use explainable machine learning to identify associated predictors. The findings will enhance healthcare professionals’ understanding of long-term health outcomes, facilitate early risk identification for HRQoL decline, and inform targeted clinical intervention strategies.

Methods

Study procedure

This prospective cohort study recruited participants meeting inclusion criteria. Baseline data, including HRQoL, pain level, activities of daily living (ADL), depression, perceived social support, and general characteristics, were collected via face-to-face interviews upon admission. HRQoL was reassessed via telephone follow-ups at 1, 3, and 6 months post-admission. Growth mixture modeling (GMM) was employed to identify heterogeneous trajectories of physical and mental HRQoL. Subsequently, predictors associated with trajectories were identified, and explainable machine learning models were developed to predict HRQoL trajectories. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for reporting [14].

Participants

Between June 2024 and March 2025, we recruited 657 hospitalized older adults with chronic pain from the Pain Medicine Department of a tertiary comprehensive hospital in Nanchang, China, using a convenience sampling approach. Eligible participants met the following inclusion criteria: (1) aged 60 years or older; (2) diagnosed with chronic pain according to the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) criteria [15], characterized by pain persisting for at least 3 months, occurring daily or nearly daily, with a Visual Analog Scale score of ≥3; and (3) capable of understanding questionnaire content, engaging in telephone communication, and willing to participate in the 6-month follow-up. Participants were excluded if they: (1) had a confirmed diagnosis of malignant tumors; (2) exhibited severe dysfunction of vital organs such as the heart or lungs; or (3) had significant impairments in the nervous, hematological, or immune systems.

Sample size estimation followed the principle of events-per-variable (EPV) ≥10 [16]. With 17 candidate variables and a 10% anticipated loss-to-follow-up rate, a minimum of 189 participants was required. To enhance the stability and predictive accuracy of explainable machine learning models, we expanded the sample size, resulting in 608 participants completing the study.

Ethical considerations

This study received approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (IIT2024714) and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent and voluntarily enrolled.

Assessment of HRQoL

HRQoL data were collected at hospital admission and at 1, 3, and 6 months post-admission. These time points were selected to capture both short-term fluctuations and medium-term trends in HRQoL among older adults with chronic pain [7]. The 1-month follow-up was chosen to detect early changes potentially influenced by post-admission pain management and interventions. The 3-month and 6-month intervals were designed to monitor sustained effects and evaluate the impact of rehabilitation and home-based care.

HRQoL was used as the primary outcome and was assessed using the Short Form-12 Health Survey (SF-12) [17]. The SF-12 comprises 12 items covering 8 domains. According to the validated SF-12 scoring algorithm based on normative data from the US general population [17], item responses were first recoded and transformed into standardized scores. These scores were then weighted using predefined coefficients, summed, and transformed to generate two summary measures: the Physical Component Summary (PCS) and the Mental Component Summary (MCS). Scores range from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating better HRQoL. To ensure measurement quality, interviewers received standardized SF-12 training, and pilot testing confirmed the clarity of item wording.

Assessment of candidate variables

The selection of candidate variables followed a systematic process. Key predictors of HRQoL were first identified through a literature review, followed by three rounds of group discussions to integrate perspectives on clinical relevance and feasibility. Multidisciplinary expert consultations in pain management, geriatric nursing, and psychological care emphasized the multidimensional nature of HRQoL and the need to include variables with both established and emerging relevance. Consequently, we selected candidate variables across physical, psychological, and social domains to comprehensively evaluate their predictive value for HRQoL trajectories.

General characteristics were collected using a structured questionnaire, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), education level, employment status, marital status, living arrangement, monthly household income, smoking status, alcohol consumption, pain characteristics (most severe complaint recorded for multiple characteristics), pain duration (longest duration for multiple pain sites), and number of pain sites.

Pain level was measured using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS). The VAS is a continuous scale ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst imaginable pain). Participants indicated their average pain level over the past week by marking a point on a 10 cm line [18].

The independence of ADL was assessed using the Barthel Index (BI), a 10-item scale with a total score ranging from 0 (completely dependent) to 100 (completely independent). Higher scores indicate greater independence in ADL and thus stronger functional capacity [19].

Depressive symptoms were measured using the 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15). Each item is scored as 0 (“no”) or 1 (“yes”), yielding a total score of 0–15. Scores of 0–4 indicated no depression, while scores > 4 reflected the presence of depressive symptoms, with higher scores representing greater severity [20].

Perceived social support was assessed using the Chinese version of the Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS), a 12-item instrument. Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree), yielding a total score ranging from 12 to 84. Higher scores indicate stronger perceived social support [21].

Data collection

Baseline data on HRQoL and candidate variables were collected at hospital admission (T0). HRQoL data were subsequently gathered at follow-up time points of 1 month (T1), 3 months (T2), and 6 months (T3) post-admission. During the data collection period, participants received routine clinical treatment, nursing care, discharge education, and community follow-up according to usual hospital practice. No standardized intervention was implemented as part of this study after discharge. Small tokens of appreciation (e.g., a thermos cup, face mask, or notebook, valued at approximately 10–30 RMB) were provided to enhance participant compliance and retention. To ensure data consistency across interviewers and time points, researchers underwent standardized training, and standardized protocols with scripted instructions were used. At T0, data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained researchers in a quiet, private setting to minimize distractions and ensure participant comfort. Follow-up data (T1, T2, T3) were collected via telephone interviews using scripted questions for consistency. Interviews were scheduled at convenient times to reduce participant burden, and responses were cross-checked for logical consistency with immediate clarification of ambiguities. Data were double-entered into a secure database, with logical checks to identify invalid questionnaires (e.g., patterned or inconsistent responses). Of 657 enrolled participants, 608 completed the study; 49 were excluded due to invalid questionnaires (n = 35), hospital transfer (n = 8), loss of interest (n = 4), or loss to contact (n = 2).

Identification of trajectories

Given that the SF-12 generates two distinct summary components, the PCS and the MCS, and to identify the distinct patterns and predictors of physical and mental HRQoL, thereby facilitating a clearer understanding of potential differences between these dimensions and improving the clinical interpretability of tailored interventions, GMM was applied to identify heterogeneous trajectories of physical and mental HRQoL separately [17, 22]. GMM captures population trends and inter-individual variability by identifying subgroups with distinct longitudinal patterns, accommodating nonlinear changes and within-class variability. Compared to latent class growth analysis or hierarchical clustering, GMM balances parsimony and flexibility, making it suitable for our heterogeneous, repeated-measures dataset [9]. Polynomial growth functions were specified, and both linear and quadratic terms were tested. The final model retained a linear growth function. Within-class variances were freely estimated. Model parameters were estimated using maximum likelihood. Multiple random start values were used during model estimation to reduce the risk of local maxima, and convergence was assessed based on the stability of the log-likelihood values. Candidate models were compared using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and sample-size adjusted BIC (SABIC), with lower values indicating better fit. Entropy assessed classification accuracy, with values closer to 1.0 indicating higher precision. The Lo-Mendell-Rubin (LMR) and bootstrap likelihood ratio tests (BLRT) compared models with varying class numbers. Final trajectory model selection was based on statistical indicators and clinical plausibility of identified trajectories.

Screening of variables

To identify the key predictors of HRQoL trajectories, variable selection was performed on the training set using a combination of Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression and Support Vector Machine Recursive-Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE) [12]. LASSO mitigates overfitting by shrinking less important variable coefficients to zero, selecting a sparse predictor set. SVM-RFE ranks variables by their contribution to classification performance, iteratively removing the least significant features. This integrated approach addresses multicollinearity and captures non-linear relationships among physical, psychological, and social variables, enhancing the interpretability and predictive performance of subsequent machine learning models [23].

Construction of explainable machine learning models

The dataset was randomly divided into a training set (70%) and a testing set (30%) to support model development and evaluation. To ensure consistent scaling across variables, all data were normalized to a 0–1 range using the min-max normalization. Nine machine learning models were selected to predict physical and mental HRQoL trajectories respectively, chosen for their suitability in handling the complex and heterogeneous relationships within our dataset [12, 23]. Logistic Regression (LR) provides a robust baseline for linear relationships. Support Vector Machine (SVM) excels at capturing complex non-linear patterns through kernel functions. Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost) enhances predictive accuracy by iteratively focusing on misclassified samples. K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) leverages local data patterns for classification. Random Forest (RF) and Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (GBDT) provide robustness to handle high-dimensional data. Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) deliver superior predictive performance through optimized gradient boosting. Stacking enhances accuracy by integrating models. Grid search was applied within the training set to optimize model performance.

Model performance was primarily internally validated in the training set using 10-fold cross-validation. Evaluation metrics included accuracy, precision, F1 score, sensitivity, specificity, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and the Area Under the Curve (AUC), with higher AUC values indicating better discriminative ability. Calibration curves assessed alignment between predicted and observed outcomes, with lower Brier scores reflecting better calibration. Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) evaluated clinical utility by assessing net benefit across threshold probabilities [12]. The testing set was used for the final evaluation of the optimal model to assess its generalizability. To help healthcare professionals understand the model, SHAP was applied to explain model predictions, quantifying each feature’s contribution and providing visualizations of the interplay among physical, psychological, and social factors influencing HRQoL trajectories [11].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 and Python 3.12, with p < 0.05 considered significant. Normally distributed continuous data were reported as means (standard deviation) and compared using t-tests or analysis of variance (ANOVA). Skewed continuous data were summarized as medians (interquartile range) and analyzed with the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were described as frequencies (%) and compared using chi-square tests.

Model performance comparison

Nine machine learning models were constructed and optimized to predict both PCS and MCS trajectories, and their predictive performance on the training set was internally validated (Fig. 3). For PCS trajectory prediction, the LightGBM model demonstrated the best overall performance. ROC analysis indicated that LightGBM achieved the highest AUC 0.892 (95%CI 0.870–0.914), highlighting its superior ability in distinguishing different PCS trajectories (Fig. 4A). The calibration curve of LightGBM was closest to the diagonal (Brier score = 0.013), reflecting optimal agreement between predicted probabilities and observed PCS trajectories (Fig. 4B). DCA further confirmed that LightGBM consistently provided the greatest net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities, underscoring its potential utility in supporting clinical decision-making (Fig. 4C). Interpretation and application of model outputs

As the best-performing models, the outputs of the LightGBM and XGBoost models were interpreted using SHAP. For the LightGBM, feature importance ranking identified ADL as the most influential predictor, followed by pain duration and perceived social support (Fig. 5A). These predictors contributed most to distinguishing patients with Stable High PCS trajectory from those with Declining or Low Stable PCS trajectories. The SHAP summary plot illustrated the direction and magnitude of each feature’s contribution to PCS trajectories predictions (Fig. 5B). Higher SHAP values (purple) for pain level and pain duration were associated with an increased likelihood of Declining or Low Stable PCS trajectories, suggesting a potential adverse association with HRQoL. Conversely, higher ADL scores and perceived social support showed positive SHAP values (Cyan), correlating with predictions of the Stable High PCS trajectory, highlighting possible protective roles. Force plots further illustrated individual prediction patterns. For example, in a patient with a perceived social support score of 41, a pain level score of 9, a pain duration exceeding 1 year, and a depression score of 6, the combination of these predictors contributed to the model predicting the Declining or Low Stable PCS trajectories (Fig. 6A). Discussion

To address the limited understanding of HRQoL trajectories in older adults with chronic pain, this study identified distinct developmental patterns in both physical and mental dimensions. For physical HRQoL, three trajectory classes were observed, whereas two classes emerged for mental HRQoL. Moreover, we developed explainable machine learning models to identify key predictors of these trajectories, highlighting the importance of factors such as ADL and pain level. These findings provide an interpretable framework that may help healthcare professionals recognize patients at risk of HRQoL deterioration and inform the development of targeted care strategies.

The identified HRQoL trajectories in this study are consistent with and extend previous research findings. For physical HRQOL, the trajectory classes were characterized by substantial baseline heterogeneity, with the SHP, DP, and LSP groups exhibiting high, moderate, and low HRQoL levels at admission, respectively. This heterogeneity tended to persist over time, potentially influenced by comprehensive pain management and multidisciplinary clinical care during the first month post-admission, which aligns with findings by Shoop et al. [24]. Notably, beyond 1 month, the SHP group demonstrated sustained or slightly increased HRQoL, whereas the DP and LSP groups exhibited continued decline or persistently low levels at 3 and 6 months, consistent with Eichler et al. [22]. These patterns may be related to the transition from hospital-based management to community care and self-management, with variations in access to continuing care resources and individual self-management capacity potentially influencing HRQoL outcomes [25]. In terms of mental HRQOL, both the IM and DM groups displayed comparable HRQoL levels at admission. Similar to the physical domain, a rise in HRQoL was observed at 1 month post-admission, possibly reflecting the benefits of inpatient treatment and clinical support. However, from 3 to 6 months, the trajectories diverged, with the IM group showing an upward trend and the DM group a decline, a pattern also noted by Cenzer et al. [26]. It is also noteworthy that participants did not receive any additional structured interventions during follow-up beyond routine discharge education and community follow-up. Consequently, the observed HRQoL trajectories may reflect the combined influence of routine clinical care, individual self-management capacity, and community-based health education. These findings may highlight the potential importance of stratified management for older adults with chronic pain who are at higher risk of HRQoL decline. Continuity of care, self-management, psychological well-being, and social support may represent important considerations for improving patient outcomes [27].

Among the predictors identified for HRQoL trajectories, ADL emerged as an important factor associated with both physical and mental HRQoL and was the most important factor for physical HRQoL. This finding is encouraging, especially when contrasted with prior reports that often highlighted a lack of predictive value for variables, as our study underscores the potential relevance of physical function in improving physical health prognosis. This likely stems from its reflection of functional independence, which enhances patients’ participation in rehabilitative activities and social interactions, thereby improving overall HRQoL [13]. Therefore, considering the maintenance or improvement of functional capacity as a priority may help inform strategies aimed at promoting patient health [27]. Previous research has established a strong negative correlation between pain level and HRQoL [5], and our findings corroborate this pattern, further identifying pain level as the most critical predictor of mental HRQoL trajectories. Pain level likely directly impairs physical functioning and heightens psychological distress, thereby worsening both physical and mental health outcomes [4]. In addition, pain duration was identified as a predictor of HRQoL trajectories, but unlike some earlier studies [6], our SHAP analysis revealed that pain duration exerted a stronger influence on physical HRQoL than pain level. This may be attributed to pain duration reflecting the chronicity of the condition, with prolonged exposure to persistent pain causing greater cumulative harm to long-term HRQoL than shorter episodes of severe pain [5]. Therefore, the potential importance of pain level and its chronicity may warrant further consideration when assessing HRQoL risk in older adults [28].

Our study underscores the potential role of psychosocial factors in shaping both physical and mental HRQoL trajectories. Specifically, depression was identified as an important predictor of HRQoL trajectories, consistent with previous research [29]. Notably, perceived social support ranked as the second most important predictor for mental HRQoL trajectories. This is not unexpected, as optimal mental health involves not only psychological functioning but also peer support, which fosters health-promoting beliefs. Social support may improve psychological well-being by buffering stress and reinforcing adherence to health behaviors [7]. These findings suggest that depression screening and social support may represent important considerations for promoting long-term HRQoL among older adults with chronic pain across the continuum of care from hospital admission to community follow-up [25].

The relationship between age and HRQoL remains inconsistent across the literature. Our findings support age as a predictor of physical HRQoL trajectories, although its relative importance was comparatively low. Age-related declines in physical function may increase difficulties in disease self-management and daily activities, potentially contributing to lower HRQoL [8]. Obesity was also identified as a predictor of physical HRQoL, consistent with the observations of Lee et al. [10]. Increased pain burden, reduced mobility, and higher comorbidity risk associated with obesity may contribute to poorer HRQoL outcomes [30]. Furthermore, Lee et al. reported that higher educational attainment predicted improved mental HRQoL [10], a conclusion supported by our results. Although factors such as age, BMI, and educational are largely non-modifiable in the short term, considering these characteristics when assessing HRQoL may help inform stratified health management approaches that account for demographic and socioeconomic differences [31].

In our explainable machine learning models, LightGBM demonstrated superior performance in predicting PCS trajectories, while XGBoost excelled in forecasting MCS trajectories, both achieving AUC (0.89 and 0.91) that surpassed those of traditional models. For instance, the HRQoL prediction model developed by Candel-Parra et al. reported an AUC of only 0.83 [32]. This difference may reflect the advantages of ensemble learning algorithms such as LightGBM and XGBoost in capturing complex and potentially nonlinear relationships within HRQoL data compared with traditional regression-based approaches [12]. These advances may provide more accurate information for anticipating potential HRQoL trajectory patterns. Beyond predictive accuracy, these models may also help offer an intuitive and clinically acceptable way to interpret the relationships between predictors and HRQoL. On one hand, SHAP summary plots provide an overview of the relative importance of predictors, which may help identify variables that warrant further clinical attention at the population level [13]. By interpreting the SHAP values associated with different feature levels, healthcare professionals may gain insights into potential HRQoL trajectory patterns across groups with varying ADL and pain level scores. On the other hand, SHAP force plots offer patient-level explanations that illustrate how individual predictors contribute to the predicted HRQoL trajectory for a given patient [23]. To further support potential clinical application, we also developed an HRQoL decision-support dashboard that visually presents model outputs. By translating predictive results into an interpretable format, SHAP-based explainable machine learning models may serve as a potential bridge between complex model outputs and clinical application, potentially assisting healthcare professionals in exploring model-derived insights in clinical contexts and informing considerations for personalized interventions; however, their practical utility still requires further evaluation in real-world clinical settings [31].

When interpreting these findings, the characteristics of the study sample should also be considered. This study focused on hospitalized older adults with chronic pain, representing a clinical population with relatively severe health conditions and functional impairments, which may result in lower HRQoL at admission but potential improvement following inpatient treatment and care. In contrast, HRQoL trajectories in outpatient or community-dwelling older adults may differ. For example, Takayama et al. identified stable, increasing, and declining trajectories of physical and mental HRQoL among community-dwelling older adults [33], while Oshima et al. reported predominantly stable and rapidly declining patterns across multiple HRQoL dimensions [34]. Future studies should further compare trajectory patterns across inpatient, outpatient, and community populations.

This study may offer insights relevant to future clinical practice and research. First, identifying heterogeneous trajectories of physical and mental health related quality of life among older patients with chronic pain may contribute to a better understanding of how health status evolves in this population [8]. Second, the key predictors of heterogeneous HRQoL trajectories identified by explainable machine learning models may offer additional perspectives on the multifactorial determinants of overall health among patients and may inform future research exploring risk stratification approaches and potential intervention targets, particularly modifiable factors [35]. Third, the HRQoL decision-support dashboard developed from the model outputs represents a potential tool for translating predictive analytics into a more interpretable format. After further validation in real world clinical settings, the dashboard may help healthcare professionals intuitively identifying potential HRQoL risk patterns, thereby providing potential support for proactive HRQoL management [31]. Finally, this study highlights a potential intersection between artificial intelligence (AI), particularly explainable machine learning, and research on the overall health of older adults. Such integration may contribute to expanding the application of technology enabled approaches in healthcare. Promoting interdisciplinary collaboration that combines AI with clinical expertise may also provide useful directions for future research on health promotion among older populations [36].

Limitation

This study is subject to several limitations. First, due to constraints in the research setting, we only tracked short-term changes in patients’ HRQoL, and the generalizability of our findings requires validation through long-term follow-up studies. Second, the data predominantly relied on patient self-reports, which may be susceptible to reporting bias. Third, the sample was drawn from a single clinical center, potentially limiting the representativeness of the results across broader populations. Fourth, the absence of laboratory-measured biomarkers may have overlooked potential insights into underlying factors influencing HRQoL trajectories. Furthermore, although GMM and machine learning approaches are powerful data-driven methods, the identified trajectories and the relationships between predictors and model outputs may vary depending on factors such as sample size, feature selection, and model assumptions. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and external validation is warranted before clinical application. Future research should address these limitations to enhance the robustness of the conclusions.

Conclusion

This study identified three heterogeneous trajectories of physical HRQoL and two trajectories of mental HRQoL among older patients with chronic pain. The explainable machine learning models developed in this study indicated that ADL and pain level were the most important predictors of physical and mental HRQoL trajectories, respectively. An HRQoL decision-support dashboard was also developed based on the model outputs. These findings may help improve healthcare professionals’ understanding of heterogeneous HRQoL trajectory patterns and their predictors, thereby potentially informing the development of prospective and targeted clinical strategies aimed at improving patient health outcomes.
