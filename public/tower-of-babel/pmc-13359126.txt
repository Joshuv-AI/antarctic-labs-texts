Steroids for Drug‐Resistant Seizures and Prolonged Stroke‐Like Episode in a Patient With Sturge–Weber Syndrome: A Case Report
Authors: Brenna N Keam, Fabiola A Morales‐Vias, Siddharth S Gupta, Doris D Lin, Anne M Comi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionSturge–Weber syndrome (SWS) brain involvement has been associated with impairments in the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and microglial activation within involved cortical regions. Acute neurological crises, including seizures, stroke‐like episodes, and/or significant headaches, are common in these patients. This report describes in detail an adolescent with SWS and acute drug‐resistant status epilepticus, headache, and stroke‐like episode who improved clinically when treated with high‐dose steroids. Introduction

Sturge–Weber syndrome (SWS) is a rare neurovascular disorder with a reported incidence of roughly 1 in 20 000 to 50 000 newborns across the world [1]. SWS is characterized by capillary malformations of the brain, skin, and eyes.

SWS brain involvement is characterized by leptomeningeal enhancement on T1‐weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [2]. Patients with SWS brain involvement can experience neurological crises such as seizures, stroke‐like episodes, and/or headaches [3]. SWS is usually caused by a sporadic somatic mosaic R183Q mutation in GNAQ. GNAQ codes for the protein Gαq, which, when mutated, causes dysfunctional Gαq deactivation mechanisms in the brain, leading to hyperactivation of downstream pathways [4, 5]. Evidence from a mutant SWS mouse model revealed that the Gαq mutation was associated with blood–brain barrier (BBB) impairments and abnormal microvascular structure, contributing to impaired perfusion and a chronic inflammatory environment [4, 6]. These factors combined can exacerbate neurological symptoms experienced by patients with SWS.

Seizures occur in approximately 70% of SWS patients with unilateral brain involvement and in 90% of SWS patients with bilateral brain involvement [7]; 90% of patients with significant brain involvement (three or more lobes of the brain involved) will have seizure onset by 2 years of age [8]. Few studies report the frequency of refractory status epilepticus in SWS patients; however, one study in the United Kingdom examined a cohort of 140 patients and described that 57% of patients had a history of status epilepticus at some point [9]. Another cohort of 136 patients found that 4.55% had a history of refractory status epilepticus in China [10].

A common symptom associated with seizures in SWS is transient hemiparesis, or stroke‐like episode, which appears as unilateral weakness or impaired motor function; often these stroke‐like episodes are prolonged, lasting days, weeks, or even months [11]. It is thought that these temporary episodes are caused by ischemia due to decreased venous drainage mechanisms in the brain [11]. Additionally, SWS patients who experience stroke‐like episodes and epilepsy may also develop headaches with or without migraine features, which can hinder daily activities [12, 13].

In general, seizures associated with SWS are managed aggressively with anti‐seizure medications (ASMs) [14]. First‐line medications include levetiracetam and oxcarbazepine for SWS patients with focal epilepsy, the most common type of seizure associated with SWS, given their effectiveness in decreasing seizure frequency [14, 15]. Low‐dose aspirin is used as an adjunctive therapy with ASMs, as it may help decrease the severity and frequency of seizures [14, 16]. Intravenous fluids can also be helpful during an acute neurological crisis. However, medical care for patients with SWS and refractory seizures is more challenging and may require additional treatment approaches [14].

In cases where ASMs fail to reduce seizure activity, alternative therapies are explored. Based on the role of inflammation and impaired BBB function in epileptogenesis [6], treatments targeting inflammatory processes and BBB dysfunction have been increasingly investigated in patients with drug‐resistant epilepsy [17]. While studies examining the effectiveness of steroids in controlling medically refractory seizures exist, there is a paucity of research regarding their use specifically in patients with SWS [18]. We present a unique case in which we were able to document and reconstruct the timeline of intervention, allowing us to observe how steroid administration was associated with clinical improvement in a patient with SWS. This report aims to highlight the potential role of steroids during an acute presentation of status epilepticus, headache, and stroke‐like symptoms in a patient with SWS.

Patient Presentation

A 13‐year‐old boy with a history of SWS and focal epilepsy presented to the emergency department after 6 days of fever, malaise, headache, and a breakthrough seizure. As a middle school student, he was independent in his daily activities and participated in sports without the use of assistive devices. His past seizure semiology included right‐gaze deviations with oral automatisms. He had been compliant and his seizures had been well controlled on an ASM regimen of levetiracetam 57 mg/kg/day, and his last seizure occurred 2 years before this presentation. Additional home medications included aspirin 3.5 mg/kg/day as maintenance treatment for his SWS.

The patient was admitted with symptoms that began with a fever and headache and gradually progressed to right upper‐ and lower‐extremity weakness, impairing his ability to walk, as detailed in the timeline (Figure 1). He was afebrile and had normal initial vital signs when he presented to the emergency department, but his examination was remarkable for significant gross right upper‐ and lower‐extremity weakness, needing support to walk, and suspected dysarthria. His evaluation included serum studies and imaging. His serum studies were remarkable for elevated C‐reactive protein but were otherwise normal for complete blood count, chemistry, urine, and respiratory panels. Initial MRI of the brain (Figure 2a,b) done with and without contrast demonstrated characteristic leptomeningeal angiomatosis involving the left temporal, parietal, and occipital lobes, and enlarged left choroid glomus related to his SWS diagnosis. He started treatment with ceftriaxone and was placed on continuous electroencephalogram (EEG).

Over the first 24 h after admission, his condition worsened with progressive weakness, dysphagia, and respiratory compromise, requiring transfer to the intensive care unit and intubation. Continuous EEG monitoring revealed electrographic seizures arising from the left posterior head region, meeting criteria for electrographic status epilepticus (Figure 3). Clinically, the seizures were characterized by his typical semiology of right‐gaze deviations and right facial‐oral automatisms. Multiple ASMs were administered, including benzodiazepines, fosphenytoin, lacosamide, and valproic acid, and lastly, he was started on a midazolam infusion. On day two, due to his refractory status epilepticus, the decision was made to titrate the midazolam infusion and to initiate low‐dose methylprednisolone (80 mg/day). Seizure control was achieved after optimizing midazolam infusion to 1 mg/kg/h; however, neurological deficits persisted. His seizures recurred and required additional ASMs, including benzodiazepines and phenobarbital.

A repeat brain MRI on day eight demonstrated progressive worsening over time with notable cerebral edema/gyral swelling in regions corresponding to known leptomeningeal angiomatosis, with increased intensity of contrast enhancement (Figure 2c,d). Given these findings, high‐dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1 g/day for 5 days) was initiated. After the first dose, his right‐sided weakness rapidly improved. He could move his right arm against gravity but still needed assistance with transferring between sitting and standing. Improvement was associated with starting high‐dose methylprednisolone. Phenobarbital was given 24 h prior to starting the high‐dose steroids; however, weakness and seizures continued until the high‐dose steroids were initiated. Of note, the patient did not exhibit any further breakthrough seizures, and the added ASMs were slowly weaned off.

The patient completed a 10‐day course of ceftriaxone. As part of the infectious evaluation, he had viral studies for enterovirus, human herpes virus 6, and herpes simplex virus, all of which were negative. Cerebrospinal fluid studies were not obtained by a lumbar puncture. Typically, cerebrospinal fluid is not obtained when patients with known SWS and epilepsy present with a cluster of seizures. The patient was discharged on day 13 with an ASM regimen that consisted of levetiracetam, lacosamide, and phenobarbital, in addition to a slow oral steroid taper with prednisone.

After discharge, the patient received outpatient physical and occupational therapy services to optimize function related to his right‐sided weakness. After completing the prednisone taper, his phenobarbital was weaned off. At his last clinic visit, one and a half months following discharge, his examination demonstrated only subtle fine neurological impairment in his right hand. He has remained seizure‐free for at least 8 months following this hospitalization and continues to be followed by his SWS neurologist (A. Comi). He has been completely weaned off the phenobarbital and lacosamide but continues levetiracetam with a goal range of 54–57 mg/kg/day and low‐dose aspirin as maintenance treatment.

Institutional Review Board Approval

We performed a consecutive medical record review through Epic (keywords Sturge‐Weber syndrome, stroke‐like symptoms, prednisone, methylprednisolone, hydrocortisone, and dexamethasone) and identified 24 patients who met criteria for inclusion in the study and had provided consent allowing the study of their clinical data (JHU IRB #NA_00043846).

Medical Record Review

In clinical practice, high‐dose corticosteroids have historically been used in the acute management of patients with SWS presenting with prolonged migraines, seizures, and stroke‐like symptoms. SWS can also involve chronically impaired cerebral perfusion, venous congestion, and vasogenic edema secondary to leptomeningeal vascular malformations [4]. At our institution, we identified two additional patients who received steroids during neurological crises.

Three total patients were identified who were treated with corticosteroids for acute neurological crises: the patient described here in detail and two additional patients, summarized in Table 1. All three were included in the report, regardless of a positive or negative response. Because the review was retrospective, the two additional patients had significantly less documentation and detailed information about their neurological crises; however, available clinical records show a potential association between corticosteroid use and neurological improvement in all three patients.

Discussion

We present an adolescent with SWS and an acute presentation of focal weakness and super‐refractory status epilepticus (SRSE) whose improved neurological condition was associated with administration of high‐dose steroid therapy. ASM and adjunctive aspirin use have been described in the literature in the context of decreasing both the frequency and severity of seizures and stroke‐like symptoms in patients with SWS [19, 20]. Notably, mTOR inhibitors would not be appropriate for an acute crisis as described in this patient, given that this class of drugs takes time to be implemented and has only been studied in the context of SWS in chronic, low doses [21]. While our patient underwent the typical evaluation and management for a patient with SWS with uncontrolled seizures, including MRI, EEG, and ASMs, he continued to experience breakthrough seizures and persistent focal weakness on a medication regimen of levetiracetam, lacosamide, phenobarbital, and aspirin. However, within a day of administering high‐dose methylprednisolone, the patient's seizures ceased, and his mental status and right‐sided weakness began to improve. Of note, this patient met criteria for SRSE; management of SRSE also involves the use of corticosteroids if seizure control has not been achieved after at least 72 h [22]. There is no consensus on optimal dosing of steroids in SRSE. Possible doses related to status epilepticus of unknown etiology or new‐onset refractory status epilepticus include intravenous prednisone 1 g/day for 3 days followed by 1 mg/kg/day [23] or 20–30 mg/kg (maximum 1 g) daily for 3 to 5 days [24]. The use of corticosteroids in the treatment of status epilepticus is recommended when an immunologic or inflammatory etiology is suspected [22, 23, 24]. Corticosteroid therapy may help decrease cerebral edema, which has proven to be beneficial for brain disorders associated with vasogenic edema [25]. For our patient, corticosteroid therapy may have helped decrease the temporal, parietal, and occipital cerebral edema found on his repeat imaging, leading to improvement in his hemiparesis. Of note, the parents of all three patients reported (after the prolonged acute crises) seizure cessation, neurological improvement, and clinical stabilization within ~24 h of starting high‐dose intravenous steroids and requested that this treatment be available for future episodes. Certainly, this association is not proof of treatment benefit but supports the short‐term safety and need for further study. The patient discussed here suggests that high‐dose steroids may be more effective than low‐dose, but an optimal dose of steroids in this context also requires further study.

Seizures and stroke‐like symptoms have been well documented in patients with SWS. Although the exact pathogenesis of epilepsy in patients with SWS has not been identified, recent research has implicated the role of inflammation and impaired BBB function in epileptogenesis [4]. Further, cortical damage and weakened venous blood flow associated with vascular malformations in SWS have been thought to contribute to epilepsy and are associated with brain atrophy and increased seizure frequency [19, 26]. During seizures, vascular endothelial growth factor may be upregulated by seizure activity, which can accelerate further angiogenesis and impair BBB function [27]. In contrast, tight junction proteins are downregulated during seizures [27], further weakening BBB integrity and function.

In patients with SWS and prolonged seizure activity, venous drainage decreases, thereby impairing focal cerebral perfusion, which can lead to transient neurological deficits, including hemiparesis and speech impairments [20]; headache/migraine processes are frequently experienced with these acute neurological crises and may also be prolonged. Our patient showed clinical signs of hemiparesis upon arrival, with subsequent worsening in clinical status and electrographic status epilepticus. Chronic seizures are also thought to worsen ischemia due to the increased metabolic demand in areas of limited blood flow [28] and further decreases in cerebral blood flow; venous congestion and vasogenic edema, as seen in our patient, may also occur. Overall, these mechanisms may lead to a self‐perpetuating cycle of worsening blood flow, seizures, breakdown of the BBB, and inflammation, leading to further seizures and neurological impairments (see Figure 4). We can argue that steroids may act through immunomodulation and reduction of cerebral inflammation by targeting the inflammatory cascade and by stabilizing the BBB, thus breaking the cycle of seizure propagation [17, 18, 27, 29]. It is important to recognize that the mechanistic basis for steroid therapy and the response may differ in patients with SWS, as compared with patients with other underlying causes for their status epilepticus. Future research is needed to evaluate these important questions.

Limitations

This case report has several limitations that are important to consider. The clinical data were collected from a single institution and yielded a small population sample (n = 3) where corticosteroids were used to address neurological crises in patients with SWS brain involvement. The authors describing these patients were not directly involved in their treatment management at the time of the hospitalizations; however, A. Comi offered recommendations as a remote consultant for the patient described in detail. Our patient had various pharmacological treatments occurring simultaneously, for which we cannot draw definitive conclusions but can only describe the association of corticosteroids with improvement in focal findings. The retrospective nature of the case review introduces the potential for missing or fragmented data. The association between treatment and response does not prove causality. Certainly, it is possible that for these three patients, their prolonged acute crises and seizures subsided on their own, or other treatments (ASMs) kicked in after being optimized to higher doses. Future prospective studies and studies utilizing multi‐center data are needed to further assess this approach.

Conclusion

This is the first published case report of a patient with SWS treated with steroids during an acute presentation of prolonged headache and status epilepticus with stroke‐like symptoms. We highlight the potential role of corticosteroids as an adjunctive therapy in managing refractory status epilepticus and prolonged stroke‐like episodes in a patient with SWS. The patient's rapid clinical improvement following the initiation of high‐dose steroids suggests that neuroinflammatory, BBB, and neurovascular mechanisms in SWS may contribute to seizure refractoriness. Although further studies are needed to clarify optimal timing, dosing, and mechanisms, this patient underscores the importance of considering high‐dose steroids early in refractory seizures and stroke‐like episodes associated with SWS.
