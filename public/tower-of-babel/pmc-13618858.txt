Dual mechanism of human ATG10S in suppressing MEF2A-Driven pro-inflammatory responses
Authors: Miao-Qing Zhang, Zheng-Hao Wang, Jing-Pu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCytokine storm, characterized by excessive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, contributes to the severity and exacerbation of various diseases. Current therapies targeting individual cytokines prove inadequate due to the complex and multifactorial nature of inflammatory cascades. Here, we report human ATG10S, a novel isoform of the autophagy-related protein ATG10, as a potential inhibitor of cytokine storms. Using SARS-CoV-2 Spike (S) protein- or LPS-induced pro-inflammatory zebrafish and co-cultured human cell models, we found that ATG10S significantly reduced the expression of key pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF/TNFA, IL8, and CCL2), all transcriptionally regulated by MEF2A (myocyte enhancer factor 2A). Mechanistically, ATG10S bound directly to MEF2A at residues D61/D63, facilitating its selective autolysosomal degradation through MAP1LC3B/LC3B interaction, while it also competed with MAPK7/ERK5 for MEF2A binding to disrupt the TLR4-MAPK7-MEF2A signaling axis. This dual mechanism reduced both MEF2A protein levels and transcriptional activity, thereby attenuating cytokine overproduction. Importantly, ATG10S restored autophagic flux impaired by inflammatory stimuli and exhibited high specificity, sparing unrelated transcription factors. These findings established MEF2A as a critical regulator of the cytokine storm and revealed ATG10S as a distinctive macroautophagy/autophagy-linked immunomodulator that integrated selective autophagic degradation and transcriptional interference. Our study provides mechanistic insight into autophagy-mediated inflammatory regulation and highlights ATG10S as a promising therapeutic candidate for cytokine storm-associated diseases.Abbreviations: ATG: autophagy related; co-IP: co-immunoprecipitation; CQ: chloroquine; dpf: days post-fertilization; ELISA: enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay; hpi: hours post-injection; LC3B: microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 beta; LIR: LC3-interacting region; LPS: lipopolysaccharide; MAPK7/ERK5: mitogen-activated protein kinase 7; MDMs: macrophages; MEF2A: myocyte enhancer factor 2A; MO: morpholino; S: spike protein; SQSTM1: sequestosome 1; TLR4: toll like receptor 4.

Introduction

Cytokine storm is a systemic inflammatory response marked by the excessive production and release of inflammatory cytokines, leading to multiple organ failure and high mortality. It poses a serious threat to human health and occurs in various diseases and immunotherapies, including severe COVID-19, sepsis, acute lung injury, autoimmune diseases, and chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapy. Cytokine storms are a hallmark of the transition from mild/moderate to severe/critical illness [1]. For example, in COVID-19 caused by SARS-CoV-2, rapid viral replication results in delayed release of type I and type III interferons, accompanied by an accumulation of inflammatory macrophages that produce high levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF/TNFA, IL8, and CCL2. This cascade ultimately triggers a cytokine storm, resulting in lung tissue damage and respiratory failure [2]. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying SARS-CoV-2-induced cytokine storm remain poorly understood.

Currently, the treatment of cytokine storms primarily relies on nonselective anti-inflammatory agents, such as glucocorticoids – the most widely used and effective immunosuppressive agents – and colchicine. However, due to severe side effects, these therapies are typically limited to the acute or critical phases [3]. Targeted anti-cytokine therapies have recently emerged, such as tocilizumab (anti-IL6 receptor), anakinra (anti-IL1 receptor), and adalimumab (anti-TNF), which are already in clinical use. However, these agents target individual cytokines or their receptors [4]. Given that cytokine storms involve the simultaneous overproduction of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines, which form a complex regulatory “cytokine cascade” [5], targeting a single cytokine is often insufficient and may trigger compensatory immune responses. Therefore, effective therapeutic strategies should be able to target multiple inflammatory mediators simultaneously. Combination therapy of multiple drugs is impractical due to their interactive toxic side effects and uncoordinated pharmacological mechanisms of multiple drugs. Although some studies suggest that inhibition of specific single cytokine may reduce cytokine storms, clinical evidence remains limited. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop novel therapeutics capable of safely and simultaneously modulating the expression and release of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines.

TLR4 (toll like receptor 4) signaling pathway has received increasing attention due to its pivotal role in initiating and propagating cytokine storms. TLR4 is widely expressed in immune cells, including monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells [6]. It recognizes both pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and danger/damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), and directly binds to the SARS-CoV-2 spike (S) protein [7] and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [8]. Upon activation, TLR4 triggers two major downstream pathways: the MYD88-dependent and TRIM69/TRIF-dependent signaling pathway. The MYD88-dependent pathway primarily induces the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, while the TRIM69-dependent pathway promotes type I interferon and chemokine production [9,10]. Upon activation, TLR4 recruits adaptor proteins such as MYD88 and TRIM69, which subsequently activate multiple downstream kinase cascades, including the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) family. Among these, MAPK7/ERK5 (mitogen-activated protein kinase 7) is an atypical MAPK involved in inflammation, apoptosis, and cell survival [11]. By activating transcription factors such as MEF2 (myocyte enhancer factor 2) and NFKB (nuclear factor kappa B), MAPK7 promotes the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL6, TNF, and CCL2 [12,13]. However, the precise role of the TLR4-MAPK7 signaling axis in cytokine storm pathogenesis remains poorly understood and warrants further investigation.

MEF2A, a member of the MEF2 transcription factors family, participates in a wide range of biological processes, including neural differentiation, muscle development, cell proliferation, and apoptosis [14,15]. MEF2A exhibits high sequence conservation across species, with approximately 72.7% homology between human and zebrafish. Structurally, MEF2A contains a highly conserved MADS-box and MEF2 domain at the N terminus, and a transcription activation domain at the C-terminus. The MEF2 domain harbors a co-factor interaction region that recruits regulatory proteins to promote the transcription of target genes [16]. Clinical studies have reported significantly elevated MEF2A levels in patients undergoing cytokine storms [17]. However, whether MEF2A serves as a key regulator of cytokine storm remains unclear, and no pharmacological agents targeting MEF2A have been reported to date.

Macroautophagy/autophagy is a highly conserved, multi-step process in which dysfunctional proteins and organelles are sequestered into double-membrane autophagosomes and subsequently degraded following fusion with lysosomes [18]. Autophagy is increasingly recognized as a key regulator of inflammation, modulating immune responses and preventing excessive inflammation by degrading inflammatory mediators [19,20]. We previously identified a novel human isoform of the autophagy-related protein ATG10, termed ATG10S, generated by a single-exon deletion and encoding a 184-amino-acid protein with low endogenous expression [21]. ATG10S overexpression enhances autophagic flux, activates innate immune responses, and suppresses the replication of diverse viruses, exhibiting broad-spectrum antiviral activity. Additionally, ATG10S can translocate into the nucleus and function as a transcription factor, making it a potential target for antiviral candidate compounds screening [22–25]. To address ATG10S protein supply for the following study and potential practical purposes, we heterologously expressed and purified the human ATG10S protein. The purified ATG10S protein is highly stable and efficiently internalized into cells via clathrin-mediated endocytosis, recapitulating the biological functions of intracellularly overexpressed ATG10S. It selectively targets virus-infected cells, exhibiting high antiviral activity with low cytotoxicity [25]. However, the potential role of ATG10S in modulating cytokine storms remains unexplored.

Recent studies have established zebrafish as a valuable in vivo model for investigating the pathophysiology of cytokine storm [26–28]. Although zebrafish lack lungs, immune cell recruitment and inflammatory responses can be effectively examined in specific anatomical regions, such as the hindbrain and tail, enabling both localized and systemic immune analysis [29]. Zebrafish larvae offer unique advantages for real-time visualization of immune cell trafficking and cytokine dynamics. Owing to their high genetic and physiological homology to mammals, transparent embryos, rapid reproduction, and suitability for live imaging and microinjection, zebrafish represent an ideal platform for studying inflammatory mechanisms and screening anti-inflammatory agents.

In this study, we utilized the macrophage-specific transgenic zebrafish line Tg(mpeg1:eGFP) to establish an in vivo model of pro-inflammatory responses induced by the SARS-CoV-2 S protein or LPS. To simulate the inflammatory microenvironment and explore the anti-cytokine storm mechanism of ATG10S, we established an in vitro co-culture model using monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) and human bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS-2B), stimulated with the S protein or LPS. Our findings demonstrated that ATG10S exhibited significant anti-inflammatory activity and effectively suppressed the expression of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines induced by the S protein or LPS. Furthermore, we identified MEF2A as a key target mediating this broad-spectrum anti-inflammatory effect. These findings provided a theoretical basis for further development of ATG10S protein as a therapeutic candidate for cytokine storms and highlighted MEF2A as a promising molecular target for novel anti-inflammatory drug discovery.

Results

ATG10S inhibits S protein- or LPS-induced pro-inflammatory responses

Our previous studies have demonstrated that ATG10S facilitates the autophagic degradation of multiple viral proteins [25]. To determine whether ATG10S modulates pro-inflammatory cytokine expression independently of S protein degradation, we employed S protein-induced pro-inflammatory response cell and zebrafish models. Western blot analysis showed that ATG10S protein significantly reduced S protein levels in MDMs and zebrafish at 5 μM and 10 μM, but had no effect at 1.25 μM and 2.5 μM (Fig. S1A). Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) results demonstrated that ATG10S protein reduced the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF, IL8, and CCL2) in a dose-dependent manner at 1.25, 2.5, and 5 μM (Fig. S1B). Therefore, 2.5 μM was selected as the optimal concentration for subsequent experiments, as it effectively reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion without significantly affecting S protein levels.

In S protein- or LPS-induced pro-inflammatory zebrafish models, in vivo fluorescence imaging at 2-, 6-, 12-, and 24-h post-injection (hpi) revealed time-dependent macrophages recruitment to the injection site, leading to significant mortality at 12 hpi (Figure 1A and Figure S1C). qRT-PCR analysis confirmed that S protein or LPS significantly upregulated pro-inflammatory cytokine expression – including ifn1, ifng, il1b, il6, tnfa, il8, and ccl2—in both whole-body and tail tissues of zebrafish larvae; ATG10S significantly reduced mRNA levels of the aforementioned cytokines (Figure 1B,C and Figure S2A). Moreover, ATG10S treatment markedly inhibited macrophage recruitment and significantly reduced mortality following S protein or LPS stimulation (Figure 1A and Figure S1C). These results suggested that ATG10S effectively attenuated S protein- or LPS-induced pro-inflammatory responses in vivo. In S protein- or LPS-induced pro-inflammatory cell models, significant induction of IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF, IL8, and CCL2 transcription was observed only under MDMs and BEAS-2B co-culture conditions (Figure 1D,E and Figure S2B), along with elevated cytokine secretion (Figure 1(F,G)). In contrast, monocultured MDMs or BEAS-2B cells showed minimal cytokine induction. ATG10S treatment significantly inhibited both mRNA and secretion levels of these cytokines in response to S protein or LPS stimulation (Figure 1D-G and Figure S2B). These results demonstrated that ATG10S effectively suppressed S protein- or LPS-induced pro-inflammatory responses in vitro.

ATG10S promotes complete autophagic flux in S protein- or LPS-induced pro-inflammatory models

Our previous studies demonstrated that ATG10S promotes complete autophagic flux, facilitating autolysosome formation to degrade viral proteins and inhibit viral replication [22,24,25]. In this study, using S protein- or LPS-induced pro-inflammatory cell models (MDMs and BEAS-2B cells were co-cultured), tandem sensor RFP-GFP-LC3B fluorescence imaging showed that both S protein and LPS impaired autophagic flux, while ATG10S restored complete autophagic flux in the two cells (Figure 2(A,B)). Western blot analysis showed that ATG10S increased the levels of ATG12–ATG5 conjugates, ATG7, LC3B (autophagosome marker), and LAMP2 (lysosome membrane marker), while reducing SQSTM1/p62 (autophagic substrate receptor) levels. Quantitative analysis of RFP-GFP-LC3B puncta confirmed that ATG10S increased autolysosome puncta (GFP− RFP+) and reduced autophagosome (GFP+ RFP+) puncta (Fig. S3A, B). These findings were consistent with enhanced autophagic flux and increased LC3B-II levels detected by western blot (Figure 2C,D and Figure S3C, D), indicating accelerated autophagosome formation followed by rapid lysosomal clearance. Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) with an anti-LC3B antibody revealed that ATG10S enhanced the interaction of LC3B with ATG12–ATG5 conjugates, ATG7, SQSTM1, and LAMP2, compared with both S protein- or LPS-stimulated and the control groups (Figure 2(C,D)). Collectively, these results suggested that ATG10S restored complete autophagic flux and facilitated autophagosome-lysosome fusion in S protein- or LPS-induced pro-inflammatory models. Unexpectedly, co-IP assays with an anti-LC3B antibody detected no interaction between LC3B and pro-inflammatory cytokines, although ATG10S significantly reduced cytokine protein levels in MDMs (co-cultured with BEAS-2B) and zebrafish following S protein or LPS stimulation (Fig. S3E). These findings suggested that ATG10S did not exert its anti-inflammatory effects via direct autophagic degradation of cytokines. Our previous studies showed that ATG10S protein enters cells via clathrin-mediated endocytosis and acts as a transcription factor [25]. Based on this, we hypothesized that ATG10S may also repress transcription of the pro-inflammatory cytokine genes (IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF, IL8, and CCL2). Nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation confirmed that ATG10S was present in both the cytoplasm and nucleus of MDMs in the S protein-induced pro-inflammatory cell models (Fig. S3F). Luciferase reporter assays showed that ATG10S significantly inhibited the transcription activity of the pro-inflammatory cytokine gene promoters in co-cultured MDMs (Figure 2(E)). However, DNA immunoprecipitation (DNA-IP) assays showed no direct binding of ATG10S to these cytokine gene promoters (Figure 2(F)). These results suggested that ATG10S suppressed the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokine genes, likely through an indirect mechanism rather than direct promoter binding.

MEF2A functions as a shared transcription factor regulating the expression of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines

The results above indicated that ATG10S did not promote the degradation of pro-inflammatory cytokines through autolysosomes. Instead, it suppressed the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokine genes without directly binding to their promoters. To elucidate how ATG10S simultaneously downregulated multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF, IL8, and CCL2), we used the JASPAR database (http://jaspar.genereg.net/) to predict transcription factors binding to the 2-kb upstream promoter regions of these genes (score > 12). Venn analysis (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/static/others/jvenn/example.html) identified MEF2A as a shared transcription factor predicted to bind the promoters of all these cytokine genes (Fig. S4A, Table 1). qRT-PCR analysis showed that MEF2A overexpression significantly increased mRNA levels of IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF, IL8, and CCL2 (Figure 3(A)). DNA IP and luciferase assays confirmed that MEF2A directly binds to the promoters of these genes and enhances their transcriptional activity (Figure 3(B,C)). However, MEF2A did not bind to the promoters of other key pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL12 or IL27, nor did it affect their transcription activity (Fig. S4B, C), suggesting that MEF2A was not a universal regulator of all pro-inflammatory cytokines. Using promoter constructs lacking the predicted MEF2A-binding motif, MEF2A failed to bind to the promoters with deletion mutation (Figure 3(D)) and could not activate their transcription activity (Figure 3(E)), indicating that these binding motifs were essential for MEF2A-mediated transcriptional regulation. Collectively, these results established MEF2A as a shared transcription factor that specifically regulated IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF, IL8, and CCL2 expression. Furthermore, western blotting and qRT-PCR showed that MEF2A protein levels were significantly increased in S protein- or LPS-induced pro-inflammatory cell and zebrafish models, while its mRNA levels remained largely unchanged (Figure 3(F-I)). These results suggested that MEF2A was upregulated post-transcriptionally and might play a key role in mediating the pro-inflammatory response induced by S protein or LPS. MEF2A functions as a key regulator of the pro-inflammatory response induced by S protein or LPS

To investigate the role of MEF2A role in the S protein- or LPS-induced pro-inflammatory response, we knocked down MEF2A in zebrafish using a mef2a-morpholino (MO). In vivo fluorescence imaging at 2, 6, 12, and 24 hpi showed that MEF2A knockdown markedly inhibited S protein- or LPS-induced macrophage recruitment to the injection site. Moreover, MEF2A knockdown significantly reduced mortality induced by S protein or LPS in zebrafish (Figure 4A and Figure S4D). qRT-PCR analysis showed that MEF2A knockdown significantly decreased mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in both whole body and tail of zebrafish following S protein or LPS stimulation (Figure 4B,C and Figure S4E). In cell models, MEF2A was silenced using MEF2A-siRNA in MDMs and BEAS-2B cells, respectively. Under co-culture conditions, qRT-PCR (Figure 4D,E and Figure S5A) and ELISA (Figure 4(F,G)) assays revealed that MEF2A knockdown in MDMs alone abolished S protein- or LPS-induced transcriptional upregulation and secretion of IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF, IL8, and CCL2 in both MDMs and BEAS-2B. In contrast, MEF2A knockdown in BEAS-2B cells alone had no significant effect on the expression and secretion of these cytokines in either cell type. These results suggested that MEF2A knockdown in MDMs alone was sufficient to abrogate the pro-inflammatory cytokine response induced by S protein or LPS. To exclude the possibility that the anti-inflammatory effects of MEF2A knockdown resulted from nonspecific cellular stress or compensatory inflammatory responses, we assessed basal cytokine expression under unstimulated co-culture conditions. When MEF2A was selectively knocked down in either MDMs or BEAS-2B cells and co-cultured with untransfected counterparts in the absence of S protein or LPS, no significant changes in IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF, IL8, or CCL2 mRNA levels were observed in either cell type compared with Control-siRNA group (Fig. S5B, C). These control experiments confirmed that MEF2A depletion alone did not intrinsically trigger a pro-inflammatory response in this model. Therefore, MEF2A in MDMs acted as a central regulator of the S protein- or LPS-induced pro-inflammatory response by promoting the expression of key pro-inflammatory cytokines. ATG10S suppresses the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines by downregulating MEF2A protein levels

In both S protein- or LPS-induced pro-inflammatory cell and zebrafish models, western blot and qRT-PCR analyses showed that ATG10S reduced the MEF2A protein increase induced by S protein or LPS, while MEF2A mRNA levels remained unchanged (Figure 5A,B and Figure S6A), suggesting that post-transcriptional regulation of MEF2A by ATG10S. As expected for a transcriptional factor, MEF2A overexpression significantly increased the mRNA levels of IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF, IL8, and CCL2 compared with S protein or LPS stimulation alone in both MDMs and zebrafish. Notably, MEF2A overexpression markedly attenuated the inhibitory effect of ATG10S on the mRNA levels of these cytokines (Figure 5C-F and Figure S6B-E). ELISA results further confirmed that MEF2A overexpression significantly increased the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines following S protein or LPS stimulation and similarly weakened the inhibitory effect of ATG10S on cytokine secretion (Figure 5(G,H)). Luciferase assays showed that MEF2A overexpression substantially reversed ATG10S-mediated inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokine genes promoter transcriptional activity in MDMs (Figure 5(I,J)). These results suggested that MEF2A play a central role in mediating the S protein- or LPS-induced upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF, IL8, and CCL2. Moreover, ATG10S likely exerted its anti-inflammatory effects by downregulating MEF2A protein levels, thereby suppressing MEF2A-mediated transcriptional activation of pro-inflammatory cytokine genes. ATG10S promotes autolysosome formation to facilitate MEF2A degradation

MEF2A has previously been reported to undergo autolysosomal degradation under both physiological and pathological conditions [30]. We observed that ATG10S significantly reduced MEF2A protein levels in both MDMs and zebrafish. Co-IP using an anti-LC3B antibody showed that ATG10S enhanced the interaction between LC3B, LAMP2, and MEF2A (Figure 6A and Figure 7(A)). In contrast, ATG10S did not promote interactions between LC3B and other transcription factors, such as IRF3 or NFKB (Fig. S3E), suggesting that ATG10S selectively facilitated the recruitment of MEF2A to autophagosomes. Consistently, immunofluorescence staining showed that ATG10S significantly increased MEF2A-LC3B colocalization in MDMs, as evidenced by a higher Pearson’s correlation coefficient and an increased number of MEF2A-LC3B colocalization puncta (Figure 6B and Figure S7B). In addition, a notable phenomenon appeared that as a transcription factor MEF2A location presented dominantly in cytoplasm and less in the nucleus. To elucidate the issue, a time-course immunofluorescence analysis under co-culture conditions was performed and revealed MEF2A from predominantly nuclear to predominantly cytoplasmic localization over time, even without stimulation (Fig. S7C, D). S protein stimulation increased the mean total MEF2A fluorescence intensity as early as 6 h post-seeding, with elevated signals detected in both nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments, progressing to predominantly cytoplasmic and perinuclear localization at later time points. In contrast, ATG10S significantly reduced MEF2A fluorescence intensity in both compartments since 12 h post-seeding, with nuclear MEF2A levels becoming lower than cytoplasmic levels over time (Fig. S7C-E). Correspondingly, LC3B-positive autophagosomes exhibited limited colocalization with MEF2A aggregates in the S protein-stimulated group, whereas ATG10S strongly enhanced LC3B recruitment to cytoplasmic MEF2A aggregates, increasing MEF2A+ LC3B+ puncta over time (Fig. S7C, F). Nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation assays confirmed that S protein elevated MEF2A levels in both nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions, whereas ATG10S decreased MEF2A levels in both compartments in co-culture condition, not in mono-culture condition (Fig. S7G). Using the iLIR autophagy database (https://ilir.warwick.ac.uk/search.php), we identified a putative LC3-interacting region (LIR) motif (residues 113–118; EKYKKI) within the MEF2A sequence. To assess its functional relevance, we generated a deletion mutant lacking this motif (MEF2A-ΔLIR). Immunofluorescence and co-IP analyses showed that MEF2A-ΔLIR failed to interact with LC3B, confirming that MEF2A bound LC3B directly through the identified LIR motif (Figure 6C,D and Figure 7(H)). To confirm the reliability of this result, MEF2A antibody specificity was validated by the marked reduction of MEF2A signal in the MDMs with MEF2A knockdown compared to the control siRNA group (Fig. S7I). These results suggested that ATG10S promoted direct interaction between MEF2A and LC3B, thereby facilitating MEF2A degradation via the autolysosomal pathway. To further clarify the degradation pathway, we used chloroquine (CQ), an inhibitor of autophagosome-lysosome fusion, and MG132, a proteasome inhibitor. Western blotting showed that CQ abolished the ATG10S-induced reduction in MEF2A protein levels under S protein or LPS stimulation, whereas MG132 had no significant effect (Figure 6(E)). These results confirmed that ATG10S promoted selective degradation of MEF2A through the autolysosomes rather than the proteasomal pathway. Additionally, luciferase reporter assays showed that CQ substantially reversed ATG10S-mediated suppression of MEF2A transcriptional activity on the promoters of IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF, IL8, and CCL2 (Figure 6(F)). Despite this reversal, MEF2A transcriptional activity remained significantly lower than in the absence of ATG10S, suggesting that ATG10S may also inhibit MEF2A-driven transcription through partly degradation-independent mechanisms, in addition to promoting its autolysosomal degradation.

The MEF2 domain of MEF2A mediates its direct interaction with ATG10S

Given that ATG10S suppressed MEF2A-mediated pro-inflammatory responses induced by S protein or LPS, we investigated the direct interaction between ATG10S and MEF2A. Upon endogenous ATG10S knockdown, ATG10S failed to affect MEF2A, LC3B-II/I, and LAMP2 protein levels compared to the control-siRNA group under S protein or LPS stimulation. Furthermore, co-IP using an anti-LC3B antibody showed that ATG10S did not enhance the interaction among LC3B, MEF2A, and LAMP2 in the endogenous ATG10S-knockdown group, likely due to insufficient ATG10S protein levels. Notably, only in the ATG10S + control-siRNA group were MEF2A levels reduced, ATG10S and LC3B-II levels increased, and LC3B interaction with MEF2A and LAMP2 enhanced (Figure 7A and Figure S8A), suggesting that a threshold concentration of ATG10S was required for MEF2A autophagic degradation. In both S protein- or LPS-induced pro-inflammatory cell and zebrafish models, co-IP using an anti-His antibody (targeting His-tagged ATG10S) confirmed that ATG10S physically interacted with LAMP2, LC3B, and MEF2A (Figure 7B and Figure S8B). In contrast, ATG10S did not interact with other transcription factors such as IRF3 or NFKB (Fig. S8C), indicating that ATG10S specifically targeted MEF2A and facilitated its recruitment to the autolysosome. Additionally, immunofluorescence and nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation combined with co-IP in MDMs revealed that ATG10S colocalized and interacted with MEF2A in both cytoplasm and nucleus, coinciding with a global reduction in MEF2A protein levels (Figure 7C,D and Figure S8D). Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assays further confirmed that ATG10S bound directly and specifically to MEF2A in a concentration-dependent manner, with detectable binding in the range of 133 to 2125 nM. The dissociation constant (KD) was calculated as 1.436 × 10−6 based on at least three independent experiments, indicating moderate binding affinity (Figure 7E). The MEF2 domain of MEF2A is a co-factor interaction region that recruits proteins to modulate its transcriptional activity [16] (Figure 7(F)). To identify the ATG10S-binding region, we constructed an HA-tagged MEF2A plasmid (pIE-HA-MEF2A) and a deletion mutant lacking the MEF2 domain (pIE-HA-∆MEF2A). Co-IP assays using anti-His antibody showed that ATG10S bound to MEF2A but not to the ∆MEF2A mutant (Figure 7(G)), indicating that the MEF2 domain was essential for this interaction. DNA-IP and luciferase assays further revealed that ATG10S significantly inhibited both the promoter-binding ability and transcriptional activity of HA-MEF2A on pro-inflammatory cytokine genes. In contrast, HA-∆MEF2A retained its promoter-binding capacity and transcriptional activity, both of which were unaffected by ATG10S (Figure 7(H,I)). These results suggest that MEF2 domain deletion did not impair MEF2A’s transcriptional function, and abolished the ATG10S inhibition on MEF2A activity. This strongly supported that the MEF2 domain was the essential region mediating MEF2A-ATG10S interaction.

ATG10S disrupts TLR4-MAPK7-MEF2A signaling axis by competing with MAPK7 for binding to MEF2A

Considering that ATG10S inhibited the MEF2A transcriptional activity (Figure 7(H,I)) and physically interacted with MEF2A in the nucleus and cytoplasm (Figure 7(C,D)). Notably, inhibition of autophagy also did not fully reverse the ATG10S-mediated suppression of transcriptional activity of pro-inflammatory cytokine promoters (Figure 6F). We suppose that, beyond promoting MEF2A degradation via the autolysosomal pathway, ATG10S may also function as a direct transcriptional repressor attenuating MEF2A transcriptional activity on multiple pro-inflammatory cytokine promoters. Since both the S protein and LPS are known to induce robust pro-inflammatory cytokine expression via the TLR4 signaling pathway [7,8], we examined the role of TLR4 and its downstream effectors. Western blotting revealed increased levels of TLR4, MAPK7, phosphorylated MAPK7 (p-MAPK7), MEF2A, and phosphorylated MEF2A (p-MEF2A) following S protein and LPS stimulation (Figure 8A and Figure S8E). TLR4 knockdown markedly decreased p-MAPK7 and p-MEF2A levels without altering total MAPK7 or MEF2A protein. Similarly, MAPK7 knockdown specifically reduced p-MEF2A levels without affecting total TLR4 or MEF2A, whereas MEF2A knockdown had no impact on TLR4, MAPK7, or p-MAPK7 levels (Figure 8B and Figure S8F). Luciferase assays further confirmed that MEF2A transcriptional activity was significantly reduced following TLR4 or MAPK7 knockdown (Figure 8(C)). To further elucidate the molecular basis of MEF2A binding with ATG10S and MAPK7, we used AlphaFold 3 to predict their interaction sites. The model predicted that ATG10S binds to aspartic acid residues D61 and D63 within the MEF2 domain (residues 58–86) of MEF2A, whereas MAPK7 binds specifically to D63 (Figure 8(G)). To validate these predictions, we generated three HA-tagged MEF2A point mutants: HA-MEF2A∆D61, HA-MEF2A∆D63, and HA-MEF2A∆D61∆D63. Co-IP assays showed that ATG10S failed to bind any of the three mutants, indicating that both D61 and D63 were essential for ATG10S-MEF2A interaction (Figure 8(H)). In contrast, MAPK7 retained binding to HA-MEF2A∆D61, but not to HA-MEF2A∆D63 or HA-MEF2A∆D61∆D63, confirming the critical role of D63 in MAPK7-MEF2A interaction (Figure 8(I)). These results provided compelling evidence that ATG10S and MAPK7 competitively bound MEF2A at overlapping sites within the MEF2 domain, particularly at D63. By occupying this shared binding site, ATG10S disrupted the TLR4-MAPK7-MEF2A axis, thereby inhibiting MAPK7-mediated MEF2A activation and reducing its transcriptional activity, which ultimately suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokine expression in response to S protein or LPS.

In conclusion, ATG10S bound to the D61 and D63 residues within the MEF2 domain of MEF2A, facilitating its recruitment to autolysosomes and promoting its selective degradation. Additionally, ATG10S competed with MAPK7 for binding to MEF2A at D63, thereby interfering with MAPK7-mediated activation of MEF2A and attenuating its transcriptional activity. Through these dual mechanisms – autolysosomal degradation and competitive inhibition – ATG10S acted as a potent negative regulator of MEF2A activity, leading to a reduction in both MEF2A protein level and transcriptional function (Figure 9). Consequently, ATG10S markedly suppressed the expression of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF, IL8, and CCL2) induced by S protein or LPS. Discussion

Despite recent advances in cytokine storm therapies, the complexity of the inflammatory networks often renders single-cytokine inhibition ineffective in suppressing the cascading inflammatory response. Here, we demonstrated that ATG10S significantly downregulated multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines in S protein- or LPS-induced pro-inflammatory models, highlighting its potential as a broad-spectrum inhibitor of cytokine storms. Mechanistically, ATG10S exerted dual anti-inflammatory effects by facilitating MEF2A degradation via autophagy and interfering with the TLR4-MAPK7-MEF2A signaling axis.

The SARS-CoV-2 S protein is highly immunogenic and plays a pivotal role in eliciting host immune response, serving as the basis for COVID-19 vaccine development [31]. Due to frequent mutations, different viral variants (such as beta, gamma, and delta variants) exhibit distinct pro-inflammatory properties of their S proteins. Notably, the S protein of the original 2019-nCoV strain displays strong pro-inflammatory activity [32]. The S protein activates TLR4 signaling, which contributes to cytokine storm development [33]. Therefore, S protein-based models are widely used to investigate SARS-CoV-2-induced inflammation in vitro and in vivo [32,34,35]. In this study, we found that S protein or LPS significantly induced the expression and secretion of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF, IL8, and CCL2) only in a co-culture system of MDMs and BEAS-2B, but not in monoculture (Figure 1(D-G)). These findings suggested that the robust cytokine induction observed in co-culture arises from synergistic, bidirectional inflammatory crosstalk between macrophages (MDMs) and epithelial cells (BEAS-2B). Upon S protein or LPS stimulation via TLR4, macrophages release initial cytokines (e.g., TNF, IL1B), which prime epithelial cells for heightened responsiveness, resulting in amplified cytokine production (e.g., IL8, CCL2) that further activates macrophages. This establishes a positive feedback loop that is absent in monocultures. Our data showed that MEF2A knockdown in MDMs alone abrogated cytokine induction in both cell types (Figure 4(D-G)), identifying macrophage as the primary driver and MEF2A as a master regulator within this multicellular network. This paracrine loop is essential for the full manifestation of the cytokine storm phenotype [36–38]. Our results are consistent with the established concept that macrophage-epithelial crosstalk is a cornerstone of pulmonary inflammation [38]. Therefore, these findings underscore the importance of multicellular models for studying cytokine storm mechanisms and support their use as a physiologically relevant platform for evaluating anti-inflammatory therapies.

Using both a zebrafish in vivo model and a human cell co-culture model, we demonstrated that low concentrations of ATG10S protein significantly suppressed S protein-induced pro-inflammatory responses without altering S protein levels (Fig. S1A), indicating that the anti-inflammatory effects of ATG10S were not solely dependent on viral protein degradation but also involve direct modulation of host immune responses. Further investigations revealed that ATG10S restored the autophagic flux disrupted by S protein or LPS, consistent with its role as an autophagy regulator. Notably, ATG10S selectively promoted autophagic degradation of the transcription factor MEF2A, without affecting other key transcription factors such as IRF3 and NFKB, and without directly degrading pro-inflammatory cytokines. This specificity was mediated by direct binding of ATG10S to defined structural domains within MEF2A, suggesting that ATG10S induced selective autophagy through protein-protein interactions rather than broad, nonspecific autophagic activation. These findings provided mechanistic insight into the targeted immunomodulatory function of ATG10S and supported its potential as a precise regulator of inflammation. Moreover, ATG10S suppressed the expression of multiple cytokines (IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF, IL8, and CCL2) by inhibiting their common transcription factor MEF2A. Together with previous findings demonstrating its role as a nuclear transcription factor regulating type III interferon genes [23,25], these results underscore the functional versatility of ATG10S in gene regulation, functioning either as a transcriptional activator or as a selective transcriptional repressor.

To date, no direct evidence has linked MEF2A to inflammation induced by SARS-CoV-2 or LPS. This study is the first to identify MEF2A as a key transcriptional regulator of S protein- or LPS-induced pro-inflammatory responses. Specifically, we demonstrated for the first time that MEF2A simultaneously activated the transcription of multiple key pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF, IL8, and CCL2. MEF2A knockdown or transcriptional inhibition significantly reduced excessive pro-inflammatory responses, suggesting that MEF2A was a promising therapeutic target for cytokine storm intervention. Our experimental validation confirmed that MEF2A selectively regulated a subset of cytokines (IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF, IL8, and CCL2) which were central drivers of cytokine storms, further reinforcing MEF2A potential as a viable and specific therapeutic target. Notably, the pro-inflammatory function of MEF2A appeared to be cell-type-specific, as its depletion in macrophages – but not in epithelial cells – markedly reduced other cytokine production. This observation is consistent with the context-dependent functions of MEF2 factors; for example, siRNA-mediated MEF2A knockdown has been reported to induce pro-inflammatory responses in fibroblasts and muscle cells [39]. Importantly, in our co-culture model, MEF2A knockdown in either MDMs or BEAS-2B cells did not alter basal cytokine expression in the absence of S protein or LPS (Fig. S5B, C). This control experiment definitively excluded the possibility that the anti-inflammatory effects of MEF2A knockdown resulted from nonspecific cellular stress or compensatory inflammation, confirming that loss of MEF2A function was not intrinsically pro-inflammatory in our system. Together, these findings established that within the multicellular inflammatory microenvironment, MEF2A functions as a non-redundant, pathogen-activated pro-inflammatory driver specifically in macrophages. Although MEF2A is classically described as a predominantly nuclear transcription factor, our data suggested that its subcellular localization was dynamically regulated depending on the cellular context and cell culture time. In our co-culture system, MEF2A gradually redistributed from a predominantly nuclear localization toward the cytoplasm over time, even without inflammatory stimulation (Fig. S7C, D). This observation suggested that signals from the co-culture microenvironment may influence MEF2A localization. S protein stimulation appeared to increase MEF2A levels in both nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments, particularly in perinuclear cytoplasmic regions. In contrast, ATG10S treatment reduced MEF2A levels in both compartments, particularly less in the nucleus, and promoted the recruitment of LC3B-positive autophagosomes to cytoplasmic MEF2A aggregates (Figure S7C-G). Together, these findings supported a model in which cytoplasmic redistribution of MEF2A may precede and facilitate its selective autophagic targeting. ATG10S may further enhance the coupling of cytoplasmic MEF2A to the autophagy machinery. Although the upstream signals controlling MEF2A relocalization remain unclear, our data indicated a temporal and spatial association between MEF2A redistribution and ATG10S-mediated autophagic degradation. Moreover, ATG10S did not alter the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in co-cultured cells or zebrafish without S protein or LPS stimulation (Figure 1(B-G)), indicating its specificity for pathogen-induced inflammation. Collectively, these findings provided a strong rationale for developing ATG10S as a novel anti-cytokine storm agent with both specificity and safety. Importantly, this study was the first to identify MEF2A as a shared transcriptional activator of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines and to propose it as a molecular target for their coordinated suppression. This discovery may facilitate the development and screening of novel anti-cytokine storm therapeutics targeting MEF2A.

This study demonstrated that S protein or LPS strongly activated the TLR4-MAPK7-MEF2A signaling axis, as indicated by elevated expression and phosphorylation of MAPK7 and MEF2A. Silencing TLR4 or MAPK7 specifically reduced MEF2A phosphorylation and its transcriptional activity, without affecting total MEF2A protein levels, confirming MEF2A as a key downstream effector of the pathway. ATG10S effectively antagonized MAPK7-mediated activation of pro-inflammatory cytokine promoters yet did not affect the expression or phosphorylation of upstream TLR4 and MAPK7, indicating a highly specific regulatory effect on MEF2A. Mechanistically, co-IP assays revealed that S protein or LPS enhanced the MEF2A-MAPK7 interaction, which was disrupted by ATG10S. Structural modeling using AlphaFold 3, coupled with site-directed mutagenesis and protein-protein interaction assays, further demonstrated that ATG10S and MAPK7 competitively bound to the MEF2 domain of MEF2A (residues 58–86), targeting overlapping key residues – D61 and D63. Notably, D63 was essential for MAPK7 binding, while both D61 and D63 were required for ATG10S interaction. These findings indicated that ATG10S disrupted MAPK7-MEF2A binding by competitively occupying this key structural domain, thereby suppressing MEF2A-driven transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokine genes. Sequence alignment showed that D61 and D63 were highly conserved across all MEF2 family members (MEF2A, MEF2B, MEF2C, and MEF2D) (Fig. S9A), indicating that these acidic residues were necessary but not sufficient to determine ATG10S binding specificity. Our functional data showed that ATG10S exerted its anti-inflammatory effect predominantly through MEF2A: MEF2A was the only isoform strongly induced by S protein or LPS, the only isoform markedly reduced by ATG10S, the only isoform that robustly interacted with ATG10S in co-IP assays, and the only isoform whose overexpression reversed the cytokine-inhibitory effects of ATG10S (Fig. S9B, C). Therefore, although the binding sites were conserved among MEF2 family members, MEF2A functioned as the primary target mediating ATG10S-dependent cytokine suppression in our system. This suggested that the specificity of ATG10S for MEF2A was not solely determined by these conserved acidic residues, but likely arised from the unique structural context and surrounding sequences of the MEF2A, which may form a distinctive binding interface [40–42]. This represented an important direction for future structural studies. In summary, this study identified ATG10S as a novel anti-inflammatory modulator that suppressed MEF2A-mediated pro-inflammatory responses induced by SARS-CoV-2 S protein or LPS via a dual mechanism. First, ATG10S directly bound to MEF2A and promoted its targeting to autolysosomes for degradation. Second, in the nucleus, ATG10S competed with MAPK7 for binding to MEF2A, thereby disrupting the TLR4-MAPK7-MEF2A signaling axis and directly suppressing MEF2A transcriptional activity. Collectively, these mechanisms effectively downregulated the overexpression of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF, IL8, and CCL2), providing compelling evidence for the anti-inflammatory potential of ATG10S and highlighting its unique regulatory mechanism (Figure 9). A limitation of this study was the reliance on S protein stimulation rather than a live viral infection system. Evaluating the effects of ATG10S in authentic infection models would be an important direction for future research.

Cell lines and recombinant proteins

Normal human macrophages derived from peripheral blood CD14+ monocytes (MDMs) and their corresponding culture medium (BEBP02-054Q) were obtained from Lonza (Anaheim, CA, USA). Human lung epithelial cells (BEAS-2B) were obtained from the National Infrastructure of Cell Line Resource. BEAS-2B cells were maintained in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, 11,965,092) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37°C in humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Recombinant wild-type SARS-CoV-2 S protein (isolate: 2019-nCoV, 40,591-V08B1) was obtained from Sino Biological (Beijing, China). Human ATG10S protein containing a N-terminal hexahistidine tag (His tag) was heterologously expressed in E. coli and purified to high purity in our laboratory using Ni-NTA affinity chromatography (Sangon Biotech, C600793), as previously described [25].

Zebrafish strains and maintenance

Adult transgenic Tg(mpeg1:eGFP) zebrafish were maintained under standard laboratory conditions with a 14 h light/10 h dark cycle at 28.5 ± 1°C. Two breeding pairs, each consisting of one male and one female zebrafish, were selected for mating. Fertilized eggs were collected, washed, and cultured in zebrafish embryo medium. Only embryos with normal morphology were selected for further development. All zebrafish experiments were approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of the Institute of Medicinal Biotechnology, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (SYXK(Jing) 2022–0023), and conducted following the NIH Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

S protein- or LPS-induced pro-inflammatory models and ATG10S protein treatment

For the S protein- or LPS-induced pro-inflammatory zebrafish model, 2 dpf Tg(mpeg1:eGFP) transgenic zebrafish were injected into the tail muscle with 1 nL of 0.25 mg/mL S protein or 10 μg/mL LPS from Escherichia coli O55:B5 (Sigma Aldrich, L2880). Macrophage aggregation in the tail region was visualized using a fluorescence microscope at 2-, 6-, 12-, and 24-hpi. At 12 hpi, the mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (ifn1, ifng, il1b, il6, tnfa, il8, and ccl2) in both the whole body and tail of zebrafish larvae were analyzed by qRT-PCR. Corresponding protein levels were assessed by western blotting.

For the S protein- or LPS-induced pro-inflammatory cell model, MDMs and BEAS-2B cells were co-cultured using a Transwell system for 24 h. The co-cultures were treated with 0.25 mg/mL S protein or 10 μg/mL LPS for another 24 h. mRNA and protein levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF, IL8, and CCL2) in MDMs and BEAS-2B cells were measured by qRT-PCR and western blotting, respectively. Cytokine secretion (IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF, and IL8) levels in the co-culture supernatant were quantified using ELISA.

For ATG10S treatment, 2.5 μM of His-tagged ATG10S was co-administered with either S protein or LPS in both zebrafish and cell models.

Plasmid construction

Promoter regions (2 kb upstream) of human pro-inflammatory cytokine genes – including IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF, IL8, and CCL2–were downloaded from the Ensembl genome database (http://grch37.ensembl.org/index.html). Each promoter sequence was cloned into the pGL3.0-basic luciferase reporter vector (Promega, E1751), generating constructs designated as IFNA-pro, IFNG-pro, IL1B-pro, IL6-pro, TNF-pro, IL8-pro, and CCL2-pro. Deletion variants of each promoter, lacking the MEF2A-binding motif, were similarly cloned into pGL3.0-basic vector and named IFNA-pro∆BD, IFNG-pro∆BD, IL1B-pro∆BD, IL6-pro∆BD, TNF-pro∆BD, IL8-pro∆BD, and CCL2-pro∆BD. Full-length coding sequences of human MAPK7, MEF2A, MEF2B, MEF2C, and MEF2D, along with MEF2A deletion mutants (∆MEF2A and MEF2A∆LIR) and site-directed mutants (MEF2A∆D61, MEF2A∆D63, and MEF2A∆D61∆D63), each tagged with an N-terminal HA tag, were cloned into the pIRES2-EGFP vector, The resulting constructs were designated as pIE-HA-MAPK7, pIE-HA-MEF2A, pIE-HA-MEF2B, pIE-HA-MEF2C, pIE-HA-MEF2D, pIE-HA-∆MEF2A, pIE-HA-MEF2A∆LIR, pIE-HA-MEF2A∆D61, pIE-HA-MEF2A∆D63, and pIE-HA-MEF2A∆D61∆D63. All plasmid constructs were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co (Shanghai, China).

Transfection

To overexpress human MEF2A, MEF2B, MEF2C, and MEF2D and zebrafish MEF2A, 5’-capped mRNAs were synthesized in vitro using the mMESSAGE mMACHINE™ T7 transcription kit (Invitrogen, AM1344). Human MEF2A, MEF2B, MEF2C, and MEF2D mRNA (100 ng per well in a 6-well plate) were transfected into cells using Lipofectamine 2000. Zebrafish mef2a mRNA (1 ng) was microinjected into each embryo at the one-cell stage.

For overexpression of MAPK7 and MEF2A mutants (HA-MEF2A, HA-∆MEF2A, MEF2A∆LIR, HA-MAPK7, HA-MEF2A∆D61, HA-MEF2A∆D63, and HA-MEF2A∆D61∆D63), each plasmid (1 μg per well in a 6-well plate) was transfected into cells using Lipofectamine 2000.

To knock down endogenous MEF2A, TLR4, and MAPK7 in human cells, siRNA targeting MEF2A (sc-35895), TLR4 (sc-40260), MAPK7 (sc-35339), and control-siRNA (sc-37007) were obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. These siRNAs were transfected into cells at a final concentration of 5 nM using Lipofectamine 2000. In zebrafish, Mef2a knockdown was achieved by microinjecting MOs at 1 nM, using a standard control MO as the negative control. The sequence of the zebrafish mef2a MO (Gene Tools, LLC) was 5’-GATCTGTATCTTCTTCCGTCCCATC-3,’ and the standard control MO sequence was 5”-CCTCTTACCTCAGTTACAATTTATA-3.”

To knock down endogenous ATG10S, as previously described [23], ATG10 siRNA (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-72576) was used, which also suppressed ATG10. To ensure the ATG10 function, co-transfection of ATG10 siRNA and ATG10 mRNA was performed.

Autophagy flux assay

As previously described [25], MDMs and BEAS-2B cells were seeded into a two-well silicone insert (ibidi, 80,206). After cell attachment, the insert was removed, and the two cell types were co-cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Co-cultures were then treated with 0.25 mg/mL S protein, 10 μg/mL LPS, or co-treated with either stimulus in combination with 2.5 μM ATG10S for 24 h. Cells were subsequently incubated for an additional 24 h with a tandem sensor RFP-GFP-LC3B probe (Thermo Fisher Scientific, P36239). The cells were fixed in 1% paraformaldehyde (Applygen Technology, B1057) for 15 min at room temperature. After three washes with 1×PBS (Applygen Technology, B1202), nuclei were stained with DAPI and visualized using a DeltaVision Imaging System (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). A representative image from one of eight randomly selected fields (20 cells per field) was shown for each group.

Chemicals treatments

MDMs and BEAS-2B cells were co-cultured in a Transwell system and exposed for 24 h with one of the following treatments: 0.25 mg/mL S protein, 10 μg/mL LPS, or a combination of either stimulus with 2.5 μM ATG10S. Additional treatment groups included co-treatment with 10 μM CQ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, P36239), or 10 μM MG132 (Sigma-Aldrich, M8699). After 24 h of treatment, MDMs and BEAS-2B were separately collected for subsequent experiments.

Co-immunoprecipitation

DNA IP assay

MDMs were co-transfected with plasmids containing either wild-type or MEF2A-binding motif-deleted promoter constructs of pro-inflammatory cytokine genes, along with MEF2A mRNA or expression vectors (pIE-HA-MEF2A or pIE-HA-∆MEF2A). After transfection, MDMs were co-cultured with BEAS-2B cells and treated for 24 h with 0.25 mg/mL of S, 10 μg/mL LPS, or co-treated with 2.5 μM ATG10S. After treatment, MDMs were harvested and subjected to DNA IP using the SimpleChIP Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, 9003). Briefly, cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde at 37°C to fix protein-DNA interactions. After 10 min, cells were resuspended in a lysis buffer containing 1% SDS and 1 mM PMSF, followed by sonication. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation, and the chromatin was immunoprecipitated using anti-MEF2A, anti-His, or anti-HA antibodies. Immunoprecipitated DNA was purified and analyzed by qRT-PCR using specific primers. Luciferase activity assay

MDMs were initially transfected with 1 μg of luciferase reporter plasmids containing either wild-type or MEF2A-binding motif-deleted pro-inflammatory cytokine promoters, along with 10 ng of pRL-SV40-N plasmid (Beyotime Biotechnology, D2762) as an internal control for transfection efficiency. After 6 h, cells were further transfected with 100 ng of MEF2A mRNA and 1 μg of either pIE-HA-MEF2A, pIE-HA-∆MEF2A or pIE-HA-MAPK7 plasmids. Subsequently, MDMs were co-cultured with BEAS-2B cells and treated with 0.25 mg/mL S protein, 10 μg/mL LPS, or co-treated with either 2.5 μM ATG10S or 10 μM CQ. After 24 h, MDMs were harvested and luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, RG027), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Luminescence was detected using a Centro XS3 LB 960 microplate luminometer (Berthold Technologies, Bad Wildbad, Germany). Relative luciferase activity was calculated by normalizing firefly luciferase signals to Renilla luciferase, and results were expressed as relative light units (RLUs).

Nucleus-cytoplasm fractionation

As previously described [23,25], MDMs and BEAS-2B cells were cultured either separately or co-cultured in a Transwell system for 24 h, followed by treatment with 0.25 mg/mL S protein, 10 μg/mL LPS, or in combination with 2.5 μM ATG10S. After an additional 24 h of treatment, the MDMs were harvested. Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractionation was performed using the NE-PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagents kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 78,835), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Nuclear and cytoplasmic extracts were obtained. Immunoblotting was performed to detect ATG10S or MEF2A in these extracts using anti-His or anti-MEF2A antibodies. LMNB1 and HSP90 were used as nuclear and cytoplasmic markers, respectively, to verify the quality and accuracy of the fractionation. Co-IP was performed using anti-His antibody to assess the interaction between ATG10S and MEF2A in nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The secretion levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFNA, IFNG, IL1B, IL6, TNF, IL8, and CCL2) in the co-culture supernatant of MDM and BEAS-2B cells were measured using ELISA kits, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Surface plasmon resonance assay

The binding interaction between recombinant human MEF2A protein and ATG10S was evaluated using surface plasmon resonance (SPR) on a Biacore T200 instrument (GE Healthcare). MEF2A (27 μg/mL) was immobilized onto a Series CM5 sensor chip (GE Healthcare) in sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.5) using the standard amine coupling method. Serially diluted ATG10S (133, 266, 531, 1062, and 2125 nM) were prepared in running buffer (1×PBS, pH 7.4) and injected over the MEF2A-immobilized surface for 60 seconds at a flow rate of 30 μL/min at room temperature. After each injection cycle, the sensor surface was regenerated using glycine-HCl (pH 1.5). The binding affinity constant was calculated using a 1:1 Langmuir binding model via BIAevaluation software (GE Healthcare).

Protein-protein interaction prediction

To predict potential interaction interfaces between MEF2A and ATG10S, and between MEF2A and MAPK7, protein-protein complex modeling was performed using AlphaFold Server 3 (https://alphafoldserver.com/). Amino acid sequences of human MEF2A (Q02078) and MAPK7 (Q13164) were obtained from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/), while the sequence of human ATG10S (XP_005248669.1) was obtained from the NCBI protein database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein). These sequences were submitted to AlphaFold Server 3 using default parameters to generate high-confidence structural models of protein-protein complexes. The predicted complexes were automatically ranked by confidence scores, and the top-scoring model for each protein pair was selected for further structural analysis. Structural visualization and image generation of predicted interaction interfaces were conducted using PyMol (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 3.0; Schrödinger, LLC).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical significance was assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
