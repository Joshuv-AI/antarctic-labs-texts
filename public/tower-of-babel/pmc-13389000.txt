Ultra-broadband wireless rectification and frequency mixing via the nonlinear Hall effect in TaIrTe4
Authors: Fanrui Hu, Jiayu Lei, Shishun Zhao, Qu Yang, Nils Dessmann, Thom Janssen, Chenhui Zhang, Hao Yu, Peng Yu, Andrei Kirilyuk, Hyunsoo Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Driven by the advancements in the Internet of Things and emerging 6 G technologies, the demand for broadband energy harvesting and frequency conversion is surging. Traditional semiconductor devices face fundamental challenges at high frequencies, particularly in the terahertz range. Here, we demonstrate an ultra-broadband rectifier and a passive mixer based on the nonlinear Hall effect (NLHE) in a Weyl semimetal TaIrTe4. The device achieves rectification across a broad range, from 19 MHz to 2.88 THz, at room temperature, operating without electrical bias or an external magnetic field. By employing series connections, we enhance the rectified voltage and accomplish polarization-insensitive operation. Furthermore, we realize a broadband (0.1–40 GHz) passive mixer with a conversion gain of − 20 dB and high linearity (IP1dB = 4 dBm) based on NLHE in TaIrTe4. These results establish NLHE in quantum materials as a powerful platform for next-generation high-frequency electronics.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of the Internet of Things (IoT), wearables, and portable electronics has created a pressing need for sustainable power solutions, especially in environments where frequent battery replacement or recharging is impractical1–3. With billions of smart devices deployed globally, developing self-powered energy sources is critical for their continuous operation4. 5 G and emerging 6 G technologies generate abundant ambient electromagnetic (EM) waves across a wide spectrum, from lower GHz to millimeter-wave (mmWave) and terahertz (THz) bands5–7. Without effective energy conversion mechanisms, this EM energy remains largely untapped. Broadband energy harvesters can convert EM waves across diverse frequency bands into usable electrical power, offering a promising solution for sustainable and portable power sources while optimizing the use of ambient EM energy.

The core of an energy harvester is a rectenna, which combines an antenna to capture EM energy and a rectifier to convert the EM wave into direct current (DC). Broadband rectifiers have been developed using nonlinear components, such as Schottky diodes8,9, field-effect transistors (FETs)10–13, and ballistic rectifiers14. Advanced broadband rectifiers based on Schottky junctions, including those utilizing two-dimensional MoS2, have demonstrated continuous operation from 0.1 GHz to 10 GHz8. However, extending their efficient operation into the THz range, which is vital for future communication bands, remains challenging due to the parasitic capacitance at higher frequencies15. Early CMOS FET rectifiers exhibited broadband responsivity from 0.27 to 1.05 THz10, while more recent FET devices have demonstrated rectification at approximately 3 THz13. Similarly, graphene ballistic rectifiers have also achieved THz broadband rectification from 0.07 to 0.69 THz14. Despite the performance in the THz range, these rectifiers are limited in harvesting energy from commonly used lower frequencies, such as 2.4 GHz for Wi-Fi. Therefore, developing a rectifier with energy-harvesting capability that covers MHz to THz frequencies is valuable for advancing self-powered IoT devices, 6 G networks, and other emerging technologies.

Frequency mixers are another critical high-frequency component in modern communication systems, utilizing nonlinear components to generate sum and difference frequencies for signal modulation and demodulation16. Mixer designs primarily rely on CMOS and III-V semiconductor technologies16,17. However, achieving high linearity and broadband operation remains challenging16,18. In addition, the fabrication complexity of CMOS mixers increases at higher frequencies, as deep-submicron scaling introduces difficulties such as increased flicker noise, reduced voltage headroom, and weaker port-to-port isolation16,18.

The nonlinear Hall effect (NLHE) has recently emerged as a promising mechanism for developing rectifiers and mixers, offering a simple, junction-free design for high-frequency applications19–22. In NLHE, an alternating EM field generates a transverse Hall voltage without requiring an external magnetic field. This Hall voltage contains both a DC component for rectification and higher-order harmonics for frequency mixing. Compared with conventional junction-based technologies, NLHE primarily arises from intrinsic properties of quantum materials, such as the Berry curvature dipole (BCD)20,23 or quantum metric dipole24,25, which enable signal modulation without junctions at high-frequency19,26,27. Extrinsic mechanisms may also contribute to NLHE28–30. Recent studies have shown NLHE-induced rectification in specific frequency regimes, including the GHz21,24,31–34 and the THz frequencies35,36, each within a restricted spectral window. Mixers driven by NLHE have so far lacked systematic evaluations of key performance metrics such as operational bandwidth and linearity22,36. Here, we show an ultrabroadband nonlinear Hall rectifier operating from tens of MHz to several THz, enabling a single-device platform for broadband wireless signal conversion. The scalability of the rectifier through array integration highlights its potential for wireless energy harvesting capability. Beyond rectification, we quantitatively evaluate key parameters for frequency mixing, underscoring the potential of NLHE for broadband high-frequency electronics.

Results

NLHE in TaIrTe4

Our experiment is conducted in Td-phase TaIrTe4, a type-II Weyl semimetal. The crystal structure of TaIrTe4, depicted in Fig. 1a, belongs to the noncentrosymmetric orthorhombic space group (Pmn21)37,38. The elemental components of the material are identified by energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (Supplementary Note 1). The broken inversion symmetry in this material separates the positive and negative Berry curvatures in different momentum regions, generating a significant BCD20,21,39, which drives the intrinsic NLHE. The linear behavior aligns with theoretical expectations, as the rectified voltage generated by NLHE is proportional to the square of the incident EM field, VDC ∝ E(ω)2 (Supplementary Note 7), and the input power scales with the square of the EM field (Pin ∝ E(ω)2). This agreement confirms that the rectification signal originates from the NLHE in TaIrTe4. Linear power dependence is consistently observed at 345 MHz, 765 MHz, 18 GHz, and 31 GHz (Supplementary Note 8), further validating the power-dependent relationship.

Building on the rectification observed at lower GHz bands, we further investigate the broadband response at sub-THz frequencies (over 100 GHz). This frequency regime is considered pivotal for the next decade due to its wide applications in 6 G communication, advanced sensing and security technologies43–45. During the experiment, we irradiate the rectifier with EM waves in the waveguide D band (110–170 GHz) and WR4 band (170–260 GHz) and measure the rectified voltage along the b axis (Methods). The color maps in Figs. 2d and 2e reveal that the maximum rectified voltage is consistently observed throughout 110–260 GHz when the EM wave aligns with the a axis, consistent with the symmetry constraints of the NLHE. Measurements of the return loss of the 110–170 GHz frequency extender integrated with the horn antenna show increased reflection from approximately 130 GHz to 140 GHz (Supplementary Note 9), leading to reduced incident power at the device and a corresponding reduction in VDC in this frequency range. The continued response across this broad frequency range substantiates the inherent nonlinearity of quantum materials as the origin for the rectification signal, enabling operation beyond the frequency limitations imposed by junctions or barrier structures26,46. As control experiments, the same 110–260 GHz EM waves are directed at Pt and Cu devices, but no observable rectification response is detected (Supplementary Note 10).

Broadband frequency mixing based on NLHE

Mixers are essential components in wireless communication systems, which have been widely used for wireless networks, radar, and satellite communications50,51. A frequency mixer combines an RF signal with a local oscillator (LO) signal to generate sum and difference frequency components through nonlinear interactions. The schematic of the NLHE mixer is shown in Fig. 5a. The device is designed with two pairs of electrodes along the a axis of TaIrTe4 for injecting RF and LO signals, while the output is measured along the b axis, corresponding to the transverse nonlinear Hall component (Methods). We select 12.5 GHz as the LO signal and 18 GHz as the RF signal, both within the Ku band, commonly used in satellite communications and radar applications. Figure 5b presents the output power of the tuned RF signal and conversion gain (CG) as a function of the input power of LO, with the input power of RF fixed at 0 dBm. The NLHE mixer exhibits a CG of around –20 dB at the up-converted frequency of 30.5 GHz and around –23dB at the down-converted frequency of 5.5 GHz. In addition, the measured input 1-dB compression point (IP1dB) of 4 dBm indicates relatively high linearity, which is crucial for minimizing intermodulation distortion in high-frequency signal processing. To further assess the broadband performance of the device, we measure CG across a wide range of LO frequencies with a fixed RF frequency of 18 GHz, as shown in Fig. 5c. The mixer demonstrates up- and down-conversion over a broad LO range from 0.1 GHz to 40 GHz. The broadband nature of the response is a direct consequence of the intrinsic material property, which is not limited by thermal voltage threshold or the transition time of the semiconductor junction26. We note that the first demonstration of NLHE passive mixers was reported in NbP and Pt22, focusing on wired signal mixing at low frequencies without reporting key parameters such as CG or linearity (Supplementary Table 3), and wireless frequency mixing shown in Td-NbIrTe4 device36. Our work builds on this concept by achieving broadband frequency mixing with defined performance metrics. We have demonstrated broadband rectification and frequency mixing based on NLHE in TaIrTe4. Specifically, wireless rectification across an ultrabroadband spanning from 19 MHz to 2.88 THz at room temperature with zero bias voltage and zero magnetic field is obtained. Utilizing a series connection, we have enhanced the rectified voltage and reduced the sensitivity of rectifiers to the polarization. In addition, we have realized NLHE-based passive mixer with broadband, high linearity, and zero DC bias. The NLHE rectifiers and mixers offer advantages not only in an extensive frequency range but also in a simple single-layer structure, which eliminates the need for creating Schottky barriers or tunnelling junctions. Looking ahead, based on the scalability of the NLHE rectifier array, we anticipate the development of NLHE rectifiers made from thin films, capable of supporting wafer-scale array topologies. Such advancements would require thin-film materials with high second-order nonlinear Hall conductivity and low first-order conductivity along the Hall direction (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{{\sigma }_{b-{aa}}}{{\sigma }_{{bb}}}$$\end{document}σb−aaσbb) at room temperature. Moreover, rectification performance can be further optimized through a series of engineering strategies, such as integrating optimized antennas, designing impedance-matching circuits, and incorporating low-pass filters to maximize energy harvesting efficiency. This work establishes a strong foundation for the practical application of NLHE in ultrabroadband rectification and frequency mixing, paving the way for advancements in next-generation energy harvesting systems, wireless sensing, and low-power communication systems.

Methods

Growth of TaIrTe4

TaIrTe4 single crystals were synthesized via a self-flux method using elemental Ta powder (99.99%), Ir powder (99.999%), and Te lump (99.999%) in an atomic ratio of 1:1:12. The stoichiometric mixture was loaded in a quartz tube and then flame-sealed under a high vacuum of 10−6 Torr. Then, the quartz tube was heated to 1,323 K and held for 7 days, and slowly cooled to room temperature for 15 days to ensure a complete reaction. Subsequently, the samples were transferred to another evacuated quartz tube with quartz wool under a vacuum of 10-6 Torr, which was centrifuged at 873 K to divide the TaIrTe4 crystals from the surroundings of excess Te. Finally, bar-shaped TaIrTe4 single crystals with metallic lustre were obtained.

Raman spectroscopy

The Raman spectra were acquired using a Renishaw inVia Raman microscope. A linearly polarized 532 nm excitation laser was focused onto the samples through a 50× objective lens. The scattered Raman signals were collected through the same lens and directed back to the spectrometer in a parallel configuration. The sample was mounted on a rotation stage and rotated with a step size of 10°. All parameters were kept identical throughout the measurements to ensure consistency.

Device fabrication

TaIrTe4 samples were prepared by mechanically exfoliating single-crystalline bulk Td-TaIrTe4 onto Si substrates (1–10 Ω·cm) with a 300 nm thermally grown SiO2 layer. Rectangular-shaped flakes were selected for investigation. A 5 nm thick SiO2 capping layer was deposited on the flakes using sputtering to prevent material oxidation and degradation, with a base pressure of less than 2 × 10-9 Torr. The flake thickness was characterized by atomic force microscopy. For electrical transport measurements, standard Hall bar geometries were patterned using electron-beam lithography (EBL) and etched using argon ion milling. Electrical contacts with a Ta (5 nm)/Cu (70 nm)/Ta (5 nm) structure was patterned by EBL and an ultraviolet maskless lithography machine (TuoTuo Technology, UV Litho-ACA), and deposited via sputtering. For devices for rectification and frequency-mixing measurements, electrical contacts were directly patterned and deposited onto exfoliated flakes without prior etching into Hall-bar geometries. The same contact structure and fabrication tools were utilized for both transport and rectification devices, ensuring consistency throughout the device fabrication process.

Electrical transport measurements

The electrical transport measurements were conducted at room temperature under zero magnetic field. During the measurement, a constant-amplitude sinusoidal current with a frequency of 13.7 Hz was applied to the devices (Keithley 6221). Fundamental and second-harmonic frequencies of both longitudinal and transverse voltages were simultaneously recorded by four lock-in amplifiers (Stanford Research SR830).

Rectification measurements

For rectification at 19 to 40 GHz, the EM wave was emitted from a signal generator (Agilent E8257D) and transmitted to a broadband horn antenna (Enrichtek ATBDH10180 for 1-18 GHz, and ATBDH18400 for 18-40 GHz). The EM wave below 1 GHz was delivered through commercial broadband antennas (MKZBH). For rectification at 110-260 GHz, the RF signal was irradiated from a vector network analyzer (Rohde & Schwarz ZNA26) with an extender and delivered to broadband horn antennas (Eravant SAR-2507-06-S2 for 110-170 GHz, and SAR-2507-04-S2 for 170-260 GHz). The device was placed parallel to and at the center of the emission surface of the pyramidal antenna to receive the linearly polarized radiation with normal incidence. The rectified voltage was recorded by a nanovoltmeter (Keithley 2182 A) at room temperature with zero magnetic field and zero electrical bias. To ensure an unbiased assessment, the input power Pin is defined as the total power output from the EM source (signal generator, vector network analyzer, or free-electron laser source). This definition does not account for losses at the device interface, the micro-meter scale device area receiving the power, power attenuation due to air, or the gain of the transmitting antenna. In polarisation-dependent measurements, the device was rotated by a motorised precision rotation stage (Thorlabs PRM1Z8). The direction of the EM wave was fixed along the a-axis of TaIrTe4 unless otherwise stated. For power-dependent measurements, the input power Pin was gradually increased from the signal generator. The input power Pin was fixed to 5 mW unless otherwise specified.

For THz-range measurements, the source is the free-electron laser (FLARE) located at Radboud University. Its radiation comes in the form of 10 μs long macropulses consisting of picosecond-scale micropulses at 3 GHz repetition rate. Linearly polarized light from the laser was focused into a 5 mm diameter focal beam spot and irradiated the device at normal incidence. The voltage signal was recorded by the digital oscilloscope (PXIe-5162) at room temperature with zero magnetic field and zero electrical bias. Similar to RF rectification, the input power Pin is defined as the actual power output from the free electron laser source. The polarisation-dependent measurements used the same motorised precision rotation stage to rotate the device. For power-dependent measurements, the emitted power was adjusted by an attenuator.

The rectified voltage was measured between two contacts of the TaIrTe4 device using a Keithley 2182 A nanovoltmeter. The HI (High) and LO (Low) inputs of the nanovoltmeter are connected to the two electrodes of the device, labeled as V+ and V- in the optical images, respectively. The LO input of the nanovoltmeter is internally connected to the instrument ground, and the instrument is connected to earth ground via the rear-panel analog output. As a result, the TaIrTe4 device electrode connected to the LO input (V-) is grounded through the nanovoltmeter, while the device itself does not include a separate ground pad.

Frequency mixing measurements

The input RF and LO signals were emitted from two signal generators (Agilent E8257D). To ensure an unbiased assessment, the input power is defined as the actual power output from the EM source. For conventional wired measurements, the EM wave was directly injected into the device through bonded wires. For wireless measurements, the EM wave was delivered to the device through two broadband antennas (Enrichtek ATBDH10180 and ATBDH18400). The output power is measured by a spectrum analyzer (Agilent E4448A).
