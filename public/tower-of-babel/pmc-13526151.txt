Occupational Mycobacterium marinum Infection Following Fish and Aquaculture Exposure: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Jiazhen Chen, Hongli Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo describe the clinical characteristics, and treatment duration and outcomes associated with different therapeutic regimens for Mycobacterium marinum infection.MethodsWe report a case caused by Mycobacterium marinum, presenting with erythema and erosions on the left forearm and left fingers, which was confirmed by histopathological examination and polymerase chain reaction (PCR)–based gene sequencing. A narrative literature review was also conducted to summarize reported antimicrobial regimens, treatment durations, and clinical outcomes.ResultsIn the present case, the diagnosis was supported by positive acid-fast staining and PCR-based gene sequencing of the lesional specimen. No culture-positive isolate was obtained. The patient was treated with a combination of clarithromycin and rifampicin for four months, resulting in marked regression of skin lesions. The literature review indicated that monotherapy is mainly applied in patients with localized and superficial disease, with a typical treatment duration of 4–6 months. Dual-drug regimens represent the most commonly used therapeutic approach, with treatment courses generally lasting 3–5 months. Triple-drug therapy is primarily reserved for disseminated or deep-seated infections, with treatment durations ranging from 2 to more than 8 months.ConclusionMycobacterium marinum infection is prone to misdiagnosis due to its nonspecific clinical presentation. Definitive diagnosis relies on histopathological examination and molecular biological techniques. Treatment strategies should be individualized according to the extent and severity of lesions. Standardized combination therapy administered for an adequate duration is associated with favorable clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Mycobacterium marinum is a slow-growing nontuberculous mycobacterium widely distributed in freshwater and saltwater environments. Human infection usually occurs after contaminated water, fish, or aquatic materials are inoculated through minor skin injuries, producing cutaneous and subcutaneous lesions known as “swimming pool granuloma” or “fish tank granuloma”.1,2 Because the hands and forearms are commonly exposed during aquatic activities, more than 90% of reported infections involve the upper extremities.3 Delayed diagnosis may allow infection to extend into deeper structures, causing tenosynovitis, arthritis, bursitis, or osteomyelitis.4 Population-based studies indicate that M. marinum infection is rare, with reported annual incidences of approximately 0.04–0.15 cases per 100,000 population.3,5 However, its true burden may be underestimated because of its indolent course, low clinical awareness, and incomplete microbiological confirmation. Infection is strongly associated with recreational and occupational exposure to aquaria, fishing, aquaculture, fish handling, and seafood processing.2,3 Occupational outbreaks among fish-farm and fish-market workers further demonstrate the increased risk associated with repeated contact between injured skin and contaminated fish or water.6,7 The clinical manifestations are nonspecific and may resemble sporotrichosis, cutaneous tuberculosis, fungal infection, or foreign-body granuloma. Consequently, diagnosis is frequently delayed, and patients may receive repeated antibacterial or antifungal treatment without improvement. The median diagnostic delay reported in previous studies ranged from several months to more than six months.3,4 Diagnosis requires a compatible aquatic exposure history together with histopathological examination, mycobacterial culture under appropriate temperature conditions, and species identification using biochemical or molecular methods. Acid-fast staining may support the diagnosis but has limited sensitivity.8 Because randomized comparative trials are lacking, the optimal antimicrobial regimen and treatment duration remain uncertain. Localized superficial lesions may respond to a single active agent, whereas extensive, sporotrichoid, recurrent, or deep infections generally require combination therapy and, in selected cases, surgical intervention.9 Observational studies have reported cure rates of approximately 87%–90%, although deep infection is associated with longer treatment and a greater risk of treatment failure.2,5 In this report, we describe a case of M. marinum infection in a middle-aged man with long-term occupational exposure to fish and aquaculture products. Persistent erythema and erosions involving the left forearm and fingers had continued for eight months despite multiple previous treatments. We also reviewed the relevant literature to evaluate antimicrobial strategies and treatment durations, aiming to provide practical evidence for the recognition and management of this uncommon occupational infection.

Case Presentation

Patient Information

A middle-aged male patient presented with an 8-month history of recurrent ulcerative skin lesions on the left upper limb. Eight months prior to presentation, an erythematous patch initially appeared on the left arm (as shown in Figure 1Ai–Aiii). The lesion repeatedly developed erosion and ulceration with a small amount of exudate. Pruritus was absent, and only occasional mild pain was reported. The patient had previously received topical medications and oral antibiotics at a local hospital; however, the lesions recurred. Three months before admission, surgical debridement and dressing changes were performed, but wound healing was poor. New lesions subsequently developed on the fingers of the left hand, and oral cefaclor therapy produced no significant improvement.

The patient denied systemic symptoms such as fever, night sweats, or weight loss. On further inquiry, he reported long-term occupational exposure to fish and aquatic products through aquaculture breeding and transportation, although he denied a definite history of fish-spine injury. His past medical history was unremarkable, and he denied previous tuberculosis or other infectious diseases.

Dermatological Examination

Physical examination revealed two coin-sized dark-red plaques located on the left arm and fingers. The lesions were well demarcated and showed surface erosion and ulceration, partially covered with crusts. Subjective symptoms were mild.

Laboratory and Histopathological Findings

Routine laboratory tests, including complete blood count, urinalysis, and liver and renal function tests, were within normal limits. Serological tests for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) antibody, Treponema pallidum particle agglutination assay (TPPA), rapid plasma reagin (RPR), and hepatitis B surface antigen were all negative. Direct microscopic examination and culture for fungi from the lesions were negative.

Histopathological examination of a skin biopsy demonstrated papillomatous epidermal hyperplasia with hyperkeratosis and parakeratosis, along with focal ulceration. Granulomatous inflammation was observed in the dermis, with nuclear debris in the center of the granulomas and dense superficial lymphocytic infiltration (as shown in Figure 2A and B). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) gene sequencing identified Mycobacterium marinum.

Diagnosis

Cutaneous Mycobacterium marinum infection associated with occupational exposure to fish and aquaculture products.

Treatment and Outcome

The patient was treated with oral clarithromycin 250 mg twice daily and rifampicin 450 mg twice daily. Locally, boric acid wet dressings were applied, and topical mupirocin ointment was used to prevent secondary bacterial infection. Complete blood count and liver and renal function tests were monitored monthly.

After 1 month of therapy, the lesions had markedly decreased in size (as shown in Figure 1Bi–Biii). After 2 months, most lesions had resolved (as shown in Figure 1Ci–Ciii). Treatment was continued for a total of 4 months, after which the lesions had nearly completely regressed, leaving residual hyperpigmentation and mild atrophic scarring (as shown in Figure 1Di–Eiii).

Literature Review Methods

A narrative literature search was conducted using PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, Embase and CNKI, up to 012026, with the terms “Mycobacterium marinum”, “treatment”, “treatment duration”, and “clinical outcome”. English- and Chinese-language case reports, case series, and retrospective studies reporting antimicrobial regimens, treatment duration, and outcomes were included. Studies without identifiable treatment or outcome data were excluded. Because of substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity, the results were summarized descriptively.

Discussion

Mycobacterium marinum is a slow-growing nontuberculous mycobacterium commonly found in fresh and salt water environments, aquariums, and fish-handling settings. Human infection typically occurs after minor trauma that allows contaminated water to be inoculated into the skin, and the upper extremities are most frequently affected. Because the disease progresses slowly and symptoms are often mild, diagnosis is frequently delayed. Patients are commonly treated empirically for bacterial infection, sporotrichosis, or cutaneous tuberculosis before the correct diagnosis is established. In the present case, the patient experienced persistent lesions for approximately eight months despite topical therapy, oral antibiotics, and surgical intervention before appropriate identification was achieved. Such prolonged clinical courses are characteristic of this infection and contribute to underrecognition in routine practice.

Clinical manifestations are variable and nonspecific. Lesions typically present as papules, nodules, plaques, or ulcerations and may spread along lymphatic channels in a sporotrichoid pattern. Epidemiological exposure therefore represents an important diagnostic clue. Occupational or recreational aquatic exposure markedly increases the likelihood of infection, particularly when lesions are located on the hands or forearms. The patient in this report had long-term occupational exposure to aquatic products, supporting the clinical suspicion. Histopathology usually demonstrates granulomatous inflammation with or without necrosis, whereas acid-fast bacilli are often sparse. Culture may require prolonged incubation and may be negative in some cases. For these reasons, molecular diagnostic techniques such as polymerase chain reaction and sequencing are particularly valuable in chronic granulomatous dermatoses suspected to be caused by nontuberculous mycobacteria.

Treatment selection primarily depends on the extent and depth of infection rather than the duration of symptoms. Superficial localized cutaneous disease represents the most common presentation. Limited lesions may respond to monotherapy using tetracyclines, macrolides, or trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole. Treatment courses generally last approximately three to four months, and favorable outcomes are common, although clinical improvement rather than complete cure may be reported.5 Relapse appears more frequent after monotherapy, and therefore single-drug therapy is typically reserved for strictly localized disease.

When lesions are multiple, progressive, or distributed in a sporotrichoid pattern, combination antimicrobial therapy is preferred. Frequently used regimens include a macrolide combined with rifampicin, rifampicin combined with ethambutol, or other macrolide-based dual therapies. Treatment duration usually ranges from three to six months (Table 1). In the present patient, therapy with clarithromycin and rifampicin for four months resulted in marked regression of lesions, consistent with reported therapeutic experience in persistent cutaneous disease. Deep infections involving tendon sheaths, joints, or bone represent a distinct clinical entity. Compared with superficial disease, these patients require longer antimicrobial therapy and frequently require surgical intervention. Treatment durations commonly range from five to eight months and may extend beyond this period. Regimens generally include a macrolide combined with ethambutol, often with the addition of rifampicin. Surgical debridement is frequently necessary because organisms may persist within poorly vascularized synovial tissue, and delayed recognition can result in functional impairment.

Disseminated infection is uncommon but occurs mainly in immunocompromised individuals. These patients typically require multidrug therapy for at least six to twelve months, and relapse may occur if therapy is discontinued prematurely. Severe systemic involvement and unfavorable outcomes have been described, highlighting the importance of evaluating host immune status when determining treatment intensity and duration.

Although localized infection may occasionally respond to monotherapy, combination therapy appears to provide more reliable outcomes in most patients. Dual-drug regimens reduce relapse risk, improve antimicrobial efficacy, and decrease the likelihood of resistance development. In this case, clarithromycin combined with rifampicin achieved near-complete resolution, supporting the effectiveness of macrolide-based combination therapy for persistent cutaneous infection.

Determining the optimal duration of therapy remains challenging. Current clinical experience suggests that treatment should be continued for one to two months beyond clinical resolution to minimize recurrence. In general, superficial disease requires approximately three to four months of therapy, sporotrichoid or extensive disease three to six months, deep infection five to eight months or longer, and disseminated infection at least six to twelve months. The patient in this report received a four-month treatment course and demonstrated lesion regression with residual post-inflammatory pigmentation and mild scarring, consistent with typical outcomes.

In conclusion, Mycobacterium marinum infection is an important cause of chronic cutaneous granulomatous disease that is frequently misdiagnosed. Careful assessment of exposure history, recognition of characteristic clinical features, and appropriate laboratory testing facilitate early diagnosis. Treatment should be individualized according to disease severity, and adequate com antimicrobial therapy administered for a sufficient duration. In this patient, clarithromycin combined with rifampicin resulted in a favorable clinical response. However, the optimal regimen and treatment duration remain uncertain because current evidence is based mainly on observational studies and case reports.

Limitations

The findings of the literature review should be interpreted cautiously because most included publications were retrospective studies, small case series, or case reports. Differences in disease severity, treatment regimens, outcome definitions, and follow-up duration limited direct comparisons among monotherapy, dual-drug therapy, and multidrug therapy.
