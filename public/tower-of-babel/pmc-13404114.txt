Genome-wide identification of the kiwifruit AcTPS family and analysis of its drought-responsive expression patterns
Authors: Yinqiang Zi, Xiaozhen Liu, Zhuo Wei, Ping Tian, Rong Xu, Hanyao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrought stress severely restricts the growth, development, yield, and fruit quality of Actinidia chinensis, making it urgent to explore drought resistance genes and their regulatory mechanisms. Trehalose-6-phosphate synthase (TPS) genes are key genes involved in trehalose synthesis and metabolism, playing important roles in plant abiotic stress tolerance. Based on the genome database, transcriptome data, and leaf soluble sugar content analysis of A. chinensis, we investigated the response of A. chinensis and its AcTPS gene family to drought stress. Through bioinformatics and phylogenetic analysis, 13 AcTPS genes were identified, which were divided into two subfamilies and unevenly distributed across 11 chromosomes. Under drought stress, seven AcTPS genes showed significant expression changes, and five randomly selected genes were validated by qRT-PCR, with results highly consistent with the transcriptome data. Drought stress severely inhibited seedling growth, reduced leaf relative water content, and significantly promoted the accumulation of trehalose in A. chinensis leaves, indicating that the plant adapts to drought by regulating soluble sugar metabolism. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses indicate that AcTPS1 may be related to trehalose biosynthesis, and a potential time-lag effect between trehalose content and AcTPS1 gene expression was observed. Introduction

Kiwifruit (Actinidia chinensis Planch.) is an economically important fruit crop worldwide [1–3]. However, its cultivation is increasingly threatened by drought stress due to its shallow root system, large leaf area, and high transpiration rate [4]. Previous studies on kiwifruit drought resistance have primarily focused on physiological and ecological responses, such as changes in photosynthetic efficiency, antioxidant enzyme activity, and osmolyte accumulation Wang et al., [5]. Nevertheless, these physiological adjustments are often insufficient to sustain long-term drought tolerance [5], and only a few drought-responsive genes have been identified to date [6–8]. Consequently, the molecular mechanisms underlying drought resistance in kiwifruit, particularly the regulatory networks of key metabolic pathways and functional genes involved in stress adaptation, remain poorly understood.

Abiotic stresses such as drought, high salinity, and low potassium severely affect plant growth and survival [9, 10]. Along with soil nutrient limitations, these stresses are major environmental factors reducing plant productivity and quality [11]. Plants, being sessile, must withstand such stresses [12]. Drought stress is classified into moderate (40–45% field water capacity) and severe (25–30%) levels [13]. Its primary effects include stunted growth, damage to the photosynthetic apparatus, reduced photosynthesis, impaired seed germination and nutrient uptake, and decreased yield and quality [14–16]. To maintain growth and productivity, plants adapt to adverse conditions by activating and regulating specific stress-related genes [17, 18]. These genes are involved in cell signaling and cellular responses, including transcriptional control and the production of stress proteins and compatible solutes such as quaternary amines, amino acids, and polyols (e.g., trehalose) [19].

Trehalose is a non-reducing disaccharide that plays a critical role in regulating both biotic and abiotic stresses in plants [20, 21]. First discovered in the resurrection plant Selaginella lepidophylla, trehalose accumulates at high levels in cypress, enabling survival in arid environments [22]. Although the trehalose content is generally low in plants, it can be induced by environmental stresses. Transgenic plants overexpressing microbial trehalose biosynthesis genes exhibit elevated trehalose levels and enhanced drought, salt, and cold tolerance [23]. Under unfavorable conditions such as drought, high salinity, or extreme temperatures, cells synthesize large amounts of trehalose, which functions in osmotic maintenance, membrane protection, and signal transduction. Once stress is relieved, trehalose is rapidly degraded into energy substrates [24].

Trehalose-6-phosphate synthase (TPS) is a key rate-limiting enzyme in the trehalose biosynthesis pathway, catalyzing the formation of trehalose-6-phosphate from UDP-glucose and glucose-6-phosphate, which is then dephosphorylated to produce trehalose [25–28]. To date, TPS genes have been identified in multiple species: 11 in Arabidopsis (AtTPS1–11), 11 in rice (OsTPS1–11), 13 in poplar, 13 in apple, 20 in soybean, and 7 in watermelon [21, 29–34]. Extensive genetic evidence indicates that overexpression of TPS genes in plants directly leads to a significant increase in trehalose levels, thereby enhancing plant tolerance to various abiotic stresses. In rice, overexpression of OsTPS1 elevates trehalose content and simultaneously increases proline accumulation, thus enhancing resistance to low temperature, high salinity, and drought [35]. The rice OsTPS8 mutant is sensitive to salt stress and abscisic acid (ABA), whereas overexpression of this gene enhances salt tolerance in plants [36]; watermelon ClTPS3 is significantly induced by salt stress, and its heterologous overexpression in Arabidopsis improves plant salt tolerance [21]; Redillas et al. further confirmed that trehalose accumulation synchronously increases soluble sugar (glucose, fructose, and sucrose) levels by 1.5 to 3.5 fold, constituting an important physiological basis for drought and salt tolerance in rice [37]. Similarly, overexpression of the ThTPS gene from Tamarix hispida increases trehalose levels and enhances tolerance to salt and osmotic stress [38]. Under low-temperature stress, the VaTPS9 gene from Vitis amurensis confers stronger cold tolerance by regulating trehalose synthesis [39]. In summary, TPS genes play a central regulatory role in plant stress tolerance by directly driving trehalose synthesis and cooperatively modulating soluble sugar metabolism.

In fruit crops, research on TPS genes is relatively extensive. In apple, 13 MdTPS genes were identified, among which MdTPS1 and MdTPS4 were significantly induced by drought and salt stress; overexpression of MdTPS1 in Arabidopsis enhanced drought tolerance by promoting trehalose accumulation and activating stress-responsive pathways [32]. In grapevine, VvTPS1 was reported to be involved in the drought stress response, with its expression positively correlated with trehalose content and drought tolerance [40]. However, compared with other fruit crops, research on the TPS gene family in kiwifruit remains in its infancy. Only a few TPS genes have been tentatively annotated in the kiwifruit genome, and their expression patterns, biological functions, and regulatory mechanisms under drought stress are largely unknown. This knowledge gap limits the application of TPS genes in kiwifruit drought-resistant breeding, highlighting the necessity and significance of the present study.

In this study, we systematically identified members of the AcTPS gene family in A. chinensis and analyzed their physicochemical properties, phylogenetic relationships, conserved motifs, gene structures, chromosomal locations, protein interactions, and cis-acting elements. Using transcriptome data and qRT-PCR, we examined the expression patterns of AcTPS genes under drought stress and measured changes in the content of soluble sugars (sucrose, glucose, and trehalose) in leaves. Furthermore, integrated omics and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that the AcTPS1 gene is involved in trehalose biosynthesis in kiwifruit and may contribute to drought resistance. Future studies should validate the function of the AcTPS1 gene to provide new insights for breeding drought-resistant kiwifruit varieties.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

In this study, kiwifruit (A. chinensis cv. ‘Hongyang’) plants obtained from the collective cultivation room of Southwest Forestry University [41] were used as research materials. The plants were transplanted into a substrate consisting of coconut coir, peat, and perlite at a volume ratio of 2:4:4 and cultivated for four months, after which uniformly grown seedlings were selected for the experiment.

Data acquisition

The kiwifruit genomic data (A. chinensis cv. Hongyang v3.0) were downloaded from the Kiwifruit Genome Database (KGD, http://kiwifruitgenome.org/). The conserved domain sequence information for the TPS gene family was retrieved from the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/) under accession number PF00982, and a hidden Markov model (HMM) profile was constructed using HMMER3 software [42]. Protein sequences and genomic data of 11 Arabidopsis TPS genes were obtained from The Arabidopsis Information Resource (TAIR, https://www.Arabidopsis.org/) [43]. Rice genomic data were downloaded from the China Rice Data Center (https://ricedata.cn) [30]. Protein sequences and genomic data of 13 apple (Malus domestica) TPS genes and seven watermelon (Citrullus lanatus) TPS genes were retrieved from the Rosaceae Genome Database (https://www.rosaceae.org/) and the Cucurbit Genomics Database (http://cucurbitgenomics.org/), respectively [21, 32].

Identification of TPS gene family members in kiwifruit

First, the hmmsearch program of the HMMER software (http://HMMER.org) was used to identify protein sequences containing the TPS conserved domain in the kiwifruit genome [32]. Second, a BLAST search was performed using TBtools software, with the protein sequences of the Arabidopsis TPS gene family as queries against the A. chinensis genomic protein dataset. Sequences showing relatively high homology to the Arabidopsis TPS protein sequences were retained. The hmmsearch program was then applied to the protein sequences obtained from the BLAST analysis to search for conserved domains, and sequences with an E-value threshold of 1 × 10⁻⁵ were selected. Finally, the protein sequences derived from both the initial hmmsearch and the combined BLAST + hmmsearch steps were merged to generate the complete A. chinensis TPS gene family information library.

Physicochemical property analysis

The obtained protein sequences were submitted to the ExPASy ProtParam online tool (https://web.ExPASy.org/protparam/) for determination of physicochemical properties, including sequence length, relative molecular weight, theoretical isoelectric point, and hydrophilicity. Subcellular localization was predicted using WoLF PSORT (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/) [44]. The gene length, the 2000 bp upstream region of the coding sequence, and the coding region length were calculated using TBtools [45].

Phylogenetic analysis

To investigate the evolutionary relationships among the TPS gene families of A. chinensis, A. thaliana, watermelon, apple, and rice, we constructed a phylogenetic tree using the neighbor-joining (NJ) method with 1000 bootstrap replicates. The tree was based on the amino acid sequences of 13 AcTPS proteins from A. chinensis and 9 AtTPS proteins from Arabidopsis. The online tool ChiPlot (https://www.chiplot.online/) was used for tree visualization. Finally, the 13 AcTPS genes were classified into subgroups according to the grouping of Arabidopsis TPS genes.

Analysis of gene structure and conserved motifs

Gene structures of the AcTPS genes were analyzed using TBtools based on the genome annotation file (GFF3) of A. chinensis [46]. Protein domain prediction was performed using the NCBI CD-search online tool (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi) with default parameters. Conserved motif analysis of the AcTPS proteins was conducted using the MEME online tool (https://meme-suite.org/meme/), with the maximum number of motifs set to 10 and other parameters kept as defaults, to identify differences among AcTPS family members. All resulting visualizations were generated using TBtools and the ChiPlot online platform (https://www.chiplot.online/).

Analysis of cis-acting elements

To analyze the cis-acting elements in the promoter regions of the AcTPS genes, the 2000 bp upstream sequences of each gene were extracted from the A. chinensis genome annotation file (GFF3) using TBtools. These sequences were then submitted to the PlantCARE online tool (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) for promoter cis-acting element analysis [47].

Chromosome localization and covariance analysis

Chromosomal location information was extracted from the genome annotation file (GFF3) of A. chinensis. The chromosome position map of the AcTPS genes was generated using TBtools software, which was also used to retrieve chromosome length, gene position, and gene density information. Intraspecific covariance analysis among AcTPS family members was performed using the MCScanX program integrated into TBtools [44].

Protein interaction analysis of TPS genes

Protein-protein interactions among the 13 AcTPS proteins in A. chinensis were predicted using the STRING database (http://string-db.org/), which provides interaction networks for a wide range of species. The resulting interaction network was visualized and refined using Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1, https://cytoscape.org/release_notes_3_9_1.html). Proteins that did not form any interactions were excluded from the final network [46].

A. chinensis drought stress treatment and A. chinensis leaf transcriptome sequencing, hormone expression measurements, and soluble sugar content measurements

Before the formal experiment, we conducted a preliminary experiment with three drought levels: mild (60–65% field capacity), moderate (40–45%), and severe (25–30%). The results showed that under mild drought, the growth and leaf relative water content of kiwifruit seedlings were not significantly different from the control group, and no obvious stress phenotype was observed. Under severe drought, the seedlings (especially the younger ones) wilted or even died within a short period, making it impossible to complete the experimental period and subsequent sampling. Therefore, to study the changes in AcTPS gene expression and sugar metabolism within a stable stress response window, we selected moderate drought (40–45%), which induces a clear but non‑lethal physiological response. This choice was based on both the preliminary experiment and the reference to Bao et al. [13].

The optimal soil humidity for A. chinensis growth is 80–85%, and moderate drought stress occurs at 40–45% [13]. In this study, 18 uniformly sized seedlings were divided into a control group (soil humidity maintained at 80–85%) and a drought‑treated group (water withheld until humidity dropped to 40–45%, then maintained for 72 h). Physiological indices, including plant height and leaf relative water content (determined by the soaking‑oven drying method), were recorded for each group. Three leaf samples were randomly collected from each group, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and designated CK1–CK3 (control) and T1–T3 (drought‑treated). The samples were sent to Myvi Metabolic Biology (Wuhan, China) for transcriptome sequencing and measurement of soluble sugar (sucrose, glucose, and trehalose) contents.

qRT-PCR analysis

Using the leaf samples obtained from the above steps, the expression levels of AcTPS1, AcTPS3, AcTPS4, AcTPS9, and AcTPS12 were analyzed by qRT-PCR (Applied Biosystems, San Francisco, CA, USA). Additionally, using the control group (CK) as a reference, kiwifruit leaves subjected to drought stress for 0, 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 h were designated treatment groups (Ta, Tb, Tc, Td, Te, and Tf, respectively), with three biological replicates per group, and the expression of AcTPS1 genes was measured again.

Total RNA was extracted from A. chinensis leaves using a plant RNA extraction kit (DP432), and cDNA was synthesized using a Monad kit. The resulting cDNA was diluted 10‑fold and thoroughly mixed with the qRT‑PCR mixture. The Actin gene was used as an internal control, and gene‑specific primers for qRT‑PCR were designed using Primer 6.0 software (see Table S1). Standard qRT‑PCRs were performed using a QIAGEN kit, with at least three technical replicates per gene. Experimental data were analyzed using independent sample t‑tests in SPSS v21 software.

For qRT‒PCR detection utilizing the QIAGEN kit, the 10 µL reaction mixture comprised 5 µL of 2× SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (1× final concentration), 0.05 µL of QN ROX Reference Dye (applicable only for Applied Biosystems instruments, 1× final concentration), 0.7 µL of Primer A and 0.7 µL of Primer B (each at 0.7 µM final concentration), 2.55 µL of RNase-free water, and 1 µL of template (gDNA or cDNA, with a maximum input of ≤ 100 ng per reaction). The thermal cycling program commences with an initial heat activation step at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and combined annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s, and concludes with melting curve analysis postamplification.

Results

Identification and physicochemical properties of the TPS gene family in A. chinensis

Using Arabidopsis TPS protein sequences as a reference, we performed BLAST and hmmsearch against the whole-genome protein sequences of A. chinensis. After predicting conserved domains and removing sequences lacking complete TPS and TPP domains, 13 TPS genes were identified and named AcTPS1 to AcTPS13 based on their chromosomal locations. Physicochemical analysis (Fig. 1, Table S1) showed that these genes vary widely in protein length (336–934 aa), molecular weight (38.22–105.92 kDa), isoelectric point (5.52–9.48), and instability coefficient (34.03–44.84). Among them, AcTPS2, AcTPS6, AcTPS11, AcTPS12, and AcTPS13 had instability coefficients below 40, indicating stable proteins. The lipolysis index ranges from 75.15 to 92.33, which favors the thermal stability of globular proteins. Subcellular localization prediction using Plant-mPloc (Table S2) indicated that over 80% of the gene products are located in vesicles and chloroplasts.

Phylogeny of the A. chinensis TPS gene family

To understand the evolutionary relationships of TPS genes among different species, we constructed a phylogenetic tree using the amino acid sequences encoded by the TPS genes of A. chinensis, rice, Arabidopsis, apple, and watermelon. The results (Fig. 2) showed that the 13 AcTPS genes could be divided into two classes. In this study, AcTPS1, AcTPS7, and AcTPS9 belonged to the class I subclade of TPS genes in the phylogenetic tree, suggesting that these three genes may have functions similar to those of AtTPS1 and OsTPS1.

Gene structure and conserved motifs of the AcTPSs

Using the MEME online tool, we analyzed the conserved motifs of AcTPS protein sequences (Fig. 3A, C). Motifs 1, 2, 3, 5, and 6 were highly conserved but showed different positional distributions among proteins, possibly reflecting the structural characteristics of this gene family. Proteins of the same TPS type shared similar motifs: the class I subfamily contained only motifs 7, 8, and 9, while the class II subfamily contained motifs 1, 2, 3, 5, and 6. Motifs 7 and 8 were unique to class I, suggesting a potential specialized function for this subfamily. The distribution of conserved motifs may also be influenced by gene structure. Gene structure analysis of the 13 AcTPS genes (Fig. 3A, B) showed that class I subfamily members were similar in length and conserved motif distribution. The number of introns (7–19) and exons (8–22) varied widely among the 13 genes, indicating substantial structural diversity.

Cis-acting elements in A. chinensis TPS gene family promoters

To understand the response mechanism of AcTPS genes, we used PlantCARE to predict cis-acting elements in the 2000 bp promoter regions of 13 AcTPS genes. A total of 265 elements were identified and classified into six groups: phytohormone-responsive, environmental stress-responsive, light-responsive, plant-specific regulatory, MYB-binding, and protein-binding sites (Fig. 4A, B, C). Their relative abundances were phytohormone-responsive (39.62%) > light-responsive (29.43%) > environmental stress-responsive (16.60%) > plant-specific regulatory (7.93%) > MYB-binding (5.66%) > protein-binding (0.76%) (Fig. 4B). Excluding light-responsive and protein-binding elements, 69.81% of the promoter elements regulate gene expression and material metabolism, thereby enhancing plant resistance and adaptation to adverse environments. Phytohormone-responsive elements were the most abundant, indicating that under external stress, TPS gene expression is likely mediated primarily through phytohormone regulation.

Chromosomal localization and colocalization of the AcTPS gene

Chromosomal localization (Fig. 5A) showed that the 13 AcTPS genes were unevenly distributed across 11 chromosomes, with chromosomes 4 and 7 each containing two genes (the most). Gene duplication is essential for plant gene family expansion. In this study, 12 segmental duplication pairs were identified among the 13 AcTPS genes, with chromosome 7 having the most (five pairs). These results indicate that segmental duplications play a key role in the expansion of the AcTPS family. Additionally, covariance analysis of TPS genes between A. chinensis and rice, A. thaliana, and apple (Fig. 5B) revealed more homologous gene pairs with A. thaliana and the fewest with rice. This suggests that A. chinensis is evolutionarily closer to A. thaliana and more distant from rice.

Protein network interactions of the A. chinensis TPS gene family

Protein interaction network analysis of the 13 AcTPS proteins (Fig. 6) revealed that all 13 proteins interact with each other, indicating close relationships within this family. Among them, three proteins—AcTPS1, AcTPS7, and AcTPS9—are located at the center of the interaction network, suggesting that they may possess specific and multifunctional roles. The remaining proteins belong to the class II subfamily, and all class II proteins interact with the three class I proteins (AcTPS1, AcTPS7, and AcTPS9).

Phenotypic and physiological changes in A. chinensis seedlings under drought stress

Drought is a common environmental stress that accelerates transpiration and inhibits plant growth. In this study, 18 A. chinensis seedlings at the same growth stage were equally divided into a control group and a drought-stressed group (nine plants each). Drought was imposed by withholding water for 72 h, maintaining soil moisture at 80–85% in the control and 40–45% in the drought group (Fig. 7b, Table S3). Drought stress significantly inhibited plant growth, as evidenced by leaf wilting (Fig. 7a), increased height variation (Fig. 7c, Table S4), and significantly reduced leaf fresh weight and relative water content (P < 0.01; Fig. 7d, Table S5). Sugars are key regulators of photosynthesis, flowering, and abiotic stress tolerance. We measured sucrose, glucose, and trehalose contents in both groups. Under drought stress, all three sugars were significantly higher than in the control (P < 0.01; Fig. 7e, Table S6), indicating that plants accumulate soluble sugars to enhance tolerance to drought and other abiotic stresses.

Expression patterns and qRT-PCR results of the AcTPS gene under drought stress

To investigate the expression patterns of AcTPS genes under drought stress, transcriptome sequencing was performed on A. chinensis leaves grown at 40–45% soil moisture for 72 h, using seedlings grown at 80–85% soil moisture as controls (CK). The results (Fig. 8A, Table S7) showed that among the 13 AcTPS genes, seven were differentially expressed under drought stress compared with the CK. Among these, AcTPS4 and AcTPS3 were upregulated, while the remaining five were downregulated.

Heatmap of AcTPS gene expression under drought stress and qRT-PCR results for five AcTPS genes. A: Heatmap of AcTPS gene expression under drought stress; B: qRT-PCR results of five AcTPS genes; CK denotes the control group, T denotes the treatment group, N: P > 0.05, *: P < 0.05, **: P < 0.01

To further validate the accuracy of the transcriptome data, five AcTPS genes were randomly selected for qRT-PCR analysis. The results (Fig. 8B, Table S8) showed that, compared with the control group (CK), drought stress upregulated the expression of AcTPS3 and AcTPS4 while downregulating AcTPS1, AcTPS9, and AcTPS12. The correlation coefficients between the FPKM values from the transcriptome data and the qRT-PCR results were all above 0.75 (Table S9), indicating a strong correlation between the two datasets. These findings confirm the reliability of the transcriptome data and the validity of the identified differentially expressed genes.

To examine the expression dynamics of AcTPS1 under drought stress, qRT-PCR was performed at 0, 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 h after drought treatment (Fig. 9A, Table S10). The results showed that AcTPS1 expression was not significantly altered at 0, 12, or 24 h but was significantly upregulated at 36, 48, and 60 h of drought stress. The expression level exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease (Fig. 10B), suggesting that AcTPS1 plays a critical role in the drought response of A. chinensis. Notably, although AcTPS1 expression was downregulated after 72 h of drought stress (Fig. 8B), the trehalose content increased significantly (Fig. 7e), indicating a time-lag effect between trehalose accumulation and AcTPS1 expression.

GO enrichment

To further explore the biological functions of the AcTPS gene family, we annotated the 13 identified AcTPS proteins using the EggNOG-MAPPERS database and performed GO enrichment analysis integrated with transcriptome data. The results (Fig. 10) showed that the AcTPS genes were classified into three major GO categories: biological process, cellular component, and molecular function. Within the biological process category, enriched terms were mainly associated with sugar metabolism and osmotic regulation, while the cellular component category was predominantly linked to biological membranes. These findings indicate that AcTPS genes are closely involved in sugar metabolism and stress response pathways in kiwifruit.

Transcriptome-metabolome conjoint analysis

By integrating transcriptomic data, metabolomic data, and TPS gene analysis, the trehalose synthesis pathway in kiwifruit (A. chinensis) under drought stress was studied. The results showed (Fig. 11) that during drought stress, the sucrose content in kiwifruit leaves increased, followed by the catalytic synthesis of a large amount of trehalose through trehalose-6-phosphate synthase (TPS) and trehalose-phosphate phosphatase (TPP). Among them, the AcTPS1 gene may be the trehalose-6-phosphate synthase gene, while two genes, including Actinidia13573, may encode trehalose-phosphate phosphatase.

Discussion

A. chinensis is rich in vitamin C, minerals, carbohydrates, and terpenoids, conferring high economic and nutritional value [48]. Global climate change is characterized by a clear trend of rising temperatures and decreasing annual precipitation. Drought is a major abiotic stress that inhibits the growth, development, and yield of crops worldwide [49]. Under drought stress, the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and H₂O₂ continuously increase, leading to reduced water potential, photosynthetic rate, and stomatal conductance in kiwifruit leaves. Consequently, drought suppresses kiwifruit growth and production, hindering the development of the kiwifruit industry [5]. Therefore, it is crucial to select new drought-tolerant A. chinensis varieties, identify drought tolerance genes, and elucidate the plant’s response to drought stress.

Trehalose plays an important role in responses to biotic and abiotic stresses, and TPS genes are key for its synthesis. In this study, 13 AcTPS genes were identified and classified into two subclades (Fig. 2), consistent with the classification in Arabidopsis, rice, and poplar. Overexpression of AtTPS1 (subclade I) in Arabidopsis and OsTPS1 (subclade I) in rice enhances stress tolerance, suggesting that the subclade I genes of A. chinensis (AcTPS1, AcTPS7, AcTPS9) may share similar functions [35, 50]. The classification of AcTPS genes into two subclades based on phylogenetic analysis aligns with the classification schemes reported for TPS gene families in Arabidopsis, rice, poplar, and Cucumis sativus, in which TPS members are also divided into Class I and Class II clades [35, 41, 50]. The presence of 13 TPS members in A. chinensis falls within the range reported for other plants—such as 7 in C. sativus and 10 in petunia (Petunia hybrida)—indicating that the size of the TPS gene family varies considerably across species and may be associated with the specific ecological and physiological demands of each plant [51, 52].

Gene structure analysis revealed that the 13 AcTPS genes exhibit considerable variation in intron and exon numbers, ranging from 7 to 19 and 8–22, respectively. Similar variability has been observed in TPS gene families from other species, including watermelon, soybean, and rose (Rosa chinensis), indicating that structural diversity is a conserved feature of TPS genes across plant taxa [33, 53]. The conserved motif distribution within subclade I members suggests functional conservation within this clade, a pattern consistent with observations in C. sativus and petunia, where members of the same TPS clade share similar motif compositions and gene structures [54, 55]. The presence of stress-related cis-elements, including drought-responsive and ABA-responsive elements, in the promoter regions of AcTPS genes further supports their potential roles in abiotic stress responses. ABRE (ABA-responsive element) is a well-characterized cis-element that mediates ABA-dependent gene activation under osmotic stress conditions, and its enrichment in stress-responsive gene promoters has been widely documented in plants such as soybean (Glycine max) and petunia [21, 34, 52, 54]. The identification of ABA-responsive elements in AcTPS promoters suggests that the expression of these genes may be regulated by ABA signaling under drought stress, a hypothesis that warrants further experimental validation.

Gene duplication is a major driving force for the expansion and functional diversification of gene families in plants [55, 56]. In this study, 12 pairs of segmental duplications were identified among the 13 AcTPS genes, and most duplication events occurred on chromosome 7, suggesting that this chromosome serves as a hotspot for TPS gene resources. Consistent with this finding, segmental duplication has been reported as the primary mechanism driving TPS family expansion in various plant species, including C. sativus, soybean, and wheat (Triticum aestivum) [51, 53, 57]. The multiple segmental duplication events observed in A. chinensis also imply that this species may have undergone polyploidization during its evolutionary history, a phenomenon widely documented in angiosperms and known to contribute substantially to genome complexity and adaptive evolution [56, 58]. Moreover, duplicate genes often undergo subfunctionalization or functional divergence after duplication events, a process that requires further investigation to fully understand the functional diversification of AcTPS genes in relation to kiwifruit growth and stress responses.

Protein interaction network analysis showed that all 13 AcTPS proteins interact with each other, with class I TPS proteins (AcTPS1, AcTPS7, and AcTPS9) also interacting with class II TPS proteins. A similar interaction pattern has been documented in rice, where yeast two-hybrid assays revealed a complicated interaction network among OsTPS proteins, with the TPS domain being essential for these interactions to occur [30]. The formation of such protein complexes may facilitate the coordinated regulation of trehalose biosynthesis or enable class II TPS proteins to modulate the enzymatic activity of class I TPS members. Indeed, recent studies in rice have shown that OsTPS8 physically interacts with OsTPS1 and suppresses its TPS enzyme activity, thereby reducing trehalose-6-phosphate (Tre6P) levels and influencing downstream metabolic and developmental processes [59]. These findings collectively suggest that TPS-TPS interactions represent an evolutionarily conserved regulatory mechanism, and the interaction network identified in A. chinensis may similarly enable fine-tuned control of trehalose biosynthesis and stress-responsive metabolism. Future studies employing yeast two-hybrid or bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays are needed to validate the predicted protein–protein interactions among AcTPS members and to elucidate their functional significance under drought stress.

In summary, the bioinformatics analyses presented here provide a comprehensive characterization of the AcTPS gene family in A. chinensis, including its phylogenetic relationships, gene structures, promoter cis-elements, duplication events, and protein interaction network. These findings are consistent with high-quality studies on TPS gene families in other plant species, such as rose and soybean, and they lay a solid foundation for future functional investigations of AcTPS genes in drought stress responses [53, 57].

Drought affects a range of physiological processes in plants, limiting overall growth, development, and productivity [16]. In the present study, drought treatment was applied to kiwifruit seedlings (Fig. 7). Under drought conditions, the growth of A. chinensis was severely inhibited, with leaf wilting and a significant decrease in relative water content. This suggests that under drought, plants reduce nutrient uptake and suppress growth, which accelerates transpiration rates and reduces leaf relative water content [60].

Transcriptomic data provide a new approach for studying gene expression patterns and potential functions. In this study, we analyzed the expression of AcTPS genes in A. chinensis under drought stress. Among the 13 AcTPS genes, seven were differentially expressed; the expression levels of five AcTPS genes were randomly validated by qRT‒PCR, and the results showed trends consistent with the transcriptomic data. GO enrichment analysis (Fig. 11) linked AcTPS genes to sugar metabolism pathways, suggesting that they help plants adapt to unfavorable environments by regulating sugar levels. Class I TPS genes are known to possess TPS activity [61]. For example, overexpression of OsTPS1 improved stress tolerance in rice [35], and overexpression of ClTPS3 enhanced salt tolerance in Arabidopsis [21]. Notably, AcTPS1, OsTPS1, and ClTPS3 all belong to Class I TPS genes.

Expression analysis showed that AcTPS1 was significantly down-regulated under drought stress. However, although the trehalose content increased after 72 h of drought, the expression of AcTPS1 at this time point decreased. Subsequently, qRT‒PCR showed that AcTPS1 was up-regulated at 36 h, 48 h, and 60 h (significantly up-regulated at 36 h and 60 h; Fig. 9), showing a trend of increasing first and then decreasing. This suggests that in the early stage of drought stress, kiwifruit may synthesize trehalose as a response mechanism, and in the later stage, the expression decreases due to systemic damage, indicating that gene expression may have a lag effect [62, 63].

Based on transcriptome data, soluble sugar content, AcTPS1 gene expression under drought stress, transgenic results of kiwifruit leaves under drought stress, as well as the studies of Sami et al. [64], Raza et al. [65], and Yang et al. [66], we found that under drought stress, sucrose in kiwifruit cells may be broken down into uridine diphosphate glucose (UDPG) and glucose-6-phosphate (G6P); the AcTPS1 enzyme may catalyze the synthesis of trehalose-6-phosphate (T6P) from UDPG and G6P; the resulting increase in trehalose content subsequently enhances the drought tolerance of kiwifruit (Fig. 11).

Nevertheless, this study has certain limitations. The potential regulatory mechanisms of the AcTPS genes were analyzed only based on omics data, and subsequent functional validation of key genes is necessary. Therefore, CRISPR/Cas9 gene-editing technology should be employed to generate overexpression or knockout lines of the candidate genes, thereby verifying their in vivo functions under drought stress.

Conclusion

In this study, 13 AcTPS genes were identified from the genome of kiwifruit (A. chinensis), and phylogenetic analysis divided them into two subfamilies. Compared with the control group, seven AcTPS genes showed differential expression under drought stress, the growth of kiwifruit seedlings was severely inhibited, the relative water content of leaves decreased significantly, and the trehalose content increased significantly. Integrated omics and bioinformatics analyses suggested that AcTPS1 might be a candidate gene involved in trehalose biosynthesis. Under drought stress, the expression of AcTPS1 first increased and then decreased, and the accumulation of trehalose showed a time lag relative to AcTPS1 expression, indicating that AcTPS1 may be an important gene in the drought response of kiwifruit.
