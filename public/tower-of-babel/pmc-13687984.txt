Introduction

Wearable devices, such as smartwatches, are increasingly used worldwide. They use photoplethysmography to estimate heart rate (HR) by detecting pulsatile changes in peripheral blood flow. More recent smartwatch models have the ability to record a single-lead electrocardiogram (ECG), which can be manually activated by the wearer. In combination with rhythm analysis algorithms, they can provide direct-to-consumer outputs and may assist in the detection and diagnosis of cardiac arrhythmias.

Smartwatches are already widely used to detect atrial fibrillation, with a reported sensitivity of 95% and specificity of 97%.1 Their ability to detect ventricular arrhythmias, such as ventricular tachycardia (VT) and ventricular fibrillation (VF), is being explored2; however, there is yet no market-ready product that claims to detect these arrhythmias. The ECG-recording function of smartwatches has nevertheless enabled patients to capture a variety of arrhythmias in real-world settings, with a handful of case reports EIdescribing smartwatch recordings of VT3,4 and supraventricular tachycardia (SVT).5,6 Although smartwatch-recorded SVT has been previously reported, we describe what is, to our knowledge, the first reported case of a symptomatic SVT recorded by a smartwatch in a patient with an implantable loop recorder (ILR) which did not auto-alert because programmed detection thresholds were not met. This is notable given that ILRs are generally considered the gold standard in syncope evaluation, precisely because patients are often unable to actively record an ECG during a syncopal episode.

This case illustrates how ILR programming directly determines which arrhythmias generate alerts, underscoring the importance of individualized detection thresholds. It also demonstrates the complementary value of wearables with ECG-recording capabilities for cardiac monitoring. This case is clinically important because there are currently no guidelines from major cardiology or heart rhythm societies on how smartwatch ECG recordings should be integrated into arrhythmia diagnostic and management pathways. As smartwatch ECG technology becomes increasingly widespread, our case highlights an emerging clinical challenge with important implications for patient care.

Case report

A 42-year-old engineer with a history of hypertension and dyslipidemia presented to hospital following a syncopal episode in his home. Collateral information from his wife supported a history of abrupt onset of syncope, suggesting a cardiac cause. An extensive workup, including troponins, D-dimer, N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide, computed tomography pulmonary angiography, coronary angiography, serial ECGs, electroencephalography, and brain magnetic resonance imaging, was all unremarkable. A vasovagal etiology was suspected, and an ILR was inserted.

The patient was readmitted 1 month later with a similar pre-syncopal episode. During this episode, the patient used his Apple Watch (Apple Inc., Cupertino, CA) to record a single-lead ECG, which demonstrated a narrow-complex tachycardia with rates up to 202 beats per minute (bpm) (Figure 1). The patient also manually activated his ILR (Figure 2). Despite the absence of an ILR alert, the clinical presentation and smartwatch ECG findings together supported a provisional diagnosis of paroxysmal SVT as the cause of syncope. He was referred for an electrophysiology study with a view to radiofrequency ablation. Antihypertensive therapy was reduced to mitigate the risk of hypotension during arrhythmic episodes.

The patient’s electrophysiology study demonstrated inducible orthodromic SVT with anterograde conduction via the atrioventricular node and retrograde conduction via an accessory pathway located in the left lateral free wall, with earliest activation recorded at the distal coronary sinus (Figure 3). The accessory pathway was successfully ablated, with no inducible SVT following ablation. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first published case in which a smartwatch enabled the diagnosis of SVT as the cause of syncope in a patient with an ILR that did not automatically alert because pre-programmed detection criteria were not met. Wearable technologies, such as the Apple Watch, may play an increasingly important role in arrhythmia detection, thereby facilitating prompt medical review.

ILRs remain the gold standard for detecting cardiac causes of syncope, but they are subject to algorithmic limitations. In our case, a LINQ II™ (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MA) insertable cardiac monitor with AccuRhythm AI (Medtronic, Minneapolis, Minnesota) was used. Review of the ILR recordings demonstrated 2 episodes with average HRs of 160 and 175 bpm (Figure 2), yet no automatic alert was generated as the HR was not sustained above the programmed threshold of 188 bpm for the required duration. This pattern reflects expected device behavior rather than a malfunction, so no manufacturer report was made. ILRs rely on programmable rate and duration criteria for automatic tachyarrhythmia detection, which may be set to default or adjusted by the clinician. Because default settings are not necessarily tailored to an individual patient’s expected arrhythmia rate, clinically relevant episodes below the programmed threshold will not trigger an alert.

This case highlights the importance of individualizing ILR detection thresholds. Where a patient has a history of palpitations with a known HR range during symptomatic episodes, or prior wearable-derived HR data, clinicians should consider lowering the rate threshold or adjusting the duration criterion to reduce the risk of non-detected episodes.7 This is supported by data from a large ILR cohort, in which sensitivity to detect true ventricular tachyarrhythmias was 96.5% at default detection settings but improved to 99.3% when the device was reprogrammed to a lower rate cutoff.8 In this patient, retrospective adjustment of the detection threshold below 188 bpm, or a shorter duration requirement, might have allowed automatic capture of both the 160 bpm and 175 bpm episodes.

By contrast, the Apple Watch ECG recorded an average HR of 202 bpm, exceeding the ILR threshold of 188 bpm. There may be 2 explanations for this discrepancy: first, the Apple Watch reported HR is an average, so the high HR was not necessarily sustained; and second, the Apple Watch may have overestimated the HR. A prior study comparing the Apple Watch and 12-lead ECGs HR reported limits of agreement from –7.32 to +9.65 bpm, indicating variability in individual HR readings and a tendency for Apple Watch measurements to overestimate HR.9

Importantly, the Apple Watch ECG function is user-activated. Therefore, to detect an arrhythmia, the subject must be aware of the symptom onset and stay conscious during the episode in order to activate the ECG by holding a finger to the watch crown for the full ECG duration. In patients who are asymptomatic or fully syncopal, it is less useful to rely on symptom-driven monitoring. Additionally, smartwatch ECG recordings of SVT have a high false positive rate and require expert clinician interpretation before being acted upon; they are not intended to replace formal diagnostic review.10 Patient-generated recordings should be interpreted alongside implantable device data and clinical history to guide diagnostic and management decisions.

This case demonstrates the algorithmic limitations of ILRs, which may not auto-detect arrhythmias that do not exceed predetermined rate or duration criteria. Smart wearables, such as the Apple Watch, may be useful as a complementary heart rhythm monitoring device. The increasing popularity of wearables may result in more frequent clinical presentation prompted by these technologies. Clinicians should consider information provided by these devices but should also be aware of its limitations, including false positives, motion artifacts,9 and reliance on patient-initiated recording. ILRs, by contrast, provide continuous, long-term monitoring independent of patient awareness or consciousness and remain the gold standard for evaluating cardiac arrhythmias as a cause of syncope.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that wearable technology can meaningfully complement gold-standard implantable monitoring in arrhythmia detection. ILRs may not generate automatic alerts when episodes remain below pre-determined thresholds, emphasizing the need to individualize programming based on documented HR profiles and to remain cognizant of default settings. Wearable-derived data should be interpreted as a complementary adjunct, with clinician oversight, rather than a substitute for implantable monitoring. Prospective studies are needed to define the sensitivity, specificity, and clinical impact of wearable technology in symptomatic arrhythmia detection, particularly in patients with syncope.
