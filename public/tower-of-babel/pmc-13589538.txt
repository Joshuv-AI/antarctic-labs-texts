Post-Traumatic Primary Cutaneous Cryptococcosis Presenting as a Chronic Refractory Forearm Ulcer with Polymicrobial Coinfection: An mNGS-Assisted Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Chengxiang Lian, Bin Feng, Jiaguang Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary cutaneous cryptococcosis (PCC) is a rare infection caused by direct inoculation of Cryptococcus through disrupted skin. We report a 52-year-old male farmer with type 2 diabetes but no HIV infection or known major immunosuppressive disease who developed chronic refractory ulcers on the right forearm following suspected insect-bite trauma. Cryptococcus neoformans was isolated from cutaneous specimens. Probe-capture metagenomic next-generation sequencing (MetaCAP) of the ulcer tissue additionally detected C. neoformans at 202 reads per million (RPM), accounting for 91.98% of the fungal sequences, with a reported confidence of 99%. Histopathology demonstrated an infectious granuloma, while bacterial culture identified methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing Escherichia coli. Chest computed tomography and cerebrospinal fluid investigations did not support pulmonary or central nervous system cryptococcosis. Liposomal amphotericin B was discontinued because of acute kidney injury, and subsequent fluconazole plus flucytosine treatment resulted in reduced exudation, granulation tissue formation, and partial ulcer healing. PCC should be considered in chronic post-traumatic ulcers, with systematic evaluation for extracutaneous involvement before establishing a primary cutaneous diagnosis.

Introduction

Cryptococcosis is an opportunistic fungal infection caused primarily by Cryptococcus neoformans and Cryptococcus gattii. C. neoformans is more frequently associated with immunocompromised hosts, whereas C. gattii more commonly causes disease in apparently immunocompetent individuals, although both species can infect either host population. Cryptococcosis most commonly involves the lungs and central nervous system, whereas isolated cutaneous involvement is uncommon. Cutaneous cryptococcosis may occur secondary to hematogenous dissemination or as primary cutaneous cryptococcosis (PCC) following direct inoculation through disrupted skin.1,2

The clinical manifestations of PCC are heterogeneous and include papules, nodules, plaques, cellulitis-like lesions, abscesses, ulcers, and granulomatous lesions.3 Consequently, PCC may be mistaken for bacterial infection, sporotrichosis, cutaneous mycobacterial infection, nocardiosis, leishmaniasis, or cutaneous malignancy. Establishing the diagnosis requires integration of the clinical history, microbiological and histopathological findings, together with systematic evaluation for pulmonary, neurological, and other systemic involvement.4

Historically, PCC has been characterized by localized lesions, frequent involvement of exposed anatomical sites, preceding skin trauma or outdoor exposure, microbiological confirmation from cutaneous specimens, and the absence of detectable systemic disease. In a French registry-based analysis of 28 patients with PCC, rural residence, outdoor activities, skin injury, and lesions on uncovered sites were common characteristics. Subsequent case series and systematic reviews have shown that PCC may occur in both immunocompetent and immunocompromised individuals and may present as cellulitis-like lesions, nodules, abscesses, ulcers, or granulomatous plaques. Diagnosis has generally been established by fungal culture and histopathological examination after exclusion of pulmonary and central nervous system involvement. Localized disease has usually responded favorably to oral fluconazole or itraconazole, although amphotericin B-based therapy has been used in patients with extensive lesions, significant immunosuppression, or concern for extracutaneous involvement.2,5–7

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) may complement conventional microbiological testing in complex cutaneous infections by enabling simultaneous detection of multiple potential pathogens.8 However, mNGS findings should be interpreted together with culture, histopathology, and the clinical context. We report a case of post-traumatic PCC presenting as chronic refractory forearm ulcers with polymicrobial coinfection in a patient with type 2 diabetes and discuss its diagnosis, treatment, and clinical implications.

Case Presentation

A 52-year-old male farmer was admitted on February 28, 2026, with a 4-month history of non-healing ulcers on the right forearm. Four months before admission, he reported a suspected insect bite while cutting saplings. A small ulcer, initially approximately the size of a mung bean, subsequently developed at the affected site and progressively enlarged, accompanied by pain, purulent discharge, and malodor. At another hospital, the patient underwent two surgical debridement procedures followed by autologous skin grafting; however, the lesions persisted without satisfactory healing.

The patient had type 2 diabetes mellitus treated with oral metformin and hypertension treated with amlodipine besylate. He had no known history of HIV infection, malignancy, organ transplantation, long-term corticosteroid use, or other major immunosuppressive conditions. He reported domestic exposure to chickens and ducks but denied known contact with pigeons or wild birds.

On admission, his body temperature was 36.8°C and blood pressure was 145/97 mmHg. Dermatological examination revealed multiple irregular ulcers of varying sizes on the right forearm. The lesions had raised, dike-like borders and uneven bases, with purulent exudation and malodor. An additional ulcer on the dorsum of the right hand was covered by a crust. Neurological examination was unremarkable, with no neck stiffness or positive Kernig sign.

Laboratory testing showed an elevated white blood cell count of 15.67 × 109/L and a high-sensitivity C-reactive protein level of 10.6 mg/L. The proportions of CD4⁺ T lymphocytes and B lymphocytes were reduced to 27.47% and 2.23%, respectively, and HIV antibody testing was negative. Direct fungal examination of the wound specimen revealed yeast-like spores. Fungal cultures of wound secretion and tissue specimens incubated at 25°C and 37°C yielded C. neoformans. Because cutaneous cryptococcosis may represent a manifestation of disseminated infection, further neurological and pulmonary evaluations were performed. Lumbar puncture revealed an opening pressure of 160 mmH2O and clear cerebrospinal fluid. Cerebrospinal fluid protein was elevated at 685.47 mg/L, and the cryptococcal antigen titer was 1:1. However, India ink staining and fungal culture of the cerebrospinal fluid were negative for C. neoformans. MetaCAP analysis of the cerebrospinal fluid did not detect bacteria, fungi, or parasites. The Q30 rate was 94.20%, and the data quantity, capture efficiency, and internal control met the laboratory’s quality-control criteria. The patient had no neurological symptoms or signs, and chest computed tomography showed no evidence of pulmonary cryptococcosis. Collectively, the available clinical, microbiological, neurological, and radiological findings did not support pulmonary or central nervous system involvement.

Histopathological examination of the ulcer tissue demonstrated epidermal atrophy and diffuse infiltration of histiocytes, lymphocytes, neutrophils, and plasma cells throughout the superficial and deep dermis, accompanied by multinucleated giant cells. These findings were consistent with an infectious granuloma.

Based on the history of preceding cutaneous trauma, localization of the lesions to the right upper extremity, isolation of C. neoformans from cutaneous specimens, and the absence of evidence supporting pulmonary or central nervous system involvement, the patient was diagnosed with primary cutaneous cryptococcosis caused by C. neoformans, accompanied by culture-confirmed bacterial coinfection with methicillin-resistant S. aureus and extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing E. coli. The comorbidities included type 2 diabetes mellitus and hypertension.

Treatment and Outcome

Following admission, antibacterial treatment with piperacillin–tazobactam (4.5 g every 8 hours) and linezolid (600 mg every 12 hours) was initiated in view of the culture-confirmed bacterial coinfection, with additional polymicrobial findings identified by MetaCAP. Given the extensive cutaneous involvement, high cutaneous fungal burden, positive serum cryptococcal antigen, and low-titer cerebrospinal fluid cryptococcal antigen, initial antifungal therapy consisted of liposomal amphotericin B, gradually increased from 50 mg/day to 200 mg/day, combined with flucytosine (1.5 g every 6 hours).

On day 6 of antifungal treatment, the serum creatinine level increased from 88 to 165 μmol/L, raising concern for amphotericin B-associated acute kidney injury. Liposomal amphotericin B was therefore discontinued, and the antifungal regimen was changed to oral fluconazole 400 mg once daily plus flucytosine 1.5 g every 12 hours. Renal function subsequently improved.

After the treatment adjustment, purulent exudation and malodor decreased substantially, healthy granulation tissue developed within the ulcer beds, and several smaller ulcers partially or completely healed. Discussion

Primary cutaneous cryptococcosis (PCC) results from direct inoculation of Cryptococcus through disrupted skin, whereas secondary cutaneous cryptococcosis generally reflects hematogenous dissemination from pulmonary, central nervous system, or other systemic infection. Previous case series and reviews indicate that PCC is commonly associated with preceding skin injury, rural or environmental exposure, involvement of uncovered anatomical sites, microbiological confirmation from cutaneous specimens, and the absence of detectable extracutaneous disease.2,3,6

The present case was broadly consistent with these characteristics. The patient was a farmer who reported a suspected insect bite before lesion development, and the ulcers remained confined to the right upper extremity. C. neoformans was isolated from both wound secretion and tissue specimens. Chest computed tomography showed no pulmonary involvement, while neurological examination was unremarkable. Cerebrospinal fluid India ink staining and fungal culture were negative, and MetaCAP did not detect bacteria, fungi, or parasites in the cerebrospinal fluid. Nevertheless, the serum cryptococcal antigen titer was 1:160, and the cerebrospinal fluid antigen titer was weakly positive at 1:1. Therefore, systemic involvement could not be excluded solely on the basis of lesion distribution. Considering the clinical, radiological, and microbiological findings together, the available evidence did not support active pulmonary or central nervous system cryptococcosis, although continued follow-up remains necessary.

PCC has been reported in both immunocompetent and immunocompromised individuals, with clinical manifestations including papules, nodules, plaques, cellulitis-like lesions, abscesses, verrucous lesions, ulcers, and granulomatous inflammation.9 The patient in this case should not be classified as unequivocally immunocompetent. Although he had no HIV infection, malignancy, organ transplantation, long-term corticosteroid exposure, or other known major immunosuppressive condition, type 2 diabetes mellitus and decreased proportions of CD4⁺ T lymphocytes and B lymphocytes may have increased his susceptibility. However, absolute lymphocyte counts and functional immune assessments were unavailable; therefore, the clinical significance of these abnormalities could not be determined.

Previous reports further illustrate the clinical heterogeneity of PCC. In a Brazilian series of 11 patients, all lesions involved the upper limbs, immunosuppressive conditions were identified in more than half of the patients, and fluconazole was the principal effective treatment.5 More recently, Panza et al reported an immunocompetent patient with chronic ulcerative lesions on the right forearm following minor trauma during countryside activity. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus was repeatedly isolated, antibacterial treatment was ineffective, and complete recovery was achieved after three months of fluconazole therapy.7 The present case shared several features with these reports, including preceding traumatic or environmental exposure, upper-limb involvement, bacterial coinfection or colonization, and initial diagnostic delay. However, the current patient had more extensive ulceration, type 2 diabetes mellitus, abnormalities in lymphocyte subsets, failed skin grafting, and amphotericin B-associated acute kidney injury, resulting in a more complex diagnostic and therapeutic course.

The heterogeneous appearance of PCC frequently leads to delayed diagnosis. In this patient, purulent exudation, malodor, and bacterial culture results initially suggested a bacterial soft-tissue infection. However, repeated debridement, skin grafting, and antibacterial treatment failed to achieve satisfactory healing. In similar circumstances, PCC should be differentiated from persistent bacterial infection, sporotrichosis, cutaneous nocardiosis, nontuberculous mycobacterial infection, leishmaniasis, pyoderma gangrenosum, and cutaneous malignancy. Failure of a chronic traumatic ulcer to respond to appropriate antibacterial therapy should prompt repeat tissue sampling for fungal culture and histopathological examination.

Another notable feature was the detection of multiple microorganisms. Conventional bacterial culture identified methicillin-resistant S. aureus and extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing E. coli, while tissue MetaCAP detected C. neoformans, Corynebacterium striatum, S. aureus, and E. coli. Polymicrobial findings have also been reported in other chronic ulcerative PCC cases, possibly because prolonged disruption of the skin barrier facilitates secondary infection or colonization.10,11 In the present case, repeated isolation of C. neoformans from wound secretion and tissue specimens, together with the compatible clinical presentation, supported C. neoformans as the principal pathogen. The bacterial culture findings supported clinically relevant bacterial coinfection. By contrast, because C. striatum was detected only by MetaCAP and was not independently confirmed by culture or organism-specific histopathology, its clinical significance remains uncertain and may represent colonization or contamination.

Metagenomic sequencing may complement conventional microbiological testing in complex cutaneous infections by enabling the simultaneous detection of multiple potential pathogens.8 The probe-capture MetaCAP assay used in this case covered 26,610 microorganisms and reported microbial abundance as reads per million (RPM), normalized to one million sequencing reads. In the ulcer tissue, C. neoformans was detected at 202 RPM, accounting for 91.98% of the fungal sequences, with a reported confidence of 99%. The Q30 rate was 94.53%, and the data quantity, capture efficiency, and internal control met the laboratory’s quality-control criteria. Nevertheless, detection of microbial nucleic acids does not necessarily demonstrate viable infection or establish causality. MetaCAP findings should therefore be interpreted together with conventional microbiological findings, histopathology, clinical presentation, and treatment response. In this case, fungal culture provided the principal species-level etiological evidence, while MetaCAP offered complementary molecular support.

Fungal culture provided the strongest etiological evidence in this case. Histopathological examination demonstrated infectious granulomatous inflammation but did not specifically identify Cryptococcus. Thus, the pathological findings supported the presence of a chronic infectious process, whereas species-level diagnosis was established primarily through fungal culture and supported by MetaCAP. This distinction is important because nonspecific granulomatous inflammation may occur in various fungal, bacterial, and noninfectious dermatological conditions.

Treatment of PCC should be individualized according to lesion extent, host immune status, evidence of extracutaneous involvement, antifungal susceptibility, and clinical response. Current guidelines support azole-based therapy for localized non-central nervous system cryptococcosis after systematic evaluation for dissemination.1 In this patient, the extensive ulcer burden, positive serum cryptococcal antigen, low-titer cerebrospinal fluid antigen positivity, and concern regarding possible extracutaneous involvement prompted initial treatment with liposomal amphotericin B plus flucytosine. Amphotericin B was discontinued after the development of acute kidney injury, and subsequent fluconazole plus flucytosine treatment was accompanied by improved renal function, reduced exudation, granulation tissue formation, and partial ulcer healing. This course highlights the need to balance antifungal intensity against toxicity and to monitor renal function closely during amphotericin B therapy.

This report has several limitations. Although the clinical MetaCAP reports provided RPM, relative abundance, reported confidence, Q30, and quality-control results, complete details of the proprietary bioinformatics pipeline, including read-processing software, database versions, taxonomic classification thresholds, absolute raw read counts, and genome coverage metrics, were unavailable. This limited a fully independent assessment of the sequencing findings. The clinical significance of C. striatum, which was detected only by MetaCAP, also remains uncertain. The significance of the low cerebrospinal fluid cryptococcal antigen titer remains unclear despite otherwise negative cerebrospinal fluid findings. In addition, the absence of long-term clinical, serological, and radiological follow-up prevented evaluation of complete healing, recurrence, or subsequent dissemination. These limitations require cautious interpretation of both the polymicrobial findings and the short-term therapeutic response.

Conclusion

PCC should be considered in chronic post-traumatic ulcers that fail to respond to antibacterial treatment. Diagnosis requires microbiological confirmation and systematic evaluation for extracutaneous involvement. Fungal culture remains fundamental, while metagenomic sequencing may provide complementary evidence and help characterize concomitant microbial findings. Treatment should be individualized according to disease extent, host factors, and drug-related toxicity.
