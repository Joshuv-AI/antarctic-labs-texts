Standard Antiplatelet Therapy Versus Thrombolysis in Acute Spontaneous Spinal Cord Infarction: A Propensity Score–Matched Analysis
Authors: Majd A. AbuAlrob, Ali Al-Salahat, Khaled Zammar, Salman Al Jerdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Spinal cord infarction (SCI) is a rare stroke subtype with no established acute treatment guidelines. Thrombolytic therapy has been used empirically based on extrapolation from cerebral stroke protocols, but comparative effectiveness data are lacking. We compared outcomes between standard care and thrombolysis in acute spontaneous SCI.METHODS:This retrospective cohort study used data from the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network. Adult patients with acute SCI (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification code G95.11) were divided into the standard care group (antiplatelet therapy within 48 hours) or the thrombolysis group (alteplase or tenecteplase). Patients who underwent aortic repair procedures were excluded. Propensity score matching (1:1) balanced 68 covariates. The prespecified primary outcome was all-cause mortality; readmission and rehabilitation utilization at 180 days were secondary exploratory outcomes. E values were calculated for the significant association.RESULTS:Of the 965 patients in the standard care cohort and 103 in the thrombolysis cohort, 96 patients in each cohort remained after matching. Standard care was associated with significantly lower mortality (13.5% versus 29.2%; hazard ratio, 0.423 [95% CI, 0.219–0.817]; P=0.008); 180-day survival was 84.27% versus 68.69% (log-rank P=0.008), with an E value of 4.16. Readmission (14.6% versus 16.7%; P=0.669) and rehabilitation utilization (43.8% versus 49.0%; P=0.507) did not differ. The mortality association was consistent in direction across 3 sensitivity analyses.CONCLUSIONS:In spontaneous SCI, standard care was associated with significantly lower mortality than thrombolysis, without significant differences in readmission rates or rehabilitation utilization. These hypothesis-generating findings raise concerns about off-label thrombolytic use in SCI and support consideration of conservative management until higher-quality evidence emerges.

Spinal cord infarction (SCI) is a rare but devastating neurological emergency that causes motor, sensory, and autonomic dysfunction. The American Heart Association/American Stroke Association definition of stroke explicitly incorporates SCI, defining central nervous system infarction as brain, spinal cord, or retinal cell death attributable to ischemia.1 SCI accounts for ≈0.3% to 1.2% of all strokes, with an estimated incidence of 3.1 per 100 000 person-years.2,3 Its rarity has impeded the development of evidence-based treatment protocols.

The causes are heterogeneous, including spontaneous and periprocedural. Aortic pathology is a major risk factor, and iatrogenic causes related to aortic and spinal procedures constitute up to 45% of reported cord infarctions. In many cases, the cause remains undetermined.4–7 Current acute management largely extrapolates from cerebral ischemic stroke guidelines, relying on antiplatelet therapy, cardiovascular risk factor management, and supportive care, although this approach has never been validated in controlled studies.8,9

Intravenous thrombolysis transformed acute cerebral ischemic stroke care following the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke (NINDS) trial.10 Its application to SCI has been explored only in scattered case reports and small series (≈20 reported cases), with variable outcomes and no comparative data.11 Outcomes after SCI are strongly influenced by the severity of initial impairment; in the largest outcome study (115 patients), severe impairment at nadir was the strongest predictor of poor outcome, although meaningful recovery occurred in a substantial minority.12 The reported mortality rate ranges from 9% to 23%.

To date, no study has directly compared clinical outcomes between standard antiplatelet therapy and thrombolysis in patients with SCI. We sought to address this gap using a large federated electronic health record network to compare mortality, readmission, and rehabilitation utilization between these approaches.

Methods

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study are not publicly available. Analyses were performed on the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network, which contains deidentified electronic health record data aggregated from participating healthcare organizations. Access to the platform is available to qualified researchers through an institutional license and data use agreement with TriNetX, LLC. The authors are not permitted to redistribute patient-level data. The cohort definitions, diagnosis and procedure code lists, and analytic specifications required to reproduce the analyses are provided in the Supplemental Material.

Standard Protocol Approvals and Patient Consents

This study used deidentified data from the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network. TriNetX, LLC is compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act. Data displayed on the platform in aggregate form, and any patient-level data provided in a generated data set, contain only deidentified data as defined under Section §164.514(a) of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act Privacy Rule. TriNetX has been granted a waiver from the Western Institutional Review Board because the research does not involve retrieval of identifiable protected health information. Because the analysis used only preexisting deidentified records and no participants were contacted, the study did not constitute human subjects research, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived.

This study is reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology ). A completed STROBE Checklist is provided in the Supplemental Material.

Study Design, Data Source, and Population

This retrospective cohort study used data from the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network, a federated health research platform providing access to electronic medical records from 171 participating healthcare organizations. Data extraction and analysis were performed on January 11, 2026. The study population consisted of adult patients diagnosed with acute SCI (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification code G95.11), divided into 2 mutually exclusive cohorts (standard care and thrombolysis) based on initial treatment.

Patients in the standard care cohort were required to have a diagnosis of acute SCI (G95.11) and received antiplatelet therapy (aspirin, clopidogrel, or dipyridamole) within 2 days of diagnosis. This antiplatelet-defined cohort is referred to as standard care throughout, reflecting nonthrombolytic management. Patients were excluded if they received thrombolytic agents (alteplase or tenecteplase) or underwent aortic repair procedures.

The thrombolysis cohort consisted of patients with acute SCI (G95.11) who received thrombolytic therapy (alteplase or tenecteplase) within 1 day of diagnosis. Patients who underwent aortic repair procedures were excluded. Because the standard care cohort excludes any thrombolytic exposure, the cohorts are mutually exclusive, and exposure overlap cannot occur.

Index Event and Follow-Up

The index date was the first occurrence of the qualifying diagnosis-and-treatment combination. Outcomes were assessed from 1 day after the index event to 180 days postindex. The outcome window began 1 day after the index date, so that the qualifying index encounter was not counted as an outcome. This configuration was applied identically to both cohorts. For the mortality analysis, patients were retained regardless of any events preceding the window, so early deaths were not differentially excluded between groups.

Outcome Measures

The prespecified primary outcome was all-cause mortality recorded in deceased demographic status. Secondary exploratory outcomes were readmission rates and rehabilitation utilization, a health services proxy rather than a measure of neurological or functional recovery. Hospital readmission and use of rehabilitation services were defined using the standard diagnosis, procedure, and admission codes (full code lists in Supplemental Methods).

Statistical Analysis

Propensity score matching used 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching to balance 68 covariates spanning demographics, stroke severity indicators (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale categories), cardiovascular and metabolic comorbidities, lifestyle factors, infectious diseases, vascular anatomy, neurological deficits, prior procedures, and baseline medications. Matching was performed using 1:1 greedy nearest-neighbor matching without replacement with a caliper of 0.1 pooled SDs of the logit of the propensity score. Patients with missing propensity score data were excluded before matching, in accordance with TriNetX platform defaults.

Balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with values <0.1 indicating adequate balance; results are displayed for all covariates in Figure S1. The baseline characteristic tables present a selected subset of covariates, comprising prespecified clinically relevant severity and vascular risk factors, the covariates with the greatest prematch imbalance, and those with residual postmatch imbalance; the full covariate set is shown in Figure S1. Risk differences, risk ratios, and odds ratios with 95% CIs were calculated. Risk differences and risk ratios were considered the primary effect measures for this matched cohort design; odds ratios were reported for comparability with prior literature but were not interpreted as risk ratios because mortality was not rare. Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank tests compared time-to-event outcomes, and Cox proportional hazards regression estimated hazard ratios (HRs) with proportionality testing. The National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale, although included among covariates, is not a validated severity instrument for SCI and was used only as a coarse proxy for stroke severity. Mortality was the prespecified primary outcome; secondary outcomes were exploratory and not corrected for multiplicity.

E values were calculated to assess the robustness of the significant finding to unmeasured confounding. Three prespecified sensitivity analyses were performed: (1) a broad case definition (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification code G95.1 vascular myelopathies inclusive); (2) a severity-enriched cohort requiring documented critical-care or mechanical-ventilation procedures; and (3) an era-restricted cohort limited to index events on or after January 1, 2016 (Tables S1 through S3).

Before matching, 965 patients met the criteria for the standard care cohort and 103 for the thrombolysis cohort. After excluding patients with missing propensity score data, 927 patients of the standard care cohort and 103 patients of the thrombolysis cohort were available for matching. Mortality

All-cause mortality occurred in 13 patients (13.5%) in the standard care cohort compared with 28 patients (29.2%) in the thrombolysis cohort (risk difference, −15.6% [95% CI, −27.0% to −4.2%]; P=0.008; risk ratio, 0.464 [95% CI, 0.256–0.841]). Kaplan-Meier survival probability at 180 days was 84.27% versus 68.69% (log-rank χ2=6.998; P=0.008; Figure 2). Cox regression yielded an HR of 0.423 (95% CI, 0.219–0.817) favoring standard care; proportionality was not violated (P=0.133). The E value for the HR was 4.16 (8.60 for the CI bound), indicating the strength of association that an unmeasured confounder would require, on the risk-ratio scale, with both treatment and mortality. However, this does not exclude residual confounding by unmeasured severity markers and should not be read as evidence of robustness.

Kaplan-Meier survival curves for mortality comparing standard antiplatelet therapy (n=96) and thrombolysis (n=96) in propensity score–matched patients. Standard care showed significantly higher survival (84.27% vs 68.69% at 180 days; log-rank P=0.008). +Censored observations.

Readmission

Readmission occurred in 14 patients (14.6%) in the standard care cohort versus 16 patients (16.7%) in the thrombolysis cohort (risk difference, −2.1%; P=0.691; HR, 0.857 [95% CI, 0.418–1.756]; P=0.679). Among patients who were readmitted, mean readmission events were 2.0±1.4 versus 1.9±1.1 (P=0.787).

Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation utilization was documented in 42 patients (43.8%) in the standard care cohort versus 47 patients (49.0%) in the thrombolysis cohort (risk difference, −5.2%; P=0.469; HR, 0.872 [95% CI, 0.575–1.322]; P=0.377). The median time to rehabilitation was not reached in the standard care group compared with 21 days in the thrombolysis group (log-rank P=0.507).

Sensitivity Analyses

Three prespecified sensitivity analyses were performed, and the mortality association across the primary and sensitivity analyses is summarized in Table S5. Using a broader vascular-myelopathy case definition (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification code G95.1 inclusive), 198 patients per arm were matched, and lower mortality with standard care persisted (HR, 0.431 [95% CI, 0.275–0.677]; log-rank P<0.001; Table S1). Restricting both cohorts to patients with documented critical-care or mechanical-ventilation requirements as a marker of catastrophic presentation (62 per arm), the mortality association attenuated and was no longer statistically significant (HR, 0.521 [95% CI, 0.248–1.095]; log-rank P=0.079; Table S2), consistent with a contribution from severity-related confounding. Restricting the index event to on or after January 1, 2016 (91 per arm), the association was preserved (HR, 0.409 [95% CI, 0.221–0.755]; log-rank P=0.003; Table S3).

Discussion

In this multicenter retrospective cohort study using real-world data, standard care was associated with significantly lower all-cause mortality than thrombolysis among patients with spontaneous SCI over a median follow-up of 180 days, without differences in readmission or rehabilitation utilization. The principal finding, an association between thrombolysis and higher all-cause mortality, was unexpected and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, given the observational design and modest matched sample. Treatment-selection bias may explain part or all of the observed association, as supported by attenuation of the signal in the severity-enriched sensitivity analysis. Residual confounding by indication remains the most plausible explanation for the observed mortality association, and the present design cannot establish a causal effect of thrombolysis.

Thrombolysis in spontaneous SCI remains largely experimental, based on case reports rather than randomized trials.13,14 A literature review identified 11 reported cases of thrombolysis in SCI with variable recovery and no reported hemorrhagic complications, likely reflecting publication bias.15 The observed thrombolysis-group mortality (29.2%) contrasts with these reports. A retrospective analysis of 12 548 SCI hospitalizations found no significant difference in in-hospital mortality with thrombolysis, although a nonsignificant trend toward higher mortality was observed. The longer follow-up here may have allowed detection of a difference.16

Several mechanisms could contribute to higher mortality after thrombolysis. The spinal cord receives a relatively sparse arterial supply with limited collateralization, and reperfusion within the nonexpansile spinal canal may predispose to edema or hemorrhagic transformation.17 Confounding by indication, whereby thrombolysis is preferentially administered to patients with more fulminant presentations, remains a competing explanation. Cause-specific mortality was not available in the analyzed data and therefore could not be reported.

Hemorrhagic complications, including spinal hemorrhagic transformation, could not be reliably ascertained because such events are inconsistently coded and not imaging-confirmed within the federated data set, thereby preventing a direct test of bleeding as a mediator of the mortality difference.

The favorable outcomes with standard care aligned with the natural history of spontaneous SCI, in which a substantial proportion of patients recovered.12,18 Our standard-care mortality (13.5% at 180 days) is comparable to prior series. Exclusion of periprocedural SCI further enriches for the better-prognosis spontaneous population.19 Long-term SCI outcomes are strongly determined by the severity of neurological impairment at nadir rather than the acute reperfusion strategy.12

There is no direct trial evidence supporting antiplatelet therapy in SCI; its use is extrapolated from cerebral ischemic stroke.20 Our findings should therefore be read as describing comparative outcomes between 2 empirically used strategies rather than as validation of either.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, confounding by indication is likely: thrombolysis is plausibly directed toward patients with more severe or fulminant presentations, and key severity markers, including detailed neurological examination, The American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) impairment grade, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) lesion extent, symptom-to-treatment time, hemodynamic instability, and intensive-care acuity, were not captured. The National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale is not validated for SCI and is only a coarse proxy. The attenuation of the mortality association in the severity-enriched sensitivity analysis supports a contribution from such confounding. Second, diagnosis relied on the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification code G95.11 without imaging confirmation, and misclassification with transverse myelitis, compressive myelopathy, spinal hemorrhage, fibrocartilaginous embolism, inflammatory myelopathies, or perioperative ischemia remains possible. To our knowledge, no published validation study for this code is available. Third, symptom-onset time was unavailable, precluding any therapeutic-window inference. Treatment after the acute phase, including secondary antiplatelet or antithrombotic therapy, was not characterized, and patients treated with thrombolysis may also have received antiplatelet therapy after the acute window, resulting in treatment overlap that could not be quantified in the available data. Fourth, rehabilitation utilization is a health services proxy influenced by local practice, insurance, severity, survival, and institutional resources, and does not measure neurological or functional recovery. Fifth, the matched sample (96 per arm) limits power and the precision of secondary estimates and may reduce representativeness. Sixth, mortality ascertainment within a federated network may be incomplete; nondifferential undercapture would bias the comparison toward the null. Finally, the outcome-window design, although applied identically to both cohorts, does not fully exclude immortal-time bias inherent to treatment-defined cohort entry.

Future Directions

Future studies with prospective registries, standardized neuroimaging confirmation, validated functional outcome measures, and detailed documentation would provide higher-quality observational data. Patient-level predictors might identify candidates who could benefit from thrombolysis, such as those with documented vertebral artery occlusion. Alternative neuroprotective strategies, including spinal cord perfusion pressure optimization, also merit exploration.

Conclusions

This analysis suggests that, among patients with spontaneous SCI, standard care is associated with lower mortality than thrombolytic therapy, without significant differences in readmission or rehabilitation utilization. These retrospective findings cannot establish causality and may reflect treatment-selection bias. Pending higher-quality prospective evidence, the results support cautious individualized decision-making and argue against the routine extrapolation of cerebral thrombolysis protocols to spontaneous SCI.
