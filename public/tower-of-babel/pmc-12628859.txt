Genetic analysis and quantitative trait loci detection of udder traits in Jersey cattle
Authors: Xubin Lu, Mingxue Long, Hao Zhu, Hui Jiang, Fuzhen Zhou, Bo Wang, Zongping Liu, Zhangping Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe udder is a crucial trait in dairy cattle breeding, influencing both milk production and quality. Understanding the genetic basis of udder traits is essential for improving breeding strategies. This study aims to estimate the genetic parameters of key udder traits in Jersey cattle, assess their impact on lactation performance, and identify quantitative trait loci associated with these traits.ResultsThe study was conducted on 1,631 first-lactation Jersey cows, evaluating eight udder traits: udder depth, central ligament, fore udder attachment, front teat position, teat length, rear udder height, rear udder width, and rear teat placement. Heritability estimates for these traits ranged from low to moderate, with udder depth showing the highest heritability at 0.291 ± 0.061, while fore udder attachment had the lowest at 0.080 ± 0.040. The udder traits significantly influenced lactation performance, including milk yield, milk fat percentage, milk protein percentage, somatic cell score, and milk urea nitrogen. All eight udder traits exhibited significant phenotypic correlations and certain genetic correlations. The highest positive genetic correlation was observed between rear teat position and front teat position, while the highest negative genetic correlation was found between teat length and rear teat position. Genome-wide association studies identified 39 significant single nucleotide polymorphisms, and transcriptome-wide association studies, along with gene ontology enrichment analysis, revealed candidate genes such as NFATC3, OSTF1, ENPP4, PRKCA, EFHB, TAS2R46, and SLC35G1, which may play a role in regulating udder traits.ConclusionsThis study provides valuable insights into the genetic architecture of udder traits in Jersey cattle. The identified genetic markers and candidate genes enhance our understanding of the factors influencing udder development and lactation performance. These findings lay the groundwork for future molecular research aimed at improving udder traits and breeding strategies in dairy cattle.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12235-z.Keywords: Jersey cattle, Udder traits, Genetic parameters, Quantitative trait loci

Introduction

Since the 1990 s, udder traits have been increasingly included in dairy cattle breeding programs due to their strong associations with milk yield, milk composition, udder health, and overall productivity [1–3]. Numerous studies have explored the genetic relationships between udder conformation traits and lactation performance in dairy cattle. For instance, study have demonstrated that positive genetic correlations of 0.15 between the central ligament (CL) and milk yield (MY), and 0.12 between udder depth (UD) and fat yield have been identified [4]. A positive correlation between udder volume and MY, with an average correlation coefficient of 0.43, was also reported [5]. Cows with deeper and wider udders were found to produce significantly higher MY [6]. Furthermore, a strong positive genetic correlation of 0.86 was reported in a study of 1,300 Holstein cows between udder width and total MY, while a strong negative genetic correlation was observed between teat length (TL) and peak MY (− 0.92) [7]. Notably, while some studies have reported strong positive genetic correlations (>0.60) between rear teat placement (RTP) and front teat placement (FTP) [8, 9], others have found moderate or even negative associations, especially in cases where udder conformation is unbalanced [2, 10]. Likewise, estimates of udder depth heritability show considerable variation across populations, ranging from as low as 0.10 to as high as 0.79 [2, 11]. These findings underscore both the importance of exterior udder traits and the variability that exists across genetic studies, highlighting the need for breed-specific investigations.

Advances in molecular genetics, particularly genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS), have greatly enhanced our ability to identify genetic variants and candidate genes associated with complex traits [12]. GWAS allows the identification of loci significantly associated with phenotypic traits across the genome, while TWAS integrates gene expression data to prioritize causal genes. By leveraging predicted gene expression, TWAS can help pinpoint the most biologically relevant genes within GWAS-identified loci, thereby improving the interpretation of association signals and revealing potential molecular mechanisms underlying the traits of interest [13]. This complementary approach enhances the ability to identify functionally important genes that may be overlooked by GWAS alone and provides a robust framework for dissecting the genetic architecture of complex traits [13].

While Jersey cattle are widely recognized for their high milk quality, calving ease, and adaptability, especially in developed livestock systems [14], genetic studies on their exterior udder traits remain limited compared to Holstein cattle. Most existing research has focused on phenotypic assessments or their association with culling risk [15, ], with few studies providing comprehensive genetic evaluations of udder morphology. In particular, little is known about how these traits genetically relate to lactation performance, or which candidate genes underlie trait variability. This gap is especially relevant as Jersey cattle are increasingly adopted in non-Western dairy systems, such as China, where environmental and management conditions differ substantially from those in traditional breeding populations.

To address these gaps, the present study provides an integrated genetic analysis of eight exterior udder traits in Jersey cattle, including heritability estimates, phenotypic and genetic correlations with lactation traits, and genome-level association mapping. To our knowledge, this is among the first comprehensive evaluations of these traits in Jersey cattle under Chinese production conditions. Leveraging data from over 1,600 first-lactation cows across three standardized farms, along with five-generation pedigree and high-density genomic information, we integrated GWAS, TWAS, and GO enrichment analyses to identify key quantitative trait loci (QTLs) and biologically relevant candidate genes. This approach offers novel insights into the genetic basis of udder morphology and its relationship with milk production and udder health. The specific objectives were to (1) estimate the genetic parameters of exterior udder traits in Jersey cattle, (2) evaluate the influence of these traits on lactation performance, and (3) identify relevant quantitative trait loci and candidate genes. These findings are expected to inform breeding strategies aimed at improving both udder health and milk production efficiency in Jersey cattle.

Materials and methods

Animals, diets, and feeding

This study used a population of 1,631 healthy first-lactation Jersey cows from three standardized farms: Zhejiang Taishun Yunlan Farm (Farm 1), Shanghai Chongming Aoshan Dairy Farm (Farm 2), and Jiangsu Yinbao Guangming Farm (Farm 3). All the 1,631 cows were born in the same year and were fed and milked three times daily, provided with a total mixed ration (TMR), and had free access to water. All eligible first-lactation Jersey cows on the three farms that were between 30 and 180 days in milk (DIM) at the time of measurement, in accordance with the Jersey cattle body type identification technical procedure (Standard No. T/JAASS 143–2024, https://www.ttbz.org.cn/StandardManage/Detail/110067/, accessed Sep. 28, 2024), and that met the health and management criteria at the start of the study were included. Cows with reproductive disorders, chronic illness, or incomplete pedigree or phenotypic records were excluded.

Phenotype collections and quality control

This study focuses exclusively on exterior udder traits, specifically physical conformation characteristics. The measurements and scoring of eight udder traits in Jersey cattle were conducted following the aforementioned Jersey cattle body type identification technical procedure. Specifically, 617 animals were evaluated between 30 and 80 DIM, 504 animals between 81 and 130 DIM, and 510 animals between 131 and 180 DIM. Specifically, 617 animals were evaluated between 30 and 80 DIM, 504 animals between 81 and 130 DIM, and 510 animals between 131 and 180 DIM. The DIM range of 30–180 days was selected in accordance with the aforementioned Jersey cattle body type identification technical procedure, which recommends evaluating linear type trait measurements for first-lactation cows within this period to ensure measurement accuracy and consistency. To account for potential stage-related variation in udder conformation while maintaining adequate statistical power, this range was further divided into three DIM groups. Measurements for CL, fore udder attachment (FUA), TL, rear udder height (RUH), and rear udder width (RUW) were obtained using rulers and protractors. UD, FTP, and RTP were subjectively scored on a scale of 1 to 9. The brief illustrations of these udder traits in Jersey cattle are shown in Figure S1 (Mendeley Data, V6, doi: 10.17632/8jsxnwvbhp.6). To ensure data reliability and consistency, exterior udder traits of all cows were assessed by the same three trained evaluators across the three farms. Prior to data collection, the evaluators underwent standardized training, including multiple rounds of practice sessions with reference images and live animals, followed by calibration exercises until their scores for the same animals were identical or differed by no more than 1 point on the 1–9 scale. During data collection, each cow was scored simultaneously by all three evaluators under identical conditions. The average of the three scores was used for subsequent analyses, and only measurements falling within the mean ± 3 standard deviations were retained.

Additionally, we collected a total of 13,517 dairy herd improvement (DHI) records from the 1,631 Jersey cows throughout their first lactation. Five traits were selected for this study from the DHI records: MY, milk fat percentage (MFP), milk protein percentage (MPP), somatic cell score (SCS), milk urea nitrogen (MUN). MY refers to the daily test-day milk yield (kg) recorded as part of routine DHI testing. Prior to statistical analyses, the distributions of all five traits were assessed for normality using skewness and kurtosis statistics to ensure approximate normality. Subsequently, the DHI records underwent the following quality control steps: retained only the DHI records of cows with udder trait measurements; included only records with lactation days between 5 and 305; kept records with MY between 2.50 and 100 kg, MFP between 2% and 10%, MPP between 2% and 6%, and SCS less than 9; and removed values for each trait that exceeded the mean plus or minus three standard deviations [16, 17]. After quality control, 11,209 DHI records from 1,631 Jersey cows in their first parity were retained for further analysis. Specifically, Farm 1 contributed 3,557 records from 515 cows; Farm 2 contributed 4,393 records from 591 cows; and Farm 3 contributed 3,259 records from 525 cows. The pedigrees of all cows were traced back at least five generations to ensure reliable variance component and heritability estimates, resulting in an average depth of 6.25 generations and a comprehensive pedigree file including 12,263 Jersey cattle (174 bulls and 12,089 cows).

Genome sequencing and genotype imputation

Tail hair follicle samples from 1,631 Jersey cows were collected, with each sample containing at least 20 hairs with intact follicles for DNA extraction and genotyping. DNA was extracted and 1× genome resequencing was performed using 150 bp paired-end reads on the Illumina HiSeq 3000 platform. The quality of the raw reads was assessed using FastQC (v0.12.1), ensuring a minimum Phred quality score of 20, and reads with adapter contamination or >10% ambiguous bases (N) were removed. Clean reads were aligned to the cattle reference genome (ARS-UCD1.2/bosTau9) using BWA (v0.7.12) with default parameters. SAM files were converted to BAM files using SAMtools (v1.2), and potential PCR duplicates were removed using MarkDuplicates in Picard (v1.119). Realignment and recalibration were performed using GATK (v3.8) following its best practices. The data were then imputed using GLIMPSE (v1.1.0) [18], with WGS data from the 8th run of the 1000 Bull Genomes Project serving as the reference panel [19]. After imputation, a total of 28,166,177 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were obtained from 1x whole-genome sequencing data, and the following quality control steps were applied to filter SNPs: (1) SNPs with an accuracy less than 0.8 were removed, (2) SNPs with a variant detection rate >90% and a minor allele frequency (MAF) >0.95 were retained, (3) individuals that significantly deviated from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) were excluded (< 1.0 × 106), (4) only individuals with an SNP detection rate >95% were included. Ultimately, 10,509,673 SNPs from 1,578 Jersey cows were retained for subsequent association analysis.

Genome-wide association analysis

To control for population structure, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on 1,578 genotyped Jersey cows using PLINK v1.9 [23]. Additionally, LD analysis was performed using PLINK software (v1.9) to assess the extent of LD across the genome. Pairwise r² values between SNPs were calculated to evaluate the decay of LD with increasing physical distance.

Transcriptome-wide association study

To estimate the statistical correlation between predicted gene expression and traits, a TWAS was performed using the online platform at https://alphaindex.zju.edu.cn/animalTwas/index.php (accessed Sep. 28, 2024) [28]. The MetaXcan (elastic net) method was applied with the genome assembly ARS-UCD1.2/bosTau9 serving as the reference. All 23 available tissues from the FarmGTEx TWAS-server were analyzed for each trait, and only gene–trait associations that remained significant after multiple-testing correction were retained for interpretation.

Annotation and enrichment analysis

Candidate genes were selected from within 200 kb upstream and downstream of significant SNPs. Using the ARS-UCD1.2/bosTau9 reference genome (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_002263795.1/, accessed Sep. 28, 2024), and a gene ontology (GO) analysis of the candidate genes was performed using the DAVID online tool (https://david.ncifcrf.gov, accessed Sep. 28, 2024) [29].

Effects of non-genetic factors on udder traits in Jersey cows

As shown in Table S1, different farms significantly influenced UD, RUH, RUW, and RTP in Jersey cows, with no significant effect observed on the remaining four traits. Cows from Farm 2 showed significantly higher UD and RTP values than those from Farms 1 and 3, while cows from Farm 3 exhibited significantly lower RUH and RUW compared to Farms 1 and 2.

Birth season also had a significant impact on UD, RUW, and RTP. Winter- and spring-born cows showed significantly higher UD than those born in summer and autumn, whereas those born in summer and autumn had significantly greater RUW. Additionally, spring-born cows demonstrated significantly higher RTP compared to those born in other seasons (Table S1, Mendeley Data, V6, doi: 10.17632/8jsxnwvbhp.6).

The calving season affected UD, CL, TL, RUH, RUW, and RTP. Cows calving in summer showed significantly greater UD and RTP compared to autumn and winter calvings. In contrast, those calving in winter had significantly higher TL, RUH, and RUW values compared to spring and summer calvings (Table S1, Mendeley Data, V6, doi: 10.17632/8jsxnwvbhp.6).

DIM had a significant effect on all udder traits except RTP. Jersey cows with DIM30- DIM80 had significantly greater UD, CL, FUA, and FTP compared to those with DIM81-DIM130 and DIM131- DIM180. Conversely, TL was significantly lower in cows with DIM30- DIM80 compared to those with DIM81-DIM130 and DIM131- DIM180. Jersey cows with DIM81-DIM130 had significantly greater RUH and RUW compared to those with DIM30- DIM80 and DIM131- DIM180 (Table S1, Mendeley Data, V6, doi: 10.17632/8jsxnwvbhp.6).

Impact of udder traits on lactation performance in Jersey cows

Multicollinearity among the independent variables in the multiple linear regression models was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). The VIF values for all traits were close to 1 (Table S2, Mendeley Data, V6, doi: 10.17632/8jsxnwvbhp.6), indicating weak multicollinearity among udder traits and confirming their suitability for inclusion in the regression models. The result for the multiple linear regression coefficients of udder traits on lactation performance is summarized in Table 3. In the MY model, the regression coefficients for UD, FUA, TL, RUW, and RTP were all significant (P < 0.05), indicating significant linear impacts. Negative coefficients were found for UD and FUA (−0.574 and − 0.048, respectively), while positive coefficients were observed for TL, RUW, and RTP (0.427, 0.352, and 0.474, respectively). For MFP, the significant coefficient for UD (positive, 0.015) was observed (P < 0.05). In the MPP model, RTP (negative, −0.040, P < 0.05) and RUW (positive, 0.012, P < 0.05) were significant. Discussion

Heritability of udder traits

Ideal udder traits are critical for improving milk production efficiency, udder health, processing quality, and animal welfare [30, 31]. In this study, exterior udder traits in Jersey cows showed considerable variation in genetic control, with heritability estimates ranging from 0.083 to 0.291. Traits like UD exhibited moderate heritability (0.291), suggesting good potential for genetic improvement through traditional or genomic selection. In contrast, traits such as FUA had lower heritability (0.083), reflecting greater environmental influence from factors like management, nutrition, and milking practices. Nevertheless, prior research has demonstrated that even low-heritability traits can be improved over time using large-scale datasets and genomic selection tools [3, 5].

Our findings align with previous studies in Holstein cattle, where the heritability of UD was reported to be substantially higher than that of other udder traits, reaching up to 0.79 in some cases [2, 32]. In contrast, we estimated a heritability of 0.291 for UD in Jersey cows, reflecting breed-specific differences likely influenced by genetic background, environmental conditions, and management systems. Similar patterns have been observed in other Jersey studies. For example, moderate heritability estimates have been reported for UD and TL, while traits such as FUA and FTP tend to exhibit lower values, reflecting stronger non-genetic influences [33]. Further evidence indicates that teat placement in Jersey cattle varies considerably due to environmental interactions, underscoring the importance of consistent management alongside genetic selection [9]. Collectively, these findings support our results and highlight the need for integrated breeding strategies that address both genetic and environmental factors in improving exterior udder traits.

Additionally, the heritability estimates for most udder traits in this study are broadly consistent with those reported for the Nordic Jersey population, though TL and RUH showed notable differences [32, 34]. In Nordic Holstein cattle, TL had a heritability of 0.44, compared to 0.12 in our study, while RUH was estimated at 0.25 versus 0.14 [34]. These differences may be attributed to regional variation in genetics, environment, and management. In particular, environmental factors can inflate residual variance and obscure genetic signals, thus lowering heritability estimates [35]. Traits like FTP and FUA are especially sensitive to influences such as milking systems, housing, feeding, and climatic stress. Notably, heritability for type traits is often higher in temperate regions than in tropical environments, where fluctuating conditions reduce the genetic contribution to phenotypic variance [10, 36]. These findings underscore the importance of accounting for gene-by-environment (G×E) interactions and applying region-specific evaluations to improve selection accuracy. Additionally, larger sample sizes in Nordic studies likely enhanced estimate precision by better controlling for environmental effects [34].

Phenotypic correlations and genetic correlations of udder traits

The udder is suspended from the ventral side of the body and supported by the superficial and deep layers of the external fascia of the trunk, forming the udder suspension apparatus [37, 38]. This apparatus consists of a lateral plate and a medial plate, with thin plates extending between the udder compartments. The medial plate is primarily composed of elastic tissue, while the lateral plate consists of dense connective tissue. Due to the different tissue compositions of the lateral and medial ligaments, the genetic correlations between the traits they influence vary [2]. In this study, one of the strongest positive genetic correlations was observed between FTP and RTP. This suggests that the positioning of teats on the udder is likely regulated by shared genetic pathways that control overall teat alignment and symmetry. Improving one teat position through selection may concurrently enhance the other, thereby facilitating better udder conformation and milking efficiency. This can be advantageous for joint trait selection, improving udder symmetry and functionality. Similar patterns have been reported in Holstein populations, where coordinated control of teat placement contributes to easier machine milking and reduced risk of injury or mastitis [9, 39]. In contrast, a strong negative genetic correlation was observed between TL and RTP, indicating that longer teats tend to deviate from the ideal central position. This antagonistic relationship may result from anatomical constraints, where excessive teat elongation disrupts the alignment of the teat base [40]. From a breeding standpoint, this trade-off underscores the need for balance—improving one trait may adversely affect another, potentially reducing milking efficiency or increasing the risk of injury [41]. Therefore, breeding decisions should carefully weigh such trade-offs, possibly by applying constrained selection indices or multi-trait optimization to avoid undesirable correlated responses.

Beyond these extremes, several moderate genetic correlations were also observed, offering further insight into the interconnected nature of udder morphology. For example, UD showed a positive correlation with FTP, and FTP with FUA, implying that deeper udders tend to be associated with forward teat placement and a steeper fore udder angle. This likely reflects shared structural influences tied to overall udder size and attachment. While such traits may support greater milk capacity, they could also increase the risk of milking difficulty or elevated somatic cell count if not paired with adequate attachment [40]. Interestingly, RUW and RUH were positively correlated with each other but showed weaker or even negative associations with TL and RTP, suggesting that rear udder structure is genetically cohesive yet distinct from teat morphology. Similar patterns have been reported in Nordic Jersey populations, where RUW and RUH clustered separately from teat traits within udder structure indices [14, 32]. These findings reinforce the value of a holistic selection approach—rather than targeting individual traits, developing composite indices that integrate multiple udder conformation traits may yield more balanced and effective improvements in overall udder health.

Influence of non-genetic factors on udder traits in Jersey cows

Birth season had a significant effect on UD, RUW, and RTP, with summer and autumn-born cows having lower UD and RTP but greater RUW (Table S1). This may be due to the heat stress experienced by cows born in summer and autumn, particularly in the three farms in this study, all located in southern China [42], and persistent high temperatures during these seasons can negatively affect the overall development and body condition of Jersey cattle, leading to issues such as low birth weight, slow growth, and poor development [43]. Many studies have shown that Jersey cattle are more tolerant of heat than Holstein cattle [44, 45]. In this study, Jersey cattle in environments with higher temperatures and more challenging conditions exhibited greater RUH. While this observation could reflect phenotypic variability influenced by environmental factors, it is unlikely that greater RUH represents a direct morphological adaptation to heat stress. Instead, it may indicate a selection or management bias favoring animals with certain udder conformations that support resilience under varying environmental conditions. Since greater RUH results in a more exposed udder area [46], changes in RUH may be a potential indicator of heat tolerance in Jersey cattle. DIM also had a significant effect on all udder traits except RTP (Table S1). Specifically, cows with DIM30-DIM80 exhibited significantly greater UD, CL, FUA, and FTP compared to those with DIM81-DIM130 and DIM131-DIM180 (P < 0.05). This is likely due to the early lactation phase (DIM30-DIM80), characterized by maximum milk production and more pronounced udder fill, resulting in greater UD, CL, FUA, and FTP.Conversely, TL was lower in cows with DIM30-DIM80 compared to those with DIM81-DIM130 and DIM131-DIM180 (P < 0.05) because the udder is more distended and the teats may appear shorter due to increased udder fill [47]. Additionally, cows with DIM131-DIM180 had greater RUH and RUW compared to those with DIM30-DIM80 and DIM81-DIM130 (P < 0.05), as the udder becomes less distended and more structurally pronounced in the mid to late stages of lactation [48].

Although non-genetic factors such as farm, calving season, and DIM significantly influenced udder traits, this study did not directly evaluate G×E interactions. However, the presence of significant fixed effects suggests that environmental variation contributes substantially to phenotypic variance (Table S1). These non-genetic factors may mask or modify genetic expression, especially in traits with low heritability like FUA and CL. Future studies using random regression or reaction norm models could better capture environmental sensitivity of genetic variances, allowing estimation of G×E interactions and informing more resilient selection strategies across variable production environments [49, 50].

Influence of udder traits on lactation performance in Jersey cows

This study demonstrated that UD, FUA, TL, RUW, and RTP were found to significantly impact MY. Specifically, the negative regression coefficients for UD and FUA with MY indicated that increases in these traits reduce MY (Table 3). This finding aligns with previous studies, which also suggested that deeper udders were associated with lower MY [51, 52]. This correlation may be because an excessively large UD can lead to higher SCS, increasing the risk of mastitis and consequently reducing milk production [53]. In contrast, TL and RUW were positively correlated with MY. Longer TL facilitate efficient milk ejection, allowing for more complete emptying of the udder during milking [54]. A wider RUW provides better support and stability to the udder, reducing the risk of udder-related issues and contributing to higher milk production efficiency [55].

Milk fat and protein percentages, crucial indicators of milk quality, were also influenced by udder traits in this study (Table 3). UD was positively correlated with MFP, suggesting that deeper udders result in higher fat content, potentially because both UD and MFP negatively impact MY [16]. In the multiple regression model for MPP, RUW was positively correlated, while RTP was negatively correlated with protein content. Improper RTP can lead to incomplete milking and increased susceptibility to mastitis, both of which can reduce milk protein content [56]. This suggests that an increase in RTP is associated with a decrease in protein percentage.

SCS, an indicator of milk quality and a diagnostic criterion for mastitis in dairy cows, was also significantly influenced by UD and RUW in this study (Table 3). Specifically, UD was positively correlated with SCS, while RUW was negatively correlated. These results corroborate previous findings, indicating that deeper, looser udders are more prone to injury and contamination from proximity to the ground, thus increasing SCS [53]. This correlation highlights the need for careful management and breeding strategies to reduce the risk of mastitis, thereby improving milk quality. Additionally, an increased likelihood of subclinical mastitis was observed in cows with udders closer to the ground and longer front teats [57]. However, this study did not find a linear relationship between TL and SCS in Jersey cows, indicating that other factors might mitigate this relationship in this specific breed.

MUN, reflecting the metabolic status of dairy cows, was influenced by CL, FTP, and RUW, with negative regression coefficients indicating lower MUN content with increases in these traits (Table 3). This may be attributed to the enhanced udder health and efficiency provided by traits such as CL, RUH, and FTP, which contribute to a well-supported and properly attached udder. Healthier udders may lead to more efficient protein utilization and reduced metabolic stress, thereby resulting in lower MUN levels [58]. Additionally, cows with proper udder conformation are likely to experience fewer udder-related health issues, further contributing to optimal metabolic function and consequently lower MUN content.

Candidate loci and functional genes associated with udder traits in Jersey cows

In this study, a total of 39 SNPs surpassed the significance threshold and were identified as significantly associated with udder traits in Jersey cows (Fig. 2). The genes closest to the significant SNPs include NFATC3, MGC134040, OSTF1, CEP55, ABCD2, TMEM26, FFAR4, and ELK3, many of which are associated with the growth and immune regulation of cattle. NFATC3, a nuclear factor of activated T cells, the latest research has been confirmed that the TRPC4/Ca2+/Calcineurin/NFATc3 pathway can regulate the proliferation of bovine muscle satellite cells [59]. OSTF1, identified in quantitative trait loci (QTL) regions associated with mastitis, may play a role in bovine mammary gland defense [60]. CEP55 inhibits the growth of bovine skeletal muscle, and interfering with its expression can promote the proliferation of bovine myoblasts [61]. ABCD2 is related to marbling and backfat traits in Korean cattle [62], while TMEM26 is involved in beige fat cell differentiation in cattle [62]. FFAR4 is implicated in the proliferation and steroidogenesis of bovine granulosa cells [63]. Notably, the HAL gene, previously found to be significantly associated with TL and RTP in Holstein cattle, was also identified in this study as being linked to the CL trait in Jersey cattle. This suggests it may play a key role in regulating udder development in dairy cows.

Following GWAS, TWAS can be utilized to identify and prioritize candidate causal genes [13]. Through TWAS analysis, four genes (EFHB, TAS2R46, SLC35G1, and ENPP4) were significantly identified (Fig. 3). Studies have shown that EFHB is a key gene for the formation of bovine muscle fibers [64], and the expression of this gene in the liver may affect the manifestation of RUH (Fig. 3). Genes associated with CL (SLC35G1 and ENPP4) were significantly expressed in monocytes and muscle, respectively. However, the functions of these genes remain unclear in corresponding organization, and further molecular biology studies are needed to verify their roles.

GO enrichment analysis of genes within 200 kb upstream and downstream of significant SNPs identified 15 genes significantly enriched in 16 GO terms (Fig. 4). AMDHD1, associated with muscle development in beef cattle, exhibited significant differential expression across various beef cattle breeds [65]. ACTC1 promotes bovine myoblast and preadipocyte differentiation, influencing cell proliferation [66]. PRKCA has been implicated in growth and milk production in cattle [67]. ENPP4, identified in both GO enrichment and TWAS analyses, is a key candidate gene influencing the CL in Jersey cows. Additionally, ENPP4 has been recognized as a candidate gene affecting MUN in Holstein cows [68]. Interestingly, in this study, CL significantly affected the performance of MUN (Table 3). Therefore, the relationship between ENPP4 expression in muscle, the size of CL, and MUN levels is an issue worthy of further exploration.

Implications, limitations, and future directions

In addition to advancing our understanding of the genetic architecture of udder traits—encompassing heritability estimates, genetic correlations among udder traits, and their quantified effects on lactation performance—these findings have important implications for practical breeding programs. The estimated genetic parameters and correlations provide a basis for predicting correlated selection responses, enabling breeders to improve multiple udder conformation traits simultaneously [69]. Moreover, the identified genetic signals and candidate genes could serve as informative markers for genomic selection or marker-assisted selection [70]. Integrating these genetic parameters, genetic correlations, and molecular markers into selection indices may help optimize both functional and production-related traits, thereby contributing to improved herd health, milking efficiency, and overall productivity.

Although this study offers valuable insights into the genetic architecture of udder traits in Jersey cattle and identifies several candidate genes and genetic signals associated with udder morphology, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The phenotypic data were obtained exclusively from cows maintained under specific management conditions on three farms, which may limit the extrapolation of the findings to other production systems. In addition, the phenotypic and genetic data represent a single time-point snapshot within early to mid-lactation, precluding direct inference of temporal dynamics or causal relationships between genetic variants, gene expression, and udder traits. Finally, the TWAS analyses relied on pre-built gene expression prediction models from the FarmGTEx TWAS-server, and the availability and accuracy of these reference panels may influence the detection power and tissue specificity of gene–trait associations. Future research should broaden the population scope by including multiple parities and production environments, and adopt longitudinal designs to capture changes in udder traits across lactation stages. The application of advanced, objective phenotyping technologies—such as three-dimensional imaging or automated sensor-based measurements—could further improve measurement precision and repeatability. Moreover, functional validation of candidate genes through in vitro assays or in vivo models will be essential for elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying genetic variation in udder morphology.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides valuable insights into the genetic architecture of exterior udder traits in Jersey cattle. We estimated genetic parameters and identified QTLs, while also evaluating the influence of udder traits on lactation performance. Traits such as UD, FUA, TL, RUW, and RTP emerged as significant predictors of milk yield, with UD, RTP, and RUW also contributing to variation in milk composition and udder health indicators. High genetic correlations between FUA and traits like FTP and TL suggest potential for simultaneous genetic improvement. Additionally, significant non-genetic effects—such as farm, birth season, calving season, and DIM—were observed. Through GWAS, 39 significant SNPs were identified, and TWAS and GO enrichment analyses revealed candidate genes including NFATC3, OSTF1, ENPP4, PRKCA, EFHB, TAS2R46, and SLC35G1 were identified. These findings offer a foundation for incorporating genetic parameters, correlations, and molecular markers into marker-assisted and genomic selection programs to improve udder conformation while maintaining or enhancing milk production. By incorporating these genetic insights into breeding programs, selection for improved udder conformation can be achieved alongside milk production, ultimately enhancing herd productivity, udder health, and milking efficiency—benefiting both farmers and breeding companies.
