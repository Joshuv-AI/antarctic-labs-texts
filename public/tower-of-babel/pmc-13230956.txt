Efficacy of a snare-based hybrid endoscopic submucosal dissection technique in the management of esophageal submucosal tumors
Authors: Bin He, Jie Xu, Yunfu Feng, Ye Ye, Xiaodan Xu, Luojie Liu, Wenwen Diao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo assess the safety and clinical outcomes of snare-assisted hybrid endoscopic submucosal dissection (H-ESD) versus conventional endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) for esophageal submucosal tumors (SMTs). A retrospective analysis was conducted on 270 patients with esophageal SMTs treated at three centers (2020–2024), including 102 H-ESD and 168 EMR cases. The primary endpoint was the en bloc resection rate. Secondary endpoints included operative duration and postoperative outcomes. Subgroup analyses focused on tumors ≤ 10 mm and muscularis mucosa (MM)-confined lesions. H-ESD achieved higher en bloc resection rates (99.0% vs. 88.7%, P = 0.002) but longer procedure times (11.2 ± 2.2 vs. 9.9 ± 2.4 min, P < 0.001) and more clip usage (P = 0.008). Postoperative complications and hospitalization duration were similar (P > 0.05). H-ESD reduced incomplete resection risk (OR = 0.08, P = 0.014), with superior performance in small tumors (98.9% vs. 86.8%, P = 0.003) and MM-layer lesions (98.9% vs. 88.1%, P = 0.006). Snare-assisted H-ESD is a safe and effective approach for esophageal SMTs. It provides near-complete en bloc resection with acceptable procedure times and may be considered in experienced centers for small, superficial esophageal SMTs.Keywords: Esophageal submucosal tumors, Hybrid endoscopic submucosal dissection, Endoscopic mucosal resection, SnareSubject terms: Cancer, Gastroenterology, Medical research, Oncology

Introduction

Submucosal tumors (SMTs) of the esophagus are benign or potentially malignant lesions originating from the submucosal or muscularis propria layers, and their clinical management requires a balance between complete tumor resection and surgical safety1,2. Conventional endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) is widely used due to its simplicity and shorter procedural time, but its en bloc resection rate is limited by tumor size and depth of invasion, particularly for lesions involving the submucosa or deeper locations, which may lead to incomplete resection or increased risk of recurrence3,4. In recent years, endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) has significantly improved en bloc resection rates by layer-by-layer dissection of the submucosa, but it demands high technical expertise, prolonged procedural duration, and may increase intraoperative complication risks5,6.

To optimize technical efficacy, hybrid endoscopic techniques have emerged, combining the rapidity of EMR with the precision of ESD, aiming to shorten procedural time while maintaining high en bloc resection rates. However, existing studies primarily focus on gastric or colorectal lesions7–10, and recent evidence also supports its use in challenging scenarios such as recurrent or fibrotic non-lifting colorectal lesions11. By contrast, the clinical value of hybrid endoscopic submucosal dissection (H-ESD) techniques specifically for esophageal SMTs remains systematically underevaluated. Furthermore, previous studies reported favorable efficacy of snare-assisted H-ESD for colorectal tumors12–14, but reports on its application for esophageal SMTs are scarce.

This study aims to investigate the safety and clinical outcome differences between snare-assisted H-ESD and conventional EMR in the treatment of esophageal SMTs. Through multi-center analysis, we focus on core indicators such as en bloc resection rate, operational efficiency, and postoperative complications, and further analyze subgroup performances in small tumors and superficial lesions, providing evidence-based support for endoscopic procedure selection. The study hypothesizes that H-ESD, by integrating the rapid resection of snare with the precise dissection of ESD, can achieve superior tumor clearance without significantly prolonging procedural time, particularly suitable for the precise treatment needs of esophageal SMTs.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study retrospectively collected data from patients with esophageal SMTs treated with H-ESD and EMR using a snare at three tertiary medical institutions: Changshu Hospital Affiliated to Soochow University, Kunshan First People’s Hospital, and Zhangjiagang First People’s Hospital. Among the 270 enrolled patients, 126 were treated at Changshu Hospital Affiliated to Soochow University, 103 at Kunshan First People’s Hospital, and 41 at Zhangjiagang First People’s Hospital. Inclusion criteria were: (1) patients who underwent surgical treatment using a snare; (2) tumor size < 20 mm; (3) lesions confirmed by endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS) to originate from the muscularis mucosa (MM) or submucosa. Exclusion criteria included: (1) history of esophageal surgery; (2) conventional ESD or submucosal tunneling endoscopic resection (STER) procedures; (3) platelet count < 100 × 10⁹/L or international normalized ratio (INR) > 1.5; (4) synchronous multiple esophageal lesions requiring surgery; (5) poor cardiopulmonary function unable to tolerate anesthesia or surgery; (6) lack of signed informed consent or refusal of surgery; (7) incomplete medical records. All endoscopic procedures were performed by senior endoscopists. The study protocol was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Changshu Hospital Affiliated to Soochow University, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Endoscopic equipment and procedures

The study employed a snare (detailed in Fig. 1), a single-channel electronic gastroscope (GIF-260 J) with CV-260 or CV-290 processors, a transparent cap, an injection needle, a CO2 pump, titanium clips from Olympus Corporation, Japan, high-frequency electrosurgical units (electrosurgical generators) (VIO 200D, VIO 300D) from ERBE Corporation, Germany, a titanium clip (AG-51042-1950-135-16) from Hangzhou Anjies Medical Technology Co., Ltd., a rotatable harmonic clip (ROCC-D-26–195) from Nanjing Micro-Tech Co., Ltd., and indigo carmine. Specifically, the same model of oval-shaped snare with a fully opened diameter of 20 mm was used in both groups across all centers. The submucosal elevating solution consisted of 0.9% normal saline, indigo carmine (0.04 mg/mL), and epinephrine at a dilution of 1:100,000. Electrosurgical settings were standardized across centers as follows: Forced Coagulation mode (Effect 2, maximum 50 W) was used for incision and coagulation, and Endocut Q mode2–4 was used for cutting, using a VIO 200D/300D generator (ERBE). All patients underwent preoperative evaluations, including blood tests, blood biochemistry, coagulation profiles, electrocardiograms, and endoscopic ultrasound examinations, to ensure no surgical contraindications existed. Patients fasted and abstained from water for eight hours prior to the procedure, during which general anesthesia was administered, and a transparent attachment was fixed to the endoscope tip. Carbon dioxide was used for insufflation. In this retrospective cohort, the assignment to either the snare-assisted H-ESD group or the conventional EMR group was based on clinical practice patterns rather than randomization. The choice of procedure was primarily determined by operator preference and clinical judgment, taking into account the endoscopist’s familiarity with the technique, lesion characteristics assessed by preoperative endoscopic ultrasonography (e.g., size, location, layer of origin). All endoscopic procedures were performed by senior endoscopists, each of whom holds advanced endoscopic qualifications and performs more than 50 esophageal ESD and/or EMR procedures annually. The surgical protocols for both groups began with submucosal injection of 0.9% normal saline, indigo carmine, and epinephrine (at a dilution of 1:100,000) at the margins of esophageal SMTs to elevate the mucosa. For the H-ESD group (Fig. 2), all operators followed a unified protocol: the snare was partially deployed, exposing approximately 2 to 3 millimeters of the tip, to perform a circumferential incision around the tumor, followed by full deployment to ensnare the tumor base, high-frequency electrocoagulation and electrocision. For the EMR group (Fig. 3), the snare was deployed to directly ensnare the tumor base, followed by high-frequency electrocoagulation and electrocision. In both groups, the wound was examined for bleeding, and hemostasis was achieved via electrocoagulation using the snare tip if necessary, followed by the application of titanium clips to close the wound. While operator experience and minor technical adaptations inevitably exist in multicenter studies, the consistent use of equipment, energy settings, and procedural steps aimed to minimize inter-operator variability.

Postoperative management

The resected tumors were washed postoperatively, and the dimensions of the lesions were measured. The specimens underwent fixation in 10% formalin solution, followed by paraffin embedding and sectioning. All patients were observed 24-hour nil per os (NPO), accompanied by proton pump inhibitor administration, fluid resuscitation, and supportive care. Postoperative surveillance was performed to monitor for complications including hemorrhage, perforation, chest pain, pyrexia, etc., with gastroscopy or chest CT imaging conducted when clinically indicated. If no complications arose after 24 h of fasting, patients were advanced to a liquid or semi-liquid diet regimen.

Data collection and definitions

The primary endpoint was the en bloc resection rate. Secondary endpoints included operative duration, postoperative complications, and length of hospital stay. Patient data included age, gender, medical history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and coronary artery disease, anticoagulant/antiplatelet medication history, preoperative symptoms (physical examination findings, dysphagia accompanied by discomfort, retrosternal discomfort), tumor location, tumor dimensions, invasion depth, operative duration, number of titanium clips deployed, postoperative complications (specifically postoperative fever, chest pain, bleeding, and perforation), en bloc resection rate, postoperative length of hospital stay, and postoperative histopathological examination results. The operative duration was measured from the insertion of the endoscope into the oral cavity until complete wound closure with titanium clips and withdrawal of the endoscope. Tumor dimensions and invasion depth were primarily assessed by preoperative EUS. Postoperative bleeding was defined as significant hematemesis or melena occurring after endoscopic intervention, accompanied by a hemoglobin decrease ≥ 2 g/dL, altered vital signs, and emergency endoscopic confirmation of mucosal defect-related hemorrhage15. Postoperative fever was identified as body temperature exceeding 37.3℃ following the procedure. En bloc resection specifically referred to the complete endoscopic removal of esophageal submucosal lesions in a single, intact procedure16.

Follow-up

All patients were instructed to follow up at gastroenterology outpatient clinics every other month after discharge, with a requirement for repeat esophagogastroduodenoscopy within 6–12 months to evaluate the surgical wound healing and detect any recurrence.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages, and analyzed using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables that follow a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and between-group comparisons are performed using the independent samples t-test. For continuous data that do not conform to a normal distribution, the median and interquartile range (IQR) are reported, and the Mann-Whitney U test is used for between-group analysis. Univariable analysis was conducted to identify potential risk factors for non-en-bloc resection. Variables demonstrating significance (P < 0.1) in this preliminary analysis were subsequently included in a multivariable logistic regression model for further evaluation. We assessed model fit using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (P = 0.62) and calculated the variance inflation factor (VIF) for each predictor (all VIF < 1.5), indicating no concerning multicollinearity. Statistical significance was established at a p-value < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26 (Chicago, IL, USA). To account for potential clustering of outcomes within participating centers, we additionally performed a mixed-effects logistic regression model with center as a random effect. The dependent variable was non-en-bloc resection (yes/no). Fixed effects included age, gender, tumor location, tumor size, invasion depth, and procedure type (H-ESD vs. EMR). The model was fitted using maximum likelihood estimation, and results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 270 patients with esophageal SMTs were enrolled, including 102 patients treated with H-ESD and 168 patients treated with EMR. The baseline clinical and tumor characteristics between the two groups were well-balanced (Table 1). No significant differences were observed in age (H-ESD: 51.5 ± 4.6 years vs. EMR: 50.8 ± 4.9 years, P = 0.245), gender distribution (male: 58.8% vs. 47.6%, P = 0.074), or comorbidities such as hypertension (2.9% vs. 4.2%, P = 0.747), diabetes (2.0% vs. 3.6%, P = 0.714), or coronary disease (1.0% vs. 3.0%, P = 0.414). Tumor characteristics, including location (upper: 40.2% vs. 37.5%; middle: 25.5% vs. 28.0%; lower: 34.3% vs. 34.5%, P = 0.876), size (< 10 mm: 85.3% vs. 80.4%, P = 0.304), and invasion depth (MM: 87.3% vs. 89.3%, P = 0.612), were comparable between groups.

Operative outcomes and complications

H-ESD demonstrated significantly higher en bloc resection rates compared to EMR (99.0% vs. 88.7%, P = 0.002). The risk difference for en bloc resection was 10.3% (95% CI: 3.8–16.8%), with a relative risk of 1.12 (95% CI: 1.05–1.19). However, H-ESD required longer operative times (11.2 ± 2.2 min vs. 9.9 ± 2.4 min, P < 0.001) and more clips (3.91 ± 0.99 vs. 3.58 ± 0.94, P = 0.008) (Table 2). Postoperative complications, including fever (6.9% vs. 3.0%, P = 0.221) and chest pain (0.0% vs. 1.2%, P = 0.528), did not differ significantly. No cases of bleeding or perforation occurred in either group. Pathological analysis revealed similar distributions of leiomyoma (98.0% vs. 98.2%), gastrointestinal stromal tumors (1.0% vs. 1.2%), and cysts (1.0% vs. 0.6%, P > 0.999). The postoperative pathological examination of 20 non-en bloc patients revealed leiomyoma in all cases, and none of them experienced recurrence during postoperative follow-up. All patients underwent at least one surveillance endoscopy postoperatively. The median follow-up duration (IQR) was 24.0 months (12.0, 36.0) in the H-ESD group and 23.0 months (11.0, 36.0) in the EMR group. No recurrences were observed in the H-ESD group, whereas 3 recurrences occurred in the EMR group.

Predictors of non-en-bloc resection

Multivariate logistic regression identified H-ESD as an independent protective factor against non-en-bloc resection (OR = 0.08, 95% CI: 0.01–0.60, P = 0.014). Tumor location (middle vs. upper: OR = 2.73, P = 0.089; lower vs. upper: OR = 1.34, P = 0.643) and operative time (≥ 10 vs. <10 min: OR = 0.62, P = 0.310) were not significantly associated with resection outcomes (Table 3). To evaluate whether the observed association between procedure type and resection outcome was confounded by center-level effects, we fitted a mixed-effects logistic regression model with center as a random effect. After adjusting for age, sex, tumor location, size, and invasion depth as fixed effects, H-ESD remained a strong independent protective factor against non-en-bloc resection (adjusted OR = 0.07, 95% CI: 0.01–0.55, P = 0.012). These results are consistent with those of the conventional multivariable logistic regression (Table 3), indicating that the superiority of H-ESD in achieving en-bloc resection is robust and not substantially influenced by clustering within centers. In addition, we performed a sensitivity analysis using Firth logistic regression, which reduces small-sample bias. The adjusted odds ratio for H-ESD remained strongly protective against non–en bloc resection (OR = 0.07, 95% CI: 0.01–0.52, P = 0.010), consistent with our primary model.

Subgroup analyses

For tumors < 10 mm, H-ESD maintained superior en bloc resection rates (98.9% vs. 88.1%, P = 0.003), with a higher proportion of prolonged operative times (≥ 10 min: 79.3% vs. 53.3%, P < 0.001) compared to EMR (Table 4). The corresponding risk difference was 10.8% (95% CI: 4.1–17.5%) and relative risk was 1.12 (95% CI: 1.05–1.20). Similarly, in tumors confined to the MM layer, H-ESD achieved higher en bloc resection success (98.9% vs. 89.3%, P = 0.006) but required longer operative times (≥ 10 min: 78.7% vs. 50.7%, P < 0.001) (Table 5). This represented a risk difference of 9.6% (95% CI: 3.1–16.1%) and a relative risk of 1.11 (95% CI: 1.04–1.18). Postoperative fever, chest pain, and hospital stay remained comparable in both subgroups (P > 0.05). Given the retrospective design and exploratory nature of these subgroup analyses, the findings should be interpreted with caution and validated in prospective studies.

Discussion

The present study addresses a critical clinical gap in the field of therapeutic endoscopy by systematically evaluating the snare-assisted H-ESD technique as an optimized approach for managing esophageal SMTs. Current endoscopic interventions for SMTs, such as conventional EMR, often face challenges in balancing procedural efficiency with complete tumor removal, particularly for lesions requiring precise histological assessment and curative resection. Building upon the foundational principles of EMR, our research introduced a novel hybrid technique integrating snare assistance to enhance dissection precision while maintaining procedural safety. By comparing the clinical performance of H-ESD against standard EMR across three high-volume tertiary referral centers, we focused on key metrics including en bloc resection rates, procedural efficiency, and postoperative outcomes. The findings unequivocally demonstrate that H-ESD achieves significantly higher en bloc resection rates (99.0% vs. 88.7%) despite marginally prolonged procedural times, with comparable safety profiles in terms of complication rates and hospital stays. These results suggest that H-ESD is a technically feasible method associated with higher en-bloc resection rates, particularly advantageous for small (≤ 10 mm) and MM-confined lesions where histological integrity is critical for accurate diagnosis and recurrence prevention.

This study corroborates that H-ESD, when assisted by a snare, demonstrates substantial technical advantages in the treatment of esophageal SMTs. The en bloc resection rate of 99.0% achieved with H-ESD is markedly superior to the 88.7% observed with traditional EMR (P = 0.002), without increasing the risk of postoperative complications. This outcome aligns closely with findings from prior studies comparing the efficacy of ESD and EMR: ESD, due to its direct submucosal dissection characteristics, generally exhibits a higher en bloc resection rate than EMR17–19, though conventional ESD is time-consuming and technically demanding20–23. Conversely, while EMR is quicker to perform, its reliance on submucosal injection followed by snare resection often leads to piecemeal resection and residual disease24–26. By integrating the submucosal dissection advantages of ESD with the precise positioning function of a snare, H-ESD successfully reconciles the conflict between efficiency and efficacy, emerging as an innovative choice for the treatment of esophageal SMTs.

In addition to the techniques compared in this study, band-assisted resection—in which a lesion is suctioned into a cap followed by band ligation prior to snare resection—represents another established endoscopic approach, particularly for smaller or pedunculated lesions27. This method is often favored for its technical simplicity, rapid execution, and generally favorable safety profile. For selected small esophageal SMTs, especially those with a prominent intraluminal growth pattern, band ligation can provide effective mucosal capture and may facilitate en bloc resection. However, its applicability may be limited for purely submucosal or broad-based lesions where adequate suction and band placement can be challenging. While the current study focused on comparing snare-based H-ESD with conventional EMR, future comparative studies incorporating band-assisted techniques could further clarify the optimal endoscopic strategy for different morphological subtypes of esophageal SMTs.

The introduction of the snare is the core modification that distinguishes H-ESD from traditional ESD. Previous studies have highlighted that traditional ESD requires full reliance on an electric knife for submucosal dissection, making the procedure complex and prone to perforation due to limited visual fields28,29. In contrast, H-ESD, assisted by a snare, allows for direct grasping of the lesion base after mucosal pre-incision, thereby reducing the scope of submucosal dissection8,30,31. This mechanism may enhance the en bloc resection rate through several pathways: EMR achieves lesion resection by lifting the lesion through submucosal injection of saline or other fluids, followed by snare ligation. However, limited by the snare’s diameter and operating space, larger lesions often require piecemeal resection, increasing the risk of tumor residue. In contrast, H-ESD combines submucosal injection with precise dissection techniques, separating the lesion from the muscularis propria through submucosal injection and accurately delineating the lesion margins via cutting, thereby enabling en bloc resection of larger lesions. EMR relies on the “lifting sign” after submucosal injection, but the thin mucosal layer and scarcity of fibrous tissue in the esophagus result in unstable lifting effects post-injection32, whereas H-ESD directly immobilizes the lesion with a snare, significantly reducing the risk of lesion slippage during resection. Although the operation time for H-ESD in this study was slightly longer than that for EMR (11.2 vs. 9.9 min), there was no statistically significant difference in complication rates between H-ESD and traditional EMR (P > 0.05), with the risks of perforation and delayed bleeding being manageable. This aligns with conclusions from prior studies on the safety of “hybrid techniques”8,10,12,13,33. Considering that H-ESD can significantly reduce the rate of incomplete resection (OR = 0.08), its marginal time cost is acceptable in clinical practice. Although H-ESD required a slightly longer mean operative time than conventional EMR (11.2 ± 2.2 vs. 9.9 ± 2.4 min, P < 0.001), the absolute difference of approximately 1.3 min is likely of negligible clinical relevance in high-volume endoscopic practice. This marginal increase in time appears to be an acceptable trade-off given the substantially higher en-bloc resection rate achieved with H-ESD, which is of paramount importance for accurate histopathological assessment and recurrence prevention in small esophageal SMTs.

It is important to emphasize that the conclusions drawn from this study are primarily applicable to a specific subset of esophageal SMTs. The cohort was predominantly composed of small tumors (≤ 10 mm) confined to the MM or superficial submucosa, with leiomyoma being the overwhelming pathological diagnosis. Therefore, the demonstrated superiority of snare-assisted H-ESD in achieving high en bloc resection rates should be interpreted within this context. Our findings may not be generalizable to larger tumors (> 20 mm), lesions originating from the muscularis propria, or those with different histopathological subtypes (e.g., gastrointestinal stromal tumors beyond very small, superficial variants). For deeper or larger lesions, alternative techniques such as STER or full-thickness resection may be more relevant and were not evaluated in this study. Furthermore, the procedures were performed in high-volume tertiary centers by experienced endoscopists, which may limit applicability in community or low-volume settings.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations warrant consideration. Firstly, the retrospective design inherently introduces risks of selection bias and unmeasured confounding factors, despite efforts to adjust for baseline differences through multivariable analysis. Secondly, the study was conducted across high-volume tertiary referral centers with experienced endoscopists, which may limit the generalizability of outcomes to community or low-resource settings where operator expertise and access to advanced endoscopic tools may vary. Third, the predominance of small lesions (≤ 10 mm) and MM-layer-confined tumors in the cohort restricts the applicability of our conclusions to this specific subgroup of esophageal SMTs. Our results may not generalize to larger (> 20 mm) or deeper submucosal lesions (especially those arising from the muscularis propria), which may require more complex resection strategies such as STER or surgical intervention. Finally, while H-ESD demonstrated superior en-bloc resection rates, the modest prolongation in procedural time (1.3 min) may hold limited clinical significance in experienced centers and should not be overinterpreted as a major procedural disadvantage. Future prospective trials with standardized protocols, extended follow-up, and broader inclusion of tumor subtypes are needed to validate these findings and refine patient selection criteria.

In conclusion, this multicenter comparative analysis demonstrates that snare-assisted H-ESD represents a significant advancement in the management of esophageal SMTs, particularly for small (≤ 20 mm, predominantly < 10 mm) and superficial (MM/submucosa-confined) esophageal SMTs, particularly leiomyomas, in experienced endoscopic centers. The technique achieves near-universal en bloc resection—a robust surrogate for complete tumor removal in this predominantly benign cohort—without compromising procedural safety, positioning it as a potentially advantageous alternative to conventional EMR. Our findings should not be extrapolated to larger, deeper (e.g., muscularis propria-originating) SMTs, for which other resection strategies may be more appropriate. While histologic margin status (R0) was not routinely assessed in this retrospective cohort, the high en bloc rate, coupled with the absence of recurrence in the H-ESD group during follow-up, strongly supports its therapeutic efficacy for well-demarcated SMTs. Although the marginally prolonged operative time and technical complexity may influence its adoption in non-specialized settings, the comparable safety profile underscores its feasibility in high-volume endoscopic centers. These findings advocate for the integration of H-ESD into therapeutic algorithms for esophageal SMTs where complete resection is paramount. Future prospective research should incorporate standardized R0 assessment and longer follow-up to further validate its oncologic adequacy and long-term outcomes.
