Institutional evolution of a robotic otolaryngology program across consecutive da Vinci Si, Xi, and SP platform transitions
Authors: Stella Chin-Shaw Tsai, Shih-Wei Chen, Mei-Wen Nian, Hung-Min Chang, Chung-Hsiung Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sustained development of a robotic otolaryngology program depends on coordinated technological, workforce, clinical, and educational development. However, how these elements evolve together over time remains poorly understood. We conducted a retrospective longitudinal program evaluation of 757 consecutive robot-assisted otolaryngology–head and neck procedures performed at a regional teaching hospital from 2013 to 2025. Annual volume increased from 10 procedures in 2013 to a peak of 122 in 2022 and remained high at 95 in 2025, showing a strong correlation with calendar year (Spearman’s ρ = 0.923, p < 0.001). Negative binomial regression estimated an average annual increase of 21.5% (incidence rate ratio 1.215, 95% confidence interval 1.157–1.276). The program progressed from the da Vinci Si to Xi and single-port platforms, with the single-port system accounting for 52 of 95 procedures (54.7%) in its first year of deployment. The workforce expanded from one to five surgeons; the founding surgeon performed 531 procedures (70.1%), while four recruited surgeons performed 226 (29.9%). Clinical scope encompassed oral cancer, radical tonsillectomy, sleep surgery, benign and malignant tongue-base resections, parotidectomy, thyroid surgery, supraglottic laryngectomy, hypopharyngectomy, and other head and neck procedures, with distributions differing significantly across the three phases (χ² = 253.43, Monte Carlo p < 0.001; Cramér’s V = 0.409). Structured multidisciplinary training and observation activities accompanied clinical growth and the program’s development into a regional educational hub. This 13-year experience demonstrates the transformation of a single-surgeon service into a multisurgeon regional clinical and educational program across successive platform transitions.

Introduction

Robot-assisted surgeries have become a crucial modality in head and neck surgery, providing access to anatomically complex subsites with reduced morbidity. Traditional limitations of transoral approaches, including restricted visualization, linear instrumentation, and poor access to deep structures, frequently necessitated highly invasive open techniques such as mandibulotomy [1]. The development of single-port (SP) robotic surgical systems has introduced new possibilities for instrument triangulation and visualization within the confined transoral workspace, while simplifying setup and reducing intraoral clutter. These technical enhancements have extended the clinical reach of transoral robotic surgery (TORS) to anatomically challenging subsites, such as the hypopharynx [2], and have been associated with comparable safety profiles and rapid adoption [3]. Furthermore, clinical transitions from multiport platforms to single-port platforms have demonstrated shortened setup times and improved maneuverability without compromising oncologic safety [4, 5].

To build a foundation for robotic surgery in head and neck diseases, our group has evaluated several clinical facets of TORS, including treatment outcomes for oral cavity cancer in an endemic region, management of patients with trismus, early institutional experience with the da Vinci SP platform, prospective evaluation of SP TORS for head and neck lesions and obstructive sleep apnea, and the clinical transition from multiport to single-port robotic surgery [6–11]. Collectively, these studies evaluated the clinical feasibility, safety, and outcomes of specific robotic head and neck applications within our institution. These indication-, cohort-, and platform-specific studies partially overlap with procedures included in the present institutional series. In contrast, the present study evaluates the entire 13-year robotic otolaryngology experience at the program level, integrating longitudinal changes in procedural volume, robotic platforms, surgical workforce, clinical indications, and educational activities within a single institutional framework.

Despite increasing reports describing clinical outcomes and technical feasibility, the long-term institutional evolution of robotic otolaryngology programs remains poorly characterized [12]. Most published studies focus on single-surgeon learning curves, limited clinical indications, or short-term prospective cohorts. Consequently, there is little evidence describing how robotic programs evolve over more than a decade while integrating successive platform generations, expanding the surgical workforce, and developing structured educational infrastructure [12, 13]. This is particularly critical because limited clinical exposure to robotic surgery remains a reported training barrier in otolaryngology residency curricula [14]. Within established frameworks for evaluating surgical innovations, such as the IDEAL framework [15], examining long-term patterns of institutional development may provide useful insights into how robotic programs evolve in mid-sized regional healthcare settings.

To address this knowledge gap, the present study examines the 13-year (2013–2025) evolution of a robotic otolaryngology–head and neck surgery program in Taiwan by integrating longitudinal data on procedural volume, robotic platform adoption, workforce expansion, clinical indications, and educational activities. Unlike our previous indication- or platform-specific reports, the current analysis examines these dimensions within an integrated program-level framework to characterize institutional development over time. The findings may offer insights into a potential developmental pathway for institutions operating in comparable settings.

Methods

Study design and procedure eligibility

This retrospective longitudinal program evaluation examined the development of an institutional robotic otolaryngology–head and neck surgery program over a 13-year period from January 1, 2013, to December 31, 2025. The unit of analysis was the robot-assisted surgical procedure. All consecutive robot-assisted procedures performed by the Department of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery during the study period were included. The analysis focused on program-level surgical activity rather than individual patient outcomes. Each eligible procedure was therefore counted separately, including procedures performed on the same patient on different occasions.

Procedures were identified from institutional robotic surgery logs and verified against the electronic medical record system. Eligible procedures included robot-assisted surgery performed for oncologic, benign, or functional indications involving the head and neck. Procedures were excluded if (1) the robotic system was docked but not used for the principal surgical procedure, (2) the operation was canceled before surgical incision, or (3) operative records were incomplete. A total of 757 procedures met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis.

The primary objective was to characterize longitudinal program development across four predefined domains: (1) technological development, assessed by robotic platform adoption; (2) workforce development, assessed by surgeon participation and annual procedural volume; (3) clinical development, assessed by changes in surgical indications; and (4) educational development, assessed by structured training activities and dissemination of the program. Program development was evaluated descriptively using observable institutional indicators rather than a validated composite measure of program maturity or sustainability. These indicators included robotic platform adoption, annual procedural volume and surgeon participation, changes in clinical indications, and structured educational activities. Terms referring to program development or maturation are therefore used descriptively and do not represent independently validated outcomes.

Robotic surgical platforms

During the study period, three generations of the da Vinci Surgical System (Intuitive Surgical, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) were used: the da Vinci Si platform (2013–2021), the da Vinci Xi platform (2017–2025), and the da Vinci Single-Port (SP) platform (introduced in 2025). Procedures were performed using standard institutional robotic surgical techniques appropriate for each platform. Platform transitions reflected routine institutional adoption of newer robotic technology during the study period.

Surgeon training and program development

The program evolved from a single-surgeon service to a multi-surgeon team during the study period. Newly recruited surgeons completed a structured institutional training pathway before independent credentialing. The training pathway consisted of (1) didactic instruction and system familiarization, (2) simulation-based dry-laboratory training, (3) operative observation of experienced robotic surgeons, (4) wet-laboratory or cadaveric training, and (5) supervised initial clinical cases under the guidance of a certified robotic surgery proctor.

Training activities were aligned with the da Vinci TR200 and TR300 curricula for console surgeons, bedside assistants, and operating room personnel. Program development was further characterized by educational activities, including designation as a robotic surgery observation site, operative observation by visiting surgeons, participation in structured training courses, cadaveric workshops, and regional educational outreach.

Data collection and classification

Program-level data were collected retrospectively from institutional robotic surgery records. Variables included procedure year, robotic platform (Si, Xi, or SP), operating surgeon, and clinical indication.

For longitudinal analysis, the study period was divided into three predefined phases based on major institutional milestones in robotic platform adoption and workforce expansion: the Foundation Phase (2013–2016), the Expansion Phase (2017–2020), and the Established Phase (2021–2025).

Clinical indications were classified into ten mutually exclusive categories: oral cancer, radical tonsillectomy, sleep surgery/obstructive sleep apnea syndrome, malignant tongue base resection, benign tongue base resection, parotidectomy, thyroid surgery, supraglottic laryngectomy, hypopharyngectomy, and other head and neck procedures. Each robotic surgical procedure was assigned to a single category according to its principal clinical indication. When a procedure could potentially meet more than one category, the underlying disease or functional indication took precedence over the procedure type. For example, tongue base resection performed for obstructive sleep apnea was classified as Sleep Surgery/OSAS rather than Benign Tongue Base Resection. Ambiguous classifications were resolved by reviewing the operative indication and principal robotic procedure documented in the medical record. The Other Head & Neck category included procedures for submandibular gland lesions, neck masses, and retropharyngeal, parapharyngeal, and nasopharyngeal lesions that were not captured by the other prespecified categories.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Temporal trends in annual institutional procedural volume and Surgeon 1 annual caseload were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Because Poisson regression demonstrated overdispersion, negative binomial regression with a log link was used as a supplementary analysis to estimate the average multiplicative change in annual institutional procedural volume, expressed as a rate ratio with a 95% confidence interval. The association between program phase and clinical indication was evaluated using Pearson’s chi-square statistic with a Monte Carlo–simulated p value because of sparse expected cell counts. Cramér’s V was reported as the effect-size measure. All tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the participating institution (Approval No. 1140099). The requirement for individual informed consent was waived because of the retrospective study design and the use of de-identified data.

Results

Overall program growth and platform transitions

Over the 13-year study period, the robotic otolaryngology program included 757 procedures. Annual procedural volume increased significantly over time, with a strong positive correlation between calendar year and annual case volume (Spearman’s ρ = 0.923, p < 0.001). In a supplementary negative binomial regression accounting for overdispersion in the annual count data, the estimated incidence rate ratio was 1.215 per calendar year (95% confidence interval, 1.157–1.276; p < 0.001). Program development was characterized by changes in annual case volume that coincided with major platform upgrades and expansion of the surgical workforce (Fig. 1).

Annual institutional robotic surgical volume in otolaryngology–head and neck surgery by platform, 2013–2025

In the initial years, case volumes remained modest, with 10 cases in 2013 and 11 cases in 2014, all performed using the first-generation da Vinci Si platform. Case volumes grew to 26 in 2015 and 21 in 2016, establishing a stable baseline. The introduction of the da Vinci Xi platform in 2017 was associated with an increase in annual case volume to 51 cases, representing a major technological transition. Following a brief plateau in 2018 (40 cases) and 2019 (28 cases), annual case volume increased again in 2020 with 61 cases, coinciding with the early phases of the COVID-19 pandemic.

In 2021, after installation of a second da Vinci Xi platform, annual case volume increased to 83. Program volume peaked in 2022 at 122 cases, the highest annual volume during the study period. High volumes were sustained in 2023 (100 cases) and 2024 (109 cases). In 2025, the program underwent another major technological transition with the acquisition of Taiwan’s first da Vinci Single-Port (SP) surgical system. In its first year of deployment, the da Vinci SP system accounted for 52 of the 95 cases (54.7%), reflecting rapid uptake of the SP platform (Fig. 1).

Workforce expansion and surgeon caseloads

During the study period, the program evolved from a single-surgeon model to a multi-surgeon service (Fig. 2). Surgeon 1 initiated the program and remained the sole operator from 2013 to 2015, performing 100% of the institutional cases. Surgeon 2 joined the program in 2016, followed by Surgeon 3 in 2020, Surgeon 4 in 2021, and Surgeon 5 in 2022.

Fig. 2Annual institutional robotic surgical volume in otolaryngology–head and neck surgery by surgeon, 2013–2025

Annual institutional robotic surgical volume in otolaryngology–head and neck surgery by surgeon, 2013–2025

As additional surgeons joined the program, operative volume became progressively shared. Surgeon 1 performed 531 cases over the 13-year period, representing 70.1% of the total institutional volume, while Surgeons 2–5 collectively performed 226 cases (29.9%) (Fig. 3). Annual case volume increased significantly over time for both Surgeon 1 (Spearman’s ρ = 0.941, p < 0.001) and the combined cohort of Surgeons 2–5 (ρ = 0.892, p < 0.001). The cumulative experience of Surgeon 1 continued to increase after additional surgeons joined the program, while cumulative case volume among Surgeons 2–5 also increased progressively (Fig. 3). These parallel trends suggest that workforce expansion was accompanied by growth in institutional activity rather than a simple redistribution of cases.

During the Foundation Phase (2013–2016), clinical indications were relatively limited, totaling 68 cases. Procedures consisted primarily of radical tonsillectomy (24 cases, 35.3%), benign or localized head and neck lesions grouped as Other Head & Neck (21 cases, 30.9%), and malignant tongue base resection (10 cases, 14.7%). No sleep surgery or oral cancer procedures were performed during this phase.

The Expansion Phase (2017–2020) saw an increase to 180 cases, accompanied by a rise in oral cancer procedures (69 cases, 38.3%), which became the leading indication. Radical tonsillectomies also increased to 43 cases (23.9%). The program performed its first sleep surgery (1 case) during this phase, representing the introduction of sleep surgery into the robotic program.

In the Established Phase (2021–2025), case volume reached 509 cases, representing 67.2% of the overall cohort. This phase was characterized by Sleep Surgery/OSAS (167 cases, 32.8%), which became the most common indication. Oral cancer surgery continued to represent a major indication, adding 130 cases (25.5%), followed closely by radical tonsillectomies at 117 cases (23.0%). The Established Phase also included additional indications such as parotidectomy (21 cases, 4.1%), reflecting expansion of the program to encompass a broader range of head and neck procedures.

These educational activities highlight the program’s educational role beyond its own institution. Key milestones include being designated as the second da Vinci Otolaryngology Observation Site in Taiwan (2023) and the first da Vinci SP Otolaryngology Observation Site in Taiwan (2025). Between 2023 and 2025, the program hosted 25 visiting observers and provided formal da Vinci TR200/300 training to 23 surgeons and team members. Training has been highly multidisciplinary, encompassing surgeons, residents, physician assistants, nurses, and the OR team, with educational activities involving participants from Taiwan, Hong Kong, India, and Thailand.

Discussion

The findings of this 13-year longitudinal program evaluation describe the transition of a robotic otolaryngology program from a single-surgeon service to a collaborative, multi-surgeon program while maintaining institutional surgical volume. Over this study period, the program completed 757 robot-assisted procedures, with annual caseloads increasing from 10 cases in 2013 to a peak of 122 cases in 2022. This expansion coincided with consecutive transitions across three platform generations—da Vinci Si, Xi, and SP—with the single-port platform accounting for 54.7% of all cases in its first year of clinical deployment. Program development was also characterized by a shift from benign tonsillectomies to oncologic, sleep surgery, and parotidectomy procedures. The expansion of the surgical workforce and clinical indications occurred alongside the development of a structured multidisciplinary training pathway and the evolution of the program into a regional observation and training hub hosting domestic and international observers and hands-on workshops.

Technological evolution and platform transitions

The platform transitions observed in this study occurred alongside changes in operational setup and the range of clinical applications within the robotic program. The initial Si and Xi multiport platforms established the programmatic foundation but were associated with spatial limitations and instrument crowding within narrow transoral corridors. In contrast, the integration of the da Vinci SP system in 2025 demonstrated rapid clinical deployment, which is associated with the platform’s single-cannula design and articulating instrument triangulation that reduce external workspace congestion and optimize ergonomic comfort for the surgical team [16, 17]. These technological attributes have expanded the clinical feasibility of TORS to anatomically restricted spaces and benign indications [18]. However, acquiring multiple platforms represents a significant capital investment, making a minimum annual institutional caseload essential to achieving cost-effectiveness and operational sustainability [19]. Geographic and institutional patterns of robotic adoption suggest that regional dissemination of robotic systems can succeed outside high-volume tertiary referral medical centers when structured workflow models are utilized [20]. Additionally, the high visualization and precise dissection capabilities of these newer platforms have been associated with safe transoral approaches to complex deep neck spaces, including retropharyngeal metastases [21, 22].

Workforce development and team-based scaling

The transition from a single-surgeon model to a collaborative team represented an important component of workforce development within our program. In specialized surgical programs, expanding the workforce often risks redistributing existing caseloads, thereby diluting individual experience. However, our data show that the consecutive addition of Surgeons 2–5 was associated with a progressive increase in cumulative institutional volume, suggesting that workforce expansion was accompanied by growth in institutional surgical activity rather than simple case redistribution. Furthermore, as clinical indications expanded during this transition to include functional indications, standardized protocols for single-port tongue base resection for obstructive sleep apnea were implemented [23, 24], with structured perioperative swallowing care to optimize nasogastric tube duration [25]. This multi-surgeon, multi-indication program development was accompanied by a structured institutional training pathway and clinical governance frameworks that guided credentialing and surgical practice [13].

Longitudinal evolution of clinical indications

The distribution of clinical indications changed substantially across the three predefined program phases. The Foundation Phase (2013–2016) was characterized by a limited scope of procedures, dominated primarily by benign or localized head and neck pathologies and radical tonsillectomy. The subsequent Expansion Phase (2017–2020) demonstrated a diversification of the clinical case mix, driven by an increase in oral cancer procedures, which became the leading indication. In the Established Phase (2021–2025), the program achieved both higher case volumes and greater diversification, with Sleep Surgery/OSAS becoming the most common indication. These temporal changes should not be attributed solely to robotic platform transitions or program development. Concurrent changes in the surgical workforce, referral patterns, institutional resources, and evolving clinical practice may also have influenced the observed case mix. During this period, standardized protocols for single-port tongue base resection were introduced, and prior studies from our institution and others have reported favorable feasibility and sleep-related outcomes for standalone TORS and combination sleep surgery [23, 24, 26]. Concurrently, the program maintained a strong oncological focus, performing 199 cumulative oral cancer procedures, including complex cases presenting with pre-existing trismus. Our prior findings indicate that while TORS remains technically feasible under limited mouth opening and achieves comparable short-term surgical margins, pre-existing trismus is an independent predictor of reduced long-term survival, necessitating careful preoperative risk stratification [7, 8]. The introduction of additional indications in the Established Phase, including parotidectomy and thyroidectomy, reflects the expanding application of robotic systems to transcervical and retroauricular approaches [27–30]. Furthermore, the Established Phase included advanced transoral resections for laryngeal and hypopharyngeal carcinomas, in which single-port systems offer comparable or improved access, visualization, and articulation compared with multiport systems [4, 5, 31, 32]. Additional procedures categorized as Other Head & Neck further extended the anatomical range of robotic applications to submandibular gland lesions, neck masses, and retropharyngeal, parapharyngeal, and nasopharyngeal lesions. Robotic access to these deep and anatomically constrained spaces has also been described in the literature, including transoral robotic retropharyngeal lymph node dissection and robotic approaches to parapharyngeal lesions [21, 22, 33, 34]. However, this clinical diversification introduces distinct perioperative challenges, including the management of acute postoperative pain [35], transient swallowing dysfunction requiring intensive rehabilitation [36], and the risk of postoperative hemorrhage [37], underscoring the need for standardized patient selection, salvage planning, and multidisciplinary clinical support.

Educational infrastructure and program evolution

Establishing a structured educational infrastructure is important for addressing learning curve barriers and supporting long-term program development. Traditional adoption of advanced surgical technologies, such as robotic surgery, has been hindered by limited exposure during residency training, with survey data indicating that a majority of residents report insufficient opportunities to actively participate in robotic procedures [14]. To address this training gap, our program established a structured, step-wise pathway aligned with the da Vinci TR200 and TR300 training curricula, emphasizing multidisciplinary team education and objective assessment of console tasks. The formal learning process for transoral robotic surgery is known to be substantial, requiring structured mentorship to maintain clinical safety and surgical efficiency [12, 38]. The educational activities of our program included designation as Taiwan’s second multiport observation site in 2023 and its first single-port observation site in 2025, providing a platform for domestic and international observation and training. Between 2023 and 2025, the program hosted 25 visiting observers and participated in a hands-on cadaveric TORS workshop involving domestic and international trainees. Furthermore, as the clinical indications of our program expand to encompass transcervical and retroauricular robotic neck dissections, the training infrastructure must continually adapt to support the entire operating room team, including bedside assistants, nursing personnel, and anesthesia teams, under unified clinical governance frameworks [13]. These activities document the scope and dissemination of the educational program; however, trainee competence, learning-curve progression, and subsequent independent robotic practice were not evaluated in the present study. The integration of complementary innovations, such as exoscopic-assisted visualization, may further optimize team ergonomics, physical freedom, and workflow efficiency in smaller surgical suites [39].

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, its retrospective, single-center design introduces inherent selection biases, and patient enrollment reflected real-world clinical referrals rather than controlled sampling. Second, although our operational data demonstrate maintenance of institutional procedural volume across 757 procedures, we did not include a concurrent non-robotic control group to directly compare long-term oncologic survival, recurrence rates, or functional swallow recovery with those of open surgery or primary chemoradiotherapy. Prior comparative studies from other centers and ours have shown that TORS achieves equivalent or superior margins and functional outcomes compared with non-robotic approaches [6, 40, 41], but our findings remain largely descriptive. Third, the present program-level analysis did not systematically evaluate patient-level safety or effectiveness outcomes, including perioperative complications, conversion, postoperative hemorrhage, reoperation, length of stay, surgical margin status, functional outcomes, recurrence, or survival. Accordingly, the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence of comparative clinical safety or effectiveness. Clinical outcomes for selected indications and platform-specific cohorts have been reported separately in our previous studies [6–11]. Finally, our data represent the experience of a single regional teaching hospital in Taiwan; therefore, these findings may not be fully generalizable to medical institutions with different referral patterns or resource allocations. This highlights the value of establishing multicenter, prospective registries to standardize reporting of TORS programmatic outcomes [15].

Conclusion

The 13-year evolution of our robotic otolaryngology program demonstrates longitudinal growth across successive platform transitions (Si to Xi to SP), expansion of the surgical workforce, and diversification of clinical indications. By transitioning from a single-surgeon service to a collaborative five-surgeon team and establishing structured educational frameworks, the program evolved from an institutional clinical center into a regional observation and training hub. This long-term institutional experience illustrates one potential developmental pathway for mid-volume regional teaching hospital with comparable resources seeking to establish and expand robotic surgery programs.
