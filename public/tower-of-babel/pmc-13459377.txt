Natural history of aortic valve disease after rheumatic mitral valve surgery: implications for concomitant aortic intervention
Authors: Hamideh Khesali, Sara Shemshadi, Pourya Kanani, Fateme Yousefimoghaddam, Yaghoob Bagheri, Soheila Salari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRheumatic heart disease (RHD) frequently affects both mitral and aortic valves. While mitral valve replacement (MVR) is often prioritized, the natural history of coexisting aortic valve disease particularly when mild or moderate is less well understood.ObjectiveThe objective of this study is to assess the progression of aortic stenosis (AS) and aortic insufficiency (AI) in patients undergoing isolated MVR for rheumatic mitral disease.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study involving 403 adult patients with rheumatic mitral valve disease who underwent isolated MVR at Rajaie Cardiovascular Medical and Research Center between 2010 and 2022. Patients with a history of aortic valve replacement or early postoperative mortality (defined as death within 30 days of surgery) were excluded. Serial transthoracic echocardiography was performed at baseline, 3 months postoperatively, and annually thereafter, with a mean follow-up duration of 78 months. We evaluated baseline and longitudinal changes in the severity of aortic stenosis (AS) and aortic insufficiency (AI), as well as alterations in left ventricular function and pulmonary artery pressures over time. The severity of aortic valve disease was classified according to the American Heart Association (AHA) 2020 and European Society of Cardiology (ESC) 2025 guidelines.ResultsAt baseline, 9.2% had AS (6.2% mild, 3.0% moderate) and 67% had AI (57.6% mild, 9.4% moderate). During our follow up among patients with mild AS, we observed sever AS is 20% progressing to severe AS, compared with 41.7% progressing to moderate AS. For AI, we observed 3.0% progression to severe AI for patients with mild baseline disease and is 7.8% for patients with moderate baseline disease. Baseline aortic valve lesion severity independently predicted progression (HR 4.2, 95% CI 1.9–9.1, p < 0.001). No other clinical or echocardiographic parameters were significant. Survival analysis showed higher cumulative progression rates among those with baseline lesions (p < 0.001).ConclusionsBaseline aortic valve involvement predicts subsequent deterioration after MVR. Routine prophylactic AVR is not supported for mild AI, but moderate AS warrants closer follow-up and individualized surgical consideration. Larger, prospective studies are needed to refine risk prediction for concomitant aortic intervention.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Rheumatic heart disease; Mitral valve replacement; Aortic valve disease; Disease progression; Aortic Stenosis, Aortic Insufficiency

Introduction

RHD continues to impose a major global health burden, particularly in developing countries where it remains a leading cause of valvular pathology. Although the mitral valve is most commonly affected, concomitant aortic valve involvement is frequent and may present across a spectrum of severity. In contemporary management, percutaneous transvenous mitral commissurotomy (PTMC) is the preferred first-line treatment for suitable cases of rheumatic mitral stenosis. Nevertheless, surgical MVR remains indicated in patients with complex or mixed valvular lesions, failed PTMC, or contraindications to percutaneous intervention. Consequently, the population undergoing MVR represents a highly selected subgroup with advanced rheumatic disease and complex multivalvular pathology [1–3].

Coexistent rheumatic aortic valve disease is common, even when less severe at the time of diagnosis. This occurs in approximately 20–30% of all RHD cases [4]. Among patients with rheumatic mitral involvement, aortic regurgitation is the most frequent concomitant lesion, reported in up to 44–45% of cases [5], while combined aortic stenosis is less common, affecting approximately 8–11% of patients with significant aortic stenosis [6].

Studies have shown that dual-valve involvement in RHD is frequently observed and should be considered part of the natural disease spectrum rather than an exception [7]. Despite this, surgical decision-making has traditionally focused on addressing the dominant lesion typically the mitral valve while deferring intervention for concurrent mild or moderate aortic pathology [8]. This approach aligns with established clinical guidelines from the AHA and the American College of Cardiology (ACC), which recommend surgical aortic valve replacement (AVR) in patients with severe AS or AI, (Class I indication) and consider intervention reasonable for those with moderate disease (Class IIa indication) [9]. However, these recommendations provide limited guidance for patients with mild or borderline aortic valve disease, leading many surgeons to adopt a conservative management strategy to minimize operative risk and prosthesis-related complications.

Recent studies have challenged this conservative approach, suggesting that rheumatic aortic lesions may progress following MVR, potentially due to altered left ventricular loading conditions or ongoing rheumatic valvulitis [10, 11]. The potential for accelerated progression raises important questions about whether early or concomitant surgical intervention on the aortic valve should be considered at the time of MVR. A clearer understanding of the natural history of rheumatic aortic valve disease in this setting is essential to guide evidence-based surgical decision-making. Accordingly, the present study aimed to examine the long-term evolution of AS and AI following isolated MVR in patients with RHD, identify factors associated with progression, and provide data to inform the timing and necessity of concomitant aortic intervention.

Methods

Study population

We retrospectively reviewed all adult patients (> 18 years) who underwent isolated MVR for rheumatic mitral valve disease at Rajaie Cardiovascular Medical and Research Center (Tehran, Iran) between January 2010 and December 2022. Exclusion criteria included concomitant AVR or other major cardiac surgery, previous AVR, bicuspid aortic valve, or death within 30 days postoperatively. Patients who had undergone PTMC were not included in this surgical cohort but are acknowledged as a relevant comparator group in the discussion.

Of 414 identified patients, 11 died within 30 days and were excluded. Early postoperative mortality was primarily related to perioperative complications. Among these 11 patients who died within 30 days postoperatively, causes of death included low cardiac output syndrome (n = 6), refractory arrhythmias (n = 2), sepsis (n = 2). This yielded 403 patients for analysis. All procedures were performed via median sternotomy with cardiopulmonary bypass. Tricuspid valve repair or replacement was performed when indicated.

Echocardiographic assessment

Transthoracic echocardiography (Philips EPIQ or GE Vivid systems) was performed preoperatively, 3 months postoperatively, and annually thereafter. Echocardiographic assessment was employed throughout the study to ensure consistency in measurements. Quantitative thresholds rather than subjective assessment were applied to ensure uniform grading and classification.

The severity of aortic stenosis (AS) was graded according to the 2025 ESC/EACTS and 2020 AHA/ACC guidelines using both AVA and MG [9]. Severity categories were defined using the AND criterion, requiring both parameters to fall within the specified range: Mild AS(AVA > 1.5 cm² AND MG < 20 mmHg), Moderate AS (AVA 1.0–1.5 cm² AND MG 20–40 mmHg), Severe AS(AVA < 1.0 cm² AND MG > 40 mmHg).

In patients with discordant AVA and MG measurements (e.g., AVA indicating moderate severity but MG indicating mild severity), the final classification was determined by consensus between two experienced echocardiographers, with priority given to MG when left ventricular ejection fraction was preserved (≥ 50%). The low-flow, low-gradient phenotype (AVA < 1.0 cm² with MG < 40 mmHg and stroke volume index ≤ 35 mL/m²) was classified as severe AS only after dobutamine stress echocardiography confirmed contractile reserve, consistent with contemporary guidelines. However, this phenotype was infrequent in our cohort. it is acknowledged as a limitation of the present analysis.

Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was calculated using the modified Simpson’s biplane method. Normal LVEF was defined as ≥ 50%, consistent with the 2020 AHA/ACC and 2025 ESC/EACTS valvular heart disease guidelines [6, 9]. Preserved systolic function was defined as LVEF ≥ 50%, while mild dysfunction was defined as LVEF 40–49%, moderate as 30–39%, and severe as < 30%.

AI severity was categorized as mild, moderate, or severe based on integrated quantitative criteria (vena contracta width, regurgitant volume, and Effective Regurgitant Orifice Area) to ensure consistency across serial evaluations [9, 11, 12].

Progression was defined as an increase of ≥ 1 severity grade or reaching severe AS (AVA < 1.0 cm², MG > 40 mmHg) or severe AI (EROA ≥ 0.30 cm² or regurgitant volume ≥ 60 mL).

In cases where full quantitative assessment (Effective Regurgitant Orifice Area, regurgitant volume) was not feasible, an integrated semi-quantitative approach incorporating vena contracta width and Doppler parameters was applied in accordance with guideline recommendations.

Follow-up and outcomes

The primary endpoint was progression to severe AS or severe AI. Secondary outcomes included all-cause mortality and reoperation.Follow-up duration was reported as median (IQR) to account for variability. To account for unequal time intervals between echocardiographic examinations, a generalized estimating equation (GEE) model was used in sensitivity analyses to correct for within-patient correlation and repeated measures. Residual variability in follow-up timing is acknowledged as a potential source of bias.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD or median (IQR), and categorical variables as number (percentage). Between-group comparisons used t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests as appropriate. Time-dependent progression of AS and AI was assessed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves and Cox proportional hazards regression models, with baseline aortic valve lesion severity included as the primary covariate.

To account for inter-patient variability in echocardiographic follow-up intervals, a GEE approach was considered in sensitivity analyses. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (SPSS v26, IBM).

Because the number of progression events was modest particularly for AI the power to detect additional independent predictors was limited. This constraint is further discussed in the Limitations section.

Results

Baseline clinical and echocardiographic characteristics

A total of 403 patients were included in the study (mean age 52 ± 10 years; 80% female) with the median follow-up duration was 78 (54 ± 102) months. The predominant surgical indication was severe rheumatic mitral stenosis (85.4%), while 14.6% underwent surgery for predominant mitral regurgitation. Concomitant tricuspid valve repair or replacement was performed in 29.3% of cases. None of the patients had a bicuspid aortic valve morphology. Mechanical and bioprosthetic valves were both used for mitral valve replacement; bioprosthetic valves were preferentially implanted in selected patients based on age and clinical considerations (e.g., all patients > 70 years received bioprostheses) .Baseline left ventricular systolic function (LVEF ≥ 50%) was present in 42% of patients, with a mean LVEF of 48 ± 8%. 45% had mild dysfunction (40–49%), and 13% had moderate-to-severe dysfunction (< 40%).

Baseline aortic stenosis (AS) was present in 9.2% (6.2% mild, 3.0% moderate), and aortic insufficiency (AI) in 67.0% (57.6% mild, 9.4% moderate). Detailed baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Echocardiographic changes after mitral valve replacement

Postoperative echocardiographic evaluation revealed a modest but statistically significant increase in mean aortic valve gradient (7.33 ± 2.1 → 8.42 ± 2.3 mmHg, p = 0.01) and a corresponding reduction in systolic pulmonary artery pressure (47.18 ± 10.2 → 39.17 ± 8.7 mmHg, p < 0.001). A slight decrease in LVEF was observed (48 ± 8 → 45 ± 8.4%, p = 0.001), without clinically significant ventricular dilatation. Left atrial and ventricular dimensions remained stable throughout follow-up (Table 2).

Progression of aortic valve disease

In the aortic stenosis (AS) cohort, 366 patients had no AS at baseline. During follow-up, 18 patients (4.9%) developed AS, including 15 cases of mild AS and 3 cases progressing directly to severe AS. Among patients with mild AS at baseline (n = 25), 11 patients (44%) exhibited progression, including 5 progressing to moderate AS and 6 to severe AS. Of the 12 patients with moderate AS at baseline, 5 (41.7%) progressed to severe AS during follow-up.

Regarding aortic insufficiency (AI), among the 133 patients without AI at baseline, 30 patients (22.6%) developed AI during follow-up, including 23 mild and 7 severe cases. In the mild AI group (n = 232), 39 patients (16.8%) progressed, including 32 to moderate and 7 to severe AI. Among the 38 patients with moderate AI at baseline, 3 patients (7.9%) progressed to severe AI.

Among patients with mixed aortic valve disease, those with at least one moderate lesion at baseline demonstrated higher observed rates of progression compared with those with only mild lesions. Specifically, patients with moderate AS and mild AI showed the highest progression rate (75.0%, n = 3/4), whereas those with mild AS and mild AI had lower rates (35.3%, n = 6/17). Overall, patients with mixed aortic valve disease exhibited higher observed progression (78.0%, n = 19/25) compared with those with isolated AI (21.3%, n = 26/122) or isolated AS (18.5%, n = 5/27). These findings should be interpreted with caution given the small subgroup sizes (Tables 3 and 4).

Discussion

The progression of aortic valve disease in patients with RHD following isolated mitral valve surgery represents a critical yet understudied aspect of long-term valvular management, with profound implications for surgical decision-making and patient outcomes [13]. While mitral valve interventions remain the cornerstone of management in the majority of RHD cases due to its predominant involvement and symptomatic burden, the insidious evolution of concomitant aortic lesions—often mild or moderate at initial presentation—can precipitate recurrent heart failure, arrhythmias, or the need for reintervention years later [14].

We evaluated the progression of aortic valve (AV) disease following isolated mitral valve replacement (MVR) to assess the necessity of concomitant prophylactic intervention. Early postoperative data revealed hemodynamic improvements and a significant decrease in systolic pulmonary artery pressure. Although minor increases in transaortic gradients and a modest decline in LVEF were observed—likely reflecting altered flow dynamics and subclinical ventricular dysfunction, respectively—ventricular dimensions remained stable. Also, mild AV lesions generally showed a benign initial clinical course.

Long-term analysis identified baseline valvular pathology as the dominant determinant of disease progression, overshadowing demographic variables such as age, sex, and preoperative LVEF. Specifically, the presence of preexisting aortic stenosis (AS) or insufficiency (AI) increased the hazard of deterioration. This risk was notably highlighted in patients with mixed valve disease; the coexistence of stenotic and regurgitant pathology appears to create a mechanically adverse environment that accelerates degeneration relative to single-valve lesions. These results reflect the compounded hemodynamic stress of dual lesions, which impose conflicting mechanical demands. This conflict outpaces the valve’s reparative capacity. Furthermore, initial disease burden served as an independent driver, with moderate lesions exhibiting a markedly steeper trajectory of deterioration compared to mild disease. Although a trend toward reduced survival was noted in patients with advanced baseline lesions, this association did not reach statistical significance. Consequently, the heightened risk associated with mixed and moderate-grade pathology suggests that these subgroups warrant closer surveillance and potentially more aggressive prophylactic intervention strategies.

Our findings on the progression of AV lesions following MVR for RHD resonate with multiple studies examining the natural history of untreated or mildly affected AV disease in similar patient populations, while also revealing points of divergence that highlight the complexity of rheumatic valvulopathy. Namboodiri et al. reported that patients with mild or no AV disease at mitral intervention rarely progressed to severe AV dysfunction, requiring AV surgery in only 2% over a 9.3-year mean follow. This was consistent with our low progression rates in mild cases [15]. Similarly, Kim et al. observed uncommon severe AV dysfunction (0.33%/patient-year) in 1155 patients post-rheumatic MVR, with progression more frequent in those with baseline mild or greater involvement, mirroring our regression results where baseline severity was the strongest predictor [16].

Hwang et al. found that untreated mild AV disease progressed to more severe grades in 18.4% of patients over 11.8 years, with AS patients showing higher progression rates than those with AR—paralleling our subgroup trends where AS conferred higher risk of progression [10]. Kim et al. in a 20-year cohort noted 12% progression to significant AV disease in mild AVD patients, emphasizing faster AS advancement, which supports our observation of baseline AS as a dominant risk factor [17]. Mishra et al. and Panda et al. in double valve replacement cohorts reported favorable long-term survival (84.4% and 78% at 10 years, respectively) without focusing on untreated AV progression, but their low reoperation rates for AV issues align with our survival trends [18, 19].

Ha et al. advocated prophylactic AVR for mild-to-moderate AVD during MVR, citing 1.45% reoperation over 8.2 years. This rate was higher than our cohort’s implied low rates for mild cases [20]. Consistent with the literature, Kim et al. observed that rheumatic aortic regurgitation appears to be more stable than stenosis following mitral intervention. While 44% of their patients with baseline Mild AS showed some echocardiographic progression (mostly to moderate), those with aortic regurgitation showed minimal change, with no instances of progression to severe AR. This underscores that concomitant repair/replacement for rheumatic AR should be reserved strictly for severe cases [21]. Luo et al.‘s meta-analysis reported a 3.3-fold progression risk within 0–5 years of follow up. This risk escalated to 6.42 beyond 5 years of follow-up, with AS showing 8.37-fold higher risk than AR—partially opposing our interpretation by emphasizing early AS vigilance, though their landmark analysis supports our observed baseline severity influence [22].

These comparisons highlight consistency in slow progression for mild AR but underscore AS as a higher-risk subset, with discrepancies likely attributable to follow-up duration and cohort demographics.

Clinical and theoretical implications

From a mechanistic standpoint, one potential explanation underlying accelerated aortic stenosis progression after MVR relates to postoperative increases in forward stroke volume following relief of mitral obstruction or regurgitation. This hemodynamic shift may unmask latent aortic stenosis or increase transvalvular flow, thereby augmenting leaflet stress and accelerating calcific and fibrotic remodeling.

Additionally, persistent rheumatic valvulitis may contribute to progressive leaflet thickening independent of hemodynamic normalization. While stroke volume index was not systematically available in our dataset, prior studies suggest that increases in forward flow after mitral intervention can significantly influence aortic valve gradients and apparent disease progression [23, 24].

We were unable to directly compare patients undergoing MVR versus conservative management, nor systematically assess stroke volume index, limiting mechanistic interpretation of flow-dependent AS progression.

Importantly, our results suggest that mild AV lesions, particularly AR, can often be observed post-MVR without aggressive intervention, given low progression rates. However, baseline moderate involvement warrants closer monitoring, as Cox models indicate a 3–4-fold risk of progression. The post-MVR AV gradient increase implies that mitral relief may unmask latent AV pathology, advocating routine echocardiographic follow-up at 1-year intervals initially, then biennially. Survival trends by baseline lesion imply that early identification could mitigate long-term mortality, supporting integrated rheumatic management protocols with secondary prophylaxis to diminish progression [25].

Theoretically, these findings reinforce rheumatic valvopathy as a progressive process, with baseline severity reflecting cumulative damage. The lack of LVEF or age influence suggests intrinsic valvular fibrosis drives progression, independent of ventricular remodeling. These findings challenge the assumptions that hemodynamic normalization halts rheumatic advancement. This may inform models of multivalvular interplay, where mitral correction alters AV stress without reversing fibrosis.

Strength and limitations

The strengths of this study lie in its large cohort of patients with rheumatic heart disease, enabling detailed echocardiographic comparisons and robust time-to-event analyses, including Kaplan–Meier curves and Cox proportional hazards models, which account for progression dynamics over time. Additionally, the inclusion of both aortic stenosis (AS) and aortic insufficiency (AI) lesions, stratified by mild and moderate severity classifications, facilitated granular subgroup analyses that elucidated differential progression patterns between stenotic and regurgitant subtypes. The focus on a homogeneous rheumatic etiology offers valuable, disease-specific insights into multivalvular progression, filling a gap in the literature dominated by degenerative valve cohorts. Comprehensive adjustment for confounders such as age, sex, and baseline LVEF further bolsters internal validity.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. Another point that stood out in this study is the predominance of female patients (80%), unlike in many cardiovascular research studies where women are underrepresented. Although this likely reflects the epidemiology of rheumatic mitral stenosis, which disproportionately affects women, importantly, this female predominance reflects a real-world disease pattern rather than selection bias, and therefore enhances the external validity of the study within populations where rheumatic heart disease remains prevalent. At the same time, we acknowledge that this distribution may limit generalizability to non-rheumatic valvular populations or cohorts with more balanced sex representation, such as degenerative valve disease seen in high-income settings.

The retrospective design introduces potential selection bias and unmeasured confounding, including active rheumatic carditis, adherence to secondary prophylaxis, or socioeconomic factors influencing access to follow-up care. Also, due to the retrospective nature of the study, time-to-event analysis for mixed aortic valve disease could not be performed due to lack of precise event timing; therefore, this subgroup is described using descriptive statistics only.

We acknowledge that key hemodynamic variables such as stroke volume index (SVi) and serial aortic valve area (AVA) measurements were not consistently available in our dataset. Given that transvalvular gradients are flow-dependent, this limitation may have influenced the assessment of AS progression, potentially leading to overestimation of progression when based primarily on gradient changes, thus limiting interpretation of flow-dependent changes in AS severity. Additionally, paradoxical low-flow AS was infrequent (approximately 1%), limiting subgroup analysis. Exact percentages of mechanical versus bioprosthetic valves were not available from our retrospective records, although bioprosthetic valves were implanted in selected patients based on age and clinical considerations (e.g., all patients > 70 years received bioprostheses). The absence of detailed prosthesis-type data precludes analysis of potential differences in aortic valve progression by valve subtype. The inability to compare patients undergoing MVR with those managed conservatively precludes definitive conclusions regarding causality between MVR and AV progression. The relatively short median follow-up (approximately 4 years for some parameters) likely underestimates late AV progression, as evidenced by meta-analyses showing escalating risks beyond 5 years [22]. As a single-center study in a specific demographic, generalizability is limited, particularly to low-resource settings with higher rheumatic burden or diverse ethnic populations. Finally, competing risks such as mortality were not formally analyzed, potentially overestimating progression-free survival.

Future directions

Prospective multicenter trials are essential to validate progression predictors and compare conservative versus proactive AVR strategies in mild AS. Incorporating quantitative imaging (e.g., vena contracta) and biomarkers of rheumatic activity could refine risk stratification. Long-term studies in diverse populations would address generalizability. Finally, exploring minimally invasive or transcatheter options for delayed AV intervention may optimize outcomes for progressing cases.

Conclusion

In patients undergoing MVR for rheumatic disease, baseline AV lesion severity drives long-term progression, with AS posing greater risk than AI. While mild lesions generally follow a benign course, moderate involvement signals need for vigilant monitoring. These insights advocate tailored follow-up to balance intervention risks, emphasizing that untreated mild AV disease rarely necessitates reoperation in rheumatic contexts.
