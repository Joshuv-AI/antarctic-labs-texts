Wearable sensing for badminton stroke recognition with one-dimensional convolutional neural network
Authors: Guohan Jin, Xin LI
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Motivated by the need to improve the performance of badminton players, various motion monitoring systems have been developed to assist coaches in badminton technique instruction. While traditional video or optical methods are limited to fixed scenarios and require tedious postprocessing, IMUs, with their compact size and lightweight design, offer a portable solution for badminton motion monitoring. This study develops a wearable sensing network consisting of two IMUs to capture wrist motions during badminton strokes. The measurements are fed into a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D-CNN) to classify six stroke actions and fifteen badminton movement trajectories. The network includes two convolutional layers with 8 and 16 filters (3 × 1 kernel size), followed by batch normalization and ReLU activation. It also features a max-pooling layer, a fully connected layer, and a Softmax classifier. The network was trained with cross-entropy loss and the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 0.005, batch size = 64 iterating over 200 epochs). Experiments were conducted with six national-level badminton athletes to collect motion data for network training and prediction and stratified 5-fold cross-validation was employed to divide the training and testing datasets, ensuring the stability of model evaluation and the reliability of the results. Feature differences between the patterns were intuitively displayed using heatmaps and t-SNE approaches. The results show that the proposed method achieves classification accuracies of 97.16 ± 0.6% for six stroke actions and 86.07 ± 1.1% for fifteen badminton trajectories, outperforming traditional machine learning methods such as KNN (94.94% / 78.71%), SVM (94.32% / 75.06%), and DTA (92.81% / 72.93%). By combining a simple sensor configuration (two sensors) with a lightweight 1D-CNN, this study achieves accurate badminton motion monitoring, which is expected to contribute to sports training and enhance athletes’ skills.

Introduction

Badminton stroke is a key motion pattern that involves coordinated bone and muscle activity, enabling athletes to perform effectively in the game. It also plays a crucial role in achieving success in high-level badminton competitions1. Although many methods have been proposed for recognizing different stroke actions, real-world training and competition environments often introduce significant disturbances. Therefore, most existing recognition techniques still rely heavily on video analysis. Using a single sensor typically limits the data to one variable, such as acceleration, making it insufficient to fully capture the biomechanical complexity of human motion2,3.This study proposes a multi-sensor data acquisition and synchronization method. For experimental validation, we use a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D-CNN) to recognize six types of badminton stroke actions (Fig. 1) and fifteen distinct shuttle trajectories (Fig. 2). In recent years, the rapid development of Internet of Things (IoT)-based smart healthcare systems has significantly driven the growing demand for wearable devices4. As a key component of such systems, wearable IoT (WIoT) devices generate massive amounts of individual health data. Various types of sensors have been widely applied in human motion monitoring, providing more accurate and reliable medical information5,6. These technologies demonstrate great potential in fields such as fall detection7, finger motion monitoring, early diagnosis of Parkinson’s disease8, and smart clothing9. In particular, they play a crucial role in the health management of the elderly population. Moreover, similar motion recognition technologies can be applied to sports, where precise action analysis can significantly improve athletic performance. Inspired by previous studies, this work further investigates the detailed recognition and analysis of badminton stroke actions and their corresponding trajectories. The accurate recognition of six fundamental badminton stroke actions—Forehand Clear, Forehand Drop, Forehand Smash, Forehand Lift, Forehand Net Tumble, and Forehand Cross-Net Shot—is essential for achieving optimal competitive performance and enhancing shot quality, particularly among lower-level players. These six stroke types serve as the foundation for generating fifteen distinct shuttlecock trajectories. Each trajectory differs in terms of angle, speed, and landing position, which are influenced by how the stroke is executed and the player’s tactical intent during the match. Consequently, the ability to recognize both the executed stroke and the resulting trajectory plays a pivotal role in developing athletes’ tactical awareness and playing styles10. Specifically, the recognition of stroke types and trajectories is crucial for improving the accuracy of shuttlecock landing spots after the shot and for reducing the likelihood of unforced errors. Thus, accurately predicting changes in stroke actions has a key effect on improving athletes’ overall competitive abilities. However, there are significant differences in the motion biomechanics of the human body when participants perform different badminton stroke actions. Specifically, the dynamic patterns of the three actions—clear, smash, and drop—differ. Additionally, when playing badminton, athletes experience eccentric muscle contractions11. Excessive eccentric contractions may lead to damage of the muscle cell membranes, triggering an inflammatory response that results in a decline in muscle function12. By improving stroke technique, athletes can enhance the efficiency of the proximal-to-distal joint kinetic chain, thereby effectively reducing joint load and minimizing the risk of injuries caused by excessive eccentric contractions13. Therefore, during daily training, participants should optimize their stroke actions based on correct technical strategies to prevent injury. In swimming training, activated muscle patterns are critical for enhancing performance, and similarly, in badminton training, activating key muscle groups is also important for improving athletic performance14. Similar to monitoring in swimming15,16, the real-time feedback system can help badminton athletes acquire an efficient and feasible method to correct mistakes in different stroke actions, which rely on the distinct biomechanical characteristics of various stroke actions. Recognition of badminton stroke actions has evolved through three stages: manual visual observation, wearable device data analysis, and combining wearable devices with artificial intelligence technology17,18. The high cost of traditional video analysis and sensor data analysis makes it difficult to apply to the rapidly changing environment of badminton competitions and individual training. Inertial measurement units (IMUs), characterized by high portability19,20, provide a wearable approach for monitoring limb joint motion to predict badminton stroke actions and stroke trajectories based on joint angle changes during training. Yu et al. proposed a simple dual-sensor setup capable of accurately identifying gait phases during unrhythmic activities such as walking, stair ascending, and descending, and distinguishing these movements from standing still21, demonstrating that IMUs can be used for complex dynamic motion segmentation. However, during long-term use, IMUs experience data drift, leading to a decline in data accuracy22. Recently, studies have explored the combination of Kalman filtering and sensors, enhancing the stability of data during dynamic motion23.

Recently, as the Internet of Things (IoT) has developed, the role of data integration has been increasingly recognized24. Beniczky et al. have shown that the fusion of wearable physiological signals with traditional EEG data within machine learning frameworks, can significantly enhance seizure prediction accuracy25.In addition, the effectiveness of swimming posture recognition26, gesture recognition27,28, and clinical rehabilitation has been validated through integration of machine learning techniques. Specifically, in the medical field, the use of a wearable device that combines the microcontroller and IMUs for knee joint angle recognition has reduced treatment costs for patients29. The use of neural networks and deep learning is not limited to image recognition; it can also be widely applied in the field of sport30,31. As of now, no study has proposed multi-sensor data acquisition and synchronization for different badminton stroke actions and stroke trajectories. Although video analysis is widely used, relevant research in this area is still scarce. Acceleration induced by changes in skeletal joint angles was detected by a single sensor for the recognition of the smash shot during the final phase of the badminton stroke10. However, human movements are accomplished through the coordination of multiple bones and skeletal muscles, and a single sensor has limitations in capturing the collaborative work of multiple joints; therefore, the coordinated operation of multiple sensors enables the capture of joint movements across multiple joints during a badminton stroke32, allowing for the detection of more subtle joint angle variations. At the same time, it is necessary to avoid the excessive computational load on the computer caused by the using too many sensors. Specifically, in a multi-IMU pattern, the spatial positions and motion trajectories of multiple joints can be effectively captured throughout dynamic movements33. Human limb motion is generated by the contraction of skeletal muscles, which pull on tendons connected to bones, resulting in coordinated joint movement. Therefore, utilizing IMUs to monitor joint rotations during the execution of a badminton stroke provides a viable and practical method for capturing kinematic data. Based on the above, this study integrates the ESP32 microcontroller(Espressif Systems 32-bit)with IMUs (Inertial Measurement Unit) sensors to recognize different badminton stroke types using a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D-CNN). Considering that various badminton strategies are composed of different shot trajectories; this work further investigates shot trajectory classification based on stroke recognition. State of machine learning in sports

Juyi Lin et al.34 used IMUs in combination with a voiceprint extraction module to accurately classify six hitting actions from ten athletes. The median accuracy rates after 10-fold cross-validation for models using four methods—Random Forest (RF), SMO with a polynomial kernel, SMO with an RBF kernel, and Naive Bayes—were 78%, 84%, 80%, and 77%, respectively. In comparison, our study employs two IMUs along with a deep learning approach to recognize fifteen different stroke actions across various trajectories performed by players. Daniel Peralta35 utilized CNNs based on acceleration data from IMUs to classify seven types of forehand strokes and two states in five amateur athletes. The classification accuracy for the five athletes was 96.73%, 89.08%, 93.02%,90.53%, and 95.63%, with an average accuracy of 93.00%. When using both acceleration and gyroscope data, the average accuracy increased to 95.91%. Tim Steels and his team3 placed IMUs on the wrist, upper arm, and racket handle, using acceleration data from these locations to classify seven types of stroke actions using CNN. When using only accelerometer data at a sampling frequency of 50 Hz, the accuracy was 86%. By combining accelerometer and gyroscope data, the accuracy improved to 99%, with weighted accuracy rates of 93% and 96% for the wrist and upper arm, respectively. This is just slightly the 98% accuracy achieved with sensors mounted on the racket. Indrajeet Ghosh et al.19 used data from IMUs on four body parts and applied four machine learning methods to identify strokes and lower limb movements of athletes at different levels. Based on the recognition of 12 types of strokes, they designed an error learning index module, which calculated the error between the ideal posture of professional athletes and that of others and used this error as a scoring index for competition. In our study, we controlled two IMUs using the ESP32, which, while sacrificing some portability, significantly improved the accuracy of the data in the time domain and reduced the adverse effects of subsequent data interpolation. Ben Van Herbruggen et al.18 have made notable progress in the field of badminton skill and tactical recognition. In their study, they utilized two IMUs and an ultra-wideband (UWB) positioning system, allowing real-time tracking of players’ positions during matches and recognition of various shot types. By using CNN to classify 13 different stroke actions, the team achieved an accuracy rate of 85% with only IMUs data. Combining it with UWB positional data, the accuracy of shot classification increased to 88.99%. Additionally, they conducted a classification study on the tactics used by players during matches. When relying solely on UWB data for four tactical categories, the accuracy was relatively low at only 53.5%. However, by integrating shot action data with UWB position information, the accuracy for tactical classification significantly increased to 80%. In the study by Z. Wang36, four IMUs data sets were used in combination with Hidden Markov Models (HMM) to classify 14 badminton hitting actions. Chang Tai Kiang highlighted10 in his article that using only accelerometers is insufficient to evaluate badminton smash performance. He developed a localized sensing system that integrates acoustic emission sensors with accelerometers mounted on the racket head, allowing for a more accurate analysis of additional information related to overhead smash techniques and offering insights into performance metrics for badminton smashes.

Srimath et al. reported that the 1D-CNN model achieves higher classification accuracy than the Bi-Directional Long Short-Term Memory (Bi-LSTM) model in RGB-based human activity recognition tasks. This improvement was achieved without relying on depth or multi-modal data37. Zhang et al. selected the 1D CNN + LSTM algorithm as the optimal method based on experimental analysis and comparison, and constructed a human motion behavior recognition network model to process data collected by smart wearable devices38. In the field of basketball, researchers developed a shooting posture recognition system based on a 1D-CNN, which collects motion data from the dominant hand and foot through sensor fusion. This system achieved an accuracy of 98.6%, which further demonstrates the applicability and robustness of CNN architectures in sport-specific motion classification tasks39. Similarly, Ma et al.40 mounted an IMU on the athlete’s right wrist and employed a GoPro camera to capture synchronized video data, enabling the recognition of nine fundamental basketball movements. Their neural network-based model achieved an accuracy of 98.9% in experiments with a single subject, validating the feasibility of the method under controlled conditions. These findings demonstrate that integrating multiple sensor data sources can significantly enhance the accuracy of badminton skill and tactical recognition, providing strong support for sports training and match analysis. The reviewed literature indicates that machine learning and deep learning have been successfully applied to motion recognition in badminton, with promising results. As a sport, badminton is characterized by rapid changes, a fast pace, and high speed. To achieve excellent performance, players need advanced technical and tactical abilities. In competitive play, the ability to adjust shot trajectories according to varying situations is a key component of a player’s proficiency. Previous studies have mainly focused on recognizing various stroke actions but have lacked an analysis of stroke actions across different trajectories. In this context, our study primarily focuses on utilizing neural networks and IMUs to recognize athletes’ stroke actions across diverse shot trajectories. Specifically, we leverage 1D-CNN and IMUs data to identify various stroke actions while also recognizing various shot trajectories, providing a more scientific reference for participants of varying skill levels.

Application of ESP32 in motion recognition

Previous studies on badminton action recognition have employed multiple sensors to capture athletes’ strokes. However, using multiple sensors for data collection presents a challenge in synchronizing their timing accurately. This often requires post-processing methods, such as interpolation, to align the data. Although interpolation can help synchronize the data, it may distort the stroke information, increase computational costs, and add complexity to the training model. With advancements on the Internet of Things (IoT) and the growing demand for miniaturized devices, Espressif Systems introduced the ESP32 microcontroller in a QFN48 package in September 2016, designed to replace the earlier ESP8266 microcontroller41. The ESP32 is a powerful microcontroller featuring built-in Wi-Fi and Bluetooth, making it an ideal solution for IoT applications. It includes two independently operating Xtensa LX6 CPU cores, each with a maximum frequency of 240 MHz, and supports external crystal oscillators, four 64-bit general-purpose timers, a PWM timer, an RTC clock, and various low-power clock modes42. Its dual-core architecture enables parallel task processing, and the timers can count up or down, freeze, or trigger events. Considering the powerful timing capabilities of the ESP32, previous studies have proposed a wireless timing system based on the ESP-to-ESP communication protocol to overcome the limitations of Bluetooth’s short-range and the dependence on internet connectivity. This system enables remote activation of the timer and real-time data transmission, while allowing the timer to be automatically stopped by a terminal sensor at the end of a competition, thereby significantly reducing human-induced timing errors and improving measurement accuracy43. To enhance training efficiency and competitive performance in ice and snow sports, Saavedra et al.44 developed a data acquisition system based on ESP32 modules and IMUs to enable real-time monitoring of athletes’ limb acceleration data. In addition, a supporting Android application was developed to facilitate data interaction and visualization, further validating the integration capability and application potential of ESP32 technology in the field of sports science. Building upon this, K. Ishida et al.24 proposed a sports framework that integrates IoT applications with sports skills research, emphasizing wearable devices with inertial sensors. In summary, advancements in IoT technology and microcontrollers such as the ESP32 have opened up new possibilities for motion recognition and data acquisition in sports. ESP32-based platforms enable more accurate and real-time data collection through wireless communication and integrated sensing. These systems also reduce computational load, minimize human intervention, and expand the scope of biomechanical analysis. Their feasibility and application potential have been demonstrated in fields such as badminton and winter sports, laying a solid foundation for the development of efficient, low-power, and scalable motion monitoring solutions.

Model construction and data processing

Data information and 1D-CNN

As shown in Fig. 1, to facilitate learning of the feature for different stroke actions, the 1D-CNN model was built to recognize the six badminton stroke actions. Two IMUs were employed in this process, positioned at the distal end of the wrist radius and the lower end of the badminton racket handle to capture motion data from both segments19. The IMUs at the wrist radius measure angular changes in the wrist joint during the strike, while the other IMUs at the racket handle record the angular variations of the racket during impact. To acquire more precise data, the ESP32 microcontroller was used to perform time synchronization of the signals from the two IMUs, forming a multi-sensor data acquisition and synchronization system. The microcontroller, using the external crystal oscillator and timers embedded in it, was employed to synchronize events in real time. This design ensures that during an athlete’s stroke motion, the triaxial acceleration values remain synchronized within the same time frame, effectively avoiding data distortion and minimizing the computational load that would otherwise arise from post-processing synchronization. The captured motion data were merged in chronological order to create a fused dataset. Specifically, the data from the wrist IMUs and the racket IMUs are represented as S(M) and S(Θ), respectively. The multi-sensor data acquisition and synchronization method performs time synchronization and data merging, allowing for comparison and analysis of different badminton strokes within the same time dimension. This approach also enhances the ability of the 1D convolutional neural network classifier to recognize various stroke types and trajectories.

The feature maps from the two IMUs are concatenated to form an input of size 18 × 1 × 1. The 1D-CNN used in this study is shown in Table 2, consisting of two convolutional blocks with ReLU activations and batch normalization, one max-pooling layer, a fully connected layer, and a Softmax classifier. Specifically, Conv1 uses 8 filters with a 3 × 1 kernel, followed by batch normalization and ReLU; a 2 × 1 max-pooling layer is then applied to reduce the feature dimension. Conv2 uses 16 filters with a 3 × 1 kernel, followed by batch normalization and ReLU. The pooled features are flattened and fed to a fully connected layer with six neurons, followed by Softmax to produce class probabilities. The network was trained with cross-entropy loss and the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 0.005, batch size = 64, iterating over the 500 epochs). In preliminary pilot experiments on a separate development set, this configuration yielded stable convergence and strong validation accuracy; therefore, we fixed it as the default in the main experiments and did not perform hyperparameter search on the study dataset. Early stopping was used with a patience of 10 epochs. Stratified 5-fold cross-validation was used for performance estimation; preprocessing was fitted on the training portion only and applied to the validation portion. Dropout was not used due to the lightweight architecture and the regularization provided by batch normalization and early stopping.

Different stroke actions and stroke trajectories

By analyzing the connection between unforced mistakes, successful shots, and match results, we can gain a deeper understanding of how crucial actions, such as unforced mistakes and scoring points, influence the final outcome45. Research has also shown that a significant number of unforced mistakes often contribute to losing a match or set. Consequently, we divided the research objectives into two parts: (1) identifying six basic badminton techniques, and (2) identifying six different shot trajectories based on these techniques. Using two IMUs controlled by an ESP32 microcontroller, we first collected data on the six basic badminton techniques. These actions include:

This technique is somewhat offensive in nature.Forehand smash(FS): The forehand smash, a highly offensive overhead stroke technique, drives the shuttle from the backcourt to the opponent’s mid- or front-court with a fast, low trajectory, often catching the opponent off guard.Forehand lift(FL): This defensive underhand stroke sends the shuttle from the front court to the opponent’s backcourt with a high, deep trajectory. It gives the player time to recover and reposition for defense, while also forcing the opponent to move.Forehand net tumble(FNT): A control-oriented defensive technique performed from a low position, in which the player gently sends the shuttle over the net, aiming to land it close to the opponent’s front court. This forces the opponent to move closer to the net, limiting their shot options.Forehand cross-net shot(FNS): A common net-front offensive and defensive technique, the shuttle is directed across the net from one side to the other, often to catch the opponent off balance.

Experiment

Hardware platform

Figure 3 illustrates the placement of two sensors during the striking action. In upper-limb-dominant tasks, energy generation and transfer generally follow a proximal-to-distal sequence. Therefore, the positioning of the two IMUs must consider this kinetic chain relationship, forming an “upper limb–wrist–racket” motion chain47.IMU-based motion analysis typically relies on wearable devices with embedded sensors to record motion and orientation data48. When mounted on sports implements, the sensor devices should be non-invasive to the human body. Referring to the sensor placement strategies in table tennis49, as well as previous IMU applications in badminton19, tennis50, and basketball51, we chose to place the IMUs at the end of the badminton racket and on the radial side of the athlete’s racket-holding wrist. The nine-axis IMUs (HWT906) are compact in size (25 × 25 × 1.6 mm³) and have low power consumption (about 0.1 watts), with measurement ranges of ± 160 m/s² for acceleration, ± 2000 deg/s for angular velocity, and ± 4900 µT for the magnetometer. The axis calibration follows the right-hand rule. Specifically, the X-axis of the wrist-radius IMUs corresponds to the direction of the thumb, and the Y-axis corresponds to the direction of the right-hand index finger. The IMUs placed at the bottom of the badminton racket handle have their Y-axis corresponding to the racket’s face and their X-axis corresponding to the side of the handle. The entire sensing system is powered by a 5-volt battery and simultaneously collects signals from the two IMUs via the SPI bus protocol at a sampling frequency of 100 Hz. To ensure the stability of the serve, we used the SIBOASI smart serving machine (SS-S4025A), which has a maximum input power of 360 watts, a serving frequency ranging from 1.2 to 5.5 s, and an elevation angle between − 15 and 33 degrees. It was connected to a mobile app to adjust the serving strategy based on different hitting positions, ensuring the quality of the serve.

Experimental protocol

In this experiment, six male national-level first-class athletes, all of whom had not been injured in the past six months, were recruited (average age: 22 ± 3 years, height: 176 ± 8 cm, and weight: 70 ± 6 kg). All subjects were right-handed and participated in the experiment with informed consent. All striking action tests were conducted in an indoor badminton court. As shown in Fig. 4, the athlete wears the sensors and stands in the center of the badminton court, with the serving machine positioned on the opposite side. Holding the badminton racket, the athlete waits to strike. The serve is directed towards the player at a frequency of 4 s, allowing ample time for the player to execute their action and prepare for the subsequent shot, Meanwhile, the two sensors are fixed at the end of the racket handle and on the inner side of the right radius using athletic tape and nylon straps, respectively. The participants were required to complete six different striking actions and fifteen different shot trajectories, with each action performed 20 times with valid results. Before each experiment, the IMUs were restarted and left stationary for calibration. A 2-minute rest period was set between each pair of striking actions to ensure recovery of the athlete’s energy system and to prevent muscle fatigue from negatively impacting the striking actions. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of Chengdu University.

Using CNN to recognize different badminton stroke actions

Based on the t-SNE plot, we analyzed the classification performance of the 1D-CNN model in recognizing six types of badminton strokes. With multi-sensor data acquisition and synchronization, the model achieved an overall classification accuracy of 97.16%. As shown in Fig. 7a, the confusion matrix indicates that the model has some difficulty distinguishing between FL and FNS. This is mainly because, during the pre-hit phase, athletes often adopt similar racket face angles to maintain consistency in their shots, making the two actions appear highly similar. In addition, individual differences in how players execute these strokes may further contribute to the confusion. Overall, this phenomenon is consistent with our expectations of the model’s recognition performance.

In actual training, coaches can address the issues in players’ shots based on the classification results. Specifically, when there is some confusion between the FC and FS, the coach, based on the classification results, can improve the stroke point or strengthen the coordination of muscles from the legs to the wrists according to the spatial height and the characteristics of technical movements. This is done in consideration of individual player differences. Targeted training for the two stroke actions is then implemented in subsequent training sessions, using the stroke data collected by IMUs. In terms of the training plan, based on the results of the confusion, the training intensity and measurement for different technical movements should be reasonably allocated. The intensity and measurement should be appropriately reduced for excellent shot types, while adjustments should be made for poor shot types.

Discussion

This study combines multi-site sensor information fusion with 1D-CNN to recognize different badminton stroke actions and flight trajectories. This is the first time that a multi-sensor data acquisition and synchronization system, consisting of ESP32 and IMUs, has been used to monitor badminton movements. The following is a further discussion of the above aspects.

As shown in Table 5, previous related studies have either used a single sensor or an excessive number of sensors. A single sensor may not fully capture the complete data of badminton stroke actions, while using too many sensors may reduce the comfort of wearable devices. By using two sensors—one on the wrist and one on the racket—complementary data on human motion can be captured more comprehensively than with a single sensor, achieving accuracies of 97.16% for stroke actions and 86.07% for stroke trajectories.In practical applications, using a two-sensor configuration not only ensures high recognition accuracy but also balances comfort by reducing the discomfort caused by sensor rigidity, allowing athletes to perform movements more comfortably. This, in turn, enhances data collection quality and improves the model’s performance in classifying different stroke actions.In Table 4, the comparison of various machine learning methods shows that CNN outperforms other methods in recognizing stroke actions and trajectories, achieving accuracies of 97.16% and 86.07%, respectively, and also demonstrates the best performance across comparisons of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{F}}^{\mathbf{S}\left(\mathbf{M}\right)}\:$$\end{document},\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{F}}^{\mathbf{S}\left(\varvec{\Theta\:}\right)}$$\end{document},and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{F}}^{\mathbf{S}\left(\varvec{\Theta\:}\right)\mathbf{a}\mathbf{n}\mathbf{d}\:\mathbf{S}\left(\mathbf{M}\right)}$$\end{document} signals. However, considering that CNN requires feature extraction and learning through multiple convolutional layers, it results in a relatively higher computational load and longer training time. Therefore, during model training, CNN takes longer to train compared to models such as KNN, SVM, and DTA.Compared to the UWB ultra-wideband system, the ESP32 offers better cost-effectiveness and, when combined with the simpler 1D-CNN, holds greater potential for commercial deployment. Conclusion

Based on wearable sensing and machine learning techniques, this study achieves accurate recognition of six badminton stroke techniques and fifteen stroke trajectories. Experiments were conducted with six national-level badminton athletes, and the wearable system consisting of two IMUs, simultaneously captures the motions of the badminton racket and human wrist. To establish the mapping between raw signals and motion patterns, the IMUs measurements are fed into a constructed 1D-CNN for training and prediction. To intuitively display feature differences in different patterns, heatmaps and t-SNE plots are used, which support the results achieved by ‘black box’ machine learning models. The effectiveness of the proposed method is demonstrated by the pattern recognition accuracy of 97.16% for six badminton stroke motions and 86.07% for fifteen badminton flight trajectories. This study provides an effective method for badminton motion monitoring, which is expected to be valuable for enhancing badminton training to improve athletes’ competitive skills.
