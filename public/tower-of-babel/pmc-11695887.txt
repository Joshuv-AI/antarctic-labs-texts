The Clinical Utility of Targeted Heart Rate Control in Septic Shock: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials with Trial Sequential Analysis
Authors: Priyankar K Datta, Prachee Sathe, Avishek Roy, Tanima Baronia, Anirban Bhattacharjee, Riddhi Kundu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesHeart rate control using beta-blockers in sepsis has traditionally been avoided because of concerns with worsening cardiac index and organ perfusion. Recent studies has explored the possible beneficial effects of targeted heart rate control in patients with septic shock who have tachycardia despite initial resuscitation. We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to explore the effects of heart rate control in septic shock patients.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted searching for studies from PubMed, Cochrane Central, and Embase registers for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that compared the mortality of patients with sepsis and septic shock treated with targeted rate control. The literature search was done to include studies from January 2013 to December 2023. Two independent researchers independently assessed the studies and included RCTS in which adult patients (>18 years of age) with septic shock were treated with targeted heart rate control vs placebo after initial resuscitation due to persistent tachycardia. The study data was extracted by two independent researchers. A random effects model was used to present the results. A trial sequential analysis (TSA) was performed for the primary outcome of 28-day mortality.ResultsA total of 9 studies with a pooled sample size of 807 participants were included in the analysis.Eight of the included studies with a pooled sample size of 766 reported 28-day mortality. Targeted heart rate control was associated with a trend toward lower 28-day mortality [risk ratio (RR): 0.78; 95% CI: 0.62–0.99; p = 0.04; I2 = 48%]. Trial sequential analysis showed the cumulative effect lying within the zone of uncertainty, with diversity-adjusted required information size of 1,756 and pooled effect size of the pooled RR 0.78 (alpha-spending adjusted 95% CI: 0.53–1.15). There was a statistically significant lower heart rate associated with rate control (MD: –16.66; 95% CI: –23.89 to –9.42; p-value < 0.001) but no difference in mean arterial pressure. cardiac index, lactate levels, norepinephrine (NE) requirements, and ICU length of stay (LOS) in between the groups.ConclusionTargeted heart rate control in patients with septic shock may be tolerated from a hemodynamic standpoint. Introduction

The Sepsis-3 update released in 2016 defined sepsis as dysregulated host response to infection manifesting as life-threatening organ dysfunction. Sepsis affects about 48.5 million people globally and accounts for 11 million deaths worldwide as per the latest World Health Organization estimates published in 2020.1 The Surviving Sepsis Campaign along with region-specific guidelines aims to educate healthcare workers in timely recognition of sepsis, appropriate antimicrobial administration, adequate resuscitation with fluids and vasopressors, supportive therapies, and the development of robust systems of care in every healthcare facility.2,3

The use of beta-blocking agents in sepsis to block the deleterious effect of peripheral adrenergic surge on the myocardium has gained traction in the last decade.4 The beneficial effect of beta-blocking agents is believed to be due to the lowering of heart rate leading to a decrease in myocardial oxygen consumption, more diastolic filling time for the heart, and less occurrence of tachyarrhythmias. In addition, downregulation of the sympathetically mediated inflammatory response might have a protective effect on organ systems beyond the heart.5 On the flip side, the catecholamine surge in sepsis is believed to be essential for maintaining systemic vascular resistance, cardiac output, and organ perfusion. Therefore, lowering the heart rate using sympathetic blocking agents in septic shock appears counterintuitive. The use of beta-blocking agents has shown survival benefits in a few animal models of septic shock while preserving organ perfusion.6,7 The premise of aggressive control of heart rate in patients with septic shock was examined in the large-scale RCT by Morelli et al., which reported a mortality benefit in the intervention group receiving intravenous esmolol infusion targeting a heart rate of 80–94 beats/minute. However, this trial was not powered to detect survival benefits and was noted for an unusually high mortality in the control group.8 Nonetheless, there were subsequent trials as well as systematic reviews that tried to explore the benefit of selective beta-adrenergic blockade with intravenous esmolol and targeted heart control in septic shock patients with a signal towards benefit.9,10 Since then, intravenous landiolol and enteral ivabradine are the other agents that have been used for managing inappropriate tachycardia in patients with septic shock.11,12 The study into the reversal of septic shock with landiolol (STRESS-L) RCT, published recently, administered intravenous landiolol in septic shock patients with the same target heart rates as the initial study by Morelli et al. However, the study was terminated early due to failure to show any outcome benefits.12 Datta et al. used ivabradine, a funny sodium channel blocker to achieve similar heart rate goals. While their study showed improved hemodynamic parameters and cardiac function in the intervention group, it did not translate into a survival benefit.13 In light of this newer evidence, we felt it worthwhile to explore the effect of targeted lowering of heart rate in septic shock on mortality and clinically relevant hemodynamic parameters. A recently conducted meta-analysis assessed the effect of esmolol in patients with septic shock.10 In light of recent trials using other pharmacological agents for heart rate reduction, we felt it worthwhile to explore the role of targeted heart rate control, irrespective of agent, in septic shock patients, and their effect on mortality and clinically relevant hemodynamic parameters. A trial sequential analysis (TSA) was also conducted to identify where we stand with respect to the need for further trials on this topic.

Methods

The study followed the existing standards for reporting systematic reviews and meta-analysis.14

Literature Search Strategy

We undertook a comprehensive literature search for eligible RCTs in Embase, PubMed, and Cochrane Central Register for clinical trials from January 1, 2010, to December 21, 2023, using the following keywords: “septic shock,” “beta-blocking agents,” “esmolol,” “landiolol,” and “ivabradine.” We simultaneously carried out a manual scanning of the bibliography of studies and review articles to search for potentially relevant studies. Prior ethical approval and consent were deemed unnecessary, as the analysis was based on data previously published elsewhere.

Study Selection

We included RCTs that specifically evaluated the benefit of targeted control of heart rate in septic shock patients requiring vasopressor support. Study inclusion criteria were: RCTs in adult patients (>18 years of age) assessing targeted heart rate control (target HR <100 bpm) vs placebo or standard care in patients with septic shock on vasopressor support. Two investigators (RK and PKD) independently scanned the abstracts and retrieved relevant articles of interest for inclusion in the study. There was no language restriction and Google Translate was used for studies that were not in English. In the case of duplicate studies, the study that was reported earlier was considered. Any disagreement between the two investigators was resolved by the mediation and judgment of another investigator (AR).

Study Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was 28-day mortality. The important Secondary outcomes that were analyzed were the effect on hemodynamic parameters, norepinephrine (NE) dose requirements, lactate levels, and intensive care unit length of stay (LOS). A meta-analysis was performed only if more than three studies reported data necessary for analyzing the outcome of interest.

Data Extraction

The requisite data was extracted independently by two investigators [Anirban Bhattacharjee and Avishek Roy respectively (AB and AR)] from the included studies using a standardized data collection form. Disagreements arising during data collection were resolved by mediation of a third reviewer. The data extracted included information about the study name, first author of the study, publication year, study design, number of patients recruited in each study, inclusion and exclusion criteria, a drug used for achieving targeted heart rate control, and primary and secondary outcomes of the study. The risk of bias of individual RCTs included in the meta-analysis was estimated using the Cochrane collaboration tool based on the following criteria, viz. method of random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants, personnel and outcome assessors, incomplete outcome reporting, and other biases.15

Statistical Analysis

The software RevMan, version 5.4.1 was used for statistical analysis. For dichotomous outcomes like 28-day mortality, we expressed the data as relative risk or risk ratio (RR). For the continuous outcomes data, we used the mean difference (MD) and standardized mean difference (SMD). Risk ratio, MD, or SMD were expressed as point estimates with 95% CI and p-value. For the analysis of binary outcomes, Mantel–Haenszel random effect model was used, while continuous outcomes were analyzed using the inverse-variance random effect model. The assessment of statistical heterogeneity was performed using the Mantel–Haenszel Chi2 test and I2 statistic.

Publication bias was estimated by generating a funnel plot for 28-day mortality by plotting the log RRs against their standard errors and testing for symmetry using the Begg rank correlation test and Egger linear regression test. For data that was not available from the full texts, the authors were approached to provide the requisite information.

We further tested the robustness of our meta-analysis result through a TSA for the primary outcome (28-day mortality) using the TSA program provided by the Copenhagen Trial Unit (www.ctu.dk/tsa). Positive and negative conventional boundaries were added assuming a two-sided alpha error of 5%. The boundaries for definite benefit or harm, zone of futility, and diversity-adjusted required information size (RIS) were identified assuming a power of 80%, and type I error of 5%, Pooled effect size (Z-score) was derived from the meta-analysis.

Results

Study Selection and Characteristics of Included Studies

The literature screening and selection process is depicted in the flowchart (Fig. 1). Our initial search yielded 293 results. After the removal of duplicates, we screened 136 articles for eligibility. The abstracts of those articles were screened and subsequently 32 articles were then shortlisted and their full texts reviewed. The MODIFY RCT, which studied the use of ivabradine in patients with multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), was excluded as it comprised patients with both septic and cardiogenic shock.16 Finally, nine RCTs meeting the inclusion criteria were incorporated into our meta-analysis.8,11–13,17–21

Nine RCTs with a pooled sample size of 807 participants were included. The publication period ranged from 2013 to 2023. The sample size of the RCTs included ranged from 40 to 154. The drug used to achieve targeted rate control was intravenous esmolol in six studies, intravenous landiolol in two studies, and enteral ivabradine in 1 study. All included studies recruited adult patients with septic shock on vasopressor support. The target heart rate was less than 100 beats/minute in two studies, while the rest of the 5 studies targeted a heart rate lower than 95 beats per minute. All the studies except for one were unblinded RCTs. The mean NE dose at the time of recruitment ranged from 0.2 μg/kg/minute to 1.06 μg/kg/minute.

A total of seven out of the nine studies directly reported 28-day mortality. The study by Datta et al.13 reported 30-day mortality, and the authors were requested to provide 28-day mortality and relevant secondary outcome data which was subsequently used for analysis. The landiolol infusion rate in both the two studies was commenced at 1 μg/kg per min and adjusted in increments of 1 μg/kg per min until the target heart rate was achieved.11,12 The esmolol infusion rates were in the range of 0.05–0.2 μg/kg/min or 25 mg/h as a continuous infusion up to a maximum dose of 300 μg/kg/min. The study drug, Ivabradine, in the study by Datta et al. was prescribed at a dose of 2.5 mg every 6 hourly administered via the enteral route. The NE dose at baseline in patients ranged from 0.2 to 1.06 μg/kg/min.

Assessment of Risk of Bias and Publication Bias

The risk of bias summary for the included RCTs included in this meta-analysis is presented in Figure 2. The risk of bias graph is shown in Figure 3. All studies were considered at low risk of selection bias except the study by Yang et al. Six out of the nine studies were found to have a high risk of performance bias, while we were unsure of studies by Yang et al. and Xinqiang et al. due to lack of data. Detection bias was considered a high risk in three studies, while the risk was uncertain in two studies. The studies included were considered at low risk of reporting bias and attrition bias.

Primary Outcome

Eight of the included RCTs in this meta-analysis reported 28-day mortality. The use of targeted heart rate control in septic shock was associated with a trend towards lower 28-day mortality compared to the placebo group (RR: 0.78; 95% CI: 0.62–0.99; p = 0.04, I2 = 48%; Fig. 4). The funnel plot of 28-day mortality on visual inspection did not reveal any evidence of publication bias (Fig. 5).

Secondary Outcomes

Seven out of nine studies with a total of 613 participants reported heart rate values at 24 hours following the commencement of the study. Patients receiving targeted heart rate control had a lower heart rate compared to the control group (MD: 16.66; 95% CI: –23.89 to –9.42; p-value < 0.001; Fig. 6). The mean arterial pressure values were reported in five studies at 24 hours after enrolment which was not significantly different between groups (SMD: 0.15; 95% CI: –0.41 to 0.11; p-value 0.26; Fig. 7). There was no difference in NE dose at 24 hours following enrolment (SMD: 0.15; 95% CI: –0.17 to 0.47; p-value 0.37; Fig. 8) in between the intervention and control groups in 5 studies. The lactate levels at 24 hours were reported in six studies (SMD: 0.13; 95% CI: –0.51 to 0.77; p-value 0.69; Fig. 9). Of the included studies, five reported cardiac index values at 24 hours following enrolment, which was comparable between groups (SMD: –0.15; 95% CI: –0.62 to 0.33; p-value 0.55; Fig. 10). Five of the included studies reported LOS in ICU which was not different with targeted rate control strategy compared to usual care (SMD: –0.22; 95% CI: –0.57 to 4.12; p-value 0.92; Fig. 11).

Sensitivity and Sub-group Analysis

In the sensitivity analysis performed after excluding the only study that used ivabradine in the intervention arm, the use of heart rate control failed to show any benefit in terms of 28-day mortality (RR: 0.77; 95% CI: 0.58–1.03; p = 0.08, I2 = 55%). A sub-group analysis was performed using studies that used esmolol in the intervention arm. This showed a significant 28-day mortality difference between groups (RR: 0.71; 95% CI: 0.54–0.93; p = 0.012, I2 = 40%). However, a subgroup analysis for landiolol failed to demonstrate any difference in 28-day mortality (RR: 0.98; 95% CI: 0.41–2.34; p = 0.96, I2 = 72%).

Trial Sequential Analysis

Trial sequential analysis shows that the cumulative Z-curve lays outside the conventional boundary of benefit, but within the zone of uncertainty (Fig. 12). The current meta-analysis includes 766 patients for the primary outcome, whereas the diversity-adjusted RIS is 1,756 patients. The alpha-spending adjusted 95% confidence interval of the pooled effect size (RR: 0.78) was 0.53–1.15.

Summary of Findings to Assess the Certainty of the Evidence

A summary-of-findings table (Fig. 13) was made using GRADEpro GDT software based on the principles and methods described in the Cochrane Handbook of Systematic Reviews for interventions.22 We included the clinically relevant primary and secondary outcomes and analyzed the certainty of evidence based on the five Grade considerations (risk of bias, inconsistency, imprecision, indirectness, and publication bias). We found that targeted heart rate control may lower mortality in septic shock patients. However, the evidence was downgraded by two levels to low quality in view of inconsistency and indirectness (I2 = 48%, implying moderate heterogeneity, and use of different rate-control agents – downgraded by 1), and imprecision (meta-analysis included 766 patients whereas diversity-adjusted RIS based on TSA was 1756 – downgraded by 1).

Grade of evidence

The effect of targeted heart rate control on other secondary outcome parameters viz; heart rate, NE dose requirements, length of ICU stay, and lactate levels were downgraded to very low quality of evidence (additional level downgraded for potential detection bias as in most studies, the outcome assessors were either unblinded, or blinding was unclear).

Discussion

We investigated the role of targeted heart rate control in the subset of septic shock patients in this meta-analysis. We included a total of 9 RCTs satisfying our inclusion criteria with a total pooled sample size of 807 participants. Our study results indicate that targeted heart rate control in septic shock might be associated with a small signal toward benefit in terms of lower 28-day mortality in the intervention group (RR: 0.78; 95% CI: 0.62–0.99).

Tachycardia is widespread in septic shock and is believed to be a manifestation of activation of compensatory sympathetic response, fluid depletion, and systemic effects of vasopressors and inotropes. Studies have also shown tachycardia to be associated with abnormal left ventricular strain in septic shock. Patients with poorly controlled tachycardia have uniformly worse outcomes.23,24 Still, interventions designed to lower heart rate in septic shock appears to challenge conventional wisdom. The sympathetic surge seen in these patients is believed to be a key driver in maintaining systemic vascular resistance, cardiac output, and organ perfusion. Therefore, any intervention attempting to block this pathway can be associated with the possibility of aggravating organ dysfunction in patients dealing with the consequences of impaired organ perfusion. We, therefore, tried to assess the important determinants and markers of organ perfusion (viz cardiac index, heart rate, mean arterial pressure, and lactate levels) in addition to clinically relevant outcomes of 28-day mortality and ICU LOS.

The hemodynamic profiles in patients with septic shock when assessed by echocardiography have shown varying clusters ranging from normal function, hyperkinetic profile, and left or right ventricular dysfunction varying with the extent of hypovolemia.25,26 The benefits of heart rate control have been postulated to be multifactorial due to a decrease in myocardial oxygen consumption, lowering of systemic inflammation, and cardio-protective effects.

The beneficial effect of targeted heart rate control seen in this meta-analysis primarily comes from the initial two studies by Morelli et al. and Xinxiang et al., while all other studies done subsequently failed to show a mortality benefit.8,17 Both the studies used esmolol as a study drug. Notably, the two more recent trials that used landiolol, an ultra-short-acting beta blocker with greater beta-1 selectivity compared to esmolol failed to show any survival benefit.11,12 Trial sequential analysis shows that from the available evidence at present, the cumulative effect of lowering heart rate in septic shock on 28-day mortality is potentially beneficial, but still lies within the zone of uncertainty. This meta-analysis was performed with 766 patients for the primary outcome, whereas around 1,756 patients would be needed to draw reasonably certain conclusions Overall, the quality of evidence in our meta-analysis was low in favor of targeted heart rate control given concerns with the study design of some of the included studies. When we compare it to earlier systematic reviews and meta-analyses on the same topic, we find our association to be considerably weaker.9,10 This may be because we included recent studies, that did not replicate the benefit of targeted heart rate control that was shown in the initial studies. The use of cardiac output monitoring to ensure adequate oxygen delivery was not used uniformly in the included studies except for the initial study by Morelli and co-authors.8 There is a possibility that lowering of heart rate could have led to worsening of forward flow in the subgroup of patients with septic cardiomyopathy. The benefit of lowering heart rate to improve oxygen delivery is doubtful in septic cardiomyopathy, as the underlying pathophysiology in these patients appears to be a mitochondrial energetic failure rather than an impairment of coronary perfusion.27 We are therefore unable to offer any recommendation in support of targeted heart rate control in septic shock patients based on current evidence.

Presumably, such an aggressive heart rate control regimen has its challenges of worsening systemic oxygen delivery, cardiac output, and potentially worsening hemodynamic indices. While the intervention arm was understandably associated with lower heart rate, it was not associated with any significant difference in mean arterial pressure, cardiac index, and NE requirements at 24 hours following study enrolment. When we closely looked at the effect of heart rate control on cardiac index, we found Datta et al. reporting an improvement in cardiac index with rate control.13 Interestingly, the authors used ivabradine, which has primarily negative chronotropic activity with negligent hemodynamic effects. Hence, the decrease in heart rate may have been associated with improved cardiac efficiency due to an improvement in diastolic filling and better oxygen delivery. The recent STRESS-L RCT reported lower mean arterial pressure and higher cumulative NE requirements in the intervention group that received landiolol.12 The incidence of hypotension was reported in 12% of patients receiving landiolol in the study by Kakihana et al. against 0% in the usual care arm.11 This might make ivabradine preferable over esmolol or landiolol, though its role needs to be explored in future studies.

There was no difference in ICU LOS between the two groups This finding is in line with previously published literature on the same topic.10 It must be noted that the sample sizes for judging the adequacy of perfusion (viz; cardiac index, lactate levels, NE requirements at 24 h) were small, and the heterogeneity of included studies was considerably high limiting the robustness of the results.

Our meta-analysis has a few strengths. Firstly, we uniformly included studies in patients with septic shock. We felt patients with septic shock are more likely to have inappropriate tachycardia, where the use of heart rate control might theoretically offer maximal benefit in terms of cardioprotection. The included studies had a well-defined protocol of study drug titration to achieve the desired heart rate in the intervention group. Secondly, this meta-analysis includes the most up-to-date literature assessing the evidence of lowering heart rate in septic shock including the recently published STRESS-L RCT.12

There are a few limitations of this meta-analysis. Firstly the study drug was esmolol in six studies, landiolol in two studies, and ivabradine in one study. While esmolol and landiolol and pharmacologically similar in being selective beta 1 blocking agents with ultra-short half-lives, Ivabradine is a funny sodium channel blocker that decreases sinus node automaticity. We are unsure if beta-blockers could offer additional benefits of immunomodulation, which might not be there with the use of ivabradine. Nonetheless, we felt any intervention targeting heart control can only be titrated to a clinical endpoint of heart rate. All the included studies uniformly targeted a heart rate of less than 100 beats/minute in the intervention group. However, the duration of heart rate control was not uniform across the studies. Only two of the studies included had mortality as the primary outcome. Most of the studies were underpowered for assessing mortality, and therefore prone to type I error. Secondly, seven out of the nine studies were from Asia, while the rest two were from Europe and the United States of America, which might make the results invalid for other ethnic populations.

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis suggests that targeting a lower heart rate in septic shock may be tolerated from a hemodynamic standpoint (very low-quality evidence). However, the beneficial effect on mortality is less certain than was reported in the initial studies. With available evidence, targeted HR control strategy is not justifiable in routine clinical practice it can only be limited to research settings. Future studies examining the benefits/harms of heart rate control in specific hemodynamic clusters of septic shock patients should offer interesting insights.
