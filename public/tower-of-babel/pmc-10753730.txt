Modeling Self-Sealing Mechanisms in Fractured Carbonates Induced by CO2 Injection in Saline Aquifers
Authors: Marcos Vitor Barbosa Machado, Mojdeh Delshad, Kamy Sepehrnoori
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
In the future, there will be competition among natural gas, CO2, and hydrogen for suitable geological storage sites. Therefore, it is crucial to evaluate all of the potential storage options. One promising option is the utilization of fractured carbonate rocks, which offer significant opportunities for gas sequestration in depleted reservoirs and saline aquifers. The objective of this study is to assess the feasibility of using carbonate matrix rocks in saline aquifers for carbon capture and storage (CCS) projects. Although carbonate reservoirs are currently not ranked highly for carbon storage due to the risk of CO2 leakage, their matrix rock with reactive minerals like calcite and dolomite, along with the possibility of natural fractures, presents an interesting opportunity. This research proposes a reassessment of the role of fractures, which are typically viewed as a risk factor, within a novel and integrated context. It combines geochemical modeling with numerical models that incorporate two distinct density levels of natural fractures. The interactions between the carbonate matrix, the formation brine, and the injected CO2 can lead to water vaporization and the deposition of evaporite minerals, known as the halite scale, within the porous medium. These minerals can be transported within highly conductive fractures that possess a permeability 100 times greater than the matrix. The study findings indicate that the fractures become filled, creating a natural seal that prevents CO2 leakage through what was previously considered a potential pathway in both models and different initial pH values. Furthermore, the results demonstrate the formation of secondary minerals through the reaction of CO2 and its aqueous counterparts with rock matrix minerals. These minerals, including Dawsonite, are then deposited within fracture apertures, compensating for the dissolution of calcite from the matrix and reducing the risk of enhanced fracture conductivity during CCS operations. The deposition of halite scales in initially acidic-brine saturated aquifers and/or Dawsonite scales in initially alkaline-brine saturated aquifers serves to partially counterbalance the permeability enhancement resulting from calcite dissolution. This phenomenon makes carbonate rocks a more secure option and highlights their potential suitability for CCS projects. The existence of these scales acts as a protective barrier, reducing the risk of increased permeability and enhancing the integrity of the storage reservoir.

Introduction

A CCS project involves the capture of CO2 from high-emission industries and injecting it into geological formations such as aquifers and depleted hydrocarbon reservoirs. One of the most critical barriers to long-term and large-volume CO2 storage in geological formations is proof of safe and reliable storage. On a more specific scale, CO2 injection in the subsurface introduces additional complexity in terms of interactions of the reservoir host pore fluid with the natural heterogeneity of the hydromechanical properties of the rock. The natural heterogeneity of the reservoir includes a spatial variation of hydromechanical properties as well as the presence of natural fractures. It was proposed to understand and consider the effect of rock mineralogy in contact with CO2 and brine ions in naturally fractured media. To achieve that, we will apply a numerical approach using the Embedded Discrete Fracture Model (EDFM) to simulate an outcrop model that closely resembles real aquifers and reservoirs found in Brazil.

EDFM uses a structured or unstructured grid to represent the matrix and creates additional gridblocks to represent the fractures and their transmissibilities.1−4 In the EDFM, the matrix is gridded regardless of the location of the conductive channels, and the fractures or faults are discretized by the matrix block boundaries. The fluid flow through these geological features is represented by non-neighboring connections (NNCs). The formulation is flexible to represent different connections (matrix fractures, fractures fractures, and fractures-wells). In previous work,5 it was shown that this methodology is feasible to simulate reactive transport flow during CO2 injection for CCS proposes. With EDFM, a high-fidelity and robust numerical investigation of coupled thermal, geochemical, and mechanical processes is developed.

The above integration is essential because a dry-out zone can be formed due to the water vaporization in high-velocity regions, such as around the CO2 injector and along the fracture planes, resulting in the salt deposition that can affect the well injectivity6−10 and the flow into the fractures, closing them as a self-sealing mechanism. Another possible self-sealing mechanism is due to secondary mineral precipitation in the fracture apertures. For instance, several authors have discussed Dawsonite precipitation in fractures, since analogues of Dawsonite-bearing carbonate veins can be found in outcrops.11−15 According to the numerical study by Xu et al.,16 Dawsonite is the leading cement mineral that can be formed during CO2 injection.

Several numerical studies have been conducted to evaluate Dawsonite precipitation.16−18 Still, there needs to be more assessment considering naturally fractured media and a full coupling to the saline scale, e.g., evaporite mineral scale (such as halite) and other mineral deposition, because those cited works only consider matrix models and do not mention the saline scale. Patil et al.19 proposed to evaluate self-sealing in geological faults, but their approach to model the fractures is simplified, assuming the entire damaged zone as an effective porous medium, e.g., they did not model individual fractures. In contrast, the core idea and main focus of this study is to model individual fractures. The aim of this study is to conduct a novel and in-depth analysis of the self-sealing potential of fractures embedded in the matrix rock, evaluating Dawsonite and halite as the main self-sealing minerals, since, according to experimental evidence,20−22 geochemical interactions coupled with fracture flow effects have implications for reservoir permeability and sealing formations. Regarding the geochemistry, a thorough investigation into the influence of initial pH on mineral reactions will be investigated and compared with lab experiments, particularly the precipitation of Dawsonite in alkaline environments.23,24

Depending on the fracture aperture, CO2 can develop high velocities, and non-Darcy effects must be considered, especially if the injector well crosses the fracture plane. As kinetic parameters will be controlling the reactions, accurate calculation of the CO2 velocity is mandatory for the mineral dissolution and precipitation. Although most of the experimental data about non-Darcy effects in gas–liquid systems consider nitrogen and water, they represent CO2–brine systems, particularly those that include the impact of mobile liquid saturation.25,26 Several authors developed correlations for the Forchheimer coefficient to match lab data.27,28 Mijic29 evaluated the correlations and proposed Wu’s28 equation (eq 1) as the most suitable for gas–liquid systems, considering the mobile liquid phase1where ke is the CO2 effective permeability, ϕ is the gridblock porosity, and Sw is the water saturation. Therefore, this paper establishes an in-depth numerical analysis of the mineralogical and geochemical phenomena involved in the storage of CO2 in carbonate rocks regarding fracture opening and sealing. The main goal is to enhance the understanding of the effects of CO2 injection on open fractures, including the effects of saline scale and mineral precipitation, reducing the fracture apertures and the effects of mineral dissolution, such as calcite and dolomite, which can increase the fracture’s apertures. This relevant study can provide significant insights into potential CCS in carbonate saline aquifers by modeling the trapping mechanisms and mapping the risks of this storage strategy.

Geological Modeling

Two patterns of natural fractures were assumed in this study. The first one represents a case with a high density of fractures (358 fractures) and will be called a “high-density case.” This is a high-resolution model from the Arapuá limestone outcrop in the Potiguar Basin in Brazil, generated by Quadros32,33 and modeled by Machado et al.34 A simplified version with a lower density of fractures (13 fractures) was created and represented as the second pattern (“low-density case”). Both models are fine grid models with gridblock sizes of 10 m × 10 m horizontally and 0.1 m thick, meaning a small area mapped in that actual outcrop.

Figures 1 and 2 show the two patterns of fractures assumed in this study.

High-density fractures with different aperture values (variable apertures - 10–5 to 10–4 m, as shown in meters in the color bar). Cartesian grid discretization: 30 × 20 × 30.

Low-density fractures with a single aperture value of 1.4 × 10–4 m. Cartesian grid discretization: 30 × 20 × 30.

In the cases of “high-density” and “low-density,” the matrix rock was assumed to be homogeneous with a horizontal permeability of 10 mD. The average permeability contrast between fracture and matrix grids is about 100 times. Hence, these cases can be classified as typical Type-2 naturally fractured aquifers, according to Nelson,35 where the fractures play an essential role in the flow, enhancing the transmissibility of the system. A vertical well is located in the center of the model (red circles in Figures 1 and 2).

An additional synthetic 2D model (called the “synthetic model”) of a saline aquifer was also built with homogeneous petrophysical properties (a porosity of 30% and a horizontal permeability of 10 mD) to test methodologies for incorporating the geochemical reactions. This model contains a vertical injector perforated in the last three bottom layers without fractures. Figure 3 shows the model size, discretized in gridblock lengths of 10 m × 10 m × 5 m. The discretization of the models used in this study has been previously evaluated5,36 and found to be suitable for representing reactive transport.

Results and Discussion

Investigations Using the Synthetic Model Dawsonite and Halite Precipitation under pH Influence.

According to Hellevang et al.,23 most Dawsonite occurrences are associated with alkaline conditions favorable with the abundance of CO32– ions and the increase of aluminum (Al) solubility in brine. Beyond the carbonate ion, to generate Dawsonite, a source of sodium and hydroxyl must exist, which comes from the brine as well as from CO32–. The main question remains about the Al source, which the dissolution of K-feldspar, albite, or kaolinite can provide.

In order to test if our model can predict the mechanism mentioned above, the “synthetic model” was used at three different initial pH values based on the data from Brazilian aquifers: an acidic pH of 5.7, an almost neutral pH of 6.8, and an alkaline pH of 7.7. A CO2 rate of 1 t/day was injected over 30 years (from 2000 to 2030). In the models, the maximum bottom hole pressure (BHP) is restricted to the maximum fracture gradient, as mentioned earlier in the Introduction, at 0.7 psi/ft (equivalent to a BHP constraint of 16 MPa). To ensure fair comparisons among the different scenarios, a consistent CO2 mass of 1 t per day was chosen, ensuring that the same quantity of CO2 was injected across all pH scenarios. Figure 5 shows no Dawsonite precipitation formation in acidic and neutral pH (green curve) in 50 years, in agreement with the conclusions from the lab experiments carried out by Hellevang et al.24 at the pH range of 0.9–5, where the Dawsonite dissolution rate is faster. However, in the alkaline pH, it is remarkable that the increase of Dawsonite (green curve) from the early years is associated with a decrease in the K-feldspar (purple curve), the primary source of aluminum, as also observed in the study by Hellevang et al.17Figure 5 with pH 7.7 still shows that Dawsonite growth is delayed due to K-feldspar dissolution, confirming the relationship between these minerals.

Different mineral concentrations (in gmol) for three initial pH values over 50 years.

Another potential mechanism for fracture sealing is water vaporization due to the higher water solubility in the CO2 phase during ScCO2 injection. It can lead to salt (halite, in this study) precipitation, hence reducing the porosity and permeability of both matrix and fracture. This mechanism depends on the solubility between CO2 and brine, which is lower at higher pH. Therefore, this explains the absence of halite precipitation (yellow curve) at alkaline pH 7.7 in Figure 5. The halite scale thus can play the role of a self-sealing mechanism at lower pHs when the Dawsonite precipitation does not occur.

Figure 6 illustrates the pH evolution over the years, considering the three different cases of initial pH. All cases present the trend to reduce the pH due to CO2 injection. The reduction is more intense near the injector, but in average numbers, as shown in Figure 6, the changes are smoother, since the entire model was considered for this calculation.

Long-Term Evaluation

Over 500 years of simulations, as shown in Figure 9, the reduction in the K-feldspar concentration and its relationship to the Dawsonite increase as a new mineral in porous media are more evident. Despite numerical instabilities experienced in these simulations, a remarkable plateau of 10,000 gmol of dawsonite was observed starting the year 2300 or after 300 years from the start of the simulation, corresponding to a mass of 1.4 tons or a volume of 0.3 m3 (in a pore volume of 1.8 × 105 m3), highlighting the stability of the Dawsonite precipitated, as already observed in Xu et al.16 This same paper noted that Dawsonite precipitates over the entire simulation time, in agreement with our results. Other authors also found this behavior.23 In addition, Dawsonite precipitation is commonly observed in outcrops that are associated with past percolation of CO2-rich flow.13,14 This occurrence demonstrates the stability of Dawsonite under certain conditions that successfully reproduced the long-term stability of Dawsonite over hundreds of years, as depicted in Figure 9. Demonstrating that our model can reproduce the stable presence of Dawsonite over an extended period reinforces the robustness of these findings and provides further confidence in the potential of Dawsonite as a stable mineral phase in carbon capture and storage scenarios.

Mineral concentrations (in gmol) for three initial pH values over 500 years.

Dawsonite Precipitation under Temperature Influence

The Dawsonite growth is improved by more than 2 orders of magnitude at a higher initial aquifer temperature of 100 °C (double the base case) because of the increase in precipitation reaction rate. This observation is in agreement with other numerical and lab works17,24 and highlights the potential of Dawsonite precipitation in high-temperature aquifers, as it can happen in carbonate reservoirs. Figure 10 presents the results of the sensitivity study, showcasing the changes in mineral concentration over the injection period. The first row corresponds to the base case at 50 °C, while the second row represents the case at 100 °C. Even at higher temperatures, no Dawsonite was observed in acidic pH (left side of Figure 10).

From this partial conclusion obtained with the “synthetic case,” the next section will address the coupling of the geochemical modeling in a fractured aquifer, including an investigation of the non-Darcy effect on the mineral reactions. Our primary concern is evaluating the effectiveness of halite and Dawsonite precipitation versus calcite dissolution on fracture properties, especially their initial permeabilities.

Results in the Fractured Models

Non-Darcy Effect

As mentioned in the Introduction, CO2 can develop high velocities, and non-Darcy effects must be considered, especially if the injector well crosses the fracture plane. As kinetic parameters will be controlling the reactions, accurate calculation of CO2 velocity is mandatory for mineral dissolution and precipitation. Figure 11 shows the impact on calcite dissolution when the non-Darcy effect is modeled or neglected for two different injection rates in the high-density case. Due to these results, all subsequent simulations will include the Forchheimer correction (eq 1) for non-Darcy flow (blue curves in Figure 11) to ensure more accurate and reliable results.

Low-Density Case

Considering the above geochemical reactions in an initial pH = 7.7 and Forcheheimer correction, the calcite dissolution was evaluated with (Figure 12A) and without (Figure 12B) halite and Dawsonite precipitations for 10 years shut-in after 1% PV CO2 injection. The evaluation time of 10 years after the injection stops, which was selected for this study, was based on previous findings that indicated notable mineral changes during this time frame. Additionally, this evaluation time aligns with the monitoring phase of a real-field CCS project. Figure 12 shows the fracture gridblocks represented as non-neighbor connections according to the EDFM formulation. The connections between these gridblocks and the matrix are controlled by transmissibility terms computed during the preprocessing step and imposed as input to the numerical simulator.

% change in permeability after 1% PV CO2 injection, considering only calcite dissolution (A) and calcite dissolution and halite and Dawsonite reactions (B) at an acidic pH of 7.7.

From Figure 12A, one can observe the percent permeability change due to the calcite dissolution mainly concentrated in the perforated gridblocks of the injection well (in the bottom layers) and around the well in the top layer, reaching 11% permeability enhancement. On average, the matrix permeability was increased by 2.3% from its original value. When halite and Dawsonite scales are modeled in Figure 12B, the self-sealing effect by these minerals is partially compensated by the permeability enhancement from the calcite dissolution. The average permeability gain dropped from 2.3% to only 0.34% in the matrix, thus preserving the original fracture permeability. The results demonstrate that a natural mechanism can occur to reduce the risk of fracture opening induced by CO2 injection during CCS operations.

High-Density Case

A similar investigation was performed in the high-density case for two initial pH values. Figure 13 shows the acidic pH of 5.7, highlighting the halite deposition in the dry-out zone around the well, concentrated only in the matrix at this time (when the injection is halted). The damage by the halite precipitation offsets the permeability gain due to the calcite dissolution in the matrix, as can be observed in the third image, which shows no permeability alteration around the well. However, some enhancement (almost 1% on average) in the fracture permeability can be observed due to calcite dissolution. 10 years after the injection stops (second column with values in Table 5), the increases in the fracture permeability are reduced from 1 to 0.03%, e.g., both matrix and fracture recover their original permeability. If halite and Dawsonite precipitations are not modeled (first column with values in Table 5), the calcite dissolution leads to a permeability gain in the matrix and fractures of 1.7 and 5% in 10 years after the injection stops, corresponding to 1 or 5% PV injected, respectively.

If an initial alkaline pH is assumed, the fracture permeability is reduced to −0.7% @ 1% PV injected and to −2% @ 5% PV due to Dawsonite precipitation. However, fractures recover their original permeability within 10 years after the injection is stopped as a result of calcite dissolution in the fractures (second column with values in Table 5). Still, in Table 5 (first column with values), fractures enhance their initial permeability by 2.2% after 10 years of injection for the case without the Dawsonite reactions.

The results show that it is possible to offset calcite dissolution, particularly in fractures, considering other mechanisms that naturally can seal or prevent opening them. The focus of this research was on a typical Type-2 carbonate rock scenario, which is commonly encountered worldwide.35 In this type of rock, fractures play a significant role in fluid propagation compared to the matrix. It is worth noting that the average permeability contrast between fractures and matrix was assumed to be the same in both cases, with a contrast of 100 times.

To further advance our understanding, future evaluations should be conducted to examine self-sealing mechanisms in carbonate rocks where fractures have less importance (Type-3) or greater significance (Type-1) in terms of fluid flow. In order to simulate these scenarios, a sensitivity analysis was carried out by reducing the initial permeability contrast to 10 to mimic a Type-3 rock and increasing it to 1000 for a Type-1 rock in a low-density case with an initial alkaline pH.

This additional investigation, summarized in Table 6, provided valuable insights into the effectiveness of self-sealing mechanisms under different fracture–matrix permeability ratios. It was observed that the potential for self-sealing in carbonate-fractured reservoirs remains significant, particularly when fractures play a more prominent role in fluid flow within porous media.
