Clinically significant portal hypertension assessed by spleen stiffness measurement and its associated outcomes among people living with HIV/AIDS
Authors: Worapong Nasomsong, Patsuda Laukongtam, Sakkarin Chirapongsathorn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeople living with HIV/AIDS (PLWHA) are at increased risk of developing portal hypertension, characterized by higher spleen stiffness. This study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of elevated spleen stiffness measurement (SSM) using vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE), to explore its associated factors, and to assess its potential role in identifying clinically significant portal hypertension among PLWHA. This prospective cross-sectional study evaluated predictors of clinically significant portal hypertension using SSM and liver stiffness measurement (LSM) performed by VCTE. Participants included two groups: 90 PLWHA and 30 healthy controls. Exclusion criteria included baseline active opportunistic infections, cirrhosis, active alcohol consumption and HBV/HCV. Demographic, biochemical, and ultrasonographic data were collected. SSM ≥ 21 kPa was used as a threshold to screen for clinically significant portal hypertension. Screening for high-risk esophageal varices was conducted using esophagogastroduodenoscopy among PLWHA with SSM ≥ 21 kPa. Follow-up data were collected to assess the occurrence of portal hypertension-related complications. A total of 120 participants were analyzed (PLWHA: n = 90, controls: n = 30). SSM and LSM values were significantly higher among PLWHA compared with that of controls. In this study, 33 participants—29 (32.2%) in the PLWHA group and 4 (13.3%) in the control group—had SSM ≥ 21 kPa, suggesting a risk of clinically significant portal hypertension (CSPH). None of these patients exhibited high risk esophageal varices on endoscopy. During a median follow-up of 15 (IQR: 13,16) months, no PLWHA developed liver decompensation or other portal hypertension-related complications. In univariable analysis, PLWHA had significantly higher odds of elevated spleen stiffness compared with controls (odds ratio 3.09, p = 0.045). However, in multivariable logistic regression, no factors were significantly associated with elevated SSM. Controlled Attenuation Parameter (CAP) did not differ significantly between PLWHA and controls. However, higher BMI was associated with increased CAP values, with median BMI values of 24.66 kg/m² (IQR: 22.12, 28.34) and 28.91 kg/m² (IQR: 23.43, 30.81) for CAP ranges of 201–300 dB/m and 301–400 dB/m, respectively. The prevalence of elevated SSM, indicating a risk for developing CSPH as assessed by VCTE, was notable among PLWHA but did not correlate with high-risk varices or clinical decompensation over a median 15-month follow-up. SSM thresholds for CSPH in PLWHA using VCTE require further validation, and its potential role as a triage tool for non-NCPH warrants additional study.Keywords: People living with HIV/AIDS, PLWA, Spleen stiffness, Vibration-Controlled transient elastographySubject terms: Medical research, Outcomes research

Introduction

Noncirrhotic portal hypertension (NCPH) poses a potential risk of developing complications such as variceal bleeding, a critical and life-threatening condition1,2. People living with HIV/AIDS (PLWHA) have been reported to develop NCPH even in the absence of viral hepatitis co-infection3–6. The mechanisms linking HIV to NCPH remain unclear but may involve antiretroviral therapy (ART) toxicity7. Exposure to didanosine (DDI) and stavudine (d4T) has been associated with significantly increased liver and spleen stiffness and a higher risk of NCPH among PLWHA2,8,9. A related study investigating PLWHA with elevated aminotransferases or unexplained liver fibrosis found that liver biopsies identified cases of nodular hyperplasia and periportal fibrosis. Some patients did not exhibit a notable increase in HIV RNA levels, while others presented a history of prolonged didanosine use for several years. After discontinuation of DDI, liver function improved7.

The definitive diagnosis of NCPH requires clinical signs, the exclusion of cirrhosis confirmed by liver biopsy and ruling out other potential causes such as chronic viral hepatitis and steatohepatitis. However, the invasive nature of liver biopsy makes diagnosing NCPH challenging, often resulting in underestimation of its true prevalence1. Using liver and spleen stiffness measurements with point shear wave elastography (pSWE) can help predict the early onset of NCPH, a condition that may eventually lead to end-stage liver disease10. Splenic stiffness measured by pSWE is highly accurate for diagnosing NCPH. A reference value of 25.4 kPa demonstrated a sensitivity of 91% and a specificity of 93%9. Additionally, the study revealed a correlation between elevated splenic stiffness and esophageal varices, suggesting that splenic stiffness measurement (SSM) could serve as a tool for predicting the future development of esophageal varices11.

SSM, performed using vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE), has emerged as a promising tool to evaluate portal hypertension and associated complications. SSM by VCTE is easier to perform as a point-of-care test in a digestive clinic. While pSWE has demonstrated high accuracy for diagnosing NCPH, with a reference value of 25.4 kPa achieving 91% sensitivity and 93% specificity, limited data are available concerning using SSM by VCTE to predict liver decompensation risk. Additionally, related studies have noted correlations between elevated splenic stiffness and the presence of esophageal varices, suggesting its potential as a predictive marker for variceal development performed using VCTE. However, no data are available regarding the magnitude of elevated SSM and its potential clinical implications in PLWHA. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of elevated SSM using VCTE, to explore its associated factors, and to assess its potential role in identifying clinically significant portal hypertension and predicting related complications in PLWHA.

Material and method

Study design

In this prospective cross-sectional study, participants were recruited from the outpatient clinic of internal medicine and the infectious disease department at Phramongkutklao Hospital, a 1200-bed teaching hospital in Bangkok, Thailand. The participants were PLWHA aged ≥ 20 years, receiving antiretroviral therapy, with an HIV-1 viral load suppressed to < 50 copies/mL for at least six months. The exclusion criteria included liver cirrhosis, hepatitis B and C, presence of HIV-related opportunistic infections, pregnancy, the presence of active implantable medical devices, active alcohol consumption up to two to three standard drinks daily (> 28 g/day) and active cancer. The controlled participants were healthy volunteers aged ≥ 20 years without liver cirrhosis, hepatitis B and C infection, active alcohol consumption up to two to three standard drinks daily (> 28 g/day), and active cancer. The primary outcomes included SSM and liver stiffness measurement (LSM) performed by VCTE among PLWHA and in the control group. The secondary outcome was the controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) an estimate of hepatic fat content measured during liver stiffness assessment with VCTE among PLWHA and in the control group. The demographic data included HIV treatment history, ART regimen, comorbidities and laboratory results were retrieved from electronic medical records.

Elastography measurement

The elastography measurement of spleen and liver stiffness using VCTE [FibroScan 630 Expert®], was performed by a well-trained medical technologist. The measurement procedure requires fasting for at least two hours before the examination. An M-sized probe is used for measurements, while an XL-sized probe is employed for individuals with a BMI greater than 30 kg/m². Measurements are taken with the patient in the supine position, at the mid-axillary line intersecting an imaginary line at the xiphoid process. A minimum of ten measurements is conducted to achieve a success rate of at least 60%, with a required interquartile range (IQR) less than 30% (IQR/M ≤ 0.30). The previous study evaluated SSM using pSWE to predict non-cirrhotic portal hypertension in PLWHA and demonstrated a cut-off value of 25.4 kPa, with 91% sensitivity and 93% specificity9. In contrast, real-world data using VCTE across various liver disease spectra reported a cut-off value of > 26.5 kPa, with 83% sensitivity and 82% specificity for detecting CSPH12. Given the very limited data on SSM cut-off values in PLWHA, particularly with VCTE, the available evidence suggests that reported cut-offs in PLWHA are consistent with those established in the general population. Therefore, we considered using this cut-off value in the following criteria. The SSM value indicates the presence of clinically significant portal hypertension (CSPH): SSM < 21 kPa indicates no risk of developing CSPH, 21 to 50 kPa indicates a risk of developing CSPH, and > 50 kPa indicates the presence of CSPH. The SSM, LSM and CAP results adhere to the guidelines established in Baveno VII - Renewing Consensus in Portal Hypertension13. Participants with an SSM > 21 kPa were offered screening for high risk esophageal varices (EV) using esophagogastroduodenoscopy. High-risk EVs are defined as medium or large varices, or small varices with red wale marks13. As platelet data were unavailable, the agreement analysis between SSM-based referral and Baveno-based noninvasive criteria for endoscopy could not be performed. The CAP was recorded and calculated. The cutoff value of ≥ 294 dB/m was used to distinguish steatosis of at least S1 in hepatic steatosis14.

Ethics statement

This study was conducted according to Good Clinical Practice Guidelines and followed the approved protocol, including any subsequent amendments, as reviewed and sanctioned by the Institutional Review Board, of the Royal Thai Army Medical Department (www.irbrta-research.com reference number R106h/63). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics, SSM, LSM and CAP were reported among all participants. Analyses were performed using complete case data, participants with missing values for relevant variables e.g. SSM value were excluded from the all analyses. Continuous data were reported in median with interquartile range, while categorical data were reported in number (percentage). Differences between baseline characteristics in both groups were calculated using the Chi square or Fischer’s exact test for categorical data and the Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous data. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05. The univariable and multivariable logistic regression model was used to obtain odds ratio with 95% CI for factors associated with high splenic stiffness. The covariates included in the multivariate analysis were age, sex, HIV status, underlying diseases, LSM, BMI, and CAP values. Sensitivity analysis was used to assess the robustness of the findings; analyses were stratified by BMI category to examine whether the associations between LSM and SSM differed across BMI strata. Correlations among SSM, LSM, BMI, and CAP were calculated using Spearman’s correlation coefficients. All statistical analyses were calculated using IBM SPSS, Version 27.

Result

Baseline characteristics

In this study, a total of 122 participants were enrolled; two in the PLWHA group were excluded due to unreliable SSM measurements (both participants underwent 10 measurements, with IQR/M values of 0.45 and 0.58). Among the 120 participant, 90 were identified as PLWHA, while 30 were designated as the control group. The median age for the PLWHA group was 51 years (IQR: 43, 56.25), which was higher than the control group’s median age of 30 years (IQR: 28.75, 44.75). The ART regimen among PLWHA primarily comprised integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI) based regimen, followed by non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI)- and protease inhibitor (PI)-based regimens. Six participants (6.67%) presented history of DDI exposure, while 15 participants (16.7%) presented history of d4T exposure. PLWHA had a higher prevalence of comorbidities compared with that of the control group, with the most common being dyslipidemia (56.7%), hypertension (31.1%) and diabetes mellitus (16.7%). Other factors including sex, BMI, liver stiffness and splenic stiffness, showed no statistically significant differences between the PLWHA and control groups (Table 1). Splenic and liver stiffness value

In the PLWHA group, none of the participants had an SSM > 50. SSM values between 21 and 50 were found among 29 of 90 participants (32.2%) in the PLWHA group, while only 4 of 30 (13.3%) participants in the control group had these values. A high proportion of fibrosis in stages F2 and F3 observed among PLWHA as shown in Table 1. No significant difference was found in demographic characteristics within the PLWHA group between the high and normal SSM value groups, as shown in Table 2. Interestingly, the duration of ART use tended to be longer, and the median CD4 + T cell count tended to be lower in the high SSM value group, but these differences were not statistically significant. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess robustness, stratifying participants by BMI (normal < 25 kg/m²; overweight 25–30 kg/m²; obese > 30 kg/m²). Interaction terms between BMI and LSM were included to test effect modification. The association between LSM and SSM was consistent across BMI categories: OR 1.29 (95% CI, 0.90–1.83; p = 0.16; n = 83) for BMI < 25, OR 1.12 (95% CI, 0.59–2.15; p = 0.73; n = 28) for BMI 25–30, and not estimable in the obese group (n = 9) due to small sample size. Overall, no significant effect modification by BMI was observed.

Controlled Attenuation parameter (CAP)

Using a CAP value of ≥ 294 dB/m to identify steatosis of at least S1, 8 of 120 participants (6.7%) exceeded the cutoff including 4/90 (4.4%) in the PLWHA group and 4/30 (13.3%) in the control group. Participants with a CAP value of ≥ 294 dB/m had a median age of 36.5 years (IQR: 29–51.25), with the majority being male (87.5%). One half of these participants showed comorbidities, most commonly hypertension (25%) and dyslipidemia (25%). Additionally, they exhibited a significant difference in BMI [28.9 (IQR: 22.9, 31.0) vs. 23.18 (IQR: 20.4, 26.2) kg/m², p = 0.026] and LSM [LSM < 6.5; 3/8 (37.5%) vs. 86/112 (76.8%), p = 0.002]. However, other parameters, including sex, comorbidities, HIV status and SSM, did not differ significantly, as presented in Table 4.

Correlation between SSM, LSM, BMI, and CAP

SSM demonstrated a weak positive correlation with LSM (ρ = 0.15, 95% CI − 0.03 to 0.33, p = 0.094), but negligible correlations with BMI (ρ = 0.11, 95% CI − 0.18 to 0.18, p = 0.996) and CAP (ρ = − 0.004, 95% CI − 0.22 to 0.14, p = 0.654). LSM showed a modest positive correlation with CAP (ρ = 0.22, 95% CI 0.035–0.39, p = 0.017). The strongest correlation was observed between BMI and CAP (ρ = 0.56, 95% CI 0.42–0.67, p < 0.001). Evidence of esophageal varices among participants with high Splenic stiffness value

Among the 29 PLWHA with splenic stiffness values ≥ 21 kPa, 16 participants consented to and underwent esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD), and none exhibited esophageal varices. The median time from SSM to perform EGD was 13.32 (IQR: 9.27, 15.71) months. All participants were followed up for a median of 15 months, with no loss to follow-up observed. Despite the absence of repeated SSM and LSM measurements, no cases of esophageal varices or clinically evident liver cirrhosis were detected during the follow-up.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study used vibration-controlled, transient elastography, a rapid, non-invasive, and highly accurate method for diagnosing NCPH, to screen SSM and LSM values among PLWHA and controls. The prevalence of elevated SSM values among PLWHA in this study was 32.2%, compared with 13.3% in the control group. All participants with elevated SSM values were classified within the 21 to 50 kPa range, indicating a risk of developing CSPH; none had definite CSPH. The LSM values in PLWHA also demonstrated a higher proportion of fibrosis in stages F2, and F3 compared with that of the control group. However, multivariable analysis did not identify any significant factors associated with elevated SSM. Despite the fact that PLWHA in this cohort had been receiving ART for more than ten years, ART toxicities previously reported as risk factors, including DDI and d4T, did not show a significant difference between those with normal and high SSM values1,2,8,9. This finding is possibly due to the very low proportion of PLWHA exposed to DDI and d4T and the short duration of exposure, as the Thai national HIV treatment guidelines have not recommended these drugs for over a decade, leading to their discontinuation in Thailand15. Moreover, most PLWHA in our study could manage their HIV status. Stable HIV may also contribute to better outcomes in liver disease. Proper management of HIV could potentially reduce complications and improve overall liver health among affected individuals.

Hepatic steastosis is also a significant finding among PLWHA in both obese and lean phenotypes, particularly those receiving INSTI-based ART. Metabolic dysfunction associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) can contribute to significant liver fibrosis and portal hypertension16–18. Therefore, we conducted additional assessment of the CAP value, a non-invasive evaluation of hepatic steatosis19,20. Using a cutoff value of ≥ 294 dB/m, demonstrating a sensitivity of 79% and a specificity of 74% to detect significant steatosis (at least S1), the overall prevalence was 6.7%14. A significant proportion of elevated CAP values was observed, predominantly among middle-aged males with a high BMI. MASLD may be a potential confounder for LSM and possibly for SSM, either through splenic congestion or measurement challenges related to obesity.

NCPH has several sequelae, such as splenomegaly, portal venous congestion and tissue hyperplasia, that may contribute to increased splenic stiffness and therefore progress to cirrhosis, which is often asymptomatic but can manifest as upper gastrointestinal bleeding in decompensated cases1,2. Given the nearly asymptomatic nature of early-stage disease, screening for portal hypertension using non-invasive measurements is a promising strategy for the early diagnosis and management of portal hypertension including NCPH21–24. SSM using pSWE was first evaluated among PLWHA and demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy9. The optimal SSM threshold for PLWHA is not well established due to limited data and may differ from that in other cirrhotic populations12. Additionally, SSM showed significant value in predicting esophageal varices from various etiologies11,25. However, using pSWE may be difficult to perform in the liver unit due to the technique. The pSWE also provides measurements from a small region of interest (ROI), which may not fully represent heterogeneous liver diseases such as focal lesions or patchy fibrosis. Moreover, different manufacturers and ultrasound machines may have variations in stiffness measurements, making standardization challenging.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to conduct splenic stiffness screening using VCTE among PLWHA, which demonstrated a high prevalence of elevated SSM values. We demonstrated the feasibility of using VCTE, accompanied by LSM and CAP, to screen for NCPH among PLWHA. Additionally, EGD screening data were collected from participants with high SSM values. In our study EGD was performed in more than one half of PLWHA with high SSM value; no esophageal varices presented. VCTE offers advantages over pSWE for assessing splenic stiffness including a larger and more consistent region of interest, better standardization and reduced operator dependency. Its automated measurements and established role in evaluating portal hypertension make it a more reliable tool, particularly in challenging cases such as obesity or poor acoustic windows. However, our study encountered limitations. Firstly, we did not confirm the definitive diagnosis of nodular regenerative hyperplasia, leading to NCPH, through liver biopsy or did not confirm the hepatic venous pressure gradient. However, we excluded other common etiologies of portal hypertension and cirrhosis including viral hepatitis, alcohol and MASLD. Secondly, not all participants with high SSM values underwent EGD due to participant preference, but we conducted a 15-month follow-up for all participants and did not observe any new diagnoses of esophageal varices or liver cirrhosis. Thirdly, due to the cross-sectional study design with a short follow-up period, and the absence of clinical events observed during follow-up in this cohort, the study may have limited power to detect clinical outcomes. A longer follow-up over several years may yield different results. Finally, the study was conducted at a single tertiary center, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Therefore, the results should be interpreted cautiously and compared with those from other cohorts. Despite these limitations, this study provides important information and raises clinical implications for splenic stiffness screening among PLWHA.

Conclusion

Elevated SSM was more common among PLWHA than in controls, with nearly one-fourth of PLWHA in this cohort exhibiting significant splenic stiffness. However, this finding did not correspond to the presence of high-risk varices or clinical decompensation over a median follow-up of 15 months. SSM thresholds for CSPH in PLWHA, particularly when measured using VCTE, require further validation, and the potential role of SSM as a triage tool for non-NCPH in this population should also be further investigated.
