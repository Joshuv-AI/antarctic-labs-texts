Predictors and Clinical Outcomes of Ciprofloxacin Resistance in Acute Gram-Negative Osteomyelitis: Insights from a Six-Year Retrospective Study
Authors: Rania A El-Kady, Ahd A Mansour, Mohamed Y Yousef
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCiprofloxacin resistance among Gram-negative bacteria causing osteomyelitis is an emerging clinical concern that may negatively influence disease severity and patient outcomes. This study sought to assess the burden, independent predictors, and clinical impact of ciprofloxacin resistance in acute Gram-negative osteomyelitis.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on patients with acute Gram-negative osteomyelitis between 2019–2024. Clinical, microbiological, laboratory, and outcome variables were compared between ciprofloxacin-resistant and susceptible cohorts. Univariable binary logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with ciprofloxacin resistance, followed by multivariable binary logistic regression analysis to identify independent predictors. Statistical significance was considered at p-value <0.05 (two-tailed).ResultsAntimicrobial resistance patterns were assessed among 279 Gram-negative isolates, while clinical and outcome analyses were conducted among 256 patients. The overall prevalence of ciprofloxacin resistance was 54.5% among Gram-negative isolates. In the univariable binary logistic regression analysis, ciprofloxacin resistance was significantly associated with chronic heart disease (p = 0.03), lower hemoglobin concentration (p = 0.04), hypoalbuminemia (p = 0.0001), and increased inflammatory biomarkers, including c-reactive protein (CRP; p = 0.001), and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR; p = 0.04). Osteomyelitis caused by ESBL-producing pathogens was also significantly associated with ciprofloxacin resistance (OR = 3.93, 95% CI: 1.87–8.31; p = 0.0001). In the multivariable analysis, hypoalbuminemia (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 0.53, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.35–0.79; p = 0.002) and infection with ESBL-producing pathogens (AOR = 0.25, 95% CI: 0.11–0.54; p = 0.0001) remained independently associated with ciprofloxacin resistance. Compared with ciprofloxacin-susceptible cohort, ciprofloxacin-resistant patients required significantly more surgical debridement (p = 0.006) and developed higher rates of chronic osteomyelitis (p = 0.005), septic shock (p = 0.03), and in-hospital mortality (p = 0.02).ConclusionCiprofloxacin resistance was prevalent among Gram-negative osteomyelitis cases and was associated with greater surgical complexity and poorer clinical outcomes. Early identification of ciprofloxacin-resistant infections and integration of clinical and inflammatory markers may support risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making. Given the retrospective, observational, single-center design, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and validated in larger multicenter studies.Keywords: gram-negative osteomyelitis, ciprofloxacin resistance, risk factors, clinical outcomes, in-hospital mortality, biomarkers

Introduction

Acute osteomyelitis is a severe and rapidly progressing infection of the bone or bone marrow, that usually develops over a period of days to less than two weeks.1 It can arise following hematogenous spread of infection from a distant focus, direct inoculation of the infectious agent during penetrating trauma or surgery, or extension from contagious infected tissues, while in some cases no clear primary source can be determined.2 In children, the infection is primarily acquired via hematogenous seeding, whereas in adults it is more likely to result from direct implantation or local spread from adjacent tissues.3

The incidence of acute osteomyelitis varies greatly according to patient characteristics, geographical region, and availability of adequate healthcare.4 Infected patients often experience sudden onset of localized pain, swelling, warmth, and redness. This may be accompanied with fever and fatigue. However, these symptoms may differ widely, making timely and accurate diagnosis difficult.5 In the early stages of acute osteomyelitis, necrotic bone (sequestrum) is typically not yet present on imaging or histopathologic examination, providing a critical window for treatment prior to irreversible ischemic damage.6

The microbiological spectrum of acute osteomyelitis depends on patient age, route of infection, and underlying host conditions. Staphylococcus aureus is the most commonly implicated organism, accounting for up to 60% of osteomyelitis cases across all age groups.7 In adults, Gram-negative bacilli, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Acinetobacter baumannii, are increasingly recognized as causative agents, especially among certain patient populations.8 Acute osteomyelitis may also occasionally be polymicrobial, notably in the setting of trauma or open injuries.9

The growing burden of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), particularly among Gram-negative bacilli, has become one of the most pressing public health issues that compromises treatment outcomes and increases the potential for adverse events.10 This concern is exemplified by ciprofloxacin, once considered a backbone antibiotic for the treatment of Gram-negative osteomyelitis due to superior oral bioavailability and excellent bone penetration.11 However, accumulating evidence indicates declining ciprofloxacin efficacy, primarily against infections caused by multidrug-resistant (MDR) organisms such as P. aeruginosa and K. pneumoniae.12

Previous epidemiological studies have mainly described AMR patterns in osteomyelitis, with limited focus on ciprofloxacin resistance and its associated clinical and microbiological factors in acute Gram-negative osteomyelitis. Furthermore, the potential impact of ciprofloxacin resistance on surgical management and clinical outcomes has not been adequately characterized, particularly in Saudi Arabia. To address this gap, we conducted a six-year retrospective study aiming to investigate: (1) the prevalence of ciprofloxacin resistance among Gram-negative isolates recovered from adult patients with acute osteomyelitis, (2) factors independently associated with ciprofloxacin resistance, and (3) the consequences of ciprofloxacin resistance on clinical outcomes.

Patients and Methods

Ethics Approval and Consent Statement

The study followed the principles outlined in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. The study design was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Dr. Soliman Fakeeh Hospital (DSFH), Jeddah, Saudi Arabia (Decision No. 532/IRB/2023). The need for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified dataset. Patient data privacy and confidentiality were respected in all stages of the study.

Study Design and Setting

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted at the Department of Orthopedic and Spine Surgery, DSFH, between January 2019 to December 2024. DSFH is a private, 500-bed tertiary healthcare facility in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. The institution provides a wide range of advanced medical and surgical care to patients from a broad demographic background, including the management of infectious diseases and orthopedic conditions. Patients were recruited from both inpatient visits and outpatient clinic visits within the department during the study period.

Study Population

Inclusion Criteria

Patients were eligible to inclusion if they fulfilled the following criteria: (1) adult (≥18 years), (2) confirmed diagnosis of acute osteomyelitis based on clinical, radiological, and microbiological evidence, and (3) positive culture for Gram-negative bacteria obtained from intra-operative bone or deep tissue samples.

Exclusion Criteria

Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) age <18 years, (2) pure Gram-positive osteomyelitis, (3) culture-negative osteomyelitis, (4) fungal infection, (5) incomplete clinical or laboratory data, or (6) loss to follow-up before completion of the 12-months post-treatment evaluation, which was considered the minimum period for outcome assessment. For patients with multiple episodes or microbiological samples during the study period, only the first eligible episode of acute osteomyelitis was considered for patient-level clinical and statistical analyses, ensuring that each patient contributed one observation. All eligible Gram-negative isolates from these episodes were retained for isolate-level antimicrobial susceptibility analyses.

Data Extraction and Variables

We conducted a retrospective review of the hospital’s electronic health records of all adult patients (≥18 years old) diagnosed with acute osteomyelitis, using the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) codes. Eligible patients were identified and their medical record numbers (MRNs) were used to extract relevant data, including demographic characteristics (age, gender, nationality), body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), and comorbidities. Clinical characteristics included presenting symptoms and duration, anatomical site of osteomyelitis, and source of infection. Surgical interventions, including the number of debridement procedures and amputation, were abstracted. Clinical outcomes included hospital admission, length of stay (LOS), intensive care unit (ICU) admission, ICU duration, progression from acute to chronic osteomyelitis during follow-up, development of septic shock, and in-hospital mortality.

Laboratory parameters at admission were recorded, including white blood cell count (WBC; ×103/µL), absolute neutrophil count (ANC; ×103/µL), absolute lymphocyte count (ALC; ×103/µL), platelet count (×103/µL), hemoglobin concentration (g/dL), C-reactive protein (CRP; mg/L), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR; mm/h), albumin (g/dL), creatinine (mg/dL), and random blood glucose (RBG; mg/dL). The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), a derived inflammatory marker, was calculated as the ratio of the absolute neutrophil count to absolute lymphocyte count.

Microbiological Work-Up

Results of intra-operative bone and deep tissue cultures were retrieved from the electronic laboratory information system and reviewed retrospectively. Bacterial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) were conducted as part of routine patient management using standard laboratory protocols, in accordance with established guidelines. The VITEK TWO automated system (BioMérieux, Brazil) was used for identification and AST of the recovered isolates. Detection of extended-spectrum β-lactamase-(ESBL) producing organisms was also done using the same system. AST results were interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100, 34th edition (2024) updated breakpoints.13

Data Analysis at the Isolate and Patient Levels

Microbiological resistance patterns were analyzed at the isolate level, as some patients with polymicrobial osteomyelitis contributed more than one Gram-negative isolate. Accordingly, antimicrobial resistance rates were calculated using the total number of Gram-negative isolates (n = 279). In contrast, clinical characteristics, predictors of ciprofloxacin resistance, and clinical outcomes were analyzed at the patient level (n = 256), with each patient contributing one observation to the statistical analyses.

Definitions

Multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negative organisms were defined as isolates resistant to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial classes.14 Prior antibiotic exposure was defined as receipt of any systemic antimicrobial therapy within the 90 days preceding index culture collection,15 based on the available medical records. Chronic osteomyelitis was defined as a persistent bone infection characterized by symptoms lasting ≥6 weeks and associated with sequestrum formation and/or a history of discharging sinus.16

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software (IBM Corp., IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed variables were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). The independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test was used to compare continuous variables between two independent groups, as appropriate. The Pearson’s chi-squared (χ2) test was performed to determine associations between categorical variables, while Fisher’s exact test was applied when >20% of the expected cell counts were <5. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to measure the associations between variables and ciprofloxacin resistance.

Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with ciprofloxacin resistance. Univariable binary logistic regression analysis was initially conducted for each candidate factor. Variables with a p-value <0.10 in the univariable analysis, together with clinically relevant covariates, were considered for inclusion in the multivariable binary logistic regression model. Eligible variables were entered simultaneously into the multivariable model using the Enter method, with consideration of the number of outcome events to minimize the risk of overfitting. Potential multicollinearity among candidate predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance values. VIF values ranged from 1.006 to 1.155, while tolerance values ranged from 0.866 to 0.994, indicating no evidence of significant multicollinearity among the variables included in the final model. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Clinical outcomes were compared between ciprofloxacin-resistant and ciprofloxacin-susceptible groups using appropriate univariable statistical tests. Clinical and regression analyses were conducted at the patient level (n = 256), with one observation per patient, whereas antimicrobial resistance rates were calculated at the isolate level (n = 279). Therefore, multiple isolates from the same patient were not treated as independent observations in the regression analyses. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value <0.05.

Results

Study Population and Baseline Characteristics

After exclusion, a total of 256 adult patients with acute Gram-negative osteomyelitis were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). The mean age of the study cohort was 62.3 ± 15.9 years (range, 18–93 years), with a predominance of males (73.8% versus 26.2%). Saudi nationals accounted for the majority of the study population (73.4%). The mean BMI was 29.1 ± 7.4 kg/m2 (range, 14.1–68.6 kg/m2). Diabetes mellitus (DM) was the most frequent comorbidity (84%), followed by hypertension (56.3%), hyperlipidemia (44.5%), and chronic heart disease (CHD; 39.8%). Clinical Characteristics of Patients with Acute Osteomyelitis

Among the study cohort (n = 256), acute osteomyelitis predominantly affected the foot and ankle (62.1%), followed by long bones (18.8%), and multifocal sites (12.9%), whereas axial skeleton involvement (vertebrae and pelvic bones) was comparatively uncommon (6.3%). The primary mechanism of infection was contiguous spread (57.4%), followed by infections of undetermined source (21.9%) and hematogenous spread (12.1%), while post-traumatic and post-implant osteomyelitis accounted for 4.7% and 3.9% of cases, respectively. Pain with functional disability was the most commonly reported clinical presentation (25.8%), followed by swelling and erythema (23.4%), while fever was rare (3.1%). The average duration of illness was 7.9 ± 3.4 days (range, 2–13 days), with most patients presenting after 7 days or more of symptom onset (60.5%). The data are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Microbiological Profile of Patients with Acute Osteomyelitis

In this study, monomicrobial infections constituted the majority of cases (n = 209, 81.6%), while polymicrobial infections contributed to 47 cases (18.4%). Among the overall cohort of acute osteomyelitis (n = 256), P. aeruginosa was the leading organism of infection (n = 50, 19.5%), followed by K. pneumoniae (n = 45, 17.6%) and E. coli (n = 41, 16%). Other Gram-negative bacteria were less frequently identified, including Morganella morganii (n = 17, 6.6%), Acinetobacter baumannii (n = 14, 5.5%), and Enterobacter cloacae (n = 12, 4.7%), with the remaining species each occurring in <4% of cases (Figure 2).

In foot/ankle infection, polymicrobial growth was the most prevalent (n = 30, 18.9%), followed closely by P. aeruginosa (n = 29, 18.2%). Antibiotic Resistance Patterns of Gram-Negative Isolates Causing Acute Osteomyelitis

Because polymicrobial osteomyelitis yielded more than one organism, resistance analyses were performed at the isolate level using a total of 279 Gram-negative isolates rather than at the patient level. The highest resistance was found to ciprofloxacin (54.5%; n = 152), followed by ceftazidime (38%). Moderate resistance rates were observed for cefepime (24%) and gentamicin (23.7%), whereas 15.1% of the isolates demonstrated resistance to piperacillin/tazobactam. Carbapenems had relatively low resistance rates, with imipenem and meropenem showing resistance of 11.5% and 10%, respectively. Across all tested antibiotics, amikacin exhibited the lowest resistance rate (6.5%), as shown in Table 2.

It is noteworthy that 49 isolates (17.6%) were identified as ESBL producers, including 32 E. coli, 16 K. pneumoniae, and 1 Proteus mirabilis. Comparison of Baseline, Clinical, and Microbiological Characteristics by Ciprofloxacin Resistance

Baseline, clinical, and microbiological characteristics were compared between patients with ciprofloxacin-resistant (n = 142) and ciprofloxacin-susceptible (n = 114) acute Gram-negative osteomyelitis. Among all analyzed variables, CHD was significantly associated with ciprofloxacin resistance, with affected patients being 1.75 times more likely to develop ciprofloxacin resistance as compared with those without CHD (95% CI: 1.05–2.93; p = 0.03). Furthermore, osteomyelitis caused by ESBL-producing organisms was significantly associated with ciprofloxacin resistance (OR = 3.93, 95% CI: 1.87–8.31; p = 0.0001). Comparison of Laboratory and Inflammatory Parameters by Ciprofloxacin Resistance

Patients with ciprofloxacin-resistant osteomyelitis had distinct laboratory and inflammatory profiles compared to those with ciprofloxacin-susceptible infections (Table 4). For example, hemoglobin concentrations were significantly lower in the resistant group (10.6 versus 11 g/dL, p = 0.04). Similarly, serum albumin levels were significantly reduced in the resistant cohort (2.9 ± 0.7 versus 3.3 ± 0.7 g/dL, p = 0.0001). By contrast, CRP levels and NLR were significantly higher in the resistant patients (p = 0.001 and 0.04, respectively).

Clinical Outcomes Associated with Ciprofloxacin-Resistant Acute Gram-Negative Osteomyelitis

Most clinical outcomes were comparable between the ciprofloxacin-resistant and ciprofloxacin-susceptible groups, including hospital admission, LOS, ICU admission, and ICU duration. However, several poor outcomes were more frequent among patients with ciprofloxacin-resistant infection. For instance, the frequency of debridement was significantly higher among patients with ciprofloxacin resistance (2.6 ± 2.2 versus 1.9 ± 1.9, p = 0.006), though the cumulative debridement rate showed a borderline significance (p = 0.06). Likewise, amputation rates were more common in the resistant cohort; however, did not reach statistical significance (30.3% versus 20.2%, p = 0.06). Importantly, ciprofloxacin resistance was significantly associated with increased rates of chronicity (p = 0.005), septic shock (p = 0.03), and in-hospital mortality (p = 0.02). Discussion

Among 256 adult patients with acute osteomyelitis (mean age, 62.3 ± 15.9 years; 73.8% male), monomicrobial infections represented the vast majority of cases (81.6%). This finding aligns with other studies across different types of osteomyelitis, including diabetic foot and fracture-related infections.17,18 Although less common than monomicrobial osteomyelitis, polymicrobial disease constituted approximately one-fifth of cases in this cohort (18.4%). Within combined Gram-positive and Gram-negative infections, the MRSA–P. aeruginosa co-isolation pattern was the most frequently observed. This observation is biologically plausible, as both organisms have robust biofilm-forming ability, which promote their synergistic survival through enhanced adhesion, immune evasion, and reduced susceptibility to antimicrobial therapies.19 A comparable polymicrobial composition has been documented in a recent study of post-traumatic osteomyelitis from conflict-affected settings of Mosul and Gaza.20

The microbial landscape of acute osteomyelitis varied considerably according to the anatomical site and source of infection. The increased recovery of P. aeruginosa in foot/ankle (18.2%) and long-bone infections (27.1%) may reflect its marked environmental adaptability, biofilm-forming capacity, and propensity to colonize traumatized or relatively hypoxic tissues.21 These findings corroborate a previous report.22 In addition, the increased recovery of polymicrobial infections in foot/ankle (18.9%) underlines the microbiological complexity of infections in this anatomical site and the importance of comprehensive microbiological evaluation to optimize pathogen detection and antimicrobial therapy. The predominance of E. cloacae and P. aeruginosa in post-traumatic osteomyelitis further mirrors findings from a UK major trauma centre demonstrating a significant contribution of these organisms to fracture-related bone infections.23

The relatively high resistance to ciprofloxacin seen in this cohort (54.5%) indicates the growing therapeutic threat posed by Gram-negative osteomyelitis and may reflect the widespread use of fluoroquinolones in both community and healthcare settings. Similarly, a retrospective study of orthopedic infections reported ciprofloxacin resistance rates approaching 50% in Gram-negative isolates.24 Among ciprofloxacin-resistant organisms, E. coli accounted for the greatest proportion (27%), followed by K. pneumoniae (23.7%) and P. aeruginosa (17.1%). In contrast, the reduced resistance observed in other bacteria may be explained by their lower prevalence in this cohort and decreased antimicrobial selective pressure. Comparable trends of rising ciprofloxacin resistance in osteomyelitis and fracture-related infections have been shown in other studies.25,26

The substantially increased rate of ciprofloxacin resistance among MDR isolates (93%) implies a strong relationship between MDR and reduced susceptibility to fluoroquinolones. Recent evidence indicates that MDR Enterobacterales frequently harbor both quinolone resistance-determining region (QRDR) mutations and plasmid-mediated resistance determinants, thereby driving high-level ciprofloxacin resistance in osteomyelitis.27 Our findings are further supported by a recent multicenter study reporting that 61% of fluoroquinolone-resistant Gram-negative bacilli from osteoarticular infections were MDR.28

Ciprofloxacin had identical MIC50 and MIC90 values of 4 µg/mL, suggesting a uniformly decreased susceptibility among the Gram-negative isolates analyzed. This MIC level lies at or above the susceptibility breakpoint, raising therapeutic concerns in acute osteomyelitis, where sustained antimicrobial activity is required to ensure adequate bone penetration and eradication of infection. In this context, Gómez-Junyent et al observed that fluoroquinolone-resistant P. aeruginosa required 3–4×MIC exposure targets for effective treatment of osteoarticular infections.29 Among comparator antibiotics, carbapenems showed the lowest MIC distributions, indicating preserved activity against Enterobacterales and P. aeruginosa, including MDR strains. By contrast, piperacillin/tazobactam had a broad range of MIC values, indicating heterogenous susceptibility and unpredictable therapeutic response. Amikacin demonstrated relatively low MICs, suggesting sustained activity and potential use as an adjunct therapeutic option. Similar susceptibility profile has been described in a recent report of fracture-related infections, where carbapenems retained consistent activity against Gram-negative isolates, as opposed to variable susceptibility observed with piperacillin/tazobactam.18

The detection of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales among 17.6% of isolates highlights a substantial burden of these organisms in acute osteomyelitis. This is clinically worrisome because infections caused by these resistant strains are characterized by limited therapeutic options, higher risk of treatment failure, and dependence on carbapenems for definitive management. The percentage of ESBL-producing bacteria observed in this study was significantly lower than that reported in a multicenter European study of osteosynthesis-associated infections of the lower limbs.30 Variations in local antimicrobial stewardship policies, prior antibiotic exposure, patient-related risk factors, and regional epidemiological differences may account for such discrepancy. Although ESBL production was associated with ciprofloxacin resistance in our cohort, it does not fully explain fluoroquinolone resistance. Other contributors include mutations in the gyrA and parC target genes, altered drug efflux or permeability, and plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance.31

In the univariable binary logistic regression analysis, CHD was significantly associated with ciprofloxacin resistance, with affected patients having 1.75-fold higher odds of harboring ciprofloxacin-resistant isolates. Frequent healthcare exposure, hospital admissions, invasive procedures, and prior antibiotic therapy may contribute to this association; however, these factors were not directly assessed as causal determinants in the present retrospective study. Although CHD demonstrated a significant association with resistance in the univariable analysis (p = 0.03), it did not show any significance after adjusting for multivariable analysis (p = 0.08), indicating potential confounding by other factors. Similar results have also been shown in a previous study.32

Furthermore, infection with ESBL-producing pathogens was significantly more common among patients with ciprofloxacin-resistant isolates in the univariable analysis, (p = 0.0001), and this association remained independently significant after multivariable analysis. This finding is consistent with previously reported link between ESBL production and fluoroquinolone co-resistance among MDR Enterobacterales.33 The observed association may reflect the frequent co-occurrence of multiple resistance mechanisms within resistant organisms rather than a direct causal relationship between ESBL production and ciprofloxacin resistance.

Among the laboratory parameters, both hemoglobin concentrations and serum albumin levels were significantly lower in ciprofloxacin-resistant group compared with the susceptible cohort (p = 0.04 and 0.0001, respectively). In the multivariable analysis, low albumin levels remained independently associated with ciprofloxacin resistance (AOR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.35–0.79, p = 0.002). While no direct evidence in acute osteomyelitis exists, both anemia and hypoalbuminemia may serve as markers of compromised host nutritional and inflammatory status. Thus, the observed associations may reflect differences in the underlying clinical condition of patients with ciprofloxacin-resistant infections rather than a direct effect of anemia or hypoalbuminemia on antimicrobial resistance. Our findings are consistent with previous literature. For example, a recent cohort study demonstrated that anemia was associated with a more severe course of osteomyelitis.34 Similarly, a Chinese study reported that low albumin levels were associated with worse clinical outcomes and treatment failure in orthopedic infections.35

The present study demonstrated significantly higher CRP levels in patients with ciprofloxacin‑resistant osteomyelitis compared with those with ciprofloxacin-susceptible infections (64.9 versus 37.6 mg/L, p = 0.001). This finding suggests a more pronounced systemic inflammatory response in patients with resistant infections. CRP is a well-established acute-phase reactant and is widely used as a biomarker for the diagnosis and monitoring of osteomyelitis, with elevated levels consistently associated with disease severity, particularly in diabetic foot infections.36 Furthermore, persistently increased concentrations in osteomyelitis have been linked to more complicated disease courses, as shown in recent studies.37 Importantly, this association was not retained after adjustment for potential confounding factors in the multivariable model, suggesting that elevated CRP may primarily reflect the severity of the underlying infection rather than serve as an independent predictor of ciprofloxacin resistance.

In the univariable analysis, patients with ciprofloxacin-resistant osteomyelitis exhibited significantly higher NLR values than those with ciprofloxacin-susceptible infections (p = 0.04). NLR is a readily available systemic inflammatory biomarker that reflects the balance between neutrophil-mediated innate immune activation and lymphocyte-mediated adaptive immune responses.38 Elevated NLR has been associated with increased infection severity, persistent inflammation, and poorer clinical outcomes in musculoskeletal and soft tissue infections, including chronic osteomyelitis.39

Ciprofloxacin resistance was associated with a more complicated course, as reflected by the significantly increased number of surgical debridement procedures (p = 0.006). This association may indicate the greater complexity of infections involving resistant pathogens, which can be more difficult to manage and may require repeated interventions to achieve adequate source control. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting an association between AMR and increased surgical burden in osteoarticular infections.40 The borderline elevation in total debridement and amputation rates observed in the resistant cohort suggests a trend toward a greater surgical morbidity that may reach statistical significance in larger samples or pooled studies. These results are further supported by a recent study demonstrating that difficult-to-treat osteomyelitis, especially in the presence of resistant pathogens, is often accompanied by higher surgical burden and unfavourable prognosis.41

In addition to the increased surgical burden, ciprofloxacin resistance was significantly associated with several adverse clinical outcomes, including chronic osteomyelitis (p = 0.005), septic shock (p = 0.03), and in-hospital mortality (p = 0.02). These associations may reflect the greater clinical complexity of infections caused by ciprofloxacin-resistant organisms and the challenges associated with effective antimicrobial treatment. However, the retrospective design of the present study precludes determination of whether ciprofloxacin resistance directly contributed to these outcomes. The observed associations may also have been influenced by baseline disease severity, delays in receiving effective antimicrobial therapy, comorbid conditions, infection extent, or other unmeasured factors that were not fully captured in the available records. The association with chronic osteomyelitis may be related to persistent or difficult-to-eradicate infection, although the underlying mechanisms cannot be established from our data. Similarly, a recent study reported that AMR and microbial persistence were major drivers of unsuccessful therapy, recurrent infection, and chronic osteomyelitis.42

Study Limitations

Our study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design of this study may have introduced selection and information bias. Second, as a single-center study, the results may be affected by the local microbial landscape and resistance trends, thereby limiting generalizability to other healthcare settings. Third, while this study evaluated phenotypic ciprofloxacin resistance, the underlying molecular mechanisms (eg, QRDR mutations or plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance genes) were not elucidated. Additionally, the retrospective design also limited the availability and completeness of potentially relevant confounding variables, including prior fluoroquinolones exposure and treatment duration. Although prior antibiotic exposure was assessed within the 90 days preceding index culture collection and included in the analysis, antibiotic exposure beyond this period, particularly fluoroquinolone exposure, may not have been captured based on the available records and could have contributed to residual confounding.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated a substantial burden of ciprofloxacin resistance among patients with acute Gram-negative osteomyelitis, underscoring a growing therapeutic challenge. Several clinical, microbiological, and laboratory characteristics, including CHD, infection with ESBL-producing organisms, anemia, hypoalbuminemia, and increased CRP and NLR were associated with ciprofloxacin resistance in the univariate binary logistic regression analysis. After multivariable adjustment, lower serum albumin concentrations and infections with ESBL-producing pathogens remained independently associated with ciprofloxacin resistance. Importantly, ciprofloxacin resistance was significantly associated with progression to chronic osteomyelitis and in-hospital mortality. These findings may help clinicians recognize patients at increased risk of ciprofloxacin resistance and emphasize the importance of obtaining antimicrobial susceptibility results when managing acute Gram-negative osteomyelitis. However, given the retrospective single-center design, these findings should not be interpreted as sufficient to guide changes in empirical antimicrobial therapy or broader clinical practice. Prospective multicenter studies are warranted to validate these associations and determine whether clinical and inflammatory indicators can reliably contribute to risk stratification and antimicrobial decision-making in osteomyelitis.
