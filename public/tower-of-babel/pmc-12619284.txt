The effect of acupressure therapy on test anxiety and vital signs in nursing students: a randomized controlled study
Authors: İnsel İşlek, Hülya Fırat Kılıç, Seda Cevheroğlu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundState test anxiety, which presents as a distinct form of anxiety, is a condition that impairs communication, learning, and academic achievement. Today, there is a growing interest in non-pharmacological methods to mitigate anxiety. One of the most prevalent methods is acupressure therapy. The objective of this study was to ascertain the influence of acupressure therapy on state test anxiety levels and vital signs among nursing students, including systolic (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP), heart rate, and arterial oxygen saturation.MethodsThe study population consisted of second-year students enrolled at a university of the 2023–2024 academic year. The study sample consisted of 118 nursing students, who were randomly assigned to either the intervention group (n = 59) or the control group (n = 59) in equal numbers. Data were collected using the Descriptive Information Form, the State Test Anxiety Scale (STAS), and the Vital Signs Follow-Up Form.ResultsIt was observed that the state test anxiety levels of students in the experimental group decreased in the post-test measurements compared to the pre-test (t = 4.357; p < 0.001). In addition, in the post-test measurements, the decrease in systolic blood pressure (t = 2.176; p = 0.032), diastolic blood pressure (t = 2.644; p = 0.009), and heart rate (t = 3.133; p = 0.002) in the experimental group was found to be significantly greater than that in the control group. No significant difference was found in oxygen saturation values between the pre-test and post-test analyses (p > 0.05).ConclusionsThe findings indicated that acupressure therapy was an effective method to reduce state test anxiety, heart rates and the SBP and DBP levels. It is therefore recommended that acupressure therapy be employed and promoted as a means of reducing nursing students’ state test anxiety.Trial registrationThis study was retrospectively registered as a randomized controlled trial in the ClinicalTrials Registry. Background

The term “anxiety” is used in psychology to describe a mental state, the definition of which has been subject to numerous interpretations over time [1–3]. Test anxiety, which presents as a distinct form of anxiety, is a condition that impairs communication, learning, and academic achievement [1, 4]. Test anxiety is manifested in three distinct domains: cognitive, affective, and behavioral. The cognitive dimension of anxiety is characterized by intrusive thoughts related to potential negative outcomes, self-evaluation, and persistent worry. The affective dimension often presents with physiological responses such as tachycardia, flushing, nausea, and headaches [5, 6]. It can be reasonably deduced that these aspects of test anxiety may have a detrimental impact on students’ academic success and general health [7]. Some studies have indicated that test anxiety may contribute to the development of problems, such as cardiovascular disease, obesity, and a diminished quality of life [8, 9].

The Fundamentals of Nursing course, which represents a cornerstone of nursing education, employs a range of assessment methods, including question and answer sessions, evaluations of care plans, and oral, clinical and written examinations [10, 11]. In these examinations, students are held accountable for all the information they have learned throughout the academic year and are expected to demonstrate a sufficient level of proficiency in theoretical knowledge and clinical skills [12]. This may consequently result in test anxiety among nursing students. A review of the literature reveals that test anxiety is a prevalent phenomenon among students, that clinical training and assessment are often perceived as more stressful than theoretical exams, and that the prevalence of test anxiety appears to increase with age [8, 12, 13]. Test anxiety is observed to be 30% to 50% more prevalent among health students than among other students [14]. Consequently, there is a growing interest in non-pharmacological methods to mitigate anxiety. One of the most prevalent methods is acupressure therapy [15].

Acupressure therapy is a form of massage that is based on the principles of traditional Chinese medicine. A number of studies have demonstrated the efficacy of acupressure in the management of stress and anxiety [16]. Furthermore, acupressure is a highly favored therapeutic modality due to its non-invasive and cost-effective nature, lack of adverse effects, and ease of administration [17]. While there are numerous body regions that can be utilized in acupressure therapy for the treatment of anxiety, three acupoints have been identified as being particularly effective: Shenmen (HT7) Pericardium 6 (PC6) and Yin Tang (EX-HN3). The Shenmen (HT 7) acupoint, which translates to ‘Spirit Gate’, is located within the triangular fossa of the upper ear [18, 19]. The Pericardium 6 acupoint, located situated two cun above the transverse crease of the wrist, has been used for the prevention and treatment of cardiovascular disease, as well as for the enhancement of stress-related activities [20]. The PC6 point has been demonstrated to reduce anxiety and stress [20, 21]. The third acupoint, Yin Tang (EX-HN3), which is also known as the “Third Eye,” is located between the medial ends of the two eyebrows. Various studies have demonstrated that the EX-HN3 is an important acupoint for the management of stress and anxiety [22–24].

Despite the extensive existing literature on the impact of acupressure therapy on anxiety, few studies have analyzed its impact on test anxiety and vital signs. To address this gap in the literature, the present study was designed to reveal the influence of acupressure therapy on nursing students’ state test anxiety and vital signs, including systolic (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP), heart rate, and arterial oxygen saturation (SpO₂). The study hypotheses included the following:H1: There is a significant difference between the state test anxiety levels of the intervention and control groups.H2: There is a significant difference between the SBP levels of the intervention and control groups.H3: There is a significant difference between the DBP levels of the intervention and control groups.H4: There is a significant difference between the heart rates of the intervention and control groups.H5: There is a significant difference between the SpO₂ levels of the intervention and control groups.

Methods

Research design

The study used a randomized, controlled experimental design.

Population and sampling

The study population consisted of 120 s-year nursing students enrolled in the Nursing Principles course in the Nursing Department of a foundation university during the fall semester of the 2023–2024 academic year. The required sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.2. The effect size was calculated to be 0.359, with reference to the randomized controlled study conducted by Yıldırım and Akman (2021) on the impact of acupressure on clinical stress management in nursing students. The minimum sample size was calculated to be 78 for α = 0.05 and 1-β = 0.95. No sampling was conducted. Students who utilized alternative therapeutic modalities or pharmacological agents for the management of anxiety were excluded from the study. The sample of the study consisted of 119 students who agreed to participate. The students were assigned to experimental and control groups by lottery. The experimental group consisted of students who received acupressure therapy (n = 60), while the control group included students who did not receive any form of therapy (n = 59). One student from the experimental group was excluded from the study for failing to complete the information forms after the practical oral exam. Therefore, the study was completed with a total of 118 students, 59 in the experimental group and 59 in the control group (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Shenmen Points

Data collection tools

The data were collected using the Descriptive Information Form, the State Test Anxiety Scale (STAS), and the Vital Signs Follow-Up Form (VSFF).

Descriptive information form

The form was developed by the researchers in accordance with the relevant literature [12, 15]. It was composed of 22 questions on age, gender, parental education, type of school graduated, general and test anxiety levels, the impact of anxiety on test performance and feelings of preparedness for the test.

State Test Anxiety Scale (STAS)

The STAS, developed by Şahin (2019), comprises 22 items distributed across three subscales: cognitive (9 items), psychosocial (5 items), and physiological (8 items). The items are scored on a four-point Likert scale, with responses ranging from “1 (None)” to “4 (A lot). " The range of possible scores is from 22 to 88, with higher scores indicating higher levels of state test anxiety. The original scale exhibited a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.94 [25]. The intervention group demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.922 before and 0.897 after acupressure therapy, respectively. The control group exhibited a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.929 and 0.943, respectively.

Vital signs follow-up form (VSFF)

The VSFF was developed by the researchers for the purpose of recording blood pressure, heart rate, and SpO₂ levels before and after the acupressure intervention. The vital signs were measured by the researchers. A digital sphygmomanometer was used to measure blood pressure, while a pulse oximeter was used to measure heart rate and SpO₂ levels. Prior to the measurements, the devices were calibrated.

Procedures

On the day preceding the examination, the researchers convened with the students enrolled in the course and provided them with an overview of the study’s objectives. The students who consented to participate in the study and who had not undergone any therapeutic intervention for anxiety disorders were randomly assigned to either the intervention or control groups. Given that six lecturers would be administering the exam, the students were divided into groups of six, comprising three experimental and three control groups. The pretest was administered 30 min prior to the commencement of the examination. The students were taken to an empty classroom and asked to fill out the descriptive information form and the STAS as pretest. Subsequently, the researchers proceeded to measure and record the subjects’ vital signs on the VSFF. The students in the control group were instructed to wait in the designated area while the students in the intervention group received acupressure therapy from the researcher, who had obtained certification in acupressure. Acupressure was performed on the Pericardium 6 (PC6), Yin Tang (EX-HN3), and Shenmen (HT7) acupoints, with each point receiving a single application for a duration of two minutes, resulting in a total of six minutes of acupressure (Figs. 1, 2 and 3). Each acupoint was compressed for a period of 15 s, followed by a 5-second interval. Subsequently, the students in the intervention group were also directed to the room, where they awaited the commencement of the examination for approximately 15 min. The examination, which lasted a total of 15 min, was comprised of two sections. The first section, which assessed theoretical knowledge, required the students to respond to two theoretical questions within a five-minute time frame. The second section, which evaluated psychomotor skills, entailed the completion of randomly selected nursing skill practices. Subsequent to the examination, vital signs were measured and recorded on the VSFF once more, and the students were asked to complete the STAS as a posttest. Randomization was performed using a lot-drawing method, and the allocation was conducted by an individual independent of the study. Blinding was not possible for the students and the acupressure practitioner. However, the researcher who performed the data analysis was blinded. Research data and acupressure therapy application were collected by graduate student who have an acupressure therapy certificate. The pressure applied was subjective and defined as “moderate” by the practitioner—firm enough to stimulate the point effectively without causing discomfort. The acupressure was applied using circular movements with the thumb, with light to moderate pressure.Fig. 2Pericardium 6 PointsFig. 3Yintang Points

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee of Eastern Mediterranean University (No. ETK00-2023-0204). Voluntary informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles set forth in the Helsinki Declaration. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06454799/05.09.2024). This study was reported following the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) Guidelines.

Data analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted using the IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 26.0. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was employed to ascertain the normality of the data distribution. An independent sample t-test was employed to compare two groups, while one-way ANOVA was utilized for comparisons of three or more groups. A paired-samples t-test was utilized for comparisons of repeated measurements between groups. Pearson’s correlation test was utilized to analyze the relationship between two continuous variables. Pearson chi-square test and Fisher’s exact chi-square test were utilized to compare qualitative variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

STAS scores

Table 3 presents the scores obtained from the STAS and its subscales. No statistically significant difference was observed between the pretest scores obtained by the intervention and control groups from the STAS (p > 0.05). A statistically significant difference was observed between the posttest STAS scores of the intervention group and those of the control group (t = 4.357; p < 0.001). SBP and DBP

No statistically significant difference was observed between the pretest SBP and DBP levels of the intervention and control groups (p > 0.05). The posttest SBP (t = 2.176; p = 0.032) and DBP (t = 2.644; p = 0.009) levels of the intervention group were statistically significantly higher than those of the control group. A statistically significant increase was observed in the posttest SBP (t = 3.965; p < 0.001) and DBP (t = 3.901; p < 0.001) levels of the control group when compared to the pretest levels. While the posttest SBP levels of the intervention group were statistically significantly lower than their pretest levels (t = 3.365; p < 0.001), no statistically significant difference was observed between the pretest and posttest DBP levels (p > 0.05). Finally, the decrease in the SBP (t = 5.194; p = 0.001) and DBP (t = 2.301; p = 0.023) levels of the intervention group was statistically significantly higher than in the control group.

Heart rate

No statistically significant difference was observed between the pretest heart rates of the intervention and control groups (p > 0.05). The posttest heart rates of the intervention group were found to be statistically significantly higher than those of the control group (t = 3.133; p = 0.002). While the posttest heart rates of the intervention group were statistically significantly lower than their pretest rates (t = 5.117; p < 0.001), the posttest heart rates of the control group were statistically significantly higher than their pretest rates (t = 2.877; p = 0.006). Finally, the decrease observed in the heart rate in the intervention group was statistically significantly higher than that observed in the control group (t = 5.689; p < 0.001).

SpO₂

When the findings related to SpO₂ levels were examined, no statistically significant difference was observed between the groups or within the groups in the pre-test and post-test analyses (p > 0.05).

Discussion

The objective of this study was to ascertain the influence of acupressure therapy on state test anxiety levels and vital signs, including systolic and diastolic blood pressure, heart rate and SpO₂, in nursing students. The findings demonstrated that acupressure therapy was an efficacious intervention for the reduction of state test anxiety levels, heart rate and systolic and diastolic blood pressure levels. However, no discernible effect was observed on oxygen saturation levels.

STAS scores

Test anxiety, which can be defined as a state of emotional distress characterized by feelings of anxiety, tension, and restlessness in anticipation of an examination, is a prevalent emotional state among students [26]. Prior to the acupressure intervention, both groups of participants exhibited moderate levels of state test anxiety. A review of the literature reveals that nursing students exhibit moderate to high levels of test anxiety [6, 14, 27]. The analysis of the posttest STAS scores revealed that the general state test anxiety levels of the control group increased, while the general state test anxiety levels of the intervention group decreased (Table 3). Although no statistically significant difference was observed between the pretest scores of the intervention and control groups on the STAS and its subscales measuring physiological, psychosocial, and cognitive anxiety (p >0.05), a statistically significant decrease was evident in the posttest scores of the intervention group (p < 0.001). These findings indicate the effectiveness of acupressure therapy in reducing state test anxiety, thereby confirming the initial hypothesis that there is a significant difference between the state test anxiety levels of the intervention and control groups. Our findings were confirmed by a number of studies. Lee and Park (2023) discovered that ear acupressure was an efficacious method for mitigating test anxiety in nursing students [28]. In a study conducted by Mosavi et al. (2023) on 114 medical students, acupressure therapy applied to the Shenmen acupoint was found to reduce test anxiety levels [19]. Yıldırım and Akman (2021) found that acupressure therapy significantly reduced the stress levels of students [29]. Usichenko et al. (2020) also investigated the efficacy of acupressure therapy applied to the Shenmen acupoint in reducing test anxiety. Their results demonstrated that this approach was indeed effective in alleviating test anxiety [30]. Overall, it should be noted that the increase in state test anxiety levels in the control group, as evidenced by within-group comparisons, highlights the importance of stress management strategies before examinations. The absence of any intervention in the control group might have contributed to this increase, further reinforcing the potential benefit of acupressure therapy.

Vital signs

Prior research has indicated that acupressure therapy has a range of beneficial effects, including the management of anxiety, the treatment of various medical conditions, the maintenance of hormonal equilibrium, and the stabilization of vital signs [31–33]. Acupressure therapy stimulates nerve endings, which increases endorphin release and reduces stress hormones like epinephrine and cortisol. This helps lower anxiety levels. It also decreases sympathetic activity and increases parasympathetic activity, helping to reduce heart rate and blood pressure and improve vital signs [34, 35]. The present study demonstrated a notable decline in both the SBP and DBP levels in the intervention group, as compared to the control group (Table 4). These findings indicate the effectiveness of acupressure therapy in reducing blood pressure levels, thereby confirming the second and third hypotheses that there is a significant difference between the SBP and DBP levels of the intervention and control groups. Existing studies support our findings. A systematic review of nine articles by Restawan et al. (2023) indicated that acupressure therapy, administered for periods ranging from three to 40 min, was an effective intervention for reducing blood pressure in patients with hypertension [36]. A systematic review and meta-analysis study conducted by Xie et al. (2023) demonstrated that acupressure therapy reduced anxiety and systolic and diastolic blood pressure levels [37]. A randomized controlled trial conducted by Lin et al. (2016) revealed that acupressure therapy applied to the Taichong (LR3) acupoint resulted in a reduction in blood pressure in patients with hypertension [38]. In contrast to the findings of our study, Yeh et al. (2015) and Santoso et al. (2015) reported that acupressure therapy had no significant effect on blood pressure levels [39]. These conflicting results may be explained with reference to variations in the study population and selected acupressure points.

A statistical analysis of the difference in heart rates between the intervention and control groups revealed a significantly higher decrease in heart rate level in the former group. These findings substantiate the efficacy of acupressure therapy in reducing heart rates, thereby confirming the fourth hypothesis that there is a significant difference between the heart rates of the intervention and control groups. However, an analysis of the literature reveals conflicting findings regarding the effects of acupressure therapy on heart rates. While the studies of Xie et al. (2023), Biçer et al. (2021), and Özkan (2019) demonstrated the effectiveness of acupressure therapy in reducing heart rates, previous research by Yeh et al. (2015) and Hamlacı (2013) indicated that acupressure therapy had no effect on heart rate [37, 39–42]. The analysis of the pretest and posttest SpO₂ levels of the intervention and control groups revealed no statistically significant difference between or within groups. These findings indicate that acupressure therapy had no effect on SpO₂ levels, thereby rejecting the final hypothesis that there is a significant difference between the SpO₂ levels of the intervention and control groups. One potential reason for this result is that oxygen saturation levels in healthy young individuals are typically stable and less prone to short-term fluctuations unless influenced by respiratory dysfunction [43]. Moreover, the 6-minute duration of acupressure may have been insufficient to produce measurable changes in SpO₂ levels. Future studies may consider longer application periods or target populations with compromised respiratory function.

The conflicting findings in the literature on the effects of acupressure therapy on vital signs can be explained by a multitude of factors, including the variation in the number and location of acupressure points, the discrepancy in pressure level and application times, the sample size, the geographical and cultural characteristics, and the disease type.

Limitations

The study was limited in two principal respects. Firstly, the study was conducted at a single university, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to the broader population. Secondly, the study was single-blinded. In addition, it is important to consider the potential influence of placebo effects and cultural attitudes toward complementary therapies. Participants’ expectations and cultural familiarity with interventions like acupressure may have contributed to the perceived effectiveness, particularly in reducing subjective measures such as anxiety. This should be acknowledged as a potential limitation. Moreover, the short follow-up period of the study did not allow us to evaluate the long-term effects of the intervention.

Conclusions

The objective of this study was to examine the impact of acupressure therapy on nursing students’ test anxiety levels and vital signs. The findings indicated that the state test anxiety levels of the intervention group, which received acupressure therapy, demonstrated a notable decline. Moreover, although the acupressure therapy intervention resulted in a decrease in heart rates and systolic and diastolic blood pressure levels, it had no impact on the oxygen saturation levels of the nursing students. In light of these findings, it is recommended that acupressure therapy be employed by nursing students as a means of managing state test anxiety and controlling heart rate and the SBP and DBP levels. Despite the application of acupressure to the Pericardium 6 (PC6), Yin Tang (EX-HN3), and Shenmen (HT7) acupoints, no definitive conclusion could be reached regarding the relative efficacy of these points. Therefore, further studies are recommended to determine which acupressure point is more efficacious in managing exam anxiety. Further studies may be conducted on different sample groups to establish standards for acupressure therapy, including the acupoints, duration, and pressure intensity.
