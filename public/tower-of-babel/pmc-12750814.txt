Prevalence and patterns of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) among diabetic patients presenting to hospitals in northern state, Sudan: a cross-sectional study during the 2025 humanitarian crisis
Authors: Ali Abdelhaleem Omar Ahmed, Moaz Alfadil Ahmed Mounfali, Tagwa Yassin, Huzaifa Ibrahim, Reem Abdelhaleem Omar Ahmed, Rana Abdelhaleem Omar Ahmed, Nafisa Ahmed, Omer Abdalhaleem Omer Ahmed, Roaa Mohamed Ali Elbashir, Khalid H Mohamed, Ahmed Manufali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic ketoacidosis is a major complication of diabetes, with the prevalence ranging from 4.8 to 30% globally for type 1 diabetes and from 1 to 10% for type 2 diabetes. The humanitarian crisis increases DKA risk due to the collapse of support systems. This study looked at the prevalence and determinants of DKA in Northern State, Sudan, during the 2025 humanitarian crisis.MethodsA prospective cross-sectional study was conducted in Northern State’s primary healthcare centers from April to May 2025. We enrolled 140 diabetic patients presenting to hospitals through consecutive sampling and this final sample was smaller than planned due to extreme field constraints. Data was collected via a pre-tested, physician-administered questionnaire and a review of available medical records. Diagnosis of DKA was based on adapted UK criteria, and statistical analysis was performed using Jamovi software (v2.3).ResultsThe prevalence of DKA was 97.9% (137/140). Patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus constituted the majority of cases (82.9%). The key factors associated with DKA included rural residence (67.1%), medication scarcity (59.3%), and recent respiratory infections (51.4%). Severe metabolic derangement was prevalent: 88.6% presented with blood glucose > 300 mg/dL, and 97.1% met the criteria for metabolic acidosis.ConclusionAmong diabetic patients presenting to hospitals during the crisis, the prevalence of DKA was catastrophic and far exceeds global averages, likely reflecting a collapse of routine care. This finding may not be generalizable to the wider diabetic population. Our findings highlight an acute need for urgent humanitarian interventions, including guaranteed insulin supply and infection prevention programs. There is a critical gap in the integration of diabetes care into emergency response frameworks that must be addressed.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-025-02114-9.Keywords: Diabetic ketoacidosis, Humanitarian crisis, Type 2 diabetes, Sudan, Insulin access

Introduction

Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), which is a life-threatening acute complication of diabetes, occurs in 25–40% of individuals with type 1 diabetes mellitus and at least 34% of individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus at the time of diagnosis [1]. While traditionally associated with T1DM, DKA is increasingly recognized in individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), particularly in African populations, often triggered by infections or insulin omission [2, 3]. The pathophysiology of DKA involves insulin deficiency which increases lipolysis, ketogenesis, and metabolic acidosis [4, 5].

Polyuria, polydipsia, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and Kussmaul respirations describe the clinical presentation of DKA [6]. The management includes insulin therapy, fluid resuscitation, electrolyte replacement and treatment of precipitating factors [7]. Mortality rates in high-income countries are 2–5% whereas rates can run even higher in resource-limited countries (6–24%) where diagnosis and treatment are delayed [8].

In Sudan, the impact of diabetes is especially problematic, with several studies demonstrating a high incidence of type 1 diabetes in children [9]. A study conducted in Khartoum State found that DKA was present in 17.6% of children with a new diagnosis of type 1 diabetes [10]. There is a different clinical picture of childhood diabetes in Sudan compared with European and North American populations. Children here tend to have more severe presentations, a greater number of severe hyperglycaemic episodes, and an earlier onset of diabetes [11]. The recent humanitarian crisis was marked by widespread displacement, an inadequate supply of clean water, shortage of electricity, disruption of medication supply chains, and health infrastructure destruction. These conditions are recognized to severely compromise non-communicable disease management, including diabetes [12], and therefore may have grossly compromised diabetes management and predisposed individuals with diabetes to a high risk of DKA. The primary objective of this study was to establish the prevalence of DKA among diabetic patients presenting to hospitals in Northern State, Sudan, during the 2025 humanitarian crisis. Secondary objectives were to identify associated risk factors and describe the patterns and severity of metabolic derangement based on laboratory findings.

Methods

Study design, period, study setting

This prospective, descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted from April 20 to May 25, 2025, at multiple hospitals in Northern State, Sudan. The study period coincided with an acute humanitarian crisis triggered by armed conflict, leading to mass displacement, intermittent electricity, critical shortages of medical supplies, and low water supply. Hospitals were managing large numbers of patients under these severely constrained conditions, which significantly affected service delivery and data collection processes.

Study population

The study group consisted of adult diabetic patients (either Type 1 or Type 2) who were 18 years of age or older and presented to the selected hospitals that were able to participate during the limits of the study time period. The inclusion was purposefully designed to examine cases where the diagnostic information was as complete as possible, necessitating laboratory results to qualify for DKA diagnosis, including blood glucose values, ketone values, and pH or bicarbonate values. We did not include pregnant and breastfeeding women, as we recognized the possibility of confounding effects from metabolic changes during gestation. In addition, we excluded any patients without the necessary laboratory results or patients with incomplete health records. Although using this type of strict exclusionary criteria to achieve diagnostic certainty could have created some sampling bias, conditions in the field were not ideal. Furthermore, the consecutive sampling of patients presenting to hospitals during a humanitarian crisis likely over-represents those with acute complications like DKA, which may have inflated the observed prevalence compared to the general diabetic population.

Variables of the study

The outcome under examination in this study was diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), which is characterized by a combination of hyperglycemia (> 200 mg/dL), ketosis (urine ketones ≥ + 2), and acidosis (pH < 7.3) as defined in this study. The variable predictors were examined through five variable categories, including: (1) Socio-demographic factors (age, sex, residential address, income); (2) Clinical factors (diabetes type, diabetes duration, treatment); (3) Behavioral factors (diet, adherence to medications); (4) Comorbidities (recent infections); and (5) Laboratory factors (glucose, ketones pH). The primary aim was to assess risk factors for DKA, especially within humanitarian crises (e.g., lack of access to medications), and for populations with barriers to health. Laboratory data were used for diagnosis and also provided measures of DKA severity in this vulnerable and marginalized population.

Data collection

Data were collected using a structured physician-administered questionnaire developed specifically for this study (Supplementary File 1). Trained personnel collected the patient’s sociodemographic information (age, sex, housing, occupation, and income) and gathered clinical data (type of diabetes, duration, current treatment, and admission status), as well as documenting details on current behaviors (physical activity level, food security, and issues with medication adherence) using a standardized patient interview. The laboratory piece was created with available resources to allow for bedside glucose monitoring through the use of portable glucometers, urine ketones using reagent strips, and venous blood gases for pH and bicarbonate. In terms of equipment limitations, we adhered to standard protocols when collecting data to enhance data reliability.

Sample collection and processing

We carried out blood and urine sampling and processing according to protocols we’d established to obtain reliable results in the challenging field conditions. Blood (venous) samples were collected as 5 ml aliquots following universal precautions, promptly (within 30 min of collection) chilled, and sent to laboratory (0–4 C). We collected 10 ml of mid-stream urine into sterile containers. Venous samples were processed via centrifugation immediately upon arrival to separate the serum. We tested serum for glucose, ketones, and electrolytes, and it was frozen for storage at − 80 °C. Urine was centrifuged and dipsticks were used to perform urine tests to measure glucose and ketones. We performed glucose and ketones point-of-care measurements with portable calibrated devices we purchased to operate in the field. Rigorous quality control included equipment calibration daily; measuring duplicates for 10% of samples; and collecting biohazard materials into designated waste disposal containers before disposal. Rigorous quality control ensured the reliability and integrity of the data required for DKA diagnosis, despite the challenging field conditions, given the constraints imposed on the study by a humanitarian crisis.

Operational definitions

According to UK-based guidelines, diagnosis requires the presence of hyperglycemia (blood glucose >11mmol/L or 200 mg/dL); ketosis (serum β-hydroxybutyrate >3mmol/L); and metabolic acidosis (venous pH < 7.3) [13]. For this study, adapted for a resource-limited environment, a “case” was defined by hyperglycemia (blood glucose >200 mg/dL), ketosis (urine ketones ≥ + 2), and metabolic acidosis (bicarbonate ≤ 18 mEq/L). We classified diabetes as per standard definitions, types 1 and 2. Type 1 typically had a younger age of presentation where the patient depended on insulin (7.89% of cohort), whereas, type 2 was typically older (84.85% of cohort), and often had concomitant obesity. BMI was established through anthropometric measurements and categories by the WHO, with obesity being >30 kg/m², overweight 25–29.9 kg/m², and underweight being < 18.5 kg/m². Rural residence was defined as living in settlements outside designated major urban centers. Income categories were based on the Sudanese pound (SDG) and reflected local economic stratification at the time of the study.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Jamovi (v2.3). Descriptive statistics were used to describe the study population and inferential tests were used to find significant associations. The Chi-square test was used to examine associations between categorical variables. Continuous variables (e.g., age, income, BMI) were categorized for analysis based on clinical relevance or standard cut-offs, and were compared using t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests. A complete-case analysis was performed, as missing data were minimal (< 2% for any variable).

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee of the Sudanese Ministry of Health. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written and verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion.

Results

Prevalence and sample description

The study consisted of 140 diabetic patients presenting to hospitals, of which 137 were diagnosed with DKA, yielding a prevalence of 97.9% (95% CI: 94.1–99.6%). Given the near-universal prevalence of DKA (97.9%), multivariate logistic regression to identify predictors was not feasible as the model failed to converge. Only 3 patients (2.1%) did not have DKA. The cohort was predominantly female (69.3%) and from rural areas (67.1%). The highest proportion of patients were aged between 45 and 60 years (49.3%). Sociodemographic characteristics were not significantly associated with DKA status, which was expected given the minimal non-DKA group (n = 3). Laboratory findings

The laboratory profile confirmed a severe metabolic crisis. The vast majority of DKA patients (88.6%) presented with blood glucose > 300 mg/dL. Nearly all patients (97.1%) met the criteria for metabolic acidosis (bicarbonate ≤ 18 mEq/L), and 98.6% had moderate to large ketonuria. There were no significant differences in laboratory severity between T1DM and T2DM patients (e.g., Blood Glucose > 300 mg/dL: T1DM 85.7% vs. T2DM 89.1%, P = 0.709).

Discussion

This study reveals a catastrophic frequency of DKA (97.9%) among diabetic patients who presented to hospitals in Northern Sudan during a severe humanitarian crisis. It is critical to interpret this prevalence as a measure of acute morbidity in a hospital-attending population during a crisis, rather than a true population-wide estimate. The near-universal rate likely reflects a profound selection bias, as only the sickest individuals with the means and ability to reach a functioning hospital were included. Therefore, this figure serves as a stark sentinel indicator of the collapse of routine diabetes care rather than a precise epidemiological measure.

High DKA prevalence in crisis situations

The overwhelming prevalence of DKA found in this study aligns with similar literature demonstrating high prevalence of DKA in conflict settings. For instance, a qualitative study from Yemen highlighted how the conflict led to critical medicine shortages and severely disrupted diabetes management, increasing the risk of acute complications [14]. Similarly, a study from Syria reported a significant surge in DKA cases and hospitalizations as a direct consequence of the armed conflict [15]. Our results confirm that humanitarian crisis situations heighten the frequency of DKA, particularly in situations where access to insulin and glucose monitoring is severely impacted by a humanitarian crisis.

DKA in type 2 DM

While DKA is usually associated with T1DM, the majority of our DKA cases, 82.9%, were associated with T2DM, a trend noted in other low-resource settings. Studies from sub-Saharan Africa show that DKA in T2DM can occur as a result of infections, starvation ketosis, and insulin omission [2, 3]. A recent cross-sectional study in Ethiopia, for example, found a high prevalence of DKA among adults with T2DM, with infections being a major precipitating factor [2]. The high levels of cholera/diarrhea (15.7%) and respiratory infections (51.4%) in our cohort corroborate this notion, because infections are known to precipitate hyperglycemia and ketosis [16].

Sociodemographic and access to healthcare

Rural Residency and Medication Stockouts: 67.1% of respondents described themselves as rural residents in this context, and 59.3% reported medication stockouts. Similar results were observed in South Sudan, where worse diabetes outcomes were observed due to geographical barriers and supply chain issues [17]. Economic Barriers: 45.7% of respondents reported they could not afford to get to a clinic, also consistent with literature indicating poverty has been a major predictor of DKA in low-income countries [18].

Metabolic and laboratory findings

The laboratory findings: hyperglycemia (>300 mg/dL in 88.6%), ketonuria (98.6%), and metabolic acidosis (97.1%), confirm the diagnostic criteria of significant DKA [12]. The lack of a normal bicarbonate level measured in almost all analyses would suggest a substantially greater metabolic crisis than would normally be seen in a stable clinical situation, which we postulate is related to patients delaying care-seeking behavior during active conflict.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths and limitations. A key strength is the collection of original clinical data during an active humanitarian crisis, providing rare and critical evidence on the impact of conflict on non-communicable disease management. Nonetheless, the limitations need to be carefully taken into account. The single-center, hospital-based design and the consecutive sampling of patients who met the laboratory criteria for DKA likely selected for the most severe cases, inflating the observed prevalence. Moreover, the exclusion of patients lacking laboratory assessments further skewed the sample toward more acutely ill patients. The use of urine ketones, while a necessary compromise, is a less specific diagnostic measure than serum β-hydroxybutyrate (BHB). Finally, the small number of non-DKA patients precluded multivariate analysis of risk factors.

Furthermore, formal sensitivity analyses, such as excluding patients diagnosed based solely on urine ketones, were not performed. However, the near-universal prevalence of metabolic acidosis (97.1%) across the cohort suggests that the core finding of a system-wide collapse in diabetes care is robust.

Recommendations for policy and practice

Based on our findings, we propose the following recommendations for policy and practice. There is a critical need to establish emergency insulin distribution mechanisms, including mobile clinics and pre-positioned stockpiles in conflict areas; however, this must be planned with feasibility in mind, including solutions for maintaining a cold chain for insulin. Infection prevention needs priority in public health programming, including vaccination programs and clean drinking water access to decrease a key precipitant for diabetic ketoacidosis. Healthcare worker capacity should be expanded by training non-specialist clinicians in simplified, protocol-driven DKA management. Ultimately, based on these findings there is a case for the formal inclusion of diabetes, and other non-communicable disease needs for humanitarian assessments and emergency programming to avoid similar catastrophic health events in future emergencies [17].

Conclusion

This research indicates an alarming rate of diabetic ketoacidosis in diabetic patients who presented to Northern Sudanese hospitals during a catastrophic emergency. These cases were primarily driven by a lack of diabetes medications and concurrent infections. The research findings indicate a significant disruption to typical diabetes care during the crisis and underscores the urgent need to integrate diabetes care into humanitarian response frameworks to prevent similar outcomes in the future.
