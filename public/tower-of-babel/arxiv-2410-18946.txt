A geometric characterization of steady laminar flow
Theodore D. Drivas, Marc Nualart

Abstract
. We study the steady states of the Euler equations on the periodic channel or annulus. We show that if these flows are laminar (layered by closed non-contractible streamlines which foliate the domain), then they must be either parallel or circular flows. We also show that a large subset of these shear flows are isolated from non-shear stationary states. For Poiseuille flow, ( v ⁡ ( y ) , 0 ) = ( y 2 , 0 ) (v(y),0)=(y^{2},0) , our result shows that all stationary solutions in a sufficiently small C 2 C^{2} neighborhood are shear flows. We then show that if v ⁡ ( y ) = y n v(y)=y^{n} with n ≥ 1 n\geq 1 , then in any C n − C^{n-} neighborhood, there exist smooth non-shear steady states, traveling waves, and quasiperiodic solutions of any number of non-commensurate frequencies. This proves the rigidity near Poiseuille is sharp. Finally, we prove that on general compact doubly connected domains, laminar steady Euler flows with constant velocity on the boundary must also be either parallel or circular, and the domain a periodic channel or an annulus. This shows that laminar free boundary Euler solutions must have Euclidean symmetry.

As such, steady Euler solution can be understood as built out of a collection of curves (streamlines) along which both the streamfunction and vorticity are constant. This simultaneous relation is highly constraining, making these streamlines act effectively as a bundle of elastic bands, constantly straining to alleviate their tension by minimizing their length while maintaining their enclosed area. As such, provided the domain

Not always must this be the case; there are some steady states on these domains that break the symmetry, where internal transition layers (accompanied by hyperbolic critical points) divide up the flow domain, serving as new effective boundaries. In this work, we show this is the only possibility:

Theorem 1.1

Let

We now give some intuition for our main result, Theorem
1.1
. Let
M
be the annulus and
\psi_{0}:M\to\mathbb{R}
be a function without critical points in
M
. In particular,
u_{0}=\nabla^{\perp}\psi_{0}
is a laminar flow. Let
\mathcal{O}_{\psi_{0}}
be the orbit of
\psi_{0}
in the area preserving diffeomorphism group
\mathcal{D}_{\mu}(M)
:

(1.6)

\mathcal{O}_{\psi_{0}}=\{\psi:M\to\mathbb{R}\ :\ \psi=\psi_{0}\circ\varphi,\ \text{for some}\ \varphi\in\mathcal{D}_{\mu}(M)\}.

Let
E[\psi]
be the Dirichlet energy of a function
\psi:M\to\mathbb{R}
:

(1.7)

E[\psi]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}|\nabla\psi|^{2}dA.

It is well known that if
\psi_{0}
extremizes the energy among all
\psi\in\mathcal{O}_{\psi_{0}}
, then
\psi_{0}
is the streamfunction of a stationary solution
[
1

2

.
We now explain why this indicates
\psi_{0}
should be radial.
We begin by expressing the energy by the coarea formula (eliciting the analogy with constant tension elastic bands, with each streamline contributing an energy equal to its velocity-weighted length) as

(1.8)

E[\psi]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\psi^{-1}(M)}{\rm d}c\oint_{\{\psi=c\}}|\nabla\psi|{\rm d}\ell

where
{\rm d}\ell
is the one-dimensional Hausdorff measure on the level curves on
\psi
. Now, by Cauchy–Schwarz:

(1.9)

{\rm length}(\{\psi=c\})\leq\mu_{\psi}(c)^{1/2}\left(\oint_{\{\psi=c\}}|\nabla\psi|{\rm d}\ell\right)^{1/2}

where
\mu_{\psi}(c):=\oint_{\{\psi=c\}}\frac{{\rm d}\ell}{|\nabla\psi|}
is the period of revolution of a particle on the level curve
\{\psi=c\}
.
Note that
\mu_{\psi}(c)
is constant on the orbit
\psi\in\mathcal{O}_{\psi_{0}}
, since
\mu_{\psi}(c)=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}c}{\rm area}(\{\psi\leq c\})
by the co-area formula, and areas of sublevel sets are preserved on the orbit
\mathcal{O}_{\psi_{0}}
. We have then

(1.10)

E[\psi]\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\psi^{-1}(M)}\frac{{\rm length}(\{\psi=c\})^{2}}{\mu_{\psi}(c)}{\rm d}c.

Now, let
\psi_{*}\in\mathcal{O}_{\psi_{0}}
be the unique radial rearrangement of
\psi_{0}
. Since
\psi_{*}\in\mathcal{O}_{\psi_{0}}
, we have
\psi^{-1}(M)=\psi_{*}^{-1}(M)
and
\mu_{\psi}(c)=\mu_{\psi_{*}}(c)
. Moreover, by the isoperimetric inequality, we have

(1.11)

{\rm length}(\{\psi=c\})\geq{\rm length}(\{\psi_{*}=c\}),

since the curves encircle regions of the same area. Thus

(1.12)

E[\psi]\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\psi^{-1}(M)}\frac{{\rm length}(\{\psi_{*}=c\})^{2}}{\mu_{\psi_{*}}(c)}{\rm d}c=E[\psi_{*}],

since the inequality (
1.10

\psi=\psi_{*}

is radial, the energy can be reduced by a suitable wiggling of the streamlines. This clearly illustrates why, in the course of settling in the minimum energy state, streamlines strive for minimal length. See Figure

Although this argument serves as motivation for Theorem

For this reason, here we take an entirely different viewpoint, one of over-constrained elliptic problems, to prove Theorem

Next, we list some consequences of our Theorem

Corollary 1.1

Let

Such a problem arises for fluids with vacuum interfaces, see

On the one hand, it shows that non-parallel steady Euler solutions on wrinkled domains either must have rich Lagrangian dynamics including islands of fluid, or the velocity field on the boundaries is non-constant. On the other hand, it implies that laminar free boundary fluid equilibria must possess Euclidean symmetry.

Next, we show that certain of these steady laminar flows on the channel are robustly isolated from non-laminar ones:

Theorem 1.2

Let

We now give an outline for the rest of the paper and the main ideas involved in the proofs. In Section
2
we state and show basic properties of streamlines of steady laminar flows. We define suitable fluid sub-domains that give rise to semilinear elliptic equations for the stream-function, with

Section

This is achieved using a sliding method together with local symmetry results from the theory of Continuous Steiner Symmetrization.

Section

Section

. To achieve this, we investigate free boundaries with constant velocity. There we establish an intermediate result concerning rigidity of solutions to the free-boundary Euler equations in the periodic strip. Its proof may be of independent interest in the theory of overdetermined boundary value problems in periodic domains and involves a moving plane method followed by a carefully devised sliding argument.

Next, Section

Key properties of laminar streamlines and its fluid sub-domains

In this section we study the streamlines of laminar flows in
M

M
. The assumption in Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.1
that all streamlines of the steady state are closed non-contractible loops has the following consequence: one can foliate
M
by these streamlines

2

Note that our assumptions rule out the existence of open sets of zero velocity. Indeed, if this were the case then the velocity field would admit contractible streamlines arbitrarily confined within these regions. A more general case where zero regions are allowed is relegated to §

2.1.
Regular, Regular-Singular and Singular streamlines

In order to define fluid sub-domains that are useful to our purposes, for each value
c
in the range of
\psi:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we classify the streamlines associated to
c
. In what follows, any streamline
\Gamma_{c}\subseteq\{\psi=c\}
is understood to be a maximally connected component of the set
\{\psi^{-1}(c)\}
and it is assumed to be a closed non-contractible loop. We say that a streamline
\Gamma_{c}
is

•

a
regular streamline
of
\psi
if for all
q\in\Gamma_{c}
there holds
\nabla\psi(q)\neq 0
.

•

a
regular-singular streamline
of
\psi
if there exists
p,q\in\Gamma_{c}
such that
\nabla\psi(q)\neq 0
and
\nabla\psi(p)=0
.

•

a
singular streamline
of
\psi
if for all
p\in\Gamma_{c}
we have
\nabla\psi(p)=0
.

We remark here that this characterization is at the level of the streamlines, so that there may exist
c
such that
\{\psi^{-1}(c)\}
contains regular, singular and regular-singular streamlines. The following result shows that regular streamlines are dense in
M
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Regular streamlines are isolated from singular ones. Additionally, all points on any singular or regular-singular streamline are not isolated from regular streamlines.

Proof.

We begin by noting that
\psi\in C^{3}(M)
because
u\in C^{2}(M)
. If a regular streamline were not isolated from singular streamlines, we would be able to find a sequence of points
p_{n}
, with
\psi(p_{n})=c_{n}\rightarrow c
and
\nabla\psi(p_{n})=0
such that
p_{n}\rightarrow p
, for some
p\in\Gamma_{c}
. By continuity,
\nabla\psi(p)=0
. But this contradicts
\Gamma_{c}
being a regular streamline.

To prove the second statement of the lemma, assume that regular-singular streamlines are isolated from regular ones. Now, let
\Gamma_{c}
denote a regular-singular streamline and let
q_{c}\in\Gamma_{c}
such that
\nabla\psi(q_{c})\neq 0
. By continuity,
\nabla\psi(q)\neq 0
, for all
q\in B_{\delta_{1}}(q_{c})
, for some
\delta>0
. Choosing
\delta>0
sufficiently small, we see that all
q\in B_{\delta_{1}}(q_{c})
belong to regular-singular streamlines. Indeed, they cannot belong to singular streamlines because they have non-zero gradient and they cannot lie on regular streamlines since otherwise those would approximate the regular singular streamline. Now, the open ball
B_{\delta}(q_{c})
is of positive area
\pi\delta^{2}
and the velocity field
u=\nabla^{\perp}\psi
has no stagnation points in
B_{\delta}(q_{c})
, it moves points along their streamlines. We flow the ball by the field
u
. However, since all
q\in B_{\delta}(q_{c})
belong to a regular-singular streamline, eventually the flowed region compresses to a set of zero area, a contradiction.

Let now
p_{c}\in\Gamma_{c}
be such that
\nabla\psi(p_{c})=0
and assume that all
p\in B_{\delta}(p_{c})
do not lie on a regular streamline, for some
\delta>0
. Now,
\nabla\psi\equiv 0
in
B_{\delta}(p)
would contradict
\psi
having non-contractible streamlines and thus there must be some
q\in B_{\delta}(p_{c})
such that
\nabla\psi(q)\neq 0
. By continuity, there exists some
\delta_{1}>0
such that
\nabla\psi(p)\neq 0
, for all
p\in B_{\delta_{1}}(q)\subset B_{\delta}(p_{c})
. As before,
B_{\delta_{1}}(q)
has non-zero area and can be flowed by the field
u=\nabla^{\perp}\psi
. However, all
p\in B_{\delta_{1}}(q)
belong to regular-singular streamlines, (they cannot belong to singular streamlines because they have non-trivial gradient) so that eventually the ball compresses to a set of zero area. Thus, all points on regular-singular streamlines are not isolated from regular streamlines.
∎

2.2.
Construction of suitable fluid sub-domains

The proof of the above lemma shows that in
M
we must have regular streamlines. Otherwise all would necessarily be singular and the velocity field would necessarily be 0. Hence, to prove Theorem
1.1
, for any regular streamline
\Gamma_{c_{0}}\subseteq\{\psi=c_{0}\}
, we shall see that the flow on
\Gamma_{c_{0}}
is parallel on the periodic channel and circular on the annulus. To do so, for each regular streamline we devise an open bounded domain containing that streamline and where the stream-function satisfies a semilinear elliptic equation. As a matter of fact, these open bounded domains are doubly connected and have either parallel/circular boundaries or free boundaries where the stream-function enjoys over-determined conditions there.

To construct these fluid sub-domains we argue for the annulus since, by identification, the construction extends to the periodic channel. Now, by the Jordan curve Theorem, the smooth non-contractible curve
\Gamma_{c_{0}}
separates the annulus into an open interior region denoted by
\Omega_{\Gamma_{c_{0}}}
and an exterior region. Since
\nabla\psi\neq 0
in
\Gamma_{c_{0}}
, we have that
\partial_{\hat{n}}\psi\neq 0
, where
\hat{n}
denotes the exterior unit normal vector of
\partial\Omega_{\Gamma_{c_{0}}}=\Gamma_{c_{0}}
, and we may as well assume that
\partial_{\hat{n}}\psi<0
throughout
\Gamma_{c_{0}}
,
Next, we define

(2.1)

c_{-}:=\inf\{\psi(\Gamma_{c}):\Gamma_{c}\text{ is a streamline},\,\psi|_{\Gamma_{c}}=c\text{ and }\Omega_{\Gamma_{c}}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\Gamma_{c_{0}}}}\neq\emptyset\text{ has no singular streamlines}\}

and likewise

(2.2)

c_{+}:=\sup\{\psi(\Gamma_{c}):\Gamma_{c}\text{ is a streamline},\,\psi|_{\Gamma_{c}}=c\text{ and }\Omega_{\Gamma_{c_{0}}}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\Gamma_{c}}}\neq\emptyset\text{ has no singular streamlines}\}.

If
\partial_{n}\psi>0
in
\Gamma_{c_{0}}
, the definitions of
c_{-}
and
c_{+}
are modified accordingly.

Proposition 2.1
.

The supremum
c_{+}
and infimum
c_{-}
are achieved on streamlines
\Gamma_{c_{+}}
and
\Gamma_{c_{-}}
, respectively. The open set
D:=\Omega_{\Gamma_{c_{-}}}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\Gamma_{c_{+}}}}
does not contain any singular streamline and
c_{-}<\psi<c_{+}
in

. Additionally, if
\Gamma_{c_{-}}\subset M

\Gamma_{c_{+}}

Proof.

We shall argue for
c_{-}
, since the proof for the statements concerning
c_{+}
is the same. Firstly, by assumption we have that
\Gamma_{c_{0}}
is a regular streamline such that
\partial_{\hat{n}}\psi(p)<0
, for all
p\in\Gamma_{c_{0}}
. For any such
p
, by continuity, we have that
\nabla\psi(q)\neq 0
, for all
q\in B_{\delta}(p)
, for some
\delta>0
. Hence, for all

\psi(p+s\hat{n}(p))<\psi(p)

. As such, for all
s\in(0,\delta)

and it is not singular, since
\nabla\psi(p+s\hat{n}(p))\neq 0
. This shows that we are taking an infimum over a non-empty set.

Now, let
\Gamma_{c_{n}}
be a sequence of streamlines such that
\psi|_{\Gamma_{c_{n}}}=c_{n}>c
and
\Omega_{\Gamma_{c_{n}}}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\Gamma_{c_{0}}}}
has no singular streamlines, with
c_{n}\rightarrow c
. We choose
p_{n}\in\Gamma_{c_{n}}
and observe that the sequence of

live in the compact space
\overline{M}\setminus\Omega_{\Gamma_{c_{0}}}
, so that, up to a subsequence, they converge to some

such that, by continuity,
\psi(p)=c_{-}
. We then define
\Gamma_{c_{-}}
as the closed streamline of

that passes through

\Omega_{\Gamma_{c_{-}}}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\Gamma_{c_{0}}}}
has no singular streamlines. If

\Gamma_{c_{-}}=\mathbb{T}\times\{1\}
, while if

\Gamma_{c_{-}}
. If

The obvious modifications to the above argument also show the existence of a streamline

has no singular streamlines. Since
\Gamma_{c_{0}}

c_{-}<\psi<c_{+}

\partial_{\hat{n}}\psi(q)<0

\Gamma_{c}

2.3.
A semilinear elliptic equation for the streamfunction

Once we have found a fluid sub-domain

containing only regular and regular-singular streamlines, the next step is to show that

D=\Omega_{\Gamma_{0}}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\Gamma_{1}}}

\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{1}
are streamlines of

.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
D=\Omega_{\Gamma_{0}}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\Gamma_{1}}}
. Let
\psi
be a steady Euler solution in
D
such that all streamlines are closes non-contractible curves and they are not singular. Then, there exists
f\in C^{1}(0,1)\cap C[0,1]
such that

(2.3)

\begin{cases}\Delta\psi=f(\psi),\quad 0<\psi<1&\text{ in }D,\\
\psi=0,&\text{ in }\Gamma_{0},\\
\psi=1,&\text{ in }\Gamma_{1},\end{cases}

Proof.

We begin by showing that
\psi
must attain different values in each connected component of
\partial D
, namely
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{1}
. Indeed, if
\psi
were to attain the same value, then the global maximum or minimum of
\psi
in
\overline{D}
is attained in
D
. If both maxima and minima are attained in the boundary, then
\psi
would be constant in
D
and thus contain singular streamlines in
D
. Assume by simplicity that the maximum is attained in
D
. Now, by hypothesis this maximal value
c
must be achieved along a closed non-contractible streamline
\Gamma_{c}
. Since it is the maximum in
D
, it must have
\nabla\psi=0
at all points where it is achieved, hence
\nabla\psi|_{\Gamma_{c}}\equiv 0
. Thus,
\Gamma_{c}
would be a singular streamline in
D
, a contradiction.

Hence, up to addition and multiplication by constants (which do not alter the streamline topology), we assume that
\psi=0
in
\Gamma_{0}
and
\psi=1
in
\Gamma_{1}
. Let
c\in[0,1]
, which is the range of
\psi
in
\overline{D}
and let
p\in\overline{D}
such that
\psi(p)=c
. We define
f(c)=\Delta\psi(p)
. We shall see that

is well-defined, it does not depend on the choice of

. To do so, consider
c\in[0,1]
and
\Gamma_{c}
the unique associated streamline. Its uniqueness stems from the fact that two or more distinct streamlines attaining the same value imply the existence of global extrema in the region bounded by these streamlines, and there are no singular streamlines in
D
. From the Euler equations, we see that
\nabla\Delta\psi
is parallel to
\nabla\psi
and thus, if
\Gamma_{c}
is a regular streamline, then
\Delta\psi
is automatically constant on
\Gamma_{c}
and

is well defined.

If
\Gamma_{c}
is a regular-singular streamline, assume there exists
p_{1},p_{2}\in\Gamma_{c}
such that
\Delta\psi(p_{1})=\omega(p_{1})<\omega(p_{2})=\Delta\psi(p_{2})
, and let
\epsilon:=\frac{\omega(p_{2})-\omega(p_{1})}{4}
. By continuity, there exists
\delta>0
such that
|\omega(q)-\omega(p_{i})|<\epsilon
, for all
q\in B_{\delta}(p_{i})
, for

. Since points on regular-singular stream lines are not isolated from points on regular streamlines, there exists
q_{i}\in B_{\delta}(p_{i})
such that
\psi(q_{i})=c_{i}<c
and
q_{i}\in\Gamma_{c_{i}}
is a regular streamline.

If
c_{1}=c_{2}
, then
\Gamma_{c_{1}}=\Gamma_{c_{2}}
, since otherwise we would have local extrema and thus a singular streamline within the fluid domain. Hence,
\omega(q_{1})=f(c_{1})=f(c_{2})=\omega(q_{2})
, it is constant on the regular streamline
\Gamma_{c_{1}}=\Gamma_{c_{2}}
. One one hand, since
q_{1}\in B_{\delta}(p_{1})
,
\omega(q_{1})
is at most
\frac{\omega(p_{2})-\omega(p_{1})}{4}
away from
\omega(p_{1})
, while on the other hand, since
q_{2}\in B_{\delta}(p_{2})
, we have that
\omega(q_{2})=\omega(q_{1})
is also
\frac{\omega(p_{2})-\omega(p_{1})}{4}
away from
\omega(p_{2})
. This is clearly impossible. If, say,
c>c_{1}>c_{2}
, then by the intermediate value theorem there exists some
q_{2}^{*}\in B_{\delta}(p_{2})
such that
\psi(q_{2}^{*})=\psi(q_{1})=c_{1}
, namely there is some point of
\Gamma_{c_{1}}
in
B_{\delta}(p_{2})
. Since
\omega=\omega(q_{1})
on
\Gamma_{c}
, we have that
\omega(q_{2}^{*})=\omega(q_{1})
and
q_{2}^{*}\in B_{\delta}(p_{2})
, a contradiction with the continuity of
\omega
. Therefore,
\omega
is constant in
\Gamma_{c}
. The case
c=0
or
c=1
is treated similarly;
f(i)
is well defined if
\Gamma_{i}
is a regular or regular-singular streamline singular streamline. If it is a singular streamline, since any point on a singular streamline is not isolated from points lying on regular streamlines, see Lemma
2.1

and following the argument for regular-singular streamlines we conclude that

is well-defined as well.

The next step in the proof is to show that

is continuous. To do so, let

, with
c_{n}\rightarrow c
, for some

. For each

we denote

the unique streamline in

where

and let

(2.4)

f(c_{n})=\Delta\psi(p_{n})\rightarrow\Delta\psi(p)=f(c),

thus concluding that

The last step in the proof is to show that actually

we shall obtain a

expression for

in

,
\Gamma_{c}
its unique associated streamline in

and

such that

. This is possible because all streamlines in

(2.5)

\begin{cases}\sigma^{\prime}(t)=\nabla\psi(\sigma(t)),&t\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon),\\
\sigma(0)=q,\end{cases}

for some

∎

Remark

Lemma 2.3

Let

\begin{cases}\Delta\psi=f(\psi),\quad 0<\psi<1&\text{ in }D,\\

If

3.
Over-determined elliptic equations in the periodic channel

In this section we assume

3.1.
The periodic channel

We study here (
2.7
), which takes place when there are no singular streamlines in the periodic channel
\mathbb{T}\times(0,1)
. In particular, the domain where the equation is satisfied already has straight horizontal boundaries, and this fact is enough to constrain all solutions
\psi
of (
2.7
) to be
x
-translational invariant, that is, one-dimensional.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\psi
be a solution of (
2.7
). Then,
\psi=\psi(y)
.

Proof.

We shall use the sliding method. Take
\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
, with
\xi_{2}>0
and
\tau>0
. We define the comparison function

(3.1)

w^{\tau}(\mathbf{x})=\psi(\mathbf{x}+\tau\xi)-\psi(\mathbf{x})

and denote

(3.2)

J:=\{\tau\in(0,1/\xi_{2}):,\quad w^{\tau}(\mathbf{x})<0,\text{ for all }\mathbf{x}\in D^{\tau}\},

where further

(3.3)

D^{\tau}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in D\,:\mathbf{x}+\tau\xi\in D\}=\mathbb{T}\times(0,1-\tau\xi_{2})

Since
\psi(x,0)=1
and
\psi(x,1)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{T}
, by continuity there holds
w^{\tau}(\mathbf{x})<0
in
D^{\tau}
, for
\tau\xi_{2}
sufficiently close to 1 and we see that
J
is non-empty. Next, we define
\tau_{0}:=\inf J
and we claim that
\tau_{0}=0
. Otherwise, if
\tau_{0}>0
then by continuity of
w^{\tau}
and by definition of infimum, there exists some
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\overline{D^{\tau_{0}}}
such that
w^{\tau_{0}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=0
. Given that
0<\psi<1
in
D
, we see that
w^{\tau_{0}}(\mathbf{x})<0
, for all
\mathbf{x}\in\partial D^{\tau_{0}}
, since either
\mathbf{x}=(x,0)
or
\mathbf{x}+\tau_{0}\xi=(x+\tau_{0}\xi_{1},1)
. Therefore, both
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and
\mathbf{x}_{0}+\tau_{0}\xi
are interior points of
D^{\tau_{0}}
, where

is
C^{1}
and thus we can conclude from the maximum principle that

, initially in a small ball centred at
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and then by unique continuation (recall

in the interior of

) and finally to the whole of

, thus contradicting

there. As a result,

. Finally, we take

so that now

in

. This requires

to be invariant by arbitrary horizontal translations, namely

alone.
∎

3.2.
Over-determined elliptic equations with free boundaries

We now turn to the over-determined elliptic problems (
2.8
) and (
2.9
). In general, we cannot directly apply the sliding method used in Proposition
3.1
here because the domain
D
may not be invariant under

-translations. Instead, since
\nabla\psi=0
on the free boundaries, we choose to extend the stream-function
\psi
from

to the periodic channel
M=\mathbb{T}\times(0,1)
through its boundary values. Namely, we extend

in the region of

bounded above by
\Gamma_{1}
and
\psi\equiv 0
in the region of
M
bounded below by
\Gamma_{0}
. This extension produces a
C^{1}
function that weakly satisfies a modified semilinear elliptic equation, now in a domain with horizontal straight boundaries.

However, the non-linearity is no longer
C^{1}
in the interior of this larger domain, and the sliding method is still not an option. Nevertheless, the periodic channel now enjoys symmetry and, once
\psi
and
M
are evenly reflected to
\mathbb{T}\times(-1,0)
, the semilinear elliptic equation falls within the applicability of the theory of local symmetry through the Continuous Steiner Symmetrization, a technique developed by Brock in
[
5
]
to ascertain the symmetry of positive weak solutions to elliptic problems in domains with symmetries. Here we introduce the concept of local symmetry of functions, and we refer the interested reader to
[
5
,
6
]
for a complete account on the Continuous Steiner Symmetrization and its usability on variational problems and elliptic equations.

Definition 1
.

Let
\psi\geq 0
and
\psi\in C^{1}(\{0<\psi<\sup\psi\})
. Suppose this last set is open. Let
\mathbf{x}_{1}=(x_{1},y_{1})
such that

0<\psi(\mathbf{x}_{1})<\sup\psi,\quad\partial_{y}\psi(\mathbf{x}_{1})>0,

and let
\mathbf{x}_{2}=(x_{1},y_{2})
be the unique point such that

y_{2}>y_{1},\quad\psi(\mathbf{x}_{1})=\psi(\mathbf{x}_{2})<\psi(x_{1},y),\quad\forall y\in(y_{1},y_{2}).

We say that
\psi
is locally symmetric in the direction
y
if

\partial_{x}\psi(\mathbf{x}_{1})=\partial_{x}\psi(\mathbf{x}_{2}),\quad\partial_{y}\psi(\mathbf{x}_{1})=-\partial_{y}\psi(\mathbf{x}_{2}).

Locally symmetric functions admit a decomposition of their support,

\{0<\psi<\sup\psi\}=\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\left(U_{k}^{1}\cup U_{k}^{2}\right)\bigcup S,

where
U_{k}^{1}
is a maximally connected component of
\{0<\psi<\sup\psi\}\cap\{\partial_{y}\psi>0\}
,
U_{k}^{2}
denotes its reflection about the hyperplane
\{y=y_{k}\}
, for some
y_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\partial_{y}\psi=0
in
S
. In other word, for any
k\geq 1
and any
\mathbf{x}=(x,y)\in U_{k}^{1}
, there holds

(3.4)

\psi(x,y)=\psi(x,2y_{k}-y)<\psi(x,z),\quad\forall z\in(y,2y_{k}-y).

The main goal of this section is to first show that appropriate extensions and transformations of solutions to (
2.8
) and (
2.9
) are locally symmetric, and then upgrade this local symmetry to global symmetry of the original solutions. To do that, we first need a preliminary technical lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
D=\Omega_{\Gamma_{0}}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\Gamma_{1}}}
and
\psi
be a solution of

(3.5)

\begin{cases}\Delta\psi=f(\psi),\quad 0<\psi<1&\text{ in }D,\\
\psi=0,&\text{ in }\Gamma_{0},\\
\psi=1,&\text{ in }\Gamma_{1},\end{cases}

for some

contains no singular streamlines of
\psi
and
\partial_{y}\psi<0
on all regular streamlines of
\psi
in

. Then, all regular-singular streamlines contain points where

Proof.

Firstly, by density of regular streamlines in

\partial_{y}\psi\leq 0
in
D
. Let
\Gamma_{c}
be a regular-singular streamline and assume towards a contradiction that

. Since it is a regular-singular streamline, there exists some

such that
\nabla\psi(p)\neq 0
, namely
\partial_{x}\psi(p)\neq 0
. By continuity, there exists some open ball
B_{\delta}(p)
such that
\partial_{x}\psi\neq 0
in
B_{\delta}(p)
. Next, we define
\gamma_{c}(s)=(\gamma_{c}^{1}(s),\gamma_{c}^{2}(s))
as the solution to

(3.6)

\begin{cases}\dot{\gamma}_{c}(s)=\nabla^{\perp}\psi(\gamma_{c}(s)),&\\
\gamma_{c}(0)=p,\end{cases}

which is well-defined for
|s|
sufficiently small, there exists some

such that
\gamma_{c}(s)\in B_{\delta}(p)
, for all
|s|<s_{0}
. By definition of
\gamma_{c}
, there holds
\psi(\gamma_{c}(s))=c
since

(3.7)

0=\dot{\gamma}_{c}^{1}(s)\partial_{x}\psi(\gamma_{c}(s))+\dot{\gamma}_{c}^{2}(s)\partial_{y}\psi(\gamma_{c}(s)),

namely,
\gamma_{c}(s)
gives a local parametrization of
\Gamma_{c}

4.
Over-determined elliptic equations in the annulus

In this section we assume
M=B_{R}\setminus\overline{B_{r}}
is an annulus, for some

. There is a rich literature on solutions satisfying over-determined elliptic boundary problems in annular domains, and so we shall draw from them, with the necessary modifications to our purposes. To deal with the equations in the annulus we first recall, see
[
5
]
, the concept of local radial symmetry.

Definition 2
.

Assume
\psi\geq 0
admits the decomposition

(4.1)

\{0<\psi<\sup\psi\}=\bigcup_{k\geq 1}U_{k}\bigcup S,

where
\nabla\psi=0
in
S

’s are pairwise disjoint annuli

0\leq r_{k}<R_{k}
and
\mathbf{x}_{k}\in\{0<\psi<\sup\psi\}
. Assume further that
\psi
is radially symmetric in
U_{k}
, namely

(4.2)

\psi(\mathbf{x})=\psi(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{k}|),\quad\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\rho}(\mathbf{x})<0,

for all
\mathbf{x}\in U_{k}
, where
\rho=|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{k}|
, and
\psi(\mathbf{x})\geq\psi|_{\partial B_{r_{k}}(\mathbf{x}_{k})}
, for all
k\geq 1
. Then, we say that
\psi
is locally radially symmetric
.

In this section we show that solutions to (
2.7
), (
2.8
) and (
2.9
) are locally radially symmetric. Exploiting the structure of the streamlines in the fluid sub-domains, we are able to prove that the set

is empty and the solutions are

2.7
) for the annular domain

must be radial. Now, the problem does not, a priori, have over-determined boundary conditions, but the boundaries are already circular sets. The following result exploits this fact to conclude solutions are radial.

Proposition 4.1
.

M=B_{R}\setminus\overline{B_{r}}

\psi

\psi=\psi(r)
.

Proof.

We extend
\psi\equiv 1
in

(4.3)

\begin{cases}\Delta\psi=f(\psi),\quad 0<\psi<1,&\text{in }M=B_{R}\setminus B_{r},\\
\psi\equiv 1,&\text{in }\overline{B_{r}},\end{cases}

for some

, with

and
\psi\in C^{3}(M)
. Hence, we can apply Corollary 7.3 of
[
5
]
to conclude that
\psi
is locally symmetric. Hence,

(4.4)

M=\bigcup_{k\geq 1}U_{k}\bigcup S,

where
\nabla\psi=0
in
S
and the
U_{k}
’s are pairwise disjoint annuli
B_{r_{k}}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\setminus\overline{B_{r_{k}}(\mathbf{x}_{k})}
, with
0\leq r_{k}<R_{k}
and
\mathbf{x}_{k}\in D
. Moreover,
\psi
is radially symmetric in
U_{k}
. Any regular streamline must be entirely contained in only one of these
U_{k}
’s, since regular streamlines are connected sets with
\nabla\psi\neq 0
on them and the
U_{k}
’s are pairwise disjoint. Moreover, since all streamlines are closed and non-contractible loops, these annuli
U_{k}
must all be nested and their centres
\mathbf{x}_{k}
must lie within
B_{r}
.

On the other hand, the set
S
may, a priori, be non-empty, given the possible existence of regular-singular streamlines. However, we shall see this is not the case. Indeed, assume
\Gamma_{c}
is a regular-singular streamline and let
q\in\Gamma_{c}
be such that
\nabla\psi(q)\neq 0
. Then,
q\in U_{k}
for some
k\geq 1
, so that
r_{k}<|q-\mathbf{x}_{k}|<R_{k}
. Therefore, the set
\Gamma:=\{\mathbf{x}\in M:|x-\mathbf{x}_{k}|=|q-\mathbf{x}_{k}|\}
is a closed non-contractible circle, contained in
U_{k}
and, more importantly, it is such that
\psi(\mathbf{x})=\psi(q)=c
, for all
\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma
. Therefore, it must be
\Gamma_{c}=\Gamma
and
\partial_{\rho}\psi<0
in
\Gamma_{c}
, thus concluding that there are no regular-singular streamlines. Hence,
S
is empty and since
M
is connected, the union is made of only one annuli,
M
itself. Therefore,
\psi
is radially symmetric and decreasing in
M
,
u
is a radial flow.
∎

We next show that solutions to the semilinear elliptic problem (
2.8
) with one overdetermined boundary must be circular.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\psi
a solution to (
2.8
) in
D=M\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\Gamma_{1}}}
such that all the streamlines of
\psi
in
D
are not singular. Then,
\psi=\psi(r)
.

Proof.

Firstly, we extend
\psi\equiv 1
in
\Omega_{\Gamma_{1}}
and define

(4.5)

f_{0}(t)=-f(t)+g(t),\quad g(t)=\begin{cases}f(1),&t=1,\\
0,&t<1.\end{cases}

It is clear to see that
\psi
now weakly satisfies

(4.6)

\begin{cases}-\Delta\psi=f_{0}(\psi),&\text{in }B_{R},\\
\psi=0,&\text{on }\partial B_{R},\\
\psi\geq 0,&\text{in }B_{R}\end{cases}

and
\{0<\psi<1\}=D
is an open set, with
\psi\in C^{1}(D)
. Note that since
f(t)
is continuous and
g(t)
is non-increasing, we can use Theorem 7.2 of
[
5
]
to deduce that
\psi
is locally symmetric. As in the proof of Proposition
4.1
, we show, thanks to the local radial symmetry, that any regular-singular streamline is in fact regular. Hence the set
S
is empty and
\psi
is globally radially symmetric and decreasing, we omit the details.
∎

We finish the section proving that solutions to the overdetermined boundary value problem (
2.9
) must also be one-dimensional and confined in an annular domains.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\psi
a solution to (
2.9
) in
D=\Omega_{\Gamma_{0}}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\Gamma_{1}}}
such that all the streamlines of
\psi
in

are not singular. Then,

is an annulus and
\psi
is radially symmetric and decreasing in

.

Proof.

The strategy of the proof is similar to that of Proposition

. We first extend
\psi\equiv 1
in
\Omega_{\Gamma_{1}}
and
\psi\equiv 0
in
M\setminus\overline{D}
. Define as well

(4.7)

f_{0}(t)=-f(t)+g(t),\quad g(t)=\begin{cases}f(1),&t=1,\\
0,&0<t<1\\
f(0),&t=0.\end{cases}

so that
\psi
now weakly satisfies

(4.8)

\begin{cases}-\Delta\psi=f_{0}(\psi),&\text{in }B_{R},\\
\psi=0,&\text{on }\partial B_{R},\\
\psi\geq 0,&\text{in }B_{R}\end{cases}

and
\{0<\psi<1\}=D
is an open set, with
\psi\in C^{1}(D)
. Since
f(t)
is continuous and

is non-increasing, we can again apply Theorem 7.2 of
[

]
to deduce that

is locally symmetric. The arguments to conclude that

is empty,

∎

5.
Free Boundary Problems: proof of Corollary
1.1

In this section we generalize Theorem
1.1
to more general annular domains in either
\mathbb{R}^{2}
or
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
in Corollary
1.1
. In order to conclude that stationary solutions must either be radial or shear, since now the boundaries are no longer assumed to be circular, respectively horizontal, and keeping in mind the flexibility results of
[
11
]
, we must assume enhanced boundary conditions of the velocity field.

We consider here the free-boundary Euler equations posed in an open bounded domain
M=\Omega_{\Gamma_{0}}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\Gamma_{1}}}
, where
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{1}
denote two non intersecting closed non-contractible curves either in
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
or in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The equations read

(5.1)

\displaystyle u\cdot\nabla u

\displaystyle=-\nabla p\quad\ \text{in}\ M,

(5.2)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot u

\displaystyle=0\quad\quad\ \ \ \text{in}\ M,

(5.3)

\displaystyle u\cdot\hat{n}

\displaystyle=0\quad\quad\ \ \ \text{on}\ \partial M=\Gamma_{0}\cup\Gamma_{1},

(5.4)

\displaystyle|u|

\displaystyle=a_{i}\quad\quad\ \ \ \text{on}\ \Gamma_{i},

for some
a_{i}\geq 0
. Given the general strategy for the proof of Theorem
1.1
, the proof of Corollary
1.1
is now reduced to establishing rigidity results for the stream-function
\psi
, which now solves a modified version of (
2.9
), namely

(5.5)

\begin{cases}\Delta\psi=f(\psi),\quad 0<\psi<1,&\text{ in }D=\Omega_{\Gamma_{0}}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\Gamma_{1}}},\\
\psi=0,\quad|\nabla\psi|=a_{0},&\text{ on }\Gamma_{0},\\
\psi=1,\quad|\nabla\psi|=a_{1},&\text{ on }\Gamma_{1},\end{cases}

for some
f\in C[0,1]\cap C^{1}(0,1)
, with
a_{0},a_{1}\geq 0
and the further assumption that
D=\Omega_{\Gamma_{0}}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\Gamma_{1}}}
contains no singular streamlines of
\psi
. In case
M
is an annular domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, Corollary
1.1
is a direct consequence of Theorem 1.15 in
[
41
]
. A close inspection of the proof shows that the assumption
\nabla\psi\neq 0
in
D
there can be replaced by the assumption of
\psi
being laminar and
D
containing no singular stream-lines of
\psi
. As we have previously remarked, see Lemma
2.2
, this already ensures the existence of a
C^{1}(0,1)
function
f
such that (
5.5
) is satisfied. We now focus our discussion for subsets
M
of the periodic strip.

5.1.
Degenerate free boundary problems in the periodic channel

We assume
M\subset\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
is diffeomorphic to a periodic channel. If
a_{0}=a_{1}=0
, we can directly use Proposition
3.3
to conclude that
u
is a shear flow. In what follows we assume that the
a_{i}
’s are not zero simultaneously. Consider first that
a_{1}=0
and
a_{0}>0
. By rotational invariance of the Laplacian, the case
a_{1}>0
and
a_{0}=0
is treated rotating the domain by
\pi
degrees. In this subsection we further assume the degenerate case
f(1)=0
, which corresponds to assuming that the vorticity is also zero on the free boundary. We have the following result.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\psi
be a solution to

(5.6)

\begin{cases}\Delta\psi=f(\psi),\quad 0<\psi<1,&\text{ in }D=\Omega_{\Gamma_{0}}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\Gamma_{1}}},\\
\psi=0,\quad|\nabla\psi|=a_{0}>0,&\text{ on }\Gamma_{0},\\
\psi=1,\quad|\nabla\psi|=0,&\text{ on }\Gamma_{1},\end{cases}

for some
f\in C([0,1])\cap C^{1}(0,1)
and assume that
D
contains no singular streamlines of
\psi
. Then,
\psi=\psi(y)
.

Proof.

The proof is based on a local symmetry result of Brock in
[
6
]
, see also Theorem 1.15 in
[
41
]
. Assume without loss of generality that
D
embeds in the periodic channel, namely that
D\subset M=\mathbb{T}\times[0,1]
, and denote
M_{0}=\mathbb{T}\times\{0\}
the bottom boundary of
M
. We extend
\psi\equiv 1
the region in
\mathbb{T}\times[0,1]
bounded above by
\Gamma_{1}
and we denote
D_{0}=\Omega_{\Gamma_{0}}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{M_{0}}}
, the region in
M
bounded above by
\Gamma_{0}
. Since
\nabla\psi=0
on
\Gamma_{1}
and
f(1)=0
, we see that
\psi
now satisfies

(5.7)

\begin{cases}\Delta\psi=f(\psi),\quad 0<\psi<1,&\text{ in }D_{0},\\
\psi=0,\quad|\nabla\psi|=a_{0}>0,&\text{ on }\Gamma_{0},\\
\end{cases}

pointwise, with
f\in C([0,1])
. As in the proof of Proposition
3.2
, let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and denote
\varphi_{\lambda}
the
\lambda
-translated reflection of
\psi
, that is

(5.8)

\varphi_{\lambda}(x,y)=\begin{cases}\psi(x,y),&(x,y)\in D_{0}\\
\psi(x-\lambda,-y),&(x,-y)\in D_{0},\end{cases}

which is defined in
\mathcal{D}=D_{0}\cup\pi_{\lambda}(D_{0})
, where
\pi_{\lambda}(D_{0})
denotes the
\lambda
-translated in
x
symmetric reflection of
D
with respect to
y=0
. Now,
\mathcal{D}
is connected and
\varphi_{\lambda}\in C^{2}_{0}(\mathcal{D})
is a strong solution to

(5.9)

\begin{cases}\Delta\varphi_{\lambda}=f(\varphi_{\lambda})&\text{ in }\mathcal{D}\\
\varphi_{\lambda}>0&\text{ in }\mathcal{D}\\
\varphi_{\lambda}=0,&\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{D}.\end{cases}

Moreover,
\{0<\varphi_{\lambda}<1\}=\mathcal{D}
. The proof of Theorem 1 in
[
6
]
, see also the proof of Proposition 5.3 in
[
41
]
, shows that for all
\epsilon>0
we have that
(\varphi_{\lambda}-\epsilon)_{+}
is locally symmetric in the
y
variable, for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence, arguing as in the proof of Proposition
3.2
, we conclude that
(\varphi_{\lambda}-\epsilon)_{+}
is symmetric with respect to the horizontal axis
\{y=0\}
, for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Therefore, for all
(x,y)\in D_{\varepsilon}=\{\epsilon<\psi<1\}
, there holds

(5.10)

\psi(x,y)=\varphi_{\lambda}(x,y)=\varphi_{\lambda}(x,-y)=\psi(x-\lambda,y),

for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence, we conclude that
\psi=\psi(y)
alone in
D_{\epsilon}
. Moreover, since
0<\psi<1
in
D
, we see that
D_{\epsilon}
exhaust
D
and, as a result, we obtain
\psi=\psi(y)
in
D
. The proof is finished.
∎

5.2.
A moving plane argument

To finish the proof of Corollary
1.1
, we still have to consider two more cases: either
a_{1}=0
,
f(1)\neq 0
and
a_{0}>0
or
a_{1},a_{0}>0
. These can be treated jointly. To conclude translational invariance of solutions to (
5.5
), we will use the sliding method presented in the proof of Proposition
3.1
. To do that, we need to adapt the sliding method to the boundaries not being horizontal and we first need to show that the boundaries are given by
C^{2}
graphs in the periodic variable
x\in\mathbb{T}
. This is the purpose of the following result, which uses a moving plane argument.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\psi
be a solution to

(5.11)

\begin{cases}\Delta\psi=f(\psi),\quad 0<\psi<1,&\text{ in }D=\Omega_{\Gamma_{0}}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\Gamma_{1}}},\\
\psi=0,\quad|\nabla\psi|=a_{0},&\text{ on }\Gamma_{0},\\
\psi=1,\quad|\nabla\psi|=a_{1},&\text{ on }\Gamma_{1},\end{cases}

for some
f\in C^{1}([0,1])
. Assume that
D
contains no singular streamlines of
\psi
and that
f(i)\neq 0
if
a_{i}=0
. Then,
\Gamma_{i}=\{(x,h_{i}(x)):\,x\in\mathbb{T}\}
, for some
h_{i}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{T})
, for
i=0,1
.

Proof.

We will show that
\Gamma_{0}
is given by a graph function. It will be clear from the arguments that the same conclusion applies for
\Gamma_{1}
. In what follows, we implement a moving plane argument to show that
\partial_{y}\psi<0
on
\Gamma_{0}
, since this is enough to then conclude, via the Implicit Function Theorem, that
\Gamma_{0}=\{(x,h_{0}(x)):\,x\in\mathbb{T}\}
, for some
h_{0}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{T})
. To that purpose, since both
\Gamma_{i}
are bounded closed curves, we embed
D
in a periodic channel
M
, which we assume to be
M=\mathbb{T}\times(0,1)
. We then extend
\psi\equiv 1
in
\Omega_{\Gamma_{1}}
, the region in
M
bounded by
\Gamma_{1}
. For any
\lambda\geq 0
, we define

(5.12)

H_{\lambda}=\{y=\lambda\},\quad H_{\lambda}^{+}=\{y>\lambda\},\quad H_{\lambda}^{-}\{y<\lambda\},

and, for any set
A\subset\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
, we denote

(5.13)

A_{\lambda}^{+}=A\cap H_{\lambda}^{+},\quad A_{\lambda}^{-}=A\cap H_{\lambda}^{-}.

Next, let
\pi_{\lambda}
denote the reflection with respect to the hyperplane
H_{\lambda}
and, to ease notation, we denote
\Omega_{i}:=\Omega_{\Gamma_{i}}
. Now, let
\mu_{m}=\min\{y:\,(x,y)\in\Gamma_{0}\}
and define

(5.14)

I=\{\mu\geq\mu_{m}:\,\forall\lambda\geq\mu,\,\pi_{\lambda}(\overline{\Omega_{i,\lambda}^{+}})\subset\Omega_{i},\quad(-1)^{i}\nu_{2}(p)>0,\,\forall p\in\Gamma_{i}\cap H_{\lambda},\,i=0,1\}.

Here
\nu_{2}(p)
denotes the second component of the exterior normal unit vector of
\Omega_{0}
. The set
I
denotes all
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
for which the reflection of
\Omega_{i,\lambda}^{+}
fall strictly inside
\Omega_{i}
. Clearly,
I
is bounded below and non-empty, since
\mu_{1}:=\sup\{y:(x,y)\in D\}
belongs to it. Let
\mu_{0}:=\inf I
and assume
\mu_{0}>\mu_{m}
. Then, the infimum takes place when either the reflected set becomes tangential to its boundary, or when the boundary becomes parallel to the vertical vector
\mathbf{e}_{2}=(0,1)
. As in the proof of Proposition
3.1
, for all
\lambda\in I
we define the comparison function

(5.15)

w^{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})=\psi(\pi_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}))-\psi(\mathbf{x}),

for all
\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma_{\lambda}:=\Omega_{0,\lambda}^{+}\setminus\pi_{\lambda}(\overline{\Omega_{1,\lambda}^{-}})
, the set of all points in
\Omega_{0,\lambda}^{+}
such that their reflection does not fall into
\overline{\Omega_{1,\lambda}^{-}}
. In particular,
\pi_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})\neq\overline{\Omega_{1}}
, for all
\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma(\lambda)
, since
\pi_{\lambda}(\Omega_{0,\lambda}^{+})\cap\Omega_{0}=\emptyset
, for all
\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma(\lambda)
. Moreover, we set

(5.16)

J=\{\lambda\in I:w^{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})>0,\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma(\lambda)\}

and we claim that
J
is non-empty. Indeed, it can be seen that
(\mu_{1}-\epsilon,\mu_{1})\subset J
, for some
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small. This is clear since
-\partial_{y}\psi=\partial_{n}\psi=|u|=a_{0}>0
at the maximal point on
\Gamma_{0}
, and thus by continuity
\partial_{y}\psi<0
in
\Omega_{0,\lambda}^{+}
, for all
\lambda<\mu_{1}
sufficiently close to
\mu_{1}
.

We next define
\lambda_{0}=\inf J
and claim that
\lambda_{0}\geq\mu_{0}
. If not,
\lambda_{0}>\mu_{0}
gives a contradiction as in the proof of Theorem 3 in
[
35
]
. One reaches a contradiction after using the maximum principle and the Hopf Lemma on balls in
D
, where
0<\psi<1
and
f
is known to be
C^{1}(0,1)
. We omit the details. We then conclude that
\lambda_{0}=\mu_{0}
. Since we assume
\mu_{0}>\mu_{m}
, one of the following is true:

Case 1. Internal tangency

There is some
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\Gamma_{i}^{+}
such that
\pi_{\mu_{0}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\in\Gamma_{i}
, for
i=0
or
i=1
. By the overdetermined boundary condition
|u|=a_{i}>0
on
\Gamma_{i}
, we have

(5.17)

\partial_{\nu}w^{\mu_{0}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=0.

Due to the
C^{2}
regularity of
\Gamma_{i}
, which is straightforward if
a_{i}\neq 0
, and follows from Lemma
2.3
if
a_{i}=0
since then
f(i)\neq 0
, we can take a sufficiently small ball
B_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{*})
in
D
tangent to
\Gamma_{i}
at
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, so that
B_{r},\pi_{\mu_{0}}(B_{r})\subset D

\pi_{\mu_{0}}

(5.18)

d_{D}(\mathbf{x})\geq r-|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}|,\quad d_{D}(\pi_{\mu_{0}}(\mathbf{x}))\geq r-|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}|,

for all

. Since

whenever

[

to obtain

(5.19)

|f(\psi(\mathbf{x}_{1}))-f(\psi(\mathbf{x}_{2}))|\leq\frac{C}{\min\{d_{D}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\,d_{D}(\mathbf{x}_{2})\}}|\psi(\mathbf{x}_{1})-\psi(\mathbf{x}_{2})|,\quad\forall\mathbf{x}_{1},\,\mathbf{x}_{2}\in D,

for some

. Together with (

(5.20)

\left|g(\mathbf{x})\right|=\left|\frac{f(\psi(\pi_{\mu_{0}}(\mathbf{x})))-f(\psi(\mathbf{x}))}{\psi(\pi_{\mu_{0}}(\mathbf{x}))-\psi(\mathbf{x})}\right|\leq\frac{C}{r-|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}|},

for all
\mathbf{x}\in B_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{*})
. Then, since
d_{B_{r}}(\mathbf{x})=r-|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}|
, there holds
g(\mathbf{x})d_{B_{r}}(\mathbf{x})\in L^{\infty}(B_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{*}))
and since
w^{\mu_{0}}\geq 0
in
B_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{*})
, by the Hopf Lemma for singular operators, see
[
36
, Proposition 4.3]
, we conclude that
w^{\mu_{0}}\equiv 0
in
B
, and then by unique continuation in
D
thus reaching a contradiction with
0<\psi<1
in
D
,
\psi=0
on
\Gamma_{0}
and
\psi=1
on
\Gamma_{1}
.

Case 2. Orthogonality of
\Gamma_{i}
and
H_{\mu_{0}}

Now, there is some
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\Gamma_{i}
such that
\nu_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=0

[

]
, see also
[
36
]
, there holds
\nabla^{2}w^{\mu_{0}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=0
thanks to the over-determined boundary conditions

in
\Gamma_{i}
. Hence, as before, we can place a sufficiently small ball

in
D
tangent to
D
at
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and such that
B,\pi_{\mu_{0}}(B)\subset D
. If

, then

is of class

up to the boundary and by the Serrin Corner Lemma, see
[

]
, we conclude that

in

and then in all

. This again gives a contradiction with

in

,
\psi=0
on
\Gamma_{0}
and
\psi=1
on
\Gamma_{1}
. Instead, if

and we can likewise use Serrin Corner Lemma for singular operators, see

to conclude that

, thus leading to a contradiction as well.

Figure 5.
On the left an internal tangency is represented at

.

Hence, it must be
\mu_{0}=\mu_{m}
. If

such that

, that is,
\Gamma_{0}=\{(x,h_{0}(x):\,x\in\mathbb{T}\}

\partial_{x}\psi(x,h_{0}(x))+h_{0}^{\prime}(x)\partial_{y}\psi(x,h_{0}(x))=0.

In case

, since

, we omit the details.

5.3.
Sliding Method for Two Free boundaries

Now that we know the free-boundaries are given by
C^{2}
graphs in the periodic variable
x\in\mathbb{T}
, we implement a sliding method to conclude that solutions must be invariant by
x
-translations, hence independent of
x
. We recall the fluid domain
D
is given by

(5.22)

D=\{(x,y):\,x\in\mathbb{T},\,h_{1}(x)<y<h_{0}(x)\}

Here,
\Gamma_{1}=\{(x,h_{1}(x))\}_{x\in\mathbb{T}}
and
\Gamma_{0}=\{(x,h_{0}(x))\}_{x\in\mathbb{T}}
denote the boundaries of
D
, and we assume at least one of them is not identically constant. Otherwise, Proposition
3.1
already ensures that solutions must correspond to shear flows.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let

|q_{0}-\mathbf{x}_{k}|<|q-\mathbf{x}_{k}|

U_{k}
are nested,
\psi
is radially decreasing there and possibly flat in
S
, in the former case we reach
\psi(q_{0})>\psi(q)
, while in the latter case we obtain
\psi(q_{0})<\psi(q)
. Both lead to
q_{0}\not\in A_{c}
, a contradiction. Hence, it must be
A_{c}=\Gamma
and
\partial_{\rho}\psi<0
in
A_{c}
, thus concluding that there are no regular-singular level sets.
∎

The channel scenario is equally similar. Sliding a point in a regular-singular level set with
\partial_{y}\psi<0
we obtain a closed non-contractible curve
\Gamma
such that
\partial_{y}\psi<0
there. Since
\partial_{y}\psi\leq 0
in
\mathbb{T}\times(0,1)
we then must have
A_{c}=\Gamma
as before, since any point
p
not in
\Gamma
must necessarily have
\psi(p)<c
or
\psi(p)>c
.

7.
Rigidity of shear flows: Proof of Theorem
1.2

After having obtained the above rigidity results for solutions to free boundary problems we are now in position to prove Theorem
1.2

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

The proof studies local properties of the background shear flow according to the vanishing of the vorticity on the set of stagnation points, in order to conclude that nearby stationary solutions are laminar. We shall see that if the vorticity is non-zero, we can find a small tubular region where sufficiently close velocity fields
u_{\varepsilon}
only vanish along an unique streamline, so that
u_{\varepsilon}
is laminar inside the tubular domain. If the background vorticity is zero, then we can construct a fluid sub-domain within a small tubular region where (1) the vorticity has no critical points there, (2) the vorticity vanishes on a closed non-contractible curve inside and (3) any stagnation points of
u_{\varepsilon}
must lie within this fluid sub-domain, so that
u_{\varepsilon}
is also laminar there. Throughout the proof we assume that
u_{\varepsilon}
is a
C^{2}(\overline{M})
steady solution of the Euler equations such that
\|u_{\varepsilon}-u\|_{C^{2}}=\varepsilon
, where
u=(U(y),0)
. For convenience, we assume that
U(0)=0
.

Step 1

Assume first that
U^{\prime}(0)=U_{0}^{\prime}\neq 0
. Then,
U(y)=\int_{0}^{y}U^{\prime}(s){\rm d}s\geq c_{0}y
, for all
\delta_{1}\geq y\geq 0
and there holds
|U(y)|\geq c_{0}\delta_{0}
, for all
\delta_{1}\geq|y|\geq\delta_{0}>0
. Moreover, by continuity,
|U^{\prime\prime}(y)|\geq c_{1}>0
, for all
|y|\leq\delta_{1}
, for some
\delta_{1}>0
sufficiently small.

Now, any sufficiently small in
C^{2}
perturbation
u_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
of the steady velocity field
(U(y),0)
is such that
|\partial_{y}\omega_{\varepsilon}|>\frac{c_{1}}{4}
, for all
|y|\leq\delta_{1}
, for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small and independent of
\delta_{1}
. On the other hand,
|u^{1}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)|\geq\frac{c_{0}}{2}\delta_{0}
, for all
|y|\geq\delta_{0}
and all
\varepsilon<\frac{c_{0}}{2}\delta_{0}
. Additionally, due to the strict monotonicity of
u^{1}_{\varepsilon}
, there exists a curve
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{T}\times(-1,1)
such that
u^{1}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=0
, for all
(x,y)\in\Gamma
. In fact, by the previous observation, such curve
\Gamma
is contained in
\mathbb{T}\times(-\delta_{0},\delta_{0})
and
u^{1}_{\varepsilon}>0
in the region bounded below by
\Gamma
and
u^{1}_{\varepsilon}<0
in the region bounded above by
\Gamma
.

Taking
\delta=\min\{\frac{c_{0}}{2}\delta_{0},\delta_{1}\}
, we see that
u^{1}_{\varepsilon}
vanish in
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{T}\times(-\delta,\delta)
and
|\partial_{y}\omega_{\varepsilon}|>0
in
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{T}\times(-\delta,\delta)
. In particular, from the Euler equations

(7.1)

0=u^{1}_{\varepsilon}\partial_{x}\omega_{\varepsilon}+u^{2}_{\varepsilon}\partial_{y}\omega_{\varepsilon}

we conclude that
u^{2}_{\varepsilon}\equiv 0
on
\Gamma
as well. Hence, in
\mathbb{T}\times(-\delta,\delta)
, we see that
u_{\varepsilon}
only vanishes on the streamline
\Gamma
.

Step 2

Assume now that
U^{\prime}_{0}=0
and
U_{0}^{\prime\prime}:=U^{\prime\prime}(0)>0
, say. Since
U\in C^{2}
, there exists some
\delta_{0}>0
such that
\frac{U_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{2}\leq U^{\prime\prime}(y)\leq\frac{3U_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{2}
in
(-\delta_{0},\delta_{0})
and there holds

-\frac{3U_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{2}y\leq\omega(y)\leq-\frac{U_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{2}y,

for all
y\in(0,\delta_{0})
and

-\frac{U_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{2}y\leq\omega(y)\leq-\frac{3U_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{2}y,

for all
y\in(-\delta_{0},0)
. Since
\partial_{y}\omega(y)=-U^{\prime\prime}(y)<-\frac{U_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{2}
any sufficiently small
C^{2}
perturbation
u_{\varepsilon}
of
u
must have
\partial_{y}\omega_{\varepsilon}\leq-\frac{U_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{4}
hence
\nabla\omega_{\varepsilon}\neq 0
in
\mathbb{T}\times(-\delta_{0},\delta_{0})
. We now find a fluid sub-domain in
\mathbb{T}\times(-\delta_{0},\delta_{0})
. Let
0<\delta_{1}<\delta_{2}<\delta_{0}
, such that
\delta_{1}<\frac{1}{8}\delta_{2}
and
\delta_{2}<\frac{1}{10}\delta_{0}<\frac{1}{8}\delta_{0}
. Then,

(7.2)

|\omega(y)|<\frac{3}{2}U_{0}^{\prime\prime}\delta,\text{ for all }|y|<\delta

and

(7.3)

\frac{U_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{2}\delta_{i}<|\omega(y)|<\frac{3}{2}U_{0}^{\prime\prime}\delta_{j},\text{ for all }\delta_{i}<|y|<\delta_{j}.

Therefore, any
\omega_{\varepsilon}
sufficiently close to
\omega
satisfies

(7.4)

|\omega_{\varepsilon}(x,y)|<2U_{0}^{\prime\prime}\delta,\text{ for all }|y|<\delta

and

(7.5)

\frac{U_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{4}\delta_{i}<|\omega_{\varepsilon}(x,y)|<2U_{0}^{\prime\prime}\delta_{j},\text{ for all }\delta_{i}<|y|<\delta_{j}.

Since
\delta_{1}<\frac{1}{8}\delta_{2}
, we deduce that for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{T}\times(-\delta_{1},\delta_{1})
the vortex-line
\Gamma_{\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})}\subset\mathbb{T}\times(-\delta_{2},\delta_{2})
and likewise, any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{T}\times(-\delta_{0}/10,\delta_{0}/10)
has its associated the vortex-line
\Gamma_{\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})}\subset\mathbb{T}\times(-\delta_{0},\delta_{0})
. More importantly, for all
\mathbf{x}=(x,y)
such that
\delta_{2}<|y|<\delta_{0}/10
, we have
|\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})|>\frac{U_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{4}\delta_{2}
and the associated vortex line
\Gamma_{\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})}\subset\mathbb{T}\times(-\delta_{0},\delta_{0})
is such that
\Gamma_{\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})}\cap\mathbb{T}\times(-\delta_{1},\delta_{1})=\emptyset
, since
|\omega_{\varepsilon}|<2U_{0}^{\prime\prime}\delta_{1}
there.

Now, let
\mathbf{x}_{1}=(x_{1},y_{1})
, for some
x_{1}\in\mathbb{T}
and
\delta_{2}<y_{1}<\delta_{0}/10
and let
\mathbf{x}_{2}=(x_{1},-y_{1})
. If
\omega_{\varepsilon}
is sufficiently close to
\omega
in
C^{1}
, there holds
\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{1})<\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{2})
. Let
\Gamma_{\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{i})}
be the associated vortex-lines. Since
\Gamma_{\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{i})}\subset\mathbb{T}\times(-\delta_{0},\delta_{0})
, we have
\nabla\omega_{\varepsilon}\neq 0
on
\Gamma_{\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{i})}
. Therefore,
\Gamma_{\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{i})}
are
C^{1}
closed non-contractible curves. Since the normal vector on
\Gamma_{\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{i})}
is proportional to
\nabla\omega_{\varepsilon}\neq 0
and
u_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\omega_{\varepsilon}=0
, we deduce that
u_{\varepsilon}
is tangent to
\Gamma_{\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{i})}
, and the region
D=\Omega_{\Gamma_{\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{1})}}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\Gamma_{\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{2})}}}
in
\mathbb{T}\times(-\delta_{0},\delta_{0})
constitutes an admissible fluid sub-domain in which
u_{\varepsilon}
is laminar.

Indeed, from the Euler equations we now observe that

(7.6)

\nabla^{\perp}\omega_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}=0

in
D
, with
u_{\varepsilon}\cdot\hat{n}=0
on
\partial D=\Gamma_{\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{1})}\cup\Gamma_{\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{2})}
. Now, since
\nabla\omega_{\varepsilon}\neq 0
, the vorticity effectively transports the stream-function along its vortex-lines. Therefore, by Lemma
2.2
, there exists some
g\in C([\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{2})])\cap C^{1}(\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{2}))
such that
\psi_{\varepsilon}=g(\omega_{\varepsilon})
in
D
, so that the stream-lines of
\psi
have the same topology as the vortex-lines of
\omega_{\varepsilon}
, which are closed non-contractible curves. Hence,
u_{\varepsilon}
is laminar in
D
. Furthermore, since
U(y)\geq\frac{U_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{4}y^{2}
in
(-\delta_{0},\delta_{0})
, we have that
U(y)\geq\frac{U_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{4}\delta_{1}^{2}
, for all
|y|\geq\delta_{1}
. Then, for sufficiently small perturbations
u_{\varepsilon}
of
u
, there holds
u^{1}_{\varepsilon}\geq\frac{U_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{8}\delta_{1}^{2}
, for all
|y|\geq\frac{\delta_{1}}{4}
.

Step 3

We can repeat the above arguments for finitely many other stagnation lines
y=y_{i}
of
U(y)
. For those
y_{i}
with
U^{\prime}(y_{i})\neq 0
, we can find a small periodic tube
\mathbb{T}\times(y_{i}-\delta_{i},y_{i}+\delta_{i})
where
u_{\varepsilon}=0
only on a closed non-contractible curve
\Gamma_{i}
. For those
y_{i}
with
U^{\prime}(y_{i})=0
, we likewise find fluid sub-domains
D_{i}=\Omega_{\Gamma_{i}^{-}}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\Gamma_{i}^{+}}}
within a small periodic tube
\mathbb{T}\times(y_{i}-\delta_{i},y_{i}+\delta_{i})
bounded by the vortex-lines (and also streamlines)
\Gamma_{i}^{-}
and
\Gamma_{i}^{+}
. Also,
u_{\varepsilon}
is laminar in
D_{i}
and any possible stagnation point of
u_{\varepsilon}
in
\mathbb{T}\times(y_{i}-\delta_{i},y_{i}+\delta_{i})
lies inside the fluid domain
D_{i}
. Set then
I=(-1,1)\setminus\cup_{i=0}^{m}(y_{i}-\delta_{i},y_{i}+\delta_{i})
and since
|U(y)|\geq c>0
on
I
, we note that
u^{1}_{\varepsilon}\neq 0
, and thus
u_{\varepsilon}\neq 0
, on
\mathbb{T}\times I
.

We now observe that
u_{\varepsilon}
is laminar in
\mathbb{T}\times(-1,1)
. To see this, note that the streamlines
\Gamma_{i}
,
\Gamma_{i}^{-}
and
\Gamma_{i}^{+}
decompose the periodic channel into regions bounded by them. Between any two
\Gamma_{i}^{-}
and
\Gamma_{i}^{+}
we have seen that
u_{\varepsilon}
is laminar, while since either
\Omega_{\Gamma_{i+1}}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\Gamma_{i}^{-}}}
or
\Omega_{\Gamma_{i+1}^{+}}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\Gamma_{i}^{-}}}
are fluid sub-domains (the boundaries are stream-lines of the flow,
u_{\varepsilon}
is tangent there) and
u_{\varepsilon}\neq 0
there,
u_{\varepsilon}
is laminar as well. Theorem
1.1
allows us to conclude that
u_{\varepsilon}
is a shear flow.
∎

Finally, we end with a much stronger rigidity result assuming non-vanishing velocity.
Consider the 2d Euler equation written for a perturbation
\omega
about a shear flow
(v(y),0)
:

(7.7)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\omega+v(y)\partial_{x}\omega-v^{\prime\prime}(y)\partial_{x}\Delta^{-1}\omega

\displaystyle=-u\cdot\nabla\omega.

with
u=K[\omega]
.
Under the non-vanishing assumption, this can be recast with
\psi=\Delta^{-1}\omega
as

(7.8)

\partial_{t}\omega+\nabla\cdot\left(|v(y)|^{2}\nabla\left(\frac{\partial_{x}\psi}{v(y)}\right)\right)=-u\cdot\nabla{\omega}.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
\bar{u}=(v(y),0)
be a non-stagnant shear flow (e.g. satisfying
\inf_{\Omega}|v|>0
) and
\Omega
be a periodic channel. There exists an
\varepsilon:=\varepsilon(\inf_{\Omega}|v|,\|v\|_{C^{1}},\Omega)
such that all stationary Euler states in
u
satisfying
\|u-\bar{u}\|_{W^{1,2+}(\Omega)}<\varepsilon
are shear flows.

Proof.

On time independence solutions (
\partial_{t}\omega=0)
, multiplying by
\frac{\partial_{x}\psi}{v(y)}
and integrating we find

\displaystyle\left(\inf_{\Omega}|v|^{2}\right)\left\|\nabla\left(\frac{\partial_{x}\psi}{v(y)}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int|v(y)|^{2}\left|\nabla\left(\frac{\partial_{x}\psi}{v(y)}\right)\right|^{2}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\partial_{x}\psi}{v(y)}u\cdot\nabla{\omega}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y=-\int{\omega}\nabla^{\perp}\psi\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{\partial_{x}\psi}{v(y)}\right){\rm d}x{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int{\omega}\left(\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}\psi\partial_{y}\psi}{v(y)}\right){\rm d}x{\rm d}y-\int v(y){\omega}\partial_{y}\left(\frac{\partial_{x}\psi}{v(y)}\right)\left(\frac{\partial_{x}\psi}{v(y)}\right){\rm d}x{\rm d}y.

Note first that there exists a universal constant
C>0
(depending only on
v
) such that

\displaystyle\left|\int v(y){\omega}\partial_{y}\left(\frac{\partial_{x}\psi}{v(y)}\right)\left(\frac{\partial_{x}\psi}{v(y)}\right){\rm d}x{\rm d}y\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\|v\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\omega\|_{L^{2+}}\left\|\nabla\left(\frac{\partial_{x}\psi}{v(y)}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Now note that

\displaystyle\int{\omega}\left(\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}\psi\partial_{y}\psi}{v(y)}\right){\rm d}x{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int v(y)\left|\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\partial_{x}\psi}{v(y)}\right)\right|^{2}\partial_{y}\psi{\rm d}x{\rm d}y+\int\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}\psi\partial_{y}^{2}\psi\partial_{y}\psi}{v(y)}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int v(y)\left|\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\partial_{x}\psi}{v(y)}\right)\right|^{2}\partial_{y}\psi{\rm d}x{\rm d}y+\frac{1}{2}\int\partial_{y}\left(\frac{\partial_{x}\psi}{v(y)}\right)\partial_{y}\partial_{x}\psi\partial_{y}\psi{\rm d}x{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int v(y)\left|\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\partial_{x}\psi}{v(y)}\right)\right|^{2}\partial_{y}\psi{\rm d}x{\rm d}y+\frac{1}{2}\int v(y)\left|\partial_{y}\left(\frac{\partial_{x}\psi}{v(y)}\right)\right|^{2}\partial_{y}\psi{\rm d}x{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\int v^{\prime}(y)\partial_{y}\left(\frac{\partial_{x}\psi}{v(y)}\right)\frac{\partial_{x}\psi}{v(y)}\partial_{y}\psi{\rm d}x{\rm d}y.

Whence, for some universal
C>0
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\int{\omega}\left(\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}\psi\partial_{y}\psi}{v(y)}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\|v\|_{C^{1}}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\left\|\nabla\left(\frac{\partial_{x}\psi}{v(y)}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

∎

We remark that by the result of Hamel and Nadirashvili
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
can already be used to say that sufficiently regular velocities within an
L^{\infty}
neighborhood of a non-vanishing shear are themselves shear flows. The interest in Theorem
7.1
is the brevity of the proof, and that it applies to much lower regularity equilibria.
Of course, Theorem
7.1
does not prevent nearby time periodic solutions with islands floating by with the current.

8.
Inviscid dynamical structures near shear flows: Proof of Theorem
1.3

We recap by recalling that Theorem
7.1
above, and also a Hamel and Nadirashvili’s result, shows that non-vanishing shear flows are isolated from non-shears in
L^{\infty}
of velocity. For Poiseuille flow
v(y)=y^{2}
, we proved in Theorem
1.2
that it is isolated in the
C^{2}
topology from non-shear flows. We will now show that this rigidity is sharp by proving that non-shear flows appear arbitrarily close to
(y^{n},0)
in the
C^{n-}
topology.

As usual, for
n\geq 0
and
0<\alpha<1
we define for
v=v(y):(-1,1)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

(8.1)

\quad\|v\|_{C^{n,\alpha}(-1,1)}:=\|v\|_{C^{n}(-1,1)}+|\partial_{y}^{n}v|_{C^{\alpha}(-1,1)},

where

(8.2)

\|v\|_{C^{n}(-1,1)}:=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\|\partial_{y}^{k}v\|_{L^{\infty}(-1,1)},\quad|v|_{C^{\alpha}(-1,1)}:=\sup_{y_{1}\neq y_{2}}\frac{|v(y_{1})-v(y_{2})|}{|y_{1}-y_{2}|^{\alpha}}.

The proof of the flexibility in spaces
C^{n-}=C^{n-1,\alpha}
, for all
0<\alpha<1
is carried out in two steps. Firstly, we approximate the shear flow
v(y)=y^{n}
by a zero velocity field in a small region. Then, we embed a compactly supported radial velocity field inside the approximating region. Since the background velocity is zero, this radial field constitutes a steady nonshear solution to Euler.

Let
\chi(\eta)
be a smooth cut-off function such that

(8.3)

\chi(\eta)=\begin{cases}0,&|\eta|\leq 1,\\
1,&|\eta|\geq 2.\end{cases}

and for
\varepsilon>0
consider the shear flow approximation
v_{\varepsilon}(y)=v(y)\chi\left(\frac{y}{\varepsilon}\right)
. The purpose of the following lemma is to show that
v(y)
is approximated by
v_{\varepsilon}(y)
in
C^{n-}
.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
. Then, there exists
\varepsilon_{1}>0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{1})
,

(8.4)

\|v-v_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{n-1,\alpha}(-1,1)}<\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{0}.

Proof.

For all
0\leq k\leq n-1
, a direct computation shows that

\displaystyle\|\partial_{y}^{k}(v-v_{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k}{k\choose i}\left\|\partial_{y}^{k-i}v(y)\partial_{y}^{i}\left(1-\chi\left(\frac{y}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{k}\left\|y^{n-k}\left(1-\chi\left(\frac{y}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varepsilon^{-i}\left\|y^{n-k+i}\chi^{(i)}\left(\frac{y}{\varepsilon}\right)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}

Recalling that
\chi(\eta)=1
for
|\eta|\geq 2
and
\chi^{(i)}(\eta)=0
for
|\eta|\not\in(1,2)
we conclude that

(8.5)

\|\partial_{y}^{k}(v-v_{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{k}\varepsilon^{n-k},

for some
C_{k}>0
. On the other hand, we have that

(8.6)

\left|\partial_{y}^{n-1}(v-v_{\varepsilon})\right|_{C^{\alpha}}\lesssim_{n}\left|y\left(1-\chi\left(\frac{y}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)\right|_{C^{\alpha}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left|y^{1+i}\varepsilon^{-i}\chi^{(i)}\left(\frac{y}{\varepsilon}\right)\right|_{C^{\alpha}},

where further, together with the properties of the smooth cut-off
\chi
, we have that

\displaystyle\left|y\left(1-\chi\left(\frac{y}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)\right|_{C^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\left|\frac{y}{\varepsilon}\left(1-\chi\left(\frac{y}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)\right|_{C^{\alpha}}=\varepsilon^{1-\alpha}\left|\eta\left(1-\chi\left(\eta\right)\right)\right|_{C^{\alpha}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{1-\alpha},

and similarly

(8.7)

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left|y^{1+i}\varepsilon^{-i}\chi^{(i)}\left(\frac{y}{\varepsilon}\right)\right|_{C^{\alpha}}=\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left|\left(\frac{y}{\varepsilon}\right)^{i+1}\chi^{(i)}\left(\frac{y}{\varepsilon}\right)\right|_{C^{\alpha}}\lesssim_{n}\varepsilon^{1-\alpha}.

The lemma follows from the definition of the
C^{n,\alpha}
norm and choosing
\varepsilon_{1}
sufficiently small.
∎

Now that we have created a region in space with zero background velocity, we embed there a compactly supported radial velocity field, which is given by a suitably scaled smooth radial profile
\mathbf{U}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathbf{U}(r)=0
, for
r>\tfrac{3}{4}
, all odd derivatives of
r\mathbf{U}(r)
vanish at 0 and
\|(r^{-1}\partial_{r})^{k}\mathbf{U}(r)\|_{L^{\infty}}<+\infty
, for all
0\leq k\leq n
. For convenience, we denote by

(8.8)

G(r):=\int_{0}^{r}s\mathbf{U}(s){\rm d}s

the stream function associated to the radial velocity field
(-y\mathbf{U}(r),x\mathbf{U}(r))
.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
\mathbf{x}=(x,y)
and
\Psi_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})=A\varepsilon^{n+2}F\left(\frac{\mathbf{x}}{\varepsilon}\right)
, where
F(\mathbf{x})=G(|\mathbf{x}|)
. Then,

(8.9)

\|\Psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{n+1}(B_{1})}\leq A\varepsilon\|F\|_{C^{n+1}(B_{\varepsilon})}<\infty

and the velocity field
w_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})=\nabla^{\perp}\Psi_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
is a radially smooth, compactly supported steady configuration of 2d Euler such that
\|w_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{n}}\lesssim\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Let us begin by noting that
G^{\prime}(r)=r\mathbf{U}(r)
. Then,

(8.10)

\partial_{x}F(\mathbf{x})=\frac{G^{\prime}(|\mathbf{x}|)}{|\mathbf{x}|}x=\mathbf{U}(|\mathbf{x}|)x,\quad\partial_{y}F(\mathbf{x})=\frac{G^{\prime}(|\mathbf{x}|)}{|\mathbf{x}|}y=\mathbf{U}(|\mathbf{x}|)y.

Since
\mathbf{U}(\cdot)
is compactly supported in
[0,1]
, a repeated iteration shows that for all
1\leq k\leq n+1
and all
i+j=k
,

(8.11)

|\partial_{x}^{i}\partial_{y}^{j}F(\mathbf{x})|\lesssim\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}\left|\left(\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}\right)^{l}\mathbf{U}(r)\right|<+\infty,

where we denoted
r=|\mathbf{x}|
. Hence,
\|F\|_{C^{n+1}}<\infty
and (
8.10
) shows that

(8.12)

w_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})=\nabla^{\perp}\Psi_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{U}\left(\frac{|\mathbf{x}|}{\varepsilon}\right)(-y,x)

is a smooth radial velocity field with compact support and
\|w_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{n}}\lesssim\varepsilon
.
∎

We are now in position to prove the flexibility of the shear flow.

Proof of Flexibility.

For
0<\varepsilon<\frac{1}{4}
, we construct the velocity field

(8.13)

u_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})=(v_{\varepsilon}(y),0)+w_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})

and we readily observe that thanks to the compact support of
\mathbf{U}
, we that for all
|\mathbf{x}|>\frac{3}{4}\varepsilon
,
w_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})\equiv 0
and thus
u_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})=(v_{\varepsilon}(y),0)
. We shall show that
u_{\varepsilon}
is a stationary solution of Euler in
\mathbb{T}\times[-1,1]
. The slip boundary conditions at
y=\pm 1
and the periodic boundary conditions follow from the compact support of
w_{\varepsilon}
. As for the stationarity of the flow, since
v_{\varepsilon}(y)\equiv 0
, for all
|y|\leq\varepsilon
and
w_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})=0
for all
|\mathbf{x}|>\frac{3}{4}\varepsilon
, the two velocity field do not interact at all, and each of them is stationary under the Euler equations (
(v_{\varepsilon}(y),0)
is a shear flow and
w_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
is a radial flow) and so we conclude that
u_{\varepsilon}
is a steady solution of the Euler equations. Finally, it remains to check
u_{\varepsilon}
is close to the shear flow
u_{*}(x,y)=(y^{n},0)
. Indeed, from Lemma
8.1
and Lemma
8.2
we have

(8.14)

\|u_{*}-u_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{n-1,\alpha}}=\|y^{n}-v_{\varepsilon}(y)\|_{C^{n-1,\alpha}}+\|w_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\|_{C^{n-1,\alpha}}<\varepsilon_{0},

for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small. The proof is concluded.
∎

The above construction can be generalized to admit shear flows that are not power laws, but locally behave like so. In particular, consider velocity profiles
u_{*}(x,y)=(v(y),0)
such that
v\in C^{n,1}
and assume there exists
y_{0}\in(-1,1)
such that

(V)

\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}|v^{(i)}(y_{0})|=0,\quad v^{(n)}(y_{0})\neq 0.

To carry out the perturbation argument, we need good estimates on
v(y)
around the critical stationary point
y_{0}
, we need to control the Taylor expansion of the shear flow
v(y)
nearby
y_{0}
.

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
v(y)\in C^{n+1}
satisfy (
V
). Then, for all
0\leq k\leq n-1
, there exists functions
h_{k}(y)\in{C}(-1,1)
such that

(8.15)

v^{(k)}(y)=\frac{1}{(n-k)!}v^{(n)}(y_{0})(y-y_{0})^{n-k}+h_{k}(y)(y-y_{0})^{n-k},

where each
h_{k}\in C^{1}(-1,1)
and is given by

(8.16)

h_{k}(y):=\frac{1}{(y-y_{0})^{n-k}}\int_{y_{0}}^{y}\int_{y_{0}}^{t_{k+1}}\ldots\int_{y_{0}}^{t_{n}}v^{(n+1)}(t_{n+1}){\rm d}t_{n+1}\ldots{\rm d}t_{n-k}.

Proof.

Note that since
\partial_{y}^{k}v(y_{0})=0
, for all
0\leq k\leq n-1
and
\partial_{y}^{n}v(y_{0})\neq 0
, we have

(8.17)

\displaystyle v^{(k)}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{y_{0}}^{y}v^{(k+1)}(t_{k+1}){\rm d}t_{k+1}

(8.18)

\displaystyle=\int_{y_{0}}^{y}\int_{y_{0}}^{t_{k+1}}\ldots\int_{y_{0}}^{t_{n-1}}v^{(n)}(t_{n}){\rm d}t_{k+1}\ldots{\rm d}t_{n}

(8.19)

\displaystyle=\frac{v^{(n)}(y_{0})}{(n-k)!}(y-y_{0})^{n-k}+\int_{y_{0}}^{y}\int_{y_{0}}^{t_{k+1}}\ldots\int_{y_{0}}^{t_{n}}v^{(n+1)}(t_{n+1}){\rm d}t_{n+1}\ldots{\rm d}t_{n-k}.

In particular,
v\in C^{n+1}([-1,1])
shows that
h_{k}(y)

is a
C^{1}
function. The proof is concluded.
∎

Once the asymptotic properties of shear flow are established, we are in position to prove the flexibility of the steady state.

Corollary 8.1
.

Let
u_{*}(x,y)=(v(y),0)
, with
v\in C^{n+1}
and such that (
V
) holds for some
y_{0}\in(-1,1)
.
Then, there exist non-shear stationary states, time periodic and quasiperiodic of any number of non-commensurate frequencies
u(t)
such that
\|u-u_{*}\|_{C^{n-}}<\varepsilon
.

Proof.

We shall again approximate the shear flow by the smooth cut-off
v_{\varepsilon}(y)=v(y)\chi\left(\frac{y-y_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right)
and introduce a radial velocity field compactly supported on the small region where we approximate the shear flow by the zero profile. In particular, the result follows once we show that for all
0<\alpha<1
there holds

(8.20)

\|v-v_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{n-1,\alpha}(-1,1)}<\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon.

for all
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small. Indeed, since

(8.21)

\|v-v_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{n-1,\alpha}}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\left\|\partial_{y}^{i}\left(v-v_{\varepsilon}\right)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left\|\partial_{y}^{n-1}\left(v-v_{\varepsilon}\right)\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}

Now, denote
g(s)=1-\chi(s)
. For all
0\leq i\leq n-1
,

\displaystyle\|\partial_{y}^{i}(v-v_{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{i}{i\choose k}\left\|v^{(k)}(y)\partial_{y}^{i-k}g\left(\tfrac{y-y_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k=0}^{i}\varepsilon^{-(i-k)}\left\|\left(v^{(n)}(y_{0})(y-y_{0})^{n-k}+h_{k}(y)(y-y_{0})^{n-k}\right)g^{(i-k)}\left(\tfrac{y-y_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|v\|_{C^{n+1}}\left\|\left(\frac{y-y_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{n-k}g^{(i-k)}\left(\frac{y-y_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\varepsilon^{n-i}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{n-i}.

Similarly, we see that

\displaystyle\|\partial_{y}^{n-1}(v-v_{\varepsilon})\|_{C^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\varepsilon^{-(n-1-k)}\left\|\left(v^{(n)}(y_{0})(y-y_{0})^{n-k}+h_{k}(y)(y-y_{0})^{n-k}\right)g^{(n-1-k)}\left(\tfrac{y-y_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|v\|_{C^{n+1}}\left\|\left(\frac{y-y_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{n-k}g^{(n-1-k)}\left(\frac{y-y_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}\varepsilon\lesssim\varepsilon^{1-\alpha}.

Here, we used that
\|h_{k}\|_{C^{\alpha}}\lesssim\|h_{k}\|_{C^{1}}
, for all
0\leq\alpha<1
and the scaling
\|f(\lambda x)\|_{C^{\alpha}}=\lambda^{\alpha}\|f\|_{C^{\alpha}}
.
∎

The above construction exploits the degeneracy of the velocity field; the perturbation is introduced in a small neighborhood of points where the background shear flow vanishes. In a sense, the location and nature of the perturbation is sharp, as can be seen from the following result, which is a straightforward consequence of the result of Hamel and Nadirashvili
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
.

Corollary 8.2
.

Let
u_{*}(x,y)=(v(y),0)
, with
v\in C^{n}
. Assume further that
v(0)=0
,
|v(y)|>0
, for all
y\neq 0
and let
\delta>0
. Then, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that any steady solution
u(x,y)
of Euler for which
u(x,y)=u_{*}(x,y)
in
(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}\times(-2\delta,2\delta)
and

(8.22)

\|u-u_{*}\|_{L^{\infty}}<\varepsilon_{0}

must necessarily be a shear flow, that is
u(x,y)=(\tilde{v}(y),0)
.

As will be clear from the proof, one may generalize the result to include background shear flow profiles
v(y)
that vanish in a finite number of points (or even in whole subintervals of
(-1,1)
), by requiring the velocity perturbation to be compactly supported away from the zeroes of the profile.

Proof.

Let

m=\min_{y\in[-1,-\delta]\cup[\delta,1]}|v(y)|>0

and choose
\varepsilon_{0}=\frac{m}{2}
. Then, since
u(x,y)=u_{*}(x,y)=(v(y),0)
for all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}\times(-2\delta,2\delta)
, we observe that the straight line

\Gamma_{\delta}=\{(x,\delta)\}_{x\in\mathbb{T}}

is a fluid boundary for
u(x,y)
, since
u(x,y)|_{\Gamma_{\delta}}=(v(\delta),0)
, it is tangential to
\Gamma_{\delta}
. The same behavior occurs for
\Gamma_{-\delta}
and, in particular, we can decompose our domain
M=\mathbb{T}\times[-1,1]
and look at the Euler equations on each subdomain
M_{+}=\mathbb{T}\times[\delta,1]
and
M_{-}=\mathbb{T}\times[-1,\delta]
. Moreover,

(8.23)

\inf_{M_{+}\cup M_{-}}|u|\geq\inf_{M_{+}\cup M_{-}}|u_{*}|-\varepsilon_{0}=\frac{m}{2}>0,

so that, in particular, the infimum on each
M_{+}
and
M_{-}
is strictly positive. Then, Theorem 1.1 of
[
11
]
applied to each
M_{+}
and
M_{-}
separately ensures that
u(x,y)
is a shear flow on each
M_{+}
and
M_{-}
. The proof is concluded.
∎

Some remarks on limitations and its generalizations are in order.

Remark

(Sharpness)
.

In the case of Couette flow (
n=1
, constant vorticity), the rigidity/flexibility with Sobolev topology established by
[
32
]
is sharp and their Hölder regularity is better than
C^{1-}
that we prove (it is flexible in
C^{1,1/2-}
). We also remark that for shear flows that are not pure power laws, the regularity threshold can be increased. Indeed, there exists a family of strictly monotone shear flows for which our
C^{1-}
threshold can be improved at least half a derivative more, see
[
40
]
. On the other hand, the Hölder regularity threshold
C^{1,\frac{1}{2}-}
of Poiseuille in
[
8
]
falls short of the
C^{2-}
threshold that we establish here and which we show to be sharp. Given the previous results, we expect the
C^{n-}
flexibility to be sharp for pure power laws
v(y)=y^{n}
, at least for even
n\geq 2
. Namely, we suspect that, as in the Poiseuille case, there exists an
\varepsilon:=\varepsilon(u_{*},M)
such that all stationary solutions
u
satisfying
\|u-(y^{n},0)\|_{C^{n}(M)}<\varepsilon
are shear flows.

Remark

(Structural Instability)
.

The non-shear steady solutions that we present are not generic, in the sense that the stream-functions of such velocity fields are not Morse functions, they are expected to be unstable. This is to be compared with the works of
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
32
]
where non-trivial Euler steady solutions are constructed. These are stable under perturbation, and exhibit truly two-dimensional features.

Remark

(Coliving Vortices)
.

The radial velocity field introduced in Lemma
8.2
is localized on the origin and its compact support is small, it is included in the ball of radius
\frac{3}{4}\varepsilon
. Thus, one may introduce additional radial velocity fields, with different velocity profiles
\mathbf{U}
whose compact supports do not intersect each other and are included in the open band
M_{\varepsilon}=\{(x,y)\in M\,:\,|y|<\frac{9}{10}\varepsilon\}
. The resulting velocity field is then a non-shear steady solution of Euler close to the background shear flow and composed of several vortices with different profiles and strengths living in the same stationary band.

Remark

(Travelling Wave Vortices)
.

Even though Corollary
8.2
shows that non-shear steady state perturbations must be non-trivial (non-vanishing) in regions close to the stationary points of the background shear flow, we may also find comoving structures (such as time-periodic traveling waves) concentrated away from these stationary points of the shear flow profile
v(y)
. Indeed, for any
y_{0}\in(-1,1)
such that
v_{0}:=v(y_{0})\neq 0
, the shear flow
\overline{v}(y)=v(y)-v_{0}
vanishes at
y=y_{0}
and thus, thanks to Corollary
8.1
, we can embed a small radial velocity field
w_{\varepsilon}(x,y-y_{0})
resulting in the non-shear steady state
\overline{u}(x,y)=\chi(\frac{y-y_{0}}{\varepsilon})(\overline{v}(y),0)+w_{\varepsilon}(x,y-y_{0})
. Hence, the traveling wave

(8.24)

u_{\varepsilon}(t,x,y)=\overline{u}_{\varepsilon}(x-v(y_{0})t,y)+(v(y_{0}),0)

is a solution to the Euler equations and it is
\varepsilon>0
close to the shear steady state
u_{*}=(v(y),0)
in
C^{n,\alpha}(M)
regularity, for all
0\leq\alpha<1
and
n
counting the number of derivatives of
v(y)
vanishing at
y_{0}
. In particular, we can have a family of coliving vortices embedded now on a traveling wave band of speed
v(y_{0})
. The method can also be generalized to allow more traveling wave bands, each of them with its own family of coliving vortices and its own wave speed. For example, for any
y_{1}\in(-1,1)
sufficiently far from
y_{0}
, we may define

(8.25)

u_{\varepsilon}^{1}(t,x,y)=\chi\left(\frac{y-y_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)\left(u_{\varepsilon}(t,x-v(y_{1})t,y)-(v(y_{1}),0)\right)+w^{1}_{\varepsilon}(x-v(y_{1})t,y-y_{1})+(v(y_{1}),0),

where now
u_{\varepsilon}^{1}
is a travelling wave solution of Euler of speed
v(y_{1})
with two travelling wave bands, one of them with relative wave speed
v(y_{0})+v(y_{1})
.

Remark

(Embedded Vortices)
.

For profiles
\mathbf{U}
such that
\mathbf{U}(r)=1
, for all
\tfrac{1}{3}<r<\tfrac{2}{3}
, the velocity field
u_{\varepsilon}=w_{\varepsilon}=A\epsilon^{n}r\mathrm{e}_{\theta}
is simply a solid body rotation in that annular region. Hence, up to a rigid rotation of speed
A\varepsilon^{n}
to adjust the solid body motion, we can embed a family of coliving smaller vortices within the annular region. The overall resulting non-shear velocity field is no longer a steady but a periodic or quasiperiodic solution of Euler, depending on the angular velocities of all the solid body motions involved. One may even choose to embed any of these smaller vortices on any (or several) members of the family of coliving vortices sitting on the traveling wave bands discussed above.

Remark

(Locally non-radial Vortices)
.

Instead of the radial velocity fields that we introduce in Lemma
8.2
, we can opt for placing there non-locally radial and
C^{n}
regular compactly supported stationary Euler flows, whose existence was recently obtained in
[
23
]
. As such, the steady states that we find in Theorem
1.3
need not have any local symmetry.

To conclude, the above remarks show the dynamical richness and endless configurations of solutions to Euler nearby arbitrary shear flows in low regularity neighborhoods.
