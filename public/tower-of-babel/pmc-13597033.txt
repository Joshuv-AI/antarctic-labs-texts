Silicosis from dry-cutting engineered stone in the fabrication of countertops

A 47-year-old man presented to the emergency department after minor blunt trauma to the right chest wall at work. He had immigrated to Canada from Vietnam 35 years earlier at the age of 9 years. He had worked with a single company for 20 years in countertop fabrication, predominantly using engineered stone in the past 10 years. His responsibilities involved dry-cutting stone; he used a cartridge respirator, with dust filters changed weekly. No program for silica surveillance existed at the workplace, which employed 3 to 5 other workers.

Initial chest radiography showed subtle nodules (Figure 1), and subsequent computed tomography (CT) revealed diffuse bilateral upper lobe nodules with areas of confluence (Figure 2). Bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage was negative for tuberculosis. The patient denied dyspnea, cough, or constitutional symptoms initially. Pulmonary function tests demonstrated a moderate restrictive pattern with reduced diffusing capacity compared with reference values (Table 1). On advice from his physician, the patient was removed from the exposure soon after his presentation. He was referred to the occupational lung disease clinic with a diagnosis of silicosis.

Over the next 18 months, repeat imaging showed worsening fibrosis with ground-glass opacities suggestive of alveolar proteinosis. Antinuclear antibodies (1:640 centromere), anti-SSA (anti–Sjögren’s-syndrome-related antigen A autoantibodies), and anti-Ro52 were positive, consistent with silicosis. We initiated an antifibrotic medication, nintedanib 150 mg twice daily, 1 year after the patient was removed from the exposure, but we discontinued it 1.5 years later after elevations of liver enzyme levels (increase from normal in aspartate aminotransferase to 36 [reference 5 to 34] U/L and alanine aminotransferase to 153 [reference < 50] U/L). We reintroduced a lower dosage of nintedanib, 100 mg daily, 3 years after his initial presentation after liver enzyme levels returned to normal, and the dosage was subsequently increased to 100 mg twice daily with no meaningful adverse effect.

The patient’s pulmonary function declined, but the rate of decline decreased after he started the antifibrotic medication (Table 1); he experienced mild exertional dyspnea (Modified Medical Research Council Grade 1). Pulmonary function tests on follow-up after his initial diagnosis showed a decline in total lung capacity and vital capacity with proportionate flow rates and reduction in gas exchange. At 3 years after the diagnosis, the patient’s total lung capacity was 61% of predicted, forced vital capacity 2.04 L (52% predicted), diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide 45% predicted. Distance on 6-minute walk test was 580 m (106% predicted) but with worsened oxygen saturation from 98% at rest to 87% during the walk. Repeat CT of the chest and chest radiography on follow-up (Figure 3 and Figure 4) showed disease progression and upper-lobe fibrosis.

The patient has continued respirology and hepatology clinical review, and is awaiting assessment for lung transplantation. He is currently undergoing vocational retraining with workers’ compensation support.

Discussion

Silicosis is a fibrotic interstitial lung disease caused by inhalation of crystalline silica and is one of the most globally prevalent causes of pneumoconiosis.1 Simple silicosis is characterized by diffuse small lung nodules on chest imaging, although patients may be asymptomatic in early stages. Later symptoms of cough, fatigue, shortness of breath, and constitutional symptoms are accompanied by worsening radiographic changes. Progressive massive fibrosis represents severe disease; it occurs when large conglomerations of nodules appear in the lung with mass-like fibrotic scar tissue. The diagnosis of silicosis is usually made on the basis of compatible exposure history and imaging. Chest radiography can show characteristic changes, but CT is helpful in excluding other conditions, such as tuberculosis and sarcoidosis. Potentially preventable with appropriate workplace protocols, silicosis can be progressive and is an incurable disease that may lead to permanent disability or death. Treatment is primarily supportive, with no definitive therapy other than lung transplantation, though the use of corticosteroids and antifibrotic medications has been explored.2

Historically, silicosis was associated with mining, quarrying, and sandblasting owing to consistent inhalation of dust.1 However, since the 1990s, several reports have indicated increased silicosis among workers in the countertop industry, which has shifted to using engineered stone because of its affordability, appearance, and durability.1,3 Engineered stone, often marketed as “quartz,” is manufactured from crushed quartz mixed with resins and pigments, and contains substantially higher concentrations of silica than natural stone. Whereas granite contains 40% to 50% silica and marble less than 10%, engineered stone slabs are composed of more than 90% silica by weight.2–4 Tasks such as cutting, grinding, and polishing these slabs, especially when performed dry, release high concentrations of respirable silica dust in fine particles that, in absence of suitable protection, are inhaled deep into the lungs and cause silicosis.5,6

This case illustrates the common features of silicosis resulting from work with engineered stone, with an initial lack of respiratory symptoms despite radiographic changes, progression that is more marked than seen with more traditional silica exposures (such as in mining), and therapeutic complexity. Development of progressive massive fibrosis and interstitial lung disease with ground-glass changes suggesting alveolar proteinosis are consistent with reported histopathologic findings in this occupational group. Autoimmune serology positivity, despite absence of overt connective tissue disease, is common and may reflect silica-induced autoimmunity.

The potential progressive course of silicosis associated with exposure to engineered stone and the challenges in management, including drug toxicity, are also illustrated by this case. Antifibrotic therapy and referral for transplantation may offer benefit, but prevention remains paramount. Primary prevention would be the avoidance of the use of engineered stone because of the high risks of silicosis from exposure to this material. Secondary prevention would include limiting exposure by means of occupational hygiene measures, such as wet-cutting, automated enclosed machine cutting, improved ventilation, worker education on safety measures, and use of respiratory protective equipment, as well as medical surveillance. However, these measures may not be completely protective in workers who are exposed to engineered stone.1

Medical surveillance for those who work with engineered stone and early removal of workers diagnosed with silicosis are currently not systematically applied in Canada. This may relate in part to the difficulty in early detection. Even in people with minimal respiratory symptoms and normal spirometry, substantial radiographic changes of silicosis can be evident on CT, with progression to end-stage lung disease within a few years, despite removal from further exposure. Workers employed in small, family-owned fabrication shops may be reluctant to seek medical attention owing to financial concerns or risk of repercussions to their employer.

This case example supports calls for regulatory reform, mandatory surveillance, and proactive worker education in industries using engineered stone. It underscores the health risks from the absence of adequate occupational health monitoring.

The earliest clusters of silicosis associated with engineered stone were reported in Spain and Italy in the early 2010s, followed by case reports in other parts of the world.2–4,7 Many patients were relatively young, in their 30s or 40s, with employment durations of 7 to 19 years before they developed symptoms.2,8 These early-onset cases differ from more classical silicosis, which typically affects older workers with decades of exposure and a longer latency period. 2,8 The accelerated form observed in those who work with engineered stone is consistent with a higher dose–response relationship and highlights the severity of silicosis from this source.

A disproportionate number of reported affected workers are immigrants or are part of racialized and marginalized communities. 1,4,7,9 For example, in the United States, more than 95% of workers diagnosed with silicosis associated with engineered-stone exposure are immigrants from Mexico or Central America.2 They often face barriers such as language limitations, underinsurance, undocumented status, and lack of access to occupational health services. Many seek care only when symptoms become severe enough to require emergency services, and only a few are identified through routine screening.7 A delay in diagnosis contributes to poorer health outcomes in these demographic groups.

Workplace conditions in smaller fabrication shops also present challenges. Whereas some larger facilities may use computer numerical control machinery equipped with wet-cutting systems that reduce dust exposure, many smaller businesses still rely on hand tools and dry processing techniques.2,8 These environments often lack proper dust-exposure safety measures, effective ventilation, and enforcement of personal protective equipment. Furthermore, the fabrication process creates not only silica dust, but also potentially hazardous volatile organic compounds from resin binders used in engineered stone, worsening respiratory health risks.2,4

Despite growing recognition of this issue, practical difficulties exist in providing effective medical surveillance for silicosis associated with exposure to engineered stone. Air-sampling data during engineered-stone slab manufacturing and industrial hygiene assessments are limited,1 which raises concerns about whether existing occupational safety regulations are sufficient to protect workers from harmful exposures. Australian studies suggested that CT may be the only effective medical-screening tool,1 and subsequently Australia banned the use of engineered stone. Some US states have been part of health-surveillance initiatives. 3,7,8 Canada has not yet announced measures to prevent silicosis from engineered stone.

Health care providers should consider this diagnosis in patients working with engineered stone, and in countertop manufacturing and fitting, since early identification and removal from further exposure can greatly improve the prognosis. When possible, primary avoidance of work with engineered stone should be strongly considered, owing to the high risks of silicosis, even with measures of occupational hygiene.2,10 In the absence of a ban on the use of engineered stone, increased knowledge among workers and health care practitioners is essential for implementing preventive measures, raising clinical awareness, informing policy decisions, and guiding future public health interventions.
