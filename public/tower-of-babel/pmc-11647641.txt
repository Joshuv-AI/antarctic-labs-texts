Marburg virus vaccine in Rwanda: A necessity for the control and prevention of future epidemics
Authors: Christian Tague, Mayar Moustafa Budair, Maher Ali Rusho, Adolphe Karegeya, Mc Juan Muco Mugisha, Hermann Yokolo, Joshua Ekouo, Dujardin Makeda, Elie Kihanduka, Aymar Akilimali
Published: 2024

In recent years, there has been an emergence of Marburg fever epidemics in some developing countries such as Rwanda. The disease constitutes a significant public health issue in the country. It is caused by the Marburg virus which belongs to the family of Filoviridae, like the Ebola virus. These Marburg epidemics particularly occur in the countries of East and Central Africa. Rwanda experiences outbreaks consistently as the virus spreads from its neighboring countries in that area. The development of vaccines constitutes a potential breakthrough in the eradication of the disease [1]. This article aims to show the importance of vaccination for the eradication and prevention of future epidemics of Marburg fever in Rwanda.

The Rwandan Ministry of Health announced the presence of Marburg virus cases in the country's health facilities on September 27, 2024. According to World Health Organization (WHO), this is the first report of Marburg virus in Rwanda. As of September 29, 2024, there were a total of 26 confirmed cases of, 8 of which were fatal. These cases were reported in 7 districts of the country: Gasabo, Gatsibo, Kamonyi, Kicukiro, Nyagatare, Nyarugenge and Rubavu. 70 % of the cases observed were of health care workers from two health facilities in Kigali [2].

Over time there has been an increase in cases relating to the Marburg virus. As of October 8, 2024, Rwanda has recorded 58 confirmed cases of Marburg virus disease, including 13 deaths. A majority of the recorded cases affected health care workers as well [3].

Marburg fever is transmitted to humans by direct contact with the body fluids of an infected person or an infected animal. After a period of 2–21 days, the virus begins to multiply in the body and invade the tissues. The first visible manifestations include: high fever, intense headaches, muscle pain and malaise. The disease can further progress into digestive disorders including episodes of diarrhea, vomiting, hematemesis and skin disorders [1,4]. In the absence of rapid diagnosis and/or adequate treatment, these manifestations can become complicated and lead to internal and/or external hemorrhage resulting in hypovolemic shock. Multi-organ failure, neurological disorders and disseminated intravascular coagulation syndrome could also occur [5].

Since the introduction of modern prophylactic methods against infections, vaccination has been at the top of the list as a response technique. It has proven its effectiveness in preventing and reducing complications associated with several viral diseases, including Marburg fever. In Rwanda, prevention through vaccination has been implemented since Sunday, October 6, 2024. 700 doses of the experimental vaccine provided by the American research institute ‘Sabin Vaccine Institutes’, have been made available at clinical trial sites for a testing phase. This campaign mainly targets health workers and close contacts of confirmed cases [[3], [4], [5]].

In Rwanda, this fight is being carried out on various levels, including the management of cases diagnosed positive for Marburg fever. This is done by isolating and monitoring infected cases in order to prevent transmission and ensure controlled surveillance of the infection. Other means of control have also proven their effectiveness, including strict hygiene measures and the disinfection of surfaces [1].

In conclusion, one of the important steps towards the eradication of Marburg virus is the establishment of a vaccination program in Rwanda. The geographical proximity of the country to others that harbor the disease increases the risk of transmission. Thus, vaccination is essential to limit the virus spreading. Targeted use of the vaccines should also be implemented by prioritizing the people that work in health care facilities. These actions must also be combined with rigorous hygiene measures and the isolation of suspected cases. This could significantly reduce future outbreaks and strengthen Rwanda's response capacity to Marburg fever and various other viral diseases.
