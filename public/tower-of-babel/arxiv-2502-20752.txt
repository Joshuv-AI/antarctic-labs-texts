Thermally driven two-sphere microswimmer with internal feedback control
Jun Li, Ziluo Zhang, Zhanglin Hou, Yuto Hosaka, Kento Yasuda, Linli He, Shigeyuki Komura

Abstract
We discuss the locomotion of a thermally driven elastic two-sphere microswimmer with internal feedback control that is realized by the position-dependent friction coefficients. In our model, the two spheres are in equilibrium with independent heat baths having different temperatures, causing a heat flow between the two spheres. We generally show that the average velocity of the microswimmer is nonzero when the friction coefficients are position-dependent. Using the method of stochastic thermodynamics, we obtain the entropy production rate and discuss the efficiency of the two-sphere microswimmer. The proposed self-propulsion mechanism highlights the importance of information in active matter and can be a fundamental process in various biological systems.



I
Introduction

Microswimmers, such as sperm cells or motile bacteria, are tiny objects moving in viscous environments,
and are expected to be relevant to microfluidics and microsystems
LaugaBook
.
By transforming chemical energy into mechanical work, microswimmers change their shapes and move in
viscous fluids
Hosaka22
.
According to Purcell’s scallop theorem, microswimmers in a Newtonian fluid need to undergo non-reciprocal
body motion for steady locomotion
Purcell1977
;
Ishimoto12
.
To realize such a non-reciprocal deformation, various microswimmer models that have more than
two degrees of freedom have been proposed
Lauga09a
.
One example is the three-sphere microswimmer in which three in-line spheres are linked by two arms of
varying lengths
Najafi04
;
Golestanian08
.

Among various generalizations of the three-sphere microswimmer model
Yasuda23
,
Hosaka
et al.
considered an elastic three-sphere microswimmer in which the three spheres are in
equilibrium with independent heat baths having different temperatures
Hosaka17
.
It was shown that such a stochastic microswimmer (without any prescribed deformation) can also acquire
net locomotion due to thermal fluctuations,
and its average velocity is proportional to the heat flow among the spheres
Hosaka17
.
Later, the average entropy production rate
Sou19
;
Sou21
and the time-correlation functions
Li24

of the same model were calculated by some of the current authors.
In particular, the existence of an antisymmetric part of the cross-correlation function reflects the
broken time-reversal symmetry of this microswimmer
Li24
.

Prior to the above thermal three-sphere microswimmer model, Kumar
et al.
proposed
a model of an active elastic dimer (AED) in which the friction coefficients of the two spheres depend on their
relative coordinate
Kumar08
;
Baule08
.
Their model is different from the traditional Brownian ratchet models
Julicher97
;
Reimann
because the
motion asymmetry is created internally and is not induced by an external periodic potential.
They found that the average velocity of an AED is proportional to the difference between
the non-equilibrium noise strengths acting on the two particles
Kumar08
;
Baule08
.
Such a self-propulsion mechanism can explain, for example, the movement of helicases on
DNA
Yu06
, the walking of Myosin VI on actin filaments
Altman04
, and
the collective migration of cell clusters
Pages22
.

Given the modern framework of stochastic thermodynamics
SekimotoBook
;
Jarzynski11
;
Seifert12
and
the accumulated knowledge of the thermal three-sphere microswimmer model
Hosaka17
;
Sou19
;
Sou21
,
the AED model offers a renewed interest, especially when the two spheres have different temperatures
without any non-equilibrium noises.
Moreover, the internal feedback control considered in the AED model is a crucial mechanism
for “informational active matter” that utilizes information (measurement and feedback) instead of energy for
various non-equilibrium processes
VanSaders24
;
Huang20
.
Recently, we have proposed models of Ornstein-Uhlenbeck information swimmers with external and internal
feedback controls
Hou24
.

In this work, we simplify the original AED model to discuss a thermally driven two-sphere microswimmer
with internal feedback control that is realized by the position-dependent friction coefficients.
In the current model, the two spheres are in equilibrium with independent heat baths having different
temperatures.
We show that a combination of heat transfer between the spheres and internal feedback control leads to
directional locomotion in the steady state under a noisy condition.
We analytically obtain the average velocity and the entropy production rate of this stochastic microswimmer
by using stochastic energetics
SekimotoBook
.
With these results, we further estimate the efficiency of the thermal two-sphere microswimmer.
We consider that our model explains the fundamental mechanism for transport and locomotion in various active
matter and biological systems.

In Sec.

, we explain the model of a thermally driven elastic two-sphere microswimmer with internal
feedback control.
In Sec.

, we obtain the steady-state probability distribution that is further used to calculate
the average velocity and the entropy production rate in Secs.
IV
and
V
, respectively.
In Sec.

, we calculate the efficiency of the two-sphere microswimmer by using specific functional

A brief discussion and summary is given in Sec.

II
Model

As schematically shown in Fig.
1
, we consider a microswimmer consisting of two
hard spheres connected by a spring.
The positions of the two spheres in a one-dimensional coordinate are denoted by
x_{i}(t)
(
i=1,2
),
and we further define the spring extension by
x=x_{2}-x_{1}
that can take both positive and negative values.
The spring potential energy is generally given by
U(x)
, which can be a harmonic potential (as
we will discuss later) or that of a finitely extensible nonlinear elastic (FENE) spring
Baule08
.
There are two important assumptions of the model: (i) the two spheres are in equilibrium with
independent heat baths having different temperatures
T_{i}
(
T_{1}\neq T_{2}
) and
(ii) the friction coefficients
\gamma_{i}(x)\geq 0
, acting separately on the two spheres, are
dependent on the spring extension
x
.
To keep the generality of the model as much as possible, we first do not specify the functional forms
of
U(x)
and
\gamma_{i}(x)
.

The coupled overdamped Langevin equations for
x_{i}
(
i=1,2
) under thermal noise are given by

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}(x)\dot{x}_{i}(t)=-\partial_{i}U(x)+g_{i}(x)\xi_{i}(t),

(1)

where
\dot{x}_{i}=dx_{i}/dt
,
\partial_{i}=\partial/\partial x_{i}

(the summation over
i
is not taken).
The thermal noise
\xi_{i}(t)
has
Gaussian statistics with zero mean and unit variance:

\displaystyle\langle\xi_{i}(t)\rangle=0,\quad\langle\xi_{i}(t)\xi_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

(2)

According to the fluctuation-dissipation relation, the prefactor of thermal noise is
given by
g_{i}(x)=\sqrt{2\gamma_{i}(x)k_{\rm B}T_{i}}
, where
k_{\rm B}
is the Boltzmann
constant
DoiBook
.
The above Langevin equations are multiplicative because the noise is coupled nonlinearly with
the stochastic variables
Kampenbook
.
Then, it is necessary to specify the interpretation of the multiplicative noise; otherwise
Eq. (
1
) is meaningless.

Here, we employ the Itô interpretation
Gardinerbook
, and the Langevin equations
in Eq. (
1
) should be rewritten with the Itô convention
x^{\ast}=x(t)
as

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}(t)=-\dfrac{\partial_{i}U(x)}{\gamma_{i}(x)}-\dfrac{[g_{i}(x)]^{2}\partial_{i}\gamma_{i}(x)}{2[\gamma_{i}(x)]^{3}}+\dfrac{g_{i}(x^{\ast})}{\gamma_{i}(x^{\ast})}\xi_{i}(t).

(3)

As pointed out by Lau and Lubensky
Lau07
, the second term on the right-hand side
is proportional to the temperature, and it is necessary to ensure the proper thermal
equilibrium.
Such a term reflects thermal fluctuation and generally exists for other interpretations
except for the anti-Itô convention
Kuroiwa14
.
Hereafter, the prime denotes a derivative with respect to the relative coordinate

x=x_{2}-x_{1}
, such as
U^{\prime}(x)=-\partial_{1}U(x)=\partial_{2}U(x)
.
Then, the coupled Langevin equations can be explicitly written as

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{U^{\prime}(x)}{\gamma_{1}(x)}+\dfrac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{1}\gamma^{\prime}_{1}(x)}{\gamma^{2}_{1}(x)}+\sqrt{\dfrac{2k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{1}}{\gamma_{1}(x^{\ast})}}\xi_{1}(t),

(4)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{U^{\prime}(x)}{\gamma_{2}(x)}-\dfrac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{2}\gamma^{\prime}_{2}(x)}{\gamma^{2}_{2}(x)}+\sqrt{\dfrac{2k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{2}}{\gamma_{2}(x^{\ast})}}\xi_{2}(t).

(5)

III
Steady-state probability distribution

From Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
), the equations of motion for both the center-of-mass
coordinate
X=(x_{1}+x_{2})/2
and the relative coordinate
x=x_{2}-x_{1}
can be obtained.
Since the former equation is a function of the relative coordinate
x
and the noise only,
we first discuss the equation of motion for
x
in the Itô interpretation given by
Baule08
.

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=a(x)+b(x^{\ast})\xi(t).

(6)

Here,
\xi(t)
is the superposition of
\xi_{1}(t)
and
\xi_{2}(t)
with the same Gaussian
white statistics, and the two functions
a(x)
and
b(x)
are defined as

\displaystyle a(x)

\displaystyle=-\left(\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{1}(x)}+\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{2}(x)}\right)U^{\prime}(x)+k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{1}\left(\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{1}(x)}+\dfrac{\theta}{\gamma_{2}(x)}\right)^{\prime},

(7)

\displaystyle b(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{1}\left(\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{1}(x)}+\dfrac{\theta}{\gamma_{2}(x)}\right)},

(8)

where we have introduced the temperature ratio
\theta=T_{2}/T_{1}
, i.e.,
\theta=1

corresponds to the thermal equilibrium.

The Fokker-Planck equation for the probability distribution
P(x,t)
associated with the Langevin
equation in Eq. (
6
) can be written in the Itô interpretation as
Riskenbook

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}P(x,t)=-\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[a(x)P(x,t)\right]+\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\left[b^{2}(x)P(x,t)\right].

(9)

Since we are interested in the steady-state properties of the microswimmer, we obtain
the steady-state probability distribution by imposing
\partial_{t}P(x,t)=0
.
Moreover, the probability flux can be set to zero since the relative coordinate is bounded
by the spring potential
U(x)
.
Then, the steady-state probability distribution
P_{\rm s}(x)
satisfies
Baule08

\displaystyle a(x)P_{\mathrm{s}}(x)-\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[b^{2}(x)P_{\mathrm{s}}(x)\right]=0,

(10)

and
P_{\rm s}(x)
can be formally solved as

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{s}}(x)=\dfrac{\mathcal{N}}{b^{2}(x)}\exp\left[\int_{0}^{x}dy\,\dfrac{2a(y)}{b^{2}(y)}\right],

(11)

where
\mathcal{N}
is the normalization factor.
By substituting Eqs. (
7
) and (
8
) into the above expression, we obtain
Baule08

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{s}}(x)=\dfrac{\mathcal{N}_{1}}{2k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{1}}\exp\left[\int_{0}^{x}dy\,\left(-\dfrac{U^{\prime}(y)[\gamma^{-1}_{1}(y)+\gamma^{-1}_{2}(y)]}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{1}[\gamma^{-1}_{1}(y)+\theta\gamma^{-1}_{2}(y)]}\right)\right].

(12)

If we further assume that the two friction coefficients are identical for all
x
, i.e.,

\gamma_{1}(x)=\gamma_{2}(x)
, the steady-state probability distribution simplifies to

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{s}}(x)=\dfrac{\mathcal{N}_{1}}{2k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{1}}\exp\left[-\dfrac{2U(x)}{(1+\theta)k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{1}}\right],

(13)

which is essentially the Boltzmann distribution modified by the two different temperatures.

IV
Average velocity

Having obtained the steady-state distribution function
P_{\mathrm{s}}(x)
for the relative coordinate
x
,
we can calculate the average velocity of the elastic two-sphere microswimmer.
Since the center of mass velocity is simply given by
V=\dot{X}=(\dot{x}_{1}+\dot{x}_{2})/2
, we use
Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
) to obtain the statistical average of
V

Kumar08
;
Baule08

\langle V\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\left(\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{1}(x)}-\frac{1}{\gamma_{2}(x)}\right)U^{\prime}(x)\right\rangle-\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{1}}{2}\left\langle\left(\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{1}(x)}-\frac{\theta}{\gamma_{2}(x)}\right)^{\prime}\right\rangle,

(14)

where the averaging
\langle\cdots\rangle
can be performed by using
P_{\rm s}(x)
.
To derive the above expression, the contribution of the multiplicative noise terms has been omitted owing
to the Itô interpretation.
It should be emphasized here that
\langle V\rangle
vanishes when
\theta=1
for any
choice of
\gamma_{1}(x)
and
\gamma_{2}(x)
, provided that the average is taken with
respect to
P_{\mathrm{s}}(x)
.

When
\gamma_{1}(x)=\gamma_{2}(x)
, the above average velocity further reduces to

\displaystyle\langle V\rangle=\dfrac{(\theta-1)k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{1}}{2}\left\langle\left(\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{1}(x)}\right)^{\prime}\right\rangle.

(15)

This result clearly demonstrates that the microswimmer acquires a finite average velocity
when
\theta\neq 1
(
T_{1}\neq T_{2}
) and
\gamma_{1}
is not constant.
In other words, the current microswimmer is driven by thermal energy, and the internal feedback
control through
\gamma_{1}(x)
plays an essential role in the locomotion.
However, when
(1/\gamma_{1}(x))^{\prime}
and
U(x)
are respectively odd and even functions of
x
,

\langle V\rangle
vanishes even if the friction coefficient is position-dependent.
A similar result to Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
) was reported for an AED
Kumar08
;
Baule08
,
for which it was shown that an asymmetry in the non-equilibrium noise strengths leads to locomotion.
In our study, we do not introduce any active fluctuations, which allows us to use the standard framework
of stochastic thermodynamics
SekimotoBook
;
Jarzynski11
;
Seifert12
, as we discuss below.

V
Entropy production rate

Next, we calculate the average entropy production rate of the elastic microswimmer in the steady state,
which is given by

\displaystyle\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle=-\frac{\langle\dot{Q}_{1}\rangle}{T_{1}}-\dfrac{\langle\dot{Q}_{2}\rangle}{T_{2}},

(16)

where the stochastic heat flow
\dot{Q}_{i}
is the heat gained by the
i
-th sphere per unit time.
According to the stochastic energetics
SekimotoBook
, it is given by

\displaystyle\dot{Q}_{i}\,dt=\dfrac{\partial U(x)}{\partial x_{i}}\circ dx_{i},

(17)

where
\circ
denotes the Stratonovich product, and the small increment
dx_{i}
can be obtained
from the Langevin equations in Eq. (
3
).
Using the Wong-Zakai theorem to deal with the Stratonovich product and taking the
statistical average
SekimotoBook
, we obtain after some calculation

\displaystyle\langle\dot{Q}_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\dfrac{[U^{\prime}(x)]^{2}}{\gamma_{i}(x)}\right\rangle+k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{i}\left\langle\left(\dfrac{U^{\prime}(x)}{\gamma_{i}(x)}\right)^{\prime}\right\rangle.

(18)

A similar expression was obtained in Eq. (32) of Ref.
Cates22
.
When the two friction coefficients are constant and identical, the above expression reduces to
Eq. (4.27) of Ref.
SekimotoBook
.

One can easily check that
\langle\dot{Q}_{i}\rangle=0
when
\theta=1
by using
the equilibrium distribution function in Eq. (
12
).
When
\theta\neq 1
, we consider the total heat flow defined by

\langle\dot{Q}\rangle=\langle\dot{Q}_{1}\rangle+\langle\dot{Q}_{2}\rangle
.
By using Eqs. (
7
) and (
8
), one can generally show that

\langle\dot{Q}\rangle
vanishes in the steady state because

\displaystyle\langle\dot{Q}\rangle=\langle U^{\prime}(x)a(x)\rangle+\dfrac{1}{2}\langle U^{\prime\prime}(x)b^{2}(x)\rangle=0.

(19)

In the second equality, we have used Eq. (
10
), which comes from the steady-state condition.
Notice that the relation
\langle\dot{Q}\rangle=0
generally holds even if the two friction coefficients

\gamma_{1}(x)
and
\gamma_{2}(x)
are different.
Since we have
\langle\dot{Q}_{2}\rangle=-\langle\dot{Q}_{1}\rangle
from Eq. (
19
),
the average entropy production rate in Eq. (
16
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle=\frac{1-\theta}{\theta}\frac{\langle\dot{Q}_{1}\rangle}{T_{1}},

(20)

which vanishes when
\theta=1
.
When
T_{1}<T_{2}
(
\theta>1
),
\langle\dot{Q}_{1}\rangle
is negative and we have

\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle>0
, as required from the generalized second law for
non-equilibrium systems.
A similar statement also holds when
T_{1}>T_{2}
(
\theta<1
).

VI
Example

So far, we have not yet specified the functional forms of the spring potential
U(x)
and the friction
coefficients
\gamma_{i}(x)
.
As an explicit example, we choose the harmonic potential energy
U(x)=Kx^{2}/2
, where
K>0
is the
spring constant, and assume that the friction coefficients have the form

\gamma_{1}(x)=\gamma_{2}(x)=\gamma[1+\delta\tanh(x/w)]
, where
\gamma
is a constant
friction coefficient,
\delta
is a dimensionless parameter satisfying
|\delta|<1
(recall

\gamma_{i}(x)>0
), and
w
is a length characterizing the change of the friction
coefficients
Kumar08
;
Baule08
.
With this choice, the steady-state distribution function in Eq. (
13
) and the average velocity
in Eq. (
15
) become

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{s}}(x)=\sqrt{\dfrac{K}{\pi(1+\theta)k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{1}}}\exp\left[-\dfrac{Kx^{2}}{(1+\theta)k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{1}}\right],

(21)

and

\displaystyle\langle V\rangle

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\delta(1-\theta)}{2\gamma}\sqrt{\dfrac{Kk_{\mathrm{B}}T_{1}}{\pi(1+\theta)}}

\displaystyle\times\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dz\,\dfrac{\sech^{2}z}{\left(1+\delta\tanh z\right)^{2}}\exp\left(-\dfrac{\varepsilon z^{2}}{1+\theta}\right),

(22)

respectively, where
\varepsilon=Kw^{2}/(k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{1})
is a dimensionless parameter.
Note that Eq. (
21
) is simply a Gaussian distribution function.
Since the dimensionless integral in Eq. (
22
) is positive (which will be evaluated numerically below),

\langle V\rangle
is finite only when
\delta\neq 0
and
\theta\neq 1
.
Moreover, the temperature ratio
\theta
determines the direction of locomotion, i.e.,

\langle V\rangle<0
when
\delta>0
and
\theta>1
, or vice versa.
Similarly, by calculating the average heat flow
\langle\dot{Q}_{1}\rangle
in Eq. (
18
),
we obtain the average entropy production rate in Eq. (
20
) as

\displaystyle\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta-1}{\theta}\frac{(Kw)^{2}}{\gamma T_{1}}\sqrt{\dfrac{\varepsilon}{\pi(1+\theta)}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dz\,\left[\dfrac{z^{2}}{1+\delta\tanh z}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\dfrac{\delta z\sech^{2}z}{\varepsilon(1+\delta\tanh z)^{2}}-\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon(1+\delta\tanh z)}\right]\exp\left(-\dfrac{\varepsilon z^{2}}{1+\theta}\right),

(23)

in which the integral is dimensionless.

In Fig.
2
(a), we numerically plot the dimensionless average velocity

\langle\hat{V}\rangle=\langle V\rangle\gamma/\sqrt{Kk_{\mathrm{B}}T_{1}}
in Eq. (
22
)
as a function of
\delta
for different values of
\theta=T_{2}/T_{1}>1
when
\varepsilon=1
.
To confirm our analytical prediction, we have also performed numerical simulations of the coupled stochastic
equations in Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
).
These Langevin equations were discretized according to the Itô convention.
We find a good agreement between the theoretical and numerical results.
Notice that
\langle\hat{V}\rangle
vanishes when either
\theta=1
or
\delta=0
, as predicted by
Eq. (
22
).
Moreover,
\langle\hat{V}\rangle
is an odd function of
\delta
because the sign of
\delta
determines
the direction of locomotion.
In Fig.
2
(b), on the other hand, we numerically plot the dimensionless average entropy production rate

\langle\hat{\dot{\sigma}}\rangle=\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle\gamma T_{1}/(Kw)^{2}
in Eq. (
23
)
as a function of
\delta
.
For
\theta>1
,
\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle
is positive and is an even function of
\delta
.
As a result, the entropy production rate is minimized at
\delta=0
and the corresponding
expression is given by

\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle_{\rm min}=k_{\mathrm{B}}K(\theta-1)^{2}/(2\gamma\theta)\geq 0
.

At this stage, it is worth evaluating the efficiency of the microswimmer defined
by
Golestanian08
;
Sou21

\displaystyle\eta=\dfrac{2\gamma\langle V\rangle^{2}}{\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle T^{\ast}},

(24)

where
T^{\ast}=(T_{1}+T_{2})/2
is the average temperature although either
T_{1}
or
T_{2}
can also be used.
The factor
2\gamma
in the numerator reflects the fact the microswimmer consists of two spheres
with the same average friction coefficient
\gamma
.
In Fig.
3
, we color-plot the efficiency
\eta
as a function of
\delta
and
\theta
.
The efficiency
\eta
vanishes when
\delta=0
and is an even function of
\delta
when
\theta
is fixed.
It is worth noting that, in the equilibrium limit of
\theta\rightarrow 1
, the efficiency
\eta
is finite because
both
\langle V\rangle
and
\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle
vanish in this limit [see Eqs. (
22
) and (
23
)].

VII
Discussion and summary

In our model, the state-dependent friction coefficients play an essential role.
The change in the friction coefficient can be caused, for example, by the change in the particle size.
In this sense, the current model has a similarity to the model of “pushmepullyou” by Avron
et al.

Avron05

or the two-sphere model by Pandey
et al.

Pandey12
.
The main difference in our model is that the spheres have different temperatures, and thermal fluctuations cause
the locomotion in a stochastic manner.
The internal heat flow combined with the feedback control through the position-dependent friction coefficient
is the main driving force for maintaining persistent locomotion.
In general, there is a contribution to the total entropy production rate coming from the center-of-mass diffusion,
which is neglected in Eq. (
24
)
Sou21
.
In the above efficiency, we have considered the entropy production rate due to the heat flow [see Eq. (
16
)]
that causes the unidirectional motion.

In summary, we have studied the locomotion of a thermally driven elastic two-sphere microswimmer that has
position-dependent friction coefficients.
Such a microswimmer can acquire nonzero average velocity in the steady state due to the heat flow between
the spheres.
We have obtained the entropy production rate and further estimated the efficiency of the microswimmer.
The proposed self-propulsion mechanism emphasizes the importance of active matter and biological systems
driven by information.
In the future, we will calculate the time-correlation functions of the microswimmer to quantitatively discuss the
degree of broken time-reversal symmetry
Li24
.
