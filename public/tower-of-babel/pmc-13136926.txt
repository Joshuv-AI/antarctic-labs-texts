Artificial intelligence and nursing science: Opportunities, challenges, implications, and guidelines
Authors: George Demiris, Oonjee Oh, Connie M Ulrich, Sang Bin You, Hannah Cho, Antonia M Villarruel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:In the era of artificial intelligence (AI), nursing science has the potential to enable transformative change in healthcare driven by the nursing clinical focus, its deep commitment to improving patient and family outcomes, its legacy of compassion, and tradition of creative innovation.Purpose:Inspired by discussions from a 2-day interdisciplinary workshop with experts in nursing, medicine, informatics and data science, bioethics, and the healthcare industry, this white paper provides guidelines for integrating AI in nursing science.Methods:Workshop proceedings were transcribed and analyzed. We examined the clinical, ethical, and social implications of AI integration in nursing science, considering AI both as a topic of study and as a methodological tool, while addressing its opportunities and concerns.Discussion:Drawing on these insights, we recommend several future directions of nursing science. Key priorities include integrating AI literacy as core components of graduate nursing education, expanding nursing scientists’ participation in interdisciplinary AI working groups, applying rigorous implementation science frameworks to optimize AI deployment, and advocating for the interests of patients and families within this evolving landscape. We also discuss the importance of sustained collaboration with industry partners.ConclusionNurse scientists contribute expertise in the clinical and relational aspects of care, while AI designers and engineers bring essential technical insight. Such reciprocal partnerships will be essential to embed nursing science into AI development and to support the iterative innovation cycle that requires ongoing validation and trust.Keywords: Nursing science, Artificial intelligence, Nursing informatics, Ethics

Introduction

Nursing science forms the foundational basis of both nursing research and practice, with its core rooted in deep commitment to patient advocacy, interdisciplinary collaboration, scientific innovation, and ethical standards. Building on clinical expertise, pedagogical and methodological scholarship, and person-centered approaches, nursing science generates knowledge and conducts critical inquiry to inform patient care and shape policies that aim to improve health outcomes for all populations. At the dawn of a new era where artificial intelligence (AI) becomes increasingly integrated into all aspects of life, including healthcare, nursing science faces unprecedented opportunities and challenges in this rapidly evolving landscape to optimize health through research. The rapid integration of AI impacts nurse scientists not only in the research questions they are motivated to answer, but also in the methods used to address these inquires (National Institute of Nursing Research, 2025). Drawing on their dual expertise as patient advocates and researchers, nurse scientists are distinctively positioned to explore how AI amplifies the profession’s capacity to generate the evidence base to address pressing health and healthcare concerns while ensuring that the humanistic and compassionate nature of patient care remains intact.

With the objective of outlining guidelines for the integration of AI into nursing science, the University of Pennsylvania School of Nursing organized an invitational expert workshop titled “AI and Nursing Science Summit.” For this 2-day event (January 29 and 30, 2025), a total of 48 interdisciplinary leading experts with backgrounds in nursing, medicine, AI and data science, ethics, and the healthcare industry were invited. Participants were purposively selected to represent diverse areas related to nursing science and AI, various roles across academia and industry, and a range of geographic locations. Predoctoral trainees in nursing were also included in the summit. The first day of the workshop focused on AI in health systems and education, featuring presentations on the role of nursing science in integrating technology into clinical settings, highlighting the trajectory of nursing science in integrating data science and AI, and strategies to effectively incorporate patient perspectives into nursing science and clinical AI. On the second day, we further explored ethics and industry partnerships in AI applications. The keynote presentation on that day addressed the ethical considerations that support unlocking AI’s potential in healthcare, followed by an industry panel on the mutual learning that is needed between nurse scientists and technology partners. Throughout both days, participants were engaged in roundtable discussions in between each presentation with guiding questions developed by the planning committee to facilitate dialog. The specific questions for each roundtable discussion are available in the Appendix. To accurately capture the insightful and thought-provoking discussions, the planning committee members took field notes and recorded the sessions. These materials were then used to extract themes of the workshop discussion, which were iteratively discussed and refined by the authorship team.

In this white paper, we summarize the prominent themes from the workshop that inspired further discussions on the opportunities and challenges of AI in nursing science, as well as its clinical, ethical, and social implications. Nursing science, education and practice are intimately intertwined. The introduction of AI into health care affects, and is affected by, the tripartite relationship. Throughout this paper we strive to demonstrate how advances in research on the application of AI to nursing science challenges may in fact lead to changes needed in the educational sector, and, importantly due to the rapid adoption of AI strategies in the practice setting, how emerging AI-informed care may contribute to nursing science. When we consider the integration of AI in nursing science we adopt a broad perspective to acknowledge both nurse scientists who focus on design and evaluation of AI systems and tools at the core of their research agenda (i.e., nurse scientists who are AI experts) as well as those who apply AI methods to enhance or accelerate their own disciplinary research (i.e., nurse scientists who adopt AI-enabled approaches). We also define nurse scientists more broadly than doctoral-prepared nurses focusing on traditional research and acknowledge the roles of nursing informaticists, educators, and leaders. We use the themes emerging from the workshop as a foundation for developing guidelines on effectively integrating AI into nursing science (both for AI experts and AI users) and outline best practices to promote partnerships with industry.

Opportunities and Challenges of AI in Nursing Science

Opportunities

Opportunity 1: Evaluating the Impact of AI on Nursing Workflow

While AI cannot and should not replace nursing activities that rely on the essence of nursing knowledge and skill (e.g., professional knowledge in health promotion, disease management and care, compassion, empathy, respect for human dignity, advocacy), nursing research can provide valuable evidence of how AI enhances efficacy in nursing workflow while not compromising professional nursing practice. AI approaches might relieve nurses of the necessity to perform administrative or logistical tasks that do not require specialized nursing knowledge or skills, such as ordering medical supplies, managing the logistics of hospital rooms, or arranging housekeeping after discharge (Ronquillo et al., 2021). Such tasks are indeed crucial to support the workflow of nursing care. Because these tasks can be perceived by many as time-consuming and burdensome, and, more importantly, reduce the time nurses use to directly interact with those they provide care for, AI solutions might improve the system’s ability to provide high level nursing care. For example, thorough examination of nursing workflows and task analysis could help nurse scientists to identify areas where AI can be applied or augment tasks suitable for automation.

Opportunity 2: Examining the Impact of AI on the Speed of Research Translation and on Reduction of the Cognitive Burden of Synthesizing Clinical Findings

As evidence-based practice results from and iteratively informs the nursing research agenda, research into facilitating access to and updating expert knowledge becomes crucial for advancing the profession. Language models that generate text such as Claude (Anthropic, San Francisco, CA) or ChatGPT (OpenAI, San Francisco, CA) may be helpful in this task as their greatest strength lies in processing vast amounts of text, images, or other multimodal data within a short period of time. Nurse scientists can explore how AI-powered knowledge discovery related to new drugs, treatments, and interventions impacts the speed of research translation and reduces the cognitive burden of synthesizing clinical findings. Particularly given the shortage of nursing workforce and experienced nurses leaving the profession (American Association of Colleges of Nursing, 2024; Hayward et al., 2016), nurse scientists can explore how AI-supported information access affects nursing education and the development of competency and readiness in new nurses entering the field.

Opportunity 3: Using Existing AI Approaches to Tailor Patient Education Materials and Ensure Their Validity

As AI technologies contribute to more personalized care, nurse scientists have the opportunity to critically evaluate the clinical efficacy of AI, while establishing evidence standards and safety protocols. Within the scope of direct patient care, nursing research can drive the development of AI-augmented individualized treatment plans by analyzing the patients’ demographic, genetic, and lifestyle data. Predictive models trained on large patient datasets forecast the risk of disease progression or other outcomes (e.g., postoperative complications, readmission) and support nurses as they tailor treatment plans to how each patient is likely to respond (Y.-H. Li et al., 2024). As clinically trained researchers, nurse scientists can explore how AI approaches impact patient outcomes in real-world clinical settings.

Beyond clinical interventions, patient education and support, a core nursing responsibility, presents another opportunity where nurse scientists devise personalization of education strategies using AI. For instance, previous studies demonstrate the use of conversational agents to meet a patient’s linguistic and comprehension abilities by adjusting the reading levels of informed consent documentations or online informational resources (Abreu et al., 2024; Mirza et al., 2024). Nurse scientists should further explore the effectiveness of these AI models in improving patient knowledge and comprehension, care satisfaction, and health outcomes. Informed by their clinical knowledge and understanding of patient learning strategies, nurse scientists are called on to develop reproducible strategies that ensure the validity of AI-developed educational material as well as the alignment of these materials with nursing guidelines.

Opportunity 4: Using AI to Extract Profiles of Patient Need From Large Data Sets

Nursing science addresses problems in both the inpatient-arena as well as care delivery in community settings or community-wide health initiatives. For instance, real-time data from community members, collected in previous studies, supported nurses’ abilities to develop algorithms that detect early signs of adverse events such as falls (Demiris et al., 2022). Nurse scientists working in community health centers or with insurance companies can further examine clinically targeted care algorithms to determine their impact on patient and family outcomes. Moreover, nursing research could employ large data sets using AI models to identify communities with high needs related to social determinants of health or chronic conditions, facilitating targeted public health support and resources. Given the well-known deep data dependency of these models, nurse scientists must develop skills to address issues of representativeness, bias and algorithm integrity to report along with the findings of such applications.

Challenges

Challenge 1: Ensuring AI Literacy Among Nurse Scientists

Insufficient attention to basic AI concepts in nursing education interferes with developing future nurse scientists. When foundational knowledge in AI concepts, methodologies, or applications become integrated into basic nursing education, future nurse scientists will be better prepared to actively engage in AI research relevant to nursing science. El Arab and colleagues identified limited AI literacy among nurse educators and the lack of standardized AI education as critical barriers to integrating AI into the nursing curricula (El Arab et al., 2025). Such lack of exposure to AI may limit nurse scientist’s capability to effectively apply, validate, and guide AI implementation in research and practice; it may also limit practicing nurses to avail themselves of emerging innovations in clinical care supports. Promoting attention to improving nurses’ AI competencies also aligns with the leadership-oriented N.U.R.S.E.S. framework proposed by Hoelscher and Pugh, where the authors advocate for structured support to improve AI literacy across all levels of nursing (Hoelscher & Pugh, 2025).

The integration of AI related competencies in nursing and nursing science can also introduce new roles and opportunities. Inspired by the recent call for a new physician role in clinical AI that emphasizes stewardship, validation literacy, drift surveillance, and risk communication (Mahajan & Bates, 2025), we recommend the parallel development of a dedicated clinical AI nursing role to ensure safe, equitable, and contextually grounded use of AI in care delivery. Nurses and nurse scientists are uniquely positioned to lead this role given their presence at the point of care, deep understanding of patient preferences and care workflows, and expertise in implementation science, patient safety, and health equity; together, these competencies enable them to translate AI outputs into actionable, trustworthy, and ethically sound nursing practice while monitoring real-world performance and unintended consequences across diverse populations.

Challenge 2: Using Appropriate Tools to Assess Model Integrity and Data Set Completeness

Model integrity is a critical requirement for the use of AI in nursing science or the development of researched-generated AI solutions for clinical practice. A key challenge for nurse scientists is to recognize when models are built on incomplete or biased data. AI tools rely on the quality of the data that have been inputted and assuring data integrity is a critical research priority. As such, nurse scientists must determine that datasets may completely reflect the diversity of patients that nurses care for and, if not, how such biases may impact solution quality, care outcomes, and equity (both in terms of representativeness of the data and the consequences of biased solutions to healthcare access). Moreover, nurse-generated data are often not standardized, with nursing documentation varying across hospitals or units (Keenan et al., 2013), and therefore may be unsuitable for use in AI algorithms. When nurse scientists evaluate the performance of AI models and their impact on patient care quality, such challenges related to data quality should be acknowledged and addressed. A related and promising area of inquiry for nurse scientists are the strategies needed to generate better data from clinical settings or creating alternative approaches that offer novel uses of data and insights into how clinical data are actually generated. For instance, an early warning system developed by Rossetti and colleagues, labeled CONCERN (Communicating Narrative Concerns Entered by RNs (Rossetti et al., 2025), draws on surveillance patterns from nursing metadata, which allows the system to operate independently of the quality or standardization of clinical notes.

Challenge 3: Safeguarding the Integrity of Nursing Practice

In addition to the generic ethical concerns related to the use of AI in health care shared with other health professions (e.g., privacy, data security, and bias mitigation), one ethical concern unique to nursing is its responsibility to preserve the integrity of nursing practice in the face of growing perception (from those outside of nursing) that much of nursing care can be accomplished through AI (Jo, 2024). Some of this false belief arises from the historical tradition of nurses undertaking many administrative and housekeeping responsibilities because of their continuous presence in the clinical setting. This unfortunately led to a concern that the scope of nursing science becomes vague, potentially losing its identity and foundational philosophy. Rony and colleagues reported that experienced healthcare professionals not only worried about job security or obsolescence when it comes to AI, but they also highlighted the potential devaluation of their expertise and years of training (Rony et al., 2024). As Ross and colleagues (2024) highlighted, the ability to have a nuanced understanding of patient needs, demonstrate emotional intelligence, and exercise ethical discernment remains at the core of nursing and cannot replace by AI (Ross et al., 2024). Engaging nurse scientists in the AI development processes will therefore promote systems that are valuable and consistent with the theoretical perspectives and epistemological contributions that define the discipline and form the hallmark of nursing’s unique impact on patient care. Well-educated nurse scientists who possess fluency in both AI and Nursing will ensure greater representation throughout the design, implementation, and evaluation of AI systems, safeguarding nursing science’s evidence-based frameworks, distinct knowledge base, and research methodologies.

Challenge 4: Addressing the Validity of AI Systems

Current AI systems continue to evolve but persist in producing unreliable results, with issues such as "hallucination” (i.e., generating inaccurate yet persuasive outputs) and incomplete reasoning abilities remaining. While the need for standardization in reviewing the accuracy of AI-generated outputs has been recognized (Asgari et al., 2025), challenges persist in ensuring the validity of AI systems. Surprisingly, even with these limitations, AI systems are often embraced and integrated into the healthcare system before they are rigorously evaluated, and literature is clear that they are already reshaping nursing roles in leadership, clinical decision-making, workflow management, and patient engagement (Burford et al., 2025). Nurse scientists have a key role to ensure that AI systems used for nursing assessments and care delivery among patients and families are adopted only following comprehensive assessments of their clinical, ethical, social implications. Nurse scientists should be critical members of interdisciplinary teams that address the validity and optimize the use of such AI applications aiming to prevent patient harm and inequitable care.

Challenge 5: Bridging the AI Development-Research Gap

An unexpected barrier to full evaluation of AI systems arises from misalignment of academic research timelines and the Research & Development (R&D) industry settings. The current academic research funding and reward models consume long periods of time for development and review, which cannot keep pace with the rapid speed of AI development. By the time nurse researchers receive funding to validate projects using AI systems, the technology may already be outdated, with newer AI iterations emerging. Such challenges suggest that traditional funding mechanisms and research incentive structures that support nursing science may not be adequate, nor timely and new funding mechanisms are urgently needed.

Nursing Science Considerations on the Clinical, Ethical, and Social Implications

Previous research has highlighted promising areas for AI integration in healthcare, from the early detection and redress of clinical problems to individualization of treatment plans based on patient profiles, data visualization to glean clinical insights, and workflows streamlining to enhance efficiency (Dixon et al., 2024). At the center of many of these innovations are clinical nurses, who generate real-world health data that power AI systems and can offer expertise in the clinical workflow within which such algorithms operate. Recognizing the crucial roles that nurses play, the National Institute of Nursing Research (NINR) working group on AI & Data Science recently issued priority research recommendations for advancing AI and data science in nursing science. NINR, first and foremost, aims to support nursing research that leverages AI-driven health innovations and validates whether the models work fairly across all populations. They also recommend promoting nursing research, as well as system and organization-level initiatives, to improve digital infrastructure for equitable technology access, as well as studies that engage patients, families, and communities throughout the development and evaluation process (National Institute of Nursing Research, 2025). Statements such as these attest to the institutional support that provides crucial foundations for nursing research addressing AI and legitimize these efforts. Nonetheless, the practical limitations of AI remain and still require much work. Nurse scientists skilled in AI and experienced in its development and use can safeguard and govern AI’s progression in ways that mitigate potential bias, preserve professional integrity, and prevent harm to the patient population. As a profession most trusted by the U.S. public and viewed as providing care with honesty and ethical standards (Brenan & Jones, 2024), nurse scientists must ensure this trust is not compromised in the era of AI integration.

An emerging ethical consideration arises from the common parlance, becoming rapidly adopted, of imbuing in AI solutions characteristics of humans, such as creative thought and agency (Floridi & Sanders, 2004; Ivcevic & Grandinetti, 2024). With recent AI approaches demonstrating the ability to generate text, create images, and simulate real-life conversations, there is a tendency to refer to AI-developed tools as “agents” (Y. Li et al., 2023; Ulrich et al., 2026). Such trends spark a critical philosophical question of whether AI can be considered agents in the same ways as humans, including nurses and other healthcare professionals are. Agency implies willful and exacting application of knowledge, judgment and intentionality. Nurses are considered moral agents because of their primary commitment, whether that is to an individual, family, community, or population, grounded in professional principles and social contracts (American Nurses Association, 2025). They have both ethical and professional obligations to support their patients that include acting to facilitate positive outcomes based on the nurses’ own self-embodied principles and knowledge (Raines, 2000). However, as AI is increasingly involved as an influential factor in clinical decision-making, the extent to which clinical decisions will be driven and impacted by AI poses an unusual threat – to which nurse scientists must guide solutions that preserve clinical autonomy and accountability. Unlike providers or institutions that can be held accountable for their own professional decisions and ethical reasonings, AI currently operates without any concrete protocols or regulations regarding accountability and moral responsibility. While there are existing AI frameworks and professional codes of conduct to help support AI governance (American Medical Association, 2023; Floridi & Cowls, 2022), they remain insufficiently actionable in practice. Nurse scientists would obviously not view these tools as equivalent to human agents. However, there is an industry trend to market or position AI tools as “AI nurse” or “robot nurse” potentially blurring the distinction between human clinical judgment and automated assistance (Ibuki et al., 2024). AI’s potential impact on nurses’ agency as moral decision-makers in their day-to-day practice and patient interactions warrants further exploration.

A key societal focus for the integration of AI into nursing science lies in assuring that patients understand how their data will be used. Issues surrounding transparency and accountability exist at the intersection of technology (AI) and professional scope, and therefore, serve as future agendas for nursing research. Nurses have a particular role in helping patients, as recipients of care, understand some of the broader issues surrounding AI in healthcare, and nurse scientists should work to devise strategies to help them do so. Patients may not realize that their data can be used for data-driven research (Ulrich et al., 2021), nor that AI may shape the clinical decisions affecting them. Yet, procedures to ensure patients’ informed consent regarding AI usage remain underdeveloped and remain a fertile area for investigation. Nurse scientists should examine and develop new approaches to understand how AI can address the limitations of informed consent, as current opt-out protocols for secondary data usage may be insufficient, and further research is needed to determine whether new consent models should be designed and implemented as healthcare workflows may be reshaped by AI.

Guidelines for Effective Integration of AI Into Nursing Science

There are two critical issues here: preparing nurse scientists to create, develop, evaluate AI methodologies and ensuring that nurse scientists devise the strategies to foster the use of AI to grow nursing knowledge and support nursing practice. We propose the following actionable guidelines to ensure the effective and responsible integration of AI into nursing science.

Expand AACN’s Essentials Domain 8 on informatics and healthcare technology (American Association of Colleges of Nursing, 2021 ) to include not only AI as an educational tool but also education about how to design, develop, deploy, and evaluate AI in nursing science. To best prepare future nurse scientists to effectively incorporate AI in their research agenda, AI literacy must first be promoted within the profession. In its recent training recommendations (drafted shortly prior to our summit and released few months later), NINR encourages all nursing programs to integrate foundational education in AI. This includes building on existing informatics education by adding AI components, as well as training on strategies to address bias and other concerns that arise with the use of AI systems (National Institute of Nursing Research, 2025). To ensure that such training is adopted consistently and widely across institutions, we urge the development of a set of AI-in-nursing competencies that can guide the design of educational curricula and courses regarding AI. Indeed, AI literacy will benefit all nurses, including nurse scientists. A basic foundation in AI literacy will be critical as AI continues to evolve in its capabilities amid day-to-day technological improvements. Specifically for pre- and post-doctoral fellows in Nursing, they should be well aware of the capabilities and limitations of AI to effectively integrate AI into their scholarly inquiry.

Expand the participation of nurse scientists in interdisciplinary AI working groups that address the development, design, deployment, evaluation, and policy-making regarding AI. These interdisciplinary AI working groups should also have a balanced representation from other domains, including academics from both STEM fields and humanities, industry professionals from informatics and data science, regulatory bodies, administrative leadership, and patients or their advocates if applicable. When formal inclusion is absent, nurse scientists should strategically pursue partnerships with administrative leadership, industry, and regulatory stakeholders as well as engage in public policy advocacy, to ensure nursing perspectives meaningfully shape AI priorities, oversight, and accountability.

Apply rigorous methods, metrics, and implementation science frameworks in deployment of AI-based solutions. Metrics to assess reliability and bias should be clearly outlined, along with anticipated outcomes and process variables, to create templates to formatively evaluate the AI deployment. Applying and adhering to implementation science frameworks throughout the deployment of AI solutions can be helpful as it explores the barriers, facilitators, and strategies related to the gaps between evidence and real-world practice (Handley et al., 2016). In the context of AI, issues surrounding accountability and risk mitigation should also be considered for successful implementation.

Recognize the financial implications of the integration of AI using a nursing science perspective. This requires moving beyond the assumption that AI adoption will yield a positive return on investment (ROI). Importantly, nursing-specific total cost of ownership (TCO) and ROI assessments may reveal neutral or even negative economic returns, particularly during early adoption or in resource-constrained settings. Rather than viewing negative TCO as failure, nurse scientists are uniquely positioned to interrogate why value fails to materialize, whether due to workflow misalignment, increased cognitive or documentation burden, inequitable deployment, unintended safety risks, or downstream labor displacement. This analytic lens differentiates nursing-led evaluation from purely technical or administrative cost analyses. Nurse scientist–led financial evaluation further differs from generic AI integration efforts by explicitly modeling costs and benefits at the level of nursing work, including time-on-task, staffing mix, surveillance burden, moral distress, training load, and impacts on care continuity—dimensions that are rarely captured in institutional ROI calculations. Comprehensive economic analyses should therefore be conducted before, during, and after implementation to capture dynamic performance over time, including learning curves, technology fatigue, and algorithmic drift. These nursing-sensitive cost profiles should be balanced not only against fiscal savings but also against care quality, workforce sustainability, patient safety, and educational outcomes (e.g., competency and cognitive load), even when financial returns are unfavorable. By explicitly surfacing when and why AI investments fail to deliver value in nursing contexts, nurse scientists can inform more responsible adoption decisions, guide system redesign, and shape industry development toward technologies that generate true clinical and workforce benefit, not merely financial promise.

Demonstrate ways to promote the ethical integration of AI in healthcare. Being involved in the creation, selection, and implementation of AI systems, nurse scientists should strive to promote transparent AI use, safeguard patient data and actively work to mitigate biases that could affect patient outcomes. As part of a broader research agenda, nurse scientists have the opportunity to explore how to promote patient trust and acceptability of AI integration across various aspects of patient care and clinical decision-making. Specific examples for this recommendation include the establishment of participatory “AI-in-practice” frameworks that integrate ethics throughout the lifecycle of AI systems. Nurse scientists can work on embedding transparency mechanisms (e.g., model cards, plain-language summaries, and data provenance documentation) into all research and clinical AI applications so patients, families, and clinicians can clearly understand what the AI does and does not do. Additionally, nurse scientists can conduct ongoing community engagement, including educational sessions and patient-family dialogs, disseminate op-eds and hold discussion groups on social media platforms to explain how AI tools inform care decisions and to gather feedback that informs iterative redesign.

Best Practices for Synergies With Industry

To foster successful and sustainable interdisciplinary collaborations between nurse scientists and industry partners, a more strategic, bidirectional approach is needed, one that moves beyond asserting the value of nursing expertise toward systematically demonstrating its impact across the AI development lifecycle. Such synergistic collaborations with industry have also been recommended for nursing science and other academic disciplines before (Beal, 2012; Halili et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2019; Byrne et al., 2025). In the context of nursing science, a critical first step is intentional awareness-building within industry about the distinct scientific, clinical, and implementation strengths nurse scientists bring to AI innovation. These include not only deep clinical domain expertise, but also methodological rigor in real-world evaluation, implementation science, human-centered design, and longitudinal patient engagement—capabilities that are often underutilized or insufficiently visible in commercial AI development.

Rather than positioning nurse scientists as peripheral contributors to technology design, effective partnerships should embed them as co-architects of problem formulation, validation strategies, and deployment pathways. Nurse scientists are uniquely positioned to translate frontline clinical phenomena into tractable AI-ready questions, define meaningful outcomes beyond technical performance, and surface contextual constraints that determine whether systems succeed or fail in real-world care. Their expertise in trust-building and patient engagement further strengthens adoption, usability, and equity; dimensions that increasingly shape regulatory approval, reimbursement, and market success. These priorities align with recent recommendations from the NINR Working Group, which emphasize early integration of patient-centered design and call for adaptive academic–industry collaboration models that support long-term sustainability (National Institute of Nursing Research, 2025). While general academic–industry partnership frameworks for AI exist (Pantanowitz et al., 2022), they are not specific to nursing science and to the translational realities of care delivery. Nurse scientists, in turn, can leverage these models to more systematically operationalize their value proposition through demonstrable contributions to product design, implementation success, regulatory readiness, and post-deployment evaluation.

Importantly, translation to industry cannot be viewed as a one-directional handoff from academia to market. Instead, it should be structured as an iterative innovation and validation cycle, in which nurse-led clinical insights inform product design, real-world performance data feed back into algorithm refinement, and ongoing evaluation sustains safety, effectiveness, and equity over time. Across this continuum, durable partnerships will depend less on appeals for inclusion and more on a track record of co-produced evidence, shared incentives, and cumulative trust built through repeated successful collaborations.

Conclusion

AI is emerging as a crucial component of healthcare and will ultimately have a great impact on the nursing profession. Nurse scientists have the responsibility to ensure that nurses’ unique strengths are fully integrated into the development of AI. In this paper, we discussed crucial research priorities that require ongoing discourse to strengthen nursing science alongside technological progress. Knowledge and competencies surrounding AI should no longer be seen as relevant only to nurse researchers with informatics expertise; all nurse scientists should be able to critically evaluate its usage, both as a creative health innovation and as a research method. However, rigorous research alone is not enough to strengthen AI in nursing science. Funding systems that align well with the fast pace and iterative nature of AI must also be supported. Additionally, academic institutions must prepare the future generations of nurse scientists and interdisciplinary partners for effective collaboration in AI research. Partnership between nursing academia and industry should also be sustained to promote real-world applications of AI-driven nursing inquiry. Nurse scientists bring clinical and research expertise along with trained perspectives on holistic and relational care. This unique combination positions them to drive meaningful contributions to the field, expanding their role as patient advocates beyond traditional clinical settings to the AI arena. It is time for the profession to empower its scientific workforce and ensure AI serves as a trustworthy tool and research method for optimizing health for all.
