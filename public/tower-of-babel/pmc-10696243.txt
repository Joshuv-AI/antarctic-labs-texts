Modified femoral neck rotational osteotomy with supercapsular approach as hip preservation surgery technique for osteonecrosis of femoral head in young patient: A case report
Authors: Aditya Fuad Robby Triangga, Rahadyan Magetsari, Hilmi Muhammad, Bernadeta Fuad Paramita Rahayu, Dandy Ardhan Fazatamma, Paramita Ayu Saraswati

Introduction

Osteonecrosis of Femoral Head (ONFH) is a debilitating condition that can lead to significant hip pain and disability caused by interruption of the artery supplying the femoral head [1]. Numerous etiological factors, including long-term corticosteroid usage, contribute to the development of osteonecrosis. When non-invasive treatments such as bisphosphonate have failed and necrosis has progressed, surgical intervention is needed in which several options are available. The joint-preserving technique is preferred in the pre‐ or early collapse stage, mainly to reserve the need of Total Hip Arthroplasty (THA) for the more advanced stage [1,2].

Osteotomy is a joint-preserving technique that involves shifting the weight-bearing region to the healthy part of the femoral head to reduce intraosseous venous pressure and stimulate neovascularization through surgery [3]. In the surgery for ONFH, preserving the integrity of vascular structures, especially the branches of the Medial Femoral Circumflex Artery (MFCA), is paramount in facilitating the process of bone healing and mitigating the risk of additional harm [4]. Therefore, several attempts to optimize vascularization have been developed through ideas of novel approaches.

In this case report, we would like to describe our experience in managing a young ONFH patient with a modified Femoral Neck Rotational Osteotomy (FNRO) using Hip Supercapsular Approach. This report was assembled in accordance with SCARE guidelines [5].

Case presentation

A 21-year-old female patient with a 5-year history of Systematic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) and long-term use of corticosteroid medication presented to our orthopaedic clinic at Dr. Sardjito General Hospital, Yogyakarta, Indonesia with complaints of left hip pain and limping worsening over the past two months. The pain was intermittent with a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score of 6/10, and worsened after physical activity. The pain has been managed by resting and diclofenac use. An X-ray examination showed an irregular articular surface of the left hip joint, which is further revealed using Computed Tomography (CT) Scan to be a deformity in the left femoral head with visible crescent sign. From the imaging, the Kerboul angle was 180o, indicating a medium necrotic area (Fig. 1). The Kerboul angle was measured by adding the arc of the necrotic area on the coronal and sagittal view of the femoral head.Fig. 1(a). From physical examination, a leg length discrepancy of 1 cm shorter was found on her left leg. No signs of swelling, crepitation, or other inflammatory signs were observed. The patient's pain was exacerbated during passive movement, while skin sensation and blood circulation in the lower limb were normal. Based on the examination results, we concluded the final diagnosis of left ONFH FICAT II-III ARCO stage 3. With the patient's young age as our main consideration, hip preservation therapy was the preferred treatment. We decided to perform a Femoral Neck Rotational Osteotomy in combination with the Hip Supercapsular Approach that is commonly used in Total Hip Arthroplasty procedures.

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia and guided with C-arm. We followed the method described by Chow and Quitmann in approaching the hip capsule through supercapsular approach [6,7]. In the lateral decubitus position, the patient's left limb was flexed, internally rotated, and maximally adducted. A 10 cm incision was made in the skin along the femur, starting from the tip of the trochanter major and extending proximally. After making the incision through the skin and subcutaneous tissue, the gluteus maximus muscle was gently dissected parallel with its fibers, to expose the bursa tissue that overlies the gluteus medius. The bursa tissue was then incised at the posterior rim of the gluteus medius. By mobilizing the gluteus medius anteriorly, the gluteus minimus and piriformis tendon were exposed. To reveal the capsule, the piriformis tendon and gluteus minimus are respectively retracted posteriorly and anteriorly. Subsequently, the capsular incision was made at its superior portion, aligning with the skin incision and the femoral neck (Fig. 2). The femoral head was visible once the capsule was opened. Upon completion of the intracapsular preparation, a K-wire was inserted to serve as a guide for the osteotomy level. The femoral neck was then cut at the basicervical region with the assistance of fluoroscopy for precise positioning and then continued with chisel blade. Schanz screw (ORMED 5.0-mm) was introduced to facilitate the rotation of the femoral head. Following the femoral head rotation, we made another 3 cm incision below the first incision to allow fixation devices to be inserted. Once the necrotic area was successfully rotated to a non-weight-bearing region, cannulated screws (SYNTHES 6.5-mm) were utilized to secure the position of the femoral head. These screws were inserted from the lateral side of the femur, perpendicular to the line of the osteotomy, and aligned with the axis of the femoral neck (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). Following the surgical procedure, the patient was given bisphosphonate and pain medication. Non weight-bearing exercises were also prescribed for three months after surgery, continued with protective weight-bearing exercise until the sixth month. On a 3-month evaluation, the patient's pain improved significantly. The Harris Hip Score (HHS) indicates a significant improvement in hip functionality (86.2) compared to the preoperative score (53.7). Discussion

Different arteries are thought to be the supplier of the femoral head throughout our lifetime. The medial and lateral circumflex femoral arteries primarily supply the femoral head in pediatric population. As life progresses, the medial circumflex femoral artery (MCFA) becomes more predominant in supplying the femoral head. Any interference with these vessels can reduce the nutrient supply to the femoral head and progress into ONFH [1]. Indirect factors such as alcoholism and prolonged use of corticosteroids play significant roles in the development of ONFH by contributing to over 80 % of the cases [3].

ONFH faces a lack of agreement regarding the optimal treatment to manage the cases [8]. Management for ONFH is generally divided into joint-preserving technique that is commonly applied in pre- or early collapse stage and THA for the advanced collapse. As joint-preserving technique has shown a significant outcome in pre- or early collapse stage, attempts should be taken to save the joint and postpone or avoid the need for an early THA [[1], [2], [3]]. Moreover, the implementation of THA in a very young age may be difficult. Firstly, fixation failures and osteolysis are likely to occur after surgery, with the revision rate ranging from 8 to 67 % [9]. Secondly, the average lifespan of THA itself is 15 years, meaning that changing the device in later years is necessary [10].

Femoral Neck Rotational Osteotomy (FNRO) is one of joint-preservation surgery technique that aims to move the weight-bearing point of the femoral head by rotating the necrotic zone anteriorly or posteriorly around the neck axis [4]. In order to promote bone healing and prevent further damage, vascular preservation – particularly the branch of MFCA, is essential when doing the surgical procedure. Preserving this vessel may pose challenges for surgeons. We selected FNRO based on the evidence provided by Zhu et al., which demonstrates its efficacy in treating ONFH lesions located laterally. Furthermore, FNRO allows for considerable rotation of the femoral head without affecting vascularization [4].

In this case, we adopted the Supercapsular method in approaching the hip capsule from the SuperPATH hip approach due to its numerous advantages. Initially developed as a THA approach, it offers a minimally invasive technique that has gained significant popularity in contemporary practice. Its main objective is to minimize the extent of dissection to the delicate soft tissue and blood vessels around the hip joint, in comparison to the conventional posterior approach that involves a dislocation maneuver [6,7]. Supercapsular approach involves maintaining the femur in its anatomical position throughout the surgery. This technique makes early mobilization possible, offering improved patient outcomes and facilitating a quicker recovery [7,11].

In addition to the requirement for small incisions, the Supercapsular approach utilizes the anatomical interval between the gluteus minimus and piriformis muscles to access the femoral head [6,7]. This technique ensures that the external rotator muscles and iliotibial band remain unharmed [7,12]. The capsule is cut vertically at the superior portion and may extend anteriorly if extra room is needed, conserving the posterior capsule [6]. Although the small surgical area limits the visibility of the field, it allows for a safe approach in assessing necrotic lesions, healthy bone and cartilage through a window created by the intact piriformis muscle [12]. On the contrary, the conventional posterior approach involves cutting the piriformis and the posterior capsule potentially compromising the branches of MFCA that mostly reside at the posterior portion of the femoral neck.

The Supercapsular approach offers better postoperative pain level and early postoperative function compared to the conventional posterior approach. It shows a lower VAS score and higher HHS score and Barthel Index (BI) for Activities of Daily Living in short-term follow-up intervals [11]. Lower complication rate, dislocation rate and shorter length of hospital stay also have been reported [13]. Another study suggests that the need for abductor dissection increases intra-operative time and longer return to daily activity in the transgluteal (Hardinge) approach [14]. Injuries to the superior gluteal nerve, which can cause a Trendelenburg gait pattern, is a possible complication in abductor dissection.

Bisphosphonates were administered to our patient as preventive measures to impede necrotic bone resorption during the revascularization phase. This adjunctive treatment serves as a protective measure against the occurrence of subchondral fracture or collapse and facilitates bone healing [3].

By keeping the goal of vascular preservation in mind, hip supercapsular is an approach worth considering for FNRO surgery. This approach protects soft tissue and vessels around the hip joint, thus giving better short-term outcome compared to the common approaches used today. However, there are limitations in this paper as we only assess the union rate and patient's functional activity. A further objective examination of vascularity (e.g.: CT angiography) and a longer follow-up time is needed.

Conclusion

This case report suggests that Modified FNRO with Hip Supercapsular Approach may be a promising surgical procedure for young patients with ONFH to delay or avoid the need for an early THA. It provides good pain relief, joint function, and radiographic outcomes. Accurate staging according to classification should be completed to ensure appropriate patient selection for the surgical treatment.
