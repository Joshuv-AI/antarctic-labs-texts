INPROVF: Leveraging Large Language Models to Repair High-level Robot Controllers from Assumption Violations
Qian Meng *, Jin Peng Zhou *, Kilian Q. Weinberger, Hadas Kress-Gazit

Abstract
This paper presents INPROVF, an automatic framework that combines large language models (LLMs) and formal methods to speed up the repair process of high-level robot controllers. Previous approaches based solely on formal methods are computationally expensive and cannot scale to large state spaces. In contrast, INPROVF uses LLMs to generate repair candidates, and formal methods to verify their correctness. To improve the quality of these candidates, our framework first translates the symbolic representations of the environment and controllers into natural language descriptions. If a candidate fails the verification, INPROVF provides feedback on potential unsafe behaviors or unsatisfied tasks, and iteratively prompts LLMs to generate improved solutions. We demonstrate the effectiveness of INPROVF through 12 violations with various workspaces, tasks, and state space sizes.



I
Introduction

Safety-critical robotic applications,
such as
autonomous driving
[
1
]
,
search and rescue missions
[
2
]
,
and
assistive caregiving robots (e.g.
[
3
]
),
benefit from formal guarantees to ensure safe operations
and task completions,
the lack of which may result in
harm to users and even
loss of life in catastrophic events.
One way to provide such guarantees is the use of formal synthesis—an automatic technique that transforms temporal logic specifications into correct-by-construction systems— to
create provably correct high-level robot controllers
that compose low-level robot skills to
satisfy temporally extended tasks
in complex environments
[
4
]
.

Among formal synthesis methods,

Generalized Reactivity(1) (GR(1))
synthesis
[
5
]

is used in robotics applications
due to its relatively low polynomial-time complexity with respect to the state space
and its expressivity
covering
many robotic tasks
[
6
]
.
A
GR(1)
specification
takes the form of
an implication from
assumptions
about the environment’s behaviors to
guarantees
on the robot’s behaviors.
Specifically, the guarantees depend on the validity of
the assumptions;
if any assumption is violated at runtime,
the synthesized controller is no longer guaranteed to be correct,
leading to potential undesirable behaviors.

To address assumption violations,
our previous work
[
7
]
leverages formal methods-based approaches
[
8
]
to repair robot behaviors by generating new skills necessary for task satisfaction following assumption violations.
However, our previous repair approach fails to scale to real-world settings with large state spaces at runtime due to the state explosion problem that has challenged formal methods for decades
[
9
]
.

Recent advances in
large language models (LLMs)
present a unique opportunity to overcome the scalability problem of formal methods-based repair.
Modern
LLMs
,
trained on Internet-scale data across diverse domains,
have strong priors and can address complex reasoning tasks–ranging from
competitive mathematics
and
coding problems
to
PhD-level science questions
[
10
]
.
Since repairing high-level robot controllers under assumption violation is itself a complex reasoning task,
we aim to
leverage the reasoning capacities of
LLMs
to scale up
repair.

In this work,
we propose INPROVF,
a hybrid framework
that
combines the strengths of
LLMs
and formal methods
to repair assumption violations in high-level robot controllers at runtime.
We use
LLMs
to generate repair candidates,
and then verify them through formal methods.
If the verification fails,
we employ formal analysis to provide feedback,
which is then used by
LLMs
to
iteratively refine the candidates.

As illustrated in Fig.
1
,
INPROVF consists of
four key steps:

In

Repair
Pro
mpt,

V

and
F

In

Informalization
,
we convert
the symbolic abstractions of both the environment
and the synthesized high-level controller
into

descriptions that capture their underlying semantics–specifically, the physical meaning of the abstraction and the behavior description of the controller (Sec.
V-A

Our intuition is that

,
primarily trained on natural language data and

[

,

Next,
in the

we assemble

the task specifications,

To ensure the soundness of the candidates,

If

).

Contributions
.
To our knowledge,
INPROVF is the first framework that leverages
LLMs
to accelerate the repair process of assumption violations in large state spaces.
By integrating LLM-based skill generation
with verification and feedback
via formal methods,
INPROVF enables
provably-correct robotic systems
to remain robust in complex, real-world environments with prohibitively large state spaces.

Abstractions
.
We model the physical state space with a discrete abstraction. An abstraction comprises a set of atomic propositions
AP
partitioned into environment inputs
{\mathcal{X}}
and system outputs
{\mathcal{Y}}
(
\textit{AP}={\mathcal{X}}\cup{\mathcal{Y}}
).
The set of inputs
{\mathcal{X}}
,
representing the environment states,
is further divided into sets of controllable inputs
{{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{c}}}
and uncontrollable inputs
{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{u}}
(
{\mathcal{X}}={{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{c}}}\cup{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{u}}

Controllable inputs

e.g. the position of the robot base.
Uncontrollable inputs
{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{u}}

represent the part of the environment that the robot has no control over,
e.g. the position of an obstacle.
An input state
\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}\subseteq{\mathcal{X}}
and a controllable input state
\sigma_{\textrm{c}}\subseteq{{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{c}}}
are subsets of inputs and controllable inputs, respectively.
Let the physical state space be

.
A grounding function

\textrm{G}:{\mathcal{X}}\to 2^{X}

maps each input
\pi\in{\mathcal{X}}

into a set of physical states

.
Intuitively, the grounding function captures the physical meaning of each input.

Specifications
.
We use the
GR(1)
fragment of
LTL
for its synthesis algorithm and expressivity.
The syntax of
LTL
is

\varphi\coloneqq\pi~|~\neg\varphi~|~\varphi\vee\varphi~|~
\bigcirc\varphi~|~
\varphi\ \mathcal{U}\varphi

where
a formula
\varphi
is constructed recursively from atomic propositions
\pi\in\mathcal{AP}
,
logical operators (
\neg,\vee
),
and temporal operators
next
(
\bigcirc
) and
until
(
\mathcal{U}
).
Additional logical operators (
\land,\to,\leftrightarrow
)
and temporal operators
eventually
(
\lozenge
) and
always
(
\square
)
can be derived from previous operators.
The semantics of
LTL
is defined over infinite traces
\tau=(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\dots)
, where each
\sigma_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{AP}
denotes the set of propositions that are true at step
i
. When a trace suffix starting at position
i
satisfies a formula
\varphi
, we denote this as
\tau^{i}\models\varphi
.
By convention, we say that a trace
\tau
satisfies a formula
\varphi
, denoted as
\tau\models\varphi
, if the formula is satisfied from the beginning of the trace (i.e.,
\tau^{0}\models\varphi
).
The satisfaction relation is defined recursively as follows:
\tau^{i}\models\pi
if
\pi\in\sigma_{i}
(
\pi
is true at step
i
);

\tau^{i}\models\bigcirc\varphi
if
\tau^{i+1}\models\varphi
(
\varphi
holds at the next step);
\tau^{i}\models\lozenge\varphi
if
\exists j\geq i
such that
\tau^{j}\models\varphi
(
\varphi
eventually holds); and
\tau^{i}\models\square\varphi
if
\forall j\geq i
,
\tau^{j}\models\varphi
(
\varphi
always holds).
A more detailed introduction of
LTL
semantics can be found in
[
13
]
.

Specifications in the
GR(1)
fragment of
LTL
take the form

\varphi=\varphi_{\textrm{e}}\to\varphi_{\textrm{s}}

where

\varphi_{\textrm{e}}=\varphi^{\textrm{i}}_{\textrm{e}}\land\varphi^{\textrm{t}}_{\textrm{e}}\land\varphi^{\textrm{g}}_{\textrm{e}}
represents the environment assumptions and

\varphi_{\textrm{s}}=\varphi^{\textrm{i}}_{\textrm{s}}\land\varphi^{\textrm{t}}_{\textrm{s}}\land\varphi^{\textrm{g}}_{\textrm{s}}
denotes the system guarantees.
The initial constraints
\varphi^{\textrm{i}}_{\textrm{e}}
and
\varphi^{\textrm{i}}_{\textrm{s}}
,
defined over
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
,
specify the initial conditions of the environment and the system, respectively.
The safety constraints,

\varphi^{\textrm{t}}_{\textrm{e}}
and
\varphi^{\textrm{t}}_{\textrm{s}}
,
capture conditions that must hold at all times
and take the form of

\varphi^{\textrm{t}}_{\textrm{a}}=\bigwedge_{i}\square\varphi^{\textrm{t}}_{\textrm{a},i}
,
for
\textrm{a}\in\{\textrm{e, s}\}
,
where
\varphi^{\textrm{t}}_{\textrm{e},i}
is defined over
\mathcal{AP}\cup\bigcirc{\mathcal{X}}

and
\varphi^{\textrm{t}}_{\textrm{s},i}
over
\mathcal{AP}\cup\bigcirc\mathcal{AP}
.
Lastly, we have the liveness constraints
\varphi^{\textrm{g}}_{\textrm{e}}
and
\varphi^{\textrm{g}}_{\textrm{s}}

which encode
environment assumptions and system goals that must be satisfied repeatedly.
For
\textrm{a}\in\{\textrm{e, s}\}
,

\varphi^{\textrm{g}}_{\textrm{a}}=\bigwedge_{i}\square\lozenge\varphi^{\textrm{g}}_{\textrm{a},i}

where each
\varphi^{\textrm{g}}_{\textrm{a},i}
is defined over
\mathcal{AP}
.

Synthesis
.

GR(1)
synthesis
[
5
]
first transforms the
GR(1)
specification into a two-player game between the environment and the system,
where the transition rules for each player are defined by the safety constraints
\varphi^{\textrm{t}}_{\textrm{e}}
and
\varphi^{\textrm{t}}_{\textrm{s}}
,
and the winning conditions are defined by the liveness goals
\varphi^{\textrm{g}}_{\textrm{e}}
and
\varphi^{\textrm{g}}_{\textrm{s}}
, respectively.
Next, synthesis computes a set of winning states from which the system is guaranteed to win the game, a process called
realizability check
.
Finally, synthesis extracts a finite automaton from the winning states as the robot
strategy
.

Definition
2.

A
strategy
is a
deterministic finite automaton
{\mathcal{A}}_{\textrm{s}}=(\Sigma,\Sigma_{0},{\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}},\delta,\gamma_{\mathcal{X}},\gamma_{\mathcal{Y}})
where

\bullet

\Sigma
is a set of states and
\Sigma_{0}\subseteq\Sigma
is the set of initial states.

\bullet

{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
are sets of inputs and outputs.

\bullet

\delta:\Sigma\times 2^{\mathcal{X}}\to\Sigma

is the transition function that
maps a state and an input state to the next state.

\bullet

\gamma_{\mathcal{X}}:\Sigma\to 2^{\mathcal{X}}

maps a state to its labeled input state.

\bullet

\gamma_{\mathcal{Y}}:\Sigma\to 2^{\mathcal{Y}}

maps a state to its labeled output state.

A
strategy description

{\mathcal{D}}
for the strategy
{\mathcal{A}}_{\textrm{s}}
is a natural language description of how
{\mathcal{A}}_{\textrm{s}}
would behave.

If the set of winning states does not overlap with all system liveness goals or does not cover the initial states,
a winning strategy for the system does not exist,
i.e. the specification is unrealizable.
Conversely, a winning strategy for the environment, or a
counterstrategy

[
19
]
, exists.

Definition
3.

A
counterstrategy
is a nondeterministic finite automaton
{\mathcal{A}}_{\textrm{e}}=(\Sigma,\Sigma_{0},{\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}},\delta_{\textrm{e}},\delta_{\textrm{s}},\gamma_{\mathcal{X}},\gamma_{\mathcal{Y}},\gamma_{\textrm{g}})
where

\bullet

\Sigma,\Sigma_{0},{\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}},\gamma_{\mathcal{X}},\gamma_{\mathcal{Y}}
are the same as in Definition
III
.

\bullet

\delta_{\textrm{e}}:\Sigma\to 2^{\mathcal{X}}

maps a state
to the next input state.

\bullet

\delta_{\textrm{s}}:\Sigma\times 2^{\mathcal{X}}\to 2^{\Sigma}

maps a state

\sigma

and the next input state

\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}

to a set of possible next states

\delta_{\textrm{s}}(\sigma,\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}})\subseteq 2^{\mathcal{X}}
.

\bullet

\gamma_{\textrm{g}}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{N}

maps a state
\sigma
to the index of a system liveness goal the state attempts to satisfy.

Given

(ii)

V-A

Informalization

We begin by leveraging
LLMs
to translate
the environment abstraction and the synthesized strategy into informal natural language descriptions.
Since

LLMs
are primarily trained on natural language data and optimized for a wide range of reasoning tasks on
NL
traces,
we hypothesize that
LLMs
better utilize the abstraction and strategy behaviors in natural languages than in the formal representations.

Abstraction informalization
.
We take
in an abstraction consisting of
the environment inputs
{\mathcal{X}}
and
a grounding function
G

that maps each input to a set of physical states.
We prompt
LLMs
to describe the physical meaning of the abstraction.

shows the informalized abstraction description
for Example

.

Strategy informalization
.
Next, we
obtain
the behavior description of the strategy
{\mathcal{A}}_{\textrm{s}}
,
synthesized from the
GR(1)
specification
\varphi
.
Since each state of the strategy consists of environment input propositions,
we leverage the previously generated environment abstraction description to
provide the context for
LLMs
to ingest the strategy behavior.
Our preliminary study found that this is a relatively challenging task
due to the complexity of the strategy, which is a finite automaton as described in Definition
III
.
So,
we use one-shot prompting–including one example translation in the prompt–to help steer
LLMs
towards generating accurate strategy behavior.

V-B

Repair

The repair process takes in
the description of the abstraction
{\mathcal{X}}
,
the existing robot skills
{\mathcal{Y}}
,
the task specification
\varphi_{\textrm{task}}\preceq\varphi
,
provided by the user,
that only contains the essential constraints of the full
GR(1)
specification
\varphi
,
the assumption violation
t=(\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}},\sigma_{{\mathcal{Y}}},\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{\prime})
,
and the behavior description
{\mathcal{D}}
of the strategy
{\mathcal{A}}_{\textrm{s}}

synthesized from
\varphi
.
We form a prompt that combines the inputs and asks
LLMs
to only produce a set of new skill candidates
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}

in a predefined

domain-specific language (DSL)
.
We also require the new skills to be formatted as JSON files only,
and we perform regular expression matching to extract
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}

from the responses.
Fig.
4

presents the repair prompt,
and Figs.
4

4

for Example

V-C

Verification

After obtaining a set of new skill candidates
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}
,
we verify the correctness of
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}

through syntax and realizability checking,
ensuring that
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}

indeed
repairs the violation.

Syntax check
.
We check the syntax of
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}
against
the
DSL
grammar
shown in the repair prompt
(Fig.
4
E).
We leverage a parser to automatically transform
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}
into a set of
abstract syntax trees (ASTs)
.
If parsing is successful,
the new skills are in the correct syntax.
We also ensure type correctness by
recursively traversing each node of the
ASTs
,
verifying each component of
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}
against its expected type.

Realizability check
.
Once
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}
is in the correct syntax,
we check whether
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}
can repair the assumption violation.
We use a compiler from
[
7
]
to add
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}
to
\varphi
and relax the assumptions in
\varphi
that are violated by the violation

t=(\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}},\sigma_{{\mathcal{Y}}},\sigma_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{\prime})
,
resulting in an updated specification
\varphi^{\prime}
.
We then leverage the realizability check of a
GR(1)
synthesizer Slugs
[
25
]
to check
the realizability of
\varphi^{\prime}
.
If
\varphi^{\prime}
is realizable,
then

{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}
is verified to be sufficient to solve the violation.

V-D

Feedback

If the new skills
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}
do not pass the verification step,
we provide three types of feedback,
each of which targets a different type of error,
to ensure that we have informative
and yet concise
NL
feedback to
LLMs
for iterative prompting.

Syntax feedback
. For syntax errors detected in the syntax-checking procedure,
we provide feedback based on the nodes of the ASTs that violate the grammar and the grammar rules that are violated.
For instance,
for a new skill
y_{\textrm{new}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}
that contains an uncontrollable input
\pi_{\textrm{u}}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\textrm{u}}
in its precondition,
we automatically provide the following feedback: “
\pi_{\textrm{u}}
in the precondition of
y_{\textrm{new}}
is not a controllable input.”

Counterstrategy
. Next, if the new skills
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}
are syntactically correct but the updated specification
\varphi^{\prime}
remains unrealizable,
meaning skills in
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\textrm{new}}
do not repair the violation,
we compute a counterstrategy
{\mathcal{A}}_{\textrm{e}}

to provide feedback.
While the counterstrategy contains information about the cause of the unrealizability of
\varphi^{\prime}
,

LLMs
may not be able to utilize the formal representation of the counterstrategy (as shown in Definition
III
).
We thus perform formal analysis on the counterstrategy to
transform it into concise
NL
feedback.

Safety analysis
.
Given the counterstrategy
{\mathcal{A}}_{\textrm{e}}
and
the system safety constraints
\varphi^{\textrm{t}}_{\textrm{s}}=\bigwedge_{i}\square\varphi^{\textrm{t}}_{\textrm{s},i}
,
we provide feedback on if and which skill violates any safety constraint
\square\varphi^{\textrm{t}}_{\textrm{s},i}
.
As shown in Algorithm
1
,
We first identify any transition
(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
in
{\mathcal{A}}_{\textrm{e}}
such that
\sigma^{\prime}
is a sink state, i.e. there are no outgoing edges from
\sigma^{\prime}
(Line
1
).
We are interested in sink states because those are the states where the system has no valid next move, indicating states that violate safety constraints.
We then iterate through all system safety constraints
\square\varphi^{\textrm{t}}_{\textrm{s},i}
(Line
1
),
and check whether the transition violates any safety constraint
(Line
1
).
We perform this check by
formulating a conjunction of
the labels of
\sigma
,
the labels of
\sigma^{\prime}
,
and the safety constraint of interest,
and then leveraging an SMT solver Z3
[
26
]

to check the satisfiability of the conjunction,
as done in
[
20
]
.
If the constraint
\square\varphi^{\textrm{t}}_{\textrm{s},i}
is violated,
we provide the following
NL
feedback:
“the skills in
\gamma_{\mathcal{Y}}(\sigma)
violate the safety constraint
\square\varphi^{\textrm{t}}_{\textrm{s},i}
” (Line
1
).
Fig.
4
F
presents an example safety analysis feedback for Example
III

Liveness analysis
.
Given the counterstrategy
{\mathcal{A}}_{\textrm{e}}
and
the system liveness goals

\varphi^{\textrm{g}}_{\textrm{s}}=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\square\lozenge\varphi^{\textrm{g}}_{\textrm{s},i}
,
we provide feedback on which liveness goals are not satisfied given the new skills.
Algorithm
2
describes our approach.
We first leverage Tarjan’s algorithm
[
27
]
to identify a set of strongly connected sets in
the nodes
\Sigma
of

{\mathcal{A}}_{\textrm{e}}
(Line
2
).
We then only consider the sinking ones, i.e. there is no outgoing edge towards other components, and are not singletons without transitions (Line
2
).
Thus, we identify a sinking strongly connected component that can trap the robot within it once any state of the component is reached.
We then identify the indices of a set of liveness goals
L
satisfied in the component
(Line
2
).
We note that
the inequality comparisons are modulo
n

(where
n\in\mathbb{N}
is the total number of system liveness goals arranged in a cyclic order);
that is,
the condition

\gamma_{\textrm{g}}(\sigma)\leq i<\gamma_{\textrm{g}}(\sigma^{\prime})\mod n

in Line
2

means that

\gamma_{\textrm{g}}(\sigma)\leq i<\gamma_{\textrm{g}}(\sigma^{\prime})

if
\gamma_{\textrm{g}}(\sigma)\leq\gamma_{\textrm{g}}(\sigma^{\prime})
,
and
(\gamma_{\textrm{g}}(\sigma)\leq i\leq n)\lor(1\leq i<\gamma_{\textrm{g}}(\sigma^{\prime}))

if
\gamma_{\textrm{g}}(\sigma)>\gamma_{\textrm{g}}(\sigma^{\prime})
.
Next, we obtain the indices of another set of liveness goals
L^{\prime}
that
the robot attempts to, but cannot, satisfy in the component
(Line
2
).
Lastly, we provide feedback which states that the robot cannot satisfy the liveness goals in
L^{\prime}
after satisfying
L

(Line
2
).

4

Both
the safety and liveness
analyses have computation complexity

O(|\Sigma|^{2})

since they only access each state and transition of the counterstrategy at most a finite number of times.
Our feedback allows
LLMs
to correct their mistakes and generate more promising repair candidates.

In the

In these larger cases,
the baseline fails to complete within the 3000-second time limit.
We also remove the time limit for the

VII
Limitations and Conclusion

We present INPROVF,
a novel hybrid framework that combines
the scalability of
LLMs
and the guarantees of formal methods
to efficiently repair high-level robot controllers under assumption violations at runtime with prohibitively large state space.
Our experiments across various state space sizes
indicate that our approach outperforms the formal methods-based approach in settings with large state spaces.

Nonetheless,
our evaluation faces several limitations.
First,
since there is no standard benchmark for runtime repair,
our evaluation instances are limited.
In future work,
we plan to leverage
LLMs
to procedurally generate a diverse range of repair instances across various abstraction types for a more comprehensive evaluation.
Moreover,
the formal-methods-based repair executes on CPUs, and its implementation has not been optimized,
while the LLM components of INPROVF run on GPUs and benefit from extensive engineering optimizations.
Thus, a direct runtime comparison may not be fair.
Future work will focus on
developing
evaluation metrics that better account for the hardware disparities.
Finally,
we currently
assess the quality of informalization indirectly
through the final repair correctness,
which is neither sound nor complete.
Future work will
explore automatic evaluation methods that directly measure
the quality of informalization.
Additionally,
we will work on
further
improving the scalability of formal methods components within our framework,
such as realizability check.
