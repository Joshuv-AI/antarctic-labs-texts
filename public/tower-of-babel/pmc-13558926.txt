Complete non-anastomotic rupture of an axillobifemoral PTFE bypass following blunt chest trauma: A case report
Authors: Nour Kassab, Alev Gümüs, Livio Solari, Françoise Lefebvre, Grégory Callebaut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-anastomotic disruption of an axillofemoral bypass graft is an exceedingly rare but potentially life-threatening complication. Most reported cases present with a delayed pseudoaneurysm, whereas acute complete graft disruption with active hemorrhage is exceptional.Case presentationA man in his late 50s with a history of a left axillobifemoral PTFE bypass performed in 2022 for chronic aorto-iliac occlusive disease presented to the emergency department with acute left thoracic and upper-limb pain and a rapidly developing pulsatile thoracic swelling following blunt chest trauma against the edge of a table. Computed tomography angiography demonstrated a large left anterior chest-wall hematoma communicating with the axillobifemoral graft and associated active bleeding. Emergency surgical exploration was undertaken. A complete transverse rupture of the graft was identified in the non-anastomotic portion, with no disruption of either the proximal or distal arterial anastomosis. The graft was repaired by direct end-to-end prosthesis-to-prosthesis anastomosis. The postoperative course was uneventful with preserved graft patency and good clinical outcome. A distal clavicular fracture was subsequently identified.ConclusionComplete non-anastomotic rupture of an axillobifemoral PTFE graft following blunt trauma is extremely uncommon but should be considered in patients with a previous axillofemoral bypass who develop acute chest-wall pain, swelling, or hemorrhage after trauma. Prompt CTA and emergency surgical management are essential because delayed diagnosis may result in life-threatening hemorrhage.

Introduction

Axillofemoral bypass is an established extra-anatomic revascularization procedure for patients with aorto-iliac occlusive disease who are considered unsuitable for direct aortic reconstruction because of hostile abdominal anatomy, severe comorbidity, or high operative risk.

The most frequently encountered complications include graft thrombosis and infection. Anastomotic disruption is a recognized complication, whereas disruption occurring in the non-anastomotic portion of the graft is exceedingly rare. 1

A nonsystematic literature review published in 2017 identified 14 cases of non-anastomotic disruption reported between 1963 and 2016. 2 These cases were more frequently associated with unifemoral grafts, ringed PTFE prostheses, blunt trauma, and a rupture located at the costal level, particularly on the left side. A subsequent report in 2019 identified 17 cases and emphasized the role of external forces in the disruption of ePTFE axillofemoral grafts. 3

Most previously described patients presented with a pseudoaneurysm developing over a longer period. In contrast, we report an acute, complete, transverse, non-anastomotic disruption of an axillobifemoral PTFE graft following blunt chest trauma, associated with active bleeding and a large chest-wall hematoma.

The reporting of this case conforms to the CARE guidelines.

We have de-identified all patient details.

Case presentation

A man in his late 50s was admitted to the emergency department of Iris South Hospital J. Bracops in Brussels in September 2024 with acute severe pain involving the left hemithorax and left upper limb, associated with a rapidly developing thoracic swelling.

The symptoms had developed less than two hours after blunt trauma to the left anterior chest. The patient reported striking his chest against the edge of a table.

His medical history included hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, coronary artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and previous vascular interventions. He was an active smoker. In 2022, he had undergone implantation of a left axillobifemoral bypass using an 8-mm ringed PTFE prosthesis measuring approximately 75 cm × 40 cm × 15 cm, in the context of chronic aorto-iliac occlusive disease causing Rutherford stage IIB intermittent claudication.

At the time of presentation, the patient was receiving dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and clopidogrel. The indication for continued dual antiplatelet therapy was made by his cardiologist for coronary artery disease.

On physical examination, a large pulsatile swelling was noted over the left anterior thoracic wall. The patient was hemodynamically stable. The left upper limb was ischemic, and distal arterial pulses were not present.

An emergency CT angiography was performed. It demonstrated a large hematoma extending along the anterior left thoracic wall and communicating with the course of the axillobifemoral bypass. A preoperative chest radiography was not obtained because of the clinically evident active arterial hemorrhage and the need for immediate operative management.

Emergency surgical exploration was therefore indicated.

Surgical treatment

The previous left pectoral incision was reopened. Upon entering the hematoma, abundant arterial bleeding was encountered.

Proximal control of the prosthesis was obtained first, followed by distal control. After control of the hemorrhage, careful inspection demonstrated that neither the proximal nor distal prosthesis-to-artery anastomosis was disrupted.

The damaged segment was inspected and the prosthetic edges were prepared. Given the short distance between the two viable graft ends and the absence of infection or significant tissue loss, a direct end-to-end prosthesis-to-prosthesis anastomosis was performed using two half-running sutures of Surgipro 6-0.

Following completion of the anastomosis, graft patency and hemostasis were verified. An aspirative drain was positioned, and the wound was closed in anatomical layers.

The intraoperative blood loss was 500 cc and the patient needed two red blood cells transfusions. The operative time was 70 minutes and there was no additional procedure.

Postoperative course

The postoperative course was uneventful.

The patient remained hemodynamically stable and the left upper limb remained well perfused. All the pulses were present.

A postoperative chest radiograph demonstrated a probable distal clavicular fracture with a small bony avulsion at the level of the inferior glenoid tubercle (Figure 3).Figure 3.A postoperative chest radiograph demonstrated a probable distal clavicular fracture with a small bony avulsion at the level of the inferior glenoid tubercle.

The patient was discharged after four days with dual antiplatelet therapy.

At six months follow-up, the patient was asymptomatic, with a patent axillobifemoral bypass. A duplex ultrasound was performed and showed good patency of the prosthesis.

Discussion

Non-anastomotic disruption of an axillofemoral bypass graft is an exceptionally uncommon complication. The most comprehensive review available before the publication of subsequent cases identified 14 cases reported over a 53-year period. The authors found a predominance of unifemoral bypasses, ringed PTFE grafts, blunt trauma, and rupture at the costal level, particularly on the left side. The most frequent presentation was a false aneurysm, occurring in 10 of the 14 cases.

A subsequent case report of complete ePTFE axillobifemoral graft disruption following shoulder dislocation summarized 17 previously reported non-anastomotic disruptions. In that case, examination of the explanted graft demonstrated rupture at the level of an external ring, suggesting that repetitive mechanical microtrauma associated with the ring, graft pulsatility, and thoracic movement may contribute to graft failure. 3

The mechanisms responsible for non-anastomotic graft disruption are probably multifactorial. Blunt external trauma is one of the most frequently described mechanisms. Kitowski and Gundersen reported traumatic disruption of a ringed PTFE axillofemoral graft following a fall, presenting with a painful chest-wall mass and large hematoma. Surgical treatment consisted of graft segment resection and interposition PTFE grafting. 5

Other mechanisms include chronic abrasion, external compression, infection, excessive traction or tension, and structural deterioration of the prosthetic material. In particular, external rings may theoretically generate repetitive local mechanical stress. 6

An additional mechanism should also be considered. Following implantation, synthetic vascular grafts become progressively incorporated into the surrounding tissues through a secondary fibrous reaction. This fibrosis may immobilize the graft and potentially contribute to progressive rigidity and local mechanical stress. In combination with repeated arterial pulsation, respiratory movements and external mechanical forces, these changes may predispose an implanted prosthesis to late structural failure. 7

The clinical presentation varies according to the extent and mechanism of graft disruption. Partial graft disruption may lead to progressive bleeding contained by the surrounding tissues, producing a pseudoaneurysm. This was the presentation in many previously reported cases. Imaging, particularly CT angiography, is therefore central to establishing the diagnosis. 8

Endovascular treatment with a covered stent has been described for selected partial disruptions and pseudoaneurysms. However, complete graft transection with active bleeding represents a different clinical situation. In such cases, immediate surgical control of the graft is generally required, followed by either interposition grafting or, in selected circumstances, direct prosthetic anastomosis.

Our case is unusual for several reasons. First, the graft was completely and transversely disrupted in a non-anastomotic segment. Second, the presentation was extremely acute, occurring less than two hours after a clearly identified blunt chest trauma. Third, there was active arterial bleeding resulting in a large thoracic hematoma rather than a delayed contained pseudoaneurysm. Fourth, the graft was successfully repaired by direct end-to-end prosthetic anastomosis rather than segmental replacement.

The associated clavicular fracture provides an additional element supporting the significant mechanical force transmitted to the chest and shoulder region. The trauma may therefore have produced a combination of direct compression and stretching of the graft against the underlying bony structures.

The mechanism is particularly relevant because axillofemoral grafts are positioned superficially along the thoracic wall and may therefore be exposed to repetitive mechanical forces. This case also highlights the importance of considering graft disruption in the differential diagnosis of an acute thoracic swelling in a patient with a previous axillofemoral bypass. Although pseudoaneurysm is more commonly described, a complete graft rupture can produce major arterial hemorrhage within a very short period.

The diagnosis should therefore be suspected early, and CT angiography is particularly useful when the patient’s hemodynamic status permits imaging. However, in the presence of hemodynamic instability or rapidly expanding hemorrhage, surgical exploration and proximal and distal graft control should not be unnecessarily delayed.

Conclusion

Complete non-anastomotic rupture of an axillobifemoral PTFE graft following blunt trauma is an extremely rare but potentially life-threatening complication.

The diagnosis should be considered in any patient with a previous axillofemoral bypass who presents with acute chest pain, thoracic swelling, a pulsatile mass or unexplained hemorrhage following trauma, even several years after graft implantation.

CT angiography is the preferred diagnostic examination in hemodynamically stable patients and can rapidly demonstrate graft disruption, active bleeding and associated injuries.

In cases of complete graft transection with active hemorrhage, urgent surgical control is mandatory. Depending on the local anatomy and integrity of the graft ends, direct prosthesis-to-prosthesis anastomosis may represent a simple and effective alternative to graft interposition.

This case emphasizes the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for late structural graft failure after blunt trauma and highlights the potentially catastrophic consequences of delayed diagnosis.
