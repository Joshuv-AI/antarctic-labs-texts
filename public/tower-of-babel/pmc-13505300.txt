Synthesis, characterization, docking and in vitro evaluation of new pyrazole-carboxylate derivatives as antibacterial, anticancer and anti-inflammatory agents
Authors: Rachel Alveera Menezes, Navas Shereef Ellyan, Mallikarjun M, K. Nagaraj Pai, Angel Treasa Alex, Ananda Kulal, P. K. Vasudeva, K. Subrahmanya Bhat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pyrazoles and chalcones have been extensively studied over time due to their broad range of therapeutic potentials. In this study, a new series of pyrazole-carboxylate derivatives were synthesized, characterized, and evaluated for their antibacterial, anticancer and anti-inflammatory activities. Among the synthesized derivatives, compound 5a exhibited significant percentage inhibition in colony counting assay. Compound 5c demonstrated significant cytotoxic activity with an IC50 value of 9.91 µg mL−1, while also exhibiting lower cytotoxicity towards non-cancerous HEK-293T cells (IC50 = 36.31 µg mL−1), indicating favourable selectivity. Mechanistic studies, including DAPI staining and flow cytometric analysis, indicated that compounds 5c and 5f inhibited cancer cell growth predominantly by inducing apoptosis rather than cell cycle arrest. Anti-inflammatory activity was determined using protein denaturation assay where compound 5f demonstrated promising activity with an IC50 value of 59.37 ± 0.149 µg mL−1. Furthermore, molecular docking analysis further provided insights into the binding interactions of the derivatives and the targeted protein. In addition, drug-likeness evaluation using swissADME indicates that the compounds satisfied Lipinski's rule of five.

Introduction

Heterocyclic moieties play a crucial role in imparting diverse properties, as they are widely found in natural products, renewable sources and drugs.1 Heterocyclic compounds are cyclic organic compounds that include at least one heteroatom, most commonly nitrogen, oxygen, or sulfur.2 Among them, N-containing heterocycles are ubiquitous in dyes, drugs and high-performance materials.3

Pyrazole, being one of the most studied compounds in the azole family,4 is a five-membered cyclic organic compound consisting of two nitrogen atoms adjacent to each other. As it possesses a planar structure with nitrogen atoms being surrounded by considerable electron density, this moiety readily participates in various chemical reactions.5 Recently, the synthesis of pyrazole derivatives has witnessed the development of diverse synthetic methodologies, including transition metal catalyzed C–H activation6 and sustainable electrochemical approaches,7 thus broadening the scope of pyrazole synthesis. Furthermore, pyrazole forms the core structure of several drugs which are used to treat various diseases including cutaneous, infectious, respiratory, neurological, cerebrovascular, cardiovascular and endocrine diseases, and tumor and metabolic disorders.8 Chalcones, known for their α–β unsaturated frameworks, hold significant importance in the field of medicinal chemistry due to their wide range of pharmaceutical activities.9 Furthermore, when the nitrogen heterocycles are paired with the chalcone scaffold, these structures can enhance the pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetic characteristics of therapeutics.10

Antimicrobial resistance has emerged as a major global concern, as microorganisms can mutate and develop resistance to existing drugs. Therefore, it is essential to develop and evaluate the new drugs to overcome these challenges.11 Two major health challenges faced worldwide are inflammation and cancer, which impact millions of people.12 Cancer is one of the major causes of death worldwide, creating an urgent need for the development of new and more effective therapies.13 On the other hand, inflammation stands to be a natural immune response that protects tissue from injury, infection or disease by involving leukocytes to destroy harmful agents and promote healing.14 Therefore, the development of low-toxic, safe and effective antibacterial, anticancer and anti-inflammatory drugs has been a major focus.

Several researchers have reported pyrazole-chalcone derivatives with promising biological activities. For instance, Norhaan et al., evaluated pyrazole-based chalcones for their anticancer activity against MCF-7, PC3, PACA2 human cancer cell lines, along with the normal BJ1 cell line.15 Monica et al. investigated the antimicrobial and anticancer activities of pyrazolyl-chalcone derivatives,16 while Asmaa et al., reported the anti-inflammatory and anticancer potential of pyrazole-chalcone derivatives.17 Collectively, these studies demonstrate that the hybridization of pyrazole and chalcone scaffolds is a promising strategy for the development of biological active molecules. However, pyrazole-chalcone hybrids held together by an ester linkage remain relatively unexplored. Motivated by these findings, we synthesized and characterized a new series of pyrazole-carboxylate derivatives. The synthesized compounds were subsequently evaluated for their antibacterial, anticancer and anti-inflammatory activities. Furthermore, molecular docking and swissADME were used to investigate their binding interactions and drug-like properties.

The rationale behind the present work was to maintain the pyrazole nucleus as the central scaffold, while incorporating the chalcone framework via a carboxylate linkage to achieve potential biological activity. The various drugs consisting of pyrazole and chalcone core is highlighted in Fig. 1.

Now, by integrating these two pharmacophores as a single compound, the resulting hybrid would combine the biological advantages of both scaffolds. Furthermore, variations of the substituents in the aromatic ring may also influence biological activity. Thus, this hybridization approach of pyrazole-carboxylate derivatives was expected to enhance the activity.

Experimental section

Chemistry

All chemical and solvents used for synthesis were of analytical grade, obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and other commercial suppliers, and were utilized without further purification. The rection progress was monitored by TLC (thin-layer chromatography), with the spots detected under UV light. Theil's tube was used to record the melting points of compounds synthesized. The IR spectrum was recorded using Shimadzu FTIR spectrometer. 1H NMR (in DMSO-d6) and 13C NMR (in DMSO-d6) spectra of all the synthesized compounds were obtained on a Bruker AM 400 MHz NMR spectrometer. Electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) was acquired using an LC-MS system equipped with a PDA and SQ detector (Waters).

Procedure for the preparation of chalcones

The required chalcones were synthesized using Claisen–Schmidt condensation reaction. A solution 4-hydroxy acetophenone (5 mmol) in ethanol was treated with aqueous KOH (40%), and the reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature. Various aromatic aldehydes (5 mmol) were then added to the reaction mixture, and the stirring was continued for 24 h. The reaction progress was monitored using thin-layer chromatography (TLC). Upon completion, the mixture was poured into ice-cold water and acidified to pH 3 using 4N HCl. The resulting precipitate was filtered, washed with water, and recrystallized from ethanol to afford the desired chalcones.18

Procedure for the synthesis for pyrazole-carboxylate compounds

A mixture of 4-hydroxy chalcone (2.5 mmol), 5-methyl-1H-pyrazole-3-carboxylic acid (2.5 mmol), N,N′-dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCC) (2.75 mmol) and DMAP (4-dimethylaminopyridine) (catalytic amount) in DMF was stirred at room temperature under a nitrogen atmosphere for 4 h. The progress of the reaction was monitored by TLC using ethyl acetate: toluene as the solvent system. After completion of the reaction, the white precipitate of dicyclohexyl urea formed during the reaction was removed by filtration. The filtrate was subsequently added to ice cold water to obtain the crude product. The crude product was purified by column chromatography over silica gel (60–120 mesh) using 30% ethyl acetate-toluene as the eluent to afford the targeted compounds.

Antibacterial activity

Disk-diffusion method

Initially, disc diffusion method was employed to evaluate the antibacterial activity of the synthesized compounds. The bacterial strain utilized in the study was Escherichia coli MTCC 1687. Nutrient agar, Petri plates, nutrient broth were subjected to autoclave sterilization at 121 °C and 15 lb pressure for 15–20 minutes. The sterilized molten nutrient agar (30 mL) was aseptically transferred into the Petri plates and were solidified under the laminar airflow cabinet. After solidification, 100 µL of the microbial suspension (100 µL of 0.5 McFarland diluted in 10 mL sterile water) was evenly spread over the agar surface. Sterile discs were placed on the inoculated plates, and 40 µL of the sample solution (5 mg mL−1) was loaded onto each disc. Antibacterial drug cephalothine (30 mcg disc) was used as a standard. All the plates were incubated at 37 °C overnight and observed for the zone of inhibition.

Colony counting method

In this technique, the nutrient broth, nutrient agar and the Petri plates were sterilized in an autoclave at 121 °C and 15 lbs pressure for 15–20 minutes. Inside the laminar air flow (LAF), approximately 30 mL of the sterilised molten nutrient agar was transferred to the Petri plates and was allowed to solidify. The bacterial strains used for the study were Escherichia coli MTCC 1687, Staphylococcus aureus MTCC 3160 and Mycobacterium smegmatis MTCC 944. Freshly grown bacterial cultures were diluted to 102- fold using sterile nutrient broth to obtain a 0.5 McFarland turbidity standard. In a 96-well plate, 50 µL of the test samples (5 mg mL−1), 50 µL of the diluted microbial suspension (prepared by mixing 50 µL of 0.5 McFarland culture with 950 µL of sterilized Milli-Q water) and 100 µL of nutrient broth were added to the each well, resulting in a final volume of 200 µL per well. Wells A1–A9 contained the strain E. coli, B1–B9 contained S. aureus, and C1–C9 contained M. smegmatis. Column 1–7 contained the test samples along with the microbial suspension and nutrient broth. Column 8 served as the microbial control (containing microbial suspension and nutrient broth without test sample), and column 9 contained the antibacterial standard, ciprofloxacin (5 mg mL−1). This plate was incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. Following incubation, 1 µL of the incubated sample mixture from each well was diluted to 1 mL with sterilized milli Q water in a vial and gently mixed. The vials were appropriately labelled according to the microbial strain. From each vial, 100 µL of the diluted suspension was spread evenly onto the agar plates, which were then incubated overnight at 37 °C for colony formation. The number of colonies in each plate were counted using colony counting method.19,20

Anticancer activity

Cytotoxicity against MCF-7 breast cancer cells

The MCF-7 cells (20 000 per well) were seeded in 96-well plates using 200 µL of complete culture medium in the absence of test samples. The plates were incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 approximately for 24 h before further treatment. The cells were then treated with appropriate concentrations (1.5, 3.75, 7.5, 15, 30, 31.25, 62.5, 125, 250, 500 µg mL−1) of the test compounds 5c and 5f, respectively. The plates were further incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2 atmosphere. Following the incubation period, the plates were removed from the incubator, and for adherent cells, the spent culture medium was carefully aspirated. Subsequently, MTT reagent was introduced into each well to achieve a final concentration of 0.5 mg mL−1, after which plate was incubated at 37 °C for 3 h. After incubation, 100 µg mL−1 of DMSO was added to each well, and the plate was gently shaken to facilitate complete solubilization of the formazan crystals. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using the spectrophotometer.21,22

Cytotoxicity against non-cancerous HEK-293T cells

The cytotoxicity of compounds 5f and 5c toward non-cancerous cells was evaluated using the MTT assay in HEK-293T (human embryonic kidney) cells. Doxorubicin was included as the standard cytotoxic agent, while DMSO-treated cells served as the vehicle control. HEK-293T cells were seeded in triplicate into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well in 100 µL of complete culture medium and incubated in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 for 24 h or until approximately 70% confluence was achieved. Upon incubation, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing DMSO (control) or various concentrations of 5f, 5c, or doxorubicin, and the cells were incubated for an additional 72 h. At the end of the treatment period, the medium was removed, and the cells were gently washed with fresh medium. Subsequently, 50 µL of MTT solution (2 mg mL−1 in PBS) and 50 µL of serum-free medium were added to each well, followed by incubation for 3 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2.23,24 The MTT-containing medium was then carefully discarded, and 100 µL of DMSO was added to each well to dissolve the formazan crystals. The plates were subjected to gentle agitation and were incubated in dark for 20 min to ensure complete solubilization. A microplate reader was used to measure the absorbance at 540 nm. The percentage of cell growth inhibition was calculated using the following equation:% Growth inhibition = [1 − (OD of treated cells/OD of control cells)] × 100

IC50 values for 5f, 5c, and doxorubicin were determined by plotting the mean percentage growth inhibition across triplicate wells against the corresponding concentrations, and then fitting the data with nonlinear regression.24–26

Mechanistic studies

Apoptosis by DAPI staining

MCF-7 cells were seeded at a density of 2.5 × 104 cells on sterile 13 mm round coverslips placed in culture plates and incubated for 24 h to allow cell attachment. The cells were then treated with compounds 5f and 5c and doxorubicin (standard) at their respective IC50 concentrations, while DMSO-treated cells served as the vehicle control. Following treatment, the cells were incubated for an additional 48 h under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). After incubation, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were gently washed three times with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The cells were then stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1 µg mL−1) for nuclear visualization, mounted on glass slides using a mounting medium, and examined under a fluorescence microscope. Representative fluorescence images were captured and analyzed using Fiji (ImageJ) software. Apoptotic cells were identified by the presence of brightly fluorescent condensed and fragmented nuclei resulting from chromatin condensation and karyorrhexis, and quantitative analysis was performed by determining the percentage of highly fluorescent nuclear fragments with an area <30% of the reference intact nucleus size.27

Cell cycle analysis

MCF-7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells/well and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 for 24 h to allow cell attachment. The cells were then treated with compounds 5f and 5c and doxorubicin (standard) at their respective IC50 concentrations, while DMSO-treated cells served as the vehicle control. Following treatment, the cells were incubated for an additional 48 h under standard culture conditions. After treatment, both floating and adherent cells were collected to ensure complete population analysis. Adherent cells were detached using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA, pooled with the floating cells, and washed twice with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The cell pellet was resuspended in cold PBS, and ice-cold 70% ethanol was added dropwise with gentle vortexing to prevent cell aggregation. The cells were fixed at 4 °C for at least 2 h. Following fixation, the cells were washed twice with PBS to remove residual ethanol and resuspended in a staining solution containing propidium iodide (PI, 50 µg mL−1) and RNase A (100 µg mL−1). The samples were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min to allow uniform DNA staining. Subsequently, the stained cells were analyzed by flow cytometry, and 10 000 events were acquired for each sample. Cell-cycle distribution was determined by measuring cellular DNA content. Debris and cell aggregates were excluded using forward scatter (FSC), side scatter (SSC), and pulse geometry gating. The percentages of cells in the Sub-G1, G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases were quantified using FlowJo software, and representative DNA-content histograms were generated. The Sub-G1 population was considered indicative of apoptotic cells with fragmented DNA.28–32

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was employed to evaluate the binding interactions of the pyrazole-carboxylate compounds with the selected protein targets. Molecular docking is a computational approach to predict and identify the binding interactions between the targeted protein and the ligands. Docking studies were conducted using Maestro molecular modelling interphase (Schrodinger). The crystal structure of the proteins (PDB ID No.: 5BNS, 3W2S) was obtained from the RCSB protein data bank and was prepared with the protein preparation wizard through optimization, minimization and removal of water molecules. The ligand structures were drawn initially using Maestro 2D sketcher and were then optimized using the LigPrep module. Following grid generation, the docking was performed between the prepared protein and the prepared ligand. Furthermore, the molecular docking study was validated by performing a redocking of the co-crystallized ligand (PDB ID No: 5BNS, 3W2S) to evaluate the accuracy of docking study. After the protein preparation, the co-crystallized ligand was selected using the split option. The ligands selected were subsequently docked with the same docking parameters that were applied to test compounds. Furthermore, the redocked co-crystallized ligand was then superimposed onto the co-crystallized ligand using the superposition tool in Maestro and the root mean square deviation (RMSD) was calculated. An RMSD value usually 1.5 or 2 Å is considered acceptable as evidence of reliable docking.35 The binding free energy of the docked protein–ligand complexes were calculated using the Prime MM-GBSA method. This analysis was to calculate the binding energy of the docked compounds. More negative binding free energy values indicate the formation of stronger complexes with the target enzyme.

Drug-likeness studies

The physiochemical studies were carried out with an aid of swissADME, which is a free web tool.36 This was initially used to ensure the compounds synthesised follow Lipinski's rules five associated with the molecular weight, molar refractivity index, partition coefficient (log P), hydrogen bond donors and hydrogen bond acceptors.

Results and discussion

Chemistry

The synthetic strategies utilized to synthesize the series of seven targeted compounds are outlined in Scheme 1. Initially, compound 3 was synthesized via Claisen–Schmidt condensation where various aromatic aldehydes and 4-hydroxyacetophenone 2 were condensed in 40% ethanolic potassium hydroxide solution.

Synthesis of pyrazole-carboxylate derivatives.

Subsequently, the synthesized chalcones were coupled with the compound 3-methyl-1H-pyrazole-5-carboxylic acid in the presence of N,N′-dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCC) and catalytic amount of 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP) under a nitrogen atmosphere. The resulting products were purified by column chromatography. Structural analyses of all the target compounds were carried using FT-IR, 1H NMR, 13C NMR and mass spectrometry.

Pyrazole-carboxylate compounds were synthesized and characterized by various spectroscopic techniques. The FT-IR spectra of the synthesized compounds showed absorption range in the range of 3411–3258 cm−1 due to –NH stretching. Characteristic absorption bands for the ester carbonyl (CO) group were observed in the range of 1725–1754 cm−1. The presence of conjugated carbonyl stretch was observed at 1649–1667 cm−1 corresponding to the chalcone group. Furthermore, two distinctive signals were observed for nitro group at 1518 cm−1 and 1420 cm−1 which attributed to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching respectively.

In 1H NMR spectra, the NH proton appeared as singlet in the range of 13.46–13.52 ppm. Signals corresponding to the aromatic protons resonated at 8.22–7.03 ppm. The proton corresponding to the pyrazole C–H moiety appeared as a singlet at 6.72 ppm. The doublet of doublets observed in the region 8.01–6.75 ppm, attributed to the conjugated CHCH protons, thus confirmed the formation of chalcone moiety. In compound 5e, the methoxy (OCH3) protons resonated at 3.87 and 3.83 ppm, whereas in compound 5g, the methyl protons appeared at 3.02 ppm.

The synthesized compounds exhibited the following peaks in 13C NMR spectra. The carbonyl carbon of the ketone group appeared at 188.00–188.51 ppm, while the carbonyl carbon of the ester group resonated in the range of 154.80–165.17 ppm. Signals observed between 160.79–111.23 ppm were attributed to the aromatic carbons. The olefinic carbons of (CHCH) appeared at 119.92–122.92 ppm and 142.31–145.83 ppm. The β-carbon adjacent to the N–H group resonated at 107 ppm. The methyl carbon attached to the pyrazole ring showed a signal at 10.71 ppm. Carbon (C–F) atom in compound 5f, resonated at 162.70 ppm.

In ESI-MS spectra of the compounds, M+ and M+1 peaks are shown, and are base peaks, in cases otherwise the fragment is assigned corresponding to the base peak.

Antibacterial activity

The synthesized compounds were screened preliminarily for the antibacterial activity against the strain E. coli by employing the disc diffusion technique. There was no significant zone of inhibition observed for any of the samples. However, no bacterial growth was observed below the discs, except in the case of the standard. This indicates that the synthesized compounds may exhibit bacteriostatic rather than bactericidal effects and may not diffuse effectively in the aqueous medium.

To further investigate the antibacterial properties of the synthesized compounds, the colony-counting method was employed. As expected, no inhibition was observed in the control group, while maximum inhibition was anticipated for the standard.

Among the synthesized compounds, compound 5a exhibited the inhibitory activity against all tested strains. Notably, excellent inhibition activity was observed against E. coli, S. aureus, and M. smegmatis, where almost no colonies were detectable under the experimental conditions. Compound 5g also demonstrated significant inhibitory activity against S. aureus (100%), M. Smegmatis (79.53%); however, it showed no inhibitory effect against E. coli.

With respect to E. coli, apart from compound 5a, only compounds 5d and 5e exhibited inhibition, with percentage inhibitions of 6.12% and 13.6% respectively. Among all the synthesized compounds, compound 5a (100%), 5g (100%) and 5e (78.53%) showed strong inhibitory activity against S. aureus. Interestingly, M. Smegmatis was the only bacterial strain which was inhibited by all the compounds. Although compound 5f did not exhibit significant antibacterial activity against other strains, it effectively resisted the growth of M. smegmatis, with a percentage inhibition of 73.64% indicating good activity against this strain. Anticancer activity

Cytotoxicity against MCF-7 breast cancer cells

As pyrazoles are well known for their anticancer activity, the synthesized compounds were expected to exhibit the antiproliferative properties. Based on this rationale, two halogen-substituted compounds, 5c and 5f, were selected for cytotoxicity evaluation since chlorine and fluorine containing derivatives are found to be frequently associated with enhanced pharmacological activity and are widely recognized in designing the bioactive molecules. Therefore, we anticipated that the compounds 5c and 5f would enhance the activity, so we chose these two compounds. Moreover, these compounds were available in sufficient purity and quantity for biological evaluation. The cytotoxicity of both compounds on MCF-7 breast cancer cells was assessed using the MTT assay, with untreated cells considered as 100% viable.

Both compounds exhibited strong cytotoxicity on MCF-7 cells, as evidenced by a marked reduction in cell viability. Increasing compound concentrations resulted in a reduction in cell viability, indicating pronounced antiproliferative potential of the synthesized compounds. Compound 5c showed a gradual reduction in cell viability, with mean values of 30.13 ± 0.07, 24.89 ± 0.04, 20.13 ± 0.03, 16.21 ± 0.14, 13.07 ± 0.03% for the concentration 31.25, 62.5, 125, 250, 500 µg mL−1 respectively. Fig. 5 represents the concentration – dependent cytotoxic effect of compound 5c on MCF-7 cells. To further evaluate its potency, the compound was evaluated at lower concentrations (1.5–30 µg mL−1). Cell viability decreased from 97.17% at 1.5 to 18.09% at 30 µg mL−1. The IC50 value was calculated to be 9.91 µg mL−1, indicating strong antiproliferative activity.

MTT assay showing the cytotoxic effect of compound 5c with increasing concentrations of untreated, doxorubicin (standard) and 5c on MCF-7 cells.

Similarly, compound 5f demonstrated significant cytotoxicity on MCF-7 cells, following a comparable concentration-dependent trend. Cell viability values of 20.05 ± 0.01%, 18.18 ± 0.09%, 16.08 ± 0.03%, 14.07 ± 0.04%, 12.36 ± 0.17% were observed for the concentrations of 31.25, 62.5, 125, 250, 500 µg mL−1 respectively. Evaluating the compound's potency at lower concentration, cell viability decreased from 96.55% at 1.5 µg mL−1 to 37.73% at 30 µg mL−1. The IC50 value exhibited by this compound was 17.15 µg mL−1. Cytotoxicity against non-cancerous HEK-293T cells

The cytotoxicity of compounds 5f and 5c was evaluated in non-cancerous HEK-293T cells using the MTT assay to assess their safety toward normal cells (Fig. 7). Doxorubicin, a clinically used chemotherapeutic agent with known toxicity to normal tissues, served as the reference standard.

Cytotoxic effect of 5f and 5c against Doxorubicin on HEK-293T cells. Percentage inhibition of cell viability was determined by MTT assay following 72 hours of treatment with increasing concentrations of doxorubicin, 5f, and 5c. Data are expressed as mean ± SEM (n = 3). IC50 values were calculated by nonlinear regression: doxorubicin, 2.51 µg mL; 5f, 11.22 µg mL; 5c, 36.31 µg mL−1.

Both compounds showed a concentration-dependent increase in growth inhibition of HEK-293T cells (Fig. 7). Doxorubicin exhibited the greatest cytotoxicity, with an IC50 of 2.51 µg mL−1 (log IC50 = 0.40). In comparison, 5f was less toxic, with an IC50 of 11.22 µg mL−1 (log IC50 = 1.05), which was approximately 4.5-fold higher than that of doxorubicin. 5c was the least cytotoxic, with an IC50 of 36.31 µg mL−1 (log IC50 = 1.56), approximately 14.5-fold higher than doxorubicin and 3.2-fold higher than 5f.

Therefore, the cytotoxicity toward HEK-293T cells followed the order: doxorubicin > 5f > 5c, indicating that both synthesized compounds were considerably better tolerated by normal cells than doxorubicin, with 5c showing the most favourable safety profile.

Both 5f and 5c exhibited substantially lower cytotoxicity toward HEK-293T cells than doxorubicin, with IC50 values 4.5- and 14.5-fold higher, respectively. Together with their previously demonstrated anticancer activity on MCF-7, these findings suggest that their cytotoxic effects are not due to general cellular toxicity but rather reflect a degree of selectivity for cancer cells.

Among the two compounds, 5c showed the most favourable therapeutic window, with an IC50 nearly an order of magnitude higher than that of doxorubicin, suggesting improved selectivity that may be attributed to the presence of chloro substituent, which emerged as a favourable structural feature for enhancing the anticancer activity. Although 5f, consisting of fluoro group was more cytotoxic than 5c, it also showed a substantially wider safety margin than doxorubicin, supporting its continued evaluation.

Effect of 5f and 5c on apoptosis in MCF-7 cells

Apoptosis is characterized by distinct nuclear morphological changes, including chromatin condensation, nuclear shrinkage, and fragmentation, all of which can be readily visualized by DAPI staining. DAPI-stained nuclei were examined for characteristic apoptotic morphological changes by quantifying highly fluorescent nuclear fragments resulting from karyorrhexis (Fig. 8). The untreated (control) cells exhibited the lowest proportion of small or fragmented nuclei (25.1% ± 1.5%), consistent with a predominantly viable cell population displaying intact, uniformly stained nuclei.

The untreated control exhibits predominantly intact nuclei with low fluorescence intensity, whereas treatment with 5c, 5f, and doxorubicin results in a progressive increase in the number and fluorescence intensity of condensed and fragmented nuclei, indicative of chromatin condensation and karyorrhexis during apoptosis. (B) Quantitative analysis of apoptotic nuclei, expressed as the percentage of highly fluorescent condensed/fragmented nuclear bodies (area <30% of the reference intact nucleus size). Treatment with 5c, 5f, and doxorubicin produced a progressive increase in the proportion of condensed and fragmented nuclei, reaching 35.3% ± 2.6%, 40.5% ± 2.7%, and 46.7% ± 1.4%, respectively. These values correspond to 1.4-, 1.6-, and 1.9-fold increases over the untreated control, indicating a treatment-dependent increase in apoptotic nuclear damage. The extent of nuclear fragmentation followed the order doxorubicin > 5f > 5c > control, which closely parallels the cytotoxicity profile observed in the MTT assay. Doxorubicin produced the highest degree of apoptotic nuclear alterations, whereas compounds 5f and 5c induced intermediate levels of apoptosis, with 5f exhibiting a greater apoptotic effect than 5c.

In the present study, both synthesized compounds increased the proportion of condensed and fragmented nuclei compared with the untreated control, demonstrating that their growth-inhibitory effects are associated, at least in part, with the induction of apoptotic cell death. The extent of apoptotic nuclear fragmentation closely mirrored the cytotoxicity observed in the MTT assay, with doxorubicin producing the greatest apoptotic response, followed by the compounds 5c and 5f. This concordance between loss of cell viability and apoptotic morphology suggests that apoptosis is a major mechanism underlying the cytotoxic effects of these compounds.

Effect of 5c and 5f on cell cycle progression in MCF-7

To investigate whether the antiproliferative effects of compounds 5f and 5c were associated with alterations in cell-cycle progression, propidium iodide (PI)-based flow cytometric analysis was performed (Fig. 9 and Table 1). Untreated cells showed a typical cell-cycle distribution, with the majority of cells in the G0/G1 phase (70.67%), followed by the S phase (16.93%) and G2/M phase (7.67%).

The histograms show that treatment with compounds 5c and 5f resulted in only modest alterations in cell-cycle progression compared with the untreated control, whereas doxorubicin induced marked accumulation of cells in the G2/M phase. Treatment with 5c slightly reduced the G0/G1 population to 66.52% while increasing the proportion of cells in the S phase (19.85%), with minimal change in the G2/M population (7.75%). A comparable pattern was observed with 5f, which reduced the G0/G1 fraction to 67.73% and increased the S-phase population to 20.12%, whereas the G2/M fraction remained largely unchanged (7.07%). These findings indicate that both compounds produced only modest perturbations in cell-cycle progression, primarily characterized by a slight accumulation of cells in the S phase.

In contrast, the reference drug doxorubicin caused marked redistribution of cells, with substantial accumulation in the G2/M phase, confirming the assay's sensitivity. Despite inducing only minor changes in cell-cycle distribution, compounds 5f and 5c produced significant cytotoxicity in the MTT assay and increased apoptotic nuclear fragmentation in the DAPI assay, indicating that their antiproliferative effects are not associated with pronounced phase-specific cell-cycle arrest.

The relationship between cell-cycle perturbation and apoptosis provides important insights into the mechanism of action of newly synthesized anticancer compounds. In the present study, compounds 5f and 5c produced only modest alterations in cell-cycle progression, characterized by a slight reduction in the G0/G1 population and a corresponding increase in the S-phase fraction, without significant accumulation of cells at any major checkpoint. These findings suggest that the antiproliferative activity of both compounds is unlikely to be mediated through prolonged arrest at a specific phase of the cell cycle.

Interestingly, this observation contrasts with the marked cytotoxicity observed in the MTT assay and the significant increase in apoptotic nuclear fragmentation detected by DAPI staining. Although neither compound induced pronounced cell-cycle arrest, both significantly increased chromatin condensation and karyorrhexis, indicating that apoptosis represents the predominant mode of cell death. This suggests that the compounds may initiate apoptotic signaling before prolonged checkpoint activation, or trigger apoptosis through mechanisms relatively independent of sustained cell-cycle arrest.

The modest increase in the S-phase population observed with both compounds may indicate replication stress or interference with DNA synthesis, thereby activating intracellular stress responses. Such replication-associated stress can activate the ATR/Chk1 signaling pathway, promote p53-dependent or p53-independent apoptotic signaling, and ultimately culminate in mitochondrial dysfunction and caspase activation rather than persistent cell-cycle blockade.37,38 Alternatively, the compounds may directly perturb mitochondrial integrity, increase intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), or modulate pro- and anti-apoptotic proteins of the Bcl-2 family, thereby initiating the intrinsic apoptotic pathway without causing substantial accumulation at a specific cell-cycle checkpoint.39

Among the two compounds, 5f consistently exhibited greater biological activity than 5c, producing higher cytotoxicity and a greater proportion of apoptotic nuclei in the DAPI assay, despite exhibiting a cell-cycle profile similar to that of 5c. This finding suggests that the superior anticancer activity of 5f is not attributable to stronger cell-cycle arrest but rather to its greater ability to activate apoptotic pathways. Structural differences between 5f and 5c may therefore influence their interactions with intracellular molecular targets involved in apoptotic signaling rather than with proteins that directly regulate cell-cycle progression.

Anti-inflammatory activity

The compound 5c exhibited a gradual and concentration-dependent increase in inhibition of protein denaturation. At 10 µg mL−1, the activity was 6.52%, then it increased to 21.19% at 25 µg mL−1, and further to 33.49% at 50 µg mL−1. At higher concentration, greater effects were observed, reaching 51.41% at 100 µg mL−1 and 66.21% at 200 µg mL−1. The standard drug, diclofenac (100 µg mL−1) showed 79.69% inhibition, demonstrating superior activity compared to the compound 5c at equivalent concentrations. Though the compound 5c displayed moderate efficacy, it did not reach the level of inhibition exhibited by the standard. The IC50 value was 96.81 ± 0.418 µg mL−1, thereby indicating the moderate anti-inflammatory potential.

Compound 5f also demonstrated the clear and concentration-dependent inhibition of protein denaturation. The activity increased from 15.60% at 10 µg mL−1 to 30.76% at 25 µg mL−1, and to 44.06% at 50 µg mL−1. A more pronounced inhibitory effect was observed at higher concentrations, reaching 59.50% at 100 µg mL−1 and 77.72% at 200 µg mL−1, indicating a strong dose-responsive anti-inflammatory potential. The standard drug exhibited 79.96% inhibition, showing slightly higher activity compared to the compound 5f. The IC50 value calculated was 59.37 ± 0.149 µg mL−1, indicating that the 5f derivative possessed good anti-inflammatory activity. Structure activity relationship (SAR) study

Structure–activity relationship (SAR) analysis of the synthesized compounds revealed that the pyrazole scaffold together with the α–β unsaturated carbonyl moiety plays an important role in antibacterial, anticancer and anti-inflammatory activities. As all the synthesized compounds possess these common structural features, the observed variations in biological activity are primarily attributed to the position and the nature of halogen substituents on the phenyl ring. The brief SAR of the synthesized derivatives is represented in Fig. 14.

Brief SAR of the compounds.

Among the synthesized compounds, the dichloro-substituted compound 5a exhibited good antibacterial activity against all the tested microbial strains, suggesting that the presence of chlorine atoms may enhance the antimicrobial activity. Similarly, the monochloro-substituted derivative 5c demonstrated significant cytotoxicity on breast cancer cell line MCF-7, which may be attributed to the higher polarizability and lipophilicity of chlorine.45 In contrast, compound 5f, containing a fluoro substituent, displayed relatively improved anti-inflammatory activity, which may be attributed to the strong electronegativity and lipophilicity of the fluorine atom. However, although increased lipophilicity associated with chlorine substitution may enhance biological activity, it may also promote non-specific off–target interactions, thus contributing towards the toxicity.45 Overall, these findings suggest that halogen substitution on the phenyl ring plays a significant role in influencing biological activities.

These observations were further supported by molecular docking studies. The pyrazole ring facilitated favourable interactions with the target proteins through π–π stacking interactions, while the ester carbonyl group participated in hydrogen bonding with key amino acid residues such as ASN 447 and LYS 745. In addition, aromatic substituents, particularly chloro and nitro containing groups enhanced binding affinity through halogen bonding, π–cation interactions and salt bridge formation with the key amino acid residues. These findings suggest that the incorporation of electron withdrawing groups favours stronger protein-ligand interactions, which may contribute to the enhanced biological activity of the synthesized derivatives. Furthermore, the drug-likeness analysis suggests that the incorporation of appropriate aromatic substituents may contribute to the enhanced biological activity of the synthesized pyrazole-carboxylate derivatives as all derivatives satisfied Lipinski's rule of five.

Conclusion

In conclusion, successful synthesis of seven pyrazole-carboxylate derivatives was achieved which were characterized by infrared (IR) spectroscopy, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), and mass spectrometry. Antibacterial activity of the compounds was carried out using colony counting method against the three bacterial strains. Compound 5a exhibited promising antibacterial activity.

Furthermore, compound 5c exhibited the most potent antiproliferative activity on MCF-7 breast cancer cells (IC50 value 9.91 µg mL−1) while demonstrating lower cytotoxicity toward HEK-293T non-cancerous cells, indicating a favourable selectivity profile. Mechanistic studies, including DAPI staining and flow cytometry results revealed that compounds 5c and 5f primarily inhibit cancer cell growth by inducing apoptosis, with only minor effects on cell-cycle progression. This mechanistic profile suggests that these compounds may act by directly activating apoptotic signalling pathways rather than by inducing sustained checkpoint-mediated arrest. Future studies investigating mitochondrial membrane depolarization, intracellular ROS generation, caspase-3/-9 activation, Annexin V/PI staining, DNA damage markers (γH2AX), and the expression of apoptosis-related proteins (Bax, Bcl-2, cytochrome c, p53, and PARP) will be valuable for defining the molecular basis of their anticancer activity.

Anti-inflammatory activity was evaluated for two compounds 5c and 5f. It was found that compound 5f exhibited good anti-inflammatory activity. It was found that both the compounds demonstrated concentration dependent inhibition. Notably, derivative 5f displayed good activity with IC50 value of 59.37 ± 0.149 µg mL−1. Additionally, molecular docking analysis was performed to investigate the binding interactions between the synthesized compounds and their respective target proteins. Moreover, all the synthesized compounds exhibited favourable drug-like characteristics and compiled with Lipinski's rule of five. Overall, these results enhance our understanding of pyrazole-carboxylate derivatives and open new opportunities for exploring their potential therapeutic roles.
