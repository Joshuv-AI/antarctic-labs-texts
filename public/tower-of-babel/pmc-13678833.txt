Legacy phosphorus as a long‐term water quality challenge in intensive farming systems
Authors: Jian Liu
Published: 2026

My interest in legacy phosphorus (P) originated during my PhD work on understanding the relationship between soil P status, degree of P saturation, and dissolved P leaching (Liu, Aronsson, Ulén, et al., 2012), and on exploring best manure management practices for reducing P leaching in high‐P soils (Liu, Aronsson, Bergström, et al., 2012). However, I did not initially link my work to the legacy P concept until reading Sharpley et al. (2013), who articulated a clear definition of the term. In my perspective, legacy P, in the context of water quality management, is P resulting from past land management practices, especially nutrient applications. This P exists in surplus relative to crop demand and poses a long‐term risk of P loss to water.

A major driver of legacy P is the overuse of manure in intensive livestock production regions. In such regions, manure is often regarded by farmers as a waste product requiring disposal rather than as a valuable nutrient resource. Due to limited land available for spreading, soils have frequently been overloaded with manure‐derived P over many years (Liu et al., 2023). Legacy P is also found in regions with potato and vegetable production systems, which typically exhibit higher P balances than cereal systems due to their greater nutrient demand and input (Bechmann, 2014; Li et al., 2015). The most severe legacy P situations may arise when intensive manure use coincides with potato and vegetable production. For instance, among seven Norwegian long‐term monitoring catchments, the largest P surplus was observed in a catchment characterized by intensive potato and vegetable production combined with poultry farming (Bechmann, 2014). Similarly, in China, where manure or other organic fertilizers are commonly applied to vegetable fields in addition to mineral fertilizers (Li et al., 2015), long‐term applications have resulted in soil test P levels in vegetable fields that are two‐ to ten‐fold higher than in nearby crop fields (Su et al., 2002). In conservation or zero tillage systems, legacy P can also arise from stratification of fertilizer, manure, or plant‐derived P due to the lack of soil mixing.

Reducing P balances by setting limits on livestock density or maximum P application rates is a direct and effective approach for improving water quality. This is because dissolved P losses increase significantly with long‐term P accumulation in soils (Liu et al., 2023), and reducing P inputs helps decrease legacy P pools built up over time. Such measures have been widely adopted in Northern Europe (Andersen et al., 2014), although they present economic and practical challenges for farmers.

Legacy P is most commonly associated with historical P accumulation resulting from excessive or inefficient P management and is often reflected in high soil test P levels. However, its impact on water quality is also influenced by multiple interacting factors, including climate, soil and field characteristics, current nutrient management practices, and broader land management (Liu et al., 2025). As with newly applied P, the effects of legacy P are most pronounced in soils vulnerable to P loss, for example, areas with high rainfall surplus or intensity, frequent freeze‐thaw cycles, steep slopes, poorly structured clay soils, high erosion risk, low P sorption capacity (Liu et al., 2024), and/or strong hydrological connectivity to streams. The impacts of legacy P can be mitigated through improved management practices. For example, work in the Canadian prairies has shown that reducing fertilizer P inputs (“P drawdown”) significantly decreased total dissolved P concentrations in both snowmelt and rainfall runoff, without compromising crop yield (Liu et al., 2019). Occasional tillage in conservation systems (e.g., once every few years) and the use of soil amendments can reduce P stratification and enhance P sorption capacity, respectively. Therefore, effective management of legacy P requires a holistic approach that accounts for both natural conditions and management practices.
