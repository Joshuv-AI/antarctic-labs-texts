Global Prevalence of Combustible Cigarettes and Noncombustible Nicotine or Tobacco Products Use Among Adolescents, 2014–2021
Authors: Seohyun Hong, Yejun Son, Seokjun Kim, Soeun Kim, Hyeon Jin Kim, Hyesu Jo, Jaeyu Park, Kyeongmin Lee, Hayeon Lee, Jiseung Kang, Damiano Pizzol, Selin Woo, Lee Smith, Jiyoung Hwang, Sang Youl Rhee, Dong Keon Yon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding global patterns of adolescent noncombustible nicotine or tobacco product (NNTP) and combustible cigarette (CC) use is vital due to rising prevalence and long-term health risks, but comparative international analyses are lacking. Thus, we aimed to assess smoking prevalence and trends among adolescents in 57 countries and territories and to identify factors associated with NNTP and CC use.MethodsWe utilized World Health Organization (WHO) Global Youth Tobacco Survey data to investigate adolescent NNTP and CC use prevalence and trends globally from 2014 to 2021. We conducted a meta-analysis using random-effects models to identify the summary effect of smoking prevalence in each country. Weighted linear regression was used to calculate weighted β coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Each analysis was stratified by sex, World Bank income category, e-cigarette regulatory policy status, and WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control ratification status. Finally, a hierarchical Bayesian statistical model was employed to precisely predict the prevalence rates of both CC and NNTP use up to 2045.ResultsAmong the 57 countries with 173,658 adolescents, NNTP use exhibited a higher prevalence than CC use in 31 countries. Demographic groups exhibiting higher smoking prevalence included male adolescents (NNTP use: 10.78% [95% CI, 9.38 to 12.18]; CC use: 10.36% [9.03 to 11.69]), adolescents in high-income countries (NNTP use: 11.29% [8.62 to 13.95]; CC use: 10.48% [7.27 to 13.68]), and those in countries with less restrictive policies (NNTP use: 11.18% [7.32 to 15.04]; CC use: 11.44% [7.75 to 15.13]). Global NNTP use showed an overall increase from 2014 to 2021 (β, 0.73 [0.16 to 1.30]), with a particularly notable rise among male adolescents (β, 0.81 [0.05 to 1.57]). In 2044–2045, NNTP use is expected to remain relatively stable (10.61%), while CC use is projected to show a downward trend (1.05%).ConclusionOver half of the countries exhibited a higher prevalence of NNTP use than CC use. Global smoking prevalence varied across diverse factors. NNTP use among adolescents has increased, especially among male adolescents.Keywords: Adolescents, Combustible Cigarettes, Noncombustible Nicotine or Tobacco Products, Trend, World Health Organization

INTRODUCTION

Cigarette smoking remains a pressing global health concern, with profound implications for public health. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that smoking-related deaths exceed 8 million annually.1 In global efforts to decrease smoking prevalence, particularly vulnerable populations, such as adolescents, are receiving increased focus.2,3 Adolescence, characterized by increased susceptibility to peer influence and engagement in risk-taking behaviors, represents a critical period for the formation of lifelong habits.4 Consequently, adolescents are particularly susceptible to initiating tobacco use—a habit that frequently persists into adulthood.5

In recent years, the emergence of noncombustible nicotine or tobacco products (NNTPs) has altered the landscape of tobacco consumption. Marketed as safer alternatives to conventional combustible cigarettes (CCs), NNTPs have gained widespread popularity, particularly among adolescents.6,7 Therefore, previous studies have shown an alarming increase in the prevalence of NNTP use among adolescents, both domestically and internationally. For instance, data from the 2022 U.S. National Youth Tobacco Survey revealed NNTPs as the most commonly used tobacco products among adolescents, with 9.4% of high and middle school students reporting NNTP use within the past 30 days.2 Furthermore, evidence associates NNTP use with increased exposure to toxic substances, nicotine dependency, and the initiation of cigarette use among adolescents.8,9 Consequently, the surge in popularity of NNTPs has prompted concerns regarding the potential for a new generation of nicotine addiction and the normalization of smoking behaviors among adolescents. Recognizing this global concern, comprehending the global trends in adolescent NNTP use is essential for developing effective public health interventions and regulatory measures.

Previous research has primarily focused on the prevalence of NNTP use within individual countries without a comparative international perspective. Through this study, we addressed this gap by examining the pattern of NNTP consumption among adolescents. Our analysis aimed to understand these trends across various countries, categorized by WHO region and country income level, utilizing data from the WHO’s Global Youth Tobacco Survey (GYTS). Understanding the various factors associated with adolescent NNTP use will help inform targeted interventions that address the needs of a diverse population. Additionally, by predicting the future use of NNTP and CC among adolescents, we aim to implement policies that will decrease smoking rates and contribute to the health of the next generation.

METHODS

Survey design and participants

We aimed to examine the prevalence and trends in CC and NNTP use across 57 countries surveyed between 2014 and 2021 using the GYTS, a school-based survey of tobacco use and related factors among adolescents. The GYTS is a collaboration between the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the WHO. The questionnaire used in this survey was translated into the local language of each country and consisted of multiple-choice response options.10 It also employed the same sampling strategy and standardized self-report anonymous questionnaire across all participating countries. A detailed description of the GYTS methodology is available on the US CDC website.11

The survey used a two-stage sampling strategy as follows: in the first stage, schools were selected by randomly sampling from the population (country or region) with probability proportional to the size of the school.12 In the second stage, classrooms of students aged 13–15 years were randomly selected within each selected school, consistent with the GYTS protocol that targets this age group due to their heightened susceptibility to tobacco use initiation.11 All selected students were eligible to participate in the GYTS survey. Although the sample included adolescents outside the 13–15 age range, their numbers were substantially smaller and were later excluded from the analysis.11 All GYTS surveys were approved by government agencies and institutional review boards or ethics committees in each country. Participant privacy was ensured through an anonymous and voluntary participation process. Data were also weighted to account for non-response and probability sampling.13

We selected country-specific datasets that included all years of the survey and variables necessary for prevalence and trend analysis. A total of 57 countries were analyzed in this study. Detailed information about each country’s characteristics during the survey year—such as sample size, response rate, proportion of males, country income level, e-cigarette regulatory policy status, and year of WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC) ratification—is presented in Table 1. Accession to the WHO FCTC can be found on the WHO website, which lists the year the FCTC was ratified for each country.11

Regulations on NNTPs were divided into four categories based on information from publicly available websites on global tobacco control and legislation, following the classification methodology of previous studies: most restrictive policies (MRP) prohibit e-cigarettes; restrictive policies (RP) regulate nicotine and/or other ingredients; less restrictive policies (LRP) allow the sale of e-cigarettes impose sales restrictions/regulations; and no regulatory policies (NRP) refer to cases where no reliable information is available.12

Country income levels were categorized as low-income country, lower-middle-income country, upper-middle-income country, and high-income country (HIC), based on the World Bank country classification for the year in question. Regions were categorized as Africa, Americas, South-East Asia, Europe, Eastern Mediterranean, and Western Pacific, based on the WHO regional classification.14

Definition of CCs and NNTPs

We selected specific questions about NNTP and CC use from several tobacco-related questions asked in the GYTS. CC and NNTP use were assessed through the questions: “During the past 30 days, on how many days did you smoke cigarettes?” and “During the past 30 days, on how many days did you use electronic cigarettes?” respectively.

Answer options included: “0 days,” “1–2 days,” “3–5 days,” “6–9 days,” “10–19 days,” “20–29 days,” and “all 30 days.” The data obtained was dichotomized for subsequent analysis by categorizing “0 days” as “no” and all other options as “yes.” If the answer to both questions was “yes,” the individual was defined as having used both CC and NNTP.

Statistical analysis

We utilized raw data from the GYTS to calculate the prevalence of CC and NNTP use based on information such as country, sex, and survey year. The data were stratified by sex, country income level, policy affiliation, and survey year using sampling weights and other variables from the GYTS. We then conducted a meta-analysis using a random effects model to calculate summary effect estimates and standard errors for the prevalence of CC and NNTP use in each stratum. This model accounts for variability between studies by considering differences in characteristics and methodology across studies. The summary effect estimates obtained were used to analyze variations in tobacco use rates by each variable.15

To analyze global trends in CC and NNTP use, we conducted meta-analyses that included all countries surveyed in a given year. As data for 2020 were collected from only one country, 2020 and 2021 data were combined for analysis. For trends over the entire period, β (95% confidence intervals [CIs]) values were estimated using linear regression models, with each year as the independent variable and the prevalence values as the dependent variable.

A hierarchical Bayesian statistical model was employed to precisely predict prevalence rates for both CC and NNTP smokers, as well as CC and NNTP smokers individually.16 To estimate the posterior distributions of model parameters and address heterogeneity across studies, a random sampling procedure was conducted. This approach effectively expresses uncertainty around the true parameter estimates.17

To accommodate the data characteristics, an ARIMA (1,1) model with p = 1 and a moving average component q = 1 was constructed.18 Convergence was verified using the Gelman–Rubin diagnostic. The posterior distribution was estimated using the Just Another Gibbs Sampler (R2JAGS) and Markov chain Monte Carlo procedure (MCMCpack) packages in RStudio (R 4.3.2; Boston, MA, USA).

All statistical analyses were conducted using the R software version 4.3.2 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria) and Python version 3.11.4 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA).19 For statistical analyses, a P value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant, using two-sided tests.3

Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kyung Hee University (KHSIRB-23-384(EA)), and data access was approved by the WHO and the US CDC. All students were provided with an information sheet about the GYTS on the first day, along with an informed consent form for parents or guardians. The GYTS does not contain data that could identify participants. The country datasets are publicly available, and our study adhered to the ethical considerations outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

RESULTS

A comprehensive analysis was conducted on a total of 173,658 adolescents (male: 48.84%) across 57 countries from 2014 to 2021. Table 1 shows a detailed overview of the baseline characteristics of the countries included in our analysis. Table 2 presents the prevalence of smoking categorized by type (NNTP, CC, and both CC and NNTP) across 57 countries, organized by WHO regions. Among these countries, 31 exhibited a higher prevalence of NNTP use compared to CC use, while 26 showed the opposite trend. Poland showed the highest prevalence for both CC and NNTP use (10.46% [95% CI, 9.30 to 11.63]), as well as for NNTP use (22.39% [95% CI, 21.81 to 24.98]). Papua New Guinea recorded the highest prevalence of CC use (22.92% [95% CI, 20.18 to 25.66]).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first study to analyze 57 countries using WHO GYTS data to explore the prevalence and trend of adolescent smoking, categorized by smoking type (CC, NNTP, and both) and various country factors (income level, e-cigarette regulatory policy status, and FCTC ratification status). In this extensive study of 57 countries, an increasing trend in NNTP use among adolescents from 2014 to 2021 was observed, especially among male adolescents. Meanwhile, more than half of the countries exhibited a higher prevalence of NNTP use compared to CC use. The prevalence of smoking among males was higher than among females, consistent for both NNTP and CC use. Furthermore, adolescents residing in HICs, under LRP e-cigarette regulations, and in countries that have ratified the FCTC, illustrated a high prevalence of NNTP and CC use. Additionally, projected analysis suggests an overall decrease in both CC and NNTP use among adolescents by 2045, with notable sex-specific trends, such as surpassing NNTP use among female adolescents and a substantial decline in CC use among male adolescents. In addition, NNTP use is expected to remain relatively stable, while CC is projected to be on a downward trend in 2044–2045. These observations highlight the need for targeted interventions and policy initiatives to curb smoking among adolescents.

The gradual increasing trend in NNTP use can be attributed to several factors. NNTPs are often perceived as less harmful alternatives to conventional cigarettes.6,20 This perception could lead to increased experimentation and use, especially among adolescents who are concerned about the health risks associated with smoking but still want to use or try tobacco products.21 Based on this perception, many marketing strategies emphasize NNTPs as a safer alternative, attracting new young users.22 Additionally, adolescent behavior is greatly influenced and shaped by peer influence. Consequently, increased use of NNTP among adolescents could lead to a swift escalation of its popularity. This trend and the features of NNTP could be the basis for the higher prevalence of NNTP use compared to CC use, which aligns with our analysis.

Male adolescents showed a higher prevalence of smoking than females, regardless of NNTP or CC use, and illustrated a higher increasing trend of NNTP use. Plausible mechanisms contributing to this sex-based difference could be social, psychological, and cultural factors. Many societies have sex-specific expectations regarding behaviors; therefore, smoking has been more socially acceptable among males. As a result, males may have easier access to tobacco products compared to females. One previous study also suggests that male adolescents may perceive smoking as a way to cope with stress or peer pressure.23

The prevalence of smoking was higher among adolescents living in HICs, regardless of NNTP or CC use. This could be due to the low relative income price (RIP) of tobacco in these countries. In the context of a global trend of increasing tobacco prices, countries with relatively low RIPs tended to be concentrated in HICs.24,25 This price advantage relative to household income might act as a risk factor for smoking among adolescents residing in HICs.26

From the perspective of adolescent tobacco regulation, countries under LRP regulation have shown a higher prevalence of smoking compared to those under RP or MRP regulation. This indicates that strong tobacco regulation can effectively prevent access to and use of tobacco in adolescents. Moreover, countries categorized under NRP regulation have recorded a relatively low prevalence of adolescent smoking, which could result from the cultural characteristics of tobacco use within these nations. This study conducted a comparative analysis of the two most used and studied tobacco types: NNTP and CC.13,21 However, unique types of tobacco might be more prevalently used in certain countries owing to cultural, social, and historical characteristics. For instance, water-pipe smoking, such as hookah, is historically a unique tobacco type popular locally in the Middle East, North Africa, Eastern Europe, and South Asia, where the prevalence and interest in hookah use can be comparable to or higher than that of NNTP use.27,28,29 NRP countries selected in this study, most of which belong to these regions, suggest that unique tobacco consumption patterns may contribute to the relatively lower NNTP or CC use prevalence observed in NRP countries.

The results of our study show that FCTC-not-ratified countries exhibited lower smoking prevalence than FCTC-ratified countries, contradicting previous research.11 This might be due to the subset of FCTC-not-ratified countries in the study not fully representing all nations that have not ratified the FCTC. The specific FCTC-not-ratified countries examined were Argentina, Cuba, Morocco, and Kosovo. Among these, Argentina, despite not having ratified the FCTC, enacted the National Tobacco Control Law in 2011 and has made substantial efforts to reduce youth smoking rates.30 Over the past decade, Argentina has implemented efforts at the subnational level to create smoke-free environments, strengthened regulations on advertising, and prohibited the sale of tobacco to individuals under 18 years, thereby achieving a substantive reduction in smoking rates.30,31,32 Cuba has substantially lowered smoking prevalence based on its strong healthcare and mandatory education systems, while Morocco has implemented tobacco control laws with advertising bans in educational facilities, further contributing to the reduction of smoking prevalence.33,34,35 These efforts could have led to the lower smoking prevalence observed in these countries, even though they have not ratified the FCTC.

The present study has several strengths. Primarily, we utilized extensive global data from the WHO's GYTS to observe worldwide prevalence and trends in adolescent NNTP and CC use. Our transnational analysis enabled us to draw global-scale results and recommendations without bias toward specific countries. Moreover, we considered various economic and political features of countries to present national risk factors for youth smoking prevalence. Furthermore, by integrating survey data across countries, we identified global longitudinal trends in smoking prevalence among adolescents across 57 countries. To our knowledge, this is the first study to present a global prevalence trend integrating NNTP and CC use to date.

Despite its strengths, the present study should be interpreted considering its limitations. First, GYTS is not completely comprehensive in terms of national and annual coverage. Some countries did not participate in the GYTS. In addition, there were variations in the survey period among countries. As a result, several countries were not included in the prevalence calculations for each year. Additionally, due to limited data availability in 2020 (only one country), we combined the 2020 and 2021 data for trend analysis. While this approach allowed for more stable estimates, it may have obscured short-term behavioral changes specific to the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic. Second, the GYTS only surveyed school-attending students, and the self-reported responses could lead to potential over-reporting or under-reporting. This design may underrepresent vulnerable populations, such as out-of-school adolescents, who could exhibit different smoking behaviors. In addition, our analysis included only 57 countries, which may limit the generalizability of global patterns. Moreover, self-reported data may be particularly vulnerable to bias when measuring sensitive behaviors such as tobacco use, especially among adolescents, where responses may be influenced by social desirability, peer norms, or perceived judgment. Although the GYTS is conducted anonymously and has exhibited strong test-retest reliability, the absence of biochemical validation (e.g., cotinine testing) limits our ability to objectively verify smoking status. Additionally, several of the prevalence estimates, particularly those from countries with smaller sample sizes or stratified subgroup analyses (e.g., by sex or policy status), exhibited relatively wide CIs. This reflects inherent sampling variability and underscores the uncertainty surrounding some of the point estimates. Although this imprecision does not negate the overall observed trends, it highlights the need for cautious interpretation and reinforces the importance of improving sample representativeness and data quality in future GYTS iterations. Nevertheless, the survey was conducted anonymously with a high participation rate (median 93.43%), and the test-retest reliability of tobacco use data in the GYTS is recognized, which can reduce bias.10 Lastly, FCTC-ratified countries exhibit varying degrees of actual regulation enforcement. Even within the same FCTC ratification status, countries autonomously comply with specific details, leading to gaps in practical implementation. Therefore, there is a need to re-examine the prevalence associated with FCTC ratification based on clear criteria for classifying the FCTC implementation status of countries. In addition, the four-category classification of e-cigarette regulatory policies may not fully capture the heterogeneity in implementation and enforcement across countries. Similarly, the use of current income level categorizations does not directly reflect tobacco affordability, which can vary considerably depending on national tobacco pricing and relative income levels. Furthermore, several important variables, such as concurrent use of other substances (e.g., alcohol or cannabis), age at smoking initiation, urban versus rural residency, and parental or household smoking status, were not included in our analysis. This was primarily due to inconsistent availability of these variables across countries in the GYTS datasets. To maintain comparability across all 57 countries and ensure the robustness of pooled estimates, we limited our analysis to core variables available across survey cycles. We acknowledge that the absence of these factors may restrict the depth of behavioral insights and encourage future studies to incorporate them when data availability allows.

The results of the study suggest the need for appropriate targeting to reduce adolescent smoking prevalence. Governments in each country should prioritize robust regulations focused on male adolescents to decrease adolescent smoking rates. Additionally, there is a need for increased attention in HICs and LRP countries that particularly exhibited higher prevalence of adolescent smoking. In particular, concerted worldwide initiative is necessary to drive a reduction in smoking prevalence among NNTP users, where a global increasing trend has been observed. NNTP use has quickly spread among adolescents through marketing strategies that emphasize their health advantages compared to CC use and leverage characteristics such as peer influence.4,21,22 These adolescent-targeted strategies are believed to have driven the current increase in adolescent NNTP use. Therefore, specialized response strategies are needed to reduce the prevalence of NNTP use among adolescents. Notably, it is crucial to highlight the risks associated with NNTP use and to exhibit the potential adverse effects on adolescents, actively countering existing marketing strategies. Additionally, measures should be developed to respond to and prevent the spread of NNTP use within adolescent groups at an early stage.

Analysis of projected trends in CC and NNTP use among adolescents suggests several policy implications. Strengthening regulations—such as restricting advertising on platforms popular with adolescents—can help decrease NNTP usage, as has been seen with CC use. Furthermore, implementing sex-specific intervention programs addressing the expected trends by sex could be helpful. Given that NNTP use is expected to become more prevalent among female adolescents by 2030–2031, targeted educational and prevention programs could be developed to specifically address this trend. Additionally, continually monitoring trends in CC and NNTP use among adolescents is needed to help refine future interventions.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the global prevalence and longitudinal trends of tobacco use among adolescents across 57 countries using WHO GYTS data, categorizing subgroups into NNTP, CC, and both use. The prevalence of NNTP use among adolescents has continuously increased, particularly among male adolescents. Moreover, more than half of the countries showed a higher prevalence of NNTP use than CC use. Global smoking prevalence varies according to various characteristics. Furthermore, NNTP use is expected to remain relatively stable, while CC is projected to be on a downward trend in 2044–2045. Therefore, it is necessary to develop customized adolescent smoking prevention strategies tailored to both national and individual levels, while also addressing variations in smoking habits.
