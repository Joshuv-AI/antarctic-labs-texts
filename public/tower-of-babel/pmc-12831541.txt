Associations between prenatal metal mixtures and allergic symptoms in childhood
Authors: Abhishek Pandey, Shachi Mistry, Cecilia Alcala, Hector Lamadrid-Figueroa, Marcela Tamayo-Ortiz, Adriana Mercado-Garcia, Guadalupe Estrada-Gutierrez, Martha María Téllez-Rojo, Robert O. Wright, Rosalind J. Wright, Maria José Rosa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Previous work has linked exposure to individual metals in pregnancy to allergic outcomes postnatally but few studies have examined metals exposure as a mixture. Analysis included 542 mother-child dyads enrolled in the Programming Research in Obesity, Growth, Environment and Social Stressors (PROGRESS) birth cohort in Mexico City. Levels of arsenic (As), cadmium (Cd), cobalt (Co), copper (Cu), manganese (Mn), nickel (Ni) and lead (Pb) were analyzed in blood collected during the 2nd and 3rd trimesters. Atopic dermatitis and allergic rhinitis symptoms were assessed via questionnaire at 4–6 years and 6–8 years of age. Mixture associations were modeled using repeated holdout WQS regression with adjustment for relevant covariates. We found evidence of a 2nd trimester metal mixture association with an increased risk of ever allergic rhinitis symptoms at 6–8 years (OR = 1.16, 95 % CI: 1.05, 1.28). Mn contributed the most to the mixture effect, followed by Cu, and Pb. We also found evidence of a 2nd trimester metal mixture association with an increased risk of current allergic rhinitis symptoms at 6–8 years (OR = 1.13 95 % CI: 1.02, 1.26) in which Mn, Cu and Pb were again the main contributors. Lastly, we saw evidence of a 3rd trimester metal mixture association with an increased risk of current allergic rhinitis symptoms at 6–8 years (OR = 1.17 95 % CI: 1.03, 1.32). As and Mn were the top contributors to the mixture association followed by Cu and Ni. Our results suggest that prenatal exposure to metals is related to childhood allergic symptoms.

Introduction

The global prevalence of allergic diseases like atopic dermatitis/eczema and allergic rhinitis, has seen a noticeable increase in children in recent years (Lv et al., 2024). The worldwide prevalence of atopic dermatitis has grown by 1.21 % per decade among children and 0.98 % per decade among adolescents (Langan et al., 2023). Worldwide the prevalence of allergic rhinitis has seen an increase in physician diagnosis over the years, from 8.39 % from 2012 to 2015 to 19.87 % from 2016 to 2022 (Licari et al., 2023). Atopic dermatitis and allergic rhinitis in childhood are strong predictors of asthma later in life (Martin et al., 2011). Atopic dermatitis has been linked to skin infections, asthma, eye problems, sleep issues, heart concerns, and poor mental health outcomes (Mostafa and Smith, 2023). Allergic rhinitis is also linked to sleeping disorders, fatigue, impaired memory, and depression which also impact quality of life (Kalmarzi et al., 2017). Therefore, understanding the mechanisms for the development of pediatric allergies can inform interventions to prevent adverse health outcomes later in life.

Exposure to metals like arsenic (As), cadmium (Cd), cobalt (Co), copper (Cu), manganese (Mn), nickel (Ni), and lead (Pb) is ubiquitous around the world as these metals can be present in water sources, food supplies, industrial emissions, pesticides, tobacco smoke and fertilizers (Tchounwou et al., 2012). During prenatal development the fetus is particularly vulnerable to environmental exposures and metals like As, Cd, and Pb can cross the placenta and reach the fetus (Vahter, 2009; Zheng et al., 2014) potentially impacting immune health through immune cell proliferation and helper T-cell function alterations (Zinia et al., 2023). Metal exposure also induces oxidative stress and inflammation, interfering with cell development, potentially impacting cell division and differentiation (Jomova and Valko, 2011).

Epidemiological evidence also links prenatal metal exposure to allergic disease. Cd but not Pb measured in cord blood was associated with increased odds of atopic dermatitis at 6 months of age in Korean infants (Kim et al., 2013). In a study in France, higher levels of Cd measured in cord blood were associated with higher odds of allergy at age 8 years (Pesce et al., 2021). A recent systematic review and meta- analysis reported associations between prenatal Pb, Cd and Ni and childhood atopic dermatitis and eczema (Chen et al., 2024). Most of these studies have focused on examining individual metals associations while in real world scenarios, exposure to multiple metals occurs simultaneously, and the toxicity of the mixture may be altered by the dynamics of the constituent metals (Carpenter et al., 2002). To date only two studies have examined associations between prenatal metal mixtures and allergic outcomes in childhood with somewhat divergent results (Ruan et al., 2022; Tsai et al., 2021) and none have examined these outcomes in both early and mid-childhood or in a Latin American population. Therefore, our study aimed to assess the association between a prenatal metal mixture of As, Cd, Co, Cu, Mn, Ni, and Pb assessed at two time points in pregnancy with allergic symptoms in children at ages 4–6 and 6–8 years. We hypothesize that higher prenatal exposure to a metal mixture will be associated with increased risk of allergic symptoms.

Materials and methods

Study population

Between July 2007 and February 2011, the Programming Research in Obesity, Growth, Environment and Social Stressors (PROGRESS) cohort recruited pregnant women receiving prenatal care at Mexican Social Security System (IMSS) clinics. The inclusion criteria were less than 20 weeks pregnant, at least 18 years old, no history of kidney or heart disease, access to a telephone, no drinking habits, no steroids or anti-epilepsy medication use, and an intention to reside in Mexico City for at least three years (Burris et al., 2013). 948 live births were in the original cohort, 681 are actively followed and from those 542 had complete exposure, outcome, and covariate data for these analyses (see Supplemental Fig. S1).

Prenatal metal exposure

Venous whole blood samples were collected during the 2nd and 3rd trimester in vacutainer tubes with Potassium Ethylenediaminetetraacetate (K2EDTA) (Becton-Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey). Prenatal metal (μg/dL) levels of As, Cd, Co, Cu, Mn, Ni, and Pb, were analyzed using a dynamic reaction cell-inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (ICP-DRC-MS) (Elan 6100; PerkinElmer, Norwalk, CT) as previously described (Midya et al., 2024; Tamayo et al., 2016). Quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC) procedures included the analyses of calibration standards in the range of 0.001 to 50 ng ml—1 and both initial and continuous calibration verification standards. QC samples consisted of mixed element standards at two different concentration levels, 5 % duplicate samples, procedural blanks, in-house pooled blood samples at three levels to monitor accuracy, and certified reference materials Seronorm Blood (L2, L3) — (SERO AS, Billingstad, Norway), and National Institute of Standards and Technology (Gaithersburg, US) standard reference material (SRM) 955c (L1, L2, L3, L4) Toxic Metal in Caprine Blood. Continuous calibration verification standards and inhouse pooled samples were run after analysis of every ten samples. QC recovery rates were 90 to 110 %, and inter-day and intra-day precision was <6 % for samples with concentrations more than the limit of quantification. The method applied undergoes periodical proficiency testing accreditation by the Quebec Multielement External Quality Assessment Scheme and the New York State Biomonitoring Program for Trace Elements. The limit of detections for the blood metal concentrations were as follows: 0.4 μg/L for As, 0.06 μg/L for Cd, 0.1 μg/L for Co, 10.9 μg/L for Cu, 0.7 μg/L for Mn, 4.5 μg/L for Ni, and 0.2 μg/L for Pb.

Assessment of allergic outcomes

The International Study of Asthma and Allergies in Childhood (ISAAC) questionnaire (Asher et al., 2006), translated and validated into Spanish (Mata Fernandez et al., 2005), was administered separately at the 4–6 and 6–8 years visit. Caregivers answered yes/no questions about their child’s history of atopic dermatitis and allergic rhinitis symptoms. Ever atopic dermatitis symptoms were defined by the question: “Has your child ever had an itchy rash which was coming and going for at least 6 months?” Current atopic dermatitis symptoms were defined by the question: “Has your child had this itchy rash at any time in the last 12 months?” The additional question “Has such an itchy rash at any time affected any of the following place: the folds of the elbows, behind the knees, below the buttocks or around the neck, ears or eyes?” was also considered but answer frequencies were virtually identical for both questions. Ever allergic rhinitis symptoms were defined by the question: “Has your child ever had a problem with sneezing, or a runny, or a blocked nose when he/she DID NOT have a cold or the flu?” Current allergic rhinitis symptoms were defined by the question: “In the past 12 months, has your child had a problem with sneezing, or a runny, or a blocked nose when he/she DID NOT have a cold or the flu?” Additionally, more specific current allergic rhinitis symptoms outcome was included with the addition of assessment of itchy/watery eyes “In the past 12 months, has this nose problem been accompanied by itchy watery eyes?”

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on those previously associated with prenatal metals exposure and allergic outcomes excluding those on the causal pathways, and validated factors as shown in the Directed Acyclic Graph (Supplemental Fig. S2). Covariates included were maternal age at the time of enrollment (measured continuously in years), maternal pre- pregnancy body mass index (measured continuously, kg/m2), parity (primiparous vs multiparous), child sex (male/female), maternal education level at the time of enrollment (less than high school, high school graduate, and more than high school) and prenatal environmental tobacco smoke (ETS) exposure (yes/no). Women’s pre-pregnancy BMI was estimated from a linear mixed effects model as previously described (Soria-Contreras et al., 2020). Since report of smoking during pregnancy was rare (8/948 participants reported smoking in pregnancy), ETS exposure was used and was defined by presence of at least one person who smoked inside the home during the 2nd or 3rd trimester of pregnancy. Report of ever maternal asthma was assessed at enrollment but only 0.9 % (5/542) of participants answered “yes”. Therefore, maternal asthma was not included as a covariate.

Statistical analyses

Demographics were analyzed using descriptive statistics and results are presented as frequencies for categorical variables and as means and standard deviations for continuous variables. Blood metal levels were log2-transformed to normalize distribution since the data were right skewed. Outcomes assessed at each visit were examined in independent models. Because tobacco smoke can be a source of trace amounts of metals we also compared metal concentration by ETS exposure.

We examined the relationship between metal mixtures (As, Cd, Co, Cu, Mn, Ni, and Pb) and allergic outcomes, using a weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression model. This model produces a weighted index to estimate the combined effect of the metal mixture on the outcome of interest, while highlighting the metals of greatest concern through calculated weights (Carrico et al., 2015). In the WQS regression, the index is expressed as the weighted sum of the mixture components, where wi is the weight assigned to the ith component, and qi represents its quantile (Carrico et al., 2015). The relative weights of each metal were calculated by dividing the average weight of each by the total, ensuring that each outcome’s weights summed to 100 %. We ran the WQS in the positive direction because we hypothesized that 2nd and 3rd trimester metal mixtures are associated with higher odds of our selected outcomes. To obtain more robust estimates, we used 110 repeated holdouts along with 100 bootstrap samples, where the data was randomly split into 40/60 % training and validation sets with replacement (Tanner et al., 2019). This procedure was repeated for each holdout, generating a distribution of estimates and metal weights across the 110 WQS regressions and 100 bootstrap samples. The results include mean estimates with 95 % confidence intervals and mean weights for the metals. We also evaluated the number of holdouts that produced estimates above or below the null. If the mean estimate indicated a significant association, we further examined the metal weights to identify which metals had the greatest impact on pediatric allergy symptom risk using the Busgang criteria (Busgang et al., 2022). A metal was considered a “probable” contributor if at least 90 % of the holdouts exceeded the 1/c cutoff (where c is the total number of metals in the combination). For confirmatory analyses we performed quantile g-Computation (q g- comp). Similar to the WQS method, q g-comp also evaluates mixture effects by calculating a weighted linear index to generate estimates and significance of the total effects and weights for each compound of interest (Keil et al., 2020). However, q g-comp models do not require the assumption of directional homogeneity and allow for non-linearity of exposure effects. Metal weights were estimated using the noboot option while overall mixture effects were estimated using the boot option after 10,000 iterations. For both WQS and q g-comp models metal concentrations were put into deciles.

In sensitivity analyses, we used modified Poisson regression models (Zou, 2004) to estimate the associations between 2nd and 3rd trimester individual metal concentrations and atopic dermatitis/allergic rhinitis symptoms. In exploratory analyses we also examined potential effect modification by ETS and child sex using a stratified WQS regression model as in previous work (Hu et al., 2024). Stratified WQS analysis generate estimates and weights for each stratum. The gwqs package version 3.0.5 was used for WQS analyses and the qcomp package version 2.18.7 was used for q g-comp analyses. All analyses were run using R version 4.2.2.

Results

Study participants and characteristics

Table 1 presents demographic characteristics, exposure and outcome distributions for our overall sample. The average maternal age at enrollment was 27.6 years and the mean maternal pre-pregnancy BMI was 26.4. The majority of participants did not have more than a high school diploma (76 %) and were primiparous (60.9 %). The child sample was evenly distributed by sex. Allergic symptoms were more prevalent at the 4–6 than at 6–8 years visits. Pearson correlations of 2nd and 3rd trimester metal concentrations are shown in supplemental Fig. S3. Most metals were weak to moderately correlated both within the same time point and across time points. Cd and Pb were the only metals with moderately strong correlations across collection time points (r = 0.66 and 0.72 respectively). 3rd trimester Cu and Mn were also moderately correlated (r = 0.64). There were no significant differences in metals concentrations by ETS status except for Cd which was higher in ETS exposed participants for both the 2nd and 3rd trimester (Supplemental Table S1).

Metal mixtures and allergic symptoms

In our WQS analyses, we did not find evidence of any association between the 2nd trimester metal mixture and any atopic dermatitis symptoms or allergic rhinitis symptoms at age 4–6 years (see Fig. 1). As shown in Fig. 2, we did find evidence of a 2nd trimester metal mixture association with an increased odds of ever allergic rhinitis symptoms at 6–8 years (OR = 1.16, 95 % CI: 1.05, 1.28). Mn contributed the most to the mixture association, followed by Cu and Pb (Fig. 2b). We also found evidence of a 2nd trimester metal mixture association with an increased odds of current allergic rhinitis symptoms at 6–8 years (OR = 1.13 95 % CI: 1.02, 1.26) with Mn, Cu and Pb as the top contributors (Fig. 2d). For models examining 3rd trimester metal mixture and allergic outcomes (Figs. 3 and 4), we found suggestive associations between higher 3rd trimester metal mixture concentrations and higher odds of ever and current allergic rhinitis symptoms at age 4–6 years. Mixture associations were driven by As and Ni and additionally Cu for the ever allergic rhinitis symptoms outcome r (see Fig. 3b and d). We found similar associations between higher 3rd trimester metal mixture levels and higher risk current allergic rhinitis symptoms at 6–8 years (OR = 1.17 95 % CI: 1.03, 1.32). Mn and Cu remained among the top contributors to the mixture association, but for 3rd trimester models As and Ni also emerged as relevant metals (Fig. 4d).

We found similar patterns in our q g-comp models (Supplemental Figs. S4–S7). We did not find evidence of an association between 2nd trimester metal mixture and any outcome of interest at 4–6 years. We found associations between the 2nd trimester metal mixture and higher odds of both ever and current allergic rhinitis symptoms at age 6–8 years, also driven by Mn, Cu and Pb (Fig. S5b and d). We also found similar patterns for higher concentrations of the 3rd trimester metal mixture and higher odds of allergic rhinitis symptoms (Figs. S6 and S7).

Sensitivity and stratified analyses

Sensitivity analyses are shown in Supplemental Tables S2–S5. We found that higher 2nd trimester Cu was associated with higher risk of both ever and current allergic rhinitis symptoms at 4–6 and 6–8 years (Tables S2 & S3). Higher 2nd trimester Cu concentrations were additionally associated with higher risk of ever atopic dermatitis symptoms at 6–8 years (Table S3). Higher 2nd trimester concentrations of Mn were associated with higher risk of ever and current allergic rhinitis symptoms at age 6–8 years (Table S3). Higher 2nd trimester Pb was associated with higher risk of current atopic dermatitis symptoms also at 6–8 years (Table S3). We saw different patterns of association for 3rd trimester metal concentrations. Higher 3rd trimester As was associated with higher risk of both ever and current allergic rhinitis symptoms at 4–6 years (Table S4). Higher 3rd trimester Co concentrations were associated with higher risk of ever and current atopic dermatitis symptoms at age 4–6 years (Table S4). Higher 3rd trimester Mn was associated with higher risk of ever atopic dermatitis symptoms and higher Ni was associated with higher risk of current allergic rhinitis symptoms at 4–6 years (Table S4). For the 6–8 year outcomes we only found associations between higher Cu and higher risk of ever atopic dermatitis symptoms, higher Pb and higher risk of current atopic dermatitis symptoms and higher Ni and higher risk of current allergic rhinitis symptoms + itchy watery eyes (Table S5).

We did not find strong evidence of effect modification by ETS. We found evidence of effect modification only for the association between 2nd trimester metal mixture and ever atopic dermatitis symptoms at age 6–8 years, with higher risk of symptoms seen in the non-ETS exposed group (see Supplemental Fig. S8 & Supplemental Table S6, all other results not shown). In the non-ETS exposed group, the mixture associations were driven by Cu, As and Pb. We did not find any significant difference in these results after exclusion of Cd and do not report any effect modification by ETS exposure on individual associations between Cd and allergic outcomes (results not shown). Lastly, we explored effect modification by child sex. We found evidence of effect modification only for the association between 2nd trimester metal mixture and current rhinitis + itchy and watery eyes at ages 6–8 years, with higher risk of symptoms seen in females versus males, driven by Co and Cu (see Supplemental Fig. S9 & Supplemental Table S7).

Discussion

We found evidence of an association between 2nd and 3rd trimester metal mixtures and higher odds of allergic rhinitis symptoms in mid-childhood. Notably, Mn, Cu, and Pb were the primary contributors to the mixture effects in both the 2nd and 3rd trimesters, while As and Ni played a larger role for 3rd trimester associations. We did not find any evidence of mixture associations at either trimester and atopic dermatitis symptoms. In individual models, we found evidence of associations between higher concentration of As, Cu, Mn, Ni and Pb with increased risk of atopic dermatitis and allergic rhinitis symptoms in childhood.

Only a few studies have examined prenatal metal mixture associations in relation to pediatric allergy diagnosis/symptoms. In a birth cohort in Taiwan, 3rd trimester urinary levels of a metal mixture of As, Cd, Pb, Co, Cu, Ni, thallium (Tl) and zinc (Zn) were associated with higher risk of report of physician diagnosis of atopic dermatitis at age 4 years with As and Cd driving the mixture association (Tsai et al., 2021) but no other allergic/atopic outcome was assessed. A study in Wuhan, China, measured metal concentrations of vanadium (V), chromium (Cr), Ni, As, Cd, Tl and Pb in maternal urine collected during the first, second, and third trimester of pregnancy (Ruan et al., 2022). Researchers reported an association between the urinary metal mixture averaged across pregnancy and higher risk of allergic rhinitis but not eczema or wheeze at age 4 with As and Tl as the major contributors to the association (Ruan et al., 2022). Similarly, we saw 3rd trimester As as a significant contributor to our mixture association with allergic rhinitis symptoms but not atopic dermatitis.

Both the 2nd and 3rd trimester of pregnancy are key periods for immune development which may be vulnerable to disruption by metal exposure. This heightened vulnerability could explain the observed associations between the metal mixtures and rhinitis symptoms across both trimesters. During the second trimester B cells develop, and T cells mature to help the immune system fight infections and lymphoid organs also develop which help activate immune cells that eventually travel to peripheral tissue and other organs for immune responses (Cenariu et al., 2021; Janeway et al., 2001). In the third trimester the fetus receives maternal antibodies through the placenta that will help provide passive immunity to the child after birth (Cinicola et al., 2021).

As, Cu, Mn, Ni and Pb emerged as key contributors to the mixture association. As exposure has been linked to changes in immunomodulatory function and immune response alterations including imbalanced differentiation of Th1/Th2 cells (Dangleben et al., 2013; Morzadec et al., 2012; Zhao et al., 2019) Prenatal exposure to As has also been associated to unbalanced T lymphocyte subpopulations (Nadeau et al., 2014) and higher IgE concentrations in umbilical cord blood (Liu et al., 2021). Cu and Mn are essential micronutrients but they have been previously linked to higher risk of allergic rhinitis and eczema (Pesce et al., 2021; Podlecka et al., 2022). Cu and Mn deficiency and excess are linked to increases in oxidative stress and inflammation. Higher levels of Mn may cause oxidative stress (Baj et al., 2023) leading to NF-kB activation and the release of inflammatory cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13) (Meng et al., 2025). Ni is a well-known trigger of contact dermatitis and prenatal exposure has been associated with increased serum levels of IL-1β, IL-2 and TNF-α measured in children at 3 years of age (Ho et al., 2022). Ni can activate dendritic cells by binding with proteins to create T cell epitopes leading to the release of inflammatory cytokines interleukin (IL)-6and IL-12 (Riedel et al., 2021). Pb exposure can cause an imbalance in immune cell populations, specifically by disrupting the Th1/Th2 balance shifting the immune response toward a Th2-dominant profile, increasing susceptibility to atopic conditions (Dietert and Piepenbrink, 2006).

Our study has several strengths including prospective design, rich covariate data and use of advanced statistical approaches to examine mixture associations of metals. We were able to assess exposure at two different time points in pregnancy and examine outcomes at two different time points in childhood. We used a validated instrument to assess our outcome data. We also acknowledge several limitations. We did not examine report of doctor diagnosis of the allergic conditions of interest; however reported doctor diagnosis may also be influenced by access to healthcare. Potential recall bias may be present, leading to under or over reporting of symptoms. All metals were measured in blood; urine is considered the gold standard matrix for measuring As and Cd (Fisher and Gupta, 2024). We were unable to measure speciated arsenic, which differentiates between organic and inorganic forms. This limitation is important because inorganic arsenic is more toxic, and without speciation, we cannot accurately assess the health risks associated with different arsenic exposures, potentially leading to less precise interpretations of its effects (Camacho et al., 2022). Lastly, residual confounding from other external variables may influence the prenatal metal concentrations in the mother and the allergy outcomes of the child. Future studies are needed to determine if these associations persist into adolescence and adulthood.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest prenatal exposure to metal mixtures during the 2nd and 3rd trimester influences pediatric allergy outcomes. These findings underscore the need to examine simultaneous metals in our environment to understand the development of pediatric allergies. By addressing this gap, our study offers a unique perspective that enhances the current understanding of pediatric allergies. Also, our results add to a growing body of evidence suggesting that prenatal exposure to environmental metal mixtures can have varying health effects, depending on the timing, dose, and combination of toxicants. This knowledge can help healthcare experts and policymakers have data driven approaches to health policy to ensure the safety of pregnant women and children. Future studies should continue to explore how early life metal exposure is linked to persistent allergic disease, as these exposures may be particularly relevant to urban, high-exposed populations.
