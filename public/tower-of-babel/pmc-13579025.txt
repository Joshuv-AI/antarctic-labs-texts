Association of antifungal treatment duration with 90-day mortality in critically ill adults with candidemia: a multicenter target trial emulation in France

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe optimal duration of antifungal therapy for candidemia in intensive care unit (ICU) patients remains uncertain. We aimed to evaluate whether shorter antifungal therapy was not associated with increased mortality using a target trial emulation framework.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter cohort study including adult ICU patients with candidemia from 16 ICUs in France (January 1, 2015 to January 1, 2023), emulating a target trial comparing short-course (<10 days) versus long-course (≥10 days) antifungal therapy. Patients treated for more than 21 days were excluded to minimize indication bias related to complicated candidemia. Patients who died within five days were excluded to ensure eligibility for both strategies. Research in context

Evidence before this study

Current guidelines recommend antifungal therapy for at least 14 days after the first negative blood culture for candidemia, corresponding to total treatment durations of 2–3 weeks. However, these recommendations are largely based on expert opinion rather than high-quality evidence. We searched PubMed and clinical trial registries (“ClinicalTrials.gov”)for studies evaluating antifungal treatment duration in critically ill patients with candidemia (search terms: “candidemia”, “antifungal”, “duration”, “intensive care unit”, “ICU”; search period: inception to January 1, 2023). Two randomized controlled trials (NCT06907992, NCT06859671) are registered but have not yet begun enrollment; both exclude the most severely ill patients, those with shock, or those requiring mechanical ventilation. Observational studies have suggested that shorter courses might be feasible in selected patients with uncomplicated candidemia, but these focused on predominantly non-ICU populations. No randomized or high-quality observational evidence specifically addresses optimal antifungal duration in critically ill ICU populations.

Added value of this study

Using a target trial emulation framework with cloning-censoring-weighting, we analyzed 237 ICU patients with candidemia from a multicenter cohort of 16 French ICUs (January 1, 2015 to January 1, 2023). After rigorous adjustment for confounding and immortal time bias, short-course therapy (<10 days) was not significantly associated with 90-day mortality (weighted HR 1.14, 95% CI 0.82–1.58). This study specifically addresses critically ill ICU patients (median SAPS II 53), a population excluded from ongoing trials. The dramatic attenuation from crude mortality (79.2% vs. 39.7%) to weighted estimates highlights the magnitude of selection bias and confounding by indication in observational studies of treatment duration.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings are hypothesis-generating and should not alter current clinical practice. The wide confidence intervals (compatible with up to 58% relative increase in mortality) preclude conclusions of safety or non-inferiority. However, these results do not indicate a clear survival benefit associated with prolonged antifungal therapy in selected ICU patients who survive the initial phase and lack obvious indications for prolonged therapy. Randomized controlled trials specifically targeting ICU populations remain essential to definitively determine optimal antifungal duration. In the meantime, stratifying candidemia into complicated and uncomplicated cases may help antifungal stewardship while awaiting higher-level evidence.

Introduction

Invasive candidiasis remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in critically ill patients, with candidemia representing the most frequent clinical presentation in the intensive care unit (ICU).1,2 Despite advances in antifungal therapy, mortality rates range from 30% to 50% in ICU populations, highlighting the need for optimized treatment strategies.3

Current guidelines recommend antifungal therapy for at least 14 days after the first negative blood culture and resolution of signs and symptoms, typically resulting in total treatment durations of 2–3 weeks.4,5 This duration was originally established following the landmark trial by Rex et al. comparing fluconazole to amphotericin B, where a minimum treatment duration was imposed.6 However, these recommendations are largely based on expert opinion in the absence of high-quality evidence from randomized controlled trials.

Recent observational studies have suggested that shorter courses might be non-inferior in selected patients with uncomplicated candidemia.7 Determining the optimal antifungal treatment duration in critically ill patients therefore remains a major unresolved clinical question, particularly given the risks of drug-related toxicity, antifungal resistance, and resource utilization associated with prolonged therapy.8,9 Conversely, premature discontinuation carries the risk of inadequate source control with secondary dissemination, particularly cardiac and ophthalmologic involvement. The true incidence of secondary complications remains debated. In a recent retrospective multicenter study in ICU patients, secondary localizations were found in nearly 22% of patients with candidemia, with longer treatment durations in this subgroup.10 Ocular involvement, particularly endophthalmitis, has been reported in 2%–16% of cases, though recent systematic reviews suggest that inconsistent definitions may have led to overestimation.11,12 Endocarditis appears less common, diagnosed in approximately 5.9% of patients undergoing transesophageal echocardiography.13 Balancing adequate infection control against minimizing unnecessary antifungal exposure therefore represents a key clinical challenge.

In observational studies of treatment duration, standard approaches are vulnerable to bias because treatment duration is not known at baseline. Patients receiving shorter therapy may differ systematically from those receiving longer therapy, and immortal time bias arises because patients must survive long enough to receive prolonged therapy. Standard propensity score matching cannot fully address these issues, requiring more advanced methods such as cloning-censoring-weighting, which explicitly emulate a randomized trial by assigning patients to both strategies from baseline.

Two prospective randomized controlled trials (NCT06907992, NCT06859671) are registered on ClinicalTrials.gov but have not yet begun enrollment. These trials compare 7 versus 14 days of therapy in patients with uncomplicated candidemia, but typically exclude the most severely ill patients, those with shock, or those requiring mechanical ventilation. Consequently, the applicability of their findings to the critically ill ICU population remains uncertain, and dedicated evidence for this high-risk group is urgently needed.

In the absence of randomized evidence specifically targeting critically ill populations, well-designed observational studies using advanced causal inference methods can provide valuable insights.14 Target trial emulation has emerged as a powerful framework to reduce bias in observational studies by explicitly designing the analysis to mimic a hypothetical randomized trial.15,16 When combined with clone-censor-weighting methods to address immortal time bias and inverse probability weighting to adjust for confounding, this approach can yield estimates that approximate those from randomized trials.17

Using this methodological framework, we aimed to emulate a target trial comparing short-course (<10 days) versus long-course (≥10 days) antifungal therapy in ICU patients with uncomplicated candidemia who survive the early. The primary objective was to compare 90-day mortality between these two strategies.

Methods

Study design and population

The CandidICU study was a large retrospective multicenter cohort study conducted in 16 French ICUs. All adult (≥18 years) patients hospitalized from January 1, 2015 to January 1, 2023 in one of the participating ICUs with at least one Candida spp. positive blood culture within seven days before ICU admission or acquired during the ICU stay were enrolled.10 The study protocol is available in the Supplementary Materials.

Ethics

The study was approved by the local ethics committee (Comité d'éthique CHU Rennes N° 23.99) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. In accordance with French regulations for retrospective observational studies, written informed consent was not required. All surviving patients received written information and were given the opportunity to object to the use of their data; for deceased patients, written information was provided to their next of kin, and no objection was recorded.

Study population

For the present analysis, we included patients who received antifungal therapy and had complete data for all variables included in the propensity score model. Patients who never received antifungal treatment were excluded. To avoid immortal time bias, patients who died within the first 5 days after candidemia onset were excluded, as this 5-day exclusion is methodologically required to ensure that all included patients are eligible for both treatment strategies at baseline. In addition, patients receiving <5 days of antifungal therapy were excluded because such short durations likely reflect treatment discontinuation for reasons unrelated to the treatment strategy under evaluation. To minimize indication bias related to complicated candidemia (such as endocarditis or deep-seated secondary infections), patients receiving >21 days of therapy were excluded, as this threshold corresponds to complicated forms requiring specific prolonged management according to international guidelines.4 The final analytic population therefore comprises patients with uncomplicated candidemia who survived at least 5 days, received between 5 and 21 days of antifungal therapy, and had complete covariate data (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Study profile.

Data collection and definitions

Standardized case report forms were used to collect baseline demographic characteristics, comorbidities, clinical and biological findings at candidemia onset, and disease severity assessed by the Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II).18 Details of antifungal management were recorded, including the type of antifungal agent, time from candidemia onset to treatment initiation, and total duration of antifungal therapy.

Candida species were categorized as Candida albicans versus non-albicans. Comorbidities and risk factors recorded included age, sex, obesity, cirrhosis, diabetes, solid organ transplantation, hematological malignancy, neutropenia, solid tumor, autoimmune or chronic inflammatory disease, longterm corticosteroid use, time from ICU admission to candidemia onset (which was calculated as days between ICU admission and candidemia diagnosis; negative values (diagnosis prior to admission) were retained without recoding), surgical reason for ICU admission, invasive mechanical ventilation within 24 h after ICU admission, temperature and leukocyte count at admission, prior antifungal exposure within 3 months, and initial antifungal class along with suspected source of candidemia (defined as catheter related suspected or proven or not).

The primary exposure was the total duration of antifungal therapy, dichotomized as short (<10 days) versus long (≥10 days) treatment. This threshold was selected as a pragmatic cutoff corresponding to the lower bound of durations observed in our cohort (Supplementary Figure S1), and reflecting the hypothesis that treatment shorter than the guideline-recommended 14 days might be sufficient in selected ICU patients. While ongoing RCTs are evaluating 7 versus 14 days in non-ICU populations, the 10-day threshold was chosen to maximize the sample size of the short-course group in our ICU cohort while still representing a duration meaningfully shorter than current guideline recommendations.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality within 90 days after candidemia onset.

Secondary outcomes included ICU and hospital length of stay, duration of mechanical ventilation, need for renal replacement therapy, occurrence of acute kidney injury, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), catecholamine use along with catecholamine duration and second fungemia (defined as a new episode of fungemia with a different Candida species or with the same species occurring more than 30 days after the initial episode, documented by at least one positive blood culture).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and categorical variables as numbers with percentages. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, confirming non-normal distribution; accordingly, non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U test) were used for continuous variables, and Chi-square or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

To emulate a target trial comparing short versus long antifungal therapy while accounting for confounding by indication, we used inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on a propensity score. The exclusion of patients dying within 5 days was chosen to remove patients who were not eligible for either treatment strategy under the emulated trial design. This exclusion was necessary to ensure eligibility for both treatment strategies under the target trial framework and avoid immortal time bias. To assess potential selection effects related to the exclusion of early deaths, we compared the baseline characteristics of patients excluded for early death (≤5 days) with those included in the analysis. The propensity score, defined as the probability of receiving short-duration treatment (<10 days), was estimated using a multivariable logistic regression model including the following baseline variables: age, sex (based on medical records), obesity, cirrhosis, diabetes, solid organ transplantation, hematological malignancy, solid tumor, immune-mediated inflammatory disease, neutropenia status, long-term corticosteroids use, temperature and leukocyte count at ICU admission, antifungal treatment during the last 3 months, mechanical ventilation at ICU admission, time from ICU admission to candidemia, surgical reason for ICU admission, the SAPS II score, Candida spp., echinocandin use and suspected source of candidemia. Center was not included in the propensity score model because the inclusion of 16 center-level indicators would have added excessive complexity given the limited number of events in the short-course group (n = 48). However, a sensitivity analysis including center as a categorical variable in the weighted Cox model was performed Stabilized weights were calculated to reduce variability, and extreme weights were truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles to improve stability. Covariate balance before and after weighting was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with values < 0.1 considered indicative of good balance. A Love plot was generated to visualize the balance achieved.

The effect of treatment duration on 90-day mortality was estimated using a weighted Cox proportional hazards model with robust variance estimators to account for the weighting. Results are presented as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Weighted Kaplan–Meier curves were constructed to visualize survival differences between the two groups. No additional adjustment was performed in the weighted Cox model beyond IPTW, as post-weighting adjustment for residual imbalances can introduce over-conditioning. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. The robustness of the findings was further evaluated using time-by-treatment interaction tests and restricted mean survival time (RMST) analysis.

To account for the sequential nature of treatment duration and further address the risk of immortal time bias, we applied a cloning approach with a grace period of 10 days. The 10-day grace period serves as a classification window for treatment duration; clones deviating from their assigned strategy were censored at day 10 (short strategy with ≥10 days) or at treatment discontinuation (long strategy with <10 days), consistent with previous clone-censor-weight applications for static treatment strategies.19 This approach emulates the design of a randomized trial comparing two treatment strategies applied from baseline (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table S1). Cloning is essential to avoid immortal time bias and to allow each patient to be assigned to both treatment strategies, preserving the per-protocol comparison as recommended in the target trial emulation framework.15,16Fig. 2Schematic representation of the target trial emulation design with a 10-day grace period. To assess the robustness of our findings to potential informative censoring, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW). A Cox proportional hazards model was fitted to estimate the probability of remaining uncensored as a function of baseline covariates, using the same set of covariates as in the propensity score model. Stabilized inverse probability of censoring weights were calculated and multiplied by the IPTW weights to obtain final weights. The performance of the censoring model was evaluated using likelihood ratio tests and concordance statistics. Due to the limited predictive ability of the censoring model (likelihood ratio test p = 1.0), indicating that censoring was not strongly predicted by measured covariates, the primary analysis is presented without IPCW adjustment. In addition, to assess the robustness of our findings to the choice of grace period, sensitivity analyses were performed using alternative grace periods of 7 and 14 days, applying the same cloning, censoring, and IPTW methodology as the primary analysis. In addition, we included patients treated for >21 days in the expanded cohort, using the same IPTW and cloning-censoring framework for a sensitivity analysis. Finally, as a sensitivity analysis, we adjusted for center as a categorical variable in the weighted Cox model to assess whether center-level differences influenced our findings. All sensitivity analyses, including alternative grace periods, inclusion of patients treated for >21 days, and center adjustment, were performed as post-hoc analyses.

All analyses were performed on complete cases due to the low proportion of baseline missing data (<5% for all variables).

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.1.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All tests were two-sided, and p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. The reporting of this study follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines20 and the TARGET (TrAnsparent ReportinG of studies Emulating a Target trial) reporting checklist.21

Results

Among the 492 patients initially enrolled in the CandidICU study, 255 were excluded, yielding a final analytic population of 237 patients (48% of the original cohort). The reasons for exclusion are detailed in Fig. 1. The excluded population comprised 122/492 patients (24.8%) who died within the first 5 days, 76/492 patients (15.4%) who received >21 days of therapy (suggestive of complicated candidemia), 41/492 patients (8.3%) who received <5 days of therapy, and 16/492 patients (3.3%) with missing data. Baseline characteristics of the overall population are presented in Supplementary Table S2. Median age was 62 years [IQR 51–70], 159/237 patients (67.1%) were male, and median SAPS II score was 53 [IQR 40–68]. C. albicans was identified in 158/237 patients (66.7%), and echinocandins were the initial antifungal therapy in 200/237 patients (84.4%). Among non-albicans species, Candida glabrata was the most common (35 cases, 14.8%), followed by Candida tropicalis (12 cases, 5.1%), Candida parapsilosis (10 cases, 4.2%), Candida krusei (6 cases, 2.5%), and other species (16 cases, 6.8%). The median time from ICU admission to candidemia onset was 4 days [IQR 0–11]. Patients excluded for early death (≤5 days) had higher baseline severity than those included, with higher SAPS II scores (63 [50–80] vs. 53 [40–68], p < 0.001), and higher rates of cirrhosis (19.7% vs. 9.3%, p = 0.009), renal replacement therapy (54.1% vs. 41.1%, p = 0.026), and ARDS (48.4% vs. 34.2%, p = 0.013) (Supplementary Table S3).

Among the 237 included patients, 189/237 (79.7%) received long-duration antifungal therapy (≥10 days) and 48/237 (20.3%) received short-duration therapy (<10 days). The specification of the emulated target trial is detailed in Supplementary Table S1 and Fig. 2. To account for immortal time bias and confounding by indication, we applied a cloning approach with a 10-day grace period. Each patient was cloned and simultaneously assigned to both treatment strategies at baseline. Clones were censored when their observed treatment deviated from the assigned strategy. This approach yielded a weighted sample of 474 clones (237 patients ×2 strategies), with 378 clones assigned to the long-strategy arm and 96 clones assigned to the short-strategy arm after accounting for deviations.

The propensity score model included 22 baseline covariates and demonstrated acceptable discriminatory performance, with a C-statistic of 0.704 (95% CI 0.614–0.704). The full model, including coefficients, standard errors, odds ratios, and 95% confidence intervals for all variables, is provided in Supplementary Table S4. Covariate balance was substantially improved after inverse probability of treatment weighting (Table 1 and Supplementary Figure S2). After weighting, the maximum SMD was 0.146 for solid tumor, and only two variables had SMD >0.1 (solid tumor: 0.146; SAPS II: 0.124) indicating acceptable but not perfect balance. The SMD for C. albicans was reduced from 0.417 at baseline to 0.108 after weighting. The IPTW weights were well-behaved (mean 1.0, ratio max/min 6.5, Supplementary Figure S3), supporting the stability of the weighted analysis.

Overall, 113/237 patients (47.7%) died within 90 days following candidemia onset. Crude mortality was significantly higher in the short-duration group compared to the long-duration group (38/48 [79.2%] vs. 75/189 [39.7%], p < 0.001), likely reflecting substantial confounding by indication. However, after applying the target trial emulation framework with cloning and inverse probability weighting, this association was substantially attenuated (weighted HR 1.14, 95% CI 0.82–1.58, p = 0.449) (Fig. 3). The proportional hazards assumption was not violated (global p = 0.866), and time-by-treatment interaction tests were not significant (p = 0.864 and p = 0.6). No significant differences were observed for most other secondary outcomes after weighting, including need for mechanical ventilation (89.0% vs. 81.7%, p = 0.226), renal replacement therapy (41.4% vs. 41.5%, p = 0.989), vasopressor use (88.1% vs. 85.5%, p = 0.652), or occurrence of secondary bacteremia (30.8% vs. 31.4%, p = 0.930) or ventilator-associated pneumonia (27.2% vs. 29.4%, p = 0.764).

Varying the grace period to 7 and 14 days yielded results consistent with the primary 10-day analysis: HR = 1.45 (95% CI: 0.82–2.56, p = 0.204) for 7 days, and HR = 1.02 (95% CI: 0.86–1.20, p = 0.847) for 14 days. These findings confirm the robustness of our conclusions across different grace period definitions. Finally, in a sensitivity analysis including patients treated for >21 days, the association between short-course therapy and 90-day mortality remained non-significant (HR 1.13, 95% CI 0.84–1.52, p = 0.427). Noteworthy, the frequency of secondary localizations was higher in patients excluded for treatment duration >21 days compared with those included in the primary analysis (39.5% vs. 16.5%, p < 0.001).

Discussion

In this multicenter cohort of critically ill patients with candidemia, we emulated a target trial comparing short-duration (<10 days) versus long-duration (≥10 days) antifungal therapy. The CandidICU study included 16 French ICUs (both university and non-university hospitals) over an 8-year period, with baseline characteristics consistent with other large ICU candidemia cohorts, supporting the generalizability of our findings to similar critically ill populations. After adjustment using a cloning, censoring, and inverse probability weighting framework, we found no significant association between treatment duration and 90-day all-cause mortality. RMST analysis suggested a trend toward longer survival in the long-treatment group at 30 days (p = 0.094), although this difference was not statistically significant and was attenuated thereafter, consistent with the non-significant HR.

For practicing intensivists, these findings suggest that shorter antifungal therapy may be reasonable to consider in patients who survive the initial phase and show clinical improvement without evidence of deep-seated complications. Then, stratifying candidemia in complicated and uncomplicated cases may help antifungal stewardship strategies to reduce antifungal pressure, toixicity and drug–drug interactions. However, this interpretation must be tempered by the recognition that our study was observational, that residual confounding cannot be excluded, and that the confidence intervals are compatible with clinically important harm. Decisions to shorten therapy should be made on a case-by-case basis and should not alter current clinical practice.

The contrast between crude and weighted analyses highlights the substantial bias that affects conventional observational studies of antimicrobial duration. In unadjusted analyses, short-course therapy appeared strongly associated with higher mortality, but this association was substantially attenuated after accounting for time-dependent processes and confounding by indication. This marked reversal underscores that the crude mortality difference is likely driven by selection effects rather than by a causal effect of shorter therapy. In clinical practice, antifungal therapy may be discontinued early in patients with rapid deterioration, therapeutic limitation decisions, or perceived treatment futility. Analyses that classify exposure solely based on observed duration without addressing these dynamics are therefore vulnerable to immortal time bias and selection bias. Target trial emulation has been proposed to mitigate these “self-inflicted” biases in non-randomized data, and our findings illustrate the magnitude of distortion that may occur when these methods are not applied.

After adjustment, the hazard ratio for 90-day mortality was close to unity. Although the confidence interval does not exclude modest harm or modest benefit, our results do not support a large survival advantage of prolonged antifungal therapy in ICU patients who survive long enough to be eligible for either strategy. Of note, sensitivity analyses using alternative grace periods yielded HRs ranging from 1.02 to 1.45, all non-significant. The higher HR and wider confidence intervals at 7 days suggest instability with shorter grace periods, while the 14-day estimate was closer to the primary analysis. Although point estimates varied, all confidence intervals overlapped with the primary estimate, supporting the robustness of our findings. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence or non-inferiority, but rather as suggesting that extended antifungal exposure may not meaningfully improve survival once major confounding mechanisms are addressed. Nevertheless, residual confounding from unmeasured clinical factors influencing treatment discontinuation, including the reason for shortening therapy, cannot be fully excluded, and our findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Similarly, although covariate balance was acceptable after weighting, modest imbalances persisted for solid tumor (SMD 0.146) and SAPS II (SMD 0.124), which may indicate residual confounding for these variables. Of note, the low proportion of missing data (<5% for all variables) and its distribution across centers and treatment groups suggest that complete-case analysis is unlikely to have introduced substantial bias.

Excluding early deaths is a commonly used approach in target trial emulation when treatment strategies require a minimum exposure duration. This restriction ensures that all included patients are eligible for both strategies at baseline and prevents structural immortal time bias. In the present study, patients excluded for early death (≤5 days) had higher baseline severity, confirming that early deaths occurred in a distinctly more severe population and supporting the methodological appropriateness of the 5-day exclusion. However, this design implies that our results apply to patients who survive the initial phase of candidemia. We also explored adjustment for informative censoring using IPCW. The censoring model showed limited predictive performance, suggesting that censoring was not strongly explained by the measured covariates included in the model. The consistency between IPTW-only and IPCW-adjusted estimates further supports this interpretation. Noteworthy, the IPCW sensitivity analysis yielded a HR of 1.20 (95% CI 0.97–1.47, p = 0.089), but the poor predictive ability of the censoring model (LRT p = 1.0) and instability of the weights (ratio 134.8) suggest caution. We therefore retain the IPTW-only analysis as our primary estimate.

From a stewardship and biological perspective, shorter antifungal strategies may be plausible in selected patients. Along these lines, our results are consistent with recent observational studies suggesting that shorter antifungal strategies may be feasible in selected patients with uncomplicated candidemia.7,14 However, these studies focused on predominantly non-ICU populations, whereas our study specifically addresses critically ill ICU patients with high severity (median SAPS II 53, 82.7% mechanically ventilated), and the generalizability of those findings to this population remains uncertain. Prolonged antifungal exposure carries risks of hepatotoxicity, drug–drug interactions and other adverse events, particularly with azoles.9 Antifungal pressure also contributes to the emergence of resistance, including echinocandin-resistant isolates after relatively brief exposure durations.8,22 Optimizing duration may therefore reduce toxicity and ecological impact without compromising outcomes in appropriately selected cases. Nonetheless, these potential stewardship benefits were not directly measured in our study and remain speculative.

A central concern with abbreviated therapy is the potential undertreatment of metastatic or deep-seated complications. Secondary localizations during candidemia have been reported in ICU cohorts, although incidence estimates vary depending on screening intensity and diagnostic definitions. Ocular involvement, in particular, appears less frequent than historically estimated when systematic definitions are applied.12 However, given limited statistical power for rare events, careful evaluation for deep-seated infection remains essential before considering shorter therapeutic strategies. In all cases, a rapid change of antifungal therapy may be appropriate depending on the first-line treatment and the secondary localization.

Our study specifically addresses critically ill ICU patients, a population underrepresented in ongoing randomized trials evaluating antifungal duration. Along these lines, in the absence of high level evidence dedicated to this high-risk group, structured causal inference approaches applied to multicenter ICU cohorts provide valuable complementary data. In addition, the 10-day threshold was selected as a pragmatic cutoff corresponding to the lower bound of durations observed in our cohort and reflecting the hypothesis that treatment shorter than guideline-recommended durations might be sufficient in selected ICU patients.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, despite the use of target trial emulation, residual confounding cannot be excluded. The validity of our estimates depends on adequate measurement and modeling of baseline confounders, and unmeasured clinical factors influencing treatment discontinuation may persist. In the present study, several clinically important determinants of treatment duration were not captured in our propensity score model, including time to antifungal initiation, microbiologic response, adequacy of source control, infectious diseases management decisions, and the patient's clinical trajectory following candidemia onset. In addition, time to blood culture clearance, which anchors guideline-recommended antifungal duration, was not systematically available in this retrospective multicenter cohort, as its assessment depends on the frequency of blood culture sampling, which varies across centers and clinical practices, and could not be accounted for in the analysis. Further RCTs should address this important point. Moreover, although the propensity score model demonstrated acceptable discrimination (C-statistic 0.704), the inclusion of 22 covariates for only 48 patients in the short-course group raises the possibility of overfitting. Similarly, modest imbalances persisted for solid tumor (SMD 0.146) and SAPS II (SMD 0.124) after weighting, suggesting possible residual confounding.

Second, patients who died within the first five days were excluded to ensure eligibility for either treatment strategy under the emulated design. While methodologically justified, this restriction limits generalizability to patients with fulminant early mortality. However, these patients are not the target of such short-course strategies. Furthermore, an important consideration is the reason for treatment discontinuation as short-course therapy may reflect either clinical improvement or treatment limitation. Our analysis does not distinguish between these scenarios, and potential residual confounding from unmeasured clinical factors (such as persistent hemodynamic instability, persistent fungemia, and adequacy of source control) likely influencing treatment discontinuation cannot be excluded. However, excluding patients who died within the first 5 days reduced the most extreme form of reverse causation.

Third, the 10-day threshold, although pragmatic and corresponding to the lower bound of observed durations, remains arbitrary. Ongoing RCTs evaluate 7 versus 14 days in non-ICU populations, and different cutoffs might yield different estimates, although sensitivity analyses using 7- and 14-day grace periods were consistent.

Fourth, interpretation of secondary outcomes such as length of stay is complicated by the competing risk of death. Additionnaly, our study was not designed as a formal non-inferiority comparison, and the confidence intervals remain relatively wide. Similarly, with only 48 patients in the short-course group, the study had 69% power to detect a 25% absolute mortality difference, reflected in the wide confidence intervals (HR 1.14, 95% CI 0.82–1.58) which are compatible with both clinically important benefit and harm.

Fifth, despite the use of sIPTW and target trial emulation, residual indication bias regarding complicated candidemia cannot be entirely excluded. To address this, we excluded patients treated for >21 days, as such prolonged therapy is highly suggestive of complicated infections (e.g., endocarditis, deep-seated candidiasis) requiring specific management regardless of clinician discretion. This approach allowed us to focus on a more homogeneous population of uncomplicated candidemia. However, this exclusion may introduce selection bias, although the higher frequency of secondary localizations in excluded patients (39.5% vs. 16.5%, p < 0.001) and the sensitivity analysis including these patients (HR 1.13, 95% CI 0.84–1.52, p = 0.427) support the robustness of our findings.

Sixth, the sharp drop in the number at risk in the short-course arm between days 10 and 20 likely reflects the heavy censoring inherent to the cloning approach, as patients assigned to the short strategy who received treatment ≥10 days were censored at day 10. This extreme attrition limits the precision and reliability of survival estimates in the short-course arm beyond the grace period, and the results should be interpreted accordingly.

Finally, and most importantly, our findings should not be used to alter current clinical practice. The confidence intervals are wide and compatible with clinically important harm (up to a 58% relative increase in mortality). Furthermore, our study was observational and subject to residual confounding. Decisions to shorten antifungal therapy should be made only in the context of clinical trials or with careful patient-specific evaluation.

To conclude, in this selected population of ICU patients with uncomplicated candidemia who survived the initial phase and did not have complicated infection requiring prolonged therapy, short-course antifungal therapy was not associated with increased 90-day mortality after rigorous adjustment for confounding and immortal time bias. While these findings do not establish equivalence, they do not indicate a clear survival benefit associated with prolonged antifungal therapy in selected critically ill patients. However, the confidence interval is wide and compatible with both clinically important benefit and harm. These findings are hypothesis-generating and should not alter current clinical practice. Randomized controlled trials specifically targeting ICU populations remain essential to definitively determine optimal antifungal duration strategies.
