Classifying disorders of consciousness using a novel dual-level and dual-modal graph learning model
Authors: Zengxin Qi, Wenwen Zeng, Di Zang, Zhe Wang, Lanqin Luo, Xuehai Wu, Jinhua Yu, Ying Mao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDisorders of consciousness (DoC) are a group of conditions that affect the level of awareness and communication in patients. While neuroimaging techniques can provide useful information about the brain structure and function in these patients, most existing methods rely on a single modality for analysis and rarely account for brain injury. To address these limitations, we propose a novel method that integrates two neuroimaging modalities, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), to enhance the classification of subjects into different states of consciousness.Method and resultsThe main contributions of our work are threefold: first, after constructing a dual-model individual graph using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), we introduce a brain injury mask mechanism that consolidates damaged brain regions into a single graph node, enhancing the modeling of brain injuries and reducing deformation effects. Second, to address over-smoothing, we construct a dual-level graph that dynamically construct a population-level graph with node features from individual graphs, to promote the clustering of similar subjects while distinguishing dissimilar ones. Finally, we employ a subgraph exploration model with task-fMRI data to validate the interpretability of our model, confirming that the selected brain regions are task-relevant in cognition. Our experimental results on data from 89 healthy participants and 204 patients with DoC from Huashan Hospital, Fudan University, demonstrate that our method achieves high accuracy in classifying patients into unresponsive wakefulness syndrome (UWS), minimally conscious state (MCS), or normal conscious state, outperforming current state-of-the-art methods. The explainability results of our method identified a subset of brain regions that are important for consciousness, such as the default mode network, the salience network, the dorsal attention network, and the visual network. Our method also revealed the relationship between brain networks and language processing in consciousness, and showed that language-related subgraphs can distinguish MCS from UWS patients.ConclusionWe proposed a novel graph learning method for classifying DoC based on fMRI and DTI data, introducing a brain injury mask mechanism to effectively handle damaged brains. The classification results demonstrate the effectiveness of our method in distinguishing subjects across different states of consciousness, while the explainability results identify key brain regions relevant to this classification. Our study provides new evidence for the role of brain networks and language processing in consciousness, with potential implications for improving the diagnosis and prognosis of patients with DoC.

Introduction

Disorders of consciousness (DoC) are a group of conditions that affect an individual’s ability to be aware of their surroundings and/or to be able to communicate with others [1]. These disorders can result from various causes, including traumatic brain injuries, stroke, infections, and degenerative neurological conditions. Some common disorders of consciousness include coma, vegetative state, and minimally conscious state. These conditions can be challenging for both the individual and their loved ones, as it can be difficult to determine the extent of the individual’s consciousness and to provide appropriate care and support. Treatment options can vary depending on the level of residual consciousness [2, 3].

Advanced neuroimaging techniques, such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) [4–9] and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) [10–13], can be used to diagnose and evaluate disorders of consciousness. These imaging techniques can provide detailed information about the brain and its function, and can help to identify any abnormalities or damage that may be contributing to the individual’s condition. For example, fMRI can detect brain activity in response to stimuli in vegetative patients, highlighting its potential as a non-invasive method for assessing consciousness [4]. Additionally, a study identified consciousness-specific patterns of brain function by comparing resting-state functional MRI data from awake volunteers, propofol-anaesthetised volunteers, and patients with disorders of cousciousness. The findings suggest that changes in brain integration and functional diversity, detected by fMRI, may serve as a generalizable biomarker for loss of consciousness [6]. Conversely, DTI metrics, such as fractional anisotropy (FA), are sensitive to changes in white matter (WM) integrity. One study found that widespread WM deficits in regions connected to the default mode network (DMN) and thalamus, correlating with clinical assessments of consciousness [11]. Another study indicated that DTI can detect disruptions in the brain’s microstructural integrity, providing insights into the neural basis of consciousness and aiding in the assessment of patients in vegetative state or minimally conscious state [13]. Neuroimaging can also be used to monitor the brain and track changes over time, which can be helpful in determining the prognosis for an individual with a disorder of consciousness. For example, neuroimaging can help to determine if there is any improvement or worsening of the condition, and can provide valuable information to guide treatment decisions. Assessing the depth of covert consciousness in patients with disorders of consciousness can be challenging, as these individuals may have limited ability to communicate and may not be able to produce or comprehend command in the same way as a fully conscious individual. One approach to assessing covert consciousness in these patients is to use behavioral observations, such as looking for nonverbal cues or responses to commands [2, 14]. For example, an individual with a disorder of consciousness may be able to blink or move their eyes in response to a specific word or phrase. Another approach is to use neuroimaging techniques, such as functional MRI (fMRI), to assess brain activity during language tasks [15, 16]. This can provide information about which brain regions are active during language processing and can help to determine the depth of language processing in the individual. Overall, assessing the depth of covert consciousness in patients with disorders of consciousness can be complex and may require a combination of behavioral observations and neuroimaging techniques.

With the development of machine learning, more and more research are utilizing convolutional neural network (CNN) for medical image analysis [17–19]. However, CNNs are only suitable for analyzing Euclidean data that is regular and has a fixed number of neighbors. Moreover, the spatial features that are obtained from CNNs typically have a small neighborhood, especially in shallow CNN models. To address the challenges associated with analyzing non-Euclidean data and to capture long-range dependencies between features, graph neural network (GNN) has been presented [20–22]. GNNs are now widely used in brain network research of fMRI, as the brain can be modeled as a graph with brain regions as nodes and connections between them as edges [23, 24]. There are two types of graph models: individual graphs and population graphs, based on how they define nodes. Individual graphs are more interpretable and can select relevant brain regions, but they ignore inter-subject connections. Population graphs use node features or non-image information to connect nodes. Inter-subject connections may improve classification performance. GNN models can be applied in fMRI data to facilitate diagnosis and prognosis in patients with disorders of consciousness by capturing the dynamic interactions of brain regions that are associated with different levels of consciousness [6]. GNN models can learn how to classify patients into different states of consciousness (such as coma, vegetative state, minimally conscious state, or locked-in syndrome) based on their fMRI patterns [20]. GNN models can also predict the likelihood of recovery or improvement for patients with disorders of consciousness by identifying the brain regions and connections that are crucial for restoring consciousness [3].

Based on the above description, we propose a dual-level and dual-modal graph learning model for the classification of disorders of consciousness. As illustrated in Fig. 1, different from other single-modality methods, our method incorporates resting state fMRI (rs-fMRI) and DTI in the individual graph. The main contributions of our work can be categorized into three key points. First, while many studies have employed dual-modal graphs for brain disorder classification, few have considered brain injury at the graph level. To address this, we introduce a brain injury mask mechanism that consolidates damaged brain regions into a single node within the graph. This innovation enables more effective modeling of damaged brains and reduces the impact of brain deformation. Second, to alleviate the issue of over-smoothing commonly associated with GNN, we dynamically construct a population graph that derives node features from individual graphs. This integration of the population graph with individual graphs promotes the clustering of similar subjects while separating dissimilar ones. Furthermore, we use a subgraph exploration model to validate the interpretability of our model using task-fMRI data. This validation demonstrates that the selected brain regions are indeed task-relevant in cognition other than being arbitrary or unrelated.

Data preprocessing

For rs-fMRI, we used the DPARSF (Data Processing Assistant for Resting-State fMRI) module within DPABI (Data Processing & Analysis for Brain Imaging) toolbox [25] to preprocess fMRI data. The preprocessing procedure is similar to the previous study [26–28], and it mainly includes remove the first 10 volumes, slice timing, realign, normalize to MNI (Montreal Neurological Institute) standard space, smoothing. For task-fMRI, we also preprocessed task-fMRI by DPABI using the same preprocessing procedure with rs-fMRI.

For DTI, we used the FSL (FMRIB Software Library) and PANDA (Pipeline for Analyzing brain Diffusion images) toolbox for preprocessing. The preprocessing procedure mainly includes skull-stripping, segmentation, FSL-based non-linear registration to the MNI stereotaxic space and parcellation. Based on the deformation field generated by FSL, we divide each individual brain volume into 90 brain regions defined in the automated anatomical labeling (AAL) template.

These preprocessing steps were performed to ensure the quality and accuracy of the rs-fMRI and DTI data for subsequent analysis.

Functional connectivity changes observed in rs-fMRI images have been identified as promising biomarkers for the classification of patients in cognitive disorder-related research. Similarly, diffusion indices obtained from DTI can directly reflect the white matter growth of each brain region, where whiter areas typically indicate more complete myelin sheaths and denser fibers. Studies have shown that widespread and inconsistent differences in white matter diffusion indices, particularly fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD), are associated with brain injury. Notably, abnormal DTI indices in white matter regions have also been linked to clinical evaluations of consciousness level. Therefore, in our method, we use traditional image features obtained from FA, comprising of gray features and texture features, as node features. We also incorporate functional connectivity matrix from fMRI as edges to construct individual graphs. This approach considers the unique characteristics of both rs-fMRI and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) while integrating information from both modalities, resulting in more accurate network construction.

Variable topology graph

Based on clinical experience and existing research, it is well-established that most conscious patients have sustained some degree of brain damage, including local deformations and lesions. As outlined in Sect. 2.2, the preprocessing steps for both rs-fMRI and DTI involve registering images to a standard brain template. However, local deformations and lesions can significantly impact the accuracy of registration, leading to inaccurate feature extraction and functional connectivity. Prior studies have attempted to mitigate these issues by registering damaged images to either individual brain templates or intermediate brain templates [29–31]. For example, a custom mid-space template was introduced for spatial normalization, with nonlinear registration methods employed to align each participant’s MRI data to this custom template [30]. However, these methods are computationally expensive, and the use of a specific template or nonlinear registration can introduce bias towards the template’s inherent characteristics, limiting their generalizability to other studies or populations. Unlike these methods, we propose a novel approach that addresses brain deformation at the graph level.

Assuming that we constructed an individual graph using a subject’s brain image as shown in Fig. 5b. In typical brain network models, each individual graph has the same number of nodes, as shown in Fig. 5a. However, in our method, the corresponding graph nodes for subjects with deformations are no longer fixed at 90 (as determined by the AAL template), but are instead reduced to 90-x + 1, where x represents the number of nodes within the deformation region. Specifically, for subjects with brain deformations, we first identify the brain regions with the most significant discrepancies by comparing the registered brain regions to the template. These regions, characterized by the greatest differences, are viewed as deformed regions. Subsequently, we construct a new brain mask in which these deformed regions are treated as a single node. Thus, we create a variable topology graph with 90-x + 1 nodes that incorporates subject-specific deformation information in a straightforward and effective manner, different from conventional brain network model.

An example of a variable topology graph is illustrated below. (a) is the traditional graph construction procedure; (b) presents our innovative variable graph, where the image upper displays the deformation regions and the integration of these regions into a single node is determined by the brain injury mask displayed at the bottom

Importantly, when extracting image features from DTI and calculating functional connectivity matrices from rs-fMRI, different individual graphs were matched with their respective brain template masks, rather than averaging the brain region features and functional connections within the deformed area. Specifically, the brain regions feature commonly are extracted by setting the brain mask of single region as 1, while other regions as 0, then average all the voxel features in the same regions as the final region features. While for the damaged area, we set the brain mask of all the brain regions belong to the deformed area, the voxel features are averaged in all of these voxels to get final feature as the common feature of the deformed area. And the procedure of the functional connectivity is the same. This ensured accurate mapping of the deformed image to its own template space.

Graph learning model with attention mechanism

We first used the spectral GCN to extract the features of each individual graph. Utilizing the message aggregation mechanism of GCN, we aggregate the neighborhood features of each node to obtain hidden features. To identify and retain important nodes while remove less important nodes, we then calculated the self-attention score based on the obtained features to perform self-attention graph pooling. Compared with other pooling methods, self-attention pooling uses graph convolution to select important brain regions, which considers both node features and graph topology.

Each pooling layer will keep nodes with the highest self-attention scores, where is the number of nodes in the layer and is the pooling rate. The node features and adjacency matrix were then updated using these retained nodes. The updated features are sent to Readout layer to get the final graph representation, where maximum pooling and average pooling were applied along the node dimension.

The graph convolution is equivalent to aggregating node information with a distance of from the current node. The final output is obtained as a weighted sum of the inputs , using the normalized attention weights.

To address the issue of increasing similarity between nodes after multiple layers of convolution, which can lead to invalid information interference in classification, we introduced a graph similarity term to improve classification accuracy. Results

Illustrative description of the datasets and experiments for model evaluation

Table 1 displays the clinical data of the 293 participants who were involved in our study. All participants were sourced from our private dataset, and the images were scanned between the years 2010 and 2022. Given that the classification criteria may vary, we divided the participants into three groups, namely NC (normal conscious), MCS (minimally conscious states), and UWS (unresponsive wakefulness syndrome), based on their CRS-R scores. We conducted NC vs. MCS and MCS vs. UWS experiments separately.

To evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed model, we conducted several ablation and comparison experiments using both rs-fMRI and DTI data. Specifically, we performed 7 ablation experiments, including using only rs-fMRI with the time series as node feature and the functional connectivity matrix as edges (with the deformable brain region mask included), using only DTI with FA maps as node features and the probability FN matrix as edges (without the mask, because the FN matrix represents the real connection between different brain regions, we didn’t use the mask here). In this paper, our proposed model consists of three key components: node mask, attention mechanism, and graph similarity. We evaluated various versions of our model in Fig. 7: the basic version, dual-modality(1), lacks node mask, attention mechanism, and graph similarity; dual-modality(2) does not include graph similarity; dual-modality(3) excludes the attention mechanism; dual-modality(4) does not incorporate the node mask; dual-modality(5) represents the complete implementation of our proposed method.

The proposed model can improve the accuracy of classifying brain states from neuroimaging data

To evaluate the outperformance of our proposed model, we compared the result with other models, including M-GCN [21], MSE-GCN [22], and BrainGNN [20]. M-GCN employs the functional connection matrix of fMRI as node features and the structural connection matrix of DTI as connecting edges. However, M-GCN is primarily designed for node classification, so it does not consider the deformed regions as the same node. On the other hand, MSE-GCN calculates the locally weighted polymerization coefficient (LWCC) for both the functional connection matrix of fMRI and the structural connection matrix of DTI. It then determines the similarity of LWCC as the connecting edges, irrespective of the deformed regions being treated as the same node. Meanwhile, BrainGNN calculates the Pearson correlation coefficient for the mean fMRI time series, using it as the node feature, and the partial correlation coefficient as the edge weight between brain regions. Similar to MSE-GCN, BrainGNN does not differentiate the deformed regions as separate nodes. For complete evaluation of the performance, we used the metrics, such as accuracy (ACC in Fig. 7), Precision, Recall, and F1-score to compare different models. Accuracy indicates the proportion of correctly classified samples out of the total samples, Precision indicates the proportion of true positive predictions among all positive predictions, Recall indicates the proportion of actual positives that were correctly identified by the model, F1-Score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, balancing both, AUC (Area Under the Curve) refers to the area under the ROC curve, measuring the model’s ability to distinguish between classes.

Comparison of experimental results for single and dual modality show that using DTI or fMRI alone achieves better results than dual-modality(1), particularly in the NC vs. MCS classification task. This may be due to the relatively simple model we used in dual-modality(1), which cannot fully utilize and learn the complementary information of the two modalities. Ablation experiments were conducted on the two proposed innovations in this study, namely, the attention mechanism and graph similarity. Results indicate that after adding the attention mechanism (dual-modality(1) vs. dual-modality(2)), classification accuracy rates increased by 6% and 5% for the two binary classification tasks, respectively. Furthermore, adding graph similarity (dual-modality(2) vs. dual-modality(5)) led to classification accuracy rates increasing by 11% and 20% for the two binary classification tasks, respectively. This finding indicates that the two proposed innovations have a significant positive impact on classification tasks. In addition, comparing the experimental results of two different graph construction (dual-modality(4) vs. dual-modality(5)) shows that the variable topology composition method proposed in this study improves the accuracy of classification.

Outcome validation based on diagnostic classification

The outcome for patients diagnosed as MCS and UWS is different in terms of recovery and prognosis. Patients with MCS have a better chance of recovery and a more favorable prognosis than patients with UWS, especially if they are in the early stages of their condition. Some studies have shown that patients with MCS can improve their functional outcome and quality of life over time, whereas patients with UWS have a high mortality rate and a low likelihood of regaining consciousness. Therefore, we further tested whether our model-based diagnosis results are related to functional outcomes.

Previous studies have reported the average or median GOS-E scores for UWS and MCS patients at different time points after brain injury. For example, one study reported that the median GOS-E score for UWS patients was 2 (vegetative state) at 1 year after brain injury, while the median GOS-E score for MCS patients was 3 (severe disability) at the same time point [32], and another study reported that the mean GOS-E score for UWS patients was 2.4 (vegetative state) at discharge from hospital, while the mean GOS-E score for MCS patients was 3.5 (severe disability) at discharge [33]. The results of our clinical diagnosis are in line with existing literatures that MCS patients generally have a higher GOS-E score than UWS patients, indicating a better functional outcome and prognosis. Meanwhile, our model showed a high agreement with the clinical diagnosis in predicting the prognosis and outcome of patients with prolonged disorders of consciousness. As shown in Fig. 9, the majority of patients who were diagnosed as UWS by our model (78%) had a poor outcome (GOS ≤ 2), while most of those who were diagnosed as MCS by our model (67%) had a better outcome (GOS = 3). By 3 months after the enrollment, six of nine (67%) patients who were diagnosed as MCS by our model had recovered to a GOS-E score of 3 compared with seven of 32 (22%) patients who were diagnosed as UWS by our model (OR = 7.143, [95% CI 1.414–36.080], p = 0.017). These results suggest that our model can accurately distinguish between UWS and MCS patients based on their brain activity patterns and predict their functional outcome and recovery potential. Our model may therefore provide a useful tool for assisting clinicians in diagnosing and treating patients with disorders of consciousness.

Exploring the interpretability of the model

Interpretability is a desirable property of deep learning models, especially in neuroimaging, where we are often interested in identifying biomarkers underlying brain disorders. However, many models are complex and opaque, making it difficult to interpret their decisions and validate their findings. This would even be worse if the model were trained and tested under rs-fMRI. Rs-fMRI data reflects the spontaneous fluctuations of brain activity without any specific task or stimulus, so it is challenging to infer the cognitive or behavioral relevance of the functional connectivity patterns derived from rs-fMRI data. Moreover, rs-fMRI data is susceptible to various sources of noise and artifacts that may confound the results. Therefore, to increase the interpretability of our model and demonstrate that our results are not purely data-driven we marked the corresponding weights of each brain region in both task-fMRI and rs-fMRI using the proposed model, where the important brain regions was explored by a subgraph exploration model SubgraphX [34]. SubgraphX uses a search-based approach, leveraging Monte Carlo Tree Search to explore the space of possible subgraphs in order to identify the most relevant subgraphs for a given prediction. This method is particularly useful in understanding how different substructures within a graph contribute to the decision-making process of a graph neural network, making it a powerful tool for interpretability in complex graph-based tasks.

Since three separate experiments were conducted following the sequence shown in Fig. 3, we divided the task-fMRI data into three corresponding parts based on the timing of these experiments. After inputting these three task-fMRI segments into the trained model, SubgraphX was employed to identify the most important brain regions contributing to the model’s decision-making process. Figure 10 showed which brain regions contribute most in the diagnostic classification under rs-fMRI. The colorbar in Fig. 10 represents the significance of the data, with blue indicating less important values and a gradient toward red representing increasing importance. As the color transitions from blue to red, it highlights areas of greater relevance, with red denoting the most critical values. We found that the posterior default mode network, the visual network and the language-related network contributed more to the accuracy of prediction under rs-fMRI. Among them, the inferior parietal lobule and the occipital lobe were most prominent, which may be because the visual network had a greater advantage under rest condition. In contrast, under task condition, when patients heard their relatives calling their names, we found that although the pattern of brain regions contributing to the model were similar to those under rest, the language-related superior and middle temporal gyri dominated the pattern and contributed most to the classification result. These regions are more sensitive and specific to auditory language processing than other regions in the language network and are also known to be involved in semantic and syntactic processing of speech, as well as speech comprehension and production [35–37]. Interestingly, when patients heard their relatives calling strangers’ names, the weight of language network did not show any preference in diagnostic classification. Therefore, our results suggest that the proposed model can capture the changes of functional networks related to the decrease of consciousness, which is insusceptible to regions/nodes with brain lesion and can capture the auditory-related and language-related changes of functional networks under auditory task.

Discussion

In this study, we propose and evaluate a dual-level and dual-modal graph learning model for the classification of disorders of consciousness. The model’s superior performance in the diagnosis task can be attributed to the incorporation of deformable masks, attention mechanisms, and graph similarity, surpassing previous graph-based methods and other deep learning techniques. The model was also able to identify the brain regions that were most relevant to the classification results and have been linked to consciousness and task performance. We therefore suggest that this novel classification model is effective, interpretable, and generalizable.

Functional MRI and DTI are two advanced neuroimaging techniques that can be used to diagnose and evaluate in patients with disorders of consciousness after brain injury. fMRI can measure brain activity and connectivity by detecting changes in blood oxygenation, while DTI can measure white matter integrity and structural connectivity by detecting water diffusion along axons. These techniques can provide detailed information about the structure and function of brain networks that are involved in various aspects of consciousness, such as awareness, attention, memory, and language [4, 35, 37–43]. They can also help to identify patients who have covert consciousness, meaning they are conscious but unable to communicate or show behavioral responses [2, 14]. Moreover, they can help to predict the prognosis and recovery of patients with DOC by assessing the extent and location of brain damage and the potential for neural plasticity [3, 33, 44, 45]. However, using fMRI or DTI separately also have some limitations in the diagnosis and evaluation of DOC. First, brain damage and brain lesions can affect the quality and reliability of the neuroimaging data by introducing artifacts, noise, or distortions. Moreover, they are based on assumptions and models that may not fully capture the complexity and diversity of brain networks and consciousness. One way to overcome the limitations of fMRI and DTI in the diagnosis and evaluation of DOC is to use multimodal fusion techniques that can combine information from different neuroimaging modalities, as well as other sources of data, such as clinical characteristics or behavioral assessments. Moreover, multimodal fusion techniques can also use advanced machine learning methods, such as graph neural networks or generative adversarial networks, to minimize the influence of brain lesions and to learn complex and nonlinear patterns that may be hidden in single-modal data. These machine learning methods can also improve the interpretability of the multimodal fusion results by identifying the most relevant features or regions for classification or prediction tasks.

Our results indicate that the proposed model can effectively handle damaged brains, which are often characterized by irregular and sparse connectivity patterns. The results also demonstrate that the proposed model can accurately classify patients into different states of consciousness, such as UWS, MCS, or normal conscious. The model achieved accuracies of 88% and 80% on the two diagnosis tasks, which are significantly higher than previous graph-based methods.

Moreover, the results show that the proposed model can identify brain regions that are most relevant to the classification results and have been linked to consciousness. Previous fMRI studies revealed that hearing a familiar voice calling one’s own name may elicit stronger and more widespread activation in the brain than hearing an unfamiliar voice calling your name [46–48]. This may reflect the greater emotional and semantic significance of a familiar voice, as well as the retrieval of associated memories and knowledge about the person in the perspective of cognition and the content of consciousness [49, 50]. The neuroscience mechanism behind this difference may involve the interaction of several brain regions, such as the temporal voice areas (TVAs) that encode voice identity, the inferior frontal cortex (IFC) that processes voice familiarity, and the medial temporal lobe (MTL) that mediates memory formation and retrieval [51]. The ability to process and recognize voices may depend on the level of awareness and cognitive functions of the listener [46]. For example, voice processing may be impaired in DoC patients, who show reduced or absent responsiveness to external stimuli. However, some studies have suggested that voice processing may persist at a subliminal level in some of these patients, defined as covert consciousness recently, as evidenced by changes in brain activity or autonomic responses when hearing familiar voices [52, 53]. We therefore hypothesized that if our model could identify the brain regions that distinguish familiar from unfamiliar voices, then it would indicate that our model has enough interpretability to explain the neural mechanisms of cognitive processes in patients with impaired consciousness. In our study, by using a subgraph exploration model [34], the model adeptly identified a subset of brain regions exhibiting high scores in the classification results. These brain regions included the default mode network (DMN), the salience network (SN), the dorsal attention network (DAN), and the visual network (VN), which have been previously implicated in various aspects of consciousness, such as self-awareness, attention, memory, and executive function [4, 7, 54, 55]. These regions are also consistent with the findings from previous familiar/unfamiliar voice recognition studies that showed differential activation patterns for familiar and unfamiliar voices in these networks [56–58].

The proposed model is novel in several aspects compared to existing literature. First, it is one of the first studies to focus on the information of brain injury in chronic DoC. While there have been previous endeavors to address the issue of deformation, these approaches typically involve template reassignment, such as utilizing the average template [31] or the intermediate template [30]. In contrast to these methods, our approach directly represents relevant information using diagrams, which offers a simpler and more intuitive solution. Second, it is one of the first studies to use a dual-level and dual-modal graph representation for disorders of consciousness. Previous studies have mainly used single-level or single-modal graph representations, which may limit their ability to capture the complexity and diversity of brain networks. By using a dual-level graph representation, the proposed model can extract both local and global features of brain networks, as well as inter-subject variability. By using a dual-modal graph representation, the proposed model can integrate information from different neuroimaging modalities, such as fMRI and DTI, which can provide complementary insights into brain structure and function. Third, it is one of the first studies to use a visualized model for disorders of consciousness. Previous studies have mainly used fixed or predefined brain regions or connections for classification or prediction tasks. By using a visualized model, the proposed model can automatically select relevant brain regions or connections based on their feature importance and connectivity strength. This can enhance the interpretability and explainability of the model results.

The findings of this study have several implications for theory or practice. For theory, they provide new evidence for the role of brain networks in disorders of consciousness. They suggest that different states of consciousness are associated with different patterns of brain networks and consciousness. They also provide new insights into the neural mechanisms underlying language processing and communication in patients with disorders of consciousness. For practice, they have potential applications for improving the diagnosis and prognosis of patients with disorders of consciousness. They could help clinicians to identify the most appropriate treatment options and interventions for each patient based on their brain network patterns and connectivity strength. They could also help to monitor the patient’s progress and response to treatment over time.

The study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the study only used two neuroimaging modalities, fMRI and DTI, which may not capture all aspects of brain structure and function. Future studies should incorporate other neuroimaging modalities, such as electroencephalography (EEG) or positron emission tomography (PET), which can provide more information about brain activity and metabolism. Second, the study only used a single task-fMRI paradigm to verify the selected brain regions, which may not reflect the full range of cognitive functions that are relevant to consciousness. Future studies should use multiple task-fMRI paradigms to test different domains of cognition, such as memory, emotion, or visual cognition. Furthermore, from the perspective of method, the fusion of fMRI and DTI in our model is simple. We can explore more refined fusion methods that enhance the integration of structural and functional connections, aligning with their intrinsic relationships more effectively. Regarding the generalizability of our model, the number of subjects in our experiment is relatively small compared to current big data standards, so the model’s generalizability remains under-explored. In future work, we aim to collect more patient data to validate the model’s generalizability or explore suitable data augmentation techniques to increase data diversity.

Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a novel graph learning model for classifying disorders of consciousness based on fMRI and DTI data. The model can handle damaged brains, classify patients into different states of consciousness, and identify the relevant brain regions for classification. The model is effective, interpretable, and generalizable for chronic DoC diagnosis. Our study contributes to the literature on disorders of consciousness by addressing the issue of brain injury, using a dual-level and dual-modal graph representation, and providing new evidence for the role of brain networks and language processing in consciousness. Our study also has implications for improving the diagnosis and prognosis of patients with DoC by helping clinicians to identify the best interventions based on their brain network patterns and connectivity strength.
