LINE1 RNA demethylation sensitizes cancer cells to PARPi through global chromatin remodeling
Authors: Zihong Kang, Ruimeng Li, Lei Xu, Yunfan Xiang, Yuhang Qiu, Yue Zhou, Lei Wang, Xuerui Yang, Jun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRepression of DNA damage repair by PARP inhibitors (PARPi) has shown great efficacy in cancer treatment. However, therapy resistance remains a significant clinical challenge.ResultsHere, we demonstrate that the N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification of long interspersed nucleotide element-1 (LINE1, or L1) facilitates DNA damage repair in cancer cells, whereas removal of L1 m6A modifications enhances the sensitivity of both BRCA-wild-type and BRCA-mutant cells to PARPi. Specifically, following olaparib treatment, METTL3 accumulates on chromatin at non-damage sites, which results in increased chromatin accessibility. Knockdown of METTL3 or removal of m6A on L1 RNAs increases H3K9me3 levels, leading to reduced chromatin accessibility. This, in turn, inhibits DNA end resection at damage sites and prevents PARP1 dissociation, ultimately impairing homologous recombination repair and enhancing tumor sensitivity to PARPi.ConclusionsOur findings unveil a novel regulatory mechanism of L1 m6A that governs the DNA damage repair response, providing a potential strategy of targeting METTL3 in combination with PARPi for cancer therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-026-04102-1.

Background

Functional repair of damaged DNA is essential for cancer cell survival and progression, resulting in opportunities for cancer therapy. Poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP), primarily involved in the repair of DNA single-strand breaks (SSBs) [1], has been a key target in anti-tumor drug development. Specifically, PARP binds to SSBs in damaged DNA and catalyzes autoPARylation, loosening the chromatin structure and facilitating the recruitment of DNA repair proteins [2, 3]. PARP inhibitors (PARPi) mainly NAD+ analogs, compete with NAD+ for the PARP active site, thereby inhibiting its catalytic activity and preventing the recruitment of DNA repair proteins [4]. Additionally, the PARP-DNA capture theory posits that PARPi retain PARP on damaged DNA, leading to S phase cell cycle arrest and accumulation of genotoxic DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), ultimately resulting in cell death [4]. Four PARPi (rucaparib, olaparib, niraparib, and talazoparib) have been approved for treating advanced cancers with homologous recombination (HR) defects, for example, BRCA1/BRCA2 mutation [4]. However, not all BRCA1/BRCA2 mutation carriers benefit from this treatment, and PARPi are less effective in BRCA-wild-type (BRCAwt) patients. Most importantly, some patients develop resistance to PARPi. Therefore, further elucidation of how cancer cells mitigate the burden of DNA damage is crucial for enhancing PARPi efficacy across diverse patient populations.

In addition to restoring BRCA1/BRCA2 function, ADP-ribosylation (PARylation), and fork stability, the regulation of epigenetic modifications has also been reported to contribute to PARPi resistance [4, 5]. For instance, histone deacetylation inhibitors can enhance the sensitivity of cancer cells to PARPi by inducing HR deficiency [6, 7]. Recently, RNA modification has emerged as a new layer of epigenetic regulation [8, 9]. As the most abundant internal modification present in multiple RNA species, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification is catalyzed by a large multi-component methyltransferase complex, with the core heterodimer METTL3–METTL14 being the main component [10–13]. The interplay between non-coding RNA m6A methylation and histone modifications can regulate chromatin accessibility and transcription, expanding the functional roles of m6A modification in genome and chromatin regulation [8, 14–17]. Furthermore, METTL3 has been shown to be recruited to DNA damage sites and promote DNA damage repair by increasing the m6A modifications of local RNA [18, 19]. However, it remains elusive whether and how m6A modification may facilitate the adaptation of cancer cells to the perturbed DNA damage responses by PARP inhibition.

Interestingly, in the present study, our reanalysis of CRISPR screening data of multiple cancer cells treated with olaparib suggested that METTL3 may be a key factor regulating cancer cell sensitivity to PARPi. We demonstrated that knockdown or inhibition of METTL3 enhances sensitivity to olaparib, in both BRCA wild-type and mutant colon cell lines. Such METTL3-mediated sensitivity to PARPi primarily relies on the chromatin enrichment of METTL3 and its regulatory function on chromatin accessibility. Mechanistically, we showed that METTL3-mediated sensitivity to PARPi primarily relies on the chromatin enrichment of METTL3 and its regulatory function on chromatin accessibility. Mechanistically, we showed that METTL3-mediated m6A modification of L1PA, a young subfamily of long interspersed nucleotide element-1 (LINE1s, or L1), reduces H3K9me3 levels and promotes global chromatin accessibility after PARPi treatment. Removal of m6A modification of L1PA leads to elevated H3K9me3 levels and reduced chromatin accessibility, which subsequently decreases end resection efficiency at DNA damage sites, hinders PARP1 dissociation, and ultimately impacts HR repair, thereby increasing cellular sensitivity to PARPi. In summary, our study highlights a novel mechanism by which METTL3-mediated L1 RNA modification regulates HR repair through modulating chromatin accessibility. The findings support a promising combined therapeutic strategy for sensitizing cancer cells to PARPi through the inhibition of L1 RNA modifications.

Results

METTL3 knockdown increases cell sensitivity to PARPi in BRCAwt and BRCA−/− cells

To identify genes associated with the adaptation of cancer cells to repressed DNA damage repair, we re-analyzed two independent genome-wide CRISPR (clustered regularly interspersed short palindromic repeats) screening datasets in multiple cancer cell lines treated with olaparib [20, 21]. Our analysis revealed 58 genes whose inactivation increased sensitivity to olaparib in BRCAwt (HeLa) and BRCA-deficient (BRCA−/−, SUM149PT and eHAP MUS81−/−) cancer cell lines but not in the normal cell line RPE1-hTERT (Fig. 1a, Additional file 1: Table S1). As expected, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed significant enrichment of biological processes related to DNA metabolism, DNA replication and RNA metabolism (Additional file 2: Fig. S1a). Protein interactions among these genes revealed a highly connected subnetwork of the DNA repair pathways (Fig. 1a). Interestingly, we also identified a subnetwork involving components of the RNA m6A methyltransferase complex, including METTL3. Drug sensitivity data from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer database indeed showed a negative correlation between METTL3 expression and cell sensitivity to olaparib (Fig. 1b). To test this hypothesis, four cancer cell lines from two cancer types were selected: colon cancer cell lines LOVO (BRCA wild-type) and RKO (BRCA-deficient), along with ovarian cancer cell lines SKOV3 (BRCA wild-type) and OVCAR8 (BRCA-deficient). All cell lines were treated with three distinct PARP inhibitors (PARPi), which confirmed that the BRCA-deficient cells displayed significantly higher sensitivity to PARPi treatment compared with their BRCA wild-type counterparts (Additional file 2: Fig. S1b). We then generated stable METTL3 knockdown LOVO, SKOV3, OVCAR8, and RKO cell lines (Additional file 2: Fig. S1c, d). Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis confirmed that overall mRNA methylation levels decreased following METTL3 knockdown in all four cell lines (Additional file 2: Fig. S1e, f). Additionally, colony formation assays demonstrated that METTL3 knockdown indeed significantly increased sensitivity to olaparib and niraparib in both cell lines (Fig. 1c, d). To determine whether the observed effect depends on m6A methyltransferase activity, wild-type (DPPW) or catalytically inactive mutant (APPA) METTL3 was reintroduced into METTL3 knockdown LOVO and RKO cells (Additional file 2: Fig. S1c). LC–MS analysis revealed that only rescue with wild-type METTL3 restored global m6A levels (Additional file 2: Fig. S1e). Colony formation assays confirmed that only reintroduction of wild-type METTL3 effectively restored PARPi resistance, whereas the mutant failed to do so (Fig. 1c). Similarly, all four cell lines treated with the METTL3 inhibitor STM2457 [22] exhibited increased sensitivity to PARPi (Fig. 1e, Additional file 2: Fig. S1g-i). Notably, METTL3 inhibition, knockdown, or mutation alone did not significantly affect cell proliferation (Additional file 2: Fig. S1j-m). These results provide strong supports to our hypothesis that the METTL3-mediated RNA modification renders resistance of cancer cells to PARP inhibitors.

METTL3 at non-damage sites facilitates HR repair by altering genome-wide chromatin accessibility

Previous studies have reported that both METTL3 and PARP are involved in HR-mediated DSB repair [18]. Particularly, METTL3 is recruited to the DNA damage sites in a PARP-dependent way [23]. Here, our reporter assays showed that HR repair efficiency was significantly reduced upon METTL3 knockdown, while non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) repair efficiency remained unaffected (Additional file 3: Fig. S2a, b). Unexpectedly, we also observed that HR repair efficiency significantly decreased upon METTL3 knockdown in the presence of olaparib (Fig. 2a). Notably, although olaparib treatment reduced the accumulation of METTL3 at the DNA damage sites, METTL3 levels in the chromatin fraction were significantly increased following olaparib treatment in all four cell lines, whereas total METTL3 expression remained unchanged (Fig. 2b, Additional file 3: Fig. S2c-e). Consistently, levels of m6A on non-ribosomal chromatin-associated RNA (caRNA) were significantly increased upon olaparib treatment, whereas total methylation of polyA RNA remained stable (Fig. 2c, Additional file 3: Fig. S2f-h). To further characterize the genomic localization of METTL3, we conducted chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) for METTL3 in wild-type and olaparib-treated LOVO and RKO cells. The results showed that METTL3 binding was consistently and broadly enhanced upon olaparib treatment (Fig. 2d, e, Additional file 3: Fig. S2i). Therefore, PARP inhibition may promote chromatin recruitment of METTL3, leading to increased caRNA methylation. Modification of caRNAs by m6A influences chromatin and transcriptional regulation [14], and chromatin structure plays a crucial role in DNA damage repair, with HR repair favoring a loose chromatin state and NHEJ repair favoring a tighter chromatin structure [24, 25]. We therefore hypothesized that translocation of METTL3 onto chromatin in response to PARPi may regulate HR repair by modulating chromatin structure.

To test this hypothesis, we first explored changes in chromatin accessibility induced by METTL3 knockdown following olaparib treatment. Using a DNaseI-treated terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay, we observed that METTL3 knockdown led to a decrease in global chromatin accessibility in all four cell lines under olaparib treatment. Furthermore, reintroduction of wild-type METTL3, but not a catalytically inactive mutant, restored chromatin openness in METTL3 knockdown LOVO and RKO cells (Fig. 2f, Additional file 3: Fig. S2j). We next conducted transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing (ATAC-seq) of control and METTL3-knowdown LOVO and RKO cells treated with olaparib. In general, the ATAC peaks were largely reduced, again indicating a more closed chromatin state, by METTL3 knockdown in the cells treated with olaparib (Additional file 3: Fig. S2k, l). Strikingly, these reduced peaks are mostly enriched in intronic and intergenic regions (Fig. 2g-i). Furthermore, over half of the intergenic peaks reduced by METTL3 knockdown were located in regions encoding repeat RNAs (Fig. 2j), leading us to speculate that repeat RNAs may play a role in METTL3-mediated regulation of chromatin accessibility.

Indeed, METTL3 knockdown in normal LOVO and RKO cells without olaparib treatment resulted in significant reduction in the total number of ATAC peaks, particularly in intronic and intergenic regions (Additional file 3: Fig. S2m-o). Specifically, much more peaks were closed than those being opened following METTL3 knockdown (Additional file 3: Fig. S2p). These reduced ATAC peaks were predominantly located in intronic and intergenic regions (Additional file 3: Fig. S2q), consistent with the results obtained under olaparib treatment, indicating that METTL3 has a pronounced effect on chromatin accessibility in these regions. Likewise, nearly half of the intergenic peaks diminished by METTL3 knockdown were located in the regions coding repetitive RNAs, suggesting significant roles of the repeat RNA regions in METTL3-mediated regulation of chromatin accessibility (Additional file 3: Fig. S2r).

METTL3 knockdown reduced chromatin accessibility in both control and olaparib-treated cells, prompting us to further investigate the impact of METTL3 knockdown in combination with olaparib on overall chromatin accessibility. We observed that most chromatin regions, regardless of their responses to olaparib treatment alone, showed a pronounced reduction in chromatin accessibility upon METTL3 knockdown in the presence of olaparib (Fig. 2k, Additional file 3: Fig. S2s). As expected, these chromatin regions were again predominantly located on the regions expressing repeat RNAs (Fig. 2l). Notably, most of the chromatin regions opened up by olaparib treatment were subjected to repression upon METTL3 knockdown, indicating that METTL3 knockdown plays a dominant role in repressing global chromatin accessibility when combined with olaparib in LOVO and RKO cells. Therefore, although METTL3 is directly recruited and involved in DNA damage repair mediated by PARP, it also holds an independent function of shaping the landscapes of chromatin accessibility that was adapted to the elevated stress of DNA damage induced by PARPi in cancer cells.

We subsequently performed H3K9me3 ChIP-seq in LOVO and RKO cells and observed a global increase in H3K9me3 levels following METTL3 knockdown (Fig. 3d, e, Additional file 4: Fig. S3n). Such increases are enriched in intronic and intergenic regions (Fig. 3f), and most of the increases in intergenic H3K9me3 peaks overlapped with repeat elements (Fig. 3g). These results agree with the genomic locations of ATAC peaks that were reduced following METTL3 knockdown (Fig. 2g, j, Additional file 3: Fig. S2q, r). This observation is further supported by a negative correlation between changes in chromatin accessibility and H3K9me3 levels upon METTL3 knockdown, especially in regions encoding repetitive RNAs, highlighting the critical role of repeat RNAs in METTL3-mediated regulation of H3K9me3 histone modification and chromatin accessibility (Fig. 3h, Additional file 4: Fig. S3o, p). Importantly, the chromatin regions that were closed following METTL3 knockdown are indeed enriched with H3K9me3 marks (Fig. 3i). Overall, these results indicate that reductions in chromatin accessibility induced by METTL3 knockdown could be attributed to increased H3K9me3 modification.

LINE1 RNAs are subjected to m6A modification by METTL3

Given the role of METTL3 as the main m6A writer protein [26], we next sought to investigate the role of RNA m6A modifications in METTL3-mediated H3K9me3 and chromatin regulation under the context of treatment with PARPi. Given increased m6A modification of caRNAs but not total RNA (Fig. 2c, Additional file 3: Fig. S2f-h) and elevated chromatin-bound METTL3 expression (Fig. 2b, Additional file 3: Fig. S2d, e) in response to olaparib treatment in all four cell lines, we hypothesized that certain m6A-modified RNAs associated with chromatin might contribute to METTL3-dependent regulation of chromatin accessibility following treatment with PARPi. We therefore performed m6A-methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-seq) on the non-ribosomal caRNAs in LOVO and RKO cells upon METTL3 knockdown (Additional file 5: Fig. S4a). As expected, more hypomethylated m6A peaks were observed upon METTL3 knockdown in both cell lines, with the canonical m6A motif "GGACU" enriched in all samples (Additional file 5: Fig. S4b, c).

Interestingly, the data further revealed a negative correlation between changes in m6A levels and alterations in H3K9me3 binding at the m6A peak locations upon METTL3 knockdown in LOVO and RKO cells (Additional file 5: Fig. S4d, e), suggesting regulation of H3K9me3 by RNA m6A, potential via cis functions. Specifically, in the case of chromosome-associated regulatory RNA species, MeRIP-seq data showed that the hypomethylated peaks upon METTL3 knockdown were predominantly enriched in genomic regions encoding repeat RNAs, particularly in LINE1 RNA regions (Fig. 4a, Additional file 5: Fig. S4f), indicating that LINE1 RNAs are major substrates of METTL3. m6A-marked L1PA RNA subfamily regulates chromatin states

LINE1 constitutes 17% of the human genome, and its overexpression is associated with apoptosis, cell plasticity, and even tumor progression [27–29]. LINE1 also regulates chromatin accessibility [8, 14]. We therefore hypothesized that m6A modification on LINE1 RNAs might affect HR repair by modulating chromatin state through H3K9me3. Indeed, after METTL3 knockdown, H3K9me3 levels at m6A-marked LINE1 RNA loci were significantly increased (Fig. 4b), and this change was negatively correlated with m6A levels on LINE1 RNAs (Fig. 4c). Furthermore, compared with the other four repeat families—Alu, ERVL-MALR, low-complexity, and simple repeats—which all showed a high proportion of hypomethylated peaks in both LOVO and RKO cells, only L1 elements exhibited a consistent decrease in chromatin accessibility in both cell lines following OLA treatment and METTL3 knockdown (Fig. 4d, Additional file 5: Fig. S4i). Together, these results suggest that LINE1 RNA methylation plays a role in METTL3-mediated chromatin regulation, potentially via H3K9me3 modification.

The functions of LINE1 are closely related to their evolutionary age [30, 31]. Interestingly, we observed that evolutionarily younger LINE1 RNAs [32] were more prone to m6A modification and exhibited a more pronounced distribution of hypomethylated peaks in response to METTL3 knockdown (Fig. 4e, Additional file 5: Fig. S4j). MeRIP-qPCR assays confirmed that METTL3 knockdown significantly reduced m6A levels in the young L1PA subfamily in LOVO and RKO cells (Additional file 5: Fig. S4k). Notably, these younger LINE1 subfamilies exhibited higher m6A levels and RNA abundance in control cells, which was accompanied by an increase in H3K9me3 levels and a stronger reduction of m6A levels following METTL3 knockdown (Fig. 4f, Additional file 5: Fig. S4l). Consistently, we observed reduced chromatin accessibility at these hypomethylated L1PA RNA loci (Fig. 4g). In line with this, olaparib treatment enhanced METTL3 binding at L1PA elements, accompanied by an increase in chromatin accessibility (Fig. 4h, i, Additional file 5: Fig. S4m, n). In contrast, METTL3 knockdown led to a decrease in chromatin accessibility (Additional file 5: Fig. S4n). Together, these results suggest a regulatory role of methylated L1PA in chromatin accessibility.

To further investigate whether the regulation of chromatin accessibility mediated by L1PA RNA methylation is involved in HR repair, we designed and synthesized ASOs targeting L1PA RNA and confirmed thier effectiveness in reducing L1PA expression. We then constructed L1PA overexpression plasmids carrying either wildtype or m6A site-mutated (m6A-deficient) sequences and performed rescue experiments in the context of L1PA RNA knockdown (Additional file 5: Fig. S4o). The results showed that L1PA RNA knockdown led to reduced global chromatin accessibility (Additional file 5: Fig. S4p), as accompanied by a significant increase in overall H3K9me3 levels, particularly at Olaparib treatment-induced DNA damage sites of interest. Rescue with wild-type L1PA effectively restored these effects; however, rescue with the m6A site-mutated L1PA RNA only partially restored chromatin accessibility and reduced H3K9me3 levels (Additional file 5: Fig. S4q, r). These new data strongly suggest that the m6A modification of L1PA RNA is essential for maintaining the chromatin state. While the physical presence of L1PA RNA is necessary, its full chromatin remodeling activity specifically depends on the presence of m6A modification.

Next, we used the CRISPR-dCas13b-FTO system to remove m6A from specific RNA sequences (Additional file 6: Fig. S5a). With specific guide RNAs (gRNAs) targeting the consensus sequence of L1PA RNAs in LOVO and RKO cell lines, we found that dCas13b-FTO treatment recapitulated the phenotypic changes induced by METTL3 knockdown. Specifically, dCas13b-FTO‑mediated m6A demethylation of L1PA RNA directly led to increased H3K9me3 modification levels. Under olaparib treatment conditions, this targeted demethylation further resulted in reduced chromatin accessibility, a sustained rise in H3K9me3 levels, and significantly decreased homologous recombination efficiency (Fig. 4j-n, Additional file 6: Fig. S5b-d). To enhance the universality and clinical translational value of these findings, we performed MeRIP-seq analysis of chromatin-associated RNA. The results showed that following METTL3 knockdown, the proportion of m6A modification on L1 element was most significantly reduced in both ovarian cancer cells, mainly including L1PA3, L1PA4, L1PA5, and L1PA7 (Additional file 6: Fig. S5e-h). Targeted removal of m6A on L1PA using the dCas13b-FTO system successfully recapitulated the METTL3 knockdown phenotype, including reduced chromatin accessibility, elevated H3K9me3 levels, enhanced histone modifications at damage sites, and increased DNA damage (Additional file 6: Fig. S5i-n). Additionally, compared with genes far from L1PA5 RNA loci with hypo-m6A peaks, METTL3 knockdown resulted in increased H3K9me3 levels at the promoter regions of genes near hypomethylated L1PA RNAs (Additional file 6: Fig. S5o). These genes are enriched in pathways related to homology-directed repair and pyrimidine/purine salvage (Additional file 6: Fig. S5p). Together, these results strongly support the hypothesis that m6A methylation of the L1PA subfamily regulates chromatin state via H3K9me3 histone modification and plays a regulatory role in HR efficiency.

Impairment of HR repair and protein trapping lead to ineffective DNA damage repair and accumulation of lesions, which may cause replication fork stalling during DNA replication and trigger apoptosis [33–35]. Indeed, following olaparib treatment, all four cell lines exhibited more severe DNA damage upon METTL3 knockdown, STM2457 treatment, or dCas13b-FTO targeting (Fig. 5e-g, Additional file 7: Fig. S6g-o). Rescue experiments in LOVO and RKO cells demonstrated that only reintroduction of wild-type METTL3, but not the catalytically inactive mutant, reduced DNA damage levels (Additional file 7: Fig. S6p-r). Subsequent measurement of DNA replication speed further confirmed that METTL3 knockdown, STM2457 treatment, or dCas13b‑FTO targeting significantly slowed DNA replication progression (Fig. 5h, i, Additional file 7: Fig. S6s, t).

Finally, the accumulation of DNA damage and impaired DNA replication can profoundly restrict tumor growth. Therefore, with a colorectal cancer (CRC) organoid model, we tested the anti-tumor effect of PARPi and METTL3 inhibition in combination. The results showed that the treatment of olaparib and STM2457 together significantly inhibited the growth of CRC organoids, showing a marked reduction in viability compared to olaparib treatment only (Fig. 5j, k, Additional file 7: Fig. S6u). Altogether, these results suggest the potential of combining L1PA demethylation with PARP inhibitors as a new cancer therapeutic strategy via synthetic lethality.

Discussion

PARP inhibitors (PARPi) represent a promising class of targeted therapy that has demonstrated significant efficacy in treating various cancers, particularly those with deficiencies in DNA damage repair. However, the emergence of resistance to these therapies poses a major clinical challenge. Our study reveals that the METTL3-mediated RNA m6A modification plays a critical role in alleviating the repressive effect of PARPi on cancer cells, potentially via regulation of the chromatin accessibility. We demonstrate that treatment with the PARPi olaparib leads to accumulation of METTL3 on chromatin and enhanced RNA modification, especially m6A modification on LINE1. This process inhibited the establishment of H3K9me3, thereby increasing chromatin accessibility. Loosening of chromatin improves the efficiency of DSB end-resection and facilitates the dissociation of PARP1 from DNA, ultimately enhancing HR repair and reducing overall DNA damage (Fig. 6). Therefore, attenuating the machinery above by inhibiting or knocking down METTL3 restores cancer cell sensitivity to PARPi, demonstrating that METTL3 may serve as a potent target to overcome drug resistance by generating a new synthetic lethality together with PARPi for cancer therapy. Our study thereby opens new therapeutic avenues to improve patient outcomes. Future research should continue to explore the roles of METTL3 and its applications in cancer therapy to achieve the goals of personalized and precision medicine.Fig. 6Mechanism underlying how m6A-modified L1PA RNAs regulate PARPi sensitivity in cancer cells

Mechanism underlying how m6A-modified L1PA RNAs regulate PARPi sensitivity in cancer cells

Dysregulation of the DNA damage repair mechanism is crucial for cancer initiation and progression. Cancer cells are subjected to frequent DNA damage, and therefore they leverage a series of signaling pathways to initiate DNA damage repairs to sustain cell viability. Although it has been reported that METTL3 can sense DNA damage signals and rapidly localize to damage sites in a PARP-dependent manner to participate in HR repair [18, 23], our findings reveal an additional PARP-independent pathway through which METTL3 contributes to DNA damage repair and thereby desensitize tumor cells to PARPi. Specifically, following PARPi treatment, METTL3 accumulates on chromatin at non-damage sites in a PARP-independent manner, facilitating DNA damage repair through increased caRNA modification, reducing H3K9me3 levels, and enhancing chromatin accessibility. Our study highlights an intriguing crosstalk between epigenetic modifications and DNA damage repair in tumor cells under drug-induced stress. Further investigation is warranted to elucidate the context-dependent roles of these two distinct mechanisms by which METTL3 participates in DNA damage repair, as well as to determine whether any interaction exists between these processes.

LINE1 elements are the most prevalent repeated sequences in the human genome, which comprise 17% of human DNA. [27] Previous studies have shown that m6A-marked LINE1 RNAs play a crucial role in shaping the chromatin state and regulating transcription in a retrotransposition-independent manner in mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) and early embryonic development [8, 14, 36]. It has been reported that m6A modification promotes the expression and retrotransposition of LINE1 RNA in leukemia cells [30]. However, the functions of m6A-marked LINE1 RNAs in human cells remain poorly understood. In our study, using BRCA wild-type and mutant colon cell lines, we uncovered that m6A-marked L1PA, a young subfamily of LINE1, plays a previously unrecognized role in promoting HR repair by increasing global chromatin accessibility via repression of H3K9me3 histone modification after PARPi treatment. Specific removal of m6A modifications on L1PA enhances cancer cell sensitivity to PARPi, highlighting the critical roles of LINE1 RNAs in cancer and its potential as a therapeutic target. This is a new expansion of the multivalent functions of repeat RNAs. Whether other types of repeat RNA or those with diverse modifications possess similar critical functions warrants further investigation in the future.

Conclusions

This study elucidates a novel mechanism by which METTL3-mediated RNA m6A modification promotes PARP inhibitor resistance through regulating chromatin accessibility. We demonstrate that PARPi treatment induces METTL3 accumulation on chromatin and enhances m6A modification of LINE1 RNAs, particularly the L1PA subfamily. This epigenetic remodeling suppresses H3K9me3 deposition, thereby increasing chromatin accessibility, facilitating DNA end resection and PARP1 dissociation, and ultimately enhancing homologous recombination (HR)-mediated repair while reducing DNA damage levels. Notably, in both BRCA wild-type and mutant colorectal cancer cells, METTL3 inhibition or selective depletion of m6A on L1PA restores PARPi sensitivity. Our findings uncover a previously unrecognized PARP-independent role of METTL3 in DNA damage repair and expand the understanding of repetitive RNA functionality in cancer. These findings provide a promising therapeutic target to overcome PARPi resistance, highlighting the critical crosstalk between epitranscriptomic regulation and DNA repair pathways. Further investigation into this mechanism may advance precision oncology strategies.

Methods

Cell culture

All cell lines used in this study were obtained from the ATCC and maintained in our laboratory. LOVO (RRID: CVCL_0399), RKO (RRID: CVCL_0504), SKOV3 (RRID:CVCL_5J11), OVCAR8 (RRID:CVCL_1629), and HEK293T (RRID: CVCL_0063) cell lines were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, 16,600) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, 15,140), and 1% glutamine (Meilunbio, MA0155) and grown at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Mycoplasma testing was performed using Lonza detection kits, and all cell types were confirmed to be negative.

Stable knockdown cell lines were constructed by inserting the corresponding short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequence for the target gene into the genome to form shRNAs for RNA interference. To generate lentiviral particles, psPAX2, pMD2.G, and corresponding shRNA plasmid were cotransfected into 293 T cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (11,668,019, Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The transfection mixture was replaced with fresh medium 6 to 8 h after transfection. Forty-eight hours after transfection, the viral supernatants were collected, sterile filtered, and concentrated by precipitating with PEG8000. The cells were then transduced by incubating with appropriate quantities of lentiviral particles and treated with 1 μg/mL puromycin to select for stable knockdown cell lines. Specifically, for dCas13b-FTO-mCherry stable cell lines, the lentivirus was produced by cotransfection of packaging plasmid (delta 8.9), envelop plasmid (VSV-G), and pLenti-dCas13b-FTO-mCherry plasmid. Stable cell lines were sorted by flow cytometry using a BD Aria Fusion following DOX induction. The primers used for constructing dCas13b-L1-gRNA plasmids are listed in Table S2. NHEJ and HR plasmids were gifts from Dr. Zhiyong Mao.

Drug sensitivity analysis

CellMiner (https://discover.nci.nih.gov/cellminer) was used to retrieve RNA-seq expression data and compound activity information for the National Cancer Institute 60 cell line.

Colony formation assay

Cells were inoculated and cultured in six-well cell culture plates at a density of 100 cells per well for 7—10 days. The medium was removed, and cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 20 min. After washing several times with PBS, the number of clones was counted.

Cell proliferation

Cells in the logarithmic growth stage were collected and inoculated at ~ 2,000 cells per well in 96-well cell culture plates. After culturing for 24 h, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution (MA0218, Meilunbio) was added to each well, and the cells were incubated at 37 ℃ for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Cell Imaging Multimode Reader (BioTek Cytation5, Agilent).

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Cells were collected, and RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (15596018CN, Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. A RiboMinus transcriptome isolation kit (Invitrogen) and Dynabeads mRNA Purification kit (Invitrogen) were used separately to isolate mRNA and nonribosomal RNA from total RNA following the manufacturer’s protocols. cDNA was then obtained by reverse transcription of RNA with HiScript III RT SuperMix (Vazyme). RT–qPCR was performed on a LightCycler 480 II system (Roche) using Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q712, Vazyme) and GAPDH as the internal reference gene. Changes in relative gene expression were calculated using the change in cycling threshold (ΔΔCt) method.

Western blots

Cell protein extraction was performed using RIPA buffer containing 1% phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, and protein concentration was measured using a bicinchoninic acid kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein lysates were mixed with 4 × SDS loading buffer and boiled at 98 °C for 10 min. Denatured protein samples were loaded into 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010). Membranes were then blocked with 5% nonfat milk in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) at room temperature (RT) for 1 h with gentle shaking. The membranes were then washed three times in TBST and incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 ℃. After washing three times with TBST, membranes were incubated with species-specific secondary antibodies at RT for 1 h with gentle shaking. Protein bands were developed using a CCD (Tanon) imager and ECL detection kit.

LC–MS/MS quantification of m6A

Twenty nanograms of mRNA or nonribosomal RNA was digested with 10 mM NH4AC (pH 5.3) containing 1 U of nuclease P1 at 42 °C for 2 h, and then 1 U of rSAP enzyme and 2 μL of 10 × rCutsmart buffer were added and incubated at 37 °C overnight. The nucleosides were separated by reverse-phase ultraperformance LC on a C18 column and detected using a triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer in positive ion multiple reaction monitoring mode. Nucleosides were quantified by retention time and nucleoside-to-base ion mass transitions (268 to 136 for A and 282 to 150 for m6A). Quantification was performed by comparing with standard curves obtained from pure nucleoside standards from the same batch of samples. Levels of m6A were calculated as the ratio of m6A to A/U/C/G according to the calibration concentration.

HR and NHEJ efficiency detection

HR and NHEJ plasmids were transfected into the corresponding cell lines using Lipofectamine 2000, and HR and NHEJ cell lines were obtained by G418 screening. Cells were placed into six-well plates 24 h in advance and transfected with related plasmids according to the experimental requirements. Fresh medium was replaced 48 h later and transfected with I-SceI expression plasmid. Forty-eight hours later, the cells were collected, and the proportion of GFP+ cells in different samples was analyzed by flow cytometry.

Cell fractionation

106 cells were lysed with 200 μL of ice-cold lysis buffer on ice for 5 min. The cell lysate was then gently pipetted with the same volume of sucrose cushion and centrifuged at 4 °C and 15,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was the cytoplasmic component. The nuclear component was gently washed with 200 μL of ice-cold PBS/1 mM EDTA three times. The nuclear pellet was resuspended in 50 μL of prechilled glycerol buffer, with gentle flicking of the tube, and 200 μL of cold nuclei lysis buffer was added and vortexed vigorously for 20 s. Nuclear pellet mixtures were incubated for 2 min on ice and centrifuged at 4 °C and 15,000 × g for 2 min. The supernatant was collected as the nucleoplasm, and the pellet was gently washed with cold PBS/1 mM EDTA three times. The chromosome-associated fraction was then collected.

ATAC-seq

ATAC-seq was performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 1 × 105 cells were collected and washed twice with PBS buffer. Libraries were constructed and sequencing was performed using a VAHTS Hyperactive ATAC-Seq Library Prep kit for Illumina.

ATAC-seq data analysis

Paired-end sequencing reads were first trimmed using Trim Galore (v0.6.6) to remove adapter sequences and low-quality nucleotides [37]. Clean reads were then aligned to the human genome (hg38) using Bowtie2 (v2.4.2) [38]. Reads aligned to the mitochondrial genome were removed as well as PCR duplicated reads. ATAC-seq peaks were called using macs2 (v2.2.7.1) callpeak, and only peaks that overlapped in two biological replicates were used for downstream analysis with “bedtools intersect -f 0.5 -F 0.5 -e” (v2.26.0) parameters [39, 40]. To identify differentially closed/open ATAC-seq peaks, Diffbind (v3.0.15) was used to perform differential analysis [41]. Mapped reads were converted into bigwig format using Deeptools (v3.5.0) bamCoverage [42]. Heat maps and profile plots were generated using Deeptools (v3.5.0) computeMatrix, plotHeatmap, and plotProfile [42]. The distribution of ATAC peaks in genomic regions was analyzed using Homer (v4.11) [43].

ChIP-qPCR and ChIP-seq

107 cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde at RT for 10 min, and cross-linking was stopped by adding glycine at a final concentration of 0.125 M. The cells were then resuspended in 1 mL of buffer and cleaved on ice for 10 min. The supernatant was retained, and the precleared lysate was mixed with antibody-coated magnetic beads and rotated overnight at 4 °C. The supernatant was saved on the second day and washed twice with 1 mL of SDS lysis buffer, twice with 1 mL of high-salt wash buffer, twice with 1 mL of LiCl buffer, and once with TE buffer. The beads were then resuspended in 240 µL of elution buffer (100 mM NaHCO3 and 1% SDS) and shaken at 30 °C for 1 h. The supernatant was saved, 14.4 µL of 5 M NaCl was added, and the samples were shaken at 65 °C for 4 h. After adding 4 µL of RNase A, samples were shaken at 37 °C for 15 min. Four microliters of proteinase K was added, and samples were shaken at 65 °C for 2 h. DNA was purified using DNA Clean & Concentration Kit with 5 × binding buffer. Purified DNA samples were used as templates for ChIP-qPCR analysis or were prepared for sequencing using a VAHTS Universal Pro DNA Library Prep kit for Illumina.

ChIP-seq data analysis

Low-quality bases and adapters were trimmed using Trim Galore (v0.6.6) [37], and trimmed reads were aligned to the human genome (hg38) using Bowtie2 (v2.4.2) [38]. PCR duplicates were removed using picard (v2.26.0) (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/) MarkDuplicates. H3K9me3 peaks were called using Homer (v4.11) [43]. METTL3 peaks were called using macs2 (v2.2.7.1) callpeak. Only peaks that overlapped in two biological replicates were used for downstream analysis with “bedtools intersect -f 0.5 -F 0.5 -e” (v2.26.0) [40] parameters. To identify differential peaks, featureCounts (v2.0.1) [44] and DESeq2 (v1.30.1) [45] were used to perform differential analysis. Mapped reads were converted into bigwig format using Deeptools (v3.5.0) [42] bamCoverage with parameters “–normalizeUsing RPKM”. Heat maps and profile plots over peaks, LINE1 RNA loci, and other genomic regions were generated using Deeptools (v3.5.0) [42] computeMatrix, plotHeatmap, and plotProfile or custom R scripts. The distribution of peaks in genomic regions was analyzed using Homer (v4.11) [43].

m6A-IP and RT-qPCR to quantify RNA methylation

We performed m6A-IP enrichment and RT-qPCR to quantify changes in the methylation of specific target genes. One microliter of 1:100 diluted m6A and non-m6A spike-in from an EpiMark N6-Methyladenosine Enrichment kit (New England Biolabs, E1610S) was added to 1 µg of nonribosomal RNA or mRNA. m6A-IP was then performed using an EpiMark N6-Methyladenosine Enrichment kit according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Purified RNA samples were used as template for RT-qPCR, with the spike-in used as a reference gene.

caRNA m6A-seq

caRNA were isolated from LOVO, RKO, SKOV3 and OVCAR8 cells. One microliter of 1:1,000 diluted m6A and non-m6A spike-in from an EpiMark N6-Methyladenosine Enrichment kit (New England Biolabs, E1610S) was added to 1 μg of purified nonribosomal RNA or mRNA, followed by fragmentation according to previously published protocols[14]. m6A-IP was performed using an EpiMark N6-Methyladenosine Enrichment kit according to the manufacturer’s protocols. caRNA m6A-seq data analysis

Low-quality bases and adapters were trimmed using Trim Galore (v0.6.6) [37], and ribosomal RNAs were removed by Bowtie2 (v2.4.2) [38]. The remaining reads were aligned to the human genome (hg38) along with spike-in genomes, including two control RNAs (one with m6A modifications and one without; New England Biolabs, E1610S), using HISAT2 (v2.1.0) [46] with “–rna-strandness RF” parameters. Strand-specific reads were separated using samtools view (v1.9) [47] with flags 99, 147, 83, and 163. m6A peak calling was performed using macs2 (v2.2.7.1) [39] with “–keep-dup 5” parameters. m6A peaks that overlapped in two biological replicates were used for downstream analysis using “bedtools intersect -f 0.5 -F 0.5 -e -s” (v2.26.0) [40] parameters. The distribution of m6A peaks in genomic regions and consensus motifs were analyzed using Homer (v4.11) [43]. Total m6A levels for each sample were calculated by dividing the number of reads aligned to the human genome by the number of reads aligned to the m6A-modified spike-in. m6A levels for each replicate were defined as the ratio of the IP sample to the input sample. m6A-normalized factors of enrichment were quantified with the m6A level values divided by the average m6A level value of control samples.

Definition and quantification of chromatin-associated regulatory RNAs (carRNAs)

We identified carRNAs, including repeat RNAs and enhancer RNAs, based on previous work [14]. Annotation of repeat RNAs for human genome assemblies (hg38) was downloaded from the UCSC Table Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu/cgi-bin/hgTables). H3K27ac peaks were analyzed by public H3K27ac ChIP-seq in LOVO and RKO cells (from GSE136888 [48] and GSE126188 [49]) using Homer (v4.11) [43] with “findPeaks -style factor -size 1000” parameters.

FeatureCounts (v2.0.1) [44] was used to quantitate read counts on m6A peaks and LINE1 RNAs, and only those with more than ten reads in two input samples were retained for downstream analysis. m6A levels for each LINE1 RNA and peak were calculated using the formula (CPMIP + 0.01)/(CPMInput + 0.01). m6A-normalized factors were then multiplied by (CPMIP + 0.01)/(CPMInput + 0.01). Differentially m6A-methylated RNAs and peaks were identified by changes (shMETTL3/shControl) in normalized m6A levels with an m6A-normalized factor of > 1.5-fold (|log2 (fold change)|> 0.58).

m6A-marked carRNAs were defined as those with m6A peaks. To compare the percentage of hypo-m6A peaks distributed at different RNA species or various LINE1 subfamilies, “bedtools intersect” (v2.26.0) [40] was used to assign hypo-m6A peaks (i.e., peak.bed) to the LINE1 RNAs (i.e., L1.bed).

DNase I TUNEL experiment

LOVO, RKO, SKOV3 and OVCAR8 cells were immobilized with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, washed three times with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min before digesting with 0.2 U/mL DNase I.

Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy

Cells were seeded on slides, incubated for 24 h, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, washed with PBS three times, and treated with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min. After washing with PBS three times, samples were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) containing 0.1% Tween 20 for 30 min, and primary antibody was added and incubated at 4 ℃ overnight. On the second day, secondary antibody was added and incubated at RT for 2 h. DAPI was added to label cell nuclei, and slides were sealed. Cells were imaged using Dragonfly and Leica fluorescence microscopy.

Detection of DSB end resection efficiency

In end resection experiments, corresponding NHEJ cells were transfected with I-SceI expression plasmid. After 16 h, the cells were collected, and genomic DNA was extracted. Three double-strand-specific restriction enzyme recognition sites (NsiI, DraIII, and BspHI) were located at different locations from the I-SceI site (43, 197, and 621 bp, respectively). The main principle of determining the efficiency of terminal excision is that after the I-SceI expression plasmid cuts the I-SceI recognition site on the genome to produce DSBs, the end of the DSB will undergo 5′ chain degradation. If the end excision length of the 5′ chain exceeds a certain restriction enzyme recognition site, this site will become an ssDNA region so that it cannot be degraded by the corresponding restriction enzymes. The samples were then divided into two parts: one part was subjected to nuclease enzyme digestion (double-stranded DNA containing the above three restriction enzyme recognition sequences was cut, and the rest was ssDNA containing the corresponding restriction enzyme recognition sequences), and the other was treated with equal amounts of water (contains all single- or double-stranded DNA at the above restriction enzyme recognition sites). DNA purification was performed 4 h after enzyme digestion at 37 °C, and purified samples were used for qPCR analysis. Ct values of the same sample with and without enzyme digestion were recorded, and proportions of ssDNA were calculated as follows to reflect the end resection efficiency: ssDNA% = 100/(1 + 2△Ct)/2. We can know the proportion of ssDNA near the DSB of this sample and then reflect the efficiency of end resection at the corresponding position [50].

CRISPR-dCas13b L1 sgRNA design

gRNA design was performed using a multi-step bioinformatic pipeline to ensure specificity for hypomethylated L1 RNAs. First, L1 transcripts showing both significant reduction in m6A levels and detectable expression were identified from MeRIP-seq data. Full-length sequences of these candidates were extracted and subjected to multiple sequence alignment with MAFFT to generate a consensus sequence. Focusing on the L1PA3, L1PA4, L1PA5, and L1PA7 subfamilies—which exhibited the most pronounced m6A loss—individual sequences were aligned against the consensus. Regions with high sequence similarity were selected as candidate targeting domains, within which gRNAs for the dCas13b-FTO system were designed. To assess potential off-target effects, a genome-wide localization analysis was conducted. All designed gRNA sequences were aligned against the hg38 reference genome using BWA-MEM. The genomic context of each aligned region was annotated using BEDTools by intersecting coordinates with the UCSC RepeatMasker L1 annotation track. The analysis confirmed that all aligned regions were exclusively located within annotated L1 repeat regions, with a distribution heavily concentrated in the intended L1PA subfamilies.

CRISPR-dCas13b plasmid transfection

The dCas13b-FTO plasmid was kindly provided by Professor Chuan He. The dCas13b-wtFTO construct was transfected into LOVO, RKO, SKOV3, and OVCAR8 cell lines. The dCas13b-FTO-gRNA plasmids were generated following established protocols. Reverse primers were designed accordingly, mixed with forward primers at a 1:1 molar ratio, and annealed using a gradient cooling procedure with a ramp rate of 1 °C per minute. The gRNA plasmids were constructed via homologous recombination and introduced into dCas13b-wtFTO-expressing cell lines to specifically target L1 RNAs.

DNA fiber assays

For DNA fiber assays, cells were labeled with 40 μM CldU (Sigma-Aldrich, C6891) for 20 min, followed by 20 min of labeling with 100 μM ldU (Sigma-Aldrich, I7125). Cells were then suspended in cold PBS at 106/mL, and unlabeled cells were diluted to the same concentration. Labeled cells were mixed with unlabeled cells at a ratio of 1:5 (vol:vol). Three microliters of mixed cells was then incubated with 9 μL of lysis buffer (0.5% SDS, 50 mM EDTA, and 200 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5)) for 3 min on a slide, and DNA fibers were stretched by tilting. After fixation with cold methanol:acetic acid (3:1), DNA was denatured with 2.5 M HCl and neutralized with 0.1 M Na3B4O7 for 7 min and blocked with PBS supplemented with 5% BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100 before staining with primary antibodies. Immunofluorescence was then conducted per the procedure described earlier.

TUNEL experiment

TUNEL assays were performed using a DeadEnd Fluorometric TUNEL System kit (Promega, G3250) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, followed by permeabilization in PBS/0.5% Triton X-100 at RT for 10 min. After washing three times in PBS, the reaction solution was added to the tube and incubated in the dark for 30 min at 37 ℃. Cell suspensions were washed in PBS and counterstained with DAPI diluted in PBS. The samples were then analyzed on a Cytoflex instrument.

Organoid culture

CRC tissue (approximately 1 cm3 in size) from CRC patients was minced, washed with chelating buffer, and cut into small pieces. Glands were pressed and centrifuged for 5 min at 200 × g and 4 °C. Approximately 100 glands per 50 μL of basement matrix were seeded in 1 well of a 24-well plate warmed to 37 °C. Five hundred microliters of medium containing growth factors was added carefully to each well. After organoids formed, the organoids were seeded in 24-well plates, cultured for 1 week, and treated with or without different doses of olaparib and STM2457. Organoid images were acquired using a Leica DMi8 system. The organoid culture was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital.

Statistics and reproducibility

Each sequencing dataset was derived from two biological replicates, and unless otherwise stated, at least three biological replicates were used in each experiment. Error bars represent mean ± standard error of the mean (S.E.M.). The R packages used in the analysis are indicated in the respective methods sections, along with the statistical tests employed. For comparisons between two groups, two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests were performed. P-values less than 0.05 were considered significant for all statistical analyses. Adjusted p values were calculated using Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s d with 95% confidence intervals to assess the magnitude of differences between groups. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess correlation or quantify the reproducibility of two biological replicates. Immunoblotting data were quantified using the open-source software ImageJ. The intensities of the proteins of interest were normalized to those of GAPDH or H3, and the resulting values were presented as bar graphs.
