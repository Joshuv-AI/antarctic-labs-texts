Quadratic Chabauty for Atkin-Lehner quotients of modular curves via weakly holomorphic modular forms: Hodge Filtrations
Isabel Rendell

Abstract
. For Atkin-Lehner quotients X 0 + ​ ( N ) X_{0}^{+}(N) , of prime level and of genus at least 2, we provide an algorithm for computing one of the main objects in the quadratic Chabauty algorithm in terms of weakly holomorphic modular forms associated to the curve. In particular, the algorithm computes a Hodge filtration on a certain unipotent vector bundle with connection related to X 0 + ​ ( N ) X_{0}^{+}(N) , which is crucial in computing the p p -adic height which is used to define the finite set of p p -adic points containing the rational points on X 0 + ​ ( N ) X_{0}^{+}(N) . This improves the current Hodge filtration algorithm by replacing the input of an explicit plane model of the curve with weakly holomorphic modular forms to produce a faster computation. We implement our algorithm on the genus 7 modular curve X 0 + ​ ( 193 ) X_{0}^{+}(193) , and discover congruences between iterated integrals of weight 2 cusp forms in the plus eigenspace for the Atkin-Lehner involution and single integrals of weight 2 cusp forms in the minus eigenspace for the Atkin-Lehner involution.

Introduction

The question of determining the rational points on the Atkin-Lehner quotient of the modular curve
X_{0}(N)

X_{0}^{+}(N)\coloneqq X_{0}(N)/w_{N}
, where

[

]

correspond to unordered pairs of elliptic curves, along with a cyclic isogeny of degree
N
between them, and the Atkin-Lehner involution,

\Q
-curves that are
N
-isogenous to their conjugates and points of this second type are known as
exceptional
. The set of rational points is known for the curves
X_{0}^{+}(N)
for
N

X_{0}^{+}(N)
is between 2 and 6 due to
[
BDM+23
]

[

]

[

that for a prime
N>\!\!>0
the curve
X_{0}^{+}(N)
has no exceptional rational points, and therefore for a prime
N>\!\!>0
there are no quadratic
\Q
-curves that are
N
-isogenous to their conjugates. In these cases, the rational points on
X_{0}^{+}(N)
have been computed using the quadratic Chabauty algorithm, created by Balakrishnan-Dogra-Müller-Tuitman-Vonk
[
BDM+23
]

nice
curve is one that is smooth, projective, and geometrically irreducible. The theory behind quadratic Chabauty is a special case of Kim’s generalisation
[
Kim05
]
of the Chabauty-Coleman method.

For a nice curve
X
of genus
g

J
be its Jacobian, and let
r
denote the rank of the rational points of

[
Fal83
]

[

]
asserted a special case: where
X
satisfies
r<g

is known as the

. Chabauty’s proof involved embedding a curve
X
into its Jacobian,
J

X(\Q_{p})\cap\overline{J(\Q)}
is finite, and thus
X(\Q)
is finite. Coleman then adapted Chabauty’s proof
[
Col85
]
to give an explicit upper bound for the size of
X(\Q)
, still requiring that the Chabauty-Coleman criterion is satisfied. Coleman did this by constructing
p
-adic line integrals called

lie in the kernel of these integrals. These integrals have finitely many zeroes, and then bounding the number of zeroes of these integrals gives an explicit upper bound on the number of rational points on
X

p
-adic points on
X
, containing
X(\Q)
, and then extract
X(\Q)
from it. Therefore when the Chabauty-Coleman criterion is satisfied one can explicitly compute the rational points of a nice curve, by computing the vanishing locus of this Coleman integral; this process is known as the Chabauty-Coleman method. Unfortunately, the condition that the Chabauty-Coleman criterion is satisfied is very restrictive, and cannot be applied to compute rational points on curves with
r\geq g
.

In 2005, Kim
[
Kim05
]
produced a vast generalisation of the Chabauty-Coleman method by taking a unipotent quotient of the
\Q_{p}
-étale fundamental group of a curve

p
-adic points. This subset of
p
-adic points is constructed so that it contains the rational points in order to obtain a

-adic subsets,

X(\Q)\subseteq...\subseteq X(\Q_{p})_{n}\subseteq...\subseteq X(\Q_{p})_{2}\subseteq X(\Q_{p})_{1}\subseteq X(\Q_{p}),

-adic points produced in the Chabauty-Coleman method, provided the Chabauty-Coleman criterion is satisfied. The Chabauty-Kim method hopes to compute the set

by first explicitly computing the Chabauty-Kim sets

, and then extracting the set
X(\Q)
from the Chabauty-Kim sets. In particular, since

A critical development, due to the work of Balakrishnan-Dogra

Therefore, the two aims of the quadratic Chabauty method for a curve

We now discuss the second aim of quadratic Chabauty: to construct explicit functions on
X(\Q_{p})
which cut out (a finite set containing)
X(\Q_{p})_{2}
. Balakrishnan-Dogra have proven that these functions can be written in terms of

-adic height function,

\lambda^{\text{Fil}}

The drawback of the current quadratic Chabauty algorithm is that it requires an explicit plane model of the curve as an input. These models are inefficient to compute with since the coefficients of such a model grow rapidly with the genus of the modular curve. All of the past examples of curves that the model algorithm has been implemented on have a genus of at most 6. This provides motivation for the creation of a more efficient algorithm, which removes the need for computing with the model of the curve. To do this, we would like to exploit the fact that we have a modular curve and replace this input of a model with modular forms associated with the curve. We refer to such an algorithms and methods as
model-free

By comparison, our method, which uses weakly holomorphic modular forms, uncovers congruences between single and iterated integrals on

[
Has23
]

2.1.
The Chabauty-Kim Method

Since quadratic Chabauty is a successful implementation of the Chabauty-Kim method, we begin §

cutting out a finite set containing

.

The remainder of this section will tackle (i), leaving (ii) for §

. To state the fundamental result regarding (i), we first need to recall the following definition.

Definition 2.3

(Néron-Severi group)
.

For a variety

. The Néron-Severi rank of

, is the rank of

, and is often referred to as the Picard number.

We can now give the fundamental result concerning finiteness of

, which was proven via Theorem

and by computing and bounding dimensions of
\text{H}_{f}^{1}(G_{T},U)
, and
\text{H}_{f}^{1}(G_{p},U)
.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
BD18
]
)
.

Suppose that

(3)

r<g+\rho(J)-1,

then
X(\Q_{p})_{2}
is finite.

Remark 2.5
.

We have
\rho(J)\geq 1
, and so this condition is always at least as strong as the Chabauty-Coleman condition that
r<g
, and therefore can be applied in more cases.

We call (

) the
quadratic Chabauty condition
. As we have already mentioned, Siksek showed that the quadratic Chabauty condition is satisfied frequently by modular curves in
[
Sik17
]
. Furthermore, work of Dogra and Le Fourn
[
DLF21
]
gives finiteness of

for quotients of modular curves in many circumstances.

Theorem 2.6

(
[
DLF21
]
)
.

•

For all prime
N
such that
g(X_{0}^{+}(N))\geq 2
,

is finite for any

.

•

For all prime
N
such that

and

,

Therefore, we have finiteness of

in many cases involving modular curves and their quotients, and so it makes sense to try to apply quadratic Chabauty to these curves to obtain

. In particular, Theorem

gives that we always have finiteness of

for the subject of this paper:

prime and

.

2.2.
Quadratic Chabauty for rational points:
p
-adic heights

In general, the objects involved in the Chabauty-Kim method are very hard to explicitly compute. One approach which has been highly successful in implementation is the method of quadratic Chabauty. The success of this is due to relating the functions involved in Kim’s method to
p
-adic heights. Suppose
r=g
(and so we don’t satisfy the conditions for Chabauty-Coleman), and that the
p
-adic closure of
J(\Q)
has finite index in
J(\Q_{p})
. Let
\text{AJ}_{b}
be the Abel-Jacobi morphism, which is the map

\displaystyle\text{AJ}_{b}:X(\Q_{p})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\text{H}^{0}(X_{\Q_{p}},\Omega^{1})^{*}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\omega\mapsto\int_{b}^{x}\omega\right).

Under our assumptions,
\text{AJ}_{b}
induces an isomorphism
J(\Q)\otimes\Q_{p}\cong\text{H}^{0}(X_{\Q_{p}},\Omega^{1})^{*}
, and we call this isomorphism
\alpha:J(\Q)\otimes\Q_{p}\rightarrow\text{H}^{0}(X_{\Q_{p}},\Omega^{1})^{*}
. Since we have an isomorphism,
\alpha
, global points cannot be cut out of local points using linear relations in the Abel-Jacobi map, which highlights the significance of the condition that
r=g
. Therefore, the idea of the quadratic Chabauty method is to replace linear relations with
bilinear
relations. Suppose we can find a function
\theta:X(\Q_{p})\rightarrow\Q_{p}

and a finite set
\Upsilon\subset\Q_{p}
with the following properties:

•

On each residue disc
]x[\subset X(\Q_{p})
, the map

(\text{AJ}_{b},\theta):X(\Q_{p})\longrightarrow\text{H}^{0}(X_{\Q_{p}},\Omega^{1})^{*}\times\Q_{p}

has Zariski dense image and is given by a convergent power series.

•

There exists an endomorphism
E
of
\text{H}^{0}(X_{\Q_{p}},\Omega^{1})^{*}
, a functional
c\in\text{H}^{0}(X_{\Q_{p}},\Omega^{1})^{*}
, and a bilinear form
B:\text{H}^{0}(X_{\Q_{p}},\Omega^{1})^{*}\otimes\text{H}^{0}(X_{\Q_{p}},\Omega^{1})^{*}\rightarrow\Q_{p}
, such that, for all
x\in X(\Q)
,

(4)

\theta(x)-B(\text{AJ}_{b}(x),E(\text{AJ}_{b}(x))+c)\in\Upsilon.

These two properties produce a finite set of
p
-adic points containing
X(\Q)
since the first property implies that only finitely many
p
-adic points can satisfy equation (
4
), and all rational points satisfy it by the second property. Finiteness of this set is proven by local analytic information and global arithmetic information. We call
(\theta,\Upsilon)
a
quadratic Chabauty pair
, and the objects
E,c,
and
B
of a quadratic Chabauty pair will be referred to as its endomorphism, constant, and pairing, respectively. We now discuss how to compute the functions in (
4
) in terms of
p
-adic heights.

The first implementation of quadratic Chabauty was computing integral points on rank 1 elliptic curves due to Balakrishnan-Besser
[
BB15
]
, and on odd degree hyperelliptic curves due to Balakrishnan-Besser-Müller
[
BBM16
]
. This is done via the global Coleman-Gross
p
-adic height function,
h
, and its decomposition into local
p
-adic heights,
h_{v}
, and utilising the different properties of the local
p
-adic height at
p
,
h_{p}
, and the local heights away from
p
,
h_{v}
, with
v\neq p
. When
x
is a rational but not integral point, the values of
h_{v}(x)
for
v\neq p
cannot be controlled and so a different
p
-adic height is adopted in order to compute the set of rational points.

Balakrishnan-Dogra
[
BD18
]
show that in order to apply quadratic Chabauty to compute rational points, quadratic Chabauty pairs are defined by associating certain Galois representations to points of
X
, so that we can then use Nekovář’s
p
-adic height functions
[
Nek90
]
on these certain Galois representations. We first discuss how these Galois representations are constructed, and then how Nekovář’s
p
-adic height functions can be used to produce quadratic Chabauty pairs. The Galois representations are defined in terms of a nice correspondence
Z
on
X\times X
, which is constructed by nontrivial elements of
\mathrm{Ker}(NS(J)\rightarrow NS(X))
. This kernel is a free abelian group of rank equal to
\rho(J)-1
, and so such a correspondence always exists when
\rho(J)>1
. Let
K\in\{\Q,\Q_{p}\}
and let
G_{K}
denote the absolute Galois group of
K
. We can attach to any such choice of nice correspondence,
Z
, a suitable quotient
U_{Z}
of the
\Q_{p}
-pro-unipotent fundamental group of
X_{\overline{\Q}}
, which in turn defines a certain family of Galois representations. We denote such representations by
A_{Z}(b,x)
, for each

, so that we have a map

\displaystyle X(K)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\{G_{K}\rightarrow\text{GL}_{2g+2}(\Q_{p})\}/\sim

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto A_{Z}(b,x).

A more detailed explanation can be found in
[
BD18
]
. Let
V=\text{H}_{\text{\'{e}t}}^{1}(X_{\overline{K}},\Q_{p})^{*}
, and let

be the image of

-functor, so that
V_{\dR}=D_{\text{cris}}(V)=\text{H}^{1}_{\dR}(X/\Q_{p})^{*}
. In addition, let
\chi_{p}:G_{K}\rightarrow\Q_{p}^{\times}
be the
p
-adic cyclotomic character, and let
\Q_{p}(1)
be the one-dimensional representation given by
\chi_{p}
. With respect to a suitable choice of basis, the representation

is lower triangular, of the form

g\in G_{K}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
*&\rho_{V}(g)&0\\
*&*&\chi_{p}(g)\end{pmatrix},

where

corresponds to

. Representations of this form, which admit a

[
BDM+19
]

We now discuss Nekovář’s
p
-adic height functions. Denote Nekovář’s global
p
-adic height by

. Firstly,
h
has a decomposition into a sum of local height functions,

, defined locally at every finite place

h(A_{Z}(b,x))=h_{p}(A_{Z}(b,x))+\sum_{v\neq p}h_{v}(A_{Z}(b,x)).

These local height functions have the following important properties:

(A)

for
v=p
, the map
x\mapsto h_{p}(A_{Z}(b,x))
extends to a locally analytic function
\theta:X(\Q_{p})\rightarrow\Q_{p}
by Nekovář’s construction
[
Nek90
]
.

(B)

for
v\neq p
the local heights

take on a finite set of values
\Upsilon\subset\Q_{p}
for

by a result of Kim and Tamagawa
[
KT08
]
. In particular, the value of

is identically zero when

is a prime of potential good reduction.

Secondly, since local

-adic height functions are functions on mixed extensions,

can also be viewed as a function on mixed extensions. Moreover,
[
BDM+19
]
§3.1, explains that, roughly, these mixed extensions can be mapped to

-adic height functions do not. As a result,

can be viewed as a function on

, and in fact Nekovář proves in
[

defines a bilinear pairing

h:\text{H}^{1}_{f}(G_{\Q},V)\times\text{H}^{1}_{f}(G_{\Q},V)\longrightarrow\Q_{p}.

Furthermore, under our assumptions at the start of §

, we have that

defines a bilinear pairing on
\text{H}^{0}(X_{\Q_{p}},\Omega^{1})^{*}
via our isomorphism
\alpha

\text{H}^{0}(X_{\Q_{p}},\Omega^{1})^{*}

-adic height functions means that

produces a quadratic Chabauty pair. In particular, we can build a function as in

with

, and the endomorphism,

Z
, by
[
BDM+19
]

By (B) above and the decomposition of

\Upsilon\subset\Q_{p}
such that for any

we have

(5)

h(A_{Z}(b,x))-h_{p}(A_{Z}(b,x))\in\Upsilon.

Moreover, we have that
h
defines a bilinear pairing on
\text{H}^{0}(X_{\Q_{p}},\Omega^{1})^{*}
and by
[
BDM+23
]
§2.3,

may be extended to a bilinear map on

so that we have
h:X(\Q_{p})\rightarrow\Q_{p}
. In addition, by (A) above we know
x\mapsto h_{p}(A_{Z}(b,x))
also extends to give a locally analytic function
\theta:X(\Q_{p})\rightarrow\Q_{p}
. Therefore, we can define a map
\rho_{Z}\coloneqq h-h_{p}
, which we call the
quadratic Chabauty function
so that we have a function

\rho_{Z}:X(\Q_{p})\longrightarrow\Q_{p},

which is known to be Zariski dense on every residue disc. Therefore, the fact that all rational points on

are mapped into the finite set
\Upsilon
under this map, as in (

is finite. Since the map
\rho_{Z}
is defined on

, it is defined on

, and Balakrishnan-Dogra

is also mapped into

under the map

. Therefore, we obtain the following tower of finite sets,

(6)

X(\Q)\subset X(\Q_{p})_{2}\subset\{x\in X(\Q_{p}):h(A_{Z}(b,x))-h_{p}(A_{Z}(b,x))\in\Upsilon\}.

The importance of constructing this largest set is that it is defined in terms of
p
-adic height functions which are computable objects. Therefore, to compute the rational points of
X
, we can compute a
p
-adic approximation of the largest finite set, which by construction contains the rationals, and employ techniques such as the Mordell-Weil sieve in the event that this set is strictly larger than
X(\Q)
.

Now, suppose that we have enough rational points,
P_{1},...,.P_{m}
, that satisfy the conditions laid out in §1.4 of
[
BDM+19
]

-adic heights below, then we have a method to explicitly compute a finite subset of

, containing the set

, as defined in (

(a)

Determine the set of values that

can take for

and

.

(b)

Expand the function

into a

-adic power series on every residue disc.

(c)

Evaluate

for

.

In the event that our curve

has potentially good reduction everywhere (for example

are trivial by the properties of

for

. In this paper, our algorithm is implemented on the modular curve

Lemma 2.7

(

]

The local height

is trivial on

As a result, there are no nontrivial local heights

p
-adic height,

, without first computing an explicit model of the curve. Now that we have established the central role the local

A key result is that

]

We have

Therefore, to compute the local

-adic height at

is equivalent to computing two unipotent isomorphisms

\lambda^{\star}(x):\Q_{p}\oplus V_{\dR}\oplus\Q_{p}(1)\xrightarrow{\sim}D_{\text{cris}}(A_{Z}(b,x)),\hskip 28.45274pt\text{ for }\star\in\{\text{\text{Fil}},\phi\},

2.3.
Hodge filtration

We have just seen that the Hodge filtration is used to define the local

-adic height function

as in (

Recall that
X
is a smooth projective modular curve of genus

over
\Q
, such that

and
\rho(J)>1
. Let

be an affine open subset. Let

be a rational point of

]

, as this is what we always have in our examples. Therefore, we have

, so

, and

is defined over

. Choose a set
\{\omega_{0},...,\omega_{2g-1}\}\in\text{H}^{0}(Y,\Omega^{1})

\text{H}_{\dR}^{1}(X)

\boldsymbol{\omega}
denote the column vector
(\omega_{0},...,\omega_{2g-1})^{\intercal}
.

We recall that
Z
is a nontrivial element in
\mathrm{Ker}(NS(J)\rightarrow NS(X))
, which is guaranteed to exist under our assumption that
\rho(J)>1
, and we compute it as follows. For the algorithm, we require the action of
Z
as a matrix on our basis of
\text{H}_{\dR}^{1}(X/\Q)
. We utilise the fact that
X
is a modular curve and choose to construct the action of

in terms of the action of a certain Hecke operator,

, for

a prime, with respect to our basis of
\text{H}_{\dR}^{1}(X/\Q)
. To compute the matrix of

, we first compute the matrix of Frobenius,

, via Tuitman’s algorithm
[
Tui16
]
,
[
Tui17
]
, and then use the Eichler-Shimura relation:

T_{q}=F+qF^{-1},

to obtain a matrix for the action of

on
\text{H}_{\dR}^{1}(X/\Q)
. We choose the prime

so that

does not lie in
\Z\subset\text{End}(J)
, and in fact we can choose

. From here we choose to construct the matrix corresponding to the action of a nice correspondence

on
\text{H}_{\dR}^{1}(X/\Q)
as a nontrivial polynomial in

, and we require that the corresponding matrix has trace equal to 0 so that

is guaranteed to be a nice correspondence. We will see in §

on

.

We will now introduce the universal connection

. Firstly, universal properties give that the rank

has a connection, a Hodge filtration, and a Frobenius structure, as discussed in

, whose restriction to

\Lambda\coloneqq-\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
\boldsymbol{\omega}&0&0\\
\eta&\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\intercal}Z&0\end{pmatrix}.

The bottom left entry of

, and we find that since

, the differential

3.
Model-free Hodge filtration algorithm

The focus of this section is the model-free algorithm for computing the Hodge filtration on
\mathcal{A}_{Z}
for
X_{0}^{+}(N)
, the Atkin-Lehner quotient of the modular curve
X_{0}(N)
, for
N
prime such that the genus,
g
, of

is at least
2
. We saw in §
2.3
that one of the first steps in the Hodge filtration, Algorithm

, is to compute a basis of de Rham cohomology,

, as the vector
\boldsymbol{\omega}
. In the model algorithm, this is defined in terms of the variables

of the model of the curve. Therefore, the first step of a model-free algorithm for the Hodge filtration is to compute a basis of
\text{H}^{1}_{\dR}(X/\Q)
in terms of
q
-expansions of modular forms, which will be dealt with in §

, we will explain how to compute the nice correspondence

, and the entries of the matrix
\lambda^{\text{Fil}}
in terms of modular forms.

The holomorphic differential 1-forms on

are a subspace of de Rham cohomology, and so we have
\text{H}^{0}(X_{0}^{+}(N),\Omega_{X_{0}^{+}(N)|\mathbb{C}})\subset\text{H}^{1}_{\dR}(X/\Q)
. Therefore, since
\text{H}^{0}(X_{0}^{+}(N),\Omega_{X_{0}^{+}(N)|\mathbb{C}})
has dimension
g
, and
\text{H}^{1}_{\dR}(X/\Q)
has dimension
2g
, half of the basis of
\text{H}^{1}_{\dR}(X/\Q)
comes from the holomorphic differential 1-forms. We obtain these elements as follows. Let
\mathcal{H}
be the upper half plane, let
\tau\in\mathcal{H}
, and let

be a weight
2
cusp form on

, so

. Therefore,

has a
q
-expansion of the form

f(\tau)=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}q^{n},\hskip 14.22636ptq=e^{2\pi i\tau},\tau\in\mathcal{H},

and from this the following
q
-expansion

\omega_{f}\coloneqq f(\tau)\frac{dq}{q}=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}q^{n-1}dq,

defines an element of
\text{H}^{0}(X_{0}(N),\Omega_{X_{0}(N)|\mathbb{C}})
. Since
X_{0}^{+}(N)
is a quotient of
X_{0}(N)
, we can think of
\text{H}^{0}(X_{0}^{+}(N),\Omega_{X_{0}^{+}(N)|\mathbb{C}})
as a vector subspace of
\text{H}^{0}(X_{0}(N),\Omega_{X_{0}(N)|\mathbb{C}})
. Hence we get a corresponding subspace of
\mathcal{S}_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
. This subspace is exactly the plus eigenspace of the action of the Atkin-Lehner involution on modular forms, and we denote this space as
\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
.

Therefore, to compute a basis of
\text{H}^{1}_{\dR}(X/\Q)
, we first compute the space
\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
, and then obtain a basis of
\text{H}^{0}(X_{0}^{+}(N),\Omega_{X_{0}^{+}(N)|\mathbb{C}})
by sending a cusp form
f
to
\omega_{f}\coloneqq f\frac{dq}{q}
. We denote the basis of
\text{H}^{0}(X_{0}^{+}(N),\Omega_{X_{0}^{+}(N)|\mathbb{C}})
by
\{\omega_{0},...,\omega_{g-1}\}
, and the corresponding cusp forms by
\{f_{\omega_{0}},...,f_{\omega_{g-1}}\}
, so that
\omega_{i}=f_{\omega_{i}}\frac{dq}{q}
for

. The process of computing the basis
\{\omega_{0},...,\omega_{g-1}\}
can be implemented in
Magma

[
BCP97
]
. Therefore, it remains to construct the other half of the basis of
\text{H}^{1}_{\dR}(X/\Q)
. To do this, we will utilise the discriminant function,

; this will be the focus of §
3.1
.

In §
3.1
, we will show that our method always produces a basis of
\text{H}^{1}_{\dR}(X/\Q)
and we will verify this by computing the cup product of pairs of our basis elements to compute a cup product matrix. If our cup product matrix has nonzero determinant, then we have successfully constructed a basis of
\text{H}^{1}_{\dR}(X/\Q)
in terms of
q
-expansions of modular forms. We will use Serre’s cup product formula:

\displaystyle\text{H}^{1}_{\dR}(X/\Q_{p})\times\text{H}^{1}_{\dR}(X/\Q_{p})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\Q_{p}

\displaystyle([\mu_{1}],[\mu_{2}])

\displaystyle\mapsto[\mu_{1}\cup\mu_{2}]=\sum_{Q\in X(\mathbb{C}_{p})}\text{Res}_{Q}\left(\mu_{2}\int\mu_{1}\right).

Therefore, if we calculate the cup product matrix of our basis and find that its determinant is nonzero, then we have successfully found a basis of
\text{H}^{1}_{\dR}(X/\Q)
. Since the differentials
\{\omega_{0},...,\omega_{g-1}\}
are holomorphic, by the definition of the cup product formula we will have
[\omega_{i}\cup\omega_{j}]=0

Previous successful implementations of quadratic Chabauty in the literature (see for example

(13)

C\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}0_{g}&I_{g}\\
-I_{g}&0_{g}\end{pmatrix},\hskip 14.22636pt\text{where }\hskip 14.22636ptC_{i,j}=[\omega_{i}]\cup[\omega_{j}],\text{ for }i,j\in\{0,...,2g-1\}.

Once we have a symplectic basis of

on

in terms of this basis of

3.1.
Computing a symplectic basis of
\text{H}^{1}_{\dR}(X/\Q)

We have already seen how we obtain the first half of a basis for
\text{H}^{1}_{\dR}(X/\Q)
from
\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
, and that we will use Serre’s cup product formula to verify when we have constructed a basis of
\text{H}^{1}_{\dR}(X/\Q)
. Since Serre’s cup product formula sums over the poles of the differentials, we would like to construct differentials that we know only have a pole at the unique cusp
\infty
of
X_{0}^{+}(N)
. To do this, we will construct a function
f_{\dR}
with poles only at infinity and then multiply this by the holomorphic differentials. We will then compute the cup product matrix of the set
\{\omega_{0},...,\omega_{g-1},f_{\dR}\cdot\omega_{0},...,f_{\dR}\cdot\omega_{g-1}\}
to verify that this set is a basis for
\text{H}^{1}_{\dR}(X/\Q)
. We will construct
f_{\dR}
as a quotient of two weight 12 cusp forms on
X_{0}^{+}(N)
. In this section we give a suitable construction of the function
f_{\dR}
for
X_{0}^{+}(N)
, and prove that our method always produces a basis. We begin by giving a function with zeroes only at
\infty
, using the discriminant function
\Delta\in\mathcal{S}_{12}(\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))
.

Proposition 3.1
.

For any odd prime
N
, the function
\sqrt{\Delta\cdot w_{N}(\Delta)}
is a weight 12 cusp form for
\Gamma_{0}(N)
in the plus eigenspace for the Atkin-Lehner involution, i.e the function
\sqrt{\Delta\cdot w_{N}(\Delta)}
is in
\mathcal{S}_{12}^{+}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
. Moreover, the function
\sqrt{\Delta\cdot w_{N}(\Delta)}
only vanishes at the unique cusp at
\infty
on
X_{0}^{+}(N)
.

Proof.

By definition,
\Delta=\eta(z)^{24}
, where
\eta(z)=q^{1/24}\prod\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{n})
is the Dedekind eta-function. We also have
w_{N}(\Delta(z))=\Delta(w_{n}\cdot z)=\Delta(-\frac{1}{Nz})=\Delta(Nz)=\eta(Nz)^{24}
, where the first two equalities follow from the definition of
w_{N}
, the third is because
\Delta
is a cusp form of weight 12, and the final equality follows from the definition of
\Delta
. Therefore, we can rewrite
\sqrt{\Delta\cdot w_{N}(\Delta)}
as
\eta(z)^{12}\eta(Nz)^{12}
. By
[
RW15
]
(2), a function
f=\prod_{\delta|N}\eta(\delta z)^{r_{\delta}}
is a modular form for
\Gamma_{0}(N)
of weight
k=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\delta|N}r_{\delta}
, if we have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\delta|N}\delta r_{\delta}\equiv 0\text{ mod }24,\hskip 11.38092pt\sum\limits_{\delta|N}\frac{N}{\delta}r_{\delta}\equiv 0\text{ mod }24,\text{ and }

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{\delta|N}\delta^{r_{\delta}}\text{ is the square of a rational number}.

Therefore, since the function
\eta(z)^{12}\eta(Nz)^{12}
has pairs of values of
(\delta,r_{\delta})
equal to
\{(1,12),(N,12)\}
, all of the above conditions are satisfied and so
\eta(z)^{12}\eta(Nz)^{12}
is a modular form for
\Gamma_{0}(N)
of weight 12. Finally,
\eta(z)^{12}\eta(Nz)^{12}
is a cusp form and invariant under the action of
w_{N}
and hence
\sqrt{\Delta\cdot w_{N}(\Delta)}\in\mathcal{S}_{12}^{+}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
. The cusp form
\Delta
is nonzero for all
\tau\in\mathcal{H}
(see, for example Corollary 1.4.2
[
DS05
]
), therefore
w_{N}(\Delta)
vanishes only at
\infty
, and so the product
\sqrt{\Delta\cdot w_{N}(\Delta)}
has a zero only at
\infty
.
∎

We will use the function
\sqrt{\Delta\cdot w_{N}(\Delta)}
to define the function
f_{\dR}
as follows. Write
val(f)
for the valuation of a
q
-expansion
f
as an element of
\Q((q))
. We let
d\coloneqq\text{dim }\mathcal{S}_{12}^{+}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
and write
\{s_{1},...,s_{d}\}
to be a basis for
\mathcal{S}_{12}^{+}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
such that
val(s_{i})<val(s_{i+1})
for all
i\in\{1,...,d-1\}
. We can compute such a basis on

[
BCP97
]
and it gives

, where
a_{\Delta}
is a constant so that the leading term of

is 1. We have
val(\sqrt{\Delta\cdot w_{N}(\Delta)})=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{N}{2}
, and so
val(s_{d})=\frac{N+1}{2}
. We will construct

by choosing a weight 12 cusp form in the plus eigenspace for the Atkin-Lehner involution,

, for a positive integer

satisfying some extra conditions, and divide it by the function
a_{\Delta}\sqrt{\Delta\cdot w_{N}(\Delta)}
. Therefore, the function

will have a pole only at

due to Proposition

We now discuss how to choose the integer
j_{\dR}\in\mathbb{N}
. We want to choose
j_{\dR}\in\{1,...,d-1\}
so that taking

results in the set
\{\omega_{0},...,\omega_{g-1},f_{\dR}\cdot\omega_{0},...,f_{\dR}\cdot\omega_{g-1}\}
being a basis of

, that

(14)

val\left(\frac{s_{d-j_{\dR}}}{s_{d}}f_{\omega_{i}}\right)\leq-1.

This is because if
val\left(\frac{s_{d-j_{\dR}}}{s_{d}}f_{\omega_{i}}\right)\geq 1
for some

, then
\frac{s_{d-j_{\dR}}}{s_{d}}f_{\omega_{i}}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
and so under the map to differentials, it produces an element of
\text{H}^{0}(X,\Omega^{1})
. Therefore, the set
\{\omega_{0},...,\omega_{g-1},f_{\dR}\cdot\omega_{0},...,f_{\dR}\cdot\omega_{g-1}\}
could not give a basis of
\text{H}^{1}_{\dR}(X/\Q)
, as the differential
\frac{s_{d-j_{\dR}}}{s_{d}}f_{\omega_{i}}\frac{dq}{q}
would lie in the span of
\{\omega_{0},...,\omega_{g-1}\}
. Moreover, if
val\left(\frac{s_{d-j_{\dR}}}{s_{d}}f_{\omega_{i}}\right)=0
for some

, then the computation of the cup product formula will fail since we will have to integrate a nonzero residue term. Therefore, we must choose
j_{\dR}\in\mathbb{N}
such that (
14
) holds.

Proposition 3.2
.

For any prime number
N
, there exists an integer
j\in\{1,...,d-1\}
such that for all
i\in\{0,...,g-1\}
we have

val\left(\frac{s_{d-j}}{s_{d}}f_{\omega_{i}}\right)\leq-1.

Proof.

We have
val(s_{d})=\frac{N+1}{2}
. We defined the set
\{\omega_{0},...,\omega_{g-1}\}
to be a basis of
\text{H}^{0}(X,\Omega^{1})
ordered by increasing valuation. Therefore, for all
j\in\{1,...,d-1\}
, we have the following upper bound for all
i\in\{0,...,g-1\}
.

\displaystyle val\left(\frac{s_{d-j}}{s_{d}}f_{\omega_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq val(s_{d-j})-val({s_{d}})+\max_{i=0,...,g-1}(val(f_{\omega_{i}}))

\displaystyle=val(s_{d-j})-\frac{N+1}{2}+val(f_{\omega_{g-1}}).

Take
j=d-1
, then
val(s_{d-j})=val(s_{1})=1
, because otherwise we will have
val(f)\geq 2
for all
f\in\mathcal{S}_{12}^{+}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
which gives a contradiction since any line bundle of degree at least
2g
on curve of genus

is basepoint free. Therefore, we have
val(s_{1})=1
. All that is left is to show that

(15)

val(f_{\omega_{g-1}})\leq\frac{N-3}{2}.

We will prove this by using the valence formula for congruence subgroups to prove that (
15
) holds (see, for example,
[
Mas15
]
Theorem 2.6.1). Since
f\in\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
,
f
lies in the larger space
\mathcal{S}_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
. Let
\text{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})=\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})/\{\pm 1\}
, and
\overline{\Gamma}
is the image of
\Gamma
in
\text{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, where
\Gamma
is a congruence subgroup. Also, define
v_{z}(f)
as the valuation of
f
at the point
z\in\mathcal{H}\cup\{\infty\}
,
n_{\Gamma}(z)
is the size of the stabiliser of
z
in
\overline{\Gamma}
,
v_{P,\Gamma}(f)
is the valuation of
f
at the cusp
P
, and
Cusps(\Gamma)
are the set of cusps of
\Gamma
. The valence formula for congruence subgroups gives that for a nonzero meromorphic function on
\mathcal{H}\cup\{\infty\}
which is weakly modular of weight

(16)

\sum\limits_{z\in\Gamma\backslash\mathcal{H}}\frac{v_{z}(f)}{n_{\Gamma}(z)}+\sum\limits_{P\in Cusps(\Gamma)}v_{P,\Gamma}(f)=\frac{k}{12}[\text{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}):\overline{\Gamma}].

Since

is a cusp form, we have

for all

, and writing

for the second cusp of

, we have
v_{w_{N}(\infty),\Gamma_{0}(N)}(f)>0
. We also have
[\text{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}):\overline{\Gamma_{0}(N)}]=[\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}):\Gamma_{0}(N)]=N+1
. Therefore, since
val(f_{\omega_{g-1}})=v_{\infty,\Gamma_{0}(N)}(f)
, the valence formula (

val(f_{\omega_{g-1}})=\frac{N+1}{6}-\left(\sum\limits_{z\in\Gamma\backslash\mathcal{H}}\frac{v_{z}(f)}{n_{\Gamma}(z)}+v_{w_{N}(\infty),\Gamma_{0}(N)}(f)\right)\leq\frac{N+1}{6}.

Finally, we have

if and only if

, which we have since we have assumed that the genus of

is at least 2, and so (

as a linear combination of elements of the set
\{\frac{s_{d-(j_{\dR}-1)}}{s_{d}},...,\frac{s_{d-1}}{s_{d}}\}

. Let
