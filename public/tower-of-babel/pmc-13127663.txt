Dual atrial rhythms after heart transplantation: implications for pacemaker lead placement
Authors: Wei-Chieh Lee, Hii Hiong-Ping
Published: 2026

Case description

A 48-year-old woman presented with recurrent syncope over 6 months. She had undergone orthotopic heart transplantation with a bi-atrial anastomosis 14 years earlier, leaving substantial residual recipient atrial tissue. Surface electrocardiography demonstrated complete atrioventricular (AV) block (Figure 1A). Intracardiac electrograms showed organized donor atrial activation with intermittent ventricular conduction failure, consistent with infra-nodal disease. Electrograms from multiple right atrial sites showed rapid atrial fibrillation (AF) without organized sinus activity (Figure 1B). Because of discordance between surface ECG findings and intracardiac recordings, electroanatomical mapping was performed to localize donor sinus rhythm (Figure 1C). High-density mapping revealed marked donor–recipient atrial dissociation with two independent atrial rhythms. Recipient right atrium (RA) showed continuous disorganized AF with low-voltage electrograms, whereas donor RA maintained organized sinus rhythm from an intact sinus node (Figure 1D). A conduction barrier along surgical suture line resulted in complete electrical isolation between donor and recipient atria. Donor atrial activation conducted normally to AV node and governed ventricular rhythm, whereas recipient AF remained electrically confined (Figure 1E–G). This dual-atrium physiology complicated pacemaker implantation, as fibrillatory activity from residual recipient RA obscured identification and stable placement of the atrial lead in donor atrial tissue. Ultimately, guided by electroanatomical mapping, atrial lead was successfully positioned within viable donor atrial tissue, resulting in stable atrial sensing and pacing. Late AV conduction disturbances after heart transplantation are uncommon and may result from conduction system fibrosis, surgical injury during atrial anastomosis, chronic graft vasculopathy, or rejection-related remodelling.1 Ultimately, her recurrent syncope was attributed to intermittent complete AV block. This case illustrates the rare phenomenon of ‘two rhythms in one heart’ after heart transplantation and highlights the value of high-density electroanatomical mapping for accurate atrial lead placement. Careful delineation of donor and recipient atrial tissue is essential to avoid ineffective sensing or inappropriate detection of fibrillatory activity.

(A) The 12-lead electrocardiogram demonstrates complete atrioventricular block with organized atrial activity. (B) Intracardiac electrograms recorded from multiple right atrial sites during pacemaker implantation show exclusively rapid, irregular atrial activity consistent with atrial fibrillation, without evidence of organized sinus rhythm. (C) Intracardiac electrograms obtained from the recipient right atrium reveal continuous, high-frequency, disorganized atrial activity consistent with atrial fibrillation, which is discordant with the organized atrial activity suggested by surface electrocardiography. (D) Intracardiac electrograms with activation mapping recorded from the donor right atrium demonstrate regular atrial depolarizations consistent with donor sinus rhythm. Donor atrial activation conducts normally to the atrioventricular node and governs ventricular rhythm, while no electrical conduction from the recipient atrium is observed, confirming complete electrical isolation. (E) Electroanatomical bipolar voltage maps of the right atrium demonstrate complete donor–recipient atrial dissociation. The recipient right atrium exhibits low-voltage, heterogeneous signals consistent with atrial fibrillation, whereas the donor right atrium shows preserved voltage with organized sinus activation. A conduction barrier (dotted line) corresponding to the surgical suture line separates the two chambers. (F) Voltage mapping of anterior portion of the donor right atrium highlights organized sinus rhythm originating from the donor sinus node (green circle). (G) Voltage mapping of posterior portion of the recipient right atrium shows rapid, chaotic atrial fibrillation without coherent propagation or an identifiable sinus pattern (green circle). AV, atrioventricular; AF, atrial fibrillation; RA, right atrium.
