Sub-Poissonian light in fluctuating thermal-loss bosonic channels
Iliya G. Stepanov, Roman K. Goncharov, Alexei D. Kiselev

Abstract
We study the photon statistics of a single-mode sub-Poissonian light propagating in the temperature-loss bosonic channel with fluctuating transmittance which can be regarded as a temperature-dependent model of turbulent atmosphere. By assuming that the variance of the transmittance can be expressed in terms of the fluctuation strength parameter we show that the photon statistics of the light remains sub-Poissonian provided the averaged transmittance exceeds its critical value. The critical transmittance is analytically computed as a function of the input states parameters, the temperature and the fluctuation strength. The results are applied to study special cases of the one-mode squeezed states and the odd optical Shrödinger cats.



I
Introduction

The nonclassical properties of optical fields
lie at the heart of quantum optics
and
from its very beginning they have been the subject of
numerous intense studies.
There are a number of indicators
introduced to measure quantumness (nonclassicality) of light
such as
negativity of the Wigner function
Kenfack and Zyczkowski 2004
,
squeezing
Teich and Saleh 1989
;
Lvovsky 2015

and sub-Poissonian statistics
Davidovich 1996

(see also a recent review on
quantumness
quantifiers based on Husimi quasiprobability
Goldberg et al. 2020
).

Note that,
for the squeezing and the sub-Poissonian statistics,
the indicators are formulated in terms of
second-order moments of fluctuations of
the experimentally measured quantities.
For nonclassical fields,
these moments violate certain inequalities.
For example,
the sub-Poissonian light is indicated when
the Fano factor defined as
the ratio of the photon number variance and the mean photon number
is less than unity.

Aside from its fundamental importance,
light nonclassicality plays a vital role
in quantum metrology
Tan and Jeong 2019
.
For sub-Poissonian fields that will be our primary concern,
there are various experimental techniques
used to generate sub-Poissonian
light
Li et al. 1995
;
Peřina et al. 2021
;
Iskhakov et al. 2016
;
Lal et al. 2019
;
Samedov 2019

and their applications in quantum imaging
are reviewed in
Berchera and Degiovanni 2019

(higher-order sub-Poissonian statistics is discussed in
Erenso et al. 2002
;
Peřina et al. 2017
).
Security analysis of BB84 protocol with sub-Poissonian light sources was performed in
Waks et al. 2002
.
Influence of temporal filtering of sub-Poissonian single-photon pulses on the expected secret key
fraction, the quantum bit error ratio, and the tolerable channel losses
is analyzed in
Kupko et al. 2020
.

It is well known that
continuous variable quantum states of
non-classical light used in
quantum metrology and
quantum communication protocols
Pirandola et al. 2020
;
Goncharov et al. 2022

are subject to loss and added noise leading to degradation of non-classicality and quantum correlations.

For free-space communication links
Peuntinger et al. 2014
;
Heim et al. 2014
;
Derkach et al. 2020
;
Pirandola 2021
,
a widely used general theoretical approach to
modeling environment-induced decoherence effects
is based on Gaussian quantum channels with fluctuating parameters.
Specifically,
a pure-loss channel with fluctuating transmittance exemplifies
a popular model that describes the propagation of quantum light
in a turbulent atmosphere
(see a review on propagation of classical eletromagnetic waves through a turbulent atmosphere
Kravtsov 1992
).

This model has been extensively used to study
nonclassical properties and quantum correlations
of light propagating in turbulent
atmospheres
Milonni et al. 2004
;
Kiesel et al. 2008
;
Semenov and Vogel 2009
;
Vasylyev et al. 2012
.
In Ref.
Gumberidze et al. 2016
,
Bell inequalities in turbulent atmospheric channels
are explored using
the probability distribution of transmittance (PDT) in the elliptic-beam approximation
with parameter suitable for
the weak to moderate-turbulence channels
Vasylyev et al. 2016
.
Gaussian entanglement in turbulent atmosphere
and protocol that enables entanglement transfer over arbitrary distances
Bohmann et al. 2016
;
Avetisyan and Monken 2016
.
The evolution of higher-order non-classicality and entanglement criteria in
atmospheric fluctuating-loss channels are investigated in
Bohmann et al. 2017
.
Theory of the classical
effects associated with geometrical features of light propagating, such as beam wandering, widening
and deflection is developed in
Vasylyev et al. 2018
;
Vasylyev et al. 2019
.
In Ref.
Klen and Semenov 2023
,
the PDT derived by numerical simulations is compared with the analytical results.

In this paper, we
adapt a generalized model of the channel
and use the thermal-loss channel with fluctuating transmittance
to examine how
the temperature effects combined with
the fluctuating losses influence
the sub-Poissonian light.

The paper is structured as follows.
In Sec.
II
we describe a temperature-loss channel
and the parameters
expressed in terms of the first-order and second-order moments
of the photon number
used to identify sub-Poissonian light fields.
In particular,
we deduce the input-output relation
for the
q
-parameter
introduced as a un-normalized version of the Mandel
Q
-parameter.
In Sec.
III

this relation is generalized to the case of the temperature-loss channel with
fluctuating transmittance.
After parameterization of
the transmittance variance, it is shown that
the output light will be sub-Poissonian
only if the average transmittance exceeds
its critical value.
In Sec.
IV

we
apply the theoretical results to the specials cases of
squeezed states, odd optical cats and Fock states
and study how the critical transmittance
depends on the temperature and the strength of transmittance fluctuations.
Finally, concluding remarks are given in Sec.
V
.

II
Channel and moments

We consider a single-mode quantized light
with the annihilation and creation operators,

\hat{a}
and
{\hat{a}}^{\dagger}
,
propagating through a quantum Gaussian channel.
The simplest and widely used
method to describe the channel
is to
introduce additional bosonic mode representing
the degree of freedom of environment
and assume that the interaction between
the light and noise modes
is determined by the channel unitary

\hat{U}_{\tau}
giving
the beam splitter transformation
of the form:

\displaystyle\hat{U}^{\dagger}_{\tau}\hat{a}\hat{U}_{\tau}\equiv\hat{a}_{\tau}=\sqrt{\tau}\hat{a}+\sqrt{1-\tau}\hat{b},

(1)

where
\tau
is the channel transmittance
and
\hat{b}
is the aninihilation operator
of the noise mode.

For the temperature loss channel,
the input state of the bipartite system
is the product of the density matrices
given by

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}=\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{in}}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{th}},

(2)

where

\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{in}}

is the density matrix of radiation

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{in}}=|{\psi_{\mathrm{in}}}\rangle\langle{\psi_{\mathrm{in}}}|

(3)

prepared in the pure state
|{\psi_{\mathrm{in}}}\rangle
,
whereas
the environment is in the thermal state

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{th}}=\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{th}}+1}\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\mathrm{e}^{-n\beta\hbar\omega}|{n}\rangle\langle{n}|,\quad n_{\mathrm{th}}=\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\{{\hat{b}}^{\dagger}\hat{b}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{th}}\}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta\hbar\omega}-1}.

(4)

where

\beta=1/(k_{B}T)
is the inverse temperature parameter
(
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant and
T
is the temperature),

\hbar
is the Planck constant,
\omega
is the photon frequency,

n_{\mathrm{th}}
is the average number of thermal photons (the mean thermal photon number).

Temporal evolution of the density matrix (
2
)
is governed by the channel unitary (
1
)
as follows

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(\tau)=\hat{U}_{\tau}\hat{\rho}\hat{U}^{\dagger}_{\tau}.

(5)

We can now use Eqs. (
1
) and (
5
)
to obtain the normally ordered characteristic function
of the light as a function of the channel transmittance

\displaystyle\chi(\alpha,\tau)=\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\{:\hat{D}(\alpha):\hat{\rho}(\tau)\}=\chi_{\mathrm{in}}(\sqrt{\tau}\alpha)\chi_{\mathrm{th}}(\sqrt{1-\tau}\alpha),

(6)

where
:\hat{D}(\alpha):=:\exp(\alpha{\hat{a}}^{\dagger}-{\alpha}^{\ast}\hat{a}):=\exp(\alpha{\hat{a}}^{\dagger})\exp(-{\alpha}^{\ast}\hat{a})

is the normally ordered displacement operator;

\chi_{\mathrm{in}}
and
\chi_{\mathrm{th}}
are the input and thermal characteristic functions
given by

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathrm{in}}(\alpha)=\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\{:\hat{D}(\alpha):\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{in}}\},\quad\chi_{\mathrm{th}}(\alpha)=\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\{:\hat{D}(\alpha):\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{th}}\}=\exp(-n_{\mathrm{th}}|\alpha|^{2}).

(7)

Note that the form of this result reproduces the characteristic function derived
by solving the thermal single-mode Lindblad equation
(see, e.g.,
Kiselev et al. 2021
)
so that
the beam splitter transformation representation (
1
)
and the approach based on the Lindblad dynamics
appear to be equivalent tools for modeling the temperature loss quantum channel.

Given the transmittance
\tau
,
it is not difficult to find
the average photon number of the output state
n_{\tau}
:

\displaystyle n_{\tau}=\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\{\hat{n}\hat{\rho}(\tau)\}=\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\{\hat{n}_{\tau}\hat{\rho}\}\equiv\langle{\hat{n}_{\tau}}\rangle=\tau n_{\mathrm{in}}+(1-\tau)n_{\mathrm{th}},

(8)

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{in}}=\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\{\hat{n}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{in}}\}=\langle{\psi_{\mathrm{in}}}|\hat{n}|{\psi_{\mathrm{in}}}\rangle,

(9)

where

\hat{n}={\hat{a}}^{\dagger}\hat{a}

and
\hat{n}_{\tau}={\hat{a}}^{\dagger}_{\tau}\hat{a}_{\tau}
.

Since
\hat{n}^{2}-\hat{n}={\hat{a}}^{\dagger}{\hat{a}}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\hat{a}\equiv:\hat{n}^{2}:
,
the difference between the variance and the mean photon number
for the quantum state
\hat{\rho}(\tau)
can be computed as
the
q
-parameter given by

\displaystyle q_{\tau}=\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\{:\hat{n}^{2}:\hat{\rho}(\tau)\}-n^{2}_{\tau}.

(10)

Clearly, for
\hat{\rho}(\tau)
,
the photon statistics is sub-Poissonian if and only if
the parameter (
10
) is negative.
Note that the ratio
q_{\tau}/n_{\tau}
gives
the well-known Mandel
Q
-parameter
Mandel and Wolf 1995
,
Q^{(M)}_{\tau}
,
introduced in Refs.
Mandel 1979
;
Short and Mandel 1983
.
Note that the
Q
-parameter is also
related to the normalized second-order correlation function

g^{(2)}_{\tau}(0)=Q^{(M)}_{\tau}/n_{\tau}+1
and
the Fano factor
F_{\tau}=Q^{(M)}_{\tau}+1
.

We can now use the relation

\displaystyle\langle:\hat{n}^{2}_{\tau}:\rangle=\tau^{2}\langle{:\hat{n}^{2}:}\rangle+2(1-\tau)^{2}n_{\mathrm{th}}^{2}+4\tau(1-\tau)n_{\mathrm{in}}n_{\mathrm{th}}

(11)

derived with the help
of the identity

\langle{{\hat{b}}^{\dagger}{\hat{b}}^{\dagger}\hat{b}\hat{b}}\rangle=2n_{\mathrm{th}}^{2}

to deduce the explicit expression for the parameter (
10
)

\displaystyle q_{\tau}=\langle:\hat{n}^{2}_{\tau}:\rangle-n_{\tau}^{2}=\tau^{2}q_{\mathrm{in}}+(1-\tau)^{2}n_{\mathrm{th}}^{2}+2\tau(1-\tau)n_{\mathrm{in}}n_{\mathrm{th}},

(12)

where

\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{in}}=\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\{:\hat{n}^{2}:\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{in}}\}-n_{\mathrm{ini}}^{2}=\langle{:\hat{n}^{2}:}\rangle-n_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}.

(13)

In the zero-temperature limit with
n_{\mathrm{th}}=0
,
formula (
12
) shows that
losses cannot destroy the sub-Poissoinian statistics of input light.
The latter is generally no longer the case at non-zero temperatures.
In the subsequent section we will discuss the conditions
for negativity of the parameter
q_{\tau}
.

III
Fluctuating losses and critical transmittance

Now we extend the results of the preceding section
to the case where the transmittance fluctuates
and thus should be treated as a random variable.
In this case, the output density matrix takes the generalized form:

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{out}}=\int_{0}^{1}P(\tau)\hat{\rho}(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau,

(14)

where
P(\tau)
is the probability density function (PDF) of the transmittance.
We can use
Eqs. (
8
) and (
5
)
with
\hat{\rho}(\tau)
replaced by
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{out}}

to introduce the output parameter
q_{\mathrm{out}}

as follows

\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{out}}=\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\{:\hat{n}^{2}:\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{out}}\}-\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\{\hat{n}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{out}}\}^{2}=\langle\langle:\hat{n}^{2}_{\tau}:\rangle\rangle_{\tau}-\langle n_{\tau}\rangle_{\tau}^{2}=\langle q_{\tau}\rangle_{\tau}+[\langle n_{\tau}^{2}\rangle_{\tau}-\langle n_{\tau}\rangle_{\tau}^{2}],

(15)

where
\langle{\cdots}\rangle_{\tau}=\int_{0}^{1}\cdots P(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau
.
After substituting formulas (
8
) and (
10
)
into Eq. (
15
),
we obtain the parameter
q_{\mathrm{out}}
in the following explicit form:

\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{out}}=\overline{\tau}^{2}(q_{\mathrm{in}}-n_{\mathrm{in}}^{2})+(\overline{\tau}n_{\mathrm{in}}+(1-\overline{\tau})n_{\mathrm{th}})^{2}+

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\tau)\{q_{\mathrm{in}}-n_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}+2(n_{\mathrm{in}}-n_{\mathrm{th}})^{2})\},

(16)

where
\overline{\tau}=\langle{\tau}\rangle_{\tau}
is the averaged transmittance and

\mathrm{Var}(\tau)=\langle{\tau^{2}}\rangle_{\tau}-\overline{\tau}^{2}
is the variance of the transmittance.

Since the transmittance varies from zero to unity,
0\leq\tau\leq 1
,
and the variance
\mathrm{Var}(\tau)
cannot be negative,

\mathrm{Var}(\tau)\geq 0
,
the mean of squared transmittance cannot exceed the average transmittance
and must satisfy the condition:

\overline{\tau}^{2}\leq\langle{\tau^{2}}\rangle_{\tau}\leq\overline{\tau}
.
As a result, the variance
is bounded from above by the product

(1-\overline{\tau})\overline{\tau}
:

0\leq\mathrm{Var}(\tau)\leq(1-\overline{\tau})\overline{\tau}
.
In our model, we
assume that

\langle{\tau^{2}}\rangle_{\tau}=F\overline{\tau}+(1-F)\overline{\tau}^{2}
,
so that the variance takes the following form

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\tau)=\langle{\tau^{2}}\rangle-\overline{\tau}^{2}=F(1-\overline{\tau})\overline{\tau}

(17)

where
0\leq F\leq 1
is the parameter characterizing
the strength of fluctuations.

In what follows, we shall treat the fluctuation strength
F

as a phenomenological parameter which is independent of
\overline{\tau}
.
The variance representation (
17
) can now be used
to express the
q
-parameter (
III
)
in terms of
\overline{\tau}
as follows

\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{out}}(\overline{\tau})=\overline{\tau}^{2}(q_{\mathrm{in}}-a)+\overline{\tau}(a-n_{\mathrm{th}}^{2})+n_{\mathrm{th}}^{2},

(18)

where

\displaystyle a=2n_{\mathrm{in}}n_{\mathrm{th}}-n^{2}_{\mathrm{th}}+gF,\quad g=q_{\mathrm{in}}-n^{2}_{\mathrm{in}}+2(n_{\mathrm{in}}-n_{\mathrm{th}})^{2}.

(19)

In the limiting case of
transparent medium described by the identity channel with
\overline{\tau}=1
,
the
q
-parameter is equal to its initial value

q_{\mathrm{in}}
given by Eq. (
13
).
For sub-Poissonian light,
the parameter
q_{\mathrm{in}}
is negative, so that
q_{\mathrm{out}}(1)=q_{\mathrm{in}}<0
.
In the opposite case with vanishing transmittance,
\overline{\tau}=1
,
from Eq. (
18
), the
q
-parameter is positive,

q_{\mathrm{out}}(0)=n_{\mathrm{th}}^{2}>0
.
Clearly, this implies that
the transmitted light will remain sub-Poissonian
provided the average transmittance exceeds its critical value:

\overline{\tau}>\tau_{c}
.

From Eq. (
18
), it is not difficult to
find the analytical expression for the critical transmittance
(the transmittance threshold).
So, we deduce the inequality

\displaystyle\overline{\tau}>\tau_{c}=-\frac{(a-n_{\mathrm{th}}^{2})+\sqrt{(a+n_{\mathrm{th}}^{2})^{2}-4n_{\mathrm{th}}^{2}q_{\mathrm{in}}}}{2(q_{\mathrm{in}}-a)}

(20)

giving the condition that
effects of transmittance fluctuations and temperature
fail to destroy the sub-Poissonian statistics of light and
the
q
-parameter of the transmitted (output) light is negative,

q_{\mathrm{out}}<0
.

We conclude this section with the remark on two important special cases
with
T=0
and
F=0
, respectively.
In the zero-temperature limit,
the expression of the transmittance threshold is simplified as follows

\displaystyle\tau_{c}|_{T=0}=\frac{F(q_{\mathrm{in}}+n_{\mathrm{in}}^{2})}{F(q_{\mathrm{in}}+n_{\mathrm{in}}^{2})-q_{\mathrm{in}}},

(21)

whereas
the critical transmittance for
the case, where fluctuations of the transmittance are negligible, is given by

\displaystyle\tau_{c}|_{F=0}=\frac{n_{\mathrm{th}}}{n_{\mathrm{th}}+\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}-q_{\mathrm{in}}}-n_{\mathrm{in}}}.

(22)

Clearly, when
F
and
T
are both vanishing, the critical transmittance is zero.
This is the well-known case of non-fluctuating pure-loss bosonic channel
where
q_{\mathrm{out}}=\tau^{2}q_{\mathrm{in}}
(see Eq. (
12
) with
n_{\mathrm{th}}=0
).

IV
Effects of fluctuations and temperature

In this section, we apply our analytical results
to the two special cases of
input states: the displaced squeezed states
and the odd Schrödinger cat states.
More specifically,
we shall study how the temperature and transmitance fluctuations
influence the transmittance threshold for these states.

IV.1
Squeezed light

The density matrix

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{in}}=|{\psi_{\mathrm{sq}}}\rangle\langle{\psi_{\mathrm{sq}}}|,\quad|{\psi_{\mathrm{sq}}}\rangle=\hat{D}(\beta)\hat{S}(\xi)|0\rangle

(23)

describes the case of displaced squeezed vacuum states

\hat{S}(\xi)

\hat{D}(\beta)

\displaystyle\hat{S}(\xi)=e^{(\xi(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{2}-\xi^{*}\hat{a}^{2})/2},\quad\hat{D}(\beta)=e^{\beta\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\beta^{*}\hat{a}},

For the squeezed state (

V
Conclusions

In this paper,
we have studied effects of the thermal-loss channel with fluctuating transmittance
on the sub-Poissonian light whose
non-classicality is characterized by
the
q
-parameter (see Eq. (
10
)).
We have combined the input-output relation for the
q
-parameter (
III
)
with the variance of the transmittance
parameterized using the fluctuation strength parameter (
17
)
to show that the condition for sub-Poissonian statistics of photon at the channel output
is determined by the critical transmittance (
20
).
For the cases of the
displaced squeezed state (see Eq. (
23
))
and the odd optical cat state (see Eq. (
29
)),
the critical transmittance is computed as a function of
the squared displacement amplitude,
|\beta|^{2}
,
at different values of temperature and the fluctuation parameter.
In contrast to what is expected, under certain conditions,
an increase in either the fluctuation strength
or the temperature may result in a decrease in
the critical transmittance.

Note that the key point greatly simplifying our analysis
is the parameterization of the transmittance
variance (
17
),
where the fluctuation strength
F

is introduced as a phenomenological parameter
which is independent of the mean transmittance

\overline{\tau}
.
A more sophisticated treatment of
the atmospheric channels
Vasylyev et al. 2016
;
Vasylyev et al. 2018
;
Klen and Semenov 2023

requires computing the first-order and second-order moments
of the transmittance,
\langle{\tau}\rangle=\overline{\tau^{2}}
and
\langle{\tau^{2}}\rangle=\overline{\tau^{2}}
,
from the correlation functions
derived using the phase approximation of
the Huygens-Kirchhoff method
Banakh and Mironov 1977
;
Banakh and Mironov 1979
.
