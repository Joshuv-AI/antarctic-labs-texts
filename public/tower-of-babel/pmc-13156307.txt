Ubiquitination-driven fibroblast dysfunction: a multi-omics blueprint for precision diagnosis and therapy in diabetic foot ulcer
Authors: Wenqiang Wang, Xiao Peng, Qikai Hua, Yanbing Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) is a severe diabetes complication affecting 15–25% of patients and frequently causing lower extremity amputations. While ubiquitination regulates diverse cellular functions, ubiquitination-related genes (URGs) in DFU pathogenesis remain unexplored. We analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing data from 29 samples (19 controls, 10 DFU) and bulk RNA sequencing from 60 samples (25 controls, 35 DFU). Using 1,610 URGs from GeneCards database, we performed single-cell analysis, pseudotime analysis, and cell communication studies. Four machine learning algorithms identified diagnostic biomarkers, followed by functional enrichment, immune infiltration, and molecular docking analyses. Key gene expression patterns were further validated by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) in independent clinical samples (n = 6 per group). Single-cell analysis identified 12 cell types with significantly elevated URGs in DFU fibroblasts. A pathogenic fibroblast subpopulation exhibited enhanced stemness and dysregulated communication networks. Machine learning identified four key URGs (MEF2A, SKIL, MAF, KRT5) as diagnostic biomarkers, achieving optimal performance (AUC 0.957). SKIL showed strongest correlation with metabolic reprogramming. Two distinct molecular subtypes with different immune profiles were identified. Lumicolchicine and ramipril were identified as potential SKIL-targeting therapeutics. This study provides the first URGs characterization in DFU, identifying pathogenic fibroblasts and SKIL as key regulators. The diagnostic signature and therapeutic targets establish foundations for precision DFU treatment approaches. These findings support the development of URG-based diagnostic strategies and provide a translational rationale for repurposing clinically approved drugs, such as ramipril, for precision management of DFU.

Introduction

Diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) is a severe complication of diabetes mellitus, affecting approximately 15–25% of diabetic patients and frequently leading to lower extremity amputations1,2. The pathophysiology of DFU involves disrupted wound healing processes, including impaired angiogenesis, delayed epithelialization, and aberrant extracellular matrix remodeling3,4. Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying these pathological alterations is crucial for developing effective therapeutic strategies. Among various regulatory mechanisms, ubiquitination is a fundamental post-translational modification that regulates protein stability, localization, and function through the covalent attachment of ubiquitin proteins to target substrates5,6. This process controls diverse cellular functions including cell cycle regulation, signal transduction, and stress responses7,8. Although ubiquitination dysregulation has been implicated in various diseases, the specific role of ubiquitination-related genes (URGs) in DFU remains largely unexplored9,10.This knowledge gap may be partly attributed to the historical focus of DFU research on inflammation, ischemia, and infection, as well as the technical challenges in systematically capturing ubiquitination-related regulation at a transcriptomic level.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has revolutionized our understanding of cellular heterogeneity and disease mechanisms by enabling gene expression profiling at individual cell resolution11,12. This technology provides unprecedented insights into cell-type-specific responses and intercellular communication networks in disease processes13,14. Despite advances in DFU research, the comprehensive landscape of ubiquitination-related molecular signatures and their cell-type-specific roles in DFU progression remain poorly characterized. Additionally, reliable biomarkers for DFU diagnosis are still lacking.

Previous DFU transcriptomic studies have largely emphasized immune dysregulation, inflammatory responses, and angiogenesis-related pathways15; however, the specific contribution of ubiquitination-related regulatory networks has not been comprehensively addressed. Given the central role of ubiquitination in regulating protein stability and signaling cascades, its potential involvement in fibroblas16 t dysfunction and impaired wound healing in DFU warrants systematic investigation17.

To address these gaps, we conducted an integrative analysis combining bulk and scRNA-seq to systematically investigate ubiquitination-related signatures in DFU. Our study aimed to characterize URGs expression patterns, identify key cellular subpopulations, develop machine learning-based diagnostic models, and explore potential therapeutic targets. By delineating ubiquitination-related molecular features at both bulk and single-cell resolution, this study seeks to advance the understanding of DFU pathogenesis and facilitate the discovery of clinically relevant biomarkers.

Methods

Data collection

The scRNA-seq data for DFU were obtained from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Specifically, dataset GSE165816 was used for single-cell analysis, which includes 29 samples comprising 19 control foot skin tissue samples and 10 DFU samples, all sequenced using the 10X Genomics platform14. In addition to GSE165816, transcriptomic data from GSE68183, GSE80178, GSE134431, and GSE199939 were retrieved from GEO and integrated for downstream analyses, including differential expression analysis and machine learning–based modeling18–20. These four datasets together contributed 25 control samples and 35 DFU samples. Prior to integration, normalization and batch effect correction corrected using the limma package, and the effectiveness of batch correction was evaluated by comparing global expression distributions across samples before and after normalization.

Furthermore, a total of 1,610 URGs were collected from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/). Genes with a GeneCards relevance score > 5 were retained, consistent with commonly adopted practices in previous URG-related studies to define genes with moderate to high confidence associations. This filtering strategy helps reduce background noise while preserving biological relevance for downstream analyses21.

ScRNA-seq data processing

Single-cell data analysis was conducted using Seurat (v5.2.1)11. After importing the datasets, quality control measures were applied to exclude cells with > 50% mitochondrial gene expression, > 1% hemoglobin gene expression, fewer than 200 or more than 2500 expressed genes22. The filtered scRNA-seq datasets were then merged and normalized. Highly variable genes (n = 2000) were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function. All genes were scaled using ScaleData, followed by dimensionality reduction via RunPCA on the selected variable genes. Clustering analysis was performed using FindNeighbors and FindClusters, and further dimensionality reduction was conducted using RunTSNE. Cell type annotation was carried out with SingleR, based on established references23. Marker genes for each cluster were identified using the FindAllMarkers function.

Fibroblast subtypes were defined based on their gene expression profiles and clustering results. By integrating both molecular and computational information, distinct fibroblast subsets were delineated. To infer the differentiation trajectories among fibroblast subtypes, pseudotime analysis was conducted using Slingshot (v2.12.0), a robust lineage inference tool that models cellular transitions in reduced dimensional space24. Cell-cell communication analysis among fibroblast subsets was performed using CellChat (v2.1.2), a comprehensive framework designed to systematically infer and visualize intercellular signaling networks based on single-cell transcriptomics data25.

To quantify the metabolic activity of different fibroblast subsets at single-cell resolution, we employed scMetabolism (v0.2.1), which enables the assessment of pathway-level metabolic variations based on gene expression signatures26. To identify key fibroblast subsets and gene modules associated with DFU, high-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (hdWGCNA) was performed using hdWGCNA (v0.4.00)27. This tool constructs single-cell co-expression networks and identifies highly co-expressed, functionally related gene modules. The optimal soft thresholding power was selected according to the scale-free topology fit index. Additionally, AUCell (v1.26.0) was used to evaluate the activity of URGs in each cell28. AUCell calculates the Area Under the Curve (AUC) score for each cell relative to a predefined gene set, which provides an effective way to assess the gene set activity at the single-cell level.

Tissue distribution of cell subsets

We computed the observed-to-expected ratio (Ro/e) across different tissues to evaluate tissue specificity29. The expected number of cells for each cell subset–tissue pair was estimated using the Chi-squared test. Ro/e values were visualized using a customized heatmap, in which each value was represented by a symbol based on its magnitude: Ro/e > 1 as “+++”, 0.8 < Ro/e ≤ 1 as “++”, 0.2 ≤ Ro/e ≤ 0.8 as “+”, 0 < Ro/e < 0.2 as “+/-“, and Ro/e = 0 as “−“. This symbolic representation allowed intuitive identification of subset–tissue preferences.

Machine learning algorithms

We employed LASSO, SVM-RFE, Boruta, and Decision Tree (DT) algorithms for feature selection to identify DFU-associated genes. All analyses were performed in R with a fixed random seed (set. seed = 12345) to ensure reproducibility.

For LASSO, a binomial logistic regression model with L1 regularization (α = 1) was implemented using the glmnet package. The optimal regularization parameter (λ) was determined by 10-fold cross-validation based on the minimum cross-validated deviance (λ_min), and genes with non-zero coefficients at λ_min were selected. SVM-RFE was conducted using a linear kernel, with features recursively eliminated based on the absolute magnitude of the SVM weight vector. When the number of features exceeded 50, approximately half of the lowest-ranked features were removed per iteration; when ≤ 50, one feature was removed per iteration until 10 features remained. Boruta feature selection was performed with 500 iterations (maxRuns = 500), retaining both confirmed and tentative features. DT-based feature selection was conducted by ranking variables according to importance, and the top 10 features were retained. The final candidate genes were defined as the intersection of features identified by these four methods.

The dataset was divided into training and testing sets at a 7:3 ratio. Multiple classification models were constructed, including PLS, RF, DTS, SVM, Logistic Regression, KNN, XGBoost, Neural Network, and glmBoost. Comparative model performance was assessed to identify the most suitable model for DFU prediction. Model performance was evaluated using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC), calculated with the pROC package (v1.18.0). Model interpretability was assessed using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) implemented via the shapviz package (v0.9.7)30.

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis is a widely used method for large-scale functional enrichment, encompassing biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), and cellular components (CC). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) is an extensively utilized database that provides information on genomes, biological pathways, diseases, and drugs31,32. Differentially expressed genes between the groups were subjected to GO annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler package in R. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant33.

To investigate key BP associated with hub genes, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), which assesses whether specific gene sets exhibit statistically significant differences between the groups34. The gene set “h.all.v2024.1.Hs.symbols.gmt” from the MSigDB database was used to analyze the biological functions of hub genes. A significance threshold of p.adjust < 0.05 was considered indicative of enrichment. Additionally, to compare the differences in related biological processes between the groups, Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) was conducted35. The gene set “h.all.v2024.1.Hs.symbols.gmt” from MSigDB was used for GSVA analysis, with a significance level of p. adjust < 0.05 indicating enrichment.

Immune microenvironment analysis

Immune infiltration was analyzed using the CIBERSORT algorithm based on 22 immune cell signatures to assess the immune landscape between DFU and control samples36. The relative abundance of immune cells was visualized using bar plots, and group differences were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, displayed as box plots. The relationship between the expression of hub URGs (upregulated response genes) and immune infiltration levels was assessed using Spearman correlation. Significant associations were then visualized using Mantel testcorrelation heatmaps, where the correlation between the distance matrices of URGs expression and immune infiltration was calculated and visualized, showing the strength and direction of the correlation with a color gradient.

Identification of URGs subtypes related to DFU

Unsupervised consensus clustering was conducted using the “ConsensusClusterPlus” package to uncover potential subtypes of DFU based on the expression profiles of URGs37. Key parameters included: the maximum number of clusters (maxK = 9), the PAM (Partitioning Around Medoids) clustering algorithm, the Euclidean distance metric, and 50 resampling iterations. The optimal cluster number was determined based on the cumulative distribution function (CDF) plots and the area under the CDF curve. To validate the robustness of the clustering and visualize the distribution of the identified subtypes, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed.

Molecular docking of key URGs with therapeutic compounds

Potential therapeutic compounds targeting key URGs were identified using Enrichr, querying the DSigDB v1.0 database. The 3D structures of the key URGs were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB), prioritizing high-resolution crystal structures (resolution < 3.0 Å) with available co-crystallized ligands when possible. Prior to docking, protein structures were prepared by removing non-essential crystallographic water molecules and ions, while retaining cofactors or metal ions essential for ligand binding. Polar hydrogens were added and Gasteiger charges were assigned. Ligand structures were converted to three-dimensional conformations and subjected to energy minimization using a molecular mechanics force field prior to docking. Molecular docking simulations were performed using AutoDock4 and AutoGrid4, with the docking grid centered on the known or predicted binding site. Docking was conducted using the Lamarckian genetic algorithm with 100 independent runs. Default parameters were applied unless otherwise specified. Docking scores lower than − 5.0 kcal/mol were considered indicative of favorable binding affinity. The top-ranked pose with the lowest binding energy was selected for visualization and interaction analysis. The docking poses and ligand-receptor interactions were visualized using PyMOL38.

qRT-PCR quantitative detection

Sex was not considered as a biological variable. For quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) validation, DFU tissue samples and control samples were collected from an independent patient cohort, which was not overlapping with the samples used for scRNA-seq analysis. A total of six biological replicates per group (n = 6) were included for qRT-PCR experiments.Statistical significance was calculated using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, and exact P values are provided in the corresponding figure.

Total cellular RNA was isolated using the E.Z.N.A.® Total RNA Kit I (Omega Bio-tek) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA purity and concentration were assessed using a microplate reader, and 1 µg of high-quality RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the HiScript III RT SuperMix kit (Vazyme).

Real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR was performed on a LightCycler® 96 system (Roche) using SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme). The amplification conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s.

The relative expression levels of MEF2A, SKIL, MAF, and KRT5 were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH used as the internal reference gene for normalization. Primer sequences for the four hub URGs used in qRT-PCR validation are listed in Table 1.

Results

Single-cell analysis

We processed and analyzed the 19-control foot skin tissue samples and 10 DFU samples included in GSE165816. After stringent quality control, we successfully obtained a Seurat object consisting of 23,572 cells and 54,046 genes. After performing dimensionality reduction using t-SNE for all cells, we generated 27 clusters (Fig. 1A) and identified 12 cell types (Fig. 1B), including MSC (Mesenchymal Stem Cells), Endothelial cells, Chondrocytes, Monocytes, Astrocytes, B cells, T cells, Keratinocytes, Smooth muscle cells, Myelocytes, Fibroblasts, and Osteoblasts. Figure 1C shows the expression patterns of cell-type specific marker genes.

To determine the differential activity of URGs in different cell types, we used the AUCell algorithm. Figure 1D illustrates the expression levels of URGs in each cell, revealing higher expression of URGs in Fibroblasts and Osteoblasts. Further analysis of the differential expression of URGs between the DFU and Control groups was performed at the single-cell level within each cell type. Statistical significance was assessed using a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Fibroblasts exhibited significantly higher URG scores in the DFU group compared to the Control group (P < 0.001), while B cells, Chondrocytes, Endothelial cells, Osteoblasts, and Smooth muscle cells showed higher URGs activity in the Control group. (Fig. 1E).

The comparison of the proportion of cell subtypes between the Control and DFU groups in foot skin tissues shows that Fibroblasts, Monocytes, T cells, and B cells are significantly increased in the DFU group (Fig. 1F). Additionally, by calculating the Ro/e (observed-to-expected ratio), we assessed the cell distribution specificity between the Control and DFU groups (Fig. 1G). The results indicate that Fibroblasts exhibit the most significant cellular disturbance between the Control and DFU skin tissues, suggesting that they may play a key role in the DFU progression mediated by URGs.

Classification of pathogenic fibroblast subtypes

To investigate the role of fibroblasts in the progression of DFU, we performed repeated t-SNE analysis to cluster the fibroblasts into subgroups. The subclustering of fibroblasts further identified six distinct subtypes. Based on tissue origin, we observed that DFU group fibroblasts harbored an additional subgroup 3 when compared to the Control group (Fig. 2A, B). Thus, we defined subgroup 3 in DFU as DFU_Fibroblast subpopulations, while the other fibroblast subpopulations from the Control group were designated as Control_Fibroblast subpopulations.

Subsequently, we performed pseudotime analysis to demonstrate the differentiation trajectory from Control_Fibroblasts to DFU_Fibroblasts (Fig. 2C). Figure 2D further confirmed the correlation between URGs activity and differentiation time. Additionally, CytoTRACE analysis indicated that DFU_Fibroblasts exhibited a higher level of stemness (Fig. 2E), and correlation analysis also revealed a positive correlation between URGs activity and CytoTRACE2_Relative (Fig. 2F).

To further elucidate the functional differences between Control_Fibroblasts and DFU_Fibroblasts, we performed cell communication analysis. DFU_Fibroblasts showed significantly higher expression and specific enrichment of multiple signaling pathways, especially in the outgoing and incoming modes of MIF, VEGF, TWEAK, CXCL, FGF, COMPLEMENT, and IGFBP (Fig. 2G). These pathways are widely involved in immune regulation, angiogenesis, cell proliferation, and immune response, suggesting that the communication network of fibroblasts in the DFU environment has undergone remodeling, which may play a critical role in pathological tissue repair and chronic inflammation. Moreover, we conducted cell communication analysis based on input and output signals. The results demonstrated that when DFU_Fibroblasts function as receptors, they interact significantly with TNFSF12 and MIF, indicating key roles in immune response and fibrosis signaling (Fig. 2H). As ligands, DFU_Fibroblasts showed significant expression of the MIF (CD74 + ACKR3) receptor in other cell subtypes (Fig. 2I). The cell communication network analysis indicated that DFU_Fibroblasts, acting as receptors, play a central role in several important signaling pathways, such as CXCL, MIF, TWEAK, and VEGF, promoting immune cell recruitment, inflammation, and angiogenesis. These pathways likely contribute to immune regulation and tissue repair in DFU. Furthermore, DFU_Fibroblasts, acting as ligands, are also crucial in signaling pathways such as CXCL12-CXCR4, MIF-CD74, and VEGF, particularly in immune cell activation, angiogenesis, and fibrosis (Fig. 2J-M).

We performed functional enrichment analysis, and the results showed that DFU_Fibroblasts exhibited upregulated pathways related to cytoskeletal reorganization, lipolysis, and protein digestion (Fig. 2N), indicating their critical role in tissue remodeling. Concurrently, downregulated pathways such as IL-17 signaling and ferroptosis (Fig. 2O) reflect a suppression of immune and stress-related processes. Metabolic differences analysis revealed increased glycolysis and altered sugar metabolism in DFU_Fibroblasts, suggesting a metabolic adaptation to the chronic environment of DFU (Fig. 2P).

Next, we conducted hdWGCNA analysis, setting the soft threshold to 8 based on the results (Fig. 2Q). Further examination showed significant enrichment of modules 2, 3, 5, and 10 in cluster 3 (DFU_Fibroblasts) (Fig. 2R). A total of 528 genes were identified from these modules. Functional enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of pathways related to extracellular matrix (ECM) organization and cytoskeletal remodeling, indicating that DFU_Fibroblasts play a crucial role in ECM remodeling and cell migration during wound healing (Fig. 2S).

Comprehensive analysis of feature genes and machine learning models in DFU diagnosis

Our analysis integrated four transcriptomic datasets (GSE68183, GSE80178, GSE134431, and GSE199939), comprising 25 control samples derived from non-diabetic foot skin tissue and 35 DFU samples derived from diabetic foot ulcer tissue, to identify 2242 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 1060 upregulated and 1182 downregulated genes. Using a Venn diagram (Fig. 3A), we identified 8 overlapping DFU URGs (HERC2, PHF2, KDM5A, SKIL, ZEB2, MAF, MEF2A, RB1). We employed four machine learning algorithms—LASSO (Fig. 3B, C), SVM-RFE (Fig. 3D, E), Boruta (Fig. 3F), and DT (Fig. 3G)—to select the most relevant feature genes. Intersection analysis of the genes selected by these algorithms identified MEF2A, SKIL, MAF, and KRT5 as the only overlapping genes retained across all four machine learning methods (Fig. 3H), with SKIL showing significant upregulation in the DFU group (Fig. 3I).

The dataset was randomly split into training and test sets (7:3 ratio), and several models (PLS, RF, DTS, SVM, Logistic Regression, KNN, XGBoost, NeuralNet, and glmBoost) were trained with 10-fold cross-validation and evaluated using the AUC value. Based on the ROC curve analysis, KNN demonstrated the best performance across both the training (AUC 0.948) and test (AUC 0.957) sets, making it the most robust model, while RF showed a significant drop in performance from the training set (AUC 1.000) to the test set (AUC 0.879), indicating overfitting (Fig. 3K, L). Additionally, the PR curve analysis further confirmed KNN’s strong performance in both sets (Fig. 3M, N).

To visually explain the importance of the predictive factors, we used SHAP to assess the contribution of four feature genes in DFU diagnosis. Figure 3O shows the importance of the four feature genes in our model. Each point in the figure represents an individual sample, where the light dots represent high-risk values and the dark dots represent low-risk values. Larger absolute SHAP values indicate a greater impact on model prediction. By calculating SHAP interaction values, we visualized the contribution of predictive factor interactions to the model prediction (Fig. 3P). Additionally, we provide a typical example to demonstrate the interpretability of the model (Fig. 3Q). Among the four feature genes, MEF2A and SKIL contributed the most to DFU diagnosis.

Our GSVA analysis revealed key correlations between the four hub pathways (MEF2A, SKIL, MAF, and KRT5) and major BP (Fig. 3R-U). High expression of KRT5 was linked to glycolysis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and oxidative stress, while negatively correlated with cell adhesion. MAF upregulated glycolysis, angiogenesis, and inflammatory response, while downregulating immune activation. MEF2A was associated with glycolysis, DNA repair, and MTORC1 signaling, while inhibiting cell differentiation. SKIL upregulated WNT/β-catenin signaling and oxidative phosphorylation, while suppressing cell apoptosis (Fig. 3R-U). GENEMANIA analysis further highlighted the central role of MEF2A, SKIL, MAF, and KRT5 in regulating key cellular processes, including glycolysis, extracellular matrix remodeling, and inflammation (Fig. 3V).

Immune cell infiltration and molecular patterns in DFU fibroblasts based on consensus clustering and correlation analysis

We employed the CIBERSORT algorithm to assess the immune cell composition in the Control and DFU groups. The results showed significant differences in immune cell infiltration between the two groups (Fig. 4A). Specifically, DFU samples exhibited elevated proportions of inflammatory immune cells such as monocytes, M0/M1 macrophages, neutrophils, and activated mast cells, while anti-inflammatory or regulatory cells including Tregs and CD4 + memory resting T cells were decreased (Fig. 4C). To further explore the immunological relevance of key URGs, we performed a correlation analysis (Fig. 4B) and visualized the associations between hub URGs (MEF2A, SKIL, MAF, KRT5) and immune cells using link plots (Fig. 4D). The results suggested strong correlations with macrophages, T cells, monocytes, and mast cells, indicating their potential roles in modulating immune responses in DFU.

To investigate the molecular patterns of hub URGs in DFU, we performed consensus clustering (Fig. 4E, F, G), identifying two subtypes, clusterA and clusterB. Immune cell infiltration analysis demonstrated significant differences in immune cell composition between these subtypes. ClusterA showed more infiltration of immune cells involved in inflammation, whereas clusterB was enriched in Fibroblasts (Fig. 4H). GSEA revealed that clusterA was enriched in pathways like adipogenesis, bile acid metabolism, and estrogen response (Fig. 4I), while clusterB showed enrichment in pathways related to angiogenesis, IL6/JAK/STAT3 signaling, and TGF-beta signaling (Fig. 4J), pointing to distinct immune and tissue remodeling processes in DFU.

In the single-cell dataset, we identified the differential expression of hub URGs in Control_Fibroblasts (control group fibroblasts) and DFU_Fibroblasts (diabetic foot fibroblasts) (Fig. 5A). The results showed a significant upregulation of SKIL in DFU_Fibroblasts, suggesting that SKIL may play a crucial role in the functionality and disease progression of fibroblasts in diabetic foot. Further analysis revealed a significant correlation between the URGs score and hub URGs in fibroblasts (Fig. 5B), with SKIL showing a notable association, indicating its potential as a biomarker.

Further studies explored the role of SKIL in fibroblast differentiation (Fig. 5C). Pseudotime analysis revealed that SKIL expression increased progressively, indicating its key role in fibroblast proliferation, migration, and ECM synthesis. Dysregulation of SKIL in diabetic foot fibroblasts may impair wound healing and tissue repair.

To understand SKIL’s role in diabetic foot, we analyzed its involvement in metabolic enrichment (Fig. 5D). The results showed that SKIL was closely associated with the activation of glycolysis, pyruvate metabolism, and amino acid metabolism. The upregulation of SKIL, linked to the activation of these pathways, suggests that SKIL dysregulation may contribute to impaired fibroblast function through altered metabolic processes, thereby hampering the healing of DFU. Based on the DSigDB database, we constructed a drug hub gene regulatory network for SKIL (Fig. 5E) and performed molecular docking of lumicolchicine and ramipril with SKIL using AutoDockTools (Figs. 5F, G). The binding free energy of lumicolchicine with SKIL was − 6.2 kcal/mol, while ramipril’s binding free energy was − 6.4 kcal/mol, suggesting that both drugs may regulate SKIL, providing a theoretical basis for future drug development. Finally, to validate our bioinformatics findings, we collected clinical samples including normal foot skin tissues and DFU tissues (6 samples per group) and performed qRT-PCR analysis to confirm the expression levels of the four hub genes. The qPCR results corroborated our bioinformatics findings (Figs. 5H).

Discussion

This study represents the first comprehensive investigation of ubiquitination-related molecular signatures in DFU using an integrated approach combining bulk RNA sequencing, scRNA sequencing, and machine learning algorithms. The data provide novel insights into the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying DFU pathogenesis, with particular emphasis on the critical role of ubiquitination-mediated processes in fibroblast dysfunction and impaired wound healing. Previous DFU transcriptomic studies have predominantly focused on immune infiltration, inflammation, and angiogenesis-related pathways. In contrast, the present study specifically interrogates ubiquitination-related transcriptional programs at both bulk and single-cell resolution, thereby expanding the current mechanistic framework of DFU pathogenesis.

Single-cell analysis revealed a distinct fibroblast subpopulation (DFU_Fibroblasts) that was predominantly observed in DFU tissues and rarely detected in healthy controls. This population exhibits several characteristic features that distinguish it from normal fibroblasts: enhanced stemness properties, altered metabolic programming, and dysregulated intercellular communication networks. Pseudotime analysis demonstrated a clear differentiation trajectory from normal fibroblasts to this pathogenic state, suggesting that DFU_Fibroblasts represent a maladaptive cellular response to the diabetic wound environment rather than a normal healing process. The identification of DFU_Fibroblasts aligns with recent advances in understanding fibroblast heterogeneity in pathological conditions. Similar pathogenic fibroblast populations have been described in other chronic inflammatory diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis39,40. However, to our knowledge, this is the first study to identify and characterize a putative transcriptionally distinct fibroblast subset in DFU using single-cell resolution, providing a foundation for understanding why diabetic wounds fail to heal normally.

The integrated analysis identified four key URGs (MEF2A, SKIL, MAF, and KRT5) that serve as both diagnostic biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets for DFU. Among these, SKIL emerged as the most significant contributor to DFU pathogenesis, showing consistent upregulation across multiple datasets and strong correlation with disease progression. SKIL is a known negative regulator of TGF-β signaling and has been implicated in various pathological processes including cancer and fibrosis41,42.Studies have shown that the TGF-β signaling pathway plays a critical role in regulating fibroblast proliferation and collagen accumulation to coordinate wound healing43. Inhibition of the TGF-β pathway is often associated with impaired wound healing44. In the context of DFU, SKIL upregulation was identified at the transcript level and validated by qRT-PCR. This metabolic reprogramming is characteristic of pathological fibroblasts and has been observed in other chronic wound conditions45. Furthermore, the upregulation of TGF-β signaling has been considered a key therapeutic target in DFU treatment strategies46. This provides a mechanistic link between SKIL and DFU: persistent upregulation of SKIL may lead to excessive suppression of TGF-β signaling, resulting in fibroblast dysfunction, reduced collagen synthesis, and impaired granulation tissue formation. Additionally, SKIL upregulation may be associated with the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and the inhibition of apoptotic pathways—mechanisms that are significant in cancer and fibrosis and may also be relevant to the pathogenesis of DFU. However, these mechanistic inferences are based primarily on bioinformatics analyses and transcript-level validation and therefore should be interpreted with caution. Functional validation, including Western blot analysis and gain- or loss-of-function experiments in fibroblast models, will be necessary to establish causal relationships.

Integration of multiple machine learning algorithms (LASSO, SVM-RFE, Boruta, and DT) provided robust feature selection and model validation for DFU diagnosis. The superior performance of the K-nearest neighbors (KNN) model (AUC 0.957) suggests that the identified URG signature has strong diagnostic potential. SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) analysis further enhanced model interpretability. For readers without a machine learning background, SHAP can be simply understood as a method that breaks down a model’s prediction into additive contributions from each feature (gene). In this study, the SHAP summary plot visually quantifies how each hub gene “votes” for or against a DFU diagnosis in individual samples, with SKIL consistently showing the largest positive contribution. This approach transforms the “black‑box” nature of machine learning into a transparent, feature‑level decision framework, making the model’s reasoning accessible to a broad biomedical audience47. The validated gene signature could potentially be translated into a clinical diagnostic tool, enabling early identification of patients at risk for poor wound healing outcomes.

Immune infiltration analysis revealed significant alterations in the immune landscape of DFU tissues, with increased infiltration of inflammatory cells including monocytes, T cells, and macrophages. Importantly, two distinct molecular subtypes of DFU were identified based on URG expression patterns, each characterized by different immune profiles and BP. ClusterA showed enrichment in metabolic pathways such as adipogenesis and bile acid metabolism, while ClusterB was characterized by enhanced angiogenesis and cytokine signaling pathways. This molecular heterogeneity may explain the variable clinical outcomes observed in DFU patients and suggests that personalized treatment approaches based on molecular profiling could improve therapeutic efficacy. The correlation between hub URGs and immune cell infiltration patterns provides mechanistic insights into how ubiquitination processes regulate immune responses in DFU. This is particularly relevant given the emerging understanding that immune dysregulation is a key driver of chronic wound formation18,48. To directly link these immune findings with the identified hub genes, we systematically examined the correlation between the four URGs and the abundance of 22 immune cell subsets (Fig. 4D). SKIL expression was most strongly and positively correlated with M1 macrophages and activated dendritic cells, whereas it showed a negative correlation with resting CD4 + memory T cells. In contrast, MEF2A was positively associated with resting mast cells and negatively associated with M0 macrophages. These correlations provide mechanistic clues: SKIL‑mediated inhibition of TGF‑β signaling may promote a pro‑inflammatory, M1‑skewed microenvironment that hinders the transition to the pro‑reparative M2 phase, a shift known to be essential for normal wound closure49. The data suggest that targeting ubiquitination pathways could modulate immune responses and promote wound healing.

Molecular docking analysis identified lumicolchicine and ramipril as potential therapeutic compounds targeting SKIL, with favorable binding affinities (-6.2 and − 6.4 kcal/mol, respectively). Of clinical significance is the identification of ramipril, an ACE inhibitor that represents a compelling opportunity for investigating the therapeutic potential of this drug class in DFU treatment. Recent epidemiological studies demonstrate that 45.5% to 74.6% of patients with type 2 diabetes have concurrent hypertension, with some studies reporting rates as high as 70–75% in certain populations50,51. The landmark HOPE (Heart Outcomes Prevention Evaluation) study specifically investigated ramipril and demonstrated significant reduction in cardiovascular events in diabetic patients, though this establishes evidence for ramipril rather than indicating superiority over other ACE inhibitors52,53. Importantly, ramipril has a well-established safety profile in patients with diabetes and hypertension, with extensive clinical experience supporting its long-term tolerability and no evidence indicating adverse effects on wound healing. ACE inhibitors improve endothelial function, enhance nitric oxide availability, and reduce oxidative stress—all critical factors in wound healing54,55. In DFU, ACE inhibitors may exert beneficial effects through two key mechanisms: first, modulating SKIL expression to normalize the aberrant metabolic programming in DFU_Fibroblasts, and second, improving microvascular function to enhance angiogenesis and nutrient delivery to healing wounds. The potential benefit of ramipril in wound healing—while treating hypertension—represents a significant clinical advantage. Many DFU patients already require ACE inhibitor therapy for cardiovascular protection and blood pressure control, making this drug class an attractive option that could address multiple therapeutic targets simultaneously. This approach aligns with current trends toward precision medicine and could improve patient adherence by optimizing existing therapy rather than adding new medications. However, molecular docking provides theoretical predictions of ligand–protein interactions and does not account for pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic factors, tissue bioavailability, or off-target effects. Therefore, the proposed interaction between ramipril and SKIL should be considered preliminary. Further validation through biochemical binding assays, cell-based functional studies, and in vivo models will be required to determine whether ramipril directly modulates SKIL activity or influences DFU-relevant signaling pathways.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analysis is based primarily on publicly available datasets, which may introduce batch effects and limit the generalizability of results. Second, functional validation of identified genes and pathways was limited to computational approaches and requires extensive experimental validation in appropriate in vitro and in vivo models. Third, the focus on transcriptomic analysis did not examine protein-level changes or post-translational modifications, which are particularly relevant for ubiquitination research. Future studies should incorporate proteomics and ubiquitinomics approaches to provide a more complete picture of ubiquitination-mediated processes in DFU. Fourth, molecular docking analysis provided theoretical evidence for drug-target interactions but lacks experimental validation. Future research should include biochemical assays, cell-based studies, and animal models to confirm the therapeutic potential of identified compounds.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this comprehensive investigation provides the first characterization of ubiquitination-related molecular signatures in DFU using integrated multi-omics approaches. The identification of DFU_Fibroblasts as a pathogenic cell population, the discovery of SKIL as a key regulatory gene, and the development of a machine learning-based diagnostic signature represent significant advances in understanding DFU pathogenesis. The data lay the foundation for the development of precision medicine approaches for DFU treatment and highlight the potential of ubiquitination-targeted therapies for chronic wound healing. Future studies should validate these ubiquitination-related signatures in larger and independent cohorts and further explore the therapeutic efficacy of targeting SKIL in preclinical DFU models, thereby facilitating translational application and clinical implementation.
