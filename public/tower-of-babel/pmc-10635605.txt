Cabozantinib inhibits HBV-RNA transcription by decreasing STAT3 binding to the enhancer region of cccDNA
Authors: Kazuyoshi Funato, Nozomi Miyake, Kazuma Sekiba, Yu Miyakawa, Takahiro Seimiya, Chikako Shibata, Takahiro Kishikawa, Motoyuki Otsuka
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:Precision medicine and customized therapeutics based on the features of each patient are important for maximizing therapeutic effects. Because most cases of HCC occur in the damaged liver through various etiologies, such as hepatitis virus infection, steatohepatitis, and autoimmune hepatitis, there should be a rationale for the choice of therapeutic options based on these etiologies. Although cabozantinib, an oral multikinase inhibitor, has demonstrated clinical effectiveness in advanced HCC, subgroup analyses showed a lower HR for death in HBV-related HCC. This study aimed to determine the therapeutic effects of cabozantinib in HBV-related HCC.Methods:Using HBV infection models and gene knockout cells, we determined the crucial signaling axis responsible for the effects of cabozantinib on HBV. A chromatin immunoprecipitation assay was performed to determine the interaction between the signaling molecules and HBV DNA. Agonists and inhibitors were used for confirmation.Results:Cabozantinib inhibited HBV replication through the HGF-mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor–signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (MET-STAT3) signaling axis. The importance of STAT3 in viral replication has been confirmed using gene-edited STAT3 knockout cells. The chromatin immunoprecipitation assay revealed that the binding levels of phosphorylated STAT3 to enhancer region 1 of HBV covalently closed circular DNA were significantly increased by HGF stimulation.Conclusions:Cabozantinib has favorable therapeutic effects on HBV-related HCC because it inhibits HCC not only directly but also indirectly by means of inhibitory effects on HBV.

INTRODUCTION

HBV infection is a major global health problem. Despite the widespread use of prophylactic vaccination, it is estimated that 296 million people were infected with HBV in 2021, and ~820,000 people die annually from complications of chronic HBV infection, including cirrhosis and HCC.1

HBV infection, hepatitis C, alcohol use, NASH, and autoimmune diseases are known risk factors for cirrhosis and HCC.2,3 These factors are associated with various signaling pathways involved in carcinogenesis,4 suggesting that carcinogenesis and cancer progression may differ depending on the background of liver disease. However, treatment selection according to patient background (precision medicine for HCC) has not been discussed much so far. Recently, despite reports that immune checkpoint inhibitors may be less effective in NASH-related HCC,5 there have been no major reports on HBV-related HCC.

Cabozantinib, an oral multikinase inhibitor, demonstrated clinical effectiveness in advanced HCC in a randomized, double-blind, phase 3 trial conducted in 2018.6 Cabozantinib inhibits multiple tyrosine kinase–type receptors such as vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR)1, VEGFR2, VEGFR3, mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor (MET), AXL, and rearranged during transfection.7 Its antitumor effect is primarily mediated through the inhibition of VEGFR2 and MET in HCC.8 Remarkably, subgroup analysis in the trial showed a lower HR for death in HBV-related HCC; however, the reason for this remains unclear.6

In addition to chronic inflammation and similar to other background liver diseases,9 HBV-related HCC is considered to involve mechanisms such as carcinogenesis through the viral protein hepatitis B virus regulatory protein X (HBx)10–12 and carcinogenesis and cancer progression through HBV sequence integration into cancer-related genes, including TERT, CCNE1, and MLL4.13,14 Based on these previous reports, we hypothesized that cabozantinib may work not only through direct inhibition on HCC but also indirectly on HBV and subsequently HCC, which may explain the better prognosis of HBV-related HCC when treated using cabozantinib. In this paper, we report that cabozantinib inhibits the HGF-mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor–signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (MET-STAT3) signaling axis, which also targets HBV sequences. This may explain, at least in part, how cabozantinib has particularly favorable therapeutic effects on HBV-related HCC.

METHODS

Cells

HepG2, human HCC, and HEK293T human embryonic kidney cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. HepAD38 cells, which express HBV transcripts under the control of the inducible tetracycline promoter, were kindly provided by Prof. C. Seeger (Institute for Cancer Research, Fox Chase Cancer Center, Philadelphia, PA).15 The cells were maintained in Ham F-10/DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum without doxycycline to maintain HBV replication.

Primary human hepatocytes, freshly isolated using the collagenase perfusion method from chimeric urokinase-type plasminogen activator/severe combined immunodeficient mice with humanized livers,16 were acquired from PhoenixBio Co. (Hiroshima, Japan). The human hepatocytes had a purity of >95%. The cells were seeded onto type I collagen-coated plates and cultured in DMSO-supplemented hepatocyte clonal growth medium (DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum, 20 mM HEPES, 44 mM NaHCO3, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, 15 μg/mL L-proline, 0.25 μg/mL insulin, 5×10−8 M dexamethasone, 5 ng/mL EGF, 0.1 mM ascorbic acid-2-phosphate, and 2% DMSO). All the cells were incubated at 37°C in 20% oxygen and 5% carbon dioxide.

Plasmids

The expression vector encoding sodium taurocholate cotransporting polypeptide (NTCP) (#FXC25216) was purchased from Kazusa DNA Research Institute (Chiba, Japan). The untagged NTCP-expressing lentiviral plasmid was constructed by subcloning the NTCP sequences using the infusion method. After amplifying the NTCP cDNA using the #FXC25216 plasmid as a template and primers designed for the infusion method, the PCR products were cloned into the pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-Neo lentivirus vector. The following primers were used: 5′-ACCATGATGGAGGCCCACAACGCGTCT-3′ (sense) and 5′- GGCTGTGCAAGGGGAGCAGTC-3′ (antisense).

Minicircle HBV covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) genotype C with a Gaussia luciferase reporter–containing plasmid [pre–minicircle HBV-Gaussia luciferase (Gluc)] was kindly provided by Prof. Su (Lineberger Comprehensive Cancer Center, Department of Microbiology and Immunology, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, NC).17 Pre-HBV circle genotype D was kindly provided by Dr. Gao (Roche Innovation Center Shanghai, Shanghai, China).18

The pCMV-Cypridina luciferase 2 Control Plasmid, which constitutively expresses luciferase secreted by the ostracod Cypridina luciferase under the control of the CMV promoter, was purchased from New England Biolabs (Ipswich, MA). This plasmid was used as a transfection control for the measurement of Gluc in HBV minicircles, as reported.19

The CRISPR v2 plasmid (#52961), which was used to establish knockout cells, was purchased from Addgene (Watertown, MA). To generate a CRISPR construct targeting each gene, guide RNA sequences (trans-activating clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats-derived RNA + clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats-derived RNA) were inserted into the Esp3I site of the CRISPR v2 plasmid. The sequences of clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats-derived RNAs are provided in the Supplemental Information (Table S1), http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641.

Compounds

Recombinant human HGF (HEK293 derived) was purchased from PeproTech Inc. (Cranbury, NJ). The c-Met inhibitors cabozantinib and PHA-665752 were purchased from Selleck Chemicals LLC (Houston, TX). Recombinant human IL-6 was purchased from Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical (Osaka, Japan).

HBV minicircle extraction

HBV minicircle extraction was performed as reported.20 Details are described in Supplemental Methods, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641.

Transfection and lentiviral transduction

Details are described in Supplemental Methods, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641.

HBV preparation and infection in an in vitro model

HBV was prepared from the culture medium of HepAD38 cells using polyethylene glycol (PEG)-it reagent (System Biosciences). Briefly, HepAD cells seeded on a 10-cm dish were cultured for 3 days without tetracycline. Then, the culture medium was collected and mixed with a one-fifth volume of PEG-it reagent and incubated for at least 12 hours at 4°C to concentrate the virus. The centrifuged pellet was resuspended in 1×PBS.

HepG2NTCP cells were infected with HBV, as previously described. Briefly, cells were inoculated with 2.5% DMSO for 2 days prior to HBV infection. The cells were then inoculated with HBV at 3000 viral genome equivalents per cell in DMEM containing 4% PEG 8000 and 2.5% DMSO for 24 hours at 37°C. The cells were washed twice with PBS and cultured in DMEM containing 2.5% DMSO. The culture medium was renewed on days 4 and 7 after infection.

Primary human hepatocytes were infected with HBV as reported.16 Briefly, cells were inoculated with serum obtained from HBV genotype C–infected humanized liver chimeric mice (PhoenixBio Co.) at 5 viral genome equivalents per cell in DMSO-supplemented hepatocyte clonal growth medium containing 4% PEG 8000 for 24 hours at 37°C. The cells were then washed with PBS and cultured in DMSO-supplemented hepatocyte clonal growth medium.

Western blot analysis, immunofluorometric assay, and Southern blotting

Details are described in Supplemental Methods, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641.

Flow cytometric analysis of cell surface NTCP expression

Details are described in Supplemental Methods, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641.

Quantification of RNA levels

Total RNA was extracted from the cells using an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). The extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed using ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix with gRNA Remover (Toyobo Co., Ltd.). Quantitative PCR was carried out using Thunderbird SYBR qPCR Mix (Toyobo Co., Ltd.) and the StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA). The concentrations of 3.5 kb and total HBV mRNA and NTCP mRNA relative to that of GAPDH mRNA were determined. The sequences of primers are provided in the Supplemental Information (Table S2), http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641. The primers for total HBV mRNA amplify the region from nucleotides 1803–1867 in HBV sequences, covering all HBV mRNA transcripts (3.5, 2.4, 2.1, and 0.7 kb).

Isolation of HBV cccDNA

For Southern blotting, HBV DNA, including cccDNA, was extracted from HepG2NTCP cells with HBV infection using the Hirt method.21,22 Details are described in Supplemental Methods, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641.

Quantification of HBV cccDNA levels

Droplet digital PCR was performed using the QX200 Droplet Digital PCR System (Bio-Rad), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 20 µL of droplet digital PCRmixture (0.05 µg template DNA, 10 µL 2×droplet digital PCR SuperMix for Probe (no 2′-Deoxyuridine-5′-triphosphate), 900 nM forward and reverse primers (and 250 nM corresponding probe), and 70 µL droplet generation oil were added to the DG8 cartridge to produce droplets. The droplets were carefully transferred to a PCR plate for amplification in a T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad) with a cycle starting at 95°C, followed by 35 cycles at 94°C for 30 seconds and 57°C for 60 seconds, and a final cycle at 98°C for 10 minutes. The droplets were analyzed on a QX200 droplet reader using the QuantaSoft software (Bio-Rad) to determine the absolute count of template DNA. As described,23 primers specific for cccDNA were designed against nucleotides 1561–1579 and nucleotides 1865–1883. To detect PCR products, an HBV cccDNA-specific probe that covers the HBV DNA minus-strand gap was used. The sequences of primers and probes are provided in Supplemental Information (Table S2), http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641.

HBV attachment assay

The HBV attachment assay was performed as reported with slight modification.24 HepG2NTCP, HepG2NTCP-KONTCP, and HepG2NTCP-KOMET cells were exposed to 3000 viral genome equivalents of HBV per cell at 4°C for 3 hours to allow HBV to attach to the cell surface by means of NTCP. Cell surface HBV DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit, followed by quantification through real-time PCR. HBV attachment was evaluated by subtracting HBV DNA levels in HepG2 (as the baseline) cells from those in HepG2NTCP-KOGFP, HepG2NTCP-KONTCP, and HepG2NTCP-KOMET. The primers used are provided in the Supplemental Information (Table S2), http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641, which amplified the region from nucleotides 2268 to 2390 of genotype D (HE815465.1).

HBV internalization assay

The HBV internalization assay was performed as reported with slight modification.24 HepG2NTCP, HepG2NTCP-KOEGFR, and HepG2NTCP-KOMET cells were exposed to 3000 viral genome equivalents of HBV per cell at 4°C for 3 hours to attach HBV to the cell surface through NTCP, followed by washing of free HBV with PBS. The cells were cultured at 37°C for an additional 24 hours to internalize HBV. After digestion of cell surface viruses with trypsin, internalized HBV DNA was extracted using a QIAamp DNA Mini Kit. HBV internalization was evaluated by subtracting the HBV DNA levels in HepG2 cells (baseline) from those in HepG2NTCP-KOGFP, HepG2NTCP-KOEGFR, and HepG2NTCP-KOMET. The primers used are provided in the Supplemental Information (Table S2), http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641, which amplified the region from nucleotides 2268–2390 of genotype D (HE815465.1).

Quantification of HBeAg levels

HBeAg levels in the culture medium of HepG2, HepG2NTCP, and knockout HepG2NTCP cells were measured using an ELISA at the SRL Clinical Laboratory (Tokyo, Japan).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay

A chromatin immunoprecipitation assay was performed using the SimpleChIP Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (#9003; Cell Signaling Technology) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, chromatin was enzymatically fragmented using micrococcal nuclease, followed by the immunoprecipitation of the fragmented chromatin using magnetic beads with antibodies of interest. To confirm the binding of Phospho-STAT3 to enhancer 1 on cccDNA, HBV-infected HepG2NTCP cells were treated with or without 50 ng/mL HGF for 48 hours. The sequences of primers that amplify the HBV enhancer region and HBV control region are provided in Supplemental Information (Table S2), http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641. The following antibodies were used: Phospho-STAT3 (#9145, 1:50; Cell Signaling Technology), histone H3 (#4620, 1:50; Cell Signaling Technology), acetyl-histone H3 (Lys27) (#8173, 1:100; Cell Signaling Technology), and rabbit IgG (#2729, 1:500; Cell Signaling Technology).

Cytotoxicity and cell proliferation assay

The Cell Counting Kit-8 (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Kumamoto, Japan) was used to test cytotoxicity and cell proliferation according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Details are described in Supplemental Methods, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are reported as the mean±SD unless indicated otherwise. Two-sample comparisons were performed using two-sided Welch t tests, and multiple comparisons were performed by means of one-way ANOVA. The statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.1.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). In all analyses, p<0.05 was taken to indicate statistical significance.

RESULTS

Cabozantinib inhibits HBV production in a HepG2NTCP infection model

To determine the reason for the favorable prognosis of HBV-related HCC in the subgroup analyses of cabozantinib against HCC, we hypothesized that cabozantinib affects HBV. To assess the effects of cabozantinib on HBV infection in an HBV persistence model, we independently established HepG2 cells stably overexpressing NTCP (HepG2NTCP) (Supplemental Figure S1A–C, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641). In these cells, HBV was persistently replicated, and viral products were monitored, as reported25 (Supplemental Figure S1D–G, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641). The infection efficiency of HBV-infected HepG2NTCP was determined using immunofluorescence staining with anti-HBc antibody (Supplemental Figure S1H, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641), consistent as described.12 The copy numbers of HBV-infected HepG2NTCP cells were ~1000–3000 copies/μg total DNA, which was presumed to be equivalent to 0.01–0.03 copies/HBV-infected cells, based on a report that calculated the copy numbers of cccDNA per cell, assuming 7 pg of total DNA per cell.26 Although the copy numbers per HBV-infected cell seem to be less than those reported,26 the absolute value of HBeAg levels in the supernatant was comparable to another result12 (Supplemental Figure S1I, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641). These results suggested that the infection had been successfully established.

Using this infection model, we assessed the effects of cabozantinib on HBV infection. First, we assessed the effect of cabozantinib on the viability of HepG2NTCP cells at the indicated doses (Figure 1A). The phosphorylation of MET and its downstream effectors extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2, AKT, and STAT3 was reduced by 100 nM cabozantinib, a concentration at which cell viability was unaffected (Supplemental Figure S2A, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641). Next, we evaluated the effects of cabozantinib on HBV infection in HepG2NTCP cells. HBV-infected cells were treated with 100 nM cabozantinib for 7 days, and viral product levels were quantified (Figure 1B). Viral RNA (total HBV mRNA and 3.5-kb mRNA) and protein levels in the treated cells were suppressed (Figure 1C, D). The ratios of viral RNA to GAPDH mRNA levels in HBV-infected cells treated with cabozantinib were comparable to those previously reported (Supplemental Figure S2B, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641). cccDNA levels were also moderately but significantly reduced by cabozantinib treatment (Figure 1E). The copy number of HBV-infected HepG2NTCP cells was about 103 copies/μg DNA.

Reduction of viral transcription leads to a reduction in new cccDNA formation and destabilization of cccDNA;27,28 thus, these results were expected. Moreover, the levels of viral protein and phosphorylated STAT3 were reduced by cabozantinib even in the IL6-rich environment (Supplemental Figure S2C, D, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641). These results suggested that cabozantinib inhibited HBV production in the HepG2NTCP infection model.

HGF/MET signaling axis is responsible for the antiviral effect of cabozantinib

Cabozantinib, a multikinase inhibitor, has several targets, including MET, VEGFR2, and AXL.7 To determine the mechanism underlying the antiviral effect of cabozantinib, we established HepG2NTCP cells stably knocked out for each target (Supplemental Figure S2E, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641). An HBV infection experiment was performed using these cells. Among these HBV-infected cells, viral RNA (total HBV mRNA and 3.5-kb mRNA) levels were significantly lower in HepG2NTCP-METKO cells (Figure 2A), indicating that MET is the most important factor for HBV treatment. Consistent with the results in knockout cells, MET inhibitor significantly reduced HBx mRNA levels (Supplemental Figure S3, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641).

Next, we evaluated the effect of HGF, an MET ligand, on HBV infection. The indicated doses of HGF did not affect the proliferation of HepG2NTCP cells (Figure 2B), whereas HGF increased viral mRNA (total HBV mRNA and 3.5-kb mRNA) levels in a dose-dependent (Figure 2C) and time-dependent manner (Figure 2D). HGF treatment also increased viral protein levels (Figure 2E). The copy number of cccDNA increased significantly with HGF treatment, which was visually confirmed by means of Southern blotting (Figure 2F). Moreover, these viral-promoting effects of HGF were abolished through MET knockout (Supplemental Figure S4A–D, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641).

Next, we investigated which step in the HBV lifecycle plays a role in the viral-promoting effect of the HGF/MET signaling axis. First, we confirmed that MET knockout did not affect HBV attachment or internalization. HepG2NTCP-NTCPKO and HepG2NTCP-EGFRKO cells were established as positive controls in the HBV attachment assay and internalization assay (Supplemental Figure S5A, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641). As expected, the levels of attached HBV on the cell surface were significantly reduced in HepG2NTCP-NTCPKO cells, while no significant changes were observed in HepG2NTCP-METKO cells (Supplemental Figure S5B, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641). Consistent with the report that EGFR is a host-entry cofactor triggering HBV internalization,24 the levels of internalized HBV were significantly reduced in HepG2NTCP-EGFRKO cells. At the same time, no significant changes were observed in HepG2NTCP-METKO cells (Supplemental Figure S5C, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641).

Subsequently, we assessed the effect of HGF on HBV replication using HBV minicircle DNA, which mimics HBV cccDNA.18 As minicircle HBV acts as a template for viral RNA transcription after transfection into hepatocytes, the amount of RNA transcribed from minicircle HBV can be evaluated independent of viral attachment or internalization. HGF treatment increased the levels of Gluc activity in the supernatant and 3.5-kb mRNA HBV in a dose-dependent manner using a minicircle-Gluc system, in which the amount of Gluc in the supernatant correlates with the amount of HBV 3.5-kb mRNA17 (Supplemental Figure S5D, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641). Similarly, HGF increased viral mRNA (total HBV mRNA and 3.5-kb mRNA) levels also in minicircle HBV without Gluc system18 (Supplemental Figure S5E, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641). These results suggest that the HGF/MET signaling axis promotes HBV replication, not attachment or internalization; thus, the antiviral effect of cabozantinib is mainly due to the inhibition of this axis. Moreover, PHA-665752, a selective MET inhibitor, exerted an antiviral effect in HepG2NTCP cells (Supplemental Figure S6A–F, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641), consistent with the above results.

STAT3 is the main factor in the HGF/MET signaling axis responsible for HBV replication

The HGF/MET signaling axis contains several downstream factors, including extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2, AKT, and STAT3. Phosphorylation of these proteins on HGF stimulation was confirmed through western blot analysis and found to be reduced in HepG2NTCP-METKO cells (Figure 3A). Next, we established that HepG2NTCP cells were stably knocked out for each factor (Figure 3B). Using these cells, viral mRNA levels were quantified in the HBV infection experiments. HBV total mRNA and 3.5-kb mRNA levels significantly decreased only in HepG2NTCP-STAT3KO cells, and the viral-promoting effect of HGF was reduced only in HepG2NTCP-STAT3KO cells (Figure 3C). Moreover, viral protein levels were significantly reduced in HepG2NTCP-STAT3KO cells, which were not restored by HGF treatment (Figure 3D). Similarly, STAT3 inhibitor (SI3-201) significantly reduced total viral RNA levels (Supplemental Figure S7A, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641). In addition, none of the inhibitors including SI3-201 significantly affected cell number at the doses used in this study (Supplemental Figure S7B, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641). These results suggest that STAT3 is the most important factor in the HGF/MET signaling axis for HBV production without affecting the cell viability.

HGF-phosphorylated STAT3 binds to cccDNA

The transcription factor STAT3 is phosphorylated by stimulation with cytokines and growth factors, including HGF, and phosphorylated STAT3 translocates to the cell nucleus to function as a transcription factor.29 STAT3 is also known to bind to the enhancer 1 region of cccDNA, resulting in the activation of viral RNA transcription.30 Based on these reports, we assessed the interaction between phosphorylated STAT3 and cccDNA. First, STAT3 phosphorylation and translocation to the cell nucleus following HGF stimulation in HepG2NTCP cells were confirmed through immunofluorescence staining (Figure 4A). Next, HBV-infected HepG2NTCP cells treated with HGF for 48 hours were harvested for chromatin immunoprecipitation assay (Figure 4B). HBV DNA immunoprecipitated by the anti-phospho-STAT3 antibody was amplified by primers targeting the enhancer 1 region, which was significantly strengthened by HGF treatment. In contrast, such amplification was not observed with primers in the control region (Figure 4C). On the other hand, HGF treatment did not alter the levels of cccDNA binding to acetyl-histone H3K27 (AcH3K27), a well-known representative cccDNA-binding histone modification involved in viral replication promotion (Figure 4D), which implies that the effect of HGF on viral transcription was not mediated by histone modification. These results suggest that phosphorylated STAT3 and its translocation to the cell nucleus through HGF stimulation results in it binding to the enhancer 1 region on cccDNA, resulting in an increase in viral RNA transcription.

Viral-promoting effect of HGF and antiviral effect of PHA-665752 in a natural HBV infection model

Next, we assessed the effects of HGF and PHA-665752 in primary human hepatocytes, which provides an even more natural model of HBV infection. The infection efficiency of HBV-infected primary human hepatocytes was determined using immunofluorescence staining with anti-HBc antibody, which was similar to that of HBV-infected HepG2NTCP (74.3%±3.9% for HepG2NTCP cells and 69.7±5.8% for primary human hepatocytes) (Supplemental Figure S6G, H, http://links.lww.com/HC9/A641). After HBV infection (genotype C), the cells were treated with HGF, PHA-665752, or control (0.1% bovine serum albumin or DMSO) for 5 days, and viral product levels were quantified (Figure 5A). At the beginning of treatment, baseline HBeAg levels in the culture medium were comparable (Figure 5H), indicating that the cells were successfully infected with HBV to a similar extent. HGF treatment significantly increased viral RNA (HBV total mRNA and 3.5-kb mRNA), viral protein, and cccDNA levels (Figure 5B–D), as well as HBeAg levels in the culture medium (Figure 5H). In contrast, PHA-665752 treatment significantly decreased viral RNA (HBV total mRNA and 3.5-kb mRNA), viral protein, and cccDNA levels (Figure 5E–G), similar to HBeAg levels in the culture medium (Figure 5H). These results suggest that the viral-promoting effect of HGF and the antiviral effect of PHA-665752 also occur in a natural HBV infection model.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we demonstrated the anti-HBV effects of cabozantinib. We showed that cabozantinib inhibits HBV production by decreasing the levels of phosphorylated STAT3, which binds to cccDNA by means of the enhancer 1 region, thereby blocking the HGF-MET signaling axis. These effects of cabozantinib on HBV may contribute to a better prognosis for HBV-related HCC when treated with cabozantinib in the CELESTIAL trial.6

In the CELESTIAL trial, cabozantinib reduced the risk of death by 31% (HR, 0.69) compared with placebo. In contrast, in the overall population, cabozantinib only reduced the risk of death by 24% (HR, 0.76).6 Moreover, in patients with serologically confirmed HBV infection, cabozantinib reduced the risk of death by as high as 42% compared with placebo (HR, 0.58).31 In The Sorafenib Hepatocellular Carcinoma Assessment Randomized Protocol and Study of Regorafenib After Sorafenib in Patients With Hepatocellular Carcinoma trials, sorafenib and regorafenib had no specific prognostic benefit in HBV-related HCC; if they do, it is smaller than that of cabozantinib.32,33 Overall, cabozantinib may have advantages over other multikinase inhibitors in treating HBV-related HCC.

It has long been known that the amounts of HBsAg and HBV DNA correlate with cumulative liver carcinogenicity.34,35 HBV infection has been reported to increase the risk of hepatocarcinogenesis not only by causing chronic inflammation resulting in cirrhosis36 but also through the presence of HBx and mutant pre-S2/S proteins,37,38 the viral product itself. HBx has been reported to induce hepatocarcinogenesis in HBx-transgenic mice.10 Moreover, we recently reported that the structural maintenance of chromosome 5/6 (Smc5/6) degradation by HBx leads to the accumulation of DNA damage and hepatocarcinogenesis.12 HBx proteins have also been shown to play a role in cell proliferation in in vitro studies.11 Considering these previous reports, it is possible that reducing viral products through cabozantinib, as shown in this study, may contribute to reductions in carcinogenesis risk and cancer progression.

Cabozantinib is a multikinase inhibitor that blocks multiple tyrosine kinase-type receptors and is thought to inhibit cell proliferation, motility, and survival in HCC, primarily by blocking MET and VEGFR2.7 STAT3 is a downstream factor in the HGF-MET signaling axis and acts as a transcription factor by undergoing phosphorylation and nuclear translocation.29 As shown in this study, STAT3 plays a role in viral RNA transcription by binding to the enhancer 1 region on cccDNA.30 Moreover, it has been reported that HBV infection activates STAT3, and activated STAT3 promotes the malignant transformation of hepatocytes by maintaining efficient HBV replication and hepatocyte survival.39 Although STAT3 has been experimentally demonstrated to have viral-promoting effects on HBV in several reports,30,39,40 there have been no major reports on the clinical application of STAT3 inhibition in HBV infection. Because cabozantinib is already administered for HCC, real-world data need to be accumulated, and its effects on HBV need to be confirmed clinically.41 Because activated STAT3 upregulates IL-17, which in turn activates STAT3 pathway further as positive feedback and enhances HCC proliferation,42,43 cabozantinib may suppress HCC proliferation through inhibition of this positive feedback. Further, because IL-17 promotes liver fibrosis,44,45 inhibition of STAT3 pathway by cabozantinib may be beneficial for HBV-infected liver from points of views of the inhibition of HCC proliferation and of liver fibrosis progression.

However, antiviral activity alone is unlikely to contribute to the improved prognosis of HBV-related HCC. HBV integration is known to underlie the mechanism of carcinogenesis characteristic of HBV-related HCC, with some reports suggesting that the frequency of HBV integration is as high as 88%.14,46 It has been reported that the integration of HBV sequences upstream of cancer-related genes such as TERT, CCNE1, KMT2B, and MLL4 may alter the expression levels of downstream genes.47–50 Moreover, the HBV enhancer 1 region focused on in this study is reported to be part of an HBV sequence that is particularly susceptible to integration. The host gene that has HBV integration is particularly often TERT.14,51. Cabozantinib may be particularly effective in HBV-related HCC by reducing the expression levels of cancer-related genes downstream of the integrated HBV enhancer 1 region, particularly TERT, although this remains to be investigated further.

Precision medicine refers to the customization of diagnostic and therapeutic processes based on the unique features of an individual patient52 and has become increasingly important in recent years. HCC is a cancer whose etiology is relatively easy to distinguish and should be compatible with precision medicine. Although precision medicine for HCC is gradually advancing, recent reports have shown that immune checkpoint inhibitors are less effective in NASH-HCC,5 and there have been no similar reports to date regarding HBV-related HCC. Although the results of this study cannot explain all the mechanisms by which cabozantinib is advantageous in the treatment of HBV-related HCC, they may provide a basis on which cabozantinib would be the preferred choice for HBV-related HCC. Given the large global prevalence of HBV,1 we believe that the results of this study could have a significant impact on precision medicine for HBV-related HCC.

In conclusion, this study found that cabozantinib inhibits the HGF-MET-STAT3 signaling axis, which results in the inhibition of HBV-RNA transcription by decreasing the binding of phosphorylated STAT3 to the enhancer region 1 of cccDNA. The results of this study may provide a rationale for selecting cabozantinib as the preferred treatment for HBV-related HCC.
