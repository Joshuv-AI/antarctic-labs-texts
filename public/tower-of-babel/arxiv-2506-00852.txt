Estimating a regression function under possible heteroscedastic and heavy-tailed errors. Application to shape-restricted regression.
Yannick BARAUD, Guillaume MAILLARD

Abstract
. We consider a regression framework where the design points are deterministic and the errors possibly non-i.i.d. and heavy-tailed (with a moment of order p p in [ 1 , 2 ] [1,2] ). Given a class of candidate regression functions, we propose a surrogate for the classical least squares estimator (LSE). For this new estimator, we establish a nonasymptotic risk bound with respect to the absolute loss which takes the form of an oracle type inequality. This inequality shows that our estimator possesses natural adaptation properties with respect to some elements of the class. When this class consists of monotone functions or convex functions on an interval, these adaptation properties are similar to those established in the literature for the LSE. However, unlike the LSE, we prove that our estimator remains stable with respect to a possible heteroscedasticity of the errors and may even converge at a parametric rate (up to a logarithmic factor) when the LSE is not even consistent. We illustrate the performance of this new estimator over classes of regression functions that satisfy a shape constraint: piecewise monotone, piecewise convex/concave, among other examples. The paper also contains some approximation results by splines with degrees in { 0 , 1 } \{0,1\} and VC bounds for the dimensions of classes of level sets. These results may be of independent interest.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we consider the problem of estimating a regression function
f^{\star}
on some set
{\mathcal{X}}
under a fixed design setting with independent and centred real-valued errors
\xi_{1,n},\ldots,\xi_{n,n}
. More precisely, we choose
n
deterministic points
x_{1,n},\ldots,x_{n,n}
in
{\mathcal{X}}
(possibly not all different) and associate each of them with a response
Y_{i,n}
with values in
{\mathbb{R}}
of the following form:

(1)

Y_{i,n}=f^{\star}(x_{i,n})+\xi_{i,n}\quad\text{so that}\quad f^{\star}(x_{i,n})={\mathbb{E}}\left[{Y_{i,n}}\right]\quad\text{for all $i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$.}

We only assume that the
\xi_{i,n}
are independent (not necessarily i.i.d.) and that all of them have
a finite moment of order
p\in[1,2]
with
{\mathbb{E}}[\xi_{i,n}]=0
. On the basis of these observations, our aim is to estimate the so-called regression function
f^{\star}
from
{\mathcal{X}}
to
{\mathbb{R}}
. Our approach is based on
a model
{\mathcal{F}}
for
f^{\star}
, which means that we choose some family
{\mathcal{F}}
of candidate regression functions and pretend that
f^{\star}
belongs to
{\mathcal{F}}
, although this may not be true. To get a good estimator of
f^{\star}
, it actually suffices that
{\mathcal{F}}
provides a good enough approximation of it. For instance,
{\mathcal{F}}
may be a finite dimensional linear space or the set of all nonincreasing functions on
[0,1]
.

Under the fixed design framework, a classical loss
\ell_{s}
(with
s\geqslant 1
) between two functions

f,g
on
{\mathcal{X}}
is given by

(2)

\ell_{s}(f,g)=\left[{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|{f(x_{i,n})-g(x_{i,n)}}\right|^{s}}\right]^{1/s},

the case of
s=2
being the most common. The corresponding risk of an estimator

\widehat{f}
of the true regression function
f^{\star}
is then

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\ell_{s}\left({f^{\star},\widehat{f}}\right)}\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left({\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|{f^{\star}(x_{i,n)}-\widehat{f}(x_{i,n})}\right|^{s}}\right)^{1/s}\right].

As this quantity may involve
{\mathbb{E}}\left[{|\xi_{i,n}|^{s}}\right]
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, it is commonly assumed in the literature that these moments are finite when one uses the
\ell_{s}
-loss. The value of
p
being unknown, but not smaller than one, the moment condition is always satisfied with
s=1
and we shall therefore measure the loss by

\ell_{1}
. Throughout the paper, this loss is simply denoted by
\ell
so that for two functions

f,g
on
{\mathcal{X}}
,

\ell(f,g)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|{f(x_{i,n})-g(x_{i,n})}\right|.

Our purpose is to bound
{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\ell\left({f^{\star},\widehat{f}}\right)}\right]
for a suitable estimator
\widehat{f}

whose construction will be described in Section
2.2
. As in the papers Baraud and Birgé
(
Baraud and Birgé, 2016
)
and Baraud, Halconruy and Maillard
(
Baraud et al., 2022
)
, both in density estimation, this construction is based on a model, namely
{\mathcal{F}}
,
and a suitable family of tests. Given
{\mathcal{F}}
, one can actually define a nondecreasing sequence
\{{\mathcal{O}}(D),1\leqslant D\leqslant n\}
of subsets of
{\mathcal{F}}
with respect to set inclusion,
for which the risk of our estimator is uniformly bounded by some quantity
R_{D}
on each subset
{\mathcal{O}}(D)
with
D\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. This bound is increasing
with
D
so that the smaller
D
the better the estimation for
f^{\star}\in{\mathcal{O}}(D)
. For a general
f^{\star}

and a given value of
D
, the risk of our estimator is the sum of
R_{D}
and an approximation
term
3\ell(f^{\star},{\mathcal{O}}(D))
, where, for
{\mathcal{G}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
,
\ell(f,{\mathcal{G}})
denotes the quantity

\inf_{g\in{\mathcal{G}}}\ell(f,g)
, with the convention that it takes the value
+\infty
when
{\mathcal{G}}
is empty.
As to
R_{D}
, it is proportional to
\sigma_{p}({\mathbf{x}})
with
{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1,n},\ldots,x_{n,n})
provided
that

(3)

\sigma_{p}^{p}({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\left|{\xi_{i,n}}\right|^{p}}\right]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\left|{Y_{i,n}-{\mathbb{E}}\left[{Y_{i,n}}\right]}\right|^{p}}\right]<+\infty,

while
D
plays a role which is similar to that of the dimension of the parameter space in Gaussian linear regression. Indeed, for
p=2
we obtain that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\ell\left({f^{\star},\widehat{f}}\right)}\right]\leqslant 3\ell(f^{\star},{\mathcal{O}}(D))+\kappa\sigma_{2}({\mathbf{x}})\sqrt{\frac{D}{n}},

for some universal positive constant
\kappa
. More generally, for every
D\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
p\in[1,2]

and every regression function
f^{\star}
on
{\mathcal{X}}
,

(4)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\ell\left({f^{\star},\widehat{f}}\right)}\right]\leqslant 3\ell\left({f^{\star},{\mathcal{O}}(D)}\right)+\kappa\left[{\sigma_{p}({\mathbf{x}})\left({\frac{D}{n}}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{p}}}\right],

this bound being also true if
\sigma_{p}({\mathbf{x}})=+\infty
.
Since it is valid for every
D
and
p
, the estimator automatically adapts to the optimal values of
D
and
p
, although neither the values
\sigma_{p}({\mathbf{x}})
nor those of
p
for which these quantities are finite are assumed to be known. The case
p=1
is the minimal requirement that ensures the existence of the regression function in (
1
). The bound also implies that the estimator is robust in the sense that if
f^{\star}
does not belong to
{\mathcal{O}}(D)
for a given value of
D
, the extra incurred loss is of the form
3\ell(f^{\star},{\mathcal{O}}(D))
. Moreover
\sigma_{p}({\mathbf{x}})

only depends on the mean of the
p
-th absolute moments of the
\xi_{i,n}
, not their maximum. It may therefore be small even if one of these moments is quite large. For instance, an outlier with a large value of
{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\left|{\xi_{i,n}}\right|^{p}}\right]
while all the others are small will not perturbate the result too much, which can be viewed as another robustness property of the method.

The problem of estimating a regression function under a fixed design has received a great deal of attention in statistics with a particular emphasis on the least squares method. Actually, we are not aware of many alternatives to the Least Squares Estimator (LSE for short), particularly in a heteroscedastic setting. A notable exception is Efromovich and Pinsker
(
Efromovich and Pinsker, 1996
)
. Under the assumption that the errors are Gaussian, the authors designed an estimator which is both adaptive and sharp minimax over classes of Sobolev spaces.

It is beyond the scope of this paper to provide a complete review of the use of least squares in statistics. As we shall mainly apply our procedure for the purpose of estimating a regression function with a given shape, we restrict our references to papers dealing with applications to such models.

The case of the class
{\mathcal{F}}
of monotone functions on an interval
{\mathcal{X}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
has been the most studied in the literature. Zhang
(
Zhang, 2002
)

studied the performance of the LSE on
{\mathcal{F}}
when the errors
\xi_{i,n}
are independent (but not necessarily i.i.d.) and their variances are not larger than
\sigma^{2}
. He proved that the
\ell_{s}
-risk of the LSE,
s\geqslant 1
, for estimating a nondecreasing regression function with variation
V
on
{\mathcal{X}}
is not larger than

C(s)\left[{\left({\frac{V\sigma^{2}}{n}}\right)^{1/3}+\sigma r_{n}}\right]\quad\text{where}\quad r_{n}=\begin{cases}1/\sqrt{n}&\text{for $s\in[1,2)$}\\
\sqrt{(\log n)/n}&\text{for $s=2$}\end{cases}

and
C(s)
is a positive constant depending on
s
only.
When
s=2
, Chatterjee
et al

(
Chatterjee et al., 2015
)
proved that the term
(V\sigma^{2}/n)^{1/3}
cannot be improved in general, at least when the errors are Gaussian and
n
grows to infinity while
V
remains fixed. From a nonasymptotic point of view, the second term
\sigma r_{n}
becomes dominant when
V
is sufficiently small, that is, when
f^{\star}
is almost constant. The fact that the
\ell_{2}
-risk of the LSE is not uniform over the class and may depend on the structure of the target regression function
f^{\star}
is often called
adaptation
. This terminology refers here to an adaptation with respect to
a shape
rather than
a smoothness
as classically studied in the literature. This property was investigated further in Chatterjee
et al

(
Chatterjee et al., 2015
)
. For i.i.d. Gaussian errors, they proved that the
\ell_{2}
-risk of the LSE is not larger than
\sigma\sqrt{6(k\log n)/n}
when the regression function is nondecreasing and piecewise constant on a partition of
{\mathcal{X}}
into
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
intervals.
Besides, they showed that this risk bound remains of the same order for any regression function
f^{\star}
that lies close enough to an element of this form. This can be viewed as a
robustness
property. It is characterised by an oracle-type inequality which is of the same flavour as those established for penalised least squares estimators in the literature (see Birgé and Massart
(
Birgé and Massart, 2001
)
and the references therein). Bellec
(
Bellec, 2018
)
obtained a sharp oracle-type inequality for the
\ell_{2}
-risk of the isotonic LSE when the errors are i.i.d. Gaussian. When the errors are only assumed to be independent with finite variances, an inequality of the same type was established by Guntuboyina and Sen
(
Guntuboyina and Sen, 2018
)
. They also extended it to
\ell_{s}
-risks with
s\in[1,2)
under the assumption of normality of the errors.

The
\ell_{2}
-risk of the LSE has also been studied for the problem of estimating regression functions satisfying other shape constraints than monotonicity. Chatterjee
(
Chatterjee, 2016
)
considered the class
{\mathcal{F}}
of convex functions on an interval. When the design points are equispaced and the regression function is affine, the author proves that the
\ell_{2}
-risk of the LSE becomes of order
\sqrt{\log n/n}
. The LSE’s adaptation properties with respect to convex piecewise linear regression functions are established in Bellec
(
Bellec, 2018
)
where the author obtained again sharp oracle inequalities.

A common feature of these classes of functions (nondecreasing or convex) lies in their convexity. A more general study of the properties of the LSE on convex sets of functions can be found in Chatterjee
(
Chatterjee, 2014
)
. Less is known about the performance of the LSE on non-convex sets. A notable exception is the class of unimodal functions for which Bellec
(
Bellec, 2018
)
(see also Chatterjee and Lafferty
(
Chatterjee and Lafferty, 2019
)
) established similar oracle-type inequalities with respect to piecewise constant functions as in the isotonic case. Minami
(
Minami, 2020
)
considered the larger class of regression functions which are piecewise monotone but the oracle-type inequality established there requires some constraints on the minimal length of the intervals of the partition. Finally, Feng
et al

(
Feng et al., 2022
)
studied the global rates and adaptation properties of the LSE for estimating a regression function with an
S
-shape on
[0,1]
, that is, which are first convex and then concave, when the location of the inflexion point is unknown.

The large amount of literature on the LSE seems to indicate the difficulty of establishing a fairly general result on its performance and the previous review also sketches out some of its limitations. All the results we have mentioned rely on the assumptions that the errors are, at least, square integrable, although the very definition of the regression function only requires their integrability. They are furthermore mostly restricted to the homoscedastic framework or provide risk bounds depending on the maximum of these variances.

The aim of this paper is to propose a surrogate for the celebrated least squares estimator. As we shall see, we establish its performance in a fairly general framework. Our construction relies on robust tests between two candidate regression functions which are, to our knowledge, new. Nevertheless, this approach enjoys some similarities with that previously adopted in Baraud, Halconruy and Maillard
(
Baraud et al., 2022
)
for estimating a density with respect to the total variation distance.

Our main result, namely the risk bound (
4
), holds under quite weak assumptions which differ substantially from the usual ones in the classical regression framework:

(i)

The random variables
\xi_{i,n}
have a moment of unknown order
p\in[1,2]
, therefore possibly
no finite variance.

(ii)

The independent variables
\xi_{i,n}
are not necessarily i.i.d. so that the values of the moments

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{|\xi|^{p}_{i,n}}\right]
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
may be quite different.

(iii)

We do not assume that
f^{\star}\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

This versatility allows us to apply our approach to various regression models (including those based on a shape constraint) when the errors are possibly non-i.i.d. and strongly heavy tailed (with a moment of order
p\in[1,2)
only). In particular, our estimator can still be consistent when the errors are heteroscedastic and the variances of a few of them larger than the sample size. Our examples include classes of regression functions which are piecewise monotone, for example unimodal, or piecewise convex/concave, for example with an
S
-shape. In this latter case, we also establish risk bounds for possibly nonequispaced design points, a problem which, to our knowledge, has not been tackled in the literature. Shape-restricted single index models are also considered. We are not aware of another approach that allows the statistician to obtain an estimator which remains stable in a possibly heteroscedastic context. This phenomenon is due to the fact that its risk bound depends on the average of the variances and not the maximum of these. For illustration, we shall see that our estimator may take advantage of this property to converge at rate
(\log n)/\sqrt{n}
in a situation where the LSE is not even consistent. This property also makes it useful for estimation in the Logistic and Poisson regression frameworks which are heteroscedastic by nature. It offers thus an alternative to the
\rho
-estimators that were studied in Baraud and Chen
(
Baraud and Chen, 2024
)
(see also the references therein) in these settings.

The paper is organised as follows. The statistical framework and the construction of the estimator are described in Section
2
which also contains a comparison between our estimator and the LSE on some simple examples. Section
3
is devoted to the main result while Section
4
deals with our applications to the models of regression functions we have previously described. The reader can also find in Section
4
some
\ell_{1}
-approximation bounds that can be achieved by using splines. These results can be of independent interest. The technical calculation of some VC-bounds that we use throughout the paper are postponed to Section
5
. The proof of our main result can be found in Section
6
and the other proofs in Section
7
.

,

2.
The statistical framework and the construction of the estimator

2.1.
The setting and first examples

As already mentioned in the introduction, our aim is to estimate the regression function
f^{\star}
defined in (
1
) from the observation of
{\boldsymbol{Z}}=\{(x_{1,n},Y_{1,n}),\ldots,(x_{n,n},Y_{n,n})\}
. Given a family
{\mathcal{F}}
of candidate functions on
{\mathcal{X}}
, we evaluate the performance of an estimator
\widehat{f}
with values in
{\mathcal{F}}
by means of the
\ell_{1}
-loss. We recall that neither the values
\sigma_{p}({\mathbf{x}})
nor those of
p
for which these quantities are finite are assumed to be known.

Let us now turn to some examples that illustrate our statistical framework.

Example 1
.

The observations
Z_{i}=(x_{i,n},Y_{i,n})
are drawn from the model

Y_{i,n}=f^{\star}(x_{i,n})+\tau_{i,n}\xi_{i}\quad\text{for $i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$}

where the
\tau_{i,n}
are nonnegative numbers and the
\xi_{i}
i.i.d. centred random variables with a
p
-th absolute moment
{\mathbb{E}}\left[{|\xi_{1}|^{p}}\right]=1
for some unknown
p\geqslant 1
. Then, our moment assumption is satisfied with
\sigma_{p}^{p}({\mathbf{x}})=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i,n}^{p}
.

Example 2
.

Given
\beta>0
, let
q_{\beta}
be the function defined on
[0,+\infty)
by

q_{\beta}:u\mapsto\frac{\left({2\log u}\right)^{\beta-1}}{G(\beta)u^{3}}1\hskip-2.6pt{\rm l}_{[1,+\infty)}(u)

where
G
denotes the gamma function, that is
G(\beta)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}t^{\beta-1}e^{-t}dt
. We consider the regression framework

Y_{i,n}=f^{\star}(x_{i,n})+\xi_{i}\quad\text{for $i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$}

where the
\xi_{i}
are i.i.d. centred random variables with density
x\mapsto q_{\beta}(|x|)
. In this case, our moment assumption is satisfied for any
p\in[1,2)
while
{\mathbb{E}}\left[{|\xi_{1}|^{2}}\right]=+\infty
.

Example 3
.

For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
Y_{i,n}
is distributed as a Bernoulli random variable with mean
f^{\star}(x_{i,n})\in[0,1]
. Then our assumptions are satisfied with
p=2
and

\sigma_{2}^{2}({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f^{\star}(x_{i,n})\left({1-f^{\star}(x_{i,n})}\right).

In particular, when
{\mathcal{X}}=[0,1]
,
x_{i,n}=(i-1/2)/n
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
f^{\star}
is continuous on
[0,1]
,

\sigma_{2}^{2}({\mathbf{x}})=\sigma_{2}^{2}({\mathbf{x}},n)\,\displaystyle{\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to+\infty}}\,\sigma_{2}(f^{\star})=\int_{0}^{1}f^{\star}(x)\left({1-f^{\star}(x)}\right)dx.

Example 4
.

For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
Y_{i,n}
is a Poisson random variable with mean
f^{\star}(x_{i,n})>0
. Then our assumptions are satisfied with
p=2
and

\sigma_{2}^{2}({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f^{\star}(x_{i,n}).

In particular, when
{\mathcal{X}}=[0,1]
,
x_{i,n}=(i-1/2)/n
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
f^{\star}
is continuous on
[0,1]
,

\sigma_{2}^{2}({\mathbf{x}})=\sigma_{2}^{2}({\mathbf{x}},n)\,\displaystyle{\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to+\infty}}\,\sigma_{2}(f^{\star})=\int_{0}^{1}f^{\star}(x)dx.

2.2.
Construction of the estimator

Our construction is based on the test statistic
T({\boldsymbol{Z}},f,g)
which is defined for
f,g\in{\mathcal{F}}
by

T({\boldsymbol{Z}},f,g)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left({f(x_{i,n})-Y_{i,n}}\right){\mathrm{\>sgn}}(f,g,x_{i,n}),

where
{\mathrm{\>sgn}}(f,g,x)={\mathrm{\>sgn}}(f(x),g(x))
for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
.
The statistic
T({\boldsymbol{Z}},f,g)
possesses the following properties.

Lemma 1
.

For every
f,g\in{\mathcal{F}}
,

(5)

n\left[{\ell(f^{\star},f)-2\ell(f^{\star},g)}\right]\leqslant n\left[{\ell(f,g)-\ell(f^{\star},g)}\right]\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}\left[{T({\boldsymbol{Z}},f,g)}\right]\leqslant n\ell(f^{\star},f).

In particular, if
\ell(f^{\star},f)
is larger than
2\ell(f^{\star},g)
, the expectation of our test statistic
T({\boldsymbol{Z}},f,g)
is necessarily large while it is small when
\ell(f^{\star},f)
is small.
By taking the supremum with respect to
g\in{\mathcal{F}}
in (
5
), we obtain that

n\left[{\ell(f^{\star},f)-2\ell(f^{\star},{\mathcal{F}})}\right]\leqslant\sup_{g\in{\mathcal{F}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{T({\boldsymbol{Z}},f,g)}\right]\leqslant n\ell(f^{\star},f)\quad\text{for every $f\in{\mathcal{F}}$.}

An element
\overline{f}
which minimises
{\mathbf{T}}:f\mapsto\sup_{g\in{\mathcal{F}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{T({\boldsymbol{Z}},f,g)}\right]
over
{\mathcal{F}}
satisfies thus for every
f\in{\mathcal{F}}
,

\displaystyle\ell(f^{\star},\overline{f})

\displaystyle\leqslant n^{-1}\sup_{g\in{\mathcal{F}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{T({\boldsymbol{Z}},\overline{f},g)}\right]+2\ell(f^{\star},{\mathcal{F}})

\displaystyle\leqslant n^{-1}\sup_{g\in{\mathcal{F}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{T({\boldsymbol{Z}},f,g)}\right]+2\ell(f^{\star},{\mathcal{F}})\leqslant\ell(f^{\star},f)+2\ell(f^{\star},{\mathcal{F}}).

Since
f
can be chosen arbitrarily in
{\mathcal{F}}
, we derive that
\ell(f^{\star},\overline{f})\leqslant 3\ell(f^{\star},{\mathcal{F}})
. This means that the minimiser
\overline{f}
of
{\mathbf{T}}
almost provides a best approximation of
f^{\star}
in
{\mathcal{F}}
. The heuristic that supports our approach lies on the fact that if
T({\boldsymbol{Z}},f,g)
remains close enough to its expectation uniformly over
f,g\in{\mathcal{F}}
, we can substitute
T({\boldsymbol{Z}},f,g)
for
{\mathbb{E}}\left[{T({\boldsymbol{Z}},f,g)}\right]
. By doing so and setting

(6)

T({\boldsymbol{Z}},f)=\sup_{g\in{\mathcal{F}}}T({\boldsymbol{Z}},f,g)\quad\text{for every $f\in{\mathcal{F}}$,}

we define our estimator
\widehat{f}
of
f^{\star}
as an (approximate) minimiser of
f\mapsto T({\boldsymbol{Z}},f)
over
{\mathcal{F}}
, that is, an element of the (random) set

(7)

{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mathfrak{c}}}({\boldsymbol{Z}},{\mathcal{F}})=\left\{{f\in{\mathcal{F}},\;T({\boldsymbol{Z}},f)\leqslant\inf_{g\in{\mathcal{F}}}T({\boldsymbol{Z}},g)+{\mathfrak{c}}}\right\}

where
{\mathfrak{c}}
denotes some (small enough) nonnegative numerical constant.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

Note that

(8)

\left[{f(x_{i,n})-g(x_{i,n})}\right]{\mathrm{\>sgn}}(f,g,x_{i,n})=\left|{f(x_{i,n})-g(x_{i,n})}\right|.

For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\left({f(x_{i,n})-Y_{i,n}}\right){\mathrm{\>sgn}}(f,g,x_{i,n})}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[{f(x_{i,n})-f_{i}^{\star}(x_{i,n})}\right]{\mathrm{\>sgn}}(f,g,x_{i,n})\leqslant\left|{f(x_{i,n})-f_{i}^{\star}(x_{i,n})}\right|.

We derive the right-hand side of (
5
) by summing over
i
. Let us now turn to the proof of the left-hand sides.

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\left({f(x_{i,n})-Y_{i,n}}\right){\mathrm{\>sgn}}(f,g,x_{i,n})}\right]

\displaystyle=\left({f(x_{i,n})-g(x_{i,n})}\right){\mathrm{\>sgn}}(f,g,x_{i,n})-\left({f_{i}^{\star}(x_{i,n})-g(x_{i,n})}\right){\mathrm{\>sgn}}(f,g,x_{i,n})

\displaystyle\geqslant\left|{f(x_{i,n})-g(x_{i,n})}\right|-\left|{f_{i}^{\star}(x_{i,n})-g(x_{i,n})}\right|

2.3.
Our estimator versus the LSE on an example

Let us consider the regression framework

(9)

Y_{i,n}=a^{\star}f_{0}\left({x_{i,n}}\right)+\xi_{i,n}\quad\text{for $i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$}

where
x_{i,n}=i/n
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
f_{0}
is a known function in
{\mathbb{L}}_{1}((0,1],dx)\cap{\mathbb{L}}_{2}((0,1],dx)

and
\xi_{1,n},\ldots,\xi_{n,n}
independent centred random variables. We shall assume that

0<\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|f_{0}(x_{i})|\,\displaystyle{\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to+\infty}}\,\left\|{f_{0}}\right\|_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{0}^{2}(x_{i})\,\displaystyle{\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to+\infty}}\,\left\|{f_{0}}\right\|_{2}^{2},

where
\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{1}
and
\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{2}
denote the
{\mathbb{L}}_{1}((0,1],dx)
and
{\mathbb{L}}_{2}((0,1],dx)
norms respectively. Our aim is to estimate the unknown parameter
a^{\star}
.
The LSE on the one-dimensional linear space
{\mathcal{F}}=\{af_{0},\;a\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
is

(10)

\widetilde{a}_{n}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i,n}f_{0}(x_{i,n})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{0}^{2}(x_{i,n})}=a^{\star}+\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i,n}f_{0}(x_{i,n})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{0}^{2}(x_{i,n})}

while we shall prove below that our estimator is given by

(11)

\widehat{a}_{n}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i,n}{\mathrm{\>sgn}}(f_{0}(x_{i,n}))}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|{f_{0}(x_{i,n})}\right|}=a^{\star}+\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i,n}{\mathrm{\>sgn}}(f_{0}(x_{i,n}))}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|{f_{0}(x_{i,n})}\right|}.

In the ideal situation where the
\xi_{i,n}
are i.i.d. centred with a second moment, say equal to 1, the quadratic risks
R_{n}^{2}(\widetilde{a}_{n}),R_{n}^{2}(\widehat{a}_{n})
of
\widetilde{a}_{n}
and
\widehat{a}_{n}
satisfy

nR_{n}^{2}(\widetilde{a}_{n})=\left({\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{0}^{2}(x_{i,n})}\right)^{-1}\,\displaystyle{\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to+\infty}}\,\left\|{f_{0}}\right\|_{2}^{-2}\in(0,+\infty)

and

nR_{n}^{2}(\widehat{a}_{n})=\left({\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|f_{0}(x_{i,n})|}\right)^{-2}\,\displaystyle{\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to+\infty}}\,\left\|{f_{0}}\right\|_{1}^{-2}\in(0,+\infty).

Both estimators converge thus at rate
1/\sqrt{n}
and it follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
that the quadratic risk of
\widetilde{a}_{n}
is smaller than that of
\widehat{a}_{n}
.

Nevertheless, the situation may be quite different when the errors are possibly heteroscedastic. To illustrate this fact, let us assume that
f_{0}(x)=(\sqrt{x}|\log(x/e)|)^{-1}
for
x\in(0,1]
and
\xi_{i,n}
is a Gaussian random variable with variance
\sigma_{i,n}^{2}=0
when
i\in\{2,\ldots,n\}
while
\sigma_{1,n}^{2}=n\log^{2}(en)
. This means that if we exclude
Y_{1,n}
, the signal is observed with no noise! The LSE
\widetilde{a}_{n}
is then distributed as a Gaussian random variable centred at
a^{\star}
with variance

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{1,n}^{2}f_{0}^{2}(x_{1,n})}{\left[{\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{0}^{2}(x_{i,n})}\right]^{2}}=\left({\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{0}^{2}(x_{i,n})}\right)^{-2}\,\displaystyle{\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to+\infty}}\,\left({\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dx}{x\log^{2}(x/e)}}\right)^{-2}=1.

It is therefore inconsistent while the variance of our estimator
\widehat{a}_{n}
, which is also Gaussian with mean
a^{\star}
, is

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{1,n}^{2}}{n^{2}}\left({\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{0}(x_{i,n})}\right)^{-2}\quad\text{with}\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{0}(x_{i,n})\,\displaystyle{\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to+\infty}}\,\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dx}{|\log(x/e)|\sqrt{x}}\approx 0.92.

The estimator
\widetilde{a}_{n}
converges thus at the rate
\sigma_{1,n}/n=(\log n)/\sqrt{n}
. Denoting by
c_{0}
the first absolute moment of a standard Gaussian variable, we deduce that the risks of the estimators
\widetilde{f}=\widetilde{a}f_{0}
and
\widehat{f}=\widehat{a}f_{0}
are respectively given by

(12)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\ell\left({a^{\star}f_{0},\widetilde{f}}\right)}\right]=\frac{c_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{0}(x_{i,n})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{0}^{2}(x_{i,n})}\,\displaystyle{\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to+\infty}}\,c_{0}\left\|{f_{0}}\right\|_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\ell\left({a^{\star}f_{0},\widehat{f}}\right)}\right]=\frac{c_{0}\log n}{\sqrt{n}}.

Note that our estimator would remain consistent as long as
\sigma_{1,n}^{2}=o(n^{2})
. This example illustrates the fact that the LSE may perform poorly when the errors are heteroscedastic, even in a Gaussian setting. Its risk may crucially depend on the largest value of the variances while the risk of our estimator only depends on their average which may be much smaller. This property is actually not specific to this example and will be established in greater generality in our main result.

Proof.

Let

3.
Extremal elements and main results

3.1.
Extremal elements of
{\mathcal{F}}

The elements
\overline{f}
of
{\mathcal{F}}
which satisfy the following property play a central role in
our main results.

Definition 1
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be a class of functions on a set
{\mathcal{Y}}
. We say that
\overline{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}
is extremal in

{\mathcal{G}}
with degree not larger than
D\geqslant 1
if the VC-dimensions of the classes of subsets of
{\mathcal{Y}}

{\mathcal{A}}_{>}(\overline{g})=\left\{\{g>\overline{g}\},\;g\in{\mathcal{G}}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathcal{A}}_{<}(\overline{g})=\left\{\{g<\overline{g}\},\;g\in{\mathcal{G}}\right\}

are not larger than
D
. The degree of an extremal element
\overline{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}
is the smallest
integer
D
for which the above property is satisfied.

We recall that the VC-dimension of a class
{\mathcal{A}}
of subsets of
{\mathcal{Y}}
is not larger than
D
if

\left|{\{B\cap A,\;A\in{\mathcal{A}}\}}\right|<2^{|B|}
for every finite subset
B
of
{\mathcal{Y}}
with cardinality larger than
D
. In other words, there exists at least one subset of
B
which is not of the form

B\cap A
with
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
. The VC-dimension of
{\mathcal{A}}
is the smallest integer
D
for which
this property is satisfied.

Going back to our regression framework with the class of functions
{\mathcal{F}}
on
{\mathcal{X}}
, we denote by
\pi_{n}f
the restriction of a function
f\in{\mathcal{F}}
to the subset
{\mathcal{X}}_{n}=\{x_{1,n},\ldots,x_{n,n}\}\subset{\mathcal{X}}
. The set
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}=\{\pi_{n}f,\;f\in{\mathcal{F}}\}
is therefore a class of functions on the finite set
{\mathcal{X}}_{n}
. For an integer
D\geqslant 1
, we define
{\mathcal{O}}(D)
as the subset of
{\mathcal{F}}
which gathers these elements
\overline{f}\in{\mathcal{F}}
whose restrictions
\pi_{n}\overline{f}
are extremal with degree not larger than
D
in
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
. Let us now state some useful properties that we shall repeatedly use throughout this paper.

(i)

Since
|{\mathcal{X}}_{n}|\leqslant n
(possibly smaller in case of ex-aequos), the
VC-dimension of a class of subsets of
{\mathcal{X}}_{n}
cannot be larger than
n
. As a consequence,
every element of
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
is extremal with a degree which is not larger than
n
,

(13)

{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{O}}(n)\quad\text{and}\quad\ell(f,{\mathcal{O}}(n))=0\quad\text{for every $f\in{\mathcal{F}}$.}

This also means that we may restrict the values of
D
to the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

(ii)

It is clear that for
\overline{f}\in{\mathcal{F}}
,

\left|{\left\{\{f>\pi_{n}\overline{f}\},\;f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\right\}}\right|\leqslant\left|{\left\{\{f>\overline{f}\},\;f\in{\mathcal{F}}\right\}}\right|.

This implies that the degree of
\pi_{n}\overline{f}
in
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
cannot be larger than that of
\overline{f}
in
{\mathcal{F}}
. The set
{\mathcal{O}}(D)
contains thus these functions which are extremal in
{\mathcal{F}}
with degree not larger than
D
.

(iii)

If
\overline{f}
is an element of
{\mathcal{F}}
for which
|{\mathcal{A}}_{>}(\overline{f})|\vee|{\mathcal{A}}_{<}(\overline{f})|\leqslant 2^{D}
, then
\overline{f}
is extremal in
{\mathcal{F}}
with degree not larger than
D
, hence belongs to
{\mathcal{O}}(D)
.

In Example
5
, the sets
\{f>\overline{f}\}
and
\{f<\overline{f}\}
are unions of intervals of
{\mathcal{I}}
. Since the cardinality of
{\mathcal{I}}
is
D
, there exist no more than
2^{D}
of such sets. Every element
\overline{f}\in{\mathcal{F}}
is therefore extremal in
{\mathcal{F}}
with degree not larger than
D
.

More generally, the following result holds.

Proposition 1
.

If
{\mathcal{F}}
is a linear space on
{\mathcal{X}}
with finite dimension
d\geqslant 1
, all the elements of
{\mathcal{F}}
are extremal with degree not larger than
d+1
. In particular,
{\mathcal{O}}(d+1)={\mathcal{F}}
.

Proof.

This proposition is the consequence of the fact that
{\mathcal{F}}
is VC-subgraph with dimension at most
d+1
(see van der Vaart and Wellner
(
van der Vaart and Wellner, 1996
)
Lemma 2.6.15). This property implies that the class of sets
\{\{f>t\},\;f\in{\mathcal{F}},t\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
are VC with dimension not larger than
d+1
. Hence, so are the subsets
{\mathcal{A}}_{<}(\overline{f})
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{>}(\overline{f})
for every
\overline{f}\in{\mathcal{F}}
.
∎

3.2.
The main result

Our estimator possesses the following property.

Theorem 1

Assume that the random variables
Y_{1,n},\ldots,Y_{n,n}
are independent and let
\widehat{f}
be an element of
{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mathfrak{c}}}({\boldsymbol{Z}},{\mathcal{F}})

(14)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\ell\left({\widehat{f},\overline{f}}\right)}\right]\leqslant 2\ell\left({f^{\star},\overline{f}}\right)+\kappa\inf_{p\in[1,2]}\left[{\sigma_{p}({\mathbf{x}})\left({\frac{D}{n}}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{p}}}\right]+\frac{{\mathfrak{c}}}{n}

where

(15)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\ell\left({f^{\star},\widehat{f}}\right)}\right]\leqslant\inf_{D\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}\left[{3\ell\left({f^{\star},{\mathcal{O}}(D)}\right)+\kappa\inf_{p\in[1,2]}\left\{{\sigma_{p}({\mathbf{x}})\left({\frac{D}{n}}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{p}}}\right\}}\right]+\frac{{\mathfrak{c}}}{n}

and when

are square integrable,

(16)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\ell\left({f^{\star},\widehat{f}}\right)}\right]\leqslant\inf_{D\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}\left[{3\ell\left({f^{\star},{\mathcal{O}}(D)}\right)+\kappa\sqrt{\left({\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathop{\rm Var}\nolimits(Y_{i,n})}\right)\frac{D}{n}}}\right]+\frac{{\mathfrak{c}}}{n}.

A straightforward consequence of Theorem
1
and Proposition

Corollary 1

The estimator
\widehat{f}
built on a linear space
{\mathcal{F}}
with dimension

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\ell\left({f^{\star},\widehat{f}}\right)}\right]\leqslant 3\ell\left({f^{\star},{\mathcal{F}}}\right)+\kappa\inf_{p\in[1,2]}\left\{{\sigma_{p}({\mathbf{x}})\left({\frac{(d+1)\wedge n}{n}}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{p}}}\right\}+\frac{{\mathfrak{c}}}{n}.

In particular, when

(19)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\ell_{2}^{2}\left({f^{\star},\widehat{f}}\right)}\right]\leqslant\ell_{2}^{2}\left({f^{\star},{\mathcal{F}}}\right)+\tau^{2}\frac{d}{n}.

n/D\geqslant\exp[\beta(e-2)]
then

\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left({\frac{n}{D}}\right)+1\leqslant L_{n}\leqslant\frac{e}{e-1}\left[{\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left({\frac{n}{D}}\right)+1}\right].

For large enough values of
n/D
,
L_{n}
is of order
\log(n/D)
, hence the left-hand side of (
22
) of order
\sqrt{D\log(n/D)/n}
. Up to a logarithmic factor, the risk of the estimator is of the same order as that we would get for errors which are square integrable.

Let us now prove (
22
). By doing the change of variables
t=(2-p)\log u
,
dt=(2-p)u^{-1}du
, we obtain that for
p\in[1,2)
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[{|\xi_{1}|^{p}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\beta}}{G(\beta)}\int_{1}^{+\infty}\frac{\log^{\beta-1}u}{u^{3-p}}du=\frac{2^{\beta}}{(2-p)^{\beta}G(\beta)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}t^{\beta-1}e^{-t}dt=\left({\frac{2}{2-p}}\right)^{\beta}.

Consequently,
\sigma_{p}({\mathbf{x}})=[2/(2-p)]^{\beta/p}
. Setting
z=(n/D)^{1/\beta}
and
L=2/(2-p)\in[2,+\infty)
, hence
p=2(1-1/L)
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\sigma_{p}({\mathbf{x}})\left({\frac{D}{n}}\right)^{1-1/p}

\displaystyle=z^{-\beta}\left({\frac{2z}{2-p}}\right)^{\beta/p}=z^{-\beta}\exp\left[{\beta f(L)}\right]\quad\text{with}\quad f(L)=\frac{L\log(Lz)}{2(L-1)}.

Since
f^{\prime}(L)=[2(L-1)]^{-1}\left({1-\log(Lz)/(L-1)}\right)
,
f
reaches its minimum at the solution
L_{n}
of the equation
\log z=L-\log L-1
on
[2,+\infty)
, which exists and is unique when
z\geqslant(e/2)
, i.e.
n\geqslant D(e/2)^{\beta}
. Since
\log(L_{n}z)=L_{n}-1
and
L_{n}=\log(eL_{n}z)
we get

\displaystyle\inf_{p\in[1,2)}\left[{\sigma_{p}({\mathbf{x}})\left({\frac{D}{n}}\right)^{1-1/p}}\right]

\displaystyle=z^{-\beta}\inf_{L\geqslant 2}\exp\left[{\beta f(L)}\right]=z^{-\beta}\exp\left[{\beta\frac{L_{n}\log(L_{n}z)}{2(L_{n}-1)}}\right]

\displaystyle=z^{-\beta/2}(eL_{n})^{\beta/2}

which is (
22
).

4.
From approximation theory to risk bounds

Throughout this section, we assume that
f^{\star}
belongs to
{\mathcal{F}}
. If it were not the case, the risk bound that we establish here would not inflate by more than the additional term
3\ell(f^{\star},{\mathcal{F}})
. For the sake of simplicity, we also assume that
\sigma_{p}({\mathbf{x}})
can be bounded independently of
{\mathbf{x}}
and
n
by some positive number
\sigma_{p}
when
\sigma_{p}({\mathbf{x}})<+\infty
and we use the convention
\sigma_{p}=+\infty
otherwise. It follows from Theorem
1
that the risk of the estimator
\widehat{f}
built on
{\mathcal{F}}
satisfies
{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\ell\left({f^{\star},\widehat{f}}\right)}\right]\leqslant B_{n}(f^{\star})+{\mathfrak{c}}/n
where

(23)

B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{F}})=\inf_{D\geqslant 1}\left\{{3\ell\left({f^{\star},{\mathcal{O}}(D)}\right)+\kappa\inf_{p\in[1,2]}\left[{\sigma_{p}\left({\frac{D}{n}}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{p}}}\right]}\right\}.

For some classes
{\mathcal{F}}
of interest, our aim is to specify the quantity
B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{F}})
by describing the subsets
\{{\mathcal{O}}(D),D\geqslant 1\}
of extremal functions as well as their approximation properties with respect to the elements of
{\mathcal{F}}
. Let us now turn to our examples.

4.1.
k
-piecewise monotone functions

Hereafter
{\mathcal{X}}
denotes a nontrivial interval of
{\mathbb{R}}
and we assume that the points
x_{i,n}
are labelled in increasing order, that is,
x_{1,n}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant x_{n,n}
. Given a positive integer
k
, we consider the class
{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{M}}_{k}
of functions which are called
k
-piecewise monotone
on
{\mathcal{X}}
which means that they are monotone on each element of a partition
{\mathcal{J}}
of
{\mathcal{X}}
into at most
k
nontrivial intervals. For
f\in{\mathcal{M}}_{k}
,
the
{\mathcal{J}}
-variation
of
f
on
{\mathcal{X}}
is the quantity

(24)

V_{{\mathcal{J}}}({\mathbf{x}},f)=\left({\sum_{J\in{\mathcal{J}}}\sqrt{\frac{n_{J}V_{J}(f)}{n}}}\right)^{2}\quad\text{where}\quad V_{J}(f)=\sup_{x\in J}f(x)-\mathop{\vphantom{p}\inf}_{x\in J}f(x)

and
n_{J}
is the number of points
x_{i,n}
that fall in
J\in{\mathcal{J}}
. It follows from Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality that
V_{{\mathcal{J}}}({\mathbf{x}},f)\leqslant V(f)
where

(25)

V(f)=\sum_{J\in{\mathcal{J}}}V_{J}(f)

is the
(total) variation
of
f
on
{\mathcal{X}}
. This latter quantity is independent of the design points
x_{i,n}
while the
{\mathcal{J}}
-variation does depend on them. Note that
V_{{\mathcal{J}}}({\mathbf{x}},f)=0
if and only if
f
is constant on each element
J
of the partition
{\mathcal{J}}
which contains at least a point
x_{i,n}
.

With this notation,
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
is the set of functions which are monotone on
{\mathcal{X}}
. The class
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
contains those which are unimodal. For general values of
k\geqslant 1
,
{\mathcal{M}}_{k}
contains the functions which are
k
-piecewise constant
on
{\mathcal{X}}
, that is, piecewise constant on a partition of
{\mathcal{X}}
into at most
k
nontrivial intervals. Such functions are sometimes called
regressograms
with
k
bins (or a spline with degree 0 on
k+1
knots). The following proposition is a consequence of Theorem
3
applied to
{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{M}}_{k}
and
r=1
.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\overline{f}
be a function which is both
k
-piecewise monotone and
K
-piecewise constant on
{\mathcal{X}}
with
K\geqslant 1
. Then
\overline{f}
is extremal in
{\mathcal{M}}_{k}
with degree not larger than
2k+K-1
.

The set
{\mathcal{O}}(D)
with
D=2k+K-1
contains thus the functions which are both
k
-piecewise monotone and
K
-piecewise constant. Denoting by
{\mathcal{O}}_{\text{pc}}(K)\subset{\mathcal{M}}_{k}
the set of such functions, we immediately derive from Proposition
3
and (
23
) that for every regression function
f^{\star}
on
{\mathcal{X}}
,

(26)

B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{M}}_{k})\leqslant\inf_{K\geqslant 1}\left\{{3\ell\left({f^{\star},{\mathcal{O}}_{\text{pc}}(K)}\right)+\kappa\inf_{p\in[1,2]}\left[{\sigma_{p}\left({\frac{2k+K-1}{n}}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{p}}}\right]}\right\}.

In particular,

(27)

B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{M}}_{k})\leqslant\kappa\inf_{p\in[1,2]}\left[{\sigma_{p}\left({\frac{2k+K-1}{n}}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{p}}}\right]\quad\text{for every $f^{\star}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{\text{pc}}(K)$.}

If
f^{\star}
is a regressogram with at most
k
bins and the errors
Y_{i,n}-{\mathbb{E}}\left[{Y_{i,n}}\right]
admit a
p
-th moment with
p\in(1,2]
,
f^{\star}
belongs to
{\mathcal{O}}_{\text{pc}}(k)
and our estimator
\widehat{f}
on
{\mathcal{M}}_{k}
converges thus to
f^{\star}
at a rate which is at least
n^{-(1-1/p)}
.

The following result describes the approximation properties of the elements of
{\mathcal{O}}_{\text{pc}}(K)
with respect to monotone functions on
{\mathcal{X}}
for the loss
\ell
. Its proof is postponed to Section
7.3
.

Proposition 4
.

Let
f\in{\mathcal{M}}_{k}
with
k\geqslant 1
. For every
\gamma>0
, there exists an element
\overline{f}
in
{\mathcal{O}}_{\text{pc}}(\left\lfloor k+\gamma\right\rfloor)
such that

(28)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|{f(x_{i,n})-\overline{f}(x_{i,n})}\right|\leqslant\frac{V_{{\mathcal{J}}}({\mathbf{x}},f)}{\gamma}.

We deduce the following result.

Corollary 2
.

Let
k\geqslant 1
. Assume that
\sigma_{p}<+\infty
for some
p\in[1,2]
and
f^{\star}
is an element of
{\mathcal{M}}_{k}
with a finite
{\mathcal{J}}
-variation
V_{{\mathcal{J}}}=V_{{\mathcal{J}}}({\mathbf{x}},f)
defined by (
24
). Then, for some numerical constant
C>0
,

(29)

B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{M}}_{k})\leqslant C\left[{\sigma_{p}^{\frac{1}{s+1}}\left({\frac{V_{{\mathcal{J}}}}{n}}\right)^{\frac{s}{s+1}}+\sigma_{p}\left({\frac{3k-1}{n}}\right)^{s}}\right]\quad\text{with $s=1-\frac{1}{p}\in[0,1/2]$.}

In particular, when the variation
V(f^{\star})
of
f^{\star}
is finite on
{\mathcal{X}}
, it follows from the inequality
V_{{\mathcal{J}}}({\mathbf{x}},f)\leqslant V(f^{\star})
that
\widehat{f}
converges to
f^{\star}
at a rate which is at least
n^{-(p-1)/(2p-1)}
when
\sigma_{p}<+\infty
.

From a nonasymptotic point of view, it is interesting to notice that when the
{\mathcal{J}}
-variation
V_{{\mathcal{J}}}({\mathbf{x}},f^{\star})
of
f^{\star}
is small enough, so that the first term in the right-hand side of (
29
) becomes sufficiently small compared to the second one, the risk bound that we get is of the same order as that we would get for estimating a regressogram on
k
bins (see (
27
) with
K=k
).

Proof of Corollary
2
.

Let
p\in[1,2]
such that
\sigma_{p}<+\infty
. It follows from Proposition
4
and (
26
) that for every
\gamma>0

\displaystyle B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{M}}_{k})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{3V_{{\mathcal{J}}}}{\gamma}+\kappa\sigma_{p}\left({\frac{2k+\left\lfloor k+\gamma\right\rfloor-1}{n}}\right)^{s}.

When
V_{{\mathcal{J}}}=0
, we choose
\gamma=1/2
so that
\left\lfloor k+\gamma\right\rfloor=k
and we get that
B_{n}(f^{\star})\leqslant\kappa\sigma_{p}[(3k-1)/n]^{1-1/p}
. Otherwise, by using the fact that
u\mapsto u^{s}
is subadditive and the inequality
\left\lfloor k+\gamma\right\rfloor\leqslant k+\gamma
, we obtain that

\displaystyle B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{M}}_{k})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{3V_{{\mathcal{J}}}}{\gamma}+\kappa\sigma_{p}\left[{\left({\frac{\gamma}{n}}\right)^{s}+\left({\frac{3k-1}{n}}\right)^{s}}\right].

The result follows by choosing
\gamma=(V_{{\mathcal{J}}}/(\kappa\sigma_{p}))^{1/(s+1)}n^{s/(s+1)}
.
∎

4.2.
k
-piecewise monotone-convex-concave functions

In this section,
{\mathcal{X}}
denotes a nontrivial compact interval of
{\mathbb{R}}
. Hereafter, we say that a function
f
is
convex-concave
on
[a,b]
, with
a<b
, if it is either convex or concave on this interval. Given a continuous convex-concave function
f
on an interval
[a,b]
, we may denote by
f_{r}^{\prime}(x)
and
f_{l}^{\prime}(x)
the right and left derivatives of
f
at
x\in[a,b]
respectively. We allow these quantities to take the values
\pm\infty
. For
f
being additionally monotone, we define the
linear index
of
f
as the quantity

\Gamma(f)=1-\frac{1}{2}\left[{\frac{\left|{f^{\prime}_{r}(a)}\right|\wedge\left|{f^{\prime}_{l}(b)}\right|}{|\Delta|}+\frac{|\Delta|}{\left|{f^{\prime}_{r}(a)}\right|\vee\left|{f^{\prime}_{l}(b)}\right|}}\right]\quad\text{with}\quad\Delta=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.

In this formula, we use the convention
0/0=1
and
1/(+\infty)=0
. The linear index
\Gamma(f)
belongs to
[0,1]
and equals 0 if and only if
f
is affine on
[a,b]
. In contrast, the linear index of the function
=(1-10^{10}x)1\hskip-2.6pt{\rm l}_{[0,10^{-10}]}
, which is nonincreasing and “strongly” convex on
[0,1]
, is
\Gamma(f)=1-10^{-10}/2
and is therefore close to 1. The linear index measures how far the graph of a continuous, monotone convex-concave function
f
on an interval
[a,b]
differs from the chord joining
(a,f(a))
to
(b,f(b))
.

Given
k\geqslant 1
, we say that
f
is
k
-piecewise monotone-convex-concave
on
{\mathcal{X}}
if it is
continuous
on
{\mathcal{X}}
and if there exists a partition of
{\mathcal{X}}
into at most
k
nontrivial intervals on the closure of each of which
f
is monotone and convex-concave. We denote by
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
the class of such functions. The set
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
contains the functions which are continuous and either convex or concave on
{\mathcal{X}}
. Those with an
S
-shape (first convex and then concave on
{\mathcal{X}}
) belong to
{\mathcal{C}}_{4}
. For
k\geqslant 1
,
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
contains all the functions which are continuous and piecewise linear on a partition of
{\mathcal{X}}
into at most
k
intervals. We shall call these functions
k
-piecewise linear on
{\mathcal{X}}
. They are splines of degree at most 1 with
k+1
knots.
We start with the following result which derives from Theorem
3
applied to
{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{C}}_{k}
and
r=2
.

Proposition 5
.

A function
\overline{f}
of
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
which is
K
-piecewise linear on
{\mathcal{X}}
with
K\geqslant 1
is extremal in
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
with degree not larger than
3k+2(K-1)
.

The set
{\mathcal{O}}(D)
with
D=3k+2(K-1)
contains thus these
K
-piecewise linear functions which belong to
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
. Let us denote by
{\mathcal{O}}_{\text{pl}}(K)
the set of such functions. We immediately deduce from Proposition
5
and (
23
) that for every regression function
f^{\star}
on
{\mathcal{X}}
,

(30)

B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{C}}_{k})\leqslant\inf_{K\geqslant 1}\left\{{3\ell\left({f^{\star},{\mathcal{O}}_{\text{pl}}(K)}\right)+\kappa\inf_{p\in[1,2]}\left[{\sigma_{p}\left({\frac{3k+2(K-1)}{n}}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{p}}}\right]}\right\}.

In particular, if
f^{\star}
belongs to
{\mathcal{O}}_{\text{pl}}(K)
and
\sigma_{p}<+\infty
for some
p\in[1,2]
,

(31)

B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{C}}_{k})\leqslant\kappa\sigma_{p}\left({\frac{3k+2(K-1)}{n}}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{p}}.

This inequality applies to every function
f^{\star}
which is
k
-piecewise linear on
{\mathcal{X}}
as it automatically belongs to
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
. For such an extremal function, the estimator
\widehat{f}
converges at a rate which is at least
n^{1/p-1}
when
p\in(1,2]
.

Given a function
f\in{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
associated with a partition
{\mathcal{J}}
of the interval
{\mathcal{X}}
, we define the
{\mathcal{J}}
-
variation-index
of
f
as

(32)

W_{{\mathcal{J}}}(f)=\left[{\sum_{J\in{\mathcal{J}}}\left({\frac{n_{J}V_{J}(f)\Gamma_{J}(f)}{n}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}}\right]^{3}

where
n_{J}
is the number of design points that fall in
J
,
V_{J}(f)
the variation of
f
on
J
and
\Gamma_{J}(f)
the linear index of
f
on the closure of
J
. Note that
W_{{\mathcal{J}}}(f)=0
if and only if
f
is affine on every interval
J
that contains at least one design point
x_{i,n}
. Besides, it follows from Hölder’s inequality that the
{\mathcal{J}}
-variation-index of
f
satisfies

(33)

W_{{\mathcal{J}}}(f)\leqslant\left({\sum_{J\in{\mathcal{J}}}\frac{n_{J}}{n}}\right)\left({\sum_{J\in{\mathcal{J}}}V_{J}(f)}\right)\left({\sum_{J\in{\mathcal{J}}}\Gamma_{J}(f)}\right)=V(f)\Gamma_{{\mathcal{J}}}(f)

where
V(f)=\sum_{J\in{\mathcal{J}}}V_{J}(f)
is the variation of
f
on
{\mathcal{X}}
and
\Gamma_{{\mathcal{J}}}(f)=\sum_{J\in{\mathcal{J}}}\Gamma_{J}\leqslant|{\mathcal{J}}|\leqslant k
.

The following approximation result is proven in Section
7.4
.

Proposition 6
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a compact interval of length
L>0
,
x_{1,n},\ldots,x_{n,n}
equispaced points in
{\mathcal{X}}
such that

for

,

an element of
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}
with variation

defined by (

) and
{\mathcal{J}}
-variation-index

defined by (

). Then, for every
\gamma>0
, there exists an element
\overline{f}=\overline{f}(\gamma)
in
{\mathcal{O}}_{\text{pl}}(\left\lfloor 2(k+\gamma)\right\rfloor)
such that

(34)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|{f(x_{i,n})-\overline{f}(x_{i,n})}\right|\leqslant\frac{W}{\gamma^{2}}+\frac{2V}{n}.

We deduce

Corollary 3
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be some compact interval of length
L>0
,

a positive integer and

equispaced points in
{\mathcal{X}}
, that is,
x_{i+1}-x_{i,n}=L/n
for

. Assume that
\sigma_{p}<+\infty
for some

and that

belongs to

with variation

defined by (

) and

B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{C}}_{k})\leqslant C\left[{\sigma_{p}^{\frac{2}{s+2}}\left({\frac{\sqrt{W}}{n}}\right)^{\frac{2s}{s+2}}+\sigma_{p}\left({\frac{7k-2}{n}}\right)^{s}+\frac{V}{n}}\right]\quad\text{with $s=1-\frac{1}{p}$.}

When

and

are positive, the rate of convergence of

to

is therefore at least

. From a nonasymptotic point of view, when

as that we would have for estimating a
k
-piecewise linear function. The quantity
W
is small when either the variation or the linear index of the regression function

is small on every interval

that contains a design point.

To illustrate Corollary

, let us assume that
{\mathcal{X}}=[0,1]
,
x_{i,n}=(i-1/2)/n
for

, and

is continuous and convex-concave on

with variation

. Applying Corollary

with

and using the fact that

by (

B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{C}}_{k})\leqslant C\left[{\sigma_{p}^{\frac{2}{s+2}}\left({\frac{\sqrt{2V}}{n}}\right)^{\frac{2s}{s+2}}+\sigma_{p}\left({\frac{12}{n}}\right)^{s}+\frac{V}{n}}\right].

Proof of Corollary
3
.

It follows from Proposition
6
and (

) that for any

\displaystyle B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{C}}_{k})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{3W}{\gamma^{2}}+\kappa\sigma_{p}\left({\frac{3k+2(\left\lfloor 2k+2\gamma\right\rfloor-1)}{n}}\right)^{s}+\frac{6V}{n}.

If

. If

, the result follows by choosing

\displaystyle B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{C}}_{k})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{3W}{\gamma^{2}}+\kappa\sigma_{p}\left[{\left({\frac{4\gamma}{n}}\right)^{s}+\left({\frac{7k-2}{n}}\right)^{s}}\right]+\frac{6V}{n}.

The previous result applies to equispaced design points. What happens when the design is not equispaced? This is what we want to investigate in the remaining part of this section. For the sake of simplicity, we restrict ourselves to the set
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
of monotone-convex-concave functions on the interval
{\mathcal{X}}
. We start with the following definitions.

Definition 2
.

Let
K
be a positive integer and
f
be a continuous, monotone and convex-concave function on the compact interval
[a,b]
,
a<b
. We say that
l
is a
K
-linear interpolation of
f
on
[a,b]
if there exists a subdivision
a_{0}=a<a_{1}<\ldots<a_{K}=b
such that
l
is affine on
[a_{i-1},a_{i}]
and coincides with
f
at
a_{i-1}
and
a_{i}
, i.e.
f(a_{i-1})=l(a_{i-1})
and
f(a_{i})=l(a_{i})
, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
.

If
l
is a linear interpolation of a continuous, monotone and convex-concave function
f
on
[a,b]
, then
l
is also continuous, monotone and convex-concave on this interval. In fact,
l
has the same monotonicity as
f
and if
f
is convex (respectively concave) so is
l
.

Definition 3
.

Let
\lambda
denote the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{R}}
. A modulus
w
of a Borel measure
\mu
on
{\mathbb{R}}
is a nonnegative, nondecreasing, concave function on
[0,+\infty)
that satisfies

\mu(I)\leqslant w(\lambda(I))\quad\text{for every bounded interval $I\subset{\mathbb{R}}$.}

In this definition, we do not require that
w(0)=0
as is usually the case for a modulus
of continuity
. By doing so, we allow the measure
\mu
to be atomic. This is the situation we have in mind as we shall mainly apply it to
\mu=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i,n}}
.

Proposition 7
.

Let
g
be an increasing continuous function on a compact interval
{\mathcal{X}}
with (concave) modulus of continuity
\overline{w}
. If
x_{i,n}=g^{-1}(i/n)
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
then
w:u\mapsto n^{-1}+\overline{w}(u)
is a modulus for
Q=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i,n}}
.

Proof.

The length of an interval
J
is at least the number of integers that fall in this interval minus 1. Given a bounded interval
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
with endpoints
a<b
we may apply this result to the interval
J=\{ng(x),\;x\in I\}
the length of which is
n\left({g(b)-g(a)}\right)
and obtain that

\displaystyle nQ(I)-1

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}1\hskip-2.6pt{\rm l}_{x_{i,n}\in I}-1=\sum_{i=1}^{n}1\hskip-2.6pt{\rm l}_{(i/n)\in g(I)}-1=\sum_{i=1}^{n}1\hskip-2.6pt{\rm l}_{i\in J}-1

\displaystyle\leqslant\lambda\left({J}\right)=n\left({g(b)-g(a)}\right)\leqslant n\overline{w}(|a-b|).

This proves the proposition.
∎

Theorem 2
.

Let
Q
be a probability measure on the bounded interval
[a,b]
,
a<b
,
K
a positive integer and
f
a continuous, monotone, convex-concave function on
[a,b]
with variation
V=\left|{f(b)-f(a)}\right|
and linear index
\Gamma\in[0,1]
. We set

and
\overline{\delta}=|f^{\prime}_{r}(a)|\vee|f^{\prime}_{l}(b)|\in[0,+\infty]

(i)

If the variation
\left|{f^{\prime}_{r}(a)-f^{\prime}_{l}(b)}\right|
of the derivatives of

is finite on

, there exists a
K
-linear interpolation
\overline{f}
of

\displaystyle\int_{[a,b]}\left|{f-\overline{f}}\right|dQ

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\left|{f^{\prime}_{r}(a)-f^{\prime}_{l}(b)}\right|(b-a)}{4K^{2}}.

(ii)

If

\Psi(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\overline{w}(t)}{t}dt<+\infty\quad\text{with $\overline{w}(\cdot)=w(\cdot)-w(0)$,}

(2K)
-linear interpolation
\overline{f}
of

(36)

\int_{[a,b]}\left|{f-\overline{f}}\right|dQ\leqslant Vw(0)+\frac{\left({\delta-\underline{\delta}}\right)(b-a)}{4K^{2}}+\frac{V}{K}\left[{\Psi\left({\frac{V}{2K\delta}}\right)-\Psi\left({\frac{V}{2K\overline{\delta}}}\right)}\right].

In particular, if

has a modulus of the form

and
\alpha\in(0,1]
, there exists a
(2K)
-linear interpolation
\overline{f}
of
f

(37)

\displaystyle\int_{[a,b]}\left|{f-\overline{f}}\right|dQ

\displaystyle\leqslant V\left[{w(0)+\left({\frac{2^{1-\alpha}A}{\alpha K^{1+3\alpha}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}}\right].

Furthermore, if
\alpha=1=A
then
\overline{f}

(38)

\displaystyle\int_{[a,b]}\left|{f-\overline{f}}\right|dQ

\displaystyle\leqslant V\left[{w(0)+\frac{\Gamma}{K^{2}}}\right].

The proof of this result is postponed to Section
7.2
. We deduce the following corollary.

Corollary 4
.

Assume that the regression function
f^{\star}
is continuous, monotone and convex-concave on
{\mathcal{X}}=[a,b]
,
a<b
, with variation
V=\left|{f^{\star}(a)-f^{\star}(b)}\right|
and that
\sigma_{p}<+\infty
for some

.

(i)

If the variation
V^{\prime}=\left|{(f^{\star})^{\prime}_{r}(a)-(f^{\star})^{\prime}_{l}(b)}\right|
of the derivatives of
f^{\star}
is finite on
[a,b]
, there exists a numerical constant
C>0
such that

(39)

B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{C}}_{1})\leqslant C\left[{\sigma_{p}^{\frac{2}{s+2}}\left({\frac{V^{\prime}(b-a)}{n^{2}}}\right)^{\frac{s}{s+2}}+\frac{\sigma_{p}}{n^{s}}}\right]\quad\text{with $s=1-1/p$.}

(ii)

If the probability
Q=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i,n}}
has a modulus
w
of the form
w(u)=w(0)+A(u/(b-a))^{\alpha}
for

with
A\geqslant 1
and
\alpha\in(0,1]
, there exists a numerical constant
C>0
such that

(40)

B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{C}}_{1})\leqslant C\left[{\sigma_{p}^{\frac{\beta}{s+\beta}}\left({\frac{c_{\alpha}(A)V}{n^{\beta}}}\right)^{\frac{s}{s+\beta}}+Vw(0)+\frac{\sigma_{p}}{n^{s}}}\right]\quad\text{with $\beta=\frac{1+3\alpha}{1+\alpha}\in(1,2]$}

and

.

In most of our applications,

is of order

. When

and the variation

is finite on

, the estimator converges at a rate which is at least

, independently of the locations of the design points. When

, this is no longer the case and the rate that we get does depend on the modulus of

. Provided that

is of order

and

, this rate is at least

with

given by (

, which means that the design is close to equispaced, the rate is at least

. When
\alpha
is close to 0, the design is very irregular,

is close to 1 and

close to

which is the rate we would get for estimating a regression function which is only monotone on

.

Proof of Corollary
4
.

Let
K
be a positive integer to be chosen later on. Under
(i)
, there exists a
K
-linear interpolation
\overline{f}
of

which satisfies (

). Since

is an element of

\displaystyle B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{C}}_{1})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{3V^{\prime}(b-a)}{4K^{2}}+\kappa\sigma_{p}\left({\frac{2K+1}{n}}\right)^{s}.

We obtain (

with

.
Under

- linear interpolation of

\displaystyle\leqslant 3\left({Vw(0)+\frac{c_{\alpha}(A)V}{K^{\beta}}}\right)+\kappa\sigma_{p}\left({\frac{4K+1}{n}}\right)^{s}.

4.3.
Monotone single index model

In this section, we consider the class
{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{S}}
of functions

on
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathfrak{m}}}
,
{\mathfrak{m}}\geqslant 1
, of the form

where
\phi
is monotone on
{\mathbb{R}}
and
\theta
an element of the unit sphere
{\mathscr{S}}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathfrak{m}}}
. The function
\phi
associated with

will be called
a link function
and we set
\phi(-\infty)=\lim_{z\to-\infty}\phi(z)
and
\phi(+\infty)=\lim_{z\to+\infty}\phi(z)
.

Proposition 8
.

If
\overline{f}=\overline{\phi}(\langle\overline{\theta},\cdot\rangle)
is an element of
{\mathcal{S}}
with a link function
\overline{\phi}
which is
K
-piecewise constant on
{\mathbb{R}}
, then
\overline{f}
is extremal in
{\mathcal{S}}
with degree not larger than
({\mathfrak{m}}+1)K
.

We denote by
{\mathcal{O}}_{\text{s-pc}}(K)
the elements
\overline{f}\in{\mathcal{S}}
which are of the form described in Proposition
8
. If
f^{\star}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{\text{s-pc}}(K)
, we deduce from (
23
) that

(41)

B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{S}})\leqslant\kappa\inf_{p\in[1,2]}\left[{\sigma_{p}\left({\frac{({\mathfrak{m}}+1)K}{n}}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{p}}}\right].

For more general regression functions
f^{\star}
, the following result holds.

Corollary 5
.

Assume that
\sigma_{p}
is finite for some
p\in[1,2]
and that the regression function
f^{\star}=\phi^{\star}(\langle\theta^{\star},\cdot\rangle)
belongs to
{\mathcal{S}}
with a monotone link function
\phi^{\star}
the variation
V=|\phi^{\star}(+\infty)-\phi^{\star}(-\infty)|
of which is finite on
{\mathbb{R}}
. Then there exists a numerical constant
C>0
such that

(42)

B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{S}})\leqslant C\left[{\sigma_{p}^{\frac{1}{s+1}}\left({\frac{V({\mathfrak{m}}+1)}{n}}\right)^{\frac{s}{s+1}}+\sigma_{p}\left({\frac{{\mathfrak{m}}+1}{n}}\right)^{s}}\right]\quad\text{with $s=1-\frac{1}{p}$.}

In particular, when
p=2
, we get that

B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{S}})\leqslant C\left[{\left({\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}V({\mathfrak{m}}+1)}{n}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}+\sigma_{p}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathfrak{m}}+1}{n}}}\right].

Proof.

Let
z_{i}=\langle\theta^{\star},x_{i,n}\rangle
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Applying Proposition
4
with
k=1
to the function
\phi^{\star}
, which is monotone on
{\mathbb{R}}
, and to the points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
, we obtain that for every
\gamma>0
there exists a function
\overline{\phi}
which is monotone and
K
-piecewise constant on
{\mathbb{R}}
, with
K=\left\lfloor\gamma+1\right\rfloor\leqslant\gamma+1
, such that
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|{\phi^{\star}(z_{i})-\overline{\phi}(z_{i})}\right|\leqslant V/\gamma
. Since the function
\overline{\phi}(\langle\theta^{\star},\cdot\rangle)
belongs to
{\mathcal{O}}_{\text{s-pc}}(K)
, we obtain that

\displaystyle B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{S}})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{3V}{\gamma}+\kappa\sigma_{p}\left({\frac{({\mathfrak{m}}+1)K}{n}}\right)^{s}.

If
V=0
, we take

, hence

, and deduce that
B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{S}})\leqslant\kappa\sigma_{p}[({\mathfrak{m}}+1)/n]^{s}
. Otherwise, we take
\gamma=(V\sigma_{p}^{-1}(n/({\mathfrak{m}}+1))^{s})
and (
42
) follows from the inequalities

\displaystyle B_{n}(f^{\star},{\mathcal{S}})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{3V}{\gamma}+\kappa\sigma_{p}\left({\frac{({\mathfrak{m}}+1)(\gamma+1)}{n}}\right)^{s}\leqslant\frac{3V}{\gamma}+\kappa\sigma_{p}\left({\frac{({\mathfrak{m}}+1)\gamma}{n}}\right)^{s}+\kappa\sigma_{p}\left({\frac{{\mathfrak{m}}+1}{n}}\right)^{s}.

∎

5.
Bounding the degrees of some extremal functions

This section is devoted to the calculation of the degrees of some extremal functions.
Let
I
be a nontrivial interval and
r
a positive integer. We say that
f
is
r
-nondecreasing
on
I
if for any
{\mathbf{v}}=(v_{0},\ldots,v_{r})\in I^{r+1}
with
v_{0}<\ldots<v_{r}

(43)

L_{{\mathbf{v}}}(f)=\left|\begin{array}[]{ccccc}1&v_{0}&\ldots&v_{0}^{r-1}&f(v_{0})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ldots&\vdots&\vdots\\
1&v_{r}&\ldots&v_{r}^{r-1}&{f(v_{r})}\\
\end{array}\right|\geqslant 0.

When
r=1
,
f
is
r
-nondecreasing if for any points
v_{0}<v_{1}
in
I
,

L_{{\mathbf{v}}}(f)=\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}1&f(v_{0})\\
1&f(v_{1})\\
\end{array}\right|=f(v_{1})-f(v_{0})\geqslant 0

which simply means that
f
is nondecreasing on
I
. A function
f
is
2
-nondecreasing if for any points
v_{0}<v_{1}<v_{2}
in
I
,

(44)

L_{{\mathbf{v}}}(f)=\left|\begin{array}[]{ccccc}1&v_{0}&f(v_{0})\\
1&v_{1}&f(v_{1})\\
1&v_{2}&{f(v_{2})}\\
\end{array}\right|=\left({v_{2}-v_{0}}\right)\left[{\frac{v_{2}-v_{1}}{v_{2}-v_{0}}f(v_{0})+\frac{v_{1}-v_{0}}{v_{2}-v_{0}}f(v_{2})-f(v_{1})}\right]\geqslant 0,

which means that
f
is convex on
I
. Similarly, we shall say that
f
is
r
-nonincreasing
on
I
if
-f
is
r
-nondecreasing or equivalently if for any
{\mathbf{v}}=(v_{0},\ldots,v_{r})\in I^{r+1}
with
v_{0}<\ldots<v_{r}

(45)

L_{{\mathbf{v}}}(f)=\left|\begin{array}[]{ccccc}1&v_{0}&\ldots&v_{0}^{r-1}&f(v_{0})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ldots&\vdots&\vdots\\
1&v_{r}&\ldots&v_{r}^{r-1}&{f(v_{r})}\\
\end{array}\right|\leqslant 0.

A function
f
is
r
-monotone
on
I
if it is either
r
-nonincreasing or
r
-nondecreasing on this interval. We denote by
{\mathcal{F}}_{r}(I)
the class of
r
-monotone functions on
I
. The set
{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(I)
is therefore the set of monotone functions on
I
while
{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(I)
is that of functions which are convex-concave on
I
. If
\overline{f}
is a polynomial function on
I
with degree not larger than
r-1
, then for any
{\mathbf{v}}=(v_{0},\ldots,v_{r})\in I^{r+1}
,

L_{{\mathbf{v}}}(\overline{f})=\left|\begin{array}[]{ccccc}1&v_{0}&\ldots&v_{0}^{r-1}&\overline{f}(v_{0})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ldots&\vdots&\vdots\\
1&v_{r}&\ldots&v_{r}^{r-1}&{\overline{f}(v_{r})}\\
\end{array}\right|=0

and
\overline{f}
is therefore both
r
-nonincreasing and
r
-nondecreasing. Since
L_{{\mathbf{v}}}(f)=L_{{\mathbf{v}}}(f-\overline{f})
for every function
f
on
I
and
{\mathbf{v}}\in I^{r+1}
, when
f
is
r
-nondecreasing (respectively
r
-nonincreasing) so is
f-\overline{f}
.

Given a nontrivial interval
{\mathcal{X}}
and two positive integers
r,k
, we denote by
{\mathcal{F}}_{r,k}
the class of functions
f
on
{\mathcal{X}}
which are
r
-monotone on each element
I
of a partition of
{\mathcal{X}}
into at most
k
nontrivial intervals. The aim of this section is to prove the following result.

Theorem 3
.

Let
r
be a positive integer,
{\mathcal{F}}
a subset of
{\mathcal{F}}_{r,k}
and
\overline{f}\in{\mathcal{F}}
a piecewise polynomial with degree at most
r-1
based on a partition of
{\mathcal{X}}
into at most
K\geqslant 1
nontrivial intervals. Then
\overline{f}
is extremal in
{\mathcal{F}}
with degree at most
(r+1)k+r(K-1)
.

Let us now turn to the proof of this result.
Hereafter, we write
i\equiv j
when the integers
i,j
share the same parity and
i\not\equiv j
otherwise.

Lemma 2
.

Let
r
be a positive integer and
v_{0}<\ldots<v_{r}
be
r+1
points in a nontrivial interval
I
. There exists no
r
-nondecreasing function
f
on
I
that satisfies

(46)

f(v_{i})>0\quad\text{for $i\equiv(r+1)$ while for $i\not\equiv(r+1)$}\quad f(v_{i})\leqslant 0

and there exists no
r
-nonincreasing function
f
on
I
that satisfies

(47)

f(v_{i})\leqslant 0\quad\text{for $i\equiv(r+1)$ while for $i\not\equiv(r+1)$}\quad f(v_{i})>0.

Proof.

The result is a consequence of the fact that

\displaystyle L_{{\mathbf{v}}}(f)

\displaystyle=\left|\begin{array}[]{ccccc}1&v_{0}&\ldots&v_{0}^{r-1}&f(v_{0})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ldots&\vdots&\vdots\\
1&v_{r}&\ldots&v_{r}^{r-1}&f(v_{r})\\
\end{array}\right|=\sum_{i=0}^{r}(-1)^{r-i}V_{i}f(v_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{r}V_{i}f(v_{i})1\hskip-2.6pt{\rm l}_{i\not\equiv r+1}-\sum_{i=0}^{r}V_{i}f(v_{i})1\hskip-2.6pt{\rm l}_{i\equiv r+1}

where
V_{0},\ldots,V_{r}
are positive Vandermonde determinants.
∎

Note that it is sufficient to prove the Theorem
3
for the larger class
{\mathcal{F}}_{r,k}
, which we shall do hereafter by taking
{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{F}}_{r,k}
. Let
n>r(k+K-1)+k
and
{\mathcal{Z}}=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}
be an arbitrary subset of
{\mathcal{X}}
with cardinality
n
. We shall prove that there exists a subset
C
of
{\mathcal{Z}}
such that

(48)

C\neq\{f-\overline{f}>0\}\cap{\mathcal{Z}}\quad\text{for every $f\in{\mathcal{F}}$}.

This would imply that the VC-dimension of
\{\{f>\overline{f}\},\;f\in{\mathcal{F}}\}
is not larger than
r(k+K-1)+k
.

Using the fact that
{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{F}}_{r,k}
is stable with respect to the transformation
f\mapsto-f
and applying this result to the function
-\overline{f}
, which satisfies the same assumptions as
\overline{f}
, we obtain that the VC-dimension
\{\{f<\overline{f}\},\;f\in{\mathcal{F}}\}
is not larger than
r(k+K-1)+k
as well, hence that
\overline{f}
is extremal in
{\mathcal{F}}
with degree not larger than
r(k+K-1)+k
.

With no loss of generality we may assume that
z_{1}<\ldots<z_{n}
. Given an interval
I\subset{\mathcal{X}}
, we denote by
N(I)
the number of these
z_{i}
that belong to
I
.

Let
{\mathcal{I}}
denote the partition of
{\mathcal{X}}
with cardinality not larger than
K\geqslant 1
for which the restriction of
\overline{f}
to each interval
I\in{\mathcal{I}}
is a polynomial with degree not larger than
r-1
. Since
n\geqslant rK+r(k-1)+k\geqslant rK+1
, there exists at least one interval
I\in{\mathcal{I}}
which contains at least
r+1
points. Let
I_{1},\ldots,I_{L}
with
L\geqslant 1
be the collection of these intervals which contain at least
r+1
points. When
L\geqslant 2
, we assume that the labelling of these sets is such that the right endpoint of
I_{l}
is not larger than the left endpoint of
I_{l+1}
for
l\in\{1,\ldots,L-1\}
. Denoting by
{\mathcal{Z}}(\overline{f})
the set
\bigcup_{l=1}^{L}\left({{\mathcal{Z}}\cap I_{l}}\right)
,
we associate with the points
z\in{\mathcal{Z}}(\overline{f})
a sign
{\varepsilon}(z)\in\{-1,1\}
which is defined as follows. Firstly, if
z=z_{i}\in I_{1}
then
{\varepsilon}(z_{i})=(-1)^{i}
. Then, if
L\geqslant 2
and

we define iteratively the signs of the points

in
I_{l}
by the formula
{\varepsilon}(z_{i+1})=-{\varepsilon}(z_{i})
where the sign of the smallest point
z_{i}
in
I_{l}
is
(-1)^{r+1}
times that of the largest point
z_{i}
in
I_{l-1}
. This means that the sequence of signs associated with the elements
z_{i}
in

starts with the same sign as that of last point
z_{i}
in
I_{l-1}
when

is even. Then the signs alternate along the sequence of
z_{i}
for those

. For example, when

,
N(I_{1})=4
,
N(I_{2})=N(I_{3})=3
and the index

of the smallest point

in
I_{1}
is even, we obtain that the sequences of signs that are associated with the points
z_{i}
in
I_{1},I_{2}
and

are respectively given by
(+1,-1,+1,-1),(-(-1)^{r+1},(-1)^{r+1},-(-1)^{r+1}),(-1,1,-1)
. Finally, we define our set

as

C=\left\{{z\in{\mathcal{Z}}(\overline{f}),\;{\varepsilon}(z)=1}\right\}.

Let us assume by contradiction that there exists an element
f
in
{\mathcal{F}}
for which equality holds in (
48
) that is

(49)

z\in{\mathcal{Z}}(\overline{f})\quad\text{and}\quad{\varepsilon}(z)=+1\iff z\in{\mathcal{Z}}(\overline{f})\quad\text{and}\quad(f-\overline{f})(z)>0.

The function
f
is associated with the partition
{\mathcal{J}}
of
{\mathcal{X}}
into at most
k
nontrivial intervals. The class of sets
\{I\cap J\neq{\varnothing},(I,J)\in{\mathcal{I}}\times{\mathcal{J}}\}
is therefore a partition of
{\mathcal{X}}
into at most

J\in{\mathcal{J}}
, let us denote by

the number of intervals

such that

I\cap J\neq{\varnothing}
. Since
\sum_{J\in{\mathcal{J}}}D_{J}\leqslant k+K-1
and

\sum_{J\in{\mathcal{J}}}\left[{N(J)-\left({rD_{J}+1)}\right)}\right]\geqslant n-r\left({k+K-1}\right)-k>0,

there exists at least one interval

such that

. Two situations may then occur

(a)

there exists an interval

with

such that

(b)

there exist two intervals

We shall prove that these two situations lead to a contradiction. Hereafter, we shall repeatedly use the following Property (P) that derives from (

Property (P)
: Let

, with

and

, be an interval which contains

. If

when

.

As a consequence, it is impossible that

, the Property (P) would be violated either by the sequence of

Let us now turn to the case

. If there exists

, then

. We note that

in

while

are the

smallest elements
z_{i}

in
I_{l+1}
. It follows from our construction that
{\varepsilon}(z_{m^{\prime}})=(-1)^{r+1}{\varepsilon}(z_{m+r})
. If
f
is
r
-nondecreasing on

Lemma 7

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|{f^{\prime}_{l}(b)-f^{\prime}_{r}(a)}\right|(b-a)}{4}\mu((a,b));

\displaystyle R_{2}
