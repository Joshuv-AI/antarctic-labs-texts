Exploring the mechanism of sunflower seed oil against Alzheimer’s disease through experimental and network pharmacology studies
Authors: Yashodhan Desai, Deepak Karunakaran, Juhi Singh, Alric Raul Noronha, Gagan Poojary, Bijaya Chettri, Rekha Shenoy, Madhavan Nampoothiri, Sree Lalitha Bojja
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVES:With the prevalence of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) increasing exponentially, there has been a shift in the focus of drug discovery for AD from treating the symptoms to preventing the development of the disease. Several natural compounds are extensively studied as neuroprotectives in preventing disease progression. Helianthus annuus seed oil (HA) is widely used as cooking oil and is abundant in antioxidant activity. Therefore, we evaluated the effect of HA in mice model of scopolamine-induced amnesia and explored the potential underlying mechanisms.METHODS:Twenty-four male mice were administered orally with either distilled water (control and scopolamine groups) or treatment groups (HA 100 and HA 200 mg/kg) for 8 consecutive days. All groups, except the control group, received an intraperitoneal injection of scopolamine at a dose of 1 mg/kg. Subsequently, novel object recognition task for cognition assessment and open field tests for locomotory activity were performed. In addition, network analysis was performed to identify the key bioactives and targets of HA against AD. Further, the binding affinity of HA bioactives to the key targets was verified by molecular docking analysis.RESULTS:HA (100 mg/kg and 200 mg/kg) significantly ameliorated recognition memory compared to the scopolamine group, suggesting the protective effect of HA against cognitive impairment. Network analysis indicated that the key bioactives of HA, chlorogenic acid, and oleic acid act through multiple targets and pathways, particularly the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, to ameliorate AD symptoms. Importantly, chlorogenic acid showed good binding affinity with MAPKs, TP53, and EP300.CONCLUSION:HA has therapeutic benefits in AD acting through the MAPK pathway. However, further studies need to be done to confirm the results derived and translate the potential use of HA as a dietary supplement for preventing AD.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia, accounting for 60%–80% of all dementia cases, globally. This chronic neurodegenerative condition is marked by the accummulation of amyloid-β fibrils and neurofibrillary tangles of hyperphosphorylated tau protein, resulting in cognitive impairment and other neuropsychiatric symptoms.[1] Meanwhile, the drug development for AD is remarkably slow entailing huge socioeconomic considerations. Since the AD symptoms are produced way longer (often 20–30 years) after the neuronal damage is initiated, early AD prevention appears to be a rational strategy rather than the current approach to treating the symptoms.[2] Recent studies highlighted the significance of multidomain lifestyle modification including exercise, diet, and cognitive stimulation for AD prevention.[3] Among these, diet is one of the promising nonpharmacological interventions through which not only the biochemical processes underlying AD development are slowed/prevented but also the AD risk factors such as aging, obesity, hypertension, and insulin resistance can be tackled.[45]

Helianthus annuus (HA) L.(sunflower) seeds are rich in nutrients, particularly polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) (linoleic acid), monounsaturated fatty acid, Vitamin E, phenolic acids (caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid, sinapic acid, ferulic acid, and gallic acid), flavonoids (quercetin and isoflavones), and trace elements, all of which with high antioxidant properties.[67] Antioxidants can combat free radical damage, which is significantly implicated in the progression of neurodegenerative disorders.[8] Existing reports indicate that PUFA particularly, omega 3 fatty acids, improves cognition by attenuating oxidative stress, apoptosis, and inflammation.[9] Studies also report the neuroprotective potential of chlorogenic acid particularly in AD, by preventing the synthesis of malondialdehyde, and acetylcholinesterase inhibition.[10] Most importantly, Vitamin E provides a variety of neuroprotective actions by ameliorating mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, apoptosis, inflammation, protein misfolding, and neurogenesis.[11] Therefore, sunflower seed oil appears as a promising dietary supplement to prevent AD.

Hence, the current study assessed the effect of HA seed oil in scopolamine-induced amnesia model of mice and explored the possible underlying mechanisms through network pharmacology and computational simulation of molecule interactions.

Materials and Methods

Experimental design

Twenty-four male Swiss Albino mice (around 25 g) were obtained from the Central Animal Research Facility at Manipal Academy of Higher Education, Manipal. Mice were kept in standard conditions 23°C ± 2°C, relative humidity of 50 ± 5%, and 12 h light-dark cycle. After a week of acclimatization, mice were randomly grouped into four groups (n = 6): Control, scopolamine, HA 100, and HA 200. The control and scopolamine groups were given water orally for 8 days, whereas the HA 100 and HA 200 groups were given sunflower seed oil orally (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) in doses of 100 mg/kg and 200 mg/kg, respectively, for 8 days. On the 8th day, all groups except the control were given an intraperitoneal injection of scopolamine (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) at a dosage of 1 mg/kg, followed by behavioral evaluations.

The Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC) reviewed and approved the study protocol with the reference number IAEC/KMC/19/2023. All the experiments were carried in accordance with Committee for Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals guidelines.

Object recognition test for memory

The novel object recognition test (NORT) is a commonly used behavioral test for assessing learning and memory in animal model. In this test, each animal was initially given the opportunity to examine two similar objects in a habituated open box as described previously.[12] Three hours following this familiarization, all groups except the control received scopolamine, and a retention test was carried out for 10 min. During the retention test, the animals were exposed to the testing environment, with one of the familiar objects replaced with a new object, and the amount of time spent investigating the novel object compared to the familiar object was recorded. Memory performance was evaluated by computing the discrimination index (DI), which is defined as the proportion of time spent examining the novel item versus the familiar object.[13]

Test for locomotor activity

Locomotor activity was monitored using an actophotometer. Actophotometer consists of an enclosed chamber with photocells, a light source, and a digital counter which provides the measure of horizontal movement. During the experiment, each mouse was placed in actophotometer for 10 min, and the number of beam breaks provided by the digital counter was recorded.[14]

Construction of phytocompound library

The phytoconstituents of HA were obtained from Dr. Duke’s Phytochemical and Ethnobotanical Databases (USDA) and Indian Medicinal Plants, Phytochemistry and Therapeutics 2.0 (IMPPAT).[15] The bioactives of HA were screened based on several ADMET properties using the SwissADME database (http://www.swissadme.ch/).[16] Bioactives with oral bioavailability ≥30%, drug-likeness, blood–brain barrier permeability, and Caco-2 permeability (Caco-2) were obtained from SwissADME.

Collection of bioactive-related targets and Alzheimer’s disease-related targets

Using the SWISS Target Prediction (STP) database, the target genes that were related to the screened HA bioactives were found.[17] Similarly, the disease genes associated with AD were acquired from DisGeNET, a database containing disease-gene associations (http://www.disgenet.org/).[18] Thereafter, the common genes between AD and HA bioactives were obtained by overlapping them using Venny 2.1 tool.

Gene ontology and pathway enrichment analysis

Using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID),[19] pathways of the HA against AD were studied. The common genes between HA and AD were subjected to DAVID analysis (search limited to Homo Sapiens) and the pathway clustering was performed.

Protein–protein interaction analysis

The common genes between HA and AD were entered in the STRING protein query of Cytoscape and the analysis was limited to Homo sapiens, maximum number of interactors to 10 and confidence limit >0.7.[20] The obtained protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was analyzed using CytoHbba plug-in application. Among the several topological analysis methods that CytoHubba employs, maximal clique centrality (MCC) effectively identifies hub proteins, which are critical nodes in the network.[21]

Molecular docking

Studies of the molecular docking were done using Schrödinger Maestro, following the methodology detailed in our previous work.[22] Key target protein crystal structures were taken from the Protein Data Bank. The preparation of the protein was done using the Protein Preparation Wizard, during which hydrogen bonds were optimized to generate a low-energy protein conformation at physiological pH (7.4), utilizing the OPLS3e force field. Similarly, the 3D structures of bioactive compounds were acquired from the PubChem database and processed using the LigPrep wizard. The Epik tool was employed to predict the possible ionization states of the ligands at pH 7.4. Rigid and extraprecision docking was done using the Glide module in Schrödinger Maestro.[23] Docking scores were recorded, and two-dimensional representations of ligand-protein interactions, particularly hydrogen bonds with key amino acid residues, were analyzed. In addition, the binding energy of protein-ligand complexes was computed with the help of Prime MM-GBSA method.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of the data was conducted using GraphPad Software, (Boston, Massachusetts, USA).

Results

Effect of Helianthus annuus on recognition memory

Scopolamine-administered mice exhibited significantly (P < 0.001) lesser DI as compared to control. These mice failed to recall the familiar object and showed less preference for the novel object, indicating cognitive decline. Whereas the treatment groups, HA 100 and HA 200 exhibited a pronounced increase (P < 0.001) in DI when compared to the scopolamine group. HA-treated mice could recognize the novel object and spent more time with it, as reflected by the positive change in the mean DI, indicating improvement in short-term memory retention. Notably, a dose-dependent effect was observed [Figure 1a].

Effect of Helianthus annuus on locomotor activity

A number of photo beam counts were taken as a measure of locomotor activity. No significant difference in the beam breaks was observed between the scopolamine and HA-treated mice indicating that locomotor activity was not influenced by HA treatment [Figure 1b].

Potential anti-Alzheimer’s disease targets of Helianthus annuus

Out of 47 phytoconstituents obtained from the USDA database, only 15 had bioactivity, better druggability, and bioavailability scores. Target genes for each of the 15 bioactives were collected from STP. 3397 genes associated with AD were extracted from the Disgenet database. 272 common genes were found between the genes related to HA bioactives and the AD-related genes [Figure 2]. Further, a HA-AD compound target network using 15 bioactives and 272 common genes was built using Cytoscape, which contained 290 nodes and 915 edges [Figure 3]. Network analysis revealed that chlorogenic acid followed by oleic, caffeic, and linoleic acids had a higher degree compared to the other bioactives, indicating that these bioactives have a higher number of targets for AD. Similarly, PTPN1, HSD11B1, PPARA, CYP19A1, POLB, and CA2 were identified to be the AD genes associated with a greater number of bioactives [Figure 3].

Pathway enrichment and gene ontology analysis

DAVID Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted to identify the functional pathways enriched in the gene list (272 common genes). Among the total 172 functional pathways derived, top 10 significant pathways are represented in Figure 4a. Metabolic pathways, pathways of neurodegeneration-multiple diseases, AD, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway, signaling pathway of P13K-Akt, and neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction represented the major pathways involved in the neuroprotective effects of HA against AD.

Most of the anti-AD targets of HA were involved in various biological processes such as inflammatory responses, proteolysis, and processes related to both positive and negative regulation of gene expression [Figure 4b]. In addition, they were mainly enriched in the plasma membrane [Figure 4b]. With respect to the molecular function, these genes were mainly associated with binding of enzyme, protein kinase activity (serine/threonine/tyrosine), and binding of zinc [Figure 4b].

Identification of hub proteins

PPI network of 272 nodes and 1270 edges was generated in the String app of Cytsocape [Figure 5a]. CytoHubba analysis employing MCC algorithm ranked the targets according to the scores. The top 10 hub genes consisted of NR3C1, nuclear receptor subfamily 3, group C, member 1; RELA, Nuclear factor of kappa light polypeptide gene enhancer in B cells; MAPKs, MAPK1, MAPK3, MAPK8, MAPK14; EP300, E1A binding protein p300; STAT3, Signal transducer and activator of transcription 3; TP53, tumor protein p53, and ESR1, Estrogen Receptor Alpha Gene [Figure 5b].

Discussion and Conclusion

AD is a complex multifactorial disease involving several pathological processes that result in the progress of the disease. Consequently, relying on the traditional, one-drug-one target approach is deemed futile. Instead, polypharmacology emerges as a rational drug design strategy, where drugs that act on multiple distinct targets within the critical network responsible for the disease pathology are explored.[24] In this context, nutraceuticals are gaining popularity due to their multiple phytoconstituents that can target various pathways. Furthermore, they hold the potential to facilitate the development of disease-modifying therapies with fewer side effects.

Sunflower seed oil is a commonly used cooking oil with several compounds of antioxidant properties such as PUFA, Vitamin E, polyphenols, and cysteine.[6] These compounds are suggested to promote brain development and provide neuroprotection in several neurological conditions. Therefore, the current study attempted to investigate the effect of HA seed oil on cognitive impairment as observed in AD. Further, we have explored the putative mechanisms and targets involved in its beneficial effect.

NORT is widely used to evaluate cognition, in particular, recognition memory. Varying the time interval between the familiarization test and retention trial allows to assess short-term, intermediate, and long-term memory with the same model.[25] Intraperitoneal administration of scopolamine (1 mg/kg) resulted in short-term memory impairment as evident by the decreased DI. However, oral administration of HA seed oil (100 or 200 mg/kg) for 8 days improved the recognition memory, ameliorating the decreased DI induced by scopolamine. In addition, lack of significant difference in locomotory ability between the groups support that the NORT results were not influenced due to any alteration in motor activity. These results support the protective effect of HA seed oil in scopolamine-induced amnesia model. However, the effect of HA on AD needs to be substantiated using additional animal models.

HA-AD compound-target network revealed that chlorogenic acid followed by oleic, caffeic, and linoleic acids target a greater number of genes implicated in AD. Chlorogenic acid has gained considerable attention for its wide range of neuroprotective effects primarily attributed to its antioxidant action.[26] It is reported to improve attention and executive function in patients diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment.[27] Further, oleic acid serves an important role in brain development as a neurotrophic factor; however, its levels are declined in AD and depressive patients. Oleic acid supplementation is shown to prevent amyloidosis and alleviate toxicity in AD animal models.[28] Therefore, the presence of such bioactives with neuroprotective properties in HA can confer beneficial action in AD.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis suggested that the targets of HA seed oil are involved in multiple pathways majorly metabolic pathways, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, cAMP signaling, lipid and atherosclerosis, and apoptosis pathways. Deregulated metabolic pathways such as insulin resistance, dysregulated lipid metabolism, altered amino acid metabolism are studied to be significantly associated with AD.[29] Meanwhile, HA through its multiple bioactives such as PUFA, linoleic acid, and oleic acid modulates these metabolic pathways and might render a neuroprotective effect. Further, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway has a pivotal role in neuron survival, proliferation and differentiation, autophagy, neurogenesis, and synaptic plasticity. Besides, it is involved in ameliorating neurotoxicity and neuronal survival.[30] Thus, HA bioactives such as polyphenols could activate PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and combat the neurotoxicity observed in AD. MAPK signaling pathway (ERK, KNK, and p38) is implicated in AD for their role in inflammation as well as upregulating the phosphorylation of APP and Tau and thereby neuronal apoptosis.[31]

PPI network analysis of HA–AD targets revealed that MAPK1, MAPK3, MAPK8, MAPK14, NR3C1, RELA, EP300, STAT3, TP53, and ESR1 could be crucial in the action of HA against AD. As MAPK was derived as a crucial gene as well as a key signaling pathway in our results, it is worthwhile to consider that HA significantly acts through MAPK modulation. MAPK proteins get activated by diverse stimuli such as oxidative stress, inflammation, and Aβ aggregation and mediate neuronal toxicity in AD.[32] Moreover, bioinformatic analysis also revealed that MAPK8 (JNK1) significantly increased in AD patients compared to control and displayed a significant positive correlation with Aβ and Tau pathology.[33] Meanwhile, chlorogenic acid showed a good binding affinity with MAPK8 (−10.02), MAPK1 (−8), MAPK3 (−8), and MAPK 14 (−6) targets, indicating a potential association of chlorogenic acid with MAPK. Mounting evidence suggests that chlorogenic acid suppresses several inflammatory pathways including p38, MAPK, and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB).[34]

In addition, chlorogenic acid, the important bioactive of HA has also shown good binding affinity with all other crucial targets, NR3C1, TP53, RELA, STAT3, and EP300. NR3C1 has a defensive role during stress responses and is found to be upregulated in AD.[35] TP53 is a powerful stress surveyor that acts as a hub for several critical functions such as cell cycle regulation, DNA damage repair, and apoptosis. Recent reports suggest that p53 aggregation directly mediates AD pathology by impairing the DNA damage repair pathway and promoting seeding and cross-seeding of other misfolded proteins.[36] RELA coding for p65 subunit of NF-κB is shown to induce BACE1 expression, causing an increase in β-secretase, resulting in the formation of amyloid fibrils. Further, Aβ is also shown to promote RELA expression in neurons and glia promoting inflammation and neurodegeneration.[37] EP300, a histone acetyltransferase, has an important role in regulating the transcription of genes, presenilin-1 and BACE-1, which are implicated in AD, by controlling their promoter acetylation.[38] Activated forms of STAT3 are associated with astrogliosis and microgliosis, causing neuroinflammation in animal models of AD.[39] Chlorogenic acid and oleic acid also showed better binding affinity with STAT3. Altogether, the results indicate the neuroprotective effect of HA in AD by targeting multiple crucial genes and pathways, notably, MAPK pathway. However, further studies are warranted to validate these in silico findings.
