Use of Inpatient Rehabilitation Facilities Among Traditional Medicare Beneficiaries, 2019-2024
Authors: Kathryn E. Linehan, Minhhy Truong, Melinda B. Buntin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study examines the variation in changes in inpatient rehabilitation facility use per traditional Medicare beneficiary and inpatient rehabilitation facility supply at the state level between 2019 and 2024.

Introduction

Inpatient rehabilitation facilities (IRFs) are specialized hospitals or units that provide intensive, multidisciplinary therapy and rehabilitation services. Traditional Medicare (TM) qualifying criteria for coverage require beneficiaries to tolerate and benefit from 3 hours of therapy per day. TM beneficiaries accounted for 51% of discharges in 2024.1 The mean Medicare payment for an IRF stay was about $24 000 in 2024. One way that Medicare limits use of this comparatively costly setting is by requiring that at least 60% of an IRF’s total patient population has 1 of 13 qualifying conditions as a primary diagnosis or comorbidity.

Nationally, IRF use per TM beneficiary has increased markedly in recent years.1 This cross-sectional study examines the variation in changes in IRF use per TM beneficiary and IRF supply at the state level between 2019 and 2024 that underlies national trends.

Methods

We calculated IRF stays per TM beneficiary in 2019 and 2024, and the change between those years, for 50 states and the District of Columbia using state-level data on the number of TM-covered IRF stays per facility from the IRF Rate Setting Final Rule file released each year. The number of TM beneficiaries by state and year were from the Medicare monthly enrollment files. We used the fourth quarter 2019 and 2024 Medicare Provider of Services file to count the supply of IRFs. This study conforms to the STROBE reporting guideline for cross-sectional studies. Because this study used publicly available data and did not involve human participants, institutional review board approval and informed consent were not sought, in accordance with 45 CFR §46. Analysis was performed using SAS, version 9.4 for Windows and Microsoft Excel 365 MSO, version 2605. All tests were 2-sided, with α = .001.

Results

Between 2019 and 2024, IRF stays increased nationally from 10.8 to 13.4 per 1000 TM beneficiaries. Forty-one states and the District of Columbia showed an increase in IRF use per beneficiary; the magnitude of the increase varied. Nine states had lower IRF use. Figure 1 arrays states from the highest use per beneficiary in 2019 to the lowest. It shows that, while a few low-use states, notably North Dakota and South Dakota, saw large increases in IRF use per beneficiary, larger increases between 2019 and 2024 were concentrated in states that already had relatively high use in 2019.

Plot Showing Inpatient Rehabilitation Facility (IRF) Stays Per 1000 Traditional Medicare (TM) Beneficiaries by State in 2019 and 2024

In 2019, national supply totaled 1152 IRFs and approximately 40 000 IRF beds. By 2024, the supply of IRFs increased by 5%, and the supply of beds increased by 8%. Like IRF use per beneficiary, the supply of IRF beds per beneficiary and the increase in beds per beneficiary were uneven across states. Figure 2 shows that states with expanded IRF bed supply per beneficiary had greater increases in stays per beneficiary (β = 0.90; 95% CI, 0.55-1.26; P < .001). With the exception of North Dakota, an expanded bed supply per beneficiary in 2024 occurred in states where supply was already relatively high in 2019.

Discussion

After recent increases in IRF use and supply, previously documented geographic variation in IRF use persists.2,3 State-level variation in IRF use and growth per TM beneficiary between 2019 and 2024 warrants further examination to understand the factors associated with the variation, including supply-induced demand and patient need or preference for IRF care.

We found an association of increased IRF supply and increased use with the supply of postacute care facilities, including IRFs; this is consistent with prior work that has shown IRF supply is associated with use.4 The increase in IRF supply over the study period is striking given the contemporaneous increase in Medicare Advantage enrollment. Medicare Advantage beneficiaries have been shown to use less IRF care than TM beneficiaries, so IRFs may be competing to serve a smaller population of beneficiaries.5,6 At 71% nationally,1 IRF occupancy rates do not suggest IRF supply is constrained, although IRF occupancy rates may vary by market and IRF use may substitute for other types of postacute care. Where state regulation permits, profitability may induce market entry, given that IRFs are among the most profitable sectors under TM payment rates.1

Factors associated with demand could also be associated with increased IRF use among Medicare beneficiaries. Lack of evidence of changes in clinical need is a limitation of this study. IRF use varies by demographic characteristics,7 so changes to the TM population may have been associated with increased IRF use among this population. Increases in the volume of stroke, traumatic brain injury, spinal cord injury, and other neurologic conditions that benefit from treatment in IRFs could also increase IRF use.7,8 Medicare alternative payment models may also be associated with changing demand for IRF use.9,10 Finally, beneficiary or clinician preferences for IRFs vs alternatives such as skilled nursing facility care could also be associated with increased rates of use.

The associations of some of these supply and demand factors are harder to quantify than others. Future work distinguishing between supply-induced demand and increased need for IRF care among TM beneficiaries would help policymakers determine whether stronger management of IRF use is warranted.
