Confined but chirally and chiral spin symmetric hot matter
L. Ya. Glozman, A. V. Nefediev, R. F. Wagenbrunn

Abstract
We investigate properties of the quark–antiquark mesons at zero and finite temperature in the framework of a solvable chirally symmetric quark model. The interquark linearly rising interaction is reminiscent of that derived in Coulomb gauge QCD, with the string tension being the only model parameter. We demonstrate that while the confining interaction induces spontaneous breaking of chiral symmetry at T = 0 T=0 , it gets restored at a temperature T ch ≃ 90 T_{\rm ch}\simeq 90 MeV for the string tension fixed to provide the phenomenological value of the quark condensate. This temperature is similar to T ch ≃ 130 T_{\rm ch}\simeq 130 MeV observed on the lattice in the chiral limit for N c = 3 N_{c}=3 . The physical mechanism responsible for chiral symmetry restoration in the confining regime is Pauli blocking of the quark levels, required for the existence of a nonvanishing quark condensate, by thermal excitations of the quarks and antiquarks. Thus, above the chiral restoration temperature, meson-like states are chirally symmetric and approximately chiral spin symmetric. A crucial property of the confined meson-like light-light states above T ch T_{\rm ch} is their size that exceeds drastically that in the chirally broken phase below T ch T_{\rm ch} . Heavy-heavy mesons nearly preserve their size irrespective of the temperature. Furthermore, the root-mean-square radius of the states with J = 0 , 1 J=0,1 diverges in the chiral limit. This unexpected property must be a key to understanding unusual features of the hot QCD matter as observed at RHIC and LHC. Consequently, the confining but chirally symmetric matter above T ch T_{\rm ch} can be considered as a dense system of very large and strongly overlapping meson-like states (“strings”).

Lessons from the Nambu–Jona-Lasinio model

The Nambu–Jona-Lasinio (NJL) model described by the interaction Hamiltonian,

H_{\rm int}^{\rm NJL}=-G\int d^{3}x\left[(\bar{\psi}\psi)^{2}+(\bar{\psi}i\gamma_{5}{\bm{\tau}}\psi)^{2}\right],

(2)

was suggested long ago
[
39
,
40
]
as a dynamical field-theory-inspired quark model for hadrons built in analogy with the theory of superconductivity
[
41
,
42
]
; see also the dedicated review papers
[
43
,
44
,
45
,
46
]
. The model (
2
) is successful in describing the phenomenon of spontaneous breaking of chiral symmetry (SBCS) in the vacuum and is able to explain at a microscopic level the appearance of a gap in the spectrum of excitations provided by the dynamically generated quark mass — here the analogy with the theory of superconductivity becomes particularly evident.
Indeed, in this model, the dynamical mass of the quark is related with the chiral condensate in the vacuum as

M=-2G\braket{\bar{\psi}\psi}_{0},

(3)

with the latter, in turn, evaluated as

\braket{\bar{\psi}\psi}_{0}=-2N_{c}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{M}{\sqrt{p^{2}+M^{2}}},

(4)

where we work at
T=0
and accordingly tag the chiral condensate with the corresponding subscript.
Equations (
3
) and (
4
) taken together result in a simple selfconsistency condition, known as the gap equation, for the effective quark mass
M
. The integral on the right-hand side of Eq. (
4
) diverges quadratically and needs to be regularised, for example, by a sharp cut-off
\Lambda_{\chi}
. Then, for a fixed value of the cut-off
\Lambda_{\chi}
and small
G
, the gap equation possesses only a trivial solution with
M=0
. However, if the strength of the effective interaction
G
exceeds some critical level,

G>\frac{\pi^{2}}{N_{c}\Lambda_{\chi}^{2}},

(5)

a solution with
M\neq 0
emerges that describes a dynamically generated gap in the spectrum.

A finite temperature
T
induces creation of particle-antiparticle excitations from the vacuum distributed in the relative momentum according to the Fermi-Dirac functions,

\displaystyle n_{p}=\left(1+e^{(\sqrt{p^{2}+M^{2}}-\mu)/T}\right)^{-1}\mathop{\to}_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2},

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{p}=\left(1+e^{(\sqrt{p^{2}+M^{2}}+\mu)/T}\right)^{-1}\mathop{\to}_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2},

with
n_{p}
and
\bar{n}_{p}
for the fermion and antifermion, respectively. Then the modified expression (
4
) for the chiral condensate at a finite
T
reads

\braket{\bar{\psi}\psi}=-\frac{N_{c}}{\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\Lambda_{\chi}}{p}^{2}dp\frac{M}{\sqrt{p^{2}+M^{2}}}(1-n_{p}-\bar{n}_{p}),

(7)

where the distribution functions in Eq. (
) effectively damp the interaction in the system, so that, for fixed values of
\Lambda_{\chi}
and
G
, a nontrivial solution to the corresponding gap equation is lost at some critical temperature
T_{\rm ch}
, even if such a solution existed at
T=0
. The physical mechanism behind chiral symmetry restoration is related to the fact that thermal excitations of quarks and antiquarks lead to Pauli blocking of the levels required for the formation of a nonvanishing quark condensate. For the properties of the chiral pion and
\sigma
-meson in the NJL model at finite temperature and baryon density see Refs.
[
47
,
48
]
.

While the NJL model provides some valuable insight into spontaneous breaking of chiral symmetry in QCD it lacks confinement — another aspect of QCD crucial for addressing the properties of excited hadrons that are in the spotlight of the present research. Thus we proceed to a chiral quark model with manifest confinement.

3
Confining and chirally symmetric model in 3+1 dimensions

3.1
Symmetries of the QCD Hamiltonian in the Coulomb gauge

The Hamiltonian of QCD in the Coulomb gauge in the chiral limit can be presented in the form
[
49
]

H_{\rm QCD}=H_{E}+H_{B}+\int d^{3}x\;\psi^{\dagger}({\bm{x}})(-i{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}})\psi({\bm{x}})+H_{T}+H_{C},

(8)

with

H_{T}=-g\int d^{3}x\;\psi^{\dagger}({\bm{x}}){\bm{\alpha}}\cdot{\bm{A}}^{a}({\bm{x}})t^{a}\psi({\bm{x}}),

(9)

H_{C}=\frac{g^{2}}{2}\int d^{3}\;xd^{3}y\;J^{-1}\rho^{a}({\bm{x}})F_{ab}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})J\rho^{b}({\bm{y}}),

(10)

where
J
is the Faddeev-Popov determinant,
\rho^{a}({\bm{x}})
is the colour-charge density of quarks and gluons at the space point
{\bm{x}}
, and
F_{ab}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
is the “Coulombic” kernel. The quark kinetic and transverse (magnetic) part of the interaction of quarks with the gluonic field are chirally symmetric. Meanwhile, the confining “Coulombic” part is invariant under larger symmetry groups:
SU(2)_{CS}
,
SU(2N_{F})
, and
SU(2N_{F})\times SU(2N_{F})

[
10
]
.

3.2
Hamiltonian of the model

The form of the instantaneous confining “Coulombic” term (
10
) suggests that a sensible QCD-inspired confining and chirally symmetric model can be built in the form

\begin{split}H=&\int d^{3}x\;\psi^{\dagger}({\bm{x}},t)\left(-i{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}+\beta m_{q}\right)\psi({\bm{x}},t)\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}x\;d^{3}y\;\rho^{a}({\bm{x}})K_{ab}(|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|)\rho^{b}({\bm{y}}),\end{split}

(11)

which includes the interaction of two quark colour charge densities,

\rho^{a}=\psi^{\dagger}\frac{\lambda^{a}}{2}\psi
, taken at the spatial points
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}
, via an instantaneous confining kernel,

K_{ab}(|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|)=\delta_{ab}V_{0}(|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|).

(12)

This quark model has a rich history and has been widely discussed in the literature — see, for example, Refs.
[

A standard approach to solving the model implies a rainbow approximation for the dressed quark Green’s function and a ladder approximation for the quark-antiquark Bethe–Salpeter equation. Such approximations are well justified in the large-
N_{c}
limit and can be treated in the spirit of the ’t Hooft model for QCD in 1+1 dimensions

We notice that qualitative conclusions deduced using the model (

3.3
Spontaneous breaking of chiral symmetry at
T=0

For a confining interquark interaction, the trivial chirally-symmetric vacuum is unstable
[
17
,
19
,
20
,
22
]
. This instability can be studied with the help of the Bogoliubov-Valatin transformation of the quark field,

\begin{split}\psi({\bm{x}},t)=\sum_{s=\uparrow,\downarrow}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}&e^{i{\bm{p}}{\bm{x}}}\Bigl[e^{-iE_{p}t}b_{s}({\bm{p}})u_{s}({\bm{p}})\\
&+e^{iE_{p}t}d_{s}^{\dagger}(-{\bm{p}})v_{s}(-{\bm{p}})\Bigr],\end{split}

(14)

with
E_{p}
for the dispersion law of the dressed fermions parameterised in terms of a function of the 3-momentum
\varphi(p)\equiv\varphi_{p}
known as the chiral angle,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}u({\bm{p}})&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\sqrt{1+\sin\varphi_{p}}+\sqrt{1-\sin\varphi_{p}}\;({\bm{\alpha}}\hat{{\bm{p}}})\right]u(0)\\
v(-{\bm{p}})&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\sqrt{1+\sin\varphi_{p}}-\sqrt{1-\sin\varphi_{p}}\;({\bm{\alpha}}\hat{{\bm{p}}})\right]v(0).\end{array}\right.

(15)

By convention, the chiral angle varies in the range

-\frac{\pi}{2}<\varphi_{p}\leqslant\frac{\pi}{2}
with the boundary
conditions
\varphi(0)=\frac{\pi}{2}
,
\varphi(p\to\infty)\to 0
.
The profile of the chiral angle is determined from a nonlinear integral mass-gap equation. Below we briefly outline its derivation. We start from the bare fermion propagator,

S_{0}^{-1}(p_{0},{\bm{p}})=\gamma_{0}p_{0}-{\bm{\gamma}}{\bm{p}}-m_{q},

(16)

and dress it by summing the Dyson series of planar rainbow diagrams as depicted in Fig.
1
,

S=S_{0}+S_{0}\Sigma S_{0}+S_{0}\Sigma S_{0}\Sigma S_{0}+\ldots=S_{0}+S_{0}\Sigma S,

(17)

that gives for the dressed quark propagator

S^{-1}(p_{0},{\bm{p}})=S_{0}^{-1}(p_{0},{\bm{p}})-\Sigma({\bm{p}}),

(18)

where the self-energy operator is

i\Sigma({\bm{p}})=\int\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}V({\bm{p}}-{\bm{k}})\gamma_{0}S(k_{0},{{\bm{k}}})\gamma_{0}.

(19)

Here
V({\bm{p}})
represents the confining gluon propagator discussed in detail in the next subsection — see Eq. (
29
) below. The dressed Green’s function in Eq. (
18
) can be conveniently decomposed into the positive- and negative-energy components,

S(p_{0},{\bm{p}})=\frac{\Lambda_{+}({\bm{p}})\gamma_{0}}{p_{0}-E_{p}+i\epsilon}+\frac{{\Lambda_{-}}({\bm{p}})\gamma_{0}}{p_{0}+E_{p}-i\epsilon},

(20)

where the projectors read

\Lambda_{\pm}({\bm{p}})=T_{p}P_{\pm}T_{p}^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{2}[1\pm\gamma_{0}\sin\varphi_{p}\pm({\bm{\alpha}}\hat{{\bm{p}}})\cos\varphi_{p}],

(21)

with
T_{p}
for the Foldy operator,

T_{p}=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}({\bm{\gamma}\hat{{\bm{p}}}})\left(\varphi_{p}-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\right],

(22)

P_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}(1\pm\gamma_{0})
, and
\hat{{\bm{p}}}
for the unit vector in the direction of the momentum
{\bm{p}}
.

Solution of the coupled equations (
18
) and (
19
) can be found in the form

\displaystyle S^{-1}(p_{0},{\bm{p}})=\gamma_{0}p_{0}-({{\bm{\gamma}}}\hat{{\bm{p}}})B_{p}-A_{p},

\displaystyle\Sigma({\bm{p}})=[A_{p}-m_{q}]+({{\bm{\gamma}}\hat{{\bm{p}}}})[B_{p}-p],

with the auxiliary functions
A_{p}
and
B_{p}
given by

\displaystyle A_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{q}+\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}V({\bm{p}}-{\bm{k}})\sin\varphi_{k},

\displaystyle B_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p+\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\;(\hat{{\bm{p}}}\hat{{\bm{k}}})V({\bm{p}}-{\bm{k}})\cos\varphi_{k}

and the chiral angle
\varphi_{p}
satisfying a mass-gap equation,

A_{p}\cos\varphi_{p}-B_{p}\sin\varphi_{p}=0.

(25)

Then the energy of the dressed fermion (dispersion law) introduced in Eq. (
14
) and entering Eq. (
20
) is calculated as

E_{p}=A_{p}\sin\varphi_{p}+B_{p}\cos\varphi_{p}.

(26)

In the limit of a vanishing interaction,

, the solution of the mass-gap equation (

\sin\varphi^{(0)}_{p}=\frac{m_{q}}{\sqrt{p^{2}+m_{q}^{2}}},\quad\cos\varphi^{(0)}_{p}=\frac{p}{\sqrt{p^{2}+m_{q}^{2}}},

(27)

and, quite predictably, the quark dispersion law (

\braket{\bar{\psi}\psi}=-\frac{N_{c}}{\pi^{2}}\int^{\infty}_{0}dp\;p^{2}\Bigl(\sin\varphi_{p}-\sin\varphi_{p}^{(0)}\Bigr),

3.4
Infrared-infinite and infrared-finite quantities

The linear potential in Eq. (
13
) is infrared-singular and a suitable infrared regularisation is required to make the equations meaningful. Clearly, physical observables cannot depend on the adopted regularisation scheme. To proceed, here we employ the prescription in Refs.
[
27
,
28
,
57
]
that consists in introducing an infrared regulator to the confining “gluon propagator” in momentum space,

V(p)=\frac{8\pi\sigma}{(p^{2}+\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\rm IR}}}^{2})^{2}}.

(29)

Then, in coordinate space, we have

V(r)=\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}V(p)e^{i{\bm{p}}{\bm{r}}}=\frac{\sigma}{\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\rm IR}}}}e^{-\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\rm IR}}}r}.

(30)

Notice that
V(r)
in Eq. (
30
) has just the opposite sign
(because of the factors
i
that appear in two interaction vertices) to the confining potential in Eq. (
13
).
Then it is easy to see that, in the limit of
\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\rm IR}}}\to 0
,

V_{\rm conf}(r)=-V(r)\mathop{=}_{\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\rm IR}}}\to 0}-\frac{\sigma}{\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\rm IR}}}}+\sigma r+\ldots,

(31)

where the ellipsis stands for the terms suppressed for
\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\rm IR}}}\to 0
.
This way the original form of the linear confinement in Eq. (
13
) is readily restored, but an infrared-divergent constant appears in addition. Since the interquark potential is not observable, the presence of a singular constant in it does not cause any trouble. Moreover, all nonobservable coloured quantities acquire such singular contributions
[
58
]
and are consequently removed
from the spectrum. In particular, it is easy to verify that, after regularisation with
\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\rm IR}}}

[
28
]
,

\displaystyle A_{p}=\frac{\sigma}{2\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\rm IR}}}}\sin\varphi_{p}+A_{p}^{\rm fin},

\displaystyle B_{p}=\frac{\sigma}{2\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\rm IR}}}}\cos\varphi_{p}+B_{p}^{\rm fin},

with
A_{p}^{\rm fin}
and
B_{p}^{\rm fin}
for infrared-finite contributions. Then the function
E_{p}
in Eq. (
26
) is also infrared divergent,

E_{p}=\frac{\sigma}{2\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\rm IR}}}}+\Bigl(A_{p}^{\rm fin}\sin\varphi_{p}+B_{p}^{\rm fin}\cos\varphi_{p}\Bigr).

(33)

Consequently, the single-quark Green’s function (
20
) does not have finite physical poles in the complex energy plane, which admits a natural interpretation that quarks and antiquarks cannot be on mass shell. Meanwhile, observable quantities are free of the infrared divergences, so that the singular terms
\propto 1/\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\rm IR}}}
must drops out from them. For example, it is easy to see that this term cancels in the mass-gap equation (
25
) and, therefore, the chiral angle
\varphi_{p}
is also finite in the infrared limit. The same holds for the observable colour-singlet meson masses since the infrared divergence of the single quark Green’s function is exactly cancelled by the infrared divergence of the kernel in the Bethe–Salpeter equation for a quark–antiquark bound state
[
28
]
.

Infrared singularity of the fermion dispersion law (
33
) implies that the latter cannot be used in a suitable definition of an infrared-finite effective quark mass. Instead, it proves convenient to introduce an infrared-finite quark energy
\omega_{p}
through the relation

B_{p}\omega_{p}=pE_{p}

(34)

that, for a free quark, trivially yields

\omega_{p}^{(0)}=E_{p}^{(0)}=\sqrt{p^{2}+m_{q}^{2}}.

(35)

In the meantime,

\omega_{p}
is finite in the infrared limit of
\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\rm IR}}}\to 0
also for interacting quarks,

\omega_{p}=p\lim_{\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\rm IR}}}\to 0}\frac{E_{p}}{B_{p}}=\frac{p}{\cos\varphi_{p}}=\sqrt{p^{2}+(p\tan\varphi_{p})^{2}},

(36)

so an infrared-finite dynamical quark mass can be conveniently defined as

M_{p}=p\tan\varphi_{p},

(37)

and, consequently,

\sin\varphi_{p}=\frac{M_{p}}{\sqrt{p^{2}+M_{p}^{2}}},\quad\cos\varphi_{p}=\frac{p}{\sqrt{p^{2}+M_{p}^{2}}}.

(38)

In the no-interaction limit, Eq. (
27
) implies that
M_{p}^{(0)}=m_{q}
while a nonvanishing interaction provides an additional contribution to
M_{p}
. Since the chiral angle is infrared-finite then, obviously, so is
M_{p}
. Indeed, a smooth
\mu_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\rm IR}}}\to 0
limit was established numerically for both the chiral angle
\varphi_{p}
and the effective quark mass
M_{p}
in Refs.
[
28
,
57
]
. More generally, from now onwards all quantities are regularised in the infrared and only those of them are studied that possess a well-defined infrared limit.

3.7
Bound state equation for quark–antiquark mesons at finite
T

The spectrum of quark–antiquark mesons can be found from the Bethe–Salpeter bound-state equation that was previously studied in many works (see, for example, Refs.
[
19
,
23
,
26
,
28
]
). Here we generalise it to a finite temperature
T
first. To this end we remind the reader that a generic quark–antiquark meson in its rest
frame is described by the matrix amplitude
\chi({\bm{p}};M)
, with
{\bm{p}}
for the momentum of the quark (
-{\bm{p}}
for the antiquark) and
M
for the mass of the meson. The Bethe–Salpeter equation for this amplitude schematically depicted in Fig.
5
reads

\begin{split}\chi({{\bm{p}}};M)=i\int&\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}V({\bm{p}}-{\bm{q}})\gamma_{0}S(q_{0}+M/2,{\bm{q}};T)\\
&\times\chi({\bm{q}};M)S(q_{0}-M/2,{\bm{q}};T)\gamma_{0},\end{split}

(56)

where the propagator
S(p_{0},{\bm{p}};T)
quoted in Eq. (
40
) can be presented as a sum of the zero-temperature term and an additional temperature-dependent contribution,

S(p_{0},{\bm{p}};T)=S(p_{0},{\bm{p}})+\Delta S(p_{0},{\bm{p}};T).

(57)

Figure 5:
Schematic representation of the Bethe–Salpeter equation (
56
) written in the ladder approximation for the interquark interaction. The single, double, and curly line correspond to the quark (antiquark), meson, and the interquark interaction, respectively.

Due to the instantaneous nature of the interaction described by the “gluon propagator”
V({\bm{p}}-{\bm{q}})
, the mesonic Bethe–Salpeter amplitude
\chi({\bm{p}};M)
in Eq. (
56
) does not depend on the temporal component of the meson momentum, so the integral in the loop energy
q_{0}
can be evaluated analytically in a closed form. As a result,
the Bethe–Salpeter equation (
56
) can be formulated as a bound-state equation for the rest-frame matrix wave function of a meson defined as

\begin{split}\phi({\bm{p}};M)=i\int\frac{dp_{0}}{2\pi}S(p_{0}+&M/2,{\bm{p}};T)\chi({\bm{p}};M)\\
&\times S(p_{0}-M/2,{\bm{p}};T).\end{split}

(58)

Then, employing (i) the decomposition of the zero-temperature Green’s function
S(p_{0},{\bm{p}})
into positive- and negative-energy contributions as provided in Eq. (
20
), (ii) the
\delta
-functions contained in the term
\Delta S(p_{0},{\bm{p}};T)
as given in Eq. (
40
), and (iii)
an easily verifiable relation,

\begin{split}\int\frac{dq_{0}}{2\pi i}\left[\frac{1}{q_{0}\pm M/2-E_{q}+i\epsilon}\right]&\left[\frac{1}{q_{0}\mp M/2+E_{q}-i\epsilon}\right]\\
&=-\frac{1}{2E_{q}\mp M},\end{split}

(59)

we find

\begin{split}\int\frac{dq_{0}}{2\pi i}&S(q_{0}+M/2,{\bm{q}})\chi({\bm{q}};M)S(q_{0}-M/2,{\bm{q}})=\\
&-(\Lambda_{+}\gamma_{0})\chi^{[+]}(\Lambda_{-}\gamma_{0})-(\Lambda_{-}\gamma_{0})\chi^{[-]}(\Lambda_{+}\gamma_{0}),\end{split}

(60)

\displaystyle\int

\displaystyle\frac{dq_{0}}{2\pi i}S(q_{0}+M/2,{\bm{q}})\chi({\bm{q}};M)\Delta S(q_{0}-M/2,{\bm{q}};T)

\displaystyle=n_{q}\left[(\Lambda_{-}\gamma_{0})\chi^{[-]}(\Lambda_{+}\gamma_{0})+(\Lambda_{+}\gamma_{0})\chi^{[0]}(\Lambda_{+}\gamma_{0})\right]

(61)

\displaystyle+\bar{n}_{q}\left[(\Lambda_{+}\gamma_{0})\chi^{[+]}(\Lambda_{-}\gamma_{0})-(\Lambda_{-}\gamma_{0})\chi^{[0]}(\Lambda_{-}\gamma_{0})\right],

\displaystyle\int

\displaystyle\frac{dq_{0}}{2\pi i}\Delta S(q_{0}+M/2,{\bm{q}};T)\chi({\bm{q}};M)S(q_{0}-M/2,{\bm{q}})

\displaystyle=n_{q}\left[(\Lambda_{+}\gamma_{0})\chi^{[+]}(\Lambda_{-}\gamma_{0})-(\Lambda_{+}\gamma_{0})\chi^{[0]}(\Lambda_{+}\gamma_{0})\right]

(62)

\displaystyle+\bar{n}_{q}\left[(\Lambda_{-}\gamma_{0})\chi^{[-]}(\Lambda_{+}\gamma_{0})+(\Lambda_{-}\gamma_{0})\chi^{[0]}(\Lambda_{-}\gamma_{0})\right],

and

\int\frac{dq_{0}}{2\pi i}\Delta S(q_{0}+M/2,{\bm{q}};T)\chi({\bm{q}};M)\Delta S(q_{0}-M/2,{\bm{q}})=0,

(63)

where

\chi^{[+]}=\frac{\chi({\bm{q}};M)}{2E_{q}-M},\quad\chi^{[-]}=\frac{\chi({\bm{q}};M)}{2E_{q}+M},\quad\chi^{[0]}=\frac{\chi({\bm{q}};M)}{M}.

(64)

Combining the results in Eqs. (
60
)-(

\phi({\bm{p}};M)
, defined in Eq. (
58
) above, in the form

\phi({\bm{p}};M)=i(1-n_{q}-\bar{n}_{q})\\
\times\int\frac{dq_{0}}{2\pi}S(q_{0}+M/2,{\bm{q}})\chi({\bm{q}};M)S(q_{0}-M/2,{\bm{q}})\\
=(1-n_{q}-\bar{n}_{q})\Bigl[(\Lambda_{+}\gamma_{0})\chi^{[+]}(\Lambda_{-}\gamma_{0})\\
+(\Lambda_{-}\gamma_{0})\chi^{[-]}(\Lambda_{+}\gamma_{0})\Bigr].

(65)

Then the Bethe–Salpeter equation (
56
) can be written as a system of two coupled equations,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}[2E_{p}-M]\chi^{[+]}({\bm{p}};M)=\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}(1-n_{q}-\bar{n}_{q})V({\bm{p}}-{\bm{q}})\\
\times\Bigl[(\Lambda_{+}\gamma_{0})\chi^{[+]}({\bm{q}};M)(\Lambda_{-}\gamma_{0})+(\Lambda_{-}\gamma_{0})\chi^{[-]}({\bm{q}};M)(\Lambda_{+}\gamma_{0})\Bigr]\\[8.53581pt]

where, like in the mass-gap equation (

The ground-state pion and scalar “
\sigma
-meson”

A crucial feature of the mesonic spectrum at
T<T_{\rm ch}
is a vanishing mass of the
1,0^{-+}
ground state that is the Nambu–Goldstone boson for spontaneously broken chiral symmetry. Meanwhile, at
T>T_{\rm ch}
, when chiral symmetry is restored, the dynamical quark mass vanishes,
M_{p}=0
, and the Bethe–Salpeter equations for the chiral pion and scalar “
\sigma
-meson’’ become identical.
6
6

6

Hereinafter, we use quotation marks in the name of the lowest scalar meson, solution to the Bethe–Salpater equation (
68
), to distinguish it from the physical
\sigma
-meson (
f_{0}(500)

[
63
]
) that has a more complicated structure than a plain quark–antiquark state. Indeed, while the energy-spin formalism
inherent to the chiral quark model (
11
) naturally accounts for multiquark components of the mesons’ wave functions that stem from the backward in time motion of the quarks and antiquarks (the so-called
Z
-graphs), the meson–meson interactions are automatically suppressed in the large-
N_{c}
limit — the corresponding terms in the Hamiltonian of the model scale as
{\cal O}(1/N_{c})
. Then a strong
\pi
-
\pi
interaction at low energies in the
S
wave cannot be naturally incorporated into the given formalism and, as a result, the properties of the physical
f_{0}(500)
cannot be captured properly.
This difference between the generic quark-antiquark mesons and dynamically generated objects can be well demonstrated employing the large-
N_{c}
arguments. Indeed, as the number of colours grows, the mass of the generic scalar
\bar{q}q
state remains nearly constant while its width tends to zero
since the effects of the light-quark pair creation from the vacuum scale as
{\cal O}(1/\sqrt{N_{c}})
in this limit. In the meantime, the physical
f_{0}(500)
pole demonstrates a severely different pattern: its real part (the mass) grows with
N_{c}
while the
N_{c}
-dependence on its imaginary part (the width) is nontrivial, at odds with the aforementioned
1/N_{C}
law specific for a generic
\bar{q}q
scalar meson
[
64
,
65
]
. It is the latter state that we denote as “
\sigma
-meson” and study in this work. As follows from the discussion above, at
T>T_{\rm ch}
,
this scalar state must be strictly degenerate with the pseudoscalar pion.
We illustrate the pattern in Fig.
6
where we plot
the dependence of the lowest scalar and pseudoscalar meson masses on the temperature to observe that the splitting between them (i) is largest at
T=0
, (ii) remains nearly constant up to
T\approx 0.5T_{\rm ch}
, and (iii) then drops fast to disappear at
T=T_{\rm ch}
.

We now proceed to the wave functions
\psi_{\pm}(p)
introduced in Eq. (
) and to this end extend the analysis in Ref.
[
28
]
to non-zero temperatures.
When chiral symmetry is spontaneously broken at
T<T_{\rm ch}
, the dynamical quark mass
M_{p}
is large and the small current quark mass
m_{q}
can be safely neglected from the beginning with no effect on the results. However, above
T_{\rm ch}
, the chiral limit implies approaching it from the chirally broken phase with
m_{q}\neq 0
and taking
m_{q}\to 0
gradually.
Indeed, in QCD the chiral limit of
m_{q}=0
and the large-
N_{c}
limit do not commute.
We also remind the reader that the positive- and negative-energy wave functions for a given meson,
\psi_{\pm}(p)
, extracted from the Bethe–Salpeter amplitudes
\phi_{\pm}({\bm{p}};M)
in Eq. (
67
) do not support a straightforward probabilistic interpretation because of their “nonstandard” normalisation condition,

\begin{split}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}&\mbox{Tr}\Bigl[\phi_{+}^{2}({\bm{p}};M)-\phi_{-}^{2}({\bm{p}};M)\Bigr]\\
=&\int\frac{p^{2}dp}{2\pi^{2}}\Bigl[\psi_{+}^{2}(p)-\psi_{-}^{2}(p)\Bigr]=2M,\end{split}

(70)

where the trace is taken over the spin indices. The relativistic normalisation in Eq. (
70
) is consistent with the condition that the charge of the state with
I=I_{3}=1
equals to unity
[
28
]
.

chiral limit of
m_{q}\to 0

(1-n_{p}-\bar{n}_{p})_{|\mu=0,M_{p}=0,T>0}\mathop{\propto}_{p\to 0}p,

(71)

that is, to the Pauli blocking effect.

3.8.2
Symmetries of the spectrum of quark-antiquark mesons

In this subsection, we discuss the symmetries of the spectrum of quark–antiquark mesons below and above the chiral restoration temperature
T_{\rm ch}
. The masses of the
\bar{q}q
bound states with different quantum numbers
nJ^{PC}
calculated at the temperatures
T=0
,
T=0.5T_{\rm ch}
,
T=0.9T_{\rm ch}
,
T=1.1T_{\rm ch}
, and
T=1.5T_{\rm ch}
are collected in Tables
4
-
8
below. The masses quoted in Table
4
for
T=0
reproduce those in Ref.
[
28
]
.

At
T<T_{\rm ch}
, a vanishing mass of the ground-state pion and, as a result, its large splitting with the ground-state scalar “
\sigma
-meson” provide a clear signal of
dynamically broken chiral symmetry as was discussed in detail in the previous subsection. Another signal of the same phenomenon is nonvanishing splittings within all chiral multiplets. As an instructive example, in Figs.
14
and
14
, we visualise the calculated spectra of the mesonic states with
J=0
and
J=1
at
T=0.5T_{\rm ch}
, taken from Table
5
, that clearly
demonstrates the above features and this way illustrates the general pattern. We observe that the spectrum at
T=0.5T_{\rm ch}
only marginally differs from that at
T=0
, and even at
T=0.9T_{\rm ch}
, noticeable changes can only be seen for the states with
J=0
and
J=1
: the splitting within the
\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)
multiplets (that is, between the states with
0^{-+}
and
0^{++}
and between the states with
1^{+-}
and
1^{--}
) and between the states with
1^{--}
and
1^{++}
belonging to the representation
(0,1)\oplus(1,0)
diminish.

At
T>T_{\rm ch}
, when chiral symmetry is restored, the dynamical quark mass vanishes,
M_{p}=0
, and the Bethe–Salpeter equations for the mesons belonging to the same chiral multiplet become identical
[
28
]
thus resulting in the corresponding degeneracy of the spectrum. We illustrate the pattern by Figs.
14
and
14
where, as before, we plot the spectra for the mesons with
J=0
and
J=1
but now at
T=1.5T_{\rm ch}
— see Table

.
As one can see, the splittings within the
\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)
and
(0,1)\oplus(1,0)
multiplets are now completely gone, which complies well with the natural expectations given that chiral symmetry is not spontaneously broken above
T_{\rm ch}
, so
the ground-state pseudoscalar meson is not the Goldstone boson any more and it becomes massive.

Furthermore, the results just obtained allow us to discuss the emergence of approximate chiral spin symmetry and its flavour extension
SU(4)\times SU(4)
that are the symmetries of the confining part of the Hamiltonian
[
10
]
. These symmetries
are broken by the quark condensate and quark kinetic term. Then, as chiral symmetry is restored and the quark condensate vanishes, the only remaining source of the symmetry breaking is the quark kinetic term. Consequently, at
T>T_{\rm ch}
, as soon as the contribution to the meson mass coming from the confining interaction exceeds that from the quark kinetic term, the aforementioned additional symmetry should reveal itself as an approximate degeneracy of all states with a given
J>0
. Indeed, one can conclude from Fig.
17
that, in the mesons with
J=1
, chiral spin symmetry is strongly broken at
T=0
but this breaking is essentially reduced above
T_{\rm ch}
. At the same time, the emergence of the approximate degeneracy
of all the states with a given spin
J
is well seen already for

J\geqslant 2
.

3.8.3
Wave functions and radii of higher-spin states

In Fig.
17
, we compare the wave functions of several mesons with different quantum numbers obtained in the chiral limit at different temperatures. In particular, at temperatures
T\lesssim 0.5T_{\rm ch}
, the pion wave function
\psi_{+}^{\pi}(p;M_{\pi}=0)=\psi_{-}^{\pi}(p,M_{\pi}=0)
is approximately the same as at
T=0
. Then, as the temperature increases towards
T_{\rm ch}
, the wave function gets enhanced at small momenta and it finally diverges at
p=0
above the chiral restoration temperature. This observation provides a natural explanation for the divergent pion radius discussed above. Naturally, since chiral symmetry is restored above
T_{\rm ch}
, the wave functions of the
J^{PC}=0^{-+}
and
J^{PC}=0^{++}
states become identical in the chiral limit, so all the conclusions made above for the pion at
T>T_{\rm ch}
equally apply to the “
\sigma
-meson”. A similar, although somewhat weaker, divergence at small momenta is also observed for the wave function of the “
b_{1}
-meson” with

J^{PC}=1^{+-}
. It can be further deduced from the corresponding plots in the lower row in Fig.
17
that the small-
p
behaviour of the wave functions for the mesons with higher spins,
J\geqslant 2
, is somewhat tamed with the temperature rise. The difference with the mesons with
J=0,1
stems from a stronger centrifugal repulsion for higher
J
’s that suppresses the meson wave function at small momenta. Consequently, Pauli blocking of the quark
and antiquark levels at
T>T_{\rm ch}
is not as important in the mesons with
J\geqslant 2
as in their siblings with
J=0,1
. This feature can be further traced by comparing the r.m.s. radii of these mesons. In Table
9
, we provide the results of the calculations of
\braket{r^{2}}^{1/2}
for the vector “
\rho
-meson”. The effect of “swelling” at
T>T_{\rm ch}
is clearly seen in this case as well, though it is less pronounced than for
J=0
— for a realistic
current quark mass, the “
\rho
-meson” increases its size at
T>T_{\rm ch}
by approximately a factor of 2 (to be compared with
3\div 5
for the pion and “
\sigma
-meson” — see Tables
3
,
3
and the discussion in the previous subsection). This pattern holds for the other
J=1
states too as can be seen in Fig.
17
:
although the radii of the
J^{PC}=1^{+-}
and
J^{PC}=1^{++}
states also demonstrate an unbound rise in the approach to the chiral limit, it is not as strong as in the case of the mesons with
J=0
—
cf.
Fig.
9
. This rise is further tamed for the states with higher spins
J
.

We, therefore, conclude that a dilute gas of hadrons, observed at temperatures below
T_{\rm ch}
, turns to a dense system of swelled, strongly overlapping meson-like states with light quarks (predominantly with
J=0,1
) above the chiral restoration temperature, and the corresponding phase of the theory deserves its name “stringy fluid”.

4
Conclusions

In this paper, we employed a manifestly confining and chirally
symmetric model for QCD to study some properties of the hot QCD
matter above the chiral restoration temperature. This model is only an approximation to QCD since all interquark interactions, except for the linear confining potential, are disregarded.
Nevertheless, it accommodates the principal aspects of QCD at low temperatures such as confinement and spontaneous breaking of chiral symmetry in the vacuum. It also shares chiral spin symmetry of confinement with real QCD. Thus we dare expect this model to provide a valuable microscopic insight into hot QCD as well.

We observed a chiral symmetry restoration phase transition at
T_{\rm ch}\simeq 90
MeV that should be confronted with the critical temperature around 130 MeV obtained on the lattice in the chiral limit. The physical reason for chiral restoration in the
confining regime is Pauli blocking of the fermionic levels, necessary for the formation of the quark condensate, by the thermal excitation of quarks and antiquarks. Since the model remains confining above
T_{\rm ch}
, its spectrum still consists of hadron-like states rather than free quarks. We solved the bound-state equation for quark-antiquark mesons at
T>T_{\rm ch}
to find their masses and wave functions near the chiral limit of
m_{q}\to 0
. A crucial property of these meson-like states with light quarks is their size (estimated from the r.m.s. radius) that, for a realistic current quark mass, may exceed several times the corresponding size at zero temperature. Furthermore, their radii diverge in the chiral limit thus driving the corresponding bound states infinitely large. The physics behind this swelling is, again, Pauli blocking of the fermionic levels with small momenta by thermal excitations. Importantly, even though the size of these hadron-like objects becomes (infinitely) large, they are still quark-antiquark bound states with well defined masses and normalisable wave functions. The most pronounced effect is observed for lower spins
J
since Pauli blocking stronger affects physical systems most sensitive to small momenta. Mesons consisting of heavy quarks are expected to preserve their size above
T_{\rm ch}
since Pauli blocking due to the thermal excitations is not operative in them.

We stress that deconfined quarks and antiquarks can not exist in the employed model since it remains confining at all temperatures. Therefore, a dilute hadron gas below
T_{\rm ch}
converts into a system of very large overlapping meson-like states (“strings”) above the chiral restoration temperature. The observed radical increase in size of the meson-like states at
T>T_{\rm ch}
naturally explains a crucial experimental property of the hot QCD matter — namely, its collectivity and a very small mean free path of the effective constituents.
