Unveiling the TGA gene family in Qingke (Hordeum vulgare L. var. nudum) and the role of HvnTGA2 in host-pathogen interaction
Authors: Shuai Wang, Hailing Lu, Kai Xu, Yuping Zhao, Li Wang, Jianping Si, Fengning Liu, Tao He, Le Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs the staple crop in plateau agriculture, Tibetan hulless barley (Hordeum vulgare L. var. nudum) experiences dual stresses from challenging environments (abiotic stress) and pathogenic attacks (biotic stress). TGACG-binding transcription factors (TGAs) serve as master regulators orchestrating plant growth, developmental processes, and defense responses against biotic and abiotic stresses, with particularly pivotal functions in regulating plant immunity. Here, 10 HvnTGAs were identified in the Qingke genome, which were phylogenetically classified into five distinct clades. HvnTGA proteins possessed conserved structural domains, characteristic motif compositions, and three-dimensional architectures typical of the TGA family. Subcellular localization assays confirmed that both HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6 were localized to the nucleus. RNA-seq profiling revealed that eight out of ten HvnTGA genes were differentially expressed following powdery mildew infection. The expression of HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6 was specifically upregulated by ethephon (ETH) and methyl jasmonate (MeJA) treatment. Yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays demonstrated that both HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6 could form homodimers. Furthermore, heterodimeric interactions were detected between HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA4, HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6, as well as HvnTGA4 and HvnTGA6. Interaction assays based on split luciferase complementation assay (SLCA) confirmed the interaction between HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6, as well as the self-interaction of HvnTGA6. The silencing of HvnTGA2 resulted in reduced expression of HvnPR-4, enhanced susceptibility of Qingke to Pyrenophora graminea, and accumulation of ROS. This study comprehensively elucidates the regulatory mechanisms of TGA transcription factors in Qingke, a highland-adapted crop, provides novel molecular insights into plant-pathogen interactions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08656-x.Keywords: hulless barley, TGA gene family, protein interaction, Pyrenophora graminea, disease resistance

Introduction

Transcription factors (TFs) are proteins typically comprising two functional domains involved in DNA-binding and transcriptional activation or repression of gene expression in response to internal or external stimuli [1]. Identification and deciphering the molecular function of TFs in regulating gene expression provide insights into signal transduction pathways and stress responses in different crops [2]. TFs can be divided into distinct families based on their DNA-binding domains, which enable them to regulate specific target genes. Basic region leucine zipper (bZIP) TFs constitute a large, extensively studied gene family found throughout the plant kingdom [3]. TGACG-binding TFs (TGAs), belonging to the D clade of bZIP family, specifically recognize and bind to the TGACG motif (also termed activation sequence-1 or as-1), and possess a characteristic zinc finger domain along with two glutamine-rich transactivation domains [4]. The DNA-binding specificity of TGAs is determined by their N-terminal DNA-binding domain, whereas protein dimerization is facilitated by the adjacent bZIP motif [5].

TGA proteins have been reported to control or regulate a wide range of processes in several important economic crops and model plants, including growth and development, adaptation to environmental stress, and hormone signaling pathways. The TGA gene was first identified in tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) [6]. Subsequent studies identified 10 TGA genes in Arabidopsis thaliana. The functions and regulatory mechanisms of the AtTGAs have been extensively studied, revealing distinct roles for individual gene members. Among these, seven members (AtTGA1-AtTGA7) contribute to defense responses [7]. Notably, AtTGA2, AtTGA5 and AtTGA6 specifically activate SA-responsive genes, including pathogenesis-related gene 1 (PR1) [8]. SA plays a crucial role in plant defense by primarily inducing systemic acquired resistance (SAR) through modulating the expression of PR genes [9]. In cultivated peanut (Arachis hypogaea), AhTGA11 acts as a positive regulator promoting plant tolerance to both low temperature and drought stress. This regulation is primarily mediated through SA, JA, and ABA signaling pathways, which function by mitigating oxidative stress under these conditions [10]. Similarly, in strawberry, exogenous SA application significantly enhanced the expression of FaTGA and other SA-responsive genes. Further correlation analysis suggested that FaTGA1, FaTGA2, FaTGA5, FaTGA7, FaTGA8, and FaTGA10 may play crucial roles in powdery mildew resistance [11]. Moreover, in rice, overexpression of OsTGAL1 significantly altered the expression of multiple SA signaling-related genes and directly bound to the promoter of OsSGT1, which encodes a key enzyme for SA metabolism [12]. Collectively, TGA transcription factors are established regulators of PR genes.

Tibetan hulless barley (Hordeum vulgare L. var. nudum), referred to locally as Qingke, is a barley variant extensively cultivated on the Qinghai-Tibetan Plateau. Population genetic analyses [13] indicate that contemporary Qingke was introduced to the region approximately 4,500–3,500 years ago and subsequently evolved a suite of unique highland adaptive traits, including UV-B radiation resistance [14], cold tolerance [15], and elevated β-glucan content [16]. However, the growth and production of Qingke is consistently compromised by abiotic and biotic stresses, particularly pathogenic microorganisms. Consequently, disease-related grain losses account for an estimated 30% of the crop’s annual production [17]. Given the constraints imposed by diseases, breeding disease-resistant cultivars represents a key strategy for enhancing yield and quality [18]. Genetic engineering, which can accelerate the breeding process, has become a principal approach in Qingke improvement [5]. Therefore, the cloning and functional characterization of target genes constitute fundamental work. Considering the pivotal roles of the TGA family and its associated signaling network in stress resistance, this study aims to expand the application value of Qingke TGA genes in resistance breeding through systematic characterization at the genome-wide scale.

We systematically analyzed the HvnTGA gene family through integrated bioinformatics approaches, encompassing physicochemical properties, phylogenetic relationships, conserved motifs, and gene structures, thereby elucidating their genetic features and functional implications. Functional analysis primarily targeted HvnTGAs that phylogenetically cluster with clade II members of Arabidopsis, which may regulate systemic resistance and disease resistance [7]. Subcellular localization analysis confirmed that both HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6 were localized to the nucleus, consistent with the typical characteristics of transcription factors. Yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays and split luciferase complementation assay (SLCA) confirmed pairwise interactions among distinct HvnTGA proteins. Using Virus-induced Gene Silencing (VIGS), we demonstrated that silencing HvnTGA2 enhances Qingke susceptibility to Pyrenophora graminea. These findings provide novel insights into the functional diversification of TGA TFs in Qingke disease resistance mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and Pyrenophora graminea strain

The hulless barley cultivar ‘Dulihuang’, provided by the Academy of Agriculture and Forestry Sciences, Qinghai University, served as the experimental material in RNA isolation, gene cloning, and expression profiling studies. Seed sterilization was performed sequentially with 70% ethanol for 30 s followed by 5% sodium hypochlorite for 5 min, and then seeds were rinsed repeatedly with sterile distilled water. Sterilized seeds (15–20 per plate) were placed on moist filter paper in Petri dishes, wrapped in foil, and germinated at 22 °C in darkness. Seedlings were transferred to pots filled with a growth substrate composed of peat moss, perlite, and vermiculite in a 1:1:1 ratio, and grown to the two-leaf stage for hormone treatments. The P. graminea strain TW-3, employed in pathogenicity assays, is maintained in our laboratory culture collection. The isolate was obtained from field-infected hulless barley leaves in Menyuan County, Haibei Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture, Qinghai Province, China. The virulence of strain TW-3 was validated through controlled inoculation assays on seedlings of multiple Qingke cultivars, following the protocol established by Hou et al. [19]. Briefly, the pure culture of P. graminea TW-3 was inoculated onto PDA solid medium and incubated at 25 °C for 7 days until mycelia completely covered the plate. Mycelial plugs were then transferred to PDB liquid medium and placed on a rotary shaker (150 rpm, 25 °C) for 10 days until the mycelia fully filled the liquid. For inoculation, three-day-old germlings (with sprouts ~ 5 mm) were incubated in P. graminea-infected PDB liquid medium at 25 °C in the dark for approximately 38 h. The seedlings were then transferred to normal growth conditions for two weeks until typical leaf stripe symptoms were observed. The disease rate index proposed by Tan et al. [20]. was employed to evaluate the leaf stripe resistance of Qingke cultivars.

Genomic identification of TGA family proteins in hulless barley

Genomic data for Tibetan hulless barley (Version 2) were downloaded from the Qingke genome database (http://show.genebang.com/project/index?n=barley) [21]. Since all TGA proteins contain conserved bZIP (PF00170) and DOG1 (PF14144) domains, this study identified TGA proteins in the barley genome using a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) search (E-value ≤ 1 × 10⁻⁵) via the TBtools software. In parallel, this study employed Arabidopsis TGA proteins as seed sequences for Local BLAST alignment to identify putative TGA family proteins in the hulless barley genome. The final TGA family members were confirmed through cross-validation of HMM search and BLAST results, supplemented by domain verification using CDD search (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd/). Multiple sequence alignment was performed using ClustalW with default parameters in MEGA 11.0. The phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbor-joining (NJ) method under the JTT model, and bootstrap analysis was conducted with 1000 replicates. Tree visualization was optimized using Evolview [22] (https://www.evolgenius.info/evolview/).

Physicochemical characterization of the HvnTGA proteins and identification of cis-acting regulatory elements in the promoters

The physicochemical properties of HvnTGA proteins were analyzed via ProtParam on the ExPASy server (https://www.expasy.org). Subcellular localization predictions were performed using Plant-mPLoc (https://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/plantmulti). Conserved motifs were identified using MEME Suite version 5.5.9 (https://meme-suite.org/meme), configured with the following parameters: maximum number of motifs = 10, minimum motif width = 6 aa, and maximum motif width = 50 aa. Exon-intron structures were generated in TBtools based on Qingke genome annotations. The three-dimensional structures of each HvnTGA protein were predicted using SWISS-MODEL, with A. thaliana TGA7 (8gyz.1) as the template. Cis-regulatory elements within 2000 bp upstream of HvnTGA translation initiation sites were predicted using PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/), with visualization implemented in TBtools [23].

Expression profiling of HvnTGAs in response to Blumeria graminis f.sp. hordei infection

Building upon prior transcriptomic evidence [17], this investigation systematically analyzed the temporal expression profiles of HvnTGA genes in two contrasting hulless barley cultivars - G7 (highly resistant) and Z13 (highly susceptible) – during Blumeria graminis f.sp. hordei infection at five time points (0, 6, 36, 72, and 168 h post-inoculation). The transcriptome re-analysis raw data, generated with three biological replicates for each treatment, are summarized in Table S1. The re-analysis was conducted using TBtools software, adhering to the computational pipeline established by Wang et al. [24]. Briefly, raw sequencing reads were quality-assessed with FastQC and filtered using Trimmomatic [25] (v0.39) with parameters: LEADING:0, TRAILING:3, SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15, MINLEN:36. The clean reads were then aligned to the Qingke reference genome using Hisat2 [26], and gene expression was quantified with StringTie [27]. Finally, the transcripts per million (TPM) values of the HvnTGA genes were calculated from the RNA-seq data and visualized as a heatmap using the Weishengxin online bioinformatics platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn; accessed on 10 October 2024).

HvnTGAs gene cloning and quantitative real-time expression analysis

Gene-specific primers were designed based on the CDS sequences of HvnTGAs for target gene cloning. PCR amplification was performed in a 50-µL reaction system containing 25 µL PrimeSTAR Max Premix (2×), 1µL each of forward and reverse primers, and 1 µL cDNA template. The thermal cycling protocol comprised: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 3 min; 35 cycles of 98 °C for 15 s, 55 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 10 s; final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were purified using an agarose gel DNA extraction kit (Tiangen Biotech), ligated into the pEASY-Blunt Zero Cloning vector (TransGen Biotech), transformed into DH5α competent cells, and sequencing-verified by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai).

To elucidate temporal expression patterns of HvnTGA family genes under phytohormone treatments, three-leaf-stage Qingke seedlings were treated with sodium salicylate (SANa, 10 mM), methyl jasmonate (MeJA, 20 µM), and ethephon (ETH, 5 mM) for designated durations (0, 2, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h). Leaf samples collected in three replicates at each time point were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C. Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus reagent, followed by cDNA synthesis with the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (with gDNA Eraser). Gene expression was analyzed by qPCR using the One Step TB Green PrimeScript RT-PCR Kit (Takara, Dalian, China) on a LightCycler 480 real-time PCR system (Roche Diagnostics). The qPCR cycling program was carried out using a three-step protocol: denaturation at 94 °C for 5 s, annealing at 56 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, repeated for 40 cycles. The HSP90 gene [28] was employed as the internal reference gene. Relative expression levels were quantified using the Relative Expression Software Tool [29] (REST), while statistical analysis was conducted via the P(H1) test. Primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

Subcellular localization analysis of HvnTGA protein

The pBI121-sGFP vector, generated by replacing the GUS gene in pBI121 with sGFP, was used to determine the subcellular localization of HvnTGA proteins. The vector was digested with both Xba I and ApaL I to linearize it for insertion. After PCR amplification and sequencing, the coding sequences (CDS) of the HvnTGA genes (lacking stop codons) were inserted into the linearized vector. These recombinant plasmids, each expressing an HvnTGA protein C-terminally fused to sGFP, were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 competent cells. Subsequently, a single colony was selected, propagated, and resuspended in infiltration buffer to an OD600 = 0.6. Transient expression of sGFP-tagged proteins was performed in 4-week-old Nicotiana benthamiana seedlings. Following infiltration, plants were incubated in the dark for 48 h and then exposed to normal light for an additional 48 h. Recombinant protein localization was then visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon A1R+, Japan) with excitation and emission wavelengths of 488 nm and 510 nm, respectively. Sequence information of the primers used for vector construction was listed in Table S2.

Interactome profiling of HvnTGAs via yeast two-hybrid assay

To validate potential interactions between HvnTGAs, we individually cloned HvnTGA2, HvnTGA4, and HvnTGA6 into both pGBKT7 and pGADT7 vectors. For the construction of the HvnTGA2-pGADT7 recombinant plasmid, the pGADT7 vector was digested with Nco I and BamH I. The insert and the linearized vector were then ligated using T4 DNA ligase and the ligation mixture was transformed into E. coli DH5α competent cells. Construction of the remaining prey and bait plasmids followed identical procedures but utilized distinct restriction sites. Details of primer sequences and restriction sites used in plasmid construction were provided in Table S2. The recombinant pGADT7 and pGBKT7 vectors were pairwise co-transformed into AH109 competent yeast cells. Single colonies of each co-transformant were cultured in DDO liquid medium (SD/-Leu/-Trp). Yeast cells were harvested by centrifugation, washed three times with sterile water, and resuspended to an OD600 of 0.6. Subsequently, tenfold serial dilutions of the yeast cell suspension were prepared, and 10-µL aliquots of each dilution were spotted onto QDO solid medium plates (SD/-Leu/-Trp/-His/-Ade) to assess protein-protein interactions.

Protein interaction analysis by split luciferase complementation assay (SLCA)

For protein interaction analysis using SLCA, the coding sequences of HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6 were individually cloned into the nLUC and cLUC vectors via homologous recombination, respectively. The A. tumefaciens GV3101 strains harboring these recombinant vectors were resuspended in infiltration buffer and adjusted to an OD600 of 0.5. Subsequently, strain cultures of each test combination were mixed at a 1:1 ratio and infiltrated into the abaxial side of 4-week-old tobacco leaves. Following infiltration, the plants were kept in darkness for 24 h, then transferred to a long-day light cycle (16 h light/8 h dark) for two days to allow protein expression. Finally, a 1 mM solution of D-Luciferin sodium salt was sprayed onto the leaves, and protein interactions were detected using the NightSHADE evo In Vivo Plant Imaging System (LB 985 N, BERTHOLD, Germany).

Barley stripe mosaic virus (BSMV)-mediated gene silencing and resistance assay

BSMV-mediated gene silencing was employed to characterize the function of HvnTGA2 in resistance against P. graminea. The magnesium chelatase subunit H (ChlH) gene was silenced to validate the reliability of BSMV-mediated endogenous gene silencing. Gene-specific fragments (317-bp for HvnChlH; 396-bp for HvnTGA2) were amplified and cloned into Apa I-digested BSMVγ vector through In-Fusion cloning. Primer specifications, including homologous arm sequences, are provided in Table S2. The recombinant vectors BSMVγ-HvnChlH and BSMVγ-HvnTGA2, along with BSMVα, BSMVβ, and BSMVγ vectors, were transformed into GV3101 competent cells. Positive transformants were selected as single colonies, cultured in LB medium, and resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MES, 100 µM AS, 10 mM MgCl₂, pH = 5.2), with the final concentration adjusted to OD600=0.7. Agrobacterium suspensions carrying either BSMVγ-HvnChlH, BSMVγ-HvnTGA2, or the BSMVγ empty vector control were each mixed separately with BSMVα- and BSMVβ-containing suspensions in a 1:1:1 (v/v/v) ratio. To amplify BSMV virus for subsequent experiments, mixtures were pressure-infiltrated into the abaxial surfaces of four-week-old tobacco leaves via needleless syringes, utilizing tobacco as an efficient viral propagation host. At 5–12 dpi, infiltrated leaf discs were homogenized in 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) with 1% (w/v) celite. The clarified sap was mechanically inoculated onto the first true leaves of two-leaf-stage hulless barley. Inoculated plants were subsequently incubated at 22 °C/20°C (14 h/10 h light/dark) for 10 days before systematic phenotypic evaluation.

To evaluate HvnTGA2 silencing effects on disease resistance, BSMVγ-HvnTGA2-silenced and BSMVγ-vector control plants were inoculated with 5-mm mycelial plugs from pure P. graminea PDA cultures. Plugs were affixed to abaxial surfaces of detached leaves and incubated at 22 °C (> 90% RH, darkness) to establish infection. Three biological replicates were set up. Post-inoculation, leaves were maintained under 16-h/8-h light/dark cycles. Disease phenotypes were assessed after 3 days.

ROS detection by histochemical staining

To detect the in situ accumulation of H₂O₂ and O₂⁻, leaves infected with P. graminea were subjected to 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) or nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) staining, with accumulation levels assessed based on histochemical coloration intensity. For DAB staining, leaves were immersed in a 1 mg/mL DAB–HCl (pH 3.8) solution and incubated overnight. Following staining, the DAB solution was discarded, and leaves were transferred to 95% ethanol for an 8-hour incubation to eliminate chlorophyll interference. For NBT staining, leaves were incubated in a 0.5 mg/mL NBT (pH 7.8) solution at 30 °C for 5 h. After staining, samples were destained in 95% ethanol until complete decolorization was achieved. Relative staining intensities of both DAB and NBT were quantified using ImageJ software [30].

Results

Identification and phylogenetics of the HvnTGA gene family

Ten TGA family proteins (designated HvnTGA1 to HvnTGA10) were identified in the hulless barley genome using an integrated HMM search and BLAST analysis approach. Analysis of the HvnTGAs’ physicochemical properties revealed the following characteristics: amino acid lengths varied from 331 to 598 residues, corresponding to molecular weights between 36.82 and 65.88 kDa (Table S3). The theoretical isoelectric points (pI) spanned from 6.2 to 8.37, with four members exhibiting basic pI values (> 7.0). All HvnTGA proteins demonstrated instability coefficients exceeding 40, indicating relatively unstable structures. Their average hydrophilicity coefficients ranged from − 0.614 to -0.189, suggesting an overall hydrophilic nature. Subcellular localization predictions consistently indicated nuclear localization for all HvnTGA proteins.

Based on prior identification results, this study performed a phylogenetic analysis of TGA family proteins across five plant species, including A. thaliana [31], O. sativa [3], G. Max [32], Z. mays [33] and Qingke (Fig. 1). Based on the phylogenetic relationships depicted in Fig. 1, TGA family proteins resolve into five distinct clades. Clade II constitutes the largest group, containing four HvnTGA proteins, while Clades I, III, IV, and V comprise one, one, two, and two member (s), respectively. Integrated with functional data from model plants (e.g., A. thaliana, O. sativa), this evolutionary framework offers insights into the potential functions of HvnTGAs. However, the specific roles of individual family members and the functional divergence among HvnTGA subfamilies remain to be experimentally validated.

Domain composition, conserved motifs and gene structure of the HvnTGAs

Conservation of bZIP and DOG1 domains across all HvnTGA proteins confirmed their identity as canonical TGA transcription factors (Figure S1). Multiple sequence alignment further confirmed that HvnTGAs exhibited high sequence similarity to OsTGAs, ranging from 47.25% to 90.06%. The HvnTGA proteins contained an N-terminal bZIP domain, a central conserved DOG1 domain, two glutamine-rich acidic domains (QI and QII), and a C-terminal bZIP-D box. The DNA-binding motif within the bZIP domain, which mediates binding of homo-/heterodimers to target sequences, was also identified. Results of PCR amplification using gene-specific primers demonstrated the successful cloning of the three genes HvnTGA2, HvnTGA4, and HvnTGA6 (Figure S2).

MEME-based motif analysis revealed that the distribution and sequence characteristics of the most conserved motifs align well with conserved domains. Notably, motifs 1–5 and 7 exhibit universal presence across all 10 HvnTGA proteins, demonstrating exceptional conservation (Fig. 2A). Domain mapping reveals: motifs 3/7 localize to the bZIP domain, motifs 1/5 to DOG1, and motifs 2/4 to glutamine-rich acidic domains (QI/QII) (Fig. 2C). The rest four motifs, 6, 8, 9, and 10, show differential presence across the HvnTGA family, reflecting domain divergence. Specifically, motif 6 is absent only in HvnTGA5 and HvnTGA8, corresponding to the group D bZIP signature Yx₂RL[RQ]ALSS[LS]W. Meanwhile, motifs 9 and 10 localize exclusively to N-terminal regions, with motif 10 restricted to the evolutionarily clustered HvnTGA1/9/10 members.

Phylogenetic relationship, motif composition, and gene structures of HvnTGAs in Qingke. A Evolutionary relationship and distribution of conserved motifs. B Gene structures. Introns, coding sequences (CDS), and noncoding sequences (UTR) are shown as black lines, yellow boxes, and green boxes, respectively. C Sequence logos corresponding to conserved motifs

The HvnTGA gene family exhibits substantial variation in gene length and structure (Fig. 2B). HvnTGA6 and HvnTGA7 possess the longest sequences (approaching 16 kb), significantly exceeding the other eight members. Exon counts range from minimal (7 exons in HvnTGA4 and HvnTGA5) to maximal (15 exons in HvnTGA7). Given minimal variation in coding region lengths, intron number and size constitute the primary determinants of gene length diversity across this family.

Prediction of tertiary structure for HvnTGAs

The three-dimensional structures of the HvnTGA family proteins were modeled based on A. thaliana TGA7 (8gyz.1.). Structural comparison of HvnTGA monomers with AtTGA7 revealed high similarity between HvnTGA proteins and the template, both exhibiting exclusively α-helical architectures (Fig. 3). The template protein AtTGA7 contains eight α-helical structures, with α1, α2, α6, α7 and α8 being long helices, and α3, α4, and α5 being short helices. Similarly, the secondary structures of the HvnTGA family proteins also consist exclusively of variable numbers of long helices (5–6) and short helices (2–7) (Table S5). The shared three-dimensional architecture and comparable arrangement of long/short helices indicate that HvnTGA proteins maintain a core structural framework with AtTGA7.

Analysis of cis-regulatory elements within HvnTGA gene promoter regions

Computational prediction of cis-regulatory elements revealed that all 10 examined TGA family gene promoters in highland barley contained the essential CAAT-box and TATA-box elements (Fig. 4). Functional classification identified major cis-regulatory elements governing responses to: (i) light signaling, (ii) phytohormones (ABA/JA/SA), (iii) abiotic/biotic stressors, and (iv) developmental regulation. Among these elements, the binding sites for plant hormones mainly include: salicylic acid (TCA-element, as-1), jasmonic acid (TGACG-motif, CGTCA-motif), abscisic acid (ABRE), ethylene (AC-I, AC-II, ARE), gibberellin (GA-motif), and auxin (TGA-element). Several conserved transcription factor binding motifs were also identified, including MYB, MYC, and W-box elements. MYB transcription factor binding sites represented the most abundant cis-regulatory motifs (77 occurrences), followed by ABA-responsive ABRE elements (60 occurrences). Notably, identical cis-elements exhibited significant heterogeneity in distribution frequency across promoters of distinct HvnTGA homologs (Table S6). Overall, the prediction results of cis-regulatory motifs suggest that the transcription of HvnTGAs is synergistically regulated by multiple signals, i.e., plant hormones, environmental cues, and growth and development. However, these potential regulatory relationships still require further validation through promoter analysis.

Expression patterns of the HvnTGA gene family in various Qingke variety and under Bgh pathogen stress

As illustrated in Fig. 5, under powdery mildew infection conditions, 8 out of 10 HvnTGAs exhibited distinct expression patterns, with no detectable expression observed for HvnTGA8 and HvnTGA10. Cluster heatmap analysis revealed that HvnTGA3 and HvnTGA9 formed an independent branch with similar expression profiles, while the remaining six genes clustered into a separate distinct branch. In the resistant cultivar G7, HvnTGA3 and HvnTGA9 maintained consistently high expression levels with minimal fluctuation throughout infection. Conversely, in the susceptible cultivar Z13, both genes showed significant induction at 36 h post-infection (hpi).

Expression profiles of HvnTGA genes in Qingke cultivars responding to powdery mildew infection. HvnTGA gene expression was quantified at multiple time points post-inoculation in resistant (G7) and susceptible (Z13) cultivars. Color gradient reflects log₂(TPM + 1) values

The remaining six genes (HvnTGA1, 2, 4, 5, 6, and 7) constituted a separate expression cluster, displaying both shared and divergent transcriptional responses. Notably, HvnTGA1 showed marked induction in the susceptible Z13 cultivar but maintained relatively stable expression in resistant G7. The other five genes (HvnTGA2, 4, 5, 6, and 7) exhibited universal induction following powdery mildew infection, yet demonstrated distinct temporal expression patterns between cultivars: while reaching synchronized peak expression at 168 hpi in Z13, their maximal induction in G7 occurred asynchronously at either 36, 72, or 168 hpi.

Expression patterns of HvnTGA gene family under hormone treatments (SA, ETH and MeJA)

Given the established role of TGAs in plant immunity via coordinated hormone signaling pathways, we investigated the response patterns of HvnTGAs to hormone treatments. Specifically, the relative expression levels of HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6 in leaves treated with SA, ETH, or MeJA at various time points were analyzed. Results demonstrated that SA suppressed the relative expression of both HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6, reaching a minimum (0.4-fold and 0.3-fold of the control, respectively) at 2 h post-treatment (hpt) (Fig. 6A, D). In contrast, ETH and MeJA significantly upregulated HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6 expression, but their magnitude of induction differed between the genes. As shown in Fig. 6B and E, MeJA elicited a stronger stimulatory effect than ETH. Following MeJA treatment, HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6 expression peaked at 6 hpt and 8 hpt, respectively, both reaching approximately 4-fold the control level (0 h) (Fig. 6C, F). ETH induced a comparatively milder response, with HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6 expression levels reaching 1.3-fold and 2.1-fold of the control at 2 hpt. The above results strongly suggested that HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6 were likely involved in the MeJA-mediated response to fungal disease stress.

Subcellular localization of the HvnTGA proteins

To validate the subcellular localization of HvnTGA proteins, sGFP was fused to the C-terminus of HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6. As shown in Fig. 7, intense green fluorescence is uniformly distributed throughout the N. benthamiana epidermal cells in the 35 S::GFP vector control. In contrast, fluorescence signals were exclusively nuclear-localized in both HvnTGA2-sGFP and HvnTGA6-sGFP fusion constructs. These results confirm the predicted nuclear localization of HvnTGA family proteins, consistent with their canonical function as transcription factors.

Protein-protein interactions of HvnTGA transcription factors

Based on the established mechanism whereby bZIP family proteins form transcriptional regulatory complexes through homodimerization or heterodimerization, this study employed yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays to analyze interactions among HvnTGA2, HvnTGA4, and HvnTGA6 (Fig. 8). As illustrated in Fig. 8A, a strong interaction was observed in the co-transformants of the positive control (pGBKT7-P53 and pGADT7-T), while no interaction was detected between the T antigen and Lam protein, or between the empty vectors. Co-transformation of HvnTGA-pGADT7 or HvnTGA-pGBKT7 recombinant vectors with their corresponding empty vectors showed no interaction (though slight auto-activation was detected for HvnTGA4) (Fig. 8B). Combination tests between HvnTGAs revealed that HvnTGA2 interacted with itself, HvnTGA4, and HvnTGA6. Specifically, HvnTGA2 homodimerization and HvnTGA2-HvnTGA4 heterodimerization exhibited weak interactions, while the HvnTGA2-HvnTGA6 heterodimer showed a strong interaction. Notably, robust interactions were observed for HvnTGA4-HvnTGA6 heterodimerization and HvnTGA6 homodimerization (Fig. 8C). Split-luciferase complementation assay (SLCA) was conducted to further confirm the interactions among HvnTGAs. As shown in Fig. 8D, no luciferase activity was observed in N. benthamiana leaves co-transformed with HvnTGA2-nLUC and HvnTGA2-cLUC. In contrast, strong luciferase activity was detected in the combinations of HvnTGA6-nLUC with HvnTGA6-cLUC, and HvnTGA2-nLUC with HvnTGA6-cLUC (Fig. 8E, F). These findings provide compelling evidence for the specific interactions between HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6, as well as between HvnTGA6 and itself. Comparison of the Y2H and SLCA results reveals that HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6, as well as HvnTGA6 and itself, exhibit strong interactions both in yeast and in plant cells. In contrast, the homodimerization-mediated weak interaction of HvnTGA2 still requires confirmation by pull-down assay or BiFC.

HvnTGA2 silencing and functional characterization in Pyrenophora graminea resistance

To analyze the function of HvnTGA2 in host resistance, the silencing effects of HvnTGA2 on Qingke resistance against P. graminea were analyzed using BSMV-mediated VIGS. The applicability of BSMV-VIGS in hulless barley was assessed through HvnChlH silencing. System efficacy was verified by a characteristic yellow-leaf phenotype coupled with qRT-PCR-confirmed suppression (> 80%) of HvnChlH expression, establishing this VIGS platform as a robust tool for functional genomics in Qingke (Fig. 9A, C, F). Three days post-inoculation with P. graminea, leaves of HvnTGA2-silenced plants displayed pronounced yellowing symptoms, whereas the empty vector control exhibited no significant chlorosis (Fig. 9B). The expression level of HvnPR-4 was quantitatively analyzed, confirming a reduction of about 40% in plants with HvnTGA2 silenced (Fig. 9E). The efficiency of HvnTGA2 silencing was confirmed using semi-quantitative and real-time quantitative PCR analyses, which demonstrated an approximate 80.1% reduction in HvnTGA2 expression in silenced leaves (Fig. 9D, G). DAB and NBT staining showed significantly higher staining intensity in HvnTGA2-silenced leaves compared to the vector control, indicating elevated levels of ROS accumulation (Fig. 9H, I, J, K). These results indicate that HvnTGA2 functions as a regulator of PR gene expression and a modulator of intricate intracellular ROS levels, with its regulatory influence on ROS signaling potentially contributing to the defense response against P. graminea.

Discussion

The barren soils of high-altitude regions, compounded by harsh climatic conditions—including low temperatures, intense solar radiation, and dramatic diurnal temperature fluctuations—pose formidable threats to agricultural production. As one of the few crops capable of producing harvestable yields on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, Qingke cultivation confronts a dual challenge: abiotic stress originating from harsh environments and biotic stress from pathogens. Given the multifaceted roles of TGA TFs in plant stress adaptation and growth regulation, this study aims to elucidate their functional mechanisms in Qingke’s stress responses, particularly in regulating disease resistance.

The advent of whole-genome sequencing technologies has facilitated the comprehensive identification of TGA transcription factors (TFs) across diverse plant species. Comparative genomic analyses demonstrate significant variation in TGA family size, with member numbers ranging from 6 to 44 among different species. Representative cases include: A. thaliana (10 members), Saccharum officinarum (44) [18], Arachis hypogaea (20) [10], Zea mays (19) [33], Fragaria × ananassa (11) [11], Malus domestica (10) [34], Triticum aestivum (38), Triticum dicoccoides (12), and Aegilops tauschii (26) [35]. Given that the genome of Saccharum officinarum is large (6.8 Gb), the expansion of certain gene families, such as TGA, may be closely linked to its genome size in the species.

Typical TGA family proteins harbor two core structural domains: the bZIP domain and the DOG1 domain [36]. The basic region of the bZIP domain serves dual roles as both the mediator of DNA binding and the nuclear localization signal (NLS) for the TGA family. Meanwhile, the leucine zipper segment mediates dimerization among bZIPs. Using double DNA affinity purification-sequencing (DAP-seq), the DNA binding sites of 20 Arabidopsis C/S1 bZIP heterodimers and S1 homodimers were genome-wide profiled [37]. The results demonstrate that 36.7% of the binding events were uniquely attributable to C/S1 heterodimers. Hence, we propose that the binding specificity of distinct HvnTGA homo- or heterodimer combinations likely varies significantly, thereby regulating transcription of divergent target genes and associated biological processes. It is noteworthy that certain TGA transcription factors, despite harboring an incomplete bZIP domain and thus lacking DNA-binding activity, can still modulate the transcriptional activity of their heterodimeric counterparts through protein-protein interactions. For example, in Solanum tuberosum, miniTGA (StTGA2.1) [38], which lacks the N-terminal bZIP sequence, cannot bind DNA directly but forms functional heterodimers with StTGA2.2 and StTGA2.3. Specifically, the StTGA2.1–StTGA2.3 heterodimer pair enhances the transcriptional activation of StPRX07, a class III peroxidase. These findings collectively suggest that the transcriptional regulatory mechanisms of TGA family proteins, mediated by both homodimers and heterodimers, exhibit significant complexity.

Comparative analysis of HvnTGA family protein sequences reveals higher sequence divergence within the N-terminal region. The significance of the N-terminal domain for the overall functionality of TGA family proteins has been validated through protein truncation experiments. Specifically, microinjection of tobacco TGA1a lacking its N-terminal 80 amino acids resulted in complete loss of transcriptional activation capacity [39]. The regulation of the N-terminal sequence on the transcriptional activity of TGA family proteins also involves post-transcriptional regulation, particularly phosphorylation. However, the impact of phosphorylation modifications on the transcriptional function of TGA proteins is inconsistent, which may be related to differences in the phosphorylation modification sites. For instance, the DNA-binding activity of tobacco TGA2.2 shows phosphatase sensitivity, suggesting that phosphorylation generally enhances its DNA-binding capacity [40]. However, Kang and Klessig [41] (2005) reported an opposing regulatory mechanism, where phosphorylation of specific serine and threonine residues in the N-terminal domain of TGA2 actually suppresses its DNA-binding activity. Unlike the N-terminal region, the C-terminal domain of HvnTGAs exhibits higher conservation and harbors both a DOG1 (Delay of Germination 1) domain and two glutamine-rich acidic domains (QI and QII). Although the DOG1 domain lacks DNA-binding capacity, it critically contributes to TGA protein functionality—likely through mediating protein-protein interactions or maintaining structural stability [42]. QI and QII, conversely, possess demonstrated transcriptional activation activity and play a pivotal regulatory role in specifically controlling PR-1 gene expression [43]. Functional characterization of poplar PpTGA9 [44], a positive regulator of salt stress tolerance, revealed that its QI and QII domains mediate the interaction with PpATP1/SDIRIP1. However, the molecular mechanism underlying how the PpTGA9-PpATP1 complex regulates the salt stress response remains to be elucidated. Indeed, while extensive phenotypic evidence has established the critical roles of TGA family proteins in plant stress resistance [45]and growth regulation [46], the domain-specific mechanisms governing their regulatory functions are still poorly understood. For example, recent studies have shown that silencing TaTGA13 in wheat enhances resistance to Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici, and TaTGA13 [47] interacts with TraesCS1A02G369800, a putative serine/threonine-protein kinase STN7 ortholog. Moreover, overexpression of wheat TaTGA16-2D [48] significantly improves drought and heat tolerance in Arabidopsis. Given the essential roles of the highly variable N-terminal region and the C-terminal domain in protein–protein interactions, transcriptional activation, and associated biological functions [49], these domains warrant focused investigation in future functional studies of TGA family proteins.

The promoter is a core genomic region that drives and regulates gene transcription, whose functionality is collectively shaped by diverse factors. Promoter analysis of the Qingke HvnTGA gene family identified seven cis-acting element categories: light-responsive, hormone-responsive, stress-responsive, circadian-regulated, growth/development-related, transcription factor binding sites, and functionally uncharacterized elements. The presence of these regulatory motifs suggests that HvnTGA genes are involved in phytohormone-mediated growth and stress response pathways. Further expression profiling demonstrated that both HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6 exhibit transcriptional responsiveness to hormonal stimuli. Both ETH and MeJA significantly upregulated the expression of HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6. Moreover, the majority of HvnTGAs demonstrated distinct expression patterns in the G7 resistant and Z13 susceptible cultivars upon infection with Blumeria graminis f. sp. hordei (Bgh). Current research indicates that the expression of clade II TGA genes [7] (represented by A. thaliana AtTGA2/5/6), which are key to systemic resistance regulation, is primarily induced by salicylic acid or bacterial pathogens. However, a systematic analysis across different species reveals that their expression patterns can be different. For instance, while AcTGA06 in Actinidia chinensis [50] is induced by infection with Pseudomonas syringae pv. actinidiae, its expression is suppressed by methyl salicylate; conversely, 1 mM salicylic acid treatment has no significant effect on the expression of CpTGA2 in Carica papaya [51].

During host-pathogen interaction, ROS serves a dual role: it acts as a signaling molecule to regulate downstream immune responses, and a localized ROS burst at the infection site can trigger cell death to restrict pathogen spread [52]. Silencing of HvnTGA2 compromised Qingke’s resistance to P. graminea and led to increased ROS accumulation, highlighting the critical link between ROS and TGA-related immune signaling. TGA family transcription factors not only directly regulate intracellular ROS levels but also serve as crucial signaling modules that respond to ROS signals to activate downstream defense genes. A recent study demonstrated that overexpression of Gossypium hirsutum GhTGA12 promotes ROS accumulation and positively regulates the expression of PR genes during SAR activation [53]. While in response to UV-B stress, AtTGA2/5/6 have been identified as transcription regulators that modulate the expression of glutathione S-transferase Tau subfamily (GSTU) genes, thereby facilitating ROS scavenging [54]. Similarly, the elevated ROS levels observed in HvnTGA2-silenced plants may be attributed to compromised ROS-scavenging capacity, a hypothesis that warrants further experimental validation. Apart from their ROS regulatory functions, the structure and transcriptional activity of TGA proteins are closely linked to the intracellular redox state, particularly in Arabidopsis clade I TGAs containing four cysteine residues [55]. Cysteine mutations directly impair their interaction with NPR family proteins [56].

Plant resistance to pathogen invasion is predominantly mediated by PR genes, whose transcriptional regulation is directly governed by the coordinated interplay of TGA and NPR family proteins. Notably, the interaction between NPR1 and TGA2 has been documented across multiple plant species, including Arabidopsis thaliana [43], lily [57], and Gladiolus hybridus [58]. In tomato, silencing TGA2.2, TGA1a, or NPR1 [59] increased susceptibility to the bacterial pathogen Ralstonia solanacearum. In the cereal model plant Brachypodium distachyon, PR-1 transcription—cooperatively activated by BdTGA1 and BdNPR2—is suppressed by BdNPR1. Additionally, TGA family transcription factors play essential roles in N-hydroxypipecolic acid (NHP)-mediated systemic acquired resistance (SAR) [60]. Genetic evidence shows complete abolition of NHP-induced SAR in tga2/5/6 triple knockouts, whereas tga1/4 double knockouts display only partially compromised SAR induction. The SA-mediated SAR was also attenuated in these mutants, thus demonstrating the critical role of TGA family proteins in systemic immunity. In tomato-virus interactions, TGA2-related signaling has been identified as a critical regulatory hub, with TEOSINTE BRANCHED1/CYCLOIDEA/PCF (TCP) transcription factors activating TGA2 transcription. The C2 protein of Tomato yellow leaf curl virus (TYLCV) interferes with this pathway by directly interacting with and inhibiting the transcriptional activity of TCP transcription factors. In this study, BSMV-mediated silencing of HvnTGA2 significantly increased Qingke’s susceptibility to P. graminea, demonstrating its pivotal role in resistance regulation. Additionally, expression of HvnTGA genes, particularly HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6, was induced by ETH and MeJA, suggesting these genes may function as important regulators in barley-fungal interactions (Fig. 10).

Conclusions

This study identified 10 HvnTGA genes in hulless barley, phylogenetically classified into five clades. The encoded HvnTGA proteins exhibit conserved TGA family features, including structural domains, motif compositions, exon-intron architectures, and three-dimensional structures. Cis-regulatory analysis revealed that HvnTGA promoters are enriched with elements linked to stress responses, growth/development, and hormone pathways, implicating their involvement in these biological processes. While all HvnTGAs were predicted to localize in the nucleus, only HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6 underwent experimental verification. HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6 expression was significantly induced by ETH and MeJA, but moderately suppressed by SA. Yeast two-hybrid assays demonstrated homodimerization of HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6, along with heterodimeric interactions between HvnTGA2-HvnTGA4, HvnTGA2-HvnTGA6, and HvnTGA4-HvnTGA6. Split luciferase complementation assay (SLCA) further confirmed the heterodimeric interaction between HvnTGA2 and HvnTGA6, as well as the homodimerization of HvnTGA6. Silencing HvnTGA2 compromised Qingke barley resistance to P. graminea, revealing the pivotal role of HvnTGA transcription factors in plant immunity.
