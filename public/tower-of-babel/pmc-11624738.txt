Plasma arginine as a predictive biomarker for outcomes with immune checkpoint inhibition in metastatic colorectal cancer: a correlative analysis of the CCTG CO.26 trial
Authors: Lucy X Ma, Emma Titmuss, Jonathan M Loree, Derek J Jonker, Hagen F Kennecke, Scott Berry, Felix Couture, Chaudhary E Ahmad, John R Goffin, Petr Kavan, Mohammed Harb, Bruce Colwell, Setareh Samimi, Benoit Samson, Tahir Abbas, Nathalie Aucoin, Francine Aubin, Sheryl Koski, Dongsheng Tu, Christopher O’Callaghan, Eric X Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNutritional stress is a mechanism that allows tumor cells to evade the immune system. Arginine (ARG), an amino acid involved in immunomodulation, aids in regulating T-lymphocyte cell activity and the antitumor response. ARG deficiency in the tumor microenvironment can impair T-cell response while ARG supplementation may promote antitumor immune activity. In this exploratory post hoc analysis of the randomized phase II CO.26 trial, we investigated the role of plasma ARG in predicting response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) in patients with microsatellite stable refractory metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC).MethodsCO.26 randomized patients with refractory mCRC to durvalumab plus tremelimumab (D+T) versus best supportive care (BSC). Plasma ARG concentrations were determined from pretreatment blood samples using high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. The median plasma ARG value was used as a cut-off stratifying patients into ARG-high (≥10 700 ng/mL) versus ARG-low (<10 700 ng/mL) groups. Overall survival (OS) was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazard models were used to analyze the prognostic and predictive impacts of ARG on OS.ResultsOf 180 patients enrolled in CO.26, 161 (N=114 treated with D+T and 47 BSC) had pretreatment blood samples for ARG analysis. There were no significant differences in baseline characteristics between patients included in this analysis and the total study patients, or between ARG-high and ARG-low patients. In the BSC arm, the median OS was 3.09 months for ARG-high versus 4.27 months for ARG-low patients (univariable HR 0.89 (0.49–1.65), p=0.72). In the D+T arm, the median OS was 7.62 months for ARG-high versus 5.27 months for ARG-low patients (univariable HR 0.68, (0.48–1.0], p=0.048). In ARG-high patients, D+T significantly improved OS (median OS 7.62 months with D+T vs 3.09 months BSC; HR 0.61 (0.37–0.99), p=0.047; adjusted p=0.042 for interaction). In ARG-low patients there was no OS benefit with D+T (median OS 5.27 months D+T vs 4.27 months BSC; HR 0.87 (0.52–1.46), p=0.61).ConclusionHigh baseline plasma ARG was predictive of improved OS in patients with mCRC treated with D+T. Further investigations are needed to validate ARG as a biomarker. Therapeutic approaches targeting the ARG pathway may augment ICI activity.Trial registration numberNCT02870920.Keywords: Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors, Colorectal Cancer, Biomarker

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionized the treatment of solid tumor malignancies, introducing the possibility of durable long-term response and improved survival in patients with metastatic disease. Agents targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), and anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen 4 are now a cornerstone of treatment in many solid tumor malignancies such as melanoma, lung, renal cell, and hepatocellular cancers.1,6

For patients with colorectal cancer, the KEYNOTE-177 trial demonstrated that the PD-1 inhibitor, pembrolizumab, has durable antitumor activity and decreased toxicity compared with cytotoxic chemotherapy in those with mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR) or microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) tumors.7 However, this patient population only comprises approximately 5% of colorectal cancers.7,10 The benefit of ICIs in patients with mismatch repair proficient (pMMR) or microsatellite stable (MSS) colorectal cancer has been more attenuated. The phase II Canadian Cancer Trials Group CO.26 trial randomized patients with refractory colorectal cancer who were predominantly MSS to tremelimumab and durvalumab versus best supportive care (BSC) and found that combined ICIs statistically significantly prolonged overall survival (OS) in this setting; however, the magnitude of benefit was modest (median OS 6.6 vs 4.1 months, HR 0.72).11

In pMMR/MSS colorectal cancer, there is a paucity of reliable biomarkers to guide patient selection and predict treatment response to ICIs. Emerging studies show that cellular metabolomics holds the potential for predicting response to ICIs, as nutritional stress is one of the mechanisms used by tumor cells to evade immune surveillance. Arginine (ARG), an amino acid involved in several cellular functions including immunomodulation, is important in regulating T-lymphocyte cell activity and the antitumor response.12,14 A recent study examining circulating ARG levels in patients with advanced cancers treated with ICIs showed that low baseline ARG levels were significantly associated with worse treatment outcomes and OS.15 In this exploratory post hoc analysis of the CO.26 trial, we investigated pretreatment plasma ARG as a predictor of response to ICIs in patients with advanced MSS colorectal cancer.

Methods

Patients and study design

The CO.26 study design, eligibility, and treatments have been published previously.11 Briefly, patients aged 18 and older with advanced adenocarcinoma of the colon or rectum refractory to all standard systemic therapies were randomized in a 2:1 ratio to receive durvalumab (1500 mg intravenously every 28 days) plus tremelimumab (75 mg intravenously every 28 days for the first four cycles) (D+T) and BSC or BSC alone. Study treatments continued until radiological or clinical evidence of disease progression, intolerable toxicity, withdrawal of consent, or death. The primary endpoint was OS (defined as the time from randomization to death from any cause). Secondary endpoints included progression-free survival (time from randomization to first objective documentation of disease progression or death from any cause).

Procedures

Blood samples were collected prior to the start of study therapy. Plasma ARG concentrations were determined using high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. The median plasma ARG value was used as a cut-off stratifying patients into ARG-high (≥ 10 700 ng/mL) versus ARG-low (< 10 700 ng/mL) groups.

Statistical analysis

OS was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared between groups using the log-rank test (performed in R V.3.6.3 using packages survival (V.3.1.8) and survminer (V.0.4.7)). Cox proportional hazard models were used to analyze the prognostic and predictive impacts of ARG on OS. Multivariable analysis to adjust for baseline patient characteristics (presence of liver metastases, plasma tumor mutational burden (pTMB)) was conducted. Sensitivity analyses were performed using (1) mean plasma ARG and (2) minimum p value approach to define the cut-off values dichotomizing ARG-high and ARG-low patients.

Results

CO.26 included 180 patients, with 119 patients assigned to D+T and 61 patients to BSC. Pretreatment plasma samples were available from a total of 161 patients, 114/119 (96%) and 47/61 (77%) that received D+T and BSC respectively. There were no significant differences in baseline characteristics between patients included in this analysis and the total study population (online supplemental table 1). Patient demographics and baseline characteristics were similar between ARG-high and ARG-low groups (table 1). There was no association between plasma ARG or prior therapies received by patients (online supplemental table 2). In agreement with the initial published trial results, D+T was associated with improved OS in the 161 patients with available baseline plasma (univariable HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.49 to 1.01, p=0.053; median 6.62 months vs 3.61 months).

Prognostic analysis of OS by baseline plasma ARG level

In patients randomized to BSC alone, the median OS was 3.09 months for ARG-high versus 4.27 months for ARG-low patients (univariable HR 0.89 (0.49–1.65), p=0.72). In patients treated with D+T, the median OS was 7.62 months in ARG-high versus 5.27 months in ARG-low patients (univariable HR 0.68, CI 0.46 to 1.0, p=0.048).

Predictive analysis of OS by baseline plasma ARG level

In the ARG-high group, median OS was significantly higher in patients receiving D+T compared with BSC alone (7.62 vs 3.09 months, HR 0.61 (95% CI 0.37 to 0.99), p=0.047; test of interaction adjusted p=0.042; figure 1 and table 2). In the ARG-low group, median OS was not significantly different between D+T and BSC treatment groups (5.27 vs 4.27 months, HR 0.87 (95% CI 0.52 to 1.46), p=0.61; figure 1 and table 2).

Discussion

This exploratory biomarker analysis of the CO.26 trial demonstrated that among patients with advanced colorectal cancer treated with D+T, those with high baseline plasma ARG levels had significantly improved OS whereas patients with low baseline plasma ARG did not benefit from combined immune checkpoint blockade. Our analysis is the first study examining plasma ARG levels as a predictor of outcomes in patients with colorectal cancer treated with ICI.

Currently, dMMR/MSI-H is the only widely accepted biomarker guiding the use of ICI in colorectal cancers. However, even MMR/MSI status has imperfect predictive capability. The reported ORR of pembrolizumab from the phase III KEYNOTE-177 trial was 44%, meaning less than half of patients with MSI-H had a partial or complete treatment response.8 The percentage of patients with progressive disease as the best response to pembrolizumab was 29%, indicating that primary resistance to immunotherapy can occur even among previously untreated dMMR/MSI-H tumors. Moreover, only a small minority (∼5%) of advanced colorectal cancers are dMMR/MSI-H. Meanwhile, as seen in the CO.26 study, some patients with MSS tumors stand to benefit from ICIs. In a recent secondary analysis of the CO.26 trial, the presence of liver metastases was associated with poorer outcomes in patients treated with ICIs.16 This finding was also supported by the recently reported phase III LEAP-017 trial which compared pembrolizumab and lenvatinib with the standard of care in previously treated MSS colorectal cancer.17 There remains an urgent need to identify additional biomarkers to guide patient selection for ICI treatment.

Several other potential predictive biomarkers for immunotherapy have been proposed, the most common of which include tumor mutational burden (TMB) and PD-L1 expression. The phase II KEYNOTE-158 trial demonstrated higher response rates to pembrolizumab in patients with solid tumor cancer with high tissue TMB (prespecified as≥10 muts/Mb), leading to the US Food and Drug Administration’s approval of pembrolizumab as a tissue-agnostic treatment for high TMB tumors.18 19 Notably, patients with colon cancer were not included in KEYNOTE-158. In the CO.26 trial, patients with plasma TMB of 28 muts/Mb had the greatest OS benefit from D+T (HR 0.34, p=0.004). However, across other studies in colorectal cancer, the correlation between TMB and ICI treatment outcomes has been inconsistent, and cut-offs to define high TMB have varied.1120,23 Similarly, while PD-L1 expression is used to guide treatment decisions in other solid tumor malignancies, testing platforms and classification criteria lack standardization and its role as a predictive biomarker in colorectal cancer remains unproven.

Both tissue TMB and PD-L1 face the challenge of spatial and temporal tumor heterogeneity, especially following exposure to other lines of therapy. Pretreatment archival tissue samples may inadequately capture the comprehensive tumor and immune landscape at the time of ICI treatment consideration. Repeat biopsy of a progressive tumor is not always safe or feasible and requires patients to undergo an invasive procedure. The wait time for the procedure and biopsy sample analysis delays the initiation of subsequent treatment. Thus, the development of plasma-based biomarkers would be valuable. Cellular metabolomics could serve as a quicker, less invasive test for a real-time depiction of the cancer’s characteristics, with the potential for predicting response to treatment.

ARG metabolism has for decades been hypothesized as a critical pathway for modulating immune cell function, with studies showing that ARG starvation inhibits T cell lymphocyte activation and proliferation in vitro.24 In the tumor microenvironment, ARG depletion by arginase-expressing myeloid cells impairs T cell responsiveness by mechanisms including downregulation of the CD3zeta subunit of the T cell receptor complex and decreased production of cytokines such as IFN-γ.12 14 25 26 Supplementation of ARG to T cells has shown potential in increasing antitumor immune activity.13 Our study supports that plasma ARG is a promising predictive biomarker for response to ICI in colorectal cancer which warrants further investigation.

The importance of ARG in immune regulation may extend beyond its predictive role for ICI and open potential therapeutic avenues. Oral arginase inhibitors have been investigated as single agent or combination therapy with ICIs (NCT02903914; NCT03910530). Other investigative approaches include the development of peptide vaccines targeting arginase-1 to induce immune responses, and T cell engineering to express ARG resynthesis enzymes in concert with chimeric antigen receptors (CAR) to enhance the activity of CAR-T cell therapies.27 28

Our study has limitations, including its retrospective nature as an unplanned exploratory analysis of the CO.26 trial. There is a potential risk of Type I error increase due to multiple hypothesis testing. We were unable to directly assess the ARG level in the tumor microenvironment, plasma ARG was used as a surrogate measure instead. Additionally, not all patients had baseline plasma available for ARG analysis, particularly in the BSC treatment group. Although OS was numerically lower in the BSC arm for ARG-high patients, the number of patients in this group was too low to definitively establish ARG-high as a poor prognostic marker. For our patient cohort, we did not have Immunoscore data, which is another emerging biomarker of prognosis and ICI response. In future validation studies, it would be of interest to also investigate the Immunoscore and its correlation with plasma ARG and ICI benefit. Nevertheless, our analysis represents the first study supporting the potential of plasma ARG as a predictive biomarker for treatment outcomes in patients with metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) receiving ICIs. Given the intricate interplay between tumor and the immune system, it is unlikely that any single biomarker will be able to perfectly predict patient outcomes. However, plasma ARG shows promise as an inexpensive test that can be easily conducted and interpreted by clinicians in conjunction with other factors. Our results underscore the need for future prospective studies to validate the predictive role of plasma ARG in colorectal cancer and explore therapeutic approaches targeting the ARG pathway.

Conclusion

Baseline plasma ARG had predictive value in patients with mCRC treated with durvalumab and tremelimumab. Prospective studies are needed to validate ARG as a biomarker for identifying patients with colorectal cancer likely to benefit from ICIs. Investigative approaches in therapeutic development targeting the ARG pathway should be considered.
