Augmented renal clearance in neurocritical patients: An epidemiological investigation and risk-factor analysis
Authors: Qile XIAO, Bohan LUO, Hainan ZHANG, Xiaomei WU, Liu SONG
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAugmented renal clearance (ARC), in contrast to renal dysfunction, refers to enhanced renal elimination of circulating solutes compared to the expected baseline. Although patients may present with normal serum creatinine (Scr) levels, the incidence of ARC is high in intensive care unit (ICU) settings. ARC is associated with subtherapeutic exposure and treatment failure of renally cleared antibiotics. However, limited research exists on the incidence and risk factors of ARC in the ICU, and even fewer data are available specifically for neurological ICU (NICU). This study aims to determine the incidence and risk factors of ARC in neurocritically ill patients.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed all available Scr data of neurocritical care patients admitted to the NICU of the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University between December 2020 and January 2023. Creatinine clearance (CrCl) was calculated using the Cockcroft-Gault equation. ARC was defined as a CrCl≥130 mL/(min·1.73 m2) sustained for more than 50% of the duration of the NICU stay. A total of 208 neurocritically ill patients were assigned into an ARC group (n=52) and a non-ARC (N-ARC) group (n=156). Clinical characteristics were compared between the 2 groups. Variables with P<0.05 in univariate analysis were included in binary Logistic regression to identify independent risk factors for ARC.ResultsThe incidence of ARC among neurocritically ill patients was 25.00%. Of the 74 patients with normal CrCl, 20 (27.03%) gradually developed ARC during hospitalization. Compared with the N-ARC group, the patients of the ARC group were younger (P<0.001), with a higher proportion of females (P=0.048) and a lower admission mean arterial pressure (MAP) (P=0.034). Moreover, patients of the ARC group were commonly complicated with severe bacterial infections compared with the patients of the N-ARC group (P<0.001). In binary Logistic regression analysis, younger age (OR=0.903, 95% CI 0.872 to 0.935) and severe bacterial infections (OR=6.270, 95% CI 2.568 to 15.310) were significant predictors of ARC.ConclusionARC is relatively common in the NICU. A considerable number of patients with initially normal renal function developed ARC during hospitalization. Younger age and concurrent severe bacterial infection are important risk factors of ARC in neurocritically ill patients.Keywords: augmented renal clearance, incidence, risk factors, neurocritical illness, severe bacterial infections

Augmented renal clearance (ARC) refers to enhanced solute elimination in patients compared with expected baseline, a process that involves changes in glomerular filtration and renal tubular function[1]. ARC is a fairly new concept without standard definition. Creatinine clearance (CrCl) ≥130 mL/(min·1.73 m2) is generally considered to be the diagnostic criterion for ARC, as most pharmacokinetics (PK) studies[2-4] have shown that it is associated with subexposure to antibiotics. Baptista, et al[5] developed a more stringent definition of ARC as a CrCl ≥130 mL/(min·1.73 m2) more than 50% of the time during intensive care unit (ICU) stay. ARC may be associated with systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) caused by stress response such as surgery, trauma, burns and sepsis. ARC may be linked with mobilization of renal function reserve (RFR) due to some physiological and pathological stimuli. In some cases, the 2 mechanisms may work together to promote the occurrence of ARC. In patients with larger RFR, such as younger patients, when SIRS occurs, the release of cytokines and pro-inflammatory mediators leads to the cardiovascular characteristics of “high output and low resistance”. In addition, active fluid replacement, commonly used positive inotropic drugs and vascular pressor agents in critically ill patients further increase cardiac output (CO) and effective circulatory blood volume, which may increase renal blood flow and glomerular filtration rate (GFR)[6-8]. It is speculated that critically ill patients are at high risk for ARC. The results of the epidemiological investigation[9] for ARC have shown that the prevalence of ARC in mixed medical/surgical ICU was 18% to 80%.

For mixed medical/surgical cohorts, younger age was identified as the most important risk factor for ARC through both univariate and multivariate analyses[10-14]. Other studies have indicated that lower illness severity[4, 10, 13, 15], trauma[3-4, 10, 16-17], and febrile neutropenia[18] are also significantly associated with ARC. Other factors that may contribute to ARC have been identified in univariate analysis but not subsequently confirmed in multivariate analysis, including male sex[3, 11-12], mechanical ventilation[2, 14], higher cardiac index[3, 19], use of vasopressor[14, 20], higher diastolic blood pressure[21], and less use of furosemide[14, 16].

Recent studies have shown that the prevalence of ARC in patients with central nervous system bacterial infection (25.0% to 47.0%)[22-24], hemorrhagic stroke such as intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) (50.0%)[25] and subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) (94.0% to 100.0%)[25-26], and traumatic brain injury (TBI) (83.0% to 88.0%)[27-28] is significantly higher compared to that in mixed-cohort ICU patients. Autonomic nervous system dysfunction after brain injury[29], neuroendocrine factors[27], and the “mutual interference” between renal and cerebral perfusion resulting in autonomic regulation disorder[28] may be the specific causes of ARC in neurocritically ill patients. Data from neurological ICUs (NICUs) has been highly scarce. More recently, the progressive enhancement of CrCl over time in ICH or SAH patients was observed in NICU[25]. In addition, multivariate Logistic regression analysis in a disease-heterogeneous ICU population showed that ICH diagnosis was a predictor of ARC[10]. These findings are essential for neurocritically ill patients, who are typically thought to be less likely to experience ARC since they are generally older and more likely to have comorbid conditions that cause renal dysfunction, such as hypertension and diabetes.

PK changes in antimicrobial therapy when ARC occurs may have a significant impact on clinical outcomes in ICU. This is particularly important for drugs that are primarily cleared by the kidney and whose renal clearance is directly correlated with CrCl, such as beta-lactams, vancomycin, and aminoglycosides. Enhanced drug clearance results in a shortened half-life, decreased peak concentrations, and reduced area under the curve over 24 hours (AUC24), which may directly compromise the efficacy of drugs and lead to treatment failure[30].

Predicting the population at high risk of ARC is extremely challenging since ARC may be associated with various factors. There is a notable lack of large-sample epidemiological investigations and risk-factor analyses of ARC from NICU. Whether specific risk factors for ARC exist independently in neurocritically ill patients compared to other types of critically ill patients remains unclear. This study aims to determine the prevalence and risk factors of ARC in NICU.

Subjects and methods

Study design and data collection

The data of neurocritically ill patients admitted to the NICU of the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University from December 2020 to January 2023 were collected retrospectively through the medical records system. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) Admission to NICU; 2) Age ≥18 years old. Patients were eligible if they met both of these criteria. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) Absolute renal impairment [including serum creatinine (Scr) ≥133 μmol/L on admission, acute kidney injury (AKI) after admission, need for renal replacement therapy during hospitalization, and evidence of renal disease by renal ultrasound, imaging, or renal biopsy during hospitalization, previous history of renal replacement therapy or chronic kidney disease]; 2) Pregnant patients; 3) Main diagnosis was non-neurological disease; 4) Height or body weight (BW) of patients were not recorded in the medical records system; 5) Scr was monitored less than 3 times during NICU stay. Patients who met any of these criteria were excluded. A total of 208 patients with complete data were eventually included in the study for statistical analysis. This study was approved by the ethics committee of the Institutional Review Board at the Second Xiangya Hospital [Research Project # (2022) Ethical Review (Clinical Research) No. (018)]. The Ethics Committee waived the requirement for written informed consent.

Definition

Neurocritical diagnosis

Patients requiring ICU life support, monitoring, and treatment due to acute cerebrovascular disease, encephalitis/meningitis, severe TBI, intracranial tumor, status epilepticus, or other neurological diseases were classified as neurocritically ill patients.

ARC

ARC was defined as CrCl ≥130 mL/(min·1.73 m2) for more than 50% of the duration of ICU stay, as proposed by Baptista, et al[5]. The remaining patients were defined as non-ARC (N-ARC) patients. In addition, continuous ARC was defined as CrCl ≥ 130 mL/(min·1.73 m2) throughout NICU stay. Sporadic ARC was defined as at least one episode of CrCl ≥ 130 mL/(min·1.73 m2) during NICU stay, but not continuous ARC. CrCl was calculated using Cockcroft-Gault formula as follows: CrCl=[(140-age)×BW]/(Scr×0.818).

The CrCl of female patients was equal to the calculation result of this formula ×0.85.

Severe bacterial infections

Severe bacterial infections were defined as clinical sepsis (even in the absence of a proven source of infection) or evidence of positive microbial cultures of blood, cerebrospinal fluid or tissue, urine, peritoneal fluid or tissue, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid or superior tracheal aspirates[4].

Demographics and clinical characteristics collection

Demographics characteristics were collected retrospectively, which included gender, age, height, BW, and body mass index (BMI). Clinical characteristics were also recorded retrospectively, which included main diagnosis on admission, mean arterial pressure (MAP), Scr, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) Ⅱ score, and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score on admission, CrCl on admission, the highest CrCl during hospitalization, the highest CrCl occurrence time, ARC duration, presence of emergency surgery, severe bacterial infections, mechanical ventilation, fluid therapy, use of vasopressor/inotropic drugs, hypertonic dehydration drugs and diuretics. In addition, we recorded length of NICU stay as well as NICU outcome.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis were performed using SPSS 26.0. Continuous data following normal distribution were expressed as mean±standard deviation, and the Student’s t-test was used for comparison between groups. The continuous data with non-normal distribution were represented by median (1st quartile, 3rd quartile), and compared between groups using the Mann-Whitney U test. Nominal data were reported as number or percentage, and the χ 2 test was used for comparison between groups. For variables that were significant in univariate analysis, binary Logistic regression was performed to calculate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) to determine the significant risk factors for ARC. A two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Prevalence of ARC

Overall, 52 patients met the ARC diagnostic criteria, and the prevalence of ARC was 25.00% (52/208). During hospitalization the incidence of sporadic ARC was 21.63% (45/208), while that of continuous ARC was 14.42% (30/208). The prevalence of ARC in hemorrhagic stroke patients was 19.30% (11/57), among which the prevalence of ARC in ICH patients was 19.61% (10/51) and that in SAH patients was 16.67% (1/6). ARC occurred in 42.31% (11/26) of patients with encephalitis/meningitis and in 24.00% (30/125) of patients with other diagnosis (Table 1). Furthermore, among 74 patients with normal CrCl on admission, 27.03% (20/74) gradually developed ARC during their hospitalization. The median (1st quartile, 3rd quartile) duration of ARC was 12.00 (6.00, 19.75) d, and 57.69% (30/52) of ARC patients showed persistent ARC throughout their hospitalization.

Clinical characteristics of ARC patients

Comparisons of demographics, circulation, renal function, admission diagnosis, comorbidities, illness severity, treatment, and clinical outcomes between the ARC patients and N-ARC patients are shown in Table 2. ARC patients had a median Scr on admission of 45.90 μmol/L. Their median CrCl on admission was 142.50 mL/(min·1.73 m2), while the highest CrCl and mean CrCl during hospitalization were 183.70 mL/(min·1.73 m2) and 157.15 mL/(min·1.73 m2), respectively, all significantly higher than that of N-ARC patients (all P<0.001). The highest CrCl appeared on the 5th and 7th day of admission in the N-ARC group and the ARC group, respectively, without statistically significant difference (P=0.282). Univariate analysis showed that ARC patients tended to be younger (P<0.001), with a higher proportion of females (P=0.048) and a lower admission MAP (P=0.034). Moreover, compared with N-ARC patients, ARC patients were commonly complicated with severe bacterial infections (P<0.001). Although ARC patients were more likely to be admitted with a diagnosis of hemorrhagic stroke or encephalitis/meningitis and to experience emergency surgery, the difference were not statistically significant (all P>0.05). In addition, there were no statistically significant differences between the 2 groups in illness severity score, treatment, and NICU mortality (all P>0.05). However, ARC patients had significantly longer NICU stays than N-ARC patients, with a median length of NICU stay of 14.00 d vs 7 d (P<0.001).

Younger age and severe bacterial infections were risk factors associated with ARC. Discussion

In this study, ARC is a relatively common phenomenon in neurocritically ill patients with normal Scr. According to ARC diagnostic criteria proposed by Baptista, et al[5], the prevalence of ARC was 25.00%. Those studies reporting higher rates of ARC tended to include more patients admitted for trauma or emergency surgery[2, 19], who were younger, and both trauma and age were associated with ARC[3, 10]. In contrast, sepsis or septic shock increases the risk not only for ARC but also for AKI[7, 10]. Therefore, the incidence of ARC in such patients may be relatively low. In addition, the definite criterion of ARC has not yet been unified. CrCl≥ 130 mL/(min·1.73 m2) has generally been regarded as the diagnostic threshold for ARC, as it has been associated with subtherapeutic exposure in PK studies[2-4]. However, lower [120 mL/(min·1.73 m2)][16, 26] or higher [150 mL/(min·1.73 m2)][31] diagnostic thresholds also affected the investigation of ARC prevalence. Importantly, several studies[11, 19] defined ARC patients as those with CrCl≥ 130 mL/(min·1.73 m2) only one episode during their hospitalization, which is not rigorous and increased ARC incidence.

In this study, the median CrCl of ARC patients on the first day of admission was 142.50 mL/(min·1.73 m2). The highest CrCl occurred on day 7.00(3.25, 15.00), with a median value of 183.70 mL/(min·1.73 m2). Upon reviewing the medical records system, most patients were not continuously monitored for CrCl during the first week of admission in this study. Consequently, changes in CrCl over a consecutive week could not be documented, leaving uncertainty regarding whether ARC patients experienced consistent improvements in CrCl following admission. A multicenter, prospective, observational study[2] indicated that a high proportion of ARC patients presented with persistently elevated CrCl during their first week of admission and the presence of ARC on the first day strongly predicted a sustained increase in CrCl during the first week of ICU stay. In addition, 27.03% of patients with normal renal function on admission developed ARC during their hospitalization in this study. A similar phenomenon was reported in a study conducted by Fuster-Lluch, et al[21], where some patients with normal CrCl on admission gradually developed ARC over time. These changes in CrCl may be explained by increased renal excretion of CrCl and drugs in critically ill patients due to the hyperdynamic state of the circulatory system caused by SIRS, aggressive fluid rehydration, and the use of hypertensive and cardiotonic drugs[6-8].

Some studies[11, 19] measured CrCl only on the first day of admission, so the duration and persistence of ARC could not be assessed. In this study, CrCl data for all patients throughout their entire hospitalization period were systematically collected. ARC duration was observed to be longer in neurocritically ill patients, with a median of 12.00 d and 57.69% of ARC patients maintained a CrCl of ≥130 mL/(min·1.73 m2) throughout their hospitalization. These are crucial findings when considering antibiotic therapy. It is challenging for ARC patients to achieve established PD targets with antibiotics excreted through the kidneys, which may be associated with worse clinical outcomes. A retrospective study[32] included patients with severe sepsis indicated that ARC is associated with longer ICU length of stay. Furthermore, a prospective observational study[33] of ICU patients demonstrated that ARC is a significant factor increasing the risk of vancomycin therapeutic failure (AUC24<400 mg·h/L). The univariate analysis of this study showed that the ARC group had a significantly longer hospital stay than the N-ARC group, although no difference in NICU mortality was observed between the 2 groups. Overall, these data highlight the association between ARC and poorer clinical outcomes, underscoring the importance of identifying ARC patients for appropriate drug dosing adjustment.

Previous studies[10-14] have reported ARC in association with various factors, but most of these data are from mixed medical/surgical cohorts. Data for this study were derived from NICU, which included subgroups of patients with different types of neurocritical disorders. In univariate analysis, ARC patients were younger in this study, with a higher proportion of females and a lower admission MAP. Furthermore, compared with N-ARC patients, ARC patients were commonly complicated with severe bacterial infections. Finally, younger age and severe bacterial infections were risk factors for ARC by binary Logistic regression analysis in this study.

Younger patients have the potential to develop ARC, which has been identified in numerous studies[10-14]. The results of this study is similar to those of previous studies. This phenomenon can be explained by the fact that younger patients tend to have higher RFR. Certain physiological or pathological conditions, such as increased protein loading and SIRS caused by various stresses, leading to an increased number of “functional” glomeruli and elevated renal blood flow, ultimately enhancing CrCl[8, 14, 16]. The proportion of females in the ARC group was higher, which is inconsistent with previous studies[3, 11-12]. Some studies[3, 11-12] have reported that male sex is a risk factor for ARC, although it is not confirmed in multivariate analysis. In fact, RFR is larger in male patients, similar to younger patients who are more likely to develop ARC[11-12]. The reason for our opposite finding may be that the female patients were younger than the male patients, although the difference was not statistically significant. However, younger age was a strong predictor of ARC in both univariate and multivariate analysis.

Univariate and multivariate analysis showed that severe bacterial infections was a strong predictor of ARC. The physiological processes driving the increase in CrCl may stem primarily from underlying inflammatory state. The release of cytokines and pro-inflammatory mediators will lead to the increase of CO and the decrease of systemic vascular resistance. In an experimental model of Gram-negative sepsis, this hyperdynamic circulation is associated with increased blood flow to major organs, including renal blood flow, leading to enhanced solute delivery to the kidneys. This may contribute to the higher clearance observed in sepsis patients without renal dysfunction[6-7]. It has been suggested that the use of vasoactive drugs and high-volume fluid resuscitation may further exacerbate this change[34-35]. However, there were no statistically significant differences in the need for vasopressors or fluid balance between the 2 groups. Since we did not monitor CO in enrolled patients, it is unclear whether these interventions might indirectly increase CrCl by enhancing CO. More recently, it has been claimed that the increase in GFR may also reflect a direct consequence of inflammatory processes, independent of hemodynamic changes[36]. By inducing systemic inflammatory responses to healthy volunteers with intravenous injections of bacterial endotoxins, Beunders R[36] found that the pro-inflammatory mediators are firmly associated with an increase in GFR induced by endotoxemia, but not with blood pressure. Logistic regression analysis conducted in our research identified younger age and severe bacterial infections are strong predictors of ARC, suggesting that ARC may represent an adaptive physiological response to systemic inflammatory conditions, particularly in younger patients with a greater RFR.

Interestingly, Nei, et al10] found that ICH diagnosis is one of the risk factors that predicted ARC in a group of disease-heterogeneous ICU populations; however, no direct association between hemorrhagic stroke diagnosis and ARC was observed in this study. This may be explained by the fact that only 14 ICH patients were included in the study conducted by Nei, et al[10], and the small sample size may have led to selection bias and limited the ability to identify risk factors. In addition, according to previous findings, it is unclear whether the disease itself or the ICU interventions experienced by hemorrhagic stroke patients, or both, promote the occurrence of ARC. On the one hand, basic studies on “brain-kidney response axis”[28], “neuroendocrine factors”[37] and “autonomic nervous system dysfunction after brain injury”[29], which speculated that the regulation mechanisms of cerebral and renal vascular bed interfered with each other, supported the promoting effect of the disease itself on the occurrence of ARC. On the other hand, cardiovascular hyperdynamic states caused by the use of vasopressors and hypertonic solutions to manage cerebral perfusion pressure[20] and volume expansion in patients with cerebral vasospasm[36] have been confirmed to be related to the occurrence of ARC. Nonetheless, the elevated prevalence of ARC among hemorrhagic stroke patients presents a significant clinical challenge, given that associated infectious complications not only adversely affect patient prognosis but also substantially escalate healthcare expenditures[38-39].

A prospective study[21] on 89 critically ill patients showed that diastolic blood pressure was significantly higher in ARC patients than in N-ARC patients; MAP also tended to be higher in the ARC group. They speculate that both higher MAP and diastolic blood pressure promote renal perfusion, leading to the increase of CrCl. However, opposite results were obtained in our study. In this study MAP was significantly lower in the ARC group than in the N-ARC group. As a matter of fact, neurocritically ill patients are more likely to be complicated with hypertension, and their MAP is significantly higher than that of previously reported patients. The automatic regulation of renal blood flow and glomerular filtration is highly complex and involves multiple factors, including myogenic autoregulation and tube-bulb feedback responses, as well as other unspecified mechanisms[40]. It is hypothesized that excessively high MAP induces a dual regulatory response in renal hemodynamics. Primarily, elevated MAP increases intravascular hydrostatic pressure, triggering myogenic autoregulatory mechanisms that, induce afferent glomerular arteriolar vasoconstruction, thereby decrease renal perfusion. Secondarily, the elevated MAP enhances glomerular filtration rate and reduces proximal tubule reabsorption. This hemodynamic alteration increases tubular flow rate subsequently elevating the concentration of sodium chloride at the macula densa. The results trigger constriction of entricular arterioles, which serves to restore glomerular filtration rate and maintain autoregulation of renal blood flow. In addition, there is evidence that sympathetic hyperactivity is particularly severe in the kidneys of the patients with brain injury[41]. Those with the high MAP may reflect overactive sympathetic activity, which has a strong effect on the microvascular system of the kidney, possibly leading to a reduction in GFR.

Previous studies[13, 15] have reported an association between ARC and lower disease severity. It has been speculated that patients with lower disease severity may have greater organ function reserve. Although a trend toward higher GCS scores was observed in the ARC group compared to the N-ARC group, no significant differences were found in APACHE II scores or SOFA scores between the 2 groups in this study. In fact, the APACHE II assesses the severity of illness based on physiological measures, age, and previous health status. The SOFA evaluates the severity of illness by grading organ dysfunction. It is possible that neither of these 2 scoring systems can adequately reflect the classification of disease severity in neurocritically ill patients. Neurocritical specialty scoring systems with greater sensitivity and specificity are warranted for screening patients at high risk for ARC.

In conclusion, the prevalence of ARC is relatively high in neurocritically ill patients. A considerable proportion of patients with normal renal function on admission may gradually develop ARC during their hospitalization. Younger age and severe bacterial infections are significant predictors of ARC.

The limitations of this study are as follows. First, the retrospective design of the single-center study may introduce selection bias. Notably, a significant proportion of patients were excluded due to missing variables of interest. The small sample size limits its ability to detect risk factors for ARC. Second, most neurocritically ill patients were not continuously monitored for Scr during hospitalization, resulting in the inability to calculate daily CrCl, thereby preventing us from accurately characterizing changes in CrCl over time and assessing its persistence. Third, the majority of neurocritically ill patients received one or more antimicrobial agents, thereby raising the possibility that nephrotoxic effects of these agents may have influenced renal function. Finally, the CrCl calculated using the Cockcroft-Gault equation tends to be lower and less accurate compared to CrCl determined through urine collection, potentially leading to an underestimation of the incidence of ARC. The gold standard for determining GFR is CrCl measured by inulin clearance; however, this technique is rarely used in routine clinical practice due to its complexity.
