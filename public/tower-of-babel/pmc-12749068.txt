First-principles calculations to explore the structural stability, elastic, thermal, and opto-electronic properties of Mg-Cu-Y ternary compounds
Authors: Hao Xu, Quan Dong, Tao Chen, Shijun Tong, Yunxuan Zhou, Jun Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ab-initio research has been used in this work to investigate the structural stability, elastic, electronic, thermal, and optical properties of Mg-Cu-Y compounds. The Mg-Cu-Y compounds’ thermodynamic stability is guaranteed by their negative cohesive energy (Ecoh) and formation enthalpy (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta H_{r}$$\end{document}), with the MgCu4Y phase having the Ecoh of − 4.464 eV/atom and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta H_{r}$$\end{document} of − 0.187 eV/atom. The determined elastic constants validate the mechanical stability of the Mg-Cu-Y compounds under research by meeting Born’s stability requirements. High resistance to axial tensile/compressive deformation and shear deformation is further guaranteed by the MgCu4Y phase’s greater Young’s and shear moduli compared to other ternary phases. In addition, the calculated electronic bonding information indicates that the d-band center of Cu atoms contributes significantly to the bonding energy and mechanical properties of their compounds. Moreover, in the Mg-Cu-Y ternary compounds, the relatively low magnesium content and shorter weighted average bond lengths contribute to the higher modulus observed in Mg2Cu9Y and MgCu4Y phases. In addition, the Mg2Cu9Y phase has the melting point of 1699.26 K. The MgCu4Y phase exhibits a relatively high minimum thermal conductivity in all the Mg-Cu-Y ternary compounds, measuring 1.03 W m−1K−1, whereas the MgCu2Y2 phase has a comparatively low value of 0.56 W m−1K−1. Lastly, the Mg2Cu9Y phase exhibits optical conductivity at a wavelength of approximately 2163 nm, with a value of approximately 37.5 1/fs.

Introduction

Reaching peak carbon dioxide emissions and carbon neutrality is a crucial tactic for combating climate change and safeguarding the planet’s biological processes1–3. Accelerating progress toward this goal is an essential path for driving the green transformation of the global economy and society4,5. In the transportation sector-such as aircraft, high-speed trains, and new energy vehicles-the development of lightweight and high-strength structural materials is crucial for improving energy efficiency and reducing emissions6–8. Magnesium alloys offer promising potential in these areas due to their high specific strength (higher than aluminum alloys and steel), excellent vibration-damping capacity (over 25 times that of aluminum alloys), good thermal conductivity, electromagnetic shielding ability, low cost, and minimal environmental impact9–11. These advantages make magnesium alloys attractive candidates for lightweight structural materials and carbon-negative applications12–14. However, their insufficient strength and stiffness constrain their broader utilization as structural materials in transportation, aerospace, and related engineering fields15,16. Consequently, the development of magnesium alloys with high strength and high modulus to promote energy savings and emission reductions in the transportation industry has emerged as a key area of materials science research within the general objective of a low-carbon economy17,18.

The family of metallic materials known as magnesium-based amorphous alloys is distinguished by its long-range disordered and short-range ordered atomic structures. Their low density, high strength, superior resistance to corrosion, and favorable biocompatibility make them highly promising for use in energy storage, biomedical domains, and structural applications19–21. As is well known, amorphous alloys are generally in a metastable state and tend to transform into crystalline phases under heat or pressure. The mechanical characteristics of the alloys may be significantly impacted by this change. In a variety of sectors, alloys and compounds in the RE-TM-Mg ternary system-where TM stands for transition metals and RE for rare earth metals-have drawn more and more interest22. Their crystal chemistry, chemical bonding, and key characteristics have been extensively studied and summarized by Rodewald and colleagues23. The RE-TM-Mg ternary system includes the Mg-Cu-Y alloy as one of its typical ternary alloys24–26. Matsuura et al., found not only an amorphous structure but also a 14H long-period stacking ordered (LPSO) structure in the rapidly solidified Mg98Cu1Y1 alloy27. Additionally, Kawamura et al., reported the presence of an 18R LPSO structure in Mg97Cu2Y1 alloy, which remained unchanged after annealing, indicating good thermal stability28. The Mg-Cu-Y alloy has been reported to contain a number of ternary phases, including Y2Cu2Mg, YCu9Mg2, Y5Cu5Mg8, Y5Cu5Mg13, Y5Cu5Mg16, YCuMg4, and YCuMg. These phases can be defined as YxCuyMgz based on the Y/Cu atomic ratio. Solokha et al., analyzed and discussed the Mg-Cu-Y ternary phases’ crystallographic features and structure types. The relevant data provided can be used for thermodynamic modeling and phase diagram calculations29. Much research has been done on the binary systems Mg-Cu, Mg-Y, and Cu-Y in the Mg-Cu-Y ternary system. For example, in the Mg-Y alloy system, three typical intermetallic compounds, MgY, Mg2Y, and Mg24Y5, have been identified. Utilizing density functional theory-based first-principles computations, Zhou et al., have examined their crystal structures, electrical properties, thermodynamic behavior, and mechanical characteristics, and the results indicated that Mg2Y exhibits the lowest elastic anisotropy and better linear compressive resistance30. Furthermore, Chen et al. synthesized Mg-Y binary alloys by a controlled diffusion solidification process and methodically examined the evolution of the mechanical properties, microstructure, and thermodynamic mechanism. The results indicated that this process significantly refines the grain size of Mg-Y alloys, with the grain refinement becoming more pronounced as the degree of superheating decreases31. Jeong et al., demonstrated that controlling the formation of intermetallic compounds in Mg–Cu binary alloys can effectively alter both their color and hardness32. Furthermore, by modifying the Cu content, Xu et al., were able to regulate the morphology and corrosion characteristics of their Mg–Cu alloys33. The structural stability and mechanical properties of ternary compounds (including Mg-Al-X (X = Si, Sn, Mn, Pb) and Mg-Y-Zn ternary compounds) have also been studied in our previous work34–37. In addition, Özer et al., investigated the structural, elastic, and thermodynamic characteristics of MgXY2 compounds (X = Zn, Cd; Y = Ag, Au, Cu). The results showed that these materials do not exhibit sudden hardening under applied stress and maintain mechanical stabilities, indicating their potential for applications in underwater vehicles and high-temperature thermal barrier coatings38. However, the structural stability, phase formation behavior, and mechanical characteristics of Mg-Cu-Y ternary compounds remain unclear. These characteristics are crucial for the practical application of the alloys. Clarifying the structural stability and phase formation behavior of Mg-Cu-Y ternary compounds can therefore serve as a theoretical foundation for designing the composition and optimizing the process of new magnesium-based alloys. It can also encourage the development of high-performance magnesium alloys to satisfy the industry’s need for materials that are both lightweight and high-strength.

Methodology section

The density functional theory (DFT) as it was implemented in the Cambridge Serial Total Energy Package (CASTEP) code served as the foundation for all first-principles computations39. For the exchange–correlation functional, the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof for solid (PBEsol) and ultra-soft pseudopotentials (USPPs) were applied40,41. After a rigorous convergence test, Mg-Cu-Y compounds were assigned a plane-wave kinetic energy cutoff of 450 eV7. Moreover, the 6 × 6 × 2, 5 × 5 × 5, 4 × 4 × 7, 5 × 5 × 8, 1 × 6 × 3, 6 × 1 × 1, 6 × 1 × 1, and 2 × 1 × 6 k-mesh were chosen for Mg2Cu9Y, MgCu4Y, MgCu2Y2, MgCuY, Mg8Cu5Y5, Mg13Cu5Y5, Mg16Cu5Y5, and Mg4CuY phases, respectively. Additionally, the valence electrons of the Mg, Cu, and Y atoms were employed as 2p63s2, 3d104s1, and 4d15s2. In addition, the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (BFGS) relaxation algorithm was used to relax the crystal structure to obtain a reasonable structure and atomic position42. The structure was completely relaxed when the forces acting on each atom were less than 0.03 eV/Å and the energy difference between two consecutive ionic relaxation stages was less than 10−6 eV.

Results and discussion

Structural stability

All results in this study are derived from idealized zero-temperature DFT simulations. Important real-world effects, such as finite-temperature behavior, phase transitions, kinetic stability, defects, and the synthesis feasibility, have not been considered. In addition, the exchange–correlation functional employed in this work (PBEsol) has certain limitations, and no related experimental validation has been conducted. The stability and structural details of the Mg-Cu-Y compounds are investigated in this work. It can be found that the proportion of Mg atoms in both Mg2Cu9Y and MgCu4Y compounds is 16.67%, while the proportion of Mg atoms in the Mg4CuY compound is 66.67%. Moreover, Mg16Cu5Y5 has the highest number of 104 atoms, of which the Mg, Cu, and Y atoms are 64, 20, and 20, respectively. In addition, the content of the Y atom is less than 40% in all the compounds. As can be seen from Fig. 1 and Table 2, there are four different crystal structures, namely hexagonal (Mg2Cu9Y and MgCuY phases), cubic (MgCu4Y phase), tetragonal (MgCu2Y2 phase), and orthorhombic (Mg8Cu5Y5, Mg13Cu5Y5, Mg16Cu5Y5, and Mg4CuY phases), respectively. Moreover, depending on the kind of crystal structure, the atoms’ positions, and their arrangement, Mg and Cu, Mg and Y atoms will form polyhedra in the Mg-Cu-Y system. According to crystallographic information and space group symmetry theory, Mg2Cu9Y belongs to the P63/mmc space group and has six distinct Wyckoff positions, that is 2a, 2b, 2c, and 12k for Cu atoms, 2d for Y atoms, and 4f for Mg atoms. MgCu4Y is an F4̅3m space group with cubic crystals and possesses 4a (Mg atoms), 4c (Y atoms), and 16e (Cu atoms) Wyckoff positions. MgCu2Y2 is a tetragonal crystal structure with a P4/mbm space group, where the Wyckoff sites of Mg, Cu, and Y atoms are 2a, 4g, and 4h, respectively. MgCuY phase is a hexagonal structure of the P6̅2m space group with four different Wyckoff sites, namely, 1a and 2d for Cu atoms, 3f for Y atoms, and 3g for Mg atoms. In addition, although Mg8Cu5Y5, Mg13Cu5Y5, Mg16Cu5Y5, and Mg4CuY are all orthorhombic crystal structures, their space groups are different, the first phase is Pmma space group, the second and third phases are Cmcm space group, and the last phase is Cmmm space group. The lattice constants before and after relaxing are found to change very little, with a maximum rate of change of about 4.43%. In addition, the optimized lattice parameters of the Mg-Cu-Y ternary compounds obtained from first-principles calculations exhibit good agreement with the corresponding experimental values29,43–45, thereby confirming the reliability of the computational method, as shown in Table 2. For instance, the calculated lattice constants of cubic MgCu4Y phase are a = b = c = 7.185 Å, which closely match the experimental values of a = b = c = 7.231 Å45, with a relative deviation of less than 1%. Similarly, for hexagonal Mg2Cu9Y phase, the computed lattice constants (a = b = 5.001 Å, c = 15.978 Å) are in close agreement with experimental measurements (a = b = 5.004 Å, c = 16.203 Å)43, indicating a deviation of less than 1.5%. Furthermore, the calculated values for hexagonal MgCuY phase (a = b = 7.384 Å, c = 4.036 Å) also agree well with experimental data (a = b = 7.444 Å, c = 3.995 Å)45. For more complex tetragonal MgCu2Y2 and orthorhombic phases such as Mg8Cu5Y5, Mg13Cu5Y5, Mg16Cu5Y5, and Mg4CuY phases, the computed lattice constants are likewise within a reasonable range of deviation (typically less than 2%) when compared to multiple reported experimental datasets29,45. These minor discrepancies are mainly attributed to the limitations of the exchange–correlation functional and the absence of thermal effects in zero-temperature DFT simulations. It can be detected from Fig. 2b and d that the △Hr and Ecoh vary depending on the composition of Mg, Cu, and Y, in which, the blue color represents lower values of △Hr or Ecoh, while the red color indicates higher values of them. A lower value suggests greater thermodynamic stability of the Mg-Cu-Y ternary phase, whereas a higher value implies poorer thermodynamic stability. The two-dimensional contour plots also make it evident that the regions appear blue when the Mg concentration is low (Cu content is high) and red when the Mg content is higher (Cu content is low). This indicates that the Mg-Cu-Y ternary compounds with lower Mg content exhibit higher thermodynamic stability. That means the MgCu4Y phase has the lowest △Hr and Ecoh, which are − 0.187 and − 4.464 eV/atom, respectively. In contrast, the Mg4CuY phase has the highest △Hr and Ecoh values, which are − 0.112 and − 2.992 eV/atom, respectively, demonstrating that, in comparison to other Mg-Cu-Y ternary compounds, the Mg4CuY phase has relatively low thermodynamic stability. This enhanced stability may be associated with the stronger metallic bonding between Cu and Y atoms. When the Mg content decreases, the compound structure tends to be dominated by stronger Cu-Y bonds, which are characterized by shorter weighted average bond lengths, thereby contributing to more stable chemical bonding. In addition, the electronic structure also plays a significant role. In the Mg-Cu-Y system, charge transfer occurs among the constituent elements, and the resulting redistribution of charge density further stabilizes the bonding environment, leading to a lower formation enthalpy and hence greater thermodynamic stability. A more detailed discussion is provided in Sect. “Electronic characteristics and chemical bonding”. In addition, phase stability predictions provide essential guidance for the selection of phases and the development of appropriate heat treatment processes. By identifying thermodynamically stable phases and evaluating their melting points and thermal robustness, it becomes possible to design alloy compositions that promote the formation of stable secondary phases, such as high-temperature strengthening precipitates. Mechanical properties and elastic anisotropy

Materials’ mechanical qualities have a direct impact on the dependability, durability, and safety of engineering structures and functional devices in real-world applications, making them important indicators for evaluation and design. By using the fundamental ideas of quantum mechanics to solve the Schrödinger equation, first-principles calculations are able to anticipate a material’s mechanical properties based on its electronic distribution and atomic structure. This approach can reveal the microscopic origins of mechanical properties, such as interatomic bonding strength, lattice stability, and the influence of defects on mechanical behavior. Elastic constants are essential characteristics for describing a material’s mechanical behavior because they explain the relationship between stress and strain in that material. They provide insights into the material’s stiffness, anisotropy, and stability. Electronic characteristics and chemical bonding

The mechanical characteristics of materials are inherently linked to electronic information, including band structure, charge distribution, and electronic structure. Chemical bonding between atoms fundamentally determines a material’s mechanical properties, and the electronic structure directly reflects bonding features (such as covalent, metallic, and ionic bonds). First-principles calculations, by revealing the mechanisms at the electronic level, provide a theoretical foundation for material design. Figures 6 and 7 in this work display the computed charge densities and density of state densities of Mg-Cu-Y compounds. From Fig. 6a, the density of states (DOS) varies in the energy range from − 44 to 5 eV. There is a relatively high peak near the energy of approximately − 42 eV, with the main contribution to the DOS coming from the Mg-p orbitals. The total DOS near the Fermi level (EF, dashed red line) also varies, which is due to the difference in the total number of atoms in different crystal structures. In the DOS diagrams, the EF intersects continuous energy bands, namely, the DOS value at EF is non-zero, confirming that all the compounds exhibit metallic characteristics. Generally, the DOS near the EF is a key “window” for understanding the thermal and mechanical properties of materials, as it determines the electronic states that actively participate in dynamic processes and is directly related to most macroscopic properties. Therefore, this study primarily focuses on the variation of the DOS near the EF, and to explore the relationship between atomic orbitals, a portion of the DOS is zoomed in (− 10 eV–5 eV), as shown in Fig. 6b–i. The presence of strong Cu-Y metallic bonding contributes to the relatively high elastic moduli observed in these compounds. The cohesive energy, elastic constants, and other structural properties of transition metals are mainly related to d-electrons. In Mg-Cu-Y compounds, changes in the DOS are usually primarily associated with d-band shifts caused by interactions between different Cu and Y metal atoms. It is evident that the d-band center of the Mg2Cu9Y and MgCu4Y phases, which have a high Cu content, is quite broad. However, as the Cu content decreases, the d-band center becomes narrower. The reflectivity of Mg-Cu-Y compounds in Mg alloys is closely related to their electromagnetic shielding effectiveness especially in the shielding of high-frequency electromagnetic waves, where reflection is one of the dominant shielding mechanisms. In general, materials with high reflectivity exhibit stronger electromagnetic wave reflection, and therefore possess better reflection-dominated electromagnetic shielding performance. Furthermore, the results in Fig. 11a reveal that the Mg2Cu9Y phase exhibits a higher reflectivity of 1.01 at approximately 98 nm, while the MgCu4Y phase shows a lower reflectivity of 0.96 at approximately 128 nm, indicating that the electromagnetic shielding effectiveness of the former is more pronounced compared to the latter. Light absorption is the process in which the energy of light is captured by a medium during its interaction with matter and converted into other forms, such as thermal energy or electronic excitation. It can be seen from Fig. 11b that the characteristic peaks of these Mg-Cu-Y compounds are different, with the peaks of the Mg2Cu9Y (2.57 × 105 cm−1 at 200 nm for the first peak and approximately 2.58 × 105 cm−1 at 953 nm for the second peak) phase being particularly distinct. An absorption coefficient of 2.57 × 105 cm−1 is observed for the Mg2Cu9Y phase at a wavelength of around 206 nm, indicating strong ultraviolet absorption behavior. Additionally, there is also a second characteristic peak near the wavelength of 953 nm, whose absorption value is approximately 2.58 × 105 cm−1. The absorption peaks of the compounds Mg13Cu5Y5 and Mg16Cu5Y5 are not very distinct and are relatively low. The slope of the MgCu4Y phase in the wavelength range of 500–1500 nm is noticeably steeper than that of the other compounds, indicating a more significant decrease in its absorption coefficient. After 1500 nm, the absorption coefficient approaches zero, and a similar trend is observed in the other compounds as well. In other word, as the wavelength increases, the absorption values gradually decrease and eventually converge in the longer wavelength range, indicating that the material becomes transparent in this region73. The loss function quantifies the energy absorption and scattering losses of light as it propagates through a medium. A greater comprehension of the material’s optical response, including refraction, absorption, and scattering, is possible by correlating the real and imaginary parts of the dielectric function. This information is vital for the design of photonic device. The illustration in Fig. 11c shows the loss function of Mg-Cu-Y ternary compounds, and the entire ternary compounds exhibit only one energy loss function peak. The calculated results illustrate that the MgCu4Y, Mg4CuY, and Mg8Cu5Y5 phases exhibit distinct loss function characteristic peaks in the range of 125–175 nm (ultraviolet region). In addition, a relatively small peak appears for MgCu2Y2 phase around the wavelength of 110 nm, and a characteristic peak is also observed for Mg16Cu5Y5 phase near 210 nm. Therefore, Mg4CuY and Mg8Cu5Y5 possess better optical storage properties compared to the other compounds in Mg-Cu-Y system. In addition, to understand the light propagation, attenuation or phase velocity and directional changes when interacting with the material, values of refractive index are estimated and discussed. Figure 11d shows the refractive index of Mg-Cu-Y ternary compounds, with the real part represented by n (solid line) and the imaginary part represented by k (dashed line). Additionally, the greater penetration ability means smaller reflectivity can be identified as refractive index goes high and vice versa. The refractive index is very low in the ultraviolet region but very high in the infrared region, indicating that the electromagnetic shielding mechanism is mainly based on reflection in the infrared region and absorption in the ultraviolet region. In the wavelength range above 1000 nm, both the real and imaginary parts of the refractive index of the Mg2Cu9Y phase exhibit relatively high refractive indices compared to other ternary compounds. Analyzing optical conductivity is essential for understanding how materials transport charge and dissipate energy under the influence of electromagnetic waves. It acts as a key link in understanding the interaction between light and matter, providing valuable insights for applications in photonic devices, optoelectronics, and material design tailored for specific optical responses. Optical conductivity is closely connected to parameters such as the dielectric function, refractive index, and other optical properties. The real part of the optical conductivity is associated with the material’s ability to conduct and absorb energy from the electromagnetic wave, reflecting its dissipation characteristics. The imaginary part is related to the energy loss due to absorption, which contributes to heating or electronic excitation. Its imaginary part of optical conductivity reaches a maximum value of approximately 24 1/fs at a wavelength of approximately 1100 nm. The dielectric function is an important physical quantity that describes a material’s response to an electromagnetic field. Its analysis involves understanding the interaction mechanisms between light and matter, such as electronic transitions, lattice vibrations, and others. The real part of the dielectric function characterizes the material’s polarization ability (phase delay), while the imaginary part represents energy absorption. Additionally, in the wavelength range above 1000 nm, the imaginary part of the dielectric function of the Mg2Cu9Y phase is much higher than that of the other compounds. Conclusions

The existing effort is focused to explore the structural stability, mechanical, electronic, thermodynamic, and optical features of the titled Mg-Cu-Y compounds through the DFT versatile method utilizing the CASTEP code. The investigated results elaborated that the formation enthalpies of all Mg-Cu-Y compounds are negative, suggesting that these phases are thermodynamically stable. Moreover, the formation enthalpies and cohesive enthalpies of the MgCu4Y phase are − 0.187 eV/atom and − 4.464 eV/atom, respectively, while those of the Mg4CuY phase are − 0.112 eV/atom and − 2.992 eV/atom. These results suggest that MgCu4Y exhibits better thermodynamic stability compared to Mg4CuY. Ductile and brittleness toughness performance are judged from the study of Poisson’s and Pugh’s ratio, and they follow the ranking order as follows: MgCu2Y2 > Mg2Cu9Y > MgCuY > Mg4CuY > Mg16Cu5Y5 > Mg8Cu5Y5 > MgCu4Y > Mg13Cu5Y5. Elastic anisotropic manner is ensured from several anisotropic factors analysis and also from 3 and 2D plots of Young’s modulus. The results show that an AU value of 1.41 is observed for the Mg16Cu5Y5 phase, suggesting that this structure possesses significant elastic anisotropy compared to the other investigated phases. According to bulk, shear and Young’s moduli these phases show hard nature. A Young’s modulus of 155.97 GPa is obtained for the MgCu4Y phase, which is greater than that of some binary or ternary phases in other magnesium-based systems. This suggests that it can play a positive role in enhancing the modulus of the alloy. The involvement in the electrical conductivity at the Fermi level is mainly comes from the Mg-p, Cu-d, and Y-d orbitals. Mulliken atomic population and differential charge densities analysis offer the ionic and covalent bonds in studied phases. The calculated extremely minimum thermal conductivity and elevated melting temperature show that the Mg2Cu9Y phase has a melting point of 1699.26 K and the MgCu4Y phase exhibits a relatively high minimum thermal conductivity of 1.03 W m−1K−1 in Mg-Cu-Y system. Moreover, the metallic nature of the Mg-Cu-Y phases is predicted from dielectric function, photoconductivity and absorption spectrum analysis. Lastly, the Mg2Cu9Y phase exhibits a higher reflectivity compared to other ternary phases, showing its good electromagnetic shielding.
