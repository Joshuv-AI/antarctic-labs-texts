Adaptation of Microbialite‐Associated Prokaryotic Communities to Natural and Experimental Anoxic Conditions
Authors: Jeanne Caumartin, Karim Benzerara, Ana Gutiérrez‐Preciado, Miguel Iniesto, Paola Bertolino, Maria Ciobanu, Christophe Thomazo, Robin Havas, Didier Jézéquel, Philippe Deschamps, Rosaluz Tavera, David Moreira, Purificaciόn Lόpez‐Garcίa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMicrobialites are rocks formed by microbial communities that influence, promote, and/or control the precipitation of mineral phases. The oldest microbialites date back to the Archean and formed under globally anoxic conditions. Accordingly, modern microbialites developing under oxic conditions are limited analogs of their earliest predecessors. We discovered microbialites in the deep, seasonally anoxic water column of crater lakes Alchichica and Atexcac (Mexico). We studied the prokaryotic communities of plankton and microbialites from oxic and anoxic layers, alongside the temporal dynamics of microbialite communities incubated under strict continuous anoxia. 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing showed shifts in the relative abundance of taxa under anoxia, rather than changes in overall diversity. Regardless of their original depth, microbialite‐associated communities from oxic and anoxic zones converged after ~25 weeks of laboratory exposure to a N2‐H2‐CO2 (85:10:5) atmosphere. Sequences assigned to likely anoxygenic photosynthetic taxa, notably within the Alpha‐ and Gammaproteobacteria, together with phyla associated with sulfate‐reduction and other sulfur‐cycling bacteria, increased in relative abundance. We isolated seven bacterial strains under anoxia, some of them biomineralizing. Several of these strains were cyanobacteria affiliated with the Leptolyngbyales, suggesting that they either continue to perform oxygenic photosynthesis or, hypothetically, switch to H2S‐dependent anoxygenic photosynthesis. Our results indicate that these microbialite communities adapt to long‐term anoxia (more than 70 weeks) under laboratory conditions, likely sustaining photosynthetic alkalinization and carbonate precipitation under such conditions.

Introduction

Microbialites are organosedimentary structures formed by benthic microbial communities that can induce and/or influence mineral precipitation in certain aquatic ecosystems (Burne and Moore 1987). When laminated, they are referred to as stromatolites. These emblematic geobiological structures bear witness to life‐mineral interactions. Notably, the oldest unambiguous traces of life on Earth are Archean fossil stromatolites dating back to more than 3.4 Ga (Allwood et al. 2006; Schopf et al. 2007; Awramik 1991; Westall et al. 2023). Fossil stromatolites of clear biogenic origin may provide insights about microbial communities prevailing under past conditions. By applying the principle of actualism, modern microbialites can thus offer valuable information for interpreting paleobiologies and reconstructing paleoenvironments (Bosak et al. 2013; Visscher et al. 2020).

Most modern microbialites are carbonate‐based (Chagas et al. 2016). They form through trapping and binding of detrital particles and/or through authigenic mineral precipitation (Dupraz and Visscher 2005; Riding 2000; Grotzinger and Knoll 1999) with some microbial influence (Görgen et al. 2020; Dong et al. 2022; Zhu and Dittrich 2016). Despite this recognized influence, the interplay between environmental geochemistry, microbial communities and their activities remains difficult to disentangle. Understanding the selective forces shaping microbialite communities and how the latter influence their mineral environment in modern ecosystems can in turn inform about past processes of microbialite formation (Bontognali et al. 2012; Ward and Shih 2019).

Microorganisms play fundamental roles in biogeochemical cycles of elements such as carbon, oxygen, and phosphorus, influencing the availability of chemical species in their environment (Olejarz et al. 2021; Lyons et al. 2021), sometimes with global consequences. An emblematic example was the transition from an anoxic to a permanently oxygenated atmosphere (Fournier et al. 2021; Schirrmeister et al. 2015). Before ~2.4 Ga, the oceans and the atmosphere were largely anoxic (Lyons et al. 2009). Surface ecosystems were likely dominated by phototrophic microbial mats, where autotrophic metabolisms, notably photosynthesis, alongside methanogenesis among others (Davín et al. 2025), fueled the carbon cycle, sometimes promoting carbonate precipitation and forming stromatolites (Tice et al. 2011; Flannery and Walter 2012). Early photosynthesizers were likely anoxygenic, relying on H2S, Fe2+ or H2 as electron donors (Canfield et al. 2006; Ward and Shih 2019; Shen et al. 2001; Kappler et al. 2005). However, some time before 2.4 Ga, the ancestor of the class Cyanobacteriia, phylum Cyanobacteriota (as per GTDB, the Genome Taxonomy Database; Parks et al. 2022), hereafter simply referred to as cyanobacteria, evolved the ability to use H2O as an electron donor, giving rise to the much more efficient oxygenic photosyntesis. Their diversification and expansion contributed to the Great Oxygenation Event (GOE, ~2.4 Ga) (Sánchez‐Baracaldo 2015; Sánchez‐Baracaldo et al. 2022), at a time when the biosphere was exclusively prokaryotic. However, despite the oxygenation of the atmosphere and the surface oceanic layer, most deep oceans remained anoxic throughout the Proterozoic (Lyons et al. 2009). The GOE drastically reshaped paleobiological and paleogeochemical systems. However, the evolution of oxygenic photosynthesis and aerobic respiration did not eliminate earlier anaerobic metabolisms, which persisted in confined anoxic environments. In particular, anoxygenic photosynthesizers continued to evolve and diversify along redox gradients, for example, in stratified water bodies and microbial mats (Gutiérrez‐Preciado et al. 2018).

Here, we study modern microbialites developing under anoxia in two alkaline Mexican lakes, Alchichica and Atexcac, which experience seasonal stratification (Zeyen et al. 2021; Iniesto, Moreira, Benzerara, et al. 2021; Iniesto, Moreira, Reboul, et al. 2021; Iniesto et al. 2022). These monomictic lakes become anoxic below the oxycline during summer–fall (April–October) (Alcocer 2022), that is, with an O2 concentration lower than 0.5 mg L−1 (United States Geological Survey; Coxe et al. 2023; Nürnberg 1995, 2019), and an oxydoreduction potential measured between −100 and −300 mV. Both lakes host extensive carbonate microbialites forming reef‐like structures along their shores; their microbial communities, largely dominated by bacteria, have been studied previously (Kaźmierczak et al. 2011; Zeyen et al. 2021; Iniesto et al. 2022). Here, we report the discovery of microbialites forming at seasonally anoxic depths of these lakes. We characterize the plankton and microbialite prokaryotic community composition in these anoxic layers using 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. To better understand how microbialite‐associated communities respond to seasonal anoxia, we also monitored temporal shifts in community composition from surface and deep microbialite fragments incubated in laboratory mesocosms under continuous anoxia for up to 73 weeks. Finally, to gain insights into the metabolically active microbial fraction in microbialites from anoxic depths, we enriched microorganisms in culture under anoxic conditions. Our work unveils microbialite communities thriving under anoxia, potentially offering improved modern analogs of Precambrian stromatolites.

Materials and Methods

Plankton and Microbialite Sampling

Microbialite and plankton samples were collected from lakes Alchichica (19°25′ N; 97°24′ W) and Atexcac (19°20′ N; 97°27′ W) during two field campaigns (May 2019 and October 2022). Physicochemical parameters were measured in situ along the water column using a YSI EXO2 Multiparameter probe as previously described (Havas et al. 2023; Table S1). For dissolved oxygen measurements, the probe detection limit is 0.03 mg L−1, with a resolution of 0.01 mg L−1 and a precision of 0.1 mg L−1 for the 0%–200% saturation range. Both lakes are monomictic, located in the Trans‐Mexican Volcanic Belt (TMVB, Mexico) and stratify from ~April to ~October, becoming anoxic at depths below ~20–30 m. In October 2022, water samples were collected from six depths in Lake Alchichica (10, 30, 35, 37, 40 and 50 m) and five in Lake Atexcac (0, 10.2, 20.1, 23.3 and 27 m; Figure 1, Table S1). For each depth, five liters of water were sequentially prefiltered through 200 and 30 μm pore‐diameter filters, and planktonic biomass was retained on 0.2 μm pore‐diameter Nucleopore filters. Biomass‐retaining filters were fixed in absolute ethanol and stored at −20°C. In 2019 and 2022, the microbialite samples were collected by divers at six depths for Lake Alchichica (5, 10, 15, 20, 40 m) and three for Lake Atexcac (0, 20 and 27 m; Figure 1, Table S1). Microbialite fragments were either fixed in ethanol for DNA analyses or placed in sterile containers with lake water until they could be settled in laboratory mesocosms. Microbialite fragments collected from anoxic layers were placed in containers fully filled with oxygen‐deprived lake water until their placement in mesocosms. One shallow microbialite sample collected in 2012 at 15 m depth in Lake Alchichica and processed similarly was also analyzed. Sample labels for plankton, microbialites and biofilms samples are noted with the tags “pl,” “mb,” and “bf,” respectively.

Mesocosms and Microbial Isolates Under Continuous Anoxia

Microbialite fragments from lakes Alchichica and Atexcac ranging from ~2 × 2 × 2 cm to ~10 × 10 × 10 cm in size were placed in transparent Plexiglass boxes inside a Vinyl glovebox (CoyLab) under a controlled anoxic N2‐H2‐CO2 (85:10:5) atmosphere. Simultaneous O2 (ppm) and H2 (%) measurements were continuously monitored with a Coy Anaerobic Monitor (CAM‐10) Analyzer (resolution 1 ppm from 0 to 1500 ppm). Anoxia ([O2] < 1 ppm) was always observed during incubation periods. Trace O2 amounts were efficiently removed by a Coy Stak‐Pak hydrogen‐palladium catalyst system placed within the chamber. Microbialites in mesocosms were completely submerged in lake water supplemented, if needed, to compensate for (low) evaporation and maintain conductivity stable over time, with Volvic water (Ca2+ at 0.29 mM, Mg2+ at 0.33 mM, Na2+ at 0.5 mM, K+ at 0.16 mM, SO4 2− at 0.08 mM, HCO3 − at 1.16 mM, NO3 − at 0.1 mM, Cl− at 0.38 mM, and Si at 1.13 mM). In 2021, microbialite fragments from Lake Alchichica collected at 15 m depth (oxic conditions, January 2012; Iniesto, Moreira, Benzerara, et al. 2021; Iniesto, Moreira, Reboul, et al. 2021) and 40 m depth (anoxic conditions; May 2019) previously maintained under their respective redox conditions and exposed to natural light (12 h day/night cycles), were placed in mesocosms in the anaerobic chamber for long‐term monitoring. A 12 h diel cycle was maintained within the chamber, with a low light intensity comparable to that found in deep lake waters. They were subsampled at t = 0, 3, 9, 13, 30, 53, 59, 64, 67 and 73 weeks. Likewise, in October 2022, freshly collected microbialite fragments from anoxic depths (40 m, Lake Alchichica; 27 m, Lake Atexcac) were also placed in mesocosms in the anaerobic chamber, and subsampled at t = 0, 1, 4, 12, 15 and 21 weeks. Samples were frozen at −20°C until processing.

To isolate photosynthetic bacteria growing under anoxic conditions, we inoculated three different agarose‐based media in triplicates in Petri dishes within the anoxic chamber: (i) the BG11 medium classically used to grow cyanobacteria, (ii) Chrom, a specific medium for Chromatium‐type bacteria (Schmidt and Kamen 1970), and (iii) Chlor, a specific medium for Chloroflexi (Zheng et al. 2021). The inoculum consisted of a microbialite fragment collected at a 40 m depth in Lake Alchichica in May 2019 and maintained in lake water in a Falcon tube sealed with a septum at room temperature for 1 year under diel cycles. Five microliters of a cell suspension prepared from this microbialite fragment were inoculated into each medium. Plates were sealed with parafilm and incubated in the anaerobic chamber under diel cycles. Different cell morphotypes were identified in colonies growing on these plates by light microscopy. The BG11, Chrom, and Chlor Petri dishes in which the first colonies appeared after 11 months were opened under anoxic conditions. For each identified morphotype, biomass from an isolated colony was transferred into a new Petri dish containing the same medium. The remaining biomass from individual colonies was suspended in 50 μL of 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, in 0.5 mL Eppendorf tubes to generate lysates for subsequent sequencing of 16S rRNA gene amplicons.

DNA Purification and 16S rRNA Gene Amplicon Sequencing

DNA from microbialites, plankton, and cultures was extracted using the Power Soil DNA Isolation Kit (Qiagen, Germany). Microbialite samples were crushed in an agate mortar before DNA extraction, hydrated in the kit suspension buffer and subjected to 3–5 freeze–thaw cycles before proceeding to DNA purification. For plankton and microbialite samples, 16S rRNA gene fragments of ~970 bp were amplified by PCR using the prokaryote‐specific primers U515F (5′‐GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA) and 1492R (5′‐TACCTTGTTACGACTT). Amplicons were tagged with 10‐nt Molecular Identifiers (MIDs) to allow multiplexing and subsequent sorting of sequences from different samples. The PCR‐amplification reaction (25 μL) contained 0.5–3 μL of eluted DNA, 0.2 mM of deoxynucleotides (dNTP) mix, 0.3 μM of each primer, 1.5 mM MgCl2 and 0.5 U of the hot‐start Platinum Taq Polymerase. Amplification reactions encompassed 35 cycles consisting of three steps: denaturation at 94°C for 30 s, hybridization at 55°C–58°C for 30–45 s, and polymerization at 72°C for 90 s, preceded by a 2‐min denaturation step at 94°C, and followed by five additional minutes of polymerization at 72°C. To eliminate dNTPs and the remaining primers, amplicons were purified with the QIAquick PCR purification kit (Qiagen). Then amplicons were sequenced by Nanopore, following the protocol supplied in the V14 (SQK‐LSK114) kit. To identify microorganisms from culture plates, two different amplification reactions were set using bacteria‐specific primers (B27F (5′‐AGAGTTTGATCATGGCTCA) and 1492R; amplicon size ~1460 bp) and cyanobacteria‐specific primers (CYA106F (5′‐CGGACGGGTGAGTAACGCGTGA) and CYA 1380R (5′‐TCGGTGGTTGTGCCAGTCG; amplicon size ~1270 bp)). As some colonies yielded more than one 16S rRNA gene amplicon (i.e., they did not represent axenic cultures, notably due to the cross‐contamination by strains gliding on the agarose substrate), we cloned these amplicons to identify organisms co‐existing in colonies. To do so, we used the TOPO TA Cloning kit (Invitrogen) following the instructions of the manufacturer. Cloned 16S rRNA gene fragments were amplified and sequenced using vector primers. Amplicons were then Sanger‐sequenced (Azenta, Takeley, UK). Individual Sanger sequences were visualized with the Ugene software (http://ugene.net/).

Sequence Analysis

In total, we obtained 8,130,276 Nanopore 16S rRNA gene amplicon raw sequence reads, which were basecalled on simplex mode with dorado v0.5.1 on super‐accuracy mode with model dna_r10.4.1_e8.2_400bps_sup@v4.2.0. Reads with a Q‐score below 15 were discarded. Demultiplexing was done with a custom script called pyplexer, which also assures all reads are in the same orientation. Reads were then first clustered using CD‐hit (Fu et al. 2012) at 100% identity to group those devoid of Nanopore errors. As expected from Nanopore accuracy, only a limited number of sequences clustered at 100%. Sequences clustering at 100% identity were retained as representative sequences for subsequently defined Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs). The remaining singletons were then clustered with CD‐hit at 95% identity to reconstruct more inclusive OTUs. OTUs were phylogenetically classified via the SINA pipeline using the SILVA database 138.1_SSURef_NR99_12_06_20 as a reference (Quast et al. 2013). However, whenever possible, we adapted the taxa names to follow the Genome Taxonomy Database r207 (GTDB, Parks et al. 2022) as a major taxonomy framework for prokaryotes (providing equivalence names for subsequent GTDB releases for local taxa). Although defined as clusters of (only) > 95% identical Nanopore sequences, OTU phylogenetic assignation was enhanced by the increased information associated with longer reads. We retained here the SILVA taxonomy to classify sequences. OTUs affiliated with chloroplasts and mitochondria were removed from our dataset. Sanger sequences obtained from axenic or enriched cultures were compared by BLAST with those in the non‐redundant GenBank database (NCBI; http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and preliminarily assigned to the taxon of their closest blast hit. OTUs were aligned with those of the closest hits and 16S rRNA gene reference sequences using MAFFT (default parameters; Katoh et al. 2019). Subsequently, phylogenetic trees were inferred from alignments of our sequences with those of the closest hits and 16S rRNA gene reference sequences. Phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using the approximate‐maximum‐likelihood method implemented in FastTree (Price et al. 2009) and visualized with FigTree (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/). Nanopore sequences have been deposited in GenBank under BioProject PRJNA625182, and Sanger sequences, under accession numbers PX000532, PX000533, PX000534, PX000535, PX000536, PX000537, and PX000538.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed on the diversity and relative proportion of taxa inferred from 16S rRNA gene sequences obtained by Nanopore sequencing using R (R Core Team 2024). Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using the “FactoExtra” package (Kassambara and Mundt 2017). Non‐metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) was carried out using the “vegan” package (Oksanen et al. 2013). Degrees of community structure dissimilarity corresponding to Bray‐Curtis (BC) distances were assigned to each sample (Bray and Curtis 1957), based on a Wisconsin standardization of OTUs relative frequencies. Samples were sorted into several groups according to their nature/provenance: microbialites or plankton. Dendrograms were then produced using the “vegan” package in R software.

Results and Discussion

In Situ Microbial Communities

Occurrence of Microbialites in the Seasonally Anoxic Hypolimnion of Lakes Alchichica and Atexcac

In May 2019, the Lake Alchichica water column (maximum depth ~60 m) was anoxic (i.e., with a measured dissolved O2 concentration of 0.05 ± 0.1 mg L−1, and negative ORP) below 20 m, whereas in October 2022, anoxia developed below 36 m depth. Similarly, the Lake Atexcac water column (maximum depth ~35 m) was anoxic below 20 m depth in May 2019 (measured dissolved O2 concentration of 0.05 ± 0.1) and below 23 m in October 2022 (Table S1; Figure 1). These observations confirm the seasonal anoxia previously reported for the hypolimnion from ~April to ~October (Alcocer 2022). In addition, notable differences in dissolved total sulfur concentrations were measured between Alchichica (~10 mM) and Atexcac (~3 mM) water columns. We observed and collected well‐developed, cm‐sized, microbialites in the anoxic depths. Taking into account that the average microbialite growth rates observed in surface microbialites from Lake Alchichica are of ~0.6 mm/year (Iniesto et al. 2022), this implies that these microbialites have been forming for many years and are, therefore, regularly exposed to periods of oxic/anoxic conditions lasting ~6 months/year. When collected, microbialite samples displayed marked color differences depending on their depth. Microbialites from the oxic zone exhibited a bright green coloration, whereas those from deeper, seasonally anoxic layers appeared reddish and/or blackish (Figure 1). Samples collected in 2019 differed in coloration from those collected in 2022, potentially due to local heterogeneities such as variations in light exposure, sampling time, slope and/or sedimentation rate (Figure S1).

Prokaryotic Community Composition in Plankton and Microbialites Along the Stratified Water Column

We characterized the prokaryotic community structure of plankton (cell fraction 0.2–30 μm) and microbialites from oxic and anoxic depths in lakes Alchichica and Atexcac based on 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. In total, we analyzed 15 plankton and 8 microbialite samples (Table S1). We generated ~1000 bp‐long amplicons with universal prokaryotic primers, which were massively sequenced (Nanopore) and used to define OTUs (Table S1). Relative abundances of major prokaryotic taxa were inferred from the frequency and phylogenetic affiliation of OTUs (Figure 2A; Table S2).

The t0‐labeled samples were used to establish the mesocosms in the laboratory (cf 3.2.). (B) NMDS biplot based on the Bray‐Curtis distances between in situ samples (excluding the t0 samples), clearly showing a distinction between planktonic and microbialite prokaryotic communities, and between communities thriving under oxia and anoxia (the outlier dark red dot corresponds to ATX19‐mb‐20m). The stress is 0.064. (C) Hierarchical clustering based on 16S rRNA gene‐based community composition. Plankton and microbialite samples are represented in blue and red, respectively. Plankton communities from the stratified water columns of lakes Alchichica and Atexcac in 2022 exhibited relatively constant composition across samples as compared to the more heterogeneous microbialite‐associated communities (Figure 2A). Biological replicates of Atexcac plankton (ATX samples labeled _01 and _02) were highly similar. As expected, the relative OTU abundances (i.e., proportions of reads, reflecting community composition) varied more markedly than OTU diversity (i.e., number of OTUs) (Figure 2A, Figure S2). Bacteria dominated the planktonic communities, with Gammaproteobacteria and Bacteroidota being dominant taxa, accounting for approximately 27% and 31% of the total normalized relative abundance in Alchichica and Atexcac, respectively. Archaeal abundance remained minimal in all fractions, indicating a marginal role in these systems (Figure 2A; Table S2).

Moreover, a pronounced peak of Alpha‐ and Gammaproteobacteria (Pseudomonadota) was observed at the oxycline in both lakes (primarily driven by members of the Chromatiales in the Alchichica water column), indicating a critical redox transition for plankton. In October 2022, Alchichica and Atexcac plankton was mainly dominated by Gammaproteobacteria, particularly members of the Burkholderiales (Figure S3). Their relative abundance was substantially higher than that recorded in Alchichica and Atexcac in January 2012 (in a fully oxygenated water column) and May 2014 (during the onset of stratification; Iniesto et al. 2022). This observation may reflect a bloom of sulfur‐oxidizing bacteria during the stratification period, involving H2S‐dependent anoxygenic photosynthetic members (e.g., Chromatiales) and members of the Ectothiorhodospiraceae (Ectothiorhodospirales) or Rhodocyclaceae (Burkholderiales; Kojima et al. 2014; Imhoff et al. 2022; Konrad et al. 2023; Tables S2 and S3). Water column data in the present study were collected during the late stratification stage (October), unlike previous data collected during early stratification (e.g., May 2014). The higher abundance of Gammaproteobacteria observed in October 2022 may therefore reflect a delayed response of sulfur‐metabolizing bacteria between the onset of the stratification (April–May), when nitrogen and phosphorus concentrations peak (Alcocer 2022), and the end of the stratification period, after 6 months of established anoxia. Consistent with an active sulfur cycle in the lower part of the water column at the end of the stratification period, the proportion of typical sulfate‐reducer taxa, notably Desulfobacterales (along with other Desulfobacterota), relatively increased below the oxycline (Figure 2A). This strongly suggests that seasonal stratification and redox status have a driving effect on plankton community structure in Alchichica and Atexcac along with other physico‐chemical parameters, notably salinity and alkalinity (Iniesto et al. 2022).

Bacteria dominated the prokaryotic fraction of microbialite‐associated communities in both lakes, whereas archaea were negligible. In May 2019, Alpha‐ and Gammaproteobacteria were highly abundant in both oxic and anoxic layers, comprising ~25% and ~39% of the total reads on average in Alchichica and Atexcac, respectively. Unlike in plankton, cyanobacteria were highly abundant, notably in microbialites from oxygenated layers of the water column, but also below the oxycline, especially in Alchichica microbialites, with ~5%–45% of the total reads (Figure 2A). Their persistence at depth even under seasonally anoxic and low‐light conditions (see below) suggests their potential to actively drive carbonate precipitation through oxygenic or anoxygenic photosynthesis. Mirroring observations throughout the Atexcac water column, members of the Desulfobacterota, likely associated with sulfate reduction, increased in relative abundance around the oxycline. Therefore, both processes may contribute to microbialite maintenance and growth, not only in surface layers, but also in the hypolimnion during periods of anoxia.

The in situ prokaryotic communities associated with plankton and microbialite fractions were very different, as shown by ordination analyses based on Bray‐Curtis distances between OTU frequency matrices (Figure 2B,C). As the t0 samples used for establishing the mesocosms (cf. 3.2.) were stored under conditions that slightly deviated from the actual in situ environment owing to transport and the delay prior to the onset of incubation, the NMDS analyses were only performed on the in situ 2019/2022 samples, excluding the t0 samples. These NMDS plots clearly showed the segregation of microbialite and plankton samples along axis 1, with samples from oxic versus anoxic layer relatively segregated along axis 2, despite an outlier sample (ATX19‐mb‐20m, dark red dot in upper left quadrant) (Figure 2B). This was further supported by hierarchical clustering, which also significantly separated them regardless of their lake origin (Alchichica or Atexcac) (Figure 2C). These distinct patterns between pelagic and benthic (microbialite‐associated) microbial communities likely reflect microbial specialization to different ecological niches, as previously demonstrated in other habitat types (Zinger et al. 2011; Robinson et al. 2025). The pelagic and benthic communities of both lakes responded to selective processes operated by general to local drivers such as seasonal water column chemistry including O2 and nutrient availability, and/or depth‐related factors, notably light.

Microbialite‐Associated Prokaryotic Communities Incubated Under Continuous Anoxia in the Laboratory

Temporal Evolution of Microbial Community Composition Under Continuous Anoxia

To characterize temporal changes of microbialite community composition under continuous anoxia, we regularly subsampled fragments of microbialites incubated in mesoscosms under a strict anoxic N2‐H2‐CO2 (85:10:5) atmosphere and analyzed their composition by 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing (Figure 3, Figure S4). Continuous monitoring in the glovebox confirmed that the pO2 was constantly below detection limit. We monitored two types of mesocosm sets: (i) The first aimed at following temporal changes in microbial communities originating from different redox contexts to see whether community structure converged over time. For this, we compared microbialite samples from Lake Alchichica collected at a permanently oxic (15 m depth, 2012; AL12‐mb‐15m) and a seasonally anoxic (40 m depth, 2019, AL19‐mb‐40m) zone. Both mesocosms were established in 2021 and maintained under global continuous anoxia (O2 < 1 ppm; see Methods) for approximately 1.5 years. (ii) The second set aimed at studying the response of seasonally anoxic microbialites from different lakes to permanent anoxic conditions. In this experiment, we monitored 40 m‐deep Lake Alchichica (AL22‐mb‐40m) and Lake Atexcac 27 m‐deep (ATX22‐mb‐27m) microbialites. The latter samples were collected below the oxycline in 2022, and the corresponding mesocosms were established shortly after collection and monitored over a 5‐month period. Transfer to laboratory conditions involved changes in environmental parameters, including light intensity and quality. However, incubation conditions were maintained constant over time, which allowed cross‐comparison among mesocosm samples. Also, although local O2 production by microbialite cyanobacteria in these experiments cannot be excluded (see below), oxygen traces were eliminated by hydrogen‐palladium catalysts in the glovebox, such that these laboratory conditions enabled the reproduction of one critical environmental parameter: the generalized anoxia in the hypolimnion during stratification periods.

(B) NMDS biplot based on the Bray‐Curtis distances between Alchichica shallow (originally oxic) and deep (originally anoxic) samples (mesocosms AL12‐mb‐15m and AL19‐mb‐40m), showing the separation of the two temporal patterns for the original samples (stress of 0.087). Across all mesocosms, microbialite‐associated OTUs assigned to high‐rank taxa were highly diverse. However, as expected, OTU diversity displayed less variation over time (Figure S4A) than the relative abundance of OTUs (proportions of reads) (Figure 3A). This suggests that most OTUs initially present persisted throughout the experiment, although their relative abundances shifted over time. In line with the composition of the starting natural samples, bacteria and specifically Pseudomonadota dominated the prokaryotic diversity, accounting for an average of ~34% of the total community across all samples (Figure 3A). Both Alpha‐ and Gammaproteobacteria tended to increase over time, Gammaproteobacteria being the dominant taxon in AL22‐mb‐40m and most particularly in the ATX22‐mb‐27m time series. Their prevalence and persistence over time may suggest a role in microbialite formation. Other abundant phyla included Bacteroidota, Verrucomicrobiota, Planctomycetota, and Actinobacteriota, which together covered between ~14% and ~45% of the total relative proportions. Interestingly, heterotrophic bacteria, including sulfate‐reducers, were consistently well‐represented in microbialites from both Alchichica and Atexcac lakes. For example, the prevalence of Firmicutes (Bacillota, > 4% of the total reads on average) in long‐lasting anoxia‐exposed microbialites highlights their capacity to adapt to anaerobic conditions and suggests also a potential role in microbialite formation. Similar observations were reported in microbialites from Lake Las Eras (Spain), where alkaliphilic Firmicutes were associated with the precipitation of Mg‐rich carbonates (Sanz‐Montero et al. 2019). This, together with the persistence over time of Desulfobacterales and other sulfate‐reducing Desulfobacterota in moderate‐to‐high relative proportions hints at an enhanced sulfur cycle, mimicking the situation in the anoxic water column. Similar patterns with potential involvement in sulfur cycling have been observed in other microbialite‐bearing alkaline environments such as Laguna Bacalar, Mexico (Yanez‐Montalvo et al. 2020). Interestingly, such enhancement of microbial S‐cycling with anoxia appears consistent with recent mineralogical characterizations of Alchichica microbialites (Caumartin et al. 2026), which showed a diversification of the S‐bearing minerals and a shift towards a more reduced sulfur speciation within the deeper microbialites of that lake forming under seasonal anoxia. Overall, the microbialite‐associated communities appeared to adapt to contrasting oxygen levels primarily through shifts in relative abundance rather than taxonomic turnover, with community trajectories strongly influenced by the composition of the initial microbiomes.

However, we observed some heterogeneity in microbial community structure in some samples (e.g., AL19‐mb‐40m sampled at t = 9 weeks). Such local‐scale differences in the subsampled microbial communities may stem from intrinsic sample heterogeneity (Iniesto, Moreira, Benzerara, et al. 2021; Iniesto, Moreira, Reboul, et al. 2021) or reflect localized transient ecological events such as bursts of predation or parasitism. For example, in the initial 2019 Alchichica deep sample, a potential shift in local environmental conditions may have triggered the proliferation of a specific host organism, resulting in the marked enrichment of Dependentiae, a group of obligate intracellular parasites typically detected in eukaryotes (Weisse et al. 2023). These differences are also likely impacted by both ecological factors and sampling stochasticity operating at spatial or temporal level (e.g., linked to seasonal dynamics such as the onset vs. the end of water column stratification).

Interestingly, despite spatial heterogeneity across microbialite samples, our 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing data showed relative community structure variation trends as a function of the exposure time to continuous anoxia. This can be seen in ordination analyses using Bray‐Curtis distances based on pairwise dissimilarity among anoxia‐exposed microbialites (Figure 3B,C). NMDS plots of Alchichica samples AL12‐mb‐15m and AL19‐mb‐40m over time showed two distinct groups separating the early‐sampled microbialites (blue, up to 13 weeks) and the late‐sampled microbialites (red, > 20–30 weeks). This temporal trend was also shown by hierarchical clustering (Figure 3C), which distinguished between samples exposed to short versus long periods of anoxia. Although the in vitro light and nutrient conditions did not fully replicate the natural environment, mesocosm conditions, notably anoxia, were constant over time, allowing for cross‐comparison. Our observations suggest that, after a certain period of exposure to anoxia (~25 weeks), microbialite community structures tend to converge, regardless of their initial context of formation in the oxic or anoxic part of the water column. Collectively, our results strongly suggest that oxygen availability (in addition to light and depth) is a fundamental determinant of microbialite community structure over time.

Anoxygenic Photosynthetic Bacteria Expand Under Continuous Anoxic Conditions

We identified genera of known oxygenic and anoxygenic photoautotrophs in our 16S rRNA gene sequence dataset of microbialites incubated in anoxic mesocosms (Tables S2 and S3). They were classified in three categories: (i) oxygenic photosynthetic cyanobacteria (i.e., class Cyanobacteriia, phylum Cyanobacteriota sensu GTDB), (ii) established anoxygenic photosynthetic taxa, and (iii) potential anoxygenic phototrophs (Imhoff et al. 2005; Imhoff 2017). Their relative proportions were plotted for each sample as a function of incubation time under continuous anoxia (Figure 4, Table S2).

The first two rows (AL12‐mb‐15m and AL19‐mb‐40m) represent mesocosms incubated for 1.5 years, and the last two (AL22‐mb‐40m and ATX22‐mb‐27m) represent those incubated for 5 months under continuous anoxia. (A) Histograms showing the 16S rRNA gene amplicon‐based relative abundances (indicated as frequency of reads) of photosynthesizers (cyanobacteria, anoxygenic phototrophs, and potential anoxygenic photoautotrophs) versus non‐phototrophic lineages in microbialites from Lakes Alchichica and Atexcac exposed to continuous anoxia. (B) Variation of the relative abundance (frequency of reads) of major anoxygenic photosynthetic taxa (within Alphaproteobacteria, Gammaproteobacteria, Cyanobacteriia, Chlorobiales, and Chloroflexales) over time. When there is a color on an y axis, this corresponds to the color of the phylum whose frequency is represented on the plot. When the frequency for a phylum in a sample exceeded the order of magnitude of its frequency in the other samples, it is displayed in red on the corresponding axis of the graph.

Overall, in the first two mesocosms (~1.5 years under continuous anoxia), and despite some heterogeneity, the relative proportion of photosynthetic bacteria increased over time, particularly that of anoxygenic photoautotrophs which reached up to 40% of total sequences in shallow Alchichica microbialites (Figure 4). This increase may potentially sustain carbonate precipitation in the absence of O2. It is possible that cyanobacteria present in the anoxic mesocosms still use oxygenic photosynthesis with H2O as an electron donor and releasing oxygen that is subsequently reduced in the anaerobic chamber space. This might be consistent with observations that cyanobacteria incubated in anoxic chambers can grow rapidly (Herrmann et al. 2021; Tamanna et al. 2024). Alternatively, some cyanobacteria might hypothetically shift towards anoxygenic photosynthesis using alternative electron donors such as hydrogen sulfide, which is abundant in the anoxic hypolimnion of lakes.

Cyanobacteria were detected in all samples, but their relative abundance varied considerably, from 1% (e.g., Atexcac sample after 12 weeks under anoxia) to ~32% (deep Alchichica 2019 sample after 67 weeks under anoxia) (Figure 4A). Interestingly, members of the Leptolyngbyales constituted an important portion of the cyanobacterial lineages in both Alchichica and Atexcac samples. They were particularly over‐represented in Alchichica samples, both shallow and deep, where they dominated the cyanobacterial peak observed after t = 15 weeks, comprising up to ~90% of the cyanobacterial population by t = 67 weeks in the Alchichica 2019 deep sample (Figure 4B; Figure S5). Previous studies have shown that certain Leptolyngbyales are specifically adapted to sulfidic conditions, exhibiting not only metabolic versatility in photosynthesis but also the capacity to shift from oxygenic to anoxygenic photosynthesis in response to H2S concentrations and irradiance (Hamilton et al. 2018). Past studies have reported H2S concentrations ranging from 0.08 to 0.13 mM in deep waters of Lake Alchichica during the stratification period (Hernández et al. 2014; Alcocer 2022). They are similar to the ones tested by Hamilton et al. (2018) supporting anoxygenic photosynthesis by Leptolyngbya sp. strain hensonii. Accordingly, a plausible hypothesis is that microbialite‐associated members of the order Leptolynbyales switch to H2S‐driven anoxygenic photosynthesis under strict anoxia. Future studies should test whether these cyanobacteria actually use H2S as electron donor, notably in the sulfur‐rich context of these Mexican lakes. Members of the Xenococcaceae family also persisted under prolonged anoxia, although at lower abundance, particularly in deep samples. This observation supports earlier reports of an increased prevalence of Pleurocapsales (classified as Xenococcaceae in SILVA, adapted to GTDB taxonomy) at depth, at the expense of Nostoc and filamentous Synechococcales (Couradeau et al. 2011; Saghaï et al. 2015; Águila et al. 2021).

Finally, OTUs assigned to typical anoxygenic phototrophs exhibited variable relative abundances over time. On average, they accounted for ~17% of the total taxa in Atexcac microbialites, ~8% in deep Alchichica microbialites, and showed a notable increase to ~27% in shallow Alchichica microbialites after 15 weeks (Figure 3B). Most anoxygenic phototrophic lineages displayed a marked increase in relative abundance after approximately 20 weeks, following two distinct temporal patterns: either reaching a peak around 30 weeks before declining (e.g., members of Rhodobacterales, Rhodospirillales, Burkholderiales, and Chlorobiales), or forming a sharp peak later, around 1.4 years under anoxia (e.g., families of the orders Rhizobiales and Chromatiales). Specifically, members of the Chromatiales showed a significant mid‐term increase in shallow Alchichica microbialites, peaking at ~30% of the total community, whereas members of the Rhizobiales became prominent in deep Alchichica microbialites, peaking at ~35% (Figure S5). This suggests that, from an initial microbial structure already enriched in Pseudomonadota, prolonged anoxic conditions selectively favored some representatives capable of anoxygenic photosynthesis, depending on the original community composition. This temporal restructuring may reflect a long‐term community adaptation to prolonged anoxia, while possibly preserving the potential for photosynthetically induced carbonate precipitation.

Isolates From Deep Microbialites Under Continuous Anoxic Conditions

To assess whether microorganisms from deep microbialites subjected to seasonal anoxia are capable of anaerobic growth, we conducted cultivation experiments aimed at enriching and/or isolating such organisms. Samples were obtained from a 40 m‐deep microbialite from Lake Alchichica, collected under anoxic conditions, and inoculated into various culture media. After 1 year of continuous incubation, seven isolates (designated MT1 to MT7) were obtained, four from BG11 medium and three from a Chromatium‐specific medium. Optical microscopy revealed a range of morphologies among the isolates, from filamentous to coccoid forms (Figure S6). The nearly full‐length 16S rRNA gene (~1500 bp) was amplified from each morphotype, cloned when necessary, and sequenced. We reconstructed phylogenetic trees using these 16S rDNA sequences, together with reference sequences, including their closest BLAST hits (Figure 5).

The four isolates obtained in BG11 medium affiliated with the class Cyanobacteriia (MT1 to MT4; Figure 5A). Of these, three belonged to the order Leptolyngbyales and one to Elainellales. Among them, three isolates (MT1, MT2, and MT4) showed high similarity to uncultured bacterial clones previously identified in Lake Alchichica shallow microbialites (GenBank accession numbers JN825337, JN825331, and JN825340). One in particular was phylogenetically close to Leptolyngbyales members previously isolated from microbialites in other alkaline, sulfur‐containing environments (Sodaleptolyngbya stromatolittii from Dziani Dzaha, Mayotte; Cellamare et al. 2018; Cadeau et al. 2022). This indicates that, despite significant shifts in environmental conditions, these cyanobacteria were able to grow under anoxic conditions, potentially suggesting facultative anaerobic capabilities (Hamilton et al. 2018). Whether these cyanobacteria are capable of anoxygenic photosynthesis using H2S as an electron donor remains to be ascertained. It is also plausible that they continue to utilize H2O as an electron donor, hence performing oxygenic photosynthesis, but that the reducing conditions of the anaerobic chamber prevent O2 accumulation and the oxidation of their immediate environment. Given the well‐established role of cyanobacteria in promoting carbonate precipitation through environmental alkalinization, both in microbialites generally and in Alchichica microbialites specifically (Couradeau et al. 2012; Saghaï et al. 2015; Iniesto, Moreira, Benzerara, et al. 2021; Iniesto, Moreira, Reboul, et al. 2021), the strains isolated here may also contribute to carbonatogenesis, particularly in microbialites developing at depth under seasonally anoxic conditions. However, confirming the carbonatogenic potential of these morphotypes under local oxic and anoxic conditions would require further study.

Two Alphaproteobacteria morphotypes (MT5 and MT6; Figure 5B) were also enriched in culture. Assigned to the order Rhizobiales, they are phylogenetically close to Salinarimonas (based on GTDB classification), a genus comprising facultatively anaerobic phototrophs (Liu et al. 2010; Cai et al. 2011). Relatives of these isolates have been identified in alkaline and/or saline environments where they can form biofilms (e.g., the Beijerinckiaceae sp. strain isolated from tuffs in Greenland, EU180990). Accordingly, these Alphaproteobacteria may play a role in Alchichica microbialite formation during stratification. The remaining enriched morphotype (MT7; Figure 5B) was ascribed to the phylum Acidobacteriota. Its closest phylogenetic neighbors are unclassified sequences, including one sequence previously recovered from native Alchichica microbialites (accession number AF498727.1, Couradeau et al. 2011), and another one (GenBank HM769665.1) retrieved from sites where anaerobic ammonium oxidation occurs. Interestingly, diverse mineral phases were observed by SEM in association with the cells or directly encrusting some of the enriched morphotypes. This includes calcium and magnesium carbonates, magnesium silicates, and iron sulfides (Figure S6). These observations highlight the biomineralization potential of these morphotypes.

Despite inherent cultivation biases, all seven isolates belonged to taxa that were consistently detected at the genus level in mesocosm microbialite samples over time, as shown by 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing (Figures 2 and 3). Hence, the isolates obtained from deep Alchichica microbialites can thrive in anoxic environments and may actively contribute to carbonatogenesis during periods of lake stratification.

Conserved Core Prokaryotic Communities in Microbialites Grown Under Continuous Anoxia

To determine whether microbialites harbored a conserved ‘core microbiome’ independent of habitat or incubation conditions, we identified the set of OTUs shared across all samples, including field‐collected microbialites and those incubated under continuous anoxia (Figure 6). We further examined the relative contribution of OTUs belonging to this ‘core microbiome’ to the overall prokaryotic diversity associated with each microbialite sample, whether collected directly from the field or incubated under continuous anoxia.

(A) Relative proportion of the core community in the total prokaryotic community of the different in situ and laboratory microbialite samples from lakes Alchichica and Atexcac. The laboratory samples were grouped according to their incubation time under continuous anoxia. Samples collected between 0 and 30 weeks were called “Early,” and samples collected between 30 and 73 weeks were called “Late.” Sample names labeled with an asterisk correspond to communities thriving under anoxia. (B) Histogram showing the relative abundance and phylogenetic affiliation of core community OTUs shared by all the sample groups or all but one (light gray dot).

A total of 109 OTUs were shared across all microbialite samples (104 bacterial and 5 archaeal OTUs; Figure 6A). This core included 19 cyanobacterial OTUs, among them representatives of the families Xenococcaceae and Leptolyngbyaceae, as well as 31 bacterial OTUs from the classes Alphaproteobacteria and Gammaproteobacteria, 6 OTUs affiliated with the phylum Acidobacteriota and 14 with Planctomycetota. Notably, 28 of the shared OTUs were assigned to phylogenetic groups whose dominant inferred metabolisms potentially contribute to carbonatogenesis (Iniesto, Moreira, Benzerara, et al. 2021; Iniesto, Moreira, Reboul, et al. 2021; Zhu and Dittrich 2016). The number of OTUs in this core microbiome as well as their proportion (up to 40% of total microbialite OTUs) closely matched observations from a previous study of core microbial communities associated with surface microbialites across nine TMVB lakes, including lakes Alchichica and Atexcac (Iniesto, Moreira, Benzerara, et al. 2021; Iniesto, Moreira, Reboul, et al. 2021).

Yet, at least in the microbial communities associated with the microbialites of Lake Alchichica, a shift in the relative abundance of core taxa occurred following transition to continuous anoxic conditions, with Gammaproteobacteria replacing Cyanobacteria as the dominant group. In deep Atexcac samples, the proportions of core microbiome OTUs dropped below 15%, potentially due to spatial or temporal heterogeneity or a Gammaproteobacteria bloom (as shown in Figures 2 and 3). Although oxygenic photosynthesizers dominated field‐collected in situ microbialite communities (~78%) with primarily Leptolyngbyales‐related taxa, they initially declined and resurged after approximately 30 weeks (Figure 6), suggesting their role in the adaptation to the transition from oxia to anoxia. Last, Firmicutes and Planctomycetota were consistently detected within the core microbiome, likely attributable to their roles in degrading extracellular polymeric substances (Wang et al. 2015; Skoog et al. 2025), whereas taxa like Chromatiales and NB1‐j, although they thrived under anoxia, were absent here, indicating a facultative place in microbialite development.

This aligns with the idea that a shared prokaryotic core may contribute to microbialite formation and persistence under fluctuating environmental conditions (Ruvindy et al. 2016; White et al. 2016), including prolonged anoxia.

Concluding Remarks

The alkaline monomictic lakes Alchichica and Atexcac (Mexico) harbor modern microbialites in the seasonally anoxic deep water column, implying that they are able to grow and/or persist for around 6 months without detectable levels of dissolved oxygen. High‐throughput 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing revealed that planktonic and microbialite‐associated microbial communities are significantly different, yet both are adapted to seasonal anoxia, primarily through shifts in taxon relative abundances that vary with depth and time. Experimental incubations of microbialites from lakes Alchichica and Atexcac under continuous anoxia and controlled conditions showed, despite initial variability, a converging trend towards a more stable community structure after approximately 4 months. This strongly suggests that these communities are active and can adapt to anoxic conditions. We observed an increase in anoxygenic photosynthesizers, notably Gamma‐ and Alphaproteobacteria members, accompanied by anaerobic heterotrophs and, most likely, an increase in microbial species associated with sulfur‐cycling. Although mesocosm incubations do not perfectly mimic the physicochemical in situ natural environment, they offer controlled conditions, notably of anoxia, under which comparative analyses can be carried out. Our results indicate that microbialite‐associated communities undergo temporal shifts in response to persistent anoxia, independently of sampling depth or season. Interestingly, some cyanobacteria, such as Leptolyngbyales, persisted under anoxia, either by switching from oxygenic to anoxygenic photosynthesis or maintaining oxygenic photosynthesis under reducing conditions where O2 is rapidly consumed, potentially relying on fermentation at night when aerobic respiration is no longer feasible. Moreover, microbial isolates from these samples grow under anoxia, further suggesting that such organisms remain active within deep anoxic microbialites and may contribute to carbonatogenesis. Across all samples, we detected a set of shared OTUs forming a “core microbiome” in these lakes capable of thriving in alkaline environments, and also under fluctuating or permanently anoxic conditions. Our study of modern microbialites under anoxia supports the hypothesis that microbialite formation under anoxic conditions partly relies on anoxygenic photosynthesis (Bosak et al. 2007). Accordingly, these microbialite communities may serve as models for the study of carbonatogenic processes under early Earth conditions.
