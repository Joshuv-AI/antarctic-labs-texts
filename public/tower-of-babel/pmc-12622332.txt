Lactate levels and heart failure: causal insights from Mendelian randomization and multi-cohort integrated analyses
Authors: Shijiu Jiang, Shuai Zhou, Runtian Dong, Yankai Xu, Haiying Hu, Kejian Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the causal link between genetic predisposition for elevated lactate levels and heart failure (HF) via Mendelian randomization (MR), and investigate lactate-related genetic mechanisms and mediating pathways.MethodsLactate and HF summary data were from genome-wide association studies (GWAS). MR analyses used inverse variance weighting (IVW) as the main method; cardiac imaging trait MR focused on UK Biobank data. Mediation analysis examined CD20+ memory B cells’ role in NUP50-HF pathway.ResultsMR showed that genetically predicted elevated lactate causally increased HF risk independent of hypoperfusion (reverse causality excluded) and reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), implying a cardiac function-impairment pathway. NUP50, a key lactate-related gene, positively associated with HF. Its HF effect was partially mediated by CD20+ memory B cells; both independently linked to HF risk.ConclusionElevated lactate may increase HF risk via impaired cardiac function. NUP50 and other lactate-related genes may regulate HF risk (NUP50 partially via CD20+ memory B cells), highlighting lactate and its genetic pathways as potential HF prevention/treatment targets.Keywords: Lactate levels, heart failure, mendelian randomization, LVEF, NUP50 CD20+ memory B cells, mediation analysis

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) remains a leading cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality worldwide [1,2], and is characterized by impaired cardiac function and complex pathophysiological mechanisms, including metabolic derangements [3], myocardial remodeling [4,5], and systemic inflammation [6,7]. Despite advances in therapeutic strategies, the underlying pathways driving HF progression remain incompletely understood, highlighting the need for novel insights into the molecular and metabolic determinants of HF.

Lactate, a key metabolite produced during anaerobic glycolysis [8], has long been recognized as a marker of tissue hypoxia and metabolic stress [9]. In the context of HF, elevated lactate levels are commonly observed and are often attributed to reduced cardiac output, tissue hypoperfusion [10], or impaired mitochondrial function [11]. However, observational studies investigating the relationship between genetic predisposition for elevated lactate and HF have yielded conflicting results, with ambiguity regarding whether lactate plays an initiating causal role in HF pathogenesis beyond its well-recognized status as a downstream marker of tissue hypoperfusion, or merely reflects secondary physiological adaptations to cardiac dysfunction. Such uncertainties arise from potential confounding by comorbidities (e.g., diabetes and renal dysfunction) and reverse causality, where HF itself may exacerbate lactate accumulation through impaired oxygen delivery.

Notably, lactate levels are dynamically influenced by acute clinical conditions (e.g., tissue hypoperfusion, infection), which complicates interpretations of observational associations. Mendelian randomization (MR) addresses this limitation by using genetic variants. These variants are fixed at conception and independent of postnatal clinical status, allowing MR to capture the long-term, inherent propensity for elevated lactate and enable robust inference of causal relationships.

To address these limitations, integrative analytical approaches are critical for determining the causal relationship between lactate and HF. Recent advances in genome-wide association studies (GWAS) [12], expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) analysis [13], and Mendelian randomization (MR) [14] offer powerful tools for overcoming biases in observational research. In particular, MR leverages genetic variants as instrumental variables to infer causal associations and minimize the impact of confounding and reverse causality. Complementary analyses, such as assessment of cardiac imaging traits (e.g., left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF], a key indicator of cardiac function), identification of lactate-related genes (e.g., NUP50), and exploration of immune-mediated pathways (e.g., CD20+ memory B cells as potential mediators), further enhance our understanding of the mechanisms linking lactate to HF.

In this study, we integrated multilayered analyses, including MR, eQTL, pQTL, SMR, and mediation analyses, across large cohorts to clarify the causal relationship between lactate levels and HF. We aimed to (1) determine whether genetic predisposition for elevated lactate levels causally increase HF risk, (2) explore the associations between lactate and cardiac function (via LVEF), (3) identify lactate-related genes contributing to HF pathogenesis, and (4) investigate immune-mediated pathways that may mediate these relationships. By leveraging these integrated approaches, we find elevated lactate levels causally increase HF risk, with lactate levels serving as a key metabolic pathway underlying this association. This directly addresses the metabolic basis of HF linked to lactate dysregulation, and we therefore sought to provide novel insights into the lactate-driven metabolic and genetic basis of HF. Our focus is on how lactate metabolism intersects with cardiac function and genetic regulators, with the goal of potentially identifying new targets for HF prevention and treatment.

Materials and methods

Data sources

Heart failure (HF) dataset: Summary statistics for HF were obtained from the FinnGen cohort [15] (finngen_R12_I9_HEARTFAIL), which included 500,348 individuals. The definition of HF for this endpoint follows FinnGen R12’s official criteria (https://r12.risteys.finngen.fi/endpoints/I9_HEARTFAIL#endpoint-definition), as detailed below: systolic heart failure: Heart failure caused by abnormal myocardial contraction during systole leading to defective cardiac emptying. This dataset was used to assess the causal relationship between the lactate levels and lactate-related genes.

Lactate levels dataset: GWAS summary statistics for lactate levels were derived from ebi-a-GCST90092882(https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/datasets/ebi-a-GCST90092882/), comprising 114,806 participants. These data served as exposure in Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses targeting HF and cardiac imaging traits. By consulting the literature, 46 genes related to lactic acid have been identified [16–19].

Cardiac imaging traits: Data on heart imaging traits support the GWAS results of 82 traits trained in the UKB British cohort [20], including 64 left ventricle traits, four left atrium traits, four right ventricle traits, four right atrium traits, three ascending aorta traits, and three descending aorta traits (https://zenodo.org/records/7239166). LVEF was prioritized for the analysis because of its clinical relevance to cardiac function.

eQTL data: Expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) were sourced from the eQTLGen Consortium (whole blood samples) (https://www.eqtlgen.org/phase1.html) [13], which includes 37 datasets from a consortium of 31,684 individuals used for instrumental variable screening of exposure factors.

pQTL data: Protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) data from deCODE Genetics [21] (https://www.decode.com/summarydata/). The summary data describe genome-wide association studies (GWASs) of plasma protein levels measured using 4,907 aptamers in 35,559 Icelanders. identified 18,084 associations between sequence variants and plasma protein levels, of which 19% were rare (minor allele frequency (MAF) < 1%). We tested plasma protein levels for their association with 373 diseases and other traits and identified 257,490 associations. These datasets were used to identify the genetic variants associated with the expression of lactate-related genes and their protein products.

Immune cell GWAS data: GWAS summary statistics for each immune trait are publicly available from the GWAS Catalog (accession numbers GCST90001391 to GCST90002121) [22,23]. A total of 731 immunophenotypes, including absolute cell (AC) counts (n = 118), median fluorescence intensities (MFI) reflecting surface antigen levels (n = 389), morphological parameters (MP) (n = 32), and relative cell (RC) counts (n = 192), were included. Specifically, the MFI, AC, and RC features contain B cells, CDCs, mature T cells, monocytes, myeloid cells, TBNK (T cells, B cells, natural killer cells), and Treg panels, while the MP feature contains CDC and TBNK panels. The original GWAS on immune traits was performed using data from 3,757 European individuals, and there were no overlapping cohorts. Approximately 22 million SNPs genotyped with high-density arrays were imputed using the Sardinian sequence-based reference panel [24] and associations were tested after adjusting for covariates (i.e., sex, age, and age2).

Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis

MR analyses were performed to investigate causal relationships following the analytical framework outlined in Figure 1.

Overview of the study design.

Instrumental variables (IVs): SNPs significantly associated with exposure (P < 5×10−8) were selected as IVs [25]. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning (r2 < 0.001, distance > 10,000 kb) was applied to ensure independence [26]. The F statistic was calculated for each SNP, and SNPs with F < 10 were removed to avoid the influence of potential weak IV bias on the results [27].

Power calculations were performed using the online mRnd power calculation tool (https://shiny.cnsgenomics.com/mRnd/) to ensure adequate statistical power for our Mendelian randomization analyses. Based on the sample sizes of our exposure (lactate levels: N = 114,806) and outcome (heart failure: N = 500,348) datasets, along with the observed variance explained by our genetic instruments (R2 ranging from 0.0003 to 0.0009 for lactate-associated SNPs), our study achieved >80% power to detect an odds ratio of approximately 1.2 per standard deviation increase in lactate levels at α = 0.05. This confirms that our study was well-powered to detect the modest but clinically relevant effect sizes observed in our analyses.

MR methods

Inverse variance weighting (IVW) was used as the primary method. Supplementary methods included MR Egger [28], weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode to validate robustness.

We acknowledge that our reliance on summary-level GWAS data precludes direct adjustment for comorbid metabolic conditions (e.g., diabetes, obesity) that may influence lactate metabolism and represent potential confounders. To address this inherent limitation of summary-data MR, we employed several strategies: (1) selecting genetic instruments specifically associated with lactate levels rather than broader metabolic traits; (2) conducting comprehensive sensitivity analyses (MR-Egger, MR-PRESSO) to detect pleiotropy; and (3) performing PhenoScanner searches to identify potential pleiotropic pathways. Future individual-level data analyses with capacity for multivariable adjustment will be valuable to confirm our findings.

Sensitivity analyses

Leave-One-Out Analysis: In Mendelian randomization (MR) studies, leave-one-out analysis is a sensitivity method used to assess the robustness of causal estimates by iteratively excluding each instrumental variable (IV) and recalculating the MR result. This approach helps identify whether the overall causal effect is driven by a single influential genetic variant (SNP), which could indicate pleiotropy or bias.

Heterogeneity test: Cochran’s Q test was used to assess heterogeneity among the IVs [29].

Pleiotropy test: The MR Egger intercept test was performed to detect horizontal pleiotropy [29].

Reverse causality assessment Reverse MR analyses (e.g., HF as exposure and lactate levels as outcomes) were conducted to rule out bidirectional effects.

Lactate-related gene analysis

SMR analysis: Summary data-based MR (SMR) integrated eQTL data (from eQTLGen) with HF GWAS data to identify lactate-related genes (e.g., NUP50) associated with HF risk.

The SMR method employs the most significantly associated cis-eQTL SNP as the top IV [30]. The main results were presented as the OR for heart failure per SD increase in gene expression. The heterogeneity in the dependent instrument (HEIDI) test, part of the SMR approach was used to determine whether the association observed between gene expression and heart failure was due to a linkage scenario, where an eQTL SNP in linkage disequilibrium with another SNP independently influenced the disease outcome, potentially violating one of the MR assumptions. A HEIDI test P-value below 0.01 was considered indicative of an association likely due to linkage rather than regulation of gene expression. We performed SMR analysis using the default parameters (https://yanglab.westlake.edu.cn/software/smr/#SMR&HEIDIanalysis; P value of cis-eQTL: <5 × 10−8, window centered around the probe to select cis-eQTLs: 100 kb).

Colocalization analysis: The “coloc” package in R was used to conduct colocalization analysis to find colocalized association information in major eQTL datasets of lactate-related genes and HF GWAS data [31]. The PPH4 test statistic from the “coloc.abf” function was used as the outcome measure, providing the posterior probability of shared causal variants between the two tested traits. Regional association mapping was also conducted, and SNP genotype-phenotype associations for lactate-related genes were studied.

Mediation analysis

A two-step MR analysis [32] was conducted to examine whether CD20+ memory B cells mediate the association between NUP50 and HF, using MR estimates from NUP50-CD20+ memory B cell (HF) analyses. The indirect effect mediated by CD20+ memory B cells was β1 × β2, where β1 represented the causal effect of NUP50 on CD20+ memory B cells, and β2 represented the causal effect of CD20+ memory B cells on HF; the ratio of the mediating effect of CD20+ memory B cells was β1 × β2/β0, and β0 represented the effect of NUP50 on HF.

Our study strictly adhered to the three core assumptions of Mendelian randomization: 1. Relevance Assumption: Genetic instruments were strongly associated with the exposure (lactate levels or lactate-related genes) at genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−8). We calculated F-statistics for all instruments, excluding those with F < 10 to avoid weak instrument bias. 2. Independence Assumption: To ensure genetic instruments were independent of known confounders, we conducted comprehensive searches using the PhenoScanner database to identify and exclude SNPs associated with potential confounders of the lactate-HF relationship (including diabetes, coronary artery disease, hypertension, renal function, and obesity). Additionally, we applied strict linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning (r2 < 0.001, distance > 10,000 kb) to ensure instrument independence. 3.Exclusion Restriction Assumption: We employed multiple methods to detect and account for horizontal pleiotropy: MR-Egger regression to test for directional pleiotropy; MR-PRESSO to identify and correct for outlier variants; and Cochran’s Q test to assess heterogeneity. For significant associations, we compared results across multiple MR methods (IVW, weighted median, MR-Egger) to ensure robustness against pleiotropy. These rigorous approaches collectively strengthen the validity of our causal inferences.

Statistical analysis

In this MR study, the IVW method was primarily used to estimate causal effects. Under the assumption of no horizontal pleiotropy, this method provides a weighted average estimate by minimizing the variance of multiple genetic variant effect size estimates to assess causal associations. When only one instrumental variable exists, the Wald ratio method [33], which directly uses the effect size estimate of a single SNP to infer the causal relationship between exposure and outcome, can be considered.

We employed rigorous statistical methods to assess heterogeneity and pleiotropy in our instrumental variables. Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q statistic, with a significance threshold of P < 0.05 indicating potential heterogeneity. For pleiotropy assessment, we implemented MR-Egger regression to test for directional pleiotropy, where a significant intercept (P < 0.05) suggests the presence of unbalanced pleiotropy. Additionally, we applied the MR-PRESSO method to detect and correct for outlier variants that might indicate horizontal pleiotropy. These analyses were conducted for all primary MR analyses, with detailed results presented in Supplementary Tables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and multiple comparisons were adjusted when necessary. All analyses in this study were performed using R software, version 4.3.1, with the packages TwoSampleMR, coloc, and smr-1.3.1-win software on the Windows platform.

Results

Causal relationship between genetic predisposition for elevated lactate levels and heart failure

Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses were conducted to evaluate the association between lactate levels and heart failure (HF) using summary statistics from lactate levels (ebi-a-GCST90092882, N = 114,806) and HF (finngen_R12_I9_HEARTFAIL, N = 500,348). Additionally, MR analyses were performed to assess the relationship between lactate levels and 82 cardiac magnetic resonance imaging traits using GWAS summary data, which ultimately identified a negative causal association between lactate levels and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), following the analytical framework outlined in Figure 1.

Forward MR analysis Ten single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with lactate levels were used as instrumental variables (Supplementary Table S1). Multiple MR methods consistently indicated a positive causal association between elevated lactate levels and an increased HF risk. The inverse variance weighted (IVW) method, the primary approach, showed an odds ratio (OR) of 1.3705 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.1433–1.6429, P = 0.000655). Consistent findings were observed with the weighted median (OR = 1.3720, 95% CI: 1.0999–1.7113, P = 0.0050) and MR Egger (OR = 2.2511, 95% CI: 1.3457–3.7655, P = 0.0149) (Figure 2(A,B); Supplementary Table S2).

For heterogeneity, Cochran’s Q test indicated no significant heterogeneity among the instrumental variables for the primary association between lactate levels and heart failure (Q = 11.83, df = 9, P = 0.223; Supplementary Table S3). Consistent results of no significant heterogeneity were also observed across all other MR analyses (Supplementary Tables S8, S12, S16), reinforcing the reliability of the causal inferences. This suggests consistent effects across SNPs in driving the observed association with heart failure, reinforcing the reliability of causal inference.

To rigorously assess potential horizontal pleiotropy, MR-Egger intercept tests were conducted for all primary analyses. For the lactate-HF association, the MR-Egger intercept was −0.0187 (SE = 0.0094, P = 0.082), indicating no significant evidence of directional pleiotropy. All MR-Egger intercept p-values for key analyses exceeded 0.05 (Supplementary Tables S4, S9, S13, S17), suggesting that pleiotropy is unlikely to substantially bias our causal estimates.

Reverse MR analysis: To exclude reverse causality, MR analyses with HF exposure and lactate levels as the outcome were performed using 12 SNPs. No significant associations were detected across all methods (IVW OR = 1.0305, 95% CI: 0.9724–1.0921, P = 0.3099) (Figure 2 C; Supplementary Table S5).

Association between lactate levels and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF)

To explore the potential mechanisms linking lactate levels to cardiac function, MR analyses were performed between lactate levels and LVEF (a key cardiac imaging trait from the UK Biobank cohort). Thirty-six SNPs associated with lactate levels were included (Supplementary Table S6). The IVW method revealed a significant inverse association between lactate levels and LVEF (OR = 0.9107, 95% CI: 0.8345–0.9938, P = 0.0359), suggesting that higher lactate levels may contribute to impaired cardiac function (Figure 2 D, E; Supplementary Table S7). Sensitivity analyses confirmed low heterogeneity (IVW Q = 31.9049, df = 35, P = 0.6183; Supplementary Table S8) and no pleiotropy (Egger intercept=-0.0036, P = 0.5916; Supplementary Table S9).

In summary, elevated lactate levels causally increase HF risk, with no evidence of reverse causality, and lactate metabolism emerges as a potential mechanistic pathway linked to cardiac dysfunction.

SMR and co-localization analyses identified lactate-related genes (NUP50 and SIRT6) with shared genetic signals for HF, and pQTL analyses highlighted RACGAP1 and PLOD2 as relevant proteins. Sensitivity analyses: Leave-one-out analyses confirmed that no single SNP disproportionately influenced the causal estimates, as the effect sizes remained stable and statistically significant upon sequential exclusion of each variant (Supplementary Figure S3 A-C). Funnel plots demonstrated symmetrical distributions of variant-specific estimates, indicating no substantial directional pleiotropy (Supplementary Figure S3 D-F).

Discussion

This study systematically explored the causal relationship between lactate levels and heart failure (HF) using integrated analytical approaches, including Mendelian randomization (MR), summary data-based MR (SMR), colocalization, and mediation analyses, following the framework outlined in Figure 1. These key findings provide novel insights into the metabolic and genetic mechanisms linking lactate metabolism to HF pathogenesis [3,34].

While elevated lactate is a well-known epiphenomenon of hypoperfusion in overt HF, our Mendelian-randomization approach indicates that genetic predisposition for lactate elevation precedes and potentially precipitates HF rather than merely reflecting its haemodynamic consequence.

A key strength of our study is that we used MR to evaluate genetically predicted lactate levels rather than single-time-point measured lactate. This approach avoids confounding by acute clinical conditions (e.g., transient hypoperfusion, acute HF decompensation) that drive short-term lactate variability. Genetic variants reflect inherent metabolic propensity rather than dynamic clinical status, and this characteristic strengthens the validity of our causal findings.

Beyond its causal role in heart failure development, lactate levels have demonstrated significant prognostic value for both in-hospital and long-term outcomes in acute cardiovascular conditions. The Intermountain Risk Score (IMRS), which incorporates lactate among other metabolic parameters, has been validated for mortality prediction in ST-elevation myocardial infarction patients and those with cardiogenic shock [35,36]. Our findings genetically support the biological basis for including lactate measurement in such risk stratification tools, suggesting that sustained elevations in lactate may contribute to both short-term clinical deterioration and long-term cardiovascular impairment.

In critically ill populations, such as patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) complicated by cardiogenic shock, lactate levels have been established as a strong predictor of in-hospital mortality [37]. This aligns with our genetic evidence implicating lactate in cardiovascular dysfunction, underscoring its utility as a biomarker for risk stratification in acute critical illness.

Our MR analyses demonstrated a robust positive causal association between elevated lactate levels and an increased HF risk. The consistency across multiple MR methods (IVW, weighted median, MR Egger) and the absence of reverse causality strongly support that lactate is not merely a marker of hypoperfusion-related HF, but also an active driver of HF development. This aligns with recent advances in lactate biology, which have redefined lactate as a multifunctional metabolite involved in cellular signaling and metabolic regulation, which are processes critical for cardiac homeostasis.

Notably, lactate levels were inversely associated with the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), a key indicator of cardiac contractility. This suggests that impaired ventricular function may be a critical intermediate pathway; elevated lactate could directly or indirectly reduce myocardial efficiency and promote HF progression. This finding bridges metabolic derangement and functional cardiac impairment [38], reinforcing the clinical relevance of lactate as a therapeutic target.

SMR and co-localization analyses identified several lactate-related genes with shared genetic signals for HF, highlighting potential molecular mediators. NUP50 emerged as a key candidate: MR analyses showed a positive association with HF, supported by strong colocalization between cis-eQTL NUP50 and HF GWAS summary data. NUP50, a component of the nuclear pore complex, is involved in nucleocytoplasmic transport, and its dysregulation may disrupt cardiac gene expression programs that are critical for adaptive responses to metabolic stress [39,40]. Howerve, we have to acknowledge that the functional role of NUP50 in lactate metabolism and cardiac pathophysiology remains preliminary and requires further experimental validation. While our integrative analyses (including SMR and colocalization) provide strong genetic evidence supporting its involvement, future studies using in vitro models (e.g., cardiomyocyte or immune cell systems) and in vivo approaches (e.g., tissue-specific NUP50 manipulation in animal models) are needed to elucidate the mechanistic links between NUP50, lactate metabolism, and cardiac dysfunction.

Additionally, SIRT6, HDAC3, and KAT5 have been linked to epigenetic regulation and metabolic homeostasis, which are associated with HF risk. SIRT6, an NAD+-dependent deacetylase [41], modulates mitochondrial function [42] and oxidative stress [43,44] and is highly relevant to cardiac metabolism. These findings suggest that lactate may influence HF through the epigenetic regulation of metabolic pathways, expanding our understanding of the “lactate-HF” axis beyond immediate metabolic effects.

Mediation analysis revealed that CD20+ memory B cells partially mediated the effect of NUP50 on HF. NUP50 is associated with increased CD20+ memory B cells, which in turn correlates with a higher risk of HF. This links lactate-related genetic variation to immune activation, which is consistent with the recognized role of B cells in cardiac inflammation and remodeling. CD20+ memory B cells may contribute to HF by promoting autoantibody production or cytokine release, thereby exacerbating myocardial damage. This immunometabolic crosstalk adds a new layer to the mechanistic understanding of the role of lactate in HF.

CD20, a transmembrane phosphoprotein expressed on the surface of B cells, is widely present in pre-B cells to mature B cells and serves as a signature molecule of memory B cells [45]. Upon re-stimulation by antigens, memory B cells can rapidly differentiate into plasma cells, produce high-affinity antibodies, and regulate immune responses through cytokine secretion [46].

Within the cardiac microenvironment, B cells account for 10% of circulating lymphocytes in normal myocardial tissue[47]. However, under pathological conditions of heart failure, CD20+ memory B cells can be recruited to myocardial injury sites via chemokines, such as CCL7. Particularly in ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) models, their numbers peak 5–7 days post-injury [48,49]. This dynamic change suggests that CD20+ memory B cells may participate in the persistent process of myocardial remodeling through local infiltration.

CD20+ memory B cells secrete various pro-inflammatory factors (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β) and chemokines (e.g., CCL7), which amplify local inflammatory responses through paracrine effects [50]. In patients with dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), an increased proportion of TNF-α+ B cells correlates significantly with the degree of myocardial fibrosis. CCL7 recruits monocytes to infiltrate the myocardium, exacerbating tissue damage after ischemia [49,51]. Additionally, memory B cells promote Th1 cell differentiation by secreting cytokines like IL-6, activating signaling pathways such as NF-κB, and forming a “inflammation-fibrosis” vicious cycle [52]. Heart failure is often accompanied by metabolic abnormalities (e.g., insulin resistance and oxidative stress), and CD20+ memory B cells can enhance their survival and effector functions through metabolic reprogramming. For instance, in pressure overload models, the glycolytic rate of memory B cells increases, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) further activate cardiac fibroblasts, promoting collagen deposition.

Based on the pathological role of CD20+ memory B cells, anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies (e.g., rituximab) offer new insights into heart failure treatment. In transverse aortic constriction (TAC) models, rituximab significantly improved cardiac diastolic function and reduced fibrosis by inhibiting CD20+ B cells. Furthermore, rituximab decreases the circulating levels of CCL7 and BAFF in patients with acute myocardial infarction, thereby reducing the risk of adverse cardiovascular events [49].

CD20+ memory B cells play a critical role in the development and progression of heart failure through antibody-mediated myocardial injury, cytokine secretion, and immune-metabolic interactions. Anti-CD20 therapy provides the basis for targeted immunomodulation.

However, this study had several limitations. First, the MR analyses relied on genetic instruments derived from cohorts of European ancestry, limiting generalizability to other populations. Second, although we explored LVEF and immune cells as potential mediators, other pathways (e.g., systemic inflammation and renal function) may also contribute and require further investigation. Third, colocalization and SMR analyses, while supportive, cannot fully rule out the influence of linked genetic variants, necessitating functional validation of candidate genes such as NUP50. Fourth, while our sensitivity analyses showed no significant evidence of horizontal pleiotropy, we acknowledge that residual pleiotropic effects through unknown pathways cannot be entirely excluded, representing a general limitation of MR methodology. Finally, while our mediation analysis suggests a role for CD20+ memory B cells in the NUP50-HF pathway, this finding requires independent validation in experimental models or single-cell datasets. Future studies using single-cell RNA sequencing of cardiac tissues or functional experiments in animal models would be valuable to confirm the mechanistic role of these immune cells in heart failure.

We outlined specific, hypothesis-driven research directions to advance the clinical field: Lactate as a prognostic and therapeutic target: We emphasized that genetically predicted elevated lactate is a causal risk factor for HF, supporting the potential of serial lactate monitoring to stratify HF risk in at-risk populations (e.g., patients with hypertension or diabetes). We also discussed feasible lactate-lowering strategies for clinical exploration, such as targeting monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs)—key mediators of lactate transport—to modulate myocardial lactate levels, complementing existing neurohormonal or hemodynamic therapies.

Immunomodulatory therapy for HF: Given that CD20+ memory B cells partially mediate NUP50-related HF risk, repurposing anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies (e.g., rituximab, already approved for autoimmune diseases) for HF subsets with evidence of B cell activation (e.g., dilated cardiomyopathy with elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines).

Genetic risk stratification: We highlighted NUP50 and SIRT6 as potential genetic biomarkers—incorporating these loci into polygenic risk scores (PRS) for HF could enhance personalized prevention, such as earlier lifestyle interventions or closer cardiac imaging surveillance in individuals with high PRS.

Future studies should validate these findings in multiethnic cohorts and explore targeted interventions to modulate lactate metabolism in preclinical HF models. Functional studies of NUP50 and its interaction with CD20+ memory B cells could uncover novel immune-metabolic checkpoints for HF therapy. Additionally, longitudinal studies integrating lactate levels, cardiac imaging, and genetic data could strengthen the clinical utility of lactate as a prognostic marker or therapeutic target.

This study redefines our understanding of lactate in HF: (1) lactate as a therapeutic target, which supports the exploration of lactate-lowering strategies (e.g., modulating monocarboxylate transporters) to reduce HF risk, moving beyond conventional treatments targeting hemodynamics or neurohormonal pathways. (2) Immune modulation: The identification of CD20+ memory B cells as mediators opens new avenues for immunomodulatory therapies (e.g., B cell-targeted agents) for HF, complementing existing anti-inflammatory approaches. (3) Genetic biomarkers NUP50 and related loci (e.g., SIRT6) may serve as genetic markers to stratify HF risk, enabling personalized prevention.

Conclusion

This study confirmed the causal role of elevated lactate levels in increasing HF risk, with reduced LVEF as a potential mechanistic pathway. Lactate-related genes (NUP50) and immune mediators (CD20+ memory B cells) contribute to this relationship, highlighting the complex interplay between metabolism, genetics, and immunity in the pathogenesis of HF. These findings provide a foundation for the development of targeted strategies to modulate the lactate metabolism and related pathways for HF prevention and treatment.
