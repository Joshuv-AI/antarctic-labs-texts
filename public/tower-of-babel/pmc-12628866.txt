PRKAA2 mediates the pathogenesis of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease via PI3K/AKT signaling pathway
Authors: Jiayi Li, Guiling Wu, Zihao Li, Xinghuan Liang, Yiyu Chen, Bing Yang, Jing Xian, Tingting Deng, Cuihong Chen, Li Li, Yang Liu, Tian Xiong, Sihui Wu, Xi Yang, Yingfen Qin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has emerged as the most prevalent type of chronic liver disease, posing a significant threat to human health. Protein kinase AMP-activated catalytic subunit alpha 2 (PRKAA2) plays a pivotal role in regulating metabolic diseases. Nevertheless, the underlying molecular mechanisms by which PRKAA2 influences the pathogenesis of MASLD remain unclear.MethodBioinformatics analysis of public datasets identified the potential role of PRKAA2 in MASLD, verified its immune cells correlation, and constructed its competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network. We assessed the mRNA and protein expression of PRKAA2, along with phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) and protein kinase B (AKT) phosphorylation levels after PRKAA2 knockdown. Pro-inflammatory cytokines were quantified by Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), and lipid species were profiled using Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). In vivo, hepatic morphology and lipid deposition were evaluated by Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining. Immunofluorescence measured PRKAA2, phospho-PI3K (p-PI3K) and phospho-AKT (p-AKT) expression. The regulatory interaction between PRKAA2 and its upstream miRNA was confirmed by dual-luciferase reporter assay.ResultsOur bioinformatics analysis identified PRKAA2 as a significantly upregulated gene in MASLD. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments consistently revealed markedly elevated PRKAA2 expression levels in MASLD models. Knockdown of PRKAA2 significantly reduced lipid accumulation, suppressed production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and attenuated the phosphorylation ratios of PI3K and AKT. Further mechanistic investigations confirmed that hsa-let-7b-5p directly targets PRKAA2 by binding to its wild-type (WT) 3’UTR, establishing this miRNA as a key upstream regulator of PRKAA2 in MASLD pathogenesis.ConclusionsOur findings collectively demonstrated that PRKAA2 serves as a crucial mediator in MASLD pathogenesis, functioning through a novel regulatory axis involving the upstream hsa-let-7b-5p and the downstream activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway.

Introduction

MASLD has emerged as the most prevalent type of chronic liver disorder and a significant metabolic disease worldwide, posing a major challenge to public health. It is commonly associated with high-risk factors such as obesity, dyslipidemia, and diabetes, etc [1]. Characterized by pathological lipid accumulation in hepatocytes, MASLD encompasses a disease spectrum ranging from simple steatosis to metabolic associated steatohepatitis (MASH), which may progress to liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma [2]. The global prevalence of MASLD continues to rise alarmingly, with projections suggesting it will affect over 30% of adult population by 2030 [3]. This epidemic parallel the increasing incidence of metabolic comorbidities, including obesity, insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes mellitus [4], contributing the recent definition of MASLD [5–7]. Current therapeutic strategies for MASLD primarily target glucose and lipid metabolism. However, the clinical management of MASLD faces significant challenges, including the lack of reliable non-invasive diagnostic biomarkers and limited effective treatment options. These limitations underscore the urgent need to elucidate the molecular mechanisms driving MASLD pathogenesis to facilitate the development of novel diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.

Emerging evidence indicated that MASLD patients typically exhibit reduced levels of hepatic adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) [8, 9], and AMPK as a central energy sensor maintains hepatic metabolic homeostasis [10]. PRKAA2, a gene encoding the AMPKα2 subunit and a member of the AMPK family [11], primarily regulates fatty acids and cholesterol biosynthesis, playing a pivotal role in modulating cellular energy metabolism and oncogenesis [12]. AMPK inhibition in adipocytes and hepatocytes is mediated through phosphorylation of Ser491 on AMPKα2 [13]. Previous studies have suggested that PRKAA2 may be involved in MASLD pathogenesis through its regulatory effects on cellular energy metabolism [14, 15]. Nevertheless, the underlying molecular mechanisms by which PRKAA2 influences the pathogenesis of MASLD remain unclear.

Therefore, this study aims to comprehensively investigate the role of PRKAA2 in MASLD development and progression, with particular focus on its regulatory mechanisms in hepatic lipid metabolism and potential upstream modulators and downstream effectors. Our findings will provide novel insights into MASLD pathogenesis and may identify PRKAA2 as a promising diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target for this increasingly prevalent metabolic liver disease.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and processing

The GSE48452, GSE159676, and GSE49541 datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The GSE48452 and GSE159676 datasets primarily focus on the occurrence of MASLD, while the GSE49541 dataset was mainly associated with the progression of MASLD. The GSE48452 dataset contained 18 MASLD tissues and 14 normal liver tissues, and it was analyzed using the GPL11532 data platform. The GSE159676 dataset included 7 MASLD tissues and 6 normal liver tissues, with the GPL6244 data platform being utilized for analysis. The GES49541 dataset contained 72 samples, comprising 32 cases of advanced MASLD (fibrosis stage 3–4) and 40 cases of early MASLD (fibrosis stage 0–1), and it was processed utilizing the GPL570 data platform. All data were transformed using log2(x + 1) for normalization.

Differential expression genes (DEGs)

The intersections of GSE48452 and GSE159676 datasets were identified as the key modules of DEGs. Samples from these datasets were categorized into MASLD and control groups, and the limma package was used to analyze and identify DEGs between MASLD and healthy liver tissues. Additionally, MASLD samples from GSE48452 and GSE159676 datasets were further divided into the high and low expression groups based on PRKAA2 expression levels (with a cutoff value set at 50%). DEGs associated with PRKAA2 were then analyzed using the R package limma. The filtering criteria for DEGs were set as an absolute log2 fold change ≥ 0.1 and a p-value < 0.05. Heatmaps were generated using the R packages heatmap to visually represent the DEGs obtained from these analyses.

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network

The DEGs between the MASLD group and the control group in the GSE48452 and GSE159676 datasets were intersected, resulting in the identification of 43 genes, including 38 mRNAs and 5 non-coding RNAs. Among these, the 38 mRNAs were identified as hub genes. The interactions between the proteins encoded by these hub genes were analyzed by utilizing the STRING database (https://string-db.org/). During the construction of the PPI network, functional and pathway enrichment analyses were automatically performed within the STRING database. Among these 38 genes, 6 genes were found to be enriched in lipid homeostasis. These 6 genes were subsequently selected for the construction of the PPI network.

Analysis of differential expression and diagnostic efficiency

In the GSE48452 and GSE159676 datasets, the difference in PRKAA2 expression between the MASLD group and the control group was compared. In the GSE49541 dataset, the disparity in PRKAA2 expression between the advanced MASLD group and the mild MASLD group was analyzed. The expression levels of PRKAA2 were extracted to differentiate MASLD samples from normal samples, and the Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was generated using the R package ROCR. The same analytical approach was employed to analyze the ACSM2A.

Functional enrichment analysis

The Gene Ontology (GO) is a widely used tool for comprehensive functional enrichment analysis, covering biological processes (BPs), molecular functions (MFs), as well as cellular components (CC) [16]. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) is a commonly used database that provides information on various biological pathways, genomes, diseases, and drugs [17]. For this study, the Cluster Profiler R software package was employed to conduct GO and KEGG analyses on the DEGs, with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 considered statistically significant [18]. To explore the biological roles of PRKAA2 in MASLD, we performed GO and KEGG analyses on the DEGs associated with PRKAA2 utilizing R package clusterProfiler.

Analysis of immune cell infiltration

CIBERSORT, a deconvolution algorithm, facilitates immune cell infiltration analysis by deconvolution gene expression microarray data [19]. It was utilized to measure the proportion of immune cells in MASLD samples. Subsequently, the correlation between PRKAA2 and immune cell infiltration was assessed using Pearson correlation analysis.

Construction of CeRNA network

To explore the ceRNA network of PRKAA2, multiple databases were utilized for analysis. Potential miRNAs targeting PRKAA2 were identified from the miRTarBase database (https://mirtarbase.cuhk.edu.cn/~miRTarBase/miRTarBase_2022/php/index.php) and the NetworkAnalyst database (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/NetworkAnalyst/home.xhtml). Common miRNAs from both databases were screened and designated as core miRNAs. Subsequently, the upstream lncRNAs regulating these core miRNAs were predicted using the ENCORI database (https://rnasysu.com/encori/index.php).

Specimen collection

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (Approval No.: 2024-E0905) and Informed consent was obtained from all the participants. MASLD and normal liver tissues samples were collected from tissue sections preserved in the Department of Pathology at our hospital. All procedures were conducted in compliance with the guidelines and regulations of Helsinki declarations.

Cell model construction and cell culture

We established in vitro MASLD models by treating HepG2 cells with free fatty acids (FFA). The HepG2 cells were obtained from the cell bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences’ Typical Culture Preservation Committee. To simulate a high-fat milieu, high-fat HepG2 cells were constructed utilizing sodium palmitate (PA) and sodium oleate (OA). When HepG2 cells reached 70%–80% confluency, approximately 1 × 105 cells were seeded into 6- well plates and incubated for 48 h (based on previous studies indicating that 48 h is the optimal incubation time [20]). A complete culture medium containing only the solvent was used as the control group. Lipid accumulation was assessed using Oil Red O staining.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

In accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (TAKERA, Tokyo, Japan), and cDNA was synthesized through reverse transcription using RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme). The target genes were amplified and detected using SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Ruizhen Bio, Guangzhou, China) on the QuantStudio 3/5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher, Scientific, USA). The relative mRNA expression of PRKAA2 was quantified by the 2-ΔΔCt technique. Primer sequences targeting PRKAA2 and PI3K/AKT pathway components (human/mouse) are detailed in Tables 1 and 2.

Western blot (WB)

We employed standard techniques to extract total proteins from HepG2 cells and utilized a biocinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit to perform protein quantification. Proteins were electrophoresed at 120 V for 90 min and subsequently transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes at 350 mA for 45 min. Membranes were blocked with a buffer solution containing 5% non-fat dry milk for 1 h at room temperature and then were washed with Tris-buffered Saline containing Tween-20 (TBST). For immunoblotting, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (ZENBIO, China) diluted 1:1000, targeting: PI3K, phospho-PI3K (p-PI3K), AKT, phospho-AKT (p-AKT), AMPKα2 (encoded by PRKAA2) [21]. Following three 10-min TBST washes, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies (ZENBIO, China; 1:10000 dilution) for 1 h. Protein loading was normalized using GAPDH/Tubulin/β-Actin (ZENBIO, China) as internal controls. Protein bands were visualized using the eBlot® electronic compression imaging device (Shanghai, China), and optical density values were calculated using the ImageJ 1.41 software.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissue specimens were prepared into paraffin sections, underwent dewaxing, hydration, and high-pressure, high-temperature antigen retrieval in sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Subsequently, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked, and sections were incubated with rabbit anti-AMPKα2 primary antibody (WANLEIBIO, shenyang, China) at 4 °C for 12 h. After phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) washes, sections were incubated with Polymer-Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit/mouse IgG polymer for 20 min, and visualization were then performed using DAB staining solution for 5 min. After rinsing with water, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin for 15 s, followed by dehydration, clearing, and mounting. IHC results were independently observed and evaluated by two pathologists. Two independent pathologists blinded to sample groups evaluated staining utilizing a semi-quantitative scoring system: percentage score categorized into 0 (< 5%), 1 (5–25%), 2 (26–50%), 3 (51–75%), 4 (> 75%); Intensity score categorized into 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), 3 (strong). A final score (range: 0–12) was calculated by multiplying the percentage and intensity scores. Samples with score > 4 were considered positive.

Cell culture and RNA interference

Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; ViaCell), penicillin and streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. For transfection experiments, cells at 70–80% confluence were transfected with PRKAA2-specific siRNA (si-PRKAA2; designed and synthesized by GenePharma, China) using siRNA-Mate plus transfection reagent (GenePharma, China) according to the manufacture’s protocol. The siRNA sequences targeting PRKAA2 were: siPRKAA2-1 (Sense: 5′-GCUGUGGAUUACUGUCAUATT-3′; Antisense: 5′-UAUGACAGUAAUCCACAGCTT-3′), siPRKAA2-2 (Sense: 5′-GCAGUGGCUUAUCAUCUUATT-3′; Antisense: 5′-UAAGAUGAUAAGCCACUGCTT-3′), siPRKAA2-3 (Sense: 5′-GCUGAAGUUUACCGAGCUATT-3′; Antisense: 5′-UAGCUCGGUAAACUUCAGCTT-3′) and siPRKAA2-4 (Sense: 5′-GCAGAGAGCCAUUCACUUUTT-3′; Antisense: 5′-AAAGUGAAUGGCUCUCUGCTT-3′). Transfection efficiency was validated by RT-qPCR analysis of PRKAA2 mRNA expression and WB assessment of AMPKα2 protein levels. To evaluate the functional consequences of PRKAA2 knockdown, lipid accumulation was assessed using Oil Red O staining.

Quantitative lipid analysis

Triglyceride (TG) levels were quantitatively assessed using TG assay kits (Shanghai Jianglai Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) according to manufacturer’s instructions.

Cytokines quantification by ELISA

The pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β and TNFα) were quantitatively measured using ELISA. Cell culture supernatant was collected after centrifugation. ELISA kits were employed: IL-6 (Shanghai Jianglai industrial Co., China), IL-1β (Shanghai Jianglai industrial Co., China) and TNFα (Wuhan USCN Business Co., China). All ELISA assays followed manufacturer protocols in technical duplicate, with standard curves exhibiting R^2 > 0.99.

Lipidomic profiling by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)

Lipidomic analysis was performed using an advanced UHPLC-Orbitrap Exploris 240 mass spectrometry system at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Samples were injected into an ACQUITY HSS T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, USA) at 40 ℃ (Flow rate: 0.4 mL/min). The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water: acetonitrile (2:98, v/v) (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B). Mass spectrometry parameters: ionization: electrospray ionization (ESI) in positive mode and negative mode; Scan mode: Data Dependent Acquisition (DDA); Mass range: 70–1050 m/z. Raw data were processed utilizing Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) software. Data were analyzed on the Majorbio cloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the “ropls” R package (Version 1.6.2). Differential lipids were selected based on Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) > 1.0, p-value < 0.05 (Student’s t-test). Pathways analysis was conducted using KEGG pathway database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) and “scipy.stats” Python packages (https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/) for enrichment analysis.

Establishment of MASLD mouse model

All animal experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (Approval No.: 2025-D0182). Briefly, 12 male C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old) were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. and randomly divided into two groups: the high-fat diet (HFD) group and the standard chow diet (SCD) group (n = 6 per group). All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) animal facility under controlled environmental conditions, with ad libitum access to water. Mice were fed their indicated respective diets for 8 weeks to establish the MASLD mouse model.

H&E staining

To evaluate the morphological features of mouse liver tissues, H&E staining was conducted following standard procedures. Briefly, liver tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and subsequently embedded in paraffin and sectioned into slices. The sections were dehydrated using gradient concentrations of ethanol and xylene, followed by brief rinse. Cell nuclei were stained with 5% Hematoxylin solution for 3–5 min. After rinsing in distilled water for 5 min, the sections were dehydrated with 85% and 95% ethanol. Counterstaining was performed with eosin solution for 5 min. After washing and dehydration, the HE-stained sections were mounted and imaged under a Nikon Eclipse E100 microscope (Japan).

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Mouse liver tissue sections were dewaxed with xylene and hydrated using gradient alcohol. According to the standard protocols, antigens were exposed by microwave repair. After deparaffinization, hydration and antigen repair, sections were incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide solution, followed by blocking with 10% goat serum. Subsequently, sections were incubated with primary antibodies against AMPK (R23464,1:500), p-PI3K (R381555,1:500), and p-AKT (R241468,1:500). Afterward, Polymer-Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated universal secondary antibody IgG (anti-mouse/rabbit, AFIHC001, AiFang Biologcal, China) was applied. The sections were then reacted with TRY-570, TRY-520 and TRY-650 fluorescent dyes, respectively. Counterstaining was performed with DAPI (AiFang Biological, China), and sections were mounted using an anti-fluorescence quenching medium. Images were captured using an eight-channel fluorescence digital slide scanner (model AF-KL-20-8, AiFang Biologcal, China).

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

To investigate miRNA-mRNA interactions, we performed a dual-luciferase reporter assay. The pmirGLO vector (No.1007039-0000) was utilized in this assay. Various 3’‐UTR sequences, synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co.,Ltd., were inserted into the vector to construct reporter plasmids. 293T cells were seeded in 24‐well plates and transfected with the respective reporter plasmid using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent. After 48 h post-transfection, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual‐Luciferase Assay System (E608001-0100) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and data calculations were performed using R software (version 4.2.3) with analyses completed by November 2024. Differential expression was analyzed using the t-test, while Pearson’s correlation analysis was applied to evaluate the relationships between variables. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, with the following designations: ns (not significant, p > 0.05), *(p < 0.05), **(p < 0.01), and ***(p < 0.001).

Results

Identification and screening of DEGs in MASLD

By comparing the MASLD group with the control group, we identified 195 up-regulated DEGs in the GSE48452 dataset (Fig. 1A) and 314 up-regulated DEGs in the GSE159676 dataset (Fig. 1B). The intersection of these two datasets revealed 43 DEGs (Fig. 1C), consisting 38 mRNAs and 5 non-coding RNAs. Subsequently, the PPI network was constructed for these 38 mRNAs (Fig. 1D), and functional enrichment analysis using GO identified 6 genes significantly associated with lipid homeostasis (Fig. 1E): FABP4, CYP7A1, PRKAA2, TREM2, ACSM2A, and LPL. A PPI network of these 6 genes was also generated (Fig. 1F). Notably, PRKAA2 and ACSM2A exhibited a node degree of the PPI network, suggesting their potential specificity in certain biological processes. Given their unique network characteristics and relevance to lipid homeostasis, PRKAA2 and ACSM2A were identified as hub genes likely involved in MASLD progression and were selected for further analyses.

Differential expression analysis and diagnostic value of PRKAA2 and ACSM2A in MASLD

To investigate the expression levels of PRKAA2 and ACSM2A, we analyzed their levels of PRKAA2 were upregulated in MASLD compared to the control group both in GSE48452 (p<0.01, Fig. 2A) and GSE159676 datasets (p<0.05, Fig. 2B). Furthermore, analysis of the GSE49541 dataset revealed that PRKAA2 expression was higher in the advanced group compared to the mild group (p<0.05, Fig. 2C). In GSE48452 and GSE159676 datasets, the levels of ACSM2A were higher in the MASLD group (p<0.05, Fig. 2D and E). However, no significant difference in ACSM2A expression was observed between the mild and the advanced MASLD group in the GSE49541 dataset (p > 0.05, Fig. 2F).

To evaluate the diagnostic potential of PRKAA2 and ACSM2A, the ROC curve was constructed. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) for PRKAA2 was 0.7619 in the GSE48452 dataset and 0.8333 in the GSE159676 dataset (Fig. 2G and H). For ACSM2A, the AUC values were 0.6944 and 0.9761 in the GSE48452 and GSE159676 datasets, respectively (Fig. 2I and J). While ACSM2A showed high diagnostic potential, its inconsistent expression across disease progression and an AUC value below 0.7 in the GSE48452 dataset limited its reliability.

Given that PRKAA2 exhibited significant differential expression not only between MASLD and control groups, but also between mild and advanced MASLD stages, along with consistently high diagnostic AUC values (AUC > 0.7) in both datasets, it was selected for further analysis. In contrast, ACSM2A was excluded from our subsequent studies due to its lack of involvement in MASLD progression and inconsistent diagnostic performance. These findings suggested that PRKAA2 may play a critical role the occurrence and progression of MASLD and holds promise as a diagnostic biomarker for MASLD.

Functional enrichment analysis was performed to explore the biological roles of the DEGs.

Relationship between PRKAA2 and immune cell infiltration in MASLD

In the GSE48452 dataset, the expression of PRKAA2 exhibited a significantly positive correlation with Macrophages M0 (R = 0.523, p<0.05) and negative correlations with Dendritic cells activated (R=-0.511, p<0.05) and Neutrophils (R=-0.532, p<0.05, Fig. 4A). In the GSE159676 dataset, PRKAA2 expression showed a strong negative correlation with T cells gamma delta (R=-0.801, p<0.05, Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that PRKAA2 may play a crucial role in modulating the immune micro-environment, potentially influencing the activity and distribution of immune cells in MASLD.

The miRNAs obtained from these two databases were intersected to obtain one miRNA, hsa-let-7b-5p, which was identified as the core miRNA (Fig. 5C). Subsequent analysis using the ENCORI database predicted four lncRNAs (RNF216P1, THBS3-AS1, MEG3 and AL359762.3) that potentially regulate hsa-let-7b-5p. Based on these interactions, we constructed a ceRNA network depicting the regulatory relationships of PRKAA2 (Fig. 5D).

PRKAA2 expression at cell levels and tissue levels

Our prior bioinformatics analysis identified that PRKAA2 was enriched in MASLD. To explore the expressions of PRKAA2, we established a free fatty acids (FFA) milieu by combining PA and OA [22]. Oil Red O staining results revealed that FFA group exhibited significantly greater lipid accumulation compared to the control cell group (Fig. 6A), confirming the successful construction of FFA and the control cell models. RT-qPCR results showed that the relative mRNA expression of PRKAA2 in the FFA group was substantially higher than that in the control group (p<0.001, Fig. 6B). To explore the relationship between PRKAA2 and hsa-let-7b-5p, PmirGLO-PRKAA2 wild-type (WT) (Fig. 10A) and PmirGLO-PRKAA2 mutant (MUT) (Fig. 10B) reporter plasmids were constructed. This assay validated hsa-let-7b-5p targeting of PRKAA2, exerting regulatory effects by binding to the predicted site of PRKAA2-WT.

Discussions

MASLD has emerged as a globally prevalent chronic hepatic disorder, pathologically defined by diffuse hepatocellular steatosis [13, 23]. Epidemiological data indicate that MASLD currently affects approximately 25% of the adult population [24]. While early intervention could facilitate complete recovery, roughly 20% of cases progress to end-stage liver disease [25], underscoring the critical need for timely diagnosis and therapeutic intervention of MASLD.

Recent therapeutic strategies have focused on targeting AMPK, a key regulator of cellular metabolism. AMPK activation demonstrates significant potential for managing various metabolic disorders, including MASLD and its progressive forms [26–31]. AMPK orchestrates hepatic lipid metabolism by modulating fatty acid synthesis, cholesterol production, triglyceride accumulation, and protein metabolism while enhancing oxidative processes [9, 32, 33]. As a heterotrimeric protein complex, AMPK composed of α1 and α2 catalytic subunits, β1 and β2 scaffolding subunits, and γ1, γ2, and γ3 regulatory subunits [31, 34, 35]. The PRKAA2 gene encodes the catalytically essential AMPKα2 subunit [21], located at chromosome 1p31 (positions 56,645,317 to 56,715,335) and comprising 12 exons [11]. Functioning as both catalytic and regulatory component of AMPK, PRKAA2 serves as a crucial cellular energy sensor through AMP/ATP ratio monitoring [36]. Inhibition of AMPK is associated with phosphorylation at Ser485/491 in AMPKα2 in a variety of tissues including skeletal muscle, adipocytes and hepatocytes [13]. Specifically, AKT-mediated Ser491 phosphorylation of AMPKα2 (encoded by PRKAA2) under high glucose suppresses AMPK activity [37].

Emerging evidence links elevated PRKAA2 expression to metabolic dysregulation, particularly through its impact on tricarboxylic acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation [38]. These molecular alterations contribute to the pathogenesis of metabolic disorders including insulin resistance and MASLD [14, 15]. Our bioinformatics analysis identified PRKAA2 as a significantly upregulated gene in MASLD. Multi-dataset examination consistently demonstrated PRKAA2 upregulation correlating with disease progression. Experimental results from RT-qPCR, WB, IHC and IF consistently revealed significant PRKAA2 upregulation in MASLD models. Functional studies involving PRKAA2 knockdown demonstrated that suppressing PRKAA2 expression effectively attenuated hepatic lipid accumulation and reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels. These findings indicate that PRKAA2 not only shows upregulated expression in MASLD but may also play a pivotal role in driving disease pathogenesis.

KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that PRKAA2 is associated with the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. In vivo studies demonstrated elevated expression of PRKAA2 and key PI3K/AKT components in MASLD mice liver tissues. Correspondingly, in vitro experiments showed that PRKAA2 knockdown significantly reduced PI3K and AKT phosphorylation ratios, indicating suppressed pathway activity. Integrating bioinformatics predictions with experimental validation, we propose that PRKAA2 contributes to MASLD development by modulating the PI3K/AKT pathway. Emerging findings suggested that targeted inhibition of PI3K/AKT pathway may offer a novel therapeutic approach for MASLD [39, 40]. Previous studies of Kim et al. [41] had shown that PI3K/AKT pathway plays a pivotal role in hepatocyte damage. Qian Ye and Mansour et al. [42, 43] indicated that the PI3K/AKT pathway activation promotes hepatic lipid accumulation and accelerates the transition to MASH. Our studies extend previous observations by identifying PRKAA2 as an upstream regulator of this pathogenic pathway, distinguishing our work from earlier research focused primarily on AMPK activation.

Immune cell infiltration analysis demonstrated a significant positive correlation between PRKAA2 expression and macrophage infiltration, while revealing negative associations with activated dendritic cells, neutrophils, and gamma delta T cells. These results strongly suggest PRKAA2’s critical role in modulating the hepatic immune micro-environment during MASLD progression. This observation aligns with existing literature documenting PRKAA2’s immunoregulatory functions [44], particularly its capacity to restore impaired macrophage immune responses when combined with AMPK activation [45]. The immunological implication of PRKAA2 appear multifaceted, as evidence by studies showing exacerbated dendritic cell activation following AMPKα2 deletion [46] and its critical involvement in neutrophil-mediated vascular repair processes [47]. Furthermore, Domin et al. [38] had indicated PRKAA2 in T-cell dysfunction, specifically through promoting CD8 + T-cell exhaustion and facilitating CD4 + regulatory T-cell development, potentially contributing to immune escape mechanisms. While our study provides novel insights into PRKAA2-mediated immune cell interactions in MASLD and identifies potential regulatory pathways, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying its immunomodulatory functions require further elucidation, and PRKAA2 orchestrates immune responses in MASLD represents a crucial direction for future research.

Our experimental evidence suggested that hsa-let-7b-5p exerted regulatory effects by specifically binding to the predicted target site in PRKAA2-WT. Through comprehensive bioinformatics analysis, we identified hsa-let-7b-5p as a core regulatory miRNA and subsequently predicted its upstream lncRNAs (RNF216P1, THBS3-AS1, MEG3 and AL359762.3). The established ceRNA network reveals that PRKAA2 plays a central role in MASLD pathogenesis through modulation by hsa-let-7b-5p. According to the ceRNA theory, miRNA can bind to mRNA, inhibiting mRNA expression, while lncRNA can sequester miRNAs to alleviate their repressive effects on target mRNA, thereby regulating disease progression [48]. This network operates through competitive molecular interaction: miRNA typically bind to mRNA 3′-UTR, leading to the degradation or translational repression [49], and dysregulation of this balance may contribute to MASLD development and progression [50, 51]. Notably, our findings align with previous predictions by Simino et al. [52], who identified PRKAA2 as a potential target of the let-7 miRNA family. This convergence of our findings and previous evidence strengthens the biological plausibility of our proposed regulatory mechanism in MASLD pathophysiology.

Although our findings have been substantiated through comprehensive bioinformatics analyses, and experimental validation in both cellular and animal models, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the relatively limited sample sizes from public databases may introduce statistical bias. Second, while we have established the correlation between PRKAA2 and immune cell infiltration, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying its immunoregulatory functions require further elucidation. Third, the stage-specific regulatory dynamics of PRKAA2 during MASLD progression remain incompletely characterized and merit additional investigation. Fourth, while we used HepG2 cells for in vitro validation, we acknowledge that this cancer-derived cell line may not fully recapitulate primary hepatocyte physiology, potentially affecting result interpretation.

Despite these limitations, our study provides compelling evidence that PRKAA2 expression is markedly upregulated in response to high-fat conditions. Functional studies employing PRKAA2 knockdown further demonstrated its significant association with hepatic lipid accumulation and pro-inflammatory cytokines production, thereby elucidating its pathogenic role in MASLD. The identified hsa-let-7b-5p/PRKAA2/PI3K/AKT axis represents a novel regulatory mechanism in MASLD pathogenesis. From a therapeutic perspective, PRKAA2 modulation through miRNA-based approaches or specific inhibitors warrants further investigation as a potential strategy for MASLD treatment. These findings position PRKAA2 as a potentially crucial molecular determinant in MASLD pathogenesis with important implications for developing targeted therapeutic strategies.

Conclusions

In summary, our study establishes PRKAA2 as both a promising diagnostic biomarker and a potential therapeutic target for MASLD. Mechanistically, PRKAA2 appears to mediate MASLD pathogenesis through dual regulatory mechanisms: upstream modulation by hsa-let-7b-5p and downstream activation of PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. The diagnostic model developed based on PRKAA2 expression demonstrates robust sensitivity and accuracy for MASLD detection, highlighting its clinical potential. However, further large-scale studies are required to fully characterize the dynamic expression patterns of PRKAA2 across different MASLD stages and progression states.
