Successful implementation of stroke risk screening for sickle cell anemia in the DISPLACE study: results of a cluster randomized trial
Authors: Alyssa M. Schlenz, Shannon M. Phillips, Martina Mueller, Daniel Humphrey, Judson Stevens, Logan P. Williams, Robert S. Nickel, Lily Dolatshahi, Robin E. Miller, Monica L. Hulbert, Margaret T. Lee, Ofelia Alvarez, Beng Fuh, Melissa A. McNaull, Neha Bhasin, Cathy L. Melvin, Robert J. Adams, Julie Kanter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStroke risk screening using transcranial Doppler (TCD) is a critical evidence-based tool for children with sickle cell anemia (SCA) that has been poorly implemented in the United States. The Dissemination and Implementation of Stroke Prevention Looking at the Care Environment (DISPLACE) study was designed to improve rates of stroke risk screening for SCA using interventions informed by an extensive multi-level barriers and facilitators assessment. This report describes the final outcomes of a large, randomized implementation trial comparing two intervention arms: 1) an application designed to track TCD implementation, ProviderMinder™, versus 2) ProviderMinder™ plus a single coordinator intervention. All sites additionally received a rebranding and educational intervention. The primary outcome was the difference in stroke risk screening rates between intervention arms. The intervention group was compared to four sites that did not implement either intervention and to their baseline rates as secondary outcomes.MethodsThe initial part of DISPLACE included 28 sites from which 16 sites with poor stroke risk screening implementation were included in the trial and randomized to intervention arms. All sites entered patient data into a secure, customized electronic database and were required to use ProviderMinder™ for stroke risk screening data entry. Three sites were unable to adopt ProviderMinder™ and a fourth site from the original DISPLACE cohort was added to this group, resulting in thirteen intervention sites and four non-implementing sites (NIS). NIS collected data retrospectively for the same period as the implementation trial. A generalized quasi-likelihood Poisson mixed effects regression model compared screening rates between groups and timepoints while controlling for baseline screening rates and site size. Unadjusted stroke risk screening rates were also compared via two-proportion Z-tests for all outcomes.ResultsThe intervention-by-timepoint interaction indicated statistically significant improvement for the ProviderMinder™ arm relative to the combined intervention arm (difference of 10.0%) and for the intervention group (both arms) compared to NIS (difference of 15.9%). Screening rates increased by 28.0% from baseline to intervention, with an overall rate of 76.8%.ConclusionsOur intervention approach in DISPLACE significantly improved stroke risk screening for children with SCA, with procedure-patient tracking emerging as an important component for improving care.Trial registrationClinical trial number: ClinicalTrials.gov; NCT04173026; 6/4/2020; https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04173026?cond=NCT04173026&rank=1Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13012-025-01462-3.

Background

Overt stroke is a severe complication of sickle cell anemia (SCA) that can result in death or life-long impairment in cognitive, academic, and vocational outcomes [1]. Prior to modern stroke prevention practices, children with SCA had an estimated 11% chance of having an overt stroke prior to 20 years of age [2]. Stroke prevention for SCA was dramatically altered over two decades ago with results of the Stroke Prevention Trial in Sickle Cell Anemia (STOP). STOP identified the utility of transcranial Doppler (TCD) to detect children with SCA at high risk for stroke. The trial found an overall stroke risk reduction of 92% for children with an abnormal result who were promptly started on transfusion therapy [3]. The STOP protocol was subsequently adopted in multiple guidelines for SCA [4], most recently via the 2020 American Society of Hematology (ASH) cerebrovascular guidelines, which recommend annual screening using TCD for children with SCA between the ages of 2 to 16 years followed by initiation of transfusion therapy following an abnormal result [1]. While evidence suggests the incidence of stroke in SCA has been reduced with the STOP protocol [5], inconsistent implementation of the protocol has been a longstanding barrier to fully realizing a “stroke free generation” for SCA [5–9].

The Dissemination and Implementation of Stroke Prevention Looking at the Care Environment (DISPLACE) study was funded by the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) to improve stroke risk screening rates for SCA using TCD. DISPLACE was conducted in four parts: 1) baseline evaluation of TCD practices across a 28-site consortium using extensive chart review; 2) multi-level ecological assessment of barriers and facilitators to stroke risk screening through qualitative interviews with patient/caregivers and providers, which was used to inform intervention development; 3) a randomized implementation trial of interventions with 16 sites with the lowest stroke risk screening rates; and 4) comparison of the intervention group to 4 sites from part 1 that did not implement the intervention (NIS). Part 1 of DISPLACE confirmed the evidence-practice gap seen in previous data showing poor implementation of stroke risk screening, with an overall rate of 49.9% of eligible children in the consortium with documented screening annually (range: 30.9–74.7%) [10]. Part 2 identified specific, remediable barriers and facilitators to stroke risk screening [11] that informed the development of three interventions for the implementation trial: 1) a custom designed tracking application for stroke risk screening called ProviderMinder™ [12]; 2) a designated site single coordinator; and 3) a rebranding and educational initiative [13, 14].

We previously reported findings from site readiness and mid- and endpoint process evaluation for the implementation trial [12, 14]. In brief, site readiness assessments suggested that most sites were motivated to adopt the interventions and had developed implementation plans, but sites expressed concern about fidelity (e.g., consistency) for the educational initiative and feasibility (e.g., potential clinic team burden, organizational approval) for ProviderMinder™. Mid- and endpoint assessments with providers and coordinators suggested implementation successes in acceptability and appropriateness (e.g., perceived value, ease of use, and fit) for both the educational initiative and for ProviderMinder™as well as low costs and sustainability (e.g., future adaptability) of ProviderMinder™. Key implementation barriers were fidelity and penetration (e.g., inconsistent use within clinic teams) for the educational initiative and feasibility (e.g., time burden; workflow challenges, such as redundancy and lack of integration with the electronic medical record, EMR) for ProviderMinder™. This report describes the final clinical outcomes of the implementation trial as well as additional implementation factors that influenced trial outcomes.

Methods

Design

DISPLACE part 3 was a randomized trial comparing two primary intervention arms within the intervention group: sites that received only a provider-focused application, ProviderMinder™, versus sites that received the ProviderMinder™ application plus a designated single coordinator intervention. All sites additionally received a third implementation intervention, a patient-level rebranding and educational initiative. Part 4 included the post-hoc addition of non-randomized sites to account for potential pre-/post-differences based on time (described below). As detailed in the introduction, part 1 of DISPLACE identified baseline stroke risk screening rates across 28 sites for years 2012–2016. The original 28-site consortium is described in a previous paper [10]. Sixteen sites with the lowest baseline screening rates and with adequate data entry were selected to join the part 3 trial and were randomized to an intervention arm stratified by site size (number of patients). Importantly, part 1 included all children with SCA ages 2 to 16 years in the assessment of baseline stroke risk screening rates while the trial (parts 3 and 4) focused on the age group at highest stroke risk (ages 2–7 years) [2]. The intervention trial spanned 6/1/2020–6/30/2022 and the trial duration was the same for all sites. All study procedures were approved by Institutional Review Boards from the central site, the University of Alabama at Birmingham, and each local site.

Implementation framework and interventions

The DISPLACE implementation method was informed by the Interactive Systems Framework for Dissemination and Implementation (ISF) [15]. The multi-ecological model of health was also used during the barriers and facilitators assessment to inform implementation interventions in part 2 (previously described) [11, 16]. The ISF is comprised of three systems that must work together to effectively move research into practice: prevention synthesis and translation, prevention support, and prevention delivery.

The prevention synthesis and translation system for this study consisted of several components. The use of TCD for stroke risk screening in SCA is aligned with the 2014 NHLBI and the subsequent 2020 ASH cerebrovascular guidelines [1], which were released during the initial stages of the study. These guidelines set the standards for this study. During part 1 of DISPLACE, we collected a baseline assessment of stroke risk screening rates to inform the trial and to provide feedback to sites about their current rates. In addition, site personnel underwent quantitative and qualitative assessment of their clinical practices specific to stroke prevention, which were collected and disseminated back to sites to understand variations in practice and areas for improvement [17, 18].

Three implementation interventions were developed following the extensive barriers and facilitators assessment in part 2. These interventions were selected with attention to intervention characteristic factors most likely to influence implementation of interventions: cost, complexity, and adaptability [11]. First, a rebranding and educational initiative was delivered to all sites (part 1 sites and all part 3 and 4 sites). For this intervention, providers and clinic teams were asked to adopt a new patient-informed rebranded term “Sickle Stroke Screen” when educating families about TCD for stroke risk screening. (Prior to this intervention, the term “TCD screening” was used among sites.) An educational pamphlet and poster templates for clinic spaces were also provided for sites to use and adapt to meet their needs [13, 14]. In year 2 of the trial, translated materials were also provided in Spanish, French, Swahili, and Haitian Creole based on site feedback.

Second, all sites received orientation on the use of a procedure-patient tracking application, ProviderMinder™ [12], for the provider and clinic team. ProviderMinder™ is a customized, web-based application developed specifically for DISPLACE. Sites were expected to use ProviderMinder™ to track scheduling, completion, results, and follow-up treatment based on screening results. ProviderMinder™ had several features designed to create awareness of screenings, including notifications for missed screenings (which required either data entry or clicking a snooze option), weekly emailed reports for patients due or overdue for screening, and the ability to run reports at any time. Sites could adapt ProviderMinder™ to meet their specific provider and clinic team needs, such as by providers entering information themselves or assigning one or more clinical or administrative team leaders to provide support. ProviderMinder™ was beta tested by two site providers prior to implementation and sites provided iterative feedback for improvement early in year 1 of the trial.

Third, half of sites were randomized to a single coordinator intervention in addition to using the ProviderMinder™ application. Prior to DISPLACE, many sites used a team of nurses, coordinators, and others to schedule children for screening and provide education. Sites in this intervention arm designated a single coordinator who would be the responsible person for education, scheduling, and coordination for stroke risk screening (e.g., coordination with other clinic team members and departments or parts of the hospital involved with scheduling or providing stroke risk screening). For education, the coordinator was expected to either provide education to families themselves or to reach out to other team members and direct patients to educational resources. When possible, the single coordinator was also asked to facilitate setting up reminders for patients about their stroke risk screening appointment.

Prevention support was provided in several ways to facilitate intervention delivery by sites, who were expected to implement the interventions throughout the entire two-year intervention period. First, regarding ProviderMinder™, sites received a live or recorded tutorial and were asked to practice prior to implementation. Site readiness surveys and follow-up calls informed by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [19] and adapted to the DISPLACE interventions were also conducted with site providers or designated team members to support implementation plans for all three interventions and to address any questions or barriers to implementation prior to the start of the formal implementation period. Of note, the site readiness period coincided with the onset of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. During the trial, monthly calls were held with site team members, and technical support from the developer for ProviderMinder™ and from the central team was present throughout the trial duration. These supports allowed local sites to engage in prevention delivery with continuous support from the central study team. Additional process evaluation using surveys and interviews with providers and coordinators was completed with sites at study mid- and endpoint, which mirrored the site readiness questions (results detailed in separate publications) [12, 14]. Initial adoption challenges and addition of non-implementing sites

Following the site readiness period, three sites were unable to overcome adoption barriers to implementation (one from the ProviderMinder™ arm and two from the combined intervention arm). The primary barrier to implementation adoption was difficulty obtaining organizational approval and support for the ProviderMinder™ application. Thus, the central team decided to adapt the original protocol and allocate these sites to a non-implementing site (NIS) group that would allow us to account for potential pre-post-differences based on time. This decision would further allow for continuation and measurement of the educational initiative alone. A fourth site was also allocated to NIS because the selected site was known to have implemented the educational and rebranding initiative (this site was not originally randomized as their baseline screening rates were 0.5% above those selected). Site characteristics

The original 16 sites included in the trial comprised four small (< 200 total patients), eight medium (201 to 400 total patients), and four large (> 400 total patients) sites. Regions of the United States represented included the Southeast (eight sites), Northeast (four sites), Midwest (three sites), and West (one site). Three sites were allocated to NIS after being unable to adopt ProviderMinder™, including one small and one medium site located in the Southeast and one medium site in the West. The fourth NIS was a large site located in the Southeast. One NIS in the Southeast had a greater role as a community facility (as opposed to other sites with greater academic affiliations).

Randomization

As the primary study interventions being compared focused on provider- and systems-level changes to care, rather than patient-level interventions, site was the unit of randomization and the unit for analysis. For site randomization, we followed the design and randomization strategy employed by the Community Intervention Trial for Smoking Cessation [21, 22]. Using this procedure, we identified site pairs based on size by "pairing" the smallest two clinical sites, the next smallest two clinical sites, etc. Within each pair, one clinical site was assigned to the ProviderMinder™ arm and one to the ProviderMinder™ and the single coordinator arm using the following double randomization procedure [22]: (1) each member within a pair was assigned the color red or black using a computer generated pseudorandom number; (2) the colors red or black were then randomly assigned to ProviderMinder™ or the combined intervention arm using a computerized "coin-toss" procedure. This randomization strategy was implemented within a customized data capture system detailed below (WebDCU™).

Data collection

Enrollment

Sites were asked to identify patients using International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9/ICD-10) codes and local databases then validate that they had SCA. Patients had to have the following: 1) SCA diagnosis (HbSS or HbS-beta zero thalassemia) or rarer genotype (that mimic SCA and are eligible for stroke risk screening) as confirmed by history of high-performance liquid chromatography, hemoglobin electrophoresis, isoelectric focusing, or other confirmatory diagnostic procedure already available in the medical record; 2) born between the years 2012–2018; 3) seen by the designated institution (documented in the medical record) a minimum of two times in either the inpatient or outpatient setting between the years 1/1/2018 and 12/31/2019 (to confirm that the patients “belonged” to the specific institution). Patients were excluded if they were already receiving primary or secondary stroke prevention with chronic red cell transfusion therapy (CRCT). For age range, our initial goal was to target children between the ages of 2 to 7; however, for eligibility purposes, it was easier to identify a date range (instead of using age) to enroll children in ProviderMinder™. At the start date of 6/1/2020, the patient age range registered in ProviderMinder™ was as low as 1 year and as high as 8 years of age; however, no children received a TCD prior to age 2 as per guidelines.

Patients meeting the above criteria were registered into a secure, customized electronic database called WebDCU™ (described previously) [10] unless they had been previously registered in part 1 of DISPLACE and assigned a unique identifier. At enrollment, birth date (to calculate current age), demographic information (race/ethnicity, sex, and insurance), sickle cell genotype, and current medications were recorded. In addition, the date of last stroke risk screening with TCD was recorded to determine when the next screening was due. At end of study, sites recorded if patients relocated to another provider, started CRCT, died, or other reason(s) for which they were no longer eligible. Data were also collected on how many patients had not been seen (at all) at the institution during the intervention period; however, these patients were not removed for analysis if they met the original eligibility criteria and were supposed to have had appropriate screening.

Data collection for the intervention group

For sites allocated to the intervention group (both arms), WebDCU™ was used to transfer information into the ProviderMinder™ application. Information was de-identified using the patient’s WebDCU™ identifier, age, date of most recent stroke risk screening, and the result of the last screening. For each new stroke risk screening appointment, the site entered: 1) whether the patient came to the appointment; 2) if the screening was completed; and 3) screening result. Depending on the result, the application would ask for the next screening date or would prompt the user about another action (e.g., repeating the screening, starting a new treatment, or other action). The application continued to alert the site every three days until information was recorded. Sites were also able to enter if the patient was no longer eligible due to relocating to another clinic, starting CRCT, death, or other reason.

Data collection for the NIS Group

For NIS, all data were entered into WebDCU™ following extraction from the medical record. Sites were asked to retrospectively enter any/all stroke risk screenings that occurred for each patient during the intervention date range (6/1/2020–6/30/2022) and the results. Similar to intervention sites, NIS recorded if the patient was no longer eligible due to relocating to another clinic, starting CRCT, death, or other reason.

Outcomes

The trial objective was to influence sites through interventions that would ultimately impact patients (as measured by screening rates). Screening rates are reported overall, by site, or by intervention arm based on aggregated patient data. The primary outcome of the implementation trial was to assess the difference in stroke risk screening rates between intervention arms (ProviderMinder™ versus combined ProviderMinder™ and single coordinator), which was examined both overall (both years combined) and for each individual intervention year. Two secondary outcomes were also examined: 1) difference in stroke risk screening rates for each intervention arm compared to NIS and 2) pre-post analysis of baseline versus intervention stroke risk screening rates. For the latter analysis, baseline stroke risk screening rates only included children ages 2 to 7 (from part 1 of DISPLACE) to accurately compare to the age group in the trial. All patients within this age range with two consecutive years of data in the part 1 database were chosen as this matched the duration of the intervention period. If a child had multiple sets of two consecutive years, the first set of years was chosen.

Sample size

Our trial sample size estimation of 16 sites was based on this initial power analysis. For comparison of the two intervention arms in stroke risk screening rate change from pre- to post-intervention, with 8 participating clinical sites per arm, we had 85% power to detect a difference of 1.6 standardized units (i.e., the means of the two intervention groups would be 1.6 standard deviations apart) considered clinically relevant based on significance level of 0.05 (two-sided test). For the comparison of pre-to-post intervention stroke risk screening rates across all sites a sample size of 16 sites had 85% power to detect a difference in mean stroke risk screening rates of 30%, for standard deviations of the differences in pre-post screening rates as large as 38% (nQuery Sample Size Software). While this power analysis used independent and paired t-tests for sample size calculations, for the final analyses, rates rather than means were compared. Therefore, Poisson regression and z-tests for comparison of proportions are reported below and in the appropriate tables.

Statistical analysis

Sites were matched by size and randomly assigned to the ProviderMinder™ or combined ProviderMinder™ and single coordinator intervention arm within matched pairs to ensure comparable group sizes. Because patients could be enrolled at different ages and in different study years, each patient’s age was calculated in reference to the trial start date (06/01/2020). Descriptive statistics are reported for the intervention and NIS groups to characterize the study population. However, this report focuses on site-level outcomes. Stroke risk screening rates are reported overall across all sites within the intervention and NIS groups as well as within each site to allow for assessment of between site variation. The primary analysis used a quasi-likelihood Poisson mixed effects model to investigate the relationship between the interaction effect of intervention arms with timepoints (baseline, years 1 + 2) on screening rates, while incorporating the population size (number of patients who should have had a risk screening) as an offset parameter, site as random effect to indicate the repeated measures, a log link function for the outcome variable, and a dispersion parameter to adjust for any overdispersion in the model. The model was adjusted for the site size (small, medium, large). Rate ratios with 95% confidence intervals are reported for the quasi-likelihood Poisson mixed effects model. For ease of interpretation, unadjusted rates were then compared via two-proportion Z-tests. Further, differences in change from baseline for the intervention and NIS groups are presented in a difference-in-difference graph (Fig. 2). An alpha level of 0.05 or less was used to determine statistical significance. Fidelity and feasibility challenges during the implementation trial

We queried two sites whose screening rates were exceptionally low compared to their baseline rates (sites 3 and 12 in Table 6). Both sites reported that fidelity issues from failure to consistently enter data directly into ProviderMinder™ contributed to their low reported rates. These sites were also affected by feasibility factors, including turnover in the provider and coordinator roles in year 2 of the study. As this inconsistency represented an implementation issue, we chose to keep the reported rates in ProviderMinder™ in the final data analysis. One site (3) agreed to a post-hoc review to determine screening rates for patients registered in ProviderMinder™ and found an actual screening rate of 58.1% (versus 21.3% entered into ProviderMinder™) highlighting the issue with timely data entry.

Discussion

DISPLACE was designed to improve stroke risk screening rates for children with SCA. The randomized trial included evaluation of sites randomized to a tracking application, ProviderMinder™, versus those randomized to ProviderMinder™ plus a single coordinator, with all sites additionally receiving a rebranding and educational initiative. In addition, DISPLACE evaluated intervention arms relative to NIS and relative to baseline stroke risk screening rates. The results showed a significant improvement in stroke risk screening across intervention arms. While the ProviderMinder™ only intervention arm demonstrated a stronger percent improvement relative to the combined ProviderMinder™ and single coordinator arm, overall percent improvement relative to baseline (28.0%) was substantial, highlighting notable success for both intervention arms.

While there have not been any previous large-scale implementation trials published for patients with SCA specifically, our results share similarities with several single-site quality improvement (QI) initiatives in SCA that improved stroke risk screening rates. A previous study by Crosby and colleagues [25] improved initial stroke risk screening rates for young children with SCA using a combination of patient and provider education, EMR tracking, Child Life, and scheduling support. Similarly, Singh and colleagues [26] used a combination of an EMR registry, case management support from nursing, and Child Life. Finally, Edwards and colleagues [27] used a combination of a TCD data repository, regular reports to provider of stroke risk screening rates, and clarification of team member roles. These QI initiatives are highlighted because they utilized tracking, coordination, and education supports like the present study, though they did not intend to compare the interventions to assess which was most effective. When comparing ProviderMinder™ to the combined ProviderMinder™ and single coordinator arm in the current study, we observed a statistically significant higher rate of success with ProviderMinder™ alone (difference of about 10.0% overall). We could not identify a systematic reason for these differences based on our process evaluation [12]. However, one key difference between intervention arms was the greater expected role of the coordinator in the combined intervention arm, which may have resulted in more coordinator-led processes in the combined arm and more provider-led processes in the ProviderMinder™arm. Providers may have had greater ability to streamline or adjust workflows or to directly impact patients, which may have led to stronger improvements during the trial. Other feasibility factors, such as personnel turnover, were also considered; however, turnover affected only two sites for the provider and coordinator roles specifically (one from each intervention arm), and there were no other systematic differences that were specific to one intervention arm versus another.

DISPLACE was focused on identifying interventions that could be feasibly implemented in diverse medical clinics with different EMR systems and with low baseline stroke risk screening rates. Most sites in DISPLACE successfully implemented the interventions, though three sites struggled with initial adoption barriers for ProviderMinder™ (who were re-allocated to NIS) and two sites reported challenges with fidelity and feasibility in implementing the interventions that affected documented screening rates in ProviderMinder™ for year 2 of the intervention period. Thus, while we expect that many clinics would benefit from these types of interventions, our trial suggests that a subset may have challenges with implementation without broader organizational support. Similarly, we excluded sites from entering the trial due to poor data entry in part 1. These excluded sites may have had similar struggles with organizational support and were not represented in the trial. In addition, it is unclear if sites with high initial baseline screening rates would achieve similar improvements or if an alternative approach is needed to reach closer to 100% screening rates, though some sites achieved very high rates in the study (as high as 95.0%).

There are important limitations to consider in this study. First, the implementation trial coincided with the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic and it is possible that overall screening rates observed in our study are lower than what would have occurred without the pandemic. We previously reported on a survey of 24 DISPLACE part 1 sites regarding stroke risk screening using TCD during the pandemic and found that only 67% of sites were still conducting screening during May 2020 (just prior to the start of the implementation trial) [28]. Given that sites were geographically dispersed, there were varying levels of in person visit restrictions during the intervention years that fluctuated with COVID-19 rates and local policies. However, given the overall success of the trial, while impacted by COVID-19, we can hypothesize the improvements seen with screening rates would have been even higher. Three sites were also unable to initiate interventions and two sites struggled with implementation fidelity during the second year of the trial. Feasibility factors were key challenges reported by these specific sites that may have been worsened by the pandemic.

Second, since all sites received the educational and rebranding initiative, the potential benefit of this specific intervention is unclear, though we attempted to measure the effect in NIS. Two of four NIS demonstrated improvement in screening rates relative to their baseline, which may reflect the educational initiative or changes in practices that resulted from participating in the trial (e.g., change in provider behavior, site provider change for one site). Because there was not an easy way to restrict provider behavior around education once sites learned this was an important facilitator to screening rates, we provided the educational intervention to all sites. One prior study found that education alone plus patient reminders were effective at improving screening rates [29], though our process evaluation results suggest that fidelity (i.e., consistency) in education is a potential factor. NIS in our study were not randomized and three may have had more barriers to screening relative to intervention sites given adoption barriers they initially faced that led them to being allocated to NIS. Finally, it is essential to consider financial support provided by the trial to sites for interventions, which may have contributed to improved screening rates in both intervention arms. Notably, our process evaluation results suggested that the majority of sites did not incur implementation costs beyond the study resources [12, 14].

The DISPLACE trial has important implications for achieving widespread adoption of stroke risk screening for SCA. In particular, the success of ProviderMinder™ and other QI initiatives utilizing centralized tracking for stroke risk screening [25–27] suggest that methods that alert clinic teams to use guideline-informed care are an important part of ensuring that children are being screened. These same methods may also help prevent children from becoming lost to follow-up when they miss a single or repeated appointments due to alarms set within the application, as previous research suggests that children overdue for TCD screening are often lost to routine hematology care [8]. While our study used a customized web-based application, there are other mechanisms that may serve a similar purpose. For example, EMR-based registries [25, 26, 30] or large-scale registries, such as the Globin Research Network for Data and Discovery (GRNDaD) may be particularly helpful [31]. The benefit of a national registry is that it allows capture of transient patients (e.g., those who move from one state to another) who might otherwise be removed from local EMR tracking. Further, a national registry would allow SCA centers to work together to enable direct EMR to registry transfer to lessen the burden of data extraction (a challenge noted in our process evaluation [12]). The recent addition of SCA (and stroke risk screening specifically) to US News and World Report rankings may also provide further incentive for health care organizations to track stroke risk screening for children with SCA and to provide more organizational and financial support when rates are low, which is an important and long overdue advancement towards health equity in SCA [32].

Conclusion

The development of guidelines and treatment recommendations are only as useful as they are implemented. Data from the initial part of the DISPLACE project shows profoundly poor implementation of sickle stroke screening in SCA. However, despite baseline findings, the DISPLACE trial demonstrates that significant improvement in stroke risk screening for SCA is achievable for many organizations. Most importantly, to meaningfully enhance clinical care, it is necessary to perform a thorough, systematic evaluation of multi-level barriers and facilitators to optimally guide interventions and improve care.
