Abstract
. We prove that non-abelian definable, definably simple groups in 1-h-minimal henselian valued fields are essentially already linear algebraic groups. Here, the group is assumed to live in the home sort. We have a similar result in pure algebraically closed valued fields of positive characteristic, under the additional assumption that the definable group is a subgroup of a linear algebraic group.

1.
Introduction

In the model theory of fields, the notion of
definable set

(solution set of a first order formula, possibly with parameters) provides an abstraction of that of
constructible set
. In the context of algebraically closed fields in the language of rings the notions coincide, whilst in real closed fields in the language of ordered rings, the definable sets are exactly the semi-algebraic sets. Definability has corresponding geometric content in other model-theoretically well-understood structures such as differentially closed fields, algebraically closed fields with an automorphism, separably closed fields,
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
, and algebraically closed valued fields. In all such structures, definable
groups
play a fundamental role, as measures of model-theoretic complexity, or of the interaction between different definable sets, or through definable group actions. Understanding the definable simple groups (or
definably simple
groups, that is, definable groups with no proper non-trivial definable normal subgroups) is often critical.

In this paper, we classify definably simple groups in the context of Henselian valued fields of characteristic 0, and, under a linearity assumption, algebraically closed valued fields of characteristic
p
. In characteristic 0, our methods apply also to
1-h-minimal
expansions of valued fields. This broad framework includes several contexts which have had extensive recent study: the ‘valued fields with analytic structure’ of Cluckers-Lipshitz
[
8
]
stemming ultimately from
Denef and van den Dries
[
12
]
; and
T
-convex expansions of power-bounded o-minimal expansions of fields
[
51
]
. See Example
2.3.3
for more detail on these settings. Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.0.1
.

(i)

Let
(K,v,\ldots)
be a 1-
h
-minimal expansion of a henselian valued field of characteristic 0. Let
G
be an infinite non-abelian group definable in the structure
(K,v,\ldots)
with universe a definable subset of
K^{n}
for some
n
, and suppose that
G
has no proper infinite definable normal subgroup.
Then there is a semisimple, almost
K
-simple
and
K
-isotropic linear algebraic
K
-group
\mathbf{H}
and a group
G^{*}
definably isomorphic to
G/Z(G)

such that

\mathbf{H}(K)^{+}\leq G^{*}\leq\mathbf{H}(K)
.

(ii)

Let
(K,v)
be an algebraically closed valued field of characteristic
p>0
, and let
G\leq\SL_{n}(K)

be an infinite non-abelian definable group which has no proper infinite definable normal subgroup. Then there is a semisimple, almost
K
-simple and
K
-isotropic linear algebraic
K
-group
\mathbf{H}
such that

G/Z(G)
is definably isomorphic to
\mathbf{H}(K)
.

For the relevant definitions concerning algebraic groups, see
Section

The conclusion in (i) says that

(Definition

) is Zariski-dense in

We are not certain whether under the given hypotheses

the conclusion will be that

Remark 1.0.2
.

We are not certain if the theorem would be correct without first replacing

Corollary 1.0.3

Proof.

The only point that needs noting is that in these cases, the underlying field is algebraically closed, real closed, or

∎

We also note a result in the

is isomorphic to a group of the form

is a non-abelian almost

We sketch the structure of the proof of Theorem
1.0.1
. There is a well-behaved notion of (topological) dimension for definable sets. A key first step, in the characteristic 0 1-
h
-minimal setting, is to endow the definable group
G
with a definable
C^{1}
-manifold structure. For this, we use results from
[
1
]
, who develop the notion of a
definable weak strictly differentiable
(dwsd) manifold, and show that any definable group can be definably equipped with structure so that it becomes a dwsd Lie group. The method has precedents in the work of Wencel

[
54
]
, itself based on a proof of Pillay
[
37
]
and ultimately on Weil’s ‘group-chunks’ argument. Given the dwsd Lie structure, the Jacobian representation then gives a homomorphism

\varphi\colon G\to\SL_{n}(K)
, and a dimension argument using definability ensures that
{\rm Im}(\varphi)
is not trivial, and hence
\Ker(\varphi)
is finite and equal to the centre
Z(G)
. We thereby replace
G
by the definable subgroup
G/Z(G)
of
\SL_{n}(K)
. This step is not needed in the characteristic
p
case
(Theorem
1.0.1
(ii)), where linearity is assumed.

The second stage works more directly when
(K,v)
is a
pure
valued field,
i.e.
does not carry definable extra (e.g. analytic) structure. Slightly over-simplifying, we take the Zariski closure of
G
in
\SL_{n}(K)
, and show that this has the form
\mathbf{H}(K)
for some semisimple almost
K
-simple
K
-isotropic algebraic group
\mathbf{H}
defined over
K
.
By dimension arguments,
G
is valuation-open in
\mathbf{H}(K)
. These dimension arguments are invalid in the more general case when
(K,v,\ldots)
is equipped with additional 1-
h
-minimal structure, and are replaced in the latter case by a Lie algebra argument: we take the Lie algebra
L(G)
of the definably almost simple linear group
G
, argue that this is a simple Lie algebra, and that
G
is embedded in the automorphism group of the Lie algebra (in place of the Zariski closure of
G
), which has the same dimension as
G
. Thus, roughly speaking, the role of
\mathbf{H}(K)
is replaced by
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(L(G))
. This last argument uses ideas from
[
34
]
.

The third stage is to show that
G
cannot be a
bounded
subgroup of
\mathbf{H}(K)
, that is, there is no
\gamma\in\Gamma
(the value group of
K
) such that all matrix entries of all elements of
G
have value greater than
\gamma
. The argument here uses ideas from Bruhat-Tits buildings, though these are not mentioned explicitly. We show that if
G
were bounded then it would embed in a variant
\nSL_{n}(\eta)
of
\SL_{n}(\mathcal{O})
, where
{\mathcal{O}}
is the valuation ring. The group
\SL_{n}(\mathcal{O})
has a chain of normal subgroups with trivial intersection (the
congruence subgroups
) which is incompatible with definable almost simplicity of
G
, and a similar argument works in
\nSL_{n}(\eta)
.

Finally, we use variants (for Krull valuations) of two results from Prasad
[
41
]
, to which he gives earlier attributions. First, by the last paragraph the group
\mathbf{H}(K)
cannot be bounded, so
a result in
[
41
]
yields that
\mathbf{H}
is
K
-isotropic, which ensures that
\mathbf{H}(K)^{+}
is almost simple and Zariski dense in
\mathbf{H}(K)
. Second, since
G
is valuation-open in
\mathbf{H}(K)
and not bounded, applying
[
41
]
with some finessing we obtain
G\geq\mathbf{H}(K)^{+}
. If
\mathbf{H}(K)^{+}
is definable then we have equality. Some additional work is involved in these applications of
[
41
]
, largely because we are dealing with arbitrary Krull valuations, rather than just valuations with archimedean value group. The biggest difficulties arise when
\Gamma
has no maximal proper convex subgroup.

2.2.
Valued fields

In most of the paper,

We implicitly always assume

As usual, the neutral element of

We write

Notation 2.2.1
.

If

of

Notation 2.2.2

For each

The family

We next introduce the (higher) leading term structures

Definition 2.2.3
.

Let

be a valued field. Let

, and define

To get some intuition about

Another point of view is that we may identify

We will consider our valued field

3.
Manifolds and Lie Groups over valued fields

Throughout this section, we shall work in a 1-
h
-minimal expansion
(K,v,\ldots)
of a valued field of characteristic 0. In Subsection
3.1
we summarise some material from
[
1
]
which ensures that our definable group
G
can be endowed with a definable differential manifold structure in an appropriate sense. Subsection
3.2
applies this, along with a line of argument in the o-minimal case from
[
34
]
, to obtain some results on the Lie algebra structure on the tangent space.

3.1.
Manifolds and Lie groups

We need a notion of definable manifold over
K
. Several such notions have been provided in
[
1
]
. The one we will be using is what the authors of
[
1
]
call a
definable weak strictly differentiable manifold
; we will abbreviate this by
dwsd manifold
. Mostly, the definition of such manifolds is what one would expect: it is a definable set
M
equipped with definable charts
U_{i}\to M
satisfying usual conditions on transition maps. However, the adjective “weak” expresses that a chart is not exactly what one might think: Instead of requiring the sets
U_{i}
to be subsets of
K^{n}
(for
n=\dim M
), one needs to allow them to be some kind of finite (étale) covers of such sets.

A concrete example where such finite covers are necessary is
the “circle”
M=\{(x,y)\in K^{2}\mid x^{2}+y^{2}=1\}
, which clearly should be a manifold. We would like to consider
U:=M\setminus\{(\pm 1,0)\}
as the disjoint union of the upper and lower half circle, but “upper” and “lower” make no sense, and there is no way to split
U

definably
into two branches. Instead, the notion of chart is modified in such a way that the entire
U
is a chart.

So here is the definition of dwsd manifolds (
[
1
, Definitions 5.1–5.3]
):

Definition 3.1.1
.

(1)

A definable map
r\colon U\to K^{n}
(where
U\subset K^{m}
for some
m
) is called
(topologically) étale
if every
u\in U
has a neighbourhood
B\subset U

such that the restriction
r|_{B}
is a homeomorphism onto its image
r(B)
(with respect to the valuation topology on
U
and on
K^{n}
).

(2)

Given two definable étale maps
r_{i}\colon U_{i}\to K^{n_{i}}
(
i=1,2
), we call a definable function
f\colon U_{1}\to U_{2}

strictly differentiable
, if every
y\in U_{1}
has a neighbourhood
B\subset U_{1}
such that
r_{1}|_{B}
is injective and
r_{2}\circ f\circ(r_{1}|_{B})^{-1}
is strictly differentiable on
r_{1}(B)
(in the sense of Definition
2.3.5
).

(3)

A
definable
n
-chart
on a definable set
M
consists
of a definable injection
\phi\colon U\hookrightarrow M
and
an étale definable map
r\colon U\to K^{n}
(where
U\subset K^{m}
for some
m
). We often call “
\phi\colon U\hookrightarrow M
” the chart, having the map
r
implicitly in mind.

(4)

A
definable weak strictly differentiable
n
-manifold
, or
dwsd
n
-manifold
for short, consists of a definable set
M
together with finitely many definable
n
-charts.
\phi_{i}\colon U_{i}\hookrightarrow M
such that

(a)

M
is covered by the images
\phi_{i}(U_{i})
;

(b)

For each pair
i,j
, the set
U_{ij}:=\phi_{i}^{-1}(\phi_{j}(U_{j}))
is open in
U_{i}
; and

(c)

the transition maps
U_{ij}\to U_{ji},\phi_{j}^{-1}\circ\phi_{i}
are strictly differentiable in the sense of (2).

We endow
M
with the unique topology turning all maps
\phi_{i}
into open immersions (which is possible by (b)). In the following, when we refer to charts of a manifold
M
, we always mean one of those
\phi_{i}
.

A
dwsd manifold
is a dwsd
n
-manifold for some
n
.

(5)

Given two dwsd manifolds
M,N
, a definable map
f\colon M\to N
is called
strictly differentiable
if for all charts
\phi_{i}\colon U_{i}\to M
and
\psi_{j}\colon V_{j}\to N
,
the set
W_{ij}:=\phi_{i}^{-1}(f^{-1}(\psi_{j}(V_{j})))
is open in
U_{i}
and
the composition
\psi_{j}^{-1}\circ f\circ\phi_{i}|_{W_{ij}}
is strictly differentiable on
W_{ij}
(in the sense of (2)). Dwsd manifolds are considered as a category using strictly differentiable definable maps as morphisms.

Remark 3.1.2
.

In the above definition, various choices have been made; we follow
[
1
]
concerning those choices; in particular:

(1)

The topology on the
U_{i}
is always the one induced from the ambient space; in contrast, its differentiable structure is induced by
r_{i}
.

(2)

On
M
, both the topology and the differentiable structure are the ones induced by the
\phi_{i}
.

(3)

It is imposed that a manifold
M
has a cover by
finitely many
charts. This implies that if
M
is an
n
-manifold, then
n
is also equal to the dimension of
M
as a definable set (in the sense of Definition
2.3.4
).

Remark 3.1.3
.

Formally, a dwsd manifold comes with a choice of finitely many charts.
However, replacing those charts by other compatible ones yields an isomorphic manifold and we care about manifolds only up to isomorphism.

Remark 3.1.4
.

In Definition
3.1.1
, one could replace “strictly differentiable” everywhere by some other condition. Most importantly,
to obtain that every Lie group gives rise to a Lie algebra (
[
1
, Proposition 6.19]
),
[
1
]
use

T_{2}
-manifolds. Here,
T_{2}
is a rather technical condition about maps which morally is something like “strictly differentiable of order two”, but which does not follow from twice strictly differentiable. Since we will use
[
1
, Proposition 6.19]
) as a black box, we do not introduce this notion here.

Remark 3.1.5
.

We justify the assertion above that the
U_{i}
are finite covers.
Consider
r\colon U\to K^{n}
as in
3.1.1
(1) above and pick any
a
in the range of
r
. Then
r^{-1}(a)
is finite, for if it were infinite, then by 1-
h
-minimality it would not be discrete; we could then choose non-isolated
u\in r^{-1}(a)
, and
the étaleness condition fails for this
u
.

Next, we recall the notion of tangent space from
[
1
]
. The definition might look a bit odd at first sight (in particular Definition
3.1.6
(1)), but it comes out naturally from the notions of differentiable maps given in Definition
3.1.1
(2), (5).

Definition 3.1.6

(
[
1
, Definition 5.4]
)
.

(1)

Given a definable étale map
r_{1}\colon U_{1}\to K^{n_{1}}
and a point
y\in U_{1}
, the
tangent space

T_{y}U_{1}
is simply defined to be
K^{n_{1}}
.

If
r_{2}\colon U_{2}\to K^{n_{2}}
is a second definable étale map and
f\colon U_{1}\to U_{2}
is strictly differentiable, the
differential

d_{y}f\colon T_{y}U_{1}\to T_{f(y)}U_{2}
is defined to be the strict derivative of the map
r_{2}\circ f\circ(r_{1}|_{B})^{-1}
from Definition
3.1.1
(2).

(2)

Given a dwsd manifold
M
with charts
\phi_{i}\colon U_{i}\to M
, and given a point
x\in M
, the
tangent space

T_{x}M
is obtained as follows:
Let
I
be the set of indices
i
such that
x
lies in
\phi_{i}(U_{i})
,
set
y_{i}:=\phi_{i}^{-1}(x)
, take the disjoint union

\coprod_{i\in I}T_{y_{i}}U_{i}

of the corresponding tangent spaces, and identify two points
v\in T_{y_{i}}U_{i}
and
w\in T_{y_{j}}U_{j}
if the
differential

d_{y_{j}}(\phi_{i}^{-1}\circ\phi_{j})

of the corresponding transition map sends
w
to
v
. Note that for every
i
, we obtain a natural identification of
T_{x}M
with
T_{y_{i}}U_{i}
.

(3)

If
N
is a second dwsd manifold,
f\colon M\to N
is strictly differentiable, and
x\in M
, then the
differential

d_{x}f\colon T_{x}M\to T_{f(x)}N
is obtained by taking any charts
\phi_{i}\colon U_{i}\to M
and
\psi_{j}\colon V_{j}\to N
having
x
and
f(x)
in their images, respectively, and defining
d_{x}f
to be the map induced by

d_{\phi_{i}^{-1}(x)}(\phi_{j}^{-1}\circ f\circ\phi_{i})\colon T_{\phi_{i}^{-1}(x)}U_{i}\to T_{\phi_{j}^{-1}(f(x))}V_{j}
.

Definition 3.1.7
.

A
dwsd Lie group
is a definable group
G
equipped with a dwsd manifold structure, such that the group multiplication
G\times G\to G
and the group inversion
G\to G
are both morphisms of dwsd manifolds (
i.e.
, are strictly differentiable). A
homomorphism of dwsd Lie groups
is a group homomorphism which is also a morphism of dwsd manifolds (
i.e.
, it is definable and strictly differentiable).

We can now state one of the central results of
[
1
]
, which asserts that definable groups are Lie groups. (Recall that the only non-imaginary sort of our structure
(K,v,\dots)
is
K
itself, so the proposition does not apply e.g. to the value group.)

Proposition 3.1.8

(
[
1
, Proposition 6.4]
)
.

Any definable group can be equipped with the structure of a dwsd manifold in such a way that it becomes a dwsd Lie group.

As noted after the proof of
[
1
, Proposition 6.4]
, the dwsd Lie group structure on a definable group
G
is unique up to a unique isomorphism. In the rest of this section, when referring to a definable group
G
, any implicit references to a manifold structure on
G
refer to this dwsd manifold structure.

3.2.
Tangent spaces and Lie algebras

In this subsection we overview some fairly standard material on tangent spaces and Lie algebras, but in the definable non-archimedean 1-
h
-minimal context, using the framework from
[
1
]
described in the previous subsection. The goal of the subsection is Theorem
3.2.12
below.
Our treatment is influenced by
[
34
]
, which itself is an adaptation of the exposition in
[
33
]
for the context in
[
34
]
of groups definable in o-minimal structures.
Any definable group which we mention is assumed to be equipped with the unique dwsd manifold structure obtained using Proposition
3.1.8
.
(In particular, the topology on the group is not necessarily the one from the ambient
K^{n}
.)
We denote the identity element of a group by
e
.

Lemma 3.2.1
.

Let
H\leq G
be definable groups. Then

(i)

H
is a closed subset of
G
(with respect to the topology on
G
given by the dwsd manifold structure on
G
), and furthermore, the inclusion
i:H\to G
is a closed embedding of dwsd manifolds.

(ii)

H
is open in
G
if and only if
T_{e}(H)=T_{e}(G)
if and only if
\dim(H)=\dim(G)
. Those conditions in particular hold if
H
has finite index in
G
.

Proof.

Part (i) and the first equivalence in (ii) follow from Proposition 6.9 (with Proposition 6.10) and Corollary 6.14 respectively of
[
1
]
. For the second equivalence in (ii), note that by Remark
3.1.2
(3), we have
\dim G=\dim T_{e}(G)
, and similarly for
H
.
Finally, if
H
has finite index in
G
, then its complement
G\setminus H
is a finite union of translates of
H
and hence closed in
G
, so
H
is open in
G
.
∎

If
H_{1}
,
H_{2}
are subgroups of a group
G
, then we say that
H_{1},H_{2}
are
commensurable
,
if
H_{1}\cap H_{2}
has finite index in each of
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
.

Lemma 3.2.2
.

[
34
, Claim 2.20]

Let
H_{1},H_{2}
be definable subgroups of a definable group
G

which are commensurable.
Then
T_{e}(H_{1})=T_{e}(H_{2})
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.3.6
(iii) we have
\dim(H_{1}\cap H_{2})=\dim(H_{i})
for each
i
. Hence, by Lemma
3.2.1
,
H_{1}\cap H_{2}
is open in
H_{i}
, so
T_{e}(H_{1}\cap H_{2})=T_{e}(H_{i})
(for
i=1,2
).
∎

For the next result,
[
1
, Corollary 6.11]
provides an important tool. In the o-minimal setting, this is
[
34
, Theorem 2.21]
.

Proposition 3.2.3
.

Let
G,H
be definable groups and
\phi\colon G\to H
a homomorphism of dwsd Lie groups (
i.e.
a group homomorphism which is definable and strictly differentiable). Let
H_{1}\leq H
be definable, and
G_{1}:=\phi^{-1}(H_{1})
. Then
T_{e}(G_{1})=(\dd_{e}(\phi))^{-1}(T_{e}(H_{1}))
.

Proof.

We clearly have the inclusion
\subset
, so it suffices to show that both sides have the same dimension.

Consider the maps

f\colon G_{1}\to G\times H_{1},a\mapsto(a,\phi(a))

and

g_{1},g_{2}\colon G\times H_{1}\to H,g_{1}(a,b)=\phi(a),g_{2}(a,b)=b.

One easily verifies that
f
is the equalizer of
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
,
i.e.
,
f
is injective and its image is exactly the set
Z
of points where
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
agree.
In a similar way, one verifies that
the equalizer of
\dd_{e}g_{1},\dd_{e}g_{2}\colon T_{e}(G)\times T_{e}(H_{1})\to T_{e}(H)
is the map

(\dd_{e}(\phi))^{-1}(T_{e}(H_{1}))\to T_{e}(G)\times T_{e}(H_{1}),w\mapsto(w,\dd_{e}(\phi)(w)).

Applying
[
1
, 6.11]
and putting everything together, we obtain

\dim T_{e}(G_{1})=\dim T_{e}(Z)=\dim Z=\dim(\Ker(\dd_{e}g_{1}-\dd_{e}g_{2}))=\dim(\dd_{e}(\phi))^{-1}(T_{e}(H_{1})).

∎

As in the o-minimal setting (see
[
34
, Theorem 2.24]
), we deduce the following:

Corollary 3.2.4
.

Let
G
be a definable group,
V
an
n
-dimensional definable vector space over
K
, and
\phi:G\to\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(V)
a definable homomorphism. Let
U
be a subspace of
V
, and put
G_{1}:=\{g\in G:\phi(g)(U)\subseteq U\}
and
G_{2}:=\{g\in G:\phi(g)|_{U}=\id|_{U}\}
.
Then, viewing
\dd_{e}(\phi)
as a linear function from

to
\End(V)
(the latter identified with
T_{e}({\rm Aut}(V))
), we have

(i)

T_{e}(G_{1})=\{\xi\in T_{e}(G):\dd_{e}(\phi)(\xi)(U)\subseteq U\}

(ii)

T_{e}(G_{2})=\{\xi\in T_{e}(G):\dd_{e}(\phi)(\xi)(U)=\{0\}\}
.

Proof.

(i)

This follows from Proposition
3.2.3
, putting
H=\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(V)
and

H_{1}:=\{x\in\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(V):x(U)\leq U\}.

(ii)

Again, apply Proposition

, putting

H_{2}:=\{x\in\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(V):x|_{U}=\id|_{U}\}=\{x\in\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(V):(x-\id)(U)=\{0\}\},

and noting that

T_{e}(H_{2})=\{x\in\End(V):x(U)=\{0\}\}.

∎

Corollary 3.2.5
.

Let
G
be a definable group, and

a definable strictly differentiable Lie group automorphism of

. Then
\dd_{e}(f)\in\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(T_{e}(G))
.

Proof.

See
[

, Corollary 2.22]
. Apply Proposition

with

and with

as the trivial group, to obtain that

has trivial kernel and so lies in

.
∎

Let
G
be a definable group. We recall the Lie algebra structure on

obtained in
[
1
, 6.15–6.19]
; see
[

, Section 2.4]
for the o-minimal analogue.
For
g\in G
, we write
\Int_{g}
for the map
G\to G
given by
\Int_{g}(x)=gxg^{-1}
. Put
\Ad g:=\dd_{e}(\Int_{g})
. Then by
Corollary
3.2.5
,
\Ad g\in\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(T_{e}(G))
. Also,
\Ad\colon G\to\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(T_{e}(G))
is a strictly differentiable definable homomorphism, below called the
adjoint
representation of

.

Now let
\adj:=\dd_{e}(\Ad)
. Then
\adj\colon T_{e}(G)\to\End(T_{e}(G))
is a definable homomorphism. Define the operation

[-,-]
on
T_{e}(G)
by
[\xi,\zeta]=\adj(\xi)(\zeta)
.

Lemma 3.2.6
.

(T_{e}(G),[-,-])
is a Lie algebra.

Proof.

See
[
1
, Proposition 6.19]
.
∎

We denote by
L(G)
the Lie algebra of

as given by the last lemma.

Remark 3.2.7
.

A standard computation shows that for
g\in G
,
\Ad g\in\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(T_{e}(G))
is even a Lie algebra automorphism: From
\Ad(ghg^{-1})=(\Ad g)\circ(\Ad h)\circ(\Ad g)^{-1}
, one obtains
\Ad\circ\Int_{g}=\Int_{\Ad g}\circ\Ad

(where
\Int_{\Ad g}\colon\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(T_{e}(G))\to\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(T_{e}(G)),\phi\mapsto(\Ad g)\circ\phi\circ(\Ad g)^{-1}
).
We take the derivative at
e
on both sides, and use that since

\Int_{\Ad g}
is a linear automorphism of the vector space
V=\End(T_{e}(G))
, it is its own derivative – indeed, for any
v\in V
the tangent space to
V
at
v
is
V
itself, and the derivative of a linear map
f
at
v
is the linear approximation of
f
, which is
f
itself. One gets
\adj\circ(\Ad g)=\Int_{\Ad g}\circ\adj
, which, evaluated at
\xi
and then at
(\Ad g)(\zeta)
, gives
[(\Ad g)(\xi),(\Ad g)(\zeta)]=(\Ad g)[\xi,(\Ad g)^{-1}((\Ad g)(\zeta))]=(\Ad g)[\xi,\zeta]
.

Recall that if
\mathfrak{g}
is a Lie algebra with Lie bracket
[-]
, then an
ideal
of
\mathfrak{g}
is a subspace
\mathfrak{h}
such that

[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{h}]\subset\mathfrak{h}
. We say
\mathfrak{g}
is
abelian
if
[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]=\{0\}
. It is
simple
if its only ideals are
\{0\}
and
\mathfrak{g}
, and is

if its only abelian ideal is
\{0\}
. Furthermore, any finite-dimensional semisimple Lie algebra is a direct sum of finitely many ideals which are simple Lie algebras.

Lemma 3.2.8
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be the Lie algebra of the definable group

and let

be a subspace of

. Then

(i)

The subalgebra
\{\xi\in\mathfrak{g}:[\xi,\mathfrak{h}]=0\}
is the Lie algebra of the subgroup

\{g\in G:\Ad g|_{\mathfrak{h}}=\Id\}
, and

(ii)

the subalgebra
\{\xi\in\mathfrak{g}:[\xi,\mathfrak{h}]\subseteq\mathfrak{h}\}

\{g\in G:\Ad g(\mathfrak{h})\subseteq\mathfrak{h}\}
.

Proof.

See
[

. This follows by applying Corollary
3.2.4
to the adjoint representation of

.
∎

Lemma 3.2.9
.

Let
G
be a definable group, and
\Ad\colon G\to\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(T_{e}(G))
be as above.

(i)

For
x\in G
, we have

x\in\Ker(\Ad)\Leftrightarrow|G:C_{G}(x)|<\infty
.

(ii)

Assume

is a definable subgroup of some

. Then
\Ker(\Ad)
centralises a definable normal subgroup of

of finite index.

Proof.

(i) is exactly (the first statement of)
[
1
, Lemma 8.7]
.

(ii) It is well-known that any subgroup of a group

has the descending chain condition on centralisers; for example, this holds for the group
\GL_{n}(K^{\alg})
because the latter is stable (see e.g.
[

is quantifier-free.

Let

. Then

is open in

of finite index by (i). Put

, a definable normal subgroup of

∎

Lemma 3.2.10
.

Let
G
be a definable subgroup of
\GL_{n}(K)
with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
.

(i)

G
has an abelian definable subgroup of finite index if and only if
\mathfrak{g}
is abelian.

(ii)

Let
H
be a definable subgroup of
G
with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}
.
Suppose that
H
is commensurable with a definable normal subgroup
H_{0}
of a definable open subgroup
G_{0}
of
G
. Then
\mathfrak{h}
is an ideal of
\mathfrak{g}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathfrak{g}
is abelian. By
[
1
, Lemma 8.7]
, this implies that
|G:\Ker(\Ad)|
is finite.
By Lemma
3.2.9
(ii),
\Ker(\Ad)
centralises a definable normal subgroup
H\subset G
of finite index. The intersection
H\cap\Ker(\Ad)
also has finite index in
G
, and it is abelian.

Conversely, suppose that
G
has a definable abelian subgroup
U
of finite index. Then for each
g\in U
the group
C_{G}(g)
is of finite index, so by Lemma
3.2.9
(i), for all
g\in U
we have
\Ad g=1
, so
\Ker(\Ad)\geq U
. It follows by Lemmas
3.2.8
(i) and
3.2.1
that
L(\Ker(\Ad))=\{\xi\in\mathfrak{g}:[\xi,\mathfrak{g}]=0\}=L(G)
. Hence
\mathfrak{g}
is abelian.

(ii) Let
G_{0}
,
H
, and
H_{0}
be as in the statement, and let
\mathfrak{h}=L(H)
and
\mathfrak{h}_{0}=L(H_{0})
. First observe that
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{h}_{0}
by Lemma
3.2.2
.
Then for every
g\in G_{0}
,
\Int_{g}(H_{0})=H_{0}
, so
\Ad g(\mathfrak{h})=\mathfrak{h}
(e.g. by Corollary
3.2.5
).
Let
G_{1}:=\{g\in G:\Ad g(\mathfrak{h})\subseteq\mathfrak{h}\}
. Then
G_{0}\leq G_{1}\leq G
,
and
L(G_{1})=\{\xi\in\mathfrak{g}:[\xi,\mathfrak{h}]\subseteq\mathfrak{h}\}
by Lemma

(ii). Also, by Lemma

,
L(G_{0})=L(G_{1})=L(G)
as

is open in

, so
\mathfrak{h}
is an ideal of
\mathfrak{g}
.
∎

Theorem 3.2.11
.

Let
G
be a definable subgroup of
\GL_{n}(K)
with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. Then the following are equivalent.

(i)
G
has an infinite definable abelian normal subgroup.

(ii)
G
has a definable open subgroup
H
with an infinite definable abelian normal subgroup.

(iii)
\mathfrak{g}
is not semisimple.

Proof.

We adapt
[
34
, Theorem 2.34]
. The direction (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) is trivial.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii). Suppose that
G
has an open subgroup
H
which has an infinite definable abelian normal subgroup
B
. We apply Lemma
3.2.10
: part (ii) yields that
L(B)
is a non-trivial ideal of
\mathfrak{g}
, and it is abelian as
B
is abelian.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i) Suppose that
\mathfrak{g}
is not semisimple. Then it has an abelian non-trivial ideal, so has non-trivial soluble radical, and the last non-trivial term in its derived series is an abelian ideal
\mathfrak{j}
which is invariant under
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\mathfrak{g})
.

Put
H:=\{g\in G:\Ad g|_{\mathfrak{j}}=\Id\}
. By Lemma
3.2.8
(i),

\mathfrak{h}:=L(H)=\{\xi\in\mathfrak{g}:[\xi,\mathfrak{j}]=0\}
. Then
\mathfrak{h}
is an ideal of
\mathfrak{g}
(use the Jacobi identity), and contains
\mathfrak{j}
as the latter is abelian. Also, by the above invariance, for every
g\in G
we have
\Ad g(\mathfrak{j})=\mathfrak{j}
,
so
g^{-1}Hg=H
from the definition of
H
, so
H
is normal in
G
.
Now let
B:=\{h\in H\colon\Ad h|_{\mathfrak{h}}=\Id\}
. Then
B
is normal in
G
(as
\mathfrak{h}
is
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\mathfrak{g})
-invariant), and
L(B)=\{\xi\in\mathfrak{h}:[\xi,\mathfrak{h}]=0\}
by Lemma
3.2.8
(i). Now by the Jacobi identity,
L(B)
is an ideal of
\mathfrak{g}
, and contains
\mathfrak{j}
, so
B
is infinite. Furthermore, the adjoint action of
B
on
L(B)
is trivial, so
(by Lemma
3.2.9
(i)) for every
g\in B
we have
|B:C_{B}(g)|
finite. It follows by the descending chain condition on centralisers for
G
that
|B:Z(B)|
is finite. Thus,
Z(B)
is an infinite definable abelian normal subgroup of
G
.
∎

Theorem 3.2.12
.

Suppose that
G
is a definably almost simple non-abelian definable subgroup of
\GL_{n}(K)
. Then its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is simple.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.2.11
,
\mathfrak{g}
is semisimple, so is a direct sum of a unique set
\{\mathfrak{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{h}_{t}\}
of ideals which are non-abelian and simple as Lie algebras. We suppose for a contradiction that
t>1
.

Let
\mathfrak{j}:=\mathfrak{h}_{2}\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathfrak{h}_{t}
. Then
\mathfrak{h}_{1}=\{\xi\in\mathfrak{g}:[\xi,\mathfrak{j}]=0\}
, and by Lemma
3.2.8
(i),
\mathfrak{h}_{1}
is the Lie algebra of
N_{1}:=\{g\in G:\Ad g|_{\mathfrak{j}}=\Id\}
. Note that
G
has no proper definable subgroup
N
of finite index, since otherwise
\bigcap_{g\in G}g^{-1}Ng
is a proper definable
normal
subgroup of
G
of finite index, contradicting definable almost simplicity. Since the collection
\{\mathfrak{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{h}_{t}\}
is uniquely determined, it follows that each
\mathfrak{h}_{i}
is
G
-invariant under the adjoint action. Thus,
N_{1}
is a normal subgroup of
G
, and is clearly definable. Also
N_{1}
is infinite
as
\mathfrak{h}_{1}
is non-trivial, so
N_{1}=G
by definable almost simplicity. Since we may replace
\mathfrak{h}_{1}
by any
\mathfrak{h}_{j}
, it follows that the adjoint representation of
G
is trivial, so
|G:Z(G)|
is finite by Lemma
3.2.9
(ii).
This contradicts the assumption that
G
is non-abelian and definably almost simple.
∎

Lemma 3.2.13
.

Let
G
be an infinite non-abelian definable and definably almost simple subgroup of
\GL_{n}(K)
. Then
G\leq\SL_{n}(K)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
G
is not contained in
\SL_{n}(K)
. Then there is a definable non-trivial homomorphism
\sigma:G\to\GL_{n}(K)/\SL_{n}(K)\cong(K^{\times},\cdot)
. As
\Ker(\sigma)
is a definable proper normal subgroup of
G
, it is finite, and its centraliser equals
G
, so
\Ker(\sigma)
is central (see Remark
2.1.4
), and
G
is nilpotent of class 2. Now for any
g\in G
,
C_{G}(g)
has finite index in
G
so equals
G
. It follows that
G
is abelian, a contradiction.
∎

The following result pulls together what is needed from this section.

Proposition 3.2.14
.

Let
(K,v,\ldots)
be a 1-
h
-minimal valued field, and let
G\subseteq K^{n}
be a definable definably almost simple non-abelian group, equipped with the dwsd Lie group structure as in Proposition
3.1.8
. Then there is
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and a definable homomorphism
\rho\colon G\to\SL_{d}(K)
with kernel
Z(G)
, given by the adjoint action of
G
on the tangent space
T_{e}(G)
. Moreover,
T_{e}(G)
carries the structure of a simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
over
K
, and
\rho(G)

acts as a group of automorphisms of
\mathfrak{g}
. We have
\dim(G)=\dim_{K}(\mathfrak{g})
.

Proof.

For
g\in G
, let
{\rm Int}_{g}\colon G\to G
be the map
x\mapsto gxg^{-1}
.
Then the differential
d_{e}{\rm Int}_{g}
is a definable linear map
T_{e}(G)\to T_{e}(G)
,
and gives the adjoint representation
{\rm Ad}\colon G\to\GL_{d}(K)
, defined by putting
\Ad(g)=d_{e}{\rm Int}_{g}
.

Suppose first for a contradiction that
G=\Ker(\Ad)
. Then for each
x\in G
,
C_{G}(x)
has finite index in
G
(by Lemma
3.2.9
(i)); hence
C_{G}(x)=G
by definable almost simplicity of
G
, for otherwise
\bigcap_{g\in G}g^{-1}C_{G}(x)g
is a proper definable normal subgroup of
G
. Thus
G
is abelian, a contradiction.

Thus,
{\rm Ad}
has finite kernel containing
Z(G)
, and
C_{G}(\Ker(\Ad))
has finite index in
G
so equals
G
by definable almost simplicity, so
\Ker(\Ad)=Z(G)
.
Since by Lemma
3.2.13

{\rm Ad}
has image in
\SL_{n}(K)
), we obtain the first assertion, putting
\rho:=\Ad
.
Furthermore, by Lemma
3.2.6
, the tangent space of
G
at the identity carries the structure of a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=L(G)
of
G
, and by Theorem
3.2.12
,
\mathfrak{g}
is a simple Lie algebra. In particular, we have
\rho(G)\leq\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(\mathfrak{g})
.

Finally,
\dim(G)=\dim(T_{e}(G))
, and this equals the
K
-vector space dimension of
\mathfrak{g}
; see e.g. Remark
3.1.2
(3).
∎

4.
Bounded subgroups

The purpose of this section is to present and adapt results from
[
41
]
which are key to our proof, and (in Proposition
4.2.1
) to eliminate the possibility that the group
G
in Theorem
1.0.1
is ‘bounded’.

If
(K,v)
is any valued field, we shall say that a subset of
K^{n}
is
bounded
if it is contained in the ball of
K^{n}
of form
B_{\gamma}(0)
around 0, for some
\gamma\in\Gamma
(see also
[
6
, Section 3]
). For example
the local ring
{\mathcal{O}}
of the valuation is a bounded set.
We say that a subgroup
G\subset\mathbf{H}(K)
is
bounded
, where
\mathbf{H}
is a linear algebraic
K
-group, if
G
is bounded as a subset of
K^{n^{2}}
when we consider
\mathbf{H}
as being embedded into
\SL_{n}
. Note that we have a running assumption that the valuation
v
is surjective, so if a set
B
is bounded then there is
a\in K
such that for all
b\in B
we have
v(a)<v(b)
.

Remark 4.0.1
.

Note that this notion of boundedness of
G
is well-defined in the sense that it does not depend on the embedding of
\mathbf{H}
into
\SL_{n}
.
Indeed, if
\mathbf{H}\subset\SL_{n}
and
\mathbf{H}^{\prime}\subset\SL_{n^{\prime}}
are linear algebraic
K
-groups and
f\colon\mathbf{H}\to\mathbf{H}^{\prime}
is a
K
-isomorphism, then a bound on
G\subset\mathbf{H}(K)
yields a bound on
f(G)\subset\mathbf{H}^{\prime}(K)
(since
f
is defined by polynomials with coefficients in
K
), and similarly for
f^{-1}
.

4.1.
Bounded subgroups of
\mathbf{H}(K)

In this subsection, we record the following two theorems, which are crucial to our proof of Theorem
1.0.1
. In the first theorem, we have added the assumption that
K
is perfect for consistency with our definitions in this paper.

Theorem 4.1.1

(
[
41
, Theorem (BTR) on p. 198]
)
.

Suppose that
K
is a perfect field with a non-trivial
henselian valuation
v\colon K\to\Gamma\cup\{\infty\}

(with possibly non-archimedean value group
\Gamma
)
and
\mathbf{H}\leq\SL_{n}
is a connected reductive linear algebraic
K
-group.
Then
\mathbf{H}(K)
is bounded if and only if
\mathbf{H}
is anisotropic over
K
.

Theorem 4.1.2
.

Suppose
(K,v,\ldots)
is a 1-
h
-minimal expansion of a henselian valued field of characteristic 0. Let

\mathbf{H}\leq\SL_{n}
be a semisimple almost
K
-simple algebraic
K
-group.
Then any unbounded definable open subgroup
G
of
\mathbf{H}(K)

contains
\mathbf{H}(K)^{+}
.

The context in
[
41
]
is that of Henselian valuations whose value group
\Gamma
is archimedean, that is, has the property that if
\gamma\in\Gamma
with
\gamma>0
then
\{n\gamma:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is cofinal in
\Gamma
. We need these statements for arbitrary value group,
i.e.
for a
Krull valuation
.
The proof of the above Theorem
4.1.1
in
[
41
]
(attributed there to Bruhat, Tits, and Rousseau) does not use the archimedean value group assumption (see also the proof of Theorem 1.1 of
[
42
]
). For Theorem
4.1.2
, which is based on a proof in
[
41
]
of an unpublished result of Tits, the situation is slightly more complicated. Our conclusion (that
G
contains
\mathbf{H}(K)^{+}
) is slightly stronger than that in
[
41
]
, and moreover, Prasad’s proof really seems to be using the assumption that the value group is archimedean. It is not very difficult to modify Prasad’s proof to get the stronger conclusion, and it is also not difficult to generalize it to value groups which have a maximal proper convex subgroup (since then, the valuation can be coarsened to an archimedean one). However, getting the result for general value groups seems to be more tricky. We at least were only able to prove the general case under the assumption that
G
is definable – an assumption which Prasad does not need.

We also need the following positive characteristic version of Theorem
4.1.2
:

Proposition 4.1.3
.

Suppose that
(K,v)
is a pure algebraically closed valued field of characteristic
p
.
Let

\mathbf{H}\leq\SL_{n}
be a semisimple almost
K
-simple algebraic
K
-group.
Then any proper definable open subgroup
G
of
\mathbf{H}(K)^{+}
is bounded.

Here, the situation is easier than for Theorem
4.1.2
: firstly, we only need the weaker conclusion which also appears in
[
41
]
, and secondly, using that we only work in the pure valued field language, we can easily reduce to the case of the value group having a maximal proper convex subgroup, so that we can then cite
[
41
]
.

Proof of Proposition
4.1.3
.

Suppose for a contradiction that
G
is an unbounded proper definable open subgroup of

\mathbf{H}(K)^{+}
. Let
\Gamma_{1}
be a divisible ordered abelian group containing
\Gamma
and having a maximal proper convex subgroup
\Gamma_{2}
, and let
\Delta=\Gamma_{1}/\Gamma_{2}
, an archimedean ordered abelian group. Also let
k
be the residue field of
K
. By model-completeness in
L_{{\rm div}}
, the field
(K,v)
is an elementary substructure of the Hahn field
(k((t^{\Gamma_{1}})),v_{1})
where
v_{1}
is the natural valuation with value group
\Gamma_{1}
. Using that
G
is definable, that
\mathbf{H}(K)^{+}
is definable when

is algebraically closed (see Lemma

(ii)) and that the conditions ‘bounded’ and ‘open’ are first order expressible, we may replace

by

, that is, we may assume

.

Now let
v_{\Delta}
be the induced coarsened valuation

. The group

is still an unbounded and open subgroup of
\mathbf{H}(K)^{+}
with respect to this valuation, and is also a proper subgroup. This contradicts Theorem (T) (the theorem attributed by Prasad to Tits) on
[
41
, p. 198]
.
∎

The remainder of this subsection is devoted to the proof of Theorem
4.1.2
. We start with a preliminary lemma which is probably known, but since we didn’t find it in the literature, we give a proof.

Lemma 4.1.4
.

Suppose that
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
are two spherically complete valued fields of equi-characteristic
0
, which both have the same value group
\Gamma
and the same residue field
k
. Suppose moreover that
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
have a common subfield
K_{0}
. Then there exists a valued field isomorphism
f\colon K_{1}\to K_{2}
which is the identity on
K_{0}
and which induces the identity on
\Gamma
and on
k
.

Proof.

Consider all the fields as two-sorted structures, with one sort for the valued field and one sort for the leading terms. We set
\mathop{\rm RV}\nolimits:=\mathop{\rm rv}\nolimits(K_{1})=\mathop{\rm rv}\nolimits(K_{2})
and
\mathop{\rm RV}\nolimits_{0}:=\mathop{\rm rv}\nolimits(K_{0})
.
Let
f
be the identity map on
K_{0}\cup\mathop{\rm RV}\nolimits
(considered as a partial isomorphism from
(K_{1},\mathop{\rm RV}\nolimits)
to
(K_{2},\mathop{\rm RV}\nolimits)
). It suffices to verify
that given any
a\in K_{1}
, we can extend
f
to an embedding

(K_{0}(a),\mathop{\rm RV}\nolimits)\to(K_{2},\mathop{\rm RV}\nolimits)
. Indeed, then this process can be repeated to obtain an embedding
(K_{1},\mathop{\rm RV}\nolimits)
to
(K_{2},\mathop{\rm RV}\nolimits)
, and if the image
f(K_{1})
would be a proper subfield of
K_{2}
, then applying the same argument to
f^{-1}
and some
b\in K_{2}\setminus f(K_{1})
would lead to a contradiction.

So let
a\in K_{1}\setminus K_{0}
be given, and suppose first that
a\in\mathop{\rm acl}\nolimits(K_{0},\mathop{\rm RV}\nolimits)
. Then, since
K_{1}\equiv_{K_{0}\cup\mathop{\rm RV}\nolimits}K_{2}
(see for example the relative quantifier elimination to the valued field sort in
[
14
, Proposition 4.3]
), there exists
a^{\prime}\in K_{2}
with
\tp(a^{\prime}/K_{0}\cup\mathop{\rm RV}\nolimits)=\tp(a/K_{0}\cup\mathop{\rm RV}\nolimits)
, so we can extend
f
by setting
f(a):=a^{\prime}
. Thus we may now assume that
K_{0}\cup\mathop{\rm RV}\nolimits
is algebraically closed in the model theoretic sense.
In that case, the type
\tp(a/K_{0}\cup\mathop{\rm RV}\nolimits)
is determined by the formulas of the form
\mathop{\rm rv}\nolimits(x-b)=\xi
, where
b
runs over
K_{0}
and
\xi
runs over
\mathop{\rm RV}\nolimits
. (This follows e.g. from
[
10
, Lemma 2.4.4]
, which states that
for
a,a^{\prime}\in K_{1}
to have the same type over
K_{0}\cup\mathop{\rm RV}\nolimits
, it suffices that the smallest ball
B\subset K_{1}
containing
a
and
a^{\prime}
is disjoint from
\mathop{\rm acl}\nolimits(K_{0})=K_{0}
. If there would exist a
b\in B\cap K_{0}
, then we would have
\mathop{\rm rv}\nolimits(a-b)\neq\mathop{\rm rv}\nolimits(a^{\prime}-b)
.) Each such formula defines a valuative ball in
K_{1}
, and those balls form a chain. The same formulas then also define a chain of balls in
K_{2}
. By spherical completeness of
K_{2}
, that chain has non-empty intersection. Any
a^{\prime}
in that intersection has the same type as
a
over
K_{0}\cup\mathop{\rm RV}\nolimits
, so that we can define
f(a):=a^{\prime}
.
∎

Here is another preliminary lemma:

Lemma 4.1.5
.

Let
K
be a henselian valued field of characteristic

(of any residue characteristic).
Let
\mathbf{H}
be a connected linear algebraic

-group and

\mathbf{H}(K)
. Then

\mathbf{H}(K)
.

Proof.

Without loss,
\mathbf{H}=\cl_{K^{\alg}}(\mathbf{H}(K))
.
By Lemma

, there exists an

such that for every ball

containing

, we have

. By left-translation, we may assume

, so using Lemma

(ii) we obtain
\dim U=\dim\mathbf{H}(K)=\zardim\mathbf{H}
and in particular also
\zardim\cl_{K^{\alg}}(U)=\zardim\mathbf{H}
. Since

is connected and hence irreducible,
this implies

and hence, using Remark

.
∎

Remark 4.1.6
.

Note that in Theorem
4.1.2
,
\mathbf{H}(K)^{+}
is generated by the set

(see Remark

contains

if and only if it contains

. Note also that

can be defined by polynomials with coefficients in

, since it is the set of those matrices

such that

is nilpotent, and since we can bound the nilpotency class of matrices in
\mathbf{H}
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1.2
.

Let
G
be a definable unbounded open subgroup of
\mathbf{H}(K)
, where
\mathbf{H}
is a semisimple almost
K
-simple linear algebraic

-group with
\mathbf{H}(K^{\alg})\leq\SL_{n}\left(K^{\alg}\right)
. Our goal is to prove that
G
contains
\mathbf{H}(K)^{+}
. We start by a series of reductions.

Firstly, note that we may suppose that
\mathbf{H}

Next, we give an argument to reduce the mixed characteristic case to that of residue characteristic 0. So suppose

Before we proceed to the next reduction, note that

Claim 2.

Proof of Claim 2.

Claim 3.
(i)

(i) Fix a flag

\mathbf{U}^{-}_{\Pi}(K_{\lambda})
being analogous.
To this end, it suffices to verify that
G_{\lambda}
contains

\mathbf{U}_{\phi}(K_{\lambda})

for each
\phi\in\Phi^{+}\setminus\langle\Pi\rangle
, so fix such a root
\phi
,
and also fix an isomorphism
\epsilon_{\phi}\colon\mathbf{G}_{a}\to\mathbf{U}_{\phi}
defined over
K_{\lambda}
, with
t\epsilon_{\phi}(x)t^{-1}=\epsilon_{\phi}(\phi(t)x)
for all
t\in\mathbf{T}
.
Since
\epsilon_{\phi}
is continuous with respect to the valuation topology, there exists a
\lambda^{\prime}\in C_{\lambda}/C^{-}_{\lambda}
such that the image of the valuative ball
B^{\prime}=B_{\lambda^{\prime}}(0)\subset K_{\lambda}
under
\epsilon_{\phi}
lies in
B_{\lambda}(1)\cap\mathbf{U}_{\phi}(K_{\lambda})
.

Since
v_{\lambda}(\phi(g))>0
(by our choice of
\phi
, and Claim 4) and since the value group
C_{\lambda}/C^{-}_{\lambda}
is archimedean,
for any
a\in K_{\lambda}
, there exists an integer
n
such that

nv_{\lambda}(\phi(g))+v_{\lambda}(a)>\lambda^{\prime}
and hence
\phi(g)^{n}a\in B^{\prime}
.
Thus
g^{n}\epsilon_{\phi}(a)g^{-n}=\epsilon_{\phi}(\phi(g)^{n}a)\in B_{\lambda}(1)\subset G_{\lambda}
, which implies
\epsilon_{\phi}(a)\in G_{\lambda}
. Since each element of
\mathbf{U}_{\phi}(K_{\lambda})
is of this form, we just proved
\mathbf{U}_{\phi}(K_{\lambda})\subset G_{\lambda}
.
∎
(Claim 5)

By
[
2
, Proposition 6.2(v)]
,
\mathbf{H}(K_{\lambda})^{+}

is generated by
\mathbf{U}_{\Pi}(K_{\lambda})
and
\mathbf{U}^{-}_{\Pi}(K_{\lambda})
, so Claim 5 implies
\mathbf{H}(K_{\lambda})^{+}\subset G_{\lambda}
. This finishes the proof of (*) and hence also of the theorem.
∎

4.2.
Non-simplicity of bounded subgroups

Our goal in this subsection is Proposition
4.2.1
, which, in combination with Theorem
4.1.2
(or Proposition
4.1.3
, in the positive characteristic case), is critical to the proof of our main theorem: It yields the unboundedness assumption that is needed to apply Theorem
4.1.2
.

Proposition 4.2.1
.

Suppose that
(K,v,\ldots)
is a 1-
h
-minimal expansion of a henselian valued field of characteristic 0, or is a pure algebraically closed valued field of characteristic
p
. Let

G
be an infinite definable and definably almost simple subgroup of
\SL_{n}(K)
. Then
G
is not bounded.

The proof of this proposition grew out of an argument with Bruhat-Tits buildings. While formally Bruhat-Tits buildings do not appear anymore in the proof, this is still the idea behind it. As an example of the basic idea, the group
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
acts on a
(p+1)
-degree tree (see Chapter II of
[
45
]
), and any bounded subgroup
G<\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
fixes a vertex and so is conjugate to a subgroup of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})
. Using that

\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})
has a chain of normal subgroups with trivial intersection (arising from congruence subgroups), one deduces that
G
cannot be definably almost simple.

Proof of Proposition
4.2.1
.

We suppose for a contradiction that
G
is bounded.

Set
V=K^{n}
, and let
\Gamma^{c}
be the set of upwards-closed subsets of
\Gamma
. Observe that
\Gamma^{c}
is an ordered set (with
\lambda\leq\lambda^{\prime}
iff
\lambda^{\prime}\subset\lambda
, and where
\infty^{c}:=\emptyset\in\Gamma^{c}
is the maximum), and that
\Gamma
has an order-preserving action on
\Gamma^{c}
by addition. We denote that action by
+\colon\Gamma\times\Gamma^{c}\to\Gamma^{c}
, and we additionally set
\infty+\lambda:=\infty^{c}
for any
\lambda\in\Gamma^{c}
.

We define a
valuation
on
V
(with values in
\Gamma^{c}
) to be a map

\eta:V\to\Gamma^{c}
satisfying the following conditions for all
u,w\in V
and
x\in K
.

(a)

\eta(u)=\infty^{c}
if and only if
u=0
,

(b)

\eta(xu)=v(x)+\eta(u)
,

(c)

\eta(u+w)\geq\min\{\eta(u),\eta(w)\}
.

(Here the addition in the second item is the above action of
\Gamma
on
\Gamma^{c}
.)

Observe that for each
u\in V
we have
\eta(u)=\eta(-u)
, and that if
\eta(u_{1})<\min\{\eta(u_{2}),\ldots,\eta(u_{k})\}
then
\eta(u_{1}+\ldots+u_{k})=\eta(u_{1})
. Indeed, to see the latter, suppose for a contradiction that
\eta(u_{1}+\ldots+u_{k})>\eta(u_{1})
, and let
w=u_{2}+\ldots+u_{k}
. Then

\eta(u_{1})=\eta(u_{1}+w-w)\geq\min\{\eta(u_{1}+w),\eta(w)\}>\eta(u_{1}),\mbox{~~a contradiction.}

Let
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
be the standard basis of
V=K^{n}
. Define
the “standard valuation”
\eta_{0}
on
V
as
\eta_{0}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}e_{i}):=\{\lambda\in\Gamma:\lambda\geq\min\{v(a_{i}):1\leq i\leq n\}\}
. We leave it to the reader to verify that
\eta_{0}
satisfies the above axioms. (Note that it sends the zero vector to the empty set
\infty^{c}\in\Gamma^{c}
.)

Define
\eta\colon V\to\Gamma^{c}
by
\eta(x):=\bigcup_{g\in G}\eta_{0}(g(x))
.
It is easily checked that
\eta
, too, is a valuation. Indeed, part (a) of the definition is clear.
To see (b), we have

\eta(xu)=\bigcup_{g\in G}\eta_{0}(g(xu))=\bigcup_{g\in G}\eta_{0}(xg(u))

=\bigcup_{g\in G}(v(x)+\eta_{0}(g(u))=v(x)+\bigcup_{g\in G}\eta_{0}(g(u))=v(x)+\eta(u).

To see (c), we have

\eta(u+w)=\bigcup_{g\in G}\eta_{0}(g(u+w))=\bigcup_{g\in G}\eta_{0}(g(u)+g(w))

\geq\bigcup_{g\in G}\min\{\eta_{0}(g(u)),\eta_{0}(g(w))\}=\min\{\bigcup_{g\in G}\eta_{0}(g(u)),\bigcup_{g\in G}\eta_{0}(g(w))\}=\min\{\eta(u),\eta(w)\}.

Let

\nSL_{n}(\eta):=\{g\in\SL_{n}(K):\forall u\in V(\eta(u)=\eta(gu))\}.

It is easily checked that
\nSL_{n}(\eta)
is a group, and is definable. Furthermore, by the definition of
\eta
we have
G\leq\nSL_{n}(\eta)
.

For
\delta\in\Gamma
with
\delta\geq 0
, put

\nSL_{n}(\eta,\delta):=\{g\in\nSL_{n}(\eta):\forall u\in V(\eta(u-gu)\geq\delta+\eta(u))\}.

Then
\nSL_{n}(\eta,0)=\nSL_{n}(\eta)
, and

\nSL_{n}(\eta,\delta)
is a definable normal subgroup of
\nSL_{n}(\eta)
. Indeed, definability and closure under inverse is clear, and we have
(for
g,h\in\nSL_{n}(\eta,\delta)
) that

\eta(u-ghu)=\eta((u-hu)+(hu-ghu))\geq\min\{\eta(u-hu),\eta(hu-g(hu))\}

\geq\min\{\delta+\eta(u),\delta+\eta(hu))\}=\delta+\eta(u);

likewise, for
g\in\nSL_{n}(\eta,\delta)
and
h\in\nSL_{n}(\eta)
and
u\in V
we have

\eta(u-h^{-1}ghu)=\eta(hu-g(hu))\geq\delta+\eta(hu)=\delta+\eta(u).

The intersection
\bigcap_{\delta\geq 0}\nSL_{n}(\eta,\delta)
is trivial; indeed, for any
g\in\SL_{n}(K)\setminus\{I\}
(where
I
is the identity matrix), there exists an
x\in V
with
gx\neq x
. Using boundedness of
G
, we know that
\eta(x)
is not equal to all of
\Gamma
. Using that orbits of
\Gamma
on
\Gamma^{c}
are cofinal we deduce that there exists a
\delta
such that
\eta(x-gx)<\delta+\eta(x)
, so
g\not\in\nSL_{n}(\eta,\delta)
.

Since
\bigcap_{\delta\geq 0}\nSL_{n}(\eta,\delta)
is trivial, there exists a
\delta_{0}
such that
N:=G\cap\nSL_{n}(\eta,\delta_{0})
is not equal to
G
. Since
G
is definably almost simple and
N
is a definable normal subgroup of
G
, the group
N
has to be finite.

By Lemma
4.2.2
below (to be proved), the group

\nSL_{n}(\eta,\delta_{0})
contains an open neighbourhood of the identity matrix
I
in
\nSL_{n}(\eta)
. Hence, since
N
is finite we obtain that
G
is a discrete subgroup of
\nSL_{n}(\eta)
.
It follows by Lemma
2.3.9
that
\dim{G}=0
, and hence by Proposition
2.3.6
(i) that
G
is finite, a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 4.2.2
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
4.2.1
, and the assumption for contradiction that
G
is bounded,
the group
\nSL_{n}(\eta,\delta_{0})
contains an open neighbourhood of the identity
I
of
\nSL_{n}(\eta)
.

Proof.

We first consider the case when
(K,v,\ldots)
is a 1-
h
-minimal expansion of characteristic 0. In this case, by Lemma
3.2.1
(ii), it suffices to show that the tangent spaces of
\nSL_{n}(\eta)
and
\nSL_{n}(\eta,\delta_{0})
are equal, and for this, it suffices to show that they have the same dimensions. Since the dimensions of the tangent spaces equal the dimensions of the corresponding groups, it suffices to show that
\nSL_{n}(\eta)
and
\nSL_{n}(\eta,\delta_{0})
have the same dimension.

Claim
.

There is
\delta_{1}>\delta_{0}
such that for all
u\in V
we have
\eta(u)+\delta_{1}>\eta(u)
.

Proof of Claim.

For each
u\in V
, define
C_{u}:=\{\mu\in\Gamma:\mu+\eta(u)=\eta(u)\}
. Then
C_{u}
is a convex subgroup of
\Gamma
, and since
G
is bounded,
C_{u}\neq\Gamma
. To prove the claim, it suffices to show that there are at most
n=\dim(V)
distinct
C_{u}
, for then we may choose
\delta_{1}>\delta_{0}
outside
\bigcup_{u\in V}C_{u}
.

So suppose for a contradiction that
C_{u_{1}},\ldots,C_{u_{n+1}}
are all distinct, where
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n+1}\in V
. There are
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n+1}\in K
, not all zero, such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}a_{i}u_{i}=0
. Removing some terms if necessary, we may suppose that
a_{i}\neq 0
for all
i
, so
C_{u_{i}}=C_{a_{i}u_{i}}
for each
i
.
Thus, the
C_{a_{i}u_{i}}
are all distinct, so the
\eta(a_{i}u_{i})
are all distinct. Hence, by the initial remarks on valuations in
\Gamma^{c}
, it follows that
\eta(\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}a_{i}u_{i})=\min_{i=1}^{n+1}\eta(a_{i}u_{i})\neq\infty^{c}
, contradicting that
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}a_{i}u_{i}=0
.
∎
(Claim)

Now fix
a\in K
with
v(a)=\delta_{1}
. Let
M_{n}(K)
be the set of
n\times n
matrices over
K
. We define a map
\phi:\nSL_{n}(\eta)\to M_{n}(K)
by putting

\phi(g)=a(g-I)+I
. Since
\phi
is injective, it remains to show that the range of
\phi
lies in
\nSL_{n}(\eta,\delta_{0})
to deduce (as desired) that
\nSL_{n}(\eta,\delta_{0})
has the same dimension as
\nSL_{n}(\eta)
.

So fix
g\in\nSL_{n}(\eta)
and put
h=\phi(g)
. We start by verifying that
h\in\nSL_{n}(\eta)
. To this end,
let
u\in V
. Observe that as
\eta(gu)=\eta(u)
, we have
\eta(gu-u)\geq\eta(u)
. Thus, we have

\eta(a(gu-u))=\delta_{1}+\eta(gu-u)\geq\delta_{1}+\eta(u)>\eta(u)

(where the last inequality holds by the choice of
\delta_{1}
using the Claim). Hence,

\eta(hu)=\eta(a(gu-u)+u)=\min\{\eta(a(gu-u)),\eta(u)\}=\eta(u),

so
h\in\nSL_{n}(\eta).
Furthermore,

\eta(hu-u)=\eta(a(gu-u)+u-u)=\eta(a(gu-u))=\delta_{1}+\eta(gu-u)\geq\eta(u)+\delta_{1},

so as desired, we obtain
h\in\nSL_{n}(\eta,\delta_{1})\subset\nSL_{n}(\eta,\delta_{0}).

Now consider the case when
K
is a pure algebraically closed valued field of characteristic
p
. In this case we can no longer appeal to the arguments involving tangent spaces,
but the argument above still yields that
\nSL_{n}(\eta)
and
\nSL_{n}(\eta,\delta_{0})
are definable groups of the same dimension. Let
\mathbf{H}_{1}=\cl_{K^{\alg}}(\nSL_{n}(\eta,\delta_{0}))
and
\mathbf{H}_{2}=\cl_{K^{\alg}}(\nSL_{n}(\eta))
be their Zariski closures in
\SL_{n}
. Then
\mathbf{H}_{1}\leq\mathbf{H}_{2}
, and using Lemma
2.3.10
(ii),
\mathbf{H}_{1},\mathbf{H}_{2}
have the same Zariski dimension. Therefore,
|\mathbf{H}_{2}:\mathbf{H}_{1}|
is finite. Also, again by Lemma
2.3.10
(ii),
\dim(\nSL_{n}(\eta,\delta_{0}))=\dim(\mathbf{H}_{1}(K))
. Hence, by Lemma
2.3.8
,

\nSL_{n}(\eta,\delta_{0})
has non-empty interior in
\mathbf{H}_{1}(K)
, so
contains an open neighbourhood
U_{1}
of the identity
I
in
\mathbf{H}_{1}(K)
.
This
U_{1}
is also an open neighbourhood of
I
in
\mathbf{H}_{2}(K)

and hence also in
\nSL_{n}(\eta)
.
∎

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.0.1
.

In this section we prove Theorem
1.0.1
. The proof makes essential use of arguments from Section
3.2
(about Lie algebras associated to definable groups) and
classical results about simple Lie algebras over arbitrary fields, along with results from Section
4
.
The Lie algebra approach was suggested by an argument used in the o-minimal case in
[
34
]
.
A key issue is that in the proof of Theorem
1.0.1
we would like to take the Zariski closure of
G
and apply Lemma
2.3.10
(ii). However the latter requires
L_{\divv}
-definability of
G
and is not available in our broader 1-
h
-minimal setting. We circumvent this problem via the next result.

In the entire section, when
K
is a field and
\mathbf{H}
is a linear algebraic
K
-group, we will consider
\mathbf{H}
as living in
K^{\alg}
(
i.e.
,
\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{H}(K^{\alg})
).

Proposition 5.0.1
.

Let
(K,v,\ldots)
be a 1-
h
-minimal expansion of a henselian valued field of characteristic 0, and let
G\subseteq K^{m}
be a non-abelian definable definably almost simple group with
\dim(G)=r
. Then there is a homomorphism
\rho:G\to\SL_{r}(K)
whose kernel is the (finite) center
Z(G)
of
G
and such that
r=\dim\rho(G)=\dim\cl_{K}(\rho(G))
.

Proof.

After replacing
G
by
G/Z(G)
, we may assume by Propositions
3.1.8
and
3.2.14
(using the adjoint representation) that
G\leq\SL_{r}(K)
(where
r=\dim G
). Furthermore, via this representation,
G
acts faithfully as a group of automorphisms of a simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of dimension
r
over
K
. We must show

\dim(G)=\dim\cl_{K}(G)
.

We use some basic theory concerning simple Lie algebras over arbitrary fields, and their automorphism groups, taken from Chapters IX and X of Jacobson
[
28
]
.
First, using that
\mathfrak{g}
is simple, there is a canonical finite extension field
F
of
K
, called the
centroid
of
\mathfrak{g}
, with the property that
\mathfrak{g}
has the structure of a Lie algebra over
F
, and for every extension field
F^{\prime}
of
F
, the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}\otimes_{F}F^{\prime}
(over
F^{\prime}
) is simple. To construct
F
, define
A
to be the associative
K
-algebra of all
K
-linear maps
\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
.
Let
T(\mathfrak{g})
be the
K
-subalgebra of
A
generated by all the maps

a_{L}\colon x\mapsto[a,x]
and
a_{R}\colon x\mapsto[x,a]
for
a\in\mathfrak{g}
. Then
F
is the centraliser of
T(\mathfrak{g})
in
A
. Since
A
has finite vector space dimension over
K
, it follows that
F
is finite dimensional over
K
, so is a finite degree extension of
K
, of degree
d
say.
Observe that

\mathop{\rm ldim}\nolimits_{K}(\mathfrak{g})=d\cdot\mathop{\rm ldim}\nolimits_{F}(\mathfrak{g})
, where
\mathop{\rm ldim}\nolimits_{K}
and
\mathop{\rm ldim}\nolimits_{F}
denote respectively vector space dimension over
K
and
F
. We shall write
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits_{K}(\mathfrak{g})
for the group of automorphisms of
\mathfrak{g}
viewed as a Lie algebra over
K
, and
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits_{F}(\mathfrak{g})
for the group of automorphisms of
\mathfrak{g}
viewed as a Lie algebra over
F
.

By Theorem 5 of Ch. X of Jacobson
[
28
]
, the group
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits_{K}(\mathfrak{g})
is semilinear over
F
, that is, if
g\in\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits_{K}(\mathfrak{g})
then there is
\sigma\in\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(F/K)
such that
g(ax)=a^{\sigma}g(x)
for
a\in F
and
x\in\mathfrak{g}
. Since
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits(F/K)
is finite,
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits_{F}(\mathfrak{g})
is a normal subgroup of
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits_{K}(\mathfrak{g})
of finite index.
Let
G^{\prime}
be the group
\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits_{F}(\mathfrak{g})
, considered as a subgroup of
\GL_{r}(K)
which is definable in our structure
K
.
Since
G
is definably almost simple, it follows that
G\leq G^{\prime}
, and since
G^{\prime}
is Zariski closed in
\GL_{r}(K)
, to obtain
\dim(G)=\dim\cl_{K}(G)
, it remains to prove that
\dim G^{\prime}\leq r
.

Let
m
be the vector space dimension of
\mathfrak{g}
over
F
, so
md=r=\dim(G)
.
Using any identification of the
F
-vector space
\mathfrak{g}
with
F^{m}
, we can consider
G^{\prime}=\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits_{F}(\mathfrak{g})
as a subgroup of
\GL_{m}(F)
. As such, it is the set
\mathbf{H}(F)
of
F
-rational points of an
F
-group
\mathbf{H}\subset\GL_{m}
. Let
e:=\zardim\mathbf{H}
be its Zariski dimension. Then
\dim G^{\prime}\leq de
. Indeed, by Noether normalization, there exists a finite-to-one
F
-morphism
\mathbf{H}\to(K^{\alg})^{e}
. This induces a finite-to-one map from
G^{\prime}=\mathbf{H}(F)
to
F^{e}
. Composing this with a
K
-linear map
F\to K^{d}
yields a finite-to-one map
G^{\prime}\to K^{de}
which is definable in the structure
K
. This implies the inequality
\dim(G^{\prime})\leq de
, as claimed. Thus, to obtain
\dim G^{\prime}\leq r
, it remains to prove that
m=e
.

We now pass to
K^{\alg}
, as follows:
Since
\mathfrak{g}\otimes_{F}F^{\prime}
is a simple Lie algebra over
F^{\prime}
for every
F^{\prime}\supset F
, also
\mathfrak{g}\otimes_{F}K^{\alg}
is a simple Lie algebra over
K^{\alg}
. Note that we have
\mathbf{H}=\mathop{\rm Aut}\nolimits_{K^{\alg}}(\mathfrak{g}\otimes_{F}K^{\alg})
(since preservation of the Lie bracket of
\mathfrak{g}\otimes_{F}K^{\alg}
can be verified on an
F
-basis of
\mathfrak{g}
).
The connected component
\mathbf{H}^{\circ}
is a simple algebraic group of the same Lie type as
\mathfrak{g}\otimes_{F}K^{\alg}
, so we have
e=\zardim\mathbf{H}=\zardim\mathbf{H}^{\circ}=\mathop{\rm ldim}\nolimits_{K^{\alg}}(\mathfrak{g}\otimes_{F}K^{\alg})=m
.
∎

Finally, we prove our main theorem, which we first restate.

Theorem 5.0.2
.

(i)

Let
(K,v,\ldots)
be a 1-
h
-minimal expansion of a henselian valued field of characteristic 0.
Let
G
be a non-abelian group definable in the structure
(K,v,\ldots)
with universe a definable subset of
K^{n}
for some
n
, and suppose that
G
is definably almost simple.
Then there is a semisimple, almost
K
-simple and
K
-isotropic linear algebraic
K
-group
\mathbf{H}
and a group
G^{*}
definably isomorphic to
G/Z(G)
,
such that

\mathbf{H}(K)^{+}\leq G^{*}\leq\mathbf{H}(K)
.

(ii)

Let
(K,v)
be an algebraically closed valued field of characteristic
p>0
, and let
G\leq\SL_{n}(K)

be a definable non-abelian group which is definably almost simple. Then there is a semisimple, almost
K
-simple and
K
-isotropic linear algebraic
K
-group
\mathbf{H}
such that

G/Z(G)
is definably isomorphic to
\mathbf{H}(K)
.

Proof.

Let
r=\dim(G)
. Suppose first that
K
has characteristic 0. After replacing
G
by
G/Z(G)
, we may assume by Proposition
5.0.1
that
G\leq\SL_{r}(K)
and that
r=\dim G=\dim\cl_{K}(G)
.

In characteristic
p
, the group
G
is assumed to be an
L_{\divv}
-definable subgroup of some
\SL_{n}(K)
, and then automatically by Lemma
2.3.10
(ii) we have that
\dim G=\dim\cl_{K}(G)
. Using
[
4
, Theorem 6.8]
to factor out the centre, we may suppose that
G
is centreless.

Thus, either way, we may assume that
G\leq\SL_{n}(K)
with
\dim G=\dim\cl_{K}(G)
, and that
G
is centreless.
In particular, definable almost simplicity of
G
already implies definable simplicity (see Remark
2.1.4
).

Claim 1.
There is a semisimple, almost
K
-simple linear algebraic
K
-group
\mathbf{H}
and a definable injective group homomorphism
\phi\colon G\to\mathbf{H}(K)
such that for
G^{*}:=\phi(G)
, the following holds:
the Zariski closure
\cl_{K}(G^{*})
(of
G^{*}
in
\mathbf{H}(K)
) is all of
\mathbf{H}(K)
, and
\dim(G^{*})=\dim(\mathbf{H}(K))
.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}:={\rm cl}_{K^{\alg}}(G)

be the Zariski closure of
G
in
\SL_{n}
.
Then

\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
is a subgroup of
\SL_{n}
(for example by
[
3
, Ch. I, Proposition 1.3(b)]
). Since any
field automorphism
\alpha
of
K^{\alg}
which fixes
K
pointwise also fixes
G
(as its universe lies in a power of
K
), we also obtain that
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
is a
K
-group, and from that, we deduce that in particular, we have
\cl_{K}(G)=\tilde{\mathbf{H}}(K)
; see also Remark
2.1.1
.

Clearly,
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
is connected.
Indeed, its connected component
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{\circ}

is normal of finite index in
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
, so

G\cap\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{\circ}
is a definable normal subgroup of
G
of finite index,
so contains
G
by definable simplicity of
G
. Thus
\cl_{K^{\alg}}(G)\subset\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{\circ}
and hence we have equality.

Let
\mathbf{N}
be a connected proper normal
K
-subgroup of
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
of maximal Zariski dimension. Then
\mathbf{N}(K)\cap G
is a definable normal subgroup of
G
. If
\mathbf{N}(K)\cap G=G
, then
\mathbf{N}=\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
contradicting properness of
\mathbf{N}
in
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
. Thus, by definable simplicity of
G
,

\mathbf{N}(K)\cap G=1
.

Let
\mathbf{H}=\tilde{\mathbf{H}}/\mathbf{N}
be a quotient in the category of algebraic
K
-groups. Then
\mathbf{H}
is connected and almost

K
-simple (by maximality of
\mathbf{N}
), and is a linear algebraic group defined over
K
, by
[
4
, Theorem 6.8]
.
Let

\rho\colon\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\to\mathbf{H}

be the canonical
K
-epimorphism with
\mathbf{N}=\Ker(\rho)
.
In particular,
\rho:\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\to\mathbf{H}
is surjective with kernel
\mathbf{N}
.

Let
\phi:=\rho_{|G}
, and put
G^{*}:=\phi(G)
. Note that
G^{*}
is Zariski dense in
\mathbf{H}
, as
G
is Zariski dense in
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
.
Since
\Ker(\phi)=G\cap\mathbf{N}(K)
is trivial, by Proposition
2.3.6
(ii) we have
\dim(G)=\dim(G^{*})\leq\dim(\mathbf{H}(K))\leq\dim(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}(K))
. Also, recall that we already established before Claim 1 that

\dim(G)=\dim\cl_{K}(G)=\dim\tilde{\mathbf{H}}(K)
. It follows that all these dimensions are equal, and in particular

\dim(G^{*})=\dim(\mathbf{H}(K)).

The solvable radical
{\mathcal{R}}(\mathbf{H})
of
\mathbf{H}
is
K
-closed and connected, so (since
K
is perfect)
{\mathcal{R}}(\mathbf{H})
is defined over

K
(see
[
3
, 5.1]
), and hence is trivial, by almost
K
-simplicity of
\mathbf{H}
. This gives semisimplicity of
\mathbf{H}
, and hence the claim.
∎
(Claim 1)

Fix any
K
-group embedding of
\mathbf{H}
into some
\SL_{m}
.
Then by Proposition
4.2.1
,
G^{*}
is not bounded, and as
G^{*}\leq\mathbf{H}(K)
, the group
\mathbf{H}(K)
is not bounded either. Hence, by Theorem
4.1.1
,
\mathbf{H}
is isotropic over
K
.

Since
\dim(G^{*})=\dim(\mathbf{H}(K))
, it follows from Lemma
2.3.8

that
G^{*}
has non-empty interior in

\mathbf{H}(K)
.
Since
G^{*}
is a subgroup of
\mathbf{H}(K)
, having non-empty interior already implies that
G^{*}
is open in
\mathbf{H}(K)
(pick an open subset of
G^{*}
and translate it around).
As noted above,
G^{*}
is not bounded.

In the case when
(K,v,\ldots)
is 1-
h
-minimal of characteristic 0, it follows by Theorem
4.1.2
that
G^{*}\geq\mathbf{H}(K)^{+}
, so we are done. In the case when
(K,v)
is a pure algebraically closed valued field of characteristic
p
, it follows from Proposition
4.1.3
that
G
is not a proper subgroup of
\mathbf{H}(K)^{+}
.
Since
K
is algebraically closed (and
\mathbf{H}
is semisimple), we have
\mathbf{H}(K)^{+}=\mathbf{H}(K)
(by Remark
2.1.8
),
and so
G=\mathbf{H}(K)
.
This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎
