Reclassification of Two MLH1 Variants of Uncertain Significance Utilizing Clinical and Functional Data
Authors: Jane Hübertz Frederiksen, Ulf Birkedal, Sarah Bachmann, Elisabeth Victoria Eliesen, Lene Juel Rasmussen, Katja Venborg Pedersen, Lana Al‐Zehhawi, Susanne E Boonen, Lotte Krogh, Karina Rønlund, Lise Graversen, Jannie Assenholt, Kjeld Schmiegelow, Karin Wadt, Anne‐Marie Gerdes, Thomas v O Hansen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPathogenic variants in the mismatch repair genes are associated with an elevated lifetime risk of colorectal cancer (CRC). We previously identified two variants of uncertain significance (VUS) in the MLH1 gene, c.696_698del, p.(Cys233del) and c.1919C > G, p.(Pro640Arg), in Danish families with numerous occurrences of CRC.MethodsTo reclassify the variants we collected clinical data, initiated tumor and co‐segregation analysis, and performed RNA splicing analysis, subcellular localization, and protein stability studies.ResultsThe functional analysis revealed that the c.696_698del, p.(Cys233del) variant had an effect at the RNA level, on subcellular localization, and on protein stability, while the c.1919C > G, p.(Pro640Arg) variant showed decreased expression in localization studies and decreased protein stability. These results suggest both variants disrupt DNA mismatch repair.ConclusionBy applying all collected data and functional results we propose to reclassify the c.696_698del, p.(Cys233del) and the c.1919C > G, p.(Pro640Arg) variants as likely pathogenic (class 4) using MMR gene‐specific ACMG/AMP guidelines. Consequently, the two MLH1 variants can now be used for risk assessment of variant carriers, while family members without the variants can be excluded from intensified cancer surveillance and follow population recommendations.Keywords: ACMG/AMP classification, Lynch syndrome, MLH1, nuclear localization, protein stability, RNA splicing analysis

Introduction

Lynch syndrome (LS) is one of the most common hereditary cancer predisposition syndromes. LS is caused by a pathogenic variant in one allele of the core mismatch repair (MMR) genes MLH1 [OMIM 120436], MSH2 [OMIM 609309], MSH6 [OMIM 600678], or PMS2 [OMIM 600259] (Lynch et al. 2015). Individuals with LS have an increased risk of developing early onset cancer, especially colorectal cancer (CRC), and for females also endometrial cancer (EC) (Lynch et al. 2015; Møller 2020). Moreover, the risk of other cancer types, e.g., gastric cancer may be increased in LS patients (Dominguez‐Valentin et al. 2020).

In the last decade, DNA sequencing has become faster, cheaper, and more available, causing identification of more genetic variants. These variants need classification according to their pathogenicity. However, some variants, e.g., missense variants or in‐frame insertions/deletions are difficult to interpret and are therefore often classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS). As of March 2024, more than 87% of the MLH1 missense variants in the ClinVar database (Landrum et al. 2014) were reported as VUS, indicating the huge implication of this problem. There are several methods available to analyze the functional impact of MMR variants (Frederiksen et al. 2021). Standards and guidelines have been developed to ease and streamline the interpretation of a given genetic variant, where several factors can be considered in variant classification, including co‐segregation, population, and in silico data, clinical data as well as data from functional studies (Richards et al. 2015). The InSiGHT Hereditary CRC/Polyposis Variant Curation Expert Panel has recently generated specifications of these criteria for the interpretation of variants in the MMR genes (https://cspec.genome.network/cspec/ui/svi/affiliation/50099).

Here we report clinical and functional data regarding two MLH1 variants, c.696_698del, p.(Cys233del) and c.1919C > G, p.(Pro640Arg), and propose the reclassification from VUS to likely pathogenic using MMR gene‐specific ACMG/AMP guideline criteria.

Materials And Methods

Patients and Clinical Data

The variants were identified via diagnostic genetic testing after genetic counseling. Clinical data regarding family phenotype, individual phenotype, cancer diagnosis, age at onset, tumor data (immunohistochemistry (IHC), microsatellite instability (MSI), BRAF p.(Val600Glu), and MLH1 promoter methylation) were obtained from the Departments of Clinical Genetics and Departments of Pathology. All patients gave informed consent to genetic analysis, and relevant patients gave consent to publication. The study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration.

Variant Nomenclature

The MLH1 variants were numbered according to GenBank accession numbers NM_000249.4 using the nomenclature guidelines of the Human Genome Variation Society (http://varnomen.hgvs.org/).

Population and In Silico Analysis

Regarding population data, the gnomAD (v.2.1.1, non‐cancer) database (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/) was used. Prior probability of pathogenicity was calculated using the online tool available at the Huntsman Cancer Institute (http://priors.hci.utah.edu/PRIORS/index.php) (Thompson et al. 2013). No protein predictions were possible for the c.696_698del, p.(Cys233del) variant. Predicted protein structure of MLH1 wt, c.696_698del and c.1919C > G was made using AlphaFold Colab and MLH1 P40692‐1 (Jumper et al. 2021) and visualized using PyMOL v.2.5.5. For in silico splicing prediction MaxEntScan and SpliceAI (Yeo and Burge 2004; Jaganathan et al. 2019) were used.

RNA Splicing Assays

Splicing analysis was carried out on RNA purified from lymphoblastoid cell lines treated with or without puromycin to inhibit nonsense‐mediated decay as previously described (Jelsig et al. 2023). Reverse transcriptase SuperScript IV (#18090050 Invitrogen) was used to produce cDNA, which was used as a template for PCR with HotStarTaq DNA polymerase (#203205 Qiagen) and forward primer in exon 7; 5′‐AGTACACAATGCAGGCATTAG‐3′ and reverse primer in exon 11; 5′‐FAM‐AACATCCACATTCTGGGGACTG‐3′, and forward primer in exon 15; 5′‐TTTGCCAATTTTGGTGTTCTCAGG‐3′ and reverse primer in exon 19; 5′‐FAM‐TTCTGTGAAATGTTTAGGAGGCAG‐3′. The RT‐PCR products from patients and two control samples were subjected to nanopore sequencing using the MinION sequencer, an R10.4.1 flow cell, and the SQK‐NBD114.96 library kit from Oxford Nanopore Technologies. The sequencing was operated by MinKNOW 23.04.5, raw reads were basecalled with Guppy 6.5.7+ca6d6af and mapped to GRCh38 with minimap2 v.2.24‐r1122. The reads were filtered to only include full‐length reads defined by coverage of the first and last exon in the target area, and an in‐house python3 script employing pysam was used to separate reads allele specifically in the one patient where a single available heterozygous SNP was available. Sashimi plots of the relevant regions were made with ggsashimi v.1.1.5 modified in‐house to take sample‐individual cut‐offs for drawing exon junctions set to 2% of the total read count (Garrido‐Martín et al. 2018).

Cells

Human HeLa and HEK293T cells were maintained as monolayer cultures in DMEM (#61965059 Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (#10500064 Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco #15140122). Cells were grown at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Vectors

Mammalian expression vectors with cDNA sequence of MLH1 fused with green fluorescent protein (GFP) mClover3 or red fluorescent protein (RFP) mRuby3 were purchased through Twist Bioscience. The MLH1 variants of interest were introduced into the GFP‐MLH1 vector by QuikChangeII XL site‐directed mutagenesis (#200522, Agilent). Primer sequences can be provided upon request. The introduction of variants was verified by Sanger sequencing. Moreover, whole cDNA sequencing was performed to exclude the introduction of alternative variants.

Localization Assay

HeLa and HEK293T cells were transiently co‐transfected with pTWIST‐mRuby3‐MLH1 and pTWIST‐mClover3‐MLH1‐VUS constructs with PolyJet (#SL100688, Tebu‐Bio) using manufacturer's instructions. The cells were co‐transfected with pRuby3‐MLH1‐wt and pClover3‐MLH1 either wt or variant. 24–48 h after transfection, the cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde, washed in PBS, and mounted onto a glass slide using vectashield antifade mounting medium with DAPI (VECTH‐1200) and nail polish. The fluorescence was detected by confocal fluorescence microscopy (FV1000, Olympus). The fluorescence intensity was measured and quantified using ImageJ. The assay was repeated at least four times for each variant or wt with three to 15 images of cells from each repeat.

Protein Stability Assay

HEK293T cells were transiently transfected with pTwist‐mClover3‐MLH1‐VUS construct by Polyjet using the manufacturer's protocol. Twenty‐four to 48 h after transfection, the protein synthesis was terminated by 25 μg/mL cycloheximide (#C4859‐1ML, Sigma). Protein lysates were harvested after 0, 4, 8, and 12 h. Harvesting was performed as follows using ice‐cold regents; cells were washed with PBS and incubated in 1X cell lysis buffer (#9803S Cell Signalling) with complete mini protease inhibitor cocktail (#11836153001 Sigma‐Aldrich) for 5 min on ice. Next, the cells were scraped off, transferred to an Eppendorf tube, and centrifuged for 15 min at 17.000×G, 4°C, and the supernatant was stored at −80°C. Total protein concentration was measured by Pierce BCA Protein Assay kit (#23225 ThermoFisher) and EPOCH spectrophotometer (BioTek). Each assay was repeated in triplicate, or more, for each variant.

Western Blotting

Ten micrograms (μg) of total protein were loaded and separated on Novex wedgewell 4%–12% tris‐glycine gel (#XP04125BOX Invitrogen) by gel electrophoresis and transferred to PVDF membranes (#IB24002 Invitrogen) using the iBlot2, according to manufacturer's instructions. To detect proteins of interest the following antibodies were used: anti‐MLH1 (#554073, BD bioscience), anti‐GAPDH (MA5‐15738, Invitrogen), and Goat anti‐mouse IgG (G21040, Invitrogen). Antibody binding was visualized using chemiluminescence (#32106 Thermo) and Syngene G: Box (Synoptics). ImageJ software was used to analyze the result. The relative MLH1 amount was calculated as the MLH1 to GAPDH (loading control) ratio and displayed as a percentage of MLH1 from steady‐state levels (T0), and an unpaired t‐test was used for analyzing any statistical difference between wt and variant.

Results

Family Data and Tumor Analysis of the MLH1 c.696_698del, p.(Cys233del) Variant

The MLH1 c.696_698del, p.(Cys233del) variant was identified in seven Danish families with LS‐related cancers (Figure 1A). In 10 carriers, 10 CRC (36–77 years, median age of 63 years) and two EC (66 and 74 years, median age of 70 years) tumors were detected, of which three CRC and one EC tumor showed no staining of MLH1 protein and unmethylated MLH1 promoter. Nine carriers had, at the time of publication, not developed cancer (36–83 years, median age 57 years). Moreover, eight untested relatives developed nine CRC tumors (51–73 years, median age 63 years).

Pedigree Data and Tumor Analysis of the MLH1 c.1919C > G, p.(Pro640Arg) Variant

The MLH1 c.1919C > G, p.(Pro640Arg) variant was reported in a single family (Figure 1B). The male index person presented with CRC at the age of 33 years. The mother had gastric cancer at age 36 years, while the maternal uncle also had CRC (age 60 years), and both were shown to carry the variant. The untested maternal grandmother had gynecological cancer at age 30 years (family information) and CRC at age 55 years. Since her husband was tested negative for the variant, we assumed the grandmother was a carrier. IHC of tumors from the proband and the proband maternal uncle showed loss of MLH1 expression and unmethylated MLH1 promotor.

Population and In Silico Data

Neither the c.696_698del, p.(Cys233del) nor the c.1919C > G, p.(Pro640Arg) variants have been reported in the gnomAD database. In silico predictions of pathogenicity of MLH1 c.1919C > G, p.(Pro640Arg) showed a high likelihood of protein damage, with a prior probability of pathogenicity of 0.9. Alphafold simulations showed that both variants changed the structure of MLH1 (Figure S1). In silico splicing prediction indicated that the c.696_698del variant generated a cryptic splice donor site at position c.695 (MaxEntScan score 6.9, canonical site 10.4). However, it was not predicted using SpliceAI (de Sainte et al. 2023). The latter tool instead showed a minor loss at both the canonical acceptor and donor site in exon 9 (0.11 and 0.12 respectively on a 0–1 scale). The c.1919C > G, p.(Pro640Arg) variant was not predicted to affect RNA splicing.

RNA Splicing Analysis

The splice analysis showed a naturally occurring minor fraction of transcripts skipping exon 9 and exon 9–10 in the control samples, and that the relative amount of the ∆Ex9 and ∆Ex9–10 transcripts were increased in a c.696_698del variant carrier (Figure S2). Besides the c.696_698del variant, patient F5 also has a nearby benign heterozygous SNP (c.655A > G in exon 8), which was used as the basis for allele‐specific analysis. This showed that the increased ∆Ex9 and ∆Ex9‐10 transcript fractions were transcribed from the variant allele (Figure 2). The c.1919C > G variant did not affect splicing (Figure S3).

Prior to plotting, the sequence reads were split allele specifically based on a heterozygous SNP (c.655A > G) present in the F5 carrier, denoted by the asterisks, and termed allele A. c.696_698del can be seen as a dent in the coverage plot in exon 9 at allele B highlighted by the black arrows. Subcellular Localization and Protein Stability Analysis

We then analyzed the effect of both p.(Cys233del) and p.(Pro640Arg) variants on subcellular localization (Figure 3A). Normally, MMR proteins are present in the nucleus, where they carry out post‐replicational repair. We found the p.(Cys233del) variant to be partially retained in the cytoplasm compared to wt protein, while the p.(Pro640Arg) variant showed low levels of fluorescent signal compared to wt MLH1 suggesting low protein level. Therefore, we investigated the MLH1 protein turnover over time (Figure 3B). The results show that both the p.(Cys233del) and p.(Pro640Arg) variant proteins were degraded significantly faster than wt MLH1 and benign control p.(Ile219Val), and to a level comparable to a pathogenic control (p.(Arg659Pro)) (Abildgaard et al. 2019).

Image (C) is taken at lower magnification to include more cells. (D) Protein stability of MLH1 variants over time. Error bars indicate the standard deviation (n ≥ 3). *: p = 0.017. **: p = 0.012 and denotes the statistically significant difference in protein level between MLH1 wt and each of the MLH1 variants after 12 h.

Discussion

In this study, we describe the reclassification of two MLH1 VUSs to likely pathogenic using MMR gene‐specific ACMG/AMP guidelines. The first variant, c.696_698del, p.(Cys233del), was identified in seven Danish families. Among the variant carriers, we found three CRC and one EC tumors with loss of MLH1 expression and unmethylated MLH1 promoter, which meets the PP4_Strong criteria. Strikingly, we also identified tumors from three carriers with loss of MLH1 expression, but with methylated MLH1 promoter in the tumor sample. Analysis of germline methylation of the MLH1 promoter in two of these carriers showed an unmethylated MLH1 promoter, and we, therefore, speculate that the MLH1 promoter methylation observed could reflect a second hit in the tumor as recently proposed (Helderman et al. 2024). The median age for CRC (36–77 years, median age of 63 years) and EC (66–74 years, median age of 70 years) is higher than previously reported (CRC median age 42 years (males)/47 years (females) and endometrial cancer median age 47.5 years) (Stoffel et al. 2009). Moreover, we identified nine unaffected carriers (36–83 years, median age 57 years), indicating the c.696_698del, p.(Cys233del) variant may have reduced penetrance.

Interestingly, the variant was shown to affect MLH1 function in several ways. Splicing analysis showed an increase in skipping of exon 9 (frameshift) and exon 9–10 (in‐frame). However, the total skipping events were found to comprise only 15%–20% of the total amount of transcripts, and hence the effect is not considered pathogenic according to the ACMG RNA splicing guidelines (Walker et al. 2023). On the protein level, the c.696_698del, p.(Cys233del) variant was shown to be unstable over time, with an MLH1 variant protein level of ~25% compared to wt, which is comparable to the p.(Arg659Pro) pathogenic control (Abildgaard et al. 2019). Furthermore, we found the variant protein to have decreased localization to the nucleus compared to wt, which is in line with previous data (Andersen et al. 2012). The results from the functional protein stability assay meet the PS3_Supporting criteria using the ACMG/AMP guidelines for functional analysis (Brnich et al. 2019). Finally, since the c.696_698del, p.(Cys233del) variant is not reported in the gnomAD database, it meets the PM2_Supporting criteria.

To sum up, the c.696_698del, p.(Cys233del) variant meets the PM2_Supporting, PP4_Strong, and PS3_Supporting criteria. Thereby, it is possible to reclassify the MLH1 c.696_698del, p.(Cys233del) variant as likely pathogenic (class 4), possibly with lower penetrance based on family data. Additional families with the c.696_698del, p.(Cys233del) variant or functional analysis of the variant may shed light on this issue in the future. Currently, the carriers in the family follow standard LS surveillance for MLH1 carriers, since the assumption is the variant still induces accelerated tumorigenesis. However, the carriers have been informed the lifetime cancer risk may be lower than for other pathogenic MLH1 variants.

The c.1919C > G, p.(Pro640Arg) variant was observed in a single family and was shown to co‐segregate with disease. CRC tumor tissue from two carriers showed loss of MLH1 expression and unmethylated MLH1 promoter thus meeting the PP4_Moderate criteria.

Subcellular localization analysis showed nuclear MLH1 localization. However, very low protein expression was observed. Moreover, our data showed that the variant protein was degraded significantly faster than wt protein, with an MLH1 protein level of ~15% compared to wt after 12 h. This meets the PS3_Supporting criteria. The data is in accordance with the analysis of other missense variants in the same codon (p.(Pro640Ser) and p.(Pro640Leu)), which also showed an effect on MLH1 protein stability, as well as in a 6‐thioguanine selection assay (Hardt et al. 2011; Hinrichsen et al. 2013; Houlleberghs et al. 2020). The variant p.(Pro640Ser) has additionally been classified as likely pathogenic by the InSiGHT variant interpretation expert panel, which then causes the p.(Pro640Arg) variant to meet the PM5_Supporting criteria. Since the variant is not reported in the gnomAD database, it meets the PM2_Supporting criteria, while a prior probability for pathogenicity score of 0.9 meets the PP3_Moderate criteria.

Taken together, the c.1919C > G, p.(Pro640Arg) variant meets the PP3_Moderate, PP4_Moderate, PM2_Supporting, PS3_Supporting, and PM5_Supporting criteria. Thereby, it is possible to reclassify the MLH1 c.1919C > G, p.(Pro640Arg) variant as likely pathogenic (class 4), which is in line with data in the ClinVar database (VCV000959603.3).

Our results suggest that the two MLH1 variants now can be used clinically for risk assessment of variant carriers, and that family members without the variants will have the population risk of CRC. However, further studies are needed to clarify if the MLH1 c.696_698del, p.(Cys233del) variant results in a reduced penetrance.
