Transcultural Adaptation and Validation of the German DIGEST-FEES
Authors: Susanne Bauer, Stephanie Rieser-Reetz, Jörg E Bohlender, Heather M Starmer, Katherine A Hutcheson, Erich Hartmann, Meike Brockmann-Bauser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFlexible endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES) is one of two diagnostic gold standards for pharyngeal dysphagia in patients with head and neck cancer (HNC). However, an evidence-based summary outcome measure was missing until recently. The Dynamic Imaging Grade of Swallowing Toxicity (DIGEST) for FEES was adapted from DIGESTV2 for videofluoroscopy for this purpose and is a validated analysis method, grading the severity of pharyngeal dysphagia based on the swallowing parameters safety and efficiency. Main aim of this study was to translate and validate DIGEST-FEES into German (DIGEST-FEES-G).MethodsFirst, the transcultural translation included forward and backward translations, cognitive interviews, and consensus in an expert committee together with the original developers. Subsequently, the 100 endoscopic examination videos of the DIGEST-FEES original validation study were retrospectively analyzed with DIGEST-FEES-G.ResultsAfter a training phase, inter-rater reliability was almost perfect for overall DIGEST-FEES-G grade (Kw = 0.89), DIGEST-FEES-G safety (Kw = 0.9), and DIGEST-FEES-G efficiency (Kw = 0.88). Intra-rater reliability for the DIGEST-FEES-G rating was almost perfect (rater 1: Kw = 0.81–0.88) and substantial to almost perfect (rater 2: Kw = 0.78–0.92). Overall DIGEST-FEES-G grade correlated with the German Murray Secretion Scale (r = 0.47; p < 0.01), German Yale Vallecular Residue (r = 0.74; p < 19 0.01), and German Yale Pyriform Sinus Residue (r = 0.66; p < 0.01).ConclusionThe validation and reliability findings of the original study could be replicated for DIGEST-FEES-G which increases validity of the DIGEST-FEES scale. The translated version, DIGEST-FEES-G, provides a psychometrically sound analysis method for the severity grading of pharyngeal dysphagia in HNC for German-speaking practitioners.Keywords: Dysphagia, Head and neck cancer, Dynamic Imaging Grade of Swallowing Toxicity, Flexible endoscopic evaluation of swallowing, Translation

Introduction

Dysphagia in patients with head and neck cancer (HNC) represents a complex and multifactorial clinical condition [1, 2]. The occurrence of dysphagia can be a direct effect of the tumor as well as a common side effect of tumor treatment. The type and severity of dysphagia in patients with HNC thus varies depending on the time in the course of disease and treatment, tumor location and classification, and type of treatment [3]. The risk of developing dysphagia, its severity, and pathophysiology varies widely depending on the clinical scenario and patient characteristics. The damage may affect structural, mechanical, and neurological components of swallowing [2]. Dysphagia in patients with HNC is an independent predictor of survival and is associated with a higher rate of pneumonia, reduced food intake, prolonged tube feeding, weight loss, and malnutrition [4].

Prevalence data for oropharyngeal dysphagia in patients with HNC vary widely in the literature [5, 6], due in part to a lack of consistent outcome measures. Dawe et al. [7] point out in their systematic review of swallowing function after HNC treatment, that there is no consistent approach for evaluating swallowing. Fundamentally, no single measure of swallowing is universally recognized or expected to fully define dysphagia. There are difficulties in interpreting the heterogeneous measurements when trying to compare treatment outcomes, and the need for verifiable outcome measures for swallowing function is emphasized [7, 8]. Clinical Outcome Assessments (COAs) are often used in global clinical trials as a means of determining whether a clinical benefit has been demonstrated. In addition to the collection of patient-reported outcomes, further types of outcome measures, including clinician-reported outcome, observer-reported outcome, and performance outcome, can provide guidance for the selection of the examination instruments used and are becoming more widespread [9].

The Dynamic Imaging Grade of Swallowing Toxicity (DIGEST) is a clinician-rated measure, initially developed and validated for videofluoroscopy in the HNC population [10]. It is in use currently as version 2: DIGESTV2 [11]. The aim of the DIGEST methodology was to provide a reproducible way to grade the severity of pharyngeal dysphagia based on both the safety and efficiency of bolus clearance that aligns to the Common Toxicity Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) framework [12]. It was followed by an adaptation and validation for FEES for the HNC population [13] that has sound psychometric properties. DIGEST uses two primary visuo-perceptual ratings of bolus clearance as the base measures: the Penetration-Aspiration Scale (PAS) for safety and the ordinal estimation of pharyngeal residue (PR) for efficiency for each bolus in a standard protocol. With the use of a decision tree that includes grading the PAS events, amount and frequency of penetration/aspiration (DIGEST safety) and combining this information with a grading of PR (DIGEST efficiency), an overall grade is derived (DIGEST grade). This grade provides a gestalt summation of pharyngeal impairment. DIGEST intends to complement to swallowing analysis tools and serve as an easily communicated endpoint measure of swallowing. Subsequent validation studies have extended its reliability and clinical utility to additional populations, including Parkinson’s disease [14], amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [15], healthy young adults [16], and post-stroke [17]. The DIGEST analysis method is becoming more widespread and translations into other languages are being published, such as the Swedish version of DIGEST-FEES [18], or are in progress.

Therefore, the main objective of the present work was to create a validated German language version of DIGEST-FEES. The first aim was to transculturally translate the DIGEST-FEES flowsheet into a German version DIGEST-FEES-G (G = German). The second aim was to assess criterion validity of DIGEST-FEES-G to the original DIGEST-FEES, and whether the results of the original DIGEST-FEES validation study in HNC could be replicated.

Methods

Phase 1 Translation Process and Transcultural Adaptation

The main goal of this step was to obtain a meaningful and culturally specific translation [19]. McKown et al. [9] reported on good practices for the translation, cultural adaptation, and linguistic validation of clinical outcome measures. A standardized and thus comparable procedure was recommended. The translation process for DIGEST-FEES-G was implemented according to the steps outlined by Sousa and Rojjanasrirat [19], which are in accordance with McKown et al. [9]. First, two forward translations (step 1) were produced by a specialist and a bilingual person without specialist knowledge. From these two versions, a first consensus version (synthesis I) was produced in consultation with another bilingual person and a member of the research team (step 2). Two blind back-translations of this synthesis I were produced (step 3). From these steps, a collection of questions was sent to the author of DIGESTV2. A committee of experts with expertise in dysphagia was formed, here defined as having a degree in speech-language pathology and clinical experience in dysphagia >10 years. After review by the committee of experts, a second consensus version (synthesis II) was produced together with the original author (step 4). This was followed by a comprehensibility test based on cognitive interviews with two experts (step 5) and a further exchange in the Expert Committee, during which the feedback was discussed (step 6). The necessary psychometric pre-testing with a bilingual group mentioned by Sousa and Rojjanasrirat [19] under step 6 was not carried out, as DIGEST is an analysis method for professionals and not a questionnaire survey. Instead, a further assessment by the Expert Committee was added to the procedure in order to incorporate the feedback from the comprehensibility test. This exchange took place with the two expert translators, members of the research team, and the developers of the two original versions DIGESTV2 and DIGEST-FEES. The final version, DIGEST-FEES-G, was created on this basis. Thereafter psychometric evaluation was conducted (step 7; online suppl. material; Table 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/fpl/acvag009). Phase 2 Psychometric Evaluation

Study Design

For validation of DIGEST-FEES-G, a retrospective analysis of the videos from the original DIGEST-FEES study was conducted. This database consisted of 100 deidentified FEES examinations in patients with HNC, stratified by severity through preselection in the original study by an experienced speech-language pathologist (SLP) to ensure 1/3 of the exams were reflective of clinically judged normal or mild impairment, 1/3 to reflect moderate impairment, and 1/3 to reflect severe impairment [13]. The examinations were performed according to a standard bolus protocol (2 × 5 mL thin liquid, 2 × 10 mL thin liquid, 2 sips of thin liquid from a cup, 2× tsp. apple sauce, and 1× bite of a graham cracker). The videos depicted real-life clinical swallowing examinations during the course of treatment. Depending on the situation, compensations and strategies (e.g., prompts to cough, re-swallow or instructions for swallowing maneuvers) were also carried out or additional textures were used. Consistent with DIGEST rating instructions, swallows were not rated after a compensation was cued.

Participants and Video Recordings

The 100 endoscopic examination videos of the original study were made available by the original author via an online platform. For further details of the study sample, please refer to the original study [13]. The study videos were not translated but evaluated using the German version DIGEST-FEES-G.

Rating

Two raters experienced in endoscopic swallowing examinations were used for the rating in this study. One of these was an SLP with over 7 years of FEES experience. The second rater was an otorhinolaryngologist and phoniatrician with 6 years of clinical FEES experience. When reviewing the examination videos, the following three graduation scales were rated in parallel: DIGEST-FEES-G (DIGEST-FEES-G safety, DIGEST-FEES-G efficiency, and overall DIGEST-FEES-G grade), the Murray Secretion Scale, and the Yale Pharyngeal Residue Severity Rating Scale. The raters operated the playback of the videos independently and could pause or repeat the video sequence as often as they wished. The rating procedure comprised three stages, with a training and two subsequent study phases (see Table 1). For the training phase, the author of the original validation study provided 20 further videos that were not among the 100 videos from study phases 1 and 2. Of these, five videos were used for training purposes through a joint video review by the two raters. To establish inter-rater reliability, 15 test videos were evaluated independently to achieve at least 80% agreement, consistent with the original study. Since no formal DIGEST-FEES training was available at the time of this study, the study author developed a FEES reference template. This included example images illustrating the laryngeal vestibular boundaries for penetration and the four residue levels for each consistency (liquid IDDSI 0, pudding IDDSI 4, solid IDDSI 7). Instructions on the timing of residue rating were also provided. Additionally, a training session was conducted with the original author, H. Starmer. For the Murray Secretion Scale and the Yale Pharyngeal Residue Severity Rating Scale, the description and image presentation from the publications were used.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to report the percentage agreement. For assessment of the inter- and intra-rater reliability, the quadratically weighted Cohen’s kappa coefficient [26, 27] was used, in which disagreements of different severity (or agreement of different degrees) are weighted accordingly. The interpretation was done according to Cohen’s kappa strength of agreement: <0.00 no; 0.00–0.20 slight; 0.21–0.40 fair; 0.41–0.60 moderate; 0.61–0.80 substantial; 0.81–1.00 almost perfect agreement [28]. To test criterion validity, two-tailed Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated (α = 0.05) between overall DIGEST-FEES grade, DIGEST-FEES-G safety, and DIGEST-FEES-G efficiency, and the clinical measures. Correlation strength was interpreted according to Cohen [29], with r = 0.10 weak, r = 0.30 moderate, and r = 0.50 strong. The data were coded in an excel sheet [30] and statistical analyses were performed with SPSS (27) [31].

Results

Translation Process

In the translation, English terms and phrases led to discussions about different interpretations of terms, e.g., “bolus trial,” “bolus presentation,” “bolus type,” “consistency,” “overall bolus,” “if any additional bolus trial,” “initial swallow attempt,” “swallows.” Depending on the situation, different processes during swallowing are labeled with these terms and different interpretations arise that influence the DIGEST classification. The choice of words in the German translation was discussed in detail by the Expert Committee, but differences in the interpretation of terminology or instructions remained to some extent. This showed the need for additional material for DIGEST in the form of a manual and training material, which was not available at the time but subsequently completed and published online by the developers. In the translated German version, adjustments were made in comparison with the original version in consultation with the original developers (online suppl. material; Table 2).

Adjustments in the DIGEST-FEES-G conceptual model: in the cognitive interviews, feedback was given on missing connecting lines, as links were also seen between safety and the risk to nutrition and between efficiency and possible effects in the context of pneumonia. In consultation with the original authors, two further links were added here: (1) from “safety” to the risk to nutrition and (2) from “efficiency” to pneumonia, as the conceptual model does not intend to reduce the links between the various aspects of safety and efficiency and their effects.

Psychometric Evaluation

Inter-Rater Reliability

The agreement between the two raters at baseline (15 videos) was substantial for the overall DIGEST-FEES-G grade (Kw = 0.71) and DIGEST-FEES-G safety (Kw = 0.72) and moderate for DIGEST-FEES-G efficiency (Kw = 0.59). The percentage exact agreement was 53% for the overall DIGEST-FEES-G grade, 33% for DIGEST-FEES-G safety, and 60% for DIGEST-FEES-G efficiency.

In the study phase 1, the agreement between the two raters was almost perfect for the overall DIGEST-FEES-G grade (Kw = 0.89), DIGEST-FEES-G safety (Kw = 0.9), and DIGEST-FEES-G efficiency (Kw = 0.88). The exact percentage agreement was 83% for the overall DIGEST-FEES-G grade, 85% for DIGEST-FEES-G safety, and 84% for DIGEST-FEES-G efficiency. The other grading scales (Murray Secretion Scale and the two Yale Residue Scales) showed lower agreement values. The rating of residue in the Pyriform sinus showed the lowest agreement (see Table 2).

Intra-Rater Reliability

The agreement between the two raters in study phase 2 (30 videos, rating interval: 3 weeks) was almost perfect (rater 1: Kw = 0.81–0.88) or substantial to almost perfect (rater 2: Kw = 0.78–0.92) for the DIGEST values. For the Murray Secretion Scale and for the two Yale Residue Scales, lower values were found for most of the kappa values and the percent agreement values (see Table 3).

Criterion Validity

The calculation of the Spearman’s rank correlation (see Table 4) showed that the overall DIGEST-FEES-G grade was significantly associated with the criterion measures: Murray Secretion Scale (r = 0.47, p < 0.01), Yale Residue Scale Valleculae (r = 0.74, p < 0.01), and Yale Residue Scale Pyriform sinus (r = 0.66, p < 0.01). According to Cohen [29], there was a moderate correlation between the overall DIGEST-FEES-G grade and the Murray Secretion Scale and a strong correlation with the Yale Residue Scale Valleculae and the Yale Residue Scale Pyriform sinus. DIGEST-FEES-G efficiency correlated more closely with the criterion measures of the Yale Residue Scale Valleculae (r = 0.82, p < 0.01) and the Yale Residue Scale Pyriform sinus (r = 0.68, p < 0.01) than with the Murray Secretion Scale (r = 0.44, p < 0.01). The criterion values obtained are consistent with the data from the original study by [13] (see Table 5).

Discussion

The ability to assign a valid and reliable pharyngeal dysphagia severity rating is critical for outcomes research in HNC. The DIGEST framework has provided such a standardized approach for VFSS and FEES using CTCAE-based nomenclature familiar to oncology. Our findings demonstrate that the German translation of the DIGEST-FEES preserves the psychometric properties of the original scale, confirming its utility for grading overall pharyngeal swallowing severity in patients with HNC. Consistent with the prior work establishing DIGEST-FEES as a valid and reliable outcome measure, the present study shows reliability and validity indices that mimic those reported by Starmer et al. [13]. These results underscore the clinical value of the DIGEST-FEES-G as a standardized, evidence-based tool that can support precise dysphagia severity grading and enhance assessment consistency in German-speaking clinical practice. In addition, its standardized grading system may facilitate interdisciplinary communication and the consistent interpretation of findings across healthcare professionals involved in dysphagia management.

Reliability

In the training phase, there was substantial agreement for the kappa value between the two raters for the overall DIGEST-FEES-G grade and DIGEST-FEES-G safety for the test videos, but DIGEST-FEES-G efficiency showed a lower agreement for the kappa value. One hypothesized reason for suboptimal agreement on the efficiency grading in the training phase was the timing of residue rating. Concerning the quantity estimation of the residue, there was disagreement between the two raters regarding the percentage estimation which showed the need for reference images for all consistencies (liquid IDDSI 0, pudding IDDSI 4, solid IDDSI 7) as well as the corresponding quantity (<10% residue, 10–33% residue, 34–66% residue >66% residue). At the time of this study, no formal DIGEST training and image material was available for FEES; therefore, the study author developed instruction and reference material to be used by our raters. In the following study phase 1, the targeted agreement of >80% was exceeded for all three grades and almost perfect agreement between the two raters was obtained. The original DIGEST-FEES study showed comparable values for inter-rater reliability. While the study design does not support causal inferences, it is possible that inclusion of reference image material led to higher level agreement in the study phases. The intra-rater reliability in the present study was evaluated 3 weeks after study phase 1 and was almost perfect and substantial to almost perfect. The reference material, which could be consulted again after the rating break, was considered helpful. This is in line with Curtis et al. [32], who demonstrate for FEES the need for precisely defined criteria for the reliable interpretation of penetration, aspiration, and PR.

Also, the original developers emphasize the importance of training for using DIGEST [33]. For this, a DIGEST/DIGEST-FEES manual and webinar training was developed to accompany the flowsheet to improve ease of use and rater reliability. A multi-site study that evaluated the implementation of DIGEST training in the context of videofluoroscopy showed that the use of the DIGEST training manual significantly improved inter-rater reliability and strengthened the rater confidence in the DIGEST method, particularly when assigning DIGEST efficiency grade [34]. The results of the present study support the premise that training may improve the reliable use of measures such as DIGEST-FEES. However, the specific effect of training on DIGEST-FEES has not been directly tested in the present study and remains to be established in future research.

Criterion Validity

Recognizing that the criterion validity estimates approximated with those of the original DIGEST-FEES [13] further strengthens the evidence for the DIGEST-FEES-G. Examining the correlation with the criterion-referenced scales, DIGEST-FEES-G safety correlated significantly with the Yale Residue Scale in both the Valleculae and the Pyriform sinus, indicating impaired airway protection with a greater amount of PR. As in the original study, this is consistent with previous studies showing that penetration and aspiration often occurs with the presence of PR in patients with HNC [35]. As expected, DIGEST-FEES-G efficiency showed a strong correlation with the Yale Residue Scale Valleculae and slightly less with the Yale Residue Scale Pyriform sinus, both of which measure the occurrence of PR.

Transcultural Translation

In the present work, a standardized translation procedure was used to evaluate the accuracy and comprehensibility of the German translation on several levels. In the translation process, questions arose regarding linguistics, the interpretation of English and German technical terms, and questions regarding instructions in the DIGEST flowsheet, which were clarified in repeated rounds of discussions with the original developers and the Expert Committee. This led to small adjustments in the German version of DIGEST-FEES-G, which served to standardize the terms in the flowsheet and make it easier to understand. The following representative examples illustrate linguistic adaptations in DIGEST-FEES-G that aim to improve comprehensibility and consistency for German-speaking clinicians. Initially, the original phrase “Do not rate for swallows after strategies are applied” was translated into German: “Keine Bewertung von Schlucken, bei denen Strategien angewandt wurden”. However, cognitive interviews revealed that this wording caused ambiguity regarding which swallow should be rated. It was therefore rephrased in German: “Bewertet werden die Bolusschlucke jeweils nur bis zu dem Zeitpunkt, bis eine Strategie eingesetzt wird” (English: “The bolus swallows are only rated until a strategy is applied”), which more clearly reflects the intended application while preserving the original concept. Second, the abbreviated descriptors of the Penetration-Aspiration Scale (PAS) used in the original DIGEST-FEES flowsheet were omitted in the German version. Instead, reference was made to the validated German PAS [21], and it was included in its complete form to ensure linguistic and conceptual consistency. In the description of the pattern of residue, the German term: “Konsistenz” (English: “consistency”) was used consistently throughout to ensure that the same term was always used. Thus, the importance of a linguistically accurate translation as well as the significance of forward and blind backward translations and cognitive interviews was demonstrated in this study.

Limitations and Strengths

First of all, the limitations of the original study also apply to the present work. Since a retrospective data analysis was done, no test-retest measurements could be implemented, which represents an important yet untested psychometric parameter. In addition, the assessments were only carried out with two raters as compared to three raters in the original study, resulting in less strict assessment of inter- and intra-rater reliability. Additionally, testing a German dataset would be important to establish the feasibility of performing DIGEST-compatible FEES examinations in routine clinical practice.

The present study has several strengths. The translation process followed a standardized procedure in collaboration with the developers of the original English version of the DIGEST and DIGEST-FEES. This leads to a review of the accuracy and comprehensibility of the German translation on several levels. Moreover, the validity and reliability of the DIGEST-FEES-G were examined using the original dataset of 100 FEES recordings and assessed in relation to three established reference measures. Thus, the present results underscore the psychometric robustness of DIGEST-FEES-G. Further, the two raters came from different professional backgrounds (physician and therapist), showing the robustness of the DIGEST framework. At the same time, this may have emphasized the relevance of structured training, demonstrating that the standardized DIGEST procedure can support consistent ratings across disciplines. Future research may benefit from conducting additional rating studies, for example, by examining multi-site rating groups that differ in professional background and level of FEES experience.

Conclusion

The present work delivers both the transcultural translation of DIGEST-FEES into the German version DIGEST-FEES-G and a psychometric evaluation using the endoscopic videos of the original study. For the translation, a detailed translation process was carried out according to a standardized and internationally recognized procedure. The present translation DIGEST-FEES-G has proven to be reliable (after training) and valid for the criteria examined (inter- and intra-rater reliability and criterion validity). The results of the original study could be replicated, which increases the evidence base for DIGEST-FEES analysis method. With DIGEST-FEES-G, this work provides an evidence-based analysis method for the German-speaking countries for the classification of the severity of pharyngeal dysphagia with HNC, thereby offering an internationally comparable practice tool for dysphagia diagnostics for German-speaking practitioners.
