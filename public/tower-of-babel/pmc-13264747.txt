Prognostic impact of extensive nodal involvement in small-sized (T1) breast cancer: a population-based and propensity score-matched analysis of the SEER database
Authors: Bingbin Dong, Zhaocai Yin, Zheng Wang, Yabing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prognostic significance of extensive nodal involvement (N2–3) in patients with small-sized (T1) breast cancer remains to be fully elucidated. This study aimed to compare the clinicopathological characteristics and survival outcomes between T1N0–1M0 and T1N2–3M0 breast cancer.MethodsPatients diagnosed with T1 breast cancer between 2010 and 2012 were identified from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. Clinicopathological features were compared using Chi-squared tests and t-tests. Overall survival (OS) and breast cancer-specific survival (BCSS) were assessed using Kaplan-Meier analysis and Cox regression. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to minimize confounding effects. Competing risk analysis using the Fine-Gray model was also conducted to compare the cumulative incidence of breast cancer-specific death, accounting for death from other causes as a competing event.ResultsOf 60,318 eligible patients with T1M0 breast cancer, 2.9% (n=1,772) had T1N2–3M0 disease. Compared to the T1N0–1M0 cohort, these patients were younger, had higher T substages, less frequent hormone receptor positivity, higher human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) positivity and tumor grade, and received more aggressive surgical and systemic therapy (all P<0.001). Before matching, T1N2–3M0 status was associated with significantly worse 5-year OS [86.9% vs. 96.4%; hazard ratio (HR) =3.50] and BCSS (89.7% vs. 98.7%; HR =7.79). In multivariable analysis, advanced nodal status remained the strongest independent risk factor for both OS [adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) =2.83] and BCSS (aHR =3.86). After 1:1 PSM (n=3,512), survival disparities persisted, though attenuated (OS: HR =2.23; BCSS: HR =2.82). Competing risk analysis accounting for death from other causes confirmed a significantly higher cumulative incidence of breast cancer-specific death in the T1N2–3M0 group (Gray’s test: χ2 =56.47, P<0.001). At 60 months, the cumulative incidence was 11.95% [95% confidence interval (CI): 10.29–13.73%] in the T1N2–3M0 group vs. 4.09% (95% CI: 3.14–5.22%) in the T1N0–1M0 group, with no significant difference in death from other causes (P=0.40). In the matched multivariable model, T1N2–3M0 status retained its strong independent prognostic value for OS (aHR =2.36) and BCSS (aHR =2.92), along with higher tumor grade and negative hormone receptor status.ConclusionsEven within T1 breast cancer, extensive nodal involvement (N2–3) defines a distinct subgroup with more aggressive tumor biology and significantly poorer survival, independent of other clinicopathological factors. This underscores the critical need for comprehensive nodal staging and potentially more intensive treatment strategies for T1N2–3 patients.

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study identifies that among T1 breast cancer patients, those with extensive nodal involvement (N2–3, ≥4 positive nodes) represent a distinct high-risk subgroup (2.9% of T1M0 cases) with significantly worse survival than T1N0–1 patients. After propensity score matching (PSM), T1N2–3 remained an independent poor prognostic factor for overall survival [hazard ratio (HR) =2.36] and breast cancer-specific survival (HR =2.92).

What is known and what is new?

• It is well-established that axillary lymph node status is a cornerstone prognostic factor in breast cancer, and extensive nodal involvement (N2–3) is associated with poor outcomes across all tumor sizes. However, the specific prognostic impact of heavy nodal burden within the uniformly small-sized (T1) tumor category has been poorly characterized.

• This manuscript provides novel quantification of the independent prognostic value of extensive nodal involvement specifically in T1 breast cancer using a large population-based cohort with PSM. It demonstrates that T1N2–3 tumors exhibit distinct aggressive biology (higher grade, more human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-positive, more hormone receptor-negative) and that nodal burden itself confers substantial incremental risk beyond these biological features.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• This study challenges complacency toward node-positive T1 breast cancers in the de-escalation era. T1N2–3 patients have prognosis substantially worse than T1N0–1 patients, arguing against applying low nodal burden trial de-escalation principles to this subgroup. Clinical practice should maintain comprehensive axillary staging and aggressive multimodal therapy for T1N2–3 patients.

Introduction

The widespread adoption of screening mammography and advanced imaging techniques has led to a steady increase in the diagnosis of early-stage, small-sized (T1) breast carcinomas, which now constitute a substantial proportion of newly diagnosed cases (1,2). While T1 tumors are generally associated with a favorable prognosis, their clinicopathological heterogeneity and corresponding outcomes are not fully uniform (3,4). This paradox is exemplified by tumors that, despite their small size, exhibit biologically aggressive phenotypes or present with synchronous axillary lymph node metastasis (5,6). Indeed, axillary lymph node status is not merely a dichotomous variable but a spectrum of disease burden, and it remains the cornerstone of staging and one of the most critical determinants for adjuvant therapeutic strategy, outweighing even primary tumor size in prognostic models like the Nottingham Prognostic Index (7,8). The management of these node-positive T1 tumors thus sits at a complex intersection between the contemporary trend toward treatment de-escalation for early-stage disease and the historical imperative for aggressive therapy in node-positive patients.

Notably, the clinical significance of extensive nodal involvement (N2–3) in the specific context of T1 primary tumors remains poorly defined and represents a notable clinical paradox. Contemporary de-escalation trials in axillary management, such as American College of Surgeons Oncology Group (ACOSOG) Z0011, primarily focused on patients with limited nodal disease (N0–1), thereby leaving the optimal management and prognostic understanding of T1 tumors with heavier nodal burden (N2–3) comparatively understudied (9,10). This specific subgroup is often excluded from early-stage trial paradigms or aggregated within broader locally advanced cohorts, obscuring its unique clinical profile.

Therefore, to address this gap, the present study utilizes a large population-based cohort from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. We aim to perform a detailed comparison of clinicopathological characteristics and survival outcomes between T1N0–1M0 and T1N2–3M0 breast cancer patients. Crucially, by employing a propensity score matching (PSM) analysis, we seek to isolate and evaluate the independent prognostic impact of nodal disease burden itself within the T1 category, controlling for other key confounding factors. Our goal is to provide evidence that may inform more precise risk stratification and tailored treatment strategies for this distinct patient population. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://gs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gs-2026-1-0063/rc).

Methods

Data source and patient selection

This population-based retrospective cohort study utilized data from the SEER database. Data were accessed and extracted using SEER*Stat software (version 8.3.5) after obtaining a SEER Research Data Agreement. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. As the SEER database contains de-identified, publicly available information, this study was exempt from institutional review board approval, and the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Patients diagnosed with primary breast cancer between 2010 and 2012 were initially considered. The following inclusion criteria were applied: age at diagnosis between 18 and 80 years; breast cancer as the first and only primary malignant tumor; unilateral; invasive carcinoma; tumor stage T1 according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system; known status of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2); underwent definitive surgical treatment. The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with distant metastasis (M1) at diagnosis; patients with incomplete clinical or demographic information; unknown survival time or survival time equal and shorter than 1-month. The final study cohort was stratified into two groups based on nodal status: T1N0–1M0 (0–3 positive regional lymph nodes) and T1N2–3M0 (≥4 positive regional lymph nodes).

Study variables and endpoints

Clinicopathological variables extracted included age at diagnosis, T substage, nodal status, race, gender, tumor grade, laterality, type of breast surgery, receipt of radiation therapy and chemotherapy, ER status, PR status, HER2 status, number of lymph nodes removed, histologic type, and marital status. Definitions of “Others” and “Unknown” categories for all variables are provided in Table S1.

The primary endpoints were overall survival (OS) and breast cancer-specific survival (BCSS). OS was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause. BCSS was defined as the time from diagnosis to death attributable to breast cancer.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics between the T1N0–1M0 and T1N2–3M0 groups were compared using Pearson’s Chi-squared test for categorical variables and the Student’s t-test for continuous variables.

To minimize selection bias and control for potential confounding factors, PSM was employed. The propensity score, estimating the probability of being in the T1N2–3M0 group, was calculated using a multivariable logistic regression model that included the following covariates: age, T substage, race, gender, tumor grade, laterality, type of breast surgery, receipt of radiation therapy and chemotherapy, ER status, PR status, HER2 status, number of lymph nodes removed, histologic type, and marital status. A 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching algorithm without replacement was used, with a caliper width set to 0.02 of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. Balance between the matched groups was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with a value <0.1 indicating good balance.

In the overall cohort and the matched cohort, survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. The independent prognostic impact of nodal burden (T1N2–3M0 vs. T1N0–1M0) was evaluated using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). In the overall cohort analysis, the Cox model adjusted for age, grade, and treatment modalities (surgery, radiation, chemotherapy). For the matched cohort analysis, a conditional Cox regression model accounting for the matched pairs was used. Considering that death from other causes may compete with breast cancer-specific death, a competing risk analysis was performed using the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model to compare the cumulative incidence of breast cancer-specific death between the T1N0–1M0 and T1N2–3M0 groups. Death from other causes was treated as a competing event. The cumulative incidence functions were estimated and compared using Gray’s test. This analysis was conducted in the propensity score-matched cohort. All tests were two-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24.0 and R software version 4.3.3, along with Zstats 1.0 (www.zstats.net).

Compared to the T1N0–1M0 cohort, patients with T1N2–3M0 disease exhibited distinct clinicopathological profiles and treatment patterns. Demographically, the T1N2–3M0 cohort was characterized by a significantly higher proportion of male patients (1.3% vs. 0.5%, P<0.001) and a significantly younger mean patient age (56.04 vs. 59.30 years, P<0.001) than the T1N0–1M0 cohort. Regarding tumor characteristics, the T1N2–3M0 cohort was characterized by more advanced T substages (P<0.001), manifested as a significantly higher prevalence of the T1c stage (83.3% vs. 58.2%). Furthermore, T1N2–3M0 tumors were characterized by lower rates of ER positivity (81.2% vs. 87.4%, P<0.001) and PR positivity (68.7% vs. 78.0%, P<0.001), a higher rate of HER2 positivity (19.0% vs. 11.7%, P<0.001), and a higher histologic grade, with 42.4% classified as poorly differentiated or undifferentiated compared to 22.7% in the T1N0–1M0 group (P<0.001). In terms of treatment, the T1N2–3M0 group underwent breast-conserving surgery less frequently (42.2% vs. 69.8%, P<0.001) but received adjuvant chemotherapy (86.3% vs. 29.7%, P<0.001) and radiation therapy (66.1% vs. 59.4%, P<0.001). More often, along with more extensive axillary surgery, as evidenced by a higher percentage of patients having ≥10 lymph nodes removed (82.9% vs. 11.3%, P<0.001).

Survival outcomes and prognostic factors of T1N0–1M0 vs. T1N2–3M0 breast cancer

Kaplan-Meier analyses demonstrated significantly worse 5-year survival in the T1N2–3M0 group compared to the T1N0–1M0 group. For OS, the rates were 86.9% vs. 96.4% (log-rank P<0.001; HR =3.502, 95% CI: 3.059–4.009) (Figure 2A). For BCSS, the rates were 89.7% vs. 98.7% (log-rank P<0.001; HR =7.788, 95% CI: 6.635–9.143) (Figure 2B).

Comparison of (A) OS and (B) BCSS between T1N0–1M0 and T1N2–3M0 patients. BCSS, breast cancer-specific survival; CI, confidence interval; HR, hazard ratio; OS, overall survival.

Prognostic factors for OS in patients with T1M0 breast cancer were analyzed using Cox regression (Table 2). In the multivariable model, advanced nodal status (T1N2–3M0 vs. T1N0–1M0) was the strongest independent risk factor for mortality (aHR =2.830, 95% CI: 2.414–3.318, P<0.001). Other factors independently associated with increased all-cause mortality risk included higher T substage (T1c vs. T1a: aHR =1.732), Black race (vs. White: aHR =1.355), higher tumor grade (poorly differentiated/undifferentiated vs. well differentiated: aHR =1.607), unmarried status (vs. married: aHR =1.496), and older age (per year increase: aHR =1.049, all P<0.001). Significant protective factors for OS included radiation therapy (aHR =0.594) and positive ER (aHR =0.602) or PR (aHR =0.796) status.

We further performed Cox regression analysis for BCSS (Table 3). For BCSS, the independent prognostic impact of advanced nodal status was markedly stronger than for OS (aHR =3.863, 95% CI: 3.176–4.700, P<0.001). The risk associated with tumor grade was substantially amplified, with poorly differentiated/undifferentiated tumors carrying a greater than fourfold increased risk of cancer-specific death compared to well-differentiated tumors (aHR =4.236, 95% CI: 3.203–5.602, P<0.001). Higher T substage (T1c) and Black race remained significant risk factors, while female gender, radiation therapy, and positive hormone receptor status (ER/PR) were significant protective factors. Notably, age at diagnosis was not an independent prognostic factor for BCSS in either univariable or multivariable analyses (P>0.05). Additionally, chemotherapy, which showed a significant association in univariable analysis, did not retain independent significance in the multivariable model for BCSS (P=0.09).

Survival outcomes and prognostic factors of T1N0–1M0 vs. T1N2–3M0 breast cancer after PSM

Following PSM, Kaplan-Meier analyses continued to demonstrate significantly worse survival outcomes in the T1N2–3M0 group compared to the T1N0–1M0 group. For OS, the HR was 2.234 (95% CI: 1.773–2.815, log-rank P<0.001) (Figure 3A). For BCSS, the HR was 2.821 (95% CI: 2.128–3.739, log-rank P<0.001) (Figure 3B).

Comparison of (A) OS and (B) BCSS between T1N0–1M0 and T1N2–3M0 patients after propensity score matching. BCSS, breast cancer-specific survival; CI, confidence interval; HR, hazard ratio; OS, overall survival.

To account for competing risks from other causes of death, a competing risk analysis using the Fine-Gray model was performed. Consistent with the Kaplan-Meier findings, the T1N2–3M0 group had a significantly higher cumulative incidence of breast cancer-specific death compared to the T1N0–1M0 group (Gray’s test: χ2 =56.47, P<0.001). At 60 months, the cumulative incidence of breast cancer death was 11.95% (95% CI: 10.29–13.73%) in the T1N2–3M0 group vs. 4.09% (95% CI: 3.14–5.22%) in the T1N0–1M0 group. No significant difference was observed for death from other causes between the two groups (P=0.40). The cumulative incidence curves for breast cancer-specific death and death from other causes are shown in Figure S2.

Prognostic factors for OS in the propensity score-matched cohort were analyzed using Cox regression (Table 5). In the multivariable model, advanced nodal status (T1N2–3M0 vs. T1N0–1M0) remained the strongest independent risk factor for mortality, with an aHR of 2.359 (95% CI: 1.871–2.975, P<0.001). Other factors independently associated with increased all-cause mortality risk included Black race (vs. White: aHR =1.792, P<0.001), higher tumor grade (poorly differentiated/undifferentiated vs. well differentiated: aHR =2.765, P<0.001), unmarried status (vs. married: aHR =1.451, P=0.002), and older age (per year increase: aHR =1.010, P=0.04). Significant protective factors for OS included chemotherapy (aHR =0.546, P<0.001) and positive ER (aHR =0.614, P=0.002) or PR (aHR =0.653, P=0.005) status. Notably, T substage, gender, laterality, radiation therapy, and HER2 status did not retain independent prognostic significance in the multivariable model for OS after matching.

Discussion

Generally, small-sized breast carcinomas are more frequently associated with limited nodal involvement; however, a small subset presents with extensive lymph node metastases, highlighting that tumor size does not consistently predict the extent of lymphatic or systemic dissemination (11-13). This large, population-based analysis of the SEER database provides critical insights into the paradoxical yet clinically significant entity of small-sized (T1) breast carcinomas presenting with extensive nodal involvement (N2–3). Our study confirms that this specific subgroup, though representing only 2.9% of T1M0 patients, harbors a distinct and aggressive clinicopathological profile and is associated with a substantially poorer prognosis compared to T1 tumors with limited or no nodal disease, even after rigorous adjustment for confounding factors.

The profound survival disparity observed is the central finding of our study. Prior to matching, patients with T1N2–3M0 disease faced a more than 3.5-fold increased risk of all-cause mortality and a nearly 8-fold increased risk of breast cancer-specific death. More importantly, after PSM balanced key demographic, tumor biologic, and treatment covariates, extensive nodal involvement retained its powerful independent prognostic value, with aHRs of 2.4 for OS and 2.9 for BCSS. This underscores that the heavy nodal burden itself, not merely associated aggressive tumor features, confers a major adverse impact on survival within the T1 category. This finding aligns with the foundational principle that nodal status is a paramount prognostic factor (14,15), but it quantifies its potent effect specifically in the context of small primary tumors, a scenario sometimes perceived as uniformly low-risk (16,17). There is a growing trend toward treatment de-escalation in breast cancer management, particularly for patients presenting with early-stage or small-volume disease (18-20). Our findings align with this trend by confirming that most T1 breast cancers exhibit a favorable prognosis. However, we identified the T1N2–3M0 subgroup as an exception, characterized by substantially elevated risk and inferior clinical outcomes. This observation calls into question the appropriateness of de-escalation strategies for this specific cohort. Consequently, our results challenge emerging complacency in the management of node-positive T1 breast cancers and underscore T1N2–3 as a distinct, high-risk phenotype that warrants careful consideration within the broader movement toward therapeutic de-escalation.

The distinct clinicopathological signature of T1N2–3 tumors offers biological plausibility for their aggressive behavior. These tumors were characterized by a higher prevalence of adverse features: they were more frequently HER2-positive, hormone receptor-negative, and of high histological grade. This aligns with known biology, as aggressive molecular subtypes (e.g., HER2-enriched and triple-negative) have a higher propensity for early dissemination (21,22). The significant association with larger T1 substage (T1c) further suggests a correlation between primary tumor size within the T1 spectrum and metastatic potential. Importantly, our matched analysis demonstrates that even when accounting for these factors—effectively comparing tumors of similar size, grade, and receptor status—the N2–3 status itself adds substantial incremental risk. This reinforces the role of nodal burden as an integrator of both intrinsic tumor biology and host-tumor interaction, representing a critical step in the metastatic cascade. The markedly stronger HR for BCSS compared to OS across all analyses further indicates that the excess mortality in this group is predominantly driven by breast cancer progression rather than competing risks.

The observation that patients with extensive nodal involvement (N2–3) had substantially higher rates of high-grade tumors and hormone receptor-negative subtypes raises the possibility that intrinsic tumor biology may be a key determinant of both nodal burden and prognosis. In the current era, genomic assays such as Oncotype DX provide important prognostic information that complements traditional clinicopathologic factors. However, due to the inherent limitations of the SEER database, we were unable to incorporate these molecular markers into our analysis. Future research integrating genomic profiling with detailed nodal staging may help clarify whether nodal burden provides independent prognostic information beyond tumor biology or whether it primarily serves as a surrogate marker of underlying biological aggressiveness.

From a clinical management perspective, our findings may have several implications. First, they reinforce the necessity of comprehensive axillary staging, including adequate lymph node retrieval, in high-risk patients with T1 tumors. Second, they call for caution against directly extrapolating findings from axillary de-escalation trials, like ACOSOG Z0011 and AMAROS (9,23), to patients with ≥4 positive nodes. Our data support the current standard of care for this group, which includes mandatory consideration of systemic chemotherapy and comprehensive regional nodal irradiation, as reflected in the high rates of these therapies in the N2–3 cohort. The loss of chemotherapy’s independent protective effect in the BCSS multivariable model of the overall cohort likely reflects confounding by indication—it was administered precisely to those at highest risk. Its significant association with improved OS in the matched analysis suggests a beneficial effect. The persistent independent prognostic weight of nodal burden after matching argues for its inclusion as a key variable in prognostic tools and clinical trial stratification for early breast cancer (24,25).

Limitations

Our conclusions must be interpreted within the context of the study’s limitations, which are inherent to its retrospective, registry-based design. The SEER database lacks detailed information on specific chemotherapy regimens, endocrine therapy duration, targeted therapies, and treatment compliance, which are crucial modifiers of outcomes. Furthermore, data on recurrence-free survival are not available, limiting our analysis to overall and cancer-specific survival endpoints. While PSM effectively balanced observed confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured variables (e.g., lymphovascular invasion, detailed surgical margin status, patient comorbidities, and performance status) may persist. The classification of molecular subtype was also based solely on ER, PR, and HER2 status without multigene assay data (e.g., Oncotype DX recurrence score) or Ki-67 proliferation index, which could further refine prognostic assessment within this group. Finally, the study period (2010–2012) precedes the widespread use of neoadjuvant therapy for high-risk early breast cancer; thus, modern treatment paradigms may influence contemporary outcomes for this patient population.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this population-based study identifies T1 breast cancer with extensive nodal involvement (N2–3) as a unique and high-risk entity, characterized by aggressive tumor biology and significantly inferior survival outcomes compared to T1N0–1 disease. The independent prognostic impact of heavy nodal burden persists even after accounting for other clinicopathological factors, underscoring its critical role in risk stratification. These findings caution against the minimalistic management of node-positive T1 breast cancer and emphasize the need for tailored, multidisciplinary treatment strategies for the T1N2–3 subgroup. Future research should focus on elucidating the molecular drivers of this phenotype and evaluating optimal therapeutic approaches within contemporary treatment frameworks.
