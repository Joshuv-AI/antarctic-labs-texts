Towards joint graph learning and sampling set selection from data
Shashank N. Sridhara, Eduardo Pavez, Antonio Ortega

Abstract
We explore the problem of sampling graph signals in scenarios where the graph structure is not predefined and must be inferred from data. In this scenario, existing approaches rely on a two-step process, where a graph is learned first, followed by sampling. More generally, graph learning and graph signal sampling have been studied as two independent problems in the literature. This work provides a foundational step towards jointly optimizing the graph structure and sampling set. Our main contribution, Vertex Importance Sampling (VIS), is to show that the sampling set can be effectively determined from the vertex importance (node weights) obtained from graph learning. We further propose Vertex Importance Sampling with Repulsion (VISR), a greedy algorithm where spatially-separated “important” nodes are selected to ensure better reconstruction. Empirical results on simulated data show that sampling using VIS and VISR leads to competitive reconstruction performance and lower complexity than the conventional two-step approach of graph learning followed by graph sampling.

Introduction

Graph signal processing (GSP) provides a powerful framework for analysis, denoising, sampling, and interpolation of signals defined on graphs
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.
Sampling graph signals

is a fundamental problem in GSP
[
2
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
, where the goal is selecting a subset of graph nodes to reconstruct a smooth signal from the signal samples.

In some applications, the graph structure is not predefined, although the data (signal values on vertices) is available
[
7
,
8
]
. In such cases, the graph structure must first be inferred from the data before graph signal sampling. Learning graph structure from data is a well-studied topic in the GSP literature
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
. However, when the objective is to determine a sampling set without a predefined graph, the conventional two-step process—first learning the graph and then performing sampling—can become computationally expensive. Our work specifically focuses on obtaining a sampling set in scenarios where graph structure is not readily available.

Graph learning from data can be formulated as a Maximum A-Posteriori (MAP) parameter estimation of Gaussian-Markov Random fields (GMRF), whose inverse covariance is graph Laplacian matrix
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
. Specifically, learning a Diagonally Dominant Graph Laplacian (DDGL) allows for the simultaneous optimization of both edge weights and
vertex importance
,
the latter represented by a diagonal matrix added to the Combinatorial Graph Laplacian (CGL)
[
8
,
10
]
.
Further, the learned vertex importance values can be interpreted as an inner-product matrix to define a generalized
Graph Fourier Transform
(GFT)
[
11
,
10
]
.

Given a graph and its corresponding graph variation operator, e.g., graph Laplacian, graph signal sampling can be formulated as Minimum Mean Squared error Estimation (MMSE) of GMRF signals from the samples
[
12
]
. Several graph sampling algorithms have been developed within this framework
[
6
,
12
,
13
]
.

In this work, we show that graph learning from data can provide insights for determining the sampling set. In particular, the common assumption in both the formulations, i.e., graph signals are realizations of independent identically distributed (i.i.d.) zero-mean multivariate Gaussian distributions with inverse covariance equal to the graph Laplacian, can be leveraged for simultaneous optimization of graph structure (edge set and edge weights) and the sampling set.
To achieve this, we formulate sampling set selection using the D-optimal objective function.
We identify similarities between the D-optimal sampling and graph learning objectives. We then show that the sampling set can be directly obtained from the
vertex importances
learned from finding the optimal diagonally dominant graph Laplacian
[
10
]
. However, the sampling set based solely on vertex importance could result in spatially close samples, leading to subpar reconstruction accuracy at higher sampling rates. To address this limitation, we propose a low-complexity sampling algorithm that selects “important” nodes that are spatially separated from each other.

Our contributions are: i) Two algorithms,
Vertex Importance sampling (VIS)
and
Vertex Importance Sampling with Repulsion (VISR)
, providing approximate solutions to the D-optimal sampling objective using learned vertex importance,
ii) a greedy algorithm for the D-optimal sampling similar to that of
[
14
, Algorithm 1]
to compare the sampling sets from VIS and VISR, and iii) an empirical evaluation showing that using the DDGL for reconstruction
leads to better signal reconstruction than using only the CGL.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. GSP preliminaries and notations are given in
Section 2
. Sampling formulation and a greedy solution to the D-optimal objective are given in
Section 3
. We propose VIS and VISR algorithms in
Section 4
. Experimental results and conclusions are in
Section 5
and
Section 6
, respectively.

Notations and Background

2.1
Graph signal processing

Throughout the paper, we represent sets using calligraphic uppercase,
{\cal S}
,
matrices by bold uppercase,
{\bf X}
, vectors by bold lowercase,
{\bf x}
, and scalars by plain lowercase,
x
.
A graph
{\cal G}=({\cal V},{\cal E})
consists of a set of vertices
{\cal V}
of size
N
and an edge set
{\cal E}\subseteq{\cal V}\times{\cal V}
. A graph signal is a vector
{\bf f}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, whose
i
-th entry corresponds to the signal at the
i
-th node of the graph. We define the edge weight between vertices
i
and
j
as
w_{ij}>0
. In this work, we consider only undirected graphs, so we have
w_{ij}=w_{ji}
, and we have a symmetric adjacency matrix,
{\bf A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, whose
(i,j)
-th entry is
w_{ij}
, if
(i,j)\in{\cal E}
and
0
otherwise. The degree of a vertex is denoted by
d_{i}=\sum_{(i,j)\in{\cal E}}w_{ij}
, and the diagonal degree matrix is defined as
{\bf D}=\mathrm{diag}(d_{1},d_{2}\cdots d_{N})
. The combinatorial graph Laplacian,
{\bf L}:={\bf D}-{\bf A}
is a symmetric, positive semi-definite matrix
[
1
]
.

The irregularity-aware graph Fourier transform (GFT) introduced in
[
11
]
is based on a positive semi-definite graph variation operator
{\bf M}\succeq 0
, which measures graph signal smoothness, and a Hilbert space with inner product defined as
\langle{\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle_{{\bf Q}}={\bf y}^{T}{\bf Q}{\bf x}
, with induced Q-norm
\lVert{\bf x}\rVert_{{\bf Q}}^{2}=\langle{\bf x},{\bf x}\rangle_{{\bf Q}}
, where
{\bf Q}\succ 0
. The generalized GFT for the
{\bf Q}
-inner product is the
({\bf M},{\bf Q})-
GFT.
The graph-Fourier basis
{\bf U}=[{\bf u}_{1},{\bf u}_{2},\cdots{\bf u}_{N}]
is now orthogonal with respect to the
{\bf Q}
-inner product, i.e.,
{\bf U}^{H}{\bf Q}{\bf U}=\mathbf{I}
. The eigenvalues corresponding to
({\bf M},{\bf Q})-
GFT,
0=\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2},\cdots,\leq\lambda_{N}
represent the graph frequencies
[
11
]
.

2.2
Graph and vertex importance learning

Assuming graph signals
\{{\bf f}^{(k)}\}_{k}
are realizations of a zero-mean, i.i.d. multivariate Gaussian distribution, the goal of graph learning is to model the signals with a graph whose combinatorial graph Laplacian is such that the data satisfies graph Wide Sense Stationarity (gWSS) with gPSD
\gamma(0)=0
and
\gamma(\lambda)=1/\lambda
, when
\lambda>0

[
9
,
10
]
. Using the
({\bf L},\mathbf{I})-
GFT, this translates into a covariance matrix
\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}={\bf L}^{\dagger}
, where
{\bf L}^{\dagger}
denotes the pseudo-inverse of
{\bf L}
. Under this setting, the graph learning problem is traditionally formulated as a maximum likelihood estimation, minimizing the cost function
[
9
]
:

J({\bf L})=-\mathrm{logdet}({\bf L}+\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T})+\mathrm{tr}({\bf L}{\bf S}),

(1)

where
{\bf S}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{k}{\bf f}^{(k)}{\bf f}^{(k)\top}
is the empirical covariance matrix. The cost function in (
1
) can be efficiently optimized using the coordinate minimization approach proposed in
[
9
]
.

The joint graph and vertex importance learning proposed in
[
10
]
requires solving the following optimization:

J({\bf L},{\bf Q})=-\mathrm{logdet}({\bf L}+{\bf Q})+\mathrm{tr}(({\bf L}+{\bf Q}){\bf S}),

(2)

where
{\bf Q}
is a diagonal matrix with
{\bf Q}_{ii}>0
representing vertex importance.
The cost function (
2
) can be solved using coordinate minimization by updating edges weights and
vertex importances
iteratively
[
10
]
.
Learning a diagonally dominant graph Laplacian (DDGL) model can be viewed as learning a combinatorial graph Laplacian
{\bf L}
and an inner-product matrix
{\bf Q}
.
Through joint optimization, we learn the graph such that the data is gWSS with gPSD
\gamma(\lambda)=(1+\lambda)^{-1}
when using
({\bf L},{\bf Q})-
GFT. The covariance matrix with respect to
({\bf L},{\bf Q})-
GFT is given by
\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}=[{\bf L}+{\bf Q}]^{-1}

[
10
, Theorem 1]
.

3
Graph Signal Sampling Formulation

Our graph signal sampling formulation is based on the same signal model used for graph learning, i.e.,
{\bf f}\sim{\cal N}({\bf 0},\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}=\hbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}^{\dagger})
, where
\Omega
is a positive semi-definite graph operator (e.g.
{\bf L}
or
{\bf L}+{\bf Q}
). We define the sampling process as
[
2
,
5
,
4
]

{\bf y}_{{\cal S}}={\bf f}_{{\cal S}}+{\bf n}={\bf I}_{{\cal S}}^{\top}{\bf f}+{\bf n},

where
{\bf n}\in\mathbb{R}^{\lvert{\cal S}\rvert}
is the noise introduced during sampling and
{\bf I}_{{\cal S}}

is
N\times\lvert{\cal S}\rvert
sampling matrix and
{\bf H}={\bf I}_{{\cal S}}{\bf I}_{{\cal S}}^{\top}
is a diagonal matrix:

{\bf H}_{ii}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }i\in{\cal S}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(3)

We assume the signal is reconstructed using the graph Laplacian regularization (GLR) approach, given by
[
6
,
13
]
:

\hat{{\bf f}}=\argmin_{{\bf f}}\lVert{\bf I}_{{\cal S}}^{\top}{\bf f}-{\bf y}_{{\cal S}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\mu{\bf f}^{\top}\hbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}{\bf f},

(4)

whose closed-form solution is
[
6
]
:

\hat{{\bf f}}=({\bf H}+\mu\hbox{\boldmath$\Omega$})^{-1}{\bf I}_{{\cal S}}{\bf y}_{{\cal S}}.

(5)

Next, we define an objective function for sampling.

3.1
Sampling objective

From (
5
),
\hat{{\bf f}}
is the solution system of linear equations where
{\bf B}:=({\bf H}+\mu\hbox{\boldmath$\Omega$})
is the coefficient matrix. Since
{\bf B}
is a positive-definite matrix for any given positive semi-definite graph operator
\Omega

[
6
]
, the inverse of
{\bf B}
always exists. Assuming that
{\bf n}\sim({\bf 0},{\bf I})
, we can write the error vector and error covariance matrix corresponding to the reconstruction in (
5
) as:

\displaystyle{\bf e}=(\hat{{\bf f}}-{\bf f})=-\mu{\bf B}^{-1}\hbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}{\bf f}+{\bf B}^{-1}{\bf I}_{{\cal S}}{\bf n}

(6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\bf e}{\bf e}^{\top}]={\bf B}^{-1}-(\mu-\mu^{2}){\bf B}^{-1}\hbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}{\bf B}^{-1}.

(7)

Theorem 3.1
.

For small
\mu
, the error covariance can be approximated as

\mathbb{E}[{\bf e}{\bf e}^{\top}]={\bf B}^{-1}-(\mu-\mu^{2}){\bf B}^{-1}\hbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}{\bf B}^{-1}\approx({\bf H}+\gamma\hbox{\boldmath$\Omega$})^{-1},

(8)

where
\gamma=2\mu-\mu^{2}

Proof.

The proof relies on the fact that
\lVert{\bf B}^{-1}(\mu-\mu^{2})\hbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}\rVert<1
. The first-order approximation of the Neumann series on matrices (Th. 5.6.12 of
[
15
]
) gives the desired result. Due to space limitations, we omit the detailed proof.
∎

Different sampling objectives can be defined to measure the reconstruction accuracy as a function of error covariance
[
4
]
. In our work, we consider the widely used D-optimality criterion, which minimizes the determinant of the error covariance matrix
[
5
]
. The D-optimal sampling objective based on the approximated error covariance can be formulated as

{\bf H}^{\star}=\argmin_{{\bf H}_{ii}=\{0,1\},\sum_{k=0}^{N}{\bf H}_{ii}=\lvert{\cal S}\rvert}-\mathrm{logdet}[{\bf H}+\gamma\hbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}],

(9)

an NP-hard combinatorial optimization problem
[
5
]
.
In what follows, we first present a greedy algorithm that approximately solves (
9
) and then a sampling algorithm based on the graph learning framework (
Section 4
).

3.2
Greedy sampling set selection

In our greedy approach, the first selected sample is,

i_{1}^{*}=\argmax_{j\in\mathcal{V}}\mathrm{det}[\hbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{j}\hbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{j}^{T}+\gamma\hbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}]=\argmax_{j\in\mathcal{V}}\hbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{j}^{T}\hbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}^{-1}\hbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{j},

where
\hbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the delta function at node
j
, with value
1
at the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
node and
0
elsewhere.
The second equality is due to the Matrix-Determinant lemma
[
15
]
. Thus, the first iteration chooses the node corresponding to the index of the maximum diagonal element of
\hbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}^{-1}
, with
{\cal O}(N^{3})
computations required to obtain
\hbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}^{-1}
.

At the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
iteration,
\lvert S\rvert=k-1
, the next sample is chosen as:

i_{k}^{*}=\argmax_{j\in{\cal S}^{c}}\hbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{j}^{T}{\bf G}_{k-1}^{-1}\hbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{j},

(10)

where
{\bf G}_{k-1}=({\bf I}_{{\cal S}}{\bf I}_{{\cal S}}^{T}+\gamma\hbox{\boldmath$\Omega$})
. Once the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
sample is updated, the inverse of
{\bf G}_{k}
can be computed using the Sherman-Morrison formula:

{\bf G}_{k}^{-1}={\bf G}_{k-1}^{-1}-\frac{{\bf G}_{k-1}^{-1}\hbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{i_{k}^{*}}\hbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{i_{k}^{*}}^{T}{\bf G}_{k-1}^{-1}}{1+\hbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{i_{k}^{*}}^{T}{\bf G}_{k-1}^{-1}\hbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{i_{k}^{*}}^{T}}.

(11)

Therefore, we do not need to compute the matrix inverse and determinant at subsequent iterations. Instead, at each iteration we select a sample
j
from
{\cal S}^{c}
such that
\hbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{j}^{T}{\bf G}_{k}^{-1}\hbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{j}
is maximum, while the inverse of
{\bf G}_{k}
can be updated using (
11
).

However, since we sample every node independently—meaning the selection of a new node does not depend on the previously sampled nodes, the algorithm may select samples that are spatially close. We address this limitation by providing a low-complexity sampling algorithm based on vertex importance that incorporates
repulsion
between selected samples.

4.1
Vertex Importance Sampling with Repulsion

We propose a low-complexity sampling algorithm that introduces repulsion between selected samples using a
p-
hop localized graph filter (
Algorithm 1
).
Following the intuition from
[
13
,
16
,
17
]
, we select the
k
most “informative” nodes that are placed as far as possible from each other. Following
[
13
]
, we achieve this by defining a
p
-hop polynomial graph low-pass filter:

{\bf Z}:=\sum_{l=1}^{p}({\bf D}^{-1}{\bf A})^{l}.

(14)

The
i^{\text{th}}
column of
{\bf Z}(p)
can be seen as a low-pass filtered delta function
\hbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{i}
, localized at node
i
. For any
p
we have:

{\bf z}_{i}^{(p)}:={\bf Z}(p)\hbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{i}=({\bf D}^{-1}{\bf A})\hbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{i}+\cdots+({\bf D}^{-1}{\bf A})^{p}\hbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{i}.

(15)

The parameter
p
defines the localization of the polynomial graph filter
{\bf Z}(p)
, i.e.,
when
p=1
,
{\bf z}_{i}^{(1)}=({\bf D}^{-1}{\bf A})\hbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{i}
which results in non-zero values only for the 1-hop neighbors of node
i
. Similarly,
{\bf z}_{i}^{(2)}=({\bf D}^{-1}{\bf A})\hbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{i}+({\bf D}^{-1}{\bf A})^{2}\hbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{i}
contains non-zero values within 2-hops of
i
. In general,
{\bf z}_{i}^{(p)}
has non-zero values at nodes within the
p
-hop neighborhood of node
i
.

We use the
p
-hop localization of
{\bf z}_{i}^{(p)}
to achieve separation between the selected samples.
Particularly, by calculating the inner-products
\langle{\bf z}_{i}^{(p)},{\bf z}_{j}^{(p)}\rangle,\forall i\neq j
, we prioritize selecting the most important nodes (based on learned vertex importance values
q_{i}
), while also ensuring that the selected samples are spaced apart by at least
p
-hop neighbors.
We adopt a greedy strategy similar to that of
[
16
,
13
]
, whereby, at each iteration, we add to the sampling set the point
x^{*}
that solves:

x^{*}=\argmax_{x\in{\cal R}}(q_{x}-\sum_{y\in{\cal S}}\langle{\bf z}_{y}^{(p)},{\bf z}_{x}^{(p)}\rangle).

(16)

To ensure sufficient repulsion, we set the value of
p
to a small value when the sampling rate (
\alpha=k/N
) is high and to a large value when the sampling rate is low. We use the same rule proposed in
[

to select

as

p=\left\lceil 1/(2\alpha)\right\rceil.

(17)

This flexibility of selecting the parameter

based on sampling rate makes

scalable to large graphs. Contrary to existing sampling algorithms
[

,
6
,
5
]
, the proposed VISR algorithm is computationally efficient because it uses learned vertex importance as an
auxiliary information
and the repulsion is obtained by precomputed inner-products to obtain the sampling set. In
[
4
,
5
]
, the inner products are computed in every iteration, making them computationally expensive.

6
Conclusion and Future work

In this work, we showed that a sampling set can be directly obtained from the learned vertex importance. Compared to the conventional two-step approach of first learning the graph (CGL) and applying expensive sampling algorithms, our proposed approach of learning DDGL and using the vertex importance as auxiliary information can reduce computations and simplify sampling set selection.
We proposed two sampling algorithms— VIS and VISR and results show promising reconstruction accuracy compared to the existing sampling approaches. Building on this work, in future work we aim to combine the D-optimal sampling objective and DDGL learning into a single framework to learn efficiently the sampling set along with the graph. We will also perform a detailed study on the effect of vertex importance in graph signal reconstruction.
