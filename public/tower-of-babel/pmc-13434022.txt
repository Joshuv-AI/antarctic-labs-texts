Double‐Row Suture Anchor Repair of a Tibial Tubercle Avulsion Fracture in an Adolescent Athlete: A Case Report
Authors: Meera Singhal, Clay B. Townsend, Vishal Saxena
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tibial tubercle avulsion fractures are uncommon injuries that typically occur in skeletally immature adolescent males. Traditional fixation methods for these injuries utilize metal hardware, which carries risks including hardware prominence, irritation, need for a removal procedure, and potential interference with future MRIs. In this case report, we describe a surgical technique for the treatment of a tibial tubercle avulsion fracture in a skeletally immature adolescent athlete with a double‐row suture anchor technique. A 15‐year‐old skeletally immature male sustained a left Ogden Type 1B tibial tubercle avulsion fracture while playing basketball. A left knee x‐ray series and MRI confirmed a tibial tubercle avulsion fracture with no other knee injuries. After performing a midline, longitudinal approach centered over the fracture site, two double‐loaded biocomposite suture anchors were placed just proximal to the fracture site. The sutures were passed from deep to superficial through the distal patellar tendon, just proximal to the fracture fragment. The fracture was reduced and provisionally held with a K‐wire. The proximal sutures were tied in horizontal mattress fashion over the tendon. The remaining sutures were incorporated into two distal suture anchors, which were tensioned and placed distal to the fracture site, reducing and compressing the fracture. The patient was placed in a hinged knee brace locked in extension postoperatively. The patient progressed uneventfully through the postoperative course with no complications. At 5 months, the patient had returned to jogging. At 7 months, the patient had returned to sport‐specific training and was cleared for full athletic activity. At final follow up at 9 months postoperatively, the patient had returned to playing tackle football without pain, weakness, or any symptoms. This technique has many potential benefits compared to traditional fixation methods, including lower risk of hardware complications or need for future hardware removal procedures.

1. Introduction

Tibial tubercle avulsion fractures are uncommon injuries that typically occur in skeletally immature adolescent males with a strong contraction of the quadriceps, often during athletic activity [1]. They account for less than 1% of all pediatric physeal injuries [2]. The tibial tubercle is a secondary ossification center that forms under traction from the quadriceps muscle through the patellar tendon. The tibial tubercle physis closes later than the adjacent proximal tibial physis, typically between ages 13 and 16 years in males. This can result in a period of time when the tubercle remains structurally weaker than the adjacent proximal tibia, making it susceptible to injury [3, 4]. These injuries are classified based on the location and severity of the fracture. The Watson‐Jones classification originally described these fractures (Type I–III), which was later modified to the Ogden classification (adding Type IV, V, and Modifier A and B) (Table 1) [5, 6]. Though these injuries are rare, improper management is associated with significant morbidity due to the disruption of the extensor mechanism, loss of function, and related complications [7].

Traditionally, the standard of care for displaced tibial tubercule avulsion fractures is open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) to restore the disrupted extensor mechanism [8]. Several fixation techniques have been described, including cannulated screws, Kirschner wires (K‐wires), and metal suture anchors [5, 9–11]. Although these traditional techniques have demonstrated reliable fracture healing and return to play for athletes generally within 6 months, these previously reported techniques carry some potential limitations. The use of metal implants can result in hardware failure, prominent symptomatic hardware requiring subsequent hardware removal, compromised fixation in smaller comminuted fractures, more complicated future surgeries, and potentially decreased quality of future MRIs if ever needed [12, 13].

Recently, suture anchor‐based constructs have become popular in a variety of orthopedic procedures, including the treatment of tibial tubercle avulsion fractures [14, 15]. Theoretically, the biocompatibility and low‐profile nature of suture anchor‐based techniques could obviate the potential downsides of traditional metal implant fixation for these injuries. In this case report, we describe the surgical technique for the treatment of a tibial tubercle avulsion fracture in a skeletally immature adolescent athlete with a double‐row suture‐bridge technique using bioabsorbable suture anchors. This technique provides a strong, low‐profile construct without use of metal implants.

2. Case Presentation

Informed consent was obtained by the patient and his parents to describe this case and technique. Institutional review board approval was not required for this case description per institutional protocols. A 15‐year‐old skeletally immature male presented to our orthopedic sports medicine clinic 9 days after injuring his left knee during basketball tryouts. The patient described going up for a layup, landing awkwardly on his left lower extremity, and sustaining a left knee injury. He had immediate pain and difficulty bearing weight and ambulating. He was initially evaluated by his primary care physician who obtained plain films and referred him to orthopedic surgery.

On physical examination, the patient had tenderness to palpation over the tibial tubercle. He was unable to achieve or maintain a straight leg raise. His knee was otherwise ligamentously stable, and he was neurovascularly intact. A left knee x‐ray series revealed skeletal immaturity and a displaced Ogden Type 1B tibial tubercle avulsion fracture (Figure 1a). An MRI was subsequently obtained confirming the tibial tubercle avulsion fracture, an intact patellar tendon, and no other concomitant knee injuries (Figure 1b).

Due to the patient′s completely disrupted extensor mechanism, his age, and his desire to return to high level explosive sport, shared decision making was made following lengthy discussion with the patient and their parents to proceed with surgical management. Due to the small size of the tibial tubercle avulsion fragment, it was determined that traditional screw fixation would likely not be feasible. Therefore, it was discussed preoperatively that the patient would likely require a double‐row suture anchor technique for repair. The patient and parents provided written informed consent for the surgery in accordance with institutional and ethical guidelines. The patient underwent surgical repair 11 days following his injury.

3. Surgical Technique

Prior to the procedure, the patient received a femoral nerve block. The patient was positioned supine on a radiolucent operating table. A longitudinal incision was made centered over the tibial tubercle fracture. Sharp dissection was carried to the level of the patellar tendon, and the fracture site was identified, exposed, and debrided. Intraoperatively, it was determined that the avulsion fragment was too small for screw fixation, so a double‐row suture anchor technique was performed.

Two Arthrex (Naples, FL) 5.5 mm biocomposite corkscrew anchors double‐loaded with 1.3 mm SutureTape were placed proximal to the fracture site into the proximal tibia distal to the physis (one medial and one lateral, Figure 2). The physis was identified on both AP and lateral fluoroscopic images. A metal punch was used to create a pilot hole for the suture anchor, and this was visualized under fluoroscopy to ensure that the punch was positioned distal to the physis. A free needle was used to pass the SutureTapes from deep to superficial through the distal patellar tendon, just proximal to the fracture fragment.

Intraoperative photograph depicting the reflected patellar tendon, with the proximal row sutures passed from deep to superficial through the patellar tendon just proximal to the avulsion fragment.

The fracture was reduced, and the reduction was provisionally held using a K‐wire placed anterior to posterior. Each set of SutureTapes was then tied in horizontal mattress fashion over the tendon. One suture end from each of the four knots was then loaded into two Arthrex 4.75 mm biocomposite SwiveLock anchors, with two limbs from each side being crossed over the fracture site to the opposite anchor. The two SwiveLock anchors were then inserted into the anteromedial and anterolateral proximal tibia distal to the fracture site, compressing the fracture. Thus, a double‐row suture repair was completed (Figures 3a and Figure 3b). The knee was taken through gentle range of motion (ROM), and the fracture and implants were stable with no obvious gapping. The operative site was irrigated, closed in a layered fashion, and sterile dressings were placed. The patient was placed in a hinged knee brace locked in extension and was permitted to be weight‐bearing as tolerated with the brace locked in extension.

4. Postoperative Course

Physical therapy was initiated 2 weeks postoperatively. Passive ROM from 0° to 90° was permitted at 4 weeks postoperatively, and advanced to unlimited ROM in the brace at 6 weeks. At 6 weeks, he was able to straight leg raise without difficulty. The hinged knee brace was discontinued at 8 weeks postoperatively. By 12 weeks, the patient had achieved full ROM of 0°–130°. At 4 months, he was permitted to start jogging. At 5 months postoperatively, he was able to jog without difficulty. At 7 months, the patient reported he was able to sprint and participate in sport‐specific training without pain, and was cleared to return to full athletic activity. Radiographs obtained at each postoperative visit confirmed successful fixation and progressive complete healing of the tibial tubercle fracture. (Figure 4).

At the patient′s final follow‐up appointment at 9 months postoperatively, he had returned to full unrestricted athletic activity, including tackle football, without pain, weakness, or any symptoms. He demonstrated 5/5 quadriceps strength, and final x‐ray again confirmed a healed tibial tuberosity fracture with symmetric patellar height compared to the contralateral knee (Figure 5).

5. Discussion

This report describes the case of a 15‐year‐old skeletally immature male who experienced a displaced Ogden Type 1B tibial tubercle avulsion fracture while playing basketball. The fracture was successfully treated with a double‐row suture anchor repair technique, resulting in fracture union and eventual return to sport with no complications. This case demonstrates the feasibility of this technique as an effective alternative to traditional fixation methods.

Traditional fixation methods for tibial tubercle avulsion fracture include screw fixation, K‐wires, plates, and tension bands. Though these techniques result in reliable fracture union, favorable short‐term outcomes, and high return to sport rates in athletes, metal implants are prone to hardware complications and need for further surgery [1, 16–18]. A recent systematic review of 336 pediatric tibial tubercle fractures treated mainly (98%) with open reduction internal fixation reported an overall 28.3% complication rate [16]. The most commonly reported complications were bursitis requiring removal of hardware (55.8%), and tenderness and/or prominence over the hardware (17.9%). The suture anchor repair technique described in this paper theoretically may minimize the risk of symptomatic hardware associated with traditional techniques; however, clinical studies to support this are currently lacking.

In skeletally immature patients, there is a risk of growth disturbance with any implant placed near the physis. However, existing literature reports relatively low rates of proximal tibial growth disturbance following operative repair of tibial tubercle avulsion fractures. In a retrospective case series of 157 operatively treated pediatric tibial tubercle fractures with metal implants, there was a 2% (2/157) rate of growth disturbance noted radiographically at final follow up [18]. Bioabsorbable suture anchors resorb over time, have smaller footprints than traditional metal fixation methods, and could theoretically confer a lower risk of growth disturbance than metal implants. However, we are not aware of any clinical studies comparing growth disturbance rates between different fixation methods for tibial tubercle avulsion fractures. Regardless of implant selection, the risk of growth disturbance can be mitigated with meticulous surgical technique and use of fluoroscopic confirmation intraoperatively.

The double‐row suture anchor repair technique has other potential benefits. Young athletes may require a knee MRI in the future, and permanent metal hardware could interfere with the quality of MRI imaging. Bioabsorbable suture anchors allow for improved image quality without metal scatter. Additionally, some tibial tubercle fractures may be too small for traditional fixation methods, such as screws. In these instances, the double‐row suture anchor repair could provide strong, reliable fixation. It is important to note that there is a lack of biomechanical and clinical data supporting one fixation method over another for the surgical fixation of tibial tubercle avulsion fractures. A single case report is unable to establish superiority of one fixation method over another. Future biomechanical and clinical studies are necessary to establish the efficacy, safety, and long‐term outcomes of double row suture anchor fixation compared to traditional metallic implants in the treatment of tibial tubercle avulsion fractures.

6. Conclusion

This case describes the successful use of the double‐row bioabsorbable suture anchor technique in the treatment of an Ogden Type 1B tibial tubercle avulsion fracture in an adolescent male athlete. This case demonstrates the efficacy of this technique with an excellent clinical outcome for this patient and return to unrestricted sport at final follow‐up. This technique has theoretical advantages over traditional metallic fixation methods; however, further clinical studies are needed to investigate superiority of different fixation options.
