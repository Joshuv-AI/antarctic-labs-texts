The Effect of Human Papillomavirus on Sperm Parameters: An Umbrella Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Mina Iravani, Maryam Dastoorpour, Solmaz Mohammadi, Maryam Zahedian, Parvin Esfandiarinezhad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a prevalent sexually transmitted virus hypothesised to contribute to unexplained male infertility that potentially affects sperm quality. The current research looks at the impact of HPV infections on sperm quality.Materials and Methods: Web of Science, PubMed, Cochrane Library, Medline, and Embase databases were searched on August 24, 2024 for reviews that examined the relationship between sperm quality and HPV infection, which were published between 1996 and 2024. PRISMA guidelines were followed for this study. The AMSTAR tool was used to assess the risk of bias. Evaluation of heterogeneity was conducted by the Q test and I² statistics. Pooled effect sizes were expressed as the standardised mean difference (SMD) with a 95% confidence interval (95% CI). These were synthesised using fixed-effect or random effects models as appropriate.Results:Out of 537 initially identified articles, 16 systematic reviews and meta-analyses were eligible for inclusion. Our umbrella review results showed that HPV infection in semen significantly reduced progressive motility (SMD = -0.48, 95% CI: -0.75, - 0.21), total motility (SMD = -0.59, 95% CI: -0.82, -0.35), sperm count (SMD = -0.27, 95% CI: -0.43, -0.10), viability (SMD = -0.38, 95% CI: -0.62, -0.14), and morphology (SMD = -0.32, 95% CI: -0.53, -0.10). Conclusion: HPV infections in semen may be a significant factor in the reduction of sperm quality, as observed in HPV-positive relative to HPV-negative individuals.

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is the most widespread sexually transmitted infection (STI) amongst men and women (1). This small, non-enveloped virus contains a circular, double-stranded DNA genome of about 8 000 base pairs. The 55 nm virion has an icosahedral shape, consisting of 52 capsomeres made of the major capsid protein L1 and fewer of the minor protein L2 (2). Over 200 HPV genotypes have been identified and adapted to epithelial tissues, such as anogenital skin and mucosa. HPV includes 12 high-risk (Hr) types that are linked to cancer and low-risk (Lr) types that typically cause benign growths or are asymptomatic (3).

Although HPV infection in men is typically a temporary condition that primarily causes warts on the external genitals, the virus's presence has been documented in various male reproductive organs, including the testes, epididymis, vas deferens, prostate, urethra, and semen (4). In 2018, the International Agency for Research on Cancer estimated there were approximately 69 400 HPV -related cancer cases in men (5). The prevalence of HPV varies significantly based on geography, age, sexual behaviour, host immune response, and the impact of screening and treatment, yet global infection rates are nearly 40% (6). The epidemiology of HPV infection shows a clear difference between females and males: in females, prevalence is high in the years following sexual debut but subsequently decreases; in males, it remains consistently high throughout life (7). Historically, HPV-related diseases have predominantly been studied in females, but recent interest in HPV infection in males is increasing. Nested polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis in a pioneering study on seminal HPV infection found HPV DNA in 10% of semen samples from young adult males who showed no signs of infection and had unprotected sex (8). A later study detected HPV DNA in both spermatozoa and exfoliated cells of seminal samples from three at-risk groups: men with genital warts, men whose female partners were HPV-positive, and infertile patients (9).

A meta-analysis indicates that HPV sperm infection significantly reduces progressive motility in infertile individuals (10). Two systematic reviews highlight that HPV in semen may affect sperm quality and reproductive outcomes (11, 12). Sperm morphology, concentration, and motility are reported to be negatively affected by HPV in another meta-analysis (13), which indicates an association between HrHPV infection and lower sperm counts compared to LrHPV (14). Two systematic reviews also suggest that HPV detection and genotyping are useful for diagnosing idiopathic infertility (15, 16).

Despite progress in understanding the effect of HPV on sperm quality, there is a lack of comprehensive evidence to guide clinical practice, particularly regarding HPV testing and management in male infertility evaluation or assisted reproductive technologies (17). The aim of our umbrella review is to assess whether HPV-positive individuals have poorer semen quality than HPV-negative individuals. The practical objective of this study is to provide comprehensive evidence that may aid in developing clinical guidelines for HPV testing in diagnosing sub-fertile couples and during assisted reproduction procedures.

Materials and Methods

Search methods

Two of the authors conducted searches of the Web of Science, PubMed, Medline, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases without language restrictions for all literature published between 1996 and August 24, 2024. Additional articles were identified through Google Scholar. The keywords 'human papillomavirus', 'HPV', 'sperm', 'sperm quality', 'semen', and 'semen quality', as well as all possible combinations of these terms, were used. A detailed account of the search strategy for each database can be found in Supplementary File 1 (See Supplementary Online Information at www.ijfs.ir).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Types of studies and outcome measures

Eligible studies included all published systematic reviews, with or without meta-analysis, that assessed the relationship between HPV infection and sperm quality, specifically focusing on the following outcomes: progressive motility, total motility, sperm count, viability, morphology, pH, concentration, and volume.

Data collection and analysis

The data reduction process involved several steps. Ini tially, the search results from all databases were compiled into an Endnote file, where duplicates were eliminated us ing software. Next, the titles and abstracts of the articles were screened; those deemed irrelevant were removed. During the second screening stage, the full texts of related systematic reviews were thoroughly examined, and addi tional duplicate reviews were manually removed. Articles that failed to meet the inclusion criteria were excluded, and relevant reviews were added to the final evaluation. Two authors (PE, SM) independently performed each step, and disagreements were resolved through discussion and, if necessary, by consulting the original authors. In the case of persistent disagreements, a third author (MI) con tributed to the discussion, and final decisions were made by consensus.

Data extraction

The research team first designed a data extraction tool to systematically collect details of the papers that pertained to the name of the first author, publication year, country, number of databases searched, means and standard deviations for the sperm quality parameters, date range, number of studies included, the review's objective, overall sample size, review type, and quality assessment tool used. In most studies, means and standard deviations for the sperm quality parameters were reported for both the HPV-positive and HPV-negative groups, which permitted direct calculation of the standardised mean difference (SMD). In two studies that reported medians with interquartile ranges (Q1-Q3), we estimated the SD as (Q3-Q1)/2. In one study that reported the median with minimum and maximum values, SD was estimated as (Max-Min)/4. In both cases, the median was an approximate mean.

Assessment of risk of bias

The quality of the studies included was evaluated by two independent authors (PE, MD). The quality of the systematic reviews was evaluated using the AMSTAR tool, noting no significant differences compared to AMSTAR-2 (18). Although the limitations of AMSTAR include focusing on reporting quality instead of methodology and insufficiently addressing sample size and study design (19), it utilises a straightforward scoring system (0 or 1) across 11 items, including publication bias. The AMSTAR method evaluates: i. Methodological quality, ii. Thoroughness of the search strategy, iii. Susceptibility to bias, and iv. Appropriateness of statistical tools in the meta-analysis (20). The evaluators completed a checklist of specific questions using the following quality scores: high (8-11 points), medium (4-7 points), and low (0-3 points) (21).

Ethical considerations

This study adhered to all research ethics laws. Permission from the Ethics Committee was not required as the research focused on publicly available studies. The authors ensured the avoidance of plagiarism and did not manipulate data for personal gain.

Statistical analysis

We used Stata software for data analysis (version 17, StataCorp LLC, America). The association between HPV and semen parameters was evaluated by SMD and was considered as the effect size. The necessary information of the reviewed articles consisted of the summary statistics - number of cases in the HPV-positive and HPV-negative groups, and the mean and standard deviations of semen parameters in each group. Heterogeneity tests were performed by Cochran’s Q statistic (low P value provides evidence of heterogeneity) and the I2 index with an unimportant heterogeneity of I2<40% and considerable heterogeneity of I2>75%. A random effects meta-analysis based on the DerSimonian-Laird method was used to incorporate the results with heterogeneity. We used subgroup analysis to find the source of heterogeneity and compare the two groups. The significance level in this study was set at less than 0.05. Publication bias was assessed by the Egger’s regression test. Egger’s regression test was conducted using a Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) method in Stata to assess small-study effects and potential publication bias. A P>0.05 was interpreted as indicating no evidence of publication bias.

Results

Our search of scientific databases yielded 537 review articles. After removing duplicates and completing the first two screening stages, 16 systematic reviews and meta-analyses remained for assessment (Fig .1).

Characteristics of the studies

Table 1 lists the articles in English that were published from 2009 to 2024. Five articles were published in China (10, 14, 22-24) and Italy (8, 25), while one each was published in Spain (11), Germany (26), the USA (27), Italy (25), the UK (28), France (29), Morocco (15), and Israel (13).

Seven studies were systematic reviews and meta-analyses (10, 11, 13, 14, 22, 24, 30), eight studies were systematic reviews only (8, 15, 25-29, 31), and one study was a metaanalysis (23).

The 16 included systematic reviews and meta-analyses collectively incorporated data from primary studies ranging in number from 8 to 135. These reviews searched between 2 and 7 electronic databases, with search periods extending from the inception of each database up to 2021. Most studies were observational (cross-sectional, cohort, case-control) analyses. Foresta et al. included observational studies and clinical trials in their systematic review (16).

Nine studies did not specify their quality assessment tool (15, 16, 20, 24-27, 29, 31); one used the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews (10), while six studies utilised the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale (11, 13, 14, 22, 23, 32). There were five high quality studies (11, 13, 14, 22, 32), seven were of moderate quality (8, 10, 15, 16, 23-25, 27), and four were of low quality (26, 28, 29, 31).

Discussion

The current umbrella review analysed studies that focused on the association between HPV infection and sperm quality. According to the results, HPV-positive semen significantly reduced progressive motility, total motility, sperm count, viability, and morphology.

According to Xiong et al. (23) and Lyu et al. (24), the risk of male fertility abnormalities increases due to seminal HPV infection. The exact mechanisms by which HPV affects sperm quality are still not fully understood. One possibility is that inflammation caused by HPV may alter the seminiferous tubules, disrupting both sperm production and maturation. Additionally, HPV might stimulate the production of reactive oxygen species or cytokines, which could impair sperm function (33). Previous studies have identified the presence of seminal HPV specifically in exfoliated cells and on sperm surfaces (34), mainly the sperm head (30). An in vitro study by Carlo et al. noted that HPV infected human sperm by localising to the equatorial region of the sperm head. This occurs when the HPV L1 capsid protein interacts with syndecan-1 (35). Additionally, when HPV binds strongly and persistently to the sperm, it indicates that conventional sperm-washing methods are ineffective in removing HPV DNA from the sperm surface (10).

Reduced progressive motility of sperm caused by HPV infection in semen may be linked to sperm DNA fragmentation, anti-sperm antibodies (ASAs), and glandular dysfunction. A high prevalence of ASAs has been reported on sperm surfaces of infertile patients diagnosed with seminal HPV infection and the presence of HPV is often associated with IgA and IgG class ASAs. This suggests that ASA production is triggered by HPV DNA on the sperm surface, which may serve as an antigenic stimulus (10, 30). The role of ASAs in impairing sperm quality remains controversial, although mechanisms such as sperm agglutination and complement-mediated cytotoxicity in the male genital tract have been proposed (36). The presence of HPV in infertile males may alter the secretory output of the prostate and seminal vesicles, thus compromising sperm motility. Additionally, HPV infection may increase DNA fragmentation and apoptosis in sperm. An in vitro study conducted by Connelly et al. (37) found that sperm cells carrying exogenous HPV E6/E7 DNA had a higher percentage of apoptotic DNA fragmentation relative to uninfected controls. Conversely, according to Cortés-Gutiérrez et al. (38), HPV-positive status does not correlate with increased sperm DNA fragmentation. Nonetheless, further research is essential to clarify if HPV-infected sperm are more prone to DNA damage.

Given that the I² values in our analyses indicated moderate to high heterogeneity, several factors may account for this variability across studies. Differences in study populations and geographic settings are likely contributors, as the prevalence of HPV and the distribution of infertility risk factors vary by region. Moreover, the diagnostic methods employed for HPV detection (e.g., PCR-based assays versus hybrid capture techniques) may produce different levels of sensitivity and specificity, and influence the reported associations. Variability in the definitions of infertility, such as distinguishing between primary and secondary infertility or the duration of attempts to conceive, may also introduce inconsistency. Finally, heterogeneity may arise from differences in study design, sample sizes and overall methodological quality, all of which could have affected the magnitude and direction of the pooled estimates.

Several limitations of this study must be considered when interpreting the results. First, the key confounding factors of male gender, age, and environmental exposures were not reliably recorded, potentially affecting how seminal HPV infection causes decreased progressive motility of sperm. Second, the predominance of non-prospective studies in the reviewed articles may diminish the reliability of our findings.

Conclusion

Our research found a notable decline in sperm qual ity among men with HPV, indicating a potential link between HPV infection and male fertility. This underscores the need for greater awareness of the impact of sexually transmitted diseases on reproductive health. The association between reduced sperm quality and HPV highlights the necessity of comprehensive fertility evaluations for couples faced with challenges in conception, especially when the HPV status of the male partner is unknown. Consequently, healthcare professionals should consider incorporating HPV screening for males in fertility assessments, given its potential impact on reproductive success. Future research should also explore the benefits of treating HPV infections on the sperm quality of the affected individuals.
