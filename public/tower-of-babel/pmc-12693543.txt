Genomic data representations for horizontal gene transfer detection
Authors: Andre Jatmiko Wijaya, Aleksandar Anžel, Hugues Richard, Georges Hattab
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHorizontal gene transfer (HGT) accelerates the spread of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) via mobile genetic elements allowing pathogens to acquire resistance genes across species. This process drives the evolution of multidrug-resistant “superbugs” in clinical settings. Detection of HGT is critical to mitigating AMR, but traditional methods based on sequence assembly or comparative genomics lack resolution for complex transfer events. While machine learning (ML) promises improved detection, several studies in other domains have demonstrated that data representations will strongly influence its performance. There is, however, no clear recommendation on the best data representation for HGT detection. Here, we evaluated 44 genomic data representations using five ML models across four data sets. We demonstrate that ML performance is highly dependent on the genomic data representation. The RCKmer-based representation (k = 7) paired with a support vector machine is found to be optimal (F1: 0.959; MCC: 0.908), outperforming other approaches. Moreover, models trained on multi-species data sets are shown to generalize better. Our findings suggest that genomic surveillance benefits from task-specific genome data representations. This work provides state-of-the-art, fine-tuned models for identifying and annotating genomic islands that will enable proper detection of transfer of AMR-related genes between species.

Introduction

Horizontal gene transfer (HGT) represents a pivotal mechanism for acquiring genetic materials in bacterial evolution. The acquired genetic materials typically form syntenic blocks, called genomic islands (GIs) [1]. HGT enables bacteria to acquire novel traits such as antimicrobial resistance (AMR), virulence, and metabolic versatility [2], which can transform benign species into pathogens that become deadly to their hosts. Detecting GIs is critical to understanding microbial evolution, adaptation, and their broader public health implications. By uncovering the role of GIs and HGT, researchers can develop targeted interventions such as antibiotics, improve surveillance of AMR, and respond rapidly to outbreaks. These insights extend beyond medicine, supporting industrial applications and ecological studies by revealing how microorganisms adapt to changing environments. As genomic data is accumulating at an exponential rate, advancing our ability to detect and analyze HGT is essential to address global public health challenges and drive innovation in biotechnology.

Over the past decade, numerous computational approaches for detecting HGT have been developed, broadly divided into four computational groups: artificial intelligence-based, sequence composition, comparative genomics, and hybrid [3]. Sequence composition approaches aim at identifying composition bias within a sequence, which involves the characterization of components present in the sequence. Comparative genomics approaches involve comparing sequences of different organisms to understand their similarities, differences, and evolutionary relations [4]. Hybrid approaches combine computational approaches from different groups to benefit from their strengths and compensate for their drawbacks. Recently, machine learning (ML) has been applied to help decipher the role of microbes in ecology [5], and there has been a growing interest in applying ML to the HGT detection problem [3]. Some utilize traditional ML models, while others employ deep learning (DL). GI-SVM utilizes a one-class support vector machine (SVM) to learn a decision function that maximizes the separation between GIs and non-GIs [6]. DeepHGT employs DL to identify HGT insertion sites on genomes [7].

The performance of ML models is heavily dependent on the choice of data representation [8]. Data representation in ML is the process of converting raw input into a format that ML models can process. This conversion includes encoding and embedding techniques. Encodings are specific conversion techniques, such as GC content or Kmer counting, that are applied to the raw data without any adjustment made to the relations of the elements. Conversely, embedding involves a step where an implicit representation of the elements is estimated, possibly accounting for the semantic relationship between elements. Embeddings can be pre-computed on reference datasets or performed jointly with the ML task. To circumvent misinterpretation of the conversion techniques employed in this study, the term “data representation” is employed, a broad term encompassing all conversion techniques. In ML, deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) sequences are often converted to numerical values for data representation and feature learning in various applications in bioinformatics [9, 10]. The conversion method is task-specific and can substantially influence the overall performance of ML models [11]. Some studies demonstrated that data representations indeed affect the performance of ML models on different tasks in bioinformatics [12–14]. Existing ML approaches for HGT detection often exploit frequently used data representations, for instance, Kmer frequencies, GC content, and codon usage. Recently, Banerjee et al. utilized word embedding to convert DNA sequences for their ML approach [15]. Few studies included a limited set of data representations to understand their importance for HGT detection [16–18]. However, the impact of all existing genomic data representations on the performance of ML approaches for HGT detection remains uncharted territory. To improve the task of HGT detection, the right genomic data representations should be chosen. However, at the time of writing, there is no clear recommendation on which representations work well, hindering the development of accurate and robust detection methods. Motivated by a previous comprehensive study in which Spänig et al. [19] investigated a wide range of peptide data representations for a variety of biomedical domains and later for proteins [20, 21], we conducted a comprehensive study of the impact of genomic data representations on HGT detection. The objectives of this study are two-fold. First, we explore the realm of genomic data representations to elucidate their impact on HGT detection. HGT encompasses a wide spectrum of events, including the transfer of genomic material between closely and distantly related lineages [22], as well as the transfer of material mediated by broad host range plasmids [23]. In this study, however, we follow the definition of GIs proposed by Bertelli et al. [24], describing them as integrated genomic segments that are present in one genome and absent in related organisms, identified based on comparative genomics. As a consequence, this definition excludes extrachromosomal HGT events, e.g. plasmid-mediated HGT. Second, we aim to provide the scientific community with recommendations on which genomic data representations work well for HGT detection.

In this study, we compared 44 genomic data representations from six feature groups—nucleic acid composition, pseudo nucleic acid composition, electron-ion interaction pseudopotentials, mutual information, autocorrelation and cross-covariance, and physicochemical property—with five ML models to determine the best combination of genomic data representation and model for HGT detection. We performed cross-validation of different combinations of data representations and ML models on four training data sets, and then selected the best-performing data representation and ML model. To evaluate the model in a real application setup, i.e. identifying GIs from the input, we compared our model against other state-of-the-art work by adopting an existing pipeline. Finally, we discussed the importance of genomic data representation, training data sets, limitations of this study, and future directions.

Materials and methods

Task definition

HGT detection problem can be split into two subtasks [15, 25]. The first subtask is segment classification, where we train an ML model to classify genomic segments as GIs or non-GIs. The second subtask is boundary prediction, where we localize the position of GIs in a sequence.

Based on our review [3], we observed that typical pipelines of ML approaches for HGT detection include several steps: input acquisition, segmentation, feature extraction, ML model training, and boundary prediction. The input can be whole genome sequences, draft genome sequences, reads, contigs from genomic or metagenomic samples, or genes. Depending on the input, segmentation is required to divide the input into multiple genomic segments. If the input is short sequences, reads, contigs, or genes, they can be treated as genomic segments. The input sequences are then converted into numerical values as features for training ML models. The HGT detection problem is often translated into either a clustering or a classification task. Once the model is trained to identify genomic segments as GIs or non-GIs, it can be used to localize the position of GIs in the input sequences for boundary prediction.

Our contribution to the pipeline is in the feature extraction and ML model training parts. We searched for the best feature extraction strategy to represent the input sequences through a comparative study. We compared different genomic data representations with different ML models in a classification task. We then evaluated the best data representation and ML model for boundary prediction.

Data sets

Despite the challenges of reliable validation data sets, we collected four data sets that have been used to benchmark computational approaches for HGT detection and a data set of reported GIs published in the literature. Each data set consists of genomic segments that are considered GIs and non-GIs, referring to positive and negative samples, respectively. Banerjee et al. [15] compiled established GI databases into a unified, non-redundant data set of 167 bacterial genomes, called the Benbow data set. Langille et al. [26] constructed a data set of 118 bacterial genomes called the IslandPick data set, predicted by a comparative genomics approach, which was then updated by Bertelli et al. [24], resulting in 104 bacterial genomes. Vernikos et al. [27] trained a relevance vector machine (RVM) model on a data set containing 32 species from three genera, namely Salmonella, Streptococcus, and Staphylococcus, which we call the RVM data set. Lu et al. [28] evaluated their GI detection tool, GI-Cluster, using a data set of nine bacterial genomes obtained from a comparative analysis performed by Wei et al. [29], which we later call the GI-Cluster data set. Literature data set is a collection of six bacterial species whose GIs were experimentally validated and were reported in literature [26]. Data representation

We employed iLearnPlus v1.5 [30] to convert data sets into various genomic data representations. iLearnPlus has a feature extraction functionality that extracts numerical vectors from biological sequences, capturing their biochemical, biophysical, and compositional properties compatible with ML tasks. Each representation maps the sequence into a vector, where denotes the dimensionality of the resulting vector, by computing a set of real values/statistics depending on the properties. iLearnPlus offers nine feature groups for DNA sequences. Some feature groups require the input sequences to have equal length, but the length of sequences in our data sets varies. As a consequence, only six feature groups can be used in our study, namely nucleic acid composition (Kmer, RCKmer, Z_curve), pseudo nucleic acid composition (pseudo dinucleotide composition, pseudo trinucleotide composition), electron-ion interaction pseudopotentials, mutual information, autocorrelation and cross-covariance (dinucleotide-based auto covariance, trinucleotide-based cross covariance, moran, geary), and physicochemical property (dinucleotide physicochemical properties, trinucleotide physicochemical properties), excluding residue composition, position-specific tendencies of trinucleotides, and similarity-based descriptor.

Table 1 shows that the length of samples in each data set varies. However, we did not fix the data sets to have samples of a certain length because the length of GIs varies naturally. As a consequence, the number of data representations that can be used in this study is reduced from 44 to 31 data representations, but we extend Kmer and RCKmer by varying from 1 to 7; additionally, we added GC content to the list as it is one of representations used in early studies of HGT detection [31]. In the end, we ended up with 44 data representations, including the variations of Kmer and RCKmer (see Supplementary Table S1 for the list of data representations used in this study). Although the data sets have samples with different lengths, the data representation automatically transformed them into a vector of numerical values, preparing tabular data for ML models.

Training details

Cross-validation

We trained five ML classifiers—Naïve Bayes, Logistic Regression (LR), Decision Tree (DT), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Random Forest (RF)—using default hyperparameters to focus on evaluating the impact of data representations rather than optimizing model-specific parameters. This approach was intended to minimize complexity and maintain consistency across different models. We applied a five-fold cross-validation strategy for each combination of data representation and model to ensure reliable performance. Each fold is composed of 80% training samples and 20% validation samples (Supplementary Table S2 provides an example of a training/validation split for each training data set). Particularly, we employed StratifiedGroupKFold cross-validation to ensure that the species in the training and validation data sets are mutually exclusive. This prevents data leakage as one of the pitfalls of applying ML in genomic data [32] and ensures that the performance metrics are not biased due to class imbalance.

Hyperparameter tuning

After we found the best data representation and model, we then optimized the hyperparameters to maximize the performance. In the hyperparameter tuning, we selected only the top 5 best-performing data representations and models based on F1 score per training data set. We found the best hyperparameters for RF (n_estimator: 500, criterion: ’gini’, max_depth: 10, min_samples_leaf: 1, min_samples_leaf: 2, max_features: ’sqrt’) and SVM (kernel: ’rbf’, C: 2, gamma: ’scale’).

Evaluation metrics

To measure the performance of the classification tasks, we used widely adopted evaluation metrics: F1 score, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), precision, recall, and accuracy. We reported the performance by averaging each evaluation metric across five-fold cross-validation. In addition, we employed the polar-diagrams Python library to visualize model outputs through an integrated polar coordinate representation of three complementary information-theoretic metrics: entropy, scaled mutual information, and variation of information [33]. This visualization framework enabled efficient comparative analysis of individual predictor (model) stability while simultaneously quantifying alignment relative to ground truth references.

Inference details

In this study, we focused on evaluating different data representations and models for HGT detection and did not design a new pipeline. To infer HGT events from the input, we adopted an existing pipeline, called TreasureIsland [15]. It employs a sliding window approach to split the input sequence into segments, then assigns a probability to the segments by passing them into the ML model. It also has a merging and a fine-tuning step to refine the predictions. The output is the location of the predicted GIs. Here, we replaced the ML model with our ML model, which is an SVM model trained on the Benbow data set with RCKmer-7 representation.

To evaluate the performance of the modified pipeline, we compared it against the original pipeline and other baselines. Evaluation metrics were measured at the nucleotide level following previous studies [15, 24] where: (i) True Positive (TP): the number of nucleotides in the positive prediction that overlaps with positive reference data. (ii) True Negative (TN): the number of nucleotides outside the positive prediction that overlaps with negative reference data. (iii) False Positive (FP): the number of nucleotides in the positive prediction that overlaps with negative reference data. (iv) False Negative (FN): the number of nucleotides outside the positive prediction that overlaps with positive reference data.

Results

Impact of data representations and training data sets

To elucidate the influence of data representations and training data sets on ML models, we trained different models on four training data sets containing different numbers of bacterial species (from 9, to 32, to 104, and to 167 species). We used 44 data representations and five ML models, including Naïve Bayes (NB), Logistic Regression (LR), Decision Tree (DT), Random Forest (RF), and SVM. We used default hyperparameters provided by scikit-learn v1.5.0 [34] for all models, as we focused on evaluating the performance of the data representations. We then performed hyperparameter optimization on the top 5 performing data representations and models using grid search. We performed five-fold cross-validation and report the average performance metrics. The performance metrics include F1 score, MCC, precision, recall, and accuracy.

For the Benbow data set, SVM with RCKmer-7 achieved the best performance across all metrics, with an average F1 score of 0.93 0.02, MCC of 0.84 0.06, precision of 0.93 0.03, recall of 0.93 0.03, and accuracy of 0.92 0.03. These results indicated that RCKmer-7 combined with SVM is highly effective for this data set (P < .001). In comparison, other configurations, such as SVM with Kmer-6 and RF with PCPseTNC, which have much lower dimensionality than RCKmer-7, showed slightly lower but competitive results. Similarly, SVM with RCKmer-7 also showed superior performance on the IslandPick data set, achieving an average F1 score of 0.90 0.05 and MCC of 0.85 0.08, the highest among other models (P < .001). The high recall (0.93 0.04) and accuracy (0.93 0.04) suggested that this configuration is particularly effective in identifying GIs. For the RVM data set, the best performance was observed with RF and Kmer-5 (P < .001), achieving an average F1 score of 0.76 0.10 and a recall of 0.78 0.15. However, the MCC values are relatively low in all configurations, the highest being 0.54 0.17 for RF with RCKmer-5. SVM with RCKmer-7 again outperformed other models on the GI cluster data set, achieving an average F1 score of 0.68 0.15, MCC of 0.57 0.21, and accuracy of 0.82 0.09 (P < .001). However, we found relatively high standard deviations in the performance metrics in this data set, indicating that the variability in performance is inversely proportional to the number of species in a data set. Overall, the SVM with RCKmer-7 consistently achieved the best results across multiple data sets, and most of the top 5 best-performing representations were from the nucleic acid composition feature group.

We performed hyperparameter tuning on the top 5 best-performing representations and models (Supplementary Table S3). As expected, we observed improved performance in all cases. The RCKmer representation was superior to other representations, followed by Kmer and Z_curve_48bit in all data sets except the GI cluster data set. We noticed that RCKmer, Kmer, Z_curve_48bit, and PCPseTNC appeared in the top 5 best-performing data representations in all data sets, except the GI cluster data set, where CKSNAP, Subsequence, and Mismatch representations appeared in the top 5 representations. The scripts used to reproduce the cross-validation results and the analyses presented in subsequent sections are available in our GitHub repository: https://github.com/andrejw27/genomic-data-representations-for-hgt-detection.

We found that RF is the most robust to data representations among the other models, consistently achieving the highest average F1 score, as shown in the boxplot in Fig. 1, regardless of the training data sets. Conversely, LR and NB generally performed worse than other models, regardless of the data representations and training data sets. SVM consistently achieved the best F1 score when trained with RCKmer-7 in most training data sets; as an exception, the best F1 score was achieved by RF trained with Kmer-5 in the RVM data set. The performance of SVM was the most widespread among the other models, indicating that SVM is more sensitive to data representations.

Overall, we found that Kmer-6 and RCKmer-7 representations were superior to other data representations, especially with higher values and when trained with RF and SVM. Our findings aligned with a previous study that reviewed the applications of Kmers-based representation in genomic analysis from profiling genomic sequences to phylogenetic analysis [35]. In addition, we observed that models trained on data sets with more species performed generally better, regardless of the data representations; the more diverse a data set, the better the performance of the models. To understand whether there is a statistically significant impact of data representations, models, and data sets on model performance, we performed the Friedman test and the Kruskal–Wallis test (Supplementary Notes S1). The model selections and choice of data representations greatly influenced the outcomes. When data representation was held constant, model selection significantly affected predictive outcomes (Benbow: P = 5.83e−21, IslandPick: P = 3.32e−28, GI-Cluster: P = 1.34e−18, RVM: P = 1.58e−24). Conversely, when the model was fixed, variations in data representation led to substantial performance differences (Benbow: P = 3.48e−7, IslandPick: P = 4.37e−5, GI-Cluster: P = .001, RVM: P = .086).

Correlation between data representations

In this work, we used data representations derived from different groups described in iLearnPlus v1.5 [30]. To understand whether data representations derived from the same group bring the same kind of information, we computed a correlation measure between the different representations that accounts for the representation dimensionality. We thus used the adjusted RV coefficient, an extension of Pearson correlation allowing for comparison of different representations [36]. The adjusted RV-coefficient is a modified version of the RV-coefficient that corrects for the bias introduced by the dimensionality of the data.

Figure 2 shows the correlation between data representations based on the adjusted RV coefficient. Some representations showed stronger correlations with certain representations from the same group, while showing different correlations with other representations from the same group and other groups. For example, Kmer-7 and RCKmer-7 were highly correlated, as were NAC, GC, Kmer-1, and RCKmer-1; however, the former subset had lower correlations with the latter group. In general, representations within groups (intra-group) exhibited stronger correlations than representations from different groups (inter-group) (see Supplementary Fig. S2); the analysis revealed that intra-group correlations were higher than inter-group correlations (t-test, P = .039), suggesting stronger associations within groups. As an exception, Mismatch and Subsequence had low correlations (close to 0) with most other representations, capturing distinct information that could be valuable for GI detection.

Correlation between data representations based on the adjusted RV-coefficient (example: Benbow data set). Color coding corresponds to the adjusted RV coefficient, where a darker color represents a higher correlation between data representations.

There was a notable relationship between representations from the autocorrelation and cross-covariance group (TAC, TACC, TCC, DAC, DACC, DCC, and NMBroto). Representations within this group formed three subgroups based on the properties of the genomic data, namely, dinucleotide-based, trinucleotide-based, and spatial autocorrelation. Representations within subgroups showed strong correlations, but correlations between subgroups were weaker, suggesting that dinucleotide, trinucleotide, and spatial autocorrelation capture different aspects of GIs. Moran and Geary had moderate correlations with the dinucleotide-based subgroup, but low correlations with the trinucleotide-based subgroup and the other groups, highlighting their unique focus on spatial dependence.

We found that the top 5 data representations were highly correlated with each other, indicating that different data representations capture similar information despite having different dimensionalities (see Supplementary data to see the dimensionality of each representation), such as RCKmer-7, Z_curve_48bit, and PCPseTNC. Overall, most data representations showed strong correlations between them, especially those derived from the nucleic acid composition group.

Baselines

To assess the applicability of our study, i.e. the ability to identify GIs from genomic sequences, we compared the best data representation and model (RCKmer-7 and SVM) with other baselines for HGT detection, including TreasureIsland [15], IslandPick [26], IslandViewer4 [37], AlienHunter [38], Islandpath DIMOB [39], SIGI-HMM [40], and Islander [41].

To create an end-to-end pipeline for HGT detection, we adopted the pipeline from TreasureIsland, which has been compared against other methods and is the current state-of-the-art. We replaced the ML model with our model, which is an SVM with optimized hyperparameters (kernel: ’rbf’, C: 2, gamma: ’scale’) trained on the Benbow data set, the same training data set used in TreasureIsland, with RCKmer-7 representation. In the training process, we ensured that the species in the evaluation data sets were not included in the training data set to avoid data leakage. We then evaluated the modified pipeline on two evaluation data sets, namely the Benbow evaluation data set and the literature data set. The evaluation metrics were measured based on nucleotide overlap following previous studies [15, 24].

Table 3 shows the evaluation results on the Benbow test data set. Our model improved the performance of an existing pipeline and outperformed other baselines in all evaluation metrics, achieving an F1 score of 0.88, MCC of 0.79, precision of 0.92, recall of 0.87, and accuracy of 0.91. Analogously, Table 4 shows evaluation results on the literature data set. Our model improved the performance of an existing pipeline and was superior to other baselines. Our model obtained an F1 score of 0.96, MCC of 0.91, precision of 0.99, recall of 0.94, and accuracy of 0.95.

Furthermore, we utilized mutual information summary polar diagrams to compare model performance across datasets using information-theoretic measures [33]. The diagrams in Fig. 3 reveal that all models perform better on the literature dataset. Our fine-tuned model consistently showed the closest alignment to ground truth, evidenced by the smallest angular deviation. This figure also demonstrates strong entropy agreement, indicating balanced generalization without overfitting. Furthermore, the model achieved superior scaled mutual information, reflecting effective capture of data patterns. Together, these results highlighted its robust and reliable performance across different evaluation sets.

Discussion

This study presents the first comparative study of different genomic data representations for HGT detection. We comprehensively analyzed different data representations based on biochemical, biophysical, and compositional characteristics. There are 44 data representations available in iLearnPlus v1.5 [30], but we did not use all of them because some of them require the input to have a fixed length, while samples in all data sets have different lengths, as shown in Table 1. Consequently, the most commonly used data representation for ML models was not included, namely one-hot encoding. However, this decision allowed our study to remain independent of the assumption that GIs must adhere to a specific length. We trained 5 ML models on 4 training data sets with different numbers of species (from 9, to 32, to 104, and to 167 species, respectively). We did not include DL models because our focus is on the impact of data representations, and DL models are not yet as widely used as ML models for HGT detection. Previous studies on HGT detection often used k-fold cross-validation, which can lead to data leakage if samples from the same species appear in both the training and validation data sets [16, 25]. In our study, we addressed this issue by using StratifiedGroupKFold cross-validation to ensure that the species in the training and validation data sets are mutually exclusive.

Our findings revealed that some data representations were superior to others, with the RCKmer-7 representation consistently performing best in most data sets, indicating that high-dimensional representations were likely to capture features associated with HGT. Our findings are consistent with a previous benchmark study showing that oligonucleotide usage is powerful for detecting HGT, but the study was limited to because of statistical reasons [42]. Here, we demonstrated how ML can overcome the statistical barriers, enabling the use of longer oligonucleotides to enhance species specificity and improve HGT detection [43]. Additionally, we observed that representations with low correlations with the top 5 representations, such as Subsequence, Mismatch, Moran, and Geary, performed slightly worse than PCPseTNC, Z_curve_48bit, and PsedEIIP when trained with the RF model. This observation suggests a potential combination of low-correlated representations to capture different information associated with GIs. Among all models, RF achieved the highest average performance across all data sets, demonstrating its robustness to data representations. However, SVM outperformed RF in some cases, especially when trained with RCKmer and Kmer representations with high values, i.e. 6 and 7. This finding is consistent with the wide adoption of SVM and RF in HGT detection tools [6, 16, 17, 44–46].

Finally, we presented the application of our study to predict GI boundaries in an isolated genome. We applied our results to an existing ML approach by replacing the given model with our model, which is an SVM trained with RCKmer-7 on the Benbow data set. We showed that the modified version outperformed the original version and other baselines in almost all evaluation metrics. Although we demonstrated that our findings have advanced the state-of-the-art, we noticed that the evaluation method, which is at the nucleotide level, might introduce fuzziness as the exact location of GIs may vary depending on the methods applied to set the ground truth. One could address the fuzziness by introducing lenient criteria, i.e. some nucleotide differences between ground truths and predictions, to measure the performance instead of an exact number of overlapping nucleotides.

Overall, our study underscores the importance of data representations and training data sets in HGT detection. It is noteworthy that an adapted data representation optimized for the task of HGT detection outperforms all previous methods that were specifically designed for the task. This observation raises a broader inquiry into the relative significance of ML model architecture compared to data representation, suggesting that meticulously designed input features may, under certain circumstances, eliminate the necessity for highly specialized models. The composition of the data sets plays a role in the resulting ML model; the more diverse the data set, the more generalizable the model (see Supplementary Fig. S3 for the comparison between models trained on different data sets). Additionally, we provided the evaluation results at the species level (Supplementary Fig. S4). Moreover, we found that low-correlated data representations can perform comparably well, suggesting that combined representations may provide a more comprehensive representation of GIs. These findings highlight the need for further research to explore the conditions under which different data representations and models excel in order to develop a more robust HGT detection tool. Future work could focus on exploring additional representations or hybrid approaches to improve the results. Although limiting the spread of AMR remains a complex challenge requiring many additional steps in clinical and environmental contexts, HGT detection can act as a precursor step to AMR surveillance. Early HGT detection enhances AMR surveillance systems by tracking transmission factors contributing to the dissemination of AMR genes [47]. This step complements conventional AMR surveillance, which primarily detects known causal genes, by enabling earlier recognition of emerging resistance trends, novel variants, and transmission patterns that can guide outbreak prevention and control [48].

While our study provides insightful findings, there are some limitations in our study. First, our study is limited to traditional genomic data representations, which may overlook intricate genomic patterns relevant to HGT. Incorporating DL in future work could capture richer sequence and structural contexts, potentially improving HGT detection. Second, we only performed hyperparameter tuning on top 5 best-performing representations and models. Although this is a practical strategy to reduce the vast search space, it would leave a pair of model and representation pairs that could have become top contenders when their hyperparameters had been tuned. Third, we used the available data sets as they are, but we acknowledge the limitation of these data sets. This limitation relates to the open-world problem, which is the difficulty of obtaining reliable data sets [49]. The position of reported GIs may differ depending on the method used to predict it, leading to inconsistencies in the evaluation. Fourth, the data leakage issue is not fully resolved, although we applied StratifiedGroupKFold. We did not consider the taxonomic profile, closely or distantly related species, in the cross-validation because we were dealing with imbalanced data sets with some over- and underrepresented species.

Conclusion

Our study presents the first comparative study of genomic data representations for HGT detection. We have shown how data representations and the composition of training data sets affect the resulting ML model. We found that representations from nucleic acid composition feature group, particularly Kmer-based with high values, offer competitive performance despite lower dimensionality, suggesting that low-dimensional representations can capture similar information as high-dimensional representations. This is supported by the correlation analysis, which shows a high correlation between them. Therefore, selecting appropriate data representations can lead to a significant improvement in the resulting ML model. Additionally, we demonstrated how ML can overcome statistical barriers faced by traditional sequence composition approaches, establishing the effective use of longer oligonucleotides to improve HGT detection. Among the ML models, RF showed the greatest compatibility with most data representations, while SVM achieved superior performance, although its compatibility is limited to only a few representations. We observed that models trained on data sets with more species tended to perform and generalize better. The StratifiedGroupKFold cross-validation strategy facilitated the investigation of model generalization and robustness across species. Our work advances the state of the art and paves the way for the advancement of HGT detection.
