(Bi)carbonate reduction as a new pathway beyond conventional CO2 reduction
Authors: Jieru Zhang, Mengdie Lv, Jianing Zhang, Lin Luo, Kun Qi, Fuxiang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electroreduction of carbonate and bicarbonate is emerging as a chemically distinct route beyond conventional gas-fed CO2 electrolysis. Yet the nominal ionic-carbon feed does not uniquely identify the immediate electroactive species. This review examines both indirect conversion, in which HCO3− or CO32− is locally protonated to regenerate molecular CO2 before conventional CO2RR, and direct conversion, in which solvated (bi)carbonate-derived species are activated at the cathode. We further discuss a surface- or lattice-mediated regime involving catalyst-bound carbonate reservoirs. Although these pathways may coexist, they differ fundamentally in carbon-species transport, interfacial reaction steps, catalyst-design requirements and reactor architectures. Using this pathway-resolved framework, we analyze the roles of acid–base speciation, proton-coupled electron transfer, cation-carbonate structuring, membrane-regulated carbon flux and catalytic microenvironments. We further evaluate catalyst and electrolyzer design, mechanistic evidence, carbon accounting and integration with carbon capture, including emerging direct-air-to-chemicals concepts. Finally, we identify the mechanistic and system-level requirements for advancing (bi)carbonate electrolysis towards energy-efficient, closed-loop capture-conversion processes.

Introduction

Electrochemical CO2 reduction (CO2RR) has emerged as a central strategy for converting renewable electricity into fuels and commodity chemicals, offering a modular route for energy storage, carbon recycling and distributed chemical manufacturing.1,2 Over the past decade, substantial progress has been made in catalyst design, mechanistic understanding and electrolyzer engineering, enabling higher current densities, improved product selectivity and increasingly sophisticated control over reaction environments.3–7 However, most established CO2RR systems still depend on purified CO2 feeds, which typically require energy-intensive regeneration, compression and pressure regulation before electrolysis.8 This dependence creates a major system-level limitation, as practical carbon-management streams rarely supply CO2 in such a purified and readily electrolyzable form.9 In industrial point-source capture and direct air capture, CO2 is commonly absorbed by alkaline or amine-based media and stored predominantly as bicarbonate or carbonate species rather than as molecular CO2. Converting these ionic carbon reservoirs back into high-purity CO2 requires thermal regeneration, gas separation, drying and compression, which introduce substantial energy penalties, process complexity and operating costs.10–12 As a result, the apparent efficiency achieved in a CO2 electrolyzer may not translate into a high overall efficiency for the full capture-conversion chain (Fig. 1a).

(b) Conventional laboratory assessments often consider only an electrolyzer supplied with purified CO2 and renewable electricity, thereby excluding the energy and process penalties associated with CO2 capture and feed conditioning.

This mismatch has become increasingly urgent as CO2 electrolysis moves from laboratory demonstrations toward integrated carbon-utilization systems.13,14 If CO2 must first be released from capture media, purified, compressed and then fed into electrolyzers, a large fraction of the energetic and economic advantage of electrochemical conversion is lost before the electrochemical step begins. Moreover, conventional alkaline CO2 electrolyzers suffer from an intrinsic carbonate problem.15,16 Although alkaline operation suppresses hydrogen evolution and enables high-rate CO2RR, incoming CO2 rapidly reacts with cathodically generated OH− to form HCO3− and CO32−. These species accumulate in the electrolyte, cross membranes, precipitate with cations or are eventually released as CO2 elsewhere in the device.17 This carbonate formation lowers single-pass carbon efficiency and creates carbon losses that cannot be fully resolved by improving intrinsic catalyst activity alone. Therefore, the field faces a fundamental question: should bicarbonate and carbonate continue to be treated as unavoidable loss products, or can they be used directly as electroactive carbon feedstocks?

The use of bicarbonate and carbonate as electrolysis feedstocks offers a conceptually distinct route for coupling carbon capture with electrochemical conversion (Fig. 1b).18 Rather than regenerating molecular CO2 before electrolysis, this strategy aims to convert capture-derived HCO3− and CO32− directly into fuels and chemicals. Such an approach could shorten the capture-conversion process, avoid energy-intensive CO2 stripping and compression, improve carbon utilization, and enable more direct coupling between carbon capture and electrochemical upgrading.19,20 These advantages are especially important for direct air capture and dilute CO2 sources, where the energetic cost of producing purified CO2 is particularly high. In this context, (bi)carbonate electrolysis is not simply a modified version of conventional CO2RR using a different feedstock; it represents an emerging electrochemical carbon-utilization paradigm that targets the dominant chemical form of captured carbon.

However, the identity of the macroscopic carbon feed does not necessarily define the immediate electroactive species.21 In aqueous electrolytes, HCO3−, CO32−, CO2(aq), OH− and cations are dynamically coupled through acid–base equilibria, transport and interfacial electric fields. Near the cathode, local pH, dissolved CO2 concentration and ion distributions can differ substantially from their bulk values because of cathodic OH− generation, membrane-mediated ion transport, water dissociation and double-layer restructuring. Consequently, products formed from bicarbonate or carbonate feeds may originate from several competing pathways: local protonation to CO2 followed by conventional CO2RR, activation of HCO3− or CO32−, or exchange with surface carbonate adlayers, oxycarbonate phases or lattice carbonate reservoirs. Distinguishing these pathways is essential, because each implies different catalyst-design principles, reactor requirements and performance limits.

In this review, as illustrated by Fig. 2, we use the identity of the immediate electroactive carbon species as its organizing principle. We distinguish indirect conversion through local CO2 regeneration, direct activation of solvated HCO3− or CO32−, and surface- or lattice-mediated conversion involving catalyst-bound carbonate reservoirs. On this basis, we examine the interfacial processes, catalyst requirements and reactor architectures associated with each pathway, together with the evidence required for mechanistic assignment. We then evaluate performance, carbon utilization, durability and capture-integrated operation. This pathway-resolved framework clarifies which principles can be transferred from conventional CO2RR and which challenges are specific to ionic-carbon electrolysis.

Mechanistic regimes of (bi)carbonate electrolysis

Carbon speciation and interfacial thermodynamics

In (bi)carbonate electroreduction, carbonate-derived species simultaneously serve as carbon feedstocks, electrolyte constituents and regulators of the solution-phase environment.22,23 The reaction medium is therefore defined by coupled acid–base equilibria among dissolved CO2, carbonic acid, bicarbonate and carbonate, which determine both carbon speciation and local reaction conditions:24CO2(aq) + H2O ⇌ H2CO3H2CO3 ⇌ HCO3− + H+HCO3− ⇌ CO32− + H+

The relative distribution of these carbonate-derived species is primarily dictated by pH, ionic strength, temperature and cation identity further modulating their activities. Under acidic to near-neutral conditions, dissolved CO2/H2CO3 and HCO3− dominate, whereas alkaline media strongly shift the equilibrium toward HCO3− and CO32−. Accordingly, in practical carbon-capture electrolytes, including KOH, NaOH and amine-assisted capture solutions, absorbed CO2 is stored predominantly as bicarbonate or carbonate. These anions become the principal carbon carriers available to the cathode, in sharp contrast to gas-fed CO2RR, where molecular CO2 is supplied directly from the gas phase.25,26 No single equilibrium potential can be assigned to (bi)carbonate reduction because the value depends on the immediate electroactive carbon species, target product, pH, temperature and species activities. At 25 °C and pH 7, where HCO3− predominates in the dissolved inorganic carbon pool, the commonly reported apparent equilibrium potentials for reduction to CO, formate/formic acid, CH3OH, CH4 and C2H4 are approximately −0.52, −0.61, −0.38, −0.24 and −0.34 V vs. SHE, respectively.27 These values correspond to approximately −0.11, −0.20, +0.03, +0.17 and +0.07 V vs. RHE. Because these potentials are calculated using molecular CO2 as the reactant, they describe the indirect pathway in which HCO3− or CO32− first regenerates dissolved CO2 before electroreduction.

For direct conversion, the half-reactions must instead be written explicitly using the predominant ionic carbon species. Bicarbonate reduction to CO and formate can be expressed as:HCO3− + H2O + 2e− → HCOO− + 2OH−CO32− + 2H2O + 2e− → HCOO− + 3OH−

At fixed reactant and product activities, the corresponding equilibrium potentials shift by −59.1 and −88.7 mV per pH unit vs. SHE at 25 °C, respectively. Relative to RHE, these slopes become −29.6 and 0 mV per pH unit. Above pH ∼10.3, CO32− becomes the dominant dissolved carbon species. The corresponding carbonate-to-CO and carbonate-to-formate half-reactions consume four and three proton equivalents per two electrons, giving potential shifts of −118.3 and −88.7 mV per pH unit vs. SHE, respectively. These thermodynamic values should be regarded as reference points rather than practical onset potentials, which additionally reflect activation barriers, interfacial speciation, mass-transport limitations and ohmic losses. Direct conversion of HCO3− or CO32− also requires activation of highly solvated, negatively charged carbon species, C–O bond reorganization, proton-coupled electron transfer in a proton-deficient environment and selective suppression of HER.28 Thus, alkaline media promote carbonate storage and may attenuate proton-driven HER, but they also introduce kinetic penalties arising from strong anion solvation, limited proton availability and sluggish interfacial carbon activation.29,30 These challenges are amplified by the dynamic nature of the near-electrode environment, where cathodic OH− generation, proton flux from bipolar membranes or acidic boundary layers, and buffer equilibria can shift local carbonate speciation away from the bulk composition. Consequently, CO2(aq), HCO3− and CO32− may coexist at the interface, enabling a continuum of mechanistic regimes: locally regenerated CO2 can dominate under acidified conditions, direct HCO3−/CO32− activation may occur under strongly alkaline CO2-deficient conditions, and mixed pathways involving dissolved CO2, adsorbed bicarbonate, carbonate adlayers and catalyst-bound carbonaceous intermediates may operate between these limits.

Interfacial electric fields further reshape the thermodynamic landscape of (bi)carbonate reduction.31 Within the electrochemical double layer, cations accumulate near the electrode, water molecules adopt preferential orientations, and polar or charged intermediates can be selectively stabilized.32 Alkali-metal cations will further promote local electric-field effects, stabilize *CO2− or *OCHO-like intermediates and regulate the activity of interfacial water.33 In (bi)carbonate electrolytes, these field effects are further intertwined with cation-dependent ion pairing, solvation structures, and the adsorption behavior of HCO3− and CO32− at the electrified interface.34 The relevant thermodynamic descriptors must move beyond intrinsic adsorption energies on idealized clean surfaces to incorporate field- and solvation-dependent free energies governing anion approach, partial desolvation, carbonate adsorption, proton transfer, and C–O bond reorganization.35 Another defining feature is the strong interaction of bicarbonate and carbonate with oxide, hydroxide and oxyhydroxide surfaces. Surface-bound carbonate motifs, including monodentate, bidentate and bridging configurations, can form stable adlayers that block active sites, act as spectators or participate directly in catalytic turnover.36 On metal oxides and reconstructed metal surfaces, carbonate adsorption may further couple with oxygen vacancies, surface hydroxyls or metal–oxygen frameworks, thereby generating interfacial carbon reservoirs that cannot be described by conventional molecular CO2 adsorption models.

Thus, the thermodynamic landscape of (bi)carbonate reduction should be considered across three coupled levels: bulk carbonate equilibria, local interfacial speciation, and catalyst-bound carbonate chemistry. Although bulk Pourbaix-type analysis provides a useful starting point, resolving the operative reaction pathway requires the free-energy picture to incorporate local pH, ion activity, interfacial electric fields, adsorption geometry, and mass transport. Thermodynamic descriptors derived solely from bulk speciation and standard potentials therefore offer an incomplete description of (bi)carbonate electroreduction. A more meaningful framework must explicitly account for local carbon speciation, interfacial water and cation organization, field-dependent stabilization, and surface-bound carbonate motifs at the catalyst–electrolyte interface. Establishing such an integrated thermodynamic framework is essential for rational catalyst design and for distinguishing intrinsic kinetic limitations from transport- or interface-controlled phenomena in direct (bi)carbonate electrolysis.

Indirect pathway: local CO2 regeneration followed by CO2RR

Among the three mechanistic regimes, two limiting solution-phase pathways can be distinguished: indirect conversion through local CO2 regeneration and direct activation of solvated (bi)carbonate-derived species.37,38 In the indirect pathway, HCO3− or CO32− does not act as the immediate electrochemical reactant. Instead, it functions as a carrier, reservoir or precursor of molecular CO2 (Fig. 3a). A local proton source, provided for example by a bipolar membrane, an acidic compartment or interfacial buffer equilibrium, protonates bicarbonate or carbonate near the cathode to generate CO2. The regenerated CO2 is then reduced through conventional CO2RR pathways involving established intermediates such as *CO2−, *COOH, *OCHO and *CO, depending on the catalyst surface and product pathway. In this sense, the core surface chemistry remains electrocatalytic CO2 reduction, and many design principles developed for gas-fed CO2RR remain applicable.25 The essential difference is not the identity of the downstream catalytic intermediates, but the mode of CO2 supply: molecular CO2 is generated in situ through protonation of bicarbonate or carbonate species rather than being delivered directly from the gas phase.39 This indirect pathway is particularly relevant in membrane-electrode assemblies, especially when a bipolar membrane or acidic anolyte supplies protons to the cathode-facing region. Under these conditions, proton flux can create a locally acidic or weakly buffered microenvironment that favors rapid conversion of HCO3− or CO32− into CO2. High apparent carbonate conversion may therefore reflect efficient local CO2 regeneration followed by fast CO2 adsorption and reduction, rather than direct activation of carbonate-derived anions. This mechanism is attractive because it avoids the need to activate strongly hydrated, negatively charged carbonate species at the catalyst surface. It also maintains a relatively high local CO2 activity, allowing catalysts to operate through familiar CO2RR pathways. However, the same feature introduces important system-level penalties. Locally regenerated CO2 may desorb, diffuse away or cross the membrane before being reduced, decreasing single-pass carbon efficiency. In addition, bipolar membrane operation relies on water dissociation and coupled ion transport, which can increase ohmic and kinetic energy losses.40,41 Thus the efficiency of the indirect pathway is governed not only by intrinsic catalytic activity, but also by the balance between CO2 generation, retention, mass transport and cathodic consumption.

Local pH is therefore a central descriptor for indirect (bi)carbonate electrolysis. Cathodic reduction reactions continuously generate OH−, making the near-electrode region more alkaline than the bulk electrolyte and shifting the CO2/HCO3−/CO32− equilibrium away from molecular CO2.42 In H-cells or weakly convective bicarbonate electrolytes, this alkalization can strongly suppress the concentration of CO2 at the catalyst surface. Under such conditions, direct HCO3− activation may become more relevant, although residual dissolved CO2 or transiently regenerated CO2 cannot be excluded.39 By contrast, in bipolar-membrane or cation-exchange-membrane electrolyzers, proton delivery from water dissociation or an acidic compartment can locally counteract alkalization and regenerate CO2 close to the cathode, shifting the apparent mechanism toward indirect CO2RR.43 The role of bicarbonate is therefore ambivalent. It can buffer local pH and sustain carbon availability through equilibrium with CO2, but it can also consume OH−, reshape ion distributions and interfacial electric fields, and alter adsorbate coverage.44

Carbon supply is another important factor in indirect carbonate conversion. Yet, a higher bicarbonate or carbonate concentration does not necessarily translate into enhanced product selectivity or efficiency. Concentrated carbonate electrolytes may induce salt precipitation, carbonate crossover, greater ionic complexity and competitive interfacial adsorption. In particular, CO32−, with its higher charge density, can strongly interact with alkali-metal cations, interfacial water and catalyst surfaces.45,46 These interactions may stabilize key intermediates, but can also block active sites or disturb the local reaction environment. Thus, the role of indirect CO2 regeneration depends not only on carbonate availability, but also on electrolyte composition, membrane architecture, current density, hydrodynamics and catalyst surface chemistry.

A representative demonstration of the indirect concept was reported by Li et al., who developed a carbonate electrolyzer for syngas production (Fig. 4a).47 In this system, carbonate species served as captured carbon reservoirs and were converted through local CO2 regeneration followed by cathodic CO2 reduction, affording faradaic efficiencies of approximately 30% for CO and 70% for H2 with negligible CO2 loss during operation. This work showed that carbonate electrolysis can, in principle, couple carbon capture directly with electrochemical conversion, thereby avoiding a separate energy-intensive CO2 concentration and regeneration step. However, the product scope and selectivity of this early indirect system remained limited compared with conventional CO2RR, which can access a wider range of C1 and C2+ products. Okatenko et al. further advanced this strategy by developing a porous solid electrolyte reactor for indirect (bi)carbonate electroreduction (Fig. 4b and c).38 KHCO3 or K2CO3 capture solutions were fed into the middle porous solid electrolyte layer, where protons generated from the anodic oxygen evolution reaction converted HCO3− or CO32− into molecular CO2. The regenerated CO2 was then reduced at the cathode through conventional CO2RR pathways. By spatially separating CO2 regeneration from CO2RR while maintaining efficient local CO2 transport, this configuration achieved up to FEformate of 96%, 95% CO2 recovery efficiency and 86% bicarbonate-to-formate conversion and operated stably for 100 h at 100 mA cm−2. The same reactor concept was also extended to concentrated formate production and C2+ electrosynthesis from bicarbonate as the sole carbon source. Together, these studies demonstrate that captured (bi)carbonate species can function as liquid-phase carbon reservoirs for in situ CO2 regeneration, while emphasizing that efficient indirect electrolysis depends on the retention, enrichment and rapid cathodic consumption of locally generated CO2.

The middle porous solid-electrolyte (PSE) layer is not an intrinsic requirement for every indirect pathway, because CO2 can also be regenerated at a bipolar-membrane or liquid–electrode interface. It is, however, a functional requirement of the three-chamber PSE architecture. The layer establishes a spatially separated acidification zone in which anode-derived protons convert HCO3− or CO32− into CO2, while preventing the regenerated gas from mixing with anodic O2 and allowing the cathode to retain a conventional gas-fed CO2RR environment. It must also complete the ionic circuit, distribute the liquid capture solution and provide connected pores for CO2 nucleation, disengagement and delivery to the cathode. Its role is therefore active rather than that of a passive spacer. In an electrochemical (bi)carbonate-regeneration reactor, replacing the sulfonate-functionalized PSE with similarly sized inert particles increased the cell voltage by approximately 6.8 V at 100 mA cm−2 in 0.5 M NaHCO3, corresponding to an approximately 80% increase in energy consumption.49 This comparison demonstrates that the ion-conducting middle layer is required to limit ohmic loss while maintaining spatially separated CO2 regeneration.

Direct pathway and elementary steps for ionic-carbon activation

Direct (bi)carbonate reduction is mechanistically attractive because it could bypass CO2 regeneration and thereby suppress CO2 loss, offering a conceptual route for electrochemical upgrading of captured carbon in its native ionic form (Fig. 3b).11,48 However, this pathway faces substantial kinetic barriers. Compared with CO2, HCO3− and CO32− are more strongly hydrated, negatively charged, and less electrophilic at the carbon center;50 their activation therefore requires catalyst surfaces capable of weakening solvation shells, stabilizing anion adsorption, and facilitating proton transfer under alkaline or locally alkaline conditions.11 Evidence for direct (bi)carbonate reduction has been inferred from several experimental observations. Product formation under CO2-free bicarbonate or carbonate feeds, particularly when dissolved CO2 is rigorously excluded, suggests that molecular CO2 is not always required as the immediate reactant.51,52In situ Raman and infrared spectroscopy have detected potential-dependent carbonate- or bicarbonate-derived surface species that correlate with product generation.53 Scanning electrochemical microscopy and microelectrode studies further indicate that bicarbonate reduction can exhibit kinetic behavior distinct from CO2 reduction, implying that bicarbonate may not simply act as a passive buffer. Nevertheless, distinguishing direct from indirect pathways remains challenging. Even nominally CO2-free carbonate electrolytes may contain trace dissolved CO2, while local pH gradients and interfacial protonation can transiently regenerate CO2 near the electrode. Conversely, the observation of carbonate-derived adlayers does not necessarily prove that these species are reduced, as they may instead function as spectators, buffers, or site-blocking species. Product formation alone is therefore insufficient to establish a direct mechanism. From an electron-transfer perspective, (bi)carbonate reduction could in principle proceed via either outer-sphere or inner-sphere pathways.54 The outer-sphere route would involve direct electron transfer to solvated HCO3−/CO32− without specific catalyst binding, but it is likely kinetically disfavored due to strong hydration and electrostatic repulsion, and has not been experimentally confirmed. In contrast, most current evidence supports an inner-sphere, adsorption-mediated mechanism, where (bi)carbonate first coordinates to surface sites (e.g., Cu–CO32− or vacancy-stabilized *CO3) before reduction, as demonstrated on oxide-derived Cu and SnO2.51,55 However, the presence of adsorbed carbonate alone does not guarantee catalytic turnover, and definitive assignment of an inner-sphere pathway requires correlated potential-dependent spectroscopic and product evidence.

As shown in Fig. 5, the direct activation of HCO3− and CO32− involves a sequence of coupled elementary processes, including transport to the electrochemical interface, partial desolvation, adsorption, protonation, electron transfer, C–O bond reorganization and product desorption. Accordingly, the rate-determining step can shift among these elementary processes depending on the catalyst surface, electrolyte composition and operating current density. Mass transport imposes a fundamental constraint. In CO2RR, CO2 can be continuously supplied to the catalyst through gas-diffusion electrodes, and its local concentration is mainly governed by gas pressure, solubility and diffusion across the electrolyte film. By contrast, bicarbonate and carbonate are transported as hydrated ions in the liquid phase, with their flux determined by diffusion, migration and convection and their concentrations simultaneously coupled to homogeneous acid–base equilibria.56 At high current densities, rapid OH− generation, carbonate depletion, cation accumulation and local electric-field gradients can substantially reshape the interfacial composition.57 Consequently, the concentration and activity of HCO3− or CO32− at the outer Helmholtz plane may differ markedly from those in the bulk electrolyte, indicating that the nominal carbonate concentration does not necessarily reflect the concentration of electroactive carbon species at the catalyst surface.

Partial desolvation and adsorption are then required before efficient electron transfer can occur. Compared with neutral CO2, HCO3− and CO32− are more strongly hydrated and negatively charged, making their direct approach to a negatively polarized cathode intrinsically unfavorable.58 Their activation therefore requires interfacial environments that weaken hydration shells, compensate electrostatic repulsion and stabilize carbonate-derived configurations.59 Lewis-acidic metal sites, oxygen vacancies, surface hydroxyl groups, positively polarized domains and specifically adsorbed cations may all contribute to this process. On oxide-derived metals, metal oxides and oxycarbonate surfaces, carbonate species can bind through oxygen atoms in monodentate, bidentate or bridging configurations.60 On more metallic surfaces, the interaction may be weaker and instead mediated by interfacial water networks, alkali-metal cations and local electric fields. These binding modes determine whether carbonate-derived species act as true reactants, spectators, proton relays, local buffers, site-blocking adsorbates or dynamic carbon reservoirs. Metal cations also act as active components of the interfacial microenvironment rather than inert charge-balancing species. In bicarbonate electrolyzers, changing the cation from Li+ to Na+, K+, and Cs+ did not substantially alter the rate of membrane-induced CO2 regeneration at a fixed HCO3− concentration, but progressively increased CO selectivity. For example, approximately 80% CO selectivity was obtained in 1.5 M CsHCO3 at 100 mA cm−2, compared with approximately 30% in saturated 0.84 M LiHCO3 under the same current density.21 Larger and more weakly hydrated alkali-metal cations can accumulate closer to the negatively polarized cathode, modify the interfacial electric field and water network, and stabilize polar *CO2, *COOH, or *OCHO like configurations. These effects remain catalyst- and potential-dependent and should not be conflated with changes in bulk carbon speciation. Hou et al. recently observed a volcano-shaped dependence of Cu-catalysed bicarbonate electroreduction on KHCO3 concentration using in situ Raman spectroscopy.61 At low concentrations, increasing KHCO3 enhanced reactivity by increasing the supply of the in situ generated CO2. At high concentrations, competitive adsorption of HCO3− and CO32− blocked sites required for CO adsorption and C–C coupling, while bicarbonate-mediated proton transfer promoted HER. An intermediate concentration therefore balanced carbon supply against interfacial anion crowding. Because varying KHCO3 concentration simultaneously changes K+ concentration, bicarbonate activity, ionic conductivity, water structure, and local pH, cation effects should ideally be examined by independently varying cation identity and concentration while controlling bicarbonate activity, ionic strength, carbon speciation, and mass transport.

Proton-coupled electron transfer further governs the conversion of adsorbed carbonate-derived species into reducible intermediates.62 Under alkaline conditions, free protons are scarce, and proton equivalents are mainly supplied by water, bicarbonate, surface hydroxyls, confined hydronium-like species or membrane-derived H+ flux. The identity, availability and orientation of these proton donors strongly influence product selectivity. Proton transfer to the carbon center can favor *OCHO formation and thus formate production, whereas protonation at oxygen, followed by C–O bond weakening or dehydration-like steps, may generate *COOH or *CO and open pathways toward CO formation.63 On Cu-based catalysts, accumulated *CO can further participate in C–C coupling and multicarbon product formation. However, maintaining sufficient *CO coverage from bicarbonate or carbonate feeds is generally more difficult than from molecular CO2, because anion activation, interfacial proton transfer and C–O bond reorganization introduce additional kinetic barriers. These elementary steps explain why direct (bi)carbonate conversion is mechanistically distinct from conventional CO2RR: the central challenge is not simply reducing a carbon-containing species, but activating a strongly solvated, negatively charged and acid–base-active anion within a dynamically evolving electrochemical double layer. Effective catalyst design must therefore integrate anion binding, desolvation, proton management, electron transfer and intermediate stabilization, rather than directly inheriting descriptors from gas-fed CO2RR.

A decisive advance in direct carbonate electroreduction was reported by Yeo and co-workers in their seminal study on the direct electroreduction of carbonate to formate (Fig. 6a).51 Prior to this work, carbonate was generally regarded as an electrochemically inert carbon sink generated during alkaline CO2 reduction, and its conversion was thought to proceed predominantly through indirect CO2 regeneration in bipolar-membrane electrolyzers. By combining in situ Raman spectroscopy, product analysis, 13C isotope labeling, deuterium labeling and DFT calculations, this study established that carbonate-derived species can adsorb on oxide-derived Cu as Cu–CO32− intermediates and subsequently undergo hydrogenation to formate. In particular, pulsed electrolysis in N2-purged K2CO3 electrolyte showed that carbonate anions could first be adsorbed at −0.05 V vs. RHE and then directly reduced at −0.50 V vs. RHE to formate, with isotope-labeling experiments confirming carbonate as the carbon source. Although the faradaic efficiency (FE) was still low, this work was conceptually important because it provided the first experimentally supported direct carbonate-to-formate pathway and transformed carbonate from a passive loss product into a potentially reducible carbon feedstock. Building on this mechanistic foundation, subsequent studies extended direct (bi)carbonate conversion to Sn-based systems (Fig. 6b–d). A thermo-electrochemical strategy on Sn foil generated a dynamic Snδ+/Sn interface that promoted direct bicarbonate hydrogenation to formate, achieving 83% FEformate and a jformate of 121 mA cm−2 in concentrated KHCO3 at 100 °C.64 More recently, oxygen-vacancy-rich SnO2 surfaces were shown to activate CO32− through strongly adsorbed *CO3 intermediates, enabling pulsed direct carbonate reduction to CO in a gas-diffusion-electrode configuration (Fig. 6e).55 Together, these studies mark the emergence of direct (bi)carbonate electroreduction as a distinct reaction regime, in which the key mechanistic challenge shifts from local CO2 regeneration to the interfacial adsorption, activation and sequential reduction of carbonate-derived anions.

Surface and lattice mediated carbonate pathways

Beyond the indirect regeneration of molecular CO2 and the direct reduction of freely solvated HCO3− or CO32−, surface- and lattice-mediated carbonate pathways are emerging as a third mechanistic regime. In these systems, carbonate species are not merely dissolved reactants, homogeneous buffers or regenerated CO2 precursors, but catalyst-bound carbon reservoirs embedded in, or strongly coupled to, the interfacial structure. Oxide, hydroxide, oxyhydroxide and oxycarbonate surfaces can bind carbonate through oxygen atoms, forming monodentate, bidentate or bridging motifs. These bound carbonate species may block active sites, modify interfacial electric fields, serve as spectators, or participate directly in catalytic turnover. Their role is particularly important for catalysts containing metal–oxygen frameworks, oxygen vacancies, hydroxylated domains, reconstructed oxides or carbonate-containing lattices, where carbonate adsorption can be coupled with vacancy filling, lattice oxygen participation, surface hydroxyl dynamics and metal redox processes. As a result, the active interface cannot be described by conventional molecular CO2 adsorption models alone, but should instead be viewed as a dynamic solid–liquid carbon reservoir in which carbonate binding, lattice exchange, surface reconstruction and product formation occur concurrently.

Representative examples illustrate how such catalyst-bound carbonate motifs can mediate electrochemical carbon conversion. Cu2(OH)2CO3 has been proposed to follow a lattice-carbonate-mediated pathway for CO2 electroreduction to ethylene, in which 13C isotope-labeling experiments indicated carbon exchange between CO2 and lattice carbonate, while in situ Raman spectroscopy and theoretical calculations suggested that carbonate-related vibrational activation facilitates CO2 activation and C–C coupling, leading to enhanced ethylene selectivity and activity compared with Cu2O.65 Under cathodic polarization, similar carbonate-containing motifs may become activated through vacancy formation, metal–oxygen bond polarization or local proton/electron transfer.66,67 Bi-based catalysts provide another instructive class of systems. BiOI-derived Bi2O2CO3 was shown to promote formate formation through a bismuth subcarbonate intermediate, preserving Bi–O structural motifs and facilitating HCO3− transport through enlarged interlayer spacing.68 In related reconstructed Bi oxide systems, carbonate-containing electrolytes can induce in situ carbonation and generate active Bi-based surfaces for CO2-to-formate conversion, underscoring that electrolyte-derived carbonate may participate in catalyst reconstruction rather than functioning solely as a homogeneous buffer.

A key feature of surface- and lattice-mediated pathways is that the catalyst-bound carbonate unit possesses solvation, geometry and electronic structure distinct from bulk HCO3− or CO32−. Such preorganization can lower the entropic penalty for activation, enrich carbon at the interface and couple carbon transfer to lattice oxygen, surface hydroxyls or redox-active metal centers. However, this mechanism also raises a critical stability question. If lattice carbonate is consumed faster than it is replenished, the observed activity may reflect transient depletion or irreversible phase transformation rather than sustained catalytic turnover. Distinguishing genuine lattice or strongly bound surface-carbon participation from simple carbonate adsorption therefore requires stringent evidence, including isotope-labeling proof of product incorporation, time-resolved isotope switching, depth-resolved isotope profiling, operando structural tracking and demonstration of reversible carbonate replenishment during continuous electrolysis. When these criteria are met, surface- and lattice-mediated carbonate pathways broaden the conceptual scope of direct (bi)carbonate electrolysis by integrating carbon capture, storage, activation and reduction within the catalyst structure itself, offering a potential route to maintain carbon-intermediate coverage under low molecular CO2 activity.

Potential pulsing provides temporal control over processes that cannot be simultaneously optimized under a constant cathodic bias. A less cathodic or relaxation pulse can decrease electrostatic exclusion of HCO3− or CO32−, replenish the interfacial carbon reservoir, redistribute cations and water within the electrical double layer, and desorb blocking species. The subsequent cathodic pulse drives proton-coupled electron transfer and product formation. This principle was demonstrated by Yeo51 and co-workers using −0.05 V vs. RHE for carbonate adsorption and −0.50 V vs. RHE for its reduction on oxide-derived Cu.69 Wang et al. extended this strategy to a dynamic Bi2O2CO3 cathode by alternating between 0 V vs. RHE for carbonate adsorption and −0.50 V vs. RHE for reduction.70 The pulsed potentials induced reversible interconversion between Bi2O2CO3 and a Bi/Bi2O2CO3 interface: Bi2O2CO3 favoured carbonate adsorption, whereas adjacent metallic Bi sites facilitated formate desorption. This adsorption–reduction–replenishment cycle maintained the reactive surface-carbonate reservoir, afforded a FEformate of approximately 24.1%, and enabled operation for more than 150 h. Potential pulsing can therefore couple anion replenishment with catalyst reactivation and dynamic phase regulation. Its effects depend on the pulse potentials, duration, frequency and duty cycle, which should be reported together with time-averaged current density, cell voltage and energy consumption. Time-resolved spectroscopy and isotope-resolved carbon balances are also required to distinguish sustained catalytic turnover from transient release of finite surface or lattice-carbon reservoirs.

Overall, (bi)carbonate electroconversion can proceed through three mechanistic modes: indirect conversion via local CO2 regeneration followed by conventional CO2RR, direct activation and reduction of solvated HCO3− or CO32−, and surface- or lattice-mediated pathways involving catalyst-bound carbonate reservoirs. Defining the operative mode in a given system requires rigorous mechanistic assignment based on isotopic, kinetic, transport and spectroscopic criteria, rather than product formation alone. First, isotope-labeling experiments are essential for identifying the true carbon source of the products.71 For instance, 13C-labeled bicarbonate or carbonate under 12CO2-free conditions can verify whether product carbon originates from the ionic feed, whereas introducing 13CO2 into an unlabeled bicarbonate or carbonate electrolyte can determine whether locally regenerated molecular CO2 serves as the dominant immediate reactant.72,73 Second, pressure-dependent measurements can help differentiate direct anion activation from an indirect CO2RR-like pathway.74 A strong dependence of reaction rate or product selectivity on external CO2 pressure generally indicates the involvement of molecular CO2, whereas weak pressure dependence may support direct activation of HCO3− or CO32−.29 Third, local pH measurements, together with finite-element transport simulations, are needed to evaluate whether sufficient CO2 can be regenerated near the cathode under realistic current densities, electrolyte compositions and membrane architectures.55 These three regimes should be treated as limiting mechanistic descriptions rather than mutually exclusive pathways. Their relative contributions depend on local pH, proton flux, carbonate concentration, catalyst identity, current density and membrane-regulated transport. A single electrolyzer may therefore shift between indirect, direct and catalyst-bound carbon conversion as operating conditions change. Assigning the dominant pathway requires identification of the immediate electroactive species rather than the ultimate carbon source, as discussed in Section 2.5.

Evidence hierarchy for pathway assignment

Product formation from a (bi)carbonate feed does not establish whether the immediate precursor was molecular CO2, a solvated anion or catalyst-bound carbonate. Pathway assignment should therefore combine carbon-source tracing with measurements of the local reaction environment. 13C-labelled HCO3− or CO32− verifies the origin of product carbon but cannot exclude prior formation of 13CO2. This experiment should be complemented by dissolved and evolved CO2 quantification, local-pH measurements and transport modelling to determine whether regenerated CO2 can account for the observed product rate. Pressure-dependent kinetics can further reveal sensitivity to molecular CO2 activity, while operando spectroscopy can identify interfacial carbonate species and catalyst reconstruction. Spectroscopic detection alone is insufficient because the observed carbonate may be a spectator or site-blocking species. For surface- or lattice-mediated pathways, isotope incorporation must be accompanied by reversible carbonate replenishment and sustained turnover to exclude sacrificial decomposition. Position-specific or transient isotope analysis is particularly important for C2+ products because different carbon atoms may originate from distinct carbon reservoirs. Competing reactions and selectivity constraints

(Bi)carbonate electrolysis is intrinsically challenged by a set of competing reactions and transport-driven losses that collectively limit product selectivity, carbon efficiency and long-term stability. The most universal competing reaction is the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER). Although alkaline media suppress proton-driven HER by lowering the concentration of freely available protons, water reduction remains kinetically accessible, particularly on catalyst surfaces that bind hydrogen strongly or generate locally proton-rich interfacial domains.75 This issue becomes particularly pronounced in (bi)carbonate electrolyzers that rely on local CO2 regeneration, where acidification or membrane-derived proton flux is deliberately introduced to convert HCO3− or CO32− into molecular CO2 near the cathode.76 Consequently, improving local CO2 availability can come at the expense of accelerated HER, creating an intrinsic trade-off between carbon-species regeneration and product selectivity. Therefore, suppressing HER in (bi)carbonate electrolysis requires not only weak H binding, but also precise control over local proton activity, interfacial water structure and electric-field distribution. For example, Fan et al. showed that intense HER in liquid-phase bicarbonate electrolysis can be mitigated by introducing a thin SiO2 buffer layer on the catalyst surface to restrict H+ transport.77 Coupled with an optimized KHCO3 concentration and a hydrophilic carbon-paper substrate that facilitated HCO3− diffusion and in situ CO2 generation, this strategy delivered a CO faradaic efficiency of 89% at a total current density of 50 mA cm−2 and a CO partial current density of 125 mA cm−2 at a total current density of 250 mA cm−2. In a complementary bipolar-membrane electrode assembly, Li et al. demonstrated that incorporating an anion-exchange ionomer into the catalyst layer improved local bicarbonate availability and increased the accessibility of regenerated CO2 to catalytic sites, approximately doubling both FECO and limiting current density.78 These examples show that HER suppression and CO2 utilization in bicarbonate electrolysis are governed by coupled proton delivery, bicarbonate transport, cation accumulation and catalyst-layer microenvironment, rather than by intrinsic catalyst activity alone.

Carbonate crossover represents another major selectivity and efficiency constraint, particularly in membrane-based electrolyzers. In anion-exchange-membrane configurations, HCO3− and CO32− can migrate across the membrane under the combined effects of concentration gradients, electric fields and reaction-generated OH−.79 This crossover removes carbon from the cathode, lowers single-pass carbon efficiency and complicates product quantification. Moreover, CO2 generated near the cathode or within the electrolyte may rapidly react with OH− to reform carbonate, establishing a parasitic carbon loop rather than productive electrochemical conversion. Bipolar membranes provide a strategy to locally acidify carbonate electrolytes and regenerate CO2 at the membrane interface, but they introduce a different constraint, the regenerated CO2 must be retained near the catalyst and reduced before it diffuses away, crosses compartments or is lost as gas. Thus, membrane architecture, ion selectivity, local acid–base management and electrode-membrane spacing are critical parameters for minimizing carbon loss while maintaining sufficient carbon availability at the cathode.

Salt precipitation differs substantially between gas-fed CO2 electrolysis and liquid-fed (bi)carbonate electrolysis.80,81 In gas-fed flow cells and zero-gap membrane electrode assembly (MEA), CO2 reacts with cathodically generated OH− to form HCO3− and CO32− within a thin electrolyte film.82,83 Simultaneous water evaporation and migration of alkali-metal cations toward the cathode can rapidly produce supersaturation inside the catalyst layer, microporous layer and gas channels. In alkaline gas-fed flow cells, carbonate deposits impaired gas-diffusion-electrode performance within 6 h at a current densities of 50 to 200 mA cm−2, with rapid loss of FECO after approximately 50% of the catalyst-layer surface was covered.84 A direct comparison using the same Ni single-atom catalyst at 200 mA cm−2 further showed that the FECO of a gas-fed AEM-based MEA decreased from approximately 100% to 73.9% within 4 h, followed by complete obstruction of CO2 transport and loss of CO production. In contrast, a capture-integrated bicarbonate-fed BPM configuration maintained a FECO above 90% for 18 h at the same current density. A liquid-fed bicarbonate-to-formate electrolyzer also maintained a nearly constant cell voltage for 44 h at 100 mA cm−2 while converting approximately 3.0 M KHCO3 to formate, suggesting that appreciable salt accumulation was avoided under these conditions.85

The lower precipitation tendency of liquid-fed systems arises from continuous electrochemical wetting process and convective removal or redissolution of salts, rather than from the absence of carbonate species.86 Salt formation remains possible when concentrated HCO3− or CO32− solutions enter poorly wetted or weakly circulated pores, particularly when membrane-mediated water transport, local OH− generation, product accumulation or cation crossover raises the local ion activity above the solubility limit. Low-solubility carbonates formed from Ca2+, Mg2+ or other impurities in capture solutions may further accumulate inside porous electrodes even when no deposits are visible on the external surface. Moreover, the durability advantage is not universal: an optimized gas-fed zero-gap electrolyzer operated for more than 144 h at 200 mA cm−2 without measurable salt deposition by controlling temperature, gas humidification, membrane thickness and cation identity.87 Conversely, bicarbonate electrolyzers can lose performance through carbon depletion, electrolyte-pH drift, trace-metal deposition, catalyst detachment and membrane degradation even when salt precipitation is limited.88 Meaningful comparisons should therefore use matched current densities, catalyst loadings, electrode structures and membrane configurations, and should report operation without periodic rinsing or electrolyte replacement. Cross-sectional SEM-EDS or X-ray diffraction, acid digestion followed by ion analysis, pressure-drop measurements and inlet–outlet ion balances are required to quantify deposits throughout the porous electrode rather than relying only on visual inspection.

Beyond these competing reactions and side processes, product selectivity is ultimately governed by the coverage, lifetime and coupling probability of key carbonaceous intermediates.89 C1 products such as CO and formate generally proceed through two-electron pathways and can form at relatively low coverages of the key intermediates (*COOH/*OCHO).90 In contrast, C2+ products require multiple PCET steps coupled with C–C bond formation, and therefore generally depend on high local coverages of CO or related C1 intermediates, typically on Cu-based or tandem catalytic surfaces.91 This requirement is difficult to meet in direct bicarbonate or carbonate electrolysis because the generation of reactive carbon intermediates is constrained by carbonate transport, desolvation, local CO2 regeneration, proton availability and sluggish C–O bond reorganization.92 As a result, direct (bi)carbonate electrolysis has more commonly produced C1 products, whereas selective C2+ formation remains substantially more challenging. Overcoming this limitation requires integrated control over reaction kinetics, ion transport and interfacial speciation. Tandem catalysts can couple carbonate-to-CO generation with downstream CO reduction; local CO2-regeneration strategies can increase CO2 availability near active sites; nanoconfinement can enrich carbon intermediates and prolong their residence times; cation engineering can tune electric fields and interfacial solvation; and lattice-carbon-mediated catalysis may provide dynamic interfacial carbon reservoirs. Collectively, these approaches show that selectivity in direct (bi)carbonate electrolysis is dictated by the interplay among HER, carbonate crossover, salt precipitation, carbon-species regeneration and intermediate coupling kinetics, requiring coordinated design of electrodes, membranes and local microenvironments to advance beyond C1 products toward efficient C2+ synthesis.

Catalyst design and interfacial engineering principles for (bi)carbonate reduction

General catalyst design guidelines

Catalyst design for (bi)carbonate electrolysis should not be regarded as a simple extension of gas-fed CO2RR.70 In conventional CO2RR, catalyst optimization primarily focuses on molecular CO2 activation and the adsorption of key intermediates such as *COOH, *OCHO, *CO and C–C coupling species.93 By contrast, (bi)carbonate electrolysis involves strongly hydrated HCO3− and CO32− anions, generally low interfacial proton activity, and a reaction environment governed by buffer equilibria, cation migration, electric fields and interfacial water structure. Efficient catalysts must therefore perform additional functions, including promoting anion approach and partial desolvation, stabilizing carbonate-derived intermediates, facilitating interfacial proton transfer, and enabling C–O bond reorganization. Surface motifs such as Lewis-acidic metal centers, oxygen vacancies, hydroxylated domains, undercoordinated atoms, positively polarized regions and cation-organizing interfaces may facilitate these processes by weakening anion hydration and polarizing carbonate-derived species.

A central design requirement is to balance carbonate binding with intermediate conversion and product release.94 If the interaction is too weak, HCO3− and CO32− remain predominantly solvated and carbon conversion relies mainly on locally regenerated CO2. Conversely, excessively strong binding can generate inert carbonate adlayers, block active sites, suppress the formation of *CO or *OCHO, promote salt accumulation and, particularly on oxides or oxide-derived metals, induce surface reconstruction. Productive catalysts should therefore preferentially stabilize reactive carbonate-derived configurations and transition states rather than spectator carbonate species.95 This volcano-like trade-off favors spatially integrated interfaces that couple moderately oxophilic adsorption sites with adjacent proton- and electron-transfer centers, including metal/oxide boundaries, defect-rich oxides, reconstructed oxyhydroxides, heteroatom-modified metals and molecularly functionalized surfaces.96 In such architectures, one site binds and polarizes the carbonate-derived species, while a neighbouring site mediates proton delivery or electron transfer to promote C–O reorganization and subsequent product formation. Rational catalyst screening should accordingly combine adsorption descriptors for O-bound carbonate species with kinetic descriptors for desolvation, proton transfer, intermediate conversion and product release.

Product-oriented catalyst platforms

Catalyst design should be tailored to the target product and the corresponding carbon-intermediate pathway.90 For formate production, the catalyst should preferentially stabilize O-bound *OCHO intermediates while suppressing competing *H and *CO pathways. Sn-, Bi-, In- and Pb-based materials are representative platforms because they favour oxygen-bound intermediates and generally bind *CO weakly.97,98 In (bi)carbonate electrolytes, oxide-derived Sn and Bi surfaces may be particularly advantageous, as residual oxide, hydroxide or oxycarbonate motifs can facilitate carbonate adsorption, interfacial proton transfer and *OCHO formation. Alloying, defect engineering and electrochemical reconstruction provide further means to tune the electronic structure, carbonate binding strength and local water-cation environment. The key objective is therefore to promote carbonate-derived *OCHO formation without inducing excessive hydrogen adsorption, carbonate passivation or structural instability.

For CO production, Ag-, Zn- and Au-based catalysts remain attractive because they stabilize *COOH sufficiently while enabling rapid *CO desorption.99,100 Under (bi)carbonate feeds, however, their activity depends not only on intrinsic *COOH energetics but also on the supply of reactive carbon to the interface. Because direct conversion of HCO3− or CO32− into *COOH-like intermediates is kinetically demanding, CO formation may rely substantially on local CO2 regeneration.101 Catalyst layers must therefore couple CO-selective surfaces with efficient proton delivery, CO2 retention and control over water activity, cation accumulation and carbonate blocking. Local acidification, confined electrolyte layers, tailored ionomers, porous solid electrolytes and carbonate-activating interfaces can all increase the availability of CO2 or reactive carbonate-derived species near the active sites. Accordingly, the performance of Ag- and Au-based systems is governed by the integrated catalyst-microenvironment architecture rather than by the metal surface alone.

The requirements are more stringent for C2+ products because C–C coupling demands a high local coverage of *CO or related intermediates.102 The lower immediate activity of molecular CO2 in carbonate feeds can limit *CO generation, while regenerated CO2 may escape before reduction. Effective Cu-based catalysts must therefore integrate carbon activation, *CO retention and C–C coupling within a confined reaction zone.103 Tandem architectures, such as Ag–Cu, Au–Cu, molecular-catalyst–Cu and oxide–Cu interfaces, can generate CO on one component and convert CO-derived intermediates on adjacent Cu ensembles.96 Nanoconfinement, porous catalyst layers and ionomer-controlled interfaces can further retain CO2 and CO, regulate local pH and ionic composition, and sustain the *CO coverage required for coupling. Grain boundaries, steps, defects and oxide-derived Cu sites may additionally modify *CO binding and coupling kinetics. The most promising systems should thus combine efficient (bi)carbonate-to-CO conversion, minimal carbon loss, a CO-retaining microenvironment and coupling-active Cu domains. Demonstrating a genuinely integrated carbonate-to-C2+ pathway will also require careful carbon-balance analysis to distinguish productive ionic-carbon conversion from pathways dominated by inefficient CO2 regeneration and escape.104

Engineering the microenvironment

The catalyst layer should be viewed as a microenvironmental reactor in which ionomers, binders, cations, pore structure, wettability and local water activity collectively regulate carbon transport, proton availability and interfacial reaction kinetics. Hydrophobic domains can suppress flooding and retain locally generated CO2, whereas hydrophilic regions sustain ionic conductivity and facilitate HCO3− or CO32− transport. Alkali-metal cations can stabilize charged transition states, polarize adsorbed intermediates and reorganize interfacial water, but excessive cation accumulation may distort the electric double layer, promote carbonate precipitation and block pores. Similarly, polymer functional groups, buffer additives and confined electrolyte films can provide proton relays, yet excessive proton availability may enhance HER or favour indirect CO2 regeneration over productive ionic-carbon conversion. An effective catalyst layer for (bi)carbonate electrolysis must therefore balance anion transport, carbon retention, controlled proton transfer, product removal and structural stability.

Solvation engineering provides a possible route to reduce the kinetic penalty associated with strongly hydrated HCO3− and CO32−. Because anion hydration is coupled to counter-cation solvation and the interfacial hydrogen-bond network, this environment can be modified without directly changing the catalyst. Crown ethers and related chelating molecules can selectively coordinate alkali-metal cations and reorganize their hydration shells. For example, Zhang et al. showed that 18-crown-6 coordination to K+ reduced interfacial water adsorption, strengthened carbonate-related interactions and decreased the apparent cation-desolvation barrier from 83.4 to 32.1 kJ mol−1 in a CO2-fed KHCO3 electrolyte, accompanied by a 7.3-fold increase in FECO.105 Polar aprotic additives can provide a complementary strategy by competing for hydrogen bonding with water. In a NaHCO3-DMSO electrolyte, DMSO decreased the connectivity of the interfacial water network, altered proton-transfer kinetics and increased the FEC2 from 9.3% to 57% at 50 mA cm−2 during conventional CO2RR.106 Molecularly grafted surface layers can similarly decrease cation hydration and restructure interfacial water near the electrode. These approaches have not yet established direct activation of HCO3− or CO32−, but they suggest that modifying cation–water–anion interactions may facilitate partial desolvation, anion approach and productive adsorption.107 The effect is unlikely to be monotonic: excessive cation complexation or disruption of water structure may reduce ionic conductivity, weaken electric-field stabilization, suppress the proton transfer required for reduction, shift carbonate equilibria or impair membrane compatibility. Solvation modifiers should therefore be evaluated using operando spectroscopy, kinetic isotope measurements and explicit-solvent simulations, together with controls that distinguish direct ionic-carbon activation from enhanced local CO2 regeneration.

Electrolyte composition provides an additional level of control over this microenvironment. Bicarbonate electrolytes can buffer local pH and serve as reservoirs for interfacial CO2 regeneration, but they also consume OH−, modify ionic strength and compete for adsorption sites. Carbonate electrolytes offer higher alkalinity and carbon-storage capacity, yet stronger ion pairing and a greater tendency towards salt precipitation can limit mass transport and durability. Overall, microenvironment engineering can tune catalytic performance without changing the bulk catalyst composition. Rather than maximizing a single property, such as hydrophobicity, cation concentration or proton activity, the catalyst layer should be designed with deliberate spatial heterogeneity. Distinct but interconnected regions are needed for ionic transport, carbon retention, proton management and catalytic turnover. The central design objective is to integrate these functions within a stable porous architecture that sustains reactive carbonate near active sites while minimizing flooding, salt accumulation and parasitic hydrogen evolution under high current density.

Data-driven and high-throughput discovery

Artificial intelligence and high-throughput computation offer powerful routes to accelerate catalyst discovery for (bi)carbonate electroreduction.108 However, predictive models must capture the complete ionic-carbon reaction network rather than relying solely on isolated adsorption energies or descriptors transferred directly from conventional CO2 reduction. A multiscale discovery workflow should integrate electric-field- and solvation-aware density functional theory, explicit or machine-learned representations of interfacial water and electrolyte ions, microkinetic modeling, and continuum-scale mass and charge transport. Relevant descriptors may include carbonate approach and partial desolvation free energies, preferred interfacial adsorption configurations, proton-transfer and C–O bond-reorganization barriers, selectivity between *OCHO and *COOH pathways, *CO formation and retention, local proton availability, and susceptibility to carbonate-induced site blocking, precipitation or surface reconstruction. Such models should ideally predict not only catalytic activity and selectivity, but also the conditions under which carbonate functions as a reactant, a CO2 reservoir or a passivating species.109

The principal limitation remains the availability and consistency of high-quality data.110,111 Carbonate-specific computational and experimental datasets are still small, operating conditions are highly heterogeneous, and the catalytically active surfaces may reconstruct dynamically under reaction conditions. Models trained predominantly on gas-fed CO2 reduction data may therefore extrapolate poorly to concentrated ionic environments, where solvation, ion pairing, electric fields and local acid–base equilibria strongly influence the reaction pathway. Reliable model development consequently requires rigorous uncertainty quantification, active-learning strategies, standardized metadata and the inclusion of negative or low-performance experimental results. Closed-loop platforms that combine automated catalyst synthesis, high-throughput electrochemical testing, operando characterization and model-guided candidate selection could progressively refine both the descriptor space and the underlying mechanistic hypotheses. Ultimately, this approach may enable the inverse design of integrated catalyst, ionomer and electrolyte, reactor assemblies, rather than the optimization of catalyst composition in isolation.

Reactor architectures, benchmarking and capture integration

Membranes and reactor configurations

Reactor architecture determines whether bicarbonate and carbonate function predominantly as electroactive reactants, mobile carbon reservoirs or sources of carbon loss. Membrane selection is particularly important because it governs ionic charge transport, local acid–base equilibria, carbon-species redistribution and the location at which molecular CO2 may be regenerated. Anion-exchange membranes support alkaline operation and generally provide high ionic conductivity, but they also permit HCO3− and CO32− transport towards the anode, where carbonate-derived carbon may be released as CO2 or retained in the anolyte. Cation-exchange membranes can restrict anion crossover, yet their use under alkaline conditions may generate steep pH gradients, complicate charge compensation and increase ohmic resistance. Bipolar membranes provide a local proton flux through water dissociation, enabling HCO3− or CO32− to be converted into CO2 near the cathode and thereby sustaining high reaction rates through an indirect pathway. However, the additional voltage required for water dissociation, together with CO2 desorption, membrane crossover and gas-phase escape, must be included explicitly in both energy and carbon balances.

Representative reactor configurations are illustrated in Fig. 7a. In a liquid-fed flow cell, carbonate-containing catholyte is circulated through the cathode compartment and separated from the anodic catalyst by an anion-exchange membrane. This configuration facilitates liquid-phase transport of HCO3− or CO32− to the cathode and allows independent control of electrolyte composition and flow rate, but carbonate crossover, catholyte composition drift and salt accumulation may limit carbon utilization and long-term stability. In a zero-gap AEM membrane-electrode assembly (Fig. 7b), the cathodic and anodic catalyst layers are placed directly against the membrane, shortening ionic transport distances and reducing ohmic losses. The confined interface can improve current density and limit bulk electrolyte resistance, although carbonate migration through the membrane and subsequent anodic CO2 release remain important carbon-loss pathways.

Porous solid-electrolyte reactors (Fig. 7c) spatially separate CO2 regeneration, cathodic reduction and anodic oxidation. The middle layer must simultaneously provide electronic insulation, low-resistance ionic conduction, uniform proton and liquid distribution, and connected gas–liquid pathways for CO2-bubble nucleation and removal. It must also maintain intimate contact with the adjacent ion-exchange membranes without blocking their surfaces. In the reported indirect (bi)carbonate reactor, the middle chamber was packed with 200 to 400 mesh Dowex cation-exchange resin.38 Sulfonated ion-exchange polymers are suitable because their hydrated ionic domains provide continuous proton- and cation-conduction pathways, whereas their interparticle voids permit liquid permeation and gas disengagement. Material selection should nevertheless be based on ionic conductivity measured under the relevant hydration and carbonate-loading conditions rather than on dry-state conductivity alone. Layer thickness, particle size, packing density, water uptake and compression jointly determine the area-specific resistance. Excessive compaction or small pores increase pressure drop and can retain CO2 bubbles, whereas heterogeneous packing or large pores promote liquid channeling, local drying and incomplete acidification.

Salt and water management impose an additional materials constraint. At 150 mA cm−2, the reported PSE bicarbonate electrolyzer exhibited flooding and salt accumulation at the back of the gas-diffusion electrode with 0.5 M KHCO3, while 1.0 M KHCO3 completely disrupted operation.38 Thus, spatially separating CO2 regeneration does not eliminate salt precipitation, particularly when carbonate consumption, proton flux, liquid flow and CO2 removal are not locally balanced. Flow rates should be matched to the stoichiometric proton flux, while uniform liquid distribution, sufficient pore connectivity and periodic water flushing should be evaluated to prevent stagnant supersaturated regions. These measures introduce trade-offs with pumping energy, single-pass conversion and product concentration. The packed-resin reactor operated for 100 h at 100 mA cm−2, but longer tests are required to distinguish catalyst or membrane degradation from PSE-specific failure through resin attrition, swelling, pore blockage, loss of membrane contact and irreversible salt deposition.49 As an alternative to loose resin particles, a free-standing composite containing poly(styrene-divinylbenzene) ion-exchange particles in a sulfonated-polysulfone matrix achieved an ionic conductivity of 10.4 mS cm−1 and maintained operation for more than 220 h at 100 mA cm−2.112 Although demonstrated in gas-fed CO2 electrolysis rather than bicarbonate regeneration, this result shows how immobilizing ion-exchange particles can improve mechanical integrity and reproducibility without sacrificing ionic conduction. Future studies should report pressure drop, water balance, area-specific resistance, CO2 recovery, salt location and mass, and post-test changes in porosity and ionic conductivity over at least 1000 h under realistic capture-solution impurities and start–stop operation.

The optimal configuration therefore depends on the intended carbon-conversion pathway. For indirect conversion, the reactor should promote controlled CO2 regeneration near the cathode and ensure that the regenerated CO2 is consumed before it desorbs, crosses the membrane or leaves the device. This requires matching the proton flux, carbonate supply and catalytic consumption rate while minimizing the energetic penalty associated with local acidification. By contrast, reactors designed for direct ionic-carbon activation should maintain low free-CO2 activity, preserve alkaline or locally alkaline conditions, deliver HCO3− or CO32− efficiently to active sites and provide controlled proton donors without inducing extensive CO2 evolution. Surface- or lattice-mediated pathways impose an additional requirement: the device must sustain continuous replenishment of catalyst-bound carbonate reservoirs without causing irreversible phase transformation or carbonate depletion.

Performance metrics and carbon accounting

The literature landscape summarized in Fig. 8a reveals a clear evolution of (bi)carbonate electroreduction from early proof-of-concept studies toward increasingly selective and device-oriented systems. CO and formate remain the most extensively investigated and best-performing products, as indicated by their high data density and generally high faradaic efficiencies. Since 2022, several systems have achieved faradaic efficiencies above 60%, particularly those based on Ag, Bi and Sn nanocatalysts, Ni single-atom catalysts and molecular catalyst platforms, reflecting rapid advances in catalyst design and interfacial regulation. This product distribution is consistent with the comparatively low kinetic and interfacial demands of two-electron C1 pathways. CO is typically formed through locally regenerated CO2 and the conventional *COOH intermediate, whereas formate generally proceeds through an oxygen-bound *OCHO intermediate stabilized on oxyphilic metals such as Sn, Bi and In. Compared with deep reduction and C–C coupling, these pathways require lower intermediate coverages and less stringent proton management, helping to explain the predominance of CO and formate in (bi)carbonate electrolysis.

In parallel, the field has progressively moved from conventional H-cell measurements toward membrane-based flow electrolyzers and membrane-electrode assemblies incorporating BPMs, CEMs or AEMs. This transition reflects the growing recognition that (bi)carbonate conversion is governed not only by intrinsic catalytic activity, but also by ion transport, local pH gradients, carbon-species regeneration and membrane-controlled crossover. The stability and current density map in Fig. 8b further illustrates the product-dependent maturity of the field. CO and formate cover the broadest ranges of current density and operating time, confirming that two-electron C1 products are currently the most developed targets. CO production has progressed from low-current screening to approximately 100–300 mA cm−2, particularly on Ag, Ni single-atom and CoPc-based catalysts, although prolonged operation at high current density remains uncommon. Formate exhibits even greater rate capability, with several Bi-, Sn-, In- and carbonate-derived catalysts approaching 300–500 mA cm−2, but these high-rate demonstrations are often limited to relatively short stability tests.

By contrast, CH4, C2H4 and other C2+ products remain considerably less developed, with fewer reports and generally lower or more scattered faradaic efficiencies. Recent Cu-based and bimetallic systems demonstrate that multicarbon formation from carbonate-derived feeds is feasible, but sustained C2+ selectivity remains limited by insufficient *CO coverage, sluggish C–C coupling and competing hydrogen evolution under low CO2-activity conditions. Unlike gas-fed CO2RR, HCO3− and CO32− must undergo local protonation, interfacial CO2 regeneration or direct surface activation before entering reductive pathways, inherently favouring kinetically accessible C1 products. CH4 and C2+ formation instead require deeper hydrogenation or C–C coupling, together with controlled proton delivery and high, sustained coverages of *CO or related C1 intermediates. These conditions are difficult to maintain because slow ionic-carbon transport, limited CO2 regeneration, strong buffering and carbonate migration restrict the accumulation of reactive intermediates. Insufficient proton flux suppresses hydrogenation, whereas excessive local acidification promotes hydrogen evolution and carbon loss.

Durability is further constrained by coupled catalyst- and device-level instabilities. Carbonate or bicarbonate crossover depletes the cathodic carbon reservoir, while continuous OH− generation, pH gradients, cation accumulation and salt precipitation alter interfacial speciation and mass transport.68 High-current operation additionally intensifies gas evolution, concentration polarization and ohmic losses.113 Meanwhile, catalyst surfaces may reconstruct, dissolve, aggregate or become blocked by carbonate deposits under strongly alkaline and high-flux conditions. Overall, (bi)carbonate electrolysis has reached an intermediate level of maturity for two-electron C1 products, particularly CO and formate, whereas CH4 and C2+ products remain unresolved targets. Progress beyond C1 chemistry will require integrated catalyst-microenvironment-electrolyzers design that simultaneously regulates carbon supply, proton activity, intermediate coverage, ion transport and long-term structural stability.

However, faradaic efficiency and partial current density alone are insufficient for evaluating (bi)carbonate electrolysis.114 The initial carbon inventory may be distributed among target products, residual dissolved inorganic carbon, released CO2, membrane-crossed carbonate species, precipitated salts and catalyst- or lattice-bound carbon reservoirs. High product selectivity may therefore coexist with poor overall carbon utilization when most captured carbon is released, transported across the membrane or retained within the reactor. Rigorous assessment consequently requires a closed carbon balance alongside conventional electrochemical metrics. At a minimum, the dissolved inorganic carbon content and composition of the catholyte feed should be quantified before electrolysis. During operation, gas-phase CO2, CO and hydrocarbon products should be monitored continuously or at defined intervals, while liquid products, residual bicarbonate/carbonate, precipitated carbonates and carbon transferred to the anolyte should be quantified after electrolysis. For flow reactors, all inlet and outlet carbon streams should be integrated over time rather than estimated from a single endpoint. Reported metrics should include faradaic efficiency, partial current density, product concentration, cell voltage, energy efficiency, carbon conversion, single-pass carbon efficiency, carbon utilization efficiency and carbon-balance closure, together with the long-term evolution of voltage, selectivity and carbon distribution. Definitions and system boundaries should be stated explicitly to enable meaningful comparisons among reactor configurations.

Capture-integrated operation and direct-air boundary conditions

Direct air-to-chemicals is an appealing long-term objective for coupling atmospheric CO2 removal with chemical production, but its feasibility is fundamentally constrained by the low CO2 concentration and correspondingly limited carbon-capture flux of air.115 The electrolysis rate must therefore be matched to the rate of atmospheric CO2 uptake; otherwise, depletion of the available carbon reservoir will shift the cathodic reaction towards water reduction and hydrogen evolution. This constraint distinguishes direct air conversion from point-source or concentrated capture-stream electrolysis and implies that a compact electrolyzer may still require a large-area air contactor, efficient gas–liquid mass transfer, thin liquid films and a capture medium with sufficiently rapid uptake and high working capacity. Direct air conversion should therefore be evaluated as an integrated absorber–electrolyzer process rather than by extrapolating the performance of a carbonate-fed cell supplied from a pre-concentrated reservoir.116,117

The choice of capture medium strongly influences both CO2 uptake and downstream electrochemical conversion (Fig. 9a and b).118 Amine-, hydroxide- and carbonate-based liquids provide distinct opportunities and limitations. Primary and secondary amines can bind CO2 as carbamates, whereas tertiary and sterically hindered amines generally favour bicarbonate formation in aqueous media, making amine solutions attractive for low-concentration capture. However, amines are not electrochemically inert. They can adsorb on catalyst surfaces, alter proton availability and double-layer structure, modify the solvation environment of carbonate-derived species, cross membranes and undergo cathodic reduction, anodic oxidation or long-term degradation. Volatility, oxidative decomposition, corrosion and solvent loss further complicate continuous operation. Amine-integrated electrolysis therefore requires compatibility among the capture agent, catalyst, ionomer, membrane and operating potential window.

Hydroxide solutions such as KOH and NaOH offer rapid CO2 absorption, high ionic conductivity and comparatively simple solution chemistry.119 Nevertheless, their strong alkalinity shifts the carbon inventory towards CO32−, suppresses free CO2 activity and proton availability, and can promote carbonate precipitation under high-current operation. Hydroxide-derived capture streams are therefore best matched with catalysts and reactor architectures capable of directly activating HCO3−/CO32− or regenerating and immediately consuming CO2 within a confined cathodic environment. Carbonate and bicarbonate electrolytes, including KHCO3, K2CO3, NaHCO3 and Na2CO3, provide more controlled platforms for independently varying carbon loading, pH, cation identity and buffer capacity. They are consequently valuable for mechanistic studies that distinguish local CO2 regeneration from direct ionic-carbon activation. However, purified model electrolytes do not reproduce realistic capture streams, which may contain mixed ions, residual amines, solvent-degradation products, dissolved O2, SOx- and NOx-derived species, metal impurities and variable water contents. Model electrolytes are therefore most useful for mechanistic clarification, whereas authentic capture liquids are essential for validating technological robustness.120,121

Liquid-fed (bi)carbonate electrolysis can be more tolerant than gas-fed CO2 electrolysis to poorly soluble gaseous impurities, particularly O2, because the capture solution acts as a selective carbon reservoir rather than simply changing the physical phase of the feed.24 During alkaline absorption, CO2 is stored as bicarbonate or carbonate, whereas N2 and O2 are not concentrated in an analogous ionic form. Interfacial acidification in the electrolyzer subsequently regenerates CO2 from the dissolved inorganic carbon reservoir without concomitantly generating O2. Combined with the low aqueous solubility of O2, this process increases the local CO2/O2 ratio at the cathode relative to that obtained by directly feeding an O2-containing gas to a gas-diffusion electrode.112 The resulting suppression of oxygen reduction has been demonstrated for both CO and multicarbon production. At 300 mA cm−2, the FEC2+ of a gas-fed membrane electrode assembly decreased from 63.4% under pure CO2 to 19.8%, 0.7% and 0.1% after introducing 5%, 10% and 20% O2, respectively.111 By contrast, the corresponding bicarbonate-derived system maintained nearly unchanged C2+ selectivity and cell voltage when the gas used to prepare the bicarbonate solution contained up to 20% O2. Similarly, only 0.5% O2 in a gaseous CO2 feed decreased CO selectivity by more than 90% after 1.0 h, whereas a 3.0 M KHCO3 liquid feed maintained approximately 65% CO selectivity at 100 mA cm−2 even after exposure to O2. These results show that bicarbonate formation followed by selective CO2 regeneration can buffer the cathode against O2 contamination.

This tolerance is selective rather than universal and depends on both impurity chemistry and reactor configuration. Dissolved O2 is not completely excluded and may still undergo oxygen reduction; depending on the catalyst, the two-electron pathway can produce H2O2 and modify the interfacial reaction environment.122 More generally, reactive oxygen species present in bicarbonate electrolytes can alter catalyst states. Hydroxyl radicals formed in KHCO3 solutions, for example, were shown to drive the dynamic reoxidation of oxide-derived Cu.123 The extent of O2 interference therefore depends on gas–liquid equilibration, O2 solubility, residence time, carbon loading and whether the capture liquid contacts the cathode before selective CO2 regeneration. More soluble or chemically reactive flue-gas components are not attenuated by the same mechanism. SOx and NOx react with alkaline capture solutions to form sulfite, sulfate, nitrite and nitrate, which remain in the liquid and can accumulate during repeated capture–electrolysis cycles. Their effects are strongly species-dependent: sulfite and sulfate had little effect on CO production from 3.0 M KHCO3, whereas 2000 ppm nitrite or nitrate decreased CO selectivity from approximately 60% to below 5.0% because these species were preferentially reduced over CO2.124

Capture-agent compatibility introduces an additional constraint. Amines, amino acids and their associated ionic species can restructure the electrical double layer or adsorb on catalytic sites, such that a sorbent suitable for CO2 absorption may not be compatible with a given electrocatalyst.117 For example, adding 0.3 M potassium glycinate to 0.5 M KHCO3 decreased the FECO of Ag from 66.4% to 34.4% at 100 mA cm−2 because of glycine adsorption and active-site blocking.125 This effect is catalyst-dependent: an amino-acid-tolerant Ni–N3 molecular catalyst delivered an FECO of 83% at 100 mA cm−2 after capture from a simulated flue gas containing 15% CO2, 10% O2, 500 ppm NO and 500 ppm SO2, matching the selectivity obtained after capture from the corresponding impurity-free CO2 feed.88 Inorganic impurities may also limit durability. Trace metal ions in concentrated bicarbonate electrolytes can deposit on the cathode and progressively suppress CO selectivity, whereas complexation with EDTA prevents metal deposition and improves stability. Multivalent cations can additionally precipitate as poorly soluble carbonates under locally alkaline conditions, potentially fouling porous electrodes and membranes.126,127

Impurity tolerance should therefore be evaluated through sustained conversion of authentic or compositionally representative capture streams, rather than short-term exposure to an isolated impurity.128,129 Future studies should report impurity concentrations and chemical speciation before absorption, after capture and after electrolysis; quantify impurity accumulation, membrane crossover and incorporation into products; and monitor catalyst structure, membrane resistance, solvent composition and capture capacity over repeated cycles. Comparisons with gas-fed CO2 electrolysis should employ matched current densities, catalyst platforms and reactor configurations to distinguish genuine chemical tolerance from impurity dilution or upstream rejection.

Direct feeding of a capture solution, however, does not by itself establish a closed process. Electrochemical conversion changes carbon speciation, proton inventories and ion balances and may progressively deplete the alkalinity required for subsequent CO2 uptake. The electrolyzer outlet must therefore retain or regenerate sufficient capture capacity, while solvent composition, water balance and charge-carrying ions remain stable over repeated absorption–electrolysis cycles. Parasitic CO2 release, carbonate crossover, salt accumulation, solvent degradation and make-up chemical requirements must all be included in the process balance. Accordingly, the term “direct air-to-chemicals” should be reserved for systems that quantitatively integrate atmospheric CO2 uptake, carbon transport, electrochemical conversion and capture-medium regeneration within a clearly defined boundary. Relevant performance metrics should include atmospheric capture flux, product formation rate, carbon utilization, solvent working capacity, regeneration energy, impurity tolerance, cycle stability and net carbon removal. Only system-level assessment can establish whether the process achieves genuine air-derived chemical production rather than merely converting a separately accumulated carbonate reservoir.

As shown by Fig. 9c and d, Almajed et al. recently emphasized that the integration of direct air capture (DAC) with (bi)carbonate electrolysis must be evaluated as a closed-loop capture-conversion process rather than as an isolated electrolyzer.109,130 The mass-balance, microkinetic and techno-economic analysis showed that the electrolyzer outlet often cannot regenerate the high-pH solvent required for efficient CO2 recapture. In a simulated K2CO3-based loop, the catholyte outlet pH decreased from 11.62 to 9.35, while the CO2 capture fraction dropped from 78.34% to 0.52%; after five iterations, the capture fraction was already below 1%. This rapid performance decay originates from HCO3− accumulation, incomplete solvent regeneration and the mismatch between the mildly alkaline conditions preferred for bicarbonate electrolysis and the strongly alkaline solutions required for rapid air capture. Therefore, future integrated systems must couple catalyst and electrolyzer design with solvent regeneration, pH management, ion balance and air-contactor sizing, potentially through electrodialysis, acid/base adjustment, dehydration or thermal stripping.

Previous system-level analyses show that direct (bi)carbonate feeding does not necessarily provide an intrinsic economic advantage over gas-fed CO2 electrolysis. In a DAC-to-syngas comparison, Moreno-Gonzalez et al. estimated production costs of $1.90 kg−1 for direct (bi)carbonate electrolysis and $1.30 kg−1 for gas-fed CO2 electrolysis.131 The avoided CO2 regeneration and compression steps were insufficient to compensate for the higher cell voltage, lower FECO and membrane cost assumed for the (bi)carbonate electrolyzer. By contrast, Debergh et al. included DAC and downstream CO2 recovery within the system boundary and found that a capture-integrated carbonate route gave a levelized syngas cost 9.0% lower than a porous-solid-electrolyte route and 15% lower than a membrane-electrode-assembly route.132 Xiao et al. also reported an energy intensity of 52 GJ t−1 of syngas for direct carbonate electrolysis, 13% below that of combined gas-fed CO2 and water electrolysis when upstream purification and downstream CO2 separation were included.37 These different outcomes indicate that direct (bi)carbonate electrolysis shifts, rather than eliminates, energy and cost. Thermal CO2 release, purification and compression are replaced by bipolar-membrane water dissociation, electricity consumption, membrane and electrolyzer area, and capture-medium management. Meaningful comparisons therefore require matched products and consistent accounting of capture, CO2 recovery, cell voltage, faradaic efficiency, current density, durability and solvent regeneration. Here, direct feeding denotes the use of a capture-derived (bi)carbonate solution without external CO2 regeneration and does not necessarily indicate direct electron transfer to HCO3− or CO32−.

Overall, future development should therefore treat the capture liquid, catalyst, membrane and reactor as an integrated unit. Catalyst surfaces must be designed for the actual carbon species present in capture media, not only for pure CO2. Membranes must regulate proton and ion transport without sacrificing carbon retention. Electrolyzers must maintain stable carbonate concentration, avoid salt deposition and enable efficient product separation. Capture units must supply carbon at a rate compatible with electrochemical conversion. When these components are optimized together, direct (bi)carbonate electrolysis could transform captured carbon from a regenerated gas feedstock into a directly usable liquid-phase reactant. This would provide a more coherent route toward capture-integrated carbon recycling, decentralized chemical synthesis and direct air-to-chemicals electrolysis.

Conclusion and outlook

Electrochemical conversion of bicarbonate and carbonate can connect carbon capture with chemical synthesis without requiring complete thermal regeneration of purified CO2. Its defining mechanistic question is the identity of the immediate electroactive carbon species. Conversion may proceed through locally regenerated CO2, direct activation of solvated HCO3− or CO32−, or catalyst-bound carbonate reservoirs. These pathways can coexist, but they impose different requirements on catalyst structure, proton delivery and reactor design. Progress in this field requires pathway-resolved catalyst and interface design. Indirect systems must balance CO2 regeneration, retention and consumption while limiting HER and CO2 escape. Direct conversion requires controlled anion desolvation, productive carbonate adsorption and proton-coupled C–O bond reorganization. Surface- or lattice-mediated systems must sustain reversible carbonate exchange rather than transiently consume a finite carbon reservoir. Across all three regimes, the catalyst layer should integrate carbon delivery, proton management, intermediate retention and product removal without promoting flooding, salt accumulation or membrane crossover.

Practical performance should be assessed using closed carbon and energy balances rather than faradaic efficiency alone. Partial current density of the product, full-cell voltage, single-pass carbon efficiency, CO2 release, crossover, salt deposition and long-term selectivity should be reported within clearly defined system boundaries. For capture-integrated operation, the decisive test is whether the electrolyte retains sufficient alkalinity and working capacity over repeated capture–electrolysis cycles. Establishing this closed-loop operation will determine whether (bi)carbonate electrolysis becomes a practical carbon-utilization technology or remains primarily a method for coupling CO2 release with conventional electroreduction.
