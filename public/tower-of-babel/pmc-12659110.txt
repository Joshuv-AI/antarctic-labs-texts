Interplay among lipoprotein(a), hepatic and vascular damage in individuals with metabolic dysfunction
Authors: Serena Pelusi, Chiara Macchi, Francesco Malvestiti, Sara Margarita, Irene De Matteis, Giulia Periti, Jessica Rondena, Stefania Mira, Francesca Iemma, Martina Tranchina, Barbara Nardi, Carla Lucci, Cesare R Sirtori, Oscar Millet, Jose M Mato, Giovanni Targher, Daniele Prati, Massimiliano Ruscica, Luca Valenti, Milan Biobank Investigators
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relationship between plasma lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] levels and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) remains unclear. The aim of this study was to examine the combined effects of Lp(a) levels on liver and vascular damage.MethodsThe study was conducted using the Liver-Bible cohort of individuals with metabolic dysfunction (n = 859, 808 with genomic information) and the Milan Biobank (n = 6963). Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and polygenic risk scores (PRS) were used to evaluate the inherited factors influencing plasma Lp(a) levels.ResultsIn the Liver-Bible cohort, genetic variation in the LPA gene was the strongest determinant of Lp(a), followed by liver stiffness measurement (LSM). Additionally, circulating Lp(a) levels, but not genetic predisposition, were inversely related to LSM, suggesting that MASLD severity may affect Lp(a) secretion. Among participants with more severe insulin resistance (n = 250), Lp(a) levels (odds ratio 6.7, 95% CI 1.0–53.0, p = 0.046) and LSM (odds ratio 13.7, 95% CI 1.4–172.2, p = 0.023) were associated with greater prevalence of carotid atherosclerotic plaques, regardless of traditional cardiovascular risk factors. In the Milan Biobank, genetically predicted higher Lp(a) levels tended to increase the risk of liver-related outcomes, whereas genetically predicted MASLD was associated with lower circulating Lp(a) levels.ConclusionsThe results of this study suggest that liver damage is more likely the cause of reduced plasma Lp(a) levels rather than a consequence. Assessing plasma Lp(a) levels and the extent of liver damage could improve the prediction of vascular damage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-025-03004-z.Keywords: MASLD, Fibrosis, Atherosclerosis, Lipoprotein, Liver stiffness measurement

Background

Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is a unique hybrid lipoprotein composed of a low-density lipoprotein (LDL)-like particle containing one molecule of apolipoprotein (apo)B-100 covalently bound to apo(a). The structure of Lp(a) includes repeats of an unusual “kringle” motif [1].

Lp(a) is the most polymorphic lipoprotein, exhibiting a huge variability in its circulating levels that spans over three log10 units in adults. Lp(a) is recognized as a genetically codominant inherited quantitative trait, with its levels largely determined by genetic variations in the LPA gene, which encodes apolipoprotein(a) [2]. The Lp(a) gene locus (LPA) accounts for up to approximately 90% of the variance in protein levels, caused by "copy number variations" (CNV) in the number of Kringle-IV 2 (KIV-2) domain repeats and, to a lesser extent, by single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within and near this gene, as well as by loss-of-function mutations [3]. Once reaching the genetically determined adult levels, plasma Lp(a) levels tend to remain stable over time and are minimally affected by lifestyle factors [4]. Indeed, by 15 months of age, circulating Lp(a) levels stabilize at adult concentrations, which correlate with parental levels [5]. However, menopausal women may have ~ 17% higher plasma levels of Lp(a) [6].

Elevated Lp(a) levels (defined as > 50 mg/dL or 105 nmol/L) [7] are an under-recognized risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and aortic stenosis, and are detected in up to one in five individuals [8]. Despite clinical guidelines recommending measuring plasma Lp(a) levels at least once in a lifetime [9], implementation is still lacking [10], but it may become more widespread if pharmacological approaches targeting Lp(a) effectively reduce long-term clinical outcomes.

The impact of plasma Lp(a) levels on cardiovascular risk is independent of traditional risk factors, such as hypertension, smoking, type 2 diabetes (T2D), obesity, and high LDL cholesterol (LDL-C) [11]. Although environmental and lifestyle factors have a minimal influence on plasma Lp(a) levels, certain pathological conditions (such as kidney disease, liver disease, hypothyroidism, acute inflammation, or rheumatoid arthritis) may affect Lp(a) levels. Among these conditions, an inverse relationship also exists between Lp(a) and T2D, although the role of inherited KIV-2 repeats in T2D risk remains debated [12, 13]. Since both metabolic syndrome and T2D are characterized by insulin resistance, individuals with metabolic syndrome tend to have lower circulating Lp(a) levels than those without metabolic syndrome [14].

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), which is becoming the leading cause of chronic liver disease worldwide, appears to be inversely related to Lp(a) levels [15], with lower levels found in patients with MASLD and advanced liver fibrosis [16]. However, it remains unclear whether reduced circulating Lp(a) levels are a cause or a consequence of liver disease, as current evidence shows a bidirectional relationship between genetically predicted Lp(a) levels and the risk of liver disease [17].

In this context, the present study aimed to characterize the clinical and genetic factors influencing circulating Lp(a) levels in a population of middle-aged, asymptomatic individuals at high risk for cardiovascular disease due to metabolic syndrome (the Liver-Bible cohort). This study also aimed to examine the relationships between plasma Lp(a) levels and liver fibrosis, using liver stiffness as the main outcome, and cardiovascular damage. To better understand the potential causal relationships with liver damage, we also analyzed a large genomic cohort enriched with individuals with severe MASLD (the Milan Biobank database), assessing the impact of genetic predisposition to higher Lp(a) levels, as indicated by polygenic risk scores, to infer the causal relationship between this atherogenic lipoprotein and liver disease phenotypes.

Methods

Liver Bible cohort

The Liver-Bible-2022 cohort included 859 apparently healthy individuals with metabolic dysfunction who underwent blood donation between June 2019 and December 2021 at the Transfusion Medicine Unit of the Fondazione IRCCS Ca’ Granda Hospital. For these participants, both plasma Lp(a) measurements (n = 859) and genomic data were available (n = 808) [18–20]. These participants were aged between 40 and 65 years old, were required to provide their written informed consent, and had at least three of the following metabolic abnormalities: overweight or obesity (body mass index [BMI] ≥ 25 kg/m2), prediabetes or T2D (fasting plasma glucose ≥ 100 mg/dl), triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dl, HDL-cholesterol < 45/55 mg/dl in M/F, increased blood pressure (systolic ≥ 130 mmHg and/or diastolic ≥ 85 mmHg at blood donation or being on anti-hypertensive treatment). Individuals with chronic degenerative disorders, except for well-controlled arterial hypertension, compensated primary hypothyroidism, and T2D not requiring medications, were excluded during initial evaluation.

All participants underwent evaluation of BMI, waist circumference, glucose and lipid levels, insulin, HbA1c, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), as well as alcohol intake (drinks per week), sweetened beverage consumption (drinks per week), moderate to vigorous physical activity (hours per week), and Mediterranean Dietary Score (MDS) [21]. Diabetes was diagnosed as HbA1c ≥ 49 mmol/mol or use of glucose-lowering medications.

The NASH/MASH index was calculated as previously reported [22]. Hepatic steatosis (assessed by controlled attenuation parameter, CAP) and fibrosis (assessed by liver stiffness measurement, LSM) were evaluated using vibration-controlled transient elastography with Fibroscan® in all participants to non-invasively assess liver damage [23].

Conventional estimation of cardiovascular risk was based on the European Society of Cardiology SCORE2 risk (ESC-2) [24]. The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Fondazione IRCCS Ca’ Granda of Milan, and each participant signed a written informed consent (ID 1650, revision June 23rd, 2020). The Milan BioBank

The Milano BioBank is a multi-center project that led to the genotyping of DNA from 6,963 subjects from the Italian population collected at the Fondazione IRCCS Ca’ Granda Policlinico Milano. This cohort includes blood donors from the FoGS cohort, individuals with metabolic dysfunction from the Liver-Bible cohort, as well as patients with MASLD from an Italian multicenter cohort collected at Fondazione IRCCS. The overall characteristics of the cohort are reported in the descriptive statistics of Table S1. Liver damage was assessed as described above for the Liver-Bible cohort. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fondazione IRCCS Ca’ Granda of Milan (reference numbers 91-2023bis, 00225305–2019-CS/21196, 485–2017, 96–2023, and 334–2020).

Plasma Lp(a) measurement

Serum samples were stored at -80 °C prior to analysis. Levels of Lp(a) were measured using a turbidimetric assay (Tina-quant Lipoprotein (a) Gen. 2; Roche), which used rabbit polyclonal anti-lipoprotein(a) detection, and was performed on a Cobas C501 from Roche (Roche Diagnostics). Bilevel internal quality control samples were measured at the start and end of each analysis day. The assay has a measurement range from 7 to 240 nmol/L. An Lp(a) concentration up to 62 nmol/L (~ 30 mg/dl) was considered not clinically relevant, with an intra- and inter-assay coefficient of variation of 3% at 88.7 nmol/L [16]. We defined high-risk plasma Lp(a) levels as > 105 nmol/L (~ > 50 mg/dl) [9].

NMR metabolomics

Serum samples were handled using standardized procedures and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Sample preparation and measurement were performed as previously described [25]. Quantification of serum metabolites and lipoprotein subclasses from 1H-NMR spectra was performed using Bruker IVDr software, specifically B.I.Quant-PS 2.0.0 for the quantification of 41 serum metabolites in mmol/L units, and B.I.LISA (Lipoprotein Subclass Analysis) PL-5009–01/001 for the quantification of 112 serum lipoprotein classes and subclasses in mg/dL, except for particle numbers, which are expressed in nmol/L, and ratios. Inflammatory biomarkers were also measured with Bruker PhenoRisk PACS RuO 1.0.0 [25].

Vascular damage assessment

In a subgroup of 250 patients at high cardiovascular risk, defined as having plasma glucose levels above 100 mg/dl, we performed a carotid duplex ultrasound. A high-resolution sonographer (Esaote MyLabTwice) with a linear 7.5 MHz probe was used to evaluate carotid intima-media thickness, and a single operator assessed the presence of supra-aortic artery plaques. The average of three measurements was taken. The presence of carotid atherosclerotic plaques was defined as focal thickening of 50% or more than the surrounding area or an intima-media thickness of 1.5 mm or greater. The percentage of stenosis was graded using the ECST definition [26].

Genomic analysis

DNA extraction was performed from whole-blood or buffy coat samples, using a Chemagic 360 (PerkinElmer) with the low-volume kit CMG-1491. For genotyping, the Global Screening Array (GSA), version 3.0 (Illumina), containing 712,189 variants, was used, except for the FoGS cohort, which was genotyped using GSA version 2.0. Details on genotyping and quality-control procedures have been previously described [27]. Briefly, samples with a call rate below 90% were removed using Genome Analysis Tool Kits (GATK), based on the initial genotype data, resulting in a total of 465,044 SNPs after quality control, data harmonization, and standardization between the two array versions. Additionally, individuals with mismatched genotypic and phenotypic sex were removed from the genotyping sample set. Variants with over 5% missing data, an MAF < 0.1% in disease sets or blood donors, differing missing genotype rates in affected and unaffected individuals (pFisher < 10−5), or those deviating from the Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium were also removed. For accurate duplicate/relatedness testing and population structure analysis, we used a linkage disequilibrium (LD)-pruned subset of SNPs based on a set of independent (MAF ≥ 5%) SNPs excluding X- and Y-chromosomes, SNPs in LD (leaving no pairs with R2 > 0.2), and 11 high-LD regions [28]. For ancestry stratification of the overall cohort, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) for the genotyped subjects using PLINK v1.9 to correct the association analysis for the first five PCs, to consider ethnic differences in the analysis. To maximize genetic coverage, we performed single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) imputation on genome build GRCh38 using the Michigan Imputation Server and 194,512 haplotypes generated by the Trans-Omics for Precision Medicine (TOPMed) program (Freeze 5). A total of 8,609,091 SNPs were included in the cohort. The candidate genetic determinants of circulating Lp(a) levels at the LPA locus (rs3798220, rs6938647, rs41272114, rs10455872, rs75692336) were retrieved from previous literature [3, 12, 13, 29, 30] to construct a candidate polygenic risk score (PRS-C) after multivariable fitting for effect size calculation. Next, an extended polygenic risk score of Lp(a) (PRS-E) was constructed based on an hypothesis free approach; a genome-wide association study (GWAS) was performed on the ranked inverse transformed (RINT) Lp(a) values for all the 808 individuals with metabolic dysfunction, whose genetic data were available, under the assumption of an additive model adjusted for age, biological sex, BMI, T2D status and the first 5 PCs, to ensure reproducibility, reliability and consistency in the analysis. Genome-wide significant variants (p < 5 × 10–8) at the LPA locus were selected, and a threshold of 0.8 was set to exclude redundant variants with high linkage disequilibrium in the clumping step of variant selection. The effect sizes of the variants in the LPA locus have been taken from summary statistics of GWAS performed in the Liver-Bible-2022 cohort.

As PRSs of MASLD/MASH, we considered the previously extensively validated PRSs for hepatic fat content (PRS-HFC) [31], the PRS of MASH with five variants (PRS-5) [32], the partitioned PRSs of MASLD due to liver lipid compartmentalization (PRS-Discordant), and those due to increased lipid synthesis (PRS-Concordant) [27], as well as the most comprehensive PRS developed by Chen et al. (PRS-Chen) [33].

Statistical analysis

For descriptive statistics, categorical variables are shown as numbers and percentages. Continuous variables are shown as means and standard deviations (SD), or medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), as appropriate. Variables that were not normally distributed, such as Lp(a), were log-transformed before analysis. To examine variables associated with continuous traits, such as circulating Lp(a) levels and other coagulation parameters, we used generalized linear models (GLM). Logistic regression models were applied to analyze binary traits. To account for confounding factors, we developed a series of multivariable regression models adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, and clinical factors identified as significant in the univariable regression analysis (selecting the most robust determinant in each category to avoid multicollinearity).

For the phenotypic associations, we used genome-wide SNP data to test for associations with allele dosage, performing a logistic regression analysis for dichotomous traits or a linear regression analysis for continuous variables, assuming additive effects with REGENIE software for chromosomes 1–22 and X, adjusting the analysis for age, biological sex, BMI, T2D and the first 5 PCs from PCA as covariates, as previously described [34]. Ranked inverse transformation (RINT) has been applied in the GWAS of Lp(a).

Data were analyzed using JMP software version 17.2 Pro (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R 4.3.3 version. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical predictors of plasma Lp(a) levels in the Liver Bible cohort

Due to the inclusion criteria of the study, the Liver Bible cohort consisted of middle-aged men who were overweight or obese and had hypertension, with a low prevalence of T2D and severe MASLD, but at significant risk of cardiovascular events (Table 1). The distribution of circulating Lp(a) levels in the cohort (expressed as nmol) is shown in Fig. 1. The median of Lp(a) was 19.6 nmol/L (IQR, 7.4–47.9 nmol/L). 145 patients (16.8%) had Lp(a) values above 62 nmol/L (~ > 30 mg/dL), and 63 subjects (7%) had values above > 105 nmol/L (~ > 50 mg/dL).

Distribution of circulating Lp(a) levels in individuals with metabolic dysfunction included in the Liver-Bible cohort

In univariable analyses, high-risk Lp(a) levels (i.e., subjects with Lp(a) > 105 nmol/L) were significantly associated with pre-existing T2D, statin use, and LDL-cholesterol (p < 0.05 for all), but these associations might be overestimated due to elevated Lp(a) levels in those with high values. Conversely, Lp(a) levels were not associated with other clinical features, including traditional cardiovascular risk factors. In particular, there was no significant association with serum liver enzymes, CAP or LSM values.

The effect of candidate genetic factors on circulating Lp(a) levels is reported in Table 2. We observed a strong, independent association of most included risk alleles with circulating Lp(a). The coefficients for the candidate polygenic risk score (PRS-C) are also shown in Table 2. PRS-C predicted circulating Lp(a) levels (p = 3.0 × 10–51), explaining 25.0% of the total variance in Lp(a) within this population.

Relationship between plasma Lp(a) levels and liver damage

After adjustment for clinical covariates, circulating Lp(a) levels were not significantly associated with liver steatosis and fibrosis biomarkers, although a nominal association (p = 0.024) with the MASH score was observed (Table 3). Similarly, genetically predicted Lp(a) levels, based on candidate genetic variants, were not associated with non-invasive liver damage biomarkers (data not shown). After adjustment for genetic heritability, an inverse association between Lp(a) and biomarkers of MASH and liver fibrosis emerged (Table 3). Specifically, there was a significant inverse association between the MASH score and LSM (p < 0.05) and with the fibrosis stage (p < 0.005). These findings support the idea that liver damage and fibrosis are a cause, rather than a consequence, of reduced Lp(a) levels.

When we stratified subjects with available carotid artery examinations (n = 250) according to statin use, we found that among those taking statins (n = 25), the presence of carotid plaques was associated with higher plasma Lp(a) concentrations (p = 0.01; Fig. S1). These data align with the notion that high Lp(a) levels can contribute to the residual cardiovascular risk in individuals with metabolic dysfunction.

Genomics of Lp(a) in the Milan Biobank

To gain further insight into the genetic factors influencing circulating Lp(a) levels in individuals with metabolic dysfunction, we applied a bias-free method by conducting a GWAS of Lp(a) levels in the Liver-Bible cohort. In multivariable analyses, we identified 11 genetic variants, all located in the LPA gene, that show genome-wide associations with this trait, thereby ruling out possible pleiotropic effects of the genetic instrument. We confirmed the rs10455872 A > G intronic variant as the top hit in the analysis (β = 1.109, p = 4.6 × 10–25; minor allele frequency (MAF) = 0.043, posterior p = 0.653) (Fig. 2A), replicating the candidate gene analysis (Table 2).

We then developed an extended PRS (PRS-E) in the Milan Biobank. First, we considered all 11 genome-wide significant SNPs for linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis (Fig. 2B). Setting an R2 cut-off at 0.8, we identified a set of 6 low-correlated variants, whose predicted effects on the gene product are shown in Table S2.

Then, we tested the association of the entire variant set simultaneously with circulating log-Lp(a) levels using multivariable regression models adjusted for age, sex, BMI, T2D status and the first five principal components (PC1-5), retaining variants with p < 0.1 for constructing PRS-E (Table S3). In these multivariable regression models, PRS-E predicted circulating Lp(a) levels (β = 0.85, p = 9.9 × 10–57), confirming the results obtained with PRS-C (Table S4).

We then examined the associations between PRS-E and metabolic and liver-related traits in the Milan Biobank (Table 6). PRS-E was independently associated with total cholesterol (β = 1.90, p = 0.001) and inversely nominally related to systolic blood pressure (β = -0.55, p = 0.022) and the presence of T2D (β = -0.13, p = 0.042). PRS-E was also related to several LDL subfraction parameters, including particle number, ApoB100, free cholesterol, and phospholipid content (Table 6). PRS-E was also marginally and nominally inversely related to acetoacetic acid (β = -0.001, p = 0.049), suggesting decreased hepatic lipid oxidation. Notably, as concerning the primary study outcome, in line with the findings we obtained from the Liver-Bible, PRS-E was not associated with liver fibrosis and other liver phenotypes in the entire Milan Biobank cohort (Table 6).

Discussion

In this large study, we examined the main determinants of circulating Lp(a) levels in a population of middle-aged individuals with metabolic dysfunction and a cohort enriched with patients with severe MASLD, exploring their relationship with hepatic and vascular damage through a genetic approach. The key finding of our study was that the severity of MASLD is more likely to be a cause rather than a consequence of reduced plasma Lp(a) levels. This Lp(a) reduction may lead to an underestimation of cardiovascular disease risk in individuals with MASLD and liver fibrosis, especially because more severe liver disease was associated with greater carotid atherosclerotic plaques independently of traditional cardiovascular risk factors and of Lp(a) levels. Additionally, our results reaffirm the role of genetic predisposition in determining plasma Lp(a) concentrations in people with metabolic syndrome. Finally, the Mendelian randomization framework approach, exploiting variation at the LPA locus and a two-sample analysis, [35] suggests that silencing of the LPA gene is unlikely to cause liver disease through Lp(a) retention, as genetically predicted lower plasma Lp(a) levels were not associated with greater severity of MASLD-related phenotypes. In this study, we found a borderline association of genetic predisposition to higher Lp(a) levels with an increased risk of liver fibrosis (as estimated by FibroScan). Conversely, when we applied a bidirectional Mendelian randomization approach, genetic predisposition to MASLD/MASH tended to be associated with lower plasma Lp(a) levels.

Over the past two decades, plasma Lp(a) levels have attracted significant interest due to their significant association with an increased risk of cardiovascular events and coronary artery disease [6]. It has recently been recommended that all individuals be tested for plasma Lp(a) levels once in their lifetime, as Lp(a) is a stable and potentially modifiable cardiovascular risk factor [7]. Although many epidemiologic studies reported a significant association between higher Lp(a) levels and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [6], the role of plasma Lp(a) concentration in cardiovascular risk assessment remains uncertain in specific patient groups, such as those with metabolic dysfunction at high risk for MASLD.

Our findings showing that circulating Lp(a) levels are inversely associated with liver stiffness measured by FibroScan® align with the existing literature. In a cross-sectional study of 600 Italian patients with biopsy-confirmed MASLD, an inverse association between Lp(a) levels and significant liver fibrosis was reported [16]. Other cross-sectional studies examining plasma Lp(a) levels across the MASLD spectrum also found inverse associations, possibly due to reverse causality, where liver disease itself may decrease plasma Lp(a) levels [17]. Similarly, in a study of 181 Japanese patients with biopsy-confirmed MASLD, an inverse association was observed between plasma Lp(a) levels and the likelihood of advanced liver fibrosis. Supporting this, a cross-sectional analysis of 2,308 participants from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) III showed a significant inverse association between Lp(a) levels and MASLD risk [36]. A meta-analysis involving 20,220 patients with MASLD and 54,471 controls confirmed that plasma Lp(a) levels were lower in those with MASLD [37]. Data from the Virginia Commonwealth University Health System also showed a discordant association, with plasma Lp(a) concentrations being 50% lower in individuals with biopsy-proven MASLD and MASH [38].

From a pathophysiological perspective, since the apolipoprotein(a) and apoB components of Lp(a) are assembled in the liver, any disease affecting hepatocytes could influence the production rate of this lipoprotein, a process that is more closely related to plasma Lp(a) concentrations [39]. However, observational epidemiological studies may not exclude the possibility that reduced Lp(a) levels might be a cause, rather than a consequence, of advanced MASLD. The detrimental impact of lower Lp(a) levels could be due to the retention of lipotoxic lipids in hepatocytes, similar to what occurs in heterozygous familial hypobetalipoproteinemia [40–42]. This question also has translational relevance for the treatment of individuals with severe MASLD and increased Lp(a) levels, because if the latter hypothesis is true, therapeutic strategies targeting Lp(a) reduction might worsen liver disease.

To address this question, we used a Mendelian randomization approach [35], taking advantage of the fact that circulating Lp(a) levels are strongly genetically determined, being mostly influenced by variation in the LPA gene itself, which limits the confounding effect of pleiotropy [1]. Indeed, common genetic variants in the LPA gene, which affect Kringle domains repetition and gene expression, lead to lifelong higher circulating Lp(a) levels, which are more common in persons who develop cardiovascular disease, while individuals carrying rare loss of function or common splice site variants associated with lower Lp(a) levels tend to have lower rates of cardiovascular disease [4]. Using both candidate and unsupervised genomic approaches, we confirmed that genetic variation at the LPA locus accounts for a significant fraction, at least a quarter, of circulating Lp(a) in individuals with metabolic dysfunction at high risk for cardiovascular disease. In comparison to previous studies [6], the slightly lower estimate in our study may be due to the presence of liver disease and unknown or unmeasured metabolic confounders in this population.

While a previous study reported an uncertain, bimodal effect of genetic predisposition to Lp(a) on the risk of liver-related outcomes [17], we did not find any causal association between lower Lp(a) levels and liver outcomes using the Milan Biobank even with a larger number of cases with severe MASLD, through both a candidate variant (PRS-C) and a genome-wide unsupervised approach (PRS-E). Conversely, considering genetic variation, the severity of MASLD showed a strong influence on Lp(a) levels, making it the second main determinant of Lp(a) levels after genetic background, thereby suggesting that the extent of liver damage is the primary explanation for the epidemiological association. In fact, genetic predisposition to MASLD tended to be associated with lower Lp(a) levels, and the association was nominally significant when considering the most complete PRS validated in the literature [33]. These data also provide reassurance about the long-term safety of Lp(a) lowering agents on liver health, particularly regarding their impact on Lp(a) secretion.

On the other hand, in an exploratory metabolomic and lipidomic analysis, genetically predicted Lp(a) levels are associated with several LDL parameters, especially particle numbers of large LDL2, which may be associated with a higher coronary disease risk [43, 44]. Genetically predicted Lp(a) levels were also associated with borderline significant protection against T2D. This finding is in line with other epidemiological studies [6] and raises the possibility that Lp(a) lowering might worsen glucose control.

Given the inverse relationship we observed between Lp(a) and biomarkers of MASH and liver fibrosis, we considered whether Lp(a) levels might also act as a cardiovascular risk factor in these patients. In our study, Lp(a) levels were associated with carotid artery atherosclerotic plaques, independently of traditional cardiovascular risk factors, as assessed by the ESC-SCORE2. This association was even more pronounced among participants treated with statins. This finding may be clinically relevant because, although individuals with MASLD tend to have lower Lp(a) levels, their increased cardiovascular risk due to liver-related factors [25, 45] makes it worthwhile to measure circulating Lp(a) levels as an additional marker of residual cardiovascular risk. Notably, in the Liver Bible cohort, we also observed an increasing risk of having carotid atherosclerotic plaques with liver damage (as measured by LSM), independent of both traditional risk factors and Lp(a) levels. These findings are further supported by the results from a large prospective cohort study involving 56,168 patients with MASLD (followed for a median of 5.0 years) showing that circulating Lp(a) levels were lower in Asian individuals with MASLD and advanced fibrosis, but the risk of developing incident cardiovascular events remained the highest among those with advanced liver fibrosis and high Lp(a) levels [46].

The results of our study should be interpreted within the context of potential limitations, including the lack of direct data on KIV repeats from direct sequencing. Furthermore, subclinical atherosclerosis data were only available for a subset of participants with more severe insulin resistance, so results should be viewed as hypothesis-generating and need independent replication. It is also important to remember that the atherogenic effect of Lp(a) depends on its molar concentration rather than the Apo(a) size [47]. Moreover, while our data primarily involve individuals of European descent, an analysis of 460,506 UK Biobank participants with measured Lp(a) levels found that, despite significant variations in Lp(a) concentrations across racial groups, the increased risk associated with any given increase in Lp(a) concentration appears consistent across these groups [48]. This latter evidence is further supported by an analysis involving 273,770 US individuals with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, which showed that higher Lp(a) levels were associated with continuously increasing risk of recurrent cardiovascular events, irrespective of sex and race/ethnicity [49]. Finally, the analyses could only be adjusted for a limited number of lifestyle and dietary factors.

In conclusion, the results of this study indicate that in Italian individuals with metabolic dysfunction and MASLDcirculating Lp(a) levels are primarily determined by the LPA gene variation and the severity of liver involvement. These results support the hypothesis that severe liver fibrosis is more likely a cause rather than a consequence of lower plasma Lp(a) levels in individuals with MASLD. The Brussel Declaration is a key milestone in cardiovascular health policy, emphasizing the importance of identifying inherited lipid disorders, including elevated Lp(a), as part of a comprehensive approach to improving cardiovascular outcomes [7]. Therefore, the assessment of both plasma Lp(a) levels and the extent of liver damage may improve vascular damage evaluation beyond traditional cardiovascular risk factors.
