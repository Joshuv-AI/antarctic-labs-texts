Nail–Schanz pin integrated external distraction technique during intramedullary nailing of comminuted femoral shaft fracture
Authors: Seungyup Shin, Seong-Tae Kim, Junho Jang, Sungho Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Femoral shaft fractures are among the most common high-energy injuries managed by orthopaedic trauma surgeons [1], [2]. The advent of reamed intramedullary nailing has markedly improved union rates and functional outcomes [3]. However, comminuted diaphyseal fractures remain particularly challenging due to the frequent presence of severe shortening, absence of cortical contact, and loss of inherent fracture stability. The Winquist and Hansen classification (1984) remains the most clinically useful framework for understanding fixation constructs [4], as it stratifies fractures according to comminution and percentage of cortical contact. In type IV comminution, reliable closed functional reduction and fixation become particularly challenging. Closed reduction is desirable to preserve soft tissue and fracture biology, but it is technically demanding in these cases.

Restoration of proper femoral length and alignment is essential to achieve optimal functional outcomes after diaphyseal femur fractures. Even small deviations can have clinically relevant consequences. Limb length discrepancy (LLD), which has been reported in up to 43% of diaphyseal fractures, may lead to altered gait mechanics, pelvic obliquity, and secondary back or knee pain [5]. Similarly, coronal malalignment alters the mechanical axis of the lower limb, potentially increasing abnormal joint contact pressures and accelerating degenerative changes in the knee [3]. Sagittal malalignment can compromise the extensor mechanism and affect stride efficiency [3]. Perhaps most importantly, rotational malalignment, which occurs in as many as 27.6% of femoral shaft fractures [6], [7], can result in functional limitations such as hip or knee pain, difficulty in running or walking, and even predisposing patients to early-onset knee osteoarthritis [8]. Because of these potential sequelae, meticulous reduction to restore length, coronal and sagittal alignment, and rotation remains the cornerstone of surgical management in comminuted femoral shaft fractures.

Manual traction by an assistant can provide temporary length restoration, but it is difficult to maintain consistently throughout the procedure, particularly during reaming and screw fixation. Although a traction table can provide continuous and stable traction, several studies have noted that the traction axis does not correspond to the true biomechanical axis of the lower limb, potentially resulting in angular deformity or malalignment [9].

To address these limitations, we utilized a femoral distractor integrated with the intramedullary nail via Schanz pins. In the proximal segment, a Schanz pin was introduced via the proximal interlocking screw hole, whereas in the distal segment it was inserted carefully to avoid conflict with the intramedullary nail. The two pins were connected externally, allowing gradual restoration of femoral length and precise correction of rotation and alignment under fluoroscopic guidance, while maintaining a stable reduction during subsequent interlocking screw fixation. We report a case demonstrating the application and outcome of this technique in comminuted femoral shaft fracture, focusing on surgical details and clinical outcomes.

Case report

Patients

A 36-year-old male was admitted following a high-energy motorcycle accident. Initial radiographs revealed a comminuted femoral shaft fracture (Fig. 1A, B). The injury was associated with an extensive degloving wound extending from the mid-thigh to the posterior aspect of the knee (Fig. 1C). No neurovascular deficits were identified.

(A, B) Initial anteroposterior and lateral radiographs showing a comminuted femoral shaft fracture. (C) Clinical photographs demonstrating extensive degloving wound extending from the mid-thigh to the posterior aspect of the knee.

Dedicated imaging of the ipsilateral femoral neck using radiography and computed tomography excluded an associated femoral neck fracture.

Surgical technique

The procedure began with selection of the anticipated nail length, considering the expected restoration of femoral length. Given the extensive posterior soft-tissue injury, the patient was positioned in the true lateral decubitus position to allow simultaneous fracture fixation and wound management. After thorough irrigation and debridement of devitalized tissue, closed intramedullary nailing was performed.

A standard entry point was made in the piriformis fossa, followed by guidewire insertion, reaming, and nail advancement.

At this stage, attention turned to temporary external distraction:

1.Proximal pin placement (Fig. 2A): Using the targeting device of the intramedullary nail, a proximal pin was placed through one interlocking screw hole after drilling with a 3.5-mm bit and inserting a 5.0-mm Schanz pin, while a proximal interlocking screw was inserted through another proximal hole.

Gradual distraction was performed under fluoroscopic guidance to restore length (Fig. 3B, C), with fine correction of alignment and rotation (Fig. 3D) achieved through continuous comparison with the contralateral femur to approximate the original length, alignment, and rotation.

Postoperative course and outcome

Postoperatively, immobilization was maintained until soft tissue healing progressed, and protected weight-bearing with crutches was initiated approximately 1 week after surgery as soft tissue stabilization began. Serial radiographs demonstrated progressive callus formation (Fig. 6).

At the 9-month follow-up, radiographic evaluation confirmed bone union and revealed a limb length discrepancy of +7 mm compared with the contralateral femur, 3° varus alignment in the coronal plane, neutral sagittal alignment, and no evidence of clinically significant rotational malalignment (Fig. 7). Clinically, the patient reported no pain, exhibited a normal gait, and demonstrated full hip and knee range of motion. Soft-tissue healing was uneventful, with stable coverage and no need for additional surgical intervention.

(A) Scanogram and (B) lateral radiograph at 9 months demonstrating bone union and negligible limb length discrepancy (+7 mm), with no evidence of significant malalignment or malrotation.

Discussion

Comminuted femoral shaft fractures are difficult to manage due to their inherent instability and the technical challenges of achieving and maintaining adequate reduction. The treatment goal is restoration of length, alignment and rotation by statically locked intramedullary nailing, which is the gold standard [3]. Closed intramedullary nailing remains the preferred treatment, but achieving anatomic reduction in severely comminuted fractures is often hindered by reliance on traction table or manual traction. The traction table, though useful, provides only gross traction and is limited in fine adjustments. Assistant-applied manual traction is inconsistent and physically demanding, leading to variability in outcomes. In contrast, the use of a nail-Schanz pin integrated femoral distractor offers controlled, incremental correction, with stable maintenance of reduction throughout fixation.

This case demonstrates that a nail-Schanz pin integrated femoral distractor-assisted intramedullary nailing provides favorable outcome in comminuted femoral shaft fracture. The technique facilitated precise restoration of length, alignment and rotation, overcoming limitations of manual traction or traction table alone.

Ideally, the nail should be inserted after achieving optimal reduction; however, in comminuted fractures, attaining an ideal reduction can be challenging. Regarding the reduction sequence, overall alignment and rotational correction were achieved using external manipulation. When the proximal or distal fragment is short or subjected to significant deforming forces, blocking screws or blocking pins are placed to prevent malalignment during nail advancement; when necessary, additional manual support or external aids such as crutches were used for sagittal alignment correction. After the anticipated length nail is inserted, length restoration is performed using the distractor, and the achieved length is compared with preoperatively obtained radiographs of the contralateral femur. Rotational alignment was assessed before inserting the proximal Schanz pin by comparing the shape of the lesser trochanter when the patella was positioned at the center of the knee joint with the contralateral femur on standardized AP fluoroscopy. At this stage, fine correction of rotation and alignment can be performed using the distal Schanz pin as a joystick while the limb is held under distraction. A standard femoral distractor with a multi-directional clamp is applied to allow simultaneous lengthening and multi-planar correction.

This structured, sequential approach improved reproducibility and reduced the risk of residual LLD or deformity in comminuted femoral shaft fractures.

This case achieved union with minimal length discrepancy. Furthermore, no significant malrotation or major alignment errors were observed, suggesting that the distractor provides reproducible correction even in complex comminuted pattern. The slight lengthening observed in this case may have resulted from intentional overcorrection during surgery to avoid residual shortening. Similarly, the mild varus alignment noted in this case could be attributed to the application of the distractor on the lateral side, which may have introduced a subtle varus tendency during distraction.

A notable difference between the present technique and conventional external distraction is that distraction is achieved through a Schanz pin inserted via the proximal interlocking hole of the intramedullary nail. This nail-guided Schanz pin allows distraction to be aligned with the nail axis, minimizing eccentric forces and facilitating accurate length restoration. Traditional external distractors often rely on pins placed independently of the nail trajectory, which may introduce angular or rotational forces during lengthening. In addition, the authors believe that independent Schanz pins, which are inserted into the proximal and distal metaphyseal regions, are less reliable than nail-integrated Schanz pins because they are more susceptible to pin-site loosening during various manipulations. In contrast, our method provides more stable and controlled distraction, while simultaneously maintaining reduction without excessive manipulation of the fracture fragments.

Additionally, by integrating the distractor with the nail construct, the need for continuous manual traction or reliance on a traction table is reduced. This is particularly advantageous in cases with severe soft-tissue injury, where repeated manipulation or excessive traction could risk further soft-tissue damage. Therefore, this technique may represent a useful alternative not only for segmental comminuted femoral fractures but also for length-unstable diaphyseal femoral fractures treated with intramedullary nailing.

This case highlights an additional advantage in patients requiring concurrent soft-tissue management. In cases with concomitant soft tissue injury, excessive manipulation to achieve reduction and maintain alignment can be avoided, thereby minimizing additional soft tissue damage. Furthermore, lateral positioning allowed simultaneous fracture fixation and wound care without compromising the accuracy of length restoration. This underscores the versatility of the technique in concomitant compromised soft tissue.

However, this technique carries several potential risks and limitations. The technique is not intended as a routine reduction method, but rather as a salvage procedure in a limited subset of cases, including some AO/OTA type B3 (fragmentary wedge), C2 (intact segmental), and C3 (fragmentary segmental) fractures in which restoration and maintenance of reduction are difficult with conventional methods.

Notably, because the construct provides correction only along the axial length, its ability to address coronal, sagittal, and rotational alignment is limited, and it therefore cannot independently correct deformities in these planes. Overall alignment and rotational correction were achieved using external manipulation and, when necessary, blocking pin or blocking screw. The purpose of the present technique is to facilitate Length restoration additional fine adjustments once these primary corrections have been accomplished.

Care should also be taken regarding Schanz pin placement through the interlocking hole, as this step may be associated with technical challenges and potential risks, including damage to the intramedullary nail, deformation or enlargement of the locking hole, malposition of the Schanz pin, and possible iatrogenic injury or fracture during pin insertion. In particular, inserting a screw at the site where the proximal Schanz pin was previously placed raises concerns regarding construct stability. The proximal pin is placed through the interlocking screw hole after drilling with a 3.5-mm bit and inserting a 5.0-mm Schanz pin. The final proximal interlocking screw also measures 5.0 mm in diameter, matching the temporary pin. Although this configuration allows proper engagement, the clearance between the Schanz pin and the interlocking hole may theoretically permit micromotion during distraction or manipulation for rotational adjustment. Such micromotion could result in minor thread wear and potentially reduce the locking strength of the definitive interlocking screw. However, because the proximal hole lies within the dense subtrochanteric cortical bone, we did not experience any subjective reduction in screw purchase during final fixation.

Furthermore, in fractures with a short distal segment located far from the isthmus, distraction after nail insertion may lead to proximal migration of the nail relative to the distal fragment, thereby reducing the effective distal working length of the implant and potentially compromising distal fixation stability. In such cases, caution is required, and the technique may not be suitable.

Finally, the present findings are based on a single case, and therefore the generalizability of this technique remains limited.

In comminuted femoral fractures without ipsilateral femoral neck involvement, where the risk of peri-implant fracture at the femoral neck is low, we generally prefer using a piriformis-entry straight nail or standard locking of a greater trochanteric–entry laterally bent nail. However, in fractures requiring reconstruction screw insertion, the nail must sometimes be rotated to align the reconstruction screw hole for proper fixation. In such cases, a Schanz pin can be inserted through the transverse interlocking screw hole to facilitate our technique. Even under these circumstances, we first ensure rotational alignment of the distal segment and then position the nail to accommodate the reconstruction screw, which typically does not significantly alter normal anatomic rotation (Fig. 8). Nevertheless, this approach has inherent limitations, particularly in patients with markedly abnormal femoral anteversion, where achieving precise rotational control may be challenging.

Conclusions

Although it is an imperfect technique with many limitations, intramedullary nailing with a nail-Schanz pin integrated femoral distractor-assisted reduction technique may be considered a useful adjunct in complex comminuted femoral shaft fractures, particularly when precise length restoration and stable maintenance of reduction are required.
