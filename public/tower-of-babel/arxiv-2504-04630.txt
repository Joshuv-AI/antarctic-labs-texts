Foundation Models for Software Engineering of Cyber-Physical Systems: the Road Ahead
Chengjie Lu, Pablo Valle, Jiahui Wu, Erblin Isaku, Hassan Sartaj, Aitor Arrieta, Shaukat Ali

Abstract
Foundation Models (FMs) , particularly Large Language Models (LLMs) , are increasingly used to support various software engineering activities (e.g., coding and testing). Their applications in the software engineering of Cyber-Physical Systems (CPSs) are also growing. However, research in this area remains limited. Moreover, existing studies have primarily focused on LLMs —only one type of FM —leaving ample opportunities to explore others, such as vision-language models. We argue that, in addition to LLMs , other FMs utilizing different data modalities (e.g., images, audio) and multimodal models (which integrate multiple modalities) hold great potential for supporting CPS software engineering, given that these systems process diverse data types. To address this, we present a research roadmap for integrating FMs into various phases of CPS software engineering, highlighting key research opportunities and challenges for the software engineering community.

Handling Textual Requirements Challenges with Foundation Models.

High-level
CPS
requirements are often elicited in natural language for stakeholder communication.
Natural language requirements can be ambiguous, incomplete, or contradictory.
Many works support requirements specifications using formal and modeling languages requiring expertise in such languages.
Given these challenges, we see
FMs
’ potential in many aspects.
First,
LLMs
with textual analysis and comprehension capabilities can use
CPS
requirements documents to identify uncertain and contradictory requirements.
Second, for
CPS
requirements specified in formal or modeling languages, multimodal
FMs
with mixed-mode capabilities can be used to verify formal specifications and requirement models. Third, more research is needed to investigate using
LLMs
for identifying requirements during
CPS
development and improving existing
CPS
requirements’ quality that may be imprecise.
Finally, to facilitate easier communication and prevent miscommunication among diverse stakeholders,
FMs
hold great potential to tailor the same set of requirements to match the backgrounds of different stakeholders.
However, further research is needed to explore how
FMs
can enhance communication among stakeholders involved in
CPS
development.

Identifying Uncertainties in
CPS
.

CPSs
face uncertainties in their operation, e.g., due to environmental conditions, sensor inaccuracies, and human interactions.
To identify uncertainties, domain experts typically use their expertise and past experiences.
In this context,
LLMs
, when provided with
CPS
requirement specifications, can use their knowledge base and analytical capabilities to assist domain experts in identifying uncertainties.
Moreover, VLMs and multimodal
FMs
can use requirements to support uncertainties specification in
CPS
models, such as Simulink models.

Requirements Traceability Analysis with Foundation Models.

Requirements traceability links
CPS
requirements with various artifacts in the development phases, such as software models, source code, and tests.
Establishing traceability in
CPSs
often requires manual traceability analysis.
To this end, future research can focus on two directions.
First, using
LLMs
’ natural language capabilities to automatically identify, establish, and update traceability links from
CPS
requirements to the development artifacts such as code and test cases in textual format.
Second, using multimodal
FMs
for requirements to ensure traceability across the entire
CPS
development lifecycle, including models, code, and
CPS
simulations.

Requirements Classification with Foundation Models.

CPS
requirements typically include diverse aspects, including functional and non-functional requirements and constraints among them.
Classifying requirements for
CPSs
is challenging due to their continuous evolution and the complex nature of these systems.
In this regard, a potential research direction involves using
LLMs
to assist domain experts in selecting and prioritizing requirements based on their criticality level while also considering aspects, such as environmental factors.
Another research direction is to explore VLMs and multimodal
FMs
for classifying requirements modeled with graphical languages.

Identifying Textual Requirements with Multimodal Foundation Models.

Many
CPSs
work with different data modalities. For instance,
autonomous vehicles (AVs)
use various sensors to identify their environment. Thus, there is potential to automatically extract textual requirements for a
CPS
from historical data available in different formats to support the requirements engineering of
CPSs
. In our example, based on past videos from traffic cameras, one can extract requirements on the operating environment of
AVs
in textual format.

Foundation Models-based
CPS
Modeling.

FMs

CPS

FMs

Foundation Models-based Mathematical Model Generation.

FMs

CPSs

FMs

FMs

FMs
for a deeper understanding of specific domain data.
Moreover, generating mathematical models requires accurately capturing
CPS
characteristics, requiring optimization of
FMs
’ analytical and reasoning capabilities. Besides, the impact of
CPS
dynamic requirements and environmental changes on the mathematical model generation raises the demands on
FMs
, requiring exploration.

Foundation Models for

Foundation Models for CPS Model Execution Support.

FMs
can support the execution of executable models created for
CPSs
(e.g., executable UML state machines). To this end,
FMs

Foundation Models for Software Development

FMs
are known for their code generation capabilities
[
38
]
. For instance, according to Sam Altman, internal OpenAI’s studies demonstrate that their
LLMs
increase software engineering productivity by threefold
[
16
]
.
Their capabilities are being significantly enhanced over time. For instance, new reasoning models, like OpenAI’s o3-mini and DeepSeek-R1
[
26
]
, have shown significant advancements on generating high-quality code. Despite these recent advancements,
CPSs
face some particularities that make
FMs
not to be directly applicable to them. We identify three core directions that will be critical to further adopt
FMs
in the software development process of
CPSs
.

Qualification of Foundation Models for Safety-Critical Code Development.

Many
CPSs
are safety-critical, and thus must be certified, which also requires software development tools to be qualified by a regulatory body
[
17
]
. For instance, when compiling safety-critical code, the employed compiler should have been qualified by a functional safety regulatory agency. Different qualification categories exist
[
17
]
, and
FM
-based code generation would require the most strict one as the code it generates might be buggy. Further, each new version of the tool has to be re-qualified. All this introduces new challenges and aspects to consider when adopting
FM
-based approaches. First, the tools need to carefully follow standards’ rules; this can involve aspects like having redundancy for code generation, or checking that the
FM
is robust to minor prompt changes. Second, standards need to be redefined and tailored to reconsider
FM
-based techniques in developing safety-critical code; this needs interdisciplinary collaborations between regulators and AI experts to see how to adapt standards (e.g., forcing the use of uncertainty to monitor the FM’s confidence when generating code). Lastly, while we see rapid advancements of
LLMs
, standards establish that each new version of the tool needs re-qualification; qualification processes are lengthy and expensive, which poses new challenges to
FM
developers to make their new model versions re-qualified. Regulatory bodies should also research lighter processes to re-qualify fine-tuned LLM versions with enhanced code generation capabilities.

Foundation Models for Domain Specific Code Generation.

Unlike widely adopted general-purpose programming languages (e.g., Python, C), many
CPSs
(e.g., robots and programmable logic controllers) rely on their own proprietary coding language. These languages often lack the extensive community support and open-source examples typically used as training data for
FMs
. This makes
FMs
less adequate for code generation of minoritarian languages than for generic programming languages. Methods to automatically generate synthetic data samples for minoritarian programming languages to train
FMs
is an open research challenge that requires further attention.

Lightweight Foundation Models for
CPS
-specific Code Generation.

Much of
CPS
software, initially developed using simulation-based techniques, is later redeveloped near operation due to the reality gap. Examples include engineers reprogramming a robot, debugging CNC code along with the physical machine, or changing the control code of trains in new facilities. Cloud access is usually unavailable near the operation. Moreover, many companies hesitate to submit their code externally. We envision a direction for researching small language models of a few million parameters that can be directly deployed on the engineers’ PCs and can still generate trustworthy
CPS
code.

In addition,

Though attempts have been made in FM-based
CPS
testing, applying
FMs
to
CPS
testing introduces several notable challenges, which, together with key research opportunities, are discussed below:

Foundation Models-based Test Generation and Test Data Augmentation.

CPS
test generation is time-consuming and resource-intensive, particularly due to the complexity and interdependencies inherent in
CPSs
.
FMs
offer a promising solution by automating the synthesis of high-fidelity test inputs, such as test scenarios and sensor data input, that closely mimic real-world conditions.

FMs
can enrich the test suite by performing noising or denoising involving perturbations injection to simulate sensor inaccuracies and environmental disturbances, and the subsequent filtering of extraneous noise to ensure data integrity. This dual capability would allow generating challenging (noisy) and clean (noise-free) test data for
CPS
, thereby broadening the spectrum of conditions under which the
CPS
is evaluated.

Integrate Foundation Models with Traditional Testing Techniques.

FMs
demonstrate limited ability in numerical solving, particularly in generating concrete scenarios with specific parameter values from logical scenarios defined by parameters and parameter ranges, which is crucial for precise simulation of CPS dynamics and its environment.
This limitation is critical because precise simulation of
CPS
dynamics and their environment relies on accurate numerical instantiation.
Consequently, integrating traditional techniques—such as search-based methods and numerical solvers—with
FMs
becomes necessary to provide a comprehensive solution. These techniques can complement the capabilities of
FMs
by enabling precise numerical computation, enhancing the fidelity of
CPS
simulations.
Such a hybrid approach would effectively leverage logical reasoning and mathematical computing abilities, addressing the limitations of
FMs
in numerical problem-solving. However, there is a need for more research to build such hybrid approaches.

Foundation Models-based Test Selection, Minimization, and Prioritization.

Regression testing helps detect bugs in
CPS
software updates. However, as test suites expand, redundant and overlapping test cases can lead to inefficiencies and increased execution times. Thus,
FMs
can be used to analyze the characteristics of tests, enabling identifying critical tests that are most likely to detect faults.
FMs
may also help minimize the overall suite size while preserving effectiveness. Moreover,
FMs
can prioritize test cases based on their potential to reveal misbehaviors early in the testing cycle, ensuring that high-impact tests are executed first. However, all these aspects require further research investigation.

Evaluate and Bridge Reality Gap with Foundation Models.

Testing
CPS
usually involves a simulated testing environment or requires synthesizing test inputs (e.g., images and videos), and ensuring the test scenarios or synthetic test inputs accurately represent all facets of the physical systems and environments is still an open problem. To this end,
FMs
can be employed to evaluate the realism of test scenarios/inputs, thereby quantifying the fidelity of simulated conditions. Besides, by fine-tuning
FMs
with domain-specific naturalistic data and environmental conditions, dedicated
FMs
can be built to generate synthetic inputs that more accurately reflect physical realities.

Foundation Models-based Test Oracle Support.

The lack of test oracles or the absence of precise test oracles is common when testing
CPSs

[
11
,
10
]
, as in many other software systems
[
31
]
. To this end, research is needed to explore the use of
FMs
for generating test oracles, based on the assumption that they have learned from a wide range of information and can assist testers in devising test oracles for
CPS
testing. Moreover, to further support test oracles in the context of differential testing—one of the key methods for addressing the test oracle problem—
FMs
can be customized to serve as a surrogate implementation of a
CPS
. This is an open research area that requires further investigation.

Reducing Test Inputs with Foundation Models.

CPS

Traditional APR methods are only able to repair certain types of bugs. For instance, in the context of CPSs, search-based methods have been proposed to repair misconfigurations

Foundation Models for
CPS
Evolution

As
CPS
software evolves, it faces persistent challenges such as real-time constraints, heterogeneous system integrations, and reliability concerns
[
4
,
49
,
55
]
.
In
CPSs
, these challenges are further amplified by real-time constraints, hardware-software dependencies, distributed processing, and safety-critical requirements, making development, testing, and maintenance increasingly complex.
CPS
software evolution currently remains manual, thereby facing scalability challenges requiring smarter and automated methods
[
44
,
73
]
.
GitHub Copilot
[
24
]
is an early step toward AI-driven solutions assisting in software evolution. While primarily a coding assistant, it can partially support software evolution tasks by aiding in code refactoring, suggesting updates for deprecated APIs, generating test cases, and assisting with debugging.
However, it mostly generates code at a local level (e.g., functions, small components) and lacks a global view of the software structure and cross-module dependencies, making it dependent on manual interventions at all times.
The literature does not study using
FMs
such as GitHub Copilot to support
CPS
software evolution.
FMs
provide a promising solution to streamlining code evolution, optimizing updates, enabling self-healing mechanisms, and enhancing testing and maintenance processes, ultimately improving the evolution of
CPSs
software.

Automating
CPS
Software Changes and Adjustments.

Software changes (e.g., due to optimization, feature updates, or new hardware) introduce risks of system instability and compatibility issues, primarily due to hidden dependencies and lack of automated support. Developers must manually navigate large and complex codebases, ensure compatibility, and prevent unintended behaviors (e.g., new change breaks existing functionality), making the process time-consuming and error-prone. Similar to GitHub Copilot, the future of
FMs
in
CPS
software evolution extends beyond simply suggesting code changes. Instead of relying on human intervention for execution,
FMs
could be used as autonomous decision-making models capable of analyzing dependencies, predicting potential failures, and applying optimized updates without breaking existing functionality.

Scalable and Efficient
CPS
Software Updates and Deployment.

Managing software updates is challenging, especially in
CPSs
that require continuous operation and minimal downtime. Updates must be carefully coordinated to ensure compatibility with existing components, avoid disruptions, and prevent failures. In complex
CPSs
, manually handling dependencies and version control slows the process and increases the risk of errors. To this end, more research is needed to explore to what extent
FMs
can improve software update processes by automating update scheduling, resolving dependencies, and optimizing deployment strategies.

Self-healing and Adaptive Mechanisms.

CPS
software failures often require manual intervention, causing downtime and increasing maintenance costs. Traditional recovery methods, such as system reboots or checkpoint rollbacks, can restore functionality but do not prevent recurring failures or adapt to new issues.
In this context,
FMs
can assist by analyzing
CPS
behavior, detecting faults early, and providing real-time insights to support adaptive decision-making.
While
CPSs
can integrate predefined recovery strategies,
FMs
enhance these processes by identifying patterns, diagnosing potential issues, and suggesting corrective actions, reducing reliance on manual intervention.

Automated
CPS
Test Evolution and Quality Assurance.

Keeping
CPSs
reliable requires testing to evolve alongside new updates and features.
Both manual and automated
CPS
testing methods often fall behind rapid software changes, requiring significant human effort to create and/or update test cases and maintain regression suites.
To this end,
FMs
are needed to generate test cases that reflect software changes and maintain realism and relevance. Moreover,
FMs
can automatically update regression tests, improving test coverage and reliability and enhancing the overall software quality. All these aspects require further research.

Long-Term Maintenance and Compliance.

CPSs
software must evolve to meet new regulations, security threats, and even hardware changes, but tracking and applying updates is often complex.
FMs
can assist by analyzing compliance requirements, suggesting necessary updates, and automating documentation. Taking the example of a medical dispenser, an
FM
can review and update software to comply with evolving healthcare standards while adapting to customized settings.

8
Discussion

This paper presents an initial set of research opportunities related to how
FMs
can aid in the software engineering of
CPSs
. As Figure
1
depicts, in addition to the challenges discussed in each phase, the use of
FMs
across all software engineering phases of
CPSs
faces some common challenges that apply to their use in any domain. For instance, how can we ensure the correctness of various software engineering artifacts produced by
FMs
? This is a research area in its own right and is not covered in this paper. Moreover,
FMs
can be used to transform software engineering artifacts from one phase to another (e.g., from requirements to tests, from models to code), which remains an open research area. Furthermore, all
FMs
face issues such as hallucinations and uncertainties, which, in our context, will also affect the quality and correctness of the artifacts they produce. These issues also require further research. In addition, there are broader open research questions, such as whether we need a customized
FM
for a specific
CPS
that spans all software engineering phases, separate models for each phase, or a multi-agent
FM
comprised of specialized models for different software engineering phases, collaborating to handle complex
CPS
software engineering tasks. Can
FMs
be reused across
CPSs
belonging to the same domain (e.g., maritime)? All these questions need further investigation.

9
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper presents research opportunities and challenges related to using
FMs
in the software engineering of
CPSs
. To this end, we discuss research opportunities across common software engineering phases and discuss open research questions that should be addressed across all phases. In the future, we will expand on these research opportunities in detail for each phase and map existing software engineering problems to various types of
FMs
to present a more comprehensive research roadmap.
