Engineering Multimodal Nanomaterials for Prostate Cancer Theranostics: Design Principles, Recent Advances, and Translational Challenges
Authors: Jia Wang, Zhongsong Zhang, Haihao Li, Yuanyin Teng, Bo Chen, Jieming Zuo, Xiangyun Li, Peiqin Zhan, Shi Fu, Yongqiang Xu, Lingxiang Wen, Bing Wang, Yuanzhi Fu, Kehan Qin, Wujie Chen, Yinglong Huang, Yihui Shi, Junhao Chen, Junxian Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProstate cancer (PCa) remains a major clinical challenge because of limitations in early detection, tumor heterogeneity, therapeutic resistance, and metastatic progression. Nanomaterials provide versatile platforms for PCa theranostics by integrating molecular imaging, biomarker detection, targeted delivery, multimodal therapy, and therapeutic response assessment. This review summarizes recent advances in major nanomaterial platforms for PCa theranostics, including lipid-based nanomaterials, polymeric nanoparticles, gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs), biomimetic nanoplatforms, and emerging smart nanocarriers, with emphasis on imaging enhancement, biosensing and liquid biopsy, targeted drug and nucleic acid delivery, phototherapy, sonodynamic and chemodynamic therapy (CDT), immunotherapy, and multimodal combination strategies. These advances reflect a shift from conventional single-function nanocarriers toward engineered multimodal theranostic systems that integrate molecular targeting, stimulus-responsive release, image-guided intervention, combination therapy, and therapeutic response monitoring. Unlike previous reviews that mainly describe nanomaterial categories or isolated applications, this review adopts an engineering-oriented design–function–application–translation framework to clarify how material properties, targeting strategies, and stimulus-responsive designs influence diagnostic performance, therapeutic selectivity, and clinical feasibility. Particular emphasis is placed on prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA)-targeted nanoplatforms as representative systems for theranostic integration. Current evidence suggests that nanoplatforms can improve tumor-selective accumulation, imaging sensitivity, biomarker detection, cargo stability, and intracellular delivery while reducing off-target toxicity. However, clinical translation remains limited by heterogeneous biodistribution, protein corona effects, nonspecific uptake, insufficient long-term biosafety data, manufacturing complexity, and batch-to-batch variability. Overall, nanomedicine may support more precise, minimally invasive, and integrated PCa management, but successful translation will require standardized characterization, scalable manufacturing, rigorous safety evaluation, clinically relevant validation, and rational integration with established diagnostic and therapeutic pathways.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the most common malignancies of the male genitourinary system and remains a major global health burden. According to GLOBOCAN 2022, approximately 1.467 million new cases of PCa were reported worldwide in 2022, with substantial associated mortality.1 In parallel, analyses from high-quality population-based registries across multiple countries have revealed marked geographic disparities in PCa incidence and mortality, which are closely related to differences in screening practices, access to diagnostic and therapeutic resources, and the evolving treatment landscape.1 PCa is characterized by marked clinical heterogeneity, ranging from low-risk localized disease suitable for active surveillance to advanced disease that may progress to metastatic and castration-resistant states.2 Among patients with metastatic PCa, bone is one of the most common sites of distant spread, frequently leading to skeletal-related events such as pathological fractures, spinal cord compression, and bone pain, all of which substantially impair quality of life.3 The substantial disease burden of PCa is largely attributable to the often occult nature of early-stage disease, difficulties in risk stratification, and the coexistence of overdiagnosis and underdetection of clinically significant disease. In recent years, prostate-specific antigen (PSA)-based early detection strategies have remained controversial. Opportunistic screening may lead to overdiagnosis of indolent tumors and consequent overtreatment, whereas the absence of structured early detection pathways may result in inadequate identification of clinically significant cancers.4,5 To improve the benefit-to-harm ratio, risk-adapted screening and triage strategies have gradually emerged as a consensus approach. These strategies include primary risk assessment based on baseline risk profiles or blood-based risk prediction models, combined with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-guided triage and targeted biopsy, thereby reducing unnecessary biopsies and overdiagnosis.6 More recent studies have suggested that risk-adapted screening may achieve a benefit-to-harm profile approaching that of breast cancer screening.7 Meanwhile, the European radiology community has emphasized the incorporation of MRI into the screening-to-diagnosis pathway to improve diagnostic accuracy while reducing unnecessary biopsies and related harms.8

Against this background, the development of minimally invasive theranostic strategies integrating early detection, targeted delivery, and multimodal treatment has become an important direction in PCa research. Clinically, these strategies are particularly relevant to early detection and risk stratification, especially in the context of risk-adapted screening, MRI-guided diagnostic pathways, and updated guideline recommendations for PCa diagnosis and staging.4–9 They are also applicable to prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT)- or PET/MRI-guided lesion characterization and treatment monitoring, as recent procedure guidelines have further standardized PSMA PET/CT imaging in PCa.10 In advanced disease, recent Phase III evidence for PSMA-targeted radioligand therapy has further strengthened the clinical relevance of theranostic strategies, particularly in metastatic castration-resistant PCa and treatment-resistant disease.11,12 Although guideline-recommended therapies have improved PCa management, their clinical benefits remain constrained by treatment-related adverse effects, tumor heterogeneity, therapeutic resistance, metastatic progression, off-target toxicity, and insufficient integration of molecular diagnosis, targeted delivery, and response monitoring.9,13,14 These limitations highlight the need for strategies that can improve tumor-selective accumulation, reduce systemic toxicity, overcome biological delivery barriers, and integrate diagnosis with therapy.15,16 To address these clinical bottlenecks, nanomaterials offer an attractive engineering-based approach for PCa diagnosis and treatment. Owing to their tunable size, morphology, surface chemistry, and capacity for multicomponent integration, nanoparticle-based platforms can serve as multifunctional systems for PCa diagnosis and therapy. On the diagnostic side, they provide platforms for multimodal imaging probes and biosensing interfaces, thereby improving lesion visualization and the sensitivity of liquid biomarker detection. On the therapeutic side, they can act as carriers for drugs, nucleic acids, and immunomodulatory molecules, enhancing local tumor accumulation while reducing systemic toxicity.15–20 In particular, when combined with PSMA-directed targeting strategies, nanoplatforms have shown promise in lesion recognition, treatment guidance, and therapeutic response monitoring, thereby supporting integrated diagnosis and therapy in PCa.16,21,22 In addition, nanoplatforms can be combined with photothermal therapy (PTT), photodynamic therapy (PDT), and other phototherapy-based approaches to enhance local antitumor effects.16,17 Overall, by integrating multiple therapeutic modalities and optimizing intratumoral delivery, nanomaterials may offer new opportunities to address therapeutic resistance and delivery barriers in PCa management.15,16,18,19

Building on these advances, this review provides an overview of recent progress in nanomaterial-enabled strategies for PCa diagnosis and treatment, with particular emphasis on their roles in integrated theranostic applications.20 The novelty of this review lies in its clinical translation-oriented and multimodal theranostic perspective. Rather than simply listing nanomaterial types or isolated applications, we organize the discussion around a design–function–application–translation framework. Specifically, this review examines how key material design parameters, including size, morphology, surface chemistry, ligand density, biodegradability, and stimulus responsiveness, affect in vivo biodistribution, lesion accumulation, diagnostic performance, therapeutic selectivity, and translational feasibility in PCa. Despite rapid progress in this field, important questions remain regarding how key nanomaterial design parameters, including size, morphology, surface chemistry, and ligand density, influence in vivo biodistribution, lesion accumulation, and therapeutic efficacy in the biological context of PCa. In particular, the interplay between these parameters and tumor stromal architecture, PSMA expression heterogeneity, and the microenvironment of metastatic lesions has not yet been comprehensively summarized.19,21,22 To improve the logical flow and reduce overlap among sections, this review is organized around five major themes: material design, diagnostic applications, therapeutic applications, theranostic integration, and clinical translation. Accordingly, this review discusses the major application scenarios and underlying mechanisms of nanomaterials in PCa, with a primary focus on diagnostic applications, including imaging enhancement and biosensing-based liquid biomarker detection; targeted delivery systems, including PSMA-mediated and other ligand-directed strategies as well as stimulus-responsive delivery; and multimodal therapeutic approaches, such as photothermal therapy, photodynamic therapy, radionuclide therapy, gene therapy, immunotherapy, and combination strategies.19,23,24 Finally, this review addresses the major challenges associated with the clinical translation of nanomaterials for PCa diagnosis and treatment and outlines future research directions for the development of clinically translatable nanotechnology-based theranostic platforms.19–21

Material Design and Structure–Function Relationships of Nanoplatforms in PCa Theranostics

In the clinical management of PCa, conventional diagnostic and therapeutic strategies continue to face substantial challenges. First, currently available screening approaches still show evident limitations in terms of accuracy and specificity. In particular, PSA-based detection strategies are associated with relatively high false-positive rates and a considerable risk of overdiagnosis, thereby limiting the accurate early detection of clinically significant disease.25 Second, conventional diagnostic modalities, including tissue biopsy and imaging, remain limited by their invasiveness, limited capacity for dynamic monitoring, and inability to comprehensively capture tumor heterogeneity, which restricts further optimization of clinical decision-making.13,26,27 At the therapeutic level, although traditional interventions such as surgery, radiotherapy, endocrine therapy, and chemotherapy have been widely adopted, their clinical efficacy is still affected by factors including tumor heterogeneity, the evolution of drug resistance, and metastatic progression. Meanwhile, treatment-related systemic toxicity and long-term adverse effects continue to increase patient burden and compromise quality of life.13,14 Therefore, the development of innovative technological strategies capable of overcoming the limitations of conventional diagnosis and treatment has become a critical unmet need in the field of PCa. Owing to their tunable size, versatile surface functionalization, and favorable biocompatibility, nanomaterials provide new opportunities for PCa diagnosis and treatment. These materials may help overcome the limitations of traditional approaches in terms of detection sensitivity, drug delivery efficiency, and therapeutic specificity, while also supporting the development of integrated theranostic systems for PCa management.28,29 Based on differences in material composition, structural characteristics, and functional properties, the nanoplatforms most commonly investigated in PCa research include lipid-based nanomaterials, polymeric nanoparticles, AuNPs, and MNPs. However, these platforms should not be considered merely as separate material categories. Their value in PCa theranostics depends on how material composition and structural design are translated into biological behavior, diagnostic sensitivity, therapeutic efficacy, and clinical feasibility. Therefore, the following section analyzes representative nanoplatforms according to a design–function–application–translation framework, with particular attention to their capacity for multimodal imaging, targeted delivery, therapeutic response monitoring, and clinical implementation. This framework is intended to clarify the unique role of multimodal nanomaterials in bridging molecular diagnosis, precision therapy, and translational development in PCa (Figure 1). Lipid-Based Nanomaterials

Lipid-based nanomaterials, including liposomes, lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), and solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs), have emerged as one of the most representative platforms in PCa nanomedicine because of their distinctive compositional features and favorable delivery performance.31–33 These systems are typically composed of phospholipids, cholesterol, ionizable lipids, or solid lipids. Because their structures closely resemble biological membranes, they generally exhibit favorable biocompatibility, high drug-loading capacity, and good adaptability for in vivo delivery.31–34 Lipid-based nanomaterials also offer substantial structural tunability, as their performance can be optimized by modulating lipid composition, particle size, surface charge, and surface functionalization.31,33,34 More importantly, differences in the structural organization of individual lipid-based systems shape their biological behavior and application profiles. For instance, liposomes are particularly well suited for the encapsulation of chemotherapeutic agents and imaging cargoes, whereas LNPs are more advantageous for nucleic acid delivery and SLNs show superior performance in improving the stability of hydrophobic drugs and enabling sustained release.3–34 Accordingly, lipid-based nanomaterials should not be regarded as a single carrier class, but rather as multifunctional platforms that can be tailored to the diagnostic and therapeutic needs of PCa. This structure–function relationship provides the material basis for imaging enhancement, biosensing, targeted delivery, and theranostic integration, which are discussed in greater detail in the following thematic sections.31–34 In addition, surface modification with PSMA-targeting ligands can further enhance the active targeting capability of lipid-based nanomaterials toward PCa cells.35,36

In diagnostic applications, lipid-based nanomaterials primarily serve as carriers for imaging probes and as platforms for signal amplification.21,37 Compared with free small-molecule probes, liposomes, LNPs, and lipid nanobubbles can improve the in vivo stability of imaging components, prolong retention at lesion sites, and enhance the recognition of PCa-associated molecular markers through surface engineering, thereby improving signal accumulation and imaging contrast within tumors.21,37,38 This potential is particularly evident in PSMA-centered molecular imaging, where lipid-based nanoplatforms have shown considerable promise.35,37–39 Previous studies have demonstrated that lipid nanobubbles loaded with anti-PSMA moieties can generate stronger and more durable ultrasound enhancement signals in PCa models, thereby improving the detection of PSMA-positive lesions.38,39 These findings suggest that lipid-based nanomaterials may partially overcome the limitations of conventional imaging modalities in sensitivity and specificity through targeted recognition, local accumulation, and signal amplification.37–39 Nevertheless, current evidence remains largely confined to preclinical investigations. Further translation is still hindered by challenges including in vivo stability, background signal interference, and reproducibility, and their ultimate clinical utility remains to be fully established.21,37

Beyond the delivery of conventional imaging probes, radionuclide-based imaging has opened additional avenues for the diagnostic application of lipid-based nanomaterials in PCa.21,40 Compared with free radiotracers, liposomes can not only encapsulate radionuclides but also enhance the recognition of PCa-associated biomarkers through surface conjugation with targeting ligands, thereby increasing signal accumulation at tumor sites and improving imaging contrast between malignant lesions and surrounding normal tissues.21,35 For example, Yari et al developed a liposomal platform functionalized with a PSMA-targeting ligand and labeled with technetium-99m. This system showed more than a threefold increase in uptake in PSMA-positive human PCa cells (LNCaP) relative to non-targeted liposomes.35 These findings highlight the importance of active targeting in lipid-based diagnostic systems: PSMA ligands promote the selective binding of liposomes to tumor cells, whereas radiolabeling enables noninvasive visualization and tracking. Building on this concept, Xiang et al proposed a strategy that integrates PSA responsiveness with PSMA-mediated targeting, in which a sequential design based on receptor recognition and microenvironmental activation enhances selective accumulation within PCa tissue.41 Compared with non-targeted nanosystems, this multilayered targeting strategy may improve discrimination between tumor and normal tissues. Nevertheless, issues such as radiolabeling stability, manufacturing complexity, and nonspecific in vivo uptake may still hinder further clinical translation.21,35,40

In therapeutic settings, lipid-based nanomaterials are primarily used as platforms for drug and gene delivery. Owing to their favorable biocompatibility and readily tunable surfaces, liposomes, LNPs, and solid lipid nanoparticles not only improve the stability of therapeutic cargoes but also enhance selective delivery to PCa cells through surface engineering.42–44 For example, polyethylene glycol (PEG)-modified docetaxel-loaded solid lipid nanoparticles have been shown to promote cellular uptake in PCa and to improve antitumor activity.42 Beyond conventional chemotherapeutic delivery, Mizutani et al developed a liposomal system encapsulating a PSA promoter-driven perforin expression vector, which produced marked tumor suppression in a 22Rv1DR xenograft model, supporting the feasibility of lipid-based platforms for PCa-specific gene therapy.43 Moreover, core–shell lipid–polymer hybrid nanoparticles co-loaded with docetaxel and FTY720 were found to resensitize castration-resistant PCa to chemotherapy while reducing the effective dose by approximately fourfold.44 Collectively, these findings highlight the promise of lipid-based nanomaterials for improving therapeutic selectivity, reducing systemic toxicity, and enabling multimodal treatment strategies in PCa.42–44

Although lipid-based nanomaterials in PCa have been primarily developed as carriers for drug and gene delivery, their interactions with tumor cells still depend largely on specific recognition mediated by surface ligands. PCa-associated targets, particularly PSMA, have been widely used for the surface functionalization of liposomes and hybrid lipid nanoparticles, thereby enhancing nanocarrier binding to tumor cell receptors, promoting cellular uptake, and improving intracellular delivery efficiency. Similarly, transferrin-modified hybrid lipid nanoparticles have been shown to enhance the uptake and antiproliferative effects of therapeutic payloads in human PCa cell lines, including PC-3 and LNCaP.35,42,45 Meanwhile, lipid-based systems can be engineered with pH-sensitive or protease-responsive properties, enabling structural destabilization or triggered release within the acidic and enzyme-rich tumor microenvironment. Such responsive designs facilitate the effective transport of drugs or nucleic acids at tumor sites and within intracellular compartments.41,45 Previous studies have shown that PEGylated docetaxel-loaded solid lipid nanoparticles enhance drug uptake and cytotoxicity in PC-3 cells, whereas PSMA ligand-functionalized liposomes and related lipid nanosystems show greater targeted delivery potential in PSMA-positive PCa models.35,42 In addition, transferrin-decorated hybrid lipid nanoparticles are capable of delivering not only docetaxel but also plasmid (DNA), underscoring the therapeutic potential of combined drug and gene delivery strategies in PCa.45 Nevertheless, dense stromal barriers within PCa tissue, heterogeneous target expression, and mononuclear phagocyte system-mediated clearance may still limit deep penetration, homogeneous intratumoral distribution, and long-circulation stability of lipid-based nanomaterials. Their further translation will therefore depend on achieving a balance among targeting accuracy, microenvironmental responsiveness, biosafety, and manufacturability.32,41

Polymeric Nanoparticles

Polymeric nanoparticles (PNPs) are mainly composed of natural or synthetic polymers, such as poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), PEG, and chitosan. By modulating polymer composition, particle size, morphology, surface chemistry, degradation rate, and ligand density, their physicochemical and biological properties can be optimized, thereby providing a material basis for diagnostic and therapeutic applications in PCa.46 These nanoparticles generally exhibit favorable structural stability and biocompatibility, and their in vivo behavior can be improved through surface modification or ligand functionalization.47 Some polymeric nanoparticles also possess biodegradability. For example, PLGA nanoparticles can undergo hydrolysis in vivo to generate lactic acid and glycolic acid, which are subsequently eliminated through normal metabolic pathways, thereby reducing long-term toxicity to some extent.48 PEG-modified polymeric nanoparticles have been widely investigated in PCa and can serve not only as drug carriers but also as imaging agents. Drug-loaded nanoparticles may accumulate in tumor tissues through the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, thereby increasing local drug concentration and improving therapeutic efficacy. Meanwhile, nanoparticles loaded with contrast agents can enhance the performance of MRI or optical imaging, enabling more precise tumor localization.49 Targeted modification can improve nanoparticle specificity. For instance, ligand conjugation, including PSMA-targeted modification, can improve receptor-mediated recognition and provide a basis for targeted imaging and selective delivery.50 In recent years, emerging high-density polymeric nanoparticles have shown advantages over some conventional carriers in terms of drug-loading capacity and imaging performance. However, their long-term biocompatibility and the in vivo safety of their degradation products still require further evaluation, which remains a challenge for clinical translation.24

In PCa therapy, nanomedicine-based delivery systems have been extensively developed to improve tumor accumulation, controlled release, and the therapeutic index of anticancer agents, among which polymeric nanoparticles have attracted sustained interest because of their favorable biocompatibility, tunability, and multifunctional potential.15,47 For example, polymeric nanoparticles such as PEG-PLGA, when functionalized with PSMA-targeting ligands, can recognize PSMA-overexpressing PCa cells and enable the targeted delivery of small interfering RNA (siRNA) or chemotherapeutic agents, thereby enhancing intratumoral drug accumulation and antitumor efficacy.50,51 PSMA-targeted surface modification confers active targeting capability on these nanoparticles, allowing ligand–receptor recognition to promote specific binding to tumor cells and induce receptor-mediated endocytosis, thus improving cellular uptake efficiency.50,51 At the same time, nanoparticle delivery in solid tumors still depends partly on passive accumulation resulting from abnormal tumor vasculature and impaired lymphatic drainage. However, recent studies have emphasized that the EPR effect is markedly heterogeneous across different tumor models and clinical settings; therefore, synergistic designs integrating active targeting with optimized tumor transport mechanisms may be of greater practical relevance.52 In addition, by incorporating photoresponsive or other functional modules, polymeric nanoparticles can convert external light energy into heat under physical stimulation, thereby enabling photothermal therapy, or can be combined with photodynamic therapy and chemotherapy to achieve synergistic therapeutic effects.53–55 For example, integrating PSMA-targeting strategies with near-infrared photothermal or photodynamic functional moieties allows localized thermal injury or reactive oxygen species-mediated damage in PCa under external irradiation, thus providing a basis for combination treatment.53 Nevertheless, current evidence indicates that photothermal therapy still faces several limitations, including heterogeneous heat distribution, heat shock protein-mediated tolerance, nonspecific injury to adjacent tissues, and inflammatory responses, all of which may hinder its further clinical translation.54,55

From a mechanistic perspective, surface-functionalized nanoparticles can achieve specific cellular recognition and uptake through ligand–receptor interactions.50,51 Upon external stimulation, the functional components embedded in these nanoparticles are able to convert exogenous energy into heat, thereby inducing membrane damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, reactive oxygen species (ROS) imbalance, and the activation of apoptosis or other programmed cell death pathways.54,55 In addition, some multifunctional nanoplatforms may further enhance overall therapeutic responsiveness by modulating tumor cell metabolism, vascular-related effects, or the immune microenvironment.56,57 With respect to payload delivery, polymeric nanoparticles generally exhibit favorable biocompatibility, drug-encapsulation capacity, and controllable release behavior, making them one of the major platforms currently explored for nanomedicine-based therapy in PCa.15,47,50 By comparison, certain metal-based or carbon-based nanomaterials may offer superior photothermal conversion efficiency, but their long-term in vivo accumulation, limited biodegradability, and potential risks of oxidative stress and inflammation still require further evaluation.54,58 For degradable polymers such as PEG-PLGA, the major degradation products are lactic acid and glycolic acid; however, them in vivo degradation kinetics, pharmacokinetic behavior, and long-term safety in the complex tumor microenvironment remain to be more fully established.59 To address these challenges, recent efforts have increasingly focused on the development of multifunctional polymeric nanoplatforms integrating targeted delivery, stimulus responsiveness, imaging guidance, and combination therapy, with the aim of improving the precision of PCa treatment while reducing systemic toxicity, thereby providing new opportunities for precision theranostics in PCa.15,53,56,57

Gold Nanoparticles

AuNPs generally range from 1 to 100 nm in size and can be prepared by chemical reduction or physical methods, with considerable tunability in their size, morphology, and surface properties.60 Owing to their localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) effect and the ease with which their surfaces can be functionalized, AuNPs have shown broad potential in biomedical applications.60,61 In PCa research, AuNPs have been explored for a wide range of applications, including molecular imaging, drug delivery, photothermal therapy, and radiosensitization.16,61 Notably, the diagnostic and therapeutic performance of AuNPs is not determined by material composition alone, but depends strongly on key parameters such as particle size, morphology, and surface chemistry. These features can in turn influence their optical behavior, cellular uptake efficiency, and in vivo distribution, thereby ultimately shaping their imaging and therapeutic outcomes.60,61 In addition, PEGylation and targeting ligand conjugation are among the most commonly used surface-engineering strategies for AuNPs and can improve colloidal stability, biocompatibility, and in vivo delivery performance to some extent.62 For example, PSMA-targeted ultrasmall AuNPs have been investigated for multimodal imaging of PCa and have demonstrated favorable tumor-targeting capability.63 Other studies have also suggested that PSMA-functionalized AuNPs may possess radiosensitizing potential in PCa radiotherapy.64 Meanwhile, local photothermal therapy based on gold nanomaterials has also shown encouraging preclinical potential in PCa.16,65 Collectively, AuNPs combine imaging enhancement, therapeutic versatility, and flexible surface engineering, making them a representative nanoplatform for the precision diagnosis and multimodal therapy of PCa.16,61

At the level of diagnostic and therapeutic applications, gold nanomaterials offer distinct advantages for multimodal imaging and targeted treatment of PCa because of their high atomic number and unique optical properties. Functionalization of AuNPs with PSMA ligands enhances their selective recognition of, and local accumulation in, tumor cells with high PSMA expression, thereby improving imaging contrast and the accuracy of lesion localization. For example, AuNPs modified with PSMA antibodies or aptamers have been shown to significantly increase selective deposition in PSMA-positive PCa cells (LNCaP) and to produce markedly stronger signal enhancement than non-targeted controls in CT and X-ray fluorescence imaging.66 In mouse models, PSMA-mediated targeted AuNPs have enabled dynamic monitoring of tumor accumulation by noninvasive imaging techniques, thereby supporting precision imaging and spatiotemporally resolved drug delivery, which is of particular importance for improving the localization accuracy of prostate tumors.63,67 Beyond their imaging-enhancing role, AuNPs have also demonstrated considerable potential in targeted drug delivery and combination therapy. AuNPs co-functionalized with PEG chains and PSMA ligands can facilitate the selective delivery of anticancer agents such as doxorubicin (DOX), thereby increasing local drug accumulation in tumors while reducing nonspecific exposure of healthy tissues. Furthermore, AuNPs possess high photothermal conversion efficiency and can induce controllable local heating under NIR irradiation, thereby acting synergistically with chemotherapeutic drugs to suppress tumor growth. When integrated with radiosensitization strategies, targeted AuNPs may further improve radiotherapy precision and enhance tumor inhibition.68,69 In addition, recent studies have used PSMA/folate dual-functionalized gold nanorods to construct combined photothermal and photodynamic therapeutic systems, which showed greater tumor-targeted accumulation and stronger synergistic efficacy in both in vitro and in vivo experiments, further supporting the potential of dual-targeting composite platforms for multimodal PCa therapy.70 Thus, gold nanomaterials should not be viewed merely as imaging enhancers or photothermal agents, but rather as versatile nanoplatforms capable of integrating imaging, drug delivery, phototherapy, and radiosensitization, thereby providing supportive preclinical evidence for their potential use in precision theranostics for PCa.

At the molecular level, AuNPs can engage in specific interactions with PCa cells and the tumor microenvironment through surface engineering. The gold core can accommodate hydrophobic drugs, such as DOX, via Au–S bonding or π–π interactions, whereas the outer PEG layer or other hydrophilic ligands can reduce protein corona formation, prolong blood circulation, and improve passive tumor accumulation. Further anchoring of PSMA ligands, small-molecule receptor ligands, or antibody fragments onto the AuNP surface enables active targeting of PSMA-overexpressing tumor cells and promotes receptor-mediated endocytosis, thereby enhancing drug delivery efficiency at tumor sites.67,71 Within the tumor microenvironment, AuNPs can also be engineered as stimulus-responsive carriers sensitive to acidic pH or overexpressed proteases, allowing drug release to be triggered under local TME conditions while inducing ROS generation. These effects can disrupt cellular homeostasis and promote the inactivation of heat shock proteins (HSP70/HSP90), thereby working synergistically with photothermal therapy (PTT) to reduce treatment resistance.72,73 In addition, as high-atomic-number (high-Z) materials, AuNPs can enhance local dose deposition during radiotherapy and increase the radiosensitivity of tumor cells. Previous studies have shown that targeted AuNPs can significantly improve the radiosensitivity of PCa cells under megavoltage (MV) irradiation and enhance radiotherapeutic efficacy in both in vitro and in vivo models, highlighting their promise as radiosensitizers.64,74 Nevertheless, several critical barriers still hinder the further clinical translation of AuNPs. On the one hand, protein corona formation in complex physiological environments may mask targeting ligands, alter biodistribution, and reduce their targeting efficiency.75 On the other hand, because AuNPs are intrinsically non-biodegradable, their long-term accumulation in organs such as the liver and spleen may raise concerns regarding chronic toxicity, which has become a major safety issue limiting their translational potential.76 Accordingly, current efforts are increasingly focused on modulating protein corona formation and in vivo distribution behavior through strategies such as surface chemical engineering, stimulus-responsive ligands, and controlled degradable architectures, with the aim of improving tumor retention, reducing off-target toxicity, and advancing the clinical application of AuNP-based systems.77

Magnetic Nanoparticles

MNPs are a class of nanostructures built around magnetic cores and have attracted broad interest in biomedical applications because of their tunable magnetic responsiveness. Representative MNPs are typically composed of superparamagnetic iron oxides, such as Fe3O4 and γ-Fe2O3, or ferrite materials doped with transition metals, with particle sizes generally ranging from 10–100 nm. At this scale, the combined effects of size confinement and magnetic anisotropy endow these particles with superparamagnetic behavior, enabling rapid response and directed migration under an external magnetic field and thereby providing an important material basis for targeted drug delivery, magnetic hyperthermia therapy (MHT), and MRI.78,79 Notably, the magnetic responsiveness of MNPs is determined not only by material composition but also by particle size, morphology, and surface chemistry. Smaller MNPs often exhibit a higher specific surface area and shorter magnetic anisotropy relaxation times and therefore may display more favorable superparamagnetic behavior and greater specific loss power. Meanwhile, surface modification strategies such as PEGylation can form a hydrophilic “stealth” layer, thereby reducing clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) and prolonging blood circulation. Further incorporation of targeting ligands, including antibodies, peptides, or small molecules, can enhance active recognition of tumor-associated receptors and improve local tumor accumulation.80,81 Taken together, MNPs provide a material foundation for PCa theranostics by integrating magnetic responsiveness, physical regulation, imaging capability, and targeted delivery within a single platform.

The therapeutic potential of MNPs in PCa is particularly notable, largely because magnetic responsiveness can simultaneously support targeted delivery, controlled release, and image-guided intervention. PSMA, a transmembrane protein highly expressed on PCa cells but relatively limited in normal tissues, provides an ideal molecular target for the active targeting design of magnetic nanoplatforms. By introducing PSMA-targeting ligands, MNPs can improve the selective accumulation of drug-loaded nanocarriers in tumor tissues and promote membrane receptor-mediated uptake, thereby enhancing therapeutic selectivity. For example, Zhang et al developed a magnetic lipid-based nanoplatform incorporating Glu-urea-Lys (GUL) as a PSMA-targeting ligand and encapsulating siRNA, in which drug release was triggered by an external alternating magnetic field (AMF) in a mouse PCa model. This system not only enhanced T2-weighted MRI contrast but also induced ferroptosis through targeted silencing of the YY1 gene, resulting in marked inhibition of tumor growth.24,78,82–85 This study suggests that PSMA-targeted MNPs may serve not only as imaging probes but also as platforms for spatiotemporal control of therapy under external magnetic fields, thus enabling genuinely image-guided precision treatment. Beyond nucleic acid delivery, MHT represents one of the most characteristic therapeutic applications of MNPs in PCa. In this strategy, MNPs are activated by AMF to generate heat locally within tumors, with tissue temperatures typically maintained in the range of 42–46 °C to directly induce cancer cell death or enhance tumor sensitivity to chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Previous studies have shown that PEGylated MNPs, following systemic administration, can raise local tumor temperature into the therapeutic range under AMF exposure and effectively suppress tumor growth without causing overt systemic toxicity.81,86 In addition, review studies have indicated that MHT can be combined with thermosensitive drug release, immunotherapy, and radiotherapy to further improve therapeutic efficacy while mitigating adverse effects86–89 (Figure 2). Therefore, the value of MNPs in PCa lies not merely in their ability to generate heat or enhance imaging, but more importantly in their capacity to integrate drug delivery, hyperthermia, and therapeutic monitoring through magnetic responsiveness. In imaging-based diagnosis, MNPs can markedly alter the local magnetic field owing to their strong magnetic responsiveness, thereby enhancing MRI contrast, particularly in T2-weighted imaging, and making the signal distinction between tumor and normal tissues more pronounced. Previous studies have shown that PSMA-targeted MNPs designed for PCa can significantly improve lesion contrast on T2-weighted MRI, thereby increasing the accuracy of tumor localization and volumetric monitoring.82–84 In addition, MPI, an emerging imaging modality developed in recent years, directly detects the magnetic response of MNPs without interference from background tissue signals, enabling lesion detection with higher sensitivity and improved spatial resolution. In one study, by optimizing the magnetic core size of MNPs and combining this design with an outer membrane engineered to display PSMA-targeting antibody fragments, MPI was able to detect extremely small numbers of tumor cells and achieve highly sensitive identification of micrometastatic bone lesions.90,91 Beyond simple imaging enhancement, another major advantage of MNPs lies in their ability to enable image-guided therapeutic intervention through multifunctional integration. In multimodal platform design, the incorporation of MNPs into drug carriers, targeted inhibitory molecules, or stimuli-responsive materials allows heat-triggered drug release, image-guided delivery, and controllable treatment within lesion sites. Relevant studies have shown that such nanoplatforms can not only achieve magnetically guided targeting and MRI visualization, but also enable thermally controlled siRNA release under AMF stimulation and induce ferroptosis, thereby significantly improving therapeutic efficacy and safety. These findings provide a representative example of how MNPs may support true theranostic integration in PCa.24,78,82–85 From the perspective of material design, performance optimization of MNPs requires the coordinated consideration of particle size, surface modification, and carrier construction strategies. Smaller MNPs generally favor prolonged circulation and improved tumor penetration, whereas PEGylation and modification with targeting peptides or antibodies help enhance targeting specificity and reduce nonspecific adsorption in off-target tissues. Further encapsulation of MNPs within polymeric nanoparticles, liposomes, or core–shell architectures not only facilitates stable drug loading and controlled release, but also enables the integration of magnetic navigation and magnetic hyperthermia functions, thereby improving the efficiency of targeted delivery and thermal responsiveness during treatment.78,82–84,86,92 Thus, the advantages of MNPs do not reside solely in a single imaging or hyperthermia function, but rather in their capacity to achieve synergistic optimization among structure, delivery behavior, and physical responsiveness through rational design. Despite the encouraging imaging and therapeutic performance of MNPs in animal models, their further clinical translation still faces multiple challenges. Key issues include long-term in vivo safety, controllability of drug-release kinetics, the effective range of external magnetic fields for deep-seated tumors, batch-to-batch consistency, and potential concerns related to biocompatibility and immune responses. In addition, the parameter settings of clinical magnetic-guidance devices, real-time monitoring technologies, and standardized operating procedures all remain to be further optimized.86–90,92–94 Therefore, for MNPs to move meaningfully toward clinical application, more robust research and evaluation frameworks will still be required, particularly in the areas of systemic toxicology assessment, predictable release-control strategies, and standardized manufacturing processes.90 Overall, through refined material design and surface functionalization, MNPs enable targeted drug delivery, controlled release, magnetic hyperthermia-assisted therapy, and highly sensitive imaging enhancement, thereby providing a multifunctional platform for the precision diagnosis and treatment of solid tumors such as PCa. With continued advances in nanomaterial synthesis, biofunctionalization strategies, and real-time imaging technologies, MNPs are expected to drive tumor therapy toward greater efficacy, safety, and personalization, and to further support their eventual clinical translation.

Application of Nanomaterials in the Diagnosis of PCa

Having outlined the material design and structure–function relationships of different nanoplatforms, this section focuses on diagnostic applications in PCa, including imaging enhancement, molecular imaging probes, biosensing platforms, and liquid biopsy-related detection. In general, nanomaterials can not only enhance lesion visualization through imaging augmentation, but also enable highly sensitive and minimally invasive molecular detection through biosensing platforms and early detection strategies.79,95 These technologies have the potential to improve the early detection rate and diagnostic accuracy of PCa, thereby providing a more reliable basis for clinical stratification and therapeutic decision-making, and may become key components in the translational pathway of nanodiagnostics.80 On this basis, the following sections focus on recent advances in the application of nanomaterials to imaging-based diagnosis, nano-contrast agents and nanoprobes, and biosensing detection in PCa (Table 1). Nanotechnology-Enhanced Imaging Diagnosis of PCa

Although multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI) and molecular imaging techniques such as PSMA PET/CT and PET/MRI have substantially improved tumor localization and staging in PCa, they still exhibit important limitations in the detection of small lesions, the identification of multifocal disease, and the discrimination between benign and malignant lesions. These limitations are largely attributable to tumor heterogeneity and the intrinsic constraints of imaging resolution.96 Conventional imaging modalities, including CT and standard MRI, still show limited sensitivity and specificity for early small-volume lesions, while currently available molecular imaging approaches are also unable to fully overcome the interference caused by the complexity of the tumor microenvironment and interindividual variability.97 Previous studies have shown that the combination of PSMA PET and MRI can improve overall diagnostic performance; however, a single imaging modality remains insufficient to comprehensively capture both molecular and anatomical information simultaneously.16 Recent guideline-level evidence has further clarified the clinical role of PSMA PET/CT in PCa imaging. The joint EANM/SNMMI procedure guideline provides updated standards for the indication, acquisition, interpretation, and reporting of PSMA PET/CT in PCa, supporting its use in staging, restaging, and theranostic evaluation.10 In addition, recent prospective evidence suggests that ^18F-PSMA-1007 PET/CT may provide higher accuracy than multiparametric MRI for locoregional staging in intermediate- and high-risk PCa, highlighting the complementary value of PSMA-based molecular imaging for tumor localization and treatment planning.98 Therefore, nanomaterial-based multimodal probes may be most clinically relevant when positioned as complementary tools for early detection, PSMA PET/MRI-guided lesion characterization, identification of small or multifocal lesions, and monitoring of metastatic foci.16,96,97 Against this background, nanomaterials have emerged as promising carriers for novel imaging contrast agents and may complement conventional imaging techniques. By constructing nanocontrast agents capable of targeting PCa-associated receptors such as PSMA or GRPR, selective binding to tumor lesions and local accumulation can be achieved, thereby enhancing imaging sensitivity and specificity. In molecular imaging studies of PCa, PSMA-targeted nanoprobes have been employed to improve contrast enhancement in modalities such as MRI and PET. For example, the conjugation of targeting peptides with superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles enables specific in vivo accumulation and enhances MRI contrast signals.99 Meanwhile, nanoparticle-based platforms may be further developed into multimodal probes by integrating MRI with optical imaging and other signal modalities, thereby improving the detection of small lesions.100 Nevertheless, substantial barriers remain to the clinical translation of nano-imaging technologies. On the one hand, the in vivo distribution of nanoprobes is highly dependent on their particle size, surface modification, and biodegradation properties, all of which directly influence targeting efficiency and off-target uptake in normal tissues. On the other hand, nanomaterials may still induce toxic effects or undergo rapid clearance during systemic circulation. In addition, most current nano-imaging strategies remain at the preclinical stage, and further evidence is required to validate their performance and safety in real-world clinical settings. Future advances in the optimization of targeting capability, biocompatibility, and multimodal integration, together with the incorporation of more sophisticated image analysis approaches, may provide a more robust technical foundation for the early and precise diagnosis of PCa.101

Nanocontrast Agents, Nanoprobes, and Nanosensing Platforms for PCa Diagnosis

Nanocontrast agents, nanoprobes, and nano-biosensing platforms have become important components of molecular diagnostic research in PCa, particularly because of their considerable potential in targeted imaging and biomarker detection.95,99,100,102,103 From a design perspective, these nanoplatforms are typically surface-functionalized with antibodies, peptides, aptamers, or small-molecule ligands to enable the specific recognition of PCa-associated targets. Among these targets, PSMA remains one of the most extensively investigated and widely applied;95,100 In addition, receptors such as the gastrin-releasing peptide receptor (GRPR) have also been exploited in the design of certain PCa-targeted nanoprobes.95 On this basis, target-recognition events can be converted into detectable signals, including magnetic resonance, fluorescence, and photoacoustic signals, thereby enabling the visualization and quantitative analysis of tumor-associated targets.99,104,105 In terms of material composition, PCa-related nanodiagnostic platforms mainly include MNPs, metallic nanoparticles, quantum dots, and polymer-based nanoplatforms.99,103,104,106 From an application perspective, these platforms can be used for MRI contrast enhancement, fluorescence/photoacoustic imaging, and nanomaterial-assisted biosensing assays.99,103,104,106 Compared with conventional molecular probes or routine detection methods, nanoplatforms generally offer several advantages, including a large specific surface area, ease of surface functionalization, the ability to incorporate signal amplification strategies, and the potential for multimodal imaging and integrated detection.103,104,107 Among them, superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) and their derivative systems represent one of the most extensively studied classes of MRI nanocontrast agents. Preclinical studies have shown that PSMA-targeted SPIONs can enhance MRI contrast in PCa models and may improve lesion visualization and detection performance.95,100 Furthermore, recently developed dual-modal MRI/fluorescence nanoprobes suggest that a single nanoplatform may enable the synergistic acquisition of multimodal imaging information, thus combining anatomical/tissue localization with molecularly specific characterization.99 In addition to MNPs, metallic nanomaterials represented by AuNPs have also been investigated in PCa-related CT, fluorescence, and photoacoustic imaging, demonstrating certain potential for multimodal applications.104,106 Meanwhile, photoacoustic imaging, as an emerging imaging modality for PCa, has also shown promising prospects for diagnosis and disease management.105 Quantum dot-based nanomaterials have attracted sustained interest in PCa-related biosensing and biomarker detection because of their high fluorescence intensity, good photostability, and tunable emission properties; however, their biocompatibility, in vivo safety, and feasibility for clinical translation still require further evaluation.107 It is crucial to highlight that nanocontrast agents/nanoprobes employed in in vivo or ex vivo molecular imaging differ in both application context and evaluation criteria from nano-biosensing platforms used for the analysis of bodily fluid samples, such as blood or urine.103,107 The former primarily facilitates lesion visualization, target expression assessment, and targeted imaging studies, whereas the latter is predominantly used for the detection of protein or nucleic acid biomarkers, including PSA, prostate cancer antigen 3 (PCA3), and miR-141.103,107 In recent years, AuNPs, graphene, carbon nanotubes, and other composite nanomaterials have been widely incorporated in the development of PCa biosensing platforms. These materials enhance detection sensitivity, reduce detection limits, and improve analytical performance by improving electron transfer, increasing the loading of recognition molecules, and amplifying interfacial signals.103,107 As a result, the integration of nanomaterials with molecular imaging and biosensing technologies has paved the way for novel approaches to early detection and precise diagnosis of PCa.103,104,107 Despite these advances, the clinical translation of nanocontrast agents and nanoprobes remains fraught with numerous challenges. For nano-imaging probes, factors such as nonspecific uptake, protein corona formation, complex in vivo distribution, and unclear clearance mechanisms may severely impact their targeting efficiency and imaging reliability;108 In the case of nano-biosensing platforms, issues such as matrix interference in complex biological samples, inadequate stability of sensing interfaces, and poor batch-to-batch consistency may undermine the accuracy and reproducibility of detection results.103,107 Future research efforts should be focused on enhancing the targeting specificity, biocompatibility, and signal stability of these nanoplatforms, in addition to advancing the integration of multimodal and multifunctional nanosystems.104,108 Moreover, integrating artificial intelligence (AI) and multisource data analysis into these platforms could significantly improve the accuracy and reliability of PCa imaging interpretation, biomarker identification, and diagnostic evaluation, thereby providing more robust technical support for the early detection and precise diagnosis of PCa.109

Application of Nanomaterials in the Treatment of PCa

Following the discussion of nanomaterials in PCa diagnosis, it is equally necessary to review their research progress in treatment.21 To date, the application of nanomaterials in PCa therapy mainly involves nanoparticle drug delivery, nanophototherapy, nano-immunotherapy, gene therapy, and combination therapy strategies.15,21,29 From a clinical perspective, these therapeutic strategies should be interpreted in relation to metastatic castration-resistant PCa, bone metastatic disease, drug resistance, and their potential integration with androgen receptor (AR) pathway inhibitors, radioligand therapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy.15,21,29,110,111 Recent phase III evidence from PSMAfore showed that ^177Lu-PSMA-617 improved radiographic progression-free survival compared with a change of AR pathway inhibitor in taxane-naive patients with PSMA-positive metastatic castration-resistant PCa.12 Updated final analyses further reported no statistically significant overall survival difference in the intention-to-treat population, likely influenced by the high crossover rate, while confirming a favorable safety profile without new safety signals.11 Procedure guidelines and best-practice recommendations for ^177Lu-labeled PSMA radioligand therapy also emphasize appropriate patient selection, treatment administration, toxicity monitoring, radiation safety, and response assessment.112,113 These clinical developments provide an important translational reference for PCa nanotheranostic platforms that aim to integrate targeting, imaging, therapy, and response monitoring. Existing studies suggest that nanoplatforms play a significant role in improving treatment selectivity, enhancing local efficacy, optimizing pharmacokinetics, and modulating the tumor microenvironment, with considerable potential in immunomodulation and multimodal combination therapies.15,110,111 Based on this, the following discussion will focus on the main advances in the application of nanomaterials in PCa therapy across these areas (Table 2). Nanoparticle-Based Drug Delivery Systems (NDDS)

Nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems (NDDS) are therapeutic and diagnostic platforms constructed using nanomaterials, with the core design goal of improving the delivery efficiency and therapeutic selectivity of drugs or imaging agents within tumor tissues.24,114 Focusing on the core issue of tumor-targeted delivery, NDDS primarily includes several basic modes, including passive targeting, active targeting, immune targeting, and magnetic targeting.24,114–116 Passive targeting primarily relies on the EPR effect, which is a result of the abnormal permeability of tumor vasculature and limited lymphatic drainage. This allows nanoparticles to accumulate to some extent in tumor tissues, thereby enhancing local drug exposure and reducing nonspecific distribution to normal tissues.114,117 Active targeting typically involves the incorporation of recognition molecules such as peptides, antibodies, or carbohydrates on the surface of the nanoparticle carriers to enhance their specific binding with high-expression receptors on the tumor cell surface, thereby promoting cellular uptake and improving delivery selectivity. In PCa research, this strategy is commonly combined with molecular targets such as PSMA24 (Figure 3). In contrast, magnetic targeting mainly utilizes magnetic materials such as iron oxide nanoparticles to respond to external magnetic fields, enabling physical guidance and local accumulation during the delivery process. This demonstrates potential for integrated diagnostic and therapeutic applications.115 Immune targeting emphasizes the use of antibodies, vaccines, or tumor-associated antigens to activate or amplify anti-tumor immune responses, improving therapeutic responses and providing new avenues for the combined application of NDDS and tumor immunotherapy.116,118 Overall, these targeting modes are not mutually exclusive but rather work together at various levels, such as distribution in the body, lesion recognition, cellular internalization, and local control, to determine the delivery efficiency of nanoparticles, thereby laying the foundation for enhancing the precision and effectiveness of tumor therapies114,118(Table 3). In addition to targeting modes, PCa NDDS can be further designed to respond to the tumor microenvironment or external physical signals, enabling spatiotemporally controlled drug release.114,115,120,155 Prostate tumors are often associated with local acidification, redox imbalance, and specific enzyme activity abnormalities, all of which can serve as triggers for stimulus-responsive nanocarriers.114,117,155 Consequently, nanoparticles can remain relatively stable in the bloodstream and selectively release the encapsulated drug at the tumor site or after cellular internalization by tumor cells.114,117,155 Besides endogenous stimuli, external signals such as light, heat, ultrasound, and magnetic fields can also regulate the release behavior, further enhancing the local selectivity and controllability of treatment.114,115,120 For PCa, such designs are particularly suitable for the delivery of chemotherapy drugs, nucleic acid drugs, and combination therapy formulations, as they not only increase drug exposure within the tumor but also help reduce nonspecific damage to normal tissues.24,114,115,120,155 Furthermore, nanocarriers are not merely passive transport tools; the material properties themselves also determine the delivery efficiency.114 Through rational structural design, nanoparticle platforms can improve the encapsulation capacity for hydrophobic drugs and biomacromolecules, enhance drug stability, and reduce the risk of premature release during circulation.114,118,155 This is particularly critical for commonly used small molecule drugs, siRNA, and other functional molecules in PCa treatment, as these drugs are often limited by poor solubility, rapid in vivo degradation, or high systemic toxicity.24,114,118,155 Additionally, nanoplatforms can integrate targeting ligands, imaging modules, and multiple therapeutic components, enabling targeted delivery, controlled release, and therapeutic monitoring within a single platform.24,114,115,120 Overall, NDDS with stimulus responsiveness and high drug-loading capacity provides an important platform for improving the precision of PCa treatment, reducing systemic toxicity, and supporting multimodal combination interventions.24,114,115,118,120,155

Nanomaterial-based delivery systems can also be further developed as multimodal therapeutic platforms, integrating strategies such as PTT, PDT, gene therapy, and immunotherapy to enhance the overall therapeutic effect in PCa.121,122,156,157 Leveraging the excellent design flexibility and functional integration capabilities of nanoplatforms, photosensitizers, photothermal conversion materials, nucleic acid drugs, and immunomodulatory molecules can be co-delivered within the same system, thereby enhancing tumor local accumulation while achieving multimodal synergistic intervention.121,122,156,157 In nucleic acid delivery, nanocarriers improve the stability of therapeutic RNA in vivo and promote cellular uptake. Among them, mRNA is more suitable for tumor vaccines or therapeutic protein expression, while siRNA/miRNA is more suitable for gene silencing.158–160 For instance, the gold nanorod-aluminum phthalocyanine tetrasulfonate complex system constructed by Jang et al showed a tumor growth inhibition rate of 79% with PDT alone and 95% with the combined PTT/PDT therapy in tumor-bearing mice, highlighting the clear synergistic advantage of nanoparticle-mediated combination phototherapy.161 Overall, NDDS not only expands the intervention dimensions for PCa treatment but also provides a crucial foundation for building multimodal synergistic precision-delivery and comprehensive therapeutic strategies.121,122,156,157,161

Although chemotherapy and radiotherapy remain important components of PCa treatment, acquired drug resistance continues to be one of the key factors contributing to treatment failure and tumor recurrence, particularly in castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC).162 Multidrug resistance (MDR) is one of the most representative manifestations, with its development involving various mechanisms, including drug efflux mediated by ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters (such as P-glycoprotein/P-gp, multidrug resistance-associated protein (MRP), and breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP), dysregulation of anti-apoptotic pathways, enhanced DNA repair capabilities, and the influence of acidic and hypoxic conditions within the tumor microenvironment. These factors collectively lead to a decrease in the effective concentration of chemotherapy drugs within tumor cells, thereby weakening therapeutic efficacy.163–165 In this context, nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems (NDDS) have increasingly demonstrated their significant value as an advanced drug delivery strategy. By improving the pharmacokinetic properties of drugs, such as enhancing stability, prolonging circulation time, and increasing drug accumulation in tumor regions, NDDS can improve therapeutic efficacy and reduce systemic toxicity.166,167 Additionally, nanocarriers can enter tumor cells through endocytosis, partially bypassing efflux pump-mediated clearance and thereby increasing intracellular drug accumulation. This provides a potential strategy for overcoming MDR.166 Furthermore, nanoparticle systems can co-deliver P-gp inhibitors or targeted siRNA to suppress the expression of resistance-related proteins, thereby enhancing drug sensitivity and reversing the resistant phenotype.168 When combined with receptor ligands, peptides, or antibodies for targeting modification, particularly PSMA-targeted strategies, NDDS can also enhance the specificity of recognition by PCa cells, enabling more precise drug delivery and further reducing exposure to normal tissues.24

Nanonucleic Acid Delivery Therapy

Nanonucleic acid delivery therapy has become a significant development direction in novel PCa treatment strategies, particularly showing great potential in CRPC. The basic approach involves using nanocarriers to deliver siRNA, microRNA(miRNA), plasmid DNA, or clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats/CRISPR-associated protein system (CRISPR/Cas) components to tumor tissues, thereby inhibiting tumor growth, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance by downregulating oncogenes, restoring tumor-suppressor signals, or editing key driver genes.15,123,124,165 Compared with traditional small-molecule drugs, nucleic acid-based therapies directly target the molecular driving networks of PCa, offering unique advantages in modulating the AR signaling pathway, resistance mediated by AR splice variants, and molecular events associated with migration and invasion.125,165 However, naked nucleic acids have poor stability in vivo, are susceptible to nuclease degradation, and face challenges such as low cellular uptake efficiency, limited endosomal escape, and insufficient effective distribution within tumors. These factors collectively limit their therapeutic efficacy in vivo.15,165

Nanodelivery systems provide a feasible solution to overcome these bottlenecks. LNPs, polymeric nanoparticles, inorganic nanomaterials, and biomimetic nanoplatforms not only improve the circulation stability of nucleic acid therapeutics but also enable active targeting through surface modification. In addition, pH-responsive, reduction-responsive, or membrane-penetrating designs can facilitate intracellular release.15,165 In PCa, PSMA is one of the most commonly used active targeting molecules. Studies have shown that nanoplatforms modified with PSMA ligands or aptamers can significantly enhance the accumulation and uptake of nucleic acid drugs in PSMA-positive PCa cells, thereby improving target gene silencing efficiency while reducing nonspecific toxicity.123,125,126 For example, LNPs delivering AR-siRNA can silence AR in vivo and inhibit the growth of PCa xenografts. Moreover, Glu-urea-Lys-modified targeted LNPs can further enhance siRNA delivery efficiency in PSMA-positive tumors, thereby increasing anti-tumor effects.123,126 These studies highlight that the key value of nanoplatforms lies not only in nucleic acid cargo delivery but also in their ability to convert otherwise difficult-to-deliver agents into viable therapeutic strategies through the coordinated design of cargo protection, active targeting, and intracellular release.

In addition to siRNA, miRNA replacement or compensation therapy also holds significant research value in PCa. miR-34a and miR-145 are representative tumor-suppressive miRNAs that are often downregulated in PCa and are involved in regulating cell proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, stemness maintenance, and metastasis.127,128 A chitosan nanoparticle-based miR-34a delivery system has been shown to significantly inhibit tumor growth in bone metastatic PCa and maintain bone tissue integrity. Moreover, a PCa-targeting polyarginine-disulfide-PEI nanocarrier enables the specific delivery of miR-145, significantly inhibiting tumor growth and prolonging animal survival in vivo.127,129 These results suggest that nanoplatforms not only improve the in vivo stability and tumor tissue distribution of miRNAs but also enhance their regulatory effects on tumor proliferation, invasion, and metastasis-related signaling networks.127–129 Therefore, miRNA nanodelivery is not merely about supplementing a single molecule but aims to intervene more deeply in the biological behavior of PCa by restoring the imbalanced regulatory network.

In recent years, the nanodelivery of the CRISPR/Cas system has further expanded the intervention boundaries of nucleic acid therapy for PCa. Unlike RNA-based interventions that mainly focus on transient silencing, CRISPR/Cas9 allows for targeted editing at the DNA level, providing new therapeutic opportunities for “undruggable” targets.130,165 Studies have shown that the CaP-TAT nanoplatform can deliver Cas9/sgRNA ribonucleoproteins and knockout CRPC-related target EphA2, thereby inhibiting tumor cell migration.124 Meanwhile, targeted nucleic acid delivery strategies designed around resistance-related molecules have also garnered continuous attention. For example, PSMA aptamer-mediated delivery of androgen receptor splice variant 7 (AR-V7) siRNA can simultaneously downregulate both full-length AR and AR-V7, restoring drug sensitivity in enzalutamide-resistant cells.125 Additionally, co-delivery systems of multiple siRNAs and chemotherapy drugs can simultaneously intervene in signaling pathways such as AR, survivin, and Bcl-2, enhancing apoptosis in PCa cells and generating synergistic anti-tumor effects.131 These studies suggest that the significance of nanonucleic acid delivery in PCa lies not only in single-target interventions but also in its ability to build more targeted and precise therapeutic strategies around resistance networks and driving pathways.

Overall, nanonucleic acid delivery therapy is driving the shift in PCa treatment from traditional cytotoxic interventions to molecular-level precision regulation. Its main advantages include the ability to protect nucleic acid drugs, enhance tumor-targeted accumulation, improve intracellular delivery efficiency, and support combined delivery and synergistic treatment designs.15,165 However, the field still faces several key obstacles to widespread clinical application, including long-term safety of carriers, batch consistency, controllable in vivo distribution, immunocompatibility, and off-target risks associated with gene editing.15,130,165 Therefore, future research should focus on more systematic and in-depth studies on material design, optimization of targeting mechanisms, and the construction of clinical translation evaluation systems, to promote the translation of nucleic acid nanotherapy into a verifiable, reproducible, and clinically beneficial therapeutic strategy for PCa.15,165

Sonodynamic Therapy and Chemodynamic Therapy

SDT and CDT have emerged as two dynamic therapeutic strategies that have attracted considerable attention in recent studies of nanomedicine-based treatment for PCa. SDT primarily relies on ultrasound to activate sonosensitizers, thereby generating ROS that induce tumor cell damage. Compared with photodynamic therapy, ultrasound offers superior tissue penetration, which confers a potential advantage for the treatment of deep-seated solid tumors such as PCa.15,132–134 By contrast, CDT mainly exploits the relatively enriched H2O2, acidic conditions, and metal ion-mediated Fenton or Fenton-like reactions within the tumor microenvironment to locally produce highly reactive species, such as hydroxyl radicals, thereby enabling selective oxidative damage to tumor cells.135–137,169 Despite their distinct mechanisms of action, both SDT and CDT are subject to several common limitations, including insufficient tumor accumulation of therapeutic components, pronounced heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment, and limited local ROS generation efficiency, all of which collectively constrain their in vivo therapeutic performance.132,135,137,169 In this context, nanoscale delivery systems have become a critical technological foundation for improving the therapeutic efficacy of both SDT and CDT. By constructing tumor microenvironment-responsive nanoplatforms, it is possible to enhance the accumulation of sonosensitizers or catalytic components in tumor tissues, while also promoting cellular uptake and intracellular release through pH-responsive, enzyme-responsive, or biomimetic modifications.15,132,133,135,137,169

For SDT, the principal value of nanomaterials lies in improving the stability of sonosensitizers, promoting their accumulation in tumor tissues, and amplifying ultrasound-triggered ROS generation. For CDT, nanoplatforms can serve as carriers for metal-based catalytic components, utilizing endogenous intratumoral H2O2 or incorporating functional modules such as glucose oxidase to further intensify oxidative stress and thereby enhance tumoricidal efficacy.132–134,136,137,169 From the broader perspective of nanomedicine development for PCa, the significance of these platforms extends beyond merely improving the efficiency of a single therapeutic modality. More importantly, they enhance adaptability to the tumor microenvironment and provide an integrable carrier basis for subsequent combination therapies.15,19,21,111,170 Therefore, whether in SDT or CDT, therapeutic efficacy largely depends on whether the nanoplatform can establish effective synergy among delivery efficiency, microenvironmental responsiveness, and ROS amplification.

Based on the currently available evidence, the research foundation for SDT in PCa appears to be relatively more direct. Hadi et al reported a dual pH/cathepsin B-responsive polymer–porphyrin nanoplatform formed by the self-assembly of a poly(L-glutamic acid-L-tyrosine) copolymer and hematoporphyrin. This system exhibited enhanced uptake in PCa cells. Upon ultrasound activation, it significantly increased cytotoxicity against both LNCaP and PC3 cells and markedly inhibited tumor growth in a xenograft model, without causing obvious systemic toxicity.171 These findings suggest that nanoplatforms rationally designed to respond to the tumor microenvironment may improve the selectivity and in vivo therapeutic efficacy of SDT in PCa, while also providing a promising strategy for localized and noninvasive intervention in deep-seated solid tumors.

In contrast, CDT-related studies in PCa remain largely at the preclinical stage, although they have already demonstrated promising potential. Hong et al developed liposome-encapsulated MnS-GOx nanoparticles, which achieved synergistic CDT in a PC-3 model through Mn2⁺-mediated Fenton-like reactions and glucose oxidase-enhanced H2O2 production, while also inducing ferroptosis and consequently producing significant antitumor effects.172 In addition, Xie et al constructed a biomimetic graphdiyne-based nanoplatform that combined enhanced CDT with cuproptosis induction, further expanding the mechanistic landscape of oxidative damage-based therapy in PCa.138 These studies indicate that the significance of CDT in PCa is not limited to functioning as a simple ROS amplification strategy; rather, it can also synergize with programmed cell death pathways such as ferroptosis and cuproptosis, thereby strengthening its antitumor efficacy and broadening its therapeutic mechanistic scope.

Overall, nanomaterials provide critical technical support for the implementation of SDT and CDT in PCa. Their central value lies in improving the delivery efficiency of therapeutic components, enhancing localized oxidative stress within tumors, and supporting the development of combination therapeutic strategies.132–137,169 Current evidence suggests that SDT has a relatively more direct research foundation in PCa, whereas CDT remains primarily in the preclinical exploratory stage.138,171,172 Future studies should further optimize their in vivo distribution, therapeutic depth, ROS generation efficiency, and long-term biosafety, while also promoting their integrated combination with chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and imaging-guided strategies, so as to enhance their potential for clinical translation in precision treatment of PCa.15,19,21,111,132,133,170

Nanomedicine-Based Photodynamic Therapy, Photothermal Therapy, and Photoimmunotherapy

Nanomedicine-mediated PDT, PTT, and photoimmunotherapy (PIT) represent several major directions in the development of optical therapies for PCa (Figure 4). PDT relies on photosensitizers that generate ROS upon irradiation at specific wavelengths, thereby inducing tumor cell damage. In some treatment modalities, particularly vascular-targeted photodynamic therapy (VTP), its therapeutic effect can also be partially attributed to the disruption of tumor-associated microvasculature.139–143 PTT, by contrast, employs photothermal conversion agents to transform light energy into heat, thereby achieving tumor ablation through localized hyperthermia.144–146,173 In contrast, PIT is fundamentally based on the conjugation of antibodies or other targeting ligands with photosensitizing moieties, thereby enabling the selective killing of specific target cells under near-infrared irradiation; current evidence further suggests that this strategy may also be accompanied by immunogenic cell death.174–176 From the perspective of technical implementation, the anatomical location of the prostate is well suited for local intervention and optical fiber-based irradiation, making optical therapies reasonably feasible in PCa. Although PDT, PTT, and PIT have all demonstrated considerable potential for localized and precise treatment, each of them also faces distinct limitations in the context of PCa. PDT is readily affected by factors such as tumor hypoxia, the hydrophobicity of photosensitizers, and insufficient tissue penetration depth. PTT, although capable of rapidly elevating local temperature and achieving focal ablation, may still be constrained by heterogeneous heat distribution and limited treatment depth. PIT, in turn, is highly dependent on a stable conjugation system as well as clearly defined and persistently expressed tumor-associated targets.144,147,174–176 Against this background, nanoscale delivery systems have attracted widespread attention primarily because they can improve the stability and in vivo distribution of therapeutic agents, enhance their accumulation at tumor sites, and provide a unified platform for imaging guidance, stimulus-responsive release, and combination therapy.144–149,173 Therefore, the role of nanoplatforms in optical therapy extends far beyond simply serving as carriers for photosensitizers or photothermal agents. More importantly, through delivery optimization and functional integration, they can transform therapeutic modalities that are otherwise limited by physical penetration barriers or unfavorable drug properties into more practical strategies for localized precision intervention.

Among the three optical therapeutic modalities, PDT—particularly padeliporfin-based vascular-targeted VTP—represents the approach that was explored earlier in PCa and is supported by relatively more direct clinical evidence. Clinical studies have shown that this strategy holds value for the focal treatment of localized low-risk PCa, making it one of the more clinically advanced optical therapeutic approaches in this setting.139–143 Beyond clinical VTP, recent efforts have sought to improve the selectivity and effective treatment depth of PDT in PCa through PSMA-targeted or upconversion nanoplatforms, thereby broadening its suitability for localized intervention.147–149 Taken together, the significance of PDT in PCa lies not only in its existing clinical foundation, but also in its continued evolution through nanotechnology toward greater selectivity, improved compatibility with deep tissue treatment, and more refined local therapeutic control. In contrast, research on PTT in PCa has been centered primarily on nanomaterial design and local ablation performance. A range of systems, including gold nanorods, gold nanostars, and PSMA-targeted melanin-like nanoplatforms, have been reported, collectively suggesting that these materials can enhance photothermal conversion efficiency while also improving tumor localization or facilitating combination with other treatment modalities.144–146,173 At present, the therapeutic promise of PTT in PCa remains grounded largely in preclinical evidence. Even so, as a localized physical treatment modality, PTT offers an interventional paradigm distinct from conventional signaling pathway inhibition, particularly for certain treatment-refractory forms of PCa.144,145 Its value, therefore, lies not simply in heat generation per se, but in its potential to function as a modular platform for localized treatment intensification that can be further integrated with drug delivery, imaging, or immunomodulatory strategies. Direct investigations of PIT in PCa remain relatively limited, although its underlying mechanism is conceptually well aligned with the broader logic of targeted therapy in this disease. Available preclinical evidence indicates that conjugation of anti-PSMA antibodies with IR700 can achieve pronounced selective cytotoxicity and tumor suppression in PSMA-positive PCa models.174 Thus, the promise of PIT in PCa stems less from the current volume of evidence than from its intrinsic compatibility with well-defined targets such as PSMA, which provides a natural basis for highly selective local therapy. Whether PIT can be further developed into a therapeutic strategy that combines direct cytotoxicity with immune activation, however, remains to be established and will require additional evidence in PCa.174–176 Overall, nanomaterials provide a critical enabling basis for the implementation of PDT, PTT, and PIT in PCa. Based on the current landscape of evidence, PDT/VTP has the most direct translational foundation, PTT is progressing rapidly at the preclinical level, whereas PIT remains under active development.139–149,173–176 Future work should focus on improving effective photoresponsiveness in deep tissues, enhancing tumor selectivity, ensuring long-term biosafety, and advancing the integration of these modalities with imaging, immunotherapy, and other precision therapeutic strategies.144–149,173–176

Nanomedicine-Based Immunotherapy for PCa

In the field of immunotherapy for PCa, the introduction of nanotechnology has opened new avenues for improving therapeutic efficacy. It should be noted that PCa, particularly advanced disease and metastatic castration-resistant PCa (mCRPC), is generally considered to exhibit low immunogenicity and a profoundly immunosuppressive phenotype, and is therefore regarded as a typical “cold” tumor. The efficacy of immunotherapy in this setting is often constrained by insufficient infiltration of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, and immune escape mediated by myeloid-derived suppressor cells and related signaling pathways.150,151,177–180 Although immunotherapy has achieved substantial progress across multiple malignancies, its clinical benefit in PCa remains relatively limited overall, with the exception of a few strategies such as sipuleucel-T; in particular, immune checkpoint blockade as monotherapy has thus far shown only modest efficacy, highlighting the urgent need for improved delivery approaches and rational combination strategies.181–183 Nanodelivery systems can enhance the therapeutic potential of immunotherapy in PCa by improving the in vivo stability, pharmacokinetic behavior, and lesion-site accumulation of immunotherapeutic agents.15,57,184 Commonly used nanoplatforms include polymeric nanoparticles, LNPs, inorganic nanomaterials, and metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), which can be employed to deliver immune checkpoint inhibitors, immunoadjuvants, tumor antigens, small-molecule immunomodulators, and nucleic acid therapeutics. In doing so, they may reduce systemic toxicity, prolong circulation time, and increase local drug exposure to a certain extent.15,57,184 However, these advantages are still supported primarily by preclinical evidence, and their clinical translation continues to face substantial challenges, including biosafety concerns, scalable manufacturing, heterogeneous in vivo distribution, and the reproducibility of therapeutic efficacy.15,57,184 Mechanistically, nanomaterials may potentiate immunotherapy in PCa through multiple complementary routes. First, nanocarriers can enable the preferential accumulation and controlled release of immunotherapeutic agents within primary tumors or metastatic lesions, thereby increasing local effective concentrations while reducing nonspecific exposure.15,57,184 Second, nanoplatforms can serve as carriers for antigens or adjuvants, facilitating their delivery to antigen-presenting cells such as dendritic cells and thereby enhancing antigen cross-presentation and tumor-specific T-cell responses.57,185,186 In addition, certain functionalized nanoplatforms can remodel the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in PCa by inducing immunogenic cell death or immunogenic ferroptosis, regulating tumor-associated macrophage polarization, reducing the accumulation of immunosuppressive cell populations, and promoting effector T-cell infiltration.57,150,151,177–180,185 Accordingly, the value of nanoplatforms in PCa immunotherapy extends well beyond simply enabling more efficient drug delivery; rather, their greater significance lies in their capacity to simultaneously modulate several critical processes, including antigen presentation, effector-cell activation, and microenvironmental reprogramming.

For example, in a preclinical PCa model, Wang et al developed a magnetically targetable nanozyme-based immunomodulatory platform (ZFPG NPs) to enhance nano-immunotherapy for PCa. This system was able to increase nanoparticle accumulation at the tumor site and alleviate the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by inducing immunogenic ferroptosis, promoting dendritic cell maturation, enhancing CD8+ T-cell infiltration, and reducing the proportion of myeloid-derived suppressor cells, thereby inhibiting tumor progression and metastasis.185 These findings suggest that the significance of multifunctional nanoplatforms in PCa extends beyond simply improving local drug exposure; more importantly, they hold the potential to integrate both tumoricidal and immune-activating effects, thereby providing preclinical support for sensitizing “cold” tumors to immunotherapy.185

In addition to the delivery of immune checkpoint inhibitors and immunomodulatory agents, nanovaccines have also emerged as an important direction in PCa immunotherapy. Nanoparticles can promote tumor-associated antigen-specific cytotoxic T-cell responses by co-delivering tumor antigens and immunoadjuvants, improving lymph node homing efficiency, and enhancing the activation of antigen-presenting cells.57,185,186 Accordingly, nanovaccines provide a rational theoretical basis for enhancing the immunogenicity and durability of immunotherapy in PCa, although their efficacy and clinical feasibility in this setting still require further investigation.57,185,186

Overall, nanotechnology offers multiple advantages in PCa immunotherapy, including improving drug delivery, enhancing tumor immunogenicity, modulating the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, and providing platform support for combination strategies involving immune checkpoint blockade, vaccines, and nucleic acid-based therapies.15,150,151,177–180,184–186 Taken together, nano-immunotherapy has shown encouraging preclinical potential in PCa, particularly in reprogramming the immune “cold” tumor microenvironment and improving the efficacy of combination treatments. Nevertheless, the current evidence remains derived predominantly from preclinical studies, and its clinical translational value still needs to be established through more systematic investigations into pharmacokinetics, safety, manufacturability, and clinical efficacy.15,57,184

Perspectives and Challenges

Overall, the rapid evolution of nanomaterials is reshaping the diagnosis and treatment of PCa, driving the field beyond conventional single-modality approaches toward increasingly precise, integrated, and personalized paradigms.15,20,21,57 Owing to their tunable size, tailorable surface physicochemical properties, favorable cargo-loading and protective capacities, and potential for multifunctional integration, nanoplatforms have emerged as versatile tools for molecular imaging enhancement, biomarker detection, targeted delivery, multimodal synergistic therapy, and theranostic applications.15,20,21,57,152 Notably, when coupled with PCa-relevant targets such as PSMA, nanomaterials provide an expanded technological basis for lesion identification, therapeutic stratification, and treatment response assessment.15,22,24 However, the intrinsic heterogeneity of PCa, together with persistent challenges related to the in vivo behavior, biosafety, scalable manufacturing, and clinical evaluation of nanomedicines, continues to constrain their broader translational advancement.15,20–22,153 Accordingly, a central challenge in PCa nanomedicine lies in how to improve manufacturability, reproducibility, and clinical translatability without compromising diagnostic or therapeutic performance. Importantly, different nanoplatforms should not be viewed as having the same translational readiness. Lipid-based nanomaterials and selected biodegradable polymeric nanoparticles may be relatively closer to clinical translation because of their favorable biocompatibility, formulation flexibility, cargo-loading capacity, and comparatively mature manufacturing basis. However, their PCa-specific application is still limited by heterogeneous tumor accumulation, nonspecific uptake, variable target expression, and batch-to-batch reproducibility.15,20–22,24,32,41 MNPs may occupy an intermediate position because they provide a clear rationale for MRI enhancement, magnetic navigation, and magnetic hyperthermia, but their clinical implementation still requires standardized magnetic-field parameters, device compatibility, heat-control accuracy, real-time monitoring, and long-term biosafety validation.78,86–90,92–94 In contrast, AuNPs, carbon-based nanomaterials, quantum dots, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), and many emerging hybrid systems remain mainly preclinical in PCa, largely because of long-term retention, limited biodegradability, organ accumulation, protein corona formation, immunotoxicity, and scalable quality-control challenges.54,58,75–77,103,107,108 Beyond differences in translational readiness, several practical bottlenecks require more systematic solutions. Protein corona formation may mask targeting ligands, alter nanoparticle surface properties, and reduce the reliability of PSMA- or ligand-mediated targeting; therefore, antifouling surface modification, biomimetic coatings, and corona-aware evaluation systems should be incorporated into future platform design.16,21,22,108,153 Nonspecific uptake by the MPS and heterogeneous tumor perfusion can reduce tumor accumulation and increase liver or spleen retention, suggesting the need for optimized particle size, morphology, surface charge, PEGylation or zwitterionic modification, and more clinically relevant orthotopic, metastatic, and bone-metastatic PCa models.16,21,22,28,108,153 Long-term biosafety concerns, especially for non-biodegradable inorganic nanomaterials, should be addressed through biodegradable or renal-clearable designs, standardized pharmacokinetic analysis, chronic toxicity assessment, immunotoxicity evaluation, and long-term organ-retention monitoring.54,58,75–77,108,153 In addition, manufacturing complexity and batch-to-batch variability remain major barriers for multifunctional nanoplatforms; simplified architectures, reproducible formulation procedures, clearly defined critical quality attributes, GMP-compatible scale-up, and standardized release criteria will be essential for future clinical translation.16,21,22,153 From a diagnostic perspective, nanomaterials offer a powerful technological framework for precision detection in PCa. Lipid-based nanomaterials, polymeric nanoparticles, AuNPs, and MNPs can all function as imaging probes or contrast-agent carriers for MRI, PET, fluorescence imaging, photoacoustic imaging, and ultrasound imaging. When integrated with targeting ligands such as PSMA, these systems can enhance signal accumulation at tumor sites, thereby improving the visualization of small lesions, multifocal disease, and metastatic foci.20,21,24,28,95,99 In parallel, nanomaterials can be incorporated into biosensing platforms for the highly sensitive detection of PSA, PCA3, exosomes, and associated nucleic acid biomarkers, thereby expanding the possibilities for noninvasive detection, early identification, and patient stratification in PCa.28,154,187 Even so, matrix interference, nonspecific adsorption, and background noise in complex biological samples, together with protein corona formation, nonspecific uptake, and variability in systemic clearance in vivo, may all undermine the targeting performance, signal stability, and reproducibility of nanoprobe-based systems.21,76,77,108 Thus, one of the major unresolved challenges in PCa nanodiagnostics is how to improve analytical sensitivity while preserving specificity, stability, and clinical reproducibility.21,28,108

From a therapeutic standpoint, the central value of nanomaterials lies in their ability to enhance the in vivo stability and delivery efficiency of therapeutic agents.15,18,21 Platforms including liposomes, LNPs, SLNs, PEG-PLGA nanoparticles, AuNPs, and MNPs have all been explored for the delivery of chemotherapeutic agents, siRNA, miRNA, plasmid DNA, and immunomodulatory molecules. Through surface-targeting modifications and stimulus-responsive release, these systems can further improve tumor-site accumulation and intracellular delivery efficiency.18,34,50–52,123,125,126,132,144,175 In particular, in the context of PSMA-targeted strategies, nanomaterials have shown potential for enhancing therapeutic selectivity, reducing systemic toxicity, and improving treatment responses associated with drug resistance.24,50,51,125,126 Nevertheless, dense stromal barriers, aberrant vascular architecture, insufficient penetration into deep tissues, and lesion-to-lesion differences in target expression and microenvironmental status may all compromise the uniform intratumoral distribution and effective exposure of nanomedicines in PCa.22,52 Therefore, overcoming delivery barriers and improving adaptability to heterogeneous lesions remain central priorities in the continued optimization of nanotherapeutic platforms.15,21,52

Compared with conventional single-modality treatments, a major advantage of nanoplatforms lies in their capacity for multifunctional integration.15,20,21,57 Available evidence indicates that nanomaterials have been widely explored in photothermal therapy, photodynamic therapy, sonodynamic therapy, CDT, nucleic acid therapy, and nano-immunotherapy, underscoring their evolution from simple delivery carriers toward multi-mechanism therapeutic systems.17,57,121,132,144,175 By integrating chemotherapeutic agents, photosensitizers, photothermal conversion materials, nucleic acid cargos, or immunomodulatory components within a single platform, these systems can, to some extent, enhance local antitumor efficacy, mitigate therapeutic resistance, and improve the tumor microenvironment.50,51,57,121,132 However, as additional functional modules are incorporated, the structural stability, in vivo metabolic behavior, and mechanisms of action of nanoplatforms also become increasingly complex, and insufficient synergy—or even mutual interference—among different therapeutic components may arise.15,21,153 In addition, the treatment depth, energy distribution, and safety margins for normal tissues in modalities such as phototherapy, magnetic hyperthermia, and sonodynamic therapy still require further optimization in deep tissues, and these issues collectively limit the broader translation of multimodal therapeutic strategies.17,121,132,144,175

A key cross-cutting direction in PCa nanomedicine is theranostic integration, in which nanoplatforms are designed to connect molecular recognition, imaging, targeted delivery, treatment guidance, and response monitoring within a unified framework.20,21,24,152 Rather than treating PSMA targeting, multimodal imaging, stimulus-responsive release, and therapeutic response monitoring as separate functions, these elements can be integrated to enable lesion localization, treatment guidance, efficacy feedback, and dynamic monitoring within a single system.20,21,24,152 For PCa, which is characterized by marked heterogeneity, complex metastatic patterns, and a strong propensity for treatment resistance, such platforms may also enable more tailored and individualized interventions according to patient-specific molecular features, tumor burden, and microenvironmental status.15,22,57 Nevertheless, substantial differences exist across disease stages and metastatic sites in terms of PSMA expression, stromal composition, immune microenvironment, and treatment responsiveness. Therefore, the design of theranostic platforms should move beyond simple functional stacking and should be guided by patient stratification, target selection, disease stage, metastatic site, and efficacy assessment.20,22

Clinical Translation: Readiness, Bottlenecks, and Possible Solutions

Despite the rapid development of PCa nanomedicine, most nanoplatforms remain at the preclinical stage, and relatively few have undergone true clinical validation or achieved broad clinical application.15,20,21,153 Several nanomaterial-based platforms have nevertheless entered clinical evaluation in PCa, providing important examples for assessing both translational progress and remaining challenges. BIND-014, a PSMA-directed docetaxel-containing polymeric nanoparticle, was evaluated in a Phase 2 clinical trial in patients with metastatic CRPC and showed antitumor activity with acceptable tolerability.188 Gold–silica nanoshell-based photothermal therapy has also been evaluated in a clinical pilot device study for localized PCa, supporting the feasibility of nanoparticle-directed focal photothermal ablation.189 In addition, NBTXR3, a hafnium oxide nanoparticle radioenhancer, represents a clinically investigated radioenhancing nanoplatform designed to increase intratumoral energy deposition during radiotherapy and has been explored across multiple solid tumors, including prostate cancer-related clinical evaluation.190 These clinical-stage examples indicate that nanomaterial-based PCa therapy is moving beyond preclinical proof-of-concept; however, durable efficacy, long-term safety, standardized activation parameters, image-guided delivery accuracy, patient selection, and scalable manufacturing remain key challenges. Major barriers include insufficient evidence regarding long-term biosafety, the potential for organ accumulation and chronic toxicity associated with certain inorganic or poorly degradable nanomaterials, and the challenges faced by complex nanosystems during scale-up manufacturing, including process amplification, batch-to-batch inconsistency, and the lack of unified quality-control standards.76,77,106,153 In addition, theranostic platforms that combine both imaging and therapeutic functions often simultaneously involve the attributes of drugs, probes, and devices, rendering their regulatory pathways and evaluation criteria more complex.20,24,153 At the same time, although a high degree of functionalization and customization may improve platform performance, it may also increase manufacturing costs and compromise clinical accessibility.152,153 Therefore, in addition to further optimizing material design and in vivo behavior, future efforts should also advance standardized evaluation systems, scalable manufacturing processes, and high-quality clinical studies in parallel, so as to facilitate the continued translation of nanodiagnostic and nanotherapeutic technologies for PCa.15,20,21,152,153

In summary, nanomaterials provide an important technological foundation for the precision diagnosis and treatment of PCa, and have demonstrated substantial potential in improving lesion detection, enhancing therapeutic selectivity, advancing multimodal synergistic treatment, and enabling theranostic integration.15,20,21,24,57 However, the biological heterogeneity of PCa, in vivo delivery barriers, long-term biosafety concerns, and practical challenges related to manufacturing and regulation remain the key factors limiting their broader clinical application.22,52,76,77,153 Future efforts should continue to focus on optimizing material design, identifying reliable targets, refining patient stratification, establishing standardized evaluation frameworks, and conducting high-quality clinical studies, thereby driving PCa nanomedicine from preclinical exploration toward clinical implementation.15,20,21,57,152,153

To accelerate the clinical translation of nanomaterials for precision diagnosis and treatment in PCa, future research should move beyond a development paradigm centered primarily on functional stacking and instead shift toward translatable design grounded in disease biology.20,191,192 Given the marked heterogeneity in PSMA expression, tumor microenvironmental features, and treatment responsiveness across different disease stages, metastatic sites, and therapeutic contexts in PCa, the optimization of next-generation nanoplatforms should not be confined to increasing drug-loading capacity, amplifying imaging signals, or incorporating additional therapeutic modules; rather, greater emphasis should be placed on their adaptability to heterogeneous lesions.20,22 On this basis, there is an urgent need to develop nanoplatforms that are biodegradable, self-clearable, and predictable in their in vivo fate, while establishing more systematic quantitative evaluation frameworks for blood circulation, biodistribution, tissue accessibility, organ accumulation, and clearance pathways, so as to reduce the risks of long-term bioaccumulation and potential chronic toxicity.193,194 At the same time, the rational design of stimulus-responsive nanosystems should be further optimized in light of the biological characteristics of PCa and its metastatic lesions, so that these platforms can respond to local tumor acidosis, redox imbalance, aberrant enzymatic activity, hypoxia, and external physical stimuli such as light, ultrasound, and magnetic fields, thereby enabling spatiotemporally precise control over diagnostic signal activation and therapeutic cargo release. In PCa, this process must also explicitly take into account the dense stromal barrier, tumor vascular and basement membrane barriers, insufficient penetration into deep-seated lesions, and differences among metastatic niches.52,191,195 On the diagnostic side, future studies should move beyond signal enhancement alone and instead promote the deeper integration of nanoprobes with mpMRI, PSMA molecular imaging, liquid biopsy, and artificial intelligence-assisted analysis, so as to improve the detection of small lesions, multifocal disease, and metastatic foci, while increasing their clinical discriminatory value in early screening, risk stratification, and dynamic monitoring.20,196,197 On the therapeutic side, greater emphasis should be placed on developing combination intervention strategies with clearly defined synergistic mechanisms for metastatic castration-resistant PCa, bone metastatic disease, therapeutic resistance, and the immunosuppressive microenvironment, particularly in combination with AR pathway inhibitors, radioligand therapy, chemotherapy, or immunotherapy.20,191,192 In addition, more clinically relevant preclinical evaluation systems should be established, including patient-derived organoids, three-dimensional models capable of recapitulating the tumor immune microenvironment, and in vitro PCa models with greater translational relevance, in order to more accurately predict the distribution, release, and therapeutic response of nanomedicines in complex physiological settings.195,198,199 Finally, for PCa nanomedicine to truly advance into clinical practice, it will also be essential to simultaneously promote systematic development in patient stratification, scalable manufacturing, batch-to-batch consistency control, quality evaluation standards, and regulatory pathways, while moving structural definition, manufacturability, and clinical endpoints upstream into the early stages of development, thereby improving real-world translational efficiency and clinical accessibility.192,194 Overall, the central priority of future research is no longer to construct increasingly complex nanosystems, but rather to develop nanoplatforms that are safer, more precise, more robust, and genuinely translatable, such that they can deliver verifiable and reproducible clinical benefit in appropriately stratified populations of patients with PCa.20,192–194

Conclusion

Overall, nanomaterials are providing important technological support for the precision diagnosis and treatment of PCa, while driving the field beyond traditional single-modality interventions toward greater precision, integration, and theranostic convergence. Owing to their tunable physicochemical properties, favorable cargo-loading and protective capabilities, and potential for multifunctional integration, diverse nanoplatforms have shown broad application prospects in molecular imaging enhancement, biomarker detection, drug and nucleic acid delivery, and multimodal combination therapy. In particular, when coupled with PCa-related targets such as PSMA, nanoplatforms exhibit unique advantages in improving lesion detection, enhancing local tumor accumulation, and supporting individualized intervention. Nevertheless, the pronounced biological heterogeneity of PCa, together with its complex in vivo delivery barriers, remains a major obstacle to the further clinical translation of nanomedicine. Accordingly, the future priority of this field should not merely be the construction of increasingly complex material systems, but rather the development of translatable platforms that can achieve safety, stability, reproducibility, and clear clinical benefit on the basis of appropriate patient stratification.
