Explainable 18F-FDG PET/CT radiomics model for predicting EGFR mutation status in lung adenocarcinoma: a two-center study
Authors: Yan Zuo, Qiufang Liu, Nan Li, Panli Li, Yichong Fang, Linjie Bian, Jianping Zhang, Shaoli Song
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo establish an explainable 18F-FDG PET/CT-derived prediction model to identify EGFR mutation status and subtypes (EGFR wild, EGFR-E19, and EGFR-E21) in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD).MethodsBaseline 18F-FDG PET/CT images of 478 patients with LUAD from 2 hospitals were collected. Data from hospital A (n = 390) was randomly split into a training group (n = 312) and an internal test group (n = 78), with data from hospital B (n = 88) utilized for external test. Further, a total of 4,760 handcrafted radiomics features (HRFs) were extracted from PET/CT scans. Candidates for the prediction model were constructed by cross-combinations of 11 feature selection methods and 7 classifiers. The optimal model was determined by combining the results of cross-center data validation and model visualization (Yellowbrick). The predictive performance was assessed via receiver operating characteristic curve, confusion matrix and classification report. Four explainable artificial intelligence technologies were used for optimal model interpretation.ResultsSex and SUVmax were selected as clinical risk factors, which were then combined with 8 robust PET/CT HRFs to establish the models. The optimal performance was obtained by combining a light gradient boosting machine classifier with random forest feature selection method achieving an optimal performance with a macro-average AUC of 0.75 in the internal test group and 0.81 in the external test group.ConclusionThe explainable EGFR mutation status prediction model have certain clinical practicability and good generalization performance, which may help in the timely selection of treatment options and prognosis prediction in patients with LUAD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00432-024-05998-7.

Introduction

Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is one of the major subtypes of non-small cell lung cancer. Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) is a key factor in the control of epithelial cell growth and survival, and its high expression or aberrant activation is associated with tumor progression and treatment resistance in LUAD (Singhi et al. 2019; Cheng et al. 2011). EGFR mutation-positive (mutant-type) patients selected for targeted therapy had significantly better efficiency and survival than standard chemotherapy, however, EGFR mutation-negative (wild-type) patients hardly benefit from targeted therapy, which performed worse than same efficiency and survival as standard chemotherapy. Therefore, determining the mutation status of EGFR is an important basis for screening populations for the potential benefit of EGFR-targeted therapies, as well as for avoiding drug toxicity and economic loss in populations that are difficult to benefit from EGFR-targeted therapies. With the high rate of development of targeted therapies, there are even different treatment modalities and prognosis for exon 19 deletion (EGFR-E19) and exon 21 L858R missense (EGFR-E21), which were the two main positive mutant subtypes of EGFR (Lopes et al. 2015; Lee et al. 2015a; Sutiman et al. 2017; Lim et al. 2014). Hence, determining the status of EGFR mutations and subtypes is crucial when making treatment decisions for individuals with LUAD, as it plays a vital role in guiding clinicians and patients’ relatives in choosing appropriate therapies and assessing prognosis. Cytological specimens or tissue sequence testing is the gold standards of EGFR mutation status, but it is time-consuming and laborious, in addition, also suffered trauma from invasive or minimally invasive test. Furthermore, there are difficulties in sampling and fully reflecting temporal and spatial heterogeneity of tumors. A noninvasive method, analysis of circulating cell-free tumor DNA, has been reported to have a high rate of false positives (Hur et al. 2018). Therefore, it is crucial to have a noninvasive, precise and reliable evaluation of EGFR mutation status in order to make prompt decisions regarding treatment options.

Machine learning is increasingly being used in healthcare, leading to significant advancements in disease detection, diagnosis, and treatment, and playing a crucial role in enhancing healthcare services. Combining machine learning and medical big data to carry out image-gene mapping research, revealing the intrinsic correlation between macroscopic image features and microscopic tumor-driven genes and signaling pathways, which have provided intelligent decision-making guidance for clinical doctors and patients and shown great potential in the field of medical imaging (Sun et al. 2018; Mu et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2022). 18Fluorine-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography-computed tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT) provides both anatomical structure details and detects metabolic and molecular alterations in tumors, offering valuable insights into tumor progression, treatment response, and prognosis. It has emerged as a leading technique in molecular imaging.

Numerous studies have demonstrated a significant correlation between EGFR mutation status and 18F-FDG PET/CT handcrafted radiomics features (HRFs) in LUAD (Caicedo et al. 2014; Liu et al. 2016; Li et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2020; Hsu et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2020). Most of them developed machine learning-based radiomics models based on data from small sample, single center or scanner. Without an external validation set for model, it is difficult to demonstrate the effectiveness of the models’ generalization performance. In addition, the clinical decision-making process of clinicians is very different from the existing radiomics model that directly predicts results based on input data. The lack of model explanation and visualization have hindered the clinical translation process. Using large samples, multi-center and even multi-tracer data is conducive to improving generalization performance of the prediction model. Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) technologies aim to help model builders and users understand the “black-box” models (Barredo et al. 2020).

As our previous studies (Liu et al. 2020; Zuo et al. 2023, 2024) showed that two or multi-center 18F-FDG PET/CT HRFs have great potential in improving the generalization performance in EGFR mutation status prediction model in LUAD. Also, XAI technologies and model visualization technology, such as Yellowbrick, could avoid over fitting to some extent and further improve clinical trust. How to establish the intelligent diagnosis and treatment model that can be explained by biomedicine is still a problem and pain point that need to be solved at this stage. In addition, the clinical validation of the results and the feasibility of translation are still in urgent need of more in-depth research. However, to our knowledge, hardly no comparative research on the strategies of post-hoc explanation of XAI techniques in EGFR-related studies. Predictive models derived from 18F-FDG PET/CT show great promise in classifying EGFR mutation status in LUAD. However, hardly any EGFR-related triple-classification prediction research, and almost no triple-classification prediction studies involving cross-center data validation, and model visualization and interpretation.

Therefore, we aimed to construct a reliable and explainable predictive model to determine EGFR mutation status using baseline 18F-FDG PET/CT data from two centers in patients with LUAD. The optimal prediction model is determined by combining the overall performance of internal test cohort and external test cohort. Additionally, four post-hoc XAI technologies, feature importance, Shapley additive explanation (SHAP) value, local interpretable model-agonistic explanations (LIME) and explain like I’m 5 (ELI5), were utilized for explaining the optimal model and their strategies were compared.

Materials and methods

Study population

Patients with a pathological diagnosis of LUAD, EGFR mutation status detected, no cancer-related treatment, and well-defined primary tumor boundaries were retrospectively collected. Herein, 390 cases from Fudan university shanghai cancer center (hospital A) between January 2016 and December 2019, and 88 cases from Ren Ji hospital affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong university school of medicine (hospital B) between August 2016 and July 2017. Hospital A’s institutional review board granted approval for this retrospective study.

Image acquisition and primary tumor segmentation

The scanners in both hospitals in this study are all manufactured by Siemens Medical Systems in Erlangen, Germany. Table S1 described the scanner information in detail. Data acquisition was performed using two hospitals’ standard imaging protocols. Two radiologists segmented the primary tumor volumes of interest (VOIs) from PET and CT imaging using ITK-SNAP version 3.8.0-beta (https://www.itksnap.org/), which were then reviewed by two chief physicians. All nuclear medicine physicians and chief physicians were blinded to clinical and pathological results of patients. The detailed imaging protocols and segmentation information were shown in supplementary information.

Feature extraction, feature reduction and selection

Using Python 3.6.3 and the Pyradiomics package (Lei et al. 2021), PET and CT HRFs were obtained from the original images and 8 filter-based images, which included local binary patterns (LBP) 3D and Wavelet, Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG), Logarithm, square, exponential, square root and gradient. To simplify the features, LBP 2D filter-based features were omitted due to their lower accuracy compared to LBP 3D filter-based features (Kohei et al. 2012). Data from two hospitals were randomly selected in 10 cases and re-segmented within one week. HRFs were obtained from the VOI results of the initial segmentation and re-segmentation, and intra- and inter-class correlation coefficients (ICC) was then calculated to assess the robustness and reliability of the segmentation results, confirming the repeatability of HRFs.

In our study, z-score was used for data harmonization to reduce the feature variation due to scanner operation errors and associated protocols. HRFs selection and models’ construction were both performed on training cohort. Initially, features that have only one distinct value are eliminated, followed by the removal of highly correlated features with a correlation coefficient exceeding 0.75. Then, 11 methods which belong to 2 types of feature selection methods were used for selecting robust and predictive HRFs. Various techniques such as random forest (RF), logistic regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), K-nearest neighbor (KNN), naive Bayesian (NB), and RF based on feature importance (RFFI) are utilized. Five additional wrapper methods, including recursive feature elimination combined with LR, gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT), RF, light gradient boosting machine (LGBM) and extreme gradient boosting (XGB), are employed. Multivariate Cox regression was used for clinical risk factors selection.

Model construction and evaluation

Seven classifiers, that were LR, RF, and LGBM, XGB, SVM, GBDT, AdaBoost (ADB), were used to construct predictive model candidates. Hyper-parameters tuning was performed using grid search cross validation. Data collected from hospital A was randomly divided into a training group and an internal test group in an 8:2 ratio, and data collected from hospital B was used for external test. Workflow

The visualization of the model was performed using Yellowbrick (https://www.scikit-yb.org/en/latest/index.html). Various evaluation indexes including the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, area under ROC (AUC), accuracy, recall, F1-score, and precision were utilized to assess the model performance. ROC curve, classification report and confusion matrix of internal test group were calculated based on Yellowbrick. The best model was determined based on the AUC performance, classification report, and confusion matrix of both the internal and external test groups. Additional debugging may be necessary to confirm the reliability and stability of the model based on interpretation results.

Post-hoc explanation strategies

SHAP, LIME, ELI5 and feature importance were utilized in this research for the optimal radiomics model’ post-hoc interpretation. Describing the potential impact of features that are related to each other is important for model visualization and interpretation. A higher importance score indicates a greater impact of the feature on the prediction model. Feature importance technique estimates the contribution of all input features of a given classifier to the final prediction. Based on scikit-learn, a feature_importances attribute when fitted is provided in ensembles of trees such as RF, GBDT, and LGBM. The feature importance visualizer of Yellowbrick in this research uses this attribute to arrange and display the importance in a graph.

SHAP includes official applications such as force plot, summary plot and dependence plot which are obtained by processing SHAP values and SHAP interaction values. The summary plot is an explanation for the prediction of all the samples, which indicates which features are most important and the range of influence they have on the data set. The SHAP force plot offers insight into the predictions of an individual model and can aid in error analysis by providing explanations for particular instance predictions.

LIME aims to obtain a model-agnostic explanatory model with local guarantees based on an understandable representation. This algorithm can be interpreted for individual samples. ELI5 is a Python library that helps debug machine learning classifiers and explain their predictions. It makes machine learning models more explainable, allowing the use of a unified application programming interface to visualize and debug various machine learning models.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism version 6 and R studio (R version 4.1.2) were utilized for statistical analyze. The Shapiro-Wilks test was used to assess if clinical data followed a normal distribution. Clinical information was statistically analyzed using the descrTable function from the compareGroups R package.

The ICC values of the HRFs were greater than 0.75, which shown good consistency. In this research, 4,760 HRFs were obtained from the PET and CT scans. Table S2 detailed the configuration settings when extracting HRFs based on Pyradiomics. Table S3 detailed the list of extracted HRFs.

Two hundred and twenty-four features (88 CT HRFs and 136 PET HRFs) remained after the feature reduction pipeline. The results indicated that LoG preprocessed image of CT with sigma value 1.0 and wavelet preprocessed image of CT always had best predictive power, whereas, wavelet preprocessed image of PET and original imaging of PET had the best predictive power. There are three kinds of SHAP plots. The standard bar chart, which represents the global importance, is obtained by averaging the absolute values of the SHAP values for each feature (Fig. 4b). Figure 4c is to simply plot the SHAP values of each feature of each sample by scattering, and through the color, we can see the relationship between the size of the feature value and the predicted impact (red is high, blue is low). Relationship between the size of the feature values and the predicted impact through the color, and at the same time show the distribution of its feature values. The y-axis position is determined by the feature, while the x-axis position is determined by the per SHAP value. Figure 4d is an interpretation of a prediction for a single sample.

Figure 4e represented an explanation for a LUAD patient being predicted as EGFR-E19 mutation status with a high probability. LIME explained the predictions made by the LGBM model, which was trained on eight PET/CT HRFs and two clinical factors, at the local level. Top features such as HHH_glcm correlation from wavelet processed PET images, 3D_firstorder_median and 3D_firstorder_10percentile feature from LoG processed CT images with a sigma value of 1.0 can be noted. The likelihood of the LUAD patient being predicted as Non-EGFR-E19 has been raised by sex and SUVmax.

Figure 4f displayed the tabular interpretation for the prediction made by LGBM. All features are ranked from highest to lowest weight, with higher weights being associated with higher positions, and also corresponding to their higher contribution to the prediction. Similar with feature importance, SHAP and LIME, ELI5 also indicated that the features 3D_firstorder_median and 3D_firstorder_10percentile feature from LoG processed CT images with a sigma value of 1.0 received the highest weight.

It is worth mentioning that through these XAI technologies, we found that our optimal model, in fact, initially had three features that did not contribute to the model at all (Fig. S6), and after we removed these three features and retrained the model, we obtained the same model results with reduced model redundancy. It indicated that XAI technologies played an important role for model developers during model development and debugging, as well as for users to interpret and trust their predictions.

Discussion

Noninvasive stratification of LUAD patients into EGFR-Wild, EGFR-E19 and EGFR-E21 subgroups could assist clinicians in specifying treatment options for patients with LUAD. In conclusion, this study has two main points of interest. Firstly, we constructed the first-of-its-kind explainable machine learning-based radiomics model for the prediction of EGFR mutation status using PET/CT images in patients with LUAD, which demonstrated certain clinical usefulness and trust. Secondly, using cross-combinations of 11 feature selection methods and 7 classifiers, the optimal predictive model was successfully established, demonstrating the complementary aid and incremental value of the radiomics signature along with clinical risk factors in predicting EGFR mutation status. Further, the optimal model used cross-center data for model validation, showing excellent generalization ability. Our model may be helpful to clinicians and relatives in developing a more rational treatment strategy for patients with LUAD, even further and better prognosis prediction.

This study initially examined the correlation between clinical factors and EGFR mutation status. The correlations between some clinical variables, semantic features, or image features and EGFR mutation status have been extensively studied and reported (Liu et al. 2016; Rizzo et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2019, 2020; Digumarthy et al. 2019). We found that patients with EGFR-E21 and EGFR-E19 were more likely to have a small tumor, whereas patients with wild-type EGFR were more likely to have a large tumor size. Similarly, Rizzo et al.’s results also supported our work (Rizzo et al. 2016). In this study, sex and SUVmax were selected as clinical risk factors, which were then combined with HRFs to develop predictive models. It’s in accordance with Liping Yang’s results (Yang et al. 2022). Certain studies have indicated a correlation between being female (Liu et al. 2016; Rizzo et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2019, 2020; Digumarthy et al. 2019) and EGFR-mutant type in lung cancer. And also, their results supported our research. Some studies have shown that EGFR-mutant tumors have comparatively lower 18F-FDG uptake than wild-type tumors (Zhang et al. 2020; Cho et al. 2016; Lv et al. 2018), which were accordance with our work. However, Caicedo et al. and Lee et al. reported that SUVmax was not a significant predictor of EGFR mutations status (Caicedo et al. 2014; Lee et al. 2015b). In response to difference of these results, we considered that sample selection bias and the complex tumor microenvironment might be the main reasons.

In this study, five LoG filter-based features of CT imaging and one each of wavelet, LBP 3D, and square filter features for PET imaging were the main robust radiomics feature to develop prediction models. The LoG filter is a type of filter that highlights regions with changes in gray levels, with sigma determining the level of detail in the highlighted texture. Eight decompositions are produced per level by the wavelet filter, along with every possible combination of high-pass or low-pass filters applied in three dimensions. Yang B et al. used six filters, LoG, wavelet, square, square root, Logarithmic and exponential, to extract radiomics features and develop prediction models for EGFR mutation status and mutation sites (Yang et al. 2020). Their results showed that the features from LoG, wavelet and Logarithmic filters were selected as the optimal feature subset, which were in accordance with our results. Also, Isaac et al.’s research also supported this result (Shiri et al. 2020). In this study, two first order features and three gray level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) features of CT imaging, meanwhile, along with two GLCM and one gray level run length matrix (GLRLM) feature of PET imaging were selected as the optimal feature subset. Different studies may have similar types of features, but their respective subsets of features are not identical. There may be two main reasons for this difference. On the one hand, different image types and feature classes of PET and CT imaging were incorporated in different study during feature extraction. The different type of feature inputs during feature selection resulted in different results of the optimal feature subset obtained at the end. On the other hand, sample selection bias is also a potential factor. In our study, we extracted features from all image types except LBP 2D, and extracted the comprehensive range of features to ensure the generalizability of the results.

The majority of research on predicting EGFR mutations using PET/CT radiomics can be divided into two main categories: predicting EGFR mutation status (wild type vs. mutant type) (Zhang et al. 2020; Lv et al. 2018), and predicting EGFR mutation status followed by predicting the two EGFR mutation subtypes (E19 vs. E21) and creating two distinct prediction models (Yang et al. 2020; Li et al. 2022). However, few studies have focused on EGFR mutation status three-classification approach. Yang et al. evaluated the value of PET/CT-based radiomics analysis in predicting EGFR mutation status and the survival benefit of targeted therapy, however, it lacked cross-center data validation and model interpretation (Yang et al. 2022). Their optimal AUC performance of EGFR-wild, EGFR-E19, and EGFR-E21 could reach 0.926, 0.805 and 0.859 in validation cohort. In comparison, in our study, the highest AUC scores for EGFR-wild, EGFR-E19, and EGFR-E21 were only 0.74, 0.75 and 0.72 in the internal test group, but could achieve 0.84, 0.77 and 0.79 in the external test group. The result of our study may be attributed to bigger sample size and greater data heterogeneity and complexity. In addition, in this study, the macro-average AUC score reached 0.75 in the internal test group, and reached 0.81 in the external test group. The optimal model achieved good AUC performance in the external test cohort, which indicated strong general applicability performance and generalization performance. Meanwhile, the performance difference in internal and external test cohorts may be attributed to the sample selection bias and good data distribution of external test cohort.

Research has shown that combining different feature selection techniques with classifiers can enhance the performance of machine learning models in classification and diagnosis, offering potential benefits for handling diverse datasets (Jiang et al. 2022). It has proven effective in various areas such as predicting prognosis of lymphoma (Jiang et al. 2022; Cui et al. 2023), predicting metabolic response of metastatic breast cancer lesions (Gomez et al. 2022), distinguishing between recurrence and inflammation in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (Du et al. 2020), differentiating between primary and metastatic lung lesions (Zhou et al. 2021), and predicting EGFR mutation status and subtypes in LUAD (Yang et al. 2020; Zuo et al. 2024). However, few studies have used cross-center datasets for model general applicability performance validation. This research involved creating potential models for predicting EGFR mutation status triple-classification by combining 11 feature selection methods with 7 classifiers. Further, the optimal model was determined by combining the AUC performance of the internal test cohort and external test cohort. The findings indicated that the optimal model performance was achieved by combining the LGBM classifier with the RF feature selection method.

XAI technologies have been used successfully in medical field, including prediction of radiation pneumonitis (Raptis et al. 2024), prediction of osteoporosis risk (Khanna et al. 2023), diagnosis of coronavirus disease (COVID-19) (Wan et al. 2023; Chadaga et al. 2023), prediction of prognosis in acute kidney injury (Hu et al. 2022), etc. Their results all indicated that XAI technologies can enhance collaboration and trust between computer technologies and medical competence by improving the understanding and clarity of predictions. LIME and ELI5 employ a comparable strategy in constructing a straightforward regression-based model near the data point for interpretation, LIME can provide interpretations for individual predictions, while ELI5 can provide both global and local interpretation. SHAP operates by allocating Shapley values to all features using a game-theoretic method to provide a feature-attributed result of the particular output linked to the data point. In this study, the results of these post-hoc XAI technologies were validated by the feature importance graphs obtained from the boosting ensemble classification methods. It was observed that these tools align with research suggesting that the overall ordering of the 10 features is not exactly the same, but the most contributing features are broadly consistent across these four XAI technologies, showing the consistency of our optimal model results.

Only a few studies have examined the prediction of EGFR mutation status in LUAD patients through model interpretation (Zuo et al. 2024), specifically using SHAP and LIME for post-hoc interpretation without comparing different XAI techniques. In this study, the results of the four XAI techniques showed that our original optimal model had three features that did not contribute at all to the model performance. By eliminating these three features, we achieved same model performance in both internal and external test cohorts, ultimately reducing redundancy in the model. Our results showed that with an explainable model, model developer can test the causality of features during the modeling process, test their reliability, and ultimately help model developer to debug the model appropriately. Meanwhile, these XAI technologies allow users to visualize the impacts of features and provide global and localized explanations for every prediction, enhancing the understandability of the “black-box” model.

Our study also has several limitations. First, although we retrospectively collected data from two medical centers, where hospital A enrolled all eligible samples during January 2016 and December 2019, there may be a potential sample selection bias in this study. However, it was still essential to gather and utilize a larger data set from multiple centers/scanners with standard clinical information for the development of models and validation of generalization performance. Second, the tumor regions were manually segmented, which could be time-consuming and susceptible to the radiologist’s subjective awareness as well as proficiency in the operation of the segmentation software. Finally, only one machine learning hyperparametric automatic optimization method was used for model tuning, without comparing the effects of the several currently dominant tuning methods on model performance. Exploring better preprocessing methods to reduce data heterogeneity, as well as enhancing the radiomics features that can adequately reflect the VOIs, may potentially improve the model performance.

Conclusions

Overall, we initiatively developed a PET/CT-derived explainable radiomics model using machine learning to predict EGFR mutation status in patients with LUAD, aiding in selecting appropriate treatment options. The potential for establishing a three-classification prediction model for EGFR mutation status is significant when cross combining different feature selection methods and classifiers. Model visualization and interpretation promotes collaboration and trust between computer technology and medical competence, help model developer to debug the model appropriately and users to understand “black-box” models. Thereby, it is expected to facilitate the clinical translation process.
