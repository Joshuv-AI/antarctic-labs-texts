Detection of mutant cancer-derived circRNAs in extracellular vesicles by rolling circle amplification
Authors: Mikołaj Piotr Zaborowski, Charles Pin-Kuang Lai, Alessandro Sammarco, Bence György, Xuan Zhang, Kyungheon Lee, Young Jeong Na, Lorenzo Ceppi, Koen Breyne, Elizabeth Marie Champagne, Jack Cardini, Robert Alexander Wesselhoeft, Marcin Iwanicki, Ralph Weissleder, Michael J. Birrer, Hakho Lee, Bakhos A. Tannous, Xandra O. Breakefield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Circular RNAs (circRNAs) form during splicing as a closed ring and are more resistant to degradation than linear RNAs. Cells release circRNAs within membrane-bound extracellular vesicles (EVs), which transport DNA, RNA, and proteins. Whether circRNAs in EVs can be used as biomarkers remains uncertain. We aimed to identify mutant circRNAs in EVs to develop a new biomarker approach. We demonstrated that ovarian cancer cells release and enrich circRNAs in EVs. We detected mutant circular transcripts from TP53, KRAS, and PIK3CA genes in EVs from cancer cells. We noticed that circRNAs in EVs undergo intense rolling circle amplification (RCA), producing multiple copies of the mutant sequence. We revealed that increased reverse transcription time and extended PCR elongation time intensified RCA. Using our RCA protocol, we detected mutant circRNAs derived from the TP53 gene in EVs from the plasma of ovarian cancer-bearing animals. In this study, we present a new model of mutant circRNA amplification from EVs, which may provide a basis for a diagnostic test for ovarian cancer patients.

Introduction

Circular RNAs (circRNAs) are formed during the splicing of precursor messenger RNA (mRNA) by connecting the splicing donor to a splicing acceptor sequence in an upstream region to create a closed ring. This process contrasts with canonical splicing (donor to downstream acceptor), which produces a linear mRNA. CircRNAs can consist of exons and combinations of exons and introns and may not match exact exon-intron boundaries.1 CircRNAs are recognized as highly stable molecules that are more resistant to degradation than linear RNAs,2,3 with the circRNA transcriptome being distinctive for specific human tissues and stages of development.1,4 The sequence conservation of circRNAs across species suggests their functional relevance.3 Computational analysis has revealed that many circRNAs contain binding sites for distinct sets of microRNAs (miRNAs), which are conserved among species.2,3 Hence, they can function as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) by sequestering miRNAs, abrogating their interaction with target mRNAs.5 CircRNAs in cell nuclei can enhance transcription of their sequences by interacting with U1 small nuclear ribonucleic proteins.6 CircRNAs may also compete with linear splicing, as regions more prone to circularization are less frequently spliced canonically.7 Some circRNAs bind proteins, e.g., the splicing factor muscleblind 1 (MBNL1) translated from the linear form of the mRNA, limiting their function as another route of negative feedback.7 Taken together, this class of non-coding RNAs may regulate cell function through multiple mechanisms.

A growing body of evidence supports the functional impact of circRNAs in oncogenesis. Since miRNAs play a critical role in cancer pathogenesis, inhibiting their activity by binding to circRNAs could be essential in cancer progression or regression.8,9,10,11,12,13,14 Interestingly, highly proliferative colon cancer cell lines have a lower abundance of circRNAs than cell lines with low proliferative rates. Specific circRNAs, such as ciRS-7 in colon cancer, have been shown to be upregulated,15 while others (Hsa_circ_002059 in gastric cancer) have been shown to be downregulated.16 Certain circRNAs, such as circLARP4 and circRNA1656, have been shown to be downregulated in ovarian cancer tissues and associated with prognosis.17,18 Thus, these reports suggest that expression profiles of circRNAs in tumors differ from normal tissues and have prognostic value.

CircRNAs can be released from cells within EVs in biofluids.19,20,21 EVs are nanosized, membrane-bound vesicles that transport cargo between cells, including DNA, RNA, and proteins, as a form of intercellular communication.22,23,24 Different EV types, including microvesicles (MVs), exosomes, oncosomes, and apoptotic bodies, have been characterized based on their biogenesis and release pathways.25 Cancer cells release EVs that promote tumor growth, invasiveness, angiogenesis, and immune suppression.26,27,28,29,30,31,32 EVs from tumor cells carry a distinctive RNA profile, mutant forms of mRNA, and abnormal levels of miRNA that are detectable in peripheral blood.33,34 Detection of defined mutations or elevated levels of extracellular RNA constitutes a highly sensitive biomarker approach. For example, mutant copies of the epidermal growth factor receptor variant III (EGFRvIII) mRNA were detected in serum and cerebrospinal fluid from glioblastoma patients.35,36 The low yield of nucleic acids can limit quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) assays of RNA extracted from plasma EVs. Increased sensitivity can be achieved by using droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) in which the sample is split into picoliter-sized droplets that serve as separate PCR reaction compartments.37,38 Some circRNAs released in EVs may be unique to cancer tissue. For instance, in colorectal cancer, circ-KLDHC10 was enriched in EVs from patient serum.39

We aimed to identify aberrant circRNAs in EVs from cancer cells to develop a new model for biomarkers. We showed that circRNAs are released and enriched within EVs from ovarian cancer cells grown in culture or in the host mouse. We observed that mutant circRNAs can be amplified based on their specific structure. As a result of rolling circle amplification (RCA), reverse transcription can generate very long cDNAs with multiple repetitions of circRNA mutant sequences. We identified conditions favoring the induction of RCA. In studying circRNAs in ovarian cancer tumors, we detected specific circular transcripts that are associated with ovarian cancer mutations. We also found high variability in the boundaries of formed splice sites identified within both exon and intron sequences. By employing RCA, we detected mutant circRNAs in EVs isolated from the peripheral blood of ovarian cancer-bearing animals.

Results

Characterization of circRNAs in ovarian cancer cells

To analyze circRNAs in EVs, we chose a panel of transcripts in databases of circRNAs2,3,4,40 from gene loci that have been reported to be deregulated in ovarian cancer.41,42 We selected the following five circRNAs to investigate: PVT1 oncogene (non-protein coding); guanine nucleotide-binding protein beta polypeptide 1 (GNB1); cyclic AMP responsive element binding protein 3-like (CREB3L); karyopherin alpha 3 (KPNA3); and suppressor of Ty 5 homolog (SUPT5H). We designed two types of primers to specifically amplify these circRNAs: (1) divergent primers (Figure 1A; for PVT1, CREB3L, KPNA3, SUPT5H), and (2) splice site-covering primers (for GNB1, Figure 1B, where the covering forward primer is made of F1 and F2 fragments). We also used primers amplifying linear RNA within the exon forming the circle and a neighboring exon to study different isoforms from the same locus (Figure S1). To confirm the circular structure, RNA from a human ovarian cancer cell line, OVCAR5, was treated with RNase R, which degrades linear RNA to a greater extent than circRNAs.43 RT-qPCR showed that circRNAs isolated from the OVCAR5 cell line appeared to be more RNase R resistant than their linear counterparts from the same loci (Figure 1C). We assumed that splice junctions were formed at the ends of exons. Gel electrophoresis of the RT-qPCR reaction products revealed the presence of expected dominant bands for all circRNAs (Figure 1D). To further corroborate the circular structures, we performed Sanger sequencing of these PCR products, revealing the backsplice site sequence characteristic of circRNAs for all five circRNAs (Figures 1E and 1F). We observed an emergence of additional peaks in some Sanger sequencing chromatograms (especially for KPNA3 in Figure 1E). Rolling circle effect

We observed multiple bands in resolving PCR amplicons from circRNAs amplified with Taq polymerase from different exons by gel electrophoresis (Figure 2A). Thus, a given exon included in a circle was incorporated into more than one type of circRNA. Hence, exon-specific divergent primers can amplify PCR products representative of many different circular isoforms formed by the exon of interest and neighboring exon and/or intron sequences (Figure S2A). However, the multiple bands observed in Figure 2A were also generated by primers covering splice sites (for GNB1) designed to amplify only one circRNA with a defined splice site (Figure 1B). Analyzing the distance between the bands, we noticed that it corresponded approximately to the additive lengths of the circle for all exons evaluated (Figure 2A). For instance, the distance between the band of the expected product for PVT1 (154 bp) and the next band above (∼560 bp) is around 410 bp, which is the length of a circular exon 2 of PVT1. There is also a band corresponding to 974 bp, which is the sum of the expected product and two lengths of the circle (154 + 2 × 410 = 974). The same principle was followed by all other circRNAs (Figure 2A). Therefore, we hypothesized that this pattern originated from reverse transcription that proceeded around the circRNAs and generated cDNAs with multiple copies of the circle sequence, illustrated for KPNA3 in Figure 2B. Applying the divergent primers to such a cDNA in the PCR reaction would yield the expected size product (e.g., 107 bp for KPNA3). One of the reverse primers, e.g., R, however, could also continue transcription past the F primer and form an amplicon with the flanking copy of the same product, giving rise to sequences longer by about one or even more complete circle lengths (Figure 2B ∗∗ and ∗∗∗). To support this hypothesis, we were also able to sequence KPNA3 amplification products using a primer within the circle’s region that is not amplified by divergent primers (primer location illustrated in Figure S2B). To verify these results further, we excised two bands from the gel electrophoresis of PVT1 products (Figure 2C) and sequenced them using the Sanger method. Indeed, the smaller band (∼150 bp) represented the sequence of the expected product, including the backsplice site. The larger band above it (∼560 bp) contained the sequence of the entire circle formed by exon 2 of PVT1, including the circle region not targeted by the divergent primers. As shown in Figure 2A, we observed that rolling circle transcription took place for at least three rounds under these amplification conditions. We reasoned that we might be able to observe even longer amplicons by extending the elongation time during the PCR reaction. Taq polymerase is predicted to produce a 1 kb amplicon sequence per minute of elongation, according to the manufacturer. To test this hypothesis, we extended the elongation time from 1 to 4 min, and indeed, we then visualized two more rolling circle cycles for PVT1 products represented by bands of 1,383 nt and 1,793 nt (Figure 2E, lane 1). To test if even longer cDNA products could be generated during the reverse transcriptase step, we applied PrimeSTAR GXL polymerase (Takara), designed to amplify long products up to 30 kb. PCR reactions with 10 min elongation times yielded amplicons far more extensive than 10 kb, barely migrating into the gel (Figure 2E, lane 2). To confirm that the pattern of bands generated with either Taq or PrimeSTAR GXL polymerase were indeed due to the rolling circle effect, we digested the reaction product with the BsrI restriction enzyme. The reaction is specific to the sequence (ACTGG) unique within the PVT1 circle region (Figure 2F). The enzyme targets the circle fragment that is not expected to be covered by the divergent primers (red and blue areas in Figure 2F), which means that the rolling circle effect would be necessary to amplify it. If the PCR products are the result of the rolling circle effect, such digestion should yield a fragment representing the circle length (410 bp) and its shorter flanking sequences (242 bp and 321 bp) (Figure 2F). Indeed, all these restriction products were visualized following gel electrophoresis, with the most intense 410 bp band, providing evidence that the entire circle is repeated multiple times in the very long amplicon (Figure 2E, lane 3 and 4, compared to lanes 1 and 2 with no digestion). Of note, PrimeSTAR GXL polymerase (10 min elongation time) yielded a markedly stronger 410 nt band (Figure 2E, lane 4) than one amplified by Taq polymerase (4 min elongation time) (Figure 2E, lane 4 compared to lane 3). This observation corroborated that the longer the amplicons were, the more repetitions of the circle sequence they contained.

The rolling circle effect depends on reverse transcriptase and transcription time

We hypothesized that if reverse transcriptase generates cDNA with multiple repetitions of the circle sequence, this phenomenon should contribute to an increased number of PCR copies of this region. To investigate that, we tested three different reverse transcriptase protocols (SuperScript VILO, SuperScript III, and Thermo Scientific Maxima Reverse Transcriptase) and quantified products of linear and circular isoforms by RT-qPCR (Figure 3A). SuperScript VILO protocol yielded twice as many linear isoforms of PVT1, GNB1, and KPNA3 compared to the other reverse transcriptases. In contrast, circular isoforms were enriched much more by this reverse transcription procedure (around 13-, 7-, and 10-fold for PVT1, GNB1, and KPNA3, respectively) in comparison to SuperScript III and Thermo Scientific Maxima Reverse Transcriptase. Hence, we reasoned that SuperScript VILO reverse transcriptase promoted the rolling circle effect more than the other two procedures. To test the length of cDNA generated by different reverse transcriptases, we amplified the cDNA of circular PVT1 using PrimeSTAR GXL DNA Polymerase with 10 min elongation time (Figure 3B). We observed that SuperScript VILO gave rise to very long amplicons that do not migrate into the agarose gels (Figure 3B, lane 1). SuperScript III protocol formed products that yielded a faint ladder-like pattern (Figure 3B, lane 2, single arrow). Some of the Maxima-generated amplicons also migrated poorly. However, this fraction was less intense than in the case of SuperScript VILO (Figure 3B, lane 3 compared to lane 1). To confirm that these products comprised multiple repetitions of a circle sequence, we digested them with the BsrI restriction enzyme (Figure 3B, lane 4–6). This reaction yielded the band corresponding to the circle length (410 bp), as explained previously (Figure 2F). The intensity of this band, as a semiquantitative measure, reflects the number of repetitions of the circle sequence within long amplicons. Indeed, the stronger the digestion band in the gel electrophoresis (Figure 3B, lanes 4–6), the lower the CT value for circular PVT1 in RT-qPCR (Figure 3C). This gel electrophoresis confirmed the results of RT-qPCR (Figure 3A), suggesting VILO transcriptase superiority in circRNA amplification and indicated RCA as its potential origin. We also hypothesized that an extension of reverse transcription time would increase the number of cycles in which reverse transcriptase goes around circRNA. Indeed, we found that the longer the reverse transcription time, the more copies of circRNA sequences are generated using VILO, as demonstrated for circRNA PVT1, GNB1, and KPNA3 using RT-qPCR (Figure 3D). Conversely, the capacity to detect linear RNA counterparts did not increase to the same extent due to prolonged reverse transcription (Figure 3E). Mutant circRNAs in tumors and cancer-derived EVs

The rolling circle effect might provide increased sensitivity in analyzing precise alterations in the repeated sequence of circRNA. We investigated circRNAs derived from genes frequently affected by high-grade serous ovarian cancer mutations, such as TP53.42,44 By applying a panel of primers and targeted next-generation sequencing of the TP53 gene, we identified multiple circRNAs in ovarian cancer tissues. Utilizing various bioinformatic algorithms (KNIFE, CIRI2, CIRCexplorer2),1,45,46 we identified circRNAs at exon-exon boundaries (Figure 4A; Figure S3A–S3C). KNIFE algorithm revealed the presence of many de novo species that were formed by the splice sites within exons and introns (Figure 4A, brown arches indicate ends that join to form a backsplice site in circRNA; each arch represents a different isoform). We determined that next-generation sequencing (NGS) reads coming from reactions specific for circRNAs contained known TP53 mutations, such as pathogenic single-nucleotide variant H179R (18% of reads) or an insertion generating frameshift P151fs∗30 (Figure 4B), previously described in ovarian cancer and other tumors.47,48 Detection of mutant circRNAs released into biofluids could serve as a diagnostic assay of the presence of driver mutations in the tumor. We hypothesized that cancer cells release EVs that contain mutant circRNAs. We isolated EVs from the conditioned medium of ovarian cancer cells by ultracentrifugation (UC) combined with filtration through 0.8 μm filters (Figure 4C). We characterized the pellets for the presence of EV markers (Alix, CD63, Figures S4A–S4B), and visualized EVs by transmission electron microscopy with immunolabelling for CD63 (Figure S4B). We assessed their quantity as well as size distribution through nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA, Figure S4C). Next, we confirmed the presence of a panel of circRNAs (PVT1, CREB3L, KPNA3, SUPT5H, and GNB1) in OVCAR5 cell-derived EVs. While analyzing expression relative to housekeeping genes (GAPDH and HPRT1), we observed that those circRNAs were relatively enriched in EVs compared to cells (Figure 4D). Conversely, their linear counterparts were less abundant in EVs than in cells (Figure 4D). We observed the same in another ovarian cancer cell line, A2780 (Figure 4E). The relative expression of transcripts in EVs cannot be accurately measured in cell cultures supplemented with fetal bovine serum (FBS) as that also provides a source of extracellular RNA.49 We also observed, however, the enrichment of circRNAs in EVs relative to cells in experiments performed without the addition of FBS (Figure S4D). To compare circular and linear RNAs between cells and EVs, we used a common normalizer, such as a combination of GAPDH and HPRT1. However, linear transcript might not be perfect for accurate relative quantification of circRNAs in EVs. We also observed that some circRNAs have consistently low levels in EVs. Therefore, we aimed at a more accurate quantification method to estimate the actual quantity of circRNAs in EVs. For that purpose, we synthesized circRNAs identical to endogenous circRNAs in sequence. We chose the shortest (SUPT5H) and the longest (PVT1) ones, as circular transcripts may amplify differently depending on their length. By titrating the known molecular mass of synthetic circRNAs we prepared the standard curves to relate Ct values to absolute copy numbers (Figure S4E). We learned that in an EV pellet, there are between 1 and 30 circRNA molecules per approximately 5 × 108 EVs from OVCAR5 cells (Figure 4F) and between 1 and 1,000 circRNA molecules per around 5 × 109 EVs from A2780 cells (Figure 4G). However, circRNAs in the pellet collected by ultracentrifugation might also come from lipoproteins or free proteins. To show that circRNAs are specifically in EVs, we performed density gradient fractionation of the pellet and RT-qPCR of the fractions to detect circRNAs (Figure S4F). We demonstrated that all three tested circRNAs were most abundant in density fractions 4–6, which are characteristic of EVs (Figure S4G). RCA for the detection of mutant circRNAs in EVs

We hypothesized that increasing the number of rolling circle cycles would generate a long cDNA with repeated sequences of circRNA with mutation sites (Figure 5A). This phenomenon could be used to analyze mutant or aberrantly spliced circRNAs. Employment of such amplification can thus significantly increase the sensitivity of mutation detection, especially in biofluids that contain disease-associated EVs, e.g., tumor-derived EVs. We isolated RNA from EVs derived from the DF30 ovarian cancer cell line that contains an R175H mutation in exon 5 of the TP53 gene.50 We amplified TP53 circRNA with covering splice site primers to generate a single isoform (Figure 5B). The length of the bands observed on electrophoresis suggested that there were multiple products whose length corresponded to the sum of the expected product (144 nt) times the multiplication of circle length (184 nt), i.e., (1) 144 + 184 = 328; (2) 144 + 2 × 184 = 512; (3) 144 + 3 × 184 = 696, etc. Such an observation implies that the rolling circle effect took place multiple times (Figure 5C). Sanger sequencing showed precise amplification of one region (Figure 5D), though electrophoresis presented multiple bands. The sequence contained a backsplice site characteristic of circRNA, with the R175H codon mutation and the region between the primers that would not have been amplified without the rolling circle effect (Figure 5D). To verify this observation further, the same product was subjected to restriction enzyme digestion with: (1) BsrI: specific to the sequence of the circle amplified only in case of the rolling circle event, and (2) BtsaI: specific to the mutant sequence (Figure 5E). The digestion yielded dominant products consistent with the circle’s length (184 nt). This confirmed that the long products comprised the repetitive mutant sequence. Next, we verified the specificity of this method by testing control ovarian epithelium (OCE, wild-type TP53) and the ovarian cancer cell line TOV-112D, which carries a R175H mutation in TP53 (Figure 5F). BsrI enzyme, specific for circular structure, collapsed the long amplicon generated by the rolling circle effect to one dominant band, both in OCE and TOV-112D. In contrast, the repetitive circle sequence was digested by the BtsaI enzyme only in cells bearing the R175H mutation in TP53. This pattern was consistent across cells and EVs. The small difference in the size of the dominant bands most likely resulted from the variety of circRNA isoforms arising from the same region. Mutant circRNAs in EVs from biofluids

We used our knowledge of RCA to detect mutant circRNAs in EVs from plasma. Our procedure for mutation detection was based on circRNA identification involving the following stages: (1) depletion of linear RNA by RNase R; (2) induction of RCA by extension of reverse transcription time and choice of reverse transcriptase (Superscript VILO); (3) extended elongation time in PCR (4–10 min) with choice of polymerase that is dedicated to the production of long amplicons (e.g., PrimeSTAR GXL DNA Polymerase); and (4) pre-amplified DNA as a substrate for the mutation detection assay, such as real-time PCR or ddPCR with the use of probes specific for single nucleotide changes. The same region can be sequenced using NGS (Figure S5A). We used the components of this procedure to detect mutant circRNAs derived from the TP53 gene in EVs in plasma in an ovarian tumor-bearing animal model. We established a xenograft murine model of ovarian cancer by implanting human TOV-112D cells (with the R175H mutation) through an intraperitoneal injection. After 4–6 weeks of tumor growth, we collected peripheral blood, obtained plasma, and isolated RNA from EVs (Figure 6A). Following the extension of reverse transcription to 6 h and elongation time in nested PCR to 4 min, we separated the reaction products by gel electrophoresis (Figure 6B). The PCR products were large and did not migrate into the gel (Figure 6B, lanes 1–3); however, upon digestion with R175H mutation-specific enzyme BtsaI, they collapsed into major single bands. Different band sizes corresponded to distinct lengths of circRNA isoforms containing TP53 mutant sequences. To further confirm that the signal came from mutant circRNAs and to analyze their sequence in detail, we applied the other optimized protocol of amplification that included RNase R treatment, followed by reverse transcription (3 h) and preamplification PCR (elongation time 4 min). Next, we confirmed the presence of the mutant sequence by qPCR with a TaqMan probe specific to the mutant variant and nested primers (Figure 6C). We successfully detected mutant circRNAs derived from TP53 in EVs from the plasma of all three tumor-bearing animals. Though one band was dominant by gel electrophoresis, we observed multiple other bands upon digestion (Figure 6B, lanes 4–6). In this experiment, we used divergent primers that could amplify different circRNA isoforms from this locus. To explore that further, we sequenced PCR products amplified from EVs in mouse plasma. NGS results were analyzed using the KNIFE algorithm, and reads containing mutations were searched. We revealed multiple isoforms of annotated and de novo circRNAs from the TP53 locus (Figure 6D). We also confirmed the presence of the reads with mutant R175H sequence in the EV RNA isolated from plasma (Figure 6E). A precise identification of aberrant sequences in biofluids can also be achieved by ddPCR. Here, we used a mutation-specific probe and nested primers to detect the R175H mutation using ddPCR (Figure 6F). Interestingly, we observed a spectrum of signal intensity in the droplets. It most likely resulted from the number of repetitions of the mutant sequence. PCR products that resulted from a higher number of rolling circle cycles yielded more mutant sequences. Consequently, it corresponded to more Taqman probes that accumulated in a single droplet and yielded a more intense signal. To test this hypothesis, we analyzed samples with more abundant mutation input amplified by different reverse transcription times (Figure S6A). We observed that 6-h reverse transcription, which should contain longer PCR products and more mutations per product, gave a higher intensity signal than 3- and 1-h reactions. Interestingly, the mutation in the sample with the least abundant mutation count based on qPCR (animal 2) was detectable by ddPCR at the highest signal intensity corresponding to the highest number of mutation site repetitions per one long PCR product. Discussion

Wild-type and mutant circRNA are shed by cancer cells in EVs but occur at low frequencies. To improve circRNA detection, we explored RCA, which is well-suited given the circular structure. We identified factors that enhanced RCA, such as reverse transcriptase choice, reverse transcription duration, and extension of elongation times in PCR. We used that knowledge to amplify mutant circRNAs in various isoforms from EVs derived from cancer cells in vitro and the plasma of tumor-bearing animals. We show that the improved RCA can increase the abundance of detected circRNAs.

By applying RCA, we studied multiple circRNA isoforms from the TP53 locus that may contain variable backsplice sites or a different number of introns and exons. RCA promoted by RNase H-minus reverse transcriptase and choice of primers for PCR was used to explore the inclusion of exons and introns in circRNA structure.53 This approach revealed previously unreported variants of variable lengths with different combinations of introns and exons in HeLa cells. Interestingly, isoforms had different capacities to bind miRNAs and ribonucleoproteins and were hypothesized to regulate different cellular processes.53 In another study, RCA was found to have a pattern of translation in human cells that produced more protein.54 We demonstrated that RCA is affected by the choice of reverse transcriptase and the duration of cDNA generation. Other studies have also shown that RCA depends on the choice of reverse transcriptase, pointing to ProtoScript as the most efficient.55 One could speculate that this type of amplification is prone to technical variability. However, it was also confirmed that circRNA expression measured by RCA changes linearly with input circRNA concentration.55 The sequence repetition achieved thanks to RCA may require a distinct NGS and bioinformatic approach. We used 150 bp paired-end reads to study circRNA structure for extensive coverage of rare backsplice isoforms. It was proposed that coupling RCA with long-read sequencing (Oxford Nanopore Technology) can reveal the entire repetitive structure of cDNAs.56 With the equimolar RNA input into RCA, circRNA yields more PCR products than linear RNA.57 This supports RCA’s ability to identify low-quantity circular isoforms. Taken together, RCA improves circRNA detection and the thorough exploration of their sequences.

We explored how cancer mutations can be identified in circRNA using the RCA method (Figures 4B, 5, and 6). So far, specific databases have compared circRNA origin and cancer mutations identified in genomic DNA within the same regions.58 Somatic mutations in seed regions of circRNA were identified in silico as disruptive to circRNA-miRNA interactions.59 Using the RCA method, we detected mutations derived from multiple TP53 circular isoforms in EVs isolated from the peripheral blood of ovarian tumor-bearing animals. Previously, the analysis of circRNA splicing in tumor tissues has revealed cancer-specific isoforms.60 The identification of somatic mutations within circRNA isoforms using the RCA method may provide additional information beyond linear RNA, given that the sequence is repeated multiple times.

Our approach may have certain limitations. We have not compared RCA side-by-side with other biomarker strategies, including, for example, miRNA, circulating free DNA (cfDNA), or plasma protein assays. Hence, we do not claim the superiority of the circRNA-based strategy and see the need for validation in more human samples. Further tests will be necessary to determine whether our RCA-based circRNA protocol complements established early-detection candidates, such as cfDNA. Some studies have revealed that circRNAs are at a lower abundance in cancer than in non-transformed cells.61,62 Our absolute quantification of circRNA revealed that in the EV pellet containing around 108–109 EVs, there are between 1 and 1,000 circRNA molecules. For this reason, when it comes to quantity, other nucleic acids or protein assays may outperform or complement circRNAs as candidate biomarkers for some cancer mutations. On the other hand, it is still possible that aberrations in circRNA sequences will yield additional information through RCA. When analyzing mutant circRNA, one should also consider the possibility of RNA editing in cancer cells,63 which might limit the potential of the proposed assay to detect somatic mutations for some genes.

In summary, we found that circRNAs contained in cancer-derived EVs can be amplified by RCA and analyzed by sequencing. We demonstrated that this type of amplification, in combination with targeted NGS, ddPCR, or RT-qPCR, provides an in-depth insight into the sequences of extracellular circRNAs. We detected multiple isoforms and mutations in circTP53, both in vitro and in plasma, in a mouse model of ovarian cancer. Future identification of circRNA isoforms specific to cancer tissue, in combination with the proposed RCA protocol, may yield a model of a diagnostic test. The following steps would include the design of a panel to target multiple circRNAs in EVs from biofluids. Developing bioinformatic tools that take advantage of repetitive analysis of sequences could make such an assay more specific and rigorous. Though RCA may require further optimization for human biofluids, this type of amplification may bring additional information on tumor status to improve currently designed diagnostic assays.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human ovarian cancer cell line OVCAR5 (female) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. A2780 (female) was acquired from the European Collection of Cell Cultures. Primary high-grade ovarian cancer cells (DF30, female) were isolated as previously described.64 Human primary ovarian cancer cell line TOV-112D (female) was obtained from Dr. Joan Brugge’s laboratory at Harvard Medical School. Normal ovarian cell epithelium culture (OCE) was generated by Tan Ince.65 OVCAR5 and A2780 were grown in RPMI-1640 medium (Corning, cat. no. 10-040-CM). TOV-112D, OCE, and DF30 were cultured in a 1:1 mixture of MCDB 105 (Cell Applications, cat. no. 117–500) medium and Medium 199 (Gibco, cat. no. 11150059). All media were supplemented with FBS (Gemini Bio-products, cat. no. 900-208) and penicillin-streptomycin solution (Corning, cat. no. 30-002-Cl) at the final concentrations of 10% and 1%, respectively, unless stated otherwise. For EV isolation, the medium was replaced after 24 h of culture with fresh medium containing 5% EV-depleted FBS (see in the following text). Cells were kept in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37°C. All cells were tested for mycoplasma infection (MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit, Lonza, cat. no. LT07-218) and found negative. Cells were counted using Bright-Line Hemacytometer (Sigma, cat. no. Z359629).

Animal protocol and collection of animal plasma

Female athymic nude mice aged 5–7 weeks of weight 25–30 g were housed in the MGH Animal Facility and handled under the policies of the MGH Review Board. Animal protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) for the Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH) following the National Institutes of Health guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Xenograft models were established by intraperitoneal injection of 3 × 106 cells (TOV-112D) thoroughly washed free of the culture medium with cold PBS by three centrifugation cycles at 1,000 rpm for 5 min and resuspended in 1 mL PBS. Blood (around 700–900 μL) was collected by cardiac puncture at the time of animal sacrifice into a tube containing EDTA and inverted 10 times. Blood was centrifuged at 1,900 × g for 10 min at 4°C in a swinging bucket rotor. The plasma (around 480–580 μL) was topped with PBS to achieve a volume of 1 mL (to minimize the loss of volume at the subsequent filtration step). To remove additional cellular debris and reduce contamination with genomic DNA and cellular RNA, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.8 μm filter (Millipore, cat. no. SLAA033SS). The filtered plasma was stored at −80°C and analyzed collectively after completion of the study.

Clinical samples

Patients were enrolled according to an Institutional Review Board-approved protocol with informed consent. Tumor samples were collected from female patients with ovarian cancer in Massachusetts General Hospital Abdominal Imaging and Intervention suites. Tumor samples were immersed in RNAlater solution right after collection. After 24 h of storage at 4°C, the samples were transferred to a −80°C freezer until RNA isolation. Cancer diagnoses were confirmed by histological examination and clinical imaging.

EV isolation from conditioned media

For EV isolation, FBS (Gemini Bio-products, Cat. No. 900-208) was EV-depleted by UC at 40,400 rpm (corresponding to average RCF 120,101 × g) in 70 Ti rotor (fixed angle, average radius: 65.7mm, k-Factor at maximum speed: 44, Beckman Coulter, cat. no. 337922) in Optima L-90K ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter, cat. no. 365670) for 17 h at 4°C and filtered through 0.22 μm (Millipore cat. no. SLGP033RS). To collect EVs, 2 × 106 cells per dish were seeded in four to eight 150 mm culture dishes in 20 mL medium supplemented with FBS at 10% concentration. The medium was replaced after 24 h with fresh medium supplemented with 5% EV-depleted FBS and collected 48 h later when cells were at approximately 90% confluency. Cells were kept in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37°C. This conditioned medium was subject to serial centrifugation steps combined with filtration: (1) 300 × g for 10 min at 4°C (Thermo Scientific Sorvall Four-Place Swinging Bucket Rotor); (2) 2,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C (Thermo Scientific Sorvall Four-Place Swinging Bucket Rotor); (3) filtration through 0.8 μm filter (Millipore, cat. no. SLAA033SS); (4) filtrate centrifuged at 40,400 rpm (corresponding to average RCF 120,101 × g) in 70 Ti rotor for 2 h at 4°C with the brake set to maximum (“0”) in polypropylene tubes (Beckman Coulter, cat. no. 342414), (5) the pellet was resuspended in cold PBS previously filtered twice through 0.22 μm (Millipore cat. no. SLGP033RS) and centrifuged again at 40,400 rpm (corresponding to average RCF 120,101 × g) in 70 Ti rotor for 2 h at 4°C in polypropylene tubes (Beckman Coulter, cat. no. 342414), unless otherwise specified. This study followed MISEV criteria for nomenclature,66 collection from tissue culture-conditioned media, and EV isolation methods.

Density gradient fractionation

For top-loaded samples, the sucrose density gradient was formed by applying 875 μL sucrose solutions in the following order: 60% (bottommost), 45%, 30%, 8% (topmost) in Beckman polyallomer tube (Beckman Coulter, cat. no. 326819). 500 μL sample (conditioned medium, EVs resuspended in double-filtered through 0.22 μm filter PBS) was loaded on top of the gradient. The tubes were centrifuged in Optima MAX–XP centrifuge, in MLS-50 swinging bucket rotor (average radius: 71.7 mm, k-Factor at maximum speed: 71, Beckman Coulter, cat. no. 367280) for 38 min at 50,000 rpm (that corresponds to average RCF 200,620 × g) with brake set to 8 (very slow) at 4°C. The fractions were collected from the top: (1) 500 μL (referred to as “Top” fraction); (2) ten fractions (350 μL) numbered 1–10. The fraction density was determined after collection.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis

The resuspended 100K pellet was analyzed by means of Nanosight LM10 instrument (Malvern, Framingham, MA) equipped with AVT MARLIN F-033B IRF camera (Allied Vision Technologies) and NTA 3.1 Build 3.1.46 software. Samples were diluted in 1 mL freshly prepared (double filtered through 0.22 μm filter) PBS kept at room temperature and were measured in the dilution range 1:50-1:100. Minimum track length and blur were set automatically. All measurements were performed with the temperature set to 22°C. Each sample was imaged in at least 5 technical replicates. All movies were recorded for the 60s with a screen gain and camera level set to 9 and 8, respectively. Processing of images was performed with detection threshold 2 and screen gain 10. All movies had at least 2,000 valid tracks.

Transmission electron microscopy and immunolabeling

The 120K pellet was centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C and, after gentle removal of supernatant, fixed for 2 h in 500 μL of 4% paraformaldehyde (32% aqueous solution, EM grade, Electron Microscopy Sciences, 15714-S) diluted in PBS. RNA isolation from cells and EVs

Following the 48-h culture, cells were trypsinized and centrifuged for 10 min at 300 g at 4°C. As described previously, EVs obtained from 80 to 160 mL conditioned media were purified by differential centrifugation. Cell or EV pellets were lysed in Qiazole reagent (QIAGEN, cat. no. 79306) for 1 min, then vortexed and incubated for another 1 min at room temperature. 140 μL chloroform was added, the sample was shaken for 15 s, let sit for 40 s, and shaken once more for 15 s and let sit for another 40 s. The solution was spun for 15 min at 12,000 × g at 4°C. The upper phase was transferred without disturbing the interphase. The volume was measured, and 1.5 volume of 100% ethanol stored at room temperature was added. The sample was mixed gently 20 times. RNA was isolated using miRNeasy kit (QIAGEN, cat. no. 217084). The sample (up to 700 μL) was transferred to the RNeasy Mini Spin column. Buffer RWT (a component of miRNeasy kit) was prepared by adding RWT concentrate (15 mL) to 45 mL molecular biology grade isopropanol (99.5%, Fisher Scientific, cat. no. BP2632-4). The protocol included DNase I (dissolved in RDD buffer included in DNase I set) treatment at 20°C–30°C for 15 min. RNA was purified by ethanol precipitation (described in the following text). Total RNA was eluted in RNase-free water (Invitrogen, cat. no. AM9915G). The concentration and purity of RNA were assessed using nanodrop. In addition, the quantity and size range of the nucleic acids were evaluated using the 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) using the RNA 6000 pico chip (Agilent).

RNA isolation from tumors

Tumor samples were immersed in RNAlater RNA Stabilization Reagent (cat. no. AM7020) at the time of surgery, stored at 4°C overnight, and moved to −80°C until analysis. The tissue was immersed in a Qiazole reagent (cat. no. 79306) and homogenized according to TissueLyser II (cat. no. 85300) protocol. The plate was shaken at 20 Hz frequency for 2 min, rotated, 20 Hz frequency for 2 min, 25 Hz for 2 min, rotated, and 25 Hz frequency for 2 min. RNA was extracted following the miRNeasy kit (QIAGEN, cat. no. 217084) protocol, including DNase digestion. Total RNA was eluted in RNAse-free water (Invitrogen, cat. no. AM9915G). The concentration and purity of RNA were assessed using nanodrop.

RNA isolation from plasma

Plasma (around 480–580 μL) was diluted with PBS (to reach a volume of 1 mL), filtered at the time of collection through a 0.8 μm filter (Millipore, cat. no. SLAA033SS) and stored at −80°C until analysis. The diluted and filtered plasma volume ranged from 680 to 740 μL. The samples were thawed on ice and processed according to the ExoRNeasy Midi Kit (cat. no. 77044) protocol. RNA from the final step of the protocol was eluted in 14 μL RNAse-free water (Invitrogen, cat. no. AM9915G). 5.5 μL eluate was used for reverse transcription right after RNA isolation without freezing. The remaining material was stored at −80°C. RNA’s quantity and size range were assessed by the 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) using the RNA 6000 pico chip (Agilent).

Ethanol precipitation

0.5 μL PCR grade glycogen (10 μg) (Invitrogen, cat. no. R0551), 1/10 volume of 3M sodium acetate (pH 5.2), 2.5 volumes of ice-cold 99.5% EtOH were added to the RNA/DNA sample and stored overnight at −20°C. The sample was spun at 15,000 × g for 25 min at 4°C. After removing the supernatant, 500 μL 70% EtOH was added to the pellet and spun at 15,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. After removal of the supernatant, the pellet was dried in a heat block (70°C) for 10 min. Nuclease-free water was added to dissolve the purified pellet.

RNase R resistance

RNA was incubated with 1 μL RNase R (20U/1 μL, Epicentre cat. no. RNR07250), 0.25 μL ribonuclease inhibitor (New England Biolabs, cat. no. M0314S) in 1 μL 10X reaction buffer and nuclease-free water (total volume 10 μL) for 1 h in 37°C. Water was added instead of RNase R in mock-treated samples. Reverse transcription was performed right after the RNase R digestion.

Reverse transcription

The purified RNA was reverse transcribed using one of three enzymes: SuperScript VILO cDNA Synthesis Kit (Invitrogen, cat. no. 11754050), Maxima (cat. no. EP0742), or Superscript (cat. no. 18080-051). SuperScript VILO cDNA Synthesis Kit protocol was performed as follows: 10X (2 μL), 5X (4 μL) buffers, nuclease-free water, input RNA were mixed on an ice block in a total reaction volume 20 μL and incubated at 25°C for 10 min, at 42°C for 30 min or 1, 3, 6, or 12 h (specified for each experiment in figure legends), at 85°C for 5 min in the Bio-Rad Thermocycler. The Superscript III protocol included random hexamers (1 μL), dNTP (1 μL), RNA, and water that were mixed in 10 μL total reaction volume and incubated for 65°C in a thermocycler and cooled down on ice for 1 min. The reaction mix (2 μL 10X buffer, 4 μL 25 nMMgCl2, 2 μL 0.1 M DTT, 1 μL RNaseOUT, and 1 μL Superscript reverse transcriptase) was added and incubated at 25°C for 10 min, at 50°C for 50 min, and finally at 85°C for 5 min and cooled down on ice. The Maxima protocol followed the steps: random hexamers (1 μL), dNTP (1 μL), RNA, and water were mixed in 14.5 μL total reaction volume and incubated at 65°C in a thermocycler and cooled down on ice for 1 min. The reaction mix (4 μL 5X buffer, 0.5 μL RNase inhibitor, New England Biolabs, cat. no. M0314S, and 1 μL Maxima reverse transcriptase) was added and incubated at 25°C for 10 min, at 50°C for 30 min, at 85°C for 5 min, and cooled down on ice. Enzyme and reaction conditions different from the aforementioned are stated in the figure legends.

RT-qPCR

The RT-qPCR reaction mix was prepared following the manufacturing protocol of Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, cat. no. 4368708), including 12.5 μL Power Sybr Green, forward primer (160 nM), reverse primer (160 nM), nuclease-free water, and cDNA in 25 μL total reaction volume. The list of primers is provided in Table S1. The reaction was performed in Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System. The cycling conditions followed the steps: (1) 1 cycle of 50°C for 2 min; (2) 1 cycle of 95°C for 10 min; (3) 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min; and (4) dissociation curve analysis (95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 1 min, 95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 15 s). All qPCR reactions were done in two technical and multiple biological replicates (as stated in the figure legends). Ct values were calculated in “auto-mode” and manually inspected for accuracy. The presence of primer dimers was searched for by evaluating the dissociation curve. If applicable, the results were normalized to the mean of housekeeping genes GAPDH and HPRT.

PCR mutation detection assay

For RT-qPCR to detect mutation (Figure 6C), following RNase R digestion and reverse transcription (VILO, 180 min), the cDNA was preamplified with PrimeSTAR GXL DNA Polymerase (in total 50 μL reaction volume, 10 μL 5X primeSTAR GLX buffer, 4 μL dNTP 2.5 mM each, 1μL primeSTAR GLX DNA Polymerase, TP53-S primer; Table S2) according to the following conditions: (1) 30 cycles: 98°C for 10 s, (2) 60°C for 15 s, (3) 68°C for 4 min. Next, to detect the R175H mutation in TP53, a custom TaqMan SNP Genotyping assay (assay ID AH1SDII, assay name P53R175H, cat. no. 4331349) was used. The assay contained the forward and reverse TP53-B5 primers (Table S2), VIC probe (AH1SDII_V: TGAGGCGCTGCCC), and FAM probe (AH1SDII_M: TGAGGCACTGCCC). The primers amplified a region nested within the DNA product generated with PrimeSTAR GXL DNA Polymerase. In the 20 μL reaction volume, the primers and probes were used at concentrations of 700 and 250 nM, respectively, 10 μL TaqMan Genotyping Master Mix (2x), and preamplified cDNA were mixed in MicroAmpTM Optical 96-well plate. The plate was covered with MicroAmp Optical Adhesive Film and centrifuged at 100 × g for 5 min. The reaction was performed in Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System according to the following cycling conditions: (1) 95°C for 10 min, (2) 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, and 60°C for 1 min. The water was used as a non-template control.

ddPCR

The ddPCR mix was prepared by adding 11 μL ddPCR SuperMix for Probes (no dUTP) (Bio-Rad, USA), with 700 nM of each TP53-B5 primer, 250 nM of each probe (VIC probe: AH1SDII_V, TGAGGCGCTGCCC, and FAM probe: AH1SDII_M, TGAGGCACTGCCC), and 6 μL of DNA, for a total volume of 22 μL. Then, 20 μL of the ddPCR mix was mixed with 70 μL of the Droplet Generator Oil for Probes (Bio-Rad, USA) using the QX200 Droplet Generator (Bio-Rad, USA). Afterward, 40 μL droplet-containing samples were transferred into a 96-well plate, sealed with aluminum foil, and incubated in a Veriti 96-well Fast Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems, USA). PCR amplification consisted of the following steps: initial denaturation (95°C, 2 min), 40 cycles of denaturation (94°C, 30 s) and annealing + extension (60°C, 1 min), final denaturation (98°C, 10 min), and cool down (4°C on hold). After amplification, the plate was transferred into the QX200 Droplet Reader (Bio-Rad, USA). Positive and negative droplets (without fluorescence signal) were measured using the QuantaSoft Software (Version 1.7) (Bio-Rad, USA).

RT-PCR

The standard PCR reaction with Taq DNA polymerase (Figure 2A) was prepared by mixing dNTP (200 μM), forward and reverse primers (400 nM), cDNA, 1.25 μL DMSO, 2.5 μL 10X Standard Taq Reaction Buffer, 0.125 μL Taq DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs, cat. no. M0273) and nuclease-free water in the total volume of 25 μL. The same primers (Table S1) were used for quantitative and regular PCR. The cycling conditions followed the steps: (1) 1 cycle of 95°C for 30 s; (2) 40 cycles of 95°C for 20 s, 59°C for 30 s, 70°C for 1 min; and (3) 1 cycle of 70°C for 5 min.

For the long-amplicon PCR reaction, we applied PrimeSTAR GXL polymerase (Figures 2E and 3B). It was prepared by mixing dNTP (200 μM), forward and reverse primers (200 nM), cDNA, 10 μL 5X PrimeSTAR GLX buffer, 1 μL PrimeSTAR GXL DNA Polymerase (Takara, cat. no. R050A) and nuclease-free water in the total volume of 50 μL. The reaction was performed under the following conditions: 30 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 68°C for 4–10 min (elongation time for specific experiments is stated in the figure legends).

To amplify the low-abundance circRNA from EVs isolated from murine plasma (Figure 6B), RNA was reverse transcribed with SuperScript VILO with a 6-h extension time. Next, we mixed 2X SeqAmp PCR buffer, forward and reverse TP53-B1 primers listed in Table S3 (200 nM each), 5 μL cDNA, 1 μL SeqAmp DNA Polymerase (Takara, cat. no. 638509), and nuclease-free water in the total volume of 50 μL. The reaction was performed according to the following conditions: (1) 1 cycle of 95°C for 1 min; (2) 25 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 60°C for 15 s, 68°C for 4 min; and (3) 1 cycle of 72°C for 10 min. The products of the reaction were used as an input to nested PCR with SeqAmp DNA polymerase with TP53-S primers according to conditions: (1) 1 cycle of 95°C for 10 min; (2) 40 cycles of 98°C for 20 s, 60°C for 15 s, 68°C for 4 min; and (3) 1 cycle of 72°C for 10 min. The products were digested with BtsaI enzyme.

All reactions were performed in the Bio-Rad Thermocycler. All PCR products were visualized on a 0.3%–2% (stated in the figure legends for each experiment) agarose gel using GelRed (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

DNA isolation

Following the PCR reaction, DNA was purified with a Quiquick PCR purification kit (QIAGEN, cat. no. 28104) for further sequencing procedures. Quiquick gel extraction kit (QIAGEN, cat. no. 28704) was used for DNA isolation from the agarose gel. The purity and concentration of DNA were assessed by Nanodrop. If the purity was insufficient, the samples were precipitated with ethanol, as described previously.

Restriction enzyme digestion

PCR reaction products were digested with BsrI (New England Biolabs, cat. no. R0527S) restriction enzyme (1 μL) with 1X NE Buffer 3.1 buffer in a 25 μL reaction volume. The samples were incubated at 65°C for 1 h, followed by enzyme inactivation at 80°C for 20 min. DNA was also digested with BtsαI (New England Biolabs, cat. no. R0667S) restriction enzyme (1 μL) with the addition of 1X CutSmart buffer in total 25 μL reaction volume. The samples were incubated at 55°C for 1 h.

Synthetic circRNA generation

Circular RNA were generated by in vitro transcription (2 h, 37°C, New England Biolabs), followed by DNase treatment (20 min, 37°C, New England Biolabs), silica column purification (New England Biolabs), and E-gel EX 2% electrophoresis for quality assessment (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For circular RNA, IVT and DNase treatment were performed as previously, followed by silica purification. Purified linear precursor RNA was then circularized by splint-mediated ligation. Briefly, RNA was heated to 70°C in the presence of 5 μM DNA splint oligo (IDT) and then cooled to room temperature to anneal. T4 RNA Ligase I buffer and ATP (1 mM final) were added, followed by T4 RNA Ligase I (50 U per 100 μL reaction volume; New England Biolabs). Ligation was carried out at 37°C for 30 min without PEG. Splint oligos were designed with a 4/1nt overhang configuration and no linker. Following ligation, reactions were treated with DNase and RNase R to remove the DNA splint and residual linear RNA, respectively. Circularized RNA was then silica column purified and analyzed on E-gel EX 2%. Splint sequences used were as follows: circPVT1, CCTTCTGGCCAAAAGATCAGGAGCCCAGCTGAGCGCCGGATG; circSUPT5H, CACTCCGCCGCTCTTCGTCTACTCGTCCAGAATGAAGCCTCC.

DNA sanger sequencing

Following the RT-PCR reaction, DNA products were purified using a column-based precipitation method with a Quiquick PCR purification kit (QIAGEN, cat. no. 28104). The purified PCR products (200–600 ng DNA) were submitted for Sanger sequencing or NGS sequencing in the Massachusetts General Hospital DNA Core, Boston. For Sanger sequencing, the forward and reverse primers were used in separate reactions unless otherwise stated. The following primer was used for sequencing a circular isoform of KPNA3: ATTCATCAGCTTGCTAAGGG.

NGS

The preamplification PCR reaction to sequence tumor tissue circRNAs by targeted NGS (Figure 4) was performed with SeqAmp DNA Polymerase (Takara, cat. no. 638509). To prepare cDNA, tumor tissue RNA (500 ng) was RNase R-treated and reverse transcribed with SuperScript VILO with 60 min extension time. We mixed 2X SeqAmp PCR buffer, forward and reverse primers TP53-S, TP53-AD, TP53-AC listed in Table S3 (200 nM each), 5 μL cDNA, 1 μL SeqAmp DNA Polymerase (Takara, cat. no. 638509) and nuclease-free water in the total volume of 50 μL. The reaction was performed according to the following conditions: (1) 1 cycle of 95°C for 1 min; (2) 40 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 60°C for 15 s, 68°C for 4 min; and (3) 1 cycle of 72°C for 10 min in the Bio-Rad Thermocycler. Each primer pair was in a separate reaction tube. The reaction products from all three primer pairs were pooled and submitted for NGS. CircRNAs from plasma EVs were prepared as described in the “PCR mutation detection assay” section. qPCR products were submitted for NGS.

Following ligation of barcoding adapters and PCR amplification, DNA fragments were sequenced in MiSeq sequencer (Illumina), generating 150 nucleotide-long paired-end reads. DNA amplicon processing and sequencing were performed by the CCIB DNA Core Facility at Massachusetts General Hospital (Cambridge, MA).

Bioinformatic analysis

The quality of the reads was inspected with the Fastqc application (Version 0.11.3). The reads were trimmed with trimmomatic/0.36 (parameters: minlen = 35, trailing = 25, window = 12, phred = 64). The trimmed paired reads were aligned and analyzed in search of circRNAs. We used KNIFE-1.4 (circularRNApipeline_Standalone) with the following versions of necessary software: python/2.7.12, R/3.6.1, bowtie2/2.2.9, and bowtie/1.2.2. We applied the following run parameters: read_id_style: complete, junction_overlap: 8, mode: phred64; ntrim: 100, denovoCircMode:1. The reads were aligned to the human genome. Next, with a custom Python script (python/2.7.12), we selected circRNAs labeled by KNIFE algorithm as “circStrong”, counted how many of them contained mutant sequence within the TP53 gene, and generated a BED file to visualize the location of identified circRNAs. In addition, all reads corresponding to “circStrong” were aligned with the STAR aligner to inspect their location and the presence of the mutation. The output BED and BAM files were visualized in IVG 2.3.68. The samples were also aligned with tophat (using bowtie/1.2.2) to hg19 and processed by CIRCexplorer2. In addition, following alignment with bwa mem (bwa/0.7.17), circRNAs were searched for with CIRI2 (using perl/5.30.0).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed in the R programming language (version 3.4.1) using RStudio (version 1.1.463). The normality of distribution was verified with the Shapiro test. Groups with normal distribution were compared with the student t test (t test). The Mann-Whitney test (wilcox.test) compared groups with distribution deviating from normal. Values of the median or mean and interquartile range are given for each group. R packages (ggplot2, ggpubr) were used to generate plot (ggboxplot and ggbarplot functions) and add p values (stat_compare_means function). The central line in the plots corresponds to the median. The upper and lower “hinges” correspond to the first and third quartiles. The relative expression (Δ Ct) was calculated as a difference between the Ct value of the gene of interest and the mean of two Ct values representing transcripts for housekeeping genes (GAPDH, HPRT). The ΔΔCt was calculated as a difference between two Δ Ct values. The relative expression between the two conditions was calculated as 100 × 2ΔΔCt [%]. The results were considered significant for p values < 0.05. p values were either specified in the figure or denoted as asterisks: ns – p > 0.05, ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
