Thermodynamic control of non-equilibrium systems Preprint: Preprint ID
Dana Kamp, Karel Proesmans

Abstract
We study the thermodynamic cost associated with driving systems between different non-equilibrium steady states. In particular, we combine a linear-response framework for non-equilibrium Markov systems with Lagrangian techniques to minimize the dissipation associated with driving processes. We then apply our framework to a simple toy model. Our results show several remarkable properties for the optimal protocol, such as diverging parameters and finite entropy production in the slow-driving limit.



I
Introduction

Thermodynamic optimization of finite-time protocols plays an important role in a broad range of fields. One can for example think about the erasure of a bit
Aurell et al. 2012
;
Zulkowski and DeWeese 2014
;
Proesmans et al. 2020
;
Zhen et al. 2021
;
Basile and Proesmans 2024
, shortcuts to adiabaticity
Tu 2014
;
Takahashi 2017
;
Guéry-Odelin et al. 2019
and the driving of molecular motors
Gupta et al. 2022
;
Brown and Sivak 2019
;
Lucero et al. 2019a
.

Based on the framework of stochastic thermodynamics, a number of methods have been developed to study this problem. One prominent method is to use mathematical optimal transport theory to find protocols that minimise entropy production
Aurell et al. 2011
;
Van Vu and Saito 2023
. The main drawbacks of this method are that one generally needs to assume full control of the energy landscape and that it becomes computationally unfeasible for high-dimensional systems. These problems are circumvented by the method developed in
Crooks 2007
;
Sivak and Crooks 2012
. Here, one uses linear response theory around equilibrium to develop a geometric picture to calculate the entropy production associated with systems with parametric control. This can then be used to minimize the entropy production in a computationally efficient way. This method can be used for slowly-driven systems that are always kept close to equilibrium.

Many realistic systems do, however, operate far from thermodynamic equilibrium. For example, cellular biological processes generally operate in a non-equilibrium environment
Ritort 2008
;
Fang et al. 2019
, and microelectronic memories constantly dissipate energy
Freitas et al. 2021
;
Freitas et al. 2022
. This makes it impossible to apply the afore-mentioned geometric framework directly to these systems. This has lead to a number of extensions of the original framework. Firstly, it can be shown that applying the framework directly to non-equilibrium systems leads to an optimization of the non-adiabatic part of the entropy production, rather than the full entropy production
Mandal and Jarzynski 2016
. Furthermore, extensions of the original framework have been made for the specific class of active matter systems
Davis et al. 2024
. However, to the best of our knowledge, no linear-repsonse framework exists to minimize the entropy production of general far-from-equilibrium systems. In this paper, we close this gap by calculating a general formula for the entropy production of slowly driven systems far from thermodynamic equilibrium. This leads to a similar optimization scheme to the one developed in
Crooks 2007
;
Sivak and Crooks 2012
for equilibrium systems.

In the next section we will discuss the theoretical background and develop the general linear response framework. Subsequently, we will look at the optimization of the entropy production in section
III
. In section
IV
, we will apply it to a simple toy model where we will also discuss the main differences and similarities with the near-equilibriium framework. Finally, in section
V
, we will discuss the main results and give a perspective for future research.

II
Stochastic thermodynamics and entropy production

We are working with a Markov system that can exist in several discrete states
i\in\{1,..,N\}
with probabilities
p_{i}(t)
. How the probability of being in a specific state,
i
, changes over time, depends on the rates at which the system is moving into,
W_{ij}
and out of that state,
W_{ji}
, and the distribution of states,
p
:

\displaystyle\frac{dp_{i}}{dt}=\sum_{j\neq i}W_{ij}p_{j}-\sum_{j\neq i}W_{ji}p_{i},

(1)

for each state
i
, or in vectorial form:

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbf{p}(t)}{dt}=W\mathbf{p}(t)

(2)

with
W_{ii}=-\sum_{j}W_{ji}
.
This is called the master equation and if all rates
W_{ij}
are kept constant, the system evolves to a steady-state
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{SS}}
,

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{SS}}}{dt}=W\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{SS}}=0.

(3)

A special case of a steady state is equilibrium , where the probability of moving from state
i
to
j
is equal to moving in the opposite direction,

\displaystyle W_{ij}p_{j}=W_{ji}p_{i},

(4)

a property known as detailed balance. In this case, there are no net flows or cycles between states, and no work can be done.

Often a system is in contact with multiple reservoirs (such as particle and heat reservoir), each of which can separately induce transitions inside the system. The transition rates associated with reservoir
\nu
are then written as
W^{(\nu)}_{ij}
and as these reservoirs are assumed to interact independently, and the master equation becomes

\displaystyle W_{ij}=\sum_{\nu}W^{(\nu)}_{ij}.

(5)

In such a case the steady state generally does not satisfy the detailed balance condition, Eq. (
4
).

The central goal of this paper is to calculate the entropy production of time-dependent systems, that is systems where the transition rates
W_{ij}
are time-dependent. For systems described by master equations, this entropy production rate is given by
Van den Broeck and Esposito 2015
:

\displaystyle\sigma(t)=k_{B}\sum_{i,j,(\nu)}W_{ij}^{(\nu)}(t)p_{j}\ln\frac{W_{ij}^{(\nu)}(t)p_{j}(t)}{W_{ji}^{(\nu)(t)}p_{i}(t)}.

(6)

We will assume that the time-dependent dynamics are driven by a parameter,
\alpha(t)
. This parameter can for example control an energy-level of a state
i
, the temperature of the environment, or the chemical potential of some species.

II.1
Near-equilibrium systems

We want to calculate the entropy production,
\Delta S_{\textrm{tot}}=\int^{t_{f}}_{0}dt\,\sigma(t)
to go from an initial parameter value
\alpha=\alpha_{i}
to a final value
\alpha=\alpha_{f}
over a duration
t_{f}
. It is generally impossible to calculate the entropy production of such time-dependent processes analytically, as
\mathbf{p}(t)
depends on
\alpha(t)
in a highly non-trivial way, but several methods have been developed to approximate the total entropy production.

One of the most influential methods to do this was developed by Sivak and Crooks
Sivak and Crooks 2012
. Essentially, the main idea is to assumptions are that there exists an equilbirium probability distribution
p^{\textrm{eq}}
at all times that satisfies the detailed balance condition, Eq. (
4
), and that the system is driven sufficiently slowly. Under these circumstances, one can use linear response theory to get an approximation for the entropy production:

\displaystyle\Delta S_{\textrm{tot}}=\int^{t_{f}}_{0}dt\,A(\alpha(t))\dot{\alpha}(t)^{2},

(7)

where

\displaystyle A(\alpha)=\int^{\infty}_{0}dt\,\left\langle\frac{\partial X(t)}{\partial\alpha}\frac{\partial X(0)}{\partial\alpha}\right\rangle_{\alpha},

(8)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\alpha}
is the equilibrium steady-state average taken over the dynamics with
\alpha
fixed at its current value, and

\displaystyle X_{i}=\ln p^{\textrm{eq}}_{i}.

(9)

This allows to calculate the entropy production of a broad range of slowly driven systems
Blaber and Sivak 2023
, by first numerically calculating
A(\alpha)
for all values of
\alpha
. One can then use this to derive the optimal protocol,
\{\alpha(t)\}_{0}^{t_{f}}
that minimises
\Delta S_{\textrm{tot}}
. Furthermore, one can use it to show a number of general properties, such as that
\Delta S_{\textrm{tot}}\sim t_{f}^{-1}
and that the protocol that minimises
\Delta S_{\textrm{tot}}
is independent of the duration of the protocol
Deffner and Bonança 2020
;
Blaber and Sivak 2023
.

II.2
General non-equilibrium systems

Several extensions to the near-equilibrium framework from the previous sub-section have been derived. These extensions include active systems
Davis et al. 2024
, and systems that only take into account part of the entropy production
Mandal and Jarzynski 2016
. Despite these advancements, there is, to the best of our knowledge, no general framework to calculate the entropy production of slowly-driven non-equilibrium systems is available. We will develop such a framework in this section.

We will start by expanding the probability distribution
\textbf{p}(t)
as a function of
\dot{\alpha}(t)
. Following
Mandal and Jarzynski 2016
, we first note that (see also appendix
A
):

\displaystyle\textbf{p}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(W^{\dagger}\frac{d}{dt}\right)^{n}\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{SS}}(t)

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(W^{\dagger}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\dot{\alpha}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\right]\right)^{n}\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{SS}}(t)

where
W^{\dagger}
is the pseudo-inverse of the transition matrix
W
, defined by:

\displaystyle W=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\cdot|\lambda_{i}\rangle\langle\lambda_{i}|\rightarrow W^{\dagger}=\sum_{i=2}^{N}\lambda_{i}^{-1}\cdot|\lambda_{i}\rangle\langle\lambda_{i}|

(11)

where
\{\langle\lambda_{i}|\}_{i\leq N}
and
\{|\lambda_{i}\rangle\}_{i\leq N}
are the left and right eigenvectors of
W
associated with eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
and
\lambda_{1}=0
.
If the driving is sufficiently slow,
\textbf{p}(t)
is well-approximated by its thirth order expansion:

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}(t)\approx p^{(0)}+\dot{\alpha}\textbf{p}^{(1)}+(\dot{\alpha})^{2}\textbf{p}^{(2)}+\ddot{\alpha}\textbf{p}^{(3)}

(12)

with

\displaystyle\textbf{p}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{SS}}(t)

(13)

\displaystyle\textbf{p}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W^{\dagger}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{SS}}(t)

(14)

\displaystyle\textbf{p}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W^{\dagger}\left(\frac{\partial W^{\dagger}}{\partial\alpha}\frac{\partial\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{SS}}(t)}{\partial\alpha}\ +W^{\dagger}\frac{\partial^{2}\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{SS}}(t)}{\partial\alpha^{2}}\right)

(15)

\displaystyle\ \textbf{p}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(W^{\dagger}\right)^{2}\frac{\partial\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{SS}}(t)}{\partial\alpha}

(16)

The advantage of doing this expansion is that the probability distribution now only depends on the instantaneous value of
\alpha(t)
and its derivatives, and the steady-state distribution, and no longer on the full trajectory history.

One can use this expansion to get an approximation for the total entropy production rate
\sigma
. In appendix
C
, we show that

\displaystyle\sigma(t)=\,

\displaystyle\sigma_{SS}

\displaystyle\,+\,\dot{\alpha}(t)\,F\left(\alpha(t)\right)

\displaystyle\,+\,\dot{\alpha}(t)\left(A^{(1)}(\alpha(t))+A^{(2)}(\alpha(t))\right)\dot{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle\,+\,\ddot{\alpha}(t)A^{(3)}(\alpha(t))

with

\displaystyle\sigma_{SS}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{B}\sum_{i,j,(\nu)}\ln\frac{W_{ij}^{(\nu)}p_{\mathrm{SS},j}}{W_{ji}^{(\nu)}p_{\mathrm{SS},i}}W_{ij}^{(\nu)}p_{\mathrm{SS},j}

(17)

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{B}\sum_{i,j,k,(\nu)}\ln\frac{W_{ij}^{(\nu)}p_{\mathrm{SS},j}}{W_{ji}^{(\nu)}p_{\mathrm{SS},i}}W_{ij}^{(\nu)}W_{jk}^{\dagger}\frac{\partial p_{\mathrm{SS},k}}{\partial\alpha}

(18)

\displaystyle A^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-k_{B}\sum_{i,j,(\nu)}p_{\mathrm{SS},i}\frac{\partial\ln p_{\mathrm{SS},i}}{\partial\alpha}W_{ij}^{\dagger}\frac{\partial\ln p_{\mathrm{SS},j}}{\partial\alpha}

(19)

\displaystyle A^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{B}\sum_{i,j,k,l,(\nu)}\ln\frac{W_{ij}^{(\nu)}p_{\mathrm{SS},j}}{W_{ji}^{(\nu)}p_{\mathrm{SS},i}}W_{ij}^{(\nu)}W_{jk}^{\dagger}

(20)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\left(W_{kl}^{\dagger}\frac{\partial p_{\mathrm{SS},l}}{\partial\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle A^{(3)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{B}\sum_{i,j,k,l(\nu)}\ln\frac{W_{ij}^{(\nu)}p_{\mathrm{SS},j}}{W_{ji}^{(\nu)}p_{\mathrm{SS},i}}W_{ij}^{(\nu)}W^{\dagger}_{jk}W^{\dagger}_{kl}

(21)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\frac{\partial p_{\mathrm{SS},l}}{\partial\alpha}\quad

One can use

\displaystyle\ddot{\alpha}(t)A^{(3)}=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\dot{\alpha}A^{(3)}\right)-(\dot{\alpha})^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}A^{(3)}

(22)

To show that the total entropy production associated with the process is given by

\displaystyle\Delta S_{\mathrm{tot}}

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\mathcal{L}(\alpha(t),\dot{\alpha}(t))dt+\left[\dot{\alpha}A^{(3)}\right]_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}

(23)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle(\alpha(t),\dot{\alpha}(t))=\sigma_{SS}(\alpha(t))+F(\alpha(t))\dot{\alpha}

(24)

\displaystyle+(A^{(1)}(\alpha(t))+A^{(2)}(\alpha(t))-A^{(3)}(\alpha(t))\dot{\alpha}^{2}(t).

This makes it, in principle, possible to calculate the entropy production for slowly driven far-from-equilibrium systems, but it can become complicated to calculate Eqs. (
17
)-(
21
) for systems with a large state space. Therefore, it can be useful to rewrite these quantities as correlation functions that can be more easily calculated in simulations or experiments, similar to Eq. (
8
). To do this, we introduce a number of observables:

1.

The average entropy production associated with a specific state:

\displaystyle O_{1;j}=\sum_{i,(\nu)}W_{ij}^{(\nu)}\ln\frac{W_{ij}^{(\nu)}p_{SS,j}}{W_{ji}^{(\nu)}p_{SS,i}}

(25)

2.

Pertubation of steady state distribution:

\displaystyle O_{2;j}=\frac{\partial\ln p_{SS,j}}{\partial\alpha}

(26)

3.

Change in rates:

\displaystyle O_{3;ij}=\delta(t-\tau_{ij})\frac{\partial\ln(W_{ij})}{\partial\alpha}

(27)

In terms of these observables, one can write

\displaystyle\sigma_{ss}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle O_{1}\right\rangle_{\alpha}

(28)

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}\langle O_{1}(\tau)O_{2}(0)\rangle_{\alpha}dt

(29)

\displaystyle A^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}\langle O_{2}(\tau)O_{2}(0)\rangle_{\alpha}dt

(30)

\displaystyle A^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}\langle O_{1}(\tau)\tau O_{2}(0)\rangle\langle O_{2}\rangle d\tau

(31)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{(\nu)}\langle O_{1}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2})\tau_{1}O_{3}(\tau_{2})O_{2}(0)\rangle d\tau_{1}d\tau_{2}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}\langle O_{1}(\tau)\tau\left(\frac{\partial O_{2}}{\partial\alpha}|_{t=0}+O_{2}(0)^{2}\right)\rangle d\tau

\displaystyle A^{(3)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\langle O_{1}(\tau)\tau O_{2}(0)\rangle d\tau

(32)

A proof of these relations is given in appendix
E
.

One can verify from Eqs. (
17
)-(
21
) that every term except for
A^{(1)}
goes to zero in the near-equilibrium limit, as
O_{1}\rightarrow 0
, and that
A^{(1)}
reduces to Eq. (
8
), showing that our results reduce to the correct expression in the near-equilibrium limit.

III
Protocol optimization

We can now use the above expressions for the entropy production to find the protocol that minimizes the dissipation while going from an initial to a final value of
\alpha
over a given protocol duration. To do this, we rely on Lagrangian techniques.

In order to do this, we need to consider the boundary contribution of the
A_{3}
-term. This term does not fit into the Lagrangian, but since it is fixed, it is determined solely by the initial value of
\dot{\alpha}
of the protocol, which is fully determined by
\alpha
and its derivative at the end points. It is then identical for all protocols of the same boundary conditions and can therefore be omitted in the optimization.

The Euler-Lagrange equation states that the protocol
\{\alpha(t)\}
that minimizes the total entropy production

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\alpha}(\alpha,\dot{\alpha})=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{\alpha}}(\alpha,\dot{\alpha}),

(33)

This equation can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\ddot{\alpha}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2(A^{(1)}+A^{(2)}-A^{(3)})}

(34)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\frac{\partial\sigma^{SS}}{\partial\alpha}-(\dot{\alpha})^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}(A^{(1)}+A^{(2)}-A^{(3)})\right]

Note that this equation can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left[(\dot{\alpha})^{2}(A^{(1)}+A^{(2)}-A^{(3)})-\sigma^{SS}\right]=0,

(35)

or

\displaystyle(\dot{\alpha})^{2}(A^{(1)}+A^{(2)}-A^{(3)})-\sigma^{SS}=E

(36)

for some constant
E
. This in turn can be written as

\displaystyle\dot{\alpha}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{E+\sigma^{SS}}{A^{(1)}+A^{(2)}-A^{(3)}}}

(37)

This finally can be integrated to find a relation between the protocol duration and
E
:

\displaystyle t_{p}=\left|{\int_{\alpha_{0}}^{\alpha_{f}}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{A^{(1)}+A^{(2)}-A^{(3)}}{E+\sigma^{SS}}}\right)d\alpha\right|.

(38)

To calculate the protocol that minimizes dissipation, one can now numerically solve Eq. (
38
) to obtain
E
as a function of
t_{p}
, and subsequently integrate Eq. (
37
) to get the optimal protocol.

IV
Example: three-state system

We now test our framework to a simple toy model, namely discrete three-state system, depicted in Fig.
1
. This model has previously been used to model a broad range of biological systems such as cellular ion channels
Uteshev and Pennefather 1996
, and ATP-synthesis
Boyer et al. 1973

Lucero et al. 2019b
;
Large and Sivak 2019
.

As the control parameter, we will take the energy level of one of the states, namely state
3
,
E_{3}(t)=\alpha(t)
. Our aim is to minimise the entropy production of driving the system from an initial state state,
E_{3}(0)=k_{B}T
to a final state,
E_{3}(t_{f})=k_{B}T/2
through various time-dependent protocols for the parameter
\alpha
, see fig.
2
.

The system has the following energy levels:

\displaystyle E_{1}=k_{B}T\quad\mathrm{(fixed)}

\displaystyle E_{2}=k_{B}T\quad\mathrm{(fixed)}

(39)

\displaystyle E_{3}=\alpha(t)\quad\mathrm{where\>}\alpha(0)=k_{B}T,\,\alpha(t_{f})=k_{B}T/2.

i.e., energy levels
1
and
2
are kept constant throughout the protocol.

This second type of protocols has not been observed in similar frameworks of systems near equilibrium
Sivak and Crooks 2012
or when only taking into account part of the entropy production

. To understand why this happens, we can think of the Lagrangian, Eq. (

V(\alpha)=0
, due to the absence of steady-state entropy production. Meanwhile, in the mechanical picture, it is clear that the system can overshoot and then return to the original value if
\sigma_{SS}(\alpha)
is non-zero.

One might wonder at this point how well these protocols are at minimising the entropy production. To answer this question, we compare the entropy production of the optimal protocols with those of linear protocols,

\displaystyle\alpha(t)=\frac{\alpha_{f}-\alpha_{i}}{t_{p}}\cdot t+\alpha_{i},\quad\mathrm{and}\quad t_{f}-t_{i}=t_{p}

(45)

The results are shown in fig.

The entropy obtained by integrating the expansion is compared to the entropy obtained by direct Euler integration of the master equation. The optimal ’overshoot’ protocol produces less entropy than the linear protocol at long protocol durations. Interestingly, the expansion and numerical integration of the optimal protocol deviates at longer protocol duration. This feature is related to the rate of the protocol
\dot{\alpha}
not being sufficiently low (see appendix

We can draw several conclusions from this. Firstly, we do see that the optimal protocol does indeed always produce less entropy than the linear protocol. The difference seems small for short durations, but becomes more pronounced for longer protocols. In particular, we see that the entropy production of the linear protocol grows linearly with protocol duration, whereas the entropy production of the optimal protocol levels off for long protocol durations. Secondly, we see that the entropy porduction calculated from Eq. (
23
) and the numerically exact entropy production up to rather short protocol duration (
t_{p}\gtrsim 1
).

V
Discussion

In conclusion, we have developed a framework to minimise the entropy production of slowly driven systems far from thermodynamic equilibrium. We have found that both the optimal protocols and the minimal entropy production are fundamentally different from systems that operate close to equilibrium.

For future research, it would be interesting to test our results experimentally, similar to what has been done for near-equilibrium systems
Tafoya et al. 2019
. Furthermore, our framework can be used to study the thermodynamics of non-equilibrium phase-transitions
Herpich et al. 2018
;
Nguyen and Seifert 2020
;
Pham et al. 2024
. In particular, it would be interesting to see how one can minimise entropy production, while driving a system through such a phase-transition.

Appendix A
Expanding
\mathbf{p}(t)

Following
Mandal and Jarzynski 2016
, we derive the expansion for the state probability vector by rephrasing the master equation in terms of
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{SS}}(t)
and
\Delta\mathbf{p}(t)=\mathbf{p}(t)-\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{SS}}(t)
.

First, we differentiate the above expression and use the master equation (See eq.
2
):

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{SS}}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathbf{p}(t)-\Delta\mathbf{p}(t)\right)

(46)

\displaystyle=W\mathbf{p}(t)-\frac{d}{dt}\Delta\mathbf{p}(t)

We now add
W\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{SS}}=0
(Definition of steady state) and exchange
\mathbf{p}(t)
with
\Delta\mathbf{p}(t)
:

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{SS}}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=

Appendix F
On the discrepancy between integration of the master equation and the expansion

For the linear protocols, the difference between the integrated expansion and the integrated master equation decreases as the duration of the protocol increases and the protocol slows down (see fig.
6
).

Oppositely, we find that the optimized protocols deviate from the numerical integration of the master equation at long protocol durations, see
6
. In contrast to the near-equilibrium picture, increasing the protocol duration does not entail
\dot{\alpha}\rightarrow 0
for the optimal protocol and therefore the approximation might not always converge, even in the long duration limit. Longer protocol durations leads to increasingly extreme overshoots and the average energy of the system diverges,
