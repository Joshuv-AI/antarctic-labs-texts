CELMoD–antibody conjugates unlock a CD38 feedback loop in multiple myeloma
Authors: Jian Jin
Published: 2025

The paradigm of antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) has successfully transformed oncology, demonstrating that pairing the specificity of an antibody with high potency of a cytotoxic payload is a powerful therapeutic strategy1,2. To date, approximately 20 ADCs have been approved worldwide, highlighting the success and potential of ADCs as a class of powerful therapeutic modalites3. As researchers contemplate the next generation of ADCs, the field is rapidly evolving beyond traditional toxins to explore novel payloads with new mechanisms of action. Among the most promising are targeted protein degraders. Benefiting from their catalytic mechanism and expanded range of druggable targets, proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) and molecular glues (MGs) are emerging as novel payloads that can expand ADC applicability4. This has given rise to the next-generation modality: degrader–antibody conjugates (DACs), some of which have now advanced to clinical development5,6.

Given the successful clinical validation of cereblon (CRBN) E3 ligase modulators (CELMoDs) such as lenalidomide in treating multiple myeloma (MM) by degrading IKZF1/3, this class of molecular glue degraders stands out as highly attractive, yet challenging, payload candidates for DACs. Developing effective CELMoD–antibody conjugates hinges on two key design challenges: rationally engineering a molecular glue degrader that preserves picomolar–nanomolar potency while incorporating a practical conjugation handle; and achieving synergy between the payload's pharmacology and the antigen's cell biology.

Recently, Dong and colleagues7 provided elegant solutions to both challenges. Their work establishes a new blueprint for the rational engineering of DACs that are not only high efficacious in vivo but also mechanistically synergistic. To address the payload problem, the authors employed a “degradation-tail” strategy. By integrating a modular CELMoD library with a high-throughput degradation screening platform, they successfully identified a novel CELMoD payload, I034. This molecule exemplifies successful rational design, achieving sub-nanomolar degradation potency against IKZF1/3 (DC50 = 0.12 nmol/L) while incorporating a tail moiety ideally suited for linker conjugation.

Armed with this superior payload, the team developed a CD38-targeting DAC, Dara-VA-I034, using the approved antibody daratumumab and a cathepsin-cleavable dipeptide linker. The preclinical results are striking: in an NCI-H929 cell line xenograft mouse model, a single low dose (3 mg/kg) of Dara-VA-I034 resulted in 100% complete tumor eradication, demonstrating remarkable in vivo efficacy and durability. Intriguingly, when comparing their CD38-DAC against a BCMA-DAC, the authors uncovered a novel and powerful positive feedback loop. They discovered that after the CD38-DAC is internalized and releases its I034 payload, the subsequent degradation of IKZF1/3, which normally act as transcriptional repressors, leads to a significant upregulation of CD38 mRNA and protein expression on the myeloma cell surface. This mechanism creates a positive feedback loop: (1) The DAC targets CD38 and is internalized; (2) The I034 payload degrades IKZF1/3; (3) The cell responds by expressing more CD38; and (4) This increased antigen density enables more DAC molecules to bind and internalize, thus amplifying the therapeutic effect. Crucially, this effect was specific to CD38 as BCMA expression was not similarly amplified, explaining the superior in vivo efficacy of Dara-VA-I034.

While these advances are highly significant, several challenges remain. First, on-target but off-tumor risk must be managed. CD38 is present on subsets of immune cells, so antigen sink and immune modulation merit dose-selection, monitoring, and potentially Fc-engineering. Second, because of inter-species differences in CRBN homology, toxicology studies in non-primate models may not fully predict safety in humans. Last, perhaps most importantly, is the question of whether the discovery of this IKZF1/3 degradation-CD38 upregulation positive feedback pair can be generalized to other protein degraders and their respective antigens. Further exploration of this type of positive feedback loop, particularly for DACs carrying transcription factor degraders, could establish a new paradigm for the rational design of this emerging class of promising therapeutics.

In conclusion, the work by Dong's group7 shifts the paradigm of DAC design beyond merely viewing the antibody as a delivery vehicle. It emphasizes the critical importance of payload-antigen compatibility. The future of rational DAC design should consider whether the biological action of the payload can synergistically amplify the target antigen, as demonstrated in this work, or if it may have an antagonistic effect. This study provides not only a potential therapeutic candidate for multiple myeloma but, more importantly, a sophisticated design principle for the entire field of DACs.
