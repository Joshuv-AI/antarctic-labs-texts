The portfolio effect: an opportunity for improving handoffs quality in ICU
Authors: Céline Monard, Josselin Carrere, Paul Abraham, Valerie Cerro, Stephanie Polazzi, Cécile Payet, Thomas Rimmelé, Antoine Duclos
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHandoffs are a major determinant of patient’s safety but their implementation remains heterogeneous and non-standardized. Organizational factors, including the order in which individual cases are handled within the handoff, may play a role in their quality. We aimed to confirm the existence of the portfolio effect (e.g. a decrease in duration allocated to individual cases as the global handoff progresses) in ICU’s morning medical handoffs. MethodsTwo research assistants observed the morning handoffs in two ICUs (ICU-1, a 20-bed trauma and surgical ICU and ICU-2 a 10-bed medical and surgical ICU) within a university hospital, over a 6-month period. They were trained to measure the duration of each case (i.e., the handoff of a single patient). Patients’ socio-demographic and clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records. The effect of the case position within the global handoff on its duration was determined using a linear regression after log transformation of duration. The case position was categorized as either before or after the median position (first and second halves). Covariates clinically associated with handoff duration were included in the model (age, sex, Charlson comorbidities index, SAPS II score, number of organ supports, center (ICU-1 or ICU-2) and reason for admission).Results2485 individual cases nested in 169 morning handoffs and related to 494 patients’ stays were observed. The mean (± SD) duration of the morning handoff was 60 minutes (± 12.5) in ICU-1 and 35.2 minutes (± 10.6) in ICU-2 with a mean number of cases presented of 18.9 (± 1.3) and 9.3 (± 1.0) respectively. The mean (± SD) duration of a case was 175 seconds (± 108). Trauma stays, patients severity and comorbidities, and the number of organ supports were associated with longer case handoffs. Asjusting for these covariates, cases in the second half were shorter compared to cases in the first half (RR 0.65, 95%CI (0.51 - 0.80)).ConclusionsWe confirmed the existence of a portfolio effect within ICU handoffs, emphasizing that interventions targeting handoffs’ improvement should focus on the content and the setting. Background

Medical handoffs are pivotal for ensuring the continuity and safety of patient care. They allow for the transfer of essential information between different healtcare teams, both before and after on-call periods. Poor quality handoffs are likely to favor the occurrence of adverse events [1, 2] and the Joint Commission stressed out that communication breakdowns are the primary factor in the majority of sentinel events, with around half of them attributed to handoffs [3]. Their role is of particular importance in intensive care units (ICU) where the complexity and severity of patients lead to rapid changes in their health status and require precise transmission of updated informations. However, despite the growing recognition of handoffs as a major determinant of safety, their implementation remains largely heterogeneous and non-standardized [4, 5].

In recent years, different approaches have been tested to improve quality and safety of handoffs. Most interventions have focused on tools to standardize content, aiming at reducing omissions [6]. While content standardization can reduce the occurrence of serious adverse events [7], there are limitations to rely solely on mnemonics and checklists [8, 9]. Thus, beyond its content, the quality of a handoff depends on a combination of human and organizational factors related to its implementation and contributing to the establishment of a shared mental representation between the sender and the receivers [10]. To date, there is a lack of data on organizational and implementation factors, the importance of which is underestimated.

Among organizational factors, the order in which individual cases are presented during the overall handoff may play a significant role. The total duration of a medical handoff is limited, and time allocation for each case should ideally be based solely on its complexity. However, a handfull of studies have suggested a systematic decrease in the time allocated to each case as the handoff progresses, a phenomenon known as the “portfolio effect” [11, 12]. In most ICUs, the order of cases is determined by the room number, and this predictable order could impact patient management and outcomes for those systematically presented at the end of the handoff if they consistently receive less time.

Our aim was to confirm the existence of the portfolio effect in ICU morning handoffs, thus unveiling an easily modifiable organisational factor to improve handoffs’ quality.

Methods

Design, data collection

We conducted a prospective observational study in two ICUs (ICU-1 and ICU-2) within a tertiary university hospital in Lyon, France. Before study initiation, it was already in force that a research assistant participated in the morning medical handoffs in each unit, for clinical research purposes. For this study, the research assistants observed and evaluated all medical morning handoffs from Monday to Friday, in both ICUs, over a six-month period. For every single patient’s handoff (case) inside the overall handoff, they collected duration, contextual quality and content. The same research assistant was attached to the same unit throughout the study, and both received a common training to assess handoff’s duration and quality.

The sender of the handoff was the doctor completing his overnight shift. He was asked to fill in a questionnaire about his status (fellow (< 5 years of experience in critical care), attending physician (5–9 years of experience), senior (> 10 years)) and his cumulated amount of sleep during the shift (number of hours).

We extracted socio-demographic and clinical data from the patient’s medical records, including severity at ICU admission, comorbidities, reason for admission, and duration (in days) between admission and the handoff day. Additionaly, we recorded the number of organ support therapies provided during the night preceding the handoff.

Handoff duration

The duration of each case was meticulously timed, commencing when the sender mentioned the patient’s name or room number and ending when the name of the following case was mentioned or when participants moved to the next case.

Handoff quality

We assessed contextual quality using the mini-CEX handoff tool, which encompasses three domains (professionalism, communication abilities and setting), each rated from 1 (very poor) to 9 (excellent) [13]. The total score was the average of the scores obtained in the 3 domains (score from 0 to 9).

Content quality was evaluated by assessing the fulfillment of each item in the I-PASS tool, which consists of five items: illness severity, patient background, to-do list, contingency plan, and synthesis by the receiver [14]. Each item received a score of 1 if mentioned during the case handoff. The total score was obtained by adding all item scores (score from 0 to 5). This tool was used only for research purpose and was not routinely used for handoffs in our center.

Setting

ICU-1 was a 20-bed trauma and surgical ICU in which morning handoffs were performed sitting in a room, using a computer to share radiological and biological results. An attending or senior intensivist was on shift with a fellow, who was responsible for conducting the morning handoffs. The receivers were members of the medical team of the day (fellows, attendings and seniors intensivists).

In ICU-2, a 10-bed medical and surgical ICU, morning handoffs were performed in a standing position, walking in the corridor from one room to the next one. There was only one doctor on shift, sending the handoff to the day-team which included attending intensivists, physiotherapists and nurses.

In both units, the morning handoffs were always performed following the room numbers and no organizational changes occurred during the study period. A printed handoff document was prepared by the senders and used by the day teams during the handoff. For each patient, this written handoff included the most relevant informations, such as age, significant comorbidities, reason for admission, and recent events. However, this document being quite brief, several crucial informations were conveyed solely during the verbal handoff (such as communication with relatives, to-do lists, and pending results). No interventions were conducted to improve handoffs’ quality during the study period and the participants did not receive any information regarding the I-Pass or mini-CEX tools.

Because clinical fellows are rotating every 6 months (in May and November), we conducted the study over a 6 months period, from November 1st to May 1st.

Statistical analyses

Continuous data were expressed as mean (± standard deviation (SD)) and compared using Student test or Wilcoxcon rank-sum test, as appropriate. Categorical data were expressed as numbers (percentage) and compared using Fisher’s exact test.

The effect of where the case was placed in the global handoff on the duration of its individual handoff was determined using a linear regression model after a log transformation of the handoff duration. For the primary outcome, case position within the handoff was categorized as either before or after the median position and for secondary analysis it was categorized by quartiles. Covariates clinically associated with handoff duration were included in the model (patients’ age, sex, Charlson comorbidities index, SAPS II score, number of organ support, center (ICU-1 or ICU-2) and reason for admission). Interactions between covariates were tested and significant interactions were incorporated into the final model. Handoffs with missing duration values were excluded from the analysis. Missing covariates were not imputed. Results were expressed as relative risk (RR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI95%).

Data curation and analysis were performed with Statistical Enterprise Guide software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), with a significance level of p < 0.05.

Ethics

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Hospices Civils de Lyon, (n°20–80). Data collection and conservation was conducted according to the methodology of reference N°004 of the Commission Nationale de l’Informatique et des Libertés (CNIL), following a declaration to the data protection officer (n° 20–160). All healthcare professionals involved in handoffs consented to participate in the study. Patients or their representatives received written information and had the opportunity to decline participation. Given the retrospective and non-interventional nature of the study, necessity of written informed consent was waived and utilization of non-genetic health-related personal data was authorized if the patient (or its legal representative) had not expressed wishes of non-participating to clinical research.

Results

Population

We observed 2485 individual handoffs, and duration was available for 2470 (99%) of them. These individual handoffs were nested within 169 morning handoffs conducted between November 3rd 2020 and April 29th 2021 (N = 98 in ICU-1 and N = 71 in ICU-2) and were related to 494 patients’ stays. The mean (± SD) duration of the morning overall handoff was 60 min (± 12.5) in ICU-1 and 35.2 min (± 10.6) in ICU-2 with an average number of presented patients of 18.9 (± 1.3) and 9.3 (± 1.0), respectively. Contextual quality of individual handoffs was consistent between units (ICU-1 and ICU-2) and across all domains, with a mean (±SD) total score of 7.5/9 (±0.9). Regarding medical content, a mean (±SD) number of 3/5 (±0.8) items from the I-PASS tool was completed. Severity and patient history were reported in 98% of individual handoffs, whereas action list, contingency planning and synthesis by receiver were reported in only 50%, 27% and 15% of handoffs, respectively, with large variations between units (Table 3). Discussion

In this large prospective observational study including nearly 2500 individual handoffs nested in more than 150 ICU morning ICU handoffs, we successfully confirmed the existence of a portfolio effect. With similar patients characteristics, the handoff duration for a case presented in the second half of the handoff was 35% shorter than the handoff duration for a patient presented in the first half. This effect was significant only in one of the two units (ICU-1) where the number of beds was greater and the total duration of handoff longer. This may suggest that time allocation decreases after a certain duration, with potentially more pronounced effects in larger units. However, when dividing the population into quartiles, we observed that time shrinkage occured early during the handoff process. Our results do not allow us to distinguish between a shortening of the last cases (with intentional or unintentional omission of information) and a lengthening of the first cases because they are better known by the sender, who provides more details, or simply because the team lingers at the beginning.

This study confirms previous observations made in smaller populations, which also suggested the existence of a portfolio effect but did not adjust for patient characteristics [11]. In addition to confirming these findings in a larger population while accounting for confounding factors, we were able to quantify the importance of this portfolio effect.

The current strategies to enhance ICU handoffs quality primarily rely on mnemonics and checklists to standardize content [15]. While this is an essential step, it may not be sufficient. Multidimensional interventions, including improvement of the setting (e.g. reducing interruptions), or providing written data sheets with key informations may contribute to a more efficient handoff [16, 17]. The identification of contextual factors that can influence the quality of ICU handoffs is thus a prerequisite for the development of such multidimensional interventions. We demonstrated that a simple and easily modifiable organizational factor (the ordering of cases) might influence the handoff process. Other organizational factors (such as location) or human factors (familiarity, fatigue and individual characteristics of the participants) may also play a role and should be further evaluated [18].

Our study may have important clinicial implications for handoffs implementation. Based on our results, we suggest avoiding the systematic presentation of the same patients at the end of the handoff. Being aware of the portfolio effect should encourage the participants to give enough time to all patients, particularly those at the end of the handoff, or to present the cases in a logical order based on their severity/complexity. Similar portfolio effect might also exist for other activities, such as daily bedside rounds and multidisciplinary concertation meetings. While awaiting dedicated investigations, clinicians should kept this in mind while planning and organizing their work.

Our study exhibits several strengths. It is the most extensive investigation to date on the portfolio effect, involving 2500 individual handoffs, and it is the first to account for patient-associated factors. Our precise definition of handoff duration and the training of both observers ahead of study beginning minimized measurements biases. In addition, the same observer evaluated all the morning handoffs in one single unit, further decreasing the interrater variability over the course of the study. The Hawthorne effect, i.e. the change in participants’ behaviour due to the fact that they are aware that they are being observed, was reduced by the extended duration of the study over a period of 6 months and by the fact that the observers were already involved in the morning handoffs before the start of the study [19].

Our study also has limits. First, we were not able to evaluate the association of patient position within the global handoff and individual handoff duration with outcomes. Future trials could explore whether being systematically presented at the end of the handoff is associated with worse outcomes or an increased occurrence of adverse events. Second, we were unable to assess the receivers’ understanding and memorization of the handoffs, which would have been an excellent evaluation of handoff quality [20]. As attention may decline with time, it is possible that handoffs performed in the second half were less well memorised. Several studies have emphasized the importance of read-back actions to correct communication errors and ensure the message was well received [21]. Notably, we found that the synthesis by receiver was the least completed item of the I-PASS, leaving considerable room for improvement. Third, our initial assumption that patients would be randomly distributed across rooms, regardless of their reason for admission, complexity, or severity, was confounded by the emergence of COVID-19 during the study period. COVID-19 patients were grouped together whenever possible. Because these patients had shorter handoffs (more standardized disease and patients with simpler history and less comorbidities), this may have influenced our results and explained the interaction found between admission reason and position within the round. Fourth, we were not able to find a difference in the quality of handoffs performed in the first or second halves of the round. This could be because the tools were not sensitive enough or because of measurement bias. It is also possible that the handoffs in the second half were more concise and direct, with less useless information and similar quality. Fifth, we used the number of organ support therapies as a surrogate for patient severity on the night before the handoff. This criteria does not accurately reflect the patient’s severity as it may for example include patients requiring high doses of vasopressors as well as patients on low dose. However, with this covariate, we aimed to measure the intensivist workload, and amount of important information to transfer. With this in mind, low dose vasopressors are also of interest (for example, starting vasopressors or speed up their weaning may have require an hemodynamic evaluation, sepsis diagnostic workups, …). Finally, the importance of the portfolio effect varied between the two units, suggesting that factors such as the number of beds, sender experience or the setting of the handoff may influence the portfolio effect. Because we only included two centers, we could not discern the impact of each of these characteristics.

Conclusion

In this large study we confirmed the existence of the portfolio effect in ICU handoffs. While further investigations are necessary to evaluate its impact on patient outcomes, our results suggest that interventions targeting handoff improvement should address both medical content and organizational factors. Specifically, we suggest avoiding systematically presenting the same patient at the end of the handoff.
