Performance-limiting metastable intragrain impurity nanoclusters in metal halide perovskites
Authors: Weizhen Wang, Mingwei Hao, Xiaofen Li, Du Chen, Kun Zhou, Peijun Guo, Lijun Zhang, Songhua Cai, Yuanyuan Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Impurity control in metal halide perovskite thin films is crucial for advancing the efficiency and long-term stability of perovskite solar cells. While previous efforts have mainly addressed impurities at grain boundaries and heterointerfaces, efforts have rarely been made to reveal and modulate (sub-)nanoscale impurities within grains. In this work, using low-dose scanning transmission electron microscopy, we observed the nontrivial existence of intragrain impurity nanoclusters, identifying a previously unknown, metastable orthorhombic non-perovskite phase with a parallel chain-like structure, in nominally processed Cs-incorporated mixed-cation perovskite films. First-principles calculations and quasi-in situ observations reveal that these intragrain nanoclusters adversely affect the optoelectronic properties and chemical stability of the perovskite. Guided by these insights, we incorporate inner salts during the solution processing, effectively reducing nanocluster density and enhancing device performance. Clarifying unconventional impurities hidden in perovskite crystal grains opens a research avenue in fundamental understanding and nano-engineering for improved perovskite solar cells and optoelectronics.

Introduction

Metal halide perovskites represent a broad group of semiconductor materials characterized by flexible compositions and diverse crystal structures. In typical three-dimensional perovskites, the chemical formula follows ABX3, where A refers to either cesium or an organic cation, B is commonly Pb, and X denotes a halide anion1. ABX3 perovskites have been proven to be excellent optoelectronic materials with long carrier diffusion lengths and tunable band gaps2–5. They can also be processed under mild conditions based on solution methods, unleashing their potential to innovate solar cells at a low cost6–15. Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have now become a major photovoltaic (PV) technology that can compete with the existing silicon PVs. The past decade has witnessed the rapid development of PSCs towards promising power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) and stability16–19.

Impurity control in perovskites has been recognized as one prominent approach because impurities can not only affect the transport of photo-generated carriers but also usually initiate the degradation of materials20–23. In the literature, most efforts have been devoted to engineering the impurity phases at grain boundaries (GBs) and device heterointerfaces, which are relatively straightforward to characterize, while the grain interiors have usually been assumed to be phase pure in these studies8,23–26. Nevertheless, owing to the soft crystal lattice, perovskite may accommodate various nanoscale heterogeneous impurity phases within the grain interiors27–30. One challenge of studying these intragrain impurities is that they are fully buried in the crystalline perovskite matrix and have no apparent surface features. As a result, they are undetectable using conventional surface characterization methods such as scanning electron microscopy (SEM), atomic-force microscopy (AFM), or X-ray diffraction (XRD)28. To overcome this limitation, low-dose scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) has emerged as a powerful tool with extremely high spatial resolution. This technique has recently been applied to directly imaging the atomic-scale structure of perovskite grains in real space while minimizing beam-induced damage27,29,31–35. It is intriguing to leverage this prominent characterization development to unravel the microstructure and performance-limiting effects of intragrain impurity phases in perovskites, which remains an uncharted territory.

Based on low-dose STEM, this work resolves the atomic structure of a previously unknown metastable A2BX4 impurity phase buried within the crystalline grains of nominally processed Cs-incorporated mixed-cation perovskite thin films. It exhibits a 1D chain-like crystal structure, imparting unfavored large band gaps and metastability, as we reveal using density functional theory (DFT) calculations. The A2BX4 impurity phase exhibits a nanocluster morphology, mostly with dimensions of less than 20 nm. Based on quasi-in situ STEM observation, we also demonstrate that these intragrain impurity nanoclusters (IGINs) can trigger the degradation of perovskite grains, causing detrimental effects on the optoelectronic processes in perovskites. This work is linked to our previous study, which has revealed the intriguing healing behavior of intragrain impurities30. However, the mechanisms behind the formation and healing of intragrain impurities remained unclear at that time. This work now clarifies the atomic-level structure and the role of the A₂PbX₄ impurity phase, leading to a plausible chemical mechanism for the formation and phase-healing of intragrain impurities. Specifically, we find that the formation of the PbI₂ and A₂PbX₄ impurity pair results from intragrain cation segregation, as described by the dissociation reaction: 2APbX₃ → PbI₂ + A₂PbX₄. The phase-healing process is the combination reaction of the impurity pair: PbI₂ + A₂PbX₄ → 2APbX₃, leading to an intragrain cation homogenization. For a proof-of-concept demonstration, we develop an IGIN-mitigation method that features the use of 3-(N,N-dimethylpalmitylammonio)-propane-sulfonate (NDPS) inner salt to homogenize solution cations and manipulate intragrain microstructures in the resultant films. Performance and stability improvements of PSCs are shown, featuring an improved PCE of 26.03% in the n-i-p device setting and a T95 operational stability of over 1000 h (T95 means the time for a PSC to decrease to 95% of its initial PCE). This work, in conjunction with our previous work30, provides atomic insights into understudied intragrain microstructural phenomena and unravels their performance-limiting mechanisms, pointing to a pathway in the nanoengineering of thin films for improved PSCs.

Results

Lattice-resolved microstructure of intragrain impurities

Mono-cation (FAPbI3, MAPbI3, CsPbI3), FA-Cs based (FAxCs1-xPbI3) (x = 0.5, 0.8, 0.9) films, and triple-cation ((FA0.95Cs0.05)PbI3)0.975(MAPbBr3)0.025 compositions (FA, formamidinium; MA, methylammonium) are selected for low-dose STEM intragrain structure investigations since they are representative compositions for achieving PSCs with high PCEs and stability. The FA0.5Cs0.5PbI3 samples were used for accurate characterization of the intracrystalline microstructure for their relatively good resistance to electron irradiation. We would like to point out that our research results based on FA0.5Cs0.5PbI3 samples are applicable to other Cs-incorporated mixed-cation perovskite films with different Cs contents, as confirmed by our low-dose STEM observations on multiple compositions. We focused on the PSC device cross-sections, which can facilitate linking microscopic observations to the resultant device performance. The focused ion beam (FIB) fabricated PSC device specimens are encapsulated by ultrathin amorphous carbon layers as demonstrated in our previous studies. This mitigates the electron-dose sensitivity issue of perovskite samples during STEM observations29,30. Figure 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1 present representative atomic-resolution high-angle dark-field STEM (HAADF-STEM) images obtained from a fresh FA0.5Cs0.5PbI3 PSC device. The beam current of the electron probe used in the present study was reduced to 1 pA to minimize electron-beam damage. All atomic positions in the perovskite grain match the projection along [010] zone axis of a perovskite phase with an orthorhombic structure (space group Pbnm), coinciding with previously reported results29,30,36. A high density of irregularly shaped IGINs, with projected area less than 20 nm2, was observed within the perovskite grains. The enlarged HAADF-STEM images for these IGINs are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. We then performed the imaging over, in, and under focus conditions, as shown in Fig. 1b-c and Supplementary Fig. 3. The images matched well with the [010]o projection of perovskite at over and under focus conditions. However, once the electron beam is in focus, the atomic lattice refers to the non-perovskite impurity phase, confirming that IGINs are buried entirely inside the perovskite grain.

Two types of nano-scale intragrain impurities, PbI2 and non-PbI2, and their intriguing healing behavior were resolved in FA-Cs based films30. However, the mechanism behind the formation and healing of these intragrain impurities was unclear. A careful comparison revealed that the previously reported non-PbI2 phase, which was speculated to have a zero-dimensional crystal structure, has the same projected structure as the IGINs shown in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2. Uncovering the atomic structure of IGINs, thus, is the key to revealing the plausible chemical mechanism for their formation and annihilation. However, only atomic-resolution projected images in two or more different directions can accurately reconstruct the three-dimensional atomic structure of a crystal phase. To elucidate the accurate atomic structure of this non-perovskite impurity phase, we obtained the atomic-resolution HAADF-STEM images of IGINs in three projections ([010]o, [001]o and [100]o) (Fig. 1d-f). The observed IGIN phase fits a C-face centered orthorhombic structure (space group Cmmm) with the lattice parameters a = 0.926 nm, b = 1.309 nm and c = 0.463 nm (Supplementary Fig. 4). The chemical formula of the unit cell is A2BX4, where A is FA+ or Cs+, B is Pb2+, and X- is I-. In an FAxCs1-xPbI3 film, the IGIN phase can be expressed as (FAxCs1-x)2PbI4. The positions of atoms within the unit cell are listed in Supplementary Table 1. We then used image simulations to determine the ratio of FA+ and Cs+, as detailed in Supplementary Fig. 5, showing the simulated atomic images of (FA0.4Cs0.6)2PbI4 match well with experimental HAADF-STEM images (Supplementary Fig. 5). The simulated HAADF-STEM images and electron-diffraction patterns along the [001]IGIN, [010]IGIN and [100]IGIN directions (Fig. 1g-i) also match well with the experimental data (Fig. 1d-f). As further revealed by the reconstructed atomic structure shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, the A2BX4 phase exhibits a one-dimensional crystallographic feature formed by edge-shared BX4- octahedral chains, and the A cations are located between these one-dimensional chains.

We then calculated the XRD pattern of the A2BX4 phase using our proposed impurity structure. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6, the main diffraction peaks of A2BX4 overlap with those of PbI2, indicating that XRD is unreliable for distinguishing A2BX4 from PbI2. In particular, the nanoscale A2BX4 phase is encapsulated in the crystalline perovskite matrix, which can hardly be detected in perovskite films where PbI2 is usually a native impurity. Furthermore, these A2BX4 IGINs generally exist in FA0.8Cs0.2PbI3, FA0.9Cs0.1PbI3, and triple-cation ((FA0.95Cs0.05)PbI3)0.975(MAPbBr3)0.025 Cs-incorporated perovskite films (Supplementary Figs. 7-9). No such IGINs was observed in mono-cation (FAPbI3, MAPbI3, CsPbI3) samples as shown in Supplementary Figs. 10-12. Since Cs doping is currently common in high-performance perovskites, it is important to reveal the impact of IGINs on perovskites. Notably, the Cs content in the A2BX4 phase is higher than that in the perovskite grain, demonstrating that the cation segregation may trigger IGIN formation. Therefore, suppressing the cation segregation can potentially avoid the formation of IGINs, which will be discussed later.

Performance-limiting effects of intragrain impurities

We then conducted quasi-in situ and ex situ low-dose STEM observations to assess the impact of IGINs on the stability of the FA-Cs perovskite grains. For quasi-in situ observations, the samples were removed from the electron microscope chamber immediately after observations to minimize electron beam-induced artifacts, and exposed to ambient conditions (25 °C, 50% relative humidity (RH)). The structural evolutions were continuously tracked through low-dose STEM imaging. The samples for ex situ observations were transferred to vacuum packages immediately after fabrication and then investigated after one week of storage. Figure 2a and b illustrate structural changes in the FA0.5Cs0.5PbI3 film upon exposure to ambient conditions. According to the atomic resolution image and the corresponding fast Fourier transformation (FFT) pattern (inset in Fig. 2a), only IGINs exist in the pristine sample, confirmed by the FFT spot of (020) plane of IGINs with 0.656 nm interplanar spacing indicated by yellow circle in the inset in Fig. 2a. After the exposure, PbI2 phases evolve at the original locations of (FA0.4Cs0.6)2PbI4 impurities, as shown in Fig. 2b. The emergence of a new spot with an interplanar spacing of 1.457 nm (corresponding to the (001) plane of 2H PbI2), indicated by white circle in the corresponding FFT pattern in the inset in Fig. 2b, confirmed the formation of PbI2. Conversely, the GB regions without IGINs showed no notable structural alterations. Moreover, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 13, after the FA0.5Cs0.5PbI3 device was stored in a vacuum package for one week, the co-existence of (FA0.4Cs0.6)2PbI4 and high-density PbI2 can be observed, which is consistent with our previous observations30. The average PbI2 particle size exceeds that of pristine (FA0.4Cs0.6)2PbI4 impurities (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1), aligning with quasi-in situ observations. Our quasi-in situ and ex situ results collectively demonstrate that the one-dimensional (FA0.4Cs0.6)2PbI4 formed during the fabrication is meta-stable, prone to decompose into PbI2 under environmental stress. We then performed DFT calculations to study the effect of A2BX4 IGINs on the structural stability and optoelectronic properties of perovskite grains (details in Methods). It was proposed that the valence band maximum (VBM) and the conduction band minimum (CBM) of perovskites and the relevant phases composed of octahedral units are determined by the length of Pb-I and the angle of Pb-I-Pb37. The cations, located at gaps between octahedral units, such as FA+, MA+, and Cs+, have a limited effect on their electronic structure38. For the sake of clarity and to reduce computational costs, we use CsPbI3 and Cs2PbI4 as models to calculate the impact of IGIN formation on the stability and electronic structure of the perovskite system. Structural optimization for the IGIN phase utilizes the experimentally determined orthorhombic structure as the initial configuration, which exhibits a quasi-face-centered cubic (FCC) sublattice of I- ions. This feature is commensurate with that of the perovskite phase, where the I- anions, along with the FA+/Cs+ cations, also form a quasi-FCC sublattice. The lattice parameters of CsPbI3 and Cs2PbI4 obtained from DFT calculations are shown in Supplementary Table 2. We then calculated the formation energies of CsPbI3 and Cs2PbI4 with reference to the stable binary compounds of CsI and PbI2. According to the computed phase diagram, Cs2PbI4 is a metastable phase at the tie line between CsPbI3 and Cs4PbI6, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 14a. The DFT calculations reveal that CsPbI3 and Cs2PbI4 have formation energies of -0.048 and -0.016 eV per formula unit (f.u.), respectively (Fig. 2c). The less negative formation energy of Cs2PbI4 indicates its lower thermodynamic stability and higher tendency to decompose into CsI and PbI2 (Supplementary Fig. 14b), the latter of which has been confirmed through quasi-in situ investigations. We also calculated the electronic density of states (DOS) for CsPbI3 and Cs2PbI4 (Fig. 2d). It is worth noting that Cs2PbI4 exhibits a considerably larger bandgap (2.84 eV) than CsPbI3 (1.83 eV). After aligning the energy bands according to the vacuum level, a type I heterojunction distribution is formed as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 15. The CBM of the perovskite phase is 0.24 eV lower than that of the IGIN phase, while the VBM is 0.77 eV higher. Therefore, extra energy barriers and charge-blocking regions within perovskite grains can be introduced by the formation of IGINs. The charge carriers generated in the perovskite phase can hardly be transferred to the IGIN phase. Instead, they will bypass the IGIN phase along the perovskite domain during their transfer. This causes the electron transfer pathway to become longer, resulting in more consumption and electron-hole recombination.

In addition, the formation of IGINs also brings about numerous additional intragrain interfaces. Three types of perovskite-IGIN interfaces were resolved at the atomic level, based on which atomic models were built for calculating the corresponding electronic structures (Fig. 2e–s). One half of each atomic model contains CsPbI3, while the other half contains Cs2PbI4. This configuration enables us to study the targeted interface between the two phases directly, without introducing other interfaces. The structural similarity of both phases, sharing the same structure type (quasi-FCC) of specific sublattices, leads to a low mismatch between specific lattice planes of CsPbI3 and Cs2PbI4, resulting in the semi-coherent interfaces as revealed by STEM imaging (Fig. 2e, j, o). As shown in Fig. 2e–g, the interface (denoted as interface I) between the (101) lattice plane of CsPbI3 and the (110) plane of Cs2PbI4 could generate multiple defect states near the CBM of CsPbI3. These defect states are primarily localized on specific PbI6 polyhedra at the interface, mainly contributed by the p orbitals of Pb atoms. In comparison to the PbI2-perovskite interface, which was previously demonstrated to be benign when there are no point defects at the interface30, the localized trap states exhibited at a pristine perovskite-IGIN interface suggest that the IGIN phase is intrinsically detrimental to the device PCEs. Even without other defects, the IGINs could potentially increase the recombination of the photo-excited charge carriers. On the contrary, the VBM of the interfacial region is contributed by the perovskite domain (Supplementary Fig. 16), exhibiting a delocalized character similar to that in the bulk phase.

The interfaces can serve as sinks for migrating point defects. Therefore, the effect of point defects on the optoelectronic properties of a device with IGINs has also been examined, as displayed in Fig. 2h and i for interface I. Given the relatively low formation energy of the iodine vacancies (VI) both in the bulk perovskite phase and at the perovskite-IGIN interfaces (Supplementary Fig. 17), it is expected that VI defects will accumulate over time and interact with the perovskite-IGIN interfaces during device operation. The calculation result shows that VI could generate localized trapped states distributed at the interface, increasing carrier recombination rate, similar to the case described in our previous study30. In addition, such analyses can be extended to other interface configurations, denoted as interface II and III as shown in Fig. 2j and o. Notably, defect states can emerge for each configuration, regardless of whether a point defect exists or not. The defect states are located near the CBM and primarily localized on the Pb atoms at the interface (Fig. 2k-n and p-s), which could significantly enhance recombination between electrons and holes as localized trap states. On the other hand, no empty states emerge near the VBM, and the highest occupied state is delocalized (Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19), meaning that the interfacial structures will only contribute to defect states near the CBM.

Synthetic control of intragrain impurity nanoclusters

Here, as IGINs may form concurrently with cation segregation, we utilized GPA to identify the mismatch of lattice between perovskites and IGINs, as well as the residual strain in perovskite grains induced by cation segregation (Fig. 3a-b and Supplementary Fig. 20). In perovskite grains containing a large density of IGINs, pronounced strain concentration appears not only around the IGINs, but also in adjacent perovskite regions. To eliminate IGINs, we introduced an amphiphilic surfactant additive of NDPS inner salt into the precursor solution. Inner salts are known to tailor the perovskite crystallization and passivate the defects39–41. DFT calculations indicate that NDPS features an electron-deficient quaternary ammonium group and a negatively charged sulfonic group42,43 (Supplementary Fig. 21), making it well-suited for interacting with both cations and anions. Functioning as a surfactant additive, NDPS molecules process both hydrophilic and hydrophobic moieties. These molecules can spontaneously organize on the surface of the precursor solution, orienting their hydrophobic ends toward the air. This behavior lowers the surface tension of solution and reduces the critical radius of the nucleus, that is, the number of solute particles required to form a stable nucleus39–41. The decrease in surface tension is corroborated by a measurable reduction in the contact angle of perovskite solution on the substrate upon surfactant addition, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 22.

Top-view SEM imaging showed that incorporating NDPS into FA0.9Cs0.1PbI3 precursor solutions does not significantly affect the perovskite grain size compared to additive-free controls (Supplementary Figs. 23 and 24). XRD analysis indicated that the NDPS with a large molecular size does not incorporate into the perovskite lattice or form large-scale impurities in films, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 25. In addition, XRD results also indicated that the peaks of impurities gradually decreased with an increase in the concentration of NDPS, suggesting strong coordination between Pb2+, I- and NDPS molecules. We then performed X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis on perovskite films with and without NDPS. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 26, the emergence of S 2p peak and a new peak around 402.5 eV in N 1 s spectra obtained from films with additives clearly show the successful incorporation of NDPS into perovskite films. Furthermore, the Pb 4 f and I 3 d peaks of films with 0.5 mg mL-1 and 1 mg mL-1 NDPS additives shifted to lower binding energy as compared with the control film, indicating the coordination between Pb2+, I-, and NDPS, as shown in Fig. 3c and d.

To evaluate the residual strain in FA0.9Cs0.1PbI3 films with and without NDPS, we performed grazing incidence x-ray diffraction (GIXRD) analyses using the 2θ-sin2(ψ) method44,45. The (012) reflection at about 31.5°, based on the cubic symmetry of FA0.9Cs0.1PbI3 (Pm\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{3}$$\end{document}3¯m, a = 0.6362 nm), was selected for strain analysis due to its multiplication and more significant diffraction intensity, which can provide the most reliable structural symmetry information. The setup details for the X-ray diffractometer are listed in Supplementary Table 3. As shown in Fig. 3e, f and Supplementary Fig. 27, when increasing ψ from 0° to 50°, the (012) peak gradually shifts to lower angles in both the control and the 0.5 mg mL-1 NDPS perovskite films, as indicated by the dot lines, confirming the presence of residual tensile strain. However, a smaller angular shift in the 0.5 mg mL-1 NDPS sample compared with the control film suggests partial strain alleviation. At NDPS concentrations above 1 mg mL-1, the peak shift vanished entirely, demonstrating the complete release of residual tensile strain (Fig. 3f).

To confirm the inhibitory effect of additives on IGINs, we further conducted low-dose atomic-resolution HAADF-STEM imaging and GPA analysis of FA0.5Cs0.5PbI3, FA0.8Cs0.2PbI3, FA0.9Cs0.1PbI3, and (FA0.95Cs0.05PbI3)0.975(MAPbBr3)0.025 with 1 mg mL-1 NDPS (target) samples, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 28. No IGINs were observed in the perovskite grains, consistent with the released strain concentration due to the homogenization of cations. To reveal the distribution of NDPS in the perovskite film, we further performed electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) measurements by exploiting the signal provided by sulfur in angular dark-field (ADF) STEM mode, which unequivocally identifies the additive from the perovskite (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 29, details in Methods). Low-magnification ADF-STEM imaging of the target films revealed no detectable impurities. Elemental mapping of S confirmed that the NDPS was concentrated at the GBs and the upper surface of the perovskite layer, consistent with XRD analysis. This distribution suggests that, due to its relatively large molecular size, NDPS is spontaneously excluded from the growing perovskite lattice and accumulates at surfaces and GBs during film formation (Fig. 3g). The long alkyl chain of NDPS enables it to self-assemble on both the perovskite grains and solution surfaces, acting as a reservoir that promotes solute ion intercalation and functions as a molecular sieve to moderate the evaporation of solvents. Furthermore, the highly polar sulfonyl (S = O) group facilitates the formation of strong hydrogen bonding networks among polar solvent molecules, thereby decelerating the crystallization process39–41. The polar hydrophilic head groups anchor to charged ions and crystal surfaces, while the hydrophobic alkyl tails aggregate around the perovskite grains, enhancing structural organization during film formation. The strong coordination between Pb2+, I-, and NDPS around GBs can passivate GBs, improving the stability of perovskite films. More importantly, as demonstrated by our quasi-in situ characterization, the grain interiors with IGINs begin to degrade prior to GBs (Fig. 2a, b), implying the importance of eliminating IGINs rather than passivating GBs.

Our results clearly demonstrate that NDPS can uniformly distribute the cations in resulting perovskite films, removing the A₂BX₄ phase in the final films. It is reported that the O atom in tetrabutylammonium anion ([TFSI]) can act as a monodentate and bidentate ligand to form dative bonds with alkali metal ions, though chelation effect46,47. This prompted us to compare the ability of NDPS and [TFSI] to interact with Cs+ through DFT calculations to reveal the intrinsic mechanism of NDPS in inhibiting cationic segregation. Solvent effects of DMF were incorporated using the SMD polarizable continuum model. The calculated results show that the cations (Cs⁺) form bidentate or polydentate complexes by coordinating with the oxygen atoms in both the DNSP and the [TFSI]. As indicated in the Gibbs free energy presented in Supplementary Table 4, this coordination is an exothermic process, which is thermodynamically favorable and contributes significantly to the overall energy stabilization of the system. Furthermore, our analysis reveals that the DNSP molecule exhibits a stronger coordination ability compared to [TFSI], aligning with our microstructural analysis. These results clearly indicate that the chelation effect occurs between DNSP and Cs+ in the precursor solution and can effectively disperse Cs+. In the subsequent spin coating process, NDPS, as a surfactant, can reduce the size of the crystal nucleus and control grain growth, thereby further benefiting cation homogeneity. We then proposed the possible mechanisms for the NDPS-induced IGINs chemo-elimination, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 3h. The amphiphilic nature of NDPS, featuring both negative and positive active groups (Supplementary Fig. 21), enables interactions with solute species, including the cationic clusters in the precursor solution. These interactions enhance miscibility and dispersion by forming kinetically stabilized cationic clusters with reduced sizes. The NDPS coordination also slows the overall crystallization, allowing sufficient time for cation intermixing. This prevents the formation of cation-segregated phases such as A2BX4.

Effect of intragrain impurities on photovoltaic properties

We further performed time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectroscopy on the control and target films with the sample structure of glass/ITO/ETL/perovskite, with results shown in Fig. 4a. Based on the fitted parameters (Supplementary Table 5), the average PL lifetime decreases from 61.81 to 9.79 ns, suggesting effective suppression of non-radiative recombination due to the elimination of IGINs. To further investigate the carrier dynamics, transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy was conducted on both films (Fig. 4b, c). Following photoexcitation, both bandgap renormalization and hot-carrier cooling were observed to occur within the initial 2 ps for all sample48. Notably, the target film exhibited accelerated delay kinetics with a narrower full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) in the absorption profile at 0.84 ps, measuring 35.3 nm for the control and 32.8 nm for the target samples. We further evaluated the ambient stability of both films under controlled environmental conditions (22 °C, 40% RH). After 40 days of storage, the absorbance of the target film remained nearly unchanged, indicating significantly improved environmental stability compared to the control film (Supplementary Figs. 30 and 31). The current density-voltage (J-V) characteristics of the best-performing PSCs are presented in Fig. 4d. The target device, free of IGINs, achieved an enhanced PCE of 26.03%, with a short-circuit current (Jsc) of 26.29 mA cm-2, an open-circuit voltage (Voc) of 1.206 V, and a fill factor (FF) of 0.821. In contrast, the control device exhibited a PEC of 24.50%, with a Jsc of 25.95 mA cm-2, a Voc of 1.165 V, and an FF of 0.810. A direct comparison of these parameters is provided in Supplementary Table 6. The notable enhancement in PCE is primarily attributed to the increased Voc. To further validate the improvement in device performance, we measured steady-state photocurrent outputs at the maximum power point (MPP) for both devices, also observing a faster transition from dark to illuminated conditions in the target PSC (Fig. 4e). Additionally, the target devices demonstrated better reproducibility, as supported by a statistical analysis of PCE across 30 devices (Supplementary Fig. 32). The PSCs without IGINs also demonstrated enhanced operational stability. As shown in Fig. 4f, the target device demonstrated an impressive 95.21% PCE retention stability upon 1000 h of continuous operation under one-sun-intensity illumination (ISOS-L-1l protocol). In contrast, the PCE of the control device degraded rapidly in the initial 100 h. We then compared the storage stability of the two cases. The cross-sectional STEM images in Supplementary Fig. 33 show that PbI2 can be observed in perovskite intragrain regions and grain boundaries in the pristine one after being stored in a glove box for two weeks. For comparison, no degradation products can be observed in the target one. Meanwhile, atomic-resolution imaging revealed that residual strain-free perovskite maintained the integrity of the crystal structure. Still, in the control sample, the perovskite partially degraded to PbI2 nanoparticles, confirming that the device without IGINs showed better stability and durability.

Discussion

The nontrivial existence of metastable A2BX4 impurity nanoclusters was resolved at the atomic level in the crystalline grains of nominally processed Cs-incorporated mixed-cation perovskite films. This nanophase exhibits a much larger bandgap than the pristine perovskite, blocking intragrain carrier transport and accelerating non-radiative recombination. More importantly, under environmental stresses, they are subject to facile decomposition into PbI2, serving as an internal factor affecting the chemical stability of perovskite grains. Chemo-elimination of these intragrain impurity nanoclusters can significantly improve the PCE and stability of PSCs. This work suggests an uncharted composition space in the confined soft-lattice environment of perovskite, which has a previously unappreciated effect on properties and device performance. Considering perovskite’s versatile composition and crystal dimensionality, continued atomic-level exploration will likely resolve more intragrain details, including unconventional phases and nanostructures, advancing the nano-engineering of perovskite optoelectronics.

Methods

Raw chemicals

Lead bromide (PbBr2, ≥99%), 4-tert-butylpyridine (TBP, 96%), bis(trifluoromethane) sulfonimide lithium salt (99.95%), dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%), dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO, 99.9%), chlorobenzene (CB, 99.8%), toluene (TB, 99.8%), acetonitrile (CH3CN, >99.9%), diethyl ether (DE, 99%) and ethyl acetate (EA, 99.8%) were supplied by Merck (USA). Lead iodide (PbI2, >99%) and 3-(N,N-dimethylpalmitylammonio)-propane-sulfonate inner salt (>98%) were obtained from TCI (Japan). Formamidinium bromide (FABr, >99.99%), formamidinium iodide (FAI, >99.99%), methylammonium chloride (MACl, >99.99%), methylammonium bromide (MABr, >99.99%), and methylammonium iodide (MAI, >99.99%) were sourced from Greatcell Solar (Australia). Tin (IV) oxide colloidal dispersion (15 wt.% in H2O), poly[bis(4-phenyl)(2,4,6-trimethylphenyl)amine] (PTAA, Mn 15,000-20,000), tetrakis(pentafluorophenyl)borate (TPFB, 99%), and cesium iodide (CsI, 99.998%) were provided by Alfa Aesar (USA). Spiro-OMeTAD (99.8%) was purchased from Borun Chemical Co., Ltd. (China). All chemicals were employed directly without additional purification steps.

Preparation of solutions and thin film synthesis

SnO2 nanoparticles were diluted with distilled water (v/v 1:5) to form the ETL solution. The suspension was spin-coated onto pre-patterned ITO substrates at 3000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 150 °C for 10 min.

To prepare the HTL, 72.3 mg of Spiro-OMeTAD, 28.8 μL of 4-tert-butylpyridine, and 17.5 μL of lithium bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide in acetonitrile solution (520 mg mL-1) were dissolved into 1 mL CB. A 50 μL portion of this solution was spin-coated onto the perovskite surface at 3000 rpm for 30 s. For stability assessments, PTAA served as the HTL. The PTAA solution was prepared by dissolving 30 mg PTAA and 3 mg TPFB in 1 mL CB, and 50 μL was spin-coated at 2000 rpm for 30 s. Subsequently, an 80 nm Au was deposited by thermal evaporation.

FA0.9Cs0.1PbI3, FA0.8Cs0.2PbI3, FA0.5Cs0.5PbI3, FAPbI3, MAPbI3, and CsPbI3 perovskite films were used for the fundamental investigation of intragrain impurities. For FA0.9Cs0.1PbI3, a 1 M precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 154. 8 mg FAI, 26.0 mg CsI, 461.0 mg PbI2, and 20.3 mg MACl in DMF/DMSO (v/v, 7:3). Similarly, the FA0.8Cs0.2PbI3 precursor (1 M) was obtained by dissolving 137.6 mg FAI, 52.0 mg CsI, 461.0 mg PbI2, 20.3 mg MACl in the same solvent mixture. Film deposition for both compositions followed an identical three-step spin-coating process. 40 μL of precursor was applied to SnO2-coated ITO substrates at 500 rpm for 5 s, 1000 rpm for 10 s, and 6000 rpm for 30 s. During the final stage, 400 μL of EA was rapidly drop-cast at 10 s of the second step. The resulting films were annealed at 150°C for 20 min. FA0.5Cs0.5PbI3 thin films were prepared from a 1 M precursor solution obtained by dissolving 86.0 mg FAI, 129.9 mg CsI, 461.0 mg PbI2, and 40.3 mg MACl in 1 mL DMSO. Film deposition was carried out by spin-coating at 1000 rpm for 10 s, followed by 5000 rpm for 60 s. During the second step, two aliquots of 380 μL EA were rapidly drop-cast at 20 and 40 s. The resulting films were annealed at 100°C for 2 min inside a glovebox, and subsequently at 140°C for 60 min under ambient conditions (30-40% RH). For FAPbI3 perovskite thin films, a perovskite precursor solution with 1 M concentration was obtained by dissolving 633.0 mg FAPbI3 single crystal in DMF/DMSO (v/v, 9:1). Film deposition was carried out by a spin coating process of 1000 rpm for 5 s, followed by 5000 rpm for 120 s. After 10 s of the high-speed step, 150 μL CB was rapidly dripped onto the center of the substrate. Th films were then annealed at 150 °C for 20 min. MAPbI3 thin films were prepared from a 1 M precursor solution containing 159.05 mg MAI and 461.0 mg PbI2 dissolved in DMF/DMSO (v/v, 7:3). The MAPbI3 perovskite films were fabricated at 500 rpm for 3 s, followed by a 4000 rpm 30 s process. During the second step, 200 μL of CB was drop-casted quickly at 20 s, followed by 150 °C 20 min annealing. For preparing CsPbI3 perovskite thin films, a 1 M perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 259.81 mg CsI and 461.0 mg PbI2 in DMF/DMSO (v/v, 4:6). The CsPbI3 perovskite films were fabricated at 1000 rpm for 10 s, followed by a 3000 rpm 30 s step. The films were annealed at 250 °C for 10 min after a resting period of 20 min.

For the device demonstration, ((FA0.95Cs0.05)PbI3)0.975(MAPbBr3)0.025 perovskite films were employed. The precursor solution (1.44 M) was prepared by dissolving 705.3 mg PbI2, 228.8 mg FAI, 18.2 mg CsI, 33.67 mg MACl, and 18.2 mg MAPbBr3 single crystal in the mixture of 890 µL DMF and 110 µL DMSO. The MAPbBr3 single crystal was synthesized separately by reacting MABr and PbBr2 in a 1:1 molar ratio at 0.2 M concentration in DMF, followed by crystallization using dichloromethane as anti-solvent. Films deposition was carried out by spin-coating 50 μL of precursor at 1000 rpm for 10 s and then at 4000 rpm for 30 s. During the final stage,1 mL of DE was quickly dropped onto the substrate center within 1 s in the last 20 s of the second step. The resulting films were annealed at 100 °C for 40 min. Additives, when required, were incorporated directly into the precursor solution at the corresponding concentrations.

Materials characterization

XRD and GIXRD measurements were performed using a high-resolution diffraction (D8 Advance, Bruker) equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å), employing a step size of 0.01° and a scan rate of 10° min-1. Top-view SEM images were acquired with a Helios 5CX (Thermo Fisher) operated at 5 kV. XPS analysis was conducted using an XR6 (Thermo Fisher) with monochromated Al Kα radiation and a pass energy of 20 eV. The TRPL measurements were carried out on a spectrograph (Ando Kymera 328i) with excitation from a 375 nm picosecond laser (LDH-DC-375, PicoQuant). The emitted signal was collected by a signal-photon avalanche photodiode. The TRPL spectra were analyzed by fitted to a bi-exponential delay function of f(t) = A1exp(-t/τ1) + A2exp(-t/τ2), where A1 and A2 represent the decay amplitudes, and τ1 and τ2 correspond to the decay time constants. TA measurements were performed using infrared laser pulses generated by an amplifier (Pharos, Light Conversion) with a pulse duration of 170 fs, a central wavelength of 1030 nm, and a repetition rate of 1 kHz. The pump beam was produced by directing the output into an optical parametric amplifier (Orpheus-F, Light Conversion), yielding a 640 nm pulse. Pump intensity was controlled with a neutral density filter wheel and a neutral density filter, resulting in a sample fluence of 2.31 μJ cm−2. A probe beam with 0.1 mJ of pulse energy was attenuated and focused onto an yttrium aluminum garnet (YAG) crystal to generate the supercontinuum probe spectrum. The probe was then directed onto the sample, spatially overlapping with the pump spot. Temporal delay between the pump and probe pulses was adjusted using a mechanical translation stage, and the transmitted signal was recorded with a USB spectrometer. UV-vis spectra were recorded using a commercial UV-visible spectrophotometer (Cary 300, Agilent, USA).

Solar cell fabrication and testing

ITO substrates were cleaned sequentially by ultrasonic cleaning in cleaning fluid, deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol. A SnO2 layer was then deposited by spin-coating at 3000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 150 °C for 30 min in ambient air. The resulting ETL-coated substrates was subsequently transferred into a nitrogen-filled glovebox (H2O < 0.01 ppm, O2 < 0.01 ppm). The perovskite layer was deposited as described previously, after which the HTL layer was spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, an 80-nm Au electrode was thermally evaporated onto the HTL. J-V characteristics of the PSCs were recorded using a source meter (2162, Keithley) under simulated AM 1.5 G spectrum (one-sun illumination; 100 mW cm-2) provided by an AAA-class solar simulator (Sirius-ss, Zolix) inside a nitrogen-filled glovebox. Measurements were conducted in reverse scan mode with a delay time of 10 ms. The device active area was defined as 0.069 cm2 using a shadow mask. Light intensity calibration was performed with an Oriel reference solar cell accredited by the National Institute of Standards and Technology to the ISO-17025 standard. Since the spiro-OMeTAD hole-transporting layer depends on molecular packing that can deteriorate under extended operation, it may compromise device durability49,50. To evaluate stability, PSCs were instead fabricated using PTAA for device stability tests. For light-soaking tests, unencapsulated devices were placed in a sealed quartz holder inside a N2-filled glovebox and exposed to a one-sun-intensity illumination. For the MPP tracking, PSCs were positioned in test chambers with a continuous flow of N2 gas to maintain a temperature of 50 °C. A bias at MPPs was applied on the devices and data points were collected at regular intervals following the ISOS-L-1l protocol.

Low-dose STEM characterization

Cross-sectional PSC specimens were prepared using a dual-beam focused ion beam (FIB) platform (Helios 5CX, Thermo Fisher). Sequential Pt protection layers of 30 nm (electron deposition) and 300 nm (ion deposition) were applied to the top electrode of the device. Lamella preparation was carried out with a gallium ion beam operated at 30 kV and 0.1-24 nA. To reduce ion-beam-induced damage, the surface amorphous layer was removed using a low-energy cleaning step at 1 kV and 39 pA. The prepared lamellas were subsequently transferred to a high-vacuum sputter coater (Leica ACE 600) for deposition of a protective carbon layer. Low-dose STEM was performed on an aberration-corrected microscope (Spectra 300, Thermo Fisher) equipped with a field emission gun and operated at 300 kV. The probe convergence half-angle was set to 29.9 mrad, and the HAADF-STEM detector collected electrons within a half-angle range of 57 to 200 mrad. To minimize beam-induced damage, the electron probe current was reduced to 1 pA, and the dwell time per pixel in atomic-resolution imaging was limited to 3 μs. For quasi-in situ investigations, the pixel dwell time was reduced to 2 µs. Atomic-resolution images were acquired with an average frame size of 34.5 × 34.5 nm2 at 2048 × 2048 pixels. Each image acquired in quasi-in situ experiments were recorded with a larger field of view of 100.8 × 100.8 nm2 at same pixel resolution. Under these conditions, the average dose rate was estimated to be 53 e Å-2 s-1 for atomic-resolution imaging and further reduced to 6 e Å-2 s-1 for quasi-in situ experiments. The EELS mapping was conducted in ADF-STEM mode using a Gatan Image Filter K3 camera, with a dispersion of 0.18 eV channel-1, a 5 mm entrance aperture, and a beam current of 10 pA.

Calculation methods

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were conducted using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP), with the projector-augmented wave approach employed to describe the electron-ion interactions51. Generalized gradient approximation of Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE)52 was used and a kinetic energy cutoff of 450 eV was set, with a Gaussian smearing width of 0.05 eV. The lattice parameters and atomic positions were fully relaxed until the interatomic forces were converged to a threshold of 0.02 eV Å−1. For the calculations of formation energy and density of states, Γ-centered 5 × 5 × 4 and 4 × 3 × 8 k-meshes were used for CsPbI₃ and Cs₂PbI₄, respectively. A slab model was employed for band alignment calculations of CsPbI3 and Cs2PbI4, in which a vacuum space of 15 Å was placed above the slab.
