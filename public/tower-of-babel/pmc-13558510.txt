Skin Impacts and Tradeoffs of GLP-1 Therapy: Improved Patient-Reported Outcomes of Inflammatory Skin Disease in the Era of “Ozempic Face”
Authors: Saroja Rao, Isabella Midura, Kathyana P Santiago Mangual, Leon I Igel, Katherine H Saunders, Jocelyn Carter, Jenny E Murase, Arianne Shadi Kourosh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWith the surge in use of glucagon-like peptide 1-receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) for weight management, “Ozempic Face,” one of the dermatologic effects describing facial hollowing, sinking cheeks, and skin laxity, has drawn public attention. While substantial media discourse has surrounded certain dermatologic effects, patient-reported data is needed to more accurately characterize them. This study identifies patient-reported changes in skin, hair, and nails associated with weight management treatment with GLP-1RAs, including the frequency of dermatologic effects relative to weight loss.MethodsOur study was conducted through an IRB-approved, voluntary, anonymous survey developed via collaboration between obesity medicine physicians and dermatologists. The survey was then distributed to patients of a virtual cardio–kidney–metabolic treatment program. Responses (n = 1226) were sorted by the percent body weight lost and categorized by medication regimen (glucagon-like peptide 1-receptor agonists; GLP-1RA, nonGLP-1RA, or combination therapy). Patients reported dermatologic and overall physical changes.ResultsDermatologic effects were reported more frequently with greater weight loss. Introduction

The term “Ozempic face,” characterized by sunken cheeks, sagging skin, and hollow eyes, has gained significant media attention alongside a surge in popularity of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA). Following a 700% rise in GLP-1RA prescriptions among patients without diabetes, approximately one in eight US adults report having used a GLP-1RA medication [1, 2]. Concurrent with national data indicating a rapid increase in GLP-1RA adoption, Google search volume for “Ozempic Face” nearly equaled that of “Ozempic weight loss” in 2024, revealing a prevalent association between GLP-1RA use and potential unintended dermatologic outcomes in the public’s perception [3].

Although existing literature suggests that GLP-1RAs may improve inflammatory skin conditions, contemporary social discourse highlights the potential adverse effects correlated with GLP-1RA use, including skin sagging, hair loss, facial volume loss, and reduced muscle strength. Despite growing media interest regarding aesthetic concerns linked with GLP-1RA-driven weight loss, patient-reported data on the dermatologic impacts of GLP-1RA use and large-scale studies investigating these outcomes remain limited. We sought to address this research gap and characterize key features of the public commentary concerning potential dermatologic side effects of GLP-1RAs by surveying patients on weight management regimens inclusive of GLP-1RA medications.

Methods

Survey Development and Administration

This institutional review board-approved study utilized a voluntary anonymous survey, developed from questions generated by dermatologists and obesity medicine physicians. These questions were based on recurring patient concerns identified in clinical practice and qualitative interviews with patients. Data collected included current weight management therapy, encompassing medication type, dosage and duration, along with pretreatment and current body weight. Participants responded to qualitative inquiries regarding dermatologic, muscle, and aesthetic impacts associated with weight management therapy, such as changes in preexisting skin conditions and perceived changes in skin, hair, nails, facial volume, and muscularity. The voluntary anonymous survey was composed of 19 questions. Response choices were in multiple choice format and included current weight management therapy, medication dosages, medical conditions, and more (Supplementary Material). Inclusion criteria were adults enrolled in a telemedicine program receiving medical weight management therapy. Exclusion criteria were incomplete survey responses, missing weight data, and duplicate responses.

Patients on prescribed weight management regimens from a virtual cardio–kidney–metabolic treatment program completed the survey, and responses (n = 1226) were captured in a REDCap database. No identifiable patient data were obtained. This study does not include any new investigations of human subjects or animals.

Ethical Approval

This article does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors. This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 and its later amendments. The Massachusetts General Brigham Institutional Review Board has approved this study, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient-reported outcomes among survey respondents, which were reported as percentages with confidence intervals calculated using the Wilson method. Cochran–Armitage trend tests were performed to determine if a statistically significant linear trend patient-reported adverse outcomes was present across weight loss categories, including < 10%, 10–20%, and > 20% body weight loss.

Discussion

As new GLP-1RA weight management medications redefine the landscape of cardiometabolic care, the potential dermatologic outcomes of greater weight loss achieved during weight management treatment, frequently involving GLP-1RA therapy, must be characterized. The findings of this large-scale, patient-reported survey suggest that inflammatory (e.g., psoriasis, eczema) and hormonally driven dermatoses (e.g., acanthosis nigricans, hirsutism) largely improved with weight loss achieved during GLP-1RA-based treatment regimens, while aesthetic concerns (e.g., skin sagging, hair loss, and decreased facial volume) increased with a greater magnitude of weight loss achieved.

When comparing between GLP-1RA and nonGLP1RA groups, rates of patient-reported skin sagging, hair loss, decreased facial volume, and reduced strength were higher in the GLP-1RA weight management group across all weight loss categories. However, the percentage-point discrepancy across each category did not increase uniformly as weight loss increased. With respect to skin sagging and decreased facial volume, the percentage-point discrepancy peaked in the 10–20% weight loss category (skin sagging: 32 percentage points, 47% in GLP-1RA versus 15% in nonGLP1RA; decreased facial volume: 19 percentage points, 38% in GLP-1RA versus 19% in nonGLP1RA) and narrowed in the > 20% weight loss category (skin sagging: 12 percentage points, 71% in GLP1-RA versus 59% in nonGLP1RA; decreased facial volume: 11 percentage points, 52% in GLP-1RA versus 41% in nonGLP1RA). In the 10–20% weight loss category, the skin may have a variable ability to retract, contributing to a peak in the percentage-point discrepancy. In the > 20% weight loss category, the skin may have less ability to retract among both GLP-1RA and nonGLP1RA weight management groups, explaining the narrowing of the percentage-point discrepancy in that weight loss category. The relative impact of GLP-1RA use on patient-reported skin sagging and facial volume loss may decrease with more substantial weight loss, with less of an impact from treatment modality.

Aesthetic concerns may be proactively addressed through holistic, interdisciplinary care. For example, identification of potential adverse dermatologic effects linked to GLP-1RAs allows for early clinical implementation of preventative skincare, hair growth, and muscle-building regimens, and potentially also facial and body skin tightening and contouring strategies to effectively support patients navigating GLP-1RA treatment regimens.

Skin sagging, particularly of the arms, abdomen, and thighs, was the most frequently reported concern among survey respondents (37.7%). Rapid weight loss depletes elastin, collagen, and subcutaneous fat, limiting the skin’s adaptive capacity to retract and conform to a new body shape [4, 5]. Increased skin laxity manifesting as folds and wrinkles may detract from the aesthetic benefits of weight loss and contribute to functional challenges that reduce overall patient satisfaction. Nonsurgical treatments, such as radiofrequency (RF), high‐intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU), fractional lasers, and fractional RF microneedling, may be utilized to promote skin elasticity and structural renewal by stimulating collagen and elastin protein synthesis [6–8].

Facial volume loss was the second most frequently reported concern among survey respondents (29.2%). Depletion of subcutaneous fat most commonly impacts the midface, temples, and periorbital region, contributing to a hollowed, aged appearance [5]. In addition, previous research suggests that GLP-1RAs reduce production of protective cytokines, increase oxidative stress, and promote adipose-derived stem cell death [9]. Dermatologic treatments, including dermal fillers and biostimulatory agents (e.g., poly-L-lactic acid (PLLA) and calcium hydroxylapatite), may prevent progressive facial volume loss by promoting natural collagen production [10].

Hair loss was reported nearly as frequently as facial volume loss (28.9%) among survey respondents. While there is evidence to suggest that GLP-1RAs may improve scalp perfusion and mitigate inflammatory fibrosis in central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia, other literature hypothesizes that GLP-1RA use may disrupt the follicular cycle, contributing to the development of telogen effluvium (TE) [11, 12]. Nutritional deficiencies that arise from caloric restriction during weight management treatment may further accelerate hair loss. TE may be a transient condition, especially with adequate nutritional support, as seen in patients in other scenarios of rapid or significant weight loss such as bariatric surgery [13]. In addition, GLP-1RA medications may be helpful in settings where scalp inflammation and hormonal imbalances contribute to alopecia [14]. Although alopecia often improves in patients with TE following nutritional recovery and weight stabilization, research among GLP-1RA users is limited. Strategies to stimulate hair growth include protein and micronutrient optimization, topical or oral minoxidil utilization, 5a-reductase and androgen receptor blockade in patients with androgenetic alopecia, and platelet-rich-plasma (PRP) therapy [15–17].

Approximately one quarter of survey respondents (24.1%) reported worsened skin quality, including skin dryness, wrinkles, and/or fine lines. Reduced protein and micronutrient intake resulting from GLP-1RA-driven hunger suppression may contribute to these effects. Dehydration and micronutrient deficiencies, such as the depletion of water-soluble vitamins including vitamin C and copper, can compound to impair collagen synthesis, barrier function, and elasticity [18–20]. Strategies to preserve skin quality include micronutrient optimization, retinoid application, antioxidant utilization, and microdepot hyaluronic acid injections (FDA-approved to enhance skin smoothness) as well as energy-based procedures, such as lasers, microneedling, and radiofrequency [21]. Optimizing adequate hydration helps prevent dehydrated skin, which is a temporary lack of moisture leading to tightness, flaking, and a dull appearance of the skin. However, hydration alone is not a sufficient strategy to manage dry skin, which primarily lacks oil, not water. A combined approach composed of internal hydration alongside topical interventions such as humectants and moisturizers can address dry or dehydrated skin [22].

A notable proportion of survey respondents (17.9%) reported reduced muscle strength, particularly in the arms, thighs, and legs. Lean tissue loss may be intensified by rapid weight loss, inadequate protein intake, and insufficient resistance training [23, 24]. Acute muscle depletion exposes patients to elevated risks of sarcopenia, functional decline, and reduced longevity, as low thigh muscle strength correlates with poor survival. Optimizing nutrition and prioritizing resistance exercise are essential for maintaining muscle strength and function. These strategies may be supplemented by energy-based technologies, such as high-intensity focused electromagnetic (HIFEM) therapy, to increase muscle tone [23, 25, 26].

Weight-Loss-Mediated versus Drug-Specific Effects

This study cannot determine whether patient-reported aesthetic changes resulted from the direct pharmacologic effects of GLP-1RAs or from the rate and extent of weight loss itself. Similar findings of skin laxity, facial volume loss, and hair shedding following substantial weight loss through bariatric surgery and other nonGLP-1 weight management interventions have long been recognized. Therefore, these outcomes may primarily indicate a physiologic consequence of adipose tissue loss rather than a finding associated with GLP-1RA medications. However, improvements in inflammatory dermatoses may result directly from immunomodulatory effects of GLP-1RAs in addition to weight reduction. Further studies comparing GLP-1 and nonGLP-1 therapies among patients with comparable weight loss are necessary to differentiate between these mechanisms.

Patients with certain preexisting skin conditions, including psoriasis, acne, eczema, hidradenitis suppurativa, acanthosis nigricans, hirsutism, seborrheic dermatitis, and skin tags, reported improvement on at least one GLP-1RA medication. For example, a patient with a history of hidradenitis suppurativa for over a decade refractory to adalimumab and sekucinumab reported reduced disease flares following the initiation of semaglutide (Fig. 2). Existing literature suggests that GLP-1RAs improve inflammatory dermatoses through direct immunomodulation (e.g., inhibition of proinflammatory cytokine secretion) [27]. Adipocytes secrete proinflammatory cytokines known as adipokines that amplify systemic inflammation. Existing literature has established that weight loss and improved insulin regulation may attenuate preexisting skin conditions [28]. Owing to the antiinflammatory role of GLP-1RA medications, patients may experience dermatologic disease flares following the discontinuation of GLP-1RA treatment (Fig. 3).

A 32-year-old woman developed hidradenitis suppurativa over a decade that worsened during pregnancy when she developed gestational diabetes. Her symptoms were characterized by swelling, inflammation, and subsequent scarring with sinus tract formation in the bilateral axillae. Prior treatments included adalimumab (80 mg every other week for 16 months) and secukinumab (300 mg monthly for 8 months), with persistent disease activity. Given that our survey results suggest a positive correlation between adverse dermatologic outcomes and percentage body weight loss, further investigation of how these changes manifest, specifically whether certain effects are linked directly to GLP-1RA use versus to the extent or rate of weight loss, is imperative. Such research is especially critical considering recent genetic evidence that variation in GLP-1RA target genes contributes to interperson variability in response. A new study by Su et al. has identified a missense variant in GLP1R that is strongly associated with increased weight loss efficacy of GLP-1 medications. In addition, the study identified associations linking variation in both “GLP1R” and “GIPR” to GLP-1RA medication side effects [29]. These findings suggest that an individual’s unique genetic makeup may enhance their therapeutic response to GLP-1RAs both in terms of weight loss and side effects, laying the foundation for dermatologic precision medicine in the context of weight management treatment.

Supposing that certain dermatologic outcomes are associated with weight loss achieved with GLP-1R-inclusive treatment regimens, future research may reveal correlations with specific genetic polymorphisms. Provided that certain dermatologic effects are linked to the extent or rate of weight loss, individuals with the GLP1R missense variant discovered by Su et al. are likely to be predisposed to these effects, highlighting opportunities for precision dermatology within the weight management landscape [29]. Identifying carriers of this variant will allow physicians to predict patients likely to experience greater weight loss and thus anticipate associated dermatologic impacts, permitting clinical development of proactive, personalized approaches to care.

Although our clinically treated sample strengthens data reliability, results reflect findings from a single telemedicine practice. Moreover, the extent of the dermatologic effects identified in this study may be significantly lower than in unsupervised settings. With one in four US adults on weight management medications reporting receiving them from a source other than their primary care provider or trained specialist (e.g., online provider, medical spa, etc.) in 2024, there may be incidence of additional dermatological changes beyond those reported by patients receiving monitored care [30]. Because the survey was cross-sectional and relied on patient-reported outcomes, causality cannot be established. This study design does not allow distinction between effects attributable to GLP-1RAs themselves and effects attributable to the degree or rapidity of weight loss. Other limitations of this study include its voluntary survey design, potential recall bias, and a study population largely consisting of white, female telemedicine patients, which may restrict the scope of application. Furthermore, data collected did not include the rate of weight loss, leaving its impact on dermatologic outcomes undetermined. These findings highlight the need for future dermatologist-assessed studies to further understand the patient experience and clarify the incidence, mechanisms, and long-term dermatologic effects associated with GLP-1RA therapy.

Conclusions

Precision medicine is an innovative approach to healthcare that tailors prevention, diagnosis, and treatment for patients based on their unique genetic makeup, environment, and lifestyle. Recent genetic evidence suggesting that variation in GLP-1RA target genes contributes to interperson variability in response regarding both weight loss and side effects, together with patient-reported dermatologic outcomes (stratified by percent body weight loss since treatment initiation and categorized by weight management regimen) identified in our study, establish the groundwork for precision dermatology within the context of weight management. By identifying patients that may be predisposed to certain dermatologic effects through genetic analysis, physicians may develop tailored treatment approaches that optimize patient outcomes while preventing unwanted results from the beginning of weight management treatment. Precision medicine can thus permit early implementation of personalized interventions and strategies for patients who may be genetically susceptible to adverse dermatologic effects.

This study highlights the importance of collaborative, interdisciplinary care between obesity medicine physicians and dermatologists within the weight management landscape. For patients with inflammatory skin conditions in addition to obesity or diabetes, clinically coordinated GLP-1RA treatment can be utilized to achieve both health improvements and dermatologic improvement. Furthermore, dermatologic guidance is essential to address adverse aesthetic and dermatologic outcomes associated with GLP-1RA therapy. Precision dermatology encourages early implementation of a tailored, holistic treatment approach that supports skin and hair health as well as facial volume and muscle strength in patients on GLP-1RAs, especially among those who may be genetically predisposed to unwanted dermatologic effects.

Combining patient-reported dermatologic outcomes with genetic evidence linked to GLP-1RA response variability informs personalized medicine at the intersection of dermatology and clinical weight management. This interdisciplinary work contributes to the growing body of knowledge around precision medicine and supports the development of proactive, personalized treatment approaches to optimize holistic patient outcomes. Further investigation of the mechanisms responsible for dermatologic effects linked to GLP-1RA therapy is imperative for future refinement of GLP-1RA-associated precision dermatology.
