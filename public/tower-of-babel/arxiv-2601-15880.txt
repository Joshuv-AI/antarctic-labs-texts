A two-sample pseudo-observation-based regression approach for the relative treatment effect
Dennis Dobler, Alina Schenk, Matthias Schmid

Abstract
The relative treatment effect is an effect measure for the order of two sample-specific outcome variables. It has the interpretation of a probability and also a connection to the area under the ROC curve. In the literature it has been considered for both ordinal or right-censored time-to-event outcomes. For both cases, the present paper introduces a distribution-free regression model that relates the relative treatment effect to a linear combination of covariates. To fit the model, we develop a pseudo-observation-based procedure yielding consistent and asymptotically normal coefficient estimates. In addition, we propose bootstrap-based hypothesis tests to infer the effects of the covariates on the relative treatment effect. A simulation study compares the novel method to Cox regression, demonstrating that the proposed hypothesis tests have high power and keep up with the z-test of the Cox model even in scenarios where the latter is specified correctly. The new methods are used to re-analyze data from the SUCCESS-A trial for progression-free survival of breast cancer patients.



Keywords:

area under the curve; bootstrap; central limit theorem; generalized linear regression model; pseudo-observations; survival analysis; U-statistics.

1
Introduction

The two-sample problem has a long-standing tradition in biostatistical research.
There exist numerous methods for testing the difference between two distributions or aspects thereof, including the two-sample
t
-test, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Mann-Whitney
U
test, (multiple) linear regression, and logistic regression.
In the context of time-to-event data, a large number of approaches for comparing two survival functions is available as well: For example, as an extension of the more classical log-rank test, the (weighted) log-rank test
(
14
)
, accelerated failure time models, or the Cox proportional hazards model
(
11
)
could be used.
Specifically, in spite of potentially suboptimal power, the log-rank test (and weighted alternatives thereof) remains the predominant method for analyzing treatment effects in randomized controlled clinical trials
(
34
)
. If one treats the treatment group indicator as a covariate, it is also possible to employ flexible approaches involving inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting
(
4
)
or the jackknife-based pseudo-observation approach for regression
(
3
)
;
here the idea is to solve an estimating equation based on re-weighted individual observations and pseudo-observations, respectively, to find an estimator of the parameter quantifying the group effect.

In this paper, we take a somewhat different approach: We focus on a statistical estimand with an appealing interpretation that intrinsically incorporates the two-sample problem. More specifically, we analyze the
relative treatment effect
,
\theta=P(T_{1}>T_{2})
, where
T_{1},T_{2}
describe the event times of two random and independent individuals from sample groups
j=1,2
. Thus, under the null hypothesis of no group differences, it holds that
\theta=0.5
if the distribution functions are continuous. As shown, e.g., by
15
, approaches based on (variants of)
\theta
offer easy-to-interpret alternatives to log-rank tests in two-sample comparisons. Building on this methodology, we propose a jackknife-based pseudo-observation technique that enables adjusting
\theta
for covariates, thereby allowing for the analysis of both randomized and observational data within a unified framework.

Motivation of the proposed method.
The development of the proposed method has been motivated by our re-analysis of the multicenter randomized phase III SUCCESS-A study (
12
;
36
). Between 2005 and 2007, SUCCESS-A enrolled a total of 3,754 female patients with primary invasive breast cancer, assigning study participants randomly to one of two treatment arms (control: standard adjuvant chemotherapy, intervention: standard adjuvant chemotherapy with the addition of gemcitabine). The primary outcome of the study was disease-free survival (DFS), defined as the period from the date of randomization to the earliest date of disease progression (distant metastases, local
and contra local recurrence, and secondary primary tumors) or death from any cause. For the analysis of the primary outcome,
12
used a univariate (“marginal”) Cox regression model with treatment as the only covariate. This model did not show any evidence for improved DFS when adding gemcitabine to standard chemotherapy (DFS hazard ratio = 0.93 [intervention vs. control]; 95% confidence interval [0.78–1.12]; p = 0.47). For secondary analysis, the authors used a multivariate Cox regression including main and interaction effects between treatment and covariates measured at baseline, investigating whether variables like age, body mass index and tumor stage were predictive for the effect of treatment effect DFS. Although not being part of the confirmatory analysis of the SUCCESS-A study, this model revealed several interesting patterns in subgroups of the study population (e.g., variations of treatment effects by tumor type and tumor stage).

While the Cox-based strategy by
12
constitutes an established analysis approach in clinical trial research, it naturally raises the question whether subgroup two-sample comparisons could alternatively be performed using a
distribution-free
statistical model. In particular, such a model could help assessing the probability of whether a specific patient will benefit from the intervention
without
making potentially restrictive assumptions like the proportional hazards assumption. As we will show below, our proposed model is able to address this question within a unified theoretical framework, specifying
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
as the survival times in the intervention and control groups, respectively, and adjusting the relative treatment effect
\theta
by the values of patient-specific covariates
{\boldsymbol{Z}_{1}},{\boldsymbol{Z}_{2}}
.

Related work.
Variants of the relative treatment effect
\theta
, also called
Mann-Whitney effect
, enjoy popularity in many statistical applications and are subject to extensive methodological research: In the uncensored case,
8
developed methodology for a tie-adjusted variant of
\theta
; optimal sample sizes were derived by
24
. Recently,
7
developed an unbiased variance estimator with the help of rank-based methods.
37
conducted a comparison between multiple Mann-Whitney effect-based tests, including a novel test with improved variance estimation.
A comprehensive and detailed nonparametric treatment of relative effects, including factorial designs, is given in the book by
6
.

In the context of randomly right-censored data,
18
was the first to propose an estimator for
\theta
.

20
extended the nonparametric multiple-sample comparison approach within general factorial designs
(
16
)
to dependent censoring models using the copula-graphic estimator. In addition to these works, there have been several approaches for establishing semi-parametric extensions of Mann-Whitney effects under fully observed data. For example,
5
,
41
, and
33
proposed generalized linear regression models for the area under the ROC curve (AUC) which is related to the Mann-Whitney effect. In bioinformatics,
27
and
28
proposed regularized AUC regression methods for binary and time-to-event outcomes, respectively.
Machine learning approaches to AUC maximization were discussed by
39
. A related methodological approach is the probabilistic index model
(
38
;
13
)
; however, in contrast to the method proposed here, probabilistic index models only incorporate the effect of covariate
differences

{\boldsymbol{Z}_{1}}-{\boldsymbol{Z}_{2}}
but not of the actual covariate values
{\boldsymbol{Z}_{1}},{\boldsymbol{Z}_{2}}
in groups
j=1,2
.

Objective and structure of this paper.

The present paper is the first to propose a regression approach for the relative treatment effect based on randomly right-censored and possibly tied data.
It is based on generalized linear regression models for
\theta
where the effects of the involved covariates
{\boldsymbol{Z}_{1}},{\boldsymbol{Z}_{2}}
have a natural interpretation as group-covariate interactions.
Using a two-sample jackknife pseudo-observation approach for model fitting, we prove a central limit theorem for the parameter estimators in both the uncensored and censored cases.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
Section
2.1
offers a gentle description of the novel approach when all measurements are completely observed. Section
2.2
extends the methodology to the censored event time case. Section
3
presents the results of a comprehensive simulation study that compares the proposed method to a Cox-based approach with group-covariate interactions. In Section
4
we use the proposed method to analyze DFS in the SUCCESS-A study data, demonstrating how our approach can be used to identify patients with a high probability of prolonged survival in the intervention group.
We conclude with a discussion and aspects for future research in Section
5
.
The appendix contains all proofs, additional mathematical considerations, and supplementary results from our data analyses.

2
Pseudo-Observation Regression Approach for Two-Sample Problems

2.1
Fully Observable Case

For each sample group
j=1,2
, let
(T_{ji_{j}},\boldsymbol{Z}_{ji_{j}}),i_{j}=1,\dots,n_{j}
, be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random vectors on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},P)
with
T_{11},\dots,T_{1n_{1}}\sim S_{1}
and
T_{21},\dots,T_{2n_{2}}\sim S_{2}
.
In view of the extension to censored observations in Section
2.2
below, we describe the model and method in terms of the survival functions
S_{1}(t)=P(T_{1i_{1}}>t)
and
S_{2}(t)=P(T_{2i_{2}}>t)
; alternative formulations in terms of cumulative distribution functions are straightforward.
For now, we consider the
uncensored
, i.e., fully observable case without censoring.
In fact, the event times
T_{ji_{j}}
only need to be ordinally scaled and not necessarily continuous random variables.
The
T_{ji_{j}}
’s are real-valued,
albeit not necessarily strictly positive in this section yet
; the covariates
\boldsymbol{Z}_{ji_{j}}
’s are
\mathbb{R}^{p_{j}}
-valued random variables,
p_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
, possibly including sets of dummy variables representing factor covariates. Note that the first entries of the
\boldsymbol{Z}_{ji_{j}}
’s are not necessarily all equal to 1 because the intercept term will be modeled separately.

We propose and fit a model for the conditional probability

\displaystyle\theta(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\boldsymbol{z}_{2})=P(T_{1i_{1}}>T_{2i_{2}}|\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}}=\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}=\boldsymbol{z}_{2}).

(1)

In principle, both covariate vectors could address completely different characteristics (or features) of an individual.
In most applications, however, they will refer to the same set of characteristics.
One interesting special case is
\boldsymbol{z}_{1}=\boldsymbol{z}_{2}=\boldsymbol{z}
.
For example, if both sample groups are defined by different treatment regimes,
then
\theta(\boldsymbol{z})=\theta(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z})
quantifies the probability that some individual
i_{1}
with covariate values
\boldsymbol{z}
under Treatment 1 would live longer than some individual
i_{2}
with the same covariate values
\boldsymbol{z}
under Treatment 2.
Considering this can allow for drawing causal inferences under correct model specification in the absence of other confounding.

In general, we allow both dimensions,
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, to differ.
For example, this could be useful if the first sample refers to data from a previous study with few covariates and the second sample was more recently collected, with additional covariates.
Then
\theta(\boldsymbol{z}^{(1)},(\boldsymbol{z}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{z}^{(2)}))
quantifies the relative effect of individuals for which only the covariates
\boldsymbol{z}^{(1)}
are taken into account for the first group’s event time but in addition also
\boldsymbol{z}^{(2)}
for the second group’s event time. Alternatively, such a scenario could be interpreted as a comparison with a subpopulation from group 2.

We assume that the estimand
\theta
follows a model that can be described with the help of a strictly monotone inverse link function
\mu:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and a parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{1+p_{1}+p_{2}}
, where
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}=(\beta_{0},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{\top},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}^{\top})
.
Then the model is defined as

\displaystyle\theta(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\boldsymbol{z}_{2})=\mu(\beta_{0}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}_{2}).

(2)

If needed, the model could of course be reduced:
For instance, if the value of the intercept term is known,
\beta_{0}=\check{\beta}_{0}
, the model could be reduced to
\theta(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\boldsymbol{z}_{2})=\check{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}_{2})
where
\check{\mu}(\ \cdot\ )=\mu(\check{\beta}_{0}+\ \cdot\ )
.

We assume in the following that model (
2
) describes the reality for a problem at hand, i.e. for a “true” parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{1+p_{1}+p_{2}}
not to be confused with the intercept term
\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
.

A nonparametric estimator for
\theta
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}=\hat{P}(T_{1i_{1}}>T_{2i_{2}})=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n_{2}}1\{T_{1i_{1}}>T_{2i_{2}}\}=\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n_{2}}\hat{S}_{1}(T_{2i_{2}})=1-\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\hat{S}_{2}(T_{1i_{1}}-),

(3)

where
1\{\cdot\}
denotes the indicator function of an event and
\hat{S}_{j}(t)=\frac{1}{n_{j}}\sum_{i_{j}=1}^{n_{j}}1\{T_{ji_{j}}>t\}
denotes the empirical survival function,
j=1,2
, and
\hat{S}_{2}(t-)
denotes the left-hand limit of
\hat{S}_{2}
at
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Later
\hat{S}_{j}
will be replaced by the Kaplan-Meier estimator based on randomly right-censored observations.

Motivated by Eq. (
3
), we define
two-sample pseudo-observations
by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}&:=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{S}_{1i_{1}}(u)\,d\tilde{S}_{2i_{2}}(u)\\
&:=-\int_{0}^{\infty}(n_{1}\hat{S}_{1}-(n_{1}-1)\hat{S}_{1}^{(i_{1})})(u)\,d(n_{2}\hat{S}_{2}-(n_{2}-1)\hat{S}_{2}^{(i_{2})})(u)\\
&=-n_{1}n_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\hat{S}_{1}(u)\,d\hat{S}_{2}(u)+(n_{1}-1)n_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\hat{S}_{1}^{(i_{1})}(u)\,\hat{S}_{2}(u)+n_{1}(n_{2}-1)\int_{0}^{\infty}\hat{S}_{1}(u)\,d\hat{S}_{2}^{(i_{2})}(u)\\
&\qquad-(n_{1}-1)(n_{2}-1)\int_{0}^{\infty}\hat{S}_{1}^{(i_{1})}(u)\,d\hat{S}_{2}^{(i_{2})}(u)\\
&=:n_{1}n_{2}\hat{\theta}-(n_{1}-1)n_{2}\hat{\theta}_{1}^{(i_{1})}-n_{1}(n_{2}-1)\hat{\theta}_{2}^{(i_{2})}+(n_{1}-1)(n_{2}-1)\hat{\theta}_{12}^{(i_{1}i_{2})};\end{split}

(4)

the integrals are to be understood in the Riemann-Stieltjes manner.
Here,
\hat{\theta}
is the full estimator defined in (
3
),
\hat{\theta}_{1}^{(i_{1})}
is the same estimator (with factor
((n_{1}-1)n_{2})^{-1}
but based on all random variables except the
i_{1}^{th}
from sample group
1
, similarly for
\hat{\theta}_{2}^{(i_{2})}
, and
\hat{\theta}_{12}^{(i_{1}i_{2})}
(with factor
(n_{1}-1)^{-1}(n_{2}-1)^{-1}
) omits the
i_{1}^{th}
random variable from group 1 and the
i_{2}^{th}
from group 2.
A similar notation has been used for the reduced estimators
\hat{S}_{j}^{(i_{j})},j\in\{1,2\}
.
In the present fully observable case, it is easy to see that each
\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}
reduces to
1\{T_{1i_{1}}>T_{2i_{2}}\}
, which has expectation

E(\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}})=P(T_{1i_{1}}>T_{2i_{2}})=:\theta.

Definition (
4
) of pseudo-observations is also feasible in the presence of right-censoring; see Section
2.2
below.

Remark 1
.

The definition in (
4
) seems to be in contrast to
19
who stated that not pairs of observations should be left out but rather all observations of both samples consecutively, without such pairings.
Efron’s arguments are based on two related papers
(
29
;
25
)
which both considered linear regression problems.
Some authors indeed apply the one-sample jackknife to both samples consecutively, also in censored data problems; see e.g.
9
.
On the other hand,
35
proposed the two-sample jackknife as applied in (
4
).

10
applied this type of two-sample jackknife method to the Jaccard index.

In order to relate
\theta
to the covariate values
\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}},\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}
in accordance with model (
2
), we fit a regression model by solving the following generalized estimating equation (GEE),
similar to the approach by
33
for completely observable outcomes
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n_{2}}\boldsymbol{A}_{i_{1}i_{2}}(\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}-\mu_{i_{1}i_{2}})=\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}}\boldsymbol{U}_{i_{1}i_{2}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\boldsymbol{0},

(5)

where

\boldsymbol{A}_{i_{1}i_{2}}=A(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}},\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}})=(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mu_{i_{1}i_{2}}^{\top})V_{i_{1}i_{2}}^{-1}=(1,\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}}^{\top},\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}^{\top})^{\top}\,\mu^{\prime}((1,\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}}^{\top},\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}^{\top})\boldsymbol{\beta})\,V_{i_{1}i_{2}}^{-1},

V_{i_{1}i_{2}}
is a working (co)variance (matrix) for
\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}
, and

\mu_{i_{1}i_{2}}=\mu(\beta_{0}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}).

This approach is motivated by the one-sample-based equation by
3
; see their Equation (3
\cdot
1).
In the present case, the estimand
\theta
is univariate, so
V_{i_{1}i_{2}}
can be set equal to 1.

Note that Condition 2 in
3
postulates the independence of the terms
\boldsymbol{U}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
contributing to their estimating equation.
They also argue that the pseudo-observations (in the one-sample case) are asymptotically independent.
While this is certainly so, the correlation structure between the pseudo-observations still plays a significant role in the asymptotic behavior of the solution
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
of (
5
); we refer to
30
who showed that the mentioned correlation structure affects the asymptotic covariance matrix of the normalized parameter estimator in the one-sample and randomly right-censored case.

In our two-sample case, the correlation of the pseudo-observations will not even diminish asymptotically:

\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}
and
\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}\tilde{i}_{2}}
as well as
\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}
and
\tilde{\theta}_{\tilde{i}_{1}i_{2}}
are generally dependent by construction, even for
i_{1}\neq\tilde{i}_{1}
and
i_{2}\neq\tilde{i}_{2}
, respectively.
However, it remains to investigate the asymptotic rate of dependence of
\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}
and
\tilde{\theta}_{\tilde{i}_{1}\tilde{i}_{2}}
for
i_{1}\neq\tilde{i}_{1}
and
i_{2}\neq\tilde{i}_{2}
. The special case of completely observable data considered in this section reveals finite sample independence; in the general case under censoring this will no longer hold.

Example 1

(Identity link function)
.

In the case of the univariate identity link function, i.e.
\mu(x)=x
,
we have

A(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\boldsymbol{z}_{2})=(1,\boldsymbol{z}_{1}^{\top},\boldsymbol{z}_{2}^{\top}).

Hence the GEE (
5
) takes the form

\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n_{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}}\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}\end{pmatrix}\Big(\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}-\beta_{0}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}\Big)=\boldsymbol{0},

which is solved by

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}:=\Big(\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n_{2}}\Big(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}}\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}\end{smallmatrix}\Big)\Big(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}}\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}\end{smallmatrix}\Big)^{\top}\Big)^{-1}\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n_{2}}\Big(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}}\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}\end{smallmatrix}\Big)\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}.

Here, the inverse
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}
is to be understood as the generalized Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
.
If the second moments of all covariates exist,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
converges almost surely to

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}:=E\Bigg(\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}}\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}}\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\Bigg)=\begin{pmatrix}1&\boldsymbol{E}_{1}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{E}_{2}^{\top}\\
\boldsymbol{E}_{1}&\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}+\boldsymbol{E}_{1}\boldsymbol{E}_{1}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{E}_{1}\boldsymbol{E}_{2}^{\top}\\
\boldsymbol{E}_{2}&\boldsymbol{E}_{2}\boldsymbol{E}_{1}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}+\boldsymbol{E}_{2}\boldsymbol{E}_{2}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}

with the expectation vectors
\boldsymbol{E}_{j}=E(\boldsymbol{Z}_{ji_{j}})\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{j}}
and variance-covariance matrices
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j}=var(\boldsymbol{Z}_{ji_{j}})\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{j}\times p_{j}}
,
j=1,2
.
It is easy to see that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
has full rank
p=1+p_{1}+p_{2}
whenever
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}
have full ranks
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, respectively:
First, being the limit of a sum of positive semi-definite matrices,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is positive semi-definite as well; second, for all
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{0},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{\top},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{1+p_{1}+p_{2}}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
, and using the stochastic independence of
\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}E\Big(\Big(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}}\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}\end{smallmatrix}\Big)\Big(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}}\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}\end{smallmatrix}\Big)^{\top}\Big)\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\Big[var\Big(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}}\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}\end{smallmatrix}\Big)+\Big(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
\boldsymbol{E}_{1}\\
\boldsymbol{E}_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\Big)\Big(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
\boldsymbol{E}_{1}\\
\boldsymbol{E}_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\Big)^{\top}\Big]\boldsymbol{x}=var\Big(\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\Big(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}}\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}\end{smallmatrix}\Big)\Big)+\big((1,\boldsymbol{E}_{1}^{\top},\boldsymbol{E}_{2}^{\top})\boldsymbol{x}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq var(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}})+var(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}})=\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}\boldsymbol{x}_{2}>0

if
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}
have full ranks. Consequently, in such a case,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is positive definite and the probability that the conventional inverse of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
exists tends to one with increasing sample sizes.

At present, i.e., in the fully observable case, the parameter estimator reduces to

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\Big(\hat{\theta}\ ,\ \frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}\bullet}\ ,\ \frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n_{2}}\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{\bullet i_{2}}\Big)^{\top}

(6)

since
\hat{\theta}=\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n_{2}}\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}
.
Here we used the definitions

\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}\bullet}:=\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n_{2}}\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}=\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n_{2}}1\{T_{1i_{1}}>T_{2i_{2}}\}=1-\hat{S}_{2}(T_{1i_{1}}-),

\tilde{\theta}_{\bullet i_{2}}:=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}1\{T_{1i_{1}}>T_{2i_{2}}\}=\hat{S}_{1}(T_{2i_{2}}).

To analyze the asymptotic properties of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, we define

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{(1)}\ :=\

\displaystyle cov\Big(\Big(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}}\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}\end{smallmatrix}\Big)1\{T_{1i_{1}}>T_{2i_{2}}\},\Big(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}}\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{2\tilde{i}_{2}}\end{smallmatrix}\Big)1\{T_{1i_{1}}>T_{2\tilde{i}_{2}}\}\Big),\quad i_{2}\neq\tilde{i}_{2},

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{(2)}\ :=\

\displaystyle cov\Big(\Big(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}}\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}\end{smallmatrix}\Big)1\{T_{1i_{1}}>T_{2i_{2}}\},\Big(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{1\tilde{i}_{1}}\\
\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}\end{smallmatrix}\Big)1\{T_{1\tilde{i}_{1}}>T_{2i_{2}}\}\Big),\quad i_{1}\neq\tilde{i}_{1}.

Assuming that
n_{1}/(n_{1}+n_{2})\to\lambda\in(0,1)
as
\min(n_{1},n_{2})\to\infty
, that
E(\boldsymbol{Z}_{ji_{j}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{ji_{j}})<\infty
,
j=1,2
, and that both
\boldsymbol{\Omega}:=(1-\lambda)\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{(1)}+\lambda\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{(2)}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
have full rank
p

then, as
\min(n_{1},n_{2})\to\infty
,

\sqrt{\frac{n_{1}n_{2}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}}\,(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\beta})=\sqrt{\frac{n_{1}n_{2}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}}\,(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Psi})\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\,\mathcal{N}_{p}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{(}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1})^{\top}).

The proof of this central limit theorem is given in Appendix
A
.

In practice, this result could be used to obtain confidence regions for
\boldsymbol{\beta}
after employing an estimator of the asymptotic variance-covariance matrix.
A consistent estimator of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
was suggested above; a consistent estimator of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
is proposed in Appendix
A
.

2.2
The Censored Case

In this section, we assume that the random variables of interest,
T_{ji_{j}}
, are
randomly right-censored.

That is, the data are presented by sample-wise i.i.d. random variables

(X_{11},\delta_{11},\boldsymbol{Z}_{11}),\dots,(X_{1n_{1}},\delta_{1n_{1}},\boldsymbol{Z}_{1n_{1}})\qquad\text{and}\qquad(X_{21},\delta_{21},\boldsymbol{Z}_{21}),\dots,(X_{2n_{2}},\delta_{2n_{2}},\boldsymbol{Z}_{2n_{2}}),

where
X_{ji_{j}}=\min(T_{ji_{j}},C_{ji_{j}})
and
\delta_{ji_{j}}=1\{T_{ji_{j}}\leq C_{ji_{j}}\}
denote the observed (possibly right-censored) event times and the status indicators, respectively, for individual
i_{j}\in\{1,\dots,n_{j}\}
in group
j=1,2
; the censoring times
C_{ji_{j}}
are assumed to be stochastically independent of
(T_{ji_{j}},\boldsymbol{Z}_{ji_{j}})
.
Note that this assumption is stronger than the assumption of
independent right-censoring
(
1
, typically used in Cox regression), but it is commonly required for mathematical analyses of pseudo-observation-based regression techniques; cf.
23
and
30
.
It can be relaxed to covariate-dependent right-censoring if the employed nonparametric estimator admits an inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting structure and if the covariate’s support is finite; see
31
.

We define the Kaplan-Meier estimator for group
j
’s survival function
S_{j},j=1,2,
by

\hat{S}_{j}(t)=\prod_{i_{j}:X_{j(i_{j})}\leq t}\Big(1-\frac{\sum_{\ell_{j}=1}^{n_{j}}\delta_{j\ell_{j}}1\{X_{j\ell_{j}}=X_{j(i_{j})}\}}{\sum_{\ell_{j}=1}^{n_{j}}1\{X_{j\ell_{j}}\geq X_{j(i_{j})}\}}\Big),

where
X_{j(1)}<\dots<X_{j(m_{j})}
,
m_{j}\leq n_{j}
, denote the ordered, distinct, uncensored event times.

Remark 2
.

In practice, longitudinal studies typically end after a finite time horizon, say
\tau>0
, before all events could have occurred.
This renders the right tail of the survival functions unidentifiable.
As a solution, there are mathematical approaches that allow the survival and censoring distributions to have the same (finite or infinite) support under stricter assumptions on the censoring distribution
(
22
;
40
;
17
)
.

One solution for the presently considered estimand
\theta
is to instead use
\hat{\theta}(\tau)=-\int_{(0,\tau)}\hat{S}_{1}(u)d\hat{S}_{2}(u)
which is a consistent estimator for
\theta(\tau)=P(\min(T_{1i_{1}},\tau)>\min(T_{2i_{2}},\tau))
;
we will consider this estimand and its estimator in the remainder of this paper.
This is analogous to the Cox model where a time horizon
\tau
should be chosen before computing the maximizer of the partial likelihood.

The estimators, their jackknife variants, and pseudo-observations
\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}
can be defined in just the same way as in the uncensored case in (
4
).
Also, in the case of the identity link function
\mu(x)=x
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
still exhibits the same structure as in (
6
), except that the first entry in the vector,
\hat{\theta}
, has to be replaced with

\tilde{\theta}_{\bullet\bullet}:=\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n_{2}}\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}.

Let us now state the GEE for defining the estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
; this GEE takes exactly the same form as (
5
) and is therefore not restated here, but it is now based on the more general pseudo-observations resulting from right-censored data sets.

We are going to analyze the large sample properties of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
as a consequence of some other convergences involving the pseudo-observations.
Before stating the central assumption and consecutively the main result (Assumption
1
and Theorem
1
below), we introduce the notation
\boldsymbol{Z}_{i_{1}i_{2}}:=(1,\boldsymbol{Z}_{1i_{1}}^{\top},\boldsymbol{Z}_{2i_{2}}^{\top})^{\top}
, which is a
p
-dimensional random vector.
All convergences are to be understood as
\min(n_{1},n_{2})\to\infty
.

Assumption 1
.

(1.1)

The function
\mu:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is strictly increasing and it is three times continuously differentiable;

(1.2)

for each
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
there exists the limit in probability of

C(\boldsymbol{\beta}):=\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n_{2}}(\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}-\tfrac{1}{2}\mu_{i_{1}i_{2}})\mu_{i_{1}i_{2}},

denoted by
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
, with
f:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
being twice continuously differentiable;

(1.3)

for each
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
there exists the limit in probability of

\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\nabla C(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n_{2}}\boldsymbol{A}_{i_{1}i_{2}}(\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}-\mu_{i_{1}i_{2}}),

denoted by
\nabla f
,
and a unique root
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
of
\nabla f
, i.e.,
\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})=\boldsymbol{0}
;

(1.4)

there exists a neighborhood
B
of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
such that, for each
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in B
, the Jacobian

D\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n_{2}}\Big(\boldsymbol{Z}_{i_{1}i_{2}}\boldsymbol{Z}_{i_{1}i_{2}}^{\top}\mu^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{i_{1}i_{2}})(\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}-\mu_{i_{1}i_{2}})-\boldsymbol{A}_{i_{1}i_{2}}\boldsymbol{A}_{i_{1}i_{2}}^{\top}\Big)

converges in probability to some
(p\times p)
-matrix
-\check{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
,
which is negative definite in
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
and continuous in
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
; further assume that the convergence in probability is uniform over
B
;

(1.5)

\sqrt{\frac{n_{1}n_{2}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}}\,\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\boldsymbol{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(\boldsymbol{0},\check{\boldsymbol{\Omega}})
for some
(p\times p)
-matrix
\check{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
n_{1},n_{2}\to\infty
such that
\frac{n_{1}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}\to\lambda\in(0,1)
.
Under Assumption
1
, the solution
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
of (
5
)

(i)

exists with a probability tending to 1,

(ii)

converges to
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
in probability, and

(iii)

satisfies

\sqrt{\frac{n_{1}n_{2}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}}\,(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\,\check{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})\boldsymbol{Z}\,\sim\,\mathcal{N}_{p}(\boldsymbol{0},(\check{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}))^{-1}\check{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}((\check{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}))^{-1})^{\top}).

A formal proof of Theorem
1
is given in Appendix
A
.

Remark 3
.

In the special case of fully observable data, i.e.,
\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}=1\{T_{1i_{1}}>T_{2i_{2}}\}
, it is fairly easy to see that Assumptions
1
.2–
1
.5 are satisfied;
to this end, let us assume that
\mu
is three times continuously differentiable and that the second moments of
\boldsymbol{A}_{i_{1}i_{2}}
and
\boldsymbol{A}_{i_{1}i_{2}}(\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}-\mu_{i_{1}i_{2}})
exist for all
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
The postulated assumptions are then consequences of laws of large numbers and a central limit theorem for two-sample
U
-statistics.

For example, after noticing that
E(\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}|\boldsymbol{Z}_{i_{1}},\boldsymbol{Z}_{i_{2}})=\mu_{i_{1}i_{2}}
,
the Jacobian
D\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})
reduces to

-\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n_{2}}\boldsymbol{A}_{i_{1}i_{2}}\boldsymbol{A}_{i_{1}i_{2}}^{\top}+o_{p}(1),

which converges in probability to
-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})=-E(\boldsymbol{A}_{i_{1}i_{2}}\boldsymbol{A}_{i_{1}i_{2}}^{\top})
by the (strong) law of large numbers for multi-sample
U
-statistics, e.g., Theorem 3.2.1 in
26
.
It remains to assume that
-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})
does not have a zero eigenvalue.

Similarly, the other convergence aspects in Assumption
1
.2–
1
.4 are seen to hold by the law of large numbers.
The local uniformity of the convergence in Assumption
1
.4 follows from the continuous differentiability of
D\boldsymbol{U}
in
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
and the pointwise convergence at each
\boldsymbol{\beta}
.
The existence of the stationary point
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
of the limiting function
f
is ensured by the unbiasedness of
\boldsymbol{U}
at the true
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
, and the law of large numbers from which it follows that
\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\to}}\boldsymbol{0}=E(\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}))=\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})
.

Finally, the convergence in distribution in Assumption
1
.5 follows from a central limit theorem for
U
-statistics; cf. Theorem 4.5.1 in
26
.

Remark 4
.

Based on Assumption
1
and the consistency of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
for
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
, a consistent estimator for
-\check{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})
is given by

\widehat{D\boldsymbol{U}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}):=\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n_{2}}\Big(\boldsymbol{Z}_{i_{1}i_{2}}\boldsymbol{Z}_{i_{1}i_{2}}^{\top}\mu^{\prime\prime}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{i_{1}i_{2}})(\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}i_{2}}-\hat{\mu}_{i_{1}i_{2}})-\hat{\boldsymbol{A}}_{i_{1}i_{2}}\hat{\boldsymbol{A}}_{i_{1}i_{2}}^{\top}\Big),

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{A}}_{i_{1}i_{2}}
and
\hat{\mu}_{i_{1}i_{2}}
are defined in the same way as
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{i_{1}i_{2}}
and
{\mu}_{i_{1}i_{2}}
, respectively, with
\boldsymbol{\beta}
replaced by
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
.
As in
30
, we expect the limiting distribution to depend on a second derivative of the functional which produces the estimators
\hat{\theta}
based on the nonparametric estimators
\hat{S}_{1},\hat{S}_{2}
.
For this reason, we introduced another notation for
\check{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
, to distinguish it from
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
in the uncensored case. In contrast to
\check{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
does not depend on such second derivative.
For practical applications, we propose to use a bootstrap procedure to avoid the explicit implementation of an estimator of
\check{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
; we will pursue this approach in the simulation study in Section
3
below.
In the uncensored case, it is fairly straightforward to estimate
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
by an appropriate empirical estimator, as explained in Appendix
A
.

Simulation Study

3.1
Description of the Simulation Settings

We conducted an extensive simulation study to evaluate the small-sample properties of the proposed methods.
In particular, we focused on the small-sample values of the estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(\hat{\beta}_{0},\hat{\beta}_{11},\ldots,\hat{\beta}_{1p_{1}},\hat{\beta}_{21},\ldots,\hat{\beta}_{2p_{2}})^{\top}
and the power of the hypothesis tests for “
H_{0}^{(1)}:\beta_{11}=0
vs.
H_{1}^{(1)}:\beta_{11}\neq 0
” and “
H_{0}^{(2)}:\beta_{21}=0
vs.
H_{1}^{(2)}:\beta_{21}\neq 0
”. The latter tests (conducted at the 5% significance level) investigated the influence of the first covariate on the relative treatment effect. Throughout the simulation study, we used the identity link function
\mu(x)=x
and considered the unrestricted relative treatment effect, setting
\tau=\infty
.

The data-generating processes for the event and censoring times were defined as follows: For the
event times, we specified Weibull distributions with survival functions
S_{j}(t|\boldsymbol{Z}_{ji})=\exp(-(t/\lambda_{ji})^{k_{j}})
,
j=1,2
, with scale parameters
\lambda_{ji}=\exp(\gamma_{j0}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{ji})
and shape parameters
k_{j}>0
.
The true parameter values
\gamma_{0},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{2}
are specified in Table
1
. We address the relation between
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{k}
,
k=0,1,2
, and in which cases the model is correctly specified in Appendix
B
. For the shape parameters, we considered two specifications: In the
Setting I
, we specified
k_{1}=2
and
k_{2}=3
, resulting in hazard rates crossing at
t=2/3\cdot\lambda_{2i}^{3}\lambda_{1i}^{-2}
. As an example, Figure
1
shows the hazard rates of the considered Weibull survival distributions for equal scale parameters
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=1
. In
Setting II
, we specified
k_{1}=k_{2}=3
, resulting in a Cox proportional hazards model. The distributions of the covariates (of dimensions
p_{1}=p_{2}\in\{2,4\}
) were defined by a set of normal and binary distributions, as specified in
Table
2
. Censoring times were sampled from uniform distributions on
[0,b_{j}]
, setting
b_{1}=10
and
b_{2}=15
. This resulted in censoring rates ranging between

8.8\%
–
16.3\%
and
5.0\%
–
8.7\%
in sample groups 1 and 2, respectively (as estimated from large data sets with 1,000,000 event and censoring times per setting and sample group). We also considered the fully observable case without censoring.

The sample sizes
(n_{1},n_{2})
were set to
(40,60)
,
(50,50)
, and
(80,50)

3.2
Description of the Hypothesis Tests

The tests for
H_{0}^{(j)}:\beta_{j1}=0
vs.
H_{1}^{(j)}:\beta_{j1}\neq 0
based on the novel pseudo-observation approach were conducted in four different ways. The first test used the empirical standard deviation of the bootstrapped parameter estimates, denoted by
\hat{\sigma}^{*}_{j,emp}
:

\varphi_{j,emp}:=1\{|\hat{\beta}_{j1}|/\hat{\sigma}^{*}_{j,emp}>z_{0.975}\},\quad j=1,2,

where
z_{0.975}
denotes the
97.5\%
-quantile of the standard normal distribution.

The second test used the inter-quartile range (IQR) of the bootstrapped parameter estimates
\hat{\beta}_{j1}^{(b)*},b=1,\dots,B
, divided by 1.349:

\varphi_{j,IQR}:=1\{|\hat{\beta}_{j1}|/\hat{\sigma}^{*}_{j,iqr}>z_{0.975}\},\quad j=1,2,

where
\hat{\sigma}^{*}_{j,IQR}=|\hat{\beta}_{j1,(75\%)}^{*}-\hat{\beta}_{j1,(25\%)}^{*}|/1.349
. By definition,
\hat{\sigma}^{*}_{j,IQR}
corresponds to an alternative estimator of the standard deviation in the context of normally distributed data.

Analogously, the third test used the median absolute deviation (MAD) of the bootstrapped parameter estimates, multiplied by 1.483:

\varphi_{j,MAD}:=1\{|\hat{\beta}_{j1}|/\hat{\sigma}^{*}_{j,MAD}>z_{0.975}\},\quad j=1,2,

where
\hat{\sigma}^{*}_{j,MAD}=\textnormal{med}(|\hat{\beta}_{j1}^{(\tilde{b})*}-\textnormal{med}(\hat{\beta}_{j1}^{(b)*}:b=1,\dots,B):\tilde{b}=1,\dots,B)|/1.483
.

The fourth test was based on the percentile method, i.e., it rejects if
\hat{\beta}_{j1}
is contained in either of the 2.5% tails of the distribution of the centered bootstrap parameter estimates, say
q^{*}_{j,2.5\%}=(\hat{\beta}_{j1}^{(b)*}-\hat{\beta}_{j1})_{(2.5\%)}
and
q^{*}_{j,97.5\%}=(\hat{\beta}_{j1}^{(b)*}-\hat{\beta}_{j1})_{(97.5\%)}
.
This leads to the test

\varphi_{j,quantile}=1\{\hat{\beta}_{j1}<q^{*}_{j,2.5\%}\}+1\{\hat{\beta}_{j1}>q^{*}_{j,97.5\%}\},\quad j=1,2.

We used the so-called
warp-speed bootstrap
to conduct the tests described above; see
21
for details. Briefly, the warp-speed bootstrap is a method that is particularly useful for accelerating extensive simulation studies. In every Monte Carlo simulation run it draws only one bootstrap sample based on which the estimators are re-calculated.
In the final step, the collection of all such bootstrapped parameter estimates, say
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}^{(b)*}
,
k=0,1,2
,
b=1,\ldots,
10,000, is used for the inferential procedure.

3.3
Comparison to the Cox Proportional Hazards Model

In a further step, we compared the performance of our method to
z
-tests obtained from Cox regression
(
11
)
. To this end, we fitted a Cox model to each of the Monte Carlo samples, using the group indicator as an additional covariate. Also, to obtain a model serving a similar purpose as the model for the relative treatment effect, we included all
p_{1}=p_{2}
interactions between the group indicator and the other
p_{1}=p_{2}
covariates.
Obviously, testing for
H_{0}^{(j)}:\beta_{j1}=0
vs.
H_{1}^{(j)}:\beta_{j2}\neq 0
with the pseudo-observation approach translates to testing for
H_{0,Cox}:\delta_{1}=0
vs.
H_{1,Cox}:\delta_{1}\neq 0
using the
z
-test in the Cox model, where
\delta_{1}
denotes the first of the treatment-covariate interaction parameters.

4
Analysis of the SUCCESS-A Study Data

We illustrate our approach using data from the multicenter randomized phase III SUCCESS-A trial (NCT02181101), which enrolled 3,754 patients with primary invasive breast cancer between September 2005 and March 2007
(
12
)
. Participants were randomized 1:1 to receive either standard chemotherapy alone (control group) or standard chemotherapy plus gemcitabine (intervention group). The primary endpoint was disease-free survival (DFS), as defined in Section
1
. Because death was included in the definition of DFS, it was not treated as a competing event in this secondary analysis. For further details on the inclusion/exclusion criteria and the design of the study, we refer to
12
.

Patients were censored at their last known disease-free date, resulting in an event rate of 12.2% (458 events among 3,754 patients). The maximum follow-up duration was 5.5 years (6 months of chemotherapy plus 5 years of follow-up), with a median follow-up of 5.2 years. Patient and tumor characteristics included age at randomization, body mass index (BMI), menopausal status, tumor stage, tumor grade, lymph node status, tumor type, HER2 status, estrogen receptor (ER) status, and progesterone receptor (PR) status. After excluding individuals with missing covariate data, the final dataset used for analysis comprised 3,652 patients.
Appendix
C
contains tables with more detailed data summaries and results from supplementary analyses.

In the original SUCCESS-A study, no overall treatment effect on disease-free survival was observed. However, exploratory analyses using a Cox regression model with treatment–covariate interaction terms suggested possible treatment effects in specific patient subgroups
(
12
)
. Hence, our objective was to explore the heterogeneity of treatment effects by identifying patient subgroups that may benefit from the intervention compared to control therapy, using a multivariable framework to account for the joint and potentially correlated effects of patient and tumor characteristics. To relax the proportional hazards assumption, we fitted a model of the form
P(\min(T_{1},\tau)>\min(T_{2},\tau)\mid\boldsymbol{Z}_{1},\boldsymbol{Z}_{2})=\mu(\beta_{0}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{2})
for DFS using the above described approach. Here, the subscripts
1
and
2
refer to the intervention and control groups, respectively. Pseudo-observations were computed as defined in Equation (
4
) and Remark 2 using
\tau=5.5
years. The effects
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}
of the patient and tumor characteristics described above were estimated according to Equation (
5
). Ninety-five percent confidence intervals were derived using the four methods described in Section
3.2
(based on
B=2,000
bootstrap replications). Analogous to the simulation study, we used the identity link function
\mu(x)=x
and considered the special case
\boldsymbol{Z}_{1}=\boldsymbol{Z}_{2}=\boldsymbol{Z}
(corresponding to equal patient and tumor characteristics in both groups). Hence, the model reduced to
P(\min(T_{1},\tau)>\min(T_{2},\tau)\mid\boldsymbol{Z})=\beta_{0}+(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2})^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}
.

In our analysis, we are interested in estimating the probability
P(\min(T_{1},\tau)>\min(T_{2},\tau)\mid\boldsymbol{Z})
. However, this probability could be very small and thus give a wrong impression of the treatment’s effectiveness when there is a substantial number of tied event times and/or when many patients are censored at
\tau
. In the SUCCESS-A data, a substantial number of ties were observed at
\tau=5.5
years (940/3652 = 25.74%).
To account for these ties, we defined and applied a
tie-corrected estimator
as follows: Let
\hat{S}_{1}(t)
and
\hat{S}_{2}(t)
denote the Kaplan-Meier survival estimates in the two groups, and
\Delta\hat{S}_{1}(t)=\hat{S}_{1}(t-)-\hat{S}_{1}(t)
,
\Delta\hat{S}_{2}(t)=\hat{S}_{2}(t-)-\hat{S}_{2}(t)
the jumps in
\hat{S}_{1}
and
\hat{S}_{2}
, respectively, at time
t
. (Note that
\Delta\hat{S}_{1}(t)
and
\Delta\hat{S}_{2}(t)
are zero if there is no uncensored event time at
t
.) To correct for ties, we estimate
0.5\cdot P(\min(T_{1},\tau)=\min(T_{2},\tau)\mid\boldsymbol{Z}=\boldsymbol{z})+P(\min(T_{1},\tau)>\min(T_{2},\tau)\mid\boldsymbol{Z}=\boldsymbol{z})
by

\displaystyle 0.5\cdot\hat{P}(\min(T_{1},\tau)=\min(T_{2},\tau)\mid\boldsymbol{Z}=\boldsymbol{z})+\hat{P}(\min(T_{1},\tau)>\min(T_{2},\tau)\mid\boldsymbol{Z}=\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle 0.5\cdot\Big[\hat{S}_{1}(\tau)\hat{S}_{2}(\tau)+\sum_{t\leq\tau}\Delta\hat{S}_{1}(t)\Delta\hat{S}_{2}(t)\Big]+(\hat{\beta}_{1}+\hat{\beta}_{2})^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}.

(7)

The rationale for the (nonparametric) correction of
(\hat{\beta}_{1}+\hat{\beta}_{2})^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}
is as follows: The sum in (
4
) captures the probability of exact ties at discrete event times
t\leq\tau
, while the product
\hat{S}_{1}(\tau)\hat{S}_{2}(\tau)
accounts for ties arising from censoring at the end of follow-up. Since it is reasonable to assign half of the probability to
T_{1}>T_{2}
and the other half to
T_{1}<T_{2}
in the presence of a tie, we multiply this term by
0.5
. Applying the tie-corrected estimator, the resulting correction term for the SUCCESS-A data was estimated as
0.3632
.

Analogous to the simulation study, we compared the proposed method to a Cox proportional hazards model with group-by-covariate interaction effects.

5
Discussion

We presented a statistical model for the two-sample problem that relates the relative treatment effect
\theta({\boldsymbol{z}_{1}},{\boldsymbol{z}_{2}})=P(T_{1}>T_{2}|{\boldsymbol{Z}_{1}}={\boldsymbol{z}_{1}},{\boldsymbol{Z}_{1}}={\boldsymbol{z}_{2}})
to group-specific covariate values
{\boldsymbol{z}_{1}},{\boldsymbol{z}_{2}}
. The proposed model is distribution-free, thereby generalizing nonparametric estimators of the unconditional relative treatment effect (like the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney statistic) to a regression setting. As a main result of this paper, we developed a model-fitting procedure based on pseudo-observations. We showed that this procedure yields a consistent and asymptotically normal estimator of the covariate effects under rather mild conditions. In the special case of uncensored observations, we explicitly verified these conditions. In future works we will investigate more detailed sufficient conditions for the validity of Assumption
1
in the randomly right-censored case.

In addition to the aforementioned model-fitting procedure, we proposed several bootstrap-based statistical hypothesis tests to infer possible covariate effects on
\theta
. Compared to other non-parametric inference procedures for the two-sample problem (like the log-rank test), our model has the advantage that it additionally yields
effect estimates
for the unconditional and conditional probabilities
P(T_{1}>T_{2})
and
P(T_{1}>T_{2}|{\boldsymbol{Z}_{1}}={\boldsymbol{z}_{1}},{\boldsymbol{Z}_{2}}={\boldsymbol{z}_{2}})
. As demonstrated by our analysis of the SUCCESS-A study data, this estimation and testing approach could be used to identify covariates that are predictive for the treatment effect, corresponding to a simultaneous analysis of subgroups in a clinical trial. Our approach further yields patient-specific probability estimates with confidence intervals, allowing for a classification scheme to identify those patients having the highest benefit from a specific treatment (cf. Section
4
). Compared to Cox regression, which, in principle, could also be used for deriving such conditional estimates, our model requires less assumptions on the relationships between the event-time outcome and the covariates. Furthermore, the patient-specific probabilities of a treatment benefit
are a
direct
result of the proposed model, whereas Cox regression requires a number of additional calculations to arrive at these probabilities. In this respect, and based on our experience from everyday work with clinicians and medical scientists, the pseudo-observation-based regression approach focuses on an estimand that is much easier to interpret than the log hazard ratios modeled by Cox regression. In our simulation study, we observed that the proposed bootstrap-based tests competed well with the
z
-tests in the Cox proportional hazards model even when the latter was specified correctly.

An attractive feature of the proposed model is its ability to deal with different sets of covariate values, and even different sets of covariates, across the two sample groups. As a result, our model allows for very flexible two-sample comparisons, e.g., when intervention and control data have been obtained from different sources (as in the case of synthetic and/or historical control arms, possibly involving different sets of covariates). In the special case where covariate values in the two groups are identical (
{\boldsymbol{z}_{1}}={\boldsymbol{z}_{2}}={\boldsymbol{z}}
), our model implicitly defines a causal estimand for a patient with baseline covariates
{\boldsymbol{z}}
and potential outcomes
T^{(1)}_{i}
and
T^{(2)}_{i}
(corresponding to the administration of the intervention and control treatments, respectively, to this patient). Although we did not use the potential outcomes framework to formulate our model, estimation of the causal contrast
P(T^{(1)}>T^{(2)})
should be possible under the usual consistency, positivity, and exchangeability conditions. For example, in the SUCCESS-A study, using a g-computation approach,
P(T^{(1)}>T^{(2)})
could be estimated by a weighted averaging the patient-specific probabilities in Figure
4
C.

From a methodological perspective, it seems possible to extend the two-sample pseudo-observation-based regression approach also to other functionals of the marginal distributions,
\theta=\phi(S_{1},S_{2})
, as long as there exists a sensible estimator,
\hat{\theta}=\phi(\hat{S}_{1},\hat{S}_{2})
.
To verify the statistical properties of the resulting estimators for such a general approach, it would be necessary to impose additional assumptions, e.g., a certain type of differentiability and that the estimand can be expressed as a conditional expectation,

E(V|\boldsymbol{Z}_{11},\boldsymbol{Z}_{21})=\mu(\beta_{0}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{11}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{21}),

see
30
for the large-sample behavior in the one-sample case.

Extensions of the proposed jackknife-based pseudo-observation regression approach to
J
-sample problems,
J>2
are more or less straightforward: This would involve a
J
-way array of pseudo-observations where each possible combination of
J
-tuples are left-out exactly once, resulting in
N:=\prod_{j=1}^{J}n_{j}
pseudo-observations in total.
The pseudo-observations related to a
J
-sample estimand
\theta
would then take the form

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}_{i_{1}\dots i_{J}}=\

\displaystyle N\cdot\Big[\hat{\theta}-\frac{n_{1}-1}{n_{1}}\hat{\theta}_{1}^{(i_{1})}-\frac{n_{2}-1}{n_{2}}\hat{\theta}_{2}^{(i_{2})}-\dots-\frac{n_{J}-1}{n_{J}}\hat{\theta}_{J}^{(i_{J})}

\displaystyle+\frac{(n_{1}-1)(n_{2}-1)}{n_{1}n_{2}}\hat{\theta}_{12}^{(i_{1}i_{2})}+\frac{(n_{1}-1)(n_{3}-1)}{n_{1}n_{3}}\hat{\theta}_{13}^{(i_{1}i_{3})}+\dots

\displaystyle-\frac{(n_{1}-1)(n_{2}-1)(n_{3}-1)}{n_{1}n_{2}n_{3}}\hat{\theta}_{123}^{(i_{1}i_{2}i_{3})}-\dots

\displaystyle+\dots+(-1)^{J}\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{J}\frac{n_{j}-1}{n_{j}}\Big)\hat{\theta}^{(i_{1}\dots i_{J})}_{1\dots J}\Big]

\displaystyle=:\

\displaystyle N\cdot\sum_{I\subset\{1,\dots,J\}}(-1)^{|I|}\Big(\prod_{k\in I}\frac{n_{k}-1}{n_{k}}\Big)\hat{\theta}_{I}^{(i_{I})},

where, in the last step, we used the obvious multi-index notation. Here,
\hat{\theta}=\hat{\theta}_{\emptyset}^{(i_{\emptyset})}
denotes the full-sample(s)-based estimator with subscripts indicating omission of the individual observations
i_{I}

from the set of sample indices
I
.

Proof of Theorem
1
:

Since
-\check{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is negative definite and continuous in
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
, it is also negative definite on the open ball
B=B_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})
of some radius
\varepsilon>0
around
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
. This is because the largest eigenvalue of
-\check{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
changes continuously with
\boldsymbol{\beta}
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
be small enough so that the largest eigenvalue of all
-\check{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in B
, is bounded away from 0, and such that the assumed uniform convergence of
D\boldsymbol{U}
to
-\check{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
in probability holds on
B
.
As a consequence, the event that the biggest eigenvalue of
D\boldsymbol{U}
is negative has a probability converging to 1 as
\min(n_{1},n_{2})\to\infty
.
Hence it is no restriction to consider all arguments in the remainder of this proof conditionally on the event

D_{n_{1}n_{2}}(\varepsilon):=\{D\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\text{ is negative definite for all }\boldsymbol{\beta}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})\}.

Using the arguments from
2
, Appendix II, the maximum of
\boldsymbol{\beta}\mapsto C(\boldsymbol{\beta})
(over the compact subset
\overline{B_{\varepsilon/2}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})}
, i.e., the closed ball) converges to the maximizing value of
C
in probability, i.e., to
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
.
Now Corollary II.2 of
2
implies that the maximizer
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
of
C
eventually lies in the open set
B_{\varepsilon/2}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})
in probability, i.e., it is stationary point of
\boldsymbol{U}
. Consequently, the probability that the root of
\boldsymbol{U}
exists tends to 1.
For these arguments we have also used that
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
is the only root of
\nabla f
and that
C
converges pointwise in probability to
f
. In combination with Corollary II.2 of
2
, it follows from
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
being the only stationary point of
f
that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
exists with a probability tending to 1.

Now, applying the mean value theorem individually to each component
U_{k}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
,
k=1,\dots,p
, of
\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
, there exist points
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}
on the line segment between
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
such that

U_{k}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})-U_{k}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})=\nabla U_{k}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k})(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}).

Denote by
\widetilde{D\boldsymbol{U}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})
the matrix consisting of the rows
\nabla U_{k}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}),k=1,\dots,p
.
Then, by the existence of the root
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
of
\boldsymbol{U}

-\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})=\widetilde{D\boldsymbol{U}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})\,(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}).

The matrix

-\check{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}

\widetilde{D\boldsymbol{U}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\to}}-\check{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})

postulated in Assumption
