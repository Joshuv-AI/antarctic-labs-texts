Preliminary investigation on the establishment of a new meibomian gland obstruction model and gene expression
Authors: Ming Sun, Huanmin Cheng, Zheng Yang, Jiangqin Tang, Shengshu Sun, Zhanglin Liu, Shaozhen Zhao, Lijie Dong, Yue Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Meibomian gland dysfunction is a chronic ocular surface disease with a complex pathogenesis, whose main clinical manifestations are meibomian gland obstruction or/and lipid abnormalities. To explore the mechanism of MGD due to meibomian gland obstruction (MGO), we established a rat model of MGO by cauterizing the meibomian gland orifice. The morphology of the lid margins and meibomian gland orifices were visualized by slit lamp. The tear production of rats was measured by phenol red cotton thread, the tear film breakup time and corneal fluorescein staining scores of rats were detected under cobalt blue light of slit lamp. Changes in the histological structure of the meibomian gland (MG) were observed by HE staining, Oil Red O staining and immunofluorescence staining (collagen IV). RNA sequencing was used to detect differentially expressed genes in MGO and normal rats, which were validated by qPCR. In the MGO group after 4, 8, and 16 weeks, the meibomian gland orifices were closed, tear film break-up time decreased and corneal fluorescein staining score increased (p < 0.05). MG acini was smaller at 8-week and 16-week MGO rats in HE staining. Oil Red O staining showed less condensed staining in the 8- and 16-week MGO groups, while more condensed staining in the 4-week MGO group. Additionally, the basement membrane was destroyed in 16-week MGO group by immunofluorescence staining of collagen IV. Meanwhile, RNA sequencing and qPCR showed that lipid peroxidation (LPO), transient receptor potential vanilloid-3 (TRPV3) and genes in PPAR signaling pathway were differentially expressed in 16-week meibomian gland obstructive rats (p < 0.05). Consequently, meibomian gland obstruction model rats were established successfully with corneal damage and lower tear film stability. Meibomian gland obstruction is a causative factor of MGD, which led to abnormal histological structure in MG, differential expression of PPAR signaling pathway and TRPV3.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73682-4.

Introduction

The meibomian glands (MGs), the largest sebaceous glands located around the eyelid, secrete lipids to form the lipid layer of the tear film, maintain tear film stability and the health of the ocular surface1,2. Abnormal lipid quality and/or quantity of meibum secreted by MGs are the immediate causes of meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD). As a chronic disease causing eye irritation symptoms and inflammation3, MGD is caused by various factors4, including eye infections, age and systemic diseases such as diabetes and hypercholesterolemia. In addition, meibomian gland obstruction (MGO) is also a common cause and clinical manifestation of MGD5,6.

PPAR signaling pathway is an important signaling mechanism in lipid synthesis and metabolism, including PPAR-α, PPAR-β/δ and PPAR-γ7. PPAR-α can regulate the expression of genes involved in lipid metabolism in liver and skeletal muscle, PPAR-β/δ can be involved in lipid oxidation in the organism and intracellularly, and PPAR-γ can promote the differentiation of adipocyte cells8.PPARs regulate the expression of downstream genes by combining with their ligand RXR, and ultimately affect lipid metabolism9. In addition, TRP channels are temperature-sensitive10and play roles in lipid metabolism11. There are six TRPs being activated by temperature including TRPV1, TRPV2, TRPV3, TRPV4, TRPM8 and TRPA1, which first five were expressed in MGs, as well as in human meibomian gland epithelial cells (hMGEC)11,12. TRPV3 is regulated by temperature and plays an important role in skin temperature sensation13. Meanwhile, TRPV3 is also regulated by lipid metabolites such as cholesterol, arachidonic acid and other unsaturated fatty acids14,15.

Animal models of MGD aimed to explore its etiology and mechanisms. Based on the fact that MGD often presents difficulty in lipid secretion, Jester et al. initially established the MGD model by inducing hyperkeratinization16, Gibard et al17. blocked the MG orifice in rabbits to induce MGD, and Dong et al. burned the ducts and orifices of MGs to establish a MGD model18. With the analysis of clinical and epidemiological studies related to MGD, it was found that systemic metabolic diseases including diabetes and hyperlipidemia were significantly correlated with MGD19,20. Thus, animal models of MGD about systemic metabolic diseases were established in recent years, which confirmed that diabetes and hyperlipidemia can lead to aberrant expression of lipid metabolism genes and pathways in MGs19,21.

Due to the absence of obstructive MGD caused by scar tissue and excessive keratinization of the eyelid, we referred to Dong et al. and chose Brown Norway rats to establish a rat model by cauterizing the meibomian gland orifice. However, they only studied inflammatory factors and ocular surface indicators including tear breakup time (TBUT), Schirmer I Test (SIT), corneal fluorescence staining scores, but did not delve into gene and pathway expression in lipid metabolism. Excluding other factors, our model explored the mechanism of MGD due to meibomian gland obstruction solely. Furthermore, since MGD is a chronic disease, a 16-week follow-up is considered for this experimental study.

Results

The meibomian gland obstruction and tear film stability

After MG obstruction rat model was established, we used slit-lamp microscopy to assess the MG orifice in rats at 4, 8, and 16 weeks in both groups. In MGO group, the MG orifice in rats was obstructed, and lipids were in the form of toothpaste; however, there was no obvious inflammation or neovascularization at the eyelid margin (Fig. 1).

The structure and function of the meibomian glands

The eyelid tissue at 4, 8, and 16 weeks in MGO group and normal group of the same age was collected for HE and ORO staining. As shown by HE staining, compared with the normal rats, MG-obstructive rats exhibited no significant difference at 4 weeks, but MG acini was smaller at 8 weeks and 16 weeks (Fig. 3a). ORO staining of the MG showed the more condensed staining at 4 weeks and less condensed staining at 8 weeks and 16 weeks (Fig. 3b). These findings indicated that MG structure and lipid metabolism were altered in MGO group.

Differential expression of the pathways and genes

To verify the abnormal lipid metabolism in MG-obstructive rats and clarify the pathogenesis of MGD due to MGO, RNA sequencing was performed. A total of 161 distinct genes exhibited differential expression between rats in two groups. It revealed the presence of 116 upregulated and 45 downregulated genes (p < 0.05) in Fig. 4a and b. The differential pathways closely related to MG function in KEGG enrichment analysis22–24 were mainly the PPAR and adipocytokine signaling pathways (Fig. 4c). The Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showed differential expression of biological processes such as cell-cell adhesion, branching involved in gland duct morphogenesis, cellular components such as the basolateral plasma membrane, cell junction, and molecular junctions, such as protein binding and protein-containing complex binding (Fig. 4d).

Discussion

The meibomian gland obstruction model

The pathogenesis of MGD is complex and involves multiple factors. Obstruction of the MG orifices and abnormal lipid are common clinical manifestations of MGD. Animal models of MGD due to diabetes and hyperlipidemia were established in recent years, revealing differential expression of lipid metabolism genes and pathways in MGs19,21, especially PPAR signaling pathway. Additionally, meibomian gland obstruction model and meibomian gland keratinization model were established, which demonstrated dry eye symptoms and MGs structural changes. Dong et al. also explored the expression of inflammatory factors, while all of them did not investigate the effects of genes/factors in lipid metabolism in MGs due to meibomian gland obstruction or lipid exclusion difficulties.

In this study, MG orifices were burned only outside using a heated and sharpened stainless steel ophthalmic cautery to form obstruction without destroy MGs in order to established a MGO model. Compared to normal rats, the MG orifices of model rats were obstructive, the corneal fluorescence staining score increased, the TBUT value decreased, but the SIT was not significantly different in MG-obstructive rats, which was consistent with the clinical symptoms of obstructive MGD25. Meanwhile, the structure of MGs changed and lipid accumulation was different from that in normal rats. RNA sequencing data also showed tight junctions among cells was abnormal, and it was found the interacinar basement membrane destroyed detected by immunofluorescence staining of collagen IV. Thus, the ocular surface manifestations and histological changes indicated that our MGO model successfully induced MGD.

Differential gene expression of the PPAR signaling pathway

PPAR signaling pathway plays a key role in the regulation of cellular metabolism, inflammation, and gene expression26,27, and PPARs are members of the ligand-activated nuclear transcription factor super-family, including PPAR-α, PPAR-β and PPAR-γ. The dimers formed by RXR and PPARs could bind to gene promoters or DNA response elements to complement and stabilize the active transcriptional complexes, ultimately regulating lipid synthesis and metabolism. In particular, previous studies have pointed out that PPAR-γ were important for adipocyte differentiation and meibomian gland function8,28,29. Meanwhile, as an important member of the RXR family, RXRg is a positive regulator of lipogenesis and promotes lipogenesis by activating the target genes of the PPAR30–33. In this study, the expression of RXRg was significantly increased, while no significant difference in PPAR-γ, leading to the differential expression of PLIN1, PLIN2, SCD1, HMGCS2 and ACSL6, which were in the downstream of PPAR signaling pathway.

Previous studies34,35reported that HMGCS2 had dual effects, acting as a co-activator to interact with PPAR-α and up-regulating transcription levels of fatty acid oxidation through nuclear translocation. HMGCS2 primarily affects fatty acid and triglyceride metabolism36, and decreased HMGCS2 enhances adipose synthesis37; meanwhile, Fang et al38. reported that decreased enzymes involved in fatty acid degradation significantly inhibited HMGCS2 expression in differentiated podocytes, suggesting that the fatty acid degradation pathway may regulate HMGCS2.

PLIN1 and PLIN2 play important roles in regulating lipid metabolism39. PLIN1 is an important regulator of lipolysis that protects lipid droplets from basal fat dissolution and maintains lipid metabolic homeostasis40. Its deficiency led to dysregulated adipose signaling, impaired adipocyte development and differentiation41. Meanwhile, knockdown of PLIN142,43in adipocytes resulted in the loss of the PLIN1 protein barrier on the surface of lipid droplets, the rate of fusion of lipid droplets slowed down, the number of small lipid droplets and the surface area of lipid droplets increased, accelerating the rate of cellular lipolysis. As a lipid droplet protein, PLIN2 is important for lipid storage in various cell types44and correlated with lipid peroxidation levels. In hepatocytes of mice fed by high-fat diet, lipid peroxidation levels increased, the expression of PLIN2 and fatty acid accumulation increased33. Additionally, the enhancement of lipid peroxidation can lead to increased LPO45, and oxidative stress is associated with lipid peroxidation. Studies in both animals and patients46,47 support oxidative stress in dry eyes, and an unbalanced antioxidant status causes inflammation and MGD in the eyelid.

SCD1 and ACSLs are downstream of PPAR signaling pathway and important for lipid synthesis and metabolism48–50. The deficiency of SCD1 up-regulated free cholesterol and changed lipid micro-environment in MGs to induce MGD51. Meanwhile, its deficiency disrupted the sebaceous and meibomian glands in mice, leading to their atrophy52. ACSL6 was responsible for lipid anabolism, and the knockdown of ACSL6 in skeletal muscle decreased triglyceride content53.

Compared with normal rats in this study, the expression of PPAR-γ was no difference in MGO rats, but RXRg was significantly differential expression, resulting in the abnormal expression of its downstream genes. These results suggested that meibomian gland obstruction led to abnormal lipid metabolism in MGs, which was supported by ORO staining.

Gene expression of the TRP channels

TRPs have been studied primarily in the context of neuronal/sensory physiology, but they also play important roles in non-neuronal cells and tissues11,54. Six genes in TRP channel have been found to produce heat or cold sensations and are found on the ocular surface: TRPV1, TRPV2, TRPV3, TRPV4, TRPM8 and TRPA155–57. However, TRPs are activated at different temperatures: TRPV1 is activated at > 42 °C, TRPV3 is activated at 33–39 °C, and TRPM8 is activated at 23–28 °C58,59.

Expressed in MGs of mouse and human, as well as in human meibomian gland epithelial cells (hMGEC)11, TRPV3 is an important temperature sensor, which accurately detects temperature differences of 1 °C60. Its reduced expression down-regulated or delayed the detection of temperature changes, thereby limiting temperature regulation. Eyelid blood flow was reduced, and conjunctival temperature was lower in patients with obstructive MGD61; TRPV3-deficientmice mostly maintain lower temperatures62. In our study, the gene expression of TRPV3 in MGO rats was lower than that in normal rats, which may indicate the lower temperature and blood flow around MGs.

TRPV3 is closely related to lipids. Nadezhdin KD and Deng Z et al. explained the critical role of lipids in TRPV3 activation from molecular structure63,64. Lipids enhanced the activity of TRPV3, such as cholesterol, arachidonic acid and other unsaturated fatty acids14,15. However, one study shown that farnesyl pyrophosphate, an intermediate in steroid hormone biosynthesis, could specifically activate TRPV3, but not other related thermosensitive TRP channels65. Thus, we guessed that the differential expression in TPRV3 was associated with the multiple effects of lipid and temperature.

Meanwhile, MGs are the largest sebaceous glands located around the eyelid. Meibomian gland epithelial cells are a crucial component of MGs. Cells adhering to the basement membrane exhibit high proliferative capacity, dividing to form new meibomian gland epithelial cells, which then differentiate to synthesize lipids and release them in a holocrine manner to maintain ocular surface health. In SZ95 sebocytes, the protein level of TRPV3decreased with differentiation66; another study also demonstrated that TRPV3plays an important role in cell proliferation67. These suggest that TRPV3 affects the lipid synthesis and metabolism of MGs by regulating the proliferation and differentiation of meibomian gland epithelial cells. Additionally, changes in temperature can also impact the fluidity of lipids and their secretion to the ocular surface.

This study was based on rats in vivo, where the temperature was within the range of TRPV3 activation while without the range of other TRPs activation. However, it is undeniable that cold treatment and hot treatment can be used to activate other TRPs to achieve the role of treating diseases, for example TRPM8-dependent impulse activity in corneal cold receptors contributes to regulating basal tear flow. MGD can also be treated by activating TRPs. It was reported that the activation of TRPV1 led to an increased lipid accumulation of hMGECs, while the activation of TRPM8 led to a decreased lipid accumulation. Activation of TRPV1 by increasing the eyelid temperature with heat compress or IPL treatment could promote lipid accumulation. Besides, activation of TRPV3 can maintain eyelid vasodilation, TRPV3 may play an important role in treating MGs, because its effects on maintaining the eyelid vasodilation accelerating the blood flow rate, restoring the normal mobility of lipids.

Laboratory animals, including rabbits, rats, and mice, have been used to study DED and MGD. Notably, no animal model has completely replicated all the clinical symptoms of MGD. Considering the MGD due to meibomian gland obstruction solely, we aimed to explore their potential mechanism in this study. Thus, because of different causes of diseases, it may be reasonable that PPAR-γ was not significantly changed, which was different from the results in previous studies of diabetes-related MGD. Additionally, the change in TRPV3 was a surprising discovery, which may provide us with new ideas for the treatment of MGD due to meibomian gland obstruction.

Conclusions

A rat model of MG obstruction was successfully established in this study. Our findings indicated that MG obstruction led to corneal damage, lower tear film stability and altered MG structure. Moreover, the mechanism of MGD due to meibomian gland obstruction was that: on the one hand, MG obstruction led to differential expression of RXRg in PPAR pathway resulting in abnormal lipid metabolism, but not PPAR-γ; on the other hand, the expression of TRPV3 down-regulated, which may affect on the development of MGD.

Materials and methods

Experimental subjects

All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Medical Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University Eye Hospital (TJYY2022122072). The experimental subjects were 48 Brown Norway (BN) rats 8 weeks old were purchased from Charles River with health reports. Using slit-lamp microscopy examination, rats with ocular surface diseases such as abnormal eyelid morphology, keratitis, conjunctivitis, and dry eye were excluded. Rats were kept in the same environment.

Experimental method

Induction of model

All rats were kept in a relatively moist (50%) environment at 24°C±2°C, with 12 h alternating light and dark cycles. Before the operation, levofloxacin (Santen, Japan) drops were administered to both eyes for 3 days, three times per day, and one drop at a time. The rats were anesthetized by an intraperitoneal injection of Xylazine Hydrochloride Injection (0.1mL /100 g; Jilin Huamu Animal Health Products CO., LTD; Changchun, China) and fixed in front of a slit-lamp microscope (Topcan, Japan). MG orifices in the upper and lower eyelid were located under slit-lamp microscopy and burned one by one for 0.5 s only outside with a heated and sharpened stainless steel ophthalmic cautery to form an MG obstruction. Every orifice was burned only once. After operation, topical ophthalmic Tobradex oculentum(Qilu Pharmaceutical, China) was administered to the rats for 3 days, twice daily to relieve inflammation in the eyelid.

Data collection

The data were collected included the morphology of the lid margin and MG orifice, Schirmer Test I (SIT), tear breakup time (TBUT), and corneal fluorescein sodium staining scores in the meibomian gland obstruction and normal groups at various time points after MG obstruction. The rats were executed by overdose anesthesia at each time point. MG tissues (including skin and lid conjunctiva) were collected for HE staining, oil red O staining and immunofluorescence staining; the skin and lid conjunctiva of the MG tissues were fully come off (Supplementary Fig. 1) for RNA sequencing and qPCR.

Meibomian gland observation

We observed the MG orifices and eyelids using slit-lamp microscopy in the MGO and normal groups every week after MG obstruction.

Schirmer test I (SIT)

During the Schirmer I test, the ocular surface of the rats was not anesthetized, and a phenol cotton thread (Jingming, Tianjin, China) was placed into the outer third of the lower conjunctiva of the rats, no more than 2 mm. After 30 s, the phenol cotton thread was removed, the wet length was measured and recorded with an accuracy of 0.5 mm. All tests were performed by the same examiner in the same environment, room temperature, humidity and light conditions. Each eye was measured three times, and the average was used as the final value. (n = 8/group)

Tear film break-up time (TBUT)

One drop of sodium fluorescein solution (2 µl, 10 mg/ml; Alcon; Geneva, Switzerland) was administered to inferior conjunctival sac of the rats, and evenly distributed on the ocular surface of the rats. The TBUT was assessed under cobalt blue light using a slit-lamp microscope. Each eye was measured three times, and the average was used as the final value. (n = 8/group)

Corneal staining and scoring

Sodium fluorescein solution (2 µl, 10 mg/ml; Alcon; Geneva, Switzerland) was dropped into inferior conjunctival sac of the rats and uniformly dispersed throughout the ocular surface. After 30 s, corneas were photographed with cobalt blue light under a slim-lamp. The corneal staining scores were as follows: 0, no punctate staining, 1 less than 30 punctate staining, 2 more than 30 punctate staining but not diffuse, 3 severe diffuse staining but not plaque-like; and 4, plaque-like staining. Each eye was measured three times. (n = 8/group)

Oil red O staining

Eyelid tissues from normal and meibomian gland obstruction rats were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) to cut into sections of 8 μm thickness. Sections were incubated with 75% alcohol for 5 s each and stained with Oil Red O staining (ORO) for 30 min using by Oil Red O solution (Baso, Zhuhai, China). After washing for 5 s with 75% alcohol and 10 min with flowing water, the sections were stained with hematoxylin dye solution for 2 min, followed by washing for 10 min with flowing water. Sections were evaluated and imaged using a microscope. (n = 6/group)

HE staining

Eyelid tissues collected from normal and meibomian gland obstructive rats were embedded in paraffin, cut into sections with 6 μm thickness, and then stored at room temperature. Paraffin sections were prepared for HE staining, and the stained sections were observed under a microscope. (n = 6/group)

RNA sequencing

One meibomian gland sample was composed of the upper and lower MG in the same rat, and RNA was isolated using a universal RNA purification kit (EZ Bioscience, Roseville, MN, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and the concentration and quality of RNA were examined using a spectrophotometer (Nanodrop 2000; Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Technical support for RNA-Seq was obtained from Wuhan Shadow Gene Technology. (n = 3/group)

The process was as follows: The concentration of the samples was determined by NanoDrop ND-1000, and 2 µg of total RNA was selected from each sample to construct RNA sequencing libraries. The libraries were enriched with oligo dT (rRNA removal) and then constructed with KAPA Stranded RNA-Seq Library Prep Kit (Roche). The quality of the libraries was characterized by Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and the libraries were quantified by qPCR. Samples were sequenced using the DNBSEQ-T7 platform and repeated three times. The sequencing quality of reads was assessed using the FastQC software, reference genome alignment by the Hisat2 software and transcript abundance estimation using the StringTie software reference annotation information from official databases. FPKM calculation at gene level and transcript level were performed using R software Ballgown and calculated gene level and transcript level expression differences separately, and selected differentially expressed genes between samples or groups. |logFC|>1 and p value < 0.05 were as threshold for significant difference. PCA analysis, correlation analysis, clustering of differentially expressed genes, GO function significance enrichment analysis, Pathway significant enrichment analysis and other further data mining analysis were completed through procedures (python / R / shell, etc.) developed by Wuhan Shadow Gene Technology.

Immunofluorescence staining

Sections of 8 μm thickness were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, Beijing, China) at room temperature for 10 min, followed by washing 3 times in PBS-Tx for 5 min each. The sections were incubated with 0.2% TritonX-100 (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 20 min and washed three times with PBS-Tx for 5 min. Then, tissue sections were blocked with 2% BSA in PBS-Tx for 2 h at room temperature and incubated with collagen IV antibodies (ab6586; 1:200, 5 µg/mL; Abcam; England) for 16 h at 4 °C. After washing three times with PBS-Tx for 10 min, the slides were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated IgG (ab150077; 1:500, 2 µg/mL; Abcam; England) for 2 h at room temperature, followed by counterstaining with DAPI (Biofroxx, Einhausen, Germany; 1:100, 1 µg/mL). The slides were then washed three times with PBS-Tx for 20 min per wash. Sections were evaluated and imaged under a confocal microscope (DM2500; Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). (n = 6/group)

Mrna extraction and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qpcr)

One meibomian gland sample was composed of the upper and lower MG in the same rat (n = 5/group), and the expression of RXRg, PLIN1, PLIN2, ACSL6, HMGCS2, SCD1, LPO and TRPV3 were evaluated by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) with β-actin as the internal reference in each reaction. Primer sequences (Supplementary Table 1) were designed using the GenBank sequences and Primer Design software. Total RNA (Nanodrop 2000; Massachusetts, MA, United States; ThermoFisher) was isolated using the universal RNA purification kit (EZBioscince; California, CA, United States, B0004DP) and synthesized cDNA at 1 µg mRNA per sample by the Color Reverse Transcription Kit (EZBioscince, A0010CGQ). qPCR was performed for each sample with SYBR Green kit (EZBioscience, A00012-R1). Based on the period threshold value of genes, 2ΔΔCT was calculated for the relative quantification and graphical representation of gene expression changes. Each sample was measured three times.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and mapping charts were performed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Prism 9.0; La Jolla, CA, USA). The normality of the data was verified using the Shapiro-Wilker normality test, and groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance. Data were expressed as the mean ± SEM, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.
