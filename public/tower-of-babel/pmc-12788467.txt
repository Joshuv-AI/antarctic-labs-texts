Comparative Evaluation of Anchorage Loss During Fixed Orthodontic Technique with or without Anterior Mini-Implants in Class I Bimaxillary Extraction Design-A Finite Element Study
Authors: Shaji T. Varghese, Ahelam Mundekat, Mohammed Althaf, Shaheera Mukthar, Lijo K. Jose, Hrudya Balachandran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:The study used finite element analysis (FEA) to assess and quantify anchorage loss during simultaneous retraction and intrusion with or without anterior mini-implants under different forces.Materials and Methods:CBCT scans of a skeletal Class I patient were used. Mimics Research 18.0 was used to construct a maxilla 3D model from scans. FEA was done with ANSYS 2024 R2 employing a model with bilateral mini-screws and a control model without mini-screws. Each model received a 100g retraction force and additionally vertical intrusive forces 90 g, 120 g, and 150 g in model 2. Both models were limited to the superior maxilla and had appliance component glued connections.Results:FEA indicated that simultaneous intrusion and retraction enhanced maxillary first molar anterior displacement. The least displacement (6.24E-06 mm) occurred in model without mini-implants, while maximum displacement (6.86E-06 mm) in model with bilateral mini-implants with highest intrusive force.Conclusion:This study showed that conventional retraction without intrusive forces minimizes anchorage loss. With increasing force magnitude of intrusion, anterior molar displacement also increases.

INTRODUCTION

Bimaxillary dentoalveolar protrusion with a gummy smile is a common aesthetic concern, often linked to vertical maxillary excess.[1] While surgery like Le Fort I impaction is effective, many patients seek non-invasive alternatives.[2] Mini-implants offer a reliable solution, enabling true intrusion and retraction of anterior teeth without relying on patient compliance. These temporary anchorage devices (TADs) provide absolute anchorage, are easy to place, and allow immediate loading.[3] Various biomechanical setups using miniscrews have been developed to achieve simultaneous intrusion and retraction.[3] This study aims to quantify anchorage loss under different force applications using finite element analysis (FEA), a non-invasive, precise method to visualize stress distribution and tissue response.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The Institutional Ethics Committee approved the study. The working group included the CAD Lab, Department of Mechanical Engineering, Manipal Institute of Technology (MIT), and the Department of Orthodontics, PSM College of Dental Sciences and Research, Thrissur. A skeletal class-I patient referred to the Department of Orthodontics gave informed consent for CBCT imaging. Using Mimics Research 18.0 (Materialise Interactive Medical Image Control System, N.V., Heverlee, Belgium), a 600 µm CBCT of a patient’s skull and teeth was processed. The models were imported into 3D Experience software and CAD/CAM using Dassault Systèmes’ 3D to build a more solid model. Maxillary dentition, alveolar bone, micro-implants, brackets, and arch wire are modelled. All geometric models were analysed in ANSYS. This reverse engineering was done at PG-CAD LAB, MIT.

The maxilla, excluding third molars, was modelled with a constant 0.25 mm periodontal ligament thickness. Brackets of 0.022″ * .028″ slots were modelled using MBT prescription and positioned with the slot point aligned to the facial axis point. An 8 mm hook was affixed to a 0.019″ × 0.025″ stainless steel arch wire to retract the anteriors. A stainless steel inter-radicular mini-screw (8 mm × 1.6 mm) was placed perpendicular to the bone surface at the mucogingival junction from the occlusal plane. Through the bracket slot, the archwire reached the second molar’s distal end. STL models were used to create meshes. Dental fast prototyping software 3 MATIC supports this file type. 3D EXPERIENCE combines maxilla and appliance models into an orthodontic system. The software improved model accuracy and mechanical load response.

ANSYS Workbench for FEA was used to input material data, load, and boundary conditions. ANSYS 2024 R2 meshed and analysed 3D Experience appliance CAD models. The model 1 had 421444 nodes and 218623 elements, whereas 437060 nodes and 227725 elements in model 2. It was important to regard model interface contacts like the arch-bracket contact as bonded contacts. The bone’s outer nodes were attached throughout to limit mobility.

Model 2: Like model 1, but with inter-radicular mini-screws between lateral incisors and canines on both sides at the mucogingival junction. For the same 100 g retraction force, the e-chain from the arch wire to the mini-screws had three intrusive forces along the Z-axis.

Model 2: Like model 1, but with inter-radicular mini-screws between lateral incisors and canines on both sides at the mucogingival junction. For the same 100 g retraction force, the e-chain from the arch wire to the mini-screws had three intrusive forces along the Z-axis.

Biomechanical processes were examined utilising orthodontic loads. All systems retracted anterior maxillary teeth with 100 g between the power-arm and buccal tube on either side using an e-chain. Model 2 had three intrusion force magnitudes. Based on simulated clinical situations and physician judgements to intrude and retract maxillary incisors, four force categories were chosen. Thus, the force points were: Load 1 involved 100 g parallel to the arch wire (S1) from the retraction hook to the buccal tube. Loads 2, 3, and 4 involved 90 g (S2), 120 g (S3), and 150 g (S4), respectively, in the Z-axis (8 mm from arch wire to mini-implants between laterals and canines) and 100 g parallel to the arch wire.

RESULTS

Y-axis FEM interpretation was performed where Y-axis represents anteroposterior plane displacement; (+Y) implies posterior displacement, while (–Y) denotes anterior displacement. The displacement was measured with colour arrows. Oblique view of the FE model for retraction with or without intrusion is shown in Figure 1. The results showed Y- and Z-axis displacements due to stress components on teeth and alveolar bone. The anterior displacement of the maxillary first molar mesiobuccal cusp (MBC) at the cemento-enamel junction along the Y-axis was measured using ANSYS Workbench to assess AL.

FEM model 3 showing simultaneous retraction and intrusion with double mini-implant system

In system S1 without mini-implants, the maxillary first molar MBC had minimal anterior displacement. In model 2, with mini-implants between the laterals and canines on both sides, the maxillary first molar’s anterior displacement was greatest in Load 4 and then decreased in Load 3 and Load 2 as force application decreased. The anterior displacement of the first molar MBC was greatest in model 2-S4 (6.86E-06 mm), followed by S3 (6.69E-06 mm) and S2 (6.55E-06 mm), and least in model 1 (6.24E-06 mm) [Table 1 and Graph 1].

DISCUSSION

Anchorage control remains a fundamental challenge in orthodontics, especially during simultaneous retraction and intrusion in bimaxillary protrusion cases.[2] Although mini-implants (TADs) offer absolute anchorage, studies indicate that their efficiency is influenced by both the magnitude and direction of applied forces.[45] Our FEM analysis revealed that greater intrusion forces, particularly above 90 g, resulted in increased mesial movement of the maxillary molars, indicating anchorage loss.

This aligns with findings by Upadhyay et al.,[6] who reported that increased intrusive forces can lead to anchorage strain and loss. Papadopoulos and Tarawneh[7] emphasized that combined vertical and sagittal force vectors may produce tipping moments if not properly counterbalanced, leading to posterior tooth movement. Similarly, Kojima et al.,[4] through FEM simulations, found that vertical force components can displace anchorage units when not adequately controlled.

Regarding optimal intrusion force, Burstone suggested light, controlled forces—25 g per central incisor, 50 g for both central and lateral incisors, and 100 g when canines are included. Proffit also recommended 10-20 g per tooth for safe intrusion.[8] Sakdakornkul et al.[5] concluded that physiologic thresholds are 16 g for single midline implant systems and 23.5 g per side in dual TAD setups, to prevent periodontal compromise.

Our study’s findings reinforce the importance of maintaining force levels near physiologic thresholds to minimize side effects such as root resorption and anchorage loss. Clinicians should use caution when applying forces beyond 90 g during combined movements, especially with anterior mini-implant configurations, as these may not sufficiently resist reciprocal posterior forces.

The study simulated biological processes using FEM instead of in vivo settings. Periodontal ligament characteristics, stress response, and patient-specific bone density were disregarded. AL was only evaluated anteroposteriorly. These may limit our findings’ applicability. More 3D models, dynamic simulation, and clinical trials are warranted.

CONCLUSION

This study found that though skeletal anchorage has specific clinical benefits, careful assessment of the magnitude and vector of force application is required to minimise AL. Excessive intrusive forces, particularly when combined with retraction, had the potential to impair anchorage stability.
