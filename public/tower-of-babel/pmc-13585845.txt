Outcomes of down syndrome-related myeloid leukemia in Turkiye: a multicenter retrospective observational cohort study by the Turkish Pediatric Hematology Association

ABSTRACT
AbstractChildren with Down syndrome (DS) have a 150-times increased risk of myeloid leukemia (ML). In DS patients, in addition to DS-ML, common acute myeloid leukemia (AML) subtypes (standard-AML), observed in patients without DS, may also be seen. We evaluated ML types developing among DS cases, along with clinical features, survival, relapse, and mortality rates. The study included DS children aged 6 months to 18 years with newly diagnosed ML. Patients were classified retrospectively into two groups according to WHO guidelines and BFM-AML protocol recommendations as DS-ML and standard AML-DS. Among 53 DS patients, 71.7% (n = 38) had DS-ML and 28.3% (n = 15) had standard AML-DS. The mean age in the DS-ML group was significantly lower than the standard AML-DS group (23 ± 9 vs. 48 ± 51 months, p = 0.033). The 5-year overall survival (OS) and event-free survival (EFS) in the DS-ML group were 84.1%±6.0% and 81.3%±6.4%, respectively. For the standard AML, OS and EFS were 53.3%±12.9% and 45.7%±13.1%, respectively. The DS-ML group had significantly higher OS and EFS than the standard AML-DS group (p = 0.039 and p = 0.025, respectively). In our study, DS-ML and standard AML-DS were evaluated separately, which significantly contributes to the literature on the clinical and laboratory features of both groups, given the limited data on these topics. In the DS-ML group, complete remission, OS, and EFS were significantly higher than in the standard AML-DS. Differentiating DS-ML from standard AML-DS will ensure that patients with these biologically distinct diseases receive treatment of appropriate intensity and increase survival by reducing early mortality and relapse risk.Keywords: Down syndrome, Myeloid neoplasia, Myeloid leukemia, De novo acute myeloid leukemia

Introduction

Children with Down syndrome (DS) have a 20-times increased risk of acute leukemia compared to healthy children [1]. The presence of trisomy 21 in the intrauterine period in DS causes an increase in megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitors during fetal liver hematopoiesis, an increase in multipotent myeloid progenitors, and disruption of megakaryocyte differentiation. In 5–30% of these patients, dyserythropoiesis and megakaryopoiesis occur with the acquisition of the Globin Transcription Factor 1 (GATA-1) mutation, and transient abnormal myelopoiesis (TAM) is observed [2]. In 80% of TAM cases, spontaneous remission occurs within the first 3 months of life. However, DS myeloid leukemia (DS-ML) develops with French-American-British (FAB) M0-M6-M7 morphology derived from the erythroid and megakaryoblastic series in 20–30% of cases [2–4]. In this group, myeloid neoplasms are defined as DS-ML, independent of blast counts, rather than as myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) or acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [5]. As a result, the ML risk is increased 150 times in children with DS under 5 years of age compared to healthy children, while the risk of acute megakaryoblastic leukemia (AMKL) is increased 500 times in children under 4 years of age [1, 6].

The common AML subtypes (standard AML or de novo AML) seen in patients without DS are also observed in patients with DS. The biological characteristics of standard AML observed in DS differ markedly from those of DS-ML, as outlined in both the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines and the Berlin-Frankfurt-Munster (BFM) AML protocol recommendations [5, 7–9].

The three diagnostic criteria for DS-ML are defined as follows;

Patients aged 6 months to ≤ 4 years at first diagnosis and/or GATA1 mutation positive and with morphology/immunophenotype FAB M0, M6 or M7.

Patients aged 6 months to ≤ 4 years at first diagnosis and/or GATA1 mutation positive and with morphology/immunophenotype FAB M0, M6 or M7.

Patients aged 4 years to 6 years at first diagnosis who are GATA1 mutation positive and have morphology/immunophenotype FAB M0, M6, or M7.

Patients aged 4 years to 6 years at first diagnosis who are GATA1 mutation positive and have morphology/immunophenotype FAB M0, M6, or M7.

Patients without recurrent genetic anomalies (t(8;21), t(15;17), MLL, t(9;11), CBFB MYH11) observed in sporadic AML (WHO 2016) and no mutations like t(1;22) and t(1;3) translocations associated with AMKL are evaluated as DS-ML [2, 8, 10, 11].

Patients without recurrent genetic anomalies (t(8;21), t(15;17), MLL, t(9;11), CBFB MYH11) observed in sporadic AML (WHO 2016) and no mutations like t(1;22) and t(1;3) translocations associated with AMKL are evaluated as DS-ML [2, 8, 10, 11].

According to the FAB classification, patients without M0, M6 and M7 morphology and with the presence of typical translocation or inversion for sporadic AML are classified as having standard AML-DS [3, 6–8]. In this study, the ML types developing in DS patients in our country (DS-ML and standard AML-DS), clinical features, and survival, relapse and mortality rates were evaluated.

Methods

This study was designed as a multicenter retrospective observational cohort study. Data were retrospectively reviewed for pediatric patients with Down syndrome who were diagnosed with ML for the first time between 1 January 2010 and 30 September 2024 in Türkiye. Data were collected from 14 participating centers with reliable institutional electronic medical records and complete treatment documentation. A standardized registration form was prepared by the Turkish Pediatric Hematology Association (TPHA), Hematological Malignancies (Leukemia) Subcommittee and sent to the participating institutions. To reduce selection bias, all consecutive eligible patients meeting the predefined inclusion criteria during the study period were included through retrospective review of institutional records.

Exclusion criteria

Patients who met any of the following criteria were excluded from the study:

Patients with DS who developed acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) or non-myeloid malignancies,

Patients with DS who developed acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) or non-myeloid malignancies,

Patients with secondary ML following prior chemotherapy or radiotherapy for another malignancy,

For the analytical purposes of this study, patients with DS and diagnosis of myeloid neoplasm were retrospectively divided into two classes as DS-ML and standard AML-DS, according to the WHO guidelines and BFM-AML protocols, as detailed in the introduction section [2, 3, 6–8, 10, 11]. Patients with DS-ML and standard AML-DS diagnosis had ages of diagnosis, gender, TAM history or not, and comorbid diseases like congenital heart disease, immune failure, and hypothyroidism recorded. In both groups, complaints when leukemia diagnosis was made, such as weight loss, fever, tachycardia, tachypnea, cutaneous mucosal hemorrhage, lymphadenopathy, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, neurological findings and extramedullary involvement, were researched. At diagnosis, clinical features like leukemia type according to FAB AML classification, white cell count, hemoglobin level, platelet level, peripheral smear and blast rate in bone marrow, treatment response, mortality, and relapse were noted. Genetic marker studies were investigated with cytogenetic analyses, GATA-1, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and molecular PCR. The BFM, Children’s Oncology Group (COG) and UK’s Medical Research Council (MRC) chemotherapy protocols given to patients were assessed. These protocols recommend reduced-intensity chemotherapy protocols with lower cumulative doses of anthracycline and cytarabine (Ara-C) for ML-DS due to the increased sensitivity of DS patients to anthracycline and cytarabine (Ara-C). This approach aims to reduce morbidity and mortality in DS patients, particularly due to treatment-related toxicities such as life-threatening infections and mucosal damage [11–16]. Early death (ED) was defined as any death within 42 days of beginning treatment before any evaluation of treatment response. Complete remission (CR) was defined as total leukocyte count ≥ 1000/mm3, neutrophils ≥ 500/mm3, and platelets ≥ 50,000/mm3 lasting at least 4 weeks, regenerative or normocellular bone marrow (less than 5% blasts) and no blasts in the extramedullary area. Relapse was defined as the reoccurrence of leukemic blasts in peripheral blood after at least 4 weeks of complete remission, blast counts in bone marrow ≥ 5% and/or reoccurrence of extramedullary blastic infiltration [11]. The treatment response, relapse, event-free survival (EFS) and overall survival (OS) data were evaluated for patients with DS-ML and standard AML-DS. The findings from our study—OS and EFS rates—were compared methodologically with published control data, large-scale meta-analyses, and landmark clinical trial outcomes from major international pediatric oncology collaborative groups and other countries (e.g., COG and BFM working group, Thailand, Ireland, Poland). This comparison enabled a direct descriptive assessment of regional treatment-related toxicity and survival outcomes in the international ML-DS context.

Statistical method

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software. Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation and median with minimum–maximum values, while categorical variables are presented as frequency and percentage. Comparisons of continuous variables between the ML-DS and AML-DS groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, given the limited sample size and non-parametric structure of the data. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test. Fisher’s exact test, or the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test, was used when expected cell counts were small.

Survival outcomes were evaluated using the Kaplan–Meier method. OS was calculated from the date of diagnosis to death from any cause; patients who were alive at the last follow-up were censored. Relapse-free survival (RFS) was calculated from the date of diagnosis to the first documented relapse; patients without relapse were censored at the last follow-up. EFS was calculated from the date of diagnosis to the first documented event, defined as relapse or death; patients without an event were censored at the last follow-up.

Survival times are expressed in months. Five-year survival rates were obtained from the Kaplan–Meier survival estimates at 60 months. If no event occurred at 60 months, the most recent Kaplan–Meier survival estimate before 60 months was used. Survival estimates are reported as percentages with standard errors. Survival curves between the ML-DS and AML-DS groups were compared using the log-rank test. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS 29.0.2.0 (IBM Corp. Released 2023. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0.2.0 Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.).

Ethic committee

This retrospective study was conducted using anonymized patient data from electronic medical records. Due to its retrospective nature, informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board of Bursa Uludağ University Health Research Ethics Committee in 2025 with decision number 2025/996/21 − 12.

Results

The study included 53 pediatric patients with DS who were diagnosed with ML for the first time from 1 January 2010 to 30 September 2024. Of these, 71.7% (n = 38) had DS-ML and 28.3% (n = 15) had standard AML-DS. Of patients, 64.2% (n = 34) were boys and 35.8% (n = 19) were girls, with median (min-max) age of 23 (6-192) months. In the DS-ML group, the median age was 22 (6–48) months, which was statistically lower (p = 0.033) than the median age in the standard AML-DS group of 30 (14–192)) months. The distribution of patients with DS-ML and standard AML-DS, demographic and laboratory findings are given in Table 1. The majority of the DS-ML group were FAB-M7, while the majority of the de novo AML-DS group were FAB-M1. Weight loss was found to be significantly more frequent in the standard AML-DS group (p < 0.03). Two patients with extramedullary involvement had AML-M4 morphology and presented with granulocytic sarcomas. In the whole group, the most commonly identified additional pathology was hypothyroidism in 26.4% (n = 14) and congenital heart disease in 11.3% (n = 6). Six patients with congenital heart disease consulted with a cardiologist before each chemotherapy session and were closely clinically monitored for chemotherapy toxicity. All six patients received low-dose chemotherapy and all survived. The mean onset of duration of complaints before first admission was 16.5 ± 19.5 days; the duration of symptoms before diagnosis was shorter in the DS-ML group (p < 0.01). There were no significant differences in mean blood cell counts, peripheral smear and bone marrow blast percentage at admission (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Most patients (81.1%, n = 43) were treated with BFM-based protocols, while others were treated with AML-MRC (n = 8) and COG (n = 2) protocols. Since BFM was the most commonly used protocol, a subgroup analysis was performed among patients treated with BFM. In this subgroup, final treatment status did not differ significantly between the BFM reduced-dose and BFM standard-dose groups (p = 0.223). Relapse rates among evaluable patients were also comparable across protocol/dose groups (p = 0.250). Early death during chemotherapy occurred in 3/40 (7.5%) patients receiving reduced-dose chemotherapy and in 2/13 (15.4%) patients receiving standard-dose chemotherapy; this difference was not statistically significant (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.586) (Table 3). Furthermore, when DS-ML and standard AML-DS groups were examined in terms of reduced or standard doses according to BFM protocols, no statistically significant difference was found in terms of death during chemotherapy, complete remission after induction chemotherapy, and resistant/relapsed disease (p = 1.000, p = 0.130).

Among all 53 patients included in the overall survival analysis, the 5-year OS was 75.1% ± 6.0%. In subgroup analysis, the 5-year OS was 84.1% ± 6.0% in patients with ML-DS and 53.3% ± 12.9% in patients with AML-DS, indicating significantly higher OS in the ML-DS group (log-rank p = 0.039) (Fig. 1a and b).

Kaplan-Meier overall survival (OS), relapse-free survival (RFS), event-free survival (EFS) curves for all patients, DS-ML and standard AML-DS groups

Relapse-free survival (RFS) was evaluated in 47 patients with available relapse data. In this cohort, the 5-year RFS was 79.9% ± 6.0%. The 5-year RFS was 90.9% ± 5.0% in the ML-DS group and 52.7% ± 14.1% in the AML-DS group, with a statistically significant difference between groups (log-rank p = 0.006) (Fig. 1c and d).

Among all 53 patients included in the event-free survival analysis, the 5-year EFS was 70.9% ± 6.4%. The 5-year EFS was 81.3% ± 6.4% in the ML-DS group and 45.7% ± 13.1% in the AML-DS group. The difference between groups was statistically significant (log-rank p = 0.025) (Fig. 1e and f).

Discussion

In our study, the features of myeloid neoplasms observed in DS were separately investigated in two groups: DS-ML and standard AML-DS. In the literature, studies generally provide data on DS-ML outcomes, and there is insufficient clinical data separately investigating diagnostic criteria and prognosis for DS-ML and standard AML observed in DS. After the 2000s, recognition of GATA-1 mutations and recurrent genetic mutations specific to AML, along with flow cytometric differentiation, allowed clearer differentiation between DS-ML and standard AML-DS diagnoses [8, 11]. This study contributes to the literature by separately evaluating clinical and laboratory data with survival analysis for these two separate groups.

The biology of DS-ML is different from standard AML-DS and is characterized by diagnostic age, FAB morphology M0-M6-M7, and does not have the typical translocation or inversions observed in standard AML (de novo AML) [8, 11]. In our study, consistent with the literature, the majority of myeloid neoplasms observed in DS were DS-ML (71.7%) [7, 8, 14]. Additionally, the mean age in this group was lower than both the standard AML-DS group in our study (p = 0.033) and the non-DS AML groups previously reported in the literature [4, 7, 8]. Nearly 50% of DS patients diagnosed with ML are reported to be diagnosed under 1 year of age, while 1–2% are diagnosed over 4 years of age [6]. The younger mean age in DS-ML is linked to disrupted fetal liver hematopoiesis in trisomy 21 and to the addition of a GATA-1 mutation [1, 17–19]. In our study, the whole DS-ML group was under 4 years of age as expected. Although standard AML-DS is generally observed in patients over 4 years of age, the mean age in the current data was younger than 4 years in this group (median 30 months) [7, 8], which differs from the literature. Only 3 cases were over 4 years of age (Table 1). Standard AML is generally reported in children over 4 years of age, although rare cases have been identified in those under 4. In particular, in large series where a clear distinction between standard AML and DS-ML has not been made, cases in children under 4 years of age have been reported in individuals with FAB morphology defined as M1-M5 [6, 15]. Further investigation is needed to determine the incidence of standard AML in children younger than 4 years of age in DS, and to define the biological characteristics of these patients.

Six of our patients with DS-ML (11.3%) had TAM history and this rate is lower than reported in the literature (37–44%) [1, 6]. This situation may be linked to the inadequate numbers of pediatric hematologists in every region in Türkiye and neonatal experts and pediatricians not recognizing silent TAM cases or not reporting cases. In our study, none of the patients with standard AML identified had TAM history. TAM is not an expected feature of standard AML-DS [7, 8].

In this cohort, AML M7 was detected in the majority of DS-ML patients, consistent with the literature [1, 6, 17–19]. When pediatric patients with 54 myeloid neoplasms were evaluated by the Polish Pediatric Leukemia and Lymphoma Group, AML M7 was most frequently identified (56.9%), with ML derived from M1-M2-M4-M5 myelocytic series reported at a rate of 28% (15/54) [6].

When complaints at first admission are evaluated, the most frequent complaints and findings were fever, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, tachycardia-tachypnea and skin-mucosal hemorrhage in both groups. Weight loss findings were identified more frequently in the standard AML-DS group (p = 0.03). A broad-scope study from Thailand reported the most frequent complaints and findings on attendance were similar to our study [19]. However, there is no comparative study of both groups. In the standard AML-DS group, findings frequently observed in leukemia, like weight loss, were more frequent than in the DS-ML group, which may be linked to the biology of standard AML being different to DS-ML. This is because the leukemogenic process for DS-ML develops more slowly beginning in the embryonic-intrauterine period, while this process is more rapid and progressive in de novo AML-DS. The inflammatory symptom of weight loss being more frequent in standard AML may be linked to this situation [7, 8].

When the genetic tests of patients are investigated, GATA-1 was studied in only 22.5% (n = 12) of all patients. Following the presence of trisomy 21 in DS, GATA-1 mutations play a critical role as a ‘second hit’ that impairs hematopoietic differentiation and triggers the hyperproliferation of megakaryocytic progenitor cells during ML development. In DS-ML patients, blast cells almost invariably harbor an acquired (somatic) mutation in exon 2 of the GATA-1 gene. This mutation leads to the loss of the N-terminal transactivation domain of the gene, resulting in the synthesis of a truncated protein isoform known as GATA1s instead of the full-length, wild-type GATA-1 protein. Consequently, these blast cells become hypersensitive to chemotherapy, particularly cytarabine (Ara-C). Conversely, in de novo AML, the overexpression of the GATA-1 protein elevates the levels of Bcl-xL, an anti-apoptotic protein, thereby mediating resistance to standard chemotherapeutic agents such as cytarabine (Ara-C) and daunorubicin. In the literature, GATA-1 mutation is more frequent at rates of 38–89% [6, 8]. In our country, the use of the Sanger sequence is common, with genetic tests studied with new-generation sequencing (NGS) in the last 5 years. As a result, GATA-1 mutations were found to be low among our patients [20]. All of our patients who were GATA-1 positive (n = 7) had DS-ML. GATA-1 was not detected in the de novo AML group. (Table 1).

In the present study, 75.5% of patients received reduced-intensity chemotherapy, with no difference between the two groups in terms of chemotherapy intensity (p = 0.480) (Table 2). In DS, treatment responses are very good due to blasts being very sensitive to cytarabine and anthracyclines [21]. However, children with DS are overly susceptible to infections [22]. As a result, the use of reduced intensity chemotherapy with BFM, COG, and AML MRC protocols have been recommended through the years, compared to classic AML protocol (14, 23–25). Differentiating DS-ML from sporadic (de novo) AML has vital importance in terms of both relapse risk and preventing the risk of mortality linked to toxicity [8, 26]. However, as this differentiation has been defined in the literature for years, clinicians have a tendency to treat these patients with lower intensity chemotherapy regimens [8, 23, 25]. In our country, the majority of patients are diagnosed with DS-ML, and receive reduced intensity chemotherapy regimens due to infection risk and linked early mortality concerns. In our data, the relapse rate (17%) is higher than European countries like the UK and the Nordic countries, which may be linked to all DS patients being given reduced-intensity chemotherapy regimens without differentiating between the two groups [15, 27]. In conclusion, the relapse rate in the standard AML-DS group (40%) was higher than in the DS-ML group (7.9%), and the CR rate was higher in DS-ML (p = 0.007). However, no statistically significant difference was found in terms of early mortality (p > 0.05) (Table 2). This situation may be linked to the low number of patients.

In the current cohort, 11.3% of all patients had early death after induction due to infection. This rate is higher than central Europe (1.2%) and Japan (1.4%), while it is similar to northern European countries (16.1%) and the UK (11%), France (9%), Poland (9%) [6, 14, 15, 25, 27]. It is lower than studies performed in Thailand (30%), Romania (28.5%) and Ireland (25.9%) [1, 4, 19]. To reduce deaths due to infection in Türkiye, cooperation between infection control committees, pediatric infection experts and hemato-oncologists will be effective.

In our study, the CR rate for all patients at the end of induction was 71.7%. This rate varies from 70.9 to 90.7% in the literature [6, 15, 24]. The DS-ML group (81.6%) had significantly higher CR compared to the standard AML-DS group (46.7%) (p = 0.007). As emphasized in the literature, the CR rate in the DS-ML group at the end of induction is better than in the standard AML-DS group [8].

In our study, all patients had 5-year OS of 75.1 ± 6% and EFS of 70.9 ± 6.4%. Survival analysis in the literature evaluated all patients together without separating DS-ML from standard AML-DS (including FAB M1-M5). Accordingly, 5-year OS varied from 57.1 to 85%, while EFS was reported to vary from 74 to 83% [4, 6, 15, 24, 28]. In our study, when the two groups are separately evaluated, the DS-ML group had significantly higher OS and EFS compared to standard AML group (p = 0.039, p = 0.025, respectively). In the literature, there is no study defining DS-ML and standard AML-DS with broad criteria and data, as in our study. There is one study sharing data for 10 patients with AML among DS patients over four years of age. In this study, the importance of evaluating DS-ML not just with age, but with clinical features, morphology, cytogenetics and presence of GATA-1 mutation together in patients over 3 years of age was emphasized, while this group of patients may require more intensive chemotherapy like patients with non-Down AML [7].

Our study has several limitations. First, its retrospective nature and the heterogeneity of institutional medical records over a 14-year period prevented the inclusion of all DS myeloid neoplasms. Second, the total sample size remains relatively small because DS-related myeloid malignancies are rare. During the study period (2010–2024), according to the Turkish Statistical Institute (TurkStat) approximately 17.5 million live births occurred in Türkiye [29]. Given that the incidence of DS is reported to be approximately 1 in 700 live births, it is estimated that approximately 22,000 babies with DS were born in Türkiye during this period [26]. Furthermore, a parallel study conducted by the TPHA Hematologic Malignancies Subcommittee evaluating non-DS pediatric AML cases reported 475 de novo cases, further emphasizing the rare nature of these malignancies [30]. Third, the absence of a centralized molecular registry system for uniform GATA-1 mutation screening via NGS between 2010 and 2020 may have led to the failure to detect subclonal mutations. Despite these constraints, this cohort provides critical real-world data for pediatric AML in Türkiye. Moving forward, the identification of cytogenetic and molecular alterations, alongside flow cytometry-based minimal residual disease (MRD) monitoring, remain vital for accurate treatment indication and response interpretation [31]. Incorporating NGS and advanced flow cytometry MRD tracking into routine clinical practice is paramount to refine future risk stratification. Looking ahead, these clinical strategies will be further enhanced by rapidly evolving technological breakthroughs. The introduction of the Internet of Things (IoT) concept into medical practice offers revolutionary potential for continuous digital monitoring and personalized tracking of patients with myeloid leukemia [32]. However, integrating these advanced methodologies into healthcare systems poses steep challenges, primarily driven by high operational costs and the specialized laboratory infrastructure.

Conclusions

Here, DS-ML and standard (de novo) AML-DS groups were examined separately. In the literature, all myeloid proliferations seen in patients with DS are generally examined as a single group. In this study, DS-AML and standard AML-DS were evaluated separately, and we believe it makes a significant contribution to the literature on the clinical and laboratory characteristics of both groups, given the limited data available on this subject. In the current cohort, all cases were younger than 4 years in the DS-ML group, whereas cases younger than 4 years in the standard AML-DS group were more common, contrary to the literature. When survival analysis was performed, the DS-ML group had significantly higher OS and EFS than the standard AML-DS group (p = 0.039 and p = 0.025, respectively). Differentiating DS-ML from standard AML-DS among myeloid neoplasms with DS will lead to an increase in survival and reduce the linked early mortality and relapse risks by ensuring patients with these two biologically different diseases receive treatment of appropriate intensity. Further research is needed on this topic.
