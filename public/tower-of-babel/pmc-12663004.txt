Investigating the potential anti-cancer mechanism for thyroid cancer: Role of Herba Epimedii via network pharmacology approach
Authors: Xuehan Wang, Lin Guo, Yiren Feng, Gang Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the effective ingredients, crucial molecular targets, and the underlying molecular mechanisms associated with Herba Epimedii (HE) that exhibits anti-cancer effects for treating thyroid cancer (TC) using the network pharmacology approach.MethodsThe Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology (TCMSP) public database was analysed to identify the major bioactive ingredients and target proteins associated with HE. The Human Gene (Gene Cards) database, National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) gene database, and Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) databases were analyzed to search for the disease targets of TC. Based on the overlapped results between HE putative targets and TC targets, the “compound-disease-target” network and a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network were constructed using Cytoscape 3.8.0. The R package was used to perform the Gene Ontology (GO) and the Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses. Finally, the “compound-disease-pathway-target” network was drawn by integrating all the information.ResultsThe results indicated that there were 23 effective bioactive ingredients associated with HE, covering 203 targets, 145 of which were related to TC. Through PPI network topological analysis, 53 targets (including AKT1, JUN, MAPK1, RELA, and IL6) were identified as the major targets of HE. Results recorded from GO functional analyses revealed that significant biological processes were enriched. Results from KEGG analyses revealed that the pathways, such as the TNF, IL-17, and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways, played vital roles in the HE intervention for TC.ConclusionWe revealed the potential mechanisms through which HE exerts anti-cancer effects in the case of TC. A holistic perspective on multiple ingredients, targets, and pathways has been presented, and the results can potentially help in developing new treatment methods for TC.Keywords: Network pharmacology, Herba epimedii, Natural products, Chinese medicine, Neoplasms, Thyroid cancer

Introduction

Thyroid cancer (TC) is the most prevalent endocrine cancer, with an incidence that has increased considerably in the last decade. TC ranks fifth in cancer incidence among women and accounts for approximately 2.1 % of all newly diagnosed tumors worldwide.1, 2 The pathogenesis of this disease involves multiple etiological factors, including but not limited to radiation exposure during childhood, genetic susceptibility, pre-existing thyroid disorders, and imbalances in iodine intake (either deficiency or excess),3, 4 Standard treatment modalities for TC encompass surgical resection of the thyroid gland, radioactive iodine treatment, thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) suppression therapy, and molecular-targeted therapies. Although TC typically demonstrates relatively indolent biological characteristics and favorable clinical outcomes, disease recurrence or distant metastasis occurs in 20–30 % of patients following initial treatment,5, 6 These clinical challenges underscore the urgent need to develop novel therapeutic strategies with improved efficacy and safety profiles to better control or potentially prevent TC progression.

Chinese herbal medicine (CHM) serves as a cornerstone of traditional and complementary medical systems, playing a pivotal role in the management of diverse diseases. With over 3,000 documented varieties, CHM has been extensively utilized in oncology due to its favorable therapeutic outcomes and minimal side effects.7, 8 Among these, Herba Epimedii (HE), derived from the dried leaves of Epimedium brevicornu Maxim, is officially recognized in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia.9 HE exhibits a broad spectrum of pharmacological activities, such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, bone-protective, and neuroprotective effects.10, 11 Emerging evidence indicates that HE exerts potent antitumor effects across multiple cancer types by modulating critical cellular processes such as proliferation, apoptosis, angiogenesis, and metastasis. These effects are mediated through the regulation of key signaling pathways, including MAPK/ERK, JAK2/STAT3, and PI3K/AKT/mTOR,12, 13 Notably, icariin, the primary bioactive compound in HE, has demonstrated substantial anticancer activity in prostate, breast, and gastric cancers by suppressing malignant cell growth, migration, and invasiveness.14, 15 Given its multifaceted antitumor mechanisms, HE represents a promising therapeutic candidate for thyroid carcinoma (TC) treatment.

Despite the demonstrated antitumor potential of HE, the precise molecular mechanisms governing its therapeutic effects against TC remain incompletely characterized. Recent advances in bioinformatics have facilitated the widespread adoption of network pharmacology, an innovative approach that enables systematic exploration of how herbal medicines interact with complex biological networks and signaling pathways.16, 17 Using network analysis to identify protein targets can predict potential drug targets and gain a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms of diseases, providing new ideas for disease treatment and prevention. The use of this promising methodology has helped in shifting from the conventional “one drug and one target” mode to a new “multi-ingredient and multi-network target” mode.18 Network pharmacology has also become a powerful tool to construct network relationships among active ingredients, relevant target proteins, signal pathways, and diseases.

Therefore, we adopted a comprehensive network pharmacology-based approach to study and understand the intrinsic molecular mechanisms through which the bioactive ingredients in HE exerts therapeutic effects on TC. The detailed workflow is presented herein (Fig. 1).

Methods

Predicting active ingredients and related targets of HE

Analysis of the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database (TCMSP; https://tcmspw.com/tcmsp.php) resulted in the identification of all chemical ingredients related to HE.19 The guidelines presented in the TCMSP database were followed to screen the active ingredients (criteria: oral bioavailability (OB): ≥30 %; drug-likeness (DL) index values: ≥0.18). These ingredients were used as the candidate active ingredients in subsequent research procedures. The TCMSP database was also retrieved to identify the related protein targets of the candidate active ingredients in HE. Subsequently, the UniProt database was retrieved to identify the gene symbol corresponding to each protein target (https://www.uniprot.org/).20.

Therapeutic targets of HE for TC t reatment

We used “thyroid cancer” as the keyword and identified TC-associated targets. Identification of the targets involved analyzing the Human Gene Database (GeneCards, https://www.genecards.org/).21). Various other databases such as the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) gene database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene),22 and the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man database (OMIM, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/omim) were also retrieved to arrive at the results.23 UniProt was used to standardize all known gene targets. The Venn diagram was used to identify the common targets between HE and TC. These common targets were identified using a free tool available online (Venny 2.1.0; https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html). The common targets were identified as the possible therapeutic HE targets for the treatment of TC and further analysis.

Construction of the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network

The common targets associated with HE for treating TC were imported into STRING (https://string-db.org/)24 to obtain the PPI data. A high confidence scoring value (> 0.9) and the species defined as “Homo sapiens” were included for analyses. Analysis and visualization of the PPI network were achieved using Cytoscape 3.8.0 (http://www.cytoscape.org/).25 The core targets in the network were detected using a Cytoscape plugin Network Analyzer.26 The topological features of the PPI network were identified, and the value of the degree of a node in the network, which can be used to predict the essential nodes of the network, was primarily studied to analyze these features. The value of the degree of each node indicated the number of interactions of a single node with all the other nodes. The values of the degree of the nodes larger than the median value were recognized as the core gene nodes of HE (against TC).

Analysing the gene Ontology (GO) and pathway enrichment results

The functional characteristics and biological pathways of intersection target genes are studied in accordance with the widely used GO functional enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis methods. We used the ClusterProfiler (an R package; threshold of P-value < 0.05) for enrichment analysis.27, 28 The significantly enriched results for GO terms and KEGG pathways have been displayed in the bubble chart and bar chart, respectively.

Network construction

Two networks (Compound-disease-target network and Compound-disease-pathway-target network) were constructed and analysed using Cytoscape 3.8.0. to better comprehend the complex pharmacological mechanisms through which HE participated in the treatment of TC. The Compound-disease-target (C-D-T) interactional network was built to reveal the complex relationship between the bioactive ingredients of HE, TC, and intersection targets. Secondly, the main active components were investigated by analysing the topological characteristic of the network. In the case of the compound-disease-pathway-target (C-D-P-T) network, the information on the signaling pathway associated with the targets was obtained from the results obtained using the KEGG biological pathway enrichment analysis method. The C-D-P-T network was established to present the multi-component, multi-pathway, and multi-target characteristics for the treatment of TC more intuitively.

One hundred and thirty HE ingredients were identified by analyzing the TCMSP database. Later, 23 ingredients were identified as the candidate active ingredients. The screening procedure was carried out on the basis of the inclusion and exclusion criteria (DL ≥ 0.18 and OB ≥ 30 %; Table 1). After deleting the duplicates, the UniProt database was used to convert 203 related targets of HE, corresponding to the 23 candidate active ingredients, into the relevant gene symbol.

The TC-associated targets were retrieved by integrating three different databases, including 1811 targets from the GeneCards, 668 targets from the OMIM, and 493 targets from the NCBI gene database. In all, 2491 TC-associated targets were acquired after removing the repeated records. Subsequently, 145 common targets between HE targets(n = 203) and TC-associated targets (n = 2491) were identified. The common targets were the therapeutic targets of HE. The gene symbols and descriptions of 145 common targets are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Constructing and analyzing the PPI network

The platform STRING was used to construct the PPI network. The 145 therapeutic targets selected were entered into STRING. Visualization of the results was achieved using Cytoscape (version: 3.8.0) (Fig. 2). The network contained 145 nodes and 554 edges. The value of the degree of the median node was 7.64. The size and redness of a node increase with an increase in the value of the node degree. Similarly, the width of the edge increases with an increase in the edge betweenness value. The nodes were classified based on the values of the degree. The classification was done by topologically analyzing the PPI diagram. The core targets were represented by the genes whose degree values were higher than the average values. Lastly, a total of 53 core targets were screened, and the top 20 core targets characterized by a high degree of connectivity were presented (Fig. 3). As demonstrated in Fig. 4, the top 10 target proteins with high degrees were AKT1, JUN, MAPK1, RELA, IL6, MAPK14, MAPK8, ESR1, FOS, and RB1.

Gene ontology and pathway enrichment analysis

We report that 2405 GO terms were enriched. The enriched terms consisted of 2219 terms corresponding to biological process (BP), 40 terms corresponding to cellular component (CC), and 146 terms corresponding to molecular function (MF). We selected the 10 most significantly enriched GO terms belonging to BP, MF, and CC categories for analysis (Fig. 4). The results revealed that the targets belonging to the BP category were predominantly associated with response toward oxidative stress, response toward drugs, cellular response toward chemical stress, response toward molecules of bacterial origin, response toward lipopolysaccharide, response toward steroid hormone, response to oxygen levels, the metabolic process associated with reactive oxygen species, response toward reactive oxygen species, and cellular response toward oxidative stress. The targets of CC were predominantly associated with membrane microdomain, membrane raft, transcription regulator complex, membrane region, protein kinase complex, vesicle lumen, serine/threonine-protein kinase complex, plasma membrane raft, caveola, and cyclin-dependent protein kinase holoenzyme complex. It was also observed that the targets of MF were related to the RNA polymerase II-specific DNA-binding transcription factor binding, DNA-binding transcription factor binding, cytokine receptor binding, cytokine activity, ubiquitin-like protein ligase binding, ligand-activated transcription factor activity, nuclear receptor activity, nuclear hormone receptor binding, steroid hormone receptor activity, and transcription coactivator binding.

The KEGG enrichment analyses were carried out to identify the 156 related signaling pathways. The 20 most significantly enriched pathways were analyzed, and the results revealed that these common targets were primarily associated with the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, AGE–RAGE signaling pathway (associated with diabetic complications), IL-17 signaling pathway, and TNF signaling pathway. These were also associated with the occurrence and progression of hepatitis B, human cytomegalovirus infection, Kaposi sarcoma-associated herpesvirus infection, prostate cancer, fluid shear stress, and atherosclerosis. Hepatitis C, Epstein–Barr virus infection, proteoglycans in cancer, and hepatocellular carcinoma were also associated with these targets (Fig. 5).

Network construction and analysis

C-D-T network construction and analysis

Twenty-three active ingredients of HE and 145 common targets between HE and TC were introduced into Cytoscape 3.8.0 to generate the C-D-T network (Fig. 6). In this network, the node without any connections was eliminated. The final C-D-T network comprised 166 nodes (1 disease node, 20 compound nodes, and 145 target nodes) and 65 edges. The compounds, disease, and targets were represented by purple, orange, and green nodes, respectively, whereas the interactions among them were represented as edges. The network topological analysis method was used to discern the key compounds associated with HE for the treatment of TC. The compounds were sorted based on the degree values. The degree value of a compound is proportional to its importance. The average degree value was 16.55, and the 6 compounds whose degree values exceeded the average value were identified to be MOL000098 (quercetin, degree = 113), MOL000006 (luteolin, degree = 49), MOL000422 (kaempferol, degree = 40), MOL004373 (anhydroicaritin, degree = 22), MOL004391 (8-(3-methylbut-2-enyl)-2-phenyl-chromone, degree = 18), and MOL003542 (8-Isopentenyl-kaempferol, degree = 17). Therefore, It was hypothesized that quercetin, luteolin, kaempferol, anhydroicaritin, 8-(3-methylbut-2-enyl)-2-phenyl-chromone and 8-Isopentenyl-kaempferol may be key active ingredients in HE that need to be tested as potential targets which could assist in the treatment development of TC.

Construction and analysis of the C-D-P-T network

Cytoscape (version: 3.8.0) was used to create a schematic for the C-D-P-T network, based on the active compounds, disease, the top 20 pathways, and common targets, to further illustrate the molecular mechanisms through which HE affected TC (Fig. 7). A total of 186 nodes (20 compounds, 1 disease, 20 pathways, and 145 targets) and 239 edges were obtained. The compounds, disease, protein targets, or pathways were expressed in terms of nodes. The edges represented the compound-target or target-pathway interactions. Each active compound corresponded to multiple targets. Each of these could potentially be associated with different biological pathways. This intuitively revealed the multiple compounds, multiple targets, and multiple pathways pharmacological effects of HE on TC.

Discussion

Main interpretation

This investigation employed network pharmacology to systematically explore the therapeutic mechanisms of HE in TC treatment. Through comprehensive screening, we identified 23 bioactive compounds in HE that interact with 145 potential therapeutic targets. PPI network analysis identified 53 hub targets exhibiting the highest network centrality, with key molecules including AKT1, JUN, MAPK1, and IL6. Functional enrichment analysis demonstrated that these core targets were significantly enriched in cancer-related pathways, particularly TNF signaling, PI3K-Akt cascade, IL-17 pathway, p53-mediated processes, and MAPK signaling transduction.

Our network analysis identifies several compounds including quercetin. Quercetin, a naturally occurring polyphenolic flavonoid found in many foods and herbs, exhibits various biological properties such as antioxidant, antiallergic, anti-inflammatory, and antiviral properties29, 30, 31). It exerts anticancer effects on different types of tumors, such as breast cancer, colorectal carcinoma, bone cancer, hematological malignancies, and TC.32, 33, 34, 35, 36 It has been demonstrated to induce cell death in human papillary TC (B-CPAP) cells.37 It was also reported to induce the expression of pro-NAG-1/GDF15 in differentiated TC cells.38 It has also been reported that quercetin can promote the functions of sorafenib that affect the growth and migration of TC cells, indicating its promising preventive effect on TC.39 Therefore, this approach finds a known interaction and here we added to it by showcasing quercetin is possibly acting through PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and MAPK signaling pathway that may help us understand its mechanism of action in TC.3′,4′,5,7-tetrahydroxyflavone, commonly known as Luteolin, is a flavonoid that is present in various types of plants. It has been previously reported that luteolin could induce apoptosis and suppress metastasis in breast cancer cells.40, 41 It was also reported to inhibit the proliferation of prostate cancer and gastric cancer cells.42, 43 Furthermore, Luteolin was demonstrated to suppress tumor progression in TC through lncRNA BANCR and via the mechanism of downstream TSHR/CCND1 signaling.44 Kaempferol, another core active component of the C-D-T network, also exhibited antitumor effects in the cases of gastric, bladder, pancreatic, and cervical cancer.45, 46, 47, 48 Current research on kaempferol's antitumor activity against TC remains limited. Preliminary findings suggest that this bioactive compound may suppress TC progression; however, additional experimental validation is required to confirm these observations and elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms.

PPI network analysis exhibited the interactions among the targets of HE. Numerous target genes belonging to the PPI network are associated with TC. AKT1, the target gene characterized by the maximum degree in the PPI network, promotes tumor development and progression in the mice model of TC.49 Mitogen-activated protein kinase 1 (MAPK1) is a member of the MAP kinase family, and the target of Circ_0058124 promotes metastasis, proliferation, and metabolic abilities in TC cells.50 Chen and his colleagues used the bioinformatics analyses method and found that JUN was closely associated with the process of TC genesis.51 The inflammatory factor IL-6 was proved to promote the growth and proliferation of TC stem cells.52.

Functional enrichment analysis of the C-D-T network targets demonstrated their involvement in diverse biological processes. These genes were particularly associated with oxidative stress response, reactive oxygen species metabolism, and cellular detoxification mechanisms. Additionally, they played regulatory roles in apoptotic pathways, including inhibition of both intrinsic and extrinsic programmed cell death signaling. Pathway analysis further identified significant enrichment in several oncogenic signaling cascades, most notably TNF, IL-17, p53, PI3K-Akt, and MAPK pathways, suggesting their potential involvement in tumorigenic processes. It has been reported that High IL-17 expression is associated with poor outcomes in TC.53 It was revealed that curcumin inhibited the invasion of TC cells via the down-regulation of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway.54 The results reported in the present research also indicated that HE might also exert its anti-tumor effects through these signaling pathways.

Strength and limitations

This investigation presents several notable strengths. First, we successfully identified seven promising candidate compounds through systematic screening, providing a focused direction for subsequent experimental validation. Second, our predictive analysis of HE-protein interactions and affected pathways offers valuable insights that will facilitate future experimental design. These methodological advantages significantly enhance the efficiency of follow-up mechanistic studies. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The research findings should be interpreted in the context of certain methodological constraints. Our analysis may not have captured all clinically relevant data due to the specific sub-methodological approaches employed. This approach led to underrepresentation regarding the number of studies, as well as some of the most recent developments in the field, which may have resulted from differences in the number of citations. In addition, we selected the CA-WoSCC as the sole data source for this research due to data comparability concerns with different bibliometric analysis tools. Therefore, despite our two-person screening approach, limited to the databases searched so potential interactions HE-TC could have been overlooked and were not included in this study. The GO level of evidence for each association gene may be limited and include wrong genes. Testing of the thresholds of interaction may have filtered out some possible important interaction, but interpretation becomes more challenging. Although the OB/DL standard is widely utilized, its threshold setting may present certain limitations that could result in the exclusion of some well-recognized compounds. For example, stringent criteria (such as OB ≥ 50 %) may overlook low-absorbing yet highly locally active components (e.g., specific glycosides). Conversely, more lenient standards (such as OB ≥ 10 %) might lead to false positives and an increase in analytical noise. Future research should focus on optimizing screening strategies through sensitivity analyses—such as gradually adjusting the OB/DL thresholds and assessing the overlap rate of core targets—or by integrating in vitro absorption/permeability experimental data (for instance, utilizing the Caco-2 model) to enhance decision-making processes. Although this study has predicted the potential targets and pathways through which HE may exert its effects on TC via systematic analysis, it is important to note that network pharmacology fundamentally relies on databases and algorithms for predictions. Consequently, the results obtained necessitate validation through subsequent experimental studies to confirm their biological activity. For instance, the accuracy of target and pathway predictions can be influenced by factors such as database coverage, algorithm parameters, or the false positive rate associated with compound-target interactions. Furthermore, the multi-component and multi-target nature of traditional Chinese medicine may result in discrepancies between predicted pathways and actual in vivo outcomes. Based on the findings of this study, future experiments should be conducted to assess both the anti-tumor efficacy of HE and its regulatory impact on the predicted pathways through in vivo and in vitro investigations. While our multi-database integration strategy aims to maximize coverage, our results could still be influenced by the known and evolving annotations within these databases. We also note that future work would benefit from incorporating additional specialized databases (e.g., DisGeNET, DrugBank) to further validate the findings.55, 56.

Conclusion

We have identified through bioinformatics approaches potential drug-protein interaction that may be benefitable in TC therapy. The results reported herein indicate that HE might exert its anti-TC functions through the functions of various active ingredients (such as quercetin, luteolin, and kaempferol) by targeting the core genes, including AKT1, MAPK1, JUN, and IL-6. The results revealed that HE could be potentially used for treating TC. Further studies should be conducted to validate these findings.
