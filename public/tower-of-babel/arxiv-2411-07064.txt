Rigorous enclosure of Lyapunov exponents of stochastic flows
Maxime Breden Hugo Chu Jeroen S.W. Lamb Martin Rasmussen

Abstract
We develop a powerful and general method to provide rigorous and accurate upper and lower bounds for Lyapunov exponents of stochastic flows. Our approach is based on computer-assisted tools, the adjoint method and established results on the ergodicity of diffusion processes. We do not require any structural assumptions on the stochastic system and work under mild hypoellipticity conditions and outside of perturbative regimes. Therefore, our method allows for the treatment of systems that were so far out of reach from existing mathematical tools. We demonstrate our method to exhibit the chaotic nature of four different systems. Finally, we show the robustness of our approach by combining it with continuation methods to produce bounds on Lyapunov exponents over large parameter regions.

Introduction

We consider stochastic flows
(\varphi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
generated by nonlinear stochastic differential equations of the form

\mathrm{d}\varphi_{t}(\omega,x)=X_{0}(\varphi_{t}(\omega,x))\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}X_{i}(\varphi_{t}(\omega,x))\circ\mathrm{d}B^{i}_{t}(\omega),\qquad\varphi_{0}(\omega,x)=x,

(1)

where
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{\ell}
denote analytic and complete vector fields on an analytic Riemannian manifold
(\mathcal{M},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, the
B^{i}
’s are independent standard Brownian motions over a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
and
\circ
denotes Stratonovich integration. A central object in the study of the dynamics of the stochastic flow
(\varphi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the (top) Lyapunov exponent

\lambda(\omega,x,v):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\|D\varphi_{t}(\omega,x)v\|.

(2)

Throughout this paper, we assume the ergodicity of the Markov process
(\varphi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, in the sense that
(\varphi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
possesses a unique stationary probability measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{M}
.
Under the existence and uniqueness of such an ergodic measure (and mild integrability conditions), it is well-established by the celebrated Osdelets multiplicative ergodic theorem
[
47
,
80
]
(see also
[
5
, Chapters 3 & 4]
) that the Lyapunov exponent
\lambda
is well-defined and constant for
(\mathbb{Pb}\times\mu)
-almost every
(\omega,x)\in\Omega\times\mathcal{M}
, Lebesgue-almost every
v\in\mathbf{P}(T_{x}\mathcal{M})
, the projective space of the tangent space
T_{x}\mathcal{M}
.

The Lyapunov exponent
\lambda
is an essential tool to characterise stochastic dynamics. It is an important ingredient in the proof of
synchronisation when
\lambda
is negative

[
13
,
45
]
and in the characterisation of various forms of
chaotic dynamics when
\lambda
is positive

[
72
,
75
]
. In particular, from (
2
), it can be seen that it is linked to sensitivity to initial conditions, which is a quintessential feature of chaos. Thus, obtaining rigorous bounds on the Lyapunov exponent is crucial to understanding the dynamics of
(\varphi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. This has been a long-standing fundamental problem, highlighted for instance by Kingman
[
69
]
: “Pride of place among the unsolved problems of subadditive ergodic theory must go to [its] calculation”. This question has already been studied extensively in
[
5
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
and in particular for stochastic flows
[
11
,
16
,
19
,
35
]
. However, most existing results are restricted to the small noise limit
X_{i}\to 0
(for
i\neq 0
) and to systems enjoying a particular structure, such as volume-preserving/incompressible/Hamiltonian stochastic flows
[
6
,
16
]
, for which establishing the positivity of the (top) Lyapunov exponent often follows from non-degeneracy conditions such as the Furstenberg criterion
[
48
]
(see
[
40
]
for a recent example), especially when the noise is small.

In this paper, we propose a computer-assisted method for
rigorously enclosing

\lambda
under mild conditions, which substantially reduces the problem of rigorously computing Lyapunov exponents for stochastic flows, and is in principle applicable to a wide range of low-dimensional Markovian random dynamical systems.

While computer-assisted methods have played an increasingly important role in the theory of dynamical systems
[
51
,
67
,
74
,
78
,
96
,
97
,
98
]
, the problem of rigorously enclosing Lyapunov exponents has thus far remained very challenging: in the more manageable discrete-time setting, a substantial body of work resolves this problem, including
[
84
]
for random products of matrices and
[
37
,
46
,
49
]
for iterated function systems (see also
[
50
,
85
,
100
]
in the deterministic setting). In the case of stochastic flows induced by stochastic differential equations of the type (
1
), the tackling of this problem with computer-assisted methods is more recent, with a first attempt in
[
31
]
that only applied to specific SDEs conditioned to a bounded domain, and the successful treatment of a class of chaotic systems in the small noise limit in
[
20
]
.

The approach proposed in this paper is much more general, but has the same starting point as several other studies, namely, the formulation of the top Lyapunov exponent
\lambda
as an ergodic average via the limit (
2
), the so-called Furstenberg–Khasminskii formula
[
34
,
48
,
68
]

\lambda=\int_{\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M}}Q\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mu},

(3)

where the integrand
Q
has an explicit formula in terms of the vector fields
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{\ell}
and is the mean infinitesimal growth rate of the linear flow along the so-called
projective process

(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(\varphi_{t},s_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M}=\bigcup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbf{P}_{x}\mathcal{M}
, where

s_{t}(\omega,x,v)=\frac{D\varphi_{t}(\omega,x)v}{\|D\varphi_{t}(\omega,x)v\|}\in\mathbf{P}_{\varphi_{t}(\omega,x)}\mathcal{M};

and
\tilde{\mu}(\mathrm{d}\xi)=\tilde{w}(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi
is the ergodic probability measure of the Markov process
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M}
, where
\mathrm{d}\xi
denotes the (Riemannian) volume measure on
\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M}
by abuse of notation.

Note that in general, the existence and uniqueness of such an invariant probability measure
\tilde{\mu}
is non-trivial, but sufficient conditions for this to hold can be found in the literature, e.g. in
[
88
]
. It is a control-theoretic problem associated to the stochastic differential equation solved by
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}

\mathrm{d}\xi_{t}=\tilde{X}_{0}(\xi_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\tilde{X}_{i}(\xi_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}B^{i}_{t},\qquad\xi_{0}=(x,v),

(4)

with vector fields
\tilde{X}_{0},\tilde{X_{1}},\ldots,\tilde{X}_{\ell}
on
\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M}
.

In all but a few cases (e.g.
[
10
,
44
]
), obtaining bounds or even only the sign for
\lambda
via the Furstenberg–Khashminskii formula (
3
) has so far proven extremely difficult. A common strategy (in both the deterministic and random settings
[
31
,
46
,
49
,
50
,
100
]
) is to try to enclose
\tilde{w}
rigorously and then integrate
Q
against
\tilde{w}
to obtain bounds on
\lambda
via (
3
). In the context of stochastic flows, we could try to make use of computer-assisted proof methods
[
55
,
79
,
86
,
97
]
to solve the stationary Fokker–Planck equation

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{*}\tilde{w}=0,

(5)

where
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
denotes the adjoint in
L^{2}(\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M})
of

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}=\tilde{X}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\tilde{X}_{i}^{2},

which is the infinitesimal generator of the process
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
written in Hörmander form. Such computer-assisted proof, if successful, would yield a precise and quantitative description of
\tilde{\omega}
, which would then allow to accurately enclose the integral (
3
) (see, e.g.,
[
31
]
). However, the rigorous general treatment of general partial differential equations (PDEs) of the form (
5
) by computer-assisted means is for now far out of reach due to the typically
non-elliptic
nature of
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
(and
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
). The gap to bridge is even greater when considering problems on an unbounded state space
\mathcal{M}

[
31
]
. This difficulty stems from the fact that both these aspects usually prevent the derivation of explicit a priori bounds to quantify errors on the computation of
\tilde{w}
. While there exist techniques
[
4
,
53
,
82
]
to prove the existence of a spectral gap for
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
(or
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
), these are typically non-constructive, and thus do not provide an explicit bound on this spectral gap. Even when explicit bounds are available, for instance in some elliptic cases, they can be very intricate and lead to unusable values in practice, typically depending exponentially on
\|\tilde{X}_{0}\|

[
27
,
28
]
. We will therefore depart from the usual strategy of estimating
\tilde{w}
and develop a more flexible approach.

Nonetheless, in certain special cases, some estimates for (
3
) can be achieved analytically in an asymptotic parameter regime e.g. as
X_{i}\to 0,\,i\neq 0

[
19
,
20
,
35
]
. In such a regime, one can, for instance, make use of the so-called
adjoint method
proposed by Arnold, Papanicolaou and Wihstutz
[
9
]
(see
[
11
,
17
]
for some recent examples).

In this paper, we combine the adjoint method with computer-assisted techniques
to obtain rigorous and tight bounds on Lyapunov exponents for general systems: we do not assume any asymptotic or perturbative regime, assuming only mild hypoellipticity conditions. Our method, which we now introduce in Section
1.1
, is applicable to unbounded domains and does not necessitate any specific structure on (
1
). We showcase this in Section
1.2
, which contains four examples of stochastic flows without specific structure or asymptotically small parameters, for which we establish the positivity of the (top) Lyapunov exponent.
The first three examples feature the phenomenon of noise-induced chaos, i.e. a transition from negative to positive Lyapunov exponent with increasing noise level (although the Lyapunov exponent may become negative again if the noise is too large), and the last two answer open conjectures.

Our method to rigorously enclose Lyapunov exponents

As already mentioned, working directly with the Furstenberg–Khashminskii formula (
3
) is often very difficult. The idea of the adjoint method introduced in
[
9
]
is to instead consider, for a well chosen
u
,

\int_{\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M}}\left(Q-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}u\right)\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mu}.

(6)

Indeed, since
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{*}\tilde{w}=0
, we formally get

\int_{\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M}}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}u\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mu}=\int_{\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M}}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}u(\xi)\tilde{w}(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi=\int_{\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M}}u(\xi)(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{*}\tilde{w}(\xi))\mathrm{d}\xi=0,

(7)

and this calculation can be made rigorous under suitable integrability and growth assumptions on
u
, see for instance Lemma
9
, which is based on
[
11
, Proposition B.1]
. Therefore, the (top) Lyapunov exponent
\lambda
is also given by (
6
), that is

\lambda=\int_{\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M}}\left(Q-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}u\right)\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mu},

(8)

and one can try to pick a function
u
for which the integrand
(Q-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}u)
is more approachable so that the right-hand side of (
8
) is easier to work with than the original Furstenberg–Khashminskii formula (
3
).
Ideally, one would like to find a function
u
and a constant
\Lambda
such that
Q-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}u=\Lambda
, i.e., such that
u
and
\Lambda
solve the
Poisson equation

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}u=Q-\Lambda,

(9)

because then we simply get
\lambda=\Lambda
from (
8
). However, solving the Poisson equation (
9
) is not necessarily easier than solving the stationary Fokker–Planck equation (
5
), and finding an explicit solution is in general not possible.

For some specific problems, in an asymptotic regime, ad hoc choices of
u
that make (
8
) more manageable than (
3
) can sometimes be found, and we again refer to
[
11
]
for a recent example where this idea is used several times. In contrast, in this work we propose a generic and robust way of using the adjoint method on a broad class of systems, which leads to quantitative and accurate estimates on the Lyapunov exponent.

The main idea is that, even if the “optimal”
u
(the one solving (
9
)) cannot be found explicitly, for many problems we can still find
numerically
an approximate solution
\bar{u}
, and then use this approximate solution in (
8
) in order to derive estimates on
\lambda
, which are going to be sharp if
\bar{u}
is an accurate approximate solution. Suppose for the moment that we have an approximation
\bar{\lambda}
of
\lambda
(e.g., via a Monte-Carlo method
[
56
,
70
,
91
]
), and that we have obtained an approximate solution
\bar{u}
to the Poisson problem (
9
), by solving numerically the equation

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\bar{u}\approx Q-\bar{\lambda}.

How to find suitable
\bar{\lambda}
and
\bar{u}
in practice will be discussed in Section
2
. Setting

\bar{Q}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\bar{u}+\bar{\lambda},

we expect to have
\bar{Q}\approx Q
. Furthermore, using
\bar{u}
in (
8
) we get

\displaystyle\lambda=\int_{\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M}}\left(Q-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\bar{u}\right)\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mu}=\int_{\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M}}\left(Q-\bar{Q}\right)\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mu}+\bar{\lambda},

hence

|\lambda-\bar{\lambda}|=\left|\int_{\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M}}(Q-\bar{Q})\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mu}\right|.

(10)

That is, we control the difference between the approximate Lyapunov exponent
\bar{\lambda}
(which we know explicitly) and the exact one
\lambda
(unknown), via the difference
Q-\bar{Q}
(where both terms are known).
Thus, the problem of enclosing
\lambda
has been reduced to the easier problem of bounding the integral in (
10
): while
\tilde{\mu}
is still unknown, various inequalities can be applied to show that this integral is small. The derivation of such a bound mostly depends on the nature of
Q
(and
\bar{Q}
) and
\mathcal{M}
and on a priori estimates on
\tilde{\mu}
. In this paper, our strategy is mainly based on directly applying
L^{\infty}
and
L^{1}
bounds globally. For instance, on a compact state space
\mathcal{M}
, the functions
Q
and
\bar{Q}
are typically
L^{\infty}
and

|\lambda-\bar{\lambda}|=\left|\int_{\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M}}(Q-\bar{Q})\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mu}\right|\leq\|Q-\bar{Q}\|_{\infty}\int_{\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mu}=\|Q-\bar{Q}\|_{\infty}.

(11)

Note that
\bar{Q}
is known explicitly in terms of
\bar{u}
and
\bar{\lambda}
, therefore there is in principle no difficulty in getting an explicit upper bound for
\|Q-\bar{Q}\|_{\infty}
. In practice, this can be done efficiently using rigorous numerics and interval arithmetic
[
95
]
, see Proposition
11
for more details. We emphasize that
\bar{u}
and
\bar{\lambda}
can and should be obtained using traditional numerics with floating-point arithmetic. The only steps that have to be carried out rigorously are the calculation of
\bar{Q}
, i.e., the application of
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
to
\bar{u}
, and then the estimation of
\|Q-\bar{Q}\|_{\infty}
. In contrast, even in specific situations in which one could have rigorously solved the stationary Fokker–Planck equation (
5
) or the Poisson equation (
9
) using a computer-assisted proof, this would be a computer-assisted proof for solving a PDE, for which the amount of rigorous numerics required would be much higher compared to the computer-assisted approach proposed here (see Remark
12
for more details). We also point out that our method does not only apply to the computation of top Lyapunov exponents but to any ergodic average of the form (
3
) where
Q
is given and explicit.

Estimate (
11
) is in some sense rather crude, as it does not seem to use any information on
\tilde{\mu}
, but this is in fact a strength of our approach. Indeed, obtaining quantitative information on
\tilde{\mu}
can often be very hard, and being able to rigorously enclose
\lambda
without knowing anything about
\tilde{\mu}
is part of what makes our method broadly applicable. Of course, if some information on
\tilde{\mu}
is available then it may be leveraged to obtain sharper estimates (see for instance Section
6
and
7
), but directly using the simple estimate (
11
) can already lead to very precise results such as the one in Theorem
1
below.

The above estimate (
11
) can be generalised to treat systems on a non-compact state space
\mathcal{M}
, where
Q
or
\bar{Q}
may not be in
L^{\infty}
, and one instead makes use of Foster–Lyapunov inequalities
[
33
,
57
,
77
]
to recover the necessary estimates (see Section
2.1
). Examples with both bounded and unbounded state spaces are provided below.

The adjoint method using an approximate solution

General framework

In this section, we lay out the main method used in this paper to enclose ergodic averages. Given a diffusion process
(x_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on a
\sigma
-compact manifold
\mathcal{X}
(i.e. the countable union of compact submanifolds) induced by a stochastic differential equation (SDE) with complete vector fields

\mathrm{d}x_{t}=V_{0}(x_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}V_{i}(x_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}B^{i}_{t},

(18)

assumed to have a unique stationary measure
\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=w(x)\mathrm{d}x
, and a
\mathcal{C}^{2}

\mu
-integrable function
q:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, our aim is to rigorously enclose the integral

\mathcal{I}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}q\mathrm{d}\mu.

(19)

This includes the problem of enclosing Lyapunov exponents discussed in the introduction, for which the diffusion process to be considered is the projective process given by (
4
) (see also Section
3.1
), but the method presented here applies to any ergodic average.

In order to study
\mathcal{I}
, we consider the operator

\mathcal{L}=V_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}V_{i}^{2}:\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathcal{X})\to\mathcal{C}^{0}(\mathcal{X}),

which is the (infinitesimal) generator of the solution process
(x_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfying Eq. (
18
). We aim to find
\bar{u}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathcal{X})
and a constant
\bar{\mathcal{I}}
such that

\mathcal{L}\bar{u}\approx q-\bar{\mathcal{I}}.

(20)

One may approach this problem by first approximating
\mathcal{I}
with a numerical scheme such as a Monte-Carlo method to produce a candidate
\bar{\mathcal{I}}\approx\mathcal{I}
, and then solve for
\bar{u}
. However, we show below that such a step is not necessary. Indeed, because
\int\mathcal{L}\bar{u}\mathrm{d}\mu=0
, for any
(\bar{u},\bar{\mathcal{I}})
satisfying (
20
) we automatically get
\bar{\mathcal{I}}\approx\mathcal{I}
. This allows us to first solve for
\bar{u}
only, and then recover
\bar{\mathcal{I}}
, which often leads to a much more precise approximation of
\mathcal{I}
. In this paper, we work under the following setting:

(S1)

We have a Hilbert (orthonormal) basis
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathcal{X})
for a judicious Hilbert space
\left(\mathcal{H},(\cdot,\cdot)\right)
such that
f_{0}=1
.

(S2)

For all
N\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
M\geq N
such that
\mathcal{L}
maps
P_{N}(\mathcal{H})=\mathrm{Span}\{f_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
to
P_{M}(\mathcal{H})=\mathrm{Span}\{f_{m}\}_{m=0}^{M}
, where
P_{n}
denotes the projection in
\mathcal{H}
onto
\mathrm{Span}\{f_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
.

(S3)

There is a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
positive function
W:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}:=(0,\infty)
such that

\sup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\left\|\frac{f_{m}}{W}\right\|_{\infty}<\infty,

and
\mu(W)<\infty
. Furthermore,
\mu(W)
and these suprema can be bounded explicitly.

(S4)

There exists a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
function
\tilde{W}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
such that
\mathcal{L}\tilde{W}\leq c\tilde{W}
for some
c<\infty
and that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

\frac{|f_{n}(x)|}{\tilde{W}(x)}\longrightarrow 0\qquad\mbox{as $|f_{n}(x)|\to\infty$}.

(S5)

There is
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
q\in\mathrm{Span}\{f_{n}\}_{n=0}^{n_{0}}
.

In practice, we take
W=\tilde{W}
, except for the Duffing–van der Pol example in Section
7
; and if
\mathcal{X}
is compact, we will simply take
W=\tilde{W}=1
.

In the heuristic description of our approach in Section
1.1
, we heavily used the fact that elements in the range of
\mathcal{L}
have
\mu
-mean equal to zero. The following lemma, which is a direct rewriting of
[
11
, Proposition B.1]
adapted to our framework, gives sufficient conditions allowing the use of this fact rigorously.

Lemma 9
.

Under assumptions
(S1)
–
(S4)
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\int_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathcal{L}f_{n})\mathrm{d}\mu=0.

(21)

Thus, by linearity, for any
u\in P_{N}(\mathcal{H})
,

\int_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathcal{L}u)\mathrm{d}\mu=0.

Proof.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
, it is clear that under assumptions
(S2)
and
(S3)
,
f_{n}
and
\mathcal{L}f_{n}
are
\mu
-integrable. These two integrability conditions together with assumption
(S4)
and the fact that
f_{n}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
imply (
21
) by
[
11
, Proposition B.1]
.
∎

The problem (
20
) is then expressed and solved numerically with respect to the Hilbert basis
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, i.e. we represent the action of
\mathcal{L}:P_{N}(\mathcal{H})\to P_{M}(\mathcal{H})
by its Galerkin representation, the finite-dimensional matrix

\left(f_{m},\mathcal{L}f_{n}\right)_{m\leq M,n\leq N},

and all the subsequent operations are equivalent to finite-dimensional linear algebra: we find
\bar{u}
, a
numerical
solution of the least-square problem

\underset{u\in P_{N}(\mathcal{H})}{\operatorname{argmin}}\|P_{0}^{\perp}(\mathcal{L}u-q)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}},

(22)

or of the linear problem

(P_{N}P_{0}^{\perp}\mathcal{L}P_{N}P_{0}^{\perp})u=P_{0}^{\perp}q.

In our experience, the first approach gives slightly better results. Note that this is a purely numerical guess; there is in general no theoretical guarantee or indication for this procedure to converge quickly as
N\to\infty
. For the main problems considered in this work, the usual compactness estimates do not hold and convergence is typically not of spectral nature
[
93
]
. The argument presented below will only provide a tight enclosure of
\lambda
if the approximate solution
\bar{u}
is accurate enough, but we stress that we do not need any a priori estimate regarding the quality of
\bar{u}
.

Remark 10
.

For very high-dimensional sparse systems, it will in general prove better (for instance in terms of memory requirements) to make use of iterative methods to solve truncated or least-square versions of this problem
[
81
,
94
]
.

We now assume that an approximate solution
\bar{u}\in P_{N}(\mathcal{H})
of (
20
) has been computed for some
N
. In practice, we do so via (
22
), but we again emphasize that the way such a
\bar{u}
is actually obtained is irrelevant for the estimates to come. In particular, none of the above computations need to have been performed rigorously. We are now ready give a fully justified and more detailed version of method presented in Section
1.1
.

Proposition 11
.

Assume that the SDE (
18
) having complete vector fields and posed on a
\sigma
-compact manifold
\mathcal{X}
has a unique stationary measure
\mu
. Let
q:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
a
\mathcal{C}^{2}

\mu
-integrable function, and
\mathcal{I}
be the integral (
19
). Assume
(S1)
–
(S5)
, and let
\bar{u}\in P_{N}(\mathcal{H})
for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Let

\bar{\mathcal{I}}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}P_{0}(q-\mathcal{L}\bar{u}),

(23)

and

\bar{q}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\mathcal{L}\bar{u}+\bar{\mathcal{I}}.

(24)

Then

\mathcal{I}-\bar{\mathcal{I}}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}(q-\bar{q})\mathrm{d}\mu.

(25)

Moreover, denoting by
\{\epsilon_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
the coefficients of
q-\bar{q}
with respect to the basis
\{f_{m}\}_{m\geq 0}
, i.e.

q-\bar{q}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\epsilon_{m}f_{m},

(26)

we have the estimate

|\mathcal{I}-\bar{\mathcal{I}}|\leq\mu(W)\sum_{m=1}^{M}|\epsilon_{m}|\left\|\frac{f_{m}}{W}\right\|_{\infty}.

(27)

Proof.

By Lemma
9
we have
\int_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathcal{L}\bar{u})\mathrm{d}\mu=0
, and integrating (
24
) immediately yields (
25
). Then, thanks to assumptions
(S2)
and
(S5)
,
q-\bar{q}
belongs to
P_{M}(\mathcal{H})
for some
M\in\mathbb{N}
, hence
q-\bar{q}
can be written as
\sum_{m=0}^{M}\epsilon_{m}f_{m}
. We note that, as claimed in (
26
), the first coefficient
\epsilon_{0}
is in fact
0
, because (
23
) and (
24
) imply
q-\bar{q}=P_{0}^{\perp}(q-\mathcal{L}\bar{u})
. We then simply estimate

|\mathcal{I}-\bar{\mathcal{I}}|=\left|\int_{\mathcal{X}}(q-\bar{q})\mathrm{d}\mu\right|\leq\mu(W)\left\|\frac{(q-\bar{q})}{W}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\mu(W)\sum_{m=1}^{M}|\epsilon_{m}|\left\|\frac{f_{m}}{W}\right\|_{\infty}.\qed

Estimate (
27
) gives us an enclosure of
\mathcal{I}
, which is expected to be tight if
\bar{u}
is an accurate approximation of the solution
u
of the Poisson equation
\mathcal{L}u=q-\mathcal{I}
. Moreover, the right hand side of (
27
) can be explicitly and rigorously bounded. This only requires

•

Rigorously applying
\mathcal{L}
to the approximate solution
\bar{u}
in order to obtain
\mathcal{\bar{I}}
and
\bar{q}
in (
23
)-(
24
), which then yields the coefficients
\epsilon_{m}
,

•

Getting computable upper-bounds for
\mu(W)
and for
\left\|\frac{f_{m}}{W}\right\|_{\infty}
, for finitely many
m
’s.

For instance, if we use Fourier series and
W=1
(as will be the case in some examples), we simply have
\left\|\frac{f_{m}}{W}\right\|_{\infty}=1
for all
m
. When using more involved bases, we get explicit bounds on
\left\|\frac{f_{m}}{W}\right\|_{\infty}
using interval arithmetic together with some elementary estimates. In this work, we use the Julia package
IntervalArithmetic.jl

[
89
]
to perform our rigorous calculations, and the required estimates are discussed case by case in the example sections.

Remark 12
.

The evaluation of
\bar{q}
(
24
) which is the most expensive calculation in interval arithmetic is only a matrix-vector multiplication. This is in contrast with most computer-assisted proofs for ODEs or PDEs, which usually at least require a matrix-matrix multiplication, the inversion of a matrix, the resolution of a linear system or many matrix-vector multiplications in rigorous arithmetic. That allows for the treatment of problems with a much higher truncation dimension
N
which are especially necessary for non-elliptic problems on an unbounded multi-dimensional domain
\mathcal{X}
. Furthermore (at least for the rigorous part of the proof), provided
\mathcal{L}
has a sparse representation with respect to
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, this approach is not so limited by memory resources (which typically restrict computer-assisted proofs), as we can take full advantage of the sparsity of
\mathcal{L}
.

Remark 13
.

The main reason for the need for a weight function
W
, is that for our class of problems which are analytic, the Hilbert basis
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
q
are typically of polynomial type and thus unbounded on an unbounded domain. In this case,
W(x)
needs to grow exponentially or superexponentially as
|x|\to\infty
. Note that the existence of such weight or Lyapunov function is a standard ingredient for the proof of ergodicity of
(x_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. When choosing
W
, there is of course a trade-off between the values of
\|f_{m}/W\|_{\infty}
and
\mu(W)
.

Remark 14
.

Some of the assumptions of our setting can be loosened. For instance, instead of
(S5)
, it is sufficient for
q
to be analytic and thus have geometrically decaying coefficients with respect to our Hilbert basis, but this would require an additional bound for the tail of this series. We could also choose to express the problem with respect to two different bases for arguments and images of
\mathcal{L}
. Moreover, working with a Hilbert basis for the domain of
\mathcal{L}
is not strictly necessary, as will be showcased in Section
7.2
. More generally, we stress that the setting presented in this section is only one approach among others that one could take for the rigorous enclosure of ergodic averages via the adjoint method. The present approach is most likely the simplest one but may not be suitable in every situation (e.g. if
w
is not exponentially decaying on an unbounded domain
\mathcal{M}
).

Example: The average Lyapunov exponent for the cellular flow with sinks

Consider the cellular flow with sinks
(\varphi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on the two-torus
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{T}^{2}
generated by the differential equation

\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}x_{t}&=(\cos(x_{t})/2-\cos y_{t})\sin x_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B^{1}_{t}\\
\mathrm{d}y_{t}&=(\cos(y_{t})/2+\cos x_{t})\sin y_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B^{2}_{t}\end{cases}

where
\mathbb{T}^{2}=(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{2}\simeq[0,2\pi)^{2}
is endowed with the Riemannian structure inherited from
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(throughout this paper, the Riemannian structure on
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
will always be inherited from
\mathbb{R}
). Since the noise is additive and the state space
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{T}^{2}
is compact, the Markov process
(\varphi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is clearly ergodic with unique stationary measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. This system is a modification of the classical Hamiltonian cellular flow
[
32
]
, and thus the incompressibility of the flow is lost. For this variant, one actually expects volume contraction along trajectories (see Figure
1(a)
). This volume growth rate is given by the so-called
average Lyapunov exponent

\lambda_{\Sigma}(\omega,x)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{2t}\log\det{D\varphi_{t}(\omega,x)}.

Before turning our attention to the actual Lyapunov exponent
\lambda
for this system in Section
4
, we first study here the simpler case of the average Lyapunov exponent, which provides an easy example to illustrate our method. By Liouville’s formula
[
5
, Theorem 2.3.32]
,

\det(D\varphi_{t}(\omega,x))=\exp\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{tr}D(X_{0}(\varphi_{\tau}))\mathrm{d}\tau=\exp\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\cos 2x_{\tau}+\cos 2y_{\tau}}{2}\mathrm{d}\tau.

Then, by Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem,
\lambda_{\Sigma}
is constant
(\mathbb{P}\times\mu)
-almost surely and

\lambda_{\Sigma}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{2t}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\cos 2x_{\tau}+\cos 2y_{\tau}}{2}\mathrm{d}\tau=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\frac{\cos 2x+\cos 2y}{4}\mu(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y).

We thus aim to find numerical solutions
\bar{u}
and
\bar{\lambda}_{\Sigma}
to the Poisson problem

\mathcal{L}u=\left(\frac{\cos x}{2}-\cos y\right)\partial_{x}u+\left(\frac{\cos y}{2}+\cos x\right)\partial_{y}u+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\left(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2}\right)u=\frac{\cos 2x+\cos 2y}{4}-\lambda_{\Sigma}=q(x,y)-\lambda_{\Sigma}.

(28)

We choose the basis
\{f_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}=\{\cos mx,\sin mx\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\otimes\{\cos ny,\sin ny\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, fix
\sigma=\sqrt{2}
(with
W=\tilde{W}=1
), and in the file
cellular/proof_volume
at
[
38
]
we construct

\bar{u}(x,y)=\sum_{m=0}^{50}\sum_{n=0}^{50}a_{mn}\cos mx\cos ny+b_{mn}\cos mx\sin ny+c_{mn}\sin mx\cos ny+d_{mn}\sin mx\sin ny,

represented in Figure
5
.

Figure 5
:
Plot of the numerical solution
\bar{u}
to Eq. (
28
) with
\sigma=\sqrt{2}
.

Remark 15
.

Of course, one should normally try to reduce the dimension of the numerical problem by exploiting the symmetries of the system. For instance, in this case, one can observe in Figure
5
that

\bar{u}(x,y)=\sum_{m=1}^{25}\sum_{n=1}^{25}a_{2m,2n}\cos{2mx}\cos{2ny}.

This is of importance when solving large linear systems.

We then choose

\bar{\lambda}_{\Sigma}:=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(q-\mathcal{L}\bar{u})\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y=-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(\mathcal{L}\bar{u})\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,

i.e., as in Eq. (
24
),
\bar{\lambda}_{\Sigma}
is the projection of
q-\mathcal{L}\bar{u}
on constants and

\bar{q}:=\mathcal{L}\bar{u}+\bar{\lambda}_{\Sigma}.

Since

\bar{\lambda}_{\Sigma}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\bar{q}\mathrm{d}\mu,

we thus have that

|\bar{\lambda}_{\Sigma}-\lambda_{\Sigma}|=\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(q-\bar{q})\mathrm{d}\mu\right|\leq\|q-\bar{q}\|_{\infty}.

Thus, by bounding the right-hand side, we find that

\lambda_{\Sigma}=-0.0308582892201142\pm 5\times 10^{-16}<0,

(29)

confirming that the stochastic flow
(\varphi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is volume contracting. Note that
q
is a trigonometric polynomial, and that we constructed
\bar{u}
as a trigonometric polynomial. Therefore,
\bar{q}=\mathcal{L}\bar{u}+\bar{\lambda}_{\Sigma}
is also a trigonometric polynomial, and explicitly bounding
\|q-\bar{q}\|_{\infty}
is straightforward (for instance using the
\ell^{1}
-norm of the Fourier coefficients).

Of course, this particular problem could instead also be treated with more traditional computer-assisted methods by computing the stationary density
w
via the stationary Fokker–Planck equation
\mathcal{L}^{*}w=0
which is a uniformly elliptic equation on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}

[
3
,
62
,
79
]
. However, as already mentioned, our method requires significantly fewer computations than what would be needed for rigorously solving the stationary Fokker–Planck equation. But most importantly, our method does not make any assumption on the ellipticity of the operator
\mathcal{L}
or on the compactness of the domain and can thus treat a much wider class of examples. This is for instance the case of the main problem of this paper: the computations of Lyapunov exponents.

Lyapunov exponents via the projective process

In this section, we first recall how the Furstenberg–Khasminskii formula can be derived, and then give sufficient conditions for proving the ergodicity of the projective process.

The Furstenberg–Khasminskii formula

For the sake of presentation, let us consider first the additive noise case, i.e.
X_{i}=\sigma_{i}e_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
for some
\sigma_{i}\geq 0
where
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{T}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
(with
d=d_{1}+d_{2}
). We then have that

\mathrm{d}\varphi_{t}=X_{0}(\varphi_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}e_{i}\mathrm{d}B^{i}_{t}.

Then the Furstenberg–Khasminskii formula can be derived as follows.
For
v\in\mathbf{P}_{x}\mathcal{M}
, we define

\varrho_{t}(\omega,x,v)=\|D\varphi_{t}(\omega,x)v\|,\qquad s_{t}(\omega,x,v)=\frac{D\varphi_{t}(\omega,x)v}{\|D\varphi_{t}(\omega,x)v\|}\in T_{\varphi_{t}(\omega,x)}\mathcal{M}.

(30)

Then
(s_{t})_{t\geq 0}
solves the random differential equation (a nonautonomous ordinary differential equation)

\frac{\mathrm{d}s_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=DX_{0}(\varphi_{t})s_{t}-Q(\varphi_{t},s_{t})s_{t},

(31)

where
Q(x,s)=\langle DX_{0}(x)s,s\rangle
and similarly

\frac{\mathrm{d}\log\varrho_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=Q(\varphi_{t},s_{t})\quad\text{and}\quad\varrho_{t}=\exp\int_{0}^{t}Q(\varphi_{\tau},s_{\tau})\mathrm{d}\tau.

Therefore,
if
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(\varphi_{t},s_{t})_{t\geq 0}
has a unique invariant probability measure
\tilde{\mu}
and
Q\in L^{1}(\tilde{\mu})
, by Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem, we obtain the Furstenberg–Khasminskii formula

\lambda=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\varrho_{t}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}Q(\xi_{\tau})\mathrm{d}\tau=\int_{\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M}}Q\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mu}.

In the multiplicative noise case,

\mathrm{d}\varphi_{t}=X_{0}(\varphi_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}X_{i}(\varphi_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}B^{i}_{t},\qquad\varphi_{0}=x,

the evolution equation of
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
has the form

\mathrm{d}\xi_{t}=\tilde{X}_{0}(\xi_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\tilde{X}_{i}(\xi_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}B^{i}_{t}\qquad\xi_{0}=(x,v),

where the vector fields
\tilde{X}_{0},\tilde{X}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{X}_{\ell}
on
\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M}
are given by
[
34
]
, which also gives a formula for the function
Q
in the multiplicative noise case,
such that

\mathrm{d}\log\varrho_{t}=Q(\varphi_{t},s_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\langle DX_{i}(\varphi_{t})s_{t},s_{t}\rangle\mathrm{d}B^{i}_{t};

where the above equation is now written in Itô form. Provided

\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\langle DX_{i}(\varphi_{\tau})s_{\tau},s_{\tau}\rangle\mathrm{d}B^{i}_{\tau}\xlongrightarrow{t\to\infty}0,\qquad i=1,\ldots,\ell,

(32)

almost surely or in expectation with respect to
\mathbb{P}\times\tilde{\mu}
, then as before, by Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem

\lambda=\int_{\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M}}Q\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mu}.

The limit (
32
) holds in expectation for instance if

\int_{\mathcal{M}}\|DX_{i}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu<\infty,\qquad i=1,\ldots,\ell,

(33)

which is a standard integrability condition for the multiplicative ergodic theorem
[
5
, Theorem 4.2.13]
. Indeed, under such integrability condition, a simple application of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the Itô isometry yields that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\int_{\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{0}^{t}\langle DX_{i}(\varphi_{\tau})s_{\tau},s_{\tau}\rangle\mathrm{d}B^{i}_{\tau}\right|\right]\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mu}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left\|DX_{i}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu\right)^{1/2}\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}0.

This implies that the limit (
32
) holds in expectation.

Remark 16
.

Lower Lyapunov exponents (see
[
8
, Chapter 3]
for a definition) can be obtained similarly
[
15
]
by generalising the projective process to a Grassmannian process. There is in principle no obstruction for our method to be applied to compute these additional Lyapunov exponents but, in this paper, we mostly restrict ourselves to the top Lyapunov exponent for the sake of clarity. In particular, the bottom Lyapunov exponents can be obtained with a system similar and of the same dimension as the one studied in this work for the (top) Lyapunov exponent.

On the ergodicity of the projective process

Let us now address the question of the existence and uniqueness of a measure
\tilde{\mu}
for the projective process
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. To this end, let us recall that the Lie bracket
[U,V]
between two vector fields
U
and
V
is the vector field given by

[U,V](x)=DV(x)U(x)-DU(x)V(x).

We can now state the following nondegeneracy conditions.

Definition 17
.

Consider a stochastic differential equation written in Stratonovich form

\mathrm{d}x_{t}=V_{0}(x_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}V_{i}(x_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}B^{i}_{t}

(34)

with vector fields
V_{0},V_{1},\ldots,V_{\ell}
on a manifold
\mathcal{X}
. Then (
34
) is said to satisfy

(WH)

the
weak
(or elliptic) Hörmander condition if
L(x)
spans
T_{x}\mathcal{X}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
where
L=LA(V_{0},V_{1},\ldots,V_{\ell})
denotes the Lie algebra generated by
V_{0},V_{1},\ldots,V_{\ell}
.

(PH)

the
parabolic
Hörmander condition if
L_{0}(x)
spans
T_{x}\mathcal{X}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
where
L_{0}
denotes the ideal generated by
V_{1},\ldots,V_{\ell}
in
L
.

(SH)

the
strong
Hörmander if
LA(V_{1},\ldots,V_{\ell})
spans
T_{x}\mathcal{X}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}.

These conditions are typically checked by iterating the Lie Brackets

\left[\,\left[\ldots\left[V_{i_{1}},V_{i_{2}}\right],\ldots\right],V_{i_{n}}\right](x)

until they span
T_{x}\mathcal{X}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, where the allowed indices
i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}
depend on the condition to be checked. These conditions may be verified by hand e.g. in Section
5
or by computer assistance e.g. in Section
4
.

Note that clearly
\ref{(SH)}\implies\ref{(PH)}\implies\ref{(WH)}
, however only in some rare cases, does
(WH)
hold without
(PH)
(see
[
63
]
and its supplements and corrections). While the parabolic Hörmander
(PH)
condition implies that the Markov process
(x_{t})_{t\geq 0}
has smooth transition probabilities and is strong Feller
[
59
, Theorem 1.3]
, the weak Hörmander condition
(WH)
implies the smoothness of stationary densities.

If a diffusion satisfies the strong Hörmander condition
(SH)
, it is usually more straightforward to prove the existence and uniqueness of a stationary density
[
7
]
.
If a diffusion does not satisfy the strong Hörmander condition
(SH)
, it is said to be degenerate. If it only satisfies the parabolic Hörmander condition
(PH)
(i.e. is strong Feller) coupled with a recurrence/Lyapunov condition, then in general, one can only conclude the existence of a stationary measure
[
33
, Section 6]
. In order to obtain uniqueness, one typically has to prove the controllability or topological irreducibility of the Markov process
(x_{t})_{t\geq 0}
via the Stroock–Varadhan theorem
[
58
,
90
]
. This last step is not needed when the diffusion satisfies the strong Hörmander condition
(SH)
, as the controllability is automatic in that case.

Unfortunately, in the case of the projective process
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(\varphi_{t},s_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, the strong Hörmander condition
(SH)
is typically not satisfied. This is for instance clear in the additive noise case (see Eq. (
31
)). One then normally has to check for the controllability of
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, in addition of the parabolic Hörmander condition
(PH)
for
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. While controllability is not so hard to check on a case-by-case basis, it can sometimes be fiddly and cumbersome. Fortunately,
San Martin and Arnold
[
88
]
give weak and general conditions for the controllability and hence uniqueness of a stationary measure for
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{M})
to hold; we state their result below.

First recall that the evolution equations of
(\varphi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
are respectively given by

\mathrm{d}\varphi_{t}=X_{0}(\varphi_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}X_{i}(\varphi_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}B^{i}_{t}(\omega),\qquad\varphi_{0}=x,

(35)

and

\mathrm{d}\xi_{t}=\tilde{X}_{0}(\xi_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\tilde{X}_{i}(\xi_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}B^{i}_{t},\qquad\xi_{0}=(x,v),

(36)

where
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{\ell}
(and thus
\tilde{X}_{0},\tilde{X}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{X}_{\ell}
) are
analytic
and complete vector fields. Then
[
88
]
asserts that if
(\varphi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
possesses a unique ergodic probability measure on
\mathcal{M}
, then it is sufficient for
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to merely fulfil the weak Hörmander condition
(WH)
for it to possess a unique ergodic probability measure on
\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M}
.

Proposition 18
.

Recall that
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{\ell}
are

and complete vector fields on
\mathcal{M}

satisfies the weak Hörmander condition

\mathbf{P}\mathcal{M}

[

which give the uniqueness of an invariant control set

Assume that the Markov process

\mathcal{M}

The randomly forced pendulum

In this section, we discuss the example presented in Theorem
3
, namely the randomly forced pendulum (or Josephson junction
[
83
]
)

``\;\ddot{x}_{t}=-\kappa\sin x_{t}-\gamma\dot{x}_{t}+\sigma\dot{B}_{t}\;"

with gravitational acceleration
\kappa>0
, friction coefficient
\gamma>0
and noise strength
\sigma>0
. It translates to the proper stochastic differential equation

\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}x_{t}&=y_{t}\mathrm{d}t\\
\mathrm{d}y_{t}&=-(\kappa\sin x_{t}+\gamma y_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t}\end{cases}

(44)

on
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
. Since this differential equation has
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-vector fields with bounded derivatives of all orders, it clearly induces a stochastic flow of diffeomorphisms
(\varphi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
(see
[
71
, Chapter II.4]
).

While this system is the stochastic version of a classical ordinary differential equation and also typically appears in applications related to superconductors
[
66
]
, its study from a mathematically rigorous point of view is so far limited.

Analogously to the previous example, this system can also be seen as an attracting version of a Hamiltonian pendulum (when the friction
\gamma=0
). We are thus interested in the possible chaotic behaviour of the system when
\sigma>0
. Note however that the mechanisms inducive of chaos for the randomly forced pendulum are not so clear: From Theorem
3
, chaos appears as a consequence of the large noise
\sigma
in the
y
-direction. However, this allows trajectories to spend more time away from the
x
-axis, where most of the dynamics seem to be happening from the deterministic point of view (see Figure
2(a)
) and does not a priori push trajectories into more expanding regions of the system. The large noise may though act as a catalyst for a stretch-and-fold mechanism when the system crosses the
x
-axis (see Figure
2(b)
).

Ergodicity of the projective process

In this section, we verify the ergodicity of the projective process. We achieve this by following Section
3.2
and first proving the ergodicity of the base process
(\varphi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
(Steps 1–3) and then showing that the projective process
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(\varphi_{t},s_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfies the weak Hörmander condition
(WH)
. As usual
[
39
,
57
]

(PH)
and showing the controllability of
(\varphi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}

Step 1

(Choice of a Lyapunov function)
.

Recall that here the generator of the process
(\varphi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is given by

\mathcal{L}=y\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-(\kappa\sin x+\gamma y)\frac{\partial}{\partial y}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{2}}=y\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-\kappa\sin x\frac{\partial}{\partial y}+L_{y},

where

L_{y}=-\gamma y\frac{\partial}{\partial y}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{2}}

(45)

is the generator of an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with stationary density
e^{-\gamma y^{2}/\sigma^{2}}

[
83
]
. Thus a natural choice for a Lyapunov function is
W(x,y)=e^{\gamma y^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}
; we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}W(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{2\sigma^{2}}(-\gamma y^{2}+\sigma^{2}-2\kappa y\sin x)W(x,y)

\displaystyle=-cW(x,y)+\left[\frac{\gamma}{2\sigma^{2}}(-\gamma y^{2}+\sigma^{2}-2\kappa y\sin x)+c\right]e^{\gamma y^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq-cW(x,y)+d\mathds{1}_{C},

\displaystyle:=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}\,\Big|\,\frac{\gamma}{2\sigma^{2}}(-\gamma y^{2}+\sigma^{2}+2\kappa|y|)+c>0\right\}

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle:=\sup_{y\in C}\left[\frac{\gamma}{2\sigma^{2}}(-\gamma y^{2}+\sigma^{2}+2\kappa|y|)+c\right]e^{\gamma y^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}.

(Parabolic Hörmander condition

.

We have that

\end{matrix}\right),\qquad X_{2}(x,y,\theta)=\left(\begin{matrix}0\\

\end{matrix}\right),

.

First note that from (

.

It now remains to be shown that the projective process

The Hopf normal form

We now come back to the system studied in Theorem
4
, namely the Hopf normal form with additive noise

\mathrm{d}\left(\begin{matrix}x_{t}\\
y_{t}\\
\end{matrix}\right)=\left[\left(\begin{matrix}\alpha&-\beta\\
\beta&\alpha\\
\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}x_{t}\\

\end{matrix}\right)-\left(\begin{matrix}a&b\\
-b&a\\
\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}x_{t}\\
y_{t}\\
\end{matrix}\right)(x_{t}^{2}+y_{t}^{2})\right]\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}\left(\begin{matrix}B^{1}_{t}\\
B^{2}_{t}\\
\end{matrix}\right).

(49)

This system was first introduced in
[
41
]
in the context of random dynamical systems as a simple perturbation of the deterministic Hopf normal form. See also
[
42
,
92
,
99
]
for applications to ocean and laser dynamics. This system has been conjectured (see also
[
43
]
) to exhibit the

scenario proposed in
[

]
, for which the perturbation of a limit cycle induces a stretch-and-fold mechanism leading to chaos. This conjecture can be quantitatively translated into proving a transition from a negative to positive Lyapunov exponent
\lambda_{b}
as
b
increases. This problem was already attacked with computer-assisted techniques in
[
31
]
, but this work only managed to establish such a transition for the
conditioned
Lyapunov exponent, which in particular meant restricting the problem to a bounded domain. In contrast, the method presented in this paper allows to deal with the full problem on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, while being much simpler than the rather involved computer-assisted techniques used in
[
31
]
. Recently, Baxendale
[
11
]
, and Chemnitz and Engel
[
35
]
did prove that the Lyapunov exponent
\lambda_{b}
associated to (

) becomes positive in the asymptotic regime

. In this work, we tackle the opposite (and complementary) problem for
b
small, and prove that
\lambda_{b}>0
for an explicit parameter range of finite and explicit values of

, while also enclosing the critical value of

at which the transition from negative to positive takes place. Note that Baxendale
[
11
]

also made use of the adjoint method in his proof, though in a quite different fashion.

Derivation of the Furstenberg–Khasminskii formula

As in
[
11
,
35
,
41
,
43
]
, we prepare the problem by reducing the formula for
\lambda
to an integral over the ergodic measure

of a lower-dimensional and uniformly elliptic diffusion.

In polar coordinates
x_{t}=r_{t}\cos\phi_{t},y_{t}=r_{t}\sin\phi_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}r_{t}

=\left(\alpha r_{t}-ar_{t}^{3}+\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2r_{t}}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B^{r}_{t}

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\phi_{t}

=(\beta+br^{2}_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\dfrac{\sigma}{r_{t}}\mathrm{d}B^{\phi}_{t}

(50)

where

are independent Brownian motions

\mathrm{d}\left(\begin{matrix}B^{r}_{t}\\
B^{\phi}_{t}\\
\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\cos\phi_{t}&\sin\phi_{t}\\
-\sin\phi_{t}&\cos\phi_{t}\\
\end{matrix}\right)\mathrm{d}\left(\begin{matrix}B^{1}_{t}\\
B^{2}_{t}\\
\end{matrix}\right).

Taking
(\varrho_{t},s_{t})_{t\geq 0}
as in (
30
) and similarly as in the previous derivations, posing

we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\varrho_{t}

=\left(\alpha-2ar_{t}^{2}+\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}r^{2}_{t}\sin(2\theta_{t}-\chi_{0}-2\phi_{t})\right)\varrho_{t}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\theta_{t}

=\left(\beta+2br_{t}^{2}+\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}r^{2}_{t}\cos(2\theta_{t}-\chi_{0}-2\phi_{t})\right)\mathrm{d}t

(51)

where
\chi_{0}=\arccos(b/\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}})
. Now, letting
\psi_{t}=\theta_{t}-\chi_{0}/2-\phi_{t}
and substracting (
51
) by (
50
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}r_{t}

=\left(\alpha r_{t}-ar_{t}^{3}+\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2r_{t}}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B^{r}_{t}

(52)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\psi_{t}

=\left(br_{t}^{2}+\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}r_{t}^{2}\cos(2\psi_{t})\right)\mathrm{d}t-\dfrac{\sigma}{r_{t}}\mathrm{d}B^{\phi}_{t}

(53)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\varrho_{t}

=\left(\alpha-2ar_{t}^{2}+\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}r^{2}_{t}\sin(2\psi_{t})\right)\varrho_{t}\mathrm{d}t

Thus,

\lambda=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}}Q(r,\psi)\mu(\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}\psi),

where
\mu
is the unique stationary distribution of
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(r_{t},\psi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
given by Eq. (
52
–
53

Note that this is a slightly different formulation than in

]
which factors in an additional symmetry. Our formulation turns out to be more practical for numerical purposes as it allows us to recover the polar coordinate Laplacian while still exploiting this symmetry. Indeed, we get that the generator of

is

Moreover, note that since the generator

of

Continuation with respect to the shear
b

We now show how our method can be combined with continuation techniques recently developed in
[
29
]
, which we implement in the file
Hopf/continuation
at
[
38
]
. We now want to enclose the parameter-dependent Lyapunov exponent

\lambda_{b}=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}}Q_{b}\mathrm{d}\mu_{b}=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}}\left(\alpha-2ar^{2}+\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}r^{2}\sin 2\psi\right)\mu_{b}(\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}\psi),

for all
b
in a parameter range
[b_{-},b_{+}]
. We first define an approximate solution
\bar{u}_{b}
for the Poisson problem
\mathcal{L}_{b}u_{b}=Q_{b}-\lambda_{b}
(where these are all now parameter-dependent) for all
b\in[b_{-},b_{+}]
of the form

\bar{u}_{b}(r,\psi)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\sum_{m,n}c_{k,m,n}T_{k}(b)h^{m}_{n}(r,\psi),

where
T_{k}
denotes the Cheybyshev polynomial of the first kind of degree
k
on
[b_{-},b_{+}]
. This is done by computing approximate solutions for
K+1
values of
b
in
[b_{-},b_{+}]
(taken at the Chebyshev nodes) and interpolating them.

We then need to rigorously apply
\mathcal{L}_{b}
to
\bar{u}_{b}
, for
b\in[b_{-},b_{+}]
. This could be done
exactly
, i.e.
\mathcal{L}_{b}\bar{u}_{b}
would have a finite expansion in the (Hermite–Laguerre)–Chebyshev basis, except for the fact that
Q_{b}
is not polynomial in
b
. We therefore first build a degree
K
polynomial
p
(in the Chebyshev basis) approximating
\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}
and compute an error bound
\varepsilon
such that

\sup_{b\in[b_{-},b_{+}]}\left|p(b)-\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}\right|<\varepsilon.

We then denote
\hat{Q}_{b}:=\alpha-2ar^{2}+p(b)r^{2}\sin 2\psi,

which is now polynomial in
b
and should approximate
Q_{b}
well in
b
, and

\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{b}:=(\alpha r-ar^{3})\partial_{r}+\left(br^{2}+p(b)r^{2}\cos 2\psi\right)\partial_{\psi}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta.

We now have that
\hat{\mathcal{L}}\bar{u}_{b}-\hat{Q}_{b}
is polynomial of degree
2K
in
b
and given that it is written in terms of Chebyshev coefficients, it is straightforward to give an upper bound on

\sup_{b\in[b_{-},b_{+}]}\|(\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{b}\bar{u}_{b}-\hat{Q}_{b}+\bar{\lambda}_{b})/W\|_{\infty},

where
\bar{\lambda}_{b}=P_{0}(\hat{Q}_{b}-\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{b}\bar{u}_{b})
.

We now incorporate the errors coming from approximating
Q_{b}
by
\hat{Q}_{b}

\displaystyle|\bar{\lambda}_{b}-\lambda_{b}|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\bar{\lambda}_{b}-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}}Q_{b}\mathrm{d}\mu_{b}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\bar{\lambda}_{b}-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}}\hat{Q}_{b}\mathrm{d}\mu_{b}\right|+\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}}(Q_{b}-\hat{Q}_{b})\mathrm{d}\mu_{b}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}}(\bar{\lambda}_{b}-\hat{Q}_{b}+\hat{\mathcal{L}}\bar{u}_{b})\mathrm{d}\mu_{b}\right|+\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}}(\hat{\mathcal{L}}\bar{u}_{b})\mathrm{d}\mu_{b}\right|+\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}}(Q_{b}-\hat{Q}_{b})\mathrm{d}\mu_{b}\right|.

We then have,

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}}(\bar{\lambda}-\hat{Q}_{b}+\hat{\mathcal{L}}\bar{u}_{b})\mathrm{d}\mu_{b}\right|\leq\mu(W)\sup_{b}\|(\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{b}\bar{u}_{b}-\hat{Q}_{b}+\bar{\lambda}_{b})/W\|_{\infty}=:\delta_{1},

which can be bounded explicitly. Similarly, using the fact that
\mathcal{L}\bar{u}_{b}
is
\mu_{b}
-mean zero

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}}(\hat{\mathcal{L}}\bar{u}_{b})\mathrm{d}\mu_{b}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}}(\hat{\mathcal{L}}\bar{u}_{b}-\mathcal{L}\bar{u}_{b})\mathrm{d}\mu_{b}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}}(\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}-p(b))r^{2}\sin(2\psi)\partial_{\psi}\bar{u}_{b}\mathrm{d}\mu_{b}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\mu(W)\sup_{b\in[b_{-},b_{+}]}\|(r^{2}\sin(2\psi)\partial_{\psi}\bar{u}_{b})/W\|_{\infty}=:\delta_{2},

which also can be bounded explicitly,
and finally

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}}(Q_{b}-\hat{Q}_{b})\mathrm{d}\mu_{b}\right|\leq\varepsilon\mu(W)\|r^{2}\sin(2\psi)/W\|_{\infty}=:\delta_{3},

so that, for all
b\in[b_{-},b_{+}]
,

|\lambda_{b}-\bar{\lambda}_{b}|\leq\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}+\delta_{3}.

.

The two important parameters of the system are
\alpha

(with a more precise treatment to make this analysis more quantitative) to obtain a diagram for
