World Dementia Council Update
Published: 2026

To make sustainable change around dementia and brain health, it is more important than ever to convene leaders across sectors to discuss gaps, opportunities, and collaborate on solutions. In recognition of this need, the World Dementia Council (WDC) holds an annual summit. this year taking place on October 20, 2026, at the Francis Crick Institute in London. This year's summit, titled “The New Patient: Before Symptoms, After Diagnosis,” will explore how advances in treatments and diagnostics are shifting dementia toward earlier detection and intervention. The program will examine how health systems and services will need to adapt, including workforce, care pathways, and the use of data and digital tools.

The summit will bring together leaders from government, research, health systems, civil society, and industry, combining plenary sessions that set shared themes and priorities with parallel discussions designed for deeper policy and implementation conversations, alongside a number of independently convened workshops and roundtables hosted by partner organizations.

This year's summit comes at an important time for the field. For the first time, it is becoming possible to detect and intervene in Alzheimer's disease (AD) earlier in the disease course. This shift brings new clinical, ethical, and social questions: how to communicate risk and diagnosis responsibly, how to organize sustained support, and how to ensure early detection leads to better outcomes. The following will detail the key sessions and information that will be covered during the October event.

MORNING SESSIONS: HOW SCIENTIFIC ADVANCES ARE RESHAPING DEMENTIA

The day will open with a plenary session, “Science Moves First: Are Treatment and Diagnostics Shifting the Meaning of Dementia?,” examining how emerging treatments and diagnostics are moving identification earlier and reshaping definitions of AD and other forms of dementia.

Parallel sessions will then dig into the scientific, ethical, and policy implications of this shift in more depth. “From Late Diagnosis to Early Detection: Alzheimer's in Transition” will explore what it means to identify AD before clinical onset, while “Can Brain Health Become the Next Public Health Movement?” will examine whether “brain health” is the right framing for driving population‐level progress on dementia risk reduction. A third session, “Will Demand Outpace Capacity? Lessons from the First Wave of Treatment,” will look at what early implementation can teach the field about capacity, bottlenecks, and real‐world systems.

A second set of parallel sessions will turn to the practical demands of scaling this new era of care. “Scaling and Integrating Biomarker and Digital Testing” will consider how to validate, finance, and scale biomarkers and digital diagnostics across systems with varying levels of resources. “If Early Diagnosis Becomes the New Normal, What Does Prevention Become?” will explore what secondary prevention looks like when individuals are identified as at risk before symptoms develop or in early stages of disease, and “What Can Technology Replace in the Dementia Pathway?” will examine the opportunities, risks, and questions raised as digital biomarkers, remote monitoring, and artificial intelligence (AI) solutions expand across the care pathway.

AFTERNOON SESSIONS: ADAPTING SYSTEMS FOR DELIVERY

The discussion will then shift from what is scientifically possible to how health systems need to adapt to actually deliver the new tools. “Making Treatment Real: Infrastructure, Capacity and System Readiness” will address what is required to deliver disease‐modifying therapies at scale. “Integrating Brain Health into Health Systems” will consider what evidence is needed to justify adoption of brain health interventions. “Data Across the Disease Course” will examine how AI and data streams, including digital cognitive testing and remote physiological measures, can support long‐term follow‐up, detect meaningful change, and guide decisions about interventions. The day will close with a plenary bringing these threads together: “Can Health Systems Keep Up with Scientific Progress?,” asking whether systems can adapt fast enough to keep up with scientific advancements.

Attendees will leave the 2026 summit with greater insight into how health systems, public health, and advocacy must evolve to new care pathways, new workforce models, new forms of data and digital infrastructure, and new approaches to brain health across the life course.
