A NASSS framework-guided systematic review and exploratory modelling of digital health interventions for polypharmacy management in older adults
Authors: Arun Vamadevan, Vijesh Vijayan, Christine Cole, KasiReddy Jayasudha, Nishad Yusuf, Lauren E Walker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolypharmacy is a growing challenge in older adults with multimorbidity, increasing the risk of adverse drug events, hospital admissions, and healthcare utilisation. Technology-supported interventions, such as clinical decision support systems (CDSS) and digital deprescribing tools, offer new opportunities to optimize medication use. However, adoption and sustainability remain variable across healthcare settings. The Non-adoption, Abandonment, Scale-up, Spread, and Sustainability (NASSS) framework offers a valuable lens to evaluate complex digital health interventions targeting medication optimization in older adults.ObjectiveThis review systematically examines the adoption, scale-up, and sustainability of technology-supported medicines optimization interventions for older adults, guided by the NASSS framework.MethodsA systematic search was conducted across Scopus, PubMed, Cochrane Library, and CINAHL. After screening and eligibility assessment, 30 studies were included. A narrative synthesis was performed, mapping intervention characteristics and outcomes to NASSS domains. An exploratory predictive modelling, including decision tree analysis, were used to identify factors influencing intervention success. This review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251006170).ResultsThe included studies evaluated diverse interventions such as CDSS, electronic prescribing platforms, pharmacist-led deprescribing systems, and telehealth-supported medication reviews. Successful adoption was associated with user-centred technology design, strong clinical leadership, workflow integration, and organizational readiness. Interventions incorporating multidisciplinary collaboration, continuous feedback, and iterative adaptation showed stronger sustainability. Common barriers included technological complexity, poor interoperability, lack of provider engagement, insufficient training, and regulatory constraints. Decision tree analysis identified usability, early stakeholder engagement, organizational alignment, and system adaptability as key factors predicting successful adoption and sustainability.ConclusionThis is the first review to integrate the NASSS framework with decision tree modelling to evaluate technology-supported medicines optimization for older adults with multimorbidity and polypharmacy. The findings emphasize that successful implementation requires a formative, contextually sensitive approach that addresses technological, organizational, and policy-level complexities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06602-4.Keywords: Polypharmacy, Multimorbidity, Older adults, Digital health, NASSS framework, Medicines optimization, Deprescribing, Decision tree modelling, Implementation science.

Introduction

The prevalence of multiple long-term conditions is rising among aging populations worldwide [1]. Managing these conditions typically requires multiple medications, which increases the risk of adverse drug reactions, drug interactions, non-adherence, and inappropriate prescribing [1]. These medication-related problems contribute to substantial healthcare costs and preventable harm. To address these critical challenges, technology-based medication optimization approaches have emerged as viable options. Advances like Clinical Decision Support Systems (CDSS), AI-based medication reconciliation solutions with e-prescribing, and telehealth interventions provide new options to improve patient safety and optimize prescribing workflows [2–4]. Although promising, implementation of these solutions in the real world is hindered by substantial barriers such as adoption strategies, regulatory limitations, provider constraints, and interoperability issues [5–7]. Studies show that CDSS effectively reduces medication errors and safeguards prescription safety for the older patient population [8]. These systems enable healthcare providers to make better prescribing choices through their real-time alert system and evidence-based suggestions [9]. By using technology supported prescribing tools healthcare providers achieve better accuracy and lessen the possibility of writing illegible medical instructions. CDSS implementation faces restrictions because of data security worries along with financial obstacles and opposition to digital changes and restrictions from integrating with electronic health records systems [10–12]. While previous studies have explored the potential benefits of digital solutions, there is a gap in understanding the factors that influence their adoption, long-term sustainability, and integration into routine geriatric care.

Polypharmacy, commonly defined as the daily use of five or more medications [13, 14], is prevalent among older adults with multimorbidity, where each condition may require multiple treatments [7, 14, 15]. Hyperpolypharmacy refers to the concurrent use of ten or more medications [16]. While polypharmacy is often clinically appropriate, distinguishing between appropriate and inappropriate polypharmacy is essential to minimize adverse drug events and negative health outcomes [15, 16]. Recent literature emphasizes the term ‘appropriate polypharmacy’ to describe the optimized, evidence-based use of multiple medications, moving beyond simple numeric thresholds [17]. The King’s Fund similarly defines appropriate polypharmacy as prescribing for complex or multiple conditions where the benefits outweigh risks and are supported by best evidence [16]. Conversely, inappropriate polypharmacy involves medications that are unsuitable or ineffective, where the risks outweigh benefits [18]. This nuanced definition better reflects clinical realities and helps distinguish between ‘many’ and ‘too many’ medications in geriatric care [15].

The Non-adoption, Abandonment and challenges to the Scale-Up, Spread and Sustainability (NASSS) framework developed and designed by Greenhalgh and colleague, is an evidence-based, theory-informed, and pragmatic framework intended to help understand and evaluate the success of technology-supported health or social care initiatives [12, 19, 20]. The NASSS framework is designed based on the understanding that multiple factors (i.e., domains) influence the implementation and adoption of a technology rather than one sole determining domain. Additionally, the dynamic interactions between different domains and their evolution overtime can play a key role in the overall ‘successful’ implementation. NASSS supports understanding the complexity of a system within which the innovative solutions fall, to help inform potential implementation or retrospective analysis of a technology’s failure/success [20]. The framework spans seven domains, including condition, technology, value proposition, adopters, organisation, wider system, and finally, evolution over time, as outlined below. The greatest challenge is often presented when complexity is identified across multiple domains [21]. NASSS is not a predictive model for successful implementation but will facilitate the deconstruction of a complex system within which technology supported interventions would be implemented [12, 19].

The “condition” domain of the NASSS framework focuses on understanding both the clinical and socio-cultural dimensions of a condition, including the presence of multimorbidity. This involves two primary considerations: the nature and complexity of the health condition itself, and the broader social and cultural contexts that may affect its management [20]. Within this domain, technology-supported interventions must be assessed not only for their clinical relevance but also for their operational demands—specifically, the knowledge, resources, and support required for their effective use. Despite their potential, these interventions often face significant barriers, including technical challenges, implementation failures, and associated financial and system-level burdens, particularly in resource-intensive settings [21]. In polypharmacy management, such barriers are amplified by the need to tailor interventions to meet the diverse and complex needs of older adults, prescribers, and healthcare organisations [22]. The Value Proposition domain complements this analysis by examining for whom the technology delivers value, assessing its clinical effectiveness, cost-efficiency, safety, and strategic rationale from the developer’s perspective [19]. Importantly, the NASSS framework facilitates both formative and summative evaluation by identifying points of friction in adoption, some of which may be addressed through targeted adaptations, while others require more systemic change. In the context of medicines optimisation for older adults with multimorbidity, implementation is further complicated by intersecting individual, organisational, and socio-cultural factors. Addressing digital exclusion and ensuring inclusive design—through both accessible digital interfaces and equitable non-digital alternatives—is therefore essential to realising the full potential of these innovations in practice.

Polypharmacy presents a persistent challenge in older adult care, particularly with rising multimorbidity. This review explores the adoption and sustainability of digital health interventions for polypharmacy management, guided by the NASSS framework. Focusing on technologies such as web-based deprescribing tools, pharmacist-led systems, and alert-based platforms, the study identifies key implementation barriers and enablers. A structured qualitative synthesis maps findings across NASSS domains, while an exploratory decision tree model visualizes pathways linked to successful intervention uptake. By combining thematic and predictive insights, the review provides practical guidance for integrating digital deprescribing strategies into routine geriatric care.

Methods

This study systematically evaluated digital health interventions for deprescribing in older adults with polypharmacy using the NASSS framework. The framework’s domains were transformed into measurable variables, enabling predictive analysis of implementation factors. This exploratory modelling aimed to identify influential factors linked to implementation success, rather than producing a generalizable predictive tool.

Framework selection and justification

Several implementation science frameworks were considered for this review, including the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), which focuses on inner and outer setting determinants [23], and RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance), which emphasizes translational outcomes [24]. However, the NASSS framework was selected as most appropriate for three key reasons. First, NASSS was specifically designed to evaluate technology-supported health interventions, making it uniquely suited to digital deprescribing tools [19–26]. Second, its seven-domain structure explicitly addresses the socio-technical complexity inherent in implementing digital health solutions across diverse healthcare settings which is a core challenge in polypharmacy management [20]. Third, NASSS incorporates temporal dynamics through its ‘Embedding and Adaptation Over Time’ domain, enabling assessment of sustainability beyond initial adoption [20]. While CFIR offers comprehensive contextual analysis and RE-AIM emphasizes population-level impact, NASSS provides the most appropriate lens for examining why technology-supported interventions succeed or fail in complex, multimorbid populations. This framework selection aligns with recent implementation research emphasizing the need for technology-specific evaluation approaches [20].

Only empirical studies were included, encompassing both quantitative (e.g., RCTs, observational studies) and qualitative or mixed-methods studies that provided original data. Non-empirical literature such as editorials, commentaries, theoretical papers, and non-data-based reviews were excluded. Only studies published in English were included due to translation resource constraints; this criterion was prespecified prior to screening.

For studies where the full text was not accessible through institutional or open-access sources, the corresponding authors were contacted via email. Studies were excluded only if full texts could not be retrieved after two contact attempts. (Multimorbidity was defined as the presence of two or more chronic conditions. Complexity was operationalized based on indicators such as medication burden, cognitive status, and care coordination needs.)

Two independent reviewers Arun Vamadevan (AV) and Vijesh Vijayan (VV) screened the titles and abstracts, followed by full-text reviews to ensure relevance. Data extraction focused on key NASSS domains, intervention type, and implementation outcomes. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (KasiReddy Jaya- KRJ).

Quality appraisal

To ensure a robust evaluation of methodological quality and evidence strength, both study-level risk of bias assessments and outcome-level GRADE evaluations were conducted. The ROB-2 tool was applied to randomized controlled trials (RCT) [27] and clustered RCTs [28], and the ROBINS-I tool [29] was used for non-randomized studies, enabling structured assessment of potential biases related to randomization, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, and selective outcome reporting. Compared to CRCTs, RCTs demonstrated greater concern in the domains of allocation concealment, selective reporting, and performance bias, largely due to lack of blinding and incomplete protocol registration. In parallel, the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) [30] approach was employed to assess the overall certainty of evidence across studies, considering not only risk of bias but also inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. This dual approach allowed for a distinction between internal validity of individual studies and the confidence in the cumulative evidence base for each key outcome. The use of both tools enhances interpretability, supports transparent decision-making, and aligns with best practices in evidence synthesis for implementation research.

Thematic analysis

A qualitative thematic analysis was conducted using Taguette [31], an open-source qualitative analysis tool, to categorize and code key themes from the included studies based on the NASSS framework. Full-text articles (PDFs and Word files) were imported into Taguette, where relevant passages were segmented and systematically coded according to the NASSS domains: Condition Complexity (e.g., multiple long-term disease burden, prescribing challenges), Technology Characteristics (e.g., clinical decision support systems, usability), Value Proposition (e.g., effectiveness in reducing inappropriate prescribing), Adopters (e.g., clinician, pharmacist, and patient perspectives), Organization (e.g., health system readiness and integration barriers), Wider System (e.g., policy, reimbursement, and regulatory factors), and Embedding & Adaptation (e.g., long-term feasibility and sustainability). Overlapping codes were refined and merged into broader themes to ensure coherence. Finally, the coded results were extracted for further synthesis and statistical analysis in SAS studio version 9.4.

Decision tree analysis

SAS studio version 9.4 was utilised to perform statistical analysis. Initially, exploratory regression modelling was considered; however, due to variability in evidence quality, limited sample size, and heterogeneity of outcome reporting, this component was omitted. The final synthesis focuses on a robust qualitative thematic analysis mapped to the NASSS framework to identify key barriers and enablers. A decision tree model was constructed using the High-Performance Split (HPSPLIT) procedure in SAS Studio 9.4 [32], with the Gini impurity index guiding node splitting and cost-complexity pruning applied to optimise interpretability and predictive performance. Cost-complexity pruning was applied to refine the model, improving interpretability and feasibility for real-world implementation. This methodological approach enabled a structured evaluation of digital health interventions, transforming qualitative NASSS domains into measurable variables for predictive analysis. A structured characterisation of each NASSS domain was developed for all included studies to enable consistent assessment of intervention complexity. This characterisation informed the development of the “tag count” variable, which was used in the SAS modelling framework to explore patterns across decision tree analyses. Full details are provided in Supplementary File 1.

Results

Screening and scrutinization of literature

A total of 515 studies were initially identified through the search terms in Scopus, PubMed, Cochrane Library, and CINAHL databases as shown in Fig. 1. The search retrieved 515 studies, and the majority originated from Scopus (346 studies) alongside PubMed (115 studies), Cochrane Library (6 studies) and CINAHL (48 studies). Initial screening involved the use of Rayyan QCRI, a semi-automated systematic review screening platform, to facilitate the identification of clearly irrelevant records. The software flagged 9 studies as potentially ineligible based on predefined exclusion keywords (e.g., pediatric, animal model, protocol-only, or non-English). All automatically flagged studies were subsequently manually reviewed by two independent reviewers to confirm ineligibility. Additionally, 41 duplicate records were removed before full screening commence and 8 studies eliminated during pre-screening due to issues with retrieval errors of broken links and inaccessible files and non-research formats. A sequential screening process eliminated 215 records from assessment because they consisted of book chapters, observational studies and systematic reviews and meta-analyses along with conference abstracts, encyclopaedia entries, review articles and letters to the editor. Additional assessment identified 88 reports for removal because their study data was irrelevant, or their outcomes or interventions were out of scope of this study. Studies were excluded if they assessed non-digital interventions or reported outcomes unrelated to deprescribing, such as hospitalisation or unrelated health service usage. A total of 124 reports were excluded during eligibility assessments because they were not in English or did not provide full text access. Risk of bias

The risk of bias (RoB) assessment for the included cluster randomized controlled trials (RCTs) as demonstrated in Fig. 3, indicates that all studies exhibit some concerns in at least one domain, with no study deemed to have a high risk of bias except for D1 (Bias due to randomization process). Most studies showed concerns in D1b (bias in recruitment timing, cluster RCTs), with the exception of Romskaug et al. (2020), where recruitment processes were appropriately timed in relation to randomization. The studies by Brünn et al. (2024) and Rieckert (2020) show concerns in D2 (bias due to deviations from intended intervention), which stem from difficulties in maintaining intervention fidelity within cluster trials. Conversely, D3 (missing outcome data) was low risk in all studies, indicating adequate follow-up and minimal attrition except for Rieckert (2020), Crotty et al. (2004) and Romskaug et al. (2020). The overall judgment for all studies is “some concerns,” highlighting limitations primarily in recruitment timing and selective reporting, rather than fundamental flaws in randomization or outcome measurement. Importantly, D4 (measurement of outcomes) was judged to be at moderate risk in Brünn et al. (2024), O’Sullivan et al. (2015), and Romskaug et al. (2020), due to reliance on outcome assessments that may have been subject to assessor awareness of intervention allocation, potentially introducing detection bias. Additionally, Concerns for D5 (bias in selective reporting) were identified only in Brünn et al. (2024). These concerns are typical in cluster RCTs, where recruitment and intervention allocation complexities can introduce systematic biases.

Critical quality appraisal of studies

The GRADE assessment of the included studies (Supplementary Table 2) indicated an overall predominance of moderate-quality evidence, with only a few studies reaching a low overall certainty [33–39] and two achieving high quality [40, 41]. Across most trials and observational studies, the primary limiting factor was risk of bias, often due to small sample sizes, pilot designs, or lack of randomization and blinding. Inconsistency was generally low, though several studies [35, 42–44] showed high variability across sites or endpoints, lowering confidence in reproducibility. Indirectness was usually minimal, as most studies directly targeted older adults with polypharmacy, though single-setting or population-specific trials [35, 45, 46] reduced external generalizability. Imprecision was a frequent concern, particularly in underpowered studies with limited recruitment or low event rates, while larger multicentre trials [33, 38, 39] provided more precise estimates. Publication bias was largely undetected, though uncertainty remained in smaller or pilot investigations. Overall, the evidence suggests that interventions to optimize prescribing in older adults are supported by moderate-quality data, but methodological limitations, heterogeneity, and imprecision constrain the certainty of effect estimates, highlighting the need for larger, rigorously designed multicenter trials.

NASSS domains

The included studies were assessed against the NASSS framework, which captures the multidimensional complexity of implementing technological interventions across seven domains. Of the 30 studies, 17 (56.67%) addressed all seven domains, 11 (36.67%) addressed six, and only 2 (6.67%) engaged with five. This distribution indicates a high degree of comprehensiveness, with 93.33% of studies engaging with at least six domains, suggesting strong alignment with complexity-aware implementation practices. A wide range of intervention types was observed. The most frequently implemented strategies included Screening Tool of Older Persons’ Prescriptions/Screening Tool to Alert to Right Treatment (STOPP/START) criteria (n = 15), deprescribing frameworks (n = 10), and alert-based systems (n = 9). Digital tools such as web-based platforms (n = 4), medication review systems (n = 4), and AI-driven applications like AiDKlinik (n = 1) were integrated to varying degrees of complexity. Structured assessment tools—including Beers Criteria (n = 7), FORTA, LESS-CHRON, and MAI—were also utilised, alongside broader review programmes such as PRIMA-eDS (n = 2), MedSafer (n = 1), and MyComrade (n = 1). Non-digital components, including pharmacist-led reviews and feedback loops from patients and clinicians, further enriched the intervention landscape.

Within the Adopters domain, tags such as “older adults,” “clinical pharmacists,” and “polypharmacy” were most prevalent, highlighting the central role of pharmacist-led interventions and patient-specific considerations in the adoption of deprescribing technologies. In the Technology Characteristics domain, “clinical decision support,” “electronic health records,” and “alert systems” dominated, reflecting the frequent reliance on integrated digital tools to enhance prescribing safety. The Condition Complexity domain was heavily marked by “multimorbidity” and “adverse drug events,” underscoring the clinical burden faced by older adults and the imperative for tailored medication management strategies. Similarly, the prominence of tags like “implementation” and “staff training” under the Organization domain reinforces the importance of institutional readiness and resource capacity. The consistent appearance of “improved outcomes” and “cost-effectiveness” in the Value Proposition domain illustrates how stakeholder perceptions of benefit directly impact intervention sustainability. Condition

The domain of Condition Complexity within the NASSS framework examines the clinical and sociocultural aspects of health conditions, recognizing that not all individuals with the same condition will respond equally to interventions. Across the 30 studies analysed, condition complexity was prominently driven by polypharmacy in the context of multimorbidity, frequently resulting in adverse drug events (ADEs), hospitalisations, and diminished treatment efficacy. Several studies (e.g., Schmader et al., 2004; Sönnichsen et al., 2016) explicitly linked these factors to reduced patient safety and poorer clinical outcomes, reinforcing the need for early identification of at-risk individuals. Importantly, this complexity was not uniformly distributed. Studies conducted in institutional settings (e.g., nursing homes) reported higher baseline risk and greater responsiveness to structured interventions, while those in community-based or outpatient settings demonstrated more variable results. Similarly, Ghibelli et al.. (2013) [46] and Romskaug et al.. (2020) [47] reinforced these findings, highlighting the interconnectedness of these risk factors and their impact on clinical outcomes. Other studies emphasized the role of polypharmacy as a primary driver of condition complexity. Sallevent et al.. (2022) [9]and Muth et al.. (2016) [48]reported polypharmacy as a critical factor in medication safety and patient burden, often leading to ADEs and hospitalizations. Condition complexity also influenced the perceived relevance and uptake of digital interventions. Studies with higher-risk populations (e.g., Muth et al., 2016; Sallevent et al., 2022) were more likely to demonstrate positive outcomes, potentially due to the greater clinical urgency and clear intervention targets. Conversely, studies reporting limited impact (e.g., Mahony et al., 2020; Malham et al., 2024) frequently lacked precision in targeting complexity-related subgroups or failed to tailor interventions to the nuanced needs of older adults.

The way complexity was operationalised varied across studies, with some using composite indices (e.g., Medication Appropriateness Index) and others relying on clinical proxies such as hospitalisation rates or Potentially Inappropriate Medications (PIM) counts. This inconsistency in definition may partly explain differences in outcome interpretation and limits comparability. These findings collectively suggest that interventions are most effective when designed with an explicit understanding of condition complexity—not just as a background characteristic, but as a determinant that can be directly addressed through tailored prescribing criteria, risk stratification, and patient-specific deprescribing pathways.

Technology

The Technology domain encompasses digital tools, clinical decision support systems (CDSS), and prescribing frameworks designed to enhance medication safety. Interventions across the included studies featured Electronic Decision Support Systems (EDSS), guideline-based tools (e.g., STOPP/START, Beers Criteria), and AI-assisted platforms, integrated into Electronic Health Records (EHRs), web-based systems, or standalone applications. EDSS tools such as PRIMA-eDS (Sönnichsen et al.., 2016) [49]and MedSafer (McDonald et al.., 2019) [50], supported structured deprescribing and improved prescribing appropriateness. Similarly, digital implementations of STOPP/START (Verdoorn et al., 2017) [51]and Beers Criteria (Schmader et al., 2004) [38] demonstrated value in identifying PIMs. However, implementation outcomes varied, with effectiveness strongly influenced by system usability and contextual fit. Several studies identified alert fatigue as a major barrier, where excessive, non-specific prompts led to clinician desensitisation and reduced adherence to recommendations (Sallevent et al., 2022; Rieckert et al., 2020) [9, 43, 52] Integration challenges were also common, with interventions failing to embed seamlessly within EHR workflows, limiting their practical utility (Niehoff et al., 2016) [36]. These issues reflect a disconnect between technological sophistication and real-world usability. AI-based systems introduced further complexity. While promising in design, concerns around transparency, contextual reliability, and clinical accountability reduced clinician trust (Muth et al., 2016) [48]. Furthermore, disparities in digital infrastructure constrained scalability, particularly in under-resourced or fragmented healthcare settings (Haag et al., 2016; Mahony et al., 2020) [53, 54].

Web-based and telephonic interventions (e.g., Brünn et al., 2022; Muth et al., 2016) [48, 55] offered more adaptable solutions, particularly where EHR integration was limited. Their success, however, remained dependent on clinician engagement, training, and organisational support. While technology-enabled interventions show clear potential, their impact is shaped less by innovation and more by integration. Tools that fail to align with clinical workflows, mitigate alert burden, or build user trust risk limited adoption—regardless of functionality. The findings underscore the need for co-designed, interoperable systems that prioritise usability and contextual relevance.

Value proposition

The Value Proposition domain examines the perceived benefits, effectiveness, and relevance of deprescribing technologies for key stakeholders, including clinicians, patients, and healthcare systems. This domain directly influences adoption, scale-up, and long-term sustainability. Across the reviewed studies, improved prescribing quality and medication appropriateness were the most consistently reported benefits. Technologies such as PRIMA-eDS and STOPP/START tools utilized by Sönnichsen et al.. (2016) [49] Sallevent et al.. (2022) [9] identified potentially inappropriate medications in approximately 60% of patients’ reviewed prescriptions across the included studies. Schmader et al.. (2004) [38] reported a 35% reduction in serious adverse drug reactions (ADRs), while McDonald et al. (2019) [50] observed a 35.8% reduction in ADR-related risks, reinforcing the clinical relevance of deprescribing interventions. Several studies demonstrated measurable improvements in deprescribing success and medication burden. For instance, Muth et al. (2016) [48] and Hanenberg et al. (2022) [56] reported significant gains in the Medication Appropriateness Index (p < 0.001), while Mejías-Trueba et al. (2023) [44] found a high deprescribing success rate (0.827). Ghibelli et al. (2013) [46] observed a decline in PIMs per patient from 0.5 to 0.1, and Frankenthal et al. (2014) [57] noted a meaningful reduction in total medication count among intervention groups.

Despite these clinical gains, the perceived value of interventions was not universally shared. Several studies (e.g., Haag et al., 2016; Mahony et al., 2020) highlighted limited impact due to alert fatigue, workflow disruption, or insufficient clinician engagement [53, 54]. Even tools like MedSafer and PRIMA-eDS, while effective in controlled settings, demonstrated variable adoption, often dependent on user trust, institutional support, and vendor-clinician alignment. This suggests that value is context-sensitive, shaped not only by outcome data but also by stakeholder perceptions and implementation conditions. Scalability and sustainability were linked to several enabling factors, including EHRs interoperability, user training, and regulatory clarity. Studies consistently emphasised the importance of aligning technological benefits with stakeholder incentives and workflow compatibility to ensure continued use. Overall, while the clinical efficacy of deprescribing tools is well supported, their broader value proposition is contingent on factors beyond algorithmic performance. User-centred design, trust in decision-support outputs, and seamless workflow integration are essential to realising long-term impact.

Adopter system

The adopter system in deprescribing technologies includes physicians, pharmacists, and older adults, with adoption influenced by clinician engagement, workflow integration, and patient participation. Studies highlight both successful adoption cases and barriers to implementation.

Among clinicians, uptake was highest when tools were perceived to enhance medication safety and integrated seamlessly into EHRs [38, 39]. Pharmacist engagement also emerged as pivotal, with studies such as Sallevent et al.. (2022) [9] reporting a 60% error detection rate using deprescribing tools, reinforcing the value of interdisciplinary models. McDonald et al.. (2019) [50] further supported this, associating tool use with a notable reduction in adverse drug reactions. However, adoption was not uniform. Barriers included workflow disruption, resistance to prescribing changes, and alert fatigue—particularly among clinicians unaccustomed to system-level prescribing oversight [53, 54, 58]. Malham et al. (2024) [35] noted physician disengagement when recommendations conflicted with clinical judgement, indicating a tension between algorithmic output and professional autonomy.

For older adults, shared decision-making and clinician-led communication were key facilitators. Studies (e.g., Verdoorn et al.. (2017) [51] and Romskaug et al.. (2020) [47]) found that patient engagement increased when deprescribing was framed collaboratively. However, barriers such as medication dependency, fear of withdrawal, and limited health literacy constrained uptake, particularly in more vulnerable cohorts (Niehoff et al., 2016; Mahony et al., 2020) [36, 54]. Overall, successful adoption depended on aligning intervention design with user expectations, workflow compatibility, and patient-centred approaches. Clinician training, patient education, and system-level incentives are critical to fostering engagement and sustaining deprescribing efforts in practice.

Organizational readiness and implementation challenges

The effective implementation of deprescribing technologies is closely tied to an organisation’s readiness for change, including leadership commitment, workflow alignment, and infrastructure capacity. Institutions with established digital systems and structured deprescribing frameworks demonstrated higher adoption rates. Hospitals and teaching centres, benefiting from centralised decision-making and interdisciplinary teams, were more likely to integrate tools successfully, particularly when embedded within electronic health records (Sönnichsen et al., 2016; Schmader et al., 2004) [38, 49]. McDonald et al. (2019) further illustrated this, linking system integration to measurable reductions in adverse drug reactions [50]. In contrast, primary care settings showed inconsistent adoption, often constrained by limited resources, workflow misalignment, and clinician resistance [54, 58]. Studies in this context emphasised the importance of collaborative models and protocol standardisation [48, 59], yet challenges such as alert fatigue and lack of funding remained persistent barriers [53].

Pharmacist-led interventions in community pharmacies and nursing homes were generally well-received, with improved medication safety outcomes reported (Verdoorn et al., 2017; García-Caballero et al., 2018). However, variability in staff engagement and guideline consistency limited long-term impact. Critically, organisational culture emerged as a key determinant of sustainability. Institutions with deprescribing champions, clear policies, and aligned digital workflows were more likely to embed practices into routine care [33, 60]. Conversely, where leadership engagement or system readiness was lacking, interventions struggled to achieve meaningful or sustained uptake. These findings highlight that technical functionality must be supported by strategic organisational investment and policy alignment to enable scalable and lasting change.

Wider context

The implementation of deprescribing technologies is significantly influenced by wider system factors, including regulatory frameworks, financial support, professional collaboration, and sociocultural considerations. These macro-level determinants shape the adoption, integration, and sustainability of deprescribing interventions across healthcare settings. Regulatory frameworks played a dual role. In studies where national or institutional deprescribing policies were in place, adoption was facilitated through standardisation and clinical legitimacy [38, 49]. However, inconsistent enforcement and fragmented policy guidance—particularly in primary care and long-term care environments—undermined coherence and led to variable application [58].

Financial resources were equally pivotal. Interventions with dedicated funding and infrastructure support, such as those reported by Romskaug et al. (2020) and Rieckert et al. (2020), achieved higher uptake and better outcomes [47, 52]. In contrast, underfunded initiatives, particularly in community or rural settings, struggled to integrate deprescribing tools effectively [53, 54]. McDonald et al. (2019) illustrated the clinical and economic benefits of well-resourced systems, including reduced ADRs and improved prescribing quality [50].

Professional collaboration—especially between pharmacists and physicians—emerged as an enabling factor, reinforcing shared responsibility and improving deprescribing success [59]. However, some studies noted clinician resistance, concerns around liability, and scepticism about algorithm-driven recommendations [34, 53], particularly in more autonomous or siloed practice settings. Sociocultural influences, including patient beliefs and public awareness, also influenced implementation. While interventions aligned with person-centred care models saw stronger uptake [33]barriers such as cognitive impairment, fear of medication withdrawal, and limited digital literacy constrained engagement in several cohorts [57, 61]. Overall, the broader system context acted as both facilitator and constraint. Successful implementation depended not only on the strength of the intervention but also on alignment with financial incentives, professional norms, and regulatory infrastructure. Without these supports, even well-designed interventions risk limited adoption or premature discontinuation.

Embedding and adaptation over time

The ability of deprescribing technologies to become sustainably embedded within healthcare systems depends on their adaptability, long-term resourcing, and integration into evolving clinical practices. This domain was the least addressed across studies, highlighting a persistent gap in evaluating longitudinal sustainability [19].

Successful examples of embedding were linked to structured implementation strategies, including routine EHR integration, continuous feedback mechanisms, and workflow standardisation [39, 51]. These approaches facilitated consistent use and promoted institutional learning, particularly in hospital and academic settings. Equally, many interventions lacked formal mechanisms for adaptation or long-term evaluation. Kirwan et al. (2022) and Mahony et al. (2020) noted limited follow-up, inconsistent use over time, and minimal system-level reinforcement [54, 58]. Infrastructural fragility—particularly in underfunded contexts—compounded these challenges, reducing the likelihood of sustained adoption [47, 53].

Organisational commitment and clinician engagement were again central. Programs with deprescribing champions, ongoing training, and protected time for review activities demonstrated greater resilience [34, 61]. By contrast, studies lacking feedback loops, technical support, or performance monitoring struggled to maintain momentum. Patient-level factors also influenced long-term uptake. Resistance to medication changes, fluctuating cognitive capacity, and lack of structured patient education limited repeated engagement with deprescribing processes [57, 61]. Ultimately, few studies explicitly addressed system adaptation or iterative redesign. This omission constrains understanding of how interventions evolve beyond initial deployment. Sustainability requires not only clinical effectiveness, but also the institutional flexibility to adapt tools to local contexts, shifting policies, and user feedback over time.

Discussion

This is the first study of author’s knowledge to integrate the NASSS framework with decision tree modelling to generate predictive insights. The NASSS framework offers a valuable lens to assess the implementation of complex digital interventions by addressing seven interrelated domains [20, 21]. In this review, digital decision-support tools helped manage condition complexity by enhancing prescribing quality, while EHR integration facilitated real-time medication reviews and reduced polypharmacy-related risks. Value propositions such as improved clinical outcomes, efficiency, and cost-effectiveness were generally well-recognized, particularly when supported by organizational leadership and training. However, barriers emerged across several domains. Technological challenges, including poor EHR interoperability and alert fatigue, limited usability. Adoption was hindered by clinician resistance, workflow disruption, and value uncertainty in some contexts. Organizational and system-level factors—such as limited resources and inconsistent policies—further complicated implementation. Sustainable integration depended on continuous adaptation, stakeholder engagement, and supportive policy frameworks.

To complement the qualitative synthesis, a decision tree model was developed using the HPSPLIT procedure. The model employed the Gini impurity index for node splitting and cost-complexity pruning to optimize performance while maintaining interpretability. The full model initially reached ten levels with 12 terminal nodes, and pruning resulted in an optimized structure with 11 leaves. This decision tree provided a transparent, hierarchical visualization of high-impact intervention pathways, mapping combinations of features associated with successful implementation.

Notably, pharmacist-led deprescribing strategies, when combined with web-based platforms and clinical decision-support tools such as MedSafer and STOPP/START criteria, were frequently associated with positive outcomes. These results align with existing evidence supporting pharmacist involvement and structured review criteria in deprescribing practices [62–64]. Variable importance scores further supported this STOPP/START (1.00), deprescribing frameworks (0.83), and alert systems (0.78) were the top contributors, followed by Beers Criteria, medication review tools, and web-based interfaces.

From a NASSS perspective, these variables primarily mapped to the domains of Technology, Value Proposition, and Adopter-System Complexity, indicating that usability, clinical relevance, and integration within existing care processes are central to successful uptake. Conversely, lower importance scores for telephone-based (0.47) and EHR-only interventions (0.39) suggest persistent challenges in interoperability and workflow alignment, consistent with earlier work on digital implementation fatigue [12, 65].

The cost-complexity pruning curve further validated model reliability, identifying the optimal pruning point at 11 leaves with a misclassification rate of 0.45. This balance between model accuracy and interpretability enhances its applicability as a decision-support tool in real-world planning. Unlike retrospective NASSS applications such as the TORPEDO study by Abimbola et al. [19] this review applies NASSS prospectively to predict implementation success. In doing so, it builds on recent conceptual work [66, 67] by quantifying the relative impact of key domains like workflow fit, user-technology alignment, and organizational readiness.

Clinical Implications

The findings of this review hold important clinical implications for optimizing polypharmacy management in older adults.

Implications for Clinical Practice: Tools incorporating STOPP/START criteria, deprescribing frameworks, and alert systems emerged as the most impactful predictors of successful implementation. Clinicians should prioritize these structured, evidence-based review mechanisms and engage in interdisciplinary medication reviews, particularly pharmacist-physician collaboration models.

Implications for Healthcare Organizations: Web-based platforms demonstrated stronger adaptability across diverse clinical settings compared to EHR-only or telephone-based systems. Investment in user-centred, interoperable web tools requires adequate infrastructure for clinical decision support system integration, including staff training, technical support, and establishment of deprescribing champions. Organizations should develop feedback mechanisms enabling continuous adaptation of digital tools to local workflows.

Implications for Health Policy: Interoperability standards for digital health tools should be mandated, alongside reimbursement models supporting deprescribing activities. Long-term sustainability studies are needed to address the evidence gap in embedding and adaptation, the least-studied NASSS domain. Policy frameworks should create incentives for collaborative care models involving pharmacists, physicians, and geriatricians.

The cost-complexity pruning curve validated the model’s robustness, achieving optimal classification with a simplified structure of 11 leaves, thereby enhancing its applicability in real-world clinical environments. These insights offer a strategic foundation for developing contextually appropriate deprescribing interventions that align with clinician workflows, enhance decision-making, and reduce adverse drug outcomes among older adults.

Limitation & Future Directions

Building upon the findings of this NASSS-guided synthesis, future work should focus on refining predictive modelling approaches to better estimate intervention success. Though, this study included 30 studies, heterogeneity in study design, outcome reporting, and intervention types limited the feasibility of robust meta-analysis. The variability in how NASSS domains were reported or operationalized also introduced challenges in establishing standardised tag definitions across studies. While the current study utilised a decision tree model to highlight high-impact implementation factors, regression-based technique such as logistic regression or multivariate adaptive regression splines were not feasible due to data limitations. In future research, larger and more standardised datasets can enable robust regression modelling that accounts for covariates, interactions, and confounders across NASSS domains. Such models can quantify the relative influence of technology characteristics, adopter behaviours, and organisational readiness on implementation success. Moreover, prospective studies with longitudinal data will be critical to validate these predictors over time and across settings. There is also scope for the development of a composite implementation readiness score based on domain-level attributes, which could guide policy-makers and health systems in technology adoption decisions. Ultimately, incorporating more granular quantitative metrics alongside qualitative domain assessments will enhance the accuracy, reproducibility, and scalability of NASSS-informed implementation planning in geriatric medication optimisation.

Conclusion

Successful implementation of technology-supported medicines optimization interventions in geriatric care requires a multifactorial approach addressing clinical, organizational, and technological complexities. This review, guided by the NASSS framework, identified usability, stakeholder engagement, and contextual adaptability as critical enablers across 30 studies. The decision tree analysis—developed using the HPSPLIT procedure and optimised through cost-complexity pruning—highlighted high-impact intervention components such as the STOPP/START criteria, deprescribing frameworks, alert systems, and web-based medication review tools. STOPP/START demonstrated the highest variable importance score (1.00), followed by deprescribing frameworks (0.83) and alert systems (0.78), confirming their central role in influencing successful implementation. Conversely, interventions relying solely on EHRs or telephonic tools showed lower predictive utility, underscoring persistent barriers in integration and workflow alignment. The optimal pruning point yielded a model with 11 terminal nodes and a misclassification rate of 0.45, balancing complexity with generalizability. These findings underscore the feasibility of using data-driven modelling to inform real-world deployment of deprescribing technologies. By aligning NASSS domains with quantifiable predictors, this study provides a novel roadmap for tailoring digital interventions to specific clinical contexts, ultimately supporting safer prescribing and improved outcomes in older adults.
