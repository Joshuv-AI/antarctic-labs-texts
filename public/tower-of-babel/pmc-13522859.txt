Nutrient and Discretionary Food Intake in Vegan Adult Australians: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Linda Smillie, Michelle Minehan, Catherine R Knight‐Agarwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInterest in plant‐based diets, including vegan diets is increasing. Yet, there has been limited assessment of the dietary intake of contemporary vegans in Australia. Sixty‐six adult Australian vegans completed a four‐day, semi‐weighed food diary on two occasions. Intake from diet and supplements and diet alone was compared to the Australian Nutrient Reference Values (NRVs) for selected nutrients. Risk of micronutrient inadequacy was assessed using the cut‐point method, with comparison to the Estimated Average Requirement (EAR) or Adequate Intake (AI). The contribution of Discretionary Food (DF) intake to total energy intake was examined. A total of 466 days of dietary data were analysed after screening for under‐reporting. Macronutrient distribution matched the Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution ranges (AMDR). Total protein intake aligned with measures of adequacy and guidelines for optimal protein intake. Nutrients most at risk in our sample were vitamin B12, calcium, iodine, selenium, zinc and omega‐3 fatty acids. Supplementation did not fully resolve under‐consumption. DFs contributed 24.2% ± 11.8% to total energy intake. Our data show that macronutrient intake for adult vegans was within acceptable ranges. However, even with the addition of dietary supplements, multiple micronutrients were under‐consumed. Theoretically, a well‐planned vegan diet can be nutritionally adequate; however support is needed to ensure adequate intake in practice.Keywords: diet records, dietary intake, dietary supplements, plant‐based diet, reference values, vegan diet

Introduction

Plant‐based diets (PBDs) are recommended for their well‐established benefits in reducing chronic disease risk and supporting planetary health (Willett et al. 2019). Replacing animal products with plant foods potentially reduces the impact on the environment via lower greenhouse gas emissions, land use, water use and other environmental measures (Aleksandrowicz et al. 2016; Dixon et al. 2023). Concurrently, PBDs are associated with reduced mortality (Orlich et al. 2014) and reduced risk of most chronic diseases including type 2 diabetes (T2D) (Pollakova et al. 2021), cardiovascular disease (CVD) (Dybvik et al. 2023; Oussalah et al. 2020) and certain cancers (Dinu et al. 2017). A vegan diet is a PBD that excludes all foods of animal origin (Hargreaves et al. 2023). When vegan diets are appropriately planned to include reliable sources of nutrients such as vitamin B12, vitamin D, calcium, iodine, iron, zinc and omega‐3 fatty acids, they are nutritionally adequate and suitable for all ages (Craig et al. 2009). However, increasing awareness of the dual health and environmental benefits of PBDs has prompted an expansion of processed vegan food lines in the food supply of Australia and other countries (Tachie et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023). It is unclear whether the availability of processed vegan food products is enhancing or compromising the nutritional intake of contemporary vegans.

Theoretically, it is possible to meet all nutritional requirements on a vegan diet. Hess modelled vegan diets at four different energy levels for healthy adults and found only small changes in nutrient provision: slight increases in energy and several micronutrients, along with modest reductions in protein, zinc, and phosphorus, while overall adequacy was maintained (Hess 2022). However, it remains unclear whether individuals consuming a vegan diet are meeting nutritional requirements in practice. A 2021 systematic review found that in Europe, veganism is associated with low intakes of vitamins B2, B3, B12, and D in addition to calcium, iodine, potassium, selenium and zinc (Bakaloudi et al. 2021). A more recent study of 40 Danish vegans found that a high proportion of participants consumed less protein than recommended (Aaslyng et al. 2023), while a cross‐sectional study of 38 German vegans found that while their vitamin B12 status was adequate due to widespread supplementation use, their B12 intake from food alone was very low (median 0.43 μg/day) (Storz et al. 2023). Approximately one quarter of vegans in a New Zealand study were found to consume less protein than the Estimated Average Requirement (EAR) with limitations in protein quality (Soh et al. 2025). Given the well‐established role of adequate protein intake in supporting skeletal muscle mass and function, it is important to determine whether vegans are consistently meeting protein requirements to maintain optimal musculoskeletal health (Paddon‐Jones et al. 2015).

The dietary intake of Australian adults has not been comprehensively assessed since the 2011–2013 Australian Health Survey and vegan data was not included in this survey (Australian Bureau of Statistics 2014a). One nutrient that is known to be particularly at risk in a vegan diet is vitamin B12 (Kent et al. 2022). A 2021 Australian cross‐sectional study surveyed 1530 vegan women aged 18–44. A food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) specifically for estimating B12 intake reported that around 80% did not meet the EAR from food sources. Almost one quarter of respondents who took B12 supplements were still below the EAR (Benham et al. 2021), possibly due to the considerable variability in the supplementation methods reported by participants. Another cross‐sectional study of 48 vegans in Australia reported inadequate intakes of vitamin B12, omega‐3 fatty acids, calcium and iodine (Austin et al. 2025).

Individuals seeking to reduce their intake of animal‐based foods frequently turn to plant‐based meat alternatives (PBMA) (Flint et al. 2023; Gallagher et al. 2021). However, several studies have shown that many PBMAs are highly processed, being classified as ultra‐processed under NOVA in one study (Rizzolo‐Brime et al. 2023), with others reporting composition and processing features consistent with ultra‐processing (Gallagher et al. 2021; Pointke and Pawelzik 2022). A cross‐sectional analysis of UK Biobank data found mean ultra‐processed food (UPF) consumption was similarly high across a number of different eating patterns, including vegans (22.7%) (Chang et al. 2024). This is concerning as a recent meta‐analysis found that higher consumption of UPFs is associated with risk of adverse health outcomes including a higher risk of cardiovascular disease related mortality (RR 1.50, 95% CI 1.37–1.63) and type 2 diabetes (dose–response risk ratio 1.12, 95% CI 1.11–1.13) (Lane et al. 2024). However, emerging evidence suggests that associations between UPF consumption and health outcomes may differ according to the type and nutritional quality of foods consumed. Recent UK Biobank data found that higher adherence to healthy plant‐based dietary patterns was associated with 8%–28% lower risk of all‐cause mortality [HRQ4vsQ1 (95% CI): high‐UPF hPDI, 0.92 (0.85–1.00); low‐UPF hPDI, 0.91 (0.84–0.98)] and type 2 diabetes [high‐UPF hPDI, 0.89 (0.79–0.99); low‐UPF hPDI, 0.72 (0.65–0.79)]. These findings were irrespective of whether the diet contained relatively high or low levels of UPFs. Notably, greater adherence to a healthy plant‐based diet remained associated with a lower risk of cardiovascular disease even when UPF intake was relatively high [0.89 (0.82–0.96)] (Thompson et al. 2026).

Discretionary foods (DFs) are products high in added sugars, salt, saturated fat and alcohol (National Health and Medical Research Council 2013a) such as most sweet biscuits, cakes, desserts, chocolate, crisps, commercially fried food, sugar sweetened beverages and alcohol. DFs are excess to nutritional requirements but may help improve diet variety. Overconsumption of these products is associated with detrimental effects on nutrition and health outcomes (GBD 2017 Diet Collaborators 2019). While many DFs are classified as UPFs, the categories are not interchangeable; some UPFs are not discretionary, and UPFs vary widely in formulation and purpose. Certain UPFs support food safety and shelf stability, whereas discretionary foods are typically consumed in excess of nutritional requirements and contribute minimally to core food group recommendations. In 2011–2012, between 31% and 37% of energy intake was from DF in Australian adults (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2024) however DF intake in the Australian vegan population remains poorly characterised. As the number of novel plant‐based products increases in the food supply, including PBMAs that are often energy‐dense, variably fortified, and frequently classified as DFs, there is a need to better understand how contemporary vegan diets are constructed and how DF intake relates to nutrient adequacy. Mapping the contribution of DFs to total energy provides insight into the potential displacement of nutrient‐dense, whole‐food sources and helps identify where fortification or targeted guidance may be required to support adequacy in key micronutrients of concern.

The aim of this study was to assess the dietary and supplementary nutrient intake of a sample of Australian vegans via four‐day, semi‐weighed food record collected on two occasions and determine the contribution of discretionary foods to energy intake.

Subjects and Methods

Study Setting

This study was conducted in Australia where several key nutrients relevant to vegan diets—such as folic acid, iodine, and vitamin B12—may be present in fortified products. However, fortification levels and the range of fortified foods vary compared with other countries.

Study Subjects

Participants were invited via Australian vegan Facebook groups. Interested participants contacted the research team for screening and were eligible if they were aged ≥ 18 years, consumed a vegan diet (defined as no animal products) for ≥ 12 months, non‐lactating, non‐pregnant and had no medical condition that required dietary protein modification (e.g., kidney disease, cancer, impaired liver function). A baseline questionnaire was administered to determine duration of the vegan diet, inclusion of meat, dairy and eggs plus nutrient supplementation. Participants who reported following a vegan diet, but consumed animal foods, as well as those with incomplete food diaries, were excluded. Sixty‐six healthy adults met the inclusion criteria. Participants resided across five Australian states and territories. Ethics approval was granted by the University of Canberra Human Research Ethics Committee (13290). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. This paper is reported according to STROBE guidelines for cross‐sectional studies (von Elm et al. 2014).

Anthropometrics

Weighing individuals can be associated with weight stigma and negative psychological impacts (Benn et al. 2016; Winter et al. 2023). To minimise the potential discomfort, participants were offered the option of being weighed by the research dietitian but were free to decline. All participants consented to being weighed, self‐weigh, or sharing their self‐reported weight. Body weight was measured in light clothing, without shoes using a Salter digital platform scale after having emptied their bladder. Weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg. Height was measured to the nearest 0.5 cm without shoes using a portable height rod stadiometer (Wedderburn, Vic, Australia) according to the International Society for the Advancement of Kinanthropometry (ISAK) method (Marfell‐Jones et al. 2006). Remote participants (n = 48) self‐reported their height. If the participant owned a set of digital platform scales, they were instructed to weigh themselves in light clothing, first thing in the morning after voiding. A self‐reported body weight was accepted for participants who did not own scales. Previous research has found that, when self‐reporting anthropometric data, individuals tend to overestimate height and underestimate weight; however mean differences between self‐reported and measured values are not large and the correlation between self‐reported and measured data is high (Fayyaz et al. 2024; Ng et al. 2011). Furthermore, over‐ and under‐estimation is observed less in normo‐weight, younger adults as characteristic of our participants (Fayyaz et al. 2024).

Dietary Data Collection

The validated Australian smart phone diet tracker ‘Research Food Diary’ (Ambrosini et al. 2018) was used to collect an eight‐day, semi‐weighed food record (WFR). A semi‐weighed food diary is a prospective dietary method in which participants weigh some items and estimate others, offering a pragmatic balance between accuracy and respondent burden compared with fully weighed records, and is recognised as a validated tool for dietary assessment (Holmes et al. 2007).

Participants were instructed to continue with their usual habitual diet. To capture maximal dietary variety while minimising participant burden, intake was measured over a 4‐day period on 2 separate occasions. Participants were instructed to have a minimum 2‐week waiting period between each 4‐day collection period. For each 4‐day period, participants were instructed to include at least one weekend day.

Participants were instructed by an Accredited Practicing Dietitian (APD) on how to weigh foods and use the Research Food Diary app via an individual education session and phone and/or Zoom support. Participants were provided with household food digital scales (Model K:43328307, Anko) and instructed to measure food to the nearest 0.1 g. All food, beverages and dietary supplements (DS) were recorded. The APD was available to support participants throughout the data collection period. Data collection took place from October 2023 to March 2024.

Dietary Data Preparation

Food intake data was exported from Research Food Diary to FoodWorks Professional dietary analysis software (version 10; Xyris Software) using the AUSNUT 2011–2013 food composition database (Food Standards Australia New Zealand 2014a). The food diaries were cross‐checked by an APD. When an exact match for a reported food or beverage was not available within the AUSNUT database, the APD selected the closest AUSNUT item based on product description and nutrient profile similarity (energy and key macro‐/micronutrients); all such selections were independently reviewed by a second APD.

DS individual micronutrient data was obtained from the supplement information label and entered manually. Questionable food quantities were cross‐checked with participants and amended when confirmed to be implausible; for example, 100 crackers were incorrectly entered instead of 10 crackers. The mean intake of protein, fat, carbohydrate, omega‐3 fatty acids, fibre, vitamins (A, thiamin, riboflavin, B6, folate B12, C) and minerals (calcium, iodine, iron, magnesium, phosphorus, potassium, selenium, sodium, zinc) was calculated for each participant from food intake and combined food and supplement intake. In this study, DS intake included micronutrient, fatty acid and protein supplements. Protein supplements were defined as commercially available protein powders or concentrates/isolates consumed as standalone supplements.

The cut‐point method was used to assess the proportion of participants with mean intake below the Australian EAR for nutrients with an EAR (National Health and Medical Research Council 2006). The EAR is a daily nutrient level estimated to meet the requirements of half the healthy individuals in a particular life stage and gender group (National Health and Medical Research Council 2006). No EAR is established for Vitamin E, fibre, sodium, potassium and omega‐3fatty acids so the Adequate Intake (AI) was used as the reference value for these nutrients. The AI is the average daily nutrient intake level based on observed or experimentally‐determined approximations or estimates of nutrient intake by a group (or groups) of apparently healthy people that are assumed to be adequate (National Health and Medical Research Council 2006). An Upper Level of Intake (UL) is established for nutrients with a potential risk of adverse effects associated with higher intake. The UL is the highest average daily nutrient intake level likely to pose no adverse health effects to almost all individuals in the general population (National Health and Medical Research Council et al. 2006). Comparison was made to the UL where one existed. Nutrient reference values (NRVs) used in this study can be found in Table S1. Contribution of macronutrients to energy intake was calculated and compared to the Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Range (AMDR). The AMDR, as defined in the Australian Nutrient Reference Values, is an estimate of the range of intake for each macronutrient for individuals (expressed as per cent contribution to energy), which would allow for an adequate intake of all the other nutrients whilst maximising general health outcome (National Health and Medical Research Council 2006).

Discretionary Food Intake

Foods were classified as discretionary according to the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) discretionary flag list (Australian Bureau of Statistics 2014b). In the AUSNUT classification, food groups are hierarchical: the first 2 digits denote the major group, 3 digits the sub‐major group, and 5 digits the minor group; individual foods are coded at the 8‐digit level (i.e., specific food items/recipes). Following ABS guidance, we classified foods predominantly at the minor (5‐digit) group level where entire minor groups are clearly discretionary or non‐discretionary (e.g., confectionery; soft drinks). Where subgroups contained mixed foods or showed wide variability in nutrient profiles (e.g., burgers, pizzas, soups, dairy alternatives), we determined discretionary status at the 8‐digit food code level using the ABS nutrient profile criteria (fat, total sugars, sodium and/or calcium, as applicable). Food items not in the AUSNUT 2011–2013 food composition database or the ABS discretionary flag list were assessed and categorised using the criteria in the Australian Health Survey (AHS) User Guide (Australian Bureau of Statistics 2014b).

Under‐Reporting

It is widely acknowledged that participants of nutrition surveys have a tendency to under‐report their food intake (Macdiarmid and Blundell 1998). The likelihood of under‐reporting was assessed using the Goldberg cut‐off method (Goldberg et al. 1991) of calculating the ratio of energy intake (EI) and basal metabolic rate (BMR). BMR was calculated using the Schofield equation for adults based on age, gender and weight (Schofield 1985). Subjects with EI:BMR ratios below 0.9 (Fayet‐Moore et al. 2019) for a single day were then classified as under‐reporters and that day was excluded from the analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Microsoft Excel and DATAtab software were used for statistical analysis. The shape of the distribution of data was inspected using histograms. Dietary data was skewed hence reported as median and interquartile ranges. Categorical data was reported as counts (n) and frequencies (%). Correlation analysis was used to assess associations between participant characteristics and dietary outcomes. Specifically, associations were examined between sex or age and (i) number of serves of discretionary food (DF) intake and (ii) number of micronutrients below the EAR, as well as between total energy intake and number of DF serves. After inspection for normal distribution, Pearson correlation was used to assess the relationship between metric variables and a point‐biserial correlation was used to determine the relationship between sex and number of micronutrients below the EAR. A p value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

Energy Intake and Macronutrient Contribution

The median (IQR) energy intake was 7772 (6991–8695) kJ/day for females and 10 017 (9103–12 719) kJ/day for males. The distribution of total energy intake aligned with the AMDR for all macronutrients for both males and females. Macronutrient contributions to total energy intake were similar between sexes; protein contributed 17.3% in males and 16.1% in females, fat 30.3% and 32.8%, and carbohydrate 46.9% in both.

When dietary intake alone was considered, over one third of males consumed less than the EAR for riboflavin (30.8%), vitamin B12 (38.5%), calcium (38.5%), iodine (30.8%) selenium (46.2%) and zinc (53.8%). The proportion of males below the EAR improved marginally when supplementation was considered (vitamin B12 23.1%, calcium 30.8%, iodine 30.8%, selenium 38.5%, zinc 46.2%).

Only 4 participants consumed above the EAR for all nutrients. On average, participants consumed below the EAR for 3.5 ± 2.6 nutrients. Pearson correlation showed a significant negative association between total energy intake and the number of nutrients below the EAR, r(63) = −0.31, p = 0.012. There was no significant correlation between age or gender and the number of nutrients below the EAR.

All females and nearly all males (92%) had a median protein intake above the EAR. Median protein intake (diet plus supplements) was 1.2 ± −0.4 g/kg BM/day for females and 1.5 ± −0.8 g/kg BM/day for males.

All participants had inadequate omega‐3 fatty acid intakes from food sources alone. Some female participants took a vegan omega 3 supplement resulting in 23.1% consuming above the AI.

Upper Level of Intake

Where a UL existed, no participant exceeded this through diet alone. Intake of most micronutrients was below the UL from diet alone and diet plus supplements. The exceptions were vitamin E, iron, dietary folate equivalents (DFE) and zinc. Ten females (19.2%) consumed intakes above the UL for iron when diet and supplement intake was considered. Four females (7.7%) and 4 males (30.8%) consumed intakes above the UL for vitamin E (αTE) when diet and supplement intake was considered. One male (7.7%) consumed an intake above the UL for zinc when diet and supplement intake was considered. Four females and three males exceeded the UL (DFEs) from food alone. Folic acid intakes from fortified foods was not assessed. The UL for DFEs only applies to fortified foods and supplements (National Health and Medical Research Council 2013b).

Discretionary Foods

Almost all participants (98.5%) consumed DF at least once during the data collection period. The average number of serves of DF consumed by the participants was 3.3 ± 1.9 per day. One serve of DF provides 500–600 kJ (National Health and Medical Research Council 2013b). In total, DF contributed 24.2% ± 11.8% of total energy intake (Figure 2). Males aged 31–50‐years consumed the most serves of DF (5.5 serves per day) while males in the 51+ age category consumed the least (2.6 serves per day). Common discretionary foods consumed included vegan chocolate, sweet baked goods (e.g., donuts, cakes), meat alternatives, desserts, savoury items (e.g., crisps and pastries) and sports and protein beverages and powders. Pearson correlation showed a significant positive association between total energy intake, and the number of DF serves consumed, r(63) = 0.53, p < 0.001.

There was a moderate, significant negative correlation between DF serves and the number of nutrients below the EAR, r(63) = −0.31, p = 0.013.

There was no significant correlation between age or sex and number of serves of DF consumed.

Discussion

Plant‐based diets, including vegan diets, are beneficial for human health and the environment, yet there is limited investigation of the dietary intake of contemporary vegans. The aim of this cross‐sectional study was to assess the risk of nutritional inadequacy and DF intake of a sample of adult vegans in Australia.

Total energy intakes reported in our data were similar to those in other cross‐sectional studies of adult vegans (Austin et al. 2025; Soh et al. 2025; Waldmann et al. 2003). Overall, macronutrient distribution aligned with the AMDR for both males and females. Some studies have reported that a vegan diet is characterised by a higher consumption of carbohydrate when compared to an omnivorous diet (Bakaloudi et al. 2021; Papier et al. 2019), however the contribution of energy from carbohydrate for our participants (46.9% ± 7.90% kJ) was similar to that of the general Australian population (45% kJ) (Australian Bureau of Statistics 2014b).

The adequacy of dietary protein in vegan diets has long been controversial. However, provided energy needs are met, and a variety of foods are consumed, protein needs can theoretically be met (Craig et al. 2009). All participants (except one male) consumed more protein than the EAR from dietary sources, indicating that the probability of inadequate protein intake was low within our cohort. This is similar to the non‐vegan Australian population, where only 1% consumed less protein than the EAR (Australian Bureau of Statistcs 2015). A 2021 systematic review of 141 studies reported a low proportion of energy from protein intake in vegans (12.9% kJ) (Neufingerl and Eilander 2021). However, median intake of our participants was above 15% kJ. A 4‐day food diary study of 40 Danish adults reported that 40% of participants did not meet protein requirements when compared to the Nordic Nutrition Recommendation (0.8 g/kg BW) (14). This value is closer to the Australian RDI—the level at which 98% of the population are likely to consume sufficient protein. Comparison with an RDI will overestimate inadequacy of a sample of people (Murphy and Poos 2002), hence explains the difference to our data. Although protein intake in our vegan cohort fell within the AMDR, protein digestibility was not assessed, warranting cautious interpretation of protein adequacy.

The development of new techniques for measuring optimal protein requirement (e.g., indicator amino acid oxidation) has challenged whether current protein recommendations are sufficient to prevent skeletal muscle loss associated with ageing (Houston et al. 2008; Morse et al. 2001). Many review papers and position statements strongly recommend a higher protein intake of 1–1.5 g/kg BM (Bauer et al. 2019; Deutz et al. 2014; Paddon‐Jones et al. 2015). Our vegan participants met this target for total protein (males 1.4 g/kg BM ± 0.7; females 1.1 g/kg BM ± 0.3). We are unable to comment on the amino acid intake of our participants. A recent study of vegans in New Zealand found low intakes of amino acids such as leucine and lysine despite adequate total intakes of protein (Soh et al. 2025). Similarly, a Danish study found lows intakes of lysine, leucine and valine (Aaslyng et al. 2023). Assessment of protein quality warrants further investigation.

Our study identified a high proportion of participants with low intakes of vitamin B12, calcium, selenium, iodine and zinc (males only). This is consistent with findings in UK vegan populations where iodine, selenium, vitamin B12 and calcium have also been identified as nutrients of concern (Fallon and Dillon 2020), suggesting that the nutrient shortfalls observed in our Australian cohort may be common across Western vegan populations. Micronutrient inadequacies, particularly vitamin B12, calcium, iodine and zinc, are clinically important, as deficiencies in these nutrients contribute to chronic disease risk through impaired neurological function (Alruwaili et al. 2023), bone health (Akinwumi et al. 2025), thyroid regulation (Zimmermann and Boelaert 2015) and immune capacity (Sanna et al. 2018). These potential health consequences reinforce the importance of offering targeted nutritional guidance to ensure vegans attain adequate micronutrient levels.

Our participants were more likely to consume above the EAR when total energy intake, which included energy intake from DFs, was higher. This aligns with other findings suggesting that achieving the EAR for micronutrients becomes more challenging when energy intake is lower (National Health and Medical Research Council 2006) rather than implying any beneficial nutritional contribution from DFs. Cross‐sectional data of other vegan samples lacks consistency when assessing micronutrient adequacy. This may reflect cultural differences in dietary patterns as well as use of different reference standards to assess adequacy. However, across a suite of recent studies, vitamin B12, calcium and iodine stand out as being most at risk in vegan diets. An Australian study of 48 vegans used a combination of food frequency questionnaire, 2‐day food record and diet history and identified that intake of 48 vegans was low in vitamin B12, calcium and iodine (Austin et al. 2025). A German study of 38 vegans reported than mean dietary intake of vitamin B12 (0.43 ± 0.6 μg/day) was below recommendations of the Societies for Nutrition in Germany (DGE) but supplementation increased intake to levels similar to omnivores (Storz et al. 2023). The median intake of vitamin B12 was higher in our sample (females 1.8 (1.0–3.1) μg/day; males 2.7 (1.6–3.6 μg/day)). This might reflect differences in the availability of fortified foods in different food supplies. Vegans are advised to consume foods fortified with vitamin B12 and use a vitamin B12 supplement as the vegan diet excludes vitamin B12 rich foods (Benham et al. 2022; Fernandes et al. 2024; National Health and Medical Research Council 2013a). Only 33.3% (n = 22) of our participants took a B12 supplement. Higher use of vitamin B12 supplements (68%–92%) have been reported in other studies (Herter et al. 2025).

Our study found that 38.5% of males and 63.5% of females failed to achieve the calcium EAR from diet alone. This improved to 30.8% of males and 53.8% of females when DSs were accounted for. A vegan diet excludes dairy products which are valuable sources of calcium (Food Standards Australia New Zealand 2014a). However, it is possible to consume sufficient calcium from sources such calcium‐fortified plant milks, calcium‐set tofu and green leafy vegetables (Mangels 2014). Some studies have shown that vegans consume inadequate calcium (Bakaloudi et al. 2021; Clarys et al. 2014) whereas other researchers found the mean intake of calcium to be above the EAR (Kristensen et al. 2015; Neufingerl and Eilander 2021). Calcium is known to have lower bioavailability in plant foods (Muleya et al. 2024) so strategies to improve calcium bioavailability, such as limiting dietary sodium (Weaver et al. 1999), should be considered.

Iron deficiency is the most common micronutrient deficiency globally (Han et al. 2022). In Australia, 22.3% of women and 3.5% of men have depleted iron stores (Zhang et al. 2024). While iron is often considered a nutrient of concern in a vegan diet (Pawlak et al. 2018) studies report little difference in vegan iron intake when compared to omnivores, with many studies reporting higher dietary intake of iron among vegan individuals (Alles et al. 2017; Davey et al. 2003; Elorinne et al. 2016; Schupbach et al. 2017). We found only one male participant failed to meet the iron EAR from diet alone. However, the use of the cut‐point method for estimating the prevalence of iron deficiency in premenopausal women is limited (de Jong et al. 2024). This is due to the lack of a normal distribution for iron requirement due to differences in menstrual iron losses (Carriquiry 1999). While we found that only 3.8% of our female participants failed to meet the iron EAR from diet alone, the true prevalence of inadequate iron intake is likely higher. Further exploration of iron intake is warranted.

Polyunsaturated fatty acids are present in cell membranes, play crucial roles in regulating blood pressure and are thought to have cardiovascular and anti‐inflammatory benefits (Gonzalez‐Becerra et al. 2019). Linoleic Acid (LA) and α‐linolenic acid (ALA) are considered essential fatty acids (EFAs) as they cannot be produced endogenously and must therefore be consumed from dietary sources such as nuts and seeds (Saunders et al. 2013). ALA can be converted to eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), but conversion rates are variable, with a higher intake of LA thought to compromise conversion (Davis and Kris‐Etherton 2003). Similar to other studies, intake of EPA and DHA was almost non‐existent for our participants, likely due to the avoidance of fish (Burns‐Whitmore et al. 2019). Participants in our study largely achieved the AI for both LA and ALA with 23.1% of males and 3.8% of females failing to meet the AI for both EFAs. Drawing on data from vegan populations across several countries, Neufingerl and Eilander (2021) reported that vegan intake of ALA is similar to omnivores while LA tends to be higher Several researchers recommend that individuals following a vegan diet should consider strategies to improve fatty acid conversion rates, such as optimising ALA:LA ratios and additionally consuming a microalgal vegan omega‐3 supplement (Craig et al. 2021; Lane et al. 2022).

DSs include products containing ingredients such as protein, fatty acids, vitamins and minerals (Food Standards Australia New Zealand 2014b). In Australia, 47% of women and 34% of men are DS users (Burnett et al. 2017) which is similar to other developed countries where prevalence is reported to be between one third and two thirds of the population (Dickinson and MacKay 2014; Ilowiecka et al. 2022). Several studies report that DS use is higher among vegan individuals (Bogataj Jontez et al. 2024; Groufh‐Jacobsen et al. 2024), perhaps due to the more restrictive nature of the diet or the increased health‐consciousness often reported in vegan individuals (Haider et al. 2023; Wirnitzer et al. 2021). Approximately 77% of our participants took a DS. While DSs can be effective in correcting nutritional deficiencies, they are often taken by individuals who have no confirmed deficiency (Zhang et al. 2020). Despite the relatively high prevalence of DS use, particularly among females (83%), inconsistent or nutrient‐specific supplement use—and lower overall use among males—may explain why intakes of some micronutrients remained below the EAR for some participants.

Furthermore, higher intake of some micronutrients has been associated with a range of negative health outcomes (Pike and Zlotkin 2019). An Upper Level of Intake (UL) indicates the highest daily nutrient intake likely to pose no adverse effect (National Health and Medical Research Council 2013b). Whilst it is possible to exceed the UL through food alone, in practice it is rare (Institute of Medicine Subcommittee on Interpretation and Uses of Dietary Reference Intakes 2000). None of our participants exceeded the UL through diet alone, with the exception of DFEs. However, we did not assess participants' intake of folic acid from fortified foods. As the folic acid UL only applies to fortified foods and supplements, we were unable to determine the true prevalence of participants exceeding the folic acid UL. High supplemental intake of folic acid has been associated with neurological effects, as it is thought to mask B12 deficiency (Miller et al. 2024; Reynolds 2006). It is thus recommended that clinicians be vigilant when assessing supplement use and B12 status in the vegan population (Miller et al. 2024).

Vegetarians tend to have a higher diet quality when compared with omnivores (Groufh‐Jacobsen et al. 2023; Parker and Vadiveloo 2019; Walsh et al. 2023) and a higher diet quality score is generally associated with lower consumption of DF (James‐Martin et al. 2021; Tran et al. 2024). Our participants consumed an average of 3.3 ± 1.9 serve of DF per day representing a contribution of 24.2% ± 11.8% to total energy intake. This is less than the average Australian adult (31%–37%) (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2024) but more than the Australian recommendation of no more than three serves of DF daily for the majority of adults (National Health and Medical Research Council 2013b). We found a positive correlation between total energy intake and number of serves of DFs consumed. This is likely due to the relatively high energy density of DFs (Rangan et al. 2009). Although higher discretionary food (DF) intake was associated with fewer nutrients below the EAR, this pattern may be driven by a subset of DFs, namely protein‐replacement products, which can include nutrient fortification relevant to vegan diets. In contrast, confectionery and savoury snack DFs do not improve nutrient adequacy and primarily contribute energy, sugars, and sodium. Accordingly, this finding should not be interpreted as support for higher DF consumption overall, but as a signal that specific fortified protein alternatives within the DF category may influence the observed association.

PBMAs were popular options for our participants but the highly processed nature of many of these products increased DF intake. The ABS discretionary flag lists used in this study contained limited options for PBMAs, perhaps due to the lower prevalence of these items in our food supply at the time of printing and thus not being in the AUSNUT 2011–2013 food composition database. We thus turned to the Australian Health Survey (AHS) User Guide (Australian Bureau of Statistics 2014a) and assessed many PBMAs as DFs due to the high sodium, sugar and saturated fat content. Some researchers question how PBMAs should be classified as different Nutritional Classification Schemes (NCS) yield different classifications (Locatelli et al. 2024). For example, the NOVA classification system (Costa et al. 2021) has been found to classify many PBMAs as UPFs due to the presence of soy and pea protein isolates when other NCSs, such as Nutri‐Score (Dickie et al. 2022), resulted in a more favourable nutrition profile verdict. Despite these classification challenges, many PBMAs and fortified plant‐based beverages can nonetheless contribute meaningful amounts of protein and key micronutrients, supporting nutrient adequacy within plant‐based dietary patterns. (Yan et al. 2024).

As the interest in vegan diets increases and the demand for PBMA continues to grow, consideration should be given to the nutrition composition of these products. Additionally, determining optimal approaches to classifying PBMAs will assist in improving nutrition and health outcomes.

A strength of this study was the use of semi‐weighed food records to determine dietary intake which is considered to be the gold‐standard method of evaluating dietary intake (Fyfe et al. 2010). Although the 8‐day dietary recall may not fully capture usual intake it represents a pragmatic balance between participant burden and dietary detail. Secondly, the ‘Research Diet Diary’ application enabled the real‐time selection of a wide variety of food items through its integration with the AUSNUT database (Ambrosini et al. 2018). Real‐time logging of dietary data reduces recall bias that is common in dietary assessment studies (Naska et al. 2017). Lastly, the use of two (2) APDs to cross‐check diary entries and substitute nutritionally comparable items when foods and beverages were not in the AUSNUT database improved the accuracy of nutritional data.

Our cross‐sectional results provide a static view of a sample of Australian vegan individuals and may not reflect long‐term trends (Wang and Cheng 2020). Non‐response bias is likely to have occurred in our study. Participants who responded to our recruitment call (responders) likely differed in their characteristics to non‐responders (non‐response bias) (Wang and Cheng 2020). For example, our responders may have been more health conscious or more highly motivated to adhere to a vegan diet. We did not conduct a sample size calculation but included all eligible participants during the time period of this cross‐sectional study. While comparable studies have recruited similar numbers (Kristensen et al. 2015; Weikert et al. 2020), our sample is small, especially for males, which increases uncertainty in this subgroup. Under‐reporting of food intake has been acknowledged as a major limitation when collecting accurate dietary intake information (Macdiarmid and Blundell 1998), however in our study, the EI:BMR ratio was acceptable in 88.3% of cases, suggesting that under‐reporting may not pose a major concern. Furthermore, the use of smartphone dietary assessment applications, such as ‘Research Food Diary’, is reported to reduce under‐reporting due to high levels of personal attachment and their ability to capture real‐time dietary data (Ambrosini et al. 2018). Use of Adequate Intake (AI) values for some nutrients is a limitation of this study, as AIs cannot be used to estimate the prevalence of inadequacy in populations (National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) 2013b). And lastly, biochemical markers were not used in this study due to the associated cost and participant burden. We can assess estimated intake but make no assessment of the nutritional status of our sample (Shim et al. 2014).

Conclusion

Our findings show that while energy intake and macronutrient distribution among contemporary Australian vegans were generally within recommended ranges, several micronutrients—particularly vitamin B12, calcium, iodine, selenium, zinc (in males), and omega‐3 fatty acids—were commonly consumed below recommended levels, even with supplement use. This highlights the practical challenges vegans face in meeting micronutrient needs despite the theoretical adequacy of a well‐planned vegan diet and suggests that approaches limiting reliance on DFs and instead emphasising whole nutrient‐dense choices may help support adequacy.

This study adds important Australian data using detailed, semi‐weighed dietary records, providing insight into the nutritional patterns of a growing population group. As vegan diets become more common, understanding where inadequacies occur is essential for informing public health messaging, nutrition education, and development of fortified foods and supplements suited to vegan preferences and needs.

These findings underscore the importance of targeted guidance to support vegans in achieving adequate micronutrient intake. Future research incorporating biochemical markers and longer‐term dietary assessment would help clarify the nutritional status and health implications for this expanding dietary community.
