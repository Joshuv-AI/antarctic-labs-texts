Clinical, social, and situational determinants of prolonged recovery after commuting
Authors: Héctor Vargas-Garrido, Sandra Alvear-Vega, Hugo Núñez-Delafuente
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProlonged recovery from work following commuting accidents entails substantial personal, social, and economic costs; however, the social and health determinants of delayed recovery remain comparatively underexplored. Identifying factors associated with extended recovery is essential for informing prevention strategies and strengthening return-to-work policies.MethodsAdministrative records from Mutual de Seguridad (Chile) were analyzed using a balanced sample of 10,940 commuting accidents (50% with recoveries of ≥ 90 days and 50% with recoveries of < 90 days). Prolonged recovery was defined as an absence of 90 days or more. A probit model was estimated, and average marginal effects (AMEs) were computed. The dataset was randomly divided into training (70%) and validation (30%) samples to assess model stability.ResultsThe validation model confirmed the robustness of the estimated effects. A greater probability of prolonged recovery was observed among male workers (10%), individuals aged 45 years or older (12%), individuals with multiple diagnoses (20%), and cases classified as severe (59%). Situational factors were also relevant: Commuting accidents occurring between 18:00 and 24:00 h (6%) and during the Friday–Sunday period (5%) were associated with an increased likelihood of extended absence. In contrast, injuries classified as traumatic were associated with a lower probability of prolonged recovery (-43%).ConclusionThe findings underscore the importance of considering not only individual and clinical characteristics but also situational factors when designing prevention and return-to-work strategies for commuting-related injuries. Preventive policies should prioritize older workers, men, and late-day and late-week (Friday–Sunday) commuting contexts while recognizing that accident frequency does not necessarily coincide with the recovery burden. A commuting-specific perspective may strengthen occupational risk management and contribute to reducing prolonged work absence.Keywords: Commuting accidents, Prolonged recovery, Return to work, Occupational health, Social and health factors

Background

Extended recovery following occupational injuries has become an increasingly relevant concern for public health and occupational safety [1–6]. From the perspective of occupational health systems, facilitating a timely return to work is a priority for multiple stakeholders—including employers, governments, families, and society as a whole [7]—but it is particularly critical for workers themselves. Extended periods away from employment are associated with a greater risk of social isolation, depressive symptoms, income loss, reduced self-esteem, and poorer overall quality of life [1, 5–7].

The likelihood of resuming employment decreases as the duration of absence increases: After approximately 20 days away from work, the probability of return is close to 70%, whereas after 70 days, it decreases to almost 35% [2]. Longer recovery periods are also linked to greater injury severity, higher healthcare costs and productivity losses [8–11]. International statistics show a recent increase in the average number of workdays lost due to occupational injuries [12, 13], highlighting the growing burden on both the working population and social protection systems.

Occupational injuries are commonly classified as occurring either in the workplace or during travel related to work activities. Workplace injuries arise directly from job-related tasks, whereas commuting injuries occur while traveling between home and the workplace [14]. Evidence indicates that these two categories differ in their epidemiological patterns, risk profiles, and outcomes and therefore warrant separate analytical approaches [15–17].

Although the incidence of workplace injuries has declined in recent years, commuting-related injuries have increased [16, 18]. Commuting injuries are also associated with more severe consequences, including higher mortality, longer work absence, permanent disability, and greater economic costs [19–21]. Sleep-related impairments have been reported to have a stronger association with commuting injuries than with workplace injuries [15]. From a regulatory perspective, commuting injuries are treated heterogeneously across countries. They are recognized and covered by occupational insurance systems in Chile, as well as in countries such as Greece, Germany, and Brazil, but are excluded in other countries, including Denmark, Poland, and the United States [19, 22]. European occupational injury statistics also typically exclude commuting events [23], whereas in Chile, these injuries are incorporated into national social security records. Beyond specific national contexts, commuting-related injuries represent a significant component of the global burden of occupational trauma. International reports and empirical studies indicate that road traffic incidents occurring during work-related travel account for a substantial proportion of occupational fatalities and serious injuries worldwide, as highlighted by the World Health Organization and recent international research [24–26]. Despite their increasing frequency, greater severity, and regulatory complexity, commuting injuries remain relatively understudied [15–17]. Therefore, the present study examines the social and health factors associated with prolonged recovery following commuting-related injuries.

Defining an operational threshold for prolonged recovery is a key methodological decision in studies of work-related injuries. Across occupational contexts, average recovery periods typically range between three and five weeks, whereas more severe cases frequently extend beyond three months. On this basis, a cutoff of 90 days provides a pragmatic and empirically grounded criterion for identifying prolonged recovery [8–10, 20].

With respect to factors associated with the severity of injury and prolonged recovery following commuting accidents, research has identified longer absences with spinal cord injuries and severe acquired brain injuries, whereas shorter absences are more commonly linked to contusions and abrasions. In addition, longer hospital stays and the use of transport modes other than trams or trains are associated with extensive recovery periods [27]. Camino-López et al. [28] reported that serious and fatal injury ratios are substantially higher among men than women, whereas women tend to experience more severe accidents when commuting to work than when returning home.

Rey-Merchán et al. [29] analyzed occupational traffic accidents (72.6% of which were commuting accidents) and found that greater injury severity was associated with male sex, foreign workers, employment in smaller companies, age over 46 years, and crashes occurring during afternoon and evening hours. Similarly, although Fontaneda et al. [21] examined occupational accidents overall, they identified time peaks associated with longer recovery periods corresponding to typical commuting periods (travel to work, midday commuting, and the return home). They also reported an increase in the duration of sick leave throughout the week from Monday to Sunday among men (and from Monday to Friday among women).

Therefore, this study examines the social and health determinants associated with prolonged recovery periods (≥ 90 days) after commuting-related injuries in Chile using administrative records from Mutual de Seguridad, one of the country’s main occupational insurance providers. This approach allows for a more precise understanding of how personal characteristics (e.g., age and sex) and situational factors influence the probability of prolonged work absence following commuting accidents. By providing evidence from a middle-income Latin American context using large administrative data, this study addresses an important geographical and empirical gap in the literature on commuting-related injuries and prolonged recovery. The findings aim to contribute to international literature and inform prevention strategies targeting modifiable risk factors that increase the likelihood of delayed recovery.

Method

Study design

This study uses anonymized secondary data within the framework of the SUSESO Research Project P200-INNO297, approved by the Mutual de Seguridad Scientific Ethics Committee (Act No. 352, April 22, 2024). Under Chilean Law No. 16,744, Mutual de Seguridad is one of the country’s private occupational accident insurance administrators and covers approximately 40% of workers insured through the private mutual insurance system, ranking second in national coverage [13, 18]. In Chile, the occupational injury insurance system currently protects approximately 7.2 million workers. However, coverage is not universal, as a segment of the labor force—primarily individuals in informal employment—remains outside the system. Recent estimates suggest that about 26–28% of the workforce (approximately 2.5 million people) lack occupational insurance coverage.

The dataset used in this study was provided by Mutual de Seguridad and originally comprised 3,004,841 records and 33 variables, covering the period from January 1, 2011, to December 31, 2023, based on the date of admission. The database included a glossary of 37 variables, four of which were contained in free-text fields corresponding to anamnesis narratives. Following data understanding and feature engineering processes, the analytical dataset was reduced to 1,196,355 observations.

Subsequently, each case was classified according to Chilean legislation (Law No. 16,744) into three categories: occupational accidents, commuting accidents, and occupational diseases.

The reduction in the number of observations resulted from the application of systematic data cleaning procedures, including: (i) removal of records corresponding to re-entries; (ii) exclusion of anomalous values, such as ages greater than 120 years or negative values; (iii) removal of cases with missing data or data entry errors; and (iv) exclusion of records with diagnostic descriptions lacking a confirmed diagnosis.

Based on this process, the final analytical dataset for commuting accidents comprised 59,956 observations and 22 variables.

Dependent variable

Prolonged recovery was operationalized as the total number of days of absence required before a worker returned to work following a commuting injury. A threshold of 90 days or more was used to define prolonged absence, consistent with previous research [8–10, 20] and supported by evidence from a systematic review conducted within the framework of the SUSESO research project [30]. Accordingly, the outcome variable was coded as a binary indicator, where 1 represents prolonged absence (≥ 90 days) and 0 indicates shorter recovery periods.

Independent variables

Independent variables were selected based on prior evidence regarding factors associated with recovery time after occupational injuries, as identified in a qualitative systematic review conducted within the same research framework [30], which highlighted the role of clinical, social, and situational determinants. Accordingly, and considering the variables available in the administrative dataset after the data cleaning process described above, predictors were constructed and grouped into three domains: clinical (injury severity, presence of multiple diagnoses, and type of injury), sociodemographic (sex and age ≥ 45 years), and situational (time of occurrence, categorized as 18:00–24:00 vs. earlier hours, and day of the week, categorized as Friday–Sunday vs. Monday–Thursday). All variables were coded as binary indicators to ensure analytical consistency and interpretability in the regression models.

Although the administrative dataset included additional variables—such as occupation, economic sector, and geographic information—not all were incorporated into the final model. Some variables were excluded due to limitations in data completeness, coding consistency, or analytical suitability, while others were not retained because they did not contribute meaningfully to the model fit (e.g., alternative CIE-based injury classifications, urban versus rural residence, company size, health insurance, pre- or post-holiday timing, and nationality). Therefore, the final set of predictors was selected based on both data quality considerations and conceptual relevance. Analysis plan

The original database includes 59,956 observations of workers who experienced commuting accidents. Of these, 5,470 cases (9.12%) involved prolonged absence, whereas 54,486 (90.88%) did not. To mitigate potential issues related to class imbalance, a random sample of 5,470 observations from the nonprolonged absence group was selected, yielding a balanced dataset, thus providing a final analytical sample comprising 10,940 observations—5,470 (50%) workers with prolonged absence following a commuting accident and 5,470 (50%) without prolonged absence.

The balanced sample was subsequently randomly divided into two subsets: 70% of the observations were allocated to model estimation, and the remaining 30% were reserved for validation. Statistical analyses were conducted via Stata/SE version 18.

The decision to employ a balanced sample (50/50) through random undersampling of the majority class was driven by the need to mitigate bias toward the majority group (non-prolonged absence), which accounted for 90.88% of the original records. In probit models with high class imbalance, maximum likelihood estimation tends to underestimate the probability of the rare event, often resulting in high specificity but low sensitivity. Balancing the dataset al.lows the model to better capture the predictors associated with prolonged recovery, rather than simply reflecting the prevalence of the majority class. Although alternative approaches such as weighting or rare-event models could be considered, the undersampling strategy was preferred to maintain model interpretability and comparability with previous probit-based analyses in similar contexts [31]. While this approach involves discarding observations, the final analytical sample (N = 10,940) remains sufficiently large relative to the number of covariates included in the model.

Furthermore, the stability of the estimated Average Marginal Effects (AMEs) across both the training (70%) and validation (30%) subsamples supports the robustness of the results. Analyses using the full dataset were beyond the scope of this study; however, the consistency of results across subsamples suggests that the main findings are not driven by the sampling strategy. This approach is consistent with previous research [31].

Both probit and logit specifications were evaluated and yielded substantively similar results. The probit model was selected for the final analysis to ensure consistency with previous research in this field and within the broader project framework. Given that both models produce comparable estimates in binary outcome settings, the choice of probit does not affect the substantive interpretation of the results. Under this specification, all independent variables were defined as binary indicators representing distinct conceptual domains (clinical, sociodemographic, and situational factors), which reduces the likelihood of multicollinearity. Although non-linear models such as probit are not inherently immune to multicollinearity, the use of conceptually distinct predictors and the absence of instability or inconsistent coefficient behavior across model estimations suggest that collinearity did not materially affect the results. Model fit

Model performance was assessed via a split-sample approach, with 70% of the observations used for estimation and the remaining 30% reserved for validation. The validation sample comprised 3,283 observations. The probit specification achieved statistical significance at the 95% confidence level on the basis of the chi-square test. The classification accuracy, measured by the count R², reached 79.3%, whereas the Cox–Snell and Nagelkerke indicators showed that the explanatory variables accounted for approximately 36% to 48% of the variation in the outcome. In terms of classification performance, the model exhibited a sensitivity of 70.6% and a specificity of 87.9%. These results indicate a moderate explanatory capacity, which is consistent with observational data, and suggest that a proportion of the variability in prolonged recovery remains unexplained by the variables included in the analysis.

In addition to the goodness-of-fit indicators reported above, model discrimination was further evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The AUC was 0.86 in the training sample and 0.85 in the validation sample, indicating good discriminative ability (see Table 1). It is important to note that some classification-based metrics, such as Count R², may be influenced by the use of a balanced dataset and therefore should be interpreted with caution. In this context, the AUC provides a more robust and threshold-independent measure of model performance. The consistency of AUC values across training and validation samples further supports the stability and generalizability of the model.

Results

Average marginal effects

Table 2 presents the average marginal effects (AMEs) estimated for the training sample. The results indicate that the probability of prolonged absence increases for male workers (7.5%), individuals aged 45 years or older (10.1%), workers with multiple diagnoses at the time of the accident (18.9%), and cases classified as severe (56.8%). In addition, accidents occurring between 18:00 and 24:00 h (4.3%) and during the late-week period (Friday–Sunday) (6.2%) are also associated with a greater likelihood of prolonged absence. In contrast, the presence of traumatic injuries is associated with a lower probability of prolonged absence (-35.0%).

The findings from the validation sample are consistent with those obtained in the training model, both in direction and magnitude (Table 2). Specifically, an increased probability of prolonged absence can be observed for male workers (10.4%), workers aged 45 years or older (11.9%), individuals with multiple diagnoses (20.1%), severe accidents (58.5%), accidents occurring between 18:00 and 24:00 h (5.8%), and those occurring during the late-week period (Friday–Sunday) (5.0%). Similarly, traumatic injuries continue to exhibit a protective association, reducing the probability of prolonged absence by 43.2%.

Discussion

The likelihood of a prolonged recovery following a commuting accident (≥ 90 days) is strongly associated with personal characteristics, clinical factors, and accident-related conditions, in line with previous evidence on prolonged recovery after occupational injuries in general [8, 20, 30, 34–37] and commuting injuries in particular [27–29]. Specifically, injury severity and the presence of multiple diagnoses are consistently linked to a greater likelihood of extended recovery, as both variables capture, directly or indirectly, the underlying clinical complexity of the event. Research has shown that more severe injuries are associated with longer treatment durations, delayed functional recovery, and prolonged return-to-work periods for both workplace [8, 11, 34] and commuting accidents [27].

In line with this evidence, the present findings suggest that severity and multiple diagnoses represent complementary expressions of the same clinical construct, reflecting both the intensity of the injury and the complexity of subsequent therapeutic management, which together translate into longer recovery periods. These results further support the relevance of the 90-day threshold as an operational indicator of long-term recovery, as previously documented in the literature [8–10, 20] and confirmed by a systematic review [30]. Moreover, when the same administrative database and identical cutoff points were used, 4.76% of all workplace accidents were classified as prolonged absence (unpublished analyses), whereas in the present study 9.12% of all commuting accidents exceeded this threshold. This contrast reinforces the notion that commuting accidents tend to involve greater severity and, consequently, a greater burden of lost work time.

A substantial body of research has highlighted the role of workforce aging in shaping recovery trajectories following occupational injuries [26, 38–40]. In the context of commuting accidents, workers aged 45 years and older have been shown to face a greater risk of serious and very serious injuries [29], which aligns with the present finding that this age group experiences longer recovery periods. Accumulating evidence further indicates that recovery tends to become slower and more complex with advancing age [10, 20, 29, 34], which has been attributed to increased biological vulnerability, a higher prevalence of comorbid conditions among older workers [26, 38], and an age-related reduction in reaction time and functional reserve [41].

As a result, population aging has emerged as a central concern for occupational health systems [39, 40]. This issue becomes even more salient when considering demographic trends, as the average age of injured workers in Spain increased from 35 to 41 years between 1996 and 2015 [10], whereas in Chile, the mean age of the working population rose from 41.3 years in 2010 to 43.8 years in 2018 [42]. These trends run parallel to the observed increase in the average number of recovery days following occupational injuries [12, 13, 18]. Given that age exerts a cumulative influence on both injury severity and recovery duration, the present findings stress the need for age-sensitive prevention strategies and tailored return-to-work policies targeting workers aged 45 years and older. With respect to gender, previous research has consistently shown that men are more likely to experience longer periods of work absence following both occupational accidents in general [10, 20, 34] and commuting accidents specifically [16, 28, 29]. The present findings are consistent with this evidence and may be explained by several interrelated factors. In Chile, the statutory retirement age is 65 years for men and 60 for women; consequently, men remain in the workforce for a longer period beyond the age of 45, which, as observed in this study, represents a threshold at which vulnerability to more severe accidents and prolonged recovery increases.

More broadly, gender differences in risk-related behaviors may also contribute to this pattern. Compared with women, men tend to engage more frequently in risk-taking behaviors, have higher levels of alcohol consumption, and participate in recreational activities that may compromise adequate rest and recovery, among other factors [43]. Together, these elements may partially account for the greater probability of prolonged absence observed among male workers.

With regard to injury type, the protective association observed between the category of traumatic injuries and prolonged absence due to a commuting accident should be interpreted in light of the broad diagnostic scope of this classification within the ICD-10 framework. Although the ICD category of traumatic injuries encompasses a wide spectrum of conditions, in this dataset it likely encapsulates a substantial proportion of relatively minor lesions, such as contusions and abrasions, which are typically associated with shorter recovery times. This interpretation is consistent with previous evidence showing that median work absence is shortest among individuals with contusions and abrasions in commuting accidents [27]. Overall, these findings suggest that the observed protective effect may reflect the predominance of less severe traumatic diagnoses within this ICD-based category rather than an intrinsic protective effect of trauma per se.

Other relevant contributions of this study include the identification of situational factors associated with commuting accidents and prolonged recovery. First, with respect to time of day, our results indicate that longer recovery periods are more likely when commuting accidents occur between 18:00 and 24:00 h. These results are consistent with previous studies reporting higher severity ratios for commuting-related crashes during the late afternoon and evening than for other times of the day [16, 29]. In a similar vein, although they examined occupational accidents overall, Fontaneda et al. [21] identified a pattern of an increasing number of lost workdays as the day progressed from 00:00 to 24:00 h, as well as a third peak in recovery time related to injuries during the evening. This evening peak was attributed to commuting activities, corresponding to the end of the workday, which typically occurs between 19:00 and 22:00 h for men and approximately 22:00 h for women. Taken together, these findings point to a greater severity and longer recovery associated with commuting accidents occurring during evenings.

Second, with respect to the day of the week, our results indicate a greater likelihood of prolonged recovery for commuting accidents occurring late in the week (Friday–Sunday) than for those taking place from Monday to Thursday. Previous evidence on all types of occupational accidents focused on a temporal gradient in lost workdays, with an increase from Monday to Sunday among men, whereas for women, this pattern was observed only from Monday to Friday [21]. The present findings may help clarify this apparent gender difference, as Fontaneda et al. did not distinguish between workplace accidents and commuting accidents. In this regard, disaggregating accident types may reveal temporal patterns that remain obscured when occupational accidents are examined as a single aggregate category.

Moreover, previous analyses using the same administrative database but focusing on workplace accidents did not identify significant associations between prolonged recovery and either the day of the week that the accident took place or the time of day, in contrast to the patterns observed in the present study for commuting accidents. This divergence suggests that commuting accidents may be more sensitive to situational and contextual factors than accidents in the workplace. Consistently, more severe injuries tend to occur when workers perform nonroutine tasks or operate outside their usual work environment [9, 21, 37, 44], conditions that are inherent to commuting activities.

Finally, the model validation results confirm the stability of the estimated effects, both in direction and magnitude, reinforcing the robustness of the identified associations. Taken together, these findings provide empirical evidence from Chile that complements international research and highlights the need for preventive strategies targeting higher-risk groups, including workers aged 45 years and older, men, individuals sustaining severe injuries, and commuting accidents occurring during late-week (Friday–Sunday) and evening hours. Moreover, the convergence of several risk factors—such as older age, late-week (Friday–Sunday) occurrence, and late-day timing—suggests that mechanisms related to fatigue accumulation, circadian disruption, and sleep deprivation may contribute to increased injury severity and delayed recovery, as documented in previous studies [8, 15, 20, 29, 30, 36, 45].

Additionally, the present findings allow the identification of an interesting pattern linking the frequency of commuting accidents with their severity and recovery duration, in which both trends tend to move in opposite directions. For example, the so-called “weekday effect” has been widely documented, showing that occupational accidents occur more frequently at the beginning of the week and gradually decline toward Sunday [45]. A similar pattern is observed for commuting accidents in the present database, where 76.28% of events occur between Monday and Thursday, whereas only 23.72% occur between Friday and Sunday; however, accidents associated with longer recovery times are disproportionately concentrated during the latter period.

A comparable contrast emerges with respect to time of day. Previous evidence indicates that most commuting accidents occur during morning hours. For example, Rey-Merchán et al. [29] reported that 54% of commuting accidents in Spain take place between 06:00 and 13:00 h. In the present dataset, 84.26% of the commuting accidents occur between 00:00 and 18:00, whereas only 15.74% occur between 18:00 and 24:00, which nevertheless corresponds to the time window with the highest probability of prolonged recovery.

Similarly, commuting accidents are more common among younger workers, with 77.31% occurring among individuals under 45 years of age, whereas more severe outcomes are concentrated among workers aged 45 years and older (22.68%) [29]. A similar pattern is observed by sex: Although a greater proportion of commuting accidents involve women in both Spain [28] and Chile [13, 18], accidents associated with longer recovery periods are more prevalent among men in both countries, as shown by the present study and prior evidence [29].

Taken together, these findings suggest that periods and population groups with higher accident frequency do not necessarily coincide with those associated with greater severity and longer recovery, highlighting the importance of distinguishing between accident incidence and clinical and functional consequences in the design of preventive strategies.

From a practical perspective, these findings suggest the need for differentiated interventions targeting both early clinical management and commuting risk prevention. Occupational health systems could prioritize early follow-up for workers with a higher probability of prolonged recovery—particularly men, workers aged 45 years or older, and those sustaining severe injuries or multiple diagnoses—through early functional assessment, targeted rehabilitation programs, and structured return-to-work monitoring. At the same time, companies and occupational insurance institutions could strengthen commuting risk-prevention strategies by promoting road safety awareness, fatigue management, and risk communication during high-risk commuting periods. Importantly, these measures should account for commuting across multiple transport modes—including private vehicles, public transport, cycling, and walking—rather than focusing exclusively on driving.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the analyses rely on secondary administrative data that, despite their large scale and comprehensive coverage, were not specifically collected for research purposes. As a result, certain relevant dimensions—such as psychosocial conditions, the organizational climate, or detailed information on preexisting health conditions—could not be examined due to restricted data availability and granularity. In addition, prolonged recovery was operationalized via a 90-day cutoff derived from prior evidence and common practice; however, this binary definition may not fully reflect the diversity and complexity of recovery trajectories across different occupational contexts and injury profiles. In line with these limitations, although the model demonstrated good predictive performance, a substantial proportion of the variability in prolonged recovery remains unexplained, as suggested by the pseudo-R² indicators. This is expected in observational studies using administrative data, where relevant determinants may not be captured. Potential factors not included in the dataset include workplace characteristics, commuting mode, urban infrastructure, and individual behavioral risk factors. Future research incorporating these dimensions may provide a more comprehensive understanding of prolonged recovery following commuting accidents.

Furthermore, descriptive visualizations of recovery duration (e.g., histograms stratified by sex and age group) were not included in the present study but may further enhance the understanding of recovery patterns and represent a useful direction for future research.

Conclusions

This study identifies a set of social, clinical, and situational factors associated with prolonged recovery following commuting-related injuries in Chile. Older age (≥ 45 years), male gender, greater injury severity, the presence of multiple diagnoses, and accidents occurring from Friday to Sunday and during evening hours were consistently associated with a greater probability of prolonged work absence. In contrast, traumatic injuries exhibited a protective association, likely reflecting the predominance of less severe diagnoses within this broad diagnostic category.

Beyond individual risk factors, the findings reveal a systematic divergence between accident frequency and recovery burden: Periods and population groups with higher incidences of commuting accidents do not necessarily coincide with those experiencing the most severe outcomes and longest recovery times. This distinction highlights the importance of targeting preventive interventions not only toward high-frequency events but also toward contexts and groups characterized by elevated clinical and functional consequences.

Overall, the results highlight the need for differentiated prevention strategies and tailored return-to-work policies that explicitly address commuting-related risks, particularly among older workers, men, and individuals exposed to higher-severity accidents during late-day and late-week (Friday–Sunday) travel. Strengthening occupational risk management and social protection systems from a commuting-specific perspective may contribute to reducing the burden of prolonged work absence and improving workforce health and productivity.
