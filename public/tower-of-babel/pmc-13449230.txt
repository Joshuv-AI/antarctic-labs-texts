Secretagogue laxatives as a potential strategy to overcome adherence barriers to over-the-counter treatment for clozapine-induced constipation
Authors: Jonathan G. Leung, Matej Markota, Xiao Jing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionClozapine-induced constipation may occur in at least 60% of patients, with first-line options typically involving polyethylene glycol or sennosides. However, there may be adherence barriers to an over-the-counter bowel regimen due to the dosage form, lack of insurance coverage, or lack of benefit. One possible solution to address these adherence barriers may include prescribing a secretagogue laxative. However, there is limited literature supporting the use of secretagogue laxatives in clozapine-associated constipation. A case series is presented in which adherence barriers impeded the use of over-the-counter laxatives, and a secretagogue laxative was implemented to manage clozapine-induced constipation.Case ReportIn 4 cases, lubiprostone or linaclotide was initiated, either as monotherapy or in combination with over-the-counter laxatives. Reasons for secretagogue laxative initiation included: (1) reducing a complex over-the-counter laxative regimen, (2) a lack of insurance coverage of over-the-counter laxatives, (3) dissatisfaction with polyethylene glycol’s palatability, and (4) cognitive impairment leading to decreased adherence to polyethylene glycol.DiscussionIt is important that clozapine-induced constipation is managed adequately to prevent severe consequences of gastrointestinal hypomotility. In addition to timely initiation and titration of constipation pharmacotherapy, adherence should be assessed. When adherence barriers to the use of over-the-counter laxatives are present, the use of a secretagogue laxative may be an option, recognizing there is limited evidence to guide this decision.ConclusionIn these cases, adherence barriers to over-the-counter laxatives were identified. A secretagogue laxative was implemented, well-tolerated, and able to be continued for the duration of follow-up.

Introduction

Clozapine is FDA approved for treatment-resistant schizophrenia and for the reduction of recurrent suicidal behavior in schizophrenia and schizoaffective disorder. Clozapine remains underused, in part due to potential adverse drug reactions such as severe neutropenia, myocarditis, and seizures.1 Constipation is another adverse drug reaction that may occur in at least 60% of patients treated with clozapine.2 Clozapine increases gastrointestinal transit time across the entire tract, with most patients having multiregional dysmotility.3 Although mild constipation from clozapine is not life-threatening, if left unmanaged, it may progress to bowel obstruction, ileus, or perforation. Severe cases of clozapine-induced gastrointestinal hypomotility (CIGH) have been associated with a mortality rate of 18% to 25%.4,5 This highlights the need to proactively monitor bowel status for patients prescribed clozapine to ensure adequate prevention and treatment of CIGH.

There is a lack of high-quality evidence supporting the use of any one specific medication for preventing and managing CIGH, and therefore, no definitive guidelines exist.6 However, there are similarities between what is typically suggested for CIGH in the literature (mostly via expert opinion) and what is recommended in chronic idiopathic constipation (CIC) guidelines. The 2023 guidelines for CIC from the American Gastroenterological Association and American College of Gastroenterology suggest the use of over-the-counter (OTC) agents, including polyethylene glycol (PEG) or sennosides, as an early consideration or “attractive first-line option” due to effectiveness, availability, and low costs.7 These guidelines give a stronger recommendation for PEG over sennosides. Guidelines published by the American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons more definitively recommend osmotic laxatives (eg, PEG, magnesium salt–based products) as first-line pharmacotherapy for CIC and stimulant laxatives (eg, senna, bisacodyl) as rescue therapy or a second-line option.8 One caveat is that fiber supplementation (ie, bulk laxatives) is suggested first before other OTC laxatives in CIC guidelines. This approach is generally avoided in CIGH management, as bulking laxatives (eg, methylcellulose, psyllium) require adequate gastrointestinal peristalsis, which is diminished by clozapine. Thus, the use of a bulking laxative with clozapine may further worsen CIGH, especially in the setting of inadequate fluid intake. Additionally, differentiation from CIC is important, as CIGH has different underlying mechanisms and can be fatal.

Although polyethylene glycol or sennosides with or without docusate are generally considered first-line options for CIGH, adherence barriers may be underrecognized. These barriers might include a lack of OTC medication coverage by a patient’s pharmacy benefit plan and the need for multiple OTC laxatives to manage CIGH, which adds complexity to a medication regimen. In general, OTC agents recommended to patients may not be formally prescribed. This places greater responsibility on a patient to obtain the medication, potentially leading to inappropriate or suboptimal use.9 This raises issues surrounding accessibility and adherence with OTC laxatives for the management of CIGH. A secretagogue laxative (ie, lubiprostone, linaclotide, or plecanatide), FDA approved for CIC, is recommended in CIC guidelines as a potential alternative after OTC laxative treatment failure.7 However, the American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons guidelines suggested studies with longer treatment duration are needed to determine the efficacy and safety of secretagogues or motility agents for CIC. Literature involving secretagogue laxatives for CIGH is limited to mostly descriptive reports, but a medication from this class might be a potential alternative when patients experience barriers to optimal adherence with OTC laxatives. Presented is a case series in which secretagogues were trialed to help overcome adherence and access barriers to an OTC-based bowel regimen for CIGH.

Case 1

A 41-year-old female with a history of schizoaffective disorder, bipolar type, and medication non-adherence was admitted to a psychiatric inpatient unit due to worsening disorganization. Her medication regimen at the time of admission included clozapine 150 mg at bedtime and metoprolol tartrate 25 mg twice daily. As adherence could not be confirmed, clozapine was retitrated. Initially, PEG 17 g daily as needed for constipation was empirically ordered. The PEG was changed to scheduled dosing by the fifth day of hospitalization for a lack of bowel movements over the preceding 3 days. The clozapine dose was 150 mg at bedtime at that time. Abdominal radiography showed a normal bowel gas pattern, moderate stool throughout the colon, and no obstruction. The PEG was increased to 17 g twice daily, and 1 tablet of sennosides/docusate 8.6 mg/50 mg twice daily was added. This was initially effective in maintaining bowel movements at least every other day; however, constipation worsened, with bowel movements about every third day, as clozapine was titrated to 300 mg at bedtime by hospital day 11.

On hospital day 20, the patient had gone 5 days without a bowel movement and was treated with a bisacodyl 10-mg suppository that was effective in producing a large bowel movement. Sennosides/docusate was increased to 3 tablets of sennosides/docusate 8.6 mg/50 mg twice daily, but the patient required a docusate 283-mg enema on hospital day 28. Lactulose 10 grams, 3 times daily, was then added, with some improvement in bowel movement frequency (every 1-3 days). Before discharging to a residential treatment program within the same institution, the residential treatment team, who had met with the patient, suggested initiation of a secretagogue with a plan to taper other laxatives as able. This was owing to concerns about adherence to a complex bowel regimen, which now consisted of PEG 17 grams twice daily, 3 tablets of sennosides/docusate 8.6 mg/50 mg twice daily, lactulose 10 g 3 times daily, and, as needed, bisacodyl 10 mg suppositories. Linaclotide 145 mcg was given in the hospital on the day of discharge. During transitions of care to the residential facility, it was discovered that linaclotide was not covered by the patient’s insurance, and the prior authorization was denied. However, a prior authorization was approved for lubiprostone and initiated at 24 mcg twice daily. Within 24 hours of starting lubiprostone, the patient was having loose stools and had 1 episode of bowel incontinence. Lactulose was then discontinued, and PEG was changed to as needed. This mitigated concern for bowel incontinence, but the patient still reported loose stools multiple times per day. Sennosides/docusate was subsequently tapered to discontinuation over the course of a week, resulting in daily formed bowel movements. The patient continued to report daily bowel movements without straining to the current day (approximately 15 months later) without tolerability concerns while continuing on lubiprostone.

Case 2

A 29-year-old man with a history of schizophrenia was discharged from the hospital following an admission for worsening psychotic symptoms. He was prescribed paliperidone palmitate 234 mg intramuscular and risperidone 5 mg by mouth daily. At an outpatient follow-up appointment, an attempt to taper risperidone resulted in worsening hallucinations and paranoia. Clozapine would be initiated at 12.5 mg at bedtime, increasing to 25 mg at bedtime a week later with continued titration by 25 mg weekly. Once he reached a dose of 200 mg at bedtime, he reported new-onset constipation, and his bowel movement pattern changed from twice per day, to every other day accompanied by straining. He was initially prescribed PEG 17 g daily, which resulted in daily bowel movements. The patient noted that PEG was not covered by insurance and that he would need to pay out of pocket. Over 3 months, clozapine was titrated to 400 mg at bedtime, and risperidone was successfully tapered and discontinued. Once clozapine reached 400 mg at bedtime, paliperidone palmitate was also discontinued without further concern for worsening psychotic symptoms. However, at this dose of clozapine, constipation worsened. Despite recommendations to increase PEG to 34 g daily, cost became a barrier to adherence. When he was able to obtain PEG, it did help maintain a bowel movement pattern of approximately once or twice per day. However, without PEG, he would have a bowel movement daily or every other day that would be difficult to pass and would be associated with nausea and a bloating sensation.

To use a less expensive alternative, it was recommended to change PEG to 1 tablet of sennosides/docusate 8.6 mg/50 mg twice daily. However, sennosides/docusate was also not covered by insurance and ultimately remained cost prohibitive. It was decided to assess the cost to the patient and try lubiprostone 24 mcg twice daily. Owing to insurance formulary reasons, this would be switched to linaclotide 145 mcg daily. Copays were noted not to be cost prohibitive, and linaclotide use resulted in the resolution of constipation. However, the patient did report daily loose bowel movements. The offer to trial linaclotide 72 mcg daily was made, but the patient wished to remain at the current dose. The patient currently remains on linaclotide 145 mcg daily (total use of 15 months to the current day) without constipation but with ongoing reports of loose bowel movements.

Case 3

A 55-year-old woman with a history of idiopathic Parkinson’s disease, major depressive disorder, and generalized anxiety disorder was admitted to a psychiatric hospital following the onset of hallucinations, paranoia, and confusion. She also had a history of chronic constipation and several related emergency department visits, which were conservatively managed with laxatives. Her medication regimen at the time of hospitalization included magnesium hydroxide 1200 mg/15 mL daily as needed for constipation, carbidopa/levodopa 25 mg/100 mg every 3 hours, and sertraline 50 mg daily. During the hospitalization, sertraline was discontinued, and quetiapine was initiated and titrated to 225 mg at bedtime. Two tablets of sennosides/docusate 8.6 mg/50 mg twice daily and PEG 17 g daily were started for constipation. Owing to ongoing paranoia and auditory hallucinations, clozapine was initiated and titrated to 200 mg/day, while quetiapine was reduced to 75 mg at bedtime, which would be the doses at the time of discharge. Carbidopa/levodopa remained unchanged. On hospital discharge after 27 days, she was prescribed 2 tablets of sennosides/docusate 8.6 mg/50 mg twice daily, sennosides 8.6 mg daily as needed, PEG 17 g daily, and bisacodyl 10-mg suppositories as needed. However, during transitions of care, the patient mistakenly started taking only 1 tablet of sennosides/docusate 8.6 mg/50 mg twice daily for her bowel regimen. Seven days after discharge, the patient was seen in a transition of care clozapine clinic, awaiting follow-up at another health system. At the appointment, the patient reported no bowel movement over the previous 3 days. Over 2 weeks, sennosides/docusate 8.6 mg/50 mg was increased back to 2 tablets twice daily, with PEG 17 g daily restarted. After 2 weeks, the patient was seen again with a recommendation to increase PEG to 17 g twice daily. However, the patient declined, noting dissatisfaction with the taste and texture of the medication, and she also reported being only partially adherent to the PEG 17 g daily for this reason. Bowel movements were described as occurring twice per week and were hard and small, and the patient also reported using 2 sodium phosphates (19 g monobasic sodium phosphate/7 g dibasic sodium phosphate) enemas that provided minimal benefit. With growing anxiety regarding constipation before this second follow-up visit, she had requested an abdominal radiography from a local provider that demonstrated a large stool burden but no indication of obstruction. Lubiprostone 24 mcg twice daily was then added, while maintaining the sennosides/docusate dose but with the discontinuation of PEG. There were no insurance barriers with lubiprostone. The patient reported a bowel movement within 48 hours of starting lubiprostone. Her reported bowel movement frequency improved to daily or every third day over the remaining 2 months of follow-up before transitioning care to another facility.

Case 4

A 65-year-old man with a history of schizophrenia, who had been prescribed clozapine for over a decade, was admitted for management of a small bowel obstruction (SBO). His psychiatric regimen included clozapine 450 mg/day, bupropion, extended release 450 mg daily, and trazodone 100 mg at bedtime. His medical history was complex, including diabetes with secondary autonomic failure, mild cognitive impairment, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease, systolic heart failure, hypertension, iron-deficient anemia, and chronic constipation. There was no history of prior abdominal surgery. Other medications prescribed included amlodipine 10 mg daily, atorvastatin 80 mg daily, empagliflozin 10 mg daily, insulin glargine 10 units daily, liraglutide 1.8 mg daily, metoprolol succinate 150 mg daily, and ferrous sulfate 325 mg 3 times weekly.

The patient’s bowel regimen before hospitalization for SBO included PEG 17 g twice daily and sennosides 17.2 mg twice daily with reported bowel movements occurring every second or third day. However, it was documented that he would frequently forget to prepare PEG for self-administration, as it “was not in his pillbox.” Despite this bowel regimen being recommended at the time of discharge, the patient was readmitted 2 months later with progressive constipation and concern for feculent aspiration in the setting of PEG nonadherence. Computed tomography of the abdomen revealed distention of the small bowel and a large amount of stool burden in the colon. Management included nasogastric decompression and an aggressive bowel regimen. Historically, prior attempts to lower clozapine resulted in worsening psychotic symptoms, and it was decided to focus on changing his bowel regimen. Lubiprostone 24 mcg twice daily would be initiated along with PEG 34 g twice daily and bisacodyl 5 mg daily, resulting in reported bowel movements about every other day. In transitions of care to the outpatient setting, no insurance barriers for lubiprostone were noted. A post-discharge gastrointestinal work-up, coordinated approximately 1 month later, included computed tomography enterography, upper and lower endoscopy, and a gastric emptying study, which was negative for obstruction and gastroparesis. The patient would relocate from independent living to an assisted living facility a month later and develop diarrhea. The patient’s PEG was reduced to 17 g twice daily, and lubiprostone was reduced to 24 mcg daily. Nine months later, he presented to the hospital for vomiting, abdominal distention, and obstipation, in which he was reported to be only partially adherent to PEG. Following this hospitalization, the patient was transitioned from lubiprostone to linaclotide 145 mcg along with twice-daily PEG 17 g and 2 tablets of sennosides/docusate 8.6 mg/50 mg twice daily. Daily loose stools subsequently developed but were acceptable to the patient. The PEG was decreased to once daily, although the patient reported skipping occasional doses. Currently, there have been no acute gastrointestinal concerns in the subsequent 7 months.

Discussion

In this series of 4 patients, a secretagogue agent was initiated to address CIGH. In each case, barriers were prohibiting optimal adherence to OTC laxatives, resulting in the selection of lubiprostone or linaclotide. Identified barriers associated with an OTC-based bowel regimen included bowel regimen complexity, cost, poor palatability of PEG, and cognitive impairment. In general, it has been estimated that 40% to 70% of patients with schizophrenia are adherent or partially adherent to antipsychotics at any given time.10-12 Nearly a quarter of patients stop antipsychotic treatment within 1 month. Medical comorbidities and the resulting complexity of medication regimens may contribute to nonadherence.12 In patients with schizophrenia, adherence to non-psychiatric medications may follow similar patterns compared with antipsychotic medication.13-15 Although adherence to psychiatric medication may be a focus during a psychiatric appointment, adherence to all medications should be routinely assessed. This is especially important during transitions of care and, as illustrated in case 3, with the patient resumed a lower dose of sennosides/docusate and then subsequently only partially adherent to PEG. Cases 3 and 4 also highlight that acute measures successfully implemented for CIGH during an inpatient hospitalization (where medications are administered to patients) may not always translate to success in the outpatient setting. In cases 1 and 4, attempts to address bowel regimen complexity and adherence concerns were driving factors in switching to a secretagogue laxative. Financial factors may also influence adherence. As seen in case 2, a prescription for linaclotide was more affordable than escalating dosing of PEG or sennosides/docusate. Patient out-of-pocket medication costs need to be considered, but there is an interesting paradox regarding overall healthcare expenditures. When OTC medications are not covered by insurance, higher out-of-pocket costs can result in a patient being prescribed an alternative that is more affordable through a copay, but ultimately more expensive to the healthcare system.

The included cases represent patients of varying complexity, with and without other medical comorbidities contributing to gastrointestinal hypomotility. Although the patients in cases 1 and 2 had no relevant gastrointestinal medical history, case 3 involved a patient with Parkinson’s disease, in which constipation occurs in at least 50% of patients.16 Patient 4 had documented diabetes with secondary autonomic failure and longstanding issues with constipation. In addition to medical history, which may impact gastrointestinal motility, evaluation of concomitant medications is also important, including anticholinergic medications and opioids. Other medications, such as amlodipine, certain antidepressants, antacids, iron supplementation, and glucagon-like-1 receptor agonists, are also associated with constipation, which patient 4 was prescribed. Where possible, contributing medications should be changed or discontinued. Though not relevant to these cases, monitoring for developing or worsening CIGH in patients who quit smoking is warranted, as clozapine serum levels would be expected to rise. From an analysis of 102 cases of severe CIGH, higher clozapine serum levels (eg, via high total daily clozapine dosing, drug-interaction, or infection) and concomitant medications were described as risk factors.5 However, in a larger study of 43,132 individuals prescribed clozapine involving 160 cases of severe CIGH, neither age, female sex, clozapine dose (which could not be correlated to clozapine serum levels), nor use of other constipation medications were related to the risk for life-threatening CIGH.4 Therefore, the authors suggested that predicting who will develop CIGH is difficult. This makes vigilant monitoring and management of CIGH crucial for all patients prescribed clozapine and lends to even prophylactically implementing a bowel regimen. Bowel prophylaxis for CIGH has been recommended in the literature, and 1 published, clozapine-specific protocol has implemented sennosides/docusate at clozapine initiation.17 This may avoid progression to severe CIGH, given 1 study has reported that only 9% of patients with severe CIGH were receiving laxatives.4

Constipation secondary to clozapine and progression to severe CIGH may be underrecognized, as product labeling only more recently, in 2020, strengthened the related warning.18 Patients should receive ongoing education on CIGH and constipation. Baseline bowel status should be established and risk factors should be addressed. Although likely to be inadequate alone, counseling points on non-pharmacologic strategies should also be reviewed, such as appropriate hydration, increased physical activity, and increased fiber intake from dietary sources.18 Patients should be educated to avoid bulk-forming laxatives. Patients should also report ongoing diarrhea with treatment of CIGH, as this may reduce absorption of some medications and is specifically noted within package inserts of oral contraceptives. Additionally, diarrhea may represent overflow diarrhea secondary to fecal impaction in the setting of CIGH. Clinicians should standardize approaches to monitoring bowel pattern along with any “red flag” symptoms (eg, severe abdominal pain, bloody diarrhea, abdominal distension, or vomiting). This is especially true, given the difficulty in predicting who may develop severe CIGH. Requests for a stool diary may be helpful in addition to asking questions such as whether there is straining or feelings of incomplete evacuation. The Bristol Stool Scale is an easy tool that can be implemented to record stool consistency and may better predict intestinal transit time over reported bowel movement frequency.19

Although CIGH may be related to the serum concentration of clozapine, lowering the clozapine dose is not always feasible from a psychiatric perspective. Therefore, management may rely on pharmacologic strategies, despite a lack of CIGH-specific guidelines, and it is often a matter of trial and error. Although OTC laxatives are considered first-line for CIGH, 1 study reported that these agents are often stopped due to suboptimal effect and limited or no satisfaction when used for CIC.20 Although it is recommended to trial OTC agents initially, guidelines for CIC also note these issues and that often a prescription-only medication is needed to be trialed before improvement.7 Guidelines for CIC also suggest secretagogues as potential options for patients who fail to respond to OTC, with a greater strength recommendation for linaclotide or plecanatide (strong recommendation) over lubiprostone (conditional recommendation).7 To date, there are limited reports on using lubiprostone or linaclotide for patients prescribed clozapine, and more research is needed. Precedent for use of secretagogues for CIGH can be derived from 1 observational study, 1 case series, and 2 case reports (Table 1).21-24 In the individual cases, all but 1 described the use of a secretagogue plus 1 or more other laxative agents for CIGH management. Thus, information on monotherapy secretagogues for CIGH is even more limited.21,23,24 In the observational study, only 1 of 9 patients received lubiprostone monotherapy.22 Thus, it is not clear if monotherapy secretagogue CIGH management is sufficient, especially when ileus or obstruction has previously occurred.

Prior publications describing use of a secretagogue for clozapine-associated gastrointestinal hypomotility

Regardless of the specific agent or combination used for CIGH, clinicians should be aware of safety parameters with laxative medications. Long-term, high-dose senna use has rarely been associated with liver injury, which is usually mild to moderate and resolves with discontinuation.25 Should severe diarrhea occur, which is more common with secretagogue laxatives, there is the potential for electrolyte abnormalities, dizziness, and syncope.26 These are relevant adverse drug reactions that might compound the hypotensive or QT interval prolonging risks associated with clozapine.

A unique aspect of this case series is how secretagogues may optimize care by addressing barriers inherent to OTC medications. The FDA-approved secretagogues include lubiprostone, linaclotide, and plecanatide, with drug information and clinical considerations summarized in Table 2. However, given their cost, insurance coverage issues are possible; there may be a need for prior authorization or prescribing of an alternative secretagogue that is covered by a patient’s pharmacy benefit insurance plan. In some of the cases presented, the prior authorizations required documentation that multiple OTC agents failed. Although secretagogue agents were ultimately covered by the 4 patients’ pharmacy benefits plan, this may not be broadly representative of the general population.

Pharmacology of secretagogue agents

Beyond secretagogue laxatives, a few other prescription-only medication options exist for constipation, but with limited data for specifically CIGH. Based on the described mechanisms of CIGH, it is not surprising that there have been reports of using serotonergic or cholinergic medications. Although the mechanism of CIGH is complex, it involves clozapine’s antagonisms of muscarinic-M1 and -M3 receptors, causing inhibition of smooth muscle contraction and delayed intestinal transit.3 Clozapine also inhibits multiple serotonergic receptors that are believed to result in decreased gastrointestinal motility.3 Prucalopride, a selective serotonin 5-HT4 receptor agonist FDA approved for CIC, functions as a prokinetic agent and has demonstrated greater effectiveness and patient satisfaction than lactulose for CIGH management.27 Other cases of successful prucalopride use in CIGH have been reported.28 The use of other mechanistically different prokinetic agents (ie, metoclopramide and erythromycin) has also been described in 2 separate case reports.29,30 There is very limited information on the use of cholinergic agents for CIGH, and systemic cholinergic adverse effects may limit use in mild to moderate cases. Notably, secretagogues have limited systemic absorption and primarily cause gastrointestinal adverse effects. Carbachol was shown to reverse clozapine-induced motility issues in an animal model study.31 However, carbachol is only commercially available in the United States as an intraocular solution, used in the setting of ophthalmic surgeries. In 1 of the reviewed cases previously published involving the use of a secretagogue laxative for CIGH, a patient was also started on the cholinergic agent, bethanechol.24 The use of off-label neostigmine, an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor, has been reported after the development of clozapine-associated acute colonic pseudo-obstruction.29

Conclusion

Polyethylene glycol or sennosides with or without docusate are commonly used as first-line agents for CIGH. However, adherence barriers to an OTC-based bowel regimen should be recognized and addressed. The use of a secretagogue laxative may help overcome barriers, such as out-of-pocket OTC costs, poor palatability of PEG, and allow for simplification of a complex bowel regimen. However, hospital formulary restrictions and outpatient cost considerations require individualized care when developing a treatment plan involving a secretagogue laxative. Additionally, further research is needed to better determine the safety and effectiveness of this class of drugs, including their role as monotherapy in CIGH and optimal timing of initiation.
