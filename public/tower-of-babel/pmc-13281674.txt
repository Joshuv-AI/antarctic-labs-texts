In Situ Hybridization Chain Reaction and Immunohistochemical Labeling of the Octopamine Production Pathway in the Central Nervous System of Lymnaea stagnalis
Authors: Victoria Tweedie‐Pitre, Yulia Reunova, Russell C. Wyeth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOctopamine (OA), a biogenic amine functionally similar to vertebrate norepinephrine, plays an important role in invertebrate neurophysiology. Previous reports of putative octopaminergic cells in the pond snail, Lymnaea stagnalis, have revealed inconsistencies, prompting our investigation that combined in situ hybridization chain reaction with traditional immunohistochemical methods. We mapped tyramine β‐hydroxylase (TBH) mRNA, the corresponding TBH protein, and the OA neurotransmitter. Approximately 40 neurons were labeled by all three methods and are presumably genuinely octopaminergic, including several neurons with previous electrophysiological evidence that OA is their neurotransmitter. Our results also revealed approximately another 40 cells that only showed evidence of mRNA and enzyme labeling, but no neurotransmitter, and another five to eight cells that only labeled for OA. Some of these results are likely explained by antibody cross‐reactivity, but multiple TBH isoforms (some of which may not produce OA) or regulatory mechanisms that block TBH function also need to be considered. Overall, this study underlines the need for a more nuanced interpretation of neuroanatomical mapping and provides new insights into the organization of molluscan octopaminergic systems.

Introduction

Several tyrosine‐derived monoamines act as signaling molecules across the metazoa. These amines are produced by two complementary pathways, one synthesizing the catecholamines (dopamine, epinephrine, and norepinephrine) and the other synthesizing the phenolamines, tyramine, and octopamine (OA). OA is synthesized via a two‐step pathway: tyrosine decarboxylase converts tyrosine to tyramine, and tyramine β‐hydroxylase (TBH) subsequently converts tyramine to OA (Livingstone and Tempel 1983). In invertebrates, OA acts as a neurotransmitter and neuromodulator and is functionally analogous to norepinephrine in vertebrates (David and Coulon 1985; Roeder 1999; Verlinden et al. 2010), including involvement in aggression, locomotion, feeding, and learning (Gallo et al. 2016; Roeder et al. 2003). Although the molecule was first discovered in Octopus vulgaris (Erspamer and Boretti 1951), subsequent investigations into the distribution and role of OA have primarily focused on arthropods, with molluscan systems receiving less attention. Our goal was to build on previous reports to develop a comprehensive map of the octopaminergic (OAergic) system in the central nervous system (CNS) of the gastropod mollusc, Lymnaea stagnalis.

The pond snail, L. stagnalis, is a freshwater pulmonate and a model organism for investigating neural circuits and how they control behavior. Several previous studies have found evidence for OA in the CNS of L. stagnalis. High‐pressure liquid chromatography (HPLC) revealed OA in the buccal, cerebral, and pedal ganglia, but failed to detect any in the visceral loop ganglia (the two pleural, two parietal, and one visceral ganglia; Hiripi et al. 1998). Moreover, electrophysiological studies demonstrated that two OAergic cells in the buccal ganglia are involved in the intrinsic modulation of feeding rhythm and also implicated OA in the modulation of pedal motor neurons (Ormshaw and Elliott 2006; Vehovszky et al. 1998, Vehovszky et al. 2005; Vehovszky and Elliott 2001). The broader neuroanatomical distribution of putative OAergic neurons has also been investigated in L. stagnalis; however, unexplained differences are apparent when comparing reports (Elekes et al. 1993, 1996; Beach et al. 2026).

Elekes et al. (1993) first described a high abundance of OA‐immunoreactive (OA‐IR) neurons in the CNS, but a later study by the same authors (Elekes et al. 1996) only reported a subset of the former, without providing any rationale for the discrepancies. More recently, a study by Beach et al. (2026) largely corroborated the observations made by Elekes et al. (1996) but also left several questions unresolved. For instance, all previous investigations in L. stagnalis found OA‐IR cells in the visceral loop, in contradiction to the HPLC evidence. Furthermore, in addition to a few discrepancies between reported OA‐IR cells, Beach et al. (2026) described further differences between OA‐IR and a second marker of putatively OAergic cells. The study validated the use of an antibody raised against a vertebrate dopamine β‐hydroxylase (DBH; responsible for the conversion of dopamine to norepinephrine) as a marker for gastropod TBH expression. There appears to be only a single gene copy and corresponding transcript in L. stagnalis, and sequence similarity, protein function, and western blots suggest that TBH and DBH are homologous proteins (Gray et al. 2006; Monastirioti et al. 1996; Beach et al. 2026). However, in L. stagnalis, immunohistochemical double labels of DBH and OA revealed several DBH‐IR cells that lacked detectable OA‐IR, and vice versa. Thus, while there is clear evidence that validates the use of anti‐DBH to target the TBH enzyme, further investigation with a TBH‐specific label is warranted.

Given the various uncertainties and discrepancies within and among past studies, our goal was to re‐examine the distribution of OAergic cells in the CNS of L. stagnalis. Consequently, we chose to compare the neuroanatomical distribution of three molecules representing three stages in the production of the neurotransmitter: TBH mRNA, TBH enzyme, and OA itself. We use the specificity afforded by in situ hybridization chain reaction (HCR) to determine the distribution of TBH mRNA and to provide corroborating evidence for the presence of the enzyme that synthesizes OA, and that cells labeled with all three markers are genuinely OAergic.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Lymnaea stagnalis were raised in a colony descended from the Dutch laboratory strain and maintained at St. Francis Xavier University. The snails were housed in freshwater aquaria kept at a temperature of 21°C–23°C and a pH of 8. Water was changed three times a week and supplemented with chalk as an additional calcium source. Photoperiod was controlled to match the natural daylight pattern outside the facility. Snails were fed romaine lettuce and consumer‐grade fish food (Nutrafin Max Spirulina; Tetra Goldfish Variety Pellets). Animals selected for this study had shell lengths of 10–20 mm. All animal use complied with guidelines from the Canadian Council for Animal Care.

Dissection and Fixation Protocol

Animals were anaesthetized by immersion in Lymnaea saline (Table 1) with 0.125% 1‐phenoxy‐2‐propanol (Sigma, 484423) for 30–120 min, depending on the size of the snail (Wyeth et al. 2009). After unresponsiveness was confirmed, the shell and viscera were removed with an oblique cut. The isolated foot and head were pinned in a petri dish lined with Sylgard‐184 (Dow‐Corning) and filled with Lymnaea saline anesthetic solution. A dorsal incision was made along the sagittal axis of the mantle and head, the cerebral commissure was cut, and the esophagus was pulled anteriorly to expose the CNS. Any remaining connective tissue was carefully severed, and the CNS was isolated, keeping the buccal ganglia attached. Tissues were fixed with freshly prepared 4% paraformaldehyde diluted in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS; Table 1; VWR, 76371–734) for 3–4 h at 4°C (Wyeth and Croll 2011). To minimize autofluorescence with the HCR method, the fixative was prepared fresh and cooled to 4°C prior to use, and both calcium chloride and magnesium chloride were avoided in the PBS (Choi et al. 2018).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) Double Labeling

All steps were performed at room temperature unless otherwise stated. After fixation, tissues were washed in saline sodium citrate (SSC; Sigma–Aldrich, S6639‐1L) solution containing 0.25% Triton X‐100 (Fisher BioReagents, BP151‐100; Table 1; five changes over 1 h in SSC‐Triton). The tissues were blocked for 1–2 h in 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Amresco, 0332‐25G) in SSC‐Triton (BSA‐SSC‐Triton) and subsequently incubated in a cocktail of both primary antisera diluted in BSA‐SSC‐Triton (Table 2) at 4°C for >48 h. Antibody concentrations (Table 2) were chosen based on Beach et al. (2026), corroborated by additional testing of various concentrations. Tissues were then washed in BSA‐SSC‐Triton (10 changes over 3 h), blocked with 1% normal donkey serum (Sigma, 566460) in SSC‐Triton (NDS‐SSC‐Triton) for 1–2 h, and finally incubated in a cocktail of secondary antibodies diluted in NDS‐SSC‐Triton (Table 3; Beach et al. 2026) at 4°C overnight (>12 h). Following incubation, tissues were washed in SSC‐Triton (eight changes over 2 h) and mounted in Vectashield (Vector Laboratories, H‐1000) on glass slides.

IHC‐HCR Double Labeling

The two methods were combined in series. The initial steps of the IHC‐HCR double labeling followed the protocol for IHC up to and including the final washing step. Subsequently, the HCR protocol largely followed the detection and amplification stages of HCR RNA‐FISH v3.0 (Choi et al. 2018), with steps drawn from the whole‐mount zebrafish embryo and larvae protocol (Choi et al. 2016) provided by Molecular Instruments (Los Angeles, CA, USA). Modifications of incubation times and amplification temperature were made either to simplify the protocol or to optimize signal versus background. Proprietary buffer solutions, DNA split initiator probes, and fluorescent hairpin amplifiers were all supplied by Molecular Instruments (Table 4). All steps were conducted in the dark to minimize fluorophore fading. Following the IHC steps, tissues first received an additional wash with 0.1% Tween‐20 (VWR, 97062–332) in SSC (SSC‐Tween; three changes over 30 min). Tissues were then incubated for 2–3 h in Hybridization Buffer at 37°C and then incubated with probes diluted with Hybridization Buffer (dilution details in Table 4) overnight (>12 h) at 37°C. After rinsing in preheated Probe Wash Buffer (four changes over 1 h) at 37°C and then SSC‐Tween (three changes over 30 min) at room temperature, the tissues were incubated in Amplification Buffer for 2–3 h. The amplifiers were prepared by heating stock hairpin h1 and hairpin h2 solutions separately for 90 s at 95°C and then cooling in the dark for 30 min on ice. Both hairpin solutions were mixed and diluted in Amplification Buffer and used to incubate the samples for at least 12 h at 26°C in the dark (Table 4). Following amplification, the tissues were washed with SSC‐Tween (10 times over 3 h) and mounted in Vectashield. Best results with the HCR labeling were found with both probe and amplification incubations lasting 24–48 h. A probe set synthesized against green fluorescent protein was used as a negative control; no labeling was observed (Table 4).

Antibody Characterization

Specificity of the OA antibody used in this study has not been directly tested in L. stagnalis. However, the antibody was used previously in the CNS of L. stagnalis, and the labeling patterns were corroborated by labeling with a second OA antibody (Beach et al. 2026). Specificity of the DBH antibody (and also a second DBH antibody) was assessed in L. stagnalis by western blot, which revealed immunoreactive bands at molecular weights consistent with the predicted membrane‐bound and cytosolic vertebrate DBH isoforms (Beach et al. 2026). Furthermore, a sequence alignment between human DBH and L. stagnalis TBH demonstrated a high degree of conservation, including a perfect match with the 400–450 peptide region used to generate the anti‐DBH (Beach et al. 2026).

Observations

Labeling was observed with laser scanning confocal microscopy in at least 10 preparations for each label combination. Confocal image stacks and projections were obtained using an Olympus Fluoview 3000 microscope and imaging software. cellSens FV desktop (Olympus) and FIJI software (Schindelin et al. 2012) were used for full image adjustments of brightness, contrast, and gamma. A consensus map was generated using a schematic outline and freehand drawing of somata and neural fibers in Illustrator (Adobe Systems). The map included (a) positively labeled cells that were consistently mapped across preparations, (b) positively labeled cells that varied in cell counts or positioning, (c) co‐expression patterns between the different labeling methods, (d) patterns of labeled neural fibers, and (e) regions that never showed positive labeling.

We have chosen terminology for describing our findings with the goal of clarity and simplicity in comparing methods. Conventionally, positive labeling with IHC is described as “immunoreactive‐like,” since it does not provide direct evidence of target protein expression (accounting for potential nonspecific binding or background signal). In contrast, positive labeling with in situ hybridization (including HCR) is typically considered as evidence of mRNA expression, despite the theoretical possibility of both nonspecific binding and background signal as well. To avoid cumbersome language while adhering to both conventions, we describe both HCR and IHC as providing evidence toward target expression, whether at the mRNA or protein level. This terminology in the study is not meant to imply absolute specificity of labeling in either method, and all interpretations in the discussion are considered within the limitations of each approach.

Results

Overview

Combinations of TBH‐HCR, DBH‐IHC, and OA‐IHC labeling revealed substantial co‐expression throughout the paired buccal, cerebral, and pedal ganglia of the CNS (Table 5). Effectively identical expression patterns were observed with TBH‐HCR and DBH‐IHC labeling (Figure 1); however, OA‐IHC only revealed a subpopulation of the other two labels (Figure 2). While there was clear overlap between the three targets, relative signal intensities of each label varied in co‐expressing somata. In addition, DBH‐IHC and OA‐IHC revealed both overlapping and distinct patterns of labeled neural fibers in the CNS (TBH‐HCR did not label fibers).

Buccal Ganglia

In the paired buccal ganglia, three prominent medial somata were consistently revealed by each of the labeling methods (TBH‐HCR, DBH‐IHC, OA‐IHC; Figure 3; Table 5). Bilaterally symmetric somata were located in each ganglion at the root of the buccal commissure, in addition to a lone soma with variable position. In most preparations, the lone soma was in the left ganglion (Figure 3G–I), while in some preparations, it was located in the right ganglion (Figure 3D–F). A smaller subset revealed the lone soma in the buccal commissure (Figure 2). In one preparation, all three somata were observed in the right ganglion (Figure 3A–C). All three somata had similarly high relative signal intensity with all labeling methods. No other somata were labeled in the buccal ganglia.

While the three somata showed co‐expression with all labels, only DBH‐IHC and OA‐IHC revealed neural fiber detail (Figure 3). The buccal cells were multipolar, with processes extending both contralaterally across the buccal commissure and ipsilaterally through the neuropil, into the cerebral–buccal connectives. The thickest fibers were also occasionally observed with TBH‐HCR, but with a relatively weak signal in comparison to other TBH‐HCR labeling. Additional DBH‐IHC‐labeled fibers were observed in the neuropil (Figure 3H, unfilled arrowhead), occasionally projecting anteriorly into the dorsobuccal nerve (data not shown).

Cerebral Ganglia

The paired cerebral ganglia revealed bilaterally symmetric co‐expression of TBH‐HCR and DBH‐IHC, with each ganglion containing 26–36 somata (Figures 1 and 4; Table 5). The anterior and central regions had higher variability in somata localization. Anteriorly, two to three somata were frequently observed: a medium soma (medium = 15–20 µm) with relatively strong TBH‐HCR signal but relatively weak DBH‐IHC signal (Figure 4A″–C″, ventral), and one or two small somata (small = 10 µm) with stronger DBH‐IHC signal and weaker TBH‐HCR (Figure 4A″–C″, dorsal filled arrows). At the anteromedial margin, a large soma (large = 30–35 µm) revealed a consistently strong DBH‐IHC label; however, its TBH‐HCR label was relatively weak and variable, and occasionally undetectable (Figure 4A’–C’, filled arrow). Centrally, a lone medium soma was consistently labeled by both methods, though always relatively dimly with DBH‐IHC (Figure 4A’–C’, filled arrowhead). The most variation in cell count, localization, and relative intensity was observed in a ventral medial cluster, a few adjacent medium somata, and a solitary small dorsal soma (Figure 4A’–C’, bracket b, unfilled arrowheads). In the posterolateral region, a cluster consisted of 12–15 small somata (Figure 4A–C, bracket a) and an anterior medium soma (Figure 4A–C, unfilled arrowhead). In some preparations, a second medium soma could be observed within the cluster with a relatively strong TBH‐HCR signal but weak or undetectable DBH‐IHC. At the base of the lateral lobe, a ventral soma with prominent fibrous projections resembling a bundle of hair had a consistently strong signal with both labels (Figure 4A’–C’, unfilled arrow; described as the “hairy cell” by Beach et al. [2026]). Approximately half of the preparations revealed an additional adjacent medium soma with weakly detectable TBH‐HCR and less frequently detectable DBH‐IHC (Figure 4A’–C’, unfilled arrow with asterisk). Finally, there was a single medium soma toward the posterior margin, with both relatively strong TBH‐HCR and DBH‐IHC signal (Figure 4A–C, filled arrowhead).

OA‐IHC double labels with both TBH‐HCR and DBH‐IHC revealed partially overlapping co‐expression in the cerebral ganglion (Figure 5; Table 5). Between 14 and 17 cells per ganglion consistently co‐expressed all three labels: the posterolateral cluster of 12–15 somata, the “hairy cell,” and the lone dorsal soma in the medial region (Figure 5, filled arrows and bracket a). Conversely, 14–19 cells that co‐expressed TBH‐HCR and DBH‐IHC in each ganglion were not labeled with OA‐IHC (Figure 5, unfilled arrowheads and bracket b). These included several anterior and central solitary somata of varying sizes, the lone soma in the posterior region, and the ventral medial cluster of small somata. Lastly, in approximately half of the preparations, a cluster of five to eight small somata was labeled with OA‐IHC in the anterior lobe (Figure 5D’/F’). However, when detected, these somata were consistently faint and never observed to co‐express with TBH‐HCR or DBH‐IHC.

The extent to which neural fibers were labeled in the cerebral ganglia also varied across labeling methods. No fibers were labeled with TBH‐HCR, other than occasionally the relatively thick proximal roots of fibers projecting from brightly labeled cells. Otherwise, a more extensive network of fibers was observed with DBH‐IHC compared to OA‐IHC (Figure 6). DBH‐IHC and OA‐IHC labeling revealed clear overlap in the fibers extending from the posterolateral cluster of small somata and the “hairy cell,” as well as a central neuropil (Figure 6A–C, bracket a). DBH‐IHC labeling of the neuropil showed less clearly defined fibers, combining both diffuse and punctate signals, compared to OA‐IHC, which more clearly delineated individual fibers. Interestingly, OA‐IHC also labeled a second small anterior neuropil that was not observed with DBH‐IHC (Figure 6A–C, bracket b). Conversely, DBH‐IHC fibers with no corresponding OA‐IHC labeling projected into all connectives and the superior labial nerve (Figure 6A–C, unfilled arrowheads). Similarly, DBH‐IHC revealed numerous strong fibers extending into the cerebral commissure, while OA‐IHC only showed weak labeling of a single corresponding fiber (Figure 6A–C, filled arrowhead).

Several variations in the relative signal intensities of all labels were notable. In particular, somata with a relatively brighter DBH‐IHC label were not necessarily the somata with a relatively brighter TBH‐HCR label, and vice versa. Intensities were generally brightest for both labels in the posterior region, including the posterolateral cluster (Figure 4A–C, bracket a), the soma toward the posterior margin (Figure 4A–C, filled arrowhead), and the “hairy cell.” In contrast, the central and anterior regions of the cerebral ganglia revealed greater variability in relative signal intensity, both within and across preparations. For example, in comparison to the intensities in the posterior region, the medial cluster sometimes revealed relatively strong and extensive TBH‐HCR, while showing a relatively weak DBH‐IHC label; in other cases, the opposite was observed. Moreover, in a small subset of preparations, some of the medial somata were only detectable with one label (TBH‐HCR or DBH‐IHC) despite the cells in the posterior region still showing consistently bright intensities in both labels (Figure 4A’–C’, unfilled arrowhead with asterisk). Interestingly, the consistently bright posteromedial cluster and the “hairy cell” generally also had relatively strong OA‐IHC signal. Meanwhile, the lone dorsal soma that had varying relative intensities with TBH‐HCR (Figure 5B’, filled arrow) and DBH‐IHC revealed OA‐IHC intensity that either mirrored or was weaker than the intensity of the relative DBH‐IHC signal in that cell (Figure 5D–F, medial filled arrow).

Pedal Ganglia

In the pedal ganglia, TBH‐HCR and DBH‐IHC were co‐expressed in four somata, with OA‐IHC only observed in two somata (Table 5). TBH‐HCR and DBH‐IHC labeled two pairs of medium somata along the lateral margin of each ganglion (Figure 7); the anterior‐most pair (lateral to the root of the anterior pedal nerves) also co‐expressed OA‐IHC (Figure 8, filled arrowheads). The anterior pair had a relatively strong signal across labels (Figures 7 and 8, filled arrowheads). Conversely, the posterior‐most pair at the base of the cerebral–pedal connective typically had a moderate TBH‐HCR relative intensity, while DBH‐IHC was often relatively weak or undetectable (Figures 7A’–C’/A″–C″ and 8, unfilled arrowheads). The posterior pair also revealed higher variability in localization, with one of the paired somata often being observed more medially than the other (Figure 7A″–C″, unfilled arrowheads).

Visceral Loop

The only marker that revealed labeling in the visceral loop was DBH‐IHC; however, labeling was variable and inconsistent. In a few preparations, somata were observed in both parietal ganglia, but the exact patterns of labeling always varied between preparations, and co‐expression with another label was never observed. In contrast, DBH‐IHC fibers were consistently labeled in the visceral loop. Dense fibers were observed projecting throughout all five ganglia, and some also projected posteriorly into the left parietal nerve.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first report for gastropods (and, indeed, molluscs) that presents the distribution of all three stages in the synthesis of a neurotransmitter: mRNA, the corresponding enzyme, and signaling molecule. Our findings corroborate and expand on the reported expression patterns of different components of the OAergic system in L. stagnalis. Double labels combining pairs of all three markers revealed a population of 63–83 neurons, 35–41 of which co‐expressed TBH mRNA, TBH protein, and the OA neurotransmitter itself. This co‐localization provides strong evidence for genuine OAergic neurons (Figure 9; red somata). We also refined our understanding of two categories of discrepancies among the labels, including approximately half of labeled cells co‐expressing TBH‐HCR and DBH‐IHC but lacking detectable OA‐IHC (Figure 9; black somata) and five to eight cells that were only labeled with OA‐IHC (Figure 8; blue somata). These subpopulations suggest underlying complexities in the OAergic system and emphasize the importance of corroborating neuroanatomical evidence when documenting neurotransmitter profiles.

To document markers of OAergic neurons, we combined IHC and HCR v3.0 (Choi et al. 2018), two complementary techniques with distinct molecular targets and methodological advantages. IHC has been the conventional approach for neuroanatomical labeling, relying on antigen–antibody binding to detect target proteins (e.g., Croll and Chiasson 1990; Gunaratne et al. 2014; Horváth et al. 2020). While widely established, specificity is dictated by protein conformation, epitope accessibility, and intermolecular interactions, making the method susceptible to nonspecific binding (Ramos‐Vara 2005; Saper 2005). This limitation may be amplified with invertebrates, as many commercially available antibodies are generated against vertebrate epitopes, as is the case for anti‐DBH used here. Indeed, variable specificity is an obvious possibility to explain the discrepancies among past studies of OA‐IR in L. stagnalis (Beach et al. 2026; Elekes et al. 1993, 1996). Thus, we incorporated in situ hybridization to visualize expression patterns of the corresponding mRNA, which can then corroborate (or not) the IHC labeling (Van Minnen et al. 1988; De Lange and Van Minnen 1998). We chose HCR v3.0 because of its high specificity and low background labeling. The method employs a cocktail of ∼20 different short probes with binding sites designed to hybridize adjacently along the target mRNA (Choi et al. 2018; Molecular Instruments, personal communication). Each DNA probe also carries half of an initiator sequence. Only when a pair of adjacent probes hybridize is the full initiator sequence formed, triggering polymerization of fluorophore‐conjugated DNA hairpins (Choi et al. 2018). Because this process requires the binding of adjacent DNA probes, any isolated nonspecific hybridization produces no signal, minimizing background label. Similarly, since detection is dependent on sequence‐specific base pairing (of multiple pairs of adjacent probes) rather than protein conformation, the HCR method also offers robust molecular specificity. The two methods complement each other because co‐localization provides corroboration of the IHC specificity, and because the distributions of neurotransmitters, neural‐specific proteins, and mRNA result in HCR usually only labeling somata, while IHC typically labels somata and neural fibers.

Co‐Expression of All Three Labels Suggests Genuine OAergic Neurons

Co‐localization of three markers across multiple stages in the OA synthetic pathway provides strong evidence of genuine OAergic neurons. TBH‐HCR confirms TBH gene expression, DBH‐IHC demonstrates TBH protein expression, and OA‐IHC reveals the corresponding neurotransmitter product of TBH activity. Any one of these labels in isolation, particularly the IHC labels, must be interpreted with caution, given the inherent possibility of cross‐reactivity (Saper 2005). However, the combination of higher‐specificity HCR with two independent IHC labels greatly reduces this uncertainty (Choi et al. 2018; Ćorić et al. 2023). Moreover, the nearly identical expression patterns for TBH‐HCR and DBH‐IHC provide compelling evidence for the presence of genuine TBH enzyme. This is consistent with reported sequence homology between vertebrate DBH and invertebrate TBH (Monastirioti et al. 1996; Beach et al. 2026), as well as western blot results in L. stagnalis that suggested anti‐DBH is in fact labeling TBH (Beach et al. 2026). The additional detection of OA‐IHC in these same cells, a direct product of TBH enzyme activity, further corroborates the presence of the neurotransmitter. Thus, while each marker alone offers indirect evidence, co‐expression across all three labels associated with the synthesis of the neurotransmitter provides the strongest neuroanatomical confirmation to date of authentic OAergic neurons.

We found a clearly defined set of genuine OAergic cells in the CNS of L. stagnalis that revealed co‐expression of mRNA, enzyme, and neurotransmitter markers. Our results were almost entirely consistent with previous patterns of OA‐IR (Elekes et al. 1996) and double labels of OA‐IR and DBH‐IR (Beach et al. 2026). In the buccal ganglia, three medial cells express OA, with substantial variation in their exact locations. Across all three studies, one, two, or all three neurons could be found in the left or right buccal ganglion, and occasionally in the buccal commissure. Two of these cells have also been confirmed as OAergic based on electrophysiological evidence (Vehovszky et al. 1998, 2000, Vehovszky et al. 2005; Vehovszky and Elliott 2001). Moreover, OAergic neurons were observed in all three neuroanatomical studies in the cerebral ganglia, including the cluster in the posteromedial region, the lone dorsal cell in the medial region, and the “hairy” cell at the margin of the lateral lobe (Elekes et al. 1996; Beach et al. 2026). Finally, a pair of anterolateral somata were found in each pedal ganglion. In all cases, the authenticity of this OAergic population is further corroborated by detectable concentrations of OA in all three of the ganglion pairs containing these cells (Hiripi et al. 1998).

DBH‐IHC and OA‐IHC revealed both overlapping and distinct immunoreactive fibers that cannot be fully reconciled with our data. DBH‐IHC consistently labeled dense networks of fibers in the central neuropils of both cerebral and pedal ganglia, as well as fibers in all major connectives, commissures, and a few peripheral nerves (Figure 8). In contrast, OA‐IHC revealed a more sparse network limited to projections toward the central neuropils arising from both the cluster and medium sized OAergic somata, a small anterior cerebral neuropil, and a small number of fibers in the buccal and cerebral commissures, with no immunoreactive fibers in the visceral loop. Interestingly, the relative intensity of DBH‐IHC proximal projections from labeled somata was generally substantially lower than that of OA‐IHC, despite other DBH‐IHC fibers in the neuropils having a relatively high intensity signal. The DBH‐IHC observations are consistent with TBH protein accumulating in vesicles stored preferentially in presynaptic terminals, which has also been observed in arthropods (Homberg et al. 2013; Koon et al. 2011). However, if we assume that relative intensities correspond to the concentrations of the target molecules, the combination of overlapping and nonoverlapping sets of fibers with both markers implies two further surprising interpretations. If all (or some) of the DBH‐IHC and OA‐IHC labeled fibers are in fact portions of the same neurons, then the implication is that the OA is accumulating in the proximal portions of the fibers, while the TBH enzyme is localized to the more distal portions of the fibers, contrary to expectations regarding where OA should be stored in presynaptic terminals. Alternatively, all (or some) of the DBH‐IHC‐labeled fibers in the neuropil are projecting from an additional population of somata that do not label with TBH‐HCR, DBH‐IHC, or OA‐IHC, suggesting potential cross‐reactivity with another protein. Three‐dimensional reconstructions, which were beyond the scope of this study, are needed to resolve the localization of both the enzyme and neurotransmitter while tracing individual fiber connections to OAergic somata.

Cells Consistently Co‐Expressing TBH‐HCR and DBH‐IHC but Lacking Detectable OA‐IHC

Between 26 and 36 TBH‐HCR and DBH‐IHC positive cells with no observable OA‐IHC label were located in each cerebral ganglion, as well as a pair in each pedal ganglion. Given that invertebrate TBH and vertebrate DBH are homologous enzymes and thus could produce either OA or norepinephrine depending on available substrates, we have to consider the possibility that these cells are noradrenergic. If the labeled cells contained tyrosine hydroxylase (the enzyme responsible for the synthesis of dopamine) in addition to TBH, then we would expect the production of norepinephrine. However, no co‐localization whatsoever was observed between DBH‐IR and tyrosine hydroxylase‐IR in the CNS of L. stagnalis (Beach et al. 2026). Moreover, there has only been variable HPLC evidence of norepinephrine in molluscs, and no labeling has been described in the CNS (Coon and Bonar 1986; Pani and Croll 1998; Saavedra et al. 1974; Sloley et al. 1990). Thus, we need to consider the possibility that the cells are indeed genuinely OAergic and that the amount of OA present in the cells was below the detectable threshold using IHC. These cells could simply have constitutively low OA concentrations or, theoretically, this might also occur if recent activity prior to fixation depleted intracellular OA through release and degradation. OA can be degraded by enzymes such as N‐acetyltransferases (Monastirioti et al. 1996; Roeder 1999) and monoamine oxidase (the main degradation enzyme in the vertebrate adrenergic system; Roeder 1999; Sloley 2004). However, reuptake into transporter vesicles is considered the primary fate of released OA, at least in arthropods (Donly and Caveney 2005; Roeder 1999; Scavone et al. 1994). The absence of detectable OA in TBH‐DBH‐positive cells could also occur if the necessary components of the synthetic pathway were not all present in the cells despite the presence of TBH (and thus the cells are not OAergic). Perhaps there was no tyramine precursor produced in the cells, or there was a limited availability of ascorbate and copper co‐factors (Lehman et al. 2000; Wallace 1976). Alternatively, OA concentrations are known to fluctuate in arthropods (Châtel et al. 2013; Harris and Woodring 1992; Kononenko et al. 2009; Lehman et al. 2006), and the discrepancies in co‐expression we observed could be similarly explained by some form of enzymatic regulation via posttranscriptional or posttranslational modifications (Andreassi et al. 2018; Bueno‐Carrasco et al. 2022; Monastirioti et al. 1996; Vyas et al. 1990). Any of these mechanisms could lead to a detectable transcript or enzyme, but no neurotransmitter product.

Independently varying signal intensities among the three labels also support the possibility that transcriptional or translational mechanisms contribute to the variable colocalization we observed. While TBH mRNA and its corresponding enzyme were consistently co‐expressed, their relative signal intensities varied, with some cells showing a relatively stronger TBH‐HCR signal and a relatively weaker DBH‐IHC signal within a given preparation (or vice versa). Signal intensity of both IHC and HCR can correlate with target expression levels (Choi et al. 2020; Kirkeby and Thomsen 2005). Thus, cells with relatively stronger TBH‐HCR labeling but weaker DBH‐IHC labeling could indicate translational repression (e.g., via microRNA; Cheng et al. 2008; Yao et al. 2019), reduced ribosomal activity, or reduced enzyme stability, whereas the opposite pattern could indicate enhanced translation efficiency or reduced protein turnover (Bueno‐Carrasco et al. 2022; Dinges et al. 2017; Flames and Hobert 2011; Vyas et al. 1990). Again, any of these could account for the apparently fluctuating levels of TBH mRNA and protein indicated by the varying labeling intensities we observed and could also lead to downregulation of OA production in some cells.

One further explanation for the apparent absence of OA in cells with detectable TBH mRNA and enzyme could be TBH isoforms that do not synthesize OA. A preprint report has suggested that multiple TBH isoforms are found in fruit flies and that some isoforms are expressed in cells lacking OA and potentially producing norepinephrine (Ruppert et al. 2024). If antibodies and HCR probes targeted relatively conserved regions among isoforms, they would all be detected regardless of enzymatic functionality. Meanwhile, divergence among isoforms in the target regions could lead to reduced labeling efficiency and diminished signals, potentially contributing to labeling variation. If such isoforms occur in gastropods, these findings raise the possibility that the TBH‐HCR‐ and DBH‐IHC‐labeled cells without OA‐IHC label are genuinely not OAergic (and possibly noradrenergic). Thus, investigations into potential TBH isoforms and their functional diversity in L. stagnalis are clearly warranted.

Cells Labeled Only With OA‐IHC, Lacking Detectable TBH‐HCR or DBH‐IHC

A single faint cluster of cells near the anterior lobe of the cerebral ganglia was labeled with OA‐IHC only and may be, in fact, tyraminergic (Figure 8, blue somata). OA and tyramine are structurally related molecules, differing only by a hydroxyl group on the β‐substituent, and thus, cross‐reactivity with tyramine is a possibility that should be considered. Tyramine is a bona fide neurotransmitter known from several invertebrate taxa, and tyraminergic cells would not be expected to contain TBH (as the enzyme would convert tyramine to OA; Alkema et al. 2005; Homberg et al. 2013; Kononenko et al. 2009; Osborne 1985). In fact, Elekes et al. (1993) reported evidence of OA antibody cross‐reactivity with tyramine in their study of OA‐IR in the CNS of L. stagnalis. We used a different antibody, and our observation of cells labeled only with OA‐IHC does not clearly correspond with any of the cells observed by Elekes et al. (1993). In contrast, our observations match one group of cells observed to show only OA‐IR by Beach et al. (2026), using the same antibody as in our study. However, Beach et al. (2026) also observed additional OA‐IR only cells (that we did not), and those cells appear similar to others described in Elekes et al. (1993). Reconciling these matched and mismatched results requires testing the antibody we used for cross‐reactivity with tyramine and direct investigation of expression patterns for tyramine and the enzyme that produces it (tyrosine decarboxylase), alongside OA.

Discrepancies Among OAergic Studies in L. stagnalis

Previous investigations of OAergic neuroanatomy in L. stagnalis have documented both similar and divergent results to those reported here. The OAergic distribution we observed aligns with reported OA‐IR (Elekes et al. 1996) and DBH‐IR and OA‐IR double labels (Beach et al. 2026), revealing only minor discrepancies. In contrast, Elekes et al. (1993) reported a much broader OA‐IR distribution, with our proposed OAergic cells only representing a subpopulation, possibly accounted for by tyramine cross‐reactivity. Beach et al. (2026) also reported a number of cells in the buccal and pedal ganglia that were labeled with only DBH‐IHC (no OA‐IHC) and did not appear to match any of the TBH‐HCR and DBH‐IHC double‐labeled cells we observed. Labeling of the cells was variable, and Beach et al. (2026) suggested cross‐reactivity as a possible explanation, which would be consistent with the absence of any corresponding TBH‐HCR label in our study. Finally, all previous studies noted variable localization and inconsistent detection of OA‐IR cells in the visceral loop (Elekes et al. 1993, 1996; Beach et al. 2026) that we did not observe. Variable and inconsistent DBH‐IHC in these ganglia was detected in our results and by Beach et al. (2026). However, OA was not detected using HPLC in these ganglia, and we observed no cells with more than one label, further supporting the possibility of some cross‐reactivity in the labeling by both OA‐IHC and DBH‐IHC in our study and previous reports. These results also emphasize the risks associated with using single antibodies for determining neural distributions and their putative neurotransmitters (Saper 2005). Corroborating evidence, ideally involving gene, protein, and neurotransmitter expression, is crucial for avoiding spurious results from cross‐reactivity or nonspecific labels.

Although antibody cross‐reactivity remains the most parsimonious explanation of many of the discrepancies, developmental and population variations provide another layer of complexity. Elekes et al. (1996) documented significant increases in OA concentrations and numbers of OA‐IR cells from hatchlings (2.6–3.9 mm), through development, to young adults (16–25 mm). Similar ontogenetic increase of OA has been described in arthropods (Lehman et al. 2000). Beach et al. (2026) and our study examined snails from 10 to 20 mm (older juveniles to younger adults; Croll and Chiasson 1989), whereas Elekes et al. (1993) used mature adult snails (size unspecified) and saw the widest distribution of OA‐IR. Finally, population differences could be another explanation for the discrepancies among the studies. Beach et al. (2026) and the present study used laboratory colonies of shared lineage, which may differ from those used in Elekes et al. 1993, 1996). Thus, the one remaining notable dissimilarity between the studies of the two populations—an additional pair of anteromedial cells in the cerebral ganglia observed by Elekes et al. (1996)—could be a consequence of genetic differences between the animals studied.

Comparison of the OAergic Systems Among Gastropods

Distributions of putative OAergic neurons appear to be relatively conserved across gastropods studied to date. HPLC evidence for OA has been found in the buccal, cerebral, and pedal ganglia (Hiripi et al. 1998; Matsuo et al. 2016; Saavedra et al. 1974). A comparison of neuroanatomical investigations among L. stagnalis, Aplysia californica, and Ambigolimax valentianus revealed similar patterns of OAergic markers in the paired buccal, cerebral, and pedal ganglia, with few or no observations in the remaining suboesophageal ganglia. Mirroring observations in L. stagnalis, three medial buccal cells, with two bilaterally symmetric somata and a third variably positioned, were described in A. californica and Planorbella trivolvis (Martínez‐Rubio et al. 2010; Wentzell et al. 2009). In addition to structural homology, the OAergic buccal cells seem to share a conserved intrinsic modulatory role of the feeding rhythm (Martínez‐Rubio et al. 2010; Quinlan and Murphy 1996; Vehovszky et al. 1998, Vehovszky et al. 2005; Vehovszky and Elliott 2001; Wentzell et al. 2009). In contrast, six medial buccal cells were observed in A. valentianus, and their physiology remains to be characterized (Matsuo et al. 2016). Across species, the cerebral ganglia consistently contained the greatest number of putative OAergic cells. Similar to L. stagnalis, a cluster and two solitary cells were described in both A. californica and A. valentianus, though the latter contained some additional pairs and solitary cells as well (Martínez‐Rubio et al. 2010; Matsuo et al. 2016). The higher number of cells observed in A. valentianus may simply be a consequence of the labeling methods. Our findings in L. stagnalis demonstrated that approximately half of the TBH mRNA and anti‐DBH positive cells did not co‐express OA‐IR. With A. valentianus, Matsuo et al. (2016) used both a custom anti‐TBH antibody and TBH mRNA in situ hybridization but did not use OA‐IHC. Thus, the differences in cells categorized as putatively OAergic may well be a consequence of different evidence used to assess the presence of OA. In L. stagnalis and A. valentianus, paired cells along the lateral edges of the pedal ganglia were observed, potentially modulating locomotor speed in L. stagnalis (Ormshaw and Elliott 2006). Conversely, in A. californica, 11 OA‐IR cells were observed in each pedal ganglion, including two lateral cells that may be homologous to those observed in other species (Martínez‐Rubio et al. 2010). In the absence of corroborating labeling of TBH mRNA or protein, it is possible that additional cells observed in the A. californica could be a consequence of antibody cross‐reactivity. Finally, TBH‐IR was also observed in one cell in the visceral ganglion of A. valentianus (Matsuo et al. 2016). A similar pattern was found with DBH‐IHC in L. stagnalis, though a positive TBH‐HCR label was never observed in any cells in the visceral ganglion in our study.

Evidence across gastropods suggests that OAergic neurons may function primarily as central interneurons rather than peripheral sensory or motor neurons. Labeling in A. californica showed few neurons with projections beyond the ganglion of origin, and no OA‐IR neural fibers or somata were detected in peripheral tissues such as buccal muscle, pharynx, esophagus, and foot. Matsuo et al. (2016) also failed to detect TBH‐IR or western blot signal in the tentacle of A. valentianus. Across studies in the CNS of L. stagnalis, OA‐IR somata revealed varicose arborizations confined to central neuropils and contralateral projections, with little to no traceable peripheral projections (Elekes et al. 1993, 1996). In contrast, qPCR indicated relatively high TBH transcript levels in the cephalic sensory organs compared to other peripheral tissues (Young 2018). Indeed, preliminary observations using HCR revealed sparse TBH mRNA expression (just one to four cells in some tentacles and lips; data not shown), but with no corresponding OA‐IHC detected. Further study is needed to resolve the various possibilities that could account for these results, including incomplete antibody or HCR probe penetration into the peripheral tissues and the specificity of the various methods for one or more different TBH isoforms. Thus, for the moment, it remains uncertain whether there is a sparse population of peripheral OAergic cells in L. stagnalis and whether those cells have any central projections.

Conclusions

Comparisons among the three different markers for OAergic cells reinforce caveats that must be considered when interpreting neuroanatomical labeling. Caution is particularly needed when using a single antibody to map neurotransmitter distribution. We found evidence consistent with cross‐reactivity with both antibodies we used. Compared to IHC, HCR specificity should be stronger for in situ hybridization with well‐designed probes (Choi et al. 2018). Thus, as expected, the discrepancies between the labels in our findings are not as easily explained by the alternate possibility of TBH‐HCR cross‐reactivity with a nontarget sequence. TBH‐HCR labeling was highly consistent across preparations, and the additional cells labeled by either DBH‐IHC or OA‐IHC did not show comparable consistency. In particular, no cells were observed with both DBH‐IHC and OA‐IHC labeling, without a TBH‐HCR label also being observed. Consequently, it seems unlikely that any cells labeled with TBH‐HCR were a result of cross‐reactivity. Working on this assumption, the results as interpreted in the preceding sections suggest several instances of cross‐reactivity in both DBH‐IHC and OA‐IHC labels. This might suggest that further neuroanatomical descriptions should just rely on HCR rather than IHC. However, the apparent absence of mRNA from neural fibers impedes that choice, and double labeling with a corresponding antibody will be needed to visualize the fibers. Furthermore, our combined labeling of mRNA, enzyme, and neurotransmitter revealed that relying on the enzyme (or mRNA and enzyme) as marker(s) may still be inadequate for mapping the true distribution of the neurotransmitter. Our study revealed that cells labeled for the neurotransmitter comprised just half the population of neurons that would have been identified as putatively OAergic using labeling of one or both of the TBH mRNA and enzyme markers involved in the production of the neurotransmitter. This reinforces the value in combining HCR with IHC and in corroborating enzyme and neurotransmitter distributions to provide the most robust approach to mapping neuroanatomy.
