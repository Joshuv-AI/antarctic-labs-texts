AI‐Based Segmentation of Wound Types Caused by Epidermolysis Bullosa
Authors: Georg Wimmer, Christian Puttinger, Pamina Schlager, Roland Zauner, Carolin Gemeier, Tobias Welponer, Christine Prodinger, Anja Diem, Katharina Ude‐Schoder, Christina Guttmann‐Gruber, Bernadette Liemberger, Martin Laimer, Johann W Bauer, Andreas Uhl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEpidermolysis bullosa (EB) defines a group of rare, inherited and currently incurable genetic disorders characterized by excessive skin fragility, with blistering and wounding of skin and mucous membranes upon minor mechanical trauma. Accurate wound assessment is imperative for measuring and longitudinal monitoring of disease activity and for determining the most accurate treatment. In this work, we describe the annotation of clinical images from EB patients and the subsequent training of both classical convolutional neural networks and state‐of‐the‐art transformer‐based architectures for the segmentation of various categories of EB skin wounds. Utilising our dataset of 260 EB images from 18 patients and the corresponding 536 expert‐annotated segmentation masks, we train and evaluate five different model architectures and compare their performance against human inter‐annotator agreement. External evaluation was not possible because no comparable annotated EB datasets are available. Our results demonstrate that transformer‐based models, particularly Mask2Former, achieve near‐expert‐level segmentation accuracy in five of the seven categories and even surpass human inter‐annotator agreement in two. Models based on convolutional neural networks perform noticeably worse and generally fail to accurately segment rarely occurring classes. Averaged over all categories, Mask2Former achieved a mean dice similarity coefficient (DSC) of 52.9%, compared to an inter‐annotator agreement of 55.9%. These results indicate that Mask2Former has the potential to serve as a clinical decision support system to improve wound segmentation and categorization. Furthermore, this model provides a foundation for planned future applications in automated wound measurement, wound progression monitoring and documentation and the development of a smartphone‐based telemedicine application.Keywords: AI‐based wound segmentation, convolutional neural networks, epidermolysis bullosa, image annotation, transformer networks

Introduction

Epidermolysis bullosa (EB) comprises a group of rare, inherited genetic conditions characterized by extreme fragility of the skin and mucous membranes. Individuals with EB experience painful skin damage and an inter‐ and intra‐individual highly variable disease course, ranging from localized blister formations to generalized chronic ulcerations that significantly impair the quality of life [1]. The accurate assessment of wound types and wound extent is essential for clinical monitoring, treatment planning and the objective evaluation of therapeutic interventions. In the absence of a definitive cure, precise longitudinal monitoring is the cornerstone of clinical management to prevent complications such as secondary infections or squamous cell carcinoma [2].

Recent advances in artificial intelligence have enabled automated wound segmentation using deep neural networks. For example, fully automatic wound segmentation using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) was demonstrated in [3] using a large dataset of foot ulcer images. Other notable efforts include a deep learning pipeline with semi‐supervised active learning for wound segmentation from smartphone images [4], and a detection‐plus‐segmentation approach that combines wound localization with pixel‐wise segmentation [5]. A comprehensive review on deep‐learning‐based methods for chronic wound segmentation is provided by [6]. Two other related studies address AI‐supported remote dermatologic assessment [7] and AI‐assisted evaluation of cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma [8]. However, existing research largely focuses on common wound types such as diabetic ulcers. To the best of our knowledge, no previous literature specifically addresses the automated segmentation of EB‐related wounds, which present unique phenotypic challenges and highly varied morphologies. This work systematically evaluates both classical CNN models and modern transformer‐based architectures for the segmentation of EB‐specific wound categories.

Automated detection and segmentation of EB‐related wounds have the potential to serve as a clinical decision support system for detecting and categorising wounds. By utilising the models' segmentation output and mapping it to a 3D surface model (3D mapping is planned in future work), wound areas can be digitally measured with high precision – capturing exact wound areas and shapes rather than relying on manual, one‐dimensional measurements with a wound ruler. The automated documentation planned in future work aims to save clinical time while providing a thorough longitudinal record of wound progression, including wound measurements and related image data. Furthermore, the automated categorization and segmentation of wounds will be essential for the effective use of telemedicine applications to monitor and document wound progression of EB patients [9]. For EB patients, who are typically geographically dispersed and for whom travel is often difficult and painful, smartphone‐based image acquisition at home could then serve as a critical alternative. The challenges associated with travelling to medical and expert facilities frequently result in the delayed identification of critical wound progressions (e.g., bacterial infections, squamous cell carcinoma). Ultimately, a future telemedicine platform integrated with our automated detection could promptly alert both patients and physicians to clinical changes, facilitating timely intervention.

This paper describes the acquisition of image data, the expert annotation process and the application of the annotated data in training and evaluating deep‐learning segmentation models. The performance of the segmentation models is measured using the annotations as ground truth and benchmarked against inter‐annotator agreement. We demonstrate that the highest‐performing segmentation model can categorize and segment EB‐specific wounds with near‐expert‐level accuracy.

Methods

Image Data and Segmentation Ground Truth

Images were recorded using the VECTRA WB360 3D imaging system (https://www.canfieldsci.com/imaging‐systems/vectra‐wb360‐imaging‐system/). This system uses 92 cameras to produce a single, accurate, high‐resolution 3D representation of the patient. Each of the 92 2D images has a high resolution of 4016 × 6020 pixels and depicts the patient from a different perspective. For each patient, we selected a subset of images that fully cover the visible skin areas and show the wounds as clearly as possible. In total, our dataset consists of 260 images from 18 EB patients. The patient cohort comprises six children (four male, two female), three adolescents (one male, two female) and nine adults (eight male, one female); the entire cohort is Caucasian. One additional patient was excluded due to the absence of visible EB‐specific disease features. In Figure 1 we present four exemplar images of one EB patient from different perspectives.

An image‐processing expert performed the first step of the image annotation process, which included segmenting all skin areas and anonymising the images by masking the head region in black. Subsequently, five medical experts with extensive experience in EB‐related skin wounds performed the wound segmentations using the CVAT annotation software. These experts defined seven wound classes to categorize EB manifestations:

Lesional, (re‐)epithelialized: Fully epithelialized skin with or without scaling, atrophy, erythema, scarring, pigment changes.

Additionally, two auxiliary classes were introduced: ‘Cream’ (for thick cream or ointment residues on the skin) and ‘Poor quality’ (for areas where low resolution or poor visibility of the skin precludes reliable detection or segmentation). Image regions depicting the background, clothing (including wound bandages), or unaffected skin are not assigned to specific classes and constitute the unsegmented areas of the images. However, unaffected skin can be isolated by subtracting the affected areas (the seven wound classes plus the ‘Cream’ class) from the total pre‐segmented skin area. In total, the medical experts generated 536 segmentation masks, with the majority of the 260 images being segmented independently by two different experts.

Quality Control and Refinement of Annotations

To measure the agreement between the segmentation masks gathered from different annotators and, later, between the AI‐based predictions and the manually segmented ground truth, we use the dice similarity coefficient (DSC) [10]. The DSC, also known as the Sørensen–Dice index, is defined as:

where A and B represent the sets of pixels labelled as positive (belonging to a particular class) in two segmentation masks. The DSC ranges from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (perfect overlap). The DSC is particularly well‐suited for segmentation tasks involving class imbalance, which is common in medical imaging scenarios such as EB wound detection. Many wound classes, such as blisters or cancerous areas, occupy relatively small portions of the images in our dataset. Unlike simple accuracy metrics, the DSC metric emphasizes the overlap between these smaller regions while being less influenced by the large background area. We report the mean DSC per class across the entire dataset. If both |A| = 0 and |B| = 0 (i.e., both segmentation masks omit the respective class), the DSC is undefined and excluded from the calculation of the mean DSC per class. Additionally, we report the average DSC across all wound classes as well as across all occurring classes, including non‐wound classes such as non‐skin areas or unaffected skin.

Initially, the wound class definitions and annotation guidelines lacked the necessary precision. The absence of workshops to unify the annotations among experts, combined with a lack of specific instructions from the AI developers—particularly regarding the requirement to annotate all visible wounds rather than only those with high diagnostic certainty—led to significant inter‐rater discrepancies and systematic under‐segmentation. In fact, for the first 239 completed image annotations, an average DSC of only 15.6% was achieved across the wound classes.

In response, precise class definitions and annotation guidelines were provided to the medical experts, and several workshops were held to unify the annotation strategy and achieve consistent annotations. All previous annotations were revised. Furthermore, the inter‐annotator variability was continuously measured during the annotation process, and further annotation workshops were held whenever significant differences were identified. More information on the evolving problems and their solutions during the earlier stages of our annotation process in our project can be found in [11]. Finally, after nearly all of the images had been annotated by at least two medical experts, the differences between the annotations were visualized and sent to each annotator for review and potential revision of the areas where the annotations differed. Despite all these measures, image‐based annotation remains inherently challenging. Unlike direct clinical examinations, static images do not allow for dynamic adjustments to resolution or viewing angles. For example, blisters are frequently hard to detect without high zoom factors during digital annotation. Furthermore, there are controversial transitions between classes.

As we can observe, the DSC values for the three individual erosion categories—Erosion–Hypergranulating (which was only used by one annotator on five images), Erosion–Coating and Erosion without other features—were notably low, ranging between 0% and 6.9%. This level of performance is insufficient for reliably training deep neural networks. To address this issue, we aggregated these three categories into a single unified class: Erosions (Lesional, Dehiscent). To better distinguish between affected and healthy tissue, we also explicitly introduced the Unaffected skin class, which was previously grouped together with the background and clothes (including wound bandages). Conversely, all categories lacking direct medical relevance (background, clothes and cream) were merged into a single class labelled as Background, clothes and cream. Furthermore, regions of the image that were labelled as ‘Poor quality’ are set to black, and the corresponding areas in the segmentation masks are relabeled as Background, clothes and cream.

AI ‐Based Segmentation Models

We employ three convolutional neural network (CNN)‐based segmentation models and two transformer‐based segmentation models to segment wounds in images of EB patients. The models are trained and evaluated using the 536 manually segmented masks and their corresponding wound images (260). Segmentation masks from the same image, but different annotators were used as separate training and evaluation samples. The source code for training and inference of the best performing model, as well as the trained model's weights, are publicly accessible at https://github.com/georgwimmer/eb‐segmentation‐mask2former/.

CNN ‐Based Segmentation Models

We employ three distinct CNN‐based segmentation architectures, each pre‐trained on the COCO dataset [12]:

FCN (Fully Convolutional Network) [13]: As one of the pioneering end‐to‐end architectures for semantic segmentation, the FCN replaces traditional fully connected layers with convolutional layers to enable dense, pixel‐wise predictions. It utilises skip connections to fuse coarse, high‐level semantic features with fine‐grained, low‐level spatial details.

DeepLabv3 [14]: This model leverages atrous (dilated) convolutions to capture multi‐scale contextual information without losing spatial resolution. It employs an Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling (ASPP) module to aggregate features across multiple receptive fields, thereby improving segmentation accuracy for clinical manifestations of varying sizes.

LRASPP (Lightweight Refined ASPP) [15]: A streamlined variant of the ASPP module. LR‐ASPP is optimized for efficient segmentation. It reduces computational overhead while maintaining effective multi‐scale feature extraction, making it particularly suitable for processing high‐resolution medical imagery or operating in resource‐constrained environments.

FCN was chosen because it is one of the most widely used baseline segmentation models; DeepLabv3 was selected as a powerful, widely adopted improvement over FCN; and LRASPP was chosen for its low resource requirements and significantly smaller number of trainable parameters.

Transformer‐Based Segmentation Models

We employ two distinct transformer‐based architectures, both of which were pre‐trained on the COCO dataset:

SegFormer [16]: SegFormer is a robust framework for semantic segmentation that integrates a hierarchical transformer encoder with a lightweight multilayer perceptron (MLP) decoder. By eliminating positional encodings, it maintains efficiency across varying input resolutions while effectively capturing long‐range dependencies. In this study, we utilize the SEGFORMER‐B5‐FINETUNED‐ADE‐640‐640 variant available via the Hugging Face Model Hub.

Mask2Former [17]: Mask2Former is a universal segmentation framework designed for semantic, instance and panoptic tasks. Unlike traditional per‐pixel classification methods, it employs a mask‐classification approach within a transformer‐based encoder‐decoder architecture. This allows the model to capture complex spatial relationships and global context more effectively. We implement the MASK2FORMER‐SWIN‐LARGE‐ADE‐SEMANTIC model sourced from Hugging Face.

SegFormer and Mask2Former were selected as state‐of‐the‐art models that represent fundamentally different architectural paradigms, namely per‐pixel classification and mask classification.

Training and Evaluation of the Models

All models are trained and evaluated using 4‐fold cross‐validation. The dataset is partitioned by patient, ensuring that all images from a single individual are assigned to the same fold. This strategy prevents data leakage and ensures that the model is evaluated on patients it has not encountered during training. In each rotation, three folds are used for training while the remaining fold serves as the validation set.

All models are trained for 500 epochs using the AdamW optimizer. Learning rates are managed via a cosine annealing scheduler. For the transformer‐based architectures, a warmup period is included, whereas the CNN‐based models are trained without warmup. The initial learning rate is set to 10−4 for all models, with the exception of Mask2Former, which utilizes a lower rate of 10−5. To mitigate the substantial class imbalance, all models—excluding Mask2Former, which employs its native loss framework—are trained using a combined loss function:

The dice loss [18] promotes spatial overlap between the predicted and ground‐truth masks, while the focal loss [19] emphasizes pixels that are difficult to classify, thereby improving learning on challenging regions.

Images are normalized using the standard ImageNet mean ([0.485, 0.456, 0.406]) and standard deviation ([0.229, 0.224, 0.225]). Data augmentation is employed during training to enhance model generalization. Input images and their corresponding masks undergo random rotations (±15°) along with random horizontal and vertical flips. Brightness, contrast and scaling are further adjusted using random factors ranging from 0.8 to 1.2. Subsequently, a patch of size 1024 × 1024 pixels is randomly extracted from the images and corresponding masks. We implement a sampling strategy to ensure that these patches contain at least some foreground (clinical) regions rather than solely background. The CNN‐based segmentation models are trained using a batch size of 8 and the transformer‐based models using a batch size of 4.

For the evaluation of our high‐resolution EB images (4016 × 6020 pixels), we employ a sliding‐window inference strategy with overlapping patches of size 2048 × 2048 pixels and a stride of 1024 × 1024. By increasing the patch size to 2048 × 2048 during inference—a modification enabled by the reduced memory overhead of the non‐gradient evaluation phase—the models benefit from a broader spatial context. This is particularly advantageous for accurately delineating large, contiguous EB manifestations and ensuring global consistency across the high‐resolution skin area. Each patch is processed independently, and the resulting predictions are subsequently fused using a Gaussian blending mask. This weighting scheme emphasizes the central region of each patch while progressively attenuating contributions near the boundaries. Consequently, this approach effectively mitigates boundary artefacts and ensures a smooth, spatially coherent segmentation across the full image.

where TP denotes true positives, FP false positives and FN false negatives respectively between the model's predicted segmentation mask (P) and the manually annotated ground truth mask (GT). Similar to the calculation of the DSC, these performance measures are computed separately for each class on a mask‐by‐mask basis, and the mean results across our dataset are subsequently reported per class. These three metrics are only calculated when they are well‐defined (i.e., when the denominator is greater than zero). Otherwise, they are excluded from the computation of the mean values per class, consistent with the procedure used for the DSC. However, while the DSC is always defined unless both P and GT are empty, sensitivity and precision are only averaged over masks in which their respective denominators are non‐zero (i.e., GT is not empty for sensitivity, and P is not empty for precision). Consequently, if either P or GT is empty (but not both), the DSC is assigned a value of 0, whereas the respective metric (sensitivity or precision) is excluded from the mask‐wise average. Due to this procedural difference, the reported mean DSC values will appear disproportionately low compared with sensitivity and precision for infrequently occurring classes (our wound classes).

The global confusion matrix (CM) is computed by accumulating the individual confusion matrices of all 536 annotated segmentation masks and their corresponding model predictions, followed by a row‐wise normalization to present the final proportions.

Finally, we apply the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test [20] to compare the best‐performing model with the remaining models. The test is applied to each class independently using the paired DSC scores from the predicted segmentation masks. We exclude cases in which a class was absent in the ground truth and in at least one of the compared model's predictions (undefined DSC score). Statistical significance is determined using a significance level of α = 0.05, after applying the Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple pairwise comparisons across the four model combinations.

As can be seen in Table 2 (top), the transformer‐based models (SegFormer and Mask2Former) consistently outperformed the traditional CNN‐based approaches (FCN, DeepLabv3 and LRASPP). SegFormer and Mask2Former showed a remarkable performance jump in the Lesional, suspected tumour category compared to CNNs, suggesting that the global contextual information captured by transformers is vital for identifying these complex structures. Mask2Former achieved the highest overall performance, reaching a mean DSC of 52.9% over all classes and 37.4% over the five wound categories. Notably, it demonstrated a clear advantage in identifying the most challenging class blister (10.6%). While all models achieved high accuracy on the Background, clothes and cream (≥ 97.7%) and unaffected skin (≥ 80.8%) classes, the performance on individual wound classes varied significantly. The Lesional, (re‐)epithelialized class, which occupies the largest proportion (6%) in our dataset among the wound classes, yielded robust scores, peaking at 63.7% with Mask2Former. In contrast, the segmentation of the rarely occurring wound classes in our dataset remained difficult. The blister category exhibited the lowest agreement across all models. This is likely attributable to its extremely low coverage (0.03%) and the inherent difficulty in distinguishing it from other dehiscent manifestations in 2D images. Table 2 (bottom) shows that, for the blister class, the precision is distinctly higher than the sensitivity for both transformer‐based models, together with a high false negative rate (FNR). This indicates that the models tend to under‐segment this class. The Mask2Former model likewise under‐segments the class suspected tumour, whereas for all other classes—except blister and suspected tumour—precision and sensitivity are relatively similar, suggesting that there is no clear tendency toward over‐ or under‐segmentation. Detailed results at the patient and fold levels and a threshold analysis for the two classes blister and suspected tumour are provided in the Supporting Information.

The third patient was a borderline case exhibiting inter‐annotator disagreement, where only one of the two experts flagged a suspected tumour. Crucially, Mask2Former never generated any false positive suspected tumour detections in the remaining 15 patients.

Finally, comparing the model results (DSC) to the inter‐annotator agreements in Table 1 (Revised class scheme) reveals that the best‐performing model, Mask2Former, has reached a performance level nearly comparable to that of medical experts. While the human expert inter‐annotator agreement averaged over all classes stands at a DSC of 55.9%, Mask2Former achieved 52.9%. Similarly, for the mean DSC over all wound classes, the model's score of 37.4% is close to the inter‐annotator agreement of 41.8%. In certain categories, the model‐to‐annotation overlap even slightly exceeds the measured inter‐annotator agreement, such as in unaffected skin (83.9% vs. 82.7%) and erosions (33.8% vs. 32.2%). However, for classes with extremely low coverage, such as blister, the model performance (10.6%) still lags significantly behind human agreement (19.3%). This discrepancy underscores the challenge of training deep learning models on highly imbalanced datasets, where rare manifestations provide insufficient training signals.

Discussion

This work demonstrates that deep‐learning‐based methods can achieve nearly expert‐level accuracy in the complex segmentation of Epidermolysis bullosa wounds. Our best‐performing model, Mask2Former, was clearly outperformed by the expert's inter‐annotator agreement in only two categories, blister (10.6% vs. 19.3%) and suspected tumour (52.4% vs. 64.2%). In all other categories, Mask2Former's performance either closely trailed the inter‐annotator agreement (BG, clothes and cream: 99.9% vs. 99.7%; Lesional, (re)‐epithalized: 64.9% vs. 63.7%; crust: 28.4% vs. 26.4%) or surpassed it (unaffected skin: 83.9% vs. 82.7%; erosions: 33.8% vs. 32.2%). Notably, the superior performance of transformer‐based architectures for the unaffected skin class (SegFormer: 84.2%, Mask2Former: 83.9%), with an model‐to‐annotation overlap exceeding the observed inter‐annotator agreement, shows great promise for the critical task of differentiating between healthy tissue and affected skin (any of the five wound classes).

The Role of Expert Consensus and Data Quality

The initial annotation phase yielded a low DSC of only 15.6%. Implementing clear annotation guidelines, conducting annotation workshops, merging ambiguous classes and reviewing areas with differing annotations were essential interventions. These steps raised the inter‐annotator agreement (average DSC over all wound classes) from 15.6% to 41.8%. However, the inherent difficulty of image‐based EB wound assessment prevented greater consistency. Unlike physical clinical examinations, static 2D images lack depth perception and the ability to adjust the viewing angle. Furthermore, certain areas of the patient's bodies may be recorded with poor quality. These factors make transitions between certain wound classes inherently ambiguous, resulting in differences in the annotations.

Architectural Advancements: CNNs versus Transformers

The significant performance gap between traditional CNNs (41.9%–46.6% average DSC over all classes) and transformer models (51.0% and 52.9%) underscores the importance of global context in dermatological segmentation. This is most evident in the suspected tumour class, where the transformer models (58.5% and 52.4%) vastly outperformed CNN models (14.2%–29.5%). While CNNs rely primarily on local textures, transformers utilize self‐attention mechanisms to model long‐range dependencies. This allows the network to recognize large‐scale morphological structures that define a suspected tumour rather than focusing solely on isolated pixel clusters.

Challenges of Class Imbalance and Rarity

Extreme class imbalance in our dataset remains the primary bottleneck for the DL‐based wound segmentation. When we compare the outcomes of the models with the expert's inter‐annotation agreement, clearly poorer results across all models were localized to two specific classes:

Blister: Occupying only 0.03% of the area in the annotated data, the models (3.2%–10.6% DSC) lagged distinctly behind the 19.3% inter‐annotator agreement.

These results suggest that neural networks struggle to generalize from such sparse training signals, where the paucity of examples prevents the model from learning the full variance of the class.

Limitations

Our dataset includes only 18 patients, whose images were all acquired using the same imaging device (VECTRA WB360). To apply the models to EB wound images from other devices with different resolutions, lighting conditions and image qualities (e.g., smartphones), additional labelled training data from these sources and a subsequent retraining of the models would likely be necessary. Validation of our models on external datasets was not possible because, to the best of our knowledge, no other datasets with labelled EB wound categories exist. Since the entire patient cohort is Caucasian, applying the model to other ethnicities would require additional training data. Finally, the Mask2Former model is intended as a clinical decision support system rather than for the autonomous detection of suspected malignancy.

Clinical Relevance and Future Work

The Mask2Former model's near expert‐level performance demonstrates its potential as a clinical decision support system for wound detection and categorization. These robust results, together with further validation and extended model training using additional labelled smartphone images, lay the foundation for the three primary future objectives:

3D‐Reconstruction: We intend to map the Mask2Former segmentation output of the images from the Canfield Vectra imaging system to a 3D segmentation using the Vectra software to enable exact wound measurements.

Telemedicine application: We will develop a smartphone‐based application utilising a predefined image acquisition protocol, enabling 3D reconstruction of the Mask2Former segmentation output from the captured images and, consequently, precise wound measurements.
