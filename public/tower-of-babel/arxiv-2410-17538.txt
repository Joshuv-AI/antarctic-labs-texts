Primal-Dual Spectral Representation for Off-policy Evaluation
Yang Hu ∗, Tianyi Chen, Na Li, Kai Wang, Bo Dai

Abstract
Off-policy evaluation (OPE) is one of the most fundamental problems in reinforcement learning (RL) to estimate the expected long-term payoff of a given target policy with only experiences from another behavior policy that is potentially unknown. The distribution correction estimation (DICE) family of estimators have advanced the state of the art in OPE by breaking the curse of horizon . However, the major bottleneck of applying DICE estimators lies in the difficulty of solving the saddle-point optimization involved, especially with neural network implementations. In this paper, we tackle this challenge by establishing a linear representation of value function and stationary distribution correction ratio, i.e. , primal and dual variables in the DICE framework, using the spectral decomposition of the transition operator. Such primal-dual representation not only bypasses the non-convex non-concave optimization in vanilla DICE, therefore enabling an computational efficient algorithm, but also paves the way for more efficient utilization of historical data. We highlight that our algorithm, SpectralDICE , is the first to leverage the linear representation of primal-dual variables that is both computation and sample efficient, the performance of which is supported by a rigorous theoretical sample complexity guarantee and a thorough empirical evaluation on various benchmarks.

Introduction

The past decade has witnessed the ubiquitous success of reinforcement learning (RL) across various domains.
Despite the original rationale that RL agents should learn a reward-maximizing policy from continuous interactions with the environment, there also exist a wide range of applicational scenarios where
online
interaction with the environment may be expensive, inefficient, risky, unethical, and/or even infeasible, examples of which include robotics
(
Kalashnikov et al. 2018
;
Kahn et al. 2018
)
, autonomous driving
(
Shi et al. 2021
;
Fang et al. 2022
)
, healthcare
(
Jagannatha et al. 2018
;
Gottesman et al. 2018
)
, education
(
Mandel et al. 2014
;
Slim et al. 2021
)
, dialogue systems
(
Jaques et al. 2019
;
Jiang et al. 2021
)
and recommendation systems
(
Li et al. 2011
;
Chen et al. 2019
)
.
These application scenarios motivate the study of
offline
RL, where the learning agent only has access to historical data collected by a separate behavior policy.

Off-policy evaluation (OPE) is one of the most fundamental problems in offline RL that aims at estimating the expected cumulative reward of a given target policy using only historical data collected by a different, potentially unknown behavior policy.
In the past decade, various off-policy performance estimators have been proposed
(
Hanna et al. 2019
;
Xie et al. 2019
;
Jiang and Li 2016
;
Foster et al. 2021
)
. However, these estimators generally suffer from the
curse of horizon

(
Liu et al. 2018
)
—step-wise variances accumulate in a multiplicative way, resulting in prohibitively high trajectory variances and thus unreliable estimators.
The recently proposed Distribution Correction Estimation (DICE) family of estimators have advanced the state of the art in OPE, leveraging the primal-dual formulation of policy evaluation for a saddle-point optimization approach that directly estimates the stationary distribution correction ratio, and hence breaking the curse of horizon
(
Nachum et al. 2019a
;
Nachum et al. 2019b
)
.

Nevertheless, as systems scale up in terms of the size of state-action spaces, the saddle-point optimization in the formulation of DICE estimators become increasingly challenging to solve. Such
curse of dimensionality
is common for RL methods in general, and people have been working to alleviate the computational burden by exploiting function approximators.
However, many known function approximators require additional assumptions to ensure computational and statistical properties
(
Gordon 1995
;
Jiang et al. 2017
;
Chen and Jiang 2019
;
Zhan et al. 2022
;
Che et al. 2024
)
, which may not be easily statisfiable in practice. Moreover, the induced optimization upon function approximators may be difficult to solve
(
Boyan and Moore 1994
;
Baird 1995
;
Tsitsiklis and Van Roy 1996
)
.
In particular, under a generic neural network parametrization, computing the DICE estimator
(
Nachum and Dai 2020
)
requires solving non-convex non-concave saddle-point optimizations, which is known to be NP-hard in theory and also yields unstable performance in practice, and is therefore regarded as intractable.

This dilemma brings up a very natural question:

Can we design an OPE algorithm that is both
efficient
and
practical
?

By “efficient” we mean its statistical complexity avoids an exponential dependence on both the length of history and the dimension of state-action spaces,
i.e.
, eliminating both
curse of horizon
and
curse of dimensionality
.
By “practical” we mean the algorithm is free from unstable saddle-point optimizations and can be easily implemented and applied in practical settings.

In this paper, we provide an
affirmative
answer to this question by revealing a novel linear structure encapsulating both
Q
-functions and distribution correction ratios via a spectral representation of the transition operator, which has many nice properties to enable efficient representation learning and off-policy evaluation.

Contributions.

Specifically, the contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows:

•

We propose a novel
primal-dual spectral representation
of the state-action transition operator, which makes both the
Q
-function and the stationary distribution correction ratio (
i.e.
, the primal and dual variables in DICE) linearly representable in the primal/dual feature spaces, and thus enhances the tractability of the corresponding DICE estimator.

•

We design
SpectralDICE
, an off-policy evaluation algorithm based on our primal-dual spectral representation, which bypasses the non-convex non-concave saddle-point optimization in vanilla DICE with generic neural network parameterization, and also makes efficient use of historical data. As far as we are concerned, our algorithm is the first to leverage the linear representation of both primal and dual variables that is
computation and sample efficient
.

•

The performance of the
SpectralDICE
algorithm is justified both theoretically with a rigorous sample complexity guarantee and empirically by a thorough evaluation on various RL benchmarks.

1.1
Related Work

Off-Policy Evaluation (OPE).

Off-policy evaluation has long been an active field of RL research. In the case where the behavior policy is known, various off-policy performance estimators have been proposed, including direct method (DM) estimators
(
Antos et al. 2008
;
Le et al. 2019
)
, importance sampling (IS) estimators
(
Hanna et al. 2019
;
Xie et al. 2019
)
, doubly-robust (DR) estimators
(
Dudík et al. 2011
;
Jiang and Li 2016
;
Foster et al. 2021
)
and other mixed-type estimators
(
Thomas and Brunskill 2016
;
Kallus and Uehara 2020
;
Katdare et al. 2023
)
, which generally suffer from the
curse of dimension
.
In an effort to settle this issue, there is also abundant literature on estimating the correction ratio of the stationary distribution
(
Liu et al. 2018
;
Uehara et al. 2020
)
, among which the distribution correction estimation (DICE) family of estimators are the state of the art that leverage a novel primal-dual formulation of OPE to eliminate the curse of horizon, and in the meantime, allow unknown behavior policies
(
Nachum et al. 2019a
;
Nachum et al. 2019b
;
Yang et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Nachum and Dai 2020
)
.
However, as discussed above, the induced saddle-point optimization becomes unstable with neural networks, impeding the practical application of DICE estimators.

Spectral Representation in MDPs.

Spectral decomposition of the transition kernel is known to induce a linear structure of
Q
-functions, which enables the design of provably efficient algorithms assuming known (primal) spectral feature maps
(
Jin et al. 2020
;
Yang and Wang 2020
;
Ren et al. 2022a
)
.
These algorithms break the curse of dimensionality in the sense that their computation or sample complexity is independent of the size of the state-action space, but rather, only depends polynomially on the feature space dimension, the intrinsic dimension of the problem.

With the growing interest in spectral structures of MDPs, representation learning for RL has recently attracted much theory-oriented attention in the online setting
(
Agarwal et al. 2020
;
Uehara et al. 2021
)
.
Practical representation-based online RL algorithms have been designed via kernel techniques
(
Ren et al. 2022b
;
Ren et al. 2023
)
, latent variable models
(
Ren et al. 2022c
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
, contrastive learning
(
Qiu et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2022a
)
, and diffusion score matching
(
Shribak et al. 2024
)
.
Recently, a unified representation learning framework is proposed from a novel viewpoint that leverages the spectral decomposition of the transition operator
(
Ren et al. 2022a
)
.

Spectral representations have also been exploited in the offline setting
(
Uehara and Sun 2021
;
Ni et al. 2021
;
Chang et al. 2022
)
, where the temporal difference algorithm is applied in the linear space induced by the primal spectral feature for estimating
Q
-functions.
The linear structure of the occupancy measure induced by the dual spectral feature is recently utilized in
Huang et al. 2023
, which leads to an offline RL algorithm for stationary density ratio estimation.
Although the algorithm is theoretically sound, the stationary density ratio breaks the linearity in occupancy, and hence the algorithm is not computationally efficient. As far as we know, there is no such offline RL algorithm that efficiently utilizes both primal and dual representations.

Markov Decision Processes (MDPs).

We consider an
infinite-horizon
discounted Markov decision process (MDP)
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P},r,\mu_{0},\gamma)
, where
\mathcal{S}
is the (possibly infinite) state space,
\mathcal{A}
is the (possibly infinite) action space;
\mathbb{P}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is the transition kernel,
r:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
is the reward function;
\mu_{0}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is the initial state distribution, and
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the reward discount factor, so that the discounted cumulative reward can be defined as
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r_{t}
.
We consider
stationary Markovian policies

\varPi:=\{\pi:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})\}
that admit an action distribution depending on the current state only. Given any policy
\pi\in\varPi
, let
\mathbb{E}_{\pi,\mathbb{P}}[\cdot]
denote the expectation over the trajectory governed by
\pi
and
\mathbb{P}
(possibly under prescribed initial conditions).
Let
d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot)\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A})
denote the
(stationary) state-action occupancy measure
under policy
\pi
,
i.e.
, the normalized discounted probability of visiting
(s,a)
in a trajectory induced by policy
\pi
, defined by

d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s,a)=(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{\pi,\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\mathbbm{1}\{s_{t}=s,a_{t}=a\}}}\right].

Similarly, let
d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(\cdot)\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
denote the
state occupancy measure
subject to the relation
d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s,a)=d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s)\pi(a|s)
.
Further, define the state/state-action value functions (a.k.a.
V
- and
Q
-functions) as follows:

\displaystyle V_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\pi,\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r(s_{t},a_{t})\;\middle|\;s_{0}=s}}\right],

\displaystyle Q_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s,a)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\pi,\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r(s_{t},a_{t})\;\middle|\;s_{0}=s,~a_{0}=a}}\right].

In this way, the value of policy
\pi
in
\mathcal{M}
is defined by

\rho_{\vphantom{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\mathbb{P}}(\pi):=(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu_{0}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[V_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s)}}\right]\\
=(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{l}s\sim\mu_{0},\\
a\sim\pi(\cdot|s)\end{subarray}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s,a)}}\right],

where the factor
(1-\gamma)
is introduced for normalization. We omit the subscript
\mathbb{P}
when the context is clear.

Remark 1
.

In order to better illustrate how the proposed method works in MDPs with continuous state-action spaces, we abuse the notation a bit to regard
\mathbb{P}
,
\pi
and
d^{\pi}
as
densities
. Parallel results for the discrete case can be analogously derived without difficulties.

The Primal-Dual Characterization of
\bm{\rho(\pi)}
.

Distribution Correction Estimation (DICE)
(
Nachum and Dai 2020
)
is a primal-dual-based method that evaluates the value of a given target policy
\pi
in the offline setting, using the linear programming (LP) formulation of policy values
(
Puterman 2014
)
. Specifically, it is known that we can equivalently characterize
\rho(\pi)
defined in (
2
) by the
primal LP
:

\displaystyle\min_{Q(\cdot,\cdot)}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{l}s\sim\mu_{0},\\
a\sim\pi(\cdot|s)\end{subarray}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q(s,a)}}\right],

s.t.

\displaystyle Q(s,a)\geqslant r(s,a)+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{l}s^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{P}(\cdot|s,a),\\
a^{\prime}\sim\pi(\cdot|s^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}\right],~\forall(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}.

(1)

Further, it can be shown that strong duality holds in (
2
), with Lagrangian multipliers exactly the state-action occupancy measures
d^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot)
. We can therefore characterize
\rho(\pi)
by the following
primal-dual LP
:

\min_{Q(\cdot,\cdot)}\max_{d(\cdot,\cdot)}\quad(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{l}s\sim\mu_{0},\\
a\sim\pi(\cdot|s)\end{subarray}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q(s,a)}}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim d^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[r(s,a)+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{l}s^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{P}(\cdot|s,a),\\
a^{\prime}\sim\pi(\cdot|s^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}\right]}}\right].

(2)

We highlight that this primal-dual LP formulation is favored in the offline RL setting in that historical experiences can be utilized to empirically approximate the expectations in (
2
) after some simple change-of-variables. In particular, for any measurable function
f(s,a)
, the importance sampling (IS) estimator for the expected value of
f(s,a)
is given by

\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim d^{\pi}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f(s,a)}}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{d^{\pi}(s,a)}{d^{\mathcal{D}}(s,a)}\cdot f(s,a)}}\right],

(3)

where
\zeta(s,a):=\frac{d^{\pi}(s,a)}{d^{\mathcal{D}}(s,a)}
is known as the
stationary distribution correction ratio
for dataset
\mathcal{D}\sim d^{\mathcal{D}}
.

The DICE family estimators
(
Nachum et al. 2019a
;
Zhang et al. 2022b
;
Dai et al. 2020
)
is designed by plugging the IS expectation estimator (
3
) into (
2
), such that the stationary distribution correction ratio
\zeta(\cdot,\cdot)
is parameterized along with the
Q
-function to formulate an optimization, with various regularization available
(
Yang et al. 2020
)
. It is evident that the DICE family estimators are applicable to the offline RL setting with unknown behavior policy.

Spectral Representation.

We can always perform spectral decomposition of the dynamic operator to obtain a spectral representation of
any
MDP
(
Ren et al. 2022a
)
. In particular,
low-rank MDPs
refer to such MDPs with intrinsic finite-rank spectral representation structures that enable scalable RL algorithms, and are thus of theoretical interest
(
Yao et al. 2014
;
Jin et al. 2020
)
. Formally,
\mathcal{M}
is said to be a
low-rank MDP
if there exists a
primal
feature map
\bm{\phi}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
dual
features
\tilde{\bm{\mu}}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\bm{\theta}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that
\mathbb{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)=\langle\bm{\phi}(s,a),\tilde{\bm{\mu}}(s^{\prime})\rangle
,
r(s,a)=\langle\bm{\phi}(s,a),\bm{\theta}_{r}\rangle
, for any
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
,
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Here both the primal feature
\bm{\phi}
and the dual features
\tilde{\bm{\mu}}
,
\bm{\theta}_{r}
are assumed to be
unknown, and thus must be learned from data
(
Agarwal et al. 2020
;
Uehara et al. 2021
)
.

Unfortunately, it is revealed in
Zhang et al. 2022a
;
Ren et al. 2022a
that learning the features of a low-rank MDP is difficult from the unnormalized density fitting point of view.
To settle this tractability issue, the above papers propose a reparameterization of the dual feature as
\tilde{\bm{\mu}}(\cdot)=q(\cdot)\bm{\mu}(\cdot)
, where we introduce an auxiliary distribution
q(\cdot)\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
that will be specified later. Therefore, we will stick to the following spectral decomposition of the transition kernel in this paper:

\mathbb{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)=\langle\bm{\phi}(s,a),q(s^{\prime})\bm{\mu}(s^{\prime})\rangle,~\forall s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A},s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}.

(4)

Under such reparameterization, it has been shown that the spectral representaton can be learned efficiently.

Additionally, we also assume
\mu_{0}
to be linearly representable in the dual feature space.

Assumption 1

(initial representation)
.

There exists
\bm{\omega}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that
\mu_{0}(s)=q(s)\langle\bm{\mu}(s),\bm{\omega}_{0}\rangle
,
\forall s\in\mathcal{S}
.

Off-Policy Evaluation (OPE).

We consider a setting where we are given
\mathcal{D}=\{(s_{i},a_{i},s^{\prime}_{i})\mid i\in[N]\}
, an offline dataset of
N
historical transitions, sampled by certain
behavior policy

\pi_{\mathrm{b}}
that could be unknown. The objective is to estimate the expected cumulative rewards
\rho(\pi)
of a different
target policy

\pi
.

For satisfactory performance, it is important that the behavior policy provides sufficient data coverage for the frequent transitions experienced by policy
\pi
. Specifically, we assume the occupancy ratio between
\pi
and
\pi_{\mathrm{b}}
satisfies the following regularity assumption.

Assumption 2

(concentratability)
.

\frac{d^{\pi}(s,a)}{d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)}\leqslant C_{\infty}^{\pi}
,
\forall s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A}
.

We point out that the concentratability assumption is standard in offline RL literature
(
Munos and Szepesvári 2008
;
Chen and Jiang 2019
)
, and is also implicitly enforced in recent work like
Huang et al. 2023
(see Definition 1 therein). We are aware that the coefficient
C_{\infty}^{\pi}
can potentially be translated into different feature-related constants
(
Uehara et al. 2021
)
, which does not change the asymptotics of sample complexity, yet only adds to the technical complexity. For clarity, we will stick to the simple
2
in this paper.

3
SpectralDICE
: OPE using Primal-Dual Spectral Representation

In this section, we first introduce a novel linear representation for the stationary distribution correction ratio using the
dual
spectral feature of transition kernel. We highlight that this linear structure, together with the known linear representation of
Q
-functions, helps to bypass the non-convex non-concave optimization required in the computation of DICE estimators, and also enables efficient utilization of historical data sampled by unknown behavior policies. Based on the above ideas, we present
SpectralDICE
, the proposed off-policy evaluation (OPE) algorithm using our primal-dual spectral representation.

3.1
Primal-Dual Spectral Representation

We start by specifying the primal-dual spectral representation used in
SpectralDICE
. At first glance, it may seem natural to directly learn the spectral representation of
\mathbb{P}
as defined in (
4
). However, it turns out that this naive approach includes the target policy
\pi
in the linear representation of
d^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot)
, which in turn induces a complicated representation for the stationary distribution correction ratio
\zeta(\cdot,\cdot)

(
Huang et al. 2023
)
, and thus, leads to an intractable optimization (
2
) for the computation of the DICE estimator.

The above challenge inspires us to properly reparameterize the spectral decomposition (
4
). Specifically, since we only work with a fixed target policy
\pi
for off-policy evaluation, we shall consider the following alternative representation of the state-action transition kernel
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a):=\mathbb{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\pi(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})
:

\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)=\Big\langle\bm{\phi}(s,a),q(s^{\prime})\pi_{\mathrm{b}}(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})\underbrace{\tfrac{\pi(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})}{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})}\bm{\mu}(s^{\prime})}_{\bm{\mu}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}\Big\rangle.

(5)

Note that
2
guarantees a non-zero denominator when the nominator is non-zero. We refer to (
5
) as the
primal-dual spectral representation
of the state-action) transition kernel
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}
, where
\bm{\phi}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\bm{\mu}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot)
are still called
primal
and
dual
spectral features, respectively. The superscript
\pi
of the dual spectral feature emphasizes its dependence on the target policy.

The primal-dual spectral representation has several nice properties. In particular, we can show that the
Q
-function
Q^{\pi}(s,a)
, the state-action occupancy measure
d^{\pi}(s,a)
, and the stationary distribution correction ratio
\zeta(s,a)
can all be represented in linear forms using the primal/dual features, as summarized below.

Lemma 1
.

With primal-dual spectral representation (
5
), the
Q
-function
Q^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot)
is linearly representable in the primal feature space with cofactor
\bm{\theta}_{Q}^{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

Q^{\pi}(s,a)=\langle\bm{\phi}(s,a),\bm{\theta}_{Q}^{\pi}\rangle,~\forall s\in S,a\in\mathcal{A}.

(6)

Further, under
1
, the state-action occupancy measure
d^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot)
is also linearly representable in the dual feature space with cofactor
\bm{\omega}_{d}^{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

d^{\pi}(s,a)=q(s)\pi_{\mathrm{b}}(a|s)\langle\bm{\mu}^{\pi}(s,a),\bm{\omega}_{d}^{\pi}\rangle,~\forall s\in S,a\in\mathcal{A}.

Specifically, when the auxiliary distribution
q(\cdot)
is selected as the state-occupancy measure
d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(\cdot)
of the behavior policy
\pi_{\mathrm{b}}
, the stationary distribution correction ratio can also be linearly represented as:

\zeta(s,a)=\frac{d^{\pi}(s,a)}{q(s)\pi_{\mathrm{b}}(a|s)}=\langle\bm{\mu}^{\pi}(s,a),\bm{\omega}_{d}^{\pi}\rangle.

(7)

Proof.

Note that the original dual feature in (
4
) can be restored by
\bm{\mu}(s^{\prime})=\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})}{\pi(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})}\bm{\mu}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})
for any
a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
. Then by Bellman recursive equation we have:

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{\phi}(s,a),\bm{\theta}_{r}\rangle+\gamma\int V^{\pi}(s^{\prime})\langle\bm{\phi}(s,a),q(s^{\prime})\bm{\mu}(s^{\prime})\rangle\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\bm{\phi}(s,a),\underbrace{\bm{\theta}_{r}+\gamma\int V^{\pi}(s^{\prime})q(s^{\prime})\bm{\mu}(s^{\prime})\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}}_{\bm{\theta}_{Q}^{\pi}}}}\right\rangle.

Similarly, by the recursive property of
d^{\pi}
we have:

\displaystyle d^{\pi}(s,a)={}

\displaystyle(1-\gamma)\mu_{0}(s)\pi(a|s)+\gamma\int d^{\pi}(\tilde{s},\tilde{a})\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s,a|\tilde{s},\tilde{a})\mathrm{d}\tilde{s}\mathrm{d}\tilde{a}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle(1-\gamma)q(s)\big\langle\pi_{\mathrm{b}}(a|s)\bm{\mu}^{\pi}(s,a),\bm{\omega}_{0}\big\rangle+\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle q(s)\pi_{\mathrm{b}}(a|s)\bm{\mu}^{\pi}(s,a),\int d^{\pi}(\tilde{s},\tilde{a})\bm{\phi}(\tilde{s},\tilde{a})\mathrm{d}\tilde{s}\mathrm{d}\tilde{a}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\biggl\langle q(s)\pi_{\mathrm{b}}(a|s)\bm{\mu}^{\pi}(s,a),\underbrace{(1-\gamma)\bm{\omega}_{0}+\gamma\int d^{\pi}(\tilde{s},\tilde{a})\bm{\phi}(\tilde{s},\tilde{a})\mathrm{d}\tilde{s}\mathrm{d}\tilde{a}}_{\bm{\omega}_{d}^{\pi}}\biggr\rangle,

where we use the initial representation (
1
) and the fact that
\pi(a|s)\bm{\mu}(s)=\pi_{\mathrm{b}}(a|s)\bm{\mu}^{\pi}(s,a)
. The representation of
\zeta(\cdot,\cdot)
is hence a direct corollary since
q(s)\pi_{\mathrm{b}}(a|s)=d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)
when
q(\cdot)=d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(\cdot)
.
∎

Then, using the linear spectral representations of
Q
and
\zeta
in (
6
) and (
7
), we shall equivalently formulate the DICE estimator as follows.

Corollary 2
.

With primal-dual spectral representation (
5
) where
q(\cdot)\equiv d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(\cdot)
, under
1
,

\displaystyle\rho_{\vphantom{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\mathbb{P}}(\pi)

\displaystyle=\min_{\bm{\theta}_{Q}}\max_{\bm{\omega}_{d}}\biggl\{(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{l}s\sim\mu_{0},\\

•

Offline data compatible.

of the behavior policy
\pi_{\mathrm{b}}
such that

•

Given (learned)

3.2
Spectral Representation Learning

In the last section, we have elaborated on how to perform OPE using off-policy data given a primal-dual spectral representation. Now it only suffices to specify how to learn such a representation, which we regard as an abstract subroutine
(\hat{\bm{\phi}},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi})\leftarrow\textsc{RepLearn}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{D},\pi)
. Here
\mathcal{F}
denotes the collection of candidate representations. We highlight that our algorithm works with any representation learning method that has a bounded learning error, without any further requirements on the learning mechanism. Given a range of spectral representation learning methods available in literature
(
Zhang et al. 2022a
;
Ren et al. 2022a
;
Ren et al. 2022c
;
Shribak et al. 2024
)
, for the sake of clarity we only consider a few candidates here, while other methods may also be applicable:

1.

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS).
Inspired by
Ren et al. 2022a
, an OLS objective can be constructed as follows. Denote by
\mathbb{Q}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime},s,a):=d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)
the joint distribution of state-action transitions under behavior policy
\pi_{\mathrm{b}}
, based on which we plug in (
5
) to obtain

\frac{\mathbb{Q}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime},s,a)}{\sqrt{d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}=\sqrt{d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}\bm{\phi}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\mu}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}),

which further induces the following OLS objective:

\min_{(\hat{\bm{\phi}},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi})\in\mathcal{F}}\int\Biggl(\frac{\mathbb{Q}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime},s,a)}{\sqrt{d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}-\sqrt{d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\Biggr)^{2}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}a\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}

Therefore,
(\hat{\bm{\phi}},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi})
can be learned by solving
(
Ren et al. 2022a
;
HaoChen et al. 2021
)
:

\min_{(\hat{\bm{\phi}},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi})\in\mathcal{F}}\Bigl\{\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{(s,a)\sim d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}},(\tilde{s}^{\prime},\tilde{a}^{\prime})\sim d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big(\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(\tilde{s}^{\prime},\tilde{a}^{\prime})\big)^{2}}}\right]-2\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{(s,a)\sim d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}},(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\sim\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}\right]\Bigr\},

where the last term becomes a constant after expansion and is thus omitted. For practical implementation, we can use stochastic gradient descent to solve the above stochastic optimization problem.

2.

Noise-Contrastive Estimation (NCE).
NCE is a widely used method for contrastive representation learning in RL
(
Zhang et al. 2022a
;
Qiu et al. 2022
)
. To learn
(\hat{\bm{\phi}},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi})
, we consider a binary contrastive learning objective
(
Qiu et al. 2022
)
:

\min_{(\hat{\bm{\phi}},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi})\in\mathcal{F}}\widehat{E}_{(s,a)\sim d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}}\biggl[\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\sim\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log\Big(1+\tfrac{1}{\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}\Big)}}\right]+\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\sim P_{\mathrm{neg}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log\Big(1+\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\Big)}}\right]\biggr],

where
P_{\mathrm{neg}}
is a negative sampling distribution.

Details of these representation learning methods along with their learning errors can be found in
Appendix

C
.

4
Theoretical Guarantee

In this section, we provide a rigorously theoretical analysis regarding the sample complexity of the proposed
SpectralDICE
algorithm. For the sake of technical conciseness, we make the following assumption on the candidate family
\mathcal{F}
. We argue that this is not a restrictive assumption, but rather, only helps to highlight the key contributions with simplified analysis.

Assumption 3

(realizability)
.

Assume a finite family
\mathcal{F}
, such that
\langle\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a),d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\rangle
is a valid state-action transition kernel for any
(\hat{\bm{\phi}},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi})\in\mathcal{F}
, and the ground-truth representation
(\bm{\phi}^{\star},\bm{\mu}^{\pi,\star})\in\mathcal{F}
.

Representation Learning Error.

The key to subsequent analyses is to first bound the error of representation learning, which is of some theoretical interest by itself. Generally speaking, we expect
probably approximately correct (PAC)
bounds for representation learning in the following format.

Claim 3
.

With probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}_{\mathbb{P}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big\|\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)-\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)\big\|_{1}}}\right]\leqslant\xi(|\mathcal{F}|,N,\delta),

where
\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a):=d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime})
,
N
is the number of samples in
\mathcal{D}
, and the upper bound
\xi
only depends on
|\mathcal{F}|
,
N
and
\delta
.

We point out that, under certain regularity assumptions, the above claim can be proven for many spectral representation learning algorithms. Specifically, when
RepLearn
is implemented by OLS or NCE, we can show that
\xi(|\mathcal{F}|,N,\delta)=\Theta\Big(\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\log\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{\delta}}\Big)
.

Policy Evaluation Error.

The performance of the proposed
SpectralDICE
algorithm is evaluated by the
policy evaluation error

\mathcal{E}:=\hat{\rho}(\pi)-\rho_{\vphantom{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\mathbb{P}}(\pi)
, which can be further bounded by the following theorem.

Theorem 4

(Main Theorem)
.

Suppose
3
holds for the
RepLearn
subroutine. Then under
1
,
2
and
3
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\mathcal{E}\lesssim\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}}+\frac{1}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\cdot\xi(|\mathcal{F}|,N,\delta/2).

Proof sketch.

We first split
\mathcal{E}
into the following terms:

\mathcal{E}\;=\;\underbrace{\hat{\rho}(\pi)-\bar{\rho}(\pi)}_{\textrm{statistical}}\;+\;\underbrace{\bar{\rho}(\pi)-\rho_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}(\pi)}_{\textrm{dataset}}\;+\;\underbrace{\rho_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}(\pi)-\rho_{\vphantom{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\mathbb{P}}(\pi)}_{\textrm{representation}},

where we introduce an auxiliary problem:

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}(\pi)

\displaystyle=\min_{\bm{\theta}_{Q}}\max_{\bm{\omega}_{d}}\biggl\{(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{l}s\sim\mu_{0},\\
a\sim\pi(\cdot|s)\end{subarray}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{Q}}}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 80.00012pt+\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{l}s\sim d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(\cdot),~a\sim\pi_{\mathrm{b}}(a|s),\\
(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\sim\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)\end{subarray}}\Bigl[\big(\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\omega}_{d}\big)\cdot{}\big(r(s,a)+\gamma\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{Q}-\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{Q}\big)\Bigr]\biggr\}.

Note that (
3
) is the empirical estimation of
\bar{\rho}(\pi)
, and that
\bar{\rho}(\pi)
is (subtly) inequivalent to
\rho_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}(\pi)
—the expectation is still taken over
(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\sim\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)
rather than
\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)=\langle\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a),\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot)\rangle
.

Intuitively, the latter two terms are directly related to the representation learning error established in
3
, which can actually be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\rho_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}(\pi)-\rho_{\vphantom{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\mathbb{P}}(\pi)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\gamma}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\cdot\xi(|\mathcal{F}|,N,\delta/2),

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}(\pi)-\rho_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}(\pi)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\cdot\xi(|\mathcal{F}|,N,\delta/2).

On the other hand, the first term is only caused by replacing the expectations with their empirical estimators, which can be bounded by concentration inequalities as:

\hat{\rho}(\pi)-\bar{\rho}(\pi)\lesssim\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{N}}.

Plugging these terms back completes the proof.
∎

6
Conclusion

In this paper, to relieve the intrinsic tension between breaking the curse of horizon and overcoming the curse of dimensionality via DICE estimators, we propose a novel primal-dual spectral representation method that establishes linear spectral representations for both the primal variable (
i.e.
,
Q
-function) and the dual variable (
i.e.
, stationary distribution correction ratio), which leads to
SpectralDICE
,
an efficient and practical OPE algorithm that eliminates the non-convex non-concave saddle-point optimization in DICE and makes efficient use of historical data.
The performance of
SpectralDICE
is justified by a theoretical sample complexity guarantee and the empirical outperformance. Future directions include taking one step further to design offline policy optimization methods using primal-dual spectral representations, and applying the algorithm for efficient imitation learning.

B.2

In
Section

3.1
, it is mentioned that directly applying the naive spectral representation (

Note that, in
Lemma

1
, the linear representation of

only builds upon the low-rank MDP assumption, and therefore it still holds with the naive spectral representation (
4

d^{\pi}(s,a)=\biggl\langle q(s)\pi(a|s)\bm{\mu}(s),\underbrace{(1-\gamma)\bm{\omega}_{0}+\gamma\int d^{\pi}(\tilde{s},\tilde{a})\bm{\phi}(\tilde{s},\tilde{a})\mathrm{d}\tilde{s}\mathrm{d}\tilde{a}}_{\bm{\omega}_{d}^{\pi}}\biggr\rangle,

which can be obtained by plugging the relation

into the linear representation of

to eliminate
\bm{\mu}^{\pi}
from the representation. Consequently, the LP formulation (
10

\displaystyle\rho(\pi)

\displaystyle=\min_{Q(\cdot,\cdot)}\max_{d^{\pi}(\cdot)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{l}s\sim\mu_{0},\\

\displaystyle=\min_{Q(\cdot,\cdot)}\max_{d^{\pi}(\cdot)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{l}s\sim\mu_{0},\\

B.3
Solving the Minimax Problem via Regularization

It is known that directly solving (
3
) leads to potential numerical instability issues due to the objective’s linearity in
\bm{\theta}_{Q}
and
\bm{\omega}_{d}

(
Nachum et al. 2019b
)
. Fortunately, it is shown in
Yang et al. 2020
that certain regularization leads to strictly concave inner maximization while keeping the optimal
solution

\bm{\omega}_{d}^{\star}
unbiased. Specifically, in practical implementation we may append the following regularizer to the objective in (
8
):

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{reg}}(\pi)

\displaystyle=\min_{\bm{\theta}_{Q}}\max_{\bm{\omega}_{d}}\biggl\{(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{l}s\sim\mu_{0},\\
a\sim\pi(\cdot|s)\end{subarray}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\bm{\phi}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{Q}}}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{l}s\sim d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(\cdot),~a\sim\pi_{\mathrm{b}}(a|s),\\
s^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{P}(\cdot|s,a),~a^{\prime}\sim\pi(\cdot|s^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\Bigl[\big(\bm{\mu}^{\pi}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\omega}_{d}\big)\cdot{}

\displaystyle\hskip 70.0001pt\big(r(s,a)+\gamma\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{Q}-\bm{\phi}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{Q}\big)\Bigr]{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{}-\lambda\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\mathcal{D}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f(\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\omega}_{d})}}\right]}\bigg\}.

(12)

Here
f
is a differentiable convex function with closed and convex Fenchel conjugate
f_{*}
(see
Section

E.1
), and
\lambda>0
is a tunable constant that controls the magnitude of regularization. It is evident that the regularized objective is concave in
\bm{\omega}_{d}
, which facilitates the inner maximization. What’s more, it has also be shown that such regularization does not alter the optimal solution
\bm{\omega}_{d}^{\star}
, as summarized below.

Lemma 5

(
Nachum et al. 2019b
;
Yang et al. 2020
)
.

The solution
(\bm{\theta}_{Q}^{\mathrm{reg},\star},\bm{\omega}_{d}^{\mathrm{reg},\star})
to (
B.3
) satisfies:

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{Q}^{\mathrm{reg},\star}

\displaystyle=\bm{\phi}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{Q}^{\star}-\lambda(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{P}^{\pi})^{-1}f^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tfrac{d^{\pi}(s,a)}{d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)}}}\right),

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}^{\pi}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\omega}_{d}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mu}^{\pi}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\omega}_{d}^{\mathrm{reg},\star},

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{reg}}(\pi)

\displaystyle=\rho(\pi)-\lambda\mathsf{D}_{f}(d^{\pi}\|d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}),

where
(\bm{\theta}_{Q}^{\star},\bm{\omega}_{d}^{\star})
is the solution to (
8
).

We emphasize that the regularized problem is unbiased only in the sense that
\bm{\omega}_{d}^{\mathrm{reg},\star}=\bm{\omega}_{d}^{\star}
. Therefore, in general we need to plug
\bm{\omega}_{d}^{\mathrm{reg},\star}
back into (
8
) and solve the outer minimization again to recover
\bm{\theta}_{Q}^{\star}
. Nevertheless, when
\lambda
is sufficiently small, we shall regard
\bm{\theta}_{Q}^{\mathrm{reg},\star}\approx\bm{\theta}_{Q}^{\star}
to relieve the computational burden.

In practice, we can only solve the empirical version of (
B.3
),
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{reg}}(\pi)

\displaystyle=\min_{\bm{\theta}_{Q}}\max_{\bm{\omega}_{d}}\biggl\{(1-\gamma)\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{l}s\sim\mu_{0},\\
a\sim\pi(\cdot|s)\end{subarray}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\bm{\phi}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{Q}}}\right]+\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{l}s\sim\mu_{0},\\
a\sim\pi(\cdot|s)\end{subarray}}\Bigl[\big(\bm{\mu}^{\pi}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\omega}_{d}\big)\cdot{}

\displaystyle\hskip 70.0001pt\big(r(s,a)+\gamma\bm{\phi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{Q}-\bm{\phi}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{Q}\big)\Bigr]{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{}-\lambda\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{(s,a)\sim\mathcal{D}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f(\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\omega}_{d})}}\right]}\bigg\}.

C.1
Ordinary Least Sqaures (OLS)

Method.

Inspired by
Ren et al. 2022a
, the objective of OLS can be constructed as follows. Denote by
\mathbb{Q}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime},s,a):=d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)
the joint distribution of state-action transitions under behavior policy
\pi_{\mathrm{b}}
. Then we plug
\mathbb{Q}^{\pi}
into (
5
) and rearrange the terms to obtain

\frac{\mathbb{Q}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime},s,a)}{\sqrt{d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}=\sqrt{d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}\bm{\phi}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\mu}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}).

Therefore, we propose to optimize over the following OLS objective:

\displaystyle\min_{(\hat{\bm{\phi}},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi})\in\mathcal{F}}\int\Biggl(\frac{\mathbb{Q}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime},s,a)}{\sqrt{d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}-\sqrt{d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\Biggr)^{2}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}a\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\min_{(\hat{\bm{\phi}},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi})\in\mathcal{F}}\biggl\{\int\frac{\mathbb{Q}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime},s,a)^{2}}{d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}a\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}-2\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}},(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\sim\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 50.00008pt+\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}},(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\sim d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big(\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\big)^{2}}}\right]\biggr\},

Note that the first term
\int\frac{\mathbb{Q}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime},s,a)^{2}}{d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}a\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}
is a constant that can be omitted in optimization, while the second and third terms can be effectively approximated by sampling from the dataset
\mathcal{D}
and the target policy
\pi
. Therefore, in practice we learn
(\hat{\bm{\phi}},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi})
by solving the following optimization:

\min_{(\hat{\bm{\phi}},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi})\in\mathcal{F}}\bigg\{\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{(s,a)\sim d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}},(\tilde{s}^{\prime},\tilde{a}^{\prime})\sim d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big(\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(\tilde{s}^{\prime},\tilde{a}^{\prime})\big)^{2}}}\right]-2\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{(s,a)\sim d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}},(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\sim\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}\right]\bigg\},

(13)

where the expectations are replaced by their empirical estimations using data sampled from
\mathcal{D}
.

Error Bound.

We proceed to show the representation learning error bound for the OLS method, which requires the following regularity assumption on the transition kernel
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}
and the occupancy measure
d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}
.

Assumption 4

(regularity for OLS)
.

(1) lower-bounded transition kernel:
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)\geqslant\frac{1}{C_{\mathbb{P}}}>0
,
\forall s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime}
; (2) effective behavior policy coverage:
\frac{d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)}{d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}\leqslant C_{\mathrm{cov}}
,
\forall s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime}
.

We point out that the major rationale behind these mild assumptions is to rule out the cases where certain transitions are scarcely sampled due to the singularity in transition kernel or behavior policy.

Theorem 6

(OLS learning error)
.

Under
1
,
2
and
3
and the additional
4
for regularity, let
(\hat{\bm{\phi}},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi})
be the solution to (
13
), and set
\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a):=d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime})
. Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big\|\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)-\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)\big\|_{1}}}\right]\leqslant\sqrt{C_{\mathbb{P}}C_{\mathrm{reg}}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\log(|\mathcal{F}|/\delta)}{N}},

where
C_{\mathrm{reg}}=\frac{4}{3}\sqrt{C_{\mathrm{cov}}}+8C_{\mathrm{cov}}
is a universal constant determined by the PAC bound for OLS..

Proof.

We would like to apply the fast-rate PAC bound for OLS regression (
Lemma

18
). For the sake of clarity, we explicitly define the family of candidate regression functions as

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{f:(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\mapsto\sqrt{d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}\bm{\phi}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\mu}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\;\middle|\;(\bm{\phi},\bm{\mu}^{\pi})\in\mathcal{F}}}\right\}.

It is evident that any

is bounded as follows:

0\leqslant f(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})=\sqrt{\frac{d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)}{d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)\leqslant\sqrt{C_{\mathrm{cov}}},

where we use the fact that
\langle\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a),d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\rangle

\tilde{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}
(by
3

18
that, with probability at least
1-\delta

\displaystyle\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f^{\star}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-\hat{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}a\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}\leqslant C_{\mathrm{reg}}\cdot\frac{\log(|\mathcal{F}|/\delta)}{N},

(14)

where
C_{\mathrm{reg}}:=\frac{4}{3}\sqrt{C_{\mathrm{cov}}}+8C_{\mathrm{cov}}
, and
\hat{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime}):=\sqrt{d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big\|\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)-\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)\big\|_{1}}}\right]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)-\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)}}\right|\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}a\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}

(15a)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathbb{Q}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime},s,a)-\hat{\mathbb{Q}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime},s,a)}}\right|\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}a\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}

(15b)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\int\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\mathbb{Q}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime},s,a)}-\frac{\hat{\mathbb{Q}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime},s,a)}{\sqrt{\mathbb{Q}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime},s,a)}}}}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}a\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}\cdot\int\mathbb{Q}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime},s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}a\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sqrt{\int\frac{d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}{\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f^{\star}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-\hat{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}a\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\sqrt{\max_{s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}{\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)}}}\right\}}\cdot\sqrt{C_{\mathrm{reg}}\cdot\frac{\log(|\mathcal{F}|/\delta)}{N}}

(15e)

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\sqrt{C_{\mathbb{P}}C_{\mathrm{reg}}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\log(|\mathcal{F}|/\delta)}{N}},

(15f)

where in (
15b
) we use the definition of
\mathbb{Q}^{\pi}

\hat{\mathbb{Q}}^{\pi}:=d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}_{\mathbb{P}}(s,a)\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)
; in (

C.2
Noise-Constrastive Learning (NCE)

Method.

NCE is a widely used method for contrastive representation learning in RL
(
Zhang et al. 2022a
;
Qiu et al. 2022
)
. To learn
(\hat{\bm{\phi}},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi})
, we consider a binary contrastive learning objective
(
Qiu et al. 2022
)
:

\min_{(\hat{\bm{\phi}},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi})\in\mathcal{F}}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{(s,a)\sim d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\sim\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log\Big(1+\tfrac{1}{\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}\Big)}}\right]+\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\sim P_{\mathrm{neg}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log\Big(1+\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\Big)}}\right]}}\right],

(16)

where
P_{\mathrm{neg}}
is a negative sampling distribution that will be specified with justification later. We highlight that the above objective implicitly guarantees an equal number of positive and negative samples.

The following derivations follow a similar pathway as those in
Qiu et al. 2022
. For notational consistency that facilitates the application of known results, we introduce the following auxiliary notations. Define

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{f:(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\mapsto\bm{\phi}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\mu}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\mid(\bm{\phi},\bm{\mu}^{\pi})\in\mathcal{F}}}\right\}.

For clarity, we augment the sampled transitions to include a label
y
indicating whether the sample is positive (
y=1
) or negative (
y=0
). Formally, given a dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(s_{i},a_{i},s^{\prime}_{i},a^{\prime}_{i})\mid i\in[N]\}
of positive transitions, we randomly sample
N
negative transitions
(\tilde{s}_{i},\tilde{a}_{i})\sim P_{\mathrm{neg}}
(
i\in[N]
, i.i.d.), and define the augmented dataset

\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}:=\big\{(s_{i},a_{i},s^{\prime}_{i},a^{\prime}_{i},1),(s_{i},a_{i},\tilde{s}_{i},\tilde{a}_{i},0)\;\big|\;i\in[N]\big\}.

In this way, the NCE objective (
16
) can be equivalently rewritten (in MLE format) as

\max_{f\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}}\;\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime},y)\sim d^{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log\psi_{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime},y)}}\right],

(17)

where the likelihood function
\psi_{f}
is defined by

\psi_{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime},y):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{f(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}{1+f(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}}\right)^{y}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{1+f(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}}\right)^{1-y}.

We point out that
\psi_{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime},\cdot)\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
for any
(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})
, where
\mathcal{Y}:=\{0,1\}
. In fact, given
f^{\star}
that optimizes the unconstrained non-empirical version of (
17
),
\psi_{f^{\star}}
can be interpreted as the probability of obtaining label
y
given
(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})
, as summarized in the following lemma that is similar to Lemma C.1 in
Qiu et al. 2022
.

Lemma 7

(non-empirical solution to NCE)
.

The optimal solution to the unconstrained non-empirical version of (
17
),
i.e.
,
f^{\star}:=\max_{f}\mathbb{E}_{(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime},y)\sim d^{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log\psi_{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime},y)}}\right]
, is characterized by

f^{\star}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})=\frac{\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)}{P_{\mathrm{neg}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}.

Proof.

Note that the objective can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime},y)\sim d^{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\log\psi_{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime},y)}}\right]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int d^{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathrm{Pr}(y|s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\log\psi_{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime},y)}}\right)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}a\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\int d^{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\cdot\mathsf{H}\big(\mathrm{Pr}(y|s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime});\psi_{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime},y)\big)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}a\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}.

Here
\mathsf{H}(\cdot;\cdot)
is the cross entropy between distributions, which, by Gibbs’ inequality, is minimized only when

\mathrm{Pr}(y|s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})=\psi_{f^{\star}}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime},y)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{f^{\star}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}{1+f^{\star}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}}\right)^{y}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{1+f^{\star}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}}\right)^{1-y}.

(18)

On the other hand, Bayes’ rule states that (note that
\mathrm{Pr}(y|s,a)=\frac{1}{2}
,
\forall y\in\mathcal{Y}
):

\mathrm{Pr}(y=1|s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})=\frac{\mathrm{Pr}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a,y=1)\mathrm{Pr}(y=1|s,a)}{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathrm{Pr}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a,y)\mathrm{Pr}(y|s,a)}=\frac{\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)}{P_{\mathrm{neg}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})+\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)}.

(19)

Comparing (
18
) and (
19
) gives

\frac{f^{\star}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}{1+f^{\star}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}=\frac{\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)}{P_{\mathrm{neg}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})+\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)}\quad\implies\quad f^{\star}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})=\frac{\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)}{P_{\mathrm{neg}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3
.

For conciseness, here we slightly abuse the notation
\mathrm{Pr}(\cdot)
to denote the distribution (density or mass) of joint and conditional distributions involving random variables
(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime},y)\sim d^{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}
. Specifically, we write
\mathrm{Pr}(\cdots,x,\cdots)
to indicate an arbitrary value
x
taken by the random variable, and we also write
\mathrm{Pr}(\cdots,x=x_{0},\cdots)
to emphasize the specific value
x_{0}
taken by that random variable.

Lemma

7
is important in that it echoes the form of primal-dual spectral representation in (
5
). Specifically, we shall take
P_{\mathrm{neg}}(\cdot,\cdot)\equiv d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(\cdot,\cdot)
for an exact match, which is also implementable using offline data since
d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}
can be effectively approximated by sampling the trajectories. We will stick to this choice of
P_{\mathrm{neg}}
from now on.

Error Bound.

We proceed to show the representation learning error bound for the NCE method, which requires the following regularity assumption on the negative sampling distribution
P_{\mathrm{neg}}
, or equivalently, as per the choice above, the state-action occupancy measure
d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(\cdot,\cdot)
for the behavior policy
\pi_{\mathrm{b}}
.

Assumption 5

(regularity for NCE)
.

d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}_{\mathbb{P}}(s,a)\geqslant\frac{1}{C_{d}}>0
,
\forall s,a
.

We point out that
5
is a standard assumption for the negative sampling distribution
(
Qiu et al. 2022
)
, aiming at eliminating the cases where certain transitions are scarcely drawn as negative samples and thus obstruct efficient representation learning for those cases. The assumption is also slightly stronger than the effective behavior policy coverage assumption required by the OLS method (see
4
).

Theorem 8

(NCE learning error)
.

Under
1
,
2
and
3
and the additional
5
for regularity, let
(\hat{\bm{\phi}},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi})
be the solution to (
16
) with
P_{\mathrm{neg}}(\cdot,\cdot)\equiv d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(\cdot,\cdot)
, and set
\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a):=d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime})
. Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big\|\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)-\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)\big\|_{1}}}\right]\leqslant 2\sqrt{2}(1+C_{d})\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\log(|\mathcal{F}|/\delta)}{N}}.

The proof of
Theorem

8
largely follows the same pathway and techniques established in
Qiu et al. 2022
. Nevertheless, our proof is less technically involved since the offline non-episodic setting significantly weakens the correlation between samples. For the sake of completeness, we restate the proof below.

Proof.

We start by observing
\mathrm{Pr}(y,s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a):=\mathrm{Pr}(y|s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\mathrm{Pr}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)
, where
\mathrm{Pr}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)
can in turn be calculated using Bayes’ rule as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a,y=0)\mathrm{Pr}(y=0|s,a)+\mathrm{Pr}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a,y=1)\mathrm{Pr}(y=1|s,a)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)+P_{\mathrm{neg}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\big).

(20)

Here we use the fact that the data distribution
d^{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}
implicitly assigns an equal number of labels as
y=0
and
y=1
by the design of NCE objective (
16
). Since
\mathrm{Pr}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)
is a constant that is independent from
f
, we can further rewrite the NCE objective to be

\arg\max_{f\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime},y)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}\big[\log\mathrm{Pr}_{f}(y|s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\big]}}\right\}=\arg\max_{f\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime},y)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}\big[\log\mathrm{Pr}_{f}(y,s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)\big]}}\right\},

(21)

where we define the shorthand notations

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}_{f}(y|s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{f(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}{1+f(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}}\right)^{y}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{1+f(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}}\right)^{1-y},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}_{f}(y,s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)

\displaystyle:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{f(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\mathrm{Pr}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)}{1+f(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}}\right)^{y}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathrm{Pr}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)}{1+f(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}}\right)^{1-y}

for any
f\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
. Note that the right-hand side of (
21
) is in the desired MLE form, with ground-truth conditional density
\mathrm{Pr}_{f^{\star}}(y,s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)
specified by some
f^{\star}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
, thanks to the realizability assumption (
3
). Now, using the PAC bound for MLE shown in
Agarwal et al. 2020
(see
Lemma

19
), we have

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{(s_{i},a_{i})\sim d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big\|\mathrm{Pr}_{\hat{f}}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot|s_{i},a_{i})-\mathrm{Pr}_{f^{\star}}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot|s_{i},a_{i})\big\|_{1}^{2}}}\right]\leqslant 8\log(|\mathcal{F}|/\delta)

Since all
(s_{i},a_{i})
pairs are sampled i.i.d. from the same distribution
d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}
, we shall further conclude that

\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big\|\mathrm{Pr}_{\hat{f}}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot|s,a)-\mathrm{Pr}_{f^{\star}}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot|s,a)\big\|_{1}^{2}}}\right]\leqslant\frac{8\log(|\mathcal{F}|/\delta)}{N}.

(22)

We proceed to further relate (
22
) with the desired format. For this purpose, note that

\displaystyle\big\|\mathrm{Pr}_{\hat{f}}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot|s,a)-\mathrm{Pr}_{f^{\star}}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot|s,a)\big\|_{1}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\big\|\mathrm{Pr}_{\hat{f}}(y=1,\cdot,\cdot|s,a)-\mathrm{Pr}_{f^{\star}}(y=1,\cdot,\cdot|s,a)\big\|_{1}+\big\|\mathrm{Pr}_{\hat{f}}(y=0,\cdot,\cdot|s,a)-\mathrm{Pr}_{f^{\star}}(y=0,\cdot,\cdot|s,a)\big\|_{1}

(23a)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle 2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\frac{\mathrm{Pr}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)}{1+\hat{f}(s,a,\cdot,\cdot)}-\frac{\mathrm{Pr}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)}{1+f^{\star}(s,a,\cdot,\cdot)}}}\right\|_{1}

(23b)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle 2\int\frac{\big|\hat{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-f^{\star}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\big|\cdot\mathrm{Pr}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)}{\big(1+\hat{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\big)\big(1+f^{\star}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\big)}\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\mathrm{d}a^{\prime},

(23c)

where in (
23a
) we use the definition of
L^{1}
-norm; in (
23b
) we use the fact that

\mathrm{Pr}_{\hat{f}}(y,\cdot,\cdot|s,a)-\mathrm{Pr}_{f^{\star}}(y,\cdot,\cdot|s,a)=(-1)^{y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathrm{Pr}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)}{1+\hat{f}(s,a,\cdot,\cdot)}-\frac{\mathrm{Pr}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)}{1+f^{\star}(s,a,\cdot,\cdot)}}}\right).

Now, plugging
Lemma

7
and (
C.2
) into the integrand in (
23c
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\big|\hat{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-f^{\star}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\big|\cdot\mathrm{Pr}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)}{\big(1+\hat{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\big)\big(1+f^{\star}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\big)}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{\big|\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)/P_{\mathrm{neg}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-\hat{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\big|\cdot\frac{1}{2}\big(\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)+P_{\mathrm{neg}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\big)}{\big(1+\hat{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\big)\big(1+\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)/P_{\mathrm{neg}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\big)}

(24a)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{\big|\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)-P_{\mathrm{neg}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\hat{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\big|}{2\big(1+\hat{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\big)}

(24b)

\displaystyle\geqslant{}

\displaystyle\frac{\big|\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)-P_{\mathrm{neg}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\hat{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\big|}{2(1+C_{d})},

(24c)

where we use the upper bound
\hat{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})=\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)/d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\leqslant C_{d}
in (
24c
). Consequently,

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)-\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)\big\|_{1}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int\big|\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)-P_{\mathrm{neg}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\hat{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\big|\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}

(25a)

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle 2(1+C_{d})\int\frac{\big|\hat{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-f^{\star}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\big|\cdot\mathrm{Pr}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)}{\big(1+\hat{f}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\big)\big(1+f^{\star}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\big)}\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}

(25b)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle(1+C_{d})\big\|\mathrm{Pr}_{\hat{f}}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot|s,a)-\mathrm{Pr}_{f^{\star}}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot|s,a)\big\|_{1},

(25c)

where we use
\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)=P_{\mathrm{neg}}(\cdot,\cdot)\hat{f}(s,a,\cdot,\cdot)
in (
25a
), (
24
) in (
25b
), and (
23
) in (
25c
).
Finally,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big\|\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)-\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)\big\|_{1}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big\|\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)-\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)\big\|_{1}^{2}}}\right]}

(26a)

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\sqrt{(1+C_{d})^{2}\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big\|\mathrm{Pr}_{\hat{f}}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot|s,a)-\mathrm{Pr}_{f^{\star}}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot|s,a)\big\|_{1}^{2}}}\right]}

(26b)

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{2}(1+C_{d})\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\log(|\mathcal{F}|/\delta)}{N}},

(26c)

where we use Cauchy-Schwartz inequality in (
26a
), (
25
) in (
26b
), and (
22
) in (
26c
).

Appendix D
Sample Complexity Guarantee

In this appendix, we derive the sample complexity guarantee for the proposed
SpectralDICE
algorithm, assuming a known bound on the representation learning error induced by the
RepLearn
subroutine (see
3
). As discussed in the main text, the objective is to bound the estimation error
\mathcal{E}:=\hat{\rho}(\pi)-\rho(\pi)
, which can be intuitively split into the following three terms that are easier to bound:

\mathcal{E}\quad=\quad\underbrace{\hat{\rho}(\pi)-\bar{\rho}(\pi)}_{\textrm{statistical error}}\;+\;\;\underbrace{\bar{\rho}(\pi)-\rho_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}(\pi)}_{\textrm{dataset error}}\;\;+\;\underbrace{\rho_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}(\pi)-\rho_{\vphantom{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\mathbb{P}}(\pi)}_{\textrm{representation error}}.

We point out that the statistical error results from replacing the expectation with empirical estimates, the dataset error comes from the offline dataset that samples transitions from the true transition kernel
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}
instead of the learned kernel
\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}
, and the representation error accounts for the error induced by plugging in the learned representation
(\hat{\bm{\phi}},\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi})
instead of the ground truth
(\bm{\phi}^{\star},\bm{\mu}^{\pi,\star})
into the DICE estimator.

As described in the proof sketch, for the rest of this appendix, we provide an upper bound for each of these three terms, and eventually conclude with an overall sample complexity guarantee.

Representation Error.

We start by bounding the representation error term, which by intuition should be a direct consequence of the representation learning error shown in
3
.

Lemma 9
.

Conditioned on the event that the inequality in
3
holds, under
1
,
2
and
3
,

\rho_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}(\pi)-\rho_{\vphantom{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\mathbb{P}}(\pi)\leqslant\frac{\gamma C_{\infty}^{\pi}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\cdot\xi(|\mathcal{F}|,N,\delta).

Proof.

By the well-known Simulation Lemma (see
Lemma

20
), we have

\displaystyle\rho_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}(\pi)-\rho_{\vphantom{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\mathbb{P}}(\pi)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\cdot|s,a)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[V_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime})}}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{P}(\cdot|s,a)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[V_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime})}}\right]}}\right]

(27a)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbb{E}_{(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\sim\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\sim\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}\right]}}\right]

(27b)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\int d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s,a)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}a\int Q_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)-\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)}}\right)\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}

(27c)

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\int C_{\infty}^{\pi}d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}a\int\big|\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)-\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)\big|\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}

(27d)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma C_{\infty}^{\pi}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big\|\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)-\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)\big\|_{1}}}\right]

(27e)

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma C_{\infty}^{\pi}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\cdot\xi(|\mathcal{F}|,N,\delta),

(27f)

where in (
27a
) we use the Simulation Lemma; in (
27b
) we use the relationship between value functions; in (
27d
) we plug in
d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s,a)\leqslant C_{\infty}^{\pi}d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)
(
2
) and the fact that
Q_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot)\leqslant\frac{1}{1-\gamma}
; in (
27f
) we use
3
.
∎

Dataset Error.

The dataset error can be accounted for by a bounded difference in the objective function, which turns out to be another consequence of the representation learning error. For this purpose, we first show the following technical lemma that formalizes the above intuition.

Lemma 10
.

\min\limits_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\max\limits_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}F_{1}(\bm{x},\bm{y})-\min\limits_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\max\limits_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}F_{2}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\leqslant\max\limits_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X},\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\big|F_{1}(\bm{x},\bm{y})-F_{2}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\big|.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon:=\max\limits_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}\big|F_{1}(\bm{x},\bm{y})-F_{2}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\big|
. Then we have

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}}\max_{\bm{y}}F_{1}(\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle\leqslant\min_{\bm{x}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\max_{\bm{y}}F_{2}(\bm{x},\bm{y})+\max_{\bm{y}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{F_{1}(\bm{x},\bm{y})-F_{2}(\bm{x},\bm{y})}}\right\}}}\right\}

(28a)

\displaystyle\leqslant\min_{\bm{x}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\max_{\bm{y}}F_{2}(\bm{x},\bm{y})+\varepsilon}}\right\}

(28b)

\displaystyle=\min_{\bm{x}}\max_{\bm{y}}F_{2}(\bm{x},\bm{y})+\varepsilon,

(28c)

where in (
28a
) we use the fact that
\max_{\bm{y}}\{f(\bm{y})+g(\bm{y})\}\leqslant\max_{\bm{y}}f(\bm{y})+\max_{\bm{y}}g(\bm{y})
.
∎

Now we are ready to show the following lemma regarding dataset error.

Lemma 11
.

Conditioned on the event that the inequality in
3
holds, under
1
,
2
and
3
,

\bar{\rho}(\pi)-\rho_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}(\pi)\leqslant\frac{C_{\infty}^{\pi}}{1-\gamma}\cdot\xi(|\mathcal{F}|,N,\delta).

Proof.

For the sake of clarity, denote the optimization objectives of
\bar{\rho}(\pi)
and
\rho_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}(\pi)
as follows:

\bar{\rho}(\pi)=\min_{\bm{\theta}_{Q}}\max_{\bm{\omega}_{d}}\bar{F}(\bm{\theta}_{Q},\bm{\omega}_{d}),\qquad\rho_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}(\pi)=\min_{\bm{\theta}_{Q}}\max_{\bm{\omega}_{d}}\hat{F}(\bm{\theta}_{Q},\bm{\omega}_{d}).

Then we can show that

\displaystyle|\bar{\rho}(\pi)-\rho_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}(\pi)|

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\min_{\bm{\theta}_{Q}}\max_{\bm{\omega}_{d}}\bar{F}(\bm{\theta}_{Q},\bm{\omega}_{d})-\min_{\bm{\theta}_{Q}}\max_{\bm{\omega}_{d}}\hat{F}(\bm{\theta}_{Q},\bm{\omega}_{d})}}\right|\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\bar{F}(\bm{\theta}_{Q},\bm{\omega}_{d})-\hat{F}(\bm{\theta}_{Q},\bm{\omega}_{d})}}\right|

(29a)

\displaystyle=\Biggl|\int d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)-\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)}}\right)\big(\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\omega}_{d}\big)\cdot{}

\displaystyle\hskip 90.00014pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r(s,a)+\gamma\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{Q}-\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{Q}}}\right)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}a\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}\Biggr|

(29b)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(s,a)\big|\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)-\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)\big|\cdot\big|\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\omega}_{d}\big|\cdot

\displaystyle\hskip 90.00014pt\big|r(s,a)+\gamma\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{Q}-\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{Q}\big|\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}a\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}

(29c)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big\|\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)-\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)\big\|_{1}\cdot C_{\infty}^{\pi}\cdot\tfrac{1}{1-\gamma}}}\right]

(29d)

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{\infty}^{\pi}}{1-\gamma}\cdot\xi(|\mathcal{F}|,N,\delta),

(29e)

where in (
29a
) we use
Lemma

10
; in (
29c
) we use the integral triangle inequality; in (
29d
) we plug in
\big|\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\omega}_{d}\big|\leqslant C_{\infty}^{\pi}
and
\big|r(s,a)+\gamma\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{Q}-\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{Q}\big|\leqslant\frac{1}{1-\gamma}
(see
Remark

2
); in (

) we use
3
.

Statistical Error.

Finally, the statistical error is caused by replacing the expectations with their empirical estimations, which can be bounded by Hoeffding’s concentration inequality (see

).

Lemma 12
.

Under
1

2
and
3
, with probability at least
1-\delta

\hat{\rho}(\pi)-\bar{\rho}(\pi)\leqslant\frac{C_{\infty}^{\pi}}{1-\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/2\delta)}{2N}}.

Proof.

For clarity, label the samples in
\mathcal{D}
as
\mathcal{D}=\{(s_{i},a_{i},s^{\prime}_{i},a^{\prime}_{i})\mid i\in[N]\}
, and define

F(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime}):=\big(\hat{\bm{\mu}}^{\pi}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\omega}_{d}\big)\big(r(s,a)+\gamma\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{Q}-\hat{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{Q}\big).

Note that

\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}F(s_{i},a_{i},s^{\prime}_{i},a^{\prime}_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{l}s\sim d^{\pi_{\mathrm{b}}}(\cdot),~a\sim\pi_{\mathrm{b}}(a|s),\\

E.1
\bm{f}
-Divergence

Definition 1

(
f
-divergence)
.

Let
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
be two probabilities distribution over a sample space
\mathcal{X}
, such that
\mathbb{P}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathbb{Q}
. Given a convex function
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f(1)=0
and
f(0):=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}f(t)
. Then the
f
-divergence
of
\mathbb{P}
with respect to
\mathbb{Q}
is defined as

\mathsf{D}_{f}(\mathbb{P}\|\mathbb{Q}):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}.

The following
variational representation
of
f
-divergences is well-known in literature.

Lemma 15

(variational representation using Fenchel conjugate)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the class of measurable real valued functions on
\mathcal{X}
that is absolutely integratable with respect to
\mathbb{Q}
. Then

\mathsf{D}_{f}(\mathbb{P}\|\mathbb{Q})=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{F}}\Big\{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[g(x)}}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathbb{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f_{*}(g(x))}}\right]\Big\},

where
f_{*}
is the Fenchel conjugate of
f
. Further, if
f
is differentiable, then the optimal dual variable is given by

g^{\star}(x)=f^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}}}\right)\quad\implies\quad\mathsf{D}_{f}(\mathbb{P}\|\mathbb{Q})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}}}\right)}}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathbb{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f_{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}}}\right)}}\right)}}\right]

Proof.

See Theorem 4.4 in
Broniatowski and Keziou 2006
.
∎

E.2
Concentration Inequalities

Lemma 16

(Hoeffding’s inequality,
Hoeffding 1994
)
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{N}
be i.i.d. random variables with mean
\mu
and taking values in
[a,b]
almost surely. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
we have

\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}-\mu}}\right|>\varepsilon}}\right]\leqslant 2\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{2N\varepsilon^{2}}{(b-a)^{2}}}}\right).

In other words, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}-\mu}}\right|\leqslant(b-a)\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/2\delta)}{2N}}.

Lemma 17

(Bernstein’s inequality,
Bernstein 1924
)
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{N}
be i.i.d. random variables with mean
\mu
, variance
\sigma^{2}
, and bounded range
|X_{i}-\mu|\leqslant B
almost surely. Then with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\pm\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}-\mu}}\right)\leqslant\sigma\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{N}}+\frac{B\log(1/\delta)}{3N}.

E.3
Statistical Learning: PAC Bounds

In this section, we present the standard PAC bounds for OLS and MLE. Although these are both classic results, we fail to trace back to the original literature of the former, and thus provide a short proof here for completeness.

Lemma 18

(PAC bound for OLS, fast rate)
.

Consider a regression problem over a finite family
\mathcal{F}=\{f:\mathcal{X}\to[a,b]\}
of bounded functions with data distribution
(X,Y)\sim\mathcal{M}
, where the objective is to solve for

\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{L}(f),\quad\textrm{where}~\mathcal{L}(f):=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim\mathcal{M}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(f(X)-Y)^{2}}}\right].

Suppose the regression function
f^{\star}(x):=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Y\mid X=x}}\right]\in\mathcal{F}
(realizability), and we have access to i.i.d. sample
(x_{i},y_{i})\sim\mathcal{M}
,
\forall i\in[N]
. Let the Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) estimator be

\hat{f}:=\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(f),\quad\textrm{where}~\hat{\mathcal{L}}(f):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(f(x_{i})-y_{i})^{2}.

Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the ERM estimator induces a regret that is at most

\mathcal{L}(\hat{f})\leqslant\mathcal{L}(f^{\star})+C_{\mathrm{reg}}\frac{\log(|\mathcal{F}|/\delta)}{N}.

Suppose further that the ground truth is deterministic such that
y=f^{\star}(x)
for some
f^{\star}\in\mathcal{F}
, in which case we have

\mathcal{L}(\hat{f})\leqslant C_{\mathrm{reg}}\frac{\log(|\mathcal{F}|/\delta)}{N}.

Here
C_{\mathrm{reg}}=8(b-a)^{2}+\frac{4}{3}(b-a)
is a universal constant depending only on the range
[a,b]
.

Proof.

Define a random variable
Z_{i}:=(f(X_{i})-Y_{i})^{2}-(f^{\star}(X_{i})-Y_{i})^{2}
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(X_{i},Y_{i})\sim\mathcal{M}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z_{i}(f)}}\right]={}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(X_{i},Y_{i})\sim\mathcal{M}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(f(X_{i})-Y_{i})^{2}-(f^{\star}(X_{i})-Y_{i})^{2}}}\right]

(30a)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(X_{i},Y_{i})\sim\mathcal{M}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big((f(X_{i})-f^{\star}(X_{i}))+(f^{\star}(X_{i})-Y_{i})\big)^{2}-(f^{\star}(X_{i})-Y_{i})^{2}}}\right]

(30b)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(X_{i},Y_{i})\sim\mathcal{M}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(f(X_{i})-f^{\star}(X_{i}))^{2}}}\right]+2\mathbb{E}_{(X_{i},Y_{i})\sim\mathcal{M}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(f(X_{i})-f^{\star}(X_{i}))(f^{\star}(X_{i})-Y_{i})}}\right]

(30c)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(X_{i},Y_{i})\sim\mathcal{M}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(f(X_{i})-f^{\star}(X_{i}))^{2}}}\right]=:\mathcal{E}(f),

(30d)

where in (
30c
) we use the following fact
\mathbb{E}_{(X_{i},Y_{i})\sim\mathcal{M}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(f(X_{i})-f^{\star}(X_{i}))(f^{\star}(X_{i})-Y_{i})}}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{X_{i}}[(f(X_{i})-f^{\star}(X_{i}))\cdot\mathbb{E}_{Y_{i}\sim\mathcal{M}(\cdot\mid X_{i})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f^{\star}(X_{i})-Y_{i}}}\right]]=0
that directly follows from the definition of
f^{\star}
. Similarly, for any
t\in[T]
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{(X_{i},Y_{i})\sim\mathcal{M}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z_{i}(f)}}\right]={}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(X_{i},Y_{i})\sim\mathcal{M}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z_{i}(f)^{2}}}\right]-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}_{(X_{i},Y_{i})\sim\mathcal{M}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z_{i}(f)}}\right]}}\right)^{2}

(31a)

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(X_{i},Y_{i})\sim\mathcal{M}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((f(X_{i})-Y_{i})^{2}-(f^{\star}(X_{i})-Y_{i})^{2}}}\right)^{2}}}\right]

(31b)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(X_{i},Y_{i})\sim\mathcal{M}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(f(X_{i})-f^{\star}(X_{i}))^{2}(f(X_{i})+f^{\star}(X_{i})-2Y_{i})^{2}}}\right]

(31c)

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle 4(b-a)^{2}\mathbb{E}_{(X_{i},Y_{i})\sim\mathcal{M}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(f(X_{i})-f^{\star}(X_{i}))^{2}}}\right]=4(b-a)^{2}\mathcal{E}(f).

(31d)

where in (
31a
) we simply drop the second term, and in (
31c
) we use the fact
f(X_{i})+f^{\star}(X_{i})-2Y_{i}\in[-2(b-a),2(b-a)]
as
f(X_{i}),f^{\star}(X_{i}),Y_{i}\in[a,b]
. Further, for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
y\in\mathcal{Y}
and
f\in\mathcal{F}
, we have
f(x)-y\in[-(b-a),b-a]
, implying
Z_{i}(f)\in[-(b-a),b-a]
and
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z_{i}(f)}}\right]\in[-(b-a),(b-a)]
. Therefore,
|Z_{i}(f)-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z_{i}(f)}}\right]|\leqslant 2(b-a)
. Then by Bernstein’s inequality (
Lemma

17
), we conclude that, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z_{i}(f)}}\right]-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}(f)\leqslant\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z_{i}(f)}}\right]}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{N}}+\frac{2(b-a)\log(1/\delta)}{3N}.

(32)

To proceed, plug (
30
) and (
31
) into (
32
), and we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(f)-(\hat{\mathcal{L}}(f)-\hat{\mathcal{L}}(f^{\star}))

\displaystyle\leqslant 2(b-a)\sqrt{\mathcal{E}(f)}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{N}}+\frac{2(b-a)\log(1/\delta)}{3N}

(33a)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}(f)+\frac{4(b-a)^{2}\log(1/\delta)}{N}}}\right)+\frac{2(b-a)\log(1/\delta)}{3N},

(33b)

where in (
33a
) we apply the AM-GM inequality. Finally, we rearrange the terms to obtain

\mathcal{E}(f)\leqslant 2(\hat{\mathcal{L}}(f)-\hat{\mathcal{L}}(f^{\star}))+\frac{C_{\mathrm{reg}}\log(1/\delta)}{N}

(34)

for any fixed
f\in\mathcal{F}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
. Finally, we take the union bound with respect to all
f\in\mathcal{F}
, such that with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\mathcal{E}(f)\leqslant 2(\hat{\mathcal{L}}(f)-\hat{\mathcal{L}}(f^{\star}))+\frac{C_{\mathrm{reg}}\log(|\mathcal{F}|/\delta)}{N},~\forall f\in\mathcal{F}.

(35)

In particular, (
35
) also applies to the ERM estimator
\hat{f}
, which gives

\mathcal{E}(\hat{f})\leqslant 2(\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\hat{f})-\hat{\mathcal{L}}(f^{\star}))+\frac{C_{\mathrm{reg}}\log(|\mathcal{F}|/\delta)}{N}\leqslant\frac{C_{\mathrm{reg}}\log(|\mathcal{F}|/\delta)}{N}.

(36)

Here we use the inequality
\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\hat{f})\leqslant\hat{\mathcal{L}}(f^{\star})
, as
\hat{f}
minimizes
\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\cdot)
within
\mathcal{F}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 19

(PAC bound for MLE,
Agarwal et al. 2020
)
.

Consider a conditional probability estimation problem over a finite family
\mathcal{F}=\{f:(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})\to\mathbb{R}\}
, where the objective is to estimate
f^{\star}(x,y):=\mathbb{P}(y|x)
. Suppose the ground truth
f^{\star}\in\mathcal{F}
(realizability), and we have access to (potentially correlated) samples
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\mid i\in[N]\}
such that
x_{i}\sim\mathcal{D}_{i}
(
\mathcal{D}_{i}
is allowed to depend on
(x_{1:i-1},y_{1:i-1})
, forming a martigale process) and
y_{i}\sim\mathbb{P}(\cdot|x_{i})
. Let the Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE) be

\hat{f}:=\arg\max_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log f(x_{i},y_{i}).

Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the error of the MLE estimator is bounded as follows:

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big\|\hat{f}(x,\cdot)-f^{\star}(x,\cdot)\big\|_{1}^{2}}}\right]\leqslant 8\log(|\mathcal{F}|/\delta).

Specifically, when
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\mid i\in[N]\}
are i.i.d. samples from a dataset
\mathcal{D}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big\|\hat{f}(x,\cdot)-f^{\star}(x,\cdot)\big\|_{1}^{2}}}\right]\leqslant\frac{8\log(|\mathcal{F}|/\delta)}{N}.

E.4
Simulation Lemma in MDPs

The following Simulation Lemma is a simplified version of Lemma 21 in
Uehara et al. 2021
.

Lemma 20

(Simulation Lemma)
.

Given two MDPs
(\mathbb{P},r)
and
(\hat{\mathbb{P}},r)
, for any policy
\pi\in\varPi
, we have

\rho_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}(\pi)-\rho_{\vphantom{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\mathbb{P}}(\pi)=\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\cdot|s,a)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[V_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime})}}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{P}(\cdot|s,a)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[V_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime})}}\right]}}\right].

Proof.

Note that, for any uniformly bounded function
f:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu_{0},a\sim\pi(\cdot|s)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f(s,a)}}\right]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{\pi,\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma^{t}f(s_{t},a_{t})-\gamma^{t+1}f(s_{t+1},a_{t+1})}}\right)\;\middle|\;s_{0}\sim\mu_{0},a_{0}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{0})}}\right]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{s,a}f(s,a)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\pi,\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma^{t}\mathbbm{1}\{s_{t}=s,a_{t}=a\}-\gamma^{t+1}\mathbbm{1}\{s_{t+1}=s,a_{t+1}=a\}}}\right)\;\middle|\;s_{0}\sim\mu_{0},a_{0}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{0})}}\right]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{s,a}f(s,a)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(s,a)-\gamma\sum_{\tilde{s},\tilde{a}}d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}(\tilde{s},\tilde{a})\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(s,a|\tilde{s},\tilde{a})}}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f(s,a)-\gamma\mathbb{E}_{(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\sim\mathbb{P}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}}\right]}}\right].

\rho_{\vphantom{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\mathbb{P}}(\pi)=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim d_{\mathbb{P}}^{\pi}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[r(s,a)}}\right]

, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}(\pi)-\rho_{\vphantom{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\mathbb{P}}(\pi)
