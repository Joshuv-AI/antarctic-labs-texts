Dyadically resolving trinomials for fast modular arithmetic
Robert Dougherty-Bliss, Mits Kobayashi, Natalya Ter-Saakov, Eugene Zima

Abstract
Residue number systems based on pairwise relatively prime moduli are a powerful tool for accelerating integer computations via the Chinese Remainder Theorem. We study a structured family of moduli of the form 2 n − 2 k + 1 2^{n}-2^{k}+1 , originally proposed for their efficient arithmetic and bit-level properties. These trinomial moduli support fast modular operations and exhibit scalable modular inverses. We investigate the problem of constructing large sets of pairwise relatively prime trinomial moduli of fixed bit length. By analyzing the corresponding trinomials x n − x k + 1 x^{n}-x^{k}+1 , we establish a sufficient condition for coprimality based on polynomial resultants. This leads to a graph-theoretic model where maximal sets correspond to cliques in a compatibility graph, and we use maximum clique-finding algorithms to construct large examples in practice. Based on the theory of graph colorings, polynomial resultants, and properties of cyclotomic polynomials, we also prove upper bounds on the size of such sets as a function of n n .

Introduction

A popular technique to speed up computations with integer arithmetic is to
reduce the input modulo several relatively prime numbers, compute with the
residues, then reconstruct the result with the Chinese remainder theorem

[
6
,
5
,
9
]
. In
[
4
]
,
it was proposed to use “trinomial” moduli of the form

2^{n}-2^{k}+1,\quad 0<k<n.

(1)

These numbers have nice binary representations which make reduction into a
modular system fast. Sometimes, they exhibit “scalable inverses”. By that, we
mean that replacing
2
with
2^{c}
does not change the sparsity or bit-pattern
of the modular inverses. For example, the moduli
2^{20}-2^{12}+1
and

2^{20}-2^{4}+1
satisfy the following property: for any integer
c\geq 1
,

\displaystyle((2^{c})^{20}-(2^{c})^{12}+1)^{-1}

\displaystyle\equiv(2^{c})^{8}+1

\displaystyle\pmod{(2^{c})^{20}-(2^{c})^{4}+1}

\displaystyle((2^{c})^{20}-(2^{c})^{4}+1)^{-1}

\displaystyle\equiv(2^{c})^{20}-(2^{c})^{12}-(2^{c})^{8}+1

\displaystyle\pmod{(2^{c})^{20}-(2^{c})^{12}+1}.

This means that the reconstruction step of the Chinese Remainder Theorem can be
carried out quickly, even with arbitrarily large moduli. Using ad-hoc methods,
the authors of
[
4
]
discovered a set of five pairwise relatively prime
moduli of this form and used them in upper-layer on top of

[
5
]
to show improvement in a standard integer
matrix multiplication benchmark.

Their work left open the following questions:

1.

When are two trinomial moduli relatively prime with scalable inverses?

2.

Are there arbitrarily large sets of trinomial moduli with the same
bit length which are pairwise relatively prime with scalable inverses?

3.

For a fixed bit length, how can we efficiently find these sets?

Our aim is to answer some of these questions by examining the pure polynomial
trinomials

x^{n}-x^{k}+1,\quad 0<k<n.

(2)

It might seem reasonable to establish the relative primality of trinomial pairs
of the form (
2
), then use this to deduce the relative primality of
moduli of the form (
1
). Unfortunately, this does not work. Almost
all pairs of trinomials of the form (
2
) are relatively prime over
the rationals, yet only a small proportion of moduli of the form (
1
)
are relatively prime integers. In other words, the substitution
x=2
in
(
2
) does not preserve relative primality.

Our main results are as follows:

1.

A simple condition based on the resultant of two trinomials which
ensures the relative primality of moduli of the form and the scalability of
their modular inverses.

2.

A proof that arbitrarily large sets of relatively prime moduli with scalable inverses exist.

3.

An upper bound on the number of such moduli for a fixed bit length.

Definition 1
.

The

and

\mathbb{Q}

\res(f,g)=\lc(f)^{\deg g}\lc(g)^{\deg f}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq\deg f\\
1\leq j\leq\deg g\end{subarray}}(\alpha_{i}-\beta_{j}),

the following properties:

\displaystyle\res(f,g)

\displaystyle=\lc(f)^{\deg g}\prod_{i=1}^{\deg f}g(\alpha_{i})

\displaystyle\res(f,g)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\deg f\deg g}\res(g,f)

\displaystyle\res(fg,p)

\displaystyle=\res(f,p)\res(g,p),\quad p\in\mathbb{Q}[x].

If
g=fq+r

\deg r<\deg f

\res(f,g)=\lc(f)^{\deg g-\deg r}\res(f,r).

Definition 2
.

A pair of polynomials with integer coefficients
dyadically
resolve
if their resultant is a signed power of 2, meaning it is of the form
\pm 2^{k}
for
some nonnegative integer
k
.

This definition is motivated by
[
4
]
, where it was desired that numbers
of the form
2^{cn}-2^{ck}+1
would be relatively prime with “stable”
inverses for sufficiently large integers
c
. By that, we mean that there exists
a polynomial
f(x)
such that

(2^{cn}-2^{ck}+1)^{-1}\equiv f(2^{c})\pmod{2^{cn}-2^{cj}+1}

for all sufficiently large values of
c
. It turns out that this is equivalent
to stating that the polynomials
x^{n}-x^{k}+1
and
x^{n}-x^{j}+1
dyadically
resolve.

The basic idea is that the inverse polynomial
f
should be a Bézout cofactor
of
x^{n}-x^{k}+1
and
x^{n}-x^{j}+1
. Assuming that the trinomials are
relatively prime, we can write

(x^{n}-x^{k}+1)f(x)+(x^{n}-x^{j}+1)g(x)=1

for some polynomials
f
and
g
over

\mathbb{Q}
. The resultant of two polynomials is the determinant of their
Sylvester matrix, so if the trinomials dyadically resolve then
f
and
g
can
be constructed using only dyadic coefficients. Then, plugging in
x=2^{c}
for
large enough

produces a statement about modular inverses. Getting the
details exactly right and proving the converse—“scalable inverses” imply
dyadic resolvability—is more complicated and handled in
Section
6
.

.

For a fixed integer

Computational results

The main computational problem is to determine the largest possible set of
pairwise dyadically resolving trinomials of degree
n
, which is equivalent to
finding the the maximum cliques of
T(n)
. The graph theoretic formulation
gives us a computational foothold, because finding the maximum cliques in a
graph is known to take less time than the brute force approach. The
Bronn–Kerbosch algorithm runs in time
O(3^{n/3})

[
3
]
and
Robson’s algorithm runs in time
O(2^{n/4})

[
7
]
.

Clique numbers.

We have constructed

for

and
determined all of their maximum cliques. These results are available at

https://github.com/rdbliss/trinomials
. The largest known clique in

[
4
]
had size five, but we now know several cliques of size ten. These
cliques were used in two-layer modular arithmetic package to further improve
performance reported in
[
4
]
. We do not know the first

for which a
clique of size 11 occurs in
T(n)
.

, but Figure

4
Properties of the trinomial graph

The goal of this section is to prove that there are cliques of arbitrary sizes
in
T(n)
as
n\to\infty
. This implies that the optimization problem of
finding the
best
cliques is well-founded. We will also prove a number of
interesting properties unrelated to our main problem.

Lemma 4.1
.

x^{n}-x^{k}+1
and
x^{n}-x^{j}+1
dyadically resolve if
k-j
divides
k
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, suppose that
k>j
. Then, the resultant of
two such trinomials can be computed as follows:

\displaystyle\res(x^{n}-x^{k}+1,x^{n}-x^{j}+1)

\displaystyle=\pm\res(x^{n}-x^{k}+1,x^{k}-x^{j})

\displaystyle=\pm\res(x^{n}-x^{k}+1,x^{j})\res(x^{n}-x^{k}+1,x^{k-j}-1).

The first factor is
\pm 1
, because

\res(x^{n}-x^{k}+1,x^{j})=\pm\prod_{w^{j}=0}(w^{n}-w^{k}+1)=\pm 1.

For the second factor, the assumption that
k-j
divides
k
implies

\res(x^{n}-x^{k}+1,x^{k-j}-1)=\pm\prod_{w^{k-j}=1}(w^{n}-w^{k}+1)=\pm\prod_{w^{k-j}=1}w^{n}.

This last product is

\prod_{w^{k-j}=1}w^{n}=\left(\prod_{w^{k-j}=1}w=\right)^{n}=\pm 1,

where we have used the fact that the product of all roots of unity of a
given order is
\pm 1
.
∎

Theorem 4.1
.

Sufficiently large trinomial graphs contain cliques of arbitrary size.
That is,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\omega(T(n))=\infty.

Proof.

Suppose that we have constructed a sequence of positive integers
k_{1}<k_{2}<\cdots<k_{m}
such that

k_{i}-k_{j}\quad\text{divides}\quad k_{i},\quad i\neq j.

(3)

Such a sequence would, by Lemma
4.1
, produce a clique in

T(n)
for any
n>k_{m}
. Set
k=\operatorname{lcm}(k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{m})
, and then define the new sequence

k_{i}^{\prime}=\frac{k}{k_{i}}(k_{i}+1).

We claim that
k<k_{1}^{\prime}<k_{2}^{\prime}<\cdots<k_{m}^{\prime}
is a sequence of values
that satisfies the divisibility condition (
3
), but is one term
longer.

To see why this is true, note that

\frac{k_{i}^{\prime}}{k_{i}^{\prime}-k_{j}^{\prime}}=\frac{(k_{i}+1)k_{j}}{k_{j}-k_{i}}.

By assumption,
k_{j}-k_{i}
divides
k_{j}
, so this quotient is an integer.
It remains to check that
k_{i}^{\prime}-k
divides both
k_{i}^{\prime}
and
k
for every

i
, but this is simple given the following equations:

\displaystyle\frac{k_{i}^{\prime}}{k_{i}^{\prime}-k}

\displaystyle=k_{i}+1

\displaystyle\frac{k}{k_{i}^{\prime}-k}

\displaystyle=k_{i}.

This inductive process constructs such sequences of arbitrary length, which
shows that every
T(n)
for
n
large enough will contain arbitrarily large
cliques.
∎

To make the preceding theorem concrete, we can begin the inductive process with

k_{1}=1
. That produces the following cliques:

\displaystyle\{1\}

\displaystyle\{1,2\}

\displaystyle\{2,3,4\}

\displaystyle\{12,15,16,18\}

\displaystyle\{720,760,765,768,780\}

\displaystyle\{48372480,48434496,48435465,48435712,48436128,48439664\}.

These sequences are theoretically interesting but grow too quickly to be useful
in practice. (It is even nontrivial to verify
a posteriori
that the last
set is a clique in
T(48439665)
.) Nonetheless, they show that there
are

large cliques in
T(n)
.

Figure
1
suggests that
T(n)
contains a cardioid, a circle, and
has some reflectional symmetry. The following proposition shows that these are

Proposition 4.1
.

T(n)
contains a cardioid and (almost) a circle. That is, it contains the
following edges:

•

\{k,2k\}
and
\{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+k+n\bmod 2,2k+n\bmod 2\}
for
1\leq k\leq n/2
.

•

\{k,k+1\}
for
1\leq k<n-1

Further,
T(n)
contains
\{i,j\}
if and only if it contains
\{n-i,n-j\}
.

Proof.

The first two families follow from simple resultant computations. For
example:

\displaystyle\res(x^{n}-x^{k}+1,x^{n}-x^{2k}+1)

\displaystyle=\pm\res(x^{n}-x^{k}+1,x^{k}-1)

\displaystyle=\pm\res(x^{n},x^{k}-1)

\displaystyle=\pm 1.

The symmetry follows from noting that the resultant changes only in sign when the
coefficients of a polynomial are reversed.
∎

Theorem 5.1
.

The largest clique in
T(n)
has size no more than
2\lfloor\log_{2}n\rfloor-\nu_{2}(n)
.

We will prove this theorem with a graph coloring argument. An
independent

in a graph is a collection of vertices which have no edges between them. A

coloring
of a graph is a partition of its vertices into independent sets,
called “colors.” In
T(n)
, an independent set is a collection of trinomials
which are pairwise non-dyadically resolving. If a graph can be colored with
k

k

The following lemma shows that independent sets in
T(n)
can sometimes be
obtained from congruence classes.

Proposition 5.1
.

Fix positive integers
i\leq d<n
. If
x^{n}-x^{i}+1
and
x^{d}-1

dyadically resolve, then
T(n)
contains the path
[i,i+d,i+2d,\dots]
. Otherwise,
\{i,i+d,i+2d,\dots\}
is an independent set.

Proof.

If
k\equiv j\pmod{d}
, then a few Euclidean divisions show that

\res(x^{n}-x^{k}+1,x^{n}-x^{j}+1)=\pm\res(x^{n}-x^{k}+1,x^{k-j}-1).

Because
d
divides
k-j
, this resultant is divisible by

\res(x^{n}-x^{k}+1,x^{d}-1).

This last resultant only depends on the value of
k\bmod d
, so we may
replace
k
with any member of the congruence class. If the congruence
class
k\bmod d
is not a path, then this final resultant is not a signed
power of
2
for some member of the class, from which it follows that the
edge
\{k,j\}
does not exist in
T(n)
.
∎

Lemma 5.1
.

If either
x^{n}+2
or
2x^{n}+1
dyadically resolve with
x^{2^{j}}+1
,
then
\nu_{2}(n)=j
.

Proof.

Richard Swan found a remarkable formula for the resultant of two binomials

[
8
]
. His formula implies

\res(x^{n}+2,x^{2^{j}}+1)=\pm((-2)^{2^{j}/g}-(-1)^{n/g})^{g}

where
g=\gcd(2^{j},n)
. This is a power of 2 if and only if

|(-2)^{2^{j}/g}-(-1)^{n/g}|=1,

which is equivalent to
2^{j}/g=1
and
\nu_{2}(n/g)=0
. In other words,
the resultant is a power of 2 if and only if
\nu_{2}(n)=j
. For the other
case, note that
2x^{n}+1
is the reciprocal polynomial of
x^{n}+2
, so the
two resultants are the same up to a sign change.
∎

Proposition 5.2
.

If
n
is odd, then all congruence classes modulo

2^{j}
are independent sets in

except possibly
0\bmod 2^{j}
and
n\bmod 2^{j}
.

Proof.

We will show, by induction on

, that
x^{n}-x^{k}+1
and
x^{2^{j}}-1

dyadically resolve only if
k\equiv 0\pmod{2^{j}}
or
k\equiv n\pmod{2^{j}}
. For the base case

, we have

\res(x^{n}-x^{k}+1,x^{2}-1)=(-1)^{n}-(-1)^{k}+1.

A simple check shows that this is a signed power of 2 exactly when
k\equiv n\pmod{2}
or
k\equiv 0\pmod{2}
.

For the inductive step, fix
j\geq 2
such that
\res(x^{n}-x^{k}+1,x^{2^{j}}-1)
is a signed power of 2. Then
\res(x^{n}-x^{k}+1,x^{2^{j-1}}-1)
is also a signed power of 2—because the latter divides the former—so the induction hypothesis applies. It follows that
k
is
0
or
n

, and so there are four possible values for

If

, then

\res(x^{n}-x^{k}+1,\Phi_{2^{j}}(x))=\res(x^{n}+2,x^{2^{j-1}}+1)

because

for any primitive
2^{j}
-th root of unity

. If this is
a signed power of 2, then the previous lemma implies
\nu_{2}(n)=j-1\geq 1
. But we know that
n
is odd, so this cannot happen. The case
k\equiv n+2^{j-1}\pmod{2^{j}}
is similarly dispatched.
∎

Proposition 5.3
.

If
n=2^{\nu_{2}(n)}n_{1}
, then the following congruences classes are

:

\displaystyle\{k\equiv 1\pmod{2}\}

\displaystyle\{k\equiv 2\pmod{4}\}

\displaystyle\{k\equiv 4\pmod{8}\}

\displaystyle\cdots

\displaystyle\{k\equiv 2^{\nu_{2}(n)-1}\pmod{2^{\nu_{2}(n)}}\}.

The vertices not included in these sets are
\{2^{\nu_{2}(n)}j\mid 0<j<n_{1}\}

.

If

where

\res(x^{n}-x^{k}+1,x^{n}-x^{j}+1)=\res(x^{n}-x^{k}+1,x^{k-j}-1)

\res(x^{n}-x^{k}+1,\Phi_{2^{i}}(x)).

If

Proof of Theorem

We will now prove
Theorem

using the preceding results.

Proof.

Write

. Lemma

states that we can

independent sets, discard them, and be left with an

. If

is a power of 2, then

and
every vertex is covered by these independent sets, meaning that
T(n)
has
been colored with
\nu_{2}(n)=2\lfloor\log_{2}n\rfloor-\nu_{2}(n)

If
n
is not a power of 2, then
n_{1}>1
is odd, and

states that the congruence classes modulo

are

except for possibly

and

. Thus, there are at least two independent sets among the congruence

. Choose any two, color them different colors, then discard

, they split into four distinct classes.

5.2
implies again that two of these will be

T(n)
. This process places
every vertex of
T(n)
into an independent set, begins at

,
terminates at

, and produced two independent

T(n)
has been colored with

2(\lceil\log_{2}n_{1}\rceil-1)+\nu_{2}(n)=2\lfloor\log_{2}n\rfloor-\nu_{2}(n)

colors.
∎

6
Connection to relative primality and the reduced resultant

Our goal in this section is to give the technical argument which links the
moduli of
[
4
]
to our trinomials. This requires two steps: an argument
relating scalability to the Bézout equation, and then an argument relating the
Bézout equation to resultants. The arguments of this section rely on a
lesser-known quantity called the
reduced resultant
. We will begin by
gathering some basic definitions and facts about the reduced resultant.

6.1
Reduced resultants

For the remainder of this section, we will assume
f,g\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
are
monic and have unique Bézout cofactors
a,b\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
,
meaning that

a(x)f(x)+b(x)g(x)=1

and
\deg a<\deg g
,
\deg b<\deg f
. When we say “resultant,” we mean
“resultant over
\mathbb{Q}
” unless otherwise specified.

Definition 6
.

Fix integer coefficient polynomials
f(x)
and
g(x)
. The smallest positive
integer obtainable as a
\mathbb{Z}[x]
-linear combination of
f(x)
and

g(x)
is their
reduced resultant
.

The set of all integers obtainable as a
\mathbb{Z}[x]
-linear combination of

f
and
g
is an ideal in

. If

then this ideal is nontrivial, contains the resultant, and is generated by the
reduced resultant. In general, the reduced resultant does not equal the
resultant.

Example

The resultant of

Proposition 6.1
.

The reduced resultant of

and

is the least common multiple of the
reduced denominators of the coefficients of the Bézout cofactors

and

Proof.

Let

(4)

for some

with no restrictions on

and

by

and

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=gq_{1}+r_{1}

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=fq_{2}+r_{2},

where
\deg r_{1}<g

\deg r_{2}<f

r_{1}f+r_{2}g+fg(q_{1}+q_{2})=m.

Because
\deg fg>\deg(r_{1}f+r_{2}g)
and the left-hand side equals a
constant, we must have

, so

It follows that every integer obtainable as a

-linear

can be obtained by one with

and
\deg B<\deg f

∎

The most useful property of the reduced resultant, for our purposes, is the
following proposition.

Proposition 6.2
.

If
f(x)
and

are monic, then their reduced resultant and resultant
have the same prime divisors.

Proof.

Because the reduced resultant divides the resultant, the primes which divide
the reduced resultant also divide the resultant. On the other hand, suppose
that a prime
p
does
not
divide the reduced resultant. If

is the
reduced resultant of
f
and

A(x)f(x)+B(x)g(x)=d

for some

. If we reduce this modulo

a nonzero value on the right-hand side, which implies that

and

are
relatively prime over

, and therefore have a nonzero

\mathbb{F}_{p}

and

\mathbb{F}_{p}
is just their resultant over

∎

Theorem 6.1
.

If the coefficients of the Bézout cofactors of
f(x)
and
g(x)
are
dyadic, then
f(x)
and
g(x)
dyadically resolve.

Proof.

If the coefficients of the cofactors of

obtain a power of 2 as a

-linear combination of

and

The reduced resultant is therefore a power of 2, because it divides all

-linear combinations of

implies that the only prime which could

6.2
Scalability

Definition 7
.

The moduli
2^{n}-2^{k}+1
and
2^{n}-2^{j}+1
are
scalable
if there
exist polynomials
a(x)
and
b(x)
of degree at most
n
such that:

•

a
and
b
have dyadic coefficients;

•

a(0)
and
b(0)
are integers;

•

the leading coefficients of
a
and
b
are positive; and

\displaystyle(2^{cn}-2^{ck}+1)a(2^{c})

\displaystyle\equiv 1\pmod{2^{cn}-2^{cj}+1}

\displaystyle(2^{cn}-2^{cj}+1)b(2^{c})

\displaystyle\equiv 1\pmod{2^{cn}-2^{ck}+1}

for all sufficiently large
c
.

Lemma 6.1
.

If
f(x)
and
g(x)
are polynomials over
\mathbb{Q}
where

f(n)
and
g(n)
are integers such that
f(n)\equiv 0\pmod{g(n)}
for
infinitely many integers
n
, then
f(x)\equiv 0\pmod{g(x)}
.

Proof.

If we Euclidean divide
f(x)
by
g(x)
, we obtain

f(x)=g(x)q(x)+r(x)

where
\deg r<\deg g
. If
g(n)
divides
f(n)
and
f(n)\neq g(n)q(n)
,
then

|r(n)|=|f(n)-g(n)q(n)|\geq\frac{|g(n)|}{\operatorname{denom}(q)}.

But
|r(n)|
is strictly smaller than any constant times
|g(n)|
for
sufficiently large
n
, because
\deg r<\deg g
. This implies that
f(n)=g(n)q(n)
for all sufficiently large
n
, thus
f(x)=g(x)q(x)
.
∎

Theorem 6.2
.

The moduli
2^{n}-2^{k}+1
and
2^{n}-2^{j}+1
are scalable if and only if
x^{n}-x^{k}+1
and
x^{n}-x^{j}+1
dyadically resolve.

Proof.

Define the polynomials

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=x^{n}-x^{k}+1

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle=x^{n}-x^{j}+1.

If the moduli are scalable, then there is some inverse
polynomial
A(x)
with dyadic coefficients such that
A(2^{c})f(2^{c})\equiv 1\pmod{g(2^{c})}
for all sufficiently large
c
. The previous lemma implies
that
A(x)f(x)-1
is a multiple of
g(x)
, say
A(x)f(x)-B(x)g(x)=1
. Further, because
A
,
f
, and
g
have dyadic coefficients, so does

B
. By clearing denominators, we obtain a power of
2
as a

\mathbb{Z}[x]
-linear combination of
f
and
g
, which implies that their
reduced resultant is a power of 2. By Proposition
6.2
,
f

and
g
dyadically resolve.

On the other hand, if
f
and
g
dyadically resolve, then their reduced
Bézout cofactors

and
b
in

a(x)f(x)+b(x)g(x)=1

can be constructed using only dyadic coefficients. These cofactors are
essentially our inverse polynomials, but they need some minor adjustments to
match our definition.

Without loss of generality, we will describe how to make
a
match the
definition. If
a(0)
is not an integer, then the equation
a(0)+b(0)=1

implies
a(0)=c/2^{m}
and

for some integers

and

. Replacing

with
a-gc/2^{m}
and
b

b+fc/2^{m}
makes the
constant terms integers (at the expense of having degree at most
n
rather
than

a(2^{c})f(2^{c})\equiv 1\pmod{g(2^{c})}

for sufficiently large
c
. If the leading coefficient of

is positive,
then we are done. If the leading coefficient is negative, then we can
replace

with

and

with
g-c^{\prime}f
for some sufficiently
large integer

. Now

satisfies the definition. (Note that the
b

which results from this process has a negative leading coefficient. It must
be repeated in full on

to get a suitable inverse polynomial.)
∎

7
Conclusion and open questions

We have given a simple, resultant-based condition for two moduli of the form

2^{n}-2^{k}+1
to have scalable modular inverses. This condition enables a fast
search for sets of trinomial moduli which are suitable for the Chinese Remainder
Theorem.

There are a number of topics which deserve further study.

1.

What is the true rate of growth of the clique number
\omega(T(n))
? We suspect that it is asymptotically smaller than
\log n
. A related question is to determine the edge density of
T(n)
, which we suspect is asymptotically smaller than
n^{2}
.

2.

Is there a formula for the resultant of two trinomials, similar to the
resultant of two binomials or the resultant of two cyclotomics

[
2
]
? A closely related project is to study sequences of the form

c(n)=\res(f(x),x^{n}-1)
for polynomials
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
.

3.

We only use “
2
” as a base for our moduli because we work with
binary computers. Many of our results carry over verbatim if
2
is replaced
with an arbitrary prime
p
. Studying “
p
-adically resolving” trinomials,
the
p
-adic valuations of these resultants, and so on, seems interesting in
its own right.
