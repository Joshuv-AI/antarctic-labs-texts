On admissibility in post-hoc hypothesis testing
Ben Chugg, Tyron Lardy, Aaditya Ramdas, Peter Grünwald

Abstract
The validity of classical hypothesis testing requires the significance level α \alpha be fixed before any statistical analysis takes place. This is a stringent requirement. For instance, it prohibits updating α \alpha during (or after) an experiment due to changing concern about the cost of false positives, or to reflect unexpectedly strong evidence against the null. Perhaps most disturbingly, witnessing a p-value p ≪ α p\ll\alpha vs p ≤ α p\leq\alpha has no (statistical) relevance for any downstream decision-making. Following recent work of Grünwald 2024 , we develop a theory of post-hoc hypothesis testing, enabling α \alpha to be chosen after seeing and analyzing the data. To study “good” post-hoc tests we introduce Γ \Gamma -admissibility, where Γ \Gamma is a set of adversaries which map the data to a significance level. A test is Γ \Gamma -admissible if, roughly speaking, there is no other test which performs at least as well and sometimes better across all adversaries in Γ \Gamma . For point nulls and alternatives, we prove general properties of any Γ \Gamma -admissible test for any Γ \Gamma and show that they must be based on e-values. We also classify the set of admissible tests for various specific Γ \Gamma .

Introduction

An epidemiologist runs a clinical trial to test the efficacy of a new drug. She does not choose a significance level
\alpha
beforehand, but waits to see the results. If the p-value is
p=0.01
she rejects at level
\alpha=0.01
and if
p=0.05
she rejects at
\alpha=0.05
.
Is this valid statistical practice?
In the standard theory of hypothesis testing—dominant since the work of Neyman, Pearson, and Fisher in the 1920s and 30s—it is not. Indeed, if the epidemiologist is prepared to reject at level
p
for all
p\leq 0.10
(say), then the true type-I error is 0.10, not
p
.

In other words, the current paradigm of hypothesis testing requires that the significance level
\alpha
be chosen independently of (thus without looking at) the data. This is well-known to statisticians, but continues to be a source of frustration and confusion for practitioners, and the epidemiologist’s mistake above is unfortunately all too common
(
Bakan 1966
;
Dar et al. 1994
;
Gigerenzer 2004
)
.
This fact is limiting: once the data has been gathered, if the selected value of
\alpha
gives a vacuous or uninteresting result, nothing more can be done. The same data should not be used in any further analysis of the same question (or, technically, even a separate question motivated by the first

This—admittedly philosophically thorny and somewhat controversial—reliance of p-values on counterfactuals is one of their many restrictive features
(
Pratt 1964
;
Wagenmakers 2007
)
.
).

The difficulties do not end there. Requiring that
\alpha
be chosen independently of the data introduces a fundamental tension between evidence and decision-making. Namely, upon observing a p-value
p\leq\alpha
, it is irrelevant whether
p
is roughly the same as
\alpha
or significantly smaller. For example, if
\alpha=0.05
, then observing
p=0.04
and
p=10^{-6}
have the same decision-theoretic consequences. As pointed out recently by
Grünwald 2024
, this is a frustrating fact: the p-value is ostensibly a measure of evidence against the null hypothesis, yet any
p
-value below
\alpha
has no formal relevance for any downstream decision task.

This limitation serves as an uncomfortable reminder that current statistical practice consists of an odd combination of both Ronald Fisher’s perspective and that of Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson. Whereas Fisher proposed the p-value as measure of evidence
(
Fisher 1925
;
Fisher 1935
)
, Neyman and Pearson were focused on decision making
(
Neyman and Pearson 1928
)
. The fact that we both report the p-value
and
make a decision at a prespecified significance level is an awkward methodological hybrid that none of these pioneers promoted.

This hybrid approach reflects (or perhaps introduces) confusion over the proper interpretation of p-values, significance levels, and hypothesis testing in general. And such confusion has substantial practical consequences. “The problem of roving alphas” as
Goodman 1993
puts it, i.e., choosing
\alpha
as a function of the data, is well-documented in business and management science
(
Hubbard and Bayarri 2003
;
Hubbard 2011
)
, medicine
(
Goodman 1999
)
, and psychology
(
Gigerenzer 2014
)
. What are statisticians to do in the face of this mistake?

One response to the problem of roving alphas is to continue to educate practitioners on the proper use of statistical tools. This, however, doesn’t solve the fundamental tension inherent in the standard theory of hypothesis testing. The epidemiologist’s mistake is understandable, after all—there are many situations where it is natural to want to use the p-value to say more than is mathematically warranted. Consider the following three examples where an analyst might be tempted to incorrectly use p-values in a downstream decision-making task.

Example 1.1

(Investment)
.

A funder is interested in investing in a company producing a new drug that looks promising based on an early clinical trial.
The funder wants to buy-in at an amount inversely proportional to the risk that the drug is ineffective. The original trial was run at
\alpha=0.05
and produced a p-value of less than 0.001. While both the company and the investor would like to use this information, the company can only give guarantees about the false positive rate at the 5% level.

Example 1.2

(Data Exploration)
.

A researcher investigates whether a particular gene is associated with increased cancer risk. An analysis yields a p-value of 0.0002. Initially, the researcher had no specific
\alpha
in mind, but now argues that the result is clearly significant. The practice of publishing the result as significant and reporting
p<0.001
leads to increased false positive rates.

Example 1.3

(Trading)
.

A quantitative analyst develops several new trading rules and back tests them on historical data. One rule produces a p-value of 0.00001 when tested against the null of zero mean return.
The quant chooses this strategy and, using this p-value, calculates the expected return of this strategy.
If the analyst would have run the same procedure regardless of the p-value, this calculation drmatically inflates the expected return.

Faced with these examples, in this work we consider an alternative response to the problem of roving alphas. Instead of pointing out flaws in the epidemiologist’s approach, we focus on building new statistical tools which function both as measures of evidence
and
allow for mathematically sound downstream decision-making. In particular, we develop the theory of
post-hoc hypothesis testing

(
Grünwald 2024
)
, which allows for data-dependent significance levels. The catch is that the guarantees that an analyst can give on their procedure changes under this theory, moving from error probabilities to expected losses. If
\alpha
is fixed beforehand, however, then the theory of post-hoc testing recovers the standard theory of hypothesis testing. Thus, in that sense, post-hoc hypothesis testing is a strict generation of the classical framework.

The reformulation of the standard theory of hypothesis testing in terms of expected losses instead of error probabilities originated with
Wald 1939
, though he never considered a post-hoc setup. Wald noticed that we may introduce a loss function such that the expected type-I and type-II loss of a test are equivalent to type-I and type-II error probabilities. To move to a post-hoc setting,
Grünwald 2024
built upon Wald’s observation by introducing a family of loss functions and allowing the loss to be chosen by an adversary with access to the data.

Our focus in this paper is to define and classify “admissible test families”, which are the analogue of uniformly most powerful tests in the post-hoc setting. While
Grünwald 2024
gave a partial classification of admissible tests, his notion of admissibility is more liberal than our own.
Roughly speaking, he requires that for a test family to be inadmissible, its type-II loss must be improved with probability one by another test family. Our notion, on the other hand, requires only that a test be beat in expectation. Consequently, we recover the Neyman-Pearson lemma as a special case when there is a single loss function while
Grünwald 2024
does not. A comparison between our two notions of admissibility is given in Appendix
B
.

One can situate our work as belonging to a recent push to resolve several issues with traditional statistical tools. The inability to handle post-hoc significance levels is just one problem with much of modern statistics—others include the inability to handle optional stopping and optional continuation. The burgeoning area of safe, anytime-valid inference (SAVI)
(
Ramdas et al. 2023
)
is focused on such issues. A fundamental tool in this line of work is the
e-value

(
Grünwald et al. 2024
;
Ramdas and Wang 2024
)
, an object which will also play a crucial role in this paper.
Overall, we view our work here as an extension of SAVI: broadly, as an attempt to refine existing statistical technology to make life easier for practitioners and to mitigate the risk of statistical malpractice.

1.1
Contributions

Following
Grünwald et al. 2024
, in order to formulate a theory of post-hoc hypothesis testing we recast the usual notions of type-I and type-II error probabilities in terms of expectations over data-dependent type-I and type-II loss functions. We require that a test has bounded type-I loss in expectation, where the type-I loss may be chosen adversarially. Such a test is called
type-I risk safe
, a notion which generalizes the property of a test having a bounded type-I error probability.

At this point we depart from the setting studied by
Grünwald 2024
and introduce a different notion of admissibility. We summarize our contributions below.
Throughout the paper it will often be useful to remark on the differences or similarities between our post-hoc setting and the traditional, non-post-hoc setting. Henceforth, we will often refer to the latter as the “traditional setting” or sometimes as the “Neyman-Pearson paradigm.” We refer to Neyman and Pearson because it was they who introduced type-I and type-II errors and the notion of a uniformly most powerful test
(
Neyman and Pearson 1928
;
Neyman and Pearson 1933
)
, concepts that we build upon and generalize in this work.

Definition of
\Gamma
-admissibility.

Definition
2.7
introduces the notion of
\Gamma
-admissibility, where
\Gamma
is a set of functions which map the data to a type-II loss function. This generalizes the definition of a uniformly most powerful test to the post-hoc setting (Observation
2.8
makes this precise).
Informally, a test is
\Gamma
-admissible if there is no test that has smaller expected type-II loss under each distribution in the alternative and across all mappings in
\Gamma
.
Our definition of
\Gamma
-admissibility departs from the notion of admissibility studied by
Grünwald 2024
, which requires that a test be dominated with probability 1 under each distribution in the alternative to be inadmissible. We require that a test be dominated only in expectation, making fewer tests admissible.

Extension of the likelihood ratio test to the post-hoc setting.

For point hypotheses, we give an extension of the likelihood ratio test—shown to be uniformly most powerful in the traditional paradigm
(
Neyman and Pearson 1933
)
—to a post-hoc setting and prove that the extension is
\Gamma
-admissible for any
\Gamma
. This is in Section
3
. This shows that our definition of admissibility is not too strict; the set of
\Gamma
-admissible tests is non-empty. This investigation also sheds light on an interesting phenomenon in the post-hoc setting: Even for continuous distributions, if randomization is allowed then admissible tests will always be randomized. More specifically, there will be losses on which the test is randomized. Since randomized tests may not be kosher for all applications, this motivates explicitly defining and studying the admissibility of
binary
post-hoc tests which reject or accept with probability one.

General properties of admissible tests.

Moving beyond the likelihood ratio test, we prove
several general properties for all
\Gamma
-admissible tests for any
\Gamma
, again under the assumption of a point null versus a point alternative. We study both the randomized and binary settings.
Of particular note, we associate with each test an “e-variable” and show how any admissible test can be written as a function of the associated e-variable. Thus, the e-variable directly determines the behavior of the test. In the opposite direction, given an e-variable, we can associate with it a test obtained by thresholding. This provides a mapping between e-variables and tests, highlighting the fundamental importance of e-variables in post-hoc hypothesis testing. See Lemmas
4.5
and
4.7
and equations (
4.5
) and (
4.8
) for the details of these relationships.
A second result worth mentioning is a Rao-Blackwell type-argument
(
Rao 1945
;
Blackwell 1947
)
, whereby we demonstrate how to (weakly) improve any given test by conditioning on a sufficient statistic.

\mathbf{U}
- and
\mathbf{C}
-admissibility.

Finally, we study two specific families of maps
\Gamma
and give necessary and sufficient conditions on tests (and e-variables) to be
\Gamma
-admissible. In particular, we study both
\Gamma=\mathbf{U}
and
\Gamma=\mathbf{C}
where
\mathbf{U}
is the set of all mappings from the data to the losses, and
\mathbf{C}
is the set of constant mappings. For a point null and alternative, we give a full characterization of all
\mathbf{U}
-admissible tests in terms of e-variables for both randomized and binary tests—see Theorem
5.1
and Theorem
5.4
. For
\mathbf{C}
, we give several necessary conditions for a randomized test to be admissible, and provide a complete characterization in the binary case—see Theorem
5.14
. This final result recovers the Neyman-Pearson lemma
(
Neyman and Pearson 1928
)
when instantiated with a single loss function.

To summarize, our main contributions are as follows:

1.

We give a definition of admissibility in the post-hoc setting—
\Gamma
-admissibility, Definition
2.7
—which generalizes the notion of admissibility in the traditional Neyman-Pearson setting;

2.

We give a
\Gamma
-admissible generalization of the likelihood ratio test to the post-hoc setting (Section
3
) for all
\Gamma
;

3.

For both randomized and binary tests, we classify the set of
\mathbf{U}
-admissible tests (Theorems
5.1
and
5.4
). For binary tests, we classify the set of
\mathbf{C}
-admissible tests (Theorem
5.14

1.2
Related Work

It should be said that the post-hoc framework studied here is not the only proposal for how to resolve the issues with traditional hypothesis testing. Bayesian decision theory
(
Bernardo et al. 1994
;
Gelman et al. 1995
)
has a devoted following, where one replaces p-values and significance levels with posterior probabilities and Bayes factors, and makes decisions by minimizing posterior loss. A great benefit of the Bayesian paradigm is that it handles data-dependent loss functions for free, which we show formally in Appendix
C
. Of course, one might be uncomfortable with Bayesian methods, given the reliance on priors.

Moreover, the Bayesian treats all losses symmetrically whereas the frequentist tends to prioritize type-I loss, and only then look to optimize type-II loss. This is the Neyman-Pearson paradigm: We first look for level-
\alpha
tests, and only then look for tests with high power. The second half of Appendix
C
explores a possible definition of type-I risk safety in the Bayesian setting, and gives a rule which satisfies it. Beyond that, it’s difficult to compare the Bayesian paradigm with the one studied here given the different definitions that are required due to the incorporation of priors.

Besides Bayesian methods, other approaches to navigate around the drawbacks of traditional hypothesis testing involve changing what researchers report, e.g., switching p-values with confidence intervals
(
Gardner and Altman 1986
)
or likelihood ratios
(
Royall 2017
)
. Post-hoc hypothesis testing has more in common with some of these than with others (it plays nicely with the law of likelihood for instance, since the likelihood ratio is an e-value), but importantly it is the only approach to seek a strict generalization of the traditional Neyman-Pearson paradigm. Given that most hypothesis testing in practice still relies on this paradigm, we believe it worthwhile to study how to expand the scope of its tools instead of casting them aside.

Our work can be viewed as part of a more general focus on e-variables/e-values, which are simple yet surprisingly fruitful mathematical objects that have proven useful across a wide variety of statistical problems. While some authors had previously used them implicitly, it was not until recently (roughly 2020) that excitement grew concerning their applications and they were given a name. Since then, there has been growing interest in both developing the underlying theory of e-variables and deploying them in various problems.
We refer to
Grünwald et al. 2024
,
Ramdas and Wang 2024
, and
Vovk and Wang 2021
for introductions to e-values and their use in hypothesis testing.

That e-values can play nicely with data-dependent parameters, thus overcoming some of the drawbacks of p-values, has been noted previously by several authors.
The first work in this vein came from the multiple testing literature and worked with e-values only implicitly
(
Katsevich and Ramdas 2020
)
.
As far as we are aware, the first result to explicitly link link e-values and data-dependent thresholds is Lemma 1 of
Wang and Ramdas 2022
in the context of false discovery rate control. Then
Grünwald 2024
put these connection on firm decision-theoretic ground and launched the original investigation into post-hoc hypothesis testing. In contradistinction to this work, Grünwald considers multiple actions, whereas we consider only accept/reject decisions. Working under a different notion of admissibility, he gives a partial classification of all admissible tests in terms of e-variables. We further discuss his results and his notion of admissibility in Appendix
B
.

As part of this work,
Grünwald 2024
notes that e-value based confidence intervals and confidence distributions (using the so-called e-posterior,
Grünwald 2023
) are safe under data-dependent selections of
\alpha
.
Since then, a number of authors have noted the promise of e-values in enabling testing and estimating when significance levels are data-dependent
(
Xu et al. 2024
;
Koning 2023
;
Hemerik and Koning 2024
;
Xu et al. 2025
;
Gauthier et al. 2025
)
. An overview of post-hoc testing and decision-making with e-values was recently given in
Ramdas and Wang 2024
.

Besides
Grünwald 2024
, several other authors have studied questions concerning the admissibility of e-variables. These include
Wang 2025
,
Vovk and Wang 2020
, and
Ramdas et al. 2020
. However, their notions of admissibility either differ from ours or the settings are incomparable.
Wang 2025
and
Vovk and Wang 2020
are concerned with designing e-values that are as large as possible under the alternative, and
Ramdas et al. 2020
is concerned with admissible anytime-valid inference.

2
Post-hoc hypothesis testing

We begin with a standard hypothesis testing setup.
Let
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
be two disjoint families of probability measures on some measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
.
We observe data encoded by a random variable
X:\Omega\to\mathcal{X}
taking values in some topological space
\mathcal{X}
whose behavior is governed by some unknown distribution
R\in\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{Q}
.
We are testing
\mathcal{P}
against
\mathcal{Q}
, meaning that we are tasked with deciding between the null hypothesis
H_{0}:R\in\mathcal{P}
and the alternative
H_{1}:R\in\mathcal{Q}
. We either
reject
or
sustain
the null hypothesis
H_{0}
. If the former, we are implicitly adopting the alternative.

In order to formalize the notion of post-hoc significance levels, we follow
Wald 1939
and
Grünwald 2024
and frame hypothesis testing in terms of expected losses instead of error probabilities.
For an index set
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
which we call a set of
scenarios
,
let
\{L_{b}\}_{b\in\mathcal{B}}
be a set of
loss functions
, where
L_{b}:\{0,1\}\times\{0,1\}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. The first argument denotes whether we are considering the loss under the null or alternative:
L_{b}(0,\cdot)
is loss under the former and
L_{b}(1,\cdot)
that under the latter. The second argument denotes whether we sustain (0) or reject (1) the null. We will assume that
L_{b}(0,0)=L_{b}(1,1)=0
for each
b\in\mathcal{B}
. In other words, making the correct decision (sustaining when
R\in\mathcal{P}
and rejecting when
R\in\mathcal{Q}
) incurs zero loss.

We choose the letters
\mathcal{B}
and
b
to accord with Example
1.1
. For instance, we might consider
L_{b}(0,1)
to be the buy in of the funding agency—that is, the amount they stand to lose if the drug turns out to be ineffective.
We do not assume that
\mathcal{B}
is finite (or even countable). For instance, we might have
L_{b}(0,1)=b
for all
b\geq 1
. (Indeed, this is arguably the most natural choice; see the discussion at the end of this section). Finally, we assume without loss of generality that the losses
L_{b}(0,1)
are increasing in
b
(if not, we may simply relabel accordingly). Note this does not imply any ordering of the losses under the alternative.

Next we define the analogue of a hypothesis test in this post-hoc setting. Our decision to sustain or reject the null takes the form of a test family, which sees both the data and the loss, and then makes a (possibly randomized) decision. We emphasize that while we will allow the loss to be data-dependent, the test knows which loss function was chosen when making a decision. If it were otherwise, a test would have to be needlessly conservative. This is also the case in the traditional setting: the test knows the parameter
\alpha
.

In what follows, let
\text{Ber}(p)
denote a Bernoulli distribution with parameter
p\in[0,1]
.

Definition 2.1

(Test family)
.

A
test family

\delta
is a map
\delta:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{B}\to[0,1]
. We call
\delta
a
family
because it represents a hypothesis test for each
b\in\mathcal{B}
.
Given
X\in\mathcal{X}
and
b\in\mathcal{B}
, the decision to reject is drawn as
D\sim\text{Ber}(\delta(X,b))
where
D=1
means reject and
D=0
means sustain.

In standard Neyman-Pearson theory, tests are also allowed to be randomized but the optimal test will only ever be randomized for discrete distributions. Intriguingly, as we will see, this is not the case in this more general framework. Indeed, if allowed, randomization will be necessary for all admissible rules, even for continuous distributions. The curious reader can skip ahead to Lemma
4.5
which gives a canonical representation of any admissible test and shows that, if it rejects with probability 1 on some loss on some observation, then it rejects with positive probability for all losses for the same observation.

This fact poses somewhat of a challenge because randomized hypothesis testing is not without its critics. It implies that two practitioners running the same test on the same data could come to two different conclusions, for instance. This may be inappropriate depending on the application. We will thus also study
binary test families
, test families which reject or accept with probability 1.

Definition 2.2

(Binary test family)
.

A
binary test family

\varphi
is a map
\varphi:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{B}\to\{0,1\}
.
Given
X\in\mathcal{X}
and
b\in\mathcal{B}
, we reject if
\varphi(X,b)=1
and sustain otherwise.

When studying binary test families, we will assume that
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
consist of continuous distributions. Even in the traditional setting, we know that hypothesis tests must generally be randomized for discrete distributions so it makes little sense to study binary test families in this case.

We will typically denote binary test families with the Greek letters
\varphi
or
\psi
—see Table
1
.
Unless binary test families are mentioned explicitly, it should be assumed in the sequel that we are discussing test families that could be either randomized or binary. We will denote such families with letters
\delta
and
\phi
. We may sometimes refer to test families that allow randomization (Definition
2.1
) as
randomized
test families to emphasize the distinction from binary test families.

For notational simplicity, for a test family
\delta
and
a\in\{0,1\}
define

L_{b}(a,\delta(X,b))\equiv\mathbb{E}_{D\sim\text{Ber}(\delta(X,b))}L_{b}(a,D),

(2.1)

which is the expected loss (type-I or type-II) when playing
\delta(X,b)
on loss
L_{b}
. Note that we can write
L_{b}(0,\delta(X,b))=L_{b}(0,1)\delta(X,b)
and
L_{b}(1,\delta(X,b))=L_{b}(1,0)(1-\delta(X,b))
, since we are assuming that
L_{b}(0,0)=L_{b}(1,1)=0
. These two identities will be used extensively. Next we define the risk of a test family.

Definition 2.3

(Type-I Risk)
.

For a test family
\delta
, set

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{P}}(\delta)

\displaystyle\equiv\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}\sup_{b\in\mathcal{B}}L_{b}(0,\delta(X,b)).

(2.2)

We call
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{P}}(\delta)
the
type-I risk
of
\delta
. If
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{P}}(\delta)\leq 1
, we say that
\delta
is
type-I risk safe
.

Definition
2.3
implicitly assumes that
\sup_{b}L_{b}(0,\delta(X,b))
is measurable. In this work we will make certain assumptions that ensure this is the case (see Assumption
1
and the discussion around Lemma
2.4
below). There is a way around making such assumptions, however: see Remark
2.6
.

Risk is the equivalent of type-I error probability in the standard Neyman-Pearson paradigm. Indeed, consider the case of a single loss
\mathcal{B}=\{b_{0}\}
. Then
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{P}}(\delta)=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}L_{b_{0}}(0,\delta(X,{b_{0}}))=L_{b_{0}}(0,1)\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[\delta(X,b_{0})]
. A risk bounded by 1 is thus identical to a type-I error probability bounded by
\alpha>0
if we take
L_{b_{0}}(0,1)=1/\alpha
. The upper bound of 1 on the risk is arbitrary. It can be replaced by any positive constant and our results will scale accordingly. See for instance
Ramdas and Wang 2024
which replaces 1 with a constant
\beta>0
.

While risk recovers type-I error probability when there is a single loss, risk is of course a different metric than an error probability in general. A framework built on replacing error probabilities with risk may therefore make some readers uncomfortable. We provide a longer defense of risk in Appendix
D
, but let us make two points here. First, risk is actually the norm in decision theory. Second, it is unclear how else to formulate a notion of post-hoc hypothesis testing.

It is worth highlighting that in the definition of type-I risk, the supremum over losses is inside the expectation. This allows the loss to be data-dependent, which can be seen explicitly by writing type-I risk in terms of the supremum over mappings from the data to losses. This is the goal of the following lemma. For the result to hold, however, we need to grapple with some measure-theoretic subtleties. For readers disinterested in such minutiae, the takeaway is that for reasonable assumptions on the data and the losses, (

Lemma 2.4
.

Let

be a Borel probability space.
Equip
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{B}
with
\sigma
-fields
\Sigma_{X}
and
\Sigma_{\mathcal{B}}
such that
(\mathcal{X},\Sigma_{\mathcal{X}})
and
(\mathcal{B},\Sigma_{\mathcal{B}})
are standard Borel spaces.
Consider any set of type-I loss functions
\{L_{b}(0,1):b\in\mathcal{B}\}
such that
b\mapsto L_{b}(0,1)
is
\Sigma_{\mathcal{B}}
-measurable.
Let
\delta
be a test family that is
(\Sigma_{\mathcal{X}}\otimes\Sigma_{\mathcal{B}})
-measurable.
If
\delta
has finite type-I risk, then:

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}\sup_{b\in\mathcal{B}}L_{b}(0,\delta(X,b))=\sup_{B:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{B}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}L_{B(X)}(0,\delta(X,B(X))),

(2.3)

where the supremum on the right is over all maps
B
that are
\Sigma_{\mathcal{X}}/\Sigma_{\mathcal{B}}
-measurable.

The proof may be found in Appendix
A.2
. It is worth stating an immediate corollary of Lemma
2.4
, which extends the result to composite null hypotheses. The proof follows by taking the supremum over
P\in\mathcal{P}
on both sides of (
2.3
).

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
be a measurable space and let
\mathcal{P}
be a collection of probability measures such that
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
is a Borel probability space for for each
P\in\mathcal{P}
. Let
(\mathcal{X},\Sigma_{\mathcal{X}})
,
(\mathcal{B},\Sigma_{\mathcal{B}})
, and
\{L_{b}(0,1):b\in\mathcal{B}\}
be as in Lemma
2.4
. Then

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}\sup_{b\in\mathcal{B}}L_{b}(0,\delta(X,b))=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sup_{B:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{B}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}L_{B(X)}(0,\delta(X,B(X))),

(2.4)

where the rightmost supremum is over all maps
B
that are
\Sigma_{\mathcal{X}}/\Sigma_{\mathcal{B}}
-measurable.

Each function
B:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{B}
in Lemma
2.4
can be thought of as an adversary who chooses a loss based on the data.
Thus, if a decision
\delta
is type-I risk safe, it is
post-hoc
type-I risk safe in the sense that it guarantees a type-I loss of at most 1 on any data-dependent loss.

For every
X
, the quantity
\sup_{b\in\mathcal{B}}L_{b}(0,\delta(X,b))
will be a fundamental object of study. We thus define

E_{\delta}(X)\equiv\sup_{b\in\mathcal{B}}L_{b}(0,\delta(X,b)).

(2.5)

Using the letter “E” is no coincidence: If
\delta
is type-I risk safe, then
E_{\delta}
is an e-value.
As we discussed in the introduction, e-values have become important objects of study in mathematical statistics over the last several years. Part of our contribution here is to show that they occupy a central role in post-hoc hypothesis testing. We give more background on e-values in Section
2.2
.

There is also a natural way to define test families from e-values which we will see in Section
4
. This creates a bidirectional mapping between e-values and tests, a relationship which will be convenient both for classifying the admissible test families (e.g., Theorem
5.1
) and in proving properties of admissible tests.

Remark 2.6
.

There exist pathological choices of
\{L_{b}\}_{b\in\mathcal{B}}
which ensure that
\sup_{b}L_{b}(0,\delta(X,b))
is not measurable, in which case Definition
2.3
breaks down and Lemma
2.4
doesn’t hold. Instead of making various measurability assumptions as we do in this work, one could circumvent this problem by following
Grünwald 2024
and calling
\delta
type-I risk safe if there exists some measurable function
\xi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}\xi(X)\leq 1\text{~ and ~}\sup_{b\in\mathcal{B}}L_{b}(0,\delta(X,b))\leq\xi(X)\quad\mathcal{P}\text{-almost surely.}

(2.6)

2.1
Admissible test families

In the standard hypothesis testing paradigm we search for tests which have the lowest type-II error (highest power) among all those which have type-I error bounded by
\alpha
(level-
\alpha
tests). A level-
\alpha
test is better than another to the extent that it has higher power, and a test is
uniformly most powerful
(UMP) if it has the highest power among all level-
\alpha
tests. In our framework, we compare two test families that are type-I risk safe by examining their losses under the alternative. In particular, the analogy of a UMP test is an “admissible” test family.

Let
\mathbf{U}\equiv\mathbf{U}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B})
be the set of all measurable functions (“adversaries”)
B:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{B}
. Intuitively, we call a test family
\delta

\Gamma
-inadmissible for some
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbf{U}
if there is another test which is type-I risk safe and never has higher, but sometimes lower, expected type-II loss under some adversary
B\in\Gamma
. More formally:

Definition 2.7

(
\Gamma
-admissibility)
.

We say the decision family
\phi
is
weakly preferable
to
\delta
relative to
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbf{U}
if it is type-I risk safe and for all
B\in\Gamma
and all
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
,

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q}[L_{B(X)}(1,\phi(X,B(X)))]\leq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q}[L_{B(X)}(1,\delta(X,B(X)))].

(2.7)

We call
\phi

strictly preferable
to
\delta
if the inequality is strict for at least one
B\in\Gamma
. If there exists some type-I risk safe
\phi
that is strictly preferable to
\delta
relatively to
\Gamma
we say that
\delta
is
\Gamma
-
inadmissible
. If no decision family
\phi
is strictly preferable to
\delta
relative to
\Gamma
then we say that
\delta
is
\Gamma
-admissible. (Said differently,
\delta
is
\Gamma
-admissible if the following holds: whenever there exists a
\phi
that is weakly preferable to
\delta
, (
2.7
) actually holds with equality for all
B\in\Gamma
and
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
.)

In what follows we will always assume that any collection
\Gamma
of adversaries under consideration includes the constant mappings. This will simplify many of the proofs. It is also intuitive: it would be odd if an adversary could play
L_{b}(1,0)
for only a subset of the observations. To formalize this assumption, we will consider only those
\Gamma\supseteq\mathbf{C}

where

\mathbf{C}\equiv\big\{B\in\mathbf{U}:B(X)=b^{*}\text{ for all }X\in\mathcal{X}\text{ and some }b^{*}\big\}.

(2.8)

In the sequel, we will often shorthand
B(X)
to
B
in equations to save space.
Note that for any
b\in\mathcal{B}
,
L_{b}(1,\delta(X,b))=L_{b}(1,0)(1-\phi(X,b))
(recall we are assuming that
L_{b}(1,1)=L_{b}(0,0)=0
), so (
2.7
) can be equivalently written as
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q}L_{B}(1,0)\delta(X,B)\leq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q}L_{B}(1,0)\phi(X,B)
. In particular, this implies that if
\phi(X,b)\geq\delta(X,b)
for some
b\in\mathcal{B}
and all
X\in F
for some
F\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, then
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[L_{b}(1,\phi(X,b))\mathbf{1}\left\{X\in F\right\}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{Q}[L_{b}(1,\delta(X,b)\mathbf{1}\left\{X\in F\right\}]
, a fact we will use often.

It is worth spending a moment to relate Definition
2.7
to a more familiar setting. Suppose there is just a single loss function
L_{b}
. Then (
2.7
) is equivalent to
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\delta(X,b)]\leq\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\phi(X,b)]
. Thus
\phi
is strictly preferable to
\delta
(relative to
\mathbf{U}
, which is a singleton if there is one loss function) if it is more powerful than
\delta
, meaning it rejects with higher probability under the alternative. Moreover, per the discussion after Definition
2.3
, if
\alpha=L^{-1}(0,1)
, then a type-I risk safe test has type-I error bounded by
\alpha
. We may therefore conclude:

Observation 2.8
.

For a single loss
L
(the traditional Neyman-Pearson setting) with
\alpha=L^{-1}(0,1)
, any admissible test family is a uniformly most powerful level-
\alpha
test.

One could of course consider other notions of admissibility.
Grünwald 2024
considers a family
\phi
to be preferable to
\delta
if, for any adversary,
\phi
rejects whenever
\delta
rejects with probability 1. We compare our notion of admissibility with that of
Grünwald 2024
further in Appendix
B
, but suffice it to say here that requiring that
\phi
be better than
\delta
with probability 1 is a strong requirement, and does not recover the notion of a uniformly most powerful test.

A second natural idea is to move the supremum over
B\in\Gamma
inside the expectation and replace (
2.7
) with

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\sup_{B\in\Gamma}L_{B}(1,\phi(X,B))]\leq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q}[\sup_{B\in\Gamma}L_{B}(1,\delta(X,B))]
. That is, we require that the worst case adversary for
\phi
is no worse than the worst case of
\delta
, in expectation. However, such a definition of admissibility is too strong: the set of admissible families is empty. This is also shown in Appendix
B
.

One might expect that if
\delta
is
\Gamma_{1}
-admissible, then it is
\Gamma_{2}
-admissible for any
\Gamma_{1}\subseteq\Gamma_{2}
. But this does not necessarily hold. Consider
\Gamma_{1}=\{B:B(x)=b_{0}\}
for some fixed
b_{0}
. The family
\delta(X,b)=\phi^{\textsc{np}}_{b_{0}}(X)
if
b=b_{0}
and
\delta(x,b)=0
otherwise is
\Gamma_{1}
-admissible, but is not
\mathbf{U}
-admissible by Lemma
4.5
if
\mathcal{B}
contains more than
b_{0}
. To put it another way, by introducing more adversaries we open ourselves to the possibility of a test family having strictly better type-II risk than
\delta
on one of those adversaries.

Remark 2.9
.

Unless we consider
\Gamma=\mathbf{U}
there is an asymmetry between the adversaries considered in the definition of type-I risk and the definition of
\Gamma
-admissibility. Lemma
2.4
shows that a type-I risk safe rule has expected type-I loss bounded by 1 across any mapping
B:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{B}
, while in
\Gamma
-admissibility we are deliberately restricting the mappings under consideration, but only for the alternative (i.e. for type II risk). This is by design.
Our first goal is to find tests which have bounded type-I risk under even the worst case adversary, as this seems the most appropriate goal for a post-hoc hypothesis test. Only then do we consider type-II risk. This is analogous to how Neyman and Pearson prioritize type-I error bounded by
\alpha
and only then look for tests with higher power.

Remark 2.10
.

Related to the previous remark, one could also consider different sets of adversaries in the definition of type-I risk safety. In particular, we could consider the notion of
(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2})
-admissibility, where
\Gamma_{1}
is the set of mappings considered in type-I risk (i.e., the supremum on the right hand side of (
2.3
)), and
\Gamma_{2}
is the set of adversaries considered in Definition
2.7
. This paper would then be concerned with
(\mathbf{U},\Gamma)
-admissibility while standard Neyman-Pearson theory focuses on
(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{C})
-admissibility. While this nicely emphasizes the relationship between our notion of type-I risk safety and that of traditional hypothesis testing, it is burdensome to continuously write
(\mathbf{U},\Gamma)
-admissibility. Therefore, to spare ourselves notational pain, in this paper we shorten
(\mathbf{U},\Gamma)
-admissibility to
\Gamma
-admissibility.

Throughout this work, we will be primarily concerned with testing a simple null
P
against simple alternative
Q
. Section
4
will give general properties of admissible test families for all
\Gamma
, and then Sections
5.1
and Section
5.2
will provide full or partial classifications of
\Gamma
-admissible test families for two particular classes
\Gamma
:

1.

\Gamma=\mathbf{U}
. The first natural set of adversaries to consider is all mappings
B:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{B}
. Under such a liberal notion of admissibility, a great many test families are admissible. Indeed, under mild assumptions on the losses, every test family
\delta
which satisfies
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{P}}(\delta)=1
is admissible. In other words, every test which is defined by a sharp e-value is admissible. This is somewhat unsatisfying, and leads us to consider the following more restricted class of adversaries.

2.

\Gamma=\mathbf{C}
for
\mathbf{C}
as in (
2.8
).
We will discuss the interpretation of
\mathbf{C}
further in Section
5.2
, but here let us say that a helpful interpretation is one based on counterfactuals: If
\delta
is
\mathbf{C}
admissible then for every type-I risk safe
\phi
, there exists some loss such that if here let us say that
for a test to be
\mathbf{C}
-admissible it must have higher power in any counterfactual world where the loss was set differently
a priori
. We will give several necessary conditions for a randomized test family to be
\mathbf{C}
-admissible and provide a full characterization of
\mathbf{C}
-admissible binary test families.

Note that
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{C}
are, respectively, the largest and smallest sets of adversaries we can consider under the assumption that
\Gamma\supseteq\mathbf{C}
. In this sense, we might say that we are studying the two extrema of Definition
2.7
.

Finally, it is very natural to take
L_{b}(0,1)=b
for
b\geq 1
. In this case,

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\sup_{b\in\mathcal{B}}L_{b}(0,\delta(X,b))\right]=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\sup_{\alpha\in(0,1]}\frac{\delta(X,1/\alpha)}{\alpha}\right].

(2.9)

That is, letting the losses take on any value in
[1,\infty)
lets us consider any post-hoc significance level
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
Arguably, the right hand side of (
2.9
) is what one would expect of a definition of post-hoc hypothesis testing (see, e.g.,
Wang and Ramdas 2022
,
Koning 2023
, or
Gauthier et al. 2025
). Indeed, the condition
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[\sup_{\alpha\in(0,1)}\alpha^{-1}\delta(X,1/\alpha)]\leq 1
is a natural way to transform the usual type-I error guarantee
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[\delta(X)]\leq\alpha
into a statement where
\alpha
is allowed to be data-dependent.

.

If
\delta
is a
\Gamma
-admissible test family for any

with

Because of this assumption, we will usually say

We will also assume that there exists some

Finally, we make the necessary measure-theoretic assumptions so that Lemma

Assumption 1
.

We assume that

is a Borel probability space for each

. and that

is

\mathcal{B}

3
Warmup: Likelihood ratio tests

Suppose that
\mathcal{P}=\{P\}
and
\mathcal{Q}=\{Q\}
are singletons. In the case of a single loss function, the Neyman-Pearson lemma
(
Neyman and Pearson 1933
)
implies that the likelihood ratio test is uniformly most powerful. What can we say about likelihood ratio-style tests in a setting with multiple losses?

For each fixed
b\in\mathcal{B}
, let
\phi^{\textsc{np}}(X,b)
denote the likelihood ratio test on loss
L_{b}(0,1)
. Let
\Lambda
be the likelihood ratio between
Q
and
P
. The likelihood ratio test has the form

\phi^{\textsc{np}}(X,b)=\mathbf{1}\left\{\Lambda(X)>\kappa(b)\right\}+\gamma\mathbf{1}\left\{\Lambda(X)=\kappa(b)\right\},

(3.1)

where
\kappa(b)
and
\gamma
are chosen such that

P(\phi^{\textsc{np}}(X,b)=1)=L_{b}^{-1}(0,1).

We use the superscript “
NP
” to refer to Neyman-Pearson. Note that the second term
\gamma\mathbf{1}\left\{\Lambda(X)=\kappa(b)\right\}
is only relevant for discrete distributions. We refer to
Shao 2008
for a modern treatment of the Neyman-Pearson lemma and the optimality of the likelihood ratio test.

Suppose that a test family
\delta
plays as
\phi^{\textsc{np}}(\cdot,b^{*})
on some
b^{*}
. How does it behave on
b\neq b^{*}
?
Naively, one would like to play
\phi^{\textsc{np}}(\cdot,b)
on each
b
. Such a test family would not be type-I risk safe, however. This is easy to see, even in the case of two losses. Consider playing
\phi^{\textsc{np}}(X,b_{1})
on
L_{b_{1}}
and
\phi^{\textsc{np}}(X,b_{2})
on
L_{b_{2}}
with
b_{2}>b_{1}
. Since
L_{b_{2}}(0,1)>L_{b_{1}}(0,1)
we have
\kappa({b_{2}})>\kappa({b_{1}})
. If an adversary
B
plays loss
b_{2}
for
X
with
\Lambda(X)\geq\kappa({b_{2}})
and
b_{1}
for
X
with
\kappa({b_{1}})\leq\Lambda(X)<\kappa({b_{2}})
, then
\mathbb{E}_{P}[L_{B}(0,\delta(X,B))]=L_{b_{2}}(0,1)P(\Lambda(X)\geq\kappa({b_{2}}))+L_{b_{1}}(0,1)P(\kappa(b_{1})\leq\Lambda(X)<\kappa({b_{2}}))>1
, so
\mathcal{R}_{P}(\delta)>1
.

However, for
b\leq b^{*}
,
\delta(\cdot,b)

can
play as
\phi^{\textsc{np}}(\cdot,b^{*})
and remain type-I risk safe. In fact, in the discrete case, for those
X
such that
\Lambda(X)=\kappa(b)
it can even raise the probability of rejection due to the extra freedom afforded by the fact that
L_{b}(0,1)\leq L_{b^{*}}(0,1)
.
For
b>b^{*}
meanwhile,
\delta(\cdot,b)
must lower the probability of rejection, but may still reject. Proposition
3.1
demonstrates that, for any
\Gamma\supseteq\mathbf{C}
, the only admissible test family which plays as
\phi^{\textsc{np}}(\cdot,b^{*})
on
b^{*}
has the following form:

\delta(X,b)=\min\left\{1,\frac{L_{b^{*}}(0,1)}{L_{b}(0,1)}\phi^{\textsc{np}}(X,b^{*})\right\}.

(3.2)

The proof is in Appendix
A.3
. One may prove the result directly, but instead we leverage some of the properties of general admissible rules proven in Section
4
. Even so, the proof is more involved than one might initially expect.

Proposition 3.1
.

\delta

\Gamma
-admissible for any
\Gamma\supseteq\mathbf{C}

\phi(X,b^{*})=\phi^{\textsc{np}}(X,b^{*})

P

\phi
acts as (
3.2
)
P
-almost surely.

The representation of
\delta
in (
3.2
) foreshadows a more general pattern that we explore further in Section
4
. In particular, after noting that
E_{\delta}(X)=L_{b^{*}}(0,1)\phi^{\textsc{np}}(X,b^{*})
, we see that we can write
\delta(X,b)=\min\{1,E_{\delta}(X)/L_{b}(0,1)\}
which is a representation that holds for all admissible tests (for any
\Gamma
) and illustrates a fundamental connection between e-values and test families.

It is worth noting that when
P
and
Q
are continuous, (
3.2
) can be rewritten as:

\delta(X,b)=\begin{cases}\frac{L_{b^{*}}(0,1)}{L_{b}(0,1)}\phi^{\textsc{np}}(X,b^{*}),&b>{b^{*}},\\
\phi^{\textsc{np}}(X,b^{*}),&b\leq{b^{*}}.\end{cases}

(3.3)

This representation is perhaps more intuitive to digest.
If an adversary
B
plays
B(X)=b^{*}
for all
X
, the expected type-I risk
\mathbb{E}_{P}[L_{B}(1,\delta(X,B)]
is precisely 1 by definition of the likelihood ratio test.
For
b\leq b^{*}
we have
L_{b}(0,1)\leq L_{b^{*}}(0,1)
so an adversary playing such a
b
can only lower the type-I risk and
\delta
can play as
\phi^{\textsc{np}}(X,b^{*})
. For
b>b^{*}
,
\delta
may still reject, but it must lower its rejection probability to make up for the increase in type-I risk. We set
\delta
such that
L_{b}(0,1)\delta(X,b)=L_{b^{*}}(0,1)\phi^{\textsc{np}}(X,b^{*})
so that the type-I risk stays the same.

The test family in (
3.3
) illustrates a general phenomenon we discussed previously, namely that admissible rules will always be randomized (or, more precisely, there will exist losses on which the test will be randomized), even for continuous distributions. Because randomization may not be desirable, it is reasonable to search for binary test families. In this case, we can show that

\varphi(X,b)=\begin{cases}0,&b>{b^{*}},\\
\phi^{\textsc{np}}(X,b^{*}),&b\leq{b^{*}},\end{cases}

(3.4)

is admissible when
P
and
Q
are assumed to be continuous. When
P
and
Q
are discrete, then
\phi^{\textsc{np}}(\cdot,b^{*})
is randomized—see (
3.1
)—so this case is not of interest. The proof of the following proposition is in Appendix
A.4
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Fix any
\Gamma\supseteq\mathbf{C}
and
suppose
P
and
Q
are continuous.
If we restrict our attention to binary test families, then (
3.4
) is
\Gamma
-admissible. Moreover, if
\psi
is any binary
\Gamma
-admissible test family and there exists some
b^{*}
such that
\psi(X,b^{*})=\phi^{\textsc{np}}(X,b^{*})
, then
\psi
acts as (
3.4
)
P
-almost surely.

Just as (
3.2
) was illustrative of a more general representation of test families when randomization is allowed, (
3.4
) is illustrative of a general representation in the binary case. Indeed, Lemma
4.7
in Section
4
will show that, for any
\Gamma\supseteq\mathbf{C}
, a
\Gamma
-admissible binary test
\varphi
can be written as
\varphi(X,b)=\mathbf{1}\left\{E_{\delta}(X)\geq L_{b}(0,1)\right\}
.

4
Properties of admissible rules

Here we prove several properties of any admissible family which is
\Gamma
-admissible for any
\Gamma\supseteq\mathbf{C}
, where we recall that
\mathbf{C}
, defined in (
2.8
), is the set of sets of adversaries that are constant. We study both randomized test families (Section
4.1
) and binary test families (Section
4.2
), but begin with several properties that pertain to both. For a first time reader, the most important results in this section are lemmas
4.5
and
4.7
, which provide explicit representations of any admissible test
\delta
in terms of its associated e-value
E_{\delta}
(defined in (
2.5
)). These representations generalize equations (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) in Section
3
. Throughout this section we assume that
\mathcal{P}=\{P\}
and
\mathcal{Q}=\{Q\}
are point hypotheses.

We begin by proving an intuitive property of any test family: If it can be modified to reject with strictly higher probability on some loss without sacrificing type-I risk safety, then it cannot be admissible. This fact is immediate in the traditional Neyman-Pearson framework for any uniformly most powerful test (indeed, it is the definition of being uniformly most powerful). But it requires proof in the post-hoc setting.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\phi,\delta
be two type-I risk safe test families such that
\phi(X,b)\geq\delta(X,b)

P
-almost surely for all
b\in\mathcal{B}
. If
\phi
is anywhere strictly greater than
\delta
, i.e., there exists some
b^{*}\in\mathcal{B}
such that
P(\phi(X,b^{*})>\delta(X,b^{*}))>0
,
then
\delta
is
\Gamma
-inadmissible for any
\Gamma\supseteq\mathbf{C}
. The same statement holds if
\phi
and
\delta
are binary test families.

Proof.

By assumption we may assume that anything with positive probability under
P
has positive probability under
Q
and vice versa.
For any
b\in\mathcal{B}
,
L_{b}(1,\phi(X,b))=L_{b}(1,0)(1-\phi(X,b))\leq L_{b}(1,0)(1-\delta(X,b))=L_{b}(1,\delta(X,b))
.
Therefore, for any
B\in\Gamma
,
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[L_{B}(1,\phi(X,B)]\leq\mathbb{E}_{Q}[L_{B}(1,\delta(X,B))]
.
Suppose
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is such that
\phi(X,b^{*})>\delta(X,b^{*})
for all
X\in A
and
Q(A)>0
.
Then

\phi
has strictly lower type-II risk on the map
B(X)=b^{*}
since

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[L_{b^{*}}(1,\phi(X,b^{*})\mathbf{1}\left\{X\in A\right\}]<\mathbb{E}_{Q}[L_{b^{*}}(1,\delta(X,b^{*})\mathbf{1}\left\{X\in A\right\}]
, making
\phi
strictly preferable to
\delta
with respect to any
\Gamma
(
B\in\Gamma
by assumption).
∎

One property which follows from Lemma
4.1
is that any admissible test must be decreasing in the loss. In other words, as the loss increases, a test should only become more conservative.

Lemma 4.2
.

Fix any
\Gamma\supseteq\mathbf{C}
and let
\delta
be
\Gamma
-admissible. Then
\delta(X,b_{1})\geq\delta(X,b_{2})

P
-almost surely if and only if
b_{1}\leq b_{2}
. The same statement holds for binary test families.

Proof.

Let
\delta
be any test family, binary or otherwise,
such that
\delta(X,b_{1})<\delta(X,b_{2})
for some
b_{1}<b_{2}
and all
X
belonging to some set
A
of positive measure on
Q
. Define a new test family
\widehat{\delta}
such that
\widehat{\delta}(X,b_{1})=\delta(X,b_{2})
for all
X\in A
. Otherwise
\widehat{\delta}
is the same as
\delta
. Since
L_{b_{1}}(0,1)\widehat{\delta}(X,b_{1})=L_{b_{1}}(0,1)\delta(X,b_{2})\leq L_{b_{2}}(0,1)\delta(X,b_{2})
, it follows that
\sup_{b}L_{b}(0,1)\widehat{\delta}(X,b)=\sup_{b}L_{b}(0,1)\delta(X,b)
. This implies that
\widehat{\delta}
is type-I risk safe and we conclude that
\delta
is
\Gamma
-inadmissible by Lemma
4.1
.
∎

A second consequence of Lemma
4.1
is that the e-value of an admissible test cannot be strictly dominated by the e-value of another admissible test. Consequently, admissible tests define sharp e-values (recall that
E_{\delta}(X)=\sup_{b}L_{b}(0,\delta(X,b))
). The proof is postponed to Appendix
A.5
. Note that the properties of
\Gamma
-admissible tests given by Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
are both illustrated by the Neyman-Pearson style tests of Section
3
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\phi,\delta
be two type-I risk safe test families. If
E_{\phi}(X)\geq E_{\delta}(X)

P
-almost surely and
P(E_{\phi}(X)>E_{\delta}(X))>0
then
\delta
is
\Gamma
-inadmissible for any
\Gamma\supseteq\mathbf{C}
. Consequently, if
E_{\delta}
is not sharp then it is
\Gamma
-inadmissible for any
\Gamma\supseteq\mathbf{C}
. The same statement holds if
\phi
and
\delta
are binary test families and
P
and
Q
are continuous.

Given a test family
\delta
we can study the properties of
E_{\delta}
, sometimes giving necessary and sufficient conditions on
E_{\delta}
for
\delta
to be admissible. But we will also be interested in the other direction. That is, given an e-variable
E
for
P
, the next sections will define an associated test family
\delta_{E}
—Section
4.1
in the randomized case and Section
4.2
in the binary case. We may then ask about the properties of
E
that lead to admissible tests. A helpful concept when reasoning about the behavior of e-variables is that of compatibility.

Definition 4.4

(Compatibility)
.

Define the set

\mathcal{L}\equiv\{L_{b}(0,1):b\in\mathcal{B}\}.

(4.1)

We say an e-variable
E
for
P
is
compatible
with
\mathcal{L}
if
E(X)\in\mathcal{L}\cup\{0\}

P
-almost surely. If
\mathcal{L}
is understood from context, we will often simply say that
E
is compatible.

Lemma
4.8
will show that if
E
defines a
\Gamma
-admissible binary test family for any
\Gamma
then
E
is compatible. Such a general result does not hold in the randomized case, which can be seen by considering the e-values of Neyman-Pearson tests when the distributions are discrete.
However, Lemma
5.7
proves that when
P
and
Q
are continuous and the randomized test
\delta_{E}
is
\mathbf{C}
-admissible, then
E
is compatible. Interestingly, the issue of compatibility also arose for
Grünwald 2024
, even when studying a different notion of admissibility. He called the condition “richness.”

Beyond this point, the properties of randomized test families and binary test families begin to diverge so we give a separate treatment of each. We begin with randomized test families. Binary test families are studied in Section
4.2
.

4.1
Randomized test families

Throughout this subsection we assume that all test families allow for randomization. The distributions
P
and
Q
can be continuous or discrete, unless stated otherwise. As usual, we assume they have the same support. Our first result provides an explicit form for any test family in terms of its associated e-value.

Lemma 4.5
.

Fix any
\Gamma\supseteq\mathbf{C}
.
If
\delta
is
\Gamma
-admissible then for all
b\in\mathcal{B}
,

\delta(X,b)=\min\left\{1,\frac{E_{\delta}(X)}{L_{b}(0,1)}\right\},\quad\text{P-almost surely}.

(4.2)

Proof.

Suppose there exists some
A
satisfying
P(A),Q(A)>0
such that for some
b\in\mathcal{B}
and all
X\in A
,
\delta(X,b)>\min\{1,E_{\delta}(X)/L_{b}(0,1)\}
. That is,

L_{b}(0,1)\delta(X,b)>\min\{L_{b}(0,1),E_{\delta}(X)\},\text{~ for all }X\in A.

(4.3)

Observe that we must have
\min\{L_{b}(0,1),E_{\delta}(X)\}=E_{\delta}(X)
. Otherwise (
4.3
) implies that
L_{b}(0,1)\delta(X,b)>L_{b}(0,1)
, which is impossible since

\delta(X,b)\leq 1
by definition. Therefore the minimum is achieved by
E_{\delta}(X)
and we have
L_{b}(0,1)\delta(X,b)>E_{\delta}(X)
, contradicting the definition of
E_{\delta}
. Thus no such
b
can exist, and we conclude that the left hand side of (
4.2
) is at most the right hand side.

Next, suppose there exists some
b
with
\delta(X,b)<\min\{1,E_{\delta}(x)/L_{b}(0,1)\}
for all
X\in A
, where again
P(A),Q(A)>0
. Consider a new decision family
\widehat{\delta}
defined as
\widehat{\delta}(X,b)=\min\{1,E_{\delta}(X)/L_{b}(0,1)\}
for all
X\in A
and which acts as
\delta
everywhere else. Note that for
X\in A
,
L_{b}(0,1)\widehat{\delta}(X,b)=\min\{L_{b}(0,1),E_{\delta}(X)\}\leq E_{\delta}(X)
, hence
E_{\widehat{\delta}}(X)=\sup_{b}L_{b}(0,1)\widehat{\delta}(X,b)=E_{\delta}(X)
(using that
\widehat{\delta}(\cdot,c)=\delta(\cdot,c)
for all
c\neq b
). Therefore,

\mathcal{R}_{P}(\widehat{\delta})=\int_{\mathcal{X}\setminus A}E_{\delta}(x)\text{d}P+\int_{A}E_{\widehat{\delta}}(x)\text{d}P=\mathcal{R}_{P}(\delta)\leq 1,

(4.4)

so
\widehat{\delta}
remains type-I risk safe. Moreover, it strictly improves type-II risk by Lemma
4.1
(
\widehat{\delta}(\cdot,b)>\delta(\cdot,b)
on a set of positive measure under
Q
), implying that
\delta
is
\Gamma
-inadmissible.
∎

We will sometimes call (
4.2
) the
canonical
representation of
\delta
, or simply say that
\delta
is canonical if written as such. Lemma
4.5
will be particularly useful in the upcoming theorems on admissibility, since it lets us assume that all test families we deal with are canonical.

Just as test families define e-variables, it will be convenient to enable e-variables to define test families. In view of Lemma
4.5
, given an e-variable
E
for
\mathcal{P}
, we define the corresponding test family as

\delta_{E}(X,b)\equiv\min\left\{1,\frac{E(X)}{L_{b}(0,1)}\right\}.

(4.5)

It is easy to verify that this definition is consistent in the sense that
\delta_{E_{\delta}}(X,b)=\delta(X,b)
for a
\Gamma
-admissible test
\delta
(this follows immediately from Lemma
4.5
). Further, if we begin with an e-variable
E
, define
\delta_{E}
and then compute
E_{\delta_{E}}
, we recover
E
under reasonable assumptions. To wit,

\displaystyle E_{\delta_{E}}(X)

\displaystyle=\sup_{b\in\mathcal{B}}L_{b}(0,\delta_{E}(X,b))=\sup_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\min\{L_{b}(0,1),E(X)\}=E(X)\wedge\sup_{b}L_{b}(0,1).

(4.6)

If
\sup L_{b}(0,1)=\infty
then the final quantity is
E(X)
. If not, then the following result implies that
E(X)\wedge\sup_{b}L_{b}(0,1)=E(X)

P
-almost surely otherwise
\delta_{E}
is inadmissible.
Thus, for e-values
E
which define admissible test families, we have
E_{\delta_{E}}(X)=E(X)
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
E
be an e-variable for
P
. If
P(E(X)>\sup_{b}L_{b}(0,1))>0
then
\delta_{E}
is
\Gamma
-inadmissible for any
\Gamma\supseteq\mathbf{C}
.

The proof can be found in Appendix
A.6
. It is helpful to view Lemma
4.6
as a weaker form of compatibility (Definition
4.4
). It doesn’t guarantee that
E
takes values in
\mathcal{L}\cup\{0\}
, but it does guarantee that it won’t take values strictly larger than those in
\mathcal{L}
. As we discussed earlier, Lemma
4.6
can be strengthened to bonafide compatibility when we consider admissibility with respect to constant adversaries.
We now move onto studying binary test families.

4.2
Binary test families

Throughout this section we assume that
P
and
Q
are continuous. We begin with the analogue of Lemma
4.5
but for binary test families. The same representation was given recently in
Ramdas and Wang 2024
, though stated as a definition, not as the unique form of any admissible binary test family.

Lemma 4.7
.

Fix any
\Gamma\supseteq\mathbf{C}
.
If
\varphi
is a
\Gamma
-admissible binary test family then, for all
b\in\mathcal{B}
,

\varphi(X,b)=\mathbf{1}\left\{L_{b}(0,1)\leq E_{\varphi}(X)\right\}\quad P\text{-almost surely}.

(4.7)

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Lemma
4.5
. First note that we can’t have
\varphi(X,b)=1
if
L_{b}(0,1)>E_{\varphi}(X)
by definition of
E_{\varphi}
. Therefore,
\varphi(X,b)\leq\mathbf{1}\left\{L_{b}(0,1)\leq E_{\varphi}(X)\right\}
. Conversely, suppose that, for some
b^{*}
,
\varphi(X,b^{*})=0
when
L_{b^{*}}(0,1)<E_{\varphi}(X)
for all
X\in A
where
P(A),Q(A)>0
. Define a new rule
\widehat{\varphi}
such that
\widehat{\varphi}(X,b^{*})=1
for
X\in A
and otherwise
\widehat{\varphi}
is the same as
\varphi
. Since
E_{\varphi}=E_{\widehat{\varphi}}
,
\widehat{\varphi}
is type-I risk safe. Now apply Lemma
4.1
with
\phi=\widehat{\varphi}
to see that
\varphi
is
\Gamma
-inadmissible.
∎

Similarly to the randomized case, let us say that a test family with the representation in (
4.7
) is
canonical
, or has canonical representation. And again as we did in the randomized case, let us associate test families to e-variables. Given an e-variable
E
for
P
, we define its associated binary test family as

\varphi_{E}(X,b)\equiv\mathbf{1}\left\{L_{b}(0,1)\leq E(X)\right\}.

(4.8)

As usual, we use
\varphi_{E}
or
\psi_{E}
to denote the binary test family associated with
E
, as opposed to the randomized test defined by (
4.5
). We will always explicitly mention whether we are considering randomized or binary tests.

Both (
4.7
) and (
4.8
) are satisfying in that they are precisely what one would obtain from naively applying the results in the previous section and rounding all non-integer values taken by a test family to zero. Similarly to the randomized case, it is easy to check that
\varphi_{E_{\varphi}}(X,b)=\varphi(X,b)

P
-almost surely. Moreover, given an e-variable
E
, we have

E_{\varphi_{E}}(X)=\sup_{b}L_{b}(0,1)\mathbf{1}\left\{E(X)\geq L_{b}(0,1)\right\},

(4.9)

which is equal to
E(X)
if
E(X)=L_{b}(0,1)
for some
b
, or
E(X)=0
.
In other words, if
E(X)
is compatible with
\mathcal{L}
then
E_{\varphi_{E}}=E
for any e-variable
E
.

If
E
is not compatible with
\mathcal{L}
, then the test family
\delta_{E}
is “wasting” some of its risk budget for no reduction in type-II loss. Indeed, suppose that for some
X

L_{b_{1}}(0,1)<E(X)<L_{b_{2}}(0,1)
where there is no other loss between
L_{b_{1}}(0,1)
and
L_{b_{2}}(0,1)
. Then
E(X)
defines the same test as taking
\widetilde{E}(X)=L_{b_{1}}(0,1)
. Moreover,
\widetilde{E}(X)
can take take advantage of the reduction in type-I risk to take larger values elsewhere and improve its type-II risk. The following result formalizes this argument. Its proof is provided in Appendix
A.7
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
E
be an e-variable for
P
and fix any
\Gamma\supseteq\mathbf{C}
. If
\varphi_{E}
is
\Gamma
-admissible then
E
is compatible with
\mathcal{L}
. Consequently, if
\varphi_{E}
is
\Gamma
-admissible then
E=E_{\varphi_{E}}
.

4.3
Improving tests via Rao–Blackwellization

What kinds of e-variables are available to us when testing point nulls and alternatives?
For the Neyman-Pearson style tests in Section
3
, the corresponding e-variable is easily seen to be
E^{\textsc{np}}(X)=L_{b^{*}}(0,1)\mathbf{1}\left\{\Lambda(X)\geq\kappa(b^{*})\right\}
for some fixed
b^{*}
. Another e-variable is of course the likelihood ratio itself which, in the binary case, defines the satisfyingly intuitive test family
\varphi_{\Lambda}(X,b)=\mathbf{1}\left\{\Lambda(X)\geq L_{b}(0,1)\right\}
.

These examples might make us wonder whether all “good” e-variables for post-hoc hypothesis testing for point hypotheses are functions of the likelihood ratio. In general, of course, there are e-variables which are not functions of
\Lambda
. Indeed,
E=\text{d}R/\text{d}P
for any distribution
R
is a (sharp) e-variable. However, unless
R=Q
, such an e-variable is not log-optimal
(
Shafer 2021
)
. We might therefore expect that it can be improved as a post-hoc test.

Here we show that Rao-Blackwellization
(
Rao 1945
;
Blackwell 1947
)
can weakly improve the type-II risk of any test family based on an e-variable, with respect to a particular set of adversaries. Recall that the Rao-Blackwell theorem says that the expected loss of an estimator can be weakly improved (with respect to a convex loss) by conditioning on a sufficient statistic. See
Wasserman 2004
for a modern statement of the result.

Let
E
be an arbitrary e-variable for
P
and let
T
be any sufficient statistic for testing
P
vs
Q
. More formally, we assume that testing
P
vs
Q
can be written as
H_{0}:\theta=\theta_{0}
and
H_{1}:\theta=\theta_{1}
and that
T
is sufficient for
\theta
. Define

S_{T}(X)=\mathbb{E}_{P}[E(X)|\sigma(T(X))],

(4.10)

where
\sigma(T(X))
is the
\sigma
-field defined by
T(X)
.
Note that
S
is an e-variable by the law of total expectation:
\mathbb{E}_{P}[S]=\mathbb{E}_{P}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E|\sigma(T)]=\mathbb{E}_{P}[E]\leq 1
. Further note that
S_{T}
is
observable
, meaning that it does not depend on the unknown parameter. This is by definition of sufficiency.

The following result shows that
S_{T}
defines a test family that is weakly-preferable to that defined by
E
. However, we must restrict ourselves to adversaries that are
\sigma(T)
-measurable.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
T
be a sufficient statistic for
P
vs
Q
.
Given any e-variable
E
for
P
, the test family
\delta_{S_{T}}
for
S_{T}
in (
4.10
) is weakly-preferable to
\delta_{E}
with respect to any
\Gamma\subseteq\Gamma_{\sigma(T)}
where
\Gamma_{\sigma(T)}
is the set of all adversaries which are
\sigma(T)
-measurable. The same statement holds for the binary test families
\varphi_{S_{T}}
and
\varphi_{E}
.

Proof.

Let

f(E,B)=\min\left\{L_{B}(1,0),\frac{L_{B}(1,0)}{L_{B}(0,1)}E\right\}.

Note that
f
is concave in
E
. Therefore, by Jensen’s inequality, if
B
is
\sigma(T)
-measurable, we have

\mathbb{E}_{P}[f(E,B)|\sigma(T)]\leq f(\mathbb{E}_{P}[E|\sigma(T)],B)=f(S_{T},B).

Since
\Lambda
is
minimally
sufficient, it follows that
\Lambda
is
\sigma(T)
measurable (apply the Fisher-Neyman characterization to write
\Lambda
as a function of
T
).
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f(S_{T},B)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}[\Lambda f(S_{T},B)]\geq\mathbb{E}_{P}[\Lambda\mathbb{E}_{P}[f(E,B)|\sigma(T)]]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}[\mathbb{E}_{P}[\Lambda f(E,B)|\sigma(T)]]=\mathbb{E}_{P}[\Lambda f(E,B)]=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f(E,B)].

We have thus shown that

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[L_{B}(1,0)\delta_{S_{T}}(X,B)]=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f(S_{T},B)]\geq\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f(E,B)]=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[L_{B}(1,0)\delta_{E}(X,B)],

thus showing that
\delta_{S_{T}}
is weakly-preferable to
\delta_{E}
with respect to all adversaries
B
that are
\sigma(T)
-measurable, as desired. When considering binary test families, we instead take
f(E,B)=L_{B}(1,0)\mathbf{1}\left\{E\geq L_{B}(0,1)\right\}
, which is also concave in
E
. The rest of the argument remains unchanged.
∎

It is worth mentioning that by the same logic, we can show that
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log S_{T}]\geq\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log E]
or indeed,
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f(S_{T})]\geq\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f(E)]
for any nonnegative, concave function
f
. That is, Rao-Blackwellization leads to an e-variable that never has worse performance under
Q
, where performance is measured by some concave loss function.
We state this as a theorem with a self-contained proof since it may be of independent interest.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
T
be a sufficient statistic for testing
P
vs
Q
. Let
E
an e-variable for
P
and define
S_{T}=\mathbb{E}_{P}[E|T]
. Then
S_{T}
is an e-variable and

Proof.

5
Classifying the admissible rules

5.1
\mathbf{U}
-admissibility

The first natural set of adversaries
\Gamma
to consider in the definition of
\Gamma
-admissibility is all mappings from the data to the losses; i.e.,
\Gamma=\mathbf{U}
. This section classifies the set of
\mathbf{U}
-admissible test families and binary test families for a point null
P
versus a point alternative
Q
. For randomized test families we will let
P
and
Q
to be either discrete or continuous. As usual, for binary test families we assume that
P
and
Q
are continuous.

When discussing randomized test families we will impose the following conditions on the losses, which restricts the behavior the ratio of type-II loss to type-I loss in the limit. In particular, we will assume that

\liminf_{b\to\sup(\mathcal{B})}\frac{L_{b}(1,0)}{L_{b}(0,1)}=0.

(C1)

Condition (
C1
) implies that an adversary can make type-II loss arbitrarily unimportant compared to type-I loss.
In other words, as the cost of false positives increases (
L_{b}(0,1)
grows) we care more about false positives than false negatives. If
\mathcal{B}
is infinite, then (
C1
) holds if
L_{b}(1,0)=o(L_{b}(0,1))
, where the asymptotics are with respect to
b\to\sup\mathcal{B}
. For example, consider
L_{b}(0,1)=b
and
L_{b}(1,0)=\sqrt{b}
, or
L_{b}(1,0)=c
for some constant
c\geq 0
.

Intuitively, given two test families
\phi
and
\delta
, if condition (
C1
) holds then an adversary
B
can make
\phi
arbitrarily small on regions where
\phi>\delta
, thus ensuring a lower type-II risk for
\delta
. Without such a condition, the set of admissible rules appear to depend in unsatisfactory ways on the precise null and alternative being tested. Condition (
C1
) can be replaced by the assumption that
\liminf_{b\to\inf(\mathcal{B})}L_{b}(1,0)/L_{b}(0,1)=0
, but this requires that
L_{b}(1,0)\approx 0
for small
b
, which is unsatisfying for practical applications. If the type-II loss is zero, then one should always accept the null.

With that, we state the main result for
\mathbf{U}
-admissibility of randomized test families. Recall that
\delta
is canonical if it is written as in (

).

Theorem 5.1

(
\mathbf{U}
-admissibility, randomized tests)
.

Let

be a randomized test family and suppose condition (

\mathbf{U}
-admissible if and only if

is sharp and
\delta

for

is
\mathbf{U}

is sharp and
E\leq\sup_{b}L_{b}(0,1)

P
-almost surely.

The proof is in Appendix

and

into regions depending on whether

is greater than

or vice versa. When

such that

which drives the contribution of

if

Remark 5.2
.

The invocation of Condition (

An immediate consequence of Theorem

(

Suppose condition (

Intriguingly, Theorem

Roughly speaking, he considers a test family to be G-admissible if it is not dominated with probability 1 by another test. (We use G-admissibility to refer to Grünwald.)

Upon reflection, the convergence of our two results is unsurprising. Given two sharp but distinct e-variables, each must be greater than the other in some region of

5.2
\mathbf{C}
-admissibility

The results of Section
5.1
were, in some sense, disappointingly liberal. As remark
5.2
points out, Condition (
C1
) ensures that Theorems
5.1
and
5.4
cannot be mapped back to the standard hypothesis testing setting. However, even if this were not the case, the result itself is unsatisfying: There are many sharp e-variables that do not correspond to the likelihood ratio test. Ideally, we would like a result which recovers the Neyman-Pearson lemma when instantiated with a single loss function. This motivates considering a different set
\Gamma
of adversaries. In this section we study
\mathbf{C}
-admissibility, where we recall that
\mathbf{C}=\{B:B(X)=b\text{ for some }b\text{ and all }X\}
.

It is worth revisiting Remark
2.9
at this point. We stress that considering a restricted family
\Gamma
of adversaries in the definition of admissibility does not change our notion of type-I risk safety, which continues to provide a guarantee with respect to all adversaries. In other words, the set of type-I risk safe test families stays the same, but the subset which are
\mathbf{C}
-admissible is smaller (or so it will turn out) than the set which are
\mathbf{U}
-admissible.

For test families which are permitted to randomize, we give two necessary conditions to be
\mathbf{C}
-admissible. The first is that the test families must be increasing functions of the likelihood ratio. The second is that, when
P
and
Q
are continuous, the e-variable associated to the test must be compatible with
\mathcal{L}
. For binary test families we give a complete characterization of all
\mathbf{C}
-admissible rules in terms of e-variables. As in Section
5.1
we consider a point null
P
and a point alternative
Q
.

Studying
\mathbf{C}
-admissibility has an interesting consequence: The type-II losses do not matter. Indeed, instantiating Definition
2.7
when
\Gamma=\mathbf{C}
, we see that
\delta
is
\mathbf{C}
-inadmissible if, for all
b\in\mathcal{B}
,
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[L_{b}(0,\delta(X,b))]\geq\mathbb{E}_{Q}[L_{b}(0,\delta(X,b))]
, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\delta(X,b))]\leq\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\phi(X,b)]
for all
b\in\mathcal{B}
, with a strict inequality for some
b
. That is, the precise values of
L_{b}(1,0)
, assuming they are not zero, do not affect the admissibility of a test.

In other words, subject to type-I risk safety, we are looking for tests which maximize their power on each fixed
b
. This leads us to the counterfactual interpretation of
\mathbf{C}
-admissibility of
\delta
: For every other test
\phi
, there exists a
b\in\mathcal{B}
such that had we set our significance level to be
L_{b}^{-1}(0,1)
at the beginning of the experiment, we would not regret using
\delta
instead of
\phi
(in terms of power, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\delta(X,b)]
).

We begin by proving that any randomized
\mathbf{C}
-admissible test must be increasing in the likelihood ratio. The result is, of course, satisfyingly intuitive—a larger likelihood ratio indicates indicates more evidence against the null. A test that rejects the null at
\Lambda(X)
but fails to reject when
\Lambda(Y)>\Lambda(X)
is doing so despite increased odds in favor of the alternative. That such a result does not hold for richer classes of adversaries
\Gamma
is yet another reason to consider
\mathbf{C}
. Indeed, tests which don’t satisfy this property are flaunting the law of likelihood
(
Royall 2017
)
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\delta
be a randomized test family. If a randomized test family
\delta
is
\mathbf{C}
-admissible then

is an increasing function of the likelihood ratio
P
-almost surely. Consequently, for each fixed
b\in\mathcal{B}
,
\delta(X,b)
is an increasing function of the likelihood ratio
P
-almost surely.

The proof may be found in Appendix
A.10
. The idea is simple: Any test which is not increasing in
\Lambda
can be perturbed such that it becomes strictly better. The details are intricate, however, requiring a careful analysis and strictly more edge cases than is healthy.

Our second result shows that when
P
and
Q
are continuous, Lemma
4.8
holds for randomized tests as well as binary ones. The proof may be found in Appendix
A.11
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
P
and
Q
be continuous. If a randomized test family
\delta
is
\mathbf{C}
-admissible then
E_{\delta}
is compatible with
\mathcal{L}
.

It is worth noting that the properties given in Lemmas

and

are both on display for the likelihood ratio test. For continuous

and

, its associated e-value is

and 0 otherwise (see Section

. However, in the discrete case, the e-value obeys

We leave it as an open question whether the conditions specified by Lemmas

and

(in addition, of course, to the canonical representation in Lemma

) are also sufficient to specify
\mathbf{C}
-admissible tests. We expect the answer is no. We do, however, conjecture that in addition to our extensions of the likelihood ratio test,

of likelihood ratio tests are
\mathbf{C}
-admissible, subject to compatibility. More formally, if

is the post-hoc likelihood ratio test on loss

(i.e., the test defined by (

is

-admissible, as long as

is
\mathbf{C}
-admissible by Proposition

.

5.2.1
Binary test families

We now consider
\mathbf{C}
-admissible binary test families. In this case we can provide a complete characterization of admissibility. As usual for binary tests, throughout this section we assume that

and

are continuous.
We begin with the analogue of Lemma

in the binary case. The proof is in Appendix

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
P
and

be continuous and let
\varphi

P

One might initially suspect that Lemma

Lemma
4.7

by comparing these thresholds. Moving forward, our analysis will make heavy use of these thresholds. Let us state this formally.

Corollary 5.9
.

Let
P
and

be continuous and let
\varphi
be a
\mathbf{C}
-admissible binary test family. Then there exists an increasing function, henceforth called a

We write the decision curve associated with

Proof.

Lemma

implies that a
\mathbf{C}
-admissible binary test

, hence so too is

in the appendix. Therefore, there exists an increasing function

∎

.

.

We say that a binary test family

\sup_{b<b^{*}}L_{b}(0,1)=L_{b^{*}}(0,1)
but
\sup_{b<b^{*}}t_{\varphi}(b)<t_{\varphi}(b^{*})
. Then, for

such that
\sup_{b<b^{*}}t_{\varphi}(b)\leq\Lambda(X)\leq t_{\varphi}(b^{*})
,
\sup_{b}L_{b}(0,1)\mathbf{1}\left\{\Lambda(X)\geq t_{\varphi}(b)\right\}=L_{b^{*}}(0,1)

on such

Lemma 5.13
.

Let

and

be continuous and let

If

-admissible then it has a minimal decision curve.

With Lemma

in hand, we can state the main result of this section. The spirit behind the result is that a binary test is

-admissible if it type-I risk safe and an increasing function of the likelihood ratio. To prove this, we appeal to decision curves: when comparing two type-I risk safe tests, typically neither of their decision-curves will dominate the other. Intuitively, if one did, then one of the e-variables is strictly greater than the other, meaning one of them is not sharp. While some subtleties emerge around minimal decision curves, it is useful to keep this high-level intuition in mind.

Theorem 5.14
.

Let
P
and
Q
be continuous.
A canonical binary test family
\varphi
is
\mathbf{C}
-admissible if and only if it has a minimal decision curve and

is sharp and an increasing function of the likelihood ratio. Moreover, given an e-variable

for

,
\varphi_{E}
is

-admissible if and only if

is sharp, compatible, and increasing in the likelihood ratio.

The proof is in Appendix
A.14
. Note that there is no mention of minimal decision curves in the second half of the theorem. This is because
\varphi_{E}
will automatically have a minimal decision curve if

is an increasing function of the likelihood ratio. Indeed, if
E(X)=h(\Lambda(X))
for some increasing function

with generalized inverse
h^{-}(y)=\inf\{z:h(z)\geq y\}
, then
\varphi_{E}(X,b)=\mathbf{1}\left\{E(X)\geq L_{b}(0,1)\right\}=\mathbf{1}\left\{\Lambda(X)\geq h^{-}(L_{b}(0,1))\right\}
, implying that the decision curve of

is

(see also the proof of Corollary

) which is minimal since

is left-continuous.

As was done in Section
5.1
, it is worth stating the following immediate corollary of Theorem

which highlights the direct correspondence between admissible tests and e-variables.

Corollary 5.15
.

Let
P
and
Q
be continuous. A binary test family
\varphi
is
\mathbf{C}
-admissible if and only if
\varphi=\varphi_{E}
for some sharp e-variable
E
for
P
which is compatible with
\mathcal{L}
and increasing in the likelihood ratio.

Theorem
5.14
makes no assumptions on the losses, hence can recover the Neyman-Pearson lemma for continuous distributions. Indeed, as we discussed previously, for a single loss
L(0,1)=1/\alpha
, the e-variable associated to the likelihood ratio
\phi^{\textsc{np}}(X)=\mathbf{1}\left\{\Lambda(X)\geq\kappa\right\}
is
E^{\textsc{np}}(X)=\alpha^{-1}\mathbf{1}\left\{\Lambda(X)\geq\kappa\right\}
and is sharp, increasing in
\Lambda
, and compatible by construction. We can also see that
\phi^{\textsc{np}}
is the unique test which satisfies Corollary
5.15
. Consider any other
\mathbf{C}
-admissible rule, which can be written as
\varphi_{E}(X)=\mathbf{1}\left\{E(X)\geq 1/\alpha\right\}
for some
E
. Since
E
is compatible with
\mathcal{L}=\{1/\alpha\}
and
\mathbb{E}_{P}[E]=1
, we have
P(E(X)=1/\alpha)=\alpha
. Additionally,
E=h(\Lambda)
for some increasing
h
by Lemma
5.8
, so
\varphi_{E}(X)=\mathbf{1}\left\{\Lambda(X)\geq h^{-}(1/\alpha)\right\}
. Therefore
\alpha=P(E(X)\geq 1/\alpha)=P(\Lambda(X)\geq h^{-}(1/\alpha))
, meaning that
h^{-}(1/\alpha)=\kappa
and
\varphi_{E}=\phi^{\textsc{np}}
.

Now consider
L_{b}(0,1)=b
for all
b>0
. In this case the test

\varphi_{\Lambda}(X,b)=\mathbf{1}\left\{\Lambda(X)\geq L_{b}(0,1)\right\},

(5.3)

is
\mathbf{C}
-admissible. Indeed,
\Lambda
is sharp, compatible with
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and obviously increasing in itself.
This test is satisfying in that it accords well with calls to report the likelihood ratio instead of the p-value
(
Royall 1986
;
Royall 2000
;
Berger and Sellke 1987
;
Perneger 2001
)
.
This nicely bridges the Fisherian and Neyman-Pearson perspectives on hypothesis testing: If a study summarizes its findings via
\Lambda(X)
as a measure of evidence, downstream analysts can reject at whatever level they like, maintaining type-I risk safety.

6
Summary

We studied post-hoc hypothesis testing, a framework which allows the significance level to be chosen as a function of the data. The notions of type-I and type-II error are replaced by type-I and type-II risk, which take the form of expectations over data-dependent type-I and type-II loss functions.
In order to generalize the notion of a uniformly most powerful test to this setting, we introduced the notion of
\Gamma
-admissibility, where
\Gamma
is a set of functions mapping the data to a type-II loss function.

Different families
\Gamma
result in different classes of admissible tests. For point hypotheses, when
\Gamma
is all mappings from the data to losses we show that all admissible tests correspond to sharp e-variables. When
\Gamma
is the set of constant mappings and the tests are binary, we give a classification of all admissible tests which recovers the Neyman-Pearson lemma
(
Neyman and Pearson 1933
)
.

This work suggests several open questions. For one, in the case of randomized tests, the set of necessary and sufficient conditions for
\mathbf{C}
-admissibility do not match. Can we close the gap and give a classification of all admissible tests in this case? As discussed in Section
5.2
, we expect that mixtures of likelihood ratio tests are admissible but we’ve been unable to prove it. Second, while we’ve focused mainly on point hypotheses, hypothesis testing in practice typically involves composite hypotheses. Can we extend some of these results to composite settings? Third, we’ve adopted the typical hypothesis testing setup of two actions: either sustaining or rejecting the null.
Grünwald 2024
, however, studies multiple actions. How must our results be modified in this case?

Overall, post-hoc hypothesis testing is in its nascency. We hope our work provides a foundation that will inspire further research. The connections we’ve established between admissible tests and e-variables connect post-hoc hypothesis testing to exciting developments in modern statistics, and our generalization of classical results suggest that post-hoc testing may serve as a principled bridge between traditional hypothesis testing and more adaptive approaches to statistical inference. While our work here has been primarily theoretical, we also hope that post-hoc hypothesis testing may offer solutions to some of the problems plaguing various empirical fields as they grapple with issues of statistical validity and reproducibility.

A.1
Technical Lemmas

Lemma A.1
.

Suppose there exist sets
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
such that
\sup_{X_{1}\in A_{1}}\Lambda(X_{1})<\inf_{X_{2}\in A_{2}}\Lambda(X_{2})
. Then both of the following hold: (a)
if
P(A_{1})\leq P(A_{2})
then
Q(A_{1})<Q(A_{2})
, and (b)
Q(A_{1})/Q(A_{2})<P(A_{1})/P(A_{2})
.

Proof.

Define

\overline{c}=\sup_{X\in A_{1}}\frac{\text{d}Q}{\text{d}P}(X),\text{~ and ~}\underline{c}=\inf_{X\in A_{2}}\frac{\text{d}Q}{\text{d}P}(X).

Observe that

Q(A_{1})=\int_{A_{1}}\text{d}Q(x)=\int_{A_{1}}\Lambda(x)\text{d}P(x)\leq\overline{c}\int_{A_{1}}\text{d}P(x)=\overline{c}P(A_{1}).

Similarly,

Q(A_{2})=\int_{A_{2}}\Lambda(x)\text{d}P\geq\underline{c}\int_{A_{2}}\text{d}P=\underline{c}P(A_{2}).

To prove (b), use the above two displays and note that
\overline{c}<\underline{c}
. Then, by assumption, we have

\frac{Q(A_{1})}{Q(A_{2})}\leq\frac{\overline{c}P(A_{1})}{Q(A_{2})}\leq\frac{\overline{c}P(A_{1})}{\underline{c}P(A_{2})}<\frac{P(A_{1})}{P(A_{2})}.

As for (a), suppose that
P(A_{1})\leq P(A_{2})
. Then,

Q(A_{1})\leq\overline{c}P(A_{1})\leq\overline{c}P(A_{2})\leq\underline{c}P(A_{2})\leq Q(A_{2}),

as desired.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let

Suppose

2.

B

-preferable to

where

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{R_{k}}L_{B}(1,\phi(x,B))-L_{B}(1,\delta(x,B))\text{d}Q

\displaystyle\Delta_{4}=\int_{R_{4}}L_{B}(1,0)\left(\frac{E_{\delta}(X)}{L_{B}(0,1)}-\frac{E_{\phi}(X)}{L_{B}(0,1)}\right)\text{d}Q>0,

Appendix B
On the definition of admissibility

First let us discuss the notion of admissibility used by
Grünwald 2024
, which we henceforth refer to as G-admissibility. We say a test family
\delta
is G-
in
admissible if there exists a type-I risk safe test family
\phi
such that, for all
b\in\mathcal{B}
and all
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
,

Q(L_{b}(1,\phi(X,b))>L_{b}(1,\delta(X,b))=0,

(B.1)

and there exists some
b\in\mathcal{B}
and some
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
such that

Q(L_{b}(1,\phi(X,b))<L_{b}(1,\delta(X,b)))>0.

(B.2)

In other words,
\phi
has loss at most that of
\delta
with
Q
-probability 1 on all losses, and has lower loss than
\delta
with positive probability. If no such
\phi
exists then we say that
\delta
is G-admissible. We note that
Grünwald 2024
defines G-admissibility in terms of type-I losses instead of type-II, after preprocessing the losses to remove any such that
L_{b}(1,a)>L_{b}(1,a)
and
L_{b}(0,a)>L_{b}(0,a)
for some
a
, which can be done without loss of generality.

Now, if we translate Grünwald’s results to our setting—in particular, we consider binary actions only—then his main result can be formulated as follows. Consider testing
\mathcal{P}
against
\mathcal{Q}
. Then:

1.

If
\delta
is G-admissible, then there exists some e-variable
E
for
\mathcal{P}
such that
\delta(X,b)=\mathbf{1}\left\{E(X)\geq L_{b}(0,1)\right\}
.

2.

If
\delta=\mathbf{1}\left\{E(X)\geq L_{b}(0,1)\right\}
for some some e-variable
E
for
\mathcal{P}
that is sharp, compatible with
\mathcal{L}_{0}
, and all
P\in\mathcal{P}
are absolutely continuous with one another, then
\delta
is G-admissible.

Next let us return to a question raised in Section
2
: Why not define admissibility with a supremum over losses inside the expectation? That is, we could say that
\delta
is admissible if it is type-I risk safe and for all other type-I risk safe tests
\phi
,

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{b\in\mathcal{B}}(L_{b}(1,\delta_{b}(X))-L_{b}(1,\phi_{b}(X))\bigg]\leq 0.

(B.3)

Such a definition, however, leads to an empty set of admissible test families. Intuitively, no test can be as good as any other on every data-dependent loss. To do so, it must be the most powerful test on every loss. That is, for a point null versus a point alternative, it must be the Neyman-Pearson test. But it is invalid to play the Neyman-Pearson on each loss, since such a test family is not type-I risk safe.
This is formalized in the following argument.

Suppose we are testing a simple null vs simple alternative,
\mathcal{P}=\{P\}
vs
\mathcal{Q}=\{Q\}
.
Consider two loss functions,
\mathcal{B}=\{0,1\}
. Suppose
\delta
is admissible according to (
B.3
) and consider the Neyman-Pearson test on each loss. That is, for
i\in\{0,1\}
, define

\phi^{(i)}(X,b)=\begin{cases}\mathbf{1}\left\{\Lambda(X)\geq\kappa_{i}\right\},&\text{if }b=i\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where, as usual,
\kappa_{i}
is chosen such that
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\phi^{(i)}(X,b)]=1/L_{i}(0,1)
.
Note that
\phi^{(i)}
is type-I risk safe.
Also,

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\sup_{b}\left(L_{b}(1,\delta(X,b))-L_{b}(1,\phi^{(i)}(X,b))\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{Q}[L_{i}(1,\delta(X,i))-L_{i}(1,\phi^{(i)}(X,i)]

\displaystyle=L_{i}(1,0)\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\phi^{(i)}(X,i)-\delta(X,i)]\geq 0,

where the last inequality follows
since
\phi^{(i)}
is the uniformly most powerful test by the Neyman-Pearson lemma. Therefore, by admissibility we conclude that
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\phi^{(i)}(X,i)-\delta(X,i)]=0
. That is,
\delta
has the same power as
\phi^{(i)}
under loss
b_{i}
and thus, by the uniqueness of the Neyman-Pearson lemma, we have
\delta=\phi^{(i)}
almost surely. But
i
was arbitrary, so
\phi^{(1)}=\delta=\phi^{(2)}
, a contradiction.

Appendix C
On the relationship to Bayesian decision theory

A common response to the drawbacks of classical Neyman-Pearson theory that we’ve highlighted is to pivot to Bayesian decision theory
(
Bernardo et al. 1994
)
. While the Bayesian often/typically omits the notion of type-I error control and focuses solely on loss minimization, we show below that he gains the ability to handle post-hoc loss functions for free. We also discuss what a Bayesian notion of type-I risk control might look like, and how to achieve it.

Moving away from the setting considered in the main paper for a moment, let
\Theta
be a parameter space,
\mathcal{A}
a set of actions, and
L:\Theta\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
a loss function. Associated to each
\theta\in\Theta
is a distribution
P_{\theta}
.
Let
\pi
be a prior on
\Theta
. Given observations
X
, Bayesian decision theory tells us to minimize the expected posterior loss. That is, we take action

\delta_{\pi}(X)\equiv\argmin_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi(\cdot|X)}L(\theta,a).

(C.1)

A fundamental result is that the
Bayes optimal decision rule

(
Lehmann and Casella 1998
, Theorem 4.1.1)
, henceforth ‘Bayes decision’ for brevity, is always given by
\delta_{\pi}
, meaning that it satisfies
B_{\pi}(\delta_{\pi})=\inf_{\delta}B_{\pi}(\delta)
where

B_{\pi}(\delta)=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\theta}}[L(\theta,\delta(X)].

(C.2)

(Note that
\theta
is drawn from the prior
\pi
in (
C.2
), not the posterior
\pi(\cdot|X)
as in (
C.1
).) Bayes estimators are, as the name suggests, the Bayesian equivalent to frequentist minimax estimators, so a unified solution for the Bayes decision regardless of the loss function is a big benefit of Bayesian decision theory.

To apply this to our setting, we take
\mathcal{A}=\{0,1\}
and we assume that
\Theta
is naturally partitioned into
\Theta_{0}
and
\Theta_{1}
, where
\Theta_{0}
corresponds to the null and
\Theta_{1}
to the alternative. This induces the conditional prior distributions
\pi_{i}(\theta)=\pi_{i}(\theta|\theta\in\Theta_{i})
and conditional posterior distributions
\pi_{i}(\theta|X)=\pi_{i}(\theta|X,\theta\in\Theta_{i})
.

Assuming as usual that
L(\theta,i)=0
for
\theta\in\Theta_{i}
and
i\in\{0,1\}
, the Bayes decision becomes

\displaystyle\delta_{\pi}(X)

\displaystyle=\argmin\big\{\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi(\cdot|X)}[L(\theta,0)],\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi(\cdot|X)}[L(\theta,1)]\big\}

\displaystyle=\argmin\big\{\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi_{1}(\cdot|X)}[L(\theta,0)]\pi(\Theta_{1}|X),\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi_{0}(\cdot|X)}[L(\theta,1)]\pi(\Theta_{0}|X)\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi_{1}(\cdot|X)}[L(\theta,0)]\pi(\Theta_{1}|X)\geq\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi_{0}(\cdot|X)}[L(\theta,1)]\pi(\Theta_{0}|X)\right\}.

(C.3)

In words, we play whichever action has the lowest cost if wrong (i.e., results in a false positive or negative), weighted by the probability under the posterior.

To move to a post-hoc setting, let’s consider a set of losses
\{L_{b}\}_{b\in\mathcal{B}}
. As before, we allow an adversary to choose
b
after seeing the data and we encode the adversary as a measurable map
B:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{B}
. As usual, we let the decision rule see
B(X)
before making a decision.

Can Bayesian decision theory accommodate a post-hoc selection of the loss? Yes, in the following sense: If we minimize the expected posterior loss as before but on loss
L_{B(X)}
(which we observe), then the resulting decision rule minimizes Bayes risk, where the Bayes risk is now defined in terms of the loss selected by the adversary. More formally:

Proposition C.1
.

The decision rule

\phi_{\pi}(X)=\argmin_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi(\cdot|X)}L_{B(X)}(\theta,a),

satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\theta}}L_{B(X)}(\theta,\phi_{\pi}(X))=\inf_{\phi}\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\theta}}L_{B(X)}(\theta,\phi(X)),

for all
B:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{B}
.

We emphasize that
\phi_{\pi}
is well-defined since, as mentioned earlier,
\phi
is allowed to see
B(X)
. We also note that this result is not new. For example,
Grünwald 2023
notes that that Bayesian decision can accommodate post-hoc loss functions, but we hope the formal treatment above clarifies in what sense this is true, and allows for easier comparison to our frequentist results.

Proof.

For any decision rule
\phi
, let
\ell(\phi(x)|x)=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}L_{B(x)}(\theta,\phi(x))
be its expected posterior loss at a given
x
on loss
L_{B(x)}
. For simplicity, assume that
P_{\theta}
has density
p(\cdot|\theta)
. Let
p(x)=\int p(x|\theta)\pi(\theta)\text{d}\theta
be the marginal and note that
p(x|\theta)\pi(\theta)=\pi(\theta|x)p(x)
. Then, by Fubini’s theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\theta}}L_{B(X)}(\theta,\phi(X))

\displaystyle=\int_{\Theta}\int_{\mathcal{X}}L_{B(x)}(\theta,\phi(x))p(x|\theta)\pi(\theta)\text{d}x\text{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\Theta}L_{B(x)}(\theta,\phi(x))\pi(\theta|x)p(x)\text{d}\theta\text{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\ell(\phi(x)|x)p(x)\text{d}\theta.

By minimizing the expected posterior
\ell(\phi(x)|x)
we’re minimizing the above integrand at every
x
, thus minimizing the value of the integral.
∎

The Bayesian paradigm is thus post-hoc valid in the sense of Proposition
C.1
. But it does not take into account the notion of type-I error—it is solely concerned with minimizing the loss. In other words, Bayesian theory treats type-I and type-II loss as symmetric, whereas Neyman-Pearson theory, and our generalized version thereof, views type-I risk as taking precedence. A natural question is whether one can incorporate a notion of type-I risk safety into Bayesian decision theory; we provide one answer below.

We might consider the following definition of type-I risk in the Bayesian context:

S_{\pi}(\delta)=\sup_{B:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{B}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\theta}}L_{B(X)}(\theta,\delta(X)),

(C.4)

where we emphasize that
\theta
is being drawn from the induced prior
\pi_{0}
over
\Theta_{0}
, not from
\pi
itself. It’s helpful to compare (
C.4
) to the frequentist counterpart in the main paper, Definition
2.3
. First, following the Bayesian philosophy, we’ve replaced the supremum over
\Theta_{0}
with an expectation (the supremum over
\Theta_{0}
appears as a supremum over
\mathcal{P}
in Definition
2.3
).
But we have also moved the supremum over
B
to the outside;
we leave moving the supremum inside
\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi_{0}}
as a possible future direction.

It turns out that we can solve a constrained optimization problem in order provide an alternative estimator which satisfies
S_{\pi}(\delta)\leq 1
. This alternative estimator is the Bayes decision for a new problem with a different loss function. Only the general form of this estimator can be given, however. It involves a parameter
\lambda
which, to calculate explicitly, would require knowledge of the particular distributions involved (and thus can be calculated for any given problem, just not in a closed form manner that applies to all problems).

Proposition C.2
.

There exists some
\lambda\geq 0
such that the Bayes decision
\delta_{\pi}
with loss

\widehat{L}(\theta,a)=\begin{cases}L(\theta,a),&\theta\in\Theta_{1},\\
\frac{\pi(\Theta_{0})+\lambda}{\pi(\Theta_{0})}L(\theta,a),&\theta\in\Theta_{0},\end{cases}

(C.5)

satisfies
S_{\pi}(\delta_{\pi})\leq 1
on the original problem.

Proof.

In order to enforce the constraint
S_{\pi}(\delta_{\pi})\leq 1
we introduce the Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
and solve the optimization problem

\min_{\delta}\big\{B_{\pi}(\delta)+\lambda(S_{\pi}(\delta)-1)\big\}.

(C.6)

Expanding, we can collect terms and obtain

\displaystyle B_{\pi}(\delta)+\lambda(S_{\pi}(\delta)-1)

\displaystyle=(\pi(\Theta_{0})+\lambda)\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\theta}}L(\theta,\delta(X))

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\theta}}L(\theta,\delta(X))\pi(\Theta_{1})-\lambda

Appendix D
On risk control vs error probabilities

The formulation of post-hoc hypothesis testing we’ve advanced here changes the kinds of guarantees that an analyst can give concerning the performance of their tests. The currency of our framework is risk control (Definition
2.3
) instead of type-I error control—one obtains a bound on the expected loss instead of the error probability. Given that modern hypothesis testing uses error probabilities, it is reasonable to be skeptical of changing the metric. Here we consider four brief arguments in favor of risk control. To provide a full-throated defense would require its own paper, but it’s worth at least sketching these considerations here.

The first argument is historical. As we’ve discussed,
Wald 1939
noted that standard Neyman-Pearson hypothesis testing is a part of classical statistical decision theory once it is reformulated in terms of loss functions. And decision theory has always been concerned with expected loss as the error metric, due to compelling arguments such as representation theorems
(
Savage 1954
)
, coherence and Dutch books arguments
(
De Finetti 1937
)
, complete class theorems
(
Wald 1950
;
Blackwell and Girshick 1954
)
, amenability to central limit theorems and laws of large numbers, and so on. Thus, our emphasis on risk control can be seen as a return to the roots of hypothesis testing as decision theory, where expected risk is the norm.

The second is a pragmatic argument. As we mentioned in the introduction, there are alternatives to Neyman-Pearson hypothesis testing, the most successful of which is the Bayesian agenda. But if one is fond of the tools of the Neyman-Pearson paradigm—such as significance levels, power, and accept/reject procedures—and seeks to generalize those tools to handle post-hoc analysis, then the formulation of post-hoc hypothesis testing studied here (and in
Grünwald 2024
) appears to be the only option currently on offer. (See Appendix
C
for a longer discussion on Bayesian decision theory and how it relates to our framework.)

The third argument is pedagogical. It is well known that p-values and error probabilities are misunderstood by practitioners, necessitating a near-constant stream of mildly condescending educational articles on their correct use and interpretation. Changing the metric to risk control (and reporting e-values instead of p-values) may be prophylactic, allowing us to leave some of this confusion behind.

Last is a normative argument. Traditional hypothesis testing, with its focus on controlling error probabilities, can mislead practitioners into treating the results as definitive solely based on whether
p\leq\alpha
. This obscures the fundamental uncertainty inherent in any single study. Risk control, by contrast, emphasizes expected loss across many applications of a testing procedure. The guarantee is only that the average loss will be controlled in the long run. It emphasizes that any individual study may be wrong, and one should remain humble when asserting conclusions.
