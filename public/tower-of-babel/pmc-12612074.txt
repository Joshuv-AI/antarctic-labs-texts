Identifying medication use clusters with the R package tame based on dose, timing and ATC codes
Authors: Anna Laksafoss, Jan Wohlfahrt, Anders Hviid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Simplified exposure classifications, such as ever exposed versus never exposed, are commonly used in pharmacoepidemiology. However, this simplification may obscure complex use patterns relevant to researchers. We introduce tame, an R package that offers a novel method for classifying medication use patterns, capturing complexities such as timing, dose, and concurrent medication use in real-world data. The core innovation of tame is its bespoke distance measure, which identifies complex clusters in medication use and is highly adaptable, allowing customization based on the anatomical therapeutic chemical (ATC) Classification System, medication timing, and dose. By prioritizing a robust distance measure, tame ensure accurate and meaningful clustering, enabling researchers to uncover intricate patterns within their data. The package also includes tools for visualizing and applying these clusters to new datasets. In a national Danish cohort study, tame identified nuanced antidepressant use patterns before and during pregnancy, demonstrating its capability to detect complex trends. tame is available on the Comprehensive R Archive Network at [https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=tame] under an MIT license, with a development version on GitHub at [https://github.com/Laksafoss/tame]. tame enhances medication use classification by detecting complex interactions and offering insights into real-world medication usage, thus improving stratification in epidemiological studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23345-9.

Introduction

Developments in the field of machine learning, improvements in computing power, and the increasing richness of data now make it feasible to conduct more nuanced analyses of medication patterns. Central to these analyses is the construction of effective distance measures, which are critical in determining the success of clustering methods. A number of recent studies have explored data-driven approaches to exposure classification. For example, methods such as longitudinal k-means and latent class analysis have been applied to longitudinal medication data to classify exposure trajectories1–5, and these methods have been implemented in software such as the R-packages kml6,7 and lcmm8. However, these existing methods exhibit significant limitations. Primarily, they focus exclusively on drug exposure timing, which restricts their ability to account for the full complexity of medication use patterns. By considering only the timing of drug exposure, these methods overlook other critical factors such as chemical and therapeutic characteristics of the medications involved. When these known medications similarities are ignored, medications must either be treated as independent, or their associations must be learned from data, necessitating more data and specific data characteristics, such as observed medication switching. The narrow focus on timing limits the applicability of these methods, particularly in scenarios where the interplay between different medications and their specific attributes is crucial, such as in studies of drug safety and efficacy. Few developments have been made in approaches that learn from both the exposure timing and the chemical and therapeutic characteristics of the medication(s) used9. To the authors’ knowledge, no publicly available software that learns from this combined information to identify real-world prescription drug use patterns exists.

In this paper, we present a novel distance measure explicitly designed for the clustering of medication use patterns, integrating exposure timing, dose intensity, and medication type based on anatomical therapeutic clustering (ATC) codes. This distance measure is the foundation of our hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) implemented in the `medic()` function in our package tame. HCA is a general and highly adaptable approach to cluster analysis that allows for the clustering of any data, provided that the user can specify the distance between any pair of observations. We developed this distance measure specifically for clustering based on three of the central characteristics of personal medication use: patterns of medication use according to the anatomical therapeutic clustering (ATC) code, exposure timing, and exposure dose. The distance measure allows for flexible specification of the component parts – ATC code, timing and dose – and their relative importance. This method avoids unwanted data reduction or oversimplification of information by defining the distance measure at the person- and medication-specific levels. Furthermore, as medication similarity is explicitly encoded using the hierarchal ATC classification system, this approach removed the need for making the reductive (and clinically unrealistic) assumption of complete medical independence. Here, we applied the method to a Danish nationwide cohort of pregnant patients treated with at least one antidepressant from 0–24 weeks prior to pregnancy onset to map Danish mothers’ use of antidepressants leading up to and during pregnancy.

Implementation

Tame is implemented in R (R Development Core Team 2022) as a package and requires the following R packages: dplyr (≥ 1.1.0), fuzzyjoin, magrittr, purrr, Rfast, rlang, stats, stringr, tibble, tidyr, tidyselect, and Rcpp (≥ 1.0.8). A development version is also available on GitHub [https://github.com/Laksafoss/tame], and collaborators are welcome to fork or make pull requests. The tame package provides methods for clustering medication data by ATC codes, dose and timing; analyzing and illustrating these clusters; and employing these learned clusters to new data in a similar format. These central functionalities are implemented in the functions `medic()`, `summary()`, and `employ()`, along with a few supporting functions. The package also includes a simulated dataset for demonstrating the code.

Clustering analysis

Our medication clustering method, implemented in the `medic()` function, utilizes a novel distance measure within an agglomerative hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) framework. The bespoke distance measure drives the clustering process by quantifying the similarities between two individual-level medication use profiles. The quality, effectiveness, and interpretability of the clustering depend on this measure, making it the critical element of our methodology.

This distance measure is a composite that integrates multiple dimensions of medication information: dose intensity, timing of medication and the ATC classification system. Integrating diverse aspects of medication information enables identification of patterns that are more clinically meaningful and more reflective of real-world medication use than clustering taking only one dimension into account. Timing ensures that temporal relationships in medication usage are preserved, reflecting adherence or shifts in treatment strategies. The ATC code of the medication adds clinical context, linking clusters to therapeutic areas or conditions. Together, these dimensions enhance the potential relevance of the identified clusters to real-world applications, and ensure that known medication similarities are explicitly incorporated into the clustering. Differences in two individual-level medication profiles by dose intensity and timing of medication are measured by a dose trajectory distance measure. This distance measure compares the medication dose at each timepoint in the study and summarizes the dose similarities across time using the Minkowski distance. A formal definition of the dose trajectory measure is given in Definition 1.

The ATC distance measure compares two medications by considering the levels of the medication ATC code. The distance between two medications is determined by identifying the deepest ATC code level that is shared between the two medications. For example, sertraline N06AB06 and clomipramine N06AA04 are both antidepressants (N06A) and are identical at ATC level 3, but sertraline N06AB06 and oxycodone N02AA05 are identical only at ATC level 1, where they share the nervous system main group (N). Thus, the distance between sertraline N06AB06 and clomipramine N06AA04 is shorter than the distance between sertraline N06AB06 and oxycodone N02AA05. Note that if two medications have the same ATC codes, their distance is 0, and they are considered to be the same medication under this distance measure. A formal definition of the ATC distance measure can be found in (Definition 2).

It is important to note that an ATC code does not necessarily only reflect the active chemical substance, but can also reflect the therapeutic use or formulation. Thus, the same medication may be linked to several ATC codes. For example, a medication like Prednisolone, which is used for different indications, has several ATC codes corresponding to distinct therapeutic classes (e.g., corticosteroids for systemic use H02AB06, antihemorrhoidals for topical use C05AA04, etc.). As the ATC distance measure reflects the underlying logic behind the ATC codes, the different ATC codes for Prednisolone use will have non-zero distances.

The dose trajectory distance measure and the ATC distance measure are then combined and balanced using the comparison measure, which is defined in Definition 3, allowing us to compare the medication pattern of two different medications by used by two different people. The multiplicative structure of this measure ensures that medication ATC codes and dose trajectory interact. This means that similarities in dose trajectories lead to an even more similar comparison measure when medications also have similar ATC codes. The interactive structure aligns closely with clinical intuition, allowing dose trajectory similarities to be meaningfully amplified when medications are closely related.

Using the comparison measure as a basis, we then define two central distance measures, referred to as the medic distances (see Definition 4), which provide distinct strategies for comparing individual-level medication use patterns. The first, the average distance measure, computes the average distance across all comparisons between two individuals’ medication use patterns, giving each comparison equal weight. The second, the similar representatives distance measure, calculates the average of the one-sided comparisons between the most similar representatives of each medication pattern.

`medic()`: identifying clusters

The `medic()` function is the central powerhouse of the tame package. This function computes the distances between all pairs of individuals, runs the hierarchical clustering algorithm, and returns the clusters. See Fig. 1 for a visual guide to the steps performed by the `medic()` function, including how it calculates the composite distance measure and applies hierarchical clustering to group individuals based on their medication use profiles.

The function requires that the user provide a dataset where each row encodes personal medication patterns for each medication. Thus, a person exposed to 3 medications has 3 rows in the data. In S6 examples of how data might be structured can be found. The columns in this dataset include the person identification variable, the medication ATC code variable, and, if available, one or more numerical variables encoding the dose and/or exposure trajectory. The user specifies the desired number(s) of clusters with the function input `k`. `employ()`: applying clusters to new data

An important functionality of the package is the `employ()` function, which allows users to apply an existing clustering to a new dataset, which has not been used to identify these clusters. The `employ()` function assigns each new individual in the new dataset to the closest cluster from the original clustering, based on the same distance measure.

This functionality enables the learning of the clustering on a subcohort such that this clustering can be applied to the entire cohort. This approach is especially advantageous for saving computational time by clustering on a representative sub-cohort and then generalizing or when working with a distinct subcohort, which should guide the learning of medication features (e.g., persons with a specific diagnosis or persons exposed to a specific medication of interest). In case–control studies, clusters learned from the medication patterns of cases can be applied to controls to better identify features associated with the outcome. Moreover, this functionality enables easy sharing of learned clusters across studies and countries. Code chunk 3 demonstrates how to apply a learned clustering model to new data using the `employ()` function. The example clusters a ‘train’ dataset and then assigns individuals from a ‘test’ dataset to the closest clusters in the learned model.

This approach assumes that there is sufficient external validity to generalize or transport the original clustering structure to the new dataset, meaning that the distributions of medication usage, dosage, and timing are comparable enough to yield meaningful assignments. Without this generalizability, the method will assign individuals to clusters, which may not fully reflect meaningful patterns in the new data.

Installation

The tame package is available on The Comprehensive R Archive Network (CRAN), and may be installed by running `install.packages(“tame”)` in R ≥ 4.2. Additionally, development versions may be available on GitHub at https://github.com/Laksafoss/tame.

USE

To demonstrate the use and utility of tame, we applied the methodology to medication use immediately before- and during pregnancy in a cohort of Danish women with a history of antidepressant use before pregnancy. We investigated how these medications are used in practice, and how their use is related to redeeming psycholeptics in the first year after pregnancy. Please note that this study primarily serves as an example of how to use the package. For a more extensive example of how the tame methodology may be applied, we refer to a recent study by Gram et al.11 on antidepressant exposure patterns during pregnancy and the risk of adverse outcomes in newborns, which showcases the utility of the approach in analyzing medication patterns and their impact on health outcomes.

Ethics declaration

This study is based solely on data from Danish national registries, which are protected under the Danish Data Protection Act. According to Danish law, ethical approval and informed consent are not required for research studies that exclusively use registry data. Therefore, ethical approval and informed consent were not required for this study. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations under Danish law.

Characterizing antidepressant use before and during pregnancy

We identified all live-born singleton births with a gestational age of at least 36 weeks between 1997 and 2016 in the Danish Medical Birth Registry12. Using the Danish National Prescription Registry13, the cohort was restricted to women who redeemed at least one antidepressant within the six months before pregnancy. To conduct outcome analyses, we also linked this cohort to a number of registries on demographic, socioeconomic and healthcare information; for more information on these variables, see S7. The anonymized individual-level data were accessed on the researcher machines of Denmark Statistics on December 12, 2023.

Assuming an exposure of WHO’s defined daily dose each day, we estimated the time varying antidepressant exposure of each individual from 24 weeks before pregnancy until gestational week 36 in the following medication groups: selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs; ATC codes starting with N06AB), serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs; ATC codes starting with N06AX), and other antidepressants (ATC codes starting with N06AA, N06AF or N06AG). For each individual and each day, antidepressant use was classified as exposed or unexposed (0/1). A description and discussion of the tuning parameters chosen for this example can be found in S3. Seven clusters were used to describe the data.

Figure 2 shows the clustering of antidepressant medication patterns learned from the data using `medic()` and then illustrated with `plot_summary()`. In the first column, the characteristics of the entire population are illustrated, and the following 7 columns display the characteristics of each cluster, highlighting distinct patterns within the data. Each row illustrates a different characteristic: number of antidepressants used per person in the cluster, frequency of use of the 5 most prevalent antidepressant ATC codes in the cluster, average cluster timing of all antidepressant medications, SSRI use, SNRI use, and other antidepressant use.

For the entire study population (column 1), row 1 shows that the majority (76%) of the mothers took only one antidepressant during the study period, one-fifth were exposed to two antidepressants, and even fewer (4%) were exposed to three or more antidepressants. In row 2, four out of five of the most commonly used antidepressants were SSRIs. Rows 3 to 6 show the average medication exposure trajectory among all medications (row 3), among SSRIs (row 4), among SNRIs (row 5), and among other antidepressants (row 6). As we are considering a binary exposure in this example, the average displayed may be interpreted as the percentage of pregnancies exposed at that particular timepoint. The overall average (row 3) shows that at any given week before conception, at least half of the studied women are exposed to one or more antidepressants; then, during the first 12 weeks of pregnancy, antidepressant use decreases. This pattern was consistent across all three groups of antidepressants, but the level of use of each antidepressant group during any given week was different, with SSRIs being used more frequently than SNRI and other antidepressants (rows 3–6). We further describe clusters II, III, and IV here, and all the other clusters in S9.

Cluster II – “Sustained use of one SSRI”: The second cluster, comprising 26% of pregnancies, included almost exclusively SSRI single drug users. The pregnancies in this cluster had a high level of sustained SSRI usage with at least 70% of pregnancies being exposed at any time point in the study period, and with more than 90% of pregnancies being exposed in weeks 0 to 13.

Cluster III – “Concomitant use of different types of antidepressants”: The third largest cluster, with 13% of pregnancies studied, is characterized by concomitant drug use of a mix of all 3 classes of antidepressants. More than 88% of pregnancies are exposed to either an SSRI, an SNRI or both, while only 18% of pregnancies are exposed to other antidepressants. The frequency of SSRI use remains fairly stable across the entire exposure period, as opposed to the frequency of SNRI use, which decreases at the start of pregnancy.

Cluster IV – “Multiple SSRIs used”: Cluster IV, which consisted of 11% of pregnancies was characterized by multiple drug use, with less than 3% using only one drug, almost 88% using 2 antidepressants, and 10% using 3 or more antidepressants. Unlike in cluster III, where pregnancies are exposed to a mix of different types of antidepressants, in this cluster, multiple SSRIs were administered.

Risk of redeeming psycholeptics within one year of birth according to medication cluster membership

To provide an example of how to use these learned medication clusters in further analysis, we applied them to analyze the adjusted cumulative incidence of adverse psychiatric postpartum outcomes. In this example, redemption of psycholeptics (ATC group N05; anti-psychotics N05A, anxiolytics N05B, and hypnotics and sedatives N05C) in the first year following birth is considered the outcome, as it serves as a concrete indicator of psychiatric distress and healthcare utilization, directly impacting maternal and child well-being.

We used stabilized inverse probability of treatment weighting. Weights were calculated as the inverse odds of being assigned to medication clusters I, III, IV, V, VI or VII versus medication cluster II adjusted for confounders (see S9 for the full list) in a multinomial regression model. Cluster II (sustained use of one SSRI throughout the exposure period) was chosen as the reference cluster because it is a large cluster (26% of pregnancies) with a simple interpretation: The pregnancies in this cluster exhibit stable, high levels of sustained SSRI usage, with over 70% exposed at any point during the study period and more than 90% exposed during the first trimester. This consistent use of a single SSRI offers a straightforward, well-defined category against which other clusters with more variable or complex medication patterns can be compared.

Discussion

The tame package implements a novel data-driven learning method for understanding individual-level medication patterns through hierarchical clustering and provides tools for illustrating and characterizing these clusters. The construction of a distance measure is the cornerstone of any clustering method, as it dictates the method’s ability to accurately group similar observations and differentiate dissimilar ones. In machine learning, the quality of a distance measure directly impacts the success of the model. Our novel distance measure, specifically tailored for medication data, uniquely incorporates the ATC code, exposure timing, and medication dosage, allowing researchers to fine-tune the relative importance of these factors. This flexibility is crucial for ensuring that the clusters reflect the true nature of the data and the research objectives. Moreover, a method for applying existing medication clusters to new medication pattern data has been developed.

Here, we demonstrated how tame can help identify and narrow down complex trends in antidepressant use before- and during pregnancy, using a national Danish cohort. In this example, medication exposure was estimated by assuming that all women took the general reference dose of WHO’s defined daily dose and used the entire filled prescription. While this approach provides a useful approximation of medication use, we acknowledge that some people may discontinue antidepressant use upon learning of their pregnancy, which could result in overestimating the duration of medication exposure, particularly in the early stages of pregnancy. Conversely, some pregnant individuals may deliberately reduce their antidepressant dose to achieve the desired therapeutic effect at a minimal dose, potentially leading to an underestimation of the exposed time. In general, clustering real-world medication data offers a valuable automated search method for detecting potential safety signals or adverse drug interactions that may not have been previously suspected. Unlike traditional approaches that rely on specific suspicions or hypotheses, clustering allows for an exploratory analysis of medication patterns. For example, through clustering, researchers may discover that a particular cluster, such as medications X and Y frequently taken together, is associated with an elevated risk of adverse outcomes. This automated identification of medications and medication combinations that pose potential risks can serve as an early warning system, prompting further investigation into these specific medication combinations and their effects.

Moreover, real-world medication patterns captured through clustering analysis reflect the diverse medication usage habits of individuals outside controlled clinical trial settings. This real-world complexity adds depth to our understanding of medication usage and its implications. Additionally, these patterns can be leveraged for statistical adjustment or stratification in epidemiological studies. By accounting for these real-world medication patterns, researchers can better control for confounding factors and obtain more accurate estimates of treatment effects in epidemiological studies and observational research.

When using this method, specifying tuning parameters provides great customizability but requires decisions from the researcher. Thus, tuning the distance measure will still have to be performed in accordance with the understanding of the studied medication and the clinical setting. However, methods for algorithmically optimizing the number of clusters and assisting in the choice of linkage according to measures of goodness of fit are currently underway.

Naturally, clustering personal medication usage according to ATC codes is limited by the ATC classification system itself. The ATC hierarchy is classified according to the main therapeutic use or mechanism of action of the main active ingredient and, as such, encode the main indication of the drug. However, many medications are used for multiple indications but are assigned only one ATC code according to the main indication. This can obscure the full spectrum of a medication’s applications. Moreover, an ATC group may be specified according to the mechanism of action, resulting in groups in which the medications have diverse indications, thereby complicating the interpretation of clustering results. In addition, a chemical substance may be given more than one ATC code if it is used for two different therapeutic purposes. For a detailed introduction to the construction of the WHO ATC classification system, see14. Thus, as the ATC classification system classifies medication according to both the therapeutic and the pharmacological aspects of a medication, the same is true of the classification method presented in this paper. Methods for extending the ATC distance measure to allow for user defined exceptions or additions to the ATC classification system are in progress. This extension will allow the user to give all medications with identical active ingredients a shorter distance, regardless of the distance in the ATC hierarchy, or to make combination products more similar than the ATC hierarchy suggests.

In addition, a central feature of hierarchical clustering, and many other clustering methods, is that all observations are assigned a cluster. Thus, if the data contain many outliers, which in fact are not comparable with other observations in the data, they will still be placed in clusters. Moreover, the assignment to a cluster is performed in a deterministic way, and the method does not provide uncertainty estimates for this assignment. These features of clustering lead to decreased robustness to changes in the study population. When using tame, the `employ()` function can be used to gain some insight into the extent of this problem in a given dataset through cross validation type strategies.

Finally, clustering methods can be computationally time consuming and demanding of RAM. This means that we may still be limited in our applications of this method to large cohorts with wide medication use due to our computational power. As discussed in S4, this central limitation has informed multiple design aspects of the code itself, and computationally intensive parts of the code are written in C + + or utilize code written in Fortran. This approach works to ensure a relatively respectable computational time for our example with just over 33,000 antidepressant exposed pregnancies of less than 10 min on a system with an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6254 CPU @ 3.10 GHz.

Conclusions

In summary, the tame package improves upon classical approaches for understanding drug exposure by clustering complex, real-world use patterns that integrate information on timing, dose, and type of medication. Here, we described how we used this method to understand antidepressant use up to and during pregnancy and then analyzed how these learned clusters were associated with the use of psycholeptics in the first year following birth. In the future, this method may be used to understand other patterns of prescription drug use and optimize drug safety surveillance through data driven learning.
