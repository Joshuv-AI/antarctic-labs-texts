Full recovery after severe bacterial encephalitis and brain abscess following deep brain stimulation surgery for Parkinson's disease
Authors: Eline AMY Rouleau, Debby EIC Falck, Carel FE Hoffmann, Victor J Geraedts, Rodi Zutt, Niels A van der Gaag, M Fiorella Contarino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEncephalitis and cerebral abscesses are rare, serious complications of deep brain stimulation (DBS) surgery. We report on two patients with Parkinson's disease (PD) who developed a critical condition with focal neurological symptoms and decreased consciousness, after an initially uncomplicated subthalamic nucleus DBS implantation. Because of the clinical and radiological suspicion of encephalitis and brain abscess, DBS was explanted and the abscess drained. After prolonged antibiotic treatment they gradually fully recovered, with persistently improved PD symptoms in patient 1. Concluding, patients with DBS-related encephalitis or brain abscesses in a critical condition can have a favourable prognosis with proper and timely treatment.Keywords: Parkinson's disease, neurosurgery, deep brain stimulation, complication, infection

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is increasingly used to treat movement disorders including Parkinson's disease (PD). 1 Intracranial haemorrhage, hardware malfunctions, skin erosions and infections can complicate the surgery. Infections occur often around the internal pulse generator or the extension wires. Intracranial infections are rare, but potentially serious. 2 We report on two PD patients, who experienced encephalitis and brain abscess after DBS surgery. Our surgical protocol with micro-electrode recordings includes wet scrubbing, single gloving (replacement every two hours) and preoperative antibiotics (repeated every four hours intraoperatively). Wounds are closed in layers. Mean operation time is around 240 min.

Patient 1 underwent uncomplicated bilateral subthalamic nucleus (STN) DBS surgery for motor fluctuations and medication-resistant tremor. She did not have comorbidities. Two weeks after, wounds were clean. One month post-surgery, she presented to another hospital with a headache, decreased consciousness, aphasia, right-sided hemiparesis and a purulent left-sided head wound. She had negative CT, increased infection parameters, and polymorph pleiocytosis in cerebrospinal fluid. Treatment with amoxicillin, ceftazidime and flucloxacillin was initiated. At day 5, imaging revealed an air-filled cavity surrounding the left electrode, oedema and midline shift, suggesting gas-producing bacteria (Figure 1(a)). Metronidazole was added. The DBS system was explanted, with initial clinical improvement. However, at day 10–12, she deteriorated. CT showed lesion increase and an abscess in the left lenticular nucleus. Drainage was stereotactically performed. The wound culture showed flucloxacillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pyogenes. Two weeks post-drainage, MRI showed unchanged abscesses’ size (Figure 1(b)). She slowly improved and 2.5 months after onset was discharged to a rehabilitation centre, with full recovery 6 weeks thereafter. She completed 12 weeks of intravenous (IV) flucloxacillin in total. Follow-up MRI 7 months after, demonstrated residual white matter lesions around the left lead trajectory and residual T2 hyperintensity in the left pallidal area (Figure 1(c1–2)). Notably, PD symptoms remained improved at 12-month follow-up (MDS-UPDRS-III scores off/on medication 14/3 (no right-sided tremor or rigidity, and minor bradykinesia on the right) vs preoperative 39/14 (and bilateral tremor during monopolar review)). Levodopa Equivalent Daily Dose (LEDD) was 300 (pre-DBS: 200). She did not wish re-implantation.

Patient 2 underwent uncomplicated bilateral STN-DBS surgery for medication-resistant tremor. He suffered from chronic lymphocytic leukaemia, now in complete remission. A low platelet count (99 × 109/l) was successfully improved preoperatively by immunoglobulin (IVIG) infusion, dexamethasone, and platelet transfusion. He also reported recurrent urinary tract infections with Klebsiella pneumonia ESBL(+), sensitive to gentamicin, tobramycin, and fosfomycin as tested 3 months pre-surgery. Therefore, we administered fosfomycin and gentamycin preoperatively instead of routine flucloxacillin. One day post-surgery, he developed seizures, aphasia, supranuclear gaze palsy, right-sided hemiparesis and neglect. Wounds appeared clean and he was afebrile with low infection parameters. CT showed a small left-sided peri-electrode hematoma and peri-electrode oedema (Figure 1(d)). After 7 days on anti-epileptics, he deteriorated with slightly increased infection parameters. MRI showed oedema around both leads with a left T1-hyperintensity with minimal ring enhancement (Figure 1(e)). Suspecting cerebritis with abscess, the DBS system was explanted. A culture of the explanted leads revealed Staphylococcus epidermidis and Klebsiella pneumonia ESBL(+), sensitive to meropenem and tobramycin. The initial treatment with metronidazole and ceftriaxone was switched to meropenem. After further deterioration, CT showed bifrontal oedema and a right-sided cortical hematoma (Figure 1(f)). The planned trajectory was not close to vascular structures and there was no mismatch with the postoperative placement. In the following 2 weeks, he slowly recovered and was discharged to a rehabilitation centre 2 months after explant, with full recovery 1.5 months thereafter, after a total of 5-month IV meropenem. Four weeks after stopping treatment, MRI showed minimal bilateral residual subcortical oedema (Figure 1(g)). One year post-surgery, MDS-UPDRS-III score on medication was 48 (preoperative: 33) with unchanged LEDD (875). He did not wish DBS re-implantation.

While DBS-related infections occur in approximately 5% of cases, with prevalence varying up to ± 20%, 3 encephalitis and brain abscesses are rare.4–7 The most common bacteria involved in post-DBS infections are Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus epidermidis and Propionibacterium acnes. 3 Most intracranial infections originate from a subcutaneous infection along the extension wires and leads. 4 Onset varies from days to months or even years postoperatively.4–6 Staphylococcus aureus infections are often more aggressive and start earlier.4,8 While in patient 1 the infection probably spread intracranially from the head wound, in patient 2, we assumed that Klebsiella pneumoniae spread endogenously from the colonized urinary tract, despite specific pre-treatment. Community-acquired brain abscesses caused by Klebsiella pneumoniae are rare, accounting for 1–2% of intracerebral infections in Western countries and 10% in Asia. 9 Klebsiella pneumoniae can also cause post-neurosurgical meningitis and encephalitis, 10 but DBS-related encephalitis and brain abscesses have not been previously described.

When suspecting infection after DBS surgery, treatment should be started promptly, to minimize the chance of spreading. In case of brain infections, it is advised to remove the DBS system completely or partially (depending on primary infection site), take cultures and start antibiotic treatment. In patient 2, the ESBL-producing Klebsiella Pneumoniae had developed new antibiotic resistance, 11 which resulted in inefficacious pre-treatment. This shows that in patients with recurrent infections, antibiotic sensitivity must be regularly monitored and the infection risk should be extensively discussed with the patient.

After the infection resolution, re-implantation of the DBS system could be considered,5,6 but persistent bacterial colonisation, as in patient 2, may be a contraindication.

The fatality rate of brain abscesses is about 10%, while full recovery rate is up to 70%. The fatality rate for post-neurosurgical encephalitis and brain abscesses is up to 20%. 12 Both our patients recovered completely, with one showing persistently improved PD symptoms, probably due to a lesion-like effect, as also reported after pallidal strokes. 13

In conclusion, we present two cases of post-DBS encephalitis and brain abscess, likely due to different mechanisms of spread, both causing critical conditions. Both recovered completely after DBS explant, prolonged antibiotic treatment and support therapy.

Prompt recognition and treatment of postsurgical infections is crucial; importantly, even after critical clinical conditions, the prognosis can be favourable, although recovery may take long.
