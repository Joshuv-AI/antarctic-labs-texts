Abstract
PARSEC v2.0 rotating stellar tracks were previously presented for six values of metallicity from subsolar to solar values, with initial rotation rates ( ω i \omega_{\mathrm{i}} , defined as the ratio of angular velocity and its critical value) spanning from the non-rotating case to very near the critical velocity (i.e. ω i = 0.99 \mbox{$\omega_{\mathrm{i}}$}=0.99 ), and for initial masses covering the ∼ 0.7 ​ M ⊙ \sim 0.7\penalty\ \mbox{$\mathrm{M}_{\odot}$} to 14 ​ M ⊙ 14\penalty\ \mbox{$\mathrm{M}_{\odot}$} interval. Furthermore, we provided the corresponding isochrones converted into several photometric systems, for different inclination angles between the line-of-sight and the rotation axes, from 0 ∘ 0^{\circ} (pole-on) to 90 ∘ 90^{\circ} (equator-on). In this work, we expand this database with seven other sets of metallicity, including five sets of low metallicity ( Z = 0.0001 − 0.002 Z=0.0001-0.002 ) and two sets of super-solar values (up to Z = 0.03 Z=0.03 ). Here, we present the new stellar tracks, comprising ∼ \sim 3 040 tracks in total ( ∼ \sim 5 500 including previous sets), along with the new corresponding rotating isochrones. We also introduce the possibility of creating isochrones, by interpolation, for values of rotating rates not available in the initial set of tracks. We compare a selection of our new models with rotating stellar tracks from the Geneva Stellar Evolution Code, and we assess the quality of our new tracks by fitting the colour-magnitude diagram of the open cluster NGC 6067. We take advantage of the projected rotational velocity of member stars measured by Gaia to validate our results and examine the surface oxygen abundances in comparison with the observed data. All newly computed stellar tracks and isochrones are retrievable via our dedicated web databases and interfaces.



1
Introduction

Rotation is known to play an important role in stellar structure and evolution.
It induces two main effects, which are the departure from the spherical shape and the enhancement of mixing processes. The first effect is due to the centrifugal forces that can lead to strong geometrical distortion of the stellar surface, directly affecting the photometry of stars and their position in the colour-magnitude diagram (CMD). This aspect has been studied by several authors in the literature
(see
Bastian & de Mink 2009
;
Yang et al. 2013
;
Girardi et al. 2019
, and references therein)
. It has been claimed that it may be the explanation for some peculiar properties of young and intermediate-age stellar clusters, such as the main sequence (MS) split
(
Milone et al. 2017
;
Costa et al. 2019a
;
He et al. 2022
)
and the extended MS turn-off
(
Bastian & de Mink 2009
;
Brandt & Huang 2015
;
Milone et al. 2018
;
Cordoni et al. 2018
)
.
The second important effect is due to rotational instabilities such as meridional circulation and shear instability
(
Maeder 2009
;
Meynet & Maeder 2000
;
Chieffi & Limongi 2013
, see)
which transport chemical elements across the stable radiative layers of the stars. This extra mixing enriches the stellar surface with processed elements and provides fresh fuel to the core, consequently increasing the MS lifetimes, and thus core mass and luminosity in the post-MS phases.

These significant effects of rotation have been included in some libraries of stellar evolutionary tracks such as, for instance, those derived from the
GENEC

(
Ekström et al. 2012
;
Georgy et al. 2012
;
Georgy et al. 2013
;
Groh et al. 2019
;
Yusof et al. 2022
;
Sibony et al. 2024
)
,
STAREVOL

(
Amard et al. 2019
;
Borisov et al. 2024
)
,
MESA

(
Paxton et al. 2019
;
Hastings et al. 2023
)
,
FRANEC

(
Limongi & Chieffi 2018
)
and Bonn
(
Brott et al. 2011
)
evolutionary codes.
Rotation was also introduced in the PAdova and tRieste Stellar Evolutionary Code (
PARSEC
) in its version 2.0
(
Costa et al. 2019a
;
Costa et al. 2019b
;
Girardi et al. 2019
)
. A large grid of
PARSEC
evolutionary tracks and isochrones for rotating stars was presented in
Nguyen et al. 2022
, comprising six values of initial metallicity (
Z
, in mass fraction) going from 0.004 to 0.017. Those tracks were released together with the corresponding isochrones in many different photometric systems for seven values of initial rotation rates
\omega_{\mathrm{i}}
. We recall that
\omega_{\mathrm{i}}
is defined as the surface angular velocity divided by the critical angular break-up velocity at which the centrifugal forces cause the equatorial layers to become detached from the star.
In this work, our goal is to expand the
PARSEC v2.0
database with seven more sets of metallicity, thus covering the range from
Z=0.0001
to
0.03
.
Moreover, an improved interpolation method
(
Ettorre et al. 2025
, already used in)
allows us to interpolate between any two values of
\omega_{\mathrm{i}}
. Therefore, we can now provide isochrones with any
\omega_{\mathrm{i}}
in the interval
0.00-0.99
.

This paper is organised as follows. Sect.
2
summarises the input physics adopted to compute the stellar tracks. Sects.
3
and
4
illustrate the tracks and isochrones newly computed. Sect.
5
shows the comparison with the observed data of the open cluster NGC 6067, and draws some final conclusions.

2
Input physics

The input physics of our models is as described in Section 2 of
86
. For the sake of clarity, we summarise the most important ingredients in this section.

Rotation was first implemented in
PARSEC
by
Costa et al. 2019b
.
They carefully investigated the concurrence of convective core overshoot and rotation in intermediate-mass stars and, by analysing a rich data sample of double-lined eclipsing binaries
(
Claret & Torres 2016
;
Claret & Torres 2017
;
Claret & Torres 2018
;
Claret & Torres 2019
)
, they retrieved a calibrated core overshooting efficiency parameter of
\lambda_{\mathrm{ov}}=0.4
.
In the framework of the ballistic overshooting approach of
Bressan et al. 1981
, this efficiency parameter corresponds to an averaged overshooting distance of
d_{\rm ov}\sim 0.2\penalty\ H_{\mathrm{P}}
, where
H_{\mathrm{P}}
is the local pressure scale height. This value should be suitable for all stars that have already developed a fully convective core. In this regard, we divide the range of mass into three subintervals with different values of
\lambda_{\mathrm{ov}}
: stars with
M_{\mathrm{i}}<M_{\mathrm{O1}}
have radiative cores in the MS thus no convective overshoot is considered, stars with
M_{\mathrm{i}}\geq M_{\mathrm{O2}}
instead have a fully convective core thus the maximum overshooting efficiency (
\lambda_{\mathrm{ov}}=0.4
) is used, while stars in the interval
M_{\mathrm{O1}}\leq M_{\mathrm{i}}\leq M_{\mathrm{O2}}
have a developing convective core, hence we apply an overshooting efficiency that linearly increases with the initial mass from
0
to
0.4
. The adopted values of
M_{\mathrm{O1}}
and
M_{\mathrm{O2}}
at each metallicity are summarised in Table
1
. The same strategy is adopted for the convective envelope overshooting, with the minimum value
\Lambda_{\mathrm{e}}=0.5
being adopted at
M_{\mathrm{i}}<M_{\mathrm{O1}}
, and the maximum
\Lambda_{\mathrm{e}}=0.7
at
M_{\mathrm{i}}\geq M_{\mathrm{O2}}

(
Alongi et al. 1991
;
Fu et al. 2018
, see)
.

In
PARSEC v2.0
, the transport of angular momentum and chemical species is treated with a diffusive approach, as detailed in
86
. Moreover, the shellular rotation scheme is adopted with the constant angular velocity (
\Omega=
constant) along the surface of each isobar
(
Maeder 2009
, see)
.

Rotation is parametrised by its rotation rate,
\omega=\Omega/\Omega_{\mathrm{c}}
, where
\Omega
is the angular velocity, and
\Omega_{\mathrm{c}}=(2/3)^{3/2}\left(GM/R_{\mathrm{pol}}^{3}\right)^{1/2}
is its critical value. In this expression,
G
is the gravitational constant and
M,R_{\mathrm{pol}}
are the stellar mass and polar radius. In
PARSEC v2.0
, rotation sets in in a few models before the zero-age main sequence (ZAMS), assuming a solid body profile with
\omega=\omega_{\mathrm{i}}
.
From the ZAMS, the angular velocity evolves as the star evolves, following the angular momentum transport, the momentum loss due to stellar winds (or mechanical removal in case of critical rotation), and ensuring the conservation of angular momentum at each time-step.

Moreover, we adopt the same methodology to impose a maximum initial rotation rate depending on the initial mass, following the observational results that low- and very low-mass stars show evidence of modest or no rotation
(
McQuillan et al. 2014
, see)
. Therefore, models with
M_{\mathrm{i}}
\leq

M_{\mathrm{O1}}
are computed with
\omega_{\mathrm{i}}=0.00
only.
For models with higher initial mass (
M_{\mathrm{i}}
\geq

M_{\mathrm{O2}}
) we compute tracks with all values of rotational rate.
For those masses with
M_{\mathrm{O1}}

\leq

M_{\mathrm{i}}
<

M_{\mathrm{O2}}
, we consider only some values of initial rotation rate, up to a maximum value, computed as

\omega_{\mathrm{i,max}}(M_{\mathrm{i}})=0.99\left(\frac{M_{\mathrm{i}}-M_{\mathrm{O1}}}{M_{\mathrm{O2}}-M_{\mathrm{O1}}}\right).

Mass loss becomes critically important in rotating stars, as it is enhanced by rotational velocity
(
Friend & Abbott 1986
;
Bjorkman & Cassinelli 1993
)
. In
PARSEC v2.0
models, mass loss is applied throughout the stellar evolution. The rotating mass loss rate is enhanced by a factor from its non-rotating counterpart,

\displaystyle\dot{M}(\omega)=\dot{M}(\omega=0)\left(1-\frac{v}{v_{\mathrm{crit}}}\right)^{-0.43},

(2)

where
v
is the surface tangential velocity, and
v_{\mathrm{crit}}
is its critical value
v_{\mathrm{crit}}=(2GM/3R_{\mathrm{pol}})^{1/2}
.
\dot{M}(\omega=0)
is the mass loss rate of non-rotating models. Several mass loss prescriptions are adopted in different mass ranges for
\dot{M}(\omega=0)
. In particular, for low-mass tracks the empirical relation
\dot{M}\propto\eta M^{-1}L^{1.5}T_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-2}
from
Reimers 1975
;
Reimers 1977
is adopted, with the efficiency coefficient
\eta=0.2

(
Miglio et al. 2012
)
. For intermediate and massive tracks, the mass loss rates from
de Jager et al. 1988
and
Vink et al. 2001
are adopted, respectively. They are both corrected by a factor that depends on the surface metallicity, that is,
\dot{M}\propto(Z/Z_{\odot})^{0.85}

(
Costa et al. 2025
, see also)
.

As in
86
, we adopt the solar-scaled compositions of
Caffau et al. 2011
, except for the primordial elements. In short, the initial mass fraction of any metal
i
,
X_{i}
, scales linearly with
Z
, that is
X_{i}=Z\frac{X_{i,\odot}}{Z_{\odot}}
, with
Z_{\odot}=0.01524
.
We derive the initial He content from the enrichment law
Y=Y_{p}+\frac{\Delta Y}{\Delta Z}Z
, where
Y_{p}=0.2485
is the primordial He content
(
Komatsu et al. 2011
)
, and the helium-to-metal enrichment ratio
\Delta Y/\Delta Z=1.78
is based on the solar calibration from
Bressan et al. 2012
.
On the other hand, for the three primordial elements:
3
He is taken as a fraction of
9\times 10^{-5}
of the He content
Y
. For deuterium and
7
Li, we distinguish between two cases: for
Z<0.001
we adopt the Big Bang-nucleosynthesis abundances (namely,
X_{\mathrm{D}}\approx 4.23\times 10^{-5}
and
X_{\mathrm{{}^{7}Li}}\approx 2.72\times 10^{-9}
, see
Coc et al. 2012
), whereas for
Z>Z_{\odot}
we use the meteoritic values (namely,
X_{\mathrm{D}}\approx 2.12\times 10^{-5}
and
X_{\mathrm{{}^{7}Li}}\approx 1.09\times 10^{-8}
). In the intermediate regime the mass fractions are computed by a linear interpolation between these values.
Meanwhile, grids of low metallicities tracks computed with
\alpha
-enhanced mixtures
(
Fu et al. 2018
)
are reserved for the coming releases.

We use an updated nuclear reaction network for a total of 72 different reactions which include the p-p chains,
the CNO tri-cycle, the Ne-Na and Mg-Al chains, the
12
C,
16
O
and
20
Ne burning reactions, and the
\alpha
-capture reactions
up to
56
Ni
(see
Bressan et al. 2012
;
Fu et al. 2018
;
Costa et al. 2021
, for details)
.
The transport of energy by convection is described by the mixing length theory of
Böhm 1958
, with the solar calibrated mixing-length parameter
\alpha_{\mathrm{MLT}}=1.74
from
Bressan et al. 2012
.

Finally, in agreement with
PARSEC v2.0
tracks already released in
86
, we compute seven initial rotation rates (that are,
\omega_{\mathrm{i}}
= 0.0, 0.30, 0.60, 0.80, 0.90, 0.95, 0.99).
More detailed information is provided in the following Section.

3
Stellar tracks

All the tracks start at the pre-main sequence (PMS) phase and end at a stage that depends on the initial mass: at an age that exceeds the Hubble time for very low mass; at the initial stages of the thermally-pulsing asymptotic giant branch (TP-AGB) for low- and intermediate-mass stars; or at carbon exhaustion for more massive stars.
We denote
M_{\rm HeF}
as the value of initial mass of the most massive star undergoing the He-flash at the tip of the red giant branch (RGB) phase. This is also the initial mass value discriminating between low- and intermediate-mass stars, and its dependence on the initial metallicity and rotational rate is summarized in Table
2
.

It should be noted that the
\mbox{$\omega_{\mathrm{i}}$}=0
models with
M_{\mathrm{i}}\leq M_{\mathrm{HeF}}
are interrupted at the beginning of the He flash, and separately resumed at the zero-age horizontal branch with the same He core mass and surface chemical compositions as the last red giant branch (RGB) model. The same models are then used for all
\mbox{$\omega_{\mathrm{i}}$}>0
sequences of the same initial metallicity.
This procedure is a first approximation needed to build the complete isochrones.
On the other hand, this procedure ignores the changes in surface chemical composition due to rotation in the previous MS, at least for the core He-burning stars in the limited mass interval between
M_{\mathrm{O1}}
and
M_{\mathrm{HeF}}
. The impact of rotation at this evolutionary stage will be addressed in the coming work.

In the following, we discuss the impact of rotation on the photometric properties of stars (Sect.
3.1
) and on their surface chemical abundances (Sect.
3.2
). Then, we compare our tracks with models from other available databases (Sect.
3.3
).

As the stars evolve along the MS, due to the transport of angular momentum, the surface angular velocity is increased and thus the rotation rate. It reaches the highest value around the turn-off region.
After the MS, the core contracts and the envelope significantly expands, leading to a significant decrease in the surface angular velocity due to the angular momentum conservation.
As low-mass stars evolve further along the red giant branch (RGB), increasing their radius, the surface velocity drops rapidly while the angular velocity of the inner core increases
(
Cantiello et al. 2014
;
Nguyen et al. 2022
, see)
.

During the MS, the intermediate-mass rotating stars become more luminous than their non-rotating counterparts, due to the significant increase in the core mass caused by rotational mixing. It also causes the increase of the core radius and MS lifetime. For example, in the case of
M_{\mathrm{i}}=6\mbox{$\mathrm{M}_{\odot}$}
, the track with
\mbox{$\omega_{\mathrm{i}}$}=0.99
has the MS lifetime
1.37
times larger than its non-rotating counterpart. At the same time, it raises approximately
0.22\mbox{$\mathrm{M}_{\odot}$}
and
0.043\mbox{$\mathrm{R}_{\odot}$}
in core mass and radius.
This increase is illustrated in Fig.
2
, for models of varying initial mass and
\omega_{\mathrm{i}}
.
This also has consequences for their post-MS phases, although their surface velocity evolves similarly to the case of low-mass stars.
Later on, in the He-burning phase (or blue loop), the contraction of their envelope leads to an increase of
\omega
.

3.2
Impact of rotation on surface abundances

)

(

evolutionary tracks provide predictions for the surface abundances of several elements (from
1

60

The first change in surface abundances occurs at the beginning of the red giant branch when the downward extension of the convective envelope causes the first dredge up (1DU).
As a consequence, the nuclear burned products
\mathrm{{}^{13}C}
and
\mathrm{{}^{14}N}
are brought up to the surface, while
\mathrm{{}^{12}C}
is depleted

When rotation is considered, rotational mixing is established. In the case of intermediate-mass stars where the CNO cycle is the main burning channel (e.g. for the
6\penalty\ \mbox{$\mathrm{M}_{\odot}$}
star in Fig.
3
), a significant decrease of the
12
C
/^{13}
C and
12
C
/^{14}
N ratios from their initial values can be seen during the MS phase. The later evolution shows further depletion mainly due to the 1DU. In general, the decrease is stronger for higher initial rotational rate.
The situation is different in the case of low-mass stars, where the pp-chains are the main burning channels during the MS, and rotational mixing becomes insignificant: overall, rotation has little impact on the variation of these ratios. This holds true also for the oxygen abundance (Fig.
3
d).
Instead, rotational mixing in intermediate-mass stars causes surface oxygen to be depleted far below the values predicted by the non-rotating 1DU models (Fig.
3
i).
More extreme rotation rates show even more significant depletions. This fact can possibly provide a good explanation for the chemically anomalous stars observed in many open clusters
(
Takeda et al. 2018
;
Lombardo et al. 2021
;
Casamiquela et al. 2022
, e.g.)
.

On the other hand, Fig.
3
e and j show the variation of surface lithium. Due to rotational mixing, a significant amount of lithium can be transferred from the envelope to the inner layers, where it is eventually destroyed at temperatures of
\sim 2.6\times 10^{6}
K.
The efficiency of Li depletion increases with the initial rotation rate, mass, and eventually to the metallicity, as will be shown below. Indeed, many works suggest the need for rotation in order to explain the observed abundances of lithium in supergiant stars
(
Lyubimkov et al. 2012
;
Magrini et al. 2021
;
Fanelli et al. 2022
, e.g.)
.
However, the discrepancy between the primordial value
(
Coc et al. 2014
, A(Li)=2.69;)
and the Spite plateau
(
Spite & Spite 1982
, A(Li)=2.3;)
measured from dwarf-MS low-mass metal-poor stars – which is commonly known as the “cosmological lithium problem” – requires extra mixing mechanisms in the early evolution up to the terminal-age mains sequence (TAMS) to explain it
(see
Richard et al. 2005
;
Fu et al. 2015
;
Nguyen et al. 2025
, for references)
. We clarify that, in the present release,
PARSEC v2.0
uses the standard schemes for extra mixing, that is, classical atomic diffusion
(
Bressan et al. 2012
)
and a fixed value for the efficiency of envelope overshooting.
Therefore, it is reasonable to expect that additional physics needs to be implemented into our models in order to accurately reproduce the Li abundance observed in low-mass, metal-poor stars.

4
Isochrones

In
86
, isochrones were derived for the same seven values of
\omega_{\mathrm{i}}
at which evolutionary tracks were computed. This involves interpolating in the plane of initial mass and age defined by every grid of evolutionary tracks of a given
\omega_{\mathrm{i}}
. Interpolation between tracks of different metallicities was also allowed.

Since then, we improved the interpolation algorithms of the
TRILEGAL
code
(
Girardi et al. 2005
;
Marigo et al. 2017
)
, so as to also allow for the interpolation between grids with different
\omega_{\mathrm{i}}
. The interpolation method is essentially the same one as described by
Bertelli et al. 2008
to produce isochrones at different values of metallicity and helium content. It means that isochrones can now be produced for any
\omega_{\mathrm{i}}
value in the interval from 0.00 to 0.99. Such a new interpolation scheme is now available at the CMD v3.8 web interface
3
3

3

http://stev.oapd.inaf.it/cgi-bin/cmd_3.8
.

To test this new interpolation algorithm, we have produced a number of isochrones of a given
\mbox{$\omega_{\mathrm{i}}$}=0.90
and
Z=0.006
that are computed: 1) with the actual set of computed tracks, and 2) by the interpolation between two neighbouring values of
\mbox{$\omega_{\mathrm{i}}$}=0.80
and
0.95
. Fig.
7
shows the produced isochrones by the two methods for ages
\log t/\mathrm{yr}=8.0-9.5
. As can be appreciated, the isochrones produced by the interpolation algorithm are very similar to those produced directly from the
\mbox{$\omega_{\mathrm{i}}$}=0.90
tracks. Small differences can be found just at the turn-off region, with the level of
\Delta T_{\mathrm{eff}}=0.006
dex (upper right panel), and at the transition mass at which rotation is assumed to start being efficient (bottom right panel). It is expected that the typical errors caused by the interpolation in
\omega_{\mathrm{i}}
will be smaller than these indicative examples.

With the new tracks, we computed the corresponding isochrones, from which we also derive absolute magnitudes by applying the bolometric corrections (BCs) by
Girardi et al. 2019
.
Such BCs are suitably computed for rotating stars as a function of the stellar inclination
i
. They are made available for the passbands of many different photometric systems
(
Chen et al. 2019
, see)
.

We note that the inclination-dependent BCs described in
Girardi et al. 2019
cannot be computed for the coolest stars due to the limitations of present-day libraries of model atmospheres. This is less of a problem in our case because stars that initially rotate rapidly have their
\omega
reduced to values of
\sim\!0.1
once they reach a mean effective temperature cooler than
\sim\!5000
K. Such stars have just minimal deviations from spherical symmetry, and they can be described by a single
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
value. Therefore, for
\mbox{$T_{\mathrm{eff}}$}<5130
K, we adopt the BCs derived from non-rotating models (hence derived from plan-parallel model atmospheres, as in
Girardi et al. 2002
). In the interval from 5370 to 5130 K, we smoothly interpolate between the BCs derived from rotating stars and those derived from non-rotating stars.

Finally, a brief explanation of the quantities presented in the isochrone tables can be found at
4

4

https://stev.oapd.inaf.it/cmd_3.8/help.html
. For the quantities presented in the track tables, see
5
5

5

https://stev.oapd.inaf.it/PARSEC/Database/PARSECv2.0_VMS/readme.html
.

5
Comparison with observations

In order to verify the quality of our models we compare them with observations of the young cluster NGC 6067. The age of this cluster is estimated between 50 and 150 Myr
(
Thackeray et al. 1962
;
Santos & Bica 1993
;
Majaess et al. 2013
)
, and the metallicity varies from solar to supersolar value; for instance,

derived a mean metallicity of

Alonso-Santiago et al. 2017
suggested a mean value of

Alonso-Santiago et al. 2017
claimed
\mathrm{[Fe/H]=0.09\pm 0.11}
dex, and
Genovali et al. 2014
claimed
\mathrm{[Fe/H]=0.07\pm 0.1}
dex. The reddening and distance modulus of NGC 6067 are also well constrained from the literature. Recent studies show a reddening

(

;
Jackson et al. 2020
;
Jackson et al. 2022

. For the true distance modulus,

reported a value of
{(m-M)_{0}}=11.37\pm 0.2
mag, while
Peña Ramírez et al. 2021
claimed a value of
11.74\pm 0.26
mag, and

provided an intermediate value of

6
Summary and conclusion

We present in this paper the new calculation of stellar evolutionary tracks and isochrones for seven metallicities to complement with the first release of
PARSEC v2.0
in
86

We verify the quality of our new computed models by comparisons with the observed data of the open cluster NGC 6067, in terms of broadening velocity, photometric colour-magnitude diagram, and oxygen abundances. The results indicate rotating models are crucial to explain the extremely fast rotating stars at the turn-off region of the cluster. On the other hand, our rotating models suggest at least two populations that harbour in the cluster with dispersion in age and initial rotation rate, and share the same metallicity. On the contrary, the observed oxygen abundance of cool star members show the need of lower metallicity. However, due to the large uncertainty from the observed [O/H] we could not find a conclusive evidence on this regard.
