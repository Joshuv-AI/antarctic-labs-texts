Call for Reforms in the NEET-UG Examination (National Eligibility cum Entrance Test - Undergraduate): Addressing Human Resources in Health for India, the World’s Most Populous Country
Authors: Piyush Ranjan, Sarthak Kain, Raman Kumar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe National Eligibility cum Entrance Test for Undergraduate (NEET-UG) in India serves as a unified admission examination for undergraduate medical courses, aiming to standardize assessment across diverse educational backgrounds. Despite its goals, NEET-UG has faced criticism over fairness, excessive reliance on coaching, and its impact on students’ holistic development. The article reviews the limitations of the current NEET-UG format and proposes reforms, emphasizing the need to align the syllabus more closely with medical requirements by reducing physics and chemistry content and prioritizing biology. It suggests restructuring NEET-UG into a two-step examination to improve differentiation among candidates and reduce malpractice. Introducing a computer-based format, expanding the question pool, and adding interdisciplinary topics relevant to healthcare, such as Psychology, Nutrition, Physical Education, Cell biology, Genetics, public health and sociology, are also recommended. Additionally, the paper compares NEET-UG with global medical entrance exams, noting that broader subject assessments and critical thinking skills could enhance candidate evaluation. The Indian medical education system is the largest in the world. Given its vast capacities, it is crucial to reform the selection process to ensure that human resources in health can effectively address the pressing public health needs of India, the world’s most populated country. The proposed reforms aim to create a fairer, more comprehensive selection process that minimizes stress, encourages holistic education, and better meets medical education standards.

Introduction

The National Eligibility cum Entrance Test for Undergraduate (NEET-UG) is the primary exam for admission to undergraduate medical programs like MBBS, BDS, BAMS, BHMS, and BPT in India. Conducted by the National Testing Agency (NTA), NEET-UG replaced numerous state and institution-specific entrance exams to create a standardized admission process. This change aimed to unify evaluation criteria across diverse educational backgrounds and set a consistent benchmark for medical admissions, helping to improve standards, especially for private and foreign medical college students.

However, despite its well-intentioned objectives, NEET-UG has faced substantial criticism. Reports of unfair practices are not infrequent,[1] and the overwhelming influence of coaching centers has been a significant concern.[2] The growing trend of students leaving regular schools to attend coaching classes—often enrolling in “dummy” schools to focus solely on competitive exams—has raised concerns about their holistic development.[3] This issue has sparked debates among intellectuals and policymakers, who are increasingly worried about the long-term implications for students’ overall personal and educational growth.

While the concept of a single entrance examination offers advantages, the real challenge lies in effectively evaluating a large number of students for a range of courses and institutions with significantly varying demands and importance. These challenges underscore the need for substantial reforms to ensure that NEET-UG meets its goal of providing an effective and valid assessment for all aspiring medical students. Characteristics of an Ideal Entrance Examination

An ideal entrance examination for medical aspirants should prioritize fairness, validity, and an accurate assessment of capability, effectively identifying candidates most likely to excel in medical programs. First, the exam must ensure valid assessment by covering core subjects and foundational topics essential to future medical studies, thereby validating candidates’ readiness.[4] Second, effective differentiation is crucial; the exam should minimize score clustering to clearly distinguish between candidates, especially for highly competitive seats.[5] Third, minimizing the role of luck is essential, with a balanced difficulty aiming for top scores in the 90–95% range and cutoffs at 50–55%, rewarding consistent knowledge over chance. Additionally, the examination must uphold integrity and fairness, with a design that deters dishonest practices and maintains credibility and equal opportunity. Last, the focus should be on genuine understanding by prioritizing inherent knowledge and problem-solving skills over coaching advantages, balancing theoretical and practical assessments to reflect actual knowledge and skills.[5] This structure ensures a fair, valid, and comprehensive evaluation of candidates’ potential for success in medical education.

Major Challenges in Conducting a Fair and Valid NEET-UG Examination

The NEET-UG exam, given its immense popularity and high stakes, faces considerable challenges in upholding fairness and validity. With over 20 lakh students appearing each year,[6] administering the exam in a single sitting is extremely difficult. The high stakes have led to concerns about unfair practices,[1] including attempts to leak question papers. The exam’s 180–200 questions determine outcomes for a vast number of candidates, meaning even minor errors can significantly affect rankings. Score clustering complicates candidate differentiation and reinforces perceptions that the assessment process lacks validity.

As the number of applicants continue to grow, the exam’s difficulty has escalated, often pushing students to study beyond the standard school syllabus and fostering a reliance on coaching centers.[2] This dependency adds financial and psychological strain on students and their families. Additionally, NEET-UG is often seen as favoring urban students,[7] particularly those from CBSE-affiliated schools, over their counterparts from state boards and rural areas—a concern raised by states like Tamil Nadu,[8] which have advocated for reforms. The extensive syllabus and demand for near-zero error increase student stress, shifting the focus from assessing academic ability to testing endurance, thereby benefiting those with access to costly coaching. The core challenge remains to make the exam both valid and equitable, ensuring fairness across all student backgrounds and resources.

Comparative Study of NEET-UG and Global Medical Entrance Examinations

Medical entrance examinations around the world vary significantly in their structure, purpose, and the knowledge and skills they primarily assess. NEET-UG, as the sole medical entrance examination in India, faces intense competition and scrutiny. Comparing NEET-UG with entrance exams from countries like USA, UK, Canada, Australia, and others highlights its unique characteristics and underscores areas for potential improvement.

NEET UG (India) versus Global Counterparts

Purpose and Structure: NEET-UG primarily focuses on admission to undergraduate medical programs such as MBBS, BDS, and allied courses. The examination covers three core subjects—physics, chemistry, and biology—and is conducted in a single stage. This contrasts with other global entrance exams like the MCAT (USA/Canada), which assesses a broader spectrum of competencies, including biological, physical, and social sciences alongside critical reasoning.[9] Similarly, the UCAT (UK, Australia, New Zealand)[10] and the BMAT (UK)[11] include sections testing verbal reasoning, decision-making, and situational judgment, aspects that NEET-UG does not currently evaluate.Duration and Exam Format: NEET-UG is a relatively short exam, lasting approximately 3 hours. In contrast, the MCAT spans about 7.5 hours and includes multiple sections designed to test a wide range of scientific knowledge and analytical skills.[9] Exams like the GAMSAT (Australia, Ireland, UK)[12] and BMAT also feature a mix of multiple-choice questions and written tasks, requiring students to demonstrate both knowledge and communication skills.[11] NEET-UG’s multiple-choice format is straightforward but lacks the depth found in exams that incorporate written essays and passage-based questions, such as the GAMSAT.[12]Subjects Covered: While NEET-UG focuses on physics, chemistry, and biology, global exams like the MCAT cover additional subjects, such as psychology and sociology, recognizing the importance of social and behavioral sciences in medical practice.[9] Similarly, the GAMSAT includes sections on humanities, social sciences, and reasoning in both sciences and humanities, offering a more holistic evaluation of a candidate’s aptitude for medicine.[12] This broader subject base aligns better with the interdisciplinary nature of healthcare, where understanding patient psychology and ethics is crucial.

Key suggestions and justifications for NEET-UG reform

Suggested Reforms in Current NEET-UG Examination

Rationalization of syllabus for NEET-UG

For a competitive exam to be truly effective, it must accurately assess a student’s preparedness for their chosen field. In the case of NEET-UG, the focus should be on subjects directly relevant to medical education. However, the current syllabus distribution is misaligned, with approximately 40% of the volume dedicated to physics, 30% to chemistry, and only 30% to biology. This allocation is more suited to an engineering entrance exam than a medical one. Consequently, student rankings often depend on their performance in physics—a subject with limited relevance to medical practice—rather than in biology, where many aspiring medical students demonstrate nearly equal proficiency.

This misalignment places an unnecessary academic burden on students, pushing them toward coaching centers to master advanced physics and chemistry concepts with minimal long-term value in medical education. The excessive focus on these subjects not only adds to students’ stress but also limits their time for more relevant medical studies, extracurriculars, or personal development.

A more suitable evaluation framework would prioritize subjects foundational to medical science, such as human physiology, biochemistry, cell biology, genetics, biotechnology, microbiology, and public health. These topics are essential for building the knowledge base needed in medical education. Additionally, subjects like psychology, sociology, human nutrition, and physical education—currently missing from the syllabus—are critical for understanding health from a holistic perspective. Many developed countries have integrated these areas into their medical entrance exams, recognizing that future doctors need a solid grasp of the social, psychological, and lifestyle factors influencing health.

To address these issues, comprehensive reforms to the NEET-UG syllabus are necessary. Shifting focus away from advanced physics and chemistry toward core areas like human physiology, genetics, biochemistry, microbiology, public health, psychology, and sociology would align the exam more closely with the demands of medical education. Such restructuring would not only make NEET-UG a more valid and effective assessment for future doctors but also equip all students with relevant, practical knowledge beneficial in daily life, creating a fairer and more meaningful evaluation process.

Computer-based tests in place of traditional methods

Computer-based examinations were first introduced in India in the early 2000s. Since then, they have become the standard format for many competitive exams, including the PG entrance examinations of AIIMS/INI, NEET PG, JEE Main, and GATE, among others. This shift has also been adopted globally for medical entrance exams like the MCAT (USA) and UCAT (UK). The transition from traditional paper-and-pencil exams to computer-based formats has brought several logistical advantages.[9] These exams are easier to manage, requiring fewer physical resources such as paper and transportation, and they reduce the risk of question paper leaks and mass copying due to randomized question papers and AI-driven monitoring systems.[9] Moreover, faster result processing minimizes delays in admissions and further selection processes.

However, challenges remain. Technical failures, such as system crashes or Internet issues, have occasionally led to the cancellation or postponement of exams.[10] While concerns about hacking or cyber threats persist, they remain largely theoretical, with rigorous security protocols in place to mitigate them. The genuine challenge lies in the infrastructure as conducting exams for millions of students in a single sitting can be difficult. To address this, exams that are spread over multiple days with different sets of questions and normalization techniques would ensure fairness across different test sets.

Two-step examination: Enhancing fairness and validity

A two-step examination format, similar to well-regarded exams like the UPSC Civil Services Examination and JEE (Main and Advanced), could replace the current one-step process to ensure a fairer and more thorough evaluation of candidates. This proposed format would consist of two stages: Step 1: Screening Test, followed by Step 2: Rank-Determining Test.

In Step 1: Screening Test: The focus would be on assessing candidates’ fundamental knowledge, covering essential topics with a difficulty level aligned with school board curricula. This design would help ensure inclusivity by allowing students from varied educational backgrounds to compete without disadvantage. Acting as a preliminary filter, this stage would allow only those with a strong foundational understanding to advance to the next stage.

Step 2: Rank-Determining Test would be more rigorous, aimed at identifying top candidates through a deeper assessment of analytical and problem-solving abilities. By selecting the top 10% of candidates from the first step—approximately three times the number of available government MBBS seats (across various categories)—this stage would prioritize advanced knowledge and critical thinking skills to distinguish the best-qualified candidates.

A two-step examination offers several key benefits. First, it significantly reduces the likelihood of malpractice as candidates would need to perform well in two separate stages, making it far more challenging to engage in unfair practices or manipulate the system through collusion with examination center staff. Additionally, this structure enhances the validity of the examination by providing a more comprehensive evaluation of candidates’ abilities, resulting in a fairer and more accurate assessment. Despite these advantages, implementing a two-step examination would require additional resources and infrastructure, and the multiphase nature of the exam may lead to increased stress for students. Nevertheless, it promises a more robust, fair, and transparent method for selecting candidates for government MBBS programs.

Increase in the number and type of questions

The current NEET-UG entrance examination consists of 180 questions conducted in a single sitting. It is important to note that students typically invest around 3000 hours over 2 years preparing for this examination. Given that only about 1 in 40 students will secure a government medical seat,[611] the examination must include an adequate number of questions to assess and evaluate the knowledge of each student reliably. The aim is to ensure that the process of selecting candidates for the highly sought-after government MBBS seats is both fair and rigorous. A general rule of thumb suggests that there should be one question for every 10 to 20 hours of study. Considering the high stakes of NEET UG—where only about 0.5% of candidates succeed[611]—this principle supports the need for a larger pool of questions to provide a comprehensive evaluation. Increasing the number of questions to around 300 would allow for a more thorough sampling of the candidates’ knowledge base, thereby reducing the role of luck in determining outcomes.

The 300 questions could be split into two sessions—one in the morning and one in the afternoon—providing students with a break and making the process less exhausting. Although increasing the number of questions and conducting the exam in two sittings would require additional resources and logistical efforts, the benefits of enhancing the exam’s validity and fairness would outweigh these challenges. A larger question set would allow for a more balanced and in-depth assessment of students’ knowledge, ultimately leading to a more accurate selection of candidates for government medical seats.

In addition to increasing the number of questions, incorporating multiple-correct answer type MCQs in about 20–25% of the exam can be beneficial. As the name suggests, these questions have more than one correct answer in varying proportions. Multiple-correct MCQs provide several advantages, including better differentiation between candidates, deeper knowledge assessment, reduced reliance on guesswork, enhanced critical thinking, fairer partial credit, increased difficulty without being unfair, and improved preparation for real-world problem-solving. Notably, examinations like JEE Advanced, GATE, MCAT, GMAT, and certain GRE subject tests use multiple-correct MCQs to assess comprehensive knowledge and analytical skills.

Strengths and challenges of the proposed system

The proposed NEET-UG examination reforms aim to foster a fair, valid, and stress-minimized evaluation system, effectively selecting candidates who are best suited for medical education. By implementing a two-step examination process, the reforms will enhance fairness by reducing malpractices and improving differentiation among candidates, especially in high-stake tie-breaking situations. This structured format ensures a comprehensive assessment of students’ foundational knowledge in the first stage, while the second stage emphasizes analytical and problem-solving abilities, enabling more precise selection of top performers. The rationalized syllabus prioritizes medical-relevant topics such as biology, human physiology, and public health, thereby reducing unnecessary academic pressure on students to master complex physics and chemistry concepts, which have limited relevance in medical practice.

Additionally, by curbing the syllabus load, students can engage more meaningfully with their studies, reducing reliance on coaching centers and fostering well-rounded development. Overall, these reforms are expected to decrease student stress, ensure equitable access across educational backgrounds, and yield a truly meritocratic evaluation that aligns with global standards for medical entrance exams. However, it is important to note that a two-step examination will require approximately double the resources compared to the current system; nonetheless, this minor drawback can be addressed given the significance of maintaining the validity and reliability of such a crucial examination.

Certain important considerations

The medical profession demands resilience, empathy, interpersonal skills, and ethical integrity to endure the sustained challenges of medical training and patient care. Without assessing these nonacademic qualities, students may be ill-prepared for the profession’s demands, potentially leading to burnout, mental health struggles, or ethical lapses. Additionally, medical education often remains disconnected from complementary fields like psychology, sociology, and management, limiting the holistic perspective essential for effective practice. Incorporating an evaluation of psychosomatic and personality traits as part of the admission process would be a reasonable step toward selecting candidates well suited for a medical career.

Furthermore, addressing the shortage of doctors in rural areas could be strengthened by prioritizing admissions for students from these communities as they are more likely to return and serve locally after graduation. This approach, successfully practiced in countries like Australia, could help bridge healthcare disparities in underserved regions of India.[13] Exploring a possibility of reserving a proportion of medical seats for local students could be a significant step toward achieving this goal.

We believe the proposed NEET-UG reforms will establish a fair, valid, and efficient assessment system that aligns with societal needs and global standards. These changes prioritize student wellbeing, reduce dependency on coaching, and promote holistic development through relevant school-level learning. We urge policymakers to give these recommendations serious consideration, fostering widespread discussion and phased implementation. An effective strategy could begin with immediate adoption of a computer-based format, an increase in the number of questions and variety, a two-step examination structure, and gradual syllabus revisions to better meet the needs of medical education.
