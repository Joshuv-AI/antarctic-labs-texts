Fast mixing of weakly interacting fermionic systems at any temperature
Yu Tong, Yongtao Zhan

Abstract
We study the mixing time of a recently proposed efficiently implementable Lindbladian designed to prepare the Gibbs states in the setting of weakly interacting fermionic systems. We show that at any temperature, the Lindbladian spectral gap for even parity observables is lower bounded by a constant that is independent of the system size, when the interaction strength (e.g., the on-site interaction strength for the Fermi-Hubbard model) is below a constant threshold, which is also independent of the system size. This leads to a mixing time estimate that is at most linear in the system size, thus showing that the corresponding Gibbs states can be prepared efficiently on quantum computers.

Introduction

Simulating non-integrable quantum systems is a notoriously difficult task.
Numerical methods designed for this purpose generally suffer from the curse of dimensionality, the sign problem, or are not efficiently scalable either in the system size or achievable precision.

Moreover, many quantum systems of interest are not spin systems, but are fermionic in nature, which requires us to deal with a different notion of locality.
We therefore ask the following question:

Can we prove fast mixing of fermionic systems at any temperature, given sufficiently weak interaction?

In this work, we will give an affirmative answer to this question.

Proof ideas.

We adopt the approach in
[
27
]
of mapping to a parent Hamiltonian and then lower bounding the spectral gap of that parent Hamiltonian.
1
1

1

In this work, whenever we talk about the spectral gap of a Hamiltonian
K
, we mean the gap between the top eigenvalue and the second largest eigenvalue, unless otherwise stated. This is because the Lindbladian spectrum lies on the negative part of the real axis, and the stationary state corresponds to the 0 eigenvalue, which is the top eigenvalue. However, all results about the gap between the ground state and the first excited state that we need still apply because one can simply consider

The hope is that for a fermionic Hamiltonian with weak interaction, it can be mapped to a parent Hamiltonian that consists of a large non-interacting part plus small and quasi-local interaction terms (though these terms can add up to be as large as linear in
n
). With such a parent Hamiltonian, we then use the stability result about the gap of free-fermion Hamiltonian
[
30
]
to obtain a gap lower bound for the parent Hamiltonian, which implies a gap lower bound for the Lindbladian. A mixing time bound then follows. However, we will see that we need to overcome several difficulties to use this approach .

The first difficulty is that the parent Hamiltonian construction in
[
10
]
does not work for fermions. In
[
27
]
, for bosonic spin systems, this construction was sufficient because it is based on a “vectorization” mapping from super-operators to operators on an enlarged Hilbert space that preserves both
locality
and the
spectrum
using the tensor product of two algebras. The mapping preserves locality because the tensor product preserves locality in bosonic spin systems. The mapping preserves the spectrum because it is a
∗
-isomorphism between two
C^{*}
-algebras. For fermions, we have the additional requirement that the parent Hamiltonian needs to be generated by a set of Majorana operators satisfying the canonical anti-commutation relations. This immediately calls into question the use of the tensor product: one would need to redefine it as a native fermionic operation. In Appendix
B
we will use an example to show that the most obvious attempt to redefine the tensor product will fail to make the mapping an algebra isomorphism. While we cannot rule out the possibility that with sufficient effort one can get a usable redefinition, it seems a highly non-trivial task, and may likely end up yielding something resembling the third quantization
[
31
]
, which is what we are going to use to partially solve the problem.

The third quantization is a method to map a quasi-free Lindbladian to a quadratic operator in an enlarged fermionic system, which we extend to arbitrary Lindbladians.
However, it does not offer as clean a solution as in the bosonic scenario, because in order to preserve locality, the third quantization mapping fails to be an algebra isomorphism, and thereby does not preserve the whole spectrum. In fact, one needs to decide to preserve the spectrum of either the even or the odd-parity sector, which leads to two different mappings.
Fortunately, the fermionic superselection rule, which prohibits the superposition of even and odd particle numbers, allows us to focus on observables that are even polynomials of Majorana operators, which correspond to the even-parity sector.
This is sufficient for establishing the mixing time.
Several other questions that are obvious for the vectorization mapping in
[
10
]
also require careful study in the new setting, such as

1.

Do errors in the Lindbladian result in amplified errors in the parent Hamiltonian through the third quantization?

2.

Is the parent Hamiltonian self-adjoint?

3.

Can the parent Hamiltonian have a odd-parity eigenstate with non-negative eigenvalue thus making the top eigenstate no longer uniquely correspond to the Gibbs state?

We answer the first question in the negative in Appendix
E
, the second question in the affirmative in Section
4
(note that it does not follow directly from the quantum detailed balance condition which only ensures self-adjointness in the even-parity sector), and the third question in the negative in Theorem
3
.

With the parent Hamiltonian constructed form third quantization, we then need to show that the non-interacting parent Hamiltonian, denoted by
H_{0}^{\mathrm{parent}}

Next, we show that the interacting part introduces a quasi-local perturbation to the parent Hamiltonian. More precisely, we show that the parent Hamiltonian

can be written as

, where
H_{0}^{\mathrm{parent}}
is quasi-local (Proposition

consists of quasi-local terms, each of which scales linearly in the interaction strength

[

[

From the above we can see that, at any constant temperature, our mixing time result allows us to efficiently prepare the Gibbs states of a wide range of non-integrable fermionic Hamiltonians on a quantum computer. On the classical side, we are not aware of any such rigorous result. This then raises the question: is this a place where we can demonstrate significant quantum advantage? There are many reasons to be cautious in this regard. First of all, the weak interaction regime we are studying is generally considered easy for classical heuristic algorithms. Commonly used algorithms such as Hartree-Fock achieve good empirical performance even though they generally cannot achieve arbitrarily high accuracy. The recent result on the lack of entanglement for high-temperature Gibbs states of quantum spin systems in
[
29
]
also raises concern that in the weak interaction regime the Gibbs states may have structures that enable efficient classical simulation. All of these lead to an intriguing question: can we design a provably efficient classical algorithm to simulate Gibbs state properties in this regime, or can we prove that no classical algorithm can do so? Either outcome would significantly advance our understanding of the simulability of such systems and quantum advantage in general.

-eigenspace and
(-1)
-eigenspace of the parity operator
i^{n}\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}\cdots\gamma_{2n}
respectively. We say that the states in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{even}}
have even parity while those in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{odd}}
have odd parity.

We can similarly define
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{odd}}
for the enlarged Hilbert space as well. For operators, we can also define

Definition 3
.

We define
B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}
to be the subspace of
B(\mathcal{H})
spanned by
\gamma_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\gamma_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots\gamma_{2n}^{\alpha_{2n}}
for even
|\alpha|
. Similarly we define
B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{odd}}
to be the subspace spanned by
\gamma_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\gamma_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots\gamma_{2n}^{\alpha_{2n}}
for odd
|\alpha|
.

Remark 2
.

These two notions of parity are related, as
v\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
has even/odd parity if and only if
\varphi^{-1}(v)\in B(\mathcal{H})
has even/odd parity. One can see this from the mapping
\varphi
defined in (
16
) and by observing that
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{odd}}
are spanned by
v_{\alpha}
for even
|\alpha|
and odd
|\alpha|
respectively.

We will focus on super-operators that are parity-preserving, as defined below

Definition 4
.

We say
\mathcal{X}\in B(B(\mathcal{H}))
is parity-preserving if
\mathcal{X}(B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}})\subset B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}
and
\mathcal{X}(B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{odd}})\subset B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{odd}}
.

We then use third quantization to build a map that preserves locality and also preserves the spectrum for the even sector.

Definition 5
.

We define a linear map
\Phi:B(B(\mathcal{H}))\to B(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
such that, for any
W,W^{\prime}\in B(\mathcal{H})
,

\displaystyle\Phi(L_{W})

\displaystyle=\varphi\circ L_{W}\circ\varphi^{-1},

\displaystyle\Phi(R_{W})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\varphi\circ R_{W}\circ\varphi^{-1}\ &\text{if }W\text{ is even,}\\
(\varphi\circ R_{W}\circ\varphi^{-1})(-1)^{\hat{n}}\ &\text{if }W\text{ is odd,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\Phi(L_{W}R_{W^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\Phi(L_{W})\Phi(R_{W^{\prime}}).

Remark 3
.

Because
L_{W}R_{W^{\prime}}
with even/odd
W
and even/odd
W^{\prime}
span disjoint subspaces of
B(B(\mathcal{H}))
, the above map is well-defined.
Because
L_{W}R_{W^{\prime}}
for
W
and
W^{\prime}
with even/odd parities span the entire
B(B(\mathcal{H}))
, the above definition uniquely defines the map
\Phi(\mathcal{X})
for all
\mathcal{X}\in B(B(\mathcal{H}))
.

Remark 4
.

\Phi
defined in Definition
5
strictly preserves locality. We can see this by computing

\displaystyle\Phi(L_{\gamma_{j_{1}}\gamma_{j_{2}}\cdots\gamma_{j_{k}}})

\displaystyle=\hat{\gamma}_{2j_{1}}\hat{\gamma}_{2j_{2}}\cdots\hat{\gamma}_{2j_{k}},

\displaystyle\Phi(R_{\gamma_{j_{1}}\gamma_{j_{2}}\cdots\gamma_{j_{k}}})

\displaystyle=-i^{k}\hat{\gamma}_{2j_{k}}\hat{\gamma}_{2j_{k-1}}\cdots\hat{\gamma}_{2j_{1}}.

Note that
\Phi
is not a homomorphism between algebras because even though
L_{W}
and
R_{W^{\prime}}
commute with each other,
\Phi(L_{W})
and
\Phi(R_{W^{\prime}})
do not necessarily commute with each other. However, it still preserves part of the spectrum, as stated in the following lemma:

Lemma 1
.

\Phi
satisfies

\Phi(\mathcal{X})|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}}=\varphi\circ\mathcal{X}\circ\varphi^{-1}|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}}.

Moreover, when
\mathcal{X}
is parity-preserving,
\Phi(\mathcal{X})|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}}
and
\mathcal{X}|_{B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}}
share the same spectrum.

Proof.

Note that for
v\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
with even parity,
(-1)^{\hat{n}}v=v
. Therefore
\Phi(L_{W}R_{W^{\prime}})v=\Phi(L_{W})\Phi(R_{W^{\prime}})v=(\varphi\circ L_{W}R_{W^{\prime}}\circ\varphi^{-1})v
. From this the equality follows by linearity. Because
\varphi
preserves parity as discussed in Remark
2
, we can then see that
\Phi(\mathcal{X})|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}}
and
\mathcal{X}|_{B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}}
must share the same spectrum if
\mathcal{X}
preserves parity.
∎

3.2
Stability of free-fermion Hamiltonians

We will introduce the stability result for the gap of free-fermion Hamiltonians proved in
[
30
]
. It relies crucially on notions of locality, which we will discuss below.

Definition 6

(Quasi-locality around
x
)
.

Let
x\in\Lambda
be a lattice site. We say an operator
W
is
(C,\mu)
-quasi-local around
x
if
\|W\|\leq C
, and for every integer
r\geq 1
, there exists
W_{r}
supported on the ball of radius
r
centered at
x
such that

\|W-W_{r}\|\leq Ce^{-\mu r},

for some constants
C,r>0
.

We adopt the same definition in
[
30
]
of the decay property of an operator, which characterize the quasi-locality of an extensive operator in the following way

Definition 7

(Decay of operator)
.

We say an operator
W
has
(C,\mu)
-decay if
W
can be decomposed as

W=\sum_{r\geq 1}\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}(r)}W_{r,B},

where
\mathcal{B}(r)
denotes the set of balls of radius
r
and
W_{r,B}
is an operator supported on a ball
B
and where

\max_{B\in\mathcal{B}(r)}\|W_{r,B}\|\leq Ce^{-\mu r},

for some constants
C,r>0
.

Naturally, an operator that is the sum of quasi-local terms has nice decay property:

Lemma 2
.

For any
C,\mu>0
, if an operator
W
can be written as
W=\sum_{x\in\Lambda}W_{x}
where each
W_{x}
is
(C,\mu)
-quasi-local around
x
, then
W
has
(2Ce^{\mu},\mu)
-decay.

Proof.

Since
W=\sum_{x\in\Lambda}W_{x}
, and
W_{x}
is
(C,\mu)
-quasi-local around
x
, we have

W=\sum_{r\geq 0}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}(W_{x,r}-W_{x,r-1}),

where
\|W_{x}-W_{x,r}\|\leq Ce^{-\mu r}
, and we let
W_{x,-1}=0
. Note that
W_{x,r}-W_{x,r-1}
is supported on the ball with radius
r
centered at
x
, and
\|W_{x,r}-W_{x,r-1}\|\leq 2Ce^{-\mu(r-1)}
by the triangle inequality. This tells us that
W
has
(2Ce^{\mu},\mu)
-decay.
∎

For quadratic operators Ref.
[
30
]
defined a somewhat different notion of decay, which we state here

Definition 8

(Decay of coefficient matrix)
.

For a matrix
B=(B_{jk})
where each
j
and
k
is associated with a lattice site
x\in\Lambda
, and no more than
n_{0}

j
or
k
is associated with the same site, then we say
B
has
[K,\nu]
-decay if

\|B_{xy}\|\leq Ke^{-\nu d(x,y)},

where
x,y\in\Lambda
, and
B_{xy}
is the submatrix of
B
whose rows and columns are associated with
x
and
y
respectively. Here
n_{0}
is a constant that is independent of
|\Lambda|
.

We remark that in this work we only need to have
n_{0}\leq 4
.
With the above notions of locality, we will state the main result in
[
30
]
:

Theorem 2

(Corollary 1 of
[
30
]
)
.

Let
H_{FF}=\sum_{jk}A_{jk}\gamma_{j}\gamma_{k}
be a Hamiltonian on lattice
\Lambda
with spectral gap
\Delta
. We assume that
A=(A_{jk})
has
[K,\nu]
-decay. Let
H=H_{FF}+V
, where we assume
V
has
(C,\mu)
-decay. Then there exists constants
C_{0},c_{1}>0
depending only on
K,\nu,\mu,\Delta,D
such that when
C\leq C_{0}
, the spectral gap of
H
is at least
\Delta-c_{1}C
.

Note that in this work we focus on the spectral gap between the top eigenvalue and the second largest eigenvalue as explained in Fn.
1
, while
[
30
]
was about the gap between the ground state and the first excited state. These two are however equivalent as one can simply consider
-H
rather than
H
.

3.3
The Lieb-Robinson bound

The Lieb-Robinson bound was used extensively in
[
30
,
27
]
and is also needed in our proof. We state the version used in
[
35
, Lemma 5]
, which follows
[
33
,
39
,
40
,
41
,
42
]
, with modification:

Lemma 3
.

Let
H
be a fermionic Hamiltonian on
\Lambda
with even parity that is
(\xi,r_{0})
-geometrically local (Definition
1
). For an operator
O_{X}
supported on
X\subset\Lambda
, and
\Omega\subset X
, we let

\ell=\lfloor\min\{d(x,y):x\in X,y\in\Lambda\setminus\Omega\}\rfloor.

Then

\|e^{iHt}O_{X}e^{-iHt}-e^{iH^{\prime}t}O_{X}e^{-iH^{\prime}t}\|\leq|X|\|O_{X}\|\frac{(2J|t|)^{\ell}}{\ell!},

where
H^{\prime}
consists of the terms in
H
that are supported within
\Omega
,
J\leq C\xi r_{0}^{2D}
for some constant
C>0
that depends only on
D
.

We remark that we adapt
[
35
, Lemma 5]
to the fermionic setting, which, as commented in
[
35
, Section 4]
, works without needing any modification.
J
is sometimes known as the Lieb-Robinson velocity. When
H
is
(\xi,r_{0})
-geometrically local, then by Definition
1
we can write it as
H=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}(r_{0})}H_{B}
with
\|H_{B}\|\leq\xi
. Then

J=\max_{x\in\Lambda}\sum_{B\ni x}|B|\|H_{B}\|\leq\xi\times C_{1}r_{0}^{D}\times C_{2}r_{0}^{D}=C\xi r_{0}^{2D}.

(25)

We have used the fact that each term involves at most
|B|\leq C_{1}r_{0}^{D}
sites and each site is involved in at most
C_{2}r_{0}^{D}
terms, for constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
that depend only on
D
.
We will also use the Lieb-Robinson velocity for the non-interacting part
H_{0}
, for which we can get a tighter bound:

J_{0}=\max_{x\in\Lambda}\sum_{B\ni x}|B|\|(H_{0})_{B}\|\leq 1\times 2\times C_{2}^{\prime}r_{0}^{D}=C^{\prime}\xi r_{0}^{D}.

(26)

In the above because
H_{0}
only contains terms that act on two Majorana modes,
B
for which the corresponding term
(H_{0})_{B}
is non-zero can have at most
|B|=2
, and
\|(H_{0})_{B}\|\leq 1
.

4
Constructing the parent Hamiltonian

Even though third quantization provides a way to map the Lindbladian to a operator, we still do not have a Hamiltonian because the resulting operator may not be Hermitian. In this section we will use the stationary state
\sigma
to construct a Hermitian parent Hamiltonian.
We define super-operator
\Gamma\in B(B(\mathcal{H}))
as follows:

\Gamma(X)=\sigma^{1/4}X\sigma^{1/4}.

(27)

We then define the map

\tilde{\varphi}=\varphi\circ\Gamma,

(28)

This map is an isometry, i.e. it satisfies

\displaystyle\braket{\tilde{\varphi}(X),\tilde{\varphi}(Y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[\Gamma(X)^{\dagger}\Gamma(Y)]

(29)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma^{1/4}X^{\dagger}\sigma^{1/2}Y\sigma^{1/4}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\braket{X,Y}_{\mathrm{KMS}},

where the first equality comes from (
18
).
We then define, for any
\mathcal{X}\in B(B(\mathcal{H}))
,

\tilde{\Phi}(\mathcal{X})=\Phi({\Gamma}\circ\mathcal{X}\circ{\Gamma}^{-1}).

(30)

By the definition of
\Phi
in Definition
5
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}(\mathcal{X})|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}}=\tilde{\varphi}\circ\mathcal{X}\circ\tilde{\varphi}^{-1}|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}}.

(31)

This immediate implies the following:

Lemma 4
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\in B(B(\mathcal{H}))
be a super-operator that preserves parity according to Definition
4
, then
\tilde{\Phi}(\mathcal{X})|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}}
and
\mathcal{X}|_{B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}}
share the same spectrum.

In particular for left- and right-multiplication we have

\tilde{\Phi}(L_{A})=\Phi(L_{\sigma^{1/4}A\sigma^{-1/4}}),\quad\tilde{\Phi}(R_{A})=\Phi(R_{\sigma^{-1/4}A\sigma^{1/4}}).

(32)

We are now ready to define the parent Hamiltonian through

H^{\mathrm{parent}}=\tilde{\Phi}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}),

(33)

where
\mathcal{L}
is the Lindbladian defined in (
10
) that satisfies the KMS-DBC as defined in (
15
).

Lemma 5
.

The parent Hamiltonian
H^{\mathrm{parent}}
has the following properties:

(i)

H^{\mathrm{parent}}
is self-adjoint in
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
.

(ii)

0
is an eigenvalue of both
H^{\mathrm{parent}}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}|_{B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}}
.
Let
\Delta
and
\Delta^{\prime}
be the gap between 0 and the largest non-positive eigenvalue for
H^{\mathrm{parent}}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}|_{B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}}
respectively.
Then
\Delta\leq\Delta^{\prime}
.

Proof.

For (i), note that by (
31
) and the fact that
\mathcal{L}
satisfies KMS-DBC (as defined in (
15
)), we can see that
H^{\mathrm{parent}}|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}}
is self-adjoint. This implies

H^{\mathrm{parent}}|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}}=(H^{\mathrm{parent}})^{\dagger}|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}}.

(34)

We consider enlarging the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
by one Dirac mode, but not adding anything to the Hamiltonian
H
. Then going through the same mapping from
H
to
\mathcal{L}
(through (
10
)) and then
\tilde{\Phi}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger})
, we obtain a new parent Hamiltonian
\bar{H}^{\mathrm{parent}}
in an enlarged Hilbert space
\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
involving
2n+2
Dirac modes. By (
24
) and the fact that
\sigma
does not involve the modes
2n+1
and
2n+2
, we can see that
\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
only acts on the Dirac modes
1
to
2n
, and is of exactly the same form as
H^{\mathrm{parent}}
when written as a multivariate polynomial of Majorana operators, even though they act on different Hilbert spaces. Moreover, by the same argument as for
H^{\mathrm{parent}}
we have

\bar{H}^{\mathrm{parent}}|_{\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}}=(\bar{H}^{\mathrm{parent}})^{\dagger}|_{\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}}.

(35)

We revert back to the creation and annihilation operators in (
20
), where
\hat{c}_{j}=(\hat{\gamma}_{2j-1}+i\hat{\gamma}_{2j})/2
. This also gives us a vacuum state
\ket{\mathrm{vac}}
for
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
that is in the kernel of every
\hat{c}_{j}
for
j=1,2,\cdots,2n
. For
\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
, we can likewise get a vacuum state
\ket{\overline{\mathrm{vac}}}
that is also in the kernel of
\hat{c}_{2n+1}
and
\hat{c}_{2n+2}
. For any state

v=\sum_{\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{2n}}v_{\alpha}(\hat{c}_{1}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots(\hat{c}_{2n}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{2n}}\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime},

we define

\bar{v}=\sum_{\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{2n}}v_{\alpha}(\hat{c}_{1}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots(\hat{c}_{2n}^{\dagger})^{\alpha_{2n}}\ket{\overline{\mathrm{vac}}}\in\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}.

Because
H^{\mathrm{parent}}
and
\bar{H}^{\mathrm{parent}}
are represented by the same polynomial of Majorana operators, we have

\braket{v,H^{\mathrm{parent}}w}=\braket{\bar{v},\bar{H}^{\mathrm{parent}}\bar{w}},

(36)

for any
v,w\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
.

Then we consider
v,w\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{odd}}
. Note that
\hat{c}_{2n+1}^{\dagger}\bar{v},\hat{c}_{2n+1}^{\dagger}\bar{w}\in\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}
. We then have

\displaystyle\braket{v,H^{\mathrm{parent}}w}=\braket{\bar{v},\bar{H}^{\mathrm{parent}}\bar{w}}=\braket{\hat{c}_{2n+1}^{\dagger}\bar{v},\bar{H}^{\mathrm{parent}}\hat{c}_{2n+1}^{\dagger}\bar{w}}

\displaystyle=\braket{\hat{c}_{2n+1}^{\dagger}\bar{v},(\bar{H}^{\mathrm{parent}})^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{2n+1}^{\dagger}\bar{w}}=\braket{\bar{v},(\bar{H}^{\mathrm{parent}})^{\dagger}\bar{w}}=\braket{v,(H^{\mathrm{parent}})^{\dagger}w},

where in the third equality we have used (
36
), and in the second and fourth equalities we have used the fact that
\bar{H}^{\mathrm{parent}}
and
\bar{v},\bar{w}
do not involve any operator on Dirac mode
2n+1
. This then implies

H^{\mathrm{parent}}|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{odd}}}=(H^{\mathrm{parent}})^{\dagger}|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{odd}}}.

Because
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{odd}}
are both invariant subspaces of
H^{\mathrm{parent}}
, the above equation combined with (
34
) yields
H^{\mathrm{parent}}=(H^{\mathrm{parent}})^{\dagger}
.

For (ii), let
\Delta^{\prime\prime}
be the gap between 0 and the largest non-positive eigenvalue of
H^{\mathrm{parent}}|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}}
.
Since the spectrum of
H^{\mathrm{parent}}|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}}
is a subset of the spectrum of
H^{\mathrm{parent}}
, and 0 is an eigenvalue of both of them (corresponding to eigenvector
v=\varphi(\sigma^{1/2})\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}
), we have
\Delta\leq\Delta^{\prime\prime}
.
By Lemma
4
we have
\Delta^{\prime\prime}=\Delta^{\prime}
.
Therefore
\Delta^{\prime}\geq\Delta
.
∎

The spectral gap of
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}|_{B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}}
is sufficient for establishing the convergence towards to stationary state.

Lemma 6
.

We assume that
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
satisfies the KMS-DBC (
15
), the spectral gap between 0 and the second largest eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}|_{B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}}
is
g>0
, and
\mathrm{ker}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}|_{B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}})=\mathrm{span}(I)
.
Let
Y\in B(\mathcal{H})
be self-adjoint with even parity (Definition
3
) satisfying
\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma Y]=0
, then, denoting
Y(t)=e^{t\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}}(Y)
,

\|Y(t)\|_{\mathrm{KMS}}\leq e^{-gt}\|Y\|_{\mathrm{KMS}},

where for any
X\in B(\mathcal{H})
,
\|X\|_{\mathrm{KMS}}=\braket{X,X}_{\mathrm{KMS}}^{1/2}
.

Proof.

Because
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
satisfies the KMS-DBC (
15
), and that
\mathrm{ker}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}|_{B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}})=\mathrm{span}(I)
, for the spectral gap we have the variational form

-g=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\in B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}\\
\braket{X,I}_{\mathrm{KMS}}=0\end{subarray}}\frac{\braket{X,\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}|{}_{B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}}X}_{\mathrm{KMS}}}{\braket{X,X}_{\mathrm{KMS}}}.

Therefore when
\braket{X,I}_{\mathrm{KMS}}=0
, we have

\braket{X,\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}|{}_{B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}}X}_{\mathrm{KMS}}\leq-g\braket{X,X}_{\mathrm{KMS}}.

The inequality we want to prove follows immediately from

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\braket{Y(t),Y(t)}_{\mathrm{KMS}}

\displaystyle=2\braket{Y(t),\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}Y(t)}_{\mathrm{KMS}}

\displaystyle=2\braket{Y(t),\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}|{}_{B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}}Y(t)}_{\mathrm{KMS}}

\displaystyle\leq-2g\braket{Y(t),Y(t)}_{\mathrm{KMS}},

where we have used the fact that
\braket{Y(t),I}_{\mathrm{KMS}}=\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma Y(t)]=\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma Y]=0
for all
t
.
∎

Corollary 4
.

We assume that
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
satisfies the KMS-DBC (
15
), the spectral gap between 0 and the second largest eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}|_{B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}}
is
g>0
, and
\mathrm{ker}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}|_{B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}})=\mathrm{span}(I)
.
Let
X\in B(\mathcal{H})
be self-adjoint with even parity (Definition
3
). For an initial state
\rho\in B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}
(i.e., satisfying the fermion superselection rule), we have

|\mathrm{Tr}[X(t)\rho]-\mathrm{Tr}[X\sigma]|\leq\frac{e^{-gt}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\min}}}\|X\|,

where
\sigma_{\min}
is the minimum eigenvalue of
\sigma
.

Proof.

We let
Y=X-I\braket{I,X}_{\mathrm{KMS}}
. Then
\mathrm{Tr}[Y\sigma]=0
. By Lemma
6
we have

\|X(t)-I\braket{I,X}_{\mathrm{KMS}}\|=\|Y(t)\|_{\mathrm{KMS}}\leq e^{-gt}\|X\|,

where we have used
\|Y\|_{\mathrm{KMS}}\leq\|X\|_{\mathrm{KMS}}\leq\|X\|
.
Note that

\mathrm{Tr}[X(t)\rho]-\mathrm{Tr}[X\sigma]=\braket{X(t)-I\braket{I,X}_{\mathrm{KMS}},\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}}_{\mathrm{KMS}},

by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have

|\mathrm{Tr}[X(t)\rho]-\mathrm{Tr}[X\sigma]|\leq\|\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}\|_{\mathrm{KMS}}e^{-gt}\|X\|.

The result follows by

\|\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}\|_{\mathrm{KMS}}^{2}=\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}]\leq\|\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}\|\mathrm{Tr}[\rho]\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}}.

∎

Corollary 5
.

We assume that
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
satisfies the KMS-DBC (
15
), the spectral gap between 0 and the second largest eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}|_{B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}}
is
g>0
, and
\mathrm{ker}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}|_{B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}})=\mathrm{span}(I)
.
For an initial state
\rho\in B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}
(i.e., satisfying the fermion superselection rule), we have

\|\rho(t)-\sigma\|_{\mathrm{1}}\leq\frac{e^{-gt}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\min}}},

where
\rho(t)=e^{t\mathcal{L}}(\rho)
,
\sigma_{\min}
is the minimum eigenvalue of
\sigma
, and
\|\cdot\|_{1}
denotes the Schatten-1 norm.

Proof.

For any observable
X\in B(\mathcal{H})
, we can decompose
X=X_{\mathrm{even}}+X_{\mathrm{odd}}
where
X_{\mathrm{even}}
and
X_{\mathrm{odd}}
have even and odd parities as defined in Definition
3
. We can readily see that
\mathrm{Tr}[\rho(t)X]=\mathrm{Tr}[\rho(t)X_{\mathrm{even}}]
because
\rho\in B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}
, and the same is true for
\sigma
. Also, by the eigenvalue interlacing theorem,
\|X_{\mathrm{even}}\|\leq\|X\|
. Therefore by Corollary
4
we have

|\mathrm{Tr}[(\rho(t)-\sigma)X]|\leq\frac{e^{-gt}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\min}}}\|X_{\mathrm{even}}\|\leq\frac{e^{-gt}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\min}}}\|X\|.

Since this is true for any
X\in B(\mathcal{H})
, by the duality between the Schatten 1-norm and the Schatten
\infty
-norm we have the desired inequality.
∎

5
Structure of the parent Hamiltonian

We can write
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
in the super-operator form

5.2
Fast mixing

The mixing time characterizes the maximal time for a quantum state to converge to the stationary state in time evolution.
In the fermionic setting, however, the superselection rule places a constraint on what quantum states should be considered. Since no superposition between even- and odd-parity sectors are allowed, we only need to consider quantum states that can be expressed as linear combinations of Majorana operators of even parity. They make up the set

D(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}=D(\mathcal{H})\cap B(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}},

(54)

D(\mathcal{H})
is the set of all positive semi-definite and trace-1 operators defined in (
5
).
This requires us to modify the definition of the mixing time in the following way:

Definition 9

(Mixing time for fermions)
.

For a Lindbladian operator
\mathcal{L}
with unique stationary state in
D(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}
, we define its
\epsilon
-mixing time to be

t_{\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon)=\inf\{t\geq 0:\|e^{s\mathcal{L}}(\rho)-\sigma\|_{1}\leq\epsilon,\forall s\geq t,\rho\in D(\mathcal{H})_{\mathrm{even}}\}.

(55)

Here
\sigma
is the stationary state of
\mathcal{L}
.

We are then ready to prove Theorem
1
, which we restate here:

Theorem

(Main result)
.

Let
H=H_{0}+V
be a Hamiltonian on a
D
-dimensional cubic lattice
\Lambda
consisting of
n
sites.
H_{0}
is quadratic in Majorana operators and is
(1,r_{0})
-geometrically local, and
V
is parity-preserving and is
(U,r_{0})
-geometrically local. Then for any inverse temperature
\beta>0
, there exists
U_{\beta}>0
such that when
U<U_{\beta}
, the Lindbladian defined in (
10
) has a unique stationary state satisfying the fermionic superselection rule, and the
\epsilon
-mixing time (Definition
9
) of the Lindbladian is at most

C(n+\log(1/\epsilon)).

where
C,U_{\beta}>0
are constants that depend only on
r_{0},D,\beta
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3
, we know that there exists
U_{\beta}>0
such that when
U<U_{\beta}
, the spectral gap of
H^{\mathrm{parent}}
is lower bounded by
\Delta>0
, and that
0
is a non-degenerate eigenvalue. This implies that
0
is also a non-degenerate eigenvalue of
H^{\mathrm{parent}}|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}}
(since
\varphi(\sigma^{1/2})
, which spans the kernel, is in

H^{\mathrm{parent}}|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}}

5.3
Recovering the Gibbs state from the top eigenstate

In this section we will discuss recovering information about the Gibbs state from the top eigenstate of the parent Hamiltonian. Suppose we want to obtain the expectation value of an observable

X=\sum_{\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{2n}}X_{\alpha}\gamma_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\gamma_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots\gamma_{2n}^{\alpha_{2n}}\in B(\mathcal{H}),

(56)

then we will show that we can get it from an observable

(57)

Proposition 4
.

Let
\sigma
be the Gibbs state of
\mathcal{L}
in (
10
) and let
v
be the (normalized) top eigenstate of the parent Hamiltonian
H^{\mathrm{parent}}
defined in (
33
). Then for any observable
X\in B(\mathcal{H})
as expressed in (
56

X^{\sharp}\in B(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
as defined in (
57

\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma X]=\braket{v,X^{\sharp}v}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3
we know that
v\propto\varphi(\sigma^{1/2})
. We can compute that, by (
18

\braket{\varphi(\sigma^{1/2}),\varphi(\sigma^{1/2})}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma]=\frac{1}{2^{n}}.

Therefore we have

(fixing an unimportant global phase). Then we have

\braket{v,X^{\sharp}v}=\braket{v,\varphi\circ L_{X}\circ\varphi^{-1}(v)}=2^{n}\braket{\varphi(\sigma^{1/2}),\varphi(X\sigma^{1/2})}=\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma^{1/2}X\sigma^{1/2}]=\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma X].

∎

Remark 5
.

This mapping
X\in B(\mathcal{H})\mapsto X^{\sharp}\in B(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
is an algebra homomorphism. One can easily check that for
X,Y\in B(\mathcal{H})
we have
(XY)^{\sharp}=X^{\sharp}Y^{\sharp}
. Moreover, we have
\|X^{\sharp}\|=\|X\|
by Lemma
25

As a result of this correspondence we can readily prove correlation decay in the Gibbs state, as stated in Corollary
3
, which we restate here:

Corollary
.

Under the same assumptions as Theorem
1
, there exists
C,\xi>0
that depends only only on
\beta,r_{0},D
, such that for an observable

supported on a constant number of adjacent sites, and any observable

X
and

3
.

Let
v
be the normalized top eigenstate of

4
and Remark

Next, we will deal with the more general case where the system consists of more than one fermionic modes. However, we will see that the general case can be reduced to this simple case.
We first rotate the Majorana operators
\gamma_{j}
to turn the non-interacting part

into a standard form. We choose a

real orthogonal matrix

and perform the basis transform

\gamma_{j}=\sum_{k}Q_{jk}\zeta_{k}.

(60)

It is easy to see that
\zeta_{k}=\sum_{j}Q_{jk}\gamma_{j}
, and that
\braket{\zeta_{k},\zeta_{k^{\prime}}}=2\delta_{kk^{\prime}}
and therefore
\{\zeta_{k}\}
for a valid set of Majorana operators. The orthogonal matrix

(61)

for

Such a

always exist because the coefficient matrix
(h_{jk})
is Hermitian and pure imaginary.
We can see that the free Hamiltonian gets decoupled into terms with non-overlapping supports.
Here
\pm\epsilon_{j}
are eigenvalues of the coefficient matrix
(h_{jk})
. Because

is
(1,r_{0})
-geometrically local as defined in Definition

We will show that this basis transform decouples the free parent Hamiltonian as well. Essentially, this is true because the free parent Hamiltonian is a quadratic function of
\gamma_{j}

Recall that the free parent Hamiltonian

is of the form (

\displaystyle H^{\mathrm{parent}}_{\mathrm{free}}=\Phi\Bigg(\sum_{j}\int\mathrm{d}\omega\eta(\omega)\left(L_{\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{free}}_{j}(-\omega)}R_{\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{free}}_{j}(-\omega)^{\dagger}}-\frac{1}{2}L_{\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{free}}_{j}(-\omega)\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{free}}_{j}(\omega)}-\frac{1}{2}R_{\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{free}}_{j}(\omega)^{\dagger}\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{free}}_{j}(-\omega)^{\dagger}}\right)\Bigg),

(63)

(64)

In this section we will show that both

For an operator
A\in B(\mathcal{H})
, we introduce the following notation: let
\{E_{i}\}
be the eigenvalues of
H
, and
\{\Pi_{i}\}
be the corresponding eigenspace projection operators, then we denote

A_{\nu}=\sum_{ij:E_{i}-E_{j}=\nu}\Pi_{i}A\Pi_{j}.

(72)

There is only a finite set of
\nu

, they are called

Using the decomposition
\gamma_{j}=\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}(\gamma_{j})_{\nu}

\tilde{A}_{j}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{\nu}\int e^{-\beta\nu/4}e^{i\nu t}(\gamma_{j})_{\nu}e^{-i\omega t}f(t)\mathrm{d}t.

(73)

Now we define

F_{1}(\nu,\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{-\beta\nu/4}e^{i(\nu-\omega)t}f(t)\mathrm{d}t,

(74)

then we have

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{j}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu}F_{1}(\nu,\omega)(\gamma_{j})_{\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{\nu}\int\check{F}_{1}(t,\omega)e^{i\nu t}(\gamma_{j})_{\nu}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\check{F}_{1}(t,\omega)e^{iHt}\gamma_{j}e^{-iHt}\mathrm{d}t,

where

\displaystyle\check{F}_{1}(t,\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int F_{1}(\nu,\omega)e^{-i\nu t}\mathrm{d}\nu

(76)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\mathrm{d}\nu\int\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}e^{-\beta\nu/4}e^{i(\nu-\omega)t^{\prime}}f(t^{\prime})e^{-i\nu t}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{1/16}}{\sqrt{\beta\sqrt{\pi/2}}}e^{-t^{2}/\beta^{2}}e^{-it(\omega-1/(2\beta))}e^{-\beta\omega/4}.

Similarly, for
\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{free}}_{j}(\omega)

\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{free}}_{j}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\check{F}_{1}(t,\omega)e^{iH_{0}t}\gamma_{j}e^{-iH_{0}t}\mathrm{d}t.

(77)

7.1
Quasi-locality of the jump operator

We will first prove that
\tilde{A}_{j}(\omega)
(defined in (
39
)) can be well-approximated by local operators.

Lemma 10
.

There exists universal constants
C,\mu_{1},\mu_{2}>0
such that for any
r\geq 1
, there exists an operator
A^{\mathrm{loc}}
supported on the ball with radius
r
centered at
j
such that

\|\tilde{A}_{j}(\omega)-A^{\mathrm{loc}}\|\leq C\left(\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{r}}e^{-\mu_{1}r}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}e^{-\mu_{2}r^{2}/(J\beta)^{2}}\right)e^{-\beta\omega/4},

(78)

where
J
is the Lieb-Robinson velocity of
H
on
\Lambda
for which an upper bound is given in (
25

C^{\prime},\mu>0
that depend only on
\beta

\|\tilde{A}_{j}(\omega)-A^{\mathrm{loc}}\|\leq C^{\prime}e^{-\mu r}e^{-\beta\omega/4}.

(79)

Proof.

We first decompose

, where

\mathrm{I}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-T}^{T}\check{F}_{1}(t,\omega)e^{iHt}\gamma_{j}e^{-iHt}\mathrm{d}t,\quad\mathrm{II}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{|t|>T}\check{F}_{1}(t,\omega)e^{iHt}\gamma_{j}e^{-iHt}\mathrm{d}t.

(80)

In the above
T>0
can be arbitrarily chosen.
From (
76

|\check{F}_{1}(t,\omega)|\leq C_{1}e^{-t^{2}/\beta^{2}}e^{-\beta\omega/4}.

(81)

Therefore we have

\|\mathrm{II}\|\leq\frac{C_{2}}{\sqrt{\beta}}e^{-T^{2}/\beta^{2}}e^{-\beta\omega/4}.

(82)

We then approximate
e^{iHt}\gamma_{j}e^{-iHt}
with a local operator. Let
H^{\prime}
be the Hamiltonian consisting of terms of
H
that are supported on the ball around
j
with radius
r
, then using the Lieb-Robinson bound
[
27
, Eq. (A1)]
, we have

\|e^{iHt}\gamma_{j}e^{-iHt}-e^{iH^{\prime}t}\gamma_{j}e^{-iH^{\prime}t}\|\leq\frac{(2J|t|)^{r}}{r!}.

(83)

Here
e^{iH^{\prime}t}\gamma_{j}e^{-iH^{\prime}t}
is supported only on this ball with radius
r
. We then let

A^{\mathrm{loc}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-T}^{T}\check{F}_{1}(t,\omega)e^{iH^{\prime}t}\gamma_{j}e^{-iH^{\prime}t}\mathrm{d}t.

(84)

This is a good approximate of
\mathrm{I}
:

\|\mathrm{I}-A^{\mathrm{loc}}\|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-T}^{T}|\check{F}_{1}(t,\omega)|\frac{(2J|t|)^{r}}{r!}\mathrm{d}t\leq C_{3}\sqrt{\beta}\frac{(2JT)^{r}}{r!}e^{-\beta\omega/4}.

(85)

Therefore

\|\tilde{A}_{j}(\omega)-A^{\mathrm{loc}}\|\leq\|\mathrm{I}-A^{\mathrm{loc}}\|+\|\mathrm{II}\|\leq C_{3}\sqrt{\beta}\frac{(2JT)^{r}}{r!}e^{-\beta\omega/4}+\frac{C_{2}}{\sqrt{\beta}}e^{-T^{2}/\beta^{2}}e^{-\beta\omega/4}.

(86)

Using Stirling’s formula we have

for

, and therefore we further have

\|\tilde{A}_{j}(\omega)-A^{\mathrm{loc}}\|\leq C\left(\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{r}}\left(\frac{2eJT}{r}\right)^{r}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}e^{-T^{2}/\beta^{2}}\right)e^{-\beta\omega/4}.

(87)

Since we can arbitrarily choose

78

\mu_{1}=1
and
\mu_{2}=1/4e^{4}

∎

9
Discussions

In this work we proved that, at any temperature, when the interacting strength in a geometrically local fermionic Hamiltonian is below a constant threshold that depends only on the lattice geometry (
r_{0},D
) and the inverse temperature (
\beta
), the mixing time of an efficiently implementable Lindbladian that prepares the Gibbs state can be bounded linearly in the system size. This immediately implies efficient preparation of the Gibbs state on a quantum computer. In the proof, we obtained a constant lower bound for the Lindbladian spectral gap restricted to the even parity subspace by mapping the Lindbladian to a parent Hamiltonian, for which the result from
[
30
]
provides a spectral gap lower bound. The mapping from the Lindbladian to the parent Hamiltonian utilizes the third quantization, and we paid special attention to ensure that the mapping preserves locality and the relevant part of the Lindbladian spectrum.

Our result motivates new questions about the competition between classical and quantum algorithms in the setting of weakly interacting fermions. The provably efficient quantum algorithm that comes as a result of our mixing time bound makes it natural to ask whether one can compute Gibbs state properties for weakly interacting fermions on a classical computer to attain arbitrarily high accuracy with provable efficiency. This may require us to use structural properties of the Gibbs states akin to what was done in
[
29
]
.

It is also natural to ask whether one can obtain spectral gap results and hence mixing time bounds by directly studying the Lindbladian rather than going through the parent Hamiltonian. If possible, this approach would enable us to deal with Lindbladians that may not satisfy the detailed balance condition. Such an approach has been studied for Lindbladian satisfying a special set of conditions in
[
24
]
, from which the idea of constructing a Lyapunov functional may be more generally useful.

The quantum algorithm for Gibbs state preparation discussed in Corollary
2
also motivates further development of Lindbladian simulation algorithms. Note that to prepare the Gibbs state we need to simulate Lindbladian dynamics for a time that is linear in
n
on a system of size that is also linear in
n
. One would naturally expect the gate complexity to be proportional to
n^{2}
, but we in the end have a
n^{3}
scaling because of an additional
n
factor from normalizing the Lindbladian. This is in contrast with Hamiltonian simulation algorithms where a gate complexity that is almost linear in the space-time volume can be attained
[
35
,
43
]
. This problem also arises in other Lindbladian simulation algorithms
[
44
,
45
,
46
,
47
,
48
]
. Whether one can attain the space-time volume scaling in Lindbladian simulation remains an interesting open question.

Appendix B
How
not
to vectorize fermionic super-operators

A crucial step used in
[
10
]
is to “vectorize” a super-operator
\mathcal{C}:X\mapsto AXB
into an operator
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{C})=A\otimes B^{\top}
.
Now we apply it to fermionic super-operators

\mathcal{C}_{1}:X\mapsto\gamma_{1}X,\quad\mathcal{C}_{2}:X\mapsto X\gamma_{2},

They are then vectorized to be

\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{C}_{1})=\gamma_{1}\otimes I,\quad\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{C}_{2})=I\otimes\gamma_{2}.

First for the operation
\top
, we can interpret it as the linear involution in the Clifford algebra (as opposed to the anti-linear involution
{\dagger}
).

We then consider how one should interpret the tensor product
\otimes
. One may be tempted to equate
\gamma_{1}\otimes I
with
\gamma_{1}
, and
I\otimes\gamma_{2}
with
\gamma_{n+2}
, which is the “image” of
\gamma_{2}
in an ancillary system. Adopting this approach, we have

\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{C}_{1})=\gamma_{1},\quad\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{C}_{2})=\gamma_{n+2}.

Now we have a problem: while
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
commute with each other,
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{C}_{1})
and
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{C}_{2})
do not; in fact they anti-commute with each other. Therefore this naive vectorization approach fails to preserve the algebraic relations between fermionic super-operators.

With a small modification one can make
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{C}_{1})
and
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{C}_{2})
commute with each other. Instead of the what was done above, we define

\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{C}_{1})=\gamma_{1},\quad\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{C}_{2})=\gamma_{n+2}(-1)^{N}.

where
N
is the particle number operator and
(-1)^{N}
is the parity operator. This is in fact one of the insights of the third quantization approach
[
31
]
. However, we can see that this mapping maps an operator that is very local (
\mathcal{C}_{2}
) to something extremely non-local (
\gamma_{n+2}(-1)^{N}
acts non-trivially on every mode except for mode
n+2
). This will prevent us from applying the result in
[
30
]
.

The approach used in
[
31
]
and in this work is based on the observation that
(-1)^{N}
yields a factor of
1
when restricted to the even-parity sector. However, this does not mean we can treat
(-1)^{N}
as if it does not exist: by applying single Majorana operators one will switch between the even- and odd-parity sectors. This is why a great deal of effort of Section
4
was made to prove things that seem obvious (but are not) from
[
10
]
.

Transforming the Majorana operators

Lemma 23
.

Let
H_{0}=\sum_{jk}h_{jk}\gamma_{j}\gamma_{k},
where the matrix
(h_{jk})
is Hermitian and pure imaginary, then

e^{iH_{0}t}\gamma_{l}e^{-iH_{0}t}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(e^{4iht}\right)_{jl}\gamma_{j}.

(126)

Proof.

We use the notation
\operatorname{ad}_{A}(B)=[A,B]
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{ad}_{H_{0}}(\gamma_{l})

\displaystyle=[H_{0},\gamma_{l}]=\sum_{jk}h_{jk}[\gamma_{j}\gamma_{k},\gamma_{l}].

(127)

Using the commutation relation, we can calculate the commutator

\displaystyle=\gamma_{j}\gamma_{k}\gamma_{l}-\gamma_{l}\gamma_{j}\gamma_{k}

(128)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{j}\gamma_{k}\gamma_{l}+\gamma_{j}\gamma_{l}\gamma_{k}-\gamma_{j}\gamma_{l}\gamma_{k}-\gamma_{l}\gamma_{j}\gamma_{k}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{j}\{\gamma_{k},\gamma_{l}\}-\{\gamma_{j},\gamma_{l}\}\gamma_{k}

\displaystyle=2(\delta_{kl}\gamma_{j}-\delta_{jl}\gamma_{k}).

Therefore

\displaystyle\operatorname{ad}_{H_{0}}(\gamma_{l})

\displaystyle=2\left(\sum_{j}h_{jl}\gamma_{j}-\sum_{k}h_{lk}\gamma_{k}\right)=2\left(\sum_{j}h_{jl}\gamma_{j}+\sum_{k}h_{kl}\gamma_{k}\right)=4\sum_{j}h_{jl}\gamma_{j}.

(129)

As a result we have

\operatorname{ad}_{H_{0}}^{r}(\gamma_{l})=\sum_{j}\left(4h\right)^{r}_{jl}\gamma_{j}

(130)

Using the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff (BCH) formula
we get

\displaystyle e^{iH_{0}t}\gamma_{l}e^{-iH_{0}t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}\frac{1}{r!}\operatorname{ad}_{H_{0}}^{r}(\gamma_{l})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(e^{4iht}\right)_{jl}\gamma_{j}.

(131)

∎

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}A_{\alpha}^{*}i^{|\alpha|}(-1)^{\alpha_{1}\hat{n}}\hat{\gamma}_{2}^{\alpha_{1}}(-1)^{\alpha_{2}\hat{n}}\hat{\gamma}_{4}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots(-1)^{\alpha_{2n}\hat{n}}\hat{\gamma}_{4n}^{\alpha_{2n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}A_{\alpha}^{*}(-i)^{|\alpha|}\hat{\gamma}_{2}^{\alpha_{1}}(-1)^{\alpha_{1}\hat{n}}\hat{\gamma}_{4}^{\alpha_{2}}(-1)^{\alpha_{2}\hat{n}}\cdots\hat{\gamma}_{4n}^{\alpha_{2n}}(-1)^{\alpha_{2n}\hat{n}},

where for the second equality we have used
(-1)^{\hat{n}}\hat{\gamma}_{2j}=-\hat{\gamma}_{2j}(-1)^{\hat{n}}
.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\varphi\circ R_{A^{\dagger}}\circ\varphi^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}A_{\alpha}^{*}\varphi\circ(-i)^{|\alpha|}R_{\gamma_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}R_{\gamma_{2}}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots R_{\gamma_{2n}}^{\alpha_{2n}}\circ\varphi^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}A_{\alpha}^{*}(-i)^{|\alpha|}\hat{\gamma}_{2}^{\alpha_{1}}(-1)^{\alpha_{1}\hat{n}}\hat{\gamma}_{4}^{\alpha_{2}}(-1)^{\alpha_{2}\hat{n}}\cdots\hat{\gamma}_{4n}^{\alpha_{2n}}(-1)^{\alpha_{2n}\hat{n}}.

We therefore have
\varphi\circ R_{A^{\dagger}}\circ\varphi^{-1}=(\varphi\circ R_{A}\circ\varphi^{-1})^{\dagger}
.
∎

Corollary 11
.

For
A\in B(\mathcal{H})
, if
A
has even parity, then
\Phi(R_{A^{\dagger}})=\Phi(R_{A})^{\dagger}
. If
A
has odd parity, then
\Phi(R_{A^{\dagger}})=-\Phi(R_{A})^{\dagger}
.

Proof.

The even case follows directly from the definition of
\Phi
in Definition
5
and Lemma
26
. For the odd case, we have

\Phi(R_{A})^{\dagger}=(\varphi\circ R_{A}\circ\varphi^{-1}(-1)^{\hat{n}})^{\dagger}=(-1)^{\hat{n}}\varphi\circ R_{A^{\dagger}}\circ\varphi^{-1}=-\varphi\circ R_{A^{\dagger}}\circ\varphi^{-1}(-1)^{\hat{n}}=-\Phi(R_{A^{\dagger}}).

∎

Lemma 27
.

If
A\in B(\mathcal{H})
is positive semi-definite with even parity, then
\Phi(R_{A})
is also positive semi-definite.

Proof.

Since
A
is positive semi-definite, there exists
Q\in B(\mathcal{H})
with even parity such that
A=QQ^{\dagger}
. Therefore by Lemma
26

\Phi(R_{A})=\varphi\circ R_{Q^{\dagger}}R_{Q}\circ\varphi^{-1}=(\varphi\circ R_{Q^{\dagger}}\circ\varphi^{-1})(\varphi\circ R_{Q}\circ\varphi^{-1})=(\varphi\circ R_{Q}\circ\varphi^{-1})^{\dagger}(\varphi\circ R_{Q}\circ\varphi^{-1})

and is therefore positive semi-definite.
∎

Appendix F
Decay of the kernel function in the coherent part

We will first prove some elementary properties of
b_{1}(t)
defined in (
13
). We recall that

\displaystyle b_{1}(t)=2\sqrt{\pi}e^{\frac{1}{8}}\left(\frac{1}{\cosh(2\pi t)}*_{t}\sin(-t)e^{-2t^{2}}\right).

We will need to use the lemma below:

Lemma 28
.

If two real functions
f(t)
and
g(t)
satisfy
|f(t)|\leq C_{1}e^{-\lambda_{1}|t|}
and
|g(t)|\leq C_{2}e^{-\lambda_{2}|t|}
with positive constants
C_{1},C_{2},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
, then

|(f*g)(t)|\leq 2C_{1}C_{2}\left(\frac{e^{-\lambda_{2}|t|/2}}{\lambda_{1}}+\frac{e^{-\lambda_{1}|t|/2}}{\lambda_{2}}\right).

(136)

Proof.

The convolution can be bounded by:

\displaystyle|(f*g)(t)|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}sf(s)g(t-s)\right|<\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left|f(s)g(t-s)\right|\mathrm{d}s

(137)

\displaystyle=C_{1}C_{2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{1}|s|}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{2}|t-s|}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=C_{1}C_{2}\left(\int_{|s-t|\leq|t|/2}+\int_{|s-t|>|t|/2}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{1}|s|}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{2}|t-s|}\mathrm{d}s

For the integral for
|s-t|\leq|t|/2
, we have

\int_{|s-t|\leq|t|/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{1}|s|}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{2}|t-s|}\mathrm{d}s\leq e^{-\lambda_{1}|t|/2}\int\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{2}|t-s|}\mathrm{d}s\leq\frac{2e^{-\lambda_{1}|t|/2}}{\lambda_{2}}.

For the integral for
|s-t|>|t|/2
, we have

\int_{|s-t|>|t|/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{1}|s|}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{2}|t-s|}\mathrm{d}s\leq e^{-\lambda_{2}|t|/2}\int\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{1}|s|}\mathrm{d}s\leq\frac{2e^{-\lambda_{2}|t|/2}}{\lambda_{1}}.

Combining the two parts yields the desired inequality.
∎

Lemma 29
.

Let
b_{1}(t)
be as defined in (
13
), then there exist universal constants
C,\lambda>0
such that

b_{1}(t-i\delta)\leq C\cosh(\delta)e^{2\delta^{2}}e^{-\lambda t}.

Proof.

Note that
b_{1}(t)
is the convolution between two parts:

h_{1}(t)=2\sqrt{\pi}e^{\frac{1}{8}}\frac{1}{\cosh(2\pi t)},\quad h_{2}(t)\sin(-t)e^{-2t^{2}}.

Therefore

b_{1}(t-i\delta)=\int h_{1}(s)h_{2}(t-i\delta-s)\mathrm{d}s.

Let
h_{2,\delta}(t)=h_{2}(t-i\delta)
, then
b_{1}(t)=(h_{1}*h_{2,\delta})(t)
. Therefore we only need to prove the decay properties of
h_{1}(t)
and
h_{2,\delta}(t)
and then apply Lemma
28
.

For
h_{1}(t)
, we can readily get universal constants
C_{1},\lambda_{1}>0
such that
|h_{1}(t)|\leq C_{1}e^{-\lambda_{1}|t|}
. Therefore we only need to focus on
h_{2,\delta}(t)
. Note that

|h_{2,\delta}(t)|=|\sin(-t+i\delta)e^{-2(t-i\delta)^{2}}|\leq\cosh(\delta)e^{2\delta^{2}}e^{-2t^{2}}.

Because

such that
|h_{2,\delta}(t)|\leq C_{2}\cosh(\delta)e^{2\delta^{2}}e^{-\lambda_{2}|t|}
. The result then immediately follows from Lemma
28
.
∎
