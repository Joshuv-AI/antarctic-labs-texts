Patterns of failure analysis and the role of consolidative thoracic radiotherapy in extensive‑stage small‑cell lung cancer treated with first‑line chemoimmunotherapy
Authors: Aihua Lan, Huiling Dong, Jie Gao, Jinghan Qu, Zhiqin Zheng, Li Chu, Xi Yang, Jianjiao Ni, Zhengfei Zhu, Xiao Chu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe efficacy of consolidative thoracic radiotherapy (cTRT) for extensive-stage small-cell lung cancer (ES-SCLC) remains controversial in the immunotherapy era, and the failure patterns in these patients require further exploration. This study aimed to investigate the failure pattern and the efficacy of cTRT in ES-SCLC patients treated with first-line chemoimmunotherapy.MethodsBrain metastases (BMs)-free ES-SCLC patients treated with first-line chemoimmunotherapy were included and divided into different groups based on whether they received cTRT or not. Failure patterns were recorded. Kaplan-Meier survival curves and log-rank tests were used to compare the survival outcomes between the groups. Additionally, the survival benefits of cTRT were further evaluated through univariable and multivariate analyses. Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to balance the baseline characteristics.ResultsA total of 100 eligible BMs-free ES-SCLC patients were enrolled, with 24 (24.0%) in the cTRT group and 76 (76.0%) in the no-cTRT group. Failure pattern analyses revealed that intrathoracic failure remained the primary failure pattern in chemoimmunotherapy-treated ES-SCLC. The addition of cTRT to chemoimmunotherapy reduced the risk of intrathoracic failure (from 60.0% to 29.4%), and both intrathoracic and intracranial progression were delayed by cTRT before and after PSM. Moreover, cTRT significantly improved the overall survival (OS) of ES-SCLC patients compared to no-cTRT (median OS: not reached vs. 14.1 months; HR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.22–0.70, P = 0.01). Grade 3 pneumonitis was recorded in 9.7% and 3% of patients in the cTRT group and the no-cTRT group, respectively. No cases of grade 4 or 5 pneumonitis occurred.ConclusionsIn conclusion, this study characterized the failure patterns in BMs-free ES-SCLC patients treated with chemoimmunotherapy. cTRT significantly reduces intrathoracic and intracranial progression, and these benefits significantly translated into improved progression free survival and OS in chemoimmunotherapy-responsive ES-SCLC. These findings warrant the validation of prospective trials in the immunotherapy era.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13014-025-02783-9.Keywords: Extensive-stage small-cell lung cancer, Consolidative thoracic radiotherapy, Chemoimmunotherapy, Failure pattern, Survival outcome

Introduction

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) accounts for approximately 15% of all lung cancers and is characterized by rapid proliferation and early, widespread metastases, leading to a 5-year survival rate of less than 7% [1]. Approximately two-thirds of SCLC patients are diagnosed at the extensive stage (ES), and 24% of SCLC patients present with brain metastases (BMs) at the time of diagnosis [2]. Among patients without baseline BMs who do not receive prophylactic cranial irradiation (PCI), nearly 60% develop BMs within the first year after diagnosis [3]. Over the past decade, the standard first-line treatment for ES-SCLC consisted of platinum-based agents combined with etoposide chemotherapy regimens, yielding a median overall survival (OS) of approximately 10 months [4]. With the advent of immunotherapy, the standard first-line therapy for ES-SCLC has gradually shifted to chemoimmunotherapy, which has demonstrated improved median OS of 13–15 months [5–7]. Nevertheless, the majority of patients still experience disease progression within the first year of diagnosis.

In the pre-immunotherapy era, intrathoracic failure had been the dominant failure pattern and led to the conduction of the CREST study [8], which demonstrated the efficacy of consolidative thoracic radiotherapy (cTRT) in ES-SCLC. However, phase III randomized controlled trial regarding cTRT in ES-SCLC is lack in the immunotherapy era. In a post-hoc analysis of IMpower133, intrathoracic recurrence at existing sites was the primary failure pattern in ES-SCLC, observed in both the atezolizumab and placebo groups (34.4% vs. 34%) [5]. The CASPIAN [9] and IMpower133 [10] studies have demonstrated that chemoimmunotherapy can delay intracranial progression. However, recent real-world studies have reported that chemoimmunotherapy did not significantly delay intracranial progression in ES-SCLC patients without baseline BMs [11, 12]. In contrast, the addition of cTRT to chemoimmunotherapy has been shown to significantly prolong intracranial progression free survival (PFS) [12].

While multiple studies have examined the role of cTRT in ES-SCLC [13–18], its therapeutic impact remains controversial. Feng et al. conducted a meta-analysis including 15 real-world studies demonstrated that cTRT significantly improved OS in patients receiving immunotherapy [19]. These findings indicate that cTRT should be considered a valuable strategy for suppressing locoregional and intracranial progressions, and further improving OS in the context of chemoimmunotherapy. However, the clinical benefits of cTRT in the immunotherapy era remain uncertain due to the absence of phase III randomized controlled trials, and the use of cTRT in ES-SCLC patients treated with immunotherapy remains controversial. The recent European multicenter study by Monaca et al. [20], which evaluated 336 ES-SCLC patients (225 no-cTRT vs. 111 cTRT). While their findings showed significant improvement in intrathoracic control with cTRT, this did not translate into PFS and OS benefits. However, both prior small retrospective studies and the latest European data did not exclude patients with baseline BMs, with BMs present in ~ 15% of patients in most studies [13, 15, 20, 21] and up to ~ 30% in others [17, 18]. This contrasts with the CREST study [8], which specifically evaluated cTRT and PCI in BMs-free ES-SCLC patients. Therefore, we conducted this study to evaluate the impact of cTRT on initial failure patterns, intrathoracic and intracranial control, and survival outcomes in BMs-free ES-SCLC.

Methods

Patients and data collection

A total of 369 ES-SCLC patients diagnosed at Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center between January 2020 and June 2023 were screened for this study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) pathologically confirmed and adequately staged as BMs-free ES-SCLC (using contrast enhanced brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) or CT + Emission CT); (II) treatment with two or more cycles of standard first-line chemoimmunotherapy; (III) age ≥ 18 years; (IV) WHO performance status score ≤ 2; (V) no history of previous malignancies; (VI) availability of sufficient clinical history records and imaging data at baseline and during follow-up. The exclusion criteria included mixed histology of non-SCLC components, immunotherapy used in post-line setting, previous anti-cancer therapy, first-line tyrosine kinase inhibitors application, insufficient baseline imaging data, incomplete patients’ history records, lost to follow-up, WHO performance status score > 2 and patients with baseline BMs. The patient selection workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1. Patients’ characteristics, treatment records and imaging data at baseline and follow-up were fully collected. The study received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (No. 2310284-2). Informed consent from individual patients was waived in this retrospective analysis. This study was conducted in line with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013).

Patient selection flowchart. ES-SCLC: extensive stage-small cell lung cancer; cTRT: consolidative thoracic radiotherapy

Treatment and follow-up assessment

In the first-line setting, all enrolled patients received a combination of immunotherapy (programmed cell death ligand-1 (PD-L1) inhibitor or PD-1 inhibitor) and platinum-based chemotherapy. Subsequently, patients were divided into two groups based on whether they received cTRT: cTRT vs. no-cTRT. The dose of cTRT administered in our study ranged from 30 to 66 Gy (median dose = 54 Gy), with bioequivalent dose (BED, α/β = 10) of 39–79.2 Gy. The dose distribution was as follows: 30 Gy in 10 fractions (n = 1), 45 Gy in 30 fractions (n = 2), 50 Gy in 25 fractions (n = 4), 54 Gy in 27 fractions (n = 4), 56 Gy in 28 fractions (n = 4), 60 Gy in 30 fractions (n = 8), and 66 Gy in 33 fractions (n = 1). 7 patients in the no-cTRT group received PCI, while none in the cTRT group did. The median follow-up time was 13.8 months (range: 3–45 months). Patients were regularly monitored through clinical assessments (including symptoms and physical examinations) and imaging evaluations, which included CT scans of the chest and abdomen, brain MRI, and ultrasonography of the neck, supraclavicular region, and abdomen. Follow-up assessments were conducted at the following time points: (I) every two cycles of chemoimmunotherapy; (II) within 1 month after radiotherapy; (III) 3-month intervals for the first 2 years; (IV) 6-month intervals thereafter.

Outcomes

Survival outcomes, including OS, PFS, and intrathoracic/intracranial PFS, were evaluated in this study. OS was defined as the time from the initiation of chemoimmunotherapy to death or the last follow-up, while PFS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to disease progression, new metastasis, or the last follow-up. Intrathoracic/intracranial PFS referred to the development of any new lesion or progression of existing lesions within the thorax/brain. Additionally, this study documented the failure patterns of the included patients. Progression sites were categorized into three groups: brain, thorax, and other organs. If the progression site was unclear, it was recorded as unknown. Pneumonitis was assessed and graded by the investigators using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (version 5.0).

Statistical analysis

The comparison of categorical variables between the groups was generated by Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Kaplan-Meier survival curve and log-rank test were conducted to exhibit the differences in survival between different groups. Cox proportional hazard regression with estimated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were employed to evaluate the risk factors for survival outcomes. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to balance the baseline characteristics between two different groups, with paired covariates including age, gender, smoking status, WHO performance status, baseline liver metastases, baseline bone metastases, oligometastatic disease status (five or fewer metastatic lesions in two or fewer organs). Nearest neighbor matching (ratio = 1:1, caliper = 0.02) was used to create propensity score and match patients in the case and control groups. SPSS 26.0 software (IBM Corp, USA) was applied for comparing variables and conducting PSM between two cohorts, and GraphPad Prism Version 9.5.0 (GraphPad Software Corp, USA) was used for survival analysis.

Results

Patients’ characteristics

As shown in Fig. 1, 100 BMs-free ES-SCLC patients receiving first-line chemoimmunotherapy with sufficient data were included in our study. In which 24 were allocated to the cTRT group, and 76 to the no-cTRT group. Meanwhile, we also included 125 BMs-free ES-SCLC patients treating with chemotherapy as historical control. The median follow-up time for the chemoimmunotherapy group and chemotherapy group were 13.8 and 14.4 months, respectively. The baseline clinical parameters between the two treatment arms were compared and presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Survival benefits of chemoimmunotherapy and cTRT

Consistent with previous studies, our study demonstrated that, compared to chemotherapy (N = 125), the addition of first-line immunotherapy to standard chemotherapy (N = 100) significantly improved both PFS (median PFS: 7.3 vs. 6.9 months; HR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.56–0.97, P = 0.028) and OS (median OS: 15.4 vs. 14.1 months; HR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.52–0.94, P = 0.018) in ES-SCLC (Supplementary Fig. 1). Additionally, chemoimmunotherapy plus cTRT (N = 24) (median PFS, 13.8 months; median OS, not reached) exhibited significant longer PFS (P < 0.0001) and OS (P = 0.001) compared to the other three treatment regimens: chemoimmunotherapy without cTRT group (N = 76) (median PFS, 6.5 months; median OS, 14.1 months), chemotherapy with cTRT (N = 78) (median PFS, 7.8 months; median OS, 15.3 months), and chemotherapy without cTRT (N = 47) (median PFS, 5.9 months; median OS, 11.5 months) (Supplementary Fig. 2). Furthermore, we performed univariable and multivariate analyses to evaluate the prognostic values of different parameters in ES-SCLC. Both univariable and multivariate analyses revealed that baseline liver metastasis and baseline bone metastasis were significantly associated with worse PFS and OS (P < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 2).

Failure pattern analysis

Failure patterns in patients who received only systemic treatments without the interference of first-line radiotherapy were analyzed in the chemotherapy cohort and the chemoimmunotherapy cohort (Fig. 2A-B). In the chemotherapy group without first-line radiotherapy, the initial progression was observed in the thorax, brain, and other organs at rates of 48.1%, 13%, and 31.5%, respectively. In the chemoimmunotherapy group without first-line radiotherapy, the first progression occurred in the thorax, brain, and other organs at rates of 60%, 15.7%, and 24.3%, respectively. In both the chemotherapy and the chemoimmunotherapy cohorts, intrathoracic progression was the dominant failure patterns. Notably, the proportion of thoracic progression was higher in the chemoimmunotherapy group (60%) compared to the chemotherapy group (48.1%). Furthermore, as illustrated in Fig. 2C-D, the addition of cTRT to chemotherapy significantly reduced the frequency of intrathoracic progression (from 48.1% to 30%). When cTRT was applied to the chemoimmunotherapy group, the rate of intrathoracic failure decreased even more markedly (from 60% to 29.4%), suggesting a greater potential clinical benefit of cTRT following chemoimmunotherapy compared to chemotherapy. These results might suggest a potential synergistic effect between radiotherapy and immunotherapy.

The impact of cTRT on intrathoracic and intracranial control

24 patients in the chemoimmunotherapy group received cTRT and 76 did not. The baseline characteristics were well-balanced after PSM (Table 1). For all included patients, cTRT (N = 24) significantly improved intrathoracic PFS (median intrathoracic PFS: not reached vs. 8.4 months, HR = 0.20; 95% CI: 0.11–0.34, P < 0.0001), compared to no-cTRT (N = 76). After PSM, 22 pairs of patients were selected for further analysis. Similarly, cTRT significantly improved intrathoracic control (median intrathoracic PFS: not reached vs. 8 months, HR = 0.15; 95% CI: 0.06–0.37, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3A-B). To investigate the efficacy of cTRT in intracranial control, we excluded 7 patients who received PCI from chemoimmunotherapy group to avoid PCI-related bias. The intracranial progression was delayed by cTRT, with median intracranial PFS of 13.8 months for cTRT group (N = 24) and 9.9 months for no-cTRT group (N = 69) (P = 0.04). Similar results were reached after PSM (P = 0.03) (Fig. 3C-D).

The survival efficacy and safety of cTRT

In chemoimmunotherapy group (N = 100), survival analysis showed that cTRT (N = 24) significantly improved PFS (median PFS: 13.8 vs. 6.5 months, HR = 0.42; 95% CI: 0.27–0.65, P = 0.001) and OS (median OS: not reached vs. 14.1 months, HR = 0.40; 95% CI: 0.23–0.70, P = 0.01) compared to no-cTRT group (N = 76). After PSM, cTRT (N = 22) was still significantly associated with improved PFS (median PFS: 14 vs. 6.5 months, HR = 0.38; 95% CI: 0.19–0.78, P = 0.003) and OS (median OS: not reached vs. 14.5 months, HR = 0.42; 95% CI: 0.18–0.96, P = 0.04) (Fig. 4A-D). Grade 3 pneumonitis was recorded in 9.7% and 3% of patients in the cTRT group and the no-cTRT group, respectively. No cases of grade 4 or 5 pneumonitis occurred.

Discussion

While several studies have investigated the role of cTRT in ES-SCLC patients receiving immunotherapy, most have been limited by small sample sizes [13–18]. Until recently, a European multicenter analysis of 336 ES-SCLC patients demonstrated that cTRT significantly improved intrathoracic control, though this did not translate into significant improvements in PFS or OS [20]. However, these previous studies did not exclude patients with baseline BMs, this contrasts with the CREST study [8], which specifically evaluated cTRT and PCI in BMs-free ES-SCLC patients. To address the confounding effect of BMs, our study excluded patients with baseline BMs. Among the included patients, 7 in the no-cTRT group received PCI, while none in the cTRT group did. We analyzed all BMs-free patients to evaluate the effects of cTRT on intrathoracic PFS, PFS, and OS, but excluded PCI-treated patients in the no-cTRT group when assessing intracranial PFS to avoid PCI-related bias. To our knowledge, the clinical benefits of cTRT in ES-SCLC with BMs remain uncertain in immunotherapy era. Our prior research investigated the survival impact of TRT in ES-SCLC with BMs, showing that TRT significantly prolonged OS in patients whose BMs were controlled at all sites and who remained asymptomatic for more than 3 months after treatment initiation [22]. Moving forward, we plan to further explore the role of TRT in BMs patients receiving immunotherapy once a sufficient sample size is available.

Notably, we conducted a comprehensive failure pattern analysis of ES-SCLC in the immunotherapy era and evaluated the impact of cTRT on progression phenotype distribution—a novel contribution, as this remains unexplored in prior literature. The failure pattern in patients treated with only systemic therapies was a major indicator for local intervention therapies, which could guide consolidation local therapy to sites that were most susceptible to disease progression. In ES-SCLC, the brain was the most frequent site of metastasis and the EORTC study established the role of PCI [23]. In ES-SCLC treated with chemotherapy and PCI, intrathoracic disease progression was the dominant pattern. The CREST study demonstrated that cTRT with a moderate dose of 30 Gy in 10 fractions significantly reduced intrathoracic progression risk and improved 2-year survival rate, which established the role of cTRT in chemotherapy era [8]. In this study, we first conducted failure pattern analysis in both chemotherapy cohort and chemoimmunotherapy cohort. As shown in the results (Fig. 2), intrathoracic failure remained the dominant initial failure pattern, and the intrathoracic failure rate was higher in the chemoimmunotherapy group (60%) than in the chemotherapy group (48.1%). These results highlighted the necessity of cTRT in ES-SCLC treated with chemoimmunotherapy. We also evaluated the impact of cTRT on initial failure patterns, the results showed that cTRT reduced the frequency of intrathoracic progression in the historical chemotherapy control group (from 48.1% to 30%) and the chemoimmunotherapy group (from 60% to 29.4%). Moreover, the chemoimmunotherapy plus cTRT group exhibited significantly better PFS and OS compared to chemoimmunotherapy alone and chemotherapy plus cTRT (Supplementary Fig. 2). These results might suggest a potential synergistic effect between radiotherapy and immunotherapy.

Previous reports regarding the efficacy of cTRT following chemoimmunotherapy has been controversial. Some reported OS and/or PFS improvement in cTRT treated ES-SCLC patients [17, 24, 25], while the study by Gross et al. [26] reported that chemoimmunotherapy-treated patients receiving cTRT at a median dose of 32.75 Gy did not achieve long-term intrathoracic disease control and showed no independent improvement in either OS or PFS. Meanwhile, in the European multicenter study [20], the majority (73.9%, n = 80) patients received a standard TRT dose (~ 30 Gy in 10 fractions), only 21.3% patients (n = 23) received higher-dose of TRT (45–60 Gy). The analysis showed that patients receiving higher-dose TRT experienced significantly improved PFS and OS compared to those who receiving the standard 30 Gy dose. However, compared to no-cTRT group, cTRT only significantly improved intrathoracic control but failed to significantly prolonged PFS and OS. In our study, cTRT exhibited significantly improved intrathoracic PFS, PFS, and OS (Figs. 3 and 4) in chemoimmunotherapy-treated ES-SCLC patients. These results remained statistically significant after PSM. The higher dose of cTRT (median dose = 54 Gy) applied in our study is likely the reason for improved survival outcome: among the 24 patients in cTRT group, only one patient received a standard TRT dose (~ 30 Gy in 10 fractions), the rest (n = 23) all received higher-dose of TRT (45–66 Gy). Besides, another reasonable explanation could be that ES-SCLC patients have increased OS in the current era of immunotherapy, which allows for a longer time for intrathoracic failure to develop due to bulky disease in the thorax and the palliative nature of lower radiation doses. Bulky disease in the thorax is quite common, and a palliative dose of 30 Gy may not be sufficient to achieve durable intrathoracic control compared to higher doses delivered in limited-stage SCLC, such as 45 Gy in hyperfractionation or 66 Gy in daily fractionation, among other higher-dose regimens described in the literature. A recent phase II study also suggested that cTRT (median dose = 50 Gy) plus chemoimmunotherapy could significantly improve OS compared to chemoimmunotherapy alone in ES-SCLC [27]. These evidences combined with our study suggest that a higher dose of cTRT might be required to efficiently control intrathoracic disease.

Additionally, we excluded patients who received PCI from the chemoimmunotherapy group to investigate the efficacy of cTRT in intracranial control. The result suggested that intracranial progression was delayed by cTRT compared to no-cTRT (median intracranial PFS: 13.8 vs. 9.9 months, P = 0.04). Although chemoimmunotherapy has been shown to delay intracranial progression in clinical studies, recent retrospective research indicates that it does not delay the occurrence of BMs in patients initially free of intracranial involvement [11, 12]. In contrast, the addition of cTRT to chemoimmunotherapy has been shown to significantly prolong intracranial PFS [12]. There are several possible mechanisms that could explain why cTRT helps suppress intracranial progression. Firstly, adding cTRT to systemic therapies could lead to better extracranial disease control, and subsequently, less shedding of metastatic cells from the tumor mass and reduced seeding into the brain. Secondly, cTRT could induce immunogenic tumor cell death which exposes of tumor-specific antigens, enhancing the efficiency of immunotherapy [28]. This provides a theoretical basis for the suppression of intracranial progression through the combination of cTRT and chemoimmunotherapy.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings: First, as a retrospective analysis, our study is inherently susceptible to selection bias and unmeasured confounding factors that could influence the observed outcomes. While we implemented rigorous statistical adjustments, these inherent limitations of retrospective designs must be acknowledged. Second, our cohort size is relatively small, particularly in the cTRT subgroup (N = 24), may limit the statistical power to detect clinically meaningful differences in survival outcomes based on radiation dose parameters. This precluded more detailed dose-response analyses that might have provided additional insights. Finally, the potential impact of cTRT timing remains uncertain and warrants dedicated investigation in future. Therefore, a large-scale, multicenter, prospective study is warranted to confirm our findings. Given that a palliative dose of 30 Gy may not be sufficient to achieve durable intrathoracic control, we are currently conducting a phase I study involving dose escalation of cTRT starting from 30 Gy/10 fractions in ES-SCLC patients treated with first-line chemoimmunotherapy to evaluate the safety of this treatment regimen. This study, registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier: NCT06187740) [29] has been initiated.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study characterized the failure patterns in BMs-free ES-SCLC patients treated with chemoimmunotherapy. cTRT significantly reduces intrathoracic and intracranial progression, and these benefits significantly translated into improved PFS and OS in chemoimmunotherapy-responsive ES-SCLC. These findings warrant the validation of prospective trials in the immunotherapy era.
