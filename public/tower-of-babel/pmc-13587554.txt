An empirical analysis of Living Well (Buen Vivir) among indigenous peoples in Latin America and the Caribbean: systematic review
Authors: Lorena P Gallardo-Peralta, Yrma Magaly López Torres, Rosa Araya Añicoy, Raquel Latorre Martínez, Karla Lara Vargas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is extensive scientific debate regarding ancestral knowledge of Living Well (Buen Vivir) in Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC), with potential policy ramifications. The debate is positioned as a decolonial response and concerns situated knowledge as opposed to the developmentalist, extractivist and anthropocentric ideas that dominate in various neoliberal LAC States.ObjectiveThe basis for this article is a systematic review of scientific articles that empirically analyse Living Well in LAC, guided by the following questions: In which countries and indigenous communities has this topic been researched? Which have been the main methodologies used? Have they been participative in nature? Which dimensions of Living Well have studies most frequently examined?MethodsThe SALSA methodology (Search, AppraisaL, Synthesis and Analysis) was used to carry out the systematic literature review. The Web of Science (WoS), Scopus, Dialnet, SciELO and PubMed bibliography databases were selected to search and identify relevant literature.ResultsThe results show 57 studies, led by Colombia (13) and Ecuador (12), mainly based on qualitative research (49), with a limited number using participative methodologies (10). This review includes 58 indigenous peoples, with the most extensively studied being the Quechua/Kichwa/Kiwcha people (16).ConclusionThe underlying dimensions can be grouped into: nature and territory; community organisation; health and holistic wellbeing; intergenerational care and gender roles; and indigenous and intercultural training and education. Although not constituting an independent dimension, several studies also explored the values and principles underpinning Living Well across these thematic area.

Introduction

Approximately 58 million people who self-identify as indigenous are living in Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC), representing 10% of the population and spanning over 800 communities (United Nations Educational et al., 2025). In most cases, these communities experience severe social vulnerability linked to high rates of illiteracy, poverty, precarious living conditions, higher rates of illness, lower life expectancy and multiple difficulties with accessing basic sanitation, education and social-health services (Anderson et al., 2016; Ferdinand et al., 2020; Flores-Ramírez et al., 2021; Sánchez-Moreno et al., 2021). To understand these structural inequalities, it is necessary to conduct a critical analysis of the impact of processes of colonisation, cultural assimilation, extractivism, territorial dispossession and pollution of ancestral lands (Dunbar-Ortiz, 2007; González et al., 2023). Added to this is a lengthy history of State-directed violent and racist actions aimed at these communities (Caumartin et al., 2008; Telles & Bailey, 2013).

Within the framework of political actions to counteract this complex sociopolitical situation in LAC, ethnicity has been included as a central element to mitigate historical damage and also to promote human development that is connected to ethno-cultural and territorial reality (Coral, 2018). However, the use of ethnicity as a category in public policy has also been subject to critical debate, insofar as it can function as a mechanism for limited recognition within state frameworks of multiculturalism (Pujadas, 2011). Under a vague policy of cultural recognition, the multicultural governance model in Latin America and the Caribbean “shapes, delimits, and produces cultural difference rather than suppressing it” (Hale, 2005, pp. 12-13). This constitutes a deliberate use of cultural rights to promote the neoliberal agenda. In this complex context of diversity management, countries such as Bolivia and Ecuador have incorporated the rights of indigenous communities into their constitutions (Coral, 2018; Hartmann, 2019). These actions are linked to the integration of the Living Well (Buen Vivir) paradigm into political and institutional spheres.

Living Well draws on the ancestral traditions of indigenous peoples in LAC, but its contemporary formulation has been shaped within the context of broader historical, left-wing political and institutional processes (Carpio & Ullán de La Rosa, 2021; Hartmann, 2019). Specifically in Ecuador, this is promoted through a socio-state approach aimed at implementing a redistribution model that secures the right to fulfil basic needs such as healthy food and drinking water (Marchand & Hérault, 2019). In the case of Bolivia, there is also a drive to promote other alternatives for economic and social development, with a strong presence of social movements and indigenous and left-wing activists (Ranta, 2016). Rather than an unmediated indigenous philosophy, therefore, Living Well is a contested concept, subject to reinterpretation, institutionalisation and politicisation.

Living Well is rooted in the ancestral traditions of indigenous communities in LAC. The Kichwa/Quechua people (Sumak Kawsay) and the Aymara people (Suma Qamaña) refer to the broad understanding of a “full life” or “life in fullness”, underpinned by harmonious and balanced links between people, territory and nature (Vásquez et al., 2021). The Mapuche also refer to Küme Mogen, which is underpinned by being part of a greater whole: nature. In this regard, the community is related to its surroundings, made up of the air, land, underground, mountains, rocks, water, plants, animals, people and spiritual beings that comprise “ixofil mogen” (biodiversity). This complex relational system integrates material, territorial, spiritual and symbolic dimensions, rather than comprising an abstract reference to nature. This interconnection is symmetric and based on horizontal and reciprocal relationships, which can be balanced to achieve the state of “wellbeing” (Beltrán-Véliz et al., 2022). Living Well is also based on the interconnection of the dimensions of life, with nature as a central element in the wellbeing of communities (Villalba, 2013): “it implies positive coexistence, based on unity, exchange, solidarity, reciprocity and respect as fundamental values” (Ortelli & Gómez, 2015, p. 144).

Developing the situated knowledge of Living Well involves going beyond the culturalist and even romantic notion of the link between indigenous communities and their territory (Carreño-Calderón & Ortega-Breña, 2021; Gerlach, 2019) to posit a decolonial proposal in response to the developmentalist, extractivist and anthropocentric vision of neoliberal LAC States (Borges et al., 2024; Ferrer et al., 2019; Godden, 2021). By advocating Living Well, one pursues the rights of ethnic and territorial self-determination for communities (Calderón et al., 2021), in addition to recognising indigenous knowledge as historical production (Carreño-Calderón & Ortega-Breña, 2021).

This study

This study consists of a systematic review of scientific articles that empirically analyse Living Well in LAC, guided by the following questions: In which countries and indigenous communities has this topic been researched? Which have been the main methodologies used? Have they been participative in nature? Which dimensions of Living Well have studies most frequently examined? This review was conducted within the framework of a R&D + i research project (Ref. 1250039) entitled “Toward a situated model of Living Well: narratives, intersectionality and life course among Aymara and Mapuche older adults”, the aim of which is to construct a situated model of Living Well among indigenous older adults in Chile. Against this backdrop, the study aims to highlight how Living Well is addressed and applied in academic literature from LAC and how it relates to public policies with an intercultural focus.

Methodology

Study design

The SALSA methodology (Search, AppraisaL, Synthesis and Analysis) was used to carry out the systematic literature review. SALSA is a four-stage method: search involves defining the searching string and types of databases; appraisal applies pre-defined literature inclusion and exclusion criteria and quality assessment criteria; synthesis entails extracting and categorising the data; and analysis is the final phase, involving presenting results and drawing conclusions (Grant & Booth, 2009). The protocol was registered in PROSPERO 2026 CRD420261325542.

Search strategy

The Web of Science (WoS), Scopus, Dialnet, SciELO and PubMed bibliography databases were selected to search and identify relevant literature, applying the following inclusion criteria: studies on Living Well, focused on LAC indigenous communities, which included empirical results (excluding essays, reports, letters to the editor, policy documents, etc.) and were written in English or Spanish.

The search was conducted in the title, abstract and keyword fields, and it was restricted to publications between 1 January 2010 and 1 September 2025. The aim was to capture the evolution of knowledge on Living Well over the last fifteen years, taking into account the political and economic transformations in LAC (Hartmann, 2019). Data collection and extraction

Five researchers participated, led by a senior researcher with the four other researchers completing doctoral studies. In the early stages of the screening process, a double-blind system was used via the Rayyan Manager systematic review app (Ouzzani et al., 2016), with all researchers participating simultaneously. The search produced a total of 829 articles. Duplicates were eliminated, resulting in the exclusion of 132 articles and a remaining total of 697. The application of the thematic inclusion criteria led to the rejection of 611 documents after reviewing title, abstract and keywords.

The team subsequently reviewed the full texts and excluded 29 articles for the following reasons: not analysing Living Well (10), research conducted outside of LAC (1), not empirical research (13) and being written in languages other than English or Spanish (5). Analytical strategy

The review employed a narrative text analysis (Ferrari, 2015). Following the data extraction process, the research team conducted an inductive thematic analysis of the selected studies in order to identify recurring concepts, empirical findings and analytical themes related to Living Well. Through an interactive process of reading, comparison and discussion among the researchers, similar themes were grouped into broader categories that structured the narrative synthesis. This process resulted in the identification of five major dimensions of Living Well in the literature: nature and territory; community organisation; health and holistic wellbeing; intergenerational care and gender roles; and indigenous and intercultural training and education. Additionally, a group of studies addressing values and principles underpinning Living Well across these dimensions was identified. Consequently, the findings include some verbatim quotes to underscore the central ideas of the dimensions of Living Well.

Ethics statement

This study had an ethical follow-up at all stages, being approved by the Ethical Committee of Alberto Hurtado University (Resolution VRIP_CE 15/2025).

The indigenous communities with regard to which research was conducted comprised 58 peoples, with the following appearing the most frequently: Quechua/Kichwa/Kiwcha (16), Nasa (6), Aymara (4), Mapuche (3), Ashaninka (2), Atacameño (2), Awajún (2), Guaraní (2), Maya (2), Misak (2), Pataxó (2), Pijao (2) and Tekoa (2). Notably, some studies made generic references to indigenous peoples and hence defined the territorial location rather than the specific ethnic group under study (Godden, 2021; Grisales-Pascuaza & Rincón-Isaza, 2024; Hartmann, 2019; Macías-Zambrano et al., 2016; Marchand & Hérault, 2019; Pérez Gañán, 2017; Rengifo et al., 2022; Ruiz Chagna & Iglesias Rodríguez, 2023).

With respect to the research design, most studies were qualitative (49) and used mainly ethnographic methodologies (12). Ten of the studies were participative and hence included communities in much or all of the research process. A significant number of mixed studies (7) were also observed, involving supplementation with research techniques such as interviews and/or group discussions, together with the application of a questionnaire. Only one study had a quantitative design, with a sample of 774 indigenous women (Gallardo-Peralta, 2025).

Nature and territory

Nature and territory are the most frequently analysed dimension in studies of Living Well in LAC. The topic of nature and territory can be divided into the following sub-dimensions: caring for Pachamama or Mother Earth (Calderón et al., 2021; Huambachano, 2018; Quintana, 2024); a foundational element of Living Well (Hoinle, 2025; Macías-Zambrano et al., 2016; Montoya et al., 2020; Nebbiai & Cáceres-Arteaga, 2023; Oikonomakis, 2020; Ponce, 2024); a space for learning and cornerstone of indigenous identity (Medina-Melgarejo et al., 2023; Merino, 2016; Ortiz, 2024); a living network of social, ecological and spiritual relationships (Ayenao, 2025; Borges et al., 2024; Kantner & Peixoto, 2023; Killick, 2020; Merino, 2021) and a foundational condition for mental health (Gutiérrez-Carmona et al., 2019). The texts analysed summarise the main qualitative findings regarding the interpretation and integration of nature within the Living Well paradigm.

Living Well is based on salvaging, maintaining and resisting through ancestral practices that are underpinned by coexistence, solidarity and shared responsibility for the care of Mother Earth (Calderón et al., 2021). Quintana (2024) shows that these practices enable the reciprocal preservation of ecosystems and human relationships, safeguarding solidarity and communal exchange.

Nature underpins the Living Well worldview, as indigenous communities understand wellbeing through balanced relationships of respect and reciprocity with their surroundings:

We act from the perspectives of a worldview that has its origins in nature, right? Just like the environment we live in, where we constantly interact with all the people who belong to our culture, our surroundings, our environments. (Montoya et al., 2020, p. 1161)

Nature is a living space for the transmission of practices and knowledge, in which children learn by observing, listening, participating and feeling. In this respect, for indigenous children living in rural contexts, “nature and community life are the first libraries that children consult, bio/epistemologies translated into relational ontologies” (Medina-Melgarejo et al., 2023, p. 132). The relationship with nature is a physical, everyday experience that is necessary for Living Well, since this knowledge and understanding facilitates a relationship of valuing and reciprocity between communities and the natural world. Territory is fundamental to intergenerational communication, as the transmission of knowledge is inextricably linked to ancestry and territoriality (Ortiz, 2024).

Territory is where human relationships and natural ecosystems are interconnected: “without territory, Indigenous and Afro-descendent peoples lose their humanity through the negation of their lifeways” (Kantner & Peixoto, 2023, p. 476). It is hence the physical space in which peoples maintain life and perpetuate culture, providing symbolic, spiritual and political support for their existence:

For Indigenous peoples, caring for the land is caring for life itself. Buen Vivir emphasizes the ethical responsibility to protect and nurture the environment, as land and nature are sacred and sustain the wellbeing of present and future generations. (Huambachano, 2018, p. 6)

It is a space of identity, where indigenous peoples are recognised based on their spiritual and historical relationship with the land: “territory is not a resource but a space of life, identity, and collective autonomy” (Merino, 2016, p. 278).

Importantly, this worldview also demonstrates the relationship between nature and positive mental health. Indigenous cultures feature a symbiotic relationship between the health of the body, the health of the people and the health of the land, comprising what is esssentially a body-territory (da Silva et al., 2024).

In summary, nature and territory are understood not as productive resources, but as living networks of spiritual, ecological and social relationships that sustain the continuity of life. Without territory, there is no Living Well: “the construction of Bem Viver and territory occurs as a joint process” (Kantner & Peixoto, 2023, p. 491).

Community organisation

The studies reviewed approach the community organisation dimension of Buen Vivir as a set of collective practices grounded in reciprocity, solidarity and mutual aid. Based on the analysis of these articles, the following sub-dimensions of community organisation were identified: practices aimed at the reproduction of life (Bueno et al., 2023; Coral, 2018; Ferrer et al., 2019; Jiménez et al., 2022; Ordoñez Sotomayor & Ochoa Cueva, 2020); forms of governance and sociocultural organisation (Ferrer et al., 2019; Manning, 2025; Martínez & Guerrero, 2019; Ortelli & Gómez, 2015; Samper et al., 2024); and tensions and disputes surrounding community organisation (Giovannini, 2016; Marchand & Hérault, 2019; Pérez Gañán, 2017; Ranta, 2016).

Regarding community organisation practices aimed at the reproduction of life, the studies emphasise collective forms of cooperation, reciprocity and economic self-management oriented towards sustaining communal wellbeing. In the experience of the Masewal people, the concept of yeknemilis (good life) materialises in the Tosepan Titataniske cooperative, where collective organisation and social learning form the basis of wellbeing and autonomy: “capacity building is interrelated to involvement in a collective agenda” (Bueno et al., 2023, p. 11). This cooperative gives meaning to community achievements such as collectives and collective work groups (mingas or tequios), assemblies and the defence of the territory, consolidating cooperation, communication and mutual care. For one cooperative member, “I learned that by being organized, we do have results (…) I have learned how to work together; we get organized like when working the land. We have also learned” (Bueno et al., 2023, p. 12). More than a means of production, this organisation offers education and autonomy, reinforcing collective action and the solidarity economy in harmony with nature.

This community-based organisation is also reflected in the economy, where the Allin Kawsay prioritises self-consumption and collective living over accumulation (Ferrer et al., 2019). On a supplementary basis from a decolonial perspective, Jiménez et al. (2022) analyse the case of Potato Park in Peru, a paradigmatic example of communitarian economy. Intercommunity organisation articulates three ayllus—runa, sallka and auki—that symbolise interdependence between the human, the natural and the sacred. The communities apply a collective protocol to manage micro-enterprises, eco-tourism and communal funds that redistribute 10% of earnings, consolidating “an Indigenous-led solidarity economy associated with traditional knowledge” (Jiménez et al., 2022, p. 1640). This intercultural governance redefines economic innovation within ecological limits and prioritises common wellbeing over profit.

Concerning forms of governance and socio-cultural organisation, Living Well is also expressed through female leadership. A study of Maya women reported a horizontal organisation based on the rotation of leadership, self-management and collaborative economy. The collectively practised art of weaving enables them to survive poverty and patriarchy, at the same time as reasserting their identity: “communitarian organising cultivates Buen Vivir through the decentralisation of power, self-management and participative decision-making based on collective, consensus-based participation by members” (Manning, 2025, p. 15). This form of feminine communality represents a resistance economy that balances the need for income with cultural reciprocity.

Symbolic practices also fulfil an organisational function. Martínez and Guerrero (2019) highlight the centrality of Tulpa, Minga and Tejido among the Nasa people as keys to communication and cultural production: “communication and education enable the Nasa to assume a position of fighting to preserve their culture, which includes different forms of economy and distribution of power” (Martínez & Guerrero, 2019, p. 687). Likewise, Samper et al. (2024) emphasise how community music generates communitas, strengthening social cohesion and material and spiritual sustainability: “the Indigenous concept of ‘buen vivir/Sumak Kawsay’ or ‘good living’ implies a holistic world-view, emphasizing harmony with nature, community well-being and a balance between material and spiritual aspects of life” (Samper et al., 2024, p. 304). In both cases, economic organisation is intertwined with the aesthetic and affective dimensions of communal life.

Intergenerational relationships are another aspect of community organisation. In this respect, the cargos (ladder of responsibilities) system acts as a “school”, where caregiving relationships are expressed through intergenerational transmission of knowledge: “In addition to providing training, the ladder of responsibilities also creates a structure for defence, monitoring and containment in response to individualism and the ongoing challenges imposed by modernity and neoliberalism” (Ortelli & Gómez, 2015, p. 168). The organisational system of indigenous communities tends to be based on the hierarchy of seniority, such that a person will acquire more significant responsibilities as they age: “The gerontocratic principle of older to younger forms the backbone of a system in which respect for elders is a fundamental value” (Ortelli & Gómez, 2015, p. 164).

Finally, tensions and disputes are emerging within community-based forms of organisation. In this regard, Pérez Gañán (2017) asserts that Buen Vivir (Ecuador) and Vivir Bien (Bolivia) have been instrumentalised by their respective States, reproducing tutelage and dependency so that “native identities and cosmovisions have been homogenised under a distorted one-size-fits-all slogan” (Pérez Gañán, 2017, p. 154), distorting the community meaning of living and coexisting under this paradigm. Ranta (2016) similarly describes how the Bolivian government adopted a model where Vivir Bien translates into growth and a lack of protection for the environment and indigenous people’s rights: “the Bolivian case rather resembles state-led extractive capitalism” (Ranta, 2016, p. 436). Marchand and Hérault (2019) add that the implementation of Living Well in Ecuador consolidated a vertical and oil-dependent institutional structure, redirecting the discourse regarding Living Well: “the concept has been instrumentalised and turned into a kind of speech of legitimization of all types of productive activities” (Marchand & Hérault, 2019, p. 289).

In short, studies show that the community organisation of Living Well is communal and underpinned by reciprocity, redistribution and cooperation, but faces the constant threat of absorption by the logics of State and capital. Communities are responding by asserting rights to decide over their economy, knowledge and territory. This collective, ethical and sustainable quest encapsulates the meanings that Living Well assumes in the reviewed literature as a vision of a dignified and pluralistic life. Reflecting the cooperative's broader concern with collective wellbeing beyond economic matters, a Masewal promoter explained:

The cooperative is there to assess people. This is not necessarily on economic matters; there is another way to be happy. We look for ways to happiness in every community, like yeknemilis, a ‘good living’ in harmony with all. (Bueno et al., 2023, p. 10)

Health and holistic wellbeing

Health and holistic wellbeing is also widely addressed in Living Well studies, and typically explored through the following sub-themes: holistic understandings of wellbeing (Gallardo-Peralta, 2025; Montoya et al., 2020); balance and harmony between community and nature (Achatz et al., 2021; Mukerji et al., 2025); illness as a result of imbalance (Túm, 2024); food autonomy (Calderón et al., 2021); ancestral indigenous medicine (Carreño-Calderón & Ortega-Breña, 2021; López, 2021); and intercultural health (Bautista-Valarezo et al., 2021; Hartmann, 2019; Téllez, 2022).

From the perspective of Living Well, health is not merely the absence of physical or mental illness but a holistic condition of wellbeing (Gallardo-Peralta, 2025; Montoya et al., 2020). An interdependent relationship between nature and individuals generates the balance of mutual wellbeing: “health care depends on an equilibrium between the individual body, tradition embodied in the community, and nature” (Téllez, 2022, p. 997). Accordingly, individual wellbeing emerges from harmonious and reciprocal relationships with both community and the natural world (Achatz et al., 2021; Montoya et al., 2020).

The forest (auadip) is beautiful to me; it makes me joyful and makes me recognise who I am. If I am not in the forest, I feel strange, unfocused and exhausted. And if the forest is attacked and hurt, I get hurt. I get sick if the forest gets sick, because my body and my speech are also made of the forest. (Achatz et al., 2021, p. 10)

Changes to this balance can hence cause illness: “When we do not walk with the moon and the sun, according to the 365-day solar calendar and the 360-day lunar calendar, there is no balance in our lives and that is why illnesses and accidents occur during labour…” (Túm, 2024, p. 186).

The holistic understanding of wellbeing also includes cultural identity, cosmovision, territoriality and food autonomy (Montoya et al., 2020). In this context, food autonomy emerges as a form of resistance to social and political crises associated with modernity (Calderón et al., 2021), referring to the capacity of peoples to govern their own nutritional practices according to their traditions, needs and collective perspectives:

We have to keep insisting that healthy foodstuffs are nutritious; that they help the body protect itself from diseases; we must continue remembering that our ancestors lived vigorously, both spiritually as well as physically. When asked ‘What do you do to last all these years?’ They answer, ‘Eat healthy’ .(Calderón et al., 2021, p. 54)

Within the Living Well paradigm, ancestral medicine is understood not merely as a therapeutic resource but as part of a broader relational system linking health, spirituality, territory and community life. Certain studies have focused on the features of ancestral or indigenous medicine through interviews with community doctors (Carreño-Calderón & Ortega-Breña, 2021; López, 2021; Túm, 2024). Communities themselves continue to draw on this ancestral knowledge and traditional practices, as one indigenous doctor explains: “At 89 years of age, I am still in demand. This is how you cure what cannot be cured in hospitals. They bring them to me in ambulances to see what I can do. I started when I was 14, and I will only stop doing this when God takes my life” (López, 2021, p. 132).

From the perspective of Living Well, intercultural health policies should recognise and support these relational understandings of wellbeing rather than subordinating them to biomedical frameworks. The reviewed studies also include a critical analysis of the application of intercultural health policies, which tend to be mechanisms for domination and cultural homogenisation (Carreño-Calderón & Ortega-Breña, 2021; Hartmann, 2019), with allopathic medicine taking precedence over indigenous medicine (Bautista-Valarezo et al., 2021; Téllez, 2022): “In the head of the doctors there is still no interculturality. Let’s say, it is only in the word but nothing more, according to them they totally know everything. Of course, we are there, but they do not take us into account” (Bautista-Valarezo et al., 2021, p. 4).

From the perspective of Living Well, health emerges as a relational dimension grounded in the balance between communities, territory and nature. Despite regulatory advances, healthcare policies remain anchored to a biomedical and individualising logic, detached from the relational and holistic understanding that underpins wellbeing in communities and causing tension between intercultural policies and the community understanding of wellbeing.

Intergenerational care and gender roles

As a philosophy centred on circular and reciprocal care between communities and nature, Living Well also involves care as a key dimension. Closely linked to gender, care is typically operationalised through the following sub-dimensions: the value of care (Coral et al., 2021); community care (van Der Boor et al., 2024); pregnancy, childbirth and maternal care (Ferrer-Pérez et al., 2025); and gender complementarity in care (Gallardo & Siqueira, 2025; García, 2016).

This is reflected in the values of the Kankuamo people, who “consider it their responsibility to care for their surroundings and for their surroundings to care for them, implying a mutual obligation rather than exclusive dependency on human agency” (van Der Boor et al., 2024, p. 10). Caring for nature is part of caring for the community surroundings: “caring for the forest is very important because all our wealth is already there” (Coral et al., 2021, p. 41).

As such, caregiving in the community involves defending the community and pursuing its wellbeing, which translates into wellbeing for each person. Understood as a whole, the community cares, shares, works and remains united in the face of individualism (Coral et al., 2021). This is also reflected in maternity: “supporting women during pregnancy is a key element of caregiving” (Ferrer-Pérez et al., 2025, p. 45). Births are hugely important insofar as they enable indigenous people to survive over time. As reported by Ferrer-Pérez et al. (2025, p. 42): “for the Nasa community, pregnancy represents a seed and a central cycle that secures the survival of the people”. Pregnancy is closely linked to these values, so the gestation process is continuously guided and supported, with a particular emphasis on food and conventional remedies. This accompaniment is predominantly led by traditional midwives and doctors, who use their knowledge to guide the pregnancy and postpartum process as part of an ongoing relationship with nature. This process culminates with the introduction of the baby to the community, in which it will come to play an active role, as explained by an indigenous midwife:

... for me, a woman’s pregnancy is a cycle that the family has to experience; it is an absolutely fundamental part, because it’s there that the mother starts to have contact with traditional medicine and midwives; so it’s where a cycle that is unique to us begins. (Ferrer-Pérez et al., 2025, p. 42)

In terms of gender roles in caregiving, and in contrast to the capitalist conception of time, caregiving requires processes that cannot be measured in monetary terms. Women have less time for self-care and dedicate more time, quantitatively and qualitatively, to caring for the family, particularly in emotional terms (Gallardo & Siqueira, 2025). The situation is balanced in terms of care for the community, where men and women make significant efforts to protect nature from extractivism and preserve the values of Living Well. Among the Mapuche, this “complementariness” between men and women is considered one of the principles of Living Well, entailing the pursuit of gender equality (García, 2016).

Living well means prioritising complementarity, which posits that all beings living on the planet complement each other. Children complement the elderly, men complement women, darkness complements light, positive complements negative. Nothing in Waj Mapu exists without its counterpart. Opposites are complementary, not antagonistic. (García, 2016, p. 27)

In summary, caregiving constitutes a foundational element of Living Well, extending across the life cycle and involving complementary forms of participation by men and women. At the same time, the reviewed studies suggest that this ideal of complementarity coexists with persistent gender inequalities, particularly in relation to women's greater responsibility for family and emotional care.

Indigenous and intercultural training and education

The transmission of knowledge and processes of decolonising knowledge have fostered a focus on this emerging dimension of indigenous and intercultural training and education, including the following sub-dimensions: educational practices based on the school-family relationship (Chireac & Guerrero-Jiménez, 2021); non-formal education for women’s entrepreneurship with an intercultural focus (Ruiz Chagna & Iglesias Rodríguez, 2023); and Indigenous universities and decolonising curriculum development (Aparicio et al., 2015).

Chireac and Guerrero-Jiménez (2021) emphasise that the values of Sumak Kawsay are fundamental in rural school contexts in Ecuador, as they help address the indigenous educational gap characterised by high levels of illiteracy and school dropout rates, perpetuating inequality and poverty. The Intercultural Bilingual Model proposed by the Ecuadorian government aims to preserve indigenous languages while simultaneously facilitating the relationship with Spanish as the dominant language. Consequently, the role of teachers must be closely linked to the sociocultural environment of their students, establishing connections with families so that together they can value, transmit and develop the prestige of indigenous languages.

It is evident that educational systems continue to marginalise women from rural and vulnerable sectors, perpetuating inequalities in access to economic opportunities. In response, Intercultural Educommunication, inspired by popular education, is proposed as a pedagogical alternative that promotes inclusion (Ruiz Chagna & Iglesias Rodríguez, 2023). This methodology recognises that audiovisual media are more effective for people with limited access to education and those living in isolated areas. Educommunication facilitates intercultural dialogue and social cohesion, promoting economic initiatives that respect diversity, foster equity and democratise knowledge. They represent a key tool to train women in inclusive entrepreneurship with a focus on reclaiming their cultural heritage.

Within the Living Well paradigm, education is understood not simply as the transmission of knowledge but as a collective process through which identity, territory and community life are reproduced across generations. Finally, three indigenous universities (Aymara, Quechua and Guaraní) were founded in 2008 under the administration of Evo Morales, creating new spaces for higher education, training, research and intercultural debate. According to Aparicio et al. (2015), three central categories emerge from these experiences: intracultural perception (“knowing how to live”), linked to identity and self-recognition; decolonisation and emergence, associated with historical struggles over territory and autonomy; and interculturality (“knowing how to live together”), understood as a dialogic encounter between forms of knowledge. The curriculum-building process has followed the guidelines proposed by the stakeholders (elders, students, organisations and communities), acting as a unique, liberating and dialogic exercise that is highly enriching from multiple perspectives.

Values, principles and a proposal regarding the dimensions underpinning Living Well

Finally, our analysis shows that Living Well is underpinned by a range of principles and values that are generally shared across LAC. Rather than constituting an independent dimension, these cross-cutting principles emerge across the five dimensions identified in this review and help to integrate them into a broader understanding of Living Well (see Figure 5). Three main dimensions were identified through the analysis: relational and community values (Godden, 2021; Morales & Calvo, 2025; Rengifo et al., 2022); relationship between nature, territory and spirituality (Hoinle, 2025; Morales & Calvo, 2025; Rengifo et al., 2022; Thompson, 2018); and cultural transmission, education and identity formation (Grisales-Pascuaza & Rincón-Isaza, 2024; Guegia-Hurtado, 2017; Pineda & Orozco, 2018).

Key values, sub-dimensions identified, in the concept of Living Well.

Studies highlight that Living Well is grounded in principles of solidarity, cooperation, balance and collective care. Godden (2021) analyses how community workers in Peru link Living Well to practices rooted in love, social justice and democratic participation, understanding wellbeing as a collective endeavour oriented toward equality and social peace. Similarly, Rengifo et al. (2022) report that indigenous communities understand wellbeing through relationships of reciprocity and complementarity, where the individual cannot be separated from the community or nature.

Another recurring theme in the studies is the relationship between nature, territory and spirituality. Hoinle (2025), drawing on an analysis of urban agroecological experiences in Bogotá, demonstrates that Living Well constitutes both a practice and a political proposal aimed at rebuilding balanced relationships between society and nature. The author notes that urban gardens function as spaces for the exchange of knowledge, territorial defence and community-building. Along similar lines, Morales and Calvo (2025) note that Living Well involves understanding territory not only as a physical space, but as a spiritual, cultural and ecological fabric that sustains collective life. Thompson (2018) further asserts that territory is conceived of as a living and ethical space that requires loving care and offers reciprocity to those who respect and protect it:

before you go to any new place, you say why you are here and what you want to do...that you will do no harm...talk to the river... talk to the pond if you want a successful catch...people would also pray before going to the sacred sites ... (Thompson, 2018, p. 977)

Similarly, several studies emphasise the role of education and cultural transmission in perpetuating Living Well. Guegia-Hurtado (2017) analyses how Nasa children and young people construct socialisation processes oriented toward Weçxweçxa (good living) through collective learning linked to family, community and their relationship with nature. Similarly, Grisales-Pascuaza and Rincón-Isaza (2024) show that peace education programs developed in indigenous territories integrate memory, cultural identity and community coexistence, strengthening processes of social fabric reconstruction.

Taken together, these studies show that Living Well is not merely an abstract notion of wellbeing, but rather constitutes an ethical, communal and territorial framework grounded in reciprocity, the care of life and continuity of collective knowledge. The reviewed articles highlight that these dimensions articulate forms of social coexistence, community organisation and relationship with nature that challenge the individualistic and extractivist logics of contemporary development. Furthermore, the findings show Living Well to be a situated practice that integrates cultural, spiritual, ecological and community elements in the construction of alternatives for collective life.

Discussion

This systematic review confirms Living Well as a paradigm, expressed in various forms, through which indigenous communities in LAC understand and inhabit the world. The purpose of this discussion is to interpret the five dimensions identified in this review in dialogue with broader theoretical and programmatic debates on Living Well, decoloniality and indigenous wellbeing. Rather than reiterating the empirical findings, this section examines their implications for contemporary scholarship, public policy and future research, while also considering the methodological implications for both community practices and symbolic systems.

The dimension most thoroughly explored in the studies is nature and territory. This is consistent with the biocentric epistemological framework (Barranquero & Sáez, 2017) and with how nature sustains relationships between natural, social and community environments (Beltrán-Véliz et al., 2022). These studies have also examined concerning extractive processes (Bolados et al., 2021), along with natural disasters, often exacerbated by climate change (Ramos-Castillo et al., 2017). A critical need in the region is to develop territorial policies that promote community self-management and local governance. As stated by Castro Díaz et al. (2026), land-use planning in the Global South should be improved by reaffirming the ancestral knowledge systems of indigenous peoples. There is a global trend towards actively listening to and involving indigenous communities in the design of environmental management models, as these communities have shown they can adapt and remain resilient in the face of extreme weather (Cajete, 2020).

The second dimension often explored is community organisation, based on reciprocity, solidarity and territorial autonomy (Ferrer et al., 2019; Jiménez et al., 2022). In particular, these practices take shape through mechanisms such as minga, cooperatives, systems of community roles and community leadership, which articulate economic, political and spiritual dimensions oriented toward the reproduction of life (Martínez & Guerrero, 2019). Likewise, a significant body of studies identifies structural tensions between these community forms of organisation and State-driven development logics. These tensions are intensified through processes of State co-optation and the reconfiguration of solidarity-based community practices (Marchand & Hérault, 2019; Pérez Gañán, 2017; Ranta, 2016).

Another area that has been analysed and represents a source of tension is the health and holistic wellbeing of indigenous communities. These elements are also grounded in and understood by the community, meaning they are interwoven in a balance with the social and natural environment (Achatz et al., 2021; Montoya et al., 2020; Mukerji et al., 2025; Téllez, 2022), again in accordance with biocentric epistemology (Barranquero & Sáez, 2017). However, indigenous medical knowledge is under serious threat, as intercultural health policies continue to prioritise allopathic medicine and associated pharmaceutical treatments (Bautista-Valarezo et al., 2021; Téllez, 2022). Despite this, regional studies have shown that complementary use of traditional indigenous medicine and allopathic medicine yields better health indicators in indigenous communities (Gallardo-Peralta et al., 2019). This highlights one of the challenges of applied research, which involves conducting comprehensive studies among indigenous populations that make complementary use of both health systems and evaluating their impact, while also critically addressing the implementation of intercultural health models (Gavilán et al., 2018).

The fourth dimension pertains to care and gender roles. The regional scientific agenda is strongly emphasising the so-called care crisis, while also examining persistent gender inequalities (Batthyány et al., 2024). The reviewed studies confirm that Living Well is articulated through a circular and reciprocal care system involving community and nature. A mutual obligation is established for/with the environment, where caring for nature and territory also implies caring for the community (Gallardo & Siqueira, 2025). In this caring process, collective wellbeing prevails over individualism and life is defended against extractivist logics. To care for the community and secure its continuity, pregnancy and maternity are seen as central to the survival of indigenous peoples (Ferrer-Pérez et al., 2025). This cycle is guided through ancestral knowledge that profoundly connects pregnancy to nature, integrating babies into the community and providing the mother with sustained accompaniment. This knowledge facilitates the provision of support and protection against ailments and maladies (Ferrer-Pérez et al., 2025). With respect to gender relations, certain gender inequalities persist, sustained by the perpetuation of traditional caregiving roles, where women continue to experience significant violence (Ayala Vargas et al., 2025). According to the Mapuche cosmovision, opposites (male/female) are not antagonists but necessary for each other, providing a balance in the defence of the territory (García, 2016). The study of care and gender relations needs to be reframed from the current perspectives of indigenous feminism in LAC (Cabnal, 2010), especially given that indigenous women are taking the lead in foregrounding feminist and decolonised knowledge.

Finally, the fifth dimension is education. This is an emerging issue in addressing the decolonisation of knowledge, whether by strengthening intercultural education that promotes bilingualism (Chireac & Guerrero-Jiménez, 2021), empowering communities (Aparicio et al., 2015) or reducing gender gaps (Ruiz Chagna & Iglesias Rodríguez, 2023). The link between Living Well and education opens various paths: a philosophical foundation for decolonial curricula, non-formal education that promotes women’s entrepreneurship in rural areas, and educational and family practices closely tied to the community. Living Well is understood as a relational concept that integrates nature, community and care. It hence provides a framework for decolonial educational practices grounded in community values, intercultural dialogue and collective futures.

From an analytical perspective grounded in these five dimensions, and in line with Escobar (2014) arguments, the findings reveal that Living Well is not merely a normative or discursive proposal, but rather a field of ontological contention in which ways of understanding life, nature and social organisation clash. Within this framework, indigenous communities assert Living Well as a situated practice of defending territory, indigenous knowledge and alternative ways of sustaining life in the face of the logic of accumulation.

In methodological terms, most of the studies analysed are qualitative and use a collaborative ethnographic approach that integrates communities, from conceptualisation to fieldwork and the presentation of findings (Rappaport, 2007). This move towards participative research also faces the challenge of incorporating decolonial methodologies. This requires the inclusion of community knowledge and interpretative frameworks, as well as their principles and values, both in interpreting reality and in defining research aims for studies in indigenous communities (Tuhiwai, 1999). Moving towards decolonial methodologies does not imply flatly rejecting the Western methodologies that are dominant in LAC (Tuhiwai, 1999). Instead, there is a need for representative data on communities, ideally collected from official statistics. Representative data are more than figures. They offer a tool to visibilise and recognise inequality gaps in communities and facilitate the design of situated public policies that are respectful of identities (Aparicio & Traldi, 2025). The social vulnerability index developed jointly by Mexico and Argentina is particularly noteworthy, demonstrating that progress can be made on regional initiatives (Ruiz Rivera et al., 2025). From here emerges one of the key challenges in LAC: extending the academic/scientific debate in line with public policies based on situated studies that are also representative of indigenous peoples (Aparicio & Traldi, 2025; Gallardo-Peralta, 2025).

Limitations

This review is subject to certain limitations. First, the team’s decision to select articles in English and Spanish may have excluded research relevant to the analysis of Living Well in the region (written primarily in Portuguese). Second, the methodological decision to include only empirical articles from scientific databases may have excluded the wealth of studies conducted by the communities themselves and classified as grey literature. Finally, the data were analysed using the SALSA model and included a narrative synthesis, meaning the interpretation of the results is subjective and may reflect researcher biases.

Conclusions

This systematic review shows that Living Well constitutes a situated paradigm through which indigenous peoples in Latin America and the Caribbean understand wellbeing, community life and their relationship with territory and nature. The review identifies five major dimensions through which Living Well has been empirically examined in the region: nature and territory; community organisation; health and holistic wellbeing; intergenerational care and gender roles; and indigenous and intercultural training and education. Rather than constituting an additional dimension, the values and principles identified across the reviewed studies operate as cross-cutting elements that integrate these dimensions into a broader understanding of Living Well.

Living Well offers an alternative paradigm in response to the historical violence of colonisation, extractivism and territorial dispossession experienced by indigenous peoples in LAC. These peoples have survived despite longstanding processes of marginalisation and unequal opportunities. In contemporary societies, ethnicity becomes a source of resistance and reconstruction, connecting cultural and territorial realities with aspirations for dignity and collective wellbeing. As Walsh (2010) argues, Living Well is not a model of development but a paradigm that restores the centrality of community life and a harmonious relationship with nature.
