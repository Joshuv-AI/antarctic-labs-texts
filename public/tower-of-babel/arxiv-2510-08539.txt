Abstract

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR), which uses simple binary feedback to post-train large language models, has shown significant empirical success. However, a principled understanding of why it works has been lacking.
This paper builds a theoretical foundation for RLVR by analyzing its training process at both the full-response (trajectory) and token levels.
Central to our analysis is a quantity called the
Gradient Gap
, which formalizes the direction of improvement from low-reward to high-reward regions of the response space. We prove that convergence critically depends on aligning the update direction with this Gradient Gap.
Moreover, we derive a sharp step-size threshold based on the magnitude of the Gradient Gap: below it, learning converges, whereas above it, performance collapses. Our theory further predicts how the critical step size must scale with
response length
and the
success rate
, thereby explaining why practical heuristics such as
length normalization
improve stability and showing that, with a fixed learning rate, the success rate can stagnate strictly below
100\%
.
We validate these predictions through controlled bandit simulations.

1
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have recently achieved significant advances through reinforcement learning post-training, which aligns them with complex tasks and preferences
(
ziegler2019fine
;
ouyang2022
;
shao2024deepseekmath
;
team2025kimi
)
.
In particular, Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has emerged as a powerful approach for post-training LLMs on tasks where success can be automatically checked (e.g. using a compiler or solver)
(
guo2025deepseek
)
.
RLVR methods have shown impressive empirical gains by leveraging binary success/failure feedback instead of human judgments, thereby simplifying the RL pipeline.
Techniques in this vein (e.g.
variants of Proximal Policy Optimization, a.k.a. PPO
(
schulman2017proximal
)
, like GRPO
(
shao2024deepseekmath
)
, DAPO
(
yu2025dapo
)
,
Dr. GRPO
(
liu2025understanding
)
, etc.) eliminate the need for learned reward or value models, relying purely on verifiable outcome signals.
This has enabled LLMs to achieve state-of-the-art results on challenging reasoning and code-generation tasks, demonstrating the promise of RLVR-driven fine-tuning.

However, empirical progress in RLVR has far outpaced our theoretical understanding. The optimization process remains largely a black box: we do not fully understand why RL-based post-training works so well, under what conditions it might falter, or how to tune it for stable convergence. Recent PPO-based variants (e.g. GRPO, DAPO, Dr. GRPO) have sprung up to improve training stability, each introducing different heuristics like normalizing gradient updates by the output length or standardizing rewards by the group’s variance. Yet it remains unclear which of these design choices truly matter; without a principled basis, their adoption is guided more by intuition than by theory. This gap is especially pronounced given RLVR’s sparse binary rewards (each episode yields just a single success/failure bit), which make it difficult to analyze how gradient descent navigates the model’s vast parameter space or how the policy’s output distribution shifts toward higher-reward answers. In practice, practitioners often resort to trial-and-error for critical hyperparameters and algorithmic choices, where a mis-tuning can destabilize training or even cause catastrophic collapse (e.g., forgetting pre-trained knowledge or converging to trivial outputs). These challenges underscore the need for a rigorous theoretical framework to demystify RLVR’s optimization dynamics and reduce reliance on guesswork.

This work establishes such a framework, providing a rigorous theoretical foundation for RLVR in LLM post-training. Our key contributions are:

•

Unified RLVR theory:
We develop a principled framework for RLVR under binary rewards, introducing the
Gradient Gap
to characterize the improvement direction from low- to high-reward responses.

•

Convergence guarantees:
We prove the existence of a sharp step-size threshold that separates stable convergence from divergence, providing clear guidance for safe hyperparameter tuning.

•

Length- and success-aware learning rates:
Our theory shows that the effective learning rate must shrink with output length and adapt to task difficulty, offering a theoretical explanation for the stabilizing effect of heuristics such as length normalization and clarifying why fixed step sizes can cause stagnation.

1.1
Related Works

Recent efforts in RL-based language model post-training have introduced a family of GRPO-style algorithms that extend or modify Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) for verifiable feedback settings.
GRPO itself eliminates the value critic by estimating advantages from a group of sampled responses, using relative reward normalization instead of a learned baseline
(
shao2024deepseekmath
)
.
Building on this idea, DAPO augmented GRPO by decoupling the PPO clipping range and dynamically filtering out cases where all responses in a batch are correct or all are incorrect
(
yu2025dapo
)
. Dr. GRPO revisits the advantage normalization procedure, arguing that removing length and variance normalizations (i.e. using only a mean baseline) can prevent bias in policy updates
(
liu2025understanding
)
.
Additional related papers are discussed in
Appendix

A
.

In parallel, theoretical work has established convergence guarantees for policy gradient (PG) methods, including REINFORCE and actor-critic algorithms. In finite Markov decision processes with softmax policies, the PG objective often satisfies a PL condition, implying that any stationary point is globally optimal and that vanilla gradient ascent converges at a sublinear rate
(
agarwal2021theory
;
xiao2022convergence
)
. Actor-critic methods also achieve provable convergence by using two-timescale updates or pessimistic value estimation
(
wu2020finite
;
zanette2021provable
)
. However, extending these guarantees to post-training large language models with verifiable binary rewards remains challenging, as sparse success/failure signals provide very limited gradient information.

2
Problem Set-Up

Language Model.

We begin with a standard language model parameterized by
\theta\in\mathds{R}^{d}
, which defines a conditional distribution
\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)
over sequences of tokens
\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,=(o_{1},o_{2},\dots,o_{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,|})
given an input prompt/question
q
. Output tokens
\{o_{t}\}_{t=1}^{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,|}
are drawn from a finite vocabulary
\mathcal{T}
, and the generation process ends when the special end-of-sequence token
o_{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,|}={\tt EOS}
is emitted.

The model generates tokens in an
autoregressive
fashion: at every step
t
, the next token
o_{t}
is sampled conditioned on the prompt
q
and all previously generated tokens
\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,_{<t}=(o_{1},o_{2},\ldots,o_{t-1})
. Formally,

\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)=\prod_{t=1}^{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,|}\uppi_{\theta}(o_{t}\mid q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,_{<t})
.
Each conditional distribution is defined by a softmax over token logits
\boldsymbol{h}_{\theta}(\cdot)
:

\displaystyle\uppi_{\theta}(o_{t}\mid q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,_{<t})\;:\,=\;\frac{\exp\{\boldsymbol{h}_{\theta}(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,_{\leq t})\}}{\sum_{o^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}\exp\{\boldsymbol{h}_{\theta}(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,_{<t},o^{\prime})\}}\,.

(1)

Post-Training: Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR).

While a supervised language model can generate fluent text, it often struggles to align with task-specific goals such as math reasoning or code generation. Post-training addresses this limitation by adapting the model parameters
\theta
to align more closely with an external reward signal that captures desirable behavior.

Formally, we assume access to an outcome reward model
r^{\star}(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)
that is directly verifiable and assessed at the end of a generated sequence:
r^{\star}=1
if the answer is correct (e.g., a valid proof step or passing code execution) and
r^{\star}=0
otherwise.
The aim of reinforcement learning in this context is to tune the model parameters
\theta
so as to maximize the expected reward under the current policy:

{\rm maximize}_{\theta\in\mathds{R}^{d}}\quad J(\uppi_{\theta})\;:\,=\;\mathds{E}_{q\sim\mathds{P}(Q),\,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q)}\big[r^{\star}(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)\big]\,.

(2)

Policy Gradient.

To optimize
J(\uppi_{\theta})
, we rely on policy gradient–based methods. At each iteration
t
, the parameters
\theta
are updated according to

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}\;=\;\theta_{k}+\eta_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\,,

(3)

where
\eta_{k}\geq 0
is the learning rate and
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
is a normalized update direction with
\|\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\|_{2}\leq 1
.
For clarity, we denote the policy and logit function at step
k
as
\uppi_{k}:\,=\uppi_{\theta_{k}}
and
\boldsymbol{h}_{k}:\,=\boldsymbol{h}_{\theta_{k}}
.

This generic formulation captures a broad family of post-training algorithms used in RLVR. Representative examples are:

REINFORCE:

The classical policy gradient method updates parameters in the direction

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\,J(\uppi_{k})\;=\;\mathds{E}_{q\sim\mathds{P}(Q),\,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{k}(\cdot\mid q)}\big[A(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{k}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\big]\,,

(4)

where the advantage function is given by
A(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)=r^{\star}(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)-\mathds{E}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{\prime}\sim\uppi_{k}(\cdot\mid q)}[r^{\star}(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{\prime})]
.
In this case, the update rule

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{k}+\alpha\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\,J(\uppi_{k})

can be rewritten in our generic form by setting

\boldsymbol{w}_{k}=\nabla_{\theta}\,J(\uppi_{k})/\|\nabla_{\theta}\,J(\uppi_{k})\|_{2}
and

\eta_{k}=\alpha\,\|\nabla_{\theta}\,J(\uppi_{k})\|_{2}
.
Viewed in this way,
Dr. GRPO

(
liu2025understanding
)
emerges as a variant that replaces the single-sample advantage with a group-wise demeaned version.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
:

GRPO has recently become a standard choice for RLVR. The full algorithm incorporates clipping ratios and multi-step updates (see
Section

B.1
). To connect it with the generic policy gradient form, we consider a simplified one-step approximation without clipping. In this case, the gradient direction is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\uppi_{k})\;=\;\mathds{E}_{q\sim\mathds{P}(Q),\,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{k}(\cdot\mid q)}\Big[\frac{A(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)}{\sigma(q)}\cdot\frac{1}{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,|}\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{k}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\Big]\,,

(5)

where the conditional standard deviation
\sigma(q)
is given by

\sigma^{2}(q)=
{\rm Var}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{k}(\cdot\mid q)}[r^{\star}(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)\mid q]
.
In practice, GRPO is typically trained with a cosine learning rate schedule, which can be locally treated as a constant step size
\alpha
. Within our generic update rule, this corresponds to setting
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}=\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}/\|\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}\|_{2}
and
\eta_{k}=\alpha\,\|\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}\|_{2}
,
so that both the response length
|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,|
and reward variability
\sigma(q)
directly influence the effective step size
\eta_{k}
.

Objective.

Our goal in this work is to understand how the choice of update direction
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}
and step size
\eta_{k}
influences the convergence of RLVR. In particular, we ask: under what conditions can we guarantee convergence, and what design choices may lead to instability or failure modes?

3
Trajectory-Level Analysis

In this section, we study the optimization scheme (
3
) on a single prompt
q
. We take a
trajectory-level
view, where each response
\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,
is treated as a single unit rather than a sequence of tokens.
By abstracting away the internal structure, the analysis becomes simpler yet still revealing. We begin by outlining the key ingredients of this trajectory-level view, then examine both its success modes and failure cases. Although this setup is only a warm-up for the more detailed token-level analysis, it already highlights several nontrivial and illuminating properties of RLVR.

3.1
Key Ingredients: Gradient Gap and Gap Alignment

Recall that the optimization objective is the
correction rate
of the model
\uppi_{\theta}
on prompt
q
:
J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta}):\,=\mathds{E}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q)}\big[r^{\star}(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)\bigm|q\big]
.
To analyze this, we partition the response space
\mathcal{O}
into two sets based on the verifiable reward
r^{\star}(q,\cdot)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+}\;:\,=\;\big\{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\in\mathcal{O}\bigm|r^{\star}(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)=1\big\}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\mathcal{O}_{q}^{-}\;:\,=\;\big\{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\in\mathcal{O}\bigm|r^{\star}(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)=0\big\}\,,

(6)

Here
\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+}
represents desirable responses (correct solutions), while
\mathcal{O}_{q}^{-}
contains undesirable ones.
Accordingly,

J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})=\mathds{P}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q)}\big[\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\in\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+}\big]

and
1-J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})=\mathds{P}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q)}\big[\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\in\mathcal{O}_{q}^{-}\big]
.

Conditional Policies.

We further define conditional distributions over the positive and negative spaces:

\displaystyle\uppi_{\theta}^{+}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)=\uppi_{\theta}\big(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\bigm|q,\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+}\big)\;

\displaystyle:\,=\;\frac{\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)}{J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})}\cdot\mathds{1}\{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\in\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+}\}\,,

(7a)

\displaystyle\uppi_{\theta}^{-}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)=\uppi_{\theta}\big(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\bigm|q,\mathcal{O}_{q}^{-}\big)\;

\displaystyle:\,=\;\frac{\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)}{1-J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})}\cdot\mathds{1}\{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\in\mathcal{O}_{q}^{-}\}\,.

(7b)

These describe how the model
\uppi_{\theta}
distributes probability mass within the “good” and “bad” regions, respectively.

Gradient Gap: A Direction for Improvement.

Using the conditional policies, we measure the expected log-probability gradient / score function in each region:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{+}(\uppi_{\theta}):\,=\mathds{E}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta}^{+}(\cdot\mid q)}\big[\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\big]~~~\mbox{and}~~~\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{-}(\uppi_{\theta}):\,=\mathds{E}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta}^{-}(\cdot\mid q)}\big[\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\big].

(8)

The difference between them,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{+}(\uppi_{\theta})-\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{-}(\uppi_{\theta}),

(9)

is the
Gradient Gap
. Intuitively, it highlights how the model’s parameters should be shifted to favor desirable responses over undesirable ones.

Crucially, the Gradient Gap is directly proportional
1
1

1

A formal proof of this is found in
Section

B.2
to the policy gradient given in equation (
4
):

\nabla_{\theta}\,J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})\;=\;J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})\{1-J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})\}\cdot\big(\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{+}-\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{-}\big)\,.

(10)

This shows that the Gradient Gap captures the true direction of improvement. Unlike the full policy gradient
\nabla_{\theta}\,J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})
,
\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{+}(\uppi_{\theta})-\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{-}(\uppi_{\theta})
is not scaled down by the variability factor
J_{q}(1-J_{q})
, making it a purer indicator of where to move.

Gap Alignment: Following the Right Direction.

Consider now the optimization scheme (
3
). At iteration
k
, define
\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{+}(k)
and
\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{-}(k)
under the current policy
\uppi_{k}
. The update vector
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}
should ideally align with the improvement direction
\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{+}(k)-\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{-}(k)
.

We measure this alignment by the inner product

\displaystyle\Delta\mu_{q}(k)\;:\,=\;\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\cdot\big\{\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{+}(k)-\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{-}(k)\big\}\,.

(11)

If
\|\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\|_{2}=1
, this equals

\Delta\mu_{q}(k)\;=\;\|\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{+}(k)-\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{-}(k)\|_{2}\cdot\cos\angle\big\{\boldsymbol{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{+}(k)-\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{-}(k)\big\}
, which depends both on the magnitude of the Gradient Gap and the angle of alignment.

In the convergence analysis,
\Delta\mu_{q}(k)
will play a central role. For stable progress we require:

(i)

\Delta\mu_{q}(k)
should be positive and preferably large, ensuring updates move in the right direction.

(ii)

The step size
\eta_{k}
should adapt to its scale, preventing over- or under-shooting.

3.2
Main Findings

We now turn to the central findings of our analysis.
Proofs will be deferred to
Appendices

C
and
D
.
Before presenting the results, let us impose a mild regularity condition on the policy score function.

Assumption 1

(Regularity of Trajectory Policy Score)
.

The policy score function
\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)
behaves regularly with respect to the parameters
\theta
:

(a)

(Boundedness)
There exists a constant
G_{\mathrm{o}}<\infty
such that for all
\theta
and
(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)
,

\displaystyle\big\|\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\big\|_{2}\;\leq\;G_{\mathrm{o}}.

(12)

(b)

(Smoothness)
The policy score function is
L_{\mathrm{o}}
-Lipschitz continuous with respect to
\theta
:

\displaystyle\big\|\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{\theta^{\prime}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)-\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\big\|_{2}\;\leq\;L_{\mathrm{o}}\cdot\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|_{2}.

(13)

Throughout this section, we use the shorthand
J_{q}(k)=J_{q}(\uppi_{k})
to denote the performance at iteration
k
.

3.2.1
Convergence and Stagnation

Armed with this set-up, we now state our main theorem, which distinguishes between two possible outcomes of learning:
successful convergence
to the optimum, or
stagnation
at a suboptimal performance plateau. The distinction hinges on how well the update directions align with the underlying objective.
To formalize this, we introduce the notion of
Cumulative Gap Alignment
,

\textstyle M(K)\;:\,=\;\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}[\Delta\mu_{q}(k)]_{+}\,\eta_{k},

(14)

which accumulates the amount of “useful progress” made up to horizon
K
. Intuitively,
M(K)
grows whenever the update direction is positively aligned with the true objective, and it stagnates when the updates fail to exploit the available signal.

Theorem 1

(Convergence and Stagnation)
.

Assume that the step sizes satisfy
\eta_{k}\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{L_{\mathrm{o}}}}
.

(a)

(Stagnation)

Consider when
J_{q}(0)<1
. If the alignment signal is too weak, in the sense that the cumulative alignment remains bounded

M(K)\leq C_{0}
and

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\eta_{k}^{2}\leq C_{0}^{\prime}/(L_{\mathrm{o}}+8G_{\mathrm{o}}^{2})
,
for some constants
0\leq C_{0},C_{0}^{\prime}<\infty
, then learning will stall. In this case, the performance remains strictly sub-optimal:

J_{q}(k)\leq J_{q}(0)\big(J_{q}(0)+\exp(C_{0}+C_{0}^{\prime})\,\{1-J_{q}(0)\}\big)^{-1}\!<1
.

(b)

(Convergence)

Consider a case where
J_{q}(0)>0
. Suppose the step size
\eta_{k}
is adapted to the strength of the alignment signal,

\displaystyle\eta_{k}\;\leq\;\frac{[\Delta\mu_{q}(k)]_{+}}{2\,(L_{\mathrm{o}}+8\,G_{\mathrm{o}}^{2})}\quad\mbox{where }[\,\cdot\,]_{+}=\max(0,\cdot).

(15a)

Then the performance is lower-bounded at any horizon
K
by

J_{q}(K)\;\geq\;\frac{J_{q}(0)}{J_{q}(0)+\{1-J_{q}(0)\}\,\exp\big\{-\frac{1}{2}M(K)\big\}}.

(15b)

Moreover, if the alignment accumulates indefinitely,
\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}M(K)=+\infty
, then the policy is guaranteed to achieve perfect performance:
\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}J_{q}(K)=1
.

The theorem establishes a clear dichotomy. Convergence is attainable only when update directions exhibit consistent alignment with the underlying objective and the step size is properly scaled to reflect this signal. In the absence of either alignment or adaptive scaling, progress stagnates and the policy remains confined to a suboptimal regime.

Sketch of Proof.

The key step is the inequality

\bigg|\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}(k+1)}{1-J_{q}(k+1)}\Big)-\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}(k)}{1-J_{q}(k)}\Big)-\Delta\mu_{q}(k)\,\eta_{k}\bigg|\;\leq\;(L_{\mathrm{o}}+8\,G_{\mathrm{o}}^{2})\,\eta_{k}^{2}\,,

(16)

which is stated formally in
Lemma

1
of
Section

C.1.1
.
This inequality shows that
\Delta\mu_{q}(t)\,\eta_{t}
captures the first-order Taylor approximation of the change in log-odds of
J_{q}
. Summing (
16
) over iterations and analyzing the resulting terms under different cases reveals that the Cumulative Gap Alignment
M(K)
governs the value of
J_{q}
. This establishes the claims in
Theorem

1
.

3.2.2
The Importance of Properly Chosen Step Size
\eta_{k}

According to condition (
15a
) in
Theorem

1
(b), the step size
\eta_{k}
must be carefully scaled to match the gap alignment
\Delta\mu_{q}(k)
. To illustrate this, we contrast two scenarios: a modest step size yields linear convergence, whereas an overly aggressive one causes failure.

Linear Convergence Under Proper Scaling.

Suppose that every update direction provides a consistent signal, so that the Gap Alignment
\Delta\mu_{q}(k)
is uniformly bounded below. In this case, a properly chosen fixed step size is sufficient to guarantee rapid improvement.

Corollary 1

(Linear Convergence with a Uniform Gap)
.

If every update direction
\boldsymbol{w}_{t}
provides a uniform gap,
\Delta\mu_{q}(k)\geq\Delta\mu_{q}>0
for all
k\geq 0
, then a simple fixed step size
\eta
satisfying

\displaystyle\eta\;\leq\;\min\bigg\{\frac{\Delta\mu_{q}}{2\,(L_{\mathrm{o}}+8\,G_{\mathrm{o}}^{2})},\frac{1}{2\sqrt{L_{\mathrm{o}}}}\bigg\}\,,

drives the error to zero at a linear rate:

1-J_{q}(K)\leq\frac{1-J_{q}(0)}{J_{q}(0)}\,\exp\big\{-\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta\mu_{q}\,\eta\cdot K\big\}
.

The Perils of Overshooting.

The picture changes sharply when the step size is too large. If condition (
15a
) in Theorem
1
(b) is violated, convergence may break down entirely. The next result shows that even with perfect update directions, learning can collapse under overly aggressive step sizes.

Theorem 2

(Catastrophic Failure from an Overly Large Step Size)
.

There exists a problem instance under
1
with
G_{\mathrm{o}}\geq\sqrt{L_{\mathrm{o}}}
where the Gap Alignment is uniformly positive,
\Delta\mu_{q}(k)\geq\Delta\mu_{q}>0
for all
k\geq 0
, yet using an overly large constant step size
\eta_{k}=\eta
leads to failure. Specifically, if the step size satisfies

\displaystyle{\frac{60\,\Delta\mu_{q}}{L_{\mathrm{o}}+G_{\mathrm{o}}^{2}}}\;\leq\;\eta\;\leq\;{\frac{1}{2\sqrt{L_{\mathrm{o}}+G_{\mathrm{o}}^{2}}}}\,,

where
0<\Delta\mu_{q}\leq\frac{1}{120}\sqrt{L_{\mathrm{o}}+G_{\mathrm{o}}^{2}}
, the policy’s performance will strictly
decrease
at every step, ultimately converging to zero:
J_{q}(k)<J_{q}(k-1)
and
\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}J_{q}(k)=0
.

While the numerical constants (e.g.,
60
,
120
) are not sharp, the phenomenon is robust: an oversized step size causes repeated overshooting, pushing the system toward collapse rather than improvement.

Intuition for the lower bound analysis.

Our convergence analysis (
Theorem

1
) relies on equation
Equation

16
, which uses a first-order approximation of the change in log-odds. For the lower bound, however, it is crucial to examine the second-order expansion. To this end, we define conditional variances over the positive (and negative) response space:
{\rm Var}^{+}:\,={\rm Var}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{k}(\cdot\mid q,\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+})}\big[\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{k}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\big]
.
The term
{\rm Var}^{-}
is defined analogously.
The second-order Taylor expansion gives

\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}(k+1)}{1-J_{q}(k+1)}\Big)-\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}(k)}{1-J_{q}(k)}\Big)\;=\;\Delta\mu_{q}(k)\,\eta_{k}+\{{\rm Var}^{+}-{\rm Var}^{-}\}\cdot\eta_{k}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\eta_{k}^{3})\,.

(17)

In our construction, the linear term is always favorable:
\Delta\mu_{q}(k)\,\eta_{k}>0
. The challenge comes from the quadratic term. If the variance over the negative space dominates,
{\rm Var}^{-}>{\rm Var}^{+}
, then for moderately large step sizes the second-order effect can overwhelm the first-order gain, pulling the log-odds downward and decreasing
J_{q}
.

This phenomenon is not just a theoretical artifact—it is highly plausible in practice. Real-world language models typically face an enormous negative space (many incorrect responses) with high variability, leading to large
{\rm Var}^{-}
. In contrast, the positive space often contains only a few consistent modes, keeping
{\rm Var}^{+}
relatively small. This imbalance highlights the danger of overshooting: unless the step size
\eta_{k}
is carefully calibrated, the variance contribution from the negative space can dominate and derail learning.
To ensure both stability and progress, the step size must respect the scale

\eta_{k}\asymp\Delta\mu_{q}(k)/(L_{\mathrm{o}}+G_{\mathrm{o}}^{2})
.

4
Token-Level Analysis

We now move towards a token-level analysis of RLVR, which sharpens the trajectory-level perspective developed earlier. While natural and general for abstract analysis, our analysis in
Section

3

Discussion and Future Directions

Our analysis is restricted to the single-prompt setting, which enabled sharp characterizations of Gradient Gap alignment and step size scaling.
In practice, however, training involves a diverse batch of prompts.
In this regime, both the alignment signal
\Delta\mu_{q}(k)
and the optimal step size
\eta_{k}
can vary substantially across prompts.
A single update direction
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}
may align well with some prompts but poorly with others, and a step size that is safe for one subset may be overly aggressive for another, leading to overshooting and limited overall gains.

These observations suggest several directions for future work: developing prompt-adaptive updates that adjust direction or scale based on batch heterogeneity, analyzing the statistical dynamics of RLVR under diverse prompt distributions, and extending the framework to sequential or curriculum-based training
(
bengio2009curriculum
;
chen2025selfevolving
;
zhang2025speedrl
)
. Such extensions are essential for a full theory of RLVR in realistic multi-prompt settings.

Appendix A
Additional Related Work

A growing body of work has begun to examine the theoretical foundations of preference-based RLHF and verifiable-reward RL. Early studies analyzed preference-driven RL with trajectory-level feedback, establishing convergence guarantees under pairwise or
K
-wise comparisons
[
15
,
8
,
30
]
. More recent results offered complexity characterizations: Want et al.
[
22
]
and Du et al.
[
10
]
compared RLHF with standard RL, identifying conditions for sample-efficient preference optimization. In parallel, techniques from function approximation and offline RL have been adapted to the fine-tuning setting
[
6
,
21
,
5
]
. Yet the optimization behavior of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)—where supervision is provided by deterministic outcomes rather than preferences—remains largely unexplored.

Building on these foundations, researchers have investigated how sparse, outcome-based rewards shape learning dynamics. For example, one study shows that off-policy updates can benefit from emphasizing positive (successful) outcomes more strongly than negative ones
[
2
]
, while another finds that purely outcome-driven signals can collapse solution diversity in the absence of exploration incentives
[
18
]
. These insights illustrate how verifiable binary rewards influence both convergence and the diversity of reasoning strategies, offering early theoretical guidance for RLVR.

Complementing this theoretical line, sequence-level optimization methods have advanced the algorithmic toolkit for RL-based fine-tuning. GSPO, for instance, defines importance ratios over whole-answer likelihoods with sequence-level clipping and updates, improving stability and efficiency compared to token-level methods
[
29
]
. To control variance from variable output lengths, specialized loss aggregation schemes such as
\Delta L
normalization have been introduced, yielding an unbiased, minimal-variance estimator of policy loss across different generation lengths
[
12
]
.

Appendix B
Background and Derivations

B.1
Review of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

Among post-training algorithms, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) has emerged as the workhorse for improving LLM reasoning ability toward the verifiable-reward objective
J(\uppi)
in equation (
2
). The idea is straightforward: instead of evaluating each generated response in isolation, GRPO leverages relative performance within a group.

Overview of algorithm.

For each question
q
, we sample a group of
G
candidate responses
\{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\cdot\mid q)
.
These raw outcomes are converted into group-normalized advantages:

\displaystyle\widehat{A}^{(i)}_{t}\;=\;\frac{r^{(i)}-{\rm mean}(\{r^{(j)}\}_{j\in[G]})}{{\rm std}(\{r^{(j)}\}_{j\in[G]})}\,.

(23)

GRPO then performs a PPO-style update, using a clipped surrogate objective and an optional KL penalty toward a fixed reference policy
\uppi_{{\rm ref}}
:

\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\uppi_{\theta})\;=\;\mathds{E}_{q,\{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}\}}\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}|}\Big[\min\big\{\rho_{t}^{(i)}\,\widehat{A}^{(i)}_{t},\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_{t}^{(i)},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\,\widehat{A}^{(i)}_{t}\big\}\\
-\;\beta\,D_{\text{KL}}\big(\uppi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}_{<t})\;\big\|\;\uppi_{{\rm ref}}(\cdot\mid q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}_{<t})\big)\Big]\,,

(24)

where the density ratio

\displaystyle\rho_{t}^{(i)}\;:\,=\;\frac{\uppi_{\theta}\big(o^{(i)}_{t}\bigm|q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}_{<t}\big)}{\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}\big(o^{(i)}_{t}\bigm|q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}_{<t}\big)}

corrects for off-policy sampling. In practice, many implementations set
\beta=0
and drop the KL term.

Simplified Form.

To simplify the analysis, we omit clipping and focus on a simplified surrogate:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\uppi_{\theta})

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathds{E}_{q,\{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}\}}\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}|}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\,\widetilde{A}^{(i)}_{t}

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathds{E}_{q,\{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}\}}\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}|}\frac{\uppi_{\theta}\big(o^{(i)}_{t}\bigm|q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}_{<t}\big)}{\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}\big(o^{(i)}_{t}\bigm|q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}_{<t}\big)}\,\widetilde{A}^{(i)}_{t}\,.

with

\displaystyle\widetilde{A}^{(i)}_{t}\;=\;\frac{r^{(i)}-\frac{1}{G-1}\sum_{j\neq i}r^{(j)}}{\sigma(q)}\,.

(25)

Here
\sigma^{2}(q)
denotes the conditional reward variance:
\sigma^{2}(q)={\rm Var}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{t}(\cdot\mid q)}[r^{\star}(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)\mid q]
.

Evaluating the gradient at
\theta=\theta_{\rm old}
yields

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\,\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\uppi_{\theta})\bigm|_{\theta=\theta_{\rm old}}

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathds{E}_{q,\{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}\}}\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}|}\frac{\nabla_{\theta}\,\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}\big(o^{(i)}_{t}\bigm|q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}_{<t}\big)}{\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}\big(o^{(i)}_{t}\bigm|q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}_{<t}\big)}\,\widetilde{A}^{(i)}_{t}

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathds{E}_{q,\{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}\}}\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}|}\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}\big(o^{(i)}_{t}\bigm|q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}_{<t}\big)\,\widetilde{A}^{(i)}_{t}

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathds{E}_{q,\{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}\}}\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}|}\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{(i)}\mid q)\,\widetilde{A}^{(i)}_{t}\,.

Because responses within a group are independent, this simplifies to

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\,\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\uppi_{\theta})\big|_{\theta=\theta_{\rm old}}\!=\mathds{E}_{q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\cdot\mid q)}\!\bigg[\frac{1}{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,|}\nabla_{\theta}\,\!\log\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\cdot\frac{r^{\star}(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)-\mathds{E}_{\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}}[r^{\star}(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,^{\prime})\mid q]}{\sigma(q)}\bigg],

which is exactly the normalized policy gradient form in equation (
5
).

B.2
Proof of Equation (
10
)

We begin with the definition of
J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})
, which is the probability of generating a response in the positive space
\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+}
:

\displaystyle J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})\;=\;\int_{\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+}}\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\,{d}\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\,.

First, we take the gradient of
J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})
and apply the log-derivative trick

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\,J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})\;=\;\int_{\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+}}\nabla_{\theta}\,\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\,{d}\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,

\displaystyle\;=\;\int_{\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+}}\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\,\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\,{d}\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathds{E}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q)}\big[\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\cdot\mathds{1}\{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\in\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+}\}\big]\,.

This integral can be written as a conditional expectation over the positive response space, which gives us our first identity:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\,J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})\;=\;J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})\cdot\mathds{E}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q,\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+})}\big[\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\big]\;=\;J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})\cdot\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{+}\,.

(26)

Alternatively, we can express
J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})
using the negative response space:

\displaystyle J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})\;=\;1-\int_{\mathcal{O}_{q}^{-}}\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\,{d}\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\,.

This gives us our second identity, this time in terms of the negative response space:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\,J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})\;=\;-\,\{1-J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})\}\cdot\mathds{E}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q,\mathcal{O}_{q}^{-})}\big[\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\big]\;=\;-\,\{1-J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})\}\cdot\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{-}\,.

(27)

From the two identities in (
26
) and (
27
), we can isolate the score terms
\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{+}
and
\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{-}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{+}\;=\;\frac{\nabla_{\theta}\,J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})}{J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{-}\;=\;-\,\frac{\nabla_{\theta}\,J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})}{1-J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})}\,.

Finally, we compute their difference:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{+}-\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{-}\;=\;\frac{\nabla_{\theta}\,J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})}{J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})\{1-J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta})\}}\,.

Rearranging this final expression gives the desired result in equation (
10
), completing the proof.

\displaystyle\bigg|\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}(k)}{1-J_{q}(k)}\Big)-\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}(0)}{1-J_{q}(0)}\Big)-\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\Delta\mu_{q}(k)\,\eta_{k}\bigg|\;\leq\;(L_{\mathrm{o}}+8\,G_{\mathrm{o}}^{2})\,\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\eta_{k}^{2}\,.

(29)

Using this inequality, we analyze two distinct outcomes.

(a) Stagnation.

First, we show that return
J_{q}(K)
can be bounded away from the optimum under the condition from
Theorem

1
(a). Rearranging the cumulative inequality gives an upper bound:

\displaystyle\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}(k)}{1-J_{q}(k)}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}(0)}{1-J_{q}(0)}\Big)+\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\Delta\mu_{q}(k)\cdot\eta_{k}+(L_{\mathrm{o}}+8G_{\mathrm{o}}^{2})\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\eta_{k}^{2}\,,

which further leads to

\displaystyle J_{q}(k)

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\frac{J_{q}(0)}{J_{q}(0)+\{1-J_{q}(0)\}\,\exp\big\{-\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\Delta\mu_{q}(k)\,\eta_{k}-(L_{\mathrm{o}}+8\,G_{\mathrm{o}}^{2})\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\eta_{k}^{2}\big\}}\,.

By applying the bounds on the cumulative alignment gap and step sizes from
Theorem

1
(a), the inequality simplifies to

\displaystyle J_{q}(k)

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\frac{J_{q}(0)}{J_{q}(0)+\exp(C_{0}+C_{0}^{\prime})\,\{1-J_{q}(0)\}}\;<\;1\,.

This result shows that the performance
J_{q}(K)
hits a ceiling and is strictly bounded away from the optimum value of
1
, proving the stagnation described in
Theorem

1
(a).

(b) Convergence.

Next, we show that an adaptive step size
\eta_{k}
guarantees convergence. The key is the rule from condition (
15a
): If the gap alignment
\Delta\mu_{q}(k)<0
, the step size
\eta_{k}
is set to zero. This is a “do no harm” policy that prevents any update in the wrong direction.
On the other hand, if
\Delta\mu_{q}(k)\geq 0
, then the step size
\eta_{k}
is chosen carefully to ensure that
(L_{\mathrm{o}}+8G_{\mathrm{o}}^{2})\,\eta_{k}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}[\Delta\mu_{q}(k)]_{+}\cdot\eta_{k}
. This guarantees meaningful progress.
The rule (
15a
) strengthens inequality (
28
) to a lower bound on progress:

\displaystyle\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}(k+1)}{1-J_{q}(k+1)}\Big)-\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}(k)}{1-J_{q}(k)}\Big)

\displaystyle\;\geq\;[\Delta\mu_{q}(k)]_{+}\,\eta_{k}-(L_{\mathrm{o}}+8\,G_{\mathrm{o}}^{2})\,\eta_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle\;\geq\;\frac{1}{2}\,[\Delta\mu_{q}(k)]_{+}\,\eta_{k}\,.

(30)

By telescoping and rearranging terms in the same manner as before, we obtain:

\displaystyle J_{q}(k)\;\geq\;\frac{J_{q}(0)}{J_{q}(0)+\{1-J_{q}(0)\}\,\exp\big\{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}[\Delta\mu_{q}(k)]_{+}\,\eta_{k}\big\}}\,.

This result shows that as the cumulative gap alignment grows, the exponential term shrinks, pushing the performance
J_{q}(K)
towards
1
. This confirms the convergence guarantee in
Theorem

1
(b), as described in inequality (
15b
).

Suppose

According to the bound (

Token-Level Analysis (Proof of Theorem
3
)

We begin by providing the formal statement of inequality (

2

1

and
3

3
. Our strategy is to synthesize the bounds from
Lemma

1
(the trajectory-level analysis) and
Lemma

2
(the token-level analysis). Our central aim is to demonstrate that for a sufficiently small step size
\eta_{k}

\displaystyle\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}(k+1)}{1-J_{q}(k+1)}\Big)-\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}(k)}{1-J_{q}(k)}\Big)\;\geq\;\frac{1}{2}\,[\Delta\mu_{q}(k)]_{+}\cdot\eta_{k}\,.

To establish this, we consider two distinct cases that cover all possibilities.

Case I:
(1-J_{q}(k))\,T_{\infty}\geq 1

In this scenario, we apply the token-level bound from

2
. The lemma guarantees that inequality (
32

\eta_{k}
meets conditions

\displaystyle\eta_{k}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\,G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\cdot T_{\psi_{1}}}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Big(L_{\mathrm{p}}\,T_{\infty}+\frac{G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\,T_{\psi_{1}}}{1-J_{q}(k)}\Big)\cdot\eta_{k}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2}\,[\Delta\mu_{q}(k)]_{+}\,.

(33)

1

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{T_{\infty}\cdot G_{\mathrm{p}}\cdot\delta}{6}+\frac{0.3G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\cdot T_{\infty}\cdot\eta}{9}

\displaystyle=T_{\infty}\cdot G_{\mathrm{p}}\cdot\Big(-\frac{\delta}{6}+\frac{0.3}{9}\cdot\eta\cdot G_{\mathrm{p}}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{T_{\infty}\cdot\eta\cdot G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}}{60}

\displaystyle\geq\Delta\mu_{q}\,,

where the last inequality is true by
\eta\,T_{\infty}\,(G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}+L_{\mathrm{p}})\geq\Delta\mu_{q}\cdot 120
and
L_{\mathrm{p}}\leq G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}
.

Appendix E
First-Order Analysis of the Log-Odds Change

In this section, we analyze how the objective value’s log-odds ratio changes over a single update step.
To simplify the notation, let us consider the state at a single update step

. We will use the following shorthand:
step size
\eta:\,=\eta_{k}
, update direction
\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{w}_{k}
, parameters
\theta_{\rm old}:\,=\theta_{k}
and
\theta:\,=\theta_{k+1}

\displaystyle\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}}{1-J_{q}}\Big)-\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}^{\rm old}}{1-J_{q}^{\rm old}}\Big)\;=\;\Delta\mu_{q}(\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}})\,\eta+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2})\,,

Essentially, this equation shows a first-order Taylor expansion of the change in the log-odds ratio. The key insight is that the term
\Delta\mu_{q}
emerges as the linear coefficient for the step size

We will prove this fundamental relationship at two distinct levels of granularity:

Trajectory Level:

The analysis for entire generation trajectories is presented in

1
, with the full proof in

E.1

Token Level:

The corresponding result for token-level consideration is established in

2
, with its proof located in

E.2
.

Proof of Lemma

Overview

Lemma 3

For policies
\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}

\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}}{1-J_{q}}\Big)-\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}^{\rm old}}{1-J_{q}^{\rm old}}\Big)\\
\;=\;\log\mathds{E}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\cdot\,\mid\,q,\,\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+})}\big[\exp\big\{(\log\uppi_{\theta}-\log\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}})(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\big\}\big]\\
-\,\log\mathds{E}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\cdot\,\mid\,q,\,\mathcal{O}_{q}^{-})}\big[\exp\big\{(\log\uppi_{\theta}-\log\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}})(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\big\}\big]\,.

(45)

The difference of logarithms from

Lemma 4

For any random variable

\big|\log\mathds{E}[e^{X}]-\mathds{E}[X]\big|\;\leq\;2\,\|X\|_{\infty}^{2}\,.

(46)

We now combine

.
In what follows, we introduce a shorthand
\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta}:\,=\log\uppi_{\theta}
.
In our analysis recall that
\theta-\theta_{\rm old}=\eta_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}_{k}
with
\|\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\|_{2}\leq 1

We denote
\eta:\,=\eta_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{w}:\,=\boldsymbol{w}_{k}

We apply the bound from equation (

4

(\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta}-\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta_{\rm old}})(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)

\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}\big(\cdot\bigm|q,\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+}\big)

\displaystyle\bigg|\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}}{1-J_{q}}\Big)-\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}^{\rm old}}{1-J_{q}^{\rm old}}\Big)-T_{1}\bigg|\;\leq\;4\,\|\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta}-\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta_{\rm old}}\|_{\infty}^{2}\,,

(47)

\displaystyle T_{1}\;:\,=\;\big\{\mathds{E}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\cdot\,\mid\,q,\,\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+})}-\mathds{E}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\cdot\,\mid\,q,\,\mathcal{O}_{q}^{-})}\big\}\big[(\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta}-\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta_{\rm old}})(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)\big]\,.

To establish the final bound in

(i)

T_{1}=\Delta\mu_{q}(\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}})\cdot\eta+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2})\qquad

\|\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta}-\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta_{\rm old}}\|_{\infty}=\mathcal{O}(\eta)

Analyzing term

For term

1

\displaystyle\big|(\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta}-\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta_{\rm old}})(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)-\big\langle\nabla_{\theta}\,\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta_{\rm old}}(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,),\,\theta-\theta_{\rm old}\big\rangle\big|\;\leq\;\frac{L_{\mathrm{o}}}{2}\,\|\theta-\theta_{\rm old}\|_{2}^{2}\,.

\displaystyle\big|(\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta}-\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta_{\rm old}})(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)-\eta\,\{\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\,\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta_{\rm old}}(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)\}\big|\;\leq\;\frac{L_{\mathrm{o}}}{2}\,\eta^{2}\,.

Recalling the definition (

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{+}-\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{-}

\displaystyle\Delta\mu_{q}(\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}})\;=\;\big\langle\boldsymbol{w},\,\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{+}-\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{-}\big\rangle=\big\{\mathds{E}_{\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}^{+}(\cdot\mid q)}-\mathds{E}_{\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}^{-}(\cdot\mid q)}\big\}\big[\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\,\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta_{\rm old}}(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)\big]\,.

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta}-\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta_{\rm old}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\bigg|\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}}{1-J_{q}}\Big)-\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}^{\rm old}}{1-J_{q}^{\rm old}}\Big)-\Delta\mu_{q}(\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}})\,\eta\bigg|

\displaystyle\;\leq\;4\,\|\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta}-\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta_{\rm old}}\|_{\infty}^{2}+\big|T_{1}-\Delta\mu_{q}(\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}})\,\eta\big|

E.1.2
Proof of
Lemma

3

Let
\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,,q):\,=\log\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)
be the log-likelihood function and define
\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta_{\rm old}}
analogously.
The updated policy
\uppi_{\theta}
can be expressed in terms of the old policy
\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\;\propto\;\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\,\exp\big\{(\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta}-\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta_{\rm old}})(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\big\}\,.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\frac{J_{q}}{1-J_{q}}

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{\sum_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\in\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+}}\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)}{\sum_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\in\mathcal{O}_{q}^{-}}\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)}\;=\;\frac{\sum_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\in\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+}}\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\,\exp\big\{(\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta}-\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta_{\rm old}})(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)\big\}}{\sum_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\in\mathcal{O}_{q}^{-}}\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\,\exp\big\{(\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta}-\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta_{\rm old}})(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)\big\}}\;=\,:\;\frac{T_{+}}{T_{-}}\,.

(51)

Regarding the numerator
T_{+}
, we use the conditional probability relationship

\displaystyle\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\;=\;J_{q}^{\rm old}\;\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q,\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+})\;=\;J_{q}^{\rm old}\;\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}^{+}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\qquad\mbox{for $\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\in\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+}$}

\displaystyle T_{+}

\displaystyle\;=\;J_{q}^{\rm old}\sum_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\in\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+}}\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}^{+}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\,\exp\big\{(\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta}-\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta_{\rm old}})(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)\big\}

\displaystyle\;=\;J_{q}^{\rm old}\cdot\mathds{E}_{\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}^{+}(\cdot\,\mid\,q)}\big[\exp\big\{(\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta}-\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta_{\rm old}})(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)\big\}\big]\,.

(52a)

Similarly, for the denominator
T_{-}
, we apply equality

\displaystyle\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\;=\;(1-J_{q}^{\rm old})\;\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q,\mathcal{O}_{q}^{-})\;=\;(1-J_{q}^{\rm old})\;\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}^{-}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\qquad\mbox{for $\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\in\mathcal{O}_{q}^{-}$}\,.

It follows that

\displaystyle T_{-}

\displaystyle\;=\;(1-J_{q}^{\rm old})\cdot\mathds{E}_{\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}^{-}(\cdot\,\mid\,q)}\big[\exp\big\{(\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta}-\boldsymbol{L}_{\theta_{\rm old}})(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)\big\}\big]\,.

(52b)

Then, substituting equations (

) and (

) into equation (

), we obtain precisely equation (

) stated in

3
.

E.1.3
Proof of Lemma
4

We start by centering the random variable
X
. Let us define
Y:\,=X-\mathds{E}[X]
, which gives us a new variable with a mean of zero. This simplifies the core expression:

\displaystyle\log\mathds{E}[e^{X}]-\mathds{E}[X]=\log\mathds{E}[e^{\mathds{E}[X]+Y}]-\mathds{E}[X]=\log\mathds{E}[e^{Y}].

By Jensen’s inequality, the term
\log\mathds{E}[e^{Y}]
is always non-negative. This allows us to safely drop the absolute value bars:

\displaystyle\big|\log\mathds{E}[e^{X}]-\mathds{E}[X]\big|=\log\mathds{E}[e^{Y}]\,.

Finally, applying Hoeffding’s lemma to this resulting log moment-generating function bounds it by at most
2\,\|X\|_{\infty}^{2}
. This establishes the result in
Lemma

4
.

E.2
Proof of
Lemma

2

E.2.1
Overview

Our proof proceeds in two main steps. First, we establish a lower bound on the improvement in the log-odds of value
J_{q}
:

\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}}{1-J_{q}}\Big)-\log\Big(\frac{J_{q}^{\rm old}}{1-J_{q}^{\rm old}}\Big)\\
\;\geq\;\big\{\log\big(\mathds{E}^{+}[\exp(X)]\big)-\log\big(\mathds{E}^{-}[\exp(X)]\big)\big\}-L_{\mathrm{p}}T_{\infty}\cdot\eta^{2}\,,

(53)

where the random variable
X
is defined as

\displaystyle X\;:\,=\;\eta\,\big\{\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\big\}\;=\;\big\langle\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q),\,\theta-\theta_{\rm old}\big\rangle\,.

(54)

Here
\mathds{E}^{+}
and
\mathds{E}^{-}
denote expectations over the conditional distributions of positive and negative responses, i.e.,
\mathds{E}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\cdot\,\mid\,q,\,\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+})}
and
\mathds{E}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\cdot\,\mid\,q,\,\mathcal{O}_{q}^{-})}
, respectively.

Second, we bound the difference between the log-moment-generating functions (log-MGFs) that appears on the right-hand side of inequality (
53
):

\displaystyle\log\big(\mathds{E}^{+}[\exp(X)]\big)-\log\big(\mathds{E}^{-}[\exp(X)]\big)\;\geq\;\Delta\mu_{q}(\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}})\cdot\eta-\frac{G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}T_{\psi_{1}}}{J_{q}^{\rm old}}\cdot\eta^{2}\,,

(55)

which holds under the condition that

\eta\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\,G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\cdot T_{\psi_{1}}}}\,.

Combining the bounds from (
53
) and (
55
) directly establishes the result in
Lemma

2
. We now prove these two intermediate claims in
Sections

E.2.2
and
E.2.3
.

E.2.2
Proof of Inequality (
53
)

Our starting point is the smoothness property of the log-policy, as stated in
2
. For any prompt-response pair
(q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,)
, the function
\log\uppi_{\theta}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)
is
(L_{\mathrm{p}}\cdot T_{\infty})
-smooth with respect to
\theta
. A standard result for smooth functions is that:

\displaystyle\big|(\log\uppi_{\theta}-\log\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}})(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)-\big\langle\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q),\,\theta-\theta_{\rm old}\big\rangle\big|\leq\frac{L_{\mathrm{p}}\cdot T_{\infty}}{2}\,\|\theta-\theta_{\rm old}\|_{2}^{2}\,.

By substituting the definition of the random variable
X
from equation (
54
) and using the gradient update rule
\theta-\theta_{\rm old}=\eta\cdot\boldsymbol{w}
(where
\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{2}\leq 1
), this inequality simplifies to:

\displaystyle\big|(\log\uppi_{\theta}-\log\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}})(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)-X\big|\leq\frac{L_{\mathrm{p}}T_{\infty}}{2}\cdot\eta^{2}\,.

This directly implies the following bounds on the conditional expectations:

\displaystyle\log\mathds{E}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\cdot\,\mid\,q,\,\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+})}\big[\exp\big\{(\log\uppi_{\theta}-\log\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}})(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\big\}\big]

\displaystyle\;\geq\;\log\big(\mathds{E}^{+}[\exp(X)]\big)-\frac{L_{\mathrm{p}}T_{\infty}}{2}\cdot\eta^{2}\,.

\displaystyle\log\mathds{E}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\cdot\,\mid\,q,\,\mathcal{O}_{q}^{-})}\big[\exp\big\{(\log\uppi_{\theta}-\log\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}})(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\big\}\big]

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\log\big(\mathds{E}^{-}[\exp(X)]\big)+\frac{L_{\mathrm{p}}T_{\infty}}{2}\cdot\eta^{2}\,.

Subtracting the second inequality from the first yields the desired bound (
53
).

E.2.3
Proof of Inequality (
55
)

To prove inequality (
55
), we will establish and combine three intermediate results.

First, we relate the ratio of conditional MGFs to the unconditional MGF:

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{\mathds{E}^{+}[e^{X}]}{\mathds{E}^{-}[e^{X}]}\right)

\displaystyle\;\geq\;-\frac{1}{1-J_{q}^{\rm old}}\log\mathds{E}[e^{X}]+\frac{1}{1-J_{q}^{\rm old}}\log\mathds{E}^{+}[e^{X}]\,.

(56)

Here
\mathds{E}
denotes the expectation over the entire response space
\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+}\cup\mathcal{O}_{q}^{-}
.

Next, we bound the two terms on the right-hand side of inequality (
56
) separately. We show that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-J_{q}^{\rm old}}\log\mathds{E}^{+}[e^{X}]

\displaystyle\;\geq\;\Delta\mu_{q}(\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}})\cdot\eta\,,\qquad\qquad\mbox{and}

(57a)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-J_{q}^{\rm old}}\log\mathds{E}[e^{X}]

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\frac{G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\cdot T_{\psi_{1}}}{1-J_{q}^{\rm old}}\cdot\eta^{2}\qquad\mbox{if }\eta\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\,G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\cdot T_{\psi_{1}}}}\,.

(57b)

Combining these three bounds (
56
), (
57a
) and (
57b
) directly yields the target inequality (
55
).

We now prove each claim in turn.

Proof of Inequality (
56
):

This result follows from a general property of logarithms derived from the weighted AM-GM inequality. For any real numbers
u,v>0
and any
p\in(0,1)
, the weighted AM-GM inequality states
pu+(1-p)v\geq u^{p}v^{1-p}
.
Dividing by
v
and taking the logarithm gives

\log(u/v)\leq\frac{1}{p}\left(\log(pu+(1-p)v)-\log(v)\right).

We apply this inequality by setting
u:\,=\mathds{E}^{-}[e^{X}]
,
v:\,=\mathds{E}^{+}[e^{X}]
and
p:\,=1-J_{q}^{\rm old}
. This substitution yields

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{\mathds{E}^{-}[e^{X}]}{\mathds{E}^{+}[e^{X}]}\right)

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\frac{1}{1-J_{q}^{\rm old}}\log\big\{J_{q}^{\rm old}\,\mathds{E}^{+}[e^{X}]+(1-J_{q}^{\rm old})\,\mathds{E}^{-}[e^{X}]\big\}-\frac{1}{1-J_{q}^{\rm old}}\log\mathds{E}^{+}[e^{X}]\,.

(58)

By the law of total expectation, the term in the curly braces is simply the unconditional expectation
\mathds{E}[e^{X}]
:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[e^{X}]\;=\;J_{q}^{\rm old}\,\mathds{E}^{+}[e^{X}]+(1-J_{q}^{\rm old})\,\mathds{E}^{-}[e^{X}]\,.

(59)

Substituting (
59
) into (
58
) completes the proof of inequality (
56
).

Proof of Inequality (
57a
):

By Jensen’s inequality,
\log\mathds{E}^{+}[e^{X}]\geq\mathds{E}^{+}[X]
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\log\mathds{E}^{+}[e^{X}]}{1-J_{q}^{\rm old}}\geq\frac{\mathds{E}^{+}[X]}{1-J_{q}^{\rm old}}\,,

The remainder of the proof is dedicated to showing that the right-hand side is exactly equal to the desired term:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathds{E}^{+}[X]}{1-J_{q}^{\rm old}}\;=\;\Delta\mu_{q}(\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}})\cdot\eta\,.

(60)

To prove this, we start with the policy gradient identity from equation (
10
):

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\,J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}})\;=\;J_{q}^{\rm old}(1-J_{q}^{\rm old})\cdot\Delta\mu_{q}(\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}})\,.

We can express policy gradient
\nabla_{\theta}\,J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}})
in terms of an expectation over negative responses.
Using the fact that

J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}})=\mathds{P}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\cdot\mid q)}\big[\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\in\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+}\big]
, we get

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\,J_{q}(\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}})\;=\;\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathds{P}_{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\cdot\mid q)}\big[\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\in\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+}\big]

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathds{E}\big[\mathds{1}\{\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\in\mathcal{O}_{q}^{+}\}\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\big]

\displaystyle\;=\;J_{q}^{\rm old}\cdot\mathds{E}^{+}\big[\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\big]\,.

Substituting this back into our gradient identity yields:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}\cdot J_{q}^{\rm old}\cdot\mathds{E}^{+}\big[\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\big]\;=\;J_{q}^{\rm old}(1-J_{q}^{\rm old})\cdot\Delta\mu_{q}(\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}})\,.

Dividing by
J_{q}^{\rm old}
and multiplying by
\eta
, we find

\eta\cdot\mathds{E}^{+}\big[\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\mid q)\big]\;=\;(1-J_{q}^{\rm old})\cdot\Delta\mu_{q}(\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}})\cdot\eta\,.

From the definition of
X
in (
54
), the left-hand side is
\mathds{E}^{+}[X]
. This confirms equation (
60
) and completes the proof of inequality (
57a
).

Proof of Inequality (
57b
):

The final step is to bound the unconditional log-MGF,
\log\mathds{E}[e^{X}]
. Our strategy is to view
X
as the final value of a martingale and apply a variant of Hoeffding’s inequality.

Martingale Formulation.

Consider a random response
\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,
generated from policy
\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}
, i.e.,
\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,\sim\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\cdot\mid q)
.
The random variable
X
is a sum over the tokens in the response
\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,
:

\displaystyle X\;=\;\sum_{t=1}^{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,|}\eta\,\big\{\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(o_{t}\mid q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,_{<t})\big\}\,.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}
be the
\sigma
-field generated by the prompt
q
and the response prefix
\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,_{\leq t}
.
We define a martingale difference sequence
\{\xi_{t}\}
:

\displaystyle\xi_{t}\;:\,=\;\eta\,\big\{\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\,\log\uppi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(o_{t}\mid q,\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,_{<t})\big\}\,.

(61)

Then
X_{t}=\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}\xi_{t^{\prime}}
is a martingale with respect to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}
, and
X=X_{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,|}
. By
2
, the increments are bounded:
|\xi_{t}|\leq G_{\mathrm{p}}\cdot\eta
.

Supermartingale and Optional Stopping.

Using Hoeffding’s lemma
[
4
, Lemma 2.6]
on the conditional expectation, we have

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[\exp(2\,\xi_{t})\bigm|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]\;\leq\;\exp\big\{2\,G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\eta^{2}\big\}\,.

This allows us to construct a supermartingale
\{{\bf M}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T_{\infty}}
:

\displaystyle{\bf M}_{t}\;:\,=\;\exp\big\{2\,X_{t}-2\,G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\eta^{2}\cdot t\big\}\qquad\mbox{for $t=1,2,\ldots$}.

Since
|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,|\leq T_{\infty}
is a bounded stopping time, Doob’s optional stopping theorem applies, giving
\mathds{E}[{\bf M}_{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,|}]\leq\mathds{E}[{\bf M}_{0}]=1
. This means

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\Big[\exp\big\{2\,X-2\,G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\eta^{2}\cdot|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,|\big\}\Big]=\mathds{E}\big[{\bf M}_{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,|}\big]\;\leq\;1\,.

Cauchy-Schwarz and
\psi_{1}
Norm.

We now isolate
\mathds{E}[e^{X}]
using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[e^{X}]\leq\mathds{E}\Big[\exp\big\{2\,X-2\,G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\eta^{2}\cdot|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,|\big\}\Big]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathds{E}\Big[\exp\big\{2\,G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\eta^{2}\cdot|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,|\big\}\Big]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\mathds{E}\Big[\exp\big\{2\,G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\eta^{2}\cdot|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,|\big\}\Big]^{\frac{1}{2}}\,,

where the last step used our supermartingale bound.
Taking the logarithm gives

\displaystyle\log\mathds{E}[e^{X}]\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2}\log\mathds{E}\Big[\exp\big\{2\,G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\eta^{2}\cdot|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,|\big\}\Big]\,.

Finally, we bound the remaining term using the sub-exponential (
\psi_{1}
-Orlicz) norm of the response length,
T_{\psi_{1}}
from
3
.
By definition,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[\exp\{|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,|/T_{\psi_{1}}\}\big]\;\leq\;2\,.

Consider a function
\phi(\lambda):\,=\log\mathds{E}[\exp(\lambda\,|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,|)]
, which is convex with
\phi(0)=0
.
Under condition
0\leq 2\,G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\eta^{2}\leq 1/T_{\psi_{1}}
, the convexity implies

\phi(2\,G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\eta^{2})\;\leq\;2\,G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\eta^{2}T_{\psi_{1}}\cdot\phi(1/T_{\psi_{1}})\;\leq\;2\log 2\cdot G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\eta^{2}T_{\psi_{1}}\,.

It follows that

\displaystyle\log\mathds{E}\Big[\exp\big\{2\,G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\eta^{2}\cdot|\vec{\boldsymbol{o}}\!\,|\big\}\Big]\;=\;\phi(2\,G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\eta^{2})\;\leq\;2\,G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\eta^{2}\cdot T_{\psi_{1}}\,.

Plugging this back into our bound for
\log\mathds{E}[e^{X}]
gives

\displaystyle\log\mathds{E}[e^{X}]\;\leq\;G_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}\eta^{2}\cdot T_{\psi_{1}}\,.

This establishes inequality (
57b
) and completes the proof.

F.2
Further Elaboration on Contextual Bandit Experiments

Our theoretical analysis so far has focused on convergence for a single prompt
q
. A natural question is: how does the theory extend to the case of multiple prompts or questions? To illustrate this, we consider a contextual bandit simulation.
In this setup, each iteration
k
draws a random context
x_{k}
(equivalent to a prompt
q_{k}
in our framework), and the update gradient
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}
is computed from that context
x_{k}
. Crucially, the same update direction is then applied globally to all prompts (via the shared parameter
\theta
).

Two scenarios can arise:
Case (a):

Alignment.
For some prompts
q
(or contexts
x
), the update direction
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}
aligns closely with the Gradient Gap
\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{+}-\boldsymbol{g}_{q}^{-}
. In these cases, the Gap Alignment
\Delta\mu_{q}(k)
is positive and large, leading to effective improvement.
Case (b):

Misalignment.
For other prompts, the Gradient Gap is nearly orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}
. Here,
\Delta\mu_{q}(k)
is small, and applying the same step size
\eta_{k}
can cause overshooting, preventing performance gains.

Our simulation confirms this intuition: some contexts consistently overshoot and are difficult to improve, while those with stronger alignment improve steadily—the better the alignment, the greater the improvement.
For overshooting contexts, there is no gradual accumulation of
\Delta\mu_{q}(k)
toward improvement; instead, crossing into the overshooting region acts as a decisive threshold, after which performance collapses toward zero with no recovery.
