Background

The new coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), caused by a virus in the coronaviruses family called severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), previously known as 2019-nCoV, was first identified in China in December 2019 and declared a pandemic by the World Health Organization in March 2020 [1]. The first case of coronavirus was observed in a Nepalese citizen returning from Wuhan city on 9 January 2020 who visited Shukraraj Tropical and Infectious Disease Hospital on 13 January 2020. The National Public Health Laboratory (NPHL) communicated about positive test results for 2019-nCoV on 23 January 2020 [2]. On the 23rd March 2020, the second case of COVID-19 was reported in Nepal. Immediately after the confirmation of the second case, nationwide lockdown was endorsed in Nepal from the 25th March 2020 [3]. The government of Nepal developed guidelines for feasts and festivals which were endorsed on 6th August 2022, highlighting the following: To minimize contact with any person during a festival or feast except living at home, if needed, there should be at least a two-meter distance between two individuals [4]. The mandatory use of masks involves either surgical or three-layered fabric masks and compulsory washing of hands or the use of sanitizers before being involved in festival or feast [4]. Furthermore, the government of Nepal prohibited the gathering of more than twenty-five people at any program [4].

A web-based cross-sectional study from Bangladesh revealed that the majority of the respondents were staying home (93.2%), wearing a mask (91.4%) and 87.1% maintained safe distances when travelling outside [5]. A study conducted in China reported that the average practice score was 3.1 (SD, 0.9) to prevent COVID-19 infection, 66.9% of the respondents were able to wear masks, and 96.1% of the respondents maintained hand hygiene and washed their hands in time [6]. Another study conducted at Debre Markos comprehensive specialized hospital from July to August 2020 reported that the prevalence of poor preventive practices among outpatient service visitors was 175 (44%) and 144 (36.2%) of them had good practices [7]. A range of containment and mitigation strategies have been implemented to address COVID-19 effectively. These measures aim to delay significant patient surges in hospitals and safeguard the most vulnerable populations, such as the elderly and individuals with underlying health conditions, from infection [8].

Owing to the lack of effective treatment, the major way to slow the spread of COVID-19 is by preventing transmission of the virus among people through awareness, vaccination and adoption of proper preventive practices [9]. Public health measures will decrease the incidence of infection and disrupt the chain of transmission. Public health authorities are continually emphasizing the need to adopt certain preventive practices related to hand and personal hygiene, social distancing, avoiding unnecessary travel, etc. Several factors such as diverse cultures, wide socioeconomic disparities and healthcare service inequalities, might pose challenges in the implementation of these preventive practices at the community level [10]. Ministry of Health and Population (MoHP) of Nepal has implemented joint measures focused on health promotion, public education, awareness, and community engagement to prevent disease spread. To support efforts against coronavirus transmission, it is essential that the public understands the outbreak and their role in controlling it. Misunderstandings, misinformation, and lack of awareness can result in neglect of basic hygiene practices and disregard for national guidelines, potentially accelerating the virus’s spread within communities [11]. However, there was a lack of recognition regarding the application of preventive practices via questionnaire-based methods.

Till date most of the study focused on assessing the Knowledge, attitude and practice of Nepali towards COVID-19 [12, 13] but none of the study use and validate the preventive practice questionnaire during COVID-19 in Nepali. As coronavirus disease is a new disease, the Questionnaire used to assess preventive practices are limited. In this context, the preventive practice questionnaire developed by All India Institute of Medical Science was used [14]. Unlike other instruments, a definite advantage of this questionnaire is its ability to identify individual level preventive practices. Applicable in the local setting of Nepal, along with reasons of not following the preventive measures, hence providing appropriate guidance according to the reasons. Although it has been validated in the English language and has yet to be adapted for use and validation in Nepal. Therefore, a questionnaire regarding preventive practices against COVID-19 pandemic in the general population was adopted. This study aims to validate the Nepali version of the questionnaire to assess preventive practices against the COVID-19 pandemic in the general population to measure accuracy and determine the cutoff point of the questionnaire.

Methods

Study design

A descriptive cross-sectional design was used to validate the questionnaire, preventive practices against the COVID-19 pandemic in the general population. The study was conducted from November 2022–April 2023. Recruitment of the respondents took place from November–December 2022.

Study setting

This study was conducted via the administration of an online questionnaire platform. A questionnaire was administered to a large sample of the general population throughout country via an online platform that includes Gmail, Facebook Messenger, Viber and WhatsApp Messenger. Online platform was chosen for the coverage of wide range of participants including diverse age, educational background, and socio-economic status.

Participants

The study population consisted of Nepali citizens with age above 18 years residing in Nepal and using any of the online platforms, including Gmail, Facebook Messenger, Viber and WhatsApp Messenger. Participants who were willing and agreed to participate in the study were included. As this Questionnaire validation was performed for the general population, respondents from all academic background and informal sector were included. Age above 18 years, Nepali citizens, a good understanding of the Nepali language and voluntary participation were included. The exclusion criteria were participants younger than 18 years, Nepali citizens but residing outside Nepal, non-Nepali citizens staying in Nepal and who were unwilling to participate were excluded.

Data collection tools and techniques

As the main aim of this study was to validate the Nepali version of preventive practice questionnaire in the general public, the online platform was suitable for internet users [15], who can comprehend Nepali and follow the instructions given by the questionnaire for more incorporation of every age group of participants for generalizable findings. The study protocol and participants’ information sheets clearly explained the purpose, method and importance of participation in the study open after clicking the link of questionnaire. The information sheet was subsequently read by clicking that the next participants moved to the page of online consent, where they could tick yes or no for their participation in the study. The main page of the questionnaire was opened only after the participants clicked on the consent form. The questionnaire was sent via an online platform that includes mail, Facebook Messenger, Viber and WhatsApp Messenger. After the link was opened, the front page included details about the researcher and the main aim of the study. The questionnaire was divided into three sections; part I included sociodemographic information, including comorbidities, part II included preventive practices against COVID-19 in the general population. The respondents were asked in detail how often they wash/ sanitize their hand with soap and water, how often they maintain a minimum distance of one meter and how often they use masks while going out of home. For questions concerning preventive practices, a questionnaire is intended to evaluate the extent of adherence to various preventive practices. It comprises questions rated on a 5-point Likert scale in which the options associated with better compliance with preventive practices are given higher points. The respondents are provided with the options of always (more than 90% of the time), mostly (approximately 75% of the time), commonly (approximately 50% of the time), occasionally (approximately 25% of the time) and rarely (less than 10% time). Study size.

The maximum required sample size was calculated applying the following formula, n = z2pq/d2 with p = 50%, q = 50% and allowable error (d) = 5%. The calculated sample size was 385. However, the online link of the questionnaire was sent to 400 respondents among them a total of 351 respondents provide consent and filled out the form with response rate of87.75%.

Description of the Nepali version of preventive practices against COVID-19 pandemic in the general population

The questionnaire used to assess preventive practices against COVID-19 in the general population consists of two sections. Section A includes preventive practices against COVID-19 in the general population, and section B consists reasons of following and not following preventive practices. Section A consists of 18 questions. Questions 1–5 were related to hand hygiene, questions 6–10 were related to social distancing, questions 11–13 were related to mask use, questions 14 and 15 were related to gadgets/ fomites, question 16 was related to government restrictions and questions 17 − 18 were related to preparedness. Section B consists of 19 questions asking for reasons of preventive practices. In this section questions 1–5 were related to reasons for not following hand hygiene, questions 6– 9 were related to reasons for not following social distancing, questions 10–12 were related to reasons for not using masks and questions 13–14 were reasons for not taking precautions. Questions 15 and 16 were technology-related Aarogya set app questions. Question number 17 was related to reasons for not obeying government restrictions, question number 18 was about the reason for not being aware of any helplines, and question 19 was open ended about the reason for the increasing number of COVID-19 cases among the general population. Section B consisted the questions of qualitative characteristics asking for reason of not following the preventive practices, so validation of the questionnaire was conducted only for section A.

The English version of the questionnaire was translated to Nepali via the following method, two Nepali teachers translated the original English questionnaire to Nepali. Translated Nepali version was discussed for linguistic and cultural adaptation. Translator were bilingual, born in Nepal and native Nepali speakers. Two professional translators then independently translated the Nepali version back to English. The translated versions and discussed discrepancies/difficulties highlighted during the translation process. Finally, two translators discussed the differences, and a consensus was reached comprising all translators, the principal investigator for finalization of the Nepali version.

Validity of the questionnaire

The face and content validity of the questionnaire was established through judgment, peer review and expert evaluation. The construct validity of the questionnaire was established through the correlation coefficient in SPSS [16], which is satisfactory. Pretesting of the questionnaire was performed to assure reliability and ensure the flow of the questionnaire. For pretesting of the questionnaire, questionnaire link was sent to 30 respondents, and the wording of the questionnaire was revised on the basis of the findings of the pretest. Reliability statistics were measured through the Cronbach’s alpha test, which was 0.88, indicating good internal consistency among the 18 items of the preventive practice questionnaire.

Operational definition

Preventive measures are the terms that represent mask use, washing hands or using alcohol-based sanitizers for 20 second and maintaining a social distance of one meter between two individuals.

Data management and analysis

The data were compiled and checked for completeness and exported from the Google link to Microsoft Excel. The data were subsequently exported to the Statistical Package of Social Science (SPSS) version 11.5. Descriptive analysis was performed for the demographic variables. The internal consistency of the questionnaire was estimated by using Cronbach’s α test, which was 0.88. Cronbach’s α is a statistic calculated from the pair-wise correlations between items. Parameters such as the sensitivity and specificity of the questionnaire were separately calculated for preventive practice. Using SPSS version 11.5 software, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed on the basis of a continuum of all possible values of questionnaire score. The identification of the best cutoff value was performed by cross-tabulating the value of sensitivity and 1-specificity for each point. The accuracy of the screening tool at best the cutoff was identified from the ROC curve by computing the area under the curve.

Ethical consideration

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Committee of Nepalese Army Institute of Health Sciences (Reg.no–670). Informed consent was obtained from all the respondents before their enrollment. Throughout the study, the confidentiality and anonymity of the respondents were rigorously maintained and ensured.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the study population

The sociodemographic characteristics were described in terms of age, marital status, ethnicity, religion, educational status, occupation, residence, family type, transportation and comorbidities. The mean age of the respondents was 39.5 years, and more than half (59.3%) of them were married. More than half of the respondents were Brahmin/Chhetri (55.0%), and the majority were Hindu (92.9%) by religion. The majority of the respondents (63.2%) had completed higher education, followed by (17.1%) who had completed primary education. Most of the respondents (32.5%) were formal sector employees, and half of the respondents (50.7%) resided in municipalities. With respect to family type, (56.7%) of the respondents were living in nuclear families and (44.4%) of the respondents use their own vehicle as a means of transportation. Hypertension was the most common comorbidity among respondents (12.3%) followed by diabetes mellitus (4.3%) (Table 1).

ROC curve for preventive practices against the COVID-19 pandemic in the general population

Receiver operating characteristic curve for preventive practices against the COVID-19 pandemic in the general population, demonstrating the area under the curve at the chosen cutoff level was 47/48. The area under the curve was 0.859. Thus, the Nepalese version of the questionnaire concerning preventive practices against COVID-19 pandemic in the general population had an accuracy of 85.9% (95% CI 0.757–0.961, p = 0.001) in screening for good and poor preventive practices with the cutoff of 47/48. The sensitivity at the cut-off of 47 and 48 are 93.2% and 91.4% respectively and specificity is 66.7% for the cut-off 47/48. Hence, the cutoff level can be considered optimal for defining the threshold level for good and poor preventive practices (Fig. 1).

Discussion

The present study revealed that the overall prevalence of good preventive practices against COVID-19 was 90.6%, with a cutoff point of 47/48. The prevalence reported in this study was higher than that reported in a study conducted at the Universal College of Medical Science, where the practice was good (≥ 3 score) for 81.5% of the participants, with a mean knowledge score of 10.73 ± 1.12 and poor for 18.5% of the participants, with a mean knowledge score of 10.46 ± 1.13 (p = 0.24) [17]. Another similar web-based study conducted with all Nepalese people aged > 18years reported that 93.1% had not visited any crowded places and that 92.4% wore masks while going out [18]. A descriptive cross-sectional study conducted in cancer patients at Bir Hospital, Kathmandu, Nepal, revealed that 76.0% of participants were adequately aware of COVID-19 whereas 83.1% wore masks always and 81.9% washed their hands for at least 20 seconds [19]. Another web-based COVID-19 national survey on level of knowledge, attitude and practice among frontline healthcare workers working at different hospitals in Nepal reported 78.9% appropriate practice [20]. The present study revealed that the prevalence of good preventive practices was greater than that reported in a study conducted in Ethiopia, where 19.1% of the respondents followed COVID-19 prevention measures either usually or always [21]. The discrepancy in the prevalence of good preventive practices might be due to variability in knowledge of COVID-19, social structure, educational status, economic status and health care service availability.

The sensitivity and specificity of the Nepalese version of preventive practices against the COVID-19 pandemic in the general population found in this study were 93% and 67%, respectively, at a cutoff level of 47/48. The original version of the questionnaire did not calculate sensitivity and specificity, but face validity was established through expert evaluation and pilot testing. Construct validity was assessed through exploratory factor analysis with varimax rotation with an eigenvalue cutoff of 1, and the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for these 18 items in section A was found to be 0.82, which indicates good internal consistency [14]. Another institutional based cross-sectional study conducted in South Gondar Zone, hospitals in Northwest Ethiopia reported preventive practices measured via 10 items; the total score ranged from 0 to 10 and a cutoff score > 8 was considered good practice, whereas a score < 8 was considered poor practice [22]. A similar study conducted among healthcare professionals in Asir Hospital, Saudi Arabia, using a standardized, self-administered questionnaire with seven questions to measure the practices of healthcare professionals reported that a mean score of ≤ 2 was considered good practice, a score of 2–3 was regarded as insufficient practice [23]. A cross-sectional study of two multispecialty hospitals in the Al-Qassim region, Saudi Arabia, using a self-administered questionnaire to assess knowledge and attitude towards Middle East respiratory syndrome, revealed that Knowledge scores ranged from 0 to 13, and cutoff level of < 9 was set for poor knowledge and that of ≥ 9 was set for good knowledge [24]. Assessment of attitudes was carried out through 7 item questions in which the responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale. A score of 1 was given to strongly agree, 2 to agree, 3 to undecide, 4 to disagree and 5 to strongly disagree. A mean score of ≤ 2 was considered a positive attitude, whereas a score of 3–5 was considered a negative attitude [24]. The variation in the cutoff score might be due to differences in design, methodology, translation process, sample size, method of administering questionnaires and timing of interviews.

A study conducted in an outpatient clinic at a tertiary care hospital in New Delhi, India, among 384 patients to evaluate clinical symptoms and validate a clinical symptom-based scoring system for the diagnostic evaluation of COVID-19 revealed that the cutoff score for this clinical symptom-based scoring system was 41.7 to obtain optimum sensitivity and specificity values via the ROC curve generated from the development dataset. The area under the ROC curve (AUC-ROC) for the development dataset was 0.71 (95% CI: 0.61–0.80) [25]. Another study conducted to generate a COVID-19 diagnosis risk score for use at the time of hospital admission via transparent reporting of a multivariable prediction model for individual prognosis or diagnosis checklist in an east London hospital among 581 individuals reported an AUC of 0.8535 (95% confidence interval 0.8121–0.8950) and a cutoff of 2, with a sensitivity of 78.1% and specificity of 86.8% [26]. A study conducted to derive a diagnostic score to rapidly identify the possibility of being affected by COVID-19 at hospital admission among 194 patients revealed that the ROC AUC for this model was 0.89 (95% CI 0.83–0.96), indicating excellent discriminatory power, with a cutoff of ≥-3 indicating the highest negative predictive value (100%), whereas a cutoff of ≥ 5 indicating the highest positive predictive value (100%) [27]. The variability in findings between these studies might be due to differences in the design of the studies. The majority of the studies were conducted to determine the risk score for developing COVID-19, whereas our study aimed to assess preventive practices.

In a literature search on Google Scholar, PubMed, ResearchGate and ScienceDirect, this is the first study to validate the preventive practice questionnaire in the Nepali version via ROC curves. On the other hand, this study has several limitations. Firstly, all the measurements in this study were based on self-reports, which may have been prone to response and information bias. Secondly, this study was cross-sectional and, therefore, cannot demonstrate causality between the variables. As the sample was collected via an online platform, the findings may not be generalizable to respondents who are not in excess of the internet.

Conclusion

The Nepalese version of the questionnaire regarding preventive practices against COVID-19 pandemic in the general population can be successfully used as a survey tool to assess good and poor practices of preventive measures during pandemics of a similar nature, such as the COVID-19 pandemic. These findings might help to detect appropriate adherence to preventive practices can be achieved in busy outpatient departments or in any local setting which allows timely counseling and advice. Additionally, detecting adherence to preventive practices may help local health authorities and policymakers in identifying specific populations for future awareness campaigns during infectious disease outbreaks. Collaborative efforts among healthcare providers, the Ministry of Health and Population, local governments, development partners, and media should be implemented as powerful tools to increase adherence to preventive practices.
