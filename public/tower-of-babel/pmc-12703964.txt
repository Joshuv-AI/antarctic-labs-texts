A fast-progressing orthotopic ovarian cancer model reveals synergistic antitumor effects of AXL-targeting nanobodies and Olaparib
Authors: Aarushi A. Caro, Alícia Gordún Peiró, Eva Hadadi, Yani Berckmans, Yvon Elkrim, Ayla Debraekeleer, Gisèle Mateu Cabrera, Kim De Veirman, An Coosemans, Damya Laoui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•P3 ID8 Thy1.1 cell line was established by three consecutive in vivo passages of the ID8-fLuc cell line.•Orthotopic inoculation of the P3 ID8 Thy1.1 cells results in a clinically representative ovarian cancer mouse model.•AXL-Fc nanobodies trigger necrosis in the ovarian cancer cells from orthotopic ovarian tumors.•AXL-Fc and Olaparib act synergistically to suppress ovarian cancer cell proliferation.

Introduction

According to the Globocan 2022 report (International Agency for Research on Cancer, World Health Organization), ovarian cancer (OC) is the 8th deadliest cancer among women worldwide, owing to the rather asymptomatic nature of the cancer, resulting in two-thirds of the patients undergoing late diagnosis, wherein the patients have reached advanced-stage disease (Lawson-Michod, 2022, Colombo, 2019). Currently, primary debulking surgery and platinum-based chemotherapy are the gold standards for OC treatment (Marchetti, 2010, Kuroki and Guntupalli, 2020). Several targeted therapies have been approved for use in OC, including anti-angiogenic bevacizumab (anti-VEGF) and PARP inhibitors (Kuroki and Guntupalli, 2020). In 2024, the antibody-drug conjugate Mirvetuximab Soravtansine targeting the folate receptor alpha (FRα) was approved in FRα-hi, platinum-resistant high grade serous OC (HGSOC) patients (Moore, 2023). Despite these advances, approximately 40 % OC patients still die from the disease (Seidman et al., 2012), underscoring the urgent need to develop novel therapeutic strategies. AXL (also known as UFO, JTK11, Tyro7, and ARK) is a receptor-tyrosine kinase belonging to the TAM (TYRO3, AXL, MER) family (Yadav, 2025). AXL is an oncogene that was initially identified in two patients with chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML), where it was found to be involved in the progression of CML to acute-phase leukemia. As such, the gene was termed AXL, derived from the Greek word “anexelekto” which means uncontrolled (O’Bryan, 1991). Given that the role of AXL was first identified in the context of cancer, subsequent research efforts were understandably directed toward elucidating its role in tumorigenesis. The TAM family of receptor tyrosine kinases shares several common ligands, including growth arrest-specific gene 6 (GAS6), protein S, Tubby, Tubby-like protein 1 (TULP-1), and Galactin-3 (Yadav, 2025). AXL in particular has the highest affinity for GAS6 compared to TYRO3 and MER (Tsou, 2014). Elevated levels of GAS6 have been reported in the serum of patients with oral squamous cell carcinoma (Jiang et al., 2015). Overexpression of AXL has been observed in several cancers, which has been shown to contribute to metastasis development and drug resistance (Yeo, 2023, Antony, 2016, Feneyrolles, 2014, Verma et al., 2011, Tang et al., 2023). Although AXL can be activated in a ligand-independent manner, the AXL-GAS6 axis has been shown to play a key role in promoting cancer cell proliferation, anti-apoptotic signaling, cell survival, migration, maintenance of stemness, and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) (Yadav, 2025, Zhu et al., 2019). Moreover, AXL is also involved in angiogenesis and modulation of immunosuppression in tumors (Yadav, 2025). Inhibition of the AXL-GAS6 axis has shown positive outcomes in several preclinical cancer models, including OC (Feneyrolles, 2014, Quinn, 2019, Rankin, 2010). In OC, AXL inhibition has been shown to increase sensitivity to PARP inhibitors and chemotherapy (Quinn, 2019, Mullen, 2022, Balaji, 2017). Currently, several therapeutic modalities have been developed to target AXL, including small molecule inhibitors, monoclonal antibodies, soluble (decoy) receptors, antibody-drug conjugates, and chimeric antigen receptors (CAR) (Bhalla and Gerber, 2023). Currently, six clinical trials targeting AXL have been listed on clinicaltrials.gov against OC. Among these, one employed AXL-targeted CAR-NK cell therapy (NCT05410717 − Phase I) and three others used Batiraxept (also known as AVB-S6-500), a recombinant fusion protein that serves as a decoy receptor for GAS6, resulting in the inhibition of AXL (NCT04729608 – Phase III, NCT04019288 – Phase I/II, NCT03607955 – Phase I). Additionally, one clinical trial used Enapotomab Vedotin (also known as HuMAX-AXL-ADC), an AXL-specific human IgG1 antibody conjugated to the microtubule-disrupting agent monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) (NCT02988817 – Phase I/II). Finally, the last clinical trial used TP-0903, a small molecule multikinase inhibitor that was designed to target AXL (NCT02729298 – Phase I). Although none of these clinical trials have reported improved anti-tumor effects of AXL inhibition in OC patients with heterogeneous AXL expression (Fuh, 2024, Knisely, 2024, Sarantopoulos, 2019), the Phase III trial (NCT04729608) demonstrated that in platinum-resistant recurrent OC patients with high AXL expression in tumors, AXL inhibition showed significantly improved overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) when treated with the AXL inhibitor (Batiraxcept) in combination with Paclitaxel, compared to Paclitaxel alone (Fuh, 2024).

Besides the therapy itself, developing effective anti-cancer therapies depends on robust, well-designed animal models that accurately translate to human disease, enabling reliable prediction of clinical outcomes. Currently, intraperitoneal (IP) inoculation of OC cell lines is most widely used to mimic the peritoneal metastasis of OC due to the experimental feasibility of this procedure (Cook, 2023, Ying, 2023). However, the lesions formed from IP inoculation were found to be different from those naturally arising from the metastasis of orthotopically inoculated tumors on the ovary (Cook, 2023). IP inoculation fails to follow the natural path of metastasis, including the enrichment of cells that are capable of leaving the primary site of inoculation, i.e., the ovaries (Cook, 2023).

In this manuscript, we focus on addressing two central aims. First, to establish a clinically relevant OC model, we developed an orthotopic mouse model for OC that is highly translatable to patients and reaches humane endpoints by day 42 post-inoculation, representing a faster progression compared to currently available orthotopic OC models (Cook, 2023, Van Vloten, 2022, Dean et al., 2019, Dean, 2019, Rodriguez, 2022, Greenaway et al., 2008). Second, to assess the potential of an alternate therapeutic modality for the inhibition of the AXL/GAS6 axis, we use a camelid-derived single-domain antibody (also known as a nanobody) (Vandewalle, 2024). Owing to their small size (∼15 kDa compared to ∼ 150 kDa for conventional antibodies) and high solubility, nanobodies offer several advantages over antibodies, particularly in the context of solid tumors (Jumapili, 2023). Nanobodies undergo fast elimination via the kidneys and can be easily engineered to enhance their half-life in circulation without compromising their small size (Jumapili, 2023). Moreover, the limitations of small-molecule drugs could be overcome by nanobodies. For instance, small-molecule drugs are less specific and can cause off-target effects, which can result in toxicities, and development of therapy resistance (Martín-Acosta and Xiao, 2021). Nanobodies, on the other hand, are highly target-specific and easy to engineer (Jumapili, 2023). Owing to these advantages, we used AXL-targeting nanobodies (Vandewalle, 2024) to determine anti-cancer effects in OC. These nanobodies were coupled to a mouse IgG2a-Fc domain (called AXL-Fc) to enhance their efficacy, half-life, and effector functions (Vandewalle, 2024). Currently, the therapeutic effect of AXL inhibitors has only been evaluated in subcutaneous or intraperitoneal OC preclinical models (Quinn, 2019, Rankin, 2010, Kanlikilicer, 2017, Tian, 2021). Our study extends these findings by evaluating their efficacy in an orthotopic setting that closely reflects the human cancer context. Finally, we show that AXL-Fc induces necrosis in murine OC cells and synergizes with the PARP inhibitor Olaparib in inhibiting cancer cell proliferation.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Female C57BL/6JRj mice aged 6–8 weeks were purchased from Janvier Labs (France). Mice were housed at 20–22 °C, 45–65 % humidity with a 12 h light/dark cycle. Mice were monitored daily. These experimental procedures, follow-up, and housing complied with the Belgian Council for Laboratory Animal Science guidelines. All animal experiments were approved by the Ethical Committee for Animal Experiments of the Vrije Universiteit Brussel (25–220-23, 23–220-35, 23–220-54) and KU Leuven (P132/2024).

Cancer cell lines

The human HEY cell line (Sanbio B.V.) and mouse ID8-derived cancer cell lines were cultured in DMEM complete medium composed of DMEM (Gibco, cat# 41965–039) supplemented with supplemented with 10 % (v/v) heat-inactivated FCS (Capricorn Scientific, cat# FBS-12A), 300 μg/ml L-glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich, cat# G8540), 100 units mL−1 penicillin, and 100 μg mL−1 streptomycin (Gibco, cat# 15140122). The human HEY-A8, SKOV3, A2780 (Sigma-Aldrich), and OVCAR8 cell lines were cultured in RPMI complete medium composed of RPMI-1640 (Gibco, cat# 52400041) supplemented with 10 % (v/v) heat-inactivated FCS (Capricorn Scientific, cat# FBS-12A), 300 μg/ml L-glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich, cat# G8540), 100 units mL−1 penicillin, and 100 μg mL−1 streptomycin (Gibco, cat# 15140122). Human OVCAR3 cell line was cultured in RPMI-1640 (Gibco, cat# 52400041) supplemented with 20 % (v/v) heat-inactivated FCS (Capricorn Scientific, cat# FBS-12A), 12.8 µg mL−1 human recombinant insulin (Gibco, cat# 12585014), 100 units mL−1 penicillin, and 100 μg mL−1 streptomycin (Gibco, cat# 15140122). The mouse HM1 cell line was kindly provided by RIKEN BRC through the national BioResource project of the MEXT, Japan. HM1 cells were cultured in MEMα, nucleosides (Gibco, cat# 22571020) supplemented with 20 % (v/v) heat-inactivated FCS (Capricorn Scientific, cat# FBS-12A), 100 units mL−1 penicillin, and 100 μg mL−1 streptomycin (Gibco, cat# 15140122). All cultures were maintained at 37 °C in the presence of 5 % CO2. When 80–100 % confluency was reached, cells were harvested using 0.05 % trypsin–EDTA (Gibco, cat# 25300054).

Cancer cell inoculation and in vivo passaging

For ID8-derived cancer cell inoculation, 30 min before the surgery, 5 mg/kg Meloxicam (Metacam, Boehringer Ingelheim) was injected subcutaneously. Mice were anesthetized using 100 mg/kg Ketamine (Ketamidor, Ecuphar) and 10 mg/kg Xylazine (Rompun, Bayer) intraperitoneally. Eye ointment (Duratears, Alcon) was applied to avoid dehydration. The mice were shaved at the right caudal portion of the dorsum from the thoracolumbar junction to the base of the tail. The surgical site was sterilized using 10 % isobetadine (Viatris Healthcare) and ethanol wipes, and the mice were placed on a heating pad. A small dorsolateral incision (0.5 – 1 cm) was made using surgical scissors. The retroperitoneum was dissected to expose the fat pad attached to the right ovary. 7 µL of 1 x 106 ID8-derived cancer cells in HBSS (Gibco, cat# 14175129) were injected into the ovary using a 20 µL Hamilton syringe with a 22 ga needle. Sham surgeries were performed by injecting HBSS in the right ovary of control mice. The peritoneum was closed with absorbable sutures (Coated Vicryl Plus Antibacterial (polyglactin 910) Suture, Ethicon, cat# VCP397H), and the skin was closed with non-absorbable sutures (Ethilon Nylon Suture, Ethicon, cat# F2412). The wound was disinfected with 10 % isobetadine (Viatris Healthcare). The mice were placed on a heatpad and monitored until they were fully awake. Mice were weighed every 2 days until they reached 25 g due to the development of ascites, after which they were weighed daily. Humane endpoints were defined as:1.20 % loss of body weight.2.Very rough coat/fur, abnormal posture, enlarged pupils, deep wounds, major infection.3.Spontaneous vocalizations, self-mutilation, immobility, or no reaction upon touching.4.Ascites leading to 75 % weight gain from the time of inoculation (with a max allowed weight of 32 g).

Upon reaching the humane endpoint, the mice were sacrificed, and the ovarian tumors were harvested in RPMI-1640 (Gibco, cat# 52400041). The tumors were cut into small pieces, incubated with 10 U/mL collagenase I (Worthington, cat# CLSS-1), 400 U/mL collagenase (Worthington, cat# CLSS-4), 30 U/mL DNase I (Worthington, cat# DCLS), in RPMI-1640 in a ThermoMixer C (Eppendorf) (1500 rpm, 20 min, 37 °C). The suspension was filtered through a 70 µm nylon filter and centrifuged at 450g. If needed, the single-cell suspension was treated with ammonium-chloride–potassium (ACK) erythrocyte lysis buffer. The single cell suspension was then cultured in cell culture flasks with DMEM complete medium. 24 h post-culturing, floating cells were discarded, and fresh medium was added. Once 80–90 % confluence was reached, the adherent (cancer) cells were passaged, prepared for injection, or frozen (90 % FCS and 10 % DMSO) for future use.

Lentiviral transduction of P2 ID8 cells

P2 ID8 cells were obtained by in vivo passaging of the P1 ID8 cells. The P2 ID8 cells were lentivirally transduced to overexpress the Thy1.1 protein constitutively. The Thy1.1 plasmid (pLKO.3 Thy1.1, Addgene #14749) was expanded in E. coli cultured in LB medium supplemented with ampicillin (100 µg/mL, Duchefa Biochemie, cat# A0104.0025). For glycerol stocks, 500 µL bacterial culture was mixed with 500 µL of 50 % glycerol and stored at −80 °C. Bacterial pellets were obtained by centrifugation (1500 g, 10 min, 4 °C). Plasmid DNA was purified using the NucleoBond Xtra Midi Plus kit (Macherey-Nagel) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified plasmid DNA was eluted in 50–100 µL nuclease-free water and stored at − 20 °C. HEK293T cells were seeded in 10 cm cell culture dishes one day prior to transfection to reach 60–80 % confluency. Cells were transfected using the calcium phosphate method. Briefly, plasmid DNA (transfer vector:psPAX2:pMD2.G at a 4:2:1 ratio) was mixed with ddH2O and 62.5 µL of 2 M CaCl2, followed by dropwise addition to 500 µL 2 × HBSS with gentle vortexing. After a 15 min incubation at room temperature, the transfection mix was added dropwise to the HEK293T cells. The following day, the growth medium (DMEM complete) was replaced with fresh growth medium. Viral supernatants were harvested at 48 h and 72 h post-transfection, clarified by centrifugation, optionally filtered, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C. P2 ID8 cells were seeded in DMEM complete medium into 6-well cell culture plates to reach 50–60 % confluency. On the day of transduction, the medium was replaced, and cells were exposed to viral supernatant at different ratios (medium:viral supernatant = 1:1, 1:0.5, or 1:0.25). Transduced P2 ID8 Thy1.1 cells were stained using the Thy1.1-BB515 (BD Biosciences, cat# 564416) and 7-AAD (Biolegend, cat# 420404) and sorted to obtain Thy1.1+ 7-AAD- P2 ID8 Thy1.1 cells using the BD FACSAriaTM II.

Bioluminescence imaging

Non-invasive bioluminescence imaging (BLI) was used to image the ovarian tumors in vivo and ex vivo. Mice were anesthetized using isoflurane gas inhalation (flow rate of 2 L/min) and shaved on the dorsal side. 126 mg/kg D-Luciferin (Promega, cat# E1601) was administered subcutaneously and allowed to incubate for 20 min, followed by imaging at 30 s of exposure to quantify the viable tumor load. Image analysis was performed on the IVIS Spectrum Preclinical in vivo Imaging System (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) at the Molecular Small Animal Imaging Centre (moSAIC) at KU Leuven. The scan was performed 6 weeks post tumor inoculation.

Immunofluorescence of ID8 cell lines

Single cell suspensions of ID8 cell lines (up to two million cells per FACS tube) were resuspended in FACS buffer composed of HBSS (Gibco, cat# 14175129) supplemented with 2 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and 0.5 % (v/v) FCS, and stained with AXL-APC (1:20 dilution, eBioscience, cat#17–1084-82). The cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Foxp3 / Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (eBioscience, cat#00–5523-00) followed by staining with DAPI (1:5000 for 5 min). The stained cells were visualized using the EVOS M7000 Imaging System (ThermoFischer Scientific) at 40X magnification using the DAPI and Cy5 light cubes for DAPI and APC, respectively. Images were analyzed using ImageJ (Fiji).

Flow cytometry of cancer cell lines

Murine cancer cell line suspensions were stained with AXL-APC (1:20 dilution, eBioscience, cat# 17–1084-82) and human cancer cells were stained with AXL-PE (1:20 dilution, eBioscience, cat# 12–1087-42). After staining, the cells were spun down in FACS buffer and stained with 7-AAD (Biolegend, cat# 420404). Flow cytometry data were acquired using BD FACS Symphony A3 (BD Biosciences) and analyzed using FlowJo.

Elisa

Blood was collected from P3 ID8 Thy1.1 tumor-bearing and age-matched naïve mice via tail snip into RPMI-1640 (Gibco, cat# 52400041) from weeks 1–6 post inoculation and spun at 10,600 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The serum was carefully collected, avoiding contamination with red blood cells, and frozen at −20 °C until further use. Quantification of the serum concentration of GAS6 was performed using the Mouse GAS6 DuoSet ELISA kit (R&D systems, cat# DY986) according to manufacturer instructions.

Kaplan-Meier Plotter to assess the correlation between genes and patient outcome

“AXL”, “GAS6” or “AXL + GAS6” genes were entered into the “KM Plotter for pan-cancer” tool within the Kaplan-Meier Plotter (https://kmplot.com/analysis/) (Győrffy, 2024). For “AXL + GAS6”, the mean expression of the two genes was assessed. The analysis was performed on “ovarian cancer” with no restrictions on subtypes or cellular content. The auto best cutoff for splitting patients was used.

AXL expression in OC patients

The interactive webtool scDVA (https://ov.cancer-pku.cn/) (Zheng, 2023) was used for this analysis. HGSOC_All data was loaded. Using the “Heatmap” option, the expression of AXL and GAS6 was visualized by grouping based on “Chemotherapy” and “Tissue” criteria.

In vitro treatments

Tumor single cell suspension or in vitro cultured cell line pellets were resuspended in RPMI-1640 (Gibco, cat# 52400041) supplemented with 10 % (v/v) heat-inactivated FCS (Capricorn Scientific, cat# FBS-12A), (Sigma-Aldrich, cat# G8540), 100 units mL−1 penicillin and 100 μg mL−1 streptomycin (Gibco, cat# 15140122), 1 mM non-essential amino acids (Gibco, cat# 11140050), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco, cat#11360039), 0.5 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, M3148). 200.000 cells were plated per well in a 48 Well Suspension Culture Plate (Cellstar, Griener Bio-One, cat# 677102). The suspension was then supplemented with different treatments: 200 µg/mL AXL-Fc, 10 µM Olaparib, PBS, or DMSO. Human cell lines were also treated with Tilvestamab (20 µg/mL, Bio-Connect, cat# HY-P99161). The plates were maintained at 37 °C in the presence of 5 % CO2 for 24 h or 72 h, after which the cells were harvested, resuspended in HBSS (Gibco, cat# 14175129) containing BD Horizon Fixable Viability Stain 575 V (1:2000; BD Biosciences, cat# 565694) or Fixable Viability Dye eFluor780 (1:2000, eBioscience, cat# 65–0865-14) and incubated for 10 min at 37 °C. Next, cell suspensions were washed with and resuspended in FACS buffer constituting HBSS (Gibco, cat# 14175129) supplemented with 2 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and 0.5 % (v/v) FCS. Tumor single cell suspensions were pre-incubated with anti-CD16/CD32 antibody (clone 2.4G2) for 10 min on ice to prevent nonspecific antibody binding to Fcγ receptors (this step was not performed for cancer cell lines). The cell suspensions were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies diluted in FACS buffer for 30 min at 4 °C and then washed with the same buffer. Cells were incubated in 1 µL CellEvent™ Caspase-3/7 Detection Reagent (Invitrogen, cat# C10432) at 37 °C for 30 min. Flow cytometry antibodies used include CD45-BUV805 (BD Biosciences, cat# 568336), Thy1.1-AF647 (Biolegend, cat# 202508), Thy1.1-PE (Biolegend, cat# 202524), and Ki67-BV650 (BD Biosciences, cat# 563757).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v10.4.2 software.

Results

In vivo passaging of the murine ID8 cancer cell line results in faster cancer progression

Orthotopic inoculations with the murine ID8-fLuc cell line (firefly luciferase (Luc1)-transduced ID8 cells) resulted in rather slow tumor progression, with mice reaching the humane endpoint at day 111 post-inoculation (Fig. 1 A-B). Humane endpoint was defined as 75 % weight gain from the time of inoculation with a maximum allowed of 32 g, in addition to physical appearance and body condition scores. To achieve faster tumor progression, we performed in vivo passaging of the ID8-fLuc cell line. Ovarian tumors harvested from ID8-fLuc-bearing mice at humane endpoint were processed into a single cell suspension and cultured in vitro, generating one-time in vivo passaged ID8-fLuc cells (P1 ID8-fLuc, referred to as P1 ID8 henceforth) (Fig. 1C). Orthotopic inoculation of 1 x 106 P1 ID8 cells resulted in mice reaching the humane endpoint at day 50 post-inoculation (Fig. 1D). In vivo passaging of the P1 ID8 cell line was performed to achieve the P2 ID8-fLuc (referred to as P2 ID8 henceforth) cell line, which resulted in mice reaching the humane endpoint at day 46 post-inoculation (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, the P2 ID8 cell line was lentivirally transduced with Thy1.1 (P2 ID8-fLuc Thy1.1, referred to as P2 ID8 Thy1.1 henceforth). Thy1.1 is a non-immunogenic surface protein commonly used as a reporter marker owing to its distinctness from the Thy1.2 allele that is congenic in C57BL/6 mice (Rabinovich, 2008). As such, the Thy1.1 protein can be detected using a specific monoclonal antibody in applications such as flow cytometry or immunofluorescence (Rabinovich, 2008, Morris et al., 2005, Zhou, 2014, Hamouda, 2024) enabling cell tracking, monitoring of cell fate, assessment of tumor burden at both primary and distant metastatic sites, as well as evaluation of the efficacy of anti-cancer therapies. Orthotopic inoculation of 1 x 106 P2 ID8 Thy1.1 cells resulted in humane endpoint 61 days post-inoculation (Fig. 1F). Upon in vivo passaging of the P2 ID8 Thy1.1 cell line, the P3 ID8-fLuc Thy1.1 cells (referred to as P3 ID8 Thy1.1 henceforth) were obtained, which resulted in humane endpoint 42 days post-inoculation (Fig. 1G). To determine whether serum levels of GAS6, the ligand of AXL, alter with tumor progression, we collected serum from mice bearing orthotopic P3 ID8 Thy1.1 tumors from weeks 1–6 post-inoculation. Serum from age-matched naïve mice was collected at the same timepoints to evaluate potential differences in serum GAS6 levels between tumor-bearing and naïve mice. No significant differences in serum GAS6 levels were observed between tumor-bearing and naïve mice. However, in tumor-bearing mice, GAS6 levels at week 4 were significantly lower than at week 6 post-inoculation (p = 0.0161) (Fig. 3I). Overall, while AXL is highly expressed in all the different (in vivo passaged) murine ID8 cancer cell lines, serum GAS6 levels do not show significant changes between tumor-bearing and age-matched naïve mice.

Currently, AXL inhibition in OC is achieved with small-molecule inhibitors or antibody–drug conjugates. To evaluate an alternate approach to inhibit AXL, we used a nanobody (AXL-Fc) that blocks the AXL–GAS6 interaction in both mice and humans (Vandewalle, 2024). Ovarian tumors from mice inoculated orthotopically with P3 ID8 Thy1.1 were collected six weeks post-inoculation and processed to a single cell suspension and treated with AXL-Fc or PBS for 24 and 72 h (Fig. 5A). At each of the time points, the samples were collected and processed for flow cytometry to detect stages of cell death (Fig. 5B). 24 h post-treatment, no significant differences in the apoptosis stages or necrosis were found between Thy1.1+ cancer cells treated with AXL-Fc or PBS (Fig. 5 C-F). Interestingly, 72 h post-treatment, the percentage of Thy1.1+ necrotic cells was significantly higher in cells treated with AXL-Fc compared to their PBS-treated counterparts (p = 0.0099) (Fig. 5G). Axl-Fc synergizes with Olaparib in inhibiting cancer cell proliferation

To explore the potential combinations that could synergize with AXL-Fc, we tested the PARP inhibitor, Olaparib, since AXL inhibition has been previously reported to induce DNA damage and sensitize cells to PARP inhibition. Tumors from P3 ID8 Thy1.1 tumor-bearing mice (four weeks post-inoculation) were processed into a single cell suspension and cultured in vitro. The suspensions were treated with either AXL-Fc only, Olaparib only, a combination of AXL-Fc and Olaparib, PBS (vehicle for AXL-Fc), or DMSO (vehicle for Olaparib) (Fig. 6A). 72 h post-treatment, the cells were processed for flow cytometry to assess the potential inhibition of the different treatments on cancer cell proliferation (Fig. 6B). Interestingly, while AXL-Fc as monotherapy decreased the proliferation of Thy1.1+ cancer cells compared to PBS (p = 0.0469) and Olaparib alone (p = 0.0089), the combination of AXL-Fc and Olaparib further decreased (p < 0.0001) the proportion of proliferating cancer cells compared to either single treatment (Fig. 6C). Upon evaluating the percentage of necrotic Thy1.1+ cancer cells in P3 ID8 Thy1.1 tumor suspensions, we found that necrosis was primarily induced by Olaparib, with no significant additional effect observed from the combination of AXL-Fc + Olaparib (Fig. 6D). Next, we aimed to determine whether the effects of AXL-Fc depend on the level of AXL expression in cancer cells. For this, we measured AXL surface expression in six in vitro-cultured human OC cell lines (A2780, OVCAR3, OVCAR8, HEY, HEY-A8, and SKOV3), revealing the highest AXL expression in HEY-A8 and SKOV3 and the lowest in A2780 (Supplementary Fig. 1A). We then treated the A2780 (AXL-low) and SKOV3 (AXL-high) cells in vitro with AXL-Fc, Olaparib, or their combination. Additionally, to compare the effects of AXL-Fc with a commercially available anti-AXL antibody, we also included Tilvestamab (anti-AXL mAb) and Tilv + Olaparib. In both AXL-low and AXL-high cell lines, cancer cell proliferation was not significantly affected by any treatment (Supplementary Fig. 1B, D). In contrast to the findings from treated mouse ID8 tumor suspensions, cancer cell necrosis was primarily driven by Olaparib, and combining it with AXL-Fc or Tilvestamab did not produce synergistic effects (Supplementary Fig. 1C, E). Overall, AXL-inhibition had similar effects in both AXL-low and AXL-high cancer cells. Importantly, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the experiments with cultured human cell lines were performed on in vitro rather than in whole tumor suspensions. While these cancer cell lines have been reported to produce GAS6 (Buehler, 2013), other typical sources of GAS6 in the TME, such as immune and stromal cells, are absent in this setting, which may influence the observed results.

Altogether, our results demonstrate that in P3 ID8 Thy1.1 tumor suspensions, AXL-Fc inhibits cancer cell proliferation on its own, and its combination with Olaparib results in a synergistic effect, enhancing cancer cell growth inhibition.

Discussion

Owing to the late diagnosis and consequent high mortality associated with OC, the identification of novel drug targets and treatment strategies is critical. Moreover, conducting these investigations in preclinical models that are highly translatable to the human disease context is key. To address this clinical need, we have developed an orthotopic mouse model for OC that reaches humane endpoint 42 days post-inoculation. Currently available orthotopic OC mouse models progress rather slowly, resulting in end-stage between weeks 8–17 post-inoculation (Cook, 2023, Van Vloten, 2022, Dean et al., 2019, Dean, 2019, Rodriguez, 2022, Greenaway et al., 2008). To accelerate tumor progression and thereby enhance the feasibility of preclinical testing of novel therapies, we performed three successive in vivo passages of the syngeneic ID8-fLuc cell line to establish the P3 ID8 cell line. The ID8 cell line is currently the most widely used syngeneic model of epithelial OC. It was originally obtained through repeated in vitro passaging of mouse ovarian surface epithelial cells (MOSEC) from virgin, mature female C57BL/6 mice (Roby, 2000). When inoculated intraperitoneally, ID8 cells give rise to multiple peritoneal tumors and ascites (Roby, 2000). Our novel, rather fast-progressing syngeneic orthotopic tumor model closely mirrors both the pathology and kinetics of human OC, recapitulating disease progression from stages I-III. According to the current FIGO classification of OC, stage I is defined as tumor confinement to the ovaries or fallopian tubes; stage II involves one or both ovaries or fallopian tubes with pelvic extensions below the pelvic brim; and stage III is characterized by one or both ovaries with cytologically and histologically confirmed spread to the peritoneum outside the pelvis (Berek et al., 2021). Interestingly, based on anatomical observations, our orthotopic OC model reproduces these stages, underscoring its high translational relevance to patients.

Upon assessing surface expression level of AXL across in vivo-passaged ID8 cell lines, we observed that P1 ID8 exhibited significantly higher AXL levels compared to all other ID8-derived cell lines. This upregulation could be driven by intrinsic in vivo tumor-microenvironmental cues, such as hypoxia or GAS6 signaling (Rankin, 2014). Furthermore, the AXL expression in subsequent passages (P2 ID8, P2 ID8 Thy1.1, and P3 ID8 Thy1.1) dropped to levels comparable to parental ID8 and ID8-fLuc cells, suggesting the increase in AXL in P1 ID8 to be transient. Similar plasticity has been observed in NSCLC, where transition between AXL-positive to AXL-negative states occurs in a stochastic manner and is regulated by reversible heritable epigenetic mechanisms (Safaric Tepes, 2021). Alternatively, the reduction in the AXL expression in the P2 ID8, P2 ID8 Thy1.1, and later passages could reflect competitive expansion of fitter clones with higher metastatic potential but lower surface expression of AXL.

Analysis of serum GAS6 levels across tumor progression showed no significant differences between tumor-bearing and naïve mice overall. However, the serum GAS6 concentration at four weeks post-inoculation was significantly lower than at six weeks post-inoculation. Our anatomical observations indicate that between weeks 3–4 post-inoculation, cancer spread can occur from the inoculated ovary to the peritoneal cavity. During this rapid dissemination, high intra-tumoral GAS6 utilization may reduce its serum levels. After week 4 post-inoculation, serum GAS6 levels returned to baseline levels, potentially indicating the increased GAS6 production to support growing metastases. Alternatively, elevated levels of soluble GAS6 receptors, including AXL and MER, have been shown to correlate with reduced serum GAS6 by potentially acting as decoy receptors for GAS6 (Weinger et al., 2009, Cummings et al., 2013).

AXL is a target that has been long studied in cancer since its discovery in 1991 (O’Bryan, 1991). AXL inhibition has been shown to improve the chemosensitivity of OC to platinum and taxane-based chemotherapies (Quinn, 2019). AXL inhibition sensitizes OC (both homologous recombination proficient and deficient) to Carboplatin and Olaparib through increased DNA damage and replication (Yeo, 2023, Mullen, 2022, Balaji, 2017). Moreover, AXL plays a role in regulating the Warburg effect, whereby AXL inhibition resulted in decreased glycolysis in Cisplatin-resistant OC cells, which, in turn, enhanced the chemosensitivity of the cancer cells to Cisplatin (Tian, 2021). However, these studies have been predominantly performed either in vitro or using subcutaneous or intraperitoneal OC mouse models, which do not optimally replicate the human disease context. As such, here we used our newly generated orthotopic OC model to evaluate the therapeutic potential of a novel AXL-inhibitor modality, namely an AXL-Fc nanobody construct (Vandewalle, 2024). We observed that while 24 h was not sufficient to induce anti-cancer effects by AXL-Fc, 72 h post-treatment AXL-Fc significantly reduced OC cell proliferation and induced cancer cell necrosis. Interestingly, combining AXL-Fc with Olaparib resulted in a further decrease in the percentage of proliferating OC cells from tumor cell suspensions, demonstrating a synergistic effect.

In all, we highlight the use of a novel AXL-inhibitor modality that has not been previously investigated in OC. Although phase 3 trial of Batiraxcept has already shown positive outcomes in the PFS and OS of AXL-hi OC patients (Fuh, 2024), the multiple advantages of nanobodies hold great promise and warrant their further investigation in OC to complement or improve existing therapies.
