Irreversibility condition and stability of equilibria in the inverse-deformation approach to fracture
Arnav Gupta

Abstract
We derive the irreversibility condition in fracture for the inverse-deformation approach using the second law of thermodynamics. We consider the problem of brittle failure in an elastic bar previously solved in [ 29 ] . Despite the presence of a non-zero interfacial/surface energy, the third derivative of the inverse-deformation map is discontinuous at the crack faces. This is due to the presence of the inequality constraint ensuring the inverse strain is nonnegative and the orientation of matter is preserved. A change in the material location of a crack results in negative entropy production, violating the second law. Consequently, such changes are disallowed giving the irreversibility condition. The inequality constraint and the irreversibility condition limit the space of admissible variations. We prove necessary and sufficient conditions for local stability that incorporate these restrictions. Their numerical implementation shows that all broken equilibria found in [ 29 ] are locally stable.

Introduction

The problem of fracture in solids is difficult due to the presence of cracks as discontinuities in the deformation map. This issue is tackled using the inverse-deformation approach to fracture in
[
29
]
. The inverse deformation
h
maps the deformed configuration to a material reference. It is extendable to a continuous, piecewise smooth function even in the presence of cracks. A nonconvex constitutive model is introduced and regularized using the gradient of inverse strain. This regularization equips the model with a surface energy. The method is used to solve the problem of brittle failure in elastic bars. Failure under hard loading occurs due to an elastic instability at a critical load. Spontaneous nucleation of sharp cracks is predicted without using any damage/phase variables or pre-existing cracks. The stress in the bar drops to zero and the crack faces in the deformed configuration precisely delineate the material from empty space.

The nucleation and development of multiple cracks in coaxial composite bars is studied using the inverse-deformation method in
[
16
]
. Multiple cracks nucleate simultaneously in the brittle outer layer of such composites
[
25
]
. The outer layer continues to interact with the stiff inner core as the cracks open. In comparison to
[
29
]
, the issue of development of cracks is nontrivial in
[
16
]
. Between
[
29
,
16
]
, the issue of nucleation and development of cracks in 1D bars is solved using rigorous bifurcation methods. In
[
21
]
, the two problems are studied in the
\Gamma
-limit of the small parameter characterizing the inverse-strain gradient energy going to zero. In this limit, the crack surfaces are endowed with a constant surface energy, akin to the Griffith’s theory of fracture.

We address two new questions in this article. First, how is the notion of irreversibility in fracture resolved for the inverse-deformation approach? Second, can an elastic bar as in
[
29
]
being stretched incrementally in tension result into a broken solution with internal or multiple cracks?

The study of fracture via an energetic approach is attributed to the seminal work of Griffith
[
15
]
. In this work, the propagation of crack is due to a competition between the energy required in creating new surfaces and the bulk elastic energy built in a solid due to the application of external forces. This viewpoint became the bedrock of modern fracture mechanics and inspired various new works in the field. The notion of irreversibility is introduced ad-hoc in Griffith’s theory
[
27
]
. In modern terms, it is defined as follows: If at time
t
,
\Gamma(t)
is the set of all material points on a crack surface, then

\Gamma(s)\subseteq\Gamma(t)\text{ for all }s\leq t.

The common loading results of equilibrium cracks in the Griffith’s theory become unstable without this condition
[
27
]
. Irreversibility in the linear elastic fracture mechanics is incorporated by postulating pre-existing cracks that never shorten in length.

The physical intuition, and the necessity of an irreversibility condition in a model of fracture is clear. It is a fundamental building block for models proposed in the finite deformation fracture mechanics as well. The equilibrium equations in the finite deformation elasticity by incorporating Griffith’s surface energy are derived using variational methods in
[
24
,
31
]
. The authors incorporate irreversibility by limiting the admissible space. A broken configuration is considered admissible if the new surface of discontinuity contains or coincides with that of the previous state. Similar condition on irreversibility is also assumed in the works of Gurtin and Podio-Guidugli on fracture
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
.

More recently, the phase-field method provided a viable regularization to sharp-crack, fracture models and rejuvenated the field of both analytical and computational fracture mechanics. This method circumvents the issue of discontinuous deformations by relying on the degradation of the elastic stiffness resulting in diffused cracks. The irreversibility condition is added ad-hoc in the form of an evolution law
[
14
,
6
]
. It is assumed that the ‘Griffith-like’ potential goes to infinity if the crack set
\Gamma(t)
decreases
[
7
]
. In other works like
[
10
]
, the change in phase variable is
a priori
constrained to be unidirectional to incorporate irreversibility within the model.

The irreversibility condition is usually incorporated as a ad-hoc assumption in previous models. In contrast, we show that irreversibility is a consequence of the second law of thermodynamics in the inverse-deformation approach. We revisit the problem of failure in a elastic bar solved in
[
29
]
. For a broken solution, the inverse deformation and its first two derivatives are continuous but its third derivative is discontinuous at the crack faces. We study the effect of the motion of such discontinuities on the entropy of the system.

Entropy production due to the motion of discontinuities is well studied in the context of phase transitions
[
1
,
4
]
. In the absence of interfacial energy, a multiphase solution has discontinuities in strain. The motion of such discontinuities in the material configuration causes change in entropy of the system. Additional kinetic relations are postulated to ensure that the second law is satisfied and a unique solution path exists during a quasistatic motion of such materials.

Unlike in phase transitions, the third derivative
h^{\prime\prime\prime}
is discontinuous in the problem of fracture even in the presence of the higher-gradient energy. This is due to the inequality constraint
h^{\prime}\geq 0
that ensures the inverse strain is nonnegative and the orientation of matter is preserved. We show that the changes in the location of cracks in the material configuration decrease entropy and violate the second law of thermodynamics. The quasistatic motions that increase entropy are kinematically inadmissible. Consequently, the motion of the crack is restricted in the material reference and the entropy remains unchanged. The crack set
\Gamma(t)
contains the cracks set of all times prior to it, resulting in the irreversibility condition.

The inequality constraint and the irreversibility condition have profound consequences on the stability of equilibria. They limit the admissible space and make the issue of stability non trivial. We study the stability of the broken equilibria of the elastic bar. Their stability is also studied in
[
29
]
. The authors show that the solution with an end crack at either of the two ends of the bar is the only stable broken solution. They construct a variation in
h
for which the second variation of the total energy is strictly negative for all other broken solution
[
29
, Proposition 4.7]
. This variation does not satisfy the restrictions due to the inequality constraint and the irreversibility condition. Consequently, it is inadmissible. We correct the error and provide rigorous necessary and sufficient stability conditions that incorporate these restrictions.

We show that the variations that have non-zero derivative in the vacuum region
(h^{\prime}=0)
are either inadmissible or are directions of increasing energy. Barring them, the variations that are non-zero in this region violate the irreversibility condition. We are only required to check for variations with support in the unbroken region, i.e.
h^{\prime}>0
. We show that a broken solution
h
is stable if each of the unbroken parts of the bar are independently stable. An unbroken part is independently stable if the energy increases locally for all admissible variation in
h
with support restricted to that part. This result aligns with our physical intuition of the problem. Furthermore, an unbroken part with crack faces on both its ends can freely translate within the empty spaces created due to opening of cracks. At a given stretch, these translations result into a one-parameter family of equilibria that have the same total energy. If the second variations of the total energy is positive for all variations that satisfy aforementioned restrictions and are perpendicular to such translations in the
L^{2}
sense, we show that the solution is locally stable.

We implement this sufficient condition numerically using finite element methods and determine the stability of broken solutions found in
[
29
]
. We show that all broken solutions, including those with multiple cracks, are locally stable. The global minima appears in the first bifurcating branch with an end crack at one of the two ends of the bar and is energetically preferred, cf.
[
21
]
. The solutions with multiple cracks appear in the higher modes and are local minimizers. When an elastic bar is stretched incrementally, the bar may break and lock into any one of these minimizers, opening the possibility of internal or multiple cracks at failure.

We give an outline of the article here. In section
2
, we provide the formulation to the inverse-deformation approach to fracture and describe the problem solved in
[
29
]
. We prove that
h^{\prime\prime\prime}
is discontinuous at crack faces. We end the section by finding the work done on a part of the body by forces external to it. We use this in section
3
to calculate the entropy production in the system during a quasistatic motion and derive the irreversibility condition. In section
4
, we prove the necessary and sufficient second-order conditions for local stability. We determine the stability of broken solution using numerical methods in section
5
and find that all of them are stable. We end the article with some concluding remarks in section
6
.

2
The inverse-deformation approach to fracture

Consider a bar occupying the region
{\Omega}:=[0,1]
in the reference configuration. We fix one end of the bar and stretch the other to a length
\lambda
using a hard-loading device. The deformation map
f:{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfies the boundary conditions

f(0)=0,\quad f(1)=\lambda.

(2.1)

The stored-energy density function of the brittle material
W:(0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
satisfies the conditions
[
29
]

\displaystyle

\displaystyle W\in C^{2}(0,\infty);\,W(F)\nearrow\infty\text{ as }F\searrow 0;\,W(F)\nearrow\gamma>0\text{ as }F\nearrow\infty;

(2.2)

\displaystyle W(1)=0;\,W(F)>0\text{ for all }F\neq 1.

In contrast with standard 1D nonlinear elasticity, the function
W
is a Lennard-Jones type potential that asymptotes towards a constant
\gamma
as
F\nearrow\infty
. The total energy of the body is

E[\lambda,f]=\int_{0}^{1}W(f^{\prime}(x))\,dx.

(2.3)

Figure 1
:
The deformation
f
maps the reference
{\Omega}
onto the deformed configuration
{\Omega_{\ast}}
. The inverse deformation
h=f^{-1}
maps the deformed configuration
{\Omega_{\ast}}
onto the reference domain
{\Omega}
.

The deformed configuration is defined as
{\Omega_{\ast}}:=[0,\lambda]
. Let
h:{\Omega_{\ast}}\rightarrow{\Omega}
be the inverse deformation, as shown in Fig.
1
. We use Shield’s
[
30
]
change of variable
x=h(y),\,f^{\prime}=1/h^{\prime}
in (
2.3
) and redefine the total energy

E^{*}[\lambda,h]:=E[\lambda,h^{-1}]=\int_{0}^{\lambda}W^{*}(h^{\prime}(y))\,dy,

(2.4)

where
y\in{\Omega_{\ast}}
and
W^{*}(H)=HW(1/H)
. In the inverse-deformation approach, we seek minimizers
h
of (
2.4
) that satisfy the boundary conditions

h(0)=0,\qquad h(\lambda)=1.

(2.5)

We include the inequality constraint
h^{\prime}\geq 0
. This ensures the orientation of matter is preserved.

The inverse-deformation formulation is mathematically equivalent to the usual
forward
formulation where we seek minimizers
f
of (
2.3
). However, the inverse-deformation formulation is better suited to the problem of fracture
[
29
,
16
]
. Cracks in a 1D bar are characterized by jump discontinuity in the deformation map, viz.
F:=f^{\prime}\sim\infty
, rendering both analysis and numerical computation difficult. In contrast, cracks appear as zero slope region in the inverse deformation, i.e.
H:=h^{\prime}=0
. Even when the actual deformation
f
may be discontinuous due to failure, the inverse deformation
h
maintains continuity. Furthermore, the Lennard-Jones type potential
W
appears as a double-well potential in
W^{*}
with the two wells at
H=0
and
H=1

[
29
]
. This is similar to phase-transition problems, but here in terms of the inverse strain. The
H=1
well is the unbroken or ‘material phase’ while the
H=0
well represents empty space or the ‘vacuum phase’. Conservation of mass implies
H\rho=\rho^{*}
where
\rho
and
\rho^{*}
are mass density in the reference and deformed configurations respectively. Consequently, the region in the deformed configuration where
H=0
has zero mass.

The energy in (
2.4
) doesn’t penalize the creation of new surfaces, i.e. jump discontinuities in
f
. The minimizers
h
of (
2.4
) have arbitrary number of cracks and cannot sustain tension. This issue is resolved in
[
29
]
by introducing a higher-gradient energy in (
2.4
)

E^{*}_{\epsilon}[\lambda,h]=\int_{0}^{\lambda}\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}(h^{\prime\prime})^{2}+W^{*}(h^{\prime})\,dy,

(2.6)

where
\epsilon
is a small parameter. This higher-gradient energy endows the model with Griffith’s surface energy in the limit of
\epsilon\searrow 0
, cf.
[
21
]
. The problem reduces to solving for the minimizers of (
2.6
), where
h
satisfies the boundary conditions (
2.5
) and the unilateral constraint
h^{\prime}\geq 0
.

2.1
Smoothness of weak solutions

We define the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}:=H^{2}({\Omega_{\ast}},{\Omega})
. The admissible set is a closed convex cone defined as

\mathcal{K}=\{h\in\mathcal{H}:h(0)=0,\,h(1)=\lambda,\,h^{\prime}\geq 0\}.

(2.7)

Using the Sobolev embedding
\mathcal{H}\hookrightarrow C^{1}(0,\lambda)
, we characterize the inequality in (
2.7
) pointwise. For any
h\in\mathcal{K}
, we define the broken set

\mathcal{B}_{h}:=\{y\in[0,\lambda]:h^{\prime}(y)=0\},

(2.8)

and the good set

\mathcal{G}_{h}:=[0,\lambda]\backslash\mathcal{B}_{h}\equiv\{y\in[0,\lambda]:h^{\prime}(y)>0\}.

Let

implies that

\int_{0}^{\lambda}\left\{\epsilon^{2}h^{\prime\prime}(g^{\prime\prime}-h^{\prime\prime})+S^{*}(h^{\prime})(g^{\prime}-h^{\prime})\right\}\,dy\geq 0\text{ for all }g\in\mathcal{K},

(2.10)

where

We derive the Euler-Lagrange equation in

Work done by external forces

\left.\frac{d}{d\alpha}E[\lambda,f+\alpha\eta]\right|_{\alpha=0}=\int_{0}^{1}\epsilon^{2}\left\{\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime 5}}\eta^{\prime\prime}-\frac{5}{2}\left(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime 3}}\right)^{2}\eta^{\prime}\right\}+S(f^{\prime})\eta^{\prime}\,dx=0

\displaystyle\left[\epsilon^{2}\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime 5}}\eta^{\prime}\right]_{0}^{1}-\left[\left\{\epsilon^{2}\left(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime 5}}\right)^{\prime}+\frac{5}{2}\epsilon^{2}\left(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime 3}}\right)^{2}-S\right\}\eta\right]_{0}^{1}+

is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}=

\displaystyle\left.\epsilon^{2}\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime 5}}\right|_{x=x_{2}}\upsilon_{2}-\left.\left\{\epsilon^{2}\left(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime 5}}\right)^{\prime}+\frac{5}{2}\epsilon^{2}\left(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime 3}}\right)^{2}-S\right\}\right|_{x=x_{2}}\Delta_{2}

(2.28)

\displaystyle-\left.\epsilon^{2}\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime 5}}\right|_{x=x_{1}}\upsilon_{1}+\left.\left\{\epsilon^{2}\left(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime 5}}\right)^{\prime}+\frac{5}{2}\epsilon^{2}\left(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime 3}}\right)^{2}-S\right\}\right|_{x=x_{1}}\Delta_{1}

In terms of the inverse deformation, (
2.28
) reduces to

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}=

\displaystyle\left.\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime}H^{2}\right|_{y=f(x_{2})}\upsilon_{2}+\left.\left\{\epsilon^{2}\left(H^{\prime\prime}H-\frac{H^{\prime 2}}{2}\right)+W^{*}-HS^{*}\right\}\right|_{y=f(x_{2})}\Delta_{2}

(2.29)

\displaystyle-\left.\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime}H^{2}\right|_{y=f(x_{1})}\upsilon_{1}-\left.\left\{\epsilon^{2}\left(H^{\prime\prime}H-\frac{H^{\prime 2}}{2}\right)+W^{*}-HS^{*}\right\}\right|_{y=f(x_{1})}\Delta_{1}

where
H=h^{\prime}
. The Cauchy stress in the bar equals

\sigma=\epsilon^{2}\left(H^{\prime\prime}H-\frac{H^{\prime 2}}{2}\right)+W^{*}-HS^{*}

and is a constant due to the balance of linear momentum
[
29
, Equation 5.6]
. Hence, (
2.29
) simplifies to

\mathcal{W}=\left.\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime}H^{2}\right|_{y=f(x_{2})}\upsilon_{2}-\left.\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime}H^{2}\right|_{y=f(x_{1})}\upsilon_{1}+\sigma(\Delta_{2}-\Delta_{1}).

(2.30)

We use this expression to determine the entropy produced in the system during a quasistatic motion.

3
Entropy production during quasistatic motion

Consider a quasistatic, time-dependent family of equilibrium solutions
h(y,t):{\Omega_{\ast}}(t)\times[0,T]\rightarrow{\Omega}
, where

{\Omega_{\ast}}(t):=[0,\lambda(t)],\qquad h(0,t)=0,\qquad h(\lambda(t),t)=1.

The function
\lambda(t)
gives the stretch value at time
t
. No inertia effects are considered here.

Figure 2
:
The domain
\mathcal{D}_{*}(t):=[\Delta_{1}(t),\Delta_{2}(t)]\subset{\Omega_{\ast}}
with a simple crack face at
y=y_{c}(t)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{*}(t):=[\Delta_{1}(t),\Delta_{2}(t)]\subset{\Omega_{\ast}}
be a domain in the deformed configuration as shown in Fig.
2
. The point
y_{c}(t)\in\mathcal{D}_{*}(t)
is a crack face such that
h^{\prime}>0
for
y\in[\Delta_{1}(t),y_{c}(t))
and
h^{\prime}=0
for
y\in[y_{c}(t),\Delta_{2}(t)]
for all
t\in[0,T]
. The elastic energy of the material in
\mathcal{D}_{*}(t)
equals

U^{*}_{\epsilon}=\int_{\Delta_{1}(t)}^{\Delta_{2}(t)}\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}(H^{\prime})^{2}+W^{*}(H)\,dy.

(3.1)

where
(^{\prime})
represents partial derivative with respect to
y
and
H=h^{\prime}
.

The function
H^{\prime\prime}
has a jump discontinuity at
y=y_{c}(t)
, cf. Theorem
2.1
. Let
(\dot{\,})
represent partial derivative with respect to
t
. Using Leibniz’s integral rule, the rate of change of energy during the motion is

\displaystyle\dot{U}^{*}_{\epsilon}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Delta_{1}(t)}^{y_{c}(t)}\{\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime}v^{\prime\prime}+S^{*}v^{\prime}\}\,dy+\int_{y_{c}(t)}^{\Delta_{2}(t)}\{\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime}v^{\prime\prime}+S^{*}v^{\prime}\}\,dy

(3.2)

\displaystyle-\left\llbracket\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}H^{\prime 2}+W^{*}(H)\right\rrbracket\dot{y}_{c}

\displaystyle+\left.\left\{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}H^{\prime 2}+W^{*}(H)\right\}\right|_{y=\Delta_{2}(t)}\dot{\Delta}_{2}-\left.\left\{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}H^{\prime 2}+W^{*}(H)\right\}\right|_{y=\Delta_{1}(t)}\dot{\Delta}_{1},

where
v=\dot{h}
. Note that
\left\llbracket\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}H^{\prime 2}+W^{*}(H)\right\rrbracket\dot{y}_{c}=0
because
H^{\prime}
and
H
are continuous in
y
. We use integration by-parts and find

\displaystyle\dot{U}^{*}_{\epsilon}=

\displaystyle[\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime}v^{\prime}+S^{*}v]_{\Delta_{1}(t)}^{y_{c}(t)}+[\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime}v^{\prime}+S^{*}v]_{y_{c}(t)}^{\Delta_{2}(t)}

(3.3)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Delta_{1}(t)}^{y_{c}(t)}\{\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime\prime}v^{\prime}+{S^{*}}^{\prime}v\}\,dy-\int_{y_{c}(t)}^{\Delta_{2}(t)}\{\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime\prime}v^{\prime}+{S^{*}}^{\prime}v\}\,dy

\displaystyle+\left.\left\{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}H^{\prime 2}+W^{*}(H)\right\}\right|_{y=\Delta_{2}(t)}\dot{\Delta}_{2}-\left.\left\{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}H^{\prime 2}+W^{*}(H)\right\}\right|_{y=\Delta_{1}(t)}\dot{\Delta}_{1}.

Using integration by-parts once again yields

\displaystyle\dot{U}^{*}_{\epsilon}=

\displaystyle\llbracket\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime\prime}v\rrbracket+\int_{\Delta_{1}(t)}^{y_{c}(t)}\{\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime\prime}-{S^{*}}\}^{\prime}v\,dy+\int_{y_{c}(t)}^{\Delta_{2}(t)}\{\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime\prime}-{S^{*}}\}^{\prime}v\,dy

(3.4)

\displaystyle+\left.\left\{\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime}v^{\prime}+S^{*}v-\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime\prime}v+\left(\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}H^{\prime 2}+W^{*}(H)\right)\dot{\Delta}_{2}\right\}\right|_{y=\Delta_{2}(t)}

\displaystyle-\left.\left\{\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime}v^{\prime}+S^{*}v-\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime\prime}v+\left(\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}H^{\prime 2}+W^{*}(H)\right)\dot{\Delta}_{1}\right\}\right|_{y=\Delta_{1}(t)}.

The Euler-Langrange equation implies that
\{\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime\prime}-S^{*}\}^{\prime}=0
in
(\Delta_{1}(t),y_{c}(t))
. Furthermore,
\{\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime\prime}-S^{*}\}^{\prime}=0
in
(y_{c}(t),\Delta_{2}(t))
because here
H=0
.

We assume that
h
maps
\mathcal{D}_{*}(t)
to a fixed domain
[x_{1},x_{2}]\in\Omega
such that

h(\Delta_{1}(t),t)=x_{1},\qquad h(\Delta_{2}(t),t)=x_{2}.

(3.5)

Differentiating these conditions with respect to
t
gives

v(\Delta_{1}(t),t)=-H(\Delta_{1}(t),t)\dot{\Delta}_{1}(t),\qquad v(\Delta_{2}(t),t)=-H(\Delta_{2}(t),t)\dot{\Delta}_{2}(t).

(3.6)

We substitute (
3.6
) in (
3.4
) and get

\displaystyle\dot{U}^{*}_{\epsilon}=

\displaystyle\llbracket\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime\prime}v\rrbracket

(3.7)

\displaystyle+\left.\left\{\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime}(v^{\prime}+H^{\prime}\dot{\Delta}_{2})+\left(\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime\prime}H-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}H^{\prime 2}+W^{*}-S^{*}H\right)\dot{\Delta}_{2}\right\}\right|_{y=\Delta_{2}(t)}

\displaystyle-\left.\left\{\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime}(v^{\prime}+H^{\prime}\dot{\Delta}_{1})+\left(\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime\prime}H-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}H^{\prime 2}+W^{*}-S^{*}H\right)\dot{\Delta}_{1}\right\}\right|_{y=\Delta_{1}(t)},

The balance of linear momentum
[
29
, Equation 5.6]
implies that

\sigma=\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime\prime}H-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}H^{\prime 2}+W^{*}-S^{*}H,

(3.8)

where
\sigma
is the Cauchy Stress and is independent of
y
. Hence, (
3.7
) reduces to

\displaystyle\dot{U}^{*}_{\epsilon}=

\displaystyle\llbracket\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime\prime}v\rrbracket+\sigma(\Delta_{2}-\Delta_{1})

(3.9)

\displaystyle+\left.\left\{\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime}(v^{\prime}+H^{\prime}\dot{\Delta}_{2})\right\}\right|_{y=\Delta_{2}(t)}-\left.\left\{\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime}(v^{\prime}+H^{\prime}\dot{\Delta}_{1})\right\}\right|_{y=\Delta_{1}(t)},

We now determine the rate of work done by the forces external to the domain
\mathcal{D}
. Using (
2.30
)

P=\left.\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime}H^{2}\right|_{y=\Delta_{2}(t)}\frac{dF(x_{2},t)}{dt}-\left.\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime}H^{2}\right|_{y=\Delta_{1}(t)}\frac{dF(x_{1},t)}{dt}+\sigma(\Delta_{2}(t)-\Delta_{1}(t)).

(3.10)

where
F(x_{1},t)=1/H(\Delta_{1}(t),t)
and
F(x_{2},t)=1/H(\Delta_{2}(t),t)
. Differentiating these with respect to
t
yields

\frac{dF(x_{1},t)}{dt}=\left(\frac{1}{H(\Delta_{1},t)}\right)^{2}\left(v^{\prime}(\Delta_{1}(t),t)+H^{\prime}(\Delta_{1}(t),t)\dot{\Delta}_{1}\right),

(3.11)

and

\frac{dF(x_{2},t)}{dt}=\left(\frac{1}{H(\Delta_{2},t)}\right)^{2}\left(v^{\prime}(\Delta_{2}(t),t)+H^{\prime}(\Delta_{2}(t),t)\dot{\Delta}_{2}\right).

(3.12)

Substituting (
3.11
) and (
3.12
) in (
3.10
) gives

P=\left.\left\{\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime}(v^{\prime}+H^{\prime}\dot{\Delta}_{2})\right\}\right|_{y=\Delta_{2}(t)}-\left.\left\{\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime}(v^{\prime}+H^{\prime}\dot{\Delta}_{1})\right\}\right|_{y=\Delta_{1}(t)}+\sigma(\Delta_{2}-\Delta_{1}).

(3.13)

The rate of energy dissipation from the domain
\mathcal{D}
is

D=P-\dot{U}^{*}_{\epsilon}=-\llbracket\epsilon^{2}H^{\prime\prime}v\rrbracket\\

(3.14)

Let
x_{c}(t)=h(y_{c}(t),t)
be the location of the crack in the reference configuration. The
material velocity
of the crack is equal to

V=\dot{x}_{c}=v(y_{c}(t),t)+H(y_{c}(t),t)\dot{y}_{c}(t).

(3.15)

Since
V
and
y_{c}
are independent of
y
, (
3.14
) simplifies to

D=-\epsilon^{2}\llbracket H^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket V-\epsilon^{2}\llbracket H^{\prime\prime}H\rrbracket\dot{y}_{c}

(3.16)

From (
3.8
), we determine that
\llbracket H^{\prime\prime}H\rrbracket=0
. Hence,

D=-\epsilon^{2}\llbracket H^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket V=\phi V.

(3.17)

where
\phi=-\epsilon^{2}\llbracket H^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket
is the “force on an interface” or “force on a defect”
[
11
,
12
,
23
]
. It is necessary that the dissipation is nonegative during a quasistatic motion. This gives the condition

D=-\epsilon^{2}\llbracket H^{\prime\prime}\rrbracket V\geq 0.

(3.18)

This condition ensures that entropy production is positive at constant temperature, and is equivalent to the second law of thermodynamics
[
23
]
. Similar conditions are used in problems with discontinuous deformation gradient, such as in phase transitions, to determine
entropy admissibility criteria

[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. Depending on the sign of
\phi
for a given equilibria
h
, the direction of
V
is such that the inequality (
3.18
) is satisfied. Using (
2.18
) and (
3.18
), we determine that

\phi=\llbracket\mu\rrbracket,\qquad D=\llbracket\mu\rrbracket V\geq 0.

(3.19)

Since
\mu
is nonnegative, the sign of
\phi
, and subsequently
V
is easily determined. We show that (
3.19
) requires
V=0
. In the following two subsections we discuss cases of an end crack and an internal crack separately and determine this criteria of admissibility. These two cases cover all situations possible in the failure of the elastic bar.

3.1
End cracks

We consider the domain
[\Delta(t),\lambda(t)]\subset{\Omega_{\ast}}
, as shown in Fig.
3
. The point
y_{c}(t)
is the crack face of an end crack such that the region
[y_{c}(t),\lambda(t)]
is empty space. That is,
h^{\prime}(y)=0
for all
y\in[y_{c}(t),\lambda(t)]
. Since
\mu
is nonnegative and
\supp\mu\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{h}
, we establish that
\llbracket\mu\rrbracket>0
at
y=y_{c}(t)
. From (
3.19
),
V\geq 0
for entropic admissibility.

Figure 3
:
The domain
[\Delta_{1}(t),\lambda(t)]\subset{\Omega_{\ast}}
with a simple crack face at
y=y_{c}(t)
. The picture shows the deformation configuration of an end crack located at
x_{c}=1
.

Since
h(\lambda(t),t)=1
and
h^{\prime}(y)=0
for all
y\in[y_{c}(t),\lambda(t)]
, the location of crack
x_{c}(t):=h(y_{c}(t),t)
equals
1
. If
V>0
, then
x_{c}
increases beyond
1
and jumps out of
{\Omega}
. This is kinematically inadmissible. Hence, the only option possible is
V=0
.

Similarly, if the end crack was at
x_{c}=0
, the sign of the driving force
\phi
is switched and
V\leq 0
for entropic admissibility. However,
V<0
is kinematically inadmissible by the same argument. We conclude once again that
V=0
.

3.2
Internal cracks

Consider the domain
[\Delta_{1}(t),\Delta_{2}(t)]\subset{\Omega_{\ast}}
, as shown in Fig.
4
. The points
y_{1}(t)
and
y_{2}(t)
represent the two crack faces of an internal crack such that
h^{\prime}(y)=0
for all
y\in[y_{1}(t),y_{2}(t)]
. Let
\phi_{1}=\left.\llbracket\mu\rrbracket\right|_{y=y_{1}(t)}
and
\phi_{2}=\left.\llbracket\mu\rrbracket\right|_{y=y_{2}(t)}
be the driving forces at the two crack faces. Using the nature of
\mu
, we determine that
\phi_{1}>0
and
\phi_{2}<0
. From (
3.19
), the material velocities at the two crack faces satisfy
V_{1}\geq 0
and
V_{2}\leq 0
.

Figure 4
:
The domain
[\Delta_{1}(t),\lambda(t)]\subset{\Omega_{\ast}}
with a simple crack face at
y=y_{c}(t)
. The picture shows the deformation configuration of an internal crack located at
x=x_{c}
.

Since
h^{\prime}(y,t)=0
for all
y\in[y_{1}(t),y_{2}(t)]
, the crack faces map to the same material point, i.e.
h(y_{1}(t),t)=x_{c}(t)=h(y_{2}(t),t)
. This implies, the two material velocities satisfy
V_{1}=\dot{x}_{c}(t)=V_{2}
. This is true if and only if
V_{1}=V_{2}=0
. Therefore, the admissibility criterion in this case also requires
V=0
.

3.3
Irreversability condition

The material velocity
V=\dot{x}_{c}
describes the motion of the crack in the reference continua. The admissibility criterion
V=0
implies that if a crack nucleates at
x=x_{c}
in the reference domain, that breakage point remains the same throughout the motion. New cracks may nucleate during a motion but the
material
location of already nucleated cracks does not change. We define the crack set

\Gamma(t)=\{x\in[0,1]:x=h(y,t)\text{ for }y\in\mathcal{B}_{h}\}.

(3.20)

The entropic admissibility criterion implies that

\Gamma(s)\subseteq\Gamma(t)\text{ for all }s\leq t.

(3.21)

Condition (
3.21
) is the irreversibility condition. Due to the non-classical nature of the broken solution
h
and the presence of inequality constraint
h^{\prime}\geq 0
,
h^{\prime\prime\prime}
is discontinuous at the crack face. Motions of this discontinuity that result into negative dissipation are disallowed. Certain motions may result into positive dissipation. However, these motions are found to be kinematically inadmissible. As a result, the cracks remain stationary in the reference configuration, resulting in (
3.21
).

This condition is used to define the space of admissible variations. At a crack face,
H(y_{c}(t),t)=0
for all
t
. We substitute this in (
3.15
) and find

V(t)=v(y_{c}(t),t)=\left.\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}\right|_{y=y_{c}(t)}.

(3.22)

The value of
h
at
y_{c}
does not change due to the irreversibility condition
V=0
. The space of admissible variations at
h
is

\mathcal{V}_{h}:=\{\eta\in H^{2}(0,\lambda)\cap H^{1}_{o}(0,\lambda):\eta^{\prime}(y)\geq 0\text{ for all }y\in\mathcal{B}_{h},\,\eta(y_{c})=0\text{ for all }y_{c}\in\mathcal{C}_{h}\}.

(3.23)

The condition
\eta(y_{c})=0\text{ for all }y_{c}\in\mathcal{C}_{h}
excludes variations that change the location of a crack in the reference domain. This has many consequences in the analysis of stability of equilibria. We discuss them in detail in the following section.

4
Stability of quasistatic equilibria

A solution
h\in\mathcal{K}
is locally stable if it is a local minimizer, i.e. there exists an open, convex set
U\subset\mathcal{K}
such that
h\in U
and
E^{*}_{\epsilon}[\lambda,g]\geq E^{*}_{\epsilon}[\lambda,h]
for all
g\in U
. In this section, we prove the necessary and sufficient conditions for the stability of a quasistatic equilibria. The total energy
E^{*}_{\epsilon}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
in (
2.6
) at a given value of
\lambda
is twice Fréchet differentiable such that,

\delta^{2}E_{\epsilon}^{*}[\lambda,h;\eta,\eta]=\int_{0}^{\lambda}\{\epsilon^{2}(\eta^{\prime\prime})^{2}+\mathcal{M}^{*}(h^{\prime})(\eta^{\prime})^{2}\}\,dy\text{ for }\eta\in\mathcal{V}_{h},

(4.1)

where
\mathcal{M}^{*}=\frac{dS^{*}}{dH}
.

Theorem 4.1

(Necessary condition)
.

Let
h\in\mathcal{K}
be a local minimizer satisfying (
2.11
). Then for all
\eta\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
such that
\eta^{\prime}=0
in
\mathcal{B}_{h}
, it follows that
\delta^{2}E_{\epsilon}^{*}[\lambda,h;\eta,\eta]\geq 0

Proof.

Let
\eta\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
such that
\eta^{\prime}=0
in
\mathcal{B}_{h}
. And let there exist
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that
h+\alpha\eta\in U
, implying

E^{*}_{\epsilon}[\lambda,h+\alpha\eta]\geq E^{*}_{\epsilon}[\lambda,h]

(4.2)

. The definition of Fréchet differentiability
[
32
]
gives

E^{*}_{\epsilon}[\lambda,h+\alpha\eta]-E^{*}_{\epsilon}[\lambda,h]=\alpha\delta E^{*}_{\epsilon}[\lambda,h;\eta]+\alpha^{2}\delta^{2}E^{*}_{\epsilon}[\lambda,h;\eta,\eta]+o(||\alpha\eta||^{2})

(4.3)

Since
h
satisfy (
2.11
),
\delta E^{*}_{\epsilon}[\lambda,h;\eta]=\int_{\mathcal{B}_{h}}\eta^{\prime}\,d\mu=0
. Therefore, (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) mean
\delta^{2}E_{\epsilon}^{*}[\lambda,h;\eta,\eta]\geq 0
for sufficiently small
\alpha
.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
h\in\mathcal{K}
be a solution of (
2.11
) at stretch length
\lambda
. If
\delta^{2}E_{\epsilon}^{*}[\lambda,h;\eta,\eta]>0
for all
\eta\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
that satisfy

(A)

\eta^{\prime}(y)=0
for all
y\in\mathcal{B}_{h}
,

(B)

\eta(y)=0
for all
y\in\mathcal{B}_{h}
,

then
h
is locally stable.

Proof.

We first consider variations
\eta\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
with
\eta^{\prime}>0
in some
B\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{h}
. Using (
2.11
), the first variation of the energy
\delta E_{\epsilon}^{*}[\lambda,h;\eta]=\int_{\mathcal{B}_{h}}\eta^{\prime}d\mu>0
. Substituting this in (
4.3
) for sufficiently small
\alpha
gives
E^{*}_{\epsilon}[\lambda,h+\alpha\eta]>E^{*}_{\epsilon}[\lambda,h]
locally for all such
\eta
irrespective of the sign of
\delta^{2}E^{*}_{\epsilon}[\lambda,h;\eta,\eta]
.

The admissible variations that remain in
\mathcal{V}_{h}
satisfy the condition
(A)
. For these
\eta
,
\delta E_{\epsilon}^{*}[\lambda,h;\eta]=0
and the sign of
\delta^{2}E^{*}_{\epsilon}[\lambda,h;\eta,\eta]
determines stability. Condition
(B)
is a consequence of the irreversibility condition. For all
\eta\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
,
\eta(y_{c})=0\text{ for all }y_{c}\in\mathcal{C}_{h}
. In conjunction with condition
(A)
, this gives condition
(B)
. Let
\delta^{2}E_{\epsilon}^{*}[\lambda,h;\eta,\eta]>0
for all
\eta\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
that satisfy conditions
(A)
and
(B)
. Then using (
4.3
),
E^{*}_{\epsilon}[\lambda,h+\alpha\eta]>E^{*}_{\epsilon}[\lambda,h]
for sufficiently small
\alpha
. We conclude that the solution
h
is locally stable.
∎

Although the admissible space is
\mathcal{K}
and admissible variations are in
\mathcal{V}_{h}
, we show that second-order condition is satisfied by a minimizer in a smaller space of variations that satisfy conditions
(A)
and
(B)
. We define this space as

\mathcal{S}_{h}:=\{\eta\in\mathcal{V}_{h}:\,\eta\text{ satisfies conditions \ref{A} and \ref{B}}\}.

(4.4)

The conditions
(A)
and
(B)
have further physical interpretations. Cracks in a broken equilibria
h
result to multiple unbroken parts that are separated by empty space from each other. Theorem
4.2
implies that an equilibrium
h
is stable if each of these unbroken parts are independently stable. We show this in the following corollary:

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
h\in\mathcal{K}
satisfy (
2.11
) and
{G_{i}}:=(r_{i},s_{i}),\,i=1,\dots,N
be open, disjoint, connected subsets of
\mathcal{G}_{h}
such that
r_{i},\,s_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{h}\cup\{0,\,\lambda\}
. Furthermore,
\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}G_{i}=\mathcal{G}_{h}
. If
\delta^{2}E_{\epsilon}^{*}[\lambda,h;\eta_{i},\eta_{i}]>0
for all
\eta_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{h}
,
\supp\eta_{i}\subseteq G_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, then
h
is locally stable.

Proof.

Let
{G_{i}}:=(r_{i},s_{i})\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{h}
as defined in the statement and
\eta\in\mathcal{S}_{h}
. The second variation in (
4.1
) equals

\displaystyle\delta^{2}E_{\epsilon}^{*}[\lambda,h;\eta,\eta]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{G}_{h}}\{\epsilon^{2}(\eta^{\prime\prime})^{2}+\mathcal{M}^{*}(h^{\prime})(\eta^{\prime})^{2}\}\,dy+\int_{\mathcal{B}_{h}}\epsilon^{2}(\eta^{\prime\prime})^{2}\,dy,

(4.5)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{G_{i}}\{\epsilon^{2}(\eta^{\prime\prime})^{2}+\mathcal{M}^{*}(h^{\prime})(\eta^{\prime})^{2}\}\,dy

(4.6)

Let
\eta_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{h}
such that
\supp{\eta_{i}}\subseteq G_{i},\,\eta_{i}=\eta
in
G_{i}
. The equation (
4.5
) further simplifies to

\delta^{2}E_{\epsilon}^{*}[\lambda,h;\eta,\eta]\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{G_{i}}\{\epsilon^{2}(\eta^{\prime\prime})^{2}+\mathcal{M}^{*}(h^{\prime})(\eta^{\prime})^{2}\}\,dy=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{G_{i}}\{\epsilon^{2}(\eta_{i}^{\prime\prime})^{2}+\mathcal{M}^{*}(h^{\prime})(\eta_{i}^{\prime})^{2}\}\,dy=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta^{2}E_{\epsilon}^{*}[\lambda,h;\eta_{i},\eta_{i}]

(4.7)

Let
\delta^{2}E_{\epsilon}^{*}[\lambda,h;\eta_{i},\eta_{i}]>0
for all
\eta_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{h}
,
\supp\eta_{i}\subseteq G_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, then
\delta^{2}E_{\epsilon}^{*}[\lambda,h;\eta,\eta]>0
for all
\eta\in\mathcal{S}_{h}
. Theorem
4.2
gives
h
is locally stable.
∎

The subsets
G_{i}
defined in Corollary
4.1
are the unbroken parts of
h
separated by empty spaces. If the second variation in (
4.1
) is positive for all variations restricted to an unbroken part, we say that this part is independently stable. Corollary
4.1

6
Concluding remarks

The irreversibility condition in the inverse-deformation approach to fracture is derived using the second law of thermodynamics. We consider the problem of failure of an elastic bar solved in
[
29
]
. We show that the third derivative of the inverse-deformation map
h^{\prime\prime\prime}

h^{\prime}\geq 0

We revisit the issue of stability of broken equilibria found in
[
29
]
. We show that the variations that may result in loss of stability are limited by the inequality constraint
h^{\prime}\geq 0
and the irreversibility condition. The support of such variations is restricted to the unbroken region
\mathcal{G}_{h}
of the bar. We show that a bar is stable if each of its unbroken parts are independently stable. We also show that the unbroken regions in a solution with a crack face at both of its ends are free to translate within the empty space created by opening of cracks. This results in one-parameter families of equilibria that have the same total energy. We prove necessary and sufficient second-order conditions for the local stability of equilibria. We implement the sufficient condition using a finite element method and show that all broken solutions obtained in
[
29
]
are locally stable. During incremental loading, an elastic bar fails and locks into one of the minimizers. The broken solutions in a
n\geq 2
bifurcation branch result in internal or multiple cracks in the bar.
