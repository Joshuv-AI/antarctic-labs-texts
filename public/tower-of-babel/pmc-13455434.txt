Sacral CSF-venous fistula: a rare entity. Illustrative case
Authors: Alexander Mastrolonardo, Annika R. Mascarenhas, Keith MacDougall, Prateek Malik, Michael Mayich, Sachin Pandey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDCSF-venous fistula (CVF) is an increasingly recognized cause of CSF leakage resulting in spontaneous intracranial hypotension (SIH). CVFs are most commonly identified in the thoracolumbar spine and are rarely reported in the sacral region.OBSERVATIONSA man in his 60s presented with symptoms consistent with SIH. CT of the brain demonstrated bilateral subdural hematomas and a decreased pontomesencephalic angle. Spinal MRI showed degenerative changes without evidence of a CSF leak. Lateral decubitus CT myelography (CTM) with delayed imaging identified a sacral CVF at the L5–S1 level, which was confirmed with an adjusted technique within digital subtraction myelography (DSM). A spinal CSF-venous fistula (CVF) constitutes an abnormal anatomical communication between the subarachnoid space and adjacent epidural veins, permitting CSF to drain into the venous system.1 This pathological shunt leads to CSF hypovolemia and manifests clinically as spontaneous intracranial hypotension (SIH), typically presenting with orthostatic headaches, tinnitus, and cognitive fog. Digital subtraction myelography (DSM), especially when performed in the lateral decubitus position, is considered the gold standard for the detection of CVFs.1,2

Although increasingly recognized, sacral CVFs remain a rare entity and can be easily overlooked without an intentionally expanded DSM field of view. In this report, we describe the case of a patient with severe SIH secondary to a sacral CVF, underscoring the critical need for a meticulous and comprehensive diagnostic approach in cases with high clinical suspicion. By sharing our diagnostic strategy, we aim to expand awareness and provide guidance for clinicians managing similar challenging presentations.

Illustrative Case

We present the case of a 65-year-old male with no significant past medical history who was admitted to a regional hospital for the evaluation and symptomatic management of suspected intracranial hypotension. The patient initially exhibited a progressive holocephalic headache that worsened over several days, culminating in what he described as the most severe headache of his life. The headache was pulsatile, rated 10 of 10 in intensity, and notably exacerbated by standing.

On initial assessment, neurological examination revealed a patient who was alert and oriented with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15. He was in no acute distress, with intact cranial nerve function, no pronator drift, and normal motor and sensory findings. Noncontrast CT imaging of the head showed bilateral subdural hematomas and a decreased pontomesencephalic angle, findings highly suggestive of intracranial hypotension (Fig. 1). Spinal MRI demonstrated diffuse degenerative changes but no focal lesions indicative of a dural tear or CSF leak. Based on this clinical picture, an initial blind epidural blood patch was performed, providing transient relief lasting approximately 6–7 hours before symptoms recurred. Given the recurrence, he was transferred to our institution for further assessment and management.

CT myelography (CTM) revealed multiple perineural cysts at the T2, T6, T11, and T12 levels with no contrast extravasation outside the thecal sac. Notably, anterior osteophytes were noted at the T3–4, T4–5, T8–9, and T12–L1 levels, whereas posterior osteophytes were absent. Further investigation with left lateral decubitus DSM via lumbar puncture at L5–S1 yielded an opening pressure of 5 cm H2O. The thecal sac was pressurized with 10 mL of normal saline, followed by 5 mL of Omnipaque 300 contrast, and a 3-mL normal saline flush. An approximately 8° Trendelenburg tilt was applied to promote cranial contrast flow.

Fluoroscopic subtracted imaging was acquired from the upper thoracic through lower lumbar spine as contrast coursed along the left thecal margin. Subsequently, CTM was performed from the tentorium to the coccyx at 15-minute and 60-minute delays. At 15 minutes, early opacification of the renal collecting system further raised suspicion for a CVF (Fig. 1). Subtle linear contrast tracking was observed at the left L5–S1 foramen, although not definitive. A repeat DSM/CT study in the right lateral decubitus position was negative, but again suggested faint left-sided opacification (Fig. 2). Ultimately, a subsequent DSM/CTM via L2–3 puncture in the reverse Trendelenburg position demonstrated branching linear contrast extending ventrally and dorsally from the left S1 neural foramen, confirming a left sacral CVF (Fig. 3). The patient’s case was deliberated at the local multidisciplinary neurovascular case conference, where both transvenous and open surgical ligation options were evaluated. Open surgery would have necessitated microsurgical dissection around the S1 nerve root sleeve and potentially involved manipulation of the dorsal root ganglion and exiting nerve root, carrying risks of neuropathic pain, neurological deficits, CSF leakage, and wound complications. Ultimately, transvenous endovascular embolization was favored due to its reduced morbidity and its capacity to directly target the venous egress abnormality, providing a higher likelihood of technical success and symptom resolution. After comprehensive review of the imaging findings, the patient underwent pelvic and lumbosacral venography followed by transvenous embolization of the fistula using Onyx-18 via right femoral vein access. Catheterization of the inferior vena cava and navigation into the left external iliac vein allowed access to the S1–2 foramen and successful embolization of the fistula (Fig. 4). FIG. 4.Procedural anteroposterior (A and C) and lateral (B and D) fluoroscopic images obtained before (A and B) and after (C and D) transvenous Onyx-18 embolization of the left S1 CVF via transvenous femoral vein catheterization.

The patient was kept flat until the morning after his procedure, and on gradual mobilization there were no new neurological deficits. Within a few days of treatment, there was a dramatic improvement in symptoms. At 6 weeks postdischarge, he described only intermittent mild headaches with no ongoing analgesia requirements. He was seen once more at 6 months postprocedure with complete resolution of his symptoms, and repeat CT confirming marked improvement of the subdural collections (Fig. 5). Discussion

Observations

SIH is a debilitating yet potentially reversible disorder most commonly attributed to a dural defect with direct CSF leakage. In a subset of patients, however, routine spinal imaging fails to identify an extradural CSF collection, necessitating consideration of alternative mechanisms of CSF loss. CVFs have emerged as an important and increasingly recognized cause of SIH in this context, although their diagnosis remains challenging due to their frequent invisibility on conventional imaging studies. The eventual finding of sacral CVF on DSM in our patient confirmed this etiology, despite its atypical location outside the thoracic or lumbar region.

CVFs represent an abnormal conduit between the subarachnoid space and adjacent epidural veins or paraspinal veins, permitting unregulated drainage of CSF into the venous system and resulting in CSF hypovolemia with the characteristic clinical manifestations of SIH. These classic symptoms include orthostatic headaches, tinnitus, brain fog, etc.1,2 Most CVFs are spontaneous, without a prior history of trauma, and commonly occur at meningeal diverticula, which are thought to be incidental “blisters” of the dura.3 Reported CVFs occur most frequently in the thoracic spine.3,4 Fistulas at lower levels are uncommon but have been documented and reported in the lower cervical and upper lumbar regions (T12–L2).1,5 Aljuboori et al. described a symptomatic L2 fistula successfully treated with transvenous embolization.1 The sacrum may be an underrecognized site of CVF formation, suggesting that a significant subset of these patients may remain undiagnosed.6 In 2022, Mark et al. identified the first sacral CVF (L5, S1, S2), highlighting the limitations of standard imaging protocols and underscoring the need for targeted, delayed decubitus CTM to increase the detection yield of these lesions.7 Since this initial observation, there have not been any further reports of sacral CVFs.

In this patient, the absence of extradural CSF on spine MRI appropriately lessened the likelihood of a direct dural leak and redirected diagnostic attention toward venous CSF loss. Spine MRI and conventional CTM typically fail to show this extradural CSF collection, since CSF drains directly into veins.8 The diagnostic inflection point is critical, as the identification of CVFs requires an ongoing high degree of suspicion and needs dynamic intrathecal imaging techniques. DSM, particularly when performed in the lateral decubitus position, remains the diagnostic gold standard. DSM involves injecting iodinated contrast into the thecal sac and using fluoroscopy to watch for rapid contrast entry into a venous channel. A positive study will show contrast leaking from a nerve root sleeve into paraspinal veins or early filling into the renal collecting system.8 Dynamic CTM with the patient in the prone or decubitus position is an alternative. It provides high-resolution cross-sectional detail and can also localize CVFs. Newer techniques can provide improved detection; for example, photon counting detector CTM has identified CVFs missed on conventional myelography.6 Respiratory maneuvers during myelography have also been tested, such as resisted inspiration (deep-breath hold against resistance) or the Valsalva maneuver, which can transiently raise spinal CSF pressure and improve fistula localization.8 In one study, resisted inspiration yielded the clearest fistula image in approximately 63% of cases. Additional modalities (adjuncts) include MR myelography with intrathecal gadolinium and prone CT.9 In practice, centers may sequentially use CTM and DSM with maneuvers to ensure maximal sensitivity.

Our case adds to this very limited body of literature surrounding sacral CVFs and highlights the importance of maintaining clinical suspicion, ensuring adequate sacral coverage during dynamic myelography, and recognizing transvenous embolization as a safe, minimally invasive, and effective therapy for sacral CVFs—a rare, but clinically significant and possibly overlooked cause of SIH. Once diagnosed, transvenous embolization has emerged as an effective and minimally invasive treatment strategy for CVFs. By directly occluding the venous outflow pathway, this approach addresses the underlying pathophysiology and has been associated with rapid clinical improvement. Although long-term outcome data remain limited due to the overall low number of reported cases, available evidence, including the present case, suggests durable symptom relief with transvenous therapies.

Lessons

SIH is a debilitating but often treatable condition, most commonly resulting from a dural defect; however, in a subset of patients, routine imaging fails to identify the source of CSF loss. This case highlights sacral CVF as a rare but clinically significant cause of SIH that may be overlooked without sustained diagnostic vigilance. When suspicion for a CVF persists despite negative routine studies, targeted evaluation of the lumbosacral junction and sacrum is warranted. Accurate interrogation of this region requires technical modifications beyond standard thoracolumbar protocols, including higher lumbar puncture sites, reverse Trendelenburg positioning, delayed image acquisition, and meticulous sacral coverage. Recognition of this entity is critical, as transvenous embolization offers an effective minimally invasive treatment with the potential for rapid clinical improvement.
