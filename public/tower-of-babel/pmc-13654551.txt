Central Message

Patients with severe mitral regurgitation in the setting of left ventricular dysfunction pose a challenge for surgical intervention. A comprehensive approach to perioperative management can lead to successful clinical outcomes.

See Commentary on page XXX.

Functional mitral regurgitation (MR) is a common lesion present in patients with both ischemic and nonischemic cardiomyopathies. Occasionally, untreated structural mitral valve (MV) regurgitation (MR) can lead to left ventricular (LV) dilatation and dysfunction. Once a significant LV dysfunction has developed (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF] <40%), operative risk increases substantially. Perhaps more importantly, the combination of LV dilation and severe LV dysfunction in the setting of significant MR can pose a prohibitive risk for surgical intervention.1 This review explores the indications for MV intervention for functional MR with a focus on pre-, intra-, and postoperative management. As this is a review article with no patient-level data, institutional research board approval was not obtained nor required. Consent was not required.

Patient Selection

There are many considerations that factor into a decision to offer MV surgery in a patient with significant LV dysfunction. The presence of significant comorbidities, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, renal insufficiency, and peripheral vascular disease, may greatly increase the perioperative risk of surgery. Renal insufficiency is particularly troublesome, as careful volume management postoperatively may be impaired by the inability to offer renal replacement therapy due to prohibitive hypotension.

One must also consider the alternative surgical options available to a patient. If the patient is a transplant candidate and the LV has significantly dilated (>6.5 cm in end-diastole), they may be better served by proceeding directly to transplant. In transplant-ineligible patients, one may choose to offer a destination therapy ventricular assist device (DT-VAD) as an alternative to conventional MV surgery.

Sadly, in transplant-ineligible patients, similar comorbidities may preclude either DT-VAD or mitral surgery. These include significant RV dysfunction, which may be present even in the absence of significant pulmonary hypertension. In addition, the need for concomitant procedures, such as coronary artery bypass grafting or aortic valve replacement, may render a patient at a prohibitive risk for conventional surgical intervention. Lastly, patients who remain on inotropic therapy or on temporary MCS likely represent a prohibitive risk for conventional surgical intervention.

A multidisciplinary approach involving heart failure cardiology and experienced heart failure surgeons is recommended to determine the best surgical strategy for each individual patient. These may include referral for percutaneous therapies, such as transcatheter edge-to-edge repair, in patients deemed unsuitable for conventional surgical intervention.

Preoperative Phase

Patients with severe MR and LV dysfunction may present with acute decompensated heart failure. Even in patients with chronic symptoms of heart failure, a period of preoperative optimization can significantly improve clinical outcomes. These patients are susceptible to secondary organ dysfunction, including pulmonary edema, cardiorenal syndrome, hepatic congestion, and systemic malperfusion, all of which bestow additional perioperative risk. Therefore, the primary preoperative goals are to optimize cardiac output, rehabilitate secondary organ dysfunction, and achieve euvolemia, as these patients are often significantly volume-overloaded. Secondary tricuspid regurgitation may also resolve with adequate preoperative diuresis. In patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy, achieving euvolemia may result in a significant reduction in the degree of MR. Similarly, a period of guideline-directed medical therapy with afterload reduction can improve both LV function and the degree of residual MR. Improving severe MR to the mild-to-moderate range has implications for intraoperative planning, as previous studies have demonstrated no benefit from concomitant MV repair during surgical revascularization in patients with only moderate functional MR.2

In contrast, patients who demonstrate persistent severe MR despite preoperative optimization require mitral intervention.

In the perioperative period, achieving adequate forward cardiac output requires careful optimization of both RV and LV performance while minimizing LV afterload. Despite declining popularity, invasive hemodynamic monitoring with pulmonary artery catheters can be invaluable for titrating pharmacological support to achieve this fine balance. A subset of patients develops type II pulmonary arterial hypertension and accompanying right ventricular (RV) dysfunction secondary to elevated left atrial pressures. Inodilators such as milrinone and dobutamine have combined inotropic and vasodilatory effects, which augment contractility while facilitating afterload reduction. However, they may exacerbate the reduced systemic vascular resistance that accompanies general anesthesia, thereby precipitating hypotension and coronary malperfusion, risking further ventricular dysfunction.3 In patients with RV dysfunction or elevated pulmonary vascular resistance, the selective use of inhaled milrinone may provide additional hemodynamic benefit, particularly when administered prior to cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB).4 Emerging evidence indicates that this administration route represents a promising therapeutic strategy for the prevention of RV decompensation following CPB, particularly in patients at high risk for postoperative RV failure. The combined application of systemic inodilators and inhaled pulmonary-selective therapy thus facilitates comprehensive perioperative hemodynamic optimization, addressing both left- and right-sided ventricular functions.4,5

The use of preoperative mechanical circulatory support (MCS) remains controversial, despite a recently published meta-analysis demonstrating the benefit of a prophylactic intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP).6 Even more controversial is the pre-emptive use of temporary mechanical support (tMCS) employing microaxial devices (ie, Impella). A recent expert consensus document from the European Association of Cardiothoracic Surgery describes the rationale, indications, and selection of tMCS devices to prevent perioperative low–cardiac output syndrome in high-risk patients undergoing cardiac surgery.7

Intraoperative Considerations

Once the patient has been medically optimized, several operative considerations must be weighed to develop an evidence-based surgical plan that maximizes the likelihood of a successful and durable intervention.

Published evidence clearly shows that not all patients with functional MR benefit from valvular intervention at the time of revascularization surgery. As previously discussed, in the National Institute of Health (NIH-sponsored) Cardiothoracic Surgery Network (CTSNet) trial, patients with mild-to-moderate MR did not demonstrate any survival benefit or significant LV remodeling compared with patients who underwent isolated coronary artery bypass grafting.2

However, in patients with severe MR, surgical intervention is indicated. In the companion CTSNet trial for patients with severe MR, formal MV replacement with preservation of the subvalvular apparatus had comparable mortality to MV repair, but was associated with a much lower rate of recurrent MR at 2 years.8 Furthermore, the high recurrence rate of MR in the repair group was clinically significant, leading to a much higher rate of rehospitalization for heart failure.

Similarly, in the Surgical Treatment of Ischemic Heart Failure trial, significant MR adversely impacted long-term survival.9,10 In patients with significant MR, failure to intervene on the MV led to lower survival as early as 5 years. Interestingly, performing an LV remodeling procedure partially mitigated the impact of MR. However, the combination of an LV remodeling procedure and MV repair significantly increased operative mortality.10

Adjunctive techniques to simple mitral annuloplasty have been proposed to reduce the recurrence of functional MR after repair. These include the division of secondary chordae, papillary muscle approximation, and/or resuspension.11, 12, 13, 14 Kron and colleagues also highlighted the presence of inferobasal aneurysms (see Figure 1) as a predictor of repair failure and advocated MV replacement with preservation of the subvalvular apparatus in patients with this anatomic feature.15 Chan and colleagues16 have reported that a leaflet tethering angle of >22° led to repair failure. A very detailed and comprehensive meta-analysis of repair techniques for secondary mitral insufficiency has shown that tailoring adjunctive repair techniques to the severity of leaflet dysfunction leads to better outcomes with respect to recurrent MR. However, this analysis also concluded that no repair technique was associated with satisfactory outcomes in patients with stage 4 disease and advanced heart failure.18

The holistic management of ischemic MR encompasses optimal medical therapy, revascularization, surgical repair or replacement, and cardiac resynchronization therapy. These interventions promote enhanced regional myocardial contractility, facilitate reverse LV remodeling, and restore LV electrical and mechanical synchrony.19

Dyssynchronous ventricular contraction represents a significant perioperative challenge, particularly during separation from CPB. Ventricular dyssynchrony can exacerbate pre-existing LV dysfunction and adversely impact MV geometry by distorting the subvalvular apparatus, displacing papillary muscles, and restricting leaflet motion. Pacing-based interventions, including atrio-biventricular pacing, have been proposed to address both the mechanical and valvular consequences of interventricular dyssynchrony. Consistent with the cardiology literature, cardiac resynchronization therapy has been shown to restore temporal coordination of ventricular contraction, improve interpapillary muscle synchrony, and reduce the severity of functional MR.20,21 In the perioperative setting, initiation of atrio-biventricular pacing following CPB has been associated with enhanced cardiac output compared with single-chamber atrial or ventricular pacing. These observations underscore the importance of early recognition and targeted management of ventricular dyssynchrony, complementing pharmacologic and mechanical strategies to optimize perioperative hemodynamics and mitigate postoperative LV dysfunction.21

In patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy, complete revascularization, where possible, is highly recommended. Preoperative viability assessments may guide regions to target; however, in our experience, even areas with minimal viability may benefit from revascularization.

Similarly, addressing concomitant lesions such as aortic and tricuspid valve disease is important. While the management of non–severe tricuspid regurgitation remains controversial in the surgical literature, we recommend surgical repair of severe tricuspid regurgitation. Significant aortic stenosis should also be addressed and can sometimes relieve functional MR once the outflow obstruction has been removed.

More complicated is the management of moderate aortic regurgitation. In addition to the impaired myocardial protection via antegrade delivery of cardioplegia, moderate aortic regurgitation may worsen after correction of the MR and the reduction in LVEDP. In these situations, we often reassess aortic regurgitation after achieving full CPB as this mimics the hemodynamic state following MV intervention.

In patients with chronic atrial fibrillation, we recommend ligation of the left atrial appendage. The decision to proceed with a surgical Maze procedure is usually dependent on the surgeon’s experience and the need for other concomitant interventions that may prolong the CPB run.

In summary, the decision to proceed with concomitant interventions is a balance between the perceived benefit and the risk of adding time and complexity to an already high-risk intervention.

Weaning From CPB

Weaning from CPB requires careful coordination between anesthesia, perfusion, and the surgical team. Hemostasis is essential as patients with marginal systolic function will not tolerate the hypotension associated with ongoing blood loss. Metabolic and hematologic parameters should be optimized prior to weaning. Atrio-biventricular pacing should be considered as an adjunct to pharmacotherapy, particularly if a patient is bradycardic and requires temporary pacing. Transesophageal echocardiography is useful for detecting new regional wall-motion abnormalities that may suggest injury to the circumflex artery or other areas of potential ischemia.22

During CPB, vasoplegia commonly occurs in patients who present for surgery after exposure to amiodarone, ACE inhibitors, or the newer angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors such as Entresto. Institution of vasopressors such as noradrenaline and vasopressin may be required throughout CPB to achieve a MAP of 60 to 80 mm Hg and should be continued when weaning from CPB. A weak heart tolerates transient hypertension better than RV malperfusion. Similarly, inotropic medications such as adrenaline, dobutamine, and/or milrinone should be initiated early, often coincident with cross-clamp release. In Europe, levosimendan is commonly utilized, although a randomized trial failed to show benefit.23 Inhaled nitric oxide or prostacyclin is also a commonly employed adjunct to improve RV hemodynamics.24 Their pulmonary selectivity enables targeted vasodilation within the pulmonary circulation, minimizing systemic hypotension and ventilation-perfusion mismatch. Consequently, inhaled pulmonary vasodilators improve RV performance without compromising systemic hemodynamics.25

When pharmacotherapy is insufficient, tMCS with an IABP can provide adjunctive benefit by simultaneously reducing afterload and increasing coronary perfusion. The IABP remains the most common form of tMCS. Although primarily designed to augment LV function, secondary improvements in RV performance may result from enhanced coronary perfusion and LV unloading. The modest improvement in cardiac output achievable with IABP support may be sufficient for patients with moderate ventricular dysfunction (ie, LVEF 35%-45%).

However, if LV or RV function is severely reduced and unable to support hemodynamics despite maximum inotropic and IABP support, the surgical team should consider advanced tMCS devices that better augment cardiac output.

The choice of tMCS is driven by price, availability, institutional factors, anatomical features, and surgical preference. Devices in contemporary practice include veno-arterial (VA) extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), Centrimag (Abbott Laboratories), TandemHeart (Livanova), and the Impella family of microaxial pumps (Johnson & Johnson MedTech).E1

In centers where ECMO requires 24/7 perfusion support, it is less ideal than a percutaneous or paracorporeal ventricular assist device (VAD), which can usually be managed bedside by a critical care nurse. There are several advantages and disadvantages of ECMO support. First, it provides biventricular support when isolated RV/LV support is inadequate. Second, it supports oxygenation if pulmonary edema is a concern. Third, it can be initiated percutaneously via the femoral or axillary vessels to permit bedside decannulation. The disadvantages include high complication rates, particularly limb ischemia with femoral cannulation. We advocate for distal perfusion cannulas to mitigate the risk of critical limb ischemia. In addition, peripheral VA-ECMO significantly increases LV afterload, increasing myocardial work, prolonging recovery, and often mandating a venting strategy. Finally, ECMO is also associated with high rates of hemolysis and is usually only tolerated for 5 to 7 days in adult patients before significant complications arise.E2

When more durable support is anticipated, we prefer the Centrimag device, which can also be configured to provide univentricular or biventricular support with the option of an inline oxygenator.E3 In contrast to ECMO, we have frequently supported patients with central Centrimag cannulation for weeks, enabling ambulation and physiologic recovery. However, central (nonpercutaneous) devices require redo sternotomy for decannulation and/or transition to a durable device or transplant. In patients who have undergone MV surgery, we recommend apical LV cannulation to preserve full flow across the MV. Left atrial cannulation risks thrombosis of the mitral prosthesis (especially mechanical valves) and predisposes to thrombus formation in the LV cavity, which may preclude decannulation after recovery.

More recently, the Impella 5.5 device is emerging as a favored device for isolated LV failure.E4 The advantages of this device include direct aortic or axillary cannulation, low-dose anticoagulation, and less hemolysis, even after prolonged support. Federal drug agency approval is for 14 days, although several publications describe safe and effective support for longer durations. Right-sided percutaneous devices, such as the Impella RP and the Protek Duo, are also commonly used. However, unlike ECMO and Centrimag, the Impella device cannot accommodate an oxygenator. Regardless of the MCS device chosen, one must consider the anticipated duration of support and the eventual exit strategy (withdrawal of care, explant for recovery, bridge to a durable device, or transplant).

Immediate Postoperative Management

The early period in the intensive care unit can be critical for a successful surgical outcome. Careful volume management is essential to prevent inadvertent positive fluid balance. Persistent chest-tube output should prompt early re-exploration, as resuscitative transfusion can impair RV function. Abrupt cessation of chest-tube output in the context of a rising central venous pressure, lactatemia, and diminishing urine output may indicate tamponade, warranting early exploration. A low threshold for exploration often fails to reveal significant pathology, but negative re-explorations are better tolerated than prolonged hemorrhage or tamponade.

Ventilation should minimize RV afterload by avoiding excessive PEEP, large tidal volumes, or hypercapnia, all of which increase pulmonary vascular resistance. Similarly, early transition to spontaneous ventilation and extubation will often benefit RV performance. Inhaled pulmonary vasodilators such as inhaled nitric oxide should be weaned slowly to prevent rebound pulmonary hypertension and RV dysfunction.

As blood pressure rises with waking from sedation, we prefer to wean vasopressors first and then inotropes slowly after extubation.

If, despite diuresis, urine output is insufficient to achieve negative daily balance after 72 hours, early renal replacement therapy may be necessary.

For patients dependent on tMCS, myocardial recovery should be evaluated regularly with echocardiography and ramp studies. Lack of myocardial recovery at 48 hours warrants a multidisciplinary discussion regarding possible exit strategies, including candidacy for transplant or durable MCS (ie, left ventricular assist device [LVAD]).

Long-Term Outcomes

Conventional cardiac surgery can yield acceptable outcomes in select patients with end-stage heart failure, with long-term survival rates comparable to those achieved with advanced therapies such as heart transplantation or LVAD implantation. This is particularly relevant in patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy, for whom surgical interventions, including MV repair or replacement and coronary artery bypass grafting, should be strongly considered before proceeding to advanced therapies.

In our retrospective study, in both unadjusted and adjusted models, survival after high-risk conventional surgery in transplant-eligible patients was comparable to heart-transplant/bridge-to-transplant VAD survival.E5 In the heart transplant–ineligible subset, the high-risk surgery group had comparable survival to destination LVAD patients.

Thalji and colleagues also reviewed their institutional experience with high-risk conventional surgery.E6 They found that patients with prior cardiac surgery, New York Heart Association functional class IV symptoms, and age ≥70 years had poor long-term survival, suggesting that these patients may be best served by durable LVAD support.

Summary

Careful preoperative patient selection and optimization are essential for successful management of patients with MV pathology and LV dysfunction. Attention to anesthetic management, surgical techniques, and early postoperative support can lead to acceptable long-term outcomes, even in this challenging patient population.
