Bias in estimating Theil, Atkinson, and dispersion indices for gamma mixture populations
Jackson Assis, Roberto Vila ∗ and Helton Saulo

Abstract
. In this paper, we derive closed-form expressions for the bias of estimators of the Theil, Atkinson, and dispersion indices when the underlying population follows a finite mixture of gamma distributions. Our methodology builds on probabilistic techniques grounded in Mosimann’s proportion-sum independence theorem and the gamma–Dirichlet connection, enabling analytical tractability in the presence of population heterogeneity. These results extend existing findings for single gamma models.

1.
Introduction

Theil and Atkinson are examples of ratio-based indices commonly used in the analysis of inequality data in a variety of disciplines, including economics, environmental science, and sustainability; see
ConceicaoGalbraith2000
,
DU20151080
,
SUN2015751
, and
Malakar2018
.

Estimation of these ratio-based indices is asymptotically consistent if the numerator and denominator are estimated consistently due to the continuous mapping theorem; see
shih2025
.
However, in practice, it is challenging to derive the finite-sample properties (e.g., bias) of a ratio-based estimator because it is constructed from two distinct estimators. Recently,
VilaSaulo2025
derived closed-form expressions for the bias of the Theil and Atkinson index estimators when the population follows a gamma distribution with fixed scale parameter. The authors employed techniques presented by
Baydil2025
, such as the Laplace transform of the distribution, which facilitates handling ratios of random functions, and Karamata’s identity, which helps in dealing with inverse functions.

Although the work of
VilaSaulo2025
has advanced on the study of the properties of the Theil and Atkinson index estimators, limitations are encountered as a single gamma distribution may not be adequate as inequality-related data is often heterogeneous, consisting of multiple populations; see the case of mixture gamma models for modeling income data studied by
Chotikapanich2008
.

In this context, we examine the estimation of the Theil, Atkinson, and the dispersion indices in gamma mixture populations, a more general class of distributions that accounts for heterogeneity in the data. In addition, we derive analytical expressions for the biases of the estimators of Theil, Atkinson, and the dispersion indices. To overcome the limitation associated with the derivation the finite-sample properties of these ratio-based estimators, we rely on the gamma-Dirichlet relationship, which establishes a connection between sums of independent gamma-distributed variables and the corresponding vector of proportions, whose distribution follows a generalized Dirichlet. This approach allows moments of ratios involving gamma sums to be expressed as moments of functions of Dirichlet vectors, which are more analytically tractable. A similar strategy was adopted by
shih2025
when studying the biases of the Gini index and the dispersion in gamma populations.

The rest of this paper unfolds as follows. In Section
2
, we present some preliminary results and definitions. In Section
3
, we derive expressions for the Theil, Atkinson, and dispersion indices based on gamma mixture populations. In Section
4
, we focus on deriving analytical expressions for the biases of the estimators of the Theil, Atkinson, and dispersion indices. Finally, in
5
, we provide concluding remarks.

2.
Preliminary results and some definitions

Definition 1

(Mixture of gammas)
.

A random variable
X
follows a gamma mixture distribution with parameter vector
\bm{\theta}=(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{m},\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m},\lambda)^{\top}
, denoted by
X\sim\text{GM}(\bm{\theta})
, if its probability density function is given by

(1)

f_{X}(x;\bm{\theta})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}f_{Z_{j}}(x;\alpha_{j},\lambda),\quad x>0,

where
m\in\mathbb{N}
is the number of components,
\pi_{j}
are the mixing proportions, with
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}=1
and
\pi_{j}>0
. Moreover,
f_{Z_{j}}(x;\alpha_{j},\lambda)
is the density of the gamma-distributed random variable
Z_{j}
with shape parameter
\alpha_{j}>0
and rate
\lambda>0
, denoted by
Z_{j}\sim\text{Gamma}(\alpha_{j},\lambda)
, that is,

f_{Z_{j}}(x;\alpha_{j},\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{\alpha_{j}}}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j})}\,x^{\alpha_{j}-1}e^{-\lambda x},\quad x>0,

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
denotes the (complete) gamma function.

Figure 1 of
VilaSaulomixture2025
illustrates how the gamma mixture distribution acts as a flexible framework that includes numerous important special cases.

Remark 1
.

Note that for
m=2
, with
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\lambda)^{\top}
and
\bm{\theta}^{\prime\prime}=(\pi_{2},\pi_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1},\lambda)^{\top}
, we have
f(x;\bm{\theta}^{\prime})=f(x;\bm{\theta}^{\prime\prime})
for all
x>0
. In other words, the distribution of gamma mixtures is not identifiable. This lack of identifiability may lead to unstable parameter estimates, convergence issues in estimation algorithms, multimodality in the likelihood function, and misleading interpretations regarding the mixture components’ structure.
To address these challenges, authors such as
mclachlan2000finite
and
fruhwirth2006finite
propose strategies like imposing parameter constraints, adopting Bayesian approaches, and applying relabeling methods to realign the samples generated by MCMC algorithms and ensure more consistent inference.

It is clear that
X
in Definition
1
has the following representation

(2)

\displaystyle X=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathds{1}_{\{Y=j\}}Z_{j},

where
Y\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
is a discrete random variable with
\mathbb{P}(Y=j)=\pi_{j}
, independent of
Z_{j}\sim{\rm Gamma}(\alpha_{j},\lambda)
, for each
j=1,\ldots,m
, and
\mathds{1}_{A}
is the indicator function of the event
A
.

Since the sequence of events
\{\{Y=j\}:j=1,\ldots,m\}
is a partition of the sample space, applying the Law of total probability and the independence of
Y
and
Z_{j}
, the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of
X
can be written as

\displaystyle F_{X}(x;\bm{\theta})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}F_{Z_{j}}(x;\alpha_{j},\lambda).

As a consequence, the following result is obtained:

Proposition 1
.

If
X\sim\text{GM}(\bm{\theta})
, then, for any Borel-measurable function
h
we have

\mathbb{E}[h(X)]=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\mathbb{E}[h(Z_{j})],

where

Z_{j}\sim{\rm Gamma}(\alpha_{j},\lambda)
,
j=1,\ldots,m
.

Proposition 2
.

If
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
is a random sample (i.i.d.) of size
n
from
X\sim\text{GM}(\bm{\theta})
, then, for any Borel-measurable function
h
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[h\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)\right]=\sum_{1\leqslant j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\leqslant m}\pi_{j_{1}}\cdots\pi_{j_{n}}\>\mathbb{E}\left[h\left(Z^{*}_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}\right)\right],

where
Z^{*}_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}\sim{\rm Gamma}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}},\lambda)
.

Proof.

The proof is immediate since, by the i.i.d. nature of the variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, for any Borel-measurable function
h
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[h\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)\right]=\int_{(0,\infty)^{n}}h\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right)f_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}}(\bm{x}){\rm d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leqslant j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{n}\leqslant m}\pi_{j_{1}}\cdots\pi_{j_{n}}\int_{(0,\infty)^{n}}h\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right)f_{Z_{j_{1}}}(x_{1})\cdots f_{Z_{j_{n}}}(x_{n}){\rm d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leqslant j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{n}\leqslant m}\pi_{j_{1}}\cdots\pi_{j_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[h\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{j_{i}}\right)\right],

where
Z_{j_{1}},\ldots,Z_{j_{n}}
are independent gamma random variables with parameter vectors
(\alpha_{j_{1}},\lambda)^{\top},\ldots,(\alpha_{j_{n}},\lambda)^{\top}
, respectively. By defining
Z^{*}_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{j_{i}}
, the proof follows.
∎

Proposition 3
.

If
X\sim\text{GM}(\bm{\theta})
, then its
p
-th moment is given by

\mathbb{E}[X^{p}]={1\over\lambda^{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\,{\Gamma(p+\alpha_{j})\over\Gamma(\alpha_{j})},\quad p>-\alpha_{(1)},

where
\alpha_{(1)}=\min\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\}
and
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the gamma function.

In particular, we have

\displaystyle\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j},

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}=\text{Var}(X)=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\alpha_{j}+1)-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}\right)^{2}\right].

Proof.

The proof follows directly from Proposition
1
with
h(x)=x^{p}
plus the fact that
Z_{j}\sim{\rm Gamma}(\alpha_{j},\lambda)
. ∎

In Statistics, Mosimann’s proportion-sum independence theorem
(
Mosimann1962
)
is a key result in the study of proportions, especially in relation to the Dirichlet distribution.

Theorem 1

(
Mosimann1962
)
.

Let
\{Y_{j}:j=1,\ldots,n\}

be
n
positive and mutually independent random variables. Define the random variable
D_{j}=Y_{j}/\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}
for
j=1,\ldots,n
. Then,
each
D_{j}
is distributed independently of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}
iff
(D_{1},\ldots,D_{n})^{\top}\sim\text{Dirichlet}(\bm{a})
on
\{(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})^{\top}:d_{j}>0,1\leqslant j\leqslant n,\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}=1\}
.

The next technical result will be crucial for the derivation of estimator biases presented in Section
4
.

Proposition 4
.

For
(D_{1},\ldots,D_{n})^{\top}\sim\text{Dirichlet}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
, we have

(3)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}D_{j}^{c_{j}}\right)^{r}\log\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}D_{j}^{c_{j}}\right)\right]=\frac{\Gamma(\sum_{l=1}^{n}\alpha_{l})}{\Gamma\bigl(\sum_{l=1}^{n}(\alpha_{l}+rc_{l})\bigr)}\\[5.69046pt]
\times\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+rc_{j})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j})}\left[c_{j}\psi(\alpha_{j}+rc_{j})-\sum_{l=1}^{n}c_{l}\psi\left(\sum_{l=1}^{n}(\alpha_{l}+rc_{l})\right)\right],

where
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\geqslant 0
,
r\geqslant 0
and
\psi(\cdot)
denotes the digamma function.

Proof.

By using the identity

x^{r}\log(x)=\lim_{p\to 0}{\partial x^{p+r}\over\partial p},\quad x>0,\ r\geqslant 0,

we can write

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}D_{j}^{c_{j}}\right)^{r}\log\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}D_{j}^{c_{j}}\right)\right]=\lim_{p\to 0}{\partial\over\partial p}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{n}D_{j}^{(p+r)c_{j}}\right],

where the application of the dominated convergence theorem permitted the exchange of the order of expectation, limit, and differentiation.
Applying the well-established formula for the mixed moment of
(D_{1},\ldots,D_{n})^{\top}

(
Kai2011
, see Item (2.5) of)
:

(5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{n}D_{j}^{d_{j}}\right]=\frac{\Gamma(\sum_{l=1}^{n}\alpha_{l})}{\Gamma\bigl(\sum_{l=1}^{n}(\alpha_{l}+d_{l})\bigr)}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+d_{j})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j})},

the expectation on the right-hand side of (
4
) can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{n}D_{j}^{(p+r)c_{j}}\right]=\frac{\Gamma(\sum_{l=1}^{n}\alpha_{l})}{\Gamma\bigl(\sum_{l=1}^{n}(\alpha_{l}+(p+r)c_{l})\bigr)}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+(p+r)c_{j})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j})}.

Consequently, the corresponding partial derivative with respect to
p
yields:

(6)

{\partial\over\partial p}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{n}D_{j}^{(p+r)c_{j}}\right]=\frac{\Gamma(\sum_{l=1}^{n}\alpha_{l})}{\Gamma\bigl(\sum_{l=1}^{n}(\alpha_{l}+(p+r)c_{l})\bigr)}\\[5.69046pt]
\times\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+(p+r)c_{j})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j})}\left[c_{j}\psi(\alpha_{j}+(p+r)c_{j})-\sum_{l=1}^{n}c_{l}\psi\left(\sum_{l=1}^{n}(\alpha_{l}+(p+r)c_{l})\right)\right].

Letting
p\to 0
in (
6
) and substituting into (
4
) completes the proof of Identity (
3
). ∎

3.
Theil, Atkinson and dispersion indices

3.1.
Theil index

The Theil (population) index is a statistical measure of economic inequality, quantifying the divergence from a perfectly equal distribution of income.

Definition 2
.

The Theil
T
index
(
Theil1967
)
of a random variable
X
with finite mean
\mathbb{E}[X]=\mu
is defined as

\displaystyle T_{T}={\mathbb{E}\left[{X\over\mu}\,\log\left({X\over\mu}\right)\right]},

and the Theil
L
index is defined as

\displaystyle T_{L}=\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left({\frac{\mu}{X}}\right)\right].

Proposition 5
.

The Theil
T
index of
X\sim\text{GM}(\bm{\theta})
is given by

T_{T}=\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}\psi(\alpha_{j})+1\right]-\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}\right),

where

\psi(\cdot)
denotes the digamma function.

Proof.

Note that
T_{T}
in Definition
2
can be written as

(7)

\displaystyle T_{T}=\frac{1}{\mu}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[X\log(X)\bigr]-\log(\mu).

From Proposition
1
we have

(8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X\log(X)]=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\mathbb{E}[Z_{j}\log(Z_{j})],

where

Z_{j}\sim{\rm Gamma}(\alpha_{j},\lambda)
, for each
j=1,\ldots,m
.
As
\mathbb{E}[Z\log(Z)]=(a/b)[\psi(a)+1/a-\log(b)
for
Z\sim{\rm Gamma}(a,b)
, and
\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}/{\lambda}
(Proposition
3
), by combining the above Identity (
8
) with (
7
), the proof follows. ∎

Proposition 6
.

The Theil
L
index of
X\sim\text{GM}(\bm{\theta})
is given by

T_{L}=\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}\right)-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\psi(\alpha_{j}),

where
\psi(\cdot)
is the digamma function.

Proof.

As
T_{L}=\log(\mu)-\mathbb{E}[\log(X)]
,
by applying Proposition
1
we have

(9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\log(X)]=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\mathbb{E}[\log(Z_{j})],

where

Z_{j}\sim{\rm Gamma}(\alpha_{j},\lambda)
, for each
j=1,\ldots,m
. As
\mathbb{E}[\log(Z)]=\psi(a)-\log(b)
for
Z\sim{\rm Gamma}(a,b)
, and

\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}/{\lambda}
(Proposition
3
), from the above identities the proof readily follows.
∎

Remark 2
.

Note that, unlike
VilaSaulo2025

T_{L}
as a function of
T_{T}
, since the digamma function is non-linear.

3.2.
Atkinson index

The Atkinson (population) index
(
Atkinson1970
)
of a random variable
X
with finite mean
\mathbb{E}[X]=\mu
is defined as

(10)

\displaystyle A(\varepsilon)=1-\frac{\mathbb{E}^{{1}/{(1-\varepsilon)}}[X^{1-\varepsilon}]}{\mu},\quad 0\leqslant\varepsilon\neq 1.

In this subsection we derive explicit forms for the Atkinson index in the special cases
\varepsilon\to 1
and
\varepsilon\to\infty
with
X\sim\text{GM}(\bm{\theta})
.

Proposition 7
.

When
\varepsilon\to 1
, the Atkinson index of
X\sim\text{GM}(\bm{\theta})
is given by

A(1)\equiv\lim_{\varepsilon\to 1}A(\varepsilon)=1-\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}}\exp\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\psi(\alpha_{j})\right\},

where
\psi(\cdot)
is the digamma function.

Proof.

Simple calculations show that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 1}\mathbb{E}^{1/(1-\varepsilon)}[X^{1-\varepsilon}]

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{\lim_{\varepsilon\to 1}\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\log\left(\mathbb{E}[X^{1-\varepsilon}]\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\lim_{\varepsilon\to 1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}\log\left(\mathbb{E}[X^{1-\varepsilon}]\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\lim_{\varepsilon\to 1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}[X^{1-\varepsilon}]\right\}

(11)

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{\mathbb{E}[\log(X)]\right\},

where in the second equality we used L’Hôpital’s rule, and in the fourth equality the identity

\frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}x^{1-\varepsilon}=-x^{1-\varepsilon}\log(x),\quad x>0.

Consequently, combining (
10
) with (
11
), we obtain

A(1)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 1}A(\varepsilon)=1-\frac{\exp\left\{\mathbb{E}[\log(X)]\right\}}{\mu}.

Since, by (
9
),
\mathbb{E}[\log(X)]=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\left[\psi(\alpha_{j})-\log(\lambda)\right]
and
\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}/\lambda
(Proposition
3
), the proof follows.
∎

Proposition 8
.

When
\varepsilon\to\infty
, the Atkinson index of
X\sim\text{GM}(\bm{\theta})
is given by

A(\infty){\equiv}\lim_{\varepsilon\to\infty}A(\varepsilon)=1.

Proof.

Note that the Atkinson index (
10
) can be written as

\displaystyle A(\varepsilon)=1-\frac{\mathbb{E}^{-{1}/{(\varepsilon-1)}}\left[\dfrac{1}{X^{\varepsilon-1}}\right]}{\mu},\quad 0\leqslant\varepsilon\neq 1.

Using the norm notation in
L^{p}
spaces (Chapter 4 of
Brezis2010
):

\|Y\|_{p}\equiv\mathbb{E}^{1/p}[Y^{p}]=\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{p}\,\mathrm{d}F_{Y}(y)\right]^{1/p},\quad p>0,

the index
A(\varepsilon)
can be expressed as

(12)

\displaystyle A(\varepsilon)=1-\frac{\displaystyle{\left\|\frac{1}{X}\right\|_{\varepsilon-1}^{-1}}}{\mu},\quad 0\leqslant\varepsilon\neq 1.

Using the well-known fact:
\lim_{p\to\infty}\|Y\|_{p}=\|Y\|_{\infty}
(see Exercise 4.6 of Chapter 4 in
Brezis2010
), where

\|Y\|_{\infty}\equiv\inf\{M\geqslant 0:|Y(\omega)|\leqslant M\text{ almost surely on}\ \Omega\}

is the essential supremum, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to\infty}\left\|\frac{1}{X}\right\|_{\varepsilon-1}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{X}\right\|_{\infty}

(13)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{M\geqslant 0:\left|\frac{1}{X(\omega)}\right|\leqslant M\text{ almost surely on}\ \Omega\right\}=\infty.

Consequently, combining (
12
) with (
3.2
), the proof follows.
∎

Deriving estimator biases

4.1.
Bias of the Theil index estimator

The next results provide closed-form expressions for the expected value of the Theil index estimators
\widehat{T}_{T}
and
\widehat{T}_{L}
, given by

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{T}=\dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\log\left(\frac{X_{i}}{\overline{X}}\right)}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

and

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{L}={\frac{1}{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left({\frac{\overline{X}}{X_{i}}}\right),\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

respectively, where
\overline{X}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}/n
is the sample mean and
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. observations from
X\sim\mathrm{GM}(\bm{\theta})
.

Remark 3
.

Note that
\widehat{T}_{T}
can be written as

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}\log(D_{i})+\log(n),\quad D_{i}={X_{i}\over S_{n}},\quad S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}.

Remark 4
.

We can write
\widehat{T}_{L}

(14)

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{L}=\log(\overline{X})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(X_{i}).

Theorem 2

If

are independent copies of

is the digamma function.

Proof.

Since

are i.i.d. with the same distribution of

are i.i.d. discrete random variables with

Corollary 2
.

The bias of

, can be expressed as

\mathrm{Bias}(\widehat{T}_{T},T_{T})=\sum_{1\leqslant j_{1},\dots,j_{n}\leqslant m}\pi_{j_{1}}\cdots\pi_{j_{n}}\,\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}}}\\[5.69046pt]
\times\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}}\psi(\alpha_{j_{i}})-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}}\right)\psi\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}}\right)+n-1\right]+\log(n)\\[5.69046pt]
-\left[\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}\psi(\alpha_{j})+1\right)-\log\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}\right)\right],

where
\psi(\cdot)
is the digamma function.

Remark 5
.

In the case where
\alpha_{j}=\alpha\text{ for all }j=1,\ldots,m
, the corresponding bias is:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Bias}(\widehat{T}_{T},T_{T})=\log(n\alpha)-\frac{1}{n\alpha}-\psi\left(n\alpha\right)<0,

which supports the recent finding stated in Corollary 3.6 of
VilaSaulo2025
.

Theorem 3
.

If
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are independent copies of
X\sim\operatorname{GM}(\bm{\theta})
, then

\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{T}_{L}\right]=\sum_{1\leqslant j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{n}\leqslant m}\pi_{j_{1}}\cdots\pi_{j_{n}}\psi\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}}\right)-\log(n)-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\psi(\alpha_{j}),

where

\psi(\cdot)
denotes the digamma function.

Proof.

By using Identity (
14
) and the fact that
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. with
X
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{T}_{L}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)\right]-\log(n)-\mathbb{E}[\log(X)].

Applying Propositions
1
and
2
, the identity above can be written as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{T}_{L}\right]=\sum_{1\leqslant j_{1},\,\dots,\,j_{n}\leqslant m}\pi_{j_{1}}\cdots\pi_{j_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(Z^{*}_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}\right)\right]-\log(n)-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\mathbb{E}[\log(Z_{j})].

As
Z^{*}_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}\sim{\rm Gamma}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}},\lambda)
and
Z_{j}\sim{\rm Gamma}(\alpha_{j},\lambda)
, for each
j=1,\ldots,m
, from the identity
\mathbb{E}[\log(Z)]=\psi(a)-\log(b)
for
Z\sim{\rm Gamma}(a,b)
, the proofs readily follows.
∎

By combining Proposition
6
and Theorem
3
the following result follows.

Corollary 3
.

The bias of
\widehat{T}_{L}
relative to
T_{L}
, denoted by
\mathrm{Bias}(\widehat{T}_{L},T_{L})
, can be expressed as

\mathrm{Bias}(\widehat{T}_{L},T_{L})=\sum_{1\leqslant j_{1},\dots,j_{n}\leqslant m}\pi_{j_{1}}\cdots\pi_{j_{n}}\,\psi\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}}\right)-\log(n)-\log\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}\right),

where
\psi(\cdot)
is the digamma function.

Remark 6
.

Observe that when
\alpha_{j}=\alpha\text{ for all }j=1,\ldots,m
, the resulting bias becomes:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Bias}(\widehat{T}_{L},T_{L})=\psi\left(n\alpha\right)-\log(n\alpha)<0.

Thus, Proposition 3.11 of
VilaSaulo2025
is verified.

4.2.
Bias of the Atkinson index estimator

The Atkinson index estimator is defined as:

\displaystyle\widehat{A(\varepsilon)}=1-\dfrac{{\dfrac{1}{n}\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{1-\varepsilon}\right)^{1/(1-\varepsilon)}}}{\dfrac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}},\quad 0\leqslant\varepsilon\neq 1,\ n\in\mathbb{N},

where
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. observations from the population
X
.

In this subsection we are interested in calculating exactly the expectation of
\widehat{A(\varepsilon)}
for the cases
\varepsilon\to 1
and
\varepsilon\to\infty
where
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are independent copies of
X\sim\mathrm{GM}(\bm{\theta})
.

Definition 3
.

The generalized mean (also called the power mean) of a set of positive real numbers
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
with exponent

p\in\mathbbm{R}\backslash\{0\}
is defined as:

\displaystyle M_{p}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\equiv\left({\dfrac{1}{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{p}\right)^{{1}/{p}}.

Using the definition and well-known limits
(
Bullen2003
)
:

\displaystyle\lim_{p\to p_{0}}\displaystyle M_{p}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\begin{cases}\sqrt[n]{x_{1}\cdots x_{n}},&\text{if}\ p_{0}=0,\\[8.5359pt]
\min\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\},&\text{if}\ p_{0}=-\infty,\end{cases}

we have

\widehat{A(1)}\equiv\lim_{\varepsilon\to 1}\widehat{A(\varepsilon)}=1-\dfrac{\displaystyle\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)^{1/n}}{\dfrac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

and

\widehat{A(\infty)}\equiv\lim_{\varepsilon\to\infty}\widehat{A(\varepsilon)}=1-\dfrac{\min\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}}{\dfrac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Theorem 4
.

If
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are independent copies of
X\sim\text{GM}(\bm{\theta})
, then

\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{A(1)}\right]=1-n\sum_{1\leqslant j_{1},\dots,j_{n}\leqslant m}\pi_{j_{1}}\cdots\pi_{j_{n}}\,\dfrac{\displaystyle 1}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}}}\,\prod_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j_{i}}+{1\over n})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j_{i}})}.

Proof.

Note that
\widehat{A(1)}
can be written as

\widehat{A(1)}=1-n\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}\right)^{1/n},

where
D_{i}={X_{i}/S_{n}}
and

S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}.
By (
16
),
given
Y_{1}=j_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}=j_{n}
the vector
(D_{1},\ldots,D_{n})^{\top}
has Dirichlet distribution with parameter vector
(\alpha_{j_{1}},\dots,\alpha_{j_{n}})^{\top}
.
Hence, by applying Identity (
5
), with
d_{i}=1/n
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{A(1)}\,\Big|\,Y_{1}=j_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}=j_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=1-n\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}\right)^{1/n}\Bigg|\,Y_{1}=j_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}=j_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=1-n\,\frac{\Gamma\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}}+1\right)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j_{i}}+{1\over n})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j_{i}})}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{n}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j_{i}}+{1\over n})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j_{i}})},

where the last line follows from the well-known identity:
\Gamma(x+1)=x\Gamma(x)
.
Finally, applying the Law of total probability, the stated result follows immediately. ∎

From Theorem
4
and Proposition
8
, it follows that:

Corollary 4
.

The bias of
\widehat{A(1)}
relative to
{A(1)}
, denoted by
\mathrm{Bias}(\widehat{A(1)},{A(1)})
, is given by

\mathrm{Bias}(\widehat{A(1)},{A(1)})=-n\sum_{1\leqslant j_{1},\dots,j_{n}\leqslant m}\pi_{j_{1}}\cdots\pi_{j_{n}}\,\dfrac{\displaystyle 1}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}}}\\[5.69046pt]
\times\prod_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j_{i}}+{1\over n})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j_{i}})}+\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}}\exp\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\psi(\alpha_{j})\right\}.

Remark 7
.

When
\alpha_{j}=\alpha\text{ for all }j=1,\ldots,m
, the resulting bias becomes:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Bias}(\widehat{A(1)},{A(1)})=\dfrac{\displaystyle 1}{\alpha}\left[\exp\left\{\psi(\alpha)\right\}-\dfrac{\Gamma^{n}(\alpha+{1\over n})}{\Gamma^{n}(\alpha)}\right],

which confirms the validity of recent result in Corollary 3.17 of
VilaSaulo2025
.

Theorem 5
.

If
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are independent copies of
X\sim\text{GM}(\bm{\theta})
, then

\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{A(\infty)}\right]=1-n\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+\cdots+k_{m}=n\\[2.84544pt]
k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}\geqslant 0\end{subarray}}\binom{n}{k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}}\pi_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots\pi_{m}^{k_{m}}\,\dfrac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}k_{j}}\\[5.69046pt]
\times\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\Gamma^{k_{1}}(\alpha_{1},u)}{\Gamma^{k_{1}}(\alpha_{1})}\cdots\dfrac{\Gamma^{k_{m}}(\alpha_{m},u)}{\Gamma^{k_{m}}(\alpha_{m})}\,{\rm d}u,

where

\displaystyle{\displaystyle{n\choose k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}}={\frac{n!}{k_{1}!\cdots k_{m}!}}}

is a multinomial coefficient. The sum is over all nonnegative integers
k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}
with total sum
n
.

Proof.

By using the identity

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-\xi z){\rm d}z={1\over\xi},\quad\xi>0,

with
\xi=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
, we get

(17)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\dfrac{\min\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\min\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left\{-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)z\right\}{\rm d}z\right].

By applying the identity

\displaystyle\min\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathds{1}_{\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\{X_{i}\geqslant t\}}{\rm d}t,

the expression in (
17
) becomes

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\{X_{i}\geqslant t\}}\exp\left\{-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)z\right\}\right]{\rm d}t{\rm d}z,

where the interchange of integrals is justified by Tonelli’s theorem. Since
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. with the same distribution of
X\sim\mathrm{GM}(\bm{\theta})
, the last expression simplifies to

(18)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}^{n}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{X\geqslant t\}}\exp\left(-Xz\right)\right]{\rm d}t{\rm d}z.

When
X\sim\text{GM}(\bm{\theta})
, direct computation shows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{X\geqslant t\}}\exp\left(-Xz\right)\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\,{\lambda^{\alpha_{j}}\Gamma(\alpha_{j},(z+\lambda)t)\over(z+\lambda)^{\alpha_{j}}\Gamma(\alpha_{j})}.

Consequently, by using the above identity and then the Multinomial theorem, the integral in (
18
) can be written as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\,{\lambda^{\alpha_{j}}\over(z+\lambda)^{\alpha_{j}}\Gamma(\alpha_{j})}\,\Gamma(\alpha_{j},(z+\lambda)t)\right]^{n}{\rm d}t{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+\cdots+k_{m}=n\\[2.84544pt]
k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}\geqslant 0\end{subarray}}\binom{n}{k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}^{k_{j}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\dfrac{\lambda^{\alpha_{j}k_{j}}\Gamma^{k_{j}}(\alpha_{j},(z+\lambda)t)}{(z+\lambda)^{\alpha_{j}k_{j}}\Gamma^{k_{j}}(\alpha_{j})}{\rm d}t{\rm d}z.

Now, making the change of variable
u=(z+\lambda)t
, the proof of theorem follows.

∎

By combining Theorem
5
and Proposition
7
we have:

Corollary 5
.

The bias of
\widehat{A(\infty)}
relative to
{A(\infty)}
, denoted by
\mathrm{Bias}(\widehat{A(\infty)},{A(\infty)})
, is given by

\mathrm{Bias}(\widehat{A(\infty)},{A(\infty)})=-n\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+\cdots+k_{m}=n\\[2.84544pt]
k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}\geqslant 0\end{subarray}}\binom{n}{k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}}\pi_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots\pi_{m}^{k_{m}}\,\dfrac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}k_{j}}\\[5.69046pt]
\times\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\Gamma^{k_{1}}(\alpha_{1},u)}{\Gamma^{k_{1}}(\alpha_{1})}\cdots\dfrac{\Gamma^{k_{m}}(\alpha_{m},u)}{\Gamma^{k_{m}}(\alpha_{m})}\,{\rm d}u.

Remark 8
.

Note that the integrals in Theorem
5
and Corollary
5
do not have closed-form expressions in terms of standard mathematical functions, and therefore require numerical methods for evaluation.

Remark 9
.

The scale invariance property of estimators
\widehat{T}_{T}
,
\widehat{T}_{L}
,
\widehat{A(1)}
and
\widehat{A(\infty)}
implies that their respective expectations are unaffected by the rate
\lambda
, as shown in Theorems
2
,
3
,
4
and
5
, respectively.

4.3.
Bias of the dispersion index estimator

The sampling estimator of the variance-to-mean ratio (dispersion index) is given by

\widehat{\mathrm{VMR}}\equiv\frac{S^{2}}{\overline{X}}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(X_{i}-\overline{X})^{2}}{\overline{X}},\quad n\geqslant 2,

where
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
denote i.i.d. samples drawn from the population
X
,
\overline{X}
is the sample mean and
S^{2}
is the sample variance.

Theorem 6
.

If
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are independent copies of
X\sim\text{GM}(\bm{\theta})
, then

\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\mathrm{VMR}}\right]=\frac{n}{(n-1)\lambda}\\[5.69046pt]
\times\sum_{1\leqslant j_{1},\dots,j_{n}\leqslant m}\pi_{j_{1}}\cdots\pi_{j_{n}}\left[\dfrac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}}+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\alpha_{j_{i}}(\alpha_{j_{i}}+1)}-{1\over n}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}}}\right].

Proof.

Notice that, using the notations of Remark
3
, the estimator
\widehat{\mathrm{VMR}}
can be written as

\widehat{\mathrm{VMR}}=\frac{n}{n-1}\,S_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(D_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}=\frac{n}{n-1}\,S_{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}^{2}-\frac{1}{n}\right).\\

Given
Y_{1}=j_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}=j_{n}
,
S_{n}
and
(D_{1},\ldots,D_{n})^{\top}
are independent (Theorem
1
). Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\mathrm{VMR}}\,\Big|\,Y_{1}=j_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}=j_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{n-1}\,\mathbb{E}\left[S_{n}\mid Y_{1}=j_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}=j_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\times\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}^{2}-\frac{1}{n}\,\Bigg|\,Y_{1}=j_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}=j_{n}\right].

As
S_{n}\mid Y_{1}=j_{1},\dots,Y_{n}=j_{n}\sim\text{Gamma}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}},\lambda)
, we have
\mathbb{E}[S_{n}\mid Y_{1}=j_{1},\dots,Y_{n}=j_{n}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}}/\lambda
. Consequently, by using Proposition
4

with
r=1
and
c_{j}=2\delta_{ji}
,
we get

\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\mathrm{VMR}}\,\Big|\,Y_{1}=j_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}=j_{n}\right]\\[5.69046pt]
=\frac{n}{n-1}\,\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}}}{\lambda}\left[\dfrac{1}{(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}})(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}}+1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\alpha_{j_{i}}(\alpha_{j_{i}}+1)}-\frac{1}{n}\right].

By applying the Law of total expectation, the result follows.
∎

As a direct result of Corollary
1
and Theorem
6
, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 6
.

The bias of
\widehat{\mathrm{VMR}}
relative to
\mathrm{VMR}
, denoted by
\mathrm{Bias}(\widehat{\mathrm{VMR}},\mathrm{VMR})
, is given by

\mathrm{Bias}(\widehat{\mathrm{VMR}},\mathrm{VMR})\\[5.69046pt]
=\>\frac{n}{(n-1)\lambda}\sum_{1\leqslant j_{1},\dots,j_{n}\leqslant m}\pi_{j_{1}}\cdots\pi_{j_{n}}\left[\dfrac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}}+1}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}}(\alpha_{j_{i}}+1)}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{j_{i}}\right]\\[5.69046pt]
-\dfrac{1}{\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}}\left[{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}(\alpha_{j}+1)-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\alpha_{j}\right)^{2}}\right].

Remark 10
.

Note that when
\alpha_{j}=\alpha\text{ for all }j=1,\ldots,m
, the resulting bias is:

\mathrm{Bias}(\widehat{\mathrm{VMR}},\mathrm{VMR})=\frac{n\alpha}{\lambda(n\alpha+1)}-\frac{1}{\lambda}=-\frac{1}{\lambda(n\alpha+1)}<0,

which corroborates the recent result reported in
shih2025
.
