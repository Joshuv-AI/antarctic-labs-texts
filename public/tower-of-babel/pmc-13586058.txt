The soil carbon saturation controls phosphate sorption and mobility in organically amended Ferralsols
Authors: Hannah Van Ryckel, Lowie Lenaerts, Toon van Dael, Erik Smolders
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Organic amendments reduced phosphate sorption in highly weathered soils.•Sorption reductions increased with organic amendment application rate.•Degree of carbon saturation explained sorption strength across soils and amendments.•Carbon saturation effects strengthened during soil incubation.•Reduced sorption translated into increased phosphate diffusion in the fertosphere.Keywords: Degree of carbon saturation, Competitive sorption, Reactive organic matter, Fertosphere, Integrated soil fertility management, Iron oxides, Highly weathered soils

Introduction

Phosphorus (P) deficiency is a major constraint to crop production in highly weathered soils (Nziguheba, 2007). These soils are typically rich in iron (Fe) and aluminium (Al) oxides, which strongly adsorb phosphate and thereby limit the effectiveness of mineral P fertilizers (Zheng, 2010). To improve P use efficiency in these soils, integrated soil fertility management (ISFM) promotes the combined application of mineral fertilizers and organic amendments (OAs) (Sanginga and Woomer, 2009, Vanlauwe et al., 2010, Vanlauwe et al., 2015). Reducing reliance on mineral fertilizers is particularly attractive to smallholder farmers because of their limited availability, high and volatile prices, and uncertain returns on investment (Chianu et al., 2012, Razanakoto et al., 2018, Sommer et al., 2013). Organic amendments are often more accessible and affordable, but their P concentrations are generally too low and variable to meet crop P requirements when applied alone (Palm et al., 1997, Sanginga and Woomer, 2009). Beyond these complementary benefits, their combined application can also result in synergistic increases in crop P uptake or yield (Andriamananjara et al., 2018, Andriamananjara et al., 2019, De Bauw et al., 2021, Kibunja et al., 2012). However, the occurrence of these interactions varies widely among studies, and underlying mechanisms remain insufficiently understood and difficult to predict (Ayamba et al., 2023).

Organic amendments are known to reduce phosphate sorption through several processes. Traditionally, this effect has often been attributed to increases in soil pH following OA application, which reduce the positive surface charge of Fe and Al oxides and thereby decrease phosphate sorption (Fink et al., 2016, Han et al., 2020, Weng et al., 2012). However, adsorption studies in simplified oxide suspensions already suggested that competition between organic anions and phosphate for reactive sorption sites may provide another explanation (Antelo et al., 2007, Sibanda and Young, 1986, Violante et al., 1991). Building on this idea, a series of surface-complexation models was developed and confirmed that phosphate sorption cannot be accurately predicted without accounting for organic matter (OM) already associated with oxide surfaces, as this OM competes directly with phosphate (Hiemstra et al., 2010, Regelink et al., 2015, Weng et al., 2011). Furthermore, these modeling studies introduced the concept of reactive OM (RO-) to represent the fraction of soil OM that is actually involved in these interactions (Gustafsson, 2006, Verbeeck et al., 2017).

While the proposed mechanism of competitive sorption predicts reduced phosphate sorption with increasing occupation of oxide surfaces by OM, experimental studies with OAs under realistic soil conditions have reported highly variable responses. Higher OA doses generally reduce phosphate sorption, but the magnitude of the effect differs widely among studies (Abboud et al., 2018, Ghodszad et al., 2022, Iyamuremye and Dick, 1996). Similarly, different OA types have been reported to have varying effects, depending on differences in P content, carbon (C) composition, C:P ratio, solubility, and reactivity (Debicka et al., 2023, Iyamuremye and Dick, 1996, Kataria et al., 2024, Ohno and Crannell, 1996, Vanden Nest et al., 2016, Varinderpal-Singh et al., 2006). Soil properties such as the initial soil pH and P status, mineralogy, and clay content further influence the responses, with the greatest effects reported in acid Fe/Al-oxide dominated systems (Ghodszad et al., 2022, Kataria et al., 2024, Sharma et al., 2023, Yu et al., 2013). This shows that the link between mechanistic soil chemical models and agronomic observations remains weak. In particular, it remains unclear how OA-induced changes in phosphate sorption depend on amendment quality, application rate, and incubation time.

In addition, the implications of these sorption processes for phosphate diffusion in the fertosphere (i.e., the zone surrounding fertilizer granules) remain poorly understood, particularly in intact organically amended soils. Phosphate transport in the rhizosphere is primarily governed by diffusion, which controls the supply of P to plant roots (Olsen and Watanabe, 1963, Santner et al., 2012). Steep concentration gradients develop around roots and around fertilizer granules, driving diffusive fluxes through the soil (Barber, 1962, Doolette et al., 2026, Hinsinger, 2001). The extent of diffusion is strongly influenced by the soil’s P buffering capacity, which is in turn governed by phosphate sorption (Degryse and McLaughlin, 2014, Holford, 1997). Consequently, reductions in sorption strength are expected to enhance the movement of phosphate from fertilizer granules to roots with reduced interception in soil.

Against this background, this study aimed to establish a mechanistic link between OA-induced changes in phosphate sorption and phosphate mobility in highly weathered soils. It was hypothesized that the effects of OAs on phosphate sorption can be explained by the soil’s degree of carbon saturation (DCS), used here as an operational proxy for the extent to which reactive Fe and Al oxide surfaces are occupied by organic matter. Within this framework, differences in amendment type, application rate, and incubation time are expected to influence phosphate sorption through their effects on the quantity and reactivity of OM associated with oxide surfaces. It was further hypothesized that OA-induced reductions in sorption strength and buffering capacity translate into enhanced phosphate mobility in the fertosphere under realistic soil conditions.

To test these hypotheses, two complementary experiments were conducted. First, three Ferralsols (from Madagascar, Vietnam, and the Philippines) were amended with three different OA types (farmyard manure (FYM), compost, and straw) at four doses (0, 0.5, 1.5, and 4 % w/w) in a full factorial design and incubated for 1 and 49 days to quantify their effects on P sorption and its relationship with DCS. Second, a diffusion experiment was conducted using the Malagasy soil collected as intact and sieved samples from a long-term field trial with and without a history of FYM application, to assess whether the observed changes in sorption behavior translate into increased P mobility from a fertilizer granule.

Materials and Methods

Soil sampling and characterization

Soils were sampled from the upper 20 cm of three strongly P-deficient Ferralsols on uncultivated land in Behenjy (Madagascar), Da Loan (Vietnam), and Luisiana (the Philippines) (Table 1). The soils were subsequently transported to Belgium for the laboratory experiments. Samples were air-dried and sieved to 2 mm. Extractable P was determined in 0.01 M CaCl2 (1:10, w/v, 2 h shaking), with phosphate concentrations measured colorimetrically using the malachite green method (Van Veldhoven & Mannaerts, 1987). The limit of quantification of this measurement was 0.008 mg P L−1, and all three soils had no detectable P in this extract, illustrating the P deficiency. Soil pH was measured in 0.001 M CaCl2 (1:5, w/v). Particle size distribution was determined by laser diffraction (LS 13 320, Beckman Coulter). Organic carbon (OC) and total nitrogen (N) were measured using an elemental analyzer (Carlo Erba EA1108). Effective cation exchange capacity (eCEC) was determined using the cobalt hexamine method (ISO 23470, 2007). Amorphous Fe and Al oxides, as well as associated P, were extracted with ammonium oxalate (Schwertmann, 1964) and quantified by ICP-OES (Thermo Scientific iCAP 7000 series). All analyses included internal reference materials and replicates, and results are reported on an oven-dry mass basis (105 °C).

In addition, intact soil samples were collected for the fertilizer diffusion experiment (Section 2.4). These soils were collected from the Behenjy site (Madagascar), where the authors had conducted a three-year field experiment. In this trial, upland rice had been cultivated for three consecutive growing seasons (2022–2025) on control plots and plots receiving annual applications of FYM (characteristics described in Section 2.2) at a rate of 10 t ha−1 yr−1. Following the final harvest (April 2025), intact soil slices were sampled in November 2025 from both treatments to preserve the natural soil structure and the effects of field-aged OM. To avoid interference from surface residues and roots, the upper 5 cm of soil was carefully removed before sampling. Petri dishes were then placed upside down on the soil surface and gently pressed into the soil to extract undisturbed samples. The dishes were subsequently excavated, and excess soil was trimmed flush with the rim. Samples were sealed immediately after collection and stored until further use.

A wet sieving procedure was performed to assess aggregate size distribution and soil structural stability in the Behenjy control and FYM-amended soils (Table 2). The method was adapted from standard wet sieving protocols (e.g. Kemper & Rosenau, 1986). Briefly, approximately 100 g of air-dried soil was placed on a 2 mm sieve and subjected to repeated vertical movement by alternately immersing it in water and exposing it to air for 2 min. The soil remaining on the sieve was collected, oven-dried at 105 °C, and weighed. The material passing through the 2 mm sieve was transferred onto a 250 µm sieve, and the same procedure was repeated. This stepwise fractionation was repeated once more using a 63 µm sieve. In this way, the soil was separated into four aggregate size classes (>2 mm, 250 µm–2 mm, 63–250 µm, and < 63 µm). All recovered fractions were oven-dried before weighing, and the < 63 µm fraction was determined by mass balance.

Organic amendment characterization

The OAs used in this study were: FYM sourced from a local farmer in Behenjy, Madagascar; green compost from ILVO in Ghent, Belgium; and wheat straw from a field trial in Bure, France (Table 3). The three OAs were selected to represent contrasting amendment properties and thereby capture a broad range of OA quality in relation to their effects on phosphate sorption. The chemical properties of the organic amendments were determined following standard procedures after air-drying and sieving to 2 mm. The pH was measured in 0.01 M CaCl2 (1:5, w/v). Total carbon (C), organic carbon (OC), and total nitrogen (N) contents were determined on oven-dried samples (105 °C) using an elemental analyzer (Carlo Erba EA1108), with or without prior carbonate removal using concentrated HCl. Dissolved organic carbon (DOC) was extracted with 0.001 M CaCl2 (1:10, w/v, 2 h shaking) and measured by catalytic combustion (Shimadzu TOC-L CPH). Aromaticity of the DOC was estimated from specific UV absorbance (SUVA254) using the empirical relationship of Weishaar et al. (2003). Total P was determined by ICP-OES (Thermo Scientific iCAP 7000 series) following acid digestion. Inorganic P was extracted with 0.5 M H2SO4 (1:25, w/v, 20 h shaking), and P concentrations were measured colorimetrically using the malachite green method (Van Veldhoven & Mannaerts, 1987). Organic P was calculated as the difference between total and inorganic P.

Incubation experiment: OA effects on phosphate sorption

Experimental design and setup

An incubation experiment was conducted to evaluate the effects of OAs on phosphate sorption and their relationship to changes in the DCS of the soil. This was set up as a full factorial of three Ferralsols (Table 1), amended with three OAs (Table 3) at four application rates (0, 0.5, 1.5, and 4 % w/w, all on an oven-dry mass basis) and analyzed at two timepoints after amendment. The factorial design was chosen to test whether the relationship between OA-induced changes in phosphate sorption and DCS was robust across different Ferralsols and contrasting OAs, application rates, and incubation times. Four replicates were included per treatment. Organic amendments were applied on a dry mass basis rather than at an equivalent C input to reflect agronomically realistic application practices. The application rate of 0.5 % w/w corresponds to 6 t dry mass ha−1, assuming a plough layer depth of 0.1 m and soil bulk density of 1.2 t m−3. That was chosen to represent a realistic amendment input. The 1.5 and 4 % w/w were included to assess whether OA-induced reductions in P sorption approached a plateau at high amendment inputs, but can result in realistic concentrations where OAs are locally applied in planting holes.

Before the amendment, soils were pre-incubated in darkness for one week at 20 °C and 20 % gravimetric moisture content to stabilize microbial activity following air-drying and sieving. After pre-incubation, OAs were thoroughly mixed with the soils, and the gravimetric moisture content was adjusted to 28 %. Samples were incubated in closed containers in darkness at 20 °C. The incubation containers were fitted with gas-permeable valves to allow gas exchange while enabling periodic respiration measurements. Destructive sampling was carried out after 1 and 49 days of incubation, with two replicate samples collected per treatment and sampling time.

Phosphate adsorption

At each sampling time, phosphate adsorption isotherms were determined for two independently incubated replicates of each soil × OA type × OA dose combination. Each incubation replicate was equilibrated with 0.01 M CaCl2 solutions containing increasing P concentrations (0, 20, 50, 100, and 200 mg P L−1) at a soil-to-solution ratio of 1:10 for 18 h under continuous shaking. Two analytical replicates were included per P concentration. After equilibration, suspensions were centrifuged at 1400 RCF for 5 min and filtered over 0.45 µm Chromafil filters. Phosphate concentrations in filtrates were measured colorimetrically using the malachite green method (Van Veldhoven & Mannaerts, 1987), with absorbance measured at 630 nm using a spectrophotometer (Perkin Elmer, Belgium). In addition, pH was measured in the zero-P adsorption suspensions after equilibration (18 h shaking in 0.01 M CaCl2) for each independently incubated replicate of every treatment.

Carbon dynamics and degree of carbon saturation

Dissolved organic carbon concentrations were determined at each sampling time by extraction with 0.001 M CaCl2 (1:10, w/v, 2 h shaking) followed by catalytic combustion analysis (Shimadzu TOC-L CPH). The degree of aromaticity of the samples was estimated from the SUVA254 using the empirical relationship of Weishaar et al. (2003).

Microbial respiration was monitored periodically during incubation, i.e., after 3, 7, 10, 16, 22, 30, and 43 days, by measuring CO2 accumulation in the two independently incubated replicate containers per treatment using a LI-COR infrared gas analyzer. The valves of the respiration pots remained open during the incubation period, but were temporarily sealed 24 h before each measurement.

Changes in soil carbon status were expressed in terms of DCS (%), used here as an operational estimate (rather than a direct measurement) for the extent to which reactive Fe and Al oxide surfaces are occupied by organic matter:

where RO- is the concentration of competing reactive organic anions (mmol kg−1), and Feox and Alox are the concentrations of oxalate-extractable Fe and Al present in the soil (mmol kg−1). Based on previous adsorption and surface-complexation models, the concentration of reactive organic anions was fitted as 1.3 mmol RO- per gram of soil organic carbon by Verbeeck et al. (2017). In their study, the conversion factor was a value that fitted best the effect of added natural (river-based) OM on KD values of arsenate (a phosphate analog) in soils. It represents an average estimate of the density of reactive organic functional groups associated with humified OM and was used to operationally define DCS.

The DCS concept is based on the assumption that organic anions and phosphate compete for reactive surface sites on Fe and Al oxides. Variations in DCS following OA addition were calculated from the initial soil carbon contents, the theoretical carbon inputs through OA addition, and corrected for the cumulative carbon mineralization losses derived from the mean respiration of the two independently incubated replicate samples.

Diffusion experiment: Phosphate mobility in the fertosphere

Experimental design

A diffusion experiment was conducted to assess whether OA-induced changes in phosphate sorption translate into increased P mobility under realistic soil conditions. Soil from the Behenjy Ferralsol was collected from a three-year field trial in plots with and without a history of FYM application. Both treatments were sampled as intact and as sieved soils and placed in Petri dishes (Ø 8.5 cm, 1.2 cm height) at a soil bulk density of 1 g cm−3. The use of intact soils is uncommon, but was included here, the idea being that P sorption studies in suspensions may overestimate the effective reactive surfaces, thereby underestimating the mobility in intact soils, as in the field. Sieved soils were adjusted to the same gravimetric moisture content as the corresponding intact samples, i.e., 33 % for the control soil and 39 % for the FYM-amended soil. These moisture contents reflected field conditions at the time of sampling and were assumed to correspond to similar soil water tensions. After overnight equilibration, a triple superphosphate (TSP) granule containing 9.4 mg P was placed at the center of each dish, just below the soil surface.

For each treatment, 15 replicate dishes were prepared for intact soils and 9 for sieved soils. Additional dishes without TSP were included as controls to account for P release from the soil itself, consisting of one replicate for the control soils and two replicates for the FYM-amended soils under both intact and sieved conditions. In total, 54 Petri dishes were incubated.

Petri dishes were sealed to prevent moisture loss and incubated in the dark at 20 °C. Destructive sampling was performed after 6, 20, and 34 days of incubation, with five replicates of intact soils and three replicates of sieved soils (for both control and FYM-amended soils) collected at each sampling time. Treatments without TSP were sampled after 20 days of incubation.

Preparation of DGT gels

The spatial diffusion of fertilizer-derived P around the TSP granule was visualized using precipitated zirconium oxide (ZrO)-based Diffusive Gradients in Thin Films (DGT) binding gels, followed by colorimetric development (Arias et al., 2021, Saracanlao et al., 2024).

DGT hydrogels were prepared following the method of Zhang & Davison (1999). Briefly, hydrogels were cast from a mixture of ultrapure Milli-Q water, acrylamide: N,N′-methylenebisacrylamide solution (37.5:1, 40 %), ammonium persulfate (10 %), and TEMED (N,N,N′,N′-tetramethylethylenediamine) between glass plates separated by 0.4 mm spacers. After 30 min of polymerization at room temperature, gels were hydrated in Milli-Q water for 24 h. Zirconium was subsequently precipitated within the gels following Guan et al. (2015) by immersion in a ZrOCl2·8H2O solution, followed by equilibration in 0.05 M MES buffer (pH 6.7). Gels were rinsed repeatedly with Milli-Q water and stored in 0.01 M NaCl solution until use.

At each sampling time, DGT units consisting of a 10 µm Whatman membrane filter, a ZrO-based binding gel, and a plastic support layer were placed on the soil surface for 24 h. After retrieval, gels were developed colorimetrically according to Ding et al. (2013). Briefly, gels were first immersed in Milli-Q water at 85 °C for 5 days, then stained for 45 min at 25 °C with a molybdate blue reagent. Developed gels were scanned using a flatbed scanner and analyzed in ImageJ (v1.54 g, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD). Images were converted to 8-bit grayscale, and a color-intensity threshold was established to delineate the high-P diffusion zone. CaCl2 extractions of soil

Following DGT deployment (and therefore one day later since the gels were deployed for 24 h), soils were destructively sampled in concentric zones at distances of 0–8 mm, 8–16 mm, and > 16 mm from the fertilizer granule to assess spatial gradients in P mobility. Soil samples were extracted with 0.01 M CaCl2 (1:10 soil-to-solution ratio, 2 h shaking), followed by centrifugation (1400 RCF) and filtration through 0.45 µm Chromafil filters. Phosphate concentrations in the extracts were determined colorimetrically using the malachite green method (Van Veldhoven & Mannaerts, 1987).

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in JMP Student Edition 19 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Incubation experiment

Phosphate adsorption isotherms were fitted to pooled adsorption data of duplicate incubation samples per treatment using nonlinear regression according to the Freundlich model (Eq. (1). The soil-to-solution distribution coefficient (KD; Eq. (2) at an equilibrium concentration of 0.05 mg P L−1 was subsequently calculated from the fitted Freundlich equations.

Relationships between KD and DCS, soil pH, and DOC concentration were evaluated using linear regression analysis. To assess the independent contributions of DCS and soil pH to phosphate sorption, multiple linear regression models including both variables were also fitted for each sampling time. Coefficients of determination (R2) were used to assess model performance.

Diffusion experiment

Diffusion radii were analyzed using a factorial linear model including the effects of soil structure (intact vs sieved), organic amendment treatment (control vs FYM), timepoint, and their interactions. Pairwise comparisons among Structure × Treatment combinations were performed using Tukey-adjusted comparisons of least square means (LSMeans) derived from the full model. Statistical significance was evaluated at p < 0.05.

Solution P concentrations were analyzed using a factorial linear model that included StructureTreatment (a combined factor with four levels: Intact Control, Intact + FYM, Sieved Control, and Sieved + FYM), timepoint, distance from the fertilizer granule, and all interactions among these factors. Treatment effects were evaluated within each Timepoint × Distance combination using Tukey-adjusted comparisons of LSMeans derived from the full model. Statistical significance was evaluated at p < 0.05.

Results

Soil and OA characteristics

The three Ferralsols used in this experiment (Table 1) were acid (pH 4.2–4.5) and strongly P-deficient, with CaCl2-extractable P concentrations below 0.008 mg P L−1, whereas the critical value for crop production is approximately 0.2 mg P L−1 (Kruse et al., 2015, Nawara et al., 2017).

The OAs (Table 3) differed greatly in ways relevant to P adsorption in weathered soils. Straw had a very high total C content (47 %) and C/N ratio (71) compared to FYM and compost (10–20 % C and C/N ratio 9–11). This suggests that straw may decompose more slowly, whereas FYM and compost are more likely to rapidly release organic compounds. The DOC increased strongly from FYM (0.2 g C L−1) < compost (2.2 g C L−1) < straw (3.4 g C L−1), while aromaticity showed the opposite trend. This suggests that straw contributes the largest pool of soluble organic hydrophilic compounds, but that FYM contained a greater proportion of aromatic compounds that may interact more strongly with mineral surfaces. Compost was the most alkaline (pH 8.3) and contained inorganic P concentrations up to 10-fold higher than those in FYM and straw.

OA effects on phosphate sorption

To evaluate the effect of OAs on phosphate sorption, adsorption isotherms were constructed for all soil × OA × dose combinations after 1 and 49 days of incubation (Figs. SI-1 to SI-3). From these isotherms, the soil-to-solution distribution coefficient of phosphate (KD, Eq. (2) was derived as an indicator of P sorption affinity under low-P conditions. Fig. 1 shows the KD values after 49 days of incubation. Similar but less consistent trends were already observed after 1 day of incubation (Fig. SI-4).

Effects of OA type and application rate on the soil-to-solution distribution coefficient of phosphate (KD; Eq. (2)) in three Ferralsols after 49 days of incubation.

The addition of organic amendment reduced P sorption in nearly all treatments compared to the unamended controls. The magnitude of the reduction depended strongly on both OA type and application rate. Across all soils, compost consistently produced the largest decreases in KD, followed by straw, whereas FYM generally showed the smallest effects. Increasing OA application rates progressively lowered KD values. Even at the highest application rate (4 % w/w), no clear plateau in the response was observed.

Despite differences in absolute sorption strength between soils, relative OA-induced reductions in P sorption were remarkably consistent. The P sorption was highest in the unamended control soils and ranged from 6000 L kg−1 in the Behenjy soil to nearly 9000 L kg−1 in the Luisiana soil. The addition of compost at 4 % w/w consistently halved the KD relative to the corresponding unamended soils.

OA effects on carbon dynamics

The Behenjy soil exhibited a considerably higher initial OC content than the other two soils (30.5 g kg−1 compared to 5.3–5.5 g kg−1, Table 1). The additional OC through OA addition ranged 0.5–4 g C kg−1 soil for FYM, 1–8 g C kg−1 soil for compost, and 2–19 g C kg−1 soil for straw, calculated from the application rates (0.5–4 % w/w) and the OC content of the OAs (Table 3). After OA application, microbial respiration was monitored throughout the incubation period (Figs. SI-5 to SI-7). The loss of C was equal to 11–70 % of the added organic C in the Behenjy soil, 2–46 % in the Da Loan soil, and 2–9 % in the Luisiana soil. Among OA treatments, the loss of C through respiration ranged from straw > compost > FYM ≈ control. Based on the initial soil C contents, C inputs through OA addition, and cumulative respiration losses during incubation, the DCS (Eq. (4) was estimated for all treatments after 1 and 49 days of incubation (Table SI-1).

Initial DCS values in the control treatments differed strongly among soils, with the Behenjy soil exhibiting a substantially higher value (58 %) than the Da Loan and Luisiana soils (17–23 %). Consequently, relative increases in DCS following OA addition were smaller in Behenjy than in the other two soils. Straw increased initial DCS values the most (up to 93 % in the Behenjy soil, 106 % in the Da Loan soil, and 73 % in the Luisiana soil), followed by compost and then FYM, corresponding to their relative C contents. This trend remained consistent in final DCS values, despite straw also showing much higher respiration rates than compost and FYM during incubation. Across all OA types, increasing application rates resulted in progressively higher DCS values even after 49 days of incubation.

Relationship between phosphate sorption and DCS

To evaluate if OA-induced changes in P sorption can be explained within a common mechanistic framework, the relationship between P sorption affinity (reflected by KD) and the DCS was examined across all soils, OA types, and application rates (Fig. 2).

Relationship between phosphate sorption, reflected by the soil-to-solution distribution coefficient of phosphate (KD; Eq. (2)), and DCS of the soil across all soil × OA × dose combinations after 1 and 49 days of incubation.

After 1 day of incubation, only a weak relationship was observed between KD and DCS (R2 = 0.28). In contrast, after 49 days of incubation, KD values decreased with increasing DCS, yielding a substantially stronger relationship (R2 = 0.66). Across all treatments at this second sampling time, DCS values ranged from approximately 17 % to 75 %, while KD values ranged from approximately 3000 to 9000 L kg−1. Even with this large variation, all treatments aligned within the same overall KD–DCS relationship.

The weak relationship observed after 1 day suggests that changes in DCS alone cannot fully capture the immediate effects of OA addition on P sorption. At this early stage, KD was more strongly related to soil pH (R2 = 0.77; Fig. SI-8). However, after 49 days of incubation, the strength of the KD–pH relationship decreased (R2 = 0.52), whereas the relationship between KD and DCS became stronger. Multiple regression analysis confirmed that soil pH was the dominant predictor of KD after 1 day of incubation and that DCS had no significant effect after correcting for pH. After 49 days, both pH and DCS contributed significantly to explaining the variation in KD, with DCS exhibiting the stronger independent effect (R2 = 0.66 for DCS alone and R2 = 0.79 for DCS and pH).

No clear relationship was observed between KD and DOC concentrations, either after 1 day (R2 = 0.03) or after 49 days of incubation (R2 = 0.13; Fig. SI-9).

Phosphate diffusion in the fertosphere

To evaluate whether reduced phosphate sorption in organically amended soils translated into enhanced P mobility under realistic conditions, the diffusion of phosphate away from a fertilizer granule was monitored over time in intact and sieved Behenjy soils with and without a history of FYM application. Visual inspection of the gels showed generally stronger coloration in FYM-amended soils than in the corresponding control soils, particularly during the early stages of incubation. Discussion

A DCS framework explains OA-induced reductions in phosphate sorption

Despite striking differences among OA types (i.e., OA quality), application rates, and soil properties, phosphate sorption converged towards a common relationship with the DCS after 49 days of incubation (R2 = 0.66; Fig. 2). Across all amended soils, DCS values at the end of incubation ranged from 17 to 75 %, while corresponding KD values ranged from roughly 3000 to 9000 L kg−1. The emergence of a KD–DCS relationship across this broad range suggests that DCS provides a useful proxy for potential occupation of reactive Fe and Al oxide surfaces by organic matter, which in turn appears to influence phosphate sorption. No clear saturation of the KD–DCS relationship was observed within the investigated range. This likely reflects both the relatively limited DCS range achieved in most treatments (<75 %) and the fact that DCS represents an operational estimate rather than a direct measurement of oxide surface occupancy.

The DCS concept is based on the assumption that organic matter and phosphate compete for reactive sorption sites on Fe and Al oxides (Antelo et al., 2007, Kaiser and Guggenberger, 2003, Sibanda and Young, 1986). Previous modeling studies already found that P sorption cannot be accurately predicted without taking into account the OM associated with mineral surfaces (Hiemstra et al., 2010, Regelink et al., 2015, Weng et al., 2011). Furthermore, some studies estimated the fraction of soil OM that is actually involved in these surface complexation reactions, called the reactive OM (RO-) (Gustafsson, 2006, Verbeeck et al., 2017, Verbeeck et al., 2019). The present study extends this concept by experimentally showing that OA-induced changes in phosphate sorption depend on OA quality and application rate, likely through their effect on the concentration of reactive OM, represented here by changes in DCS. The role of pH only enhanced the model from R2 = 0.66 to R2 = 0.79, with increasing pH decreasing the KD. This also follows the P adsorption models qualitatively.

Soil organic carbon alone explained a similar proportion of the variation in KD after 49 days (R2 = 0.65) as DCS (R2 = 0.66), indicating that, in the present dataset, DCS provided little additional explanatory power over SOC. This similarity may partly reflect the relatively narrow range in Fe + Al contents among the three soils investigated here, such that SOC and DCS provide similar information. In contrast, Vermeiren et al. (2022) found DCS, but not SOC, to be related to labile P across soils spanning a much wider range in Fe + Al contents, suggesting that accounting for the size of the reactive oxide pool may become particularly important when comparing soils with contrasting sorption capacities.

According to the proposed mechanism of competitive sorption, KD should also correlate well with the concentration of free reactive binding sites, defined here as 0.5∗Feox+Alox-Pox-RO-. However, the concentration of free sites was a poor predictor of KD after 49 days (R2 = 0.17, model not shown) compared to DCS (R2 = 0.66). This suggests that phosphate sorption is influenced more strongly by the degree of surface occupation than by the absolute concentration of remaining sorption sites. Organic molecules are substantially larger and chemically more complex than phosphate ions. Humic and fulvic substances typically contain multiple carboxylic and phenolic functional groups capable of interacting simultaneously with mineral surfaces (Kaiser and Guggenberger, 2003, Kleber et al., 2015). Consequently, OM may modify surface accessibility, charge characteristics, and steric conditions beyond what is captured by free binding sites.

Part of the observed reduction in phosphate sorption may additionally reflect increased P saturation of reactive sorption sites due to inorganic P contained in the OAs (Table 3), particularly in the compost treatments. However, if direct P loading were the dominant mechanism, treatment responses would be expected to cluster according to amendment P content rather than converge towards a common relationship with DCS. Straw treatments, despite their low P content, also reduced phosphate sorption substantially (Fig. 1). In addition, the strengthening of the KD–DCS relationship over time and the persistence of increased P mobility in field-aged FYM soils are more consistent with progressive organic matter–mineral interactions than with solely instantaneous increases in soil P saturation. The highest inorganic P dose in the compost treatment is 4.8 mmol P kg−1 soil, equivalent to a maximal increase in the degree of P saturation of Fe and Al oxides from 2 to 9 % in the Behenjy soil, 2 to 18 % in the Da Loan soil, and 0.7 to 12 % in the Luisiana soil. These values are still within the linear P sorption range, suggesting that the P doses derived from OA are unlikely to affect the reduced P sorption. The observed effects, therefore, likely result from a combination of mechanisms, including competitive sorption by OM, altered surface charge, and partial occupation of reactive sorption sites by amendment-derived P.

Amendment quality determines how closely OA effects follow the DCS framework

Although the overall KD–DCS relationship was strong, its strength depended on the OA type. The relationship was substantially stronger for FYM and compost than for straw treatments; when straw treatments were excluded, the R2 of the model fit increased from 0.66 to 0.87 (not shown). This suggests that amendment quality determines how closely the observed OA effects follow the DCS framework.

Compost and FYM had already undergone microbial processing before soil application (Leifeld et al., 2002). Therefore, they likely contained large proportions of microbially processed and aromatic compounds with a potential to associate with mineral surfaces (Kleber et al., 2015, Lavallee et al., 2020, von Lützow et al., 2007). In contrast, straw is largely composed of undecomposed plant structural material such as cellulose and lignin (Bhatia et al., 2020, Ghanney et al., 2023), which have a high C/N ratio that may prevent fast microbial processing because microbes become N-limited (Cotrufo et al., 2019, Mooshammer et al., 2014). Straw, therefore, likely contained a large proportion of relatively unprocessed particulate organic matter, which contributes to total OC but interacts only weakly with Fe- and Al-oxide surfaces (Lavallee et al., 2020).

The DCS values calculated in this study were based on a conversion factor of 1.3 mmol RO- g−1 OC (Verbeeck et al., 2017), which represents an average estimate of the density of reactive organic functional groups associated with organic matter. Applying this factor for all amendments assumes that each unit of OC contributes equally to competitive sorption, regardless of its origin or degree of processing. Since our results suggest that this assumption is not completely valid, DCS values reported here should be interpreted as operational estimates of potential oxide surface occupancy rather than direct measurements thereof. A more mechanistically accurate approach may therefore require explicit distinction between particulate and mineral-associated organic matter fractions.

The differences in C quality between the amendments may also explain why DOC concentration alone poorly predicted phosphate sorption in this study (Fig. SI-9). No meaningful relationship was observed between KD and DOC concentration, either after 1 day or after 49 days of incubation. This suggests that the instantaneous concentration of dissolved carbon is less important than the cumulative occupation of reactive oxide surfaces by organic matter, as much of the DOC may be non-reactive or transient (Lavallee et al., 2020).

Time dependence: Transition from pH-driven to carbon-driven effects

The mechanisms through which OAs reduced phosphate sorption changed markedly over the course of incubation. After 1 day, the KD-DCS relationship remained weak (R2 = 0.28; Fig. 2), whereas KD was strongly related to soil pH (R2 = 0.77; Fig. SI-8). Multiple regression analysis further showed that DCS did not contribute significantly to explaining KD after accounting for pH at this early stage. After 49 days of incubation, the KD-DCS relationship strengthened (R2 = 0.66) while the KD-pH relationship weakened (R2 = 0.52). At this later stage, both pH and DCS contributed significantly to explaining variation in KD, with DCS exhibiting the stronger independent effect. Together, these observations suggest that rapid changes in soil solution chemistry initially dominated OA effects on P sorption while competitive sorption became increasingly important over time.

Phosphate sorption in highly weathered soils is extremely sensitive to pH because Fe and Al oxides are variable-charge minerals. Increasing the pH decreases protonation of oxide surfaces, reduces their positive surface charge, and therefore weakens electrostatic attraction and ligand exchange of phosphate (Fink et al., 2016, Han et al., 2020, Weng et al., 2012). Such effects may occur almost immediately upon the addition of organic amendments and are therefore likely to dominate after 1 day of incubation. Over time, physical fragmentation and microbial processing may gradually transform part of the added OM into compounds with greater affinity for Fe and Al oxides (Kleber et al., 2015, Lavallee et al., 2020). Taken together, these results suggest that short-term studies may overestimate pH effects and underestimate OM-mineral interactions.

From reduced sorption to enhanced fertosphere mobility under realistic conditions

The Behenjy soil × FYM combination was selected from the incubation experiment to evaluate whether OA-induced reductions in P sorption translated into altered spatial P mobility under realistic soil conditions. Diffusion radii of fertilizer P derived from DGT images were significantly wider in FYM-amended soils compared to the corresponding control soils (Fig. 3 & Table 4). Similarly, CaCl2-extractable P concentrations within the diffusion zone were significantly higher in FYM-amended soils (Table 5). These effects were significant in the full factorial models across sampling times and distances. Together, these observations support the proposed mechanism that OA addition reduces phosphate sorption and weakens soil P buffering capacity (Holford, 1997), thereby allowing higher P concentrations to persist in solution and spread over a larger soil volume.

An important aspect of this study is that these effects persisted under structurally realistic conditions, even when evaluated in the small Petri-dish set-up. Most mechanistic studies use simplified systems, often pure minerals in suspension (Andrade et al., 2003, Antelo et al., 2007, Fu et al., 2013, Sibanda and Young, 1986, Violante et al., 1991), whereas this experiment included intact soils in which natural aggregate structure and pore organization were preserved. Diffusion radii were significantly wider in intact compared to sieved soils, and P-CaCl2 concentrations within the diffusion zone were also higher in intact soils (Table 4, Table 5). These observations suggest that soil structure influences P mobility beyond the effects of phosphate sorption measured in sieved samples. One possible explanation is that part of the sorption capacity is physically protected within aggregates and therefore less accessible to diffusing phosphate (Jarvis, 2007). However, soil structure may also directly affect diffusion pathways through changes in pore connectivity and tortuosity (Young & Crawford, 2004). Because both mechanisms were altered simultaneously in the present experiment, their relative contributions cannot be separated. Regardless of the underlying mechanism, the results demonstrate that OA-induced changes in phosphate sorption measured in laboratory systems persist under realistic structured soil conditions and translate into enhanced fertosphere P mobility.

Importantly, the FYM present in the Behenjy soil used in the diffusion experiment had been field-aged through three consecutive years of application rather than being freshly mixed into the soil immediately before the experiment. The persistence of increased P mobility in these field-aged soils is therefore consistent with the idea that OA effects on phosphate availability are governed not only by transient increases in organic matter, but also by longer-term progressive occupation of reactive Fe and Al oxide surfaces by organic matter. In addition, FYM-amended soils contained a larger proportion of water-stable macroaggregates than the control soils (Table 2), indicating that long-term FYM application had itself altered soil structure. Consequently, the effects of OM accumulation and improved soil structure likely reinforced each other in promoting P mobility in the fertosphere.

The consistency between the incubation and diffusion experiments supports the mechanistic interpretation that OA-induced reductions in phosphate sorption can translate into altered spatial P dynamics under realistic soil conditions. From a management perspective, these results suggest that OA selection and application rate could be optimized based on their potential to occupy reactive Fe and Al oxide surfaces, thereby reducing phosphate sorption. The DCS framework provides a potential basis for such optimization, although its predictive value for crop P availability and optimal field application rates remains to be tested. The agronomic relevance of these findings extends beyond the relatively small changes in diffusion distance observed in the present study. Numerous field experiments have demonstrated that combining OAs with mineral P fertilizers can substantially increase crop P uptake and yield in highly weathered soils (Andriamananjara et al., 2018, Andriamananjara et al., 2019, Ayamba et al., 2023, De Bauw et al., 2021, Sanginga and Woomer, 2009, Vanlauwe et al., 2001). Although the present data do not allow a direct quantitative link between increased diffusion distance and plant P uptake, they provide a plausible mechanistic explanation for part of the observed synergy. By reducing phosphate sorption and buffering capacity, OAs may both enlarge the soil volume enriched in fertilizer-derived P and slow down the fixation of phosphate onto reactive mineral surfaces. Together, these processes are expected to increase the probability that diffusing phosphate encounters roots before becoming immobilized in the soil. The results therefore suggest that reduced phosphate sorption may be an important, yet often overlooked, mechanism contributing to the improved P use efficiency frequently observed when OAs are combined with mineral P fertilizers.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that OA-induced reductions in phosphate sorption in highly weathered soils can be mechanistically linked to the DCS across different soils, OA types, and OA doses. The observed relationship supports the idea that OM competes with phosphate for reactive sorption sites on Fe and Al oxides, thereby reducing soil P buffering capacity. While short-term effects were mostly related to changes in soil pH, the importance of competitive sorption increased over time as OM likely became progressively associated with mineral surfaces. Reduced phosphate sorption translated into broader diffusion zones and higher solution P concentrations around a fertilizer granule in FYM-amended soils under realistic conditions, confirming that OA-induced changes in sorption can alter spatial P mobility in the fertosphere. Together, these findings provide a mechanistic framework for understanding OA effects on P dynamics in tropical agroecosystems and highlight the potential of OAs to improve the availability of fertilizer P in highly weathered soils. The DCS metric may serve as a simple indicator of the potential of OA applications to improve P availability by reducing phosphate sorption in highly weathered soils. Further validation under field conditions is needed to determine whether DCS can reliably predict improvements in P availability and crop P uptake following OA application.
