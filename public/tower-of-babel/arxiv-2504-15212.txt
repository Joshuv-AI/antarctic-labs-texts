A universal threshold for geometric embeddings of trees Thanks: 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification : 05C05. Thanks: Key words : geometrical embeddings, geometric graphs, metric embeddings, trees.
Dylan J. Altschuler, Pandelis Dodos, Konstantin Tikhomirov and Konstantinos Tyros

Abstract
. A graph G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) is geometrically embeddable into a normed space X X when there is a mapping ζ : V → X \zeta\colon V\to X such that ‖ ζ ⁡ ( v ) − ζ ⁡ ( w ) ‖ X ⩽ 1 \|\zeta(v)-\zeta(w)\|_{X}\leqslant 1 if and only if { v , w } ∈ E \{v,w\}\in E , for all distinct v , w ∈ V v,w\in V . Our result is the following universal threshold for the embeddability of trees. Let Δ ⩾ 3 \Delta\geqslant 3 , and let N N be sufficiently large in terms of Δ \Delta . Every N N –vertex tree of maximal degree at most Δ \Delta is embeddable into any normed space of dimension at least 64 ​ log ⁡ N log ⁡ log ⁡ N 64\,\frac{\log N}{\log\log N} , and complete trees are non-embeddable into any normed space of dimension less than 1 2 ​ log ⁡ N log ⁡ log ⁡ N \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\log N}{\log\log N} . In striking contrast, spectral expanders and random graphs are known to be non-embeddable in sublogarithmic dimension. Our result is based on a randomized embedding whose analysis utilizes the recent breakthroughs on Bourgain’s slicing problem.



1.
Introduction

Given a normed space
X
and a graph
G=(V,E)
, a map
\zeta\colon V\to X
is a
geometrical embedding
of
G
into
X
if

E=\big\{\{v,w\}\subseteq V\colon\|\zeta(v)-\zeta(w)\|_{X}\leqslant 1\big\}.

Say that
G
is non-embeddable into
X
if no such embedding exists. Note that the mapping
\zeta
exists if and only if
G
is isomorphic
to an
intersection graph
in
X
generated by parallel translates
of the unit ball
B_{X}
.

The problem of identifying geometrically embeddable graphs
has attracted significant attention,
especially in the Euclidean setting.
It was proved in
[
FM88
]

(see, also,
[
RRS89
]
and
[
RRS92
]
)
that every
N
–vertex graph of maximal degree at most
\Delta
admits
a geometrical embedding into a Euclidean space
of dimension
C\Delta^{2}\log N
, where
C>0

is a universal constant.
On the other hand, the Poincaré inequality implies that any spectral expander graph is non-embeddable into Euclidean space of sublogarithmic dimension
[
AT25
]
. More generally, spectral expanders also do not embed into any spaces of sublogarithmic dimension satisfying a so-called nonlinear Poincaré inequality
(see, for example,
[
Na14
,
MN14
,
Esk22
,
ADTT24
]
for a discussion of such inequalities).

The smallest
m
such that a graph
G
can be embedded into an
m
–dimensional
Euclidean space is called the
sphericity
of
G
, and denoted by
{\rm Sph}(G)
.
The aforementioned results then imply that,
suppressing dependence on
\Delta
, the sphericity of every
N
–vertex expander graph is of order
\Theta(\log N)
.
We refer to
[
MP93
, Section 2]
and
[
BL05
, Section 3]

for a review of results on sphericity of various families of graphs.
In the non-Euclidean setting, it was recently shown in
[
AT25
]

that with high probability, a random
\Delta
–regular graph
on
N
vertices cannot be geometrically embedded into
any

normed space of dimension
o(\log N)
.
It remains an interesting open question
whether this dimension bound is asymptotically optimal across all normed spaces.

The purpose of this note is to study geometrical embeddings of trees
into arbitrary normed spaces. The setting of
trees is fundamentally different from embedding
random graphs despite the fact that
sparse random graphs are locally tree-like,
i.e., have few short cycles.
As an illustration, in
[
FM86
]
it is shown that
the complete rooted binary tree
T
on
N
vertices admits a geometrical embedding into
\ell_{2}
of dimension at most

C\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}
. (Recall that, in contrast, a random
3
–regular graph on
N
vertices
does not embed geometrically into a Euclidean space of dimension
o(\log N)
.) On the other hand, a slight adaptation of
a volumetric argument from the same paper
[
FM86
]

(which we reproduce in Section
4
for the reader’s convenience)
yields that
T
is not geometrically embeddable into
any

normed space of dimension less than
c\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}
,
for a universal constant
c>0
.

It is therefore natural to ask if the “transition window”

\Theta\big(\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}\big)

for complete binary
N\text{--vertex}
trees is universal across
all norms. We completely resolve this problem and, moreover, provide embedding results for arbitrary bounded degree trees.

Theorem 1.1

(Universal threshold for embedding of trees)
.

There exist absolute positive constants
\alpha_{0}<\alpha_{1}
, and for every integer
\Delta\geqslant 3

\Delta

N\geqslant N_{0}
be an integer.

(A)

If
\dim(X)\leqslant\alpha_{0}\,\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}
, then there exists a tree
T

N

with maximum degree at most
\Delta
that is not geometrically embeddable into
X
.

(B)

On the other hand, if
\dim(X)\geqslant\alpha_{1}\,\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}
, then every tree
T
on
N
vertices
with maximum degree at most
\Delta

X

(Trees of growing maximal degree)
.

Determine an explicit function

and some universal constants

and

so that the following holds. Any tree on

Proof overview

As already mentioned, part
(A)
of Theorem
1.1
is known, and it appears (in a slightly different form) in the work of Frankl and Maehara
[
FM86
]
. We also note that a tree
T
certifying the first part of the theorem
can be constructed explicitly: if
N=\frac{\Delta^{n+1}-1}{\Delta-1}
for an integer
n
,
then
T
can be taken to be the complete rooted
\Delta
-regular tree of height
n
.

Part
(B)
is the bulk of the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
Given a normed space
X=(\mathbb{R}^{m},\|\cdot\|_{X})
and a tree
T
, we
attach to each edge of
T
an independent vector drawn uniformly from
the unit ball
B_{X}
. After arbitrarily choosing a root
\mathrm{o}
, each vertex
v
is embedded by summing the vectors on the edges along the path from
\mathrm{o}
to
v
, plus an independent “regularizing” Gaussian. Our goal is to show that this embedding is geometric with positive probability.

The main challenge is to verify that, with a non-zero probability, all pairs of non-adjacent vertices are at distance greater than one under the embedding.
Note that the spatial distance between the images of two vertices with graph distance
\ell
is given by a sum of
\ell
independent vectors, plus some Gaussian regularization.
To control the distance, we utilize small ball probability estimates
for log-concave distributions; in particular, we apply thin-shell estimates (for small
\ell
), or density bounds on isotropic log-concave vectors that arise in the context of the recent resolution of the slicing conjecture
[
KL24b
]
. In either case, these probability estimates
are not strong enough to survive the trivial union bound for arbitrary
N
–vertex trees. To deal with this problem, we
apply a combination of a conditioning argument (using the aforementioned Gaussian regularization) and the asymmetric Lovász local lemma.

2.
Background material

2.1.
Notation

All graphs in this paper have no multiple edges and no self-loops. Given a graph
G
, we denote its vertex set by
V(G)
and its edge set by
E(G)
. For every
v\in V(G)
, the
neighbourhood of
v
in
G
is denoted by
N_{G}(v):=\big\{u\in V(G)\colon\{u,v\}\in E(G)\big\}
, and
\mathrm{dist}_{G}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the shortest-path
graph distance on
V(G)
. Recall that a
tree
is a connected graph with no cycles. For a normed space
X
,
B_{X}
denotes the closed unit ball.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
T
be a tree, and let
u,v\in V(T)
. We say that a set
P\subseteq E(T)

connects
u
and
v

if there exist
u_{0},\ldots,u_{p}\in V
, where
p=|P|
, such that
u_{0}=u
,
u_{p}=v
and
P=\big\{\{u_{i-1},u_{i}\}\colon i\in[p]\big\}
.

If
u\neq v
, then
P(u,v)
denotes the smallest, under inclusion, set of edges that connects
u
and
v
;
by convention, we set
P(u,v)=\emptyset
if
u=v
. We shall refer to sets of the form
P(u,v)
as
paths
.

2.2.
Isotropic random vectors, and normed spaces in isotropic position

Recall that a random vector
\mathbf{U}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is
isotropic
if its components are mean-zero random variables, and its covariance matrix is the identity.

Now, let
X=(\mathbb{R}^{m},\|\cdot\|_{X})
be an
m
-dimensional normed space, and let
\mathbf{X}
be a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
uniformly distributed in
B_{X}
. We say that
X
is in
isotropic position
if the random vector
\mathbf{X}
is isotropic;
we emphasize that in asymptotic geometric analysis literature, an alternative definition involving normalization of the Lebesgue volume of
B_{X}
is used occasionally. It is a standard fact that any finite-dimensional normed space can be placed in isotropic position by applying an invertible linear transformation
(see, e.g.,
[
KL24a
]
).

2.4.
Gaussian concentration

We will need the following simple consequence of gaussian concentration for Lipschitz functions.

Fact 2.5
.

If
\mathbf{G}
is a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with i.i.d. standard normal entries, then for any
t>\sqrt{m}
,

(2.3)

\mathbb{P}\big(\|\mathbf{G}\|_{2}>t\big)\leqslant 2\exp\Big(-\frac{(t-\sqrt{m})^{2}}{2}\Big).

Proof.

Notice that the map
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
f(x)=\|x\|_{2}
, is
1
-Lipschitz, and it satisfies

\mathbb{E}\big[f(\mathbf{G})\big]\leqslant\sqrt{m}
. Using these observations, the result follows from the standard
gaussian concentration for Lipschitz functions (see, e.g.,
[
Le01
]
).
∎

2.5.
John’s theorem

Recall that if
X
and
Y
are two normed spaces of the same dimension, then their

is defined by

d_{\mathrm{BM}}(X,Y):=\inf\big\{\|T\|\cdot\|T^{-1}\|\colon T\colon X\to Y\text{ is an isomorphism}\big\}.

Also recall that, by a classical result due to John (see, e.g.,
[
JL01
]
), we have

d_{\mathrm{BM}}(X,\ell_{2}^{m})\leqslant\sqrt{m}
for any
m
-dimensional normed space
X
.
We will use this estimate in the following form.

Fact 2.6
.

Let
X=(\mathbb{R}^{m},\|\cdot\|_{X})
be an
m
-dimensional normed space. Then there exist vectors

x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that for any
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\in\mathbb{R}

(2.4)

\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leqslant\Big\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}x_{i}\Big\|_{X}\leqslant\sqrt{m}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

2.6.
Lovász local lemma

The last result we will need is the so-called asymmetric version of Lovász local lemma (see, e.g.,
[
AS16
]
).

Lemma 2.7

(Asymmetric Lovász local lemma)
.

Let
G
be a graph, and let
(A_{v})_{v\in V(G)}
be a collection of measurable events in some probability space
such that for every
v\in V(G)
, the event
A_{v}
is independent of the algebra generated by the events

\big\{A_{u}\colon u\not\in N_{G}(v)\cup\{v\}\big\}
. Assume that there exists
h\colon V\to[0,1)
such that
for any
v\in V(G)
,

(2.5)

\mathbb{P}(A_{v})\leqslant h(v)\prod_{u\in N_{G}(v)}\big(1-h(u)\big).

Then,

(2.6)

\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\bigcap_{v\in V(G)}A_{v}^{\complement}\Bigg)\geqslant\prod_{v\in V(G)}\big(1-h(v)\big).

3.
Preparatory lemmas

In this section we shall present three preparatory lemmas that are used in the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
We start with the following volumetric estimate.

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists an absolute constant
C_{B}>0
such that for any
m
-dimensional normed space

X=(\mathbb{R}^{m},\|\cdot\|_{X})
in isotropic position, we have
\lambda(B_{X})\leqslant C_{B}^{m}
, where
\lambda

denotes the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{U}
be a random vector uniformly distributed in
B_{X}
. Then
\mathbf{U}
is an isotropic, log-concave, random vector
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with density function
f:=\lambda(B_{X})^{-1}\mathbbm{1}_{B_{X}}
.
By Theorem
2.2
, there exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that

\frac{1}{2}\leqslant\mathbb{P}\big(\|\mathbf{U}\|_{2}\leqslant c\sqrt{m}\big)=\frac{\lambda\big(B_{X}\cap c\sqrt{m}\,B_{\ell^{m}_{2}}\big)}{\lambda(B_{X})}.

Therefore,

\lambda(B_{X})\leqslant 2\lambda\big(B_{X}\cap c\sqrt{m}\,B_{\ell^{m}_{2}}\big)\leqslant 2\,c^{m}\,\lambda\big(\sqrt{m}\,B_{\ell^{m}_{2}}\big)\leqslant 2\big(\sqrt{2c^{2}\pi e}\big)^{m}.\qed

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
X=(\mathbb{R}^{m},\|\cdot\|_{X})
be a normed space in isotropic position, let
k
be a positive integer,
and let
0<c_{1}<\frac{1}{2}
such that

(3.1)

k\geqslant\big(C_{B}\,L\big)^{\frac{4}{1-2c_{1}}},

where
C_{B}
is as in Lemma
3.1
, and
L
is as in Theorem
2.3
.
Finally, let
(\mathbf{Y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{Y}_{k})
be i.i.d. random vectors uniformly distributed in
B_{X}
. Then,

(3.2)

\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\Big\|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\Big\|_{X}\leqslant k^{c_{1}}\Bigg)\leqslant\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{1}{2}-c_{1}\Big)m\log k\Bigg).

Proof.

Let
g
denote the density function of the random vector
\mathbf{Y}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{Y}_{i}
.
Notice that
\mathbf{Y}
is isotropic and log-concave; consequently, by Theorem
2.3
, the function
g

is pointwise bounded by
L^{m}
. Hence, by Lemma
3.1
, we have

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\Big\|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\Big\|_{X}\leqslant k^{c_{1}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\Big\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\Big\|_{X}\leqslant k^{c_{1}-\frac{1}{2}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\int g(x)\mathbbm{1}_{k^{c_{1}-\frac{1}{2}}B_{X}}(x)\,dx\leqslant L^{m}\,\lambda\big(k^{c_{1}-\frac{1}{2}}B_{X}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\Big(\frac{C_{B}L}{k^{\frac{1}{2}-c_{1}}}\Big)^{m}\leqslant\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{1}{2}-c_{1}\Big)m\log k\Big).\qed

The next lemma complements Lemma
3.2
, and it will used when
k
is smaller than the threshold
appearing in the right-hand-side of (
3.1
).

Lemma 3.3
.

There exists a positive integer
m_{1}
with the following property. Let
m\geqslant m_{1}
be an integer, let

X=(\mathbb{R}^{m},\|\cdot\|_{X})
be a normed space in isotropic position, let
k\geqslant 2
be an integer,
and let
(\mathbf{Y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{Y}_{k})
be i.i.d. random vectors uniformly distributed in
B_{X}
. Then,

(3.4)

\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\Big\|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\Big\|_{X}\leqslant 1+\frac{1}{2\,m^{1-\kappa}}\Bigg)\leqslant\exp\big(-m^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\big),

where
\kappa
is as in Theorem
2.2
.

Proof.

Set
\gamma:=\frac{2-\sqrt{2}}{4}
,
\varepsilon:=\frac{1}{2\,m^{1-\kappa}}
,
and

\Gamma:=B_{X}\setminus\Big((1+\varepsilon)^{-1}(1-\gamma)\sqrt{k}\sqrt{m}\,B_{\ell_{2}^{m}}\Big)
. Notice that

(3.5)

\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\Big\|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\Big\|_{X}\leqslant 1+\varepsilon\Bigg)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\in(1-\gamma)\sqrt{k}\sqrt{m}\,B_{\ell^{m}_{2}}\Big)+\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\in(1+\varepsilon)\Gamma\Big).

As in the proof of Lemma
3.2
, we observe that the random vector

\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{Y}_{i}
is isotropic and log-concave. Therefore,

2.2
, if
m_{1}
is sufficiently large, we have

(3.6)

\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\in(1-\gamma)\sqrt{k}\sqrt{m}\,B_{\ell_{2}^{m}}\Bigg)=\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\Big\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\Big\|_{2}\leqslant(1-\gamma)\sqrt{m}\Bigg)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\exp\big(-m^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\big).

Notice that

(1-\gamma)\sqrt{2}=\frac{\sqrt{2}+1}{2}>1

Hence, by Theorem

\lambda(\Gamma)\leqslant C\exp\big(-m^{\kappa}\big)\,\lambda(B_{X}).

It follows immediately that, conditioned on any realization of

\frac{\lambda\big((1+\varepsilon)\Gamma\big)}{\lambda(B_{X})}\leqslant C(1+\varepsilon)^{m}\exp\big(-m^{\kappa}\big)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\exp\big(-m^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\big),

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

As mentioned, part
(A)
of Theorem
1.1
is due to Frankl and Maehara
[
FM86
]
.
For the convenience of the reader, we recall their argument. We set

(4.1)

\alpha_{0}:=\frac{1}{2}.

Fix an integer
\Delta\geqslant 3
, and let
N
be an arbitrary positive integer that is sufficiently large in terms of
\Delta
.
We define a tree
T
on
N
vertices with maximum degree
\Delta
as follows. There exists a root
r_{T}\in V(T)
such that,
setting
h_{0}:=\big\lceil\log\big((N-1)\frac{\Delta-2}{\Delta}+1\big)/\log(\Delta-1)\big\rceil
, the following hold.

\bullet

For every
v\in V(T)
, we have
\mathrm{dist}_{T}(r_{T},v)\leqslant h_{0}
.

\bullet

For every
v\in V(T)
with
\mathrm{dist}_{T}(r_{T},v)\leqslant h_{0}-2
, we have
|N_{T}(v)|=\Delta
.

Notice, in particular, that if
N=\frac{\Delta^{n+1}-1}{\Delta-1}
, then
T
is just the
\Delta
-regular tree of height
n
.
Let
X=(\mathbb{R}^{m},\|\cdot\|_{X})
be a normed space for which there exists a geometric embedding
\zeta\colon V(T)\to X
. Since
T
is a bipartite graph (being a tree), there exists an independent
set
V_{0}\subseteq V(T)
with
|V_{0}|\geqslant\frac{N}{2}
. Using the fact that every node of
T
has distance from
r_{T}

at most
h_{0}
, we see that

(4.2)

\bigcup_{v\in V_{0}}B_{X}\big(\zeta(v),1/2\big)\subseteq B_{X}\big(\zeta(r_{T}),h_{0}+1/2\big).

On the other hand, since
\zeta
is a geometric embedding, for every
u,v\in V_{0}
with
u\neq v
we have that

B_{X}\big(\zeta(u),1/2\big)\cap B_{X}\big(\zeta(v),1/2\big)=\emptyset
.
Thus, denoting by
\lambda
the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, by (
4.2
),

\frac{N}{2}\lambda\big(B_{X}(0,1/2)\big)\leqslant\lambda\big(B_{X}(0,h_{0}+1/2)\big),

which is easily seen to imply, if
N
is sufficiently large, that

\dim(X)=m>\alpha_{0}\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}
, as desired.

We proceed to the proof of part
(B)
. We shall construct a random embedding of a given tree
T
on
N
vertices
with maximum degree
\Delta
in the normed space
X=(\mathbb{R}^{m},\|\cdot\|_{X})
, and we shall show that with positive probability
this is a geometric embedding. To that end we start by setting

(4.3)

\alpha_{1}:=64,\ \ \ \ c_{1}:=\frac{1}{4},\ \ \ \ c_{2}:=\min\Big\{\frac{1}{4},\frac{\kappa}{4}\Big\},

where
\kappa
is as in Theorem
2.2
. The parameter
N_{0}
will be determined in the course
of the proof. For notational convenience, we shall denote by
V
the vertex set of
T
, and by
E
its edge set.
Clearly, we may assume that
\dim(X)=m=\big\lceil\alpha_{1}\log N/\log\log N\big\rceil
and that
X
is in isotropic position.

4.1.
Construction of the random embedding

Let
(\mathbf{Y}_{e})_{e\in E}
be a collection of i.i.d. random vectors uniformly distributed in
B_{X}
.
Also let
(\mathbf{g}_{i}^{e})_{i\in[m],e\in E}
be a collection of i.i.d. scalar standard normal random variables that are
independent of
(\mathbf{Y}_{e})_{e\in E}
. (As usual,
[m]:=\{1,\dots,m\}
denotes the discrete interval of length
m
.)
Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be as in Fact
2.6
, and define for every
e\in E
,

(4.4)

\mathbf{G}_{e}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{g}_{i}^{e}\,x_{i}.

Fix a distinguished vertex
r_{T}\in V
that we view as the root of
T
, and set

(4.5)

\delta:=\exp\Big(-\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{c_{1}}{2}\Big)\log^{c_{2}}N\Big).

We define the random embedding
\boldsymbol{\zeta}\colon V\to X
by

\boldsymbol{\zeta}(v):=\Big(1-\frac{1}{\log N}\Big)\sum_{e\in P(r_{T},v)}\mathbf{Y}_{e}+\delta\sum_{e\in P(r_{T},v)}\mathbf{G}_{e}

with the convention that the sum over the empty set is equal to the zero vector.
(Here,
P(r_{T},v)
is as in Definition
2.1
.) For every
u,v\in V
with
u\neq v
, define the “good” event

\mathcal{A}_{u,v}:=\begin{cases}\big[\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}(u)-\boldsymbol{\zeta}(v)\|_{X}\leqslant 1\big]&\text{if }\,\{u,v\}\in E,\\
\big[\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}(u)-\boldsymbol{\zeta}(v)\|_{X}>1\big]&\text{if }\,\{u,v\}\not\in E.\end{cases}

Part
(B)
of Theorem
1.1
will follow once we show that

(4.6)

\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in V\\
u\neq v\end{subarray}}\mathcal{A}_{u,v}\Bigg)>0.

4.2.
Auxiliary events

Set

(4.7)

\ell_{0}:=\exp\big(\log^{c_{2}}N\big)\ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ k_{0}:=\max\Big\{3,\big\lceil(C_{B}L)^{4/(1-2c_{1})}\big\rceil,\big\lceil 4^{1/c_{1}}\big\rceil\Big\},

where
C_{B}
is as in Lemma
3.1
, and
L
is as in Theorem
2.3
.
Taking
N_{0}
sufficiently large, we may assume that
k_{0}\leqslant\ell_{0}
.

Next, for every
u,v\in V
with
2\leqslant\mathrm{dist}_{T}(u,v)\leqslant\ell_{0}
, define the event

\mathcal{L}_{u,v}:=\begin{cases}\Big[\big\|\underset{e\in P(r_{T},u)}{\sum}\mathbf{Y}_{e}-\underset{e\in P(r_{T},v)}{\sum}\mathbf{Y}_{e}\big\|_{X}\leqslant 1+\frac{1}{2\,m^{1-\kappa}}\Big]&\text{if }\,2\leqslant\mathrm{dist}_{T}(u,v)\leqslant k_{0},\\
\Big[\big\|\underset{e\in P(r_{T},u)}{\sum}\mathbf{Y}_{e}-\underset{e\in P(r_{T},v)}{\sum}\mathbf{Y}_{e}\big\|_{X}\leqslant\mathrm{dist}_{T}(u,v)^{c_{1}}\Big]&\text{if }\,k_{0}<\mathrm{dist}_{T}(u,v)\leqslant\ell_{0},\end{cases}

where
\kappa
is as in Theorem
2.2
. Our goal in this subsection is to show that

(4.8)

\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in V\\
2\leqslant\mathrm{dist}_{T}(u,v)\leqslant\ell_{0}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{L}_{u,v}^{\complement}\Bigg)>0.

4.2.1.
The “path” graph

Define the “path” graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
, which will act as the dependency graph in our application of the Lovász local lemma, by setting

\mathcal{V}:=\big\{\{u,v\}\colon u,v\in V\text{ and }2\leqslant\mathrm{dist}_{T}(u,v)\leqslant\ell_{0}\big\},

\mathcal{E}:=\Big\{\big\{\{u,v\},\{u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\}\big\}\colon\{u,v\},\{u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\}\in\mathcal{V}\text{ and }P(u,v)\cap P(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\neq\emptyset\Big\}.

Moreover, for every
k\in\{2,\dots,\ell_{0}\}
set

\mathcal{V}_{k}:=\big\{\{u,v\}\in\mathcal{V}\colon\mathrm{dist}_{T}(u,v)=k\big\}
.

Claim 4.1
.

Let
k,\ell\in\{2,\dots,\ell_{0}\}
, and let
\{u,v\}\in\mathcal{V_{\ell}}
. Then,

|N_{\mathcal{G}}(\{u,v\})\cap\mathcal{V}_{k}|\leqslant\min\big\{N^{2},5\ell k(\Delta-1)^{k-1}\big\}.

Proof.

Clearly,
|N_{\mathcal{G}}(\{u,v\})\cap\mathcal{V}_{k}|
is upper bounded by
N^{2}
.
Thus, it suffices to show that

(4.9)

|N_{\mathcal{G}}(\{u,v\})\cap\mathcal{V}_{k}|\leqslant 5\ell k(\Delta-1)^{k-1}.

Let
s\in[k]
be arbitrary, and set

a_{s}:=\big|\big\{\{u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\}\in N_{\mathcal{G}}(\{u,v\})\cap\mathcal{V}_{k}\colon|P(u,v)\cap P(u,v)|=s\big\}\big|
.

We shall consider various cases. First, assume that
s>\min\{\ell,k\}
; then, clearly,

(4.10)

a_{s}=0.

Next, assume that
s=\ell\leqslant k
; then, since
\ell,k\geqslant 2
, we have

(4.11)

a_{\ell}\leqslant\sum_{x=0}^{k-\ell}(\Delta-1)^{x}(\Delta-1)^{k-\ell-x}=(\Delta-1)^{k-\ell}(k-\ell+1)\leqslant\frac{\ell k}{4}(\Delta-1)^{k-1}.

If
s=\ell-1\leqslant k
, then

(4.12)

a_{s}\leqslant 2\big((\Delta-1)^{k-\ell+1}+(\Delta-2)(\Delta-1)^{k-\ell}(k-\ell+1)\big)\leqslant\ell k(\Delta-1)^{k-1}.

Finally, if
s\leqslant\min\{\ell-2,k\}
, then

(4.13)

\displaystyle a_{s}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\big((\Delta-1)^{k-s}+(\Delta-2)(\Delta-1)^{k-s-1}(k-s)\big)+

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ +(\ell-s-1)\big(2(\Delta-2)(\Delta-1)^{k-s-1}+(\Delta-2)^{2}(\Delta-1)^{k-s-2}(k-s-1)\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant 3\ell k(\Delta-2)(\Delta-1)^{k-s-1}.

By (
4.10
) and (
4.13
), we have

(4.14)

\sum_{s=1}^{\ell-2}a_{s}\leqslant 3\ell k(\Delta-1)^{k-1}.

The desired estimate (
4.9
) follows from (
4.10
)–(
4.12
) and (
4.14
).
∎

4.2.2.
Application of asymmetric Lovász local lemma

Set

k_{1}:=\Big\lfloor\frac{\log N}{\log(\Delta-1)}\Big\rfloor;

since
N_{0}
can be taken sufficiently large, we may assume that for every
k\in\{2,\dots,k_{1}\}
we have

(4.15)

6k\ell_{0}(\Delta-1)^{k}\leqslant N^{2}.

For every
k\in\{2,\dots,\ell_{0}\}
set

b_{k}:=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2^{k+1}6\ell_{0}k(\Delta-1)^{k}}&\text{if }\,2\leqslant k\leqslant k_{1},\\
\frac{3}{2\pi^{2}k^{2}N^{2}}&\text{if }\,k_{1}<k\leqslant\ell_{0},\end{cases}

and define
h\colon\mathcal{V}\to[0,1)
by
h(\{u,v\})=b_{\mathrm{dist}_{T}(u,v)}
.
By the choice of
(b_{k})_{k=2}^{\ell_{0}}
and (
4.15
), we see that

\sum_{k=2}^{\ell_{0}}b_{k}\cdot\min\{N^{2},6\ell_{0}k(\Delta-1)^{k}\}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
.
Hence, by Claim
4.1
, for every
\{u,v\}\in\mathcal{V}
,

(4.16)

\prod_{\{u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\}\in N_{\mathcal{G}}(\{u,v\})}\big(1-h(\{u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\})\big)\geqslant\prod_{k=2}^{\ell_{0}}(1-b_{k})^{\min\{N^{2},5\ell k(\Delta-1)^{k-1}\}}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}.

Claim 4.2
.

For every
\{u,v\}\in\mathcal{V}
, we have
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{u,v})\leqslant\frac{1}{2}h(\{u,v\})
.

Proof.

Fix
\{u,v\}\in\mathcal{V}
, and set
k:=\mathrm{dist}_{T}(u,v)
. We distinguish three cases.

First, assume that
k\in\{2,\dots,k_{0}\}
. Since the random vectors

\sum_{e\in P(r_{T},u)}\mathbf{Y}_{e}-\sum_{e\in P(r_{T},v)}\mathbf{Y}_{e}
and

\sum_{e\in P(u,v)}\mathbf{Y}_{e}
have the same distribution, then provided that
N_{0}
is sufficiently large,
by Lemma
3.3
,

(4.17)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{u,v})\leqslant\exp\big(-m^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\big)\leqslant\exp\Big(-\alpha_{1}^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\frac{\log^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}N}{(\log\log N)^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}}\Big).

On the other hand, by the choice of
\ell_{0}
in (
4.7
),

(4.18)

\frac{h(\{u,v\})}{2}\geqslant b_{k_{0}}\geqslant\exp\big(-\log 24-k_{0}\log(2\Delta-2)-\log k_{0}-\log^{c_{2}}N\big).

Since
c_{2}<\frac{\kappa}{2}
and using the fact that
N_{0}
can be taken sufficiently large, the claim
follows in this case by (
4.17
) and (
4.18
).

Next, assume that
k\in\{k_{0}+1,\dots,k_{1}\}
. Using again the fact that

\sum_{e\in P(r_{T},u)}\mathbf{Y}_{e}-\sum_{e\in P(r_{T},v)}\mathbf{Y}_{e}
and

\sum_{e\in P(u,v)}\mathbf{Y}_{e}
have the same distribution, by Lemma
3.2
,
the choice of
k_{0}
in (
4.7
) and the fact that
c_{1}\leqslant\frac{1}{4}
, we obtain that

(4.19)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{u,v})\leqslant\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{1}{2}-c_{1}\Big)m\log k\Big)\leqslant\exp\Big(-\frac{\alpha_{1}}{8}\cdot\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}\log k\Big).

Since the function
f(x)=\frac{x}{\log x}
is increasing for
x\geqslant 3
and
k>k_{0}\geqslant 3
, we see that

\displaystyle\frac{h(\{u,v\})}{2}

\displaystyle=\exp\big(-\log 24-k\log(2\Delta-2)-\log k-\log^{c_{2}}N\big)

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(-\Big(\frac{\log 24}{\log k}+\frac{k}{\log k}\log(2\Delta-2)+1+\frac{\log^{c_{2}}N}{\log k}\Big)\log k\Big)

\displaystyle\geqslant\exp\Big(-\Big(\frac{\log 24}{\log 3}+\frac{k_{1}}{\log k_{1}}\log(2\Delta-2)+1+\frac{\log^{c_{2}}N}{\log k}\Big)\log k\Big)

\displaystyle\geqslant\exp\Big(-\Big(4+\frac{\log N}{\log\log N-\log\log(\Delta-1)}\cdot\frac{\log(2(\Delta-1))}{\log(\Delta-1)}+\frac{\log^{c_{2}}N}{\log k}\Big)\log k\Big).

Taking
N_{0}
sufficiently large and observing that
\frac{\log(2(\Delta-1))}{\log(\Delta-1)}\leqslant 2

and
\frac{\alpha_{1}}{8}>4
, we conclude that

(4.20)

\frac{h(\{u,v\})}{2}\geqslant\exp\Big(-\Big(4+4\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}+\log^{c_{2}}N\Big)\log k\Big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:021}}}{{\geqslant}}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{u,v}).

Finally, assume that
k\in\{k_{1}+1,\dots,\ell_{0}\}
. As before, since

\sum_{e\in P(r_{T},u)}\mathbf{Y}_{e}-\sum_{e\in P(r_{T},v)}\mathbf{Y}_{e}
and

\sum_{e\in P(u,v)}\mathbf{Y}_{e}
have the same distribution, by Lemma
3.2
,
the choice of
k_{0}
in (
4.7
) and the fact that
k>k_{0}
and
c_{1}\leqslant\frac{1}{4}
, we have

(4.21)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{u,v})\leqslant\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{1}{2}-c_{1}\Big)m\log k\Big)\leqslant\exp\Big(-\frac{\alpha_{1}}{8}\cdot\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}\log k\Big).

Taking
N_{0}
sufficiently large, we may assume that

\log k\geqslant\log\log N-\log\log(\Delta-1)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\log\log N
.
Thus, by (
4.21
) and the choice of
\alpha_{1}
in (
4.3
), we have that

(4.22)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{u,v})\leqslant\exp(-4\log N).

Since
k_{1}<k\leqslant\ell_{0}
, taking
N_{0}
sufficiently large, we see that

(4.23)

\displaystyle\frac{h(\{u,v\})}{2}

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(-\log\Big(\frac{4\pi^{2}}{3}\Big)-2\log k-2\log N\Big)

\displaystyle\geqslant\exp\Big(-3\log N\Big).

Thus, this final case the claim follows from (
4.22
) and (
4.23
).
∎

By Claim
4.2
and inequality (
4.16
), for every
\{u,v\}\in\mathcal{V}
,

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{u,v})\leqslant h(\{u,v\})\prod_{\{u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\}\in N_{\mathcal{G}}(\{u,v\})}\big(1-h(\{u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\})\big).

Therefore, inequality (
4.8
) follows from Lemma
2.7
.

4.3.
Completion of the proof of part
(B)
of Theorem
1.1

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be the vectors selected in Subsection
4.1
.
It is convenient to introduce an auxiliary norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}^{*}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
as follows.
Given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
written as
x=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}x_{i}
, we set

\|x\|_{2}^{*}=(a_{1}^{2}+\dots+a_{m}^{2})^{1/2}
. By (
2.4
), we see that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

(4.24)

\|x\|_{2}^{*}\leqslant\|x\|_{X}\leqslant\sqrt{m}\|x\|_{2}^{*}.

Moreover, recalling (
4.4
) and Fact
2.5
, for every subset
E^{\prime}
of the edge-set
E
of
T
and
every
t>\sqrt{m}
,

(4.25)

\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\Big\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{|E^{\prime}|}}\sum_{e\in E^{\prime}}\mathbf{G}_{e}\Big\|_{2}^{*}>t\Bigg)\leqslant 2\exp\Big(-\frac{(t-\sqrt{m})^{2}}{2}\Big).

Now, set

\mathcal{L}:=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in V\\
2\leqslant\mathrm{dist}_{T}(u,v)\leqslant\ell_{0}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{L}_{u,v}^{\complement}.

Note that in order to show (
4.6
), it suffices to prove that for every
u,v\in V
with
u\neq v
,

(4.26)

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{A}_{u,v}^{\complement}\,\big|\,\mathcal{L}\big)\leqslant\frac{1}{N^{2}}.

To that end, let
u,v\in V
with
u\neq v
and set
k:=\mathrm{dist}_{T}(u,v)
.
Notice that

(4.27)

\boldsymbol{\zeta}(u)-\boldsymbol{\zeta}(v)=\Big(1-\frac{1}{\log N}\Big)\Big(\sum_{e\in P(r_{T},u)}\mathbf{Y}_{e}-\sum_{e\in P(r_{T},v)}\mathbf{Y}_{e}\Big)+\delta\Big(\sum_{e\in P(r_{T},u)}\mathbf{G}_{e}-\sum_{e\in P(r_{T},v)}\mathbf{G}_{e}\Big);

moreover, due to the independence of
(\mathbf{Y}_{e})_{e\in E}
and
(\mathbf{G}_{e})_{e\in E}
, for every measurable

A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

(4.28)

\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{e\in P(r_{T},u)}\mathbf{G}_{e}-\sum_{e\in P(r_{T},v)}\mathbf{G}_{e}\in A\;\Big|\,\mathcal{L}\Big)=\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{e\in P(u,v)}\mathbf{G}_{e}\in A\Big).

We consider four cases according to the value of
k
.

Case 1:
k=1

In this case, we have
e:=\{u,v\}\in E
. Since
\mathbf{Y}_{e}
is uniformly chosen from the unit ball of
X
,
by the definition of
\mathcal{A}_{u,v}
and (
4.27
) and (
4.28
), we have

(4.29)

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{A}_{u,v}^{\complement}\,\big|\,\mathcal{L}\big)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\big(\|\mathbf{G}_{e}\|_{X}>\delta^{-1}\log^{-1}N\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:026}}}{{\leqslant}}\mathbb{P}\big(\|\mathbf{G}_{e}\|_{2}^{*}>m^{-\frac{1}{2}}\delta^{-1}\log^{-1}N\big).

Since
N_{0}
can be taken sufficiently large, we see that

\displaystyle\frac{\big(m^{-\frac{1}{2}}\delta^{-1}\log^{-1}N-\sqrt{m}\big)^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ =\frac{1}{2}\Big(\exp\Big(\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{c_{1}}{2}\Big)\log^{c_{2}}N-\frac{1}{2}\log m-\log\log N\Big)-\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\log m\Big)\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \geqslant\exp\big((1-2c_{1})\log^{c_{2}}N\big)>2\log N+\log 2.

By (
4.25
) and (
4.29
), we conclude that

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{A}_{u,v}^{\complement}\,\big|\,\mathcal{L}\big)\leqslant 2\exp\big(-2\log N-\log 2\big)=\frac{1}{N^{2}}.

Case 2:
k\in\{2,\dots,k_{0}\}

By the definitions of
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{A}_{u,v}
and (
4.27
), (
4.28
) and (
4.24
),

(4.30)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{A}_{u,v}^{\complement}\,\big|\,\mathcal{L}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\delta\Big\|\sum_{e\in P(u,v)}\mathbf{G}_{e}\Big\|_{X}\geqslant\Big(1-\frac{1}{\log N}\Big)\Big(1+\frac{1}{2\,m^{1-\kappa}}\Big)-1\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\Big\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{e\in P(u,v)}\mathbf{G}_{e}\Big\|_{2}^{*}\geqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{k_{0}}\sqrt{m}\delta}\Big(\Big(1-\frac{1}{\log N}\Big)\Big(1+\frac{1}{2\,m^{1-\kappa}}\Big)-1\Big)\Bigg).

Taking
N_{0}
sufficiently large and invoking the definitions of
m
,
\ell_{0},k_{0}
and
\delta
, we see that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k_{0}}\sqrt{m}\delta}\Big(\Big(1-\frac{1}{\log N}\Big)\Big(1+\frac{1}{2\,m^{1-\kappa}}\Big)-1\Big)-\sqrt{m}\Big)^{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\,\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k_{0}}\sqrt{m}\delta}\cdot\frac{1}{4\,m^{1-\kappa}}-\sqrt{m}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\Big(\exp\Big(\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{c_{1}}{2}\Big)\log^{c_{2}}N-\Big(\frac{3}{2}-\kappa\Big)\log m+\log\Big(\frac{1}{4\sqrt{k_{0}}}\Big)\Big)-\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\log m\Big)\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant\exp\big((1-2c_{1})\log^{c_{2}}N\big)>2\log N+\log 2.

Hence, by (
4.25
) and (
4.30
), we obtain that

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{A}_{u,v}^{\complement}\,\big|\,\mathcal{L}\big)\leqslant 2\exp(-2\log N-\log 2)=\frac{1}{N^{2}}.

Case 3:
k\in\{k_{0}+1,\dots,\ell_{0}\}

Using (
4.24
), the fact that
1-\frac{1}{\log N}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}
and that in this case we have that

k>k_{0}\geqslant 4^{\frac{1}{c_{1}}}
, we see that

(4.31)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{A}_{u,v}^{\complement}\,\big|\,\mathcal{L}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\delta\Big\|\sum_{e\in P(u,v)}\mathbf{G}_{e}\Big\|_{X}\geqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{\log N}\right)k^{c_{1}}-1\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\Big\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{e\in P(u,v)}\mathbf{G}_{e}\Big\|_{X}\geqslant\frac{k^{c_{1}-\frac{1}{2}}}{4\delta}\Bigg)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\Big\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{e\in P(u,v)}\mathbf{G}_{e}\Big\|_{2}^{*}\geqslant\frac{\ell_{0}^{c_{1}-\frac{1}{2}}}{4\delta\sqrt{m}}\Bigg).

On the other hand, by the definitions of
\ell_{0}
and
\delta
,

\frac{1}{\delta}\,\ell_{0}^{c_{1}-\frac{1}{2}}=\exp\Big(\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{c_{1}}{2}\Big)\log^{c_{2}}N+\Big(c_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)\log^{c_{2}}N\Big)=\exp\Big(\frac{c_{1}}{2}\log^{c_{2}}N\Big),

and so, taking
N_{0}
sufficiently large and using our starting assumption on the dimension
m
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,\Big(\frac{\ell_{0}^{c_{1}-\frac{1}{2}}}{4\delta\sqrt{m}}-\sqrt{m}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\Big(\exp\Big(\frac{c_{1}}{2}\log^{c_{2}}N-\frac{1}{2}\log m+\log\frac{1}{4}\Big)-\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\log m\Big)\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant\exp\Big(\frac{c_{1}}{2}\log^{c_{2}}N\Big)>2\log N+\log 2.

By (
4.25
) and (
4.31
), we obtain that

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{A}_{u,v}^{\complement}\,\big|\,\mathcal{L}\big)\leqslant 2\exp\big(-2\log N-\log 2\big)=\frac{1}{N^{2}}.

Case 4:
k>\ell_{0}

By (
4.27
), (
4.28
) and (
4.24
) and the fact that
\mathbf{G}_{e}
is symmetric for every
e\in E
, we have

(4.32)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{A}_{u,v}^{\complement}\,\big|\,\mathcal{L}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}(u)-\boldsymbol{\zeta}(v)\|_{X}\leqslant 1\,\big|\,\mathcal{L}\big)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\big(\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}(u)-\boldsymbol{\zeta}(v)\|_{2}^{*}\leqslant 1\,\big|\,\mathcal{L}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Big(\delta\sum_{e\in P(u,v)}\mathbf{G}_{e}\in-\Big(1-\frac{1}{\log N}\Big)\Big(\sum_{e\in P(r_{T},u)}\mathbf{Y}_{e}-\sum_{e\in P(r_{T},v)}\mathbf{Y}_{e}\Big)+B_{\|\cdot\|_{2}^{*}}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{L}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}\Big(\delta\sum_{e\in P(u,v)}\mathbf{G}_{e}\in B_{\|\cdot\|_{2}^{*}}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{L}\Big)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\Big\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{e\in P(u,v)}\mathbf{G}_{e}\Big\|_{2}^{*}\leqslant\frac{1}{\delta\sqrt{\ell_{0}}}\Bigg).

Next observe that, by the choices of
\ell_{0}
and
\delta
,

(4.33)

\frac{1}{\delta\sqrt{\ell_{0}}}=\exp\Big(-\frac{c_{1}}{2}\log^{c_{2}}N\Big).

Moreover, the random variable
\big\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{e\in P(u,v)}\mathbf{G}_{e}\big\|_{2}^{*}

has the same distribution with
\|\mathbf{G}\|_{2}
, where
\mathbf{G}
denotes a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{m}

with i.i.d. standard normal entries. Thus, by (
4.32
),

(4.34)

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{A}_{u,v}^{\complement}\,\big|\,\mathcal{L}\big)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\left(\|\mathbf{G}\|_{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{\delta\sqrt{\ell_{0}}}\right).

Since the density function
of
\mathbf{G}
is pointwise bounded by
1
, this implies

(4.35)

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{A}_{u,v}^{\complement}\,\big|\,\mathcal{L}\big)\leqslant\Big(\frac{C_{B}}{\delta\sqrt{\ell_{0}}}\Big)^{m},

where
C_{B}
is as in Lemma
3.1
. Finally, taking
N_{0}
sufficiently large, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{A}_{u,v}^{\complement}\,\big|\,\mathcal{L}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\Big(m\Big(-\frac{c_{1}}{2}\log^{c_{2}}N+\log C_{B}\Big)\Big)\leqslant\exp\Big(-m\frac{c_{1}}{4}\log^{c_{2}}N\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\Big(-\frac{\alpha_{1}c_{1}}{4}\cdot\frac{\log^{1+c_{2}}N}{\log\log N}\Big)\leqslant\exp\big(-2\log N\big)=\frac{1}{N^{2}}.

The above cases are exhaustive, and so the entire proof of Theorem
1.1
is completed.

.

,

4.7
).
