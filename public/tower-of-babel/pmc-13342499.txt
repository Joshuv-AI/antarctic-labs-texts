The plastidial PHS1-DPE1 complex drives efficient malto-oligosaccharides synthesis in rice starch metabolism
Authors: Jian Liu, Xinru Wu, Haitao He, Xi Yang, Yuanhuai Hu, Fei Zhang, Rong Fan, Xuecui Wang, Shenao Yang, Lian Xiong, Delin Zhang, Ping Yin, Jianping Guo, Zhu Liu, Junjie Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Starch serves as a vital energy reserve in plants. During its biosynthesis, malto-oligosaccharides (MOS) are essential primers. One of the key pathways for MOS production involves plastidial α-glucan phosphorylase (PHS1/Pho1) and disproportionating enzyme (DPE1). However, the functional relationship between these enzymes is unclear. Here, we demonstrate that rice PHS1 and DPE1 assemble into a multimeric complex. Cryo-EM structures of the PHS1-DPE1 complex reveal an assembly mechanism and suggest a potential substrate tunnel. Biochemical assays show the complex dramatically enhances catalytic efficiency over individual enzymes. Single-molecule fluorescence resonance energy transfer (smFRET) visualizes conformational dynamics, enabling rapid substrate transfer between the enzymes. We further identify the unique L80 loop in PHS1 as a potential regulator. Its deletion reduces catalytic efficiency and prolongs conformational state lifetimes during substrate transfer, thereby reducing the production of longer MOSs. Our findings establish that the PHS1-DPE1 complex facilitates efficient MOS primer synthesis through efficient substrate transfer or diffusion between the two enzymes, providing mechanistic insight into a critical step of starch biosynthesis with agronomic implications.

Introduction

Starch is a key component of the human diet and holds significant importance in various industrial applications1. It is composed of glucose polymers, primarily amylopectin and amylose, and accumulates in specialized organelles such as amyloplasts in storage tissues (e.g., roots, tubers, and seeds) and chloroplasts in green leaves2–4. The synthesis of starch involves two main stages: initiation and elongation. During elongation, enzymes such as starch synthase (SS), starch branching enzyme (SBE), and debranching enzyme (DBE) play critical roles5,6. Starch granule initiation requires the synthesis of malto-oligosaccharides (MOS) as primers7,8; however, the molecular mechanism underlying MOS generation remains largely unknown.

The α-glucan phosphorylase PHS1 plays a crucial role in MOS metabolism by catalyzing a reversible reaction. In this reaction, glucose-1-phosphate (G1P) acts as a donor to add glucose moieties to the non-reducing end of α-glucans, or is released as a product during α-glucan phosphorolysis (Fig. 1a)9. Accumulating evidence indicates that PHS1 plays a synthetic, rather than degradative, role in starch metabolism9,10. In Chlamydomonas, mutations in PHS1 (also named PHOB) resulted in lower starch accumulation and abnormal morphology of starch granules11. In rice, mutants lacking PHS1 exhibit a shrunken endosperm phenotype specifically under cold stress conditions12, characterized by the accumulation of short MOS and reduced starch levels during early seed development12,13; and PHS1 demonstrates significant synthetic activity even in the presence of vast excess amounts of inorganic phosphate (Pi)14. In wheat endosperm, PHS1 is associated with the initiation of reserve starch accumulation15, and is particularly exclusive to the formation of B-type granules, as phs1 mutants produce fewer B-type granules compared to the wild type16. In the potato tuber, loss of phs1 results in smaller starch granules and lower starch content17. PHS1 is known to form protein complexes with various starch biosynthetic enzymes20. In rice, co-immunoprecipitation evidence suggests a direct interaction between PHS1 and the SBE SBEIIa; however, this interaction appears weak or transient, as it is not detected by gel filtration21,22. In wheat, PHS1 interacts with SBEs SBEI and SBEIIb in a phosphorylation-dependent manner23,24. In barley SBEIIa/b mutants, PHS1 co-precipitates with multiple starch biosynthetic enzymes, including SBEI, SSI, and SSIIa25, but whether these associations are direct or indirect remains elusive. In maize, the interactions of PHS1 with SSI, SBEI, and SBEIIa are detectable only upon SBEIIb deletion and are likely mediated by glucan bridges rather than direct protein–protein contacts26. In Arabidopsis, PHS1 interacts with the starch initiation factor SS4 independently of glucan mediation27. These interactions with biosynthetic enzymes strongly indicate a role for PHS1 in starch synthesis9,10. In Arabidopsis SBE mutants, compromised in starch synthesis, the loss of PHS1 leads to excessive soluble glucan accumulation, impaired insoluble glucan formation, and severe growth defects, highlighting its essential role in maltodextrin turnover28. A unique structural feature of plastidial PHS1 is the presence of the L80 segment29, which has been proposed to function in phosphorylation, protein stability, or the modulation of substrate binding preference30–32. Despite these findings, the molecular mechanisms underlying MOS metabolism by PHS1, as well as the functional role of its L80 region, remain largely unknown.

The disproportionating enzyme DPE1 is an α−1,4-δ-glucanotransferase that catalyzes the cleavage of the α−1,4-glucosidic bond of α-glucan, transferring the glucanosyl group to the nonreducing end of another glucan or free glucose (Fig. 1a)33. In Arabidopsis and potato, the dpe1 mutants exhibit a starch-excess phenotype, characterized by a reduced rate of starch degradation and the accumulation of maltotriose, suggesting a key role for DPE1 in starch degradation and MOS metabolism34,35. In rice, while DPE1 deficiency alone does not significantly affect MOS mobilization or starch metabolism, the knockout of DPE1 in the pho1 mutant completely blocks MOS mobilization and disrupts endosperm development, resulting in empty and starch-less endosperm13,36. Biophysical and in vivo analyses demonstrated that rice DPE1 interacts with PHS1 through co-immunoprecipitation, reciprocal pulldown, and co-elution chromatography, confirming the formation of a protein complex in developing seeds37. Furthermore, the L80 region of PHS1 was found not to be responsible for this interaction37. Subsequently, a similar interaction relationship between PHS1 and DPE1 was validated in sweet potato38. Together, these interactions coordinately regulate MOS mobilization, thereby controlling starch granule initiation13,37,38. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the association between PHS1 and DPE1, as well as their coordinated function in MOS metabolism, remain largely elusive.

In this study, we successfully reconstituted the rice PHS1–DPE1 complex in vitro, demonstrating its high MOS synthetic activity, and determined the cryo-EM structures of the complex. Structural, kinetic, and smFRET analyses collectively suggest that the PHS1–DPE1 multimeric assembly facilitates the synthesis of longer MOS chains, thereby promoting starch synthesis. Interestingly, the L80 loop in PHS1 appears to function as a potential regulator, modulating the intermediates transfer between the enzymes via either intra- or intermolecular pathways. These findings uncover the molecular basis of MOS primer production in starch synthesis.

Results

Rice PHS1 interacts with DPE1 to form a multimeric complex to drive efficient MOS production

PHS1 and DPE1 belong to different enzymatic families that catalyze distinct biochemical reactions on MOS metabolism in the plastid (Fig. 1a, b). Protein sequence alignment of PHS1 and DPE1 across higher plants revealed high similarities (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Fig. 2), suggesting their evolutionarily conserved role in starch metabolism. Studies have revealed the coordinated function of PHS1 and DPE1 in MOS synthesis in rice and sweet potato37,38, but the underlying molecular basis remains unclear. To this end, we attempted to assemble the PHS1–DPE1 complex and investigate the structural and biochemical characteristics of the complex in rice. Rice PHS1 (residues: R45-P978) and DPE1 (residues: A38-L594), without the N-terminal transit peptide, were individually expressed in E.coli BL21 (DE3), and purified to homogeneity using sequential nickel affinity, ion exchange, and size exclusion chromatography (SEC) on a Superose 6 column, with the affinity tags retained (Fig. 1b, c). According to SEC analysis, PHS1 and DPE1 each exist as dimers in solution. Their elution volumes (14.7 mL for PHS1 and 15.8 mL for DPE1) corresponded to molecular masses of approximately 240 kDa and 160 kDa, respectively, as calibrated against standard protein markers (Fig. 1c). These values are roughly twice the predicted masses of their monomers (PHS1: ~110 kDa; DPE1: ~70 kDa), confirming their dimeric states. Upon incubation, the two proteins formed a heteromeric complex that eluted earlier with a major peak at 12.5 mL, corresponding to a molecular weight of ~800 kDa. This significant size increase indicates the complex is an octamer, most likely with a 4:4 stoichiometry comprising a dimer of PHS1 dimers and a dimer of DPE1 dimers. We further assessed the complex’s stability and oligomerization state by native PAGE and analytical ultracentrifugation (AUC). Native PAGE showed broad, smeared bands, suggesting heterogeneous assemblies (Supplementary Fig. 3a). AUC resolved this heterogeneity into distinct species with molecular weights of ~306 kDa (a putative 2:2 heterotetramer), ~167 kDa (a putative 1:1 heterodimer), and additional ~91 kDa PHS1 and ~55 kDa DPE1 monomers, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3b). The enzymatic activities for PHS1 and DPE1 were examined. The MOS molecules with a degree of polymerization (DP) ranging from 2 to 7 (G2–G7) were used as substrates. PHS1 catalyzes reversible reactions using G1P as substrate to add glucose molecules to the nonreducing ends to elongate the glucan chains and release Pi, or vice versa (Fig. 1a). We investigated the enzymatic activity of PHS1 by the detection of free Pi using the phosphoantimonylmolybdenum blue method39. In the presence of G1P, PHS1 exhibits enzymatic activities on MOSs ranging from G4 to G7 but with no activity on either G2 or G3 (Fig. 1d), suggesting the minimal MOS substrate of PHS1 is with a DP value of 4. The PHS1–DPE1 complex exhibits strikingly increased enzymatic activities on MOSs from G4 to G7, and more significant activity on G3 compared to PHS1 alone (Fig. 1d), indicating that DPE1 might convert G3 to longer MOSs that could be further used by PHS1.

DPE1 catalyzes a disproportionation reaction, where it transfers a segment of a glucan chain from one MOS to another, resulting in the elongation of one chain and the shortening of another (Fig. 1a). We examined the enzymatic activity of DPE1 by quantifying the released glucose. DPE1 has enzymatic activity on MOSs ranging from G3 to G7 but shows no activity on G2 (Fig. 1e). The result provides the reason why PHS1 exhibits enzymatic activity on G3 in the presence of DPE1; DPE1’s high activity on G3 likely primes the substrate for subsequent elongation by PHS1. Notably, the PHS1–DPE1 complex exhibits significantly higher activity than DPE1 alone when G3 and G4 are used as substrates. It should be noted that this method may not fully reflect total enzymatic activity when longer MOSs (>G4) are used as the substrates, as free glucose is not a direct product of the initial disproportionation reaction that can be visualized in subsequent biochemical assays. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PHS1 and DPE1 function coordinately in MOS metabolism.

Fluorophore-assisted carbohydrate electrophoresis (FACE) was further conducted to analyze the distribution of MOS profiles catalyzed by PHS1 and DPE1. This technique allows for the separation and visualization of fluorescently labeled carbohydrates, providing detailed insights into the specific MOS profiles produced by each enzyme. The enzymatic MOS products were fluorescently labeled with APTS (8-amino-1,3,6-pyrenetrisulfonic acid) that can be visualized by laser scanning at 488 nm after electrophoresis (Supplementary Fig. 4a). The results reveal that PHS1 exhibit enzymatic activities on MOSs ranging from G4 to G7 rather than from G1 to G3 to synthesize longer MOSs in the presence of G1P (Fig. 1f). The starch phosphorolysis activity of PHS1 was assayed using maltodextrins (G2–G7) as substrates. Detectable activity was observed on longer MOSs ranging from G5 to G7 in the presence of Pi (Supplementary Fig. 4b), but no activity occurred in the absence of both G1P and Pi (Supplementary Fig. 4c). DPE1 shows enzymatic activities on MOSs ranging from G3 to G7 to synthesize longer MOSs and generate shorter MOSs simultaneously (Fig. 1g). For example, in the G3 reaction systems, there are obvious accumulations of G1, G5 and G7 (Fig. 1g), consistent with the speculation that DPE1 can convert two G3 molecules to G5 and G1 and might further catalyze G3 and G5 to G1 and G7, respectively. We further examined the MOS product profiles catalyzed by the PHS1–DPE1 complex. Notably, the complex exhibits strikingly higher enzymatic activities on MOSs ranging from G3 to G7 to synthesize longer MOSs than PHS1 or DPE1 alone (Fig. 1h–k and Supplementary Fig. 4d). Collectively, these findings indicate that PHS1 and DPE1 function cooperatively to drive the efficient synthesis of longer MOS primers.

Cryo-EM structure of rice PHS1–DPE1 multimer

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which the PHS1–DPE1 complex facilitates the synthesis of long MOS molecules, we attempted to determine the cryo-EM structure of the complex. Protein samples from the peak fractions of the PHS1–DPE1 complex were pooled and subjected to single-particle cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) for high-resolution structure determination. The cryo-EM particles of PHS1–DPE1 complex exhibited a characteristic of extended multimer as revealed by initial 2D classifications (Supplementary Fig. 5). Three-dimensional classification and refinement yielded an EM density map with an overall resolution of 2.85 Å for the 2:2 tetrameric unit (Supplementary Fig. 5, Fig. 6, and Supplementary Table 1). By applying C2 symmetry, the map of the 1:2:1 tetrameric DPE1–PHS1–DPE1 was obtained and refined to a resolution of 2.48 Å (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Ultimately, we resolved the high-resolution filament-like structure of the PHS1–DPE1 multimer (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 5). In this assembly, the tetrameric PHS1–DPE1 complex, with a stoichiometric ratio of 2:2 (comprising a PHS1 dimer and a DPE1 dimer), defines the minimal repeat unit, measuring approximately 150 Å in height and 110 Å in width (Fig. 2a). Within one unit, one protomer of the DPE1 dimer interacts with the dimeric PHS1 (Fig. 2a, b), while the other DPE1 protomer contacts the dimeric PHS1 of the adjacent neighboring unit (Fig. 2a). The PHS1 dimer forms two clefts flanking its dimer interface, with each cleft accommodating one protomer of DPE1 from neighboring repeat units (Fig. 2c, d). Consequently, the PHS1 dimer and DPE1 dimer are alternately arranged, facilitating the formation of extended PHS1–DPE1 multimers (Fig. 2a). SEC analysis reveals a stable hetero-octameric core (Fig. 1c), while native PAGE and AUC demonstrate that this core can dynamically assemble into larger, polymorphic complexes of heterogeneous size or dissociate (Supplementary Fig. 3).

PHS1 belongs to glycosyltransferase family 35 (GT35), a group of enzymes widely distributed in plants, animals, and prokaryotes29. Structural analysis of PHS1 protomers revealed minimal structural variation, as demonstrated by structure superposition (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Each PHS1 protomer consists of two distinct domains: an N-terminal segment (residues 77–623) and a C-terminal segment (residues 624–978) (Fig. 2d). Notably, the L80 region (residues 490–569) within the N-terminal segment is not resolved in the structure, likely due to its high flexibility, as predicted by AlphaFold3 (Supplementary Fig. 7a). The quaternary structure of PHS1 is stabilized by intermolecular interactions, primarily involving the Tower helix (residues 302–332) and N-terminal helices, which facilitate dimer formation (Fig. 2d). To assess structural homology, a Dali structural similarity search was conducted (Supplementary Table 2). The results indicated that rice PHS1 exhibits a dimeric architecture similar to other starch and glycogen phosphorylases, including barley plastidial PHS1 (PDB: 5LR8)32, Arabidopsis cytosolic AtPHS2 (PDB: 4BQF)40, yeast glycogen phosphorylase (PDB: 1YGP)41, human brain glycogen phosphorylase (PDB: 5IKO)42, and bacterial maltodextrin phosphorylase (PDB: 1AHP)43. The root mean square deviations (RMSD) for the main chain of one protomer were calculated as 0.797 Å, 0.835 Å, 0.844 Å, 0.852 Å, and 1.016 Å, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 7b–f). Despite these structural similarities, the protein sequence of rice PHS1 shows lower conservation with homologs from yeast, humans, and bacteria compared to its counterparts in higher plants such as barley and Arabidopsis (Supplementary Fig. 7g). Notably, the L80 region is uniquely present in the plastidial PHS1 and is absent in both the cytosolic PHS2 and glycogen phosphorylase (Supplementary Fig. 7g).

DPE1 belongs to the glycoside hydrolase family 77 (GH77), where it functions as a disproportionating enzyme in plants and as an amylomaltase in bacteria and archaea. The DPE1 dimer adopts an elongated architecture (Fig. 2e). DPE1 protomers display strong structural alignment, featuring a distinctive N-terminal arm-like region (residues 70–95) that extends outward in each protomer (Supplementary Fig. 8a). A Dali search indicated that the dimeric structure of rice DPE1 closely resembles the plant homologs (Supplementary Table 3): including potato StDPE1 (PDB: 6LX2)44 and Arabidopsis AtDPE1 (PDB: 5CPS)33, with RMSD values of 0.714 Å and 0.731 Å, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 8b, c). Additionally, rice DPE1 demonstrates significant structural similarity to the amylomaltase from Thermus aquaticus (TaqAM, PDB: 5JIW)45, particularly in the core canonical (β/α)8 barrel fold, with an RMSD of 1.001 Å, despite lower sequence identity (Supplementary Fig. 8d, e). A key structural distinction between rice DPE1 and TaqAM is the absence of the N-terminal arm in TaqAM, which likely accounts for TaqAM’s monomeric state, in contrast to the dimeric structure of rice DPE1 (Supplementary Fig. 8d, e).

The N-terminal arm of DPE1 is essential for DPE1 dimerization and PHS1–DPE1 multimerization

In the tetrameric assembly, one protomer of the dimeric DPE1 is positioned within the side cleft of the dimeric PHS1 (Fig. 2). The N-terminal arm of each DPE1 protomer wraps around the opposing protomer, playing a crucial role in stabilizing the dimerization of DPE1 (Fig. 3a). Close-up inspection revealed a cluster of acidic residues within the N-terminal arm, including E78, E81, and D84, which engage in salt-bridge interactions with R415, R419, and R394 of the opposing subunit, respectively (Fig. 3b). To investigate the functional significance of the N-terminal arm, we generated a DPE1ΔN (residues: 96-594) construct with the arm region deleted. As anticipated, the elution volume of DPE1ΔN was delayed, and its calculated molecular mass of ~70 kDa was consistent with a monomeric state (Fig. 3c), confirming that the arm deletion disrupts DPE1 dimerization. To investigate the effect on the PHS1–DPE1 complex, we incubated DPE1ΔN with PHS1. The resulting PHS1–DPE1ΔN complex eluted later, with a calculated mass of ~600 kDa, which is significantly smaller than the ~800 kDa wild-type complex (Fig. 3c). While the SEC result provides reliable molecular weight estimates for globular proteins, it may not accurately reflect the mass of the extended PHS1–DPE1 multimer. Nonetheless, this reduction in mass indicates a disruption of the multimerization, highlighting the critical contribution of the N-terminal arm of DPE1 to the assembly of the multimeric structure. We next assessed the enzymatic activity of the N-terminal arm deletion mutant. The activity of both DPE1ΔN alone and the PHS1–DPE1ΔN complex was nearly abolished toward the G3 substrate (Fig. 3d). Consistent with this, FACE assays revealed that neither DPE1ΔN nor the PHS1–DPE1ΔN complex could catalyze polymerization from a G3 substrate, regardless of the presence of G1P (Fig. 3e, f). These results indicate that the N-terminal arm is essential for DPE1 catalysis, potentially by facilitating substrate access to the active site. We further assessed enzymatic activity using G4 as the substrate (Fig. 3g, h). As observed with G3, DPE1ΔN alone showed no detectable activity with or without G1P. In contrast, the PHS1–DPE1ΔN complex displayed residual activity, generating longer MOS molecules only in the presence of G1P, consistent with the intrinsic activity of PHS1 toward G4. However, its efficiency in producing longer MOS species (DP > 8) was markedly reduced compared to the wild-type complex (Fig. 3g, h). This result indicates that without a functional DPE1, the coordinated activity required for efficient elongation is severely compromised. Collectively, these results underscore the essential role of the N-terminal arm in the dimerization and enzymatic activity of DPE1.

Interactions between PHS1 and DPE1

In the PHS1–DPE1 tetramer, the extended DPE1 dimer is inserted into the cleft of the dimeric PHS1, with one DPE1 protomer interacting with both protomers of PHS1 through two distinct interfaces (Fig. 4a–c). In interface I, two salt bridge pairs are observed between DPE1 and protomer B of PHS1: DPE1A E237 – PHS1B K226 and DPE1A E270 – PHS1B H776 (Fig. 4b). To assess the functional significance of these residues, alanine substitution mutations were generated, and pull-down assays were conducted to evaluate their binding affinities. The results showed that single mutations of either PHS1K226A or DPE1E237A had minimal impact on the interaction between PHS1 and DPE1 (Fig. 4d, e). In contrast, mutations of either PHS1H776A or DPE1E270A significantly reduced the binding affinity between the two proteins (Fig. 4d, e). Furthermore, the double mutant PHS1K226A/H776A completely lost the ability to interact with DPE1 (Fig. 4d), and the DPE1E237A/E270A double mutant nearly abolished its interaction with PHS1 (Fig. 4e). Evolutionarily, this interface is highly conserved across higher plants, underscoring its fundamental role in this key protein-protein interaction (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Fig. 2). These findings highlight the critical role of interface I in mediating the interactions between PHS1 and DPE1. In interface II, a hydrogen bond is formed between DPE1 Y255 and protomer A of PHS1 G272. To investigate the impact of disrupting the interactions between PHS1 and DPE1 on MOS synthesis, we incubated wild-type PHS1 with DPE1 mutants or wild-type DPE1 with PHS1 mutants and analyzed the MOS profiles using the FACE assay. The results revealed that incubations containing either mutant DPE1 (E237, E270A or E237A/E270A) or mutant PHS1 (K226A, H776A or K226A/H776A) produced fewer longer MOS molecules (DP > 8) than the wild-type PHS1–DPE1 complex (Fig. 4f). The L80 loop acts as a potential regulator for substrate transfer

An extra L80 peptide is unique in plant plastidial PHS1 (Supplementary Fig. 1), which is absent from cytosolic PHS2, as well as mammalian and bacterial glycogen phosphorylase (Supplementary Fig. 7g). Structural alignment of the ligand-bound PHS1–DPE1 complex with the Alphafold3-predicted PHS1 revealed good overall alignment, with the L80 loop positioned near the catalytic pocket of DPE1 and the interaction interface between PHS1 and DPE1 (Supplementary Fig. 11e). Given that the L80 region is rich in polar and charged residues, including serine, aspartate, glutamate, and arginine (Supplementary Fig. 1), we hypothesized that the L80 loop might play a role in intermediate transfer between the enzymes. To test this, we generated a truncated construct PHS1ΔL80 (residues: 45–978, excluding 490–569), which lacks the L80 loop. Although the protein yield of PHS1ΔL80 was nearly 20-fold lower compared to wild-type PHS1 under similar expression conditions, it retained the ability to interact with DPE1, forming a DPE1–PHS1ΔL80 complex upon co-expression (Supplementary Fig. 11f). We evaluated the impact of the L80 loop on MOS production by comparing the activities of wild-type PHS1 and the DPE1–PHS1 complex with their L80-deletion counterparts (PHS1ΔL80 and DPE1–PHS1ΔL80). FACE analysis revealed that the loss of L80 led to a modest reduction (~20%) in the production of longer MOSs (DP > 8) for the DPE1–PHS1ΔL80 mutant complex compared to the wild-type complex when G3, G4, or G5 were used as substrates, whereas the difference was less apparent when G6 was used (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. 11g, h). In contrast, when PHS1ΔL80 was assayed alone, the reduction in longer MOSs (DP > 8) was more pronounced, particularly with longer substrates: an approximate 50% reduction was observed for G5 and G6, whereas less than 20% reduction was seen for G4. These findings indicate that L80 plays a more critical role in processing longer substrates when PHS1 functions independently.

To understand the biochemical basis of this observation, we performed continuous time-course assays and Michaelis-Menten analysis. The results unequivocally showed that the deletion impairs the intrinsic catalytic rate of the enzyme (Supplementary Fig. 12). The initial velocity (V₀) was reduced in both PHS1ΔL80 and the DPE1–PHS1ΔL80 complex, with a more pronounced reduction observed for the isolated mutant enzyme than for the complex (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b). Crucially, Michaelis-Menten analysis confirmed a substantial decrease in Vmax (and thus kcat) for the mutants, while their Km for the G4 substrate remained largely unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 12c, d). This combination of data demonstrates that the L80 loop is essential for optimal catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km) by specifically enabling a high catalytic turnover rate, without affecting substrate binding affinity. A previous study reported that the presence of the L80 loop (also referred to as L78) in barley PHS1 reduces the enzyme’s ability to bind and utilize large glucans, favoring shorter MOSs instead, thereby ensuring that the enzyme acts primarily on soluble MOS substrates32. The wild-type PHS1–DPE1 complex exhibits a dramatically enhanced catalytic performance over PHS1 alone. This functional enhancement was modestly compromised in the DPE1–PHS1ΔL80 complex.

Collectively, these data demonstrate that the unique L80 loop in PHS1 is not required for the complex assembly but is essential for optimizing its catalytic architecture. We conclude that the L80 loop acts as an integral structural regulator, facilitating rapid substrate turnover to achieve the high catalytic efficiency of both PHS1 and the PHS1–DPE1 complex. This mechanism likely underpins the efficient synthesis of longer MOSs by optimizing the catalytic step itself.

Single-molecule fluorescence resonance energy transfer (smFRET) analysis reveals substrate transfer in the DPE1–PHS1 complex

Structural analysis of the PHS1–DPE1 tetramer revealed a potential substrate tunnel between PHS1 and DPE1 protomers (Fig. 6g). The L80 loop, which is flexible and surrounds the tunnel path, might play a role in substrate diffusion between the two enzymes, thereby modulating the synthesis of longer MOS chains.

To directly probe the hypothesized substrate-transfer between DPE1 and PHS1, we employed smFRET. This technique provides unambiguously resolved, real-time dynamics of the transient enzyme complex that are obscured in ensemble measurements. In our assay, the substrate maltotriose (G3) was labeled with the donor fluorophore Cy3. This Cy3-G3 was pre-incubated with biotinylated DPE1, and the complex was immobilized on a streptavidin-coated surface. After washing away unbound substrate, Cy5-labeled PHS1 was introduced, followed by a final wash to remove unbound enzyme (Supplementary Fig. 13a). Therefore, the observed Cy3 (donor) and Cy5 (acceptor) signals originate exclusively from molecules engaged in the DPE1–PHS1 complex. This configuration allowed us to monitor the proximity between the G3 substrate and PHS1 in real-time by measuring FRET efficiency.

Analysis of single-molecule time traces revealed that the G3 and related MOS products within the DPE1–PHS1 complex dynamically transition between two distinct conformational states (Supplementary Fig. 13b, top). Compilation of FRET efficiencies from multiple molecules into a histogram and subsequent fitting with a double Gaussian distribution confirmed the presence of two major populations (Supplementary Fig. 13b, bottom). We identified a low-FRET state (FRET ~ 0.3, ~72% probability), interpreted as a G3-dissociated state where the substrate is distal to PHS1. Conversely, a high-FRET state (FRET ~ 0.7, ~28% probability) represents a G3-associated state where the substrate is in close proximity to PHS1.

Quantification of the state lifetimes showed that the wild-type complex exhibited rapid dynamics, with lifetimes of 0.66 s for the low-FRET state and 0.58 s for the high-FRET state (Supplementary Fig. 13c). To investigate the functional importance of the L80 loop in PHS1, we performed identical experiments with a Cy5-labeled PHS1ΔL80 mutant. This mutation significantly elongated the lifetimes of both states to 0.75 s and 0.78 s, respectively. The prolonged lifetimes observed in the ΔL80 mutant indicate a less efficient transition between the substrate-dissociated and -associated states. This kinetic impairment demonstrates that the L80 loop serves as a potential regulator mediating substrate transfer or diffusion between the two enzymes, providing a direct mechanistic explanation for the mutant complex’s reduced production of longer MOS products (Fig. 6e, f).

In conclusion, our results suggest that plastidial PHS1 and DPE1 interact to form a multimeric complex (Fig. 6h). The close spatial arrangement of PHS1 and DPE1 within the assembly enables the transfer of reaction intermediates between the two enzymes via either intra- or intermolecular pathways, thereby reducing substrate diffusion and enhancing metabolic flux. This synergistic interaction between PHS1 and DPE1 facilitates the efficient synthesis of longer MOS chains (Fig. 6h). Our model highlights the importance of the PHS1–DPE1 complex in regulating MOS primer biosynthesis and underscores the functional synergy between these enzymes in maintaining efficient metabolic pathways.

Discussion

Plant metabolism demands rapid and coordinated regulation in response to developmental and environmental cues. In this study, we demonstrate that the rice plastidial enzymes PHS1 and DPE1 form a functional complex. Their structural and functional interplay drives the efficient synthesis of longer MOSs, revealing a crucial mechanism for optimizing starch biosynthesis.

Cryo-EM structural analysis elucidated the assembly of this multimeric complex. To complement our experimental findings, we employed AlphaFold 3 for structural prediction. Using a defined stoichiometry of 2:2 (PHS1:DPE1), the prediction generated a high-confidence model of a 1:2:1 PHS1–DPE1 tetramer, with a predicted template modeling (pTM) score of 0.72 and an interface predicted template modeling (ipTM) score of 0.65 (Supplementary Fig. 14a, b). This model closely resembles our experimentally determined Protomer II structure (Supplementary Fig. 4), wherein a PHS1 dimer is centrally positioned, with individual DPE1 subunits nestled within the cleft of the dimer. Notably, the ipTM score (0.65) for this tetrameric assembly falls below the high-confidence threshold of 0.8, underscoring the importance of experimental validation for such predictions. Attempts to predict larger assemblies (e.g., 4:4 stoichiometry) were unsuccessful, likely due to limitations in modeling large molecular complexes. Structural superposition of the AlphaFold 3 model with the cryo-EM structure shows the agreement, with root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) values of 0.901 Å for PHS1A (over 817 aligned atom pairs; but 2.376 Å over all 837 residues) and 0.712 Å for DPE1A (over 461 aligned atom pairs; but 9.670 Å over all 524 residues), respectively (Supplementary Fig. 14c). The larger deviations correspond to flexible or disordered regions, including the L80 loop of PHS1 and the N-terminal arm of DPE1. A closer inspection of the PHS1–DPE1 interaction surface reveals that the predicted interface residue pairs are consistent with those identified in the experimental structure (Supplementary Fig. 14d). Collectively, the combined cryo-EM structural determination and AlphaFold 3 modeling establishes a robust architectural framework for the assembly of the PHS1–DPE1 complex in higher plants.

Biochemically, PHS1 catalyzes reversible reactions in both synthetic and degradative directions (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 4b). A previous study revealed that PHS1 exhibits a strong preference for synthetic activity over degradative activity14. Even in the presence of Pi, PHS1 processes MOSs in a net synthetic manner, degrading shorter chains while simultaneously utilizing the released G1P to synthesize longer ones14. Our findings solidify the primary role of PHS1 involved in longer MOS production in starch synthesis, distinguishing it from glycogen phosphorylases in animals, fungi, and bacteria, which predominantly degrade glycogen through phosphorolysis47,48. This biochemical behavior is consistent with functional studies demonstrating that PHS1 is essential for starch synthesis. For example, rice phs1 (pho1) mutants show significantly seed phenotypes, ranging from shrunken to pseudo-normal grains, with reduced starch accumulation12,13,49, and their endosperm accumulates shorter chains (DP 5–7), indicating hindered chain elongation in the absence of PHS1 activity50. DPE1 plays a cooperative role with PHS1 in MOS metabolism during starch synthesis. The rice phs1dpe1 double mutant exhibits a dramatically enhanced lethal seed phenotype and accumulates high levels of short MOSs compared to the phs1 single mutant13. This synthetic genetic interaction provides strong in vivo evidence that DPE1 and PHS1 function in the same pathway and that DPE1 can compensate for the loss of PHS113. In Arabidopsis leaves, the phs1dpe1 double mutant exhibits increased starch content but accumulates MOSs with longer average chain lengths51. These longer MOSs may act as aberrant primers that disrupt the normal regulation of starch synthesis, thus resulting in the production of starch granules with a more heterogeneous size distribution and larger average size in the phs1dpe1 double mutant than the single mutants51. These in vivo phenotypes demonstrate that the functional coordination of PHS1 and DPE1 is critical for maintaining normal MOS homeostasis.

The discovery of the physical association of PHS1 and DPE137,38, and the structural determination of the complex in this study, provide the mechanistic basis for this function. The kinetic analysis confirms that the PHS-DPE1 complex creates a more efficient catalytic unit, exhibiting a dramatic increase in catalytic efficiency compared to PHS1 alone. Most importantly, smFRET analysis provides evidence for a substrate transfer or diffusion between the two enzymes (Supplementary Fig. 12). We observed real-time transitions between a low-FRET state (substrate distal to PHS1) and a high-FRET state (substrate channeled to PHS1), demonstrating the dynamic conformational changes that facilitate the intermediate transfer. However, a key limitation of the current approach is that it does not resolve whether the observed substrate proximity arises from an intramolecular event, where a single substrate molecule is sequentially processed within a preformed PHS1–DPE1 complex, or an intermolecular collision between the two enzymes. Distinguishing between these pathways is technically challenging and would require additional approaches, such as mixing experiments with differentially labeled enzyme populations. Thus, whether the substrate transfer occurs via inter- or intramolecular pathways remains unknown, and further studies are needed to address this. Collectively, our in vitro biochemical and structural investigations are consistent with the in vivo data, confirming the critical function of PHS1–DPE1 complex in MOS primer synthesis. Given that PHS1 may also interact with other starch-metabolizing enzymes, including branching enzymes and SSs, a toggle between the PHS1–DPE1 complex and other PHS1-associated protein complexes may exist, thereby ensuring efficient starch synthesis.

A key finding is the elucidation of the function for the L80 region, which is uniquely present in plastidial PHS1 isoform but absent in cytosolic PHS2 isoform, as well as in mammalian, fungal, and bacterial glycogen phosphorylases (Supplementary Fig. 7g). Previous studies have suggested potential roles for L80 in protein phosphorylation and proteolysis, based on the presence of serine/threonine (putative phosphorylation sites) and PEST regions—sequences rich in proline (P), glutamic acid (E), serine (S), and threonine (T) that are often associated with proteolytic targeting9,29. Structural alignment of barley PHS1 with glycogen phosphorylase revealed that the L80 insertion protrudes from the protein surface and spatially overlaps with the glycogen/starch binding site, suggesting that L80 creates steric hindrance that limits access to large polysaccharides such as starch or glycogen, thereby shifting the enzyme’s binding preference from these substrates toward MOSs32. Biochemically, the L80 region contributes to the enzyme’s heat stability as PHS1ΔL80 possessed less catalytic activity than wild-type PHS1 when incubated at higher temperature39. However, its precise biochemical function remains unclear. Our findings elucidate the role of L80 in PHS1 as well as within the PHS1–DPE1 complex. Structural alignment of the PHS1–DPE1 complex with the AlphaFold-predicted PHS1 structure revealed that the disordered L80 loop is positioned adjacent to the interaction interface between PHS1 and DPE1 (Supplementary Fig. 11e). Although the L80-deleted variant still interacts with DPE1, the production of longer MOSs is reduced in the DPE1–PHS1ΔL80 mutant complex compared to the wild type (Fig. 6e, f). The smFRET experiments and kinetic analysis directly demonstrated the mechanistic consequence of this deletion, that the PHS1ΔL80 mutant complex exhibited prolonged conformational state lifetimes and a lower substrate turnover rate (Supplementary Fig. 12 and Fig. 13), indicating a less efficient substrate transfer. A previous study revealed that the relative protein abundance of PHS1 is much higher (>25-fold) than that of DPE1 in developing rice seeds37, suggesting that PHS1 may function independently rather than as part of a PHS1–DPE1 complex. The L80 deletion mutant PHS1ΔL80 exhibits a greater reduction in initial velocity (V₀) compared to wild-type PHS1, and this decrease is more pronounced than that observed for the PHS1ΔL80-DPE1 relative to the PHS1–DPE1 complex (Supplementary Fig. 12a). These findings suggest an alternative function for L80 in PHS1, wherein L80 increases the flexibility of PHS1 domains, thereby enhancing the rate of conformational changes or substrate movement required for catalysis.

Beyond its canonical role in MOS metabolism, PHS1 was identified to associate with photosynthetic proteins like PsaC and LHCB49,52, thereby directly linking starch metabolism with photosynthetic efficiency. The PHS1ΔL80 rice plant showed altered electron transport rates and enhanced CO₂ assimilation49, suggesting it acts as a regulatory module that fine-tunes enzyme activity and interaction capacity49,52. The decreased catalytic efficiency we observed in the ΔL80 mutant in vitro likely disrupts the fine-tuned metabolic passage of MOS in vivo. This disruption in carbon processing might influence the photosynthetic apparatus to optimize electron flow and carbon fixation via the enhanced interaction between PHS1 and PsaC of PSI49. Therefore, the L80 loop emerges as a potential integrator that physically links starch metabolism in the stroma with photosynthetic electron transport in the thylakoids, coordinating source and sink processes in plants.

In conclusion, our work redefines PHS1 as the core of a plastidial metabolic complex that drives starch biosynthesis. We present a model in which the PHS1–DPE1 complex acts in a coordinated manner to facilitate efficient substrate transfer for the synthesis of longer MOS chains. These findings establish a mechanistic foundation for optimizing starch synthesis in future bioengineering and crop improvement efforts.

Methods

Molecular cloning, protein expression, and purification

TargetP − 2.0 was used to predict the transit peptide of rice PHS1 (UniProt: B3IYE3) and DPE1 (UniProt: F2ZBU6). The coding sequences of mature PHS1 (residues: R45-P978) and DPE1 (residues: A38-L594) were amplified from Oryza sativa cDNA and cloned into a modified pET15D vector that adds a 6×His tag at the N terminus of the encoded protein. Primers used are listed in Supplementary Table 4. The point mutants were constructed by overlapping PCR. All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing. The plasmids were transformed into E. coli strain BL21 (DE3). One liter of LB medium containing 100 mg/mL ampicillin was inoculated with a transformed bacterial pre-culture and shaken at 37 °C until the cell density reached an OD600 of approximately 1.0–1.2. Protein production was induced with 0.2 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside at 16 °C for 14–16 h. The cells were collected by centrifugation, homogenized in buffer A (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl), and lysed using a high-pressure cell disrupter (JNBIO, China). Cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 20,000 g for 40 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was loaded onto a column equipped with Ni2+ affinity resin (Ni-NTA, Qiagen), washed with buffer B (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 15 mM imidazole), and eluted with buffer C (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 250 mM imidazole). The eluent was then separated by anion exchange chromatography (Source 15Q, GE Healthcare) using a linear NaCl gradient in buffer A. The purified protein was concentrated and subjected to gel filtration chromatography (Superose 6, GE Healthcare) in a buffer containing 25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, and 150 mM NaCl. Purity of the proteins was examined using SDS-PAGE and visualized by Coomassie brilliant blue staining. The peak fractions were collected and stored at –80 °C. The mutant proteins were purified similarly to the wild-type proteins. The 6×His tags were retained throughout the purification process, as well as during the subsequent biochemical and structural analyses.

SEC

The purified PHS1 was mixed with DPE1 at a molar ratio of approximately 1:1.5, incubated at 4 °C for 40 min, and further purified by gel filtration chromatography using a Superose-6 Increase column (GE Healthcare). The chromatography was performed in a buffer containing 25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, and 150 mM NaCl. The relevant fractions from each independent injection were collected and analyzed by SDS-PAGE. The peak fractions, corresponding to the PHS1–DPE1 complex, were pooled and used for subsequent biochemical assays and cryo-EM grid preparation.

In vitro pull-down assays

To validate the critical roles of residues on the interaction interface of the PHS1–DPE1 complex, Strep-tagged PHS1 and DPE1 were employed to pull down His-tagged DPE1 or PHS1 mutants, respectively. Both Strep-tagged PHS1 and DPE1, as well as His-tagged PHS1 and DPE1 mutants, were individually expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) and purified to homogeneity as described previously. For the pull-down assay, equal volumes of wild-type Strep-tagged PHS1 (50 µg) and His-tagged DPE1 (50 µg), or wild-type Strep-tagged DPE1 (50 µg) and His-tagged PHS1 (50 µg), were mixed and loaded onto 100 µL of streptavidin-Tactin beads (IBA). The mixture was incubated at 4 °C for 40 min to allow binding. Subsequently, the beads were washed with strep lysis buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM EDTA-NaOH, pH 8.0) to remove unbound proteins. To elute the target proteins, 5 mM D-desthiobiotin in lysis buffer was added to the beads and incubated for 30 min. The eluted samples were then analyzed by SDS-PAGE, followed by Coomassie brilliant blue staining to visualize the protein interactions.

Enzymatic assay of PHS1 by the phosphoantimonylmolybdenum blue method

The activity of PHS1 was assayed in the direction of α-glucan synthesis using the phosphoantimonylmolybdenum blue (PAMB) method, following a previously described protocol with minor modifications39. Briefly, the assay was performed in a 50 µL reaction system containing 50 mM MES-NaOH (pH 6.5), 5 mM MgCl₂, 5 mM glucose 1-phosphate (G1P), 1 mM MOS (ranging from G2 to G7), and 20 nM enzyme. The reaction was incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. To stop the enzymatic reaction, 100 μL of solution C was added. Solution C was prepared by mixing solution A (12% w/v L-ascorbic acid in 1 M HCl) and solution B (2% w/v ammonium molybdate tetrahydrate in deionized water) in a volume ratio of 2:1. Solution C reacts strongly with Pi but weakly with phosphate esters (such as G1P) present in the reaction mixture. After 5 min of incubation at room temperature, 100 μL of solution D (2% w/v sodium citrate tribasic dihydrate and 2% v/v acetic acid in deionized water) was added to halt color development. The final reaction mixture (250 μL) was transferred to a 96-well plate, and the absorbance at 650 nm was measured using a SpectraMax Plus microplate spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices). All assays were performed in at least three technical replicates. This method allowed for the quantification of Pi released during α-glucan synthesis, providing a reliable measure of PHS1 activity.

For time-course assays, reactions were stopped after 0 to 90 min (10-min intervals). Pi accumulation data were fitted with a nonlinear regression model in GraphPad Prism to confirm reaction linearity. For kinetic analysis, G4 was used as the substrate at concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 8 mM, with reactions incubated for 15 min (within the initial linear phase). Kinetic parameters (Km, Vmₐₓ) were determined by fitting the data to the Michaelis–Menten model using GraphPad Prism.

Enzymatic assay of DPE1 by detection of free glucose

The enzymatic activity of DPE1 toward MOS was determined by measuring the amount of free glucose produced. The reaction buffer consisted of 50 mM MES-NaOH (pH 6.5), 5 mM ATP (Sigma, sodium salt, pH 7.0), 5 mM MgCl₂, 10 mM β-NAD (Sangon, oxidized form, pH 7.0), 10 mM maltooligosaccharide, 20 U mL⁻¹ hexokinase (Yuanye, S10025), and 40 U mL⁻¹ glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (Yuanye, S10078). The reaction system (100 μL) was initiated by adding 10 nM DPE1 or PHS1–DPE1 complex and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. The reaction was stopped by heating at 95 °C for 5 min. After centrifugation at 12,000×g for 2 min, the entire reaction mixture was transferred to a 96-well plate. The reduction product, NADH, was monitored by measuring the absorbance at 340 nm using a SpectraMax Plus microplate spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices). Each assay was performed in at least three biological replicates to calculate the mean activity value. This method allowed for the quantification of free glucose produced by DPE1, reflecting its enzymatic activity.

FACE

The enzymatic reactions for DPE1, PHS1, and PHS1–DPE1 complex were performed at 37 °C for 15 min in a 50 μL system. For DPE1 activity, the reaction mixture contained 50 mM MES-NaOH (pH 6.5), 5 mM MgCl₂, 1 mM MOS, and 20 nM protein. For PHS1 activity, the reaction was conducted under the same conditions as DPE1 but with the addition of 5 mM glucose 1-phosphate (G1P) or 5 mM Pi in the reaction system. After the 15-min incubation, the reactions were stopped by heating at 95 °C for 5 min. Once the mixture cooled to room temperature, the reaction products were collected by centrifugation at 12,000×g for 30 s. The supernatant was then dried at 45 °C using a centrifugal vacuum concentrator. The products were fluorescently labeled with 8-amino-1,3,6-pyrenetrisulfonic acid (APTS; MCE, #HY-D0031). Separation was performed via denaturing urea-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (urea-PAGE), following a previously described method with minor modifications53. Briefly, the dried sample was resuspended in 10 μL of 200 mM APTS in 15% acetic acid and 10 μL of 1 M sodium cyanoborohydride in dimethyl sulfoxide. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 15 h in the dark to allow for the labeling of oligosaccharides with APTS. Following incubation, the sample was diluted to 200 μL using an electrophoresis sample buffer containing 6 M urea in 0.04 M Tris - boric acid, pH 8.6.

For analysis, 10 µL of each resulting sample was loaded onto a 20% polyacrylamide gel to resolve and measure the APTS-labeled oligosaccharides. Electrophoresis was performed at 250 V and 8 °C. The attachment of APTS confers a negative charge on the MOS molecules, enabling their separation based on size. The gel contained 8 M urea to improve resolution, and the running buffer containing 89 mM Tris-borate, containing 2 mM EDTA were used for optimal separation. The APTS-labeled MOS standard mixture, ranging from G1 to G7, was prepared in the same manner as the experimental samples, with each oligosaccharide present in the same molar amount but without undergoing any enzymatic reaction. After electrophoresis, the gel, still attached to its glass plate, was briefly rinsed with deionized water and visualized. Fluorescence was detected using an Amersham Typhoon scanner equipped with a 488 nm argon-ion laser, operated by the manufacturer’s control software. For quantification, fluorescent bands (DP > 8) were analyzed in ImageJ (version 1.54 g). Data represent at least three independent biological replicates. Raw data are provided in the Supplemental Information.

AUC analysis

The molecular masses of DPE1, PHS1, and PDC were investigated by AUC experiments, which were performed in a Beckman Coulter XL-I analytical ultracentrifuge using two-channel centrepieces. The protein sample was in solutions containing 25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl. Data were collected by absorbance detection at 18 °C for proteins at a concentration of ~0.8 mg/mL at a rotor speed of 35,000× rpm. The SV-AUC data were globally analyzed using the SEDFIT program and fitted to a continuous c(s) distribution model to determine the molecular mass.

Native PAGE

Aliquots of the purified proteins (2 μM) were mixed with 2× loading buffer (50% [v/v] glycerol, 0.05% [v/v] Bromophenol Blue) in a 1:1 ratio to a 5 mL system. The samples were then loaded onto a 6% native PAGE and pre-electrophoresed at 150 V for 30 min at 4 °C. Then electrophoresed at 150 V for 1.5 h. After electrophoresis, the gels were visualized by silver staining with a Fast Silver Stain kit (Beyotime, Cat. No. P0017S).

smFRET

The DPE1 gene was cloned to encode a fusion protein with an N-terminal 6×His tag followed by an AVI tag (sequence: GLNDIFEAQKIEWHE). DPE1 was purified using the standard protocol for the wild-type enzyme, supplemented with 2 μM BirA, 5 mM MgCl2, 4 mM ATP, 1 mM biotin, and 2 mM DTT to enzymatically biotinylate the AVI tag in vitro during purification. Maltotriose (G3) was commercially sourced as a conjugate with the FRET donor dye, Cy3 (Xi’an Qiyue Biology). The FRET acceptor dye, Cy5, was conjugated to cysteine residues on purified PHS1. Briefly, a 1 mM stock of Cy5-NHS ester in DMSO was incubated with PHS1 at a 6:1 molar ratio (dye:protein) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Conjugation was carried out for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. The reaction mixture was then passed through a desalting column (equilibrated with 25 mM Tris-Cl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl) to remove unconjugated dye. Microfluidic imaging chambers were passivated with a mixture of polyethylene glycol (PEG) and biotin-PEG (Laysan Bio). Streptavidin (0.01 mg/mL; Invitrogen) was introduced and incubated for 2 min to bind the biotinylated PEG, followed by an incubation with 8 mg/mL bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Buffer A (50 mM MES, pH 6.5, 5 mM MgCl₂) for 10 min to block non-specific binding. Chambers were thoroughly washed with Buffer A before use.

Biotinylated DPE1 (0.2 μM) was pre-incubated with a 10-fold molar excess of Cy3-labeled G3 (2 μM) in assay buffer (50 mM MES, pH 6.5, 5 mM MgCl2, 2 mM DTT, 5 mM G1P) at 37 °C for 5 min to form the enzyme-substrate complex. This mixture was then diluted 2000-fold and introduced into the streptavidin-functionalized chamber, allowing the biotinylated DPE1 to immobilize on the surface. Unbound protein and substrate were removed by extensive washing with Buffer B (50 mM MES, pH 6.5, 5 mM MgCl2, 2 mM DTT). Subsequently, Cy5-labeled PHS1 (0.06 μM) was flowed into the chamber and incubated for 2 min. A final wash with Buffer B was performed to remove any unbound PHS1 prior to data acquisition.

All single-molecule experiments were conducted at room temperature using a custom-built objective-type total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscope. The system was based on a Nikon ECLIPSE Ti2 inverted microscope frame equipped with a 100× oil-immersion objective (NA 1.49, Nikon). A 532 nm solid-state laser (Coherent Inc. OBIS) served as the excitation source for the Cy3 donor fluorophore. Fluorescence emission was captured by an electron-multiplying CCD (EMCCD) camera (Andor iXon Ultra 897). The emitted light was spectrally separated using a dichroic mirror (T635lpxr, Chroma) and bandpass filters (ET585/65 m for Cy3 and ET690/70 m for Cy5, Chroma) housed within a Dual-View image splitter (Photometrics). The entire hardware setup was controlled, and movies were acquired, using Cell Vision software (Beijing Coolight Technology).

Sm-FRET were extracted and filtered using the SPARTAN software (v3.7)54, a customized MATLAB (Mathworks) program with the following criteria: single-step donor photobleaching, total intensity ~2000 Counts, fewer than 4 donor blinking events, and FRET efficiency above 0.1 for a minimum of 10 consecutive frames. The filtered trajectories were analyzed using vbFRET55. The FRET efficiency histogram was calculated from >30 fluorescence trajectories, fitted with a two-Gaussian model to resolve distinct FRET states. To analyze dwell times from FRET traces, the traces were using the following procedure: each individual trace was subjected to two-state HaMMy fitting to identify transitions between Low and High FRET states. Using a custom-written MATLAB (MathWorks) script, the distribution and occurrence of Low and High FRET levels were determined. All consecutive data points assigned to the same FRET level were merged into a single step. The step lengths corresponding to each FRET state were then collected and statistically analyzed across all traces. Finally, dwell times were obtained by fitting the step length distributions with a single-exponential decay model56.

Grid preparation and data acquisition

The purified PHS1–DPE1 wild-type complex obtained from gel filtration fractions was used directly for cryo-EM sample preparation without additional treatment. To prepare samples with ligand-bound, MOSs of maltopentaose and maltoheptaose, they were individually added to the purified PHS1R709A-DPE1D391A mutant complex (both enzymes are catalytically inactive). The final concentration of both maltopentaose and maltoheptaose was adjusted to 5 mM to ensure adequate substrate binding. The ligand was incubated with the protein on ice for approximately 45 min prior to vitrification. For cryo-EM grid preparation, 3.5 μL aliquots of the PHS1–DPE1 complex (1.06 mg/mL) and the ligand-bound PHS1R709A-DPE1D391A mutant complex (1.08 mg/mL) were applied onto glow-discharged Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 300 mesh Cu grids (Quantifoil, MicroTools GmbH, Germany). The grids were blotted using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a blotting time of 3.5 s at 100% humidity and 8 °C, and then plunge-frozen into liquid ethane cooled by liquid nitrogen.

Cryo-EM datasets were collected automatically using the EPU software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a FEI Titan Krios transmission electron microscope operating at 300 kV57. The microscope was equipped with a K3 direct electron detector (Gatan) and a GIF BioQuantum energy filter (Gatan). For the PHS1–DPE1 complex, a total of 1160 images were collected at a defocus range of −1.2 to −1.8 μm and a nominal magnification of 105,000× in super-resolution mode, yielding a nominal pixel size of 0.84 Å. For the ligand-bound inactive PHS1–DPE1 complex, a total of 1,936 images were collected at a defocus range of −1.4 to −2.0 μm and a calibrated magnification of 105,000× in super-resolution mode, resulting in a nominal pixel size of 0.824 Å. Each movie stack consisted of 32–40 frames, with a total accumulated dose of 40–50 e− Å−2.

Cryo-EM data processing, model building, and refinement

The cryo-EM data processing was carried out using cryoSPARC v4.6.058. During motion correction, the dataset was binned, and the resulting micrographs were imported for contrast transfer function (CTF) correction and exposure curation. For the ligand-free PHS1–DPE1 complex, micrographs were sorted based on CTF quality, yielding a subset of 1073 micrographs. Initial particle picking was performed using the blob picker, followed by 2D classification to generate reference-free 2D classes. These classes were used for reference-based template picking, resulting in a total of 1,077,846 particles. After two rounds of 2D classification, ab initio reconstruction, and iterative rounds of 3D heterogeneous refinement and non-uniform refinement, a 3.0 Å density map was generated from 218,533 particles. Further CTF refinement and local refinement improved the resolution to 2.9 Å. Additionally, the template-picked particles were subjected to ab initio reconstruction with C2 symmetry, followed by iterative rounds of heterogeneous refinement and non-uniform refinement, producing a 2.7 Å density map from 243,835 particles. Final CTF refinement and local refinement resulted in a 2.5 Å density map.

For the ligand-bound PHS1–DPE1 complex, micrographs were binned during patch motion correction. After CTF estimation, particles were picked using the blob picker and curated through reference-free 2D classification. Selected 2D classes were used for reference-based template picking, yielding a total of 2,236,025 particles. These particles were processed through the same pipeline as the apo complex, including 2D classification, ab initio reconstruction, and heterogeneous refinement. Following non-uniform refinement and local refinement, a 3.1 Å density map was generated from 116,368 particles.

The atomic models of PHS1 and DPE1 were initially generated using the AlphaFold 3 server59, which served as the starting point for building the atomic models. For model building, the apo models of PHS1 and DPE1 were fitted into their corresponding cryo-EM density maps using UCSF ChimeraX60 and manually adjusted in COOT61. Ligands and their geometric constraints were generated using the Coot get monomer function, with glucose (GLC) as the reference. The final models for all datasets were refined using PHENIX62 and the real_space_refine application63, with secondary structure and geometry restraints applied. The structures were validated using the phenix.comprehensive_validation (cryo-EM) application, which assessed clash scores, MolProbity scores64, and Ramachandran plot statistics to ensure model quality. Resolution was estimated with the gold standard Fourier shell correlation 0.143 criterion. All structural figures were prepared using UCSF ChimeraX or Open-Source PyMOL (Schrödinger, LLC). Data collection and refinement statistics are given in Supplementary Table 1. Diagrams of the procedures for data processing of ligand-free and ligand-bound PHS1–DPE1 complex are presented in Supplementary Figs. 4 and 8, respectively.
