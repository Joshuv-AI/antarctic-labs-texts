Emergence of amoxicillin resistance in refractory Helicobacter pylori infection
Authors: Cheng-Yu Lin, Chia-Jung Kuo, Chih-Ho Lai, Wei-Pin Lin, Chun-Wei Chen, Wey-Ran Lin, Ming-Ling Chang, Yu-Pin Ho, Shu-Wei Huang, Ming-Yao Su, Cheng-Tang Chiu, Yin-Yi Chu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives:This study investigated temporal changes in resistance patterns among treatment-refractory Helicobacter pylori infections in Taiwan.Background:Antibiotic resistance in H. pylori is a growing global concern.Design:We retrospectively analyzed adult patients at Linkou Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (2018–2024) with ⩾2 prior eradication failures and confirmed active infection via [13C]-urea breath test.Methods:Gastric biopsy specimens were cultured, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the Epsilometer test for amoxicillin, clarithromycin, metronidazole, levofloxacin, tetracycline, rifabutin, ciprofloxacin, and moxifloxacin. Breakpoints were defined according to the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing.Results:Among 144 patients (mean age 57.1 ± 10.8 years; 27.8% male), 88 were culture positive (61.1%), yielding 109 isolates. Resistance rates were high for clarithromycin (89.9%), levofloxacin (86.2%), metronidazole (71.6%), and ciprofloxacin (87.2%), while tetracycline resistance was 15.6%. A temporal trend analysis revealed a marked and statistically significant increase in amoxicillin resistance (odds ratio per year, 1.60; 95% confidence interval, 1.31–1.96; p < 0.001), while resistance to other key antimicrobials remained persistently high but stable.Conclusion:In refractory H. pylori infection, there is persistently high resistance to clarithromycin, fluoroquinolones, and metronidazole, with a notable surge in amoxicillin resistance. These findings underscore the need for strengthened antimicrobial stewardship and reconsideration of empiric treatment strategies in high-resistance regions.Keywords: amoxicillin resistance, antibiotic resistance, refractory Helicobacter pylori infection

Background

Antibiotic resistance in Helicobacter pylori presents a significant and growing global health challenge. A recent meta-analysis (2013–2023) documented a worldwide increase in primary resistance, with rates being notably higher in adults than in pediatric populations. The patterns of resistance exhibit substantial heterogeneity based on antibiotic class and geographic region, with clarithromycin, metronidazole, and levofloxacin demonstrating critically high levels of resistance. 1 In the Asia-Pacific region, clarithromycin resistance has remained elevated, averaging approximately 22%, while metronidazole resistance reached 52% during the period from 1990 to 2022. 2 Data from a nationwide study in Taiwan between 2013 and 2019 revealed a significant upward trend in primary resistance to clarithromycin (increasing from 17.3% to 38.8%), levofloxacin, and metronidazole. During the same interval, dual resistance to clarithromycin and metronidazole also rose substantially from 2.4% to 10.4%. Conversely, resistance to amoxicillin and tetracycline remained low. 3 In a separate 5-year analysis of a Taiwanese cohort with prior eradication failure, resistance to clarithromycin (65%–75%) and metronidazole (30%–40%) was high but stable, suggesting a plateau at these elevated rates in treatment-experienced patients. 4 In recognition of this public health threat, the World Health Organization has classified clarithromycin-resistant H. pylori as a high-priority pathogen for research and development of new antibiotics. 5 The objective of this study was to analyze the temporal trends of H. pylori antibiotic resistance in a cohort of patients with multiple treatment failures in Taiwan.

Methods

Study design and patient population

The reporting of this study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement (Supplemental Material). 6 This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Linkou Chang Gung Memorial Hospital. Between January 2018 and December 2024, we consecutively enrolled adults with persistent H. pylori infection, defined as a history of at least two prior eradication treatment failures. All enrolled patients were referred for specialized evaluation. The presence of active H. pylori infection was confirmed using the [13C]-urea breath test.

Specimen collection, H. pylori culture, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing

All participants underwent esophagogastroduodenoscopy, during which four gastric biopsy specimens were collected—two from the antrum and two from the gastric body—for microbiological culture. The specimens of gastric biopsy were incubated at 37°C under microaerophilic conditions for 10–14 days. The minimal inhibitory concentration for H. pylori isolates to each antibiotic was determined by the Epsilometer test (AB Biodisc, Solna, Sweden), as described previously. 7 The cutoff points for antimicrobial resistance of H. pylori were defined as 0.12 mg/L for amoxicillin, 0.5 mg/L for clarithromycin, 8 mg/L for metronidazole, 1.0 mg/L for tetracycline, 1.0 mg/L for levofloxacin, and 1.0 mg/L for rifabutin, according to the guideline of European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST; https://www.eucast.org/fileadmin/src/media/PDFs/EUCAST_files/Breakpoint_tables/v_10.0_Breakpoint_Tables.pdf, accessed on January 2, 2021).

Statistical analysis

The clinical manifestations, recent medical insult, and initial biochemical data of the included patients are introduced with the calculation of descriptive statistics and compared with non-parametric tests using the SPSS statistical software (version 27.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are described as the mean with standard deviation. Categorical variables are described as numbers (percentages). To assess temporal trends in antimicrobial resistance for each antibiotic from 2018 to 2024, binary logistic regression was performed. The results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) per year with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-sided p-value of <0.05 was regarded as statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Demographic characteristics

The study cohort consisted of 144 patients with a mean age of 57.1 ± 10.8 years; 27.8% were male. The detailed patient selection process is illustrated in Figure 1. A total of 109 H. pylori isolates were obtained and available for antimicrobial susceptibility testing (Figure 2). Notably, only a limited number of isolates (4–8 per year) were obtained between 2020 and 2022, coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic in Taiwan.

Resistance rates of H. pylori strains between different years

The overall resistance rates were as follows: clarithromycin, 89.9%; amoxicillin, 41.3%; levofloxacin, 86.2%; tetracycline, 15.6%; metronidazole, 71.6%; rifabutin, 40.4%; ciprofloxacin, 87.2%; and moxifloxacin, 82.6%.

Year-to-year analysis (Figure 3) showed persistently high resistance to clarithromycin (81.3%–100%) and levofloxacin (80.0%–100%), with ciprofloxacin and moxifloxacin following similar trends. Metronidazole resistance remained high but fluctuated (56%–100%). Tetracycline and rifabutin exhibited lower and more variable resistance (0%–50% and 20%–56.3%, respectively).

CI, confidence interval.

A temporal trend analysis using logistic regression was performed to formally assess changes in resistance rates over the study period (Table 1). Of particular note, the analysis revealed a highly significant and steep increase in amoxicillin resistance (Figure 4) from 2018 to 2024 (OR per year, 1.60; 95% CI, 1.31–1.96; p < 0.001). In stark contrast, no significant temporal trends were observed for resistance to clarithromycin (p = 0.379), levofloxacin (p = 0.320), metronidazole (p = 0.674), or tetracycline (p = 0.898). Resistance rates for these antibiotics remained persistently high but stable throughout the study period.

Discussion

Amoxicillin is a key component of most regimens for H. pylori eradication because the primary amoxicillin resistance rate is relatively low in most countries. A meta-analysis of antimicrobial resistance in the Asia-Pacific region revealed that the primary resistance rate of amoxicillin was approximately 3%, compared with 20% and 21% for clarithromycin and levofloxacin, respectively. 8

Globally, the prevalence of amoxicillin resistance varies greatly. While resistance rates remain relatively low in some regions—such as Russia, where a recent review reported a stable and low prevalence of amoxicillin resistance despite a marked increase in clarithromycin resistance from 11.9% (pre-2015) to 21.0% (2020–2024) 9 —they are alarmingly high in many developing areas. Previous studies have documented rates as high as 72.6% in Africa, 10 20.7% in North India, 11 and 25.7% in Vietnam. 12

After failure of repeated unsuccessful eradication therapy, the amoxicillin resistance rate was increased. Our previous study reported that the amoxicillin resistance rate in patients after at least two courses of treatment was as high as 34.1%. 13 These findings indicate that an increase in amoxicillin use directly impacts the development of amoxicillin resistance in H. pylori.

Increased amoxicillin resistance may be linked to the higher consumption of penicillin–β-lactamase inhibitor combinations (e.g., ampicillin–sulbactam, amoxicillin–clavulanate) and carbapenems during the pandemic, as reported in Korea. 14 Similarly, a European study found that amoxicillin was among the most frequently prescribed antibiotics during the first wave of the pandemic in both primary and secondary healthcare settings. 15

Molecular studies indicate that amoxicillin resistance in H. pylori is primarily mediated by point mutations in the gene encoding penicillin-binding protein 1A (PBP1A). 16 Previous research also demonstrated that amoxicillin resistance in refractory H. pylori is associated with multiple, strain-specific PBP1A mutations in Taiwan. 17 Notably, our research group has previously characterized these molecular mechanisms within the same Taiwanese patient population with refractory infections. Our prior work demonstrated that amoxicillin resistance in these cases is highly correlated with multiple, strain-specific amino acid substitutions in the C-terminal region of PBP1A. Therefore, the sharp increase in phenotypic resistance reported in the present study likely reflects the intense selective pressure from antibiotic use, leading to the clonal expansion of H. pylori strains harboring these specific pbp1A mutations. This study provides the crucial epidemiological context for our previously elucidated molecular findings, supporting their growing clinical relevance.

Similarly, for other critical rescue therapies such as rifabutin, our group has also investigated the underlying resistance mechanisms. Resistance to rifabutin is known to be conferred by mutations in the rpoB gene 18 and our previous work has established the clinical utility of rifabutin-based regimens for treating patients infected with dual drug-resistant strains to clarithromycin and levofloxacin in Taiwan. 7

The widespread and often inappropriate empirical use of amoxicillin for various infections—without prior susceptibility testing—creates strong selective pressure, promoting the survival and dissemination of resistant H. pylori strains. This inappropriate antimicrobial use remains a key driver of the rapid rise in drug resistance. In fact, in the treatment of refractory H. pylori infection, antimicrobial susceptibility testing and genotypic resistance-guided therapy are more effective than empirical therapy.

The H. pylori culture success rate is 61.1%, which is modest compared to rates reported in other literature. 19

COVID-19 is associated with a wide spectrum of symptoms, with gastrointestinal manifestations being particularly prominent. Beyond its direct clinical impact, the pandemic profoundly disrupted healthcare systems worldwide, leading to changes in antibiotic prescription patterns. Several studies from Egypt, 20 Bulgaria, 21 and Croatia 22 have reported increased resistance of H. pylori during the pandemic, primarily among treatment-naïve patients. In addition, the pandemic has significantly altered healthcare-seeking behavior and disrupted conventional medical practices, leading to reduced outpatient visits 23 and fewer elective endoscopic procedures being performed. 24 Consequently, during this period, there has been a notable decline in H. pylori culture and antibiotic susceptibility testing (Figure 2), with a corresponding increase in empirical treatment rather than culture-guided therapy in Taiwan.

A pooled analysis of five randomized clinical trials in Taiwan (n = 2339) demonstrated that amoxicillin resistance significantly compromises the efficacy of amoxicillin-containing H. pylori regimens. 25 However, few studies have specifically addressed treatment strategies for amoxicillin-resistant strains. Evidence from patients with penicillin allergy suggests that bismuth-based quadruple therapy or quinolone-based regimens achieve high eradication rates without reliance on amoxicillin, 26 and these may represent suitable alternatives for patients with confirmed amoxicillin resistance.

According to the Maastricht VI/Florence Consensus Report 27 on the management of H. pylori infection, the recommended approach after the failure of first-line clarithromycin-based triple therapy and second-line levofloxacin-based triple therapy is bismuth-based quadruple therapy. However, in settings where bismuth is unavailable, high-dose PPI-amoxicillin dual therapy or a rifabutin-containing regimen should be considered as alternatives. However, rising amoxicillin resistance challenges these recommendations and demands further investigation.

This study has several limitations. First, a single-referred center design may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, the reduced patient volume during the COVID-19 pandemic represents an important constraint. Finally, this study was intentionally focused on the clinical epidemiology and phenotypic surveillance of emerging resistance trends. While we did not perform concurrent molecular sequencing on the isolates from this specific cohort, the underlying genetic mechanisms of amoxicillin resistance in our patient population have been previously elucidated by our group, as discussed above. Future large-scale, multicenter prospective studies are warranted to continuously monitor both the phenotypic and genotypic evolution of H. pylori resistance in Taiwan.

Conclusion

In refractory H. pylori infection, resistance to clarithromycin, fluoroquinolones, and metronidazole remains persistently high. The key finding of this study is the specific and alarming surge in amoxicillin resistance in recent years. These findings highlight the urgency of antimicrobial stewardship, the necessity of susceptibility testing, and the reconsideration of empiric treatment strategies in high-resistance regions. Emerging amoxicillin resistance in refractory H. pylori infection represents a major clinical challenge.
