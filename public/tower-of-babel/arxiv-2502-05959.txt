Ensemble-Tight Second-Order Asymptotics and Exponents for Guessing-Based Decoding with Abandonment
Vincent Y. F. Tan, Senior Member, IEEE Hamdi Joudeh, Member, IEEE

Abstract
This paper considers guessing-based decoders with abandonment for discrete memoryless channels in which all codewords have the same composition. This class of decoders rank-orders all input sequences in the codebook’s composition class from “closest” to “farthest” from the channel output and then queries them sequentially in that order for codebook membership. Decoding terminates when a codeword is encountered or when a predetermined number of guesses is reached, and decoding is abandoned. We derive ensemble-tight first-order asymptotics for the code rate and abandonment rate, which shows that guessing-based decoding is more efficient than conventional testing-based decoding whenever the capacity of the channel exceeds half the entropy of the capacity-achieving input distribution. The main focus of this paper is on refined asymptotics, specifically, second-order asymptotics, error exponents, and strong converse exponents. The optimal second-order region is characterized in terms of the minimum of the second-order code and abandonment rates. The error (resp. strong converse) exponent is characterized in terms of the minimum (resp. maximum) of the usual channel coding exponent and an abandonment exponent, which turns out to be a special case of the exponent of conditional almost-lossless source coding.



I
Introduction

Consider a discrete memoryless channel (DMC) with finite input and output alphabets
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, respectively, and with transition probability law
W:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
.
A transmitter wishes to communicate a message from the set
[M_{n}]:=\{1,2,\ldots,M_{n}\}
to a receiver
over
n
uses of the DMC.
To communicate
w\in[M_{n}]
, the transmitter sends a corresponding length-
n
codeword
\bm{x}(w)\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
.
We assume that the employed codebook
\mathcal{C}_{n}:=\{\bm{x}(1),\bm{x}(2),\ldots,\bm{x}(M_{n})\}
has fixed composition, i.e., all codewords come from the same type class.

In proving coding theorems for the DMC, a standard approach is to analyze a randomly generated codebook
\mathcal{C}_{n}
in tandem with a decoding procedure that, given a channel output
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{n}
, tests all codewords in
\mathcal{C}_{n}
and selects one which maximizes some decoding metric.
Notable special cases include standard maximum likelihood (ML) decoding
[
1
]
and maximum mutual information (MMI) decoding
[
2
]
.
We shall refer to this conventional approach as
testing-based decoding
, due to its roots in
M_{n}
-ary hypothesis testing.

In this paper, we follow a different approach to channel decoding based on guessing (or guesswork)
[
3
,
4
]
, which works as follows.
Given a channel output
\bm{y}
, the decoder rank-orders all sequences
\bm{x}
, codewords or otherwise, in the codebook’s type class from “closest” to “farthest” from
\bm{y}
with respect to some “distance” measure, and then queries them sequentially in that order for codebook membership.
Decoding stops at the first instance a codeword is encountered, or if a predetermined maximum number of guesses
m_{n}
is reached. In the latter case, search is abandoned and an error is declared.
We shall refer to this approach as
guessing-based decoding with abandonment
, or
guessing-based decoding
for short.

In a somewhat loose sense, the above procedure searches for the closest codeword to the output
\bm{y}
, contained in an input-space preimage of some neighborhood of
\bm{y}
.
Intuitively, if the DMC is not too noisy, then it suffices to choose a maximum number of guesses
m_{n}
that is much smaller than the number of codewords
M_{n}
; rendering guessing-based decoding more efficient than testing-based decoding in terms of search complexity (i.e., the number of queried sequences).
1

1

This intuition is made concrete later on in this paper, and is done so via three asymptotic regimes—second-order asymptotics

]
, error exponents (or reliability function)
[
1
,
7
]
, and strong converse exponents
[
8
,

]
.

The guessing-based decoding approach we describe above is directly inspired by guessing random additive noise decoding (GRAND), proposed by Duffy
et al.

[
10
]
for discrete modulo-sum channels, where the output is a modulo-sum of the input and independent noise (all drawn from the same alphabet).

Note that the idea of decoding by guessing the noise pattern has a long history predating GRAND, e.g., syndrome decoding of linear codes.

GRAND rank-orders noise sequences from most to least likely and subtracts them sequentially from the channel output, at each stage checking if the result is a codeword, and stopping at the first encountered codeword or if a maximum number of guesses is reached.
Various extensions of GRAND have appeared since then, e.g.,
[
11
,

,
13
,
14
,
15
,

, mostly focusing on Gaussian channels and practical implementations. The original discrete setting in
[
10
]
is most relevant to our current work.

Viewed in light of the guessing-based decoding procedure described earlier, and under memoryless noise, GRAND can be seen as a special case that exploits the structure of modulo-sum channels, i.e., an ordered list of noise sequences induces an ordered list of input sequences by subtraction from the channel output sequence.
A main motivation of our current paper is to extend the GRAND paradigm to general
DMCs without the additive structure.

I-A

Contributions

For a channel code that employs guessing-based decoding with abandonment, define the
code rate
and the
abandonment rate
respectively by

R_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\log M_{n}\ \ \text{and}\ \ r_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\log m_{n}.

For any given decoding error probability, it is clearly desirable to make
R_{n}
as large as possible (efficiency) while keeping
r_{n}
as small as possible (complexity).
Our three main contributions in this paper are as follows.

1.

We characterize the asymptotic first- and second-order fundamental limits for
R_{n}
and
r_{n}
for non-vanishing error probabilities. These characterizations, as well as those involving exponents below, involve direct (achievability) and ensemble converse parts. The latter is deemed appropriate in this setting since we consider a
pre-specified
and
fixed
class of coding schemes with random constant composition codebooks and guessing-based decoders. The first-order result suggests that guessing-based decoding is more efficient than testing-based decoding whenever the capacity
C(W)
of the DMC
W
exceeds half the entropy of the capacity-achieving input distribution (CAID)
H(P_{X})
. We show that the optimal second-order region is characterized in terms of the
minimum
of the second-order code and abandonment rates.

2.

We also characterize ensemble-tight error exponents for guessing-based decoding with abandonment. This regime is of interest for code rates
R=R_{n}

below
the channel capacity
C(W)
and abandonment rates
r=r_{n}
above the conditional entropy
H(X|Y)
(evaluated at the CAID). Denoting the usual random coding exponent for constant composition codes
[
7
]
at rate
R
as
E_{\mathrm{r}}(R,P_{X})
, where
P_{X}
here is the composition limiting distribution, our result shows that the error exponent for our setting when
R
is larger than the critical rate
R_{\mathrm{cr}}
and
r
is smaller than
H(P_{X})-R_{\mathrm{cr}}
is
E_{\mathrm{r}}(\max\{R,H(P_{X})-r\},P_{X})
, underscoring again that the performance of the system is dominated by either the code rate
R
being large or the abandonment rate
r
being small.

3.

Finally, considering code rates
above

C(W)
or abandonment rates
below

H(X|Y)
, we derive ensemble-tight strong converse exponents for the decoder under consideration. Denoting a sphere-packing variant of the strong converse exponent
[
9
,
8
,
17
]
at rate
R
and a composition limiting distribution
P_{X}
as
K_{\mathrm{sp}}(R,P_{X})
, analogous to the error exponent setting, our result shows that the ensemble strong converse exponent is
K_{\mathrm{sp}}(\max\{R,H(P_{X})-r\},P_{X})
.

Taken together, the above results suggest that either the code rate or the abandonment rate dominates the behavior of the ensemble error probability in the three asymptotic regimes considered.
To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first work that conducts second-order and comprehensive exponent analyses, with ensemble converses, for guessing-based decoding with abandonment
[
10
]
.

Before we proceed, it is worth noting that while the original work by Duffy
et al.

[
10
]
also treats error and success probability exponents for guessing-based decoding, the analysis there is only applicable to the class of
symmetric additive
channels, and is limited to achievability results with no converses (see also
[
16
]
).
Moreover, the analysis of abandonment in
[
10
]
is focused on the case
r=H(X|Y)
, and does not consider the whole range of abandonment rates. On the flip side, the tools we use in the current paper are applicable mainly to memoryless channels, while those used in
[
10
,
16
]
apply more broadly to channels with memory (albeit in the restricted class of modulo-sum channels).

II
Setting and Preliminaries

We consider a DMC as described in Section
I
, and a coding scheme with a constant composition codebook
\mathcal{C}_{n}
of
M_{n}
length-
n
codewords and a guessing-based decoder with at most
m_{n}
guesses.
We denote the composition (i.e., type) of
\mathcal{C}_{n}
by
P_{X}^{(n)}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}(\mathcal{X})
.
Given
\bm{y}
, the decoder rank-orders all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{T}_{P_{X}^{(n)}}
and queries them one by one for membership in
\mathcal{C}_{n}
, until a codeword if found or guessing is abandoned.

We are interested in universal decoding rules that do not explicitly depend on the channel transition law, and hence we propose to rank input sequences in an increasing order of their empirical conditional entropy
\hat{H}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})
, breaking ties arbitrarily.
Let
\hat{G}:\mathcal{T}_{P_{X}^{(n)}}\times\mathcal{Y}^{n}\to[1:|\mathcal{T}_{P_{X}^{(n)}}|]
be a rank function such that
\hat{G}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})
is the order in which
\bm{x}
is guessed given that
\bm{y}
is received.
The rank function of choice satisfies

\displaystyle\hat{G}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})<\hat{G}(\bm{x}^{\prime}|\bm{y})

\displaystyle\implies\hat{H}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})\leq\hat{H}(\bm{x}^{\prime}|\bm{y})

(5)

\displaystyle\hat{H}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})<\hat{H}(\bm{x}^{\prime}|\bm{y})

\displaystyle\implies\hat{G}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})<\hat{G}(\bm{x}^{\prime}|\bm{y})

(6)

for any pair of input sequences
\bm{x}
and
\bm{x}^{\prime}
from
\mathcal{T}_{P_{X}^{(n)}}
.

With the above rank function, if guessing is allowed to proceed without abandonment, by choosing
m_{n}=|\mathcal{T}_{P_{X}^{(n)}}|
, then it is not difficult to verify that the decoded codeword minimizes the empirical conditional entropy, which is known as minimum entropy (ME) decoding. Since we are using constant composition codes, this is equivalent to maximizing the empirical mutual information
\hat{I}(\bm{x}\wedge\bm{y})
, known as maximum-mutual information (MMI) decoding
[
2
]
.
MMI decoding
[
2
]
is known to achieve capacity, the reliability function above the critical rate
[
7
]
, and optimal second-order asymptotics when used in combination with an appropriately chosen sequence of constant composition codes
[
19
]
.
The main question we wish to answer is that of how small can we make the maximum number of guesses
m_{n}
(or the abandonment rate
r_{n}
), while still achieving near-MMI performances?
To this end, we seek to characterize the set of achievable code-abandonment rate pairs
(R_{n},r_{n})
in the asymptotic regime of large
n
. What we mean by this will be made precise in what follows.

II-A

Decoding Rule and Ensemble Error Probability

To simplify the error analysis, we shall assume that ties in the empirical conditional entropy are also declared as errors. Such events can be detected by slightly modifying the guessing procedure as follows. If a codeword
\bm{x}
is encountered, then guessing continues until all
equivalent
conditional type classes are exhausted, and the decoder declares an error if at least one more codeword is found.
By “equivalent” conditional type classes, we mean all
\mathcal{T}_{V_{X|Y}}(\bm{y})
such that the conditional entropies
H(V_{X|Y}|\hat{P}_{\bm{y}})
are equal.
Moreover, abandonment is not allowed to occur before equivalent conditional type classes are exhausted.
With this in mind, it helps to define the slightly modified rank function

G(\bm{x}|\bm{y})=\sum_{V_{X|Y}:H(V_{X|Y}|\hat{P}_{\bm{y}})\leq\hat{H}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})}\big|\mathcal{T}_{V_{X|Y}}(\bm{y})\big|.

(7)

Note that
G(\cdot|\bm{y})
is not one-to-one; this is in contrast to
\hat{G}(\cdot|\bm{y})
.

Given that
\bm{x}(w)\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
is sent, and with the modified rank function in (
7
), an error is made if the channel produces an output
\bm{y}
such that
G(\bm{x}(\bar{w})|\bm{y})\leq G(\bm{x}(w)|\bm{y})
for some
\bar{w}\in[M_{n}]
other than
w
, or
G(\bm{x}(w)|\bm{y})>m_{n}
.
Given the codebook
\mathcal{C}_{n}
, let
\varepsilon_{n}(\mathcal{C}_{n})
be the error probability of the guessing-based decoder averaged over messages.
An
(n,R_{n},r_{n})
-code
is specified by a codebook
\mathcal{C}_{n}
of length-
n
codewords and rate
R_{n}
, and a guessing-based decoder with abandonment rate
r_{n}
. This code has corresponding error probability
\varepsilon_{n}(\mathcal{C}_{n})
.

We consider random coding ensembles in which each codeword
\bm{X}(w)
,
w\in[M_{n}]
, is drawn uniformly at random from a type class
\mathcal{T}_{P_{X}^{(n)}}
independent of other codewords. Furthermore, the sequence of types
\{P_{X}^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is assumed to converge to a particular distribution
P_{X}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
(oftentimes a CAID). In this case, the codebook
\mathsf{C}_{n}
is random and the
ensemble average error probability

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n}:=\mathbbmss{E}[\varepsilon_{n}(\mathsf{C}_{n})]=\sum_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}\mathbbmss{P}[\mathsf{C}_{n}=\mathcal{C}_{n}]\varepsilon_{n}(\mathcal{C}_{n})

(8)

is computed by taking a further average over
\mathcal{C}_{n}
. We call such a random code ensemble an
(n,R_{n},r_{n},P_{X}^{(n)})
-code
.

II-B

Alternative Ensembles and Decoding Rules

For the remainder of this paper, we focus exclusively on:

(i)

Ensembles of codes with fixed composition
P_{X}^{(n)}
;

(ii)

The use of a guessing-based decoder with abandonment, employing the modified rank function (
7
).

Before proceeding, one might wonder how the results would be impacted by adopting variations of our setting such as the following:

(I)

Ensembles of i.i.d. random codes with distribution
P_{X}
;

(II)

The use of a guessing-based decoder with abandonment based on a non-universal criterion, e.g., maximum-likelihood or maximum a posteriori decoding.

Given the well-known similarities between (i) and (I) across various asymptotic regimes (such as the second-order regime), as well as between (ii) and (II), we expect that the theorems to follow will remain valid if we replace (i) with (I), (ii) with (II), or both.

III
Ensemble-Tight First-Order Rates

Here we present a first-order rate result for guessing-based decoding as described in previous sections. While this may be of interest in its own right, our main goal is to derive a first-order
capacity
pair, around which we “center” the second-order rate analysis in the next section. In this and next section,
P_{X}
always denotes a CAID. To commence, we formally define the notion of first-order rates.

Definition 1
.

The pair of first-order rates
(R,r)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
is
(\varepsilon,P_{X})
-achievable
if the sequence of
(n,R_{n},r_{n},P_{X}^{(n)})
-codes satisfies (i)
P_{X}^{(n)}
converges to the CAID
P_{X}
and (ii) the code and abandonment rates and random-coding error probabilities
\{\varepsilon_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfy

\liminf_{n\to\infty}R_{n}\geq R,\ \ \limsup_{n\to\infty}r_{n}\leq r,\ \text{and}\ \limsup_{n\to\infty}\varepsilon_{n}\leq\varepsilon.

The closure of the set of all
(\varepsilon,P_{X})
-achievable first-order rates for the DMC
W
is denoted as
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}^{*}(P_{X},W)
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
P_{X}\in\Pi(W)
. Then, for all
\varepsilon\in[0,1)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}^{*}(P_{X},W)\!=\!\bigg\{(R,r):\parbox[c]{128.6405pt}{$\qquad\qquad\quad\;\;\,0\leq R\leq C(W)$, \vskip 2.8903pt\\
$H_{P_{X}\times W}(X|Y)\leq r\leq H(P_{X})$}\!\!\bigg\}.

(9)

Theorem
1
implies that the optimal rate pair is
(R,r)=(I(P_{X},W),H(P_{X})-I(P_{X},W))
, where
P_{X}\in\Pi(W)
.
Thus, when operating at these optimal rates, guessing-based decoding is more efficient than testing-based decoding whenever
I(P_{X},W)>\frac{1}{2}H(P_{X})
; this has also been observed in
[
16
]
, albeit in the restricted context of modulo-sum channels.

III-A

Proof of Theorem
1

Due to symmetry in the random codebook ensemble,
\varepsilon_{n}
remains unchanged if we condition on the event that message
1
is sent. Therefore, we can write
\varepsilon_{n}=\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{A}_{1}\right]
, where the incorrect decoding event
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and the abandonment event
\mathcal{A}_{1}
for message
1
are respectively defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{w\neq 1}\left\{G(\bm{X}(w)|\bm{Y})\leq G(\bm{X}(1)|\bm{Y})\right\}\quad\mbox{and}

(10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}

\displaystyle:=\left\{G(\bm{X}(1)|\bm{Y})>\mathrm{e}^{nr_{n}}\right\}.

(11)

Therefore,

is bounded from above and below as follows

(12)

Before we proceed, it is useful to note from (
7

\mathrm{e}^{n\hat{H}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})}\leq G(\bm{x}|\bm{y})\leq(n+1)^{|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|}\mathrm{e}^{n\hat{H}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})}.

(13)

Now for the achievability part, consider a sequence of
(n,R,r,P_{X}^{(n)})
-codes for which

and

.
Using (

), the probability of incorrect decoding is bounded above as

\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}\right]\leq\mathbbmss{P}\Bigg[\bigcup_{w\neq 1}\big\{\hat{H}(\bm{X}(w)|\bm{Y})\leq\hat{H}(\bm{X}(1)|\bm{Y})+\delta_{n}\big\}\Bigg]

(14)

where
\delta_{n}=\frac{|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|}{n}\log(n+1)
.

Hence
\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}\right]
is
bounded (up to the presence of the remainder term

) by the ensemble average error probability
achieved by the ME (therefore the MMI) decoder. From standard analysis and results, we know that
\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}\right]
tends to zero as

grows large.
On the other hand, using the upper bound in (

\displaystyle\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{A}_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbbmss{P}\big[\hat{H}(\bm{X}|\bm{Y})\geq r-\delta_{n}\big]

(15)

\displaystyle=\mathbbmss{P}\big[\hat{I}(\bm{X}\wedge\bm{Y})\leq I(P_{X},W)-\delta+\delta_{n}\big]

(16)

Using properties of conditional types
[
7
]
, we can show that the probability in (

) tends to

).
As a result,
\varepsilon_{n}\leq\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}\right]+\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{A}_{1}\right]\to 0
as

.

Next, we show an ensemble strong converse. To this end, consider a sequence of
(n,R_{n},r_{n},P_{X}^{(n)})
-codes with rates that satisfy at least one of the following two conditions:

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}R_{n}

\displaystyle\geq R=I(P_{X},W)+\delta\ \ \text{or}

(17)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}r_{n}

\displaystyle\leq r=H_{P_{X}\times W}(X|Y)-\delta

IV
Ensemble-Tight Second-Order Rates

In this section, we derive ensemble-tight second-order asymptotics for guessing-based decoding with abandonment.
Since the error probability is a function of both the code and abandonment rates, we consider the scenario in which the two rates involved deviate from their first-order fundamental limits derived in Theorem
1
by terms that vanish as
\Theta\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\big)
. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 2
.

The pair of second-order rates
(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is
(\varepsilon,P_{X})
-achievable

-codes satisfies (i)

converges to

\displaystyle P_{X}\in\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\argmin_{P\in\Pi(W)}V(P,W)&\mbox{if }s\wedge t\geq 0\\
\argmax_{P\in\Pi(W)}V(P,W)&\mbox{if }s\wedge t<0\end{array}\right.

and (ii) the code and abandonment rates and random-coding error probabilities
\{\varepsilon_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfy

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{n}\big(R_{n}-C(W)\big)

\displaystyle\geq-s,

(24)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{n}\big(r_{n}-H_{P_{X}\times W}(X|Y)\big)

\displaystyle\leq t,\quad\mbox{and}

(25)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\varepsilon_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon.

(26)

The closure of the set of all

In view of Theorem

Unlike most works on second-order rates

[

Theorem 3

For any

E^{*}(R,r)=\max_{P_{X}}E^{*}(R,r,P_{X})

Using the known properties of the random coding exponent
E_{\mathrm{r}}
and the sphere packing exponent

For any fixed

In the regime

Here we can see two distinct effects that influence the ensemble error exponent in relation to the code rate

and the abandonment rate

the side information, a result that can be deduced from
[

,
32
,
33

Finally, by setting

\mathrm{e}^{nR}
guesses while noise-centric decoding
[

\mathrm{e}^{nr}

Proof of the Achievability Part of Theorem
3

Fix an
input distribution
P_{X}
and a sequence of
n
-types
P_{X}^{(n)}
that converges to
P_{X}
. Since the approximation of distribution by types does not affect the exponents (by continuity), for brevity, we write
P_{X}^{(n)}
as
P_{X}
in this and subsequent proofs.

Recall from (
12
) that the ensemble average error probability is bounded above as
\varepsilon_{n}\leq\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}\right]+\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{A}_{1}\right]
.
Next, we treat the incorrect decoding probability
\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}\right]
and the abandonment probability
\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{A}_{1}\right]
separately. Starting with
\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}\right]=\mathbbmss{P}\bigg[\bigcup_{w\neq 1}\left\{G(\bm{X}(w)|\bm{Y})\leq G(\bm{X}(1)|\bm{Y})\right\}\bigg]

(62)

\displaystyle=\mathbbmss{E}\Bigg[\mathbbmss{P}\bigg[\bigcup_{w\neq 1}\left\{G(\bm{X}(w)|\bm{Y})\leq G(\bm{X}(1)|\bm{Y})\right\}\Big|\bm{X}(1),\bm{Y}\bigg]\Bigg]

(63)

\displaystyle\leq\!\mathbbmss{E}\Bigg[\!\min\bigg\{\!1,\!\sum_{w\neq 1}\!\mathbbmss{P}\big[G(\bm{X}(w)|\bm{Y})\!\leq\!G(\bm{X}(1)|\bm{Y})\big|\bm{X}(1),\bm{Y}\big]\!\bigg\}\!\Bigg]

(64)

\displaystyle=\mathbbmss{E}\Big[\min\big\{1,(\mathrm{e}^{nR}-1)\mathbbmss{P}\left[G(\overline{\bm{X}}|\bm{Y})\leq G(\bm{X}|\bm{Y})\big|\bm{X},\bm{Y}\right]\big\}\Big]

(65)

\displaystyle=\mathbbmss{E}\Big[\min\big\{1,(\mathrm{e}^{nR}-1)\Psi(\bm{X},\bm{Y})\big\}\Big],

(66)

where, as in Section
IV-A
,
\overline{\bm{X}}\sim P_{\bm{X}}
is independent of
(\bm{X},\bm{Y})
, distributed identically as
\bm{X}
, and
\Psi(\bm{X},\bm{Y})
is as defined in (
37
).
Note that (

) is the so-called random coding union bound (RCU)
[
20
]
, or more precisely, a mismatched version of it
[
34
]
.

According to Lemma
1
, we can upper bound
\Psi(\bm{x},\bm{y})
by
(n+1)^{3|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|}\mathrm{e}^{-n\hat{I}(\bm{x}\wedge\bm{y})}
. Plugging this upper bound back into (
66
), while conditioning on
\bm{X}=\bm{x}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}|\bm{X}=\bm{x}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{n}}W^{n}(\bm{y}|\bm{x})\min\left\{1,(n+1)^{3|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|}\mathrm{e}^{nR-n\hat{I}(\bm{x}\wedge\bm{y})}\right\}

(67)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{n}(\mathcal{Y};P_{X})}\mathrm{e}^{-nD(V\|W|P_{X})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\min\left\{1,(n+1)^{3|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|}\mathrm{e}^{nR-nI(P_{X},V)}\right\}

(68)

\displaystyle\leq(n\!+\!1)^{3|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|}\!\!\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{n}(\mathcal{Y};P_{X})}\!\mathrm{e}^{-nD(V\|W|P_{X})}\mathrm{e}^{-n|I(P_{X},V)-R|^{+}}

(69)

\displaystyle\leq(n\!+\!1)^{4|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|}\max_{V\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}|\mathcal{X})}\mathrm{e}^{-n(D(V\|W|P_{X})+|I(P_{X},V)-R|^{+})}

(70)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-n(E_{\mathrm{r}}(R,P_{X})-4\delta_{n})},

(71)

where (
68
) follows from splitting the sum over
\bm{y}
into
V
-shells and noting that
W^{n}(\mathcal{T}_{V}(\bm{x})|\bm{x})\leq\mathrm{e}^{-nD(V\|W|P_{X})}

[
7
, Lemma 2.6]
and in (
71
),
\delta_{n}:=\frac{|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|}{n}\log(n+1)
.
Since the bound only depends on
\bm{x}
through its type
P_{X}
, and
\bm{X}
is supported on the type class
\mathcal{T}_{P_{X}}
, then the bound is also valid for
\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}\right]
.

Now we proceed to bound the probability of abandonment

\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{A}_{1}\right]=\mathbbmss{P}\left[G(\bm{X}(1)|\bm{Y})>\mathrm{e}^{nr}\right]=\mathbbmss{P}\left[G(\bm{X}|\bm{Y})>\mathrm{e}^{nr}\right].

(72)

From the upper bound in (
13
), we know that
G(\bm{x}|\bm{y})>\mathrm{e}^{nr}
implies that
\hat{H}(\bm{x}|\bm{y})\geq r-\delta_{n}
.
We proceed to bound
\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{A}_{1}\right]
while conditioning on
\bm{X}=\bm{x}
as follows

\displaystyle\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{A}_{1}|\bm{X}=\bm{x}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbbmss{P}\big[\hat{H}(\bm{x}|\bm{Y})\geq r-\delta_{n}\big]

(73)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{n}}W^{n}(\bm{y}|\bm{x})\mathbbm{1}\big[\hat{I}(\bm{x}\wedge\bm{y})\leq\hat{H}(\bm{x})-r+\delta_{n}\big]

(74)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{n}(\mathcal{Y};P_{X})}\mathrm{e}^{-nD(V\|W|P_{X})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\mathbbm{1}\left[I(P_{X},V)\leq H(P_{X})-r+\delta_{n}\right]

(75)

\displaystyle\leq(n+1)^{|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|}\max_{V:I(P_{X},V)\leq H(P_{X})-r+\delta_{n}}\mathrm{e}^{-nD(V\|W|P_{X})}

(76)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-nE_{\mathrm{a}}(r-\delta_{n},P_{X})+n\delta_{n}}.

(77)

Yet again, the bound depends on
\bm{x}
only through its type
P_{X}
, and is therefore valid for
\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{A}_{1}\right]
.

From the two bounds, we proceed to upper bound

as

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{-n(E_{\mathrm{r}}(R,P_{X})-4\delta_{n})}+\mathrm{e}^{-n(E_{\mathrm{a}}(r-\delta_{n},P_{X})-\delta_{n})}

(78)

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathrm{e}^{4n\delta_{n}}\max\Big\{\mathrm{e}^{-nE_{\mathrm{r}}(R,P_{X})},\mathrm{e}^{-nE_{\mathrm{a}}(r-\delta_{n},P_{X})}\Big\}

(79)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-n(\min\{E_{\mathrm{r}}(R,P_{X}),E_{\mathrm{a}}(r-\delta_{n},P_{X})\}-4\delta_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\log 2)}.

(80)

Since
\delta_{n}\to 0
as

, and by continuity, it follows that

\!\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}}\!\geq\!\min\{E_{\mathrm{r}}(R,P_{X},W),E_{\mathrm{a}}(r,P_{X},W)\}

(81)

which concludes the achievability part.

Proof of the Ensemble Converse Part of Theorem
3

We now show that (

For this, we start from
\varepsilon_{n}\geq\max\left\{\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}\right],\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{A}_{1}\right]\right\}

Mirroring the achievability proof, we lower bound

and

Starting with
\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}\right]

; see also Scarlett
et al.

[

]
. Here, the mutual independence of the codewords
\bm{X}(w)
,

, in the random codebook ensemble is essential. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}\right]\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathbbmss{E}\Big[\min\big\{1,(\mathrm{e}^{nR}-1)\Psi(\bm{X},\bm{Y})\big\}\Big].

(82)

Next, we recall from Lemma
1
that
\Psi(\bm{x},\bm{y})
is lower bounded by
(n\!+\!1)^{-2|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|}\mathrm{e}^{-n\hat{I}(\bm{x}\wedge\bm{y})}

\bm{X}=\bm{x}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}|\bm{X}=\bm{x}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{n}}W^{n}(\bm{y}|\bm{x})\min\left\{1,(n\!+\!1)^{-2|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|}\mathrm{e}^{nR-n\hat{I}(\bm{x}\wedge\bm{y})}\right\}

(83)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{n}(\mathcal{Y};P_{X})}(n+1)^{-|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|}\mathrm{e}^{-nD(V\|W|P_{X})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\min\left\{1,(n+1)^{-2|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|}\mathrm{e}^{nR-nI(P_{X},V)}\right\}

(84)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{-nE_{\mathrm{r}}(R,P_{X})-3n\delta_{n}},

(85)

where (
85
) follows from similar steps as those leading to (
71
).

Next, we proceed to lower bound
\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{A}_{1}\right]
.
Conditioning on
\bm{X}=\bm{x}
, and recalling that

and

both take positive integer values, we observe that

\displaystyle\mathbbmss{P}\big[\mathcal{A}_{1}|\bm{X}=\bm{x}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbbmss{P}\big[G(\bm{x}|\bm{Y})>\mathrm{e}^{nr}\big]

(86)

\displaystyle=\mathbbmss{P}\big[G(\bm{x}|\bm{Y})\geq\mathrm{e}^{nr}+1\big]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbbmss{P}\big[G(\bm{x}|\bm{Y})\geq 2\mathrm{e}^{nr}\big].

(88)

From the lower bound in (

), we know that

.
In what follows, we let

\displaystyle\mathbbmss{P}\big[\mathcal{A}_{1}|\bm{X}=\bm{x}\big]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbbmss{P}\big[\hat{H}(\bm{x}|\bm{Y})\geq r+\delta_{n}^{\prime}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbbmss{P}\big[\hat{I}(\bm{x}\wedge\bm{Y})\leq\hat{H}(\bm{x})-r-\delta_{n}^{\prime}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{n}(\mathcal{Y};P_{X})}\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{T}_{V}(\bm{x})}W^{n}(\bm{y}|\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-nE_{\mathrm{a}}(r+\delta_{n}^{\prime},P_{X})-n\delta_{n}}.

(93)

VI
Ensemble-Tight Strong Converse Exponents

In this section, we consider the regime in which the ensemble probability of correct decoding
1-\varepsilon_{n}
decays exponentially fast with the blocklength. The exponential rate of decay of
1-\varepsilon_{n}
is known as the
strong converse exponent

[
8
,
9
]
. This regime is of interest, in usual channel coding with arbitrary decoders and in the absence of abandonment, when the code rate is
above
the channel capacity
C(W)
. The main result in this section (Theorem
4
) implies that the strong converse exponent is positive if
either
the code rate is larger
C(W)

or
the abandonment rate
r
is smaller than
H_{P_{X}\times W}(X|Y)
.

For fixed
R,r,P_{X}
, and
W
, we define

\displaystyle K_{\mathrm{r}}(R,P_{X})

\displaystyle:=\min_{V}D(V\|W|P_{X})+|R-I(P_{X},V)|^{+},

(94)

\displaystyle K_{\mathrm{sp}}(R,P_{X})

\displaystyle:=\min_{V:I(P_{X},V)\geq R}D(V\|W|P_{X}).

(95)

The subscripts
\mathrm{r}
and
\mathrm{sp}
allude to the fact that these two functions are the strong converse counterparts of the
r
andom coding and
s
phere-
p
acking error exponents.
It is clear that
K_{\mathrm{r}}(R,P_{X})\leq K_{\mathrm{sp}}(R,P_{X})
. Arimoto
[
8
]
demonstrated that the strong converse exponent for the DMC
W
is at least
\min_{P_{X}}K_{\mathrm{r}}(R,P_{X})
, proving this converse result in an equivalent dual form. Later, Omura
[
17
]
proved that
\min_{P_{X}}K_{\mathrm{sp}}(R,P_{X})
is achievable, thereby establishing an upper bound on the strong converse exponent. Subsequently, Dueck and Körner
[
9
]
refined Omura’s approach by employing a code extension argument, showing that
\min_{P_{X}}K_{\mathrm{r}}(R,P_{X})
is also achievable. This result ultimately established the strong converse exponent for the DMC
W
for all rates
R
.

It was claimed by Oohama
[
36
, first part of Property 3(b)]
that
K_{\mathrm{r}}(R,P_{X})=K_{\mathrm{sp}}(R,P_{X})
for all
0\leq R\leq\log|\mathcal{X}|
. However, the proof in
[
36
]
contains a gap. In particular, it is claimed
5
5

5

The error in the first part of Property 3(b) in
[
36
]
was confirmed by Prof. Oohama to the authors in a private communication.
that the function
g(V):=-I(P,V)+D(V\|W|P)
is
linear
. Unfortunately, one can only verify that
g(V)
is convex but not necessarily linear.

In analogy to Definitions
2
and
3
, we define the ensemble strong converse exponent as follows.

Definition 4
.

Fixed a limiting distribution
P_{X}
. The strong converse exponent
K\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is
achievable
at rate pair
(R,r)
if the sequence of
(n,R,r,P_{X}^{(n)})
-codes with
random-coding
error probabilities
\{\varepsilon_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon_{n}}

\displaystyle\leq K.

(96)

The infimum of all achievable strong converse exponents for a given rate pair
(R,r)
, limiting input distribution
P_{X}
and DMC
W
is denoted as
K^{*}(R,r,P_{X})
. Minimizing
K^{*}(R,r,P_{X})
over
P_{X}
yields the
minimium ensemble strong converse exponent

K^{*}(R,r)
.

We now present the strong converse exponent result.

Theorem 4
.

For any
P_{X}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
and
(R,r)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
,

\displaystyle K^{*}(R,r,P_{X})=K_{\mathrm{sp}}(\max\{R,H(P_{X})-r\},P_{X}).

(97)

Moreover,
K^{*}(R,r)=\min_{P_{X}}K^{*}(R,r,P_{X})
.

Similar to Theorem
3
, Theorem
4
demonstrates that two factors influence the ensemble strong converse exponent: the code rate
R
and the abandonment rate
r
. The interpretations for the cases
r\geq H(P_{X})-R
and
r<H(P_{X})-R
are analogous to those discussed following Theorem
3
. The primary difference is that these interpretations apply across all rates in the strong converse regime, as there is no critical rate in this scenario
[
9
]
.

An illustration of
K^{*}(R,r,P_{X})

If
r\geq H(P_{X})-R
, then
K^{*}(R,r,P_{X})
in (
97
) simplifies to
K_{\mathrm{sp}}(R,P_{X})
. While this resembles
K_{\mathrm{r}}(R,P_{X})
, the strong converse exponent for channel coding with a fixed input distribution
P_{X}

[
9
]
, it is important to note that they are not identical. As noted earlier, we generally have
K_{\mathrm{r}}(R,P_{X})\leq K_{\mathrm{sp}}(R,P_{X})
. Consequently, when
r\geq H(P_{X})-R
(implying the code rate is dominant), our ensemble strong converse exponent result is weaker than the classical strong converse exponent established by Dueck and Körner
[
9
]
. This is because, unlike standard information-theoretic proofs in the strong converse regime, our approach involves bounding the
ensemble
average correct decoding probability, which may result in some looseness. A similar situation occurs for rates below capacity, where Gallager showed that the exponent of the ensemble average error probability is the random coding error exponent
[

]
.

If

(implying that the abandonment rate is dominant), then

specializes to

, which coincides with the strong converse exponent for the conditional source coding setting with source
\bm{X}\sim\mathrm{Unif}\big(\mathcal{T}_{P_{X}^{(n)}}\big)
and side information
\bm{Y}\sim W^{n}(\cdot|\bm{X})
.
In this case,
K_{\mathrm{sp}}(H(P_{X})-r,P_{X})
is equal to
K_{\mathrm{r}}(H(P_{X})-r,P_{X})=\min_{V}\big\{D(V\|W|P_{X})+|H_{P_{X}\times V}(X|Y)-r|^{+}\big\}
. The equivalence between these two exponents was established by Oohama and Han
[
32
, Property 3]
.

For this part, we are required to

\displaystyle=\mathbbmss{P}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\mathsf{c}}\cap\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\mathsf{c}}\right]

It is easy to see that

\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon_{n}=\sum_{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\mathcal{S}_{r}}P_{\bm{X}}(\bm{x})W^{n}(\bm{y}|\bm{x})\overline{\varphi}(\bm{x},\bm{y}).

We now lower bound

VII
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper elucidates the interplay between the code and abandonment rates in terms of the second-order, error exponent and strong converse exponent asymptotics for guessing-based decoders with abandonment. The second-order results suggest that unless the two “backoff terms”
s
and
t
are equal, either (but not both) the effect of regular decoding or abandonment dominates the ensemble error probability if it is designed to be non-vanishing. The exponent results underscore the dichotomy between the code rate and abandonment rate.

For future work, one can consider extending the current results to other families of channels such as additive white Gaussian noise channels. Additionally, a more ambitious direction involves establishing a “partial information-theoretic converse” of the following form: consider all decoding strategies where all input sequences are ranked according to a metric
q:\mathcal{X}^{n}\times\mathcal{Y}^{n}\to\mathbb{N}
(using, for example, the metric
G(\bm{x}|\bm{y})
in this work), but the search is terminated after
m_{n}=\mathrm{e}^{nr_{n}}
guesses. An important question is to determine the fundamental limits of such schemes in various asymptotic regimes, especially when we are permitted to optimize over both the code design and the decoding metric
q
. This is in contrast with our specific setup of constant composition codes and empirical entropy-based ranking with abandonment.
