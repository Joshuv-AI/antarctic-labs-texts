On the maximal volume of empty convex bodies amidst multivariate dilates of a lacunary integer sequence
Eduard Stefanescu

Abstract
. Let ( a n ) n ∈ ℕ (a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}} be a lacunary sequence of integers satisfying the Hadamard gap condition. For any fixed dimension d ≥ 1 d\geq 1 , we establish asymptotic upper bounds for the maximal gap in the set of dilates { 𝜶 ​ a n } n ≤ N \{\bm{\alpha}a_{n}\}_{n\leq N} modulo 1 as N → ∞ N\to\infty , for Lebesgue–almost all dilation vectors 𝜶 ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] d \bm{\alpha}\in[0,1]^{d} . More precisely, we prove that for any lacunary ( a n ) n ∈ ℕ (a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}} and Lebesgue–almost all 𝜶 \bm{\alpha} , every convex set in [ 0 , 1 ] d [0,1]^{d} of volume at least ( log ⁡ N ) 2 / N (\log N)^{2}/N must contain an element of the set { 𝜶 ​ a n } n ≤ N \{\bm{\alpha}a_{n}\}_{n\leq N} mod 1, for all sufficiently large N N . We also establish a generalized version of this result, where the d d -dimensional Lebesgue measure is replaced by a general measure satisfying a certain Fourier decay condition. Our result is optimal up to logarithmic factors, and recovers as a special case a recent result for dimension d = 1 d=1 .

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we study the dispersion of multi-dimensional sequences which arise as dilates of a lacunary sequence of integers, taken modulo 1. The concept of “dispersion” generalizes the notion of the largest gap of a one-dimensional point set in
[0,1]
to the multi-dimensional case of a set in
[0,1]^{d}
, where instead the “largest empty range” (under certain restrictions on the shape of admissible ranges) is studied. Of course there is no unique way for such a generalization to the multi-dimensional case, and one classical version of dispersion studies the largest volume of an empty axis-parallel box amidst a point configuration in
[0,1]^{d}
. In the present paper, we will use a stronger notion of dispersion, which asks for the existence of a “large” (in terms of volume) convex set which does not contain an element of a point set. Clearly, for any set of
N
points in
[0,1]^{d}
, by the pigeon hole principle there always is an empty box of volume at least
1/(N+1)
, and it is known that for a random point set of
N
points in
[0,1]^{d}
the probability of finding an empty box of volume larger than
c_{d}\log N/N
is very small (for suitable
c_{d}
), see
[
17
]
. In this sense, allowing only empty sets of relatively small volume can be seen as a pseudo-randomness property of a point configuration, thus placing dispersion among other classical measures of pseudo-randomness of
[0,1]^{d}
-point sets such as discrepancy and diaphony; see
[
10
]
for a comprehensive exposition of these topics. It is clear that small dispersion can also be a very desirable property in the context of the construction of multi-dimensional point sets for sampling purposes, and accordingly questions on the existence and construction of low-dispersion point sets have received wide attention in the numerical analysis, complexity theory, and computational geometry communities; see for example the groundbreaking work of Rote and Tichy
[
21
]
, and the recent works
[
3
,
4
,
11
,
19
,
27
,
28
]
.

In this paper we will be concerned with dilated lacunary sequences
\{\bm{\alpha}a_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
modulo 1, where
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of integers satisfying the Hadamard gap condition
a_{n+1}\geq ra_{n}
for all
n\geq 1
and some growth factor
r>1
, and
\bm{\alpha}
is a dilation parameter in
[0,1]^{d}
which is understood to belong to a generic set of “typical” parameters (with respect to some measure). Such dilated lacunary sequences have a long and rich history in analysis, probability theory, and number theory, and are in particular well-known for often exhibiting behavior which is in accordance with the typical behavior of independent random sequences. For a comprehensive treatment of this subject, we refer to the recent survey article
[
2
]
. The behavior of dilated lacunary sequences (for almost all dilation parameters) has been studied in great detail with respect to the discrepancy (see in particular Philipp
[
20
]
, Fukuyama
[
13
]
, and Technau and Zafeiropoulos
[
26
]
) and with respect to pair correlation and gap distribution (see Rudnick and Zaharescu
[
22
]
, Chaubey and Yesha
[
7
]
, and Yesha
[
29
]
). Recently, Chow and Technau
[
8
]
investigated the size of the maximal gap for
\{\alpha a_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
modulo 1, and they proved that for Lebesgue-almost all
\alpha\in[0,1]
the maximal gap is of order at most
(\log N)^{3+\varepsilon}/N
for all sufficiently large
N
. A crucial aspect of their work was that their method also allowed them to prove an analogous result with respect to more general measures, subject to a certain Fourier decay condition on the measure. This was particularly interesting since the range of admissible measures included the so-called Kaufman measure, a certain measure which is supported on the set of badly approximable numbers, which allowed Chow and Technau to apply their metric dispersion result in the context of Littlewood’s problem, a central open problem in multiplicative Diophantine approximation. We refer to
[
8
]
for the details, and to
[
6
]
for the wider context. In
[
24
]
, the author of the present paper managed to improve Chow and Technau’s result by a logarithmic factor, and proved that for Lebesgue-almost all dilation factors
\alpha
, the maximal gap of
\{\alpha a_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
modulo 1 is of size at most
(\log N)^{2+\varepsilon}/N
for all sufficiently large
N
. Again, the method allowed a generalization to general measures, and an application in multiplicative Diophantine approximation.

The purpose of this paper is to establish a multidimensional analogue of the main result from
[
24
]
, while also strengthening the bound by eliminating the
\varepsilon
-dependence in the exponent. As we will see, the multidimensional results is as strong as the one-dimensional one, even if the class of test sets is much larger than in the one-dimensional case (now it is the class of convex sets in
[0,1]^{d}
, rather than the class of sub-intervals of
[0,1]
). Before coming to the precise statement of our results and to the proofs, we point out one further connection to a classical problem in geometry, which also has connections to Diophantine approximation. Danzer’s problem, which has been unsolved since 1965, asks whether there exist a set of finite density in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that the insection of this set with any convex set of volume at least 1 is non-empty. This problem, the state of research, and the connections with Diophantine approximation are explained in great detail in Adiceam’s paper
[
1
]
. See also Solomon and Weiss
[
23
]
.

2.
Main Result

In this section we give the precise statement of the main result of this paper. The notation is explained in the next section.

Definition 1

(Lacunary sequence)
.

A sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}
is called a
lacunary sequence
, if for a fixed
growth factor

r>1
:

a_{n+1}\geq ra_{n}

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}.

Let
\mathfrak{N}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be finite. We say a set
\{a_{n}\}_{n\in\mathfrak{N}}:=\{a_{n}:n\in\mathfrak{N}\}
is
lacunary of type
r
if
a_{m}/a_{n}\geq r
for any
n,m\in\mathfrak{N}
and
m>n
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a lacunary sequence with growth factor
r>1
. Let a normalized measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}([0,1]^{d})
be given and assume that its Fourier transform decays as
\left|(\mathcal{F}\mu)(\mathbf{x})\right|\ll(1+\|\mathbf{x}\|)^{-\Upsilon}
for some
\Upsilon>0
. Then, for
\mu
-almost all
\bm{\alpha}\in[0,1]^{d}
there exists an
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
N\geq N_{0}
any convex body
\mathcal{C}\subset[0,1]^{d}
with volume Vol
(\mathcal{C})\simeq(\log N)^{2}/N
contains at least one point of the set
\{\bm{\alpha}a_{n}\}_{n\leq N}
mod
\bm{1}
.

Remark
.

For the assumption
\left|(\mathcal{F}\mu)(\mathbf{x})\right|\ll(1+\|\mathbf{x}\|)^{-\Upsilon}
, it does not matter which norm
\|\cdot\|
we consider, since all norms are equivalent in finite dimensions.

Remark
.

As a special case for
d=1
, Theorem
1
implies
[
24
, Theorems 2.3 and 2.4]
with improved bound by a factor of
\log(N)^{\varepsilon}
.

1

We build upon the one-dimensional argument used by the author in

Proof.

In the following we will define a class of

is indeed always positive almost surely, meaning that the supporting hyperrectangle contains at least one element of the point set. During the proof it will be visible that the result is essentially independent of the location, form or orientation of the support of the functions, and instead depends only on its volumes and the overall cardinality of the point set.
To move from a statement about convex sets to a statement about rotated hyperrectangles, we will construct a discrete set

Thus
\mathfrak{Q}
is a set of rotated and translated hyperrectangles of fixed volume
M/N
.
Next, define

\mathfrak{M}:=\left\{\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\middle|\mathbf{V}^{-1}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{S},\ \mathbf{R}\in\mathfrak{R},\ \mathbf{S}\in\mathfrak{S}\right\}.

Let
f\in C_{[-1,1]^{d}}^{\infty}
be a real, non-negative and even function such that
f\leq 1
, and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\mathbf{x})\,d\lambda(\mathbf{x})=1
. This implies that
\mathcal{F}f
(the Fourier transform of
f
) is real and even,
\mathcal{F}f(0)=1
,
|\mathcal{F}f|\leq 1
and
\|f\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|f\|_{L^{1}}=1
. Such a function exists, see e.g.
[
9
, Lemma 1.26]
.
Furthermore let
\mathbf{V}\in\mathfrak{M}
and
\mathbf{t}\in\bm{\tau}
, hence
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{V}^{-1}\cdot[-1,1]^{d}+\mathbf{t}\in\mathfrak{Q}
, and
define the following function:

(4.7)

\omega_{N,\mathbf{Q}}(\bm{\alpha}):=\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{n=1}^{N/Q}f\left(\mathbf{V}(\tilde{a}_{n}\bm{\alpha}-\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{t}}-\mathbf{u})\right).

The sum over
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
accounts for the fractional parts in each component of the evaluated vector; the matrix
\mathbf{V}\in\mathfrak{M}
dilates the support of
\omega_{N,\mathbf{Q}}
, and the vector
\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined such that it translates the support of
\omega_{N,\mathbf{Q}}
to be centered in
\mathbf{t}
. This means we define
\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{t}}
such that
\mathbf{V}\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{t}}=\mathbf{t}
, and hence
f(\mathbf{V}(\tilde{a}_{n}\bm{\alpha}-\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{t}}-\mathbf{u})=f(\mathbf{V}(\tilde{a}_{n}\bm{\alpha}-\mathbf{u})-\mathbf{t})
.

Since the support of
\omega_{N,\mathbf{Q}}
is contained in a
d
-dimensional hypercube, we can imagine
\mathbf{S}
as an operator dilating the edges of the hypercube resulting in a hyperrectangle. By Lemma
2
, we can represent the matrix
\mathbf{R}
as a succession of rotations of the resulting hyperrectangle by the angles
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{\tilde{d}}
sequentially within a plane spanned by two unit vectors, where
\tilde{d}:=d(d-1)/2
.

Using Poisson summation formula (
3.1
), and noting that
\det(\mathbf{V}^{-1})=2^{-d}M/N
we get:

\omega_{N,\mathbf{Q}}(\bm{\alpha})=2^{-d}\frac{M}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{n=1}^{N/Q}(\mathcal{F}f)\left(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{k}\right)e(\mathbf{k}\cdot(\tilde{a}_{n}\bm{\alpha}-\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{t}})).

We now define:

(4.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{\omega}_{N,\mathbf{Q}}(\bm{\alpha})&:=2^{-d}\frac{M}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{n=1}^{N/Q}(\mathcal{F}f)\left(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{k}\right)e(\mathbf{k}\cdot(\tilde{a}_{n}\bm{\alpha}-\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{t}}))-2^{-d}Q\\
\ &=2^{-d}\frac{M}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}}\sum_{n=1}^{N/Q}(\mathcal{F}f)\left(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{k}\right)e(\mathbf{k}\cdot(\tilde{a}_{n}\bm{\alpha}-\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{t}})).\end{split}

Here we used that
(\mathcal{F}f)(0)=1
and
M/Q=Q
.

Since after rotating the support of
f
by
\mathbf{R}
the total number of hyperrectangles needed to cover
[0,1]^{d}
via translations does not change, up to a finite factor dependent on the dimension
d
, we can assume without loss of generality
\mathbf{R}=\mathbb{I}
, thus

\mathbf{V}^{-1}=\mathbf{S}=\begin{pmatrix}s_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&s_{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&s_{d}\end{pmatrix}.

We truncate the first sum in equation (
4.8
), by summing
\mathbf{k}
over

\mathbf{\Sigma}:=\left(\bigtimes_{i=1}^{d}[-N^{\frac{1}{d}}/s_{i}-1,N^{\frac{1}{d}}/s_{i}+1]\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}\right)\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\},

where
|\mathbf{\Sigma}|\leq\prod_{i=1}^{d}(2N^{\frac{1}{d}}/s_{i}+3)\leq(2^{2d+1})N^{2}/M
for
N
large enough. We work with

\omega^{*}_{N,\mathbf{Q}}(\bm{\alpha}):=2^{-d}\frac{M}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{\Sigma}}\sum_{n=1}^{N/Q}(\mathcal{F}f)\left(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{k}\right)e(\mathbf{k}\cdot(\tilde{a}_{n}\bm{\alpha}-\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{t}})).

Let us define
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\prime}:=\left(\bigtimes_{i=1}^{d}[-N^{\frac{1}{d}}/s_{i},N^{\frac{1}{d}}/s_{i}]\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}\right)\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
. Using the rapid decay of
\mathcal{F}f
(which follows from
\mathcal{F}
being an automorphism and from the definition of Schwartz functions), we obtain, for any
\Xi>0
,

(4.9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left|2^{-d}\frac{M}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\notin\mathbf{\Sigma}}\sum_{n=1}^{N/Q}(\mathcal{F}f)\left(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{k}\right)e(\mathbf{k}\cdot(\tilde{a}_{n}\bm{\alpha}-\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{t}}))\right|\ll\left|Q\sum_{\mathbf{k}\notin\mathbf{\Sigma}}\frac{1}{\left(1+|\mathbf{V}^{-1}k|\right)^{\Xi}}\right|\\
\ll&Q\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{\left(1+|\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{x}|\right)^{\Xi}}d\lambda(\mathbf{x})=\frac{N}{Q}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\left[-N^{\frac{1}{\delta}},N^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\right]^{d}}\frac{1}{\left(1+|\mathbf{y}|\right)^{\Xi}}d\lambda(\mathbf{y})\leq O\left(N^{-1000d}\right),\end{split}

for
N
and
\Xi
sufficiently large.

Consequently, we can write:

\tilde{\omega}_{N,\mathbf{Q}}(\bm{\alpha})=\omega^{*}_{N,\mathbf{Q}}(\bm{\alpha})+O\left(N^{-1000d}\right).

Remark
.

In the paper
[
24
]
by the author, there was a minor inconsistency regarding the above estimate, which is correctly stated in the present paper. Specifically, we need to sum over essentially
N^{2}
rather than
N\log(N)^{\varepsilon}
. Theorem
1
is stronger and provides correctness of Theorems 2.4-2.6 in
[
24
]
.

Define

(4.10)

p_{\mathbf{k}}:=(\mathcal{F}f)\left(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{k}\right)e(-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{t}}),

where in particular
|p_{\mathbf{k}}|\leq 1
.

Step 2: Moment estimates (Lebesgue measure):

To make the proof more accessible, we will first prove the result in the case where
\mu=\lambda
is the Lebesgue measure. Subsequently, we will extend the argument to the more general case in the next step.
We estimate the following integral, which will be useful later, when using the first Borel–Cantelli Lemma.

(4.11)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left\|e^{-\frac{1}{10}\omega^{*}_{N,\mathbf{Q}}}\right\|_{L^{1}}=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{10}\omega^{*}_{N,\mathbf{Q}}(\bm{\alpha})\right)d\lambda(\bm{\alpha})\\
=&\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\prod_{n=1}^{\frac{N}{Q}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{10\cdot 2^{d}}\frac{M}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{\Sigma}}p_{\mathbf{k}}e(\mathbf{k}\cdot\tilde{a}_{n}\bm{\alpha})\right)d\lambda(\bm{\alpha})\\
\leq&\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\prod_{n=1}^{\frac{N}{Q}}\Biggl(1-\frac{1}{10\cdot 2^{d}}\frac{M}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{\Sigma}}\Bigl(p_{\mathbf{k}}e(\mathbf{k}\cdot\tilde{a}_{n}\bm{\alpha})\Bigr)\\
&+\biggl(\frac{1}{10\cdot 2^{d}}\frac{M}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{\Sigma}}\bigl(p_{\mathbf{k}}e(\mathbf{k}\cdot\tilde{a}_{n}\bm{\alpha})\bigr)\biggr)^{2}\Biggr)d\lambda(\bm{\alpha}).\end{split}

Here, we can remove the absolute values in the first line, since
\omega^{*}_{N,\mathbf{Q}}
is real, making
\exp\left(\frac{1}{10}\omega^{*}_{N,\mathbf{Q}}\right)
positive. The transition from the second to the third line involves applying the Taylor series expansion for exponential functions. Specifically, for
y\in\mathbb{R}
, and
|y|\leq 1
, we have
e^{y}\leq 1+y+y^{2}
.

We define the function
h:=f(\mathbf{V}(\cdot/2-\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{t}}))
. Then three substitutions and the fact that
f
is supported in
[-1,1]^{d}
yield

\hat{h}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{M}{2^{d}N}\mathcal{F}f\left(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{k}\right)e(-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{t}})=\frac{M}{2^{d}N}p_{\mathbf{k}}.

Using
that
\mathcal{F}f
is strictly radially decreasing, we split the sum over
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
into disjoint subsets corresponding to lattice points inside disjoint translates of the hyperrectangle
\mathbf{V}\cdot[-1,1)^{d}
.

Let
\Xi>0
, and
\mathbf{m}\in 2\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be arbitrary. Then
for
\mathbf{k}\in V([-1,1]^{d}+\mathbf{m})
, we have

\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{k}\in[-1,1]^{d}+\mathbf{m}
and we see

|\mathcal{F}f(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{k})|=O\big(1/(1+|\mathbf{m}|)^{\Xi}\big),

which yields

(4.12)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|\mathcal{F}f(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{k})|=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in 2\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{V}([-1,1)^{d}+\mathbf{m})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|\mathcal{F}f(\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{k})|\\
\leq&\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in 2\mathbb{Z}^{d}}O\big(1/(1+|\mathbf{m}|)^{\Xi}\big)\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{V}([-1,1)^{d}+\mathbf{m})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}}1\simeq\frac{N}{M}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in 2\mathbb{Z}^{d}}O\big(1/(1+|\mathbf{m}|)^{\Xi}\big)\end{split}

This, together with an almost identical argument as (
4.9
) yields

\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{\Sigma}}|\hat{h}(\mathbf{k})|\ll\frac{M}{2^{d}N}\frac{N}{M}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in 2\mathbb{Z}^{d}}O\big(1/(1+|\mathbf{m}|)^{\Xi}\big)+O\left(N^{-1000d}\right)<\infty.

Hence
\hat{h}
is absolutely summable independent of
N
and
\mathbf{t}
, due to the rapid decay of
\mathcal{F}f
. This implies, that

(4.13)

\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\frac{M}{2^{d}N}p_{\mathbf{k}}e(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x})=h(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{V}(\frac{\mathbf{x}}{2}-\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{t}}))\leq 1,

for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
.
Utilizing the above together with a truncation argument as in (
4.9
) we get

(4.14)

\left|\frac{1}{10\cdot 2^{d}}\frac{M}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{\Sigma}}p_{\mathbf{k}}e(\mathbf{k}\cdot\bm{\alpha}\tilde{a}_{n})\right|\leq\left|\frac{1}{10}h(2\bm{\alpha}\tilde{a}_{n})+O\left(N^{-1000d}\right)\right|<1,

Furthermore, we observe that
\omega^{*}_{N,\mathbf{Q}}
is real. This explains the estimate in (
4.11
).

Next, we rewrite:

e(\mathbf{k}\cdot\tilde{a}_{n}\bm{\alpha})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}e(\tilde{a}_{n}k_{i}\alpha_{i}),

By Fubini’s theorem it is sufficient to regard every factor individually. Hence, we can treat this problem similarly to the one-dimensional case.
Due to the linear independence of
\{\tilde{a}_{n}\}_{n\leq K}
within the interval
[-N,N]
, see estimate (
4.3
) and the
L^{2}[0,1]
-orthonormality property, the integrals involving products of phases
e(\tilde{a}_{n}k_{j}\alpha_{j}),\ j=1,\dots,d
with other phases of significantly higher frequencies vanish, which yields the second inequality below. The inequality in the fourth row is a direct consequence of Bernoulli’s and the triangle inequality. In the penultimate step, we used Parseval’s identity. The final step is doing a substitution. Continuing from equation (
4.11
), we have

(4.15)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\|e^{-\frac{1}{10}\omega^{*}_{N,\mathbf{Q}}}\right\|_{L^{1}}\ll&\prod_{n=1}^{\frac{N}{Q}}\Biggl(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}1-\frac{1}{10\cdot 2^{d}}\frac{M}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{\Sigma}}\Bigl(p_{\mathbf{k}}e(\mathbf{k}\cdot\tilde{a}_{n}\bm{\alpha})\Bigr)\\
&+\biggl(\frac{1}{10\cdot 2^{d}}\frac{M}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{\Sigma}}(p_{\mathbf{k}}e(\mathbf{k}\cdot\tilde{a}_{n}\bm{\alpha})\biggr)^{2}d\lambda(\bm{\alpha})\Biggr)\\
\leq&\prod_{n=1}^{\frac{N}{Q}}\Biggl(\biggl(1+\frac{1}{100\cdot 2^{2d}}\biggl(\frac{M}{N}\biggr)^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{\Sigma}}p_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\Biggr)\\
\leq&\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\frac{N}{Q}}\frac{1}{100}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{\Sigma}}\left|{\left.\kern-1.2pt\hat{h}(\mathbf{k})\mathchoice{\vphantom{\big|}}{}{}{}\right|_{\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{t}}=0}}\right|^{2}\right)\\
\leq&\exp\left(\frac{N}{Q}\frac{1}{100\cdot 2^{d}}\left\|f\left(\mathbf{V}\frac{(\cdot)}{2}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+O\left(N^{-1000d}\right)\right)\\
=&\exp\left(\frac{N}{Q}\frac{1}{100\cdot 2^{d}}\frac{M}{N}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}+O\left(N^{-1000d}\right)\right)\leq\exp\left(\frac{Q}{50\cdot 2^{d}}\right).\end{split}

Step 3: Moment estimates (general measures):

After defining
M
and
Q
as in (
4.4
), and
p_{\mathbf{k}}
as in (
4.10
), and changing the index of the dilated lacunary set of type
r
to
\{\bm{\alpha}\tilde{a}_{n}\}_{K<n\leq 2K}
, we look at the
d-
dimensional Fourier series of

g(\bm{\alpha}):=\prod_{n=K+1}^{K+\frac{N}{Q}}\Biggl(1-\frac{1}{10\cdot 2^{d}}\frac{M}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{\Sigma}}\Bigl(p_{\mathbf{k}}e(\mathbf{k}\cdot\tilde{a}_{n}\bm{\alpha})\Bigr)+\biggl(\frac{1}{10\cdot 2^{d}}\frac{M}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{\Sigma}}\bigl(p_{\mathbf{k}}e(\mathbf{k}\cdot\tilde{a}_{n}\bm{\alpha})\bigr)\biggr)^{2}\Biggr).

g(\bm{\alpha})=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}q_{\mathbf{m}}e(\mathbf{m}\cdot\bm{\alpha}),

where

q_{\mathbf{m}}=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}g(\bm{\alpha})e(-\mathbf{m}\cdot\bm{\alpha})d\lambda(\bm{\alpha}).

We apply the estimate (
4.14
) and rewrite the first moment:

(4.16)

\left\|e^{-\frac{1}{10}\omega^{*}_{N,\mathbf{Q}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mu)}\leq\left\|g\right\|_{L^{1}(\mu)}=\left|\int_{[0,1]^{d}}g(\bm{\alpha})d\mu(\bm{\alpha})\right|\ll\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left|q_{\mathbf{m}}\right|\left(1+\|\mathbf{m}\|_{\ell^{\infty}}\right)^{-\Upsilon},

and split the sum into two parts, according to whether
\|\mathbf{m}\|_{\ell^{\infty}}\leq a_{N}
or
\|\mathbf{m}\|_{\ell^{\infty}}>a_{N}
.

The first sum can be treated identically to the calculations in (
4.11
)–(
4.15
), as the additional arising factors have frequencies too low compared to the existing ones in order to affect the calculation. This is again supported by
L^{2}
-orthogonality and yields the following bound:

(4.17)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sum_{\|\mathbf{m}\|_{\ell^{\infty}}\leq a_{N}}\left|q_{\mathbf{m}}\right|\left(1+\|\mathbf{m}\|_{\ell^{\infty}}\right)^{-\Upsilon}\\
\leq&\sum_{\|\mathbf{m}\|_{\ell^{\infty}}\leq a_{N}}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\prod_{n=K+1}^{K+\frac{N}{Q}}\Biggl(1-\frac{1}{10\cdot 2^{d}}\frac{M}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{\Sigma}}\Bigl(p_{\mathbf{k}}e(\mathbf{k}\cdot\tilde{a}_{n}\bm{\alpha})\Bigr)\\
&+\biggl(\frac{1}{10\cdot 2^{d}}\frac{M}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{\Sigma}}\bigl(p_{\mathbf{k}}e(\mathbf{k}\cdot\tilde{a}_{n}\bm{\alpha})\bigr)\biggr)^{2}\Biggr)e(-\mathbf{m}\cdot\bm{\alpha})d\lambda(\bm{\alpha})\left(1+\|\mathbf{m}\|_{\ell^{\infty}}\right)^{-\Upsilon}\\
\leq&\exp\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\frac{N}{Q}}\frac{1}{100\cdot 2^{d}}\frac{M}{N}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}+O\left(N^{-1000d}\right)\right)\leq\exp\left(\frac{Q}{50\cdot 2^{d}}\right).\end{split}

In the other sum, we make use of the rapid decay of the Fourier transform of the measure. We estimate as before:

(4.18)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sum_{\|\mathbf{m}\|_{\ell^{\infty}}>a_{N}}\left|q_{\mathbf{m}}\right|\left(1+\|\mathbf{m}\|_{\ell^{\infty}}\right)^{-\Upsilon}\\
\leq&\sum_{\|\mathbf{m}\|_{\ell^{\infty}}>a_{N}}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\prod_{n=K+1}^{K+\frac{N}{Q}}\Biggl(1-\frac{1}{10\cdot 2^{2d}}\frac{M}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{\Sigma}}\Bigl(p_{\mathbf{k}}e(\mathbf{k}\cdot\tilde{a}_{n}\bm{\alpha})\Bigr)\\
&+\biggl(\frac{1}{10\cdot 2^{2d}}\frac{M}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{\Sigma}}\bigl(p_{\mathbf{k}}e(\mathbf{k}\cdot\tilde{a}_{n}\bm{\alpha})\bigr)\biggr)^{2}\Biggr)e(\mathbf{m}\cdot\bm{\alpha})d\lambda(\bm{\alpha})a_{N}^{-\Upsilon}.\end{split}

Multiplying out the above product we get at most
3^{N/Q}
terms, all of which have factors either
1
,

\frac{1}{10R}\frac{M}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{\Sigma}}p_{\mathbf{k}}e(\mathbf{k}\cdot\tilde{a}_{n}\bm{\alpha}),

for
n\in\{K+1,...,2N/Q\}
. Upon furhter multiplication, considering the sums in
\mathbf{k}
, we get not more than
3^{N/Q}(2^{2d+1}N^{2}/M)^{2N/Q}=:(C(d)N^{2}/M)^{2N/Q}
terms. Consequently, there are at most that many terms which are not orthogonal to some
m>a_{N}
.
This implies

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sum_{\|\mathbf{m}\|_{\ell^{\infty}}>a_{N}}\left|q_{\mathbf{m}}\right|\left(1+\|\mathbf{m}\|_{\ell^{\infty}}\right)^{-\Upsilon}\leq\left(\frac{C(d)N^{2}}{M}\right)^{\frac{2N}{Q}}a_{N}^{-\Upsilon}\\
=&\exp\left(\left((\log C(d))+2(\log N)-(\log M)\right)\frac{2N}{Q}-\Upsilon N\right)<1\leq\exp\left(\frac{Q}{50\cdot 2^{d}}\right),\end{split}

for
N
large enough.

Step 4: Almost sure existence:

We need to cover
[0,1]^{d}
by hyperrectangles
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathfrak{Q}
which contain the support of
\omega_{N,\mathbf{Q}}
for providing uniformity in
N
, i.e. there exists an
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
N\geq N_{0}
every convex body
\mathcal{C}
satisfies the statement of Theorem
1
. Details will be explained in the next two steps.

The calculation in step 2 and 3 have been carried out in order to allow an application of Markov’s inequality and utilize the first Borel-Cantelli-Lemma. We have

(4.19)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mu\left(\left\{\bm{\alpha}\in[0,1]^{d}\,:\,\frac{Q}{2^{d}}-\omega_{N,\mathbf{Q}}(\bm{\alpha})\geq\frac{Q}{2^{d+1}}\right\}\right)\\
\ \leq&\mu\left(\left\{\bm{\alpha}\in[0,1]^{d}\,:\,-\omega^{*}_{N,\mathbf{Q}}(\bm{\alpha})\geq\frac{Q}{2^{d+2}}\right\}\right)\\
\ =&\mu\left(\left\{\bm{\alpha}\in[0,1]^{d}\,:\,\exp\left(-\frac{1}{10}\omega^{*}_{N,\mathbf{Q}}(\bm{\alpha})\right)\geq\exp\left(\frac{Q}{40\cdot 2^{d}}\right)\right\}\right)\\
\ \leq&\frac{\|e^{-\frac{1}{10}\omega^{*}_{N,\mathbf{Q}}}\|_{L^{1}(\mu)}}{e^{\frac{Q}{40\cdot 2^{d}}}}\leq\frac{e^{\frac{Q}{50\cdot 2^{d}}}}{e^{\frac{Q}{40\cdot 2^{d}}}}\leq e^{-\frac{Q}{200\cdot 2^{d}}}=N^{-5d^{2}\max\{\Upsilon^{-1},1\}}\leq N^{-5d^{2}}.\end{split}

Following this, we will need to consider at most
N^{3d^{2}}
different
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathfrak{Q}
regarding
\omega_{N,\mathbf{Q}}
, namely those
\mathbf{Q}\in\bar{\mathfrak{Q}}
for
\bar{\mathfrak{Q}}
defined below.

Since

(4.20)

\sum_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{\mathbf{Q}\in\overline{\mathfrak{Q}}}\mu(\{\bm{\alpha}\in[0,1]^{d}\,:\,\frac{Q}{2^{d}}-\omega_{N,\mathbf{Q}}(\bm{\alpha})\geq\frac{Q}{2^{d+1}}\})\leq\sum_{N\in\mathbb{N}}N^{3d^{2}}N^{-5d^{2}}<\infty,

the first Borel Cantelli Lemma (
3.3
) yields that for all sufficiently large
N
and for almost all
\bm{\alpha}\in[0,1]^{d}
, we get

\omega_{N,\mathbf{Q}}(\bm{\alpha})>0,

uniformly for all
\mathbf{Q}\in\bar{\mathfrak{Q}}
.
In other words, for every subset
\mathbf{Q}\in\bar{\mathfrak{Q}}
there exists an
N_{0}
such that for for all
N\geq N_{0}
the hyperrectangle
\mathbf{Q}
contains at least one point of
\{\tilde{a}_{n}\}_{n\leq N}
.

Step 5: Discretization:

We will now construct a subset
\overline{\mathfrak{Q}}\subseteq\mathfrak{Q}
of cardinality at most
N^{3d^{2}}
, ensuring that every convex body
\mathcal{C}
in
[0,1]^{d}
with volume
2^{d(\tilde{d}+2)}d^{d}M/N
contains at least one element of
\overline{\mathfrak{Q}}
.

We define

\overline{\mathfrak{Q}}=\left\{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathfrak{Q}|\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}\cdot[-1,1]^{d}+\mathbf{t};\mathbf{R}\in\overline{\mathfrak{R}},\mathbf{S}\in\overline{\mathfrak{S}},\mathbf{t}\in\overline{\bm{\tau}}\right\},

where, for
1\leq\delta<2
, that is allowed to depend on the matrix
\mathbf{S}
below, we define

(4.21)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\overline{\mathfrak{S}}&:=\left\{\mathbf{S}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{k_{1}}{N}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\frac{k_{2}}{N}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\frac{k_{d}\delta}{N}\end{pmatrix}\middle|k_{i}\in[1,N]\cap\mathbb{Z},\left\lceil\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{k_{i}}{N}\right\rceil=\frac{M}{2^{d}N},\delta\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{k_{i}}{N}=\frac{M}{2^{d}N}\right\},\\
\overline{\bm{\tau}}&:=\left\{\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\middle|\mathbf{t}=\left(\frac{k_{1}}{N},\dots,\frac{k_{d}}{N}\right)^{T},k_{i}\in[1,N]\cap\mathbb{Z}\right\},\\
\overline{\mathfrak{R}}&:=\left\{\mathbf{R}\in\mathfrak{R}\middle|\mathbf{R}=\prod_{l=1}^{\tilde{d}}\mathbf{R}_{l}\left(\frac{2\pi k_{l}}{N}\right),k_{l}\in[0,N-1]\cap\mathbb{Z}\right\}.\\
\end{split}

Then
\overline{\mathfrak{Q}}
is for sufficiently large
N
of cardinality at most
N^{d}\cdot N^{d}\cdot N^{d(d-1)/2}\leq N^{3d^{2}}
.

To achieve this, we
will prove the following three statements:

Step 5.1:

Every hyperrectangle with origin-center of volume
2^{d}M/N
that is not rotated nor translated (hence only a scaled hypercube) contains a hyperrectangle of volume
M/N
constructed by scaling
[-1,1]^{d}
by an element
\mathbf{S}\in\overline{\mathfrak{S}}
;

Step 5.2:

Every rotated hyperrectangle with origin-center of volume
2^{d(\tilde{d}+1)}M/N
contains a similar (proportional) hyperrectangle of volume
2^{d}M/N
, which is rotated by a rotation matrix
\mathbf{R}\in\overline{\mathfrak{R}}
where we recall
\tilde{d}=d(d-1)/2
;

Step 5.3:

Every translated hyperrectangle of volume
2^{d(\tilde{d}+2)}M/N
that is not rotated contains a homothetic hyperrectangle of volume
2^{d(\tilde{d}+1)}M/N
translated by an element
\mathbf{t}\in\overline{\bm{\tau}}
.

Combining Theorem
A
with the above construction ensures that every convex body
\mathcal{C}
of volume
