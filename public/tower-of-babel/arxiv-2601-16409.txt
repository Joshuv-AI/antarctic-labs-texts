Gen-DBA: Generative Database Agents (Towards a Move 37 for Databases) Conference: Preprint; 2026 ;
Yeasir Rayhan, Walid G. Aref

Abstract
. Move 37 marks one of the major breakthroughs in AI in terms of its ability to surpass human expertise and discover novel strategies beyond the traditional game play in the strategic two-player board game of Go. The domains of Natural Language Processing, Computer Vision, and Robotics have also undergone a similar phenomenon through the advent of large foundational models in the form of Large Language Models (LLMs), Vision Language Models (VLMs) and Vision Language Action models (VLAs), respectively. In this paper, we investigate the current state of Artificial Intelligence for Database Systems research (AI4DB), and assess how far AI4DB systems are from achieving their own Move 37 moment. We envision a Generative Database Agent (Gen-DBA, for short) as the pathway to achieving Move 37 for database systems that will bring generative reasoning and creativity into the realm of database learning tasks. This vision paper explores this direction by presenting the recipe for building Gen-DBA that encompasses but is not limited to a Transformer backbone, a hardware-grounded tokenization mechanism, a two-stage Goal-Directed Next Token Prediction training paradigm, and a generative inference process.

In this paper, we envision having a
Generative Database Agent (Gen-DBA, for short)

The Transformer architecture is the backbone for Gen-DBA, enabling the agent to leverage its high degree of parallelism and the capability to scale to millions of parameters. Inspired by the two-phase training process of LLMs, Gen-DBA undergoes a pre-training stage followed by a post-training stage. During pre-training, Gen-DBA adopts a holistic view of database optimization. Rather than training separate models for different database learning tasks and execution environments, Gen-DBA trains a single model end to end on a single dataset, referred to as the experience dataset (cf. §
2.2

DB-Tokens

(
53

This generalist-over-specialist training paradigm enables the agent to learn the underlying dynamics of the database optimization design space across heterogeneous environments and database learning tasks. This approach not only promotes generalization, but also reduces the startup cost of database learning tasks, as there is now a single entry point for training. Once the agent is trained on the experience dataset, any database learning task and execution environments can be efficiently bootstrapped from it.

The pre-trained Gen-DBA model is further fine-tuned on a high-quality and task-specific dataset during the
post-training stage
.
This phase follows a specialist training paradigm, where the goal is to adapt Gen-DBA to the needs of a specific deployment. For example, a database may only require support for query optimization, it might target a particular configuration, e.g., PostgreSQL running on an Intel hardware running JOB
(
35

(

One key aspect that distinguishes Gen-DBA from a typical AI4DB system is that they are generative in nature. Gen-DBA does not just predict a numerical value or pick a choice from a given set of options. Rather, they generate structured policies token by token for database learning tasks leaving room for creative strategies to emerge from the large search space of possible actions.

Despite recent efforts, current AI4DB systems fall short of this vision, naturally raising the question: What keeps AI4DB systems from achieving the equivalent of Move 37 for database systems? AI4DB systems employ models that are relatively small in scale, typically comprising only thousands of parameters. Historically, this choice has been guided by multiple factors.
The scope of database learning tasks has remained narrow, mostly due to how we formulate the learning tasks. This has resulted in distinct models trained on different datasets, customized for specific databases, workloads, and hardware configurations. This choice in turn also increases the startup cost of learning tasks significantly high, i.e., it requires significant labor to collect data and train models
targeting a particular scenario, e.g., any of the scenarios stated above.
This restricts the AI4DB system’s ability to benefit from shared knowledge across tasks, workloads, databases, and hardware configurations, despite being from the same database domain.

Moreover, the architectural choices made to fit these narrow domains result in high training overhead and inference cost, particularly in latency-sensitive systems. Fundamentally, the underlying paradigm to train these AI4DB systems is restrictive. They are non-generative in nature, i.e., most database learning tasks are formulated as classification or regression problems. For example, in cardinality estimation

An AI4DB model trained to pick the best hint from a given set of options can only act upon the search space of the provided hints. Likewise, an AI4DB model trained to mimic the true cardinality can only go as far as predicting the true cardinality without explicitly optimizing for the query latency, which is the ultimate goal.

This is not coincidental, given the relatively late adoption of AI in database systems. Gen-DBA takes the current state of the database as input (cf. §

The nature of this policy depends on the specific task. For example, in scheduling, Gen-DBA would produce a scheduling policy that determines which queries are assigned to which processor cores.
For query optimization, Gen-DBA would generate a physical query plan for a given SQL query. For database tuning, Gen-DBA would propose a configuration of system knobs and parameters.

2.1.
Perceive

A Generative Database Agent continuously perceives its environment and captures signals from its execution environment, e.g., workloads, hardware telemetry, query execution states, and system configurations. One of the key challenges of this step is to decide on the signals that are generalizable across databases, workloads, hardware and learning tasks. This is unlike the domain of NLP, where the signals translate to language tokens, i.e., a word, a sub-word, or a character. The signals in NLP are homogeneous in the sense that the fundamental unit of information that the LLMs process to train and infer remain in the realm of language tokens. In contrast, in database learning tasks, the signals may span different
abstractions
including but not limited to language (e.g., SQL queries), the hardware telemetry (e.g., the number of cache misses, the number of memory misses, the number of retired instructions), the software telemetry (e.g., the database knobs), the distributional statistics (the histograms over relational tables, the workload composition), and the performance metrics (e.g., query throughput, query latency, level of concurrency).

From the perspective of database systems infrastructure, the perception stage comprises the following three steps, i.e., sensing, integrating, and signaling.
During sensing, the DBMS must make two key decisions: 1) The field of view, i.e., the granularity at which the DBMS needs to be observed, and 2) the dimension, i.e., the signals that need to be monitored. For example, during query optimization, a DBMS is to be monitored at query-level granularity. Since QO is performed at compile-time, the SQL query and the distributional statistics can be sufficient for this particular task. On the other hand, for run-time database learning tasks, e.g., scheduling and storage optimization, the DBMS is to be monitored at workload level. Aside from the distributional statistics, the run-time observations, e.g., the software and hardware telemetry are imperative for these learning tasks.

During integration, the DBMS aggregates and aligns the collected signals across different granularities (query vs. workload) and modalities (SQL query vs. hardware telemetry).
This involves synchronizing signals from heterogeneous sources, e.g., hardware performance counters (hardware telemetry), operating system (software telemetry), database configurations (database knobs), database execution traces (SQL queries and workload), and transforming them into a consistent
generalizable
representation. Finally, during signaling, these representations are offloaded to the experience dataset.

2.2.
Experience Dataset

The experience dataset consists of the perceptions offloaded during the signaling stage of the perception pipeline. It serves as the
knowledge base
of Gen-DBA, capturing the DBMS state, workload characteristics, hardware and software telemetry, distributional statistics, and the corresponding performance outcomes. The experience dataset is analogous to the internet-scale text corpora used to train large language models (LLMs). Hence, the perceptions collected during the perception stage must exhibit high diversity to enable generalization. This diversity should span multiple dimensions, including database schemas (e.g., JOB
(
35
)
, StackOverflow, TPC-H, TPC-DS), query workloads (e.g., read–write, insertion, update, range scan), hardware configurations (e.g., Intel, AMD, NVIDIA, cloud servers), and DBMS implementations (e.g., PostgreSQL, MySQL, SQL Server, and the academic prototypes).
The primary objective of the experience dataset is to provide a broad and diverse collection of training samples to Gen-DBA, thus enabling the agent to learn the underlying optimization space in which the database learning tasks operate. Moreover, the training samples in the experience dataset should contain both good and bad examples, e.g., query evaluation plans, enabling the Gen-DBA to distinguish between alternatives and learn to favor better decisions.

2.3.
Tokenization

As discussed in the perception stage (Section
2.1
)
the
token space
on which the database learning tasks operate is multi-modal in nature, i.e., the observed signals can span multiple modalities ranging from SQL language and hardware telemetry to tree-structured query plans and hardware performance statistics. The tokenization stage involves converting the raw perceptions collected in the experience dataset to actionable tokens that can drive the policy generation of Gen-DBA. Depending on the modality, Gen-DBA can employ different encoders, e.g., a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)
(
34
)
to process the hardware telemetry of the hardware die, Tree Convolutional Network (TCNN)
(
44
)
to process query plan trees, Feed-forward Neural Networks (FNN) to process software telemetry.
One key challenge in this stage is:
How can Gen-DBA efficiently process these diverse information-rich tokens collected from different modalities and transform them into a coherent representation with the sole purpose of optimizing database engines?
We take inspiration from Vision Language Models (VLMs)
(
8
;
50
;
3
)
,
where both the image and the language tokens are projected into a shared embedding space. Similarly, Gen-DBA unifies the tokens from different modalities into a shared embedding space that serves as the foundation for subsequent policy generation. Depending on the characterization of Gen-DBA, the structure and semantics of the shared representation space can vary significantly as will be discussed in Section
3
.

While tokenizing the experience dataset, which is inherently a cross-embodied and multi-modal dataset, i.e., dataset comprising data samples from different database learning tasks, database systems, hardware configurations, and database knobs, a key question arises:
What is the fundamental unit of knowledge, i.e., token that unifies these diverse signals, allowing Gen-DBA to reason about the optimization landscape?
For example, consider query optimization. The query plans and the cost functions of two different databases exist within their own internal semantic representations, making a direct comparison between the query plans and the cost functions
undesirable or even misleading. Simply projecting the query plans and the associated costs as features
into a shared embedded space does not suffice.
Rather, a unified token is needed to link these heterogeneous components and enable meaningful cross-database, cross-task, cross-hardware, and cross-workload reasoning. In both generations of Gen-DBA, we envision the hardware counters from the Performance Monitoring Unit (PMU, for short), termed DB-Token
(
53
)
, as the glue that can make this possible.

DB-Tokens correspond to per-core hardware profiles from the following four key hardware components: The front-end, the cache sub-system, the memory sub-system, and the network interconnects. These DB-tokens present a bird’s eye view of the entire database systems conditioned on all the different policies governing its own individual components, e.g., the query optimizer, the cardinality estimator, and the cost model. DB-Tokens are computationally inexpensive to retrieve from the hardware PMU registers and can be incorporated into any database system with minimal effort
(
26
;
30
)
. Aside from grounding all the different modalities under the same shared embedded space, the DB-Tokens serve as a low-level performance metric, as they provide fine-grained signals of resource utilization and the underlying data distributions
(
69
;
55
)
. Note that the performance statistics from different hardware PMUs can still vary across hardware platforms. Thus, a mapping is still required to align platform-specific hardware events. However, this alignment is far more feasible given the relatively small number of CPU hardware vendors and the PMU statistics over the vast number of database systems and software-level knobs. Moreover, hardware evolves at a much slower pace than software, making such alignments stable and durable across hardware generations.

2.4.
Gen-DBA Model Architecture

2.4.1.
Backbone

The model architecture of a Generative Database Agent primarily consists of a Language Transformer backbone. Depending on the choice of backbone (e.g., GPT-1 vs. GPT-5), the model size can significantly vary from millions to billions of parameters. The main intent
is to use larger Transformer-backed models drawing inspiration from the recent paradigm shift in AI.
One fundamental lesson learned from the recent AI boom
is that scaling models improves generalization.
Depending on the backbone of the model architecture, we further categorize Gen-DBAs into two distinct categories. Please refer to the discussion in Section
3
.

2.4.2.
Action Expert

Action expert is one of the key components in Gen-DBA and is responsible for generating task-specific policies. It serves as the decision-making module that attends to each token of the Transformer backbone and translates the learned representations from the shared embedding space to actionable outputs, i.e.,
action tokens
.
The vocabulary of the action tokens consists of all the
actions
across all tasks present in the experience dataset. For example, action tokens corresponding to query scheduling may range from 1 to 10, while action tokens corresponding to query optimization may range from 11 to 20. Notice that, similar to the
tokenization
stage where all the observation tokens are projected into a shared embedding space, the same principle applies to action tokens.
Action tokens are projected into the same shared embedding space where the rest of the multi-modal tokens
reside.
This enables Gen-DBA to reason jointly over both the observation and action tokens.

2.5.
Training

Inspired by the two-phase training paradigm of LLMs,
Gen-DBA
has
pre-training and post-training
phases.
The objective of the pre-training phase is to train a “base" model that can generalize across diverse database learning tasks on heterogeneous environments.
In contrast,
the goal of post-training is to align the pre-trained “base" model with
target
learning tasks and execution environment.

2.5.1.
Pre-training

During the pre-training stage,
Gen-DBA utilizes
Goal-conditioned Next Token Prediction (NTP, for short)
(
13
)
via Teacher Forcing
(
66
)

for training.
This is in contrast to
the standard training objective used by LLMs during pre-training that employ NTP to predict the next token in natural language sequences from internet-scale corpora.
For any sequence
\bm{\tau}
, let
\tau_{i}
be the
i
-th token, and let
\bm{\tau_{<i}}
be the
i-1
tokens preceding
\tau_{i}
. Then, the goal of the traditional NTP is to estimate the probability distribution of
\tau_{i}
, given
\bm{\tau_{<i}}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{i}|\bm{\tau_{<i}})
. In goal-conditioned NTP, an additional target goal
\mathcal{G}
is included to model the probability distribution of
\tau_{i}
so that it can condition on both the preceding tokens as well as the target goal, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{i}|\bm{\tau_{<i}},\mathcal{G})
. This aligns with the inherent nature of the database learning tasks that is to improve certain performance metrics, e.g., query throughput, query latency, and scalability.

During pre-training, Gen-DBA is trained on the experience dataset. For each sample in the dataset, the agent is trained to predict the policy pertaining to the origin of the sample in terms of the database learning task and the execution environment.
For example, for a query scheduling sample, the agent is trained to predict the scheduling policy, while for a query optimization sample, it predicts the corresponding physical query plan of the input query using the same model. Similarly, for a tuning task, it outputs the optimal configuration of database knobs.
This way,
the agent learns a unified mapping from heterogeneous system states to task-specific actions while sharing representations across diverse database learning tasks.

2.5.2.
Post-training

During the post-training stage, we train Gen-DBA
on a fine-tuning dataset and employ the same Goal-conditioned NTP paradigm. Unlike the experience dataset, the fine-tuning dataset contains samples exclusively pertaining to the target database learning tasks that are being optimized. The fine-tuning dataset consists of high-quality samples to align the “base" model pre-trained on the experience dataset with the behavioral characteristics required for the
optimization tasks in hand. For example, for a query scheduling task, during the post-training stage, the pre-trained base model will only be trained on samples that correspond to high-quality schedules. Depending on the deployment scenario, the base model can be further fine-tuned to handle training samples pertaining to a specific execution environment. Notice that while pre-training is performed generally once, post-training can be performed at regular intervals to refine the behavioral characteristics of the model as the learned policy itself generates high quality samples over time.

2.6.
Inference

During inferencing, Gen-DBA follows an auto-regressive process to generate the policy as a sequence of action tokens for the target learning task. The action token generated by the agent is fed back into itself to generate the next action token. Formally, given previously generated action tokens, i.e.,
\bm{a_{<i}}
, the model generates the next action token
a_{i}
that is then fed back into the model to generate the subsequent action token
a_{i+1}
. This process continues until the full learned policy is rolled out. This process of policy generation is inherently probabilistic. At each step, the agent outputs a probability distribution over possible actions in the action space either through greedy selection or stochastic sampling.

3.
Characterization of Generative Database Agents

Depending on the integration of the generative language models and the data modalities (natural language, database configurations, hardware telemetry) leveraged for learning database optimization tasks, we categorize Gen-DBA into two distinct generations.

3.1.
0th Generation Gen-DBA

In this generation of Gen-DBA, the observed signals used for learning database tasks are limited to database states, e.g., hardware telemetry, database configuration knobs, and query workloads. Natural language is not used as a data modality in any form. Instead, learning is entirely driven by structured, system-level signals collected from the database execution environment. Consequently, an uninitialized Transformer serves as the backbone of the model architecture for this generation of Gen-DBA.

The decision to exclude natural language as an optimization interface is consistent with the design philosophy of traditional AI4DB systems. However, a key limitation of this approach lies in its inability to leverage pre-trained LLMs, which embodies a vast amount of semantic world knowledge. LLMs are trained on large-scale internet corpora that inherently include database components, ranging from SQL examples and schema definitions to tuning heuristics and query optimization discussions. Hence, the possibility of non-trivial knowledge transfer is non-existent in a 0th generation Gen-DBA. Besides, this fundamentally limits the ability of a 0th generation Gen-DBA to distill the learned knowledge across humans in the form of tangible heuristics (Recall our earlier discussion from Section
1
).

3.2.
1st Generation Gen-DBA

The 1st generation Gen-DBA integrates natural language as an optimization interface within its model architecture. The backbone of this Gen-DBA is a pre-trained language model. This design choice enables a 1st generation Gen-DBA to inherit the internet-scale semantic knowledge, and problem-solving abilities of language models. The 1st generation Gen-DBA employs language modality as a first-class citizen. The agent further augments signals perceived from the workloads, hardware telemetry, query execution states, and system configurations. These database-specific signals, along with the action tokens discussed in Section
2.4.2
, are projected into the same representational space as the language tokens. This is due to the rich semantic priors embedded within the pre-trained LLMs. Hence, in this generation of Gen-DBA, the language surface becomes the knowledge bus for the whole system, not just an interface to prompt LLMs.

Aside from distilling the rich semantic capability of the pre-trained LLMs into database systems, the inclusion of language models as the backbone of the 1st generation Gen-DBA can be justified by the multi-modal nature of the database signals. It is far more convenient to represent a query or the full workload in its native form, i.e, SQL over some highly complex feature space used in today’s AI4DB systems. By adopting a pre-trained LLM as its backbone, Gen-DBA can directly encode and reason over language-native signals in their original form. In addition to using natural language to represent database signals, Gen-DBA also uses natural language as the control interface. For example, for query optimization, along with the distributional statistics of the participating tables, Gen-DBA is fed the query in its native SQL form. As a control signal, Gen-DBA is instructed through a natural language prompt, e.g., “Generate the physical query plan". Depending on how Gen-DBA is trained, the control interface can be leveraged to express more complex commands, e.g., “Generate the physical query plan under 5 GB memory limit".

Moreover, natural language is an inevitable choice to distill the learning of Gen-DBA across humans, given that it is regarded as one of the best APIs humankind has ever
developed. The language interface through the pre-trained LLM provides a natural pathway to language-embodied intelligent database systems, as humans can communicate with the Gen-DBA agent about its decision and reasoning process. The shared embedding space where the database-specific tokens, language tokens and action tokens jointly live, can be further leveraged to produce structured summaries behind Gen-DBA’s decision. Notice that leveraging a natural language interface for database systems is nothing new. Refer to the
What-if
index analysis utility interface
(
12
)
of the AutoAdmin project
(
43
)
project from Microsoft Research, where the language modality is fully integrated with the system and is not considered an afterthought. This serves as a key inspiration behind the model architecture of such 1st generation Gen-DBA.

4.
Open Challenges Towards Generative Database Agents

One of the key challenges in realizing the vision of Gen-DBA is the availability of large-scale training data. While foundational LLMs are pre-trained on massive internet-scale corpora, collecting data of a similar magnitude for database learning tasks remains a major challenge. Hence, leveraging these pre-trained LLMs becomes crucial as it can ease the burden of gathering data at such scale. Although Gen-DBA will still require substantial amount of data encompassing different learning tasks and execution environment, the reliance on pre-trained LLM models can significantly lower this barrier. This is exactly why we envision pre-trained LLMs as the backbone of the 1st Generation Gen-DBA. This is quite ironic in the sense that the industry that specializes in managing massive-scale data is now reliant on comparable volumes to train its own intelligent systems. However, this very fact offers a head start.

The database community has developed a wide range of benchmarks over the years across different domains. Examples include the TPC-H, TPC-DS benchmarks for evaluating business intelligence applications, the JOB
(
35
)
, JOB-Complex
(
63
)
and JOB-Extended benchmarks for query optimization, the CardBench benchmark
(
15
)
for evaluating cardinality estimation, the SemBench benchmark
(
33
)
for evaluating semantic query engines. While they provide strong foundations to evaluate a system in terms of performance metric, they do not tie explicitly to database learning tasks. This is unlike the domain of NLP (e.g., FineWeb
(
49
)
), vision (e.g., Web Language Image
(
14
)
), or even robotics (e.g., Open X-Embodiment
(
47
)
), where they have precise large-scale datasets available for the whole community to use. Consider the task of query optimization. A comprehensive dataset for this task will include not only the query workload from the above stated benchmarks, but also the corresponding query plans and estimated cost from the same database across different versions (e.g., PostgreSQL v12.0, v17.0), and even from different database systems (e.g., PostgreSQL, DuckDB) altogether, given the set of available operators to choose from. This enables Gen-DBA to reason about and compare alternate strategies under varying execution environments. These datasets also lower the barrier of initial startup cost as the research focus now shifts to the learning task itself.

Another key challenge in realizing the vision of Gen-DBA is distilling knowledge from AI4DB systems to humans. Current AI4DB systems are performance-driven, focusing on optimizing a performance metric over relaying knowledge. To truly have a chance of finding Move 37, a paradigm shift is required, where in addition to database optimization, equal emphasis is placed on presenting the learned knowledge in tangible forms, e.g., rules and heuristics. Consequently, the first generation of Gen-DBA projects the database related signals into the shared space of language tokens, which makes it possible to query and reason about the actions taken by the system. This shift also calls for changes in how the experience dataset is designed. For instance, in query optimization, aside from the query plan and estimated costs, for a Select-Project-Join (SPJ) query, the corresponding sample in the experience dataset should include the rationale behind this plan, i.e., “the selection operator is pushed below the join operator to reduce the number of intermediate tuples".

Besides, the performance-driven paradigm of AI4DB systems poses a deeper conundrum:
Are these systems truly learning the internal principles of database engine optimization, or are they merely acting as good lossy compressors?
For emergent behavior to emerge from these AI4DB systems, compression is necessary but not sufficient. The language surface revealed by the 1st generation of Gen-DBA can provide insights into the true learning capabilities of AI4DB systems. Nevertheless, this remains an open question that requires deeper investigation.

From systems point of view, for database systems to truly benefit from Gen-DBA and vise-versa, i.e., for Gen-DBA to truly benefit from the complex eco-system of inter-connected policies,
database systems need to be further compartmentalized
(
24
;
38
)
. Much of the modularity already exists in the form of well-defined sub-systems within the database systems, e.g., query optimizer, cardinality estimator, cost function. Beyond that, further decomposition and well-defined interfaces can fully expose the decision space of the database systems to Gen-DBA, thereby expanding the optimization space in which it can operate on. For example, the routing policy, concurrency control policy within an index can be modularized as independent decision modules to increase the learning scope of Gen-DBA.

6.
Related Work

There exists a recent body of work in the field of AI4DB that frames database learning tasks as a question-answering problem in natural language. These AI4DB systems operate in a semantic pick-and-present manner, i.e., they prompt a pre-trained LLM with relevant information, e.g., database state, query workload, hardware telemetry as language context, and in return receive the solution to the target database learning task in natural language. By querying an LLM with a task-relevant context, these AI4DB systems leverage the internet-scale knowledge baked in to the LLMs to improve database systems. They benefit from the semantic reasoning capabilities of these LLMs, and depending on the quality of the prompt, can further transfer non-trivial knowledge to the database
system.
Several recent works fall into this category
of

2
. Even though the underlying philosophy of leveraging large foundational models to benefit from its pre-trained knowledge is complementary to the vision of Gen-DBA, this framework restricts the pathway towards achieving the Move 37.
The effectiveness of a prompt-driven framework relies on the efficiency of the prompt itself to distill as much knowledge as possible from the LLMs into the database learning task through semantic reasoning. Fundamentally, these works suffer from a representational impedance between database-specific knowledge representations and the token-based knowledge representations of LLMs.
Encoding database state as natural language rather limits the expressive power. Besides, LLMs are not treated as first-class citizens in these AI4DB systems. Rater, these systems integrate LLMs as an afterthought, i.e., they are external oracles that cannot benefit from prior database knowledge.

Besides, all these works overlook the crucial aspect of leveraging the semantic knowledge from pre-trained LLMs and distilling knowledge back to humans. Unlike the MTMLF and foundation databases framework, Gen-DBAs are generative, which makes them capable of synthesizing new strategies.

7.
Conclusion

In this paper, we present the core vision and motivation for Generative Database Agents (Gen-DBA), a foundational generative model for database systems that unifies learning, reasoning and optimization under a unified framework. We introduce the recipe for building Gen-DBA that includes an experience dataset spanning diverse learning tasks, hardware telemetry, database knobs, query workload and databases, a hardware-grounded tokenization mechanism via DB-Tokens, a two-stage Goal-Directed Next Token Prediction training paradigm, and a generative inference process. Together, these concepts lay the foundation of a new generation of AI4DB systems that extend the focus from purely performance-driven learning to knowledge-augmented learning.
