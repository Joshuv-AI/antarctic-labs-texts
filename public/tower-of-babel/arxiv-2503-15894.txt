The Gaussian central limit theorem for a stationary time series with infinite variance Thanks: Muneya Matsui’s research is partly supported by the JSPS Grant-in-Aid for Scientific Research C (19K11868).
Muneya Matsui, Thomas Mikosch

Abstract
. We consider a borderline case: the central limit theorem for a strictly stationary time series with infinite variance but a Gaussian limit. In the iid case a well-known sufficient condition for this central limit theorem is regular variation of the marginal distribution with tail index α = 2 \alpha=2 . In the dependent case we assume the stronger condition of sequential regular variation of the time series with tail index α = 2 \alpha=2 . We assume that a sample of size n n from this time series can be split into k n k_{n} blocks of size r n → ∞ r_{n}\to\infty such that r n / n → 0 r_{n}/n\to 0 as n → ∞ n\to\infty and that the block sums are asymptotically independent. Then we apply classical central limit theory for row-wise iid triangular arrays. The necessary and sufficient conditions for such independent block sums will be verified by using large deviation results for the time series. We derive the central limit theorem for m m -dependent sequences, linear processes, stochastic volatility processes and solutions to affine stochastic recurrence equations whose marginal distributions have infinite variance and are regularly varying with tail index α = 2 \alpha=2 .

1.
Introduction

1.1.
The central limit theorem for iid sequences

We start with an iid regularly varying (strictly) stationary sequence
(X_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}}

with generic element
X
and tail index
\alpha>0
. This means, in particular,
that there exist a slowly varying function
L(x)
and constants
p_{\pm}\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cccc}{\mathbb{P}}(|X|>x)&=&\dfrac{L(x)}{x^{\alpha}}\,,&\qquad x>0\,,\\
\dfrac{{\mathbb{P}}(\pm X>x)}{{\mathbb{P}}(|X|>x)}&\to&p_{\pm}\,,&\qquad x\to\infty\,.\end{array}\right.

Consider the partial sum process

\displaystyle S_{n}=X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n}\,,\qquad n\geq 1\,.

We choose normalizing constants as the unique solutions to the equations

(1.4)

\displaystyle n\,{\mathbb{P}}(|X|>a_{n})+\dfrac{n}{a_{n}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}[X^{2}\ {\mathbf{1}}(|X|\leq a_{n})]=1\,.

Classical central limit theory yields the following results in the iid case;
see
[
Pruitt (1981)
]
,

[
Gnedenko and Kolmogorov (1954)
]
,
[
Petrov (1995)
]
,
[
Ibragimov and Linnik (1971)
]
,
[
Feller (1971)
]
:

1.

The case
\alpha<2
.
Then

(1.5)

\displaystyle\dfrac{S_{n}-b_{n}}{a_{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\xi_{\alpha}\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,,

where
\xi_{\alpha}
has an
\alpha
-stable distribution, the centering constants can be chosen such that
b_{n}=n\,{\mathbb{E}}[X\,{\mathbf{1}}(|X|\leq a_{n})]
. Moreover,
b_{n}=n\,{\mathbb{E}}[X]
for

\alpha\in(1,2)
and
b_{n}=0
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
are possible centering constants.

2.

The finite-variance case.

The
\alpha
-stable central limit theorem (
1.5
)
is supplemented
by the classical central limit theorem: if
\sigma^{2}={\rm var}(X)\in(0,\infty)
,
{\mathbb{E}}[X]=0
, then (
1.5
)
holds with
b_{n}=0
,
a_{n}=\sigma\,\sqrt{n}
and a standard normal
limit
\xi_{2}
. The tail behavior of
X
is not relevant.
We can choose
a_{n}
according to (
1.4
) while

n\,{\mathbb{P}}(|X|>a_{n})=o(1)
as
n\to\infty
. The finite-variance case includes certain
distributions satisfying
(
1.1
) for
\alpha=2
.

3.

The case
\alpha=2
,
{\rm var}(X)=\infty
.
Assume
(
1.1
) and without loss of generality that
{\mathbb{E}}[X]=0
.
Then (
1.4
) turns into

(1.6)

\displaystyle\dfrac{n}{a_{n}^{2}}\,L(a_{n})+\dfrac{n}{a_{n}^{2}}\,{\mathbb{E}}[X^{2}\,{\mathbf{1}}(|X|\leq a_{n})]=1\,,

and
K(x)={\mathbb{E}}[X^{2}\,{\mathbf{1}}(|X|\leq x)]
,
x>0
, is a
slowly varying function increasing to infinity, and
{\mathbb{P}}(|X|>x)=o(x^{-2}K(x))
as
x\to\infty
. Hence we can choose

a_{n}=\sqrt{n}\,\ell(n):=\sqrt{n\,K(a_{n})}
where
\ell(x)\to\infty
as
x\to\infty
is a slowly varying function. Moreover, the central limit theorem with normal limit
\xi_{2}
remains valid with the
unconventional normalizing constants
(a_{n})
. As a matter of fact, the necessary
and sufficient condition for the central limit theorem with normalization (
1.6
) is
slow variation of
K(x)
. It is implied by regular variation of
X
with index
\alpha=2
in the sense of
(
1.1
) but slow variation of
K(x)
is slightly more general. In Example
B.2
we consider an iid sequence
(X_{t})
with marginal tail
{\mathbb{P}}(X>x)=x^{-2}N(x)
,
non-slowly varying
N(x)
such that
{\rm var}(X)=\infty
,
K(x)
is slowly varying and
the Gaussian central limit theorem holds.

1.2.
Goals of the paper

We will study the central limit theorem with Gaussian limit
when
(X_{t})
constitutes a real-valued
regularly varying
(strictly) stationary time series with
index
\alpha=2
and
{\rm var}(X)=\infty
.
This means that a generic element
X
satisfies
(
1.1
), and there exist a Pareto
(\alpha)
random variable
Y_{\alpha}
with tail

{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{\alpha}>x)=x^{-\alpha}
,
x>1
, and a sequence of random variables
(\Theta_{t})
which is independent
of
Y_{\alpha}

such that for every
h\geq 0
,

(1.7)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\big(x^{-1}(X_{-h},\ldots,X_{h})\in\cdot\,\big|\,|X_{0}|>x\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\rightarrow}}\big(Y_{\alpha}\,(\Theta_{-h},\ldots,\Theta_{h})\in\cdot\big)\,,\qquad x\to\infty\,.

The process
(\Theta_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is the
spectral tail process
of the
regularly varying sequence
(X_{t})
.

The notion of a regularly varying stationary time series (for general
\alpha>0
) was introduced in different form
by
[
Davis and Hsing (1995)
]
, and in the present form (
1.7
) by
[
Basrak and Segers (2009)
]
. For recent book treatments, see

[
Kulik and Soulier (2020)
]
,
[
Mikosch and Wintenberger (2024)
]
. These references also contain chapters about
\alpha
-stable central limit theory
in the infinite variance case when
\alpha<2
, and about extreme value theory for general
\alpha>0
.

Under the conditions
\alpha=2
and
{\rm var}(X)=\infty

central limit theory for time series with a Gaussian limit has been treated
in the literature quite rarely. We are aware of work by

[
Jakubowski and Szewczak (2020)
]
who proved the central limit theorem for
\alpha=2

for a

GARCH
(1,1)
process. The regular variation properties of such processes are well known; see

[
Buraczewski et al. (2016)
]
for
a book treatment.
[
Peligrad and Sang (2013)
]
,
[
Peligrad et al. (2022)
]
proved the central limit theorem for general
linear processes with iid and martingale difference noise,
including the infinite variance case corresponding
to regular variation with index
\alpha=2
. In this case, the marginal tail of
|X|
of the form

{\mathbb{P}}(|X|>x)\sim c\,x^{-2}L(x)
,
x\to\infty
, is inherited from the regularly varying tail of the
underlying noise; see
[
Mikosch and Samorodnitsky (2000)
]

for best possible conditions under which these results hold
in the case of iid noise.
[
Jakubowski and Szewczak (2020)
]

proved the central limit theorem for a GARCH(1,1) process by an application
of the central limit theorem for martingale differences.
[
Peligrad and Sang (2013)
]
,
[
Peligrad et al. (2022)
]

exploited the structure of the linear process and also used martingale central
limit theory to derive their results. The structure of linear processes with iid
regularly varying noise with index
\alpha<2
was also exploited by
[
Davis and Resnick (1985a)
,
Davis and Resnick (1985b)
]
who proved the

\alpha
-stable central limit theorem under more restrictive conditions
than in

[
Peligrad and Sang (2013)
,
Peligrad et al. (2022)
]
.

[
Philipps and Solo (1992)
]
provided
some general techniques for dealing with asymptotic theory for linear processes.

In our paper we follow a different path of proof. Assuming mixing conditions,
we split the sample
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
into
k_{n}\to\infty
asymptotically independent
blocks of size
r_{n}=n/k_{n}\to\infty
. This reduces the central limit theorem for
(S_{n})
to a central limit theorem for
k_{n}
iid block sums of the size
r_{n}
. Then we can apply
classical limit theory for row-wise iid triangular arrays. The necessary
and sufficient conditions for these results require
uniform large deviation approximations of the
type
{\mathbb{P}}(S_{r_{n}}>a_{n}\,y)\approx r_{n}\,{\mathbb{P}}(X>a_{n}\,y)
where
(a_{n})
is defined in (
1.4
), (
1.6
), and
y>0
is chosen from a suitable region. We refer to
Theorem
2.2
below for the necessary and sufficient conditions
for the central limit theorem for row-wise iid triangular arrays.
Using precise large deviation bounds in these conditions, we can
derive the central limit theorem
(S_{n}-{\mathbb{E}}[X])/a_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,\sigma^{2})
in the boundary case
\alpha=2
and
determine the value
\sigma^{2}
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2

we introduce the basic mixing condition (
2.1
)
that ensures the asymptotic independence
of the block sums based on the increasing sample. This condition
is assumed throughout the paper. It is expressed in terms of characteristic functions and can easily
be verified under classical mixing conditions such as
m
-dependence, strong mixing or
\beta
-mixing with appropriate rates.

The basic central limit theorem for independent block sums is provided in Theorem
2.2
. Its conditions require uniform bounds for the tails of the block sums,
partly hidden in the truncated moments of these sums. The consequent
use of regular variation for time series and related large deviation techniques are
perhaps the main differences to the literature on the central limit theorem for regularly varying time series with tail index
\alpha=2
;
see
[
Jakubowski and Szewczak (2020)
,
Peligrad and Sang (2013)
,
Peligrad et al. (2022)
]
and
the preceding discussion. An advantage of our approach is its generality: it does not
depend on a particular structure of the time series. Moreover, under the mixing condition
(
2.1
) the conditions of Theorem
2.2
are necessary and sufficient for the central limit theorem to hold. The aforementioned literature
[
Jakubowski and Szewczak (2020)
,
Peligrad and Sang (2013)
,
Peligrad et al. (2022)
]
depends on the martingale central limit theorem when
\alpha=2
,
{\rm var}(X)=\infty
; cf. Theorem 8 in Chapter V of

[
Liptser and Shiryaev (1986)
]
. Writing
V_{n}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}^{2}
and assuming
{\mathbb{E}}[X]=0
, the latter approach also allows
one to derive the joint convergence of
a_{n}^{-1}(S_{n}-b_{n},V_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}(\xi_{2},1)
and the
corresponding studentized central limit theorem
(S_{n}-b_{n})/V_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}\xi_{2}
as
n\to\infty
.

In Section
2
we discuss how some of the necessary
conditions for the central limit theorem of independent block sums can be verified.
The main difficulty of Theorem
2.2
is the control
of the asymptotic variance
\sigma^{2}
in the central limit theorem: it appears as a scaled
limit of the
variance of truncated block sums. In this context the large deviation results show its full power.
In Section
3.1
we provide the form of the asymptotic variance in terms
of the spectral tail process
(\Theta_{t})
for an
m
-dependent process
(X_{t})
.
This is due to a large deviation result proved in
[
Mikosch and Wintenberger (2016)
]
. It depends on the crucial condition
that
{\mathbb{P}}(\Theta_{-m}=\cdots=\Theta_{-1}=0)>0
. The derivation of the backward
spectral tail process is, in general, a hard problem but it is simple
for
m
-dependent linear processes and stochastic volatility models; see Section
3.2

and Example
3.3
, respectively. Regularly varying linear and stochastic volatility models with
infinite memory can be approximated by regularly varying
m
-dependent versions of these
processes; see Sections
3.2
and
3.3
.
These approximations work because the regular variation of the aforementioned
processes is caused by their underlying iid regularly varying noise.

This is in sharp contrast to the regularly varying solution of an affine stochastic recurrence equation; see

[
Buraczewski et al. (2016)
]
for the theory of such processes. Truncations of the infinite series representations of these solutions
are not regularly varying, in general, and may have all moments. Therefore
m
-dependent approximations to the infinite series do not
work in general. In Sections
3.4
and
3.5
we deal with the central limit theorem for
\alpha=2
by applying precise
large deviation results obtained in
[
Buraczewski et al. (2013)
]
in the so-called Kesten-Goldie case and in
[
Konstantinides and Mikosch (2005)
]

in the so-called Grincevičius-Grey case. These results allow us to determine the asymptotic variance in the corresponding central limit theorems via Theorem
2.2
.

The proofs of central limit theorems in the borderline case
\alpha=2
show
that both extreme value theory and large deviation results get into a marriage. For
\alpha>2
one can work with the autocorrelation function of the underlying time series and derive central limit theory with Gaussian
limits. This is in stark contrast with
\alpha<2
where large deviations and extremes are
at the heart of the problem for deriving
\alpha
-stable limit theory; see
[
Mikosch and Wintenberger (2013)
,
Mikosch and Wintenberger (2014)
,
Mikosch and Wintenberger (2016)
]
and
[
Matsui et al. (2024a)
,
Matsui et al. (2024b)
]
.

2.
Preliminaries

In what follows, we consider a real-valued regularly varying strictly stationary sequence
(X_{t})

(in the sense of (

\alpha=2

\displaystyle\varphi_{S_{n}/a_{n}}(u)=\big(\varphi_{S_{r_{n}}/a_{n}}(u)\big)^{k_{n}}+o(1)\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,.

This relation holds under classical conditions
such as

[
Mikosch and Wintenberger (2024)
]

for sufficient conditions for (

arguments leading to this condition.
In the

-dependent case one can choose

If

is strongly mixing with mixing coefficients

Example 2.1
.

Assume

Under (

), the central limit theorem for

holds if and only if it does

.

.

Condition (

\displaystyle\leq c\,\dfrac{k_{n}}{a_{n}}r_{n}\,\int_{a_{n}}^{s_{n}}{\mathbb{P}}(|X|>y)\,dy+\dfrac{k_{n}}{a_{n}}\int_{s_{n}}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\big(|S_{r_{n}}|>y\big)\,dy+k_{n}{\mathbb{P}}\big(|S_{r_{n}}|>a_{n}\big)

\displaystyle=:J_{1}+J_{2}+J_{3}\,.

The fact
J_{3}=o(1)
follows directly from (
2.5
).
By Karamata’s integral theorem (see

[
Bingham et al. (1987)
]
) and the definition of
(a_{n})
,
J_{1}=O(n\,{\mathbb{P}}(|X|>a_{n}))=o(1)
.
For
J_{2}
we apply Markov’s inequality of
the order
2-\delta
for an arbitrarily small
\delta>0
:

\displaystyle J_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c\,\dfrac{k_{n}}{a_{n}}\,{\mathbb{E}}[|S_{r_{n}}|^{2-\delta}]\,\int_{s_{n}}^{\infty}y^{-2+\delta}\,dy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c\,\dfrac{n\,{\mathbb{E}}[|S_{r_{n}}|^{2-\delta}]}{r_{n}\,a_{n}\,s_{n}^{1-\delta}}=O\Big(\dfrac{n^{1/2}\,r_{n}^{\gamma-1}}{\ell(n)\,s_{n}^{1-\delta}}\Big)\,.

In the last step we used the structure of
a_{n}=\sqrt{n}\,\ell(n)
and the bound

{\mathbb{E}}[|S_{r_{n}}|^{2-\delta}]\leq c\,r_{n}^{\gamma}
for some positive
\gamma
;
see (
2.6
). The right-hand side converges to zero by virtue of (
2.7
).

∎

While the conditions

3.1.
The
m
-dependent case

We consider an
m
-dependent stationary sequence
(X_{t})

condition (

r_{n}\to\infty

arbitrarily slowly.
Recall the definition of the spectral

(\Theta_{t})
for a regularly varying sequence

By its definition, the spectral tail process for an

-dependent process degenerates, i.e.,

for

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume
m
-dependence for a
regularly varying stationary sequence
(X_{t})
with index
\alpha=2

Then (

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}={\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big(\sum_{t=0}^{m}\Theta_{t}\Big)^{2}\,{\mathbf{1}}\big(\Theta_{-i}=0,i=1,\ldots,m\big)\Big].

Proof.

Due to
m
-dependence we can choose the block size
r_{n}\to\infty

arbitrarily small.
We have for every
\varepsilon>0
and arbitrarily small

\displaystyle k_{n}\,{\mathbb{E}}\big[(S_{r_{n}}/a_{n})^{2}{\mathbf{1}}\big(|S_{r_{n}}|\leq\varepsilon a_{n}\big)\big]

\displaystyle=:I_{1}+I_{2}-I_{3}\,.

We start with
I_{2}
. In the
m
-dependent case we have the following large deviation result from
[
Mikosch and Wintenberger (2016)
]

{\mathbb{P}}(|S_{r_{n}}|>\sqrt{y})\sim\sigma^{2}\,r_{n}\,{\mathbb{P}}(X^{2}>y)

uniformly in the

where we choose

Then we obtain

\displaystyle I_{2}\sim\sigma^{2}\,\dfrac{n}{a_{n}^{2}}\,\int_{r_{n}^{1+\delta}}^{(\varepsilon a_{n})^{2}}{\mathbb{P}}(X^{2}>y)\,dy\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,.

By Karamata’s integral theorem (see
[
Bingham et al. (1987)
]
)
and
the definition of
(a_{n})
,

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\,n\,{\mathbb{E}}\big[(X/a_{n})^{2}{\mathbf{1}}(r_{n}^{(1+\delta)/2}<|X|\leq\varepsilon a_{n})\big]

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\dfrac{{\mathbb{E}}[X^{2}{\mathbf{1}}(r_{n}^{(1+\delta)/2}<|X|\leq\varepsilon a_{n})]}{{\mathbb{E}}[X^{2}{\mathbf{1}}(|X|\leq\varepsilon a_{n})]}\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,.

The function

By the large derivation result we also have

.

Finally,

so small that

\displaystyle I_{1}\leq\dfrac{k_{n}}{a_{n}^{2}}\,r_{n}^{1+\delta}=\dfrac{r_{n}^{\delta}}{\ell^{2}(n)}=o(1)\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,.

This finishes the proof.

3.2.
Linear process

In this section we will provide the central limit theorem for a causal linear process

(3.3)

\displaystyle X_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_{j}\,Z_{t-j}\,,\qquad t\in{\mathbb{Z}}\,,

where
(Z_{t})
is an iid regularly varying sequence with index
\alpha=2
,
{\rm var}(Z)=\infty
,

{\mathbb{E}}[Z]=0
, and
(\psi_{j})
is an absolutely summable sequence of real numbers.
Then the infinite series in (
3.3
) converges a.s. by virtue of the
3-series theorem. We assume
\psi_{0}=1
, and it will be convenient
to write
\psi_{j}=0
for
j\leq-1
.

The process
(X_{t})
is regularly varying with index
\alpha=2
;
see
[
Mikosch and Wintenberger (2024)
]
, Section 5.2.6.
In particular, its spectral tail process is given by

(3.4)

\displaystyle\Theta_{t}=\Theta_{Z}\,\dfrac{\psi_{J+t}}{|\psi_{J}|}\,,\qquad t\in{\mathbb{Z}}\,,

where
{\mathbb{P}}(\Theta_{Z}=\pm 1)=\lim_{x\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(\pm Z>x)/{\mathbb{P}}(|Z|>x)
,
\Theta_{Z}
is
independent of
J
with distribution
{\mathbb{P}}(J=j)=|\psi_{j}|^{2}/\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}
,
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
where
\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\psi_{k}^{2}
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Under the aforementioned conditions, the
linear process
(X_{t})
satisfies the central limit theorem
a_{n}^{-1}S_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,\sigma^{2})
where

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}:=\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_{j}\Big)^{2}/\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}\,,

and
(a_{n})
is defined in (
1.4
). Alternatively, replacing
(a_{n})
by the
corresponding normalization
(a_{n}^{Z})
for the iid sequence
(Z_{t})
, we have

(a_{n}^{Z})^{-1}S_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,\widetilde{\sigma}^{2})
where
\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}=\big(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_{j}\big)^{2}
.

Remark 3.5
.

If
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_{j}=0
the Gaussian limit law degenerates to zero. The result of Proposition
3.4
remains valid for two-sided linear
processes
X_{t}=\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\psi_{j}\,Z_{t-j}
,
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Indeed, one can follow the lines of the proof below, first considering

X_{t}^{(m)}=\sum_{|j|\leq m}\psi_{j}\,Z_{t-j}
,
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, for fixed
m\geq 1
, and then letting
m\to\infty
.

Proof.

We split the proof into two parts.
First we apply the central limit theorem for an
m
-dependent
linear process and then we extend it to the general case.

Finite moving averages
.
Consider the process

\displaystyle X_{t}=X_{t}^{(m)}:=\psi_{0}\,Z_{t}+\psi_{1}\,Z_{t-1}+\cdots+\psi_{m}\,Z_{t-m}\,,\qquad t\in{\mathbb{Z}}\,,

with generic element
X^{(m)}
and
where
\psi_{m}\neq 0
. We have assumed
\psi_{0}=1
, and we
write
\psi_{j}=0
for
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}\backslash\{0,\ldots,m\}
. Then
the random variable
J
in
(
3.4
) takes values in
\{0,\ldots,m\}
.

According to (
3.4
) and since
\psi_{0}=1\neq 0
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\big(\Theta_{-m}=\cdots=\Theta_{-1}=0\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m}\dfrac{\psi_{j}^{2}}{\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}}\,{\mathbf{1}}\big(\psi_{j-m}=\cdots=\psi_{j-1}=0\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\psi_{0}^{2}}{\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}}\,{\mathbf{1}}\big(\psi_{-m}=\cdots=\psi_{-1}=0\big)=\dfrac{1}{\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}}>0\,.

Hence

(3.5)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}:=\sigma_{m}^{2}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big(\sum_{t=0}^{m}\Theta_{t}\Big)^{2}\,{\mathbf{1}}(\Theta_{-m}=\cdots=\Theta_{-1}=0)\Big]=\dfrac{\big(\sum_{t=0}^{m}\psi_{t}\big)^{2}}{\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}}\,.

By Proposition
3.2

we have the central limit theorem
(a_{n}^{(m)})^{-1}S_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,\sigma_{m}^{2})

where the normalizing sequence
(a_{n}^{(m)})
is given by (
1.4
)
for
X=X^{(m)}
.

Our next goal is to show that the normalizations
(a_{n}^{(m)})
for

m\leq\infty
are asymptotically equivalent. As before, we write
X
and
X^{(m)}
for generic elements in the cases
m=\infty
and
m<\infty
, respectively.

We have
{\mathbb{P}}(|X^{(m)}|>x)/{\mathbb{P}}(|Z|>x)\to\sum_{j=0}^{m}\psi_{j}^{2}
as
x\to\infty

for
m\leq\infty
; see (5.2.23) in
[
Mikosch and Wintenberger (2024)
]
. Then by Karamata’s theorem and uniform convergence,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[(X^{(m)}/x)^{2}\,{\mathbf{1}}\big(|X^{(m)}|\leq x\big)\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{-2}\int_{0}^{x^{2}}\dfrac{{\mathbb{P}}\big(|X^{(m)}|>\sqrt{y}\big)}{{\mathbb{P}}(|Z|>\sqrt{y})}\,{\mathbb{P}}(|Z|>\sqrt{y})\,dy-\dfrac{{\mathbb{P}}\big(|X^{(m)}|>x\big)}{{\mathbb{P}}(|Z|>x)}\,{\mathbb{P}}(|Z|>x)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}\,\Big(x^{-2}\int_{0}^{x^{2}}{\mathbb{P}}(|Z|>\sqrt{y})\,dy-{\mathbb{P}}(|Z|>x)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}\;{\mathbb{E}}\big[(Z/x)^{2}\,{\mathbf{1}}(|Z|\leq x)\big]\,,\qquad x\to\infty\,.

Writing
a_{n}^{Z}
for the solution to (
1.4
) in the case
X=Z
, we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{n}{(a_{n}^{Z})^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[(X^{(m)})^{2}\,{\mathbf{1}}\big(|X^{(m)}|\leq a_{n}^{Z}\big)\big]

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,.

Since
{\mathbb{E}}\big[(X^{(m)})^{2}\,{\mathbf{1}}\big(|X^{(m)}|\leq x\big)\big]
,
x>0
, is a slowly varying function this implies that

\displaystyle\dfrac{n}{(a_{n}^{Z}\|\psi\|_{2})^{2}}\,{\mathbb{E}}\big[(X^{(m)})^{2}\,{\mathbf{1}}\big(|X^{(m)}|\leq a_{n}^{Z}\|\psi\|_{2}\big)\big]\to 1\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,.

Since all normalizing constants in the central limit theorem are asymptotically equivalent
we can choose
a_{n}^{(m)}=a_{n}^{Z}\|\psi\|_{2}
both for
m
finite and infinite
(we do not indicate
m
in
\|\psi\|_{2}
).

In particular, by the first part of the proof we have for
m<\infty
,

(3.6)

\displaystyle(a_{n}^{(m)})^{-1}S_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N\Big(0,\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{m}\psi_{j}\Big)^{2}/\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}\Big)\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,.

Letting
m\to\infty
on the right-hand side we derive the desired Gaussian limit distribution in
the case
m=\infty
.

The case of a causal linear process with infinitely many non-zero
\psi_{j}
.

For any finite
m
we write
S_{n}^{(m)}:=\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}^{(m)}
.
We will show that for every
\delta>0
,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\big(\big|S_{n}-S_{n}^{(m)}\big|>\delta\,a_{n}\big)=0\,.

This together with (
3.6
) (and the asymptotic equivalence of the

(a_{n})
and

)
will prove that

\displaystyle a_{n}^{-1}S_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N\Big(0,\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_{j}\Big)^{2}/\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}\Big)\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,;

]

\displaystyle\delta^{-1}\,{\mathbb{E}}\big[|(S_{n}-S_{n}^{(m)})/a_{n}|\big]

\displaystyle c\,\sum_{j=m+1}^{\infty}|\psi_{j}|\,{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big|\sum_{t=1}^{n}Z_{t-j}/a_{n}\Big|\Big]\,.

A stochastic volatility model

(a_{n}^{(m)})^{-1}S_{n}^{(m)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,1)

,

is given by

\displaystyle n\,{\mathbb{P}}(|Z|>a_{n}^{Z})+{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\Big|\sum_{t=1}^{n}(\overline{Z}_{t}-{\mathbb{E}}[\overline{Z}])\,(\sigma_{t}-\sigma_{t}^{(m)})\Big|>\gamma\,a_{n}^{Z}/2\Big)

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{P}}\Big(n\,{\mathbb{E}}[|Z/a_{n}^{Z}|{\mathbf{1}}(|Z|>a_{n}^{Z})]\,\Big|\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}(\sigma_{t}-\sigma_{t}^{(m)})\Big|>\gamma/2\Big)

\displaystyle I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}\,.

We have

(a_{n}^{Z})
.

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}(\sigma_{t}-\sigma_{t}^{(m)})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm a.s.}}{{\rightarrow}}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\sigma_{0}-\sigma_{0}^{(m)}]\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,.

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c\,n\,{\mathbb{E}}[(Z/a_{n}^{Z})^{2}\,{\mathbf{1}}(|Z|\leq a_{n}^{Z})]\,{\mathbb{E}}\big[(\sigma_{0}-\sigma_{0}^{(m)})^{2}\big]\,.

The first factor is bounded by definition of
(a_{n}^{Z})
while

as

.
Thus we have proved the following result.

Proposition 3.6
.

Consider the stochastic volatility model
X_{t}=\sigma_{t}\,Z_{t}
,
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, where
(Z_{t})
is centered
iid regularly varying with index
\alpha=2
and infinite variance independent
of the log-volatility sequence
\log\sigma_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_{j}\,\eta_{t-j}
,

t\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, where
(\psi_{j})
is a square-integrable sequence of real numbers and

(\eta_{t})
is iid standard normal. Then the following central limit theorem holds

(3.10)

\displaystyle(a_{n}^{Z})^{-1}S_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N\Big(0,\exp\Big(2\,\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_{j}^{2}\Big)\Big)\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,.

Remark 3.7
.

The condition of iid standard normal noise
(\eta_{t})
can be relaxed. It suffices that

\eta_{0}
has a finite moment generating function such that
{\mathbb{E}}[\sigma_{0}^{2}]=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}[{\rm e}\,^{2\,\psi_{j}\,\eta_{0}}]
is finite. Then the asymptotic variance in
(
3.10
) depends on the concrete form of this moment generating function.

Remark 3.8
.

Consider an iid standard normal sequence
(N_{t})
independent of
(X_{t})
.
We observe that the process
\widetilde{X}_{t}=X_{t}\,N_{t}=\sigma_{t}\,(Z_{t}\,N_{t})
,
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
is again a regularly varying stationary stochastic volatility process with index
\alpha=2
. Indeed,

(Z_{t}\,N_{t})
is iid symmetric and by Breiman’s lemma
(Lemma 3.1.11 in
[
Mikosch and Wintenberger (2024)
]
),

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(|Z_{0}\,N_{0}|>x)\sim{\mathbb{E}}[N_{0}^{2}]\,{\mathbb{P}}(|Z|>x)={\mathbb{P}}(|Z|>x)\,,\qquad x\to\infty\,.

Choosing the normalizing constants according to
(
3.2
), we have
a_{n}^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}\,N_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,1)
.
According to Lemma
A.1
this relation holds if and only if
a_{n}^{-2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}^{2}=:a_{n}^{-2}V_{n}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\rightarrow}}1
. Thus we also have
the central limit theorem for the studentized sum
S_{n}/V_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,1)
as
n\to\infty
.

3.4.
Affine stochastic recurrence equations: the Kesten-Goldie case

In contrast to the previous examples (linear process, stochastic volatility model),
in this section we will consider
a stationary stochastic process
(X_{t})
whose regular variation property is not caused
by the iid regularly varying innovations: the stationary solution to a particular affine stochastic recurrence equation.
The marginal tails of this process are almost of Pareto-type, hence the marginal
distribution has infinite variance if
\alpha=2
.
We will show that the central limit theorem for this process can be
explained by its large deviation properties.

We consider the stationary solution to the stochastic recurrence equation

(3.11)

\displaystyle X_{t}=A_{t}\,X_{t-1}+B_{t}\,,\qquad t\in{\mathbb{Z}}\,,

under the following conditions on the
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}
-valued iid sequence
(A_{t},B_{t})
,
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, with generic element
(A,B)
:

•

The equation
{\mathbb{E}}[A^{\alpha}]=1
has the solution
\alpha=2
,
m_{2}:={\mathbb{E}}[A^{2}\,\log A]<\infty
and
{\mathbb{E}}[B^{2}]<\infty
.

•

The law of
\log A
conditioned on
\{A>0\}
is non-arithmetic and for every
x
,
{\mathbb{P}}(A\,x+B=x)<1
.

Under these conditions, (
3.11
) has a unique solution
(X_{t})
with generic element
X
, the process is regularly varying with index
\alpha=2
, and the tails of the marginal distribution satisfy the asymptotic relation

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\pm X>x)\sim c_{\pm}\,x^{-2}\,,\qquad x\to\infty\,,

where
m_{2}>0
and the constants
c_{\pm}
are given by

\displaystyle c_{\pm}:={\mathbb{E}}\big[(A\,X+B)_{\pm}^{2}-(A\,X)_{\pm}^{2}\big]/(2\,m_{2})\,.

In particular, since
{\mathbb{E}}[X]={\mathbb{E}}[X]\,{\mathbb{E}}[A]+{\mathbb{E}}[B]
, as
x\to\infty
,

\displaystyle x^{2}\,{\mathbb{P}}(|X|>x)\to c_{+}+c_{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{{\mathbb{E}}\big[(A\,X+B)^{2}-(A\,X)^{2}\big]}{2\,m_{2}}=\dfrac{{\mathbb{E}}[B^{2}+2\,A\,B\,X]}{2\,m_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{{\mathbb{E}}[B^{2}]\,(1-{\mathbb{E}}[A])+2\,{\mathbb{E}}[A\,B]\,{\mathbb{E}}[B]}{2\,(1-{\mathbb{E}}[A])\,m_{2}}=:c_{\infty}\,.

When we choose
B=1
a.s. the constant
c_{\infty}
turns into

(3.12)

\displaystyle c_{0}:=\dfrac{1+{\mathbb{E}}[A]}{1-{\mathbb{E}}[A]}\,\dfrac{1}{2\,m_{2}}\,.

These results are known as the Kesten-Goldie theorem; see
[
Kesten (1973)
]
,
[
Goldie (1991)
]
; cf. Theorem 2.4.4 in
[
Buraczewski et al. (2016)
]
.

In the following result we assume slightly stronger assumptions on the moments
of
A
and
B
as well as suitable mixing conditions. The additional moment
conditions are needed in the large deviation results of Lemma
3.13
below.

Proposition 3.9
.

Assume the aforementioned conditions on the stationary solution
(X_{t})
to
the affine stochastic recurrence equation (
3.11
). In addition, we require

{\mathbb{E}}[A^{\gamma}+|B|^{\gamma}]<\infty
for some

\gamma>2
and that
(X_{t})
is strongly mixing with geometric rate.
Then
the central limit theorem
a_{n}^{-1}(S_{n}-d_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,c_{0})
holds where
d_{n}=n\,{\mathbb{E}}[X]=n\,{\mathbb{E}}[B]/(1-{\mathbb{E}}[A])
,

(a_{n})
satisfies (
1.4
) and
c_{0}
is defined in (
3.12
).

Remark 3.10
.

Since
{\mathbb{P}}(|X|>x)\sim c_{\infty}\,x^{-2}
we can choose
the normalization
a_{n}=\sqrt{c_{\infty}\,n\,\log n}
; see the proof of the proposition below.
Then the central limit theorem can be reformulated as follows:

\displaystyle\dfrac{S_{n}-n\,{\mathbb{E}}[B]/(1-{\mathbb{E}}[A])}{\sqrt{n\,\log n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,c_{0}\,c_{\infty})\,.

Remark 3.11
.

Under mild conditions
the solution
(X_{t})
to an affine stochastic recurrence equation is
\beta
-mixing with geometric rate,
hence strongly mixing with geometric rate. For example,
the geometric rate is achieved if
A_{t}=A(Z_{t})
,
B_{t}=B(Z_{t})
,
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
are polynomials of an iid sequence
(Z_{t})
, and a generic element of
Z

has a Lebesgue density on some open interval containing zero;
see
[
Mokkadem (1990)
]
.
For further discussions on this topic we refer to Section 4.2.2 in

[
Buraczewski et al. (2016)
]
. We will apply strong mixing with geometric rate to achieve the mixing condition (
2.1
) for suitable
(r_{n})
.
An alternative way
of verifying (
2.1
) is by using the fact that

(X_{t})
is a Markov chain satisfying a contractive stochastic recurrence equation. In this case, coupling arguments
can be applied; see Section 9.1.3
in
[
Mikosch and Wintenberger (2024)
]
, in particular the proof
of Theorem 9.3.6,
where the
\alpha
-stable central limit theorem is derived in the case
\alpha<2
.

Remark 3.12
.

[
Jakubowski and Szewczak (2020)
]
proved a central limit theorem for a stationary
GARCH
(1,1)
process
(X_{t})
in the infinite variance case.
These processes are known to have tails of the form
{\mathbb{P}}(\pm\,X>x)\sim c_{\pm}\,x^{-\alpha}
,
x\to\infty
, under mild conditions on the underlying innovations sequence; see
[
Mikosch and Stărică (2000)
]
; cf.
[
Buraczewski et al. (2016)
]
. The squared volatility process of
(X_{t})
satisfies an affine
stochastic recurrence equation of Kesten-Goldie type, and the tails of
(X_{t})
inherit the power-law tails from the latter process. The results in

[
Jakubowski and Szewczak (2020)
]
focus on
the case
\alpha=2
. They use elegant martingale techniques providing the central limit theorem for
(S_{n}/a_{n})
and the law of large numbers

V_{n}^{2}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}^{2}/a_{n}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\rightarrow}}1
.
Thus
S_{n}/V_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,1)
as
n\to\infty
. This approach avoids knowledge of the normalizing constants
a_{n}
.

Proof.

We start by observing that the mixing condition (
2.1
) holds, and we can choose

\ell_{n}=[C\log n]
for a suitable
C>0
and
r_{n}=[(\log n)^{1+\varepsilon}]
for arbitrarily small
\varepsilon>0
.
This
follows from the strong mixing condition with geometric rate for
(X_{t})
;
see Example
2.1
for a discussion on the mixing properties of
(X_{t})
.

We will apply Theorem
2.2
and the following large deviation result in
the particular case
\alpha=2
taken from

[
Buraczewski et al. (2013)
]
, Theorem 2.1.

Lemma 3.13
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
3.9
we have for
M>2
,

(3.13)

\displaystyle\sup_{n}\sup_{n^{1/2}(\log n)^{M}\leq x}\dfrac{{\mathbb{P}}(|S_{n}-d_{n}|>x)}{n\,{\mathbb{P}}(|X|>x)}<\infty\,,

and, if
(t_{n})
is chosen such that
t_{n}\geq\log\big(n^{1/2}(\log n)^{M}\big)
for large
n

and
\lim_{n\to\infty}t_{n}/n=0
then

(3.14)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{n^{1/2}(\log n)^{M}\leq x\leq\exp(t_{n})}\Big|\dfrac{{\mathbb{P}}(|S_{n}-d_{n}|>x)}{n\,{\mathbb{P}}(|X|>x)}-c_{0}\Big|=0\,.

The constant
c_{0}
is defined in (
3.12
).

We start by verifying (
2.2
) in Theorem
2.2
.
We choose
(r_{n})
such that
a_{n}^{2}=c_{\infty}\,n\,\log n>r_{n}\,(\log r_{n})^{2\,M}
for some
M>2
.
This is always possible since
r_{n}/n=o(1)
. By (
3.13
) we have

(3.15)

\displaystyle k_{n}\,{\mathbb{P}}\big(|S_{r_{n}}-d_{r_{n}}|>a_{n}\big)\,\leq c\,n\,{\mathbb{P}}(|X|>a_{n})\to 0\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,.

Next we verify (
2.4
). Since
{\mathbb{E}}[S_{r_{n}}-d_{r_{n}}]=0
it suffices to
show that the following quantity vanishes as
n\to\infty
:

\displaystyle v_{n}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\dfrac{k_{n}}{a_{n}}\,{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big|S_{r_{n}}-d_{r_{n}}\big|{\mathbf{1}}\big(|S_{r_{n}}-d_{r_{n}}|>a_{n}\big)\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{k_{n}}{a_{n}}\int_{a_{n}}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\big(\big|S_{r_{n}}-d_{r_{n}}\big|>y\big)\,dy+k_{n}\,{\mathbb{P}}\big(|S_{r_{n}}-d_{r_{n}}|>a_{n}\big)\,.

Due to the large deviation result the second term is bounded by

c\,n\,{\mathbb{P}}(|X|>a_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
By the choice of
(r_{n})
and by virtue of (
3.13
) we have

\displaystyle v_{n}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c\,\dfrac{k_{n}}{a_{n}}\,r_{n}\,\int_{a_{n}}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(|X|>y)\,dy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c\,\dfrac{n}{a_{n}}\,\int_{a_{n}}^{\infty}y^{-2}\,dy=O(n/a_{n}^{2})=o(1)\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,.

Finally, we prove (
2.3
). We have

\displaystyle\dfrac{k_{n}}{a_{n}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[(S_{r_{n}}-d_{r_{n}})^{2}\,{\mathbf{1}}\big(|S_{r_{n}}-d_{r_{n}}|\leq\varepsilon\,a_{n}\big)\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{k_{n}}{a_{n}^{2}}\Big(\int_{0}^{r_{n}\,(\log r_{n})^{2\,M}}+\int_{r_{n}\,(\log r_{n})^{2\,M}}^{(\varepsilon\,a_{n})^{2}}\Big){\mathbb{P}}\big(|S_{r_{n}}-d_{r_{n}}|>\sqrt{y}\big)\,dy

\displaystyle-\varepsilon^{2}k_{n}{\mathbb{P}}\big(|S_{r_{n}}-d_{r_{n}}|>\varepsilon a_{n}\big)=:I_{1}+I_{2}-I_{3}\,.

From (
3.15
) we also have
I_{3}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
By assumption,
M>2
. Then we can use the large deviation result (
3.14
) to obtain

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\dfrac{n}{a_{n}^{2}}\,c_{0}\,\int_{r_{n}\,(\log r_{n})^{2\,M}}^{(\varepsilon a_{n})^{2}}{\mathbb{P}}(|X|>\sqrt{y})\,dy

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\dfrac{n}{a_{n}^{2}}\,c_{0}\,c_{\infty}\log(a_{n}^{2})\to c_{0}\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,.

We also have by definition of
(r_{n})
,

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\dfrac{k_{n}}{a_{n}^{2}}\,r_{n}\,(\log r_{n})^{2\,M}\leq c\,\dfrac{(\log r_{n})^{2\,M}}{\log n}\to 0\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,.

∎

3.5.
Affine stochastic recurrence equations: the Grincevičius-Grey case

The Kesten-Goldie theory is supplemented by the Grincevičius-Grey theory.
Here one considers the stationary
solution to the affine stochastic recurrence equation (
3.11
) under the conditions
that
(A_{t},B_{t})
,
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, is an
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{2}
-valued iid sequence such that a generic element
(A,B)
satisfies
{\mathbb{E}}[A^{\alpha}]<1
and
B
is regularly varying with index

\alpha>0
. Then
(X_{t})
is regularly varying with index
\alpha
; see
[
Mikosch and Wintenberger (2024)
]
, Proposition 5.6.10. The stationary solution has representation
\sum_{j=-\infty}^{t}\,B_{j}\,A_{j+1}\cdots\,A_{t}
,
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. The regular variation of
(X_{t})
is inherited from the regular variation of the iid sequence
(B_{t})
. This is in stark
contrast to the Kesten-Goldie situation where the regular variation of a single
element
X_{t}
(and of the whole sequence) is due to its infinite series representation.
In other words, in the latter case the truncated
m
-dependent series

X_{t}^{(m)}=\sum_{j=t-m}^{t}\,B_{j}\,A_{j+1}\cdots\,A_{t}
is not regularly varying with index
\alpha

while, in the Grincevičius-Grey case,
(X_{t}^{(m)})
is regularly varying due to the
regular variation of
B
. The Grincevičius-Grey case has some similarity with
linear processes where the regular variation is inherited from the regular variation of the iid
noise.

In addition to the aforementioned conditions we assume:

•

(A_{t})
and
(B_{t})
are independent.

•

B
is regularly varying with index
\alpha=2
,
{\mathbb{E}}[A^{2}]<1
and
{\mathbb{E}}[A^{4}]<\infty
.

Consider the unique solution to the affine stochastic recurrence equation
C_{t}=1+A_{t}\,C_{t-1}
,
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, given by

(3.16)

\displaystyle C_{t}=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{t}A_{j+1}\cdots A_{t}\,,\qquad t\in{\mathbb{Z}}\,,

write
C
for a generic element and
\sigma_{x}^{2}:={\rm var}(B\,{\mathbf{1}}(B\leq x))
,
x>0
.

The following large derivation result is taken from
[
Konstantinides and Mikosch (2005)
]
, Theorem 4.2.

Proposition 3.14
.

Assume the aforementioned conditions in the Grincevičius-Grey case.
Consider a sequence of positive numbers
(x_{n})

such that
n\,{\mathbb{P}}(B>x_{n})\to 0
and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{x\geq x_{n}}\dfrac{{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{n}C_{t}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}>x/(\sqrt{\log x}\,\sigma_{x})\Big)+{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\Big|\sum_{t=1}^{n}(C_{t}-{\mathbb{E}}C)\Big|>x\Big)}{n\,{\mathbb{P}}(B>x)}=0\,.

Then we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{x\geq x_{n}}\Big|\dfrac{{\mathbb{P}}(S_{n}-{\mathbb{E}}S_{n}>x)}{n\,{\mathbb{P}}(B>x)}-{\mathbb{E}}[C^{2}]\Big|=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{x\geq x_{n}}\dfrac{{\mathbb{P}}(S_{n}-{\mathbb{E}}S_{n}\leq-x)}{n\,{\mathbb{P}}(B>x)}=0\,.

Remark 3.15
.

We observe that
{\mathbb{E}}[C]=1+{\mathbb{E}}[C]\,{\mathbb{E}}[A]
and

{\mathbb{E}}[C^{2}]=1+{\mathbb{E}}[A^{2}]\,{\mathbb{E}}[C^{2}]+2\,{\mathbb{E}}[A]\,{\mathbb{E}}[C]
. Hence

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[C]=\dfrac{1}{1-{\mathbb{E}}[A]}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad{\mathbb{E}}[C^{2}]=\dfrac{1}{1-{\mathbb{E}}[A^{2}]}\,\dfrac{1+{\mathbb{E}}[A]}{1-{\mathbb{E}}[A]}\,.

Corollary 3.16
.

Assume the conditions of Proposition
3.14
on the
solution
(X_{t})
to the stochastic recurrence equation (
3.11
)
and
the Kesten-Goldie conditions for the unique solution to the
stochastic recurrence equation
C_{t}=A_{t}\,C_{t-1}+1
,
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}
: there exists
\beta>2
such that
{\mathbb{E}}[A^{\beta}]=1
,

{\mathbb{E}}[A^{\beta}\,\log A]<\infty
, and the law of
\log A
conditioned on
\{A>0\}
is non-arithmetic.
Then the following large deviation relation holds for every
\delta>0
:

(3.18)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{x\geq n^{0.5+\delta}}\Big|\dfrac{{\mathbb{P}}(|S_{n}-{\mathbb{E}}S_{n}|>x)}{n\,{\mathbb{P}}(X>x)}-{\dfrac{1+{\mathbb{E}}[A]}{1-{\mathbb{E}}[A]}}\Big|=0\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,.

Remark 3.17
.

The Kesten-Goldie conditions ensure that
(C_{t})
is strictly stationary and regularly varying with index
\beta>0
. In particular,

{\mathbb{P}}(C>x)\sim c\,x^{-\beta}
as
x\to\infty
where

\displaystyle c=\dfrac{{\mathbb{E}}\big[(1+C\,A)^{\beta}-(C\,A)^{\beta}\big]}{\beta\,{\mathbb{E}}[A^{\beta}\,\log A]}\,.

Proof.

By (5.6.11) in
[
Mikosch and Wintenberger (2024)
]
,
X
and
B
are tail-equivalent, i.e.,

(3.19)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(X>x)\sim{\mathbb{P}}(B>x)/{(1-{\mathbb{E}}[A^{2}])}\,,\qquad x\to\infty\,.

Therefore it suffices to check the conditions of Proposition
3.14
. We choose

x_{n}:=n^{0.5+\delta}
. Since
B
is regularly varying with index
\alpha=2
we have

n\,{\mathbb{P}}(B>n^{0.5+\delta})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Lemma
3.13
(Theorem 2.1 in
[
Buraczewski et al. (2013)
]
) yields a large deviation result
in the Kesten-Goldie case for the solution to the stochastic recurrence equation
C_{t}=A_{t}\,C_{t-1}+1
:
for every
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{x\geq n^{0.5+\delta}}\dfrac{{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\Big|\sum_{t=1}^{n}(C_{t}-{\mathbb{E}}[C])\Big|>x\Big)}{n\,{\mathbb{P}}(C>x)}<\infty.

Since
B
and
C
are regularly varying with indices
2
and
\beta
, respectively, and
2<\beta
,
we have
\lim_{x\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(C>x)/{\mathbb{P}}(B>x)=0
, hence

(3.20)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{x\geq n^{0.5+\delta}}\dfrac{{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\Big|\sum_{t=1}^{n}(C_{t}-{\mathbb{E}}[C])\Big|>x\Big)}{n\,{\mathbb{P}}(B>x)}=0\,.

This proves that the second part in (
3.14
) vanishes.

Next we bound the first part
in (
3.14
). Choose
\gamma\in(2,\beta)
. By Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{n}C_{t}^{2}\Big)^{\gamma/2}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{\gamma/2}\,{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{n}C_{t}^{2}/n\Big)^{\gamma/2}\Big]\leq n^{\gamma/2}\,{\mathbb{E}}\big[C^{\gamma}\big]\,.

Then, also observing that
{\mathbb{P}}(B>x)=x^{-2}L_{0}(x)
for some
slowly varying
L_{0}
, an application of Markov’s inequality yields for
x\geq x_{n}=n^{0.5+\delta}
,

\displaystyle\dfrac{{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{n}C_{t}^{2}\Big)^{0.5}>x/(\sqrt{\log x}\,\sigma_{x})\Big)}{n\,{\mathbb{P}}(B>x)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c\,(n/x^{2})^{\gamma/2-1}\,\dfrac{(\sqrt{\log x}\,\sigma_{x})^{\gamma}}{L_{0}(x)}\leq c\,n^{-\delta\,(\gamma/2-1)}\,,

where in the last step we also applied the Potter bounds (see
[
Bingham et al. (1987)
]
)
to the slowly varying function
(\sqrt{\log x}\,\sigma_{x})^{\gamma}/L_{0}(x)
.

Since the right-hand side converges to zero as
n\to\infty
, (
3.14
) holds.
The conditions of Proposition
3.14
are satisfied and the corollary follows.
∎

Now we turn to the central limit theorem in the borderline case when

X
is regularly varying with index
\alpha=2
and
{\rm var}(X)=\infty
. Since
X
and
B
are tail-equivalent (see (
3.19
)) this means that
B
is regularly varying with index

\alpha=2
and
{\rm var}(B)=\infty
.

Proposition 3.18
.

Assume the conditions of Corollary
3.16
on the stationary solution
(X_{t})
to the stochastic recurrence equation (
3.11
)
in the case
\alpha=2
,
{\rm var}(B)=\infty
, that
(X_{t})
is strongly mixing
with geometric rate and such that we can choose the block length
r_{n}
so small
that
(n/a_{n}^{2})\,r_{n}^{\delta}=o(1)
as
n\to\infty
for some
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Then the central limit theorem
a_{n}^{-1}(S_{n}-d_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,c_{0})
holds where

(a_{n})
is defined in (
1.4
),
d_{n}=n{\mathbb{E}}[X]=n\,{\mathbb{E}}[B]/(1-{\mathbb{E}}[A])
and
c_{0}=(1+{\mathbb{E}}[A])/(1-{\mathbb{E}}[A])
.

Remark 3.19
.

Since
{\rm var}(B)=\infty
the sequence
(a_{n})
satisfies
a_{n}=\sqrt{n}\ell(n)
for a slowly varying function
\ell(n)\to\infty
. Therefore the growth condition on
(r_{n})
means that it must be chosen such that
r_{n}^{\delta}/\ell^{2}(n)\to 0
for an arbitrarily small
\delta>0
.
The required relation

(n/a_{n}^{2})r_{n}^{\delta}=r_{n}^{\delta}/\ell^{2}(n)=o(1)
as
n\to\infty

between the block lengths
r_{n}
and the normalizing constants
a_{n}
is delicate.
For example, for a Pareto-like tail
{\mathbb{P}}(B>x)\sim c_{1}\,x^{-2}
as
x\to\infty
for some
c_{1}>0

we have
K(x)={\mathbb{E}}[X^{2}{\mathbf{1}}(|X|\leq x)]\sim c_{2}\,\log x
as
x\to\infty
for some constant
c_{2}>0
, hence

\ell^{2}(n)\sim(c_{2}\,\log n)/2
. Then, choosing
r_{n}\sim(\log n)^{(1-\varepsilon)/\delta}
for small
\varepsilon>0
, the condition
r_{n}^{\delta}/\ell^{2}(n)=o(1)
in Proposition
3.18
is satisfied, while the mixing condition (
1.6
) is kept.

Proof.

Fix
\delta>0
such that
r_{n}^{\delta}/\ell^{2}(n)\to 0
. Then the condition

\varepsilon a_{n}>r_{n}^{(1+\delta)/2}
is trivially satisfied and we can apply
(
3.18
):

(3.21)

\displaystyle k_{n}\,{\mathbb{P}}\big(|S_{r_{n}}-d_{r_{n}}|>a_{n}\big)\leq c\,n\,{\mathbb{P}}(X>a_{n})\to 0,\qquad n\to\infty\,.

The convergence to zero follows from the definition of
(a_{n})
in the case
\alpha=2
; see the comment after (
1.6
). Thus (
2.2
) holds.

Next we prove (
2.4
).
Since
{\mathbb{E}}[S_{r_{n}}-d_{r_{n}}]=0
it suffices to show that the following quantity vanishes as
n\to\infty
.

\displaystyle v_{n}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\dfrac{k_{n}}{a_{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[|S_{r_{n}}-d_{r_{n}}|\,{\mathbf{1}}\big(|S_{r_{n}}-d_{r_{n}}|>a_{n}\big)\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{k_{n}}{a_{n}}\int_{a_{n}}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\big(|S_{r_{n}}-d_{r_{n}}|>y\big)\,dy+k_{n}\,{\mathbb{P}}\big(|S_{r_{n}}-d_{r_{n}}|>a_{n}\big)\,,

where the second term vanishes as
n\to\infty
by virtue of (
3.21
). From
(
3.18
) and Karamata’s theorem we also obtain

\displaystyle v_{n}\leq c\,\dfrac{k_{n}}{a_{n}}\,r_{n}\int_{a_{n}}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(X>y)\,dy+o(1)\leq c\,n\,{\mathbb{P}}(X>a_{n})+o(1)\to 0\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,.

Finally, we prove (
2.3
). We have for every
\varepsilon>0
, by virtue of (
3.21
),

\displaystyle\dfrac{k_{n}}{a_{n}^{2}}\,{\mathbb{E}}\big[(S_{r_{n}}-d_{r_{n}})^{2}\,{\mathbf{1}}\big(|S_{r_{n}}-d_{r_{n}}|\leq\varepsilon\,a_{n}\big)\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{k_{n}}{a_{n}^{2}}\,\int_{0}^{(\varepsilon a_{n})^{2}}{\mathbb{P}}\big(|S_{r_{n}}-d_{r_{n}}|>\sqrt{y}\big)\,dy{-}k_{n}\,\varepsilon^{2}\,{\mathbb{P}}\big(|S_{r_{n}}-d_{r_{n}}|>\varepsilon\,a_{n}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{k_{n}}{a_{n}^{2}}\Big(\int_{0}^{r_{n}^{1+\delta}}+\int_{r_{n}^{1+\delta}}^{(\varepsilon a_{n})^{2}}\Big){\mathbb{P}}\big(|S_{r_{n}}-d_{r_{n}}|>\sqrt{y}\big)\,dy+o(1)

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle I_{1}+I_{2}+o(1)\,.

By assumption
we have chosen
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle I_{1}\leq\dfrac{k_{n}}{a_{n}^{2}}r_{n}^{1+\delta}\leq\dfrac{r_{n}^{\delta}}{\ell^{2}(n)}\to 0\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,.

Finally, we bound
I_{2}
by an application of (
3.18
)

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\dfrac{n}{a_{n}^{2}}\,c_{0}\,\int_{r_{n}^{1+\delta}}^{(\varepsilon a_{n})^{2}}{\mathbb{P}}(X>\sqrt{y})\,dy\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,.

We also observe that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r_{n}^{1+\delta}}{\mathbb{P}}\big(|X|>\sqrt{y}\big)\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[X^{2}{\mathbf{1}}({|X|}\leq r_{n}^{(1+\delta)/2})\big]+o(1)\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,.

The right-hand function is slowly varying with argument
r_{n}^{(1+\delta)/2}
. Hence it is
O(r_{n}^{\gamma})
for arbitrarily small
\gamma>0
.
Thus by definition of
(a_{n})
,

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{0}\,\dfrac{n}{a_{n}^{2}}\,\int_{0}^{(\varepsilon a_{n})^{2}}{\mathbb{P}}(X>\sqrt{y})\,dy+o(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{0}\,\dfrac{n}{a_{n}^{2}}\,{\mathbb{E}}[X^{2}{\mathbf{1}}({|X|}\leq\varepsilon a_{n})]+o(1)\to c_{0}\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,.

This finishes the proof.
∎

Appendix A

Lemma A.1
.

Assume that the regularly varying stationary centered sequence
(X_{t})
with index
\alpha=2
satisfies the relation

(A.22)

\displaystyle\dfrac{X_{1}^{2}+\cdots+X_{n}^{2}}{a_{n}^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\rightarrow}}\sigma^{2}\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,,

for some sequence
a_{n}=\sqrt{n}\,\ell(n)
, a slowly varying function
\ell
and a constant
\sigma>0
.
Then the following relation is equivalent to (
A.22
):

(A.23)

\displaystyle\dfrac{X_{1}N_{1}+\cdots+X_{n}N_{n}}{a_{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,\sigma^{2})\,,\qquad n\to\infty\,,

where
(N_{t})
are iid standard normal random variables independent of
(X_{t})
.

Proof.

For any
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[\exp\big(-0.5\lambda^{2}\,(X_{1}^{2}+\cdots+X_{n}^{2})/a_{n}^{2}\big)\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[\exp\big(i\,\lambda\,(X_{1}N_{1}+\cdots+X_{n}N_{n})/a_{n}\big)\big]\,.

Under (
A.22
) (or (
A.23
)) we have convergence of the left- and right-hand
expressions to

\exp(-0.5(\sigma\,\lambda)^{2})
. Therefore (
A.22
) and (
A.23
)
are equivalent.
∎

Appendix B
An example of an infinite variance iid sequence which satisfies the central limit theorem but is not regularly varying

We start by recalling a classical result; see
[
Petrov (1995)
, Section 4.1, Theorem 4.1]
.

Theorem B.1
.

Let
(X_{t})
be a real-valued iid sequence and
(a_{n})
be real numbers such that
a_{n}\to\infty
.
Then

S_{n}/a_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,\sigma^{2})
for some
\sigma>0
if and only if
the following three conditions are satisfied for every
\varepsilon>0
: as
n\to\infty
,

(B.1)

\displaystyle n\,{\mathbb{P}}\big(|X/a_{n}|>\varepsilon\big)

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle 0\,,

(B.2)

\displaystyle n\,{\rm var}\big((X/a_{n})\,{\mathbf{1}}(|X/a_{n}|\leq\varepsilon)\big)

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\,,

(B.3)

\displaystyle n\,{\mathbb{E}}\big[(X/a_{n})\,{\mathbf{1}}(|X/a_{n}|\leq\varepsilon)\big]

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle 0\,.

We will apply this result to the following example.

Example B.2
.

We modify the example in Remark 5 of
[
Damek et al. (2014)
]
, cf. also

[
Jacobsen et al. (2009)
]
.
Choose constants
\theta_{0}\neq 0
,
a
and
b
such that
0<a^{2}+b^{2}\leq 1
.
Consider a symmetric random variable
X
with density, for some
r>0
,

\displaystyle f(x)=c_{r}\,|x|^{-3}\,\big(1+a\,\cos(\theta_{0}\log|x|)+b\,\sin(\theta_{0}\log|x|)\big)\,{\mathbf{1}}(|x|>r)\,,\qquad c_{r}^{-1}={\mathbb{P}}(|X|>r)\,.

First we observe that
{\mathbb{P}}(X>x)
is not regularly varying. For
y>r
we have

(B.4)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(X>y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c_{r}}{2}\int_{0}^{y^{-2}}\,\big(1+a\,\cos\big((\theta_{0}\,\log z)/2\big)-b\,\sin\big((\theta_{0}\log z)/2\big)\big)dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c_{r}}{2}\Big[z+C\,z\,\cos\big((\theta_{0}\,\log z)/2\big)-D\,z\,\sin\big((\theta_{0}\,\log z)/2\big)\Big]^{y^{-2}}_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c_{r}}{2}y^{-2}\,\big(1+C\,\cos(\theta_{0}\,\log y)+D\,\sin(\theta_{0}\,\log y)\big)=:\frac{c_{r}}{2}\,y^{-2}\,N(y)\,.

where
C:=(a+\theta_{0}b/2)/(1+\theta_{0}^{2}/4),D:=(b-\theta_{0}\,a/2)/(1+\theta_{0}^{2}/4)
are obtained by differentiating
the quantity in brackets in (
B.4
) with respect to
z
and comparing the coefficients of trigonometric functions.
In particular,

0<C^{2}+D^{2}=(a^{2}+b^{2})/(1+\theta_{0}^{2}/4)\leq 1
. The function
N(x)
is not
slowly varying.

Next we verify the slow variation of
K(x)
. For
x>r
we have

\displaystyle K(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2{\mathbb{E}}[X^{2}{\mathbf{1}}(0\leq X\leq x)]=2\,\Big(\int_{r^{2}}^{x^{2}}{\mathbb{P}}(X>\sqrt{y})dy-x^{2}\,{\mathbb{P}}(X>x)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{r}\int_{r^{2}}^{x^{2}}y^{-1}N(\sqrt{y})\,dy-c_{r}\,N(x)\,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,c_{r}\log x+c_{r}\,\big(2C/\theta_{0}-D\big)\,\sin(\theta_{0}\log x)-c_{r}\,\big(2D/\theta_{0}+C\big)\cos(\theta_{0}\log x)+c

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle 2\,c_{r}\,\log x\,,\qquad x\to\infty\,.

We choose the normalizing constants
(a_{n})
according to (
1.4
). Then
it is immediate that
a_{n}=\sqrt{n}\ell(n)
for a slowly varying function
\ell(n)\to\infty

as
n\to\infty
and for every
\varepsilon>0
,
n\,{\mathbb{P}}(|X|>\varepsilon\,a_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
Then (
B.1
), (
B.3
) are satisfied and (
B.2
) holds with

\sigma^{2}=1
. An application of Theorem
B.1
yields the central limit theorem
S_{n}/a_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,1)
as
n\to\infty
.
