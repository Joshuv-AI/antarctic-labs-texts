Feasibility of routine clinical liquid‐based cytology for lung cancer compact panel testing
Authors: Yoshiki Shinomiya, Kanako C Hatanaka, Asami Okumura, Ayae Nange, Mamiko Yamamoto, Manabu Azuma, Katsuji Marukawa, Yuko Ishida, Tomohiro Shimizu, Ryoko Watanabe, Yamato Hashimoto, Mitsuharu Abiko, Yoshiharu Sato, Jun Sakakibara‐Konishi, Tatsuya Kato, Shinya Tanaka, Yutaka Hatanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Lung Cancer Compact Panel (cPANEL) is a recently approved highly sensitive multiplex gene panel in Japan that supports both DNA‐ and RNA‐based next‐generation sequencing. Although cytological specimens are acceptable for cPANEL, unfixed cell pellets or dedicated preservation tubes are typically recommended. However, evidence remains limited regarding whether residual liquid‐based cytology (LBC) cell suspensions prepared for routine cytological diagnosis can be used directly for cPANEL testing without dedicated molecular preservation or additional preanalytical processing. In this study, we evaluated the feasibility of applying LBC specimens that are widely used in contemporary clinical practice to cPANEL.MethodsWe analyzed DNA and RNA quality in 69 clinical LBC specimens. Among these, 51 specimens containing non‐small cell lung cancer cells with previously determined driver alteration status were subjected to cPANEL testing to evaluate assay concordance with clinical companion diagnostic results.ResultsDNA integrity was generally well preserved (DNA Integrity Number [DIN]: 6.2 ± 1.5). In contrast, RNA integrity showed greater variability (DV200: 16.4 ± 12.1%). ThinPrep‐fixed specimens demonstrated lower DIN and DV200 values compared with CytoRich Red‐fixed specimens. Although all samples successfully passed the DNA‐based cPANEL assay, six cases (11.8%) failed the RNA‐based assay, with RNA yield being a major contributing factor. Among the 46 evaluable specimens, concordance was 95.7% and sensitivity was 92.3%, or 88.9% including RNA module failures as cPANEL‐negative.ConclusionsWith appropriate fixative selection and adequate cellularity, cPANEL using clinical LBC specimens may serve as a practical diagnostic platform. We demonstrated that routine LBC specimens can be directly applied to cPANEL without special preanalytical processing.Keywords: cytology, companion diagnostics, non‐small cell lung cancer, nucleic acid integrity, next‐generation sequencing

INTRODUCTION

In recent years, the expansion of precision oncology has led to the development of numerous molecular targeted agents across a wide range of malignancies. In lung cancer, approximately 50% of patients are reported to harbor actionable driver alterations, making biomarker testing an essential component of clinical management. 1 , 2 Accordingly, most patients undergo companion diagnostic (CDx) testing to determine eligibility for targeted therapies. These assessments are typically performed on tumor specimens using next‐generation sequencing (NGS), polymerase chain reaction, or immunohistochemistry.

In Japan, the Lung Cancer Compact Panel (cPANEL; DNA Chip Research Inc., Tokyo, Japan), a gene panel test that uses both DNA and RNA, was approved for insurance reimbursement in 2023. In this assay, EGFR mutations, BRAF V600E, and KRAS G12C are detected by the DNA module, whereas ALK, ROS1, and RET fusions and MET exon 14 skipping are detected by the RNA module. This high‐sensitivity multiplex NGS assay covers most known driver alterations in lung cancer and requires only minimal input material. Given the increasing number of molecular target drugs in lung cancer, the ability to perform highly sensitive and multiplex testing with small sample volumes is of significant clinical benefit. Several studies have demonstrated the high analytical performance of this panel, 3 , 4 including its potential use with cytological specimens. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Accordingly, its clinical application is expected to expand.

Cytological specimens can be prepared in various forms, including conventional smears, formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded cell blocks, and liquid‐based cytology (LBC), in which cells are fixed in suspension before slide preparation. LBC was introduced in Japan in the early 2000s for gynecologic applications and has since gained widespread use in nongynecologic fields. 9 LBC offers several advantages, including improved sample adequacy, 10 , 11 enrichment of tumor cells on glass slides, 12 and the ability to preserve residual cell suspensions while maintaining cellular morphology. 13 These LBC suspensions are not only suitable for additional cytopathological preparations but also allow for preservation of both antigenicity and nucleic acids, especially DNA. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Nucleic acid stability is particularly important when molecular testing is performed after cytological diagnosis.

Although several studies have examined nucleic acid quality and utility for NGS in LBC specimens, many of these have been based on cultured cells, 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 rather than the complexity of clinical samples. Although DNA quality in clinical settings has been relatively well characterized, 20 , 21 , 22 data on RNA integrity remain limited. With the emergence of both DNA‐ and RNA‐based NGS tests, it is increasingly important to evaluate the actual quality of both DNA and RNA in routine cytological specimens. Furthermore, although cPANEL generally recommends unfixed frozen cell pellets or preparation using a GM tube for cytological specimens, 5 this approach may be difficult to incorporate into routine workflows in clinical laboratories. The ability to use LBC specimens without modifying existing preparation protocols would represent a practical and scalable solution for cytopathology laboratories.

In this study, we assessed the quality of nucleic acids extracted from LBC specimens collected in routine clinical practice, while also evaluating the feasibility of directly applying cPANEL to these specimens, focusing on assay success and concordance with reference CDx results.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample selection and preparation

A total of 69 LBC specimens were collected at Hokkaido University Hospital between 2021 and 2024. The specimens included bronchial brush/forceps washings, needle washings, and pleural fluid samples. Residual materials from routine cytological examinations were used for this study. LBC samples were prepared by fixing the specimens using either ThinPrep (TP; Hologic, Inc., Marlborough, MA, USA) or CytoRich Red (CR‐R; Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) for a defined period at room temperature before processing. The two fixatives were used randomly across specimens, and fixation was performed by research personnel blinded to the clinical background of each case at the time of specimen processing. A portion of the cell suspension was used to prepare smears for Papanicolaou staining, whereas the remainder was allocated for nucleic acid extraction. No special preanalytical processing for molecular testing, such as immediate freezing as a cell pellet or transfer to a nucleic acid stabilization tube, was performed before routine cytological evaluation and allocation for nucleic acid extraction.

Nucleic acid extraction and quality check

Nucleic acids were extracted separately using the Maxwell RSC Blood DNA Kit (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA) for DNA and the Maxwell RSC simplyRNA Cells Kit (Promega Corporation) for RNA, according to the manufacturer's instructions. The quality of DNA and RNA was assessed using the TapeStation system (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) by measuring the DNA Integrity Number (DIN) and the DV200 value, respectively. Quantification of nucleic acids was performed using Qubit fluorometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA).

Implementation of the lung cancer compact panel test

Of the 69 LBC specimens collected, 51 specimens from 50 cases were selected for cPANEL analysis. These specimens were obtained from cases that were cytologically positive on Pap‐stained LBC smears. Among these cases, only those in which the driver alteration status had been determined by CDx in routine clinical practice were included in the analysis. The results of the cPANEL assay were evaluated in samples in which the assay was successful by comparison with the known driver alterations to assess the feasibility and clinical concordance of using LBC specimens. An overview of the study workflow, including the specimen selection process for cPANEL analysis, is shown in Figure 1. Cytological results were considered positive when any findings suggestive of malignancy, including atypical or malignant cells, were reported, and negative when “no evidence of malignancy” was documented in clinical practice. Samples with RNA module assay failure and no driver alteration detected by the cPANEL DNA module were excluded from the concordance analysis because the presence or absence of RNA‐targeted alterations could not be evaluated.

Statistical analysis

The mean ± SD was used to present experimental data. The Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was used to compare distributions between two groups. For comparisons among three or more groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test with Bonferroni correction when appropriate. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed using linear regression, as appropriate. For analyses based on predefined candidate thresholds, proportions were calculated with exact binomial 95% CIs, and Fisher’s exact test was used to compare groups, as appropriate. A p value < .05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using R software (version 4.3.3).

RESULTS

Nucleic acid quality in liquid‐based cytology specimens

Table 1 summarizes the sample cohort used in this study for the evaluation of nucleic acid quality. Among the 69 LBC specimens collected, all but three were cytologically positive. The specimen types included bronchial brush/forceps washings (15 samples, 21.7%), needle washings (41 samples, 59.4%), and pleural fluid (13 samples, 18.8%). Two different LBC preservation solutions with distinct chemical compositions were used randomly across specimens: CR‐R was used in 36 samples (52.2%) and TP in 33 samples (47.8%). The average storage duration in the preservation solution was 10.48 ± 2.77 days. A detailed summary of specimen types, preservation solutions, and storage durations is shown in Table S1. The yield of extracted DNA was 1975.3 ± 1945.9 ng, and the DIN was 6.2 ± 1.5. For RNA, the yield was 467.8 ± 973.0 ng, and DV200 was 16.4 ± 12.1% (Table S2). By specimen type, pleural fluid samples yielded significantly more DNA compared with needle washings (p = .0015) and brush/forceps washings (p = .0013) (Figure 2A), although there were no significant differences in DIN across specimen types (Figure 2B). No significant difference in DNA yield was observed between the two preservation solutions (Figure 2C); however, DIN was significantly higher in CR‐R–preserved samples compared to TP (p < .001) (Figure 2D). Regarding RNA, pleural fluid samples had the highest yields (vs. needle washings, p = .0105; vs. brush/forceps washings, p < .001), followed by needle washings (vs. brush/forceps washings, p = .0273) (Figure 2E). DV200 was significantly lower in pleural fluid than in the other specimen types (vs brush/forceps washings, p = .0272; vs needle washings, p = .0070) (Figure 2F). RNA yield was also significantly higher in CR‐R–preserved samples compared with TP (p < .001) (Figure 2G). In terms of RNA quality, DV200 was significantly higher in CR‐R–preserved samples than in TP (p < .001) (Figure 2H). To further investigate potential factors affecting DNA and RNA yield and quality, univariate and multivariate analyses were performed. Among the tested variables, multivariate linear regression analysis identified pleural fluid specimens as being independently associated with higher dsDNA yield (p < .001). A similar association was observed for RNA yield (p < .001). In contrast, the use of TP as a fixative was independently associated with lower RNA yield (p = .02). Regarding nucleic acid quality, TP fixation was independently associated with decreased DIN (p < .001) and reduced DV200 values (p < .001). Needle wash specimens were independently associated with lower DIN (p = .03), whereas pleural fluid specimens were independently associated with reduced DV200 values (p < .001). Within the relatively narrow storage window examined in this study, storage duration in LBC fixative was not independently associated with any of the evaluated outcomes (Table 2).

Feasibility and performance of cPANEL testing in LBC samples

A summary of the 51 samples that underwent cPANEL, including driver alteration status, is shown in Table 3. Of the 51 samples tested using cPANEL, six failed the RNA module assay. No samples failed the DNA module assay. Of the six samples that failed the RNA module assay, five with negative DNA module results were excluded from the LBC‐based cPANEL performance analysis (Figure 1), yielding 46 evaluable samples. Notably, one of these five excluded samples was a clinically ALK fusion–positive case. Overall, cPANEL detected clinically actionable driver alterations relevant to molecular targeted therapy in 21 cases, excluding non‐G12C KRAS mutations (Table S3).

In this study, clinical CDx results obtained from adequate specimens were used as the reference standard for driver alteration status in each case. Of the 46 samples analyzed, cPANEL yielded false‐negative results in two cases: one case with EGFR L858R and another with MET exon 14 skipping. No cases with driver or variant mismatches were observed (Figure 3A, Table 4). Based on these results, among the 46 evaluable samples, the concordance between cPANEL using LBC‐fixed specimens and clinically detected driver alteration status was 95.7% (95% CI, 85.2–99.5), with a sensitivity of 92.3%, specificity of 100%, positive predictive value of 100%, and negative predictive value of 90.9%. When the five samples initially excluded from the per‐protocol analysis, all of which had RNA module failure and no driver alteration detected by the DNA module, were included in an intention‐to‐test analysis, sensitivity decreased from 92.3% (24/26) to 88.9% (24/27). Among the full cPANEL‐tested cohort (n = 51), seven samples harbored driver alterations requiring RNA module analysis, including ALK fusion, ROS1 fusion, and MET exon 14 skipping. cPANEL detected five of these seven alterations, corresponding to a detection rate of 71.4%. The two undetected RNA module‐dependent alterations consisted of one ALK fusion that was not detected because of RNA module failure and one MET exon 14 skipping false‐negative case.

In two false‐negative cases, DIN values were 5.9 and 7.5, which were not particularly low. However, DV200 values were relatively low (3.6% and 10%) relative to the overall mean. The fixatives used were TP in one case and CR‐R in the other. The storage duration in LBC fixative for both samples was close to the average (10.33 days). Furthermore, both were LBC specimens prepared from pleural fluid, and the cytological assessment was either indeterminate or suspected malignant (Table S3). Observation of the Pap‐stained LBC specimens revealed marked inflammation in both cases, with only a very small number of atypical cells (Figure 3B, 3C). The tumor content (TC) of the false‐negative cases and cases with RNA module assay failure is summarized in Supplementary Table S4.

cPANEL test success and factors associated with RNA module failure

The cPANEL RNA module assay failed in six of 51 specimens (11.8%). All six failed cases were TP‐fixed samples. To explore the factors contributing to RNA module failure, we compared RNA yield and DV200 values between successful and failed cases, stratified by preservation method. To investigate factors associated with RNA module success or failure, RNA yield and DV200 values were compared among three groups: CR‐R (success), TP (success), and TP (failure). As shown in Figure 3D, RNA yield was significantly lower in the TP failure group compared to both the CR‐R and TP success groups (p = .0016 and p = .045, respectively). However, DV200 values were not consistently lower in the failure group than in the success groups (Figure 3E). An exploratory threshold analysis further supported the importance of RNA yield for RNA module success (Table S5). Samples with RNA yield ≥50 ng showed a success rate of 97.4% (37/38; 95% CI, 86.2–99.9), and all samples with RNA yield ≥100 ng were successfully analyzed (31/31; 100.0%; 95% CI, 88.8–100.0). In contrast, DV200 showed a less consistent association with RNA module success.

DISCUSSION

In the present study, we comprehensively evaluated nucleic acid integrity and the driver alteration detection performance of cPANEL using clinical LBC specimens and investigated the clinical feasibility of directly applying LBC specimens to cPANEL in lung cancer.

The major novelty of this study is not the analytical validation of cPANEL itself, but the demonstration that residual LBC material, after being used for routine cytomorphologic diagnosis, can be assessed for both nucleic acid quality and multiplex CDx performance within the same clinical workflow.

DNA integrity was generally well preserved in LBC specimens. Although two samples were not evaluable, the mean DIN was approximately 6.3, substantially exceeding the commonly accepted threshold of 2.5 required for library preparation. 23 Significantly higher DNA yields were obtained from pleural effusion specimens; however, sufficient DNA was also recovered from needle/brush/forceps washings, and all samples successfully passed the DNA module assay. In contrast, RNA quality showed a clear tendency toward degradation. The DV200 value fell below the recommended 30% threshold for RNA library preparation in the majority of cases. This finding contrasts with previous reports describing high RNA quality in cytology specimens. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 However, many of those studies were conducted using cultured cells under strictly controlled conditions and are therefore not directly comparable to real‐world clinical samples. In routine clinical workflows, multiple preanalytical variables, including sampling procedures, transport time, delays before fixation, fixative composition, storage conditions, and extraction procedures, may contribute to RNA degradation, similar to tissue specimens. 24 , 25 Indeed, other studies evaluating RNA quality in clinical cytology specimens have also reported decreased RNA integrity and marked variability. 5 Furthermore, pleural effusion specimens, which primarily consist of exfoliated cells, demonstrated significantly lower RNA quality than washings from needles, brushes, and forceps derived from freshly collected cells. These findings may reflect the influence of multiple pre‐analytical factors on RNA integrity in clinical cytology specimens, although the specific phase responsible for RNA degradation could not be determined in this study.

Despite the reduced RNA integrity observed in clinical LBC specimens, RNA‐based cPANEL testing was successful in the majority of cases. Specifically, among the 51 specimens analyzed with cPANEL, only six failed the RNA module assay. Notably, RNA yield showed a stronger association with assay failure than DV200 values, underscoring the importance of adequate cellular input for successful RNA‐based testing. Although the number of failed cases was limited, the exploratory threshold analysis also supported the importance of RNA yield for RNA module success. High success rates were observed when RNA yield exceeded approximately 50 to 100 ng, suggesting that RNA yield may serve as a practical indicator for successful RNA‐module analysis. These findings indicate that, in addition to nucleic acid integrity, sufficient cellularity is a critical determinant for successful cPANEL assay performance using clinical LBC specimens. The fixation process itself—particularly the type of fixative used—also had a substantial impact on nucleic acid preservation. CR‐R consistently showed better DNA and RNA preservation than TP, suggesting that fixative composition plays an important role in maintaining nucleic acid integrity in LBC specimens. In particular, for RNA, the presence of a small amount of formalin in the fixative may have helped suppress RNase activity and thereby contributed to RNA preservation. 26 Although LBC fixative‐dependent differences in nucleic acid quality have previously been reported, 27 the associations between storage duration, specimen type, collection method, and nucleic acid quality may vary according to the fixative used, and these interrelated factors may have influenced the observed differences, warranting further stratified investigation. Importantly, the failure pattern was not explained solely by DV200, suggesting that biochemical quality indices cannot fully substitute for cytological assessment of cellularity. This point is particularly relevant for cytopathology practice, where molecular test selection and interpretation should be guided by direct review of the corresponding cytology preparation whenever possible.

Although most of the LBC specimens used for cPANEL testing were not identical to the clinical CDx specimens and the sampling sites and time points were not necessarily the same, cPANEL showed a concordance of 95.7% with clinically detected driver alteration status in successfully analyzed specimens (n = 46), with a sensitivity of 92.3%. However, clinical feasibility depends not only on detection performance in successfully analyzed specimens but also on assay success and specimen suitability. Indeed, one clinically detected driver alteration was not detected because of RNA module assay failure, and when this case was included in the analysis, the sensitivity decreased to 88.9%. Nevertheless, the high concordance observed among successfully analyzed specimens suggests that LBC‐based cPANEL testing remains clinically feasible when assay success and specimen adequacy are appropriately considered. Among successfully analyzed specimens, the two false‐negative cases were both pleural effusion specimens and may not be fully explained by a single common factor. One case involved undetected MET exon 14 skipping, an RNA module–targeted alteration. Although the RNA yield was sufficient, the DV200 value was markedly low at 3.6%. Therefore, substantial RNA degradation may have contributed to the false‐negative result. The other case involved undetected EGFR L858R, a DNA module–targeted variant. In this case, both nucleic acid yield and DNA quality were sufficient. However, the specimen contained abundant inflammatory cells and only a very small number of tumor cells, with a visually estimated TC of approximately 0.7%. Even with a highly sensitive assay such as cPANEL (limit of detection for EGFR mutations: 0.10–0.54% variant allele frequency), 3 , 28 driver mutations may be undetectable when tumor fraction is insufficient, as previously reported. 7 Furthermore, it should be noted that the specimens used for clinical CDx testing and those used for cPANEL testing in the present study were not necessarily obtained from the same site or at the same time point. Therefore, in addition to low‐TC, tumor heterogeneity may also have contributed to the observed results. 29 , 30 Collectively, these findings emphasize that tumor fraction, as well as nucleic acid quality, remain key determinants of diagnostic performance. These cytologic observations indicate that LBC‐based molecular testing should be triaged with attention to tumor‐cell adequacy on the corresponding Pap‐stained slide or cell‐rich preparation. Thus, LBC provides an opportunity to integrate morphology‐based adequacy assessment with molecular testing, rather than treating the molecular specimen as independent from the diagnostic cytology specimen. On the other hand, accurate assessment of TC in cytology specimens can be more challenging than in tissue specimens. This is partly because, depending on the specimen type, such as body cavity fluids, tumor cells may be admixed with a complex background of nonneoplastic and inflammatory cells. A key issue is that the TC of the cytology slide used for assessment may not necessarily correspond to that of the residual specimen used for NGS testing, which represents an additional source of uncertainty. Such a discrepancy may occur, for example, when the cell suspension is insufficiently mixed. This highlights the importance of careful handling and adequate mixing of the cell suspension during slide preparation. Moreover, unlike tissue specimens, a certain proportion of cells may be lost into the staining solution during the preparation of cytology slides, potentially altering the cellular composition observed on the slide and further increasing the uncertainty of TC assessment. Another important consideration is that the LBC systems used in the present study may relatively enrich tumor cells during slide preparation. 31 Although this represents a major advantage from the perspective of cytological screening, it may cause the TC estimated on the cytology slide to be higher than that in the residual specimen actually used for NGS testing.

The practical advantages of using LBC specimens for cPANEL, a highly sensitive multigene companion diagnostic assay for lung cancer, are clinically relevant. In cytological diagnosis, additional stained slides may be required when the initial cytological diagnosis is uncertain. However, if residual specimens are submitted as unfixed frozen cell pellets or preserved in nucleic acid stabilization solution (GM tubes), opportunities for additional staining may be lost. Conversely, delaying the transition to freezing or GM tube preservation may increase the risk of RNA degradation. Our study demonstrates that direct application of cPANEL to LBC specimens is feasible and provides a practical solution to this tradeoff in routine clinical practice.

In the present study, cPANEL testing was performed only on cases with available clinical CDx results. Therefore, the analyzed cohort was limited to cases in which molecular diagnosis using conventional clinical specimens had already been feasible. Accordingly, this cohort may not fully reflect patients with limited tissue availability or those in whom tissue rebiopsy is clinically difficult, and this potential selection bias should be considered when interpreting the results. On the other hand, cPANEL has been reported to be a highly sensitive panel with a low limit of detection. 3 , 4 , 7 , 8 In patients for whom tissue specimens are difficult to secure but cytology specimens containing tumor cells are available, LBC‐based cPANEL testing may reduce missed opportunities for molecular testing. Furthermore, efficient use of highly sensitive panels within the clinical workflow may contribute to avoiding delays in treatment initiation associated with rebiopsy and may also have implications for cost‐effectiveness. Future studies should therefore evaluate LBC‐based cPANEL testing not only in terms of detection performance, but also from the perspective of patient management.

Furthermore, several limitations should be acknowledged. This was a single‐institution study with a relatively small number of cPANEL‐tested samples, and the use of TP and CR‐R was not controlled prospectively. In addition, cases were selected based on the availability of prior clinical CDx results, and the minimum tumor–cell threshold required for reliable LBC‐based cPANEL testing could not be formally defined. Further prospective studies with standardized LBC processing and quantitative cytology‐based adequacy criteria are warranted.

Conclusion

Although clinical cytology specimens may exhibit nucleic acid degradation, particularly in RNA quality, our findings suggest that cPANEL analysis remains feasible in LBC‐fixed specimens provided that sufficient cellularity and tumor‐cell fraction are maintained.

The cPANEL approach enables the integration of cytomorphologic diagnosis and highly sensitive multiplex gene testing from the same LBC specimen, offering potential advantages in diagnostic flexibility and workflow efficiency. For molecular cytopathology, this workflow may help preserve the diagnostic value of LBC specimens while expanding their use as a clinically actionable substrate for multiplex companion diagnostics.
