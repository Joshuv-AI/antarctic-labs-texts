Comparative genomics of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors reveals early divergence of the α6 subunit in cryptic Anopheles species
Authors: Caroline Fouet, Desiree E. Rios, Fred A. Ashu, Matthew J. Pinch, Cesar A. Hernandez, Marilene M. Ambadiang, Basile Kamgang, Colince Kamdem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) are pentameric ligand-gated ion channels that mediate fast cholinergic transmission in the insect central nervous system and serve as targets for several widely used classes of insecticides. nAChR-targeting formulations are being deployed in malaria vector control programs to mitigate widespread resistance to pyrethroids. However, the extent of genetic variation at insecticide target sites that may facilitate evolutionary responses to chemical exposure among vector species remains poorly understood.ResultsHere, we used whole-genome and Sanger sequencing to examine amino acid substitutions across all 11 nAChR subunits in sibling species of the Anopheles gambiae complex in Africa. The gene family is highly constrained, with only 33 nonsynonymous mutations detected at very low frequency in wild populations throughout the continent. No substitutions were observed at canonical ligand-binding residues within orthosteric domains, suggesting that acetylcholine and neonicotinoid binding affinities are unlikely to differ between the cryptic species. However, despite the evolutionary constraint, we identified two linked amino acid substitutions in the α6 subunit indicating early divergence of the receptor between species.ConclusionsThese findings show that relaxed constraint can enable subtle amino acid changes in specific subunits of a conserved receptor family. The role of these mutations in shaping heterogeneous responses to insecticide exposure among mosquito species warrants further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12994-3.

Background

Nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) are pentameric ligand-gated ion channels that mediate fast cholinergic neurotransmission in the central nervous system and belong to the Cys-loop superfamily [1]. Activation of nAChRs by the neurotransmitter acetylcholine induces rapid depolarization of postsynaptic neurons and is essential for synaptic transmission, sensory processing, and motor control [2, 3]. In insects, nAChRs are encoded by a small multigene family comprising 10–12 subunits, whose composition and sequence divergence generate substantial functional diversity [4–6]. Because nAChRs are the primary molecular targets of neonicotinoids, as well as related insecticide classes such as spinosyns and butenolides, extensive efforts have focused on identifying the structural determinants underlying insecticide-receptor interactions [7–9].

Synthetic insecticides can act either as partial or full agonists at the orthosteric binding site of nAChRs or as allosteric modulators that regulate receptor function indirectly [7, 8]. Although imidacloprid can act as an antagonist, neonicotinoids typically function as agonists at the orthosteric binding site, located at the extracellular interface between adjacent subunits of the mature pentameric receptor [7, 8, 10]. This site is defined by structural regions known as loops that form the ligand-binding pocket. Insect nAChRs assemble as either homopentamers composed of α subunits or heteropentamers containing both α and β subunits. In both cases, the ligand-binding pocket is formed through interactions among seven loops (A–G) contributed by adjacent subunits [3, 11, 12]. Site-directed mutagenesis, electrophysiology, and molecular modeling have identified conserved basic residues within these loops as major determinants of neonicotinoid and acetylcholine affinity [13–15]. A prerequisite for nAChR–neonicotinoid binding is the formation of electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonds between functional groups characteristic of neonicotinoid molecules and amino acid residue within the binding loops [16–18]. In contrast, spinosyns act as allosteric modulators that interact with residues located at the interface between transmembrane helices TM2 and TM3 [9, 19, 20].

Neonicotinoid sensitivity varies remarkably among animal species. Insects are highly susceptible, whereas mammals and ticks generally display lower sensitivity [8, 21, 22]. This differential toxicity is associated with amino acid substitutions at canonical ligand-binding residues that reduce receptor affinity for active ingredients and diminish sensitivity [16, 21, 23]. Although not to the magnitude observed between mammals and insects, lethal and sublethal effects of neonicotinoid exposure can also vary among insect species [22, 24, 25]. Physiological factors, such as detoxification capacity, contribute to divergence in sensitivity among species, but molecular variation in nAChRs remains a key determinant of baseline susceptibility in insects [26–32].

Some insect species naturally express receptor subtypes with reduced affinity for synthetic compounds, resulting in limited toxicity [33–35]. Another major driver of reduced target-site sensitivity is the occurrence of substitutions at canonical binding residues within orthosteric loops. Such mutations, particularly within the β1 subunit, have been documented in agricultural pests exhibiting strong neonicotinoid resistance [36–38]. However, orthosteric binding domains are likely subject to strong evolutionary constraint due to their essential role in receptor function and therefore vary primarily under intense directional selection [6, 12, 39]. In contrast, substitutions that induce structural modifications in less constrained regions may contribute to receptor differentiation and regulation, potentially altering sensitivity to synthetic agonists [9, 19, 20].

Insecticide formulations containing the neonicotinoid clothianidin are being deployed in malaria vector control programs to mitigate widespread resistance to pyrethroids [40–42]. Recent field studies in areas where clothianidin-based indoor residual spraying was deployed have documented rapid shifts in vector species composition, driven by the expansion of less susceptible species and the decline of more sensitive populations [43, 44]. Anopheles coluzzii and An. gambiae are among the most important malaria vectors in Africa [45, 46]. These sister species diverged recently and are undergoing ecological differentiation across the continent [47–49]. Multiple studies have indicated that larvae and adults of An. coluzzii are more sensitive to neonicotinoids than those of An. gambiae [50–53]. A study of 13 distinct An. gambiae receptor subtypes expressed in Xenopus laevis oocytes revealed differences in neonicotinoid affinity among receptor assemblies, underscoring the importance of target-site variability in determining neonicotinoid sensitivity in mosquitoes [18].

Here, we analyzed the distribution of amino acid substitutions in nAChR subunits among species of the An. gambiae complex using whole-genome and Sanger sequencing data. Our objective was to assess whether such substitutions facilitate receptor divergence among cryptic species and could contribute to heterogenous responses to insecticide exposure. Despite the evolutionary constraint in this essential gene family, two linked mutations segregating by species were identified in the α6 subunit. How standing genetic variation in nAChRs contributes to functional divergence and insecticide susceptibility among mosquito populations remains to be elucidated.

Methods

Whole-genome dataset and nAChR variants identification

We analyzed genetic variation within the nAChR gene family using the Anopheles gambiae 1000 Genomes Phase 2-AR1 dataset, which includes whole-genome sequences from 1,142 wild-caught mosquitoes sampled from 33 sites across 13 sub-Saharan African countries [54, 55]. This dataset was previously described [55]. For downstream analyses, we retained a subset of 532 individuals identified as An. gambiae (n = 343) or An. coluzzii (n = 189), sampled from 10 countries representing the geographic diversity of sub-Saharan Africa (Supplementary Material, Table S1).

The An. gambiae AgamP4 genome assembly contains 11 genes encoding nAChR subunits (8 α, 2 β, and 1 αβ) [56]. α subunits are characterized by two adjacent cysteine residues in ligand-binding loop C, which are absent from β subunits. In An. gambiae, the α9 subunit represents an atypical αβ subunit and produces two transcript isoforms, only one of which contains the adjacent cysteine motif [57]. Protein sequences for each subunit were retrieved from VectorBase [58], and multiple sequence alignment of all 11 subunits was conducted using MAFFT [59] to verify homology. Positions of binding loops (A-G) and transmembrane domains (TM1-TM4) within each subunit were determined using consensus sequences obtained from [56]. To assess genetic variation within coding sequences, we retrieved exon coordinates for each gene from AgamP4 genome annotation (GFF) using accession numbers [58]. Using bcftools v1.21 [60], we then extracted variants from the VCF files of the 532 selected individuals, retaining only those within exon regions and with a minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.01 across each nAChR gene. We also analyzed whole-genome sequencing data from four other species in the Anopheles gambiae complex—An. arabiensis (n = 12), An. quadriannulatus (n = 10), An. melas (n = 9), and An. merus (n = 10). The samples were collected from several locations throughout the continent and were previously described [61]. We analyzed the corresponding VCFs of these samples to explore the distribution of genotypes for two mutations in the α6 subunit (I198M and D202E) in other species of the An. gambiae complex.

Variant annotation

We annotated variants with SnpEff v4.3 [62] using the An. gambiae reference database and VCF files to classify variants by predicted functional impact within each nAChR subunit. Predicted effects on protein coding sequences were classified into three categories of impact as defined in SnpEff: (i) low impact: changes expected to have little or no influence on protein activity, here referred to as synonymous (e.g., synonymous variant, start retained variant, stop retained variant, 5′UTR variant, and 3′UTR variant); (ii) moderate impact: alterations that may affect protein function without completely disrupting it, here referred to as nonsynonymous (e.g., missense variant, in-frame insertion, in-frame deletion, and splice region variant) and (iii) high impact: mutations predicted to cause major disruption of the protein, here referred to as loss-of-function (e.g., stop gained, frameshift variant, splice acceptor variant, splice donor variant, and start lost). Annotation information was extracted from the annotated VCF files using SnpSift [63]. We used the predicted coordinates of the ligand binding loops (A-G) and the four transmembrane domains (TM1-TM4) to identify nonsynonymous variants occurring within these canonical domains [56].

Genetic diversity and divergence of nAChRs

We calculated genotype frequencies across the continent for each nAChR subunit separately in An. gambiae and An. coluzzii using PLINK v2.0 [64, 65]. Allele frequencies of nonsynonymous mutations were visualized using heatmaps, and Fisher’s exact test was applied to allele counts to assess whether alternative alleles with frequencies > 50% differed significantly between species. To examine genetic differentiation, we extracted synonymous and nonsynonymous mutations with MAF > 0.01 from the merged, annotated VCF of 532 individuals for each nAChR gene. We then converted these data to genind objects using the adegenet package (v2.1.10) [66]. Nei’s genetic distances [67] were subsequently calculated, and population structure was visualized using neighbor-joining trees [68]. To quantify divergence between An. gambiae and An. coluzzii, we calculated relative genetic differentiation (FST) per variant using Hudson’s estimator in PLINK v2.0. For each nAChR subunit, FST values were computed separately for synonymous and nonsynonymous sites, and median FST values were used to summarize overall differentiation between species.

Detecting signatures of positive selection around fixed mutations

To test whether divergence between An. gambiae and An. coluzzii is driven by recent strong positive selection, we analyzed signatures of extended haplotype homozygosity (EHH) around fixed nonsynonymous mutations between species [69]. We restricted this analysis to two linked substitutions in α6 that were fixed in An. gambiae and absent in An. coluzzii. To test for signatures of a selective sweep around the linked mutations, we computed EHH decay within a ± 200 kb window centered on the two SNPs (I198M and D202E) in α6 (400 kb total). We assessed EHH decay on both sides of the focal D202E mutation using phased haplotypes from individuals carrying the reference allele (n = 189) and those exhibiting the alternative allele (n = 322). From the merged VCF, we extracted all biallelic SNPs within the ± 200 kb window around D202E and phased genotypes with PHASE v2.1 [70]. We computed EHH as a function of distance from the D202E codon for each allele using ehh_decay in scikit-allel [71]. We compared the EHH decay curves between alternative and wild-type alleles to test for signals of strong positive selection linked to each haplotype.

Distribution of fixed differences in Anopheles species

We identified nonsynonymous mutations that were fixed between An. gambiae and An. coluzzii and assessed their distribution in 18 Anopheles species. To achieve this goal, we compared protein sequences of α6 exon 7 carrying the two fixed difference (I198M and D202E). Within the An. gambiae complex, allele frequencies for I198M and D202E were extracted from the Anopheles gambiae 1000 Genomes VCFs (An. gambiae: n = 342 and An. coluzzii: n = 189) across Africa. Genotype calls were obtained from [61] for four additional members of the complex (An. merus, An. melas, An. quadriannulatus, and An. arabiensis). For each of the four species, we matched the VCF to its reference assembly using bcftools. We then located α6 by mapping the An. gambiae α6 protein to the target genome with Exonerate (–-model protein2genome) [72], which produced a GFF file. From this GFF file, we extracted an α6 CDS and rebuilt the coding sequence by concatenating CDS blocks using samtools [60] and custom Python scripts. The resulting α6 CDS FASTA for each species was aligned to the reference An. gambiae α6 sequence using MAFFT v7.526 [59]. We then queried the 3-bp codon windows at positions 198 and 202 using samtools and bcftools to determine genotypes for each sample. α6 sequences of additional 12 Anopheles species were retrieved from VectorBase. A multiple sequence alignment was performed using exon 7 sequences from the 12 Anopheles species retrieved from vectorbase and consensus sequences for six members of the An. gambiae complex obtained from VCFs. The Aedes aegypti ortholog was used as an outgroup. Alignments were performed in ClustalW [73] and visualized in ESPript [74] to highlight residues 198 and 202. To estimate linkage disequilibrium between the two mutations, we computed Lewontin’s D′ [75] using PLINK v1.19 [76].

To further characterize the distribution of fixed differences (α6 I198M and D202E) at the scale of a country, we sequenced a section of α6 in populations from Cameroon. We previously designed a primer pair to amplify a region encompassing exons 6, 7, and 8 of the An. gambiae α6 subunit [31]. We used this primer pair to sequence An. gambiae and An. coluzzii populations sampled from diverse ecogeographic regions across Cameroon. In parallel, we sequenced the same region in individuals from the An. gambiae Kisumu and An. coluzzii Ngousso laboratory colonies. Genomic DNA was extracted from individual mosquitoes, and the target region was amplified using the designed primers. Amplicons were sequenced in both forward and reverse directions. Chromatograms were processed using Tracy v 0.7.8 [77] to generate consensus sequences, which were then aligned to the AgamP4 reference using MAFFT to identify the I198M and D202E mutations and calculate genotype frequencies. Species were identified as An. gambiae or An. coluzzii using molecular diagnostic PCR [78].

Results

nAChRs are constrained in An. gambiae and An. coluzzii

Sequences of the 11 nAChR subunits from the An. gambiae AgamP4 genome assembly were first compared, and regions corresponding to canonical domains (ligand-binding loops and transmembrane domains) were mapped to their positions within each gene (Figs. 1 and 2). Multiple sequence alignment of the corresponding proteins confirmed the presence of seven ligand-binding loops (A–G) and four transmembrane domains (TM1–TM4) across all subunits (Fig. 2). The eight α subunits were confirmed by the presence of adjacent cysteine residues in loop C, which are absent in the two β and the αβ subunits.

Across the 11 nAChR subunit genes present in Anopheles, SnpEff annotations identified 36 nonsynonymous, 418 synonymous substitutions, and no predicted loss-of-function mutations in continental populations of An. gambiae and An. coluzzii (Table 1). Figure 3A shows the distribution of missense and synonymous mutations within each subunit. One subunit carried no nonsynonymous variants (α5) while four genes harbored only a single missense mutation (α1, α4, α7, and β1) throughout the distribution ranges of An. gambiae and An. coluzzii in Africa. The subunits β2 and α3 revealed the highest numbers on nonsynonymous mutation: 9 and 10, respectively. Intermediate levels of variation were observed in α9 (6 nonsynonymous variants) and α2, α6 (3 nonsynonymous variants each). The density of nonsynonymous mutations, computed as the number of missense variants divided by the length of coding exons, ranged from 0 in α5 to 0.0069 in β2 (Table 1).

No missense mutations were detected within transmembrane domains, and only two of the 36 nonsynonymous substitutions occurred within ligand-binding loops (D202E in the α6 loop F and T220P in the α3 loop C; Fig. 3A). Neither substitution involved residues previously identified as canonical determinants of acetylcholine or neonicotinoid binding. No variation was detected at analogous positions of basic residues known to interact with neonicotinoids within ligand-binding loops.

Most nonsynonymous mutations were low-frequency variants occurring in heterozygotes (Fig. 3B). Across nonsynonymous sites, the mean frequency of wild-type homozygotes was 78.08% in An. gambiae and 86.08% in An. coluzzii. Homozygous alternative genotypes were rare, averaging 9.55% in An. gambiae and 3.62% in An. coluzzii (Fig. 3B). Of the 36 SnpEff-annotated nonsynonymous mutations, 22 were detected exclusively in heterozygotes. Only three amino acid substitutions showed consistently high frequencies among populations. This included S215A in α9, for which the homozygous alternative allele was nearly fixed as a in both species, and the α6 substitutions I198M and D202E, which reached high homozygous frequencies in An. gambiae (95.63% for both variants).

Two residues in α6 are fixed between An. gambiae and An. coluzzii

Patterns of clustering revealed by neighbor-joining trees constructed for each nAChR subunit using both synonymous and nonsynonymous variants showed substantial differences among subunits (Fig. 4A). Subunits α3, α4, α5, and α9 exhibited little evidence of structuring by species or geography, while β2 exhibited moderate differentiation that was not aligned with species boundaries. In contrast, α1, α2, and α8 displayed clear separation into two clusters corresponding to An. gambiae and An. coluzzii. The strongest segregation was observed for α6, α7, and β1, suggesting that these subunits may contribute to interspecific divergence in nAChRs.

Estimates of genetic differentiation based on FST values revealed differences between synonymous and nonsynonymous mutations. Overall, FST values for both classes of mutation were very low (median < 0.05), consistent with weak genome-wide divergence between the two sister species (Fig. 4B). Nevertheless, several subunits, including α6, α7, and β1, contained SNPs with FST values exceeding 0.8, and α2 harbored variants with FST values above 0.4. Synonymous variants mirrored the tree-based clustering results, with α6, α7, and β1 showing higher median FST values and a few highly differentiated SNPs. In contrast, analysis of nonsynonymous variants identified α6 as the only subunit exhibiting interspecific differentiation, indicating a single focal point of divergence within protein-coding regions of the nAChR gene family. This differentiation was driven primarily by two high-frequency nonsynonymous substitutions (I198M and D202E in α6) that were fixed in An. gambiae and nearly absent from An. coluzzii (Fisher’s exact test, p < 0.001; Fig. 4C).

Distribution of the α6 I198M and D202E mutations across Anopheles species

Linkage disequilibrium analyses indicated that α6 D202E and I198M segregate together (Lewontin’s D′ = +1) and define two haplotypes in populations of An. gambiae and An. coluzzii at the continental scale. In ten countries, the I198–202E haplotype was almost fixed in An. gambiae but remained extremely rare in An. coluzzii (Fig. 5A). Among 343 An. gambiae samples, no homozygotes for the wild-type alleles (I198 and D202) were detected, although 15 D202 heterozygotes (4.37%) were observed in Central and West Africa. Conversely, none of the 189 An. coluzzii samples carried the alternative alleles as homozygotes. Six heterozygotes (3.17%) were identified, all otherwise homozygous for the wild-type alleles.

Throughout the main ecogeographic domains of Cameroon, Sanger sequencing of exon 7 containing the D202E and I198M mutations confirmed the distribution of two haplotypes along species boundaries, consistent with continental-scale observations (Fig. 5C-D). Among 161 field-collected An. gambiae, all individuals were homozygous for the alternative alleles 198 M and 202E, except four heterozygotes detected in Mangoum, an agricultural site. In contrast, all An. coluzzii individuals, including field populations as well as the laboratory strain An. coluzzii Ngousso, were homozygous for the wild-type alleles. Both alternative alleles occurred at lower frequencies in the An. gambiae Kisumu strain compared with conspecific field populations (36.8% vs. 96.2% for 202E; Fisher’s exact test, p = 4.78 × 10⁻¹²).

Despite being tightly linked in An. gambiae and An. coluzzii, the α6 D202E and I198M segregate independently in other species of the An. gambiae complex. In An. arabiensis, the alternative allele M at 198 was fixed (12/12) and E at 202 was nearly fixed (11/12, one D/E). In An. quadriannulatus, mutation 198 remained wild-type (10/10 I), whereas the alternative allele E at 202 was nearly fixed (9/10, one D/E). In contrast, An. melas and An. merus showed only the wild-type alleles at both sites: I at 198 and D at 202 in all genotyped samples (Supplementary Table S2).

Based on multiple alignment of α6 exon-7 protein sequences from a total of 18 taxa, we found that valine (V) is common at α6-198 in more distant Anopheles species and in the outgroup Ae. aegypti (Fig. 5B). The predominance of isoleucine at this position among members of the An. gambiae complex suggests that this residue is ancestral within the complex while the derived allele methionine (M) occurs on a shared haplotype in An. gambiae field populations and An. arabiensis. At α6-202, species of the complex are split in two groups: An. gambiae, An. arabiensis, and An. quadriannulatus carry glutamate (E), whereas An. coluzzii, An. melas, and An. merus retain aspartate (D). Most Anopheles outgroups and Ae. aegypti also carry D, supporting D as ancestral and E as derived.

Signals of positive selection around D202E are weak or absent

To test whether the α6 locus has been subjected to recent strong selection pressures, we assessed signals of recent positive selection associated with the I198M–D202E haplotype. Because the two mutations are in perfect linkage (D’ = 1), we used extended haplotype homozygosity (EHH) in a 400-kb window to explore signatures of selection centered on one of them (D202E). Across the 5-kb flanking regions on either side of the mutation, EHH decay was indistinguishable between haplotypes carrying the wild-type or the alternative alleles (Fig. 6). In both populations, EHH values were > 0.95 at the D202E codon but dropped sharply to < 0.1 within 200 bp. Beyond this point, both traces remained close to zero, showing no evidence of extended haplotype structure as would be expected under a recent selective sweep. This result is consistent with a lack of strong positive selection on either haplotype, although weaker forms of selection not detectable by EHH decay cannot be excluded.

Discussion

Evolutionary constraint in nAChRs

Here, we analyzed genetic variation across 11 nAChR subunit genes throughout the geographic ranges of two Anopheles cryptic species in Africa. Our results indicate an absence of predicted loss-of-function mutations in the nAChR gene family in both species. Given the essential role of nAChRs in insect neural signaling [1, 6], loss-of-function variants are likely removed from wild populations or maintained at very low frequencies due to their deleterious effects. Nevertheless, naturally occurring loss-of-function mutations—such as premature stop codons in the α6 subunit—have been reported in viable populations of some insect species [79, 80]. This contrast suggests that disruptions to nAChR function may impose stronger fitness costs in Anopheles mosquitoes than in other insects [39, 81–83].

At a continental scale, we detected only 36 nonsynonymous mutations in 11 nAChR subunits, most of which were present at low frequencies. The α5 subunit was the most constrained gene, with no detectable nonsynonymous mutations in An. gambiae or An. coluzzii populations across Africa. Although the precise functional role of this subunit in these species remains unclear, its evolutionary conservation suggests that it may represent a critical structural or functional component of anopheline nAChR assemblies [5, 39, 56].

Most amino acid substitutions detected in this study were located outside canonical functional domains of the receptor. Only one low-frequency substitution (T220P in α3) occurred within a primary ligand-binding loop (loop C). The high-frequency mutation D202E in α6 was located in loop F. However, loops D–F in α subunits are generally considered to play a secondary role in stabilizing the ligand-binding pocket [3, 11, 84]. Insect sensitivity to neonicotinoids is primarily determined by amino acid residues whose roles in canonical ligand binding have been demonstrated through functional and mutational studies [13, 14, 85]. All of these residues were conserved in both species, suggesting that orthosteric binding affinity is unlikely to vary between An. gambiae and An. coluzzii [5, 39].

No missense mutations were detected in any of the four transmembrane helices (TM1–TM4), consistent with studies in other insect species that rarely report amino acid substitutions in these domains [39, 79, 86]. The M1, M3, and M4 segments contribute to structural stability of the pentameric receptor, with M4 interacting with the surrounding lipid environment, while the M2 segment forms the pore-lining helix that controls gating and cation selectivity [87]. The absence of nonsynonymous variation within TM domains therefore suggests conservation of key biophysical properties, including ion selectivity, desensitization kinetics, and membrane interactions in nAChRs among An. gambiae and An. coluzzii populations. Similarly, no nonsynonymous mutations fell within motifs characteristic of N-glycosylation sites or predicted cAMP-, PKC-, CK2-, or tyrosine kinase phosphorylation sites [56].

Overall, these results indicate that differences in baseline susceptibility to neonicotinoids between An. gambiae and An. coluzzii are unlikely to be associated with amino acid changes within canonical orthosteric or transmembrane domains, or by variation in phosphorylation or N-glycosylation of nAChRs [50–52]. Nevertheless, functional divergence of pentameric ligand-gated ion channels can occur in the absence of changes at canonical ligand-binding residues [88, 89]. Amino acid substitutions in non-orthosteric regions can impact ion conductance and selectivity, desensitization kinetics, interactions with membrane lipids, protein kinase–mediated regulation, and allosteric modulation [33, 90, 91]. In insects, an allosteric site targeted by spinosad has been identified in the α6 subunit at the TM2–TM3 interface [19, 20]. The two high-frequency nonsynonymous substitutions identified here (I198M and D202E) are located near loop F in α6 and are therefore structurally very distant from this proposed allosteric site. This spatial separation makes a direct effect of I198M and D202E on spinosad binding unlikely.

Early divergence of the α6 subunit between cryptic species

Despite the evolutionary constraint preserving the nAChR gene family and its canonical functional domains, we identified two linked substitutions in the vicinity of α6 loop F that circulate at very high frequencies in wild populations. The first substitution, D202E, involves the replacement of aspartate by glutamate. This change extends the side chain by one methylene group while maintaining a negative charge. Although chemically conservative, such substitutions can subtly modify receptor properties by altering local geometry, modulating electrostatic interactions, or affecting stability and pKa values [92–94].

The second substitution, I198M, is in perfect linkage with D202E and replaces isoleucine with methionine. Given their similar hydrophobicity and side-chain volumes, this mutation is generally considered conservative and unlikely to cause major structural disruption. However, even conservative substitutions can introduce detectable changes in stability or subtly affect protein dynamics [95].

An important feature of the I198M and D202E substitutions is their near fixation between species. The wild-type and derived alleles were fixed or nearly fixed in several members of the An. gambiae complex, with only a few heterozygotes detected in wild populations. Fixed amino acid differences in canonical ligand-binding regions have been reported between distantly related species and are often associated with shifts in ligand affinity or sensitivity to chemical agonists [7, 10, 21]. In contrast, our study identifies fixed differences at two residues in closely related species, highlighting the importance of examining genetic variation beyond orthosteric domains. The biochemical and evolutionary implications of the linked I198M and D202E substitutions remain unclear but their segregation among cryptic species suggests that such variants may contribute to subtle receptor functional differentiation during early stages of ecological and genetic divergence.

To assess whether the high frequency of these substitutions resulted from recent strong selection in wild populations, we examined extended haplotype homozygosity (EHH) decay. The absence of extended haplotype structure argues against a recent hard selective sweep, which would be expected to generate long haplotypes of reduced diversity via hitchhiking [69, 96]. Nonetheless, weaker or older forms of selection may not generate detectable EHH signals. A more parsimonious explanation is that the current distribution of the linked I198M and D202E substitutions reflects either ancient polymorphisms predating species divergence within the An. gambiae complex or the action of weak, context-dependent selection. The observed genotype frequencies, particularly the scarcity of homozygous mutants in An. coluzzii, raise the possibility that these mutations may be deleterious in this species, but not in An. gambiae [97].

The main limitation of this study is the lack of link between the mutations detected in nAChRs and their potential functional implications. The location of the two linked mutations driving nAChR divergence between An. gambiae and An. coluzzii suggests they may not directly affect the binding of neonicotinoids and Spinosad, but an experimental validation is still lacking. A recent study identified signatures of positive selection in the β2 subunit of An. gambiae populations resistant to pirimiphos-methyl, an organophosphate insecticide [98]. This intriguing finding highlights the importance of complementary functional studies that will help elucidate the contribution of target-site evolution in the development of resistance to several classes of insecticides used in vector control.

Conclusions

With the progressive deployment of nAChR-targeting insecticides in malaria vector control, investigating genetic variation underlying receptor evolution across Anopheles species has become increasingly important. In this study, we show that large-scale genomic data can illuminate patterns of amino acid variation within the nAChR gene family in multiple vector species. The recent resurgence of malaria incidence in regions where clothianidin-based indoor residual spraying was implemented highlights the need to better understand genetic variation that may facilitate development of target-site resistance in vectors [43, 44, 99]. The growing availability of high-quality genomic datasets for major mosquito vectors now provides an unprecedented opportunity to characterize standing variation in nAChRs and to assess its potential role in adaptation to anthropogenic disturbance [100–102].
