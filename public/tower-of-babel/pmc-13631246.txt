Antimicrobial resistance among clinical isolates associated with combat-related infections in Ukraine: first nationwide surveillance, 2023-2024
Authors: Ludmila Kasatkina, Ihnat Havrylov, Anzhela Sboieva, Valentyna Yanovska, Tetiana Glushkevych, Artem Horbachevskyi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a critical global health threat, which reduces the effectiveness of treatment and drives higher morbidity, mortality, and costs. In Ukraine, the problem has been further aggravated by Russia’s full-scale invasion, with favorable conditions for the spread of resistant pathogens. We characterized the isolation frequencies and resistance patterns among main ESKAPE pathogens in combat wound infections in Ukraine in 2023-2024 – Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Among 40,823 microbial strains isolated 18,848 were identified for surveillance. Approximately 1,000 isolates of MDR Gram-negative organisms submitted for verification at the Reference Laboratory confirm the ongoing spread of multidrug-resistant (MDR) hospital-acquired pathogens in Ukraine. We found that ~60% of verified K. pneumoniae strains carried MBLs conferring near-pan resistance to β-lactams. We provided enhanced epidemiological surveillance data on antimicrobial resistance in microorganisms causing wound infections among combat-injured patients, along with antimicrobial prescribing practices, which underscore the urgent need for coordinated antibiotic stewardship and infection control programs adapted to wartime conditions. Our findings further support the establishment and strengthening of sustained surveillance of ESKAPE pathogens in wartime wound infections, alongside broader capacity-building efforts within healthcare systems.Subject terms: Diseases, Medical research, Microbiology

Introduction

As one of the most formidable global threats, antimicrobial resistance (AMR)1 is increasingly significant in regions destabilized by war2, where infections spread unchecked. In Ukraine today, AMR represents one of the most serious threats to national security within the healthcare sector. Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine has led to unprecedented population displacement3, including among medical professionals, the destruction of healthcare facilities, deterioration of sanitary and hygienic conditions, and significant economic losses. The critical burden on the medical system4 has further exacerbated the existing problem of irrational consumption of antimicrobial drugs and the spread of AMR in Ukraine.

AMR reduces the effectiveness of treatment, complicates the rehabilitation of the wounded, and increases disability and mortality rates among both civilian and military patients. The costs of infection treatment and subsequent complications caused by resistant bacteria are rising, placing additional strain on healthcare systems with limited resources5,6.

One of the main drivers of AMR development is the overburdened medical system resulting from the growing number of wounded military personnel and civilians. By 2022, Ukraine’s infection prevention and control (IPC) system exhibited significant structural and operational gaps, including limited availability of isolation facilities, insufficient microbiological diagnostic capacity, inconsistent supply of essential IPC materials (e.g., personal protective equipment and disinfectants), shortages of trained IPC personnel, and incomplete implementation and enforcement of standardized protocols7. Since the onset of the full-scale invasion, intensified patient movement has facilitated the inter-facility spread of resistant microorganisms through increased microbial exchange and suboptimal adherence to infection control practices. The transfer of patients carrying AMR pathogens (either infected or colonized) between medical facilities via medical evacuation further aggravates the problem. In addition, irrational use of antibiotics to treat infected wounds, particularly in frontline pre-hospital care settings and in the absence of adequate antimicrobial stewardship, contributes to AMR development and dissemination. This includes antibiotics supplied through international humanitarian aid and informal domestic volunteer channels to military personnel.

The quality and rational use of antibiotics remain insufficiently regulated. Failure to maintain hygiene standards in crowded temporary shelters and field hospitals further facilitates the spread of infections. Another challenge is the underdeveloped bacteriological laboratory infrastructure. In some laboratories, limited implementation of Quality Management Systems, staffing shortages, and inadequate resources hinder compliance with EUCAST standards, timely result turnaround, and test reliability. Finally, a key objective of policies addressing AMR is to increase physicians’ awareness of the indications for bacteriological testing and proper sampling, ensuring effective antibiotic use and timely treatment.

The only nationally coordinated antimicrobial resistance surveillance data in Ukraine come from CAESAR (Central Asian and European Surveillance of Antimicrobial Resistance)8. As of 2023, 75 bacteriological laboratories in Ukraine report data to CAESAR; however, the number of submitted isolates remains very low (only dozens), which is clearly insufficient to represent the national situation. Available peer-reviewed studies remain limited in number and scope, are predominantly hospital-based or regional, and do not provide consolidated national-level estimates. The full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 has significantly affected both healthcare conditions and AMR transmission dynamics, underscoring the need for systematic national AMR surveillance.

In response to the worsening AMR situation and the urgent need for surveillance among war-wounded patients, Ukraine has launched nationwide monitoring of antimicrobial resistance in combat-related injuries and approved the guidelines for enhanced epidemiological surveillance of antimicrobial resistance of microorganisms causing purulent inflammatory wound infections in combat wounded9. This study aimed to provide the first data on AMR surveillance in Ukraine (2023-2024), identify the main trends in resistance, and relate these findings to antimicrobial prescribing practices.

Results

General characteristics of the sample

In the frame of annual reporting, in 2024, microbiological laboratories in hospitals and regional centers for disease control and prevention (CDC) in Ukraine examined 48,771 samples from patients with combat-related injuries (bullet wounds, blast injuries, FPV-drone injuries, burns, etc.), representing an 85% increase compared with 2023 (26,296), even accounting for the shorter reporting period (Fig. 1a). Patients were actively monitored for signs of bloodstream infection or meningitis; where systemic infection was clinically suspected, including sepsis or central nervous system involvement secondary to combat-related wounds, standard diagnostic criteria were followed (see Methods), and blood cultures and/or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples were obtained accordingly. A total of 40,823 microbial strains were isolated in 2024, of which 18,848 (46.2%) were identified for surveillance (Supplementary Table 1), including the key ESKAPE pathogens, defined by the WHO10,11 – Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Escherichia coli (representing Enterobacter spp.) – as well as Enterococcus faecalis.

Samples were categorized according to the interval between injury and hospital admission, and the length of hospitalization at the time of sampling: hospitalization <48 h and injury <72 h before admission – interpreted as admission to the first hospital within the trauma evacuation pathway with a recent (“fresh”) wound; hospitalization >48 h and injury <72 h before admission – interpreted as admission to the first hospital within the trauma evacuation pathway, with a stay of up to 72 h; hospitalization <48 h and injury >72 h before admission – interpreted as admission to a second or subsequent hospital within the trauma evacuation pathway, indicating recent transfer and an older wound; hospitalization >48 h and injury >72 h before admission – interpreted as admission to a second or subsequent hospital, with prolonged hospitalization likely reflecting extended treatment.

The number of strains under surveillance was lowest in the <48 h/<72 h group (“fresh” wounds) and increased threefold in the >48 h/>72 h group (prolonged treatment). Overall, the highest numbers were observed in patients wounded >72 h before sampling (Fig. 1b).

For confirmatory testing and further analysis, bacteriological laboratories of regional CDC, as well as hospitals testing wounded patients, submitted selected bacterial isolates of MDR microorganisms to the Reference Laboratory (RL) (Fig. 1a). These submissions represent a targeted subset of isolates meeting predefined criteria (as listed in Supplementary Table 1). In total, 1,317 cultures in 2024 and 1,136 in the three quarters of 2023 met the inclusion criteria outlined in Supplementary Table 1 and were analyzed. The WHONET program was used for data processing and analysis.

The findings of our study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, not all designated hospitals fully comply with national procedures for specimen collection, bacteriological testing, result reporting, antibiotic prescription, and transfer of MDR isolates to the RL. Second, it remains impossible to validate data submitted by hospitals. Third, the reliability of AMR trend analysis is affected by variability in antibiotic panels used for isolates from the same sample type. Fourth, resistance levels may be slightly overestimated in the annual reporting data due to occasional inclusion of repeated isolates from the same patient in routine surveillance, although such cases are estimated by the Reference Laboratory to be infrequent (~2%) and are unlikely to affect overall resistance estimates. Additionally, the absence of quality assurance systems and non-compliance with standardized methods due to shortages of test kits introduce errors in bacteriological testing. Finally, most laboratories do not assess S. aureus susceptibility to vancomycin and/or teicoplanin using minimum inhibitory concentration testing, nor do they test Gram-negative isolates for colistin susceptibility by broth microdilution. As a result, the proportion of E. coli, K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, and A. baumannii resistant to colistin, or S. aureus resistant to vancomycin and/or teicoplanin, cannot be reliably estimated.

Prevalence of MDR cultures submitted to the Reference Laboratory for confirmatory testing

Among the submitted cultures, Gram-negative microorganisms dominated (88.8% in 2024, 94% in 2023), confirming their markedly higher prevalence of MDR strains. The proportion of Gram-positive microorganisms nearly doubled, increasing from 6% in 2023 to 11.2% in 2024, with a particularly notable rise in S. aureus (Fig. 1c, d, Supplementary Fig. 1). Most isolates were recovered from wound specimens. Blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples accounted for smaller proportions, with blood-derived isolates comprising 300 (22.8%) in 2024 and 185 (16.3%) in 2023, and CSF-derived isolates comprising 48 (4.2%) in 2024 and 45 (3.4%) in 2023 (Fig. 1e).

In 2024, the proportion of E. coli and S. aureus bloodstream isolates increased (Fig. 1e). The distribution of wound isolates (Fig. 1f) mirrored the overall sample pattern (Fig. 1d), with A. baumannii, K. pneumoniae, and P. aeruginosa being most frequently recovered in comparable proportions. Over half of the blood isolates in 2023-2024 were K. pneumoniae (Fig. 1f), with overall pathogen profiles remaining stable except for a moderate decrease in P. aeruginosa and rise in S. aureus in 2024. From 2023 to 2024, a decrease in K. pneumoniae CSF isolates was observed alongside an increase in A. baumannii (Fig. 1f), while other pathogens showed minimal change.

Pathogen prevalence and resistance post-injury and during hospitalization

Pathogen isolation increased with time post-injury and hospitalization. Acinetobacter spp., P. aeruginosa, K. pneumoniae, and S. aureus predominated in patients hospitalized >48 h and wounded >72 h (Fig. 1g), showing an ~3-6-fold rise, compared to fresh wounds, suggesting substantial hospital-acquired transmission.

Acinetobacter spp

Colistin resistance among isolated Acinetobacter spp. showed a slight decrease, from 7.2% in 2023 to 4.0% in 2024 (Fig. 2a). MDR A. baumannii increased with prolonged hospitalization, though even fresh-wound isolates often exhibited high resistance (Fig. 2b). In 2024, isolates submitted for confirmatory testing showed increased susceptibility to aminoglycosides, while all remained resistant to fluoroquinolones (Fig. 2c). Tobramycin showed the strongest in vitro activity, whereas the modest improvement in carbapenem susceptibility remains insufficient to support their reliable empirical use against hospital-acquired strains.

P. aeruginosa

Resistance of P. aeruginosa to most antimicrobials declined in 2024 (Fig. 2d), reflecting a positive trend and the impact of local AMR control measures, with colistin resistance dropping from 15.1% to 10.2% (Fig. 2d). Resistance increased with longer hospitalization and time from injury (Fig. 2e).

In 2024, isolates sent to the RL showed improved susceptibility to carbapenems, with resistance to meropenem decreasing from 97.1% to 91.6%, and to aminoglycosides, with resistance to tobramycin decreasing by 10.0% and to amikacin from 95.2% to 88.8%; Fig. 2f). P. aeruginosa rapidly acquires resistance to most agents, retaining reliable susceptibility only to colistin, rendering effective empirical therapy with routine antibiotics largely unfeasible.

K. pneumoniae

K. pneumoniae exhibited a rise in colistin resistance, from 11.1% in 2023 to 32.4% in 2024 (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Data 1). This represents the highest level of colistin resistance among all pathogens included in the surveillance. The level of antimicrobial resistance in K. pneumoniae is very high (Fig. 3b) and, as with A. baumannii, increases slightly with longer hospital stays. The level of resistance to aminoglycosides and fluoroquinolones is also high, making their use for empirical treatment probably inefficient.

In 2024, verification of cultures at the RL showed a slight increase in K. pneumoniae susceptibility to aminoglycosides, although overall susceptibility remained critically low (0–5%) for most agents (Fig. 3e). Aminoglycosides retained modest activity (10–35%), while carbapenem susceptibility remained very low (5–15%) with no improvement from 2023. Alarmingly, colistin resistance increased from 3.4% to 10.9% (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Data 1), raising significant concerns. Mirroring P. aeruginosa, K. pneumoniae rapidly acquires resistance to most routinely tested antibiotics, retaining limited susceptibility only to colistin, which is now also decreasing. This trend is particularly troubling given the rising number of confirmed MDR strains in 2023–2024, with the true burden likely higher due to underreporting.

E. coli

In E. coli isolates collected from wounds, resistance was highest to penicillins (amoxicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, ampicillin, Fig. 3c). Resistance to ceftriaxone – particularly in patients hospitalized >48 h and wounded >72 h – as well as to cefotaxime and ceftazidime, remained elevated (Fig. 3d). Notably, resistance to the last-line antibiotic colistin dropped to 5.1% (2023: 12.5%, Fig. 3c), although this figure may be inflated due to technical limitations in testing Gram-negative bacteria. Among carbapenems, meropenem showed the lowest resistance at 15.9% (21.5% in 2023), but rates more than doubled with prolonged hospitalization, from 6.6% to 16.1% (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Data 1).

In 2024, E. coli isolates submitted for confirmatory testing at the RL showed susceptibility to aminoglycosides, cephalosporins and carbapenems (Fig. 3f). Resistance to aminoglycosides decreased by ~1.5–2.5-fold, depending on the agent, and to fluoroquinolones from 95.7% (2024) to 82.4% (2023). Piperacillin-tazobactam resistance decreased to 33.3% in 2024.

Colistin remained highly active, with 98% of isolates susceptible in 2024. Although in vitro susceptibility to other antibiotics improved in 2024, it remained low (Fig. 3f), limiting their suitability for empirical treatment of hospital-acquired E. coli infections. Among monitored Gram-negative pathogens, E. coli retains the highest antibiotic susceptibility.

We analyzed resistance determinants in E. coli and K. pneumoniae, key reservoirs and global sentinels for ESBL and carbapenemase production, making them highly informative for surveillance (Fig. 3g). About 60% of K. pneumoniae strains submitted for verification carried MBLs, which are known to confer resistance to nearly all β-lactams, including last-resort carbapenems.

S. aureus

Resistance of S. aureus wound isolates was highest to benzylpenicillin (Fig. 4a, b), consistent with widespread penicillinase production. Reflecting the generally lower resistance potential of Gram-positive pathogens, S. aureus resistance remained stable regardless of hospitalization or injury (Fig. 4b).

In 2024, 8.8% of S. aureus isolates submitted to the RL were susceptible to benzylpenicillin, reflecting an emergence of susceptibility compared with 2023 (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Data 1). Rifampicin and tetracycline resistance decreased, dropping to 25.6% and 32.2%, respectively. Notably, susceptibility to vancomycin and teicoplanin remains near-universal, indicating that despite a high MRSA prevalence, resistance is still controlled and less concerning than that observed for MDR Gram-negative pathogens.

E. faecium

Data for E. faecium wound isolates should be interpreted cautiously due to the small sample size (<20 isolates). Resistance to ampicillin and amoxicillin (Fig. 4d, f) is intrinsic. Vancomycin resistance more than doubled, from 8.9% to 24.9%, and teicoplanin resistance rose from 5.2% to 19.2% (2023 vs 2024).

Among isolates submitted to RL for confirmatory testing in 2024, vancomycin and teicoplanin resistance decreased (Fig. 4g, Supplementary Data 1), while linezolid resistance emerged at 5.6%.

E. faecalis

Enterococcal resistance to antibiotics remained low overall (Fig. 4e, h). High-level gentamicin resistance remained the most prevalent at 24.6% (23.5% in 2023). E.faecalis is frequently found in “fresh” wounds, with its incidence rising only about twofold during prolonged treatment (Fig. 1g).

Isolates submitted for confirmatory testing in 2024 showed high susceptibility to all tested agents, consistent with 2023 data (Fig. 4i). National guidelines mandate submission of E. faecalis isolates resistant to ampicillin, vancomycin, or teicoplanin (Supplementary Table 1). Three isolates were submitted in 2023 and eighteen in 2024. However, in most isolates, resistance was not confirmed at the RL.

Antimicrobial prescription patterns across clinical sites

Data from healthcare facility reports indicate that within the Access group of antimicrobials (WHO AWaRe classification12, Fig. 5a), the most frequently prescribed agents were metronidazole (the first-line treatment for mixed anaerobic infections), cefazolin, clindamycin, amoxicillin, and amoxicillin-clavulanate. Prescription of metronidazole, amoxicillin-clavulanate, and clindamycin in the fourth quarter of 2024 increased slightly compared with 2023, suggesting improved adherence to national treatment guidelines. The ratio of Reserve to Access antimicrobials improved significantly relative to 2023 (Fig. 5a).

The spread of ESBL-producing pathogens, resistant to β-lactam antibiotics, is linked to excessive use of third- and fourth-generation cephalosporins. In 2024, third-generation cephalosporins accounted for 20.05% of all antibiotic prescriptions, representing a decrease from 33.07% in 2023 (Fig. 5b), primarily due to reduced ceftriaxone prescription (Fig. 5c). Trends in aminoglycoside use were heterogeneous: amikacin prescribing increased in late 2024 relative to both the preceding quarter and the same period in 2023, whereas gentamicin use declined but later stabilized at 2023 levels. Cumulative annual prescription of gentamicin and amikacin rose 1.8- and 1.6-fold, respectively.

Among the 20 most frequently prescribed antibiotics, accounting for ~85% of all prescriptions (Fig. 5c), the leading agents for wounded patients in 2024 were cefazolin (15.7%), ceftriaxone (15.4%), metronidazole (14.0%), and levofloxacin (4.9%). In contrast, the top agents in 2023 were ceftriaxone (24.4%), metronidazole (13.2%), cefazolin (6.9%), and levofloxacin (9.4%).

The 2.3-fold rise in cefazolin prescription reflects the active implementation of two national guidelines: one on the prevention of infections associated with combat-related injuries (pre-hospital stage)13, and another on perioperative antimicrobial prophylaxis14. Cefazolin is recommended as the first-line agent for most prophylactic indications in both.

Monitoring visits revealed that during early evacuation of combat-injured patients, broad-spectrum antibiotics are often overprescribed – frequently exceeding clinical indications or guideline recommendations. Third-generation cephalosporins, particularly ceftriaxone, are commonly administered empirically until microbiological results become available, sometimes for extended periods. Such prolonged empirical use is clinically unjustified and poses long-term risks for both individual and public health.

Linezolid (Reserve group) and vancomycin use in Q4 2024 and overall was notably higher than in 2023, reflecting an increase in S. aureus-related infections. However, prescribing linezolid without confirmed vancomycin resistance in S. aureus or Enterococcus spp. is not considered appropriate. Given that most referred isolates remained susceptible to vancomycin, a substantial proportion of linezolid use appears unwarranted. Colistin prescription in 2024 remained high, reflecting either the continued circulation of multidrug-resistant Gram-negative pathogens and/or potentially inappropriate prescribing practices for this agent across medical institutions.

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed enhanced epidemiological surveillance data on antimicrobial resistance in microorganisms associated with wound infections among combat-injured patients, along with antimicrobial prescribing practices in Ukraine during 2023–2024. Approximately 1000 isolates of MDR Gram-negative organisms (K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, A. baumannii) submitted for verification to the RL confirm the ongoing spread of MDR hospital-acquired pathogens in Ukraine. Empirical treatment against these pathogens remains largely ineffective, while reliance on last-resort agents such as colistin should be approached with caution and is associated with high cost and considerable toxicity.

Notably, even colistin susceptibility shows a downward trend among all major pathogens. Given the limited treatment options and increasing difficulty in managing such infections, strict implementation and enforcement of infection prevention and control measures are critically important. Immediate isolation of colonized or infected patients should be carried out per the approved measures15.

In fresh injuries, wound cultures are typically dominated by environmental contaminants and the patient’s normal microbiota. Over time, with hospitalization and medical evacuation, the microbial composition shifts toward hospital-acquired and MDR organisms.

Samples collected more than 48 hours after hospitalization or more than 72 hours post-injury were obtained from patients with longer hospital stays, more severe conditions, and greater medical needs. A direct correlation was observed between the duration of hospitalization and detection of resistant strains. This reflects both the selection of more severely injured patients and the accumulation of hospital-acquired resistant microorganisms transmitted during further stages of medical evacuation.

The low proportion of blood culture samples relative to wound samples suggests potential underdiagnosis of septic conditions among wounded patients. In Ukraine, blood cultures are performed in only about 2 per 100 hospitalizations – far below the 10–40 per 100 observed in European hospitals and the 10–20 per 100 recommended for middle-income countries16.

Given their clinical importance among wound pathogens, K. pneumoniae and E. coli were further analyzed for resistance determinants. Klebsiella spp. are opportunistic pathogens that primarily affect hospitalized patients with compromised immunity17,18. K. pneumoniae infections in hospitalized patients mainly result from diverse opportunistic strains, alongside nosocomial AMR and community-acquired hypervirulent variants19. K. pneumoniae remains the most clinically significant species and a leading cause of healthcare-associated infections. Its multiple resistance genes confer extensive antimicrobial tolerance, while carbapenem resistance severely limits treatment options.

Importantly, resistance mechanisms do not always translate into clinical resistance, as they may not be expressed or may be expressed at levels insufficient to produce a resistant phenotype. The presence of resistance determinants such as ESBLs or carbapenemases in Gram-negative bacteria does not necessarily imply phenotypic or clinical resistance. Identification of these mechanisms is therefore more relevant for infection control and public health than for direct clinical decision-making. Carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales typically exhibit reduced susceptibility to carbapenems and are often resistant to broad-spectrum cephalosporins (cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, ceftazidime, and/or cefepime). However, some carbapenemase types (e.g., OXA-48) may retain cephalosporin susceptibility. Thus, detecting ESBLs or carbapenemases remains clinically important even when isolates appear susceptible by routine testing, as it informs risk assessment and guides targeted therapy.

Excessive prescription of ceftriaxone and levofloxacin contributes to antimicrobial resistance and reduces treatment efficacy when used empirically without confirmed susceptibility. National guidelines introduced pre-authorization procedures in hospitals in 2023 to strengthen oversight of their use. Limited use of narrow-spectrum antibiotics and aminoglycosides reflects inadequate microbiological testing, delayed or ignored culture results, and restricted hospital access, undermining evidence-based prescribing.

This study represents an important first step in establishing routine epidemiological surveillance of ESKAPE pathogens among wounded patients in Ukraine. The findings enable monitoring of AMR patterns, antimicrobial prescribing practices, and key resistance mechanisms. Data highlight critical gaps in microbiological surveillance, particularly at the regional level, and underscores the need for regular monitoring and capacity strengthening within hospitals and regional CDC. Ensuring the timely transfer of microbiological results across successive stages of medical evacuation should also be prioritized to support continuity of care and optimize antimicrobial treatment decisions.

In summary, this surveillance study integrates national data on resistant strains, microbiological testing, prescribing practices, hospitalization patterns, and a comprehensive assessment of AMR dissemination and treatment gaps. Prolonged hospitalization was associated with acquisition of resistant organisms, underscoring the role of in-hospital transmission and patient evacuation pathways in AMR dynamics. Extensive reliance on broad-spectrum agents such as ceftriaxone and levofloxacin, together with limited microbiological testing, highlights systemic vulnerabilities that require targeted intervention. These findings support the initiation and strengthening of sustained surveillance of ESKAPE pathogens in wartime wound infections and may inform coordinated capacity-building efforts and antimicrobial stewardship within wartime healthcare systems.

Methods

Study design

Hospitals providing specialized inpatient care for injured patients conducted bacteriological testing of biological material in accordance with the approved guidelines9. Bacteriological laboratories serving these hospitals reported testing results and data on antibiotic prescription for wound treatment to the RL (Fig. 1a). Bacteriological tests were performed on samples of wound exudate, blood, and CSF collected from individuals wounded as a result of hostilities. Hospital reporting forms recorded the frequency of isolation and antibiotic susceptibility of key ESKAPE pathogens, as defined by the WHO)10,11: Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Escherichia coli (representing Enterobacter spp.), as well as Enterococcus faecalis. Strains were categorized by the duration of hospitalization in the reporting facility ( < or > 48 h) and by the interval from injury to sampling ( < or > 72 h). Viable cultures meeting the criteria specified in Supplementary Table 1 were submitted to the RL. From April 1, 2023, to December 31, 2024, the quarterly hospital reports were received containing data according to these parameters. Data analysis and plotting were performed using OriginPro v.9.5 (OriginLab).

Sample collection and processing from infected wounds

Samples were collected during surgery or postoperative wound dressing. For abscesses, fistulas, gangrene, tissue necrosis, etc. the skin around the wound was treated with an antiseptic; necrotic masses, detritus, pus were removed and at least 1 ml of discharge was collected from the base of the lesion. Sample was taken from the depth of the affected area at the border with healthy tissue and placed in a sterile tube, transport medium, or syringe with a closed needle. A swab may exceptionally be used to collect pus when only a very small amount is present or when sampling from a sensitive anatomical area, such as the eye. If swabs were used, material was collected with two sterile swabs in circular motions from the wound center to the periphery until evenly soaked; one swab was used for bacterioscopy and the other for culture.

In cases of draining fistulas or inflamed lymph nodes, the material was carefully collected using a sterile Pasteur pipette and placed into a sterile tube. If there was no spontaneous drainage, the sample was obtained using a sterile syringe with needle, or probe; swabs were avoided. For burns, ulcers, erosions, and cellulitis, biopsy samples at least 3 mm in diameter were used. The dissected tissue pieces were placed in a sterile container with 1–3 drops of sterile 0.9% NaCl solution. In burns, microorganisms may not colonize the surface evenly, so sampling from several areas within the inflamed site is required.

Surgical samples (e.g., bone or soft tissue biopsies) are placed in a sterile container with 0.9% NaCl solution. If drains are present, 1–2 ml of secretion is aspirated with a sterile syringe into a sterile tube. Swab or container samples are stored at room temperature for no longer than 2 h.

Exudates from body cavities (e.g., pleural and abdominal cavities, knee joint) are collected by aseptic puncture and placed in a sterile container. If the patient has an open drainage, the drainage fluid for bacteriological examination is collected under aseptic conditions.

The sterile, tightly sealed primary container, which prevents aerosol formation, is placed in a bag with an absorbent pad and then in a transport container, with the referral placed in the designated pocket. Samples transported to the microbiological laboratory without nutrient transport media must be delivered immediately or within 2 h to preserve pathogen viability, prevent excessive microbial growth, maintain the original microbial ratio, minimize exposure to topical antiseptics with antimicrobial activity, and support accurate diagnosis and treatment evaluation.

If delivery within 2 h is not possible, transport systems are used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transport media preserve the viability of fastidious microorganisms for 48–72 h. Unless otherwise specified, specimens in transport systems are stored at room temperature (18–20 °C).

Blood and CSF sampling in suspected systemic and CNS infections

The main indications for bacteriological blood testing include the presence of two or more symptoms of systemic inflammatory response syndrome: fever of 38 °C or higher or hypothermia below 35 °C; heart rate exceeding 90 beats per minute; respiratory rate greater than 20 per minute; white blood cell count >12 × 109/L or <4 × 109/L, or the presence of >10% immature forms.

Blood samples (for hemoculture) are obtained from patients with burns if at least one of the following criteria is present: a change in the appearance or nature of the burn wound (e.g., rapid scab separation, dark brown, black, or purple discoloration of the scab, or edge swelling); the presence of at least two of the following signs or symptoms without any other identifiable cause: fever ≥38 °C or hypothermia ≤36 °C; hypotension; oliguria; hyperglycemia (blood glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L without changes in dietary carbohydrate intake); confused consciousness.

The blood culture kit contains one sample obtained by a single venipuncture and inoculated into two vials for aerobic and anaerobic culture; sampling should not delay antibiotic therapy initiation by more than 45 min. Blood is collected into two culture vials: one for aerobic/facultative anaerobes and one for anaerobes (or, two vials for aerobic/facultative anaerobic microorganisms). Blood is drawn from at least two sites (both accesses can be percutaneous, and one access may be percutaneous and the other through the port of a vascular catheter, placed ≤48 h before collection, except for the diagnosis of catheter-associated bloodstream infections). In suspected infective endocarditis, at least three sets (two vials each) were collected at ≥6-h intervals. The puncture site is disinfected with 70% ethanol (30 s), followed by iodine antiseptic or 2% chlorhexidine (30 s), and allowed to dry; vein palpation is permitted only with sterile gloves. Blood volume per draw was 10–30 ml in adults and ≥2 ml in children (Supplementary Table 2). Blood-to-culture medium ratio is 1:5 to 1:10 or as specified by the manufacturer of the blood culture vials. Blood cultures were transported to the laboratory as soon as possible after collection. If delayed, vials for automated culture systems were stored at room temperature, while those for manual processing were kept at 37 °C; refrigeration was not permitted.

CSF was collected by a clinician via lumbar puncture under strict aseptic conditions. The puncture was performed between the spinous processes of L4-L5 or L3-L4. The puncture site was disinfected with 70% ethanol (30 s), followed by the application of an iodine-containing antiseptic (iodopyrone or povidone-iodine), which was allowed to dry completely. A total of 3-4 ml of CSF was collected into three sterile tubes, with approximately 1 ml per tube. If an adequate volume could not be obtained, collection of less than 1 ml was considered insufficient. Supplementary Table 3 outlines the recommended laboratory tests based on the number of tubes collected. Samples were delivered to the laboratory immediately for culture and were protected from cold exposure during storage and transport.

Identification of microorganisms in the Reference Laboratory

Microbial cultures submitted to the RL for confirmation were identified using the automated microbiological analyzers VITEK 2 Compact (BioMérieux, France) and MALDI Biotyper Sirius (Bruker Daltonics, Germany), according to the manufacturers’ instructions. For the VITEK 2 Compact system, a standardized bacterial suspension was prepared in 0.45% saline and inoculated into VITEK ID and AST cards specific to the organism group. The cards were automatically incubated and analyzed by the instrument, and the results were interpreted using the VITEK 2 software database.

Microbial identification with the MALDI Biotyper Sirius system was performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). A fresh colony was applied to a MALDI target plate, overlaid with α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid (HCCA) matrix, and air-dried. Spectra were acquired in linear positive mode, and identification was achieved by matching the protein profile to the Bruker reference database using Biotyper software.

Determination of antimicrobial susceptibility of microorganisms

Microbial susceptibility to antibacterial agents was evaluated using the automated VITEK 2 Compact system and the disc diffusion method, following EUCAST Guidelines20. Colistin susceptibility was assessed by the broth microdilution method using a commercial test system (ComASP Colistin kit, Liofilchem, Lot 101923504).

Presence of extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBL), AmpC, and carbapenemases

Enterobacterales isolates were tested for extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBL), AmpC β-lactamases, and carbapenemases (including K. pneumoniae carbapenemase, metallo-β-lactamases, and class D OXA-48-type β-lactamases) using the combined disk method in accordance with EUCAST guidelines. ESBL (Lot 003), KPC, MBL, OXA (Lot 002), and AmpC (Lot 003) kits were employed (Sanimed-M, Ukraine). The testing algorithm differentiates between metallo-β-lactamases, class A and D carbapenemases, and non-carbapenemase mechanisms (ESBL and/or AmpC combined with porin loss). All assays were performed according to the EUCAST disk diffusion method for non-fastidious organisms20, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Ethical considerations

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with applicable ethical standards and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board of the Public Health Center of the Ministry of Health of Ukraine (IRB expertise No. IRB2025-155), which determined that the study was exempt from full ethical review and waived the requirement for informed consent and further IRB oversight (approval No. 483, dated 27 March 2025).
