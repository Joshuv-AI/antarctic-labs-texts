Divergence in MiRNA targeting of AchAco and its role in citrate accumulation in kiwifruit
Authors: Li-Zhen Ling, Xiao-Di Hu, Shu-Dong Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMicroRNA (miRNA) is a crucial post-transcriptional regulatory factor in plant growth and development. Duplicated genes often exhibit functional divergence due to competition for the identical miRNA binding sites. Kiwifruit (Actinidia spp.) is an economically significant horticultural crop renowned for its distinctive flavor, which is largely attributable to elevated citrate levels during fruit development. However, the mechanisms through which miRNA-targeted modules evolve following duplication events and regulate citrate biosynthesis, thereby influencing the unique flavor profile of kiwifruits, remain poorly understood.ResultsIn this study, we examined the expression patterns of miRNAs and interactions with their targets in kiwifruit fruit samples from various pulp tissues and developmental stages. Our analysis identified 46 miRNAs, comprising 44 known miRNAs and two novel/kiwifruit-specific miRNAs, which targeted a total of 1,474 genes. Correlation analysis revealed weak relationships between the expression levels of miRNAs and their target genes. Among these targets, 27 tandemly duplicated genes, and 782 whole genome duplication (WGD) genes exhibited a loss of miRNA binding sites in one of their duplicated copies. Furthermore, weighted gene co-expression network analysis demonstrated that most duplicated genes clustered into distinct gene modules. These findings suggest that the loss of miRNA targets following duplications contributes to expression divergence among gene duplicates, thereby facilitating stable gene expression within the miRNA-targeted network. For instance, the aconitate hydratase genes AchAco4 and AchAco6 were each targeted by different miRNAs, ach-miR-3774 and ach-miR-10371, respectively. Notably, these genes exhibited distinct expression patterns compared to their duplicated counterparts.ConclusionsThis study enhances our understanding of how the miRNA-AchAco module regulates citrate content and provides insights into the molecular network that influences the flavor profile of kiwifruit.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05877-w.Keywords: MicroRNA, Duplicated genes, Kiwifruit, Citrated acid

Background

Gene expression during plant fruit growth and ripening is regulated at multiple levels [1–3]. Among these levels, non-coding RNA has emerged as a crucial post-transcriptional regulatory mechanism, garnering significant attention in recent years. Non-coding RNAs are categorized based on their transcript length into small non-coding RNAs (snRNAs) ranging from 18 to 24 nucleotides (nt) and long non-coding RNAs, which exceed 200 nt in length. Within this classification, microRNAs (miRNAs), a subset of snRNAs, exert pivotal roles in plant development by either repressing translation or inducing degradation of their mRNA targets [4, 5]. Unlike other snRNAs, which are derived from double-stranded RNAs and modulated by RNA-dependent RNA polymerase 6 (RDR6) [6], RDR2 [7], or antisense mRNAs [8], miRNA precursor RNAs form hairpin structures through the action of DICER-LIKE 1 (DCL1) gene [9, 10]. Recent studies have unveiled specific interactions between miRNAs and their target genes that are crucial for plant fruit development. For example, miR858 interacts with MYB9/MYBPA1 to influence apple anthocyanin biosynthesis [11], miR164a targets NAM2/3 to affect tomato ripening [12], and miR393 regulates peach fruit softening via auxin signaling by targeting TIR1 [13].

The sequences of the miRNA family are conserved across various plant species, underscoring their evolutionary significance [14, 15]. Additionally, species-specific and tissue-specific miRNA expression patterns have been associated with species divergence and phenotypic development [16, 17]. Operating through complementary base pairing, a single miRNA can target multiple mRNAs, rendering these target genes competitors within the miRNA regulatory network. Recent advances in miRNA biology have introduced the concept of competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) regulatory networks, in which genes and non-coding RNAs compete for interaction with shared miRNAs, thereby influencing critical processes in plant morphogenesis and development [18, 19]. Several studies on competing endogenous RNA networks have revealed their roles in responses to abiotic stresses [20, 21]. In the context of plant evolution, most flowering plant genomes have undergone one or more whole genome duplication (WGD) events as a means of adapting to environmental challenges [22, 23]. In addition to WGD events, small-scale duplication events frequently occur in plant genomes [24, 25]. These events generate a diverse array of duplicated genes, which can be divided into four groups: WGD, dispersed, proximal, and tandem duplicated genes. Consequently, duplicated genes with similar sequences may be targeted by the same miRNA, potentially leading to competitive interactions among these genes. Natural selection pressures on genomic sequences, expression patterns, and DNA methylation modifications vary among different types of duplicated genes [26, 27]. Nevertheless, the specific role of miRNAs in regulating the expression patterns of these duplicated genes in plants remains largely unclear.

Kiwifruit (Actinidia spp.) is a significant agricultural crop renowned for its distinct flavor profile. Studies have highlighted the pivotal role of key transcription factors in regulating fruit growth and development [1, 28, 29], with sugars and organic acids contributing to this unique flavor profile [1, 30]. Aconitate hydratase (Aco) functions as a bidirectional catalytic enzyme within the citrate metabolic pathway, with the expression of Aco genes correlating with citrate levels in plant fruits [31–33]. However, despite the availability of high-quality genomic data for various kiwifruit cultivars/species, the precise relationship between Aco genes and citrate content in kiwifruit has not yet been elucidated [34, 35]. Besides, the role of miRNAs in kiwifruit fruit growth and development has been insufficiently investigated. In this study, we conducted a comprehensive identification of miRNAs, analyzed their expressions across various developmental stages and tissues of the fruit, and elucidated their regulatory effects on target gene expression patterns using RNA-seq data. By assessing the differential regulation of miRNAs in duplicated genes through weighted gene co-expression networks, we aim to clarify the miRNA-AchAco regulatory networks that govern citrate content in kiwifruit fruits.

Results

Identification of miRNAs in kiwifruit fruit

Small RNA-seq data from inner and outer pulp tissues of A. chinensis cv Hongyang fruits, collected at 8, 10, and 12 weeks after full bloom, were utilized to identify miRNA genes. After removing adaptors and low-quality reads, an average of 26.6 million clean reads per sample were obtained from the 18 tissue samples, which were then mapped to the reference genome. On average, 5.8 million of these filtered reads (about 22.03% per sample) mapped successfully to the reference genome. The miRBase database was used to identify known miRNAs. A total of 46 miRNAs were identified, with most (44) exhibiting high conservation, while only two miRNAs were novel and unique to kiwifruit (Additional file 1: Table S1). Among the known miRNAs, three (ach-miR-11601, ach-miR-3730, and ach-miR-856) were found to be derived from multiple MIRNAs (miRNA genes), suggesting a potential association with whole-genome duplication. These miRNAs were distributed across 20 chromosomes of A. chinensis cv Hongyang (Additional file 1:Table S1).

The lengths of the identified mature miRNAs ranged from 17 to 25 nt, with a predominant length of 19 nt observed in fruit pulps (Additional file 2: Fig. S1). This finding contrasts with the majority of miRNAs in Botrytis cinerea-infected fruits, which are predominantly 21 or 24 nt in length [36]. Moreover, the frequency of the first nucleotide in miRNAs of varying lengths was analyzed (Additional file 3: Fig. S2). For miRNAs of 24 and 25 nt, there was a preference for ‘A’ as the first nucleotide, which is consistent with distributions observed in other plants [37, 38]. In contrast, no such bias was detected in miRNAs of 17 to 19 nt.

Distinct expression patterns of miRNAs

To investigate the expression patterns of miRNAs in fruit tissue samples from various developmental stages and tissue types, we calculated the read counts for each miRNA. The analysis revealed that 11 known miRNAs were silent across all tissue samples (Fig. 1a). In contrast, 35 miRNAs were expressed in at least one tissue sample (Fig. 1a). Differential expression analysis identified 13 miRNAs with significant expression changes across various comparisons (Fig. 1b). Notably, only three miRNAs (ach-miR-3130, ach-miR-54, and ach-miR-167) were differentially expressed between the 8-week and 12-week inner pulp samples, suggesting stable expression patterns of miRNAs across different developmental stages of fruit pulp. In addition, three miRNAs (ach-miR-3730, ach-miR-novel1, and ach-miR-novel2) were down-regulated in comparisons between inner and outer pulp tissues at the same developmental stage (Fig. 1b, Additional file 4: Table S2). Furthermore, ach-miR-167 was found to be down-regulated in the 10-week outer pulp compared to the inner pulp.

Identification and expression regulation of targeted genes

Utilizing well-annotated protein-coding sequences from the reference assembly and applying stringent screening criteria (see Material and Methods), we predicted 1,474 miRNA-target genes. Most of these genes were regulated by miRNAs located on different chromosomes (Fig. 2a) and a significant proportion of these targeted genes (1,261) were regulated by a single miRNA. Additionally, we identified four genes that were targeted by eight miRNAs, suggesting a competitive relationship among these miRNAs. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that these miRNA-targeted genes are significantly enriched in the categories of “carbohydrate metabolic process” (GO:0005975) and “transport” (GO:0006810) biological processes (Fig. 2b). These results suggest that miRNAs may play a vital role in shaping the specific flavor profiles of fruit by regulating the associated target genes.

To further investigate the expression patterns of targeted genes in the small RNA-seq samples, transcriptome data from the corresponding tissue samples were retrieved from the SRA dataset and mapped to the reference genome of ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit. Figure 2c illustrates the expression patterns of miRNA-targeted genes across various developmental tissue samples. Comparative analysis of gene expression across different conditions indicated a higher number of down-regulated genes in both mature fruits and outer pulp tissues (Additional file 5: Fig. S3). In addition, miRNA-targeted genes represented a minor proportion of the differentially expressed genes (Additional file 6: Fig. S4). We further assessed the impact of miRNAs on target gene expression by calculating the correlation coefficients of their expression patterns. Based on these coefficients, the interactions were categorized into four groups: strong negative (cor < -0.5), weak negative (-0.5 ≤ cor < 0), weak positive (0 < cor ≤ 0.5), and strong positive (cor > 0.5). It is worth noting that the percentages of targeted relationships exhibiting strong negative and strong positive correlations were low (Fig. 2d), suggesting that miRNAs do not fully repress their target genes nor completely lose regulatory control over their expression. Notably, most targeted relationships exhibited weak correlations between miRNAs and their target genes (Fig. 2d). We hypothesize that competitive interactions among target genes may contribute to the partial evasion of miRNA-mediated inhibition.

Distinct miRNA targets contribute to the functional divergence of duplicates

Given the sequence similarity of duplicated genes, miRNAs can target both gene copies simultaneously. However, the processes of neofunctionalization and subfunctionalization introduce sequence variations [39], which may lead to the addition or loss of miRNA binding sites on these genes. Herein, we identified both whole-genome and small-scale duplication events using the well-annotated gene loci from the kiwifruit reference genome. Genes were classified into five categories: singletons (3,814), dispersed duplicates (6,321), proximal duplicates (1,270), tandem duplicates (2,538), and whole-genome duplicates (WGD, 27,034). To identify highly confident duplicate gene pairs, we retained only those that were within the collinearity blocks for whole-genome duplicates and those classified as tandem duplicates. Among these filtered duplicates, 59 tandem duplicated gene pairs and 1,028 WGD pairs were targeted by at least one miRNA. However, our analysis revealed that 64% of tandem duplicated gene pairs and 81% of WGD pairs were not simultaneously targeted by miRNAs (Fig. 3a), suggesting that most duplicates lose miRNA target sites following duplication events. Furthermore, a higher proportion of WGD pairs were targeted by the same miRNA compared to tandem duplicated gene pairs (Fig. 3a), indicating that tandem duplicated genes undergo more pronounced sequence variations. In contrast, only 5% of WGD pairs exhibited distinct miRNA targets, suggesting that different miRNAs may play a role in driving the expression divergence of WGD genes (Fig. 3a).

We also constructed a weighted gene co-expression network to further investigate how miRNAs regulate the expression patterns of these duplicated genes. It was found that genes with similar expression patterns were clustered into the same gene module. The duplicated genes in different gene modules may exhibit distinct biological functions [40]. After excluding silent and stably expressed genes, we categorized 30,422 genes into 14 gene modules, each represented by a distinct color (Additional file 7: Fig. S5). Among these, eight gene modules were specifically associated with tissue types (Additional file 8: Fig. S6). For example, the midnight blue and tan modules were significantly associated with the 8-week outer pulp tissue of the fruit. We focused on duplicated gene pairs for which both genes were present in the co-expression network, comprising 1,050 tandem duplicated gene pairs and 20,009 WGD pairs. By comparing the gene modules of duplicated pairs, we observed that most duplicated genes derived from tandem (70.66%) and whole-genome (73.37%) duplication were clustered into different gene modules. This result suggests that duplicated genes exhibited varying expression patterns, indicating functional divergence. Furthermore, we identified 27 tandem duplicated genes and 782 WGD genes that were targeted by miRNAs. Among the miRNA-targeted tandem duplicated gene pairs, only five pairs were targeted by the same miRNA and clustered into the same module. The majority (77.27%) of miRNA-targeted tandem duplicated gene pairs, where one gene lost the miRNA target site, were allocated to different modules within the co-expression networks. Similarly, 70.8% of miRNA-targeted WGD gene pairs that lost miRNA regulation were also clustered into different modules (Fig. 3b). We found that a significantly greater number of WGD pairs targeted by the same or different miRNAs clustered into the same modules compared to those that lost miRNA targeting (chi-square test, p-value < 0.01) (Fig. 3b). Consequently, miRNAs contributed to the conserved expression patterns of WGD genes, even when different miRNAs were involved. Collectively, our results suggest that distinct miRNA targets in duplicated genes are associated with their expression divergence.

The miRNA regulatory network of AchAco affects the citrate content of fruits

A previous study demonstrated that citrate content in fruits increases gradually during the 8–12 weeks following full bloom [28]. Given the crucial regulatory roles of Aco genes in other plants [31–33], we focused our investigation on the AchAco genes in kiwifruits. Consequently, we identified 10 candidate AchAco copies based on gene functional annotations and orthologous analyses. We then constructed a phylogenetic tree comprising ten AchAco and four AthAco genes utilizing the Aco genes from the model plant Arabidopsis as a reference (Additional file 9: Table S3 and Additional file 10: Fig. S7). Our analysis revealed two primary clusters of AchAco genes that were distinct from AthAco genes (Additional file 10: Fig. S7). Additionally, miRNA target analysis showed that two AchAco genes (AchAco4 and AchAco6) were specifically targeted by ach-miR-3774 and ach-miR-10371, respectively (Additional file 10: Fig. S7). Interestingly, these two genes were not collinear, and their whole genome duplication was not targeted by miRNAs. The correlation coefficients for these target relationships were 0.34 for ach-miR-3774 and AchAco4 and − 0.1 for ach-miR-10371 and AchAco6. Given the miRNA target inhibition and negative regulation, these weak correlations suggest that AchAco genes are involved in miRNA regulatory networks. Consequently, we constructed miRNA regulatory networks for AchAco4 and AchAco6 (Fig. 4a, Additional file 11: Table S4), highlighting the competitive target genes from different color modules. Importantly, both AchAco genes clustered into the midnight blue module, which was significantly associated with the 8-week outer pulp tissue of the fruit. We found that most of the targeted genes for ach-miR-3774 (43 genes, 67.1%) and ach-miR-10371 (85 genes, 58.6%) also clustered into the midnight blue and tan modules (Fig. 4a, Additional file 11: Table S4). This finding indicates that the competitive target genes of AchAco4 and AchAco6 are primarily associated with the 8-week outer pulp tissue of the fruit. Furthermore, real-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) results indicated that both genes were expressed at higher levels in the inner pulp compared to the outer pulp across different developmental stages (Fig. 4b-c). In the same tissue, the expression levels of AchAco4 and AchAco6 decreased as the fruit developed. Consequently, less citric acid might be catalyzed into cis-aconitate, leading to an increase in citrate content in the kiwifruit [28].

Discussion

Since the onset of the 21st century, elucidating the mechanisms underlying the development of desirable flavors in kiwifruit has become a central focus for plant breeders. Consequently, there has been a surge in research investigating the sugar and organic acid content in kiwifruit, which has garnered significant attention from the scientific community [28, 34, 41]. Previous studies have identified key genes and transcription factors that regulate sucrose and citrate metabolism [28, 34]. The expression of genes involved in the endogenous biosynthesis of organic acids is complex and regulated by intricate networks rather than by the isolated genes [42–44]. MiRNAs, essential post-transcriptional regulators, have been shown to influence fruit growth and ripening processes [45–47]. Although earlier studies have elucidated the roles of miRNAs in various aspects, such as red flesh development [48], phosphatidic acid metabolism [49], and response to biotic stress [36], their contributions to the development of distinctive flavors in kiwifruit remain largely unexplored. In the present study, we analyzed a small RNA-seq dataset with the overarching goal of identifying the expression profiles of mature miRNAs.

High-throughput sequencing technology has been extensively utilized in miRNA studies across various species. The occurrence of false positives can be significantly reduced by comparing sequenced reads against miRNA databases and excluding their corresponding miRNAs. Herein, we performed a rigorous miRNA identification process, which identified 46 miRNAs with clearly defined MIRNA loci within the genomes. The relatively smaller number of identified miRNAs in this study, compared to other investigations of kiwifruit miRNAs, can be attributed to the tissue-specific nature of miRNA expression. Notably, two novel miRNAs (ach-miR-novel1 and ach-miR-novel2) were differentially expressed between the inner and outer pulp tissues of kiwifruit, suggesting a potential link between specific flavors and their unique regulatory mechanisms. Additionally, several differentially expressed miRNAs across various developmental stages of the fruit exhibited stable expression patterns, indicating consistent miRNA regulation during fruit growth. A previous study on cluster beans demonstrated that miR3130, which is involved in galactomannan metabolism, is down-regulated during fruit maturation [50]. A similar expression pattern was observed for ach-miR-3130 in maturing kiwifruit. Whether this miRNA gene plays a role in galactomannan metabolism warrants further investigation. Furthermore, miR-167 has been proposed to regulate the development of locular and placental tissues in tomato fruits [51] and to mediate responses to abiotic stresses [52, 53]. Our analysis revealed that ach-miR-167 was significantly down-regulated in the 12-week inner pulp tissue. Therefore, the role of this conserved miRNA-167 in regulating fruit growth warrants further functional verification. Our findings suggest that variations in the expression of species-specific miRNAs are associated with the development of kiwifruit fruits.

Endogenous competition among identical miRNAs plays a crucial role in tissue development and phenotypic variations [18, 54]. To further explore this, we investigated whether duplicated genes compete for miRNA targeting. Leveraging the telomere-to-telomere reference genome of A. chinensis enabled the identification of homologous collinear regions and duplicated genes arising from various duplication events [34]. Our findings indicated that in most WGD and tandem duplicated gene pairs, only one copy was targeted by miRNAs, suggesting a high mutation rate in miRNA target site sequences. Since tandem duplicates emerge more recently than those resulting from whole-genome duplication events [22], the reduced targeting by the same miRNA in tandem duplicated gene pairs may be associated with the maintenance of functional redundancy. Furthermore, our results highlighted how distinct miRNA targets contribute to the expression divergences observed between whole-genome and tandem duplicated genes. Notably, both types of duplicates were targeted by either the same or different miRNAs, which led to conserved tissue expression patterns. This supports the co-evolution of miRNAs and their targets, particularly in duplicated genes [55]. Collectively, these findings imply that miRNAs may influence the functional divergence of duplicated genes through distinct targeting mechanisms.

Post-transcriptional regulation of miRNAs in the citrate cycle is essential for various plant tissues during growth and development due to the diverse functions of their targets [56]. For example, in Medicago sativa, miR2587 regulates the citrate cycle by targeting phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase [57]. Our study is noteworthy for identifying the target models of ach-miR-3774-AchAco4 and ach-miR-10371-AchAco6, thereby elucidating how these miRNA-target interactions influence citrate content throughout kiwifruit growth. The Aco gene, which is pivotal in the citrate cycle, catalyzes the conversion of citrate into cis-aconitate [58]. We also constructed endogenous competitive networks of miRNA for these two AchAco genes and discovered that most of the targeted genes of ach-miR-3774 and ach-miR-10371 are associated with the 8-week outer pulp tissues of the fruits. Coupled with the high expression levels of AchAco4/6 in the 8-week outer pulp, our findings suggest that the interaction between these targets is most pronounced at this developmental stage. The down-regulation of AchAco4/6 inhibits the production of cis-aconitate, thereby increasing the citrate content as a raw material.

In conclusion, we conducted a systematic analysis of miRNAs across six fruit tissues at various developmental stages to identify the mature miRNAs present in kiwifruit. Our investigation revealed differentially expressed miRNAs and indicated a weak correlation between the expression levels of miRNAs and their targets. Notably, the miRNA target sites across the whole genome and tandem duplicated genes were largely distinct. Furthermore, we observed that the genes targeted by miRNAs often exhibit similar expression patterns within co-expression networks. In addition, our results demonstrated that miR-3774 and miR-10371 regulate citrate content through their interactions with AchAco4 and AchAco6. Overall, the insights gained from the miRNA-mRNA network in kiwifruit may enhance our understanding of citrate content in fruits.

Materials and methods

Plant material and RNA extraction

Fruits of A. chinensis cv Hongyang were collected at 56, 70, and 84 days (i.e., 8, 10, and 12 weeks) after full bloom. Notably, three biological replicates were sampled for each developmental stage. The fruits were sliced lengthwise to separate the inner and outer pulp tissues. These tissue samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted from each tissue sample using the RNAprep pure Plant Kit (DP432, TIANGEN, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The extracted RNA was used exclusively for qRT-PCR validation experiments.

Small RNA-seq analysis

A high-throughput small RNA-seq dataset was downloaded from the NCBI Short Read Archive (SRA) database under accession number PRJNA721028 28. This dataset includes 18 small RNA-seq samples collected from the inner and outer pulp tissues at 8, 10, and 12 weeks after full bloom. Low-quality reads and adaptor sequences were removed from the raw data, resulting in clean reads ranging from 17 bp to 29 bp in length. To identify the miRNAs, mature plant miRNA sequences were sourced from the miRBase database (version 24). Known miRNAs from other plants were extracted from the clean reads of kiwifruit samples using a Perl script within the miRDeep2 package [59]. The non-redundant hairpin sequences of the identified miRNAs were then mapped to the telomere-to-telomere reference genome of A. chinensis using the Bowtie [60], and the obtained mapping results were filtered with a threshold of 1e-5. Only miRNAs corresponding to the mapped hairpin sequences were retained and named according to their respective miRNA families as designated in miRBase. Subsequently, the clean reads from the kiwifruit samples were mapped to the reference genome to predict novel miRNAs using miRDeep2 [59]. Read counts were calculated for each tissue sample to quantify miRNA expression. Differentially expressed miRNAs between various developmental stages and tissue samples were identified using DESeq2, with a p-value threshold of < 0.05 and a log2(fold change) > 2 [61].

Identification of miRNA-targeted genes

Protein-coding gene sequences were extracted from the kiwifruit genome (http://kiwifruitgenome.org/). The potential interactions between miRNAs and these genes were evaluated based on sequence complementarity, identifying potential target relationships. An accumulated scoring system was employed to assess the credibility of these potential target relationships, considering nucleotide mismatches (scoring + 1 per position), sequence bulges or gaps (scoring + 1 per position), and G: U pairs (scoring + 0.5 per position). Target relationships containing more than one nucleotide bulge or gap were excluded from further analysis. A stringent threshold was established, where candidate miRNA-targeted sites with scores less than four were eliminated. Additionally, GO enrichment analysis of miRNA-targeted genes was conducted using TBtools based on the functional annotations of the identified genes.

Transcriptome analysis

To further investigate the expression patterns of miRNA-targeted genes across different developmental stages, RNA-seq samples corresponding to the same tissue samples used in the small RNA-seq analysis were retrieved from the SRA database under accession number PRJNA721028. The raw reads underwent quality control using Trimmomatic with default parameters. Clean reads for each tissue sample were then mapped to the reference genome using HISAT2 [62]. Gene expression levels were calculated based on well-annotated gene loci, using read counts and normalized to the FPKM (fragments per kilobase per million) values. Differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2, applying a p-value threshold of < 0.05 and a log2(fold change) > 2 [61]. For each miRNA-target relationship, the correlation coefficient (cor) of expression levels between miRNAs and their target genes was calculated using the same tissue samples. Based on the correlation coefficients, miRNA-target relationships were classified into four groups: strong negative (cor < -0.5), weak negative (0 > cor ≥ -0.5), weak positive (0.5 ≥ cor > 0), and strong positive (cor > 0.5). This analysis was conducted exclusively for expressed miRNAs and their corresponding target genes.

Classification of duplicated genes on the kiwifruit genome

Various genomic duplication events have contributed to the emergence of duplicated genes with similar sequences. These duplications can be categorized into four groups based on the spatial arrangement of duplicates within the genomic regions: whole-genome duplication (WGD)/segmental duplications, dispersed duplications, proximal duplications, and tandem duplications. To investigate genome duplication in kiwifruit, homologs were initially identified using BlastP with an e-value threshold of < 1e-5. These homologs were subsequently employed to delineate syntenic blocks using MCscanX [63]. Next, singleton genes and the four types of duplicated genes were identified by analyzing gene positions within the syntenic blocks. Furthermore, whole-genome and tandem duplicated gene pairs were validated by examining the corresponding syntenic genomic regions.

Co-expression network analysis

To examine the tissue-specific gene expression patterns during development, we constructed co-expression networks using 18 RNA-seq samples and performed analysis with the Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) R package [64]. Initially, genes with a silent expression (average FPKM < 0.1) or low variability (coefficient of variation < 0.1) were excluded. Subsequently, a correlation analysis was performed on the remaining gene expression matrix to generate a correlation matrix. A soft threshold was selected using the scale-free topology criterion to achieve a correlation exceeding 0.9, which was then employed to construct an adjacency matrix. Hierarchical clustering, combined with the dynamic tree-cut method, was subsequently utilized to identify gene color modules with a minimum module size of 200. For each gene module, the correlation coefficient between the module and the corresponding tissue was calculated. Tissue-specific gene modules were defined as those significantly associated with a specific tissue state, with a correlation coefficient > 0.5 and a p-value ≤ 0.01. Genes clustered within the same color module are presumed to exhibit co-expression interactions.

AchAco gene identification and phylogenetic analysis

Candidate AchAco genes were filtered based on the functional annotations of genes in the reference genome. All reported Aco protein sequences from Arabidopsis thaliana were retrieved from the TAIR11 database (http://www.arabidopsis.org). A. thaliana Aco (AthAco) protein sequences were aligned to the candidate AchAco genes using BlastP, with an e-value threshold of < 1e-5. The filtered candidate AchAco and AthAco protein sequences were aligned using MAFFT v7, and alignment positions with over 95% gaps were removed using Phyutility v2.7. A Neighbor-Joining tree was then constructed using MEGA, with 1,000 bootstrap replicates [65].

qRT-PCR verification of candidate genes involved in the citrate metabolic pathway

Specific primers for miRNAs and their target genes were designed using Primer Premier v.5.0 (Additional file 12: Table S5). For each tissue sample, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect kit (R333-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China), which was subsequently diluted three times for the qPCR assay. The qRT-PCR reaction system consisted of 0.4 µL forward primer, 0.4 µL reverse primer, 10 µL SYBR qPCR Master Mix, 2 µL cDNA, and ddH2O to achieve a total volume of 20 µL. The reaction was conducted at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s, utilizing the instrument’s default melting curve acquisition program (QuantStudio 6 Flex, Life Technologies, America). The relative expression levels of miRNAs and their target genes were quantified using the 2-△△CT method, with the actin gene (Achn107181) serving as an internal standard.
