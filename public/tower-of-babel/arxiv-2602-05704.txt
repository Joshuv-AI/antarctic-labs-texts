Limitations of SGD for Multi-Index Models Beyond Statistical Queries
Daniel Barzilai, Ohad Shamir

Abstract
Understanding the limitations of gradient methods, and stochastic gradient descent (SGD) in particular, is a central challenge in learning theory. To that end, a commonly used tool is the Statistical Queries (SQ) framework, which studies performance limits of algorithms based on noisy interaction with the data. However, it is known that the formal connection between the SQ framework and SGD is tenuous: Existing results typically rely on adversarial or specially-structured gradient noise that does not reflect the noise in standard SGD, and (as we point out here) can sometimes lead to incorrect predictions. Moreover, many analyses of SGD for challenging problems rely on non-trivial algorithmic modifications, such as restricting the SGD trajectory to the sphere or using very small learning rates. To address these shortcomings, we develop a new, non-SQ framework to study the limitations of standard vanilla SGD, for single-index and multi-index models (namely, when the target function depends on a low-dimensional projection of the inputs). Our results apply to a broad class of settings and architectures, including (potentially deep) neural networks.

Introduction

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) methods are the workhorse of modern machine learning algorithms, and are very successful in many practical applications. However, SGD is not universally effective, and
it has long been recognized that there exist learning problems on which it can fail (even on problems that are tractable with other methods; see for example
Shalev-Shwartz et al. 2017
). Rigorously understanding the complexity of gradient-based methods such as SGD remains an important open question in learning theory.

For such algorithm-specific complexity results, a standard and increasingly popular tool is the Statistical Query (SQ) framework
(
Kearns 1998
)
, which studies how certain problems are provably intractable with noise-tolerant algorithms. In that framework, the algorithm can only receive noisy estimates of various quantities associated with the data. This framework is frequently invoked as a proxy for SGD methods, based on the intuition that a stochastic gradient step effectively serves as a noisy estimate of the population gradient. As such, SQ bounds have been used in recent years to suggest computational and statistical complexity of various learning tasks
(
Feldman et al. 2017
;
Shamir 2018
;
Chen and Meka 2020
;
Song et al. 2021
;
Abbe et al. 2023
;
Diakonikolas and Kane 2023
;
Damian et al. 2024b
;
Joshi et al. 2024
;
Damian et al. 2025
)
. Consequently, SQ-based lower bounds are often viewed as a heuristic indicator for the limitations of gradient-based methods.

However, as noted in many of these works, the
formal
connection between SGD and the SQ framework is tenuous, due to differences in the gradient noise model. In the SQ framework, it is assumed that the noise can be adversarial (or at best, stochastic with a particular convenient structure, such as an isotropic Gaussian, see
Yang 2005
;
Abbe and Boix-Adsera 2022
;
Abbe et al. 2025
;
Shoshani and Shamir 2025
). In contrast, in actual SGD, this noise is the difference between the stochastic gradient on a particular data sample (or mini-batch) and the population gradient. This zero-mean noise vector is not adversarial, but also has a distribution that is data-dependent, generally anisotropic, and evolves throughout training. It is therefore notoriously difficult to analyze SGD directly. Thus, existing complexity results often apply to stylized variants of SGD, where it is assumed that some additional stochastic or adversarial noise is added to the gradient updates.

Moreover, although SQ-based lower bounds for SGD are believed to be indicative in many settings, there do exist settings where these bounds do not fully explain the performance of actual SGD. One interesting example is learning parities, namely functions of the form
\mathbf{x}\mapsto f_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}x_{i}\mod 2
over the Boolean hypercube.
Such functions are well-known to be efficiently learnable (via Gaussian elimination), but provably not by any SQ-based algorithm
(
Kearns 1998
)
. This is consistent with strong empirical evidence that parities are hard to learn using standard predictors trained using SGD (e.g.,
Shalev-Shwartz et al. 2017
;
Barak et al. 2022
;
Nachum and Yehudayoff 2020
).
However, more recently,
Abbe and Sandon 2020
;
Abbe et al. 2021
showed that parities
can
be learned with SGD, at least when used to train a certain (highly synthetic) neural network.
Thus, the SQ-based explanation for the hardness of learning parities with SGD appears to be incomplete.
Moreover, as we discuss in more detail in Appendix
A
, there exist several other natural learning problems, where the behavior of standard learning procedures is not correctly captured by the SQ framework, due to its adversarial noise assumption being too pessimistic. These include situations such as matrix mean estimation
(
Li et al. 2019
)
, tensor PCA problems
(
Dudeja and Hsu 2021
)
, sparse prediction, and more. This raises the concern that SQ-based predictions of the behavior of SGD may sometimes be inaccurate.
Beyond these issues, existing analyses for SGD often make additional non-trivial assumptions about the algorithm, such as restricting the parameter vector to a sphere, using a very small learning rate so that SGD is approximated by gradient flow, layer-wise training, etc. (see
(
Bruna and Hsu 2025
)
for a survey).

Motivated by all this, we consider the following natural question:

Can we rigorously understand why standard SGD (without SQ-based or other algorithmic modifications) on natural predictors struggles to solve certain learning problems?

In this paper, we consider this question for a well-studied family of high-dimensional settings, where the goal is to learn a single-index or a multi-index target function which depends on a low-dimensional linear subspace of the inputs. This topic has received much attention in recent years, including both upper and lower bounds for gradient-based methods, often invoking SQ machinery
(
Ben Arous et al. 2021
;
Barak et al. 2022
;
Mousavi-Hosseini et al. 2023a
;
Abbe et al. 2023
;
Bietti et al. 2023
;
Kou et al. 2024
;
Dandi et al. 2024
;
Simsek et al. 2025
;
Ren et al. 2025
;
Bruna and Hsu 2025
)
.
In such problems, achieving a small loss requires the predictor parameters to align with a small number of task-relevant directions.

Our main contribution is a new framework for deriving formal lower bounds for SGD in such settings. Sidestepping SQ-based arguments, we directly show that vanilla SGD over individual samples (without any modifications) can fail to build meaningful correlations with task-relevant directions over a long time horizon, despite the absence of statistical or representational barriers. This failure will, in turn, be used to provide lower bounds on the number of samples required by SGD to achieve a non-trivial loss. In more detail, our contributions are as follows:

•

In Section
4
, we present a general lower bound for learning multi-index models with standard SGD. The bound is based on formalizing the intuition that for difficult problems (where the gradients carry a weak signal about the target function), SGD fails to align with any specific task-relevant directions. To show this, we identify a concrete “gradient condition number” quantity, which ensures that the martingale processes governing the alignment evolve similarly to those of an isotropic random walk.
The bound we obtain applies to a wide range of architectures and problem settings, including neural networks with a linear first layer. We precede this with an informal discussion of our approach, presented in Section
3
.

•

In Section
5
, we provide several applications of our general result, yielding concrete lower bounds for SGD in various settings. We begin in Section
5.2
with periodic target functions (which can be seen as an extension of parity functions to continuous input distributions), and show that standard SGD on
2
-layer networks cannot learn these with samples/iterations polynomial in the dimension
d
. Previous results of this kind relied on SQ arguments and did not apply to standard SGD. We then consider single-index and multi-index target functions in Section
5.3
. We show that for a broad set of predictors, SGD requires
\tilde{\Omega}(d^{\max(k_{\star}-1,1)})
samples/iterations, where
k_{\star}
is the
information-exponent
of the target function. While such lower bounds were previously shown in SQ-based frameworks or for stylized variants of SGD, here we prove that such bounds apply to standard SGD.

Moreover, in Appendix
A
, we further discuss limitations of the SQ framework, presenting several examples where SQ-based arguments provably lead to overly-pessimistic predictions about the behavior of standard SGD.

Overall, we hope our results help to clarify when and why standard SGD struggles,
and provide insights that may guide the analysis and design of gradient-based algorithms.

Setting and Preliminaries

Predictor class.

We study the use of SGD for training predictors that depend on inputs
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
through a linear map. Formally, such predictors take the form

f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}):=h\left(W\mathbf{x}\,;\,\bar{\theta}\right),

(1)

where
h
is a differentiable function
1
1

1

One may also consider neural networks with activations like ReLU, which have a well-defined sub-gradient.
,
W
is a learned linear map (a matrix of size
m\times d
for a width parameter
m
),
\bar{\theta}
denotes optional additional parameters, and
\theta=(\bar{\theta},W)
. Crucially, all dependence on the inputs is through
W\mathbf{x}
. In particular,
f_{\theta}
may be a neural network of any architecture with a linear first layer (such as MLPs and transformers). Our general result does not depend explicitly on the specific structure of
h
, only on regularity assumptions about its gradients (formalized later).

Low-dimensional target function.

In many learning problems, the target function generating the output depends on the input only through a small number of task-relevant directions.
To capture this, fix an integer
p\ll d
and an unknown matrix
U\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
that defines the task-relevant directions.
We assume the target function depends on inputs
\mathbf{x}
only through the
p
-dimensional representation
U\mathbf{x}
:

f^{\star}(\mathbf{x})=\phi(U\mathbf{x}),

(2)

for some function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
.
We think of
p
as a small intrinsic dimension, while
d
is large. Target functions of this form are often referred to as
multi-index
models, or
single-index
if
p=1
. Some examples include generalized linear models and single neurons (for
p=1
); Intersection of half-spaces (or signed sparse parities)
f^{\star}(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\mathrm{sign}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{x})
for
\{\mathbf{u}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
; and neural networks with a width-
p
first layer, e.g.
U_{k}\sigma\left(U_{k-1}\left(\ldots,U_{1}\mathbf{x}\right)\right)
for weight matrices
U_{i}
and depth
k
.

Sample and population losses.

Let
\ell:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable loss function, which measures the discrepancy between a prediction and the target function’s output. Given an input
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and parameters
\theta
, this induces the
sample loss

\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x}):=\ell\left(f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}),f^{\star}(\mathbf{x})\right),

where in the above, we slightly abuse the
\ell
notation to take two different types of arguments (the meaning should be clear from context).
Let
\mathcal{D}
be a distribution over inputs
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The population loss is defined as

\mathcal{L}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x})\big].

Standard SGD.

Given a learning rate parameter
\eta>0
and initial parameters
\theta_{0}
, we consider parameters updated by vanilla stochastic gradient descent (SGD) on individual samples:

\theta_{t}=\theta_{t-1}-\eta\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta_{t-1};\mathbf{x}_{t}),\qquad\text{where }\mathbf{x}_{t}\overset{\scriptstyle{\text{i.i.d.}}}{\sim}\mathcal{D}.

(3)

While SGD updates all parameters of the predictor, our analysis focuses on tracking only the evolution of the first-layer weights
W_{t}
. Our lower bounds are based on the first layer weights
W_{t}
failing to capture the relevant directions in
U
. All other parameters are treated as part of the function
h
, and our theorems are applicable even if they are not updated using SGD, but rather via some other method (as long as the required assumptions hold).

Additional notation.

For
d\in\mathbb{N}
, let
[d]:=\{1,\dots,d\}
.
For a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
, we denote by
s_{1}(A),\ldots,s_{\min(m,d)}(A)

s_{i}(A)\geq s_{i+1}(A)

. We let

. We denote by

\mathrm{Row}(A)

P_{A}=A^{\dagger}A
. We use
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}
for the operator norm,
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{F}
for the Frobenius norm, and unless specified otherwise,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and
\left\|\cdot\right\|
denote the standard (Frobenius) inner product and norm.
Given filtrations
\left(\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)_{t=1}^{\infty}
, we write
\Pr_{t}(\cdot):=\Pr(\cdot\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
and for any random variable

. We use standard big-O notation, with
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)

3
Capturing Hardness for SGD

In this section, we informally describe the main ideas used to derive our results. We begin by noting that there are two broad scenarios in which SGD may struggle. The first involves a loss landscape with many poor local minima that are difficult to avoid. However, empirical and theoretical evidence suggests that either such landscapes do not arise naturally in many high-dimensional cases, or that the local minima are not too bad
(
Choromanska et al. 2015
;
Li et al. 2018
;
Safran and Shamir 2018
)
.
In contrast, SGD may struggle for the opposite reason: The loss landscape is
too flat
. In this scenario, optimization is hindered not by spurious minima but by noisy gradients containing insufficient signal.
More specifically, consider a case where the zero-mean
SGD noise
, defined as
\nabla\ell(\theta_{t-1};\mathbf{x}_{t})-\nabla\mathcal{L}\left(\theta_{t-1}\right)
, is large relative to the population gradients
\nabla\mathcal{L}\left(\theta_{t-1}\right)
.
In this case, the parameter updates are dominated by the SGD noise, and one may imagine that SGD behaves approximately as a zero-mean random walk in parameter space.
The main difficulty in formalizing such intuition lies in the structure of the SGD noise, which is data-dependent, anisotropic, and whose distribution evolves as the parameters change.

Alignment bounds the population gradient.

A key feature of our setting, and the central source of optimization complexity, is that the target depends
on
\mathbf{x}
only through a low-dimensional
task subspace
given by the row-span of
U
, which we denote by
\mathrm{Row}(U)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In contrast, the predictor we train accesses the input only through the

learned subspace

\mathrm{Row}(W)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Intuitively, if
\mathrm{Row}(W)
has little overlap with
\mathrm{Row}(U)
, then for high-dimensional isotropic input distributions,
W\mathbf{x}
is nearly uncorrelated with
U\mathbf{x}
.
As we will later show, in this regime, both predictions and gradients provide only a weak signal about the target, implying that SGD noise dominates the dynamics for many iterations. We capture this phenomenon through the
alignment

\rho(W,U)~:=~\|P_{W}P_{U}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\in[0,1]~,

where
P_{W}
and
P_{U}
denote the orthogonal projection matrices onto
\mathrm{Row}(W)
and
\mathrm{Row}(U)
, respectively. In particular,
\rho(W,U)
is the cosine of the smallest principal angle between the two subspaces, and is negligible unless
W
is well aligned with
U
. When
p=1
, the region where the alignment is larger than some threshold reduces to a (double) cone centered at
\pm\mathbf{u}
. Thus, SGD fails as long as the SGD trajectory does not enter this cone. We note that while alignment-based analyses have appeared in many prior works (e.g.
Ben Arous et al. 2021
;
Dandi et al. 2024
;
Ren and Lee 2024
), they are typically tailored to spherical SGD, where the scale of the weights plays no role. In such a case, the SGD dynamics depend entirely on the alignment, and the analysis reduces to analyzing the one-dimensional dynamics of the alignment. For standard SGD, the situation is more complicated.

Connection between SGD and isotropic random walks.

To explain the intuition of our approach, consider the case where the width parameter
m
equals
1
. In that case, the predictor matrix
W
reduces to a vector
\mathbf{w}
, and at iteration
t
, the gradient w.r.t. the current iterate
\mathbf{w}_{t-1}
is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{w}_{t-1}}\ell\left(\theta_{t-1};\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)=a_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}\qquad\text{where}\qquad a_{t}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial(\langle\mathbf{w}_{t-1}\mathbf{x}_{t}\rangle)}\ell\left(\theta_{t-1};\mathbf{x}_{t}\right)\in\mathbb{R}~.

(4)

Since the weights are updated by SGD (Eq. (
3
)), at time
T
they can be written as

\mathbf{w}_{T}=\mathbf{w}_{0}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}_{t-1})=\mathbf{w}_{0}-\eta\sum_{t=1}^{T}a_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}~.

Now, suppose for simplicity that the inputs
\mathbf{x}_{t}
are standard Gaussians in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and that the scalar coefficients
a_{t}
are fixed constants. In this idealized scenario, the iterates
\mathbf{w}_{T}
would form a zero-mean Gaussian random walk, the marginal distribution of
\mathbf{w}_{T}
would be isotropic, and as a result, the alignment
\rho(\mathbf{w}_{T}^{\top}\,,\,U)
would concentrate around
\sqrt{p/d}
. Since we assume
p\ll d
, it follows that with high probability, there would be no significant alignment and the algorithm would fail.

However, with actual SGD, the situation is much more complicated (even for Gaussian inputs). This is because the random variables
a_{t}
are generally non-constant, and very much depend on
\mathbf{x}_{t}
as well as on the previous inputs.
Our main technical contribution is to prove that, despite these dependencies, the idealized scenario described above still roughly holds in many cases.
In particular, if
\mathbb{E}_{t}[a_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}]=\mathbb{E}_{t}[\nabla_{\mathbf{w}_{t}}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})]\approx 0
(where
\mathbb{E}_{t}
denotes the expectation conditioned on past events) and if
a_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t}
are “sufficiently well-behaved” then we show that for any
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\frac{\left|\mathbf{w}_{T}^{\top}\mathbf{u}\right|}{\left\|\mathbf{w}_{T}\right\|}\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
. In this manner, if the alignment is small at initialization, then it remains small for many iterations, and the loss remains nearly trivial.

Gradient condition number

To quantify what “well-behaved” means, it turns out that it suffices for the coefficients
a_{t}
in Eq. (
4
) to not be dominated by rare heavy-tailed events.
Under this condition, the martingale processes governing
\mathbf{w}_{T}^{\top}\mathbf{u}
and
\left\|\mathbf{w}_{T}\right\|
resemble those of an isotropic random walk as described earlier, so that the alignment remains small. In our width-
1
setting, the norm of the weights grows roughly as
\left\|\mathbf{w}_{t}\right\|\gtrsim\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[a_{t}^{2}]\cdot d}
, whereas high-probability upper bounds on
\left|\mathbf{w}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{u}\right|
are on the order of
\sup|a_{t}|
(or more generally, the sub-Gaussian norm). Thus, when the ratio
\sup a_{t}^{2}/\mathbb{E}[a_{t}^{2}]
is of constant order,
\mathbf{w}_{t}
cannot align strongly with
\mathbf{u}
.
We identify a quantity
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle T}
(see Definition
10
), which we call the gradient condition number, that bounds the width-
m
analogue of this ratio.
We then show that the intuition above holds as long as
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle T}
does not scale strongly with the input dimension
d
, which is generally expected, since
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle T}
depends on an
m
-dimensional projection of the inputs rather than directly on the input dimension.
In practice, many tricks such as batch normalization, skip-connections, and adaptive learning rates are used to keep the stochastic gradients well-behaved.
This allows us to capture a wide range of possible architectures and predictors, where our dependence on the loss function
\ell
and the architecture
h
is only through
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle T}
.

4
General Lower Bound

We start by stating the assumptions under which we establish a general lower bound on the rate at which SGD can discover task-relevant directions.
Our goal here is not to restrict attention to a narrow class of predictors or losses, but rather to identify a small number of structural conditions that isolate the source of optimization complexity described in Section
3
.
In particular, the assumptions below are designed to
(i) prevent rare pathological events from dominating the dynamics, and
(ii) control the scale of the stochastic gradients, when the input dimension
d
is large, and in a manner that reflects what is common in practice. We begin with our assumptions on the input distribution:

Assumption 1

(Sub-Gaussian inputs with norm concentration)
.

(A)

The inputs
\mathbf{x}
are mean-zero, and there exists some
K_{1}>0
such that for any unit vector
\mathbf{v}
,

\forall\gamma>0,~\Pr\left(|\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{x}|\geq\gamma\right)~\leq~2\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{K_{1}^{2}}\right)~.

(B)

There exists some
\alpha_{2}>0,K_{2}>0
such that
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{2}\right]=\alpha_{2}d
, and

\displaystyle\forall\gamma>0,~\Pr\left(\left|\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{2}-\alpha_{2}d\right|\geq\gamma\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\min\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{K_{2}^{4}d},\frac{\gamma}{K_{2}^{2}}\right)\right).

Both conditions are satisfied, for example, for well-conditioned Gaussians and uniform distributions on the cube
\{-1,1\}^{d}
or the (scaled) sphere
\sqrt{d}\cdot\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. More generally, assumption
1.A
is standard in high-dimensional settings and ensures that all one-dimensional projections of the input are of constant order with high probability. Assumption
1.B
ensures that the overall scale of the input remains stable across samples, so that SGD updates are not dominated by rare large-norm inputs. In many common settings, this condition follows directly from Assumption
1.A
via the Hanson-Wright inequality
(
Vershynin 2025
)
.
We state it separately to emphasize its role in controlling the magnitude of stochastic gradients uniformly over time.
Finally, we note that the precise scaling of the inputs is not fundamental. Indeed, if we consider instead rescaled inputs of the form
c\cdot\mathbf{x}
for some
c>0
, then rescaling the learning rate to
\eta/c
for the weights
W_{t}
leads to the same SGD dynamics, and our guarantees can be translated accordingly.

Our next assumption concerns the initialization of the first layer weights. In practice, the weights are typically sampled i.i.d. from a Gaussian or a uniform distribution with variance roughly
1/d
. This ensures that
f_{\theta_{0}}
and its gradients are well-behaved at initialization, even when there are many parameters. The following assumption indeed holds for such standard initializations.

Assumption 2

(Standard initialization)
.

There exists some
K_{3}>0
such that the entries of
W_{0}
are i.i.d. random variables with zero mean, variance
1/d
, and

\displaystyle\Pr\left(|(W_{0})_{ij}|\geq\gamma\right)~\leq~2\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{dK_{3}^{2}}\right)~.

Such initializations do not encode any prior information about the task subspace
U
.
Consequently, when
d\gg m,p
, the alignment
\|P_{W_{0}}P_{U}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
is small at initialization with high probability. Furthermore, we note that the exact scaling of the coordinates is not fundamental for our results.

The final assumption controls the maximal scale of gradients with respect to
W\mathbf{x}
:

Assumption 3

(Bounded gradients)
.

There exists some
G>0
such that for any
\theta
and for (almost-surely) any
\mathbf{x}
,
\left\|\nabla_{W\mathbf{x}}\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x})\right\|_{2}\leq G
.

We emphasize that the gradient here is with respect to
W\mathbf{x}
, not the parameters
\theta
. Assumption
3
is satisfied in many common settings.
For example, it holds for neural networks with activation functions that have bounded derivatives (such as ReLU-type activations or tanh-type activations), provided the downstream weights (if any) remain bounded,
and for standard Lipschitz losses such as the logistic loss
\log\left(1+\exp\left(-f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})f^{\star}(\mathbf{x})\right)\right)
or correlation loss
-f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})f^{\star}(\mathbf{x})
.

The exact value of
G
is not important for our results, as it can be absorbed into the learning rate.
What matters is that typical gradient magnitudes are not negligible compared to this bound.
If
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[\|\nabla_{W\mathbf{x}}\ell(\theta;\mathbf{x})\|_{2}]\ll G
, then gradients may effectively be heavy-tailed, meaning that occasional rare events can have a significant impact on the SGD trajectory.
As discussed in the previous section, we therefore introduce the following quantity that will be used in our bounds:

Definition 1
.

Given any
T\in\mathbb{N}
, the
gradient condition number

\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle T}
is defined as

\displaystyle\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle T}:=\frac{G^{2}}{\inf_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\min_{t\leq T}\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\big(\mathbf{v}^{\top}\nabla_{W_{t-1}\mathbf{x}_{t}}\ell(\theta_{t-1};\mathbf{x}_{t})\big)^{2}\right]}~.

As before, we emphasize that the gradient is with respect to
W\mathbf{x}
, and is an
m
-dimensional vector. As such,
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle T}
may depend on the width parameter
m
, but importantly, it should not depend on the input dimension
d
. As we will see later, one can indeed show this occurs in many cases.
Representing the general result in terms of
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle T}
allows us to avoid specific assumptions about the predictor and the target function.

As discussed previously, our bounds will be based on the fact that as long as the task-alignment is small, then so is the population gradient.
We capture this dependence through an increasing function
\psi:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
such that

\|\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L}(\theta)\|_{F}\leq\psi\left(\|P_{W}P_{U}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\right)\qquad\text{for all }\theta.

In many structured learning problems,
\psi(r)
decays rapidly as
r\to 0
, reflecting the fact that
when the learned representation is nearly orthogonal to the task subspace, the population gradient is very small and
provides only a weak signal.
Concrete examples of such functions
\psi
will be provided in the next section.
We now state the main theorem, with the full proof appearing in appendix
B
.

Theorem 2
.

Under Assumptions
1
-
3
, let
\delta>0
,
\bar{\kappa}\geq 1
, and let
\psi:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
be an increasing function such that
\left\|\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L}(\theta)\right\|_{F}\leq\psi\left(\left\|P_{W}P_{U}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\right)
for all
\theta
. There exist constants
C,c,c^{\prime}>0
(that may depend on
K_{1},K_{2},K_{3},\alpha_{2},G
), such that if
d\geq c\bar{\kappa}^{2}m^{2}\log\left(\frac{Tdp}{\delta}\right)^{2}
,
\eta\leq\frac{c^{\prime}}{\bar{\kappa}^{2}\sqrt{md\log\left(Tdp/\delta\right)}}
and

T\leq\frac{1}{\psi\left(C\sqrt{\frac{\bar{\kappa}mp\log\left(Tdp/\delta\right)}{d}}\right)^{2}}~,

then conditioned on
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle T}\leq\bar{\kappa}
it holds with probability at least
1-\delta
that

\forall\,t\,\in[T]~,\qquad\left\|P_{W_{t}}P_{U}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C\sqrt{\frac{\bar{\kappa}mp\log\left(Tdp/\delta\right)}{d}}~.

The theorem shows that if the population gradient can be bounded using the alignment,
SGD fails to significantly increase alignment with the task subspace across many iterations.
In particular, until the alignment exceeds the scale
\Theta(\sqrt{\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle T}mp/d})
, the learned representation
remains effectively uncorrelated with the task directions.
The appearance of the scale
1/\sqrt{d}
reflects the typical alignment between random subspaces in
high dimensions, while the square arises from the accumulation of stochastic noise over time. We note that the theorem is stated using a value
\bar{\kappa}\geq 1
which serves as an inferred upper bound on
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle T}
.

The proof formalizes the intuition presented earlier in Section
3
.
In the following sections, we instantiate the function
\psi
for a variety of learning problems,
yielding explicit lower bounds on the samples/iterations required for SGD to begin discovering task-relevant
features.

In this section, we instantiate our general theorem to concrete settings, leading to more explicit bounds.

5.1
Hermite Polynomials

To study the efficiency of SGD with Gaussian inputs in various settings, it is often convenient to study the
Hermite expansion
of
f^{\star}
. We provide here a brief exposition on Hermite polynomials, and defer the reader to Appendix

for more details. To make our results applicable beyond single-index models and two-layer networks, we will need to work with the high-dimensional version of Hermite polynomials.
Let
\mu(\mathbf{x})
be the PDF of the standard Gaussian distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
L_{2}

can be represented (with convergence in
L_{2}
) as

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\langle H_{k}(\mathbf{x}),C_{k}\rangle\qquad\text{for}\qquad C_{k}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mu(\mathbf{x})}[g(\mathbf{x})H_{k}(\mathbf{x})].

5.2
Failure to Learn Periodic Functions in Polynomial Time

The simplest setting we will consider involves a special type of single-index models, where the link function is periodic. Concretely, we will consider a target function given by
f^{\star}(\mathbf{x}):=\sin\left(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)
where
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an unknown vector with possibly large norm. Such target functions serve as a prototypical example of functions difficult to learn under the SQ framework. In particular, paralleling the situation with parity functions, they are known to be exponentially hard to learn with gradient methods under (small)
adversarial
gradient noise (when
\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|^{2}
is polynomially large in
d
), even though they can be efficiently learned with non-SGD methods
(
Shamir 2018
;
Song et al. 2021
;
Damian et al. 2024b
)
. We also note that such functions can be seen as a generalization of parities to inputs in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, since a parity function on a subset
\mathcal{I}\subseteq d
coincides with the function
\sin(\gamma\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}x_{i})
on the Boolean hypercube, for an appropriate
\gamma>0
.

As to the class of predictors, we will consider here
two-layer networks (more general predictors will be considered later). Specifically, for any
t
let
\mathbf{w}_{t,1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{t,m}
be the rows of
W_{t}
, and suppose that
f_{\theta_{t}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma(\langle\mathbf{w}_{t,i}\,,\,\mathbf{x}\rangle)
.
The activation function
\sigma
is assumed to be well-behaved in the following sense:

Assumption 4
.

\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is differentiable (or sub-differentiable) with (1) bounded derivative
\sigma^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})\leq G_{1}
for
G_{1}>0
, a.s any
x
, and (2)
\inf_{s>0}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,s^{2})}[\sigma^{\prime}\left(x\right)^{2}]\geq G_{1}^{2}/K_{\sigma}
for
K_{\sigma}>0
.

As we discuss in Appendix
D.1
, standard activation functions such as ReLU, Leaky-ReLU, GeLU, softplus, and the (logistic) sigmoid all satisfy Assumption
4
.

Despite SQ-style results, to the best of our knowledge, it remains unknown whether periodic functions are indeed hard to train with standard SGD. In the following theorem, we show unconditionally that it is impossible to learn such functions using SGD with polynomially (in
d
) many iterations, on a predictor class of two-layer networks as described above.

Theorem 3
.

Let
W_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{md}\right)
,
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
,
\eta\leq 1/d
,
\ell
be the correlation loss and
f_{\theta_{t}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma(\langle\mathbf{w}_{t,i}\,,\,\mathbf{x}\rangle)
for
\sigma
satisfying Assumption
4
. Let
f^{\star}(\mathbf{x}):=\sin(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{x})
for some
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|=\sqrt{d}
. Then there exist
d_{0},C>0
which depend only on
G_{1},K_{\sigma}
, such that for any
d\geq d_{0}
it holds with probability at least
1-2m\exp\left(-d^{1/3}\right)
that

\displaystyle\forall\,t\,\leq\exp\left(d^{1/3}\right)~,\qquad\left|\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})-\mathcal{L}(\theta_{0})\right|\leq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\|\sigma(\langle\mathbf{w}_{t,i}\,,\,\mathbf{x}\rangle)\right\|_{{L_{2}}}+\left\|\sigma(\langle\mathbf{w}_{0,i}\,,\,\mathbf{x}\rangle)\right\|_{{L_{2}}}}{\exp\left(Cd\right)}.

The proof can be found in Appendix
D.3
, and follows from Theorem
2
. For the proof intuition, consider for simplicity the case of
m=1
, so that
f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})=\sigma(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x})
. We bound
\nabla\mathcal{L}
as

\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\right\|\leq\psi\left(\left\|P_{\mathbf{w}^{\top}_{t}}P_{\mathbf{u}^{\top}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\right)\qquad\text{where}\qquad\psi(\rho):=(1+\sqrt{d})\exp\left(-\frac{d}{2}\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)\right).

Moreover, we show that in the above setting,
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle T}\lesssim K_{\sigma}
.
Theorem
2
then implies that the alignment remains on the order of
1/\sqrt{d}
for at least
\exp(d^{1/3})
steps. This, in turn, implies the loss remains exponentially close to the loss at initialization.

The choice of exponent
1/3
is not fundamental to the result; the theorem can be proven with a lower bound of
\exp(d^{\alpha})
for any
\alpha<1/2
. Lastly, we note that the activation function can be taken to be
\sin
, which satisfies the assumption of the theorem, and the predictor may be
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sin(\mathbf{w}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{x})
. In this case, each neuron matches the form of the target function, and learning is impossible in polynomial time despite overparameterization (as long as the width is at most polynomial in
d
).

5.3
Information Exponent Based Bounds

We now turn to a different source of complexity, based on a quantity known as the
information exponent

(
Ben Arous et al. 2021
;
Bietti et al. 2022
)
defined as the first
k\geq 1
such that the Hermite coefficients
C_{k}
of the target function
f^{\star}
do not vanish:

Definition 4

(Information exponent)
.

Let
f^{\star}(\mathbf{x})\in L_{2}(\mu)
, the
information exponent
of
f^{\star}
is

k_{\star}:=\min\{k\geq 1\mid\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mu(\mathbf{x})}[f^{\star}(\mathbf{x})H_{k}(\mathbf{x})]\neq\boldsymbol{0}\}.

The information exponent quantifies the lowest-order statistical interaction between the target and the input distribution. When
k_{\star}>1
, all correlations between
f^{\star}(\mathbf{x})
and polynomials of degree strictly smaller than
k_{\star}
vanish. It is by now well-known that the information exponent governs lower-bounds of the form
\tilde{\Omega}_{d}(d^{\max(k_{\star}-1,1)})
either for single-index models trained with spherical SGD, or in certain SQ-based frameworks (CSQ in particular, see
Ben Arous et al. 2021
;
Damian et al. 2022
;
Abbe et al. 2023
). Here, we show that the same bounds actually hold for standard SGD
2
2

2

With “pre-processing” labels
f^{\star}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
or other algorithmic modifications, one can obtain better bounds
(
Damian et al. 2024a
;
Damian et al. 2025
)
. However, as we focus here on standard SGD with the correlation loss, this is a different setting.
. For most functions,
k_{\star}\leq 2
, but there are nevertheless many cases where
k_{\star}>2
, and SGD provably struggles when the dimension is large. For example, if
f^{\star}(\mathbf{x})=\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}\rangle^{2}\exp(-\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}\rangle^{2})
for some nonzero
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
then
k_{\star}=4

(
Damian et al. 2024b
)
.

We note that this setting technically differs from that of periodic functions as studied in the previous section. For periodic functions, the information exponent is
k_{\star}=1
, and the hardness comes from the fact that the gradients are exponentially small in
d
when the target vector
\mathbf{u}
is of norm
\geq\sqrt{d}
. Here, in line with the existing literature on the information exponent, we will treat the magnitude of
\mathbf{u}
(or more generally
U
in the target function) as fixed independent of
d
, in which case the complexity does depend on the information exponent.

5.3.1
Single Index Models

We first consider here learning a single index model of the form
f^{\star}(\mathbf{x})=\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{x})
(for
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
) using two-layer networks
f_{\theta_{t}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma(\langle\mathbf{w}_{t,i}\,,\,\mathbf{x}\rangle)
. In the subsequent Section
5.3.2
, we will consider the general multi-index model and more general predictors, such as deep networks.

Notably,
Ben Arous et al. 2021
showed that for learning single index models with a small learning rate and spherical SGD, the information exponent characterizes the rate of convergence. However, their results require a small learning rate
\eta\lesssim d^{-\max(k_{\star}/2,1)}
, so that gradient descent behaves approximately like gradient flow (its continuous-time counterpart). It is well-known that the dynamics of SGD can differ significantly from those of gradient flow in many practical settings
(
Cohen et al. 2021
;
Andriushchenko et al. 2023
)
.
Furthermore, as demonstrated in
Mousavi-Hosseini et al. 2023b
, their simplification of spherical SGD can fail to capture the learning dynamics of standard SGD in some settings.
Recently,
Braun et al. 2025
were able to extend single-index
upper
bounds to standard SGD, highlighting that moving beyond spherical SGD is possible but entails highly nontrivial complications. The following theorem, proved in Appendix
D.5
, provides the desired
lower
bound for standard SGD.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\delta,\epsilon\in(0,1)
,
W_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{md}\right)
,
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
, and
\ell
be the correlation loss.
Let
f_{\theta_{t}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma(\langle\mathbf{w}_{t,i}\,,\,\mathbf{x}\rangle)
for
\sigma
satisfying Assumption
4
.
Let
f^{\star}(\mathbf{x}):=\phi(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{x})
for
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\left\|f^{\star}\right\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq G_{2}
,
\phi^{\prime}\in L_{2}(\mu)
and
f^{\star}
having information exponent
k_{\star}
.
There exist
d_{0}=\tilde{\Theta}\left(k_{\star}^{2}\epsilon^{-2/k_{\star}}\right)
,
\eta_{0}=\tilde{\Theta}\left((dk_{\star})^{-1/2}\right)
and

T=\left(\frac{d}{\tilde{\Theta}\left(k_{\star}\right)}\right)^{k_{\star}-1}+\Theta\left(d\epsilon^{2}\right),

such that for any
d\geq d_{0}
and
\eta\leq\eta_{0}
it holds with probability at least
1-\delta
that

\displaystyle\forall\,t\,\leq T~,\qquad\left|\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})-\mathcal{L}(\theta_{0})\right|\leq\left\|f^{\star}\right\|_{{L_{2}}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\|\sigma(\langle\mathbf{w}_{t,i}\,,\,\mathbf{x}\rangle)\right\|_{{L_{2}}}+\left\|\sigma(\langle\mathbf{w}_{0,i}\,,\,\mathbf{x}\rangle)\right\|_{{L_{2}}}\right)\cdot\epsilon.

Here,
\Theta
hides constants depending on
G_{1},G_{2}
,
K_{\sigma}
,
\left\|f^{\star}\right\|_{L_{2}}
and
\left\|\nabla f^{\star}\right\|_{L_{2}}
, and
\tilde{\Theta}
additionally hides polylogarithmic dependencies on the parameters (including
1/\delta
and
m
).

In words, for vanilla SGD, the sample complexity is governed by the information exponent, where roughly
\tilde{\Omega}_{d}(d^{\max(k_{\star}-1,1)})
iterations are needed for SGD to converge. This matches the bounds of
Ben Arous et al. 2021
for spherical SGD and small learning rates. The proof follows from the more general Theorem
6
that will be presented in the next subsection. Compared with more general architectures, here we leverage the fact that the dynamics of each neuron in the predictor are uncorrelated, and that Assumption
4
causes
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle T}
to remain bounded.

5.3.2
Multi-Index Models and Deep Network Predictors

In this section, we consider more generally learning target functions of the form
f^{\star}(\mathbf{x})=\phi(U\mathbf{x})
for
U\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
,
p>1
, using general predictors of the form

Theorem 6
.

Let
\delta,\epsilon\in(0,1)
,
\bar{\kappa}\geq 1
,
W_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{md}\right)
,
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
, and
\ell
be the correlation loss.
Let
f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}):=h(W\mathbf{x}\,;\,\bar{\theta})

\left\|\nabla_{W\mathbf{x}}h\right\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq G_{1}
.
Let
f^{\star}(\mathbf{x}):=\phi(U\mathbf{x})
for

, where
\left\|f^{\star}\right\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq G_{2}
,
\nabla\phi\in L_{2}(\mu)
, and

has information exponent
k_{\star}
.
There exist

d_{0}=\tilde{\Theta}\left(\bar{\kappa}^{2}m^{2}p^{2}k_{\star}^{2}\,\epsilon^{-2/k_{\star}}\right)

\eta\leq\eta_{0}

\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle T}\leq\bar{\kappa}

\displaystyle\forall\,t\,\leq T~,\qquad\left|\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})-\mathcal{L}(\theta_{0})\right|\leq\left\|f^{\star}\right\|_{{L_{2}}}\left(\left\|f_{\theta_{t}}\right\|_{{L_{2}}}+\left\|f_{\theta_{0}}\right\|_{{L_{2}}}\right)\cdot\epsilon.

Here,
\Theta

and

\tilde{\Theta}
additionally hides polylogarithmic dependencies on the parameters (including

The theorem shows that standard SGD generally requires
\tilde{\Omega}_{d}(d^{\max(k_{\star}-1,1)})

f^{\star}(\mathbf{x}):=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\varphi(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{x})

\varphi

\tilde{\Omega}_{d}(d^{\max(p-1,1)})

Compared with the previous results, the price we pay for this added generality is twofold.
First, we require

Second, we can no longer unconditionally bound the gradient condition number
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle T}
, as we did for two-layer networks. Nevertheless, as discussed in Section
3
, it is reasonable to assume that this parameter (which depends on the
m

W\mathbf{x}

, which is enough for our results. We also note that if the predictor is a two-layer network,

To prove the theorem, we first show in Lemma

\left\|\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L}(\theta)\right\|_{F}\leq\psi\left(\left\|P_{W}P_{U}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\right)\qquad\text{where}\qquad\psi(r):=G_{1}\left\|\nabla f^{\star}(\mathbf{x})\right\|_{{L_{2}}}\left(\sqrt{m}r+1\right)r^{k_{\star}-1}~.

Intuitively, the SGD noise is larger than the “signal” given by

If we ignore all factors other than the dimension, Theorem

suggest that if

for all

6
Discussion and Some Open Questions

In this paper, we developed a framework for studying the complexity of standard vanilla SGD when learning high-dimensional single-index and multi-index models. Unlike SQ-based results, which rely on noise assumptions that may not hold in practice, our analysis directly addresses the data-dependent, anisotropic noise of SGD, and contributes to the broader understanding of computational-statistical gaps in high-dimensional learning.

A natural direction for future work is to extend these techniques beyond vanilla SGD, and possibly, identifying sources of hardness applicable to all gradient-based learning methods. Another interesting direction is to explicitly bound the gradient condition number
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle T}
for more general architectures, and to understand whether the gradients tend to be well-behaved (as captured by
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle T}
) for “any” standard predictors. As a sanity check, we note that this does not seem to occur for the synthetic neural network construction of
Abbe and Sandon 2020
;
Abbe et al. 2021
, discussed in the introduction, which learns parities via SGD
3
3

3

Their construction involves data-memorization mechanisms, where gradients of certain neurons vanish at some time point, resulting in quantities such as
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle T}
diverging to infinity.
. However, understanding whether
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle T}
is large only for “pathological” constructions remains an open question. Finally, it would be interesting to extend this framework to other classes of learning problems beyond multi-index models.

Appendix A
Limitations of the Statistical Queries Framework

In this appendix, we discuss in more depth some limitations of the SQ framework for analyzing gradient-based learning methods, and SGD in particular.

As mentioned in the introduction, the Statistical Queries or SQ framework
(
Kearns 1998
)
is a well-established framework for studying noise-tolerant learning algorithms. In this model, rather than directly receiving data samples, it is assumed that the algorithm can only receive (approximate) expectations of various quantities associated with the data. In a nutshell, given an underlying data distribution
\mathcal{D}
, the algorithm can iteratively choose a query function
\phi_{t}
mapping to
[0,1]
, and receive a value
v_{t}
such that
|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{D}}(\phi_{t}(\mathbf{z}))-v_{t}|\leq\tau
, where
\tau
is a tolerance parameter. In this framework, one can often provide information-theoretic lower bounds on the number of queries or the required tolerance
\tau
in order to succeed in learning.

Although originally designed to study noise-tolerant algorithms, the SQ framework has also been increasingly used in recent years to provide evidence of computational and statistical complexity for various learning and estimation tasks (as discussed in the introduction). The underlying rationale is that a “natural” algorithm should be able to withstand some amount of noise. Thus, if SQ lower bounds imply that many statistical queries or a very small tolerance are required, this provides evidence that the runtime or sample complexity of “natural” algorithms will be large (to execute many queries or estimate expectations to sufficiently high accuracy from an empirical sample). In particular, stochastic gradient descent (SGD) has often been viewed as a “noise-tolerant” algorithm, in the sense that it iteratively descends along noisy approximations of the gradient of the population loss.

In contrast, algorithms that can circumvent SQ lower bounds are considered to be necessarily brittle and impractical. The classic example for this, as mentioned in the introduction, is learning parity functions
\mathbf{x}\mapsto f_{I}(\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}~\text{mod}~2
for
\mathbf{x}
in the Boolean hypercube and
I\subseteq[d]

(
Kearns 1998
)
. Given
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d)
i.i.d. samples
(\mathbf{x},f_{I}(\mathbf{x}))
, where
\mathbf{x}
is sampled uniformly at random,
f_{I}
can be identified in polynomial time by reducing the problem to solving a set of linear equations over the GF(2) field, using Gaussian elimination. However, such an algorithm is extremely noise-intolerant and does not resemble any standard learning method. Moreover, it is well-known that noise-tolerant algorithms (that can be simulated in the SQ framework) provably cannot solve this problem. More recently,
Abbe and Sandon 2020
;
Abbe et al. 2021
showed that parity learning can also be solved with SGD on a certain neural network, but the architecture is extremely unnatural (causing SGD to simulate arbitrary Turing machines, and Gaussian elimination in particular). So again, it seems that SQ lower bounds can be circumvented only via “unnatural” methods.

However, as acknowledged in many papers on this topic, the connection between SQ lower bounds and computational/statistical complexity is merely a heuristic: We have no formal theorem establishing that SQ lower bounds necessarily apply to all “natural” algorithms commonly used in practice, such as SGD. Moreover, SQ lower bounds rely on the noise being adversarial (or at best, stochastic with some particular convenient structure). This generally differs from the noise encountered by such algorithms on actual samples.

To further motivate our research question, we note that there are already a few examples in the literature where SQ-based lower bounds provably mis-predict the complexity of various learning and estimation problems when using standard gradient-based methods. In particular:

•

Li et al. 2019
provides SQ lower bounds on the problem of estimating the mean of random matrices, when the estimation error is measured in terms of Schatten-
p
norms. In particular, Theorem 19 in that paper implies that estimating the mean of a random
d\times d
matrix in the unit ball up to some fixed error (all with respect to the operator norm), using statistical queries, requires either exponentially many (in
d
) queries, or the error tolerance is
1/\text{poly}(d)
, implying
\text{poly}(d)
predicted sample complexity. However, by the matrix Hoeffding bound
(
Tropp 2012
)
, the average of only
\mathcal{O}(\log(d))
samples suffices to approximate the mean (in operator norm) up to any constant error. Moreover, SGD can easily simulate the computation of the average (say by applying it on the stochastic problem
\min_{W}\mathbb{E}[-\text{trace}(WX)]
for
\mathcal{O}(\log(d))
iterations with an appropriate step size). Thus, we see that SQ lower bounds predict
\text{poly}(d)
sample complexity for this problem, whereas the actual sample complexity is
\mathcal{O}(\log(d))
, easily attainable with SGD.

•

Dudeja and Hsu 2021
discuss SQ lower bounds for tensor PCA problems. A specific instantiation of their results is for the so-called spiked Wigner model: Namely, we are given i.i.d. samples of a
d\times d
random matrix, whose distribution is

\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\top}+\Xi

for some unknown unit vector
\mathbf{v}
and where
\Xi
has i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries. The paper presents an SQ lower bound that implies a predicted sample complexity of order
d^{2}
. However, the correct sample complexity, both information-theoretically and via gradient-based methods, is only of order
d
. As the authors discuss in their paper, the issue appears to be the adversarial nature of the noise assumed in SQ lower bounds, which does not correctly capture the noise structure encountered by actual stochastic gradient methods for this problem. In particular, by considering gradient-based methods on the empirical version of the problem,
\max_{\mathbf{w}:\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|\leq 1}\mathbf{w}^{\top}\tilde{X}\mathbf{w}
(where
\tilde{X}
is the empirical matrix average), gradient-based methods will generally initialize close to the saddle point at the origin, where the gradient tends to be small, and hence can be zeroed out with adversarial perturbations, preventing any information about
\mathbf{v}
to leak. However, for analyzing actual gradient methods on the empirical average, the noise has a more favorable structure, which leads to faster convergence

•

In a somewhat different direction, recent literature on single-index and multi-index learning used correlational statistical query (CSQ) arguments (a weaker variant of the SQ framework), to argue about the tightness of the information exponent
(
Ben Arous et al. 2021
)
in characterizing the complexity of learning such problems using gradient methods. However, as noted in
Dandi et al. 2024
;
Joshi et al. 2024
;
Lee et al. 2024
, these lower bounds can be easily circumvented by making small modifications to the gradient-based learning procedure, leading to faster convergence. While only showing an issue with the CSQ framework, which is weaker than SQ, it does show how lower bounds using SQ-based arguments can be brittle and depend on the precise assumptions used.

In what follows, let us introduce yet another, self-contained example of how SQ-based lower bounds can lead to incorrect predictions for simple supervised learning problems. To that end, it will be convenient to slightly expand the SQ framework to vector-valued queries (so it can directly capture, e.g., computation of stochastic gradients). Concretely, given some tolerance parameter
\tau
, we assume that the algorithm can iteratively choose a query function
\phi
, mapping training examples
(\mathbf{x},y)
to the Euclidean unit ball, and get a vector
\mathbf{v}
such that

\left\|\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)}[\phi(\mathbf{x},y)]-\mathbf{v}\right\|_{2}\leq\tau~.

Now, let us consider specifically the Boolean function class

\mathcal{F}=\{f_{i}(\mathbf{x})=x_{i},i\in[d]\}~~,~~\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{d}:=\{-1,+1\}^{d}~.

Moreover, let
\mathcal{D}_{i}
be the distribution of
(\mathbf{x},y)=(\mathbf{x},f_{i}(\mathbf{x}))
, where
\mathbf{x}
is uniform on
\mathcal{X}_{d}
.

Since our function class contains only
d
candidate target functions, it is easy to see that information theoretically, only
\mathcal{O}(\log(d))
samples from any
\mathcal{D}_{i}
are required to identify
f_{i}
. Moreover,
Amid and Warmuth 2020
show that using a simple sparsely-connected two-layer neural network, SGD on the squared loss identifies
i
after
\mathcal{O}(\log(d))
iterations. However, as the following theorem shows, the SQ framework predicts
\text{poly}(d)
complexity, which is exponentially worse:

Theorem 7
.

For any
\tau\in(0,1)
, there is an SQ oracle as above, such that any SQ algorithm with tolerance
\tau
will require at least
\frac{1}{2}\tau^{2}d
queries to identify
f_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
with probability
\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{d}
.

In particular, any choice of
\tau
will imply either
\mathrm{poly}(1/d)
tolerance (leading to predicted sample complexity polynomial in
d
, in order to estimate the expectation up to that tolerance), or
\mathrm{poly}(d)
queries (again leading to predicted sample complexity polynomial in
d
, for SGD methods). The proof is based on standard SQ arguments and is presented in the following section of the appendix (Appendix
A.1
).

Remark 8
.

A stronger oracle common in the SQ literature, namely the VSTAT oracle, requires the tolerance to be smaller if the query tends to have small values, to capture the fact that the expectation of random vectors with small moments can be estimated more accurately. In our vector-valued setting, such a requirement can take the form

\left\|\mathbb{E}[\phi(\mathbf{x},y)]-\mathbf{v}\right\|\leq\tau\cdot\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\left\|\phi(\mathbf{x},y)^{2}\right\|]}
.
It can be easily verified that the theorem and its proof apply directly, even to such a stronger oracle.

The reason that this SQ framework leads to a wrong prediction is that its Euclidean geometry is not commensurate with the geometry of the stochastic noise in this particular problem. One way to see this is to consider perhaps the most natural statistical query for this problem, namely
\phi(\mathbf{x},y)=y\mathbf{x}/\sqrt{d}
. Assuming the target function is
f_{i}(\mathbf{x})=y=x_{i}
for some
i
, we observe that
\phi(\mathbf{x},y)
is a random vector, whose
i
-th coordinate is
1/\sqrt{d}
with probability one, whereas all the other coordinates are independent and uniform on
\{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}},+\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\}^{d}
. Thus, averaging
\phi(\mathbf{x},y)
over
\mathcal{O}(\log(d))
samples and picking the coordinate which equals
1
will identify
i
with high probability. However, the SQ framework we use here views
\phi(\mathbf{x},y)
as a random vector in the unit Euclidean ball, where we need to identify its mean from several possible alternatives, each only
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{d})
-far from the origin (in Euclidean distance). In general, estimating the mean of a random vector in the unit Euclidean ball up to accuracy
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{d})
, requires
\Omega(d)
samples. However, this is tight only in the worst-case over all such distributions, and ignores the fact that for our particular problem, the random vector has a more specific structure, which leads to a much better sample complexity.

Proof of Theorem
7

The proof relies on well-known SQ techniques, but we present it in a self-contained way for completeness. In what follows, we let
\mathcal{D}_{0}
denote a “reference distribution”, which is simply the uniform distribution on
(\mathbf{x},y)\in\{-1,+1\}^{d+1}
.

Lemma 9
.

For any
\phi
as above,

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{i}}[\phi(\mathbf{x},y)]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}[\phi(\mathbf{x},y)]\right\|^{2}~\leq~\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}[\left\|\phi(\mathbf{x},y)\right\|^{2}]~\leq~1.

Proof.

It will suffice to prove the above in the case where
\phi(\mathbf{x},y)
is scalar-valued, namely

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{i}}[\phi(\mathbf{x},y)]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}[\phi(\mathbf{x},y)]\right)^{2}~\leq~\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}[\phi^{2}(\mathbf{x},y)]~.

(6)

The vector-valued case follows by applying and summing the inequality for each coordinate of
\phi(\mathbf{x},y)
, and noting that by definition of
\phi
, the overall squared norm of
\phi(\mathbf{x},y)
is at most
1
.

Let
p_{i}(\mathbf{x},y),p_{0}(\mathbf{x},y)
denote the probability density of
\mathcal{D}_{i},\mathcal{D}_{0}
respectively. Noting that the marginal distribution of
\mathbf{x}
is the same for
\mathcal{D}_{i},\mathcal{D}_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{i}}[\phi(\mathbf{x},y)]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}[\phi(\mathbf{x},y)]~

\displaystyle=~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\sum_{y}\phi(\mathbf{x},y)\left(p_{i}(y|\mathbf{x})-p_{0}(y|\mathbf{x})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\phi(\mathbf{x},1)\left(\mathbf{1}_{f_{i}(\mathbf{x})=1}-\frac{1}{2}\right)+\phi(\mathbf{x},-1)\left(\mathbf{1}_{f_{i}(\mathbf{x})=-1}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\frac{\phi(\mathbf{x},1)-\phi(\mathbf{x},-1)}{2}\right]~.

Letting
\psi(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{\phi(\mathbf{x},1)-\phi(\mathbf{x},-1)}{2}
and plugging in the above, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{i}}[\phi(\mathbf{x},y)]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}[\phi(\mathbf{x},y)]\right)^{2}~=~\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\psi(\mathbf{x})]\right)^{2}~\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(1)}}{{\leq}}~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[\psi^{2}(\mathbf{x})]

\displaystyle=~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\left(\frac{\phi(\mathbf{x},1)-\phi(\mathbf{x},-1)}{2}\right)^{2}\right]~\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(2)}}{{\leq}}~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\frac{\phi^{2}(\mathbf{x},1)+\phi^{2}(\mathbf{x},-1)}{2}\right]~=~\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}[\phi^{2}(\mathbf{x},y)]~,

where
(1)
is by Bessel’s inequality (observing that
f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}
are orthonormal functions in
L^{2}
space equipped with the standard inner product
\left\langle f_{i},f_{j}\right\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[f_{i}(\mathbf{x})f_{j}(\mathbf{x})]=\mathbb{E}[x_{i}x_{j}]=\mathbf{1}_{i=j}
), and
(2)
is by the elementary inequality
\left(\frac{a-b}{2}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{2}
. This implies Eq. (
6
), from which the lemma follows.
∎

We now show how the lemma implies our theorem. Slightly rewriting the lemma, we have that

\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{i}}[\phi(\mathbf{x},y)]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}[\phi(\mathbf{x},y)]\right\|^{2}~\leq~\frac{1}{d}.

By Markov’s inequality, this implies that for any fixed
\phi
, if we choose
i\in[d]
uniformly at random, then

\Pr_{i}\left(\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{i}}[\phi(\mathbf{x},y)]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}[\phi(\mathbf{x},y)]\right\|^{2}\geq\tau^{2}\right)~\leq~\frac{1}{\tau^{2}d}~.

Now, let us consider how any SQ algorithm behaves when
i
and the target function
f_{i}
are chosen uniformly at random. Initially, the algorithm chooses a query
\phi_{1}
that does not depend on
i
. By the inequality above, with probability at least,
1-\frac{1}{\tau^{2}d}
,
\phi_{1}
is such that its expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{i}}[\phi_{1}(\mathbf{x},y)]
is
\tau
-close to the vector
E_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}[\phi_{1}(\mathbf{x},y)]
, so the SQ oracle may return this vector. Crucially, this vector does not depend on
i
, so the algorithm does not obtain any information about
i
. As a result, its next query
\phi_{2}
will also not depend on
i
, in which case (using the same reasoning), it may get
E_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}[\phi_{2}(\mathbf{x},y)]
with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{\tau^{2}d}
. By a union bound, after at most
\tau^{2}d/2
queries, the algorithm still receives no information on
i
, with probability at least
\frac{1}{2}
. Hence, its output will be independent of
i
, so it will succeed with probability at most
1/d
. Overall, again by a union bound, we get that the algorithm cannot succeed with probability more than
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{d}
.

∎

B.3
Covering Arguments

Our proof will utilize relatively standard covering arguments. Given
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, an
\epsilon
-net

\mathcal{N}
of
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
is a finite subset of the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
such that for every

\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
there exists
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}
satisfying
\|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
.

A classical result in high-dimensional geometry (see, e.g.,
(
Vershynin 2025
)
[Corollary 4.2.11]) states that one
can construct such a net with cardinality

|\mathcal{N}|\leq\left(1+\frac{2}{\epsilon}\right)^{m}.

As a consequence, uniform control over all directions in
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
can be reduced to control over
the finite set
\mathcal{N}
, at the cost of an
\epsilon
-dependent slack. So we can derive bounds that hold uniformly over all directions by applying
union bounds over the net
\mathcal{N}
, and this will be used to extend the alignment control results from the single-neuron case to high-width settings.

Lemma 21
.

For any
\epsilon\geq 0
, there exists a set
\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
of cardinality at most
(3/\epsilon)^{m}
such that for any
t\geq 0
, and nonzero vector
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\left\|P_{W_{t}}\mathbf{u}\right\|}{\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|}\leq\frac{\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{N}}\frac{\left|\mathbf{u}^{\top}W_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\right|}{\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|}+\epsilon\left\|W_{t}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}}{s_{m}(W_{t})}\leq\frac{\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{N}}\frac{\left|\mathbf{u}^{\top}W_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\right|}{\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|}+\epsilon\left\|W_{t}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}}{\inf_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{N}}\left\|W_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\right\|-\epsilon\left\|W_{t}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}},

where the last inequality assumes the denominator is non-negative.

Proof.

We will apply a covering argument. Let
\mathcal{N}
be an
\epsilon
-net of the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
with
\epsilon\in(0,1/2]
. By
(
Vershynin 2025
)
[Corollary 4.2.11], we may take
\mathcal{N}
to have cardinality at most
\left|\mathcal{N}\right|\leq\left(3/\epsilon\right)^{m}
.

Fix any
t\geq 0
, and note that
\left\|P_{W_{t}}\mathbf{u}\right\|/\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\left\|P_{W_{t}}\mathbf{u}\right\|=\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1},\mathbf{v}^{\top}W_{t}\neq\boldsymbol{0}}\frac{\left|\mathbf{u}^{\top}W_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\right|}{\left\|W_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\right\|\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|}\leq\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\frac{\left|\mathbf{u}^{\top}W_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\right|}{s_{m}(W_{t})\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|},

(16)

Now we may bound
s_{m}(W_{t})
as follows:

\displaystyle s_{m}(W_{t})=

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\left\|W_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\right\|\geq\inf_{\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\inf_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{N}}\left\|W_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\right\|-\left\|W_{t}^{\top}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\left\|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\right\|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{N}}\left\|W_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\right\|-\epsilon\left\|W_{t}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

Similarly, we may bound
\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\frac{\left|\mathbf{u}^{\top}W_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\right|}{\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|}
as

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\frac{\left|\mathbf{u}^{\top}W_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\right|}{\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|}\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{N}}\frac{\left|\mathbf{u}^{\top}W_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\right|}{\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|}+\frac{\left\|\mathbf{u}^{\top}W_{t}^{\top}\right\|}{\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|}\left\|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{N}}\frac{\left|\mathbf{u}^{\top}W_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\right|}{\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|}+\epsilon\left\|W_{t}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

Plugging these inequalities back into Eq. (
16
) completes the proof.
∎

B.4
Proof of the Theorem

Proposition 22
.

Under Assumptions
1

2
,
3

T=\left\lfloor\frac{d\epsilon^{2}}{4p\max\left(\bar{\kappa}(C_{1}K_{1}G)^{2},1\right)}\right\rfloor
, we obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{t}\leq C\sqrt{p}\frac{\sqrt{\frac{m\log\left(\frac{d^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}\delta}\right)}{d}}+\frac{\epsilon}{2\sqrt{\bar{\kappa}p}}}{\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\bar{\kappa}}}-\frac{\epsilon}{2\sqrt{\bar{\kappa}p}}}=\frac{2C}{\sqrt{p}-\epsilon}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\bar{\kappa}p^{2}m\log\left(\frac{d^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}\delta}\right)}{d}}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right).

By the assumed lower bound on
d
in the proposition statement, the square root term is at most
\epsilon/2
. Moreover, since
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
,
\sqrt{p}-\epsilon>1/2
. So the alignment is bounded as
\rho_{t}\leq 4C\epsilon
. If
4C>1
, we may replace
\epsilon
by
\epsilon/4C
(and adjust the absolute constants in the proposition statement) to obtain the desired result.
∎

Appendix C
Hermite Polynomials and the Structure of Gaussian Multi-Index Models

Throughout this section, we assume that the inputs
\mathbf{x}
are standard Gaussians
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},I_{d})
. Let
\mu(\mathbf{x})
be the PDF of the standard Gaussian in
d
dimensions, and
H_{k}(\mathbf{x}):=(-1)^{k}\frac{\nabla^{k}\mu(\mathbf{x})}{\sqrt{k!}\mu(\mathbf{x})}
denote the normalized (probabilist’s) Hermite polynomials. We will not specify the dimension
d
, as the dimension should be inferred by the dimension of the inputs
\mathbf{x}
. We refer the reader to
Grad 1949
for background on Hermite polynomials in high dimensions.

We let
L_{2}(\mu)
denote the space of square integrable functions, with
\left\|f\right\|_{L_{2}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mu}\left[f(\mathbf{x})^{2}\right]^{1/2}
. We may also extend this to vector valued functions as
\left\|f\right\|_{L_{2}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mu}\bigl[\left\|f(\mathbf{x})\right\|^{2}\bigr]^{1/2}
. We denote the
L_{\infty}
norm by
\left\|f\right\|_{L_{\infty}}:=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|f(\mathbf{x})\right|
(the
\sup
can be taken with probability
1
). To ease notation in the proofs, we let

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{d}:=\{f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\mid f\text{ is weakly differentiable and }\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{x})^{2}]<\infty~,~\mathbb{E}[\left\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\right\|^{2}]<\infty\}.

Tensors and basic operations.

For
k\geq 1
, we identify a (real)
k
-tensor over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with an array

A=(A_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}})_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\in[d]}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes k}.

We use the Frobenius inner product and norm:

\langle A,B\rangle:=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}=1}^{d}A_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}B_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}},\qquad\|A\|_{F}:=\sqrt{\langle A,A\rangle}~.

We denote by
\text{Sym}(A)
the symmetrization of a
k-
tensor, defined as
\text{Sym}(A):=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\pi\in S_{k}}\pi(A)
where
S_{k}
is the group of all permutations on
[k]
and
\pi(A)
denotes the tensor obtained by permuting indices of
A
according to
\pi
.

Tensor action of a matrix.

Let
M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
and let
A\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes k}
. We define the tensor
M^{\otimes k}A\in(\mathbb{R}^{m})^{\otimes k}
by

(M^{\otimes k}A)_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}:=\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{k}=1}^{d}M_{i_{1}j_{1}}\cdots M_{i_{k}j_{k}}A_{j_{1},\dots,j_{k}}.

Intuitively,
M^{\otimes k}
applies the linear map
\mathbf{v}\mapsto M\mathbf{v}
to each index of the tensor. For
k=1
this is just matrix-vector multiplication, and for
k=2
this is
MAM^{\top}
. This operation is linear in
A
, and satisfies the composition rule

(MN)^{\otimes k}A=M^{\otimes k}(N^{\otimes k}A),\qquad\forall M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p},N\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}.

When
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is square and symmetric, the operator
M^{\otimes k}

is self-adjoint with respect to the Frobenius inner product on

(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes k}
, meaning that for all
k
-tensors
A,B\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes k}
,

\langle A,M^{\otimes k}B\rangle=\langle M^{\otimes k}A,B\rangle.

We also denote by
\left\|M^{\otimes k}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}
the induced operator norm with respect to the Frobenius norm. It is well known that
\left\|M^{\otimes k}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\left\|M\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{k}
.

Basic Facts for Hermite Polynomials.

Some known facts include:

•

Hermite Expansion
: If
f\in L_{2}(\mu)
, then

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\langle H_{k}(\mathbf{x}),C_{k}\rangle\qquad\text{for}\qquad C_{k}:=\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{x})H_{k}(\mathbf{x})],

(28)

where convergence is in
L_{2}(\mu)
, and
C_{k}
are symmetric.

•

Orthonormal Basis
:
H_{k}
form a complete and orthonormal basis for
L_{2}(\mu)
, in the sense that for any
j
-tensor
A
and
k
-tensor
B
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\langle H_{j}(\mathbf{x}),A\rangle\langle H_{k}(\mathbf{x}),B\rangle]=\delta_{jk}\langle\text{Sym}(A),\text{Sym}(B)\rangle.

(29)

•

Generating Function:
If
f\in L_{2}(\mu)
then for any
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\exp\left(\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{v}\|^{2}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{j!}}\left\langle H_{j}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{v}^{\otimes j}\right\rangle.

(30)

This is because the left-hand side is equal to
\mu(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{v})/\mu(\mathbf{x})
, so Taylor expanding
\mu(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{v})
and using the definition of
H_{j}
gives the desired equality.

•

Relation to Dimension one:
Let
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a vector with unit norm, then for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
k\geq 0
,

\displaystyle H_{k}\left(\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)=\left\langle H_{k}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{v}^{\otimes k}\right\rangle,

(31)

where on the left-hand side we use the one-dimensional Hermite polynomial.

•

Recurrence Relation:

For any index
i\in[d]
:

\displaystyle x_{i}H_{k}(\mathbf{x})-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}H_{k}(\mathbf{x})=\sqrt{k+1}\left(H_{k+1}(\mathbf{x})\right)_{i},

(32)

where
(H_{k+1}(\mathbf{x}))_{i}
denotes the slice of the tensor with the first index fixed to
i
.

•

Derivative Formula:

For a function smooth function
f
that is
k
times weakly-differentiable and if
\mathbb{E}[\left\|\nabla^{k}f(\mathbf{x})\right\|_{F}^{2}]<\infty
with Hermite coefficient tensors
C_{k}
,

\displaystyle C_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k!}}\mathbb{E}[\nabla^{k}f(\mathbf{x})]

(33)

We note that this property cannot be used for ReLU and Leaky ReLU. Hence, we will avoid using it. Nevertheless, it helps conceptually to understand why some of the lemmas are true.

Useful Lemmas.

We note that while there is extensive literature on the Hermite polynomials in dimension
1
, there are far fewer sources for higher dimensions. It is possible that variants of a couple of the following lemmas appear in the literature (especially Lemma
27
). Nevertheless, for completeness, we prove everything we need.

We begin with a lemma that relates to the Hermite expansion in
d
dimensions and one dimension:

Lemma 24
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
, and let
b_{k}(\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|):=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[\sigma(\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|x)H_{k}(x)]
be the one dimensional Hermite expansion of
\sigma(\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|x)
. Then the
d-
dimensional Hermite expansion of
f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{x}):=\sigma(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x})
is given by

\displaystyle f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\langle H_{k}(\mathbf{x}),B_{k}(\mathbf{w})\right\rangle,\qquad\text{where}\qquad B_{k}(\mathbf{w})=b_{k}(\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|)\left(\frac{\mathbf{w}}{\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|}\right)^{\otimes k}.

Proof.

For any
s>0
, consider the Hermite expansion of
\sigma(sx)
, with coefficients given by
b_{k}(s):=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[\sigma(sx)H_{k}(x)]
. Following Eq. (
31
), the one dimensional Hermite polynomials satisfy
H_{k}\left(\frac{~\mathbf{w}^{\top}}{\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|}\mathbf{x}\right)=\left\langle H_{k}\left(\mathbf{x}\right),\left(\frac{\mathbf{w}}{\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|}\right)^{\otimes k}\right\rangle
. Using this and the one-dimensional Hermite expansion of the function
\sigma(sx)
,

\displaystyle f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{x})=

\displaystyle\sigma\left(\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|\cdot\frac{~\mathbf{w}^{\top}}{\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|}\mathbf{x}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}(\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|)H_{k}\left(\frac{~\mathbf{w}^{\top}}{\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|}\mathbf{x}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\langle H_{k}\left(\mathbf{x}\right),b_{k}(\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|)\left(\frac{\mathbf{w}}{\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|}\right)^{\otimes k}\right\rangle

At the same time,
f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{x})
has a Hermite expansion
f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\langle H_{k}\left(\mathbf{x}\right),B_{k}(\mathbf{w})\right\rangle
. By the uniqueness of the Hermite expansion (since Hermite polynomials form a complete and orthonormal basis), it follows that
B_{k}(\mathbf{w})=b_{k}(\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|)\left(\frac{\mathbf{w}}{\left\|\mathbf{w}\right\|}\right)^{\otimes k}
which completes the proof.
∎

We now show that the Hermite coefficients lie in a subspace determined by the span of the rows of
W
.

Lemma 25
.

Let
W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
and
f_{W}(\mathbf{x}):=\sigma(W\mathbf{x})
where
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
and
f_{W}\in\mathcal{F}_{d}
. Let
A_{k}(W):=\mathbb{E}[f_{W}(\mathbf{x})H_{k}(\mathbf{x})]\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes k}
be the
k
-th Hermite coefficient tensor of
f_{W}
, then

\displaystyle A_{k}(W)=P_{W}^{\otimes k}A_{k}(W).

Proof.

Let
P:=P_{W}
and
Q:=I-P_{W}
, and let
r:=\text{rank}(W)
. We decompose
\mathbf{x}
into
P\mathbf{x}
and
Q\mathbf{x}
, which (because
\mathbf{x}
is Gaussian) are independent Gaussians supported on orthogonal subspaces of dimensions
r
and
d-r
respectively. Using this and that
f_{W}(\mathbf{x})=f_{W}(P\mathbf{x})
,

\displaystyle A_{k}(W)=

\displaystyle\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[f_{W}(P\mathbf{x})H_{k}(P\mathbf{x}+Q\mathbf{x})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \mathbb{E}_{P\mathbf{x}}\left[f_{W}(P\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbb{E}_{Q\mathbf{x}}\left[H_{k}(P\mathbf{x}+Q\mathbf{x})\right]\right].

We now show that for every fixed
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the range of
P
(i.e.,
P\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{z}
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q)}\left[H_{k}(\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{y})\right]=P^{\otimes k}H_{k}(P\mathbf{z}).

To this end, fix
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and use Eq. (
30
) so that

\displaystyle\exp\left(\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{v}\|^{2}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{j!}}\left\langle H_{j}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{v}^{\otimes j}\right\rangle,\qquad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(34)

Applying Eq. (
34
) with
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{y}
and taking expectation over
\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q)
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{j!}}\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\left[H_{j}(\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{y})\right],\mathbf{v}^{\otimes j}\right\rangle=\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\left[\exp\left(\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{y}\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{v}\|^{2}\right)\right].

Since
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}[e^{\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{y}\rangle}]=\exp\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}^{\top}Q\mathbf{v}\right)
for
\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q)
, the right-hand side becomes

\displaystyle\exp\left(\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{z}\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{v}\|^{2}\right)\exp\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}^{\top}Q\mathbf{v}\right)=\exp\left(\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{z}\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}^{\top}P\mathbf{v}\right)=\exp\left(\langle P\mathbf{v},\mathbf{z}\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2}\|P\mathbf{v}\|^{2}\right),

where the last equality uses that
P\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{z}
. To recap, so far we have shown

\displaystyle\exp\left(\langle P\mathbf{v},\mathbf{z}\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2}\|P\mathbf{v}\|^{2}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{j!}}\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\left[H_{j}(\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{y})\right],\mathbf{v}^{\otimes j}\right\rangle.

(35)

Now consider applying Eq. (
34
) again, but now with
\mathbf{x}=P\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{v}
replaced by
P\mathbf{v}
. Then

\displaystyle\exp\left(\langle P\mathbf{v},\mathbf{z}\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2}\|P\mathbf{v}\|^{2}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{j!}}\left\langle H_{j}(P\mathbf{z}),(P\mathbf{v})^{\otimes j}\right\rangle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{j!}}\left\langle P^{\otimes j}H_{j}(P\mathbf{z}),\mathbf{v}^{\otimes j}\right\rangle.

Comparing this series to the one in Eq. (
35
) and matching coefficients of
\mathbf{v}^{\otimes k}
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\left[H_{k}(\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{y})\right]=P^{\otimes k}H_{k}(P\mathbf{z}).

(36)

Returning to
A_{k}(W)
and using Eq. (
36
) with
\mathbf{z}=P\mathbf{x}
, we obtain

\displaystyle A_{k}(W)=\

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P\mathbf{x}}\left[f_{W}(P\mathbf{x})\cdot P^{\otimes k}H_{k}(P\mathbf{x})\right]=P^{\otimes k}\mathbb{E}_{P\mathbf{x}}\left[f_{W}(P\mathbf{x})H_{k}(P\mathbf{x})\right]=P^{\otimes k}A_{k}(W),

completing the proof.
∎

Lemma 26
.

Let
W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
,
U\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
, and let
f_{W}(\mathbf{x})=\sigma(W\mathbf{x}),g_{U}(\mathbf{x})=\phi(U\mathbf{x})
where
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
and
f_{W},g_{U}\in\mathcal{F}_{d}
.
Let
\rho:=\|P_{W}P_{U}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
and let
B_{k}(U):=\mathbb{E}[g_{U}(\mathbf{x})H_{k}(\mathbf{x})]\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes k}
the
k
-th Hermite coefficient tensor of
g_{U}
. Then

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}[f_{W}(\mathbf{x})g_{U}(\mathbf{x})]\right|\leq\left\|f_{W}(\mathbf{x})\right\|_{{L_{2}}}\sqrt{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\|B_{k}(U)\right\|_{F}^{2}\cdot\rho^{2k}}.

Proof.

Let
A_{k}:=A_{k}(W):=\mathbb{E}[f_{W}(\mathbf{x})H_{k}(\mathbf{x})]\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes k}
be the
k
-th Hermite coefficient tensor of
f_{W}
and
B_{k}:=B_{k}(U)
the
k
-th Hermite coefficient tensor of
g_{U}
. By the Hermite expansion and the fact that
A_{k},B_{k}
are symmetric,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f_{W}(\mathbf{x})g_{U}(\mathbf{x})]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\langle A_{k},B_{k}\rangle.

By Lemma
25
,
A_{k}=P_{W}^{\otimes k}A_{k}
and similarly
B_{k}=P_{U}^{\otimes k}B_{k}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f_{W}(\mathbf{x})g_{U}(\mathbf{x})]=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\langle P_{W}^{\otimes k}A_{k},P_{U}^{\otimes k}B_{k}\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\langle A_{k},P_{W}^{\otimes k}P_{U}^{\otimes k}B_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\langle A_{k},(P_{W}P_{U})^{\otimes k}B_{k}\rangle.

Applying Cauchy-Schwarz,

\displaystyle\left|\langle A_{k},(P_{W}P_{U})^{\otimes k}B_{k}\rangle\right|\leq

\displaystyle\left\|A_{k}\right\|_{F}\left\|(P_{W}P_{U})^{\otimes k}B_{k}\right\|_{F}\leq\left\|A_{k}\right\|_{F}\left\|(P_{W}P_{U})^{\otimes k}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\left\|B_{k}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle=

{}_{(*)}\left\|A_{k}\right\|_{F}\rho^{k}\left\|B_{k}\right\|_{F},

where (*) uses that
\left\|M^{\otimes k}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\left\|M\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{k}
for any matrix
M
.

Summing over
k
and applying Cauchy-Schwarz again yields

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}[f_{W}(\mathbf{x})g_{U}(\mathbf{x})]\right|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\|A_{k}\right\|_{F}\left\|(P_{W}P_{U})\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{k}\left\|B_{k}\right\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\|A_{k}\right\|_{F}^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\|B_{k}\right\|_{F}^{2}\rho^{2k}}.

Finally, using that
\sum_{k}\|A_{k}\|_{F}^{2}=\|f_{W}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}^{2}
completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 27

(Gradient shifts Hermite coefficients)
.

Let
g\in\mathcal{F}_{d}
with Hermite coefficient tensors
B_{k}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes k}
. Let
D_{k}:=\mathbb{E}[\nabla g(\mathbf{x})\otimes H_{k}(\mathbf{x})]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes k}
be the
k
-th Hermite coefficient tensor of
\nabla g(\mathbf{x})
. Then:

\|D_{k}\|_{F}^{2}=(k+1)\|B_{k+1}\|_{F}^{2}.

Proof.

We compute the
i
-th slice of this tensor, denoted
(D_{k})_{i}
, which corresponds to the Hermite coefficients of the partial derivative
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}g(\mathbf{x})
. Applying Stein’s identity (Gaussian integration by parts), we obtain:

(D_{k})_{i}=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}g(\mathbf{x})\cdot H_{k}(\mathbf{x})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[g(\mathbf{x})\left(x_{i}H_{k}(\mathbf{x})-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}H_{k}(\mathbf{x})\right)\right].

Using the recurrence relation Eq. (
32
):

(D_{k})_{i}=\sqrt{k+1}\mathbb{E}\left[g(\mathbf{x})(H_{k+1}(\mathbf{x}))_{i}\right]=\sqrt{k+1}(B_{k+1})_{i}.

Squaring the Frobenius norm and summing over
i
yields the result.
∎

Lemma 28
.

Let
W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
,
U\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
, and let
f_{W}(\mathbf{x})=\sigma(W\mathbf{x}),g_{U}(\mathbf{x})=\phi(U\mathbf{x})
where
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
and
f_{W},g_{U}\in\mathcal{F}_{d}
.
Let
\rho:=\|P_{W}P_{U}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
and let
B_{k}(U):=\mathbb{E}[g_{U}(\mathbf{x})H_{k}(\mathbf{x})]\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes k}
the
k
-th Hermite coefficient tensor of
g_{U}
. Then

\displaystyle\Big\|\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{W}f_{W}(\mathbf{x})g_{U}(\mathbf{x})\right]\Big\|_{F}\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left\|\left(\nabla\sigma(W\mathbf{x})\right)_{i}\left(P_{W}\mathbf{x}\right)_{j}\right\|_{L_{2}}^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\|B_{k}(U)\right\|_{F}^{2}\cdot\rho^{2k}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\|\left(\nabla\sigma(W\mathbf{x})\right)_{i}\right\|_{L_{2}}^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k+1)\left\|B_{k+1}(U)\right\|_{F}^{2}\rho^{2k}}.

Proof.

By the chain rule,

\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{W}f_{W}(\mathbf{x})g_{U}(\mathbf{x})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[g_{U}(\mathbf{x})\nabla\sigma(W\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}^{\top}\right].

We decompose
\mathbf{x}
into its projection onto
W
and its orthogonal complement, so that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{W}f_{W}(\mathbf{x})g_{U}(\mathbf{x})\right]=

\displaystyle\underset{=:M_{1}}{\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[g_{U}(\mathbf{x})\nabla\sigma(W\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}^{\top}P_{W}\right]}}+\underset{=:M_{2}}{\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[g_{U}(\mathbf{x})\nabla\sigma(W\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}^{\top}(I-P_{W})\right]}}.

(37)

To bound
M_{1}
, first note that

\displaystyle\left\|M_{1}\right\|_{F}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[g_{U}(\mathbf{x})\left(\nabla\sigma(W\mathbf{x})\right)_{i}\left(P_{W}\mathbf{x}\right)_{j}\right]\right)^{2}}.

Applying Lemma
26
to the functions
\left(\nabla\sigma(W\mathbf{x})\right)_{i}\left(P_{W}\mathbf{x}\right)_{j}
and
g_{U}(\mathbf{x})
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left\|M_{1}\right\|_{F}\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left\|\left(\nabla\sigma(W\mathbf{x})\right)_{i}\left(P_{W}\mathbf{x}\right)_{j}\right\|_{L_{2}}^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\|B_{k}(U)\right\|_{F}^{2}\cdot\rho^{2k}}.

To bound
M_{2}
, first we will use Stein’s lemma, which states that for any function
\psi
and matrix
A
,
\mathbb{E}[\psi(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}^{\top}A]=\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\psi(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\right]A
. Applying this to
g_{U}(\mathbf{x})\left(\nabla\sigma(W\mathbf{x})\right)_{i}
and using that
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\left(\nabla\sigma(W\mathbf{x})\right)_{i}(I-P_{W})=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|M_{2}\right\|_{F}^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[g_{U}(\mathbf{x})\left(\nabla\sigma(W\mathbf{x})\right)_{i}\mathbf{x}^{\top}(I-P_{W})\right]\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\nabla\sigma(W\mathbf{x})\right)_{i}\nabla g_{U}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\right](I-P_{W})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\nabla\sigma(W\mathbf{x})\right)_{i}\left(\nabla g_{U}(\mathbf{x})\right)_{j}\right]\right)^{2}\left\|(I-P_{W})\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}.

Define the (vector-valued) Hermite coefficient tensors for
\nabla\phi(U\mathbf{x})
by

\displaystyle D_{k}(U):=\mathbb{E}[(\nabla g_{U}(\mathbf{x}))\otimes H_{k}(\mathbf{x})]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes k}.

Note that by Lemma
27
,
\left\|D_{k}(U)\right\|_{F}=\sqrt{k+1}\left\|B_{k+1}(U)\right\|_{F}
.
Applying Lemma
26
for the correlation between
\left(\nabla\sigma(W\mathbf{x})\right)_{i}
and
\left(\nabla g_{U}(\mathbf{x})\right)_{j}
yields

\displaystyle\left\|M_{2}\right\|\leq

Lemma 30
.

Let

Example: Product of Functions

Let
\phi
be a smooth, bounded and mean-zero function (e.g.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\}\subseteq[p]}\left(\prod_{i\notin\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\}}\phi(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{x})\right)\left(\prod_{i\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\}}\phi^{\prime}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{x})\right)\mathbf{u}_{j_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathbf{u}_{j_{k}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\left(\mathbb{E}_{G\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[\phi^{\prime}(G)]\right)^{p}\sum_{\pi\in S_{p}}\mathbf{u}_{\pi(1)}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathbf{u}_{\pi(p)}&k=p\end{cases}.
