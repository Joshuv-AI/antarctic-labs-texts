Structural Empowerment and Nurses' Intention to Stay in the Profession: A Descriptive Cross‐Sectional Survey
Authors: Mohammad Torabi, Parisa Hajilo, Rasoul Salimi, Mohammadreza Shokouhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimNursing, as a critical profession in the healthcare system, faces challenges such as limited access to resources, information, and professional opportunities, which may contribute to nurses' intention to leave the profession. Structural empowerment may improve the work environment and enhance job motivation. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between structural empowerment and nurses' intention to stay in the profession.DesignA descriptive cross‐sectional survey.MethodsThis descriptive cross‐sectional study was conducted among 186 nurses working in Hamedan, Iran, using a convenience sampling method. Data were collected using a demographic questionnaire, the Conditions of Work Effectiveness Questionnaire‐II (CWEQ‐II), and the Wiener and Vardi Intention to Stay Questionnaire. Data were analysed using SPSS version 16.ResultsA significant positive correlation was found between structural empowerment and nurses' intention to stay in the profession (r = 0.49, p < 0.001). The overall levels of structural empowerment and intention to stay were moderate. Among the dimensions of structural empowerment, access to opportunities had the highest mean score (3.7 ± 0.85), whereas access to resources had the lowest (2.5 ± 0.91). Employment type was also significantly associated with nurses' intention to stay in the profession.No Patient or Public ContributionNo patient or public contribution was involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.Keywords: cross‐sectional study, intention to stay, nurses, structural empowerment

Introduction

Structural empowerment refers to organizational structures and processes designed to enhance employees' access to decision‐making and increase their participation in organizational activities (Priyadharshany and Sujatha 2015). In nursing, this concept involves providing nurses with the necessary resources, opportunities, and support to enable them to perform effectively in their work environment (Fragkos et al. 2020). The delivery of high‐quality and effective healthcare services depends on nurses' professional competence, which is closely associated with organizational conditions, including structural empowerment (Xu et al. 2025). The stakes are particularly high in operating room and perioperative settings. Surgical teams depend on rapid coordination under constant time pressure, and when nurses lack access to resources or decision‐making authority, patient safety can suffer (van Harten et al. 2025).

Nurses face numerous challenges in their profession, including high workload, heavy responsibilities, and shortages of resources (Mehrafshan et al. 2025). Operating rooms add their own layer of difficulty: chronic staff shortages combined with physically and mentally demanding procedures leave perioperative nurses at heightened risk of burnout (Mousavi et al. 2025). Therefore, structural empowerment may play a crucial role in maintaining and enhancing nurses' job motivation. In addition, healthcare systems require empowered staff to achieve and maintain high standards of care (7).

Globally, the shortage of nurses has become one of the most significant challenges facing healthcare systems. According to the World Health Organization, the global nursing workforce remains insufficient to meet the increasing demand for healthcare services, making nurses' Intention to Stay in the Profession a major priority for health policymakers (World Health Organization 2025; Torabi et al. 2022). Similar challenges exist in Iran, where shortages of nursing staff, unequal workforce distribution, and increasing workloads have placed considerable pressure on healthcare organizations (Shamsi and Peyravi 2020). Despite efforts to recruit new nurses, enhancing nurses' Intention to Stay in the Profession among experienced nursing staff remains a persistent challenge (Roth et al. 2022). Therefore, identifying organizational factors that enhance nurses' Intention to Stay in the Profession has become increasingly important for improving workforce stability and ensuring the delivery of high‐quality healthcare services (Marufu et al. 2021).

However, despite the importance of structural empowerment in clinical settings, findings from Hauck et al. (2011) indicate that limited access to essential resources remains a barrier to achieving adequate levels of structural empowerment (Ahmed et al. 2024; Ozbozkurt et al. 2021). The turnover of skilled nursing staff is a critical issue in healthcare services and has created significant challenges for healthcare systems (Hajilo et al. 2025). This issue not only reduces the quality of care but also imposes substantial financial burdens on healthcare organizations (Bae 2022). Currently, nurse turnover is a global concern, contributing to shortages of skilled and competent healthcare professionals (Maleki et al. 2012). Several studies have shown that strengthening organizational structures can improve nurses' job satisfaction and reduce their intention to leave the profession (Al‐Otaibi et al. 2024; Meng et al. 2015). Furthermore, in a structural equation modelling study, structural empowerment, which has a positive and direct relationship with psychological empowerment, had a direct positive effect on nurses' intention to stay in the profession through enhancing psychological empowerment, with psychological empowerment acting as a mediator in this relationship (Ahmed et al. 2024). In addition, Manojlovich's study highlighted that the recruitment and retention of qualified nursing staff are strongly associated with structural empowerment within hospitals (Aggarwal et al. 2018; Hajilo et al. 2024).

Although nurses in all healthcare systems work within interdisciplinary teams and collaborate closely with physicians, the degree of professional autonomy and authority in clinical and organizational decision‐making varies considerably across countries. In developed healthcare systems, such as those in the United States, Canada, and Scandinavian countries, nurses have greater autonomy in independent patient assessment, care planning, patient education, and participation in clinical and organizational decision‐making (Lavoie 2026; Levy‐Malmberg et al. 2024; Oshodi et al. 2019).

In contrast, in Iran, cultural and organizational characteristics of the healthcare system limit nurses' professional autonomy and organizational position (Balasi et al. 2023). These limitations are reflected in several areas. Nurses have limited participation in departmental policy‐making and the development of clinical protocols. Their role in independent clinical assessment and decision‐making is less formally recognized than in many developed countries. Furthermore, nurses have less professional influence within the hierarchical structure of the healthcare system (AllahBakhshian et al. 2017; Cheraghi et al. 2015). These conditions may adversely affect nurses' job satisfaction and organizational commitment, ultimately increasing their intention to leave the profession (Marufu et al. 2021).

In the present study, nurse power refers to nurses' ability to exercise professional influence, participate effectively in organizational decision‐making, and enhance their professional standing within the healthcare system. The concept encompasses both individual authority in clinical decision‐making and the structural power to influence the work environment and care processes. Considering these structural characteristics, the present study examines the relationship between structural empowerment and nurses' intention to remain in the profession within the context of Iran's healthcare system. The findings may provide evidence for healthcare managers and policymakers regarding the importance of organizational conditions in nurse retention. Furthermore, delegating authority to frontline staff may enhance their engagement and willingness to provide high‐quality healthcare services.

Most existing studies on structural empowerment have been conducted in developed countries, and limited evidence is available regarding its association with nurses' Intention to Stay in the Profession within the Iranian healthcare system. Given differences in hospital management structures, nursing workforce distribution, and working conditions, the generalisability of findings from other healthcare systems to Iran may be limited. Addressing this research gap is important because understanding the relationship between structural empowerment and nurses' Intention to Stay in the Profession can inform nursing managers and healthcare policymakers in developing strategies to improve nurse retention, strengthen workforce stability, and ultimately enhance the quality and continuity of patient care. Therefore, the present study investigated the association between structural empowerment and nurses' Intention to Stay in the Profession among nurses working in various hospital departments, including operating room and acute care units. Although the study included nurses from different hospital departments, the inclusion of operating room nurses enables the findings to provide insights that may also be applicable to perioperative and operating room settings. Accordingly, the research question of this study was: Is structural empowerment associated with nurses' Intention to Stay in the Profession among hospital nurses in Iran?

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This descriptive cross‐sectional study was conducted between February and April 2024 among nurses working in three public hospitals affiliated with Hamadan University of Medical Sciences in Hamadan, Iran. The participants were recruited from different hospital wards, including the Emergency, Operating Room, Internal Medicine, ICU, and CCU. Participants were selected using a convenience sampling method. Inclusion criteria were having at least a bachelor's degree in nursing, a minimum of one year of clinical work experience to ensure that participants had sufficient clinical exposure and familiarity with the professional environment to provide informed and reliable responses regarding the study variables, and willingness to participate in the study by providing informed consent. To increase sample diversity and reduce limitations associated with convenience sampling, data collection was carried out in different hospital wards and across various work shifts (morning, evening, and night). The researchers visited different hospital units and invited all eligible nurses who were present during data collection to participate in the study. After providing necessary explanations about the study objectives and questionnaire completion process, the questionnaires were distributed to the participants and completed voluntarily and anonymously. Completing the questionnaires took approximately 15 min on average.

Selecting participants from different specialized wards and various work shifts increased the likelihood of including nurses with diverse professional characteristics, work experiences, and clinical backgrounds. This strategy helped reduce selection bias related to accessing a specific group of nurses and improved the representativeness of different groups of hospital staff.

Sample Size Calculation

The required sample size was estimated based on Cohen's conventional criteria for a medium effect size (f 2 = 0.15), assuming a significance level of 0.05, a statistical power of 0.85, and seven predictor variables in the multiple linear regression model. The minimum required sample size was estimated to be 186 participants. To account for potential non‐response or incomplete questionnaires, 200 questionnaires were distributed (Hajilo et al. 2022).

Instruments

A three‐section questionnaire was used for data collection. The first section collected demographic information of the nurses. Background variables included age, gender, work experience, marital status, clinical department, and employment status.

In the second section, nurses' structural empowerment was assessed using the 21‐item abbreviated Conditions of Work Effectiveness Questionnaire‐II (CWEQ‐II). This instrument was developed by Spence Laschinger et al. (2001) based on Kanter's structural empowerment theory and adapted from the original Chandler (1986) instrument. It consists of 21 items organized into three sections. The first section includes 12 items assessing four dimensions of structural empowerment based on Kanter's model: access to opportunities (career growth and advancement), access to information (awareness of organizational policies), access to resources (availability of necessary tools for work), and access to support (guidance and feedback from supervisors and colleagues). The second section includes three items on job activities (JAS), assessing employees' involvement in decision‐making. The third section includes four items on organizational relationships (ORS), evaluating professional interactions and support networks. Finally, two general items (GE) assess overall perceptions of empowerment. The questionnaire is rated on a five‐point Likert scale (1–5), with total scores ranging from 6 to 30; higher scores indicate greater perceived structural empowerment, with scores between 613 indicating low empowerment, 14–22 indicating moderate empowerment, and 23–30 indicating high empowerment (Spence Laschinger et al. 2001). Mahmoud et al. (2013) confirmed the construct validity of the instrument and reported acceptable reliability (Cronbach's α = 0.79–0.82) (Hussein 2013).

The third section assessed nurses' intention to stay using the Intention to Stay in Nursing Questionnaire (ITSN) developed by Wiener and Vardi (1980). This instrument was designed to measure an individual's intention to remain employed in their current organization and reflects the motivational tendency to continue working rather than leaving the organization. The questionnaire consists of four items that evaluate different aspects of intention to stay, including the likelihood of remaining in the current organization, thoughts about leaving, preference for continuing employment in the organization if given a choice, and the importance of spending one's professional career in the current organization. Each item is rated on a five‐point Likert scale. For items 1 and 3, responses are scored directly from 1 to 5, whereas items 2 and 4 are reverse‐scored. The total score ranges from 4 to 20, with higher scores indicating a stronger intention to remain in the current organization. Based on the total score, intention to stay can be interpreted as low (4–8), moderate (9–12), or high (13–20) (Wiener and Vardi 1980).

Validity and Reliability

In this study, two instruments were used: the Conditions of Work Effectiveness Questionnaire‐II (CWEQ‐II) and the Wiener and Vardi Intention to Stay Questionnaire (ITSQ). The validation process for both instruments was conducted similarly.

The questionnaires were translated into Persian using the standard forward–backward translation method. Initially, the original instruments were translated into Persian, and then the Persian versions were back‐translated into English by an independent translator to ensure conceptual equivalence, semantic accuracy, and preservation of item meanings. Following the translation process, the instruments were reviewed and culturally adapted to ensure their appropriateness for the Iranian context.

Content validity and linguistic clarity were assessed by a panel of five nursing faculty members and two language specialists. The experts evaluated the items in terms of relevance, clarity, simplicity, and cultural appropriateness. Based on their feedback, minor revisions were made to several items, and the final versions of the instruments were developed.

The internal consistency reliability of the instruments was assessed using Cronbach's alpha coefficient. The Cronbach's alpha values were 0.86 for CWEQ‐II and 0.88 for ITSQ, indicating acceptable internal consistency and satisfactory reliability of both instruments in the present sample. The Wiener and Vardi (1980) Intent to Stay questionnaire has demonstrated acceptable validity and has been widely used in previous studies. Its reliability has also been confirmed, with Cronbach's alpha coefficients of 0.74 reported by Azarnoosh et al. (2012) and 0.81 reported by Jafari et al. (2022).

Statistical Analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS version 16. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants' demographic characteristics and study variables. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Independent t‐tests, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), Welch's ANOVA, and Kruskal–Wallis tests were applied, as appropriate, to compare structural empowerment and nurses' Intention to Stay in the Profession across demographic groups. Pearson's correlation coefficient was used to examine the relationship between structural empowerment and nurses' Intention to Stay in the Profession. Multiple linear regression analyses were performed to examine the associations between structural empowerment dimensions and nurses' Intention to Stay in the Profession, and to estimate the relative contribution of overall structural empowerment within the cross‐sectional study design. Given the cross‐sectional nature of the study, regression coefficients were interpreted as measures of association rather than evidence of causality. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

All instruments were scored according to the original scoring guidelines. Incomplete questionnaires were excluded from the analysis, and no imputation was performed for missing data. Data were entered independently by trained research assistants and verified through double‐entry checking to minimize data entry errors.

The results of the structural empowerment questionnaire showed that the highest and lowest scores obtained by nurses were 25 and 5.75, respectively. The mean total structural empowerment score (possible range: 6–30) was 15.3 ± 3.24, indicating a moderate level of perceived empowerment. Based on the mean scores of the structural empowerment subscales, access to opportunities had the highest mean score (3.7 ± 0.85), followed by access to information (3.2 ± 0.87), Organizational Relationships Scale (ORS) (3.1 ± 0.9), access to resources (2.5 ± 0.91), Job Activities Scale (JAS) (2.44 ± 0.8), and access to support (2.1 ± 0.93), which had the lowest mean score. The mean score of the Global Empowerment (GE) items was 2.5 ± 0.97, reflecting a moderate perception of empowerment among nurses.

The mean score of intention to stay in the profession (possible range: 4–20) was 12.6 ± 3.8, indicating a moderate level of intention to stay. Overall, 19.4% of nurses reported a low intention to stay in the profession, 48.4% reported a moderate intention, and 32.2% reported a high intention to stay in the profession.

Pearson's correlation analysis demonstrated a positive and statistically significant relationship between overall structural empowerment and nurses' intention to stay in the profession (r = 0.494, p < 0.001). Among the structural empowerment dimensions, access to resources (r = 0.347, p = 0.006), access to support (r = 0.281, p = 0.027), Organizational Relationships Scale (ORS) (r = 0.297, p = 0.019), and Global Empowerment (GE) (r = 0.498, p < 0.001) were also significantly and positively correlated with intention to stay in the profession. In contrast, access to opportunities (r = 0.246, p = 0.054), access to information (r = 0.219, p = 0.087), and Job Activities Scale (JAS) (r = 0.218, p = 0.089) were not significantly correlated with intention to stay in the profession (Table 2).

To examine the statistical association between structural empowerment dimensions and nurses' intention to stay in the profession, a multiple linear regression analysis using the Enter method was conducted. The dimensions of structural empowerment were entered simultaneously to identify which specific dimensions were independently associated with intention to stay in the profession. Among all dimensions entered, only access to resources and global empowerment showed statistically significant associations, while the remaining dimensions did not. Access to resources (β = 0.29, p < 0.05) and global empowerment (β = 0.31, p < 0.05) were significantly associated with intention to stay in the profession. Although access to support and Organizational Relationships Scale (ORS) were significantly associated with intention to stay in the bivariate correlation analysis, these associations were no longer statistically significant after controlling for the other structural empowerment dimensions in the multiple regression model, suggesting that their relationships with intention to stay may be explained by variance shared with the other empowerment dimensions. The model was statistically significant (F = 11.7, p < 0.05) and explained 28% of the variance in intention to stay in the profession (R 2 = 0.28) (Table 3). Access to opportunity, access to information, access to support, Job Activities Scale (JAS), and Organizational Relationships Scale (ORS) were not significant predictors (p > 0.05).

Given that among the four subscales of structural empowerment (access to opportunities, access to information, access to resources, and access to support) and the global empowerment items entered in the dimension‐level model, only access to resources and global empowerment reached statistical significance, an additional simple linear regression analysis was conducted using the overall structural empowerment score as a single variable. This second model served two purposes: first, to determine whether structural empowerment, treated as a unified construct, explains a comparable or greater proportion of variance than its individual dimensions, which would suggest that the dimensions act jointly rather than independently; and second, to provide a more parsimonious model that could be used in contexts where measuring all four dimensions separately is impractical. The results indicated that overall structural empowerment was significantly associated with intention to stay in the profession (β = 0.62, p < 0.001), explaining 38% of the variance (R 2 = 0.38, F = 37.6, p < 0.001)—a notably higher proportion than the 28% explained by the dimension‐level model. This finding suggests that the dimensions of structural empowerment may contribute to intention to stay through shared or overlapping variance, such that the overall construct demonstrates a stronger association than the individual dimensions considered separately (Table 3). It should be noted that, as a cross‐sectional study, these regression analyses reflect concurrent statistical associations and do not allow for causal or predictive inferences.

Analysis of group differences based on demographic characteristics indicated that age (intention to stay: p = 0.23; structural empowerment: p = 0.64), gender (p = 0.072 and p = 0.78), work experience (p = 0.63 and p = 0.96), marital status (p = 0.52 and p = 0.37), and ward (p = 0.61 and p = 0.71) were not significantly associated with either study variable. In contrast, employment status was the only factor that showed a significant difference in intention to stay in the profession (p = 0.008), while no significant difference was observed in structural empowerment across employment groups (p = 0.54). Overall, the findings suggest that, except for employment status, the examined individual and occupational characteristics did not substantially contribute to variations in intention to stay in the profession or structural empowerment among employees (Table 4).

Discussion

This study found a significant positive relationship between structural empowerment and nurses' intention to stay in the profession, indicating that nurses who perceived higher levels of structural empowerment were more likely to stay in the profession. This finding is consistent with previous studies confirming the role of structural empowerment in strengthening nurses' intention to stay and organizational commitment (Hauck et al. 2011; Meng et al. 2015). In a similar study, Rajan (2021) stated that improving organizational structure is necessary for attracting and retaining employees (Rajan 2021). Xu et al. (2025) reported that structural empowerment enhances nurses' professional competence and contributes to a more person‐centered clinical environment (Xu et al. 2025). Although the study did not directly examine intention to stay, these organizational benefits may indirectly support nurse retention. In addition, a systematic review by Fragkos et al. (2020) reported that structural empowerment is strongly associated with organizational commitment among nurses, suggesting that empowering work environments may contribute to workforce retention (Fragkos et al. 2020). The consistency of the present findings with previous studies may reflect the important role of organizational factors, including access to resources, managerial support, effective communication, and professional development opportunities, in enhancing nurses' intention to stay in the profession. This may be particularly relevant in specialized settings such as operating rooms, where high workloads and interdisciplinary collaboration increase the need for an empowering work environment.

The findings of the present study showed that among the dimensions of structural empowerment, access to opportunities received the highest mean score, whereas access to support received the lowest. This finding is consistent with the studies by Hauck et al. (2011) and Hewko et al. (2015), which highlighted access to organizational support, resources, and professional development opportunities as key determinants of nurses' perceptions of structural empowerment (Hauck et al. 2011; Hewko et al. 2015). In contrast, Sarıköse and Çelik (2024) found that newly graduated nurses reported access to information as the highest‐rated dimension of structural empowerment, whereas access to resources received the lowest score. These differences may be explained by variations in participants' professional experience, organizational characteristics, and healthcare system contexts (Sarıköse and Çelik 2024). These discrepancies are likely attributable to differences in leadership practices, organizational culture, resource availability, and professional support systems across healthcare settings.

These findings can also be interpreted through the lens of Social Exchange Theory, which posits that employees who perceive genuine and equitable organizational support are more likely to develop stronger organizational commitment and a greater intention to stay (Cropanzano and Mitchell 2005). The low score for access to support emphasizes that organizational structures alone are insufficient unless nurses perceive them as genuinely available and supportive in everyday clinical practice (Al‐Otaibi et al. 2024).

The moderate level of structural empowerment observed in this study indicates that nurses experience only part of the empowering conditions available in their work environment. A key contributor to this pattern is the gap between access to professional development opportunities and access to the resources needed to apply them: nurses may receive training, yet without adequate organizational support or suitable working conditions, they are unable to translate these skills into practice (Shokouhi et al. 2024; Torabi et al. 2022), which may in turn reduce motivation and job satisfaction. This is consistent with previous studies reporting limited resources and organizational support (Al‐Otaibi et al. 2024; Cowden and Cummings 2015); however, the present study suggests that within Iranian hospital settings, this gap is not incidental but structurally embedded in clinical environments, systematically constraining nurses' ability to make use of available opportunities. This gap may be particularly important in perioperative environments such as operating rooms, where nurses rely heavily on adequate staffing, equipment availability, effective communication, and interdisciplinary teamwork to deliver safe patient care.

Nurses' intention to stay in the profession was moderate in the present study. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in different countries. These studies have identified inadequate managerial support, limited opportunities for career advancement, heavy workload, and job‐related stress as major barriers to nurses' intention to stay in the profession (Al‐Otaibi et al. 2024; Halter et al. 2017). In contrast, studies have shown that supportive work environments and stronger organizational support are associated with higher levels of nurses' intention to stay in the profession (Marufu et al. 2021). These differences have been attributed to greater organizational support, better professional development opportunities, and more favourable working conditions. Variations in healthcare systems and nurse retention policies may also explain these inconsistent findings.

In acute care and surgical settings, heavy workloads, complex clinical decision‐making, and time pressure may further strengthen the impact of organizational factors. In such environments, even small improvements in organizational support or workload management may enhance nurses' intention to stay in the profession.

In the present study, employment status was significantly associated with nurses' intention to stay in the profession. Permanent staff reported higher levels of intention to stay in the profession than contractual and mandatory service nurses. This finding may be explained by the greater job security, organizational commitment, and perceived career stability typically experienced by permanently employed nurses. Consistent with Kanter's Structural Empowerment Theory, secure employment may facilitate greater access to organizational resources, support, and professional development opportunities, which can strengthen employees' commitment to their profession and increase their intention to stay in the profession (Sarmiento et al. 2004). From this perspective, the observed differences in nurses' intention to stay in the profession across employment groups may reflect differences in access to these organizational conditions.

In the context of the Iranian healthcare system, these findings highlight the importance of reducing disparities associated with employment status. Healthcare organizations should therefore ensure equitable access to organizational resources, support, and opportunities for participation in decision‐making for nurses regardless of their employment status, thereby fostering structural empowerment and ultimately strengthening their intention to stay in the profession.

Overall, the findings of this study suggest that nurses' intention to stay in the profession is not solely a reflection of individual career decisions but is also shaped by the organizational work environment. From a nursing perspective, these findings underscore the importance of structural empowerment as a modifiable organizational attribute that should be considered in nursing management. Accordingly, promoting equitable access to organizational resources, information, support, and professional opportunities may inform managerial and organizational strategies aimed at supporting nurses' intention to stay in the profession.

These findings also have practical implications for nursing practice and leadership by highlighting structural empowerment as a modifiable organizational factor that can be addressed through supportive management, equitable access to resources, and greater nurse participation in decision‐making. Although the study was conducted within the Iranian healthcare context, it adds context‐specific evidence to the international nursing literature by demonstrating the relevance of structural empowerment to nurses' intention to stay in a healthcare system with distinct organizational and workforce characteristics. The findings may therefore be informative for healthcare systems facing similar challenges related to nurse retention and workforce stability. Future multicenter, longitudinal, and intervention‐based studies are recommended to examine the generalizability of these findings and determine whether strategies to strengthen structural empowerment can effectively improve nurses' retention.

Limitations

One of the main limitations of this cross‐sectional study is its temporal constraint. Since this type of study is conducted at a single point in time, it cannot capture long‐term changes or trends, and the findings may only reflect the current situation. A related and critical methodological limitation concerns the interpretation of the regression analyses. Although multiple linear regression was applied, the cross‐sectional design precludes any causal or truly predictive conclusions. All reported regression coefficients reflect concurrent statistical associations only; directionality and causality cannot be established without longitudinal data. Accordingly, terms such as ‘predictor’ are used in a strictly statistical sense throughout this article and should not be interpreted as implying temporal precedence or causal influence. In addition, limitations related to self‐reported data should be considered. All data were collected using questionnaires, which may be influenced by personal bias or inaccurate reporting by nurses. This may lead to potential systematic errors in the data. Furthermore, the use of convenience sampling may introduce selection bias and volunteer bias, as participants who chose to participate may not be fully representative of the overall population of nurses. Therefore, the generalizability of the findings to the wider nursing population should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

The findings suggest that structural empowerment may strengthen nurses' motivation and commitment, thereby supporting their intention to stay in the profession. This may hold particular value in operating room settings, where sustained motivation directly supports surgical team performance and patient safety. In addition, employment stability appears to enhance feelings of security and engagement, which may reduce nurses' intention to leave the profession. Examples of structural empowerment strategies include providing access to continuing professional development opportunities, involving nurses in clinical and organizational decision‐making, ensuring adequate staffing and access to essential resources, and establishing clear career advancement pathways. For perioperative units, this stability also helps preserve the specialized technical skills and team familiarity that take years to develop. Overall, organizational support and improved structural conditions are important factors in promoting nurse retention. Within surgical and acute care settings specifically, these conditions may translate into more effective staffing models, stronger clinical leadership, and ultimately, safer patient outcomes.
