1.
Introduction

One of the most fundamental tools in the analysis of weak solutions to evolution equations is the identity

(1)

\dfrac{d}{dt}\|u(t)\|^{2}_{H}=2(u^{\prime}(t),u(t))_{H},\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in[0,T],

valid for functions
u\in W^{1,2}(0,T;H)
, where
H
is a Hilbert space. This relation plays a central role in deriving energy estimates and a priori bounds in the classical theory of partial differential equations.

In particular, identity (
1
) is a key ingredient in the Faedo-Galerkin method, a classical technique for proving existence of weak solutions by approximating the original problem with a sequence of finite-dimensional ones. The energy identity enables the derivation of uniform bounds for the Galerkin approximations, which are instrumental in extracting weak and weak-* convergent subsequences.

1.1.
The Fractional Version of (
1
) and the Main Result

When dealing with evolution equations involving Riemann–Liouville (i.e.,
D_{t}^{\alpha}
) or Caputo (i.e.,
cD_{t}^{\alpha}
) fractional derivatives of order
\alpha\in(0,1)
, it is well known that the identity (
1
) no longer holds; see
[
6
]
for a detailed discussion. Nevertheless, within the same work, the second author and R. Fehlberg Júnior established a fractional analogue of a generalized version of this identity (cf.
[
23
, Chapter 3, Lemma 1.2]
), under certain additional regularity assumptions:

Theorem 1

(
[
6
, Theorem 4.16]
)
.

Let
H
and
V
be Hilbert spaces satisfying:

(a)

V
is dense in
H
and continuously embedded into it;

(b)

H\equiv H^{\prime}
via the Riesz representation theorem, where
H^{\prime}
denotes the dual of
H
.

Under these conditions, if
V^{\prime}
denotes the dual of
V
, then we have the continuous embeddings

V\subset H\equiv H^{\prime}\subset V^{\prime}.

(i)

Suppose that
p>1/(1-\alpha)
and
p\geq 2
. If
u\in L^{p}(t_{0},t_{1};V)
satisfies
D_{t_{0},t}^{\alpha}u\in L^{2}(t_{0},t_{1};V^{\prime})
and
J_{t_{0},t}^{1-\alpha}\|u(t)\|_{H}^{2}\in W^{1,1}(t_{0},t_{1};\mathbb{R})
, then
u
is almost everywhere equal to a continuous function from
[t_{0},t_{1}]
into
H
, and

D_{t_{0},t}^{\alpha}\|u(t)\|_{H}^{2}\leq 2\langle D_{t_{0},t}^{\alpha}u(t),u(t)\rangle_{V^{\prime},V},\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in[t_{0},t_{1}].

(ii)

If
u\in L^{2}(t_{0},t_{1};V)
,
cD_{t_{0},t}^{\alpha}u\in L^{2}(t_{0},t_{1};V^{\prime})
, and
J_{t_{0},t}^{1-\alpha}\|u(t)\|_{H}^{2}\in W^{1,1}(t_{0},t_{1};\mathbb{R})
, then
u
is almost everywhere equal to a continuous function from
[t_{0},t_{1}]
into
H
, and

cD_{t_{0},t}^{\alpha}\|u(t)\|_{H}^{2}\leq 2\langle cD_{t_{0},t}^{\alpha}u(t),u(t)\rangle_{V^{\prime},V},\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in[t_{0},t_{1}].

In the particular case where
t_{0}=0
,
t_{1}=T
,
H=V
, and either
D_{0,t}^{\alpha}u\in L^{2}(0,T;H)
or
cD_{0,t}^{\alpha}u\in L^{2}(0,T;H)
, the duality pairing reduces to the inner product in
H
, and the inequalities above become

(2)

D_{t}^{\alpha}\|u(t)\|_{H}^{2}\leq 2(D_{t}^{\alpha}u(t),u(t))_{H},\quad\text{a.e. }t\in[0,T],

or

(3)

cD_{t}^{\alpha}\|u(t)\|_{H}^{2}\leq 2(cD_{t}^{\alpha}u(t),u(t))_{H},\quad\text{a.e. }t\in[0,T],

depending on whether one adopts the Riemann–Liouville or Caputo definition of the fractional derivative. Both expressions can be viewed as fractional analogues of the classical identity (
1
), which is fundamental to energy inequalities in the standard theory.

Motivated by this technical obstacle, we present a new proof of (
2
) and (
3
) in the setting
H=L^{2}(\Omega)
, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open, under the additional assumption that
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
.

A key feature of our argument is that the regularity
J_{t_{0},t}^{1-\alpha}\|u(t)\|_{H}^{2}\in W^{1,1}(t_{0},t_{1};\mathbb{R})
follows directly from the properties of the function
u
, rather than being assumed a priori. This refinement simplifies the assumptions required to apply the fractional energy inequality, broadens its applicability, and provides a rigorous foundation for employing the Faedo–Galerkin method in fractional evolution problems.

Our contribution advances the theory of fractional differential equations and paves the way for future developments, such as the study of fractional heat equations, as explored in the final section, and further topics to be addressed in ongoing work.

2.
Notation and Preliminaries

We begin by recalling some classical function spaces, focusing on the Bochner–Lebesgue and Bochner–Sobolev spaces, which generalize the usual
L^{p}
and
W^{1,p}
spaces to the setting of Banach space-valued functions. Throughout this section, we assume that
X
is a Banach space and that
T>0
is fixed. Integrability and differentiability for functions
f:[0,T]\to X
are understood in the Bochner sense; for details, see
[
4
,
14
,
15
]
.

(i)

For
1\leq p\leq\infty
, the space
L^{p}(0,T;X)
consists of all Bochner-measurable functions
f:[0,T]\to X
such that
\|f(t)\|_{X}\in L^{p}(0,T)
. Endowed with the norm

\|f\|_{L^{p}(0,T;X)}:=\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|f(t)\|_{X}^{p}\,dt\right)^{1/p},\quad\text{for }1\leq p<\infty,

and

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;X)}:=\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{t\in[0,T]}\|f(t)\|_{X},

this space is a Banach space.

(ii)

For
1\leq p\leq\infty
, the Sobolev space
W^{1,p}(0,T;X)
consists of all functions
f\in L^{p}(0,T;X)
whose weak derivative
f^{\prime}
exists and belongs to
L^{p}(0,T;X)
. Equipped with the norm

\|f\|_{W^{1,p}(0,T;X)}:=\|f\|_{L^{p}(0,T;X)}+\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(0,T;X)},

this is also a Banach space.

(iii)

For
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}:=\{0,1,\ldots\}
, we denote by
C^{k}([0,T];X)
the Banach space of functions
f:[0,T]\to X
that are
k
times continuously differentiable, equipped with the norm

\|f\|_{C^{k}([0,T];X)}:=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|f^{(j)}(t)\|_{X}.

In this context,
f^{(j)}
denotes the
j
-th classical (i.e., pointwise) derivative of
f
. When
k=0
, we can also write
C([0,T];X)
instead of
C^{0}([0,T];X)
.

(iv)

We denote by
C^{\infty}([0,T];X)
the space of functions that are infinitely differentiable from
[0,T]
into
X
, that is,

C^{\infty}([0,T];X):=\bigcap_{k=0}^{\infty}C^{k}([0,T];X).

(v)

Recall that the support of a function
\varphi:(0,T)\to X
is defined as

\operatorname{supp}(\varphi):=\overline{\{t\in(0,T)\mid\varphi(t)\neq 0\}}.

Finally, the space of test functions, denoted by
C_{c}^{\infty}((0,T);X)
, is the space of all functions
\varphi\in C^{\infty}([0,T];X)
such that
\operatorname{supp}(\varphi)
is compactly contained in
(0,T)
.

The notions introduced above are sufficient for us to define the fractional integral and derivatives considered in the present work. These concepts have been widely employed in the study of fractional calculus (see, for instance,

,

,

,

]
as few examples).

Definition 2
.

Let
0<\beta<\infty
and
f:[0,T]\to X
be given.

(i)

The Riemann–Liouville fractional integral of order
\beta
of
f
is defined by

J_{t}^{\beta}f(t):=\dfrac{1}{\Gamma(\beta)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\beta-1}f(s)\,ds,

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
such that the right-hand side is well defined.

(ii)

The Riemann–Liouville fractional derivative of order
\beta
of a function
f
is defined as

D_{t}^{\beta}f(t):=\dfrac{d^{[\beta]}}{dt^{[\beta]}}\left(J_{t}^{[\beta]-\beta}f(t)\right),

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
for which the right-hand side is well defined. Here,
[\beta]
denotes the smallest integer greater than or equal to
\beta
, and the symbol
(d^{[\beta]}/dt^{[\beta]})
stands for the
{[\beta]}
-th weak derivative of
f
.

(iii)

The Caputo fractional derivative of order
\beta
of
f
is defined by

cD_{t}^{\beta}f(t):=D_{t}^{\beta}\left[f(t)-\sum_{j=0}^{[\beta]-1}\left(\dfrac{t^{j}}{j!}\right)f^{(j)}(0)\right],

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
such that the right-hand side is well defined. Here,
f^{(j)}(0)
denotes the
j
-th classical derivative of
f
evaluated at
t=0
.

From this point on, we present and, when necessary, prove all the results that are used in Section
3
, the main section of this work, where we establish inequalities (
2
) and (
3
).

Proposition 3
.

Let
0<\alpha<1
and
X
a Banach space.

(i)

If
p>1/(1-\alpha)
and
f\in L^{p}(0,T;X)
, it holds that

J_{t}^{1-\alpha}f(t)\big|_{t=0}=0.

(ii)

If
f\in L^{1}(0,T;X)

D^{\alpha}_{t}\left[J_{t}^{{\alpha}}f(t)\right]=f(t),\textrm{ for a.e. }t\in[0,T].

If additionally
J_{t}^{1-{\alpha}}f\in W^{1,1}(0,T;X)
, then

J^{\alpha}_{t}\big[D^{\alpha}_{t}f(t)\big]=f(t)-\frac{t^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\Big(J^{1-\alpha}_{s}f(s)|_{s=0}\Big)

(iii)

If
f\in C([0,T];X)

cD^{\alpha}_{t}\left[J_{t}^{{\alpha}}f(t)\right]=f(t),\textrm{ for every }t\in[0,T].

(iv)

For
f\in W^{1,1}(0,T;X)
we have

cD_{t}^{\alpha}f(t)=J^{1-\alpha}_{t}f^{\prime}(t),\textrm{ for a.e. }t\in[0,T].

Proof.

The proof of item
(i)
can be found in
[
6
, Theorem 4.6]
. Items
(ii)
and
(iii)
are classical identities whose proof can be found in
[
5
, Proposition 2.35]
. Finally, item
(iv)
is proved in
[
5
, Proposition 2.34]
.
∎

Proposition 4

(
[
7
, Theorem 3.1]
)
.

Let
\alpha>0
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
and
f\in L^{p}(0,T;X)
. Then
J_{t}^{\alpha}f(t)
is Bochner integrable and belongs to
L^{p}(0,T;X)
. Furthermore, it holds that

\left[\int_{0}^{T}{\left\|J_{t}^{\alpha}f(t)\right\|^{p}_{X}}\,dt\right]^{1/p}\leq\left[\dfrac{T^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}\right]\|f\|_{L^{p}(0,T;X)}.

In other words,
J_{t}^{\alpha}
is a bounded linear operator from the space
L^{p}(0,T;X)
into itself.

Proposition 5

(
[
10
, Theorem 7]
)
.

Consider
1<p<\infty
,
1/p<\alpha<\infty
and let
f\in L^{p}(0,T;X)
. Then
J_{t}^{\alpha}f\in C\big([0,T];X\big)
and there exists
K_{\alpha,p,T}>0
such that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|J^{\alpha}_{t}f(t)\|_{X}\leq K_{\alpha,p,T}\left[\int_{0}^{T}{\|f(t)\|_{X}^{p}}\,dt\right]^{1/p}.

As a consequence,
J_{t}^{\alpha}
defines a bounded linear operator from the space
L^{p}(0,T;X)
into the space
C\big([0,T];X\big)
.

Corollary 6
.

Let
0<\alpha<1
and
\varphi\in C^{\infty}([0,T];X)
such that
\varphi^{(k-1)}(0)=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}:=\{1,2,\ldots\}
. Then
\psi:=J_{t}^{\alpha}\varphi
belongs to
C^{\infty}([0,T];X)
and satisfies
\psi^{(k-1)}(0)=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

From the regularity of
\varphi
, the condition
\varphi^{(k-1)}(0)=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and item
(iv)
of Proposition
3
, we deduce that

\psi^{(k-1)}(t)=\dfrac{d^{(k-1)}}{dt^{(k-1)}}\Big\{J^{\alpha}_{t}\big[\varphi(t)-\varphi(0)\big]\Big\}=\dfrac{d^{(k-2)}}{dt^{(k-2)}}\Big\{J^{\alpha}_{t}\varphi^{\prime}(t)\Big\}\\
=\dfrac{d^{(k-2)}}{dt^{(k-2)}}\Big\{J^{\alpha}_{t}\big[\varphi^{\prime}(t)-\varphi^{\prime}(0)\big]\Big\}=\dfrac{d^{(k-3)}}{dt^{(k-3)}}\Big\{J^{\alpha}_{t}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t)\Big\}=\ldots=J^{\alpha}_{t}\varphi^{(k-1)}(t),

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and almost every
t\in[0,T]
. Then, Proposition
5
implies that
\psi\in C^{\infty}([0,T];X)
. Moreover, by applying item (i) of Proposition
3
, we conclude that
\psi^{(k-1)}(0)=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
∎

Proposition 7

(
[
8
, Theorem 5]
)
.

Consider
1<p<\infty
and
0<\alpha<1/p
. If
f\in L^{p}(0,T;X)
, then
J_{t}^{\alpha}f(t)
is Bochner integrable in
[0,T]
and belongs to
L^{p/(1-p\alpha)}(0,T;X)
. Moreover, there exists
K_{\alpha,p,T}>0

\left[\int_{0}^{T}{\left\|J^{\alpha}_{t}f(t)\right\|_{X}^{p/(1-p\alpha)}}\,dt\right]^{(1-p\alpha)/p}\leq K_{\alpha,p,T}\left[\int_{0}^{T}{\|f(t)\|_{X}^{p}}\,dt\right]^{1/p}.

In other words,
J_{t}^{\alpha}
defines a bounded linear operator from the space
L^{p}(0,T;X)
into the space
L^{p/(1-p\alpha)}(0,T;X)
.

(
[
9
, Theorem 28]
)
.

For
1<p<\infty

K_{p,q}>0

\left[\int_{0}^{T}{\|J_{t}^{1/p}f(t)\|_{X}^{q}}\,dt\right]^{1/q}\leq K_{p,q}\left[\int_{0}^{T}{\|f(t)\|_{X}^{p}}\,dt\right]^{1/p},

3.
Auxiliary Results and the Fractional Energy Inequality

The goal of this section is to demonstrate that the most delicate condition in Theorem
1
, namely
J^{1-\alpha}_{t}\left\|u\right\|_{H}^{2}\in W^{1,1}(0,T;\mathbb{R})
, is indeed satisfied under the additional assumptions outlined in the introduction. To make the discussion more concrete, we begin by assuming that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an open set and that
T>0
is fixed. In what follows, we derive inequalities (
2
) and (
3
) under appropriate hypotheses, thus extending the applicability of Theorem
1
, as anticipated from the outset of this work.

To keep this work self-contained, we start by recalling the following particular case of
[
23
, Chapter 3, Lemma 1.2]
.

Proposition 9
.

If
f\in W^{1,2}\left(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
, then

\frac{d}{dt}\left\|f(t)\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=2\big(f^{\prime}(t),f(t)\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
. Note that the derivatives above are understood in the weak sense.

With this in mind, we now turn to the main results of this work: Theorem
18
, which deals with the Riemann–Liouville fractional derivative of order
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and Corollary
19
, which addresses the Caputo fractional derivative of the same order.

Let us now present a technical lemma.

Lemma 10
.

Let
0<\alpha<1
. If
f\in W^{1,2}\left(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
, then

2\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(t-s)^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}f^{\prime}(x,s)\big[f(x,t)-f(x,s)\big]ds\,dx\\
=\dfrac{\alpha}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{t}(t-w)^{\alpha-1}\left(\int_{0}^{w}(t-s)^{-\alpha}f^{\prime}(x,s)\,ds\right)^{2}dw\,dx,

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

By applying the Fubini-Tonelli theorem, we obtain

(4)

\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(t-s)^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}f^{\prime}(x,s)\big[f(x,t)-f(x,s)\big]ds\,dx\\
=\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(t-s)^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}f^{\prime}(x,s)\int_{s}^{t}f^{\prime}(x,w)\,dw\,ds\,dx\\
=\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(x,w)\int_{0}^{w}\frac{(t-s)^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}f^{\prime}(x,s)\,ds\,dw\,dx,

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
.

Now, noting that for almost every
w\in(0,t)
and almost every
x\in\Omega
we have

\dfrac{(t-w)^{\alpha}}{2}\frac{d}{dw}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{w}(t-s)^{-\alpha}f^{\prime}(x,s)ds\right)^{2}\right]=f^{\prime}(x,w)\left(\int_{0}^{w}(t-s)^{-\alpha}f^{\prime}(x,s)ds\right),

we deduce from (
4
) the identity

(5)

\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(t-s)^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}f^{\prime}(x,s)\big[f(x,t)-f(x,s)\big]ds\,dx\\
=\dfrac{1}{2\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{t}(t-w)^{\alpha}\frac{d}{dw}\left(\int_{0}^{w}(t-s)^{-\alpha}f^{\prime}(x,s)ds\right)^{2}dw\,dx,

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
.

At this point, notice that an integration by parts yields,

(6)

\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{t}(t-w)^{\alpha}\frac{d}{dw}\left(\int_{0}^{w}(t-s)^{-\alpha}f^{\prime}(x,s)\,ds\right)^{2}dw\,dx\\
=\int_{\Omega}\left[(t-w)^{\alpha}\left(\int_{0}^{w}(t-s)^{-\alpha}f^{\prime}(x,s)\,ds\right)^{2}\right]\Bigg|_{w=0}^{w=t}dx\\
+\alpha\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{t}(t-w)^{\alpha-1}\left(\int_{0}^{w}(t-s)^{-\alpha}f^{\prime}(x,s)\,ds\right)^{2}dw\,dx,

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
.

We now show that the boundary terms vanish. Since
(t-w)^{\alpha}|_{w=t}=0
, we have

(7)

\int_{\Omega}\left|(t-w)^{\alpha}\left(\int_{0}^{w}(t-s)^{-\alpha}f^{\prime}(x,s)\,ds\right)^{2}\right|_{w=t}dx=0.

Secondly, using
0\leq s\leq w
implies
(t-s)^{-\alpha}\leq(t-w)^{-\alpha}
, and applying the classical Minkowski’s inequality for integrals (see
[
19
, Theorem 202]
for details), we obtain

\left(\int_{\Omega}\left|\int_{0}^{w}(t-s)^{-\alpha}f^{\prime}(x,s)\,ds\right|^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}\leq\int_{0}^{w}(t-s)^{-\alpha}\|f^{\prime}(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,ds\\
\leq(t-w)^{-\alpha}\int_{0}^{w}\|f^{\prime}(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,ds,

which implies

(8)

\int_{\Omega}\left|(t-w)^{\alpha}\left(\int_{0}^{w}(t-s)^{-\alpha}f^{\prime}(x,s)\,ds\right)^{2}\right|_{w=0}dx\\
\leq\lim_{w\rightarrow 0}\left[(t-w)^{-\alpha}\left(\int_{0}^{w}\|f^{\prime}(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,ds\right)^{2}\right]=0.

Combining (
6
), (
7
), and (
8
), along with (
5
), we conclude the desired identity.

We now establish a preliminary, weaker version of inequality (
3
), using the lemma above. This intermediate result is instrumental in proving the main results that follow. It is worth emphasizing that the proof of this proposition was inspired by
[

Proposition 11
.

Let
0<\alpha<1
. If
f\in W^{1,2}\left(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
, then

cD_{t}^{\alpha}\left\|f(t)\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq 2\left(cD_{t}^{\alpha}f(t),f(t)\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

Firstly, since
f\in W^{1,2}\left(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
, item
(iv)
of Proposition
3
and Proposition
9
ensure that we can express the Caputo derivative as

(9)

cD_{t}^{\alpha}\left\|f(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=J^{1-\alpha}_{t}\left[\frac{d}{dt}\left\|f(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right]=2J^{1-\alpha}_{t}\big(f^{\prime}(t),f(t)\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
.

On the other hand, item
(iv)
of Proposition
3
also ensures that

(10)

\left(cD_{t}^{\alpha}f(t),f(t)\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\left(J^{1-\alpha}_{t}f^{\prime}(t),f(t)\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
.

Combining (
9
) and (
10
), we obtain

2\left(cD_{t}^{\alpha}f(t),f(t)\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-cD_{t}^{\alpha}\left\|f(t)\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
=2\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(t-s)^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}f^{\prime}(x,s)\,ds\,f(x,t)\,dx-2\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(t-s)^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(x,s)f(x,s)\,dx\,ds,

and, applying the Fubini-Tonelli Theorem, we conclude that

2\left(cD_{t}^{\alpha}f(t),f(t)\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-cD_{t}^{\alpha}\left\|f(t)\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
=2\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(t-s)^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}f^{\prime}(x,s)\big[f(x,t)-f(x,s)\big]\,ds\,dx,

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
. Therefore, it follows from Lemma
10
that

2\left(cD_{t}^{\alpha}f(t),f(t)\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-cD_{t}^{\alpha}\left\|f(t)\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
=\dfrac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{t}\alpha(t-w)^{\alpha-1}\left(\int_{0}^{w}(t-s)^{-\alpha}f^{\prime}(x,s)\,ds\right)^{2}dw\,dx\geq 0,

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
.
∎

The previous proposition established the first fractional counterpart of Proposition
9
. We now aim to refine the result presented in Proposition
11
. To this end, we begin by proving a few auxiliary results.

Proposition 12
.

Let
0<\alpha<1
and
f\in L^{\infty}(0,T;X)
such that
D^{\alpha}_{t}f\in L^{2}(0,T;X)
. Then there exists a sequence
(\psi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{\infty}([0,T];X)
satisfying
\psi^{(k-1)}_{j}(0)=0
for all
j,k\in\mathbb{N}
, such that

\psi_{j}\to f\quad\text{and}\quad D^{\alpha}_{t}\psi_{j}\to D^{\alpha}_{t}f,\text{ in }L^{2}(0,T;X).

Proof.

Since
D^{\alpha}_{t}f\in L^{2}(0,T;X)
, there exists
(\phi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{\infty}_{c}((0,T);X)
with

\phi_{j}\to D^{\alpha}_{t}f,\quad\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;X).

Define
\psi_{j}(t):=J^{\alpha}_{t}\phi_{j}(t)
. Thanks to Corollary
6
, we have
\psi_{j}\in C^{\infty}([0,T];X)
with
\psi^{(k-1)}_{j}(0)=0
, for all
j,k\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, Proposition
4
allows us to deduce that

(11)

\psi_{j}=J^{\alpha}_{t}\phi_{j}\to J^{\alpha}_{t}\big(D^{\alpha}_{t}f\big),\textrm{ in }L^{2}(0,T;X).

However, since
f\in L^{\infty}(0,T;X)
, items
(i)
and
(ii)
of Proposition
3
ensure that

(12)

J^{\alpha}_{t}\big(D^{\alpha}_{t}f(t)\big)=f(t)-\frac{t^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\left[J^{1-\alpha}_{s}f(s)\right]\Big|_{s=0}=f(t),

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
. Therefore, from (
11
) and (
12
), we deduce the convergence

\psi_{j}(t)\to f(t),\quad\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;X).

Moreover, observe that, from item
(ii)
of Proposition
3
, we also obtain

D^{\alpha}_{t}\psi_{j}(t)=D^{\alpha}_{t}J^{\alpha}_{t}\phi_{j}(t)=\phi_{j}(t)\to D^{\alpha}_{t}f(t),\textrm{ in }L^{2}(0,T;X).

This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

An analogous version of the previous proposition, but involving the Caputo fractional derivative, is the following:

Corollary 13
.

If
0<\alpha<1
and
f\in C([0,T];X)
is such that
cD_{t}^{\alpha}f\in L^{2}(0,T;X)
, then there exists
(\psi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{\infty}([0,T];X)
such that
\psi_{j}(0)=f(0)
,
\psi^{(k)}_{j}(0)=0
for all
j,k\in\mathbb{N}
, and

\psi_{j}\to f\quad\text{and}\quad cD_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{j}\to cD_{t}^{\alpha}f,\text{ in }L^{2}(0,T;X).

Proof.

If we define
g(t)=f(t)-f(0)
, then
g\in L^{\infty}(0,T;X)
and
D_{t}^{\alpha}g=cD_{t}^{\alpha}f\in L^{2}(0,T;X)
. Thus, Proposition
12
ensures the existence of a sequence
({\tilde{\psi}}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
that belongs to
C^{\infty}([0,T];X)
such that
{\tilde{\psi}}^{(k-1)}_{j}(0)=0
for all
j,k\in\mathbb{N}
, and

{\tilde{\psi}}_{j}\to g\quad\text{and}\quad D^{\alpha}_{t}{\tilde{\psi}}_{j}\to D^{\alpha}_{t}g\text{ in }L^{2}(0,T;X).

Define
\psi_{j}(t)={\tilde{\psi}}_{j}(t)+f(0)
. Then the result follows.
∎

At this point in our work, we need to extend the result of Alsaedi et al. in
[
3
, Lemma 1]
so that it can be applied to vector-valued functions. To this end, we first recall the notions of Hölder continuous and fractionally continuously differentiable functions, as well as the relationship between them (see
[
11
]
for more details).

Definition 14
.

Given
0<\beta<1
, we define:

(i)

H^{\beta}([0,T];X)=\big\{f\in C([0,T];X):\text{ there exists }M>0\text{ such that }

\big\|f(t)-f(s)\big\|_{X}\leq M{|t-s|^{\beta}},\ \forall\,t,s\in[0,T]\big\};

(ii)

C^{\beta}([0,T];X)=\big\{f\in C([0,T];X):cD_{t}^{\beta}f\in C([0,T];X)\big\}.

Proposition 15

(
[
11
, Corollary 23]
)
.

Assume that
0<\gamma<1
. Then
C^{\gamma}([t_{0},t_{1}],X)\subset H^{\gamma}([t_{0},t_{1}],X)
.

Theorem 16
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and let
H
be a Hilbert space with inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{H}
. If one of the following conditions is satisfied:

(i)

f_{1}\in C([0,T];H)
, the Riemann–Liouville fractional derivative of
f_{1}
of order
\alpha
exists at
t\in(0,T]
, and
f_{2}\in H^{\beta}([0,T];H)
, with
0<\alpha<\beta<1
;

(ii)

f_{1}\in H^{\beta}([0,T];H)
and
f_{2}\in H^{\delta}([0,T];H)
for some
0<\beta<1
,
0<\delta<1
, with
0<\alpha<\beta+\delta
, and the Riemann–Liouville fractional derivatives of both
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
of order
\alpha
exist at
t\in(0,T]
,

then the following identity holds at each
t\in(0,T]
:

D^{\alpha}_{t}\big(f_{1}(t),f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}=\big(D^{\alpha}_{t}f_{1}(t),f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}+\big(f_{1}(t),D^{\alpha}_{t}f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}\\
-\frac{\alpha}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\big(f_{1}(s)-f_{1}(t),f_{2}(s)-f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}}{(t-s)^{\alpha+1}}ds-\frac{\big(f_{1}(t),f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)t^{\alpha}},

Proof.

We focus on the proof of item
(ii)
, as the argument for
(i)
follows in a similar way. By definition, it holds that

(13)

D^{\alpha}_{t}\big(f_{1}(t),f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left\{\frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}\big(f_{1}(s),f_{2}(s)\big)_{H}\,ds\right]\right\}\\
\qquad\quad=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left\{\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\int_{0}^{t+\varepsilon}(t+\varepsilon-s)^{-\alpha}\big(f_{1}(s),f_{2}(s)\big)_{H}\,ds\right.\right.\\
\left.\left.-\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}\big(f_{1}(s),f_{2}(s)\big)_{H}\,ds\right]\right\}.

Now notice that for
t,s\in[0,T]
we have

(14)

\big(f_{1}(s),f_{2}(s)\big)_{H}=\big(f_{1}(s)-f_{1}(t),f_{2}(s)-f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}\\
+\big(f_{1}(s),f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}+\big(f_{1}(t),f_{2}(s)\big)_{H}-\big(f_{1}(t),f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}.

Inserting (
14
) into identity (
13
), and assuming that the limit is taken from the right of
0
(i.e.,
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}
), we obtain an expression that can be rearranged as the sum of four pairs of terms. For clarity, we denote these terms by

\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left\{\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\int_{0}^{t+\varepsilon}(t+\varepsilon-s)^{-\alpha}\big(f_{1}(s)-f_{1}(t),f_{2}(s)-f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}ds\right.\right.\\
\left.\left.-\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}\big(f_{1}(s)-f_{1}(t),f_{2}(s)-f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}\,ds\right]\right\}=:\mathcal{I}_{1}(t),

\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left\{\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\int_{0}^{t+\varepsilon}(t+\varepsilon-s)^{-\alpha}\big(f_{1}(s),f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}\,ds\right.\right.\\
\left.\left.-\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}\big(f_{1}(s),f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}ds\,\right]\right\}=:\mathcal{I}_{2}(t),

\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left\{\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\int_{0}^{t+\varepsilon}(t+\varepsilon-s)^{-\alpha}\big(f_{1}(t),f_{2}(s)\big)_{H}\,ds\right.\right.\\
\left.\left.-\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}\big(f_{1}(t),f_{2}(s)\big)_{H}ds\,\right]\right\}=:\mathcal{I}_{3}(t),

and finally

\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left\{\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\int_{0}^{t+\varepsilon}(t+\varepsilon-s)^{-\alpha}\big(f_{1}(t),f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}\,ds\right.\right.\\
\left.\left.-\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}\big(f_{1}(t),f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}\,ds\right]\right\}=:\mathcal{I}_{4}(t),

for every
t\in(0,T)
such that the four limits above exist. In fact, we shall prove that each of these limits does indeed exist.

We begin with
\mathcal{I}_{2}(t)
and
\mathcal{I}_{3}(t)
, for which it is straightforward to verify that

\mathcal{I}_{2}(t)=\big(D^{\alpha}_{t}f_{1}(t),f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}\qquad\textrm{ and }\qquad\mathcal{I}_{3}(t)=\big(f_{1}(t),D^{\alpha}_{t}f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}.

For
\mathcal{I}_{4}(t)
, since we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\int_{0}^{t+\varepsilon}(t+\varepsilon-s)^{-\alpha}ds-\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}ds\right]\\
=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\frac{(t+\varepsilon)^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}-\frac{t^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}\right]=t^{-\alpha},

we obtain

\mathcal{I}_{4}(t)=\frac{\big(f_{1}(t),f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)t^{\alpha}}.

Finally, let us address the most complex term, which requires a more careful analysis. First, we rewrite
\Gamma(1-\alpha)\mathcal{I}_{1}(t)=\mathcal{H}_{1}(t)+\mathcal{H}_{2}(t)
, where

\mathcal{H}_{1}(t)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\left[\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{t}^{t+\varepsilon}(t+\varepsilon-s)^{-\alpha}\big(f_{1}(s)-f_{1}(t),f_{2}(s)-f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}\,ds\right]

and

\mathcal{H}_{2}(t)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{t}\left[\frac{(t+\varepsilon-s)^{-\alpha}-(t-s)^{-\alpha}}{\varepsilon}\right]\big(f_{1}(s)-f_{1}(t),f_{2}(s)-f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}\,ds

To deal with
\mathcal{H}_{1}(t)
, we apply the change of variables
w=t+\varepsilon-s
, which gives

\mathcal{H}_{1}(t)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}w^{-\alpha}\big(f_{1}(t+\varepsilon-w)-f_{1}(t),f_{2}(t+\varepsilon-w)-f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}\,dw.

Then, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, along with the Hölder regularity, ensures that

\mathcal{H}_{1}(t)\leq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\frac{w^{-\alpha}}{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon-w)^{\beta+\delta}\,dw=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(\varepsilon h)^{-\alpha}}{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon-\varepsilon h)^{\beta+\delta}\varepsilon\,dh\\
=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{\beta+\delta-\alpha}B(1-\alpha,\beta+\delta+1)=0,

where, above,
B(\cdot,\cdot)
stands for the Beta function.

On the other hand, to handle
\mathcal{H}_{2}(t)
, let us first denote by
g_{\varepsilon}:[0,t]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
the integrand function that defines it, given by

g_{\varepsilon}(s):=\left[\frac{(t+\varepsilon-s)^{-\alpha}-(t-s)^{-\alpha}}{\varepsilon}\right]\,\big(f_{1}(s)-f_{1}(t),f_{2}(s)-f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}.

Note that

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}g_{\varepsilon}(s)=-\alpha(t-s)^{-\alpha-1}\big(f_{1}(s)-f_{1}(t),f_{2}(s)-f_{2}(t)\big)_{H},\quad\text{for a.e. }s\in[0,t].

Moreover, we have

\left|g_{\varepsilon}(s)\right|\leq\left|\frac{(t+\varepsilon-s)^{-\alpha}-(t-s)^{-\alpha}}{\varepsilon}\right|\,(t-s)^{\beta+\delta},\quad\text{for a.e. }s\in[0,t],

thanks to the Hölder continuity of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
.

Consider now the function
h:[0,\varepsilon]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by
h(w)=(t+w-s)^{-\alpha}
. By the Mean Value Theorem, there exists
\theta\in(0,\varepsilon)
such that

\left|\frac{(t+\varepsilon-s)^{-\alpha}-(t-s)^{-\alpha}}{\varepsilon}\right|=\left|\frac{h(\varepsilon)-h(0)}{\varepsilon}\right|=\left|h^{\prime}(\theta)\right|\\
=\alpha(t+\theta-s)^{-\alpha-1}\leq\alpha(t-s)^{-\alpha-1}

Therefore,

\left|g_{\varepsilon}(t)\right|\leq(t-s)^{\beta+\delta-\alpha-1},

for almost every
s\in[0,t].

The aforementioned properties are enough for us to apply the Dominated Convergence Theorem in order to obtain

\mathcal{H}_{2}(t)=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{t}g_{\varepsilon}(s)ds=\int_{0}^{t}-\alpha(t-s)^{-\alpha-1}\big(f_{1}(s)-f_{1}(t),f_{2}(s)-f_{2}(t)\big)_{H}\,ds,

for every
t\in(0,T)
.

By combining the results obtained for
\mathcal{I}_{1}(t),\mathcal{I}_{2}(t),\mathcal{I}_{3}(t)
, and
\mathcal{I}_{4}(t)
in (
13
), and noting that the limit from the left of
0
can be treated analogously, we conclude the proof.
∎

Before presenting our main result, we recall one final theorem from Gorenflo et al.
[
18
]
and generalize it to vector-valued functions. In that work, for
0<\gamma<1
, the authors consider the space
W^{\gamma,2}(0,T;\mathbb{R})
, which denotes the standard fractional Sobolev space (see
[
22
]
for more details), consisting of functions
f\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R})
such that

\big[f\big]_{W^{\gamma,2}(0,T;\mathbb{R})}:=\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}\dfrac{|f(t)-f(s)|^{2}}{|t-s|^{2\gamma+1}}\,ds\,dt\right)^{1/2}<\infty.

Endowed with the norm

\|f\|_{W^{\gamma,2}(0,T;\mathbb{R})}:=\|f\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R})}+\big[f\big]_{W^{\gamma,2}(0,T;\mathbb{R})},

this space becomes a Banach space. In that context, they prove Theorem 2.1, which is the result we intend to employ in our final theorem. It states that there exist constants
m,M>0
such that

(15)

m\|J_{t}^{\gamma}f\|_{W^{\gamma,2}(0,T)}\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}\leq M\|J_{t}^{\gamma}f\|_{W^{\gamma,2}(0,T)},

for every
f\in L^{2}(0,T)
.

Now let us consider the fractional Sobolev-Bochner space
W^{\gamma,2}(0,T;X)
of all functions in
L^{2}(0,T;X)
such that

(16)

\big[f\big]_{W^{\gamma,2}(0,T;X)}:=\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}\dfrac{\|f(t)-f(s)\|_{X}^{2}}{|t-s|^{2\gamma+1}}\,ds\,dt\right)^{1/2}<\infty.

Like before, endowed with the norm

(17)

\|f\|_{W^{\gamma,2}(0,T;X)}:=\sqrt{\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;X)}+\big[f\big]^{2}_{W^{\gamma,2}(0,T;X)}},

it becomes a Banach space. This allows us to prove the following result.

Corollary 17
.

There exist
m,M>0
such that

m\|J_{t}^{\gamma}f\|_{W^{\gamma,2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq M\|J_{t}^{\gamma}f\|_{W^{\gamma,2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))},

for every
f\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
.

Proof.

Let
f\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. Observe that, for almost every
x\in\Omega
, we have
f(x,\cdot)\in L^{2}(0,T)
. Therefore, from (
15
), it follows that

m\|J_{t}^{\gamma}f(x,\cdot)\|_{W^{\gamma,2}(0,T)}\leq\|f(x,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}\leq M\|J_{t}^{\gamma}f(x,\cdot)\|_{W^{\gamma,2}(0,T)},

for almost every
x\in\Omega
, with constants
m
and
M
independent of
x
. Squaring both sides of the inequality, then integrating it over
\Omega
and applying the Fubini–Tonelli Theorem, we obtain the desired result.
∎

We have now established all the necessary results to prove our main theorems. It is worth emphasizing that the next theorem, which we consider the most challenging in the paper, is built from a sequence of ideas that are carefully detailed in the proof, in order to make the argument as clear and organized as possible.

Theorem 18
.

Assume that
0<\alpha<1
and that
f\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
satisfies
D^{\alpha}_{t}f\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. Then
D^{\alpha}_{t}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\in L^{1}(0,T)
and

(18)

D^{\alpha}_{t}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 2\big(D^{\alpha}_{t}f(t),f(t)\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

for almost every
t\in(0,T)
.

Proof.

To clarify the structure of the proof, we divide it into four steps. In the first step, we construct an auxiliary sequence
(\xi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{\infty}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
such that

J^{1-\alpha}_{t}\left\|J^{\alpha}_{t}\xi^{\prime}_{j}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\to J^{1-\alpha}_{t}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},\quad\text{in }L^{1}(0,T).

In the second step, we explain why it is necessary to prove that the sequence

\left(D^{\alpha}_{t}\left\|J^{\alpha}_{t}\xi^{\prime}_{j}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)_{j\in\mathbb{N}}

is Cauchy in
L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{R})
. In the third step, we show that the existence of such a limit allows us to conclude that
D^{\alpha}_{t}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
exists and belongs to
\in L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{R})
. Finally, in the fourth step, we prove inequality (
18
).

Step 1: Construction of an approximating sequence.

We begin by recalling that Proposition
12
ensures the existence of a sequence
(\psi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{\infty}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
such that
\psi^{(k-1)}_{j}(0)=0
for all
j,k\in\mathbb{N}
, and

(19)

\psi_{j}\to f\quad\text{and}\quad D^{\alpha}_{t}\psi_{j}\to D^{\alpha}_{t}f,\quad\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

Define the auxiliary sequence
\xi_{j}(t):=J^{1-\alpha}_{t}\psi_{j}(t)
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Observe that Corollary
6
ensures that
(\xi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{\infty}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
and satisfies
\xi_{j}^{(k-1)}(0)=0
for all
j,k\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, Proposition
4
guarantees the convergence

\xi_{j}\to J^{1-\alpha}_{t}f,\quad\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

Consequently, using the second convergence in (
19
), we obtain

(20)

\xi^{\prime}_{j}=\frac{d}{dt}\Big[J^{1-\alpha}_{t}\psi_{j}\Big]=D^{\alpha}_{t}\psi_{j}\to D^{\alpha}_{t}f,\quad\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

Since
f\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
, items
(i)
and
(ii)
of Proposition
3
ensure that
J^{\alpha}_{t}D^{\alpha}_{t}f(t)=f(t)
for almost every
t\in[0,T]
. This identity, together with Proposition
4
, applied to (
20
), allows us to conclude that

(21)

J^{\alpha}_{t}\xi^{\prime}_{j}\to J^{\alpha}_{t}D^{\alpha}_{t}f=f,\quad\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

Therefore, from (
21
) and, once again using Proposition
4
, we obtain

(22)

J^{1-\alpha}_{t}\left\|J^{\alpha}_{t}\xi^{\prime}_{j}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\to J^{1-\alpha}_{t}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},\quad\text{in }L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{R}).\vskip 2.84544pt

Step 2: The Cauchy sequence in
L^{1}(0,T)
.

In this step, we aim to prove the existence of the weak derivative of
J_{t}^{1-\alpha}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
and to show that it belongs to
L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{R})
. This is the technical obstacle mentioned in the introduction.

To this end, we begin by observing that, by a recursive argument already used in the previous step, it holds that

(23)

J^{\alpha}_{t}\xi_{j}^{\prime}(t)=J^{\alpha}_{t}D^{\alpha}_{t}\psi_{j}(t)=\psi_{j}(t),

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
. Combining this with the fact that
\psi_{j}(0)=0
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
, and applying item
(iv)
of Proposition
3
together with Proposition
9
, we deduce

D^{\alpha}_{t}\left\|J^{\alpha}_{t}\xi^{\prime}_{j}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\frac{d}{dt}\left[J^{1-\alpha}_{t}\left\|\psi_{j}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right]=2J^{1-\alpha}_{t}\left(\psi_{j}^{\prime}(t),\psi_{j}(t)\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
. Hence,

D^{\alpha}_{t}\left\|J^{\alpha}_{t}\xi^{\prime}_{j}(\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\in L^{1}(0,T).

Therefore, for any
\theta\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
, it follows that

(24)

\int_{0}^{T}J^{1-\alpha}_{t}\left\|J^{\alpha}_{t}\xi^{\prime}_{j}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\theta^{\prime}(t)dt=-\int_{0}^{T}\left[D^{\alpha}_{t}\left\|J^{\alpha}_{t}\xi^{\prime}_{j}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right]\theta(t)dt.

and from (
22
)

(25)

\int_{0}^{T}J^{1-\alpha}_{t}\left\|J^{\alpha}_{t}\xi^{\prime}_{j}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\theta^{\prime}(t)dt\to\int_{0}^{T}J^{1-\alpha}_{t}\left\|f(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\theta^{\prime}(t)dt.

Now, if we show that

(26)

\left(D^{\alpha}_{t}\left\|J^{\alpha}_{t}\xi_{j}^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)_{j\in\mathbb{N}}

is a Cauchy sequence in the space
L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{R})
, then there exists a function
g\in L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{R})
such that

(27)

D^{\alpha}_{t}\left\|J^{\alpha}_{t}\xi_{j}^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\to g,\quad\text{in }L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{R}).

Consequently, combining (
24
), (
25
), and (
27
), we obtain

\int_{0}^{T}g(t)\theta(t)\,dt=-\int_{0}^{T}J^{1-\alpha}_{t}\left|f(t)\right|^{2}\theta^{\prime}(t)\,dt,

for every
\theta\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
. In other words, the weak fractional derivative
D^{\alpha}_{t}\left\|f(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
exists and coincides with
g(t)
for almost every
t\in[0,T]
.

Step 3: Existence of the fractional derivative.

The previous step shows that, to complete the first part of the proof, it remains to verify that (
26
) forms a Cauchy sequence in
L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{R})
. We now turn our attention to this task. To begin, observe that for every
j,k\in\mathbb{N}
,

(28)

\left|\big(D^{\alpha}_{t}\psi_{j}(t),\psi_{j}(t)\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\big(D^{\alpha}_{t}f(t),f(t)\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right|\\

for almost every

. Consequently, by combining (

(36)

\big(D^{\alpha}_{t}\psi_{j},\psi_{j}\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\to\big(D^{\alpha}_{t}f,f\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\text{in }L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{R}).

for all

Before stating our final result, we find it useful to include a brief remark, as noted by Carvalho-Neto and Fehlberg Júnior in
[
6
, Remark 4.17]
, concerning the well-posedness of the Caputo fractional derivative
cD_{t}^{\alpha}f
for functions
f\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R})
.

In general, the value
f(0)
may not be well defined for functions in
L^{2}(0,T;X)
, which poses a technical issue in defining the Caputo fractional derivative at the initial time. To overcome this difficulty, some authors assume
f\in C([0,T];X)
, but this requirement is stronger than necessary. In our setting, it suffices to assume that
f
admits a continuous representative on an interval
[0,\delta]
, for some
0<\delta\leq T
.

As a consequence of Theorem
18
, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 19
.

Let
0<\alpha<1
and suppose
f\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap C([0,\delta];L^{2}(\Omega))
for some
0<\delta\leq T
, with
cD_{t}^{\alpha}f\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. Then
cD_{t}^{\alpha}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\in L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{R})
, and

cD_{t}^{\alpha}\left\|f(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 2\big(cD_{t}^{\alpha}f(t),f(t)\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

For this proof, let us define the auxiliary function
g(t):=f(t)-f(0)
. Observe that
g\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
and
D^{\alpha}_{t}g=cD_{t}^{\alpha}f\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. By Theorem
18
, it follows that
D^{\alpha}_{t}\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\in L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{R})
and

(38)

D^{\alpha}_{t}\|g(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 2\big(D^{\alpha}_{t}g(t),g(t)\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
.

Note that

(39)

\big(D^{\alpha}_{t}g(t),g(t)\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\big(cD_{t}^{\alpha}f(t),f(t)\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\big(D^{\alpha}_{t}[f(t)-f(0)],f(0)\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
. On the other hand,

D^{\alpha}_{t}\|g(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=D^{\alpha}_{t}\int_{\Omega}|f(x,t)-f(x,0)|^{2}\,dx\\
=\int_{\Omega}D^{\alpha}_{t}[f(x,t)]^{2}-2f(x,0)D^{\alpha}_{t}f(x,t)+D^{\alpha}_{t}[f(x,0)]^{2}\,dx\\
=\int_{\Omega}D^{\alpha}_{t}\left([f(x,t)]^{2}-[f(x,0)]^{2}\right)-2f(x,0)D^{\alpha}_{t}f(x,t)+2D^{\alpha}_{t}[f(x,0)]^{2}\,dx,

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
, which leads to

(40)

D^{\alpha}_{t}\|g(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=cD_{t}^{\alpha}\|f(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-2\big(D^{\alpha}_{t}[f(t)-f(0)],f(0)\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
.

Comparing (
38
), (
39
) and (
40
), we conclude the proof.
∎

4.
The Fractional Heat Equation

In this final section, we apply the Faedo–Galerkin method together with Corollary
19
to the fractional heat equation in order to prove the existence of a weak solution.

We emphasize that standard notations for Sobolev spaces, Lebesgue spaces, and test functions are adopted throughout this last section. As these are classical, we refer the reader to
[
16
, Chapter 5]
for precise definitions and notations. Furthermore, routine computations are omitted to keep the exposition concise and focused on the essential new arguments required by the fractional setting.

Consider
1/2<\alpha<1
,
\Omega
an open and bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
T>0
fixed,
f:[0,T]\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
such that

(41)

\displaystyle\mathop{\operatorname{ess\,sup}}_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}\|f(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,ds<\infty,

and
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Let us build a weak solution to the fractional parabolic problem

(42)

\begin{cases}cD_{t}^{\alpha}u(x,t)-\Delta u(x,t)=f(x,t),&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T],\\
u(x,t)=0,&(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times[0,T],\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),&x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

Let us begin with the definition of a weak solution to problem (
42
). In this work, we follow exactly the same classical definition of a weak solution for the heat equation, in order to keep our focus on the main subject of this study, namely the fractional energy equations. However, we shall broaden this discussion in future works.

Definition 20
.

We say that a function
u\in L^{2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))
with
D_{t}^{\alpha}\big(u(\cdot)-u_{0}\big)\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
is a weak solution of (
42
) if:

(i)

for each
v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and for almost every
t\in[0,T]
, we have

\big\langle D_{t}^{\alpha}(u(t)-u_{0}),v\big\rangle_{H^{-1}(\Omega),H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}+\big(\nabla u(t),\nabla v\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\big(f(t),v\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)};

(ii)

the initial condition is satisfied, i.e.,
u(0)=u_{0}
.

Remark 21
.

We now turn to three topics previously introduced in this section that may not have been entirely clear. Although they are related, each requires a separate discussion.

(i)

First, observe that the regularity condition in (
41
), imposed on function
f
, is more restrictive than the classical assumption
f\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. Indeed, Hölder’s inequality yields

\qquad\int_{0}^{t}\|f(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,ds=\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{1-\alpha}\Big[(t-s)^{\alpha-1}\|f(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\Big]\,ds\\
\leq\big[T^{2-\alpha}/(2-\alpha)\big]\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}\|f(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,ds.

This additional regularity is essential in the present proof to obtain that
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
, a key property to use Theorem
18
.

(ii)

We also require that
1/2<\alpha<1
to ensure the continuity of the solution from
[0,T]
into
L^{2}(\Omega)
. This restriction arises from the limited regularity properties of the Riemann–Liouville fractional integral; see Theorems
5
and
7
for details.

(iii)

Finally, we need to discuss condition
(ii)
in Definition
20
, since it only satisfies
u\in L^{2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))
and
D_{t}^{\alpha}(u(\cdot)-u_{0})\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
, which is not sufficient to rigorously assign meaning to the initial condition.

To ensure the well-posedness of this requirement, we also need the assumption
1/2<\alpha<1
. In fact, as will be shown below, we prove that
D_{t}^{\alpha}\big(u(\cdot)-u_{0}\big)\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. Then, combining this with the regularity assumed in item
(i)
above, i.e.,
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
, we may apply items
(i)
and
(ii)
of Proposition
3
to deduce that

u(t)=u_{0}+J_{t}^{\alpha}\Big[D_{t}^{\alpha}\big(u(t)-u_{0}\big)\Big],

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
. Moreover, since Proposition
5
ensures
J_{t}^{\alpha}\big[D_{t}^{\alpha}(u(t)-u_{0})\big]
belongs to
C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
, it follows that
u
also belongs to
C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
. Hence, we may conclude that the initial condition is well-posed.

Classically, we begin by considering
(w_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
as an orthogonal basis of
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
that is also an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega)
(the complete set of normalized eigenfunctions of the operator
-\Delta
in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
; see
[
16
, Section 6.5.1]
).

Fix now
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Let us prove the existence of
\{g_{im}\}_{i=1}^{m}
a sequence of functions, where
g_{im}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
, such that, by defining

(43)

u_{m}(t):=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}g_{im}(t)w_{i},\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,m\},

we have
u_{m}(t)
satisfies

(44)

\left\{\begin{aligned} cD_{t}^{\alpha}(u_{m}(t),w_{j})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+(\nabla u_{m}(t),\nabla w_{j})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&=(f(t),w_{j})_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\\
u_{m}(0)&=u_{0m},\end{aligned}\right.

for every
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
and
t\in(0,T]
, where
u_{0m}
is the orthogonal projection of
u_{0}
onto
\text{span}\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}\}
, given by

(45)

u_{0m}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}(u_{0},w_{j})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}w_{j}\rightarrow u_{0},\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\Omega),\quad\text{as }m\to\infty.

From (
43
) and (
44
), we conclude that the
(g_{1m},\ldots,g_{mm})
must satisfy the following ODE:

(46)

\left\{\begin{aligned} cD_{t}^{\alpha}U(t)+B_{m}U(t)&=F_{m}(t),\\
U(0)&=U_{0m},\end{aligned}\right.

with

\displaystyle B_{m}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}(\nabla w_{1},\nabla w_{1})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\ldots&(\nabla w_{m},\nabla w_{m})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle F_{m}(t)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}(f(t),w_{1})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
\vdots\\
(f(t),w_{m})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\end{array}\right]\hskip 28.45274pt\textrm{ and}\hskip 28.45274ptU_{0m}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}(u_{0},w_{1})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
\vdots\\
(u_{0},w_{m})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\end{array}\right].

Since
F_{m}\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{m})
(recall item
(i)
of Remark
21
), (
46
) is a system of fractional ordinary differential equations with a forcing term given by a Carathéodory function, which has already been studied in
[
12
,
20
]
. Therefore, the existence of
g_{jm}\in C([0,T];\mathbb{R})
with
cD_{t}^{\alpha}g_{jm}\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R})
, for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, is guaranteed.

4.1.
Energy Estimates

Here, we need to carry out two distinct constructions in order to derive the energy estimates required to prove the existence and uniqueness of solutions to problem (
44
).

(i)

If we multiply the first equation in (
44
) by
g_{jm}
for each
1\leq j\leq m
and sum the resulting equations, we obtain

(cD_{t}^{\alpha}u_{m}(t),u_{m}(t))_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+(\nabla u_{m}(t),\nabla u_{m}(t))_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=(f(t),u_{m}(t))_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
. Since
u_{m}\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
and we also have
cD_{t}^{\alpha}u_{m}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
, Corollary
19
ensures
cD_{t}^{\alpha}\|u_{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\in L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{R})
and

(1/2)cD_{t}^{\alpha}\|u_{m}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\nabla u_{m}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq(f(t),u_{m}(t))_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
. This, together with Young’s inequality, implies that there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

(47)

D_{t}^{\alpha}\Big(\|u_{m}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\|u_{0m}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Big)+c_{1}\|u_{m}(t)\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq c_{2}\|f(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
.

(a)

By integrating both sides of (
47
) from
0
to
t\in(0,T]
, thanks to item
(i)
of Proposition
3
, we obtain

\qquad\qquad\quad\dfrac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}\Big(\|u_{m}(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\|u_{0m}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Big)\,ds\\
+c_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\|u_{m}(s)\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}^{2}\,ds\leq c_{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|f(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,ds,

what implies that there exists
C_{1}>0
such that

(48)

\|u_{m}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}\leq C_{1}\left(\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|f\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\right).

For the fact that
\|f\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}
is finite, see item
(i)
of Remark
21
.

(b)

We now proceed in a standard way. Fix
v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
with
\|v\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}\leq 1
, and decompose
v=v_{1}+v_{2}
, where
v_{1}\in\text{span}\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}\}
and
(v_{2},w_{k})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0
for every
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. Since functions
(w_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
are orthogonal in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, it follows that

\qquad\qquad\quad(v_{2},w_{k})_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(v_{2},w_{j})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}w_{j},w_{k}\right)_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\\
=(v_{2},w_{k})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}(w_{k},w_{k})_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}=0,

for each
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, and therefore

\|v_{1}\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\|v\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}\leq 1.

Then, from the first equation in (
44
), since
(w_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
are orthonormal in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, we obtain

\qquad\qquad\quad(cD_{t}^{\alpha}u_{m}(t),v)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=(cD_{t}^{\alpha}u_{m}(t),v_{1})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
=(f(t),v_{1})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-(\nabla u_{m}(t),\nabla v_{1})_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
. Combined with (
48
), this implies the existence of a constant
C_{2}>0
such that

(49)

\|cD_{t}^{\alpha}u_{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))}\leq C_{2}\left(\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|f\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\right).

(c)

Finally, by applying
J_{t}^{\alpha}
to both sides of (
47
) and using items
(i)
and
(ii)
of Proposition
3
, we obtain

\qquad\qquad\quad\|u_{m}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+c_{1}J_{t}^{\alpha}\Big(\|u_{m}(t)\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}^{2}\Big)\leq\|u_{0m}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+c_{2}J_{t}^{\alpha}\Big(\|f(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\Big),

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
, which leads to the existence of a constant
C_{3}>0
such that

(50)

\qquad\qquad\quad\|u_{m}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\leq C_{3}\left(\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right.\\
+\left.\displaystyle\mathop{\operatorname{ess\,sup}}_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}\|f(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,ds\right).

(ii)

We now need to perform an estimate that is not typically used in the classical analysis of the heat equation, but which is of fundamental importance in our setting. If we multiply the first equation in (
44
) by
cD_{t}^{\alpha}g_{jm}
for each
1\leq j\leq m
and sum the resulting equations, we obtain

\qquad\quad(cD_{t}^{\alpha}u_{m}(t),cD_{t}^{\alpha}u_{m}(t))_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\big(\nabla u_{m}(t),\nabla\big[cD_{t}^{\alpha}u_{m}(t)\big]\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
=(f(t),cD_{t}^{\alpha}u_{m}(t))_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
. Since
\nabla u_{m}\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
and
cD_{t}^{\alpha}\nabla u_{m}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
, Corollary
19
, together with Young’s inequality, implies that
cD_{t}^{\alpha}\|\nabla u_{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\in L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{R})
and

(51)

\|D_{t}^{\alpha}u_{m}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+cD_{t}^{\alpha}\|\nabla u_{m}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\|f(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
. By integrating both sides of (
51
) from
0
to
t\in(0,T]
, we obtain

\qquad\quad\int_{0}^{t}\|cD_{t}^{\alpha}u_{m}(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\,ds\\
+\dfrac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}\|u_{m}(s)\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}^{2}\,ds\leq\int_{0}^{t}\|f(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,ds,

what implies that

(52)

\|cD_{t}^{\alpha}u_{m}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}.

4.2.
Existence, Uniqueness, and Some Regularity Properties of the Solution

We now aim to prove that the sequence
(u_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
admits a subsequence that converges weakly to a function, which we shall show to be the weak solution of (
42
). For clarity and organization, we state this result explicitly below.

Theorem 22
.

There exists a unique weak solution
u
to problem (
42
). Moreover,
u\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
, with
cD_{t}^{\alpha}u\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
.

Proof.

We shall split this proof in 4 steps.

Step 1: The Weak Convergences.

From the energy estimates (
48
), (
49
), (
50
) and (
52
), by the Banach–Alaoglu Theorem, there exists a subsequence
(u_{m_{l}})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(u_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
and a function
u\in L^{2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
such that
D_{t}^{\alpha}(u(\cdot)-u_{0})\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
and

(53)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}u_{m_{l}}&\rightharpoonup u,&\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)),\\
u_{m_{l}}&\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}u,&\text{in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),\\
D_{t}^{\alpha}(u_{m_{l}}-u_{0{m_{l}}})&\rightharpoonup D_{t}^{\alpha}(u-u_{0}),&\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).\end{array}\right.

Step 2: Proving that
u
is the Weak Solution.

Fix an integer
N
and consider a test function
v\in C^{\infty}_{c}((0,T);H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))
of the form

(54)

\nu(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}d^{k}(t)w_{k},

where each
d^{k}(t)
belongs to
C^{\infty}_{c}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
. Choosing
m\geq N
, we multiply the first equation in (
44
) by
d^{k}(t)
for
k=1,\dots,N
, and integrate over
[0,T]
to obtain

\int_{0}^{T}(D_{t}^{\alpha}(u_{m}(t)-u_{0m}),\nu(t))_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}(\nabla u_{m}(t),\nabla\nu(t))_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\,dt\\
=\int_{0}^{T}(f(t),\nu(t))_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,dt.

Now, by taking
m=m_{l}
and using the convergences (
45
) and (
53
), we can pass to the limit and derive

(55)

\int_{0}^{T}(D_{t}^{\alpha}(u(t)-u_{0}),\nu(t))_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}(\nabla u(t),\nabla\nu(t))_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\,dt\\
=\int_{0}^{T}(f(t),\nu(t))_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,dt.

Since functions of the form (
54
) are dense in
L^{2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))
, we conclude that (
55
) holds for every
\nu
in this space. In particular, for almost every
t\in[0,T]
and every
v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, it holds that

(56)

\big\langle D_{t}^{\alpha}(u(t)-u_{0}),v\big\rangle_{H^{-1}(\Omega),H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}+\big(\nabla u(t),\nabla v\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\big(f(t),v\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.\vskip 5.69046pt

Step 3: Regularity of
u
and the Initial Condition.

Here we simply observe that, by using items
(i)
and
(ii)
of Proposition
3
and the regularities of
u
given in (
53
), we obtain

J^{\alpha}_{t}\big[D^{\alpha}_{t}\big(u(t)-u_{0})\big]=u(t)-u_{0},

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
. Then, by applying Proposition
5
and noting that
1/2<\alpha<1
, we conclude that
u\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
, and finally, thanks again to item
(i)
of Proposition
3
, that
u(0)=u_{0}
.

Step 4: Uniqueness of Solution.

Consider a pair of weak solutions
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
satisfying the properties established so far. Define
\sigma=u_{1}-u_{2}
, and observe that, thanks to (
56
), we have:

(D_{t}^{\alpha}\sigma(t),\nu(t))_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+(\nabla\sigma(t),\nabla\nu(t))_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}=0

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
.

Now, given that
\sigma\in L^{2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))
, we set
\nu(t)=\sigma(t)
. Since we already know that
\sigma\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
and
D_{t}^{\alpha}\sigma\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
, we obtain from Theorem
18
that
D_{t}^{\alpha}\|\sigma\|^{2}
belongs to
L^{1}(0,T;\mathbb{R})
and

(57)

D_{t}^{\alpha}\left\|\sigma(t)\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq 0,

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
. Finally, item
(ii)
of Proposition
3
ensures that, by applying
J_{t}^{\alpha}
to both sides of (
57
), we obtain

\left\|\sigma(t)\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq 0,

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
, which leads us to conclude that
\sigma(t)\equiv 0
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
for almost every
t\in[0,T]
, thus proving uniqueness.
∎
