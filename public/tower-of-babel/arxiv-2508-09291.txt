1+O(1) asymptotics for loop percolation in five and higher dimensions
Quirin Vogel 1

Abstract
. We calculate the one-arm probability and the two-point function for loop percolation in dimensions five and higher on the lattice up to first order. This answers a question posed by Y. Chang and A. Sapozhnikov in Probability Theory and Related Fields (2016) 164:979–1025.

Our contribution
: the purpose of this article is to study subcritical loop percolation in dimensions five and higher. Define
\alpha_{\#}
as the largest
\alpha>0
such that the expected size of the cluster at zero is finite.

We prove that for
\alpha<\alpha_{\#}
, the limit

\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{d-2}\mathbb{P}\left(0\longleftrightarrow\partial B_{n}\right)\,,

(1.1)

exists. We also show that

\lim_{\left|x\right|\to\infty}\left|x\right|^{4-2d}\mathbb{P}\left(0\longleftrightarrow x\right)\,,

(1.2)

exists. We also compute the value of limit in both cases and show that the law of the processes conditioned to connect can be realized by adding a single long loop to the soup.

In
[
CS16
]

0<\alpha_{\#}\leq\alpha_{c}
and that for
\alpha<\alpha_{\#}

C_{1}\alpha n^{2-d}\leq\mathbb{P}\left(0\longleftrightarrow\partial B_{n}\right)\leq C_{2}(\alpha)n^{2-d}\,,

(1.3)

where
0\longleftrightarrow\partial B_{n}
denotes the event that there exists a nearest-neighbor path of open edges connecting the origin to the boundary of the ball of radius

[

Our work was inspired by
[
DCRT20
]
, where it was shown that for Poisson-Boolean percolation with polynomial tails in the subcritical domain, connecting the origin to the complement of the centered ball
B_{n}
with radius
n
, has the same price as facilitating this connection with a single mark. However, the same cannot be true for loop percolation because the trajectories of random walks are less dense. We show that for
d\geq 5
and
\alpha
small enough, forcing to origin to be connected to
\partial B_{n}
is realized as follows: sample a subcritical loop soup. To its (highly localized) cluster at the origin, attach (harmonically) a long loop
\omega
that intersects
\partial B_{n}
. Note that it could happen that this long
\omega
intersects the origin, but with a positive probability, it does not. We furthermore show that a similar story holds for the two-point function: to connect the origin to a faraway
x
, first independently sample clusters at
0
and at
x
. Then, add a big loop connecting the two clusters. In the proof, we also explicitly give error bounds depending on
n

\left|x\right|
.

Our main tools are refined loop/percolation estimates together with the use of the Mecke equation, which has not been applied in this context to the best of our knowledge. The Mecke equation allows us to separate the law conditioned to connect into an unconditioned law decorated with a long loop. We expect this behavior to hold for a variety of long-range subcritical percolation models based on Poisson point processes, and hence expect that our method is useful to other cases.

We stress the importance of

Next, we state our result for the two-point function:

Proof

3.1.
Background

It is convenient to describe loop percolation using a Poisson point process. For this, set

\Omega=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\left\{f\colon\left\{0,\ldots n\right\}\to\mathbb{Z}^{d}\textnormal{ with }\left|f(m)-f(m+1)\right|=1\textnormal{ for all }m\in\left\{0,\ldots,n-1\right\}\right\}\,,

(3.1)

the space of all finite nearest neighbor trajectories (here,
\left|x\right|
is the standard Euclidean norm). For
\omega\in\Omega
, let
\ell(\omega)
be the duration of
\omega
. Define then
\omega
with
\ell(\omega)=n
, the loop weight
\mu(\omega)
as

\mu(\omega)=\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{1}{2d}\right)^{n}\!\mathbbm{1}\!\left\{\omega(0)=\omega(n)\right\}\,.

(3.2)

Alternatively, we may write
\mu=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{P}_{x,x}^{n}
where
\mathbb{P}_{x,x}^{n}(A)=\mathbb{P}_{x}\left(A\cap\left\{\omega(n)=x\right\}\right)
is the unnormalized bridge measure of a simple random walk.

We can then define
\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}
, the measure introduced in the Section
1
as the Poisson point process (see
[
LP18
]
for a reference on Poisson point processes) with intensity measure
\alpha\mu
. Formally, we may think
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}={\rm e}^{-\alpha\mu[\Omega]}\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\alpha^{n}\mu^{\otimes n}}{n!}
. A sample
\eta
from
\mathbb{P}
can be described as a random point measure or as a collection of loops and can be written as

\eta=\sum_{\omega\in\eta}\delta_{\omega}=\left\{\omega\in\Omega\colon\omega\in\eta\right\}\,.

(3.3)

Here, we mention a subtle point, as a loop
\omega
can appear multiple times in
\eta
and hence we need a multiset to describe
\eta
(
\mathbb{P}
is not simple in point-process terminology). However, for loop percolation, we can ignore this, as a loop being sampled twice does not change connectivity. Formally, we can avoid the issue of double loops by considering the Poisson point process on
continuous-time random walk bridges
, which is simple, since the waiting times of the walker do not change connectivity; see
[
LJ24
]
for the loop measure on continuous-time random walk bridges.

Write
\omega\in\eta
if the loop
\omega
is contained in
\eta
. Write
\eta\cup\omega=\eta+\delta_{\omega}
for the configuration obtained when adding loop
\omega
to
\eta
, similarly for
\eta\cup\zeta=\eta+\zeta
for
\zeta

\omega\setminus\omega
for the configuration where we remove the loop
\omega

The Poisson point process is convenient when working with loop percolation. It has several implications, which we list now.

Lemma 3.1

(Inequalities)
.

Define an event as increasing if
\eta\in A
satisfies
\eta+\zeta\in A
for all
\zeta
. Then for increasing
A,B
, we have
(FKG-inequality)

\mathbb{P}\left(A\cap B\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(A\right)\mathbb{P}\left(B\right),.

(3.4)

Furthermore, write
A\Box B
if
A
and
B
are realized through a disjoint set of loops (not edges!). We then have for
A,B

(BK-inequality)

\mathbb{P}\left(A\Box B\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(A\right)\mathbb{P}\left(B\right)\,.

(3.5)

See
[
LP18
, Theorem 20.4]
for a reference of the FKG inequality for Poisson point processes and
[
MR96
, Theorem 2.3]
for the BK inequality.

Recall the Mecke equation, an important tool in the theory of (Poisson) point processes.

Lemma 3.2

(The Mecke equation)
.

If
\eta

\mathbb{P}
is a Poisson point process with intensity
\mu

f\geq 0

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\omega\in\eta}f\left(\eta\setminus\omega,\omega\right)\right]=\int\mathbb{E}\left[f(\eta,\omega)\right]{\rm d}\mu(\omega)\,.

(3.6)

See
[
LP18
, Theorem 4.5]
for a reference.

We compute for

(

for the domain).

Thus, we conclude

(3.26)

Finally, we know that by the strong Markov property and the additivity of the stopping times, we get that

\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\tau_{2n},\mathrm{diam}(\omega)>m,\tau_{2n}<\infty\right]\leq Cn\left(m^{2-d}\right)^{n}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\tau_{2},\mathrm{diam}(\omega)>m,\tau_{2}<\infty\right]\\
={\mathcal{O}}\left(nm^{4-d}\left(m^{2-d}\right)^{n}\right)\,.

(3.27)

Inserting this into Eq. (

\mu\left[\#{\mathcal{R}}\left(\omega\right),\mathrm{diam}(\omega)>m,0\in\omega\right]={\mathcal{O}}\left(m^{4-d}\right)\,,

and hence (with Eq. (

∎

y\in\omega

\left\{0\longleftrightarrow\partial B_{M}(y)\right\}\Box\left\{x\longleftrightarrow B_{M}(y)\right\}\,.

(3.72)

Now, either
\mathrm{dist}(y,x)>n/3
or
\mathrm{dist}(y,0)>n/3
. Assume without loss of generality the latter. We can then bound the probability of
E_{3}
by

\sum_{a\in B_{k}}\mathbb{P}\left(0\xleftrightarrow{\,\,{{\mathcal{C}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\subseteq k)}}}}_{0}}\,\,}a\textnormal{ and }\exists\omega\colon a\xleftrightarrow{\,\,{\omega}\,\,}\partial B_{m}\textnormal{ and }B_{M}(y)\xleftrightarrow{\,\,{\eta\setminus\left(\omega\cup{\mathcal{C}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\subseteq k)}}}}_{0}\right)}\,\,}x\right)\,.

(3.73)

By the BK-inequality again, we can bound the above by

{\mathcal{O}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\right]m^{2-d}\mathbb{P}\left(x\longleftrightarrow B_{M}(y)\right)\right)\,.

(3.74)

Since
\mathbb{P}\left(x\longleftrightarrow B_{M}(y)\right)={\mathcal{O}}\left(M^{d-1}n^{4-2d}\right)
, we have that Eq. (
3.74
) is at most of order
n^{(2-d)(1+\varepsilon)+(4-2d)+(d-1)(1-\delta)}
. However, as
\varepsilon>\frac{1}{7}
, we can choose
\delta<\varepsilon
such bound is in fact
o\left(n^{4-2d}\right)
:

(1+\varepsilon)(2-d)+(d-1)(1-\delta)<(1+\delta)(2-d)+(d-1)(1-\delta)\leq 1-7\delta\leq 0\,,

(3.75)

if
\delta\geq\frac{1}{7}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Lower bound of Theorem
2
. We have

\mathbb{P}\left(0\longleftrightarrow x\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\exists!\omega\colon{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\left(\eta\setminus\omega\right)\subseteq B_{K}\textnormal{ and }{\mathcal{C}}_{x}\left(\eta\setminus\omega\right)\subseteq B_{K}(x)\textnormal{ and }{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\left(\eta\setminus\omega\right)\xleftrightarrow{\,\,{\omega}\,\,}{\mathcal{C}}_{x}\left(\eta\setminus\omega\right)\right).

(3.76)

Using the Mecke equation, we can rewrite this as

\alpha\int{\rm d}\mathbb{P}(\eta)\mu\left[{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\left(\eta\right)\xleftrightarrow{\,\,{\omega}\,\,}{\mathcal{C}}_{x}\left(\eta\right)\right]\!\mathbbm{1}\!\left\{\mathrm{diam}\left({\mathcal{C}}_{0}\right)\leq K,\,\mathrm{diam}\left({\mathcal{C}}_{x}\right)\leq K\right\}\,.

(3.77)

By Lemma
3.5
, the above is equal to up to first order

\alpha C_{d}^{2}\left|x\right|^{4-2d}\int{\rm d}\mathbb{P}(\eta)\mathrm{Cap}\left({\mathcal{C}}_{0}\right)\mathrm{Cap}\left({\mathcal{C}}_{x}\right)\!\mathbbm{1}\!\left\{\mathrm{diam}\left({\mathcal{C}}_{0}\right)\leq K,\,\mathrm{diam}\left({\mathcal{C}}_{x}\right)\leq K\right\}\,.

(3.78)

Taking the limit
K\to\infty
gives

\lim_{\left|x\right|\to\infty}\left|x\right|^{2d-4}\mathbb{P}\left(0\longleftrightarrow x\right)\geq\alpha C_{d}^{2}\lim_{\left|x\right|\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Cap}\left({\mathcal{C}}_{0}\right)\mathrm{Cap}\left({\mathcal{C}}_{x}\right)\right]\geq\alpha C_{d}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Cap}\left({\mathcal{C}}_{0}\right)\right]^{2}\,,

(3.79)

where the last step follows from translation invariance and the FKG-inequality.

Upper bound of Theorem
2
. Write
n=\left|x\right|
for convenience.

Case 1:
suppose that we have shown that for
m=\frac{n}{\log(n)^{4}}

\mathbb{P}\left(0\longleftrightarrow x\right)\sim\mathbb{P}\left(x\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\textnormal{ and }\exists!\omega\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\colon\mathrm{diam}(\omega)>m\right)\,.

(3.80)

Note that
{\mathcal{C}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\leq m)}}}}_{0}\subseteq B_{M}
outside a set of negligible probability, if we choose
M=\frac{n}{\log(n)^{2}}
, on account of Lemma
3.8
.

For
K<n-M
, we can exclude the case

\left\{x\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\textnormal{ and }\exists!\omega\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}\colon\mathrm{diam}(\omega)>m\textnormal{ and }\mathrm{dist}(x,\omega)>K\right\}\,,

(3.81)

as in that case there would exist a
y\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\leq m)}}}}\cup{\mathcal{R}}\left(\omega\right)
that connects to
x
. However, such
y
would necessarily have to travel a distance of
\min\left\{K,n-M\right\}=K
to connect to
x
which costs
K^{4-2d}
. If we choose
K=K_{\alpha}=m^{\frac{\alpha}{2d-4}}
, we get that the probability of the event above is bounded by

{\mathcal{O}}\left(m^{2-d}m^{2}K^{4-2d}\right)={\mathcal{O}}\left(m^{4-2d-\alpha}\right)=o\left(n^{4-2d}\right)\,,

(3.82)

where the last equality is true if
\alpha>d
(and therefore
\alpha/\left(2d-4\right)>\frac{5}{6}
). Hence, we know that there exists a unique loop
\omega
connecting
{\mathcal{C}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\leq m)}}}}
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{x}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\leq m)}}}}\subseteq B_{M}(x)
. By symmetry, we obtain that
\mathrm{dist}(0,\omega)\leq K
. We then can conclude as before: using the Mecke equation and Lemma
3.5
, we get as in Eq. (
3.77
)

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists!\omega{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\subseteq M)}}}}\xleftrightarrow{\,\,{\omega}\,\,}{\mathcal{C}}_{x}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\subseteq M)}}}}\right)\sim\frac{C_{d}^{2}}{n^{4-2d}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Cap}\left({\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\subseteq M)}}}}\right)\mathrm{Cap}\left({\mathcal{C}}_{x}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\subseteq M)}}}}\right)\right]\,.

(3.83)

However, since
\mathrm{Cap}\left({\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\subseteq M)}}}}\right)
and
\mathrm{Cap}\left({\mathcal{C}}_{x}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\subseteq M)}}}}\right)
are independent, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Cap}\left({\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\subseteq M)}}}}\right)\mathrm{Cap}\left({\mathcal{C}}_{x}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\subseteq M)}}}}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Cap}\left({\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\subseteq M)}}}}\right)\right]^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Cap}\left({\mathcal{C}}_{0}\right)\right]^{2}\,.

(3.84)

Note that there must exist at least one
\omega
of diameter exceeding
m
, on account of Lemma
3.8
. It therefore remains to exclude the possibility of two such
\omega
’s existing.

Case 2
: denote the two large
\omega
’s by
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}
. Note that it cannot hold that both intersect
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\leq m)}}}}
, since we can employ the same reasoning as in Case 1, but need to pay an additional price of
m^{2-d}
. Suppose that
{\mathcal{C}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\leq m)}}}}\cap\omega_{1}\neq\emptyset
. Recall that
K=K_{\alpha}=m^{\frac{\alpha}{2d-4}}
. We can exclude the case that
\mathrm{dist}(x,\omega_{1})>K
, again as in Case 1.

Assume now that
\mathrm{dist}(x,\omega_{1})\leq K
. Note that we can choose
\alpha>d
such that
m/K>K^{1/7}
since
(2d-4)7/8>d
if
d\geq 5
. However, this means that we can apply Lemma
3.10
to bound the probability that the connection between
0
and
x
needs
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
, as this event is bounded by

o\left(m^{4-2d}m^{2}K^{d-2}K^{4-2d}\right)=o\left(m^{6-2d-\alpha/2}\right)=o\left(n^{4-2d}\right)\,.

(3.85)

Hence, we can conclude that

\mathbb{P}\left(0\longleftrightarrow x\right)\sim\mathbb{P}\left(\exists!\omega\colon{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\leq m)}}}}\xleftrightarrow{\,\,{\omega}\,\,}{\mathcal{C}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\leq m)}}}}_{x},\,{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\leq m)}}}}\subset B_{M}\textnormal{ and }{\mathcal{C}}_{x}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{{(\leq m)}}}}\subset B_{M}(x)\right)\,.

(3.86)

This shows that we only need to consider Case 1. Hence, the proof of the upper bound is finished.
