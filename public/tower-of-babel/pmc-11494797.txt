Diarrhea and its associated factors among children aged under five years in Madagascar, 2024: a multilevel logistic regression analysis
Authors: Gebeyehu Lakew, Amlaku Nigusie Yirsaw, Eyob Ketema Bogale, Getnet Alemu Andarge, Demis Getachew, Eyob Getachew, Abiyu Abadi Tareke
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiarrhea, defined as three or more loose stool per day, is a major cause of child mortality. Exploring its prevalence, and influencing factors is crucial for public health decision and targeted interventions. This study aimed to investigate these aspects using 2021 Madagascar demographic health survey data.MethodThis study employed a cross-sectional design, analyzing data from a total of 8,740 weighted under-five children. The dependent variable was the occurrence of diarrhea, which was measured based on caregiver-reported incidents within the previous two weeks prior to the survey. To identify factors associated with diarrhea, a multilevel logistic regression model was utilized, allowing for the examination of both individual and contextual factors. Statistical significance was determined at a p-value of less than 0.05.ResultsThe analysis identified several significant factors associated with diarrhea in children under five. Children aged 7–12 months had a significantly higher risk of diarrhea (OR = 2.699, 95% CI: 2.028–3.591, p < 0.001) compared to those aged 0–6 months, while those aged 13–24 months were also at increased risk (OR = 2.079, 95% CI: 1.579–2.737, p < 0.001). Children using improved water sources had a lower likelihood of diarrhea (OR = 0.742, 95% CI: 0.583–0.944, p = 0.015). Female children were less likely to experience diarrhea compared to males (OR = 0.823, 95% CI: 0.708–0.956, p = 0.011). Mothers aged 25–34 years (OR = 0.741, 95% CI: 0.626–0.878, p = 0.001) and 35–49 years (OR = 0.628, 95% CI: 0.502–0.787, p = 0.03) had lower odds of their children having diarrhea compared to younger mothers (15–24 years). Primary maternal education was associated with a lower risk of diarrhea (OR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.622–0.958, p = 0.019). Children from poorer (OR = 0.744, 95% CI: 0.591–0.937, p = 0.012) and middle-income households (OR = 0.732, 95% CI: 0.564–0.949, p = 0.019) had lower odds of diarrhea compared to those from the poorest households. Media exposure was significantly associated with higher odds of diarrhea (OR = 1.462, 95% CI: 1.208–1.769, p < 0.001).ConclusionMaternal age over 25 years, child age between 7 months to 2 years, and media access are risk factors for diarrhea. In contrast, medium or low wealth levels, the mother’s educational status, the child’s sex, and the source of drinking water seem to be protective factors. Public health interventions should prioritize improving access to clean water and sanitation facilities, along with promoting handwashing with soap. Educational campaigns targeted towards mothers, especially those with lower education levels, can significantly improve hygiene practices, safe water handling, and early symptom recognition of diarrhea.

Background

Most cases of diarrhea (passing loose stools frequently) come from germs that get into our body. These germs can be bacteria, viruses, or parasites [1–3]. Diarrhea can be categorized into acute, persistent, and chronic based on its duration. This classification is crucial for diagnosis and treatment planning.The epidemiological context where diarrhea occurs can help clinicians narrow down potential causes and guide appropriate investigations [4].They spread through contaminated food or water, or from close contact with someone who is infected, especially if hygiene is poor. In fact, a whopping 90% of diarrhea cases was associated with things like bad sanitation, dirty water, and not washing hands well enough.

Diarrhea is a serious illness, especially for young children in low-income countries. It’s the second biggest cause of death among kids under five and a major reason why they become malnourished. The prevalence of stunted growth among children in Madagascar is 42%, which translates to approximately 420 per 1,000 children or 4,200 per 10,000 children. This highlights the significant public health issue, as Madagascar has one of the world’s highest rates of stunting prevalence [5]. The incidence in childhood diarrhea in Madagascar is predicted to be 8.16% [6]. Research done in the past has shown that diarrhea in children is a major health concern in Madagascar [7].

Past research on childhood diarrhea in Madagascar has either been limited to studies within hospitals or clinics (institution-based), or focused on smaller rural areas and just the individual child’s risk factors (individual-level). This new study aims to provide a broader picture [8, 9]. This is the first nationwide study in Madagascar to investigate both individual and community-level factors that contribute to childhood diarrhea.

This study improves on past research in Madagascar by going beyond the limitations of traditional methods. Previous studies used a simpler statistical approach (traditional binary logistic regression) to find factors linked to diarrhea. This new study uses a more powerful method that can better account for the bigger picture [10–12]. There is a notable drawback of traditional binary logistic regression that its failure to account for the hierarchical structure of the DHS data [13]. Because the old method (traditional binary logistic regression) doesn’t account for how the data is structured (children grouped within communities), the results of past studies might be misleading. They could overestimate or underestimate how important certain factors are (model parameters). This new study uses a more advanced method (multivariable multilevel logistic analysis) that takes this clustering into account. This will give us more accurate and reliable results because it considers the full picture of the data.

This study breaks new ground in Madagascar by examining childhood diarrhea on a national scale for the first time. It goes beyond past research methods that couldn’t fully account for how children are grouped within communities. By using a more powerful statistical approach, the study gains a clearer picture. The data comes straight from the latest national survey, giving the most up-to-date look at Madagascar’s fight against diarrhea in young children. The large sample size and national reach make the findings more reliable and relevant than previous studies. Ultimately, the research zeroes in on how common diarrhea is in different parts of Madagascar among children under five. By analyzing data from the 2021 Madagascar Demographic Health Survey, the study will identify factors that contribute to these illnesses and help improve prevention efforts.

Methods

Study area, data source and study period

This study employed data obtained from the Madagascar Demographic Health Survey (DHS), conducted between March and July 2021. Multilevel logistic regretion analysis was performed to study this demographic health survey. The DHS is a nationally representative survey designed to collect comprehensive information on the health, population, and socioeconomic conditions of the country.

Madagascar’s administrative framework is hierarchical. Since 2009, the country is divided into 23 regions as its primary administrative units. These regions are further subdivided into 114 districts, which constitute the second tier of administration. At the grassroots level, districts are composed of commune, classified as either urban or rural. There are a total of 1,549 communes in Madagascar. Each commune is governed by an elected mayor and council.

The MDHS utilized a robust sampling methodology, building upon the Enumeration Areas defined in Madagascar’s 2018 Population and Housing Census. By employing this framework, the survey guaranteed the representativeness of its results through the integration of comprehensive and current census data.

The study employed a two-stage sampling technique to ensure a balanced representation of both urban and rural populations. Initially, 500 enumeration areas were selected, with a deliberate distribution of 112 in urban and 388 in rural settings. Subsequently, 26 households were systematically chosen from each enumeration area, resulting in a sample of 13,000 households. For all 7,756 children under five residing in these households, their mothers were interviewed to determine the incidence of diarrhea within the two weeks prior to the survey.In the context of the study at hand, the resulting sample size comprises 8,740 under-five children. Note that this sample is not a mere enumeration but is weighted, meaning that the data is adjusted to account for the complex survey design and to accurately represent the entire population. This careful consideration of the sampling strategy and the subsequent weighting of the sample enhance the validity and reliability of the findings, providing valuable insights into the health and demographic dynamics of under-five children in Madagascar as of 2021.

Study variables

Dependent variable

The primary outcome of interest in this study was diarrhea among children under five years of age(yes). To measure this, children were categorized as having or not having diarrhea in the past two weeks. A binary variable was created, with “1” indicating the presence of diarrhea and “0” representing the absence (reference group).

Independent variables

Factors considered at the individual level for their association with diarrheal cases in Madagascar included the child’s age, child’s sex, immunization status, maternal age, maternal educational status, wealth status, types of latrine, sources of drinking water, maternal working status, number of under-five children, and media exposure. Additionally, community-level factors such as community-level education, and community-level poverty were also taken into account.

Operational definition

Media exposure: This study created a single category, “exposed to media,” to capture a wider range of participants. People were included in this category if they reported using any of the three media sources: newspapers, radio, or television.This approach avoids the potential drawback of losing participants if researchers analyzed each media source separately. For example, some questions might only be relevant for specific subgroups, like newspapers for educated women or television for those with electricity. By combining the information, the study ensures a more inclusive analysis.

Type of latrines: This study categorized toilets into two main groups: “unimproved” and “improved.”

Unimproved toilets: include those that flush waste somewhere else (like pit latrines without a slab), bucket toilets, hanging toilets, and other non-standard options.

Improved toilets: encompass those with better sanitation practices. This includes toilets that flush to a piped sewer system, septic tank, or even a properly built pit latrine (with ventilation or a slab). Even composting toilets were considered improved in this study [14].

Sources of drinking water: This study categorized household access to drinking water based on how “improved” the source is:

Improved drinking water: This includes piped water accessed inside the house, piped water in the yard/plot, public taps, piped water from a neighbor’s house, wells or boreholes with proper protection, springs with protection, rainwater collection, and even delivered water by tanker truck or small cart.

Unimproved drinking water: This refers to any source considered less safe, like unprotected wells or springs, surface water (like rivers or lakes), or any other unspecified source [14].

Child’s immunization status: The study created a strict definition for “fully vaccinated” children. To be categorized as fully vaccinated, a child must have received a specific combination of vaccines. This includes one dose each of the BCG and measles vaccines, three doses each of the polio and pentavalent (DTP-hepB-Hib) vaccines, three dose of the pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV), and two doses of the Rotavirus vaccine. Missing any of these vaccinations meant a child was classified as “not fully vaccinated.“” [15].

Community level of poverty: This study considered how poverty and education levels affect communities. To measure community poverty, we looked at the proportion of households categorized as “poorest” or “poorer” based on a wealth index. This wealth index considers factors like household assets and amenities. Communities with a higher proportion of these poorer households were classified as having a “low poverty level.” Conversely, communities with more households falling above the median wealth index (richer households) were considered to have a “high poverty level.” The skewed distribution of wealth within communities meant using the median (middle point) as the dividing line. we applied a similar approach to categorize community education levels.

Community-level of educational status: The study also investigated the education level within communities. They looked at the proportion of mothers or caregivers who had at least a primary education. Communities with a higher proportion of mothers or caregivers with primary education or higher were classified as having a “high community level of educational status.” Conversely, communities where most mothers or caregivers lacked primary education were considered to have a “low community level of educational status.” This approach essentially gauges the overall educational attainment within a particular community.

Data management and analysis

This study described the key features of the data using medians, tables, and percentages. We cleaned the data using Excel to ensure accuracy. To compensate for any unequal representation of different groups within the sample, they used weighted frequencies. In other words, we adjusted the data’s influence based on how well each group represents the larger population.

Mixed model

This study used a special method called multilevel logistic regression to pinpoint factors linked to diarrhea in Madagascar. This method is particularly useful for analyzing data with nested structures, like the data from the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS).

The DHS data has information on both individual children and the communities they live in. For example, they have details about each mother’s education and the household’s wealth. The researchers expect that mothers with higher education and income will have children with a lower chance of getting diarrhea.

However, the analysis goes beyond just individual characteristics. It also considers how community-level factors might influence these individual traits. For instance, a mother’s education and income might be affected by where she lives (enumeration area), the overall media exposure in her community, the community’s poverty level, and even the region of the country. By taking these community factors into account, the study can provide a more comprehensive picture of what contributes to childhood diarrhea.

The data used in this study comes from the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS), which collects information at both the individual and community level. This creates a nested structure, where children within the same community (enumeration area) are likely to be more similar than children from different communities. This similarity in characteristics (like education or wealth) violates the assumption of independence in traditional regression models.

Multilevel logistic regression analysis is specifically designed to address this issue. It takes into account the nested structure of the data, allowing us to analyze the factors associated with diarrhea while acknowledging that children within the same community might be more alike. This provides a more accurate picture of how individual and community-level factors contribute to childhood diarrhea.

This study used a two-level binary logistic regression model to explore factors linked to diarrhea in Madagascar. This model considers both individual and community-level characteristics.The researchers compared four different models. The first model, called the “null model” or “random intercept model,” didn’t include any explanatory variables. It was used to assess how much variation in diarrhea prevalence exists between communities. To identify the best-fitting model, they looked at different criteria: Likelihood Ratio test (LLR), deviance, Akaike information criterion (AIC), and deviance information criterion (DIC). The model with the lowest value for one of these four criteria was chosen as the most accurate representation of the data. In other words, this model best explains the factors associated with childhood diarrhea.

To assess the variation of diarrhea prevalence between communities in Madagascar, we used three key measures. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) indicated what proportion of the overall difference in diarrhea cases could be attributed to factors specific to each community. The proportional change in variance (PCV) measured how much this variation between communities decreased after accounting for other factors in the analysis, compared to a model with no explanatory variables. Finally, the median odds ratio (MOR) provided a different perspective. It showed how much the likelihood of getting diarrhea could differ between the community with the highest risk and the one with the lowest risk. For instance, an MOR of 1.2 would suggest that children in the highest-risk community are 20% more likely to contract diarrhea compared to those in the lowest-risk community. In this context, if we selected two children having the same personal characteristics one from cluster with high risk of diarrhea and the other one from low risk cluster, relocating the child from the low-risk to high-risk cluster would result in a 1.2-fold increase in the odds of contracting diarrheal diseases. In the absence of any area-level variation, the MOR is equal to 1. The value of ICCs and MORs was estimated from intercept-only models (null model) to examine the presence of clustering and heterogeneity of diarrhea cases between areas. Finally, variables with p-value < 0.2 were considered for multivariate analysis. Adjusted odds ratio with 95% confidence interval (CI) and p-value < 0.05 were used to declare statistical significance.

Results

The prevalence of children younger than 6 months, 6–24 months, and older than 24 months was 1,087, 2,638, and 4,790, respectively. The prevalence of mothers younger than 18 years, 18–35 years, and older than 35 years was 2,732, 3,999, and 1,739, respectively.

A majority of the study participants, comprising 8,470 individuals (82.41%), were from rural areas, and a significant portion, specifically 3,446 individuals (40.6%), were found to be not fully immunized. Most children were aged between 25 and 59 months (54.8%), and more than two-third of these children were born to mothers with an educational background at the primary level (Table 1).

Prevalence of diarrhea

The prevalence of diarrhea among children under five years in Madagascar was 9.4% (95% CI: 8.8, 10.05).

Model comparison

To understand how much diarrhea prevalence differed between communities, the study looked at three random effects measures: intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), median odds ratio (MOR), and proportional change in variance (PCV). The ICC specifically assessed the variability within communities. Since children in the same community share similar environments, they’re more likely to have similar experiences with diarrhea compared to children in other communities. The intercept-only model (null model) revealed an ICC of 7.9%. In other words, nearly 8% of the variation in diarrhea cases can be explained by factors that differ between communities.

The study’s analysis considered how the community a child lives in affects their likelihood of getting diarrhea. A key measure for this was the median odds ratio (MOR) from the null model (model with no explanatory variables). An MOR of 1.66 means that if you take two children with similar characteristics, but one from a high-risk community and the other from a low-risk community, the child from the high-risk area is 66% more likely to have diarrhea. This highlights the impact of community-level factors.

Another measure, the proportional change in variance, reinforces this point. By comparing a full model (with explanatory variables) to the null model, we can see how much the variation in diarrhea prevalence between communities shrinks. In this study, 7.9% of the variation in the likelihood of getting diarrhea is due to both individual and community-level factors (as shown in Table 2). This suggests that community factors play a significant role alongside individual characteristics. Model three, incorporating both individual and community-level factors, demonstrates the best fit for this data, as evidenced by low DIC and AIC values.

Discussion

This study aimed to examine the prevalence of diarrhea among under-five children in Madagascar using data from the most recent demographic health survey of Madagascar. It also examined individual and community-level factors associated with diarrhea diseases among under-five children. Beyond the poverty and access problems in those areas, other socio-economic, environmental, and healthcare-related variables may contribute to the observed clustering of diarrhea cases.

The prevalence of diarrheal diseases among under-five children in Madagascar was 9.4% (95% CI: 8.8, 10.05). This is lower than the studies done in Moramanga and Antananarivo, Madagascar(42.4%) [17],Nigeria (12.7%) [18]Ghana (19.2%) [19], India (25.2%) [20] and Ethiopia (23.1%) [21] and higher than the national prevalence of diarrhea amog underfive children in madagascar. The study acknowledges that the observed variations in diarrhea rates between communities likely have complex causes. Socioeconomic factors like income and education, along with location, climate, and even cultural practices around hygiene and food preparation, could all play a role.Women in lower age [15–24], compared to the higher age cohorts [25–34] and (35–49 years), had increase the occurrence of diarrhea among under-five children. This result is similar to research conducted in east Africa [22], Uganda [23], and Nepal [24]. The study suggests that mothers’ age might be a factor in childhood diarrhea. Older mothers could have a greater knowledge and experience in caring for children’s health in general, and specifically in managing diarrheal illnesses .Additionally, older mothers might have older children who can help care for younger siblings, potentially reducing the risk of exposure to germs that cause diarrhea.

Compared to infants aged 0–6 months, infants aged 7–12 months and 13–24 months have increased odds of acquiring diarrheal diseases. This finding is consistent with other studies [23, 25–28]. This may be due to two factors. First, introducing solid foods (complementary feeding) after six months might expose children to germs if the food is not prepared or handled hygienically. Contaminated food can introduce diarrhea-causing organisms into a child’s digestive system. Second, as babies reach six months old, they naturally explore their surroundings by putting things in their mouths. This increased hand-to-mouth contact can increase their risk of ingesting germs and getting sick [29]. As they wean, crawl, and explore their surroundings, young children become increasingly vulnerable to a variety of things that can cause diarrhea. These factors can be linked to both the child’s own development and the environment they interact with [30].

This study found that children born to mothers with no formal education are more likely to experience diarrhea compared to children whose mothers completed at least elementary school. This aligns with findings from a similar community-based study conducted in Nigeria, suggesting a potential link between maternal education and childhood diarrhea risk [31]. The association between lower maternal education and higher diarrhea risk in children might be because mothers with more education are more likely to practice healthy behaviors during pregnancy and childcare. This aligns with other research suggesting a positive impact of elementary education on mothers’ health habits, which could indirectly benefit their children’s health [32].

Compared to children born to middle income households, children born to a low-income family increase the likelihood of having diarrhea. Many studies done in Uganda [33], Ghana [34], three east African countries [12] and Rwanda [10]also fortify this evidence. This disparity can be attributed to a couple of factors. Firstly, economically stressed families might hesitate to seek medical attention for their children due to concerns about transportation costs and the overall expense of healthcare services. Secondly, children from poorer households may have less access to a balanced diet, increasing their susceptibility to malnutrition. Malnutrition, in turn, can make children more prone to diarrhea and potentially worsen the duration and severity of diarrheal illness [35].

The occurrences of diarrhea among female children were less likely as compared to male children. This finding is in line with other study findings from Ethiopia [35]. Biological differences and potential gender discrimination in access to healthcare or sanitation resources could contribute to this variation.

The incidence of diarrhea was significantly associated with a source of water supply. This study revealed a clear connection between the quality of a child’s drinking water and their risk of contracting diarrhea. Children whose mothers reported using unprotected water sources, like untreated wells or springs, were significantly more likely to experience diarrhea compared to children who had access to protected water sources. This suggests that ensuring access to clean, safe drinking water is crucial for preventing childhood diarrhea.This finding is in agreement with a research report from Gamo Gofa Zone, Southern Ethiopia [36] Jamma district, Northeast Ethiopia [37] and Malaysia [38] which showed that children use of unprotected water was highly affected by diarrhea. his finding likely results from the presence of diarrhea-causing pathogens in untreated water sources. These harmful microorganisms can contaminate the water and cause diarrhea when ingested.

This study found a significant link between mothers’ access to mass media and a lower prevalence of diarrhea in children under five. This aligns with previous research suggesting that information access reduces childhood diarrhea [39, 40]. Mass media, particularly national television and radio broadcasts, have the unique ability to raise awareness and reach remote areas. By delivering health information in local languages through these channels, a wider population can be educated on best practices. Increased access to such information can empower mothers of young children to seek appropriate healthcare, ultimately promoting positive health-seeking behaviors. This is especially beneficial for rural and disadvantaged communities where internet access and information sources are limited.

Implications and future research

Our research has implication for madagascar government minister of health and public health researchers:

The study’s findings on the high prevalence of diarrhea in children under five emphasize the need for targeted healthcare initiatives in this age group. These initiatives could focus on improving access to healthcare services, vaccination programs, and most importantly, educating caregivers on preventive measures.

Furthermore, the link between maternal age and diarrhea suggests a crucial role for maternal health education. By providing information and resources to mothers, particularly those in the younger age groups (15–24 and 25–34 years), we can empower them to make informed choices that contribute to better health outcomes for their children.

Public health researchers should prioritize investigations into the reasons behind low healthcare access and utilization in the specific communities identified by this study.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study benefits from several strengths. First, the use of substantial, nationally representative data likely increased the study’s statistical power and generalizability. The true strength lies in the use of the national Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) database, with a representative sample, the hierarchical nature of the survey data was acknowledged through the application of an appropriate multilevel statistical model. However, limitations are also present. The cross-sectional research design used in this study cannot establish causation between factors and diarrheal disease. Furthermore, social desirability bias, where participants may underreport negative behaviors, is unavoidable [53]. To mitigate this, the study relied on data from a secondary survey. Finally, the study does not account for other potentially relevant behavioral and cultural factors.

Conclusion

In Madagascar, a study found that several factors are associated with an increased risk of diarrheal diseases in children under five years old. These factors include lack of exposure to media, having a young mother (aged less than 24 months), being between 7 and 24 months old, having a mother with lower education, being male, belonging to a low-wealth household, and lacking access to improved drinking water.

To effectively combat diarrheal diseases in Madagascar’s under-five population, a multi-faceted approach is crucial. Public health interventions should prioritize improving access to clean water and sanitation facilities, along with promoting handwashing with soap. Educational campaigns targeted towards mothers, especially those with lower education levels, can significantly improve hygiene practices, safe water handling, and early symptom recognition of diarrhea. Additionally, promoting exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months and continued breastfeeding with complementary foods up to two years can provide vital nutritional defenses.
