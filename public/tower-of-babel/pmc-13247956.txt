Missed diagnosis of knee tuberculosis on X-ray: contributing factors and diagnostic value of CT and MRI
Authors: Jia-Lu Wei, Jia-Qi Guo, Xu-Wen Fu, Jian-Jie Jiang, Ying Pu, Min Qi, Yan Bi, Xiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundX-ray examinations often fail to detect early-stage knee tuberculosis (KTB), yet reports on the comparative diagnostic performance of advanced imaging modalities remain limited. This study aimed to descriptively investigate the factors contributing to missed diagnoses of KTB using X-ray examinations and characterize the findings detected by multidetector computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on patients diagnosed with KTB based on pathological or etiological confirmation at Kunming Third People’s Hospital between January 2022 and March 2025. Specifically, only the cases in which the initial X-ray examination failed to establish the diagnosis were included to analyze the reasons for missed diagnosis. CT and MRI findings were reviewed, and the reasons for missed diagnosis on X-ray were analyzed.ResultsA total of 93 patients with KTB were included, of whom 36 cases were not diagnosed by X-ray (26 males, 10 females; mean age 40.2±16.3 years; 35 with pulmonary tuberculosis; 20 right-sided and 16 left-sided). CT imaging demonstrated bone erosions in 25 cases (69.4%), joint effusion in 23 cases (63.9%), and osteoporosis or soft tissue swelling in 22 cases each (61.1%). MRI demonstrated bone marrow edema in all cases (100%), synovial thickening in 33 cases (91.7%), joint effusion in 32 cases (88.9%), soft tissue edema in 31 cases (86.1%), bone erosions in 25 cases (69.4%), and soft tissue abscess in 20 cases (55.6%). Missed diagnoses on X-ray were primarily attributed to lesions with absent or minimal bone destruction and the limited sensitivity of unilateral anteroposterior radiographs in detecting osteoporosis and soft tissue swelling. Among 12 patients who underwent weight-bearing full-length imaging of the lower extremities, osteoporosis was observed in 8 cases, reduced joint space in 4 cases, and soft tissue swelling in 6 cases.ConclusionsEarly-stage KTB was frequently underdiagnosed when X-ray examinations were used alone. MRI demonstrated a higher frequency of detection for key early signs such as bone marrow edema and soft tissue pathology compared to CT, and weight-bearing full-length X-ray examinations of bilateral lower extremities may provide additional diagnostic value.Keywords: Knee tuberculosis (KTB), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), missed diagnosis, multidetector computed tomography (multidetector CT), radiography

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) of the bone and joints accounts for approximately 1–3% of all TB cases and 25–41% of extrapulmonary TB cases, making it one of the most common forms of extrapulmonary disease (1-4). The skeletal sites most frequently affected are the spine, hip, and knee (5). Pathologically, joint TB can be classified into tuberculous synovitis, early-stage joint TB, late-stage joint TB, and destructive-stage joint TB (6). Radiographically, five stages have been described: the first stage is characterized by osteoporosis with soft tissue swelling; the second stage by marginal bone erosion and joint space narrowing; and the third to fifth stages by progressive degrees of osseous and articular destruction (7).

Previous studies on wrist TB indicate that X-rays may miss subtle early-stage erosions (8). With advances in imaging, X-ray, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are now commonly used for joint evaluation. However, reports remain limited regarding the relative diagnostic strengths and limitations of these modalities for knee tuberculosis (KTB), particularly in early disease stages. Although X-ray examination is commonly used as a simple and accessible method for evaluating bone and joint conditions, its diagnostic sensitivity for KTB is relatively low.

In this study, retrospectively collected cases of KTB that were not identified by X-ray examination were analyzed. The contributing factors to missed diagnoses were analyzed, and CT and MRI findings were reviewed. The objective was to descriptively analyze the imaging characteristics of KTB in a specific subgroup where diagnosis is challenging on initial X-ray examinations, and to investigate the contributing factors to these missed diagnoses. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-aw-2266/rc).

Methods

Study participants

Patients diagnosed with KTB through pathological or etiological confirmation at Kunming Third People’s Hospital between January 2022 and March 2025 were included. Exclusion criteria comprised incomplete imaging data, presence of comorbid conditions associated with knee bone erosions, and history of prior surgical interventions involving the knee. Therefore, this study was a descriptive case series analysis specifically targeting the diagnostically challenging subgroup of KTB patients with initially inconclusive or normal X-ray findings. The findings and conclusions were primarily applicable to this subgroup and may not be representative of the entire KTB population, especially those with advanced, radiographically obvious disease. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kunming Third People’s Hospital (No. KSLL20230711001). Informed consent was obtained from all patients aged ≥18 years, and from the legal guardians of patients aged 11–17 years.

Imaging examinations of the knee joint

X-ray examinations

Anteroposterior and lateral radiographs of the knee joint were obtained using the uDR780 (United Imaging, China). For anteroposterior views, patients were positioned supine with the knee extended, whereas lateral views were acquired with the knee flexed to 30°. Imaging parameters included a tube voltage of 55 kV and a tube current of 25 ms.

Weight-bearing full-length X-ray examinations

Full-length radiographs were performed using the uDR780 system (United Imaging, China). Patients were positioned standing in the anteroposterior orientation, and three exposures were obtained to cover the region from both hip joints to both ankle joints. Automated full-length stitching of the bilateral lower extremities was subsequently performed.

CT examinations

CT was conducted using either the uCT960 + system (United Imaging, China) or the LightSpeed VCT system (GE Healthcare, USA). Scanning was performed at 120 kV, with automatic tube current modulation. Images were acquired with a 5-mm slice thickness, followed by multiplanar reconstruction in the coronal and sagittal planes at a slice thickness of 0.55 or 0.625 mm.

MRI examinations

MRI examinations were conducted using the uMR780 system (United Imaging, China) with a 12-channel knee coil. Imaging sequences included sagittal T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), sagittal proton-density-weighted imaging (PDWI), and PDWI in the coronal and transverse planes. Slice thickness was 3 mm. Imaging parameters for T1WI included repetition time (TR) =600 ms and echo time (TE) =9 ms; for PDWI, TR =2,900 ms and TE =40 ms.

Image interpretation

Imaging data were retrieved from the Image Application and Transmission System of the hospital. During the research, the researchers downloaded all the images and blinded the patient information. The physicians who read the X-rays were unaware of the patients’ pathological diagnoses or clinical histories; they only made diagnoses based on the X-ray images. Knee joint X-rays were independently reviewed using a DICOM viewer by two physicians holding intermediate or higher-level professional titles to determine whether a diagnosis of KTB could be established from the radiographs. In cases of disagreement, the images were reviewed by a senior physician, and a final consensus was reached through discussion among the three physicians. A “missed diagnosis” on X-ray was strictly defined as follows: the initial anteroposterior and lateral knee X-rays were interpreted by the reviewing physicians as either (I) completely normal, showing no abnormalities suggestive of KTB, or (II) demonstrating non-specific findings (e.g., mild soft tissue swelling or osteoporosis) that were considered insufficient to establish or strongly suggest a diagnosis of KTB, leading to no definitive radiological diagnosis. This definition specifically focuses on the failure of X-ray to provide a conclusive diagnosis, which was subsequently confirmed by pathological or etiological tests.

For patients in whom KTB could not be diagnosed by X-ray examination, subsequent assessments were performed using CT and MRI. CT evaluations included assessment of osteoporosis, bone erosions, bone sclerosis, sequestrum formation, soft tissue swelling, joint effusion, and distribution of bone erosions across weight-bearing and non-weight-bearing surfaces of the knee joint. MRI evaluations included bone erosions, bone marrow edema, synovial thickening, joint effusion, abscess formation, soft tissue edema, and intra-articular rice body formation.

Comparative assessments of the bilateral knee joints were conducted on weight-bearing full-length radiographs of the lower extremities, focusing on joint space narrowing, osteoporosis, and soft tissue swelling in the affected knee. In cases of interobserver discrepancy, the images were re-examined by a senior physician, and consensus was achieved through group discussion.

All imaging findings were interpreted with reference to Diagnostic Medical Imaging (5th Edition) (9). The weight-bearing surfaces of the knee joint were defined as the medial femorotibial joint, while the non-weight-bearing surfaces included the lateral femorotibial joint, patellofemoral joint, and non-articular surfaces (10).

Data processing

Descriptive statistical methods were used to calculate composition ratios and rates of the collected data. Given that the research design was a descriptive case series analysis and the main objective was not to compare differences between groups, inferential statistical tests were not employed.

Results

General data

A total of 143 patients were diagnosed with KTB during the study period. Exclusions comprised of 3 cases with comorbid knee gout, 2 cases with comorbid pigmented villonodular synovitis, 6 cases with a history of surgical intervention, and 39 cases lacking CT and MRI imaging data. Consequently, 93 patients with complete knee X-ray, CT, and MRI imaging data, and without comorbidities associated with knee bone erosions, were included in the analysis.

Among these, 36 patients were not diagnosed with KTB based on X-ray examinations. Of these 36 patients, 26 were male (72.2%) and 10 were female (27.8%), with an age range of 11–66 years and a mean age of 40.2±16.3 years. Thirty-five patients (97.2%) had comorbid pulmonary TB. Right knee involvement was observed in 20 patients (55.6%) and left knee involvement in 16 patients (44.4%). Additionally, 12 patients underwent weight-bearing full-length imaging of the affected lower extremity within 1 week after the initial anteroposterior and lateral knee radiographs. X-ray findings

Among the 36 patients, 29 (80.6%) demonstrated normal X-ray findings, while 7 (19.4%) exhibited osteoporosis.

CT findings

CT indicated osteoporosis in 22 patients (61.1%) and bone erosions in 25 patients (69.4%). Among those with bone erosions, periosteal sclerosis adjacent to the erosions was observed in 9 patients (25.0%), and sequestrum formation within the erosions was detected in 5 patients (13.9%). Soft tissue swelling around the knee joint was present in 22 patients (61.1%), and joint effusion was observed in 23 patients (63.9%).

The distribution of bone erosions included the distal femur in 17 patients (47.2%), the proximal tibia in 24 patients (66.7%), and the proximal fibula in 2 patients (5.6%). No bone erosions were detected in the patella. Bone erosions were localized to non-weight-bearing surfaces in 25 patients (69.4%) and to weight-bearing surfaces in 16 patients. Analysis of missed diagnoses using X-ray examinations

Observation of bone erosions

Among the 36 patients, 11 (30.6%) exhibited no bone erosions, which were therefore undetectable by X-ray examination and resulted in missed diagnoses. Small-scale bone erosions, also undetectable on X-ray, were present in 24 patients (66.7%), leading to diagnostic oversight. In 1 patient (2.8%), overlapping bony structures of the proximal tibia and fibula obscured fibular bone erosions, contributing to a missed diagnosis.

Observation of osteoporosis

Bilateral comparisons of CT findings demonstrated osteoporosis in 22 patients involving the affected knee joint, whereas X-ray examinations detected osteoporosis in only 7 patients.

Observation of soft tissue swelling

CT scans indicated soft tissue swelling in 22 patients, which was not detected by X-ray examinations.

The comparison between CT and MRI

The common imaging findings observed in both CT and MRI include: (I) bone erosion; (II) joint effusion; (III) soft tissue swelling. Since the observed contents of bone erosion are consistent, no statistical analysis was conducted. Regarding joint effusion and soft tissue swelling, the positive findings detected by MRI were higher than those by CT (Table S1).

Findings of the weight-bearing full-length X-ray examination

Among the 36 patients, 12 underwent weight-bearing full-length X-ray examinations. Unilateral knee X-ray examinations detected osteoporosis in only 2 of these patients. Bilateral comparison using weight-bearing full-length imaging identified osteoporosis in 8 patients, joint space narrowing in 4 patients relative to the unaffected knee, and soft tissue swelling surrounding the affected knee in 6 patients (Figure 4).

Discussion

In this study, most patients had comorbid pulmonary TB and underwent imaging due to knee symptoms. X-ray is the preferred initial imaging for bone and joint diseases owing to tissue contrast and simplicity (11-13), yet nearly 40% of KTB patients were misdiagnosed by X-ray, with CT/MRI confirming these were early-stage cases. Classic tuberculous arthritis features the Phemister triad (peri-articular osteoporosis, joint space narrowing, multiple marginal erosions) (14). X-ray’s low sensitivity in detecting osteoporosis (7 cases vs. 22 on CT) causes missed diagnoses, as it requires over 30% bone loss to show osteopenia (15), unlike CT and MRI, which identify early metabolic changes. Estrogen deficiency and physical activity may also affect osteoporosis (16).

For joint space narrowing, limited knee extension during X-ray can cause femoral-tibial overlapping and misinterpretation. Early KTB may show widened joint space due to synovial TB-induced effusion, offsetting cartilage erosion-related narrowing. Minor marginal erosions are hard to detect on X-ray due to bony overlapping. Nearly 70% of patients had bone erosions, but they were too small or overlapped to be seen on X-ray. CT confirmed X-ray failed to assess soft tissue swelling in 22 cases, as X-ray cannot visualize synovial hyperplasia, effusion or edema—hallmark features of tuberculous synovitis poorly differentiated without bilateral comparison (17).

The absence of bilateral knee X-rays increases missed diagnoses or reduces diagnostic confidence. Some patients underwent weight-bearing full-length lower extremity imaging after initial knee X-rays. Unlike routine sitting/lying X-rays, standing weight-bearing imaging enables bilateral comparison (clearly showing osteoporosis and soft tissue swelling), checks lower limb length equality, observes biomechanical lines, and detects affected joint space narrowing/semi-dislocation. However, only 12 of 36 patients had this imaging, requiring further validation. X-ray detected no bone erosions in 11 cases (30.6%) and only small ones in 24 (66.7%), corresponding to early osseous involvement (confined to marrow edema or minimal cortical disruption), obscured by bony overlapping.

CT and MRI are cross-sectional imaging techniques. CT clearly shows the location and extent of bone erosions, surrounding sclerosis and sequestrum, and assesses osteoporosis via bilateral density comparison but has limited sensitivity for synovial TB (18). MRI has high soft tissue resolution, evaluating bones, ligaments, menisci and synovial thickening, and outperforms CT in early KTB. It detected bone marrow edema in all 36 early cases—the earliest TB inflammatory response preceding CT/X-ray-detectable bone destruction (19,20). MRI also identifies synovial thickening (91.7%), joint effusion (88.9%), soft tissue edema (86.1%) and abscesses (55.6%) precisely, while CT shows non-specific soft tissue lesions (18). Additionally, its multi-planar/multi-parametric sequences detect intra-articular rice bodies (19.4%) missed by CT (21), making it superior for early KTB detection. Most patients had arthroscopic treatment (22), but postoperative outcomes between synovial thickening- and effusion-dominant lesions were not compared, requiring further research on MRI’s role in differentiating these lesions.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First and foremost, the study design intentionally included only KTB cases that were missed on initial X-ray. While this allowed for an in-depth analysis of the diagnostic challenges and the value of advanced imaging in this specific context, it introduced a selection bias and fundamentally limited the generalizability of the reported frequencies of imaging findings to all KTB patients. The study’s aim was not to describe the epidemiological or radiological spectrum of KTB, but to address a specific clinical dilemma—the evaluation of patients with suspected KTB but non-diagnostic X-rays. Second, the subgroup of patients who underwent weight-bearing full-length X-ray examination was relatively small (n=12). This limited sample size affects the statistical power and generalizability of the findings related to this specific modality. Consequently, the observations regarding its potential added value (e.g., in detecting osteoporosis and joint space narrowing through bilateral comparison) are preliminary and hypothesis-generating, necessitating cautious interpretation and further investigation in larger cohorts. Third, the study lacked correlation with clinical outcomes (e.g., treatment response or functional recovery) and did not include a statistical analysis comparing the diagnostic efficacy across imaging modalities. Future multi-center, prospective studies with larger sample sizes, standardized imaging protocols, and outcome correlations are warranted to validate and extend our descriptive findings.

Conclusions

For early-stage KTB that is not diagnosed on initial X-ray, MRI frequently identified bone marrow edema and soft tissue pathology in this case series, while CT detailed bony changes. These descriptive observations highlight the potential complementary roles of advanced imaging in this setting. Osteoporosis can be evaluated through X-rays, while the formation of dead bones is less likely to occur in the early stage of KTB. Therefore, based on the preliminary observations from a small subgroup in this study, it is suggested that when medical conditions permit, consideration could be given to using full-length weight-bearing radiographs of both lower limbs combined with MRI for a comprehensive assessment. However, due to the small sample size of the full-length radiographs in this cohort, this finding is preliminary and requires confirmation. A prospective study with a larger sample size should be conducted to verify the diagnostic efficacy of full-length weight-bearing radiographs for the early detection of KTB.
