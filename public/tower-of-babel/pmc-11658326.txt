How bereaved families conceptualise good care and communication in intensive care in South Australia
Authors: Drew Carter, Stewart Moodie, Benjamin Reddi, Nikki Yeo, Heylen Laver, Krishnaswamy Sundararajan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSince 2018, DonateLife South Australia and the Royal Adelaide Hospital (RAH) have collaborated to deliver routine family follow-up after bereavement in the intensive care unit (ICU). This follow-up includes a telephone interview that invites bereaved family members to comment on the quality of care and communication experienced in the ICU. To identify bereaved families’ experiences, including how they conceptualise good care and communication in intensive care, an analysis was conducted on all qualitative data collected during 118 interviews completed between 1 February 2018 and 30 May 2019. Reflexive thematic analysis was undertaken in an inductive and semantic way, with coding and theme generation being directed by the explicit content of the data, as conceptualised by Braun and Clarke. Initial codes were based on the interview questions, then additional codes were created during data analysis. Coding was informed by philosophical insights about concepts and the spirit of interpersonal engagement developed by Wittgenstein and Cordner, respectively. A concept map of the relationships observed between patterns of meaning in the data was created. Participants deeply appreciated staff providing them and their loved one with practical expressions of care and hospitality, however modest. These, along with staff sometimes crossing professional boundaries, expressed staff’s spirit of engagement, which in turn helped to maintain the patient’s dignity. Private space also helped to maintain the patient’s dignity, and it helped family to have enough time to say goodbye. Family not feeling rushed and being informed about their loved one dying also helped family to have enough time to say goodbye. Being informed depended on the quality of doctors’ communication. When family were not clearly informed, or had to wait long periods, they felt rushed and that they did not have enough time to say goodbye. Documents written to guide the assessment of intensive care comment on almost all of these matters, but the present study newly maps how they interact based on the extensive empirical evidence that it collected. Guidance documents should comment on giving staff scope to occasionally traverse a boundary, such as an institutional border or rule, to better support the patient and family, since families experience exceptionless practice insensitive to context as callous or disruptive of care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11877-1.Keywords: Intensive care unit, End of life care, Quality of care, Communication, Ethics, Patient dignity, Bereavement follow-up, Resource allocation, Bereavement, Family perspectives, Grief, Complicated grief, Organ donation

Background

Good care and communication in the intensive care unit (ICU) are incontrovertibly important. This is because, for instance, they stand to safeguard and improve the health and welfare of patients and their families. Care and communication in the ICU also instantiate and express defining commitments of the health care professions and society to adequately responding to people who are vulnerable and in need. In particular, we would argue that good communication with families can improve patient care, since a family’s advocacy for their loved one is often the means by which the values and preferences of the patient are relayed to the clinical team. Because good care and communication in the ICU matter greatly, so too do well-aimed activities of assessment, whereby staff behaviours, institutional processes and supporting structures can be assessed as either up to the mark or needing improvement in some area.

The perspectives of families whose loved one has died in the ICU are important to such assessment. Families are in a position to provide direct feedback about the care and communication provided. They can also awaken assessment designers to aspects that might be under-appreciated or over-looked. This is because, along with the patient, families are recipients of the care and communication provided in the ICU. The Australian and New Zealand Intensive Care Society (ANZICS) uses the term “Patient and family-centred care” [1]. ANZICS states that “Care of the family and significant others during the dying process and after bereavement is an integral part of intensive care” [2]. Likewise, the Australian College of Critical Care Nurses (ACCCN) states that “Family-centred care is widely accepted as an important component of patient care in critical care and is of particular importance at the end of life” [3]. The Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care and the American College of Critical Care Medicine concur, expressly identifying family as belonging to the central focus of good care [4, 5].

Therefore, families, along with patients, benefit or suffer depending on what happens in the ICU. They do so both indirectly, through what happens in connection with their loved one, and directly, as themselves recipients of care and communication. Risk factors for complicated grief include problematic communication and not having the opportunity to say goodbye [6]. Conversely, good communication contributes to families feeling prepared for the possibility of their loved one dying [7]. Being unprepared can increase bereavement intensity [8]. In a 2022 US study, “five out of nine bereaved parents experienced feelings of abandonment by the medical team after the death of their child” [9]. Such evidence suggests that the people responsible for designing assessment frameworks must solicit and incorporate the feedback of bereaved families to be justifiably confident of adequately capturing key aspects of good care and communication.

In Australia, this is explicitly recognised. The Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care states that measures of the safety and quality of end-of-life care could include feedback from families and carers concerning their experiences [4]. ANZICS advises that “Follow-up of all families whose members die in the ICU and feedback to all ICU staff is an important quality activity” [2]. ICU staff can collect and act on feedback from families to improve the care and communication they provide. However, the peer-reviewed literature features few examples of this being done in Australia for adult ICUs. Australian researchers have examined the impact on families of ICU visitor restrictions [10, 11] and of bereavement follow-up [12, 13], but rarely have Australian researchers collected data from a large number of families to guide improvements and inform assessment and funding.

This article reports findings from a study of the quality of care and communication experienced by bereaved families in the ICU of a major hospital in Australia: “The Royal Adelaide Hospital is an 800-bed metropolitan hospital with a 48-bed quaternary ICU” [14]. It does so with a view to guiding improvements in the assessment and provision of care and communication in ICUs, along with a subsidiary view to guiding improvements in the assessment and funding of health care generally.

This article reports findings derived from a novel analysis of interviewee comments. An earlier publication reported findings focussing on quantitative data collected and emphasised issues relating to organ donation [14]. Its reporting on qualitative data was more limited than the present article’s. It focussed on “areas done well” and “areas for improvement” in the Royal Adelaide Hospital (RAH) ICU, not the more general question of how bereaved families conceptualise good care and communication, then the implications of this for assessing and funding intensive care [14].

Methods

This study's design and data collection process have been extensively detailed in an earlier article [14]. That article explains how, in the RAH ICU:

The bedside nurse highlighted to the family member the follow-up call … which included the option to decline participation in the project. Consent for the follow-up call was assumed if no objection was raised. Family members were asked to nominate a representative to receive the follow-up call [14].

The earlier article also includes the full interview schedule, which was adapted from [15] and focussed on bereaved families’ satisfaction with: support for the patient and family; maintenance of the patient’s dignity; pain control; the opportunity to discuss patient and family wishes; staff communication; the extent of treatment; having enough time to say goodbye; and organ donation conversations [14]. Interviewees were invited to answer questions with a yes-or-no response or using a five-point Likert scale (e.g., from “completely dissatisfied” to “completely satisfied”). They were then invited to comment freely and expand on their response to each question. The term ‘loved one’ is used in the present article because it was used in the interview schedule and the questionnaire on which that was based [15]. An advantage of the term is that it encompasses close friends.

The telephone interviewers were five experienced clinicians (medical and nursing) from the RAH ICU who underwent face-to-face telephone counselling training [14]. The qualitative data consisted of notes made by the interviewers during the interview, with audio recording not being used “due to concerns that it may disconcert family members and hamper open feedback” [14].

Opportunities for improving care and communication were identified and reported back to clinicians on an ongoing basis as part of the bereavement follow-up. Complaints were directed to the hospital’s consumer advocate, and the organisation’s usual procedures were followed [14]. Strategies were in place in the event that an interviewee demonstrated severe anxiety or distress. These included providing the interviewee with telephone numbers for crisis support or emergency services, along with general practitioner referral, with the interviewee’s consent [14]. The project received ethics approval from the Central Adelaide Local Health Network Human Research Ethics Committee (Reference no. R20180526). The present article’s data analysis was supported with funds from DonateLife South Australia.

Approximately 30,000 words, collected from 118 interviews, were analysed. The interviews were the 117 reported elsewhere, conducted between February 2018 and February 2019 [14]. However, 14 of those interviews were excluded because the patient had in fact died outside of the ICU. Furthermore, 15 new interviews were added, being completed between 1 February 2019 and 30 May 2019. A note on the age of the data is warranted. Manuscript drafting was interrupted by the COVID-19 pandemic. Because bereaved families’ conceptions of good care and communication are unlikely to have significantly changed since data collection, there is reason to believe that the findings remain valid.

Reflexive thematic analysis was undertaken in an inductive and semantic way, with coding and theme generation being directed by the explicit content of the data, as conceptualised by Braun and Clarke [16]. Initial codes were based on the interview questions, then additional codes were created during data analysis. (See Box S1 and Table S1 in the supplementary material for the list of initial codes and the complete final coding structure.)

Coding was informed by a Wittgensteinian perspective on words and practices [17–20]. These are seen as deriving at least part of their meaning from one another, and as forming a network of meaning (a ‘grammar’). In turn, this grammar is scaffolded by very general facts, including spontaneous human responses and near-universal agreement in some judgements.

Throughout data analysis, attempts were made to oscillate between a feeling, empathetic response to the interview data and a detached, analytical response. This was done in an effort to appreciate, but also gain some critical distance from, the emotional power of what participants were discussing.

During data familiarisation, it was evident that small acts performed by staff, such as providing families with a cup of tea at just the right time or with toiletries to have a shower, could be deeply appreciated by families, for their practical impact but even more so for the care and compassion that the acts expressed. This observation partly owed to Cordner’s moral philosophy [21]. Cordner writes about a person’s spirit of engaging with others and how the recipient of a kindness can appreciate the spirit of engagement even more than the practical aid:

If someone is kind, gentle, tender, compassionate to another, the latter may be very grateful for whatever material benefit he thereby receives. (His wounds are dressed, his belly is filled.) But his gratitude may also have a further object. He may also be grateful for the kindness or tenderness itself. Indeed the nourishment (to use Simone Weil’s term) he finds in the kindness or tenderness might be the most important thing of all ([21], p. 111).

Thus, when coding, attention was given to comments about the manner in which staff acted, not simply the actions staff took. During data familiarisation, it was also evident that families appreciated staff occasionally breaking a rule or crossing a boundary out of generosity. For instance, staff sometimes relaxed rules about family visits or visited a patient outside of their formal responsibilities. Therefore, attention was given to comments about this when coding.

This article reports findings that relate to a concept map created on the basis of coding (see Fig. 1). While coding, associations were recorded between codes. (All coding, recording of associations, and early mapping were conducted using the software NVivo 12 [22]). For example, participants made many comments about privacy. Some of those comments suggested that privacy given to the patient by staff helped to maintain the patient’s dignity. This was recorded as the code ‘privacy’ ‘is associated with’ the code ‘maintaining the patient’s dignity’. The map was consolidated by pruning tendentious associations as well as codes with fewer associations or with less voluminous or coherent data. Finally, arrows were created to represent the associations and their direction, and the phrase ‘is associated with’ was refined to more precise terms, such as ‘contributed to’ and ‘expressed’.

Results

Staff’s spirit of engagement and closely related concepts

In Fig. 1, concepts marked in orange closely relate to the concept of staff’s spirit of engagement. In the text of this results section, direct quotes from the primary data (interviewer notes) are presented in italics.

Participants overwhelmingly remarked that they deeply appreciated staff providing them and their loved one with practical expressions of care and hospitality. These acts and gestures were typically modest but greatly significant for families. Staff provided family with tissues, warm bedding, toiletries and towels to have a shower, and food and drink (a cup of tea … at just the right time). Staff said goodbye at the end of a shift, and sat with family and joined them in holding the patient’s hand, especially in the final hours of the patient’s life. Staff helped family to contact one another and to organise some matters.

Everyone cared; the nurses managed to contact my brother’s friend, whom he hadn’t seen for a long time to come in and say goodbye before he passed away. They didn’t have to do that, they went above and beyond.

Perhaps most importantly for participants, staff tended to the comfort, cleanliness, privacy, and grooming of the patient, including by putting on sounds or music that the patient would like. Staff made efforts to present the patient to family so as to keep their loved one recognisable and even beautiful or peaceful, instead of unlike their usual self or confronting.

Participants tended to experience these things, not as trivialities or acts incidental to care, but as themselves instances and expressions of care, compassion and respect for their loved one or themselves. When these things were lacking, participants tended to experience this as a failure to provide adequate care and to maintain their loved one’s dignity.

Participants occasionally complained of there being no comfortable chair in which to rest for long periods of time at the bedside, of problems with hospital food, and of the significant burden of car parking fees at the hospital.

Car park in the hospital is very expensive. I think the hospital should provide free parking for those who lost their family members.

Again, participants experienced these things, not as trivialities or incidental omissions, but as failures to provide adequate support, which at times distressed them or made a difficult time even harder.

Participants frequently praised the way in which staff were willing to cross boundaries to better support them or care for their loved one. These boundaries often comprised unit borders internal to the hospital or institutional rules, which staff sometimes traversed or relaxed. Participants tended to experience this as an expression of care and compassion.

Huge thank-you to medical and nursing staff who made the exception for my husband to have his dog visit, and share a bottle of scotch in his last days.

Participants were often deeply thankful that staff (especially nurses) relaxed rules around family visiting the bedside. Some staff allowed sleepovers or larger families to visit together when normally only two visitors at a time were allowed. Other participants greatly wished for this to have occurred when it did not. Participants frequently characterised the volunteers who managed visitor access to the ICU as inflexible in their practice and unable or unwilling to make exceptions sensitive to context. Participants occasionally praised staff who stayed back after their shift had ended or who visited the patient outside of their formal responsibilities.

Overwhelmingly participants commented on staff’s spirit of engagement. Participants tended to portray staff as dedicated and capable professionals. Staff were very respectful, dignified, very calm and professional amongst the chaos, polite and not intrusive, honest, and non-judgemental. Participants also tended to portray staff as combining this professionalism with warmth and congeniality. Staff were open and friendly, so very nice, very genuine, very approachable, and present and companionate. Staff were diligent and sensitive attendants to the needs of family and patients. Staff were very understanding and accommodating, very thoughtful, very kind, so helpful, so caring and supportive, comforting, sensitive, so patient, tender, and so gentle. Finally, staff were very empathic, very compassionate, very gracious, and even very loving.

Participants tended to highly value not being made to feel like they or their loved one were a burden on staff or just another number, another job to be sorted, something they had to check off their list.

Dad’s dignity was maintained right until the end. He didn’t want a catheter even though it was getting hard for him to use the bottle. The staff never made dad feel like it was an inconvenience.

Only came across one nurse who was clearly not cut out for this type of nursing. We were made to feel a bother and very much in her way.

Occasionally, participants described staff (usually just one staff member who stood out to them) as problematically lacking in warmth, sensitivity or respectfulness. Some staff were described as quite impersonal, quite cold, a little guarded, not as kind or personable as another staff member, or as having a poor bedside manner. More than this, some staff were described as quite insensitive, quite rude, very dismissive, uncaring and disinterested, or unhelpful. Finally, some staff were even described as disrespectful, abrupt and harsh, very forceful and pushy, or a bit rough when physically handling the patient.

Participants overwhelmingly praised staff (especially nurses) for treating their loved one as a person and with respect and dignity. Numerous participants deeply appreciated that staff asked about their loved one’s interests and likes, spoke to their loved one at every opportunity, as if he was awake, and used their loved one’s name.

The nurse used mum’s name when she washed her face and brushed her hair.

Participants frequently appreciated the privacy that their loved one was given in not having their naked body exposed to family or passers-by. However, numerous complaints related to a lack of privacy and respect in terms of excessive noise and laughter outside the bedspace.

Other aspects of care and communication

In Fig. 1, the concepts marked in blue identify the remaining main aspects of care and communication experienced by bereaved families.

Participants frequently praised how doctors communicated. Doctors were informative and thereby reassuring. Doctors listened and spoke mostly in clear terms and with respect, honesty and compassion, which included speaking in a private room instead of at the bedside. Doctors were commendably non-directive.

They never forced their opinions, they advised us medically and we had the chance to ask questions.

On the other hand, participants often singled out a particular doctor or encounter for criticism.

Everyone but one was [a] great listener and showed great empathy.

Participants variously criticised one or more doctors for being impersonal, a little guarded or pushy, or for speaking in a manner that was abrupt, harsh, vague, non-commital, or replete with medical jargon. Participants tended to greatly value explanations that were thorough and clear, sensitive rather than blunt. They greatly valued explanations that came every step of the way, including from the onset of care right to the end. Participants tended to suggest that they wanted to know whether their loved one was dying and that they didn’t want this knowledge to come too late, so that family could have enough time to say goodbye.

Participants overwhelmingly affirmed that it was very important to them and their family to have enough time to say goodbye to their loved one. Sometimes this just meant being with their loved one. It meant having enough time and privacy with their loved one in the knowledge that their loved would soon die or had just died.

We knew a few days before that he was not going to come home, we had time to sit and just be with him.

Numerous participants remarked that they appreciated being given a private meeting room for our family to gather and spend time, instead of all having to be at the bedside or home.

We used the large internal waiting room as [a] family base to receive family and friends over [name redacted]’s last 24 hours. The privacy was much easier for us and being close was reassuring.

Other participants suggested that more such rooms were needed.

There wasn’t always a place to go that was private, especially for my son who didn’t cope well. Sometimes I just wanted to sit in a quiet place.

Participants frequently made comments suggesting that they had enough time to say goodbye. It seemed to help participants have enough time when staff explained the situation clearly and early on, when staff allowed family to choose when treatment should be withdrawn, and when staff gave family ample opportunity and privacy to spend time with their loved one both before and after their loved one had died.

The doctor was very clear that dad would be unlikely to survive. It gave us time to call relatives from interstate and give everyone the chance to say goodbye.

We were told we didn’t have to hurry, to take our time. We got to decide when everything was turned off, when we were ready.

Yes we were given all the time we needed. We chose to leave when we left. The whole process was well handled.

Participants also frequently made comments suggesting that they did not have enough time to say goodbye, especially due to a rapid or unexpected deterioration in the health of their loved one. The other reasons for this were: having to wait for too long to get into the ICU; not wanting their loved one to suffer or linger any longer; there never possibly being enough time; other family members wanting to leave; feeling pressured by staff to leave after their loved one had died; and the need for organ donation procedures to begin.

Participants tended to make remarks suggesting that feeling rushed or not was a key dimension of their experience. Participants frequently reported that they were given time: to understand the situation; to express their wishes or their loved one’s wishes; to make important decisions; to gather together as a family; to say goodbye to their loved one; and to stay with their loved one after their loved one had died. Because of this, they did not feel rushed. Participants tended to value this highly.

They explained everything to us. They gave us time and waited until everyone in the family was here and gave us time to be with my son, it wasn’t rushed.

However, participants also frequently reported feeling rushed, especially due to a rapid or unexpected deterioration in the health of their loved one. Participants also felt rushed for the following reasons: having to understand so much information; having to make a lot of decisions; having to comprehend that their loved one was dying; missing out on time with their loved one by having to wait in the waiting room; doctors being too busy and not having enough time to talk; doctors wanting to withdraw treatment immediately; feeling pressured to leave after their loved one had died; and organ donation procedures needing to begin.

Participants frequently stated that the possibility of organ donation was not raised though they would have been receptive to considering it. Participants often suggested that, even if donation was not possible, they would have valued being told this and why. When the possibility of organ donation was first raised, often this came as a bit of a shock to participants. Despite organ donation being an added stress, participants often viewed donation as helping to realise their loved one’s wishes and found comfort in knowing that their loved one had helped others. Participants often deferred to whether their loved one had formally registered their wishes regarding organ donation.

Discussion

First, we discuss how our findings both reinforce and add to existing literature on bereaved families’ experiences of care and communication in the ICU. Then we discuss the implications of our findings for the assessment of care and communication in ICUs. This includes examining standard frameworks of assessment and some philosophical considerations.

Bereaved families’ perspectives

Our findings reinforce many of the findings produced by previous studies on bereaved families’ perspectives. Good communication involves family: being kept up to date [23–26], being readily able to speak with staff [23, 27], having appropriate input to the patient’s treatment [23], and getting clear explanations in plain language, delivered sensitively [7, 24, 25].

Good care involves good communication [7, 23, 24], along with giving family ready access to the bedside and supporting family togetherness in general [7, 23–25]. Family togetherness includes visits from pets [25], overnight family stays [25], privacy to help family “properly say goodbye” [27], and family staying with their loved one for as long as they wish after their loved one has died [7, 25]. Good care involves staff sometimes “bending the ‘rules’ … to maintain family togetherness” [24]. Good care also involves providing a private space for family to grieve away from the bedside [7]. It involves some continuity with the same staff [7, 27] and family having access to support after leaving the hospital [7].

Staff provide good care when they are professional and non-judgemental toward the patient’s “worth” and the family’s “choices and responses” [24]. Staff provide good care when they act in a “kind, caring and respectful manner” [25], when they are compassionate [7, 24, 27] and when they are warm rather than cold or overly clinical [7, 24]. Good care involves “genuine interactions” [27] and sincerity [7]. It involves not making others feel like they are a burden [24] and showing family that caring is “not just a job, it’s a heart … an attitude” [24]. Staff provide good care when they help family to cope emotionally [7, 26, 27] as well as practically [7, 24], for instance with “food or toiletries … transport home … organising a place to sleep … tracking down a lost parcel” [24]. Good care involves keeping the cafeteria open and the coffee machine working [25]. Perhaps above all, good care involves maintaining the patient’s dignity or humanity [7, 24, 27], including by relating to the patient as an individual with their own name and likes, by performing “small acts of kindness” [7] like tending to the patient’s grooming and cleanliness [24], and by giving the patient privacy, especially protecting the patient against exposure of their naked body [25].

Our findings also add to the literature. They provide a clear overview, visually set out as a concept map, of how some components of care and communication relate to one another, especially in terms of them expressing or contributing to one another (see Fig. 1). Notably, in Fig. 1, there are four concept bubbles where arrows point to the concept bubble but not away. These four concept bubbles represent: maintaining the patient’s dignity; family being present when their loved one died (in accordance with family wishes); and having (or not having) enough time to say goodbye. This suggests that all other aspects of care and communication mapped in Fig. 1 finally lead to these matters of importance for bereaved families. This is important to keep in mind when assessing and sustaining the quality of care and communication in intensive care, perhaps especially during times like the COVID-19 pandemic, when to curb viral transmission limits were sometimes placed on family being at the bedside [10, 11, 23, 28, 29].

Implications for ICU assessment

Standard frameworks of assessment

Many of the aspects of good care and communication identified in Fig. 1 already appear, in some form, in documents written to guide the provision and assessment of intensive care. These documents provide a framework for understanding quality. Some aspects are under-emphasised or missing, however.

Guidance documents commend a particular spirit of engagement when they assert that patients have “a right … to be cared for respectfully and with compassion”, such that staff should “be compassionate and sensitive” and “express empathy for the patient’s situation” [4] (see also [2]). Put differently, the success of intensive care is partly to be measured by the “the quality of human relationships involved in each death” [30] cited in [2]. One of the “clinical indicators” of quality for intensive care enumerated by the Australian Council on Healthcare Standards reads “Empathetic practice toward families of ICU patients”, though the only measure of this is “the number of occasions that at least one family member was contacted within 12 weeks after the patient’s death” [31].

Guidance documents commend practical expressions of care when they instruct staff to provide “practical support for patients and families” and to attend to patients’ “appearance and hygiene” [32]. Documents also declare that “patients have a right to maintain their dignity” [4] (see also [2]). Guidance documents commend maximising continuity and coordination of care across staff, settings and episodes of care [4, 32]. Furthermore, they commend particular aspects of good communication when advising “open and honest communication about what to expect” in “plain language”, not “medical jargon” [4] (see also [2]). Staff should communicate “in a clear, sensitive, unhurried manner, and in an appropriate setting” [32]. Staff should “allow adequate time for those involved to absorb, process and react to the information they are being given” [4]. This guidance implies some regard for families not feeling rushed, but given the importance of not feeling rushed in contributing to families having enough time to say goodbye (see Fig. 1), guidance documents should potentially comment more on this, even though some factors that lead to families feeling rushed (especially a rapid or unexpected deterioration in the health of their loved one) may lie beyond staff control.

Guidance documents assert that patients “have a right to maintain” their privacy [4] and commend maximising privacy for the patient and family [2, 3, 32]; “conversations about end-of-life care” ought to occur “in an appropriately quiet and private environment” [4]. Our findings show that families place considerable importance on having their own private space in which to gather or be alone. Private space contributes to families having enough time to say goodbye (see Fig. 1). Guidance documents commend supporting families to have enough time to say goodbye [2], with implicit advice against making families wait: “Family and carers should be supported to spend time with a dying patient in accordance with their wishes, including in the period immediately after death” [4]. Documents also commend supporting families to be present when their loved one dies (if families wish to be present) [2, 32].

However, in the above-cited guidance documents, we find no mention of the potential role of staff crossing boundaries to better support patients and families. Our findings show that families appreciate staff visiting the patient outside of formal responsibilities and relaxing rules around family visits, including in how this expresses staff’s spirit of engagement and contributes to continuity and coordination of care (see Fig. 1). Given this, guidance documents should perhaps explicitly commend giving staff scope to use their professional judgement as to whether a boundary, such as an institutional border or rule, should occasionally be traversed or relaxed to better support the patient and family, thereby avoiding inflexible, exceptionless practice insensitive to context, which our findings suggest families can experience as callous or disruptive of care. It may seem odd for a guideline to guide practitioners to sometimes cross a line, but we would argue that good guidance can contain acknowledgement of its proper scope and stringency. Put differently, the rigidity of some lines can create problems, and promoting appropriately flexible and autonomous practice could help.

Culyer and Cordner

We now raise some philosophical considerations to examine implications of our findings for assessing and funding health care.

The influential health economist, Culyer, writes the following.

Whatever is morally compelling about a need comes from the end served. A need for health services exists only if there are grounds for believing that these services will promote health, restore it, prevent ill-health or postpone death. These are the principal benefits sought from healthcare (less critical benefits include reassurance, legitimization of status for purposes of receipt of benefit and hotel services), and it follows that a need for health care can exist only when there is a capacity to benefit in one or more of these ways ([33], p. 235).

For Culyer, need is a need for something that reasonably stands to benefit you. Moreover, health care, and health itself, are “instruments” [33] in the service of the end or goal of “human flourishing” (Culyer cites Aristotle’s concept of eudamonia) [34]. On this picture, human flourishing is what really matters; health and health care “derive” their significance “from their ability to enable people to “flourish”” ([34], p. 276).

Culyer’s perspective needs adding to, if not replacing, to account for why ICU staff are right to provide, in the manner that they do, all the forms of care and support that they do provide and are guided to provide. There is no clear sense in which tending to the cleanliness and grooming of an unconscious patient benefits anyone, yet ICU staff take care to do it, family deeply appreciate it, and both staff and family might conceivably speak of the need for it. (Staff don’t do it for the family; they do the same for patients without family.) There is a perspective that contrasts with Culyer’s instructively. On it, the full significance of tending to a person can extend beyond any benefits. That perspective is articulated by Cordner.

Cordner’s fundamental challenge to a perspective like Culyer’s is as follows. Actions need not derive their moral significance from a goal:

the one who is helped can experience what is done in helping him as an affirmation of him, where this is something that reaches beyond the content of what is done ‘practically’ to help him … There is no ‘goal’ here [in adopting a spirit or way of helping that the person helped experiences as affirming him] – not even one as noble as helping the other become who he truly is. The affirmation of him that the beneficiary may experience is – simply – of him, this here-and-now existing human being ([35], p. 323).

Such affirmation endures independent of anyone recognising it at the time, namely as the general meaning or moral significance of many acts of help. In sum, Cordner would take issue with Culyer’s idea that “Whatever is morally compelling about a need comes from the end served” ([33], p. 235). Cordner might instead say that, if it comes from anything, it comes from the person served.

ICU staff clearly are goal-oriented in that they work to safeguard and restore the health of the patient and to support the family through a difficult time. However, fundamentally staff appear oriented to serve, not the goal of human flourishing or the means of health instrumental to it, but the people they encounter. This is because staff do things without clear benefit, like tending to the cleanliness and grooming of an unconscious patient, and most of all because staff typically act in a spirit deeply appreciated by family as showing respect for them and their loved one as human beings. Family feel aggrieved or wounded when staff do not act so. Why not say that staff fundamentally work toward the goal of maintaining the patient’s dignity? Cordner would probably say that, at best, this expression merely paraphrases ‘doing something simply because they are a human being’.

Standard frameworks for assessing the quality of care and communication in the ICU reflect, to varying degrees, both Culyer’s focus and Cordner’s. Culyer’s focus on health gains is reflected when indicators of the safety and quality of intensive care target things like whether patients have been admitted and discharged without delay or during daylight hours, whether calls for a rapid response have been met without delay, whether rates of infection are low, and whether ICUs have provided data to central databases for performance monitoring [31]. Meanwhile, Cordner’s focus on a particular spirit of engagement is reflected when guidance documents commend, for instance, compassion, sensitivity, empathy, and respect.

Michaels argues that “attributes that are highly valued by society, particularly those related to care processes, may be omitted or undervalued” when experts assess the value of a health care service for the purpose of determining whether or how much to fund that service [36]. This is because scholars have refined methods for measuring health outcomes (which align with Culyer’s focus) but less so “processes” [36]. Under the aegis of processes, Michaels includes “more abstract concepts such as dignity, respect, compassion and autonomy” (which align with Cordner’s focus). Maintaining people’s dignity, showing respect and compassion, and promoting and respecting someone in their autonomy can require resourcing, if indirectly. For instance, they can require “staffing levels that are adequate and consistent … facilities to provide privacy for patients receiving end-of-life care … [and] time for professionals to adequately present the pros and cons of all treatment options” [36]. Reflectively acknowledging both Culyer’s focus and Cordner’s may help to ensure that all, and not merely some, of the components of good care and communication in intensive care are duly assessed and resourced.

Such reflective acknowledgement may also help one to assess and resource some components of good care and communication on a proper footing. If one solely adopts a focus like Culyer’s, then forms of care that help to maintain people’s dignity and which show respect and compassion risk being reduced to acts (or health interventions or health technologies) which, to warrant funding, must prove their worth in terms of, for instance, securing health benefits for the average patient (and indeed sufficient health benefits relative to costs). However, this could put the cart before the horse, if the more important mode of engaging with people is to show them respect and compassion and only then worry about whether it is possible to benefit them, how much and at what cost.

Limitations

This study involved the collection of interview data in South Australia only, which may limit the transferability of findings to settings with, for instance, cultural norms or expectations of health services that differ from those present in South Australia. The data were collected in 2018–2019, some five years before publication. However, bereaved families’ expectations of health services are unlikely to have changed greatly since then. A strength of the present study lay in its large number of interviewees (n = 118), which should increase confidence in the validity of its findings. The interviewers were clinicians (medical and nursing) from the RAH ICU, therefore the interviewers brought an insider’s perspective. The interviews also occurred as part of a bereavement follow-up service. This may have shaped participants’ responses and how those responses were noted down by interviewers. However, some participants commented that they found it useful to talk about their experience with a medical professional involved in the ICU where their loved one was cared for, as distinct from an independent outsider.

Conclusion

This study provides extensive empirical evidence of what ultimately matters for bereaved families in intensive care and of what helps in trying to attain that. The study provides a clear, simple overview of this, visually set out as a concept map. Specifically, what seems to ultimately matter for bereaved families is maintaining the patient’s dignity, being present when their loved one dies (if this is desired by family), and having enough time to say goodbye. What helps in trying to attain these things are: staff’s spirit of engagement, privacy (in turn, helped by a space dedicated to the family), family not feeling rushed, family not having to wait to see their loved one, continuity and co-ordination in care, and family being informed about their loved one dying (in turn, helped by good communication from doctors). The spirit of engagement that staff express in even modest acts of care and in traversing boundaries when needed is central to how bereaved families experience and conceptualise good care and communication in intensive care. There is scope to refine frameworks for assessing the quality of care and communication in intensive care so that they more fully reflect what matters to bereaved families. Our findings also reinforce the importance of duly assessing and resourcing those components of good care and communication that help to maintain the patient’s dignity and express respect and compassion, in the ICU and beyond.
