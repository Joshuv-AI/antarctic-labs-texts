Ketamine Dose and Duration Effects in Internalizing Disorders
Authors: Neil McNaughton, Ben Beaglehole, Paul Glue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe dose‐response profile of racemic ketamine in internalizing disorders is poorly characterized. This was a secondary analysis of outcome responses in patients with treatment‐resistant depression, post‐traumatic stress disorder, generalized and/or social anxiety, or obsessive‐compulsive disorder. For each disorder, they received single intramuscular doses of racemic ketamine (0.5 and 1.0 mg/kg) and a psychoactive control, using a three‐way within‐subject double‐blind active‐controlled cross‐over design. Intramuscular racemic ketamine doses of 0.5 and 1.0 mg/kg produced robust effects on diagnosis‐appropriate outcome scales for all these internalizing disorders, compared with psychoactive controls. These effects were most obvious at 24 h after dosing and noticeably smaller at 168 h. The magnitude of change after 1 mg/kg did not differ significantly from 0.5 mg/kg at either time point, and did not differ significantly between the internalizing disorders at 24 h. When combined with other ketamine dose‐response data, we propose that ketamine may have a narrow inverted‐U dose‐response profile (peaking in the regions of 0.5‐1.0 mg/kg) in patients with internalizing disorders.

Introduction

Ketamine has been touted as a potential wonder treatment for internalizing disorders for over 25 years. “One would imagine that with a breakthrough discovery this important, ketamine would have been quickly repurposed as an antidepressant and receive US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval. Wrong. … [Even] the optimal dosing regimen remains largely undefined.” 1

There is limited information on ketamine's dose‐response relationship for internalizing disorders. A systematic review and meta‐analysis of 39 racemic ketamine randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in participants with treatment‐resistant depression (TRD) included an analysis of ketamine dose on change in depression ratings. 2 Dose was categorized as a binary factor of “high” if delivered intravenously (IV) at the equivalent of ≥0.5 mg/kg (or option of titrating up to this level) or “low” if <0.5 mg/kg. Greater effect sizes were noted for high doses compared with low doses in analyses of single dose data. A narrative review of racemic ketamine in treatment resistant depression (TRD) supported an initial IV infusion dose of 0.5 mg/kg over 40 min; however, it is noted that there were limited evidence‐based data to guide subsequent dose escalation. 3 In addition, Fava reported greater mood responses in patients with TRD 24 h after single IV ketamine doses of 0.5 and 1 mg/kg compared with 0.1 and 0.2 mg/kg doses, in a parallel group study. 4 In all of these cases, delivery was IV, and the threshold effective dose appeared to be in the region of 0.5 mg/kg.

We previously reported safety and short‐term efficacy of intramuscular racemic ketamine in patients with generalized and/or social anxiety (GAD/SAD 5 , 6 ), major depression (MDD 7 ), post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD 8 ), and obsessive‐compulsive disorder (OCD 9 ) who were resistant to conventional pharmacological and psychological treatment. In this secondary analysis, we compare the internalizing disorders—reporting dose‐ and diagnosis‐related effects of ketamine on relevant symptom rating scales up to 1‐week post‐dose.

Methods

Full methodological details of the studies contributing data for this analysis have been published previously. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 These studies were registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trial Registry (ACTRN 12615000617561 and ACTRN12619000311156). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. In brief, all studies used a three‐way within‐subject double‐blind, active‐controlled crossover design, with each patient receiving single intramuscular doses of racemic ketamine (0.5 and 1.0 mg/kg), and a psychoactive control (midazolam 0.01 mg/kg or fentanyl 50 mcg), with doses separated by intervals of at least 1 week with balanced randomization. Patients’ diagnoses included treatment resistant PTSD (n = 33), MDD (n = 25), GAD/SAD (n = 24) and OCD (n = 10). Diagnosis‐appropriate outcome scales 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 were administered pre‐dose and at 1, 2, 24, 72, and 168 h post‐dose.

Outcome scale data at 24 and 168 h post‐dose were converted to percentage change relative to 0 h baseline: (scale score − baseline) / baseline × 100. This transformation allowed for direct comparison across diagnoses and specific diagnostic measures. A separate within‐subject analysis comparing the Fear Questionnaire 10 and Hamilton Anxiety Scale 11 that were used to assess generalized and social anxiety disorder found no differences between the scales. The average of the two scales had better statistical power than either. We, therefore, used this average (labelled F+H in Figures) for final analysis. We carried out a similar comparison of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression scales 12 that were used to assess MDD cases. These differed significantly from each other (as would be expected for anxiety versus depression); so, we retained them separately—treating them as if they were distinct between‐subjects group measures to allow analysis together with the other between‐subject group scales.

SPSS Statistics Version 31.0.1.0 (Build 49) was used for all ANOVAs. It routinely extracts the orthogonal polynomial components reported here from repeated measures. P < .05 was taken as significant. The final mixed, between‐ and within‐subject design (scale × [dose × time]) included a small number of missing values (HADS‐A – 2/120; HADS‐D – 2/120; IESR – 3/178; YBOCS – 0/90) interpolated to avoid data loss (SPSS removes the entire participant wherever it finds a missing value). The missing values were from different participants, doses, and times. We then carried out an overall omnibus mixed‐effects analysis, followed by post‐hoc analyses as required.

Discussion

Our analyses indicate that, compared with psychoactive controls, intramuscular racemic ketamine doses of 0.5 and 1 mg/kg produced robust effects on diagnosis‐appropriate outcome scales for a range of internalizing disorders. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 These effects were most obvious at 24 h after dosing and noticeably smaller at 168 h. The magnitude of change after 1 mg/kg did not differ significantly from 0.5 mg/kg at either time point.

A meta‐analysis of single‐dose IV racemic ketamine studies in patients with TRD reported a larger effect size for studies using doses ≥0.5 mg/kg (ES ‐0.73, 95% CI −0.91 to −0.56) compared to studies using doses <0.5 mg/kg (ES −0.33, 95% CI −0.54 to −0.12) 2 . Another meta‐analysis of single‐dose IV studies found a dose‐response relationship for number needed to treat (NNT) for treatment response, with an NNT of 7 for 0.2 mg/kg, compared with an NNT of 3 for both 0.5 and 1 mg/kg doses. 15 This is consistent with data from patients with generalized and social anxiety disorders where intramuscular ketamine doses of 0.25 mg/kg produced much smaller changes in outcome scales compared with 0.5‐1 mg/kg doses. 5 , 6

In contrast, there are few published data reporting on the effects of doses greater than 1 mg/kg IV. An open‐label study in patients with TRD that permitted dose escalation during a course of IV treatment reported the greatest reduction in depression ratings at doses between 0.75 and 1.25 mg/kg, with smaller reductions at lower and higher doses. 16 In patients with TRD who received ketamine along with ECT, there were progressively smaller improvements in depression ratings with increasing ketamine dose in the range of 0.8‐1.2 mg/kg, and no improvement above 1.4 mg/kg. 17 One explanation for these findings is that ketamine has a narrow inverted‐U dose‐response profile. A similar narrow effective dose range was reported in a preclinical study that assessed the effect of ketamine dose on escape failures in a learned helplessness assay of antidepressant effectiveness in male mice. 18

There are a number of limitations of our analyses. The number of study participants in each study was relatively small (N = 33, 25, 24, and 10, respectively). We did not test doses >1 mg/kg, and there are relatively few data characterizing ketamine's dose‐response profile at higher doses. Ketamine was administered by IM injection in the analyzed studies rather than by IV infusion as in previous studies. However, a recent narrative review concluded that racemic IM ketamine appears to have comparable effects to IV ketamine. 19 We combined response data from four patient populations, post hoc. However, we had previously hypothesized that ketamine's broad activity across internalizing disorders may be due to a common effect on neuroticism across all the disorders. 20

In conclusion, ketamine may have a narrow inverted‐U dose‐response profile in patients with internalizing disorders, peaking in the regions of 0.5‐1.0 mg/kg. Future exploratory ketamine dose‐response studies will need larger patient numbers than ours, with a wider range of doses, and will also need to assess the effects of repeat dosing.
