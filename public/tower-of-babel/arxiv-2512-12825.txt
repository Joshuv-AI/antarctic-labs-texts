Introduction

In this paper we describe new exact results about the time evolution and steady states of a quantum system strongly coupled to a Markovian external reservoir. The reservoir acts only on part of the system, e.g. on its surface. More generally we consider a system consisting of two parts,
A
and
B
with finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{AB}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
, whose density matrix
\rho
evolves according to the Lindblad equation

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\rho(t)=\mathcal{K}\rho+\gamma\mathcal{D}\rho(t)=:\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}\rho(t)~.

(1)

Here,

\mathcal{K}\rho:=-i[H,\rho]

(2)

describes the unitary evolution of the isolated system due to its time-independent Hamiltonian
H
on
\mathcal{H}_{AB}
, while
\mathcal{D}
is a dissipative Lindblad generator of the form

\mathcal{D}:=\mathcal{D}_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}~,

(3)

where
\mathcal{D}_{A}
acts only on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
, here
\mathds{1}_{B}
is the identity super-operator on
\mathcal{H}_{B}
, and
\gamma
is a coupling constant.
By Lindblad’s Theorem
[
23
]
, a super-operator
\mathcal{D}_{A}
is such that
e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}}
is a completely positive trace preserving (CPTP) operation if and only if
\mathcal{D}_{A}
can be written in the form

\mathcal{D}_{A}\rho=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left(2L_{j}\rho L_{j}^{\dagger}-L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j}\rho-\rho L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j}\right)-i[K_{L},\rho]

for some operators
L_{1},\dots,L_{r}
on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
, often called
jump operators
and some self-adjoint operator
K_{L}
on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
. We refer to such super-operators as
Lindblad generators
.

We always assume that

\mathcal{D}_{A}
is ergodic so that there is a unique density matrix
\pi_{A}
on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
satisfying
\mathcal{D}_{A}\pi_{A}=0
.
We also assume that
\mathcal{D}_{A}
is
gapped
, meaning that the real parts of all of its nonzero eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}
satisfy
\Re(\lambda_{j})\leqslant-a
,
a>0
.
Since
\mathcal{D}_{A}
is a Lindblad generator, the two assumptions together are equivalent to
0
being a non-degenerate eigenvalue of
\mathcal{D}_{A}
, and
\mathcal{D}_{A}
not having any eigenvalues that are purely imaginary.

Without loss of generality, a constant has been added to the Hamiltonian so that
\textnormal{Tr}H=0
,
H=H_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}+H_{AB}+\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes H_{B}
; here
\mathds{1}_{A}
and
\mathds{1}_{B}
are the identity operators on those subspaces,
H_{A}=(1/d_{B})\textnormal{Tr}_{B}H
,
H_{B}=(1/d_{A})\textnormal{Tr}_{A}H
, and
d_{A}
and
d_{B}
are the dimensions of those subspaces.

In a recent paper
[
10
, Theorem 1]
, we proved that for such systems, if
\bar{\rho}
is a steady state solution of (
1
) and
[\bar{\rho},H]=0
, then necessarily
\bar{\rho}
is a product state of the form

\bar{\rho}=\pi_{A}\otimes\pi_{B}
for some density matrix
\pi_{B}
on
\mathcal{H}_{B}
. It then follows
[
10
, Corollary 2]
that a steady state
\bar{\rho}
can be a Gibbs state
\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}}e^{-\beta H}
for the system Hamiltonian
only if
\beta H_{AB}=0
. In particular, when the system Hamiltonian does couple the two parts of the system so
H_{AB}\neq 0
, no steady state of (
1
) will be a Gibbs state with
\beta\neq 0
. The question then arises: What is the nature of the steady-state solutions of (
1
), and what can be said about the approach to them in the limit
t\to\infty
?

We study these questions for large
\gamma
. The limit
\gamma\rightarrow\infty
is known as the Zeno limit. In this limit, there is a reduced description of the dynamics that has been investigated in
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
36
]
.

Because
\mathcal{D}_{A}
is ergodic and gapped, every density matrix
\rho
on
\mathcal{H}_{AB}
satisfying
\mathcal{D}\rho=0
has the form

\rho=\pi_{A}\otimes R
for some density matrix
R
on
\mathcal{H}_{B}
. Define
\mathcal{M}
, the
steady-state manifold
, by

\mathcal{M}:=\{\pi_{A}\otimes R\ :\ R\ {\rm is\ a\ density\ matrix\ on}\ \mathcal{H}_{B}\}\ .

(4)

Moreover, for all density matrices
\rho_{0}
on
\mathcal{H}_{AB}
,
{\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{t\mathcal{D}}\rho_{0}}
exists and belongs to
\mathcal{M}
. The super-operator

\mathcal{P}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{t\mathcal{D}}\

(5)

is a projection, though not necessarily an orthogonal projection. Its range consists of all operators on
\mathcal{H}_{AB}
of the form
\pi_{A}\otimes Y
for some operator
Y
on
\mathcal{H}_{B}
.

Under these assumptions, it was shown in
[
36
]
that after a relaxation time
t_{\gamma}
of order
\gamma^{-1}
,
\rho(t)=e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\rho_{0}

has the form

\rho(t)=\pi_{A}\otimes R(t)+\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{-1})\

(6)

uniformly in
t\geqslant t_{\gamma}
,

where

is a density matrix on
\mathcal{H}_{B}
. That is, solutions of (
1
) for a general initial state
\rho_{0}
and large

rapidly approach
\mathcal{M}
and then stay
close to
\mathcal{M}
, uniformly in time. In Section
3
we give simple proofs of results of this genre in which “closeness” is measured in terms of the trace norm, denoted by

.
In particular, we prove in Theorem

that for all
\rho_{0}=\pi_{A}\otimes R_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
, there is a constant

independent of
\gamma
such that for all

\|e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\pi_{A}\otimes R_{0}-\mathcal{P}e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\pi_{A}\otimes R_{0}\|_{1}\leqslant\frac{C}{\gamma}\ ,

(7)

which quantifies the sense in which solutions of (
1
) with initial data in
\mathcal{M}
stay close to
\mathcal{M}
. A result of this type, in a more general framework but with a more complicated proof may be found in
[
36
]
.

To deal with general initial data, we must take the initial relaxation time into account.
Define
t_{\gamma}:=\frac{2\log(\gamma)}{a\gamma}
where

is the spectral gap of
\mathcal{D}_{A}
.
We prove in Theorem

that there is a constant

independent of
\gamma
such that
for all

and all
\rho_{0}

\|e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\rho_{0}-e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\mathcal{P}\rho_{0}\|_{1}\leqslant\frac{\log(1+\gamma)}{\gamma}C\ .

(8)

Note that in (

), the projector
\mathcal{P}
follows
e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}
while in (
7
),
\mathcal{P}
preceded
e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}
.
These complementary results from Section

are important for the study of approximate equations of motion, to which we now turn.

It was shown in
[
36
]
that, after the initial relaxation time,

satisfies

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}R(t)=-i[H_{P},R(t)]+{\mathcal{O}}(\gamma^{-1})\ ,

(9)

where

H_{P}=\textnormal{Tr}_{A}[(\pi_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B})H]

(10)

is a self-adjoint operator called the
projected Hamiltonian
. The coherent evolution equation obtained by neglecting the
{\mathcal{O}}(\gamma^{-1})
term in (
9
) cannot be expected to give an accurate
description of the evolution of
R(t)
on long time scales of order

\gamma
since by then the error terms may accumulate to an effect of order one, and we expect to see dissipation on long times scales.

A description of the evolution of
R(t)
on time scales of order
\gamma
was obtained in
[
26
]
. The approximate equation obtained there is

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}R(t)=-i[H_{P},R(t)]-\gamma^{-1}\textnormal{Tr}_{A}[\mathcal{K}\mathcal{S}\mathcal{K}(\pi_{A}\otimes R(t))]+{\mathcal{O}}(\gamma^{-2})

(11)

where
\mathcal{S}
denotes a certain generalized inverse of
\mathcal{D}
that will be precisely defined in Section
2
below. Define the super-operators
\mathcal{K}_{P}
and
\mathcal{D}_{P}
acting on operators on
\mathcal{H}_{B}
by

\mathcal{K}_{P}R:=-i[H_{P},R]\quad{\rm and}\quad\mathcal{D}_{P}R:=-\textnormal{Tr}_{A}[\mathcal{K}\mathcal{S}\mathcal{K}(\pi_{A}\otimes R]\ .

(12)

These operators are studied in Section
4
.
It is evident that
\mathcal{K}_{P}
generates a CPTP evolution.
Under additional assumptions, the most important of which is a certain positivity condition, it was shown in
[
26
]
that
\mathcal{D}_{P}
is a Lindblad generator.
In this case,
\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}
defined by

\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}:=\mathcal{K}_{P}+\gamma^{-1}\mathcal{D}_{P}\

(13)

is a Lindblad generator, and hence
e^{t\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}}
is also CPTP for all
t\geqslant 0
.

We show here that under only the assumption that
\mathcal{D}_{A}
is ergodic
and gapped, the positivity condition required in
[
26
]
is
always satisfied, so that
\mathcal{D}_{P}
is always a Lindblad generator; see Theorem
4.4
. Thus we
may re-write (

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}R(t)=\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}R(t)+{\mathcal{O}}(\gamma^{-2})\ ,

(14)

and the equation
{\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\widetilde{R}(t)=\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}\widetilde{R}(t)}
describes a CPTP evolution that closely tracks the projection onto
\mathcal{M}
of the evolution generated by
\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}
. We prove the following precise expression of this: Let
R_{0}
be any density matrix on
\mathcal{H}_{B}
and define
R(t):=\textnormal{Tr}_{A}e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\pi_{A}\otimes R_{0}
so that
\mathcal{P}(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\pi_{A}\otimes R_{0})=\pi_{A}\otimes R(t)
.
Making essential use of the fact that
\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}
generates a CPTP evolution, we prove in Theorem
5.1
that there exists a constant
C
depending only on
\mathcal{D}_{A}
and the operator norm of
H
such for all
T>0
,

\|R(t)-e^{t\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}}R_{0}\|_{1}\leqslant\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}CT\

(15)

for all
0\leqslant t\leqslant T
.

The generator in (
13
) contains only the first order corrections to the coherent evolution. At the end of Section
5
, we derive the explicit form of the next order corrections, yielding the approximate equation

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}R=\mathcal{K}_{P}R(t)+\gamma^{-1}\mathcal{D}_{P}R(t)+\gamma^{-2}\mathcal{B}_{P}R(t)\ .

(16)

(See equation (
92
) for the explicit form of
\mathcal{B}_{P}
). However, a simple 2 qubit example (See Example
6.1
) shows that it can be the case that (
16
) does not describe a CPTP evolution for any
\gamma>0
.
That is,
\mathcal{K}_{P}+\gamma^{-1}\mathcal{D}_{P}+\gamma^{-2}\mathcal{B}_{P}
cannot be put in
Lindblad form for any
\gamma>0
.

We are particularly interested in stationary states of
(
1
); that is
density matrices
\bar{\rho}_{\gamma}
on
\mathcal{H}_{AB}
that satisfy

\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}\bar{\rho}_{\gamma}=0
. These may be realized in the
long time limit:
\bar{\rho}_{\gamma}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\rho(t)
,
when
\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}
has no purely imaginary eigenvalues, and always by

{\displaystyle\bar{\rho}_{\gamma}=\lim_{T\to\infty}T^{-1}\int_{0}^{T}\rho(t){\rm d}t}
.

We may apply (
15
) to study the long time behavior of solutions of (
1
) on account of (
7
) and (
8
):
Let
\rho_{0}
be any density matrix on
\mathcal{H}_{AB}
, and define
R_{0}:=\textnormal{Tr}_{A}[\rho_{0}]
so that

\mathcal{P}\rho_{0}=\pi_{A}\otimes R_{0}
. Then by the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\|e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\rho_{0}-\pi_{A}\otimes e^{t\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}}R_{0}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\|e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\rho_{0}-e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\pi_{A}\otimes R_{0}\|_{1}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\|e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\pi_{A}\otimes R_{0}-\pi_{A}\otimes R(t)\|_{1}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\|\pi_{A}\otimes R(t)-\pi_{A}\otimes e^{t\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}}R_{0}\|_{1}\ .

Using (
7
), (
8
) and (

) to bound the three terms on the right leads to the proof of Theorem

which gives a tight relation between the evolutions governed by
\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}

\|e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\rho_{0}-\pi_{A}\otimes e^{t\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}}R_{0}\|_{1}\leqslant C\frac{\log(1+\gamma)+1+T}{\gamma},

(17)

uniformly on

In particular, for all

\lim_{\gamma\to\infty}\|e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\rho_{0}-\pi_{A}\otimes e^{t\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}}R_{0}\|_{1}=0\ ,

but since for large

does not differ much from

, it follows that (see Theorem

for any fixed

In other words, in the Zeno limit,
all of the dissipation takes place instantly
during the initial passage from
\rho_{0}
to
\mathcal{P}\rho_{0}=\pi_{A}\otimes R_{0}
, and from then on the evolution is coherent.

The situation near, but not at, the Zeno limit is
more interesting, and then there is dissipation on time scales of order
\gamma
, and the more precise bound (
17
) can be used to realte the rates of approach to stationarity
for
\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}
and
\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}
. However, the analysis of both of these
Lindblad generators is complicated because in both the coherent and dissipative effects operate on different times scales. For instance, with
\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}
the coherent evolution due to
\mathcal{K}_{P}
acts on time scales of order one, while the dissipative evolution due to
\mathcal{D}_{P}
acts on time scale
\mathcal{O}(\gamma)
.

To overcome this we introduce a new Lindblad generator
\mathcal{D}_{P}^{\sharp}
, which is independent of
\gamma
.
The new Lindblad generator

\mathcal{D}_{P}^{\sharp}
is derived from
\mathcal{D}_{P}
and
\mathcal{K}_{P}
through an averaging
operation introduced by Davies
[
13
]
in his derivation of Lindblad
evolution equations in the weak coupling limit.
The
\sharp
notation is Davies’. More specifically, for
any super-operator
\mathcal{T}
on
\mathcal{H}_{B}
, define

\mathcal{T}^{\sharp}(X)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}e^{-t\mathcal{K}_{P}}\mathcal{T}(e^{t\mathcal{K}_{P}}X){\rm d}t\ .

(20)

This limit always exists, and the rate at which convergence
takes place in (
20
) can be bounded in terms of the
spectral properties
H_{P}
. Davies’ averaging operation is studied in Section
6
,
and we apply these results in Section

.

In Section
7
we study
e^{-t\mathcal{K}_{P}}e^{t\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}}R_{0}
in the Zeno limit,
\gamma\to\infty
for general initial data

. We run the initial data
forward under
the dissipative time evolution governed by
\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}
, and then backwards under the coherent evolution generated by
\mathcal{K}_{P}
. In the language of scattering theory, we are passing to
the “interaction picture” to compare the coherent evolution and its perturbation by a weak dissipation. This is what Davies
[
13
]
did in his derivation of Lindblad equations in the weak coupling limit,
and hence it is no surprise that his averaging operation introduced there shows up again here.

Since

\mathcal{K}_{P}
and
\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}
differ by a term of order
\gamma^{-1}
,
we expect

to differ significantly
from the identity only for times of order
\gamma
. It is therefore
natural to introduce the rescaled time
\tau
given by

t=\gamma\tau\ .

(21)

We shall then show that

\lim_{\gamma\to\infty}e^{-\gamma\tau\mathcal{K}_{P}}e^{\gamma\tau\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}}=e^{\tau\mathcal{D}_{P}^{\sharp}}\ .

(22)

The rescaling of time (

) is different than in Davies’ work because the Zeno limit is not the weak coupling limit, but as in

[

]
we use the elementary theory of perturbation of Volterra integral equations to
compare the evolutions.

The precise version of (

is ergodic and gapped, then for all

independent of

on

,

This does not directly refer to the evolution governed by

The results (

\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}

\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}

In particular, we use (

This is best done in terms of the

, which plays an important role in several recent investigations of open quantum systems
[

33
,
34
,
35

For any two density matrices
\rho_{0}
,
\rho_{1}

\mathcal{H}
, define their
total variation distance

d_{{\rm TV}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})=\frac{1}{2}\|\rho_{0}-\rho_{1}\|_{1}\ ,

(25)

and hence

with equality if and only if
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
are supported on mutually orthogonal subspaces of
\mathcal{H}

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a Lindblad generator acting on operators on
\mathcal{H}
a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, and let

.
The

is defined here by

(26)

with the convention that

if there is no finite time
t
such that

d_{{\rm TV}}(e^{t\mathcal{L}}\rho_{0},e^{t\mathcal{L}}\rho_{1})<\epsilon
for all
pairs density matrices
\rho_{0},\rho_{1}
.
It is known that if
t_{{\rm mix}}(\mathcal{L},\epsilon)<\infty
for some
0<\epsilon<\tfrac{1}{2}
, then this is true for all such
\epsilon
; see
Theorem
E.3
.

This definition does not
require
\mathcal{L}
to be ergodic, but
t_{{\rm mix}}(\mathcal{L},\tfrac{1}{4})<\infty
if
and only if
\mathcal{L}
is ergodic. We will use comparison of mixing times
to prove ergodicity, hence this definition, which does not refer to a
putative unique steady state
\pi
, is the one adapted to our applications.
In our finite dimensional setting, a spectral gap implies finite mixing times, but the mixing times do not depend on the spectral gap
a
and
\epsilon
alone . For these facts and more on mixing times, see Appendix
E
.

Returning to
\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}
,
\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}
and
\mathcal{D}_{P}^{\sharp}
, we prove in
Theorem
7.5
that for all but at most contably many values of
\epsilon_{0}\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
,

\lim_{\gamma\to\infty}\frac{t_{{\rm mix}}(\mathcal{L}_{\gamma},\epsilon_{0})}{\gamma}=\lim_{\gamma\to\infty}\frac{t_{{\rm mix}}(\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma},\epsilon_{0})}{\gamma}=t_{{\rm mix}}(\mathcal{D}_{P}^{\sharp},\epsilon_{0})\ .

This has the consequence that if
\mathcal{D}_{P}^{\sharp}
is ergodic and gapped, then so are
\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}
and
\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}
for all sufficiently large
\gamma
. See Theorem
7.5
for a precise statement.

This shows one way in which
\mathcal{D}_{P}^{\sharp}
governs the long-time behavior of the evolution described by both

\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}
, and d by
\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}
, near the Zeno limit. For another, suppose that
\mathcal{D}_{P}^{\sharp}
is ergodic and gapped. and let
\bar{R}
be the unique density matrix on
\mathcal{H}_{B}
such that
\mathcal{D}_{P}^{\sharp}\bar{R}=0
. Let
\bar{\rho}_{\gamma}
be the unique (for
\gamma
sufficiently large)
density matrix on
\mathcal{H}_{AB}
satisfying
\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}\bar{\rho}_{\gamma}=0
. Then we prove that

\lim_{\gamma\to\infty}\bar{\rho}_{\gamma}=\pi_{A}\otimes\bar{R}\ .

(27)

Note the difference with (
18
): here we first let
t\to\infty

to obtain
\bar{\rho}_{\gamma}
and then take
\gamma\to\infty
, rather than
the other way around. By (
19
), if we first take

\gamma\to\infty
, after an initial dissipative projection onto
\mathcal{M}
,
the motion is coherent, and

\lim_{\gamma\to\infty}e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}\rho_{0}}=\pi_{A}\otimes e^{t\mathcal{K}_{P}}\textnormal{Tr}_{A}\rho_{0}
has no limit as
t\to\infty
.

In Section
8
we turn to the computation of non-equilibrium steady states (NESS) for
(
1
). Even in the general setting of open quantum
systems, boundary driven or not, there are few cases in which NESS can be computed for all but the smallest of systems. Extremely valuable examples in which this has be done are given in the work of Prosen
[
30
,
31
]
and Popkov and Prosen
[
29
]
. However, in most cases, to understand the natures of currents in NESS, one must use perturbation theory.

There is a large literature on the perturbative approach; see
[
20
, Section III.D]
. The simplest cases concern a Linblad equation of the type

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\rho(t)=(\mathcal{L}_{0}+\epsilon\mathcal{L}_{1})\rho(t)

(28)

in which
\mathcal{L}_{0}
is ergodic so that there is a unique denisty matrix

\bar{\rho}_{0}
satisfying
\mathcal{L}_{0}\bar{\rho}_{0}=0
. One then
assumes

that for
\epsilon
sufficiently small, there is a unique density matrix

\bar{\rho}_{\epsilon}
satisfying
(\mathcal{L}_{0}+\epsilon\mathcal{L}_{1})\bar{\rho}_{\epsilon}=0
and moreover (the key assumption), that
\bar{\rho}_{\epsilon}
has an
expansion

\bar{\rho}_{\gamma}=\bar{\rho}_{0}+\epsilon\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{k}\bar{n}_{k}\ .

(29)

Inserting this expansion into the equation (
28
), and equating terms with like powers of
\epsilon
leads to the system of equations

\mathcal{L}_{0}\bar{n}_{0}=-\mathcal{L}_{1}\rho_{0}\quad{\rm and,\ for}\ k\geqslant 1,\quad\mathcal{L}_{0}\bar{n}_{k}=\mathcal{L}_{1}\bar{n}_{k-1}\ .

(30)

When
\mathcal{L}_{0}
is ergodic, its range consists of all traceless operators, and hence if
\mathcal{L}_{1}
is also a Lindblad generator, so that everything in its range is traceless, each of the equations in (
30
)
is solvable, and there will be a unique traceless solution. If one can
then prove that the series converges in the trace norm, and can prove that
\mathcal{L}_{0}+\epsilon\mathcal{L}_{1}
is ergodic for all sufficiently small
\epsilon
, one will have justified the expansion (
29
), from which
\bar{\rho}_{\epsilon}
can now be confidently computed.

Our problem is more complex. Here,
\mathcal{D}
plays the role of
\mathcal{L}_{0}
and it is far from ergodic. Therefore, there is no obvious candidate for
\bar{\rho}_{0}
. A related problem arises in
[
21
]
for a boundary driven spin chain model (but not near the Zeno limit). In that case, a conjectured ansatz
[
21
, eq. (6)]
based on exact calculation for short chains provides starting point
\rho_{0}
, but there is no proof that is the correct starting point of the expansion.

In our setting, the Lindblad generator
\mathcal{D}_{P}^{\sharp}
provides an equation for
\bar{\rho}_{0}
, at least when
\mathcal{D}_{P}^{\sharp}
is ergodic and gapped. Then by (
27
),
\lim_{\gamma\to\infty}\bar{\rho}_{\gamma}=\pi_{A}\otimes\bar{R}
, where
\mathcal{D}_{P}^{\sharp}\bar{R}=0
,
and thus we take
\pi_{A}\otimes\bar{R}
as the starting point of an expansion

\bar{\rho}_{\gamma}=\pi_{A}\otimes\bar{R}+\gamma^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{-k}\bar{n}_{k}\ .

Now the equations to be solved are

\mathcal{D}\bar{n}_{0}=\mathcal{K}(\pi_{A}\otimes\bar{R})

(31)

and then for each
k\geqslant 1
,

\mathcal{D}\bar{n}_{k}=\mathcal{K}\bar{n}_{k-1}\ .

(32)

On account of the large null space of
\mathcal{D}
, and correspondingly small range, the solvability conditions are not so simple as they were for
(
30
), and even when these equations are individually solvable, they will have infinitely many solutions. Nonetheless, under the conditions described above, we show the existence of a unique solution of the hierarchy (
31
)
and (
32
). Moreover, for finite constants
C_{0}
and
C_{1}
,
\|\bar{n}_{k}\|_{1}\leqslant C_{0}C_{1}^{k}
for all
k
so that
the series converges in trace norm for all
\gamma>C_{1}
, and one can easily estimate the remainder if the sum is truncated.
These results are the content of Theorem
8.3
which presents them in a more explicit form.

The final section of the paper discusses an analogy with the theory of hydrodynamic limits that provides an interesting perspective on the relations between the evolution equations specified by
\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}
,
\mathcal{K}_{P}
,
\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}
and
\mathcal{D}_{P}^{\sharp}
.

2
Notation and some mathematical preliminaries

Let
\mathcal{H}
be any finite dimensional Hilbert space. Then
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
will always denote the Hilbert space consisting of operators on
\mathcal{H}
equipped with the Hilbert-Schmidt inner
product
\langle X,Y\rangle_{\widehat{\mathcal{H}}}^{\phantom{x}}:=\textnormal{Tr}[X^{*}Y]
. For
X\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
,
\|X\|_{\infty}
denotes the
operator norm
of
X
which is the largest singular value of
X
,

\|X\|_{1}
denotes the trace norm of
X
, which is the sum of the singular values of
X
, repeated according to their multiplicity. The Hilbert-Schmidt norm of
X
is denoted by
\|X\|_{2}
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
be two finite dimensional Hilbert spaces. Let
\mathcal{T}
be a linear operator from
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
to
\widehat{\mathcal{K}}
; that is a super-operator from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{K}
.
The adjoint super-operator from
\widehat{\mathcal{K}}
to
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
will always be denoted by
\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}
.

Let
Y
be an operator on
\mathcal{K}
and
X
be an operator on
\mathcal{H}
.
Then we may regard
Y
and
X
as vectors
\widehat{\mathcal{K}}
and

\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
respectively, and define the
super-operator
|Y\rangle\langle X|
from
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}

to
\widehat{\mathcal{K}}
by

|Y\rangle\langle X|Z=\langle X,Z\rangle_{\widehat{\mathcal{H}}}^{\phantom{x}}Y

(33)

for all
Z
in
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
. In our analysis, this plays the
role of the “vectorization” discussed in
[
20
, Section III.B]
,
but avoids identifying
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
with
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
and

\widehat{\mathcal{K}}
with
\mathcal{K}\otimes\mathcal{K}
which requires a choice of basis.

The symbol
\mathds{1}
will be used both for the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}
and the identity super-operator
on
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
; which is intended will always be clear from the context.

Define

\|\mathcal{T}\|_{1\to 1}=\sup\{\|\mathcal{T}(X)\|_{1}\ :\|X\|_{1}=1\ \}\ .

(34)

This is a norm on the space of super-operators from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{K}
; in particular, it satisfies the triangle inequality. We refer to it as the
super-operator trace norm

For example, with
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{AB}
, let
\mathcal{K}
be the super-operator defined by (
2
). Then for all
X\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{AB}
,

\|\mathcal{K}X\|_{1}=\|HX-XH\|_{1}\leqslant\|HX\|_{1}+\|XH\|_{1}\leqslant 2\|H\|_{\infty}\|X\|_{1}

where the last inequality is Hölder’s inequality for traces. (See e.g.
[
9
, Section 6.3]
.) Therefore,

\|\mathcal{K}\|_{1\to 1}\leqslant 2\|H\|_{\infty}\ .

(35)

It is known that if
\mathcal{T}
is completely positive and trace preserving (CPTP), then

\|\mathcal{T}\|_{1\to 1}=1
; see e.g.
[
9
, Theorem 6.26]
. In other words, CPTP maps are contractive (non-increasing) in the trace norm, and this figures in the next lemma which will be used repeatedly in what follows.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and let
\mathcal{L}
be a Lindblad generator acting on the trace class operator on
\mathcal{H}
, so that for all
t\geqslant 0
,
e^{t\mathcal{L}}
is CPTP. Let
\rho(t)
solve

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\rho(t)=\mathcal{L}\rho(t)+F(t)

(36)

with
\rho(0)=\rho_{0}
, and
where for all
t\leqslant T
,
\int_{0}^{t}\|F(s)\|_{1}{\rm d}s\leqslant\epsilon
. Then

\|\rho(t)-e^{t\mathcal{L}}\rho_{0}\|_{1}\leqslant\epsilon
for all
t\leqslant T
.

Proof.

Writing (
36
) as a Volterra integral equation and using a Picard iteration
shows that this initial value problem has a unique solution given by

{\displaystyle\rho(t)=e^{t\mathcal{L}}\rho_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}}F(s){\rm d}s}
.
By the triangle inequality, the fact that
\|e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}}\|_{1\to 1}\leqslant 1
, and (
34
),

{\displaystyle\|\rho(t)-e^{t\mathcal{L}}\rho_{0}\|_{1}\leqslant\int_{0}^{t}\|F(s)\|_{1}{\rm d}s}
.
∎

Now let
\mathcal{D}_{A}
be an ergodic gapped Lindblad generator acting on
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{A}
, and let
\pi_{A}
be the unique steady state. Because of the gap, as in the definition (
5
) of
\mathcal{P}
,

\mathcal{P}_{A}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}}

(37)

exists in the super-operator trace norm – or any other norm in this finite dimensional setting. As a limit of CPTP maps, it is CPTP. In fact, it is easy to see that for all
X\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{A}
,

\mathcal{P}_{A}X=\textnormal{Tr}[X]\pi_{A}
, and that
\mathcal{P}_{A}
is a projection onto the subspace of
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{A}
that is spanned by
\pi_{A}
. One readily computes that for all
Y\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{A}
,

\mathcal{P}_{A}^{\dagger}Y=\textnormal{Tr}[\pi_{A}Y]\mathds{1}
, so that while
\mathcal{P}
is a projection, it is not an orthogonal projection on
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{A}
.
In the notation (
33
),
\mathcal{P}_{A}=|\pi_{A}\rangle\langle\mathds{1}|
and

\mathcal{P}_{A}^{\dagger}=|\pi_{A}\rangle\langle\mathds{1}|
.

Define the complementary projection
\mathcal{Q}_{A}
by

\mathcal{Q}_{A}:=\mathds{1}-\mathcal{P}_{A}\ .

(38)

Because of the spectral gap,

\mathcal{S}_{A}:=-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}}\mathcal{Q}_{A}{\rm d}t

(39)

exists and satisfies

\mathcal{S}_{A}\mathcal{D}_{A}=\mathcal{D}_{A}\mathcal{S}_{A}=\mathcal{Q}_{A}\ ,

(40)

so that
\mathcal{S}_{A}
is a generalized inverse of
\mathcal{D}_{A}
, though it is not the Moore-Penrose
[
24
,
25
]
generalized inverse since
\mathcal{Q}_{A}
is not an orthogonal projection.

Because
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}
,
e^{t\mathcal{D}}=e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}}\otimes\mathds{1}
. Therefore, the projection
\mathcal{P}
defined by (
5
) satisfies

\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}\ .

(41)

Define

\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{Q}_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}\quad{\rm and}\quad\mathcal{S}:=\mathcal{S}_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}\ .

(42)

Then
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{P}
are complimentary projections, and
it follows from (
40
) that

\mathcal{S}\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{Q}\ ,

(43)

so that
\mathcal{S}
is a generalized inverse of
\mathcal{D}
. Since
\mathcal{P}_{A}X=\textnormal{Tr}[X]\pi_{A}
, if follows that for all
Z\in\widehat{H}_{AB}
,
\mathcal{P}X=\pi_{A}\otimes\textnormal{Tr}_{A}Z
where
\textnormal{Tr}_{A}
denotes the super-operator from

\widehat{H}_{AB}
to
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{B}
sending
Z
to its partial trace over
\mathcal{H}_{A}
,
\textnormal{Tr}_{A}[Z]
. As is well-known,
\textnormal{Tr}_{A}
is CPTP.
Define the super-operator
\mathcal{V}
from
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{B}
to
\widehat{H}_{AB}
by

\mathcal{V}(X):=\pi_{A}\otimes X\ .

(44)

This is also CPTP; if
X
is a density matrix, it corresponds to adding ancilla in the steady state of
\mathcal{D}_{A}
. Therefore, we have the factorization
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{V}\textnormal{Tr}_{A}
.

3
Bounds on the projected evolution of
\rho(t)

Recall that
\mathcal{M}
denotes the steady state manifold (
4
). As noted in the introduction, it has been shown in
[
36
]
under very general conditions that
solutions of (
1
) with general initial data
\rho_{0}
approach
\mathcal{M}
in a time of order
1/\gamma
, and then stay close to
\mathcal{M}
, within a distance of order
1/\gamma
, uniformly in time.
In this section we derive results of this type under the assumption that
\mathcal{D}_{A}
is a Lindblad generator that is ergodic and gapped with stationary state
\pi_{A}
.
Using this additional information on the generator, we derive more precise bounds in a simple manner.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{A}
be ergodic with spectral gap
a>0
. There are positive finite and calculable constants
C_{0}
and and
C_{1}
depending only on
\mathcal{D}_{A}
and
\|H\|_{\infty}
(but not on the dimension of

\mathcal{H}_{B}
) such that for all
t>0
,

\|\mathcal{Q}e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\|_{1\to 1}\leqslant C_{0}e^{-\gamma at/2}+\frac{C_{1}}{\gamma}\ .

(45)

and

\|\mathcal{Q}e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\mathcal{P}\|_{1\to 1}\leqslant\frac{C_{1}}{\gamma}\ .

(46)

Remark 3.1
.

Let
\rho_{0}
be any density matrix on
\mathcal{H}_{AB}
, and define
\rho(t):=e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\rho_{0}
. Then as a consequence of (
45
) and
\mathcal{Q}=\mathds{1}-\mathcal{P}
,

{\displaystyle\|\rho(t)-\mathcal{P}\rho(t)\|_{1}\leqslant C_{0}e^{-\gamma at/2}+\frac{C_{1}}{\gamma}}
.
For
t>\frac{2\log(\gamma)}{a\gamma}
, this simplifies to

\|\rho(t)-\mathcal{P}\rho(t)\|_{1}=\|\mathcal{Q}\rho(t)\|_{1}\leqslant\frac{C_{0}+C_{1}}{\gamma}\ .

This is a precise trace norm version of the result (
6
) of
[
36
]
. The proof below, which makes crucial use of the fact that the CPTP super-operator
e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}
is a contraction in the trace norm, is much simpler than the analysis in
[
36
]
which however is not restricted to CPTP evolution equations.

Proof.

By Duhammel’s formula,

e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}=e^{t\gamma\mathcal{D}}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\gamma\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{K}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}{\rm d}s\ .

(47)

By the triangle inequality and the fact that
[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{D}]=0
(see (
40
) and (
42
)),

\|\mathcal{Q}e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\|_{1\to 1}\leqslant\|e^{t\gamma\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{Q}\|_{1\to 1}+\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{(t-s)\gamma\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{K}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\|_{1\to 1}{\rm d}s\ .

(48)

By the definition of the super-operator trace norm,

\|e^{(t-s)\gamma\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{K}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\|_{1\to 1}\leqslant\|\mathcal{Q}e^{(t-s)\gamma\mathcal{D}}\|_{1\to 1}\|\mathcal{K}\|_{1\to 1}\|e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\|_{1\to 1}

Since
e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}
is CPTP,
\|e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\|_{1\to 1}=1
, and by (
35
),
\|\mathcal{K}\|_{1\to 1}\leqslant 2\|H\|_{\infty}
. By Theorem
C.2
, for a finite constant
C
depending only on
\mathcal{D}_{A}
,

\|\mathcal{Q}e^{t\gamma\mathcal{D}}\|_{1\to 1}\leqslant Ce^{-t\gamma a/2}
.
Using these estimates in (
48
),

\|\mathcal{Q}e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\|_{1\to 1}\leqslant Ce^{-t\gamma a/2}+2\|H\|_{\infty}C\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\gamma(t-s)a/2}{\rm d}s

which yields (
45
) with
C_{0}=C
and
C_{1}=\frac{4C\|H\|_{\infty}}{a}
.
The proof of (
46
) is even simpler: Since
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
commute with
e^{(t-s)\mathcal{D}}
,
\mathcal{Q}e^{(t-s)\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{P}=0
, and the contribution of the term involving
\mathcal{K}
is no larger than before.
∎

Crucially for what follows, the projected evolution is globally Lipschitz, and after a relaxation time of order
\gamma^{-1}
, so is the full evolution.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any density matrix
\rho_{0}
on
\mathcal{H}
, define
\rho(t):=e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\rho_{0}
and
R(t):=\textnormal{Tr}_{A}\rho(t)
. Then for all
t>s>0
,

\|R(t)-R(s)\|\leqslant\|\mathcal{K}\|_{1\to 1}(t-s)\ .

(49)

Moreover, for all
t>s>\frac{2\log(\gamma)}{a\gamma}
,

\|\rho(t)-\rho(s)\|_{1}\leqslant L(t-s)

(50)

where
L=(\|\mathcal{K}\|_{1\to 1}+C_{0}+C_{1})
, with
C_{0}
and
C_{1}
as in Theorem
3.1
. If
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
, then (
50
) is valid for all
t>s\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}
, and since
\textnormal{Tr}_{A}\mathcal{D}_{A}=0
,

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}R(t)=\textnormal{Tr}_{A}(\mathcal{K}+\gamma\mathcal{D})\rho(t)=\textnormal{Tr}_{A}\mathcal{K}\rho(t)\ .

Then since
\|\textnormal{Tr}_{A}\mathcal{K}\rho(t)\|_{1}\leqslant\|\textnormal{Tr}_{A}\|_{1\to 1}\|\mathcal{K}\|_{1\to 1}\|\rho(t)\|_{1}=\|\mathcal{K}\|_{1\to 1}
. This proves (
49
).

Next, using the fact that
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}\mathcal{Q}
, we compute

{\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\rho=\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\rho)=\mathcal{K}(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\rho)+\gamma\mathcal{D}\mathcal{Q}e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\rho}
.
Therefore, by Remark
3.1
, for
t>t_{\gamma}=\frac{2\log(\gamma)}{a\gamma}

\left\|\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\rho(t)\right\|_{1}\leqslant\|\mathcal{K}\|_{1\to 1}+\gamma\frac{C}{\gamma a}=\|\mathcal{K}\|_{1\to 1}+\frac{C}{a}\ .

Now integration yields the bound. When
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
, the condition
t>\frac{2\log(\gamma)}{a\gamma}
is superfluous.
∎

The next theorem says that near the Zeno limit, we may replace general initial data
\rho_{0}
by its projection onto
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{P}\rho_{0}
, and after a time of order
\gamma^{-1}
, the trace norm
difference between the two solution is (essentially) of order
\gamma^{-1}
, uniformly in time.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{A}
be ergodic with spectral gap
a>0
. There is a constant
C>0
independent of
\gamma
such that
for all
t\geqslant t_{\gamma}:=\frac{2\log(\gamma)}{a\gamma}
,

\|e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\rho-e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\mathcal{P}\rho\|_{1}\leqslant\frac{\log(1+\gamma)}{\gamma}C\ .

(51)

Proof.

Fix
t>0
, and let
\gamma
be sufficiently large that
t>t_{\gamma}
. Then

e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\rho=e^{(t-t_{\gamma})\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}(\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{Q})e^{t_{\gamma}\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\rho=e^{(t-t_{\gamma})\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\mathcal{P}\rho+e^{(t-t_{\gamma})\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\mathcal{Q}e^{t_{\gamma}\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\rho\ .

By the definition of
t_{\gamma}
, Theorem
3.1
, and the fact that
e^{(t-t_{\gamma})\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}
is a contraction in the trace norm,
\|e^{(t-t_{\gamma})\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\mathcal{Q}e^{t_{\gamma}\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\rho\|_{1}\leqslant\frac{C_{0}+C_{1}}{\gamma}
. By Lemma
3.2
,

{\displaystyle\|e^{(t-t_{\gamma})\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\mathcal{P}\rho-e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}}\mathcal{P}\rho\|_{1}\leqslant Lt_{\gamma}}
,
and now (
51
) follows by the triangle inequality.
∎

4
The super-operators
\mathcal{K}_{P}
and
\mathcal{D}_{P}

Recall the that the super-operator
\mathcal{V}
acting on
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{B}
has been defined in (
44
) by
\mathcal{V}X=\pi_{A}\otimes X
. The super-operator
\mathcal{K}_{P}
defined in (
12
) can be expressed
in terms of
\mathcal{V}
which is how it shall arise in expansions considered here. The following lemma
is due to
[
36
]
; its simple proof is included for convenience.

Lemma 4.1
.

For all density matrices
R
on
\mathcal{H}_{B}
,

\textnormal{Tr}_{A}\mathcal{K}\mathcal{V}R=-i[H_{P},R]=:\mathcal{K}_{P}R

(52)

where

H_{P}:=\textnormal{Tr}_{A}[(\pi_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B})H]
as in (
10
).

Proof.

By the definition (
44
) of
\mathcal{V}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{P}R=\mathcal{P}\mathcal{K}\pi_{A}\otimes R

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\mathcal{P}\left(H(\pi_{A}\otimes R)-(\pi_{A}\otimes R)H\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\mathcal{P}\left(H(\pi_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B})(\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes R)-(\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes R)(\pi_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B})H\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\left(\pi_{A}\otimes\textnormal{Tr}_{A}[H(\pi_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B})(\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes R)]-\pi_{A}\otimes\textnormal{Tr}_{A}[(\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes R)(\pi_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B})H]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\left(\pi_{A}\otimes\textnormal{Tr}_{A}[H(\pi_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B})]R-\pi_{A}\otimes R\textnormal{Tr}_{A}[(\pi_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B})H]\right)\ .

By the partial cyclicity of the partial trace,

\textnormal{Tr}_{A}[H(\pi_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B})]=\textnormal{Tr}_{A}[(\pi_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B})H]=H_{P}
, which shows that
H_{P}
is self-adjoint, and proves (
52
).
∎

For future use,
note that since

and

are CPTP hence hence trace norm contractions,

\|\mathcal{K}_{P}\|_{1\to 1}\leqslant\|\mathcal{K}\|_{1\to 1}\leqslant 2\|H\|_{\infty}

(53)

where the final inequality is from (

).

As an immediate consequence of Lemma

,
\mathcal{K}_{P}
generates a coherent CPTP evolution. We now turn to
\mathcal{D}_{P}
as defined by

\mathcal{D}_{P}:=-\textnormal{Tr}_{A}\mathcal{K}\mathcal{S}\mathcal{K}\mathcal{V}\ .

(54)

It was shown in
[

]
that under certain conditions,

is a Lindblad generator so that

generates a CPTP evolution,
and therefore so does

\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}:=\mathcal{K}_{P}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\mathcal{D}_{P}\ .

(55)

.

We show that
\mathcal{D}_{P}
is a Lindblad generator assuming only that

is ergodic and gapped. This was shown in
[
26
]
under the additional assumptions that

is diagonalizable, and more important, that a certain matrix determined by

is positive semidefinite, but conditions for this matrix to be positive semidefinite are not discussed in
[

.

The next lemma presents the argument of
[
26
]
adapted to remove the assumption that

is diagonalizable. Apart from the small changes needed to avoid assuming that

is diagonalizable, we closely follow the argument in
[
26
]
. For background on Jordan bases and dual bases, see Appendix
B
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{A}
be ergodic and gapped with the unique steady state
\pi_{A}
. Let
\{Y_{0},\dots,Y_{n_{A}}\}
be a Jordan basis for
\mathcal{D}_{A}
such that
Y_{0}=\pi_{A}
, and let

\{X_{0},\dots,X_{n_{A}}\}
be the dual basis. Define the operators
\{G_{0},\dots,G_{n_{A}}\}
on
\mathcal{H}_{B}
by

H=\sum_{j=0}^{n_{A}}X_{j}\otimes G_{j}\ .

(56)

Define an
n_{A}\times n_{A}
matrix
M
by

M_{j,k}:=-\langle\mathcal{S}_{A}^{\dagger}X_{k},X_{j}\pi_{A}\rangle

(57)

and define
A=\frac{1}{2}(M+M^{\dagger})
and
B:=\frac{1}{2i}(M-M^{\dagger})
.
Suppose that
A
is positive semidefinite so that it has an spectral decomposition

A_{j,k}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\mu_{\ell}v^{(\ell)}_{j}\overline{v^{(\ell)}_{k}}

(58)

where
r
is the rank of
A
, and each
\mu_{\ell}
is strictly positive.

Then
\mathcal{D}_{P}
, defined in (
54
), is a Lindblad generator, and
for all operators
R
on
\mathcal{H}_{B}
,

\mathcal{D}_{P}R=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\left(2V_{\ell}RV_{\ell}^{\dagger}-V_{\ell}^{\dagger}V_{\ell}R-RV_{\ell}^{\dagger}V_{\ell}\right)-i[H_{L},R]\ ,

(59)

where

V_{\ell}:=\sqrt{\mu_{\ell}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{A}}\overline{v^{(\ell)}_{k}}G_{k}\quad{\rm and}\quad H_{L}:=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n_{A}}B_{j,k}G^{\dagger}_{k}G_{j}\ .

(60)

Proof.

Since
H
is self-adjoint, (
56
) can be extended to

H=\sum_{j=0}^{n_{A}}X_{j}\otimes G_{j}=\sum_{j=0}^{n_{A}}X^{\dagger}_{j}\otimes G^{\dagger}_{j}\ .

(61)

Then
{\displaystyle\mathcal{K}\mathcal{V}R=-i\left(H(\pi_{A}\otimes R)-(\pi_{A}\otimes R)H\right)=-i\sum_{j=0}^{n_{A}}\left(X_{j}\pi_{A}\otimes G_{j}R-\pi_{A}X^{\dagger}_{j}\otimes RG^{\dagger}_{j}\right)}
, and
since
\mathcal{S}(Y_{0}\otimes\left(G_{0}R-RG_{0}^{\dagger}\right)=0
,

{\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\mathcal{K}(\pi_{A}\otimes R)=-i\sum_{j=1}^{n_{A}}\mathcal{S}\left(X_{j}\pi_{A}\otimes G_{j}R-\pi_{A}X^{\dagger}_{j}\otimes RG^{\dagger}_{j}\right)}
.
Using the expression for
\mathcal{S}
provided in Theorem
C.1
, namely

{\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{A}}|Y_{k}\rangle\langle\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}X_{k}|}
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(X_{j}\pi_{A}\otimes G_{j}R)=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{A}}\langle\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}X_{k},X_{j}\pi_{A}\rangle Y_{k}\otimes G_{j}R=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{A}}M_{k,j}Y_{k}\otimes G_{j}R\ .

Using the same expression for
\mathcal{S}
, but this time for the dual pair of bases
\{Y_{0}^{\dagger},\dots,Y_{n_{A}}^{\dagger}\}
and
\{X_{0}^{\dagger},\dots,X_{n_{A}}^{\dagger}\}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\pi_{A}X_{j}^{\dagger}\otimes RG_{j}^{\dagger})=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{A}}\langle\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}(X_{k}^{\dagger}),(X_{j}\pi_{A})^{\dagger}\rangle Y_{k}^{\dagger}\otimes RG_{j}^{\dagger}\ .

By cyclicity of the trace,

\langle e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}}X_{k}^{\dagger},(X_{j}\pi_{A})^{\dagger}\rangle=\textnormal{Tr}[(X_{j}\pi_{A})^{\dagger}e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}}(X_{k})]=\overline{\langle e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}}X_{k},X_{j}\pi_{A}\rangle}
. (For
z\in\mathbbm{C}
,
\bar{z}
denotes its complex conjugate.) Integrating in
t
,

{\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}(X_{k}^{\dagger}),(X_{j}\pi_{A})^{\dagger}\rangle=\overline{\langle\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}X_{k},X_{j}\pi_{A}\rangle}}
.
Altogether,

\mathcal{S}\mathcal{K}(\pi_{A}\otimes R)=i\sum_{j,k=1}^{n_{A}}\left(M_{k,j}Y_{k}\otimes G_{j}R-\overline{M_{k,j}}Y_{k}^{\dagger}\otimes RG_{j}^{\dagger}\right)\ .

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}\mathcal{S}\mathcal{K}(\pi_{A}\otimes R)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{n_{A}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n_{A}}M_{k,j}\left(X^{\dagger}_{\ell}Y_{k}\otimes G^{\dagger}_{\ell}G_{j}R-Y_{k}X^{\dagger}_{\ell}\otimes G_{j}RG^{\dagger}_{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{n_{A}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n_{A}}\overline{M_{k,j}}\left(X_{\ell}Y^{\dagger}_{k}\otimes G_{\ell}RG^{\dagger}_{j}-Y^{\dagger}_{k}X_{\ell}\otimes RG^{\dagger}_{j}G_{\ell}\right)\ .

Taking the partial trace over
\mathcal{H}_{A}
, which produces a factor of
\delta_{k,\ell}
in each term,

-\textnormal{Tr}_{A}[\mathcal{K}\mathcal{S}\mathcal{K}(\pi_{A}\otimes R)]=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n_{A}}M_{k,j}\left(G_{j}RG_{k}^{\dagger}-G^{\dagger}_{k}G_{j}R\right)+\sum_{j,k=1}^{n_{A}}\overline{M_{k,j}}\left(G_{k}RG^{\dagger}_{j}-RG^{\dagger}_{j}G_{k}\right)\ .

(62)

Note that

{\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=1}^{n_{A}}\overline{M_{k,j}}G_{k}RG^{\dagger}_{j}=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n_{A}}\overline{M_{j,k}}G_{k}RG^{\dagger}_{j}=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n_{A}}M_{k,j}^{\dagger}G_{k}RG^{\dagger}_{j}}
.
In the same way,
{\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=1}^{n_{A}}\overline{M_{k,j}}RG^{\dagger}_{j}G_{k}=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n_{A}}M_{k,j}^{\dagger}RG^{\dagger}_{k}G_{j}=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n_{A}}(A-iB)_{k,j}^{\dagger}RG^{\dagger}_{k}G_{j}}
.
Therefore, (
62
) becomes

-\textnormal{Tr}_{A}[\mathcal{K}\mathcal{S}\mathcal{K}(\pi_{A}\otimes R)]=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n_{A}}A_{k,j}\left(2G_{j}RG_{k}^{\dagger}-G^{\dagger}_{k}G_{j}R-RG^{\dagger}_{k}G_{j}\right)-i[H_{L},R]

(63)

where

{\displaystyle H_{L}:=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n_{A}}B_{j,k}G^{\dagger}_{k}G_{j}}
. Now assuming that
A
is positive semidefinite, it has a spectral decomposition of
the form (
58
) where
\mu_{\ell}>0
for
\ell=1,\dots,r
.
Substituting this into (
63
) yields (
59
).
∎

It remains to prove that the self-adjoint part of the matrix
M
defined in (
57
) is positive semidefinite. We use the Gelfand-Naimark-Segal (GNS) inner product associated to
\pi_{A}
, defined as follows:

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite dimensional Hilbert space, and let
\mu
ve a density matrix on
\mathcal{H}
. For operators
X
and
Y
on
\mathcal{H}
, their GNS inner product
\langle X,Y\rangle_{\mu}
is given by

\langle X,Y\rangle_{\mu}:=\textnormal{Tr}[X^{*}Y\mu]=\langle X\mu^{1/2},Y\mu^{1/2}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathcal{H}}}\ .

(64)

If
\mathcal{L}
is a Lindblad generator acting on
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
with invariant state
\pi
, it is
said to satisfy the
GNS detailed balance condition
with respect
to
\pi
if its Hilbert-Schmidt adjoint
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
is self-adjoint
with respect to the GNS inner product associated to
\pi
. In the
quantum setting, as in the classical, detailed balance corresponds to
microscopic reversibility
[
6
]
. In the
quantum setting, there are other way to weight the inner product, for
example
\langle\mu^{1/4}X\mu^{1/4},\mu^{1/4}Y\mu^{1/4}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathcal{H}}}
, which
defines the Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) inner product and another
notion of detailed balance
[
14
]
. The GNS notion of detailed balance is the
most restrictive
[
3
]
, and a theorem of Alicki
[
5
]
gives
the form of all Lindblad generators satisfying this condition, which is very helpful
[
11
]
when studying the rate of approach to stationarity.

It is easy to see that when
\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}
is
self-adjoint with respect to the GNS inner product induced by
\pi_{A}
,
which is the case in the example considered in
[
26
]
, the matrix

A
specified in Lemma
4.2
is
diagonal with non-negative entries and
H_{L}
in (
59
) will always be identically zero.
The following general inequality will be shown to yield the
positivity of
A
without any extra assumption on
\mathcal{D}_{A}
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{A}
be an ergodic and gapped Lindblad generator on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
with steady state
\pi_{A}
.
Define
\mathcal{P}_{A}
,
\mathcal{Q}_{A}
and
\mathcal{S}_{A}
in terms of
\mathcal{D}_{A}
as in (
37
), (
38
) and (
39

Then for all

\langle\mathcal{S}_{A}^{\dagger}Z,\mathcal{Q}_{A}^{\dagger}Z\rangle_{\pi_{A}}+\langle\mathcal{Q}_{A}^{\dagger}Z,\mathcal{S}_{A}^{\dagger}Z\rangle_{\pi_{A}}\leqslant 0\

where the inner products are taken in the GNS inner product associated to
\pi_{A}
, as in (
64
).

Proof.

Since
e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}}
completely positive and
e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}}\mathds{1}_{A}=\mathds{1}_{A}
, an inequality of Lieb and Ruskai
[
22
]
(a slightly more general form was proved by Choi
[

, Corollary 2.8]
, but we only need the original; see
[
9
, Section 5.6]
) says that for all operators
W
on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
,

\left(e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}}W\right)^{\dagger}\left(e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}}W\right)\leqslant e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}}(W^{\dagger}W)
.
Differentiating in
t
at
t=0
yields

\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}(W)^{\dagger}W+W^{\dagger}\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}(W)\leqslant\mathcal{D}_{A}(W^{\dagger}W)\ .

(65)

The inequality (
65
), derived in this manner, also appears in Lindblad’s work
[
23
, (3.1)]
on the generators of CPTP semigroups. Now apply (
65
) as follows:

Define
W:=\mathcal{S}_{A}^{\dagger}Z
, so that
\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}W=\mathcal{Q}_{A}^{\dagger}Z
. Then

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{S}_{A}^{\dagger}(Z),\mathcal{Q}_{A}^{\dagger}Z\rangle_{\pi_{A}}+\langle\mathcal{Q}_{A}^{\dagger}Z,\mathcal{S}_{A}^{\dagger}(Z)\rangle_{\pi_{A}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle W,\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}W\rangle_{\pi_{A}}+\langle\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}W,W\rangle_{\pi_{A}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textnormal{Tr}\left[\left(W^{\dagger}\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}(W)+\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}(W^{\dagger})W\right)\pi_{A}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\textnormal{Tr}\left[\left(\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}(W^{\dagger}W)\right)\pi_{A}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\pi_{A},\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}(W^{\dagger}W)\rangle=\langle\mathcal{D}_{A}(\pi_{A}),W^{\dagger}W\rangle=0\ ,

where the only inequality is (
65
).
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{A}
be gapped and ergodic, and let

\mathcal{D}_{P}=-\textnormal{Tr}_{A}\mathcal{K}\mathcal{S}\mathcal{K}\mathcal{V}

\mathcal{D}_{P}
is the Lindblad generator given by (

Proof.

By Lemma
4.2

for arbitrary

,

{\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=1}^{n_{A}}\overline{z_{k}}A_{k,j}z_{j}\geqslant 0}
.
By (
57

{\displaystyle A_{k,j}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\langle\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}X_{k},X_{j}\pi_{A}\rangle+\langle X_{j},\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}X_{k}\pi_{A}\rangle\right)}
.
Define
{\displaystyle Z:=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{A}}z_{j}X_{j}}
, and note that
Z=\mathcal{Q}^{\dagger}Z
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=1}^{n_{A}}\overline{z_{k}}A_{k,j}z_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n_{A}}\left(\left\langle z_{j}\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}X_{j},z_{k}X_{k}\pi_{A}\right\rangle_{\text{\tiny HS}}+\left\langle z_{j}X_{j},z_{k}\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}X_{k}\pi_{A}\right\rangle_{\text{\tiny HS}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\langle\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}(Z),\mathcal{Q}^{\dagger}Z\pi_{A}\right\rangle_{\text{\tiny HS}}+\left\langle\mathcal{Q}^{\dagger}Z,\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}(Z)\pi_{A}\right\rangle_{\text{\tiny HS}}\right)\ .

By Theorem
4.3
the left side of (

Theorem 5.2

\mathcal{Q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathcal{S}\mathcal{K}(\pi_{A}\otimes R(t))

\displaystyle n_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=

8

In this section, we make the additional assumption that
\mathcal{D}_{P}^{\sharp}

\bar{R}

Moreover,

As we have seen, for large enough

\bar{n}_{k}
,

, that has a unique solution. Moreover, we then show with this choice of the

) converges for sufficiently large

\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}
. This finally proves that
\bar{\rho}_{\gamma}
does indeed have the expansion in (

).

Inserting (
128

\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}\bar{\rho}_{\gamma}=0
and equating like powers of
\gamma
leads to the equations

\mathcal{D}\bar{n}_{0}=-\mathcal{K}(\pi\otimes\bar{R})

(129)

and for

\mathcal{D}\bar{n}_{k}=-\mathcal{K}(\bar{n}_{k-1})

(130)

Throughout this section,
\mathcal{X}
denotes the space of traceless operators on

,

denotes
{\rm ran}(\mathcal{K}_{P})
and

is skew adjoint, its kernel and range are orthogonal complements in

,
and evidently every operator in the range of

is traceless. That is,
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathcal{X}
. It follows that

is the orthogonal direct sum of

and

\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{B}
onto

is the orthogonal projection in
\mathcal{X}
onto
\mathcal{W}

It follows that

Hydrodynamic limits

An interesting perspective on the relation between the evolution equations involving the generators
\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}
,
\mathcal{K}_{P}
,
\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}
and
\mathcal{D}_{P}^{\sharp}
is provided by an analogy with the theory of hydrodynamic limits in classical kinetic theory. The Boltzmann
equation for the phase space density
f(v,x,t)
of a dilute gas has the form

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f(v,x,t)=-v\cdot\nabla_{x}f(v,x,t)+\gamma\mathcal{C}f(v,x,t)

(159)

where the first term on the right describes the effects of the free motion of molecules between collisions, while
\mathcal{C}
is the collision kernel, an operator describing the effects of binary collisions between molecules. The parameter
\gamma
is then the inverse of the
Knudsen number
which gives the ratio of the microscopic times scale (the mean time between collisions) and the macroscopic time scale (the time for a molecule to travel a macroscopic distance).

In the analogy with the boundary driven quantum systems discussed here, our starting point

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\rho(t)=-i[H,\rho(t)]+\gamma\mathcal{D}\rho(t)

(160)

corresponds to the Boltzmann equation. The space
\mathcal{H}_{A}
accounts for the degrees of freedom corresponding to the the velocity variables in (
159
), while

\mathcal{H}_{B}
accounts for the degrees of freedom corresponding to the spatial variables. The parameter
\gamma
corresponds to the inverse Knudsen number, and
\mathcal{D}
corresponds to the collision operator.

When the Knudsen number is small, and hence
\gamma
is large, this formal similarity between (
159
) and (
160
) is brought out more clearly by noting that the phase space densities densities
f(v,x)
satisfying
\mathcal{C}f(v,x)=0
are precisely the
local Maxwellian densities

{\displaystyle\frac{\rho(x)}{(2\pi T(x))^{3/2}}e^{-|v-u(x)|^{2}/2T(x)}}

where
\rho(x)
,
u(x)
and
T(x)
are the
hydrodynamic moments
of
f(v,x)
. The analog of
\mathcal{M}
as in (
4
) is then the space of all local Maxwellian densities, and when
\gamma
is large, one expects solutions of (
159
) to quickly become, and then remain, approximately locally Maxwellian. To describe the further evolution after this initial layer, one need only keep track of the evolution of the hydrodynamic moments, which correspond physically to the density, bulk velocity and temperature. Hydrodynamic equations for the evolution of these quantities, such as the Euler equations or the Navier-Stokes equations, had been derived before the Boltzmann equation, not starting from molecular dynamics, but in the framework of continuum mechanics.

The problem of deriving equations for the hydrodynamic moments from molecular dynamics was discussed by Hilbert in his follow-up
[
18
]
to the famous list of problems that he proposed at the International Congress of Mathematicians in 1900. His original Sixth Problem was, briefly put, to axiomatize mechanics and probability. In
[
18
]
he expounded more, and highlighted the specific problem of deriving the hydrodynamic equations of continuous mechanics from the kinetic equations of Maxwell and Boltzmann, and deducing expression for, e.g., the viscosity, in terms of the microscopic interaction of molecules. Later, he took up this problem himself and proposed an expansion method
[
19
]
that yields a hierarchy of systems of equations for the hydrodynamic moments. The expansion leading to this hierarchy is known as the Hilbert expansion.

The first system of equations in the hierarchy is the Euler equations, and it does not contain
\gamma
. The Euler equations are
formally
reversible and conservative, and their analog here is the leading order coherent approximation

{\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}R(t)=\mathcal{K}_{P}R(t)}
.

The remaining systems of equation in the hierarchy do contain
\gamma
. The first of these corresponds to the equation

{\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}R(t)=\mathcal{L}_{P,\gamma}R(t)}
. This next term in Hilbert’s hierarchy is not the Navier-Stokes equations themselves, but rather a precursor to them. The
Navier-Stokes equations do not contain the Knudsen number,
1/\gamma
. To obtain the Navier-Stokes equations, one rescales space and time depending on
\gamma
so that all terms in
the precursor equations are multiplied by a common power of
\gamma
that can then be cancelled out, leaving a negative power of
\gamma
in the remainder terms. One then takes
\gamma\to\infty
. For a more complete discussion, see
[
7
,
8
]
.

In our quantum setting, one can rescale time, but there is no really direct analog of spatial scaling. To cancel
\gamma
in this setting, we must rescale time and make a suitable unitary transformation which,
as in
[
13
]
, is the passage to the interaction picture. This leads to
{\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}R(t)=\mathcal{D}_{P}^{\sharp}R(t)}
. This equation, not involving
\gamma
, may be regarded as the counterpart of the
Navier-Stokes equations in this analogy.

It is unclear whether some analogous procedure, possibly involving a restriction on the initial data, may be used to extract an analog of the Burnett equations from the next equation in our quantum hierarchy, (
16
).
However, given that (
16
) may not describe a CPTP evolution, and given the problematic nature of the Burnett equations, there may be little point in pursuing this.

Appendix A
Bounding
\|e^{t\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{Q}\|_{1\to 1}
in terms of
\|e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}}\mathcal{Q}_{A}\|_{1\to 1}

A generalization of the super-operator trace norm plays an important role in quantum information theory because of its connections with quantum entropy.
For
1\leqslant p<\infty
the Schatten
p
-norm on
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
is defined by
\|X\|_{p}=\left(\textnormal{Tr}[(X^{*}X)^{p/2}]\right)^{1/p}
.
For
p=2
this is simply the Hilbert space norm associated to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product. For
p=1
, this is the trace norm. For all
X
,
\|X\|_{p}
is
monotone non-increasing, and
\lim_{p\to\infty}\|X\|_{p}=\|X\|_{\infty}
where
\|X\|_{\infty}
is
the operator norm of
X
, which is equal to its largest singular value of
X
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite dimensional Hilbert space, and let
\mathcal{T}
be a linear operator on
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
For
1\leqslant p\leqslant\infty
. Recall the definition (
34
) of the super-operator trace norm:

\|\mathcal{T}\|_{1\to p}=\sup\{\|\mathcal{T}(X)\|_{p}\ :\|X\|_{1}=1\ \}\ .

Now let
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
be two finite dimensional Hilbert spaces,
and let
\mathcal{T}_{A}
and
\mathcal{T}_{B}
be super-operators on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
respectively.
For five years it was an open conjecture
[
4
]
that if both

\mathcal{T}_{A}
and
\mathcal{T}_{B}
are CPTP, then for
1\leqslant p\leqslant\infty
,

\|\mathcal{T}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{T}_{B}\|_{1\to p}=\|\mathcal{T}_{A}\|_{1\to p}\|\mathcal{T}_{B}\|_{1\to p}
. It was shown

[
32
]
that this conjecture,
at least for
p
sufficiently close to
1
, would have a wide range
of applications. While the conjecture was eventually
proved to be false
for all
p>1
in
[
17
]
, there is a positive result
in the orignal paper
[
4
]
, where it is proved
that if
\mathcal{T}_{B}=\mathds{1}_{B}
, multiplicitivity does hold for
\mathcal{T}_{A}

CPTP:

\|\mathcal{T}_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}\|_{1\to p}=\|\mathcal{T}_{A}\|_{1\to p}\ .

(161)

We need a result of a similar nature. Let
\mathcal{D}_{A}
be a Lindblad generator on
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{A}
, and let
\mathcal{D}:=\mathcal{D}_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}
, as in the introduction. Let
\mathcal{Q}_{A}
and
\mathcal{Q}
be the projections onto the ranges of
\mathcal{D}_{A}
and
\mathcal{D}
respectively defined in (
38
) and (
42
). We need to know that when
\|e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}}\mathcal{Q}_{A}\|_{1\to 1}
is small, then so is

\|e^{t\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{Q}_{A}\|_{1\to 1}=\|e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}}\mathcal{Q}_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}\|_{1\to 1}\ .

While
e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}}
is CPTP,
e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}}\mathcal{Q}_{A}
is not; it is instead the diference between two CPTP maps,
e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}}
and

e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}}\mathcal{P}_{A}
. Thus we
cannot
apply the bound (
161
)
to conclude that
\|e^{t\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{Q}\|_{1\to 1}=\|e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}}\mathcal{Q}_{A}\|_{1\to 1}
.

The following lemma will however allow us to transfer a certain bound on
\|e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}}\mathcal{Q}_{A}\|_{1\to 1}
directly to
\|e^{t\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{Q}\|_{1\to 1}
, thus bounding the latter that is independent of the dimension of
\mathcal{H}_{B}
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}
be finite dimensional Hilbert spaces. Let
X,Y\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
, and define
|Y\rangle\langle X|
as in (
33
). Then the super-operator
|Y\rangle\langle X|\otimes\mathds{1}
on
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{K}
satisfies

\||Y\rangle\langle X|\otimes\mathds{1}\|_{1\to 1}=\||Y\rangle\langle X|\|_{1\to 1}=\|Y\|_{1}\|X\|_{\infty}\ .

Proof.

Let
Z
be any operator on
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{K}
with
\|Z\|_{1}=1
. Then
(|Y\rangle\langle X|\otimes\mathds{1})Z=Y\otimes\textnormal{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}}[(X^{\dagger}\otimes\mathds{1})Z]
,
and hence
\|(|Y\rangle\langle X|\otimes\mathds{1})Z\|_{1}=\|Y\|_{1}\|\textnormal{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}}[(X^{\dagger}\otimes\mathds{1})Z]\|_{1}\leqslant\|Y\|_{1}\|\|(X^{\dagger}\otimes\mathds{1})Z\|_{1}
since the partial trace is a CPTP map and hence a trace norm contraction. By Hölder’s inequality for operators,
\|(X^{\dagger}\otimes\mathds{1})Z\|_{1}\leqslant\|X^{\dagger}\otimes\mathds{1}\|_{\infty}\|Z\|_{1}
, and
\|X^{\dagger}\otimes\mathds{1}\|_{\infty}=\|X\|_{\infty}
. Altogether,

\|(|Y\rangle\langle X|\otimes\mathds{1})Z\|_{1}\leqslant\|Y\|_{1}\|X\|_{\infty}=\||Y\rangle\langle X|\|_{1\to 1}\ .

This proves that
\||Y\rangle\langle X|\otimes\mathds{1}\|_{1\to 1}\leqslant\||Y\rangle\langle X|\|_{1\to 1}
and the opposite inequality is trivial.
∎

Appendix B
Dual bases and Jordan bases

In this section
\mathcal{H}
is any Hilbert space of finite dimension
n
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. We begin with some elementary facts about dual Jordan bases for which we do not know a convenient reference.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
\{{\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{n}\}
and
\{{\bf y}_{1},\dots,{\bf y}_{n}\}
be two sets of vectors in
\mathcal{H}
such that for all
j,k
,

\langle{\bf x}_{j},{\bf y}_{k}\rangle=\delta_{j,k}\ .

(162)

Then both
\{{\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{n}\}
and
\{{\bf y}_{1},\dots,{\bf y}_{n}\}
are linearly independent, and hence are bases, not necessarily orthogonal, of
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

Suppose that

{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}{\bf x}_{j}=0}
. Then
0=\left\langle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}{\bf x}_{j},{\bf y}_{k}\right\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\overline{\beta_{j}}\delta_{j,k}=\overline{\beta_{k}}
. Since this is true for each
k
,

\{{\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{n}\}
is linearly independent. Since the dimension of
\mathcal{H}
is
n
,
\{{\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{n}\}
spans
\mathcal{H}
, and hence is a basis for
\mathcal{H}
. By symmetry, the same is true of
\{{\bf y}_{1},\dots,{\bf y}_{n}\}
.
∎

Lemma B.2
.

Let
\{{\bf y}_{1},\dots,{\bf y}_{n}\}
be any basis of
\mathcal{H}
, not necessarily orthonormal. Then there exists a uniquely determined set of vectors
\{{\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{n}\}
such that (
162
) is satisfied.

Proof.

Every
{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\in\mathcal{H}

has an expansion

{\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\psi}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}{\bf y}_{j}}
with uniquely determined coefficients. For each
j=1,\dots,n
, define a linear functional
\Lambda_{j}
on
\mathcal{H}

by

\displaystyle{\Lambda_{j}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}{\bf y}_{j}\right)=\alpha_{j}}
.
By the Riesz Representation Theorem, there is a uniquely determined
{\bf x}_{j}\in\mathcal{H}
such that

\langle{\bf x}_{j},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\rangle=\Lambda_{j}({\boldsymbol{\psi}})\ .

(163)

Taking
{\boldsymbol{\psi}}={\bf y}_{k}
yields (
162
). Conversely, given any set
\{{\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{n}\}
such that
(
162
) is satisfied for all
j,k
, then (
163
) is satisfied where
\Lambda_{j}
is given by (
163
). Hence the uniqueness of
\{{\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{n}\}
follows from the uniqueness in the Reisz Representation Theorem.
∎

Definition B.3
.

Let
\{{\bf y}_{1},\dots,{\bf y}_{n}\}
be any basis of
\mathcal{H}
. Then the unique basis
\{{\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{n}\}
of
\mathcal{H}
such that (
162
) is satisfied for all
j,k
is the
dual basis to
\{{\bf y}_{1},\dots,{\bf y}_{n}\}

It is clear that
\{{\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{n}\}
is the dual basis to
\{{\bf y}_{1},\dots,{\bf y}_{n}\}
if and only if
\{{\bf y}_{1},\dots,{\bf y}_{n}\}
is the dual basis to
\{{\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{n}\}
.

Let
A
be any operator on
\mathcal{H}
. Then
A
may or may not be diagonalizable, but there always exists a direct sum decomposition of
\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{\ell=1}^{m}\mathcal{V}_{\ell}
into subspaces (not in general mutually orthogonal) that are invariant under
A
, with each
\mathcal{V}_{\ell}
contained in a generalized eigenspace of
A
for some eigenvalue
\lambda_{\ell}
of
A
, and finally such that within
\mathcal{V}_{\ell}
the eigenspace corresponding to
\lambda_{\ell}
is one dimensional. Each of these spaces
\mathcal{V}_{\ell}
corresponds to a block in a Jordan normal form matrix representation of
A
. There are two of these normal forms, upper and lower, which we need to distinguish. Let
d_{\ell}
denote the dimension of
\mathcal{V}_{\ell}
. A basis
\{{\bf y}_{1},\dots,{\bf y}_{d_{\ell}}\}
for
\mathcal{V}_{\ell}
is an
upper Jordan block basis
in case

A{\bf y}_{1}=\lambda_{\ell}{\bf y}_{1}\quad{\rm and\ for}\quad j=2,\dots,d_{\ell}\ ,A{\bf y}_{j}=\lambda_{\ell}{\bf y}_{j}+{\bf y}_{j-1}\ .

For
d_{\ell}=3
, the matrix representing the action of
A
on
\mathcal{V}_{\ell}
in an upper Jordan block basis is

\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\lambda&1&0\\
0&\lambda&1\\
0&0&\lambda\end{array}\right]\ .

A basis
\{{\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{d_{\ell}}\}
for
\mathcal{V}_{\ell}
is a
lower Jordan block basis
in case

A{\bf x}_{d_{\ell}}=\lambda_{\ell}{\bf x}_{d_{\ell}}\quad{\rm and\ for}\quad j=1,\dots,d_{\ell}-1\ ,A{\bf x}_{j}=\lambda_{\ell}{\bf x}_{j}+{\bf x}_{j+1}\ .

For
d_{\ell}=3
, the matrix representing the action of
A
on
\mathcal{V}_{\ell}
in a lower Jordan block basis is

\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\lambda&0&0\\
1&\lambda&0\\
0&1&\lambda\end{array}\right]\ .

The distinction between upper an lower Jordan block bases is minor; reversing the order of the basis elements changes from on to the other. However, the distinction maters when considering dual bases. Concatenating bases for each
\mathcal{V}_{\ell}
yields a basis for
\mathcal{H}
. A basis for
\mathcal{H}
is an
upper Jordan basis
in case it is the concatenation of upper Jordan block bases for each
\mathcal{V}_{\ell}
, and is a

lower Jordan basis
in case it is the concatenation of lower Jordan block bases for each
\mathcal{V}_{\ell}
.

Theorem B.4
.

Let
\{{\bf y}_{1},\dots,{\bf y}_{n}\}
be an upper Jordan basis for an operator
A
on
\mathcal{H}
. Then the dual basis
\{{\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{n}\}
is a lower Jordan basis for
A^{\dagger}
. Furthermore, if for some
j
,
A{\bf y}_{j}=\lambda{\bf y}_{j}
, and
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
A
of algebraic multiplicity one, then
A^{\dagger}{\bf x}_{j}=\overline{\lambda}{\bf x}_{j}
.

Proof.

Consider first the case
m=1
so that there is only one Jordan block. Then
A
has only one eigenvalue
\lambda
and for each
1\leqslant j,k\leqslant n
,

\langle{\bf x}_{k},A{\bf y}_{j}\rangle=\lambda\delta_{j,k}+\delta_{j-1,k}
. Therefore,

\langle{\bf y}_{j},A^{\dagger}{\bf x}_{k}\rangle=\overline{\langle{\bf x}_{k},A{\bf y}_{j}\rangle}=\overline{\lambda}\delta_{j,k}+\delta_{j-1,k}=\overline{\lambda}\delta_{j,k}+\delta_{j,k+1}\

so that

A^{\dagger}{\bf x}_{n}=\overline{\lambda}{\bf x}_{n}
and for
k=1,\dots,n-1
,
A^{\dagger}{\bf x}_{k}=\overline{\lambda}{\bf x}_{k}+{\bf x}_{k+1}
. The general case follows easily by concatenation.

For the final part,
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
A
of algebraic multiplicity one if and only if the generalized eigenspace of
A
for
\lambda
coincides with the eigenspace for
\lambda
, and the latter is one dimensional.
Therefore,
A{\bf y}_{j}=\lambda{\bf y}_{j}
where
\lambda
has algebraic multiplicity one if and only if
\{{\bf y}_{j}\}
is an upper and lower Jordan block basis for the single Jordan invariant subspace of
A
with eigenvalue
\lambda
. It then
follows from the first part that
\{{\bf x}_{j}\}
a Jordan upper and lower basis for the single Jordan invariant subspace of
A^{\dagger}
with eigenvalue
\overline{\lambda}
. In particular,
A^{\dagger}{\bf x}_{j}=\overline{\lambda}{\bf x}_{j}
.
∎

Appendix C
Generalized inverses of Lindblad generators

We apply the results of the previous subsection to the Hilbert space
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{A}
consisting of operators on
\mathcal{H}_{A}

n_{A}+1
denote the dimension of
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{A}

Let
\mathcal{D}_{A}

\mathcal{H}_{A}

\mathcal{D}_{A}

\pi_{A}

\{Y_{0},\dots,Y_{n_{A}}\}

\mathcal{H}
for
\mathcal{D}_{A}

,
\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}X_{0}=0
. Since
\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}

\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}\mathds{1}_{A}=0
,
and hence
\mathds{1}_{A}
spans the eigenspace of
\mathcal{D}_{A}

a>0

\lambda

\Re(\lambda)\leqslant-a

e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}}=\sum_{j=0}^{n_{A}}e^{t\lambda_{j}}|Y_{j}\rangle\langle X_{j}|\ ,

(164)

and hence

\mathcal{P}_{A}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}}=|Y_{0}\rangle\langle X_{0}|=|\pi_{A}\rangle\langle\mathds{1}_{A}|\ .

(165)

\mathcal{D}_{A}

.

Since
\mathcal{P}_{A}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{A}
are complementary projections, so are
\mathcal{P}_{A}^{\dagger}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{A}^{\dagger}
.
Evidently
\mathcal{P}_{A}^{\dagger}=|\mathds{1}_{A}\rangle\langle\pi_{A}|

.

\mathcal{P}_{A}

\mathcal{Q}_{A}

\mathcal{D}_{A}

\mathcal{P}_{A}^{\dagger}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{A}^{\dagger}

\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\dagger}
.

An operator

on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
belongs to

\mathcal{Q}_{A}

\|e^{t\mathcal{D}_{A}}\rho_{0}-\pi_{A}\|_{1}\leqslant
for all choices of
\rho_{0}
and all
t
, if
C
is large, the bound (
173
) will be trivial for
t\leqslant\frac{2}{a}\log(2C)
.

Appendix D
Volterra integral operators and the comparison of evolution equations

In what follows, we wish to compare various evolution mechanisms for density matrices and for super-operators. A powerful method for doing this involves writing the equations governing the evolution mechanisms as
Volterra integral equations. The elementary Theorem
D.1
stated below will be applied a number of times. We give the short proof for convenience.

In this section
\mathcal{X}
denote an arbitrary Banach space with norm
\|\cdot\|
. In the application made here his will always be the space of operators on a finite dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}

equipped with the trace norm
\|\cdot\|_{1}
, or else the space of super-operators on
\mathcal{H}
equipped with the super-operator trace norm
\|\cdot\|_{1\to 1}
.

Let
\mathcal{T}>0
be given and define

\mathscr{C}
to be the space of continuous functions
A:[0,T]\to\mathcal{X}
equipped with the norm

{\displaystyle|\!|\!|A|\!|\!|:=\sup_{\tau\in[0,T]}\|A(\tau)\|}
.
For an operator
\mathscr{H}
on
\mathscr{C}
, define

\|\mathscr{H}\|_{\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{C}}=\sup\{|\!|\!|\mathscr{H}A|\!|\!|\ :\ |\!|\!|A|\!|\!|\leqslant 1\}\ .

(174)

The operator
\mathscr{H}
is a
Volterra integral operator
on
\mathscr{C}
in case it has the form

\mathscr{H}Y(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathcal{G}(\tau,\sigma)Y(\sigma){\rm d}\sigma

(175)

where
\mathcal{G}(\tau,\sigma)
is a continuous function of
0\leqslant\sigma\leqslant\tau\leqslant T
with values in the space of operators on
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
.
Define
\mathcal{G}(\tau,\sigma)\|_{\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}}:=\sup\{\|\mathcal{G}(\tau,\sigma)\|\ :\ \|X\|\leqslant 1\}
and then

G:=\sup_{0\leqslant\sigma\leqslant\tau\leqslant T}\|\mathcal{G}(\tau,\sigma)\|_{\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}}\ ,

(176)

and then
\|\mathscr{H}\|_{\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{C}}\leqslant TG
.

We will work with perturbations of Volterra integral operators.
Let let
\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}
be another bounded Volterra integral operator defined in terms of
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\tau,\sigma)
as in
(
175
). Then
\mathscr{H}-\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}
is a Volterra integral operator and

{\displaystyle(\mathscr{H}-\widetilde{\mathscr{H}})Y(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\tau}(\mathcal{G}(\tau,\sigma)-\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\tau,\sigma))Y(\sigma){\rm d}\sigma}
.
Therefore,

\|\mathscr{H}-\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}\|_{\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{C}}\leqslant\sup_{0\leqslant\tau\leqslant T}\left\{\ \int_{0}^{\tau}\|\mathcal{G}(\tau,\sigma)-\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\tau,\sigma)\|_{\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}}{\rm d}\sigma\ \right\}\ .

(177)

For given
Z\in\mathscr{C}
and Volterra integral operator
\mathscr{H}
, the equation

Y(\tau)=\mathscr{H}Y(\tau)+Z(\tau)\ .

(178)

is a Volterra integral equation of the second kind, and it may be solved by iteration. The following theorem collects the elementary results we need.

Theorem D.1
.

For all bounded Volterra integral operators
\mathscr{H}
on
\mathscr{C}
, and all
Z\in\mathscr{C}
, the equation (
178
) has a unique solution
Y\in\mathscr{C}
which is given by

\displaystyle{Y(\tau):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathscr{H}^{n}Z(\tau)}

where the sum converges in norm in
\mathscr{C}
. Moreover, let
\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}
be another bounded Volterra integral operator defined in terms of
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\tau,\sigma)
as in
(
175
). Let
C:=\max\{G,\widetilde{G}\}
where
G
and
\widetilde{G}
are given by (
176
). Let
\widetilde{Z}
be an element of
\mathscr{C}
,
and define
\widetilde{Y}
to be the solution of
(
178
) with
\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}
in place of
\mathscr{H}
, and
\widetilde{Z}
in place of
Z
. Then

\|Y-\widetilde{Y}\|_{\mathscr{C}}\leqslant\frac{1}{TC}(e^{2TC}-1)\|\mathscr{H}-\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}\|_{\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{C}}\min\{\|Z\|_{\mathscr{C}},\|\widetilde{Z}\|_{\mathscr{C}}\}+e^{2TC}\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\mathscr{C}}\ .

(179)

Proof.

Iteration of (
178
) yields, for each
N\in\mathbbm{N}
,

Y(\tau)=\mathscr{H}^{N+1}Y(\tau)+\sum_{n=0}^{N}\mathscr{H}^{n}Z(\tau)

(180)

Note that

{\displaystyle\mathscr{H}^{2}Y(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{0}^{\sigma_{2}}\mathcal{G}(\tau,\sigma_{2})\mathcal{G}(\sigma_{2},\sigma_{1})Y(\sigma_{1}){\rm d}\sigma_{2}{\rm d}\sigma_{1}}
,
and hence

{\displaystyle\|\mathscr{H}^{2}\|_{\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{C}}\leqslant\frac{T^{2}}{2}G^{2}}
. Likewise, one finds that for all
n\in\mathbbm{N}
,
\|\mathscr{H}^{n}\|_{\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{C}}\leqslant\frac{T^{n}}{n!}G^{n}
. Likewise,

\|\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}^{n}\|_{\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{C}}\leqslant\frac{T^{n}}{n!}\widetilde{G}^{n}
. Therefore,

\max\{\ \|\mathscr{H}^{n}\|_{\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{C}}\ ,\ \|\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}^{n}\|_{\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{C}}\ \}\leqslant\frac{T^{n}}{n!}C^{n}\ ,

and
{\displaystyle Y(\tau):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathscr{H}^{n}Z(\tau)}

is well-defined since the sum converges
\mathscr{C}
. One readily checks that it satisfies (
178
), and by (
180
) it is the unique solution.
For the second part,

Y(\tau)-\widetilde{Y}(\tau)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(\mathscr{H}^{n}-\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}^{n})Z(\tau)+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}^{n}(Z(\tau)-\widetilde{Z}(\tau))\ .

By the telescoping sum formula,

{\displaystyle(\mathscr{H}^{(\gamma)})^{n}-(\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}^{(\gamma)})^{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(\mathscr{H}^{(\gamma)})^{n-1-j}(\mathscr{H}^{(\gamma)}-\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}^{(\gamma)})(\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}^{(\gamma)})^{j}}

and therefore

\displaystyle\|(\mathscr{H}^{(\gamma)})^{n}-(\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}^{(\gamma)})^{n}\|_{\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{C}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\|\mathscr{H}^{(\gamma)}\|_{\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{C}}^{n-1-j}\|\mathscr{H}^{(\gamma)}-\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}^{(\gamma)}\|_{\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{C}}\|\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}^{(\gamma)}\|^{j}_{\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{C}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{j!(n-j)!}\left(TC\right)^{n-1}\|\mathscr{H}^{(\gamma)}-\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}^{(\gamma)}\|_{\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{C}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{2^{n}}{n!}\left(TC\right)^{n-1}\|\mathscr{H}^{(\gamma)}-\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}^{(\gamma)}\|_{\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{C}}\ .

Therefore,

\|Y-\widetilde{Y}\|_{\mathscr{C}}\leqslant\frac{1}{TC}(e^{2TC}-1)\|\mathscr{H}^{(\gamma)}-\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}^{(\gamma)}\|_{\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{C}}\|Z\|_{\mathscr{C}}\|\widetilde{Z}+e^{2TC}\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{\mathscr{C}}\ .

By the symmetry in
Y
and
\widetilde{Y}
, we have the same bound with
Z
and
\widetilde{Z}
swapped, and this proves (
179
) .
∎

Appendix E
Mixing times

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a Lindblad generator acting on
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
,
\mathcal{H}
a finite dimensional Hilbert space. By by (
173
), if
\mathcal{L}
is ergodic and gapped with unique steady state
\pi
,
t_{{\rm mix}}(\mathcal{L})<\infty
. The next lemma provides a converse.

Lemma E.1
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a Lindblad generator acting on
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
. Assume only that
t_{{\rm mix}}(\mathcal{L})<\infty
.
Then
\mathcal{L}
has a unique steady state
\pi
, and
{\rm ker}(\mathcal{L})
is spanned by
\pi
. Moreover,

\mathcal{L}
has no purely imaginary eigenvalues so that in our finite dimensional setting
\mathcal{L}
is ergodic and gapped. In particular, if
\mathcal{L}\rho-0
and
\textnormal{Tr}[\rho]=1
, then
\rho=\pi
.

Proof.

Every Lindblad generator acting on
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
has at least one steady state, and since
t_{{\rm mix}}(\mathcal{L})<\infty
, there is a unique steady state
\pi
.
Since for all
X
,
\mathcal{L}(X^{\dagger})=\mathcal{L}(X)^{\dagger}
, if
X\in{\rm ker}(\mathcal{L})
then
X^{\dagger}\in{\rm ker}(\mathcal{L})
. Hence if
\pi
does not span
{\rm ker}(\mathcal{L})
there would be a self-adjoint
X\in{\rm ker}(\mathcal{L})

that is linearly independent of
\pi
. Subtracting an appropriate multiple of
\pi
from
X
and normalizing, we may assume that
\textnormal{Tr}[X]=0
but
\|X\|_{1}=2
. Let
X=X_{+}-X_{-}
be the spectral decomposition of

X
into its positive and negative parts. Then
X_{+}
and
X_{-}
are both density matrices. Since
\mathcal{L}X=0
, for all
t>0
,

X=e^{t\mathcal{L}}X=e^{t\mathcal{L}}X_{+}-e^{t\mathcal{L}}X_{-}\ .

Therefore,
2=\|X\|_{1}=2d_{{\rm TV}}(e^{t\mathcal{L}}X_{+},e^{t\mathcal{L}}X_{-})
, but for
t>t_{{\rm mix}}(\mathcal{L})
,
d_{{\rm TV}}(e^{t\mathcal{L}}X_{+},e^{t\mathcal{L}}X_{-})<\tfrac{1}{4}
. This contradiction proves that

\pi
span
{\rm ker}(\mathcal{L})
.

Likewise, suppose that
X\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
satisfies
\mathcal{L}X=i\omega X
for some non-zero real
\omega
and that
X\neq 0
. Since
\mathcal{L}(X^{\dagger})=\mathcal{L}(X)^{\dagger}
,
\mathcal{L}X^{\dagger}=-i\omega X^{\dagger}
.
Since
\textnormal{Tr}[\mathcal{L}X]=0
,
\textnormal{Tr}[X]=0
.
Define
A=\frac{1}{2}(X+X^{\dagger})
, and note that
A\neq 0
since
\omega\neq 0
, but
\textnormal{Tr}[A]=0
. Let
t:=2\pi\omega
. Then
e^{t\mathcal{L}A}=A
. Rescaling
A
, we may assume
\|A\|_{1}=2
. Then

2=\|A\|_{1}=\|e^{kt\mathcal{L}}A\|_{1}
and exactly as in
the first part of the proof,
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|e^{kt\mathcal{L}}A\|_{1}=0
. This contradiction precludes the existence of any purely imaginary eigenvalues.
∎

We next show that when
t_{{\rm mix}}(\mathcal{L})<\infty
, so that there is a unique stationary state
\pi
,
d_{{\rm TV}}(e^{kt_{{\rm mix}}(\mathcal{L})})
decreases to zero exponentially fast in
k
.
For
t>0
, let
P_{t}:=e^{t\mathcal{L}}
. In preparation for this, define the super-operator
\Pi
by
\Pi X:=\textnormal{Tr}[X]\pi
. Then
\Pi
is a CPTP map. It easy to write down an explicit Kraus representation showing this, but also

\Pi=\lim_{t\to\infty}P_{t}
, and each
P_{t}
is CPTP. A super-operator
\mathcal{T}
is said to be
Hermitian
in case
\mathcal{T}(X)^{\dagger}=\mathcal{T}(X^{\dagger})
for all

X\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
. Since all CPTP operations are Hermitian (see e.g.
[
9
, Lemma 5.2]
), and hence
P_{t}-\Pi
is Hermitian.

In this appendix, let
\mathcal{X}
denote the space of
self-adjoint
operators on
\mathcal{H}
equipped with the trace norm, and let
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
denote the space of
Hermitian

super-operators on
\mathcal{H}
equipped with the norm

|\!|\!|\mathcal{T}|\!|\!|:=\sup\{\|\mathcal{T}(X)\|_{1}\ :\ \|X\|_{1}=1\quad{\rm and}\quad X=X^{\dagger}\}\ .

(181)

This is closely related to the super-operator trace norm
\|\cdot\|_{1\to 1}
, but may be smaller because of the restriction that
X
must be self-adjoint.

Lemma E.2
.

For all
t>0
,

|\!|\!|P_{t}-\Pi|\!|\!|=\sup\{\|P_{t}\rho-\pi\|_{1}\ :\ \rho\ {\rm a\ density\ matrix\ on}\ \mathcal{H}\}\ .

(182)

Proof.

For any density matrix
\rho
, since
\|P_{t}\rho-\pi\|_{1}=\|(P_{i}-\Pi)\rho\|_{1}
and since
\rho=\rho^{\dagger}
and
\|\rho\|_{1}=1
,
\|(P_{t}-\Pi)\rho\|_{1}\leqslant|\!|\!|P_{t}-\Pi|\!|\!|
. This proves that the left side of

Next, in our finite dimensional setting the supremum in (

be such that

,

and

|\!|\!|P_{t}-\Pi|\!|\!|=\|(P_{t}-\Pi)X\|_{1}

be the decomposition of

This proves that the right side of
(

∎

Let

Proof.

Let

For each
