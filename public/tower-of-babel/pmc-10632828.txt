Child eye health in Ethiopia: a mixed methods analysis of policy and commitment to action
Authors: Sadik Taju Sherief, Samson Tesfaye, Zelalem Eshetu, Asim Ali, Helen Dimaras
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChild eye health is a serious public health issue in Ethiopia, where children under the age of 15 account for over half of the population. Our aim was to review Ethiopian health policy and practice to reveal approaches and commitment to promotion and delivery of child eye health services.MethodsWe conducted a mixed-methods situational analysis employing documentary analysis and key informant interview methods. Government publications touching on any element of child eye health were included. Key informants were eligible if they were leaders, authorities, researchers or clinicians involved in child health. Data was combined and analysed by narrative synthesis, using an adaptation of the Eye Care Situation Analysis Tool as a framework.FindingsEleven documents developed by the Ministries of Health and Education were included and interviews with 14 key informants were conducted. A focus on child eye health was lacking in key health policy documents, demonstrated by limited allocation of funds, a shortage of human resources, and a subpar referral system across all levels of child eye care.ConclusionThe study identified several gaps and limitations in child eye health in Ethiopia. There is a need for health policies that strengthen ownership, finance and partnerships for improved coordination, and collaboration with line ministries and other stakeholders to improve child eye health services in Ethiopia.Keywords: Paediatric ophthalmology, Community child health, Health policy

Introduction

Eye health is an important component of children’s overall well-being.1 Childhood blindness is a significant contributor to the overall global economic burden of blindness and disability-adjusted life years. The projected cost of global blindness by the year 2020 was US$110 billion; childhood blindness contributes to one-third of the global economic costs associated with blindness.2 In 2020, the estimated percentage of gross domestic product loss in sub-Saharan Africa due to childhood blindness was 0.5%.2 The control of blindness in children is closely linked to child survival.3 In low-and-middle income countries (LMICs), up to 60% of children die within a year of going blind—usually due to underlying conditions such as measles, meningitis, rubella, prematurity, genetic diseases and head injuries.4 5 The prevalence of childhood blindness varies according to the socioeconomic development of the country and the mortality rate of those under 5 years of age.3 6 Estimates show that there are 19 million children who are visually impaired globally, of which 1.26 million children are blind.1 7 Almost 75% of these children live in LMICs,8 yet there are limited data on prevalence and causes of childhood blindness in LMICs. In 2010, the prevalence of child blindness in sub-Saharan African countries was estimated to be 1.5 per 1000 children, based on an extrapolation model using under-5 mortality figures.3 8

Ethiopia is the second-most populous country in Africa with a population of ~105 000 000. A 20-year health sector development strategy governs Ethiopia’s public health system and is implemented through successive 5-year health sector development programmes.9 Ethiopia’s health service is structured into a three-tier system consisting of primary, secondary and tertiary levels of care. The primary care level includes hospitals, health centres and health posts. Health posts are the lowest primary staffed, with two women health extension workers focusing on health promotion, whereas the tertiary levels are referral hospitals with advanced specialty services.10 Despite its low human development index ranking of 173/184 countries, Ethiopia has made remarkable progress in achieving many of its national health and social targets.9 These include significant progress in quadrupling primary school enrolment rates and the implementation of a National Child Survival Strategy (2005–2015), which reduced child mortality from 48.2 deaths per 1000 live births in 2000 to 27.6 deaths per 1000 live births in 2019.9 11

Nevertheless, the eye health situation in Ethiopia is one of the most critical in the world. The First National Survey on Blindness, Low Vision and Trachoma (2005/2006) reported a prevalence rate of 1.6% for blindness and 3.7% for low vision.12 Estimates from the International Agency for the Prevention of Blindness showed that in 2020, Ethiopia (780 000) had more blind people than Kenya (290 000), Sudan (210 000), South Sudan (91 000), Somalia (82 000) and Eritrea combined (27 000).13 The prevalence of childhood blindness in Ethiopia is 0.1% and accounts for over 6% of the total blindness burden.12

In 1991 the Ethiopian government signed the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child, which states that children have the right ‘to the enjoyment of the highest attainable standard of health and to the facilities for the treatment of illness and rehabilitation of health’.14 In 2002, the Ethiopian government signed the VISION 2020 Global Declaration and launched its eye health programme.15 In this initiative, 10 ‘key activities for healthy eyes in children’ were also recommended for integration into primary maternal and child health services and inclusion in national prevention of blindness plans, underscoring the close relationship between eye health and overall child health and well-being.16 In addition to embracing a number of international initiatives like these, Ethiopia’s Ministry of Health (MOH) has also developed its own national policies focused on the improvement of children’s health. However, it is not clear how comprehensively child eye health (CEH) is addressed by these government policies.

We aimed to assess the government’s approaches and commitment to CEH by reviewing its published policies and interviewing key informants to understand how policies translate into delivery of CEH services. A comprehensive understanding of Ethiopian health policies with respect to CEH will help identify strengths, reveal policy gaps and inform future implementation of CEH services in Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study design

This was a mixed-methods situational analysis composed of a documentary analysis and key informant interviews. Participation in the study as a key informant was voluntary, and those who signed a consent form were enrolled in the study. This study is reported against the COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research (COREQ) checklist (online supplemental file 1).

Data collection

Documentary analysis

The documentary analysis included policies, standards or reports that (1) were developed by the MOH or Ministry of Education (MOE) of Ethiopia, (2) touched on any element of CEH and (3) were published in or after the year 2000. The cut-off date of 2000 was chosen as child health became a policy focus because of the Millennium Development Goals, and prior policies were presumed to be limited in their child health focus. All documents were sourced from MOE and MOH websites or requested from the ministries in hard copy or pdf form. The document review entailed extracting data that focused on governance, human resources (health workforce), service delivery, financing, health information systems and access to essential medication from health policies and school health documents.

Key informant interviews

Participants were eligible to serve as key informants if they were leaders, authorities, researchers or clinicians involved in child health in general or CEH in particular, and employed in the federal MOH, MOE, Addis Ababa University or University of Gondar (ie, the two leading academic institutions regarding CEH). Key informants were selected purposively by their respective department heads based on their extensive training, service and research in the field of child health.

Key informants were invited to take part in an in-depth interview, following a predetermined open-ended question guide developed by author STS (online supplemental file 2). The interviewees were approached face-to-face, and the interviews were conducted at the workplace. Besides the interviewer (author ZE) and the study participant, there was no one else present during the interview. The interviewer introduced the purpose of the study to the participants. To obtain more detailed narrations, the interviewer used facilitation and expanding interview approaches. There was no repeat interview and all interviews were audio recorded with participant permission. Summary field notes were taken during the interview and shortly after the sessions, writing out the details, to ensure comprehensive data collection. The duration of the interviews ranged from 40 to 60 min.

Reflexivity statement of the research team

The study team consisted of two Ethiopian (STS and ZE) and one Canadian (AA) ophthalmologists, one Ethiopian public health professional (ST) and one Canadian scientist (HD). At the time of interview conduct, author ZE worked as a consultant for the Ethiopian MOH. The study team collectively held expertise in paediatric ophthalmology, global public health, quantitative and qualitative research methods. The Canadian and Ethiopian members of the study team have been collaborating on a wide range of projects since 2016. The study team acknowledges that their perspectives come from a capacity building lens with respect to CEH in Ethiopia, and recognise this may have influenced the study results.

Data analysis

Data extracted from the documentary analysis was tabulated in a spreadsheet (MS Excel). Audio recordings from the key informant interviews were transcribed in full. The transcribed data were closely examined by coauthors STS, ZE, HD and ST. The WHO designs an Eye Care Situation Analysis Tool (ECSAT) for national and district MOH eye care planners and policymakers, and we used this tool to analyse the data as it covers the building blocks of the healthcare system. Data was synthesised and analysed by narrative synthesis, using an adaptation of the ECSAT as a framework.17 Specifically, the six components of ECSAT and their core indicators were adapted for the context of CEH (online supplemental file 3). To ensure consistency, data was independently categorised according to the ECSAT domains and compared; discussions among coauthors settled discrepancies until consensus was achieved.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were involved in the design or conduct of this study. Participant checking of interview findings was not employed.

Narrative synthesis

The narrative synthesis findings are presented according to the six domains of the adapted ECSAT,17 namely: (1) leadership and governance, (2) workforce and infrastructure, (3) service delivery - access, (4) service delivery - quality, (5) health financing and (6) health information systems (online supplemental file 2).

Leadership and governance

Theme 1.1: limited policy focus on CEH leadership

Of the 11 policies reviewed, 3 (27%) had CEH indicators related to leadership and governance (table 3). The National Health Policy of Ethiopia19 made no mention of eye health in general or CEH in particular, but spoke of bolstering the school health programme (table 3). The Health Sector Transformation Plan II20 acknowledged that eye disease is a major contributor to the non- communicable disease burden, and included measures to prevent trachoma (table 3). The National Strategic Action Plan for Eye Health mentioned the need to establish or strengthen refractive error services, in particular recommended the strengthening of school eye health and refractive error services; however, it did not speak to the integration of CEH within the existing child health services.18

The National Strategic Action Plan for Eye Health18 stated that the MOH’s Disease Prevention and Control Directorate oversees the coordination of eye care programmes through two teams: the non-communicable disease team, which handles all eye-related issues except trachoma, and the neglected tropical diseases team, which handles trachoma. The National Trachoma Taskforce and National Committee for the Prevention of Blindness are two coalition and coordination platforms that support the MOH at the federal level. These forums include representatives from the MOH, non-health partners, universities, professional associations and development partners.

Key informants representing the MOH mentioned that one national eye care focal point and one technical assistant work under the non-communicable disease team. Despite occasional communication between the non-communicable disease and neglected tropical diseases teams, neither team collaborates with the other on planning or execution.

… because we work in various teams. Information and activities among us are frequently not clearly shared. – CEH1, eye health manager, MOH

Another common conclusion from the key informants was that the Ethiopian eye care system is largely dependent on the support of non-governmental organisations (NGOs).

Workforce and infrastructure

Theme 2.1: critical need for increased skilled health workers for CEH

Only 3 of the 11 (27%) reviewed documents outlined health workforce plans of Ethiopia (table 1). They referred to the critical need for adequate numbers and skills of health workers to deliver high impact priority interventions. In particular, the National Human Resource for Health Strategic Plan for Ethiopia21 states that the goal of improved human resource is to ‘ensure availability of an adequate number of well qualified health professionals with an appropriate mix of skills; equitably distributed, motivated, retained and perform well to achieve universal access to healthcare in Ethiopia’ and additionally outlines targets for increasing paediatric ophthalmologists, ophthalmic nurses and optometrists in the country by the end of 2025 (table 3). The National Strategic Action Plan for Eye Health18 states ‘the plan includes Human Resource for eye health management as one of the strategic objectives that will realize equitable, rational development and distribution of eye care workers in line with VISION 2020’. The National School Health and Nutrition Strategy22 suggested that training of teachers could support screening and correction of vision disorders.

Key informants, particularly academic ophthalmologists and eye health managers from non-governmental organisations supported this idea, and narrated that the number of ophthalmologists and optometrists had significantly increased over the last decade due to the expansion of training programmes in Ethiopia. A senior ophthalmologist from the university reflected his optimism in this aspect:

In recent years the number of Ophthalmologists in the country is increasing as the number of training centers increased from one to five. Additionally, pediatrics Ophthalmology fellowship program is designed at Addis Ababa University in collaboration with the University of Toronto. – CEH10, senior ophthalmologist.

However, despite the growing number of eye health workers in the country, the strategic documents underlined that Ethiopia continues to have inequitable distribution of skilled health workers particularly in the rural parts of the country. The documents neglect to prioritise and plan for addressing this gap.

Service delivery - access

Theme 3.1: low availability of CEH services

Five (45%) of the reviewed documents discussed about access to CEH service delivery in Ethiopia (table 1). Three documents in particular discussed strengthening school eye health services in detail.21 23 26 The establishment of low vision centres in all tertiary eye care units (TECUs) was highlighted as one of the strategic activities in the National Strategic Action Plan for Eye Health.18 This document, while advocating for the integration of refraction services into the existing healthcare system, fails to mention other causes of prevalent childhood ocular morbidities or the extent of integration within the existing child healthcare systems.

The National Health Sector Strategic Plan for Early Childhood Development in Ethiopia23 emphasised the need for comprehensive and coordinated neonatal care, but it omitted an action point on retinopathy of prematurity, a newly emerging cause of early childhood blindness. The National School Health and Nutrition Strategy22 document broadly discussed eye health promotion, primary management for common and acute cases and screening of school children (table 3).

The key informants from the MOH and University mentioned the five TECUs in the country as the major public eye care service providers in Ethiopia. Regarding the access to CEH within the secondary eye care units (SECUs) a focal person from the MOH said that:

Secondary eye care units [SECUs] provide cataract surgery [for adults] and refractive error services for all age groups. All of the SECUs don’t have a focused child eye heath service. – CEH7, ophthalmologist.

Theme 3.2: equitable access for CEH services

None of the reviewed documents mentioned integration of CEH into the existing child health system, neither in service nor in training. The National Health Sector Strategic Plan for Early Childhood Development23 included establishment of child friendly facilities as one of the core indicators to monitor and evaluate the implementation of the strategic plan; however, this was not discussed with respect to CEH (table 3). The Health Extension Programme Profile24 document did not include CEH as any of its focus areas for instruction and responsibility.

Key informants involved in eye health management or service delivery mentioned challenges faced by public health institutions in providing accessible and affordable spectacles:

Running the optical workshop has been another challenges from our side, as the cost of the spectacles within the hospitals was expensive in comparison with the private clinics. The cause has been excessive expenditure as the procurement policy is lengthy and costly. – CEH5, university leader.

Almost all key informants held the view that there is no regular and effective nationwide school health programme. The MOE developed and endorsed the National School Health and Nutrition Strategy,22 but this document is not yet disseminated to stakeholders. During one interview a clear communication gap was noted between the MOE and MOH, as a key informant from the education sector stated:

I don’t have information regarding school health. As to my understanding such types of initiatives should come from the Ministry of Health. And we have to work jointly, and our role is facilitation, as the MOH having expertise in the field. – CEH4, education expert

The National Health Sector Plan for Early Childhood Development in Ethiopia23 emphasised the focus for children with disability (including vision impairment) for early identification and referral to centres that can appropriately address special needs (table 3). However, according to the key informants, the limited availability of schools for visually impaired children, along with the difficulty in transporting the children from one place to the other, social stigma and psychological trauma remain significant challenges for families with visually impaired children. One of the key informants shared his experience in this aspect:

Families with visually impaired children will face difficulty in sending their kid to schools and make them productive. Sometimes I hear that families with blind children and other mental illness hide them from public. – CEH13, eye health manager, NGO.

Service delivery - quality

Theme 4.1: importance of referral system to improve quality of CEH services

Only three documents (27%) discussed quality of CEH service delivery (table 1). The National Strategic Action Plan for Eye Health18 discussed the development of standard operating procedures to strengthen the referral system across the eye health tiers, noted as a significant strategic area of focus (table 3). Yet, all key informants representing the clinical sector reported that the referral system remains poor, despite this policy focus. The late presentation of patients was commonly attributed to the poor referral system:

One of the challenges in child eye health in this country is late presentation. This is mainly due to the poor health-seeking behavior of parents and a poor referral system within our health institutions (from health centre to tertiary hospital like ours). – CEH7, ophthalmologist

Theme 4.2: importance of quality metrics

None of the national health plans for child health described a system to track the quality metrics for eye care. No national guidelines or standard operating procedures for CEH were uncovered in this work. However, of the 76 core indicators cited in the Health Sector Transformation Plan II20 to monitor and evaluate its implementation, one was associated with CEH, related to the monitoring of trachoma (ie, ‘Proportion of Trachoma endemic woredas with Trachomatous Inflammation Follicular to <5% among 1 to 9 years old children’). Furthermore, the Ethiopian Standard ES 3620:2012,25 published by the federal Food, Medicine and Healthcare Administration and Control Authority of Ethiopia, set standards for requirements of all specialty clinics, including eye clinics. Specifically, the standard specified the space, equipment, human resources and practices required to be certified as ophthalmological practice, for each level of eye care centre in the country.25 However, the requirements for refraction and optical services in the country were not covered by this standard.

Health financing

Theme 5.1: absence of a defined budget line

Health financing was included in four (36%) of the documents (table 1). The Health Sector Transformation Plan II20 included health financing as one of its five priorities, aimed at mobilising adequate and sustainable health financing for health and enhancing effectiveness through changes to the health system’s financial and management system. The anticipated budget to meet its goals over the 5 years of the plan was estimated to be between USD$21.89 billion and USD$27.55 billion however, the proportion allocated to CEH was not noted.

The total 5-year budget for the National Strategic Action Plan for Eye Health18 was USD$13 million in 2016 and USD$21 million in 2020. However, there was no clear financial plan for CEH other than an estimated USD$48 000 to USD$87 000 2020 to conduct vision screening in school children. The National Health Sector Strategic Plan for Early Childhood Development23 estimated a budget of USD$37 million, however there was no specific mention of the share required for CEH. According to the 2013 Annual Performance report of the MOH,26 13.2% of the total government budget was allocated to the health sector but it was unclear how much of this was allocated to CEH.

Several key informants indicated that the limited budget allocation by the government was a substantial barrier to improving CEH in Ethiopia and a major concern. Accessibility and sustainability of CEH was thought to be significantly impacted by the amount of budget allocated by the government. The limited government allocation was thought to put CEH in a position to be dependent on donors:

The budget allocated for the field [eye health]. And in terms of budget and programs [we can say] it is donor derived field. –CEH2, eye health manager, MOH

Health information systems

Theme 6.1: limited CEH indicators in the national health management system

Five documents (42%) discussed health information systems (table 1), however indicators related to CEH were limited. The Health Management Information System Monitoring and Evaluation Strategic Plan27 outlined 169 indicators of which only three were related to eye care (cataract, trachomatous trichiasis and glaucoma) but not specifically CEH (table 3). However, the Demographic Health Information System 2,28 outlined 12 indicators for eye health, which were disaggregated by age to allow for CEH monitoring (table 3).

Lack of comprehensive CEH data was seen by key informants to affect the ability to identify the extent of the problem and areas to intervene by the government and NGOs.

…his 21st century is era of data and information. In this regard we don’t have enough data [on CEH]. That is a challenge. If we are able to generate these data, it will help us to convince policy makers. – CEH11, eye health manager

Theme 6.2: importance of evidence-based care for CEH

The Health Management Information System Monitoring and Evaluation Strategic Plan27 and Demographic Health Information System 228 do not collect information about outcomes and quality of eye care services in general and CEH. According to the National Strategic Action Plan for Eye Health,18 generating evidence for informed decision-making and quality control was considered as a key strategic area to be implemented between 2016 and 2020. All of the key informant participants mentioned that lack of nationwide data on the burden, distribution and trends of CEH was one of the main challenges in CEH in Ethiopia.

Being busy with day-to-day clinical activities was mentioned as the main reason for limited publications from the clinician’s side:

As to my knowledge, there is a huge gap on evidences on eye care. Majority of the staffs in the department are engaged in clinical activities. – CEH9, optometrist

Discussion

This is the first study to evaluate the extent to which CEH has been integrated into the Ethiopian healthcare system through policy and practice. Prior to this, there has been little research on the integration, workforce and sustainable funding concerns that face eye health services and policies in LMICs.29 30

Our research revealed that collectively, the Ethiopian MOH and MOE have developed several health-related policies and initiatives which include provisions for enhancing children’s health, but there remains insufficient focus on CEH. According to the Grand Challenges in Global Eye Health, challenges in eye health are diverse and addressing them requires a variety of responses—including advocacy, coordinated action and research—from stakeholders such as patients, policymakers, researchers, funders, programme managers and industry.31 Over the last three decades, there have been hopeful improvements in terms of putting children’s rights on the international, regional and national agendas.32 Studies have demonstrated that enacting legislation and policies governing vision screening for children are initial steps for better CEH.33 34 Concerningly, child eye care services are not prioritised on the public health agenda in many African countries despite the existence of legislation and guidelines. This underlines the fact that these countries continue to face obstacles to the implementation of policies such as politics, underperforming economies and inadequate health financing systems.35 36 In the case of Ethiopia, the government has ratified the VISION 2020 declaration and established a national commission for the prevention of blindness that reports to the federal MOH.37 However the regional health bureau did not establish a VISION 2020 committee, which is one of the top strategies for implementing, managing and evaluating eye health. Similarly, a report from Nigeria suggested that the limited progress on eye health was in part due to programming and implementation challenges, as less than half of its 36 states had a VISION 2020 plan and even fewer had an active Prevention of Blindness committee focused on implementation.35

The 2018 Lancet Global Health Commission highlighted that accountability is key to producing high quality health services.38 Our study indicated poor coordination and communication across various MOH departments with respect to CEH, and key informants emphasised the lack of a clear devoted ownership and accountability within the government system at all levels for eye care in general. Along with clear leadership and accountability, a national budget is at the centre of public policy, allowing governments to concretely implement their policy objectives.39 Global trends indicate that eye health is frequently overlooked in general healthcare financing mechanisms, is rarely included in national health policy frameworks (especially for basic care) and is not taken into account when planning the health workforce.31 Our analysis made it abundantly evident that the Ethiopian policies lack clarity in defining the amount of budget allotted for general eye care service, let alone for CEH. The Abuja declaration states that the health sector should receive 15% of the national budget,40 but in African nations, the median expenditure of the government budget allocated to healthcare has been reported at 10%.36 The limited funding dedicated to CEH in Ethiopia reportedly causes an over-reliance on donor funds, which puts sustainability of eye care at risk in the long term.

Our study revealed concerns for access to CEH and in particular an insufficient health workforce to meet the growing demands. Accessibility to eye care in the African health eye care system is also impacted by the uneven geographical distribution of specialists, which is heavily skewed toward urban areas.36 For instance, in Ethiopia, 62.4% of ophthalmologists work in the capital. Furthermore, ophthalmologists, and in particular paediatric ophthalmologists, are limited across the African continent. As a minimum level of service to meet children’s requirements, the WHO recommends one paediatric ophthalmic centre or CEH tertiary facility for every 10 million people.41 42 With only 26 centres located across 11 of the 48 countries, sub-Saharan Africa is drastically underserved.36

Emphasising the close connection between general child health and CEH, the WHO has identified 10 ‘key activities for healthy eyes in children’ that were also recommended for integration into primary maternal and child health services and inclusion in national prevention of blindness plans.16 43 Although Ethiopia has made some headway in tertiary eye care for children by establishing five public TECUs in the past 10 years, primary eye care has not received much attention. Our documentary analysis revealed that eye health was not included in the Health Extension Programme Profile24; given that in the Ethiopian healthcare system, health extension workers (HEWs) play a crucial role in both a national community health programme as a primary level eye care service provider, this represents a missed opportunity. Prior research in Ethiopia revealed that HEWs had little knowledge of common eye illnesses that might cause blindness.44 The knowledge and competency gaps among general healthcare providers are mostly caused by a lack of basic eye care training.45–48 Several other studies have shown successful training of primary care workers in ocular health, including primary health clinics in Tanzania,49 nurses in Rwanda,50 community health workers in Nepal51 and community physicians in rural China.52

Availability of services and a robust health workforce are only part of enhancing CEH; quality of such services are key to improving CEH outcomes. Strong referral networks were seen as essential to the provision of high-quality CEH in our study. Our documentary analysis showed that the national Health Management Information System and Demographic Health Information System fails to capture key CEH indicators (table 3). Even the WHO’s tracking of Universal Health Coverage fails to capture eye health outcomes.53 Enhancing quality tracking will go a long way in improving CEH in Ethiopia.

The main limitation of this study was that it did not evaluate the practice of CEH services within health facilities nor include the perception of the service seekers; these could be the focus of future studies. Furthermore, there was a significant gender imbalance among the key informants, perhaps reflective of an imbalance in the make-up of the workforce. This may have biased the study results; thus, an expanded inclusion criterion in future studies may help obtain gender balance among participants. Despite these limitations, the study is the first of its kind, revealing sufficient evidence of gaps in policy and strategic issues of CEH in Ethiopia.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study indicates that there is insufficient focus of CEH in key Ethiopian health policy documents. Strengthening ownership of CEH and improving coordination between line ministries may have positive downstream effects on implementation of policies relevant to CEH. Policies could be further improved to more directly address CEH, particularly in mandating dedicated funding, integrating CEH into existing health services, and including relevant CEH indicators into health information management systems to track quality and performance.
