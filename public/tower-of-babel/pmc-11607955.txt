Comparative genomics and virulence potential of Campylobacter coli strains isolated from different sources over 25 years in Brazil
Authors: Carolina Nogueira Gomes, Andrei Giacchetto Felice, Giovana do Nascimento Pereira, Victor Augusto Sallum Ceballos, Siomar de Castro Soares, Ludmilla Tonani, Patrícia Helena Grizante Barião, Márcia Regina von Zeska Kress, Sheila da Silva Duque, Maria Balkey, Marc William Allard, Juliana Pfrimer Falcão
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCampylobacter spp. have been reported as a common cause of gastroenteritis in humans in many countries. However, in Brazil there is insufficient data to estimate the impact of Campylobacter in public health. In light of the importance of this foodborne pathogen, the aim of this study was to perform comparative analyses on 80 Brazilian Campylobacter coli genomes isolated from human feces, animals, the environment, and food. Methods include Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI), Gegenees, genomic plasticity, presence of pathogenicity, resistance, and metabolic islands. In addition, virulence analysis in Galleria mellonella were also performed for 18 selected C. coli strains.ResultsThe ANI values confirmed that all strains belonged to the C. coli species. Phylogenetic analyses demonstrated the evolutionary relationships among the studied strains, highlighting the genetic diversity among them. The differences in shared and deleted regions of the studied genomes were demonstrated, with 16 genomic islands identified, including 4 metabolic islands, 4 resistance islands, and 8 pathogenicity islands. We detected genes associated with chemotaxis, exotoxin production, antimicrobial resistance, stress response, defense mechanisms, and intracellular survival among these islands, highlighting the pathogenic potential of these strains. Two strains isolated from human and one from animal showed high virulence, killing 100% of Galleria mellonella larvae. Two strains isolated from the environment and two isolated from food killed 70–90% of the larvae and were classified as virulent. Three strains isolated from animal, two from human, two from the environment and one from food killed 30% to 60% of the larvae and were considered of intermediate virulence. Campylobacter jejuni ATCC 33291, one strain isolated from human and one from food killed 10 to 20% of the larvae and were considered of low virulence. One strain isolated from food did not kill any larvae and was considered avirulent.ConclusionsThe results obtained highlighted the genetic diversity, pathogenic and virulence potential of many of the C. coli strains studied, contributing for a more complete characterization of this important pathogen recognized as a cause of human gastroenteritis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-024-03642-5.Keywords: Campylobacter coli, Genomic analyses, Genetic diversity, Pathogenic potential, Virulence

Introduction

Campylobacteriosis is a zoonosis of worldwide distribution with significant repercussions to public health with high socioeconomic impact. Campylobacter is reported more commonly than Salmonella as a bacterium causing gastroenteritis in several countries [1, 2]. Due to its zoonotic nature and the complex interactions between humans, animals, and the environment, Campylobacter has garnered significant attention in the realm of One Health, an interdisciplinary approach that recognizes the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health [1–4].

The genus Campylobacter belongs to Campylobacteraceae family, which has 45 validly described species at the time of writing [5] and most of the reported Campylobacter infections are caused by Campylobacter jejuni and C. coli [1, 2]. The disease caused by Campylobacter is characterized by self-limited bloody diarrhea, accompanied by fever, abdominal pain, headache, muscle pain, and with rare vomiting. Furthermore, post- infectious complications such as Guillain- Barré syndrome and reactive arthritis are described in the literature [6]. The incubation period is usually 2 to 5 days and can extend for up to 10 days [7, 8]. In severe cases hydration and electrolytes replacement is not enough and the use of antimicrobials is recommended [9].

The severity of disease, Campylobacter pathogenicity and treatment of campylobacteriosis is directly related to the presence of virulence factors (VFs) and antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) that they carry. The presence of some VFs may increase the pathogenicity and ability to survive within a given host, which can exacerbate disease severity [10] Likewise, ARGs can be located on plasmids or resistance genomic islands (GEIs) which are generally transferable, and may lead to the emergence of resistant strains [11].

The genomic islands play an important role in the genome plasticity. They are large regions in the DNA sequence (∼6–200 kb) acquired from other organisms through horizontal gene transfer (HGT) and carry genes that may contribute to the adaptation of bacteria to their host or their specific ecological niche, such as genes related to pathogenesis, novel metabolic pathways, resistances to antibiotics among others [12].

Genome sequencing is currently an important discriminating and highly informative tool that allows more comprehensive analyses of resistance, virulence and phylogenetic relationships [13]. Although the next-generation sequencing (NGS) has greatly increased the amount of genetic information available for the characterization of bacterial isolates, there is a lack of genomic information about vast number of emerging Campylobacter species [14].

Regarding the mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of Campylobacter spp., despite the high incidence of this pathogen in several countries, the pathogenesis is not well understood to date and much of what is known is related specifically to the species C. jejuni [8, 15]. In this context, in vitro models of human and animal cell culture and alternative to in vivo models have helped to identify the various mechanisms that facilitate the infection of species considered emerging, such as C. coli [3]. Recently, Galleria mellonella larvae were shown to be susceptible to infection by pathogens such as Campylobacter and could be used as a powerful tool to verify the virulence of this poorly understood pathogen [16].

Due to the importance of this foodborne pathogen, the aim of the present study was to perform comparative analyses of C. coli genomes of strains isolated from diverse sources in Brazil by Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI), Gegenees, genomic plasticity, presence of pathogenicity, resistance, and metabolic islands. In addition, virulence analysis in Galleria mellonella were also performed for some selected C. coli strains.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains

A total of 80 C. coli strains were studied. Those strains were isolated from human feces (12 strains), animals (34 strains), environments (20 strains) and foods (14 strains) from Rio de Janeiro, São Paulo and Minas Gerais States in the Southeast region of Brazil between 1995 and 2019. These strains were selected from the collection of the Campylobacter Reference Laboratory of the Oswald Cruz Institute of Rio de Janeiro (FIOCRUZ), their genomes were sequenced, and the accession numbers were previously reported in [17, 18]. Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) analysis

In order to establish an accurate analysis, the 80 C. coli genomes of this study were annotated de novo using the Prokka software [19]. Initially the ANI was performed to determine the correct classification of the strains in the present study. The 80 C. coli genomes examined and the reference genomes Campylobacter jejuni NCTC11168, Campylobacter fetus CFF00A031, Campylobacter lari LMG21009 and Campylobacter coli FDAARGOS_735 were also subjected to ANI analysis, which was performed using the ANIb option of Python program “pyani” (v0.2.7) [20]. The analysis was performed based on the BLAST algorithm on a local computer using default settings of the BLAST [21]. The heatmap software package (v1.0.12) in R was used to plot the graphical heatmap and dendrogram for the ANI values.

Phylogenetic analyses

The phylogenetic analyses of the 80 genomes studied and 4 reference genomes mentioned above were performed using the software Gegenees 2.0 [22]. The software uses BLASTn with sequence fragmentation length of 200 bp and a step size of 100 bp. The resulting similarity matrix was exported into NEXUS format, and used in the softwareSplitsTree4 [23] for the phylogenetic inference was built by using the Neighbor-Joining method.

Genomic plasticity, identification of genomics islands and functional annotation of the genes

The analysis of genomic plasticity with identification of genomic islands was carried out using two different softwares. Due to a restriction in the number of genomes to be analyzed simultaneously, the 80 C. coli genomes studied were divided into two groups based on their isolation source. In this way, the genomes of the 46 strains of C. coli isolated from humans and animals and the 34 genomes of C. coli isolated from the environment and food were compared, separately, with the genome of reference Campylobacter coli FDAARGOS_735 using Blast Ring Image Generation (BRIG) software. This software uses the BLASTn method to compare genomes and generates a circular image in which each ring represents the genome of a lineage [24]. At this preliminary stage, all strains studied were compared with the reference strain and we observed that the strain Cc05 showed the lowest number of deletions. Thus, it was used as a reference genome for the analysis of genomic plasticity and prediction of genomic islands.

The Genomic Island Prediction Software (GIPSy) was used to predict genomic islands, which are regions of the genome that can be acquired by horizontal gene transfer and can be classified into pathogenicity islands, resistance islands, metabolic islands, among others. To identify the presence of these islands, the software searches for regions with characteristics that differ from the rest of the genome, such as guanine and cytosine (G + C) content, presence of insertion sequences and/or regions flanked by transporter RNA genes, integrases and transposases [25]. The islands were plotted in the outermost ring of the image generated by the BRIG software and the genes present on the islands were aligned and classified according to the functional categories of the Cluster of Orthologous Genes (COG) [26, 27]. To this end, eggNOG-mapper (version 2.1.11) database that performs this annotation according to orthologous genes was used [28] and each sequence was mapped using either the hidden Markov model (HMM) or DIAMOND to align with the eggNOG database. In addition, the genes present on the islands were also classified using AMRFinderPlus software at NCBI Pathogen Detection, Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) and The Universal Protein Knowledgebase (UniProt) [29–31].

Virulence analysis in Galleria mellonella

The analysis was performed comparatively for 18 C. coli strains with 5 isolated from humans, 5 from food, 4 from animals and 4 from the environment, each highlighted with asterisk (*) in Table 1. These strains were selected based on the results obtained from comparative genomic analyses which provided information for selecting the most genetically distinct strains. The assay was carried out according to Champion et al. [16], with some modifications.

Briefly, the larvae were maintained at 28˚C in the dark in glass containers (30 cm height—20 cm wide—2 L capacity) with appropriate oxygen and access to food until reaching the sixth instar, whose weight is between 200 and 250 mg. After complete development, the larvae were deprived of food and separated into groups of 10 units in glass Petri dishes for each bacterial isolate and controls. A Hamilton micro-syringe (model 7000.5KH of 10 μL) was used for artificial inoculation of 5 μL of C. coli (5 × 106 CFU/mL) into the center of the last right pro-leg of G. mellonella larvae for each of the 18 isolates studied and for the positive control infected with Campylobacter jejuni strain ATCC 33291. The negative control was inoculated with PBS and for each strain 10 G. mellonella larvae were infected in a single infection event. After inoculation, the larvae were incubated at 37˚C, deprived of food and direct light. During the experimental period of 8 days the larvae were removed every 24 h of the pre-pupae, in order to delay their metamorphosis with data recorded daily.

The graph and statistical analysis of the obtained results were performed using the Log-rank method (Mantel-Cox) in Prism5 for Windows program of the GraphPad software (version 5.01) and the level of significance was α = 5%.

Results

Average Nucleotide Identity analysis

For the 80 C. coli strains and including the reference strain Campylobacter coli FDAARGOS_735, ANI values were observed ranging from 98.1% to 100%. In addition, the 80 genomes were analyzed in comparison to the other reference strains used and the ANI values observed ranged from 71.1% to 75.3% compared to Campylobacter lari LMG21009, from 71.1% to 71.3% compared to Campylobacter fetus CFF00A031 and 84.3% to 85.0% compared to Campylobacter jejuni NCTC11168 (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1).

The different colors correspond to the different clades found in the analysis by Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI)

Phylogenetic analyses

The phylogenetic analysis of the 80 genomes studied and including the 4 reference genomes was conducted using the Gegenees software which resulted in a distance matrix based on the similarity and plotted in a heatmap (Fig. 2). The analysis of this matrix allowed us to observe genetic similarities ranging from 76 and 100%. Specifically, the range from 76 to 90% of similarity was represented by the yellow color and the range from 90 to 100% was represented by green color in the heatmap. In addition, the phylogenetic tree generated in the SplisTree4 software grouped the strains studied into six different clusters named A, B, C, D, E, and F. Two genomes were not grouped into any of these six clusters.

Cluster B grouped 26 strains with similarity between 90 and 97% with 3 isolated from sewage between 1995 to 1997, 7 from chicken (liver, gizzard, wing and heart) between 2010 to 2017, 7 from humans between 1998 to 2003 and 9 isolated from chicken faeces between 1995 to 2016. The cluster C grouped 16 strains studied and the reference strain Campylobacter coli ASM73039v1, with similarity between 84 and 95% with 2 isolated from humans in 2003, 7 isolated from sewage, environmental and potable water between 1995 to 2004 and 7 isolated from monkey between 1999 to 2019. Cluster D grouped 2 strains with similarity of 88% isolated from monkey and swine in 2007 and 2016. The cluster E grouped 3 strains with similarity of 87% being 1 isolated from monkey in 2002 and 2 isolated from potable water in 2004. The strains CCAMP 494 isolated from human in 1998 and CCAMP502 isolated from human in 1999 did not group to any other lineage and showed similarity of 79% and 76%, respectively (Table 1, Fig. 2).

Genomic plasticity, identification of genomics islands and functional annotation of the genes present on the islands

The comparison analysis performed by the BRIG software previously divided into two groups for comparison allowed us to observe the difference between the shared and deleted regions in the genomes studied (Fig. 3A and B). In the comparison among the strains isolated from humans and animals it was possible to observe, for example, that the strain Cc05 used as reference strain and the strains Cc03, CCAMP 667, CCAMP 1665, CCAMP 1670 and CCAMP 1672 did not present the same deletion regions observed in the other genomes analyzed (Fig. 3A).

Similarly, when analyzing the comparison between the strains isolated from the environment and food, we observed that the strain Cc 05, used for comparison, and the strains CCAMP 465, CCAMP 466, CCAMP 467, CCAMP 834, CCAMP 840 and CCAMP 1064 also presented important differences in the deletion regions in comparison to the other genomes analyzed (Fig. 3B).

In addition, the Gipsy software predicted 16 genomic islands of which 4 are metabolic islands (MI) (~ 9.4 kb to ~ 24.7 kb), 4 are resistance islands (RI) (~ 11.5 kb to ~ 39.6 kb) and 8 are pathogenicity islands (PAI) (~ 11.0 kb to 36.4 kb). These genomic islands can be observed in the outermost ring in the image generated by the BRIG software (Fig. 3A and B).

Among the 4 predicted MI, a total of 55 genes were detected, with variations in their presence across the analyzed strains. Of these, 27 genes currently have no known function, while 10 are associated with biosynthesis processes, 9 with transport, 5 with metabolism, 3 with exotoxin production, and 1 with chemotaxis.

For the 4 predicted RI, a total of 91 genes were identified, with 45 having no known function to date, 18 genes are related to transport, 9 to motility, 6 to biosynthesis processes and 4 to antimicrobial resistance. Likewise, the analysis of the 8 predicted PAI revealed a total of 124 genes, with 53 currently lacking known functions, 14 are linked to adherence, 14 to transport, 12 to biosynthesis processes, 11 to metabolism, 7 to motility, 3 to stress response, 3 to DNA replication, 2 to transmembrane transporter activity, 2 to transcription regulation, 2 to intracellular survival, and 1 to chemotaxis (Table 2).

Virulence analysis in Galleria mellonella

The results obtained for C. coli strains isolated from human and animal are shown in Fig. 4A and the results obtained for isolates from the environment and food are shown in Fig. 4B. After 8 days of experiment, the strains Cc 03, Cc05 isolated from human and CCAMP 1589 isolated from animal showed high virulence killing 100% of the larvae of Galleria mellonella (Fig. 4A). Two strains isolated from the environment (CCAMP 764 and CCAMP 818) and two strains isolated from food (CCAMP 1066 and CCAMP 1619) killed 70–90% of the larvae and were classified as virulent (Fig. 4B). The strains Cc 04 and CCAMP 494 isolated from humans, CCAMP 768, CCAMP 1000 and CCAMP 1672 isolated from animals, CCAMP 773 and CCAMP 819 isolated from the environment and CCAMP 1073 isolated from food killed 30% to 60% of the larvae and were considered of intermediate virulence. The strain Campylobacter jejuni ATCC 33291, CCAMP 502 isolated from human and CCAMP 1578 isolated from food killed 10 to 20% of the larvae and were considered of low virulence (Fig. 4A and B). The CCAMP 1062 strain isolated from food did not kill any larvae and was considered avirulent (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

Foodborne diseases are an important cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), it is estimated that 420,000 deaths occur each year due to the consumption of food contaminated by bacteria, viruses, parasites or chemicals [32].

Campylobacter species, primarily Campylobacter jejuni and C. coli have been reported as a common cause of gastroenteritis in humans [1, 2]. However, in Brazil, the study and isolation of this bacterial genus, especially the C. coli species, is not very frequent which makes it difficult to assess the extent of the involvement of this species causing disease in humans and animals, as well as to determine the impact of its presence in food and the environment [9, 15, 17].

In the present study, a comparative analysis of genomes by ANI, Gegenees, genomic plasticity and presence of pathogenicity, resistance and metabolic islands was performed. In addition, the virulence analysis using Galleria mellonella model was performed comparatively for 18 C. coli strains of which 5 were isolated from human, 5 from food, 4 from animals and 4 from environmental sources.

The analysis of next-generation sequencing data can compare genomic information among strains, from distant phylogenetic relationships to the highest level of subtyping [22]. ANI analysis is an easy, fast and reproducible methodology that has been widely used to characterize, identify genomic relationships between two or more strains and has been considered the new gold standard for determining bacterial species [33]. The ANI values were calculated to estimate the genetic relationships among the strains and we observed a variation of 98.1% to 100% between the 80 strains of C. coli and the reference strain Campylobacter coli FDAARGOS_735. However, ANI values were ≤ 85.0% when comparing C. coli strains with other Campylobacter species included in this analysis. According to [34], ANI values ≥ 95% may represent the same species. Thus, in agreement with the study mentioned above, all strains of the present study were correctly classified as belonging to the C. coli species.

Campylobacter is a rapidly evolving bacterial genus with a huge recombination potential. Thus, this genus may exhibit greater diversity of genome sequences among strains [35]. The Gegenees software performs phylogenetic analysis of strains using fragments of the complete genome, including repeated regions, genomic islands, duplicated genes and other elements. The similarity matrix obtained by the Gegenees software and used in the SplitsTree4 software for phylogenetic analysis demonstrated the evolutionary relationship between the strains of the present study, highlighting the genetic diversity among the strains.

Additionally, the methodology mentioned above grouped the 80 strains of C. coli studied in 6 distinct clusters and 2 strains were not grouped to any other. A more detailed analysis of the clusters allows us to observe a similarity ≥ 80% among some strains belonging to the same cluster which suggested that a possible contamination between different isolation sources may have occurred over these 25 years in Brazil. To date, there are no published studies that have used this methodology to analyze C. coli strains isolated over the years. However, these results corroborate with the data from Pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) published by our research group [36].

In the genomic era, the number of sequenced bacterial genomes has increased rapidly, and the identification of genomic islands has been shown to be important in obtaining information on differences between species and strains [37, 38]. Genomic plasticity analysis using the BRIG software allows us to observe the presence of regions that may be correlated with the adaptation processes of microorganisms with the environment and/or host [39]. Differences in the shared and deleted regions were observed between the studied genomes, which corroborates with the data found in the literature that classify Campylobacter as an organism with high recombination capacity that regularly acquire genetic elements by horizontal gene transfer (HGT) [35, 40, 41].

The Gipsy software predicted 4 metabolic, 4 resistance and 8 pathogenicity islands and the genetic content of each of these islands was analyzed and classified. Specifically, a greater number of genes present in MI were classified as related to biosynthesis and transport. In the RI and PAI predicted islands, the genes classified as related to transport and adherence were more abundant than the others. However, genes associated with chemotaxis, exotoxin production, antimicrobial resistance, stress response, defense mechanisms, and intracellular survival were also found on the predicted genomic islands, highlighting a potential advantage in the colonization and interaction process with the host for strains that acquired these genes through horizontal transfer.

In addition, due to lack of genomic information related to the species C. coli, a large number of detected genes have unknown functions. Thus, a more in-depth study of the genetic content of these islands will be conducted in the future so that we can better understand the specific functions of each predicted gene and in which strains these genes are present or deleted contributing to characterization of the strains studied.

The Galleria mellonella assay were performed with the 18 C. coli strains more genetically distinct from each other and the results allowed us to observe differences between the strains in the ability to kill the larvae. Despite the fact that 3 strains isolated from humans (Cc03, Cc05) and animal (CCAMP1589) presented high virulence, it was possible to observe that the ability to kill the larvae was strain dependent and was not directly related to the source of isolation. Interestingly, the strains Cc05 and Cc03 which promoted the death of 100% of the larvae more quickly among the strains studied (3 and 4 days, respectively) showed less deleted regions in comparison to the other genomes studied in the analyzes performed by the software BRIG and Gipsy and the strain CCAMP 1589 showed a genome with more deleted regions when compared to the 2 strains mentioned above, and promoted the death of 100% of the larvae in 7 days.

In opposition to the result obtained in this present work, data from the literature has shown that the pathogenicity has been associated with genome reduction and gene loss, which is preceded by the horizontal acquisition of PAIs [42, 43]. However, a more in-depth analysis has allowed us to observe that the strains Cc 05 and Cc03 did not present deletions in the regions of the genomes referring to the islands M1-1, MI-3, MI-4, RI-4, PAI-2 and PAI-8 (Fig. 4A).

The initial analysis of the genetic content present in these islands demonstrated the presence of important and known genes related to virulence. Specifically, we can highlight the gene cobB present in MI-1 related with Chemotaxis, the genes cdtA, cdtB and cdtC detected in MI-4 related with exotoxin production, the genes fliA, fliM, fliY, flgE, flgD and fliK related with motility and mcrC related with defense mechanisms detected in RI-4, the genes waaC, waaF, waaV, gmhA, hldE and gmhB detected in PAI-1 related with adherence, the genes Cj1450 and dut related with Intracellular survival and gmhA related with adherence present in PAI-8. Although a large number of genes present in the genomic islands do not have known function, the presence of the genes with the functions described above in the strains Cc 05 and Cc 03 may have strongly contributed to a higher pathogenic potential observed in these strains in the Galleria mellonella assay.

In conclusion, the results obtained provided important information on the genetic diversity, pathogenic potential and virulence of the C. coli strains studied and contributed to a more complete characterization of this pathogen that is considered an important cause of human gastroenteritis. In addition, C. coli strains isolated in Brazil and sequenced contribute to the limited amount of genomic data of this species.
