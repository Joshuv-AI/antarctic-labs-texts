Social emotional learning and autonomic regulation enhance social connectedness in female adolescents with extensive exposure to adverse childhood experiences
Authors: Nathan D. Chabin, Justine Chen, Matthew Abikenari, Maxim Dembow, Prabha Siddharth, Vickie Mays, Denise Chavira, Susan Ramsundarsingh, Dara G. Ghahremani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are associated with social isolation, a known contributor to diminishing mental health with consequences for adolescent development. We evaluated effects of the SKY Schools program, which combines social emotional learning (SEL) with training in autonomic regulation (breathwork), on social connectedness (SC) in female Latinx adolescents with high ACEs, a group particularly vulnerable to ACEs effects. In this longitudinal study, female Latinx students (N = 174, 14–18 years; M = 14.97; SD = 0.97) in two Southern California high schools with high (≥4) and low (<4) ACEs either completed the four‐week SKY Schools program plus 8 weeks of daily breathwork or underwent their usual physical education curriculum (control). We administered the Social Connectedness Scale before and after the program and again after 8 weeks. Relative to baseline, the high ACEs intervention group showed increased SC (7%) after daily breathwork compared with the control groups (low ACEs: 3%, high ACEs: −3%) and the low ACEs intervention group (−3%) (p < .01). Biopsychosocial programs that combine SEL and breathwork can reduce feelings of social isolation in socially vulnerable Latinx female adolescents, facilitating healthy adolescent development.

INTRODUCTION

Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) are linked to physical, mental, and social health problems in adolescents (Gajos et al., 2022; Swedo, 2024). Male and female adolescents show distinct patterns of ACE exposure and sequelae (Haahr‐Pedersen et al., 2020). Relative to males, females are more likely to report physical abuse and sexual assault and to exhibit related internalizing symptoms, including self‐harm, suicidal ideation and attempts (Duke et al., 2010), social isolation, and loneliness (Leban, 2021). These stress‐related withdrawn social behaviors are associated with psychiatric outcomes, such as depression and anxiety (Loades et al., 2020), as well as adverse cardiovascular outcomes, including coronary heart disease and stroke (Valtorta et al., 2016). The latter association is largely attributed to chronic activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and sympathetic nervous system. Over time, this chronic activation dysregulates autonomic nervous system functioning, promotes chronic inflammation, and elevates risk for cardiovascular disease in adulthood (Porges, 2007; Shonkoff et al., 2012). Collectively, these findings underscore the urgent need for early interventions that address ACEs before they contribute to the development of chronic mental and physical illness.

Racial and ethnic minority youth are at a high risk of ACEs, including both family and community‐related ACEs (Zhen‐Duan et al., 2023). Risk factors for ACEs among minoritized adolescents include racism, discrimination, and systemic oppression (Bernard et al., 2022). Latinx youth are at elevated risk of ACEs (e.g., immigration‐related exposure to violence and abuse) (Zetino et al., 2020) and have reported more ACEs compared to other ethnic groups (e.g., non‐Hispanic white, Somali, Hmong) (Landry et al., 2022). ACEs are highly correlated with suicidality in racially and ethnically minoritized youth, including Latinx females (Jewett et al., 2025). These compounding vulnerabilities highlight the importance of addressing the unique ACE profiles of Latinx and other racially and ethnically minoritized adolescents.

Social connectedness (SC) is a protective factor against negative impacts of ACEs, including stress, anxiety, depression, and suicidality (Arango et al., 2024; Landry et al., 2022; Weisblum et al., 2024). Defined as the sense of belonging and feeling close to others, SC reflects an individual's subjective awareness of being part of an interconnected social world. It captures the degree to which individuals perceive themselves as meaningfully integrated into their social environment rather than feeling separate or alienated from others (Lee & Robbins, 1995). With Latinx adolescents reporting lower peer connectedness than their non‐Hispanic counterparts (Arango et al., 2024), SC is an especially relevant interventional target for this population for reducing ACE‐related health disparities.

A growing focus of stress recovery research has been on the integration of cognitive and biological systems that contribute to resiliency and recovery (Calhoun et al., 2022). Such biopsychosocial models offer a more comprehensive framework for trauma recovery by simultaneously addressing autonomic dysregulation and building psychosocial skills. For instance, biopsychosocial interventions may cultivate emotion regulation by integrating psychosocial education (e.g., cognitive reappraisal) with mind–body practices, such as controlled breathing techniques (breathwork), that directly regulate the autonomic nervous system. This integrative approach is particularly relevant for Latinx adolescents, as scholars have emphasized the importance of incorporating biopsychosocial approaches, in addition to ethnic and cultural factors, into models of Latinx development (Azmitia, 2021). Moreover, biopsychosocial interventions may offer a culturally acceptable alternative to traditional mental health services, such as psychological counseling, which are often stigmatized in Latinx communities. When delivered in school settings, such interventions can reach a broader audience while circumventing the cultural barriers associated with clinical mental health care.

School‐based social emotional learning (SEL) interventions equip students with skills for managing emotions, building healthy relationships, and making responsible decisions, thereby promoting social connection and supporting mental health in a non‐stigmatizing context (Durlak et al., 2011; Pollak et al., 2024; Sklad et al., 2012). Biopsychosocial‐oriented SEL programs that incorporate mind–body practices, such as breathwork, are particularly well‐suited for youth with high ACE exposure, as these practices help restore autonomic balance (Goldstein et al., 2022) and may address the chronic dysregulation of the autonomic nervous system characteristic of this population (Dempster et al., 2023). By integrating physiological regulation with psychosocial skill‐building (e.g., SEL), such interventions have potential to reduce stress reactivity and increase parasympathetic activity, shifting students from states of sympathetic arousal towards greater physiological calm, more than SEL programming alone (Blewitt et al., 2024). This restoration of autonomic balance creates the neurophysiological conditions necessary for students to effectively internalize and apply the cognitive and emotional skills taught within SEL, ultimately improving trauma‐related mental health outcomes, including PTSD, anxiety, depression, and substance use (Cochrane et al., 2019).

Although SEL has demonstrated promise in minoritized groups (Lee et al., 2023), these populations remain underrepresented in SEL‐based intervention studies (Jones et al., 2025), highlighting the need for SEL interventions that account for the distinct ACE profiles and lived experiences of racially and ethnically minoritized adolescents. The current randomized controlled trial examined the efficacy of the SKY Schools program, a school‐based biopsychosocial intervention that integrates SEL and breathwork, to improve SC. The study focused on female Latinx adolescents with high ACE exposure, defined as four or more ACEs, a threshold consistently associated with poor mental and physical health outcomes (Alhowaymel et al., 2023; Felitti et al., 1998). While such programs have been shown to improve autonomic balance and SC in young adults (Fincham et al., 2023; Goldstein et al., 2022; Seppälä et al., 2020), their efficacy has not been examined among youth with high ACEs. We hypothesized that female Latinx adolescents with high ACEs would demonstrate greater improvements in SC following participation in the SKY Schools program compared to both non‐participating peers and those with low ACE exposure. We additionally examined whether continued breathwork after program completion further enhanced these gains.

Participants

Participants were primarily socioeconomically disadvantaged (majority received free or reduced‐price meals; median completed education level for mothers was high school) 9th and 10th grade Latinx female students from two Southern California high schools (Table 1). Study participation spanned 5 months (January through May 2023); the intervention occurred during physical education classes.

Intervention

The SKY Schools program (skyschools.org; formerly Youth Empowerment Seminar) has been offered in 243 schools across the United States and 14 countries worldwide, including six Latin American countries. It has been shown to improve mental health outcomes (e.g., externalizing behavior) and promote resilience among adolescents, including those from primarily Latinx backgrounds (Ghahremani et al., 2013; Newman et al., 2020; Newman et al., 2024; Rovnaghi, et al., 2025). Designed to target negative affect and stress while fostering a sense of belonging and connection, the program takes a ‘whole child’ approach to student development by integrating an SEL curriculum with breathwork. Lessons are delivered through active experiences, such as games and physical activities, and facilitated dialogue, with the aim of making content engaging and accessible.

Core SEL lessons emphasize cognitive reframing (e.g., approaching challenges as adventures and responsibility as an opportunity to increase ability), stepping outside one's comfort zone, embracing commitment, and developing self‐regulation strategies for managing behavior and emotions (Newman et al., 2024). The curriculum also includes light physical exercise, yoga postures, and education in healthy lifestyle choices, including diet, sleep, substance use, and technology use, as well as communication, conflict resolution, and teamwork. Central to the program's ethos is the cultivation of community and a sense of belonging through structured group processes that foster empathy and acceptance.

The breathwork component is delivered in staged sequences, progressing from slow deep breathing to vigorous inhalations and exhalations, and culminating in rhythmic breathing cycles of varying speeds. Developmentally appropriate content and breathing practices are tailored across three grade bands: pre‐K to grade 2, grades 3 to 7, and grades 8 to 12. Instruction is delivered by trained SKY Schools instructors who monitor student practice to ensure adherence to the breathwork techniques. Instructors undergo a two‐week residential, intensive teacher training program and complete 80 hours of supervised instruction prior to eligibility to teach independently (ultimately determined by teacher trainers with years of experience). The manualized procedures are adhered to closely, and instructors receive an annual evaluation by teacher trainers to ensure fidelity of delivery. The instructors for this study were former school teachers and had extensive experience teaching this program to students from diverse backgrounds in high school settings. As long‐time practitioners of the breathing technique, they also had expert knowledge of breathwork from personal experience.

Cultural responsiveness is achieved through a diverse teaching team, an intentional focus on building authentic relationships, and student‐led discussions that center on students' lived experiences, which may include immigration, racism, online bullying, and substance use. Cross‐cultural competency training is mandatory for all SKY Schools staff, further reinforcing the program's commitment to cultural humility. Collectively, these elements position SKY Schools as a trauma‐informed, culturally responsive intervention that addresses mental health by building emotion regulation and SC through breathwork and SEL.

Procedure

Each of two high schools (~5 miles from each other) in the same Los Angeles County school district was randomly assigned to either receive the SKY Schools intervention or their usual physical education curriculum (control). This included standard school physical education activities, encompassing both individual exercises, such as calisthenics, track and field, and weight training, and team sports, including basketball, dodgeball, soccer, baseball, softball, and volleyball. The control group received the program after study completion. SKY Schools instructors led daily breathwork sessions in the classroom on school days (5 days per week) and closely monitored students for adherence. A brief five‐minute practice (three short techniques) occurred on four days with a longer 25 minutes SKY breathing session on one day.

Students were invited to participate in the study after a classroom presentation describing the study's goals, procedures, risks, and benefits. Parental informed consent was obtained by distributing bilingual (English/Spanish) permission forms for interested students to take home for parental review and signature. Parents were encouraged to contact the research team with any questions or concerns. All procedures were approved by the UCLA Office of the Human Research Protection Program (i.e., institutional review board, IRB) and the school district's IRB.

Measures

ACEs were captured using the Center for Youth Wellness Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaire (ACE‐Q) (Harris & Renschler, 2015). The ACE‐Q included both the traditional ACEs categories (abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction) as well as the expanded ACE categories (foster care, bullying, parental death, separation of the parent via deportation or migration, medical trauma, exposure to community violence, discrimination, personal incarceration, or personal domestic violence). A cumulative ACEs score consisted of the sum of ACEs experienced across both traditional and expanded ACEs categories, resulting in a possible score range of 0–17. Individuals with four or more ACES comprised the high ACEs group, and those with 3 or fewer were in the low ACEs group. SC was assessed using the Social Connectedness Scale‐Revised (SCS‐R) (example item: “I see people as friendly and approachable.”) (Lee et al., 2001), a 20‐item validated questionnaire requiring responses on a six‐point scale (from ‘strongly agree’ to ‘strongly disagree’). The SCS‐R demonstrates strong internal consistency (α = 0.88–0.93; Lee et al., 2001), with total scores ranging from 20 to 120 (calculated by summing all items after reverse‐scoring the 10 negatively worded items), where higher scores indicate a greater subjective sense of social belonging and connectedness to others, and lower scores reflect greater social isolation and psychological distress (Lee et al., 2001).

At Session 1 (baseline), 1 week prior to the four‐week SKY curriculum, participants completed demographic measures, the ACE‐Q, and baseline SCS assessments. SCS data were collected again immediately following the intervention (Session 2) and again after 8 weeks of in‐school daily breathwork (Session 3).

Data analytic plan

Data were analyzed using linear mixed‐effects models with participant as a random effect (lmer function, R version 4.21). SCS‐R total score served as the dependent variable. ACEs Level, Session, and Group were independent variables. Age, sex, teacher, and class period were included as covariates. Participants were excluded from analyses if they did not complete the ACE‐Q (13.4%). If a participant failed to respond to three or more of the 20 SCS items, data from that session were excluded from analyses (3.6% of sessions). Mean imputation was conducted in sessions with ≤2 missing SCS items (8.1% of intervention sessions and 6.1% of control sessions). Further, to ensure internal consistency and response validity, sessions with inconsistent responses on items that included similar statements were removed (Steinmann et al., 2022). For example, responses to the items “I don't feel I participate with anyone or any group” and “I have little sense of togetherness with my peers” were compared. If participants responded to these items with more than two points difference, that session was excluded (5.2% of sessions). Missing data points were handled by utilizing Maximum Likelihood estimation, as is typically used in linear mixed model estimation. Diagnostic checks were conducted to verify that assumptions of normality of residuals and homoscedasticity were satisfied.

RESULTS

Participants

The final sample included 130 participants in the SKY Schools intervention group and 44 in the control group, all of whom self‐identified as female (Table 1). Age varied significantly across groups and was included as a covariate in all analyses. The intervention group consisted of students from 12 classes taught by four school teachers; the control group contained students from 10 classes taught by four school teachers. There were no significant differences in the socioeconomic demographics of the two groups.

Effects of experimental group, ACEs, and session on SC

We found a significant main effect of ACEs level (high/low) on baseline SC, showing that those with high ACEs report lower baseline SC than those with low ACEs [F(1,147) = 20.86; p < .001; Table 2]. Importantly, we found a three‐way interaction of Group (intervention/control) × ACEs Level (high/low) × Session (1/2/3) [F(2, 264.13) = 4.88; p < .01; Figure 1], with Tukey's pairwise tests indicating a significant increase in SC from Baseline to Session 3 in those with high ACEs (t[258] = −2.57; p < .03; Cohen's D effect size = −0.38) with no significant effects of Session in the low ACEs group. The control group similarly showed no change (p > .8).

DISCUSSION

Integrating SEL and breathwork training (SKY Schools program) plus 2 months of daily breathwork boosted SC, a protective factor against ACEs‐related mental and physical health concerns (Arango et al., 2024; Landry et al., 2022; Weisblum et al., 2024), in female Latinx adolescents with high ACEs. SC showed a trend toward improvement following the four weeks of SEL/breathwork training, with gains becoming larger and statistically significant after 2 months of regular breathwork practice that followed.

Our results suggest that a cumulative, progressive effect of SEL and autonomic regulation (via breathwork) may enhance SC in these minoritized youth with high ACEs. The progressive nature of these effects, unfolding over 3 months, may reflect the time required for prosocial SEL skills to be internalized and applied within the context of sustained autonomic regulation via consistent breathwork practice.

These findings are especially relevant for Latinx adolescent females with high ACES given their tendency towards internalizing symptoms (Duke et al., 2010; Leban, 2021), including somatic complaints likely resulting from autonomic dysregulation (Der Sarkissian et al., 2022). The SKY Schools program is well‐suited for this population, with its emphasis on breathwork for autonomic regulation and the pro‐social nature of the curriculum. Additional evidence supporting the role of breathwork for populations affected by trauma comes from adults with post‐traumatic stress (PTS) showing long‐term reduction of PTS symptoms after participating in SKY breathwork programs (Bayley et al., 2022; Seppälä et al., 2014).

Latinx individuals, including youth, are less likely to seek mental health care than non‐Hispanic white individuals for various reasons (e.g., unfavorable attitudes towards mental health services; insufficient resources); therefore, discrete, accessible, and acceptable alternative strategies for supporting mental health are essential (Lu et al., 2021). The SKY Schools SEL/breathwork intervention is low‐cost and integrated as an activity during the school day, without the cultural stigma associated with students engaging mental health services.

Some limitations of the current study should be considered. Because of school scheduling limitations, balancing age and sample sizes across groups was not feasible; however, our results were controlled for age. Although an assessment of developmental stages (e.g., pubertal stage) would have been informative, this was not included due to classroom time constraints. We could not distinguish between the type of ACEs affecting participants – only the number of types. Future studies could examine how the intervention may differentially impact SC based on ACEs subtypes. With respect to our procedures for handling missing or inconsistent data, removal of sessions that didn't meet our criteria may have excluded those with attention issues or problems with task engagement, potential characteristics of individuals with ACEs. However, since a small proportion of sessions were eliminated, this likely introduced only a slight bias in our sample. Generalizability of the findings may be limited given that only two high schools participated. Future studies with multiple sites are recommended to examine adaptability of programming and consistency of results.

The effect of the SKY Schools program was likely due to the integration of the program's prosocial elements with autonomic regulation resulting from extended breathwork (Zaccaro et al., 2018); however, it was not possible to disentangle the impact of SEL from breathwork given the integration of the two within the program. The breathwork may have driven the impact on SC, as the effects emerged after 2 months of regular breathwork practice. A future “dismantling” study may distinguish the SEL and breathwork components of the intervention to determine the degree to which each promotes SC and in combination. Moreover, mixed methods studies would provide students' perspectives on how the shift towards greater SC occurs and what is needed to sustain it. Future studies may incorporate longer‐term follow‐up assessments to evaluate the sustainability of these effects and extend this work to other minoritized groups disproportionately affected by ACEs. The SKY Schools program's current implementation in over 200 schools in the US and in 14 countries underscores its potential for broad scalability across diverse cultural and educational contexts.

CONCLUSIONS

Our findings highlight the effectiveness of integrating SEL and breathwork within a school environment in promoting SC in female Latinx adolescents. Use of the intervention in a school setting increases its accessibility, thereby enhancing the likelihood of uptake among Latinx females during a socially fragile stage of development.
