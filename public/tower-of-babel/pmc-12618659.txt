Effects of gestational diabetes mellitus on the intestinal microbiota of the offspring
Authors: Ling Zhang, Xiao Tan, Zhiru Guo, Lixia Liang, Bin Yi, Xuhui Liu, Fei Li, Yunyun Wang, Yanxia Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) significantly impacts maternal and neonatal health, yet its influence on the neonatal intestinal microbiota remains poorly characterized. This study investigated the effects of maternal GDM on offspring microbiota by analyzing meconium samples from newborns of GDM-affected mothers (n = 16) and healthy controls (n = 16). Microbiome analysis revealed distinct composition alterations in the intestinal microbiota of GDM-exposed neonates. Significant differences in microbial abundance were observed between groups: at the phylum level, the GDM group exhibited a significant increase in Bacteroidetes but significant reductions in Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria. At the genus level, opportunistic pathogens including Bacteroides and Escherichia-Shigella were significantly enriched in the GDM group. These findings demonstrate that maternal GDM perturbs the initial colonization of the neonatal intestinal microbiota. Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), defined as an abnormal glucose tolerance first recognized during pregnancy, is one of the most common pregnancy complications1. With rising global incidence, GDM poses a significant threat to maternal health and offspring outcomes2. The occurrence of GDM during pregnancy increases the risk of gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, preterm labor, and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) later in life3. In addition, GDM increases the risk of perinatal macrosomia, respiratory distress, neonatal hypoglycemia, as well as metabolic syndrome, obesity, and T2DM later in childhood4,5. Given its transgenerational health implications, elucidating GDM’s pathophysiological mechanisms remains imperative6.

The intestinal microbiome - a complex ecosystem of approximately 100 trillion microorganisms - critically regulates host metabolism and immunity7. Dysbiosis in this community is implicated in metabolic disease pathogenesis8, with particular relevance to perinatal health. Maternal microbiota perturbations during gestation may vertically transmit to offspring, potentially influencing neonatal development9,10. A study noted that the maternal health may affect her microbiota during pregnancy and be passed vertically to her offspring11. The meconium microbiota are the first colonizers of the newborn, primarily originating from the maternal intestine, vagina, and skin12. They develop with the host after colonization13, and their presence is associated with immune and metabolic disorders12,14, emphasizing the potential for maternal transmission of microbiota. Early microbiota colonization is critical to the newborn’s metabolic health later in life. Some research suggested that GDM may alter the diversity and composition of the neonatal intestinal microbiota15, potentially leading to increased health risks, such as obesity, insulin resistance, and even diabetes in offspring. Manipulating the composition of the intestinal microbiota may be a promising approach to improving health outcomes in women with GDM and their offspring. However, the association between maternal GDM and the offspring’s intestinal microbiota remains controversial and requires further elucidation.

This study investigated maternal GDM’s effect on offspring intestinal microbiota composition through comparative analysis of first-pass meconium from neonates born to GDM-affected and healthy mothers.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

Pregnant women who gave birth in Gansu Maternal and Child Health Hospital from December 10, 2023, to January 11, 2024, and had regular obstetric examination were selected as the study participants. The participants were divided into two groups: 16 pregnant women diagnosed with GDM formed the GDM group and 16 pregnant women with normal blood glucose formed the healthy group. All participants underwent a standardized 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) at 24–28 weeks of pregnancy. GDM was diagnosed if any one of the following criteria were met: (a) fasting blood glucose between 5.1 mmol/L and 7.0 mmol/L; (b) 1-hour postprandial blood glucose ≥ 10.0 mmol/L; or (c) 2-hour postprandial blood glucose between 8.5 mmol/L and 11.1 mmol/L16. A structured questionnaire was used to collect demographic and potential risk factors data, including age, pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), and a history of adverse pregnancy outcomes, antibiotic exposure, delivery mode, feeding mode. In addition, information on gender, mode of delivery, birth weight, and height were extracted from the hospital’s information system. Inclusion criteria for the healthy group were pregnant women without underlying diseases, normal prenatal checkups, and normal pregnancy outcomes. Exclusion criteria included pre-pregnancy and pregnancy-related comorbidities, autoimmune diseases, genetic metabolic diseases, incomplete obstetric or clinical data, non-consent to participation, and poor compliance.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Gansu Provincial Maternity and Child-Care Hospital (REC 2022, GSFY 20). We confirmed that all experiments were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. We hereby affirm that a formal declaration was obtained to ensure that all participants enrolled in this study provided informed consent. Specifically, for infants, written informed consent was obtained from their legal guardians prior to participation.

Meconium collection, DNA extraction, and sequencing

Neonatal meconium samples (approximately 200 mg) were collected by trained nurses using sterilized diapers during the first few hours after birth in the delivery room. The samples were then stored in sterile Eppendorf tubes and frozen at −80℃ for later analysis. The samples were thawed at room temperature, and genomic DNA was extracted using the MagPure Soil DNA LQ Kit (Magan). DNA concentration and purity were assessed by using NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis. The extracted DNA was stored at −20℃. PCR amplification of the bacterial 16SrRNA gene was performed using the extracted genomic DNA as a template, with specific primers with Barcode and Takara Ex Taq high fidelity enzyme. Universal primers 343 F (5’-TACGGRAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 798R (5’-AGGGTATCTAATCCT-3’) were used to amplify the V3-V4 variable regions of 16SrRNA gene for bacterial diversity analysis.

Statistical analysis

Fisher’s exact tests and T-tests were employed for categorical variables and continuous variables respectively to compare demographic variables between the GDM and healthy group due to small sample sizes. Differences were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05. Alpha (α) diversity of microbiota was assessed by using the Shannon-Wiener diversity index (Shannon), Simpson diversity index (Simpson), Chao1 richness estimator (Chao1), and Abundance-based Coverage Estimator (ACE). Inter-group differences in microbial α-diversity metrics (Shannon, Simpson, Chao1, ACE) were statistically assessed via the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) using Bray-Jaccard and Unweighted-unifra distance matrices was used to assess the beta (β) diversity. In addition, the Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis was used to identify species with significant abundance differences between the two microbiota groups. Microbial taxa exhibiting significant differences between groups were identified with an absolute values of LDA score greater than 2. Finally, the Kruskal-Wallis test and Wilcoxon test were used to assess differences in species composition and community structure between the two groups.

Distribution of intestinal microbiota in neonates

The results showed that the dominant phyla in the fetal fecal samples of newborns from both groups of pregnant women were Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, Actinobacteria (Fig. 2A). Compared with the healthy group, the GDM group showed a significant increase in the relative abundance of the Bacteroidetes at the phylum level and a significant decrease in the relative abundance of the Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria (Fig. 2A). At the genus level, the dominant genera in the fetal fecal samples of newborns from both groups of pregnant women were Escherichia-Shigella, Muribaculaceae, Pelomonas, Bacteroides, and Lactobacillus. Bacteroides and Escherichia-Shigella in the GDM group showed higher abundance, whereas Pelomonas, Enterococcus, Streptococcus, and Prevotella showed reduced abundance (Fig. 2B). Notably, Bacteroidetes showed a significant increase in abundance in the GDM group (Fig. 2B). Based on the differences in microbiota species abundance between validation groups at the phylum and genus level, the top 15 genera with significant differences in abundance at the phylum and genus level were identified and plotted as clustered heat maps (Fig. 2D and E). As a result, a significant abundance difference was found between the GDM and Healthy group (P < 0.05).

Based on the genus level, bacteria with significant abundance differences in different groups of intestinal microbiotas were selected. The main genera enriched in the GDM group include NK4A214_group, f_Peptostreptococcaceae, o_Flavobacteriales, CAG_352, and Lachnospiraceae_UCG_010. The main genera enriched in the Healthy group included the genera f_Methylophilaceae, f_Rhodanobacteraceae, f_Weeksellaceae, g_Chujaibacter, and Elisobacter (Fig. 3).

Discussion

The rising global incidence of GDM heightens concerns about its long-term transgenerational impacts, including elevated risks of metabolic syndrome, obesity, and type 2 diabetes in offspring. Given the gut microbiota’s established role in metabolic regulation and insulin sensitivity8, our findings demonstrate that maternal GDM significantly alters the initial microbial colonization of neonates—highlighting maternal metabolic status as a critical determinant of early-life microbial programming.

We observed distinct phylum-level perturbations in GDM-exposed neonates, Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, Actinobacteria were the dominant phyla in fetal stool samples. Additionally, the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes phylum was increased in neonates from the GDM group, which is consistent with previous research15,17. GDM may increase the likelihood of cesarean section and antibiotic use, which could affect genera within the Actinobacteria phylum, thereby increasing the ecological niche for colonization by genera in the Bacteroidetes phylum12,18. The maternal environment, including nutrition and microbiota, affects the diversity of the microbiota in both the intestine and breast milk. These changes can be transferred to the offspring through delivery and breastfeeding, ultimately impacting neonatal microbial colonization and immune system maturation19. Previous studies have similarly shown that maternal GDM not only influences the diversity and abundance of gut microbial colonization in offspring, but may also impact the resilience of the offspring’s gut microbiota following early-life microbial exposure20.

Comparison of α-diversity indices between neonates of mothers with GDM and healthy controls revealed no statistically significant differences (all P > 0.05, Wilcoxon rank-sum test). This suggested that maternal GDM status may not substantially alter the overall richness and evenness of the neonatal gut microbiota. GDM induces specific taxonomic alterations rather than global community disruption, while functional consequences may persist even when structural homeostasis is maintained. However, due to the restricted sample size in this study (n = 16 per group), we cannot rule out this potential confounding effect. Multivariate PERMANOVA analysis revealed profound β-diversity shifts in neonates of GDM mothers. Unweighted Unifrac showed exceptionally strong separation (R²=0.789, P < 0.001), indicating that maternal GDM status reshapes the phylogenetic architecture of neonatal microbiota. Bray-Jaccard confirmed significant compositional differences (R²=0.179, P = 0.018), suggesting species turnover. Collectively, these results demonstrated that GDM exposure fundamentally restructured the neonatal gut microbiome, predominantly through altering the evolutionary relationships of rare taxa and modifying microbial colonization patterns.

At the genus level, GDM-exposed neonates showed significant enrichment of pro-inflammatory taxa (Escherichia-Shigella, Muribaculaceae, and Bacteroides). Previous studies have shown that the intestinal microbiota of pregnant women with GDM is enriched in genera such as Bacteroides and Escherichia. Maternal intestinal microbiota serves as an important source for colonizing the infant’s intestine18, and the GDM status may facilitate microbial transfer between the mother and the infant21. The intestinal microbiota plays a key role in various metabolic processes within the body, with an increased Bacteroides and Escherichia potentially having multifaceted effects on the health of the offspring. The increased presence of Escherichia not only affects nutrient absorption and metabolism in the intestinal tract, but also leads to an imbalance in the immune response, promotes inflammatory reactions, and heightens susceptibility to allergies and autoimmune diseases22,23. An overabundance of Shigella spp. can even lead to intestinal dysbiosis, causing intestinal inflammation and other digestive disorders. Research has shown that early-life exposure to Escherichia promotes prolonged colonization of the gut by Escherichia in offspring, particularly in the context of maternal GDM20. Overall, our study emphasizes the impact of GDM on the microbiota composition of offspring, which may be associated with the increased risk of future disease development.

There are some limitations in this study. First, perinatal confounders (antibiotic exposure, breastfeeding practices) were not analyzed due to data constraints17,24. Second, GDM is closely associated with intestinal dysbiosis in pregnant women6, the maternal intestinal microbiota were not sequenced in this study. Therefore, we could not observe trends in microbial differences between maternal and neonatal microbiota that could explain the intergenerational consistency of microbial changes associated with GDM. Addressing this limitation is a high priority for our future research. We are actively designing follow-up studies that explicitly include maternal microbiome sampling at key time points (e.g., pre-delivery, birth, and during the infant’s early life), which will allow us to directly investigate vertical transmission patterns, and provide the deeper biological understanding. Third, meconium is typically expelled and collected several hours after delivery, during which time the microbiota may acquire and multiply21. Therefore, collecting sample types (e.g., pharyngeal aspirates, saliva, etc.) from areas deep within the neonate, which can be collected immediately at birth, may be ideal for examining the initial microorganisms in offspring affected by GDM25. Fourth, methodological constraints merit acknowledgment: The restricted cohort size (n = 16/group) reduces sensitivity to detect subtle microbial alterations and elevates Type II error risk. Crucially, while this may obscure small-effect phenomena, large-effect signals (e.g., Unifrac R²>0.75) remain detectable. Future longitudinal mother-infant dyad analyses, metagenomic functional profiling, and multi-center cohorts (targeting n > 40/group) controlling for early-life covariates are needed to establish causal links between maternal GDM, microbial transmission, and offspring health outcomes and to validate large-effect signals in our study.

Conclusion

This study observed an association between maternal gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and alterations in the meconium gut microbiota of offspring, showcasing an enrichment of pro-inflammatory taxa (e.g., Escherichia-Shigella, Bacteroides) and a reduction of beneficial genera (e.g., Mycobacterium). These findings suggest that GDM may be a modulator of early-life microbial ecosystems, potentially influencing offspring metabolic and immune development. However, due to the acknowledged limitations of this study, including its sample size and the absence of maternal microbiota data, the observed associations warrant cautious interpretation and further validation. Future studies with larger cohorts, maternal microbiome profiling, and longitudinal designs are necessary to establish causality and elucidate the mechanisms of microbial transmission. Ultimately, a deeper understanding of these interactions could inform future strategies aimed at optimizing early microbial colonization and mitigating long-term health risks.
