Variability in crop responses as a function of environment affects the NDVI relationship with grain yield in wheat
Authors: Chamaporn Paiboonvorachat, Kirti Rajagopalan, Michael O Pumphrey, Arron H Carter, Chongyuan Zhang, Worasit Sangjan, Sindhuja Sankaran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvancing wheat breeding requires reliable digital traits that capture genotype × environment interactions and improve yield prediction across diverse growing conditions. Although vegetation indices such as the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) are widely used, their performance relative to yield variability and environmental stress remains underexplored in multi-environment trials. This study utilized unmanned aerial vehicle multispectral imagery to derive NDVI and assess its relationship with grain yield in 34 spring and winter wheat variety trials. These trials included data across seven Washington State locations in different precipitation zones, five years (2019 to 2023), and some irrigated trials. Environments were grouped into high-, moderate-, and low-stress clusters based primarily on precipitation and temperature. Variability was quantified using the coefficient of variation, and correlations between grain yield and NDVI were evaluated within and between varieties across environments based on market classes (hard and soft spring and winter wheat). Across all environments and varieties, NDVI strongly correlated with grain yield (r = 0.79–0.82, p < 0.001) in each market class. Stress-prone environments exhibited greater variability in both traits, and higher yield variability could be associated with higher NDVI variability, particularly in spring wheat (r = 0.72 in hard spring, r = 0.53 in soft spring). These conditions also improved discrimination between varieties. Although heritability patterns were not clearly differentiated by stress clusters, environments with higher genetic control of yield also tended to show stronger NDVI heritability. Overall, NDVI reliably captured wheat grain yield, which is governed by the genotype × environment driven variability, with its predictive value strongest in stress-prone conditions. These findings underline NDVI's usability as a practical digital trait for improving variety testing and guiding breeding decisions in challenging environments.Keywords: Wheat breeding, Variability, Wheat yield, Yield prediction, Phenomics, Heritability, Normalized difference vegetation index

Introduction

Traditional phenotyping approaches can evaluate important yield-related traits such as plant height, heading date, kernel weight, protein concentration, test weight, and kernel diameter in wheat (Triticum aestivum) [1,2]. However, these approaches remain labor-intensive and time-consuming — limiting the large-scale data collection needed for reliable yield prediction across multi-environment trials (METs) [3]. Phenomics offers a solution by enabling non-destructive, high-throughput monitoring of traits in large populations and across diverse environments [4,5]. In this regard, satellite and drone imaging systems are not only tools for data collection but also facilitate detailed evaluation of crop traits across growth stages, growth patterns, biomass accumulation, and plant health [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Vegetation indices (VIs) are derived from spectral measurements for quantitative assessment of crop traits. These indices can speed up the selection of environmentally resilient wheat cultivars [6,10].

Vegetation indices have become fundamental tools for phenotyping in modern wheat breeding programs. Numerous studies consistently show that the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) and other related indices—such as green normalized difference vegetation index (GNDVI) and enhanced vegetation index (EVI)—improve the accuracy of pedigree-based selection and grain yield predictions [8,[11], [12], [13]]. Several studies have also demonstrated improved yield prediction when combining VIs with other features. For example, Sun et al. [13] combined GNDVI with canopy temperature and genetic information to predict wheat grain yield using genomic selection models with an average improvement of 146%—increasing predictive ability from about 0.1 to 0.3 to nearly 0.6 in heat stress environments. Similarly, Naqvi et al. [12] accurately predicted pre-harvest wheat yield in a semi-arid region of Pakistan by using satellite-derived EVI (R2 = 0.89) and GNDVI (R2 = 0.82). Naghdyzadegan et al. [8] also reported improved yield predictive performance during the heading stage by combining multiple VIs (NDVI, EVI, EVI2) with evapotranspiration and temperature data, outperforming models based only on temperature. Recent studies further demonstrate the utility of various VIs for yield prediction.

Herr & Carter [6] and Montesinos-López et al. [7] reported strong correlations (r = 0.75–0.86) between winter wheat yield and indices like NDVI and Normalized Difference Red Edge index (NDRE), noting that spectral reflectance alone sometimes matched or surpassed other predictors. These studies also showed high heritability of these indices, reflecting high observed variation in a trait due to genetic differences rather than environmental influences [14]. Sangjan et al. [9] likewise found a strong correlation between VIs and wheat yield. In that study, vegetation indices such as the chlorophyll index green, modified chlorophyll absorption ratio index 2, and couple red-edge based vegetation indices (including NDRE) extracted from satellite and unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) imagery produced Pearson's correlation coefficients of up to 0.78 and up to 0.92 between the VI and grain yield, respectively. NDRE is often used in addition to NDVI, as it is less sensitive to leaf area index, especially when crops reach maturity [15]. It showed an increase in selection efficiency by an average of about 5% at different growth stages in Pacific Northwest winter wheat crop, especially during early grain fill and under high biomass conditions [16]. Altogether, these findings underscore the strong potential of VIs for predicting wheat yield and accelerating selection in breeding pipelines across spatial scales and environmental conditions.

Despite these successes, VIs do not always perform consistently. Many studies have predicted grain yield and other agronomic traits using NDVI and related VIs, with varying levels of success [[6], [7], [8],[11], [12], [13]]. In some experiments in a given year, these indices correlate strongly with yield and improve prediction accuracy, whereas at other times, the predictive performance of these indices can be weak (e.g. Ref. [9,17]). For example, Rutkoski et al. [11] reported low or even negative correlations between spectral traits and yield under severe drought, but strong correlations (r > 0.7) under heat stress. Herr & Carter [6] similarly observed substantial year-to-year variation in the relationship between wheat yield and NDVI, potentially due to both calibration issues and environmental differences. Montesinos-López et al. [7] applied cross-validation to evaluate soft white winter wheat breeding lines across environments, integrating genomic, phenomics, and environmental covariates for yield prediction. Although they achieved high prediction accuracies (r > 0.75) in tested environments, performance declined in untested sites, suggesting the ongoing need to further investigate the complexity of genotype × environment (G×E) interactions.

While many studies demonstrate that VIs can predict yield, the conditions in which the predictions are accurate are unclear. One likely explanation lies in weather factors. Multiple studies have shown that temperature variations and precipitation patterns during critical growth stages significantly influence crop performance and genetic selection [[18], [19], [20]]. These factors can account for as much as 60% of the variation in yield differences [2]. Moreover, most prior work has evaluated VIs in simplified controlled experiments with anticipated yield differences (e.g., irrigated vs. drought treatments). Very few studies have tested VIs across the heterogeneous, real-world environments typical of METs, even though such environments are crucial for identifying wheat varieties that can be successfully grown in different locations, years, and management systems [[21], [22], [23], [24]]. Thus, it remains unclear whether the predictive effectiveness of VIs is stable across environments or whether it depends on the favorability of growing conditions. Therefore, understanding why VIs can be strong predictors under certain circumstances but sometimes fail under others, and the reason for such inconsistencies, requires further investigation.

NDVI was primarily used for this analysis because of its proven performance in wheat yield prediction across various platforms, including satellite images [8,9,25], UAV images [9,26], and handheld reflectance measurement tools [26]. A central question is whether NDVI's effectiveness in predicting grain yield is affected by environmental favorability. We hypothesize that NDVI's predictive ability is conditioned by the degree of environmental stress. In favorable environments, where crop growth is relatively uniform across varieties, both grain yield and NDVI exhibit low data variability, lowering the predictability of grain yield using NDVI. In contrast, in unfavorable or stress-prone conditions, environmental constraints amplify the differences in crop responses, resulting from high variability in canopy growth and development, adaptation, and thus grain yield, which may strengthen the relationship between grain yield and NDVI.

Extreme environments might also result in low variability, impacting the relationship between NDVI and grain yield. To test this hypothesis, our study utilized multi-year, location, and irrigation treatments in wheat breeding trials with UAV-based NDVI measurements. This dataset allowed us to explore the complexity of G×E interactions and to assess the reliability of NDVI in capturing grain yield and its variability as a function of environments. By classifying environments into high-, moderate-, and low-stress categories, we evaluate whether stress increases the variability of grain yield and NDVI, and how this variability affects the grain yield-NDVI relationship. The specific objectives are: (1) to analyze the variability of grain yield and the NDVI within and between wheat varieties across environments, and (2) to determine whether changes in both traits’ variability across different stress-prone environments alter the strength and consistency of the grain yield-NDVI relationship.

Materials and methods

Field trials data and variety selection

Spring and winter wheat variety trials were conducted from 2019 to 2023 near seven locations in Washington State: Connell, Colton, Ritzville, Farmington, Lind, Othello, and Pullman (Fig. 1). These trials included breeding lines and widely grown commercial check varieties across four market classes (hard spring, soft spring, hard winter, and soft winter wheat). Experiments were conducted using a randomized complete block design with three replications. The plot sizes were typically 1.5 m wide and 5.0 to 6.0 m long. Row spacing was between 18 and 20 cm, with an approximately 1.2-to-1.5-m alleys between plots, depending on the site. These dimensions were consistent with regional breeding practices [9].

The individual environments across all locations are presented in the Supplementary Materials, Fig. S1.

Each environment was defined as a unique year–location–treatment combination, resulting in 34 environments overall (15 spring wheat and 19 winter wheat). In 2021, yield data were available only from winter wheat trials because extreme heat stress (the “heat dome") destroyed spring wheat trials before harvest. Trial data, including grain yield, variety, and market class information, were obtained from the Washington State University Extension Cereal Variety Selection and Testing Program (https://smallgrains.wsu.edu/variety/). A summary of the 34 environments, including location, year, coordinates, and the number of varieties and plots, is provided in Supplementary Materials, Table S1. The workflow of data acquisition, image processing, feature extraction, and statistical analysis is illustrated in Fig. 2.

UAV multispectral imagery was processed to derive plot-level NDVI. METs were grouped into three clusters of environmental conditions based on weather and management information. Statistical analyses assessed the variability in grain yield and NDVI across environments and how their relationship differs under contrasting conditions.

The within-variety responses across environments were examined with six common spring wheat and fourteen winter wheat cultivars/varieties. The six spring wheat varieties included: Alum (PVPO 201600077; U.S. Department of Agriculture [27], Glee (PI 666940 [28]), Kelse (PI 653842 [29]), Louise (PI 634865 [30]), Ryan (PVPO 201700204 [31]), and Seahawk (PVPO 201600078 [32]). The fourteen winter wheat varieties included: Appleby CL+ (PVPO 202000432 [33]), ARS-Crescent (PI 665048 [34]), Curiosity CL+ (PI 675007 [35]), LCS Artdeco (PVPO 201300198 [36]), LCS Hulk (PVPO 201800518 [37]), LCS Jet (PVPO 201600094 [38]), Norwest Duet (PVPO 201700199 [39]), Norwest Tandem (PVPO 201700198 [40]), Otto (PI 667557 [41]), Piranha CL+ (PI 699241 [42]), Resilience CL+ (PI 686413 [43]), WB4303 (PVPO 201600378 [44]), WB4311 (PVPO 201800096 [45]), and Xerpha (PI 645605 [46]).

Unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) data acquisition

Multispectral imagery was collected during the heading stage, which is identified as the growth stage most strongly correlated with crop performance traits such as yield and biomass [2,5] and has been shown to significantly improve the accuracy of wheat yield predictions [13,47]. Two multispectral sensors were employed for data collection. The first system utilized a 1.2-megapixel multispectral camera Red Edge-MX Dual camera (Micasense Inc., Seattle, WA, USA), which captured imagery in five spectral bands: blue (475 nm), green (560 nm), red (668 nm), red edge (740 nm), and NIR (842 nm). Flight missions were planned using Mission Planner, an open-source ground control station software package (ArduPilot Development Team and Community) and were flown in a single grid with 80% horizontal and 70% vertical image overlap. The sensor was mounted at a 90° nadir angle and operated at a flight speed of 3 m/s at 45 m above ground level, resulting in a ground sampling distance (GSD) of 3.1 cm per pixel.

The second system consisted of a quad multispectral sensor (Sentera, LLC., Minneapolis, MN, USA) mounted on a quadcopter UAV, capturing imagery in four spectral bands: blue (470 nm), green (550 nm), red (660 nm), and red edge (750 nm). Flights were conducted at 3 m/s in a single-grid pattern with 80% horizontal and 75% vertical overlap. The sensor was positioned at a 90° nadir angle and operated at 50 m above ground level, resulting in a spatial resolution of 2.2 cm per pixel. For radiometric calibration, a 99% reflectance panel (Spectralon® Diffuse Reflectance Targets, SRS-99-120, Labsphere Inc., North Sutton, NH, USA) was placed in the field during image acquisition and used in subsequent image processing steps [9]. Additional calibration procedures were performed using an 85% reflectance panel following established protocols [6,48].

Image processing and vegetation index extraction

Images were processed in Pix4Dmapper photogrammetry software (Pix4D S.A., Lausanne, Switzerland) for radiometric correction and orthomosaic generation. Plot shapefiles were digitized in Quantum Geographic Information System (QGIS, version 3.26) based on trial maps from the WSU Wheat & Small Grains database (https://smallgrains.wsu.edu/variety/archives/) and utilized the GRID tool and Advanced Digitizing Tools to create precise polygon Shapefiles. These polygons were aligned with the original plot maps and matched with ground truth data to facilitate VI extraction.

NDVI has a range from −1 to 1, with values typically between 0.2 and 0.8 used to effectively identify green vegetation cover [49]. Soil-adjusted vegetation index (SAVI) includes a soil adjustment factor (L = 0.5) designed to address variations in soil background based on vegetation density [50]. Weather data and environmental clustering

Historical meteorological data were sourced from gridMET ([52]; https://www.climatologylab.org/gridmet.html), a gridded dataset that combines temporal attributes of reanalysis products with spatial attributes of the PRISM dataset. This approach produces a spatial resolution of 4 km and temporally complete record of surface climatic variables that has been validated using an extensive network of automated weather stations in the western United States. Daily precipitation, temperature, solar radiation, and humidity data were extracted using Python 3.12 and aggregated for each growing season, defined as September of the previous year through August (harvest month) of the current year, across five growing seasons (2019–2023).

Environments with similar weather patterns were grouped using k-means clustering algorithm — an unsupervised machine learning algorithm that partitions data into clusters by iteratively updating centroids and minimizing within-cluster variance [53] — to explore the variability and relationship between grain yield and NDVI across environmental clusters. Clustering variables and time windows were selected based on expert judgment to reflect the distinct environmental requirements of each wheat type (spring and winter). For spring wheat, clustering variables included cumulative precipitation (including fall precipitation, which is one of the critical water sources in rainfed regions), and mean and maximum temperature during the growing period (April–August). For winter wheat, additionally, minimum temperature during the dormancy period (September–March) was added to account for potential freeze damage. The optimal number of clusters was determined using the elbow method, which identified three distinct environmental groups. Since the Othello location included multiple irrigation treatments, its environments were manually reassigned by comparing irrigation levels with the precipitation patterns of each cluster. Finally, the clusters were reviewed and confirmed by breeders based on their experience.

Results

Overall relationship between grain yield and NDVI

Across all wheat varieties and METs, grain yield showed a significant positive correlation with NDVI (Fig. 3). Analyzing each wheat market class (hard or soft spring and winter wheat) separately resulted in consistently high Pearson's correlation coefficients, ranging from 0.79 to 0.82 (p < 0.001). Given the broad distribution of grain yield (265.64 to 11,116.60 kg ha−1), this strong relationship demonstrates that NDVI reliably captures crop performance resulting from diverse genetic backgrounds and environments. Hard and soft spring wheat showed the same correlation coefficient (r = 0.82) with similar scatter patterns, despite the differences in the market class grain quality. In general, hard spring wheat has higher protein than soft spring wheat based on desired end-use quality [60]. The average protein content in the hard and soft spring wheat was about 15% and 12%, while in the hard and soft winter wheat was about 13% and 11%, respectively. At heading, both hard and soft spring wheat would reach their maximum growth and development, after which the resources will be shifted to grain development. Hard winter wheat had a slightly lower correlation (r = 0.79), though comparable. Although NDVI appeared to be a strong predictor of overall wheat yield, environmental clusters appeared to exhibit variable associations between grain yield and NDVI. This observation further led us to dive into whether different environments, characterized by varying levels of stress, could influence the strength of the relationship between these two traits.

Relationship between grain yield and NDVI across wheat varieties (dots with same color indicate different varieties within same environment) and environments (represented in color) for each wheat market class. Pearson's correlation coefficients indicate strong positive relationships (r = 0.82) for spring (hard and soft) and soft winter wheat and slightly lower correlation for hard winter wheat (r = 0.79), with all relationships significant at p < 0.001.

Cluster 1 represented high-stress environments characterized by low annual precipitation and high temperatures. Cluster 2 included moderately stressful conditions with intermediate precipitation and temperature levels. Variability and the relationship between grain yield and NDVI across varieties and environments

In general, grain yield exhibited greater variability than NDVI across all environments. For spring wheat, the coefficients of variation (CV) for yield reached 32.08% for hard spring wheat and 22.49% for soft spring wheat, while the variability for NDVI remained lower, at less than 11.22%. At the same time, winter wheat showed lower variability compared to spring wheat (yield CV ≤ 18.24%; NDVI CV ≤ 8.97%). Supplementary Materials, Table S3 presents the complete range of CV of grain yield and NDVI at each environment. Although previous studies have reported that VIs do not predict grain yield well under some extreme conditions [11], our dataset does not include environments with extremely severe weather (e.g., in 2021), because wheat plants were sparse with few or no harvestable heads. These METs were therefore unavailable for analysis. Among the included environments, Cluster 1 represents the highest stress relative to the other two clusters (Cluster 2 and Cluster 3). In general, the NDVI variability was higher in Cluster 1 (the high-stress environment) and lower in Cluster 3 (low-stress environment). The variation for each environment is illustrated in Supplementary Materials, Fig. S2 for spring wheat and Fig. S3 for winter wheat. While the CV values were lower in winter wheat than in spring wheat, a similar pattern was observed— NDVI showed more variability in higher stress conditions, and lower variability in lower stress conditions. It is important to note that variability within some environment trials—such as those in Othello—may appear lower because of fewer varieties (max. 8) evaluated in those trials. When we consider multiple METs within each cluster, the pattern of high stress environments consistently showing higher variability — particularly in NDVI (sometimes in grain yield as well – e.g. soft and hard spring wheat)— compared with the low stress environments, becomes clearer.

In most cases, environments with higher grain yield variability also exhibited higher variability in NDVI (Supplementary Materials, Fig. S4). This relationship was significant in hard spring wheat (r = 0.72, p < 0.001), soft spring wheat (r = 0.53, p < 0.001), and soft winter wheat (r = 0.54, p < 0.001). In hard winter wheat, the relationship was weak and non-significant (r = 0.21, p = 0.528). These results suggest that environments with higher yield variability also tend to show higher variability in NDVI, particularly under stressful conditions such as those observed in Lind dryland locations. Consistent with the variability analysis, the strength of the yield–NDVI relationship varied across weather clusters (Fig. 4). In Cluster 1 (high stress) and Cluster 2 (moderate stress), correlations between yield and NDVI were significant and stronger than in Cluster 3 (low stress) in both spring and winter wheat. The stronger relationship between NDVI and grain yield in Cluster 1 suggests that incorporating some degree of environmental stress into trial conditions can enhance the ability of NDVI to distinguish varietal differences, particularly as the G×E interactions become pronounced under such conditions.

Correlation between grain yield and NDVI across environmental clusters. Higher correlations were observed in high- and moderate-stress environments (Cluster 1 and 2) compared with lower-stress environments (Cluster 3).

In a given environment, the spatial variability between varieties across plots was higher than the variability computed from the average of three replicate plots of a given variety (Supplementary Materials, Table S3). For example, in the 2023 Lind hard spring wheat trial, the CV of grain yield and NDVI were 32.08% and 11.22%, respectively, while the spatial variability was 44.51% and 15.91%, respectively. Since three to four replicate plots were used per variety, the number of plots analyzed was approximately three times the number of varieties in each market class (Supplementary Materials, Table S1), providing a larger dataset for plot-level correlation analysis. Nevertheless, the correlation between grain yield and NDVI at plot level (r = 0.78–0.80, p < 0.001, Supplementary Materials, Fig. S5) remained high and significant, similar to the variety-level analysis (Fig. 3).

Evaluating the variability and relationships between grain yield and NDVI of common wheat varieties

The variability and relationship between grain yield and NDVI at the plot level were examined using common varieties cultivated across most environments. This approach allowed us to determine whether the variability and trait correlations observed across environments and clusters can also be observed within a single genetic background. When examining within individual varieties, all selected common varieties showed significant positive correlations (p < 0.001) between grain yield and NDVI across environments (Fig. 5). Detailed coefficient of variation (%CV) for grain yield and NDVI by variety and environment are provided in Supplementary Materials, Table S4–S7. In general, the variability in both traits was high in Cluster 1. For spring wheat, Fig. 6 illustrates that Clusters 1 and 2 exhibited higher %CV in both grain yield and NDVI, compared to Cluster 3.

In contrast, this pattern was less pronounced in winter wheat (Supplementary Materials, Fig. S6). Although winter wheat also showed variability in both traits, the differences in %CV among the three clusters were relatively small for most of the common varieties included in this analysis. This suggests that winter wheat varieties were generally more stable under varying environmental conditions, which may be attributed to their longer growth and development period [[61], [62], [63]]. Having shown that high stress environments exhibit higher variability and strong correlation between grain yield and NDVI across environments and within individual wheat varieties, we next evaluated whether these environmental patterns are reflected in the underlying genetic signals. Therefore, in the following section, the broad-sense heritability (H2) was estimated for each environment.

Heritability

Broad-sense heritability (H2) estimates for grain yield and NDVI for each environment are summarized in Supplementary Materials, Table S8. Unlike the variability results, heritability did not show significant differentiation between the three weather clusters. The average H2 values remained relatively consistent across these clusters, showing no clear pattern of increase or decrease with stress level (Supplementary Materials, Fig. S7). This observation suggests that stress levels alone did not significantly influence the magnitude of heritability for either trait. However, a closer comparison of the heritability estimates of grain yield and NDVI in different market classes revealed important contrasts (Fig. 7). In hard spring wheat, environments with lower yield heritability also tended to have lower NDVI heritability, resulting in a strong positive correlation between the heritability of the two traits (r = 0.82, p = 0.043). This suggests that in environments where genotypic differences had a more significant influence on grain yield expression, NDVI expression was also under stronger genetic control in hard spring wheat.

Relationship between heritability (H2) of grain yield and NDVI across environments. In hard spring wheat, yield and NDVI heritability were significantly correlated (r = 0.82, p = 0.043). Positive but non-significant relationships were observed in hard and soft winter wheat.

In soft spring wheat, however, the relationship was absent, as the correlation between grain yield and NDVI heritability was near zero and non-significant. This contrast indicates that the genetic determinants affecting grain yield and NDVI in soft spring wheat may work separately, or that environmental noise plays a strong role in masking their genetic influence. For winter wheat, the correlations were positive but weaker and not significant, suggesting an inconsistent relationship between the genetic control of the two traits.

Comparison of NDVI and NDRE

NDRE had a strong correlation with NDVI (r = 0.89, p < 0.001) overall, with weather cluster wise correlation ranging from 0.55 to 0.88 between the two indices (Supplementary Material, Fig. S8). The correlation between NDVI and NDRE in each environment was significant (p < 0.001) with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.47 to 0.99 (Data not shown). When analyzing the correlation between NDRE and grain yield (Supplementary Materials, Fig. S9) based on weather-based environmental clusters, NDRE demonstrated stronger correlations with grain yield than NDVI (Fig. 4), particularly in winter market classes in more favorable weather conditions (Cluster 2 and 3). For instance, hard and soft winter wheat in Cluster 2 showed a correlation coefficient of 0.97 and 0.91, respectively. In general, NDRE performed well under medium stress conditions (Cluster 2), while NDVI performed better in higher stress environment (Cluster 1). Often NDRE is found to be more sensitive to chlorophyll content [64] and can perform better than NDVI in conditions that limit its applicability. Kanke et al. [15] reported that NDVI has limited applications when crops reach canopy closure or when the canopy vigor and leaf area index are high. Further assessment of other near infrared and red-edge-based indices is necessary to validate these findings.

Discussion

Variability and G×E interaction effects and heritability patterns

Vegetation indices are good predictors of grain yield if the environments are conducive to producing differences in crop performance (Fig. 3). NDVI shows a strong correlation with grain yield across different market classes if there is yield variability resulting from stress. Variation in NDVI–grain yield relationships across clusters reveals the impact of weather on the crop performance in open environments. Cluster 1, with low rainfall and high temperatures, showed limited canopy growth and higher NDVI variability, increasing its sensitivity to grain yield variation. On the other hand, Cluster 3, characterized by high rainfall and low temperatures, resulted in excessive vegetative growth, disrupting the relationship between NDVI and grain productivity and making the relationship unclear (potentially saturating NDVI signals), especially in winter wheat. A complete list of environments for each cluster is provided in Supplementary Materials, Table S2. In stress environments, both grain yield and NDVI variability increase, indicating that stress amplifies G×E interaction-driven differences among varieties. This increased variability strengthens the relationship between grain yield and NDVI and supports our initial hypothesis that environmental stress enhances trait variability and improves the ability of NDVI to predict grain yield. Our results, aligning with previous studies, show that yield variability in rainfed production systems — where water and heat stress are prevalent — is more than in irrigated or otherwise favorable environments. High precipitation, characteristic of favorable environments, can mitigate the negative effects of rising temperatures [57,65], thereby reducing yield variability. Favorable conditions, such as optimal temperatures and sufficient water, can often mask genetic variation and lead to uniformly high performance [66]. Christopher et al. [67] reported that the relationship between grain yield and NDVI was noticeably stronger in well-watered and moderately stressed environments than in extremely stressed environments (with rainout shelter), which had a lower yield variability.

Moreover, Macholdt et al. [68] found that winter crops typically exhibit less variability than spring crops due to their more extended growth period, better access to winter precipitation, and potentially deeper roots. This observation is consistent with our results, in which winter wheat showed lower variability than spring wheat across environment clusters. Notably, our results suggest that the degree to which variability increases under stress differs by wheat market classes. Although heritability values were similar across stress levels, previous studies have found higher heritability for yield and digital traits (water-based/vegetation indices and canopy temperature) under drought and heat stress [11,13,69]. Thus, stress conditions allow different genotypes to show their unique stress-response traits, enhancing their genetic adaptability [70]. It should be noted that the number of genotypes/varieties tested in a given trial will also influence the variability, correlation, and heritability analysis.

Predictive value of NDVI and implication for breeding and yield prediction

While NDVI as a reliable digital trait for wheat yield prediction ([12,13]; Saad et al., 2022; [6,7,9]), its performance is not consistent in all environments. In lower-stress environments with sufficient precipitation and cooler temperatures, NDVI values frequently reached above 0.8 (Fig. 3), reducing their variability and weakening predictive power. This saturation, also noted by Apsparuth et al. (2025), occurs when high canopy density makes it difficult for the NDVI-based technique to detect subtle but agronomically meaningful differences among crop varieties. Therefore, NDVI alone may be less informative under favorable and high productivity conditions. Extreme stress (as seen in the 2021 field season) can similarly lead to a reduction in variability, as all varieties will experience difficulty adapting to the extreme weather (data not available). This, in turn, will impact the relationship (potentially lower correlation) between grain yield and NDVI, particularly when uniformly low yields limit differences among varieties and NDVI values are influenced by increased soil exposure rather than canopy variation. In high-stress environments such as the Lind dryland area, both grain yield and NDVI presented greater variability, and their correlation was stronger, making NDVI especially valuable for variety selection. These findings are consistent with previous research, showing that vegetation indices such as NDVI, GNDVI, water-based indices, canopy temperature, and stomatal conductance tend to exhibit stronger relationships with yield under heat-stress and water-limited environments [11,69]. These results emphasize that some environmental stress can be beneficial in breeding trials because it reveals genotypic differences in both yield and digital traits. Under such conditions, NDVI becomes a particularly effective tool for capturing yield variability and helping breeders improve selection accuracy. Overall, our findings confirm the value of incorporating stress-prone environments into breeding trials to improve identification of resilient wheat varieties.

NDRE and NDVI were both effective at predicting grain yield at the heading stage, but they performed differently across environments. As reported by Lozada et al. [16], NDRE was better than NDVI for winter wheat in the Pacific Northwest. Our findings support the findings, showing that NDRE performed better in low-stress conditions (Clusters 2 and 3), especially in winter wheat compared to spring wheat in most cases. Winter wheat often has higher biomass and canopy vigor than spring wheat due to its longer growth cycle and extended growth period. Given that NDVI and NDRE capture different information of canopy characteristics, NDRE can complement NDVI in estimating wheat yield potential.

Limitations and future directions

While the strong relationship between grain yield and NDVI across contrasting environments was demonstrated, several limitations should be acknowledged. The correlations within individual varieties were limited, largely due to the small sample size, particularly in irrigated trials. Likewise, heritability results were less consistent across the weather stress-based clusters than the variability patterns suggested, likely reflecting the complexity of G×E interactions that cannot be fully explained by clustering environments only by precipitation and temperature. Additionally, NDVI saturation in high-productivity and high-biomass environments highlights the importance of integrating complementary traits that remain sensitive at later growth stages. Future research should therefore combine NDVI with other traits, such as canopy temperature, stomatal conductance, and hyperspectral features, to improve yield prediction under both favorable and stress environments. Integrating genomic and physiological data is also promising; multi-omics approaches have been shown to enhance predictive accuracy across diverse environments [[5], [6], [7]]. These integrated models could ultimately improve genotype selection and accelerate the identification of resilient wheat varieties. In this study, though data from 34 different environments were included, a larger dataset would enhance the findings, given the different classes, number of varieties, and increase in seasonal variability experienced in Pacific Northwest and other regions of the world.

Conclusion

Digital traits, represented here by NDVI, demonstrated significant and robust predictive capability for wheat grain yield across multi-environment trials and across wheat market classes. Correlations between grain yield and NDVI remained strong within and among varieties, with the predictive power particularly pronounced in high-stress environments characterized by higher temperatures and limited precipitation, especially in spring wheat. Under these conditions, grain yield and NDVI values have more variability, indicating genotype-specific differences in stress adaptation — confirming that weather conditions in open field environments (Supplementary Materials, Table S2) are a key moderating factor affecting crop performance and predictive reliability of digital traits. Contrary to this, in less stressful conditions with sufficient moisture availability, trait stability and NDVI exceedingly above 0.8 reduced variability and, consequently, decreased the accuracy of grain yield prediction. This relationship between grain yield and NDVI provides breeders with a valuable indicator to identify variety differences, particularly in stress-prone environments where trait variation is maximized and G×E interactions are most evident. To build upon these findings, integrating NDVI with other digital traits, such as canopy temperature and hyperspectral indices, along with genomic data, will further improve the performance of yield prediction across diverse environments. Such multi-trait, multi-omics approaches will significantly accelerate the development of resilient wheat varieties that can adapt to rapidly changing environments.
