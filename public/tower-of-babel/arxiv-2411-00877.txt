Revisiting the Schedule Graph Generation for the Exact and Sustainable Analysis of Non-preemptive Scheduling
Marek Vlk, Marek Jaroš, Zdeněk Hanzálek

Abstract
This paper addresses the problem of scheduling non-preemptive tasks with release jitter and execution time variation on a uniprocessor. We show that the schedulability analysis based on schedule graph generation, proposed by Nasri and Brandenburg [RTSS 2017], produces negative results when it could be easily avoided by slightly reformalizing the notion of non-work-conserving policies. In this work, we develop a schedulability analysis that constructs the schedule graph using new job-eligibility rules and is exact and sustainable for both work-conserving and enhanced formalization of non-work-conserving policies. Besides, the experimental evaluation shows that our schedulability analysis is substantially faster.



keywords
online scheduling, release jitter, schedulability analysis, schedule abstraction graph

1
Introduction

Ensuring the temporal correctness of safety-critical real-time systems is a fundamental challenge, often addressed through response-time analysis (RTA). The primary objective of RTA is to ascertain the worst-case response time (WCRT) for a set of jobs scheduled by a given policy on computing resources. A real-time system is schedulable if the WCRT of each job does not exceed its deadline. However, the complexity of the RTA problem escalates, particularly for periodic tasks scheduled by a job-level fixed-priority policy, even on a uniprocessor platform, rendering most variations of the problem
\mathcal{NP}
-hard
Ekberg (2020)
or co-
\mathcal{NP}
-hard
Baruah and
Goossens (2004)
. This computational intractability not only poses formidable theoretical challenges but also hampers the practical application of these scheduling policies in real-world safety-critical systems. As such, there is a pressing need to delve into innovative approaches that not only address the inherent complexity but also pave the way for more efficient and scalable analyses, thereby facilitating the dependable design and implementation of safety-critical real-time systems.

While there are efficient algorithms for scheduling preemptive tasks
Park and Cho (2004)
, the difficulty of scheduling non-preemptive tasks with release jitter and execution time variation lies in the fact that it may exhibit anomalies, i.e., the scenario deemed worst-case does not result in a deadline miss, but a different scenario does. A schedulability analysis is sustainable if any problem instance deemed schedulable by the analysis is schedulable not only for the worst-case but for all conceivable scenarios
Baruah and Burns (2006)
.

1.1
Related Work

The conventional methods employed for sustainable schedulability analyses involve a pessimistic over-approximation of the true worst-case scenario with contrived situations that may be unrealistic at runtime. For instance, in the traditional RTA applied to non-preemptive fixed-priority scheduling
Davis et al. (2007)
;
Tindell et al. (1994)
, the bound on the response time of a task is derived assuming that, in the worst case, a job experiences maximal blocking from lower-priority jobs and maximal interference from higher-priority jobs, which may be overly pessimistic for periodic tasks.

On the contrary, the potential for exact and sustainable analysis results is offered by alternative approaches that are based on model checking (e.g.,
Guan et al. (2007)
;
Sun and Lipari (2016)
) or exhaustive exploration of all system states
Burmyakov et al. (2015)
;
Baker and Cirinei (2007)
;
Bonifaci and
Marchetti-Spaccamela (2012)
. Nonetheless, these approaches grapple with significant state-space explosion issues, making them impractical even for small problem instances.

Recently, a new branch of research emerged, centered around the concept of the schedule graph
Nasri and Brandenburg (2017)
. The schedule graph (or schedule abstraction graph) abstracts the space of available decisions that can be taken by a given scheduler when dispatching a set of jobs onto a resource. This exploration of decision space involves constructing a combinatorial graph where nodes correspond to the state of the resource after the execution of a set of jobs, and whose edges correspond to possible scheduling decisions (which is to determine a job to be processed next) that shape the transition of the system states. Throughout the construction of the graph, the response time of each job dispatched along an edge is examined. Upon completing the graph, the method thus yields the minimum and maximum response time for each job across all scenarios.

Since their seminal work on a uniprocessor
Nasri and Brandenburg (2017)
, the notion of the schedule graph has been extended to global scheduling (multiple processors)
Nasri et al. (2018)
and global scheduling with precedences
Nasri et al. (2019)
;
Nogd et al. (2020)
. The methodology has been improved by a so-called partial order reduction, which is applicable to the problems both with
Ranjha et al. (2022)
and without
Ranjha et al. (2023)
precedences.

A key strength of the schedule graph lies in its ability to accommodate non-work-conserving policies, known for their significant schedulability advantages
Nasri and Kargahi (2014)
;
Nasri and Fohler (2016)
. However,
Nasri and Brandenburg (2017)
is the only work addressing the non-work-conserving policies using the schedule graph. The authors devised a schedule graph generation algorithm that is exact and sustainable for both work-conserving and non-work-conserving policies. Despite these claims, our investigation has revealed instances where the schedulability analysis used with non-work-conserving policies produces negative results when it could be easily avoided by slightly reformalizing the notion of non-work-conserving policies.

Problem Description

This section gives a formal description of the problem.
All variables defined in Section
2
are integers. All proposed algorithms in later sections also work with integer values only.

2.1
Definition of Tasks and Jobs

The problem is given by a set of tasks
\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{T}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{n})
, where each task
\mathcal{T}_{i}
is specified by period
\tau_{i}
, the earliest release time
r^{min}_{i}
, the latest release time
r^{max}_{i}\geq r^{min}_{i}
, best-case execution time
C^{min}_{i}
, worst-case execution time
C^{max}_{i}\geq C^{min}_{i}
, deadline
d_{i}
and priority
p_{i}\geq 0
.

Each task
\mathcal{T}_{i}
consists of a set of jobs
J_{i,1},\dots,J_{i,h_{i}}
, where
J_{i,j}
is the j-th job of task
\mathcal{T}_{i}
and
h_{i}
is the last job of task
\mathcal{T}_{i}
in the
observation interval
. Each job
J_{i,j}
is specified by the following parameters that are inferred from task
\mathcal{T}_{i}
as follows. The priority
p_{i,j}=p_{i}
, the earliest release time
r^{min}_{i,j}=r^{min}_{i}+(j-1)\cdot\tau_{i}
, the latest release time
r^{max}_{i,j}=r^{max}_{i}+(j-1)\cdot\tau_{i}
, best-case execution time
C^{min}_{i,j}=C^{min}_{i}
, worst-case execution time
C^{max}_{i,j}=C^{max}_{i}
and deadline
d_{i,j}=d_{i}+(j-1)\cdot\tau_{i}
.

In practice, tasks generate infinite sequences of jobs, that is why we need to work with the observation interval, which is the representative timeframe in which the release pattern of the set of tasks
\mathcal{T}
repeats. It must satisfy the requirement that if the set of tasks is proved to be schedulable on the observation interval, then the set of tasks is also schedulable on the infinite horizon. Since the work we are revising
Nasri and Brandenburg (2017)
is described technically for a non-periodic finite set of jobs and since finding the smallest possible observation interval is a very complex problem, it stays out of the scope of this paper.

2.2
Scheduling of Jobs

The base of the problem is online scheduling of a set of jobs defined by a set of tasks
\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{T}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{n})
on a uniprocessor.

During run time, each job
J_{i,j}
is released at a priori unknown time
r_{i,j}\in[r^{min}_{i,j},r^{max}_{i,j}]
. This behavior is referred to as
release jitter
. Once job
J_{i,j}
is released, the value
r_{i,j}
becomes known. If job
J_{i,j}
is started at time
t
, then it occupies the processor during interval
[t,t+C_{i,j})
where
C_{i,j}\in[C^{min}_{i,j},C^{max}_{i,j}]
is a priori unknown execution time. This behavior is referred to as
execution time variation
. If job
J_{i,j}
finishes execution at time
t+C_{i,j}>d_{i,j}
, then the online scheduler yields a
deadline miss
.

Additionally,
J_{i,j}
is
finished
if it has been executed, i.e., it was picked by the online scheduler and then occupied the processor for
C_{i,j}
units of time. A job is
unfinished
if it is not finished yet. A job
J_{i,j}
is
certainly released
at time
t
if
r^{max}_{i,j}\leq t
and
possibly released
at time
t
if
r^{min}_{i,j}\leq t<r^{max}_{i,j}
.

We define a set of
applicable jobs
as a set of jobs that contains the first unfinished job of each task, i.e., a set of applicable jobs
J^{A}
contains jobs
J_{i,j}
that satisfy:
J_{i,j}
is unfinished
\wedge\;(j=1~\vee~J_{i,j-1}
is finished
)
. Notice that the set of applicable jobs is calculated from the set of finished jobs. Also note that the set of applicable jobs may contain at most
n
jobs, one from each task, and may also be empty, which happens when all jobs are finished.

2.3
Scheduling Policies

Scheduling policy is a function
\Pi(t,J^{A})
which for a given set of applicable jobs
J^{A}
and time
t
returns a job that should be scheduled next. The policy can also return
null
, which can happen if the set
J^{A}
is empty or if the non-work conserving policy chooses to idle until some unreleased job releases.

It is assumed that the scheduling overhead resulting from the run time of an online scheduler executing a scheduling policy is insignificant and is therefore ignored. The priority value
p
of tasks and jobs is used only in some scheduling policies. Tasks with the numerically smallest value of
p
have the highest priority.

We will work with the following scheduling policies. The Earliest Deadline First (EDF) picks the job with the earliest deadline, i.e., smallest
d_{i,j}
, out of all released applicable jobs. Fixed Priority - EDF (FP-EDF) picks the job with the highest priority, breaking ties by taking the job with the earliest deadline. Precautious FP-EDF (P-FP-EDF) works like FP-EDF but avoids picking a job with a priority lower than the highest one if it may cause a deadline miss for some applicable job of the highest priority. It is assumed that all tasks have unique identification numbers which are used in scheduling policies to break ties to keep the policies deterministic. The scheduling policies are elaborated in more detail in
6
.

2.4
Execution Scenarios

For a set of tasks
\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{T}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{n})
, an
execution scenario

\gamma=(C,R)
is a set of execution times
C=(C_{1},\dots,C_{n})
and release times
R=(R_{1},\dots,R_{n})
, where
C_{i}=(C_{i,1},\dots,C_{i,h_{i}})
,
R_{i}=(r_{i,1},\dots,r_{i,h_{i}})
,
C_{i,j}\in[C^{min}_{i,j},C^{max}_{i,j}]
and
r_{i,j}\in[r^{min}_{i,j},r^{max}_{i,j}]
. In other words, an execution scenario specifies the release time and execution time for every job of every task in
\mathcal{T}
. The values of
r_{i,j}
and
C_{i,j}
are unknown a priori and are revealed during the run time execution. Note that for a given execution scenario
\gamma
and policy
\Pi
, the times when the jobs start their execution are deterministic.

Given policy
\Pi
, a set of tasks
\mathcal{T}
is
schedulable
if there exists no execution scenario resulting in a deadline miss for an online scheduler that uses policy
\Pi
. On the contrary, a set of tasks
\mathcal{T}
is
non-schedulable
if some execution scenario results in a deadline miss.

3
Schedulability Analysis

In this section, we propose a new algorithm for schedulability analysis, which is an extension of
Nasri and Brandenburg (2017)
such that it is exact and sustainable also for enhanced formalization of non-work-conserving policies. We refer to our new algorithm as SGA-ME. SGA-ME also leverages the schedule graph generation, but what is completely different are the eligibility rules for adding new vertices in the expansion phase. Also, thanks to these new definitions of eligibility rules, SGA-ME is significantly faster than SGA-SE.

First, we focus on the basics of the schedule graph. We then provide a rough description of the generation algorithm and a step-by-step illustrative example of graph generation. The rules for schedule graph generation are described in detail afterward.

3.1
Schedule Graph

The schedule graph is a directed acyclic graph, in which each arc corresponds to the execution of some job and each vertex
v_{k}
corresponds to a set of finished jobs (determined by the arcs on the path from the root vertex to
v_{k}
). Besides, each vertex
v_{k}
contains a range of times
[e_{k},l_{k}]
, during which the last job in the set of finished jobs may finish.
In other words, the processor may become available at any time point from the interval
[e_{k},l_{k}]
.

The graph starts with root vertex
v_{0}
with no finished jobs and a range of times
[0,0]
. The schedule graph is gradually built from the root vertex up to a graph that encapsulates all possible sequences of job executions.

Formally, the schedule graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,A)
is defined as a directed acyclic graph, where each vertex
v_{k}
has a label consisting of its ID and
finish time interval

[e_{k},l_{k}]
, where
e_{k}
and
l_{k}
are the earliest finish time and the latest finish time, respectively. Furthermore, each arc
a\in A
has a label corresponding to a single job that is to be scheduled next. Note that there may be several vertices with the same set of finished jobs and multiple arcs can have the same job label.

It is useful to see the schedule graph as a directed level-structured graph
Díaz et al. (2002)
. This means
V
can be split into disjoint sets based on the distance from root vertex
v_{0}
. Here, the distance of vertex
v_{k}
from root vertex
v_{0}
is the length of the path with the smallest number of arcs from
v_{0}
to
v_{k}
. Let
L_{i}
denote a set of vertices for which the distance from root vertex
v_{0}
is
i
. Note that
L_{0}=\{v_{0}\}
.

3.2
Graph Generation

The schedule graph is generated based on a set of tasks
\mathcal{T}
and scheduling policy
\Pi
. The generation algorithm initializes with root vertex
v_{0}
with
[e_{0},l_{0}]=[0,0]
and
L_{0}=\{v_{0}\}
. It then keeps performing two alternating phases called
expansion phase
and
merge phase
. The expansion phase generates a new set of vertices
L_{i+1}^{ex}
from
L_{i}
. The merge phase then tries to merge some vertices from
L_{i+1}^{ex}
with each other which results in
L_{i+1}
.

The two alternating phases are performed until
L_{n^{\prime}}
is generated, where
n^{\prime}=\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}h_{i^{\prime}}
is the total number of jobs in all the tasks in
\mathcal{T}
. The algorithm may also end earlier if it finds a deadline miss. If a deadline miss is detected, a set of tasks
\mathcal{T}
is not schedulable under policy
\Pi
. If the entire schedule graph is generated with no deadline misses found, then the set of tasks
\mathcal{T}
is schedulable under policy
\Pi
.

3.2.1
Expansion Phase

A path taken from the root vertex to vertex
v_{k}
determines jobs that are finished in vertex
v_{k}
. Notice that all paths from the root vertex to vertex
v_{k}
contain the same set of finished jobs. This set of finished jobs is denoted as
J^{v_{k}}
and is used to determine applicable jobs for vertex
v_{k}
.
The expansion phase evaluates each vertex in
L_{i}
individually by using its applicable jobs and its finish time interval
[e,l]
. It determines at what time which jobs can be executed next. This in turn means generating new vertices and arcs (more details later in Section
3.3.3
). The result is a set of new vertices
L_{i+1}^{ex}
, some of which may be merged in the merge phase.

3.2.2
Merge Phase

The merge phase takes the result of the expansion phase
L_{i+1}^{ex}
and attempts to merge its vertices. Two vertices
v_{k}
and
v_{k^{\prime}}
can be merged if
J^{v_{k}}=J^{v_{k^{\prime}}}\wedge[e_{k},l_{k}]\cap[e_{k^{\prime}},l_{k^{\prime}}]\neq\emptyset
. If vertices
v_{k}
and
v_{k^{\prime}}
are merged, then all arcs pointing to
v_{k^{\prime}}
are reoriented so that they point to
v_{k}
, time interval of
v_{k}
is updated as
[e_{k},l_{k}]\leftarrow[e_{k},l_{k}]\cup[e_{k^{\prime}},l_{k^{\prime}}]
and
v_{k^{\prime}}
is removed from the graph. Note that the schedule graph is not a multigraph.

[e_{0},l_{0}]=[0,0]
and no jobs finished. At this point, there are two certainly released jobs
J_{1,1}
and
J_{2,1}
. The expansion phase concludes that the EDF policy would pick
J_{2,1}
at time
0
as it has an earlier deadline than
J_{1,1}
. Job
J_{3,1}
is not considered as it would not be released at time
0
for any execution scenario. The expansion results in creating a new vertex
v_{1}
with
[e_{1},l_{1}]=[e_{0}+C^{min}_{2,1},l_{0}+C^{max}_{2,1}]=[1,1]
. Additionally, new arc pointing from
v_{0}
to
v_{1}
is created and labeled with the picked job
J_{2,1}
. This concludes the expansion phase on level 0. The merge phase is performed next but it does not change the graph in any way as the result of the expansion phase is only one vertex.

The algorithm continues with the expansion phase on level 1 with
v_{1}
. Here, two scenarios may occur. In the first scenario, job
J_{3,1}
has just released at time
1
. If that is the case,
J_{3,1}
would be picked by EDF policy as it has an earlier deadline than
J_{1,1}
. In the second scenario, job
J_{3,1}
does not release at time
1
, and
J_{1,1}
is picked instead as there are no other released jobs. These two scenarios result in vertices
v_{2}
and
v_{3}
with
[e_{2},l_{2}]=[e_{1}+C^{min}_{1,1},l_{1}+C^{max}_{1,1}]=[2,3]
and
[e_{3},l_{3}]=[e_{1}+C^{min}_{3,1},l_{1}+C^{max}_{3,1}]=[4,5]
. This is the result of the expansion phase. The merge phase does not merge vertices
v_{2}
and
v_{3}
as
J^{v_{2}}\neq J^{v_{3}}
.

At level 2, the expansion phase individually evaluates
v_{2}
and
v_{3}
. When expanding a vertex, which has the earliest finish time strictly smaller than the latest finish time, we need to check all time points in the interval
[e,l]
. In the case of
v_{2}
, the only jobs left are
J_{3,1}
and
J_{2,2}
, but
J_{2,2}
will not release until time
5
and is therefore ignored. At time
2
, there are two scenarios. If
J_{3,1}
releases at time
2
, then it would be picked by EDF policy. If
J_{3,1}
releases at time
3
, then the processor would stay idle until time
3
(the online scheduler would be invoked at time
3
). At time
3
, the job
J_{3,1}
is certainly released and would be picked by EDF policy. Hence, EDF will always pick job
J_{3,1}
in time interval
[2,3]
. The earliest time it would start its execution is
2
and the latest time is
3
. This results in a new vertex
v_{4}
with
[e_{4},l_{4}]=[e_{2}+C^{min}_{3,1},l_{2}+C^{max}_{3,1}]=[5,7]
.

The expansion phase proceeds to expand
v_{3}
. Vertex
v_{3}
has finish time interval
[e_{3},l_{3}]=[4,5]
. At time
4
, job
J_{1,1}
would be picked by EDF because
J_{2,2}
is not yet released and there are no other unfinished jobs. However, at time
5
, job
J_{2,2}
is certainly released and because it has a lower deadline than
J_{1,1}
,
J_{2,2}
would be picked by EDF. This results in two new vertices
v_{5}
and
v_{6}
, where
[e_{5},l_{5}]=[4+C^{min}_{1,1},4+C^{max}_{1,1}]=[5,6]
and
[e_{6},l_{6}]=[5+C^{min}_{2,2},5+C^{max}_{2,2}]=[6,6]
. Notice that since different jobs would execute at different times, the new finish time intervals of
v_{5}
and
v_{6}
are computed from the earliest and latest time they would be picked by EDF.

After the expansion phase on level 2 is done, the merge phase takes over. Since
J^{v_{4}}=J^{v_{5}}
and
[e_{4},l_{4}]\cap[e_{5},l_{5}]\neq\emptyset
, vertices
v_{4}
and
v_{5}
are merged. This is done by merging the intervals, arcs pointing to
v_{5}
are redirected to
v_{4}
, and
v_{5}
is removed. The resulting interval is
[e_{4},l_{4}]=[5,7]\cup[5,6]=[5,7]
.

On level 3, the final iteration of the expansion and merge phase is performed. The expansion phase individually evaluates
v_{4}
and
v_{6}
. For
v_{4}
, the last remaining job is
J_{2,2}
, which releases at time
5
, and for
v_{6}
, the last remaining job is
J_{1,1}
, which released at time
0
. The expansion phase creates vertex
v_{7}
as expansion of
v_{4}
and
v_{8}
as expansion of
v_{6}
. The resulting finish time intervals are
[e_{7},l_{7}]=[e_{4}+C^{min}_{2,2},l_{4}+C^{max}_{2,2}]=[6,8]
and
[e_{8},l_{8}]=[e_{6}+C^{min}_{1,1},l_{6}+C^{max}_{1,1}]=[7,8]
.

Since
J^{v_{7}}=J^{v_{8}}
and
[e_{7},l_{7}]\cap[e_{8},l_{8}]\neq\emptyset
, vertices
v_{7}
and
v_{8}
are merged. This results in
v_{7}
being the only vertex on level 3 with
[e_{7},l_{7}]=[6,8]
.

There was no deadline miss in this example, therefore, the given set of tasks is schedulable under the EDF scheduling policy. A deadline miss could be easily detected during the expansion phase. When creating new vertex
v_{k}
with job
J_{i,j}
, there is a deadline miss if
l_{k}>d_{i,j}
.

3.3
Detailed Description of Schedule Graph Generation

The structure of the algorithm so far is the same as that of SGA-SE
Nasri and Brandenburg (2017)
. The main novelty in our algorithm SGA-ME lies in the schedule graph generation rules for expanding vertices.
To ease the description, we will use the dot notation hereinafter. For instance, the deadline of job
J
will be denoted as
J.d
.

Let us first define the parameters of each vertex and arc. Every vertex
v
has earliest finish time
v.e
, latest finish time
v.l
, incoming arcs
v.in
and outgoing arcs
v.out
. Every arc
a
has a job label
a.J
, source vertex
a.s
and destination vertex
a.d
.

As mentioned before, we can determine the finished jobs of vertex
v
from its position in the graph. This is done by taking any directed path from root vertex
v_{0}
to vertex
v
and then transforming the set of arcs on the path into a set of jobs by taking the labels of the arcs. This set of finished jobs is then used to determine the applicable jobs. The set of applicable jobs of vertex
v
is denoted as
v.J^{A}
.

3.3.1
Job Eligibility for Work-Conserving Policies

In this subsection, we deal with the rules for work-conserving policies.
Recall that the scheduling policy is a function
\Pi(t,J^{A})
. We say that job
J_{i,j}
has higher
\Pi
-priority
than
J_{x,y}
if
\Pi
(
\infty
, {
J_{i,j}
,
J_{x,y}
}) returns job
J_{i,j}
. That is,
J_{i,j}
would be picked by the scheduling policy given that both
J_{i,j}
and
J_{x,y}
are certainly released. If one of the jobs is null, then the scheduling policy picks the one that is not null. The policy function
\Pi
(
\infty
, {
J_{i,j}
,
J_{x,y}
}) can be replaced with any specific policy, e.g., FP-EDF(
\infty
, {
J_{i,j}
,
J_{x,y}
}).

For vertex
v
, set of applicable jobs
v.J^{A}
and time
t
, job
J^{t}_{ce}\in v.J^{A}
is
certainly-eligible
at time
t
if
J^{t}_{ce}
is certainly released at time
t
and there is no other certainly released applicable job at time
t
with higher
\Pi
-priority than
J^{t}_{ce}
.
Formally, job
J^{t}_{ce}\in v.J^{A}
is certainly-eligible at time
t
iff

J^{t}_{ce}.r^{max}\leq t\wedge\nexists J_{i,j}\in v.J^{A}\;st.\;J_{i,j}\not=J^{t}_{ce}\;\wedge J_{i,j}.r^{max}\leq t\wedge J_{i,j}=\Pi(\infty,\{J_{i,j},J^{t}_{ce}\})

(1)

Note that there may be at most one certainly-eligible job at time
t
. If there is no certainly-eligible job at time
t
, we say that
J^{t}_{ce}
does not exist.

Similarly, job
J^{t}_{pe}\in v.J^{A}
is
possibly-eligible
at time
t
if it is possibly released at time
t
and
J^{t}_{ce}
(if exists) has lower
\Pi
-priority than
J^{t}_{pe}
. Formally, we define the set of possibly-eligible jobs at time
t
as

\{J_{i,j}\mid J_{i,j}\in v.J^{A}\;\wedge\;J_{i,j}.r^{min}\leq t<J_{i,j}.r^{max}\;\wedge J_{i,j}=\Pi(\infty,\{J_{i,j},J^{t}_{ce}\})\}

(2)

To understand the definitions better, consider the illustration in Table

\Pi

\Pi

\times
for each job according to whether the job is certainly released (3rd column), possibly released (2nd column), or neither (1st column). The certainly-eligible job, marked with the symbol
\boxtimes
, is the one with the highest priority from the column of certainly released jobs. The possibly-eligible jobs, marked with the symbol
\otimes
, are those jobs from the column of possibly released jobs that have higher priority than the certainly-eligible job.

The certainly-eligible job
J^{t}_{ce}
is the job that would be picked by the policy function at time
t
in such a scenario when all possibly-eligible jobs are released at time
t+1
or later, because in this scenario, all other jobs, which are neither certainly-eligible nor possibly-eligible at time
t
, either have lower
\Pi
-priority than
J^{t}_{ce}
or cannot be released at time
t
.

If one or more of the possibly-eligible jobs at time
t
release at time
t
or earlier, then one of these jobs is picked by the policy function at time
t
because the possibly-eligible job has higher
\Pi
-priority than the certainly-eligible job
J^{t}_{ce}
. However, we do not know in advance which possibly-eligible jobs will be released at time
t
or earlier. Any of the possibly-eligible jobs may release at time
t
or earlier while all other possibly-eligible jobs release at time
t+1
or later. This means that each of the possibly-eligible jobs may be picked by the policy function in some scenarios.

Now, we explain how we create the exploration interval that is used for finding eligible jobs and thus for creating the next nodes. Given a set of applicable jobs
v.J^{A}
and a work-conserving scheduling policy, jobs that are certainly-eligible or possibly-eligible at time
t\in[v.e,v.l]
may begin execution at time
t
as the next job in some execution scenario. Since there may be no certainly released job in the interval
[v.e,v.l]
, we extend the interval to a time point when a certainly-eligible job exists. Hence, the exploration interval is
[v.e,{l}^{ext}]
, where
{l}^{ext}=min\{t\mid t\geq v.l\wedge J^{t}_{ce}\;exists\}
.

3.3.2
Job Eligibility for Non-Work-Conserving Policies

In this subsection, we extend the eligibility rules for non-work-conserving policies (e.g., P-FP-EDF).
What the considered non-work-conserving policies have in common is that they all entail some critical job
J^{c}
and critical time
t^{c}
, where
t^{c}
is the latest time when
J^{c}
must be started to avoid missing its deadline. Hence, no job, except for
J^{c}
, can start its execution if it may finish after
t^{c}
. How to obtain
t^{c}
and
J^{c}
is described in
6
.

While the original SGA-SE
Nasri and Brandenburg (2017)
aims to work with the special class of non-work-conserving policies that augment the job priority ordering with an idle-time insertion policy (IIP)
Nasri and Brandenburg (2017)
, which the authors adjust to job-set-dependent IIPs, our algorithm SGA-ME allows working with any non-work-conserving policy from which
t^{c}
and
J^{c}
can be inferred.

We redefine certainly and possibly-eligible jobs based on vertex
v
, set of applicable jobs
v.J^{A}
, critical time
t^{c}
, critical job
J^{c}
and time
t
. Given vertex
v
and time
t
, job
J_{i,j}\in v.J^{A}

violates

v.t^{c}
if
t+J_{i,j}.C^{max}>v.t^{c}\wedge J_{i,j}\neq v.J^{c}
. That is,
J_{i,j}
violates
v.t^{c}
if
J_{i,j}
is not the critical job and it may finish after
v.t^{c}
when started at time
t
.

Now,
certainly-eligible
job is the one with the highest
\Pi
-priority from the set of certainly released jobs that do not violate
v.t^{c}
.
Formally, given vertex
v
, job
J^{t}_{ce}\in v.J^{A}
is certainly-eligible at time
t
iff

J^{t}_{ce}.r^{max}\leq t~\wedge~\underline{(t+J^{t}_{ce}.C^{max}\leq v.t^{c}\vee J^{t}_{ce}=v.J^{c})}~\wedge~\nexists J_{i,j}\in v.J^{A}\;\text{st.}\\
J_{i,j}\not=J^{t}_{ce}~\wedge~J_{i,j}.r^{max}\leq t~\wedge~J_{i,j}=\Pi(\infty,\{J_{i,j},J^{t}_{ce}\})~\wedge~\underline{(t+J_{i,j}.C^{max}\leq v.t^{c}\vee J_{i,j}=v.J^{c})}

(3)

The underlined parts emphasize what is added in Equation (
3
) compared to Equation (
1
). Similarly, job
J^{t}_{pe}
is
possibly-eligible
at time
t
if it is possibly released at time
t
, does not violate
v.t^{c}
, and
J^{t}_{ce}
(if exists) has lower
\Pi
-priority than
J^{t}_{pe}
. Formally, we define the set of possibly-eligible jobs at time
t
as

\{J_{i,j}\mid J_{i,j}\in v.J^{A}\;\wedge\;J_{i,j}.r^{min}\leq t<J_{i,j}.r^{max}\;\wedge\\
J_{i,j}=\Pi(\infty,\{J_{i,j},J^{t}_{ce}\})~\wedge~\underline{(t+J_{i,j}.C^{max}\leq v.t^{c}\vee J_{i,j}=v.J^{c})}\}

(4)

Again, the underlined part emphasizes what is added in Equation (
4
) compared to Equation (
2
). Just as with work-conserving policies, jobs that are certainly-eligible or possibly-eligible at time
t\in[v.e,v.l]
may begin execution at time
t
in some execution scenarios.

We shall now explain in detail how the algorithms SGA-ME and SGA-SE differ in the process of the schedule graph generation on the problem instance described in Table
4
using the P-FP-EDF policy.

Throughout the entire schedule graph generation process, the only critical job may be job
J_{1,1}
as it is the only job with priority
p=0
. Therefore,
v.t^{c}=J_{1,1}.d-J_{1,1}.C^{max}=12-2=10
and
v.J^{c}=J_{1,1}
for all nodes
v
, where
J_{1,1}
is unfinished.

Both algorithms begin by scheduling job
J_{2,1}
as it is the only certainly released job at time
t=0
and it also does not violate the critical time because
J_{2,1}.C^{max}+t\leq v_{0}.t^{c}
, i.e.,
8+0\leq 10
. In both cases, the expansion phase of
L_{0}
results in a single new vertex
v_{1}
with
v_{1}.e=1
and
v_{1}.l=8
.

Where the two algorithms differ is during the expansion of vertex
v_{1}
. Here, job
J_{3,1}
is eligible at times
[1,2]
and job
J_{4,1}
is eligible at times
[3,6]
. Note that
J_{3,1}
is not eigible at times
[3,6]
because
J_{4,1}
has higher priority. Both approaches expand vertex
v_{1}
by creating new vertices
v_{2}
and
v_{3}
. However, the difference lies in
v_{4}
. SGA-ME finds that job
J_{3,1}
is also eligible at times
[7,8]
. Indeed, if job
J_{2,1}
starts its execution at time 0 and finishes at time 7 or 8, then according to the P-FP-EDF policy, the only released job that does not cause a deadline miss for the highest priority job
J_{1,1}
is job
J_{3,1}
and thus should be scheduled next. Hence, SGA-ME finds that job
J_{3,1}
is eligible in vertex
v_{1}
in the time interval
[7,8]
. Hence, vertex
v_{4}
is the result of expanding
v_{1}
with job
J_{3,1}
in time range
[7,8]
, which results in
v_{4}.e=7+J_{3,1}.C^{min}=9
and
v_{4}.l=8+J_{3,1}.C^{max}=10
.
On the contrary, SGA-SE
Nasri and Brandenburg (2017)
concludes that there are no eligible jobs in the time interval
[7,9]
and job
J_{1,1}
is scheduled next at its release time
t=J_{1,1}.r^{min}=J_{1,1}.r^{max}=10
. As mentioned, this is due to the fact that according to
Nasri and Brandenburg (2017)
, once a job is eligible during the expansion of a single vertex, it cannot become eligible again. In other words, according to
Nasri and Brandenburg (2017)
, no vertex can have two outgoing arcs with the same job label. Therefore, job
J_{1,1}
is scheduled next, which results in vertex
v_{4}
with
v_{4}.e=J_{1,1}.r^{min}+J_{1,1}.C^{min}=12
and
v_{4}.l=J_{1,1}.r^{max}+J_{1,1}.C^{max}=12
.
This eventually results in vertices
v_{8}
and
v_{11}
having different time intervals
[v_{8}.e,v_{8}.l]
and
[v_{11}.e,v_{11}.l]
which causes a deadline miss only for SGA-SE
Nasri and Brandenburg (2017)
.

To sum up, SGA-SE checks the eligibility of each job
J_{i,j}
only at time
t_{E}=max(v.e,J_{i,j}.r^{min})
as follows from Definition 5 in
Nasri and Brandenburg (2017)
. This means that once a job stops being eligible, it cannot become eligible again and hence no vertex can have two outgoing arcs with the same job label. That is why we distinguish single eligibility (SE) and multiple eligibility (ME) in the names of the algorithms.

5
Conclusion

This paper addresses the problem of schedulability analysis for non-preemptive tasks on a uniprocessor.
We focused on existing schedulability analysis and improved the approach presented in
Nasri and Brandenburg (2017)
such that it builds a schedule graph using new definitions of eligibility rules and thus is exact and sustainable for both work-conserving and enhanced formalization of non-work-conserving policies. Besides, we compared our algorithm to that of
Nasri and Brandenburg (2017)
in terms of solving time, showing that our algorithm was faster by an order of magnitude.

The proposed algorithms based on schedule graph generation can be further accelerated by partial-order reduction as presented in
Ranjha et al. (2022)
, which keeps the exactness of the schedulability analysis but introduces over-estimation in the worst-case response times.

The notion of the schedule graph presents an exceptionally valuable framework for integrating online scheduling of event-triggered jobs and offline schedule synthesis for time-triggered jobs. We already extended the algorithm presented in this paper by initial study on the integration of the offline schedule synthesis on a uniprocessor
Jaroš (2022)
as well as on dedicated resources and precedences
Halaška (2023)
.
