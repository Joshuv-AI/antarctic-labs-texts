Grading and detecting gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms with dual-tracer (18F-AlF-NOTA-octreotide/18F-FDG) PET/CT: a metabolic parameter-based study
Authors: Qing Dong, Qiuchen Zhou, Yang Liu, Qian Wang, Zhao Yang, Qian Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate quantitative metabolic indices from dual-tracer 18F-AlF-NOTA-octreotide (18F-AlF-OC) and 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET/computed tomography (CT) for grading gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms (GEP-NENs), focusing on total lesion glycolysis ratio (TLGR).MethodsThis retrospective study included 34 patients with GEP-NENs (198 target lesions) who underwent both 18F-AlF-OC and 18F-FDG PET/CT. Parameters included maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax), total lesion glycolysis (TLG), SUV ratio (SUVR), and TLGR (ratio of 18F-AlF-OC volumetric somatostatin receptor uptake to 18F-FDG TLG by conventional TLG framework). Diagnostic performance for differentiating G1/G2 from G3/neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) and neuroendocrine tumor (NET) from NEC was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Incremental value was evaluated using Δarea under the curve (AUC) and continuous net reclassification improvement with patient-level cluster bootstrap resampling. Generalized estimating equation (GEE) models accounted for multiple lesions per patient. Internal validation used leave-one-patient-out cross-validation.Results18F-AlF-OC detected more lesions in G1/G2 NETs, while detection was similar in NEC. In ROC analysis, TLGR, SUVR, and TLGR + SUVR yielded AUCs of 0.760, 0.760, and 0.767 for G1/G2 versus G3/NEC, and 0.811, 0.829, and 0.827 for NET versus NEC, respectively. Adding TLGR to SUVR or FDG SUVmax provided only modest improvement. TLGR was significant alone in GEE models but attenuated after SUVR adjustment, and cross-validation showed reduced out-of-sample performance.ConclusionDual-tracer PET/CT provides complementary information for GEP-NEN grading. TLGR is an exploratory volumetric dual-tracer parameter, but its incremental value beyond SUVR and FDG SUVmax is limited. Further validation is required.

Introduction

Neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are uncommon tumors originating from peptidergic neurons and neuroendocrine cells, with neuroendocrine differentiation and marker expression [1]. They may arise throughout the body, most frequently in the lungs and gastroenteropancreatic (GEP) system. Globally, the incidence of NENs has risen significantly in recent decades, particularly that of GEP-NENs [2]. According to the 2022 WHO classification system [3], NENs are categorized based on the Ki-67 proliferation index and mitotic count per 2 mm2. Neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) are graded as follows: G1 (mitotic count <2 per 2 mm2 and/or Ki-67 index <3%), G2 (mitotic count 2–20 per 2 mm2 and/or Ki-67 index 3–20%), and G3 (mitotic count >20 per 2 mm2 and/or Ki-67 index >20%). Neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs) are further subdivided into small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma and large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma. Both subtypes require a mitotic count >20 per 2 mm2 and/or Ki-67 index >20% (typically exceeding 70%), accompanied by definitive morphological characteristics. Clinically, G3 NETs and NECs are associated with more aggressive biological behavior and poorer outcomes than G1/G2 NETs.

Somatostatin receptors (SSTRs) are expressed in approximately 80% of NENs, which has led to the widespread adoption of SSTR imaging using radiolabeled somatostatin analogs (SSAs) through both single-photon emission computerized tomography/computed tomography (CT) and PET/CT modalities [4]. Currently, 68Ga-labeled SSAs, such as 68Ga‑DOTATOC, 68Ga‑DOTATATE, and 68Ga‑DOTANOC, serve as the principal tracers for SSTR PET/CT and are considered the reference standard for diagnosing, localizing, and staging NENs, with similar diagnostic performance across these agents [5,6]. More recently, 18F‑AlF‑NOTA‑octreotide (18F‑AlF‑OC) has gained attention as a promising 18F‑based SSA for SSTR imaging. Emerging clinical evidence supports its utility in NEN visualization, and Pauwels et al. indicated that 18F-AlF-OC may show comparable diagnostic performance to conventional 68Ga-labeled SSA imaging [7].

18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) and SSA PET/CT provide complementary imaging profiles for NENs: 18F-FDG reflects glycolytic metabolism in aggressive, poorly differentiated lesions, whereas SSA PET/CT detects well-differentiated tumors through SSTR expression. Existing dual-tracer integration frameworks, such as the NETPET score, have been developed to combine SSTR-targeted and FDG PET findings and to characterize tumor heterogeneity more comprehensively [8,9]. While dual-tracer imaging with 18F-AlF-OC and 18F-FDG enables comprehensive biological characterization, clinical interpretation remains predominantly visual and subjective [10]. To improve quantitative characterization of dual-tracer PET findings, this study explored the total lesion glycolysis ratio (TLGR), calculated as the ratio of a TLG-like volumetric uptake parameter from 18F-AlF-OC to the conventional TLG from 18F-FDG. Although the term TLGR is retained because the calculation follows the conventional TLG framework, the 18F-AlF-OC component reflects SSTR-positive volumetric uptake rather than glycolysis. This metric may reflect the relative volumetric burden of SSTR-positive versus glycolytically active tumor components, with lower TLGR values potentially indicating a more glycolytically dominant and aggressive phenotype. Building on the previously reported standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) proposed by Liu et al. [11], this study explored whether TLGR, as a volumetric dual-tracer ratio, provides complementary diagnostic information beyond SUVR and FDG SUVmax [11]. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the apparent diagnostic performance of TLGR and other dual-tracer PET-derived parameters for differentiating GEP-NEN grades and clinically relevant subtypes, and to assess whether TLGR provides incremental value beyond SUVR and FDG SUVmax.

Ethical approval

This retrospective study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University Cancer Hospital (approval no. E20251009). The requirement for written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized, de-identified clinical and imaging data. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional regulations.

Materials and methods

This study consecutively enrolled patients from our institution between April 2022 and June 2024 who met the following inclusion criteria: (a) diagnosis of primary or recurrent GEP-NENs, confirmed either histopathologically or through imaging follow up; (b) completion of both 18F-FDG and 18F-AlF-OC PET/CT scans prior to any relevant therapeutic intervention, with an interval of no more than 3 weeks between the two scans. The imaging indications included primary tumor localization, initial staging at diagnosis or before surgery, and restaging or detection of suspected recurrent lesions during follow-up.

Exclusion criteria were: (a) patients with other coexisting malignancies; (b) patients who had undergone systemic therapy prior to PET/CT examination. All patient data were anonymized and de-identified prior to analysis (Fig. 1).

The reference standard was categorized at the patient level as histopathological diagnosis or imaging follow-up. Because not all lesions were individually biopsied, lesion grade for non-biopsied lesions was assigned according to the corresponding patient-level or representative histopathological diagnosis combined with imaging follow-up and multidisciplinary clinical assessment. To evaluate potential verification bias, the distribution of reference standards was reported, and sensitivity analyses restricted to patients with histopathological diagnosis were performed.

Automated synthesis and quality control of 18F‑AlF‑OC

The synthesis of 18F‑AlF‑OC was performed using a Tracelab FX N Pro automated module (GE Healthcare, USA). Following system conditioning with helium, the reaction vial was preloaded with 60 µl of 2 mmol/l AlCl3 and 100 µl anhydrous ethanol. 18F-fluoride produced via a MINItrace cyclotron (GE Healthcare, USA) with H218O (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, USA) as target was trapped on a QMA cartridge (MN, Germany), rinsed with 3 ml water for injection, and eluted into the reaction vial using 0.2 ml of 0.5 M sodium acetate solution. After 30 s of gas purging, 0.6 ml of precursor solution was added and the mixture reacted at 100 °C for 10 min. The crude product was purified on a Sep-Pak C18 Light cartridge (Waters, USA) through sequential steps: washing with 16 ml formulation solution to remove impurities, elution with 2 ml anhydrous ethanol, and dilution with 16 ml formulation solution. The final solution was sterile-filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane (Millex-GV, Millipore, USA).

Quality control analysis by HPLC and thin-layer chromatography (Mini scan, Bioscan, USA) demonstrated >99% chemical purity and >95% radiochemical purity, with a labeling yield of 10–20% (end of bombardment). Radioactivity was quantified using a CRC-15R dose calibrator (Capintec, USA). The preparation satisfied all Chinese Pharmacopoeia requirements for abnormal toxicity, sterility, and bacterial endotoxins (LAL kit, ACC, USA), confirming production of a sterile, pyrogen-free radiopharmaceutical with pH approximately 6.8.

Imaging acquisition protocol

All imaging was performed using a GE Discovery Elite PET/CT scanner (GE Medical Systems, Waukesha, WI, USA). Prior to 18F-FDG PET/CT, patients were required to fast for approximately 6 h, with serum glucose levels maintained below 11.1 mmol/l. No fasting was required before 18F-AlF-OC imaging. Image acquisition commenced 60 min after intravenous injection of either 18F-FDG or 18F-AlF-OC at a dose of 3.7–4.44 MBq/kg. The scanning range extended from the distal femur to the skull base, covering eight bed positions. A spiral CT scan was first performed with the following parameters: 80 mA, 130 kV, and a slice thickness of 5 mm, followed by PET acquisition. PET data were collected for 2 min per bed position in 3D mode, with an axial increment of 16.2 cm. PET images were reconstructed using the ordered subsets expectation maximization iterative algorithm, yielding a final voxel size of 5.3 × 5.3 × 2.5 mm3. Both tracers were acquired on the same PET/CT scanner and reconstructed using the same institutional reconstruction protocol to reduce variability related to scanner type and reconstruction settings.

Image analysis

All PET/CT images were independently reviewed by two experienced nuclear medicine physicians, and disagreements were resolved by consensus. A third reviewer was consulted when consensus could not be reached. Lesions were identified using both 18F-FDG and 18F-AlF-OC PET/CT images, with reference to contrast-enhanced CT or MRI when lesions were negative or equivocal on either PET modality.

Quantitative lesion analysis was performed using PET VCAR software (PET Volume Computer Assisted Reading, GE Healthcare, USA). Regions of interest were semi-automatically delineated using a fixed threshold of 41% of SUVmax. This threshold was selected because fixed relative thresholds around 40–41% of SUVmax are commonly used for PET-derived metabolic tumor volume (MTV) and TLG estimation and have been recommended in European Association of Nuclear Medicine-related PET segmentation settings when tumor-to-background contrast is sufficiently high [9,12,13]. The same thresholding approach was applied to both 18F-FDG and 18F-AlF-OC PET images to maintain consistency in dual-tracer volumetric comparison. Nevertheless, because the 41% SUVmax threshold has not been specifically validated for 18F-AlF-OC PET in GEP-NENs, threshold dependence was considered a methodological limitation.

For each target lesion, SUVmax and TLG were extracted from the corresponding region of interest. SUVR was defined as 18F-AlF-OC SUVmax divided by 18F-FDG SUVmax. For each tracer, the volumetric uptake parameter was calculated using the conventional TLG formula, SUVmean × MTV. For 18F-FDG, this parameter corresponds to conventional TLG and reflects glycolytic tumor burden. For 18F-AlF-OC, the same formula was used to derive a TLG-like volumetric SSTR uptake parameter, reflecting SSTR-positive tumor burden rather than glycolysis. TLGR was then defined as the ratio of the 18F-AlF-OC TLG-like parameter to 18F-FDG TLG.

Given that some patients presented with extensive multi-organ metastatic disease, a target-lesion approach was used to avoid overrepresentation of patients with very high lesion burden in lesion-level analyses. When more than five lesions were visible within a single organ, up to five target lesions were selected for standardized metabolic parameter measurement. Because this approach was based on selected target lesions rather than whole-body tumor segmentation, additional index-lesion sensitivity analyses were performed. The primary index-lesion analysis selected the lesion with the highest FDG SUVmax for each patient. Additional index-lesion strategies using the highest FDG TLG, lowest SUVR, and lowest TLGR lesions were performed as supplementary sensitivity analyses.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range) unless otherwise specified, and categorical variables are presented as frequency and percentage. Differences in PET-derived parameters across lesion grades were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Spearman’s rank correlation was used as an exploratory lesion-level analysis to evaluate associations between PET-derived parameters and lesion grade.

Apparent diagnostic performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The area under the ROC curve (AUC), 95% confidence interval (CI), optimal cutoff, sensitivity, specificity, and Youden’s index were reported. To evaluate the incremental value of TLGR beyond established PET-derived parameters, head-to-head model comparisons were performed, including SUVR versus SUVR + TLGR, FDG SUVmax versus FDG SUVmax + TLGR, and FDG SUVmax + SUVR versus FDG SUVmax + SUVR + TLGR. Incremental value was assessed using ΔAUC and continuous net reclassification improvement. Because multiple lesions could be derived from the same patient, 95% CIs and P values for ΔAUC and net reclassification improvement were estimated using patient-level cluster bootstrap resampling.

To account for within-patient correlation due to multiple lesions per patient, generalized estimating equation models with an exchangeable working correlation structure were used, with patient ID specified as the clustering variable. Odds ratios and 95% CIs were reported per 1-SD increase in the natural log-transformed parameter.

Several sensitivity analyses were performed. First, index-lesion ROC analyses were conducted by selecting one lesion per patient. The primary index lesion was defined as the lesion with the highest FDG SUVmax. Additional index-lesion strategies included the highest FDG TLG, lowest SUVR, and lowest TLGR lesions. Second, to evaluate potential verification bias, analyses were repeated in the histology subset, restricted to patients with histopathological diagnosis. Third, internal validation of the TLGR + SUVR model was performed using leave-one-patient-out cross-validation, in which all lesions from one patient were held out together in each iteration. Cross-validated AUC, Brier score, calibration intercept, calibration slope, and calibration plots were used to assess model performance and calibration.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Given the exploratory nature of this retrospective study and the limited sample size, validation and sensitivity analyses were interpreted cautiously.

Results

A total of 198 target lesions from 34 patients with GEP-NENs were included in the complete-case analytical cohort. The median age was 53 years, and the cohort included 22 male and 12 female patients. At the lesion level, 25 lesions were classified as G1 NET, 135 as G2 NET, 27 as G3 NET, and 11 as NEC. Detailed patient-level and lesion-level characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Association of PET-derived parameters with lesion grade

PET-derived metabolic parameters differed across lesion grades (Table 3). FDG SUVmax tended to increase with higher tumor grade, whereas TLGR and SUVR tended to decrease with higher grade. In exploratory lesion-level Spearman correlation analysis, FDG SUVmax was positively correlated with lesion grade (ρ = 0.327, P < 0.001), whereas TLGR and SUVR were inversely correlated with lesion grade (TLGR: ρ = −0.341, P < 0.001; SUVR: ρ = −0.260, P < 0.001) (Table 4).

Apparent target-lesion receiver operating characteristic analysis

Apparent ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of PET-derived parameters for differentiating G1/G2 from G3/NEC and NET from NEC (Table 5; Fig. 4 and Supplementary Figure 1, Supplemental digital content 1, https://links.lww.com/NMC/A414). For differentiating G1/G2 from G3/NEC, TLGR and SUVR showed similar moderate apparent performance, with AUCs of 0.760 and 0.760, respectively. FDG SUVmax showed an AUC of 0.759. The combined TLGR + SUVR model yielded a slightly higher AUC of 0.767, but the numerical improvement over individual parameters was small. For differentiating NET from NEC, SUVR showed an AUC of 0.829, FDG SUVmax showed an AUC of 0.827, and TLGR showed an AUC of 0.811. The TLGR + SUVR model showed an AUC of 0.827.

Incremental value and clustered lesion-level analyses

To evaluate whether TLGR provided incremental value beyond established PET-derived parameters, head-to-head model comparisons were performed (Table 6). For differentiating G1/G2 from G3/NEC, adding TLGR to SUVR resulted in only a small increase in AUC from 0.760 to 0.767 (ΔAUC = 0.007, 95% CI: −0.014 to 0.084; P = 0.472). Adding TLGR to FDG SUVmax increased the AUC from 0.759 to 0.784, but the improvement was not statistically significant (ΔAUC = 0.025, 95% CI: −0.007 to 0.088; P = 0.162). Similarly, adding TLGR to the FDG SUVmax + SUVR model yielded only a modest and nonsignificant increase in AUC.

To account for multiple lesions derived from the same patient, clustered lesion-level generalized estimating equation (GEE) models were fitted with patient ID specified as the clustering variable (Table 7). In univariable GEE analyses, both TLGR and SUVR were inversely associated with tumor grade. For differentiating G1/G2 from G3/NEC, TLGR showed an odds ratio (OR) of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.85–0.98; P = 0.009), while SUVR showed an OR of 0.82 (95% CI: 0.70–0.96; P = 0.014). However, when TLGR and SUVR were included simultaneously, SUVR remained statistically significant, whereas TLGR was attenuated and no longer independently significant.

Internal validation was performed using leave-one-patient-out cross-validation to avoid data leakage across lesions from the same patient (Supplementary Table 2, Supplemental digital content 1, https://links.lww.com/NMC/A414). Reference standards were also summarized to evaluate potential verification bias. At the patient level, 26 patients (76.5%) had histopathological diagnosis and 8 patients (23.5%) were confirmed by imaging follow-up. After mapping patient-level reference standards to target lesions, 165 lesions (83.3%) were derived from patients with histopathological diagnosis and 33 lesions (16.7%) from patients confirmed by imaging follow-up (Supplementary Table 3, Supplemental digital content 1, https://links.lww.com/NMC/A414). Discussion

The clinical management of NENs relies heavily on accurate imaging assessment of receptor expression and metabolic activity. SSTRs are highly expressed in most NENs, supporting the use of SSTR-targeted therapies such as long-acting SSAs and peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT) [14–16]. Current clinical guidelines recommend SSTR PET/CT for localization, staging, and receptor expression evaluation in well-differentiated NETs [17]. In recent years, 18F-labeled SSTR tracers, such as 18F-AlF-OC, have gained attention because of their favorable physical and logistical properties, including a longer half-life, high image resolution, and stable supply [7,18]. In contrast, 18F-FDG PET/CT provides complementary information on glycolytic activity and aggressive tumor biology, particularly in high-grade NETs and poorly differentiated NECs [19]. Therefore, dual-tracer PET/CT may help characterize tumor heterogeneity and provide complementary information for grading assessment; however, its direct impact on treatment selection requires validation using treatment response and outcome data [20–22].

Established dual-tracer integration frameworks, such as the NETPET score [9], have been proposed to combine SSTR-targeted and FDG PET findings for more comprehensive characterization of tumor heterogeneity. Recent prospective evidence in grade 2 and grade 3 GEP-NETs also showed heterogeneous NETPET score distributions and suggested that dual-tracer imaging may influence management in selected patients. Compared with these visual or semiquantitative frameworks, our study focused on lesion-level quantitative PET-derived parameters, particularly SUVR and TLGR.

This study included 34 patients with GEP-NENs, analyzing a total of 198 lesions. The results showed that in low-grade tumors (G1/G2), 18F-AlF-OC exhibited a higher tendency for lesion detection compared to 18F-FDG, consistent with previous reports [6,23]. Notably, in G3 NETs and NECs, 18F-AlF-OC still detected a substantial number of lesions, with detection capabilities comparable to those of FDG imaging [21]. This finding suggests that residual SSTR expression may still be present in some high-grade lesions. However, its therapeutic relevance cannot be determined from the present dataset and requires validation in studies with PRRT response and survival outcomes [24]. Based on the diagnostic performance analysis of dual tracers, this study systematically evaluated two parameters: SUVR (the ratio of 18F-AlF-OC to 18F-FDG SUVmax) and TLGR. Recent studies have confirmed that SUVR is an effective tool for predicting NEN grading, showing a significant negative correlation with lesion grade [11] whereas TLGR was explored as a volumetric ratio based on the established relevance of TLG to tumor burden and prognosis [25–27]. In the present study, TLGR was inversely correlated with lesion grade and showed moderate apparent discrimination for both G1/G2 versus G3/NEC and NET versus NEC. Importantly, in clustered lesion-level GEE models accounting for multiple lesions within patients, TLGR remained significantly associated with tumor grade in univariable analyses. These findings suggest that TLGR captures biologically relevant dual-tracer information related to the relative volumetric burden of SSTR-positive and glycolytically active tumor components. However, after adjustment for SUVR, the association of TLGR was attenuated, indicating partial overlap between the two ratio-based parameters. Therefore, TLGR should be interpreted as a complementary and hypothesis-generating volumetric marker rather than an independently validated or practice-changing diagnostic tool.

Based on the 2022 WHO classification criteria (Ki-67 > 20%), this study further categorized NENs into G1/G2 and G3/NEC groups. In the apparent target-lesion ROC analysis, 18F-FDG SUVmax, TLGR, and SUVR showed moderate discriminatory performance for differentiating G1/G2 from G3/NEC, with AUCs of 0.759–0.760. The combined TLGR + SUVR model yielded a slightly higher apparent AUC of 0.767, but the numerical improvement over individual parameters was small. Given the fundamental differences in biological behavior and treatment strategies between NETs and NECs, we further evaluated the discriminative performance of these parameters for distinguishing NET from NEC [28]. In this comparison, 18F-FDG SUVmax, TLGR, SUVR, and the TLGR + SUVR model showed apparent AUCs of 0.827, 0.811, 0.829, and 0.827, respectively. Taken together, these findings suggest that TLGR may provide exploratory volumetric dual-tracer information for GEP-NEN grading. However, its incremental and independent value beyond SUVR was limited in the present cohort, and the TLGR + SUVR model should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than clinically validated.

The clinical utility of TLGR should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Although the TLGR + SUVR model showed moderate apparent diagnostic performance in the target-lesion ROC analysis, leave-one-patient-out cross-validation demonstrated reduced out-of-sample performance and suboptimal calibration, suggesting potential overfitting, particularly for the NET-versus-NEC comparison. Moreover, treatment response, PRRT eligibility decisions, chemotherapy response, PFS, and overall survival were not available in this retrospective dataset, although these outcomes are clinically relevant for evaluating dual-tracer PET biomarkers in NENs [22]. Therefore, TLGR and the TLGR + SUVR model should be regarded as exploratory imaging biomarkers rather than validated tools for treatment selection or prognostic stratification.

This study provides preliminary evidence for further exploration but also has some notable limitations. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective study with a small number of independent patients, particularly for the NEC subgroup. Although lesion-level analyses included 198 target lesions, multiple lesions could originate from the same patient; therefore, GEE models and patient-level validation approaches were added to address within-patient clustering and reduce data leakage. Second, the analysis was based on selected target lesions rather than complete whole-body tumor segmentation. Although index-lesion sensitivity analyses showed broadly similar trends, true whole-body tumor burden metrics could not be reliably calculated retrospectively. Third, reference standards were heterogeneous. Not all lesions were individually biopsied, and some lesions were classified according to patient-level histopathological diagnosis or imaging follow-up. Although histology-subset sensitivity analyses were performed, potential verification bias and lesion-level grade misclassification cannot be excluded. Fourth, MTV and TLG measurements are threshold-dependent. A fixed 41% SUVmax threshold was applied consistently to both tracers, but this threshold has not been specifically validated for 18F-AlF-OC PET in GEP-NENs. Volumetric parameters may also be affected by partial-volume effects, lesion size, tumor-to-background contrast, reconstruction settings, uptake-time variability, and inter-scan differences between the two tracers. Finally, no external validation cohort or clinical outcome data were available. Larger multicenter studies with standardized imaging protocols, robust reference standards, whole-body tumor segmentation, and treatment or survival outcomes are needed before TLGR can be applied in clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

Dual-tracer PET/CT with 18F-AlF-OC and 18F-FDG provides complementary metabolic information for grading GEP-NENs. TLGR, as a volumetric dual-tracer ratio, showed exploratory value in reflecting the balance between SSTR-positive and glycolytically active tumor components. Although TLGR was associated with tumor grade in univariable clustered analyses, its independent incremental value beyond SUVR was limited after adjustment for SUVR. Therefore, TLGR and the TLGR + SUVR model should be regarded as hypothesis-generating imaging biomarkers rather than validated tools for treatment selection. Larger prospective studies with standardized lesion assessment, external validation, and clinical outcome data are required to confirm their clinical utility.
