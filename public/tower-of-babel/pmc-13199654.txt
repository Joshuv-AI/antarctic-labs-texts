Auditing clinical AI in oncology: Strengthening assurance frameworks and nursing leadership in the Asia–Pacific context
Authors: Karthik Adapa, Mounika Metta, Suguna Kotte, Anusmitha Sathuluri, Vibhav Mithal, Resham Sethi, Grace Achungura, Lukasz Mazur
Published: 2026

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly being used in cancer care across the Asia–Pacific region. However, AI tools that work well in one country or hospital can fail in another because of differences in patients, equipment, language, and clinical practice. This commentary explains why three different checks—validation (does it work for our patients?), verification (is it built correctly?), and auditing (is it safe and fair over time?)—are all needed to ensure AI is used safely. We argue that oncology nurses, who spend the most time with patients, are essential partners in these checks but are usually left out. To address this, we propose the Integrated Clinical AI Audit Principles (ICAAP): seven evidence-based principles that combine the best parts of existing AI governance frameworks. Embedding nurses as formal AI auditors can help ensure that AI-enabled cancer care is safer and more patient-centered.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is now being developed and deployed across the oncology pathway, including population risk stratification, cancer screening, diagnostic imaging, pathology, treatment planning, symptom monitoring, and care workflow support, but the evidentiary and governance architecture required for safe clinical use has not matured at the same pace.1, 2, 3 In oncology, that lag is consequential because errors may alter time-sensitive diagnostic and treatment decisions, and because implementation occurs within health systems that differ substantially in staffing, infrastructure, case mix, language, and access to specialist care.4, 5, 6, 7 The Asia–Pacific region faces a particularly urgent version of this problem: it carries a large and rising cancer burden while encompassing delivery settings ranging from comprehensive cancer centres to resource-constrained district hospitals.2, 3, 4,8, 9, 10 Oncology practice in the region, therefore, needs more than enthusiasm for innovation; it needs audit-ready AI systems that are validated prospectively, monitored after deployment, and governed in ways that are clinically credible and ethically defensible.1,5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11

Our previous editorial established that rigorous AI evaluation must extend well beyond technical accuracy metrics and proposed a five-pillar framework for lifecycle-oriented assessment.12 We further argued that effective AI governance in oncology within Asia–Pacific health systems requires the active and structured leadership of nurses.13 Yet evaluation and governance alone do not guarantee safe deployment. Three assurance activities — validation, verification, and auditing — together govern the lifecycle of clinical AI systems, but they are frequently conflated in both policy discourse and clinical practice. This commentary examines each concept, reviews leading AI auditing frameworks, proposes a sector-specific synthesis— the Integrated Clinical AI Audit Principles (ICAAP), and argues that auditing requires formal oncology nursing participation to be clinically meaningful.

Validation, verification, and auditing are not interchangeable

Although the three activities are often bundled under the generic label of “testing,” they answer different questions and rely on different evidence.14 Validation refers to whether a system is fit for its intended clinical use in the target setting and population. A model with strong internal performance can still fail when workflows, disease prevalence, equipment, or patient characteristics change.7,15,16 Reddy et al. emphasise that validation must encompass clinical workflow assessment, user acceptance, and patient-centred outcomes in the actual deployment setting.15 A 2024 scoping review identified only 86 randomised controlled trials of AI in clinical practice, predominantly single-centre and geographically concentrated in the United States and China, with limited demographic reporting, underscoring that the prospective evidence base for clinical AI validation in Asia–Pacific contexts remains critically thin.17

Verification refers to whether the software and its implementation conform to design specifications, interfaces, and quality requirements. In software-engineering terms, verification asks whether the system was built right, whereas validation asks whether the right system was built.14 The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)’s framework for AI-enabled device software functions requires that planned algorithmic modifications remain within pre-specified performance boundaries, embedding change control as a continuous governance obligation rather than a one-time exercise.18 Van der Vegt et al.’s SALIENT framework addresses interface integrity between the AI component and the host clinical information system, data pipeline integration, and reproducibility of outputs across implementation stages as core technical requirements.19

Auditing adds an independent layer of oversight by examining not only performance but also bias, governance, documentation, post-deployment monitoring, model change control, and accountability over time.1,2,5, 6, 7, 8, 9,20,21 Audits are a subset of assurance and can be either internal or external: they “examine an end result against an objective benchmark of compliance or non-compliance,”22 where the benchmark may be binding law or soft-law instruments such as frameworks, standards, or guidelines.23 Audits can be approached through the lenses of ethics, bias, privacy, trust, and cybersecurity, and are a practical means of operationalising Responsible AI. Two broad types are distinguished: process audits, which mitigate risk across the AI lifecycle through standards such as ISO/IEC 42001 and specially curated AI audit schemes (framed on law and or voluntary frameworks); and product audits, which verify an output against a specific attribute (e.g., bias) using a predetermined verification method. The shared purpose of both is to build justified trust in AI systems.22,23 Liu et al. propose a medical algorithmic audit framework for the proactive investigation of algorithmic errors and failure modes in deployed clinical systems, aimed at identifying patterns of error affecting specific patient subgroups before they cause harm.21 Mökander and Floridi, reporting a 12-month ethics-based audit of AI systems at AstraZeneca, a biopharmaceutical company with a major oncology portfolio, demonstrate that effective auditing requires cross-functional teams and is an ongoing organisational process rather than a one-time event.24 The FUTURE-AI international consensus guideline identifies fairness, universality, traceability, usability, robustness, and explainability as conditions for trustworthy healthcare AI — conditions that only systematic auditing can verify in practice.20 These distinctions matter because a clinically acceptable benchmark score can coexist with poor transportability, weak subgroup performance, or silent performance drift after implementation (Table 1).9,16,21Table 1Practical distinctions between three assurance activities for clinical AI.Table 1ActivityCore questionTypical evidencePrimary actorsValidationIs the system fit for the intended population, task, and setting?External validation, prospective testing, workflow assessment, and subgroup analyses.9,15, 16, 17,20Clinical teams, implementation teams, and health systemsVerificationWas the system built and integrated according to specification?Software testing, interface checks, and version control.18,19,25Developers, engineers, and informatics teamsAuditingIs the system governable, equitable, and accountable over time?Bias review, documentation review, change control, incident monitoring, and periodic reassessment.1,7,9,19, 20, 21,24,26Independent reviewers, governance teams, clinical leaders, and quality and safety teamsAI, artificial intelligence. Why auditing matters for oncology nursing

The strongest lesson from health AI to date is that high apparent performance in development does not guarantee safe or useful clinical deployment.3, 4, 5 In the oncology domain, Somashekhar et al. reported concordance between Watson for Oncology and an expert tumour board for breast cancer treatment recommendations in India, with concordance varying substantially by cancer type and stage.27 Zhou et al. found variable agreement between Watson and Chinese clinical practice, with discordance particularly pronounced where local guidelines, drug availability, and insurance coverage differed from the U.S. training context.10 These findings illustrate that performance claims are context-dependent and that transportability cannot be assumed across institutions or countries. A 2025 cross-sectional study of 903 FDA-approved AI-enabled devices identified incomplete subgroup reporting and limited real-world transferability across the majority of approved systems.11 Together, these findings confirm that auditing is the mechanism by which health systems determine whether an AI tool remains safe, equitable, and operationally reliable after procurement, local configuration, and software updating.

Oncology nurses serve as the essential human control layer within AI governance, uniquely positioned to close this gap: they are often the first clinicians to observe when AI outputs do not fit the patient in front of them, when alerts increase rather than reduce workload, when explanations are insufficient for patient counselling, or when a model’s advice conflicts with local feasibility and patient preferences.8,28 This frontline vantage point is central to detecting clinically meaningful failure modes that may be invisible in technical validation datasets. Safety mindfulness — the sustained cognitive vigilance required to detect latent errors in complex health information technology environments — is a patient safety resource that audit processes must incorporate rather than assume.29 Yet O’Connor et al. found that nurses are rarely involved in the design or governance of AI tools,28 and Zhou et al. confirmed that nursing roles remain confined to end-user adoption rather than quality assurance.8 That exclusion is problematic because many outcomes that determine whether AI helps or harms cancer care — including symptom recognition, escalation timeliness, continuity, patient understanding, emotional burden, and workflow fit — are nursing-sensitive outcomes. If oncology nursing is absent from assurance processes (including audits), health systems risk overvaluing accuracy statistics while undervaluing usability, trust, equity, and the practical consequences of acting on algorithmic advice at the bedside.

Robust risk mitigation for health AI systems depends on diversity in how risks are identified, assessed, and addressed.30 Diverse-input frameworks emphasise that perspectives from disciplines such as sociology, psychology, and anthropology, together with those of clinicians in direct patient contact, reveal risks that technical teams alone cannot see.30 Oncology nurses are precisely the stakeholders these frameworks call for; without structured audit processes that embed nursing voice, the exclusion described above will persist.13

Asia–Pacific health system realities that shape AI assurance

The Asia–Pacific framing of this commentary reflects specific structural conditions that intensify the need for context-sensitive validation, verification, and auditing. The region encompasses more than 60% of the world’s rural population, and many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) face chronic maldistribution of the clinical workforce: in India, for example, more than two-thirds of allopathic doctors and the majority of nurses are concentrated in urban areas, leaving rural cancer services dependent on a small number of overstretched specialists.31 Ugalde et al. reported that even in high-income countries such as Australia, rural communities experience delayed diagnosis, fewer treatment options, and poorer outcomes than urban populations.32 Globally, only about 10% of teletherapy machines are located in LMICs, and approximately 36 countries lack any radiotherapy capacity at all—disparities that disproportionately affect Asia–Pacific patients and that any oncology AI deployed in the region must explicitly account for.33

Three further regional features warrant explicit consideration in AI assurance design. First, linguistic and cultural diversity is unusually high: a single tertiary cancer centre in Southeast Asia may serve patients in five or more languages, complicating informed consent for AI-assisted decisions and limiting the transferability of patient-facing explanations.8, 9, 10 Second, digital infrastructure is uneven: broadband reliability, electronic health record maturity, and imaging-system standards vary widely across and within countries, affecting the verification and continuous monitoring requirements that AI auditing depends upon.5 Third, regulatory frameworks are heterogeneous: some countries (e.g., Singapore, Australia, South Korea, Japan) have advanced national AI governance instruments, while others have no AI-specific regulation in place, which complicates cross-border deployment of audit-ready systems.5,13 These realities mean that audit frameworks designed for high-resource, single-language, regulated environments require deliberate adaptation before they can be applied across the Asia–Pacific region.

The rationale for a sector-specific framework

The frameworks summarised in Table 3 establish that auditing is consistently recognised as a necessary function for ensuring oversight of performance, safety, and fairness in real-world deployment contexts. Algorithmic auditing has emerged as a distinct layer of AI assurance that extends beyond validation and verification by systematically interrogating real-world performance, bias, and governance over time, particularly in high-stakes domains such as healthcare.26 Unlike pre-deployment evaluation, auditing focuses on identifying failure modes that only become visible after deployment, including context-specific errors, subgroup disparities, and unintended workflow consequences.7,26 This reflects growing recognition that AI systems require continuous, lifecycle-oriented accountability rather than one-time assessment.

Existing frameworks, however, provide limited explicit guidance on oncology-specific clinical indicators and on nursing roles within AI auditing. This gap is consequential for four reasons. First, oncology AI operates in a uniquely high-stakes environment where misclassification may alter time-sensitive treatment decisions. Second, patient populations in the Asia–Pacific are ethnically, linguistically, and epidemiologically diverse, and most existing frameworks were not developed with such diversity at their centre, amplifying bias risks that generic frameworks do not adequately address.5,28,37 Third, oncology nurses — the clinicians with the most sustained patient contact — are not currently identified as formal stakeholders in the audit methodologies we reviewed, despite being positioned to detect clinically meaningful failure modes.8,21,28 Fourth, the Watson for Oncology experience across India and China demonstrated that validation alone is insufficient when AI is deployed across heterogeneous health systems with different guidelines, formularies, and patient populations — a lesson that applies directly to the Asia–Pacific region.10,27

The Integrated Clinical AI Audit Principles (ICAAP)

ICAAP synthesises the strongest principle from each of the seven source frameworks into seven operational pillars, each with a corresponding nursing role that demonstrates the value of nurses as key stakeholders providing diverse input into AI auditing. Principles were selected based on their (i) applicability to real-world clinical deployment, (ii) ability to be operationalised into measurable audit actions, (iii) coverage across the AI lifecycle, and (iv) relevance to socio-technical and equity considerations in diverse oncology settings (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Integrated clinical AI audit principles (ICAAP) framework.

Integrated clinical AI audit principles (ICAAP) framework.

Principle 1 – Lifecycle Risk Governance (from NIST)

AI audit activities must be embedded within a structured risk lifecycle, from system design through post-deployment monitoring, with risk mapping that accounts for cancer-specific variables — such as tumour heterogeneity — and population-specific epidemiological profiles.

Nursing role

Oncology nurses contribute to risk mapping by identifying clinical scenarios the AI may encounter and by supplying nursing-sensitive outcome data (e.g., patient distress levels, triage accuracy) for the Measure function.

Principle 2 – Independent Three-Line Governance (from IIA)

AI oversight must be structured across management controls (First Line), quality assurance (Second Line), and independent internal audit (Third Line).

Nursing role

Ward-level nurses maintain structured incident logs (First Line); nursing quality leads participate in performance review (Second Line); nurse representatives are included in independent audit teams (Third Line).

Principle 3 – End-to-End Socio-Technical Bias Assessment (from EDPB E2EST/AA)

Audits must systematically identify and test for bias across the AI lifecycle using the EDPB’s taxonomy of bias sources, including historical, selection, label, aggregation, and deployment bias.

Nursing role

Nurses contribute to the model card by documenting clinical context, to the system map by describing workflow integration, and to bias testing by logging AI outputs disaggregated by patient demographics and computing fairness metrics across protected groups.

Principle 4 – Clinical Error Analysis and Failure-Mode Identification (from the medical algorithmic audit)

Audits must include systematic investigation of algorithmic errors categorised by patient subgroup, with contrastive case analysis to identify recurring failure modes.

Nursing role

Oncology nurses identify cases the AI should have handled correctly — what Liu et al. term “surprising errors” — and document failure modes with actionable implications for retraining or deployment restriction.21

Principle 5 – Human-Centricity and Transparency (from Singapore PDPC)

AI systems must be audited with the affected individual at the centre, with transparency obligations extending to patients, clinicians, and governance bodies.

Nursing role

Nurses conduct patient experience surveys assessing understanding of AI’s role in care, availability of explanations in local languages, and patient empowerment to question AI-derived recommendations.

Principle 6 – Certifiable Management System Integration (from ISO/IEC 42001)

AI audit activities must be embedded within the hospital’s management systems using the Plan–Do–Check–Act cycle.

Nursing role

Nursing departments integrate AI audit metrics into existing accreditation cycles, triggering corrective action when override rates rise or concordance scores decline below pre-defined thresholds.

Principle 7 – Ethics-Based Organisational Auditing (from Mökander and Floridi, and FUTURE-AI)

Audits must assess proportionality, necessity, informed consent, data sovereignty, and broader societal impact.

Nursing role

Oncology nurses participate in cross-functional ethics review panels assessing whether the AI deployment is proportionate to clinical benefit and whether data governance respects cultural and community values.

ICAAP is not solely an aggregation of existing frameworks. It offers a purposive synthesis that explicitly formalizes nursing as a stakeholder group within the audit process; integrates clinical error analysis alongside governance frameworks; contextualises bias assessment for the diversity of Asia–Pacific oncology populations; and connects pre-deployment assurance to post-deployment auditing in a continuous lifecycle.

Operationalising ICAAP: a nurse-led breast cancer screening audit

To illustrate how ICAAP functions in practice, consider an AI-assisted mammographic lesion classification system deployed at a public breast screening programme in a multi-ethnic Southeast Asian country.

During validation, the hospital tests the model on 500 local mammograms. The oncology nurse notices that the sample includes very few women under 40 and almost no women with dense breast tissue patterns common in Asian populations. She flags this to the clinical team, but without a formal protocol mandating nursing input, her concern is documented informally. During verification, IT staff confirm that the software integrates correctly with the hospital’s PACS system, images transmit at correct resolution, and the interface displays outputs as designed. The nurse is not involved.

Six months post-deployment, the nurse observes a pattern: the system flags disproportionate false positives among younger Malay women, triggering unnecessary biopsies and significant patient distress. She also notices confidence scores drifting downward since a software update. These are precisely the findings a structured audit, with nurses positioned as stakeholders for diverse input, would capture. Under ICAAP:

Principle 1 – Lifecycle Risk Governance

The nurse identifies clinical risk scenarios during the Map phase: the screening programme serves a multi-ethnic population with different breast density profiles and cancer incidence patterns, which she documents as risk factors requiring ongoing monitoring. This early risk identification informs the mitigation actions developed in the Measure phase.

Principle 2 – Three-Line Governance

She maintains a structured incident log of AI discrepancies, which is the operational layer (First Line); participates in monthly quality review meetings, which are the oversight layer (Second Line); and presents findings to the annual independent audit team, which is the external assurance layer (Third Line).

Principle 3 – Bias Assessment

She contributes to the model card by documenting the local clinical context, population demographics, imaging equipment, and preparation protocols, and logs AI outputs disaggregated by ethnicity and breast density, computing Risk Difference and Risk Ratio across ethnic groups to detect disparate impact.

Principle 4 – Error Analysis

She systematically reviews false positive and false negative cases, identifies the failure mode (reduced accuracy for BI-RADS density categories C and D), and documents “surprising errors” — cases the AI missed despite obvious clinical findings.21

Principle 5 – Human-Centricity

She conducts structured patient surveys in Malay, Mandarin, Tamil, and English, assessing whether patients understand AI’s role in their screening and whether they feel empowered to seek a second opinion.

Principle 6 – Management System Integration

She integrates AI audit metrics into the department’s Joint Commission International (JCI)/National Accreditation Board for Hospitals & Healthcare Providers (NABH) accreditation quality cycle, with trigger thresholds: if the false positive rate exceeds 12% for any ethnic subgroup or concordance drops below 85% post-update, corrective action is automatically initiated.

Principle 7 – Ethics-Based Auditing

She participates in a quarterly cross-functional review panel — comprising oncologists, radiologists, nurses, IT staff, and a patient representative — that assesses whether the screening programme’s benefit is proportionate to the anxiety caused by false positives, particularly among younger women.

Throughout this case, the nurse-collected indicators in Table 2 — alert burden, override patterns, patient comprehension, escalation timeliness, symptom deterioration, and documentation quality — form the empirical backbone of the audit, transforming the nurse’s role from passive AI end-user to active audit participant and stakeholder, contributing structured clinical intelligence and diverse input that technical auditors alone cannot provide.

A pragmatic audit agenda and future directions

Six priorities emerge for the Asia–Pacific region.

First, health systems should specify intended use before procurement, including the target decision, intended user group, workflow location, escalation pathway, acceptable error profile, and patient populations in whom performance must be demonstrated locally.1,7,9,15,19,20 The FUTURE-AI guideline emphasises that intended-use specification is a prerequisite for meaningful validation and auditing, because without a defined clinical purpose and context it is not possible to determine whether a system is fit for purpose.20 This principle is operationalised in implementation frameworks such as Van der Vegt et al.’s SALIENT framework, which provides structured pre-deployment checklists linking intended use to specific clinical endpoints, user workflows, and governance responsibilities.19

Second, local external validation should be regarded as a minimum requirement rather than a surrogate for implementation success, and should include subgroup analyses relevant to regional practice, with reporting following Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials–Artificial Intelligence (SPIRIT-AI) and Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials–Artificial Intelligence (CONSORT-AI) standards.20,37, 38, 39 The Watson for Oncology experience in India and China demonstrated that concordance with local practice varied substantially by cancer type, disease stage, and treatment availability — findings that only emerged through site-specific validation and would have been amplified by structured auditing.10,27 Reporting should follow SPIRIT-AI and CONSORT-AI standards so that oncology teams can judge intervention details, workflow integration, outcome choice, and reproducibility with transparency.38,39

Third, post-deployment monitoring should be planned before the system goes live and embedded as a routine governance function. Monitoring should include incident reporting, override tracking, periodic review of subgroup performance, and structured oversight for model updates aligned with the FDA’s predetermined change control plan framework.7,9,18, 19, 20, 21 Bedoya et al. propose that local deployment frameworks should mandate performance monitoring dashboards, trigger-based reassessment protocols, and clear escalation pathways when performance degrades below pre-specified thresholds.9 The FDA’s 2025 guidance on predetermined change control plans for AI-enabled devices provides a regulatory model for managing algorithmic modifications within an approved safety envelope.18 The EDPB’s E2EST/AA methodology specifies that audits should result in public reports and periodic follow-up assessments, with adversarial auditing recommended for high-risk systems.25

Fourth, oncology nurses should be embedded in governance structures as data stewards, assessors of workflow impact, contributors to model surveillance, and advocates for patient-centred implementation.1,8,28,40 Audits should adopt the nursing-sensitive indicators in Table 2 (alert burden, override patterns, patient comprehension, escalation timeliness, symptom deterioration, and documentation quality) as part of their routine reporting set. Liu et al.’s medical algorithmic audit methodology is directly operationalisable by nurses: it requires systematic documentation of algorithmic errors categorised by patient demographics, contrastive case analysis comparing correctly and incorrectly classified cases, and identification of failure modes — patterns of recurring error that affect specific patient subgroups.21 Oncology nurses, who manage clinical documentation and observe patient trajectories over time, are uniquely positioned to detect these failure modes at the bedside. Mökander and Floridi’s longitudinal audit of AstraZeneca’s oncology AI further demonstrates that effective auditing requires cross-functional teams with clinical representation, and that ethical challenges — particularly around informed consent and cross-institutional data sharing — are best identified by professionals with direct patient contact.24

Fifth, regional regulatory harmonisation may build on Singapore’s PDPC framework35 and align with ISO/IEC 42001,36 with editorial, research, and policy communities insisting that evidence for oncology AI move beyond retrospective proof-of-concept studies toward pragmatic trials and ongoing audit mechanisms.11,15,17,18,20,38,39 Harmonisation efforts must accommodate the regulatory heterogeneity of the Asia–Pacific region, where countries with mature governance instruments coexist with jurisdictions that lack AI-specific regulation entirely.5,31 Without these shifts, the field risks repeating a familiar pattern in digital health: strong claims of innovation accompanied by weak evidence about transportability, equity, safety, and sustained clinical value.1,11,16,17,20

Sixth, empirical evidence generation and iterative validation are themselves core functions of AI auditing. While existing frameworks provide normative guidance, their legitimacy and relevance in oncology care depend on validation in real-world settings. Health systems in the Asia–Pacific region should therefore prioritise prospective pilot implementations of audit frameworks across diverse care settings — including tertiary cancer centres, district hospitals, and resource-constrained environments — to evaluate feasibility, implementation burden, and impact on clinical outcomes, workflow integration, and equity.

These priorities align with the FUTURE-AI international consensus guideline, which emphasises fairness, universality, traceability, usability, robustness, and explainability as conditions for trustworthy and deployable healthcare AI.20 They also align with the WHO’s guidance on ethics and governance of AI for health, which identifies transparency, accountability, inclusiveness, and responsiveness as foundational principles.1

Conclusions

Clinical AI in oncology should be validated, verified, and audited, but those activities answer different questions and should not be collapsed into a single claim of having been tested.7,9,16 Among them, auditing is the function that links technical performance to real-world accountability by examining whether a system remains safe, equitable, understandable, and governable in routine care.20,21,24 For oncology nursing in the Asia–Pacific, this is a professional as well as a scientific priority because nurses are indispensable to identifying failure modes that matter to patients and to ensuring that AI serves care delivery rather than distorting it.1,8,28,40 The most responsible path forward is not faster deployment alone, but deployment coupled with rigorous local validation, transparent reporting, continuous monitoring, and formal nursing leadership in assurance processes.20,38,39 The frameworks exist, the case is increasingly compelling, and what remains is the institutional will to position the nurse not as an end-user of AI, but as a formal participant in its assurance.
