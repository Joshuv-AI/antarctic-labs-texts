Effect of waste paper aggregate and polyethylene terephthalate on mortar performanCE
Authors: Houmame Harabi, Mohamed Lyes Kamel Khouadjia, Sara Bensalem, Haytham F Isleem, Mohammad Khishe
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractConcrete’s widespread use in construction is increasing due to urbanization and population growth. While it offers durability and strength, its production poses environmental challenges. Researchers and global initiatives are working towards more sustainable concrete production methods and effective waste management, particularly for paper waste (WPA) and polyethylene terephthalate waste (PET). This study aims to investigate the effects of waste paper aggregate (WPA) on the flow time, compressive strength, flexural strength, and thermal conductivity of mortars. Furthermore, it explores the potential of polyethylene terephthalate (PET) to enhance the compressive and flexural strengths of these mortars. To conduct this study, the materials were characterized using EDX spectroscopy and SEM analysis. Gwyddion software was employed to conduct a comprehensive analysis of the grain segmentation and microstructural characteristics of the WPA mortars. Subsequently, four distinct mixtures were selected for further investigation. The first mixture involved substituting sand with various percentages of waste paper aggregate (WPA) (1–4%) without PET. The second mixture included WPA (1–4%) and 5% PET. Due to the ineffective plasticizer in the first two mixtures, the third mixture replaced 20% of quarry sand with dune sand (DS) while maintaining the same percentages of WPA and PET as in the previous mixtures. The results indicate that mortars RM1 (0% WPA + 0% PET) and RM2 (0% WPA + 0% PET + 20% DS) demonstrated the best flow time. This suggests that incorporating WPA and PET into the mortar mix can negatively impact its workability, making it more challenging to handle, shape, and apply. Sample PW4, composed of 4% WPA and 0% PET, displayed a significant increase in compressive strength from 46.36 MPa to 49.81 MPa and flexural strength from 10.5 MPa to 11.47 MPa, indicating that incorporating WPA into the mortars can enhance resistance to both compressive and flexural strengths. This same mixture, PW4, demonstrated that the mortars are poor conductors of heat and would be more effective as thermal insulators. Introduction

The construction industry is actively seeking sustainable concrete alternatives to reduce the environmental impact of traditional concrete production1,2. Researchers are exploring innovative methods to promote a circular economy3 and conserve natural resources4–9 by encouraging the replacement of natural coarse aggregate with alternative materials like steel slag, ceramic, brick, and glass waste, and to reduce waste generation, Nihar Ranjan Mohanta & al10 provide a comprehensive review. Challenges may arise in terms of consistency, durability, and long-term performance, particularly when using materials with low mechanical properties. To address these challenges, ongoing research and development are essential to optimize the use of waste-based materials like paper and plastic in mortar and concrete formulations, ensuring their sustainability and reliability11–13.

Previous research has demonstrated that concrete incorporating paper waste, such as recycled office paper, newspapers, and cardboard, exhibits significant advantages, including enhanced insulation properties and increased durability, making it a promising material for sustainable and eco-friendly construction14–17. Shermale and Varma. et al.18 and Krade. et al.19 have found that incorporating paper waste into concrete can reduce its density by up to 15%, making it a viable option for building construction, especially in regions with seismic activity or limited load-bearing capacity. Furthermore, as confirmed by Sangrutsamee et al.17, the addition of different types of recycled paper reduces the thermal conductivity and apparent density of mortar, making it a more energy-efficient and lightweight building material. However, as noted by Hurtado et al.20 and Yeon et al.21, the high water absorption capacity of recycled paper waste can adversely affect the workability of the mixture. This can lead to increased difficulty in handling and applying the material, potentially impacting the final quality of the construction. To enhance workability while incorporating WPA, optimizing the water-to-cement ratio and using additives like plasticizers or dune sand can be effective. Polycarboxylate-based superplasticizers, as investigated by Ghosh and Mandal22, can significantly reduce water consumption while simultaneously enhancing workability and mechanical strength. Alternatively, dune sand, as studied by Bederina et al.23 and Khouadjia et al.24, offers a sustainable and locally sourced alternative to traditional construction materials. It can improve the physical properties of mortar and concrete, such as capillary absorption, thereby enhancing durability and performance. Even with the use of plasticizers or dune sand, it is crucial to carefully control the percentage of waste paper added, as an excessive amount can still decrease the workability and overall strength of the mortar, as demonstrated by Huang et al.25. Studies by Balwaik et al.26, Ferrándiz Mas et al.27 have shown that incorporating recycled paper into mortar can significantly improve its mechanical properties, including flexural and compressive strength, making it a promising material for construction applications that demand durability and load-bearing capacity. Additionally, Nehdi et al.28 found that the inclusion of recycled paper in mortar can significantly reduce plastic shrinkage and cracking, leading to improved durability and longevity. Therefore, the use of paper waste in construction materials, such as concrete and mortar, represents a significant opportunity to valorize waste streams and promote sustainable building practices.

On the other hand, Polyethylene Terephthalate (PET), derived from recycled plastic waste, offers significant advantages. Its properties, including lightweight, chemical resistance, impact resistance, and formability, as highlighted by Siddique et al.29 make it a promising material for various applications. Studies by Li et al.30 shown that incorporating plastic aggregates into concrete can decreased workability, compressive strength, while increasing porosity. The workability of concrete with plastic waste is influenced by various factors, such as the water-to-cement ratio, the quantity of plastic aggregates used, and the size and shape of the plastic aggregates, as noted by Gu and Ozbakkaloglu31, Silva et al.32 and Li et al.30. Research by Ganesan et al.33 found that the incorporation of 5% PET in mortar improves both compressive and flexural strength, thus increasing durability and resilience. Almeshal et al.34 and Rahmani et al.35 observed a slight reduction in compressive strength when PET aggregates partially replaced natural aggregates, even with replacement percentage up to 20% and 15% respectively. Nadjai et al.36 and Mohammed and Faqe Rahim37 highlight that incorporating PET fibers improve resistance to microcracking and enhance their absorption capacity in construction materials. Rahmani et al.35 also demonstrated that the elastic modulus of concrete decreased linearly as the replacement ratio of PET aggregates increases from 0 to 15%. Frigione et al.38 observed that PET- containing concrete has higher drying shrinkage, which may affect durability in environments with temperature fluctuations due to its lower elastic modulus. Consequently, using plastic waste in the construction industry as a substitute for natural aggregates is a promising approach to recycle and repurpose plastic materials, providing both environmental and economic advantage39,40. Current standards (ACI 318, Eurocode 2) have not yet established guidelines for the incorporation of waste materials into concrete. Moreover, the conflicting research findings on the mechanical properties of mortar and concrete reinforced with plastic and paper waste underscore the need for further investigation to fully realize its potential and promote its widespread adoption.

This research aims to assess the feasibility of using waste paper aggregates (WPA), polyethylene terephthalate (PET), and dune sand (DS) as sand substitutes in mortar formulations. It examines the effects of WPA on the flow time, compressive strength, flexural strength, and thermal conductivity of mortar. Additionally, the study explores PET’s potential to improve compressive and flexural strengths and evaluates dune sand’s contribution to enhancing the workability of these mortar mixtures. To our knowledge, no study in the literature has examined the influence of replacing aggregate with waste plastic aggregates (WPA), polyethylene terephthalate (PET), and dune sand. A total of 138 samples of 18 formulations were produced for this purpose. All the tests were carried out in triplicate. Gwyddion software was employed to analyze the grain structure of WPA mortars. This involved segmenting individual grains within sample images to quantify their size, shape, and distribution. Grain segmentation of WPA mortars using Gwyddion software indicated a clear alignment pattern: shorter WPA fibers were primarily oriented along the width of the section, whereas longer WPA fibers were predominantly aligned with its length.

Mix proportions

In this study, we prepared a reference mortar mixture consisting of 75% sand and 25% cement, with a water-to-cement (W/C) ratio of 0.5. To investigate variations, we systematically replaced volumes of sand with corresponding volumes of paper aggregate (1%, 2%, 3%, and 4%). For this experiment, we will conduct two experimental campaigns (S1 and S2). The first campaign (S1), will include a plasticizer at a specified percentage of 1.33%, while the second campaign will incorporate 20% dune sand. This will result in a total of eight distinct mixtures for each campaign. The second campaign (S2), will consist of repeating the eight mixtures from the first campaign with the addition of 5% PET as a sand substitute, creating a new set of eight mixtures. In total, 16 mixtures were prepared, with a reference control. The detailed compositions are shown in Table 6.

Testing procedures

Flow time

The flow time of the mortars was characterized using the Maniabilimetre LCPC test, according to standard NF P 18-45250. The flow time test involves a rectangular mold with a movable wall and a vibrator. The test measures the time it takes for mortar, under vibration after the movable wall is removed, to reach a marked point on the mold’s inner surface. Figure 4 shows the LCPC Maniabilimetre.

LCPC Maniabilimetre.

Flexural and compressive strength

For these two tests, we used a hydraulic press with a capacity of 3000 kN, following the standard NF EN 196-151. First, we used a 3-point bending device to measure the flexural strength of three specimens. After the test, each specimen was divided in half, and each section was placed in a compression device. The final flexural strength result was the average of three tests, while the compression strength result was the average of six tests. Figure 5 shows the flexural and compressive strength testing setup.

(A) 4 cm x 4 cm x 16 cm samples (B) Compression test (C) Flexural strength.

Thermal conductivity test

The thermal conductivity (TC) test of the mortars samples was assessed as per the German standard DIN 52,61252 using WL 376 device. Each sample, with identical dimensions (30 cm x 30 cm x 2 cm), is placed between a heated plate and a water-cooled plate. A clamping mechanism ensures constant contact pressure and thermal contact. Sensors monitor temperatures at the cooling water inlet and outlet, as well as at the center of the plates. The device includes software for precise adjustment of hot and cold plate temperatures during the process. Figure 6 shows the flexural and compressive strength testing setup.

Results and discussion

Microstructure analysis

The SEM image of mortar PW4 (Fig. 7A) shows that WPA aggregates have a random fibrous structure, creating irregularly shaped pores. This fibrous structure enables high water absorption capacity while acting as a barrier that hinders water migration to the aggregate surface.

SEM– EDX spectrum of mortar PW4 (A) SEM image (5 μm) (B) : EDX spectrum of mortar PW4.

On the other hand, the SEM image shows good adhesion between the cement particles and the fibers in the aggregate structure. The fibers appear to be non-uniformly dispersed and embedded within a cement matrix which is characterized by its denser suggesting good adhesion between the two components. This interpretation is in accordance with paper53,54. Another study55 demonstrated that non-uniform fiber dispersion within the matrix adversely affected the mechanical properties of waste paper fiber-cement composites. Therefore, the presence of waste paper fibers can potentially impact the mechanical properties of the mortar, such as compressive and flexural strength. This is confirmed by the EDX analysis of the sample (Fig. 7B), which indicates the presence of Oxygen (O) likely from the mortar’s binder, Calcium (Ca) prominent peak, confirming the presence of calcium compounds, typically from the cement, traces of silicium (Si), indicating the presence of silica, likely from the sand in the mortar and Carbon (C) suggesting the presence of organic materials (WPA) which could potentially affect the mortar’s mechanical properties. The presence of organic matter can create voids which increased porosity within the mortar matrix, increasing its permeability to water and other harmful agents.

Flow time of mortars

The flow time test, performed according to standard NF P 18–45250, was used to evaluate the workability of the mortars by measuring the time required for the mortar to flow under vibration. The results of this testes are presented in Fig. 8.

Flow time of fresh mortars without PET and with (A): plasticizer (B): dune sand.

Figure 8 shows that reference mortars RM1 and RM2, containing plasticizer and dune sand, exhibited the lowest flow times of 1.8 and 1.6 s, respectively, indicating relatively low viscosity. Mortars incorporating 1% and 2% WPA and plasticizer (PW1 and PW2) and those incorporating 1% and 2% WPA and dune sand (DSW1 and DSW2) showed slightly increased flow times compared to the reference mortars (RM1 and RM2) but still within a reasonable range according standard NF P 18–45250. Adding more than 2% WPA significantly increased the flow time, reaching 55 s for mortars with plasticizer and 47 s for mortars with dune sand. This increase in viscosity is proportional to the percentage of paper added, regardless of the presence of dune sand or plasticizer. While the initial addition of plasticizer and dune sand aimed to improve workability, the results unexpectedly revealed that both additives contributed to increased viscosity, leading to the formation of very firm mortars. It seems that the impact of waste paper may have overshadowed the effects of these additives. These findings can be attributed to the structural properties of WPA, which significantly influence its workability by altering its water absorption and viscosity characteristics. These results align with the findings reported in previous studies20,21 concerning the fibrous nature of WPA endows it with absorptive properties, enabling it to draw water from the mortar matrix. This increased water absorption leads to less water availability in the paste, subsequently decreasing the effective water-to-cement ratio (w/c ratio), a critical factor influencing mortar’s workability.

Compressive strength

The 28-day compressive strength was determined by averaging the results of six individual tests, conducted in accordance with standard procedures NF EN 196-151. The results of this testes are presented in Figs. 9 and 10.

Compressive strength of samples without PET and with: (A): plasticizer (B): dune sand.

Compressive strength of samples with PET and with: (A): plasticizer (B): dune sand.

As illustrated in Fig. 9A,B a clear upward trend is observed: an increase in the percentage of added paper (from 1 to 4%) corresponds to a significant enhancement in compressive strength. The mixture containing 4% WPA and a plasticizer showed a 6.92% increase in compressive strength compared to the control mortar RM1. Similarly, the mixture containing 4% WPA and 20% dune sand exhibited a more substantial 19.75% improvement over the control mortar RM2. The increased compressive strength in mortars containing waste paper (WPA) can be attributed to two primary factors. Firstly, as the WPA percentage increases from 1 to 4%, it absorbs water from the mortar matrix, effectively reducing the water-to-cement ratio. This leads to a denser and stronger mortar due to decreased porosity and enhanced hydration. Secondly, the fibrous structure of WPA acts as a physical reinforcement within the mortar matrix. These fibers bridge voids and cracks, improving the overall structural integrity and resistance to compressive loads. This finding aligns with previous research conducted by Balwaik et al.26, Ferrándiz Mas et al.27, and Nehdi et al.28, which attributed the increased compressive strength in WPA-containing mortars to the reinforcing effect of the fibers and reduced water-cement ratio.

Regarding the use of PET, the results presented in Fig. 10. demonstrate a decrease in compressive strength compared to the mixture without PET. This decrease reaches 14.09% for the mixture containing 3% WPA and 5% PET and up to 17.62% for the mixture with 4% WPA and 5% PET. The observed results can be attributed to the smooth, flat surface of PET (Fig. 2B), which may hinder adhesion between PET and the cement paste. Moreover, the presence of PET particles within the mortar matrix can create microscopic voids, potentially hindering the cement hydration process and impeding the formation of a dense, robust microstructure. This could negatively impact the overall mechanical properties and durability of the mortar. This result corroborates previous studies by Li et al.30 Almeshal et al.34 and Rahmani et al.35, which suggests that replacing natural aggregates with PET particles in mortar can impede the cement hydration process. This disruption can result in a decrease in mechanical properties like compressive, as well as a reduction in durability.

Flexural strength

Flexural strength was determined by averaging the results of three tests, conducted in accordance with standard NF EN 196-151. The results of this testes are presented in Figs. 11 and 12.

Flexural strength of samples without PET and with: (A): plasticizer (B): dune sand.

Flexural strength of samples with PET and with: (A): plasticizer (B): dune sand.

Figure 11A clearly demonstrates a positive correlation between the percentage of added paper and flexural strength. This finding corroborates previous research by Balwaik et al.26 and Ferrándiz Mas et al.27, which demonstrated enhanced flexural strength with the incorporation of WPA. This effect is particularly evident when comparing control mortar RM1 with sample PW4 containing 4% WPA and plasticizer, which exhibits up to a 10% increase in flexural strength. Additionally, as shown in Fig. 11B, incorporating 20% dune sand significantly improved flexural strength, with DSW4 exhibiting a 44.85% increase compared to RM2. The finer grain size of dune sand contributes to a denser, more compact mortar microstructure, leading to a slight increase in its flexural strength. The enhancement in flexural strength observed in WPA-containing mortars can likely be attributed to the same mechanisms that contribute to the increased compressive strength. WPA functions as a physical reinforcement within the mortar matrix, contributing to a denser microstructure with fewer voids. This reduced porosity enhances the material’s resistance to crack initiation and propagation, thereby improving the overall durability of the samples.

The incorporation of PET fibers, as illustrated in Fig. 12A and B, can significantly reduce the flexural strength of mortar. This finding contradicts previous research by Gu and Ozbakkaloglu31, Silva et al.32 and Li et al.30. Ganesan et al.33, who observed improved flexural strength with the addition of 5% PET to mortar. This discrepancy may be attributed to factors such as fiber length, fiber orientation, and the interfacial bonding between the fibers and the matrix. Waste paper aggregates (WPA) were more effective than PET in enhancing the flexural strength of the mortars. PET additions led to a decrease in flexural strength, reaching up to 27.96% in the mixture with 3% WPA, 5% PET, and 1.33% plasticizer, and up to 31.78% in the mixture with 3% WPA, 5% PET, and 20% dune sand. The presence of PET particles within the mortar can create microscopic voids, which can hinder the cement hydration process. This interruption in hydration can lead to a weaker and less dense microstructure, ultimately resulting in a decrease in overall strength. Under deformation, these microscopic voids act as stress absorbers, distributing stress more uniformly throughout the material. This prevents the localized concentration of stress that can lead to crack formation, thereby enhancing the material’s ductility (Fig. 13) .

Thermal conductivity

The thermal conductivity of the mortars was determined according to the standard DIN 52,61252. The results of these tests are presented graphically in Fig. 14, providing insights into the thermal insulation properties of the different mortar formulations.

Thermal conductivity of samples without PET and with: (A): plasticizer (B): dune sand.

Figure 14A,B demonstrate that the reference mortars RM1 and RM2, devoid of WPA, exhibit the highest thermal conductivity. As the incorporation of WPA increases from 1 to 4%, a corresponding decrease in thermal conductivity is observed. This reduction is most pronounced in mortars PW4 (4% WPA and 1.33% plasticizer), reaching up to 38.78%, and in mortars DSW4 (4% WPA and 20% DS), reaching up to 23.10%. This implies that these materials are poor conductors of heat and would be more effective as thermal insulators. This can be attributed to the random distribution of WPA fibers may lead to voids that the cement paste cannot completely fill, and to the potential reduction in the overall density of the mortar. Lower-density materials typically exhibit lower thermal conductivity. This is consistent with the findings of Khedari.J et al.16 and Sangrutsamee, V. et al.17 which demonstrated that mortar incorporating paper waste, exhibits superior insulation properties and enhanced durability, making it a promising sustainable building material.

Characterization of WPA mortars with software Gwyddion

Gwyddion software was used to analyze the fiber distribution and segmentation of WPA mortar surfaces from SEM images. Indeed, Gwyddion software was employed in previous studies56,57 to generate a graph illustrating the quantity of WPA fibers distributed across the length and width of the section. Gwyddion software was employed to conduct a detailed analysis of the grain structure in WPA mortars. Advanced SEM image processing techniques were used to identify and separate individual grains within sample images which generated curves illustrating the quantity and distribution of fibers according to their width and length. This enabled the quantification of various grain parameters, such as size, shape, and arrangement. The segmentation and distribution of WPA mortars are depicted in Figs. 15 and 16; Table 7.

Surface fiber distribution with Gwyddion software.

The quantity of fibers obtained using Gwyddion software distributed at: (A) the width of section (B) length of section.

The Gwyddion analysis of the segmented surface (Fig. 15) reveals a heterogeneous distribution of WPA fibers. The fibers exhibit irregular spacing and alignment, indicating a random orientation. The graphs in Fig. 16A,B, which depict the distribution of fiber quantities based on width and length in the analyzed section, reveal a significant concentration of fibers in the shorter fiber range (Dmax=3). This finding suggests that a majority of the WPA fibers within the mortar are relatively short. This is likely due to the sieving process employed on the WPA fibers prior to incorporation into the mortar.

As detailed in Table 7, an analysis of WPA fiber distribution using Gwyddion software indicates that fibers with a maximum diameter (Dmax) of 0.8641 are most likely to be found at the width of the Sect. (47%). Conversely, WPA fibers with a Dmax of 1.0381 tend to be concentrated at the length of the Sect. (33%). The remaining diameter groups demonstrated a consistent trend, with shorter WPA fibers preferentially aligning with the width of the section and longer WPA fibers aligning with the length. This alignment behavior is likely influenced by the fiber aspect ratio and the flow characteristics of the mortar mix during casting. Shorter WPA fibers, being less rigid, are more likely to become randomly oriented within the matrix or align with the flow direction during mixing. In contrast, longer WPA fibers, due to their greater stiffness, tend to maintain their original orientation or align with the direction of the applied force.

Conclusion

This study investigated the effects of recycled paper (WPA) fibers and polyethylene terephthalate (PET) on the performance of mortar. The major conclusions are summarized as follows:

The addition of waste paper aggregate (WPA) reduces mortar workability due to its fibrous nature and high-water absorption. Plasticizers and dune sand can improve workability, but their effect diminishes with increasing WPA content.

The use of waste paper aggregate (WPA) proved beneficial in enhancing both compressive and flexural strengths. WPA’s fibrous structure reinforces, bridges voids, and improves mortar integrity, leading to denser and stronger results.

Further enhancements were observed when dune sand was used, leading to an 11% increase in compressive strength and a significant 44.85% increase in flexural strength.
