The Potential of Apelin-Targeted Therapeutic Lymphangiogenesis Therapy for Viral Myocarditis
Authors: Yuuki Shimizu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: apelin, cardiac lymphangiogenesis, cardioprotection, myocarditis

The lymphatic vasculature has long been relegated to the status of a passive drainage system; however, burgeoning evidence now repositions it as a sophisticated orchestrator of tissue proteostasis and immune surveillance. As elucidated by recent insights into the meningeal and peripheral lymphatic niches,1 these vessels facilitate a meticulously regulated transport of antigens and immune cells, thereby modulating the delicate balance between peripheral tolerance and inflammatory activation. Furthermore, the discovery of specialized lymphatic subtypes highlights their organ-specific roles in metabolic regulation and fluid dynamics. Recognizing the lymphatic system as a proactive participant in disease pathogenesis — ranging from neurodegeneration to metastatic progression — is no longer optional but essential for the advancement of precision medicine.

The cardiac lymphatic vasculature, once regarded as a mere anatomical bystander, has emerged as a fundamental determinant of myocardial homeostasis and injury recovery. Historical observations initially hinted at its structural complexity,2 yet contemporary evidence underscores its active role in modulating the myocardial microenvironment. Following ischemic insult, the expansion of the lymphatic network—lymphangiogenesis—is not merely a reactive phenomenon but a prerequisite for efficient infarct healing; as demonstrated in seminal studies, the impairment of this process exacerbates myocardial edema and chronic inflammation.3 Recent insights further elucidate that lymphatic dysfunction is a hallmark of the failing heart, where compromised fluid clearance contributes to the progression of cardiac fibrosis and diastolic impairment.4 Consequently, pharmacologic or genetic augmentation of cardiac lymphatic function represents a transformative therapeutic frontier, offering the potential to resolve persistent inflammation and promote functional regeneration.5 Integrating these perspectives, it is clear that the "forgotten circulation" of the heart is, in fact, a central orchestrator of cardiovascular resilience, necessitating its inclusion in future precision-based strategies for heart failure.

The intricate interplay between cardiac inflammatory diseases and the cardiac lymphatic vasculature represents a pivotal mechanism governing myocardial homeostasis, edema resolution, and inflammatory resolution. In myocarditis models, cytokine-driven (eg, interleukin-1β) impairment of lymphatic function through the COX-2/PGE2 pathway leads to compromised drainage, perpetuating mediator accumulation, sustained inflammation, and eventual transition to dilated cardiomyopathy.4 Human histopathological evidence corroborates these observations, revealing lymphatic hyperplasia juxtaposed with dense inflammatory infiltrates within the myocardium. Such maladaptive remodeling aligns with broader insights demonstrating that lymphatic insufficiency exacerbates myocardial edema and fibrosis across diverse cardiac pathologies.6 Consequently, targeted promotion of lymphangiogenesis—via vascular growth factor C (VEGF-C) or analogous interventions—emerges as a promising therapeutic avenue to enhance immune cell trafficking, curtail chronic inflammation, and attenuate adverse cardiac remodeling in inflammatory heart diseases.

Apelin, an endogenous bioactive peptide originally identified as the cognate ligand for the orphan G protein-coupled receptor APJ (also known as APLNR), exerts profound regulatory effects on cardiovascular homeostasis. Derived from a 77-amino-acid prepropeptide and processed into multiple isoforms—most notably apelin-13 and apelin-36—the peptide is widely expressed in endothelial cells, cardiomyocytes, and vascular tissues. Through APJ activation, apelin promotes potent positive inotropy, vasodilation, reduced peripheral resistance, and enhanced cardiac output while mitigating preload and afterload.7 These actions confer cardioprotective benefits, including attenuation of ischemia-reperfusion injury, suppression of fibrosis, and amelioration of adverse remodeling in heart failure models. Consequently, the apelin/APJ axis has emerged as a promising therapeutic target for cardiovascular disorders, with synthetic analogs and agonists under active investigation to harness its vasodilatory and contractile properties.

The emerging evidence underscores the pivotal role of apelin in modulating lymphangiogenesis and lymphatic vessel maturation, with particular relevance to cardiovascular pathophysiology. Apelin, acting via its receptor APJ, promotes lymphatic endothelial cell migration, tube formation, and vessel sprouting in developmental and pathological contexts, as demonstrated in zebrafish models where apelin signaling is essential for lymphatic development through AKT activation independent of VEGF-C/VEGFR3 pathways.8 In adult settings, apelin expression is restricted to newly formed lymphatic endothelium post-myocardial infarction, where genetic ablation results in dilated, leaky lymphatics associated with heightened inflammation and impaired remodeling, whereas apelin overexpression restores vessel integrity and attenuates proinflammatory responses.9 These findings position the apelin/APJ axis as a critical regulator of lymphatic function and a promising therapeutic target for enhancing lymphatic-mediated resolution in cardiac inflammatory and ischemic diseases.

In the complex landscape of acute viral myocarditis, the inflammatory fire often leaves a trail of architectural devastation. Although much attention has historically been paid to the coronary vasculature and myocardial fibrosis, a compelling study now turns the spotlight toward a neglected key player of cardiac homeostasis: the lymphatic system. In this issue of JACC: Basic to Translational Science, the study from Lin et al10 shows that coxsackievirus B3–induced acute viral myocarditis triggers pathologic cardiac lymphangiogenesis using a robust combination of murine models and cellular assays. Crucially, they reveal that this is not merely a quantitative expansion but a qualitative failure; the newly formed vessels exhibit profound structural dysfunction, leading to impaired drainage, persistent inflammation, and subsequent cardiac decline.

The true brilliance of this work lies in its mechanistic discovery of Apelin as a pivotal regulator of this process. The study shows that overexpressing Apelin in cardiomyocytes effectively rescues the heart from coxsackievirus B3-induced damage. Beyond simply promoting vessel growth, apelin reinforces the cardiac lymphatic endothelial barrier function via the AKT signaling pathway. By stabilizing these "leaky" pathologic vessels, apelin restores effective drainage, thereby clearing the inflammatory milieu and preserving cardiac function. What makes this study particularly noteworthy—and moves it beyond conventional vascular biology—is its comprehensive approach. By investigating not only the kinetics of lymphangiogenesis but also the functional integrity of the lymphatic barrier under pathologic conditions, this research offers a unique perspective. It provides crucial findings that extend to a sophisticated functional analysis, shifting the paradigm from "how many vessels are made" to "how well those vessels work." This work identifies the cardiomyocyte-derived Apelin/AKT axis as a promising therapeutic target, offering a novel strategy and restore homeostasis in the inflamed heart (Figure 1).

There are some limitations to the current study. First, this study did not set up an apelin antagonist (such as ML221, etc). Perhaps a negative control group could be added to verify the specific effect of apelin in enhancing cardiac lymphatic return and enhancing viral myocarditis. These will need to be verified by future additional experiments. Nevertheless, in the meantime, the current study has shed light on a new mechanistic concept regarding the role of apelin in response to viral-induced myocarditis and has opened the door to targeting apelin as a cardioprotective strategy through lymphangiogenesis.
