S100A10 drives fibrinolytic failure in sepsis
Authors: David M Waisman
Published: 2026

TO THE EDITOR:

The clinical observations reported by Lim et al1 documenting sepsis-specific proteolysis of annexin A2 (A2) in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells and its association with impaired cell-surface fibrinolysis and organ dysfunction1 are compelling. However, the mechanistic framework used to interpret these findings misidentifies the plasminogen-binding subunit of the A2-S100A10 heterotetramer (AIIt), with direct consequences for the therapeutic strategy proposed by the authors.

Lim et al state that AIIt “binds plasminogen at K308-R309 near the C-terminus” of A2, and their Figure 1A depicts K308-R309 as the plasminogen-binding site.1 This claim repeats an earlier model2 but does not reflect the published biochemistry. Using recombinant subunits, Kassam et al3 showed that isolated S100A10 stimulates tPA-dependent plasminogen activation 46-fold, whereas A2 alone stimulates only twofold, establishing S100A10, not A2, as the dominant plasminogen-regulatory subunit. Deletion of the 2 C-terminal lysines of S100A10 (Lys95-Lys96) reduced the stimulatory activity of reconstituted AIIt to ∼12% of wild type. By contrast, equivalent mutations in A2 at K307 and K328 had no significant effect on activity.3

MacLeod et al4 addressed the orientation issue directly by anchoring AIIt onto a phospholipid bilayer on a BIAcore biosensor chip, presenting the complex in the same membrane-bound configuration it adopts on the cell surface. Under these conditions, membrane-associated A2 failed to bind plasminogen or tPA by 3 independent approaches, whereas S100A10 bound both (dissociation constant [Kd] for plasminogen, 1.81μM; Kd for tissue plasminogen activator [tPA], 0.45μM). Removing the C-terminal lysines from S100A10 abolished these interactions without affecting plasmin binding. The C-terminal Lys95-Lys96 of S100A10, not K308-R309 of A2, is the physiological tPA and plasminogen-binding site of AIIt.4

Lim et al's own data support this conclusion and are difficult to reconcile with the K308-R309 model. The C-terminal antibody (CST D11G2, which recognizes the epitope surrounding F307) detects proteolytic fragments in all 35 septic samples examined, confirming that the region encompassing K308-R309 survives proteolysis and remains membrane-associated.1 Yet, cell-surface plasmin generation is severely reduced. If K308-R309 of A2 were the functional plasminogen receptor, its survival should preserve binding capacity. It does not point directly to S100A10 as the functional unit. Even if tPA recruitment via A2 is separately impaired by proteolysis, the biosensor data of MacLeod et al establish that membrane-anchored A2 does not bind plasminogen at all, making K308-R309 of A2 an implausible plasminogen receptor regardless of the activation step.

The subcellular fractionation data further localize the cleavage site. Lim et al show that proteolytic fragments of A2 appear exclusively in the membrane fraction and not the cytosol.1 Because A2 membrane attachment requires the intact core domain (residues ∼40-334), membrane-retained fragments must preserve this region, placing the cut within the N-terminal tail, precisely where the S100A10-binding domain resides (residues 1-14). This is consistent with well-established biochemistry: Glenney and Tack5 and Drust and Creutz6 were the first to show that limited proteolysis of AIIt releases the N-terminus of A2 along with the p11 subunit, and MacLeod et al4 subsequently mapped plasmin cleavage by N-terminal sequencing to the bond between Lys27 and Ala28, squarely within the S100A10-binding domain. Lim et al themselves acknowledge that “even partial cleavage of the N-terminus is likely to render A2 inactive as a fibrinolytic receptor, given the critical binding sites for p11 and tPA that it harbors,”1 a statement that implicitly endorses the S100A10-centric model.

The interaction of plasminogen kringles with internal lysine residues is governed by more than the mere presence of a lysine in a protein sequence. The lysine-binding site of plasminogen kringles comprises 3 coordinating centers: an anionic center (2 conserved Asp/Glu residues), a hydrophobic groove, and a cationic center. Together, these orient the lysine side chain for proper binding. The best-characterized internal plasminogen-binding motifs are found in streptococcal and Haemophilus influenzae enolases. The Streptococcus pneumoniae enolase motif 248FYDKERKVY256 contains a glutamate (E252) whose flanking position is essential for plasminogen binding, as demonstrated directly by site-directed mutagenesis.7 Similarly, the H influenzae enolase peptide 251EFYNKENGMYE261 engages plasminogen through direct hydrogen-bond contacts involving E251 and K255, as shown by in silico and in vitro assays.8 In both cases, a glutamate residue flanking the critical lysine is an indispensable structural feature of the binding interface. The sequence proposed by Lim et al as the annexin A2 plasminogen-binding site, IRSEFKRKYGKSLYYYIQ, contains no acidic residue immediately adjacent to or flanking K308. The glutamate within this sequence is several residues removed from K308 and is not positioned to coordinate the anionic center of a plasminogen kringle; that is, the requirement for neighboring negatively charged residues is not met at K308-R309. This conclusion is reinforced by functional data from our laboratory showing that the K307T annexin A2 mutant retains essentially wild-type cofactor activity in the tPA–plasminogen activation assay, with activity only marginally above the background level of bovine serum albumin.

Taken together, the data presented by Lim et al are most directly explained as follows: a sepsis-induced membrane-associated serine protease cleaves A2 within the N-terminal tail, destroying the S100A10-binding domain and releasing S100A10 from the complex. Unanchored S100A10 is then rapidly degraded via ubiquitylation, removing the functional plasminogen and tPA receptor from the cell surface. The C-terminal A2 fragments detected by D11G2 are membrane-retained structural remnants with no fibrinolytic function, because S100A10 is no longer present.

This distinction matters for therapy. The relevant target for preserving cell-surface fibrinolysis in sepsis is the N-terminal S100A10-anchoring domain of A2, not K308. Strategies aimed at reconstituting or protecting AIIt integrity should focus on that domain, not the C-terminal region of A2. However, we acknowledge that the broader therapeutic implications proposed by Lim et al may not depend entirely on the precise identification of the plasminogen-binding site on annexin A2. Their study reports sepsis-associated annexin A2 proteolysis, reduced cell-surface plasmin generation, and an association between annexin A2 loss and organ dysfunction, observations that could remain of biological and therapeutic interest even if the assignment of plasminogen binding to K308-R309 is incorrect. Our concern is therefore not with the possibility that annexin A2–directed interventions may have therapeutic relevance, but with the specific mechanistic claim that plasminogen binds directly to K308-R309 of annexin A2 rather than to the C-terminal lysines of the S100A10 subunit. We agree that potential therapeutic approaches arising from the observations of Lim et al need not depend entirely on the precise plasminogen-binding site assigned to annexin A2. For example, strategies aimed at preventing annexin A2 proteolysis, preserving annexin A2–dependent fibrinolytic activity, or inhibiting the relevant A2-cleaving protease could still be of interest even if plasminogen does not bind directly to K308-R309.
