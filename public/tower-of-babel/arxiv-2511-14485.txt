Chapter 1
Mathematical and Statistical Preliminaries

1.1
Probability Spaces and Random Variables

To rigorously describe random phenomena, we begin with the concept of a

probability space
, which formalizes the notion of uncertainty and provides
the mathematical foundation for probability theory
[
7
,
19
,
41
]
.
This framework specifies which outcomes are possible, which subsets of outcomes
are measurable, and how numerical probabilities are consistently assigned.

1.1
Sample Space.

The
sample space
is a set

\Omega=\{\,\omega:\text{possible outcomes of an experiment}\,\}.

Each element
\omega\in\Omega
represents an
elementary outcome
.
The sample space constitutes the domain on which events and
random variables (RVs)
are defined.

1.2
.

For a single toss of a fair coin,
\Omega=\{\text{H},\text{T}\}
.
For two coin tosses,
\Omega=\{\text{HH},\text{HT},\text{TH},\text{TT}\}
.
If we observe a particle’s position in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, then
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

1.3
\sigma
-Algebra.

A
\sigma
-algebra
on
\Omega
is a collection

\lx@glossaries@gls@link{notation}{sigma-algebra}{{{}}\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\lx@glossaries@gls@link{notation}{powerset}{{{}}2^{\Omega}}

of subsets of
\Omega
satisfying:

1.

\Omega\in\mathcal{F}
;

2.

If
A\in\mathcal{F}
, then its complement
A^{\mathrm{c}}=\Omega\setminus A
also lies in
\mathcal{F}
;

3.

If
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, then
\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
.

These axioms imply closure under countable intersections by De Morgan’s laws.
The pair
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
is called a
measurable space
.

1.4
Examples of
\sigma
-Algebras.

A
\sigma
-algebra specifies which subsets of
\Omega
are regarded as

measurable events
, i.e., events to which probability can be assigned.
We now present several illustrative examples, ordered from simplest to most commonly used.

1.5
Trivial
\sigma
-Algebra.

For any sample space
\Omega
, the collection

\Sigma_{\mathrm{triv}}=\{\,\emptyset,\;\Omega\,\}

is a
\sigma
-algebra.
Indeed:

•

it contains
\Omega
;

•

the complement of either set is still in the collection;

•

any countable union of sets in the collection equals either

\emptyset
or
\Omega
.

It is the
smallest
possible
\sigma
-algebra on
\Omega
.

1.6
Power Set.

The power set
\lx@glossaries@gls@link{notation}{powerset}{{{}}2^{\Omega}}=2^{\Omega}
, consisting of

all
subsets of
\Omega
, is also a
\sigma
-algebra:

•

it contains
\Omega
by definition;

•

complements and countable unions of subsets of
\Omega

are still subsets of
\Omega
.

Thus
2^{\Omega}
is the
largest

\sigma
-algebra on
\Omega
.
This is typically used when
\Omega
is finite or countable.

1.7
Borel
\sigma
-Algebra on
\mathbb{R}
.

When
\Omega=\mathbb{R}
, one commonly works with the
Borel
\sigma
-algebra
,
denoted
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
.
It is defined as the
collection of all sets that can be constructed
from open intervals using countable unions, countable intersections,
and complements
.
For instance:

•

every open interval
(a,b)
belongs to
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
;

•

therefore every closed interval
[a,b]
, every half-line
(-\infty,a]
,
and every finite union of intervals belongs to
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
;

•

more complicated sets formed by repeatedly applying countable unions,
intersections, and complements also belong to
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
.

The Borel
\sigma
-algebra provides the standard notion of measurability
for real-valued
RVs

[
7
,
19
]
.

1.8
Probability Measure.

A
probability measure
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})

is a function

P:\mathcal{F}\longrightarrow[0,1]

satisfying:

1.

Non-negativity:

P(A)\geq 0
for all
A\in\mathcal{F}
;

2.

Normalization:

P(\Omega)=1
;

3.

Countable additivity:
For any countable collection of pairwise disjoint events

\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
,

P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}P(A_{i}).

The triplet
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
is called a
probability space
.

1.9
Basic Consequences.

From these axioms follow the fundamental properties of measures:

•

P(\emptyset)=0
;

•

Monotonicity:
if
A\subseteq B
, then
P(A)\leq P(B)
;

•

Finite additivity:
if
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
are disjoint,
then
P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)
;

•

Continuity from below:
if
A_{n}\uparrow A
, then
P(A_{n})\uparrow P(A)
;

•

Continuity from above:
if
A_{n}\downarrow A
, then
P(A_{n})\downarrow P(A)
.

These properties ensure consistency: probabilities are additive,
non-negative, and behave continuously under limits of increasing or decreasing event sequences
[
7
,
19
]
.

1.10
Random Variable.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
be a probability space,
and let
(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))
denote the measurable space of real numbers with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra.
A
random variable (RV)

is a function

X:\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

that is
measurable
with respect to
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
; that is,
for every Borel set
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})

[
7
,
19
]
,

X^{-1}(B)=\{\,\omega\in\Omega:X(\omega)\in B\,\}\in\mathcal{F}.

1.11
.

The measurability condition ensures that the image of measurable sets under
X

corresponds to measurable events in
\Omega
.
Hence, probabilities involving
X
—such as
P\!\left(X\leq a\right)
or
P\!\left(a<X\leq b\right)
—are well-defined.
Formally, the probability measure
P
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})

induces a new measure
P_{X}
on
(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))
, called the

distribution
or
law
of
X
, defined by

P_{X}(B)=P\!\left(X^{-1}(B)\right),\qquad B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}).

Thus, the random variable
X
transfers the probability structure from
\Omega

to the real line, allowing us to compute probabilities and expectations directly in
\mathbb{R}

[
7
]
.

1.12
Discrete and Continuous Random Variables.

A
RV

X
is said to be:

•

Discrete
if its range (the set of values it may take) is countable,
and its
probability mass function (PMF)
is defined by

p(x)=P\!\left(X=x\right),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},

satisfying
\sum_{x}p(x)=1
.

•

Continuous
if there exists a nonnegative function
p:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
such that
for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,

P\!\left(a<X\leq b\right)=\int_{a}^{b}p(t)\,dt,\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)\,dx=1.

In this case
p
is called the
probability density function (PDF)
of
X
.

1.13
.

Both discrete and continuous
RVs
fall under the same measure-theoretic framework:
the distribution
P_{X}
is a probability measure on
(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))
.
A discrete distribution assigns positive probability only to a countable set of points,
while a continuous one is characterized by the existence of a density function
p

such that probabilities of intervals can be obtained by integration.
In practice, many real-world distributions exhibit both discrete and continuous components
[
7
,
41
]
.

1.2
Expectation, Variance, and Covariance

In probability theory, the most fundamental numerical summaries of a random variable
are its
expectation
and
variance
.
Expectation captures the average or central value of a distribution,
while variance quantifies how much the random variable fluctuates around that center.
Together they constitute the first two
moments
of a distribution.

1.14
Expectation and Variance.

Let
\bm{X}
be a
RV
with law
P_{X}
and, when applicable,
probability mass or density function
p(x)
.
The
expectation
\mathbb{E}

(or
mean
) of
\bm{X}
is

\E{X}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{x}x\,p(x),&\text{if $\bm{X}$ is discrete},\\[6.00006pt]
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\,p(x)\,dx,&\text{if $\bm{X}$ is continuous},\end{cases}

whenever the series or integral converges absolutely.
More generally, for any measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\E{f(X)}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\,dP_{X}(x).

The
variance
\mathrm{Var}

of
\bm{X}
is

\Var{X}=\E{(X-\E{X})^{2}},

and its square root
\sigma_{X}=\sqrt{\Var{X}}
is the
standard deviation
.

1.15
.

Expectation and variance summarize two complementary aspects of a random variable:
location and dispersion.
While
\E{X}
describes the typical or central value,

\Var{X}
measures the spread of its probability mass around that value.
They are the first and second moments of the distribution, respectively
[
7
,
19
]
.

1.16
Covariance and Independence.

Given two
RVs

X
and
Y
defined on the same probability space,
their
covariance
\mathrm{Cov}

is

\Cov{X}{Y}=\E{(X-\E{X})(Y-\E{Y})}.

They are said to be
uncorrelated
if
\Cov{X}{Y}=0
.
Moreover,
X
and
Y
are
independent
if, for all measurable sets

A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
,

P\!\left(X\in A,\,Y\in B\right)=P\!\left(X\in A\right)P\!\left(Y\in B\right).

Independence implies uncorrelatedness (provided the variances are finite),
but the converse is not generally true
[
7
,
19
]
.

1.17
.

Covariance measures the extent to which two random variables vary together:
positive values indicate that they tend to increase or decrease jointly,
negative values indicate opposing tendencies,
and
\Cov{X}{Y}=0
means the variables are linearly uncorrelated.
Independence, however, is a much stronger property:
it implies that the knowledge of one variable gives no information about the other.

1.18
Joint and Marginal Distributions.

When multiple
RVs
are defined on the same probability space,
their combined behavior is described by a
joint distribution
.
For instance, a pair
(X,Y)
may have joint probability mass or density function
p(x,y)
,
such that for measurable sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
,

P\!\left(X\in A,Y\in B\right)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{x\in A}\sum_{y\in B}p(x,y),&\text{if discrete},\\[6.00006pt]
\displaystyle\int_{A}\!\!\int_{B}p(x,y)\,dx\,dy,&\text{if continuous}.\end{cases}

The
marginal distributions
of
X
and
Y
are obtained by summing or integrating out the other variable:

p_{X}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x,y)\,dy,\qquad p_{Y}(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x,y)\,dx.

These operations correspond to the projection of a multivariate measure
onto lower-dimensional spaces
[
7
,
41
]
.

1.19
Notation.

When a random variable
X
is fixed or clear from context,
its distribution
P_{X}
is denoted simply by
P
.
Hence
\E{f(X)}=\int f(x)\,dP(x)
,
and we often write
P\!\left(A\right)
for
P_{X}(A)
.

The concepts introduced in this section establish the probabilistic foundation
for statistical analysis.
Having formalized probability spaces, random variables, and their key numerical
characteristics such as expectation and covariance, we now turn to the

empirical
perspective: how these theoretical quantities can be estimated
from observed data.

1.3
Estimation from Data

In most practical settings, the true probability law governing a random variable
is unknown and must be inferred from data.

Statistical estimation
provides methods to approximate expectations,
variances, and model parameters from a finite sample
drawn from an underlying—often unknown—distribution
[
41
,
9
]
.

1.3.1
Empirical Estimation of Moments

Let
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
be an independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) sample
from a random variable
\bm{X}
with law
P_{X}
.
A natural first step is the empirical estimation of expectation and variance:

\widehat{\E{X}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i},\qquad\widehat{\Var{X+}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_{i}-\widehat{\E{X}})^{2}.

These quantities, known respectively as the
sample mean
and

sample variance
, approximate their theoretical counterparts
\E{X}
and
\Var{X}

[
41
,
9
]
.

1.20
Law of Large Numbers.

Let
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
be
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.)

RVs
with common mean
\E{X_{i}}=\mu
.
Define the sample mean

\widehat{\E{X}}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}.

•

Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN).
If
\E[|X_{i}|]<\infty
, then

\widehat{\E{X}}_{N}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\mu,

meaning that for every
\varepsilon>0
,

P\!\left(|\widehat{\E{X}}_{N}-\mu|>\varepsilon\right)\;\longrightarrow\;0\quad\text{as }N\to\infty.

In words, the probability that the sample mean deviates from the true mean by more than

\varepsilon
becomes negligible as the sample size grows.
A sufficient condition ensuring this convergence is
\Var{X_{i}}<\infty
,
via Chebyshev’s inequality.

•

Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN).
If
\E{|X_{i}|}<\infty
(in particular, if
\E{X_{i}^{2}}<\infty
),
then

\widehat{\E{X}}_{N}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\mu,

that is,

P\!\left(\lim_{N\to\infty}\widehat{\E{X}}_{N}=\mu\right)=1.

This stronger result states that for almost every realization (sample path)
of the sequence
(X_{i})
, the sample mean eventually converges to the true mean.

Thus, under mild moment assumptions, the sample mean
\widehat{\E{X}}_{N}

is a consistent estimator of the population mean
\E{X_{i}}
:
the Weak Law ensures convergence in probability,
while the Strong Law ensures convergence for almost all sample outcomes
[
7
,
19
]
.

1.21
Central Limit Theorem.

Let
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
be independent and
i.i.d.

RVs
with finite mean
\E{X_{i}}=\mu
and finite, nonzero variance
\Var{X_{i}}=\sigma_{X}^{2}
.
Define the sample mean

\widehat{\E{X}}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}.

Then the
Central Limit Theorem (CLT)
states that

\sqrt{N}\,\frac{\widehat{\E{X}}_{N}-\mu}{\sigma_{X}}\;\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}\;\mathcal{N}(0,1),

where
\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}
denotes
convergence in distribution
.

Specifically, convergence in distribution means that for every bounded and continuous function

f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\E{f\big(\sqrt{N}\,(\widehat{\E{X}}_{N}-\mu)/\sigma_{X}\big)}\;\longrightarrow\;\E{f(Z)},

where
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
Equivalently, the
cumulative distribution function (CDF)
of the normalized sample mean
converges pointwise to that of the standard normal distribution:

P\!\left(\sqrt{N}\,\frac{\widehat{\E{X}}_{N}-\mu}{\sigma_{X}}\leq z\right)\;\longrightarrow\;\Phi(z),

where
\Phi
is the
CDF
of
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

Consequently, for large
N
, the estimation error of the sample mean is approximately Gaussian
with variance
\sigma_{X}^{2}/N
.
This asymptotic normality underlies classical confidence intervals and hypothesis tests
[
7
,
19
]
.

1.3.2
Parametric Estimation and the Likelihood Principle

The previous results show how empirical averages converge to their theoretical
counterparts, providing consistent estimators for quantities such as the mean and variance.
However, in many applications one is not only interested in these moments but in
the entire distribution of the data, which may depend on unknown parameters.
This leads to the framework of
parametric estimation

[
9
,
41
]
.

Suppose that observations arise from a parametric family of distributions

\{p(x\mid\theta):\theta\in\Theta\}
,
where
\theta
denotes an unknown parameter (possibly multidimensional).
Given an
i.i.d.
sample
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, the
likelihood function
is

L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p(x_{i}\mid\theta),

which evaluates how plausible each parameter value is in light of the data.

It is often more convenient to work with the
log-likelihood
,

\ell(\theta)=\log L(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log p(x_{i}\mid\theta).

The log-likelihood enjoys several advantages:

•

Numerical stability.

Products of many probabilities can underflow; logarithms avoid this.

•

Additivity.

The log-likelihood is a sum rather than a product, simplifying analysis
and enabling gradient-based optimization.

•

Same maximizer.

The logarithm is strictly increasing, so

\arg\max_{\theta}L(\theta)=\arg\max_{\theta}\ell(\theta).

Thus no information is lost by working in log-space
[
9
]
.

Maximum likelihood estimation.

The principle of
maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)
selects

\widehat{\theta}_{\mathrm{MLE}}=\arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\,L(\theta)=\arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\,\ell(\theta),

the parameter value that makes the observed sample most probable
under the assumed model
[
15
,
9
]
.

1.22
Asymptotic Properties of MLE.

Under standard regularity conditions, the maximum likelihood estimator
enjoys the following large-sample properties:

•

Consistency:

\widehat{\theta}_{\mathrm{MLE}}\xrightarrow{P}\theta^{*}

as
N\to\infty
.

•

Asymptotic normality:

\sqrt{N}\,\big(\widehat{\theta}_{\mathrm{MLE}}-\theta^{*}\big)\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\,I(\theta^{*})^{-1}\right),

where
I(\theta)
denotes the
Fisher information
.

•

Asymptotic efficiency.

The estimator attains the
Cramér–Rao lower bound
:
if
\widehat{\theta}
is any unbiased estimator of
\theta
, then

\Var{\widehat{\theta}}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{I(\theta)}.

This bound characterizes the minimum achievable variance among unbiased estimators.

Together, these properties position MLE as a central tool in both classical
and modern statistical inference
[
24
,
9
]
.

1.3.3
Example: Gaussian Mean Estimation

Consider
\bm{X}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
with known variance
\sigma^{2}

and unknown mean
\mu
.
Given an observed sample
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, the log-likelihood is

\ell(\mu)=-\frac{N}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^{2})-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_{i}-\mu)^{2}.

Maximizing
\ell(\mu)
is equivalent to minimizing the sum of squared deviations,
yielding the closed-form estimator

\widehat{\mu}_{\mathrm{MLE}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}.

Thus, the MLE for the Gaussian mean coincides with the sample mean.
This equivalence illustrates the deep connection between likelihood maximization
and least-squares optimization, a principle that recurs throughout statistics
and machine learning
[
9
,
41
]
.

1.23
.

In more general models, parameters may encode location, scale, shape, or mixture weights.
Closed-form solutions are rare, and numerical optimization methods
(e.g., gradient ascent, Newton methods, expectation–maximization)
are routinely employed.
Alternative frameworks—such as Bayesian inference—augment the likelihood
with prior distributions, yielding posterior distributions over parameters.

In summary, this section connects empirical estimation—supported by
the Law of Large Numbers and Central Limit Theorem—with
model-based inference through the likelihood principle.
These tools form the bridge between probability theory and practical data analysis,
paving the way for more advanced concepts such as information measures,
geometric viewpoints, and kernel-based estimation
[
3
,
6
,
33
]
.

1.4
Entropy and Information Measures

Statistical moments such as the mean and variance describe specific aspects
of a distribution, but they do not fully capture its overall uncertainty.
Many problems in data analysis, coding, and machine learning require a way to
quantify how unpredictable a random variable is, or how much “information”
is gained when an outcome is observed.
Entropy and related information-theoretic quantities provide these measures,
linking probability, uncertainty, and information
[
36
,
11
]
.

1.4.1
Self-Information and Shannon Entropy

The
self-information
of observing outcome
x
from a discrete

RV

X
with probability mass
p(x)
is

I(x)=-\log_{b}p(x),

where
b
denotes the logarithm base (
b=2
for bits and
b=e
for nats).
Rare events carry high information (large “surprise”),
while common events carry little.

1.24
Shannon Entropy.

For a discrete random variable
X
, the
entropy
is the expected
self-information:

H(X)=\E[I(X)]=-\sum_{x}p(x)\,\log_{b}p(x).

For a continuous
X
with density
p(x)
, the analogous quantity is the

differential entropy

[
36
,
11
]

h(X)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)\,\log_{b}p(x)\,dx.

1.25
.

Entropy measures the average uncertainty of a random variable.
For example, a uniform distribution on
M
equally likely outcomes
achieves the maximum entropy
H(X)=\log_{b}M
,
while a degenerate distribution with all mass on a single point has entropy
0
.

1.4.2
Joint, Conditional, and Mutual Information

Entropy naturally extends to multiple random variables.
Let
X
and
Y
be discrete with joint pmf
p(x,y)
.

•

Joint entropy
:

H(X,Y)=-\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log_{b}p(x,y)

measures the total uncertainty of the pair
[
11
]
.

•

Conditional entropy
:

H(Y\mid X)=-\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log_{b}p(y\mid x)

measures the remaining uncertainty in
Y
once
X
is known.

•

Mutual information
:

I(X;Y)=H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y)

quantifies the reduction in uncertainty in
X
due to observing
Y

(and vice versa).

1.26
.

Mutual information is always nonnegative and equals zero if and only if

X
and
Y
are independent.
Thus it provides a general measure of statistical dependence,
capturing nonlinear relationships that variance-based measures (like covariance)
may miss
[
11
]
.

1.4.3
Relative Entropy and Divergences

To compare two probability distributions
P
and
Q
on the same domain,
information theory uses the
Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence
:

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)=\sum_{x}P(x)\,\log_{b}\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}.

The continuous analogue replaces the sum by an integral.
Although asymmetric,
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
satisfies

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)\geq 0
,
with equality if and only if
P=Q
almost everywhere
[
23
,
11
]
.

1.27
.

KL divergence measures how hard it is to represent data drawn from

P
using a coding scheme tailored to
Q
.
It appears in maximum likelihood estimation, Bayesian inference,
variational methods, and many optimization problems in machine learning.

1.4.4
Kolmogorov–Nagumo Means and Generalized Entropies

Expectation can be viewed as a special case of a more general framework of
averaging, introduced by Kolmogorov and Nagumo.
Given a strictly monotonic and continuous function
g
,
the
Kolmogorov–Nagumo (KN) mean
of a discrete random variable
A
with
pmf
p(a)
is

\E[g]{A}=g^{-1}\!\left(\sum_{a}p(a)\,g(a)\right).

The choice
g(a)=a
yields the usual arithmetic mean.
Other choices of
g
control how the averaging operator weights extreme,
rare, or dominant outcomes
[
22
,
31
,
21
]
.

These generalized means give rise to alternative entropy definitions.
Different transformations
g
lead to generalized notions
of uncertainty, two important examples being Rényi and Tsallis entropies.

1.4.5
Rényi and Tsallis Entropies

For
\alpha>0
,
\alpha\neq 1
, the
Rényi entropy
of order
\alpha
is

H_{\alpha}(X)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log_{b}\sum_{x}p(x)^{\alpha}.

This corresponds to choosing
g(a)=a^{1-\alpha}
in the KN-mean framework.
The parameter
\alpha
adjusts sensitivity:
\alpha<1
emphasizes rare events,
while
\alpha>1
emphasizes likely outcomes
[
31
,
21
]
.

1.28
Convergence to Shannon Entropy.

The Rényi entropy converges to Shannon entropy as
\alpha\to 1

[
31
]
:

\lim_{\alpha\to 1}H_{\alpha}(X)=-\sum_{x}p(x)\log_{b}p(x)=H(X).

Proof.

The proof follows by applying L’Hôpital’s rule to the defining expression.

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to 1}H_{\alpha}(X)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\to 1}\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log_{b}\!\left(\sum_{x}p(x)^{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\ln(b)}\lim_{\alpha\to 1}\frac{\sum_{x}p(x)^{\alpha}\ln p(x)}{\sum_{x}p(x)^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{x}p(x)\log_{b}p(x).

∎

A closely related quantity is the
Tsallis entropy
,

S_{q}(X)=\frac{1-\sum_{x}p(x)^{q}}{q-1},

which also reduces to Shannon entropy as
q\to 1
.
Where Rényi entropy keeps a logarithmic averaging structure,
Tsallis entropy uses a linearized form, often applied in
statistical physics and robust statistical modeling
[
40
,
21
]
.

Entropy and its generalizations provide a unified language for expressing
uncertainty, diversity, and information.
They appear in areas ranging from coding theory and thermodynamics
to modern machine learning, where they motivate loss functions,
regularization strategies, and information-based criteria.
In later chapters, these concepts will connect to geometric and functional
perspectives, offering deeper insights into how similarity and uncertainty
can be quantified in high-dimensional or continuous spaces.

Chapter 2
Hilbert Spaces and the Geometry of Random Variables

Random variables can be studied not only through algebra and calculus,
but also through geometry.
By viewing random variables as elements of an inner-product space,
statistical concepts such as expectation, variance, and regression
acquire geometric interpretations.
This chapter introduces Hilbert spaces as the mathematical setting in which
these ideas naturally arise
[
27
,
4
,
33
]
.

2.1
Hilbert Spaces: Definition and Geometry

Hilbert spaces generalize Euclidean geometry to possibly infinite dimensions.
They provide a unifying framework for geometry, functional analysis, and
statistics, in which notions such as distance, angle, orthogonality, and
projection are defined through an inner product
[
13
,
30
,
10
]
.

2.1
Metric Space.

A
metric space
is a pair
(M,d)
consisting of a set
M
and a function

d:M\times M\to[0,\infty)
, called a
metric
, satisfying for all

x,y,z\in M
:

1.

Positivity:

d(x,y)\geq 0
and
d(x,y)=0
if and only if
x=y
;

2.

Symmetry:

d(x,y)=d(y,x)
;

3.

Triangle inequality:

d(x,z)\leq d(x,y)+d(y,z)
.

The metric
d(x,y)
specifies the distance between
x
and
y
.

2.2
Normed Space.

Let
V
be a vector space over
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
.
A
norm
is a function
\|\cdot\|:V\to[0,\infty)
satisfying, for all

x,y\in V
and all scalars
\alpha\in\mathbb{K}
:

1.

Positivity:

\|x\|\geq 0
and
\|x\|=0
if and only if
x=0
;

2.

Homogeneity:

\|\alpha x\|=|\alpha|\,\|x\|
;

3.

Triangle inequality:

\|x+y\|\leq\|x\|+\|y\|
.

A pair
(V,\|\cdot\|)
is called a
normed space
.
Every norm induces a metric via
d(x,y)=\|x-y\|
.

2.3
Inner Product Space.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a vector space over
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
.
An
inner product
on
\mathcal{H}
is a mapping

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{K}

satisfying, for all
f,g,h\in\mathcal{H}
and scalars
a,b\in\mathbb{K}
:

1.

Linearity in the first argument:

\langle af+bg,h\rangle=a\langle f,h\rangle+b\langle g,h\rangle
;

2.

Conjugate symmetry:

\langle f,g\rangle=\overline{\langle g,f\rangle}
;

3.

Positive definiteness:

\langle f,f\rangle\geq 0
, with equality if and only if
f=0
.

An inner product induces a norm

\|f\|=\sqrt{\langle f,f\rangle},

and therefore a metric
d(f,g)=\|f-g\|
.
Thus every inner-product space is a normed space and, consequently, a metric space.

2.4
Cauchy Sequence.

A sequence
(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
in a normed space
(V,\|\cdot\|)
is called a

Cauchy sequence
if, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists

N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\|f_{n}-f_{m}\|<\varepsilon\qquad\text{for all }n,m\geq N.

In other words, the elements of the sequence eventually become arbitrarily
close to each other with respect to the norm.

2.5
Hilbert Space.

A
Hilbert space
is a complete inner-product space

(\mathcal{H},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
,
meaning that every Cauchy sequence in
\mathcal{H}
converges to a limit
that also belongs to
\mathcal{H}

[
13
,
30
,
10
]
.

Completeness ensures that limit operations familiar from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
extend to
infinite-dimensional settings.
Since an inner product induces both a norm and a metric,
Hilbert spaces sit at the top of the following hierarchy:

\text{Hilbert space}\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{inner-product space}\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{normed space}\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{metric space}.

2.6
Classical Hilbert Spaces.

We illustrate two fundamental Hilbert spaces that arise throughout analysis and
probability.

•

The space
\ell^{2}
of square-summable sequences.

This space consists of all infinite sequences of real or complex numbers

x=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)\qquad\text{such that}\qquad\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|x_{n}|^{2}<\infty.

The inner product

\langle x,y\rangle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x_{n}\,\overline{y_{n}}

generalizes the Euclidean dot product to infinitely many coordinates.
The condition
\sum|x_{n}|^{2}<\infty
guarantees that the series defining the
inner product converges.

Intuitively,
\ell^{2}
contains sequences whose “energy” or “length”
remains finite in the limiting sense.
Completeness here means that any Cauchy sequence of such vectors converges
to another square-summable sequence.

•

The space
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)
of square-integrable functions.

This space consists of all measurable functions

f:\Omega\to\mathbb{K}\qquad\text{such that}\qquad\int_{\Omega}|f|^{2}\,d\mu<\infty.

Two functions are considered the same element of
L^{2}
if they differ only
on a set of measure zero.
The inner product is

\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{\Omega}f(\omega)\,\overline{g(\omega)}\,d\mu(\omega).

This space generalizes the idea of the dot product to functions:
instead of summing coordinate products, we integrate pointwise products.
The requirement
\int|f|^{2}<\infty
ensures finite “energy” or
“signal strength.”

A key fact is that
L^{2}
is complete: limits of mean-square convergent
sequences of functions always remain in the space.
This makes
L^{2}
the natural home for random variables with
finite variance
[
13
,
30
,
27
]
.

2.7
Geometric Interpretation.

Inner-product spaces extend Euclidean geometry:

•

Orthogonality:

f\perp g
if
\langle f,g\rangle=0
, meaning the vectors
“point in independent directions.”

•

Pythagorean identity:

if
f\perp g
, then

\|f+g\|^{2}=\|f\|^{2}+\|g\|^{2}.

This generalizes the familiar right-triangle relation in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

•

Projection theorem:

for any closed subspace
\mathcal{M}
and any
f\in\mathcal{H}
,
there exists a unique
f_{\mathcal{M}}\in\mathcal{M}
minimizing

\|f-g\|
over
g\in\mathcal{M}
.
Moreover, the error
f-f_{\mathcal{M}}
is orthogonal to
\mathcal{M}
.
This is the geometric principle underlying least-squares approximation
[
10
,
13
]
.

These ideas are not merely formal: in the next section we apply them to

L^{2}(\Omega)
, where random variables behave exactly like vectors and
statistical quantities acquire clear geometric meaning.

2.2
The Hilbert Space
L^{2}
and Probabilistic Geometry

2.2.1
Definition of Measure Spaces

2.8
Measure Space.

Let
X
be a set and let
\mathcal{A}
be a
\sigma
-algebra of subsets of
X
.

A
measure
on
(X,\mathcal{A})
is a function

\mu:\mathcal{A}\to[0,\infty]

that assigns a nonnegative “size” or “weight” to each measurable set and
satisfies the property of
countable additivity
:
whenever
(A_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
are pairwise disjoint sets in
\mathcal{A}
,

\mu\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu(A_{i}).

The value
\mu(\emptyset)=0
follows automatically from this axiom.

A triplet
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
consisting of a set, a
\sigma
-algebra, and a
measure is called a
measure space

[
7
,
4
]
.

To describe random variables using geometric ideas, we need a space in which
functions can be compared, measured, and combined much like vectors in

\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

L^{p}
spaces provide this framework.
We begin with the general idea, and then specialize to probability
[
30
,
10
,
8
]
.

2.2.2
Definition of
L^{p}
Spaces

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a measure space and let
1\leq p<\infty
.
A measurable function
f:X\to\mathbb{K}
(with
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
) is said
to be
p
-integrable
if

\int_{X}|f(x)|^{p}\,d\mu(x)<\infty.

Two measurable functions that differ only on a set of measure zero are identified,
since they are indistinguishable from the point of view of integration.

2.9
L^{p}
Space.

For
1\leq p<\infty
, the space
L^{p}(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
is defined as

L^{p}(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)=\big\{f:X\to\mathbb{K}\text{ measurable }\big|\,\int_{X}|f|^{p}\,d\mu<\infty\big\},

where functions equal
\mu
-almost everywhere are regarded as the same element.

2.10
L^{p}
is a Normed Space.

For
1\leq p<\infty
, the quantity

\|f\|_{p}:=\left(\int_{X}|f(x)|^{p}\,d\mu(x)\right)^{1/p}

defines a norm on
L^{p}(X,\mu)
.
Thus
(L^{p}(X,\mu),\|\cdot\|_{p})
is a normed space.

Idea of the proof.

The expression
\|f\|_{p}
behaves like a length:

•

\|f\|_{p}\geq 0
, and
\|f\|_{p}=0
only if
f
is zero almost everywhere;

•

scaling by a constant scales the norm:
\|\alpha f\|_{p}=|\alpha|\|f\|_{p}
;

•

the triangle inequality
\|f+g\|_{p}\leq\|f\|_{p}+\|g\|_{p}
follows from
classical inequalities due to Hölder and Minkowski.

Thus
L^{p}
functions behave very much like vectors with “length”
\|f\|_{p}

[
30
,
10
]
.
∎

A deep theorem in analysis states that every
L^{p}
space (
1\leq p<\infty
) is

complete
with respect to this norm, meaning that Cauchy sequences of
functions have limits in the same space.
Such spaces are called
Banach spaces

[
30
,
10
]
.

2.11
L^{2}
as a Hilbert Space.

On
L^{2}(X,\mu)
, the quantity

\langle f,g\rangle:=\int_{X}f(x)\,\overline{g(x)}\,d\mu(x)

defines an inner product whose induced norm satisfies
\|f\|_{2}=\sqrt{\langle f,f\rangle}
.
Since
L^{2}(X,\mu)
is also complete under this norm, it is a Hilbert space
[
30
,
10
,
27
]
.

2.12
Why Only
L^{2}
is a Hilbert Space.

A norm comes from an inner product if and only if it satisfies the

parallelogram law
:

\|u+v\|^{2}+\|u-v\|^{2}=2\|u\|^{2}+2\|v\|^{2}.

The
L^{2}
norm satisfies this identity, allowing one to reconstruct the inner
product from
\|\cdot\|_{2}
.
For
p\neq 2
, the norm
\|\cdot\|_{p}
does
not
satisfy this law (even in
simple spaces like
\mathbb{R}^{2}
), so it cannot arise from an inner product.
Thus
L^{p}
is a Hilbert space if and only if
p=2

[
30
,
10
,
13
]
.

Specialization to Random Variables

In probability theory, the underlying measure space is a probability space

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
.
In this case,

L^{p}(\Omega):=L^{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)=\{X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\,\mid\,\E{|X|^{p}}<\infty\},

so
L^{p}(\Omega)
consists precisely of
p
-integrable random variables.
For
p=2
, the space
L^{2}(\Omega)
is a Hilbert space under the inner product

\langle X,Y\rangle=\E{XY}.

This makes it an ideal setting for a geometric interpretation of expectation,
variance, covariance, and regression
[
27
,
4
]
.

2.2.3

In

\Cov{X}{Y}=\langle X-\E{X},\,Y-\E{Y}\rangle

Kernel Methods and Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces

Kernel methods generalize linear algebra and Euclidean geometry to nonlinear feature spaces.
They allow statistical and learning algorithms to operate in high- or even infinite-dimensional
spaces without explicitly computing feature coordinates

,
12
,
20
]

.
This chapter formalizes the concepts of positive definite kernels,

Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS)
, and Hilbert–Schmidt operators,
and connects them to probabilistic notions such as covariance and entropy
[

,

,
26
]

.

3.1
Positive Definite Kernels

Definition and Properties

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a non-empty set.
A
positive definite kernel
is a symmetric function

k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{i}c_{j}\,k(x_{i},x_{j})\geq 0\quad\text{for all }N\in\mathbb{N},\;x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in\mathcal{X},\;c_{1},\dots,c_{N}\in\mathbb{R}.

For any finite collection
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, the matrix

K=[k(x_{i},x_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{N}
is symmetric and positive semidefinite,
and is called a
Gram matrix

[
3
,
5
]

.
This property ensures that
k
behaves like an inner product in some (possibly
high-dimensional) feature space.

3.1
Classical Kernels.

Common examples include:

•

Linear:

k(x,x^{\prime})=\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle
,

•

Polynomial:

k(x,x^{\prime})=(\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle+c)^{p}
, with
c\geq 0
,
p\in\mathbb{N}
,

•

Gaussian (RBF):

k(x,x^{\prime})=\exp(-\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}/2\sigma^{2})
,

•

Laplacian:

k(x,x^{\prime})=\exp(-\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{1}/\sigma)
.

These kernels induce different geometries in feature space and correspond to
different notions of similarity on
\mathcal{X}

[
33
,
12
]

.

Feature Space Interpretation

Every positive definite kernel defines an implicit mapping

\Phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{H}

into a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
such that

k(x,x^{\prime})=\langle\Phi(x),\Phi(x^{\prime})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

The vectors
\Phi(x)
are called
feature representations
of the inputs.
Within
\mathcal{H}
, the kernel
k
plays the role of an inner product,
inducing distances, angles, and projections among feature vectors
[
3
,
5
]
.

3.2
.

The
kernel trick
exploits this representation:
whenever an algorithm can be expressed solely in terms of inner products

\langle\Phi(x),\Phi(x^{\prime})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
, one can replace these by

k(x,x^{\prime})
and work directly with the kernel.
This allows nonlinear regression, classification, or principal component
analysis to be carried out without ever constructing the feature map

\Phi
explicitly
[
33
,
20
]

.

Figure 3.1:

Illustration of the feature mapping
\Phi
induced by a Gaussian kernel.
Each input
x\in\mathcal{X}
is mapped to a feature vector
\Phi(x)
in
the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, and the kernel values satisfy

k(x,x^{\prime})=\langle\Phi(x),\Phi(x^{\prime})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
.

3.2
Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS)

Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces provide a rigorous framework for the feature
spaces induced by positive definite kernels.

3.3
Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a non-empty set and
\mathcal{H}
a Hilbert space of functions

f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
.
We say
\mathcal{H}
is a
Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS)

if there exists a function
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
[
3
,
5
]

:

1.

For every
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the function
k(x,\cdot)
belongs to
\mathcal{H}
;

2.

Reproducing property:

for all
f\in\mathcal{H}
and all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\langle f,k(x,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=f(x).

The function
k
is called the
reproducing kernel
of
\mathcal{H}
.
The reproducing property shows that pointwise evaluation is a continuous linear
functional on
\mathcal{H}
, a fact guaranteed by the Riesz Representation
Theorem
[
5
]

.

3.4
Riesz Representation Theorem.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space over
\mathbb{R}
,
and let
\varphi:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded (i.e., continuous) linear functional.
Then there exists a unique element
h_{\varphi}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
[
10
,
30
,
13
]

\varphi(f)=\langle f,h_{\varphi}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\quad\text{for all }f\in\mathcal{H}.

3.5
.

Riesz representation states that every continuous linear functional on a Hilbert
space can be expressed as an inner product with a unique element of that space.
Applied to the evaluation functional
\delta_{x}(f)=f(x)
, it guarantees the
existence of a unique element
k(x,\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}
such that

f(x)=\langle f,k(x,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},

which is precisely the reproducing property.

3.6
Moore–Aronszajn.

For every positive definite kernel
k
on
\mathcal{X}
,
there exists a unique Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{k}
of functions
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}

such that
k
is its reproducing kernel and
[
3
]

\langle k(x,\cdot),k(x^{\prime},\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=k(x,x^{\prime})\quad\text{for all }x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}.

Conversely, every RKHS has a unique reproducing kernel of this form.

3.7
Gaussian RKHS.

For the Gaussian kernel

k(x,x^{\prime})=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right),

the associated RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{k}
consists of functions that can be expressed
as limits (in the RKHS norm) of finite linear combinations of Gaussian bumps:

f(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\,k(x_{i},\cdot).

For two such functions

f(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\,k(x_{i},\cdot)

and

g(\cdot)=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\beta_{j}\,k(x^{\prime}_{j},\cdot),

the inner product is

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}\,k(x_{i},x^{\prime}_{j}).

[
5
,
33
]

3.8
.

An RKHS extends linear geometry to spaces of functions:
inner products between functions become kernel evaluations between data points.
This viewpoint allows Hilbert-space tools—such as projections, angles, and
eigen-decompositions—to be applied directly in statistical learning problems.

3.3
Hilbert–Schmidt and Covariance Operators

Definition and Basic Properties

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
be Hilbert spaces.
A bounded linear operator
T:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}

is called a
Hilbert–Schmidt operator
if there exists
an orthonormal basis
\{e_{i}\}
of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
such that

\|T\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\|Te_{i}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}^{2}<\infty.

The quantity
\|T\|_{\mathrm{HS}}
is called the
Hilbert–Schmidt norm

[
30
,
13
]

.

3.9
Integral Operators.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\mu)
be a measure space and
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}

a square-integrable kernel.
The operator
T_{k}:L^{2}(\mathcal{X})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
defined by

(T_{k}f)(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(x,x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime})\,d\mu(x^{\prime})

is Hilbert–Schmidt, with

\|T_{k}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}=\int_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}|k(x,x^{\prime})|^{2}\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(x^{\prime}).

[
30
,
13
]

3.10
.

Hilbert–Schmidt operators generalize finite-dimensional matrices
to infinite-dimensional settings: they are compact operators
whose entries have finite total squared magnitude, analogous to matrices with
finite Frobenius norm.

Covariance Operators in RKHS

Let
\mathcal{H}_{k}
be an RKHS associated with a kernel
k
, and
let
X
be a random variable taking values in
\mathcal{X}
.
The
mean embedding
of the distribution
P_{X}
into
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is

\mu_{X}=\E[X]{k(X,\cdot)}\in\mathcal{H}_{k}.

[
38
,
39
,
26
]

The

Entropy and divergence measures introduced earlier can be estimated

Kernel Density Estimation (KDE)

Given samples
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}\subset\mathcal{X}
,
the
Parzen window
or
kernel density estimator
is

\widehat{p}(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}k_{\sigma}(x,x_{n}),

where
k_{\sigma}
is a normalized positive kernel
(e.g., a Gaussian with bandwidth
\sigma
).
This nonparametric estimate provides a flexible way to approximate the
underlying density from finite data and forms the starting point for
kernel-based entropy estimators
[
32
,
28
,
37
]

.

Estimating Rényi Entropy with Kernels

For Rényi entropy of order
\alpha=2
,
the empirical estimator based on KDE can be written as

\widehat{H}_{2}(X)=-\log\sum_{n=1}^{N}\widehat{p}(x_{n})^{2}=-\log\!\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{N}k_{\sqrt{2}\sigma}(x_{n},x_{m})\right).

This expression admits a natural geometric interpretation in
\mathcal{H}_{k}

[
29
]

.

3.12
.

Let

\mu_{\widehat{P}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}k_{\sqrt{2}\sigma}(x_{n},\cdot)

be the empirical mean embedding of the sample in the
RKHS
.
Then

\widehat{H}_{2}(X)=-\log\|\mu_{\widehat{P}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}.

Thus, the Rényi entropy of order 2 is (up to a logarithm) the squared norm of
the embedded empirical distribution:
entropy measures how spread out the sample is in feature space.
Highly concentrated embeddings correspond to low entropy; diffuse embeddings
correspond to high entropy

Mean Embeddings and Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)

The
RKHS
mean embedding
\mu_{X}=\E{k(X,\cdot)}
represents a distribution as
an element of
\mathcal{H}_{k}
.
Given two random variables
X
and
Y
, their discrepancy can be quantified by the

Maximum Mean Discrepancy
(MMD):

\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(X,Y)=\|\mu_{X}-\mu_{Y}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}=\E[k(X,X^{\prime})]+\E[k(Y,Y^{\prime})]-2\E[k(X,Y)],

where expectations are taken over independent copies

X,X^{\prime}\sim P_{X}
and
Y,Y^{\prime}\sim P_{Y}
.

,

The MMD provides a kernel-based notion of distance between distributions,
analogous in spirit to Wasserstein or Kullback–Leibler divergences,
and is widely used in two-sample testing and generative modeling.
It ties together information measures and Hilbert-space geometry,
completing the bridge from probability and statistics to kernel methods
