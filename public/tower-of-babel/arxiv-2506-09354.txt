“Is This Really a Human Peer Supporter?”: Misalignments Between Peer Supporters and Experts in LLM-Supported Interactions CCS: Human-centered computing Empirical studies in HCI CCS: Human-centered computing Empirical studies in collaborative and social computing
Kellie Yu Hui Sim, Roy Ka-Wei Lee, Kenny Tsu Wei Choo

Abstract
. Mental health is a growing global concern, prompting interest in AI-driven solutions to expand access to psychosocial support. Peer support , grounded in lived experience, offers a valuable complement to professional care. However, variability in training, effectiveness, and definitions raises concerns about quality, consistency, and safety. Large Language Models (LLMs) present new opportunities to enhance peer support interactions, particularly in real-time, text-based interactions. We present and evaluate an AI-supported system with an LLM-simulated distressed client ( SimClient ), context-sensitive LLM-generated suggestions ( Suggestions ), and real-time emotion visualisations. 2 mixed-methods studies with 12 peer supporters and 5 mental health professionals (i.e., experts) examined the system’s effectiveness and implications for practice. Both groups recognised its potential to enhance training and improve interaction quality. However, we found a key tension emerged: while peer supporters engaged meaningfully, experts consistently flagged critical issues in peer supporter responses, such as missed distress cues and premature advice-giving. This misalignment highlights potential limitations in current peer support training, especially in emotionally charged contexts where safety and fidelity to best practices are essential. Our findings underscore the need for standardised, psychologically grounded training, especially as peer support scales globally. They also demonstrate how LLM-supported systems can scaffold this development–if designed with care and guided by expert oversight. This work contributes to emerging conversations on responsible AI integration in mental health and the evolving role of LLMs in augmenting peer-delivered care.

Yet, little is known about how trained peer supporters engage with such tools in real-time, emotionally nuanced conversations.
It is also unclear how these interactions align with the expectations of mental health professionals, who may prioritise emotional pacing, relational tone, and safety.

To address growing mental health needs, a range of digital initiatives have emerged in recent years.
These platforms include digital self-help tools, moderated online forums, text-based crisis services, and anonymous peer support communities, often blending volunteer and professional staffing models.
Survey data suggest that individuals are more likely to seek informal or peer-based support than clinical services when facing mental health challenges

The growth of peer support has paralleled the expansion of volunteer-operated crisis services, which serve as lifelines for individuals in acute distress.

As peer support becomes increasingly digitised, there is a growing need for scaffolding tools that can guide conversations, enhance empathy, and support peer supporters in navigating emotionally complex exchanges.

As recent work highlights, while LLM-generated responses may outperform human peer counsellors in adherence to structured therapy models such as Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT), they continue to fall short in areas such as empathy, collaboration, and cultural attunement

These findings underscore the limitations of LLMs as standalone tools.
They also highlight the importance of studying how they may be meaningfully integrated as collaborative aids in human-led support environments, especially in emotionally sensitive or peer-based contexts.

Building on the challenges and opportunities identified in peer support practice and the potential of LLM-based interventions, we investigated how LLMs might be used to support peer support conversations.
Specifically, we explore the following research questions:

•

RQ1
: How do peer supporters engage with an LLM-simulated client, LLM-generated suggestions, and emotional state visualisations during multi-turn, real-time peer support interactions, and what shapes their perceptions of these components’ usefulness?

•

RQ2
: How do mental health professionals evaluate peer supporter responses and the LLM-supported components (see RQ1), and what implications do these evaluations have for future system development?

To explore these questions, we conducted two studies.
In the first, peer supporters engaged in real-time conversations with an LLM-simulated distressed client (
SimClient
), receiving context-sensitive LLM-generated suggestions (
Suggestions
) and viewing dynamic emotional state visualisations.
In the second, mental health professionals with relevant expertise, including a clinical psychologist, several counsellors, and a medical social worker, conducted expert reviews of screen recordings from these interactions.
Their evaluations focused on assessing the quality of the peer supporters’ responses, including alignment with established support principles, as well as the various system components used during the sessions.

Our contributions are as follows:

(1)

We provide empirically grounded insights into how trained peer supporters interact with LLM-supported tools during real-time conversations–including their strategies, concerns, and tensions around authenticity, emotional labour, and trust.

(2)

Our triangulated system evaluation reveals both affordances and limitations, as well as divergences between peer supporter practices and expert expectations.

(3)

We outline design considerations for the responsible integration of LLMs into volunteer-based peer support, derived from peer supporter-and expert-informed reflections, with implications for tool customisation, training support, and culturally sensitive deployment.

2.1.
Defining Peer Support

Peer support, defined as the mutual provision of emotional or practical assistance between individuals with shared lived experiences, has long been recognised as a valuable component of mental health care
(
Mead and MacNeil 2004
;
Repper and Carter 2011
)
.
Unlike professional services such as psychotherapy or formal crisis intervention, peer support emphasises empathy, relatability, and shared understanding.
Although both may draw on common communication techniques (e.g., active listening, validation), they differ in scope, training requirements, and expectations of emotional labour
(
Solomon 2004
)
.
Peer support is often voluntary and community-driven, intended to complement rather than replace clinical care.

While commonly associated with individuals offering mutual aid based on shared lived experiences, the term itself, alongside related concepts such as peer helping, peer counselling, and peer education, is used inconsistently across academic, clinical, and community settings.
In some contexts, peer support is informal and community-driven, while in others, it is institutionalised and professionalised, with structured training and certification pathways.

For example, in the United States,
Certified Peer Support Specialists
are formally recognised within mental health systems
(
Shalaby and Agyapong 2020
;
Lee et al. 2019
)
.
Similarly professionalisation is seen in Australia, where national initiative have embedded Peer Workers into clinical and community mental health settings such as the
Peer Work Hub

1

https://www.nswmentalhealthcommission.com.au/content/peer-work-hub
, formalising training and employment pathways and emphasising recovery-oriented practice and co-production
(
Shalaby and Agyapong 2020
;
Franke et al. 2010
)
.
Elsewhere, including parts of Europe and Canada, peer support has likewise been integrated into public mental health services, guided by frameworks that emphasise recovery-oriented practice
(
Shalaby and Agyapong 2020
;
Repper and Carter 2011
)
.

Conversely, in parts of Asia and the Global South, peer support initiatives often remain informal or semi-formal, typically being integrated into school- and community-based programmes
(
Kaur and Singh 2025
;
Sehgal et al. 2025
;
Palaniappan et al. 2023
;
Ma et al. 2022
;

.
In Singapore, for instance, peer support has evolved from a role in clinical mental health organisations through programmes such as the

to volunteer-based support groups.
However, challenges remain in balancing formalisation within mental health systems with the core values of lived experience, mutuality, and non-hierarchical support
(
Lee et al. 2019
)
, particularly in collectivist societies where social hierarchies and community norms could shape help-seeking practices
(
Green et al. 2024

.
This definitional plurality underscores the importance of situating peer support interventions within their sociocultural and institutional contexts.
Such contextualisation requires attention to how cultural norms shape perceptions of mental health, help-seeking behaviours, and role relationships between peers and professionals.
For example, pervasive stigma surrounding mental health topics and limited mental health literacy could limit the willingness of individuals to seek or even provide peer support, potentially constraining the reach of such programmes
(
Chua and Lee 2024
;
Subramaniam et al. 2022
)
.
Moreover, the framing of peer support within a

Institutional factors, such as availability of funding, supervision structures, and recovery-oriented workplace cultures, could also affect how peer support is implemented and sustained
(
Chua and Himawan 2025

.
Designing for such contexts thus requires sensitivity to not only local operational constraints, but also to deeper cultural expectations, boundaries, and relational norms governing these interactions.
By examining how peer supporters interact with various LLM components and how experts view such interactions, our study contributes to an understanding of how new technological interventions may align, or even conflict with culturally embedded norms and their fit within specific sociocultural contexts.
We extend this cultural lens by embedding our analysis within a Southeast Asian context, addressing a notable gap in existing literature that remains largely Western-centric.
By attending to the sociocultural specificity of peer support, we contribute to ongoing debates around culturally adaptive and sensitive design for mental health solutions.

Complementing these scaffolding approaches, Li et al.
(

introduced a robust framework for evaluating the therapeutic alliance in online text-based counselling.
Their work leverages LLMs to assess key relational dimensions, including goal alignment, collaborative engagement, and affective bonding, grounded in established psychotherapy theories.
Notably, their study demonstrates how detailed guidelines and chain-of-thought prompting can enhance LLMs’ alignment with human expert evaluations, offering interpretable and relationally nuanced feedback.
While their focus centres on professional therapist-client relationships, the underlying principles of alliance-building and relational attunement are equally pertinent to peer support contexts.
Their findings underscore opportunities to adapt such frameworks to assess and refine AI-supported peer support, particularly in terms of relational depth and conversational authenticity.

While prior work has demonstrated the technical feasibility of AI guidance or feedback in online peer support, most studies have focused on either professional counselling contexts
(
Li et al. 2024
)
or controlled experimental tasks
(
Hsu et al. 2025
;
Sharma et al. 2021
)
, often with limited attention to the lived realities of volunteer peer supporters.
Few studies explore how AI tools are perceived and engaged with by trained peer supporters operating in real-time, emotionally dynamic conversations, or how such tools align with the relational norms, expectations, and constraints of peer support practice.
Moreover, there remains a gap in understanding how AI-generated suggestions interact with peer supporters’ existing conversational strategies, affective labour, and perceptions of authenticity and empathy in practice.
In our study, we provided peer supporters with LLM-generated suggestions across three distinct categories: motivational interviewing (
S_{MI}
), empathetic response (
S_{ER}
), and informational/emotional support (
S_{PS}
), affording them the freedom to engage with or disregard suggestions.
Crucially, we move beyond single-turn or researcher-scripted settings by capturing real-time, multi-turn interactions.
Rather than testing only whether LLM-generated suggestions are accepted, we empirically examine how they are interpreted and adapted, whether rephrased, blended into participants’ voices, or ignored altogether.
Experts also evaluated the LLM-generated suggestions in their original form, alongside how peer supporters used them during the conversation.
This enables a fine-grained analysis of how peer supporters exercise conversational judgement and relational sensitivity when presented with LLM-generated suggestions.

In doing so, we move beyond static or researcher-scripted evaluations
(
Sharma et al. 2023
;
Hsu et al. 2025
)
to study live, unfolding interactions.
Our evaluation, combining peer supporter reflections, expert assessments, enables a more wholistic understanding of human-AI collaboration in emotionally charged settings.
These findings also extend therapeutic alliance theories into peer support contexts and explore how AI interventions can foster (or disrupt) relational depth and emotional pacing in volunteer-driven peer support settings.

2.3.
LLM-Driven Conversational Agents and Simulated Clients in Mental Health

Conversational agents (CAs) have become increasingly popular across a range of digital mental health interventions
(
Li et al. 2023
)
, offering scalable and accessible support for users through chatbots or voice assistants.
Applications for these range from cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)
(
Fitzpatrick et al. 2017
)
and expressive writing
(
Park et al. 2021
)
to solution-focused counselling approaches
(
Fulmer et al. 2018
)
, complemented by developments of broader digital mental health technologies like virtual reality, mobile platforms, and self-guided tools
(
Hollis et al. 2017
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Emmelkamp et al. 2020
;
Lim Ding Feng et al. 2020
)
.
Real-world evaluations, such as that of the
Wysa
app, suggest CAs may improve depressive symptoms through empathetic, text-based interaction
(
Inkster et al. 2018
)
.

The emergence of LLMs has significantly enhanced the capabilities of CAs, enabling them to engage in more fluid, naturalistic, and context-aware dialogue, instead of rigid, script-based interactions
(
Kovačević et al. 2024
)
.
For example,
MindfulDiary
enables psychiatric patients to document their experiences through conversational journaling, creating a therapeutic self-reflection space powered by LLMs
(
Kim et al. 2024
)
, while
ComPeer
integrates peer support strategies and conversation history to generate proactive, LLM-driven peer interactions tailored to users’ emotional needs
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
.
Such systems emulate supportive communication principles that are traditionally taught in mental health training programmes, such as empathy, emotional validation, reflective listening, and motivational interviewing
(
Sharma et al. 2021
;
Chaszczewicz et al. 2024
)
.
This alignment enables CAs to produce responses that resemble those of trained supporters, potentially lowering the barrier to accessing psychologically grounded care in digital settings.
Concurrently, there has been a growing trend of individuals turning to general-purpose LLMs such as ChatGPT to self-administer emotional support, drawn by their immediacy, anonymity, and accessibility
(
Alavanza 2025
)
.
However, these uses fall outside clinical or peer support safeguards, raising expert concerns about their inability to intervene in crises and the risk of producing unsafe or misleading advice
(
Lou 2023
;
Martinengo et al. 2022
)
.

These trends highlight broader limitations in current LLM capabilities.
While LLMs excel at generating fluent and contextually appropriate text, their ability to handle ambiguity, emotional pacing, and high-stakes disclosures remains limited, sometimes giving rise to over-reliance or misplaced trust
(
Song et al. 2024
;
Tanana et al. 2021
)
.
Ensuring ethical and effective use hence demands thoughtful design and, where needed, human oversight.

Parallel to their use in supporting users, LLMs have also been used to simulate patients or clients for training and evaluation purposes.
These systems aim to replicate the conversational behaviours, emotional trajectories, and presentation styles of real clients, creating safe and scalable environments for therapists, clinical students, and researchers to practise and refine their skills
(
Steenstra et al. 2025
;
Yang et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2024b
;
Wang et al. 2024a
)
.
Such simulations typically maintain consistent personas across multi-turn conversations, enabling standardised practice with clients across diverse levels of psychological distress
(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
that follow structured protocols and include embedded cues for active listening, de-escalation, and therapeutic rapport-building.

In some cases, LLMs have also been used to simulate therapists or counsellors themselves
(
Kampman et al. 2025
;
Yang et al. 2025
;
Chiu et al. 2024
)
.
However, this line of work has focused primarily on formal or professional contexts.
Little is known about how LLM-simulated clients might be used in non-clinical peer support settings, where conversations are often less structured, highly emotional, and facilitated by volunteers with minimal training.
Peer support introduces unique design challenges, including the need to calibrate emotional pacing, conversational realism, and interpersonal tone in ways that match the expectations and capacities of non-professional supporters.
Simulating clients for such settings requires careful calibration of tone, pacing, and relational nuance.

Our work extends existing methods and foundations into the peer support domain, where the stakes, expectations, and relational dynamics differ substantially.
We capture real-time, multi-turn interactions across peer supporters and LLM-simulated distressed clients, whose computed emotional states are visualised for participant use.
During these same interactions, peer supporters received live LLM-generated suggestions intended to scaffold their responses in real time.
Alongside our analysis of peer supporter interactions, expert evaluations guide further analysis of the LLM-simulated client, emotional state visualisations, and the relational sensitivity shown by peer supporters.
This dual lens enables a more holistic understanding of how simulated clients may support training, reflection, and future system design within peer support settings.

Overview

3.1.
System Overview and Study Design

We developed and evaluated an LLM-supported system designed to enhance peer support interactions through three integrated components: an LLM-simulated distressed client (
SimClient
), context-sensitive LLM-generated suggestions (
Suggestions
), and real-time emotional state visualisations.
These were deployed in a text-based chat interface that enabled participants to engage in psychologically realistic peer support scenarios.

To investigate the system’s effectiveness and implications for peer support practice, we conducted two studies (see Figure
1
):

(1)

Study 1: Lab Study with Peer Supporters.

12 trained peer supporters engaged in synchronous, multi-turn conversations with the LLM-simulated client (
SimClient
) via the interface. The system provided real-time emotional state visualisations and three LLM-generated response suggestions (
Suggestions
). We examined how participants interacted with these components in a controlled lab setting.

(2)

Study 2: Expert Evaluation of Study 1’s Interactions.

5 mental health professionals reviewed anonymised recordings from Study 1 and evaluated the realism and appropriateness of
SimClient
, the quality of
Suggestions
, and the utility of emotional state visualisations. They also raised design and ethical considerations for future deployments.

Study 2 (N=5 expert professionals): demographics, interviews on professional support experiences, and review of Study 1 logs with evaluations of \client{}, AI suggestions, and visualisations.

Through these studies, we examined the use and perception of AI tools in real-time peer support contexts, identifying points of synergy and tension between human practice and machine-generated assistance.
This design allowed us to examine human-AI collaboration in which peer supporters played a central role, ensuring that AI-generated input supported rather than replaced their contributions.
This aligns with calls for multi-layered, context-sensitive evaluations of AI in mental health care, as emphasised by Sim and Choo
(
Sim and Choo 2025
)
, who advocate for integrating empirical testing with broader ethical and cultural analysis.
Across both studies, Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained, and all procedures adhered to ethical research guidelines.
Participants were compensated approximately USD$15 and USD$23 for Studies 1 and 2 respectively.

3.2.
Design of LLM-Simulated Client:
SimClient

SimClient
was powered by GPT-4 Assistants (gpt-4o-2024-08-06) and designed to emulate an emotionally expressive support-seeker that engaged in a multi-turn conversational exchange with participants.
Responses were generated dynamically throughout the conversation, guided by structured conversational phases (see Appendix
D
).

To simulate affective realism, each client message was paired with a computed emotional state based on two models
(
Sim et al. 2024
)
:

•

Dimensional
: valence (-1 to +1) and arousal (-1 to +1), visualised using sentiment labels and time-series graphs (Figure
2
, B
1
, B
2
)

•

Categorical
: one of seven discrete emotions (i.e., sadness, fear, anger, disgust, happiness, surprise, neutral), presented through a sentiment label and time-series graph (Figure
2
, B
1
, B
2
)

These were computed from the emotional intent embedded in each GPT-generated message, consistent with the prompt template (see Appendix
D
).
Visualisations were designed to support participants in interpreting shifts in emotional tone, especially in the absence of paralinguistic cues inherent to text-based environments.
The combination of dimensional and categorical modelling enabled
SimClient
to simulate dynamic emotional trajectories.
Emotional states were also linked to how
SimClient
responded, with high-arousal negative states yielding more urgent or fragmented message styles.
This coupling of emotion modelling and verbal output aimed to simulate realistic fluctuations in distress, uncertainty, and self-expression during support-seeking scenarios.

3.3.
Design of LLM-Generated Suggestions:
Suggestions

Suggestions
was powered by GPT-4 Assistants (gpt-4o-2024-08-06) and designed to assist in formulating responses.
Three LLM-generated suggestions were provided following each
SimClient
message.
These were designed to scaffold key peer support competencies across three categories:

(1)

Motivational Interviewing (
S_{MI}
)
: similar to Hsu et al.
(
Hsu et al. 2025
)
and Shah et al.
(
Shah et al. 2022
)

(2)

Empathetic Responses (
S_{ER}
)
: grounded in affective and rapport-building strategies
(
Sharma et al. 2021
;
Majumder et al. 2020
)

(3)

Providing Informational or Emotional Support (
S_{PS}
)
: providing normalisation, reassurance, or relevant support resources
(
Peng et al. 2020
;
Kruzan et al. 2021
)

For example, when
SimClient
stated, "I’ve been feeling really anxious about school lately and unsure about my future. It’s like everyone is moving forward and I’m stuck.",
Suggestions
generated:

•

S_{MI}
: "Can you tell me more about what specifically is making you anxious about school and the future?"

•

S_{ER}
: "It sounds like you’re feeling overwhelmed with the uncertainty and pressure of what’s to come. That’s completely understandable."

•

S_{PS}
: "You’re not alone in these feelings. Let’s work through them together."

Suggestions were rendered below the chat window and updated at each turn.
Participants could choose to adopt, modify, or disregard them.
Examples of prompt structure and system output are provided in Appendices
D
and
E
.

4.
Study 1: Lab Study with Peer Supporters

Participants
.
We recruited 12 participants (5 female), all aged 18 and above (
mean=34.04
,
SD=14.67
), each with prior experience and training in peer mental health support and/or PFA.
Participants were drawn from both community- and institutional-based peer support programmes/settings, with varying modalities: 5 only provided support online, 2 offline, and 5 in both.
Most held at least a Bachelor’s degree (
N=11
), including two with degrees in Psychology.
Peer support experience ranged from 1 to over 10 years (
mean=3.6
years across both modalities).

Method
.
Each session comprised three segments: Pre-Chat, Chat, and Post-Chat, all conducted in person and screen-and audio-recorded.

Pre-Chat Segment.

In the Pre-Chat segment, participants completed three questionnaires:

(1)

A demographics questionnaire,

(2)

Personality scales (BFI-2-XS
(
Soto and John 2017
;
McCrae and Costa 1997
;
McCrae and John 1992
)
), Need for Cognition-s
(
Cacioppo et al. 1984
;
Cacioppo and Petty 1982
)
, and

(3)

A questionnaire on familiarity with AI, focusing on generative AI tools like ChatGPT.

Chat Segment.

As described in Section
3
, participants engaged in a multi-turn text-based conversation with
SimClient
.
For each message from
SimClient
, they could respond freely or draw from three categories of LLM-generated suggestions (
S_{MI}
,
S_{ER}
,
S_{PS}
).
Physiological and attentional data were also collected to provide secondary indicators of participant stress and engagement.
Heart rate variability (HRV) was measured using a Polar H10 chest strap, and eye gaze data was recorded via WebGazer.js.

Post-Chat Segment.

Participants took part in retrospective think-aloud interviews while reviewing their session recording.
Facilitator-noted moments guided the discussion around decision-making, suggestions usage, and perceived challenges.
Participants also rated
SimClient
’s responses on response naturalness in terms of fluency and ease (yes/no), human-likeness (non-robotic qualities), and realism (resemblance to real-world clients) on 7-point Likert scales.
They were also asked about
Suggestions
’s alignment with their past training experiences, and the usefulness of the emotional state visualisations.
These measures assessed
SimClient
’s ability to simulate a believable peer support interaction and participants’ general impressions of its responses.

Emergent Strategies and Moments of Hesitation.

Participants employed a range of peer support and PFA principles during their interaction with
SimClient
, including affirmation, active listening, emotional regulation, and promoting client autonomy.

PS2
reflected that their approach was
“a very subconscious effort”
, aimed at helping
SimClient
feel
“like they are in it together”
, rather than pushing advice or suggestions.

PS1
preferred keeping responses simple to avoid personal entanglement, while
PS3
described their strategy as simply
“being there for [
SimClient
]”
.
Similarly,
PS6
described a comforting style:
“More like a parent kind of support…consoling and steering [
SimClient
] to [the] right path”
.
Others highlighted structured frameworks, with
PS5
emphasising a progression of
“probing, normalising, affirming, [and] help-seeking”

PS10
.

Moments of hesitation revealed
thoughtful restraint
.

PS5
described backing off when
SimClient
gave a vague answer:
“Asked if [
SimClient
was] in any clubs…[they] replied that they used to enjoy playing sports… I didn’t want to probe again”
.
Others delayed their responses to consider emotional tone or reframe their approach.
This aligned with observations from the screen recordings and eye-tracking data, showing that several participants had brief pauses before replying to emotionally vague content.

PS11
noted a sense of co-construction and agreement:
“It was a good feeling knowing [
SimClient
] is picking up whatever I just mentioned and building it up”
.

Overall, participants adapted familiar support practices based on context, actively calibrating tone, timing, and involvement based on perceived cues.

Taken together, these reflections suggest that while

9 out of 12 participants used

PS5
and
PS10
regularly modified suggestions for clarity, tone, or personal voice.

PS1

often combined elements from different options, preferring the structure provided by

over composing replies from scratch.

Perceived Usefulness and Limitations.

Seven participants found
Suggestions
aligned with principles taught during trainings and useful for initiating conversations as well as structuring emotionally sensitive and empathetic replies.
They also appreciated having three categories to choose from.
While it helped
PS1
with ideation, it provided confidence and ensured alignment with fundamental principles for
PS12
, who highlighted a potential use case for novice peer supporters.
For some,
Suggestions
was used to evaluate and refine responses that were typed before its generation.

PS5
and
PS10
found
Suggestions
helpful for expanding ideas or offering alternative phrasings, with
PS10
noting that they were
“better than what [they] would have typed”
.
While
PS7
and
PS11
maintained personal styles like rapport-building approaches over using
Suggestions
verbatim, they took inspiration from it.

However, limitations were also noted.

Suggestions
was unable to adapt to diverse conversational styles and contexts, and did not build rapport before addressing
SimClient
’s concerns.

PS1
observed that
“[
Suggestions
] didn’t always match what [
SimClient
] was actually saying”
.

PS2
and
PS8
found them impersonal, while
PS8
described them as
“textbook examples”
, lacking humans’ spontaneity and flexibility.

PS11
found phrases like
“I understand”
hard to use authentically and was worried that overly supportive language such as
“I’m here for you, you are not alone”
might foster co-dependency if used too early.

PS7
similarly preferred approaches that emphasised gentle questioning or distractions over emotionally loaded reassurance.

Usability Challenges and Design Suggestions.

Participants reported mixed experiences with the timing and responsiveness of
Suggestions
.

PS4
and
PS6
noted that
Suggestions
often appeared too late, sometimes after they had composed a full response.

PS2
appreciated
Suggestions
but found the latency disrupted their response flow.

PS1
and
PS8
also noted repetition across suggestions and called for more contextual variation tailored to chat history.

Design suggestions included integrating resource links for critical cases (
PS12
), real-time feedback on typed replies and contextual adaptation with professional insights (
PS8
), and dynamic response progression across categories as conversations evolve (
PS9
).
Usability ideas included quick help commands (
PS10
), drag-and-drop tailoring of responses (
PS5
), thumbs-up/thumbs-down feedback systems (
PS7
), and a mix of full suggestions and keyword prompts (
PS3
).

PS7
suggested allowing
Suggestions
to temporarily handle conversations, enabling short breaks to be taken without interrupting support.

4.1.3.
Use of Emotional State Visualisations

Patterns of Use.

Participants differed in their engagement with the emotional state visualisations.
Three participants (
PS2
,
PS9
, and
PS12
) reported active use, primarily consulting the arousal graph to guide conversational pacing and tone.

PS12
and
PS2
used the graph to confirm when to wrap up the conversation, while
PS9
adjusted their tone upon seeing a
“medium to negative”
shift.
Eye-tracking data corroborated this, showing consistent glances at the graphs before typing responses in key moments of tone shift.

Others used the feature passively or sporadically.

PS5
,
PS10
, and
PS11
reported glancing at the visualisations but found direct chat content more informative, finding the graphs more suited for retrospective analysis.

PS3
only paid attention to the visualisations mid-session, rating their influence as
“1.5 out of 5 stars”
.
Five participants, including
PS1
,
PS6
, and
PS8
, did not know what to make of the visualisations and focused solely on the chat.
Overall, while some found the tool helpful, most relied on textual cues and interpersonal intuition.
Eye-tracking data showed little to no attention to the graph for these participants, who instead focused on composing responses in the chat window.

Interpretation, Adjustment, and Attunement.

The visualisations were found to occasionally shape participants’ tone modulation, pacing, and empathy strategies.
Participants demonstrated attunement even when cues were subtle, adapting tone, avoiding premature closure, or pausing to reflect.
These responses aligned with strategies used in real-world settings, such as affirmation (
PS1
,
PS2
), emotional containment and boundary-setting (
PS1
), open-ended questioning (
PS3
,
PS11
), reflective listening (
PS5
), and non-directive reframing (
PS6
).

Suggestions for Design Improvements.

Some participants experienced uncertainty when interpreting the visualisations and suggested several refinements.

PS12
described it as
“stressful yet beneficial”
, explaining that watching the graphs heightened their own emotional investment.

PS3
,
PS8
, and
PS10
struggled with technical terms such as
“valence”
suggesting layperson alternatives like
“engagement level”
.

PS5
,
PS7
, and
PS12
proposed a time-based view showing 2-3 dominant emotions simultaneously to capture trends without visual overload.
Others recommended moving the indicator closer to the "Client" label (see Figure
2
, A) (
PS10
), using colour-coded volume bars (
PS12
), or embedding contextual cues within the chat interface.

PS2
noted that the valence-arousal indicators were distracting and unclear, preferring that
“basic emotions”
be shown more directly, such as through traffic-light colours or simplified labels.
While a few participants found the tool informative or affirming, most ultimately preferred relying on text and interpersonal cues.
The visualisation was seen as a promising adjunct but in need of refinement to better support real-time peer support.

4.1.4.
Individual Differences in Engagement

Familiarity with AI

Participants reported moderate familiarity with AI (
mean=3.75
,
SD=1.06
on a 5-point Likert scale) and moderate usage frequency (
mean=3.83
,
SD=1.80
) on a 7-point Likert scale).
Notably, 6 participants (50%) reported using AI tools fewer than 4 times per week.
However, correlations with
Suggestions
usage were weak: familiarity (
r=0.12
) and usage frequency (
r=0.11
), suggesting that general AI familiarity may not reliably predict how participants integrate
Suggestions
into their interactions.
While higher-frequency AI users like
PS5
and
PS10
(6-7 times and 10+ times per week, respectively), adopted
Suggestions
more readily (70% and 64.71% respectively), other frequent users like
PS11
avoided them entirely, citing stylistic misalignment.
Conversely,
PS1
adopted suggestions at a modest rate (15.38%) despite rating themselves as not familiar (1) and reporting no AI use.

PS3
, who rated infrequent usage (2-3 times per week), remarked that
“suggestions will be helpful for someone…just starting out"”
highlighting how suggestions could serve as scaffolds in moments of uncertainty.

Personality Traits.

Participants showed moderate levels of Conscientiousness (
mean=3.17
,
SD=0.50
on a 5-point scale), indicating a tendency toward being organised and goal-oriented, though not strongly so. Conscientiousness also showed the clearest relationship with suggestion usage, with participants who scored higher being less likely to use
Suggestions
(
r=-0.25
), possibly reflecting a preference for crafting their own responses.
Other personality traits showed no meaningful associations.

These findings suggest personality traits alone do not reliably predict suggestion adoption, though they may interact with support style and perceived system alignment.

Physiological Indicators.

As preliminary HRV data revealed no consistent patterns, we did not pursue further analysis.
Eye-tracking data revealed that even participants who did not use
Suggestions
typically glanced at them briefly, but then returned focus to the chat.
This supports the idea that
Suggestions
functioned more as background support tools than directive input.

5.
Study 2: Expert Evaluation of Study 1’s Interactions

Participants
.
We recruited 5 mental health professionals (3 female), comprising 1 clinical psychologist, 3 licensed counsellors, and 1 medical social worker, all with 5 to 20 years (
mean=11.6
,
SD=7.70
) of professional experience in psychosocial support.
All participants were aged 25 and above (
mean=41.5
,
SD=7.48
), and had formal qualifications in clinical psychology, counselling, or social work, with at least a Bachelor’s degree in Psychology, Social Work, or a related field.
The medical social worker in this study held advanced certifications and was actively engaged in delivering mental health interventions alongside broader medical social services.
Outside their professional career, two of the participants also volunteered on text-based peer support platforms.

Method
.
Each session comprised five segments: Pre-Interview, Video Review 1, Post-Video Interview 1, Video Review 2, and Post-Video Interview 2, all conducted in person and screen- and audio-recorded.

Pre-Interview Segment.

Participants completed a short demographics questionnaire and a brief semi-structured interview designed to establish their professional background, experiences providing psychosocial support, and perspectives on digital mental health interventions.
This provided contextual grounding for subsequent evaluations.

Video Review and Think-Aloud Segments.

Each participant reviewed two anonymised videos of peer supporter-
SimClient
interactions from Study 1.
Videos were selected to vary in duration, strategies used, and use of
Suggestions
and emotional state visualisations (see Appendix
G
).
While watching, participants were prompted to think aloud, providing spontaneous reflections on conversational appropriateness, realism, and the peer supporter’s decision-making processes.

Post-Video Questionnaire and Interview Segments.

Following each video, participants first completed a short questionnaire assessing
SimClient
in similar ways to Study 1, the perceived appropriateness and usefulness of the three categories of
Suggestions
(
S_{MI}
,
S_{ER}
,
S_{PS}
), and the usefulness of the emotional state visualisations.
This was followed by a semi-structured interview to discuss the peer supporters’ responses, highlight strengths and limitations, and reflect on the LLMs’ role in shaping the interactions.
After answering questions about the second video, participants offered overarching reflections on the potential and pitfalls of LLM-supported peer support.

5.1.
Results

5.1.1.
Evaluation of Peer Supporter Responses

Observed Strengths and Gaps.

Experts highlighted strengths across peer supporter responses, such as through the use of PFA and counselling-aligned techniques like paraphrasing, affirmation and normalisation.
For example,
E1
noted that
PS1
responded to
SimClient
’s comment about enjoying social aspects of gaming with a mirrored prompt inviting elaboration.
Affirmation was observed when
PS1
responded to
SimClient
with
“Those sound like concrete suggestions”
, while
E3
noted
PS5
encouraging
SimClient
’s openness with
“I appreciate your bravery in opening up to me”
.
Similarly,
E3
noted that
PS8
normalised
SimClient
’s emotions and provided reassurance with
“Yes, it is indeed normal”
, after
SimClient
asked
“Is it normal to feel that way?”
.

Experts also highlighted the effective use of empathetic statements grounded in personal insight without shifting focus from
SimClient
.
For example,
E3
commended
PS5
’s framing of personal strategies to create resonance without overshadowing
SimClient
, creating a sense of shared experience.

E3
noted

However, some peer supporters were noted to rely too heavily on advice-giving or solutions, sometimes crossing into quasi-therapeutic territory.

E5

PS12

SimClient

E4
critiqued

Similarly,

SimClient

PS5

SimClient

Finally, experts noted that some attempts at validation felt mechanical.

PS4

Tensions and Misalignments with Professional Standards

Experts consistently noted that while many peer supporter responses were well-intentioned, they occasionally diverged from professional counselling expectations, which they believed could be due to differences in training, role clarity, and situational judgement.
Premature advice-giving was common, particularly before fully understanding
SimClient
’s concerns.
For example,
E1
critiqued
PS4
’s response to
SimClient
’s comment about wanting to
“escape from all these”
, moving too quickly into solutions instead of gently probing the underlying distress, missing the opportunity to explore potential risk.

Peer supporters also sometimes made assumptions about
SimClient
’s emotions or skipped steps in holding space for
SimClient
.

E2
suggested
PS3
may have overlooked people-pleasing tendencies in
SimClient
, mistaking agreement for progress.
Similarly,
E4
critiqued
PS7
’s use of distraction techniques (e.g., focusing on leaves turning yellow) as unnecessarily diverting the conversation without addressing core issues.
While such strategies may reduce arousal temporarily, experts believed they were not sustainable long-term solutions and could risk suppressing rather than processing emotions.

Eight interactions were evaluated to be overly cognitively demanding for
SimClient
.

E5
highlighted that
PS12
’s responses, though well-meaning, became cognitively demanding due to their length and complexity, risking overwhelming
SimClient
.
In
PS7
’s case,
E4
similarly noted that long and suggestive messages risked overwhelming
SimClient
, observing that
“there were too many words to take in”
, particularly given the emotional tone and pacing of the conversation.

E4
went as far as questioning whether the peer supporter was human, describing their tone as so scripted and disconnected that it resembled an AI-generated interaction, almost akin to an AI-generated peer supporter planted to test their judgement.

Overall, experts acknowledged the inherent role tensions, highlighting that the non-clinical setting of peer support suggested that they were not expected to perform therapeutic tasks such as risk assessment or problem-solving.
However, they also discouraged the use of vague encouragement (e.g.,
“you should give yourself credit”
), overgeneralised phrases (e.g.,
“I understand”
), or abrupt conversational endings without clear summarisation, warning that such practices could erode client trust and leave emotional needs unmet.
These misalignments underscore the need for more aligned training and stronger scaffolding in emotionally nuanced scenarios.

Recommendations for Improvement

Experts offered recommendations to enhance the quality, safety, and emotional efficacy of peer support interactions.
A recurring theme was the need for warmer, more affirming openings to reduce initial client hesitation and promote openness.
For instance,
E2
suggested that
PS10
’s starting greeting could shift from
“what brings you to us today?”
to
“hello, welcome…your presence here matters”
, noting that such a change would put
SimClient
at ease and remove barriers to sharing.

Experts also recommended refining the use of self-disclosure.

E2
pointed out that rather than offering full personal disclosures,
‘I’
or
‘we’
statements (e.g.,
“I’m really glad you reached out; friendships can be tough”
) could be used to show empathy without centring
PS10
’s personal life.
Similarly,
E5
noted
PS9
’s responses were appropriate but could have been more empathetic before they moved on to follow-up questions.

Another key area was pacing.
Experts recommended that peer supporters could slow down advice-giving, focus on reflective listening, and de-prioritise problem resolution.
Instead, they suggested open-ended questions, rephrasing of client statements, and gentle invitations to elaborate to better understand
SimClient
’s underlying needs and emotions.
This was particularly evident in cases like
PS3
,
PS8
, and
PS7
, evaluated by
E2
,
E3
, and
E4
respectively.
Another example included
E3

PS5
could have extended the conversation to explore the evolution of
SimClient
’s difficulties rather than immediately offering small-step solutions.

In addition, experts emphasised the importance of holding space and allowing
SimClient
to process emotions.

E5
highlighted that
PS12
’s long, complex responses risked overwhelming
SimClient
before they were ready for solutions.
Similar concerns were flagged by
E4
about
PS7
’s verbose responses.

Finally, experts also encouraged peer supporters to end conversations with intentional summarisation and expressions of ongoing support, particularly in cases where acts of closure felt abrupt or vague.
For example,
E4
critiqued
PS7
’s final line,
“Peace be with you”
, as vague and emotionally uncontained.
They recommended more structured endings that clarify progress, provide closure, and signal availability for follow-up.

Experts expressed cautious optimism about the value of the emotional state visualisations.
Although they were only rated helpful across 40% of the chat logs, all five experts saw potential for improving emotional attunement and pacing.

E1

E1
noted that even if

E3
hypothesised that

E4

However, in one case,

More broadly,
E4

Despite these benefits, experts voiced reservations.

E5

Nevertheless, they felt that novice peer supporters might benefit, particularly from the top-bar displays.

E3

For real clients,
E2

5.1.3.
Implications for System Design and Training

System Design Considerations.

Experts offered suggestions concerning the design and deployment of LLM-supported systems in peer support contexts.
While they acknowledged the promise of tools such as
SimClient
,
Suggestions
, and emotional state visualisations, they emphasised the need for clearer framing, design safeguards, and pedagogical scaffolds to maximise benefit and minimise risk.

Several interface enhancements were proposed.

E1
suggested using distinct colours or labels to help peer supporters distinguish between multiple clients if needed.
They also emphasised calibrating expectations for peer supporters, noting that their role was inherently non-professional and involved offering emotional support rather than formal therapeutic interventions.
For example, when presented with a set of emotional states (e.g., six possible states of life), peer supporters could proactively ask whether
SimClient
agrees or offer suggestions to gauge client receptiveness.

To reduce peer supporter burnout and support their affective attunement,
E3
proposed extending unobtrusive emotion-tracking tools such as typing patterns or message content to peer supporters.

E4
added that integrating elements, such as tracking valence over time, could be valuable if validated carefully and refined iteratively.
This might allow AI systems to support not only client wellbeing but also the evolving emotional and cognitive states of peer supporters themselves.

Implications for Peer Supporter Trainings.

While experts acknowledged the potential of
SimClient
and
Suggestions
in peer supporter training, they cautioned that improvements are needed for them to effectively simulate complex emotional dynamics, client resistance, and human-like distress.

SimClient
was described as too cooperative and emotionally consistent, offering limited opportunities to practise attunement, boundary-setting, or managing distress escalation.
Experts proposed embedding personality traits, backstories, and emotionally ambiguous cues to simulate more realistic clients for training.

For
Suggestions
,
E4
warned against presenting them as prescriptive.
Instead, suggestions should be framed as optional prompts, with training modules that teach users how to interpret, adapt, or reject them.
Without this explicit guidance, novice users risk adopting them uncritically, reducing opportunities to practise key skills such as reflective listening and paraphrasing.

Experts agreed that emotional state visualisation tools may support pacing and empathy, particularly in asynchronous contexts.
However,
E2
and
E5
emphasised the need for simplified presentation, clear terminology, and training in interpretation.
To support accurate and constructive use, they recommended clearer integration of both categorical and dimensional models, simplified terminology, and built-in feedback mechanisms.

6.
Discussion

6.1.
AI as Collaborative Partners: Balancing Support and Autonomy

Our findings highlight both the potential and limitations of integrating LLMs into peer support settings, particularly the tension between structured guidance and the conversational flexibility required for authentic, context-sensitive interactions.
Participants viewed LLM-generated
Suggestions
as scaffolds that offered phrasing guidance, reinforced training principles, and at times bolstered confidence, particularly during moments of uncertainty.
However, they also noted a potential additional layer of cognitive effort, having to read, evaluate, and adapt suggestions while simultaneously managing the emotional flow of the conversation.
This was especially burdensome when
Suggestions
appeared misaligned with
SimClient
’s input or arrived late.
For some,
these disruptions risked detracting from the relational immediacy of supporting
SimClient
.

This corroborates prior work on co-writing systems, where users balanced benefits of AI scaffolding against the need for authenticity.
Some participants
noted that while
Suggestions
was conceptually helpful, they often preferred their own experience-informed phrasing, desiring greater control over the emotional tone and intentionality of their responses.
This aligns with Hwang et al.
(
Hwang et al. 2025
)
, who described the burden of "double work" in AI-assisted writing, and highlights the need for better timing, adaptability, and better-framed suggestions.
Other participants expressed concerns that accepting AI prompts verbatim could result in a “robot talking to robot” dynamic, which undermines the goal of authentic human connection.

Future systems could offer more phase-sensitive support, both in phase detection and in limiting
Suggestions
to specific moments or allowing users to manually trigger them as needed, enabling AI to modulate its role as the conversation deepens.
For instance, initial phases might prioritise rapport-building while later phases could focus on problem-solving or emotional validation.
Echoing findings from Li et al.
(
Li et al. 2025
)
, features like confidence scores or emotional tone cues could also help signal more relevant prompts, though these should be designed as reflective prompts that preserve autonomy and promote learning through deliberative practice.

LLM-based training systems that integrate simulated practice with automated feedback show promise for developing supportive communication skills
(
Hsu et al. 2025
;
Louie et al. 2025
;
Chaszczewicz et al. 2024
)
.
For instance,
CARE
demonstrated that feedback significantly improved novices’ empathetic and reflective skills, compared to practice alone, which sometimes led to declines in empathy expression
(
Louie et al. 2025
)
.
This suggests that future systems could embed real-time or post-session feedback mechanisms to scaffold skill acquisition and self-reflection.

Some participants in our study preferred modular, inline prompts over full-text replacements and valued manual control over suggestion refreshes, aligning with prior work on human-AI collaboration
(
Sharma et al. 2023
)
and rewriting user responses
(
Sharma et al. 2021
)
.
Differentiated support by expertise level was also proposed, with more directive prompts for novices and refinements for advanced users.

6.2.
Standards and Misalignments: Bridging Peer Support and Professional Practices

Our study surfaced tensions between peer supporter practices and expert standards, underscoring the fragmented landscape of peer support.
While peer supporters frequently demonstrated affirmation, reflective listening, and rapport-building, experts identified gaps in pacing, space-holding, as well as peer supporters giving advice or quasi-therapeutic responses, highlighting a need to better understand differences between professional and peer support roles.

A major challenge lies in the definitional ambiguity of peer support.
In Southeast Asia, where lived experience is not always a prerequisite, peer support encompasses a wide spectrum of roles and approaches, giving rise to differences in protocols and boundaries.
Experts in our study called for clearer frameworks to guide both AI development and peer support practice.

Evidence from Table
2
highlights these misalignments.
Peer supporters often rated
SimClient
more favourably than experts, with weak agreements and correlations across all evaluation criteria.
These divergences reflect differing expectations and evaluative criteria between both groups.

LLM-supported systems could help address such gaps by incorporating training prompts, ethical nudges, or escalation flags in real time, particularly in informal peer support contexts with limited expert oversight.
As such networks proliferate, such tools could support more calibrated responses, especially given the lack of standardised protocols.
However, such interventions must remain locally and culturally grounded.
Rather than relying solely on base models (e.g., GPT-4o, as per our study), fine-tuned LLMs should reflect local norms and communication styles, ideally via fine-tuning or co-design with peer supporters and professionals.

6.3.
Designing Realistic, Responsive, and Supportive Systems

Participants across both studies emphasised the need for more emotionally realistic and relationally complex simulations.
While
SimClient
expressed relatable concerns, it often lacked the ambiguity, resistance, and conversational irregularity of real-world distress.
Quantitative analysis from Study 1 supported this, showing weak synchrony in message pacing (
r=0.18
), indicating limited mutual adjustment or elaborative scaffolding.
Experts further critiqued
SimClient
for being overly agreeable, topically shallow, and grammatically polished.
These qualities limited opportunities to practise repair, boundary-setting, or navigating resistance.
Together, these findings suggest that while
SimClient
was topically responsive, it lacked the natural rhythms and unpredictability of human distress.
Across both studies, participants suggested that future work might model emotional trajectory alignment, disfluency, or strategic silence to better reflect real client interactions.

Nevertheless, a controlled simulation like
SimClient
remains valuable for peer support training, offering a consistent and safe environment for skill development.
One participant noted that their engagement with
SimClient
renewed their interest in volunteering, highlighting the motivational value of well-designed simulations.

Improving
SimClient
could include consistent personality profiles,
scripting varied distress behaviours (e.g., withdrawal, over-disclosure), and using features such as emojis or ellipses to signal affect.
Progressively increasing issue complexity may also help peer supporters build confidence gradually.

Expert-guided simulations can further enhance realism and alignment with established counselling practices
(
Louie et al. 2024
)
, improving training fidelity by reducing the risk of inappropriate responses.
Simulation diversity should also be expanded across demographic and emotional dimensions.
Culturally grounded behaviour, including local norms of distress expression
(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
, is essential for inclusivity and contextual relevance.

Relational analysis frameworks, such as those by Li et al.
(
Li et al. 2024
)
, could help model and evaluate the evolving alliance between peer supporters and LLM-simulated clients.

Participants also highlighted the need to improve emotional state visualisations.
Quadrant-based or layered models capturing multiple co-occurring emotions, dynamic indicators (e.g., motion or colour), and clearer word choices were proposed to enhance interpretability.

Although preliminary HRV data did not yield conclusive patterns, HRV remains a promising indicator for capturing the physiological impact of emotionally supportive interactions.
Prior research suggests increased HRV is associated with reduced stress, improved emotion regulation, and enhanced capacity for social engagement
(
Goodyke et al. 2022
;
Kim et al. 2018
;
Mather and Thayer 2018
)
.
In the context of peer support, tracking HRV alongside behavioural and textual signals may offer a multimodal understanding of how volunteers regulate their emotions while attending to distressed clients.
This is especially crucial in situations where poor self-regulation might contribute to lower-quality interactions, in turn affecting client outcomes.
Future studies might explore whether moments of rapport, rupture, or co-regulation align with fluctuations in autonomic activity, offering richer insight into emotional labour and stress.

6.4.
Key Design Tensions

6.4.1.
Human vs AI Identity.

In Study 2,
E4
suspected
PS7
to be AI, not due to polished, formulaic language or uncanny perfection, but because of the perceived poor quality of support provided.
This reversal draws on the "reverse Turing" phenomenon
(
Sejnowski 2023
)
, raising questions on how conversational performance influences judgments of human- and machine-likeness.
While
SimClient
locally mirrored
PS7
’s verbosity, broader analyses showed weak synchrony across sessions (
r=0.18
), suggesting that adaptive alignment was not consistently achieved.
This raises questions about whether simulations should mimic human variability or maintain a stable tone to avoid confusion.
These findings suggest that adaptive mirroring by
SimClient
, though intended to promote alignment, may at times validate ineffective peer support patterns or obscure relational asymmetries.
Future work should investigate when human-to-human interactions risk being misclassified as AI-driven, and how such misclassifications shape perceptions of empathy, competence, and trust.
Understanding how AI responsiveness influences perceived expertise and relational quality will be critical as LLMs become more integrated into support contexts.

6.4.2.
Trust vs Deference.

While
Suggestions
provided valuable scaffolding and guidance, over-reliance risks compromising peer supporters’ autonomy and suppressing learning.
Confidence displays may help calibrate trust and reassurance but must be well-designed
(
Li et al. 2025
)
.
Trust in such systems has also been shown to be shaped by domain expertise and prior experience
(
Swinger et al. 2025
)
.
Future research should examine how these factors affect peer supporter behaviour in terms of using
Suggestions
, trust, and learning trajectories.

6.4.3.
Rapport vs Guidance.

Overly formal or scripted prompts from
Suggestions
were seen to hinder rapport-building, with some participants opting not to use them due to perceived relational disconnect.
This mirrors concerns from past work, where peer supporters expressed discomfort with polished AI responses that felt impersonal or lacking empathy
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
.
However, incorporating tone-adaptive cues, social signals, and informal language or disfluencies could potentially support authenticity
(
Wang et al. 2025
;
Bedmutha et al. 2024
)
.
Future research should investigate how AI-generated suggestions are adapted across different phases of a conversation, how support seekers perceive and respond to these suggestions, and whether AI can even intelligently modulate its guidance across conversational phases.
Longitudinal studies may also shed light on whether AI-assisted rapport-building contributes to sustained trust and engagement over time.

6.4.4.
Realism vs Diversity.

In our work,
SimClient
was designed to be largely uniform in personality and distress expression.
Although this consistency supported comparability and safety, it may have limited opportunities to practise adaptability, cultural sensitivity, or rapport across varied client types.
Future work on LLM-simulated clients should reflect diverse distress expressions and demographics.
Without variation in age, gender, culture, or emotional style, simulations risk reinforcing narrow interaction patterns
(
Yang et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2024a
)
.
Grounding responses in personality traits
(
McCrae and John 1992
)
and incorporating multimodal behaviours
(
Steenstra et al. 2025
)
may enhance realism and skill transfer.

6.4.5.
Support vs Cognitive Load.

Findings across both studies indicate that the usefulness of
Suggestions
was also closely tied to conversational timing.
In Study 1, participants frequently reported that
Suggestions
was most useful during early-phase interactions, but quickly became repetitive or not contextualised enough as conversations progressed.
Experts in Study 2 echoed these concerns, noting that
Suggestions
imposed a substantial cognitive load, especially when they arrived late or misaligned with the conversational phase.
For instance, suggestions prompting solution-giving were sometimes surfaced too early, risking premature advice or emotional misattunement.

These findings suggest that
Suggestions
could potentially be more useful during planning or ideation phases, rather than real-time conversation.
Preparing peer supporters with strategic conversation planning tools beforehand, or offering reflective feedback after sessions, may reduce real-time cognitive effort while preserving skill development
(
Hwang et al. 2025
)
.
However, longitudinal studies are needed to investigate whether AI mediation cumulatively reduces supporter burnout or reshapes the nature of their emotional labour.

7.
Limitations

While the system was designed for simplicity and replicability, several limitations constrained its ecological validity.
First, modern messaging affordances such as auto-correct, auto-complete, emoji support, typing indicators, and overlapping threads were absent.
These omissions could have reduced naturalism and may have limited peer supporters’ abilities to practise managing interruptions, ambiguity, and multi-threaded dialogues typical of real-world peer support.

Across both studies, participants noted that
SimClient
’s overly compliant and polished style constrained opportunities for emotional attunement and holding space.
Although the distress narratives were relatable, the lack of ambiguity and emotional complexity limited realism.
Fine-tuning
SimClient
with real-world conversational data and integrating personality-grounded behaviours or social signals would enhance its authenticity and training value.

Suggestions
also exhibited technical and design limitations.
While we designed
Suggestions
to replace entire text boxes rather than offering modular or inline corrections, participants noted that this restricted their ability to refine or supplement their own phrasing without overwriting it.
Latency in generation in some sessions led participants to pre-emptively type their responses without waiting.
Participants expressed a desire for more granular, interactive scaffolding that could adapt to their evolving needs and allow greater control over when and how suggestions were refreshed, potentially limiting exploration of how AI might function as a more dynamic conversational partner.

Another limitation concerns the risk of AI-AI conversations.
If participants relied exclusively on
Suggestions
while conversing with
SimClient
, interactions risk devolving into machine-to-machine exchanges, undermining the intended human-in-the-loop model.
This raises important questions about authenticity, ownership of emotional labour, and the relational quality of such interactions.
However, this study focused only on peer supporter behaviours.
Without input from support seekers, we were unable to assess how AI-assisted interactions affect perceptions of empathy, trust, or emotional resonance.

Emotional state visualisations were occasionally misunderstood or underutilised, which may have reduced participants’ ability to attune to
SimClient
’s emotional state and shaped their responses.
Participants highlighted opportunities for future enhancement, such as multi-dimensional emotion displays (e.g., valence-arousal quadrants) and stronger salience of emotional alerts, particularly in crisis scenarios.

In Study 2, we required each video to be treated independently.
Participants were interviewed after Video 1 before viewing Video 2, which may have primed them to focus on specific features like emotional state visualisations or peer supporter performance.
While this mitigated recall decay, it may have shaped comparative judgments.

Two recordings from Study 1 (
PS6
and
PS11
) have not yet been analysed at the time of writing and are thus excluded from Study 2 results.
Although their omission minimally affects the diversity of evaluative insights, their inclusion in future analyses will strengthen the robustness of findings.

Both studies were conducted in controlled lab settings, with a simulated distressed client and one-message-at-a-time structure, inherently differing from real-world peer support contexts.
Real-world peer support could involve managing multiple conversations and external distractions, which were absent here.
The asynchronous chat format and controlled conditions may have also dampened emotional reactivity.
Additionally, while the system was culturally grounded in a Southeast Asian context, reflecting local norms and colloquialisms of peer support, we note that findings may not fully generalise to regions with different norms and emotional expression styles.

Finally, the short-term nature of Study 1 limits insight into long-term effects on retention, skill development, and emotional labour.
We also did not explicitly design to anticipate the reverse Turing phenomenon identified in the study, highlighting the need for more robust evaluative tools that distinguish between human and AI-generated responses.

8.
Conclusion

As mental health systems face rising demand and limited resources, AI-supported peer support tools offer promising yet complex opportunities to expand support pathways.
LLMs offer new possibilities to scaffold this work, enhancing the capacity of peer supporters to respond empathetically, reflectively, and confidently in emotionally nuanced conversations.

Through two studies, we explored how LLM-supported components (i.e., LLM-simulated distressed client, real-time LLM-generated suggestions, and emotional state visualisations) might support peer support practice.
While our findings highlight the potential of these tools to enhance confidence, structure reflection, and support conversational flow, they also raise important cautions.
Authenticity, emotional nuance, and role clarity remain central to meaningful support.
Misalignments between peer supporter practices and expert expectations and standards point to the need for psychologically grounded and context-sensitive scaffolding.

We argue that LLMs should be positioned not as replacements but as collaborative partners that augment peer supporters’ capacity for empathy, responsiveness, and reflective practice.
To achieve this, future systems must support conversational adaptability, cultural grounding, and emotionally realistic simulation.
Longitudinal deployment and co-design with peer supporters and experts will be critical for achieving both safety and sustained engagement.

More broadly, our work contributes to growing conversations in HCI and mental health about how AI might responsibly support emotionally sensitive contexts.
As digital mental health ecosystems evolve, the challenge lies not only in what AI can do, but in how it can be integrated to uphold the emotional and ethical complexity of human care.
Looking ahead, with thoughtful co-design, longitudinal evaluation, and ethical grounding, we envision such ecosystems empowering peer supporters through AI integration to deliver more responsive, inclusive and effective support.
