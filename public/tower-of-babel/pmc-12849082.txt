Ultrasound-guided percutaneous core biopsy of renal sinus masses: diagnostic yield, safety, and clinical impact
Authors: Liang Xia, Zeng Xiantao, Su Miaojiao, Hong Zhiliang, Wu Songsong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRenal sinus lesions present diagnostic challenges due to their complex anatomy and heterogeneous pathology. Reliable tissue acquisition is essential for guiding treatment decisions, yet evidence on the performance of ultrasound (US)-guided biopsy in this setting remains limited.ObjectiveTo evaluate the diagnostic yield, safety profile, and clinical impact of US-guided percutaneous core needle biopsy(CNB) for renal sinus masses at a tertiary care center.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 51 patients who underwent US-guided percutaneous CNB for indeterminate renal sinus masses between 2010 and 2020. Diagnostic yield, histopathological results, complication rates (graded by Clavien-Dindo classification), and subsequent changes in clinical management were assessed. Univariate logistic regression was employed to identify factors associated with complications.ResultsBiopsy achieved a diagnostic yield of 90.2% (46/51). Malignant lesions accounted for 88.2% of cases, most commonly upper-tract urothelial carcinoma (43.1%) and clear cell renal cell carcinoma (31.4%). Benign lesions comprised 11.8%. The overall complication rate was 7.8% (4/51), limited to grade I–II events (perirenal hematoma, hematuria); no major complications or needle tract seeding were observed. Biopsy findings altered treatment in 23.5% of patients, and unnecessary surgery was avoided in 7.8%. Tumor size, location, and number of needle passes were not significantly associated with complications (all P > 0.05).ConclusionUS-guided percutaneous CNB of renal sinus masses is a safe and effective diagnostic approach, providing high tissue yield with low morbidity. By enabling accurate diagnosis and reducing overtreatment, this technique represents a valuable tool in the management of complex renal tumors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40644-025-00983-w.

Introduction

Image-guided percutaneous biopsy plays a central role in modern oncology, contributing not only to initial diagnosis and tumor staging but also to treatment monitoring, evaluation of residual disease, and confirmation of recurrence [1]. For patients who are poor surgical candidates, biopsy provides a pathway to alternative strategies such as active surveillance or thermal ablation. With the expansion of targeted therapies in the era of precision medicine, the ability to obtain high-quality tissue samples has become increasingly critical for molecular subtyping and biomarker testing [2–4]. Among imaging modalities, ultrasound (US) and computed tomography (CT) remain the most widely used. US has gained particular prominence owing to its convenience, real-time visualization, and lack of ionizing radiation, making it one of the preferred methods for image-guided interventions [5].

Recent advances have further extended the role of US in biopsies of lesions located in anatomically complex regions. Biopsies of the renal sinus, however, remain technically demanding because of the intricate anatomy and proximity to critical structures such as the renal vasculature and ureter [6, 7]. Nevertheless, US guidance offers precise targeting and real-time monitoring, enabling safe tissue acquisition even in this challenging location [8]. The renal sinus accommodates a diverse spectrum of pathologies, including upper-tract urothelial carcinoma(UTUC), renal cell carcinoma(RCC), lymphoma, metastases, and benign entities such as angiomyolipoma and inflammatory pseudotumor [9–12]. UTUC is the most common malignancy in this region [13], yet the overlapping imaging features of other tumors often hinder accurate differentiation. Given this histological heterogeneity, imaging alone is frequently insufficient, and histopathological confirmation is essential for guiding appropriate treatment strategies [14].

Although previous studies have established the safety and efficacy of US-guided biopsy for renal parenchymal masses [15–18], systematic investigations specifically addressing renal sinus lesions remain limited. The available data are largely restricted to small series, leaving diagnostic accuracy and complication profiles insufficiently characterized. At our institution, clinical experience has allowed the accumulation of a relatively large cohort of patients who underwent US-guided renal sinus biopsy. This provides an opportunity for a more robust evaluation. Accordingly, the present study retrospectively analyzes these cases to assess the diagnostic performance, technical feasibility, and safety of US-guided core needle biopsy(CNB) of renal sinus masses, as well as its impact on subsequent clinical management.

Materials and methods

Declarations

The requirement for informed consent was waived by the institutional review board due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Patient identification and data collection

Using the ultrasound workstation (Lanwon V7 Build1560312), we retrospectively screened all patients referred for ultrasound-guided percutaneous CNB of renal sinus masses between 2010 and 2020. A keyword search (“kidney,” “biopsy”) identified 133 records; 71 parenchymal biopsies were excluded, leaving 62 patients with renal sinus lesions(Fig. 1). Clinical and imaging data were reviewed, including demographics, lesion characteristics (size, location, composition, RENAL score), serum creatinine, urine cytology when available, history of ureteroscopic evaluation, and renal morphology (solitary kidney or bilateral lesions), as well as procedural details, pathological diagnosis, and follow-up information. Eleven patients with incomplete imaging or clinical data were excluded, yielding a final cohort of 51 patients(Fig. 2).

Pre-biopsy assessment and biopsy eligibility

All patients underwent contrast-enhanced CT or magnetic resonance imaging(MRI) to confirm an indeterminate renal sinus lesion or to establish the need for histological confirmation. Urine cytology and ureteroscopy were recorded when performed but were not routinely required. Biopsy eligibility was determined by a multidisciplinary tumor board.

Biopsy procedure

Ultrasound examinations were performed using Philips iU22 and GE Vivid 7 Dimension systems equipped with linear (5–12 MHz) and convex (2–5 MHz) transducers. All patients underwent standard pre-procedural laboratory tests. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound(CEUS) was routinely used to localize enhancing, viable tissue.

Under in-plane ultrasound guidance, a 15G coaxial introducer needle was advanced to the lesion margin. A 16G automated core-cutting biopsy needle (Zhejiang Curaway Medical Technology Inc., China) was used to obtain samples (Fig. 3). Following tissue acquisition, radiofrequency tract coagulation was performed using continuous energy delivery at 20–40 W, targeting 60–80 °C, applied for 3–5 s during incremental withdrawal of the introducer, until complete removal from the kidney. The number of cores was determined by macroscopic adequacy; no on-site cytopathology was available. All procedures were performed under local anesthesia by two senior interventional radiologists (20 and 15 years of experience).

Post-procedure management

After the procedure, manual compression was applied for 5 min followed by sterile dressing. Patients were monitored with ultrasound for 30–60 min, kept on bed rest for 2 h, and discharged the following day after overnight observation.

Pathological evaluation

All specimens were reviewed by a dedicated urogenital pathologist (15 years of experience). RCC diagnoses were assessed using WHO/ISUP criteria, and UTUC was graded per contemporary EAU guidelines [13]. For patients undergoing surgery, biopsy results were compared with final pathology for subtype, grade, and adverse features, including sarcomatoid/rhabdoid differentiation, lymphovascular invasion(LVI), and perineural invasion (PNI).

Follow-up and outcome assessment

All patients underwent standardized post-biopsy surveillance with ultrasound at 1, 3, and 6 months and contrast-enhanced CT/MRI at 6–12-month intervals for at least 12 months. Two radiologists independently reviewed all imaging for delayed complications or tract seeding; discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

For malignant cases, long-term oncological outcomes—including overall survival (OS), cancer-specific survival (CSS), and recurrence-free survival (RFS)—were assessed from the date of biopsy. Stable imaging for ≥ 12 months without growth, new enhancement, morphologic change, or metastasis was considered confirmation of benign disease in nonsurgical cases.

Definitions and evaluation criteria

RENAL nephrometry scores were assigned using the method of Kutikov and Uzzo [19]. Complications were graded according to the Clavien–Dindo classification [20]. A biopsy was defined as diagnostically successful if malignancy was identified or if a benign result was supported by ≥ 12 months of stable imaging. A diagnosis was considered inaccurate if benign biopsy results were followed by progression or malignancy at repeat biopsy/surgery.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp.). Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages; continuous variables as medians (range). Diagnostic success and complication rates were calculated. Univariate logistic regression using Firth’s penalized likelihood was applied to identify predictors of diagnostic success and complications, including tumor size (> 30 vs. ≤30 mm), location (upper/middle vs. lower pole), hilar proximity, and number of biopsy passes. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Survival outcomes were assessed using Kaplan–Meier analysis. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient and tumor characteristics

Fifty-one patients underwent ultrasound-guided percutaneous CNB of renal sinus masses. The cohort included 29 men (56.9%) and 22 women (43.1%), with a median age of 63 years (19–85). The median lesion diameter was 31 mm (13–78), and heterogeneous enhancement was observed in 56.9%. Most lesions were highly complex: 45 (88.2%) had RENAL scores of 10–12, and 6 (11.8%) had scores of 7–9. Median serum creatinine was 1.02 mg/dL; one patient (2.0%) had a solitary kidney, and none had bilateral renal sinus lesions. Detailed baseline characteristics are provided in Table 1.

Diagnostic performance and histopathology

The overall diagnostic success rate was 90.2% (46/51), yielding 41 malignant and 5 benign diagnoses(Table 2). Five biopsies (9.8%) were nondiagnostic due to insufficient tissue, necrosis, or fibrosis. In two cases, the initial biopsy yielded only one tissue core, which was insufficient for diagnosis; both were later confirmed by surgery to be UTUC. One lesion exhibited extensive central necrosis with no viable area on CEUS, and was confirmed postoperatively as RCC (WHO/ISUP Grade III). In one case, severe fibrosis in the biopsy specimen precluded diagnosis; laparoscopic biopsy later confirmed a solitary fibrous tumor. One case was complicated by intraoperative bleeding that interfered with sampling; a second CT-guided biopsy confirmed metastatic adenocarcinoma originating from the lung. Only one UTUC patient underwent ureteroscopy, which was nondiagnostic; similarly, urine cytology was negative in all three patients tested. No clinical or lesion-related variables—including tumor size, location, hilar proximity, or number of biopsy passes—were significantly associated with diagnostic success (Table 3).

Complication analysis

The overall complication rate of ultrasound-guided percutaneous biopsy of renal sinus masses was 7.84% (4/51), including three cases of perirenal hematoma (15–20 mm in diameter) and one case of gross hematuria, all classified as Clavien–Dindo grade I–II. Details of management and outcomes are as follows:

Case 1

Immediate postoperative ultrasound revealed a 15 mm hematoma. The patient remained hemodynamically stable and was managed with local compression and extended bed rest (6 h). The hematoma resolved spontaneously within one week, as confirmed on follow-up ultrasound.

Case 2

Minor bleeding occurred during the procedure. Thrombin (500 IU) was injected via the coaxial sheath to embolize the needle tract. The patient was observed for 24 h postoperatively without the need for further intervention.

Case 3

The patient developed mild flank pain 24 h post-procedure. Ultrasound confirmed a 20 mm perirenal hematoma. Symptomatic treatment with analgesia and an extended hospital stay (48 h) was provided. The hematoma resolved completely by one-month follow-up.

Case 4

(hematuria): Gross hematuria developed 6 h after biopsy. The patient was treated with intravenous tranexamic acid and absolute bed rest. Hematuria resolved within 24 h without evidence of coagulopathy or thrombotic events.

No major hemorrhage, intervention, arteriovenous fistula, or tract-ablation–related complications were observed. No clinical or lesion characteristics were associated with complication risk (Table 5).

Follow-up and oncological outcomes

All available post-biopsy axial imaging data were retrospectively reviewed to evaluate for delayed complications and needle tract seeding. As of the time of data collection, five patients had no imaging or clinical follow-up. Among the remaining 46 patients, post-biopsy follow-up imaging was available (median 5 years; range 0.2–12). No delayed complications or needle-tract seeding were identified. For malignant cases, 5-year OS, CSS, and RFS were 51.2%, 61.0%, and 46.3%, respectively. RCC demonstrated consistently better survival outcomes than UTUC, which showed higher recurrence and metastasis rates. Detailed oncological results are presented in Suppl Tables 1 and Suppl. Figure 5.

Impact on clinical decision-making

Biopsy altered management in 23.5% of patients (12/51), avoiding unnecessary surgery in 4 patients (7.8%)(Fig. 4、Suppl. Figure 1). Among the 46 patients with treatment data, 27 underwent surgery alone (23 RNU, 4 PN) (Suppl. Figure 2、Suppl. Figure 3、Suppl. Figure 4). One patient received systemic therapy only, two underwent ablation, and one received chemotherapy alone for unresectable disease. Five patients with benign biopsy findings entered active surveillance; none showed radiologic progression or malignant transformation. Ten patients received combination therapy (7 surgery plus chemotherapy; 3 chemotherapy plus radiotherapy). Chemotherapy was administered as neoadjuvant (n = 3), adjuvant (n = 5), or palliative treatment (n = 2). UTUC patients predominantly received gemcitabine–cisplatin, while RCC patients received TKI-based regimens. Treatment details are shown in Table 6; Fig. 5.

Non-surgical diagnostic confirmation

Twelve patients did not undergo surgery. All had ≥ 12 months of stable imaging, and 10 had > 24 months of follow-up (median 38 months). Serial CT/MRI demonstrated no interval growth, new enhancement, morphologic change, or metastatic progression. No delayed malignancy was identified, supporting the accuracy of biopsy-based diagnosis in nonsurgical cases.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that ultrasound-guided percutaneous renal sinus CNB provides high diagnostic performance while maintaining a favorable safety profile in the evaluation of complex renal sinus masses. Compared with current guideline-recommended diagnostic modalities—CT urography, urine cytology, and ureteroscopic biopsy—ultrasound-guided CNB offers complementary diagnostic value, particularly when imaging findings are indeterminate or tissue diagnosis is required to guide systemic therapy. CT urography has limited sensitivity for small or flat urothelial lesions, urine cytology shows suboptimal performance in low-grade disease, and ureteroscopic biopsy is technically demanding with nondiagnostic rates ranging from 10–30% [21]. In clinical practice at our institution, ureteroscopy is selectively performed due to long-standing practice patterns, limited accessibility of extraluminal renal sinus lesions, and patient-related factors such as frailty or advanced disease. Correspondingly, only one UTUC case underwent ureteroscopic biopsy in our cohort, and it yielded nondiagnostic tissue. Within this context, the 90.2% diagnostic success rate observed in our study underscores the clinical utility of ultrasound-guided CNB as an effective adjunctive diagnostic tool.

Diagnostic outcomes for percutaneous renal biopsy are well-established, with reported accuracies of 85–97% [22–31]. Our findings extend this evidence to the renal sinus, an anatomically complex region traditionally considered challenging for percutaneous access. The diagnostic accuracy of ultrasound-guided CNB in our cohort (90.2%) was comparable to MRI-guided techniques reported in the literature (90–96%) [32], yet ultrasound offers broader accessibility, lower cost, and real-time procedural flexibility. The systematic use of core needles and CEUS guidance likely contributed to our high diagnostic yield by improving visualization of viable enhancing tissue and reducing sampling from necrotic or fibrotic areas that commonly produce nondiagnostic specimens. Among surgically treated patients, biopsy demonstrated high concordance with final histopathology for RCC subtyping and UTUC grading, although focal adverse features were infrequently detected—consistent with the inherent sampling limitations of needle biopsy. These results collectively support ultrasound-guided CNB as a reliable modality for histologic characterization of renal sinus lesions, while acknowledging its expected limitations in capturing focal aggressive features.

The oncological implications of tissue diagnosis are substantial. More than half of malignant renal sinus lesions in our cohort were UTUC, a pathology that often necessitates nephroureterectomy rather than nephron-sparing surgery. Accurate preoperative differentiation between UTUC and RCC is therefore essential [33], especially in the era of precision oncology where treatment increasingly relies on histologic and molecular profiling. In our study, biopsy results modified treatment in 23.5% of patients, including avoidance of unnecessary surgery in 7.8%. This underscores the role of CNB in personalized decision-making, allowing clinicians to distinguish between tumors requiring radical extirpation, those amenable to nephron-sparing approaches, and benign entities best managed conservatively. Moreover, the ability to obtain tissue suitable for molecular analysis is increasingly relevant for selecting targeted therapies or immunotherapies in advanced RCC and UTUC.

With a median follow-up of five years, our cohort demonstrated favorable oncologic safety. No biopsy-tract seeding, delayed procedure-related complications, or unexpected patterns of progression were observed. Although historical concerns exist regarding the risk of tumor seeding in UTUC [34], contemporary evidence indicates this event is exceedingly rare, with only isolated case reports despite decades of percutaneous interventions involving the collecting system [35]. Unlike transurethral manipulation, percutaneous renal sinus biopsy typically samples the extraluminal tumor component without breaching the urothelial lumen, thereby minimizing theoretical dissemination risk. Furthermore, tumor upstaging is primarily driven by biological aggressiveness rather than procedural manipulation, and no data from our cohort or prior studies suggest that percutaneous biopsy accelerates UTUC progression. These findings reinforce that, when performed by experienced operators and integrated into multidisciplinary decision-making, renal sinus CNB is oncologically safe.

The safety profile observed in this study aligns with existing data for renal biopsies. Complication rates were low (7.8%), all minor (Clavien–Dindo I–II), and all resolved with conservative management. No major bleeding, vascular injury, or need for intervention occurred. Real-time ultrasound guidance allows precise needle trajectory adjustment to avoid vessels, which likely contributed to the absence of major hemorrhagic events even in tumors with high RENAL complexity scores. Immediate tract ablation using a coaxial system was routinely performed at our center and may have contributed to the absence of tract seeding, although evidence supporting its universal use remains limited.

This study has several limitations. The retrospective, single-center design introduces potential selection bias and limits external generalizability, especially because all procedures were performed by two expert radiologists. Follow-up was incomplete in a small subset of patients and relatively short for evaluating very late-onset seeding or recurrence. Only one UTUC patient underwent ureteroscopic biopsy, preventing direct comparison with guideline-endorsed gold-standard sampling. Although most malignant cases were surgically confirmed, the absence of surgical pathology in the remaining patients may have resulted in overestimation of diagnostic accuracy. Additionally, the lack of comparison with CT- or MRI-guided biopsy limits conclusions regarding relative modality performance.

Future research should focus on multicenter prospective studies that evaluate reproducibility across varying operator experience levels. Emerging technologies—including radiomics-based lesion characterization, AI-assisted needle trajectory planning, and multimodal image-fusion platforms—may further improve target selection, reduce sampling error, and enhance individualized risk stratification. Integrating these tools with CEUS-guided biopsy represents a promising avenue toward precision-guided renal tumor diagnosis and intervention.

Conclusion

Ultrasound-guided percutaneous CNB of renal sinus masses is a safe and effective method that provides essential diagnostic information and supports clinical decision-making. Despite anatomical challenges, its real-time imaging and lack of radiation highlight its value in renal tumor management. With ongoing technological advances, this technique may further optimize patient care pathways.
