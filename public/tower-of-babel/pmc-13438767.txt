Keratotic macules and plaques with mild erythema in deficiency of interleukin 36 receptor antagonist cases with monoallelic pathogenic variants of IL36RN
Authors: Michiya Omi, Takuya Takeichi, Kana Tanahashi, Hidesada Adachi, Takeshi Kambara, Yoshinao Muro, Masashi Akiyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTClassic deficiency of interleukin-36 (IL-36) receptor antagonist (DITRA) is characterized by severe, recurrent generalized pustular psoriasis (GPP) with systemic inflammation and is typically caused by biallelic IL36RN variants. However, monoallelic c.115+6T>C and c.28C>T variants of IL36RN have also been reported to underlie DITRA. To the best of our knowledge, no cases of a heterozygous IL36RN c.28C>T variant presenting with the unilateral localization of symptoms have been reported. Our present case emphasize that IL36RN-related disease should be considered even with the atypical skin symptoms, thereby broadening the spectrum of DITRA.

INTRODUCTION

Deficiency of interleukin-36 (IL-36) receptor antagonist (DITRA) confers a predisposition to inflammatory skin diseases, and the most common phenotype of DITRA is generalized pustular psoriasis (GPP) without preceding psoriasis vulgaris.1,2 We report two cases of DITRA with monoallelic null variants in IL36RN and showing atypical manifestations.

CASE REPORT

Case 1 (a proband) is a 2-year-old boy (Figure 1A, II-1). He had had a keratotic macule on the right groin from three months of age (Figure 1B). The number of lesions gradually increased, and keratotic macules and plaques were observed on the right upper and lower extremities (Figures 1C, D). No clinical information was available regarding whether the skin lesions were in a more inflammatory erythematous phase initially, or whether they were keratotic from the beginning. The lesions failed to respond to topical vitamin D3. A skin biopsy from the keratotic macule on the right thigh revealed acanthosis with hyperkeratosis and hypergranulosis (Figure 1E). Neither neutrophilic infiltrates, nor microabscess formation, nor spongiosis was observed in the epidermis or dermis.

Case 2 (a proband) is an 18-year-old male (Figure 1F, ii-1). From around the age of eight, keratotic macules with mild erythema appeared on the trunk and limbs without subjective symptoms (Figure 1G). The patient has been treated with topical steroids, but the symptoms have not improved. A skin biopsy from a keratotic macule revealed mild hyperkeratosis without parakeratosis and with perivascular inflammatory cell infiltrate, predominantly of lymphocytes and histiocytes, in the dermis (Figure 1H). Similar to Case 1, neither neutrophilic infiltrates, nor microabscess formation, nor spongiosis was present.

This study was conducted in accordance with Declaration of Helsinki after ethical approval by Nagoya University Graduate School of Medicine Institutional Review Board, and informed consent was obtained from both families. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) identified the heterozygous nonsense variant c.28C>T (p.Arg10*) in exon 2 of IL36RN in Case 1, which was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. This variant was detected in DNA from Case 1’s peripheral blood, lesional epidermis, and lesional dermis, as well as in his father’s peripheral blood (Figure 1A). Quantitative analysis of the next-generation sequencing data revealed consistent variant allele frequencies (VAFs) based on read depth (DP) and alternative allele depth (AD): peripheral blood (DP, 43; AD, 25; VAF, 0.58), lesional epidermis (DP, 43; AD, 20; VAF, 0.47), and lesional dermis (DP, 73; AD, 32; VAF, 0.44). These findings confirmed that the mutation was a germline heterozygous one, rather than postzygotic mosaicism. A heterozygous c.115+6T>C variant in IL36RN was detected from the peripheral blood of Case 2 and his father (Figure 1F). Previous studies established that the c.115+6T>C variant in IL36RN causes the skipping of exon 3, resulting in a frameshift and a premature stop codon (p.Arg10Argfs*1), leading to nonsense-mediated mRNA decay.2 We found no potentially pathogenic variants in any other genes implicated in cutaneous inflammatory diseases. Immunohistochemical staining for IL-36 receptor antagonist (IL-36Ra) and IL-36γ proteins on biopsy specimens from the two cases found no discernible differences in staining compared to healthy skin from age-matched healthy individuals (data not shown).

DISCUSSION

Classic DITRA is characterized by severe, recurrent GPP with systemic inflammation and is typically caused by biallelic IL36RN variants.2 However, monoallelic c.115+6T>C3,4 and c.28C>T2,5 variants of IL36RN have also been reported to underlie DITRA. Regarding intrafamilial phenotypic heterogeneity where the fathers of Case 1 and Case 2 remained asymptomatic, it is well-documented in DITRA that clinical penetrance varies.3

When a pathogenic variant is identified in only one allele, an undetected variant in a non-coding region or a structural abnormality may be present in the identical gene, or other pathogenic genes and/or environmental factors may contribute multifactorially to disease pathogenesis. The manifestations of Case 1 and Case 2 were distinct from typical GPP. We hypothesize that the phenotypic difference between our cases and typical GPP lies in the intensity of pathway activation. While the complete loss of IL-36Ra function leads to uncontrolled IL-36 overactivation and massive neutrophil recruitment, heterozygous null variants may result in only attenuated activation of the IL-36–chemokine–neutrophil axis. This partial activation might be insufficient to induce the robust production of neutrophil-recruiting chemokines (eg, IL-8, C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 [CXCL1], CXCL2).6,7 Interestingly, upregulation of the IL-36 pathway has been reported in the lesional skin of patients with SPINK5-syndromic epidermal differentiation disorder (SPINK5-sEDD; formerly known as Netherton syndrome), which typically presents as scaling and erythroderma rather than as pustules.8 The clinical manifestations of our cases (erythema and scaling without pustules) partially share phenotypic features with SPINK5-sEDD, suggesting that a specific mode of IL-36 pathway dysregulation can lead to inflammatory keratinization disorders manifesting as scaling.

To the best of our knowledge, no cases of a heterozygous IL36RN c.28C>T variant presenting with the unilateral localization of symptoms have been reported. The reason for the unilateral distribution could not be elucidated by our present analyses. Regarding the distribution of the skin lesions in Case 1, the continuous extension of the lesion from the distal medial right thigh to the right patella, as well as the satellite-like spread observed on the extensor aspect of the right thigh, do not strictly conform to typical Blaschkolinear patterns. Although we referred to the established classification of cutaneous mosaicism patterns,9 the distribution in Case 1 could not be classified into a specific category. Long-term follow-up is warranted, as further expansion of the lesions might reveal a clearer pattern in the future.

Given the inherent limitations of WES, pathogenic intronic variants or epigenetic alterations in these two cases cannot be ruled out.

CONCLUSION

These cases emphasize that IL36RN-related disease should be considered even with the atypical skin symptoms, thereby broadening the spectrum of DITRA. Patients who initially present with phenotypes other than psoriasis vulgaris may be unrecognized carriers of IL36RN pathogenic variants, and careful follow-up is warranted because they may later develop GPP.
