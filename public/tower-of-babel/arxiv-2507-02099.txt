A Hybrid DEC-SIE Framework for Potential-Based Electromagnetic Analysis of Heterogeneous Media
Amgad Abdrabou, Luis J. Gomez, Weng Cho Chew

Abstract
Analyzing electromagnetic fields in complex, multi-material environments presents substantial computational challenges. To address these, we propose a hybrid numerical method that couples discrete exterior calculus (DEC) with surface integral equations (SIE) in the potential-based formulation of Maxwell’s equations. The method employs the magnetic vector and electric scalar potentials ( 𝐀 \mathbf{A} – Φ \Phi ) under the Lorenz gauge, offering natural compatibility with multi-physics couplings and inherent immunity to low-frequency breakdown. To effectively handle both bounded and unbounded regions, we divide the computational domain: the inhomogeneous interior is discretized using DEC, a coordinate-free framework that preserves topological invariants and enables structure-preserving discretization on unstructured meshes, while the homogeneous exterior is treated using SIEs, which inherently satisfy the radiation condition and eliminate the need for artificial domain truncation. A key contribution of this work is a scalar reformulation of the SIEs, which reduces the number of surface integral operators from fourteen to two by expressing the problem in terms of the Cartesian components of the vector potential and their normal derivatives. This simplification motivates a corresponding adaptation in the DEC domain: each vector potential component is represented as a discrete 0-form, in contrast to the conventional 1-form representation. This novel treatment improves compatibility at the interface and significantly enhances numerical performance. The proposed hybrid method thus offers a unified, efficient, and physically consistent framework for solving electromagnetic scattering and radiation problems in complex geometries and heterogeneous materials.



Keywords:

Maxwell’s equations, Potential-based formulation , Discrete exterior calculus , Surface integral equations , Radiation , Scattering

Introduction

The analysis of electromagnetic phenomena across heterogeneous and unbounded domains presents a significant computational challenge, with solution methodologies based on differential and integral equations each facing limitations. Differential equation (DE) methods are highly effective for analyzing heterogeneous materials, as they solve Maxwell’s equations spatially. This mechanism, however, requires a truncating boundary with prescribed field information, making it challenging to apply to open regions.

To overcome this, the application of DE methods can be extended to unbounded domains through approximate techniques like Absorbing Boundary Conditions (ABCs)
[
1
]
or Perfectly Matched Layers (PMLs)
[
2
]
. Furthermore, DE methods can be rigorously coupled with integral equation methods—which are naturally suited for unbounded problems—to create a hybrid Finite Element-Boundary Integral (FE-BI) solver that precisely truncates the computational domain
[
3
]
. A separate, critical issue is that most conventional DE solvers suffer from low-frequency breakdown
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
. This numerical instability arises as the frequency approaches zero, causing the underlying matrix equations to become ill-conditioned and yielding inaccurate results for static or low-frequency applications.

Addressing the low-frequency breakdown in differential equation solvers has been a significant area of research in computational electromagnetics
[
7
,
8
]
. Many proposed solutions involve modifying standard finite element methods, for instance, by employing divergence-conforming basis functions such as loop-tree or loop-star decompositions
[
9
,
10
]
, or by adding stabilizing terms that enforce the charge-current continuity equation
[
11
,
12
]
. However, studies have demonstrated that a more fundamental solution lies in the choice of formulation itself
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
. By representing the unknowns as the magnetic vector potential (
\mathbf{A}
) and the electric scalar potential (
\Phi
), the formulation becomes inherently immune to the low-frequency breakdown phenomenon
[
6
,
16
]
. This approach leverages the natural geometric structure of Maxwell’s equations and provides superior numerical stability across the entire frequency spectrum. Furthermore, this formulation offers significant theoretical advantages for multi-physics coupling and certain classes of problems. For example, potential equations are used in the Hamiltonian formulation that describes the interaction of charged particles with electromagnetic fields (e.g., the Aharonov-Bohm effect)
[
17
]
. Thus far, the DEC framework has only been tested using approximate truncating conditions
[
16
]
.

The primary challenge in implementing surface integral equation (SIE) truncation with potential-based formulation for DEC lies in the computational complexity arising from numerous integral operators. Specifically, the existing dominant approach for applying surface potential integral equations necessitates the evaluation of 14 distinct integral operators, each characterized by singular kernels
[
18
,
19
]
. Furthermore, the nature and degree of singularity varies significantly across these operators, creating a heterogeneous computational framework that demands specialized numerical treatment for each kernel type. This substantial computational overhead, combined with the intricate singularity handling requirements constitutes a formidable barrier to the practical implementation of
\mathbf{A}
-
\Phi
formulations in hybrid computational electromagnetics methods.

For
\Omega_{1}
denoting the exterior domain, our treatment depends on the formulation:

\mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol{r})={\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{(inc)}}(\boldsymbol{r})-\mathcal{S}[(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}^{\prime}\cdot\nabla^{\prime})\mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})]+\mathcal{D}[\mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})],\quad\boldsymbol{r}\in\Omega_{1}

(1)

where

\mathcal{S}[u](\boldsymbol{r})=\int\limits_{\Gamma}\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})u(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})d\Gamma(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),

This is equivalent to the dominantly used formulation of the vector potential integral equation, given by
[
4

\mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol{r})={\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{(inc)}}(\boldsymbol{r})+\mathcal{S}[\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})\times\nabla^{\prime}\times\mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})]+\nabla\times\mathcal{S}[\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})\times\mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})]-\nabla\mathcal{S}[\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})]-\mathcal{S}[\nabla^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})],

(2)

where the formulation given by Eq. (
1
) dramatically simplifies the complexity of the formulation (
2
) by directly representing the components
A_{\tau},\,\tau=\left\{x,y,z\right\}
and their normal derivatives on the surface as

A_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{r})={A}^{\mathrm{(inc)}}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{r})-\mathcal{S}\left[\frac{\partial A_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}\right]+\mathcal{D}[A_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})],\quad\boldsymbol{r}\in\Omega_{1}

(3)

The integral equation of each vector potential component
A_{\tau}
is similar to the integral equation for
\Phi

\Phi(\boldsymbol{r})={\Phi}^{\mathrm{(inc)}}(\boldsymbol{r})-\mathcal{S}\left[\frac{\Phi(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}\right]+\mathcal{D}\left[\Phi(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})\right],\quad\boldsymbol{r}\in\Omega_{1}

(4)

which significantly reduces the required integral operators to just two—precisely the same operators used for
\Phi
.

With the simplification of the SIE formulation in the exterior homogeneous domain, special care should be given to the approach used to discretize the interior inhomogeneous domain. Decades of research have been devoted to the finite element method (FEM) and in particular for computational electromagnetics
[
7
]
. The process involves a discretization of the weak form of Maxwell’s equations through the Galerkin method where appropriate basis and test functions are chosen.

In this paper, we choose discrete exterior calculus as the framework used to analyze the fields in the interior domain. DEC offers a compelling alternative to finite element methods (FEM) that provides discrete versions of fundamental geometric and topological structures of the problem domain
[
20
,
21
]
, such as manifolds and differential forms
[
22
,
23
]
, directly on the mesh ensuring that critical physical invariants and conservation laws are maintained at the discrete level, enhancing both numerical stability and physical fidelity. This makes DEC suitable for formulating Maxwell’s equations in a coordinate-free manner
[
24
,
16
]
. Moreover, DEC naturally supports unstructured and irregular meshes without requiring higher-order continuity, and its formulation leads to sparse, well-conditioned matrices that often result in improved computational efficiency for large-scale problems, especially when topological consistency is critical. While conventional hybrid approaches that couple finite element formulations for the interior with surface integral equations (SIE) for the exterior—such as in
[
25
]
—typically enforce boundary conditions by matching tangential field components (e.g.,
\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{E}
) are effective, these methods often inherit the challenges associated with choice of suitable vector basis functions and representing vector fields across interfaces. In potential-based formulations, the complexity is further compounded by the need to construct and manage a large number of specialized operators
[
18
,
19
]
. To maintain mathematical consistency across the interface, we implement an unconventional treatment of the vector potential within the DEC framework. We first derive continuous equations for each component of
\mathbf{A}
in the Cartesian 3D space with the Lorenz gauge imposed. As such, each field component will be represented in the DEC domain as a 0-form. Conventionally, the magnetic vector potential
\mathbf{A}
is represented as a discrete 1-form within the DEC framework
[
16
]
. In contrast, this paper advocates for representing
\mathbf{A}
in terms of 3 discrete 0-forms in the DEC domain since it provides a significantly enhanced compatibility with the SIE representation in the exterior domain. Our numerical experiments confirm that this approach yields substantially better results than conventional 1-form representations of
\mathbf{A}
. This work integrates three core concepts:

1.

Potential-based formulation
: Rather than working directly with the electric and magnetic fields
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{H}
, we adopt a four-potential formulation in Lorenz gauge, where the electric and magnetic fields are expressed in terms of the three scalar component of the vector potential and an electric scalar potential
\Phi
. This choice yields equations that naturally account for sources and material inhomogeneities, while also aligning with modern gauge-theoretic interpretations of electromagnetism. This approach greatly simplifies the challenges at the level of boundary conditions and numerical implementation.

2.

Hybrid DEC-SIE formulation
: We consider domains consisting of a bounded, inhomogeneous interior region surrounded by a homogeneous unbounded region. The exterior region is treated using surface integral equations, which capture the correct radiation behavior at infinity without the need for artificial truncation. The interior region is discretized using discrete exterior calculus, a geometric discretization framework that preserves topological invariants and supports natural treatment of differential forms on unstructured meshes. This hybridization allows the use of exact Green’s function representations outside and structured, local operators inside.

3.

Simplification of the SIE and DEC equations
. The conventional application of the
\mathbf{A}
–
\Phi
formulation within SIE leads to a proliferation of surface operators, due to the variety of tangential and normal components that must be matched across the interface. For the vector potential alone, this includes terms such as
\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{A}
,
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A}
, and
\mathbf{n}\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{A})
, which in total contribute to up to 14 integral operators. Recasting the SIE problem in terms of the scalar components of the vector potential, namely
A_{x},A_{y},A_{z}
, and their normal derivatives at nodes on the interface reduces the required operators to only two. As a result, the SIE becomes not only simpler but also more symmetric and computationally efficient. This scalar representation on the interface naturally suggests a reformulation of the DEC discretization where we treat each Cartesian component of
\mathbf{A}
as a 0-form in the DEC domain.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the theoretical framework, beginning with Maxwell’s equations in the
\mathbf{A}
-
\Phi
formulation, and introducing both the surface integral equations for the homogeneous exterior domain and a continuous exterior calculus representation for the inhomogeneous interior. These continuous formulations set the foundation for the discrete treatment. Section 3 introduces the discrete exterior calculus (DEC) framework, detailing the discretization of the simulation domain, the definition of discrete differential forms, and the associated operators required to express the governing equations in matrix form. In Section 4, the method of moments is employed to discretize the surface integral equations using a boundary mesh approximation. Section 5 develops the hybrid DEC–SIE formulation by incorporating the discretized surface integral equations into the DEC framework to fully represent the coupled system. Section 6 validates the proposed method through a suite of numerical tests, including comparisons with analytical solutions, robustness and stability assessments, and convergence studies on both homogeneous and inhomogeneous geometries. Finally, Section 7 concludes the paper and outlines potential directions for future work.

Exterior calculus provides an elegant framework for expressing Maxwell’s equations through differential
k
-forms, which are mathematical objects naturally suited for integration over

[

23
,
30

31
]

\boldsymbol{E}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}

\displaystyle:=E_{x}\mathbf{d}x+E_{y}\mathbf{d}y+E_{z}\mathbf{d}z,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}

\displaystyle:=B_{x}\mathbf{d}y\wedge\mathbf{d}z+B_{y}\mathbf{d}z\wedge\mathbf{d}x+B_{z}\mathbf{d}x\wedge\mathbf{d}y,

, has been used. For the constitutive relations

\mathbf{D}=\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{r})\mathbf{E},\quad\mathbf{B}=\mu_{0}\mu(\boldsymbol{r})\mathbf{H},

the identities
\boldsymbol{D}=\star\mathbf{D}^{\flat}
and
\boldsymbol{B}=\star\mathbf{B}^{\flat}
lead to

\boldsymbol{D}=\varepsilon_{0}\star_{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{E},\quad\boldsymbol{B}=\mu_{0}\star_{\mu}\boldsymbol{H}.

Here
\star_{\varepsilon}
and
\star_{\mu}
emphasize the dependence of the Hodge star operators on the underlying metric. Their discrete constructions are detailed in Section
3.4
.

This differential forms representation naturally aligns with the fundamental laws of electromagnetism. For instance, Faraday’s law states that
\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}+\partial_{t}\int_{S}\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{S}=0
, relating the circulation of the electric field along a closed loop
\partial S
to the time rate of change of the magnetic flux density through surface
S
. This physical relationship is elegantly captured by representing
\boldsymbol{E}
as a 1-form and
\boldsymbol{B}
as a 2-form. For readers unfamiliar with exterior calculus, references
[
16
,
33
]
provide a concise introduction.

In this exterior calculus (EC) notation, the system of equations (
13
) takes the form

\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{EC}}&0&0&-i\star_{\partial_{x}\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{r})}\\
0&\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{EC}}&0&-i\star_{\partial_{y}\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{r})}\\
0&0&\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{EC}}&-i\star_{\partial_{z}\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{r})}\\
-ik_{0}^{2}\star_{\partial_{x}\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{r})}&-ik_{0}^{2}\star_{\partial_{y}\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{r})}&-ik_{0}^{2}\star_{\partial_{z}\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{r})}&\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EC}}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{A}_{x}\\
\tilde{A}_{y}\\
\tilde{A}_{z}\\
{\Phi}_{s}\end{bmatrix}=-\eta_{0}k_{0}\star\begin{bmatrix}J_{x}\\
J_{y}\\
J_{z}\\
\frac{\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}}{i}\end{bmatrix},

(22)

where

\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{EC}}:=\mathbf{d}\star\mathbf{d}+k_{0}^{2}\star_{\varepsilon},

(23)

and

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EC}}:=\mathbf{d}\star_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{d}+k_{0}^{2}\star_{\varepsilon^{2}}.

(24)

Notice that the 0-forms
\tilde{A}_{x},\,\tilde{A}_{y},\,\tilde{A}_{z}
, and
{\Phi}_{s}
are simply scalar fields in the exterior calculus notation.

3
The DEC framework

In this section, the discrete exterior calculus (DEC) framework is briefly introduced to solve the system (
22
) on the bounded simulation domain
\Omega_{2}=\Omega_{a}\cup\Omega_{s}
(see Fig.
1
). The notation of the simplicial complex, which provides a discrete approximation to the
\Omega_{2}
is introduced first. This is followed by the description of discrete differential forms (also called cochains, or degrees-of-freedom arrays (DoF-arrays)
[
34
]
), defined on the simplicial complex and its dual. The discrete exterior derivative and Hodge star operators are then briefly discussed. Such operators act on the discrete differential forms and make it possible to write the operators in Eqs. (
22
)-(
15
) as matrices. For more details on the definitions of simplicial and cell complexes and discrete exterior calculus operators, the readers may refer to
[
20
,
21
]
.

3.1
The simplicial complex

Let
\mathcal{K}
be the simplicial complex approximating the bounded domain
\Omega_{2}
(see Fig.
1
). The complex
\mathcal{K}
is a collection of
k
-simplices. For
k=0,\ldots,3
, a
k
-simplex
\sigma^{k}\in\mathcal{K}
is defined by the nodes forming it as
\sigma^{k}=[v_{1},\ldots,v_{k+1}]
, where the subscripts designate the nodes indices
[
20
]
. The set of 0-simplices contains the nodes
\left\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{N_{0}}\right\}
, where
N_{0}
is the number of nodes. The set of 1-simplices contains the edges
\left\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{N_{1}}\right\}
, where
e_{k}\equiv\sigma^{1}_{k}
is an edge defined by the nodes
v_{k_{1}}
and
v_{k_{2}}
forming it as
e_{k}=[v_{k_{1}},v_{k_{2}}]
, and
N_{1}
denotes the number of edges. The orientation of the edge is implied by the order of its nodes. Similarly, the set of 2-simplices contains all the triangles
\left\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{N_{2}}\right\}
, where
t_{k}\equiv\sigma^{2}_{k}
is a triangle defined as
t_{k}=[v_{k_{1}},v_{k_{2}},v_{k_{3}}]
, and
N_{2}
is the number of triangles. Finally, the 3-simplices contains the number of
N_{3}
tetrahedra
\left\{\mathcal{T}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{T}_{N_{3}}\right\}
, where
\mathcal{T}_{k}\equiv\sigma^{3}_{k}
is a tetrahedron defined as
\mathcal{T}_{k}=[v_{k_{1}},v_{k_{2}},v_{k_{3}},v_{k_{4}}]
. The highest-dimensional 3-simplices are assumed to have the same orientation, which is typically ensured by the mesh generation tool. The orientation of
k
-simplices with
k<3
is arbitrary. Here we assume the edges are oriented such that any edge
e_{k}=[v_{k_{1}},v_{k_{2}}]
is oriented such that the indices
k_{1}<k_{2}
. Similarly, we assume any triangle
t_{k}=[v_{k_{1}},v_{k_{2}},v_{k_{3}}]
is oriented such that the indices
k_{1}<k_{2}<k_{3}
.

In addition to the primal simplicial complex, one can also construct its dual, denoted by
\ast\mathcal{K}
, consisting of cells. The circumcentric or barycentric dual complexes can be used to construct discrete approximations to the Hodge star operators
[
20
,
35
]
. The choice of the dual mesh affects both the accuracy and structure of the resulting discrete Hodge star matrices. For example, choosing the circumcentric dual mesh leads to diagonal Hodge star matrices
[
20
]
. It is not always ensured that all circumcenters are located inside their tetrahedra. Hence, Hodge-optimized triangulations have been shown to solve this problem by promoting the use of power diagrams to create a weighted dual mesh
[
36
]
. Another discrete definitions of the Hodge star are based on Whitney forms
[
37
]
. They either require the barycentric dual mesh to construct a geometrical barycentric-based Hodge matrices
[
35
]
or use the Galerkin approach that does not require the dual mesh for its definition
[
34
]
. The Hodge star approximations based on Whitney forms are sparse but non-diagonal matrices. In this paper, we use the Galerkin Hodge star definitions.

3.2
Discrete differential forms

Since the simulation domain is now approximated by a simplicial complex, the smooth differential forms are also discretized. By discretization of a smooth differential
k
-form over a complex, we think of its integration over all
k
-dimensional mesh objects. In the smooth case, there is no distinction made between the 1-forms
\boldsymbol{E}
and
\boldsymbol{H}
, and the 2-forms
\boldsymbol{D}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
. However, for discrete differential forms, a distinction between the nature of these forms must be clearly made
[
13
]
. The forms
\boldsymbol{E}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
have internal orientations, so they are known as ordinary forms. The forms
\boldsymbol{H}
and
\boldsymbol{D}
have external orientations and are referred to as twisted forms. It is then convenient to discretize each kind on a distinct mesh. Ordinary forms can be associated with a primal simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
, while the twisted forms can be associated with a dual cell complex
\ast\mathcal{K}
. The resulting spaces of discrete differential
k
-forms are denoted by
\mathcal{C}^{k}(\mathcal{K})
for discrete forms defined on the primal mesh complex, and are denoted by
\mathfrak{D}^{k}(\ast\mathcal{K})
for discrete forms defined on the dual mesh complex. Discretization of the smooth differential forms on the primal and dual complexes is performed via the de Rham map. The maps that discretize the primal zero form
\Phi
, 1-form
\boldsymbol{E}
and 2-form
\boldsymbol{B}
to yield the DoF-arrays
\overline{\mathbf{\Phi}}
,
\overline{\mathbf{e}}
and
\overline{\mathbf{b}}
, respectively, are defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\Phi}:{\bigwedge}^{0}(\mathcal{M})\longrightarrow{\mathcal{C}}^{0}(\mathcal{K}),\quad\Phi\longmapsto\overline{\mathbf{\Phi}}=[\Phi(v_{1}),\ldots,\Phi(v_{N_{0}})]^{\mathsf{T}}

(25)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{E}}:{\bigwedge}^{1}(\mathcal{M})\longrightarrow{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\mathcal{K}),\quad\boldsymbol{E}\longmapsto\overline{\mathbf{e}}=\left[\int_{e_{1}}\boldsymbol{E},\ldots,\int_{e_{N_{1}}}\boldsymbol{E}\right]^{\mathsf{T}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{B}}:{\bigwedge}^{2}(\mathcal{M})\longrightarrow{\mathcal{C}}^{2}(\mathcal{K}),\quad\boldsymbol{B}\longmapsto\overline{\mathbf{b}}=\left[\int_{t_{1}}\boldsymbol{B},\ldots,\int_{t_{N_{2}}}\boldsymbol{B}\right]^{\mathsf{T}},

where the superscript
\mathsf{T}
denotes the transpose. Although our exterior calculus formulation in (
22
) involves only 0-forms, it is essential to understand how discrete differential forms of various degrees are defined. This is because higher-order forms inevitably appear in the discretization process. For example, the exterior derivative
\mathbf{d}\Phi
is a 1-form and is discretized as values on primal edges, while the Hodge dual
\star\Phi
is a 3-form, represented by scalar values associated with dual volumes, which correspond to the primal nodes.

3.3
Discrete exterior derivatives

The discrete exterior derivative operator denoted by
\fourierbb{D}_{k}
maps primal
k
-forms to primal
(k+1)
-forms. Consider the 1-form
\mathbf{d}\Phi
, which is discretized over the primal edges
e_{k}
. By applying Stokes’s theorem (
20
), the
k
th entry of the degree-of-freedom (DoF) array obtained via the de Rham map, denoted by
[\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{d}\Phi}]_{k}
, is given by

[\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{d}\Phi}]_{k}=\int_{e_{k}}\mathbf{d}\Phi\overset{Stokes}{=}\int_{\partial e_{k}}\Phi=\Phi(v_{k_{2}})-\Phi(v_{k_{1}})=\sum_{\ell}[\fourierbb{D}_{0}]_{k,\ell}\Phi_{\ell},\quad k=1,\ldots,N_{1}.

where
\Phi_{\ell}=\Phi(v_{\ell})
. Hence the discrete exterior derivative
\fourierbb{D}_{0}
is an
N_{1}\times N_{0}
matrix defined as

\left[\fourierbb{D}_{0}\right]_{k\ell}=\begin{cases}+1,&\text{if the node $\ell$ is the end node of the edge $k$},\\
-1,&\text{if the node $\ell$ is the start node of the edge $k$},\\
0,&\text{if the node $\ell$ is not a face of the edge $k$},\end{cases}

that maps the discrete 0-form
\overline{\mathbf{\Phi}}
to the discrete 1-form
\fourierbb{D}_{0}\overline{\mathbf{\Phi}}
as

\overline{\mathbf{\Phi}}\longmapsto\fourierbb{D}_{0}\overline{\mathbf{\Phi}}=\left[\int_{e_{1}}\mathbf{d}\Phi,\ldots,\int_{e_{N_{1}}}\mathbf{d}\Phi\right]^{\mathsf{T}}.

Similar definitions follow for
\fourierbb{D}_{1}
and
\fourierbb{D}_{2}
. Consider the discretization of the 2-form
\mathbf{d}\boldsymbol{E}
over the triangle
t_{k}
, we have

[\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{d}\boldsymbol{E}}]_{k}=\int_{t_{k}}\mathbf{d}\boldsymbol{E}\overset{Stokes}{=}\oint_{\partial t_{k}}\Phi=\sum_{\ell}[\fourierbb{D}_{1}]_{k,\ell}\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{\ell},\quad k=1,\ldots,N_{2}.

where
\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{\ell}
is the
l
th entry of the array
\overline{\mathbf{e}}
. The discrete exterior derivative
\fourierbb{D}_{1}
is an
N_{2}\times N_{1}
matrix defined as

\left[\fourierbb{D}_{1}\right]_{k\ell}=\begin{cases}+1,&\text{if the edge $\ell$ is a face of the triangle $k$, with consistent orientation},\\
-1,&\text{if the edge $\ell$ is a face of the triangle $k$, with opposite orientation},\\
0,&\text{if the edge $\ell$ is not a face of the triangle $k$},\end{cases}

that maps the discrete 1-form
\overline{\mathbf{e}}
to the discrete 2-form
\fourierbb{D}_{1}\overline{\mathbf{e}}
as

\overline{\mathbf{e}}\longmapsto\fourierbb{D}_{1}\overline{\mathbf{e}}=\left[\int_{t_{1}}\mathbf{d}\boldsymbol{E},\ldots,\int_{t_{N_{2}}}\mathbf{d}\boldsymbol{E}\right]^{\mathsf{T}}.

Finally, the discrete exterior derivative
\fourierbb{D}_{2}
is an
N_{3}\times N_{2}
matrix defined as

\left[\fourierbb{D}_{2}\right]_{k\ell}=\begin{cases}+1,&\text{if the triangle $\ell$ is a face of the tetrahedron $k$, with consistent orientation},\\
-1,&\text{if the triangle $\ell$ is a face of the tetrahedron $k$, with opposite orientation},\\
0,&\text{if the triangle $\ell$ is not a face of the tetrahedron $k$},\end{cases}

that maps the discrete 2-form
\overline{\mathbf{b}}
to the discrete 3-form
\fourierbb{D}_{2}\overline{\mathbf{b}}
as

\overline{\mathbf{b}}\longmapsto\fourierbb{D}_{2}\overline{\mathbf{b}}=\left[\int_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}\mathbf{d}\boldsymbol{B},\ldots,\int_{\mathcal{T}_{N_{3}}}\mathbf{d}\boldsymbol{B}\right]^{\mathsf{T}}.

The orientation of the primal mesh
k
-simplices induces their dual orientation. The discrete exterior derivative operator
\fourierbb{D}_{k}^{\ast}
maps dual
k
-forms to dual (
k+1
)-forms and are given by
[
21
]

\fourierbb{D}_{k}^{\ast}=(-1)^{(n-k)}\,\fourierbb{D}_{(n-k-1)}^{\mathsf{T}}.

(26)

For
n=3
, using Eq. (
26
) gives

\fourierbb{D}_{0}^{\ast}=-\fourierbb{D}_{2}^{\mathsf{T}},\quad\fourierbb{D}_{1}^{\ast}=\fourierbb{D}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}},\quad\fourierbb{D}_{2}^{\ast}=-\fourierbb{D}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}.

3.4
The Galerkin Hodge star

The discrete Hodge star operators we use are the Galerkin Hodge star operators denoted by
\fourierbb{H}_{k}
. The operator
\fourierbb{H}_{k}
maps discrete differential
k
-forms to
(n-k)
-forms. For the constitutive relations
\boldsymbol{D}=\star_{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{E}
and
\boldsymbol{H}=\star_{\mu^{-1}}\boldsymbol{B}
, we designate the material parameters dependence as

\overline{\mathbf{d}}=\fourierbb{H}_{1}(\varepsilon)\overline{\mathbf{e}},\quad\overline{\mathbf{h}}=\fourierbb{H}_{2}(\mu^{-1})\overline{\mathbf{b}},

where
\fourierbb{H}_{k}(\cdot)
is an
N_{k}\times N_{k}
matrix,
k=0,\ldots,3
. The construction of the matrices
\fourierbb{H}_{k}
involves the use of Whitney forms
\mathcal{W}^{(k)}
or their proxy fields obtained via the sharp
\sharp
operator that maps 1-forms to vector fields. For details on Whitney forms and their proxy fields, the reader may refer to
[
37
,
38
]
.

Given a tetrahedron
\mathcal{T}
with nodes denoted locally as
[v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4}]
, a Whitney 0-form
\mathcal{W}^{(0)}_{\ell}
is associated with each node
v_{\ell},\,\ell=1,\ldots,4
, and is given by

\mathcal{W}^{(0)}_{v_{\ell}}=\lambda_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{r}),

where
\lambda(\boldsymbol{r})
is the barycentric coordinates for the tetrahedron
\mathcal{T}
. Since 0-forms are scalars, there is no distinction between the Whitney 0-forms as their proxy fields. The Whitney 1-forms are associated with edges. For a tetrahedron
\mathcal{T}
, we have the locally-indexed edges
e_{\ell},\,\ell=1,\ldots,6
, where the edge
e_{\ell}
is defined by the nodes forming it as
e_{l}=[v_{l_{1}},v_{l_{2}}]
. The Whitney 1-form associated with the edge
e_{\ell}
is

\mathcal{W}^{(1)}_{e_{\ell}}=\lambda_{l_{1}}(\boldsymbol{r})\mathbf{d}\lambda_{l_{2}}(\boldsymbol{r})-\lambda_{l_{2}}(\boldsymbol{r})\mathbf{d}\lambda_{l_{1}}(\boldsymbol{r}),

while its proxy field
\mathbf{W}^{(1)}_{e_{\ell}}
is

\mathbf{W}^{(1)}_{e_{\ell}}=\lambda_{l_{1}}(\boldsymbol{r})\nabla\lambda_{l_{2}}(\boldsymbol{r})-\lambda_{l_{2}}(\boldsymbol{r})\nabla\lambda_{l_{1}}(\boldsymbol{r}).

(27)

The Whitney 2-forms are associated with the triangles of
\mathcal{T}
. The triangle of
\mathcal{T}
are
t_{\ell},\,\ell=1,\ldots,4
. As before, the triangle
t_{\ell}
is defined by the nodes forming it as
t_{l}=[v_{l_{1}},v_{l_{2}},v_{l_{3}}]
and the Whitney 2-form associated with this triangle is

\mathcal{W}^{(2)}_{t_{\ell}}=2\left[\lambda_{l_{1}}(\boldsymbol{r})\mathbf{d}\lambda_{l_{2}}(\boldsymbol{r})\wedge\mathbf{d}\lambda_{l_{3}}(\boldsymbol{r})+\lambda_{l_{2}}(\boldsymbol{r})\mathbf{d}\lambda_{l_{3}}(\boldsymbol{r})\wedge\mathbf{d}\lambda_{l_{1}}(\boldsymbol{r})+\lambda_{l_{3}}(\boldsymbol{r})\mathbf{d}\lambda_{l_{1}}(\boldsymbol{r})\wedge\mathbf{d}\lambda_{l_{2}}(\boldsymbol{r})\right].

The proxy field
\mathbf{W}^{(2)}_{t_{\ell}}
of the 2-form
\mathcal{W}^{(2)}_{t_{\ell}}
can be written as
(\star\mathcal{W}^{(2)})^{\sharp}
, and is given by
[
38
]

\mathbf{W}^{(2)}_{t_{\ell}}=2\left[\lambda_{l_{1}}(\boldsymbol{r})\nabla\lambda_{l_{2}}(\boldsymbol{r})\times\nabla\lambda_{l_{3}}(\boldsymbol{r})+\lambda_{l_{2}}(\boldsymbol{r})\nabla\lambda_{l_{3}}(\boldsymbol{r})\times\nabla\lambda_{l_{1}}(\boldsymbol{r})+\lambda_{l_{3}}(\boldsymbol{r})\nabla\lambda_{l_{1}}(\boldsymbol{r})\times\nabla\lambda_{l_{2}}(\boldsymbol{r})\right].

Given any bounded function
\xi(\boldsymbol{r})
that defines material properties, e.g.
\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{r})
, the Galerkin Hodge star matrices
\fourierbb{H}_{k}(\xi),\,k=1,2
, are given by
[
34
]

\left[\fourierbb{H}_{1}(\xi)\right]_{i,j}=\int_{\Omega}\xi\,\mathbf{W}^{(1)}_{e_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{W}^{(1)}_{e_{j}}d\Omega,\quad 1\leq i,j\leq N_{1}

(28)

and

\left[\fourierbb{H}_{2}(\xi)\right]_{i,j}=\int_{\Omega}\xi\,\mathbf{W}^{(2)}_{t_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{W}^{(2)}_{t_{j}}d\Omega,\quad 1\leq i,j\leq N_{2}

(29)

For
k=0,3
, a diagonal representation of the operators
\fourierbb{H}_{0}
and
\fourierbb{H}_{3}
is used
[
39
]
, and they are given by

\left[\fourierbb{H}_{0}(\xi)\right]_{i,i}=\left|\ast\sigma^{0}_{i,\xi}\right|,\quad 1\leq i\leq N_{0}

\quad\left[\fourierbb{H}_{3}(\xi)\right]_{i,i}=\frac{\xi_{i}}{\left|\sigma^{3}_{i}\right|},\quad 1\leq i\leq N_{3}

where
\xi_{i}=\xi(\boldsymbol{r}_{i})
and
r_{i}
is the centroid of tetrahedron
\mathcal{T}_{i}
. The diagonal entry
\left|\ast\sigma^{0}_{i,\xi}\right|
is the effective dual volume to the node
v_{i}
accounting for the variations of the function
\xi
over the tetrahedra, and is given by

\left|\ast\sigma^{0}_{i,\xi}\right|=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{l}\xi_{l}\left|\sigma^{3}_{i,l}\right|,

where the sum runs over index
l
of all tetrahedra sharing the node
v_{i}
, and the factor
1/4
accounts for the portion of the tetrahedron volume
\left|\sigma^{3}_{i,l}\right|
added to the effective dual volume. The following diagram shows how the discrete exterior derivative and Hodge star operators relate the spaces of discrete differential forms for the 3-D case:

4
Discrete potential SIEs: method of moments

In this section, we briefly present the method of moments (MoM)
[
40
]
to discretize the SIE (
18
). As mentioned in Section 3, the interior domain
\Omega_{2}
is approximated by a simplicial complex, and the boundary of this simplicial complex—a surface triangulation—approximates
\Gamma
. The boundary mesh consists of
N_{2}^{\partial}
boundary triangles,
N_{1}^{\partial}
boundary edges, and
N_{0}^{\partial}
boundary nodes. The position vectors of the boundary nodes are denoted by
\boldsymbol{r}_{n}
,
n=1,\ldots,N_{0}^{\partial}
. The scalar fields
\alpha
and
\beta
in Eq. (
18
) are approximated by a finite expansion in nodal basis functions
h_{n}(\boldsymbol{r})
as

\alpha(\boldsymbol{r})\approx\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N_{0}^{\partial}}\alpha_{n}h_{n}(\boldsymbol{r}),\quad\beta(\boldsymbol{r})\approx\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N_{0}^{\partial}}\beta_{n}h_{n}(\boldsymbol{r}),

(31)

where
\alpha_{n}:=\alpha(\boldsymbol{r}_{n})
and
\beta_{n}:=\beta(\boldsymbol{r}_{n})
. The basis functions
\left\{h_{n}(\boldsymbol{r})\right\}_{n=1}^{N_{0}^{\partial}}
are linear functions defined over each boundary triangle using barycentric coordinates, and satisfy
h_{m}(\boldsymbol{r}_{n})=\delta_{mn}
.

For each boundary triangle
T_{m},m=1,\ldots,N_{2}^{\partial}
, with vertex position vectors
\boldsymbol{r}_{1}^{(m)},\boldsymbol{r}_{2}^{(m)}
, and
\boldsymbol{r}_{3}^{(m)}
, a point
\boldsymbol{r}\in T_{m}
is associated with barycentric coordinates
\lambda_{1}^{(m)}(\boldsymbol{r})
,
\lambda_{2}^{(m)}(\boldsymbol{r})
, and
\lambda_{3}^{(m)}(\boldsymbol{r})
. Defining

\boldsymbol{v}:=\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}_{3}^{(m)},\quad\boldsymbol{v}_{1}:=\boldsymbol{r}_{1}^{(m)}-\boldsymbol{r}_{3}^{(m)},\quad\boldsymbol{v}_{2}:=\boldsymbol{r}_{2}^{(m)}-\boldsymbol{r}_{3}^{(m)},

the barycentric functions
\lambda_{1}^{(m)}
,
\lambda_{2}^{(m)}
, and
\lambda_{3}^{(m)}
are given by

\lambda_{1}^{(m)}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\right)\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\right\rVert^{2}-\left(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\right)}{4S_{m}^{2}},\quad\boldsymbol{r}\in T_{m},

(32)

\lambda_{2}^{(m)}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\right)\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\right\rVert^{2}-\left(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\right)}{4S_{m}^{2}},\quad\boldsymbol{r}\in T_{m},

(33)

and

\lambda_{3}^{(m)}(\boldsymbol{r})=1-\lambda_{1}^{(m)}(\boldsymbol{r})-\lambda_{2}^{(m)}(\boldsymbol{r}).

(34)

Here,
S_{m}
is the area of
T_{m}
, and we have used the identity

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\times\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\right\rVert^{2}-\left(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\right)^{2}=4S_{m}^{2},

(35)

which relates the triangle area to the edge vectors
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{2}
.

With
h_{m}(\boldsymbol{r})
defined, we first substitute Eqs. (
31
) into Eq. (
18
) to obtain

\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N_{0}^{\partial}}\alpha_{n}h_{n}(\boldsymbol{r})-\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N_{0}^{\partial}}\alpha_{n}\left[\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}h_{n}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})d\Gamma(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})\right]+\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N_{0}^{\partial}}\beta_{n}\left[\int_{\Gamma}\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})h_{n}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})d\Gamma(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})\right]={\alpha}^{\mathrm{(inc)}}(\boldsymbol{r}).

(36)

Second, Eq. (
36
) is tested against
h_{m}(\boldsymbol{r}),\,m=1,\ldots,{N_{0}^{\partial}}
, to obtain the linear system

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}\,{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\boldsymbol{f}_{\alpha}.

(37)

Here,
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
are
N_{0}^{\partial}
-dimensional column vectors, with their respective
n
th components given by
\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{n}
. The entries of the
N_{0}^{\partial}\times N_{0}^{\partial}
matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}
, denoted by
[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}]_{m,n}
and
[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}]_{m,n}
, respectively, are given by

[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}]_{m,n}=\frac{1}{2}\int\limits_{\Gamma}h_{m}(\boldsymbol{r})h_{n}(\boldsymbol{r})d\Gamma(\boldsymbol{r})-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\limits_{\Gamma}\,\int\limits_{\Gamma}{h_{m}(\boldsymbol{r})h_{n}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}^{\prime}\cdot\nabla^{\prime}\frac{e^{ik_{0}\left|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right|}}{\left|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right|}d\Gamma(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})d\Gamma(\boldsymbol{r}),

(38)

and

[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}]_{m,n}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\limits_{\Gamma}\,\int\limits_{\Gamma}{h_{m}(\boldsymbol{r})h_{n}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}\frac{e^{ik_{0}\left|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right|}}{\left|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}\right|}d\Gamma(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})d\Gamma(\boldsymbol{r}).

(39)

The
m
th entry of
\boldsymbol{f}_{\alpha}
, denoted by
\left[\boldsymbol{f}_{\alpha}\right]_{m}
is given by

\left[\boldsymbol{f}_{\alpha}\right]_{m}=\int\limits_{\Gamma}{h_{m}(\boldsymbol{r}){\alpha}^{\mathrm{(inc)}}(\boldsymbol{r})}d\Gamma(\boldsymbol{r}).

(40)

For more details on the evaluation of the matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}
, the reader may refer to
[
41
]
and the references therein.

5
The hybrid DEC-SIE formulation

In this section, we present the hybrid approach in which the surface integral equation (
37
) is incorporated into the DEC framework. To obtain the discrete representation of the governing system (
22
) within the DEC formulation, we proceed as follows. The continuous 0-forms
\tilde{A}_{\tau}
,
\tau\in\{x,y,z\}
, and
\Phi_{s}
are approximated by discrete 0-forms
\overline{\mathbf{a}}_{\tau}
and
\overline{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{s}
, respectively. These discrete variables represent the evaluations of the corresponding continuous 0-forms at the nodes of the primal mesh, as discussed in Section 3. With these approximations, the system of equations (
22
) can be fully expressed within the DEC framework. For simplicity, we assume that the excitation arises solely from the incident fields in
\Omega_{1}
and set
\mathbf{J}=0
. Accordingly, in the DEC domain, we obtain

\left[-\fourierbb{D}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\fourierbb{H}_{1}\fourierbb{D}_{0}+k_{0}^{2}\fourierbb{H}_{0}(\varepsilon)\right]\overline{\mathbf{a}}_{\tau}+\fourierbb{D}_{2}^{\mathrm{(c)}}\partial_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}}{\mathbf{a}}_{\tau}^{(\Gamma)}-i\fourierbb{H}_{0}\left(\partial_{\tau}\varepsilon\right)\overline{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{s}=0,\quad\forall\,\tau\in\left\{x,y,z\right\}

(41)

and

\left[-\fourierbb{D}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\fourierbb{H}_{1}(\varepsilon)\fourierbb{D}_{0}+k_{0}^{2}\fourierbb{H}_{0}(\varepsilon^{2})\right]\overline{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{s}+\fourierbb{D}_{2}^{\mathrm{(c)}}\partial_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}}{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{s}^{(\Gamma)}-ik_{0}^{2}\sum\limits_{\tau}\fourierbb{H}_{0}\left(\partial_{\tau}\varepsilon\right)\overline{\mathbf{a}}_{\tau}=0.

(42)

Here, the terms
\fourierbb{D}_{2}^{\mathrm{(c)}}\partial_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}}{\mathbf{a}}_{\tau}^{(\Gamma)}
and
\fourierbb{D}_{2}^{\mathrm{(c)}}\partial_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}}{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{s}^{(\Gamma)}
arise due to the incomplete dual mesh at the boundary
\Gamma
, and are essential for coupling the DEC formulation with surface integral equations. The operator
\fourierbb{H}_{0}\left(\partial_{\tau}\varepsilon\right)
is constructed using the Galerkin approach with Whitney 0-forms, and reduces to surface integrals on the tetrahedra common faces.

Assuming
\Gamma
is approximated by a surface triangulation with
N_{0}^{\partial}
boundary nodes number, the operator
\fourierbb{D}_{2}^{\mathrm{(c)}}
complements the dual exterior derivative operator
-\fourierbb{D}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}
. To define the complement operator
\fourierbb{D}_{2}^{\mathrm{(c)}}
, we first observe that the operator
-\fourierbb{D}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}
acts on dual 2-cochains. For example, in the term

-\fourierbb{D}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\fourierbb{H}_{1}\fourierbb{D}_{0}\overline{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{s},

the quantity
\fourierbb{H}_{1}\fourierbb{D}_{0}\overline{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{s}
is a dual 2-cochain. However, due to the incompleteness of the dual mesh near the boundary, the operator
-\fourierbb{D}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}
alone is insufficient; it must be complemented with boundary contributions
[
32
]
. To motivate the required complement, we invoke the identity
\star\mathbf{d}\Phi=(\nabla\Phi\cdot\boldsymbol{n})\nu
, where
\nu
is the surface 2-form on the boundary
\Gamma

[
42
, p. 506]
. This suggests that boundary flux terms supplement the incomplete dual structure. Accordingly, the operator
\fourierbb{D}_{2}^{\mathrm{(c)}}
is an
N_{0}\times N_{0}^{\partial}
matrix that augments the dual 3-cells by traversing along the primal boundary triangles in the orientation direction of the dual 3-cells, and is given by

\fourierbb{D}_{2}^{\mathrm{(c)}}=\frac{1}{6}\,\left|\fourierbb{D}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\right|\,\left|\fourierbb{D}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\right|\,\mathbf{P}_{2}^{\mathsf{T}}\,\Pi_{S}\,\mathbb{Q},

(43)

where
\left|\fourierbb{D}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\right|
denotes the matrix
\fourierbb{D}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}
with all entries replaced by their absolute values,
\mathbf{P}_{2}
is an
N_{2}^{\partial}\times N_{2}
selection matrix that extracts values on boundary triangles,
\Pi_{S}
is a diagonal matrix whose entries are the areas of boundary triangles, i.e.,
\mathrm{diag}(S_{1},S_{2},\dots,S_{N_{2}^{\partial}})
, and
\mathbb{Q}
is an
N_{2}^{\partial}\times N_{0}^{\partial}
that interpolates nodal values of the scalar field on the boundary to the centroids of boundary triangles, and is given by

\mathbb{Q}=\frac{1}{6}\,\left|\fourierbb{D}_{1}^{\partial}\right|\,\left|\fourierbb{D}_{0}^{\partial}\right|,

where
\fourierbb{D}_{k}^{\partial}
denotes the discrete exterior derivative of degree
k
on the boundary mesh
\Gamma
, defined analogously to
\fourierbb{D}_{k}
. The
N_{0}^{\partial}\times 1
vectors
\partial_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}}{\mathbf{a}}_{\tau}^{(\Gamma)}
and
\partial_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}}{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{s}^{(\Gamma)}
are the outward normal derivatives
\partial_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}}\tilde{A}_{\tau}
and
\partial_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}}\Phi_{s}
, respectively, evaluated at the boundary nodes. Notice that the prefactor
1/6
in Eq. (
43
) arises as the product of two terms: (1) a factor
1/2
, which ensures that the entries of
\left|\fourierbb{D}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\right|\,\left|\fourierbb{D}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\right|
are either
0
or
1
; and
(2) a factor
1/3
, which accounts for the contribution of each primal boundary triangle to the dual area, completing the dual mesh on
\Gamma
.

The discrete SIE system given by Eq. (
37
) can be coupled to the DEC Eqs. (
41
) and (
42
) by imposing continuity conditions of the scalar fields as given by Eq. (
19
). In particular, the column vectors
\partial_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}}{\mathbf{a}}_{\tau}^{(\Gamma)}
and
\partial_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}}{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{s}^{(\Gamma)}
in Eqs. (
41
) and (
42
) must be obtained by imposing the conditions (
19
) and using (
37
) to obtain

\partial_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}}{\mathbf{a}}_{\tau}^{(\Gamma)}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}^{-1}\left[\boldsymbol{f}_{\tilde{A}_{\tau}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}{\mathbf{a}}_{\tau}^{(\Gamma)}\right],\quad\forall\,\tau\in\left\{x,y,z\right\}

(44)

and

\partial_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}}{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{s}^{(\Gamma)}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}^{-1}\left[\boldsymbol{f}_{\Phi_{s}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{s}^{(\Gamma)}\right],

(45)

where
{\mathbf{a}}_{\tau}^{(\Gamma)}
and
{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{s}^{(\Gamma)}
are
N_{0}^{\partial}\times 1
vectors whose entries are the values of
\tilde{A}_{\tau}
and
\Phi_{s}
at the boundary nodes. Defining the
N_{0}^{\partial}\times N_{0}
selection matrix
\mathbf{P}_{0}
that maps a 0-form on
\Omega_{2}
to its evaluations at the boundary nodes, we obtain

\mathbf{a}_{\tau}^{(\Gamma)}=\mathbf{P}_{0}\overline{\mathbf{a}}_{\tau},\quad{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{s}^{(\Gamma)}=\mathbf{P}_{0}\overline{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{s},

where
\mathbf{P}_{0}
is obtained by starting with the
N_{0}\times N_{0}
identity matrix and deleting the rows corresponding to interior node indices. Substituting these relations into Eqs. (
44
) and (
45
) yields

\partial_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}}{\mathbf{a}}_{\tau}^{(\Gamma)}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{f}_{\tilde{A}_{\tau}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\mathbf{P}_{0}\overline{\mathbf{a}}_{\tau}

(46)

and

\partial_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}}{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{s}^{(\Gamma)}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{f}_{\Phi_{s}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\mathbf{P}_{0}\overline{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{s}.

(47)

By substituting Eqs. (
46
) and (
47
) into Eqs. (
41
) and (
42
), respectively, we obtain the system

\begin{bmatrix}\fourierbb{V}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&-i\fourierbb{H}_{0}(\partial_{x}\varepsilon)\\
\mathbf{0}&\fourierbb{V}&\mathbf{0}&-i\fourierbb{H}_{0}(\partial_{y}\varepsilon)\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\fourierbb{V}&-i\fourierbb{H}_{0}(\partial_{z}\varepsilon)\\
-ik_{0}^{2}\fourierbb{H}_{0}(\partial_{x}\varepsilon)&-ik_{0}^{2}\fourierbb{H}_{0}(\partial_{y}\varepsilon)&-ik_{0}^{2}\fourierbb{H}_{0}(\partial_{z}\varepsilon)&\fourierbb{L}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\overline{\mathbf{a}}_{x}\\
\overline{\mathbf{a}}_{y}\\
\overline{\mathbf{a}}_{z}\\
\overline{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{s}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\fourierbb{D}_{2}^{(\mathrm{c})}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{f}_{\tilde{A}_{x}}\\
\fourierbb{D}_{2}^{(\mathrm{c})}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{f}_{\tilde{A}_{y}}\\
\fourierbb{D}_{2}^{(\mathrm{c})}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{f}_{\tilde{A}_{y}}\\
\fourierbb{D}_{2}^{(\mathrm{c})}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{f}_{\Phi_{s}}\\
\end{bmatrix}

(48)

where

{\fourierbb{V}}:=-\fourierbb{D}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\fourierbb{H}_{1}\fourierbb{D}_{0}+k_{0}^{2}\fourierbb{H}_{0}(\varepsilon)-\fourierbb{D}_{2}^{(\mathrm{c})}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\mathbf{P}_{0},

and

\fourierbb{L}:=-\fourierbb{D}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\fourierbb{H}_{1}(\varepsilon)\fourierbb{D}_{0}+k_{0}^{2}\fourierbb{H}_{0}(\varepsilon^{2})-\fourierbb{D}_{2}^{(\mathrm{c})}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\mathbf{P}_{0}.

Numerical results

This section presents the results of a comprehensive validation study designed to assess the accuracy, stability, and robustness of the proposed hybrid DEC-SIE method for electromagnetic analysis of inhomogeneous media under diverse scenarios. The validation is performed using two distinct approaches. First, we leverage the analytical Mie series solution for a dielectric sphere embedded in free space and the case of a multilayered sphere. These experiments are designed to demonstrate
(i) convergence of the method with increasing discretization density for a fixed permittivity, (ii) robustness against high permittivity contrasts by varying the relative permittivity to large values, and (ii) broadband stability of the formulation, particularly in the low-frequency regime. To assess the accuracy of the fields computed using the hybrid DEC-SIE method within the inhomogeneous domain
\Omega_{2}
, we invoke the equivalence principle and the extinction theorem
[
43
]
. These theorems assert that the fields generated by equivalent surface potentials—obtained from surface integral equations (SIE) formulated in the exterior domain
\Omega_{1}
—must identically cancel the incident field when evaluated inside the equivalent surface, i.e., within the interior domain
\Omega_{2}
. Any discrepancy between the directly computed interior field and the field reconstructed from boundary data, therefore, serves as a quantitative measure of numerical error.

Let
u\in\{\tilde{A}_{x},\tilde{A}_{y},\tilde{A}_{z},\Phi\}
denote a component of the computed field in
\Omega_{2}
obtained from the DEC-SIE solution. Then,
\left\lVert u\right\rVert
serves as a reference magnitude of the computed field. Using the Dirichlet and Neumann traces
u|_{\Gamma}
and
\partial_{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}}u|_{\Gamma}
on
\Gamma
, we reconstruct the field in
\Omega_{2}
. Denote this reconstructed field by
e_{u}
, given by

e_{u}(\boldsymbol{r}_{j})=u^{(\mathrm{inc})}(\boldsymbol{r}_{j})-\mathcal{S}\left[\frac{\partial u(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{\partial\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}\right](\boldsymbol{r}_{j})+\mathcal{D}[u(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})](\boldsymbol{r}_{j}),

where ideally, due to the extinction theorem, we expect
e_{u}(\boldsymbol{r})=0,\,\forall\,\mathbf{r}\in\Omega_{2}
. This field captures any residual interior response generated solely from the surface data, and thus directly reflects the consistency of the boundary data with the interior solution. The extinction relative error,

In the above numerical experiments, the Mie series benchmark provides an exact analytical reference for a canonical geometry with a smooth dielectric interface, enabling quantitative assessment of accuracy, convergence, and robustness to material contrast under controlled conditions. Complementarily, the extinction theorem validation rigorously verifies correct enforcement of the equivalence principle and accurate coupling across the DEC-SIE interface for general inhomogeneous structures where no analytical solution is available. The error functions directly measure the physical requirement of field cancellation inside the equivalent surface, providing a sensitive indicator of any inconsistency in the hybrid formulation. Together, these two validation frameworks comprehensively cover both analytically tractable and general geometries.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we have presented a novel and computationally efficient hybrid DEC-SIE method for the electromagnetic analysis of heterogeneous media. The method leverages the
\mathbf{A}
-
\Phi
formulation to ensure robustness across various frequencies and material properties, offering natural compatibility with multi-physics problems and immunity to low-frequency breakdown. A key innovation of this work lies in the reformulation of the SIE, dramatically reducing the number of surface integral operators by expressing it in terms of the scalar components of the vector potential and their normal derivatives. This simplification of the SIE, combined with the adaptation of the DEC discretization to represent each Cartesian component of
\mathbf{A}
as a 0-form, enables a more efficient and consistent coupling between the DEC and SIE domains. The resulting framework provides a unified and physically consistent approach for solving complex electromagnetic problems, with significant potential for future extension to multi-physics applications, and immediate application to metasurfaces and open resonant structures.
