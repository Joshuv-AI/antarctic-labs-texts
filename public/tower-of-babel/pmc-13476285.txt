Reverse shoulder arthroplasty vs. hemiarthroplasty for proximal humerus fracture: in-hospital outcomes in a national trauma registry
Authors: Abdullah Raizah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundReverse shoulder arthroplasty (RSA) has rapidly supplanted hemiarthroplasty for acute proximal humerus fracture, driven by superior functional outcomes and lower revision rates. Whether this shift has altered perioperative morbidity at the population level remains poorly characterized. This study compared in-hospital outcomes between RSA and hemiarthroplasty in a national trauma cohort.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used the National Trauma Data Bank (2017–2022). Adults ( > = 18 years) admitted with proximal humerus fracture who underwent RSA (n = 2657) or hemiarthroplasty (n = 428) during the index hospitalization were included. The primary outcome was non-home discharge; secondary outcomes were any in-hospital complication, extended length of stay exceeding 7 days, and intensive care unit (ICU) admission. Multivariable logistic regression and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) were used to address confounding by indication.ResultsAmong 3085 patients (median age, 73 years; 2304 [74.7%] female), RSA patients were older (median, 74 vs. 64 years; P<.001) and more frequently female (77.3% vs. 58.6%; P<.001). After multivariable adjustment, RSA was not significantly associated with non-home discharge (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.20; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.93–1.55), any complication (aOR, 1.01; 95% CI 0.58–1.77), extended length of stay (aOR, 1.27; 95% CI 0.99–1.62), or ICU admission (aOR, 1.05; 95% CI 0.74–1.50). IPTW analyses were concordant for three of four outcomes; extended length of stay was the only outcome reaching significance under IPTW (OR, 1.38; 95% CI 1.06–1.81), a finding not replicated in the primary model. In a pre-specified subgroup, RSA was associated with higher risk of non-home discharge among patients younger than 65 years (aOR, 1.59; 95% CI 1.06–2.40).ConclusionIn this national trauma registry analysis, RSA for proximal humerus fracture was not associated with significantly different in-hospital outcomes compared with hemiarthroplasty after adjustment.Level of evidence Level III Retrospective Cohort Comparison Treatment Study.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00402-026-06453-7.

Introduction

Proximal humerus fractures account for approximately 5% of all adult fractures and represent the third most common osteoporotic fracture after those of the distal radius and proximal femur [1, 2]. Their incidence increases sharply with advancing age, disproportionately affecting women older than 65 years, and is projected to rise as populations age worldwide [3]. Although most proximal humerus fractures are minimally displaced and amenable to nonoperative management, complex 3- and 4-part fracture patterns in elderly patients with osteoporotic bone frequently necessitate arthroplasty [4, 5].

For decades, hemiarthroplasty served as the primary arthroplasty option for unreconstructable proximal humerus fractures. Functional outcomes after hemiarthroplasty depend heavily on healing and anatomic reattachment of the greater tuberosity, and unpredictable tuberosity resorption or malunion has been associated with pseudoparalysis and diminished patient satisfaction [6, 7]. Reverse shoulder arthroplasty (RSA), originally developed for rotator cuff arthropathy, circumvents this dependence on tuberosity integrity by recruiting the deltoid as the primary elevator. Multiple meta-analyses have demonstrated that RSA achieves superior forward flexion, abduction, pain scores, and functional outcomes compared with hemiarthroplasty, with lower reoperation and revision rates [8–10]. These meta-analyses established the comparative functional and reoperation-related advantages of RSA; whether the accompanying shift in procedure selection has altered perioperative safety during the index hospitalization, a distinct question from functional recovery, has not been characterized in a contemporary national trauma-registry cohort. Consequently, RSA utilization for acute proximal humerus fracture has increased substantially across multiple national registries [11, 12].

Despite evidence favoring RSA for functional recovery, a critical knowledge deficit persists regarding perioperative safety. Most comparative studies have focused on range of motion, patient-reported outcome scores, and medium-term reoperation rates, with limited attention to in-hospital outcomes such as discharge disposition, acute complications, intensive care utilization, and length of stay. Whether the rapid procedural shift toward RSA has introduced population-level changes in early postoperative morbidity remains unknown, particularly in the trauma setting where patient acuity and comorbidity profiles differ from those in elective arthroplasty populations.

This retrospective cohort study used the National Trauma Data Bank (NTDB) to compare in-hospital outcomes between RSA and hemiarthroplasty for proximal humerus fracture from 2017 to 2022. It was hypothesized that, after adjustment for confounders, RSA would not be associated with significantly different rates of non-home discharge, in-hospital complications, extended length of stay, or ICU admission compared with hemiarthroplasty.

Materials and methods

Study design and data source

A retrospective cohort study was performed using the NTDB Trauma Quality Programs (TQP) Participant Use File for admission years 2017 through 2022. The NTDB is the largest aggregation of trauma registry data in the United States, capturing demographic, injury, procedural, and outcomes information from more than 900 participating trauma centers [13]. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational research [14]. Because the NTDB contains de-identified data, the study was exempt from institutional review board approval.

Study population

The cohort comprised all adult patients (≥ 18 years) admitted with proximal humerus fracture (ICD-10-CM codes S42.231 A/B through S42.249 A/B; 3- and 4-part fractures, initial encounter) who underwent shoulder arthroplasty during the index hospitalization. Procedure groups were defined using ICD-10 Procedure Coding System (ICD-10-PCS) codes for RSA and hemiarthroplasty. Patients who were dead on arrival, had missing discharge disposition, or had missing procedure type were excluded. After applying exclusion criteria, the final analytic cohort comprised 3085 patients (RSA, n = 2657 [86.1%]; hemiarthroplasty, n = 428 [13.9%]; Fig. 1).

Exposure and outcomes

The primary exposure was procedure type (RSA vs. hemiarthroplasty). Four outcomes were evaluated during the index hospitalization. Non-home discharge, the primary outcome, was defined as any discharge destination other than home without services; home without services was the sole home-equivalent discharge category, with all other destinations (skilled nursing facility, rehabilitation center, home with health services, long-term care, and hospice) coded as non-home. Any complication was a composite endpoint including unplanned return to the operating room, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, acute kidney injury, cardiac arrest, delirium, sepsis, stroke/cerebrovascular accident, and unplanned ICU transfer. Extended length of stay was defined as total hospital stay exceeding 7 days. ICU admission was recorded as any ICU stay during the index hospitalization. Unplanned ICU transfer overlapped almost completely with the ICU admission outcome, as 54 of the 55 patients with an unplanned transfer also met the ICU admission definition; the two outcomes are therefore not mutually exclusive.

Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literature [15] and included age (continuous; missing values [4.0%] imputed to 90 years per HIPAA age-blanking protocol), sex, Injury Severity Score (ISS, continuous), comorbidity count (sum of documented comorbidities), polytrauma status (AIS severity ≥ 2 in ≥ 2 body regions), fall mechanism, and COVID-era admission (2020–2021). Individual comorbidities included in the regression model were hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, congestive heart failure, anticoagulant therapy, dementia, chronic renal failure, functional dependence, current smoking, alcohol use disorder, substance use disorder, and disseminated cancer.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test after Shapiro-Wilk and Anderson-Darling tests confirmed non-normality (all P < .001). Categorical variables were summarized as frequency (percentage) and compared using the Pearson chi-square or Fisher exact test when expected cell counts were fewer than 5. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated for all Table 1 variables; SMD greater than 0.10 was considered a clinically meaningful imbalance.

Multivariable logistic regression models were fitted for each primary outcome, adjusting for the pre-specified covariates and individual comorbidities. Results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and calibration was evaluated with the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. As a complementary approach to address confounding by indication, IPTW was performed. Propensity scores for RSA receipt were estimated using logistic regression including all covariates. Stabilized average treatment effect weights were computed and truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles. Covariate balance after weighting was assessed using absolute SMD, with values below 0.10 considered adequate. Weighted logistic regression with heteroskedasticity-consistent robust (HC0) standard errors was used to estimate treatment effects.

Pre-specified subgroup analyses for the primary outcome tested for effect modification by age (≥ 65 vs. < 65 years), polytrauma status, and COVID-era admission. Two pre-specified sensitivity analyses were conducted by separately excluding interfacility transfers and COVID-era admissions (2020–2021). All analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Two-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant. No adjustment was made for multiple comparisons given the pre-specified, hypothesis-driven nature of the outcomes.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

A total of 3085 patients who underwent shoulder arthroplasty for proximal humerus fracture between 2017 and 2022 met inclusion criteria: 2657 (86.1%) received RSA and 428 (13.9%) received hemiarthroplasty. Table 1 summarizes baseline characteristics by procedure type. RSA patients were older (median age, 74 [IQR, 67–80] vs. 64 [IQR, 54–77] years; P < .001; SMD, 0.732) and more frequently female (77.3% vs. 58.6%; P < .001; SMD, 0.407). Age 65 years or older was present in 81.3% of RSA patients compared with 48.2% of hemiarthroplasty patients (SMD, 0.737). Fall-related injury was the predominant mechanism in both groups but was more common among RSA patients (79.6% vs. 58.9%; P < .001; SMD, 0.460). RSA patients had a higher comorbidity burden, with greater prevalences of hypertension (68.4% vs. 55.6%; SMD, 0.266) and anticoagulant therapy (17.0% vs. 12.4%; SMD, 0.130). In contrast, the hemiarthroplasty group had higher rates of current smoking (18.0% vs. 11.0%; SMD, 0.200), alcohol use disorder (7.9% vs. 3.6%; SMD, 0.188), and substance use disorder (6.1% vs. 1.4%; SMD, 0.246). Polytrauma was more common in hemiarthroplasty patients (22.9% vs. 16.0%; P < .001; SMD, 0.175). Median ISS (5 [IQR, 4–6] vs. 5 [IQR, 4–9]; P = .027), time to surgery (48.2 vs. 50.0 h; P = .984), and length of stay (6 days in both groups; P = .689) did not differ meaningfully between groups. In-hospital outcomes

Six in-hospital deaths occurred (RSA, 5 [0.2%]; hemiarthroplasty, 1 [0.2%]). Because of the sparse events, mortality was reported descriptively and not modeled. On unadjusted analysis, non-home discharge was significantly more common in the RSA group (70.1% vs. 54.4%; P < .001). The composite complication rate did not differ significantly between groups (RSA, 4.4%; hemiarthroplasty, 4.0%; P = .780), nor did the proportions with extended length of stay exceeding 7 days (38.7% vs. 37.1%; P = .569). ICU admission was numerically more common in the hemiarthroplasty group (15.4% vs. 12.0%; P = .057). Individual complications, including delirium (1.1% vs. 0.2%), deep vein thrombosis (0.6% vs. 0%), and unplanned return to the operating room (0.5% vs. 0.5%), were infrequent with no significant between-group differences (Table 2).

Multivariable-adjusted outcomes

After adjustment for age, sex, ISS, comorbidity burden, polytrauma status, fall mechanism, individual comorbidities, and COVID-era admission, no significant differences were observed between RSA and hemiarthroplasty for any of the four primary outcomes (Table 2; Fig. 3). The adjusted odds of non-home discharge were not significantly elevated with RSA (aOR, 1.20; 95% CI, 0.93–1.55; P = .153). Procedure type was not associated with increased odds of any complication (aOR, 1.01; 95% CI, 0.58–1.77; P = .962), extended length of stay (aOR, 1.27; 95% CI, 0.99–1.62; P = .058), or ICU admission (aOR, 1.05; 95% CI, 0.74–1.50; P = .786). Full multivariable regression coefficients for the primary outcome are reported in eTable 1. Precision differed materially across outcomes: non-home discharge and extended length of stay, the most frequent events, yielded relatively narrow confidence intervals, whereas the any-complication estimate, based on only 134 events, was imprecise and should be interpreted as inconclusive rather than as evidence of equivalence between procedures.

Inverse probability of treatment–weighted analysis

The propensity score model for treatment assignment achieved an AUC of 0.714. After IPTW with stabilized, truncated weights, covariate balance improved substantially, with absolute standardized mean differences below 0.10 for all covariates except age, which remained marginally above the threshold (eFigure 1). Consistent with the multivariable regression, IPTW analyses revealed no significant association between RSA and non-home discharge (OR, 1.02; 95% CI, 0.79–1.33; P = .857), any complication (OR, 1.22; 95% CI, 0.65–2.29; P = .539), or ICU admission (OR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.63–1.34; P = .668). Extended length of stay exceeding 7 days was the sole outcome reaching statistical significance under IPTW (OR, 1.38; 95% CI, 1.06–1.81; P = .019), a finding that was not replicated in the primary multivariable model (P = .058) (eTable 2; Fig. 3).

Subgroup analyses

Pre-specified subgroup analyses for the primary outcome of non-home discharge were conducted across three clinically relevant strata (eFigure 2). No significant interaction was detected between procedure type and age category, polytrauma status, or COVID-era admission. Point estimates remained directionally consistent with the overall analysis across strata; the only subgroup in which the 95% confidence interval excluded unity was patients younger than 65 years, in whom RSA was associated with higher adjusted odds of non-home discharge (aOR, 1.59; 95% CI, 1.06–2.40), a finding that should be regarded as hypothesis-generating given the multiplicity of subgroup comparisons.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses produced results concordant with the primary analysis (eTable 3). After excluding interfacility transfers, adjusted odds ratios remained non-significant for non-home discharge, any complication, extended length of stay, and ICU admission. Exclusion of COVID-era years (2020–2021) yielded similarly non-significant findings across all four outcomes, consistent with the primary analysis.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of 3085 patients from the National Trauma Data Bank, RSA for proximal humerus fracture was not associated with significantly different in-hospital outcomes compared with hemiarthroplasty after adjustment for age, sex, injury severity, comorbidity burden, polytrauma status, fall mechanism, individual comorbidities, and COVID-era admission. All four primary outcomes yielded adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals crossing unity, and these null findings persisted across IPTW analyses, pre-specified subgroup evaluations, and separate sensitivity cohorts. Taken together, the analyses did not identify statistically detectable between-procedure differences in the evaluated in-hospital outcomes; however, these findings do not establish equivalence.

The rapidly increasing adoption of RSA observed in this analysis aligns with national and international trends documented over the past decade. Sabesan et al. [11] analyzing the National Inpatient Sample from 2004 to 2012, documented substantial growth in RSA use and projected continued expansion. McLean et al. [12] reviewing Australian hospitalizations from 2008 to 2017, observed that RSA rose from 4.1% to 24.5% while hemiarthroplasty declined in parallel. Cognetti et al. [16] analyzed the NSQIP database from 2007 to 2018 and found that the proportion of proximal humerus fracture arthroplasties performed as RSA increased from 4% to 34%. Population-level data from South Korea similarly demonstrated that RSA accounted for more than 50% of arthroplasties for proximal humerus fracture by 2016 [17]. This analysis extends these observations through 2022 in a trauma-specific cohort drawn from the NTDB, a registry that captures patients across the full spectrum of injury severity.

Prior comparative studies and meta-analyses have generally favored RSA over hemiarthroplasty for functional recovery. Austin et al. [8] in a meta-analysis of 15 studies comprising 913 geriatric patients, found that RSA produced significantly better forward flexion, pain scores, and functional outcome scores, with a 2.8-fold lower reoperation rate. Shukla et al. [9] pooled data from 7 comparative studies and confirmed the superiority of RSA in forward elevation and abduction, with only external rotation favoring hemiarthroplasty. Gallinet et al. [10] reported more reproducible functional outcomes after RSA and a lower revision rate, though the overall complication rate was higher with RSA. These meta-analyses primarily evaluated functional, radiographic, complication, revision, and reoperation outcomes in smaller comparative series; none specifically characterized discharge disposition, ICU utilization, or extended hospitalization, and their constituent studies largely predate the RSA-dominant practice era captured in the present national cohort. The present analysis complements this functional evidence by characterizing in-hospital perioperative outcomes in a large trauma registry, a domain that has been underrepresented in the RSA literature. Large single-institution and registry series have similarly reported durable functional gains and acceptable revision rates after RSA for acute fracture, reinforcing its expanding role [20, 21].

The unadjusted non-home discharge rate was considerably higher in the RSA group (70.1% vs. 54.4%), consistent with the older age and greater comorbidity burden of RSA recipients. After multivariable adjustment, this disparity was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (aOR, 1.20; 95% CI, 0.93–1.55), and the IPTW estimate was near-null (OR, 1.02; 95% CI, 0.79–1.33). These results suggest that the higher raw non-home discharge rate among RSA patients reflects patient selection rather than a procedural effect. Malik et al. [15] identified age greater than 65 years, functional dependence, and longer length of stay as the primary drivers of non-home discharge after proximal humerus fracture surgery in NSQIP data, consistent with the covariate profile observed in this RSA cohort.

Extended length of stay exceeding 7 days was the sole outcome to reach statistical significance, and only under the IPTW framework (OR, 1.38; 95% CI, 1.06–1.81; P = .019). This finding was not replicated in the primary multivariable model (P = .058) and did not persist in any sensitivity cohort. The discordance between analytic methods warrants cautious interpretation; IPTW can be more sensitive to residual unmeasured confounding, and the borderline confidence interval lower bound suggests a fragile association. In absolute terms, the median length of stay was 6 days in both groups, and the proportions exceeding 7 days were nearly identical (38.7% vs. 37.1%). However, the adjusted and weighted estimates were discordant, and a clinically meaningful between-procedure difference in hospital duration cannot be excluded.

The overall in-hospital complication rate was low in both groups (RSA, 4.4%; hemiarthroplasty, 4.0%), consistent with contemporary population-level trends. Cognetti et al. [16] documented a decline in short-term complication rates across all surgical modalities for proximal humerus fracture from 2007 to 2018 in NSQIP data. Schairer et al. [18] similarly found that in-hospital complication rates were similar between RSA and hemiarthroplasty patients in Nationwide Inpatient Sample data, despite RSA patients being significantly older. Kelly and Myeroff [19] proposed that the more predictable biomechanics of the RSA construct, which does not depend on tuberosity healing for function, may partly explain this convergence of perioperative complication profiles. ICU admission was numerically more common in the hemiarthroplasty group (15.4% vs. 12.0%), likely reflecting the greater polytrauma burden in that cohort, and this difference was not significant after adjustment. The precision of these estimates varied: the any-complication comparison, based on only 134 events overall, was the least precise and should be interpreted with caution.

COVID-era admission (2020–2021) was independently associated with higher odds of non-home discharge and any complication in the full regression model, yet no interaction was detected between COVID-era status and procedure type. This indicates that the procedural comparison was not modified by COVID-era admission. Sensitivity analysis excluding COVID-era years produced results concordant with the primary analysis, reinforcing the consistency of the non-significant findings.

Although these data demonstrate comparable perioperative safety, they should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence across all domains. Zhao et al. [7] reported satisfactory long-term outcomes after hemiarthroplasty, though 21.8% of patients developed greater tuberosity complications with significantly worse function. The Cochrane review by Handoll et al. [22] found only very low-certainty evidence of differences between RSA and hemiarthroplasty in self-reported function, but reoperation was confined almost exclusively to the hemiarthroplasty group. These data suggest that hemiarthroplasty may retain a selective role in younger patients with good bone stock, even as RSA becomes the dominant procedure.

Several strengths merit acknowledgment. The NTDB offers a large, multicenter trauma cohort with standardized data collection spanning 6 years, allowing comparison of perioperative outcomes in a real-world setting. The sample size of 3085 patients provides adequate statistical power for the outcomes examined. The concurrent use of multivariable regression and IPTW as complementary approaches to confounding by indication, combined with pre-specified subgroup and sensitivity analyses, enhances the credibility of the null findings.

Several limitations merit consideration. The retrospective observational design precludes causal inference despite statistical adjustment. The NTDB captures only the index hospitalization, preventing assessment of functional outcomes, readmissions, reoperations, and implant longevity. Fracture classification (Neer type, number of parts) and surgeon or center volume are not reliably coded in the NTDB, introducing potential residual confounding. The absence of patient-reported outcome measures limits evaluation of the full spectrum of treatment effects. The unbalanced procedure distribution (RSA, 86.1% vs. hemiarthroplasty, 13.9%) reflects contemporary clinical practice but may reduce statistical power for detecting smaller between-group differences. This is particularly consequential for the any-complication outcome (134 total events; aOR, 1.01; 95% CI, 0.58–1.77), for which the wide confidence interval should be regarded as inconclusive rather than as evidence of equivalence between procedures. In addition, the any-complication composite and the ICU admission outcome are not fully independent: unplanned ICU transfer, one component of the complication composite (55 events), overlapped almost completely with the broader ICU admission outcome (54 of these 55 patients), and the two outcomes should not be interpreted as orthogonal measures of morbidity. Finally, discharge destination codes within the NTDB may not perfectly capture downstream care trajectories or functional recovery. The NTDB does not provide unique hospital identifiers in the Participant Use File, precluding adjustment for hospital-level clustering effects that may influence both procedural selection and outcomes.

Conclusions

In this national trauma registry study of patients undergoing shoulder arthroplasty for proximal humerus fracture from 2017 to 2022, RSA was not associated with significantly different in-hospital outcomes compared with hemiarthroplasty after multivariable adjustment and inverse probability of treatment weighting. Non-home discharge, in-hospital complications, extended length of stay, and ICU admission rates did not differ significantly between procedures. These findings do not establish equivalence between the procedures, and the complication estimate in particular was imprecise; longer-term functional and implant survival outcomes require further investigation.
