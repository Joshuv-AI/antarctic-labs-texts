Practical Recommendations for Cardiology Follow-Up in Patients Treated with Fenfluramine Based on Expert Opinion and Clinical Experience
Authors: Antonio Gil-Nagel, Leopoldo Pérez de la Isla, Elena Sanz Pascual, Enrique J Balbacid Domingo, Juan J García Peñas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFenfluramine (FFA) is approved as an adjunctive treatment for epileptic seizures associated with Dravet syndrome (DS) and Lennox–Gastaut syndrome (LGS) in patients aged 2 years and older. Owing to its pharmacological characteristics and history of cardiovascular adverse effects, specific cardiological monitoring is required throughout the course of treatment.MethodsA set of recommendations for FFA was developed by a multidisciplinary panel comprising specialists in neurology/paediatric neurology, epilepsy, and cardiology (both adult and paediatric). The process involved a literature review, methodological design, working group sessions, and preparation of recommendations structured around three key phases: before treatment, during treatment, and after treatment discontinuation.ResultsAn expert-led algorithm for the use of FFA was developed, helping to establish a common language that promotes a better understanding between cardiologists and neurologists/paediatric neurologists, facilitating the prevention and early management of potential cardiovascular events.ConclusionThe management of FFA treatment should be holistic, with coordinated follow-up involving neurologists, paediatric neurologists, and cardiologists. Implementing a structured, expert-based protocol will help enhance treatment safety and optimize clinical outcomes for patients with developmental and epileptic encephalopathies.Keywords: Valvular heart disease, Echocardiography, Epilepsy, Fenfluramine, Pulmonary arterial hypertension, Clinical Practice Guidelines

Key Summary Points

Fenfluramine (FFA) treatment requires structured cardiac monitoring due to potential valvular heart disease and pulmonary arterial hypertension, where coordinated workflows between neurology and cardiology specialists are key to assure early detection of cardiac issues, optimization of patient selection and follow-up, as well as patient-centred decisions.Although the Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC) for FFA establishes general recommendations for cardiac monitoring of patients, there is currently a lack of standardized procedures to implement these recommendations into real-world clinical practice.This article provides practical recommendations on the cardiology follow-up in patients treated with fenfluramine, based on a review of available literature and author’s professional clinical experience.A multidisciplinary expert panel formulated recommendations on a patient’s monitoring pathway which covers the baseline assessment, on-treatment, and post-discontinuation echocardiography.This actionable framework provides a guideline for common echocardiographic findings and supports consistent decision-making and escalation when clinically relevant abnormalities are suspected.

Introduction

Fenfluramine (FFA) is indicated as an adjunctive treatment for epileptic seizures associated with Dravet syndrome (DS) and Lennox–Gastaut syndrome (LGS) in patients aged 2 years and older [1]. FFA is the only approved therapy for DS and LGS with a dual action on serotonin receptors and as a modulator of sigma-1 receptors, and has been shown to have beneficial effects on both seizure control and associated comorbidities [2]. Historically, FFA was used in the treatment of obesity in adults, often in combination with phentermine, but at much higher doses (60–120 mg) than those currently approved for DS and LGS. However, high-dose FFA was linked to some cases of valvular heart disease and pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), leading to its withdrawal from the market in the late 1990s. These cardiovascular effects were primarily attributed to the stimulation of serotonergic 5-HT2B receptors present in heart valves, triggering proliferation of valvular interstitial cells and pathological tissue remodelling [3].

During clinical development of FFA for DS and LGS, thorough cardiovascular safety assessments were conducted using significantly lower doses (up to a maximum of 26 mg/day) than previously used for obesity. Across these trials, no cases of valvular heart disease or PAH were observed [4–10]. Detailed analyses of echocardiographic data collected from trial participants further confirmed the absence of valvular heart disease and PAH [11]. Since the approval of FFA for use in DS and LGS, post-marketing data show that they can also occur with the doses used to treat Dravet syndrome and Lennox–Gastaut.

Based on expert opinion and clinical experience with valvular heart disease and PAH, a collaborative, multidisciplinary approach is recommended both before initiating and throughout treatment with FFA. This approach should involve neurologists, paediatric neurologists, and cardiologists, with careful consideration of the individual risk–benefit profile for each patient [1, 12].

General Cardiac Monitoring Protocol

According to the Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC) for FFA [1], systematic monitoring of patients is recommended throughout the course of treatment. This monitoring is structured into three key phases: before treatment initiation, during treatment, and following treatment discontinuation. The recommended tool for cardiac monitoring is TTE. Owing to the pharmacological profile of FFA, its historical association with adverse cardiovascular events, and the monitoring requirements outlined in the SmPC, several specialists involved in the treatment of DS and LGS recognized the need to establish a collaborative protocol for FFA use. The objective of the project presented in this article was to develop a series of recommendations for the use of FFA that would establish a shared language between cardiologists and neurologists/pediatric neurologists, thereby optimizing the therapeutic approach and facilitating the prevention and early management of potential cardiovascular complications.

Methods

The methodology was organized into several phases to ensure achievement of the proposed objectives, including a kick-off meeting, a comprehensive literature review, methodological design, a series of working sessions with the expert panel, and the development of expert-based recommendations. The process was overseen by an independent, specialized consulting team responsible for maintaining methodological rigour.

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Phase 1: Kick-Off Meeting and Literature Review

To initiate the development of these recommendations, a multidisciplinary group of experts was assembled, comprising: neurologists (adult and paediatric) specializing in epilepsy, including members of scientific societies and those involved in the clinical development and early access programmes for FFA; and cardiologists (adult and paediatric) with expertise in cardiac imaging who have contributed to both the cardiac monitoring during clinical trials and the drug’s use in clinical practice.

The expert panel was supported by a consulting team with experience in moderation and facilitation of expert recommendations methodologies. Their role was to help define the context, objectives, and scope of the recommendations document. A comprehensive literature review was conducted to assess the current body of evidence, identify existing knowledge gaps, and establish the need for a published set of recommendations.

Phase 2: Methodological Design

During this phase, the structure and methodology for the working sessions were defined, with the aim of establishing a systematic procedure to collect expert input in a structured way and achieve a strong, evidence-based agreement to inform the final recommendations document. In addition, support materials were developed to facilitate the working sessions in Phase 3.

Phase 3: Working Sessions with the Expert Panel

A series of working sessions were conducted with the multidisciplinary panel of experts, composed of four specialists: Antonio Gil-Nagel, Juan José García Peñas, Elena Sanz Pascual and Leopoldo Pérez de Isla.

Regarding the methodology, the initiative consisted of structured working sessions, organized around predefined thematic blocks, with discussion of clinical scenarios and synthesis of agreed conclusions. These sessions were coordinated by the consulting team, and the discussion was structured into four main blocks: terminology from the SPmC, the general monitoring protocol, key considerations at each treatment stage, and the procedure for communication between specialists. In addition, within the treatment block, the discussion was organized around four key decision points, and a decision tree was proposed to structure the findings and support benefit–risk assessment.

The aim of these sessions was to engage in collaborative discussions, compare perspectives, and reach a strong agreement on a set of robust and well-supported recommendations for the use of FFA and the management of various clinical scenarios. The recommendations were developed by integrating the clinical perspectives of the different specialties involved in patient care.

Phase 4: Development of Recommendations

In the final phase, a document of recommendations agreed by the expert panel was drafted. The aim of this document was to provide a practical, visually accessible decision-making algorithm to support healthcare professionals. The algorithm was developed based on recommendations on the use of FFA from the initial literature review and information gathered during sessions with the expert panel. The algorithm covered the key stages of the care process (i.e., before treatment, during treatment, and after treatment), allowing guidelines for action in each case to be easily and schematically visualized.

Before Treatment

According to the SmPC, FFA treatment is contraindicated in patients with aortic or mitral valvular heart disease, as well as in those with PAH.

A baseline TTE performed before treatment can help identify conditions that affect the suitability of FFA, such as the presence of valvular insufficiency (VI) or signs suggestive of, or compatible with, PAH.

If PAH or a PAH-compatible finding is detected, action will depend on the estimated likelihood of PAH. By referring to PAH-compatible findings, we indicate that measurements obtained by transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) represent only an indirect estimation of the probability of PAH. Confirmed PAH was defined exclusively in cases assessed by catheterisation, which allows direct haemodynamic measurement. After Discontinuation of Treatment

If FFA treatment is discontinued, it is recommended to perform a final TTE between 3 and 6 months after discontinuation, in accordance with the SmPC [1].

If previously observed abnormalities are no longer present during the final TTE, two scenarios may arise: if VI has resolved, it may be appropriate to repeat the tests and reconsider treatment with FFA (given that VI is theoretically irreversible, its resolution could suggest that the initial TTE finding was a false positive); if the probability of PAH has decreased or disappeared, the benefit/risk balance should be reassessed and reintroduction of treatment could be considered [12].

Discussion

Before initiating FFA treatment, it is important to evaluate conditions that may predispose a patient to valvular heart disease or PAH, as these could mistakenly be attributed to adverse effects of the drug. Factors to consider include a family history of connective tissue disorders, such as Marfan syndrome or Ehlers–Danlos syndrome [13], as well as valvular heart conditions, thrombopathies, or thrombotic diathesis. In these cases, a more frequent cardiology monitoring protocol is recommended. Personal history of mitral valve prolapses, ventricular dysfunction, or thrombotic diathesis should also be considered, along with interactions with other drugs [1, 14], including methylphenidate, fluoxetine, or other medications affecting platelet function.

Regarding the validity of baseline TTE to assess suitability for FFA, this is considered acceptable if performed within 6 months prior to the start of treatment, unless new symptoms or cardiac signs arise that would warrant repeating the test before treatment initiation.

During the monitoring process, it is recommended that all TTEs be performed, whenever possible, using the same equipment and by the same professional. This helps reduce technical inconsistencies and inter-observer variability, both of which can impact interpretation of the results. Consistent imaging parameters should also be maintained to ensure uniform resolution across studies. Additionally, it is advisable to digitize and archive the imaging data in a sequential manner throughout the monitoring period to facilitate longitudinal assessment [15].

If VI is suspected, the projections that best demonstrate insufficiency should be recorded. In cases of suspected PAH, the cardiologist should specify the methodology used for estimation in the report and include a comparison with previous TTE findings. If necessary, and following joint evaluation by the specialists involved, additional diagnostic procedures such as transeoesophageal echocardiography or cardiac catheterisation may be considered.

From an organisational standpoint, it is recommended to establish regular and effective communication channels between specialists to support a multidisciplinary evaluation and shared, patient-centred decision-making. This collaborative approach ensures that care is tailored to the individual needs of each patient.

Given the relatively small number of patients eligible for this treatment, it is advisable, wherever feasible, for each centre to implement a standardised management protocol. Ideally, a designated reference cardiologist should oversee and coordinate the cardiac monitoring process.

It is also essential that the cardiologist performing the evaluations has access to relevant materials and receives specific training on FFA treatment beforehand, allowing them to tailor the assessments to the unique requirements of the monitoring process.

To help ensure adherence to the timelines for cardiological monitoring outlined in the SmPC, it is recommended that, following the initial baseline TTE, subsequent follow-up appointments be scheduled during the same visit. This facilitates timely and consistent follow-up.

Limitations

The main limitation of this study is the reduced number of healthcare professionals that comprise the expert panel. However, the number of patients affected by epileptic seizures and treated with FFA is limited, so the number of healthcare specialists with a contrasted experience and strong knowledge is also restricted. Nevertheless, the expert panel was composed to ensure a diverse and representative group of specialists, including cardiology and neurology experts from both paediatric and adult disciplines who had experience managing these patients in referral hospitals.

Additionally, the proposed recommendations arise from expert agreement and clinical experience, considering the suitability and benefits of establishing a coordinated procedure. Notwithstanding, future studies should evaluate the clinical outcomes associated with the implementation of this structured follow-up protocol in patients treated with FFA.

Conclusion

This document represents a significant advancement in national clinical practice by providing an expert-based framework for the appropriate use of fenfluramine in accordance with its approved indications. Grounded in expert clinical experience and developed following a structured methodology for expert recommendations, the guidance is designed to be practical, applicable to real-world clinical settings, and aligned with the structure and needs of the Spanish healthcare system, with potential adaptability to other countries. By establishing clear criteria for treatment initiation, monitoring, and discontinuation, the document supports therapeutic efficiency and promotes equitable access to care. Furthermore, the need for future updates incorporating data from observational studies and national clinical registries is acknowledged, allowing the recommendations to evolve in line with emerging real-world evidence.
