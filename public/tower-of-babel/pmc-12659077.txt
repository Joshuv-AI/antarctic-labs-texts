Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome following surgery for Libman–Sacks endocarditis: A case report and literature review
Authors: Yinxia Li, Qingyu Huang, Rui Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo report a rare case of catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS) following surgery for Libman–Sacks endocarditis (LSE), and to clarify the clinical relationship between antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), LSE and CAPS.MethodsWe analysed the clinical course of a patient who developed CAPS after mitral valve replacement surgery for LSE. A literature review was conducted to explore the systemic nature of APS and its cardiac and thrombotic manifestations.ResultsA 53-year-old woman initially presented with cerebral infarction and was found to have mitral valve vegetations. Although APS-associated features were present before surgery, a diagnosis was not established at that time. Postoperatively, the patient developed multiple thrombotic events affecting the brain, spleen, kidneys and extremities. Serological testing and clinical deterioration led to the diagnosis of CAPS. Despite supportive therapy, the patient died of multi-organ failure.ConclusionThis case highlights the importance of recognising APS as the systemic autoimmune basis of LSE and the need for early immunologic and thrombotic evaluation in patients with unexplained valve lesions. Failure to identify underlying APS before surgery may increase the risk of CAPS and poor outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-025-03692-4.Keywords: Antiphospholipid syndrome, Libman–Sacks endocarditis, Catastrophic APS, Valve vegetations, Thrombotic complications

Introduction

Libman–Sacks endocarditis (LSE), also known as non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis, is a typical cardiac manifestation of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and antiphospholipid syndrome (APS). It is characterised by the formation of sterile, wart‐like vegetations on heart valves, primarily affecting the mitral valve, followed by the aortic valve, while the tricuspid and pulmonary valves are less frequently involved [1]. Echocardiography or transoesophageal echocardiography can reveal localised thickening of the heart valve or the presence of abnormal echogenic masses or attached spots, which vary in echo patterns, size and shape. These findings need to be differentiated from infective endocarditis, valvular thrombosis, and cardiac tumours [2]. In addition to considering the patient’s medical history, clinical presentation and laboratory findings, surgical pathology is the gold standard for diagnosing these valvular heart diseases [3]. A non‐inflammatory autoimmune disease, APS is clinically characterised by arterial and venous thrombosis, adverse pregnancy outcomes (early miscarriage and stillbirth in mid‐to‐late pregnancy) and thrombocytopaenia. If antiphospholipid antibodies (APLAs) are detected in the serum, these symptoms may occur individually or in combination [4]. LSE is now widely regarded as a thrombotic cardiac manifestation of APS and may precede other systemic features. However, a diagnosis of APS is often missed preoperatively in patients presenting solely with valvular lesions. Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS) is a rare but life-threatening subtype of APS, characterised by acute microvascular thrombosis involving three or more organs within a short time frame. It is often triggered by surgery, infection or medication withdrawal, and carries a high mortality rate [5]. Prompt recognition of APS before surgical intervention is essential to avoid such devastating outcomes.

This case report presents a unique progression from LSE to postoperative CAPS in a patient with undiagnosed APS. The diagnosis of APS was only established retrospectively after the onset of multiple organ thromboses. The case underscores the importance of early autoimmune evaluation in patients with unexplained valve vegetations. A targeted literature review is included to contextualise the pathophysiology, diagnostic pitfalls, as well as treatment strategies for APS, LSE and CAPS.

Case presentation

Medical history and physical examination

A 53-year-old woman was initially admitted to a local hospital due to right-sided limb weakness and speech impairment. Brain magnetic resonance imaging confirmed cerebral infarction involving the basal ganglia and frontal cortex. Initial transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed a hypoechoic, nodular mass measuring approximately 12 × 9 mm attached to the anterior mitral leaflet. The vegetation exhibited limited mobility, raising suspicion for valvular thrombosis. Coagulation tests showed a fibrinogen level of 0.59 g/L (normal: 2.0–4.0 g/L), D-dimer 7.86 mg/L (normal < 0.5 mg/L) and positive anti-Smith antibodies, as well as lupus anticoagulant with mildly reduced complement C3. The patient had no fever or other clinical signs suggestive of infective endocarditis; blood cultures were not obtained, and empirical antibiotics were administered as a prophylaxis for stroke-associated infection rather than as targeted treatment for endocarditis. The patient received anticoagulation and antihypertensive therapy, after which her neurological symptoms stabilised, and she was discharged.

Within days of discharge, however, her symptoms worsened, including gum bleeding, cognitive decline and impaired executive function. She was re-admitted locally and repeat TTE confirmed persistent mitral valve vegetations. Due to embolic risk and diagnostic uncertainty, a surgical consultation was initiated.

One month later, the patient was transferred to our hospital for advanced neurological and cardiac management. On admission, vital signs were stable: temperature was 36.7 °C, pulse 83 bpm, respiratory rate 20/min and blood pressure 102/72 mmHg. She was alert but exhibited mild right hemiparesis and slowed cognitive responses. Dispersed, deep purple rashes were noted on the lower limbs, with intact skin and no ulceration. There was no facial rash, joint swelling or signs of arthritis. Lung auscultation was normal; cardiac auscultation revealed a regular rhythm without murmurs. The abdomen was soft, non-tender, and the lower limbs had no oedema. Based on cumulative findings, including cerebral infarction, mitral vegetations, elevated thrombosis markers and cutaneous findings, further autoimmune and thrombotic evaluations were pursued.

laboratory tests and auxiliary examination

Upon admission, TTE revealed multiple hyperechoic masses attached to the left atrial surface of the anterior and posterior leaflets of the mitral valve, with the largest measuring approximately 12 × 9 mm. These masses showed no significant mobility on echocardiography and exhibited no abnormal metabolic uptake on PET-CT, indicating the absence of infective or neoplastic activity. Colour Doppler imaging indicated mild mitral regurgitation, mild aortic regurgitation and no obvious tricuspid regurgitation. The heart chambers were of normal size, and left ventricular segmental contraction was normal, with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 60% (see Fig. 1).

Repeat serological testing confirmed positive anti-Smith antibody and weakly positive lupus anticoagulant. Antinuclear antibody (ANA), anti-extractable nuclear antigen, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody and anticardiolipin antibodies were negative. The C-reactive protein result was 3 mg/L. Tumour markers showed carcinoembryonic antigen 8.57 ng/ml and carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 at 270.6 U/mL.

A complete blood count revealed: white blood cell count 9.68 × 109/L, red blood cell (RBC) count 4.22 × 1012/L and platelet count 100 × 10⁹/L. Coagulation profile showed: prothrombin time 12 s, thrombin time 19.5 s, activated partial thromboplastin time 2.7 s (measured using the local clotting ratio method, equivalent to approximately 2.7-fold prolongation of normal range), fibrinogen 0.5 g/L, D-dimer 17.7 µg/mL and fibrinogen degradation products (FDPs) 13.1 µg/mL. Renal function and cardiac biomarkers were within the normal range: creatinine 68 µmol/L, estimated glomerular filtration rate 88.3 mL/min and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) 36.25 pg/mL. No fever or clinical signs of infection were observed, and blood cultures were not performed, further supporting the absence of infective endocarditis. Peripheral blood smear showed no significant schistocytes.

The normal reference ranges are as follows: fibrinogen 2.0–4.0 g/L, D-dimer < 0.5 µg/mL and FDPs < 5.0 µg/mL. Based on the patient’s examination results, the following diagnoses were considered: LSE, SLE, cerebral infarction complicated by renal and splenic infarctions and deep vein thrombosis in the lower limbs.

Treatment process

After comprehensive preoperative evaluation, the patient underwent mitral valve vegetation excision and mechanical valve replacement. Histopathological examination of the excised mitral valve revealed mucinous degeneration of the valve interstitium, consistent with LSE. No evidence of bacterial infection was observed histologically. However, detailed histopathologic descriptions were unavailable, as the surgery was performed at a referring tertiary hospital. During the early postoperative course, the patient showed signs of systemic thrombotic activation. Cyanosis gradually developed in both lower extremities and distal fingers. Laboratory monitoring revealed a progressive decline in fibrinogen and platelet levels, with platelet transfusions showing poor efficacy. These findings raised concerns about consumptive coagulopathy.

On postoperative Day 1, there was stable recovery under routine monitoring. By Day 2, fingertip cyanosis emerged. By Day 3, ecchymosis and petechiae appeared on both legs. On postoperative Day 4, intravenous methylprednisolone (500 mg/day) was initiated for 5 days, along with 600 mg rituximab and subcutaneous low-molecular-weight heparin (q12h). Supportive treatments included potassium and calcium supplementation, proton pump inhibitors, liver and myocardial protection, erythropoiesis and thrombopoiesis stimulants, antivirals, prophylactic antibiotics, diuretics and agents for ventricular remodelling.

Despite the interventions, the patient’s symptoms progressed. By Day 6, laboratory tests showed serum amyloid A > 200 ng/mL, IgM 1.53 g/L, IgG 15.5 g/L and IgA 3.56 g/L. Antinuclear antibody remained positive at a titre of approximately 1:100, based on the local hospital laboratory’s non-standard dilution scale (not following conventional 1:80, 1:160, etc.). Anti-Smith antibodies turned negative. A peripheral blood smear revealed approximately 0.5% schistocytes and an immature reticulocyte fraction of 30.9%. A positive platelet antibody test and reduced ADAMTS13 activity (18.7%) were noted. Based on the acute multiorgan thrombosis, haematologic abnormalities and positive autoimmune markers, a diagnosis of CAPS was established.

On postoperative Day 7, follow-up TTE demonstrated new echogenic vegetations (10 × 8 mm) on the aortic valve with moderate regurgitation. The mitral prosthesis was functioning well with only mild regurgitation. Hyperechoic vegetations were observed on the septal (11 × 8 mm) and anterior (13 × 11 mm) leaflets of the tricuspid valve, associated with severe tricuspid regurgitation. These findings are illustrated in Fig. 2 with updated legends. These vegetations were not identified in the preoperative or intraoperative assessments and were, therefore, not removed during mitral valve surgery. Their rapid postoperative appearance suggested de novo formation, likely associated with the thrombotic process of CAPS. Pulmonary artery systolic pressure was 51 mmHg, and the inferior vena cava was dilated with reduced respiratory variation (see Fig. 2).

On postoperative Day 8, the patient developed acute right-sided hemiparesis, progressive cognitive decline, oral mucosal bleeding and haematuria. Cranial CT revealed an acute infarction in the left parietal lobe. By Day 9, the patient had died of multiple organ failure despite aggressive medical treatment.

Discussion

Diagnostic challenges in Libman–Sacks endocarditis

LSE primarily affects heart valves and may present diagnostic difficulties when coexisting with complex systemic diseases. Its pathogenesis involves fibrin and platelet deposition, neovascularisation, as well as varying mononuclear inflammation, leading to fibrosis and valve dysfunction over time [6, 7]. Its echocardiographic features—poorly defined immobile vegetations with uneven echogenicity—help to differentiate it from infective endocarditis (IE) and papillary fibroelastomas. Papillary fibroelastomas typically appear as pedunculated, mobile and well-defined lobulated masses, aiding differential diagnosis [8]. LSE is more likely when APLAs positivity, low inflammatory markers and negative blood cultures are present [9–11]. Transoesophageal echocardiography offers superior detection of LS vegetations compared to TTE [12]. In patients with SLE, LSE occurs in approximately 11%, predominantly affecting mitral and aortic valves [13]. Early recognition is essential, especially in patients who are APLA-positive, to guide surgical timing and approach. Although valve repair may be attempted in stable cases, recurrence has been reported [14], favouring valve replacement in high-risk patients [15, 16].

Antiphospholipid syndrome-associated valvular disease and missed preoperative diagnosis

Antiphospholipid syndrome is a systemic autoimmune disorder characterised by thrombosis and elevated APLAs. Valvular lesions such as LSE may be the earliest cardiac manifestations [17]. The annual thrombotic risk in secondary APS is around 4%, while in primary APS it ranges from 0 to 3.8% [18]. In our case, APS was not recognised preoperatively, despite SM antibody positivity and mitral vegetations, leading to delayed immunologic management and contributing to CAPS post-surgery. Antiphospholipid antibody positivity in patients with SLE with valve disease can reach 90%, and 4%–6% may require surgery [19–23]. Echocardiography, anticoagulation and immunomodulatory therapy are critical. An early APS diagnosis could allow for immunosuppressive therapy and reduce the risk of CAPS. For example, low-dose aspirin is advised for patients without vegetations; anticoagulation is recommended if an embolism is present; and adjuncts such as hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) or statins may be used, depending on the risks involved [24–28]. In asymptomatic APS cases with vegetations, HCQ, corticosteroids and anticoagulation may reduce vegetations and improve prognosis [29, 30].

Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome and its trigger by surgery

Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome is an acute, life-threatening subtype of APS marked by widespread thrombosis in multiple organs [31, 32]. Its incidence is < 1% in APS, but mortality may reach 50% if untreated [33–35]. Surgery, infection or inflammation may serve as triggers in predisposed individuals [36, 37].

In this case, the diagnosis of CAPS was rigorously established according to the international consensus criteria. (1) The involvement of three or more organs, systems or tissues: The patient developed a cerebral infarction, renal and splenic infarcts, deep vein thrombosis in the lower limbs and subclavian vein, as well as signs of distal ischaemic necrosis in the extremities, fulfilling this criterion. (2) Manifestations developing simultaneously or within less than one week: The thrombotic events occurred acutely after mitral valve surgery, with new ischaemic insults and haematologic deterioration progressing rapidly over several days. (3) Histopathological confirmation of small-vessel occlusion in at least one organ or tissue: Although no postmortem was performed, the pathologic examination of the excised mitral valve confirmed sterile vegetations typical of LSE, suggesting thrombotic microangiopathy and excluding IE or malignancy. (4) Laboratory confirmation of APLAs: During hospitalisation, the patient tested weakly positive for lupus anticoagulant, while anti-Smith antibody positivity was noted historically but is not a criterion for APS or CAPS diagnosis. Therefore, lupus anticoagulant positivity during the acute phase was considered sufficient to meet this criterion. Given the fulfilment of all four criteria, the diagnosis of CAPS was established.

Thrombotic microangiopathy features, including thrombocytopaenia, anaemia, and RBC fragmentation, were present and further supported the diagnosis. Although the differential diagnosis of thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura was considered, it was ruled out based on an ADAMTS13 activity of 18.7%, which exceeded the < 10% threshold for TTP diagnosis [38–42]. It is important to note that ADAMTS13 levels between 10% and 30% may still reflect underlying TMA, however, and do not exclude other aetiologies such as CAPS. Emerging studies suggest the existence of CAPS with overlapping TMA features, where microangiopathy manifests as part of the CAPS spectrum. In this patient, follow-up echocardiography revealed hyperechoic vegetations on the tricuspid and aortic valves, associated with severe regurgitation (see Fig. 2), supporting the diagnosis of a CAPS-associated thrombotic storm with secondary TMA phenotype, rather than isolated TTP.

The patient received empirical treatment with corticosteroids, anticoagulation and rituximab, alongside supportive therapy. Despite the interventions, the patient developed progressive limb ischaemia, persistent thrombocytopaenia and worsening multi-organ dysfunction. Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome remains a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge, highlighting the importance of early APS identification, perioperative risk assessment and appropriate immunomodulatory intervention [43–57].

Lessons learned and limitations

The current case illustrates a progression from unrecognised APS to LSE and ultimately CAPS. Early autoimmune markers were overlooked, delaying treatment. The postoperative thrombotic storm may have been prevented with timely APS diagnosis and management. Although vegetations on the aortic and tricuspid valves were not seen preoperatively, they emerged post-surgery, raising the possibility of new thrombus formation or intraoperative oversight [58]. Histopathological analysis of the excised mitral valve revealed mucinous degeneration of the valve interstitium, consistent with LSE, without evidence of IE or malignancy. However, detailed histopathologic slides and further descriptions were not available, as the surgery was performed at a referring tertiary hospital. The lack of postmortem evaluation limited our understanding of the nature of lesions. Additionally, the patient’s 46XY karyotype and underdeveloped uterus suggested a rare chromosomal anomaly, possibly linked to autoimmunity, warranting further study. No autopsy was performed due to rapid disease progression, financial constraints and the family’s decision to forgo additional invasive procedures.

Conclusion

This case highlights a rare but clinically instructive progression from unrecognised APS to LSE and ultimately to CAPS following mitral valve surgery. Failure to identify APS preoperatively resulted in the absence of immunosuppressive and anticoagulant therapy, which may have contributed to the postoperative thrombotic cascade. This underscores the need for early autoimmune evaluation in patients with unexplained valvular vegetations, especially when accompanied by cerebral infarction or thrombotic events.

If APS or CAPS had been diagnosed preoperatively, timely initiation of immunomodulatory interventions such as corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin, anticoagulation or even plasma exchange could have been considered to reduce the thrombotic burden and perioperative risk. Although individual responses to these treatments may vary, early identification would have allowed for closer perioperative monitoring and risk stratification, potentially improving prognosis.

Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for APS and LSE even in the absence of classic systemic autoimmune symptoms. Ongoing clinical vigilance and accumulated experience will be essential to optimise management strategies for APS-related cardiac complications and prevent catastrophic events.
