Implementation of a multi-disciplinary antimicrobial stewardship program in a tertiary hospital in Boyacá, Colombia: a quasi-experimental evaluation of prescription quality and antibiotic use
Authors: Santiago Sanchez-Pardo, Sonia Reyes-Peña, Edna Mora-Robayo, Laura V Ramírez-Benítez, July V Mejía-Angel, María A Fernández-Rueda, Tania A Suarez-Gómez, María A Palacios-Ariza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAntimicrobial stewardship interventions are essential for optimizing antibiotic use and combating resistance. We implemented a multi-disciplinary, non-restrictive Antimicrobial Stewardship Program (ASP) at a tertiary hospital in Boyacá, Colombia.ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of the ASP on antimicrobial consumption prescription patterns.MethodsA quasi-experimental study was used to compare antimicrobial prescriptions before (June–December 2023) and after (January–July 2024) ASP implementation. Adult inpatients receiving broad-spectrum antimicrobials for ≥48 h were included in the study. Prescription appropriateness and dose/schedule deviations were evaluated through prospective audit and feedback by a multi-disciplinary team. Consumption was measured using defined daily doses per 100 patient-days. A probit regression model was used to assess the association between prescriber type and prescription appropriateness. Confounding was addressed using a directed acyclic graph to determine the minimal adjustment set.ResultsOut of 3845 prescriptions from 2491 patients, a significant improvement was observed in dosing accuracy and appropriateness of prescriptions after ASP implementation (P < 0.001). Use of amikacin and piperacillin/tazobactam decreased by 47.4% and 48.9%, respectively, while ceftriaxone use increased by 52.9%. The adjusted probit model showed that, compared with infectious disease specialists, orthopaedic surgeons were 48.7% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 36.0–61.4%) and general surgeons 33.9% (95% CI: 22.4–45.5%) less likely to issue appropriate prescriptions, after controlling for patient sex and infection type.ConclusionThe ASP improved antimicrobial prescribing and reduced use of selected broad-spectrum antibiotics. Sustained improvements may require further efforts targeting surgical services and institutional integration.Keywords: Antimicrobial stewardship, Drug prescriptions, Antibacterial agents

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a growing threat to global public health, with around 4.95 million deaths associated with resistant bacterial infections in 2019, and 1.27 million directly attributable to AMR. [1] The most pessimistic projections warn that this figure could exceed 10 million deaths per year by 2050 if effective measures are not implemented [1,2]. Its impact is particularly severe in low- and middle-income countries, where deficiencies in diagnosis, access to treatment, and epidemiological surveillance exacerbate inequalities [1,2]. Antimicrobial Stewardship Programs (ASPs) have been proven efficacious in mitigating inappropriate use of antibiotics. Meta-analyses have documented reductions of up to 28% in antibiotic consumption and a 10% decrease in unnecessary prescriptions [[3], [4], [5]]. Similarly, another meta-analysis found an 18% reduction in relative mortality risk (Relative Risk [RR]: 0.82; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.70–0.97) and a statistically significant shortening of hospitalization time [6,7]. However, evidence on mortality remains heterogeneous, with some studies reporting no significant changes [8].

Specifically in Latin America, a multi-center study in 77 intensive care units in nine countries showed that institutions with better established ASPs (≥75th percentile) achieved better adherence to clinical guidelines (92.5% vs 59.3%), lower antibiotic consumption (143.4 vs 159.4 defined daily doses (DDDs)/100 patient days), lower crude mortality (15.9% vs 17.7%), and lower incidence of infections associated with multi-drug-resistant micro-organisms (9.45 vs 10.96 per 1000 patient-days) [9]. In the Colombian context, studies have shown savings of 25–28% in the consumption of high biological value antibiotics, with significant decreases in antimicrobials such as meropenem, ceftriaxone, and vancomycin, and an accompanying reduction in healthcare-associated infections [1,10].

This quasi-experimental study, conducted in a tertiary hospital in the department of Boyacá, Colombia, aimed to measure the effect of the implementation of a multi-disciplinary ASP composed of an infectious disease specialist, a supporting general practitioner, a pharmacist, and a nurse, who performed prospective audits with feedback antimicrobial prescription patterns.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study followed a quasi-experimental, before-and-after design conducted at a single tertiary care teaching hospital with 290 beds for adult patients, including 40 intensive care unit beds. We compared antimicrobial prescriptions during a pre-intervention period (June–December 2023) with those from a postintervention period (January–July 2024). The ASP began activities in December 2023, from which point recommendations were issued on antimicrobial prescriptions, including endorsement of continued use, suspension, or dose adjustment.

Study population

The study included prescriptions of broad-spectrum antimicrobials in adult patients (aged ≥18 years) admitted to the participating centre between June 2023 and July 2024. The antimicrobials selected for this study were chosen based on the World Health Organization (WHO) AWaRe classification 2021 and local prescription rates, with additional agents included due to their high prescribing frequency.

For each patient, only one prescription per antimicrobial agent lasting at least 48 hours was included, corresponding to a single episode of infection. Prescriptions were collected regardless of the unit of origin or speciality of the prescriber. Prescriptions were excluded if the patient was discharged before the prospective audit by the ASP team, if the prescription was part of antimicrobial therapy initiated at another institution, or if the patient was transferred to another institution within 48 h of admission.

The antimicrobial stewardship program

The ASP was a non-restrictive program aligned with current guidelines and recommendations on antimicrobial use. It was implemented by a multi-disciplinary team comprising an infectious disease specialist, a supporting general practitioner, a pharmacist, and a professional nurse, each with a 1.0 full-time equivalent allocation.

The ASP conducted daily audit rounds during which recommendations were provided regarding the selection and dosing of antimicrobials for individual patients, at least 48 h after the initial prescription. Treating physicians could choose whether to implement these recommendations; disagreements were addressed in follow-up meetings between the ASP team and the treating physician. Prior to ASP implementation, prescriptions were reviewed solely by a pharmaceutical chemist.

Additional ASP activities included monthly educational sessions on AMR, quarterly in-person dissemination of hospital guidelines (complemented by promotion on the hospital website), monthly training sessions for nursing staff on the safe dispensing of antimicrobials, and monthly meetings with pharmacy managers to facilitate dispensing.

Variables

Baseline characteristics

Patient-level baseline variables included age, sex, diagnosis (infection type), prescriber speciality, and whether microbiological cultures were obtained prior to initiating antimicrobial therapy.

Primary outcome: antimicrobial prescription frequency and consumption

The primary outcome was the pattern of antimicrobial prescriptions, including the types of antimicrobials used. The number of days of antimicrobial therapy received per prescription was recorded as an outcome measure. Antimicrobial consumption was estimated monthly for each agent as DDDs per 100 patient-days, following the WHO definition of DDD as the average daily maintenance dose for a drug’s main indication in adults, according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC)/DDD Index 2024. Length of hospital stay was recorded as a secondary outcome.

The dose and dosing schedule deviations were determined retrospectively by examining Electronic health records (EHRs) and the relevant local clinical practice guidelines, which allowed us to compare their frequencies before and after ASP inception.

Descriptive characteristic of the postintervention period

Prescription appropriateness, however, was determined only for the period after ASP was implemented since this required a complete assessment by the ASP team. A prescription was considered appropriate if the ASP team endorsed continued use without modifications. Appropriateness was further defined at the prescription level by adherence to recommended dose, frequency, and route of administration according to local clinical practice guidelines for the given infection type.

All data were collected retrospectively using the participating institution’s electronic medical record and pharmacy management systems.

Statistical analysis

Qualitative variables are presented as absolute and relative frequencies. The distribution of quantitative variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test; results are reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). The unit of analysis was the individual antimicrobial prescription, except for age and sex distributions, for which individual patients served as the unit of analysis.

Differences in the distribution of diagnoses and prescriber specialities between the pre-intervention and postintervention periods were assessed using Fisher’s exact test. This test was also used to evaluate associations between ASP implementation and the likelihood of obtaining cultures prior to initiating antimicrobials, as well as the occurrence of dose and dosing schedule deviations. Differences in DOTs between periods were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Monthly DDDs per 100 patient-days were compared between the pre-intervention and postintervention periods using the Mann–Whitney U-test, and the percentage change between periods was calculated.

To assess differences in prescription appropriateness according to prescriber type, a probit regression model was fitted. A directed acyclic graph (DAG) was constructed using DAGitty (v.3.1) to identify the minimum adjustment set required to control for known confounders (Supplementary Figure 1). The final model included prescriber type, patient sex, and infection type as independent variables. Mean marginal effects are reported for each prescriber type. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses. All statistical analyses and data visualizations were performed using the R software environment for statistical computing, version 4.5.0 (R Core Team, 2025). Code development and execution were managed via the RStudio Desktop Integrated Development Environment (IDE) (Posit Team, 2025).

Results

Study population

Records were extracted from a total of 3845 antimicrobial prescriptions in 2491 unique patients between June 2023 and July 2024. The included patients had a median age of 60 years (IQR: 40–74 years), and 44.88% (N = 1118) were female. The most frequent infections associated with prescriptions were gastrointestinal infections (including biliary tract infections, appendicitis, peritonitis, and others), accounting for 19.9% (N = 767) of all prescriptions (Table I). Upper respiratory tract infections were the least frequent, accounting for 0.4% (N = 15). The observed increase in the number of prescriptions by internal medicine, critical care, and infectious disease specialities mirrored a decrease in prescriptions by general practitioners. The frequency of both dose and dosing schedule deviations decreased significantly between the periods before and after ASP inception. Dose deviations decreased from 96.1% to 3.6%, while dosing schedule deviations decreased from 96.4% to 2.5% (Table I). The median hospital stay overall was 10 days (IQR: 5–21). No statistically significant differences in length of stay were observed between the pre-intervention and postintervention periods (Table I).

Primary outcome: antimicrobial prescription frequency and consumption

Piperacillin/tazobactam was the most frequently prescribed antimicrobial agent overall, followed by cefepime (Table II). Following ASP implementation, prescription frequency declined for both agents, with piperacillin/tazobactam requests decreasing by 5.2%. This reduction in prescription frequency was accompanied by significant changes in consumption measured as DDD per 100 patient-days. The most pronounced reductions were observed for amikacin (−47.4%; P = 0.003) and piperacillin/tazobactam (−48.9%; P = 0.002) (Table III). The remaining agents showed non-significant variations: moderate reductions were observed for cefepime, vancomycin, and daptomycin; small increases for ertapenem; and relative stability for meropenem and linezolid. Notably, the use of ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, cefuroxime, intravenous fosfomycin, ganciclovir, and valganciclovir was discontinued entirely following ASP implementation. In contrast, ceftriaxone showed a significant increase in consumption of over 50% (P = 0.017), suggesting a pattern of therapeutic substitution towards narrower-spectrum agents (Table III).

Additional outcomes

Characteristics of the postintervention period – prescription appropriateness

Following the onset of the ASP, the proportion of prescriptions considered appropriate fluctuated between 39.13% (May 2024) and 52.05% (June 2024), with no discernible trend towards increasing or decreasing appropriateness over time. Adoption of ASP recommendations occurred in 95.05% of cases (N = 1862).

Infections of the upper respiratory tract and those grouped as other types showed the lowest frequency of appropriate prescriptions (41.67%), while central nervous system infections had the highest (58.97%) (Table IV). The prescribing speciality with the highest proportion of appropriate prescriptions was infectious disease, while critical care displayed the lowest at 38.99% (Table II). Using infectious disease specialists as the reference category, a probit model showed that orthopaedic surgery specialists had a 48.71% (95% CI: 36.04–61.37%) lower probability of issuing an appropriate prescription, and general surgery specialists had a 33.93% (95% CI: 22.40–45.47%) lower probability. While all specialities displayed a significantly lower probability of appropriate prescriptions compared to infectious disease, no significant differences were identified among them (Figure 1).

Discussion

The implementation of ASP is a key strategy in managing antimicrobial-resistance, increasing the quality of prescriptions, and optimizing the use of healthcare resources [4]. Evidence from low- and middle-income countries shows that these programs are feasible and effective and that their impact hinges on context-dependent factors like the availability of specialized personnel, the program’s approach (restrictive vs persuasive), and the extent of integration with other clinical services [2,9,10]. The results of this study offer a detailed overview of how a multi-disciplinary, non-restrictive and prospective auditing–based ASP modified the prescription patterns of broad-spectrum antimicrobials in a tertiary care center in Colombia.

Baseline characteristics

The age and sex distribution of the individuals included in this study is similar to the samples of prior studies in Colombia and Latin America [9,10]. Pallares et al. reported a median age of 62 years in Colombian hospitals, while Quirós et al., in a multi-centric study including 77 intensive care units in nine Latin American countries, found that older adults were the age group in which broad-spectrum antibiotics were most commonly used [9,10]. This pattern also reflects a higher comorbidity burden in this age group, which increases both the risk of infection and the complexity of its treatment. Regarding the types of infections, gastrointestinal and urinary tract infections were most common, followed by infections of the skin and soft tissue and of the lower respiratory tract. This differed from other reports of tertiary care centers in the region, in which lower respiratory tract infections were the most common [9,11]. The high proportion of gastrointestinal infections in our sample is likely a reflection of the profile of healthcare services provided by the participating centre, with a high surgical throughput and frequent management of postoperative intra-abdominal complications. Likewise, the lower frequency of lower respiratory tract infections may be related to the referral of complex cases to other centres or to seasonal variations in the incidence of these infections. Central nervous system and upper respiratory tract infections both showed a low frequency, which is consistent with national and international reports, in which these infections account for only a small percentage of the overall use of broad-spectrum antimicrobials [11,12].

Before the onset of the ASP program, general practitioners and non-infectious disease specialists accounted for the majority of prescriptions. After the implementation of the program, we observed a significant increase in the frequency of prescriptions by internal medicine, critical care, and infectious disease specialists, which mirrored a decrease in prescriptions by general practitioners. This shift is attributable to the educational component of the ASP, which encouraged general practitioners to consult specialists rather than initiate empirical therapies independently, which was one of the program’s explicit behavioural targets. Studies like that by Valderrama-Ríos et al. have shown that including an infectious disease specialist in the prospective auditing and feedback process increases the number of prescriptions reviewed and approved, which facilitates the adoption of recommendations [13].

Primary outcome: antimicrobial prescription frequency and consumption

Analysis of DDD per 100 patient days showed statistically significant reductions in amikacin and piperacillin/tazobactam use without an increase in carbapenem use. These reductions are consistent with the ASP’s auditing priorities since both agents were frequently flagged during prospective review rounds as candidates for de-escalation or discontinuation based on microbiological results and clinical evolution. The reduction in amikacin is clinically relevant, given its renal and ototoxicity profile and its association with the selection of resistant Gram-negative bacilli. This change suggests a more rational use, possibly restricted to cases with confirmed indication and microbiological support, as promoted by international guidelines and studies [14,15]. The decrease in piperacillin/tazobactam coincides with national studies such as the aforementioned study by Pallares et al., where combined auditing and educational interventions achieved sustained reductions in its use [10]. The magnitude of the reduction observed in this study exceeds the average reported in systematic reviews (20–30%), indicating a significant effect of ASP on this widely used empirical antibiotic [5].

The ASP was also associated with marked reductions in dose and dosing schedule deviations, which decreased from 96.1% to 3.6% and from 96.4% to 2.5%, respectively. These improvements likely reflect the direct corrective effect of prospective auditing, where individualized feedback on dose appropriateness represents one of the most actionable and immediate targets of stewardship interventions. Despite these changes in prescribing quality, no statistically significant difference in length of hospital stay was observed between periods, with an overall median of 10 days (IQR: 5–21) and a difference of only one day between periods. While this finding could be interpreted as a lack of clinical impact, it is more likely explained by the complexity and comorbidity burden of the patient population, the short follow-up inherent to the before-and-after design, and the well-documented lag between improvements in antimicrobial prescribing and measurable reductions in length of stay [7].

In contrast, the increase in ceftriaxone could reflect a therapeutic substitution phenomenon, where the reduction in aminoglycosides and antipseudomonal beta-lactams led to an increase in third-generation cephalosporins. A similar pattern has been documented in Costa Rica and carries the risk of favouring the proliferation of extended-spectrum Beta-lactamases-producing Enterobacteriaceae if close microbiological monitoring is not implemented [16]. Other antimicrobials, such as meropenem and ertapenem, showed no significant changes. The absence of significant reductions in carbapenem consumption is likely explained by the non-restrictive nature of this ASP. In the absence of prior authorization requirements or formal restriction mechanisms, audit recommendations regarding carbapenems appear to have had limited effect on prescribing behaviour. The non-restrictive nature of the ASP may explain this stability as the literature indicates that marked reductions in carbapenems are usually achieved with restrictive or prior authorization strategies [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]]. These findings partially coincide with the meta-analysis by Zay et al., which reported an average decrease of 28% in overall antimicrobial consumption following the implementation of ASP, although in this study, the reductions were more pronounced for certain drugs [5].

Additional outcomes

Characteristics of the postintervention period – prescription appropriateness

Probit regression analysis showed that, compared with infectious disease specialists, all other prescribers were less likely to issue appropriate requests, with particularly marked differences in orthopaedic surgery (−48.7%) and general surgery (−33.9%). This finding reflects a differential reach of the ASP’s auditing and feedback strategy across clinical services. This pattern is consistent with that described in the international literature, where multiple studies have documented that surgical specialities show lower rates of adherence to ASP recommendations [19]. In the study by Duane et al., which evaluated more than 2300 interventions from a stewardship program at a high-complexity university hospital, the overall rate of compliance with recommendations was significantly lower in surgical services (70%) than in medical services (83.5%), a difference that remained consistent for both ‘appropriate coverage’ interventions (82.1% vs 90.5%, respectively) and those aimed at reducing selective pressure (69.5% vs 81.1%, respectively) [18]. This finding suggests a consistent tendency for surgeons to more readily accept recommendations involving adding or maintaining antibiotic coverage but to show greater resistance to therapeutic reduction or de-escalation. Factors proposed to explain this lower surgical adherence include the persistence of prolonged empirical regimens due to fear of postoperative infectious complications, a heightened perception of prescriptive autonomy, and resistance to unsolicited recommendations [18].

In this study, the most pronounced gap was observed between orthopaedics and general surgery, which may reflect, in addition to the factors mentioned earlier, the nature of the conditions treated in these specialities, where there is high concern about deep infections (e.g. osteomyelitis, surgical site infections), as well as the frequent absence of early microbiological confirmation to justify a change or de-escalation. These results suggest that the non-restrictive, feedback-based approach of this ASP had limited penetration in surgical services and that targeted adaptations may be necessary to improve adherence in these groups. This behaviour is consistent with previous reports in Latin America, where surgical specialities, particularly orthopaedics and general surgery, have been identified as priority groups for educational interventions and audits focused on ASP [13]. The accumulated evidence reinforces the need to implement specific strategies to improve adherence in these services, including the participation of surgical leaders in the infection control committee, periodic feedback sessions, and adaptation of institutional guidelines to specific surgical contexts.

As the appropriateness of prescriptions was not evaluated during the pre-intervention phase, a comparison between the two phases is not possible. However, in other studies, a correlation has been identified between length of hospital stay and inappropriate antimicrobial use, with surgical teams demonstrating a higher rate of such use. This is a particularly salient finding when considering the reduced likelihood of these teams complying with recommendations to improve such use through the implementation of the ASP [18].

The Latin American literature is consistent in that the most effective ASPs combine three components: prospective auditing with feedback, continuing education, and, in certain cases, temporary restrictive measures for critical antibiotics [[9], [10], [11],13]. In this study, the model implemented included only the first two, without formal restrictive measures, which could explain why the most marked changes were limited to certain frequently used antimicrobials (e.g. amikacin, piperacillin/tazobactam) without a uniform impact across the entire spectrum. Taken together, the pattern of changes observed in this study, including selective reductions in frequently audited agents, limited impact on carbapenems, and differential adherence by speciality, is consistent with what would be expected from a non-restrictive, education- and feedback-based ASP in its early implementation phase. In other Latin American settings where selective restrictions have been applied, such as in the work of Fernández Barrantes et al., reductions covered a wider range of drugs, including carbapenems and third-generation cephalosporins, along with faster and more sustained improvements in adherence [16]. These findings reinforce the hypothesis that the structure of the ASP and its maturity are critical factors in maximizing its impact. In the case of this article, the transition to a hybrid model (integrating auditing/feedback, continuing education, and strategic time restrictions) could promote a broader and more sustained effect on the rational use of antimicrobials.

This study has several methodological strengths that increase the validity of its findings. First, the detailed characterization of requests by type of infection and by prescribing speciality provides a robust clinical framework for understanding the nature of the changes observed after the implementation of the ASP. The inclusion of pre-intervention and postintervention periods of equal duration is another strength as it reduces the influence of seasonal variations and facilitates a more accurate assessment of the temporal evolution of the indicators analysed. However, the quasi-experimental design without a control group imposes limitations on the causal attribution of the observed changes as it does not rule out the influence of concurrent external factors. The absence of an economic analysis limits the possibility of estimating the financial impact of the intervention, which is relevant in justifying the sustainability of ASPs, especially in contexts with limited resources. Similarly, the exclusion of ‘hard’ clinical outcomes, such as mortality, or incidence of multi-drug-resistant pathogen infections, limits the ability to directly link changes in prescribing to tangible benefits for patients. Finally, as this study was conducted in a single hospital, caution should be exercised when generalizing the results to other healthcare settings, considering the particularities of each institution in terms of structure, epidemiological profile, and available resources.

Future research could include multi-centre studies with controlled designs, which would strengthen the causal evidence on the impact of ASPs on prescribing quality and clinical outcomes. The incorporation of economic analyses would also be essential to determine the cost-effectiveness of these interventions, a key aspect for their sustainability in low- and middle-income countries. In addition, it would be relevant to investigate strategies that minimize undesirable phenomena, such as the initial decline in adherence to guidelines observed in this study, by exploring the behavioural and organizational determinants that cause it. Finally, the evaluation of hybrid models that combine persuasive and restrictive components could provide valuable information on their ability to generate broader and more sustainable changes in antimicrobial prescribing.

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Hospital San Rafael de Tunja (Evaluation Report 105–2024, dated 21st May 2024). Given the retrospective nature of data collection, which relied exclusively on routinely collected information from the institution’s electronic medical record and pharmacy management systems, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived. All data were handled in accordance with applicable confidentiality standards to protect patient privacy.
