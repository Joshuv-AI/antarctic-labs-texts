The application of QCT in the prognostic assessment of mCRC undergoing first-line treatment based on bevacizumab
Authors: Xudong Liu, Shusheng Wu, Wenxi Dang, Hao Shen, Mingjie Sun, Yaolin Chen, Zhihua Zhang, Mengge Li, Zhirun Cai, Haoyu Wang, Fei Gao, Yifu He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks third among the most common malignancies globally and is the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1]. For patients with advanced or recurrent metastatic colorectal cancer, the integration of targeted therapy with chemotherapy is a pivotal approach for enhancing survival and improving quality of life [2]. Currently, chemotherapy regimens that have been confirmed to be safe and effective include XELOX and mFOLFOX6, which are based on oxaliplatin, and FOLFIRI and XELIRI, which are based on irinotecan, along with other derivations [3]. Bevacizumab, an antibody that binds to and inhibits vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), serves as a first- and second-line targeted treatment option for patients with mCRC harboring RAS gene mutations. Additionally, RAS wild-type left-sided colon cancer patients who are ineligible for first-line anti-epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) antibody therapy can benefit from bevacizumab [4]. Notably, following the failure of first-line treatment, mCRC patients have a poorer prognosis after subsequent therapy. The progression-free survival in patients receiving second-line treatment is merely 4–7 months, while in patients receiving third-line treatment, it diminishes to 2–5 months [5–7]. Therefore, incorporating a broader array of potential prognostic factors and establishing a novel clinical predictive model hold significant importance in identifying the dominant population for first-line treatment.

Previous studies have indicated that bevacizumab may induce muscle atrophy by downregulating the Timp4 gene and upregulating the Cdkn1a gene according to the genomic profile of muscle tissue [8]. Muscle content, bone density, and body fat content serve as critical parameters of body composition. In recent years, numerous studies have confirmed the close association between changes in body composition during cancer treatment and treatment-related toxicity, nutritional deficiencies, and the invasive nature of cancer, ultimately significantly impacting patient prognosis [9,10]. Quantitative computed tomography (QCT) provides comprehensive, accurate, and efficient measurements of body composition.

In this retrospective study, QCT was employed to measure the lumbar vertebral bone mineral density (BMD), subcutaneous fat area (SFA), visceral fat area (VFA), and skeletal muscle index (SMI) in patients receiving first-line chemotherapy combined with bevacizumab treatment, both before and after two consecutive CT scans. Following time standardization, the study explored the predictive value of the body composition parameters ΔBMD, ΔVFA, ΔSMI, and ΔVFA for the treatment prognosis of patients receiving first-line chemotherapy combined with bevacizumab. Ultimately, a nomogram predicting the progression-free survival of metastatic colorectal cancer patients who underwent first-line chemotherapy combined with bevacizumab for 9 months was established.

Materials and methods

Patients

Data were collected from October 2017 to October 2022 from a cohort of 102 patients with metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) receiving first-line chemotherapy combined with bevacizumab at the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China. This study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 version) and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China (approval number:2024-ZNY-01). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) had pathological confirmation of colorectal cancer, complete medical records, and an uninterrupted treatment course; (2) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS)≤ 2; (3) underwent two CT scans taken within 1 month before the initiation of treatment and 1 month after treatment; (4) received a treatment regimen involving bevacizumab in combination with chemotherapy for a minimum of two cycles; (5) underwent at least one efficacy assessment during the treatment period. The exclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (1) had tumor lesions in the L1 or L2 vertebrae; (2) had concurrent primary tumors in other locations; (3) underwent abdominal surgery during the treatment period (Figure S1).

Treatments

The chemotherapy regimens administered to the patients included the CAPEOX or mFOLFOX6 protocols, which are based on oxaliplatin, and the FOLFIRI, XELIRI, and mXELIRI first-line chemotherapy protocols, which are based on irinotecan. Bevacizumab was administered intravenously at a dose of either 5 mg/kg every 2 weeks or 7.5 mg/kg every 3 weeks, depending on the specific combined chemotherapy protocol. Patients who demonstrated effective or stable disease after 4–6 months of first-line treatment progressed to the maintenance chemotherapy phase.

Scanning methods

The CT scans were conducted using the American GE Discovery 750 hD 64-slice CT, scanner covering the entire abdominal region. The scanning parameters were as follows: tube voltage of 120 kV, tube current of 250 mAs, scan layer thickness of 1.25 mm, and field of view (FOV) of 500 mm × 400 mm.

Measurement methods

The “3D spine exam analysis” function of the QCT PRO V5.0 system (Mindways, USA) was utilized to measure the bone mineral density (BMD) of the L1 and L2 vertebrae. The region of interest (ROI) was marked at the central position of the vertebra, avoiding cortical bone and vertebral venous grooves while encompassing as much trabecular bone as possible. Manual adjustments were made when necessary, and the software automatically generated the BMD (mg/cm3) for the L1 and L2 vertebrae, calculating the average (Figure 1(a,b)). The “tissue composition” function of the QCT PRO software was used to measure the total area of all skeletal muscles (psoas major, erector spinae, quadratus lumborum, transversus abdominis, external oblique, internal oblique). The thin cross-sectional images at the mid-level of the L3 vertebra were obtained as the measurement plane. The areas of skeletal muscle and fat tissue measured on transverse images at the L3 level have been validated to represent individual body composition [11]. Using predefined thresholds, the ROIs were manually outlined, and software automatically obtained the muscle area (MA) within the ROIs (Figure 1(c,d)). After normalization by the square of the height, the L3 level skeletal muscle index (SMI) was calculated as follows: SMI = muscle area/height2. The “Auto Snake” feature in the “tissue composition” function was used to automatically obtain the subcutaneous fat area (SFA) and visceral fat area (VFA) at the L3 level. BMD and MA measurements were independently performed by two senior radiologists (with 8 and 13 years of experience, respectively).

Calculation of the rate of body composition change

The body composition parameters measured before treatment in patients can be denoted as pre-BMD, pre-VFA, pre-SMI, and pre-SFA. After treatment can be denoted as post-BMD, post-VFA, post-SMI, and post-SFA. Due to variations in the time intervals between QCT scans before and after treatment among different patients, standardization was carried out based on the actual days between QCT scans following the approach of previous studies [12,13]. The change in measurement values before and after treatment was normalized by calculating the change rates for BMD, SMI, SFA, and VFA at 120 days, denoted as ΔBMD, ΔSMI, ΔSFA, and ΔVFA, respectively. Taking BMD as an example, the calculation formula was as follows: ΔBMD = (BMD after treatment – BMD before treatment) × 120/(BMD before treatment × days between QCT scans) × 100%. The same method was applied to calculate ΔSMI, ΔSFA, and ΔVFA.

Follow-up of patients

Follow-up was conducted through outpatient or inpatient medical record reviews, telephone calls, and SMS communication with patients. PFS was defined as the time from the initiation of bevacizumab treatment to the time of tumor progression or patient death for the first time. Overall Survival (OS) is defined as the period from the initiation of treatment with bevacizumab in a patient to either disease-related death or until the last follow-up date. The follow-up period extended until 1 April 2023.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0, MedCalc 20.010, and R 4.2.3. The Shapiro‒Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous data. Normally distributed continuous data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (xˉ±s), while nonnormally distributed continuous data are expressed as the median (Q1, Q3). The interclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to evaluate the consistency between the measurements of two physicians (BMD, MA), with ICC > 0.75 considered as good consistency. In cases of good consistency, data measured by the more experienced attending physician were selected for subsequent statistical analysis. Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to calculate survival rates and generate survival curves. Patients were stratified into good and poor prognosis groups based on the median PFS. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted to obtain cutoff values for ΔBMD, ΔSMI, ΔSFA, and ΔVFA. Group comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test, Mann‒Whitney U-test or independent samples t-test. Cox regression analysis was employed for univariate and multivariate analyses, with independent factors influencing the prognosis of mCRC patients receiving first-line chemotherapy and bevacizumab selected at p < 0.05. A nomogram was constructed by combining all the independent predictive factors. ROC curves were constructed, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate the predictive performance of the prognostic model. Calibration curves were plotted, and the C-index was computed to quantify the stability and predictive value of the prognostic model. Decision curve analysis (DCA) demonstrated the net benefit of the prognostic model. p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Patient characteristics

This retrospective study included 102 patients with metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC). The age of the patients ranged from 22 to 80 years, with a mean age of 59.58 ± 10.65 years. Among the patients, there were no cases of complete response (CR) (0%), 67 cases of partial response (PR) (65.7%), 21 cases of stable disease (SD) (20.6%), and 14 cases of progressive disease (PD) (13.7%). The disease control rate (DCR) was 86.3%, and the objective response rate (ORR) was 65.7%. The patients were randomly divided into a training set and a validation set at a 7:3 ratio (Table S1).

The relationship between different body composition parameters and treatment efficacy assessment

Based on the best efficacy evaluation during treatment for the 102 patients, they were categorized into the PR+SD group and PD group. Using both the Mann-Whitney U-test and independent samples t-test to compare the differences in body composition parameters between two groups. The results indicated significant differences between the PD and PR+SD groups in terms of ΔSFA (−3.27 vs. −0.12, p = 0.015), ΔVFA (9.08 vs. 1.62, p = 0.01), and ΔSMI (−4.71 vs. −1.18, p = 0.01) (Table S2). Patients in the PD group exhibited a significant loss of subcutaneous fat, a decrease in the skeletal muscle index, and a greater degree of visceral fat infiltration during treatment than did those in the PR+SD group (Figure S2).

QCT parameters

The consistency between the BMD and MA measurements taken by two physicians before and after treatment was high. The ICC values were as follows: for BMD before treatment, 0.885 (95% CI 0.790 to 0.904) and 0.808 (95% CI 0.829 to 0.922) for the two physicians; for BMD after treatment, 0.785 (95% CI 0.808 to 0.912) and 0.810 (95% CI 0.836 to 0.925) for the two physicians. A total of 102 mCRC patients who underwent first-line chemotherapy with bevacizumab were subjected to CT scans before treatment (labeled CT1) and after treatment (labeled CT2) (Table S3). After standardizing the scan time, the median values for ΔBMD, ΔSMI, ΔSFA, and ΔVFA for all patients were as follows: ΔBMD, −2.57% (−3.25%, −0.03%); ΔSMI, −2.68% (−4.19%, −0.04%); ΔSFA, −3.49% (−4.85%, 0.58%); and ΔVFA, 5.89% (−0.04%, 6.85%).

The body composition parameters’ ability to predict patient prognosis and determine optimal cutoff values

The Kaplan–Meier survival curve analysis results showed that among 72 patients in the training set, the median follow-up time was 24.63 months, the median OS was 20.97 months, and the median PFS was 8.47 months. Patients were stratified into a good prognosis group (PFS >8.47 months or OS >20.97 months) and a poor prognosis group (PFS ≤8.47 months or OS ≤20.97 months). ROC curves were constructed separately to evaluate the predictive ability of pre-treatment body composition, post-treatment body composition, and body composition change values standardized over time in predicting PFS and OS (Figure 2). The results indicated that pre-treatment body composition and post-treatment body composition had relatively low predictive value for PFS and OS, the predictive ability of time-standardized changes in body composition for OS also tends toward random events. However, the ΔSFA (AUC = 0.765, 95% CI 0.650–0.857), ΔVFA (AUC = 0.816, 95% CI 0.707–0.897), and ΔSMI (AUC = 0.803, 95% CI 0.692–0.887) demonstrate good prognostic predictive value for PFS. The cutoff values for ΔSFA, ΔVFA, and ΔSMI were −5.80%, 6.60%, and −3.12%, respectively. Patients were categorized into groups according to these criteria: ΔSFA > −5.80% and ΔSFA ≤ −5.80%, ΔVFA ≤6.60% and ΔVFA >6.60%, and ΔSMI > −3.12% and ΔSMI ≤ −3.12%.

Survival analysis for ΔSFA, ΔSMI, ΔVFA and CEA

Single-factor analysis revealed significant correlations (p < 0.05) between different PFS outcomes and baseline CEA levels; primary tumor T stage; ΔSFA > −5.80% group vs. ΔSFA ≤ −5.80% group; ΔVFA ≤6.60% group vs. ΔVFA >6.60% group; and ΔSMI > −3.12% group vs. ΔSMI ≤ −3.12% group (Table 1). Kaplan–Meier analysis further demonstrated that the ΔSFA > −5.80% group had longer PFS than did the ΔSFA ≤ −5.80% group (12.50 vs. 5.87, p < 0.01); the ΔSMI > −3.12% group had longer PFS than did the ΔSMI ≤ −3.12% group (13.03 vs. 4.07, p < 0.01); and the ΔVFA ≤6.60% group had longer PFS than did the ΔVFA >6.60% group (13.03 vs. 3.97, p < 0.01) (Figure 3).

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses

The results of the multivariate analysis indicated that the primary tumor T stage (p = 0.025, HR: 0.443, 95% CI 0.217–0.904), ΔVFA (p < 0.01, HR: 0.176, 95% CI 0.072–0.430), and ΔSMI (p < 0.01, HR: 4.631, 95% CI 2.079–10.319) were found to be independent prognostic factors for PFS (Table 1).

Nomogram

Based on the results of univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, a predictive nomogram for the prognosis of metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) patients receiving bevacizumab in combination with first-line chemotherapy was constructed. The nomogram incorporates factors such as the primary tumor T stage, ΔSMI, and ΔVFA (Figure S3).

Evaluation and validation of the prognostic nomogram

The ROC curve results indicated that the prognostic model had excellent predictive performance in both the training set and the validation set (training set AUC = 0.764, validation set AUC = 0.771) (Figure 4(a,b)). The calibration curves demonstrated good stability of the prognostic model in both the training and validation sets (Figure 4(c,d)). The prognostic model exhibited high predictive value in the training set (C-index = 0.800) and the validation set (C-index = 0.597). Decision curve analysis (DCA) for both the training and validation sets showed that the prognostic model had the maximum net benefit (Figure 4(e,f)).

Discussion

In recent years, changes in treatment-related body composition have received widespread attention as predictive indicators of chemical toxicity. Even in the absence of changes in body weight during treatment, cancer patients exhibit strong correlations between changes in body composition and prognosis [14–16]. However, there is still controversy regarding the impact of different changes in body composition on the prognosis of patients with different cancers and who are receiving different treatment regimens [17]. Patients with CRC receiving bevacizumab may experience weight and muscle loss, even in the absence of cancer progression [18]. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the value of body composition information in the prognostic assessment of mCRC patients receiving bevacizumab in combination with first-line chemotherapy.

This study retrospectively analyzed the clinical pathological features and changes in body composition before and after treatment in 102 patients with mCRC receiving bevacizumab in combination with first-line chemotherapy. After standardizing the time interval to 120 days, we observed that approximately 74% of mCRC patients experienced varying degrees of loss in skeletal muscle mass and deposition of visceral fat during the course of bevacizumab in combination with first-line chemotherapy. Adeline et al. assessed changes in muscle mass in mCRC patients receiving chemotherapy based on bevacizumab over an interval of 70 days and found that 47% of patients experienced a decrease in skeletal muscle mass [19]. In the study by I. Trestini et al. involving 91 breast cancer patients receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy, high deposition of visceral fat was associated with shorter disease-free survival [20]. These findings align with our findings. However, Adeline et al.s study included a mixed discussion on bevacizumab in combination with first-line and second-line chemotherapy, without further exploring the impact of body composition changes on patient prognosis. While I. Trestini et al. investigated the influence of changes in body composition before and after treatment on patients disease-free survival, they lacked a validation sequence and did not construct a reliable prognostic model, limiting assistance in auxiliary clinical decision-making. This study, which involved the use of a larger patient population and the use of an extended treatment period, identified changes in body composition during treatment as independent risk factors affecting the PFS of patients. In our cohort, analysis indicates that pre-treatment body composition is not a determinant of patient prognosis. However, with prolonged use of bevacizumab, patients experience varying degrees of muscle atrophy or visceral fat deposition. Following standardization at 120 days, when muscle tissue declines by more than 3.12% or visceral fat deposition exceeds 6.60%, patients undergoing first-line treatment exhibit significantly shortened PFS. Additionally, we noted that changes in body composition during first-line treatment did not significantly impact patient OS in this study. On the one hand, different patterns of progression post-first-line treatment and variations in tumor burden and subsequent treatment strategies influence patients’ final OS, resulting in non-positive statistical analyses. On the other hand, due to economic constraints, patients receive varying levels of nutritional support during the long-term cancer treatment process. Therefore, the impact of changes in body composition resulting from the use of bevacizumab during first-line treatment on patient OS is not a significant factor. Finally, the predictive model analysis, which included all the independent risk factors, generated a well-performing column chart model, indicating that QCT-based body composition analysis can serve as an effective tool for assisting in clinical decision-making. Introducing QCT body composition analysis technology based on regular CT scans enables early analysis of changes in patients’ body composition, facilitating timely interventions that are crucial for improving patient prognosis. On the one hand, body composition directly reflects patients’ nutritional status, underscoring the indispensable significance of assisting patients in adjusting their dietary structure and lifestyle. On the other hand, body composition analysis is a crucial supplement to calculations based solely on body weight in assisting with the selection of chemotherapy drug doses, demonstrating potential value.

In advanced cancer patients, a decrease in the skeletal muscle index is considered a hallmark of cachexia [21]. A decrease in the skeletal muscle index is the result of multiple factors, where reduced nutritional intake, metabolic changes (tumor burden and cancer treatment), and decreased physical activity levels can lead to alterations in body composition, ultimately resulting in the loss of cachectic muscle mass and quality [22–26]. As a major targeted therapy, bevacizumab is influenced by low muscle mass, altering pharmacokinetic parameters such as changes in distribution volume and increased drug exposure, leading to increased treatment-related toxicity and ultimately compromised therapeutic efficacy [27]. Visceral fat tissue is a metabolically active organ, not just a fat storage tissue. It can secrete or synthesize many cell factors, such as vascular endothelial growth factor, angiopoietin-2, and insulin-like growth factor-1, that promote tumor growth [28,29]. Elevated levels of vascular endothelial growth factor, a target of bevacizumab, due to high visceral fat deposition result in suboptimal treatment outcomes for mCRC patients receiving first-line therapy based on bevacizumab [30].

However, as a single-center investigation, this study constructed a nomogram based on data from a small sample. Therefore, multicenter prospective studies and external validation are needed to enhance the generalizability of the experimental findings.

In summary, our findings strongly support the substantial correlation between changes in body composition during treatment and patient prognosis. Patients with less decline in skeletal muscle index and lower visceral fat deposition during treatment tended to achieve longer survival periods. The predictive model, which combines body composition information with clinicopathological features, can aid clinicians in identifying the subset of mCRC patients who would benefit most from first-line chemotherapy based on bevacizumab.

Conclusions

T stage, ΔVFA, and the ΔSMI are independent prognostic factors influencing the PFS of patients with mCRC who underwent first-line treatment with bevacizumab in combination with chemotherapy. Post-treatment visceral fat obesity and a decrease in the skeletal muscle index suggest a poorer prognosis. The nomogram established based on T stage, ΔVFA, and the ΔSMI were effective at predicting the PFS of mCRC patients.
