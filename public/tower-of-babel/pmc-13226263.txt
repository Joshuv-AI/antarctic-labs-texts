Functionalized polysaccharides for infection-resistant and regenerative therapies in gynecology
Authors: Yingying Hao, Daming Chu, Hui Li, Lufeng Zheng, Yan Liu, Qi Xi, Sishi Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGynecological infections and implant-related complications, such as bacterial vaginosis (BV), pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), and endometritis, are exacerbated by biofilm-forming pathogens such as G. vaginalis, E. coli, and S. aureus, posing significant clinical challenges worldwide. These biofilms, resistant to antibiotics, contribute to chronic infections and device failures in gynecological implants. Rising antibiotic resistance underscores the need for innovative biomaterials. Chitosan, hyaluronic acid (HA) and alginate are three representative natural polysaccharides, showing broad application potential in antibacterial materials and tissue engineering due to their excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability and modifiable structures. In their natural forms, they exhibit a certain degree of anti-biofilm activity by inhibiting bacterial adhesion and disrupting bacterial membranes. Functional modification significantly enhances their antibacterial performance such as AMPs grafting, cross-linking, quaternization, and nanocomposites. This review explores the antibacterial mechanisms of natural polysaccharides in the vaginal microbiome and endometrial tissue, highlighting advances in functionalization with antimicrobial peptides, nanoparticles, and chemical modifications to enhance antibacterial efficacy. The toxicological effects of natural polysaccharide materials on gynecological tissues generally show the characteristics of mainly protective and restorative effects, rather than causing damage. Natural polysaccharides and their applications offer promising potential for infection-resistant, regenerative therapies in gynecology, promising safer and more effective interventions.Keywords: Polysaccharides, Antibiofilm, Gynecology, Tissue Engineering, Functionalization

Introduction

Gynecological infections and implant-related complications represent significant challenges in clinical practice, contributing to morbidity and reduced quality of life for millions of women worldwide. Bacterial vaginosis (BV), pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), and endometritis are among the most prevalent infections, often driven by biofilm-forming pathogens such as Gardnerella vaginalis, Escherichia coli, and Staphylococcus aureus (Usyk et al., 2022). The vaginal and vulvar regions of women have a unique microbial community. The microecology dominated by Lactobacillus maintains a weakly acidic environment, and this balance is crucial for preventing infections (Rönnqvist et al., 2006). Biofilms, structured communities of bacteria encased in extracellular polymeric substances, are notoriously resistant to antibiotics and host immune responses, exacerbating infection chronicity and recurrence (Fang et al., 2020). In parallel, gynecological implants, such as intrauterine devices (IUDs) and pelvic floor repair meshes, are increasingly utilized to address reproductive and structural health issues. However, these devices are prone to microbial colonization, with studies reporting infection rates ranging from 5% to 20% depending on the implant type and patient population (Cassard et al., 2025). Biofilm formation on these implants not only heightens the risk of persistent inflammation but also contributes to device failure, necessitating surgical removal in severe cases (Gao et al., 2025). The rise of antibiotic resistance further complicates treatment, underscoring the urgent need for innovative biomaterials that can prevent bacterial adhesion and biofilm development while supporting tissue repair.

Natural polysaccharides, especially those polysaccharide derivatives widely applicable for the biomedical field, have emerged as promising candidates in tissue engineering and biomedical applications due to their inherent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and tunable antimicrobial properties (Alfinaikh et al., 2025) (Table 1). Naturally derived polysaccharides exhibit unique abilities to disrupt bacterial cell membranes, inhibit biofilm formation, and reduce microbial adhesion, making them well-suited for addressing infection-related challenges in gynecology (Yuan et al., 2022). Chitosan, hyaluronic acid and sodium alginate, as natural polysaccharide biomaterials, have attracted much attention in biomedicine and other fields due to their excellent biocompatibility (Liao et al., 2026). For instance, chitosan's cationic nature enables it to interact with negatively charged bacterial surfaces, effectively combating pathogens commonly implicated in gynecological infections (Salunkhe et al., 2025). Alginate, porphyran, fucoidan, and chitin and its derivatives, which are marine polysaccharides, have recently been found to influence allergic responses by strengthening the innate immune system, modifying the Th1/Th2 balance, and suppressing IgE production (Vo and Kim, 2014). Cuttlebone, the inner shell of cuttlefish, is employed in traditional medicine in numerous countries due to its significant medicinal value in the biomedical industry (Kong et al., 2025). Cuttlebone chitosan exhibited significant superoxide radical scavenging activity at 76.1%, DPPH radical scavenging activity at 62.1%, and chelating activity at 127.5% when measured at 100 g/mL (Allapitchai et al., 2024), showing high antioxidant activity. In the context of gynecology, these polysaccharides may hold particular promise for applications such as endometrial regeneration, vaginal tissue reconstruction, and the development of infection-resistant implants. Their adaptability is further enhanced through functionalization with antimicrobial agents, such as peptides or nanoparticles, which can amplify their antibiofilm and antifouling capabilities without compromising biocompatibility (Bernal-Bayard et al., 2023). This antifouling ability of polysaccharides refers to build a physical barrier using polysaccharide skeletons to achieve passive antifouling. Meanwhile, the material is endowed with active antibacterial and smart responsiveness properties through functional modifications such as AMPs grafting, cross-linking, quaternization, and nanocomposites. Chemical grafting modification significantly broadens the functional spectrum of polysaccharides by covalently attaching molecules with specific biological activities to their skeletons. Among them, the grafting of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) is the most representative strategy (Sperandeo et al., 2020). Crosslinking strategy is the key to regulating the physical properties and degradation rate of materials. Stable hydrogel networks can be constructed by using Genipin, glutaraldehyde or ion cross-linking (Riacci et al., 2021). Quaternization modification refers to the introduction of permanent positive charge groups, such as the preparation of N,N,N-trimethyl chitosan (TMC), and the modified polysaccharides can still maintain strong cationic characteristics in neutral and even alkaline environments (Kulkarni et al., 2017). Nanocomposite technology constructs an antibacterial platform by incorporating inorganic nanoparticles like AgNPs and ZnO, or photothermal reagents into a polysaccharide matrix (Ran et al., 2017). Thus, at the molecular level, it blocks the adhesion of microorganisms to receptors and disrupts the formation of a microenvironment by biofilms, ultimately achieving long-lasting sterile protection for the surface of gynecological implants or the interface of damaged tissues.

Despite their potential, the application and toxicology of polysaccharides in gynecological contexts remains underexplored. In the context of toxicology, studies have shown that natural polysaccharides alleviate epithelial barrier damage, oxidative stress, and apoptosis through multiple mechanisms (Meng et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2019), and have become important biomaterials for the treatment of gynecological diseases. This review aims to bridge this gap by examining the role of polysaccharides as antibiofilm and antifouling agents in gynecological tissue engineering and implant design. We explore their antibacterial mechanisms in gynecologically relevant environments, such as the vaginal microbiome and endometrial tissue, and evaluate recent advances in polysaccharide functionalization. This study proposes future directions to harness polysaccharides for safer and more effective gynecological interventions.

Polysaccharides in antibacterial mechanisms for gynecological applications

Bacterial vaginosis (BV), pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), endometritis, and implant-associated complications are frequently driven by biofilm-forming pathogens, including G. vaginalis, E. coli, S. aureus and P. aeruginosa (Sousa et al., 2023). These pathogens thrive in the unique microenvironments of the vaginal tract and uterus, where biofilms exacerbate infection chronicity and resistance to conventional therapies. Chitosan has inherent antibacterial properties and can inhibit common Bacillus subtilis (Gram-positive) and E. coli (Gram-negative) (Pulvirenti et al., 2025). Alginate and hyaluronic acid have relatively weak antibacterial activity by themselves, but their antibacterial performance can be significantly enhanced through chemical modification or combination with active agents (Ding et al., 2025). This section provides a comprehensive exploration of these antibacterial mechanisms, evaluates their efficacy in gynecologically relevant environments such as the vaginal microbiome and endometrial tissue, and discusses current evidence alongside limitations, supported by quantitative data and emerging insights.

Chitosan, a deacetylated derivative of chitin, is renowned for its robust antibacterial activity, making it a cornerstone of polysaccharide-based strategies in gynecology. Natural chitosan usually does not carry a positive charge at strict physiological pH values (pH 7.4) and may even precipitate. Its cationic nature arises from protonated amino groups, which become positively charged at acidic pH, enabling strong electrostatic interactions with negatively charged bacterial cell membranes (Kuprikova et al., 2025). This interaction disrupts membrane integrity, leading to pore formation, leakage of intracellular contents such as potassium ions and proteins, and eventual cell lysis (Zhang et al., 2025a). At the molecular level, this interaction displaces stabilizing Ca2+ and Mg2+ ions on the membrane surface, increasing membrane fluidity and permeability. Quaternization modification of chitosan introduces a permanent positive charge, enabling it to maintain cationic properties at pH 7.4 or even higher, thereby preserving its antibacterial and antifouling capabilities. Studies demonstrate chitosan's efficacy against key gynecological pathogens: minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) range from 0.1 to 0.5 mg/mL for E. coli, 0.2–1 mg/mL for G. vaginalis, and 0.1–0.8 mg/mL for S. aureus, depending on molecular weight (MW, typically 50–1000 kDa) and degree of deacetylation (DD, 70–95%) (Eickelpasch et al., 2025; Wathoni et al., 2024). Higher MW and DD enhance antibacterial activity by increasing charge density, though solubility decreases with MW, posing formulation challenges (Kızılkonca et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2016). Beyond direct bactericidal effects, chitosan inhibits biofilm formation by disrupting quorum sensing, a cell-to-cell communication process mediated by autoinducers like acyl-homoserine lactones, and suppressing extracellular matrix polysaccharides and proteins production, reducing biofilm biomass by 60–80% in S. aureus models (Pasquantonio et al., 2008). This dual action is particularly valuable in chronic gynecological infections, where biofilms contribute to over 80% of cases (Liu et al., 2025a). Chitosan antibacterial gel for treating vaginitis is pushed deep into the vagina using a special syringe due to the bioadhesive properties, which can adhere to the moist mucosal surface, prolong the local retention time of the drug, and even swell or gelate in response to the pH value or temperature change.

Alginate, derived from brown algae, has relatively weak antibacterial activity by itself. Its antibacterial effect needs to rely on chemical modification or compound antibacterial agents such as silver ions, calcium ions and antimicrobial peptide to be significantly enhanced (Kapoor et al., 2025). Composed of mannuronic and guluronic acid units, alginate forms hydrogels or coatings when crosslinked with divalent cations, creating a hydrated barrier that impedes bacterial adhesion, which is the critical first step in biofilm initiation (Sun et al., 2012). This barrier reduces E. coli and S. aureus attachment to surfaces by 50–70% in vitro, as measured by colony-forming unit (CFU) counts on alginate-coated substrates (Yi et al., 2025a). While alginate lacks inherent bactericidal activity, its porous structure and high-water content enable encapsulation of secondary antimicrobial agents, such as Ag+, antibiotics metronidazole, or antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), enhancing its utility (Lozeau et al., 2020). For example, Ag+-loaded alginate hydrogels release ions at 0.1–0.5 μg/cm2/day, achieving a 2–3 log reduction in G. vaginalis CFUs over 48 h, making them suitable for gynecological implant coatings (Kong et al., 2025). Alginate's biocompatibility and tunable gelation properties further support its role in tissue engineering applications (Farshidfar et al., 2023).

Hyaluronic acid (HA), a glycosaminoglycan abundant in extracellular matrices, exhibits milder direct antibacterial effects but plays a crucial supportive role in infection control (Keil et al., 2020). HA exerts its anti-fouling effect through steric stabilization and hydration repulsion. High water-binding capacity of HA creates a hydrated layer that reduces bacterial attachment by steric hindrance and lubrication effects, decreasing S. aureus adhesion by 30–50% on HA-coated surfaces (Liu et al., 2025b). When hyaluronic acid is used alone, its antibacterial spectrum is relatively narrow, and its effect on Gram-negative bacteria is weaker than that on Gram-positive bacteria, and it lacks long-lasting bactericidal ability (Han, 2025). HA also alters the local microenvironment, lowering pH slightly through its carboxyl groups, which may enhance the efficacy of co-administered cationic agents like chitosan (Wang et al., 2025). The deacetylated groups of chitosan interact with the carboxyl groups of hyaluronic acid to form a dense network structure, effectively blocking bacterial penetration (Alkabli, 2024). The hyaluronic acid-stabilized silver nanoparticles (HA-AgNPs) have been widely used as a potential antibacterial agent. In the presence of hyaluronidase, HA is degraded, releasing silver ions and achieving responsive treatment to specific environments. Hyaluronic acid could also bind to CD44, which is highly expressed on the surface of tumor cells. As a targeted ligand, the killing effect of HA-AgNP on CD44+ tumor cells is significantly better than that on CD44− cells (Kousar et al., 2022). Intrauterine infusion and retention is the main approach for treating intrauterine adhesions (IUA) or thin endometrium.

Additionally, phosphorylated cuttlebone of Sepia pharaonis could enhance the antimicrobial properties, demonstrated by the inhibition against P. aeruginosa and E. coli, particularly on dental clinical pathogens (Subramanian et al., 2024). Another result determines that phosphorylated chitosan displays concentration-dependent antibacterial effects, varying against multiple human pathogenic bacterial strains, which suggests its potential use as an antibacterial agent (Shanmugam et al., 2016). These findings potentially provide valuable insight into how structural modification can improve the biological performance of marine-derived chitosan, particularly against pathogenic microorganisms.

Efficacy in gynecologically relevant environments

The vaginal microenvironment, characterized by an acidic pH (3.5–4.5) maintained by Lactobacillus spp. and a delicate microbial balance, presents unique challenges and opportunities for polysaccharide-based strategies. Chitosan's efficacy peaks in this acidic milieu, where its amino groups are fully protonated, increasing charge density and membrane interaction strength (Huang et al., 2022). In vitro studies show chitosan-based gels inhibit G. vaginalis biofilm formation, a primary contributor to BV, while preserving Lactobacillus viability, suggesting a selective antibacterial effect (Malli et al., 2017). This selectivity arises from Lactobacillus's thicker peptidoglycan layer and lower surface negativity, reducing chitosan's impact compared to pathogens (Pranantyo et al., 2018). Topical chitosan formulations also disrupt G. vaginalis hemolysin production, mitigating vaginal epithelial damage and supporting homeostasis (Sousa et al., 2023). However, efficacy varies with vaginal pH fluctuations, where protonation decreases, reducing MIC efficacy by 20–30% (Elmi et al., 2021). Alginate hydrogels, when loaded with metronidazole, maintain efficacy across pH 3.5–7.0, releasing the drug within 24 h, achieving a 2-log CFU reduction in G. vaginalis (Kondaveeti et al., 2018). HA complements these systems by reducing S. aureus adhesion in the vaginal mucosa, enhancing mucoadhesion and prolonging residence time of co-delivered agents (Palumbo et al., 2015) (Fig. 2).

The endometrial environment, with its nutrient-rich conditions, dynamic pH, and regenerative demands, poses distinct challenges for infection control. Chitosan's hemostatic properties combined with its antibacterial activity make it ideal for endometrial repair, such as preventing intrauterine adhesions (IUAs) or post-surgical infections (Liu et al., 2025c). In preclinical models, chitosan scaffolds reduce E. coli adhesion and inhibit biofilm formation, protecting regenerating endometrial surfaces (Omidian et al., 2024). Alginate's physical barrier effect is equally valuable; alginate-coated implants decrease S. aureus colonization in rat uterine models, with sustained Ag+ release extending protection to 14 days (Monteiro et al., 2020). HA supports endometrial regeneration by promoting epithelial cell growth and reducing S. aureus adhesion, though its efficacy wanes in high-protein environments due to competitive binding (Li et al., 2024). Dynamic conditions, such as menstrual flow or inflammation-induced pH shifts, can accelerate polysaccharide degradation, which needs crosslinking strategies for gynecological treatment (Zhao et al., 2025). Hydrogels experienced faster degradation at lower pH levels, and those with reduced cross-linking density exhibited extended gelation times and degraded more swiftly than those with increased cross-linking density (Jalalvandi and Shavandi, 2018).

Current evidence and Limitations

Extensive in vitro and preclinical evidence validates polysaccharides' antibacterial mechanisms in gynecology. Chitosan's efficacy against G. vaginalis is well-documented, with studies reporting a 3-log CFU reduction in BV models and 80% biofilm inhibition in 48 h (Araujo et al., 2021). Alginate-Ag+ systems achieve similar reductions against S. aureus and E. coli on implant surfaces, with biofilm coverage dropping from 90% to 10–15% (Puccetti et al., 2023). HA's supportive role is evident in combination therapies, enhancing chitosan's efficacy by 20–30% in P. aeruginosa models (Kapoor et al., 2025). Emerging research indicated that the synergistic effects of chitosan-HA blends reduce S. aureus CFUs by 4 logs in endometrial infection models, outperforming single-polysaccharide systems by 1–2 logs (Chernos et al., 2017).

Despite these strengths, limitations persist. Chitosan's efficacy varies with bacterial strain and pH: gram-negative bacteria like P. aeruginosa show 20–40% higher MICs (0.5–2 mg/mL) under neutral pH due to reduced protonation, limiting its standalone use in endometrial contexts (Simůnek et al., 2012). Alginate's reliance on high Ag+ doses (>50 μg/mL) increase cytotoxicity with cell viability dropping to 25% (Keshavarz et al., 2023). The weak direct antibacterial effect of HA necessitates Rose Bengal combination strategies, in which RB-HA exhibits effective antibacterial activity against Gram-positive S. aureus and Gram-negative E. coli bacteria for at least 80 days (Atrash et al., 2024). In addition, the production cost of polysaccharide materials is usually higher than that of traditional synthetic materials, but few studies have analyzed the impact of this cost difference on patient accessibility. Future research should optimize formulations for specificity, accessibility and durability.

Emerging polysaccharide combinations

Recent advancements in polysaccharide research have shifted focus toward combinations of chitosan, alginate, and HA to leverage their complementary strengths, creating synergistic systems with enhanced antibacterial and antibiofilm properties tailored for gynecological applications (Liao et al., 2026). These combinations address the limitations of individual polysaccharides such as chitosan's pH sensitivity, alginate's lack of bactericidal activity, and HA's weak direct effects, while introducing multifunctional capabilities like improved mechanical stability, prolonged activity, and selective pathogen targeting.

One promising combination is chitosan-HA blends, which integrate chitosan's bactericidal action with HA's hydration and anti-inflammatory benefits. In vitro studies show that chitosan-HA hydrogels reduce S. aureus and G. vaginalis CFUs by 4–5 logs within 48 h, surpassing chitosan alone (3 logs) by enhancing membrane disruption through a dual cationic-anionic interaction mechanism (Alkabli, 2024). The HA component reduces inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and increases mucoadhesion, making these blends ideal for vaginal topical therapies (Alfinaikh et al., 2025). In vulvar vaginal candidiasis (VVC) models, CMCS-OHA-Lp/Lr hydrogel using oxidized hyaluronic acid (OHA) and carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS) as carriers reduced vulval edema symptoms and diminished C. albicans colonies in the vagina, which helped in reducing vaginal inflammation (Zhao et al., 2025). Crosslinking with genipin or glutaraldehyde further stabilizes these blends, extending degradation time from 2 to 4 weeks to 4–6 weeks, aligning with endometrial healing timelines (Wang et al., 2011).

Chitosan-alginate composites combine chitosan's bactericidal properties with alginate's barrier function, creating robust coatings or hydrogels for implant protection and tissue engineering (Bernal-Bayard et al., 2023). The addition of AgNPs or antibiotics to chitosan-alginate hydrogels enhances efficacy, achieving a 3–4 log CFU reduction in G. vaginalis over 72 h, with a biphasic release profile (50% burst in 24 h, 40% sustained over 48 h) (Oe et al., 2023). In vaginal models, these composites maintain Lactobacillus viability (>90%) while disrupting polymicrobial biofilms (G. vaginalis and P. aeruginosa), reducing biofilm thickness by 70% compared to 40–50% for single polysaccharides (Hoang et al., 2022). Their mechanical strength and flexibility make them suitable for dynamic environments like the uterus or vagina (Cerdá-Bernad et al., 2023; Teng et al., 2025). In addition, the histopathological findings indicated that chitosan-collagen biopolymer biofilm derived from cephalopod gladius could enhance the healing process due to good biocompatibility and enhanced osteoblastic differentiation (Zhou et al., 2024), indicating its potential in gynecological healing processes.

Alginate-HA systems focus on physical and supportive roles, leveraging alginate's barrier properties and HA's regenerative capacity. These combinations, often loaded with AMPs, reduce P. aeruginosa adhesion by 70–80% and degrade biofilms by 60% within 24 h, enhancing implant safety (Pangjantuk et al., 2024). In endometrial repair, alginate-HA scaffolds with embedded ZnO nanoparticles increase angiogenesis and inhibit S. aureus colonization by 85%, offering a dual regenerative-antibacterial platform (Athamneh et al., 2025). Their high-water retention and tunable porosity (100–250 μm) support cell infiltration while reducing the risk of bacterial infections, though standalone antibacterial effects remain modest without functionalization (Shuai et al., 2021). Preclinical studies demonstrate that ternary hydrogels of reduce E. coli and S. aureus CFUs by 5 logs in 48 h and promote vaginal epithelial growth (Yuan et al., 2023). The porous structure and hydrophilicity of hydrogels simulate natural extracellular matrix (ECM), providing physical support for the adhesion and proliferation of epithelial cells. These combinations address pH variability, biofilm complexity, and durability, though scalability and cost (20–50% higher than single polysaccharides) remain challenges (Meng et al., 2021). Future research could explore bioinspired blends mimicking vaginal mucus or incorporating probiotics to restore microbial balance, enhancing their clinical relevance in gynecology.

Advances in functionalized polysaccharides for gynecological applications

While natural polysaccharides such as chitosan possess inherent antibacterial properties and alginate and HA perform antibacterial effects through chemical modification or combination with active agents, their efficacy and applicability in gynecological contexts can be significantly enhanced through functionalization (Li et al., 2026, Zang et al., 2025). By incorporating antimicrobial agents such as peptides, nanoparticles, or chemical modifications, functionalized polysaccharides achieve improved specificity, durability, and broad-spectrum activity against biofilm-forming pathogens prevalent in gynecological infections and implant-related complications (Li et al., 2025, Liao et al., 2025). This section provides an in-depth review of recent progress in polysaccharide functionalization strategies, emphasizing their applications in vaginal drug delivery systems, infection-resistant intrauterine devices (IUDs), tissue engineering constructs, and emerging therapeutic platforms. It highlights their potential to address unmet clinical needs in gynecology, supported by quantitative data and cutting-edge developments.

Functionalization strategies

Functionalization of polysaccharides typically aims to amplify their antibacterial and antibiofilm capabilities while preserving biocompatibility, a critical requirement for gynecological applications where tissue sensitivity is paramount (Table 2). One prominent approach involves conjugating antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) to polysaccharide backbones. AMPs, such as cathelicidins or defensins, exhibit potent bactericidal effects by disrupting bacterial membranes through pore formation and inhibiting quorum sensing (Muhamad et al., 2019). While the polysaccharide backbone provides mucoadhesion and sustained release via entanglement with the extracellular matrix, the grafted AMPs (e.g., LL-37) utilize their amphipathic α -helical structure to insert into the lipid bilayer. Chitosan modified with LL-37 demonstrates a synergistic effect, reducing the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) against G. vaginalis and E. coli by approximately 50% compared to unmodified chitosan (Karaki et al., 2016). This enhanced activity is attributed to the peptide's ability to target bacterial membranes with high specificity, while chitosan's cationic nature facilitates initial adhesion to pathogens. Studies also report minimal cytotoxicity to vaginal epithelial cells, positioning AMP-conjugated chitosan as a promising candidate for topical gynecological therapies, such as gels for bacterial vaginosis (BV) treatment (Li, 2025). However, challenges include the potential instability of AMPs in protease-rich environments and the complexity of conjugation processes, which require optimization for scalable production.

Another widely explored method is the incorporation of nanoparticles (NPs), such as AgNPs or ZnO, into polysaccharide matrices. AgNPs, when embedded in alginate or chitosan hydrogels, release Ag+ ions that disrupt bacterial cell walls, inhibit ATP synthesis, and induce oxidative stress, achieving broad-spectrum antibacterial effects (Liao et al., 2026). For example, while nanoparticle incorporation enhances broad-spectrum antibacterial activity and supports sustained release over several days, concerns about cytotoxicity at higher loadings may limit their clinical applicability, particularly in sensitive gynecological tissues. Based on current data, AMP-combined polysaccharides may offer a safer profile for topical vaginal applications, while nanoparticle-loaded systems might be preferable for coating implants where sustained antibacterial release is critical (Lin et al., 2013). Similarly, ZnO nanoparticles integrated into HA scaffolds enhance antibacterial efficacy against Pseudomonas aeruginosa while promoting tissue regeneration through the release of Zn2+ ions, which stimulate fibroblast proliferation (Higuchi et al., 2022). These composite materials leverage the mechanical stability and biocompatibility of polysaccharides with the potent antimicrobial properties of nanomaterials. However, high NP loadings can induce cytotoxicity, reducing vaginal epithelial cell viability, which necessitate careful dose optimization (Rosales García et al., 2025). Recent innovations of core-shell NP designs aim to mitigate toxicity by controlling ion release rates, achieving a 50% reduction in cytotoxicity while maintaining efficacy (Carbone et al., 2025).

Chemical modification, such as quaternization, represents a third functionalization strategy with significant potential in gynecology (Liu et al., 2025). Quaternized chitosan such as N,N,N-trimethyl chitosan (TMC) introduces permanent positive charges, enhancing solubility and antibacterial efficacy across a broader pH range, unlike unmodified chitosan, which loses protonation at neutral pH (Zhao et al., 2020). TMC exhibits improved performance against gram-negative bacteria like P. aeruginosa and inhibits biofilm formation by disrupting extracellular polysaccharide synthesis (Jabli et al., 2023). In the vaginal environment (pH 3.5–4.5), TMC's enhanced solubility ensures consistent antibacterial activity, making it ideal for topical formulations or implant coatings (Li et al., 2020). The retention time of quaternized chitosan on vaginal mucosa was significantly prolonged. Research shows that the positive charge density of TMC is higher than that of natural chitosan, enabling it to adhere more firmly to the negatively charged mucosal surface and thus achieve sustained drug release (Wongwanakul et al., 2023). Additionally, sulfation of HA introduces anionic groups that enhance its interaction with cationic antimicrobial agents, creating hybrid systems with dual antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects (Oh et al., 2017). While chemical modifications improve functionality, they can increase production costs and may introduce mild inflammatory responses in sensitive tissues, requiring further biocompatibility studies (Gallant et al., 2015).

Emerging strategies include stimuli-responsive functionalization, where polysaccharides are engineered to release antimicrobial agents in response to environmental triggers like pH, temperature, or light (Liu, 2026). For instance, pH-sensitive chitosan hydrogels loaded with antibiotics metronidazole release their payload at acidic pH, achieving a 3-log reduction in G. vaginalis CFUs within 24 h (Zhu et al., 2021). Photothermal-responsive systems, integrating chitosan with gold nanorods, enable on-demand bacterial eradication under near-infrared (NIR) light, reducing S. aureus biofilms by 99% with minimal thermal damage (Che et al., 2022). The chitosan prodrug connected by disulfide bonds has become a new type of antibacterial modified material (Sahu et al., 2023). The infection sites in the gynecological area usually contain abundant glutathione (GSH). This modified material may specifically break the disulfide bonds at the infection sites, releasing the drug, thereby killing bacteria while minimizing damage to healthy tissues. These advanced approaches offer precision and adaptability, addressing the dynamic conditions of gynecological environments.

Applications in gynecology

Functionalized polysaccharides have been increasingly applied to address specific gynecological challenges, with applications spanning drug delivery, implant protection, and tissue regeneration. Vaginal drug delivery systems leverage chitosan-based hydrogels for localized treatment of infections like BV. Functionalized with AMPs or antibiotics, these hydrogels provide sustained release, reducing G. vaginalis CFUs by 2–3 logs in vitro while maintaining Lactobacillus viability, thus supporting vaginal microbiome restoration (Yuan et al., 2022). Hybrid systems combining TMC with mucoadhesive polymers polycarbophil extend residence time in the vaginal mucosa, enhancing therapeutic efficacy (Porwal et al., 2022). These systems reduce systemic side effects and resistance risks, offering a patient-friendly alternative.

In gynecological implants, polysaccharide coatings enhance infection resistance for IUDs and pelvic floor meshes. Quaternized chitosan coatings on copper IUDs reduce E. coli and S. aureus adhesion in simulated uterine conditions, mitigating endometritis risk linked to copper ion release (Yu et al., 2020). Alginate-AgNP composite coatings on polypropylene meshes decrease S. aureus colonization, addressing infection rates affecting pelvic organ prolapse patients (Marin et al., 2023). Multilayered coatings, such as chitosan-HA-AgNP assemblies via layer-by-layer (LbL) techniques, provide sustained protection, with Ag+ release rates tunable to 0.1–0.5 μg/cm2/day, balancing efficacy and safety (Andreica et al., 2023). These coatings preserve implant mechanical properties and reduce revision surgeries in preclinical models.

Beyond traditional applications, functionalized polysaccharides are explored in novel therapeutic platforms. Chitosan-based nanoparticles delivering siRNA targeting bacterial virulence genes such as G. vaginalis hemolysin achieve a reduction in gene expression, offering a non-antibiotic approach to infection control (Yuan et al., 2022). HA scaffolds functionalized with growth factors and ZnO NPs promote endometrial angiogenesis and antibacterial activity simultaneously, increasing vessel density in animal models (Heiniger et al., 2024). These multifunctional systems highlight the versatility of polysaccharides in addressing complex gynecological needs (Table 3).

Advances and Future potential

Recent advances in functionalization techniques have expanded the capabilities of polysaccharides. Layer-by-layer (LbL) assembly enables precise control over coating thickness and release kinetics. LbL-assembled chitosan-HA coatings with AgNPs exhibit a biphasic release profile (Khodadadi Yazdi et al., 2024; Khan and Ahmad, 2013). Click chemistry facilitates covalent AMP attachment to polysaccharide scaffolds, enhancing stability in protease-rich environments; for example, azide-alkyne cycloaddition increases LL-37 retention by 60% in endometrial models (Visansirikul et al., 2020). Electrospinning produces nanofibrous chitosan scaffolds with embedded ZnO NPs, offering high surface area and 95% bacterial inhibition, suitable for vaginal tissue engineering (Kirschning et al., 2018; Lai et al., 2025).

Challenges remain, including cytotoxicity from high NP loadings and scalability of complex processes (Zhang et al., 2022; Teng et al., 2024). However, emerging technologies promise transformative potential. Photothermal therapy (PTT) with chitosan‑gold nanorod composites achieves 99% biofilm reduction under NIR, with localized heating (42–45 °C) safe for tissues. Enzyme immobilization, such as lysozyme on alginate, degrades G. vaginalis biofilms by 80% in 24 h, offering a targeted approach for IUD coatings (Han et al., 2022). Future research could explore smart hydrogels responsive to infection markers, releasing antimicrobials only when needed, or bioinspired functionalization mimicking vaginal mucus to enhance mucoadhesion and pathogen trapping (Shi et al., 2025). As these innovations mature, functionalized polysaccharides are poised to revolutionize gynecological infection management and implant design.

Specific Application scenarios in Gynecology

The unique antibacterial, antibiofilm, and tissue-supportive properties of polysaccharides make them highly adaptable for addressing gynecological challenges, including tissue regeneration and infection prevention. Functionalized or natural polysaccharides have been tailored for specific applications such as endometrial repair, vaginal tissue engineering, and implant improvement. Efforts are still underway to develop a superior antibacterial wound dressing that exhibits strong antibacterial properties against highly drug-resistant bacteria and possesses excellent wound-healing abilities (Alizadeh et al., 2024). This section examines these scenarios including subuterine endometrial repair, vaginal tissue engineering, and gynecological implant modification in detail, highlighting how polysaccharides contribute to improved clinical outcomes by simultaneously combating bacterial colonization and promoting healing. It integrates preclinical data, technical advancements, and clinical implications to underscore their transformative potential (Fig. 3).

Subuterine endometrial repair: polysaccharide self-healing hydrogels

Endometrial damage from intrauterine adhesions (Asherman's syndrome), miscarriage, or surgical interventions increases infection risk and impairs fertility, affecting 1.5–3% of infertile women (Cen et al., 2022). Polysaccharide-based self-healing hydrogels, such as chitosan-HA composites, offer a dual-purpose solution by providing an antibacterial barrier and supporting tissue regeneration. HA enhances hydrogel viscoelasticity and maintains a moist environment, promoting endometrial cell proliferation (Fang et al., 2020). Reversible crosslinking via Schiff base reactions enables self-repair under mechanical stress, ensuring structural integrity in the dynamic uterine environment (Cengiz, 2023).

Preclinical studies demonstrate that chitosan-HA hydrogels reduce bacterial load by 3–4 log units in infected endometrial models within 48 h and promote epithelial regeneration within 14 days, outperforming estrogen therapy (Gao et al., 2025). In rat models, these hydrogels increase endometrial thickness by 50–60% and reduce inflammation markers IL-6, aiding fertility restoration (Yuan et al., 2022). Emerging designs incorporate growth factors like VEGF, boosting angiogenesis, or pH-responsive antibiotics for targeted release in infected states (Salunkhe et al., 2025). Challenges include optimizing degradation rates and ensuring uniform crosslinking to prevent premature breakdown, necessitating advanced synthesis techniques like dynamic covalent bonding (Vo and Kim, 2014).

Vaginal tissue engineering: 3D bioprinted polysaccharide scaffolds

Congenital vaginal agenesis and vaginal atrophy require reconstructive approaches where infection control is critical for tissue integration (Vermel et al., 2021). 3D bioprinting of polysaccharide-based scaffolds, such as alginate or chitosan, enables customized, porous structures that support vaginal epithelial growth while resisting bacterial colonization (Mikos et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2025b; Kim et al., 2023). Alginate scaffolds, crosslinked with Ca2+, provide mechanical stability and can be loaded with antibiotics or AgNPs. While chitosan scaffolds exhibit superior intrinsic antibacterial activity due to their cationic nature, alginate scaffolds provide enhanced mechanical stability but rely on secondary agents like antibiotics for antibacterial efficacy. Compared to chitosan, alginate alone is not applicable as an antibiofilm agent but it offers better structural support, making it more suitable for load-bearing applications such as pelvic floor meshes. Conversely, chitosan's bioactivity makes it more appropriate for infection-prone mucosal environments like the vaginal tract. Future studies should explore combining these materials to balance mechanical strength with antibacterial effectiveness. Carrageenan has excellent thermally reversible gel properties, which makes it highly suitable for hot extrusion 3D printing (Liang et al., 2023). κ -carrageenan has a selective inhibitory effect on Gram-positive bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus. The sulfuric acid groups in its structure can disrupt the stability of bacterial cell membranes and interfere with the formation of extracellular polymers (EPS) in biological membranes (Santhamoorthy et al., 2023). Xanthan gum has a unique rigid helical structure. When it is mixed with chitosan or carrageenan for printing, it can form a polyelectrolyte complex (PEC) through electrostatic interaction (Xiong et al., 2022). This complex not only enhances the mechanical strength of the scaffold, but also increases its density, making it more difficult for bacteria to penetrate and colonize.

Advanced bioinks combine chitosan with collagen or HA to mimic the vaginal extracellular matrix (ECM), enhancing cell adhesion and antimicrobial performance (Witkin et al., 2013; Fang et al., 2025). In rabbit models, 3D-printed chitosan scaffolds implanted in vaginal defects exhibit reduced inflammation and enhanced mucosal regeneration compared to synthetic polymer controls, with no detectable bacterial colonization (Shynlova et al., 2013). Innovations like coaxial bioprinting create multilayered scaffolds with gradient properties, consisting of outer antibacterial layers and inner regenerative cores, which achieves infection resistance and tissue integration in preclinical studies (Zheng et al., 2024). Challenges include scalability (printing time 2–4 h per scaffold) and long-term biocompatibility, with some scaffolds showing 10–15% fibrosis after 8 weeks, requiring optimization of crosslinking density and degradation profiles (Feola et al., 2011).

Implant improvement: polysaccharide antifouling coatings

Gynecological implants, such as IUDs and pelvic floor repair meshes, face bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation risks, leading to endometritis or mesh erosion (Sharma et al., 2014). Polysaccharide-based antifouling coatings, such as quaternized chitosan or alginate-AgNP composites, enhance implant safety. Quaternized chitosan coatings on copper IUDs reduce E. coli and S. aureus adhesion by 85–90% in simulated uterine environments, mitigating inflammation from copper ion release (Yu et al., 2020).

Multilayered coatings, developed via LbL assembly such as chitosan-HA-AgNP, provide extended protection without compromising IUD contraceptive efficacy or mesh tensile strength (Kuprikova et al., 2025). Preclinical studies show coated IUDs reduce endometritis incidence by 40% in rat models, while coated meshes lower revision rates by 25% in rabbits (Wu et al., 2022; Gai et al., 2023). Advanced designs incorporate lysozyme into alginate coatings, degrading G. vaginalis biofilms within 24 h, or photothermal agents for NIR-triggered sterilization (Eickelpasch et al., 2025). Challenges include coating stability under shear stress and cytotoxicity from high AgNP loads, necessitating surface passivation techniques like PEGylation to reduce toxicity (Park et al., 2019).

Challenges and future perspectives

The integration of polysaccharides into gynecological applications, ranging from endometrial repair to implant coatings, offers transformative potential for combating infections and enhancing tissue regeneration. However, several challenges hinder their widespread clinical adoption, including issues related to biocompatibility, degradation control, and scalability. In a gynecological environment, vaginal epithelial cells and endometrial cells not only serve as physical barriers but also participate in immune regulation. AgNPs can induce the collapse of mitochondrial membrane, leading to apoptosis of epithelial cells, showing its potential cytotoxic effects (Chang et al., 2023). There are various glycosidases in the vagina (Tester and Al-Ghazzewi, 2018), which may accelerate the degradation of polysaccharide materials and affect their long-term stability. Studies show that some polysaccharide materials can only maintain their structural integrity for 3 to 6 months in a vaginal environment. Long-term implantation of materials may cause low-grade chronic inflammation and affect the function of surrounding tissues. For instance, some biological materials may create an acidic environment during the degradation process, which could lead to local tissue damage (Frank et al., 2014). Addressing these limitations requires innovative strategies and interdisciplinary efforts. This section evaluates the primary obstacles facing polysaccharide-based systems in gynecology and outlines future directions, such as the incorporation of advanced technologies like photothermal therapy and enzyme immobilization, to unlock their full therapeutic potential (Fig. 4).

Current challenges

Biocompatibility and Cytotoxicity: While most natural polysaccharides like chitosan, alginate, and HA are biocompatible, functionalization with antimicrobial agents such as AgNPs or AMPs can introduce cytotoxicity risks. High AgNP loadings, for instance, have been shown to induce oxidative stress and apoptosis in vaginal epithelial cells. Similarly, quaternized chitosan derivatives, while effective against bacteria, may trigger mild inflammatory responses in sensitive endometrial tissues (Teotia et al., 2023; Xiang et al., 2025). Balancing antibacterial efficacy with host cell safety remains a critical challenge, particularly in the delicate gynecological microenvironment.

Degradation Control: The in vivo stability of polysaccharides is a double-edged sword. Rapid degradation can compromise their antibacterial and structural functions, while overly slow degradation may lead to foreign body reactions. For example, chitosan hydrogels degrade within 2–4 weeks in vivo via enzymatic hydrolysis, often outpacing endometrial healing timelines, resulting in diminished infection protection. Conversely, alginate scaffolds crosslinked with divalent cations may persist too long, potentially causing fibrosis in vaginal tissue engineering applications (Sergeeva et al., 2019; Ma et al., 2024). Tailoring degradation rates to match specific gynecological healing processes is essential but technically complex.

Scalability and Cost-Effectiveness: The production of functionalized polysaccharide systems such as 3D bioprinted scaffolds or multilayered coatings often involves sophisticated techniques of click chemistry or layer-by-layer assembly, which are costly and difficult to scale. Although click chemistry can achieve precise connection of polysaccharide molecules, it requires strict control of reaction conditions of temperature, pH value, and catalyst concentration, and the operation window is narrow. For instance, in the copper-catalyzed azine-alkyne cycloaddition reaction, the concentration of copper ions needs to be precisely controlled (Testa et al., 2018). Too high level will lead to cytotoxicity, while the low level induces the incomplete reaction. The construction of multi-layer coatings requires the repeated and alternating deposition of polysaccharide layers with opposite charges. The thickness of each layer needs to be controlled at the nanometer level, and the requirements for solution concentration, deposition time and cleaning steps are extremely strict. Even a slight deviation can lead to structural defects (Borges et al., 2024). For instance, the synthesis of AMP-conjugated chitosan requires multiple purification steps, increasing costs by 50–100% compared to unmodified polysaccharides (Xie et al., 2020). These economic barriers limit accessibility, particularly for widespread use in gynecological implants like IUDs or meshes, where cost-effectiveness is a key consideration for healthcare systems.

Future perspectives

Advanced Functionalization Strategies: Combining polysaccharides with emerging technologies could overcome current limitations. Photothermal therapy (PTT), for example, integrates polysaccharides with near-infrared (NIR)-responsive nanoparticles, enabling on-demand bacterial eradication (Wang et al., 2025). A chitosan‑gold nanorod composite has demonstrated a 99% reduction in S. aureus biofilms upon NIR activation, with minimal thermal damage to surrounding tissues (Nirmal et al., 2024). This approach could enhance the precision of infection control in endometrial or vaginal applications, reducing reliance on high antimicrobial loadings.

Enzyme Immobilization: Immobilizing enzymes like lysozyme or DNase on polysaccharide surfaces offers a targeted strategy for biofilm degradation (Wu et al., 2026). Lysozyme-conjugated chitosan coatings have been shown to degrade G. vaginalis biofilms by 70% within 24 h in vitro, outperforming free enzyme treatments (Lotfali and Meshkini, 2021). This technique could be particularly valuable for IUDs or pelvic meshes, where persistent biofilms drive chronic infections. Optimizing enzyme stability and activity in the gynecological environment remains a priority for future research.

Personalized and Sustainable Solutions: Advances in 3D bioprinting and material design pave the way for patient-specific polysaccharide constructs. Tailoring scaffold porosity or coating thickness to individual anatomical and microbial profiles could improve outcomes in vaginal reconstruction or endometrial repair (Zheng et al., 2024). The pelvic anatomical data of the patient is obtained through 3D vaginal ultrasound, MRI or CT scans, and an accurate three-dimensional model is constructed to identify key structures such as the thickness of the vaginal wall, the thickness of the endometrial layer, and the distribution of blood vessels. For example, the research team used porcine vaginal acellular matrix (dECM) combined with bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) to construct 3D printed scaffolds, and customized the shape and pore structure of the scaffolds based on the patients' MRI data. Three months after implantation, the thickness of the patient's vaginal epithelium increased by 40%, and the elasticity returned to 85% of the normal level (Yi et al., 2025b). Additionally, leveraging abundant, renewable polysaccharide sources and green synthesis methods could enhance sustainability and reduce production costs, aligning with global healthcare goals.

Clinical Translation: Despite promising preclinical results, the lack of large-scale clinical trials remains a bottleneck. Future efforts should prioritize in vivo studies to validate the long-term safety and efficacy of polysaccharide-based systems in gynecological settings. For instance, randomized controlled trials comparing chitosan-coated IUDs to standard devices could quantify reductions in endometritis rates, providing the evidence needed for regulatory approval (Zhang et al., 2025c). Collaborative initiatives between material scientists, gynecologists, and industry partners will be crucial to bridge this gap.

Outlook

Through this review, we summarized the antibacterial mechanisms in gynecologically relevant environments, such as the vaginal microbiome and endometrial tissue, and evaluate recent advances in polysaccharide functionalization. However, the challenges of biocompatibility, degradation, and scalability are significant but not insurmountable due to limited clinical study. By integrating cutting-edge technologies and refining polysaccharide formulations, these materials can evolve into next-generation solutions for gynecological care. As research progresses, polysaccharides are poised to redefine standards in infection prevention and tissue engineering, offering safer and more effective options for women's health.
