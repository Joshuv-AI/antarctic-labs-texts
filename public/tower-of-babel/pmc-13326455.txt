Single-cell genomic analysis of cancer cells from one treatment-naïve patient with metastatic prostate cancer

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProstate cancer is among the most prevalent malignancies in men and a leading cause of cancer mortality worldwide. While localized prostate cancer is often curable, progression to metastatic and castration-resistant disease either in lymph nodes or bone/bone marrow remains the major cause of death. Understanding the genomic events that drive metastasis—particularly in treatment-naïve patients—is critical to improving early detection and individualized therapy. Bulk tumor sequencing has revealed key mutational signatures but cannot resolve the cellular heterogeneity and clonal dynamics underlying metastatic spread. Single-cell genomic approaches now enable high-resolution dissection of tumor evolution, uncovering the diversity of cancer clones across disease sites.ResultsWe performed whole-genome and whole-exome sequencing on single cancer cells from a treatment-naïve patient with metastatic prostate cancer, isolating cells from the primary tumor, circulating tumor cells (CTCs), disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) in bone marrow, and metastatic bone lesions. Copy number aberrations (CNAs) and single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) were characterized to define genomic heterogeneity and infer clonal relationships. Frequent monoallelic losses in tumor suppressors (PTEN, TP53, FOXO4, STAG2) and gains in oncogenes (MTOR, RAF1, HRAS) and an angiogenic growth factor (VEGFB), were observed. Metastatic cells displayed fewer genomic alterations than CTCs or DTCs. While this observation is consistent with the hypothesis that metastatic competence may be associated with relative genomic stability, normal cell contamination of the metastatic biopsy cannot be excluded, and this interpretation should be considered preliminary. Clonal evolution analysis revealed a complex branching pattern consistent with multidirectional dissemination, suggesting bidirectional seeding between the primary tumor, circulation, and metastatic sites as one possible model of spread, though alternative explanations including phylogenetic reconstruction artefacts cannot be excluded from a single-patient study.ConclusionsThis study provides a single-cell genomic map of metastatic prostate cancer from a treatment-naïve patient, highlighting the coexistence of diverse subclones across disease sites and supporting a multidirectional model of cancer spread.These findings raise the hypothesis that metastatic progression can emerge from multiple subclones with distinct CNA and SNV profiles. Background

Prostate cancer shows striking genomic and phenotypic heterogeneity, both within individual tumors and across patients [1, 2]. Although localized disease is often indolent, metastatic progression is responsible for nearly all prostate cancer–related deaths [3–9]. The transition from localized to metastatic disease involves complex evolutionary processes shaped by clonal selection, genetic instability, and microenvironmental pressures [10]. Advances in genomic technologies have identified recurrent alterations in PTEN, TP53, RB1, SPOP, and FOXA1, among others, which contribute to treatment resistance and disease progression [11–17]. However, most studies rely on bulk sequencing, which masks intratumoral heterogeneity and limits understanding of the cellular origins of metastasis.

Single-cell sequencing provides a transformative approach to studying tumor evolution and has confirmed that tumor cells arise and perpetuate via Darwinian evolution, resulting in a heterogeneous polyclonal population [18]. By capturing genomic and transcriptomic variation at the level of individual cells, single-cell genomics enables precise reconstruction of clonal architecture, evolutionary trajectories, and mechanisms of metastatic dissemination [19, 20]. Tumor heterogeneity also contributes to the selection of treatment-induced drug-resistant phenotypes [21]. These insights are particularly valuable in treatment-naïve patients, where mutational patterns reflect natural disease evolution without therapy-induced selection pressures.

Metastasis is a complex cascade of events where cancer cells spread to distal sites in the body, resulting in high levels of patient morbidity [3–9]⁠⁠⁠. Metastatic cancer cells detach from the solid tumor, intravasate into blood vessels, and spread through circulation as circulating tumor cells (CTCs) [22, 23]. Alternatively, cancer cells from prostate tumors can cross the prostate basal cell layer, immerse in interstitial fluid, and enter the lymphatic system as CTCs [24]. CTCs can also lay dormant, referred to as disseminated tumor cells (DTCs), and have been found in bone marrow ⁠ [25]. While most CTCs are eliminated by immune surveillance or fail to establish new colonies, a small subset survives to seed secondary sites [26, 27]. Therefore, DTCs and CTCs are intermediates linking the prostate cancer primary tumor and metastatic lesions, and metastasis in prostate cancer primarily occurs in the bone and lymph nodes ⁠ [22–25, 28]. The mechanisms enabling these cells to persist and colonize remain poorly defined. By characterizing the genomes of individual tumor, circulating, and metastatic cells, we can better understand the genomic events driving dissemination and identify early clones that give rise to incurable metastases.

In this study, we used single-cell whole-genome and whole-exome sequencing to characterize genomic variation in cancer cells from a treatment-naïve patient with advanced metastatic prostate cancer. We identified copy number aberrations (CNAs) and single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) across primary, circulating, disseminated, and metastatic tumor cells and reconstructed their clonal evolution. Our data reveal a multidirectional model of tumor spread, highlighting the genomic diversity and interconnectivity between tumor sites.

Methods

Patient recruitment and sample collection

We recruited prostate cancer patients who presented with metastatic disease at the Cross Cancer Institute and Northern Alberta Urology Centre in Edmonton, Alberta, Canada, for two and a half years using informed consent protocols approved by the Health Research Ethics Board of Alberta (HREBA) Cancer Committee (HREBA.CC-26199), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Blood and bone marrow samples were collected when the patient underwent a bone biopsy.

Cancer cell isolation and genome amplification

For the primary tumor, formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections from prostate biopsies, previously assessed by a clinical pathologist, were obtained from the Cross-Cancer Institute. The samples were further sectioned into 10-micron-thick longitudinal sections using a microtome, stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and analyzed by microscopy. The Gleason score was then determined. We collected eighteen core punched sections with Gleason scores ranging from 4 to 5 and stored them at -80 °C until DNA extraction.

We used a CELLSEARCH® instrument (Menarini Silicon Biosystems, Bryn Athyn, Pennsylvania) to analyze single-cell suspensions of CTCs from blood, DTCs from bone marrow, and Mets from a pelvic metastatic lesion. Positive controls were paramagnetic bead-conjugated antibodies against cytokeratin and EpCAM, and an anti-CD45 antibody as a negative control. The CELLSEARCH instrument is intended for whole-blood samples. Therefore, to use bone marrow samples in the CELLSEARCH protocol, we isolated nucleated cells from bone marrow aspirates via a sucrose gradient. For metastatic biopsies, tumor cells were dissociated with a cocktail of collagenases. In brief, the tissues were washed in BHSS buffer containing Ca and Mg and then incubated in 10 volumes of dissociation buffer containing 1 mg/ml collagenase I (Gibco 17100-017) and 0.5 mg/ml collagenase IV (Gibco 17104-019) for two hours at 37 °C, after which the suspension was mixed every 30 min by vortexing. The cell suspension was washed with BHSS buffer and recovered by centrifugation. After CELLSEARCH enumeration of the cancer cells, the cells trapped in the cartridges were recovered by pipetting and inspected under an inverted fluorescence microscope. The nucleated cells (DAPI positive) exhibited a positive signal for the phycoerythrin-conjugated anti-cytokeratin antibody. Cells were identified as cancer cells if they had no signal for the allophycocyanin-conjugated anti-CD45 antibody. Conversely, nucleated cells exhibited a positive signal for CD45. Cells were considered noncancerous (control) if they showed no signal for the anti-cytokeratin antibody. The cells were sorted as singletons or in pools of ten cells to assess the uniformity of the amplification.

We used the Ampli1 method for genome amplification, which is deterministic and may have fewer amplification biases than multi-displacement amplification [29]. Genomic DNA was digested with the Mse I restriction enzyme, which generated 100–200 bp fragments ligated to linkers with the protruding ends (TAs) left by the restriction enzyme. Linker-ligated fragments were then PCR-amplified. A genome integrity index (GII) was defined based on the number of target fragments amplified [19]. The short length of the digested genomic fragments warrants a quasilinear, uniform amplification process (see Fig. 1C4 and Fig. 3C).

Whole-genome sequencing libraries

FFPE sections of the primary tumor were deparaffinized with QIAGEN deparaffinization solution, and the DNA was extracted with a QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (QIAGEN). One nanogram of genomic DNA extracted from primary tumor samples or amplified from single cells was used to construct whole-genome libraries using the NexteraXT DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina). After quality control, we sequenced the libraries using the NextSeq 500 instrument following a 75-cycle paired-end protocol at an average depth of 5x.

Whole-exome libraries

We constructed libraries from primary tumor biopsy FFPE sections using the Accel-NGS 2 S Hyb DNA Library Kit (Swift Biosciences). The SureSelect XT Target Enrichment System for Illumina Paired-End Multiplexed Sequencing Library (Agilent) was used to construct libraries from individual cells. Libraries were then captured either with the Agilent SureSelect XT Target Enrichment System for Illumina Paired-End Multiplexed Sequencing Library and a probe array covering the entire human exome and the UTRs or with the MGIEasy Exome Capture V4 Probe Set (MGI, China). Before the analyses, both capture array coordinates were consolidated in a single BED file, and only regions assessed by the two arrays were considered for analysis. Exome libraries were sequenced on a HiSeq-2500 instrument (Illumina) using a 150 bp paired-end protocol or a DNBseq-650 instrument (MGI, China) following a 100 bp paired-end sequencing workflow.

Bioinformatics analysis

The quality of the sequences was inspected, and when needed, bases with a quality score (Q) < 30 were trimmed. Ampli1 adapters were trimmed with in-house scripts. A preprocessing workflow recommended by the Genome Analysis ToolKit (GATK) best practices (Broad Institute) was used [30]. All reference files used were from the GATK resource bundle, which includes 38 assemblies of the human genome [31]. High-quality sequences were aligned to the reference genome GRCh38 with the BWA MEM algorithm, duplicates were marked, and base quality scores were recalibrated [32]. Single-nucleotide somatic variants and short indels in the primary tumor were called using the Mutect2 algorithm of GATK (v4.0.6.0), and a library derived from peripheral blood mononuclear cells was used as a proxy for the germline. For single cells, SNVs were detected with SCIΦ (v0.1.4) software [33] to control the germline bulk sequences and two lymphocyte cell lines from the same patient. 5x WGS was used solely for CNA detection and that SNVs were called from independent deep WES data. All libraries were processed through the same GATK best-practices preprocessing pipeline (quality trimming, BWA MEM alignment, duplicate marking, and base quality score recalibration), which normalises read-level quality differences across instruments.

Clonal evolution

Clonal evolution analyses were conducted with the software OncoNEM (v1.0) [34]⁠. Initially, the false positive and false negative rates were determined as recommended by the OncoNEM authors, and they were used to construct and plot a clonal evolutionary tree. The results were validated with the software SiFit [35]⁠.

Copy number aberrations

The sequences were preprocessed as described above. CNAs in the primary tumor were identified with CNVkit (v0.9.6) using default parameters [36]. A germline library was used as a negative control. Libraries derived from two noncancer lymphocytes from the same patient were used as negative controls for identifying CNAs with CNVkit software [36]. For single cells, CNAs were also labeled with the CNVkit, and the results were validated with Ginkgo software [37]⁠. In all the cases, the same assembly of the human genome was used [38]. Figure S1 shows the methods as a schematic diagram.

Results

One treatment-naïve patient (identified as SCG003) with metastatic disease, high prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels, and high CTC counts was selected for cancer cell genomics analysis in the absence of selection pressure from drug therapy (Fig. 1A). We sequenced CTCs from blood, DTCs from bone marrow, and individual metastatic cancer cells (Met) from a lytic pelvic bone lesion. We used a stringent single-cell purification protocol described by Polzer et al. [29] and depicted in Fig. 1C to ensure that the isolated cells were cancer cells and their genomes uniformly amplified⁠. Individual noncancerous lymphocyte cells were also isolated using the same protocol and used as the negative control for genomic analyses. Briefly, the single cell purification protocol combines the CELLSEARCH system for CTC enrichment with an automated system for precise isolation of single cells, followed by a non-random whole-genome amplification (Ampli1™ WGA). Polzer et al., also defined a “genome integrity index” (GII) to pre-screen amplified single-cell DNA, allowing for the identification of high-quality samples suitable for single-cell analysis of CTCs provide high-quality genomic information.

Individual noncancerous lymphocyte cells were also isolated using the same stringent protocol and used as the negative control for genomic analyses. After quality control, we obtained 212 Met cells, 47 DTCs, and 12 CTCs (Fig. 1D). We also sampled and bulk-sequenced sections of the primary prostate tumor (Fig. 1B) and conducted low-pass whole-genome sequencing (WGS) to analyze copy number aberrations (CNAs) and deep whole-exome sequencing (WES) to detect single-nucleotide variants (SNVs). The libraries produced during this study are described in Supplementary Tables S1-S4.

Primary tumor: copy number aberrations

Eighteen sections of the primary tumor were sequenced separately for CNA detection at an average depth of 5x (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Table S1). Sixteen sections exhibited cancerous histology (Fig. 1B; right panel), while two sections (S09 and S10) exhibited normal histology (Fig. 1B; left panel) with fewer CNAs (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. S2). Although hierarchical clustering distinguished three different clusters, the genetic distances were short since sections S09 and S10 branched apart from the other samples closer to the root (Fig. 2A). Supplementary Fig. S3 shows the frequency of CNA events per chromosome in each section of the primary tumor.

The remaining samples had varying numbers of CNA events and exhibited a cancer-like histological phenotype. The scale bar on the right represents the CNA values on a log2 scale. (B) CNA event mutations in several tumor suppressor genes and oncogenes in different primary tumor sections. The arbitrary color scale denotes mono- or hemizygous loss (gray) or monoallelic amplifications (turquoise). (C) Relative abundance of SNV types found in the primary tumor. We mapped copy number abberations affecting each primary tumor section and identified ten tumor suppressor genes (TSGs) and ten oncogenes (Fig. 2B). Most primary tumor sections had biallelic or monoallelic deletions of the phosphatase and tensin homolog gene (PTEN), the breast cancer type 1 susceptibility gene (BRCA1), or the tumor suppressor protein (TP53). All the samples had hemizygous loss of MTCP1 and of TSGs, including the genes FOXO4, FOXP3, RBM10, and STAG2. Conversely, many oncogenes gained an additional allele in most primary tumor sections. These included the vascular endothelial growth factor VEGFB, the serine/threonine protein kinase MTOR, the proto-oncogene BRAF, and the mucin glycoprotein MUC16, among others. Sections S09 and S10, identified as noncancerous by histology, exhibited the opposite trend compared to that of the majority of the primary tumor sections (Fig. 2B). The Catalog of Somatic Mutations in Cancer (COSMIC) included all the genes in Fig. 2B [39]⁠. In Supplementary Fig. S4, given their reported relevance in prostate cancer⁠, monoallelic and biallelic alterations of genes in the PI3K, cell cycle, and DNA repair pathways are presented [40]. Supplementary Table S5 shows the copy number status of all COSMIC genes with CNAs in primary tumor samples.

Primary tumor: single-nucleotide variants

We used the GATK pipeline to call SNVs in the WES of 16 primary tumor sections and a library derived from peripheral blood mononuclear cells of the same patient as a control. The Mutect2 method identified 539 short indel deletions or insertions and 2258 SNVs (Supplementary Table S6). The average number of SNVs in the primary tumor was 0.75 mutations/Mbp. The most common substitutions were G > A (16.8%), C > T (15.4%), G > T (15.4%), and C > A (14.5%). Although any mutation has the potential to affect the biology of cancer cells, more relevant mutations were assumed to include missense variants, mature miRNA variants, nonsense-mediated decay transcript variants, and frameshift variants, which together resulted in 489 mutations (Fig. 2C). We plotted mutations in all primary tumor sections side-by-side to determine the genomic locations of the mutations in each section. (Fig. 2D). Out of all the samples, S10 had the lowest number of CNAs but the highest number of SNV mutations. Since S10 showed normal histology, SNVs may not be a major driver of the prostate cancer phenotype.

Among the missense variants, we found mutations in 13 cancer-related genes listed in the COSMIC database [39]⁠ within different primary tumor sections (Fig. 2E). These included the methyltransferase KMT2A, the Rho GTPase ARHGAP26, the transcription factor RUNX1T1, and the transcriptional repressor SPEN genes. Most of those gene mutations were detected in one tumor section only, except for the FAT3 mutation, which was detected in two primary tumor sections. All the sections, except for S09 and S10, had mutations in BIRC6 and CTNND2. BIRC6 (BRUCE) encodes an inhibitor of apoptosis [41]⁠ and has been found to play a role in diverse cancers [42–44]⁠. CTNND2 encodes a δ-Catenin that is often overexpressed in prostate cancer and other cancers [38, 45]⁠. Further research is needed to determine if the mutations found in the coding regions of BIRC6 and CTNND2 have an oncogenic effect.

Other genes with potential to contribute to cancer onset and progression including the MUCIN17 gene (MUC17) with mutations at 12 different loci in seven tumor sections are listed in Fig. S4. Mutations in the CNTNAP4 gene, which encodes Contactin-associated-protein-like-4, a cell-adhesion molecule, and the CPA4 gene, which encodes a carboxypeptidase protein associated with hyperacetylation, were detected in 14 primary tumor sections. Carboxypeptidase mutants have been linked to a high risk of prostate cancer [46]⁠.

Single-cell copy number aberrations

We conducted WGS on 119 individual cancer cells, at an approximate depth of 5x, and 105 passed quality control. Two lymphocyte cell types (cytokeratin-negative and CD45-positive) were used as normal controls for detecting CNAs and SNVs. For consistency with the primary tumor data, we called the CNAs using the CNVkit software [36]⁠. Two major clusters were identified using the complete linkage method (Fig. 3A). The first cluster (lower half of the heatmap) included cells with frequent copy number variation events and 71% DTCs (30 out of 42), the CTCs that passed quality control (7 out of 7), and 11% of the metastatic cells (Mets; 6 out of 54). The second cluster (upper half of the heatmap) was much more uniform, contained very few CNA events, and included 29% of the DTCs (12 out of 42) and 89% of the Mets (48 out of 54). Twenty-five Mets and one DTC located in the upper cluster were also deep-sequenced for SNV calling (marked with black dots on dendrogram leaves), and multiple mutations were identified in all the cells. The high frequency of SNVs provided evidence that these cells were cancer cells, even though the frequency of CNAs was low. These results were validated using the software Ginkgo [37]⁠. The CNVkit and Ginkgo software default parameter settings identify CNAs using a circular binary segmentation algorithm. In addition, principal component analysis (PCA) of the CNA profiles revealed similar spatial distributions (Supplementary Fig. S6). Notably, when we included the two control lymphocytes, they clustered together with the larger cluster of Met single cells with few CNAs (black dots in Supplementary Fig. S6A). This finding suggested that the metastatic cell CNA profile was similar to that of normal, noncancer cells, with a much lower frequency of CNAs than circulating or disseminated tumor cells (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Fig. S6).

CNAs were associated with cancer-related genes in single cancer cells (Supplementary Table S7). Some of these genes had hemizygous loss events, while others were amplified by gaining one (highest frequency), two, or three or more copies (lowest frequency). Supplementary Fig. S7-S9 show representative ideograms for CTCs, DTCs, and Mets. Supplementary Fig. S10 shows the CNA number per chromosome and per sample.

When focusing on COSMIC cancer-related genes [39]⁠, almost all single cells, except for Met cells 217 and 218, exhibited hemizygous loss of MTCP1 and the TSGs FOXO, FOXP3, STAG2, PHF6, and ATRX (Fig. 3F). Except for the six Met cells exhibiting high CNA events, all of the Met cells exhibited monoallelic deletion of the TSGs TP53, BRCA1, and BRCA2, which may represent a less suppressive condition for cancer cell proliferation than wild-type TSGs. Conversely, amplification of known oncogenes was also observed; for example, the oncogenes ETV1, MACC1, and HOXA13 gained an additional copy in almost all cancer cells. Amplification of the genes VEGFB, MTOR, RAF1, MAP2K2, HRAS, and JAK1 was more variable across cancer cells, with Met cells showing more amplification events than CTCs or DTCs. As an outlier, the transducin (beta)-like 1X related protein-1 gene (TBL1XR1), which is upregulated in many solid tumors and associated with metastasis and poor prognosis [47], was massively amplified in some of the cells. For instance, on average, 17 copies of TBL1XR1 were detected for all seven CTCs analyzed for CNA. Three DTCs and six Met cells with high CNA events gained 19 extra copies of TBL1XR1. Thirty-six other cells gained one additional copy of TBL1XR1, while 12 other Met cells and most DTCs lost one copy. CNAs dysregulated genes in the PI3K, cell cycle, and DNA repair pathways (Supplementary Fig. S11).

Hierarchical clustering of CNA profiles in the primary tumor sections and single cells showed that the primary tumor samples were closely related to the single-cell samples with abundant CNA. However, the primary tumor samples and single-cell profiles differed, suggesting independent evolution, although this hypothesis requires further experimentation (Supplementary Fig. S12).

Single-cell single-nucleotide variants

We deep-sequenced the whole exome of individual cells (34 DTCs, 25 Mets, 12 CTCs, and two control lymphocytes) at depths ranging from ~ 300x to 1000x. Initially, we conducted an autocorrelation analysis to assess the uniformity of exome library amplification, as described by Lohr et al., [48]⁠. This method excludes libraries that had the logarithm of (1 / autocorrelation coefficient) lower than − 1.8.

All the analyzed cells passed quality control, with autocorrelation coefficients similar to those of the germline bulk-sequenced sample and noncancer cultured cells (F12878) or lymphocytes (Fig. 3B). We then generated eight samples by pooling ten cells and showed that these pooled samples had similar autocorrelation coefficients. Thus, the genome amplification of our samples was uniform.

We used SCIΦ software to call SNVs in single cells [33]. SCIΦ allows bulky sequenced DNA and individual noncancer cells to be used as negative controls. We detected a total of 3,618 mutations (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Table S8), with a predominance of C > T (17.8%), G > A (17.5%), C > A (12.5%), and G > T (12.2%) substitutions. When we compared mutations among different types of cancer cells, 710 mutations were common to all cell types, 67 mutations were shared exclusively for DTCs and Mets, and 53 were shared exclusively for CTCs and Mets; however, no mutations were shared exclusively between DTCs and CTCs (Fig. 3D). We observed a similar profile when comparing the abundance of potentially relevant (missense and nonsense-mediated mRNA decay variants) mutations among cell types (Fig. 3E). Thus, the mutational landscape was more similar between DTCs and Mets and more distant from CTCs. Although the average numbers of mutations in CTCs, DTCs, and Mets were similar, the mutational diversity in CTCs was much greater than that in DTCs, and Mets, which harbored an average of 181, 52, and 71 different mutations per cell, respectively.

The distribution of the 30 high-frequency mutations did not show any cell type specificity (Supplementary Fig. S13A). Missense mutations were detected in eleven COSMIC genes (Fig. 3G). These genes were absent in Met cells, except for BAZ1A and ZNF429. BAZ1A is a chromatin remodeling factor involved in DNA repair [49]⁠, while ZNF429 is a zinc finger protein involved in transcription regulation. Other mutations that may be associated with cancer, given the function of the encoded proteins, are presented in Supplementary Fig. S13B but were also absent in most Met cells. Supplementary Fig. S14 and S15 show mutations detected in individual cells or only missense mutations.

Clonal evolution

We used the oncogenetic nested effects model (OncoNEM) software to infer clonal evolution from SNVs [34]⁠. OncoNEM assumes that each locus is mutated only once and that mutations are never lost; thus, mutations in an ancestral clone are a subset of mutations in descendant cells. A clonal evolutionary tree was constructed when all the mutations were modeled, rooted at CTCs 85 and 88 (Fig. 4A). Downstream, the branches were intermingled with DTCs, Mets, and CTCs, suggesting a reflux mechanism that promoted the multidirectional spread of cancer cells, from the bloodstream to bone marrow and metastases, from DTCs to metastases and the bloodstream, and from metastases to bone marrow and the bloodstream. However, when only the missense mutations were modeled, we generated a more straightforward tree diagram (Fig. 4B). The simpler structure of the missense mutations is perhaps a reflection of the selection pressure imposed by mutations that affect protein structure and function. To visualize the coherence of the OncoNEM trees, we selected two paths from the tree derived from all the mutations (left path in yellow; right path in blue; Fig. 4A) and plotted the mutational profile of the cells in each path using Circos. The Circos plots show a coherent evolution of mutations from cells in the upper part of the tree to cells at the bottom of the tree, i.e., samples in inner circles (top branches) have mutational profiles that constitute subsets of mutations in samples located outward (lower branches).

We validated the results with SiFit⁠, a likelihood-based, finite-site approach that extends the error model of OncoNEM to accommodate possible genotypes excluded by infinite-site models [35]⁠. The clonal evolutionary tree derived with SiFit also supported a multidirectional flux on different cancer cell types (Supplementary Fig. S16).

Discussion

Our study provides a single-cell genomic portrait of metastatic prostate cancer in one treatment-naïve patient, revealing genetically diverse tumor subclones and a clonal branching pattern consistent with multidirectional dissemination. As this study is based on a single patient, all findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating observations that require validation in larger cohorts.The predominance of CNAs over SNVs in the patient’s primary tumor and single prostate cancer cells supports the classification of prostate cancer as a “C-class” tumor [50, 51]. The widespread monoallelic loss of tumor suppressor genes and amplification of oncogenes underscores their role in facilitating clonal expansion and survival during dissemination. Interestingly, metastatic cells displayed relatively stable genomes with fewer CNAs and SNVs. This observation is consistent with the hypothesis that metastatic competence may be associated with relative genomic stability; however, the possibility that this pattern partly reflects contamination of the metastatic biopsy with genomically normal stromal cells cannot be excluded (see Limitations). This interpretation therefore remains preliminary pending validation.

Clonal reconstruction suggested that tumor cell dissemination is not confined to a single, linear evolutionary pathway. This observation is consistent with a multidirectional model of disease progression, and aligns with findings from multi-region and longitudinal sequencing studies showing polyclonal seeding and re-seeding among metastatic lesions [15, 51–55]. We note, however, that tree topology in OncoNEM is sensitive to SNV calling errors, allelic dropout, and potential violations of the infinite-sites model; these factors could alter inferred branching patterns and should be considered when interpreting the evolutionary reconstruction.We found that both mono- and hemizygous loss of key tumor suppressor genes (TSGs) occurred frequently in primary tumor sections and most single cells, while oncogene amplifications and mutations were also common. Metastatic cells showed fewer CNAs but still had notable deletions of TSGs and oncogene amplifications, suggesting these alterations may drive cancer progression. In contrast, SNV profiles provided less insight, as mutations in common TSGs were rare and mostly outside coding regions, indicating CNAs likely play a larger role than SNVs in the progression of prostate cancer.

In contrast to a model of continuous evolution driven by point mutations and small insertions or deletions, the frequent occurrence of CNAs in prostate cancer supports a model of punctuated evolution [14]. In our study, metastatic cancer cells showed fewer CNAs than CTCs and DTCs. This pattern is consistent with selection for genotypes carrying monoallelic deletions of tumor suppressor genes and monoallelic amplification of oncogenes, although we acknowledge that this interpretation is based on a single patient and that the lower CNA burden could also partly reflect the presence of non-neoplastic cells in the metastatic biopsy.

Analysis of clonal evolution in single cancer cells revealed a complex branching pattern suggesting multidirectional movement of tumor cells across disease sites. Examination of various primary tumor sections demonstrated substantial genetic divergence, underscoring the presence of a highly heterogeneous tumor structure. This high degree of heterogeneity is associated with poor disease prognosis [11].If validated in larger studies, multidirectional dissemination could facilitate the circulation of a genetically diverse pool of cancer cells, increasing the likelihood of multiclonal seeding and establishment of new metastatic sites It is important to note that the majority of prior research in this area used samples from patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer who received chemo and radiation therapy. These treatments are known to introduce additional mutations, particularly in androgen signaling, PI3K/Akt signaling, and DNA repair pathway gene products [11, 29]. As a result, they can obscure the original mutational landscape present in treatment-naïve patients.

Limitations of this study

Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. Findings derive from a single patient and should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating observations rather than generalisable conclusions. Replication in independent patient cohorts is required before any of the patterns described here can be elevated to the level of established biological principles. Single-cell genomic data generated from whole-genome amplification are subject to allelic dropout (ADO), amplification bias, and variant calling uncertainty. We used the SCIΦ SNV caller, which explicitly models ADO rates, and we pre-screened all cells using the genome integrity index and autocorrelation analysis to exclude poorly amplified libraries; nevertheless, residual technical noise cannot be excluded. The observation that metastatic cells carry fewer genomic alterations, a central finding of this study, is complicated by the co-clustering of control lymphocytes with the low-CNA Met cell cluster in PCA. While all Met cells used for CNA analysis were immunophenotypically defined as CK+/CD45−, and deep WES of 25 Met cells confirmed the presence of somatic mutations consistent with a cancer origin, we cannot formally exclude that a fraction of the low-CNA Met cells represents non-neoplastic cells that co-purified during collagenase dissociation of the bone biopsy. This possibility limits the strength of conclusions about genomic stability as a determinant of metastatic competence. Fourth, the clonal evolutionary trees generated with OncoNEM and SiFit are dependent on the underlying assumptions of these models, including the infinite-sites assumption for OncoNEM. Violations of these assumptions, together with the effects of ADO on SNV completeness, could alter inferred tree topologies. The multidirectional dissemination pattern described here is therefore best understood as one plausible evolutionary scenario consistent with the data rather than a definitive reconstruction of disease history. Another limitation concerns the cancer identity of the low-CNA metastatic cell cluster. In principal component analysis, the two control lymphocytes co-clustered with the majority of Met cells, raising the possibility that some cells in this cluster could represent non-neoplastic stromal or hematopoietic cells that co-purified during collagenase dissociation and CELLSEARCH processing of the bone metastatic biopsy. All 212 Met cells were positively identified as CK+/CD45 − by immunofluorescence, and cells were sorted individually to minimise contamination. However, as CK+ cells have been reported in bone marrow of non-cancer patients [56], we cannot formally exclude the possibility that a fraction of the low-CNA Met cells are not neoplastic. Deep WES of 25 Met cells, selected to span the full range of CNA profiles including the low-CNA cluster, confirmed the presence of multiple somatic mutations in all cells analysed, including mutations in cancer-relevant genes such as FAT3 and PIK3CD, which are also mutated in the primary tumour. Furthermore, a systematic overlap analysis identified 18 genes carrying relevant somatic mutations in both the primary tumour and Met cells, with 8 genes (CDCA7L, CHL1, FAT3, MUC12, NUMA1, PCDH15, PIK3CD, ZBTB4) carrying missense variants in both compartments, supporting the cancer cell identity of the WES-profiled Met cells. However, we acknowledge this analysis covers only 25 of the 212 Met cells profiled for CNAs. If a proportion of the remaining low-CNA Met cells are indeed normal cells, the observed difference in CNA burden between Met cells and CTCs/DTCs may be partly attributable to normal cell contamination rather than exclusively reflecting a biologically distinct, genomically stable metastatic phenotype. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the hypothesis that metastatic competence is associated with genomic stability. Definitive confirmation would require somatic mutation profiling of a larger fraction of Met cells or orthogonal validation such as fluorescence in situ hybridisation for known somatic copy number changes.

The observed plasticity in clonal evolution may play a significant role in the development of therapeutic resistance and disease recurrence. This highlights the necessity for treatment strategies that address both primary and disseminated tumor cell populations to effectively treat prostate cancer patients, improve outcomes, and save lives.

Conclusions

Single-cell genomic analysis of treatment-naïve tumors offers a unique opportunity to capture the natural evolutionary state of prostate cancer before therapeutic intervention. Our findings from this single-patient study raise several hypotheses, regarding the role of genomic stability in metastatic competence, the multidirectional spread of tumour subclones, and the prevalence of CNA-driven over SNV-driven oncogenesis in prostate cancer, that merit investigation in larger, prospective cohorts. These observations highlight the potential value of integrating single-cell genomic data into clinical research to refine models of disease progression and guide precision oncology. The diversity of cancer drivers between patients underscores the need to analyse each patient’s molecular profile to design and implement personalized therapies [57, 58]⁠.
