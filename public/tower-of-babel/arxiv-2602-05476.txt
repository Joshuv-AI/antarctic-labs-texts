Tight FPT Approximations for Fair k k -center with Outliers
Ameet Gadekar

Abstract
The k k -center problem is a fundamental clustering objective that has been extensively studied in approximation algorithms. Recent work has sought to incorporate modern constraints such as fairness and robustness, motivated by biased and noisy data. In this paper, we study fair k k -center with outliers , where centers must respect group-based representation constraints while up to z z points may be discarded. While a bi-criteria FPT approximation was previously known, no true approximation algorithm was available for this problem. We present the first deterministic 3 3 -approximation algorithm running in fixed-parameter tractable time parameterized by k k . Our approach departs from projection-based methods and instead directly constructs a fair solution using a novel iterative ball-finding framework, based on a structural trichotomy that enables fixed-parameter approximation for the problem. We further extend our algorithm to fair k k -supplier with outliers and to the more general fair-range setting with both lower and upper bounds. Finally, we show that improving the approximation factor below 3 3 is W ⁡ [ 2 ] \mathrm{W[2]} -hard, establishing the optimality of our results.

Introduction

Clustering is one of the central problems in optimization, with wide applications in operations research, computational complexity, machine learning, and data mining, among others.
In center-based clustering, we are given a set
X
of
n

k

X
into
k

Classical clustering problems include
k
-median,
k
-means, and
k
-center, where the objective is to minimize the sum of distances, the sum of squared distances, and the maximum distance to the nearest center, respectively.
In the unconstrained setting, a solution is simply a set
S\subseteq X
of
k
centers, and each point is assigned to its closest center in
S
.

Among these problems,
k
-center is perhaps the most combinatorial in nature and has been extensively studied since the mid 1980s
24
;
28
;
23

2
-approximation algorithm, which is optimal assuming
\textsf{P}\neq\textsf{NP}
.
However, the
k
-center objective is highly sensitive to noise, and even a few aberrant points can significantly distort the solution.
To address this issue, Charikar et al.
14
introduced the robust variant known as
k
-center with outliers
.
In this formulation, one is allowed to discard up to
z

points.
Formally, the goal is to select a set of centers
S\subseteq X
with
|S|\leq k
and an outlier set
Z\subseteq X
with
|Z|\leq z
so as to minimize

{\rm cost}(S;Z):=\max_{x\in X\setminus Z}d(x,S)
.
Charikar et al.
14
presented a simple combinatorial
3
-approximation algorithm for this problem, which was later improved to a tight
2
-approximation by Chakrabarty et al.
12
.

In recent years, there has been growing interest in incorporating fairness considerations into clustering.
Data points may carry attributes such as gender, expertise, or race, and the goal of
fair clustering
is to ensure that the output respects prescribed fairness constraints based on these attributes.
In this work, we focus on fairness in the selection of centers.
Specifically, the point set
X
is partitioned into
t

X_{1},\dots,X_{t}
, and each group
X_{i}
is associated with an upper bound
0\leq k_{i}\leq k
.
The task is to select a set of
k

|S\cap X_{i}|\leq k_{i}
for every

, thereby avoiding over-representation of any demographic group in the chosen centers
27
;
34
;
33
.
Related notions include constraints that prevent under-representation
38

fair-range clustering
model
29

We study the combination of robustness and fairness in the
fair
k
-center with outliers
problem, recently introduced by Amagata
4

In many real-world datasets, demographic groups may be unevenly represented

;
33

36
;
30
;
11

Fairness constraints alone can force centers to be placed near anomalous points belonging to underrepresented groups, while robustness alone may systematically discard minority data as outliers.
Combining these two requirements is therefore essential to ensure that clustering solutions are both representative and resistant to noise.
Amagata

For any

Thus, the approximation is twofold: both in the objective value and in the number of allowed outliers.
The running time of their algorithm is

A natural goal is to obtain tight

Following prior work, we focus on

Such

Challenges in existing techniques.

A natural approach for fair
k
-center with outliers is to adapt the classical algorithm of Charikar et al.
14
for the unconstrained problem.
That algorithm proceeds recursively as follows: at iteration
j\in[k]
, it selects a densest ball
B_{j}
of radius
\mathrm{OPT}

A key structural property of this process is that the optimal clusters can be ordered as

\big|\bigcup_{i=1}^{j}E_{i}\big|\;\geq\;\big|\bigcup_{i=1}^{j}\pi^{*}_{i}\big|.

Intuitively, if

Otherwise,

\pi^{*}_{j}

These points can then be charged to
\pi^{*}_{j}

To extend this approach to the fair setting, one must additionally ensure that the center of each chosen ball respects the color constraints.
A natural strategy is to guess the color of the center of the densest remaining optimal cluster, which leads to an
FPT
running time in
k
.
However, the main obstacle is that even when restricting to the points of the guessed color, the selected densest ball
B_{j}
may intersect a

In this case, expanding
B_{j}
prematurely settles the wrong cluster, while the intended densest cluster
\pi^{*}_{j}
may remain uncovered.
Since the center colors no longer match, the feasibility of the solution breaks down and there is no mechanism to retract or correct this choice.

For fair clustering, a prominent line of work follows a two-phase paradigm.
In the first phase, one computes an
\alpha
-approximate solution ignoring fairness constraints; in the second phase, this solution is projected onto a fair solution.
This projection typically incurs an additional loss in approximation quality, yielding an
(\alpha+1)
-approximate fair solution.
Since a
2
-approximation is known for
k
-center

;
28

3

21

More concretely, given a
2

T

\mathrm{OPT}
.
This approach fundamentally fails in the presence of outliers.
Although a
3
-approximate solution can be obtained for unconstrained
k
-center with outliers
14
, the approximate centers in
T
may themselves correspond to outliers in the optimal fair solution.
In such cases, no nearby fair center exists to match against, and the projection step breaks down completely.

1.1
Our Results

In this paper, we take a fundamentally different approach from projection-based techniques commonly used in fair clustering.
Rather than first computing an unconstrained solution and subsequently enforcing fairness, we design algorithms that directly construct fair solutions throughout their execution.
Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1
.

There is a deterministic
3
-approximation algorithm for fair
k
-center with outliers running in time
2^{O(k\log k)}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(n)
. Moreover, assuming
W[2]\neq\textsf{FPT}
, this approximation factor is tight for any
FPT
algorithm.

At a high level, our algorithm uncovers the hidden structure of an optimal clustering and settles clusters one by one while maintaining fairness constraints.
We first transform the original instance into a colorful formulation that rigidly enforces the selection of exactly one center from each group.
For this structured problem, we develop an iterative ball-finding framework that leverages
FPT
time to exhaustively probe dense regions at the scale of the optimal radius.
A key structural lemma shows that these dense balls must reveal one optimal cluster in every iteration, enabling the algorithm to recover the full solution without resorting to projection-based techniques. See
Section

1.2
for a technical overview.

Our approach naturally extends to a more general setting known as
fair
k
-supplier with outliers
, where centers must be chosen from a prescribed set (see
Definition

2
).
As a consequence, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 2
.

There is a deterministic
3
-approximation algorithm for fair
k
-supplier with outliers running in time
2^{O(k\log k)}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(n)
.

Note that even in the classical
k
-supplier problem, i.e., without fairness constraints or outliers, a factor
3
approximation is known to be optimal.
Thus, our result is tight already in this setting.

Finally, our framework extends to the a more general problem, called
fair-range
k
-center with outliers
, in which each demographic group is subject to both lower and upper bound requirements.

Theorem 3
.

There is a deterministic
3
-approximation algorithm for fair-range
k
-center with outliers running in time
2^{O(k\log k)}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(n)
.

The proof follows by reducing fair-range constraints to unit-requirement groups and applying the same structured reduction and iterative ball-finding algorithm developed for the upper-bound setting (see Section
5
).

Hardness of Approximation.

We complement our algorithmic results with a matching hardness bound, showing that achieving an approximation factor better than
3
is unlikely even in
FPT
time.

Our hardness follows from the work of Charikar et al.
14
, who studied
k
-center with outliers in the presence of
forbidden centers
, where certain points are disallowed from being chosen as centers.
They proved that for any
\varepsilon>0
, it is NP-hard to approximate this problem within a factor of
3-\varepsilon
.
Moreover, their reduction originates from
Max
k
-Coverage
, which is known to be
\mathrm{W[2]}
-hard, and preserves the parameter
k
.
The forbidden-centers variant can be viewed as a special case of fair
k
-center with outliers.
Indeed, partition the point set into two groups: one consisting of forbidden centers with an upper bound of
0
, and the other containing all remaining points with an upper bound of
k
.
Any feasible fair solution is then forced to select all centers from the allowed group, exactly reproducing the forbidden-centers constraint.

Since the reduction preserves the parameter
k
, it directly implies that achieving a
(3-\varepsilon)
-approximation for fair
k
-center with outliers is
\mathrm{W[2]}
-hard for any
\varepsilon>0
.
Consequently, unless
\mathrm{W[2]}=\mathrm{FPT}
, no
FPT
algorithm can improve upon our factor-
3
approximation.

1.2
Technical Overview

Previous approaches to fair clustering typically follow a two-phase paradigm: first computing an approximate unconstrained solution, and then projecting it onto a fair solution at the cost of an additional approximation loss. While such methods are effective for fair
k
-center without outliers, they fundamentally break down in the presence of outliers, since approximate centers may correspond to discarded points in the optimal solution and hence admit no meaningful projection.

In contrast, we design an algorithm that directly constructs a fair solution, bypassing projection altogether. Our approach consists of two main components: an approximation-preserving reduction to a highly structured colorful instance, and a new FPT-time iterative ball-finding algorithm for solving this structured problem.

1.2.1
From fair constraints to colorful structure

Our first step is an approximation-preserving reduction from fair
k
-center with outliers to a structured instance of
k
-center with unit group requirements.
Each demographic group is duplicated according to its allowed quota, producing a collection of groups from which at most one center may be selected per copy.
Any feasible solution to this instance therefore satisfies the original fairness constraints.
A major difficulty is that this duplication can create a number of groups far exceeding
k
, making the instance unsuitable for algorithms whose complexity depends exponentially on the number of groups.
In particular, it is not clear whether one can reduce the problem to exactly
k
groups while preserving the optimal cost using only polynomial-time transformations.

Here, the power of
FPT
time in
k
becomes crucial.
We exploit this by applying the color-coding technique that randomly assigns each group to one of
k
colors, thereby merging groups of the same color into a single color class.
This produces an instance of
colorful
k
-supplier with outliers
, in which a feasible solution must select exactly one center from each color class.
With probability at least
2^{-\Omega(k)}
, this randomized transformation preserves the optimal cost of the original instance.

The reduction to unit group requirements is essential for this merging step.
Since each unit group contributes at most one center, merging several such groups into a single color class simply enforces the selection of exactly one center among all points in the merged class.
In contrast, if one were to apply color coding directly to the original instance with general upper bound requirements, merging two groups with bounds would require determining an appropriate bound for the merged group.
As these upper bounds may be large (e.g.,
\Theta(n)
) and depend on the unknown optimal solution, it is not clear if we can efficiently guess the correct requirement bound of the merged group w.r.t. to some fixed optimal solution.

By derandomizing the above procedure, we generate
2^{O(k)}
colorful instances and guarantee that at least one of them has optimal cost equal to that of the fair
k
-center with outliers instance.
Thus, although the initial reduction produces many groups,
FPT
time enables us to compress the instance down to exactly
k
groups without losing optimality.
Consequently, it suffices to design an
FPT
approximation algorithm for the colorful problem.

Coverage via Charging.

To bound the total number of covered points, we introduce a charging scheme similar to
13

Points belonging to an optimal cluster charge themselves whenever they are contained in one of the expanded balls of radius
3\cdot\mathrm{OPT}

Otherwise, they are charged to free points present in these balls—namely, points that do not belong to any remaining optimal cluster or that are designated as outliers.
This guarantees that the algorithm covers at least as many points as the optimal solution, up to the allowed

As mentioned before, the algorithm runs in phases, settling one optimal cluster in each phase.
In each phase, we introduce an iterative subroutine that explicitly uncovers the structure of an unknown optimal solution in the remaining points. If there is no nearby ball, then by construction every selected ball

Otherwise, each constructed ball must draw a significant portion of its mass from uncovered optimal clusters.

\pi^{*}_{i}

Otherwise, each ball has at least half of its mass on the free points; such balls are referred to as

Therefore, both these balls lie close to the optimal center of that cluster, giving rise to the two-light-balls configuration.

Thus, in all cases, one of the three structural outcomes must occur, enabling the algorithm to settle an optimal cluster in the current iteration.
After identifying the appropriate configuration, the algorithm removes all points covered by the corresponding expanded ball.

k

A sequence of works

Parameterized Approximation Frameworks for Clustering.

Parameterized approximation has recently emerged as a powerful paradigm for obtaining tight guarantees for clustering problems when the number of clusters is small.

4.3
Running time

We next bound the running time of our algorithms without using the
guess
keyword. Specifically, we show how to replace each
guess
keyword using a branching program.

In each iteration of
Algorithm

1
, it has to guess the index of the densest unmarked optimal cluster, which results into
k
choices.
Next, at every call of
Algorithm

2
, the structural trichotomy implies three possible cases.
For the nearby-ball and good-ball cases, we guess which of the constructed balls satisfies the corresponding property, yielding
O(k)
possibilities in each case.
For the two-light-balls case, we guess the relevant pair of balls and the corresponding unmarked cluster index, yielding
O(k^{3})
possibilities.
Thus, each call of
Algorithm

2
branches into at most
3\cdot O(k^{3})=O(k^{3})
subcases.

As
Algorithm

1
runs at most
k
iterations
since each iteration settles at least one optimal cluster, the total number of nodes in this branching program is bounded by
(k\cdot O(k^{3}))^{k}=k^{O(k)}=2^{O(k\log k)}
.
Each node in the branching program performs only polynomial work in
n
.
Together with the derandomized color-coding step and the polynomial guessing of the optimal radius, this yields an overall running time of
2^{O(k\log k)}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(n)
.

5
Extension to Lower and Upper Bound Requirements

We extend our results to the
fair-range
k
-center with outliers
problem, where the point set
X
is partitioned into demographic groups
G_{1},\dots,G_{t}
, and each group
G_{i}
is associated with a lower bound
\ell_{i}
and an upper bound
u_{i}
.
The objective is to select a set of centers
S\subseteq X
with
|S|=k
and an outlier set
Z\subseteq X
such that

\ell_{i}\leq|S\cap G_{i}|\leq u_{i}\qquad\text{for all }i\in[t],

while minimizing
\max_{x\in X\setminus Z}d(x,S)
.
We assume feasibility, i.e.,
\sum_{i=1}^{t}\ell_{i}\leq k\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t}u_{i}
.

Our reduction converts the fair-range constraints into a collection of unit-requirement groups, combining equality and upper-bound constraints in a unified framework.

For each group
G_{i}
, we first enforce the lower bound by creating
\ell_{i}

equality groups
, each of which is a copy of
G_{i}
and must contribute exactly one center.
This guarantees that any feasible solution selects at least
\ell_{i}
centers from
G_{i}
.

After these mandatory selections, the group
G_{i}
has a remaining allowance of
u_{i}-\ell_{i}
additional centers.
To capture this flexibility, we create
u_{i}-\ell_{i}
additional copies of
G_{i}
, referred to as
slack groups
, each with unit requirement allowing the selection of at most one center.
Selecting centers from these slack groups permits additional representatives from
G_{i}
but caps the total number of selections at
u_{i}
.
Thus, the equality groups enforce the lower bounds, while the slack groups encode the upper bounds, and every group in the resulting instance has a unit requirement.

We now apply the same color-coding technique as in Section
3.2
to reduce the instance to all unit-requirement groups into exactly
k
color classes, obtaining a colorful
k
-supplier instance with outliers.
Since a feasible colorful solution selects exactly one center from each color class, it corresponds to selecting exactly one center from each equality and slack group, thereby satisfying all lower and upper bound constraints.

Our iterative ball-finding algorithm operates directly on this colorful formulation and produces a
3
-approximate solution in
FPT
time.
The correctness and approximation guarantee follow identically from the analysis of the upper-bound case.
