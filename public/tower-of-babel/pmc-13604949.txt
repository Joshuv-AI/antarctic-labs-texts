Multidomain Arterial Afterload and Left Atrial–Left Ventricular Coupling in Hypertensive Phenotypes
Authors: Yiran Luo, Lina Zhao, Sha Li, Yueyao Zhao, Fangfang Lu, Bei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBrachial blood pressure may not fully reflect the multidimensional arterial load associated with left atrial–left ventricular (LA–LV) coupling. In this cross‐sectional study, we examined local carotid pulse wave velocity (PWV), cuff‐derived arterial pressure–volume index (API), and arterial velocity–pulse index (AVI), separately and as a composite afterload index (ACI), in 336 inpatients (71 controls, 183 with hypertension, and 82 with hypertension and type 2 diabetes). Covariate‐adjusted linear models related standardized exposures to the left atrial stiffness index (LASI) and LA–LV coupling index (LACI). Greater ACI was associated with higher LACI (β = 0.0142 per 1‐SD, 95% CI 0.0011–0.0273; p = 0.033). The LASI estimate was directionally similar but less precise (β = 0.0232, 95% CI −0.0024–0.0488; p = 0.076), and all single‐domain CIs included zero. These findings support further evaluation of accessible multidomain vascular assessment as a complement to brachial blood pressure. External calibration and longitudinal validation are needed to establish the clinical value of ACI.

Introduction

Brachial blood pressure alone may not fully capture the multidimensional arterial load imposed on the heart. Arterial stiffness and wave transmission determine when and how systolic pressure reaches the left ventricle (LV), meaning that comparable cuff pressures can accompany different myocardial loading patterns. In hypertension, prominent late‐systolic load has been associated with impaired left atrial (LA) function [1]. Ventricular—arterial stiffening and LA myopathy are also important features of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, in which abnormal LA mechanics carry clinical and prognostic information [2, 3, 4]. Early vascular–cardiac dysfunction may therefore be present before it is apparent from brachial pressure alone.

Noninvasive vascular measures provide different views of this underlying load. Carotid–femoral pulse wave velocity (PWV) is the reference measure of aortic stiffness [5], whereas local carotid PWV reflects segmental carotid stiffness and wave propagation [6, 7, 8]. The cuff‐derived arterial pressure–volume index (API) and arterial velocity–pulse index (AVI) describe peripheral pressure–volume behavior and waveform augmentation [9, 10]. Central arterial stiffness has been associated with impaired LA mechanics independently of systolic blood pressure and LV structure [11]. Because these measures are related but not interchangeable, no single measure may fully capture the combined afterload burden.

In this study, LA–LV coupling was operationalized using complementary pressure–strain and volumetric indices. The LA stiffness index (LASI) combines estimated LV filling pressure with LA reservoir strain and has been proposed as a marker of early hypertension‐mediated cardiac injury [12]. Pulsatile arterial hemodynamics provide a mechanistic link between vascular load and cardiac dysfunction [13], while the LA–LV coupling index (LACI) provides a volumetric measure associated with diastolic dysfunction [14]. We evaluated local carotid PWV, API, and AVI separately and as a composite afterload index (ACI). We hypothesized that higher ACI would be associated with higher LASI and LACI. Mutually adjusted models examined whether individual domains retained residual associations, while additional analyses assessed the explanatory contribution beyond brachial blood pressure and possible modification by type 2 diabetes.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This single‐center cross‐sectional study included consecutive adult inpatients admitted to the cardiology service between October 2024 and June 2025. Vascular assessments (PWV, API, and AVI) and transthoracic echocardiography, including speckle‐tracking strain and 3D volumetry, were performed during the same hospitalization and, whenever feasible, on the same day. Of the 403 individuals assessed, 67 were excluded for objective data‐quality or completeness reasons. Fifty‐three had vascular or echocardiographic recordings that were inadequate, nonstandardized, or unsuitable for reliable quantification. Fourteen did not have key variables required for the primary analysis. The remaining 336 participants formed the complete‐case analytic cohort. The sample size was determined by the number of eligible participants enrolled during the study period. No a priori sample‐size calculation was performed. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University (approval no. 2023 Ethics Review No. 279). All participants provided written informed consent.

Participants

Eligible participants were adults aged 18–85 years. HTN and T2D were identified from a documented diagnosis, condition‐specific treatment, or a diagnosis established during the index hospitalization. Participants were categorized as controls without HTN or T2D, HTN without T2D, or HTN with T2D (HTN+T2D). A T2D‐only phenotype was not included in the study design.

Cardiac exclusion criteria were persistent atrial fibrillation or another rhythm precluding reliable strain analysis; prior myocardial infarction; LV ejection fraction <50%; moderate‐to‐severe valvular disease; dilated or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy; or congenital heart disease. Patients with known pulmonary arterial hypertension or severe pulmonary disease were also excluded. Technical exclusions related to data quality and completeness were applied as described above. Vascular Measurements

Local carotid PWV was measured in the left common carotid artery, 1–2 cm proximal to the bifurcation, using a Mindray Resona R9 ultrasound system with the Holo‐PWV module (Mindray, China). Participants were examined at rest in the supine position. Five consecutive 1–4‐second recordings were obtained from the same straight arterial segment with a stable probe position. The device automatically reported the mean of the five recordings.

API and AVI were measured using a PASESA AVE‐2000 PRO system (Shisei Datum, Japan) as cuff‐derived indices of peripheral pressure–volume behavior and waveform morphology. Brachial blood pressure and heart rate were recorded simultaneously from the left upper arm in a quiet resting state. Three measurements were obtained and averaged.

Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed using a GE Vivid E95 system and analyzed offline with EchoPAC software (GE Healthcare). A stable electrocardiographic signal was used for cardiac phase identification. The R‐wave peak defined end‐diastole, the end of the T wave defined end‐systole, and the onset of the P wave defined the start of atrial contraction. Three consecutive sinus cycles were acquired. When occasional premature beats occurred, only sinus cycles with adequate image quality were analyzed. At least five cycles were recorded for spectral Doppler and M‐mode measurements. Two‐dimensional images were acquired at frame rates of 40–80 frames/s. For calculation of E/e′, e′ was defined as the mean of the septal and lateral mitral annular early‐diastolic velocities. LA reservoir strain (LASr) was measured in the apical four‐ and two‐chamber views. The final value was averaged across both views and three cardiac cycles. Three consecutive single‐beat, high‐volume‐rate full‐volume 4D datasets were acquired, with each dataset covering one complete cardiac cycle. The dataset with the best image quality was selected for volume analysis. 3D‐derived LAVmin and 3D LVEDV were measured using dedicated 4D LA and 4D LV analysis software on the GE platform. Echocardiographic analyses were performed without knowledge of the vascular measurements or clinical group.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized overall and by group. Continuous variables were compared using one‐way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests, and categorical variables using χ2 or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate. Primary associations were examined in separate linear models for PWV, API, AVI, and ACI. Single‐domain models estimated the overall association of each measure with the outcomes, whereas the ACI model evaluated combined multidomain burden. All exposures, including ACI, were standardized before regression; coefficients are reported per 1‐SD increase. Models used HC3 robust standard errors and adjusted for age using a natural cubic spline (df = 4), sex, body surface area, heart rate, LV mass index, and clinical group.

Mutually adjusted models included PWV, API, and AVI simultaneously and estimated the residual association of each domain conditional on the other two. Collinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors and a condition index. Restricted cubic splines examined nonlinearity, with knots at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles; overall and nonlinearity p values were obtained from nested‐model F‐tests. Because pulse pressure may lie downstream of arterial stiffness, PP‐anchored models were treated as contextual sensitivity analyses rather than estimates of direct effects.

Sensitivity analyses included standardized PCA‐derived PC1, an age‐overlap subset (40–70 years), and models further adjusted for hypertension duration, diabetes duration, and four antihypertensive medication classes: renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, calcium‐channel blockers, diuretics, and beta‐blockers. Among participants with hypertension, an ACI×T2D interaction term assessed modification by T2D. Pairwise interaction and subgroup analyses were exploratory and were evaluated using Benjamini–Hochberg false‐discovery‐rate correction within the relevant analysis families. Additional ACI‐tertile and bootstrap procedures are described in the Supplementary Methods.

Analyses used complete cases without imputation. Missingness and exclusion counts are reported in Tables S5–S6, and available characteristics of included and excluded participants are compared in Table S7. Analyses were conducted in Python 3.11.2; package versions, scripts, and machine‐readable outputs are provided in the submitted code archive. All tests were two‐sided, with P<0.05 indicating nominal statistical significance. Interpretation also considered effect size, 95% CI, multiplicity, and analysis hierarchy.

Intra‐Observer Repeatability

To assess intra‐observer repeatability, the same operator reanalyzed stored image datasets from 20 participants at least 2 weeks apart and was blinded to the first‐session results. Endocardial borders were retraced, and speckle tracking was repeated de novo. Agreement was assessed using ICC(A,1) with 95% CIs, Bland–Altman analysis, and the within‐subject coefficient of variation.

Discussion

This study examined whether accessible afterload surrogates provide separate or combined information about LA–LV coupling. The principal finding was that greater ACI was associated with higher LACI, whereas the LASI estimate was directionally similar but less precise. Single‐domain associations were weaker, and each domain contributed limited residual information when the three measures were modeled together. PC1 yielded results similar to those of ACI. Together, these findings show a modest association between integrated afterload burden and LACI, whereas the single‐domain estimates were less precise.

Hypertension is an important upstream contributor to HFpEF [15]. Current frameworks emphasize that diastolic dysfunction reflects the interaction of ventricular mechanics, vascular aging, and time‐varying arterial load [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. Previous studies have linked late‐systolic loading to LA dysfunction in hypertension [1], related arterial stiffness to subsequent cardiac remodeling in young adults with T2D [22], and identified distinct arterial waveform clusters [23]. Atrial dysfunction is also an important HFpEF phenotype, and atrial strain may assist in detecting hypertensive HFpEF [24, 25]. Our study extends this work by integrating local carotid stiffness and cuff‐derived waveform indices with LA strain and 3D volumetry. This design links accessible vascular phenotyping with complementary pressure—strain and volumetric measures of LA–LV coupling. Emerging reference data for LASI and LACI may further support interpretation of these coupling phenotypes [26].

ACI represents relative multidomain burden within the study population. PC1 explained 55.1% of the variance in the three measures and was almost perfectly correlated with ACI. The similar PC1 and ACI estimates support the stability of the integrated signal across two composite approaches. Mutually adjusted models addressed whether any domain retained a distinct association after accounting for the other two. Low VIFs indicated that severe numerical multicollinearity was unlikely. Each domain nevertheless contributed limited residual explanatory information once the other two were included.

Secondary analyses reinforced the overall pattern. Because PP may partly mediate stiffness‐related load, PP‐anchored models were interpreted as contextual analyses. Their larger ACI coefficients and minimal change in model fit did not alter the primary interpretation. The association was adequately represented by a linear model over the observed range. Its direction was maintained after adjustment for disease duration and antihypertensive medication classes, and no clear modification by T2D was detected. The API×AVI interaction did not remain significant after BH FDR correction. These findings extend broader evidence linking arterial stiffness to heart‐failure risk and shared vascular–metabolic pathways [27, 28, 29] while supporting continued validation of device‐specific vascular measurements [30].

Clinically, local carotid PWV can be obtained during routine vascular ultrasound and characterizes segmental stiffness in a proximal elastic conduit artery, whereas API and AVI can be acquired rapidly using an upper‐arm cuff assessment [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Their complementary accessibility provides a practical basis for multidomain vascular phenotyping. Greater ACI was associated with higher LACI and provided modest incremental explanatory value beyond clinical variables and brachial blood pressure. This supports ACI as an integrative research phenotype and motivates external calibration against established vascular‐aging and HFpEF frameworks [31, 32, 33]. Previous studies support the prognostic relevance of LACI and LASI [14, 34, 35], while echocardiographic LACI has also shown diagnostic value in HFpEF [36]. More broadly, ventricular—arterial coupling remains relevant to functional HF phenotyping [37, 38]. With longitudinal and external validation, automated or AI‐assisted models could integrate accessible vascular measures with LA–LV coupling indices to identify patients who may benefit from further cardiac assessment.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. The cross‐sectional design precludes temporal or causal inference. This was a single‐center inpatient study based on complete cases; individuals with greater clinical or imaging complexity may therefore be underrepresented. Residual confounding by disease duration, treatment intensity, metabolic or renal status, and unmeasured disease severity remains possible. Because no T2D‐only group was included, we could not distinguish the association of T2D alone with LA–LV coupling from that observed when T2D coexisted with hypertension. Local carotid PWV is a device‐dependent segmental measure and is not interchangeable with carotid–femoral PWV, while API and AVI are cuff‐derived surrogates of vascular load. ACI is standardized within the study cohort and has no fixed external reference or clinical cutoff. The sample size also limited the precision of interaction and nonlinear analyses, and the findings may not generalize to community‐based or untreated populations. Multicenter prospective studies are required for external calibration and clinical validation.

Conclusion

We found that greater multidomain arterial afterload burden, summarized by ACI, was associated with higher LACI. The LASI estimate was similar in direction but less precise. No single afterload measure retained a clear residual association when all three were modeled together. These findings support further evaluation of integrated vascular assessment as a complement to brachial blood pressure. External calibration should test whether ACI adds information beyond brachial blood pressure in community cohorts. Longitudinal studies should assess its ability to predict incident HFpEF and detect treatment‐related changes before clinical application.
