Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement in Patients With Interstitial Lung Disease
Authors: Rachel Schenkel, Andreas Polycarpou, David Perlman, Sara Shumway
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with interstitial lung disease (ILD) are at higher risk of complications with cardiac surgery than the general population, leading to interest in transcatheter technologies such as transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) as an alternative to surgery for this patient population. However, outcomes of TAVR in patients with ILD remain under assessment.Case SummaryWe present 2 cases of mortality likely due to acute exacerbation of ILD following TAVR in patients with ILD who underwent TAVR.DiscussionThe risk of complications with TAVR is likely underrecognized in patients with ILD.Take-Home MessageThere is a need for better risk stratification among patients with ILD undergoing TAVR.Key words: aortic valve, hypoxemia, treatment, valvular heart disease

Case 1

History of present illness

A 68-year-old man with a complex medical history including idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) receiving baseline supplemental oxygen at 5 L/min presented with progressive aortic valve stenosis and symptoms of worsening dyspnea, fatigue, and dizziness.

Past medical history

Additional comorbidities included chronic pulmonary thromboembolic disease with pulmonary hypertension (PH) status post pulmonary thromboendarterectomy (most recently estimated systolic pulmonary artery pressure was 38.6 mm Hg above right atrial pressure correlating to mild-moderate PH), coronary artery disease status post coronary artery bypass graft to the left anterior descending artery, non-ST-segment myocardial infarction with percutaneous coronary intervention to the left circumflex and first diagonal arteries, systolic and diastolic heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), Hodgkin lymphoma status post radiation and chemotherapy, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, congenital solitary kidney with stage 4 chronic kidney disease, rheumatoid arthritis on daily prednisone, and peripheral vascular disease status post toe amputations.

Differential diagnosis

Considerations included heart failure and worsening of his known COPD and interstitial lung disease (ILD).

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated worsening aortic valve gradient (28 mm Hg) with an aortic valve area of 1.1 cm2.

Management

Following a heart team discussion, the diagnosis of low-flow, low-gradient moderate-to-severe aortic stenosis was made. The patient was counseled that improvement in symptoms could not be guaranteed with transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR); the patient wished to proceed anyway because of the severity of worsened symptoms. The evening before his scheduled TAVR procedure, he awoke with 10/10 chest pain and was found to be experiencing a non-ST-segment myocardial infarction. He underwent coronary angiography, which demonstrated severe in-stent restenosis of the first diagonal artery; this was successfully dilated with a drug-coated balloon, with resolution of chest pain.

He proceeded with transfemoral TAVR the following day with a 26-mm Edwards Sapien 3 Ultra Resilia valve. A small paravalvular leak was noted, which improved after postdeployment balloon aortic valvuloplasty. The postdeployment valve gradient was 11 mm Hg. He did not receive periprocedural nitric oxide, nor was ILD immunotherapy withheld.

His postprocedural course was complicated by acute-on-chronic kidney injury requiring dialysis for volume overload unresponsive to diuretics and acute-on-chronic hypoxic respiratory failure. Echocardiography on postoperative day 7 demonstrated severe PH with an estimated right ventricular systolic pressure of 62 mm Hg above right atrial pressure and a slightly depressed ejection fraction of 45%-50%. The aortic valve was well seated with no valvular or paravalvular aortic insufficiency. His PH was not detected on preoperative echocardiogram despite known history; however, the presence of ILD reduces the sensitivity of echocardiography for the detection of PH. His PH was thought to be a combination of World Health Organization group II and III factors with some pre-existing group III PH factors affected acutely by volume overload. Pulmonary consultation was obtained because of continued worsening respiratory status in the setting of his hypervolemia, COPD, and IPF. Rheumatology consultation was also obtained, and his home tofacitinib and sulfasalazine were held because of possible infection and renal impairment. His home dose steroids and anakinra were continued. He was treated with empiric antibiotics given a mild leukocytosis and chronic immunosuppression for his rheumatoid arthritis. All cultures and infectious panels remained negative over the course of his hospitalization. It was thought that volume accumulation was the main driver of his respiratory deterioration; however, his respiratory status did not improve after initiation of daily dialysis with good fluid removal. He continued to worsen and required intubation. Repeat computed tomography scan on postoperative day 12 was worsened with diffuse ground-glass opacities and a more nodular appearance concerning for ILD acute exacerbation (Figures 1A and 1B.) The patient continued to do poorly and on postoperative day 18 experienced a pulseless electrical activity arrest with significant hypoxia on maximum ventilatory support. Return of spontaneous circulation was achieved, but upon discussion with the family and the patient himself, the decision was made to pursue comfort care and terminally extubate.

Case 2

History of present illness

An 87-year-old woman presented after recent hospitalization for decompensated heart failure and hypoxic respiratory failure. She was found to have aortic stenosis during that admission. Her main symptom was dyspnea on exertion.

Past medical history

Her comorbidities included heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, advanced pulmonary fibrosis requiring baseline supplemental oxygen at 1 to 2 L/min, prior cerebrovascular accident, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. She did not have a history of PH.

Differential diagnoses

Considerations included heart failure and known advanced pulmonary fibrosis.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated moderate-to-severe aortic stenosis with a mean gradient of 28 mm Hg and an aortic valve area of 1.0 cm2. In addition, she had mild-to-moderate mitral regurgitation, mild aortic insufficiency, and preserved biventricular function.

Management

Following a multidisciplinary heart team discussion, the patient was evaluated for TAVR. She elected to undergo transfemoral TAVR using a 23-mm Edwards Sapien 3 Ultra valve. She was found to have a paravalvular leak near a calcium deposit after deployment, and postdeployment balloon aortic valvuloplasty was performed, after which the leak was reduced to trace. The valve gradient after the procedure was 8 mm Hg. She did not receive periprocedural nitric oxide, nor was ILD immunotherapy withheld. Postoperatively, her course was complicated by progressive hypoxemic respiratory failure requiring high-flow nasal cannula. To rule out pulmonary embolism, a chest computed tomography was obtained on postoperative day 1, which showed extensive interstitial and alveolar edema in the setting of her pulmonary fibrosis (Figures 1C and 1D). Before the procedure, her fluid status was euvolemic, but given her pulmonary edema and history of decompensated heart failure, aggressive diuresis was started in addition to steroids for ILD acute exacerbation. She continued to worsen and was intubated on postoperative day 3 for respiratory failure. Repeat echocardiogram showed unexpectedly increased mild-to-moderate paravalvular aortic insufficiency. Given her clinical decompensation, there was concern that this acute paravalvular leak was significant enough to be symptomatic, especially in the setting of her noncompliant and hypertrophied left ventricle which was accustomed to severe aortic stenosis. She underwent repeat balloon valvuloplasty of her prosthetic valve with appreciable improvement in the degree of aortic insufficiency to trace later that day. However, she continued to decline, and by postoperative day 5, the patient and family decided to move toward comfort measures and compassionate extubation.

Discussion

ILD is a heterogeneous group of chronic lung pathologies characterized by inflammation and scarring of lung parenchyma. ILD is a known risk factor for complications with both noncardiac and cardiac surgery.1 This has led to increased interest in utilizing transcatheter technologies in this patient population. However, outcomes among patients with ILD who undergo procedures such as TAVR continue to be evaluated. One feared complication of ILD is an acute exacerbation of interstitial lung disease (AEILD) with a mortality ranging from 80%-90%.1 AEILD is a known complication of thoracic and extra-thoracic surgery, as well as cardiothoracic procedures.2

The exact mechanisms behind AEILD are not well understood. The histopathology of AEILD typically shows diffuse alveolar damage similar to Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome.2 In patients with ILD undergoing intubation and mechanical ventilation, several mechanisms have been proposed, including mechanical stress and exposure to high oxygen levels.3,4 In patients undergoing nonintubated procedures, such as TAVR, the mechanism is likely related to triggering a systemic inflammatory response.2,5 Circulating proinflammatory cytokines are significantly elevated in acute exacerbation of IPF, especially IL-6 and IL-8, and elevated C-reactive protein has been shown to be a risk factor for AEILD.6,7 IL-6 and IL-8 have also been found to be elevated after TAVR with subsequent development of systemic inflammatory response syndrome in 40.1% of patients.8 The inflammatory cascade results in epithelial dysfunction/damage and circulating fibrocyte recruitment, often leading to rapid progression of the pulmonary fibrosis.9

Currently, neither the Society of Thoracic Surgery operative risk calculator nor the TAVR In-Hospital mortality risk calculator include ILD as a variable, which likely reflects a lack of granular evidence regarding the impact of ILD on risk vs that of other chronic lung diseases. However, preoperative imaging findings of ILD and pulmonary fibrosis are a powerful predictor of adverse events after TAVR.10 Although both patients presented here were highly comorbid, both likely had AEILD contributing to their deaths following TAVR.

Take-Home Message

•There is a need for better risk stratification amongst patients with interstitial lung disease undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement.
