Variance Reduction Based Experience Replay for Policy Optimization
Hua Zheng, Wei Xie, M. Ben Feng, Keilung Choy

Abstract
Effective reinforcement learning (RL) for complex stochastic systems requires leveraging historical data collected in previous iterations to accelerate policy optimization. Classical experience replay treats all past observations uniformly and fails to account for their varying contributions to learning. To overcome this limitation, we propose Variance Reduction Experience Replay (VRER), a principled framework that selectively reuses informative samples to reduce variance in policy gradient estimation. VRER is algorithm - agnostic and integrates seamlessly with existing policy optimization methods, forming the basis of our sample - efficient off - policy algorithm, Policy Gradient with VRER (PG - VRER). Motivated by the lack of rigorous theoretical analysis of experience replay, we develop a novel framework that explicitly captures dependencies introduced by Markovian dynamics and behavior - policy interactions. Using this framework, we establish finite - time convergence guarantees for PG - VRER and reveal a fundamental bias–variance trade - off: reusing older experience increases bias but simultaneously reduces gradient variance. Extensive empirical experiments demonstrate that VRER consistently accelerates policy learning and improves performance over state - of - the - art policy optimization algorithms.



Keywords
Reinforcement Learning, Policy Optimization, Importance Sampling, Variance Reduction, Experience Replay

Introduction

In recent years, various policy optimization approaches are developed to solve challenging control problems in healthcare
(

These approaches often consider parametric policies and search for optimal solution through policy gradient approach
(

, whose performance and convergence crucially depends on the accuracy of gradient estimation.
Reusing historical samples is one way to improve gradient estimation, especially when historical data is scarce.
In this paper, we address an important question in policy optimization methods:
How to intelligently select and reuse historical samples to accelerate the learning of the optimal policy for complex stochastic systems?

According to the different basic unit of historical samples to reuse, policy gradient (PG)
algorithms can be classified into episode-based and step-based approaches
(
32
)
.
Episode-based approaches reuse historical trajectories through importance sampling (IS) strategy accounting for the distributional difference induced by the target and behavior policies.
In this case, the importance sampling weight is built on the product of likelihood ratios (LR) of state-action transitions occurring within each process trajectory. As a result, the likelihood-ratio-weighted observations can have large or even infinite variance, especially for problems with long planning horizons
(
44
)
.
On the other hand, the step-based approaches take individual state-action transitions as the basic reuse units.
This overcomes the limitation of episode-based approaches, provides a more flexible reuse strategy, and supports process online control.

In this paper, we will develop a variance-reduction based experience replay framework applicable to step-based approaches.

A noticeable limitation of current reinforcement learning (RL) techniques is the low sample efficiency
(
2
)
. This challenge becomes especially pronounced in complex stochastic systems, such as healthcare
(
16
;
63
)
and biopharmaceutical manufacturing
(
66
)
, where each real or simulated experiment can be financially or computationally expensive. Another growing application of RL is RLHF, short for Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback
(
6
)
, used extensively in large language model-based chat models
(
58
)
to align with human preferences. However, it’s crucial to underscore that RLHF heavily depends on accumulating significant volumes of expensive human feedback data.
Traditional on-policy methods only utilize newly generated samples to estimate the policy gradient for each policy update.
Ignoring the relevant information carried with historical samples can lead to low sample efficiency and high uncertainty in policy gradient estimation. In light of these considerations, it becomes important to fully utilize all available information when addressing RL optimization problems.

Fortunately, this information loss can be reduced through a combination of experience replay (ER)
(
26
;
34
;
56
)
and off-policy optimization methods, which can store and “replay" past relevant experiences to accelerate the search for optimal policy. Off-policy methods typically employ importance sampling (IS) to address distribution mismatches
(
38
;
17
)
, yet this often inflates variance when behavior and target policies diverge
(
38
;
64
)
. Furthermore, the theoretical mechanisms governing experience replay (ER)—specifically the impact of buffer size, policy age, and mixing rates—are lack of discussion. To address this, we present a novel framework that investigates a fundamental bias-variance trade-off: reusing older samples introduces bias but reduces gradient estimation variance. To address these challenges, we introduce Variance Reduction Experience Replay (VRER). By selectively reusing historical samples, VRER reduces gradient variance and speeds up convergence; meanwhile, it employs a downsampling strategy and controlled replay capacity to mitigate the bias typically caused by policy lag and correlated data.

The key contributions of this study are summarized as follows.

We introduce a Variance Reduction Experience Replay (VRER) framework for policy gradient optimization that accelerates step

Extensive empirical studies demonstrate that the proposed VRER framework can efficiently utilize past samples, reduce the policy gradient estimation variance, and accelerate the learning of optimal policy for complex stochastic systems.

, and surrogate objectives that explicitly balance performance against variance inflation (POIS)

(

;

. While

Experience replay (ER) and its extension, such as prioritized experience replay
(

The proposed VRER method stands out from existing approaches by tackling the problems of biased policy gradient estimation and inflated variance, supported by our novel and rigorous theoretical analysis on bias-variance trade-off. This approach incorporates a selection rule and a managed buffer capacity that collectively reduce the variance induced by reusing correlated historical samples and mitigate bias due to the use of outdated samples. Furthermore, this selection process imposes a control on the magnitude of parameter updates, ensuring that the discrepancy between successive policies remains constrained.

Likelihood Ratio Based Policy Gradient Estimation

In this section, we describe how to utilize IS or likelihood ratio (LR) to improve the estimation of
policy gradient
\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})
at each
k
-th iteration
through reusing the selected historical samples.
Let
\mathcal{F}_{k}
denote the set of all behavioral distributions that have been visited by the beginning of the
k
-th iteration.
Let
\mathcal{U}_{k}
be
a reuse set
with
\mathcal{U}_{k}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{k}
including the MDP model candidates
whose historical samples are selected and reused for improving the estimation of policy gradient
\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})
. Denote its cardinality as
|\mathcal{U}_{k}|
.
For discussions in this section, we assume the reuse set
\mathcal{U}_{k}
is given. We will present how to select it in Section

4.1
Step-based Policy Optimization via Importance Sampling

While IS is effective for episode-based finite-horizon MDPs
(
64
;
32
)
, extending it to step-based infinite-horizon settings is non-trivial. Unlike the i.i.d. nature of episodic reuse, step-based samples are sequentially correlated due to Markovian noise. This dependency invalidates the standard IS identity because the transient state distribution during sampling differs from the target stationary distribution. Consequently, the standard likelihood ratio estimator is biased:

{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{s}\sim\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i}\in\cdot|\boldsymbol{s}_{1}),\boldsymbol{a}\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{s})}\left[\varrho_{i,k}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})g(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{s}\sim\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{s}_{k}\in\cdot|\boldsymbol{s}_{1}),\boldsymbol{a}\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{s})}\left[g(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})\right]\neq\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})

(6)

where
\varrho_{i,k}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})=\frac{\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{s}_{k}=\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}|\boldsymbol{s}_{1})\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{s})}{\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i}=\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}|\boldsymbol{s}_{1})\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{s})}
and
\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})={\mathbb{E}}_{(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})\sim d^{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}}}(\cdot,\cdot)}[g(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}]
with the scenario-based policy gradient estimate

g(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})
. Notice that we write the probability density function of
k
-step transition under a sequence of evolving behavior policies
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k-1})
induced by the optimization search as
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{s}_{k}=\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}|\boldsymbol{s}_{1})\vcentcolon=\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{s}_{k}=\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}|\boldsymbol{s}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k-1})
. Despite this bias, the simplified likelihood ratio
f_{i,k}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})\vcentcolon=\frac{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{s})}{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{s})}
is widely adopted in off-policy methods
(
7
;
30
)
for its computational feasibility, ignoring the state distribution mismatch.

4.2
Likelihood Ratio (LR) Policy Gradient Estimator

The policy gradient
\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})
can be estimated by the individual likelihood ratio (LR) estimator, i.e., using
n
historical samples collected under the behavior policy
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}
with
i<k
,

\widehat{\nabla}J^{LR}_{i,k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{j=1}f_{i,k}\left(\boldsymbol{s}^{(i,j)},\boldsymbol{a}^{(i,j)}\right)g\left(\boldsymbol{s}^{(i,j)},\boldsymbol{a}^{(i,j)}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}\right)~\mbox{with}~f_{i,k}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})=\frac{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{s})}{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{s})}.

(7)

The likelihood ratio
f_{i,k}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})
weights the historical samples
to account for
the mismatch between
the behavior and target policies specified by parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}
.

One way to reuse all the observations associated with the behavioral MDP distributions included in the reuse set
\mathcal{U}_{k}
is to average all individual LR estimators for
{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}\in\mathcal{U}_{k}
, which we call the
likelihood ratio (LR) policy gradient estimator
,

\widehat{\nabla}J^{LR}_{k}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{U}_{k}|}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}\in\mathcal{U}_{k}}\widehat{\nabla}J^{LR}_{i,k}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{U}_{k}|n}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}\in\mathcal{U}_{k}}\sum^{n}_{j=1}\frac{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}}(\boldsymbol{a}^{(i,j)}|\boldsymbol{s}^{(i,j)})}{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{a}^{(i,j)}|\boldsymbol{s}^{(i,j)})}g\left(\boldsymbol{s}^{(i,j)},\boldsymbol{a}^{(i,j)}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}\right).

(8)

For simplification, we allocate a constant number of replications (i.e.,
n
) for each visit at
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

4.3
Clipped Likelihood Ratio (CLR) Policy Gradient Estimator

Though the individual/average LR estimators (
7
) and (
8
) are popular, their variances could be large or even infinite, as the likelihood ratio
f_{i,k}(\cdot,\cdot)
can be large or unbounded
(
55
)
. A technique for mitigating this problem is weight clipping
(
18
)
, which truncates the LR by applying the operator

\min(f_{i,k}(\cdot,\cdot),U_{f})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla}J^{CLR}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{U}_{k}|}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}\in\mathcal{U}_{k}}\widehat{\nabla}J^{CLR}_{i,k},

(9)

\displaystyle\mbox{ with }\widehat{\nabla}J^{CLR}_{i,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{j=1}\min\left(\frac{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}}(\boldsymbol{a}^{(i,j)}|\boldsymbol{s}^{(i,j)})}{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{a}^{(i,j)}|\boldsymbol{s}^{(i,j)})},U_{f}\right)g\left(\boldsymbol{s}^{(i,j)},\boldsymbol{a}^{(i,j)}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}\right).

(10)

Here

is a constant that caps the likelihood ratio at a prescribed upper bound, thereby controlling its variance. This truncation offers a simple and practical way to mitigate the potentially extreme values the likelihood ratio can otherwise take.
Similarly, we refer to
\widehat{\nabla}J^{CLR}_{i,k}
and
\widehat{\nabla}J^{CLR}_{k}
in (
10
) and (
9
) as the individual and average clipped likelihood ratio (CLR) policy gradient estimators.
We conclude this section by
pointing out that we proceed with both theoretical and experimental analysis using biased estimators in (
8
) and (
9
)
in the following sections.

5
Variance Reduction Replay for Policy Optimization

Effective experience replay requires selectively reusing historical samples to balance computational cost, stability, and gradient variance reduction. To address this, we propose a selection criterion in Section
5.1
that targets variance minimization for LR/CLR estimators, supported by the variance estimation techniques in Section
5.2
. To scale with growing history sizes, we derive a computationally efficient approximation of this rule in Section
5.3
. These components culminate in the Variance Reduction Experience Replay (VRER) framework and the generic PG-VRER algorithm presented in Section
5.4
, designed to enhance state-of-the-art policy optimization methods.

5.1
Selection Rule for Reducing Policy Gradient Variance

We aim to construct a reuse set
\mathcal{U}_{k}
that minimizes gradient estimation variance by prioritizing historical observations with stable likelihood ratios. For this purpose, Theorem
1
establishes a selection criterion that bounds the variance inflation of individual LR/CLR estimators (
\widehat{\nabla}J^{R}_{i,k}
with
R\in
{LR,CLR}) relative to the classical PG estimator (
\widehat{\nabla}J^{PG}_{k}
). By enforcing a threshold
c>1
on this ratio, we include current samples while guaranteeing that the estimators (
8
) and (
9
) achieve a variance reduction factor of
c/|\mathcal{U}_{k}|
; see Eq. (
12
). The proof is provided in Appendix

Theorem 1
.

At the
k
-th iteration with the target policy distribution
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}}

\mathcal{U}_{k}
is created to include the behavioral distributions, i.e.,

) and

}

\text{{Selection Rule 1:}}~~~{\rm Tr}\left({\rm Var}\left[\widehat{\nabla}J^{R}_{i,k}\right]\right)\leq c{\rm Tr}\left({\rm Var}\left[\widehat{\nabla}J^{PG}_{k}\right]\right).

(11)

Then, based on such reuse set

, the total variances of the average LR/CLR policy gradient estimators (
8
) and (

) are no greater than the total variance of the PG estimator scaled by the averaged max correlation between individual LR/CLR policy gradient estimates,

{\rm Tr}\left({\rm Var}\left[\widehat{\nabla}J^{R}_{k}\right]\right)\leq\frac{c}{|\mathcal{U}_{k}|^{2}}{\rm Tr}\left({\rm Var}\left[\widehat{\nabla}J^{PG}_{k}\right]\right)\sum_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{k}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{U}_{k}}\max_{\ell=1,2,\ldots,d}\left({\rm Corr}^{(\ell)}_{i,i^{\prime},k}\right).

where

Moreover, it holds that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\widehat{\nabla}J^{R}_{k}\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{c}{|\mathcal{U}_{k}|^{2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{k}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{U}_{k}}\max_{\ell=1,2,\ldots,d}\left({\rm Corr}^{(\ell)}_{i,i^{\prime},k}\right){\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|\widehat{\nabla}J^{PG}_{k}\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+2\left\|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})\right]\right\|^{2}+2\left\|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\widehat{\nabla}J^{R}_{k}\right]-{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})]\right\|^{2}.

5.2
Variance Estimation for Policy Gradient Estimators

When the selection criterion in (
11
) is used to determine the reuse set
\mathcal{U}_{k}
,
we need to estimate the variances of the PG estimator and the individual LR/CLR estimators in (
7
) and (
10
). One key problem is the dependence structure of scenario-based sample gradients. To address it, the Moving Block Bootstrap (MBB) technique
(

;
28
)
is utilized.
Unlike traditional bootstrap methods that resample individual observations, MBB uses a strategy of resampling blocks of consecutive observations so that
the dependence structure inherent in the original dataset is preserved in each block.

Let
g_{i,j}\vcentcolon=\frac{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}}\left(\boldsymbol{a}^{(i,j)}|\boldsymbol{s}^{(i,j)}\right)}{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}\left(\boldsymbol{a}^{(i,j)}|\boldsymbol{s}^{(i,j)}\right)}g\left(\boldsymbol{s}^{(i,j)},\boldsymbol{a}^{(i,j)}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}\right)

observation and suppose
l\in[1,n]
is an integer. Let
X_{j}=(g_{i,j},\ldots,g_{i,j+l-1})
denote the block of length
l
starting with
g_{i,j}
with
1\leq j\leq N
where
N=n-l+1
. To obtain the MBB samples, we randomly select a suitable number of blocks from
the collection
\{X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{N}\}
. Accordingly, let
\{X_{1}^{*},X_{2}^{*},\ldots,X_{N}^{*}\}
denote a bootstrapped sample through drawing with replacement from
\{X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{N}\}
. Let
\bar{g}_{i,j}=(g_{i,j}+g_{i,j+1}+\ldots+g_{i,j+l-1})/l
denote the average of the block. Let
\bar{g}_{i}=N^{-1}\sum^{N}_{j=1}\bar{g}_{i,j}
. Then, the conditional variance estimate of individual LR estimator (
7
)
(
23
, Chapter 3.2.1)

and its trace are

\widehat{{\rm Var}}\left[\widehat{\nabla}J^{LR}_{i,k}\right]=\frac{l}{n}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{j=1}\bar{g}_{i,j}\bar{g}_{i,j}^{\top}-\bar{g}_{i}\bar{g}_{i}^{\top}\right]\mbox{ and }{\rm Tr}\left(\widehat{{\rm Var}}\left[\widehat{\nabla}J^{LR}_{i,k}\right]\right)=\frac{l}{n}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{j=1}\|\bar{g}_{i,j}\|^{2}-\|\bar{g}_{i}\|^{2}\right].

(13)

This approach can be applied to the individual CLR estimator (

) by clipping the likelihood ratios in

.
Similarly, the variance of the PG estimator and its trace are

\widehat{{\rm Var}}\left[\widehat{\nabla}J^{PG}_{k}\right]=\frac{l}{n}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{j=1}\bar{g}_{k,j}\bar{g}_{k,j}^{\top}-\bar{g}_{k}\bar{g}_{k}^{\top}\right]\mbox{ and }{\rm Tr}\left(\widehat{{\rm Var}}\left[\widehat{\nabla}J^{PG}_{k}\right]\right)=\frac{l}{n}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{j=1}\|\bar{g}_{k,j}\|^{2}-\|\bar{g}_{k}\|^{2}\right].

(14)

The consistency of the MBB estimators has been rigorously established.

demonstrates that under mild conditions of the moment and strong mixing of the stochastic process

for the verification of the regularity conditions.

5.3
Gradient Variance Ratio Approximation

The selection rule (
11
) could be computationally expensive for complex systems, especially as the size of the historical set, i.e.,
|\mathcal{F}_{k}|
, becomes large since it requires the repeated calculation of the bootstrapped variances of individual LR policy gradient estimator in (
7
) for each
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
.
To support real-time decision making, we provide second-order Taylor
approximations to the policy gradient variance ratio,

{\rm Tr}({\rm Var}[\widehat{\nabla}J^{LR}_{i,k}])/{\rm Tr}({\rm Var}[\widehat{\nabla}J^{PG}_{k}])
, for step-based algorithms.

To develop an efficient selection rule, we now turn to a more adaptable scenario. Instead of iteration-based selection, we scan historical samples individually in the buffer and select those that are likely to exhibit lower gradient variance. By focusing on individual samples, we have the flexibility to adjust the number of steps per iteration to
n=1
. We then provide the approximation of selection rule (
11
) in Proposition
2
by using second-order Taylor expansion. The proof is given in Appendix
E
. A similar approach can be applied to the individual CLR policy gradient estimator in (
10
) by clipping all likelihood ratios in the proof and the conclusion in Proposition
2
holds for the CLR estimator by replacing the KL divergence term in (
15
) with
{\mathbb{E}}[\min(\frac{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{s})}{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{s})},U_{f})]
.

Proposition 2
.

Under Assumptions
A.1
and
A.2
, let
\eta_{k}=\eta_{1}k^{-r}
denote the learning rate used in the
k
-th iteration with two constants
\eta_{1}\in(0,\frac{1}{4L}]
and
r\in(0,1)
. For any
t
such that
t<i\leq k
, if
t=o(k^{r/2})
and the buffer size
B_{k}=o(k^{r/2})
, the total variance ratio of the individual LR policy gradient estimator and PG estimator has the approximation

\frac{{\rm Tr}\left({\rm Var}\left[\widehat{\nabla}J^{LR}_{i,k}\right]\right)}{{\rm Tr}\left({\rm Var}\left[\widehat{\nabla}J^{PG}_{k}\right]\right)}\approx e^{{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm{KL}}\left(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{s})\|\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{s})\right)\right]}\left(1+\zeta_{k}^{-1}\right)-\zeta^{-1}_{k},

(15)

where
\zeta_{k}={{\rm Tr}({\rm Var}[\widehat{\nabla}J^{PG}_{k}])}/{\|{\mathbb{E}}[\widehat{\nabla}J^{PG}_{k}]\|^{2}}
is the relative variance.

To avoid the intensive computation, the moving averages of stochastic gradients and their element-wise square from Adam optimizer
(
20
)
to first and second moment of policy gradient are used, i.e.,

\displaystyle\tilde{m}_{k}

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}\tilde{m}_{k-1}+(1-\beta_{1})g_{k}~~~\mbox{and}~~~m_{k}=\frac{\tilde{m}_{k}}{1-\beta_{1}^{k+1}},

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{k}

\displaystyle=\beta_{2}\tilde{v}_{k-1}+(1-\beta_{2})g_{k}^{2}~~~\mbox{and}~~~v_{k}=\frac{\tilde{v}_{k}}{1-\beta_{2}^{k+1}},

with
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in(0,1)
and
g_{k}
is the gradient estimate at iteration

. Based on the insights presented in

, the term
v_{k}-m_{k}^{2}
can be seen as an estimate of the stochastic gradient variances and
\hat{\zeta}_{k}=(v_{k}-m_{k}^{2})/m_{k}^{2}
is an estimate of the relative variance. Utilizing the first and second moment estimators from the Adam optimizer eliminates the need for additional computational resources to estimate gradient variance in
\zeta_{k}
. The only computational demand arises from calculating the KL divergence between policies.

Then by plugging the relative variance estimates
\zeta_{k}\approx\hat{\zeta}_{k}
in the approximation (
15
), after rearranging both sides, the selection rule (
11
) becomes

\mbox{{Selection Rule 2:}}~~~{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mbox{KL}\left(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{s})\|\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{s})\right)\right]\leq\log\left(1+(c-1)\frac{\hat{\zeta}_{k}}{\hat{\zeta}_{k}+1}\right).

(16)

This new selection rule can be viewed as a dynamic threshold of the distance between behavior and target policies depending on the relative variance
\hat{\zeta}_{k}
. When
c=1
, it becomes a very conservative bound that only selects samples generated from the target policy
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}
.

Remark 1

(Bias Reduction)

The correlation in state transitions

not only affects the estimation variance but also introduces bias in the gradient estimators. Consequently, when applying the variance-based selection rule (

Remark 2

There is always a trade-off on specifying the replay buffer size to balance exploration and exploitation.
As

the improvements caused by increasing the replay capacity and covering large state-action space;
and (2) the deterioration caused by having older policies in the

Remark 3
.

The importance sampling approach, used to leverage the information from historical samples, requires sampling distributions to be independent with each other.
The interdependencies between historical samples can lead to an obstacle for both the stability and convergence of policy optimization. To reduce this interdependence, we utilize randomly sampling strategy, i.e., draw with replacement
n_{0}
(with
n_{0}<n
) samples from selected historical observations with

8
Conclusion

To improve the sample efficiency of policy optimization algorithms, we introduce a novel experience replay approach known as Variance Reduction Experience Replay (VRER) by integrating the classical experience replay method with a sample selection rule designed to reduce gradient variance.
By selectively reusing the most relevant historical samples and automatically allocating more weights to those samples that are more likely generated from the target distribution, VRER guarantees to reduce the policy gradient estimation variance. This integration represents a significant advancement in improving the efficiency of policy optimization algorithms according to both theoretical and empirical studies.

Despite their popularity, experience replay methods in RL lack theoretical investigations into their finite-time convergence and limiting properties, highlighting a gap in the literature. Our proposed theoretical framework addresses this gap by considering sample dependence due to Markovian noise and behavior policy interdependence. It elucidates the effectiveness of experience replay methods and explains (1) why reusing too old samples could reduce the policy optimization performance; and (2) how the performance is affected by the buffer size through a bias-variance trade-off in policy gradient estimation. By applying this new theory, we further study the finite-time convergence of the PG-VRER approach, providing a theoretical grounding for practical applications.

The proposed VRER is general and it can be seamlessly integrated with SOTA policy gradient approaches to improve their performance. In practice, it is simple to apply VRER as it does not require any structural change of the original algorithms (e.g., PPO, TRPO, and A2C); only
by incorporating the selection rule before the training phase.
Extensive empirical studies have shown that our approach can substantially increase the convergence rate of SOTA policy gradient methods and enhance their performance.
