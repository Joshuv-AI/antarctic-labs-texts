Exploring the dynamics of symbiont Enterobacter aerogenes expressing Cry1Ac for potential biocontrol of Helicoverpa armigera
Authors: Abhishek Ojha, Mohd Usman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParatransgenesis, the use of genetically modified symbiotic microbes to deliver bioactive molecules, offers a promising strategy for sustainable pest management. However, the colonization dynamics and ecological interactions of such engineered microbes remain insufficiently understood. This study evaluated the potential of Enterobacter aerogenes, a gut symbiont of Helicoverpa armigera, as a paratransgenic vector for delivering the Bacillus thuringiensis Cry1Ac toxin. Two plasmid constructs were introduced into E. aerogenes: one encoding Cry1Ac (construct 2: cry1Ac-KmR-pUC18) and another encoding green fluorescent protein (GFP) (construct 3: KmR-pGFP). GFP-expressing E. aerogenes (construct 3) successfully colonized the gut of H. armigera larvae within 48 h (SE ± 0.051) and the tomato phyllosphere within 24 h (SE ± 0.026), demonstrating its ability to establish in both insect and plant environments. The insecticidal activity of Cry1Ac-producing Escherichia coli (K12) and engineered E. aerogenes was evaluated using diet incorporation and leaf-dip bioassays. E. coli (K12) served as the control expression host for comparison. Feeding assays with diets containing 105–10⁹ transformed cells produced larval mortality of 86% to 100% (SE ± 0.962–1.354) for E. coli (K12) and 79% to 100% (SE ± 0.959–2.348) for E. aerogenes. Foliar application of Cry1Ac-producing E. aerogenes at 1.0 × 108 cells per leaf resulted in 100% larval mortality within 192 h (SE ± 0.540). These findings establish a foundation for the development of E. aerogenes as a paratransgenic vector for integrated pest management. Introduction

Numerous economically important insect pests cause substantial damage to global agriculture. However, the management of these pests is increasingly undermined by the widespread development of insecticide resistance (Robinson et al. 2004). This growing challenge highlights the urgent need for innovative, sustainable pest management strategies (Beard et al. 1993). One such strategy is paratransgenesis, which involves genetically modifying symbiotic microorganisms within insect hosts to express molecules that negatively affect insect survival and fitness (Chapco 1994; Tang et al. 2004). By targeting insect-microbe mutualisms, paratransgenesis offers a potentially species-specific and environmentally sustainable approach to pest suppression.

Host specificity is a critical determinant of the success of paratransgenic systems, as native gut-associated microorganisms are more likely to persist within the insect host and efficiently deliver heterologous gene products. Compared with non-native microorganisms, endogenous strains typically exhibit superior colonization and persistence within the host, whereas non-native strains often fail to establish stable populations (Chapco 1994; Husseneder & Grace 2005; Thimm et al. 1998; Veivers et al. 1982). The use of well-adapted, host-specific microorganisms not only enhances delivery efficiency but also reduces ecological risks, including unintended effects on nontarget organisms and environmental dissemination (Irvin et al. 2004; van Elsas et al. 1994). These concerns may be further mitigated through the incorporation of genetic containment strategies, such as stability-enhancing constructs or biological kill-switch mechanisms (Hillman 2002).

In polyphagous insect pests such as Helicoverpa armigera (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae), a diverse and adaptable gut microbiota contributes to the digestion and detoxification of a wide range of plant-derived compounds, enabling feeding on numerous host plants (Gayatri et al. 2012; Cazemier et al. 2003; Lemke et al. 2003). Several bacterial taxa have been consistently identified in the gut microbiota of H. armigera (Gayatri et al. 2012), Melolontha melolontha (Egert et al. 2005), and Dermolepida albohirtum (Pittman et al. 2008), indicating the presence of stable, host-adapted microbial communities across different geographic populations. These persistent symbionts represent promising candidates for paratransgenic strategies in pest management (Pittman et al. 2008). By engineering these microorganisms to express insecticidal proteins or gene-silencing RNA molecules, it may be possible to disrupt essential physiological processes in the insect host, thereby providing a targeted and environmentally sustainable alternative to conventional chemical insecticides.

Previous studies have identified Enterobacter aerogenes (Enterobacterales: Enterobacteriaceae) as consistent members of the gut microbiota of H. armigera larvae collected from multiple host plants and geographic regions in India (Gayatri et al. 2012). Based on its stable association with the host, we hypothesized that E. aerogenes could serve as a paratransgenic delivery vehicle, particularly if it is capable of colonizing both the larval gut and the tomato phyllosphere. In this study, we assessed the colonization ability of a genetically engineered E. aerogenes strain expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP) and kanamycin resistance (KmR) in both environments. We further evaluated the insecticidal efficacy of a Cry1Ac-expressing strain by assessing larval mortality following ingestion in both gut colonization and phyllosphere feeding bioassays. These findings provide a foundation for evaluating E. aerogenes as a dual-niche paratransgenic agent for the sustainable management of this economically important pest species.

Materials and methods

Insect larval sampling

A laboratory strain of H. armigera was maintained at the International Centre for Genetic Engineering and Biotechnology (ICGEB), New Delhi, India, under controlled conditions (25 ± 5 °C, 70 ± 5% relative humidity (RH), 14:10 h light:dark photoperiod). Neonates were reared on an artificial diet following the protocols of Shorey and Hale (1965) and Sachdev et al. (2014). First-instar larvae were used for all bioassays, with ten larvae per replicate and three replicates per treatment. Each experiment was independently repeated three times.

Recombineering, transformation, and protein expression

The cry1Ac gene was amplified from 10 ng of the cry1Ac-pKK223-3 vector (Bacillus Genetic Stock Center, Columbus, OH, USA) by polymerase chain reaction (PCR; Bio-Rad, USA) using the forward primer 5'-CCCGGGATGTGAACAGTGCCCTTACAA-3' (containing an NdeI recognition site) and the reverse primer 5'-CATATGCCACGCTGTCCACGATAAATG-3' (containing a Cfr9I or XmaI site). Each 25 µl PCR mixture contained 200 µM dNTPs, 5 U of Taq DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs, USA), and 200 µM of each primer. Amplification was performed with initial denaturation at 95ºC for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95ºC for 30 s, 55ºC for 30 s, and 72ºC for 45 s, with a final extension at 72ºC for 5 min. The resulting ~ 3000 bp amplicon was separated on a 1.5% agarose gel prepared in TAE buffer (Sambrook & Russell 2001), stained with ethidium bromide, and visualized under UV light. The DNA fragment was gel-purified using the QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Germany) and quantified using a NanoVue Plus spectrometer (data not shown). The purified cry1Ac fragment was subsequently digested with NdeI and Cfr9I and ligated into the pUC18 vector to generate construct 1 (cry1Ac-pUC18). Competent Escherichia coli DH5α cells were transformed with 10 ng of construct 1 following Sambrook and Russell (2001). Transformants (E. coli cells transformed with construct 1) were selected on Luria–Bertani (LB) agar supplemented with 100 µg/ml ampicillin (HiMedia Laboratories Pvt. Ltd.), and positive colonies were verified by restriction digestion, PCR, and Sanger sequencing (data not shown). A 1.5 kb kanamycin-resistance (KmR) cassette was amplified from pET-28a using primers containing PvuI restriction sites (forward primer: 5’-CGATCGGATAAACCCAGCGAACCATTTGAG-3’, and reverse primer: 5’-CGATCGCCACGCTGTCCACGATAAATG-3’) (Banai et al. 1985; Chandra et al. 2008). The PCR products were separated on a 1.0% TAE agarose gel, gel-purified, and quantified (data not shown). The KmR PCR product was then purified, digested with PvuI, and ligated into construct 1 and the pGFP vector (Clontech, Takara, Inc.) to generate constructs 2 (cry1Ac-KmR-pUC18) and 3 (KmR-pGFP), respectively.

Each construct was independently transformed into E. coli cells, and transformants were selected on LB agar supplemented with ampicillin (50 µg/ml). The resulting constructs were confirmed by PCR and restriction enzyme digestion. Constructs 2 and 3 were subsequently introduced into E. aerogenes and E. coli K12 strains isolated from the gut of H. armigera (Gayatri et al. 2012). Expression of construct 3 (in the transformed strains) was confirmed by growth on LB-agar and broth containing kanamycin after overnight incubation at 37ºC. E. coli K12 served as a control host for recombinant Cry1Ac expression alongside E. aerogenes (Sahu & Kar 2012).

Protein gel blot analysis

Western blot analysis was performed to detect Cry1Ac expression in recombinant E. aerogenes and E. coli K12 strains carrying construct 2. Cell pellets were resuspended in protein extraction buffer according to Ojha and Zhang (2021), and whole-cell lysates were denatured at 99ºC for 10 min in 1 × SDS-PAGE loading buffer. Fifty micrograms of total protein were separated on a 7.5% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred to a 0.4 µm nitrocellulose membrane (GE Healthcare) using a wet-transfer system at 180 mA for 45 min. Membranes were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in 10 mM phosphate buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) (Ojha et al. 2014) and incubated for 1 h at room temperature (RT) with rabbit anti-Cry1Ac primary antibody (1:5,000). After washing, membranes were incubated for 1 h with alkaline phosphatase (ALP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:3,000). Immunoreactive bands were visualized using BCIP/NBT (5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl phosphate/nitro blue tetrazolium) substrate (Genei Laboratories Pvt Ltd, Bangalore, India).

Feeding of helicoverpa armigera larvae with GFP-expressing bacteria

To evaluate the colonization potential of recombinant E. aerogenes and E. coli K12 strains harboring construct 3 (expressing GFP and kanamycin resistance) in the gut of H. armigera, 60 first-instar larvae (neonates) were initially fed a sterile artificial diet supplemented with antibiotics for 48 h, following the protocol described by Brummel et al. (2004). The artificial diet was supplemented with ampicillin (500 μg/mL), tetracycline (50 μg/mL), and rifamycin (200 μg/mL). This antibiotic treatment was intended to reduce the abundance of the native gut microbiota and thereby facilitate monitoring of colonization by the introduced recombinant strains. The treatment was designed to suppress the native microbial community rather than generate axenic larvae. Because microbiota depletion was not quantified using culture-independent methods or measurements of total bacterial load, the reduction was considered partial and was not assumed to represent complete elimination of the resident gut microorganisms. Following antibiotic treatment, the larvae were transferred to a diet inoculated with recombinant bacteria, as described below. Each larva was provided with approximately 1 g of diet in cube form inoculated with 100 µl/cm2 of an overnight culture of recombinant E. aerogenes harboring construct 3. After 48 h, the larvae were transferred to a regular artificial diet without antibiotics. A control group was maintained on the same artificial diet inoculated with the native E. aerogenes strain isolated from H. armigera. Fecal samples were collected, homogenized in sterile LB broth, and serially diluted (10–1, 10–2, 10–3, 10–4, 10–5, and 10–6). For the analysis, gut colonization data were first standardized to a common scale. Colonization was quantified as CFU equivalents per mg of feces using 10% fecal homogenates and serial dilution plating. For statistical analysis and graphical presentation, all values were log-transformed. Aliquots (100 µl) from each dilution were plated on LB agar supplemented with kanamycin (20 μg/ml) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The resulting colonies were examined for GFP fluorescence to confirm the presence of construct 3. An identical experiment was performed using recombinant E. coli K12 harboring construct 3, with a corresponding control group fed the native E. coli K12 strain.

Colonization of the tomato phyllosphere by Enterobacter aerogenes

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) plants were maintained under greenhouse conditions at the ICGEB, New Delhi. Leaves (20–25 days old) were rinsed five times with sterile distilled water before inoculation to remove surface contaminants. Overnight cultures of E. aerogenes carrying construct 3 were harvested by centrifugation, and the resulting cell pellets were washed three times with 20 ml of PBS (pH 7.4). The resulting bacterial suspension was adjusted to a final concentration of 8 × 108 cells/ml and sprayed uniformly onto leaf surfaces. After spray bacterial suspension, the plants were enclosed in plastic tents and maintained at 22ºC for 6 h on the laboratory bench. They were then transferred to a growth chamber (28ºC, 95% RH, 12-h light/12-h dark photoperiod) and incubated for an additional 3 days. Leaf samples (three leaves per plant; three biological replicates) were collected at 24, 96, and 192 h after inoculation. Each leaf was washed five times with PBS (pH 7.4) and transferred to a 15 ml culture tube containing 10 ml LB broth containing kanamycin (50 µg/ml). The tubes were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h in a shaking incubator at 200 rpm. From each culture, aliquots (50 µl) were plated on LB agar containing kanamycin (20 µg/ml) and incubated at 37 °C for 15–17 h to assess bacterial colonization of the phyllosphere. Phyllosphere colonization was normalized to leaf biomass and expressed as CFU per gram of leaf tissue. All values were log₁₀-transformed prior to statistical analysis and graphical presentation. The experiment was repeated independently three times.

Diet incorporation bioassays

The insecticidal activity of recombinant E. aerogenes carrying construct 2 was evaluated using a diet incorporation assay. Artificial diet was inoculated with bacterial suspension at concentrations of 105, 106, 107, 108, and 109 cells/cm2. Thirty first-instar larvae were tested at each bacterial concentration, whereas larvae fed an artificial diet containing bacteria harboring Construct 3 (10⁹ cells/cm2) served as the control group. Bacterial suspensions were prepared in PBS (pH 7.4). 100 µl was applied to the surface of the diet in each well of a 12-well plate. After air-drying for 1 h under sterile conditions, ten larvae were introduced into each well. Plate lids contained six ventilation holes (2.5 mm diameter). Mortality was recorded after 24, 48, and 72 h and corrected using Abbott’s formula (Abbott 1925) to account for natural mortality. An identical bioassay was conducted with recombinant E. coli K12 carrying construct 2 using the same concentrations and corresponding construct 3 controls at 109 cells/cm2. All assays were performed under standard insectary conditions (25 ± 5 °C, 70 ± 5% RH, and 14:10 h light:dark photoperiod).

Leaf dip bioassay

A leaf dip bioassay was performed to evaluate the insecticidal activity of recombinant E. aerogenes strains against H. armigera larvae. Leaves were collected from 20–25-day-old tomato plants. Harvested leaves were rinsed twice with sterile distilled water for 10 min each to remove surface contaminants. Overnight cultures (16–18 h at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm) of wild-type E. aerogenes, E. aerogenes (construct 2), and E. aerogenes (construct 3) were prepared. The cultures were centrifuged, and the cell pellets were washed three times with sterile PBS (pH 7.4). The washed cells were resuspended in 1 ml of PBS (pH 7.4), and the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was measured. Based on the OD600 values, the cell concentrations were adjusted to approximately 1.5 × 108 cells/ml for the wild-type and construct 3 strains and 1.0 × 108 cells/ml for construct 2. For each treatment, 100 µl of the bacterial suspension was evenly applied to the surface of an individual tomato leaf. The treated leaves were air-dried under a laminar flow hood for 1 h and then placed on 10 ml of 0.8% solidified agarose in Petri dishes. Ten first-instar H. armigera larvae were transferred onto each treated leaf. The treatment consisted of four replicates. Larval mortality was recorded at 24, 48, 72, 96, and 192 h postexposure. Mortality was corrected for natural mortality using Abbott’s formula (Abbott 1925).

Statistical analysis

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) version 27.0 to assess differences in symbiont colonization and larval mortality among treatment groups. Homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. When the assumption of equal variance was rejected (p < 0.05), differences among groups were analysed using Welch’s one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Pairwise comparisons were performed using the Games-Howell post hoc test, which is appropriate for unequal variances. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The green fluorescent protein (GFP)-encoding plasmid (pGFP) was successfully expressed in both E. coli K12 and E. aerogenes, as confirmed by green fluorescence on ampicillin-supplemented agar plates. Only a few untransformed E. aerogenes colonies appeared on ampicillin-containing medium, indicating that most cells were resistant to ampicillin (data not shown). Because previous studies have reported susceptibility of E. aerogenes to kanamycin (Davin-Regli et al. 2019), the kanamycin susceptibility was further evaluated. No growth was observed on agar containing 30 mg/ml kanamycin, confirming that E. aerogenes was susceptible to kanamycin under the experimental conditions (data not shown).

Colonization of the H. armigera gut and the tomato phyllosphere by E. aerogenes

To enable selection of transformed bacteria, a kanamycin resistance gene (KmR) was inserted into the pGFP plasmid, generating a 4689 bp recombinant plasmid (construct 3, KmR-pGFP; Fig. S1). This construct was successfully introduced into E. aerogenes, producing stable GFP-expressing transformants (data not shown). The construct was designed to establish a heterologous protein expression system in E. aerogenes by utilizing the core transcriptional machinery (RNA polymerase, RNAP) and promoter elements conserved among natural isolates of the order Enterobacterales (Gao et al. 1997; Severinov 2000). GFP expression was confirmed by fluorescence microscopy, UV transilluminator, and fluorescence spectroscopy, which showed excitation and emission maxima at 488 and 513 nm, respectively (data not shown). Under standard culture conditions, all transformants exhibited uniform green fluorescence (data not shown).

To evaluate gut colonization, H. armigera neonates were initially fed an antibiotic-supplemented artificial diet for two days to reduce the native gut microbiota. The larvae were then transferred to a diet containing GFP-expressing E. aerogenes (construct 3) for two days before being returned to an antibiotic-free diet. Gut colonization by GFP-expressing E. aerogenes increased from 1.01 × 101⁰ to 4.81 × 101⁰ CFU equivalents per mg of feces over 192 h, corresponding to an increase from log₁₀ 5.00 to 6.68. This suggests stable persistence in the larval gut environment (Fig. 1a and Table S1a). Levene's test indicated that the assumption of homogeneity of variances was violated (P = 0.008) (Table S2a). Therefore, bacterial colony counts were analyzed using Welch's one-way ANOVA (Table S2a), followed by the Games–Howell post hoc test for pairwise comparisons (Table S2a). Welch's ANOVA revealed no statistically significant differences in bacterial colony counts among the sampling times (F (7, 9.322) = 1.827, P = 0.193). Consistent with this finding, the Games-Howell post hoc analysis detected no significant pairwise differences between sampling times (all adjusted P > 0.05) (Table S2). These results indicate that the observed increases in bacterial colony counts over time were not statistically significant after accounting for unequal variances.

The ability of GFP-expressing E. aerogenes to colonize the tomato phyllosphere was evaluated following foliar application. Bacterial colonization of tomato leaves increased from 5.0 × 102 to 2.55 × 103 CFU/g of leaf tissue over 192 h (log₁₀ 2.70–3.41), indicating progressive establishment on the phyllosphere (Fig. 1b and Table S1b). The number of colonies formed by E. aerogenes (construct 3) isolated from the tomato surface increased dramatically over the sampling period. Levene's test indicated that the group variances were homogeneous (P = 0.672) (Table S2b), confirming that the assumption of equal variances for a one-way ANOVA was met. The one-way ANOVA revealed that sampling time had a significant effect on the number of bacterial colonies (F(2, 6) = 709.04, P < 0.001) (Table S2b). Tukey's HSD and Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons showed significant differences between all sampling times (24 vs. 96 h, 24 vs. 192 h, and 96 vs. 192 h; all adjusted P < 0.001) (Table S2b), indicating that the number of E. aerogenes colonies increased significantly throughout the study period.

Cloning and expression of the cry1Ac and KmR genes

To generate construct 2 (cry1Ac-KmR-pUC18), the 3000 bp cry1Ac gene and the 1489 bp KmR gene fragment were amplified by PCR (data not shown). The cry1Ac gene was first cloned into the pUC18 vector (Fig. 2a) in-frame with the start codon of the lacZ open reading frame (ORF), generating a 5435 bp intermediate construct (construct 1: cry1Ac-pUC18) (Fig. 2b, data not shown). Subsequently, the 1489 bp KmR fragment was inserted into construct 1, resulting in the 6924 bp final construct (construct 2: cry1Ac-KmR-pUC18) (Fig. 2c). Construct 2 was successfully introduced into both the E. aerogenes and E. coli K12. Expression of the cry1Ac gene, driven by the lac promoter, was detected in both hosts following overnight culture (Fig. S2). Western blot analysis using an anti-Cry1Ac antibody confirmed the expression of the expected ~ 133 kDa Cry1Ac protein in both E. aerogenes construct 2 and E. coli K12 construct 2 (Fig. S2).

Diet and leaf-dip incorporation insect bioassays

A total of 420 H. armigera neonates were used to evaluate the insecticidal activity of recombinant bacterial constructs. The treatments consisted of overnight cultures of E. coli K12 (constructs 2 and 3) (Table 1) and E. aerogenes (constructs 2 and 3) (Table 2). Five concentrations of Cry1Ac-expressing E. coli K12 cells (105, 106, 107, 108, and 109 cells per treatment) were tested. All concentrations caused larval mortality, with 100% corrected mortality observed at 106, 107, 108, and 109 cells. The lowest concentration (105 cells) resulted in 86% corrected mortality (Table 1). Negative controls using KmR-pGFP-expressing E. aerogenes (Table 2) and KmR-pGFP-expressing E. coli K12 at 10⁹ cells (Table 1) resulted in no mortality (0% corrected), indicating that plasmid carriage and expression of the marker genes alone were not associated with detectable larval mortality under the experimental conditions. The SE values were consistent across biological replicates. For the negative control (E. coli K12 construct 3 at 109 cells), the SE was low (± 0.272), showing minimal variation across the six replicates. For E. coli K12 construct 2, SE values ranged from ± 0.962 to ± 1.354, indicating minor variation despite complete (100%) mortality at concentrations above 106 cells (Table 1). Overall, these low SE values suggest good experimental reproducibility. One-way ANOVA showed that exposure time significantly affected insect mortality at all bacterial concentrations (105–10⁹ cells) (Table S3). Homogeneity of variance was satisfied for 105–10⁸ cells (Levene's test, p > 0.05), whereas partial heterogeneity was observed at 10⁹ cells (See Table S3 for detailed analysis). Mortality differed significantly among exposure periods at 105 (F (2,6) = 24.80, p = 0.001), 10⁶ (F (2,6) = 31.20, p = 0.001), 10⁷ (F (2,6) = 36.60, p < 0.001), 10⁸ (F (2,6) = 25.80, p = 0.001), and 10⁹ cells (F (2,6) = 109.50, p < 0.001) (See Table S3 for detailed analysis). Tukey's HSD and Bonferroni tests consistently showed significantly higher mortality at 48 h than at 24 h and 72 h for 105–10⁸ cells, while differences between 24 and 72 h were not significant. In contrast, at 10⁹ cells, mortality increased progressively from 24 to 72 h, with all pairwise comparisons showing significant differences (p ≤ 0.003), indicating maximum mortality at 72 h (Table S3).

Similarly, five concentrations of Cry1Ac-expressing E. aerogenes (construct 2) cells (105, 106, 107, 108, and 109 cells) were evaluated. Complete mortality (100% corrected mortality) was observed at 108 and 109 cells, whereas the lowest concentration (105 cells) resulted in the lowest corrected mortality (79%) (Table 2). These results suggest that both E. coli K12 and E. aerogenes can serve as effective delivery systems for Cry1Ac-mediated insecticidal activity against H. armigera. The SE values reflected the reproducibility of mortality outcomes. For the negative control (E. aerogenes construct 3 at 10⁹ cells), the SE was low (± 0.136), indicating minimal variation across the six replicates. For Cry1Ac-expressing E. aerogenes (construct 2), SE values ranged from ± 0.959 to ± 2.348 (Table 2), with the highest variability observed at 10⁸ and 109 cells despite 100% mortality, suggesting minor differences in larval responses among replicates. One-way ANOVA revealed that exposure time significantly affected insect mortality at all bacterial concentrations (105–10⁹ cells) of E. aerogenes (construct 2) expressing Cry1Ac (Table S4). Homogeneity of variance was satisfied at 10⁶−10⁸ cells (Levene's test, p > 0.05), while partial heterogeneity was observed at 105 and 10⁹ cells (Table S4). Significant differences among exposure periods were observed at 105 (F (2,6) = 16.13, p = 0.004), 10⁶ (F (2,6) = 39.20, p < 0.001), 10⁷ (F (2,6) = 49.33, p < 0.001), 10⁸ (F (2,6) = 16.29, p = 0.004), and 10⁹ cells (F (2,6) = 109.50, p < 0.001) (See Table S4 for detailed analysis). Post hoc analyses (Tukey's HSD and Bonferroni) showed that mortality generally increased with exposure time, peaking at 72 h for 105 and 10⁹ cells, at 48 h for 10⁶ and 10⁸ cells, while all pairwise comparisons were significant at 10⁷ cells (See Table S4 for detailed analysis).

To evaluate the bioefficacy of E. aerogenes (construct 2) under conditions mimicking natural exposure, a leaf-dip bioassay was performed. Detached tomato leaves were dipped in suspensions of E. aerogenes wild-type (1.5 × 108 cells), construct 3 (1.5 × 108 cells), or construct 2 (1.0 × 108 cells). A total of 120 neonates (10 neonates per leaf, with 4 replicates per treatment) were then introduced onto the treated leaves (Table 3). Only the Cry1Ac-expressing E. aerogenes (construct 2) treatment resulted in 100% larval mortality (40/40), whereas neither the wild-type strain nor the KmR-pGFP-expressing construct 3 strain caused mortality (0/40 in both treatments), supporting an association between insecticidal activity and Cry1Ac expression (Table 3). SE values confirmed high reproducibility. SE could not be calculated for treatments with zero mortality due to the absence of variation among replicates. In contrast, construct 2 (1.0 × 108 cells) showed an SE of ± 0.540, indicating low variability and consistent insecticidal activity. One-way ANOVA showed a significant effect of exposure time (24–192 h) on larval mortality in the leaf-dip bioassay with Enterobacter aerogenes (construct 2) expressing Cry1Ac at 1.0 × 10⁸ CFU mL⁻1 (F (4,15) = 3.75, p = 0.026) (See Table S5 for detailed analysis). Homogeneity of variance was satisfied (Levene's test, p = 0.059) (Table S5). Tukey's HSD indicated significantly higher mortality at 72 h than at 24 h (p = 0.042), whereas Bonferroni-adjusted comparisons detected no significant pairwise differences (p > 0.05) (See Table S5 for detailed analysis).

Discussion

The gut microbiota of H. armigera, including E. aerogenes, has attracted increasing attention because of its important role in host physiology and its potential application in paratransgenic pest management. Previous studies have demonstrated that insect-associated symbionts can be genetically modified to express biologically active molecules, including insecticidal toxins, within their insect hosts (Pittman et al. 2008; Durvasula et al. 1997; Kuzina et al. 2002). Building on previous paratransgenic studies, the present work demonstrates that a naturally occurring gut symbiont can be engineered to deliver an insecticidal protein while retaining characteristics that are desirable for paratransgenic applications. The ability of recombinant E. aerogenes to establish in both the insect gut and the plant phyllosphere supports the feasibility of symbiont-mediated delivery of Cry1Ac under laboratory conditions and highlights the potential of host-associated bacteria as biologically relevant alternatives to conventional laboratory expression hosts.

Although these investigations have demonstrated that E. aerogenes can function as a paratransgenic agent under controlled laboratory conditions, its practical application in agricultural settings requires careful consideration of ecological, evolutionary, and biosafety challenges. Key factors that will determine the long-term success and sustainability of this approach include the persistence of the engineered symbiont within natural microbial communities, the stability of transgene expression under environmental stresses, the evolutionary responses of the target pest, and the potential environmental impacts of the recombinant genes. Therefore, this study should be regarded as an initial proof of concept demonstrating the feasibility of symbiont-mediated delivery of Cry1Ac to H. armigera.

We previously reported that H. armigera harbors a diverse gut microbial community in (Gayatri et al. 2012). The repeated detection of E. aerogenes in geographically distinct larval populations supports its ecological association with H. armigera and strengthens its suitability as a candidate symbiont for paratransgenic applications. Similar associations have been reported in other lepidopteran insects, underscoring the broader ecological and functional significance of E. aerogenes in host-microbe interactions. These results support the potential application of E. aerogenes in paratransgenesis, wherein symbiotic gut bacteria are genetically engineered to express insecticidal proteins within insect hosts. However, previous efforts to express proteins such as Cyt1A in recombinant Enterobacter strains have faced challenges, including low yields and protein misfolding. These issues likely stem from incompatibilities in transcriptional regulation and plasmid replication compared with conventional expression systems such as E. coli or Bacillus thuringiensis (Kuzina et al. 2002).

One of the major challenges in developing paratransgenic systems is ensuring that genetically modified symbiotic bacteria are efficiently acquired by insect larvae during feeding and can stably colonize the gut environment (Watanabe et al. 2000). To address this, the present study evaluated the colonization capacity of E. aerogenes strains in both the larval gut and the plant phyllosphere, demonstrating their dual potential for horizontal transmission and ecological persistence. A limitation of this study is that gut colonization was evaluated in larvae treated with antibiotics prior to bacterial introduction. This approach was used to reduce variability in the background microbiota and facilitate tracking of the engineered strain of E. aerogenes. The extent of microbiota reduction was not quantitatively assessed; therefore, the antibiotic treatment was considered to have partially depleted the native microbial community rather than completely eliminating it. Accordingly, the observed colonization dynamics should be interpreted as occurring in a microbiologically simplified gut environment rather than in a fully intact native microbial community. However, the native gut microbiota may influence the establishment and persistence of the introduced bacteria through competitive and ecological interactions. Consequently, colonization patterns observed under laboratory conditions may differ substantially from those in insects with an intact microbial community. Antibiotic pretreatment may also have influenced host physiology, and potential sublethal effects on development or immune function were not assessed in this study. Future studies should assess the efficacy of recombinant E. aerogenes in larvae that have not undergone antibiotic treatment and under conditions that more closely resemble natural populations. E. aerogenes, a member of the Enterobacteriaceae family, shares several genetic and physiological traits with E. coli, a model organism widely used for recombinant protein expression because of its well-characterized promoter systems, including the T7 system (Madigan et al. 2000; Cronan 2014). However, E. coli has a limited capacity for gut colonization in insects, which limits its applicability in paratransgenic approaches. In contrast, E. aerogenes is a naturally occurring symbiont in H. armigera, making it a more ecologically compatible candidate for in vivo delivery of insecticidal proteins. A possible reason for the slightly lower insecticidal effect of recombinant E. aerogenes at the lowest bacterial concentration compared with recombinant E. coli K12 may be differences in host bacterial biology rather than a single mechanism. Although Cry1Ac production was confirmed in both hosts by Western blot analysis (Fig. S2), this study did not quantify relative protein expression or bacterial survival in the larval gut. Therefore, differences in toxin production levels, bacterial persistence after ingestion, or other host-specific physiological traits remain plausible explanations that require further investigation.

Differences in host compatibility and expression systems limit the direct transferability of high-efficiency E. coli-based constructs to alternative bacterial hosts such as E. aerogenes. Consequently, engineering E. aerogenes for field-scale applications poses distinct technical challenges. In contrast, genetically modified crops such as Bt cotton, which expresses Cry1Ac toxins, have shown high efficacy against H. armigera (Abbas 2018). However, the adaptation of genetically modified crops remains limited in several developing countries, including India, due to regulatory restrictions and sociopolitical resistance. Against this backdrop, the present study proposes a promising alternative. Taken together, these findings suggest that a gut symbiont co-evolved with its host can serve as an efficient vehicle for delivering Cry1Ac to insect larvae. However, further testing under environmentally relevant conditions is needed before this approach can be considered as an alternative biological control strategy where planting transgenic crops is limited. Importantly, the negative control strains harboring construct 3, which encoded GFP and the kanamycin resistance marker (KmR) but lacked the cry1Ac gene, resulted in 0% larval mortality across all bioassays. Similarly, no larval mortality was observed with the wild-type E. aerogenes in the leaf-dip assays. These findings indicate that plasmid carriage and expression of the marker genes alone did not alter the bacterial physiology in a manner that could induce larval mortality (Kobilov et al. 2025). Therefore, the insecticidal activity observed in the recombinant strains can be attributed specifically to the presence and expression of the cry1Ac gene rather than to nonspecific effects associated with plasmid maintenance or marker gene expression (Kobilov et al. 2025).

Another potential challenge for the long-term application of this system is ecological stability, particularly the persistence of the engineered symbiont following its release into complex natural environments.

The long-term effectiveness of this approach will depend on the ecological stability of the engineered symbiont following its introduction into complex natural environments, naturally occurring microbial communities may impose selective pressures that affect its abundance, persistence, or displacement through competition for nutrients and attachment sites. Similarly, persistence on leaf surfaces may be influenced by environmental factors such as low humidity, temperature extremes, precipitation, and interactions with phyllosphere microorganisms, all of which may affect bacterial survival and transmission. In addition, the metabolic burden associated with plasmid maintenance and the constitutive expression of Cry1Ac may impose fitness costs on the engineered bacterium. From an evolutionary biology perspective, repeated and continuous exposure of Helicoverpa armigera to symbiont-mediated Cry1Ac could impose sustained selection pressure on susceptible populations, potentially facilitating the emergence of resistance. In lepidopteran pests, this type of resistance has generally been associated with alterations in midgut Cry toxin receptors, including cadherin-like proteins, aminopeptidase-N, alkaline phosphatase, and ABC transporters, which can reduce toxin binding and thereby disrupt membrane integrity. Considering the potentially long-lasting nature of bacterial delivery systems in the insect gut, this mode of exposure may differ from conventional Bacillus thuringiensis treatments, where exposure is typically short-lived. Therefore, this approach should be integrated with insect resistance management (IRM) strategies, including refuge deployment, rotation or combination with other control methods, and comprehensive integrated pest management (IPM) programs, to reduce the risk of resistance development. Understanding the consequences of these ecological and evolutionary factors will be important for the successful development and deployment of symbiont-based pest management strategies.

In addition, ecological interactions beyond those involving the target host should be carefully considered. Engineered symbionts released into agricultural environments may encounter diverse microbial communities associated with plants, soil, and non-target insects. Such interactions could influence the ability of the engineered bacteria to colonize, persist, and maintain the stability of their genetic traits. Consequently, the effectiveness of the paratransgenic system under field conditions may differ from that observed in laboratory studies (Arora et al. 2015). Therefore, evaluating these interactions under semi-field and field conditions would be an important step toward achieving a comprehensive understanding of the ecological implications of this strategy.

Regulatory considerations will be critical in the future development of paratransgenic systems that employ genetically modified symbionts. The recombinant strain constructed in this study carries a cry1Ac expression cassette and a kanamycin resistance marker; therefore, future risk assessments should evaluate the potential for horizontal transfer of these recombinant genetic elements and their ecological effects on environmental microbial communities. In addition, investigations into the dissemination of the modified symbiont beyond the target host and the exposure of non-target organisms will be essential components of a comprehensive biosafety assessment prior to field application. Together, these evaluations will provide the evidence needed to determine whether genetically modified symbionts can be released under existing regulatory frameworks governing genetically modified microorganisms.

The genetically engineered E. aerogenes strain developed in this study carries both the cry1Ac gene and a kanamycin resistance marker; therefore, robust biological containment measures are essential before any environmental application. As noted in the manuscript, such measures aim to prevent the survival and dissemination of the modified bacteria outside the target host or environment, thereby reducing the risk of horizontal gene transfer while preserving functionality within the host. Accordingly, this work should be regarded as a laboratory proof of concept, and comprehensive biosafety and environmental impact assessments are required before any field release is considered.

In conclusion, E. aerogenes, a symbiont isolated from the gut of H. armigera, was successfully engineered to express the cry1Ac gene and demonstrated stable colonization of both the larval gut and the phyllosphere of tomato plants. Ingestion of the recombinant strain resulted in significant larval mortality, supporting its potential use in paratransgenic strategies for the control of lepidopteran pests. This study highlights how gut-associated bacteria can be used as vehicles for delivering therapeutic proteins. It also demonstrates that gut microbes can be engineered to function as pest control agents. However, these findings were obtained from experiments conducted under relatively controlled laboratory conditions and should therefore be interpreted within that context. To better understand the potential and safety of these engineered strains in real-world environmental settings, further studies are needed. Such research would provide valuable insights into several key aspects. These issues must be thoroughly addressed before determining whether paratransgenic approaches to agricultural pest management can serve as sustainable, safe, and regulatory-compliant solutions.
