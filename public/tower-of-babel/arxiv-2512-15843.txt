Efficient Simulation of Sparse, Non-Local Fermion Models
Reinis Irmejs, J. Ignacio Cirac

Abstract
Efficient simulation of interacting fermionic systems is a key application of near-term quantum computers, but is hindered by the overhead required to encode fermionic operators on qubit hardware. Here, we consider models with N N fermionic modes in which each participates in at most a constant number d d of interactions and study the circuit depth required to implement the Trotterized time evolution on qubit hardware with all-to-all connectivity. We introduce an encoding that augments each physical fermionic mode with a small number of auxiliary fermions, enabling the removal of Jordan–Wigner strings. Although the preparation of the auxiliary fermion state incurs an initial overhead, this state remains invariant under time evolution. As a result, long-time evolution can be implemented with asymptotically optimal circuit depth, reducing a previously multiplicative O ⁡ ( log ⁡ N ) O(\log N) overhead to an additive overhead. Our results thus establish that the simulation of sparse fermionic models on qubit hardware matches the performance achievable on ideal fermionic hardware up to constant factors and O ⁡ ( d ​ N ) O(dN) ancillary qubits.



I
Introduction

Accurate and large scale simulation of interacting fermionic models is widely regarded as one of the earliest practically useful applications of near term quantum computers. Progress in this direction would not only deepen our understanding of fundamental physics but also support applied goals such as the design and characterization of novel materials
12
,
37
,
16
. The two dimensional Fermi Hubbard model exemplifies this opportunity, as it is believed to capture essential features of high temperature superconductivity
8
,
46
. Despite its apparent simplicity, the model remains poorly understood in the low temperature regime, where experimental access is limited
38
,
7
,
56
. Recent advances in quantum computing platforms
3
,
47
and in digital fermion simulation
4
now point toward overcoming these limitations.

A central computational task in the simulation of fermionic models is the implementation of time evolution under a given Hamiltonian
22
,
15
. Access to time evolution enables the estimation of correlation functions
10
,
32
,
31
,
18
, the evaluation of finite energy properties through filtering techniques
34
, the approximation of ground state properties via adiabatic evolution
39
,
26
,
5
, and serves as a key component in variational algorithms
44
,
52
. Simulation of time dynamics is believed to be difficult on classical computers
29
, emphasizing the need for efficient quantum computing algorithms.

The simulation of fermionic quantum many body systems on standard quantum computers requires encoding fermionic operators into qubit degrees of freedom. Implementing such encodings is challenging because fermionic operators satisfy canonical anticommutation relations that are not naturally captured by qubit operator algebras. To avoid this problem altogether, fermionic quantum computers have been proposed, in which fermionic statistics are implemented directly at the hardware level
11
. While this approach eliminates the need for fermion to qubit encodings, it introduces substantial challenges for the implementation of quantum error correction, which remains significantly less developed than in qubit based architectures
41
,
49
.

Motivated by these challenges, considerable effort has been devoted to optimizing fermionic encodings on qubit hardware. The simplest and most widely used mapping is the Jordan–Wigner transformation
28
, in which fermionic modes are ordered along a one dimensional chain. Fermionic anti commutation relations are enforced through non local string operators acting on all preceding sites, commonly referred to as Jordan–Wigner strings. As a consequence, fermionic interactions between sites
i
and
j
acquire a Pauli weight proportional to
|i-j|
. This leads to severe overheads for non local interactions, and already in two dimensional lattice models it is impossible to represent both horizontal and vertical nearest neighbor couplings with constant Pauli weight
23
. More generally, models with non local fermionic terms result in encodings whose Pauli weight can scale extensively with system size.

The quality of more advanced fermionic encodings is naturally quantified by the circuit depth required to implement a single Trotter step of the time evolution. Achieving optimal depth requires both low Pauli weight of the encoded Hamiltonian terms and the ability to parallelize their implementation. For this reason, the Trotter step depth has been widely adopted as a benchmark for fermionic encodings
9
,
30
,
16
,
36
,
17
. To assess the optimality of a given encoding, this depth, together with the number of ancillary qubits, must be compared to the resources required to perform the same simulation directly on fermionic hardware with identical connectivity.

Whether a fermionic encoding can achieve constant overhead in circuit depth depends crucially on two factors: (i) the locality and sparsity of the Hamiltonian interactions and (ii) the geometry of the underlying quantum hardware. For local fermionic models in two or higher dimensions, numerous local to local encodings have been developed that achieve constant depth overhead on local qubit architectures, in some cases with additional error correcting structure
54
,
50
,
27
,
19
,
14
,
33
,
43
,
42
,
6
. For all to all interacting fermionic models, such as Fermi–Hubbard type Hamiltonians, fermionic SWAP networks allow for optimal linear depth simulation on local qubit hardware
30
. The remaining open scenario is the simulation of fermionic models with non local but sparse interactions on hardware with all to all connectivity. In this regime, each fermionic mode interacts with at most a constant number
d
of other modes, as realized, for instance, in sparse instances of the SYK model or, more generally, in fermionic models defined on
d
-regular graphs.

51
,
21
,
24
,
25
,
53
. Furthermore, such sparse and random models are believed to have ground states that can be efficiently prepared using quantum phase estimation, further emphasizing the need for efficient time evolution algorithms
13
. On fermionic hardware with all to all connectivity, a single Trotter step for such models can be implemented in
\mathcal{O}(d)
depth. We consider the simulation of this regime on qubit hardware with identical connectivity
47
and show that the same scaling can be achieved, thereby establishing that fermionic quantum computation offers no asymptotic advantage beyond constant factors and ancillary qubits in this regime.

Recent work has made substantial progress toward this goal. Using general fermionic permutations, it was shown that a single Trotter step can be implemented with depth
\mathcal{O}(d\log N)
and an extensive number of ancillary qubits
36
,
17
. In this work, we build on these results and develop an approach that reduces the
O(\log N)
overhead from multiplicative to additive for long-time simulation.

Our method is inspired by
54
and introduces a constant number of auxiliary fermions per physical site, which are used to construct stabilizer operators that eliminate Jordan–Wigner strings. To ensure equivalence with the original fermionic model, the auxiliary fermions are initialized in the joint
+1
eigenspace of all stabilizers. While preparing this state constitutes the dominant cost of the approach, it remains invariant under the subsequent time evolution. Indeed, since the preparation of the auxiliary fermion state is required only once, all subsequent Trotter steps can be carried out with a constant overhead. As a result, if the total number of Trotter steps satisfies
M=\Omega(\log N)
, the total asymptotic cost of the simulation scales as
\mathcal{O}(M)
. In this regime, the total computational cost matches that of a fermionic quantum computer with the same connectivity.

In the next section, we introduce our approach and show how it maps fermionic operators to qubit operators with constant Pauli weight, together with the construction of the required auxiliary fermion initial state. In
Section

III
, we then summarize the full protocol and derive the total cost of the time evolution simulation. We conclude in
Section

IV
.

II.1
Sparse, non-local Hamiltonian

The objective of this work is to improve the overhead of performing long-time evolution of non-local fermion models. In this work, we consider Hamiltonians with sparse interactions and hoppings. To illustrate the idea, we consider a non-local hopping Hamiltonian:

H_{\mathrm{hop}}=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}t_{ij}\,(a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}+a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{i}).

(1)

The main aim of our work is to perform a transformation on each Hamiltonian term and map it to a qubit term that has a constant Pauli weight. We consider a transformation of form:

\displaystyle a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}+a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{i}\xrightarrow{}(a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}+a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{i})P_{ij},

Note that for local Hamiltonians on local hardware this corresponds to

II.3
Constructing the encoding

The key idea in constructing this transformation is to create a fermionic, hermitian operator
P^{(\ell)}_{ij}
from the auxiliary fermion modes possessing the same
JW string
. Subsequently, by transforming the initial Hamiltonian terms as in (
3
), we will be able to eliminate the extensive
JW strings
, resulting in a term that has a Pauli weight independent of the distance between the two interacting modes
\absolutevalue{i-j}
. However, such transformation does not preserve the physics in the full Hilbert space. To achieve that, we require that the auxiliary fermion modes are prepared in the
+1
eigenstate of
P^{(\ell)}_{ij}
. We will refer to
P^{(\ell)}_{ij}
as stabilizers. Thus, the main two challenges of this construction are:

1.

constructing the stabilizers
P^{(\ell)}_{ij}
, such that they can eliminate the
JW strings
for all present interaction terms in the initial Hamiltonian,

2.

efficiently preparing the initial state on the auxiliary modes
\ket{\Psi_{\aux}}
, such that:

P^{(\ell)}_{ij}\ket{\Psi_{\aux}}=+\ket{\Psi_{\aux}}

(10)

for every relevant stabilizer
P_{ij}^{(\ell)}
.

The condition
Eq.

10
asserts that the physics of the initial Hamiltonian remain unchanged, and the auxiliary state remains invariant:

e^{-i\tau h_{ij}P_{ij}^{(\ell)}}\ket{\Psi_{\aux}}\ket{\Psi_{\phys}}=\ket{\Psi_{\aux}}e^{-i\tau h_{ij}}\ket{\Psi_{\phys}}.

(11)

Constructing the stabilizers.

Firstly, to construct the stabilizers we use the Majorana operators from
Eqs.

7

8

P_{ij}^{(\ell)}=ic_{i}^{(\ell)}d_{j}^{(\ell)}=-iS_{i}Z_{i,<\ell}X_{i}^{(\ell)}S_{j}Z_{j,<\ell}Y_{j}^{(\ell)}

(12)

One can easily check that

is hermitian and
P_{ij}^{(\ell)2}=1

\displaystyle(a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}+a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{i})P_{ij}^{(\ell)}\xrightarrow{}\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{j}+\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{j})S_{i}S_{j}\times

\displaystyle\times(-iS_{i}Z_{i,<\ell}\mathrm{X}_{i}^{(\ell)}S_{j}Z_{j,<\ell}\mathrm{Y}_{j}^{(\ell)})

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2}(\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{j}+\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{j})Z_{i,<\ell}Z_{j,<\ell}\mathrm{X}_{i}^{(\ell)}\mathrm{Y}_{j}^{(\ell)}.

Appendix

Stabilizer initial state.

Secondly, to ensure that the Hamiltonian evolution remains unchanged under the transformation we need to ensure that

is a
+1

Depth per Trotter step.

To implement a single product
\prod_{\alpha=1}^{\chi}e^{-i\tau\tilde{h}_{\alpha}}
on qubit hardware, we first note that, within a given

, no two interaction terms overlap and can therefore be implemented in parallel. Moreover, each term in
\tilde{h}_{\alpha}
has Pauli weight

, and can thus be implemented in depth

using the Pauli gadget technique

\mathrm{Depth}(e^{-i\tau\tilde{h}_{\alpha}})=\mathcal{O}(\log(\chi)).

(25)

Since the Hamiltonian is decomposed into
\chi

\mathrm{Depth}_{\mathrm{step}}=\mathcal{O}(\chi\log(\chi))

\displaystyle\mathrm{Depth}(W^{M})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Depth}_{\mathrm{init}}+M\mathrm{Depth}_{\mathrm{step}}

(26)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\chi\log(\chi)+\chi\log( N)+M\chi\log(\chi)).

Trotter Error.

Performing
M

\mathcal{O}(\chi^{2}NM\tau^{2})=\mathcal{O}(\chi^{2}NT^{2}/M)
, where the total evolution time is
T=M\tau
. To ensure that the Trotter error remains small, it suffices to choose

steps for first-order Trotter evolution. By appropriately adjusting the value of
\tau
in

Asymptotic optimality.

On ideal fermionic hardware,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Depth}_{\mathrm{fermion}}(W^{M})=\mathcal{O}(M\chi),

non-overlapping terms. In the regime
M\gg\log N
, the ratio of the circuit depth on qubit hardware to that on fermionic hardware satisfies

\frac{\mathrm{Depth}(W^{M})}{\mathrm{Depth}_{\mathrm{fermion}}(W^{M})}=\mathcal{O}(\log\chi),

(28)

demonstrating that our encoding achieves the same asymptotic scaling for long-time dynamics as a fermionic quantum computer, up to a constant factor.

IV
Conclusions

In this work, we established asymptotically optimal circuit depth for the simulation of sparse non-local fermionic Hamiltonians on qubit hardware. By introducing auxiliary fermionic modes and defining stabilizers that eliminate Jordan-Wigner strings from all even interaction terms, we map the Hamiltonian to one with terms of constant Pauli weight. The costliest component of our approach is the initialization of the auxiliary fermions in the mutual
+1
eigenstate of all stabilizers, which can be achieved in
\mathcal{O}(\log(N))
depth. While a single Trotter step therefore requires
\mathcal{O}(\log(N))
depth, matching previous approaches, the auxiliary state remains invariant under time evolution, allowing each subsequent step to be implemented with depth independent of the system size. These results provide a practical route to efficient quantum simulation of sparse fermionic models and close the open gap by showing that fermionic quantum computation offers no asymptotic advantage in circuit depth, but only in requiring a constant number of ancillas per site.

Appendix A
Detailed JW transformations

In this appendix we show how to use the stabilizers
P^{(\ell)}_{ij}
to cancel the long JW strings of non-local hopping and four-fermion interaction terms. The guiding idea is that we will work in the simultaneous
+1
eigenspace of all relevant
P^{(\ell)}_{ij}
, so that multiplying Hamiltonian terms by these operators leaves all physical matrix elements unchanged while improving locality in the JW representation.

A.1
Hopping term

We begin with the hopping term between two arbitrary sites
i<j
. Using (
4
)

\displaystyle a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}+a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{n=i}^{j-1}\prod_{k=0}^{\nu}\mathrm{Z}_{n}^{(k)}\right)\frac{2\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{j}+2\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{j}}{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{j}+\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{j}\right)\left(\prod_{n=i}^{j-1}\prod_{k=0}^{\nu}\mathrm{Z}_{n}^{(k)}\right).

(29)

This is the standard JW form of the hopping term: a two-qubit operator on the physical modes at sites
i
and
j
multiplied by a string of
\mathrm{Z}
operators between
i
and
j
.

We now consider the transformed operator

\displaystyle(a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}+a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{i})P^{(\ell)}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2}\left(\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{j}+\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{j}\right)\left(\prod_{n=i}^{j-1}\prod_{k=0}^{\nu}\mathrm{Z}_{n}^{(k)}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\prod_{n^{\prime}=i}^{j-1}\prod_{k^{\prime}=0}^{\nu}\mathrm{Z}_{n^{\prime}}^{(k^{\prime})}\right)\left(\prod_{m=0}^{\ell-1}\mathrm{Z}_{i}^{(m)}\mathrm{Z}_{j}^{(m)}\right)\mathrm{X}_{i}^{(\ell)}\mathrm{Y}_{j}^{(\ell)}.

(30)

Since all
\mathrm{Z}
operators commute and
(\mathrm{Z})^{2}=1
, the product of the two extensive JW strings collapses to the identity:

\displaystyle(a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}+a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{i})P^{(\ell)}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2}\left(\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{j}+\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{j}\right)\left(\prod_{m=0}^{\ell-1}\mathrm{Z}_{i}^{(m)}\mathrm{Z}_{j}^{(m)}\right)\mathrm{X}_{i}^{(\ell)}\mathrm{Y}_{j}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}\left(\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{j}+\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{j}\right)\left(\prod_{m=1}^{\ell-1}\mathrm{Z}_{i}^{(m)}\mathrm{Z}_{j}^{(m)}\right)\mathrm{X}_{i}^{(\ell)}\mathrm{Y}_{j}^{(\ell)}.

(31)

Expression (
31
) is fully local: it acts only on the physical qubits at sites
i
and
j
and on the auxiliary qubits with indices
1,\dots,l
at those sites. The Pauli weight of this operator is

\displaystyle w_{\mathrm{hop}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{2}_{\text{physical }(0)\text{ at }i,j}+\underbrace{2(\ell-1)}_{\mathrm{Z}_{i}^{(m)},\mathrm{Z}_{j}^{(m)},\,1\leq m\leq\ell-1}+\underbrace{2}_{\mathrm{X}_{i}^{(\ell)},\mathrm{Y}_{j}^{(\ell)}}

\displaystyle=2(\ell+1).

(32)

Thus the transformation cancels the JW strings of length
\mathcal{O}(N)
, leaving a local term of bounded Pauli weight
w_{\mathrm{hop}}=2(\ell+1)
.

A.2
Interaction terms

Here we consider Fermi–Hubbard (FH) type density–density interaction
n_{i}n_{j}
with
n_{i}=a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}
. For this term no transformation is necessary because
n_{i}
is already local in the JW representation.

n_{i}=a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}=\frac{1}{2}(1+\mathrm{Z}^{(0)}_{i}).

(33)

The FH density–density term
n_{i}n_{j}
is therefore a product of single-site operators in the JW representation and is already local.

To tackle more general interaction terms, in particular chemistry-style four-fermion terms, we use the identity for
a_{i}^{\dagger}P^{(\ell)}_{ij}a_{j}
, which we now derive.

A.3
Identity for
a_{i}^{\dagger}P^{(\ell)}_{ij}a_{j}

We consider the term
a_{i}^{\dagger}P^{(\ell)}_{ij}a_{j}

\displaystyle a_{i}^{\dagger}P^{(\ell)}_{ij}a_{j}

\displaystyle=\left(S_{i}\,\frac{\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{i}+i\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{i}}{2}\right)\left(-i\,S_{i}Z_{i,<\ell}\mathrm{X}^{(\ell)}_{i}S_{j}Z_{j,<\ell}\mathrm{Y}^{(\ell)}_{j}\right)\times

(34)

\displaystyle\times\left(S_{j}\,\frac{\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{j}-i\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{j}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{4}S_{i}S_{i}S_{j}S_{j}(\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{i}+i\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{i})Z_{i,<\ell}\mathrm{X}^{(\ell)}_{i}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times Z_{j,<\ell}\mathrm{Y}^{(\ell)}_{j}(\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{j}-i\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{j})

(35)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{4}(Z_{i,<\ell}Z_{j,<\ell})\mathrm{X}^{(\ell)}_{i}\mathrm{Y}^{(\ell)}_{j}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left[\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{j}+\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{j}-i(\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{j}-\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{j})\right].

(36)

It will be the building block for transforming four-fermion (and higher) interaction terms. Note that the Pauli weight of (
36

w_{a^{\dagger}Pa}=2(\ell+1).

(37)

A.4
Transformation of four-fermion terms

We now consider a generic chemistry-style four-fermion interaction term of the form

h_{ijkl}=a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{k}a_{l}+a_{l}^{\dagger}a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{j}a_{i}.

(38)

We assume that the indices

We transform

\displaystyle h_{ijkl}

\displaystyle\longmapsto h_{ijkl}\,P^{(\ell)}_{ik}P^{(\ell^{\prime})}_{jl}

\displaystyle=\bigl(a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{k}a_{l}+a_{l}^{\dagger}a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{j}a_{i}\bigr)P^{(\ell)}_{ik}P^{(\ell^{\prime})}_{jl}.

(39)

Reordering the fermionic operators and using the definition of

\displaystyle h_{ijkl}P^{(\ell)}_{ik}P^{(\ell^{\prime})}_{jl}

(40)

From (
36

37

\ell

\ell^{\prime}

w_{4\text{-body}}=2(\ell+1)+2(\ell^{\prime}+1)=4+2\ell+2\ell^{\prime}

(41)

Regarding the choice of

\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}

Here we show how this four-fermion interaction term is transformed to a qubit term. We define

\displaystyle=a_{i}^{\dagger}P^{(\ell)}_{ik}a_{k},

\displaystyle B_{jl}^{(\ell^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=a_{j}^{\dagger}P^{(\ell^{\prime})}_{jl}a_{l},

Using (

\displaystyle A_{ik}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}(Z_{i,<\ell}Z_{k,<\ell})\mathrm{X}^{(\ell)}_{i}\mathrm{Y}^{(\ell)}_{k}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{k}+\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{k}-i(\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{k}-\mathrm{Y}^{(0)}_{i}\mathrm{X}^{(0)}_{k})\right],

\displaystyle h_{ijkl}P^{(\ell)}_{ik}P^{(\ell^{\prime})}_{jl}=-\frac{1}{8}(Z_{i,<\ell}Z_{k,<\ell})(Z_{j,<\ell^{\prime}}Z_{l,<\ell^{\prime}})

All operators in (

up to level

Appendix B
Stabilizer Properties

Here we summarize the relevant identities for the stabilizers
P_{ij}^{(\ell)}
. We saw in
Eq.

12
that the stabilizers are defined as:

P_{ij}^{(\ell)}=ic_{i}^{(\ell)}d_{j}^{(\ell)}.

Firstly, we show that the stabilizers square to an identity:

P_{ij}^{(\ell)2}=ic_{i}^{(\ell)}d_{j}^{(\ell)}ic_{i}^{(\ell)}d_{j}^{(\ell)}=c_{i}^{(\ell)}c_{i}^{(\ell)}d_{j}^{(\ell)}d_{j}^{(\ell)}=\mathbb{1},

(56)

where we have used the Majorana algebra relations
Eq.

9
. Since it squares to an identity, this implies that the eigenvalues of the stabilizer are
\pm 1
.
The respective eigenstates are:

P_{ij}^{(\ell)}\frac{c_{i}^{(\ell)}\mp id_{j}^{(\ell)}}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{0_{\aux}}=\pm 1\frac{c_{i}^{(\ell)}\mp id_{j}^{(\ell)}}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{0_{\aux}}.

(57)

Secondly, we verify that:

P_{ij}^{(\ell)\dagger}=(ic_{i}^{(\ell)}d_{j}^{(\ell)})^{\dagger}=-id_{j}^{(\ell)}c_{i}^{(\ell)}=ic_{i}^{(\ell)}d_{j}^{(\ell)}=P_{ij}^{(\ell)},

(58)

the stabilizers are indeed hermitian. Once again we have used the
Eq.

9
.

Third, we want to formally establish when different stabilizers commute:

\displaystyle\commutator{P_{ij}^{(\ell)}}{P_{kl}^{(\ell')}}=\commutator{c_k^{(\ell')}d_l^{(\ell')}}{c_i^{(\ell)}d_j^{(\ell)}}

\displaystyle=c_{i}^{(\ell)}c_{k}^{(\ell^{\prime})}d_{j}^{(\ell)}d_{l}^{(\ell^{\prime})}-c_{k}^{(\ell^{\prime})}c_{i}^{(\ell^{\prime})}d_{l}^{(\ell^{\prime})}d_{j}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=c_{i}^{(\ell)}c_{k}^{(\ell^{\prime})}d_{j}^{(\ell)}d_{l}^{(\ell^{\prime})}-(2\delta_{ik}\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}-c_{i}^{(\ell)}c_{k}^{(\ell^{\prime})})(2\delta_{jl}\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}-d_{j}^{(\ell)}d_{l}^{(\ell^{\prime})})

\displaystyle=2\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(c_{i}^{(\ell)}c_{k}^{(\ell)}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{ik}d_{j}^{(\ell)}d_{l}^{(\ell)}-2\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}),

(59)

where going to the third line we have used
Eq.

9
.
The
Appendix

B
shows that if
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}
, the stabilizers always commute, as well as when both
i\neq k,j\neq l
on the same auxiliary register.

Appendix C
Grouping of the stabilizers using graph coloring

In this section, we focus on the task of generating a set of commuting stabilizers
P^{(\ell)}_{ij}
, given an arbitrary Hamiltonian.

C.1
Constructing the interaction graph

Firstly, it is trivial to generate a hopping Hamiltonian. Given a Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{\mathrm{hop}}}
on
N
modes we construct a graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
with
\absolutevalue{V}=N
. The graph
\mathcal{G}
encodes the locality pattern: we assume that
(i,j)\in E
exactly when the hopping term between sites
i
and
j
is present (
t_{ij}\neq 0
).

However, one might also be interested in higher order interaction terms like the four-fermion Hamiltonian:

H_{4}=\sum_{ijkl}U_{ijkl}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{k}a_{l}.

(60)

Since this is a four-body interaction, there is no notion of an unique interaction edge. However, as we saw from
Sections

A.4
and
A.5
any even fermionic term on
2n
modes can be simplified using
n
stabilizers. In the case of these higher order interactions there is freedom on choosing the stabilizers we wish to use. For example, when simplifying the four-fermion term:

a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{k}a_{l},

one can use stabilizers between edges in various combinations. In particular, there are
\binom{4}{2}=6
different ways to choose between which vertices to insert the stabilizers. Just to illustrate two of them, both multiplication with
P_{ik}P_{jl}
or
P_{il}P_{kj}
lead to a local qubit term.

Thus, to incorporate terms like
H_{4}
in the interaction graph
\mathcal{G}
we:

1.

pick one stabilizer configuration that simplifies the higher order terms

2.

for each stabilizer
P_{ij}^{(\ell)}
that we require, we add an edge
(i,j)
to
E
, if it is not already present.

This strategy works for creating an appropriate (albeit not unique) interaction graph. We do not concern ourselves with finding the best possible solution here. In the next section, we assume that we are given an instance of such an interaction graph and show how to group the stabilizers across the minimal number of auxiliary modes, ensuring that they all mutually commute.

C.2
Edge coloring and auxiliary layers

To assign commuting stabilizers, we use a proper
edge coloring
of the graph
\mathcal{G}
. An edge coloring is a map

\mathrm{col}:E\to\{1,\dots,\chi\}

(61)

such that no two edges incident on the same vertex share the same color. The minimum number
\chi
of colors for which such a coloring exists is the
chromatic index
(or edge chromatic number) of
\mathcal{G}
.

For each color
\gamma\in\{1,\dots,\chi\}
we denote by
E_{\gamma}\subseteq E
the set of edges of color
\gamma
,

E_{\gamma}=\{(i,j)\in E\mid\mathrm{col}(i,j)=\gamma\}.

(62)

Because
\mathrm{col}
is a proper edge coloring, each
E_{\gamma}
is a
matching
: no two edges in
E_{\gamma}
share a common endpoint.

We first imagine that we have at least one auxiliary mode per color, that is,
\nu\geq\chi
, and that we temporarily assign a distinct auxiliary index
\ell=\gamma
to each color class
E_{\gamma}
. For each edge
(i,j)\in E_{\gamma}
we choose an arbitrary orientation and define a stabilizer

P_{ij}^{(\gamma)}=i\,c_{i}^{(\gamma)}d_{j}^{(\gamma)}.

(63)

The following lemma makes the commutation properties of each color class precise.

Lemma 1
.

Fix a color
\gamma
and an auxiliary index
\ell
. Let

\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}^{(\ell)}=\{P^{(\ell)}_{ij}\}_{(i,j)\in E_{\gamma}}

(64)

be the set of operators defined as above from the edges of
E_{\gamma}
and an arbitrary orientation. Then the operators in
\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}^{(\ell)}
mutually commute.

Proof.

Note that
E_{\gamma}
is a matching, so any two distinct edges
(i,j)
and
(k,l)
in
E_{\gamma}
are vertex-disjoint:

\{i,j\}\cap\{k,l\}=\emptyset.

(65)

Therefore, from
Appendix

B
the respective stabilizers commute. Since this holds for any pair of distinct operators in
\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}^{(\ell)}
, all of them mutually commute.
∎

Next we show that we can
reuse
ancillas across colors: stabilizers from any two colors can be encoded in the same auxiliary index
\ell
while retaining commutativity. This allows us to reduce the number of auxiliary modes per site by almost a factor of two.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
be two colors with edge sets
E_{\gamma}
and
E_{\gamma^{\prime}}
. Consider the graph

\mathcal{G}_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}=(V,E_{\gamma}\cup E_{\gamma^{\prime}}).

(66)

Then there exists an orientation of all edges in
E_{\gamma}\cup E_{\gamma^{\prime}}
such that, if we fix a single auxiliary index
\ell
and define

P^{(\ell)}_{ij}=ic_{i}^{(\ell)}d_{j}^{(\ell)}

(67)

for every oriented edge
(i,j)\in E_{\gamma}\cup E_{\gamma^{\prime}}
, all these stabilizers commute mutually. In particular, the two color classes
E_{\gamma}
and
E_{\gamma^{\prime}}
can be represented using the same layer
\ell
of auxiliary fermions.

Proof.

Because
E_{\gamma}
and
E_{\gamma^{\prime}}
are both matchings, the degree of any vertex in
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}
is at most two:

\deg_{\mathcal{G}_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}}(v)\leq 2\quad\text{for all }v\in V.

(68)

Therefore,
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}
is a disjoint union of paths and cycles. On each connected component (path or cycle) we choose an orientation that is
consistent along the component
: for a path
(v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{r})
we orient all edges as

v_{1}\to v_{2},\quad v_{2}\to v_{3},\quad\dots,\quad v_{r-1}\to v_{r},

(69)

and for a cycle
(v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{r},v_{1})
we orient all edges, for example, as

v_{1}\to v_{2},\quad v_{2}\to v_{3},\quad\dots,\quad v_{r-1}\to v_{r},\quad v_{r}\to v_{1}.

(70)

With this choice, at every vertex
v
there is at most one outgoing and at most one incoming edge in
E_{\gamma}\cup E_{\gamma^{\prime}}
. For each, now oriented, edge
(i,j)
in
E_{\gamma}\cup E_{\gamma^{\prime}}
, we now define the stabilizer
P^{(\ell)}_{ij}=ic_{i}^{(\ell)}d_{j}^{(\ell)}
. Due to the orientation of the operators all
P^{(\ell)}_{ij}
are products of distinct Majorana terms, hence the elements in the set

\mathcal{P}_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}^{(\ell)}=\{P^{(\ell)}_{ij}\}_{(i,j)\in E_{\gamma}\cup E_{\gamma^{\prime}}}

(71)

commute pairwise (see
Appendix

B
).
∎

As an immediate corollary, we can pack at most two colors into each auxiliary layer without destroying commutativity. If
\chi
is the chromatic index of
\mathcal{G}
, we can therefore choose

\nu=\left\lceil\frac{\chi}{2}\right\rceil

(72)

auxiliary modes per site, and assign colors to layers as

\text{layer }\ell\text{ carries colors }2\ell-1\text{ and }2\ell,

(73)

with the understanding that if
2\ell>\chi
then color
2\ell
is absent. For each layer
\ell
we orient all edges of colors
2\ell-1
and
2\ell
as in Lemma
2
, and define stabilizers
P_{ij}^{(\ell)}
for all such oriented edges.

For regular graphs, it is possible to bound their chromatic index

Appendix D
Ordered state circuit construction

In

we saw that the initial state can be constructed by application of

19
). We discuss the implementation of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{ord}}^{(\ell)}
here.

D.1
Mapping to a unitary

First, note that the operation
\frac{c^{(\ell)}_{i}-id^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}
is not unitary. To embed it into a unitary we introduce an additional auxiliary qubit:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{(\ell)}_{ij}=\outerproduct{0}{0}_{\mathrm{anc}}\otimes c^{(\ell)}_{i}+\outerproduct{1}{1}_{\mathrm{anc}}\otimes(-id^{(\ell)}_{j}).

(75)

One can verify that

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{(\ell)\dagger}_{ij}\mathcal{U}^{(\ell)}_{ij}=\mathcal{U}^{(\ell)}_{ij}\mathcal{U}^{(\ell)\dagger}_{ij}=

\displaystyle=\outerproduct{0}{0}_{\mathrm{anc}}\otimes c^{(\ell)2}_{i}+\outerproduct{1}{1}_{\mathrm{anc}}\otimes d^{(\ell)2}_{j}

\displaystyle=\outerproduct{0}{0}_{\mathrm{anc}}\otimes\mathbb{1}+\outerproduct{1}{1}_{\mathrm{anc}}\otimes\mathbb{1}=\mathbb{1},

\mathcal{U}^{(\ell)}_{ij}
is indeed unitary. To prepare the desired eigenstate of
P_{ij}^{(\ell)}
we perform:

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{anc}}\mathcal{U}^{(\ell)}_{ij}\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{anc}}\ket{0_{\anc}}\ket{\Psi_{\aux}}=

(76)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{anc}}\left(\frac{\ket{0_{\anc}}}{\sqrt{2}}c_{i}^{(\ell)}-\frac{\ket{1_{\anc}}}{\sqrt{2}}id_{j}\right)\ket{\Psi_{\aux}}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\ket{0_{\anc}}}{2}(c_{i}^{(\ell)}-id_{j}^{(\ell)})+\frac{\ket{1_{\anc}}}{2}(c_{i}^{(\ell)}+id_{j}^{(\ell)})\right]\ket{\Psi_{\aux}}.

Following this circuit, we measure the ancillary register:

•

\ket{0_{\anc}}
indicates that we have a
+1
eigenstate of
P_{ij}^{(\ell)}

•

\ket{1_{\anc}}
indicates that we have a
-1
eigenstate of
P_{ij}^{(\ell)}

which can be easily verified from
Eq.

57
. One option to ensure that we have the correct eigenstate would be to post-select the outcome on
\ket{0_{\anc}}
, however we argue that it isn’t even necessary. One can simply notice that:

(-P_{ij}^{(\ell)})\frac{c^{(\ell)}_{i}+id^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{0_{\aux}}=\frac{c^{(\ell)}_{i}+id^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{0_{\aux}}.

(77)

Hence, the outcome
\ket{1_{\anc}}
also indicates that we have prepared the
+1
eigenstate of the
-P_{ij}^{(\ell)}=-ic^{(\ell)}_{i}d^{(\ell)}_{j}
. Thus, in the case of the
\ket{1_{\anc}}
outcome we can simply redefine the respective stabilizer to be
P_{ij}^{(\ell)}\xrightarrow{}-ic^{(\ell)}_{i}d^{(\ell)}_{j}
. Note that this simple change of sign does not affect the commutation properties of the stabilizers, nor the weight of the transformed Hamiltonian terms. It will only affect the sign in front of the terms, whose transformation feature the respective stabilizer.

D.2
Circuit decomposition of the ordered state preparation

Having showed how we can initialize the respective state, let us show how we can prepare an efficient circuit in the ordered case. To try that, let us first try to simplify
\mathcal{C}^{(\ell)}_{\mathrm{ord}}
(
Eq.

19
) in the qubit picture:

\displaystyle 2^{\frac{N}{4}}\mathcal{C}^{(\ell)}_{\mathrm{ord}}=\prod_{k=1}^{N/2}(S_{2k-1}Z_{2k-1,<\ell}\mathrm{X}^{(\ell)}_{2k-1}+iS_{2k}Z_{2k,<\ell}\mathrm{Y}^{(\ell)}_{2k})

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{N/2}S_{2k-1}(Z_{2k-1,<\ell}\mathrm{X}^{(\ell)}_{2k-1}+iZ_{2k-1,\leq\nu}Z_{2k,<\ell}\mathrm{Y}^{(\ell)}_{2k})

(78)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{N/2}(Z_{2k-1,<\ell}\mathrm{X}^{(\ell)}_{2k-1}+iZ_{2k-1,\leq\nu}Z_{2k,<\ell}\mathrm{Y}^{(\ell)}_{2k})\prod_{k=1}^{N/2}S_{2k-1}.

Notice that in the first product, the terms are local and only supported on the sites
2k-1,2k
. To fully simplify it to a product of local contributions, we need to simplify the product
\prod_{k=1}^{N/2}S_{2k-1}
:

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{N/2}S_{2k-1}=\prod_{k=1}^{N/2}(Z_{2k-1,\leq\nu})^{N/2+1-k}(Z_{2k,\leq\nu})^{N/2-k},

(79)

where we have used the fact that any
\mathrm{Z}^{2}=1
. Plugged back into
Section

D.2
it yields:

\displaystyle 2^{\frac{N}{4}}\mathcal{C}^{(\ell)}_{\mathrm{ord}}=\prod_{k=1}^{N/2}\Bigg(Z_{2k-1,<\ell}\mathrm{X}^{(\ell)}_{2k-1}

\displaystyle+iZ_{2k-1,\leq\nu}Z_{2k,<\ell}\mathrm{Y}^{(\ell)}_{2k}\Bigg)\prod_{k=1}^{N/2}S_{2k-1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{N/2}\Bigg(\mathrm{X}^{(\ell)}_{2k-1}Z_{2k-1,<\ell}(Z_{2k-1,\leq\nu})^{N/2+1-k}

\displaystyle+i\mathrm{Y}^{(\ell)}_{2k}Z_{2k-1,\leq\nu}Z_{2k,<\ell}(Z_{2k,\leq\nu})^{N/2-k}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{N/2}\Bigg(\tilde{c}_{2k-1}^{(\ell)}-i\tilde{d}_{2k}^{(\ell)}\Bigg),

(80)

where at the end we have introduced simplified, transformed operators:

\displaystyle\tilde{c}^{(\ell)}_{2k-1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{X}^{(\ell)}_{2k-1}Z_{2k-1,<\ell}(Z_{2k-1,\leq\nu})^{N/2+1-k},

(81)

\displaystyle\tilde{d}^{(\ell)}_{2k}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{Y}^{(\ell)}_{2k}Z_{2k-1,\leq\nu}Z_{2k,<\ell}(Z_{2k,\leq\nu})^{N/2-k}.

(82)

Notice that in
Section

D.2
, the
\mathcal{C}^{(\ell)}_{\mathrm{ord}}
has the same form as originally in
Eq.

19
, just now with
\tilde{c}_{i}^{(\ell)},\tilde{d}_{j}^{(\ell)}
that have simplified, local
JW strings
. Furthermore, notice that the Pauli weights of these terms are:

\displaystyle w(\tilde{c}^{(\ell)}_{2k-1})

\displaystyle=\min(\ell,\nu-\ell)\leq\nu,

(83)

\displaystyle w(\tilde{d}^{(\ell)}_{2k})

\displaystyle=\min(\ell+\nu,2\nu-\ell)\leq 2\nu.

(84)

Thus, we can proceed to define the circuit
\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ord}}
that will implement
\mathcal{C}^{(\ell)}_{\mathrm{ord}}
as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ord}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{N/2}\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{k},

(85)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{k}=\outerproduct{0}{0}_{\mathrm{anc},k}\tilde{c}^{(\ell)}_{2k-1}+\outerproduct{1}{1}_{\mathrm{anc},k}(-i\tilde{d}^{(\ell)}_{2k}).

(86)

Note that to implement this, we need to introduce additional
N/2
ancillary qubits. Furthermore, the key of this transformation is that different
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{k}
act on disjoint qubits. Thus, we can implement all
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{k}
in parallel.

Finally, to establish the circuit depth required to prepare the ordered auxilliary state we need to establish the depth required to implement any
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{k}
. To do that, notice the structure of the terms in
Eq.

81
. It consists of a product of
\mathrm{Z}
operators, along with a
\mathrm{X}
(
i\mathrm{Y}
) Pauli operator. The circuit
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{k}
is simply their controlled version. Thus, the most expensive part of this circuit will be to implement the control-
\mathrm{Z}
(
\mathrm{CZ}
) operations, controlled by the ancillary register, acting on all the relevant
\mathrm{Z}
operators. Importantly, such circuits are called
\mathrm{CZ}

fanout
circuits. It has been shown, that a
\mathrm{CZ}
fanout on
n
qubits can be implemented in a
\log(n)
circuit depth
48
,
40
,
20
. The circuit for it would look like:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{X}_{\mathrm{anc}}\mathrm{CX}^{(\ell)}_{\mathrm{anc},2k-1}\prod_{t\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}^{c}}\left(\mathrm{CZ}_{\mathrm{anc},t}\right)\mathrm{X}_{\mathrm{anc}}\times

(87)

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{C}(i\mathrm{Y}^{(\ell)})_{\mathrm{anc},2k}\prod_{t\in\mathcal{Z}_{k}^{d}}\left(\mathrm{CZ}_{\mathrm{anc},t}\right),

(88)

where the sets
\mathcal{Z}_{k}^{c}
(
\mathcal{Z}_{k}^{d}
) contain the information of the respective target
\mathrm{Z}
operator indices from
Eq.

81
that are involved in the
fanout
. Note that for implementation
i\mathrm{Y}^{(\ell)}_{2k}=\mathrm{Z}^{(\ell)}_{2k}\mathrm{X}^{(\ell)}_{2k}
.
Thus, also
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{k}
can be implemented in
\mathcal{O}(\log(\nu))
depth. Note that one needs to take the same steps with regards to measuring the ancilla and adjusting the sign of the stabilizer as in
Section

D.1
. To sum it up, the operation
\mathcal{C}^{(\ell)}_{\mathrm{ord}}
can be embedded into a unitary operation and realized as a quantum circuit of depth
\mathcal{O}(\log(\nu))
along with
N/2
additional ancillas.

Appendix E
Trotter Implementation

E.1
Commutator bounds

Consider the commutator bounds on the transformed Hamiltonian:

\commutator{\tilde{h_{ij}}}{\tilde{h_{kl}}}=\commutator{h_{ij}P^{(\ell)}_{ij}}{h_{kl}P_{kl}^{(\ell')}}=\commutator{h_{ij}}{h_{kl}}P^{(\ell)}_{ij}P_{kl}^{(\ell^{\prime})},

(89)

since the projectors commute with themselves (our construction) and the physical operators. This also straightforwardly extends to higher order fermion interactions. Thus,

\displaystyle\norm{\comm{\tilde{h_{ij}}}{\tilde{h_{kl}}}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\norm{\comm{h_{ij}}{h_{kl}}P^{(\ell)}_{ij}P_{kl}^{(\ell')}}_{2}=\norm{\comm{h_{ij}}{h_{kl}}}_{2},

(90)

since
P_{ij}^{(\ell)\dagger}P_{ij}^{(\ell)}=\mathbb{1}
.

The total nested commutator for the first-order Trotter evolution is:

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{\chi}\sum_{\delta=\gamma+1}^{\chi}\norm{\comm{\tilde{h}_{\gamma}}{\tilde{h}_{\delta}}}_{2}=

(91)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{\chi}\sum_{\delta=\gamma+1}^{\chi}\norm{\comm{h_{\gamma}}{h_{\delta}}}_{2}=

(92)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{\chi}\sum_{\delta=\gamma+1}^{\chi}\mathcal{O}(N)=\mathcal{O}(\chi^{2}N),

(93)

where we assumed that between any two color sets, each term in the set overlaps with only at most two others and that the norm of all couplings have been normalized to
\mathcal{O}(1)
.

E.2
Implementing
n-
qubit evolution

Consider an evolution of a Hamiltonian term supported on
n-
qubits. Such an evolution can be implemented with a single qubit rotation, conjugated between a
n-
qubit
\mathrm{CNOT}
staircase (Pauli gadget method). Such a
\mathrm{CNOT}
ladder can be implemented with a
O(\log(n))
depth circuit without any ancillas
48
. Thus, any evolution of a
n-
qubit Pauli term can be done in a
O(\log(n))
depth.

Appendix F
Example models

F.1
Fermi–Hubbard model

Consider a Fermi–Hubbard model on a
d-
regular graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
, meaning that each vertex
v\in V
has exactly
d
edges:

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{FH}}

\displaystyle=H_{\mathrm{hop}}+H_{\mathrm{int}}=

(94)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}t_{ij}\,(a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}+a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{i})+\sum_{(i,j)\in E}V_{ij}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}.

(95)

On a
d-
regular graph
d\leq\chi\leq d+1
(see
Section

C.3
), thus we need at most
\nu=\lceil(d+1)/2\rceil
ancillary fermions per site. Even though the interaction part of the
Eq.

94
is a four-fermion term, it already has a local support as we saw in
Section

A.2
:

H_{\mathrm{int}}=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}V_{ij}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\frac{V_{ij}}{4}(\mathbb{1}+\mathrm{Z}_{i}^{(0)})(\mathbb{1}+\mathrm{Z}_{j}^{(0)}),

thus requiring no transformation. Therefore, the initial auxiliary state preparation proceeds identically to the one for
H_{\mathrm{hop}}
as discussed in the main text, since we do not require any stabilizers for the
H_{\mathrm{int}}
.

Finally, when performing the Trotterized time evolution:

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{FH}}=e^{-i\tau H_{\mathrm{FH}}}=

(96)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{\chi}\prod_{\alpha=1}^{\chi}\left(e^{-i\tau\tilde{h}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{hop}}}e^{-i\tau\tilde{h}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{int}}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\Lambda\tau^{2}),

(97)

where note that
[\tilde{h}^{\mathrm{hop}}_{\alpha},\tilde{h}^{\mathrm{int}}_{\alpha}]=0
and
\Lambda=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\chi}\sum_{\beta=\alpha+1}^{\chi}\norm{[\tilde{h}^{\mathrm{hop}}_\alpha+\tilde{h}^{\mathrm{hop}}_\alpha, \tilde{h}^{\mathrm{hop}}_\beta+\tilde{h}^{\mathrm{hop}}_\beta]}_{2}
, hence
\Lambda=\mathcal{O}(\chi^{2}N)
. Thus, the depth for implementing a single-color Trotter layer is still
O(\log(\chi))
, and the full cost for implementing
M
steps of the Trotterized time evolution is:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Depth}(W_{\mathrm{FH}}^{M})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Depth}_{\mathrm{init}}+M\mathrm{Depth}_{\mathrm{step}}

(98)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(d\log(d)+d\log( N)+Md\log(d)).

F.2
SYK model

We consider a quartic Sachdev–Ye–Kitaev (SYK) type Hamiltonian
35
acting on
N
Majorana fermions
\{\gamma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
,

H_{\mathrm{SYK}}=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}J_{e}\prod_{i\in e}\gamma_{i},

(99)

where
\mathcal{E}
denotes the edge set of a 4-uniform hypergraph, such that each interaction term involves exactly four fermionic operators. No assumption is made on the distribution of the couplings
J_{e}
, which may be arbitrary. We restrict to a model defined on a 4-uniform
d
-regular hypergraph, so that each Majorana fermion participates in exactly
d
quartic interaction terms. This defines a sparse SYK
4
-type model, in contrast to the fully connected SYK
4
Hamiltonian, where the number of interaction terms per Majorana fermion scales as
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
. In the following, each
\gamma_{i}
is taken to be either the
c^{(0)}_{i}
or
d^{(0)}_{i}
Majorana operator. Note that for
N
-Majorana fermion modes it is sufficient to choose
N/2
fermions.

The first challenge in simulating the model is to construct a stabilizer graph
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{stab}}
from the underlying 4-uniform hypergraph
\mathcal{G}
. A simple choice is to split each hyperedge
e=(i,j,k,l)\in\mathcal{E}
into two edges
(i,j)
and
(k,l)
in the stabilizer graph
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{stab}}
. Since each Majorana mode participates in exactly
d
hyperedges, this construction ensures that it also participates in exactly
d
edges in
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{stab}}
. Since there are two Majorana modes associated with each physical site, this graph has degree at most
2d
, thus we need
\nu=d
ancillas per fermion mode. In
Section

A.6
, we show that such a splitting allows the stabilizers
P_{ij}^{(\ell)}
to cancel the
JW strings
appearing in the terms of
H_{\mathrm{SYK}}
.
In
Section

C.3
, we show that the chromatic index of a regular graph is directly proportional to its degree, hence the initial state preparation of the auxilliary modes can be implemented with depth

\mathrm{Depth}_{\mathrm{init}}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(d\log(d)+d\log(N)\right).

(100)

Furthermore, the transformed interaction terms have Pauli weight scaling as
w_{\mathrm{SYK}}=\mathcal{O}(d)
(
Section

A.6
). To determine the full depth of a single Trotter step, we require the chromatic index of the hypergraph
\mathcal{G}
, since this bounds the number of Trotter layers into which the terms can be grouped. In
45
, it was shown that the chromatic index of a
d
-regular hypergraph also scales as
\mathcal{O}(d)
. Consequently, the depth of a full Trotter step is

\mathrm{Depth}_{\mathrm{step}}\!\left(W_{\mathrm{SYK}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(d\log(d)\right).

(101)
