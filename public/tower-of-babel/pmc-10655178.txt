MicroRNA-Mediated Host Immune Genes Manipulation Benefits AcMNPV Proliferation in Spodoptera frugiperda
Authors: Jie Zhang, Junaid Zafar, Jinrong Kong, Fei Wang, Xuehua Shao, Ruonan Zhang, Rui Pang, Hanhong Xu, Xiaoxia Xu, Fengliang Jin
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpodoptera frugiperda is a highly destructive migratory pest that threatens various crops globally. Autographa californica multiple nucleopolyhedrovirus (AcMNPV) is an effective biocontrol agent against lepidopteran pests. Here, we explored the molecular mechanisms underlying the immune response to AcMNPV infection in S. frugiperda. RNA-seq and quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analyses identified the Toll, IMD, and apoptosis pathways as primary immune responses. Investigation into AcMNPV-induced apoptosis in the S. frugiperda cell line (Sf9) revealed that the Toll pathway activated the JNK via the TRAF6 (TNF receptor-associated factor 6) adapter. In addition, AcMNPV-induced the differential expression of several host-encoded microRNAs (miRNAs), with significant negative regulatory effects, on S. frugiperda antiviral immune genes. RNAi and miRNA-mimic mediated silencing of these genes resulted in increased AcMNPV proliferation. Our findings reinforce the potential of AcMNPV as a potent biocontrol agent and further our understanding of developing biotechnology-based targeted pest control agents.Keywords: innate immunity, host−pathogen interaction, Spodoptera frugiperda, baculovirus, miRNAs

Introduction

Baculoviruses make up a group of highly virulent arthropod-specific DNA viruses. Found in a variety of environmental habitats, these viruses have been isolated from several insect orders, including Coleoptera, Lepidoptera, Hymenoptera, and Diptera.1Autographa californica multiple nucleopolyhedrovirus (AcMNPV) is an alpha baculovirus that primarily infects lepidopteran insects. The AcMNPV genome encodes ∼150 open reading frames (ORFs) and forms two morphologically distinct virions: budding virus (BV) and occlusion-derived virus (ODV).2 Once ingested, AcMNPV initiates infection in the larval midgut epithelium, disseminating to other tissues and ultimately killing the insects. The infected larvae liquefy, causing the occlusion bodies (OBs) to disperse and spread the virus horizontally.3 Viruses have evolved the ability to manipulate and hijack host cellular components to promote their replication and proliferation. They interact with host cells by affecting host immune responses, apoptosis, energy distribution, and the acquisition of beneficial genes from the host genome.4

To combat viral infection, insects mount various immune responses, including activation of the Toll, immune deficiency (IMD), Janus kinase/signal transduction and activators of transcription (JAK/STAT), Jun-N-terminal kinase (JNK), and RNA interference (RNAi) pathways.5 The Toll and IMD pathways are central to the immune response against Gram-positive bacteria, fungi, and Gram-negative bacterial infections.6 Studies have suggested their potential role in antiviral immunity in Drosophila.7 The JAK-STAT and JNK pathways are induced by viral infection. The JNK pathway is crucial in producing proteins that activate apoptosis, an insect’s primary antiviral defense mechanism.8,9 Studies have shown that orally ingested West Nile virus can induce apoptosis in the midgut cells of Culex pipiens mosquitoes.10 Similar observations were made in dengue virus (DENV-2)-infected female Aedes aegypti,11 stressing the importance of apoptosis. To prevent apoptosis, AcMNPV expresses a potent antiapoptotic gene, p35, which effectively suppresses the transcription of apoptosis-related genes, such as p53.12 The p53 protein is a vital regulator of cellular stress response and apoptosis.13 In Drosophila melanogaster, p53 protein-mediated apoptosis prevents the expression of viral genes and blocks the infection.14Bmp53, a p53 homologue in Bombyx mori, was shown to regulate apoptosis in response to Bombyx mori nucleopolyhedrovirus (BmNPV) infection.15,16

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are a class of endogenously encoded noncoding RNAs that regulate gene expression by complementary binding to the target mRNA (mRNA).17 Recent studies have shown that virus-derived and insect-encoded miRNAs play a critical role in host–virus interactions.18 Host-encoded miRNAs are known to regulate viral replication. Overexpression of bmo-miR-2819 suppresses BmNPV replication in silkworms by regulating the BmNPV ie-1 gene.19 Similarly, B. mori-encoded miR-274-3p targets nonstructural protein 5 (NS5) from Bombyx mori cytoplasmic polyhedrosis virus (BmCPV) and affects its replication.20 miR-2b controls the ubiquitin-related modifiers to regulate Chikungunya virus replication in Ae. aegypti.21 On the contrary, viruses may hijack and manipulate host miRNA machinery to boost their infection and proliferation.22 These findings highlight the importance of miRNAs in both host antiviral responses and viral replication. However, the underlying mechanism of these cross-talks remains relatively less explored in insects.

S. frugiperda, a major migratory agricultural pest, has caused extensive damage to various crops globally.23 Here, we conducted RNA-seq of AcMNPV-infected S. frugiperda larval midgut tissues to identify the insect host’s immune response to viral infection. Bioinformatic analysis identified Toll, IMD, and apoptosis pathways as primary immune responses. Investigation into AcMNPV-induced apoptosis in the S. frugiperda cell line (Sf9) revealed that the abundance of phosphorylated JNK and p38 increased as the infection progressed, indicating the involvement of the JNK pathway. Following AcMNPV infection, SfToll3 (Toll pathway) was highly expressed in the midgut; we theorized that the Toll pathway might be interacting with the JNK.

Furthermore, we also identified multiple differentially expressed S. frugiperda-encoded miRNAs that regulate crucial immune genes, including p53. RNAi and miRNA-mimic mediated silencing of p53 resulted in increased AcMNPV proliferation, suggesting that AcMNPV benefits from host miRNAs. Our findings provide insight into a multilayered and complex insect host–virus interaction, which will help us to understand and implement AcMNPV as an effective biocontrol agent for S. frugiperda management.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture and Insects

Sf9 cells were cultured in the SF-X insect cell culture medium (HyClone, Invitrogen, USA) supplemented with 5% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (HyClone, SV30087.02, Invitrogen, USA) at 27 °C.

S. frugiperda larvae were reared on an artificial diet consisting of malt powder, casein, sucrose, vitamins, cellulose, and agar. Adults were provided with a 10% w/v honey solution. The rearing conditions were as follows: temperature 26 ± 2 °C, relative humidity 70–80%, and a 12 h light/12 h dark photoperiod.

Virus and Virus Infection

The OBs of AcMNPV were kindly provided by Manli Wang (State Key Laboratory of Virology, Wuhan Institute of Virology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Wuhan, Hubei, China). The OBs of AcMNPV were produced by infecting the fourth instar S. frugiperda larvae, as described previously.24 Fifth instar day 1 (L5D1) S. frugiperda larvae were fed on a diet containing AcMNPV (107 OBs/larva), whereas larvae supplemented with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were used as controls. Midgut tissues were collected from both groups 6, 48, and 72 h postinfection (hpi). The samples were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and instantly transferred to −80 °C for subsequent experiments. Each group had three biological replicates.

Recombinant baculovirus AcMNPV/BmNPV-GFP, constructed by inserting the GFP gene in the locus of the polh promoter of AcMNPV/BmNPV, was used to infect Sf9 cells. The cells were plated on six-well plates and incubated until they covered 70–80% of the area. Subsequently, the cells were infected with AcMNPV/BmNPV-GFP (Multiplicity of infection (MOI) = 1.2) for 1.5 h and then medium was refreshed. The cells were further cultured for 6, 24, 48, and 60 h and collected to detect green fluorescence cell number, gene expression, and virus proliferation.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The samples for total RNA extraction were collected from the AcMNPV-infected and uninfected larval midgut and Sf9 cells. First-strand cDNAs were synthesized using a Color Reverse Transcription Kit (EZB, no. A0010CGQ) and a Mir-X miRNA First-Stand Synthesis Kit (Takara, no. 638313), following the manufacturer’s instructions. qRT-PCR reactions were performed using 200 ng of cDNA and TB Green Premix ExTaqII (Takara, no. RR520A) with three technical replicates on a BioRad CFX96 real-time PCR detection system. The primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Supporting Information Table S.1.

Construction of an Expression Vector and Sf9 Cell Transfection

The intracellular domains (TIR3) of the SfToll3 gene from S. frugiperda larvae were amplified by using cDNA prepared from the mRNA as a PCR template. The resulting PCR product was then cloned into a pIEx-4 vector with the IE promoter using EcoRI and XhoI restriction enzyme sites. To confirm the sequence identity, the recombinant construct was subjected to Sanger sequencing (Tsingke, Beijing, China). The primer sequences used are listed in Supporting Information Table S.1.

Sf9 cells were seeded overnight in SF-X medium with 5% FBS to ∼70% confluence in a six-well plate. All subsequent transfections, including pIEx-TIR3 plasmid, double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), and miRNA mimics, were performed using the Effectene Transfection Reagent (Qiagen).

RNA Interference (RNAi) in Sf9 Cells

For RNAi experiments in Sf9 cells, DNA templates used for dsRNA synthesis were amplified by PCR using a pair of primers listed in Supporting Information Table S.1. A T7 RNA polymerase-binding site (5′-GGATCCTAATACGACTCACTATAGG-3′) was attached to the 5′-end of each primer. The dsRNA was synthesized using the T7 RiboMAX Express RNAi kit (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Red fluorescent protein (RFP) dsRNA was used as a control. Sf9 cells were then transfected with dsRNA (10 μg/well), as described previously. After transfection for 72 h, the cells were collected for qRT-PCR and immunoblotting analyses.

MicroRNA Mimic Assay in Sf9 Cells

Based on the sequences of miR-317, miR-10494-3p, miR-6497-5p, miR-4, miR-275-3p, and miR-153, the mimics and NC (negative control) were designed and synthesized (GenePharma, Shanghai China) to analyze the significance of miRNA overexpression in Sf9 cells. The Sf9 cells were seeded and transfected with miRNA mimics (5 μg per well) or NC. After transfection for 8 h, the cells were infected with AcMNPV-GFP and subsequently collected at different time intervals (6, 24, 48, and 60 h) for qRT-PCR or for evaluating green fluorescence cells. The sequences of mimics and NC are listed in Supporting Information Table S.2.

RNA Sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Takara, Japan) from the AcMNPV-infected larval midgut or control group according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The ligation products were size selected by agarose gel electrophoresis, PCR amplified, and sequenced using an Illumina Genome Analyzer (San Diego, CA, USA) at the Beijing Genomics Institute (BGI, Shenzhen, China). The raw reads were filtered to obtain high-quality clean reads. Short Reads alignment tool, Bowtie2, was used for mapping reads to the rRNA (rRNA) database. The remaining reads were further used to assemble and analyze the transcriptome.

The small RNA libraries were constructed from each time point using a TruSeq small RNA sample preparation kit (Illumina). Briefly, RNAs were ligated to a 3′ adapter and, after size fraction, ligated to a 5′ adapter. The small RNA fractions were then used for reverse transcription following PCR. After purification, the final PCR products were sequenced using an Illumina Genome Analyzer (San Diego, CA, USA) at the Beijing Genomics Institute (BGI, Shenzhen, China). To screen clean reads, the raw data reads were filtered to eliminate low-quality transcripts, reads containing 3′ and 5′ adapters, insert tags, and sequences with fewer than 18 nucleotides. The remaining high-quality reads were mapped to the S. frugiperda genome (GCF_011064685.1) and then annotated into different classes to remove rRNA, scRNA, snoRNA, snRNA, and tRNA using the Rfam database. Finally, the unannotated clean sequences were used to predict novel miRNAs using miRDeep2 software. Three programs, including RNAhybrid, miRanda, and TargetScan, were used to predict and analyze the potential target genes of differentially expressed miRNAs (DEmiRNAs).

The gene expression level was normalized using the FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) method. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across samples or groups were identified using the edgeR package (http://www.rproject.org/). Genes with a fold change of ≥2 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.01 in comparison were identified as significant DEGs. Finally, the Gene Ontology (GO) database and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Gene & Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of all DEGs were performed for functional annotations. Statistics associated with the transcriptomic data used in this study are provided in Supporting Information Table S.3.

Western Blotting

Total proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, no. P0013B) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein concentrations were determined by using the BSA protein quantitative determination method. For Western blot analysis, aliquots of total proteins (20 μg each) were resolved by electrophoresis on sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gels, and the proteins were subsequently transferred to poly(vinylidene difluoride) (PVDF) membranes at 110 V for 90 min. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the respective primary antibodies, including anti-V5-tag, antitubulin mouse monoclonal antibody (Sigma-Aldrich), anti-p-JNK, anti-p-p38, and anti-JNK rabbit polyclonal antibody (Cell Signaling Technology; diluted 1:3000 with Tris-buffered saline (TBS) containing 0.05% Tween-20 (TBS-T)). Then, the membranes were rinsed three times with TBST (each for 10 min) and incubated with goat or mouse antirabbit secondary antibodies (1:3000) for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, the membranes were analyzed using an Image Quant LAS GEL Imaging System (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Inc., 4000 Biomolecular Imager).

Statistical Analysis

All of the results were obtained from at least three independent experiments. The data were analyzed by using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) via SPSS 25 (Chicago, IL, USA). Statistically significant differences were indicated as * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, or *** p < 0.001. Data with values of p < 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Expression Patterns of Immune Genes in S. frugiperda Midgut during AcMNPV Infection

To systematically identify the AcMNPV-induced DEGs in the S. frugiperda larval midgut, whole-transcriptome sequencing was performed at different time points (6, 48, and 72 h). Differential expression analysis was carried out to determine variations in gene expressions followed by AcMNPV infection. Pairwise comparisons between the control and AcMNPV-infected groups revealed a total of 3201 DEGs (Figure 1). Among these, 1984 (578 upregulated and 1406 downregulated), 1079 (361 upregulated and 718 downregulated), and 1311 (677 upregulated and 634 downregulated) DEGs were identified at 6, 48, and 72 hpi, respectively (Figure 1A,B). GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs revealed that genes associated with Toll/IMD and apoptosis pathways (crucial antiviral immune pathways) responded to AcMNPV infection (Figure 1C). Notably, most immune-related DEGs were downregulated, particularly at 6 and 48 hpi.

The log 2 fold change (log2FC) represents mean gene expression levels. Black dots denote no significant differences between control and AcMNPV-treated groups, while red and green dots signify up- and downregulated genes, respectively. (C) KEGG pathway enrichment analysis with immune-related pathways highlighted. (D) Expression profiling of DEGs in the Toll pathway. (E) Expression profiling of DEGs in the IMD pathway. (F) Expression profiling of DEGs involved in apoptosis. Note: T1, T2, and T3 refer to 6, 48, and 72 hpi.

Profiling of the Toll pathway-associated genes showed that most genes were downregulated during the initial stages of infection (6 and 48 h). The expression levels of βGRP-2 (β-1,3-glucan recognition protein) and of PGRP-SA (peptidoglycan recognition protein), a gene family involved in pathogen recognition, and core components of Toll including Spaetzle4 (toll activation) Myd88, Toll6-6, Toll7-1, and Toll7-2 were significantly downregulated at 6 and 48 h, gradually upregulating at 72 h (Figure 1D). Similarly, the key genes involved in the IMD pathway, such as PGRP-LB, PGRP-SC2, Pirk, IMD, Tak1, Tab2, and Relish, were also downregulated at the initial time points (Figure 1E). Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), the crucial downstream effectors of the Toll and IMD pathways, effectively neutralize and eliminate invading microbial threats. Studies have shown that AMPs, such as Cecropin and Defensin, have inhibitory effects on viruses.25,26 We observed a significant reduction in the expression levels of Cecropin A, Cecropin B, Gloverin, and Defensin at 6 and 48 hpi, although at 72 h the Defensin expression was dramatically upregulated (Figure 1D). These findings indicated that AcMNPV adapts to S. frugiperda immune responses during the early stages by interfering with the Toll/IMD pathways and successfully establishes the infection.

Apoptosis is a crucial antiviral immune response that helps limit infection by eliminating virus-infected cells.10 Our findings showed that the crucial apoptotic genes, including Eiger-2, Hep, Fos1, and p53, were all downregulated at 6 and 48 h, indicating the ability of AcMNPV to suppress the host apoptosis machinery. Caspases, a family of proteases central to apoptosis, were also identified, and the results showed that Dronc and Caspase3 were continually expressed at all time points (Figure 1F). It is worth highlighting that the genes involved in all three pathways (Toll/IMD and apoptosis) followed a similar expression pattern, implying that these pathways may interact/cross-talk and respond to AcMNPV infection.

Validation of Toll/IMD and Apoptosis-Associated DEGs in the Midgut and Sf9 Cells

To validate the RNA-seq results, we performed qRT-PCR to detect the expression of immune-related DEGs central to the Toll/IMD and apoptosis pathways in the AcMNPV-infected and uninfected midgut tissues at 6, 48, and 72 hpi (Figure 2). The results showed that at 6 hpi, genes selected from the Toll pathway showed similar trends as presented in the transcriptome data. At 72 hpi, all genes except βGRP-1, Spaetzle4, and Toll-7 exhibited no differences or decreased compared to the control. AMPs, including Cecropin A, Cecropin B, Gloverin, and Defensin, were also consistent with the transcriptome findings (Figure 2A). In the IMD pathway, the expression trends were consistent with RNA-seq data at all time points. However, minor differences were observed, such as continual upregulation of Pirk at 48 and 72 hpi and a significantly high expression of Relish at 72 hpi (Figure 2B). Similarly, the expression profile of genes from apoptosis pathways also supported the results of transcriptome data, with the exception of Eiger and Wengen, which were exceedingly expressed at 48 hpi (Figure 2C). Overall, the gene expression trends detected via qRT-PCR were consistent with the RNA-seq results, indicating the reliability of the sequencing data for further functional studies.

(C) Expression analysis of genes related to apoptosis. The significance of differences was determined by a t test and is denoted by *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

To further validate and establish the role of selected genes, AcMNPV-infected Sf9 cells were used for identifying the DEGs’ pattern (Figure 3). Sf9 cells were infected with AcMNPV-GFP to observe fluorescence. At 24 hpi, the cells began to show green fluorescence. The number and intensity of fluorescent cells increased over time, and by the 60 h mark, more than half of the observable area exhibited green fluorescence (Figure 3A). The infected Sf9 cells were collected (6, 24, 48, and 60 hpi) to detect the expression of Toll/IMD and apoptosis pathway genes. The results showed that at 6 hpi, all Toll pathway genes were either downregulated (i.e., βGRP-2, Spaetzle4, and Cecropin A and B) or had nonsignificant changes in expression. At 24 and 48 hpi, AMPs, including Defensin, Gloverin, and CecropinB, were significantly upregulated (Figure 3B). In the IMD pathway, the expression of PGRP-LC, Tab2, and IKKα decreased substantially at 6 hpi, while the remaining genes had no significant change. At 24 h, Tab2, PGRP-LC, IKKα, Relish, and Dredd were significantly upregulated, whereas at 48 and 60 h, IKKβ and Tab2 were expressed consistently (Figure 3C). Similarly, all genes were downregulated in the apoptosis pathway at 6 hpi, apart from Wengen. The expression of TRAF6 significantly increased at 60 hpi though it remained unchanged at 24 and 48 hpi (Figure 3D). These findings show that most immune-related genes are suppressed during the initial stages of infection and as the infection progresses, the changes in gene expression become rapid. The overall expression trend of these genes in Sf9 cells was consistent with the pattern in larval midgut tissues, strengthening our findings that these immune pathways play a crucial role in antiviral defense.

Toll Pathway Interaction with JNK-p38 Was TRAF6-Dependent in S. frugiperda

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses showed that apoptosis genes responded to AcMNPV infection. Next, we detected JNK and p38 phosphorylation, which have been shown to mediate apoptosis activation under virus infection.27 We detected the abundance of phosphorylated JNK (p-JNK), p38, and endogenous JNK in Sf9 cells at various time points (6, 24, 48, and 60 hpi). Western blot showed that p-JNK and p-p38 were significantly enhanced as the AcMNPV infection progressed at 48 and 60 h compared to the early stages of infection (6 and 24 h). The increase in p-JNK and p-38 indicated that JNK-mediated apoptosis occurs in the later stages of infection (Figure 4A).

The relative protein level in the control samples was arbitrarily set as 1. Protein bands from at least three membranes were scanned for each protein using imageJ. (B) Western blot analysis to detect the cytochrome C (Cyt C) release from the cytoplasm and mitochondria in Sf9 cells. (C) Expression profiling of several Toll receptors in the larval midgut and Sf9 cells at different time intervals. (D) Relative expression of TRAF6 and relative protein levels of p-JNK (d1) and p-p38 (d2) were scanned for each protein using imageJ. (E) Detection of JNK and p38 phosphorylation levels in TIR3 overexpressing AcMNPV-infected Sf9 cells. The significance of differences was determined by a t test and is denoted by *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

The cytochrome C (Cyt C)-mediated mitochondrial pathway is a major pathway that leads to apoptosis.28 To investigate whether Cyt C is involved in AcMNPV-induced apoptosis in Sf9 cells, Cyt C protein levels were detected using anti-Cyt C (antibody) in the cytoplasm and mitochondria at different time intervals (6, 24, 48, and 60 hpi). The results showed that Cyt C protein was mainly present in the mitochondria. Notably, the abundance of Cyt C in the control was significantly higher compared to that in the treatment group. Since the release of Cyt C in the cytoplasm is essential for initiating the mitochondrial pathway, we explored the presence of Cyt C in the cytoplasm. The results revealed a lack of Cyt C content in the cytoplasm, demonstrating that AcMNPV infection does not induce the release of Cyt C from the mitochondria into the cytoplasm. These results indicated that AcMNPV-induced apoptosis operates independently of Cyt C (Figure 4B).

To determine whether the Toll pathway plays a role in apoptosis induction, we quantified the basal expression of various Toll receptors in larval midgut tissue and Sf9 cells at different time intervals by qRT-PCR. We observed that the Toll3-2 expression level was higher in the larval midgut than in Sf9 cells, where it was essentially absent (Figure 4C). To further investigate the potential interaction between the Toll and JNK pathways during AcMNPV-induced apoptosis, we proceeded to clone the intercellular domain of Toll3-2 (the TIR3 domain) into an expression vector (pIEx-TIR3-V5). Following this, we transfected the construct into Sf9 cells to examine whether the Toll activation triggers signal transduction that affects the JNK pathway. Studies have shown that Toll-like receptors (TLRs) can recruit TRAF6, which initiates a signaling cascade that activates MAPKs (JNK and p38) and caspases, leading to apoptosis.29 Given these findings, we investigated whether TIR3-mediated apoptosis relied on TRAF6 by conducting dsTRAF6 transfection in TIR3 overexpressing Sf9 cells. We observed that the overexpression of TIR3 in Sf9 cells elevated the phosphorylation levels of JNK and p38, suggesting that the Toll pathway mediates JNK activation. RNAi-mediated silencing of TRAF6, a potential adaptor of the Toll receptor, led to a significant decrease in the phosphorylation levels of JNK and p38 compared to the control group (dsRFP), indicating the involvement of TRAF6 in the Toll-mediated JNK pathway in apoptosis (Figure 4D,E).

Additionally, we infected TIR3 overexpressing cells with AcMNPV. We found that the phosphorylation levels of JNK and p38 were substantially reduced during the initial stage (6 hpi) of infection, indicating that AcMNPV potentially inhibits the Toll pathway to mediate apoptosis in the earlier stage of infection. However, during the later phase (60 hpi), both p-JNK and p-p38 significantly upregulated (Figure 4D,E), suggesting the Toll pathway cross-talk with JNK via the TRAF6 adapter, ultimately leading to apoptosis.

Dynamics of AcMNPV-Induced miRNAs

MiRNAs present an extra layer of gene regulation that integrates with transcriptional and post-transcriptional processes and fine-tunes gene expression. After analyzing the crucial immune pathway genes associated with AcMNPV infection in S. frugiperda, we investigated DEmiRNAs from the AcMNPV-infected midgut and the control (6, 48, and 72 hpi) by employing RNA-seq. The DEmiRNAs targeting Toll/IMD and apoptosis pathway-related genes were screened, and 14 candidates were filtered (9 known and 5 novel) (Figure 5A).

Furthermore, qRT-PCR was performed to detect the expression of immune-related DEmiRNAs in AcMNPV-infected Sf9 cells at 6, 24, 48, and 60 hpi. The results showed that DEmiRNAs, including sfr-miR-6094-5p, bmo-miR-6497-5p, bmo-miR-275-3p, bmo-miR-278-3p, mse-miR-317, sfr-miR-34-5p, novel-miR-153, novel-miR-52, and novel-miR-2, were significantly expressed at 6 hpi. However, most of these miRNAs downregulated at later stages of infection, except for bmo-miR-275-3p, novel-miR-153, and novel-miR-52, which were significantly expressed at 48 and 60 hpi (Figure 5B).

Multiple miRNAs Target AcMNPV-Induced Host Immune Genes

To further evaluate the regulatory effects of DEmiRNAs on their immune-related target genes, we transfected miRNA mimics (overexpression) to detect the potential target genes in AcMNPV-infected Sf9 cells (Figure 6). The relative expression of p53, Eip74EF, AIF1-2, Cactus, ANKRD, and Eiger-2 was significantly reduced after the transfection of mse-miR-317, sfr-miR-10494-3p, bmo-miR-6497-5p, bmo-miR-275-3p, novel-miR-153, and novel-miR-4, respectively (Figure 6A). These six miRNAs exhibited significant regulatory effects on AcMNPV-induced immune genes and were subsequently selected for downstream studies. Bioinformatic analysis was employed to identify potential interaction sites between miRNAs and their target mRNAs. The binding sites of mse-miR-317, bmo-miR-6497-5p, novel-miR-153, and novel-miR-4 were located in CDS regions of p53, AIF1-2, ANKRD, and Eiger-2, respectively. In the case of sfr-miR-10494-3p and miR-275-3p, the target regions were in 3′UTR of Eip74EF and Cactus (Figure 6B).

Host miRNAs Benefit AcMNPV Proliferation in Sf9 Cells

To elucidate the role of selected miRNAs in virus proliferation, we transfected Sf9 cells with miRNA mimics and infected them with AcMNPV-GFP. Interestingly, the fluorescence intensity increased in the miRNA-mimicking groups compared to the WT and mimic NC groups (Figure 7A). Western blotting also presented similar results, where the miRNA mimic-treated group showed a higher abundance in protein bands, particularly in mimic-10494-3p and mimic-6497-5p treatments (Figure 7B-b1). The relative gray value comparison analysis results of the protein bands were consistent with the Western blot findings (Figure 7B-b2). The relative fluorescence cell counts of the mimic-317, mimic-10494-3p, mimic-6497-5p, and mimic-275-3p treatment groups were higher than those of the WT and mimic NC groups (Figure 7C-c1). This indicated that the ratio of AcMNPV-infected Sf9 cells was increased. The transcription level of GFP was also significantly higher in the treatment group (Figure 7C-c2). To further support our findings, we quantified the expression of multiple AcMNPV genes, including gp64, ie1, vp39, and polh, in both the mimic-treated and control groups. A significant increase in the activity of AcMNPV genes was recorded, potentially indicating enhanced viral activities (Figure 7D d1–d4). Our results concluded that the overexpression of mse-miR-317, sfr-miR-10494-3p, bmo-miR-6497-5p, bmo-miR-275-3p, novel-miR-4, and novel-miR-153 promotes AcMNPV replication and proliferation likely by suppressing the crucial S. frugiperda antiviral immune responses.

The significant difference was determined by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison tests using GraphPad Prism, with different letters indicating a significant difference (p < 0.05) and identical letters for nonsignificant differences (p > 0.05).

Different Expression Patterns of miRNAs and Target Genes under BmNPV Infection in Sf9 Cells

To verify whether the S. frugiperda-encoded miRNAs and their immune-related target genes are specifically associated with AcMNPV infection and contribute to its proliferation, we infected Sf9 cells with BmNPV, a virus that is specific to silkworms. The results showed that the infection rate of BmNPV-treated cells was significantly lower than in AcMNPV-treated cells, even at the higher MOI of BmNPV (Figure 8A), suggesting that Sf9 cells are more sensitive to AcMNPV infection and resistant to BmNPV. As predicted, the qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that, in contrast to the observed upregulation trend of the DEmiRNAs following AcMNPV treatment, the expression levels of these miRNAs were downregulated or completely absent after BmNPV treatment (Figure 8B). Similarly, AcMNPV infection led to the downregulation of target genes for these miRNAs, while no changes in expression were observed in the BmNPV-treated group except for the upregulation of p53 (Figure 8C). The findings indicate that AcMNPV, as a specific pathogen for S. frugiperda, employs host miRNAs to escape the immune response and benefit from its infection and proliferation.

Discussion

Over the past decade, S. frugiperda has emerged as a widespread invasive agricultural insect pest. Notorious for its ability to neutralize a broad range of synthetic insecticides, the pest has caused severe economic losses, prompting researchers to probe for alternative and ecofriendly management strategies.23 Entomopathogenic viruses (EPVs) provide a promising alternative to chemical insecticides for controlling insect pests. However, limitations, such as a narrow host range and low virulence, have restricted broad-scale application. Therefore, it is imperative to study the underlying molecular interactions between EPVs and insect hosts to improve the efficacy of existing viral biocontrol agents. In the current study, we employed RNA-seq to investigate the effects of AcMNPV infection on the midgut tissues of S. frugiperda. The insect midgut is the first organ that AcMNPV encounters when it enters the insect’s body, where it damages midgut epithelial cells, replicates, and disseminates while triggering the midgut immune responses.30 These interactions highlight the importance of studying the midgut immunity during AcMNPV infection.

Insects, like other organisms, mount a complex transcriptional response to virus infection. This response is triggered by the recognition of viral nucleic acids or capsid protein, leading to the activation of several signaling pathways such as JAK/STAT, apoptosis, and Toll/IMD pathways. These pathways, in turn, regulate the expression of antiviral proteins to combat viral infections.5 Transcriptome profiling of the AcMNPV-infected S. frugiperda midgut revealed changes in the expression of genes associated with the Toll/IMD and apoptosis pathways, indicating the crucial antiviral functions of these immune pathways, similar to an earlier study conducted in AcMNPV-infected Trichoplusia ni.31 In the initial stages of infection (6 and 48 h), a majority of the genes, i.e., βGRP-2, Spz4, Myd88, Toll6-6 (Toll pathway), PGRP-SC2, Pirk, IMD, Relish (IMD pathway), Eiger-2, Hep, Fos1, and p53 (apoptosis pathway), were significantly downregulated, as previously described in B. mori, where baculovirus infection suppressed the expression of numerous host genes at 12–18 h.32 βGRPs/PGRPs are recognition proteins and are considered to have antiviral potential. The BmBDV (Bombyx mori bidensovirus) infection significantly induces the expression of BmPGRP-LB and BmPGRP-LE in B. mori and initiates the IMD pathway,33 though the mechanism of virus recognition remains unclear. The BmNPV infection significantly suppressed BmβGRP4 in the silkworm midgut, facilitating apoptosis inhibition and evasion of the host antiviral defense.34 The downregulation of several immune-related genes involved in recognition proteins, signaling, activation of immune pathways, and effectors has also been reported in the SeMNPV- (Spodoptera exigua multiple nucleopolyhedrovirus) and AcMNPV-infected S. exigua larval gut,35,36 consistent with our results.

Research has demonstrated the antiviral role of AMPs in B. mori and Drosophila.37,38 The expression of AMPs, including Gloverin and Cecropin-1, was significantly induced in AcMNPV-infected Helicoverpa armigera.39 Gloverins isolated from AcMNPV-infected T. ni hemocytes demonstrated their antiviral activity via the destruction of the virus envelope,40 signifying the inhibitory effect of AMPs on AcMNPV. However, the host–virus is a complex interaction, and viruses deploy proteins to limit or evade the insect antiviral peptides, particularly during the initial stages of infection. Kallithea virus infection can suppress the immune system of Drosophila, which also leads to a general decrease in the expression of AMP genes.41 The immunosuppression by BmCPV in silkworms reduced the expression levels of Cecropin A and Cecropin B,25 a trend similar to our findings, where we detected the lower expression levels of AMPs during the early stages of infection. These findings showed that AcMNPV evades the S. frugiperda midgut immune responses by interfering with downstream signaling and the activation of antiviral immune pathways.

Apoptosis, a central antiviral defense mechanism in insects,10 exhibited a downregulation trend in many of its pathway-related genes, including p53 (tumor suppressor gene/regulator of stress response in insects), Eiger, Wengen (tumor necrosis factor receptor, TNFR), and Relish (NF-κB), during the early stages of AcMPNV infection in S. frugiperda. Baculoviruses suppress host apoptosis by producing antiapoptotic proteins such as p35, inhibitor of apoptosis (IAP), and apoptotic suppressor (Apsup).42 These suppressors function at different steps in the apoptotic pathways, though typically only one of these proteins has a dominant role in suppressing apoptosis.43 In our findings, the expression of p53 was significantly suppressed by mse-miR-317, leading to increased viral proliferation. To investigate the function of p53 in AcMNPV-infected S. frugiperda and to further elucidate the host miRNA–AcMNPV interaction, RNAi-mediated silencing of p53 was performed. Sf9 cells were transfected with dsp53 and observed under a fluorescence microscope every 12 h. The RNAi-mediated p53 silencing was validated by quantifying the relative gene expression using qRT-PCR (Figure S1B). Compared to that of the WT and NC (dsRFP) groups, the fluorescence intensity in the dsp53-treated group increased significantly at 48–84 hpi, indicating an increase in the number of AcMNPV-infected Sf9 cells. Finally, we observed a significant increase in the ratio of dsp53-treated Sf9 cells under AcMNPV infection (Figure S1A,C). Viral particles can continue replicating in apoptotic cells by inhibiting or delaying the apoptosis of host cells,44 as described in our findings.

Studies have shown that AcMNPV infection triggers apoptosis via activation of the JNK pathway in Sf9 cells. In silkworms, JNK phosphorylation occurs during BmNPV infection, and the JNK signaling pathway is involved in BmNPV-induced apoptosis.45 In our findings, the JNK-mediated activation of apoptosis occurred during the later stages of infection (48 and 60 h), and the JNK pathway remained inactive during the initial stages (6 and 24 h), suggesting that AcMNPV inhibits the early activation of apoptosis via JNK. Consistent with our results, the delayed activation of JNK was observed in BmNPV-infected BmN cells.32 The mitochondrial pathway, one of the major pathways leading to apoptosis, releases Cyt C, a protein that plays a role in apoptosis.46 In our results, the absence of Cyt C in the cytoplasm of AcMNPV-infected cells indicated that viral infection did not induce the release of Cyt C from the mitochondria to the cytoplasm, implying that AcMNPV-induced apoptosis may be independent of the Cyt-C-mediated mitochondrial pathway.

We theorized that the Toll pathway interacts with JNK and induces apoptosis. Toll3-2 was identified due to its significantly elevated expression levels during infection. The role of TIR3 in AcMNPV-infected Sf9 cells was verified, and the results demonstrated the involvement of TRAF6 (potential adapter of Toll-3) in the Toll-mediated JNK pathway. Evidence suggests that TLRs can bind to adapter protein MyD88 and transmit downstream signals. Concurrently, MyD88 recruits TRAF6 to engage in various signaling pathways, including the NF-κB and MAPK pathways, leading to apoptosis.47 Our transcriptome results and subsequent functional experiments showed that AcMNPV inhibits JNK-mediated apoptosis by blocking the Toll signaling pathway. The overexpression of TIR3 during the late stage of infection resulted in the heightened phosphorylation of JNK and p38. Furthermore, we observed a similar upregulation of Toll/IMD and apoptosis-related genes, notably Toll receptors, MyD88, Tab2, TRAF6, and others, at 72 hpi, implying that the Toll pathway participates in JNK-mediated apoptosis by conducting signals through the TRAF6 adaptor.

MiRNAs are considered to be vital players in insect host–pathogen interactions.48 Insects, as well as viruses, exploit miRNAs for their specific benefits. Studies conducted on several insect species have shown the response of host miRNAs to pathogen stress and revealed the differential expression of a vast number of miRNAs.49 In BmNPV-infected larvae, 38 DEmiRNAs were identified to be involved in several functions, including innate immune responses.50 Several viral genes, including odv-e18, orf118, me53, orf98a p43, etc., were predicted to be potential targets of the B. mori miRNA, bmo-miR-390.51 Bantam miRNA is crucial in anti-AcMNPV responses in S. litura and S. exigua, where the inhibition of bantam increases the viral load and upregulated viral genes.52,53 Investigations conducted in AcMNPV-infected Sf9 cells revealed the differential expression of numerous conserved and novel miRNAs.54 Similar to the above-mentioned studies, we identified multiple DEmiRNAs that regulate Toll/IMD and apoptosis pathway-related genes. Among those, some miRNAs are notably involved in the antiviral immune response, including sfr-miR-6094-5p, an inhibitor of MbMNPV (Mamestra brassicae multiple nucleopolyhedrovirus) proliferation.55 sfr-miR-34-5p inhibits AcMNPV replication by targeting virus genes, i.e., ac78 and ie2.56 Interestingly most of the DEmiRNAs were downregulated in AcMNPV-infected Sf9 cells after initial upregulation. Viruses can hijack and manipulate the host miRNA machinery to promote infection and proliferation. The profiling of H. armigera-encoded miRNAs in response to Helicoverpa armigera single nucleopolyhedrovirus (HaSNPV) infection revealed that the virus suppresses the expression of har-miR-14 to regulate Ha-EcR (ecdysone receptor gene) and thrive in a favorable cellular environment.22 Likewise, har-miR-8 or har-miR-429 significantly enhanced viral replication,57 hinting at the virus-mediated regulation of host behavior by manipulating host-encoded miRNAs. Based on our findings and previous studies, we theorized that AcMNPV might be regulating S. fruigperda-encoded antiviral miRNAs to promote its dissemination.

In our research, the overexpression of multiple miRNAs, including mse-miR-317, sfr-miR-10494-3p, bmo-miR-6497-5p, bmo-miR-275-3p, novel-miR-4, and novel-miR-153, efficiently suppressed the expression of p53, Eip74EF, AIF1-2, Cactus, ANKRD, and Eiger-2, respectively. Recent research has shown that miR-317 negatively regulates the Toll immune response and impacts Drosophila survival.58 Intriguingly, p53, the target gene of mse-miR-317, is known to play a key role during viral infection in several insect species by rapidly inducing apoptosis.13−15Eip74EF, a gene essential for development, was significantly suppressed by sfr-miR-10494-3p. BmE74A, reported in silkworms, promotes BmNPV proliferation by directly interacting with viruses.59 In S. frugiperda, AIF1 is crucial during stress-induced apoptosis.60 Similarly, Eiger-2 was significantly suppressed by novel-miR-153. Eiger interaction with the Wengen membrane receptor leads to the activation of JNK and mediates apoptosis.61 These findings suggest that AcMNPV manipulates the expression of S. frugiperda-encoded miRNAs to facilitate viral infection and proliferation.

The initial stage of baculovirus infection in the lepidopteran midgut is crucial to the virus’s success, as the midgut cells mount important host defensive reactions. These transcriptional responses involve strategies to prevent productive viral replication in the midgut and transmission to other host tissues. Our results concluded that AcMNPV infects the S. frugiperda midgut and upregulates several host-encoded miRNAs during the early stages of infection, suppressing antiviral immune genes. Further experiments revealed that JNK-mediated apoptosis causes cell lysis and death, thereby promoting the further spread of progeny viruses. In conclusion, the findings from these and future studies hold promise for leveraging beneficial viruses, such as baculoviruses, for effective biological control of important insect pests, including S. frugiperda. Furthermore, gaining a more profound comprehension of virus–host cell interactions in the midgut can contribute to developing innovative strategies to impede the transmission of harmful viruses by insects. By unraveling the intricate networks involved in insect–virus interactions, we can better understand insect antiviral defense mechanisms and the coevolutionary dynamics between host antiviral defenses and virus counter-defense.
