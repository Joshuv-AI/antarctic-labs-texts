Three-dimensional printing of multilayer stretchable electronics with inclined interconnect accesses
Authors: Wenbo Zhao, Yifan Deng, Yu Zhang, Hongbo Tu, Kanghuan Huang, Fan Bu, Sufeng Zhou, Jie Pu, Qinghe Cao, Cao Guan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multilayer stretchable electronics offer revolutionary potential for next-generation smart wearables, soft robotics and human-machine interfaces, but they face considerable challenges in achieving reliable three-dimensional (3D) interconnections with satisfied stretchability, stability and manufacturing efficiency. Herein, we report additive manufactured (3D printed) multilayer stretchable electronics with unpunched inclined interconnect accesses. Contrary to the widely adopted subtractive manufactured electronics with punched vertical interconnections, the printed interlayer access with inclined geometry effectively mitigates stress concentration and minimizes Poisson effect-induced deformation, thus ensuring stable mechanical and electrical properties. In addition, the 3D printing strategy also significantly promotes the design freedom with rapid manufacturing process. As a result, such 3D printed multilayer electronics illustrate high stretchability (646% strain limit) and excellent stability (over 11000 cycles at 100% strain), surpassing previously reported multilayer stretchable devices. A wireless-powered display system maintains stable performance under large-scale stretching, and a four-layer conformal physiological monitoring system is also demonstrated.

Introduction

Stretchable electronics that offer interfacial conformality and maintain original functionality under large-scale deformation have transformative potential in the field of physiological monitoring1–5, soft robots6–8, human-machine interaction9–11, and virtual reality/augmented reality12–14, representing a disruptive innovation for the next generation electronics and information technology. To improve the integration density with optimized performance for stretchable electronics, research efforts have been devoted to manufacturing multilayer stretchable electronics that well integrate various devices and functionalities15–17. However, the lack of reliable 3D interconnections remains a major obstacle in manufacturing multilayer stretchable electronics18,19, which imposes significant constraints on stretchability, electrical robustness, and manufacturing efficiency.

Copy from rigid silicon-based electronics, vertical interconnect accesses (VIAs) are widely used to electrically connect multilayered architectures in flexible multilayer electronics20. The manufacturing process typically involves with punched via formation by photolithography or laser drilling, conductive filling and mask removement21,22. For instance, Lin et al. proposed a stretchable Cu-based VIA-serpentine structure by combining UV laser drilling, carbon-assisted plating, and layer-to-layer alignment, which enabled the manufacturing of multilayer stretchable circuit that integrated with electronic skin patches, conformal antennas, and stretchable light-emitting diode (LED) displays23. The multilayer stretchable electronic system shows stable performance for 680 cycles at 20% strain. Despite this, stress concentration and mechanical mismatch at the punched rigid Cu-based VIAs still limit the application under larger-scale deformation, which may even cause flaws to severely restrict the stretchability and stability of the electronics. With this concern, liquid metal (LM) based soft interconnects have also been developed for stretchable electronics owing to their intrinsic ultra-low modulus, high conductivity, and excellent fluidity24,25. For example, Kramer-Bottiglio et al. reported a laser drilling-based bilayer stretchable circuit by filling the punched vias with LM, and successfully utilized it in soft robots26. Although the bilayer circuits performed well under 300% stretching for 200 cycles, the punched LM-based interconnects still suffered both transverse and axial stress induced by the Poisson effect of the elastomer, which caused severe geometrical deformations. Because of such Poisson effect-induced deformation, the stretchability and stability of the electronics are still far from satisfactory27.

Although VIAs with subtractive manufacturing shows promising potential for stretchable electronics, they are generally useful for constructing single-layer or two-layer architectures28,29. When extended to three or more layers, the processing complexity and failure risk not only increase exponentially, but the inherent stress concentration of the punched VIAs is also exacerbated30. To overcome this, Jiang et al. reported a layer-by-layer fabrication strategy for multilayer stretchable electronics, which involves spin-coating and punched stamp protection/removing. The resulted electronics shows stable performance of 1000 cycles at 50% strain, but the complex coating and layer-to-layer alignment processes undoubtedly reduce manufacturing efficiency31. Although multilayer stretchable electronics have also been manufactured by gravitational field-assisted liquid metal droplets settling32 and 3D printing-assisted microchannel filling33, the overall stretchability, stability and manufacturing efficiency remain unsatisfied. Therefore, it is highly desirable to develop interlayer interconnections with superior stretchability, stability and manufacturing efficiency, thereby fully unlocking the potential of highly integrated multilayer stretchable electronics for diverse applications in the coming intelligent era.

Herein, contrary to the widely adopted subtractive manufactured electronics with punched vertical interconnections, we report additive manufactured (3D printed) multilayer stretchable electronics with unpunched inclined interconnect accesses. Digital light processing (DLP) is used for the manufacturing of multilayered stretchable substrate with inclined areas for interlayer accesses, and conductive interconnections are seamlessly manufactured across the inclined areas by direct ink writing (DIW). Such 3D printed inclined interlayer interconnections not only effectively dissipate vertical stress through horizontal redistribution at the interconnections, but also efficiently reduce Poisson effect-induced deformation under large-scale deformation. In addition, the 3D printing strategy also significantly promotes the customized design and rapid manufacturing of multilayer stretchable electronic systems. As a result, such 3D printed multilayer electronics illustrate high stretchability (646% strain limit) and excellent stability (over 11000 cycles at 100% strain), outperforming previously reported multilayer stretchable devices. A three-layer wireless & supercapacitor-powered stretchable display system is manufactured by integration of an LED array, a supercapacitor array, and a wireless charging coil, which shows stable charging and illumination performance under large-scale stretching. And a wireless conformable physiological monitoring system with four-layer architecture is also demonstrated, showing the promise potential of the 3D printing strategy with unpunched inclined interconnections for highly integrated multilayer and multifunctional stretchable electronics.

Results

Design and 3D printing of multilayer stretchable electronics

As illustrated in Fig. 1a, conventional subtractive manufacturing of multilayer stretchable circuits typically involves basic processes with mask coverage, laser ablation, conductive filling and mask remove26. Extending this approach from single layer device to multilayer configurations requires sequential fabrication with printing/assembling the bottom circuit (steps 1–4), creating the middle circuit through laser drilling and conductive material (e.g. LM) injection (steps 5–9), bottom and middle layer stacking (steps 10–12), and finally constructing the top circuit by repeating steps 5–11 (steps 13–19). Such tedious subtractive manufacturing process requires a minimum of 20 steps to fabricate a three-layer display system, e.g. a structure that integrates an LED array, a supercapacitor array, and an LM antenna in a hierarchical layout. In contrast, Fig. 1b illustrates our additive manufacturing scheme for stretchable substrates and LM-based conductive traces. For an equivalent three-layer system, the process initiates from DLP-printing of bottom substrate with inclined areas (step 1), followed by DIW-printing of conductive traces and interlayer interconnections (step 2), and subsequent device printing/assembly (step 3). The sequence continues with printing the second substrate layer interlocking with the bottom structure, DIW-printing of conductive traces and interconnections, and final device printing/assembly with encapsulation (steps 4-7). This 3D printing-based approach efficiently fabricates the similar multilayer circuit in only 7 steps, significantly fewer than that required by subtractive manufacturing-based process. In addition, the 3D printing strategy will result in inclined interconnect accesses with geometric angle θ, which shall effectively dissipate vertical stress through horizontal redistribution, and significantly reduce Poisson effect-induced deformation under large-scale stretching. And compared to the conventional laser drilling approach, such additive manufacturing technique eliminates the high-power laser processing and subtractive material removal, thereby reducing energy consumption, avoiding potential damage risks, and minimizing material waste. Last but not the least, the inherent flexibility of 3D printing could dramatically enhance both design freedom and manufacturing efficiency for customized multilayer stretchable electronics.

Material characteristics and design freedom

To illustrate the design freedom of the 3D printing strategy, Fig. 2a shows a black box circuit with three-layer architecture containing 4 different conductive paths and 6 inclined interconnect accesses, where points 1 and 2 represent the bilayer and three-layer structures, respectively. The customized stretchable substrate is fabricated by DLP, where the precursor solution of elastomer substrate is prepared by mixing and stirring butyl acrylate (BA) as linear chain builder, aliphatic urethane diacrylate (AUD) as cross linker, isobornyl acrylate (IBOA) as diluent, and diphenyl (2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl) phosphine oxide (TPO) as photoinitiator (Supplementary Fig. 1). Abundant free radicals generate during UV-patterned irradiation, and then propagate through BA, AUD, and IBOA molecules to initiate polymerization, resulting in a transition from liquid precursor to elastomer (Supplementary Fig. 2). Such polymerization process and interface formation is characterized by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) test. As illustrated in Fig. 2b, the C = C stretching peaks at 810 cm−1 and 1405 cm−1 disappear after UV curing, which attributes to polymerization crosslink. In addition, two typical infrared absorption peaks at 690 cm−1 and 970 cm−1 remain in both precursor and elastomer, which are disubstituted to cis- and trans-vinyl bending vibrations, respectively, indicating residual vinyl groups at the substrate surface34,35. As shown in Fig. 2c, the interfacial adhesion strength of homologous substrates is investigated by applying 180° peeling test. The result exhibits characteristic tensile fracture behavior rather than interfacial delamination, indicating that the bonding strength at the homologous interface is comparable to that of the substrate bulk36. Such robust interfacial adhesion could be originated from the additional polymerization of residual vinyl groups during the secondary photocuring process, which further enables the overall stretchability and stability of the multilayer electronic device. Compared with rigid buck conductors that follow Pouillet’s law and exhibit large resistance increases at moderate strains, strain-insensitive and conformal LM materials maintain stable conductivity under large deformations37. However, the manufacturing of complicated LM-based circuits is hindered by the high surface tension and poor adhesion of LM (Supplementary Fig. 3a–c)38,39. To address this challenge and facilitate direct DIW printing of LM-based conductive connections, an LM-based ink is developed by incorporating LM droplets within a thermoplastic urethane (TPU) framework (Supplementary Fig. 3d-f). Bulk GaIn alloy is processed into micron-scale particles through ultrasonication, followed by homogeneous mixing with TPU to form LM-based ink (Supplementary Fig. 4a and b)40. As illustrated in Fig. 2e, the rheological test reveals high viscoelasticity and distinct shear-thinning behavior of the LM-based ink41, which forms continuous filaments at the nozzle tip during extrusion printing (while pure LM exhibits spherical agglomeration). Furthermore, conductive traces with well-defined edges and uniform widths can be obtained by adjusting the printing pressure and speed, achieving minimum trace width and gap of 100 µm and 30 µm, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 5). Noting that the obtained LM-based ink not only offers superior printability and processability, but also maintains superior dynamic deformation adaptability due to the intrinsic stretchability of the LM-TPU matrix. As shown in Fig. 2f, the conductive trace printed via DIW reveals well-defined linear pattern, and exhibits metallic luster after sintering by mechanical pressure due to the formation of electron pathway, which attributes to the destruction of surface oxide layer and the deformation of TPU three-dimensional network (Supplementary Fig. 4c)42. The sintered conductive material exhibits an electrical conductivity of 4.54 × 104 S m−1 with a resistance change (ΔR/R0) of less than 7 at 620% strain, highlighting the dual mechanical and electrical advantages of the 3D-printed LM-based ink traces (Supplementary Fig. 6). Although the mechanical pressure sintering leads to a slight change in the minimum line width (increasing from 100 µm to 105 µm) and gap (decreasing from 30 µm to 25 µm) of the conductive traces, the well-defined geometric features are still maintained (Supplementary Fig. 7). The resistance of the printed conductive trace scales linearly with its length (Fig. 2g), demonstrating the excellent uniformity of the LM-based conductive connection by DIW, which is suitable as interconnect for stretchable circuits28. In addition, such LM-based ink can be directly printed on various substrates, such as PI, PET, glass and PDMS, further proving the promising processability for practical usage (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Compared to traditional subtractive approaches, our additive manufacturing strategy constructs 3D electronic devices with significantly enhanced design freedom and manufacturing efficiency. Figure 2h illustrates the design schematic of printed interlayer interconnects with various inclined angles (θ) from 0° and 90°. In addition, six representative inclined interconnect accesses are printed with precisely controlled geometries, demonstrating the superiority of DLP in manufacturing customized multilayer stretchable electronic systems (Fig. 2i and Supplementary Fig. 9). Following the DIW printing of LM-based ink, mechanical sintering and subsequent interlocking assembly, a black-box circuit is successfully fabricated (Fig. 2j). Although this obtained black box circuit seems interwoven, it contains 4 independent and different conductive pathways through 6 distinct inclined interconnect accesses (Supplementary Fig. 10). Notably, commercial electrical components can be easily integrated by the proposed 3D-printed framework, such as resistance/capacitance (R/C), LED, comparators (COMP), operational amplifiers (OPA), and field effect transistors (FETs), demonstrating excellent compatibility and design freedom (Fig. 2k). Due to the deformation adaptability of stretchable substrate and conductive trace, the integrated components maintain stable performance under bending, twisting, and stretching (Fig. 2l). And benefiting from the residual surface groups on the elastomeric substrate as well as the intrinsic stretchability and viscosity of the LM‑TPU matrix, the commercial electronic components can be assembled without detachment, and meanwhile, the homologous photocurable precursor (additional substrate layer) serves as an encapsulation, ensuring stable operation under large deformations (Supplementary Fig. 11). In addition, such 3D printing strategy readily enables the fabrication of large-area electronics with complex patterns, which also illustrate promising flexibility (Fig. 2m and n).

Mechanical properties of 3D printed multilayer stretchable electronics

To evaluate the mechanical properties and optimize inclined angle designs, bilayer elastic substrates with different inclined angles (θ) ranging from 15° to 75° are constructed (Fig. 3a). The in-plane connection (θ = 0°) is compared as a reference, and punched sample (θ = 90°) by subtractive method is also tested. Figure 3b exhibits stress-strain results of different samples, where 0°, 15–75° and 90° are defined as reference, printed samples and punched sample, respectively. The strain limits (εf) of all printed samples reach 360% to 646%, which significantly exceed that of punched counterparts (274%) and are close to the reference value (673%), indicating that such inclined interconnection design substantially enhances the stretchability of multilayer systems (Supplementary Video 1). Among all printed samples, the 30° interconnection demonstrates the highest stretchability (646%), even slightly outperforming the sample of 15° design, which is preliminarily identified as the optimal interconnect design (Fig. 3c). The robustness and evolutional behavior of printed inclined interconnections and punched samples are further investigated. As shown in Fig. 3d, cyclic loading-unloading tests at 100% strain reveal that the stress response of the punched interconnect fails after only 37 cycles, while the printed interconnect maintains stable even after 70 cycles, which demonstrates the superior fatigue resistance and dynamic deformation adaptability of the printed inclined design. In situ optical imaging is employed to reveal the failure mechanisms at the interlayer connections. As shown in Fig. 3e, the printed inclined interconnect access maintains seamless connection even at 300% strain, further demonstrating excellent deformation adaptability of such unpunched geometric design. In contrast, the punched connection in Fig. 3f exhibits significant geometric transformation from circular to spindle shape under stretching. Such unwanted distortion is induced by Poisson effect of elastomer, which creates mechanical and electrical failure risks at the interconnections, thus severely limiting the lifespan and applicability of multilayer stretchable circuits.

To elucidate the optimized inclined interconnect accesses, finite element analysis (FEA) is employed to simulate and visualize stress distribution. As illustrated in Fig. 3g, the punched 90° sample exhibits severe stress concentration at the vertical interconnection, showing a tendency toward tensile fracture. Such simulated result is fully consistent with experiments, proving that Poisson effect-induced deformation constitutes the fundamental mechanical constraint in multilayer stretchable electronics. In contrast, all printed samples show substantially mitigated stress concentration at the inclined interconnects without serious deformation and failure risk. Noting that the maximum stress concentration of the 15° sample with an increased interconnect length fraction shifts to the ends of the uniform parts, which further demonstrates the non‑monotonic relationship between the overall stretchability and the angle design. Among all samples, the 30° and 45° configurations achieve optimal balance in stress distribution and structural efficiency, while the 15° sample suffers from excessive interconnection length (more LM material) and the 60°/75° samples develop small cracks. Electrical properties of 3D printed multilayer stretchable electronics

Benefiting from the superior design freedom of 3D printing, a three-layer stretchable circuit with inclined interconnect access is fabricated to determine the electrical properties, with a bilayer punched version as a controlled comparison (Supplementary Fig. 18). As shown in Fig. 4a, when uniaxial stretching is applied to dog-bone samples, the printed interconnection remains mechanical stable up to 250.8% strain, with a small ΔR/R0 of 2.72 (Supplementary Video 2). In contrast, the bilayer punched interconnection fails at only 133.4% strain with substantial resistance shift, demonstrating significantly inferior electrical performance and stretchability compared to the 3D printed sample with inclined interconnection geometry (Supplementary Fig. 19). Such results confirm that, although with a higher failure risk by an additional layer, the three-layer stretchable device with inclined interconnect access effectively dissipate vertical stress through horizontal redistribution and reduce Poisson effect-induced deformation, which brings about enhanced mechanical and electrical performance (Supplementary Fig. 20). In addition, the electromechanical properties under bending and twisting deformations are also evaluated. As illustrated in Fig. 4b and c, ΔR/R0 of the printed interconnect circuits remain highly stable, while the punched connections exhibit abnormal negative values and significant fluctuations (Supplementary Fig. 21 and Video 2). The advantage of 3D-printed inclined interconnect accesses is further demonstrated and visualized by using an LED-integrated circuit with three-layer architecture, incorporating punched connections for comparison (Fig. 4d). Figure 4e shows the practical performance of the punched-interconnection LED circuit under different strains, revealing complete failure at just 80% strain. And a distinct crack is observed at VIA, which has deformed into a spindle shape due to the Poisson effect, creating severe stress concentration. On contrast, as illustrated in Fig. 4f, the LED circuit with printed interconnections remains illuminated during stretching from 0% to 250%. And no apparent damage or deformation is observed at the printed interconnect access, demonstrating stable seamless interlayer integration. The above results confirms that the inclined interconnects significantly mitigate stress concentration and significantly suppress Poisson effect-induced deformation, which would bring about a paradigm for the next-generation flexible electronics.

To further reveal the stability, the electromechanical robustness of the 3D printed connections under 100% stretching and bending is evaluated by cyclic tests. The resistance of the 3D printed connections remains stable after more than 11000 stretching cycles, whereas the punched connections fail within only 5000 cycles, highlighting the superior mechanical durability of the 3D printed device (Fig. 4g). The bending fatigue test in Fig. 4h further demonstrates that the 3D printed connections maintain regular resistance fluctuations (ΔR/R0 < 2%) for over 13200 bending cycles, while the punched connections fail after around 8000 cycles.

Multilayer stretchable wireless & supercapacitor-powered display system

The above results show that our 3D printed strategy brings about enhanced mechanical stretchability and electrical stability, which is promising for the interlayer multifunctional integration in various applications21,23. Figure 5a illustrates a 3D schematic of a three-layer stretchable wireless & supercapacitor-powered display system, which integrates an LED array, a supercapacitor array and a wireless charging coil. As shown in Fig. 5b, Z-axis integration results in higher integration density, which reduces the overall area to 33% compared to a single-layer system with equivalent functionality. The functional three-layer circuit is manufactured using 3D printing, and then a secondary UV-induced in-situ polymerization facilitates the seamless interlayer integration of the stretchable wireless display system. Consequently, such advantages enable the further functional design of the 3D-printed stretchable wireless display system, holding potential applications in status monitoring and feedback of embodied intelligent robot, human-machine interaction, and multifunctional Internet of Things (IoT) tags.

Four-layer wireless conformal physiological monitoring system

Health monitoring has attracted considerable research attention as a cornerstone technology for next-generation flexible electronics49,50. However, achieving comfortable, integrated and stable health monitoring system remains technically challenging due to immature design strategies. To further reveal our 3D printed strategy for wireless and conformal physiological monitoring, a four-layer stretchable electronic device is designed and constructed by integrating commercial temperature-pressure sensors (for real-time monitoring of human throat activities), microcontroller unit (MCU) (for acquiring and processing voltage signals), Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) module (for real-time visualization) and micro-supercapacitors (for power supply) (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 23). The stretchable multilayer circuit board is fabricated through 3D printing strategy, integrating MCU circuit, BLE module, power management, and signal indication (Supplementary Fig. 24). The MCU circuit and power module occupy layers 3–4 while the BLE module is distributed on layers 1–2, and commercial sensors are seamlessly integrated with the multilayer stretchable system through a flexible interconnect. Such four-layer system achieves four times higher integration density than functionally equivalent single-layer system. As shown in Fig. 6d, each layer features well-defined conductive traces and component pads, maintaining excellent flexibility and full compatibility with commercial board layouts (Supplementary Figs. 25 and 26). All functional components can be precisely positioned, and LM-based ink can be printed as antenna for the BLE module, enabling complete device patterning via DIW (Supplementary Fig. 27). To note, distinct inclined interconnect accesses in red dashed circles can be clearly observed (Fig. 6d), which ensure robust mechanical and electrical connections. Figure 6e shows top and bottom optical images of the system, where flexible integrated pressure-temperature sensor is connected to the four-layer circuit alongside commercial components (R, C, LEDs, MCU). The system operates well and maintains full functionality under bending, stretching, and twisting (Fig. 6f-h and Supplementary Video 3). In addition, the thickness of a single layer can be designed as thin as 0.2 mm, which is crucial for conformal electronic system, and such customizable design can be further optimized by employing equipment with higher precision and processing capability (Supplementary Fig. 28). Benefiting from the manufacturing consistency of DLP printing and the assistance of pre‑designed positioning holes, manual layer‑by‑layer fabrication can be precisely achieved, with an interlayer misalignment below 80 µm, which does not significantly affect non‑dense stretchable circuits based on commercial components (Supplementary Fig. 29). For multilayer stretchable electronic designs requiring higher integration and precision, interlayer alignment will be a core challenge, and more advanced alignment assistance techniques need to be explored.

Benefiting from the sensitive and stable response of sensors and the signal processing and transmitting capabilities of four-layer circuit, this stretchable monitoring system can track various throat conditions (Supplementary Fig. 30, 31 and Video 4). As shown in Fig. 6i, the conformal system monitors the entire process of cough-induced stuck in real-time, revealing the relatively stable skin temperature (~33 °C) alongside characteristic pressure signatures that clearly identify coughing, swallowing, and choking events. Figure 6j further shows the temperature and pressure curves recorded during hot water intake, where the typical temperature fluctuations and pressure signals identically distinguish throat movements and abnormalities. Such four-layer wireless conformal physiological monitoring system transmits data stably to the user interface even under external deformations, offer a promising way for future multilayer stretchable electronics in various wearable health-related applications. Furthermore, this wireless and conformal design eliminates physical constraints by cumbersome external connections and enables uninterrupted physiological monitoring during daily activities, demonstrating significant potential in long-term home healthcare, athletic performance tracking, and neonatal monitoring.

Discussion

In summary, we reported a 3D printing strategy for the manufacturing of multilayer stretchable electronics with unpunched inclined interconnect accesses, which significantly promoted the design freedom and accelerated the manufacturing process. With the integration of DLP and DIW, the obtained inclined interconnections not only effectively dissipated vertical stress through horizontal redistribution at the interconnections, but also efficiently reduced Poisson effect-induced deformation under large-scale stretching. As a result, such 3D printed multilayer electronics illustrated both high stretchability (646% strain limit) and excellent stability (over 11000 cycles at 100% strain), outperforming previously reported multilayer stretchable devices. With the 3D printing strategy, LEDs, supercapacitors and a wireless charging coil were well integrated into a three-layer stretchable system, which showed stable performance under large-scale stretching, and a four-layer stretchable physiological monitoring system was also demonstrated. Our 3D printed strategy with inclined interlayer interconnections would provide an innovative manufacturing paradigm for next-generation multilayer stretchable electronics.

Methods

Materials

Butyl acrylate (BA) and isobornyl acrylate (IBOA) were purchased from Adamas-beta. Aliphatic urethane acrylate (AUD) and Diphenyl (2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl) phosphine oxide (TPO) were purchased from Allnex and Sigma-Aldrich, respectively. GaIn alloy was purchased from HuaTai Metal, and it was heated at 60 °C for 10 mins to reduce the surface tension before use. Commercial electronic components employed in the study were not modified unless specified.

Materials preparation for DLP and DIW

The light curable precursor solution for DLP is prepared by mixing IBOA, BA, AUD and TPO (with the ratio of 20:50:30:1) and stirred over 10 h. For DIW printing, the LM droplet solution was prepared by treating 2 g bulk GaIn alloy in 3 mL DMF with ultrasonication at 75% power for 10 minutes. Separately, 1 g TPU powder was added to 4 g DMF and stirred at 90 °C for 5 h to form a transparent solution. Finally, 0.2 g TPU solution was thoroughly mixed with the LM droplet solution in a mortar to obtain the LM-based ink.

3D printing of stretchable multilayer electronics

Firstly, a 3D prototype with preserved micro-channels and connecting slopes was built using SolidWorks software and then exported as STL file into the DLP printer (ASIGA). Prior to printing, the 3D model was sliced at a 0.05 mm layer thickness, with the exposure time, burn-in exposure time, light power density, and printer heater temperature configured to 1.5 s, 1.7 s, 30.45 mW/cm2 and 35 °C, respectively. The printed samples were then detached from the build platform, subjected to ultrasonic washing in 75% ethanol for residual monomer removal, and dried in a 60 °C vacuum oven for 20–40 min.

The conductive traces for DIW were patterned by using a toolpath designed in AutoCAD software. The LM-based ink was loaded into a syringe and extruded in a controlled path by a pressure-assisted system, wherein the nozzle speed and pressure were kept at 2 mm/s and 150 kPa, respectively. And the deposited width ranging from 0.1 mm to 0.96 mm is achieved by adjusting the printing speed. The thermal curing at 60 °C for 1 h and sintering by applying a mechanical pressure using a weight (a 1.25 kg cylinder) or a simple stylus were implemented to ensure the functional stability and conductivity of the printed traces. Commercial electronic components were manually assembled onto designated areas of the elastomeric substrate and gently pressed to bond the components and pins to the substrate and LM-based traces (pads), respectively, ensuring no detachment under gravity or stretching. Finally, the light curable precursor solution was applied around the components as an encapsulation to ensure mechanical and electrical stability during practical operations.

Materials characterization

Scanning electron microscope (SEM, Gemini ZEISS) with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) were carried out to examine the microstructure of materials and interfaces. The rheological properties of all samples were measured using an MCR 302 rotational rheometer (Anton Paar, Graz, Austria) with a diameter of 25 mm and a plate-plate gap of 0.5 mm. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Bruker) was employed to characterize chemical bond changes. In situ or quasi-in situ optical images during stretching were captured using an optical microscope. A standard four‑point probe method was used to measure the electrical conductivity of the LM-based material before and after sintering, with measurements performed four times by three different operators.

Mechanical and electrical tests

A universal tensile testing machine (Instron 3344) was used to measure the mechanical properties of multi-layered flexible electronics. The adhesion test was conducted in a 180° peeling mode on multi-layered substrates and the specimens (20 mm × 15 mm × 1 mm) were peeled at a rate of 100 mm/min. Similarly, the tensile properties of the samples with different interconnection angles (20 mm × 4 mm × 1.25 mm) were evaluated at an identical loading rate of 100 mm/min. For the comparative analysis of the fatigue performance between printed and punched connections, the tests were conducted at a tensile strain of 100% and a loading rate of 1000 mm/min.

The electrical related tests were conducted using a multifunctional testing platform (FlexTest-TM-L) connected with a source meter (2450, Keithley). For the stretching test, the samples with size 20 mm×4 mm × 1.25 mm were stretched with motion speed 5 mm/s, and the fatigue was evaluated by stretching the samples to 100% strain. In bending and twisting tests, the samples were prepared as 50 mm × 4 mm × 1.25 mm for convenient testing. Bending was applied by moving the two ends of sample (length S) closer to reduce the distance L between the endpoints. For LED-related tests, a DC voltage was applied across the LM-based interconnects, and a source meter (Keithley, 2450) together with the software Kickstart are employed to characterize the electromechanical behavior of multi-layered devices.

Fabrication of multilayer stretchable wireless & supercapacitor-powered display system

The first substrate layer was printed by DLP, where LM coil was printed onto the substrate by DIW (positioning repeatability: ±20 µm). All structural layers were designed with unified coordinate benchmarks, integrated positioning holes and fiducial alignment features in SolidWorks software to guarantee the precise coaxial connection of interlayer circuits. Subsequently, the second layer was cured using fixed printing parameters to minimize structural deviation, followed by the printing of interconnection circuits for this layer and implanting the supercapacitor in the reserved cavity. Additionally, the third layer was printed together with the top-level circuit following the pre-calibrated positioning references. Finally, a rectifier and SMD LEDs were mounted, and extra light-curable resin was introduced for overall encapsulation, forming stable and precisely connected cross-layer circuit integration without misalignment.

To wirelessly power the multilayer LED system, a commercial antenna (Shenzhen Xinketai Semiconductor Co., Ltd., 6.5 μH, XKT-L14) was used to transmit an alternating electromagnetic field. The system was activated by applying AC signal of 10 V and 873.15 kHz to the commercial transmitting antenna. The resulting electromagnetic field was inductively coupled to a receiving antenna, enabling wireless power transfer to the display system. The received voltage was monitored using an oscilloscope (TBS 1000 C SERIES, Tektronix). Additionally, a rectifier integrated on the top layer assisted converting the AC power to DC, which was stored in the supercapacitor for sustained operation. To demonstrate mechanical reliability, the whole multilayer system was subjected to bending and tensile stress, demonstrating stable performance under deformation.

Fabrication of four-layer wireless conformal physiological monitoring system

The fabrication process began with preparing the first substrate layer with connecting slopes by DLP, then the conductive traces were deposited on the substrate with interlayer slopes by DIW. The construction of the second, third, and fourth layers was achieved by alternately utilizing DLP for the substrate and DIW for the LM-based conductive ink, prior to the assembly of electronic components on the front and back surfaces with final encapsulation. The interlayer assembly of different circuits is performed in the same manner as that of the multilayer stretchable wireless and supercapacitor‑powered display system.

The system was powered by commercial micro-supercapacitors. The force and temperature signals were recorded by commercial sensing module bought from Luoyang Guantuo Electronic Technology Co., Ltd. To evaluate the performance in capturing physiological signals of the system, the module was attached to the throat of a tester, who was instructed to perform a series of simulated activities, such as speaking, coughing, swallowing, etc. Distinctive pressure and temperature waveforms were successfully recorded during each activity, demonstrating the system’s responsiveness to various physiological motions. All procedures involving the attachment of a four-layer wireless conformal physiological monitoring system to human skin comply with ethical guidelines, which have been approved by the Medical and Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Northwestern Polytechnical University (approval number: YX202602061). A healthy male subject, aged 21 and also a co-author of this study, was recruited to evaluate the wearable performance of the developed multilayer stretchable electronic system. The experimental subject participated voluntarily after providing informed consent, and the collection, processing, and use of the data were conducted with the subject’s consent. The authors affirm that human research participants provided informed consent for publication of the images in Fig. 6b.

Finite element analysis simulation method

The finite element analysis (FEA) was performed by SOLIDWORKS (v.2021). The model was assigned a Young’s modulus of 0.118 MPa and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.176 based on the previous mechanical characterization. To ensure computational accuracy, the 3D model was discretized using an appropriate mesh (global element size: 0.748 mm, tolerance: 0.0374 mm, Jacobian points: 16 unit). After applying the necessary boundary constraints to fix the specimen, specific loads were applied according to different mechanical scenarios: 1 N tensile force, 0.1 N normal pressure and 0.001 N·m torque was applied for stretching, bending and torsional simulations, respectively. These setups replicated realistic loading conditions, allowing for systematic simulation of a variety of mechanical behaviors.
