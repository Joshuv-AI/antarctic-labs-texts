Random coverage of a manifold with boundary
Mathew D. Penrose, Xiaochuan Yang University of Bath and Banque Internationale à Luxembourg S.A.

Abstract
Let A A be a compact d d -dimensional C 2 C^{2} Riemannian manifold with boundary, embedded in ℝ m \mathbb{R}^{m} where m ≥ d ≥ 2 m\geq d\geq 2 , and let B B be a nice subset of A A (possibly B = A B=A ). Let X 1 , X 2 , … X_{1},X_{2},\ldots be independent random uniform points in A A . Define the coverage threshold R n R_{n} to be the smallest r r such that B B is covered by the geodetic balls of radius r r centred on X 1 , … , X n X_{1},\ldots,X_{n} . We obtain the limiting distribution of R n R_{n} and also a strong law of large numbers for R n R_{n} in the large- n n limit. For example, if A A has Riemannian volume 1 and its boundary has surface measure | ∂ A | |\partial A| , and B = A B=A , then if d = 3 d=3 then ℙ [ n π R n 3 − log n − 2 log ( log n ) ≤ x ] \mathbb{P}[n\pi R_{n}^{3}-\log n-2\log(\log n)\leq x] converges to exp ( − 2 − 4 π 5 / 3 | ∂ A | e − 2 x / 3 ) \exp(-2^{-4}\pi^{5/3}|\partial A|e^{-2x/3}) and ( n ​ π ​ R n 3 ) / ( log ⁡ n ) → 1 (n\pi R_{n}^{3})/(\log n)\to 1 almost surely, while if d = 2 d=2 then ℙ [ n π R n 2 − log n − log ( log n ) ≤ x ] \mathbb{P}[n\pi R_{n}^{2}-\log n-\log(\log n)\leq x] converges to exp ( − e − x − | ∂ A | π − 1 / 2 e − x / 2 ) \exp(-e^{-x}-|\partial A|\pi^{-1/2}e^{-x/2}) . We generalize to allow for multiple coverage. For the strong laws of large numbers, we can relax the requirement that the underlying density on A A be uniform. For the limiting distribution, we have a similar result for Poisson samples. Our results still hold if we use Euclidean rather than geodetic balls.

1
Introduction

The
random coverage
problem asks whether
a set of
n
balls in a metric space
A
, with centres
placed independently at random according to
some probability distribution

\mu
on
A
, fully covers a specified set

B\subset A
(possibly
B=A
).
Restricting attention to balls of equal radius, one may ask
what is the minimum radius required to achieve coverage.
This is a random variable (determined by the locations of the centres),
often called the
coverage threshold
, and here denoted
R_{n}
.
The exact distribution of
R_{n}
is often intractable, motivating
the study of large-
n
asymptotics.

An important special case of this problem arises when

\mu
is the uniform distribution on
a compact subset
A
of Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
,
and
the closure of
B
is contained in the interior of
A
.
The large-
n
limiting distribution
for
R_{n}
(suitably scaled and centred)
was derived for this case in
[
10
]
, and also in

[
8
]
. For
d=1
, the exact distribution was
determined much earlier in
[
18
]
.

Another case arises where
\mu
is uniform
on compact
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
as before, but
now
B=A
so boundary effects come in to play.
For this case,
the limiting distribution for
R_{n}
was determined
in
[
15
]
, for the case where
A
has a smooth boundary.
It turns out that

R_{n}
scales differently in this case than in the
previous case.

A third case arises where
A
is a compact
d
-dimensional Riemannian manifold
(without boundary)
and
\mu
is uniform on
A
, with
B=A
.
For this case the limiting distribution for
R_{n}
was also determined
by Janson in
[
10
]
.

It would be desirable to
generalize all of the cases above, by
taking

A
to be a compact
d
-dimensional Riemannian manifold, possibly
with boundary, and
B
a closed subset of
A

(possibly
B=A
)
and with
\mu
uniform on
A
.

In this article we derive the limiting
distribution of
R_{n}
in this general setting, provided
(i)
A
is a submanifold-with-boundary
of a further Riemannian manifold
{\cal M}
(without boundary);
(ii)
{\cal M}
is isometrically embedded
in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{m}
for some
m\geq d
, and (iii)
either
B\cap\partial A
has non-zero surface measure or

B
is bounded away from
\partial A
.

As well as weak convergence results for
R_{n}
, we also
derive a strong law of large numbers (SLLN), exhibiting
a sequence of constants
c_{n}
such that
R_{n}/c_{n}\to 1

as
n\to\infty
, almost surely. For the SLLN we
consider more general probability density functions than
just the uniform on
A
.

Since we take
{\cal M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, there
is a choice of two natural measures of distance on
{\cal M}

(used to define the balls); we can either
use the geodetic distance induced by the Riemannian metric,
or the Euclidean distance in the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
We generally use the Riemannian distance, but will also comment
on the Euclidean distance.
Our results to hold whichever of these two
choices of distance we use.
We write
R_{n}^{*}
to identify the
coverage threshold using balls with the Euclidean rather than
geodetic distance.

While the Euclidean coverage problem is rather classical (see

[
1
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
18
]
),
more recent impetus for investigating
these problems on manifolds comes from the modern
discipline of topological data analysis (TDA).
One goal of TDA is to learn about the topological
structure an underlying space from a random sample
of points in that space. Often the space is a lower-dimensional
submanifold embedded in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. One common
way to create a topological structure from the sample of points
is to place Euclidean balls of radius
r
around the points, and use these
to create a simplicial complex such as the Čech or
Vietoris-Rips complex.
See
[
4
]
for a survey.

Niyogi et al.
[
13
, Proposition 3.1]
have shown
that in the case
A=B={\cal M}
, if

r
is below a certain constant
\tau({\cal M})
but greater than
R_{n}^{*}
,
the homology of the union of balls
equals that of
{\cal M}
; Wang and Wang
[
19
]

extend this to the case where
A=B
is a manifold with boundary.
These results have created a further incentive to understand the probabilistic
behaviour of
R_{n}
and
R_{n}^{*}
. Motivated by applications to
TDA, Bobrowski and Weinberger
[
3
,
5
]

have derived (among other things)
results on the coverage threshold
for manifolds without boundary, that are similar
to those of Janson
[
10
]
, while Kergorlay et al.

[
11
]
derive a weaker result
for manifolds with boundary (we discuss this further later on).

2
Statement of results

2.1
Mathematical framework

To be able to state the results of this paper,
we need to elaborate on the mathematical setup which was sketched
in the introduction.
We shall review definitions of concepts relating to manifolds
in Section
2.4
.

Let
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
with
d\leq m
. Suppose

{\cal M}
is a Riemannian manifold (without boundary)
of dimension
d
,
and moreover that
{\cal M}
is isometrically embedded in the Euclidean
space
\mathbb{R}^{m}
; more precisely, we shall assume

{\cal M}
is a
C^{2}
- submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Suppose
A
is a compact submanifold-with-boundary
of
{\cal M}
.
Let
B\subset A
(often we shall take
B=A
).
Assume
B
is closed (and hence compact).

Let
f
be a probability density function on
{\cal M}
, with respect
to the Riemannian volume measure; we assume throughout that

f
is supported by
A
. Let
\mu
be the measure on

A
with density
f
.
Suppose on some probability space
(\Omega,{\cal F},\mathbb{P})
that

X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
are independent, identically distributed
random elements of
A
with common distribution
\mu
.
For
x\in{\cal M}
and
r>0
let
B(x,r):=\{y\in{\cal M}:{\rm dist}(x,y)\leq r\}
,
where

{\rm dist}(x,y)
is the geodetic distance.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{X}_{n}:=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
.
Given also
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we
define the
k
-coverage threshold

R_{n,k}
by

\displaystyle R_{n,k}:=\inf\left\{r>0:\#(\mathcal{X}_{n}\cap B(x,r))\geq k~~~~\forall x\in B\right\},~~~n,k\in\mathbb{N},

(2.1)

where for any point set
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}

we let
\#(\mathcal{X})
denote the number of points of

\mathcal{X}
,
and we use the convention
\inf\{\}:=+\infty
.
In particular
R_{n}:=R_{n,1}
is the coverage threshold. Observe that

R_{n}=\inf\{r>0:B\subset\cup_{i=1}^{n}B(X_{i},r)\}
.
In the case
B=A
,
R_{n}
is the Hausdorff distance between the
sample
\mathcal{X}_{n}
and the region
A
.

Thus,
R_{n,k}
is a random variable, whose value is determined
by
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
. We investigate its probabilistic behaviour
as
n
becomes large. In particular, we would like to determine
the limiting behaviour of
\mathbb{P}[R_{n,k}\leq r_{n}]

for any fixed
k
and any sequence of numbers
(r_{n})
. Equivalently,
we are concerned with the limiting distribution of
R_{n,k}
, suitably
scaled and centred, as
n\to\infty
with
k
fixed.

We also consider similar results for a high-intensity
Poisson

sample in
A
, which may be more relevant in some applications.
Let
(Z_{t},t\geq 0)
be a unit rate Poisson counting
process, independent of
(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots)

and on the same probability space

(\Omega,{\cal F},\mathbb{P})

(so
Z_{t}
is Poisson distributed with mean
t
for each
t>0
).
The point process
{\cal P}_{t}:=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{Z_{t}}\}
is
a Poisson point process in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with intensity measure
t\mu

(see e.g.
[
12
]
).
For
t\in(0,\infty),k\in\mathbb{N}
we define a secondary
k
-coverage threshold

\displaystyle R^{\prime}_{t,k}:=R_{Z_{t},k}:=\inf\left\{r>0:\#({\cal P}_{t}\cap B(x,r))\geq k~~~~\forall x\in B\right\},~~~t>0,

(2.2)

with
R^{\prime}_{t}:=R^{\prime}_{t,1}
.

We mention further notation used throughout. For

D\subset{\cal M}
, let
\overline{D}

denote the closure of
D

and let
D^{o}:={\cal M}\setminus(\overline{{\cal M}\setminus D})
,
the interior of
D
with respect to
{\cal M}
.
Let
\partial D:=\overline{D}\setminus D^{o}
,
the boundary of
D

and let
\partial_{\partial A}(D\cap\partial A):=\overline{D\cap\partial A}\cap\overline{\partial A\setminus D}
, the boundary
of
D\cap\partial A
relative to
\partial A
.
Let
v(D)
denote the Riemannian volume of
D
, when defined, and
if
D\subset\partial A
,
let
\tilde{{v}}(D)
denote the Riemannian surface measure of

D
(see Section
2.4

for further details).
Given
t>1
, we write
\log\log t
for
\log(\log t)
.
Given two sets
\mathcal{X},{\cal Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we
write
\mathcal{X}\oplus{\cal Y}
for the set
\{x+y:x\in\mathcal{X},y\in{\cal Y}\}
.
Given
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote by
[x,y]
the line segment from

x
to
y
, that is, the convex hull of the set
\{x,y\}
.
Given
d\in\mathbb{N}
, define the constant

\displaystyle c_{d}:=\frac{1}{d!}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\;\Gamma(1+d/2)}{\Gamma((d+1)/2)}\right)^{d-1}.

(2.3)

Note that
c_{1}=c_{2}=1
, and
c_{3}=3\pi^{2}/32
.
Set
\theta_{d}:=\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(1+d/2)
,
the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
\theta_{0}:=1
.
Given also
k\in\mathbb{N}
, for
d\geq 2
set

\displaystyle c_{d,k}:=\left(\frac{c_{d-1}}{(k-1)!}\right)\theta_{d}^{2-d-1/d}\theta_{d-1}^{2d-3}\theta_{d-2}^{1-d}(1-1/d)^{d+k-3+1/d}2^{-1+1/d}.

(2.4)

Note that
c_{2,k}=2^{1-k}\pi^{-1/2}/(k-1)!
and
c_{3,k}=2^{k-5}3^{1-k}\pi^{5/3}/(k-1)!
.
In all of our limiting statements,

n
takes values in
\mathbb{N}
while
t
takes values in
(0,\infty)
.

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we write
[n]
for
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Sometimes, given
a\in(0,1)
and

b>0
, we shall write
(1\pm a)b
for the
interval
[b-ab,b+ab]
.

2.2
Main results

From now on, we always assume
d\geq 2
.
Our first result is a weak limit for the coverage threshold
of a manifold
A
with boundary or subset thereof, when the
centres are uniformly distributed on
A
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Set
f_{0}:=1/v(A)
and assume
f\equiv f_{0}
on
A
.
Assume

v(\partial B)=0
and
\tilde{{v}}(\partial_{\partial A}(B\cap\partial A))=0
.
Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\left(\frac{n\theta_{d}f_{0}R_{n,k}^{d}}{2}\right)-\left(\frac{d-1}{d}\right)\log(nf_{0})-\left(d+k-3+1/d\right)\log\log n\leq\zeta\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\left(\frac{t\theta_{d}f_{0}(R^{\prime}_{t,k})^{d}}{2}\right)-\left(\frac{d-1}{d}\right)\log(tf_{0})-\left(d+k-3+1/d\right)\log\log t\leq\zeta\right]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\exp\left(-v(B)e^{-2\zeta}-c_{2,1}\tilde{{v}}(B\cap\partial A)e^{-\zeta}\right)&{\rm if}~d=2,k=1\\
\exp\left(-c_{d,k}\tilde{{v}}(B\cap\partial A)e^{-\zeta}\right)&{\rm otherwise.}\end{cases}~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

(2.5)

Remark 2.2
.

Theorem
2.1

was previously proved in

[
15
, Theorem 2.1]
for the special case
where
A
is a smoothly bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
i.e. when
{\cal M}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
with the Euclidean inner product as Riemannian metric,
and where moreover
B=A
.
Even in this setting, our result generalises that one by allowing
for more general
B
.

Remark 2.3
.

When
d=2,k=1
and
B=A
,
the exponent in (
2.5
) has two terms.
This is because
the location in
A
furthest from the sample
\mathcal{X}_{n}
might lie
either in the interior of
A
, or on the boundary.
If
d>2
or
k>1
then for large
n
, the location in
A

furthest from
\mathcal{X}_{n}
is very likely to lie near the boundary of
A
.

Remark 2.4
.

When
d>2
or
k>1
,
and
\tilde{{v}}(B\cap\partial A)=0
, the limit in
(
2.5
) equals 1, so in this case taking

\frac{1}{2}n\theta f_{0}R_{n,k}^{d}-(1-1/d)\log n-(d+k-3+1/d)\log\log n+{\rm const.}

is the ‘wrong’ transformation of
R_{n,k}

to yield a sequence of random
variables converging in distribution to
a non-degenerate random variable. In
the case where
\overline{B}\subset A^{o}
,
the ‘right’ transformation is identified in
Proposition
2.11
below, but in intermediate
cases where
\tilde{{v}}(B\cap\partial A)=0

but
B
touches
\partial A
, finding the right sequence
of transformations remains an open problem.

Remark 2.5
.

One possible direction of future research would be to give a more
quantitative version of Theorem
2.1
, specifically a
bound on the rate of convergence (for either choice
of pre-limit) in (
2.5
). In
[
16
]

we make a start in this direction by providing
an
O((\log\log t)/\log t)
rate of convergence for
convergence of the second pre-limit in (
2.16
)
below,
in the special case where
{\cal M}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Our next result provides
a SLLN for
R_{n,k}
. For this,
we relax the condition that the density function

f
be constant on
A
.
Moreover, we allow
k
to vary with
n
.
Assume we are given
a constant
\beta\in[0,\infty]

and a sequence
k:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
with

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(k(n)/\log n\right)=\beta;~~~~~\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(k(n)/n\right)=0.

(2.6)

We make use of the following notation throughout:

\displaystyle f_{0}:=\inf_{x\in B}f(x);~~~~~~~f_{1}:=\inf_{x\in B\cap\partial A}f(x);

(2.7)

\displaystyle H(t):=\begin{cases}1-t+t\log t,~~~&{\rm if}~t>0\\
1,&{\rm if}~t=0.\end{cases}

(2.8)

If
B\cap\partial A=\varnothing
we interpret
f_{1}
as
+\infty

and
1/f_{1}
as 0.
Observe that
-H(\cdot)
is unimodal with a maximum
value of
0
at
t=1
. Given
a\in[0,\infty)
, we define
the
function
\hat{H}_{a}:[0,\infty)\to[a,\infty)
by

\displaystyle y=\hat{H}_{a}(x)\Longleftrightarrow yH(a/y)=x,~y\geq a,

(2.9)

with
\hat{H}_{0}(0):=0
.
Note that

\hat{H}_{a}(x)
is increasing in
x
,
and that

\hat{H}_{0}(x)=x
.

Throughout this paper, the phrase ‘almost surely’ or
‘a.s.’ means ‘except on a set of
\mathbb{P}
-measure zero’.
Let
B_{A}^{o}:=B\setminus(\overline{A\setminus B})
,
the interior of
B
relative to
A
, and
let
f|_{A}
denote the restriction of
f
to
A
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Suppose
that
f_{0}>0
, and that
f|_{A}
is continuous on
A
.
Assume also that
B=\overline{B^{o}}
, and

B\cap\partial A=\overline{B_{A}^{o}\cap\partial A}

with
\int_{B}fdv>0
,
and that (
2.6
) holds.
Then as
n\to\infty
, almost surely

\displaystyle(n\theta_{d}R_{n,k(n)}^{d})/k(n)\to\max\left(1/f_{0},2/f_{1}\right)~~~~~{\rm if}~\beta=\infty;

(2.10)

\displaystyle(n\theta_{d}R_{n,k(n)}^{d})/\log n\to\max\left(\hat{H}_{\beta}(1)/f_{0},2\hat{H}_{\beta}(1-1/d)/f_{1}\right),~~~~~{\rm if}~\beta<\infty.

(2.11)

In particular, if
k\in\mathbb{N}
is a constant, then
as
n\to\infty
, almost surely

\displaystyle(n\theta_{d}R_{n,k}^{d})/\log n\to\max\left(1/f_{0},(2-2/d)/f_{1}\right).

(2.12)

In the special case where

\mu
is the uniform distribution on

A
(i.e.
f(x)\equiv f_{0}
on
A
),
the right hand
side of (
2.12
) comes to
(2-2/d)/f_{0}
.

Remark 2.7
.

We can compare the above result with
[
11
, Corollary 4.2]
.
Translated into our notation, the latter result says
that when
k(n)\equiv 1
(so
\beta=0
)
and
f\equiv 1
, then

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\left(t\theta(R^{\prime}_{t})^{d}/\log t\right)\leq 2^{d}
, in probability.

Also related are results of Chai
[
6
]
, which show
limiting behaviour for the expected number of balls of radius
r

needed to cover
A
, and allow for other singularities of

\partial A
rather than just considering smooth boundaries.

Remark 2.8
.

Since we are considering a submanifold
{\cal M}
of

\mathbb{R}^{m}
, a natural alternative,
instead of the geodetic distance in
{\cal M}
, is
to consider the Euclidean distance in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m},r\geq 0
let
B_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(x,r)
denote
the closed Euclidean ball of radius
r
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
centred on
x
.
Then define
R^{*}_{n,k}

like
R_{n,k}
at (
2.1
) but using
B_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(x,r)

instead of the geodetic ball
B(x,r)
.

Theorems
2.1

and
2.6
still hold with
R^{*}_{n,k}
instead of
R_{n,k}

and
R^{*}_{Z_{t},k}
instead of
R^{\prime}_{t,k}
.
We shall justify this assertion in
Remark
3.4
.

Remark 2.9
.

We conjecture that Theorems
2.1

and
2.6
still hold if we assume only
that
A
is a compact Riemannian manifold-with-boundary,
of dimension
d\geq 2
and Riemannian volume
v(A)\in(0,\infty)
,
dropping the assumption that
A
is a submanifold of
a further manifold
{\cal M}
that is isometrically embedded
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Remark 2.10
.

When
B=A
, with some extra work one can
relax the condition ‘
f|_{A}
is continuous on
A
’
to ‘
f|_{A}
is continuous at all points of
\partial A
’
in Theorem
2.6
, provided we define
f_{0}
as
an essential infimum.

2.3
Coverage in the interior region

Given
r>0
, we define the
‘
r
-interior’ of
A
by

A^{(r)}:=\{x\in D:B(x,r)\subset A^{o}\},

i.e.
the set of points in
D
at geodetic distance
(strictly) more than
r
from
{\cal M}\setminus A
.

Given closed
B\subset A

as before,
let
\tilde{R}_{n,k}
be the smallest
r
such that
B\cap A^{(r)}

is covered
k
times by the balls of radius
r
centred on the points of
\mathcal{X}_{n}
, i.e. set

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{n,k}:=\inf\left\{r>0:\#(\mathcal{X}_{n}\cap B(x,r))\geq k~~~\forall x\in B\cap A^{(r)}\right\},~~~n,k\in\mathbb{N},

(2.13)

with
{\tilde{R}}_{n}:={\tilde{R}}_{n,1}
. Also, for
t>0
define

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{Z_{t},k}:=\inf\left\{r>0:\#({\cal P}_{t}\cap B(x,r))\geq k~~~\forall x\in B\cap A^{(r)}\right\},~~~n,k\in\mathbb{N}.

(2.14)

In Proposition
2.11
,
we give the limiting distribution for
{\tilde{R}}_{n,k}
.
This is simpler than Theorem
2.1

because boundary effects are avoided. We shall be
using this result to prove Theorem
2.1
.

Proposition 2.11
.

Suppose
f(x)=f_{0}=1/v(A)
for all
x\in A
, and

v(\partial B)=0
. Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then

2.4
Manifolds

Our results refer to a number of concepts pertaining to manifolds,
and we review these now. We mainly follow the approach of

[
17
]
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\|\cdot\|
be the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
Let
o
denote the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
d\in\mathbb{N}
with
d\leq m
.
We write
\lambda_{d}

for Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\lambda_{d-1}

for
(d-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure.
Let
\mathbb{H}
denote the half-space
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times[0,\infty)
,
and set
\partial\mathbb{H}:=\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times\{0\}
.

A nonempty subset
{\cal M}
of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
endowed with the subset topology,
is called a
d
-dimensional
C^{2}

submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
if for each
y\in{\cal M}
there exists an open
subset
U
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a twice continuously differentiable
injection
g
from

U
to
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, such that (i)
y\in g(U)\subset{\cal M}
,
and (ii)
g
is an open map from
U
to
{\cal M}
, and
(iii) the linear map
g^{\prime}(u)
has full rank for all
u\in U
(see
e.g. Theorem 2.1.2(v) of
[
2
]
). The pair
(U,g)
is called a

chart
. Let
{{\bf M}}:={\bf M}(d,m)
denote the class of all

d
-dimensional
C^{2}
submanifolds of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
which are also closed
subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Given
{\cal M}\in{\bf M}
, using a routine compactness argument we can
choose an index set
\mathcal{I}\subset\mathbb{N}
,
and a set

\{(y_{i},\delta_{i},U_{i},g_{i}),i\in{\cal I}\}
of ordered quadruples
with
y_{i}\in{\cal M}
,
\delta_{i}\in(0,\infty)
, and

(U_{i},g_{i})
a chart for each
i
, such that
(i)
{\cal M}\cap B_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(y_{i},3\delta_{i})\subset g_{y_{i}}(U_{i})
for each

i
, and (ii)
{\cal M}\subset\bigcup_{i\in{\cal I}}B_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(y_{i},\delta_{i})
.

We refer to
((U_{i},g_{i}),i\in{\cal I})
as an
atlas
for

{\cal M}
. Given such an atlas, we can find a
partition of unity

\{\psi_{i}\}
subordinate to the atlas, that is, a collection of
functions
(\psi_{i},i\in{\cal I})
from
{\cal M}
to
[0,1]
, such that

\sum_{i\in{\cal I}}\psi_{i}(y)=1
for all
y\in{\cal M}
, and such
that for each
i
,
\psi_{i}(y)=0
for
y\notin g_{i}(U_{i})
, and

\psi_{i}\circ g_{i}
is a measurable function on
U_{i}
.
The more common definition of a partition of unity has some extra
differentiability conditions on
\psi_{i}
but these are not needed
here. With our more relaxed definition, the existence of a partition
of unity is completely elementary to prove.

Given
i\in{\cal I}
and
x\in U_{i}
, let
D_{g_{i}}(x):=\sqrt{\det(J_{g_{i},x}^{T}J_{g_{i},x})}
, with
J_{g_{i},x}
standing for the
Jacobian of
{g_{i}}
, evaluated at
x
.
For bounded measurable
h:{\cal M}\to\mathbb{R}
, the integral
\int_{{\cal M}}h(y)dy
is defined by

\int_{{\cal M}}h(y)dy=\sum_{i\in{\cal I}}\int_{U_{i}}h(g_{i}(x))\psi_{i}(g_{i}(x))D_{g_{i}}(x)\lambda_{d}(dx)

(2.20)

which is
well-defined in the sense that it does not depend on the choice of
atlas or the partition of unity.
Also, if
h={\bf 1}_{D}
for some set
D\subset{\cal M}
, then set
v(D):=\int_{{\cal M}}h(y)dy
, the
Riemannian volume of
D
.

Here is further justification of Equation (
2.20
).
For bounded measurable
A\subset U_{i}
,
and
x\in U_{i}
,
it is a fact from linear algebra
that

\displaystyle\lambda_{d}(g^{\prime}_{i}(x)(A))=D_{g_{i}}(x)\lambda_{d}(A).

(2.21)

Indeed, the columns of
the Jacobian matrix
J_{g_{i},x}
are the images under
g^{\prime}(x)
of
the standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
so (
2.21
)
clearly holds when the standard basis vectors map to an orthonormal
system, but then it can be deduced in the general case using
standard properties of determinants. Equation (
2.21
) is the
basis of the formula (
2.20
).

Given any manifold
{\cal M}\in{\bf M}
and
A\subset{\cal M}
,
recall our notation
A^{o}:={\cal M}\setminus(\overline{{\cal M}\setminus A})

and
\partial A:=\overline{A}\setminus A^{o}
.
Also, set

{\rm diam}(A):=\sup\{\|x-y\|:\ x,y\in A\}
.
We say

A
is a
d
-dimensional
C^{2}

submanifold-with-boundary
of
{\cal M}
if for all
y\in\partial A
,
there exists a choice of chart
(U,g)
for
{\cal M}

such that
o\in U
, and
g(o)=y
, and
g(U\cap\mathbb{H})=g(U)\cap A
,
where we set
\mathbb{H}:=\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times[0,\infty)
.
This includes the possibility that
\partial A=\varnothing

(but if
\partial A\neq\varnothing
then it implies
A
is closed).

For such
A
, we claim
\partial A
is a
(d-1)
-dimensional
submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Indeed, let
y\in\partial A
and
take a chart
(U,g)
with
o\in U
,
g(o)=y
and

g(U\cap\mathbb{H})=g(U)\cap A
. Set
U^{\prime}:=U\cap\partial\mathbb{H}
.
This is an open set in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
(where we identify
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}

with
\partial\mathbb{H}
). Let
\tilde{g}
denote the restriction of

g
to
U^{\prime}
. Then
\tilde{g}
is continuously differentiable
on
U^{\prime}
(since
g
is continuously differentiable on
U
),
and
y=g(o)\in\tilde{g}(U^{\prime})\subset\partial A
. Also,
if
W\subset U^{\prime}
is open, then
W^{\prime}:=W\cup(U\setminus U^{\prime})

is open in
U
, so
g(W^{\prime})
is open in
{\cal M}
,
so that
g(W)=g(W^{\prime})\cap\partial A
is open in
\partial A
;
hence,
\tilde{g}
is an open map from
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
to
\partial A
.
Finally, for all
u\in U^{\prime}
, since
g^{\prime}(u)
has full rank,
the
m\times d
matrix
(\frac{\partial\tilde{g}_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}|_{u})

has rank
d
, and therefore the same matrix with the last
column removed must have rank
d-1
, i.e.
\tilde{g}^{\prime}(u)
has full rank.

By the preceding claim, we can define surface integrals over
\partial A

analogously to the earlier definition given at (
2.20
).
In particular, for measurable
E\subset\partial A

the
surface measure
\tilde{{v}}(E)
is given by

\tilde{{v}}(E)=\sum_{i\in{\cal I}}\int_{U_{i}\cap\partial\mathbb{H}}{\bf 1}_{E}(g_{i}(x))\psi_{i}(g_{i}(x)){\tilde{D}}_{g_{i}}(x)\lambda_{d-1}(dx),

(2.22)

where
\lambda_{d-1}
is
is
(d-1)
-dimensional Lebesgue measure,
and where, for
x\in U_{i}\cap\partial\mathbb{H}
,
we set
{\tilde{D}}_{g_{i}}(x)=\sqrt{\det({\tilde{J}}_{g_{i},x}^{T}{\tilde{J}}_{g_{i},x})}
, with
{\tilde{J}}_{g_{i},x}
standing for
the
m\times(d-1)
matrix obtained by removing
the last column from
J_{g_{i},x}
.

Given
{\cal M}\in{\bf M}
, a
probability density function

on
{\cal M}
is
a non-negative scalar field

f
on
{\cal M}

satisfying

\int_{{\cal M}}f(y)dy=1
.

Remark 2.13
.

Theorems
2.1
and
2.6

generalize results from
[
15
]

(Theorems 2.1 and 3.1 there), which
are concerned with the
case where
{\cal M}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

A
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a
C^{2}
boundary.
It can be shown using the proof of

[
15
, Lemma 6.7]
that in this case
A
is indeed
a submanifold-with-boundary of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

3
Approximating the manifold by Euclidean space

In this section we write
\lambda_{d}
for
d
-dimensional
Lebesgue measure, either on the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
or on any
d
-dimensional subspace of a higher-dimensional
Euclidean space. We let
v_{d}
denote
d
-dimensional Hausdorff
measure, and
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}:=\{e\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|e\|=1\}
,
the
(d-1)
-sphere.

Let
I_{d}
denote the identity map from

\mathbb{R}^{d}
to itself, and let
\|\cdot\|_{d\times d}

be the operator norm on
linear
maps from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(with the Euclidean norm) to itself.
Denote the unit coordinate vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}
.

To prove
Theorems

2.1
and
2.6
,
we need to quantify the idea that a
d
-dimensional
manifold can be approximated locally by Euclidean
d
-space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The next result is key to doing this.

Lemma 3.1

(Comparing manifold with Euclidean space)
.

Let
x_{0}\in{\cal M}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
.
Then there exists an open set
V\subset{\cal M}
,
with
x_{0}\in V
, a constant
K_{1}\in(0,\infty)
,
and for each
{\tilde{x}}\in V
an injective open map

\phi_{\tilde{x}}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with

\phi_{{\tilde{x}}}(x_{0})=o

such that:

(I)
If
{\tilde{x}}\in V\cap\partial A
then

\phi_{\tilde{x}}(V\cap A)=\phi_{\tilde{x}}(V)\cap\mathbb{H}
.

(II) The mapping
\phi_{\tilde{x}}\circ\phi_{x_{0}}^{-1}:\phi_{x_{0}}(V)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

extends to a linear mapping of of full rank (also
denoted
\phi_{\tilde{x}}\circ\phi_{x_{0}}^{-1}
) from

\mathbb{R}^{d}

to itself, such that

\|\phi_{\tilde{x}}\circ\phi_{x_{0}}^{-1}-I_{d}\|_{d\times d}<\varepsilon

and

\phi_{{\tilde{x}}}\circ\phi_{x_{0}}^{-1}(\mathbb{H})=\mathbb{H}
.

(III) For all measurable
F\subset V
and
{\tilde{x}}\in F
,

\left|\frac{\lambda_{d}(\phi_{\tilde{x}}(F))}{v(F)}-1\right|\leq K_{1}{\rm diam}(F).

(IV) If
x_{0}\in\partial A
then
for all measurable
F\subset V\cap\partial A
and
{\tilde{x}}\in F
,

\left|\frac{\lambda_{d-1}(\phi_{\tilde{x}}(F))}{\tilde{{v}}(F)}-1\right|\leq K_{1}{\rm diam}(F).

(VI) For all
{\tilde{x}},y,z\in V
, we have

\left|\frac{\|\phi_{\tilde{x}}(y)-\phi_{\tilde{x}}(z)\|}{{\rm dist}(y,z)}-1\right|\leq K_{1}({\rm dist}({\tilde{x}},y)+{\rm dist}(y,z)).

(VII)
We have

|(\|\phi_{\tilde{x}}(y)-\phi_{\tilde{x}}(z)\|/{\rm dist}(y,z))-1|\leq\varepsilon

for all distinct
y,z\in V

and all
{\tilde{x}}\in V
.

Remark 3.2
.

Since we take
\varepsilon\leq 1/2
, and

(1+x)^{-1}\geq 1-x
and
(1-x)^{-1}\leq 1+2x

for all
x\in[0,1/2]
, we have from (VII)
a similar bound for the ratio the other way, namely

|({\rm dist}(y,z)/\|\phi_{\tilde{x}}(y)-\phi_{\tilde{x}}(z)\|)-1|\leq 2\varepsilon
;
likewise for other ratios such as those in (III) and (IV) when

{\rm diam}(F)
is sufficiently small.
We shall use this fact from time to time without further comment.

Proof.

Let
(U,g)
be a chart with

x_{0}\in g(U)=:V
, so
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
g:U\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

is
C^{2}
. Without loss of generality, we can and do assume
that
o\in U
and
g(o)=x_{0}
, and if
x_{0}\in\partial A
, that

g(U\cap\mathbb{H})=V\cap A
.
Assume moreover that
U
is convex.

Given
{\tilde{x}}\in V
, set
{\tilde{u}}=g^{-1}({\tilde{x}})
and
let
\psi_{{\tilde{x}}}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be given by

\psi_{\tilde{x}}:=g^{\prime}({\tilde{u}})\circ g^{-1}
; note that
the matrix representation of the linear map

g^{\prime}({\tilde{u}})
is the Jacobian matrix
J_{g,{\tilde{u}}}
in
the notation of Section
2.4
.
Let
\rho_{\tilde{x}}

be a linear isometry
from
\phi^{\prime}({\tilde{u}})(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, to be specified below.
Let
\phi_{{\tilde{x}}}:=\rho_{{\tilde{x}}}\circ\psi_{{\tilde{x}}}
.
Then
\phi_{{\tilde{x}}}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an open map. Also

\phi_{x_{0}}=\rho_{x_{0}}\circ g^{\prime}(o)\circ g^{-1}
.
Note
g^{\prime}(o):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is linear and of full rank.
Thus
\psi_{x_{0}}(V)=g^{\prime}(o)(U)
is part of the
d
-dimensional
subspace
g^{\prime}(o)(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

We now specify the linear isometry

\rho_{{\tilde{x}}}

explicitly, for each
{\tilde{x}}
in
V
,
using a Gram-Schmidt type procedure.
Set
L_{\tilde{u}}:=g^{\prime}({\tilde{u}})

(where
{\tilde{u}}=g^{-1}({\tilde{x}})\in U
). Then

L_{{\tilde{u}}}
is a linear
map of full rank from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Set
v_{1}=L_{\tilde{u}}(e_{1})
, set

w_{1}=\|v_{1}\|^{-1}v_{1}
, and
set
\rho_{{\tilde{x}}}(w_{1})=e_{1}
.

Set
v_{2}=L_{\tilde{u}}(e_{2})-\langle L_{\tilde{u}}(e_{2}),w_{1}\rangle w_{1}
,
set
w_{2}=\|v_{2}\|^{-1}v_{2}
and
set
\rho_{{\tilde{x}}}(w_{2})=e_{2}
.

Continuing in this way if
d\geq 3
, for
3\leq j\leq d
set

v_{j}=L_{\tilde{u}}(e_{j})-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\langle L_{\tilde{u}}(e_{j}),w_{i}\rangle w_{i}
,
then set
w_{j}=\|v_{j}\|^{-1}v_{j}
and
\rho_{{\tilde{x}}}(w_{j})=e_{j}
.
Since
L_{\tilde{u}}
has full rank, we have
v_{j}\neq o
for all
j
.

Extend
\rho_{{\tilde{x}}}
to all of
L_{\tilde{u}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by linearity.
Since
w_{1},\ldots,w_{d}
are orthonormal and
mapped to orthonormal vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\rho_{\tilde{x}}
is an isometry.
Then the matrix representation of

\rho_{g({\tilde{u}})}\circ L_{\tilde{u}}
,
with
(i,j)
entry given by

\langle\rho_{g({\tilde{u}})}\circ L_{\tilde{u}}(e_{i}),e_{j}\rangle
for
each
i,j\in[d]
,
has entries which depend continuously on
{\tilde{u}}
.
It is a lower triangular matrix with diagonal entries
\|v_{1}\|,\ldots,\|v_{d}\|
.

Since its matrix representation
is lower triangular with positive diagonal entries,

\rho_{g({\tilde{u}})}\circ L_{\tilde{u}}
maps
\mathbb{H}
to itself,
that is,
\rho_{\tilde{x}}\circ L_{\tilde{u}}(\mathbb{H})=\mathbb{H}
.
Since
g(U\cap\mathbb{H})=V\cap A
, if
y\in V\cap A
then

g^{-1}(y)\in\mathbb{H}
and hence

\phi_{\tilde{x}}(y)=\rho_{{\tilde{x}}}\circ L_{\tilde{u}}(g^{-1}(y))\in\mathbb{H}.

Conversely, if
z\in V\setminus A
then
g^{-1}(z)\notin\mathbb{H}
,
so
\phi_{\tilde{x}}(z)\notin\mathbb{H}
. This yields part (I).

By definition we have
\phi_{x_{0}}=\rho_{x_{0}}\circ g^{\prime}(o)\circ g^{-1}

and
\phi_{{\tilde{x}}}=\rho_{{\tilde{x}}}\circ g^{\prime}({\tilde{u}})\circ g^{-1}
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\phi_{{\tilde{x}}}\circ\phi_{x_{0}}^{-1}=\rho_{{\tilde{x}}}\circ g^{\prime}({\tilde{u}})\circ(\rho_{x_{0}}\circ g^{\prime}(o))^{-1}.

(3.1)

This is a composition of linear maps
(defined on
\rho_{x_{0}}\circ g^{\prime}(o)(U)
),
and therefore extends to
a linear map defined on the whole of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(and also
denoted
\phi_{\tilde{x}}\circ\phi^{-1}
).

Note
g(o)=x_{0}
.
By the continuity in
{\tilde{u}}

of
\rho_{g({\tilde{u}})}\circ L_{\tilde{u}}
, the matrix

(\rho_{g({\tilde{u}})}\circ L_{\tilde{u}})\circ(\rho_{g(o)}\circ L_{o})^{-1}

is close to the identity matrix
I_{d}
, so all of
the entries of

(\rho_{g({\tilde{u}})}\circ L_{\tilde{u}})\circ(\rho_{x_{0}}\circ L_{o})^{-1}-I_{d}

can be taken to be close to zero, by taking
U
small
enough. Hence by (
3.1
), by taking
U
to
be small enough we can arrange
that
\phi_{{\tilde{x}}}\circ\phi_{x_{0}}^{-1}
is close
to the identity for all
{\tilde{x}}\in U
,
which yields part (II).

Given measurable
F\subset V
and
{\tilde{x}}\in F
,
we have
v(F)=\int_{g^{-1}(F)}D_{g}(w)dw
by (
2.20
).
Also setting
{\tilde{u}}=\phi^{-1}({\tilde{x}})
,
we have that

\lambda_{d}(\phi_{{\tilde{x}}}(F))=\lambda_{d}(\psi_{{\tilde{x}}}(F))=\int_{g^{-1}(F)}D_{g}({\tilde{u}})dw
,
and

\sup\left\{\left|\left(\frac{D_{g}(w)}{D_{g}({\tilde{u}})}\right)-1\right|:w\in g^{-1}(F)\right\}=O({\rm diam}(F)),

because
D_{g}(w)
is continuously differentiable in
w
on
the convex set
U
, so by the Mean Value theorem

w\mapsto D_{g}(w)
is
Lipschitz on
U
, and is bounded away from zero. Thus,

|(\lambda_{d}(\phi_{{\tilde{x}}}(F))/v(F))-1|<K{\rm diam}(F)
.
This yields part (III).

Now suppose
F\subset\partial A\cap V
and
{\tilde{x}}\in F
;
again set
{\tilde{u}}=g^{-1}({\tilde{x}})
. Then
by (
2.22
),

\tilde{{v}}(F)=\int_{g^{-1}(F)}{\tilde{D}}_{g}(w)\lambda_{d-1}(dw)
.
Also

\lambda_{d-1}(\phi_{{\tilde{x}}}(F))=\lambda_{d-1}(\psi_{{\tilde{x}}}(F))=\int_{g^{-1}(F)}{\tilde{D}}_{g}({\tilde{u}})\lambda_{d-1}(dw)
,
and

\sup\left\{\left|\left(\frac{{\tilde{D}}_{g}(w)}{{\tilde{D}}_{g}({\tilde{u}})}\right)-1\right|:w\in g^{-1}(F)\right\}=O({\rm diam}(F)),

because
{\tilde{D}}_{g}(w)
is continuously differentiable and hence
Lipschitz on
U\cap\partial\mathbb{H}
, and is bounded away from zero,
so that

|(\lambda_{d-1}(\phi_{{\tilde{x}}}(F))/\tilde{{v}}(F))-1|=O({\rm diam}(F))
.
This yields part (IV).

Now suppose
\gamma
is a path in
U
, i.e. a
continuously differentiable function from
[0,1]
to
U
.
Then
g(\gamma)
is a path
in
V
.
Writing
x(s):=g(\gamma(s))

and (for

)
\gamma_{j}(s):=\langle\gamma(s),e_{j}\rangle
(the
j
-th coordinate
of
\gamma(s)
)
for
0\leq s\leq 1
,
we have for
1\leq i\leq m
that
dx_{i}(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\partial g_{i}/\partial u_{j})d\gamma_{j}(s)

and hence

|dx(s)|=\sqrt{(d\gamma)^{\prime}J_{g,\gamma(s)}^{T}J_{g,\gamma(s)}(d\gamma)},

where
J_{g,u}=(\partial g_{i}/\partial u_{j})_{i\leq m,j\leq d}

is the Jacobian matrix of
g
, evaluated at
u\in U
. Writing

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{u}
for this inner product,
i.e.

(3.2)

while

(3.3)

For

of

, while

We assert that if

(3.4)

We work towards proving (

Given

given

in

there exist
\delta>0

with

,
such that

\displaystyle\delta\leq\langle f,f\rangle_{w}\leq\delta^{-1},~~~\forall f\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1},~w\in U^{\prime}.

(3.5)

Next, for all
u\in U^{\prime}
,

e\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}

\langle e,e\rangle_{u}=e^{\prime}J_{g,u}^{T}J_{g,u}e

For
i\leq m,j\leq d

Hence there exists

\displaystyle|\langle v,v\rangle_{w}-\langle v,v\rangle_{\tilde{{w}}}|=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}v_{i}(h_{i,j}(w)-h_{i,j}(\tilde{{w}}))v_{j}\right|\leq K\delta\|w-\tilde{{w}}\|,

3.4
).

With

Comparing this with (

\displaystyle\delta{\rm dist}(y,z)\leq\|g^{-1}(y)-g^{-1}(z)\|\leq\delta^{-1}{\rm dist}(y,z).

such that

\displaystyle\sqrt{\langle e,e\rangle_{w}}\leq(1+K^{\prime}{\rm dist}(g(w),g(\tilde{{w}})))\sqrt{\langle e,e\rangle_{\tilde{{w}}}}.

(3.7)

For any
{\tilde{x}},y,z\in g(U^{\prime})

Therefore by setting

\displaystyle\sqrt{\langle e,e\rangle_{{\tilde{u}}}}\leq(1+K^{\prime}[{\rm dist}(z,y)+{\rm dist}(y,{\tilde{x}})])\sqrt{\langle e,e\rangle_{\gamma(s)}}.

\displaystyle\|\phi_{{\tilde{x}}}(y)-\phi_{{\tilde{x}}}(z)\|\leq(1+K_{3}[{\rm dist}({\tilde{x}},y)+{\rm dist}(y,z)]){\rm dist}(y,z).

(3.8)

To derive an inequality the other way, note that
with
{\tilde{u}}:=g^{-1}({\tilde{x}})

\displaystyle\|\phi_{{\tilde{x}}}(z)-\phi_{{\tilde{x}}}(y)\|

\displaystyle=\|\psi_{{\tilde{x}}}(z)-\psi_{{\tilde{x}}}(y)\|=\|g^{\prime}({\tilde{u}})(g^{-1}(z)-g^{-1}(y))\|

\displaystyle=\|J_{g,{\tilde{u}}}(g^{-1}(z)-g^{-1}(y))\|

\displaystyle|g(\gamma)|=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\langle g^{-1}(z)-g^{-1}(y),g^{-1}(z)-g^{-1}(y)\rangle_{\gamma(s)}}ds,

(3.10)

\displaystyle\|\gamma(s)-{\tilde{u}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\gamma(s)-g^{-1}(y)\|+\|g^{-1}(y)-{\tilde{u}}\|

Hence for any

\displaystyle\sqrt{\langle e,e\rangle_{\gamma(s)}}

\displaystyle\leq(1+K^{\prime}{\rm dist}(g(\gamma(s)),{\tilde{x}}))\sqrt{\langle e,e\rangle_{\tilde{u}}}

Remark 3.4

We now describe how to adapt Lemma

Suppose

|g(\gamma)|

\gamma

to

in

to

from

to

and show that it is

from
y
to

in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The particular path

of
the straight-line path
\gamma_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
from

to

, where we set

That is, set

Then by definition

Also

\displaystyle|g(\gamma_{0})|=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{(v-u)^{\prime}J_{g,\gamma_{0}(t)}^{T}J_{g,\gamma_{0}(t)}(v-u)}\;dt.

(3.11)

We need to compare this with

For

,
we have that

\displaystyle|g(\gamma_{0})|-\|g(v)-g(u)\|=\|v-u\|\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sqrt{e^{\prime}J_{\gamma_{0}(t)}^{T}J_{\gamma_{0}(t)}e}-\sqrt{e^{\prime}{\tilde{J}}^{T}{\tilde{J}}e}\right)dt.

(3.12)

By the continuous differentiability in

\partial g_{i}/\partial u_{j}

and the Mean Value theorem,
there exists a constant

\displaystyle|(J_{\gamma_{0}(t)}-{\tilde{J}})_{i,j}|=|(\partial g_{i}/\partial u_{j}|_{\gamma_{0}(t)}-\partial g_{i}/\partial u_{j}|_{w_{i}})|\leq K\|u-v\|.

such that

and

|(\|J_{\gamma_{0}(t)}e\|-\|{\tilde{J}}e\|)|\leq\|(J_{\gamma_{0}(t)}-{\tilde{J}})e\|\leq K^{\prime}\|u-v\|.

Therefore since

\|J_{\gamma_{0}(t)}e\|-\|{\tilde{J}}e\|
is the integrand

\displaystyle{\rm dist}(y,z)\leq|g(\gamma_{0})|\leq\|y-z\|+K^{\prime}\|v-u\|^{2}.

(3.13)

By a further application of the Mean Value theorem,
there is further constant
K^{\prime\prime}
such that

\displaystyle\|y-z-g^{\prime}(u)(v-u)\|\leq K^{\prime\prime}\|v-u\|^{2}.

By the compactness of
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
there exists

such

\displaystyle 1\leq\frac{{\rm dist}(y,z)}{\|y-z\|}\leq 1+K^{\prime}\delta^{-2}\|y-z\|.\leq 1+K^{\prime}\delta^{-2}{\rm dist}(y,z).

Lemma 4.1

(Subsequence trick)

Suppose

See
[
15

Lemma 6.5

(Second stochastic domination lemma)
