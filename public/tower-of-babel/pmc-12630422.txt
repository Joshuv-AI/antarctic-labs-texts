DOCK2 modulates immune checkpoint inhibitor responsiveness and prognosis in cutaneous melanoma through multi-omics and single-cell T cell dynamics profiling
Authors: Huicong Wang, Chao Feng, Chengjun Lao, Yitong Liu, Min Yan, Jiliang Wang, Zhenyan Li, Juan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSkin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM) is a highly aggressive malignancy that arises from melanocytes and has been associated with a notable increase in incidence and mortality rates across the globe. The current treatment modalities for SKCM, which encompass surgical interventions, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, have notably enhanced survival outcomes for certain patient subsets. Nonetheless, these approaches are frequently limited by factors such as variable efficacy, adverse side effects, and the emergence of resistance mechanisms. In this context, there is a pressing need to explore novel therapeutic targets that may enhance treatment effectiveness.MethodsThis study examines the Dedicator of cytokinesis 2 (DOCK2) gene, which has been implicated in immune regulation, yet its functional roles within oncogenesis, particularly in melanoma, remain inadequately characterized. We employed an integrated bioinformatics approach utilizing clinical datasets from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), the Genotype-Tissue Expression project (GTEx), and the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) to evaluate DOCK2 expression across various malignancies and to assess its prognostic significance in SKCM.ResultsOur analyses revealed that DOCK2 expression is differentially modulated across several cancer types and serves as a favorable prognostic marker specifically in SKCM. Notably, elevated DOCK2 levels were significantly correlated with improved therapeutic responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), suggesting that DOCK2 may play a pivotal role in modulating the tumor immune microenvironment. Furthermore, functional enrichment analyses indicated that DOCK2-associated genes are critically involved in vital immune pathways, encompassing leukocyte-mediated immunity and immune receptor activity. Increased expression of DOCK2 was also associated with heightened T cell infiltration in SKCM tumor tissues.ConclusionsCollectively, these findings highlight the promising prognostic and therapeutic implications of DOCK2 in melanoma, emphasizing its potential as a biomarker for assessing immunotherapy efficacy. Our research advocates for further investigation into DOCK2 as a viable target for precision immunotherapy, which could ultimately lead to improved treatment strategies for patients battling SKCM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03983-1.

Introduction

Skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), a malignant tumor derived from melanocytes, is characterized by a steadily increasing global incidence, particularly in high-SDI (Socio-demographic Index) countries, and is associated with considerable morbidity and mortality [1]. According to global cancer statistics, melanoma accounts for just 7% of all skin cancers but contributes to 65% of skin cancer-related deaths. While early-stage localized melanoma can often be managed with surgical resection, advanced cases involving lymphatic or systemic metastases typically require chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy. However, these treatments show significant interindividual variability and are frequently hindered by the development of resistance [2–4]. Thus, identifying novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets is crucial to improving clinical outcomes.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting Programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/ Programmed cell death 1 ligand 1 (PD-L1) and Cytotoxic T-lymphocyte protein 4 (CTLA-4) have revolutionized the management of advanced melanoma [5]. Yet, despite their transformative potential, not all patients derive durable benefits, with many experiencing resistance or relapses. The tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), particularly the infiltration density and activity of cytotoxic T cells, plays is a critical role in determining responsiveness to ICIs [6, 7]. DOCK2, initially identified in lymphocytes across multiple organs, functions primarily as guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) for Rac GTPases, regulating processes such as lymphocyte migration and chemotaxis [8, 9]. Emerging evidence indicates that DOCK2 deficiency promotes tumorigenesis and chemoresistance [10, 11]. Additionally, DOCK2 regulates diverse functions in B and T cells, including chemotaxis, adhesion, and differentiation [8, 12, 13], suggesting a potential role in tumor progression and immune microenvironment dynamics. Although PD1 blockade has become a cornerstone in melanoma treatment, patient responses remain heterogenous [14]. An important mechanism that impedes the effectiveness of ICI therapy is the absence of a driving force for T cell chemotaxis within the tumor microenvironment. This deficiency fails to recruit a sufficient number of T cells into the tumor tissue to exert anti-tumor effects. Given the finding that T cell migratory function is attenuated in DOCK2-deficient mice [15], we postulate that DOCK2 may influence the efficacy of ICI therapy by regulating T cell chemotaxis. Whether DOCK2 contributes to modulating the melanoma TIME and enhancing ICI efficacy remains unclear.

In this study, we employed retrospective clinical analyses, survival modeling, and integrative multi-omics approaches to investigate the role of DOCK2 in SKCM. By leveraging bulk transcriptomic data, single-cell profiling, and pseudotime trajectory analyses, we demonstrate that DOCK2 serves as a central hub governing T cell infiltration, intercellular communication, and metabolic adaptation. Our findings identify DOCK2 not only as a robust biomarker of ICI responsiveness but also as potential therapeutic target, offering new avenues for personalized melanoma treatment.

Methods

Gene expression data acquisition

RNA sequencing data in TPM format were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov) for the following tumor types: Adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA), breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC), bile duct cancer (CHOL), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBC), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), kidney chromophobe (KICH), clear cell renal cell carcinoma (KIRC), papillary renal cell carcinoma (KIRP), acute myeloid Leukemia (LAML), brain lower grade glioma (LGG), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), mesothelioma (MESO), ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma (OV), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma (PCPG), prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), rectum adenocarcinoma (READ), sarcoma (SARC), skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), testicular germ cell tumor (TGCT), thyroid carcinoma (THCA), thymoma (THYM), uterine corpus endometrioid carcinoma (UCEC), uterine carcinosarcoma (UCS) and uveal melanoma(UVM). Corresponding clinical data was also downloaded. To enable comparison with normal tissues, gene expression data from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project (https: All transcriptomic data underwent log2(TPM + 1) transformation to reduce skewness and improve analytical robustness. DOCK2 expression across tumor types was assessed using R software (version 4.2.2, URL: https://www.r-project.org/) with the ggplot2 package (version 3.5.1, URL: https://ggplot2.tidyverse.org/). Median expression values served as thresholds for differential expression analysis. Statistical significance was determined using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Survival analysis

The prognostic value of DOCK2 expression was assessed across multiple tumor types using the R survival package. Kaplan-Meier survival analyses were conducted for cancers, including ACC, BLCA, BRCA, CESC, CHOL, COAD, DLBC, ESCA, GBM, HNSC, KICH, KIRC, KIRP, LAML, LGG, LIHC, LUAD, LUSC, MESO, OV, PAAD, PCPG, PRAD, READ, SARC, SKCM, STAD, TGCT, THCA, THYM, UCEC, UCS, and UVM. DOCK2 expression was dichotomized using the median value within each cohort as the cutoff, an approach selected to ensure balanced group sizes and to maximize statistical power. Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to compare overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI) between high and lowDOCK2 expression groups. For SKCM, additional stratified analyses were performed by T stage, clinical stage, Breslow depth, and ulceration status. The proportional hazards assumption was tested, and model fitting were performed using the survival package (version 3.6-4, URL: https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=survival). All plots were generated using ggplot2.

Functional and gene set enrichment analysis

DOCK2 expression data for SKCM patients from TCGA database were stratified into high and low expression groups. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package [16]. The top 150 upregulated genes and the top 150 downregulated genes were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses using the clusterProfiler package [17]. Gene ID conversion was carried out using the org.Hs.eg.db package [18]. Pairwise correlation analysis was used to assess the correlation between DOCK2 and known immune checkpoint regulatory genes, using gene lists curated from published literature(Supplementary Table S1) [19]. Genes were classified based on their documented functional roles in immune regulation as follows: (1) Activators, referring to genes that promote immune cell activation or cytokine production; (2) Inhibitors, referring to genes associated with immune suppression or exhaustion; and (3) Dual function regulators, referring to genes with context-dependent roles. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was then on genes identified from the correlation analysis following gene ID conversion. Results from GO, KEGG, and GSEA were visualized using the ggplot2 package.

Immune cell infiltration analysis

We first assessed the tumor microenvironment across multiple cancer types (BLCA, BRCA, CESC, CHOL, COAD, DLBC, ESCA, GBM, HNSC, KICH, KIRC, KIRP, LAML, LGG, LIHC, LUAD, LUSC, MESO, OV, PAAD, PCPG, PRAD, READ, SARC, SKCM, STAD, TGCT, THCA, THYM, UCEC, UCS, and UVM) by calculating stromal scores, immune scores, and tumor purity using the `ESTIMATE` R package [version 1.0.13] [20]. We then analyzed the correlation between these metrics and DOCK2 expression. To further investigate immune cell infiltration in SKCM, we employed two complementary approaches: the single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm from the GSVA R package (version 1.46.0) [21], and the CIBERSORT algorithm, using immune cell gene signatures provided by the CIBERSORTx platform (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/). The ratios of immune cells to tumor cells and other cell types were calculated using the ImmuneScore derived from the ESTIMATE algorithm. Tumor purity was determined using the formula: Tumor Purity = cos(θ), where θ = 0.6049872018 + 0.0001467884 × ESTIMATE Score.

The ssGSEA analysis quantified the enrichment of various immune cell types, including activated dendritic cells (aDC), B cells, CD8+ T cells, cytotoxic cells, dendritic cells (DCs), eosinophils, immature dendritic cells (iDC), macrophages, mast cells, neutrophils, NK CD56bright cells, NK CD56dim cells, NK cells, plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC), T cells, T helper cells, central memory T cells (Tcm), effector memory T cells (Tem), follicular helper T cells (TFH), gamma delta T cells (Tgd), Th1 cells, Th17 cells, Th2 cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs). In parallel, The CIBERSORT algorithm profiled an additional set of immune cell subsets, including naïve B cells, memory B cells, plasma cells, CD8 + T cells, naïve CD4+ T cells, resting memory CD4+ T cells, activated memory CD4+ T cells, TFH, Tregs, gamma delta T cells, resting natural killer (NK) cells, activated NK cells, monocytes, M0 macrophages, M1 macrophages, M2 macrophages, resting dendritic cells, activated DCs, resting mast cells, activated mast cells, eosinophils, and neutrophils.

Single-cell sequencing data processing and analysis

Single-cell sequencing data were processed using the Seurat R package. The dataset was imported from the SKCM_GSE120575.h5 file, and a Seurat object was generated using the CreateSeuratObject function. Because this dataset includes tumor samples from patients both before and after receiving ICI therapy, it was selected for further in-depth analysis. Cells were filtered to include only those with at least 300 detected genes, and genes were retained if expressed in at least five cells. Mitochondrial gene expression percentage (percent.mt) was calculated for each cell, and low-quality cells were excluded based on the following criteria: nFeature_RNA over 80,000, nCount_RNA over 4,000, or percent.mt over 15%. Violin plots for nFeature_RNA, nCount_RNA, and percent.mt were generated to visualize data quality. The data were normalized using the “Log Normalize” method with a scaling factor of 10,000. Highly variable genes (n = 2,000) were identified using the “vst” method. Principal component analysis (PCA) was then performed on these genes, following standardization. The top 50 principal components (PCs) were computed, and related visualizations, including feature gene plots, PCA plots, heatmaps, and p-value distribution plots, were generated. Cell-cell similarity was assessed using the FindNeighbors function, and clustering was conducted FindClusters with a resolution of 0.8. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was used for dimensionality reduction, and the clustering results were visualized using UMAP plots. Cell-type annotation was performed using the “SingleR” package (version 1.8.1) [22, 23] with celldex::HumanPrimaryCellAtlasData as the reference. Cluster identities were assigned based on annotation results, mapped back to the Seurat object, and visualized again using UMAP to show the distribution of annotated cell types.

To investigate intercellular communication, the CellChat package (version 1.6.0) and the CellChatDB. human database [24] were used to infer potential ligand-receptor interactions among cell populations.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis was conducted to investigate the dynamic expression of DOCK2 in T cell subsets. T cell subpopulations were extracted from the preprocessed Seurat object [25] using SingleR-annotated labels [22] and imported into the Monocle3 framework [26] for trajectory inference. After data normalization, dimensionality reduction via UMAP, and clustering, the cell with the lowest DOCK2 expression was manually designated as the root node for initiating pseudotime computation. Trajectories were visualized in UMAP space with pseudotime gradients overlaid to illustrate developmental progression. DOCK2 expression was then plotted along the pseudotime axis to reveal its temporal expression pattern. To identify genes co-varying with DOCK2, a graph-autocorrelation-based statistical model was applied. Genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05, adjusted p-value < 0.01 and an absolute correlation coefficient >0.4 with DOCK2 were considered significant. These genes were further visualized using heatmaps and parallel coordinate plots.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and graphical visualizations were performed using R (version 4.2.2) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA; URL: www.graphpad.com). Differences in gene expression between tumor and normal tissues were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated, and group differences were evaluated using the log-rank test. Multivariate survival analysis was conducted using Cox proportional hazards regression models. Correlations between two variables were quantified using Pearson’s correlation coefficient for normally distributed data or Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient for non-parametric data. Statistical significance thresholds were defined as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Results

Expression variability and prognostic significance of DOCK2 in pan-cancer

To determine the expression patterns of DOCK2 in tumors versus normal tissues, we analyzed data TCGA and GTEx databases. As shown in Fig. 1a, DOCK2 expression varied significantly between normal and tumor tissues. Specifically, DOCK2 was significantly upregulated in CHOL (p < 0.05), GBM (p < 0.01), HNSC (p < 0.01), KIRC (p < 0.001), KIRP (p < 0.001), and STAD (p < 0.05), whereas it was downregulated in BLCA (p < 0.01), COAD (p < 0.001), LUAD (p < 0.001), LUSC (p < 0.001), PAAD (p < 0.01), and READ (p < 0.001). Paired sample analyses further confirmed elevated DOCK2 expression in KIRC (p < 0.001) and KIRP (p < 0.001), and reduced expression in COAD (p < 0.001), LUAD (p < 0.001), LUSC (p < 0.001), PAAD (p < 0.05), and UCEC (p < 0.01) compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1b). Due to limited data availability, expression analyses could not be conducted for ACC, DLBC, LAML, LGG, OV, SARC, SKCM, TGCT, THYM, UCS, and UVM (Fig. 1a-b). These findings suggest divergent roles for DOCK2 across tumor types.

To assess the prognostic value of DOCK2, we extracted overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI) data from TCGA. Patients were stratified into high and low DOCK2 expression groups using the median expression value. As shown in Fig. 1c, high DOCK2 expression was associated with worse OS in LGG (HR = 1.301, 95% CI = 1.091–1.552, p < 0.01) and UVM (HR = 1.741, 95% CI = 1.099–2.760, p < 0.05), but appeared protective in HNSC (HR = 0.849, 95% CI = 0.746–0.967, p < 0.05), LUAD (HR = 0.842, 95% CI = 0.731–0.970, p < 0.05), SKCM (HR = 0.782, 95% CI = 0.701–0.872, p < 0.001), and THYM (HR = 0.513, 95% CI = 0.280–0.939, p < 0.05). For DSS, DOCK2 was again at risk remained a risk factor in LGG (HR = 1.328, 95% CI = 1.104–1.597, p < 0.01) and UVM (HR = 1.664, 95% CI = 1.030–2.687, p < 0.05) but conferred protective effects in CESC (HR = 0.698, 95% CI = 0.507–0.960, p < 0.05), HNSC (HR = 0.803, 95% CI = 0.676–0.953, p < 0.05), LUAD (HR = 0.822, 95% CI = 0.689–0.982, p < 0.05), and SKCM (HR = 0.789, 95% CI = 0.703–0.885, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1d). In terms of PFI, DOCK2 predicted poorer outcomes only in LGG (HR = 1.307, 95% CI = 1.133–1.508, p < 0.001), while showing favorable prognostic associations in CESC (HR = 0.739, 95% CI = 0.561–0.972, p < 0.05), CHOL (HR = 0.557, 95% CI = 0.326–0.953, p < 0.05), HNSC (HR = 0.842, 95% CI = 0.732–0.969, p < 0.05), and SKCM (HR = 0.900, 95% CI = 0.824–0.984, p < 0.05) (Fig. 1e).

Further examination using the HPA database revealed that approximately 75% of T cells in the skin express DOCK2 (Fig. 1f), suggesting its functional relevance in the immune microenvironment of both normal skin and cutaneous tumors.

DOCK2 is associated with SKCM staging and prognosis

Our prior pan-cancer analysis identified DOCK2 as a protective factor for OS, DSS, and PFI for SKCM patients. We further compared survival metrics between patients with high versus low DOCK2 expression. While no statistically significant differences in DOCK2 expression were observed between subgroups based on survival status (alive vs. deceased), recurrence (recurrent vs. non recurrent), or clinical outcome (Fig. 2a and c). Kaplan-Meier analyses demonstrated that high DOCK2 expression was significantly associated with improved OS, DSS, and PFI in SKCM patients (Fig. 2d and f). To assess whether DOCK2 correlates with clinical staging, we analyzed its expression across different T stages in SKCM (Fig. 3a) and generated corresponding Kaplan-Meier curves (Fig. 3b and g). DOCK2 expression exhibited a downward trend with advancing T stage (Fig. 3a). Notably, in early T-stage disease (T1 and T2), high DOCK2 expression was associated with favorable prognosis, reflected hazard ratios of 0.51 (95% CI = 0.29–0.89) for OS and 0.49 (95% CI = 0.27–0.87) for DSS.

We also evaluated the relationships between DOCK2 expression and other clinical variables, including tumor invasion depth and ulceration status. When stratifying patients by Breslow depth (cutoff: 3 mm), DOCK2 expression differed significantly between groups (Fig. 4a–c), with higher expression correlating with thinner lesions. Similarly, DOCK2 levels were significantly lower in ulcerated tumors (Fig. 4d–f). Importantly, in patients with Breslow depth < 3 mm or non-ulcerated tumors, high DOCK2 expression retained prognostic significance. Taken together, these findings indicate that elevated DOCK2 expression is associated with improved prognosis in SKCM patients with early stage or less aggressive disease.

Having confirmed the prognostic relevance of DOCK2 in SKCM, we next investigated its potential functional mechanisms. Differential gene expression analysis was performed by stratifying SKCM patients into high and low DOCK2 expression groups. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) revealed enriched signaling pathways (Fig. 5a), including those related to immune function such as “leukocyte-mediated immunity” (Biological Processes) and “immune receptor activity” (Molecular Function).

To better understand DOCK2’s relationship with immune checkpoint regulation, we categorized immune checkpoint genes into activators (genes associated with immune suppression or exhaustion), inhibitors (genes that promote immune cell activation or cytokine production), and two sides(genes with context-dependent roles) [19], and assessed their correlations with DOCK2 expression. DOCK2 was significantly correlated with numerous immune checkpoint-related genes across these categories (Fig. 5b–d). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), visualized via bubble plots (Fig. 5e–f), further highlighted enrichment of the PD-1 signaling pathway (enrichment score >0.9, p < 0.05). Key components of this pathway, including CD274 (PD-L1), PDCD1 (PD-1), PDCD1LG2 (PD-L2, programmed cell death 1 ligand 2), and CD3E (T cell surface glycoprotein CD3 epsilon chain), were annotated and visualized (Fig. 5g), supporting the role of DOCK2 in modulating responsiveness to ICIs.

To directly assess this potential, we analyzed DOCK2 expression in SKCM patients stratified by ICI (anti-PD-1 or anti-CTLA-4 or anti-PD-1 + anti-CTLA-4) treatment phase (on-treatment vs. baseline) and clinical response (responders vs. non-responders). DOCK2 expression was significantly upregulated in on-treatment samples (Fig. 5h) and responders (Fig. 5i), indicating that DOCK2 may serve as a dynamic biomarker of ICI treatment efficacy.

Pan-cancer analysis of DOCK2 and T cell infiltration

Given the observed upregulation of ICI-related signaling pathways in high-DOCK2 expressing SKCM tumors, we next sought to identify the immune cell subsets potentially involved in mediating this effect. Using ESTIMATE (Fig. 6a), ssGSEA (Fig. 6b), and CIBERSORT (Fig. 6c) algorithms, we assessed the correlation between DOCK2 expression and components of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) across cancers. DOCK2 exhibited strong positive correlations (p < 0.001) with stromal content, immune infiltration, and reduced tumor purity in most cancer types, including SKCM (Fig. 6a). In Figs. 6b-c, we analyzed the correlation between DOCK2 expression levels and the infiltration levels of various immune cells in different tumor types using the ssGSEA and CIBERSORT algorithms. We found that in a considerable number of tumor types, DOCK2 expression was significantly correlated with T cells and NK cells. These findings highlight DOCK2’s critical role in shaping the TIME.

Single-cell profiling reveals therapy-specific DOCK2 dynamics in T cells and NK cells predicts anti-PD-1 response

After confirming the correlation between DOCK2 expression and T cell infiltration, we explored the relationship between DOCK2 expression and ICI treatment response at the single-cell level. using UMAP clustering, we classified melanoma cells into 18 clusters and annotated them as T cells, B cells, Monocytes, and NK cells (Fig. 7a-b). We then examined DOCK2 expression across these four immune cell types (Fig. 7c). To assess differential DOCK2 expression in SKCM patients receiving ICI therapy (anti-PD-1 or anti-CTLA-4 or anti-PD-1 + anti-CTLA-4), we used the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. DOCK2 expression was significantly upregulated in T cells (p = 1.2 × 10− 06) and B cells (p = 6.5 × 10− 07) (Fig. 7d-e) but showed no significant change in Monocytes (p = 0.15) and NK cells (p = 0.65) (Fig. 7f-g). A further stratified analysis according to the ICI regimens indicated that significant alterations in DOCK2 expression occurred in T cells and B cells within the tumors of patients following anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 + PD-1 treatments (Fig. 7h-k). Specifically, DOCK2 expression in intratumoral T cells was markedly upregulated after anti-PD-1 treatment (p = 3.1 × 10− 07), whereas it was significantly downregulated following the combined anti-CTLA-4 + PD-1 treatment (p = 0.035). In B cells within the tumors, DOCK2 expression levels were significantly upregulated after both anti-PD-1 (p = 0.022) and anti-CTLA-4 + PD-1 (p = 1.3 × 10− 4) treatments. Interestingly, DOCK2 expression in intratumoral monocytes was significantly downregulated following anti-CTLA-4 + PD-1 treatment (p = 0.032). These findings suggest that DOCK2 expression in T cells, B cells, and monocytes may influence the responsiveness to ICI therapy.

Analysis of treatment response groups further confirmed that DOCK2 expression in T cells (p = 0.0027) and monocytes (p = 0.049) were significantly higher in the Responder group than in the Non-responder group (Fig. 8a-d). Stratified analysis revealed that DOCK2 expression in monocytes was significantly elevated in responders receiving anti-CTLA-4 + PD-1 treatment compared to non-responders (p = 9.3 × 10− 08) (Fig. 8e-h) and in T cells of responders treated with anti-PD1 (p = 3.7 × 10− 07) (Fig. 8i-l). Furthermore, DOCK2 expression in T cells (p = 0.0018) and B cells (p = 0.0082) was significantly higher in patients treated with anti-PD-1 compared to combination therapy. Collectively, these findings support the role of DOCK2 expression as a marker of favorable ICI response in SKCM.

DOCK2 expression stratification unveils monocyte enrichment and T/NK cell depletion in SKCM patients under ICI therapy

To determine whether DOCK2 expression levels are associated with changes in immune cell composition following ICI therapy, we categorized SKCM patients into high and low DOCK2 expression groups and compared the proportion of four major cell types. Across all samples, monocyte proportions were higher in the high-DOCK2 group (15.28% vs. 5.95%), while T cell and NK cell proportions were lower (T cells: 70.25% vs. 77.83%, NK cells: 4.16% vs. 5.48%) (Fig. 9a). When comparing pre- and post-treatment immune profiles, these trends remained consistent (Fig. 9b-c). Notably, in patients with high DOCK2 expression, T cell proportions decreased following ICI therapy, while monocyte levels increased, suggesting that DOCK2 may play an important role in driving the differences in treatment responses. Following stratification according to treatment type, we observed that in patients with high DOCK2 expression, anti-PD-1 treatment led to a decrease in the proportion of T cells (77.064% vs. 81.581%) and NK cells (4.725% vs. 6.190%) within the tumor, along with an increase in the proportion of monocytes (11.350% vs. 6.324%). After treatment with anti-CTLA-4 + PD-1, the proportion of T cells (55.42% vs. 68.25%) and B cells (18.01% vs. 21.11%) within the tumor decreased, while the proportion of monocytes increased (23.69% vs. 7.47%) (Fig. 9d).

DOCK2 coordinates MHC-I-mediated T cell-NK cell crosstalk through HLA-E–KLRK1 axis in SKCM immune microenvironment

To investigate immune cell interactions potentially regulated by DOCK2, we analyzed cell-cell communication using CellChat and the CellChatDB.human database to calculate the potential connections between ligands and receptors in four types of immune cells. Among the signaling pathways identified, the MHC-I signaling pathway showed the highest interaction probabilities, particularly between T cells-T cells and T cells-NK cells (Fig. 10a). Signal transmission analysis of intercellular communication revealed that T cells function as major receivers, mediators, and influencers, while NK cells act primarily as senders (Fig. 10b). Ligand-receptor analysis highlighted HLA-E–KLRK1 as the most prominent interaction in the MHC-I network (Fig. 10c-d). Expression profiling of this pathway confirmed high HLA-E and KLRK1 expression in both T cells and NK cells, consistent with previous findings (Fig. 9a-d). These results further implicate DOCK2, particularly in T cells and monocytes, in modulating ICI response in SKCM.

Analysis of dynamic expression and covarying regulatory networks of DOCK2 in T cells using pseudotime trajectory

Given the association between DOCK2 expression and T cells, we conducted pseudotime trajectory analysis to explore its temporal dynamics. UMAP-based dimensionality reduction displayed the distribution of T cell subsets in a two-dimensional space. Figures 11a–c presents the distribution of T cell subset clustering on the UMAP plot in the Monocle3 analysis, facilitating an understanding of the classification structure of T cell subsets. Principal component variance analysis informed dimensionality reduction parameters (Fig. 10d). We used pseudotime as a variable to demonstrate the developmental trajectory of T cells (Fig. 11e). The trajectory was mapped across pseudotime, with DOCK2 expression showing dynamic regulation throughout the course of T cell development. We observed that the expression level of DOCK2 gradually declined during T cell differentiation. (Fig. 11f). Co-expression analysis identified CD38 as a gene that covaried with DOCK2 (Fig. 11g), suggesting potential co-regulation during T cell activation. Together, these results provide new insights into how dynamic DOCK2 expression may influence T-cell mediated responses to ICI therapy in SKCM patients.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the role of DOCK2 in shaping the responsiveness of SKCM patients to ICI treatment and explored its underlying molecular mechanisms. Using a range of bioinformatics methods, we analyzed clinical information and transcriptomic data gene expression data TCGA, GTEx, and HPA databases. Our findings highlight the dual role of DOCK2 as a prognostic biomarker and as a key modulator of tumor-immune interactions in SKCM. Pan-cancer analysis revealed context-dependent expression patterns of DOCK2. In SKCM and several other malignancies (e.g., LUAD, LUSC) lower DOCK2 expression correlated with advanced tumor stage and poor survival outcomes (OS, DSS, and PFI). Notably, its inverse association with Breslow depth and ulceration status underscores DOCK2’s potential as an early diagnostic and prognostic biomarker in SKCM.

GO/KEGG enrichment analysis demonstrated that DOCK2 is significantly involved in lymphocyte differentiation and immune regulation pathways, supporting previous reports (12, 13). In SKCM, genes co-expressed with DOCK2 were enriched in immune checkpoint signaling and leucocyte-mediated immunity, aligning with DOCK2’s known role in activating Rac signaling for T cell chemotaxis and immune synapse formation [27, 28]. Importantly, the co-variation of DOCK2 with PD-L1, PD-1, and CD3E suggests that it may enhance TCR clustering [29] and promote downstream interferon-γ production [30], thereby increasing tumor sensitivity to immune checkpoint blockade, This hypothesis is further supported by the significant upregulation of DOCK2 in anti-PD-1 responders. This mechanism is in line with previous findings that DOCK2 deficiency disrupts MHC-I-dependent NK cell cytotoxicity [31], suggesting a broader role in coordinating adaptive and innate immune responses. Furthermore, while DOCK2 positively correlates with several inhibitory checkpoint genes (e.g., CTLA-4, LAG-3(Lymphocyte activation gene 3 protein)) adding complexity, as DOCK2 may paradoxically maintain T cell exhaustion in advanced tumors. GSEA enrichment of the PD-1 pathway, and the improved survival rate in patients with high DOCK2 expression, indicate that DOCK2 likely enhances pre-existing anti-tumor immunity rather than driving T cell exhaustion. Additionally, elevated expression of immune checkpoints (PD-1, PD-L1, CTLA-4) in high DOCK2-expressing tumors may render them more susceptible to ICI therapy. These observations are consistent with the pseudotime trajectory results, in which DOCK2 co-varies with CD38. In addition to being a marker of metabolically active and proliferative T cells, CD38 has also been shown to enhance tumor control when its expression is inhibited in cancer treatment. This finding may point to a novel therapeutic target for melanoma [32, 33]. The treatment-induced upregulation of DOCK2 in responders may reflect enhanced TCR signaling during T cell activation, consistent with DOCK2’s known function [29].

Single-cell analysis further revealed that DOCK2 dynamics in T cells and B cells are therapy specific. Anti-PD-1 therapy significantly upregulated DOCK2 expression in T cells, while anti-CTLA-4 + PD-1 combination therapy decreased it (Fig. 7d, h). These opposing trends suggest that PD-1 and CTLA-4 signaling may differentially regulate DOCK2 activity, potentially through distinct ligand-receptor or feedback mechanisms. Importantly, DOCK2 expression was significantly higher in T cells and monocytes from ICI responders (Fig. 8), reinforcing its potential as a predictive biomarker of treatment response. Analysis of intercellular communication highlighted the MHC-I signaling axis, particularly the HLA-E-KLRK1 interaction being dominant, as a dominant pathway mediating T cell-NK cell crosstalk. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that DOCK2 supports NK cell cytotoxicity via Rac-dependent cytoskeletal remodeling [31]. However, our data uniquely indicate that T cells function are both transmitters and receivers of DOCK2-mediated signaling, suggesting a bidirectional regulatory loop. The elevated monocyte proportions in high-DOCK2 patients, combined with their increased DOCK2 expression in responders, hints at a potential myeloid-T cell axis facilitated by DOCK2, possibly through Rac-mediated cytokine production or antigen presentation [34]. While DOCK2 expression was detected in B cells, its functional relevance in this context remains unclear. Our focus on T cells reflects existing evidence linking DOCK2 to T cell signaling, as well as the availability of relevant datasets. Further investigation into the involvement of B cells is warranted in future studies.

Despite these insights, some limitations remain. The mechanistic link between DOCK2 expression and T cell functional states (e.g., exhaustion vs. activation) requires further experimental validation. Additionally, the distinct effects of anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 + PD-1 treatments on DOCK2 dynamics warrant in-depth investigation in preclinical models. Finally, the therapeutic potential of targeting the DOCK2-Rac axis, via mRNA-based strategies or small-molecule inhibitors, should be evaluated in translational and clinical settings.

In summary, our findings identify DOCK2 as a pivotal orchestrator of T cell infiltration and ICI responsiveness in SKCM. Its context-dependent regulation across immune cell subsets and treatment regimens highlights its promise as both a predictive biomarker and a therapeutic target for enhancing adoptive ICI efficacy and overcoming resistance in melanoma.

Conclusion

Our integrated analysis of multi-omics datasets identifies DOCK2 as a transcriptomic feature consistently associated with ICI responsiveness in SKCM patients. Exploratory analyses suggest that elevated DOCK2 expression correlates with enhanced T cell effector function, dynamic intercellular communication networks, and improved clinical outcomes in cohorts treated with immunotherapy. Mechanistically, computational pathway inference reveals potential associations between DOCK2 expression levels and PD-1/CTLA-4 signaling pathway activity. These findings position DOCK2 as a candidate biomarker warranting further investigation for patient stratification in immunotherapy.
