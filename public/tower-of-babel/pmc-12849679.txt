Abdominal ectopic bronchogenic cysts: a retrospective single-institution case series and literature review
Authors: Nai Wu, Tiankang Li, Shuai Gong, Yi Zhang, Chong Zhang, Xiuzhong Zhang, Zeqiang Ren, Pengbo Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAbdominal ectopic bronchogenic cysts (EBCs) are rare congenital malformations that are frequently misdiagnosed preoperatively due to their nonspecific and often asymptomatic presentation. This study aimed to elucidate the clinical characteristics, diagnostic challenges, and optimal management of abdominal EBCs by integrating data from a sizable single-institutional case series with a comprehensive literature review.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 12 patients with pathologically confirmed abdominal bronchogenic cysts who were treated at our institution from January 2015 to January 2024. Data on demographic characteristics, clinical presentation, imaging features, surgical management, and pathological findings were collected. A comprehensive review of the pertinent literature was also performed to identify published case reports and series. Data are presented using descriptive statistics, including medians and proportions.ResultsThe cohort comprised 12 patients (7 male, 5 female) with a median age of 46 years. Eight patients (66.7%) were symptomatic. The most common symptom was epigastric pain, presenting in 7 patients (58.3%). Eleven cysts (91.7%) were located in the upper abdominal retroperitoneum. Eleven patients (91.7%) were misdiagnosed preoperatively. Laparoscopic complete resection was achieved in 9 patients (75%), while 3 patients (25%) underwent open surgery. The diagnosis was histologically confirmed in all cases by the identification of ciliated pseudostratified columnar epithelium. One patient (8.3%) experienced recurrence following initial fenestration and required reoperation.ConclusionsAbdominal bronchogenic cysts present a considerable diagnostic challenge, resulting in a high preoperative misdiagnosis rate. It should be considered in the differential diagnosis of an abdominal cystic neoplasm. Laparoscopic complete resection represents a safe and effective therapeutic approach, yielding favorable outcomes. Complete surgical excision is paramount to prevent recurrence. Introduction

Bronchogenic cysts (BCs) are congenital malformations arising from aberrant development of the embryonic foregut [1]. During the 4th to 7th weeks of embryogenesis, as the foregut differentiates to form the tracheobronchial tree, abnormal budding or sequestration of the bronchial anlage may occur. These displaced tissues retain respiratory potential, producing mucus and forming cysts due to their lack of communication with the central airways [2, 3]. Pathologically, BCs are characterized by a cyst wall lined with ciliated pseudostratified columnar epithelium, typically containing smooth muscle bundles and mucous glands. The presence of cartilage in some cases provides definitive histological evidence of bronchial origin [4]. The cyst lumen usually contains viscous, clear to pale yellow mucoglycoprotein-rich fluid, which may become purulent or hemorrhagic if secondary infection or bleeding occurs [5].

Epidemiologic studies estimate the prevalence of BCs at 1 in 42,000–68,000 hospital admissions [5, 6]. The condition affects a wide age range (17–81 years), with a median age of 43 years, and shows a slight female predominance [7]. Many BCs remain clinically silent and are often discovered incidentally in adulthood [8]. While most are located in the mediastinum or pulmonary parenchyma, a small subset arises in ectopic sites, most commonly within the abdomen or retroperitoneum, particularly near the left adrenal gland, perigastric region, or subdiaphragmatic space [9]. These lesions are referred to as abdominal ectopic bronchogenic cysts (EBCs) [10].

The preoperative diagnosis of abdominal EBCs is clinically challenging [7, 11, 12]. Most patients are asymptomatic, while others may experience vague abdominal discomfort, bloating, or nausea, which can easily be mistaken for other abdominal conditions [13]. Cross-sectional imaging, such as CT and MRI, serves as the primary diagnostic tool, but features frequently overlap with more common abdominal pathologies, including gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs), lymphatic malformations, and adrenal tumors, resulting in a high rate of misdiagnosis [14–16]. No reliable serological biomarkers or specific imaging criteria are available for definitive preoperative differentiation [17]. Therefore, surgical excision followed by histopathological examination remains the gold standard for diagnosis [18].

Current evidence on abdominal EBCs is limited, consisting mainly of isolated case reports and small series with little systematic synthesis. As a result, several critical questions remain unresolved, including the precise epidemiology and natural history of the disease, key preoperative diagnostic indicators, the choice between laparoscopic and open surgery, and long-term postoperative outcomes such as recurrence risk [19]. To address these gaps, we report an institutional case series of 12 patients and a comprehensive literature review, totaling 185 cases. This study aims to identify preoperative clinical and imaging features that may reduce diagnostic errors and to evaluate the safety and efficacy of laparoscopic versus open resection. By integrating a large cohort of abdominal EBCs to date, we provide updated insights into the clinical characteristics of this rare condition.

Methods

General design

We conducted a single-institution, retrospective study of all patients who underwent surgical excision of an abdominal ectopic bronchogenic cyst between January 2015 and January 2024. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants for the use of their data in this analysis. Baseline parameters collected included demographic data (sex, age at diagnosis), clinical presentation, imaging findings, hematological investigations, cyst characteristics, and surgical details. Outcome measures included intraoperative parameters, postoperative complications, and histopathological confirmation. Statistical analysis employed standard measures of central tendency, including medians and ranges, along with total counts. Unpaired t-tests or Pearson’s chi-square tests were used when appropriate. All analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0. This study was reported in accordance with the PROCESS guidelines for surgical case series [20].

Assessment of diagnosis and histopathology

All patients were ultimately diagnosed with ectopic bronchogenic cysts and exhibited diverse clinical and imaging features. None had coexisting abdominal diseases. Preoperative diagnosis was based on CT and MRI in conjunction with clinical evaluation. Image-guided biopsy was avoided due to the deep anatomical location and the potential risk of cyst rupture. Definitive diagnosis was established postoperatively through histopathological examination, which revealed cysts lined by ciliated pseudostratified columnar bronchial epithelium. For histopathological processing, excised cystic tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Sections 5 μm thick were prepared, deparaffinized with xylene, dehydrated through graded ethanol, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). All slides were examined under a light microscope.

Surgical intervention and follow‑up

All patients underwent complete surgical excision of the identified cyst(s). The choice between laparoscopic and open approaches was determined based on cyst location, size, and the degree of adhesion to adjacent structures. One patient experienced recurrence after an initial fenestration procedure performed elsewhere and subsequently required repeat surgery for complete removal. Another patient underwent excision of cysts at two distinct abdominal sites. Patients were followed up at 1–2 months and again at 12 months after discharge. Follow-up assessments included contrast-enhanced CT imaging to evaluate recurrence. None of the patients experienced significant postoperative complications during the follow-up period.

Literature review

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in the PubMed database for articles published up to July 2025 using the keywords “bronchogenic cyst,” “bronchial cyst,” and “abdominal.” Included patients were histopathologically diagnosed with bronchogenic cysts, and detailed demographic and clinical information was collected.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the study population

All patient data were anonymized prior to analysis. This study retrospectively analyzed 12 patients diagnosed and treated for abdominal bronchogenic cysts in our institution between January 2015 and January 2024. All cases were confirmed as bronchogenic cysts through surgical excision and histopathological examination. Demographics, presenting symptoms, and specific features are summarized in Table 1. Among the 12 patients, seven were male and five were female, with ages ranging from 23 to 69 years (mean age: 46 years). Additionally, four patients (33.3%) were asymptomatic at presentation and were diagnosed incidentally during routine health screenings. The remaining eight patients (66.7%) presented with symptoms, with abdominal pain being the predominant symptom (58.3%). Notably, one patient had elevated Carcinoembryonic Antigen (CEA) levels (6.63 ng/ml; normal range, 0–5 ng/ml), and another patient demonstrated elevated Carbohydrate Antigen 50 (CA50) concentrations (28.23 IU/ml; normal range, 0.21–25 IU/ml). The remaining patients exhibited normal tumor marker levels.

Features on imaging studies

All patients underwent preoperative examinations, including multi-detector computed tomography (MDCT). Imaging characteristics and data are summarized in Table 2. The anatomical distributions of lesions showed significant heterogeneity, including locations at the cardia, anterior to the left psoas major muscle, and right retroperitoneal region (Fig. 1A–C). Among the 12 patients, 11 (91.7%) showed lesions in the retroperitoneal space, while one (8.3%) had an intraperitoneal lesion. Notably, one patient (8.3%) exhibited bilateral multifocal distribution along the abdominal aorta and underwent preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which demonstrated well-defined lesion margins and no enhancement effects (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, even though bronchogenic cysts typically present as isodense aqueous images, they can also exhibit hyperdense shadows in the presence of elevated protein content and viscous fluid [21] (Fig. 1E). Finally, the preoperative diagnostic accuracy in this study was only 8.3% (n = 1), markedly lower than the previously reported diagnostic rates for mediastinal bronchogenic cysts (approximately 35%–40%) [4]. This difference in the diagnostic rates highlights the particularly challenging aspect of the differential diagnosis of retroperitoneal lesions.

Treatment and final diagnosis

In the context of diagnostic confirmation and treatment, three patients (25.0%) underwent open abdominal surgery for tumor resection, while the remaining nine were managed laparoscopically (Fig. 2A–C). Several technical challenges were encountered intraoperatively (Table 3). The most frequent challenge was dense adhesions between the cyst wall and adjacent critical structures, including the gastric wall, ileocecal region, and major vasculature. A key finding was a case of recurrence: this patient initially underwent fenestration for a misdiagnosed hepatic cyst and required reoperation five years later for complete excision, which was successfully performed laparoscopically (Fig. 2D–F). Finally, the cystic fluid was observed to be transparent or brownish and turbid during the surgical procedure in our study patients (Fig. 2G–H). Final histopathological analysis confirmed the diagnosis of bronchogenic cyst in all 12 cases. Characteristic microscopic features included a cyst wall lined with ciliated pseudostratified columnar epithelium, accompanied by varying combinations of smooth muscle bundles and cartilaginous tissue (Fig. 2I-J). No evidence of malignant transformation was identified in any of specimens.

Outcome and follow-up

Generally, all patients in this study recovered rapidly after surgery and had an uneventful postoperative course without perioperative complications. We attempted to contact all patients for information on recurrence and long-term complications in April, 2025. Although not all patients underwent regular imaging examinations after surgery, they reported no signs of recurrence or long-term complications, such as incision hernia, before the most recent imaging assessment except the first case. The median follow-up period was 36 months (range: 15–105 months).

Summary of literature review

Abdominal bronchogenic cysts were first described by Miller et al. [22] in 1953. Subsequent comprehensive literature reviews were performed by Liang et al. [23] in 2005, Govaerts et al. [21] in 2012, and Xiao et al. [24] in 2022. In the present study, 153 publications underwent comprehensive review, including full-text evaluation and assessment of associated pathological findings for each case. Details of all included studies are presented in Supplementary Table 1, which represents the largest sample analyzed to date. The median patient age was 42 years (IQR: 30–52; range: 0.25–81 years), with a balanced sex distribution (97 females and 88 males; P = 0.554) (Table 4). Cysts were predominantly solitary (95.7%). Only 8 cases (4.3%) exhibited multiple cysts (up to 4). This included one bicystic case documented in our institutional cohort (representing 8.3% of our cases). The median cyst diameter was 5.0 cm (IQR: 3.6–7.3 cm; range: 1.5–18.9 cm). Collectively, these findings indicate that abdominal bronchogenic cysts exhibit substantial growth potential [25].

Symptoms occurred in 53% of patients, with epigastric pain being the predominant manifestation. Other symptoms included nausea, vomiting, and abdominal discomfort. Among patients reporting pain (back or abdominal), the median cyst diameter was 4.25 cm versus 5.5 cm in pain-free patients (Table 5). Notably, our institutional cohort exhibited a higher symptomatic rate (66.7%). This finding is potentially attributable to the older median patient age (49.5 years) within this subgroup. Preoperative laboratory testing revealed a notable diagnostic pitfall: elevated tumor marker levels occurred in 19 cases (10.3%), including CA19-9 (n = 12), CEA (n = 3), CA72-4 (n = 1), CA242 (n = 1), and CA50 (n = 2). Although these markers normalized postoperatively, their elevation may readily result in misdiagnosis [26].

Laparoscope constituted the predominant treatment modality (65.7%), substantially surpassing open surgery (30.1%). This distribution pattern was similarly observed in our institutional cohort. Since Okuda's initial report of laparoscopic resection in 1997 [22], the technique has been successfully extended to larger cysts, including an 11.1 cm cyst in our series. Follow-up demonstrated a very low malignant transformation rate (1.1%), represented by one well-differentiated papillary adenocarcinoma [16] and one neuroendocrine tumor [27]. Both malignancies occurred in cysts exceeding 5 cm in diameter. Consequently, patients considering conservative management should be counseled regarding potential symptom exacerbation with cyst enlargement. Symptomatic cysts larger than 4.25 cm can be treated through surgery, and cysts larger than 5.5 cm are recommended for surgical removal regardless of symptoms (Table 5). Furthermore, our study advances the understanding of abdominal bronchogenic cysts through recent findings and addresses previously unreported nuances in the literature.

Discussion

Abdominal ectopic bronchogenic cysts pose a significant diagnostic dilemma in clinical practice and must be considered in the differential diagnosis of an abdominal neoplasm [28–30]. This study synthesizes data from an institutional cohort (n = 12) and a comprehensive review of the literature (n = 173), offering updated insights into the clinical spectrum, diagnostic challenges, and management of this rare condition. The principal findings highlight a remarkably high preoperative misdiagnosis rate and establish laparoscopic complete excision as a safe and efficacious definitive therapy.

The fundamental diagnostic challenge of abdominal EBCs arises not only from their rarity but also from the convergence of several overlapping clinical and radiologic features [31, 32]. Although these cysts share histologic continuity with thoracic bronchogenic cysts, their abdominal location disrupts the usual diagnostic paradigm, leading clinicians to prioritize more common differentials such as gastrointestinal stromal tumors, adrenal neoplasms, or ovarian cysts [29, 33]. This anchoring bias, compounded by nonspecific symptoms and the inherent inability of cross-sectional imaging to identify respiratory-type epithelium, contributes to the remarkably low preoperative diagnostic accuracy observed in our cohort. The sporadic elevation of tumor markers, such as CEA and CA50, further complicates diagnostic reasoning by introducing a misleading signal suggestive of malignancy. Collectively, these factors underscore an important conceptual point: abdominal EBCs are not simply “rare cysts” but represent a diagnostic blind spot in abdominal imaging, where embryologic remnants can masquerade as common surgical diseases [34]. Consequently, definitive diagnosis ultimately depend on histopathological confirmation.

The management paradigm for abdominal EBCs has increasingly shifted toward minimally invasive surgery. Our data support laparoscopic complete resection as the preferred and effective therapeutic approach. In our cohort, laparoscopic surgery was successful in most cases (75%), with no recurrences observed in this subgroup. This is consistent with the broader literature, in which laparoscopic resection is reported as the predominant approach (65.7%). The principal intraoperative challenges, such as dense adhesions to vital structures or a deep retroperitoneal location, were key determinants of operative strategy, necessitating open conversion in select cases. A key finding from our series is the single recurrence, which occurred in a patient who underwent cyst fenestration rather than complete excision. This outcome reinforces a key biological principle: the ciliated respiratory epithelium retains secretory capacity, making complete excision a biological necessity for achieving cure and preventing recurrence, rather than merely a surgical preference [35].

The optimal management of asymptomatic cysts remains a subject of ongoing debate. Although conservative surveillance has been proposed [36], the approach at our center, supported by the present findings, favors proactive surgical intervention. This recommendation is supported by several considerations: the current inability to establish a definitive preoperative diagnosis, the potential for symptom development with cyst enlargement (our integrated analysis indicated a larger median diameter of 5.5 cm for symptomatic cysts), and the risk of malignant transformation (1.1% in our comprehensive literature review). Documented cases of well-differentiated papillary adenocarcinoma [8] and a neuroendocrine tumor [18], both in cysts exceeding 5 cm, suggest that malignant potential is a real, albeit rare, consequence of untreated lesions. Surgical excision thus provides both definitive histopathological diagnosis and curative therapy, supporting its use even in asymptomatic patients.

This study has limitations inherent to its retrospective, single-center design. The sample size, while substantial for this rare entity, precludes robust multivariate analysis. The follow-up duration, although sufficient for evaluating early recurrence, may be inadequate to detect very long-term outcomes or exceedingly rare events, such as delayed malignant transformation. Another limitation is that the literature review was not systematic; thus, the selection and inclusion of studies may have been influenced by publication availability and reporting heterogeneity. Future research should prioritize multi-institutional studies to develop refined diagnostic algorithms, potentially leveraging advanced imaging biomarkers, and to further validate the long-term outcomes of minimally invasive surgery.

In summary, abdominal bronchogenic cysts represent rare entities that warrant consideration in the differential diagnosis of cystic abdominal masses, particularly those located in the upper abdomen or retroperitoneum. Preoperative diagnosis remains challenging and is frequently incorrect. Laparoscopic complete excision constitutes the cornerstone of management, providing definitive diagnosis, curative therapy, low morbidity, and favorable long-term outcomes. Future research should prioritize multi-institutional studies aimed at refining preoperative diagnostic algorithms and validating the long-term efficacy of laparoscopic resection.

Conclusions

Although abdominal EBCs represent a rare clinical entity, they should be considered in the differential diagnosis of abdominal masses, particularly when cystic lesions are located near the gastroesophageal junction or within retroperitoneal compartments. Laparoscopic resection, recognized for its diagnostic accuracy and minimally invasive advantages, has emerged as the preferred diagnostic and therapeutic modality for this condition. Complete surgical excision via this approach achieves durable recurrence prevention with excellent long-term outcomes.
