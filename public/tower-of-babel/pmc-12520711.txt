Prescription Fills Among Patients With Type 2 Diabetes After Hospitalization for Acute Coronary Syndrome
Authors: Michelle D. Kelsey, Cassie Ford, Megan Oakes, Samir Soneji, Hayden B. Bosworth, Neha J. Pagidipati
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionHow do evidence-based prescription fills among individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D) change from admission to discharge after hospitalization for acute coronary syndrome (ACS)?FindingsIn this cohort study of 188 651 Medicare beneficiaries, prescription fills for all cardiovascular medication classes increased at discharge and decreased from discharge to 91 to 180 days after discharge, except for sodium glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists, which had very low rates of initiation overall. Not filling a cardiovascular medication after discharge was associated with not filling that medication at the time of discharge.MeaningThese findings suggest that hospitalization for myocardial infarction or coronary revascularization represents a critical period to optimize evidence-based cardiovascular medication use for individuals with T2D and ACS, because prescription fills during this time are strongly associated with longer-term medication use.

Introduction

In the US, approximately 37 million individuals (10% of the population) have diabetes.1 Cardiovascular events, such as myocardial infarction (MI), remain one of the leading causes of mortality in this population.2,3 Although outcomes following MI have improved over the last few decades, individuals with diabetes and acute coronary syndrome (ACS) have higher rates of mortality during hospitalization for MI and have a higher risk of complications.4,5,6 Individuals with diabetes are also more likely to be readmitted to the hospital following MI or coronary revascularization procedures.7

Although hospitalization for MI represents a high-risk time for individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D) and ACS, these episodes of care also present an opportunity to increase evidence-based medication use. Multiple agents are recommended for cardiovascular risk reduction for individuals with T2D and coronary artery disease. These agents include an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEI), an angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) (or an angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor [ARNI] in the appropriate clinical scenario), a β-blocker, some form of lipid-lowering therapy, an antiplatelet regimen, and a sodium glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT2I) or glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonist (GLP-1RA).8,9,10,11 Despite guidelines urging uptake of these preventive agents for patients with T2D and coronary artery disease,12 their use in clinical practice remains low.13,14 In this analysis, we sought (1) to characterize evidence-based medication use during the transition from hospitalization for MI or coronary revascularization to after discharge among individuals with T2D and ACS and (2) to identify factors associated with prescription fills for evidence-based medication during these periods.

Methods

Because this cohort study used deidentified claims data from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS), it was deemed exempt from review by the Duke University Institutional Review Board and informed consent was waived. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

Data Sources

We obtained a custom sample of research-identifiable claims data from the CMS, accessed through the Virtual Research Data Center. These data included demographic information from Master Beneficiary and Summary Files (MBSF), as well as inpatient, outpatient, and professional claims (Medicare Part A and Part B) and drug prescription fill records (Medicare Part D) from January 1, 2017, through December 31, 2020, for beneficiaries with a prior history of T2D and with a hospitalization for MI or coronary revascularization including coronary artery bypass graft or percutaneous coronary intervention, as defined by International Statistical Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) diagnosis codes (eTable in Supplement 1).

Study Population

The index hospitalization was defined as the first hospitalization from January 1, 2018, to June 30, 2020, for MI or coronary revascularization. Beneficiaries were included if they were aged 18 years or older, had a diagnosis of T2D in the year prior to index hospitalization, and had a full year of prior fee-for-service (Part A and Part B) Medicare coverage and continuous Part D Medicare coverage for both 100 days prior to and 180 days following the index hospitalization. Beneficiaries living outside of the continental US were excluded. Those who did not survive at least 180 days following index hospitalization and those with a hospitalization for MI or coronary revascularization in 2017 were excluded.

Demographics, Comorbidities, and Medication Outcomes

Demographic variables were abstracted from the MBSF. Rural or urban status was defined using the rural-urban commuting area classification C algorithm.15 Race variables included Black, White, and other race (includes American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, and any other or unknown race). Race variables in the MBSF are derived from Social Security Administration records, in addition to survey and US Census data.16 Data on race were included in order to identify disparities in prescription fill rates. Ethnicity was not specifically included for comparison in this dataset. Comorbidities were abstracted from inpatient, outpatient, and carrier claim files in the year prior to, and inclusive of, beneficiaries’ index hospitalization admission date using ICD-10 diagnosis codes.

We examined Medicare Part D prescription fill records during 3 time periods surrounding the index hospitalization: prior to admission (filled <90 days before index hospitalization, including the day of admission), at discharge (filled the day of hospital discharge to 90 days after), and after hospitalization (filled 91-180 days after discharge) (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). The 90-day intervals for each period were used to capture 90-day prescription fills. Medication classes of interest included the following: (1) ACEIs, ARBs, or ARNIs; (2) β-blockers; (3) platelet adenosine diphosphate receptor inhibitors (P2Y12Is); (4) statins or proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitors (PCSK9Is); and (5) GLP-1RAs or SGLT2Is.

Statistical Analysis

We described baseline characteristics using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and medians with 25th and 75th percentiles for continuous variables. Overall prescription fills and percentages for each medication class and period were examined. Patterns of change were assessed by comparing those who filled prescriptions prior to admission with (1) those who did or did not fill prescriptions at the time of discharge and (2) those who did or did not fill prescriptions in the 91 to 180 days after discharge. We compared these patterns of use overall and stratified by race, sex, and rurality. For each medication class, a logistic regression model was then fit with not having a prescription fill between 91 and 180 days after discharge as the outcome. Covariates included baseline demographic characteristics. The 2-sided threshold for statistical significance was P < .05. Data analysis was performed in June 2023 using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc).

Results

Among 857 314 hospitalizations identified for MI or coronary revascularization, 188 651 beneficiaries met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analytic cohort (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1). Overall, beneficiaries with T2D and hospitalization for MI or coronary revascularization had a median age of 73.0 (IQR, 67.0-79.0) years, 59.4% were men and 40.6% were women, and 29.9% were from rural areas (Table 1). A total of 9.7% of beneficiaries were Black, 81.3% were White, and 8.9% were of other race. Comorbidities were common: 68.6% of beneficiaries had coronary artery disease at baseline, 66.4% had hypertension, 31.2% had kidney disease, and 21.1% had atrial fibrillation or flutter. Dual eligibility for Medicare and Medicaid was present for 27.5% of beneficiaries, and only 14.9% of the total beneficiaries included were younger than 65 years.

At baseline, use rates for medications were 51.6% for ACEIs, ARBs, or ARNIs as well as 30.2% for β-blockers, 19.4% for P2Y12Is, 56.7% for statins or PCSK9Is, 3.4% for GLP-1RAs, and 2.1% for SGLT2Is. Prescription fills for all medication classes increased at discharge. Prescription fills for P2Y12Is increased most, compared with β-blockers, SGLT2Is, statins or PCSK9Is, GLP-1RAs, and ACEIs, ARBs, or ARNIs (in descending order) (Figure). Prescription fills for ACEIs, ARBs, or ARNIs as well as for β-blockers, P2Y12Is, and statins or PCSK9Is decreased from discharge to 91 to 180 days after hospitalization, whereas prescription fills for GLP1-RAs and SGTL2Is increased during the same period. Despite a drop-off among some classes, prescription fills were higher after hospitalization for all cardiovascular medications than prior to admission (Figure).

Beneficiaries who had a medication stopped at discharge seldom restarted it in the 91 to 180 days after discharge. Only 8.1% of patients taking an ACEI, ARB, or ARNI at admission who had the medication stopped at discharge restarted after discharge. Similarly, only 13.0% of patients taking a β-blocker, 11.0% taking a P2Y12I, 3.4% taking a statin or PCSK9I, and 14.0% taking an SGLT2I or a GLP-1RA who had these medications stopped at discharge restarted after discharge (Table 2). Conversely, individuals who were started on a medication at discharge more frequently continued this medication in the 91 to 180 days after discharge. This finding was observed across medication classes and regardless of medication status at admission (Table 2). A large number of patients had no prescription fills for an evidence-based medication class at any time point, with never-fill rates of 27.0% for ACEIs, ARBs, or ARNIs as well as 37.8% for β-blockers, 27.5% for P2Y12Is, 9.0% for statins or PCSK9Is, and 90.9% for SGLT2Is or GLP-1RAs.

There were few differences by race. Black beneficiaries had slightly lower prescription fill rates prior to admission, at discharge, and after hospitalization for all medication classes except for β-blockers (eFigure 3 in Supplement 1). There were no clinically meaningful differences in medication prescribing rates by sex and by rural vs urban setting (eFigures 4 and 5 in Supplement 1).

Factors Associated With Not Filling Prescriptions of Guideline-Recommended Cardiovascular Medications

Overall, the factor with the largest point estimate associated with not filling a given cardiovascular medication 91 to 180 days after discharge was not being prescribed that medication at discharge (Table 3). Having a prescription filled at discharge was associated with a significantly reduced risk of not filling this prescription in the 91 to 180 days after hospitalization (adjusted risk ratio [ARR], 0.27 [95% CI, 0.27-0.28] for ACEIs, ARBs, or ARNIs; 0.24 [0.24-0.25] for β-blockers; 0.20 [0.19-0.20] for P2Y12Is; 0.31 [0.31-0.32] for statins or PCSK9Is; and 0.27 [0.26-0.28] for SGLT2Is or GLP-1RAs). This factor had the highest point estimate in association with prescription fills after discharge than all other variables examined, including age, sex, race, rural status, and medical comorbidities (Table 3).

Given the importance of discharge medication status in longer-term medication use, we examined factors associated with medication use at the time of discharge. Similarly, we found that the risk factor with the highest point estimate associated with not filling a cardiovascular medication at discharge was not taking it at admission (Table 4). Beneficiaries with prescription fills prior to admission were less likely to not be taking these medications after discharge from hospitalization for MI or coronary revascularization (ARR, 0.41 [95% CI, 0.41-0.42] for ACEIs, ARBs, or ARNIs; 0.35 [0.34-0.35] for β-blockers; 0.51 [0.50-0.52] for P2Y12Is; 0.41 [0.40-0.42] for statins or PCSK9Is; and 0.22 [0.21-0.23] for SGLT2Is or GLP-1RAs). Some comorbidities were associated with higher rates of not taking a medication at discharge, although to a lesser extent than medication status prior to admission. History of atrial fibrillation or flutter was associated with increased risk of not filling a prescription for P2Y12Is (ARR, 1.44 [95% CI, 1.41-1.47]). Kidney disease was associated with increased risk of not filling a prescription for an ACEI, ARB, or ARNI at discharge (ARR, 1.39 [95% CI, 1.37-1.41]) or a statin or PCSK9I at discharge (1.22 [1.19-1.25]). A history of dementia was associated with not filling prescriptions at discharge across multiple classes (ARR, 1.10 [95% CI, 1.08-1.13] for β-blockers; 1.29 [1.25-1.32] for P2Y12Is; and 1.23 [1.18-1.28] for statins or PCSK9Is) (Table 4). A history of coronary heart disease demonstrated the opposite pattern: individuals with this diagnosis were less likely not to fill prescriptions for an ACEI, ARB, or ARNI (ARR, 0.75 [95% CI, 0.73-0.78]), β-blocker (0.73 [0.71-0.76]), P2Y12Is (0.52 [0.49-0.55]), and statins or PCSK9Is (0.66 [0.61-0.71]).

Discussion

Evidence gathered in this large retrospective analysis of Medicare beneficiaries suggests an opportunity to improve guideline-recommended cardiovascular medication prescriptions during hospitalizations for MI or coronary revascularization among individuals with T2D. In this study, prescription fills for evidence-based cardiovascular medication increased modestly between admission and discharge and decreased in the 91 to 180 days after discharge across medication classes, except for SGLT2Is and GLP-1RAs, which increased only a small amount. A substantial proportion of the study population with T2D and ACS who were hospitalized for an MI or coronary revascularization never filled a prescription for a given medication class at any time point. The risk factor with the highest point estimate associated with not taking a cardiovascular medication in the longer term after discharge was not taking that medication at the time of discharge. Comorbidities played a smaller role, with decreased medication use associated with atrial fibrillation, kidney disease, and dementia for some medication classes.

Compared with prior literature on all-comers with and without T2D, prescription fills for cardiovascular medications at discharge were lower in our cohort. Other contemporary analyses of medication use following hospitalization for MI among those with and without T2D reported use rates of more than 82% to 96% for β-blockers,17,18 83% to 90% for statins,17,18,19 and 89% for P2Y12Is20 around the time of discharge. These rates are higher than those of prescription fills in our study of individuals with T2D only. This may be related to differences in the comorbidity burden in our study population,20 variability in medication access between cohorts,17 or the way in which medication use was defined (eg, any prescription written vs prescription filled in our analysis).18,19 Regardless, those with T2D are known to be at higher risk of complications during hospitalization for MI and at higher risk of mortality following MI compared with individuals without T2D.6 Thus, these findings suggest that it is critical that protective cardiovascular medications are used when clinically appropriate for this high-risk group.

Cardiovascular medication prescription fills at discharge were associated with reduced risk of not taking that medication after hospitalization in our analysis. Other studies have also demonstrated the importance of in-hospital or peridischarge initiation of preventive medications after MI. Early initiation is associated with higher rates of long-term use and improved outcomes.21,22 As shown in our cohort, the discharge period following a hospitalization for MI or revascularization represents a critical time to optimize evidence-based medication use for individuals with T2D.

Comorbidities had a smaller association with cardiovascular prescription fills in our analysis. A diagnosis of atrial fibrillation or flutter was associated with slightly higher rates of not filling a P2Y12I, perhaps related to bleeding complications in the setting of anticoagulation. Kidney disease, likewise, was associated with increased risk of not filling ACEI, ARB, or ARNI prescriptions, perhaps related to changing kidney function during or after hospitalization. Beneficiaries with dementia were less likely to fill prescriptions for multiple medication classes, which is consistent with other literature on secondary prevention medication use among those with cognitive impairment,23 and may be the result of judicious medication use among a cohort with greater frailty. A diagnosis of coronary artery disease was, however, associated with a lower risk of not filling a cardiovascular medication after discharge across multiple medication classes. We suspect this is because patients who carried a baseline diagnosis of coronary artery disease were more likely to be treated with cardiovascular medications prior to admission (which was also associated with postdischarge medication use).

Although we found no difference in cardiovascular prescription fills by sex or rurality, we did find lower rates of cardiovascular prescription fills among Black individuals in our cohort before and after hospitalization. This finding is consistent with other literature, which has demonstrated reduced rates of guideline-directed medical therapy among Black adults hospitalized for MI.24 Further work is needed to address these disparities in care.

Limitations

This study should be interpreted in the context of its limitations. First, we examined prescription fill records from Medicare Part D data; this does not necessarily represent medication adherence, because filled prescriptions may not always be taken. Second, these data describe medication initiation and discontinuation but do not capture medication titration, which likely also happened during and after MI hospitalization. Third, it is possible that some beneficiaries were not eligible for treatment with all of the cardiovascular medications described in this dataset at every time point. Some individuals may not have a class I indication for certain therapies, and some may have had (or developed) contraindications to use of these agents over time. Although we present some information on comorbidity status, there may be other intolerances or barriers not captured in our analysis. This may account for some decrease in the use of these medications over time, but it likely does not account for the entirety of the low prescription rates seen. Finally, as with all observational research, there may be other factors not captured in this analysis that affected medication prescription fill rates within our cohort.

Conclusions

In this cohort study of Medicare beneficiaries with T2D hospitalized for MI or coronary revascularization, we found a modest increase in evidence-based cardiovascular medication use following hospitalization, which persisted in the 91 to 180 days after discharge. Medication use at the time of discharge was associated with longer-term medication use after discharge, highlighting the critical importance of this time period to optimize preventive care for these high-risk individuals. Further implementation science research is needed to develop strategies to improve use of these evidence-based medications.
