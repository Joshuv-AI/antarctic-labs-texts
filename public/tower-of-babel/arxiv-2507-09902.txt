Tie-breaking Agnostic Lower Bound for Fictitious Play
Yuanhao Wang

Abstract
Fictitious play (FP) is a natural learning dynamic in two-player zero-sum games. Samuel Karlin conjectured in 1959 that FP converges at a rate of O ( t − 1 / 2 ) O(t^{-1/2}) to Nash equilibrium, where t t is the number of steps played. However, Daskalakis and Pan disproved the stronger form of this conjecture in 2014, where adversarial tie-breaking is allowed. This paper disproves Karlin’s conjecture in its weaker form. In particular, there exists a 10 10 -by- 10 10 zero-sum matrix game, in which FP converges at a rate of Ω ( t − 1 / 3 ) \Omega(t^{-1/3}) , and no ties occur except for the first step.



1
Introduction

A two–player zero–sum game can be written as the bilinear saddle–point problem

\max_{x\in\Delta_{n}}\min_{y\in\Delta_{m}}x^{\top}Ay,

(1)

where
A\in[-1,1]^{n\times m}
is the payoff matrix and
\Delta_{k}
denotes the
k
–dimensional simplex. Von Neumann’s celebrated minimax theorem
(
v. Neumann 1928
)
guarantees the value of (
1
) and the existence of a
Nash equilibrium

(x^{\star},y^{\star})
satisfying

x^{\top}Ay^{\star}\;\leq\;(x^{\star})^{\top}Ay^{\star}\;\leq\;(x^{\star})^{\top}Ay,\qquad\forall\,x\in\Delta_{n},\;y\in\Delta_{m}.

Computing such an equilibrium remains a central problem in game theory, online learning, and optimization.

One of the earliest algorithms for computing a Nash equilibrium is
fictitious play
(FP)
(
Brown 1951
)
. FP is an iterative procedure, where in every iteration, each player plays the best response to the empirical mixture of the opponent’s past actions. More specifically, the update rule at round
t
is
1
1

1

Here the initial points are
x_{0}=\mathbf{0}
,
y_{0}=\mathbf{0}
. Note that
\|x_{t}\|_{1}=\|y_{t}\|_{1}=t
.

\displaystyle\begin{cases}i_{t}&\leftarrow\argmax_{i}(Ay_{t-1})[i]\\
x_{t}&\leftarrow x_{t-1}+e[i_{t}]\\
j_{t}&\leftarrow\argmin_{j}(A^{\top}x_{t-1})[j]\\
y_{t}&\leftarrow y_{t-1}+e[j_{t}]\end{cases}.

(2)

The averaged play
(x_{t}/t,\,y_{t}/t)
is guaranteed to converge to Nash equilibrium, but the rate of convergence is delicate and, in spite of seventy years of study, still not completely understood.

The classical convergence analysis
(
Robinson 1951
;
Shapiro 1958
)
implies a convergence rate of
O\bigl(t^{-1/(m+n-2)}\bigr)
. Meanwhile,
Karlin 1959
conjectured the much faster
O\bigl(t^{-1/2}\bigr)
rate. A seminal counterexample by
Daskalakis and Pan 2014
refuted Karlin’s conjecture under
adversarial tie–breaking
; their construction achieves
\Omega\bigl(t^{-1/n}\bigr)
convergence in the
n\times n
identity matrix. However, their construction heavily relies on a time-varying adaptive tie-breaking rule. In fact, it has been shown by
Abernethy et al. 2021
that for diagonal payoff matrices, which include the identity matrix, the convergence rate is upperbounded by
O(t^{-1/2})
under
lexicographic tie-breaking
.

Consequently, the following weaker form of Karlin’s conjecture has remained open:

Does fictitious play converge at the
O(t^{-1/2})
rate when ties are broken lexicographically, or when no ties occur at all?

This paper resolves the question negatively. A
10\times 10
matrix game is constructed in which FP, regardless of the tie-breaking rule, has a duality gap lower bounded by
\Omega(t^{-1/3})
at the
t
-th iteration.

1.1
Related work

Analysis of Fictitious Play

Harris 1998
gave an alternative proof of FP convergence by analyzing the continuous-time version of fictitious play. However, the proof is asymptotic in nature and does not imply a finite time rate. The fast
O(1/t)
rate of continuous-time fictitious play also highlights a large gap between the continuous and discrete time algorithms. For general-sum games, FP is not guaranteed to converge to the Nash equilibrium
(
Gaunersdorfer and Hofbauer 1995
)
, except for special classes of games, such as
2\times n
games
(
Berger 2005
)
.

Best-response oracle

FP belongs to a class of game-solving algorithms that access the game through best-response oracles. Indeed, as observed by
Gidel et al. 2017
, fictitious play can be interpreted as an online Frank-Wolfe method with step size
1/(t+1)
. This property made the algorithm amenable to extensions to learning Nash equilibrium in extensive-form games
(
Heinrich et al. 2015
)
and with neural networks
(
Heinrich and Silver 2016
)
.

Preliminaries

2.1
Notations

For a pair of mixed strategies
(x,y)
, the duality gap is defined as

\mathrm{Gap}_{A}(x,y):=\max_{\hat{x}\in\Delta_{n}}\hat{x}^{\top}Ay-\min_{\hat{y}\in\Delta_{m}}x^{\top}A\hat{y}.

The subscript will be omitted when the payoff matrix referred to is clear from context. It is known that
\mathrm{Gap}(x,y)\geq 0
and
\mathrm{Gap}(x,y)=0
if and only if
(x,y)
is a Nash equilibrium.

For a vector
w
, we use
w[i]
to refer to its
i
-th entry. We will use
\max\{w\}
to denote
\max_{i}w[i]
, and
\argmax\{w\}
to denote
\argmax_{i}w[i]
.
t
and
k
will be reserved for integer indices. In addition, we define
{\overrightarrow{k}}:=[k^{2};k;1]^{\top}
to simplify the presentation of quadratic polynomials.

2.2
Symmetric games

Symmetric games are zero-sum matrix games where
A=-A^{\top}
. In a symmetric game, assuming the same tie-breaking method is applied to both
x
and
y
players, one could immediately see that
x_{t}=y_{t}
for all
t
. Therefore, FP can be simplified as (for
t=1,\cdots
):

\displaystyle\begin{cases}i_{t}&\leftarrow\arg\max\{Ax_{t-1}\}\\
x_{t}&\leftarrow x_{t-1}+e[i_{t}]\end{cases}.

(3)

Equivalently, one can track the change of
U_{t}:=Ax_{t}
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}i_{t}&\leftarrow\arg\max\{U_{t-1}\}\\
U_{t}&\leftarrow U_{t-1}+A[:,i_{t}]\end{cases}.

(4)

The construction presented in this paper is a symmetric game. Thus, the simplified updates (
3
) and (
4
) will always be used. In a symmetric game, the duality gap can be expressed as

\mathrm{Gap}_{A}(x,x)=\max\{Ax\}-\min\{-Ax\}=2\max\{Ax\}.

This enables us to use (
4
) and track the convergence rate through the growth of
\max\{U_{t}\}
: if
\max\{U_{t}\}=\Theta(t^{a})
, then
\mathrm{Gap}(x_{t}/t,x_{t}/t)=\Theta(t^{a-1})
.

An important fact about FP that is made obvious by (
4
) is that
\max\{U_{t}\}
grows monotonically.

Fact 1
.

\forall t\geq 0
,
\max\{U_{t+1}\}\geq\max\{U_{t}\}
. In particular,
\max\{U_{t+1}\}>\max\{U_{t}\}
only if
i_{t+2}\neq i_{t+1}
.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\max\{U_{t+1}\}=U_{t+1}[i_{t+2}]\geq U_{t+1}[i_{t+1}]=U_{t}[i_{t+1}]+A[i_{t+1},i_{t+1}]=\max\{U_{t}\}.

Here
A[i_{t+1},i_{t+1}]=0
since
A=-A^{\top}
.
∎

4
A Hard Instance for Fictitious Play

This section gives the full construction of the hard
9\times 9
matrix game that attains the
\tilde{\Omega}(t^{-1/3})
lower bound. Building on the periodic structure exhibited in the
3\times 3
rock-paper-scissors game, we embed three coupled copies into a larger
9\times 9
game so that FP follows a double-loop trajectory:

1.

an inner loop in which FP plays one of the RPS instances for
\Theta(t^{2/3})
steps;

2.

an outer where the “active” RPS copy transitions from block 1 to block 2 to block 3, and back to block 1.

The benefit of a double-loop structure is that the three RPS instances will interact with each other in a way that “cancels” the progress of each other. This ensures that in every outer loop block,
\max\{U_{t}\}
increases by
\sqrt{t^{2/3}}=t^{1/3}
– as if the FP was re-initialized every time the RPS instance is played.

This section is organized as follows. Sec
4.1
will provide the motivation for the sufficient conditions of a counterexample. Sec
4.2
presents one solution of the sufficient condition. Sec
4.3
will prove the lower bound.

4.1
Deriving the instance

The
9\times 9
game will be a block matrix that contains three RPS instances that interact with each other in a cyclical symmetric way:

M=\begin{pmatrix}A_{\mathrm{rps}}&B&-B\\
-B&A_{\mathrm{rps}}&B\\
B&-B&A_{\mathrm{rps}}\end{pmatrix}.

(9)

To ensure that
M=-M^{\top}
, the
3\times 3
interaction matrix
B
needs to be symmetric. This is the key part of the hard instance which needs to be designed such that FP on
M
has a double-loop phased structured.

To be more precise, the desired effect is a phased structure shown in Fig.
1
. Here

V_{1}

[T_{k},T_{k+1})

It is straightforward to see that if the phased structure in Fig.

(11)

By expressing
