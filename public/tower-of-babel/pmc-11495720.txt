Utility of Clinical Features in Identifying Electrographic Seizures in Hospitalized Patients Admitted for Non-Neurological Diagnoses
Authors: Carolyn Tsai, Courtney Blodgett, Sunghyun Seo, Rizk Alghorazi, Lang Li, Bahjat Qaqish, William J. Powers, Clio Rubinos
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IMPORTANCE:Electrographic seizures (ESz) are seizures without prominent motor activity diagnosed with electroencephalogram and are a common complication in critically ill patients with alterations of consciousness. Previous studies suggested clinical signs, including ocular movement abnormalities, facial/periorbital twitching, or remote seizure risk factors, are sensitive for presence of ESz.OBJECTIVES:To assess the utility of clinical features in identifying ESz in critically ill patients with alterations of consciousness.DESIGN, SETTING, AND PARTICIPANTS:This is a retrospective case-control study of 50 patients admitted to the University of North Carolina (UNC) Medical Center and UNC Rex Hospital. Inpatients older than 18 years old undergoing continuous video electroencephalogram (cEEG) were included. Patients admitted for neurologic diagnoses were excluded. A total of 25 patients with ESz (Sz-EEG) were matched with 25 controls by electroencephalogram duration ± 12 hours (No-Sz-EEG). Elements of patient’s history and physical findings previously shown to be sensitive for presence of ESz were collected. Descriptive statistical analyses were used.RESULTS:Most patients were admitted to medical ICUs (72%; n = 36). There was no difference between groups in clinical findings previously shown to be sensitive for ESz. Positive and negative likelihood ratios for these findings generally fell between 1–2 and 0.5–1, respectively, indicating they are inaccurate predictors for ESz. Patients with ESz had significantly higher mortality (p = 0.012).CONCLUSIONS AND RELEVANCE:Our matched case-control study showed that in the critically ill patient population hospitalized in tertiary care centers and admitted for non-neurologic primary diagnoses, incidence of ocular movement abnormalities, facial/periorbital twitching, and presence of remote risk factors for seizures had low predictive accuracy for ESz. However, these findings are not generalizable to patients with neurologic diseases or to other practice settings with different levels of access to cEEG. We concluded that in this exploratory analysis of hospitalized critically ill patients with non-neurologic diagnoses, these clinical signs did not reliably stratify risk for ESz on cEEG. However, further prospective studies are needed to better evaluate these conclusions.

KEY POINTS

Question: This retrospective case-control study of adults admitted without neurologic diagnoses who underwent continuous video electroencephalogram (cEEG) characterizes the predictive value of clinical features for electrographic seizures (ESz).

Findings: Twenty-five patients with ESz were matched with 25 controls by electroencephalogram duration ± 12 hours. When comparing elements of history and examination, variables previously shown to be sensitive for ESz were not significantly different between the groups.

Meanings: In hospitalized critically ill patients with non-neurologic diagnoses, if there is clinical concern for seizure, a lack of clinical examination features should not dissuade from further evaluation with cEEG.

Electrographic seizures (ESz), also known as nonconvulsive seizures, have been defined as any seizures without prominent motor activity and are a common complication in critically ill patients with various etiologies (1–6). Given the subtle clinical presentation of ESz, they cannot be definitively diagnosed without electroencephalogram (7). Thus, according to the 2015 American Clinical Neurophysiology Society Critical Care Continuous Electroencephalogram Task force consensus statement, continuous video electroencephalogram (cEEG) is recommended to identify ESz in critically ill patients with persistently abnormal mental status after generalized convulsive status epilepticus (SE) or other clinically evident seizures or in patients with fluctuating mental status or unexplained alteration of mental status without known acute brain injury (8). The 2021 American Clinical Neurophysiology Society guidelines outline standardized electrographic criteria for diagnosis once electroencephalogram has been placed (9, 10). One population-based study found the annual incidence of electrographic SE (ESE) to be 12.1 per 100,000 adults, or one third (34.4%) of all SE cases, and that the incidence of ESE has doubled since these guidelines have become available (11). Despite their subtle clinical nature, ESz have been associated with poor prognosis, especially in critically ill or comatose patients, and thus early recognition and treatment are important to improving patient outcomes (1, 2, 4, 6, 11–13).

Previous studies largely including patients with primary neurologic disorders have suggested that some clinical features on neurologic history and examination, such as a combination of ocular movement abnormalities with the presence of remote risk factors for seizures (defined as history of stroke, tumor, prior neurosurgical intervention, dementia, or meningitis) (14) or the presence of facial/periorbital twitching (15) may be more highly associated with the presence of ESz. It has been suggested that these features can predict patients with a higher probability of having ESz, and thus assist in early recognition and triaging of patients for urgent electroencephalogram, especially in resource-limited settings. In this case-control study, we aim to determine the predictive value of various clinical features that are commonly obtained during neurologic consultation for identifying ESz in a predominantly critically ill inpatient population with a non-neurologic primary diagnosis. We aimed to include cases and clinical variables that closely mirrored those commonly encountered by consulting neurologists or neurohospitalists.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient Selection

This study was approved on April 7, 2020 by the University of North Carolina (UNC) Institutional Review Board (IRB) for human research (IRB No. 200998, “Developing criteria for the evaluation and prognosis of patients with encephalopathy and concern for nonconvulsive status epilepticus”). Given its retrospective nature, the IRB waived the need for study specific informed consent. Data collection and storage was performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the IRB and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975. We performed a retrospective chart review of patients who underwent electroencephalogram monitoring on the medical and surgical floors and ICUs between August 1, 2012, and January 1, 2021, at the UNC Medical Center in Chapel Hill and UNC Rex Hospital. These dates included the earliest electronic medical records available at UNC (prior records were not easily searchable) and spanned up until the date of data collection. Adults over age 18 whose primary admission diagnoses were not neurologic were included. All patients admitted to the neurology floor or neurologic ICU, or diagnosed with anoxic brain injury, were excluded.

A portable cEEG monitoring system (Natus NeuroWorks; Natus Medical Incorporated, Middleton, WI) was used with standard 10–20 electrode placement for recording all electroencephalograms. Electroencephalograms were reviewed by one of eight board-certified epileptologists on faculty at UNC (four certified in clinical neurophysiology by the American Board of Clinical Neurophysiology and four certified in epilepsy by the American Board of Psychiatry and Neurology) who generated an electroencephalogram report as part of standard of care. Only the first instance of cEEG monitoring for each patient was included. All patients’ electroencephalogram reports were first screened for the presence of seizures (Fig. 1).

Data Collection

Clinical variables were collected via chart review for the 50 case and control patients and included demographic data such as admission location, age, and gender. Clinical history variables included acute or chronic neuroimaging findings before electroencephalogram (acute defined as onset within 7 d before electroencephalogram placement [16]), presence of sepsis or metabolic dysfunction within 3 days before placement of electroencephalogram, number of previous lifetime seizures, and presence of remote risk factors for seizures (14). Neuroimaging findings included CT or MRI findings. Sepsis was defined according to the criteria described by Cawcutt and Peters (17). The presence of metabolic dysfunction was defined as acute renal failure or hepatic failure, diabetic ketoacidosis, or acute pancreatitis as diagnosed by the primary medical team in the chart, or electrolyte abnormalities per value range laid out by Beghi, et al (16). Variables collected from the initial clinical presentation included the presence of ocular abnormalities (eye deviation, nystagmus, hippus), facial or periorbital/eyelid twitching, extremity movement abnormality, or episodic behavior change. This information was collected from initial history and examination performed by the primary treating team. Variables collected from the neurologic examination included the presence of unresponsiveness, ocular movement abnormalities, eyelid or facial twitching, and extremity movement abnormality. Extremity movement abnormality included tonic-clonic activity, stiffening, shaking, jerking, myoclonus, tremor, convulsion, or any subtle motor activity. The neurology consultation team performed a neurologic examination within 24–72 hours of electroencephalogram placement. For patients who were not seen by neurology, their neurologic examination findings were extracted from the primary care team’s neurologic examination documentation, and thus not all clinical examination variables were available for every patient. If a clinical examination variable was not available for a patient, that patient was excluded from that variable’s counts and analysis. Antiseizure medications (ASMs) used before and after cEEG monitoring, length of ICU stay, and ultimate discharge location (acute care hospital, acute inpatient rehabilitation, subacute nursing facility, home, or death) were also recorded. The Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II score, a validated measure of the severity of a patient’s illness, which takes into account information from the patient’s vitals, blood chemistry, cell count, age, chronic medical conditions, and Glasgow Coma Score, was calculated (18). Clinical data for calculation of the score was obtained from the day closest to first day of electroencephalogram recording. Patient data were recorded in a de-identified secure database.

Statistical Analysis

Analysis was done using statistical measures, including Student t test for numerical data, Fisher exact test for categorical variables, chi-square or Fisher exact (Freeman-Halton extension) test for ordered categorical variables with more than two groups, and positive and negative likelihood ratios (LR+ and LR–). If specific imaging or findings were not available in the chart on review, the patients were excluded from counts, and excluded from the denominator when calculating percentages. Tables 1–3 report uncorrected p values. We planned to use the Holm’s method (19) with a family wise error rate p value of less than 0.05 to correct for increased type 1 error due to multiple comparisons among the 18 candidate predictive clinical variables in Table 1 and Supplemental Table 1 (http://links.lww.com/CCX/B421). However, none of the individual comparisons reached an alpha value of p value of less than 0.05. RESULTS

From a total of 432 patients who had cEEG on nonneurologic units, a total of 50 patients were selected (Fig. 1). Ultimately, 25 patients with seizures based on electroencephalogram reports were identified who met all inclusion and exclusion criteria; these were designated the case or “Sz-EEG” group. Subsequently, 25 patients without seizures (based on electroencephalogram report) were identified and matched to patients in the “Sz-EEG” group by total duration of monitoring within 12 hours; these were designated as the control or “No-Sz-EEG” group.

As summarized in Table 1, there were no significant differences in admission location at the time of electroencephalogram (ICU vs. non-ICU), age, gender, APACHE II score, or average length of stay in the ICU between the two groups.

Table 2 and Supplemental Table 2 (http://links.lww.com/CCX/B421) summarize the history, clinical presentation, and assessed examination findings. All patients in the Sz-EEG group and 23 patients (92%) of no-Sz-EEG group were evaluated by a neurologist. There was no significant difference in any of these findings between the two groups. Notably, there was no significant difference in the incidence of ocular movement abnormalities or facial/periorbital twitching on initial presentation or neurologic examination, and no significant difference in the presence of remote risk factors for seizures in chart history between the Sz-EEG and No-Sz-EEG group. Likelihood ratios (LRs) were calculated to better assess the probability that any of these findings would produce clinically important change in the probability of Sz-EEG. For reference, LR+ greater than or equal to 10 and LR– less than or equal to 0.1 produce large and usually clinically important changes in probability, LR+ 2–5 and LR– 0.2–0.5 produce small changes, and LR+ 1–2 and LR– 0.5–1 produce only very small and rarely clinically relevant changes in probability. Accounting for the wide 95% CIs, LR+ and LR– for all findings fell in the 1–2 or 0.5–1 range, respectively, indicating they are not helpful clinically predictive markers for the presence of seizures on electroencephalogram (20).

Table 3 demonstrates whether patients were treated with ASMs before or after cEEG placement and where patients were ultimately discharged. Before electroencephalogram monitoring, around half of Sz-EEG and No-Sz-EEG patients were started on ASMs (48.1% and 65%, respectively). Almost all (n = 23, 92.3%) of Sz-EEG patients were on ASMs after electroencephalogram monitoring, indicating cEEG influenced patient management. All patients who were started on ASMs before electroencephalogram were continued on ASMs despite negative electroencephalogram results for seizures. Despite there being no significant difference in APACHE II score, indicating the mortality risk due to the level of patients’ medical illness was similar between the two groups (18), there was significantly higher in-hospital mortality in the Sz-EEG group compared with the No-Sz-EEG group (p = 0.012), suggesting the negative influence of ESz on patient outcomes, which has been shown by prior studies (1–4, 12).

DISCUSSION

Our aim in this study was to determine the predictive accuracy of different clinical features commonly obtained during neurologic consultation in identifying ESz in hospitalized patients with primary medical diagnoses. This was done by calculating LRs based on available data, with missing values left out. This reflects real-world practice where situations do not always allow for a detailed neurologic examination or availability of all neuroimaging tests. The 95% confidence bounds of the LRs indicate the precision of the estimates as affected by both the overall sample size of 25 in each group and any missing data. These LRs indicate for each factor how much the presence will raise (LR+) or absence will lower (LR–) the pretest probability of seizures. LRs are not dependent on the prevalence of disease in the evaluated sample. In this study, all of the LR+ were less than 2 and all of the LR– were greater than 0.5 indicating that each of the factors tested changed the probability of ESz only to a very small degree. Furthermore, for LR+ the upper bounds of the 95% CI were less than 5 for nine of 11 factors indicating that they would be unlikely to show clinical relevance even with a larger sample size. Similarly, for LR– the lower bounds of the 95% CI were greater than 0.2 for all 11 factors and greater than 0.5 for eight of 11 factors, again indicating that they would be unlikely to show clinical relevance even with a larger sample size. Additionally, when comparing the incidence of clinical factors between groups, none showed significant differences. Thus, our study illustrates that in the hospitalized, critically ill medical patient population, the presence of clinical features (including ocular movement abnormalities, presence of remote risk factors for seizures, and presence of facial/periorbital twitching) would be unlikely to change the probability of ESz incidence.

Although clinical features may be noted in some patients with ESz (14, 15), they are not sensitive or specific enough to safely triage patients for electroencephalogram when there is clinical concern for ESz. This supports the practice of placing cEEG on all patients where there is clinical concern raised for ESz. Recent work has produced validated risk stratification tools based on electroencephalogram criteria, which can help determine the optimal duration of electroencephalogram monitoring for the detection of ESz (21, 22) and will be especially useful in triaging electroencephalogram in limited resource settings. Continued research in this area is needed to improve management strategies for patients with ESz monitored on long-term cEEG.

Despite clinical features having low utility in making electroencephalogram placement decisions, the neurologic history and examination still maintain a crucial role in diagnosing and managing patients with ESz, and may have utility in determining prognosis. Leitinger et al (11) found that in patients presenting with SE, nonconvulsive semiology at the end of the semiologic sequence conferred a higher risk of mortality than at the beginning of the sequence (25.6% for convulsive to nonconvulsive SE vs. 10% for nonconvulsive to convulsive SE, respectively) and that presence of coma was associated with higher mortality. Thus, attention to clinical presentation timeline and level of consciousness also has prognostic value in the care of patients with ESz.

There were several limitations to our study. First, the retrospective nature of the study limited the history and neurologic examination variables that could be collected to those available in the patient’s chart. This limitation arises from the lack of standardized documentation for specific clinical findings in these patients. Additionally, the retrospective nature of the study limited our ability to definitively determine the clinical features that lead the treating teams to order cEEG. However, this is not unlike real-world situations for many critically ill medical patients. Second, although most patients in the Sz-EEG group were matched by electroencephalogram duration ± 12 hours, four patients were kept on cEEG for over 5 days. In these cases, finding a demographically matched control patient in the No-Sz-EEG group that was also kept on electroencephalogram for over 5 days was challenging. However, the likelihood of ESz detection after 3 days of cEEG monitoring is low (< 7%) (6). Regardless, to minimize confounders these patients were matched with the control patients on electroencephalogram for the longest, which fell between 3 and 4 days in duration. Third, while all electroencephalograms were interpreted by a Clinical Neurophysiology or Epilepsy certified reader reflecting real-world clinical circumstances, we acknowledge that the absence of an adjudication committee limits the consensus opinion of electroencephalogram interpretation. Future studies could enhance this aspect by involving at least two certified readers in electroencephalogram interpretation. Fourth, we recognize that as these findings came from a cohort admitted to an academic center with access to cEEG 24/7, they may not be directly applicable to settings in which cEEG is not as readily available.

Finally, an important limitation to note is the exclusion of patients with primary neurologic diagnoses from this study, which resulted in a small number of overall participants. As most patients monitored on electroencephalogram from 2012 to 2021 in our institution were admitted to the neurology floor or neuroscience ICU with known acute brain injuries or admitted to the epilepsy monitoring unit for seizure diagnosis and characterization, the remaining population after exclusion of these patients was limited. As with all studies, generalizing to other patient populations should be limited to those who are similar to the participants in this study, that is, hospitalized patients with non-neurologic diagnoses with sufficient suspicion for ESz to undergo up to 5 days of electroencephalogram monitoring and even then done with caution. Future multicenter studies on the history and standardization of documentation of neurologic examination features in hospitalized patients with primary medical diagnoses who are monitored with electroencephalogram (regardless of whether electroencephalogram seizure is captured) would help to further characterize this population and clarify the preliminary findings that have been introduced in this study.

Our study focused on patients with primary medical diagnoses who were monitored on electroencephalogram to evaluate for ESz, as this is a population that has not been as well characterized in the literature. The majority of patients, 92% in Sz-EEG group and 80% in No-Sz-EEG group, were either altered or comatose on examination (variable defined as presence of unresponsiveness; Table 2). In our study, the incidence of ESz was 6% (26/432), which is comparable to what has been reported in previous studies of non-neurologic patients monitored on electroencephalogram (8–16%) (2, 4). However, it is lower than the previously reported incidence of ESz in all hospitalized patients, which ranges from 8% to 37% depending on the subpopulation studied (1, 2, 6, 13, 23–25). Including patients with primary neurologic diagnoses could contribute to higher incidence of ESz. Therefore, a higher index of suspicion for ESz and a lower threshold for electroencephalogram placement should be maintained when assessing patients presenting with primary neurologic diagnoses.

A significantly higher proportion of deceased patients on discharge were noted in the Sz-EEG group (p = 0.012), despite no significant difference in APACHE II score at start of electroencephalogram recording. This finding supports previous work regarding the negative association of ESz on patient outcomes (1, 4, 5, 11, 12). It also suggests that ESz independently contribute to negative patient outcomes regardless of underlying severity of medical illness. There was also a significant difference in patients on ASMs after electroencephalogram use in the Sz-EEG group compared with the No-Sz-EEG group (p ≤ 0.001), which supports findings shown in previous work (26, 27). However, this study was not designed to assess if the use of ASMs in these patients affected long-term outcomes. The influence of electroencephalogram findings on ASM management and long-term patient outcomes is an important topic for ongoing research.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, in this study of the hospitalized medical patient population at two academic medical centers with 24/7 cEEG capabilities, clinical features identified on history and neurologic examination by either the primary treating team or the consulting neurology team were not found to significantly influence the pretest probability for incidence of ESz, and thus could not accurately risk stratify patients when making a decision regarding electroencephalogram placement. While these signs remain important in overall clinical care of the patient, our findings suggest the absence of clinical features classically associated with seizure should not dissuade clinicians from placing cEEG when seizure is on the differential diagnosis. The reliability of clinical signs in predicting seizures in this patient population needs to be evaluated further with prospective studies, with a larger cohort, and standardized, detailed, and careful neurologic evaluations. Further studies investigating the influence of electroencephalogram findings on the appropriate duration of cEEG monitoring, clinical management, and outcomes for non-neurologic patients with ESz will better inform future care of this growing population.
