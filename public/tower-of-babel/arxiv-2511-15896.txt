Abstract
Causal inference starts with a simple idea: compare groups that differ by treatment, not much else. Traditionally, similar groups are constructed using only observed covariates; however, it remains a long-standing challenge to incorporate available outcome data into the study design while preserving valid inference. In this paper, we study the general problem of covariate adjustment, effect estimation, and statistical inference when balancing features are constructed or selected with the aid of outcome information from the data. We propose cross-balancing, a method that uses sample splitting to separate the error in feature construction from the error in weight estimation. Our framework addresses two cases: one where the features are learned functions and one where they are selected from a potentially high-dimensional dictionary. In both cases, we establish mild and general conditions under which cross-balancing produces consistent, asymptotically normal, and efficient estimators. In the learned-function case, cross-balancing achieves finite-sample bias reduction relative to plug-in-type estimators, and is multiply robust when the learned features converge at slow rates. In the variable-selection case, cross-balancing only requires a product condition on how well the selected variables approximate true functions. We illustrate cross-balancing in extensive simulations and an observational study, showing that careful use of outcome information can substantially improve both estimation and inference while maintaining interpretability.



Keywords:
Causal Inference; Observational Studies; Weighting Methods; Variable Selection

Introduction

1.1

Using outcome information for weighting

Causal inference begins with a simple idea: compare groups that differ by treatment, but little else.
In observational studies, this requires adjusting for differences between treatment groups, a process known as balancing covariates.
A classic approach uses the propensity score---the probability of receiving treatment given observed covariates
(
rosenbaum1983central
)
---to achieve balance.
However, because this approach ensures balance only asymptotically, more recent methods also focus on balancing the covariates directly.
Examples include the approaches by
hainmueller2012entropy
,
zubizarreta2015stable
, and
chan2016globally
, among others; see
ben2021balancing
for a review.

These balancing weighting methods have several appealing features.
First, they are transparent and easy to interpret, as they aim to compare ‘‘apples to apples.’’
Second, by mimicking certain aspects of randomized experiments, their construction can be seen as part of the design stage of an observational study, as they use only pre-treatment information, preserving the objectivity of the analysis and permitting valid inference
(
rubin2007design
)
.
Furthermore, the resulting estimators often admit a linear representation, enabling straightforward diagnostics where each unit receives a distinct weight
(
3
)
.

Despite its appeal, a fundamental question concerns which covariates one should select for balance.
This choice is central to the estimator’s performance and involves a careful trade-off between model expressiveness and interpretability.
This choice is governed by the dual role of the balancing weights, as they eliminate finite-sample bias in the part of the outcome model attributable to the selected features, and implicitly define a propensity score model based on them
(
imai2014covariate
;
zhao2017entropy
;
wang2020minimal
)
.
Although this decision process often relies on domain expertise, the complexity of modern datasets makes it desirable to complement subjective knowledge with data-driven approaches to determine which covariate adjustments to make for accurate causal estimates.

This raises, in turn, a broader challenge: how to leverage not only observed covariates but also outcome information in a manner that can be considered part of the study design and yields valid inferences.
Leveraging outcome information holds much unrealized potential, as imbalances in the prognostic score---the expected outcome given the covariates
(
hansen2008prognostic
)
---can translate directly into treatment effect estimation bias
(
10
)
.
Indeed, methods that incorporate outcome information, such as outcome kernels
(
zhao2019covariate
)
, the outcome-adaptive lasso
(
9
)
, and small planning samples for the design of observational studies
(
4
)
, have proven effective.

Two natural ideas emerge for constructing balancing features using outcome information:

(i)

Learn features as functions of raw covariates
: following the outcome modeling principle, learning the true regression function (the prognostic score) using flexible prediction methods can help address finite-sample bias in the outcomes due to imbalance. The resulting learned function can then be used as a balancing feature.

(ii)

Select features from a candidate set:
starting with raw covariates and their transformations (e.g., quadratic or kernel functions), one can use variable selection methods to find features most relevant to the outcome. Using the selected variables as the balancing features can help reduce complexity and improve interpretability.

However, these approaches pose several design and analysis challenges. It is often unclear which properties of the constructed features ensure accurate estimation and inference.
Using the same data for both feature construction and weight estimation also introduces ‘‘double-dipping’’ bias, compromising the validity of the inferences.
Moreover, because these methods use outcome information at the design stage, they may further raise concerns about the objectivity of the study.
The core challenge is to account for learning and selection errors to ensure accurate estimation and valid inference, while maintaining the interpretability and transparency of the balancing approach.
Technically, the nature of statistical errors differs: in (i), the uncertainty comes from the randomness in learned functions, while in (ii), it arises from selecting a random subset of features, which is inherently discrete.

1.2

Overview of cross-balancing

In this paper, we study the general problem of covariate adjustment, effect estimation, and statistical inference when balancing features are constructed in a data-driven manner.
To address the aforementioned challenges, we propose cross-balancing, a method based on sample splitting that disentangles the estimation error in feature construction and selection from that in weight estimation, and achieves asymptotic optimality under relatively weak conditions.
This method builds on the intuitive idea of randomly splitting a dataset into two parts
(
chernozhukov2018double
)
, so that knowledge gained from one part can be applied to the other, and their results are subsequently integrated.
In this way, we leverage the outcomes as an additional source of information that is typically not utilized in balancing.

Figure 1
:
Diagram of the cross-balancing process.

As depicted in Figure
1
, cross-balancing proceeds in two stages: in the first stage, features are built using one fold of the data; and in the second stage, a balancing algorithm is applied to the other fold to obtain the weights.
The roles of the two folds are then interchanged to obtain the weights of the first fold.
This method is flexible, as the feature learning/selecting stage can leverage any black-box prediction model to learn the prognostic score, or can employ any variable selection method to find a subset of relevant features to balance.
By not restricting to any specific algorithm in the learning/selecting stage, we characterize the general conditions on the quality of data-driven features, allowing slow convergence rates, under which cross-balancing yields consistent estimation, valid inference, and asymptotic efficiency. Along the way, we also show the finite-sample bias reduction and multiple robustness due to the unique calibration property of balancing weights.
Our analysis provides practical guidance in building data-driven balancing features in both cases: while a consistent prognostic score suffices for a consistent estimator, including treatment-relevant features protects against misspecification and yields valid inference under mild conditions.

As discussed, a useful principle in observational studies is to approximate the randomized experiment that would ideally have been conducted under similar conditions. In cross-balancing, this principle is maintained, as in concept there are two hypothetical experiments that inform each other by using the outcome information from the other experiment. In this sense, one fold serves as the ‘‘pilot study’’ for the other
(
4
)
.
Importantly, sample splitting directly addresses the ‘‘double-dipping’’ bias that arises when the same data is used both for feature construction and weight estimation, thereby ensuring the validity of inferences.
In doing so, cross-balancing preserves the interpretability and transparency of the balancing approach, while leveraging the power of modern machine learning tools.

1.3

Related literature and outline

There has been much work on what variables should be adjusted when estimating treatment effects. At the center of these proposals, either implicitly or explicitly, is the propensity score. This is because the propensity score provides the most concise summary of the data that balances all covariates; and conversely, balancing all covariates requires estimating this single propensity score. In practice, researchers typically aim to balance as many variables as possible
(
rosenbaum1995overt
)
. However, when only a subset of variables is relevant to the treatment, including more than the necessary variables leads to ‘‘overadjustment’’
(
7
)
. The underlying principle is that when the true model is smaller (involving a smaller set of variables), it is more efficient to only adjust for the relevant subset.

This reasoning leads us to focus on variables that play a key role in the outcome model. A covariate is worth adjusting for only if it is related to the outcome, as otherwise the imbalance in this covariate would not incur confounding bias in the outcomes. Following this logic,
8
use the covariate-outcome relationship to inform variable selection in propensity score methods. Through simulations,
1
suggest that including variables related only to the outcome may improve efficiency.
9
propose outcome adaptive lasso for selecting covariates for inclusion in propensity score models. Similarly, balance in the prognostic score can be used as an effective metric to assess the quality of propensity score models
(
10
)
.

Moving beyond propensity score only methods, other approaches employ outcome regression to augment the propensity score model, resulting in the semiparametrically efficient AIPW estimator
(
robins1994estimation
)
. Its double robustness property makes it well suited to use machine learning for both propensity and outcome models, each of which individually can converge at slow rates, yet their combination enables fast rates (e.g.,
chernozhukov2018double
,
bruns2025augmented
,
yang2025outcome
). Given this general property, it is natural to include all variables to fit these models, but there is room for improvement by focusing on relevant variables. Along this line,
belloni2014inference
and
5
show that the ‘‘double selection’’ strategy, which selects variables related to either outcome or treatment, yields favorable performance in high-dimensional settings.
Cross-balancing echoes this line of research, and it turns out that selecting features relevant to treatment and outcome is important for asymptotic optimality under relatively weak conditions.

Section

Section
6
concludes with a case study.

Setup

2.1

Estimand and assumptions

We begin with the standard observational study setting, where each subject is described by observed covariates
X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, a treatment indicator
T_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, and an observed outcome
Y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
, such that the triplets
\{(X_{i},T_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
are independent and identically distributed.
We define causal effects in terms of potential outcomes,
Y_{i}(1)
if treated and
Y_{i}(0)
if control
(
neyman1923application
;
rubin1974estimating
)
, and assume the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA), so that
Y_{i}=Y_{i}(T_{i})=T_{i}Y_{i}(1)+(1-T_{i})Y_{i}(0)

(
rubin1980randomization
)
.
We also require the following conditions on the treatment assignment
(
rosenbaum1983central
)
.

Assumption 2.1
.

Treatment assignment is unconfounded given observed covariates:
T\perp\!\!\!\perp

\{Y(1),Y(0)\}{\,|\,}X
. Each individual has a positive probability of receiving either treatment:
0<e(x)<1
for all
x
, where
e(x)=\mathbb{P}(T=1|X=x)
is the propensity score.

Our main objective is to estimate the counterfactual mean
\theta_{0}=\mathbb{E}[Y(0)|T=1]
.
This is the building block for the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT), as

\mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)|T=1]=\mathbb{E}[Y|T=1]-\theta_{0}
.
Because we rely on information about
Y(0)
from control subjects to estimate the counterfactuals for the treated, the primary challenge under Assumption
2.1
is to properly adjust for imbalances in observed covariates between the two groups.

Although our focus is causal inference, the same issues arise when transferring results from one population to another.
Suppose we observe
(X_{i},Y_{i})
from a source distribution
P
, and covariates
X_{j}
from a target distribution
Q
, and our objective is to estimate the mean outcome in the target population,
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[Y]
.
We consider a classical transfer problem under covariate shift, assuming the distributions differ only in
X
.
Denote by
w(x)
the (unknown) density ratio relating the two covariate distributions, so

\frac{dQ}{dP}(x,y)=w(x)
.
The methods we present for adjustment and inference in causal studies are equally suited to this setting.

Define the prognostic score
m_{0}(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y(0)\mid X=x]
, and the covariate shift between the treated and control groups through the weight function

w^{*}(x)=\frac{e(x)}{1-e(x)}\cdot\frac{\mathbb{P}(T=0)}{\mathbb{P}(T=1)}.

The estimand of interest can be represented in two equivalent forms

\theta_{0}=\mathbb{E}[w^{*}(X)Y\mid T=0]=\mathbb{E}[m_{0}(X)\mid T=1].

This forms the basis of several seminal approaches to estimating
\theta_{0}
, including inverse propensity weighting (IPW), which estimates
e(x)
and reweights control units, and outcome regression which models
m_{0}(x)
and averages the predictions over treated units; combining them leads to the doubly robust augmented IPW (AIPW) estimator.

2.2

Minimal weights

Within the weighting framework, one approach to construct weights is to directly estimate the inverse propensity score by solving an optimization problem of the form

\displaystyle\arg\min_{w}\left\{\mathcal{D}(w):\,w\in\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{A}\right\},

(1)

where
\mathcal{D}
is a measure of dispersion of the weights,
\mathcal{B}
are general balance conditions, and
\mathcal{A}
are weight adjustment considerations.
This approach allows for the construction of various types of weights, such as those in
hainmueller2012entropy
,
zubizarreta2015stable
, and
chan2016globally
.
See
wang2020minimal
for an analysis of the asymptotic properties and practical considerations of such weights (
1
).
In what follows, we restrict
\mathcal{D}
to the variance of the weights in order to control the variance of a linear estimator, define
\mathcal{B}
via finite-sample balancing conditions on a vector of transformations of the raw features, and set
\mathcal{A}
to ensure that the estimator is translation invariant.
That is, we solve

\displaystyle\widehat{w}=\arg\min_{w}\left\{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c}}w_{i}^{2}:\,\bigg|\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c}}w_{i}\widehat{\phi}_{k}(X_{i})-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{t}}\widehat{\phi}_{k}(X_{i})\bigg|\leq\delta_{k},k\in\mathcal{K};\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c}}w_{i}=1\right\},

(2)

where
\widehat{\phi}_{k}(X_{i})
are the
balance features
, which are transformations of the covariates (we emphasize that they can be data-dependent to fit in our framework),
\delta_{k}
are the tolerances, and
\mathcal{I}_{t},\mathcal{I}_{c}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
are the index sets of treated and control units, respectively.

The key challenge we address is choosing the functions
\widehat{\phi}_{k}
in a data-dependent fashion while enabling reliable estimation and inference (though the exact form of our balancing optimization program differs from (
2
) by procedure design).
We present methods and theory of cross-balancing when
\widehat{\phi}_{k}
’s are learned transformations of the raw covariates in Section
3
and when
\widehat{\phi}_{k}
’s are a selected subset of (a dictionary of) features in Section
4
.

Notation.

We close the section with the notation used throughout the paper.
We use the standard symbols
o(\cdot)
,
o_{P}(\cdot)
, and
O_{P}(\cdot)
, where
X_{n}=o_{P}(1)
means
X_{n}\xrightarrow{P}0
and

means
\{X_{n}\}
is stochastically bounded.
We also use
\Omega(\cdot)
to denote the inverse of stochastic boundedness where
X_{n}=\Omega(a_{n})
if
X_{n}/a_{n}
is bounded away from zero in probability. For a fixed function
f(\cdot)
, we denote its
L_{2}
-norm under a distribution
P
by
\|f\|_{L_{2}(P)}:=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}[f(X)^{2}]
.
For a vector
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we denote

as its Euclidean norm,
\|\eta\|_{1}:=\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\eta_{j}|

as its
L_{\infty}
-norm.

3

Cross-balancing with learned features

As discussed, there has long been interest in leveraging outcome information such as the estimated prognostic score, since it provides the most succinct summary of the relationship between outcome and covariates
(
rubin2000combining
)
.
According to
hansen2008prognostic
, however, ‘‘same-sample estimation of the prognostic score tends to make inference less reliable’’ due to overfitting.
This challenge persists with balancing weights.
In this section, we introduce the cross-balancing method when estimated prognostic scores and other learned transformations of the raw covariates are used as balancing features, analyze its consistency and inferential properties, and provide recommendations on the choice of such features.

3.1

Procedure

The procedure begins by randomly dividing the control and treated indices into two equal-sized folds,
\mathcal{I}_{c}=\mathcal{I}_{c,1}\cup\mathcal{I}_{c,2}
and
\mathcal{I}_{t}=\mathcal{I}_{t,1}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,2}
, respectively.
Using data from the first fold,
\mathcal{I}_{c,1}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,1}
, we estimate a set of features
\widehat{\phi}^{(2)}(\cdot)
which will be applied to the second fold
\mathcal{I}_{c,2}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,2}
.
The process is repeated in the other direction: we estimate
\widehat{\phi}^{(1)}(\cdot)
on data from
\mathcal{I}_{c,2}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,2}
and apply it to
\mathcal{I}_{c,1}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,1}
.
For notational convenience, we let
\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})=\widehat{\phi}^{(1)}(X_{i})
for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,1}
and
\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})=\widehat{\phi}^{(2)}(X_{i})
for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,2}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,2}
.

The weights
\widehat{w}_{i}
for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}
are obtained by solving the problem analogous to (
2
):

\hskip-7.11317pt\small\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{w}\Bigg\{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}w_{i}^{2}:\,\bigg|\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}w_{i}\widehat{\phi}_{k}(X_{i})-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{t}}\widehat{\phi}_{k}(X_{i})\bigg|\leq\delta_{k},k\in\mathcal{K};\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}w_{i}=1\Bigg\}.

(3)

Similarly, we compute
\widehat{w}_{i}
for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,2}
by solving the same optimization problem on the corresponding folds.
The estimator for the mean potential outcome is then

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{0}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c}}\widehat{w}_{i}Y_{i}.

Throughout this section, we assume that each
\widehat{\phi}
maps to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for some fixed
d\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, and consider the asymptotic regime where
n\rightarrow\infty
with
d
held fixed.
When appropriate, the program in (
3
) can be modified to enforce nonnegativity on the weights, which can improve the interpretability and robustness of our method.

3.2

Convergence analysis

We first establish the convergence of the weights and the resulting estimator.

Theorem 3.1

(Convergence and consistency)
.

Suppose
\delta_{n}=o(1)
and
\|\widehat{\phi}^{(k)}-\phi^{*}\|_{L_{2}}=o_{P}(1)
for some fixed function
\phi^{*}\colon\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\mathbb{E}[\phi^{*}(X)\phi^{*}(X)^{\top}\mid T=0]
is full rank, for
k=1,2
.
Then the cross-balancing estimator satisfies
\widehat{\theta}_{0}=\overline{\theta}_{0}+o_{P}(1)
, where
\overline{\theta}_{0}=\mathbb{E}[\overline{w}(X)Y(0)\mid T=0]
and
\overline{w}(x)=\phi^{*}(x)^{\top}\overline{\lambda}
for some fixed
\overline{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Moreover,
\overline{\theta}_{0}=\theta_{0}
, and thus
\widehat{\theta}_{0}
is consistent, provided that at least one of the following holds:

(i)

(Treatment model.) There exists a vector
\lambda^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that
\frac{e(x)}{1-e(x)}\propto\phi^{*}(x)^{\top}\lambda^{*}
.

(ii)

(Outcome model.) There exists a vector
\beta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that
m_{0}(x)=\phi^{*}(x)^{\top}\beta^{*}
.

The proof of Theorem
3.1
relies on convex analysis and is provided in Appendix
B.1
.
The first part of the theorem establishes convergence of the cross-balancing estimator to some
\overline{\theta}_{0}
under arbitrary rates of convergence for the learned features.
The second part connects to existing work on model double robustness in balancing estimators
(
zhao2017entropy
;
wang2020minimal
)
, showing that balancing weighting methods implicitly estimate two models: one for the prognostic score and another for the inverse propensity score, where the model class is linear in
\widehat{\phi}(\cdot)
.
Unlike existing work, allowing
\widehat{\phi}(\cdot)
to be learned from a separate fold increases the expressiveness and robustness of the estimator.

Theorem
3.1
further suggests that any model consistent for the true prognostic score suffices for consistent estimation; however, to maximize robustness to model misspecification,
\widehat{\phi}
should ideally approximate both the true weights and the prognostic score.
A straightforward idea is to fit models for the propensity and prognostic scores, set
\widehat{w}^{(k)}(x):=\widehat{e}^{(k)}(x)/\{1-\widehat{e}^{(k)}(x)\}
and
\widehat{m}^{(k)}(x)
for
m_{0}(x)
, and then take
\widehat{\phi}^{(k)}(x)=\{\widehat{m}^{(k)}(x),\widehat{w}^{(k)}(x)\}
.
By Theorem
3.1
,
\widehat{\theta}_{0}
is consistent if either
\widehat{m}^{(k)}(x)
is consistent for
m_{0}(x)
or
\widehat{e}^{(k)}(x)
is consistent for
e(x)
, or if any linear combination of the two converges to either
m_{0}(x)
or
e(x)
.
This form of double robustness naturally motivates a comparison with the AIPW estimator, which uses the same two models with cross-fitting
(
chernozhukov2018double
)
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{0}^{\text{AIPW}}:=\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c}}\widehat{w}(X_{i})\{Y_{i}-\widehat{m}(X_{i})\}+\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t}}\widehat{m}(X_{j}).

Interestingly, our cross-balancing estimator has a finite-sample bias reduction property when compared to the AIPW estimator.
To see this, let
\Pi^{(k)}_{\widehat{\phi}}
denote the projection residual operator onto the linear span of
\widehat{\phi}
.
Specifically, for any function
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, define

\Pi^{(k)}_{\widehat{\phi}}[f](X_{i}):=f(X_{i})-\widehat{\eta}^{\top}\widehat{\phi}^{(k)}(X_{i})
for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,k},

where

\widehat{\eta}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\eta}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,k}}\big\{f(X_{i})-\eta^{\top}\widehat{\phi}^{(k)}(X_{i})\big\}^{2}
is the vector of ordinary least squares coefficients. The proof of Proposition
3.2
is in Appendix
B.2
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose
\widehat{\phi}^{(k)}
includes both
\widehat{w}^{(k)}(\cdot)
and
\widehat{m}^{(k)}(\cdot)
, each estimated using the held-out fold, and
\delta=0
.
Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\widehat{\theta}_{0}^{\textnormal{AIPW}}-\theta_{0}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{k=1}^{2}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,k}}\big(\widehat{w}^{(k)}-w^{*}\big)(X_{i})\cdot\big(\widehat{m}^{(k)}-m_{0}\big)(X_{i})\bigg].

(4)

In contrast, the cross-balancing estimator obeys

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\widehat{\theta}_{0}-\theta_{0}\big]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{k=1}^{2}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,k}}\Pi^{(k)}_{\widehat{\phi}}\big[\widehat{w}^{(k)}-w^{*}\big](X_{i})\cdot\Pi^{(k)}_{\widehat{\phi}}\big[\widehat{m}^{(k)}-m_{0}\big](X_{i})\right].

(5)

The bias formula (
4
) for the AIPW estimator, which involves the product of two estimation error terms, is well established in the literature.
In contrast, formula (
5
) reveals that the bias of the cross-balancing estimator is governed by the product of the projection residuals of these two error terms.
In other words, cross-balancing
projects out
the component of each estimation error that is explainable by the linear span of
\widehat{\phi}
, even though
w^{*}
and
m_{0}
are entirely unknown.
Technically, this is due to the linear calibration enforced by (i) the balancing conditions and (ii) the
L_{2}
-norm minimization objective.
Although Proposition
3.2
does not guarantee that
|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\theta}_{0}-\theta_{0}]|\leq|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\theta}_{0}^{\text{AIPW}}-\theta_{0}]|
in all cases, our simulations indicate that cross-balancing often achieves considerably smaller bias than the AIPW estimator when using the same models
\{\widehat{m}^{(k)},\widehat{w}^{(k)}\}
, especially when they are estimated using generic machine learning algorithms without carefully tuned hyperparameters.

3.3

Inferential guarantees

We now examine the inferential properties of
\widehat{\theta}
with fitted features.
The following theorem establishes its asymptotic normality when the learned features converge at nonparametric rates.
For brevity, the details regarding variance estimation are in the proof in Appendix
B.3
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose the following conditions hold:

(i)

\mathbb{E}\left[\{Y(0)-m_{0}(X)\}^{2}\mid T=0\right]\leq M_{1}
for some constant
M_{1}>0
.

(ii)

The imbalance tolerance satisfies
\delta_{n}=o(n^{-1/2})
.

(iii)

For
k=1,2
,
\|\widehat{\phi}^{(k)}-\phi^{*}\|_{L_{2}}=o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
for some fixed
\phi^{*}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\mathbb{E}[\|\phi^{*}\|^{2}]\leq M_{2}
for a constant
M_{2}>0
, and
\mathbb{E}[\phi^{*}(X)\phi^{*}(X)^{\top}\mid T=0]
is full rank.

(iv)

There exist unknown vectors
\lambda^{*},\beta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
w^{*}(x)=(\lambda^{*})^{\top}\phi^{*}(x)
and
m_{0}(x)=(\beta^{*})^{\top}\phi^{*}(x)
, where
w^{*}(x)\equiv(1-p)e(x)/[p(1-e(x))]
.

Then,
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{0}-\theta_{0})\rightsquigarrow N(0,\sigma_{0}^{2})

where
\sigma_{0}^{2}=\Var\big\{\frac{(1-T)w^{*}(X)[Y(0)-m_{0}(X)]}{1-p}+\frac{Tm_{0}(X)}{p}\big\}

can be consistently estimated and attains the semiparametric efficiency bound.

In Theorem
3.3
, condition (i) imposes a mild moment restriction, while condition (ii) ensures that the bias due to approximate balance is of lower order.
The core requirements are (iii) and (iv), which specify that the learned features converge to a deterministic feature vector whose linear span contains both the oracle weight function
w^{*}(\cdot)
and the prognostic function
m_{0}(\cdot)
.
As discussed following Theorem
3.1
, a natural idea is to include in the feature set
\{\widehat{m}^{(k)}(x),\widehat{w}^{(k)}(x)\}
, which estimate
m_{0}(\cdot)
and
w^{*}(\cdot)
, respectively. Asymptotic normality follows
as long as these estimators converge at the rate
o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
to (functions whose span includes) the true
m_{0}(\cdot)
and
w^{*}(\cdot)
.
Condition (iii) is similar in spirit to the well-known rate-doubly-robust results
(
belloni2014inference
;
chernozhukov2018double
)
.
In contrast to the usual consistency conditions required for the AIPW estimator, condition (iv) is arguably milder: if one is not confident as to which model class is correct, one can incorporate a handful of them into
\widehat{\phi}^{(k)}
.
Valid inference is still attainable as long as at least one of the estimators converges to a limit whose linear span contains the true functions.

Theorem
3.3
underscores the importance of incorporating both the propensity score and the prognostic score in the choice of balancing features for the adaptive design of observational studies, especially when the estimated prognostic score is expected to converge at a slow rate.
This aligns with practical guidelines for covariate balancing which emphasize the use of variables that are predictive of either the treatment or the outcome; see, e.g.,
kainz2017improving
.
While prognostic score balancing approaches have been explored in the literature
(
hansen2008prognostic
;
10
;
6
, e.g.,)
, they may be insufficient for robust inference when the prognostic score is estimated with slow convergence rates.
In such cases, explicit modeling of the propensity score remains necessary to achieve fast convergence rates, unless the functions
\{m_{0}(x),w^{*}(x)\}
are perfectly linearly related.

Remark 3.4

(Multiple validity with parametric models)
.

As a final remark, the requirement that the limiting function
\phi^{*}
provides a good approximation to both
m_{0}(x)
and
w^{*}(x)
is necessary for root-
n
inference rates only when
\widehat{\phi}^{(k)}(\cdot)
converges slowly, as with nonparametric methods.
In contrast, when
\widehat{\phi}^{(k)}
is fitted using parametric models that converge quickly to some
\phi^{*}
, it suffices for valid inference that any entry of
\phi^{*}
is well-specified for either
m_{0}(\cdot)
or
w^{*}(\cdot)
.
This observation is formalized in Theorem
A.1
presented in Appendix
A.1
.

4

Cross-balancing with selected variables

This section introduces a second strategy for adaptive observational study design, which selects covariate features from a broader set or dictionary defined by the investigator. Standard guidelines typically recommend including covariates that are related to the treatment and/or outcome, guided by domain knowledge. However, supplementing domain expertise with data-driven feature selection techniques can be highly valuable as these methods harness information in the data itself.
Selecting features from such a dictionary can improve interpretability and support integration with subject-matter expertise, since the selection process remains transparent and the resulting covariates are accessible for scientific scrutiny.

4.1

Procedure

Throughout this section, we slightly override the notation and let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
denote the entire dictionary of candidate features, which may consist of the (potentially high-dimensional) raw covariates in the data or transformations thereof.
In keeping with the previous strategy, we randomly split the data, using one fold for covariate selection and the other for balancing the selected covariates.
In the first step, we use data from
\mathcal{I}_{c,k}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,k}
to find covariates that are predictive in a linear outcome or an inverse linear treatment model.
In the second step, we balance the selected covariates using data from the other fold.
For example, taking
k=1
, let
\widehat{S}:=\widehat{S}^{(1)}
denote the set of selected covariates from
\mathcal{I}_{c,2}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,2}
.
The weights
\widehat{w}_{i}
for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}
are then obtained by solving

\small\arg\min_{w}\left\{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}w_{i}^{2}:\,\bigg|\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}w_{i}X_{i,\widehat{S}}-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{t}}X_{i,\widehat{S}}\bigg|\leq\delta_{n};\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}w_{i}=1\right\},

(6)

where we set a constant tolerance
\delta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
for all selected covariates.
Similarly, we obtain weights
\widehat{w}_{i}
for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,2}
.
The resulting estimator is then
\widehat{\theta}_{0}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c}}\widehat{w}_{i}Y_{i}.

In contrast to the preceding section with a fixed number of balancing features, we now allow both the original and selected feature dimensions to grow with the sample size
n
.
The sources of estimator error also differ: here, the main source of uncertainty stems from
\widehat{S}
being a random subset of the given dictionary.
In this context, we highlight two key technical challenges that arise from feature selection in both low- and high-dimensional settings:

•

The first is the analysis with a random, fuzzy set of balancing features. When
\widehat{S}
is chosen from low-dimensional features, model selection often involves searching over an exponentially large space, and the selected set
\widehat{S}
may not converge to any fixed subset.
In high-dimensional settings where
p
grows with
n
, even in the well-studied regime with sparsity, accurately recovering the true predictors (if any) typically requires strong conditions
(
van2009conditions
;
2
)
.

•

The second challenge is analysis with misspecification. In any regime, it may be unrealistic to assume that the outcome is a perfect linear combination of a subset of the available features. This prompts the question of how much deviation from linearity can be permitted while still achieving valid inference.

To accommodate broad settings, we develop conditions for
\widehat{S}
that are mild and general enough to yield theoretical guarantees.
We begin with a standard sub-Gaussian condition.

Assumption 4.1
.

For each
j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
, the variables
\{X_{ij}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d., sub-Gaussian random variables with mean zero.
Furthermore, there exists an unknown function
\sigma(S)
such that for any subset
S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,p\}
, the random vector
X_{i,S}
is
\sigma^{2}(S)
-sub-Gaussian; that is, for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{|S|}
, the linear combination
X_{i,S}^{\top}\lambda
is sub-Gaussian with parameter
\|\lambda\|^{2}\sigma^{2}(S)
.

The general function
\sigma(S)
here allows for feature correlation which is useful when
X_{i}
’s are given transformations of raw features.
In the special case where entries of
X_{i}
are independent, we may simply set
\sigma(S)=1
.
In high-dimensional statistics, it is common to assume
\sigma(\{1,\ldots,p\})=\sigma^{2}
for some constant
\sigma^{2}

(
wainwright2019high
;
rigollet2023high
)
.

4.2

Convergence analysis

Rather than characterizing the selection set
\widehat{S}
, our analysis centers on its approximation capacity.
For any fixed subset
S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,p\}
and any function
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define

\displaystyle\epsilon(S;f)=\inf_{\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{|S|}}\left\|f(X)-\eta^{\top}X_{S}\right\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\cdot\mid T=0})},\vskip-5.0pt

with the minimizer denoted by
\eta(S;f)\in\mathbb{R}^{|S|}
.
Intuitively,
\epsilon(S;f)
measures how well
f
can be approximated by a linear combination of variables in
S
.
Similar quantities are used in
belloni2014inference
to describe the quality of variable selection and its role in inference when ordinary least squares, instead of generic balancing, is applied to the selected variables.
Theorem
4.1
establishes the convergence of
\widehat{\theta}
when the selection size may grow with
n
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that for each
k=1,2
, the following conditions hold:

(i)

Approximation error:
There exist fixed functions
\overline{w},\,\overline{m}\colon\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\|\overline{w}-\eta(\widehat{S}^{(k)},w^{*})^{\top}X_{\widehat{S}^{(k)}}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\cdot\mid T=0})}=o_{P}(1)
,
\epsilon(\widehat{S}^{(k)};\overline{m})=o_{P}(1)
,
and
\mathbb{E}[(Y(0)-\overline{m}(X))^{2}\mid X]\leq M_{1}
as well as
\overline{w}(X)\leq M_{2}
almost surely, for some constants
M_{1},M_{2}>0
. Moreover,
\|\eta(\widehat{S}^{(k)},w^{*})\|\leq M_{3}
for some constant
M_{3}>0
.

(ii)

Imbalance tolerance:

\delta_{n}\|\eta(\widehat{S}^{(k)};\overline{m})\|_{1}=o_{P}(1)
,
\delta_{n}\|\eta(\widehat{S}^{(k)};w^{*})\|_{1}=o_{P}(1)
, and

\delta_{n}=\Omega_{P}\big(\sqrt{\log|\widehat{S}^{(k)}|/n}+\|\overline{w}-\eta(\widehat{S}^{(k)},w^{*})^{\top}X_{\widehat{S}^{(k)}}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\cdot\mid T=0})}\big).

(iii)

Selection size:

|\widehat{S}^{(k)}|=o_{P}(n)
.

Then there exists a fixed value
\overline{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\widehat{\theta}_{0}=\overline{\theta}+o_{P}(1)
.
Moreover, if either
\overline{w}=w^{*}
or
\overline{m}=m_{0}
, then
\widehat{\theta}_{0}=\theta_{0}+o_{P}(1)
.

The proof is provided in Appendix
C.2
, which relies on analysis techniques in penalized regression.
The key technical challenge is to trade off the bias (controlled by
\delta_{n}
) and variance (controlled by the eigenvalues of
X_{\widehat{S}}X_{\widehat{S}}^{\top}
) when balancing a
random
,
large
set of features in the presence of model misspecification. To avoid strong eigenvalue conditions on
X_{\widehat{S}}X_{\widehat{S}}^{\top}
, we leverage a sufficiently large value of
\delta_{n}
(in analogy to penalized linear regression).
A parallel result via convex analysis is in Appendix
A.2
; it posits stronger eigenvalue conditions but allows smaller
\delta_{n}
, which may suit low-dimensional settings.

4.3

Inferential guarantees

Finally, we establish inferential guarantees for the cross-balancing estimator following variable selection.
The proof of Theorem
4.2
is provided in Appendix
C.3
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose the following conditions hold for
k=1,2
:

(i)

Approximation error.

\epsilon(\widehat{S}^{(k)};w^{*})=o_{P}(1)
and
\epsilon(\widehat{S}^{(k)};m_{0})=o_{P}(1)
, with
\epsilon(\widehat{S}^{(k)};w^{*})\cdot\epsilon(\widehat{S}^{(k)};m_{0})=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
. In addition,
\mathbb{E}\big[(Y(0)-\overline{m}(X))^{2}\mid X\big]\leq M_{1}
and
w^{*}(X)\leq M_{2}
,
|w^{*}(X)-\eta(\widehat{S}^{(k)};w^{*})^{\top}X_{\widehat{S}}|\leq M_{2}
almost surely, for some constants
M_{1},M_{2}>0
. Also,
\|\eta(\widehat{S}^{(k)},m_{0})\|_{1}\leq M_{3}
for some constant
M_{3}>0
.

(ii)

Imbalance tolerance.

\delta_{n}=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
.

(iii)

Eigenvalues.
There exists a function
\xi(\cdot)\colon 2^{[p]}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that, with probability tending to one,

\frac{1}{n}\|X_{c,\widehat{S}^{(k)}}v\|_{2}^{2}\geq\xi(\widehat{S}^{(k)})\|v\|_{1}^{2}

for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{|\widehat{S}^{(k)}|}
,
where
X_{c,\widehat{S}^{(k)}}
denotes the data matrix whose
i
-th row is
X_{i,\widehat{S}^{(k)}}
for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,k}
. Moreover,
\log(|\widehat{S}^{(k)}|)/\xi(\widehat{S}^{(k)})=o_{P}(n)
and
\epsilon(\widehat{S}^{(k)};m_{0})\cdot[\log(|\widehat{S}^{(k)}|)/\xi(\widehat{S}^{(k)})]^{1/2}=o_{P}(1)
.

Then
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{0}-\theta_{0})\rightsquigarrow N(0,\sigma_{0}^{2})
, where
\sigma_{0}^{2}=\Var\big\{\frac{(1-T)w^{*}(X)[Y(0)-m_{0}(X)]}{1-p}+\frac{Tm_{0}(X)}{p}\big\}
achieves the semiparametric efficiency bound for
\theta_{0}
.

Remark 4.3

(Finite-sample analysis)
.

Although Theorem
4.2
establishes an asymptotic result, we also present a finite-sample analysis of the cross-balancing estimator in Appendix
A.3
.
This analysis gives an explicit error bound in terms of
\delta_{n}
,
\epsilon(\widehat{S}^{(k)},w^{*})
,
\epsilon(\widehat{S}^{(k)};m_{0})
, and
\xi(\widehat{S}^{(k)})
, without using
o_{P}(\cdot)
terms.
These finite-sample results can be used to recover both Theorem
4.1
and Theorem
4.2
as special cases under their respective assumptions.

In Theorem
4.2
, condition (i) requires that the product
\epsilon(\widehat{S}^{(k)};m_{0})\cdot\epsilon(\widehat{S}^{(k)};w^{*})
is
o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
, which quantifies the robustness of cross-balancing to both variable selection errors and model misspecification.
Notably, this condition does not require that
m_{0}
or
w^{*}
be exactly linear functions of any subset of variables, nor that
\widehat{S}^{(k)}
include the set of truly relevant variables (if any).
Instead, it suffices that linear combinations of the selected variables can jointly approximate the prognostic score and the true weights up to the stated product rate.

This has important practical implications for data-driven variable selection in cross-balancing procedures. For example, selecting only outcome-related variables may yield a small
\epsilon(\widehat{S}^{(k)};m_{0})
but may not guarantee a sufficiently small product with
\epsilon(\widehat{S}^{(k)};w^{*})
; analogous issues arise when selecting only propensity-related variables.
Our results theoretically justify the practical guidance of
kainz2017improving
: to select the union of outcome-related and treatment-related variables.
This strategy is particularly useful when one is concerned with variable selection uncertainty or model misspecification.

The last two assumptions in Theorem
4.2
address different sources of bias.
Condition (ii) ensures that the bias in the outcomes from inexact balancing does not undermine root-
n
inference. Condition (iii) controls the error in approximating the implicit weight model with an imperfect
|\widehat{S}|
; it is automatically satisfied if
|\widehat{S}|
remains fixed (or bounded) as
n\to\infty
and the covariance matrix of
X
is full rank.
When
|\widehat{S}|
grows with
n
, condition (iii) resembles the restricted eigenvalue or compatibility conditions commonly used in high-dimensional statistics, which we discuss below.

Remark 4.4
.

In condition (iii), setting
\xi(S)=|S|^{-1}
recovers the compatibility condition
(
van2007deterministic
)
, which is typically weaker than the restricted isometry property
(
candes2006near
)
and the restricted
\ell_{2}
-eigenvalue condition
(
bickel2009simultaneous
)
(see also
van2009conditions
for a comparison). With this choice, condition (iii) is a combination of three requirements: (a)
\frac{1}{n}\|X_{c,\widehat{S}^{(k)}}v\|_{2}^{2}\cdot|\widehat{S}^{(k)}|\geq\|v\|_{1}^{2}
for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{|\widehat{S}^{(k)}|}
; (b)
|\widehat{S}^{(k)}|\log(|\widehat{S}^{(k)}|)=o_{P}(n)
; and (c)
\epsilon(\widehat{S}^{(k)};m_{0})\cdot|\widehat{S}^{(k)}|^{1/2}\cdot\log(|\widehat{S}^{(k)}|)^{1/2}=o_{P}(1)
. Here, (a) ensures that the selected columns of
X_{c}
are not excessively correlated, a requirement commonly imposed in high-dimensional linear regression for the true sparse support, yet here applied to the selected features and can be readily checked empirically. Technically, (a) facilitates control of the bias arising from the (implicit) Lasso estimation of weights with a small penalty
\delta_{n}=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
.
Conditions (b) and (c) restrict the size of
\widehat{S}^{(k)}
, which further controls the error in the implicit weight estimation. Under typical rates such as
\epsilon(\widehat{S}^{(k)};w^{*})=o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
and
\epsilon(\widehat{S}^{(k)};m_{0})=o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
, condition (c) is satisfied if
|\widehat{S}^{(k)}|\log(|\widehat{S}^{(k)}|)=O_{P}(n^{1/2})
.

4.4

Examples of variable selection procedures

Theorem
4.2
treats the variable selection procedure as a black-box, meaning that the selected set
\widehat{S}^{(k)}
produced by any selection procedure that satisfies the required conditions leads to the stated inferential properties.
To further contextualize our results, we now discuss two concrete examples of selection procedures tailored to low- and high-dimensional settings, respectively, and their impact on the cross-balancing estimator.
For convenience, we use
\widehat{S}=\widehat{S}^{(k)}
to refer to variables selected from
\mathcal{I}_{t}\cup\mathcal{I}_{c}:=\mathcal{I}_{t}^{(k)}\cup\mathcal{I}_{c}^{(k)}
. The first example uses linear regression to select variables
\widehat{S}
related to both the prognostic and propensity scores.

Example 4.5

(Linear regression)
.

For the prognostic score, define
\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[XX^{\top}]

\widehat{\beta}=\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[XY]

\widehat{S}_{m}=\{j\colon|\widehat{\beta}_{j}/\widehat{\sigma}_{j}|\geq\underline{\sigma}\}

\underline{\sigma}>0

\widehat{\sigma}_{j}^{2}
estimates the variance of
\widehat{\beta}_{j}

\underline{\sigma}=\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)/\sqrt{n_{c}}

\widehat{\lambda}=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[XX^{\top}]^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[X]

\widehat{\lambda}_{j}

5.1

Evaluation of cross-balancing with learned features

We first evaluate the variant where the features are learned, random functions.
To assess the roles of the training algorithms and the data-generating process (DGPs), we consider two broad settings:
(i)
Low-dimensional setting,
with nonlinear regression functions (Section
5.1.1
) fit by random forests in the grf R package
(
athey2019generalized
)
.
(ii)
High-dimensional setting,
where the true functions depend on a sparse subset of variables (Section
5.1.2
), fit by cross-validated Lasso in the glmnet R package
(
hastie2014glmnet
)
.

Following Theorem
3.3
, throughout this section we define
\widehat{m}(x)
as an estimated prognostic score for
\mathbb{E}[Y(0)\mid X=x]
.
We also define
\widehat{w}(\cdot)\propto\widehat{e}(\cdot)/(1-\widehat{e}(\cdot))
as a preliminary weight, where
\widehat{e}(\cdot)
is a fitted propensity score model for
e(x)
.
We compare seven methods.
Two cross-balancing procedures: (i) Cross_Bal: the method introduced in Section
3
, with
\widehat{\phi}(x)=(\widehat{m}(x),\widehat{w}(x))
; (ii) Cross_Bal_prog: the same as above but with
\widehat{\phi}(x)=\widehat{m}(x)
, i.e., only the estimated prognostic score, included to show the role of the propensity score in our procedure.
Two non-sample-splitting counterparts: (iii) Naive_Bal: using all data to fit
\widehat{m}(x),\widehat{w}(x)
and to obtain balancing weights without sample splitting; (iv) Naive_Bal_prog: the non-sample-splitting version of Cross_Bal_prog.
Also, (v) AIPW: the cross-fitting version of the AIPW estimator
(
robins1994estimation
)
as in
chernozhukov2018double
, with the same sample splitting scheme and same fitted outcome models and preliminary weights; we include this method to examine bias reduction properties (Proposition
3.2
).
Finally, two oracle methods: (vi) Oracle_CBal: the oracle version with
\widehat{\phi}(x)=(m_{0}(x),w^{*}(x))
, which describes cross-balancing in the absence of estimation error;
(vii) Oracle_AIPW: the AIPW estimator using
(m_{0}(x),w^{*}(x))
, which serves as a benchmark for the highest achievable performance.

We evaluate estimators by bias, standard deviation, and root mean squared error (MSE).
For inference, we construct confidence intervals (CIs) using two approaches. (i) Bootstrap: we apply the procedures to
B=1000
resamples with replacement from the original data, and define the CI by the empirical
\alpha/2
-th and
(1-\alpha/2)
-th quantiles of the bootstrap estimates. (ii) Wald-type: we estimate the asymptotic variance in Theorem
3.3
and construct CIs by normal approximation.
All simulations are repeated independently
N=500
times.

5.2

Evaluation of cross-balancing with selected variables

We next study the variant in Section
4
where balancing covariates are selected from a large dictionary of features.
We consider two settings where variable selection can be helpful: (1)
Selection from raw features
(Section

).
Among high-dimensional (
d=100

Selecting a smaller set of balancing covariates improves robustness by reducing the number of balancing constraints, and enhances interpretability. (2)
Selection from a basis expansion
(Section

).
Given a handful of raw features (
d=10
), to increase model expressiveness, one includes nonlinear transformations of them and yields a much higher-dimensional set of basis functions.
Selecting the most relevant ones to balance improves expressiveness, interpretability, and robustness.

We assess cross-balancing when combined with different variable selection strategies.
To streamline the exposition, we focus only on cross-balancing-based methods and Wald-type CIs.
We compare four estimators:
(i) Cross_Bal: In one fold of data, we select variables relevant for the prognostic and propensity score models, and take their union as the balancing covariates for creating weights in the other fold, and vice versa; (ii) Outc_Only: Only variables selected in the prognostic model are used in balancing; (iii) Prop_Only: Only variables selected in the propensity model are used in balancing; (iv) SBW: Stable balancing weights, using all the raw features or their transformations without variable selection.
Methods (i)-(iii) are combined with three widely-used variable selection strategies, without any parameter tuning:
(a) lm: linear regression, where a (generalized) linear model is fitted for the outcome or treatment using all features, and variables with
t
-test p-values less than 0.05 are selected; (b) aic: forward stepwise regression with the AIC criterion from the stats R library, where the selection starts from an empty model and iteratively adds variables from the dictionary; (c) lasso: cross-validated-Lasso (for the prognostic score) or GLM-Lasso (for the propensity score), using the glmnet R package with the 1se rule, and variables with nonzero fitted coefficients are selected.
We assess estimation accuracy, the average number of selected balancing covariates, and the coverage and width of the CIs.
Across all settings, we set the total sample size
n=1000
, tolerance
\delta=0.01
, and enforce positive weights in the sbw R package

Selection from original variables

In this section, we specify three DGPs in which only a small subset of the

The second DGP specifies both models as linear in a subset of the features, but includes two features in both models that have a very weak signal in the prognostic score. Thus, variable selection methods often fail to identify them in the prognostic model.

Specifically, in settings 1 and 3, the propensity-only approach suffers from elevated error and poor coverage, while in setting 2, Outc_Only performs worse (as discussed, in this setting important variables can be missed due to weak signals in the outcome model).

5.2.2

Selection from a dictionary of basis functions

We next consider settings where transformations (basis functions) of a few raw features are generated, and selection is performed from this expanded set before balancing.
We specify three settings inspired by
kang2007demystifying
, where prognostic and/or propensity scores may be complex nonlinear functions of the raw features (details in Appendix
A.7

In the first setting, the true prognostic and propensity scores are linear in certain transformations of the raw features that differ from, but can be approximated by, the basis functions.
For settings 2-3, we modify either the prognostic or propensity score in setting 1 to be linear in several basis functions of the raw features, with a small nonlinear additive term.
Here, the true models are not exactly in the span of the basis functions but can be well approximated. The goal is to select a small subset of relevant basis functions to improve expressiveness while preserving interpretability.
More specifically, starting with the raw features, we construct a set of basis functions that includes: (i) all raw features
X_{j}

X_{j}^{2}

X_{j}^{3}

Outc_Only also yields competitive performance, whereas Prop_only results in poorer estimates.

Observational study

In this section, we demonstrate the use of cross-balancing in an observational study of the effect of binge drinking on high blood pressure
(
rosenbaum2023second
)
. The data come from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, 2017–March 2020, prior to the pandemic) and are available through the iTOS package for R

We primarily focus on the variable selection variant of cross-balancing to estimate the counterfactual mean

We randomly split the data into two folds, construct a dictionary of basis functions and apply the method in Section

to perform variable selection and compute balancing weights.
(For comparison, cross-balancing with learned features using random forests produces an estimate of similar magnitude.)
Variable selection is performed using three procedures: lm, aic, and lasso, following the same steps as in the simulation study.
We consider a nested sequence of four feature dictionaries.
Each successive dictionary extends the previous by including: (i) raw features; (ii) cubic spline transformations of the raw features; (iii) all pairwise interactions among the raw features, including terms up to cubic order; and (iv) all pairwise interactions among the spline-transformed features, also up to cubic order.

\widehat{S}_{m}

\widehat{S}_{w}

(Outc_only), and the propensity-only set
\widehat{S}=\widehat{S}_{w}
(Prop_only).

We find that incorporating cubic terms expands the expressiveness of the balancing features and yields visible differences compared to estimates that use only the original features.
Increasing the size of the feature dictionary causes a slight increase in the length of the confidence intervals.

7

Discussion

This paper presents two complementary strategies for building data-informed balancing features in observational studies: learning key functions and selecting relevant features. Although they rely on distinct techniques, both strategies yield estimators with strong theoretical guarantees and robust empirical performance in simulation and real-world studies.

We emphasize that the strategies in sections
3
and
4
are not mutually exclusive; indeed, they can—and arguably should—be combined. For example, the estimated prognostic score can be included in the candidate feature dictionary. A straightforward extension of our theory would then establish consistency and asymptotic normality of the resulting estimator, provided the combined set approximates the prognostic and weight functions with a product rate of
o_{P}(1/\sqrt{n})
.

Also, these strategies do not stand in opposition to the traditional guidelines of using subject-matter knowledge to inform the design of observational studies; rather, they can complement and strengthen each other.
Cross-balancing provides a principled framework for observational study design, where one half of the data can be used to design the study, while the other half is reserved for analysis, and vice versa.
If data-informed feature selection happens to omit covariates deemed essential by expert judgement, these variables can always be incorporated into the analysis to preserve study interpretability and scientific relevance.
Ultimately, cross-balancing supports both efficient estimation and valid inferences, helping to maintain the rigor of the study.
Future work includes the extension of cross-balancing to longitudinal settings with limited overlap.

Proof of Theorem

Without loss of generality, we incorporate the normalization constraint into the features
\widehat{\phi}
and balancing tolerance
\delta

\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})
is the intercept, and the first entry of

We also fix the fold

By convex optimization theory, the Lagrangian of (

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(w,\lambda)=\|w\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{1}^{\top}(\widehat{\Phi}_{c}^{\top}w/n-\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[\widehat{\phi}]-\delta)-\lambda_{2}^{\top}(\widehat{\Phi}_{c}^{\top}w/n-\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[\widehat{\phi}]+\delta_{n}),\quad\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\succeq 0.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{dual}}(\lambda)=-(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})^{\top}\widehat{\Phi}_{c}^{\top}\widehat{\Phi}_{c}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})/(4n^{2})-(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})^{\top}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[\widehat{\phi}]-(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})\delta_{n}.

\widehat{w}=-(2/n)\cdot\widehat{\Phi}_{c}^{\top}(\widehat{\lambda}_{1}-\widehat{\lambda}_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
where

\displaystyle L(\lambda)=\lambda^{\top}\Sigma_{c}\lambda-2\lambda^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{t}[\phi^{*}],

\overline{\lambda}

By the definition of the AIPW estimator,

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{0}^{\textrm{AIPW}}-\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{AIPW}}}^{*}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\big(\widehat{w}(X_{i})-w^{*}(X_{i})\big)\big(m_{0}(X_{i})-\widehat{m}(X_{i})\big)

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}(\widehat{w}(X_{i})-w^{*}(X_{i}))(Y_{i}(0)-m_{0}(X_{i}))}_{\text{term (ii)}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}(\widehat{m}(X_{j})-m_{0}(X_{j}))-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}w^{*}(X_{i})(\widehat{m}(X_{i})-m_{0}(X_{i}))}_{\text{term (iii)}}.

Note that
\mathbb{E}[\text{term (ii)}]=0
since
m_{0}(x)
is the true regression function and
\widehat{w}
is obtained independent of the data it applies to. Since
w^{*}(X_{i})
is the true density ratio and
\widehat{m}
is independent of the data in
\mathcal{I}_{c,k}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}w^{*}(X_{i})(\widehat{m}(X_{i})-m_{0}(X_{i}))\,\Big|\,\mathcal{I}_{c,2}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,2}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[w^{*}(X)[\widehat{m}-m_{0}](X){\,|\,}T=0]=\mathbb{E}[[\widehat{m}-m_{0}](X){\,|\,}T=1]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}(\widehat{m}(X_{j})-m_{0}(X_{j}))\,\Big|\,\mathcal{I}_{c,2}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,2}\Bigg],

where
(X,T)
is an independent copy. Therefore, term (iii) is also mean zero. Finally, noting
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{AIPW}}}^{*}]=\theta_{0}
yields the first equation in Proposition
3.2
.

On the other hand, by definition we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{0}-\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{AIPW}}}^{*}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\widehat{w}_{i}m_{0}(X_{i})-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}m_{0}(X_{j})

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\big(\widehat{w}_{i}-w^{*}(X_{i})\big)\big(Y_{i}(0)-m_{0}(X_{i})\big)}_{\text{term (i)}}.

Here, since
\widehat{w}_{i}
only depends on the covariates in
\mathcal{I}_{c,1}
, by the tower property we know
\mathbb{E}[\text{term(i)}]=0
.
For the remaining part in this decomposition, due to the balancing conditions, for any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\widehat{w}_{i}m_{0}(X_{i})-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}m_{0}(X_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\widehat{w}_{i}\cdot\big\{m_{0}(X_{i})-\beta^{\top}\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})\big\}-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}\big\{m_{0}(X_{j})-\beta^{\top}\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})\big\}.

Since
\widehat{\phi}
contains
\widehat{\mu}
, equivalently, for any (potentially data-dependent)
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\widehat{w}_{i}m_{0}(X_{i})-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}m_{0}(X_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\widehat{w}_{i}\cdot\big\{m_{0}(X_{i})-\widehat{\mu}(X_{i})-\beta^{\top}\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})\big\}-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}\big\{m_{0}(X_{j})-\widehat{\mu}(X_{j})-\beta^{\top}\widehat{\phi}(X_{j})\big\}.

We take
\beta
as the sample OLS projection coefficient of
m_{0}(X_{i})-\widehat{\mu}(X_{i})
onto the space of
\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})
,
and write

\displaystyle\Pi^{(k)}_{\widehat{\phi}}[\widehat{m}^{(k)}-m_{0}](x)=\widehat{m}^{(k)}(x)-m_{0}(x)-\beta^{\top}\widehat{\phi}(x).

Since
\beta
is the projection coefficient, we have (we now use the superscript
k
to emphasize the data fold)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})\cdot\Pi^{(k)}_{\widehat{\phi}}[\widehat{m}^{(k)}-m_{0}](X_{i})=0.

Thus, for any
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\widehat{w}_{i}m_{0}(X_{i})-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}m_{0}(X_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\big\{\widehat{w}_{i}-\eta^{\top}\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})\big\}\cdot\Pi^{(k)}_{\widehat{\phi}}[\widehat{m}^{(k)}-m_{0}](X_{i})-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}\Pi^{(k)}_{\widehat{\phi}}[\widehat{m}^{(k)}-m_{0}](X_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\big\{\widehat{w}_{i}-w^{*}(X_{i})-\eta^{\top}\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})\big\}\cdot\Pi^{(k)}_{\widehat{\phi}}[\widehat{m}^{(k)}-m_{0}](X_{i})

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}w^{*}(X_{i})\cdot\Pi^{(k)}_{\widehat{\phi}}[\widehat{m}^{(k)}-m_{0}](X_{i})-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}\Pi^{(k)}_{\widehat{\phi}}[\widehat{m}^{(k)}-m_{0}](X_{j})}_{\text{term (iv)}}.

Note that term (iv) has mean zero since
w^{*}(X_{i})
is the true density ratio between a control covariate
X_{i}
and treated covariate
X_{j}
.
In addition, by (
8
),
\widehat{w}_{i}
is a linear combination of
\widehat{w}(X_{i})
and
\widehat{\mu}(X_{i})
, and thus it lives in the linear space spanned by
\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})
. Consequently, for any
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, there exists some
\tilde{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\big\{\widehat{w}_{i}-w^{*}(X_{i})-\eta^{\top}\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})\big\}\cdot\Pi^{(k)}_{\widehat{\phi}}[\widehat{m}^{(k)}-m_{0}](X_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\big\{\widehat{w}(X_{i})-w^{*}(X_{i})-\tilde{\eta}^{\top}\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})\big\}\cdot\Pi^{(k)}_{\widehat{\phi}}[\widehat{m}^{(k)}-m_{0}](X_{i}),

and vice versa (i.e., the mapping between
\eta
and
\tilde{\eta}
is a bijection).
We now set
\eta
as the sample OLS coefficient that projects
\widehat{w}(X_{i})-w^{*}(X_{i})
onto the space of
\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})
. This leads to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\widehat{w}_{i}m_{0}(X_{i})-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}m_{0}(X_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\big\{\widehat{w}(X_{i})-w^{*}(X_{i})-\tilde{\eta}^{\top}\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})\big\}\cdot\Pi^{(k)}_{\widehat{\phi}}[\widehat{m}^{(k)}-m_{0}](X_{i})+\text{term(iv)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\Pi^{(k)}_{\widehat{\phi}}[\widehat{w}^{(k)}-w^{*}](X_{i})\cdot\Pi^{(k)}_{\widehat{\phi}}[\widehat{m}^{(k)}-m_{0}](X_{i})+\text{term(iv)}.

The above arguments lead to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\widehat{\theta}_{0}+\widehat{\theta}_{\textrm{AIPW}}^{*}\big]=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\Pi^{(k)}_{\widehat{\phi}}[\widehat{w}^{(k)}-w^{*}](X_{i})\cdot\Pi^{(k)}_{\widehat{\phi}}[\widehat{m}^{(k)}-m_{0}](X_{i})\Bigg],

since
\mathbb{E}[\text{term (i)}]=\mathbb{E}[\text{term (iv)}]=0
.
That is, cross-balancing projects out the component in the space of
\widehat{\phi}
from the biases in
\widehat{w}
and
\widehat{\mu}
, completing the proof for the second conclusion in Proposition
3.2
.
∎

B.3

Proof of Theorem
3.3

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

We first fix a fold
k=1
, and similar results naturally apply to the other fold.
For notational simplicity, we sometimes write
\widehat{\phi}
instead of
\widehat{\phi}^{(k)}
.

Following the convex analysis in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, we know

\widehat{w}=\widehat{\Phi}_{c}^{\top}\widehat{\lambda}
, where
\widehat{\Phi}_{c}
is the data matrix for
\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})
,
i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}
, and
\widehat{\lambda}
minimizes the (unconstrained) convex objective

\displaystyle\widehat{L}(\lambda):=\lambda^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}\lambda-2\lambda^{\top}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[\widehat{\phi}]+2|\lambda|^{\top}\delta,\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{p},

and
\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}=\widehat{\Phi}_{c}^{\top}\widehat{\Phi}_{c}/n
is the sample covariance matrix.
Consider the limiting objective

\displaystyle L(\lambda)=\lambda^{\top}\Sigma_{c}\lambda-2\lambda^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{t}[\phi^{*}],

where
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\phi^{*}]:=\mathbb{E}[\phi^{*}(X){\,|\,}T=1]
, and
\Sigma_{c}:=\mathbb{E}[\phi^{*}(X)\phi^{*}(X)^{\top}{\,|\,}T=0]
is the limiting covariance matrix.
Let
\overline{\lambda}
be the minimizer of
L(\lambda)
, which is
\overline{\lambda}=\Sigma_{c}^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{t}[\phi^{*}]
.
Similar to the proof of Theorem
3.1
, for sufficiently large
n
, there exists some
\nu>0
such that
\nabla^{2}\widehat{L}(\lambda)\succ\nu\cdot\mathbf{I}_{p\times p}
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Invoking Lemma
D.1
with
f=\widehat{L}
,
\theta_{0}=\overline{\lambda}
and
\theta=\widehat{\lambda}
,
we know that

\displaystyle\widehat{L}(\widehat{\lambda})

\displaystyle\geq\widehat{L}(\overline{\lambda})+\nabla\widehat{L}(\overline{\lambda})^{\top}(\overline{\lambda}-\widehat{\lambda})+\nu/2\cdot\min\big\{\|\widehat{\lambda}-\overline{\lambda}\|^{2},c\|\widehat{\lambda}-\overline{\lambda}\|\big\}

\displaystyle\geq\widehat{L}(\widehat{\lambda})+\nabla\widehat{L}(\overline{\lambda})^{\top}(\overline{\lambda}-\widehat{\lambda})+\nu/2\cdot\min\big\{\|\widehat{\lambda}-\overline{\lambda}\|^{2},c\|\widehat{\lambda}-\overline{\lambda}\|\big\}.

since
\widehat{\lambda}
minimizes
\widehat{L}
. By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we further have

\displaystyle\min\big\{\|\widehat{\lambda}-\overline{\lambda}\|,c\big\}\leq\|\nabla\widehat{L}(\overline{\lambda})\|.

(10)

Using the fact that
\nabla L(\lambda^{*})=0
, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla\widehat{L}(\overline{\lambda})\|=\|\nabla\widehat{L}(\overline{\lambda})-\nabla{L}(\overline{\lambda})\|\leq\|(\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}-\Sigma_{c})\overline{\lambda}\|+2\|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[\widehat{\phi}]-\mathbb{E}_{t}[\phi^{*}]\|\cdot\|\overline{\lambda}\|+2\delta.

Since
\delta=o(n^{-1/4})
and
\|\widehat{\phi}-\phi^{*}\|_{L_{2}}=o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
, we know that
\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}-\Sigma_{c}\|_{2}=o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
and
\|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[\widehat{\phi}]-\mathbb{E}_{t}[\phi^{*}]\|=o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
. This further implies
\|\nabla\widehat{L}(\overline{\lambda})\|=o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
and thus
\|\widehat{\lambda}-\overline{\lambda}\|=o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
due to (
10
). Finally, similar to the proof of Theorem
3.1
, under the given conditions we have
\overline{\lambda}=\lambda^{*}
.

We now proceed to analyze the estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{0}
related to the first fold, i.e.,

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{0}^{(k)}:=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,k}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,k}}\widehat{w}_{i}Y_{i}(0).

We will show that under the given conditions, it is asymptotically equivalent to the oracle AIPW estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{AIPW}}}^{(k)}:=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,k}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,k}}w^{*}(X_{i})(Y_{i}-m_{0}(X_{i}))+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,k}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,k}}m_{0}(X_{j}).

Define the difference after balancing as

\displaystyle\Delta^{(k)}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\widehat{w}_{i}\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

The balancing condition implies
|\Delta^{(k)}|\leq\delta
where
|\cdot|
denotes entry-wise absolute value for a vector. As such,

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{0}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\widehat{w}_{i}\cdot(Y_{i}(0)-\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})^{\top}\beta^{*})+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})^{\top}\beta^{*}+(\beta^{*})^{\top}\Delta^{(k)}.

(11)

Using (
11
) and reorganizing terms, we thus obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{0}^{(k)}-\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{AIPW}}}^{(k)}=

\displaystyle(\beta^{*})^{\top}\Delta^{(k)}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}(\widehat{w}_{i}-w^{*}(X_{i}))(Y_{i}-m_{0}(X_{i}))}_{\text{term (i)}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}(\widehat{w}_{i}-w^{*}(X_{i}))(m_{0}(X_{i})-\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})^{\top}\beta^{*})}_{\text{term (ii)}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}w^{*}(X_{i})(m_{0}(X_{i})-\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})^{\top}\beta^{*})-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}(m_{0}(X_{j})-\widehat{\phi}(X_{j})^{\top}\beta^{*})}_{\text{term (iii)}}.

First of all, define
\mathcal{D}_{1,X}:=\{X_{i},T_{i},Y_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,2}}\cup\{X_{i},T_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{t,2}}\cup\{X_{i},T_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,2}}
, which contains data in
\mathcal{I}_{c,2}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,2}
and the covariates in
\mathcal{I}_{c,1}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,1}
.
By construction,
\widehat{w}_{i}
is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{D}_{1,X}
, and conditional on
\mathcal{D}_{1,X}
, every
Y_{i}
is conditionally independent across
i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}
with mean
m_{0}(X_{i})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[|\text{term (i)}|^{2}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{D}_{1,X}\Big]=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|^{2}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\mathbb{E}\Big[(\widehat{w}_{i}-w^{*}(X_{i}))^{2}(Y_{i}-m_{0}(X_{i}))^{2}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{D}_{1,X}\Big].

By the tower property and the bounded conditional variance condition, we know

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[|\text{term (i)}|^{2}\Big]\leq M_{2}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|^{2}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}(\widehat{w}_{i}-w^{*}(X_{i}))^{2}\Bigg].

By the definition of
\widehat{w}_{i}
and the convergence of
\widehat{\phi}
to
\phi^{*}
and
\widehat{\lambda}
to
\lambda^{*}
, we know that
\mathbb{E}[|\text{term (i)}|^{2}]=o(1/n)
. Therefore, by Markov’s inequality, this further implies
\text{term (i)}=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
.

By Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\big|\text{term (ii)}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}(\widehat{w}_{i}-w^{*}(X_{i}))^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}(m_{0}(X_{i})-\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})^{\top}\beta^{*})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\big[\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})^{\top}\widehat{\lambda}-\phi^{*}(X_{i})^{\top}\lambda^{*}\big]^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\big[\phi^{*}(X_{i})^{\top}\beta^{*}-\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})^{\top}\beta^{*}\big]^{2}}.

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\big[\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})^{\top}\widehat{\lambda}-\phi^{*}(X_{i})^{\top}\lambda^{*}\big]^{2}\leq\frac{2}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\Big\{\big[\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})^{\top}\widehat{\lambda}-\phi^{*}(X_{i})^{\top}\widehat{\lambda}\big]^{2}+\big[\phi^{*}(X_{i})^{\top}\widehat{\lambda}-\phi^{*}(X_{i})^{\top}\lambda^{*}\big]^{2}\Big\},

where due to the
o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
convergence rates of
\widehat{\phi}
to
\phi^{*}
,

\displaystyle\frac{2}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\big[\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})^{\top}\widehat{\lambda}-\phi^{*}(X_{i})^{\top}\widehat{\lambda}\big]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\big\|\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})-\phi^{*}(X_{i})\big\|^{2}\|\widehat{\lambda}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\widehat{\lambda}\|^{2}\cdot O_{P}(\|\widehat{\phi}-\phi^{*}\|_{L_{2}}^{2})=o_{P}(n^{-1/2}).

On the other hand, due to the
o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
convergence rates of
\widehat{\lambda}
to
\lambda^{*}
,

\displaystyle\frac{2}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\big[\phi^{*}(X_{i})^{\top}\widehat{\lambda}-\phi^{*}(X_{i})^{\top}\lambda^{*}\big]^{2}\leq\|\widehat{\lambda}-\lambda^{*}\|^{2}\cdot O_{P}(\mathbb{E}[\|\phi^{*}(X)\|^{2}{\,|\,}T=0])=o_{P}(n^{-1/2}).

Similarly, we can show

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\big[\phi^{*}(X_{i})^{\top}\beta^{*}-\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})^{\top}\beta^{*}\big]^{2}=o_{P}(n^{-1/2}).

Putting these bounds together, we know
|\text{term (ii)}|=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
.

Since
w^{*}(X_{i})
is the true density ratio and
\widehat{\phi}(\cdot)
is obtained from another fold, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[w^{*}(X_{i})(m_{0}(X_{i})-\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})^{\top}\beta^{*})\,\Big|\,T_{i}=0,\mathcal{I}_{c,2}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,2}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[m_{0}(X_{j})-\widehat{\phi}(X_{j})^{\top}\beta^{*}\,\Big|\,T_{j}=0,\mathcal{I}_{c,2}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,2}\Big]=:\mu_{0}

for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}
and
j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\text{term (iii)}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\big[w^{*}(X_{i})(m_{0}(X_{i})-\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})^{\top}\beta^{*})-\mu_{0}\big]-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}\big[m_{0}(X_{j})-\widehat{\phi}(X_{j})^{\top}\beta^{*}-\mu_{0}\big].

(12)

Conditional on full data in
\mathcal{I}_{c,2}\cup\mathcal{I}_{c,2}
(and
\{T_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}
to be rigorous), each item in the summation is conditionally independent and mean zero. This means

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[|\text{term (iii)}|^{2}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{I}_{c,2}\cup\mathcal{I}_{c,2}\cup\{T_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\mathbb{E}\Big[(w^{*}(X_{i})(m_{0}(X_{i})-\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})^{\top}\beta^{*})-\mu_{0})^{2}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{I}_{c,2}\cup\mathcal{I}_{c,2}\cup\{T_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}\mathbb{E}\Big[(m_{0}(X_{j})-\widehat{\phi}(X_{j})^{\top}\beta^{*}-\mu_{0})^{2}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{I}_{c,2}\cup\mathcal{I}_{c,2}\cup\{T_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}\Big].

It’s straightforward to see that each conditional expectation in the summation is
O_{P}(\|\widehat{\phi}-\phi^{*}\|_{L_{2}}^{2})=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
, hence by the Markov’s inequality, for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(|\text{term (iii)}|>\varepsilon\cdot\sqrt{n}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{I}_{c,2}\cup\mathcal{I}_{c,2}\cup\{T_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}\Big)\leq\frac{o_{P}(1)}{\varepsilon^{2}}=o_{P}(1).

Taking expectation over the random variable on the LHS of the above equation and applying the dominated convergence theorem (with any subsequence of
n
) then yields
|\text{term (iii)}|=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
.

Putting the bounds on all three terms together, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{0}^{(k)}-\widehat{\theta}_{{\textrm{AIPW}}}^{(k)}=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})+(\beta^{*})^{\top}\Delta^{(k)}=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})

since
|\Delta^{(k)}|\leq\delta
and
\delta=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
. Using the same result for
k=2
, we then have

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1-T_{i}}{n_{c}/n}w^{*}(X_{i})[Y_{i}-m_{0}(X_{i})]+\frac{T_{i}}{n_{t}/n}m_{0}(X_{i})+o_{P}(n^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-p}w^{*}(X_{i})[Y_{i}-m_{0}(X_{i})]+\frac{T_{i}}{p}m_{0}(X_{i})+o_{P}(n^{-1/2}),

which leads to the desired result by the central limit theorem.

Variance estimation.

Finally, we can estimate the asymptotic variance
\sigma_{0}^{2}
via

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{0}(1-\widehat{p})}\sum_{T_{i}=0}\big[\widehat{w}_{i}\cdot(Y_{i}-\widehat{\beta}^{\top}\widehat{\phi}(X_{i}))\big]^{2}+\frac{1}{n_{1}\widehat{p}}\sum_{T_{i}=1}(\widehat{\beta}^{\top}\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})-\widehat{\beta}^{\top}\overline{\phi})^{2},

where
\widehat{w}_{i}
is the cross-balancing weights. For
i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,k}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,k}
, we write
\widehat{\phi}(X_{i})=\widehat{\phi}^{(k)}(X_{i})
, and
\overline{\phi}=\overline{\phi}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,k}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{t,k}}\widehat{\phi}^{(k)}(X_{i})
, and
\widehat{\beta}
is the OLS coefficient of

(17)

which further implies

i

X_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|\times|\widehat{S}|}
as the data matrix whose
i

Define

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{t,j}:=X_{j,\widehat{S}}-\mu_{t},\quad\mu_{t}:=\mathbb{E}[X_{j,\widehat{S}}{\,|\,}T_{j}=1],\quad\varepsilon_{c,i}:={w}^{*}(X_{i})X_{i,\widehat{S}}-\mu_{c},\quad\mu_{c}:=\mathbb{E}[w^{*}(X_{i})X_{i,\widehat{S}}{\,|\,}T_{i}=0]

viewing
\widehat{S}
as fixed.
Thus,

and
\{\varepsilon_{c,i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}
are i.i.d. mean zero random variables, respectively. We also define
\overline{\varepsilon}_{t}
as the sample mean of
\{\varepsilon_{t,j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}
, and
\overline{\varepsilon}_{c}
as the sample mean of
\{\varepsilon_{c,i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}
. In addition, since
w^{*}
is the true density ratio, we have
\mu_{t}=\mu_{c}
.
Finally, we define

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{w,i}={w}^{*}(X_{i})-\overline{\lambda}^{\top}X_{i,\widehat{S}},

and
\varepsilon_{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}
is the vector whose
i
-th element is
\varepsilon_{w,i}
for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}
.
By definition,
\overline{\lambda}
minimizes
\mathbb{E}[(w^{*}(X_{i})-\lambda^{\top}X_{i,\widehat{S}})^{2}{\,|\,}T_{i}=0]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[X_{i,\widehat{S}}\varepsilon_{w,i}\,\big|\,T_{i}=0\big]=0,

where again we remind that
\widehat{S}
is viewed as fixed. In addition, by definition we have

\displaystyle\overline{\varepsilon}_{c}+\mu_{c}=X_{c}^{\top}\varepsilon_{w}/|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|+X_{c}X_{c}^{\top}\overline{\lambda}/|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|.

Returning to (
22
), we then have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\big\|X_{c}\widehat{\lambda}-X_{c}\overline{\lambda}\big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2(\overline{\lambda}-\widehat{\lambda})^{\top}\Big(-\overline{\varepsilon}_{t}-\mu_{t}+\overline{\varepsilon}_{c}+\mu_{c}-X_{c}^{\top}\varepsilon_{w}/|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|\Big)+2\delta_{n}(\|\overline{\lambda}\|_{1}-\|\widehat{\lambda}\|_{1})

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\overline{\lambda}-\widehat{\lambda}\|_{1}\cdot\big(\|\overline{\varepsilon}_{t}\|_{\infty}+\|\overline{\varepsilon}_{c}\|_{\infty}+\|X_{c}^{\top}\varepsilon_{w}/|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|\|_{\infty}\big)+2\delta_{n}\cdot(\|\overline{\lambda}\|_{1}-\|\widehat{\lambda}\|_{1}),

(23)

where the first inequality uses the definitions, and the second inequality is due to
\mu_{t}=\mu_{c}

With similar arguments as in the proof of Theorem
4.1
and a union bound, with probability at least
1-2\varepsilon

\displaystyle\|\overline{\varepsilon}_{t}\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2|\widehat{S}|/\varepsilon)}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}},\quad\|\overline{\varepsilon}_{c}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\overline{w}(\cdot)\|_{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2|\widehat{S}|/\varepsilon)}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}}

\mathbb{E}[X_{i,\widehat{S}}\epsilon_{w,i}{\,|\,}T_{i}=0]=0

is the mean of i.i.d. mean zero random vectors each entry of which is
M_{2}^{2}
-sub-Gaussian, where the constant
M_{2}>0
is given in condition (ii). Applying Chernoff’s bound and a union bound over all entries in
\widehat{S}
, with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
,

\displaystyle\|X_{c}^{\top}\varepsilon_{w}/|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|\|_{\infty}\leq M_{2}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2|\widehat{S}|/\varepsilon)}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}}.

Combining these arguments, we have

\displaystyle A_{n}:=\|\overline{\varepsilon}_{t}\|_{\infty}+\|\overline{\varepsilon}_{c}\|_{\infty}+\|X_{c}^{\top}\varepsilon_{w}/|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|\|_{\infty}=O_{P}(\sqrt{\log(|\widehat{S}|)/n}).

Thus, continuing with (
C.3
) and applying the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\big\|X_{c}(\widehat{\lambda}-\overline{\lambda})\big\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2A_{n}\cdot\|\overline{\lambda}-\widehat{\lambda}\|_{1}+2\delta_{n}\cdot(\|\overline{\lambda}\|_{1}-\|\widehat{\lambda}\|_{1})\leq 2(A_{n}+\delta_{n})\cdot\|\overline{\lambda}-\widehat{\lambda}\|_{1}.

(24)

Applying condition (iii) to
\widehat{\lambda}-\overline{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\widehat{S}|}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\big\|X_{c}(\widehat{\lambda}-\overline{\lambda})\big\|_{2}^{2}\geq\xi(\widehat{S})\cdot\|\overline{\lambda}-\widehat{\lambda}\|_{1}^{2},

which further implies

We now proceed to analyze the estimator.
Define the imbalance gap

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}:=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}w_{i}X_{i,\widehat{S}}-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t}^{(1)}}X_{j,\widehat{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\widehat{S}|},

which obeys

by the balancing constraint. Thus, by the balancing condition (recall
\beta=\eta(\widehat{S};m_{0})
)

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}^{(k)}:=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\widehat{w}_{i}Y_{i}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\widehat{w}_{i}(Y_{i}-\beta^{\top}X_{i,\widehat{S}})+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}\beta^{\top}X_{j,\widehat{S}}-\beta^{\top}\widehat{\Delta}.

Reorganizing the above equation, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}{w}^{*}(X_{i})(Y_{i}-m_{0}(X_{i}))+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}m_{0}(X_{j})

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}(\widehat{w}_{i}-w^{*}(X_{i}))(Y_{i}-m_{0}(X_{i}))}_{\textrm{term (a)}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}(\widehat{w}_{i}-w^{*}(X_{i}))(m_{0}(X_{i})-\beta^{\top}X_{i,\widehat{S}})}_{\textrm{term (b)}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}w^{*}(X_{i})\cdot(m_{0}(X_{i})-\beta^{\top}X_{i,\widehat{S}})-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}(m_{0}(X_{j})-\beta^{\top}X_{j,\widehat{S}})}_{\textrm{term (c)}}-\beta^{\top}\widehat{\Delta}.

Conditions (i)-(ii) imply
|\beta^{\top}\Delta|\leq\|\beta\|_{1}\|\Delta\|_{\infty}\leq\delta_{n}\|\beta\|_{1}=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
.
We now bound terms (a)-(c).

Let
\mathcal{D}_{X}
be the union of the full data in
\mathcal{I}_{c,2}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,2}
and the covariates in
\mathcal{I}_{c,1}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,1}
.
Since
\widehat{w}_{i}
only depends on the covariates in
\mathcal{I}_{c,1}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,1}
, conditional on
\mathcal{D}_{X}
, each term in the summation of (a) is independent and mean zero. Thus, term (a) is mean zero and (since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|\text{term (a)}|^{2}\,\big|\,\mathcal{D}_{X}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M_{1}}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|^{2}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}(\widehat{w}_{i}-w^{*}(X_{i}))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2M_{1}}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|^{2}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}(\widehat{w}_{i}-X_{i,\widehat{S}}^{\top}\overline{\lambda})^{2}+\frac{2M_{1}}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|^{2}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}(X_{i,\widehat{S}}^{\top}\overline{\lambda}-w^{*}(X_{i}))^{2}

where the second line uses Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. By the tower property,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|\text{term (a)}|^{2}\big]=O_{P}\bigg(\frac{\log|\widehat{S}|}{n^{2}\cdot\xi(\widehat{S})}+\frac{\epsilon(\widehat{S};w^{*})^{2}}{n}\bigg)=o_{P}(1/n)

since
\epsilon(\widehat{S};w^{*})=o_{P}(1)
by condition (i) and
\log|\widehat{S}|/\xi(\widehat{S})=o_{P}(n)
by condition (iii). This implies
|\text{term (a)}|=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
by Markov’s inequality.

Second, by Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\big|\text{term (b)}\big|\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}(\widehat{w}_{i}-w^{*}(X_{i}))^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}(m_{0}(X_{i})-\beta^{\top}X_{i,\widehat{S}})^{2}},

where using similar ideas as how we bound term (a),

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}(\widehat{w}_{i}-w^{*}(X_{i}))^{2}}=O_{P}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\log|\widehat{S}|}{n\cdot\xi(\widehat{S})}}+\epsilon(\widehat{S};w^{*})\bigg).

On the other hand, by Markov’s inequality we know

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}(m_{0}(X_{i})-\beta^{\top}X_{i,\widehat{S}})^{2}}=O_{P}\big(\epsilon(\widehat{S};m_{0})\big).

Since

by condition (iii) and
\epsilon(\widehat{S};w^{*})\cdot\epsilon(\widehat{S};m_{0})=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
by condition (i), we have
|\text{term (b)}|=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
.

Let
\mu:=\mathbb{E}[m_{0}(X_{j})-\beta^{\top}X_{j,\widehat{S}}{\,|\,}T_{j}=1]=\mathbb{E}[w^{*}(X_{i})\cdot(m_{0}(X_{i})-\beta^{\top}X_{i,\widehat{S}}){\,|\,}T_{i}=0]
, viewing
\widehat{S}
as fixed. Then

\displaystyle\textrm{term (c)}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}\big[w^{*}(X_{i})\cdot(m_{0}(X_{i})-\beta^{\top}X_{i,\widehat{S}})-\mu\big]}_{\textrm{term (c,1)}}-\underbrace{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}\big[m_{0}(X_{j})-\beta^{\top}X_{j,\widehat{S}}-\mu\big]}_{\textrm{term (c,2)}}.

Each term in the summation in term (c,1) is i.i.d. and mean zero, hence by Chebyshev’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(|\textrm{term (c,1)}|>\varepsilon\,\Big|\,\mathcal{I}_{c,2}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,2}\Big)\leq\frac{\Var(w^{*}(X_{i})\cdot(m_{0}(X_{i})-\beta^{\top}X_{i,\widehat{S}}))}{\varepsilon^{2}\cdot|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\leq\frac{M_{2}^{2}\cdot\epsilon(\widehat{S};m_{0})^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}\cdot|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}

for the constant
M_{2}>0
given in condition (ii). Thus by Lemma
D.4
we know
|\textrm{term (c,1)}|=O_{P}(\epsilon(\widehat{S};m_{0})/n^{1/2})=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
by condition (i). Using exactly the same idea we have
|\textrm{term (c,2)}|=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
as well.

Putting the three bounds together, we then have

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{c,1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c,1}}{w}^{*}(X_{i})(Y_{i}-m_{0}(X_{i}))+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{t,1}}m_{0}(X_{j})+o_{P}(n^{-1/2}).

Similar results apply to
k=2
in the same way, hence we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{0}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg(\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-p}{w}^{*}(X_{i})(Y_{i}-m_{0}(X_{i}))+\frac{T_{i}}{p}m_{0}(X_{i})\bigg)+o_{P}(n^{-1/2})

which leads to the desired result by noting that the efficient influence function of
\theta_{0}
is
\frac{1-T_{i}}{1-p}{w}^{*}(X_{i})(Y_{i}-m_{0}(X_{i}))+\frac{T_{i}}{p}m_{0}(X_{i})
(see, e.g.,
hahn1998role

Similarly, with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
,

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\Var(m_{0}(X)-\beta^{\top}X_{\widehat{S}}{\,|\,}T=1)\log(2/\varepsilon)}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}}+\frac{M_{2}\log(2/\varepsilon)}{|\mathcal{I}_{t,1}|}

Proof of Proposition

Define the population limits of
\widehat{\beta}
and
\widehat{\lambda}
by

\displaystyle\overline{\beta}:=(\mathbb{E}_{c}[XX^{\top}])^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{c}[XY],\quad\overline{\lambda}:=(\mathbb{E}_{c}[XX^{\top}])^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{t}[X].

Then, by definition we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}=\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[XX^{\top}\overline{\beta}+X\epsilon_{0}]=\overline{\beta}+\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[X\epsilon_{0}]=\overline{\beta}+O_{P}(1/\sqrt{n_{c}}).

Re-organizing the terms, we obtain the asymptotic linear expansion

\displaystyle\sqrt{n_{c}}(\widehat{\beta}-\overline{\beta})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{c}}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c}}\Sigma_{c}^{-1}X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\overline{\beta})\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\beta}),

where the asymptotic covariance matrix
\Sigma_{\beta}=\Cov(\Sigma_{c}^{-1}X_{i}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\overline{\beta}){\,|\,}T_{i}=0)
can be consistently estimated by

\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}_{\beta}=\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}^{-1}\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c}}[X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\beta})^{2}]\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}^{-1}.

That is, we have
\sqrt{n_{c}}(\widehat{\beta}_{j}-\overline{\beta}_{j})/\widehat{\sigma}_{j}\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{N}(0,1)
for all
j\in[p]
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle\widehat{\lambda}-\overline{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[X]-\Sigma_{c}^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{t}[X]

\displaystyle=\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}^{-1}(\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[X]-\mathbb{E}_{t}[X])+(\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}^{-1}-\Sigma_{c}^{-1})\mathbb{E}_{t}[X]

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{c}^{-1}(\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[X]-\mathbb{E}_{t}[X])-\Sigma_{c}^{-1}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}-\Sigma_{c})\Sigma_{c}^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{t}[X]+O_{P}(1/n_{c}).

Re-organizing terms, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{n_{c}}(\widehat{\lambda}-\overline{\lambda})=\frac{\sqrt{n_{c}}}{n_{t}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{t}}\Sigma_{c}^{-1}(X_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{t}[X])-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{c}}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c}}\Sigma_{c}^{-1}(X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}-\Sigma_{c})\Sigma_{c}^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{t}[X]+O_{P}(1/\sqrt{n_{c}})\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\lambda}),

where the asymptotic covariance matrix
\Sigma_{\lambda}=n_{c}/n_{t}\Cov(\Sigma_{c}^{-1}X_{i}{\,|\,}T_{i}=1)+\Cov(\Sigma_{c}^{-1}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}\Sigma_{c}^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{t}[X]{\,|\,}T_{i}=0)

\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}^{-1}\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{c}}\big\{X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[X]\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[X]^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}^{-1}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}\big\}\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}^{-1}

\sqrt{n_{c}}(\widehat{\lambda}_{j}-\overline{\lambda}_{j})/\widehat{\tau}_{j}\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{N}(0,1)
for all

where

are standard Gaussian random variables.

Due to the fact that
\|\epsilon_{m}\|_{L_{2}}=o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
and
\|\epsilon_{w}\|_{L_{2}}=o_{P}(n^{-1/4})

\overline{\beta}_{j}=\beta^{*}_{j}+o_{P}(n^{-1/4})

\overline{\lambda}_{j}=\lambda_{j}^{*}+o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
for any

\widehat{\beta}_{j}/\widehat{\sigma}_{j}=\beta_{j}^{*}/\widehat{\sigma}_{j}+Z_{j1}/\sqrt{n_{c}}+o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
for any
j\in S_{m}
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{j}/\widehat{\tau}_{j}=\lambda_{j}^{*}/\widehat{\lambda}_{j}+Z_{j2}/\sqrt{n_{c}}+o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
for any
j\in S_{w}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(S_{m}\subseteq\widehat{S}_{m}\text{ and }S_{w}\subseteq\widehat{S}_{w}\big)\to 1.

Proof of Proposition

Proof of Proposition
4.8
.

Our analysis largely follows the seminal results on the prediction risk of the Lasso under the compatibility condition (e.g.,
12
;
van2000asymptotic
;
2
), with minor modifications to handle the approximately linear models and implicit outcomes in the weight estimation.

We first consider
\widehat{S}_{m}
for the outcome model. Recall that
\widehat{\beta}
is the minimizer of the convex objective

\displaystyle\widehat{L}(\beta)=\beta^{\top}\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}\beta-2\beta^{\top}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[XY]+\nu_{1}\|\beta\|_{1}.

This implies
\widehat{L}(\widehat{\beta})\leq\widehat{L}(\beta^{*})
, which, after reorganizing terms, means

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{c}}\|X_{c}^{\top}\widehat{\beta}-X_{c}^{\top}\beta^{*}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2(\beta^{*}-\widehat{\beta})(\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}\beta^{*}-\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[XY])+\nu_{1}\big(\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}-\|\widehat{\beta}\|_{1}\big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\beta^{*}-\widehat{\beta}\|_{1}\cdot\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}\beta^{*}-\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[XY]\|_{\infty}+\nu_{1}\big(\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}-\|\widehat{\beta}\|_{1}\big),

where
X_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{I}_{c}|\times p}
is the data matrix of
\mathcal{I}_{c}
. Noting that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[XY]=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[XX^{\top}\beta^{*}]+\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[X\epsilon_{m}]+\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[X(Y-m_{0}(X))],

we have

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}\beta^{*}-\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[XY]\|_{\infty}=\big\|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[X\epsilon_{m}]+\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[X(Y-m_{0}(X))]\big\|_{\infty}\leq\big\|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[X\epsilon_{m}]\big\|_{\infty}+\big\|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[X(Y-m_{0}(X))]\big\|_{\infty}.

Since each entry of
X(Y-m_{0}(X))
is the average of
n_{c}=|\mathcal{I}_{c}|
many i.i.d. mean zero random variables, each
X_{j}
is sub-Gaussian, and
|Y-m_{0}(X)|\leq M_{2}
, by Chernoff’s inequality and a union bound, we know that with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
, it holds that

\displaystyle\big\|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[X(Y-m_{0}(X))]\big\|_{\infty}\leq M_{2}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2p/\varepsilon)}{n_{c}}}.

In addition, since
|\epsilon_{m}(X)|\leq M_{2}
and each entry
X_{j}
is sub-Gaussian, by Chernoff’s inequality and a union bound, with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
, it holds that

\displaystyle\big\|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[X\epsilon_{m}]\big\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\mathbb{E}_{c}[X\epsilon_{m}]\big\|_{\infty}+\big\|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[X\epsilon_{m}]-\mathbb{E}_{c}[X\epsilon_{m}]\big\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq M_{2}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2p/\varepsilon)}{n_{c}}}+\max_{j}\mathbb{E}[X_{j}\epsilon_{m}(X)]=M_{2}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2p/\varepsilon)}{n_{c}}}+\|X\epsilon_{m}\|_{\infty}.

Combining the above two arguments, we know that with probability at least
1-2\varepsilon
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{c}}\|X_{c}^{\top}\widehat{\beta}-X_{c}^{\top}\beta^{*}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\beta^{*}-\widehat{\beta}\|_{1}\cdot\bigg(\|X\epsilon_{m}\|_{\infty}+2M_{2}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2p/\varepsilon)}{n_{c}}}\bigg)+\nu_{1}\big(\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}-\|\widehat{\beta}\|_{1}\big)

(31)

\displaystyle\leq\|\beta^{*}-\widehat{\beta}\|_{1}\cdot\bigg(2\|X\epsilon_{m}\|_{\infty}+4M_{2}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2p/\varepsilon)}{n_{c}}}+\nu_{1}\bigg).

(32)

Using (
31
) and the non-negativity of the LHS, and denoting the constant
A_{n}=\|X\epsilon_{m}\|_{\infty}+2M_{2}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2p/\varepsilon)}{n_{c}}}
, we know that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq 2A_{n}\|\beta^{*}_{S_{m}}-\widehat{\beta}_{S_{m}}\|_{1}+2A_{n}\|\beta^{*}_{S_{m}^{c}}-\widehat{\beta}_{S_{m}^{c}}\|_{1}+\nu_{1}(\|\beta_{S_{m}^{c}}^{*}\|_{1}-\|\widehat{\beta}_{S_{m}}\|_{1}-\|\widehat{\beta}_{S_{m}^{c}}\|_{1})

\displaystyle\leq 2A_{n}\|\beta^{*}_{S_{m}}-\widehat{\beta}_{S_{m}}\|_{1}+2A_{n}\|\beta^{*}_{S_{m}^{c}}-\widehat{\beta}_{S_{m}^{c}}\|_{1}+\nu_{1}(\|\beta_{S_{m}^{c}}^{*}\|_{1}-\|\widehat{\beta}_{S_{m}}\|_{1}-\|\widehat{\beta}_{S_{m}^{c}}-\beta_{S_{m}^{c}}^{*}\|_{1})

\displaystyle\leq 2A_{n}\|\beta^{*}_{S_{m}}-\widehat{\beta}_{S_{m}}\|_{1}+2A_{n}\|\beta^{*}_{S_{m}^{c}}-\widehat{\beta}_{S_{m}^{c}}\|_{1}+\nu_{1}(\|\beta_{S_{m}^{c}}^{*}-\widehat{\beta}_{S_{m}}\|_{1}-\|\widehat{\beta}_{S_{m}^{c}}-\beta_{S_{m}^{c}}^{*}\|_{1})

\displaystyle=(\nu_{1}+2A_{n})\|\beta^{*}_{S_{m}}-\widehat{\beta}_{S_{m}}\|_{1}-(\nu_{1}-2A_{n})\|\beta^{*}_{S_{m}^{c}}-\widehat{\beta}_{S_{m}^{c}}\|_{1}.

Since
\nu_{1}\geq 4A_{n}
, the above inequality leads to

\displaystyle\|(\widehat{\beta}-\beta^{*})_{S_{m}^{c}}\|_{1}\leq 3\|(\widehat{\beta}-\beta^{*})_{S_{m}}\|_{1}.

Invoking the compatibility condition for
\beta=\widehat{\beta}-\beta^{*}
, together with
32
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|(\widehat{\beta}-\beta^{*})_{S_{m}}\|_{1}^{2}\leq 3/2\cdot\nu_{1}\|(\widehat{\beta}-\beta^{*})_{S_{m}}\|_{1}\cdot|S_{m}|/\phi_{0}^{2},

Thus, the approximation error of
\widehat{S}=\widehat{S}_{m}\cup\widehat{S}_{w}
satisfies

\displaystyle\epsilon(\widehat{S};m_{0})^{2}\leq\epsilon(\widehat{S}_{m};m_{0})^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\big[(\widehat{\beta}^{\top}X-m_{0}(X))^{2}\big],

where the expectation is over an independent new sample
X
, viewing
\widehat{\beta}
as given. The triangle inequality further yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[(\widehat{\beta}^{\top}X-m_{0}(X))^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\big[(X^{\top}\beta^{*}-m_{0}(X))^{2}\big]+2\mathbb{E}\big[\{(\widehat{\beta}-\beta^{*})^{\top}X\}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\epsilon(\widehat{S};m_{0})\leq\sqrt{2}\|\epsilon_{m}\|_{L_{2}}+2\sqrt{3}M_{1}|S_{m}|\nu_{1}/\phi_{0}^{2}=\Omega\Big(\max\big\{\|\epsilon_{m}\|_{L_{2}},~|S_{m}|\nu_{1}\big\}\Big).

We then proceed to analyze the estimator
\widehat{\lambda}
. With similar techniques as before, we can show that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{c}}\|X_{c}^{\top}\widehat{\lambda}-X_{c}^{\top}\lambda^{*}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2(\lambda^{*}-\widehat{\lambda})(\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}\lambda^{*}-\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[X])+\nu_{2}\big(\|\lambda^{*}\|_{1}-\|\widehat{\lambda}\|_{1}\big)

Here we note that, by the fact that
\mathbb{E}_{c}[Xw^{*}(X)]=\mathbb{E}_{t}[X]
since
w^{*}(x)
is the true density ratio, as well as the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}\lambda^{*}-\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[X]\|_{\infty}=\big\|-\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[X\epsilon_{w}]+\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[Xw^{*}(X)]-\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[X]\big\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[X\epsilon_{w}]-\mathbb{E}_{c}[X\epsilon_{w}]\big\|_{\infty}+\big\|\mathbb{E}_{c}[X\epsilon_{w}]\big\|_{\infty}+\big\|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{c}[Xw^{*}(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{c}[Xw^{*}(X)]\big\|_{1}+\big\|\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[X]-\mathbb{E}_{t}[X]\big\|_{1}.

A union bound together with repeated application of Chernoff’s inequality then yields, with probability at least
1-3\varepsilon
, that

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{c}\lambda^{*}-\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[X]\|_{\infty}\leq\big\|\mathbb{E}_{c}[X\epsilon_{w}]\big\|_{\infty}+3M_{2}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2p/\varepsilon)}{n_{c}}}.

Now denote
B_{n}=\big\|\mathbb{E}_{c}[X\epsilon_{w}]\big\|_{\infty}+3M_{2}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2p/\varepsilon)}{n_{c}}}
. Again with similar ideas as our analysis of the outcome regression model, we can show that

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\lambda}-\lambda^{*}\|_{1}\leq\frac{6|S_{w}|\nu_{2}}{\phi_{0}^{2}}.

The approximation error of
\widehat{S}=\widehat{S}_{m}\cup\widehat{S}_{w}\supseteq\{j\colon\widehat{\lambda}_{j}\neq 0\}
thus satisfies

\displaystyle\epsilon(\widehat{S};w^{*})^{2}\leq\epsilon(\widehat{S}_{w};w^{*})^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\big[(\widehat{\lambda}^{\top}X-w^{*}(X))^{2}\big],

where the expectation is over an independent new sample
X
, viewing
\widehat{\lambda}
as given. This further yields

\displaystyle\epsilon(\widehat{S};w^{*})^{2}\leq 2\mathbb{E}\big[(X^{\top}\lambda^{*}-w^{*}(X))^{2}\big]+2\mathbb{E}\big[\{X^{\top}(\widehat{\lambda}-\lambda^{*})\}^{2}\big]=2\|\epsilon_{w}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+2M_{1}^{2}\|\widehat{\lambda}-\lambda^{*}\|_{1}^{2}.

We thus have

\displaystyle\epsilon(\widehat{S};w^{*})\leq\Omega\Big(\max\big\{\|\epsilon_{w}\|_{L_{2}},|S_{w}|\cdot\nu_{2}\big\}\Big)

Therefore, we conclude the proof of Proposition
4.8
.

A_{n}
be a sequence of non-negative random variables. If
\mathbb{E}[A_{n}{\,|\,}\mathcal{F}_{n}]=o_{P}(1)
, then
A_{n}=o_{P}(1)
.

A direct consequence of Lemma
D.3
is the next lemma allowing to turn conditionally
o_{P}(1)
quantities to marginally
o_{P}(1)
quantities, which will be used from time to time in analyzing cross-balancing estimators.

Lemma D.4
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{n}
be a sequence of
\sigma
-algebra, and let
R_{n}
be a sequence of random variables such that

\mathbb{P}(R_{n}>\epsilon{\,|\,}\mathcal{F}_{n})\to 0

as
n\to\infty
, then we also have
\mathbb{P}(R_{n}>\epsilon)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

The proof of Lemma
D.4
is simply to take
A_{n}=\ind\{R_{n}>\epsilon\}
and invoke Lemma
D.3
. As a result, if we take
\mathcal{F}_{n}
to be the information in one fold
\mathcal{I}_{c,1}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,1}
and show some random variable
R_{n}
to be
o_{P}(r_{n})
for some deterministic sequence
r_{n}
, where the probability is conditional on
\mathcal{I}_{c,1}\cup\mathcal{I}_{t,1}
, then
R_{n}=o_{P}(r_{n})
marginally by Lemma
D.4
.
We will use this idea many times in our proofs, but we do not explicitly mention it all the time.
