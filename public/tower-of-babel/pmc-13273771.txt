Vitamin D and antimicrobial peptides in skin health and disease: Mechanisms and therapeutic potential
Authors: Hanlin Gao, Jianwei Zhu, Zhi Chen, Gang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As the body's frontline barrier against the external environment, the skin maintains homeostasis through a complex network of innate defense factors, among which antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) play pivotal roles in pathogen defense, inflammation regulation, and tissue repair. Apart from its classical role in bone metabolism, Vitamin D has been shown, mainly in experimental and translational studies, to modulate the expression and processing of selected AMPs through VDR-dependent and non-classical pathways. These findings suggest a possible link between Vitamin D signaling and cutaneous innate immunity. However, the functional consequences of Vitamin D-mediated AMP regulation appear to be highly context-dependent. Evidence from cell-based studies, animal models, human skin samples, and limited clinical studies suggests that this axis may participate in antimicrobial defense, inflammatory regulation, angiogenesis, and tissue repair, but direct clinical evidence demonstrating therapeutic benefit through AMP modulation remains limited and sometimes inconsistent. Finally, building on current evidence, we analyze the limitations of research on the Vitamin D–AMP axis and highlight future directions, including the context-dependent functions of AMPs in diverse skin environments, their interactions with the skin microbiota, and the design and evaluation of translational studies. We argue that this review not only advances our understanding of skin immune mechanisms but also provides valuable insights and references for future research.

Introduction

The skin is the largest organ in direct contact with the external environment, accounting for approximately 15–16% of body weight. Its surface area and multifaceted functions make it one of the body's most critical protective barriers [1,2]. In addition to serving as a physical barrier against physical, chemical, and biological insults, the skin also fulfills essential roles in metabolism, immune regulation, and sensory perception [[3], [4], [5]]. As the first line of immune defense, the skin blocks ultraviolet radiation, temperature fluctuations, and mechanical stress via the stratum corneum, while the sebum film, acidic surface pH, and natural moisturizing factors help preserve barrier integrity [[6], [7], [8]]. Moreover, keratinocytes, Langerhans cells, dendritic cells, and resident immune cells act in concert, enabling the skin to play pivotal roles in barrier defense, immune recognition, and inflammation regulation [[9], [10], [11]]. Recent advances further highlight the skin as an integrated neuro–immuno–endocrine organ, in which environmental cues are sensed and transduced through coordinated neural, immune, and hormonal pathways to maintain systemic and local homeostasis [12].

Vitamin D has traditionally been regarded as a cornerstone of bone health due to its critical role in calcium–phosphate homeostasis and bone metabolism [13]. However, emerging research has uncovered non-classical functions of Vitamin D, particularly its multifaceted roles in immune regulation [14,15]. Evidence shows that Vitamin D signaling profoundly influences the function and differentiation of multiple key cell types within the adaptive immune system, including dendritic cells (DCs), T lymphocytes, and B lymphocytes [[16], [17], [18]]. Through transcriptional regulation mediated by the Vitamin D receptor (VDR), Vitamin D modulates the expression of numerous immune-related genes, most notably the upregulation of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) [19,20].

AMPs are essential effector molecules of cutaneous innate immunity, exhibiting broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity while also contributing to immune modulation and tissue repair [21,22]. In humans, representative skin-associated AMPs include LL-37 and several β-defensins. These peptides not only disrupt microbial membranes to eliminate bacteria but also participate in antiviral and antifungal defense, inflammation modulation, wound healing, and maintenance of the skin's microbial equilibrium [[23], [24], [25], [26]]. For instance, LL-37 exerts both antimicrobial and immunomodulatory effects, promoting angiogenesis and keratinocyte migration during wound repair [27,28]. β-defensins help maintain the dynamic balance of the skin microbiota, preventing the overgrowth of harmful microbes [29,30].

Clinical studies investigating the Vitamin D–AMP axis remain limited. Existing randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have primarily focused on patients with atopic dermatitis (AD), and some studies suggest that oral Vitamin D supplementation may influence AMP expression, including LL-37 and β-defensins, in lesional skin [31]. However, whether these molecular changes directly reduce infection risk or improve clinical outcomes remains uncertain, because available clinical data are limited and not fully consistent. In other dermatological conditions, such as psoriasis, diabetic foot ulcers, and chronic wound healing, most evidence remains mechanistic or experimental, suggesting a possible involvement of Vitamin D-regulated AMP pathways rather than establishing a validated therapeutic effect [[32], [33], [34]].

Against this backdrop, this review systematically summarizes the mechanistic roles of the Vitamin D–AMP axis in skin health and disease, with a focus on its functions in infection defense, inflammation regulation, angiogenesis, and tissue repair. By synthesizing and integrating current mechanistic insights, this work aims to lay a foundation for exploring the clinical applications of the Vitamin D–AMP axis in skin-related diseases and to provide a reference for developing novel strategies in immune modulation and barrier repair.

Materials and methods

This review was conducted through a comprehensive literature search to summarize current knowledge on Vitamin D–mediated antimicrobial peptide regulation and its role in skin physiology and disease. Relevant articles were identified using electronic databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus, by combining keywords such as “Vitamin D,” “VDR,” “antimicrobial peptides,” “LL-37,” “β-defensins,” “skin immunity,” “psoriasis,” “atopic dermatitis,” and “rosacea,” as well as terms related to metabolic pathways (e.g., “CYP27B1,” “CYP24A1,” and “CYP11A1”). Articles published in English were considered, with a primary focus on recent studies. Both original research articles and relevant review papers were included, with priority given to studies providing mechanistic insights derived from in vitro experiments, animal models, and human clinical or translational data. Studies were selected based on their relevance to Vitamin D signaling, antimicrobial peptide regulation, and skin-related immune or barrier functions. The identified evidence was synthesized and organized thematically according to molecular mechanisms, biological functions, and disease contexts to provide a structured and integrative overview. In interpreting the evidence, we distinguished among findings derived from in vitro or cell-based systems, animal models, human tissue or lesional-skin studies, and clinical cohorts or interventional trials. Therapeutic implications were interpreted cautiously when conclusions were based mainly on experimental, indirect, or observational evidence.

Non-classical functions of vitamin D3

Vitamin D is a fat-soluble Vitamin that exists primarily in two forms: Vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol) and Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) [35,36]. Vitamin D3 is synthesized in the skin when 7-dehydrocholesterol (proVitamin D3) undergoes photolysis upon exposure to ultraviolet B (UVB) radiation at wavelengths of 290–315 nm, producing pre-Vitamin D3, which then spontaneously converts to Vitamin D3 through thermal isomerization [37,38]. In addition, both Vitamin D2 and D3 can be obtained through dietary intake and absorbed via the intestine. However, with excessive sun exposure, pre-Vitamin D3 can be further converted into biologically inactive isomers, such as lumisterol and tachysterol [39,40]. Recent studies have demonstrated that these compounds can undergo further metabolism via CYP11A1-and CYP27A1-dependent pathways, generating a spectrum of hydroxylated secosteroids, including 20-hydroxylumisterol and related derivatives [41,42]. In line with this, recent studies have shown that CYP11A1-derived lumisterol metabolites possess biological activity. For example, 24(OH)L3 has been demonstrated to reduce UV-induced cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers (CPDs) and oxidative DNA damage in primary human keratinocytes and skin explants, suggesting a role in cutaneous photoprotection [43]. These findings further suggest that UVB exposure may exert beneficial effects beyond classical Vitamin D synthesis by generating photoproducts with photoprotective properties. Notably, approximately 90% of Vitamin D is synthesized in the skin, yet its efficiency is influenced by multiple factors, including skin pigmentation, body mass index, age, sunscreen use, as well as the duration and surface area of sun exposure [44,45].

Once in circulation, Vitamin D is hydroxylated in the liver by the endoplasmic reticulum–localized enzyme CYP2R1 to form 25-hydroxyVitamin D [25(OH)D], a key intermediate in the activation pathway [46]. Subsequently, 25(OH)D, bound to Vitamin D–binding protein (VDBP), is transported to the kidney, where it is filtered by the glomerulus and internalized into proximal tubular cells via the megalin/cubilin receptor complex, undergoing a second hydroxylation step [47,48]. This process is catalyzed by the mitochondrial enzyme 1α-hydroxylase CYP27B1, producing 1α,25-dihydroxyVitamin D3 [1,25(OH)2D3], the biologically active form of Vitamin D [49]. Interestingly, keratinocytes and melanocytes express not only CYP27A1 but also CYP27B1, enabling local activation of Vitamin D3 within the skin, independent of hepatic and renal hydroxylation [50,51]. Other cutaneous cell types, including endothelial cells and fibroblasts, have also been shown to express CYP27B1 [52]. Collectively, these findings indicate that the skin constitutes a relatively complete microenvironment for Vitamin D metabolism. Disruption or deficiency of Vitamin D3 levels may impair this local regulatory network, thereby compromising normal cellular functions and tissue homeostasis in the skin.

The role of vitamin D in adaptive immunity

The VDR is a critical member of the nuclear receptor superfamily and functions as a steroid hormone–like, ligand-dependent transcription factor [53]. Comprising 427 amino acids, VDR exhibits a highly conserved molecular structure that includes the canonical DNA-binding domain (DBD) and ligand-binding domain (LBD) [54]. Its transcriptional regulation relies not only on binding to the ligand 1,25(OH)2D3 but also on the coordinated actions of cofactors, including coactivators (e.g., SRC-1, DRIP205/MED1), epigenetic modifiers (e.g., HDACs, LSD1, PRMT5), and corepressor/de-repressor complexes (e.g., NCoR/SMRT). Upon ligand induction, these factors dynamically interact with VDR to remodel chromatin and thereby achieve precise gene regulation [[55], [56], [57]]. Notably, nearly all adaptive immune cells express VDR, enabling them to directly respond to Vitamin D signaling and undergo functional and differentiation-related regulation.

Beyond the classical VDR-dependent pathway, accumulating evidence indicates that Vitamin D metabolites, particularly those generated via CYP11A1-mediated metabolism, can engage additional nuclear receptors. CYP11A1-derived secosteroids such as 20(OH)D3 have been shown to function as inverse agonists of RORα and RORγ, thereby suppressing pro-inflammatory transcriptional programs associated with Th17 responses [58]. In parallel, several hydroxylated derivatives of Vitamin D and lumisterol can bind to LXRα/β and activate lipid metabolism–related transcriptional networks [59]. Moreover, selected Vitamin D metabolites have been reported to activate AhR signaling, as evidenced by the induction of canonical target genes such as CYP1A1, suggesting crosstalk with environmental sensing pathways in the skin [60,61]. These findings support the existence of a multi-receptor signaling framework that extends the biological actions of Vitamin D beyond canonical VDR-mediated transcription.

Another emerging regulatory layer involves the interaction between vitamin D signaling and circadian rhythm [62]. Recent evidence suggests a bidirectional relationship in which vitamin D metabolism, VDR-mediated transcription, clock gene networks, and melatonin-related signaling may influence one another [62]. This interaction may be particularly relevant in the skin, which is continuously exposed to rhythmic environmental cues such as light, temperature, and UV radiation. However, direct evidence linking circadian regulation of vitamin D signaling to AMP expression or function in human skin remains limited, and further studies are needed to clarify its relevance to cutaneous innate immunity.

Early studies demonstrated that 1,25(OH)2D3 induces human T cells to upregulate the chemokine receptor CCR10, rendering them responsive to keratinocyte-derived CCL27 and thereby acquiring a skin-homing phenotype. This process is partly dependent on dendritic cell–mediated Vitamin D metabolism [63]. In inflammatory skin disorders such as psoriasis, Vitamin D signaling also modulates the effector functions of lesional T cells. Clinical evidence indicates that topical calcipotriol significantly reduces CD8+IL-17+ T-cell infiltration in psoriatic lesions, correlating positively with clinical improvement [64]. Further studies revealed that combining Vitamin D analogs with glucocorticoids not only effectively suppresses Th17-cell infiltration and ex vivo expansion but also disrupts the IL-36–IL-23/IL-17 inflammatory amplification loop, suggesting synergistic effects in targeting key proinflammatory pathways [65,66]. Cytokine profiling of patient lesions further showed that Vitamin D treatment upregulates IL-10 and Th2-associated mediators while downregulating the IL-23/Th17 axis, collectively shaping an anti-inflammatory immune milieu [67]. Consistently, interventional cohort studies demonstrated that combined formulations of calcipotriol and betamethasone reduce the infiltration of CD8+ T cells and CD8+IFN-γ+ subsets in lesions, further supporting the role of Vitamin D in modulating cutaneous adaptive T-cell immunity [68].

In addition to T cells, the role of Vitamin D in B cells has garnered increasing attention in recent years, with its functions not only shaping systemic immunity but also directly contributing to the maintenance of cutaneous immune homeostasis. Early studies demonstrated that 1,25(OH)2D3 suppresses the proliferation of activated B cells and limits their differentiation into plasma cells and class-switched memory B cells. This effect is accompanied by the upregulation of the cell cycle inhibitor p27, suggesting that Vitamin D attenuates antibody production through cell cycle regulation [69]. At the molecular level, VDR directly binds to the human B-cell ε germline promoter, thereby suppressing IgE class switching [70]. This finding is particularly significant, as IgE plays a central role in atopic dermatitis and other allergic skin diseases, implying that Vitamin D may ameliorate allergic phenotypes by reshaping B-cell antibody repertoires. In addition to antibody differentiation, Vitamin D also promotes a more immunoregulatory phenotype in B cells.In vitro, treatment of human B cells with 1,25(OH)2D3 significantly enhances IL-10 secretion, indicative of a Breg-like function that exerts immunosuppressive effects in inflammatory settings [71]. Concurrently, Vitamin D downregulates the expression of costimulatory molecules (e.g., CD86) on B cells and diminishes their ability to activate autologous T cells, thereby reducing the production of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-2, IFN-γ, and IL-4 [72]. Collectively, these findings indicate that Vitamin D not only dampens humoral immune responses at the B-cell level but also fosters a more tolerogenic immune environment by reshaping B-cell–T-cell interactions.

In addition, Vitamin D plays pivotal roles in regulating innate immune cells of the skin.Studies have shown that 1,25(OH)2D3 maintains monocyte-derived dendritic cells in an immature, low-immunogenic state by downregulating MHC-II and costimulatory molecules (CD80/CD86), suppressing IL-12 production, and enhancing IL-10 secretion, thereby inducing a tolerogenic DC phenotype [[73], [74], [75]]. Topical calcipotriol has also been reported to reduce Langerhans cell numbers and attenuate their antigen-presenting capacity, suggesting that Vitamin D signaling directly regulates DC-mediated immune initiation in the skin [76]. Furthermore, in macrophages, Vitamin D strengthens antimicrobial defense by inducing AMP expression [77]. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that Vitamin D maintains cutaneous immune homeostasis not only by modulating adaptive immunity through T and B cells but also by regulating innate immune cells such as dendritic cells and macrophages.

Functions and regulation of AMPs in skin

As the largest barrier organ of the human body, the skin harbors a highly complex cellular architecture. The epidermis is primarily composed of keratinocytes, but also includes melanocytes, Langerhans cells, and γδ T cells, which collectively sustain barrier homeostasis and immune defense [78]. Within the dermis, diverse populations of immune and stromal cells—including dendritic cells, macrophages, fibroblasts, mast cells, as well as B cells, T cells, and plasma cells—endow the skin with robust immune defense and reparative capacity [79]. In primates, the skin also contains two functionally distinct sweat glands: eccrine glands distributed across the body, and apocrine glands associated with the pilosebaceous unit [80]. Skin antimicrobial peptides

Keratinocytes provide a primary source of AMPs, expressing hBD1 and RNase7 at baseline and upregulating cathelicidin, hBD-2, hBD-3, and S100A8/A9 during inflammation, whereas their expression is reduced in atopic dermatitis. Immune cells contribute additional AMPs, including α-defensins, lactoferrin, granulysin, and elafin. Skin appendages serve as compartment-specific reservoirs: sebaceous glands secrete histone H4, lysozyme, cathelicidin, and β-defensins, while sweat glands predominantly release dermcidin. Commensal S. epidermidis produces bacteriocins that synergize with host AMPs to maintain microbial homeostasis.

The intrinsic defense of the skin barrier depends on a coordinated AMP network, with key members including cathelicidin LL-37, β-defensins, and S100 family proteins such as psoriasin (S100A7) [[82], [83], [84]]. These peptides differ in precursor organization, structural motifs, cellular sources, and antimicrobial spectrum, but they collectively contribute to microbial control, immune modulation, and barrier repair within the epidermal microenvironment [[85], [86], [87], [88], [89], [90]]. For example, LL-37 is generated from the hCAP18 precursor through proteolytic processing, β-defensins are inducible cysteine-rich peptides, and S100A7 contributes to antimicrobial defense partly through metal ion chelation and interactions with fatty acid–binding proteins [[86], [87], [88], [89]]. Importantly, the biological consequences of AMP induction are not determined by expression level alone. Peptide processing, extracellular release, local ionic conditions, protease activity, and the inflammatory microenvironment strongly influence whether AMPs exert protective, reparative, or proinflammatory functions [82,88,[91], [92], [93], [94]].

AMP expression is regulated by multiple interacting signals rather than by a single pathway. Vitamin D–VDR signaling is an important inducer of CAMP/LL-37 transcription through VDREs in the CAMP promoter, and this response can be modified by inflammatory cytokines, PPARγ signaling, and epigenetic regulation [[95], [96], [97], [98]]. In parallel, pathogen- and inflammation-related signals also regulate β-defensin expression; for example, IL-1 derived from stimulated monocyte-lineage cells contributes to epithelial hBD-2 induction, and Staphylococcus aureus infection can upregulate hBD-3 expression [84,99]. These observations indicate that AMP regulation should be interpreted in a disease-specific manner. Reduced AMP activity may weaken barrier defense and increase susceptibility to infection, as observed in some AD settings, whereas excessive expression, extracellular release, or aberrant processing of AMPs may amplify inflammation, as seen in psoriasis and rosacea [100,101]. This context-dependent framework provides the conceptual basis for the disease-specific discussion below.

The vitamin D–antimicrobial peptide axis in skin health

Antimicrobial activity

The skin surface functions not only as a physical barrier but also as a complex microbial ecosystem. Under normal conditions, resident skin microbiota and the host maintain a dynamic equilibrium [102,103]. Commensals such as Staphylococcus epidermidis, Cutibacterium acnes, and certain Corynebacterium species help sustain barrier homeostasis by producing metabolites or modulating host immunity [[104], [105], [106]]. In contrast, opportunistic pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Gram-negative enterobacteria can expand when the barrier is disrupted, immune function declines, or the inflammatory milieu changes, leading to local or systemic infections [107,108]. Experimental evidence has shown that psoriasin (S100A7), secreted by keratinocytes, exerts highly selective bactericidal activity against Escherichia coli, and neutralization of psoriasin markedly diminishes this protective capacity in the skin [88].

Vitamin D signaling is regarded as an important regulator of AMP-mediated defense in the skin. Mechanistic studies in keratinocytes have shown that 1,25(OH)2D3 induces expression of the cathelicidin precursor hCAP18/LL-37 through VDR activation, which may contribute to cutaneous innate immune responses [109]. In wound settings, experimental studies indicate that keratinocytes at the wound edge upregulate CD14/TLR2 and CYP27B1, enabling local conversion of 25(OH)D into 1,25(OH)2D3 and subsequent induction of LL-37 expression [110]. However, direct evidence linking this mechanism to protection against infection in human skin remains limited. This mechanism is markedly attenuated when Vitamin D signaling is impaired. Human studies show that UVB exposure increases hCAP18/LL-37 levels by stimulating Vitamin D3 synthesis, and topical calcipotriol further augments this effect, suggesting that Vitamin D promotes AMP expression under both physiological and therapeutic conditions [111].

Notably, similar mechanisms have been observed in innate immune cells, where in vitro and ex vivo studies show that TLR activation upregulates VDR and CYP27B1 expression and promotes LL-37 production, potentially enhancing clearance of intracellular pathogens [112]. Functionally, LL-37 has been shown in biochemical and microbiological assays to disrupt microbial membranes, inhibit bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation at sublethal concentrations, and dismantle pre-formed biofilms [113,114]. However, the antimicrobial efficacy of LL-37 reported in vitro is not consistently recapitulated in vivo, where peptide stability, protease-mediated degradation, and local ionic conditions can markedly influence its activity. In addition, while Vitamin D–induced LL-37 upregulation is well documented in keratinocytes, clinical studies have not consistently demonstrated a corresponding reduction in infection rates or microbial burden. These discrepancies suggest that AMP induction alone may be insufficient to predict functional antimicrobial outcomes in human skin. Future studies should therefore focus on integrating AMP expression with functional antimicrobial outcomes in human skin, particularly by addressing the effects of protease activity, microenvironmental factors, and peptide stability in vivo.

Regulation of inflammation

Cutaneous inflammation typically arises when the barrier is disrupted, or pathogen-associated molecular patterns invade [115]. Keratinocytes, dendritic cells, and macrophages sense microbial or damage signals via pattern recognition receptors, subsequently producing cytokines and chemokines that recruit neutrophils and T cells into the skin [116,117]. A controlled inflammatory response is essential for pathogen clearance and repair initiation, but excessive or uncontrolled inflammation can cause tissue damage and contribute to chronic skin disorders. For example, in psoriasis and atopic dermatitis, excessive cytokine release disrupts barrier function and establishes a self-perpetuating inflammatory cycle [118,119].

Vitamin D may influence cutaneous inflammation partly through AMP regulation, but this relationship is not linear. In keratinocyte-based and experimental models, Vitamin D has been shown to induce hCAP18/LL-37 and β-defensins while modulating inflammatory mediators such as IL-8 and IL-17-associated responses [96,[120], [121], [122], [123]]. LL-37 and hBD-3 have also been linked to autophagy, AhR-related signaling, and epidermal barrier regulation in experimental skin models [121,124]. However, the downstream effects of these peptides depend strongly on disease context. In some settings, AMPs are associated with barrier protection and reduced inflammatory signaling, whereas in others, particularly when LL-37 is aberrantly released, processed, or complexed with nucleic acids, they may promote inflammatory amplification [100,101,125]. Thus, the key issue is not simply whether Vitamin D induces AMPs, but under which microenvironmental conditions AMP regulation becomes protective, neutral, or pathogenic.

Angiogenic activity

During skin repair, rapid remodeling of dermal microvasculature is essential to ensure oxygen and nutrient delivery and to recruit immune cells [126]. Keratinocytes are the major source of early pro-angiogenic signals such as VEGF during acute injury, whereas impaired angiogenesis is a hallmark of chronic non-healing ulcers [127,128]. As noted above, Vitamin D signaling regulates the local induction of LL-37 in injured skin [110,111]. Previous in vitro and in vivo studies have shown that LL-37 can promote angiogenesis [129,130]. Specifically, LL-37 promotes neovascularization by binding to the endothelial receptor FPRL1 and has been shown to enhance vascular growth in animal models [94]. In keratinocytes, mechanistic studies indicate that LL-37 upregulates VEGF expression via the HIF-1α pathway, which may further contribute to angiogenic responses [131].

In addition to LL-37, other AMPs also contribute to angiogenic regulation. For example, in vivo studies have shown that hBD-3 can accelerate wound healing, increase the number and caliber of newly formed vessels, and stimulate fibroblasts to express VEGF, PDGF, and FGF via the FGFR1–JAK2–STAT3 pathway [132]. Psoriasin (S100A7), which is strongly upregulated under oxidative stress and inflammatory skin disease, enhances angiogenesis in dermal endothelial cells, suggesting a potential role in inflammation-associated vascular remodeling [133].

It should be noted that direct evidence linking Vitamin D–induced AMP expression to angiogenesis in human skin is still lacking. Current findings primarily suggest indirect or sequential associations. To establish whether Vitamin D directly regulates angiogenesis via AMPs, further studies are needed to determine whether blocking AMP activity attenuates the pro-angiogenic effects of Vitamin D. In summary, current evidence supports a role for the Vitamin D–AMP axis in creating a pro-angiogenic environment within wound microenvironments, but its direct mechanisms in clinical skin repair remain to be fully elucidated.

Tissue repair

Skin wound repair relies on keratinocyte migration and proliferation, fibroblast-mediated matrix synthesis, and the coordinated action of multiple growth factors within the local microenvironment [[134], [135], [136]]. Human tissue and experimental studies have shown that LL-37 is markedly upregulated at wound edges, where it is associated with re-epithelialization, but is relatively deficient in the epidermis of chronic ulcers [82]. Exogenous supplementation of LL-37 has been shown in in vitro and in vivo models to enhance keratinocyte migration and accelerate wound closure. Mechanistic studies further indicate that LL-37 promotes keratinocyte migration by binding heparin and mediating EGFR transactivation [137].

Clinically, a randomized controlled trial demonstrated that topical LL-37 treatment significantly accelerates healing of venous leg ulcers (VLU) with a favorable safety profile [138], suggesting its potential as a therapeutic agent. Notably, in a two-dimensional cell culture model of recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa (RDEB), low-dose calcipotriol induced AMP production and accelerated wound repair [139]. Given that Vitamin D induces LL-37 expression in wounded skin, this suggests that it may promote tissue repair through AMP regulation.

Overall, accumulating evidence supports a potential role for the Vitamin D–AMP axis in skin tissue repair. Post-injury activation of local Vitamin D metabolism and VDR signaling has been shown in experimental settings to induce AMPs such as LL-37, which are associated with keratinocyte migration and re-epithelialization. Both experimental studies and limited clinical data suggest that AMP upregulation is linked to improved wound healing outcomes. Vitamin D-antimicrobial peptide axis in skin diseases

In inflammatory skin diseases such as psoriasis, rosacea, and atopic dermatitis, as well as in various infections, AMP expression is frequently dysregulated, leading to either enhanced inflammation or impaired antimicrobial defense and ultimately disrupting skin barrier integrity. Vitamin D3, through VDR-dependent and non-classical signaling pathways, may influence LL-37, β-defensins, and other AMP-related responses. However, the biological consequences of this regulation are disease-specific and depend on the local inflammatory milieu, AMP processing, peptide localization, and the level of clinical evidence available. Therefore, the Vitamin D–AMP axis may provide a useful framework for interpreting disease-specific immune dysregulation, rather than a uniformly validated therapeutic target. Psoriasis

In psoriatic lesions, AMPs are aberrantly overexpressed and interact with multiple proinflammatory pathways. Accumulating evidence suggests that the Vitamin D pathway exerts important regulatory effects in this context, at least in part through modulation of AMP expression [33]. For example, topical calcipotriol has been reported to reduce the expression of hBD-2 and hBD-3 in psoriatic lesions, along with decreased transcription of inflammatory mediators such as IL-17A, IL-17F, and IL-8.

In contrast, the cathelicidin precursor hCAP18 is upregulated, suggesting that Vitamin D may reshape the overall AMP repertoire in the skin, which could contribute to the modulation of IL-17–driven inflammatory responses [33]. Mechanistic studies further indicate that, in IL-17A/IL-22–stimulated human keratinocytes, calcipotriol enhances hCAP18 transcription while reducing the release of LL-37 peptides into the culture medium, accompanied by IL-8 downregulation and intracellular retention of LL-37 [120]. These findings suggest that Vitamin D may not only promote LL-37 production but also influence its processing and localization, which could potentially limit its ability to bind nucleic acids and modulate downstream inflammatory signaling. In addition, Vitamin D has been shown to downregulate Th17-associated members of the S100 protein family, including psoriasin (S100A7) and koebnerisin (S100A15), which are implicated in inflammatory amplification in psoriasis [153].

These apparently divergent findings may be explained by the dual and compartment-dependent role of LL-37 in psoriasis. Although LL-37 is classically recognized as an antimicrobial peptide, extracellular LL-37 can form complexes with self-DNA or RNA and activate nucleic-acid sensing pathways, thereby amplifying plasmacytoid dendritic cell activation, type I interferon responses, and downstream IL-23/Th17 inflammation [101]. Therefore, the effect of Vitamin D analogs in psoriasis should not be interpreted simply as AMP induction. Rather, available evidence suggests that Vitamin D may reshape the AMP repertoire, reduce selected proinflammatory AMPs such as hBD-2/hBD-3 and S100 family members, and alter LL-37 processing, localization, or release [33,120,153]. Thus, the unresolved question is not whether Vitamin D increases or decreases LL-37 expression, but whether it modifies the functional form and inflammatory context of LL-37 in lesional skin. Further human studies are required to determine whether AMP remodeling directly contributes to clinical improvement in psoriasis.

Atopic dermatitis

In atopic dermatitis (AD), impaired skin barrier function is closely associated with type 2 inflammation. Cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-13 have been shown to suppress AMP expression in keratinocytes, which may facilitate Staphylococcus aureus overcolonization and increase susceptibility to infection. This phenomenon has been observed in both patient lesions and in vitro models [[154], [155], [156], [157]].

Some studies have reported that oral Vitamin D3 supplementation can induce LL-37 expression in the skin of patients with AD [31]. However, evidence from randomized controlled trials remains inconsistent. For instance, although Vitamin D supplementation significantly increased serum 25(OH)D levels, no corresponding changes were observed in lesional LL-37 or hBD-3 expression, nor in disease severity [158]. These findings suggest that the effects of Vitamin D on AMP regulation may be context-dependent and influenced by factors such as patient characteristics, disease subtype, and treatment duration. Clinical studies further indicate that Vitamin D supplementation is associated with reduced S. aureus colonization and symptomatic improvement in some cohorts. However, as most studies did not concurrently assess AMP expression, it remains difficult to determine whether these clinical effects are mediated through AMP modulation [159].

The inconsistent findings in AD may reflect the complex interaction between Vitamin D status, type 2 inflammation, barrier dysfunction, and microbial colonization. IL-4 and IL-13 can suppress keratinocyte AMP expression, which may limit the ability of Vitamin D supplementation to restore LL-37 or β-defensin responses in lesional skin [[154], [155], [156], [157]]. In addition, clinical outcomes may vary according to baseline serum 25(OH)D levels, disease severity, treatment duration, dosing regimen, skin microbiota composition, and whether studies simultaneously assessed AMP expression, peptide activity, and S. aureus burden [31,158,159]. Therefore, current evidence suggests that Vitamin D may influence AD-associated innate immune dysfunction in selected contexts, but its role as a primary driver of AMP-mediated clinical benefit remains uncertain.

Rosacea

In the pathogenesis of rosacea, dysregulated innate immunity and aberrant AMP expression are considered important contributors. Studies have reported that LL-37 levels are elevated in the skin of patients with rosacea, with processing patterns that differ from those observed in healthy individuals [160]. This altered processing has been associated with increased activity of epidermal serine proteases, particularly kallikrein-5, which may contribute to vasodilation and inflammatory responses. Mechanistic studies further suggest that epidermal TLR2 is upregulated in rosacea and may promote the release of KLK5 from keratinocytes, potentially exacerbating abnormal LL-37 processing and amplifying proinflammatory signaling [161].

Some studies have reported that 1,25(OH)2D3 can also induce KLK expression, which may influence protease activity and AMP processing, highlighting the complexity of Vitamin D in regulating AMP function [162]. However, clinical evidence has not established a consistent association between serum 25(OH)D levels and LL-37 expression in rosacea patients. In some studies, reduced serum 25(OH)D levels were observed alongside increased cutaneous LL-37 expression, suggesting that dysregulated LL-37expression may be driven, at least in part, by Vitamin D–independent mechanisms, such as activation of TLR and KLK pathways [142].

These discrepancies suggest that LL-37 dysregulation in rosacea is unlikely to be explained solely by Vitamin D-dependent transcriptional induction. Instead, the pathogenic relevance of LL-37 appears to depend strongly on protease-mediated processing. Increased TLR2 expression and KLK5 activity can generate LL-37 fragments with enhanced proinflammatory and vasoactive properties, which may drive rosacea-like inflammation independently of systemic Vitamin D status [160,161]. Although 1,25(OH)2D3 may influence KLK expression or AMP-related pathways in experimental systems, available clinical evidence has not established Vitamin D as the dominant upstream driver of LL-37 abnormalities in rosacea [142,162]. Therefore, the Vitamin D–AMP axis should be interpreted as a possible modulatory framework in rosacea rather than a confirmed pathogenic or therapeutic pathway.

Antibacterial, antifungal, and antiviral roles

In antibacterial defense, experimental studies have shown that 1,25(OH)2D3 enhances keratinocyte resistance to Staphylococcus aureus by upregulating CAMP transcription and epigenetic regulation [98,163]. Clinical studies likewise indicate that oral Vitamin D supplementation increases cutaneous LL-37 levels in atopic dermatitis patients and is associated with improved infection defense [31]. Together, these findings support a possible role of the Vitamin D–AMP axis in antibacterial immunity. However, direct clinical evidence demonstrating that Vitamin D-mediated AMP induction reduces infection burden in human skin remains limited.

In antifungal defense, LL-37 and hBD-3 exhibit fungicidal activity against Candida, while keratinocytes exposed to fungal stimuli upregulate hBD-2, hBD-3, and LL-37, highlighting the role of AMPs in cutaneous antifungal protection [164,165]. Vitamin D can upregulate LL-37 expression in the skin; however, whether this directly enhances antifungal capacity remains to be clarified. In antiviral defense, LL-37 has been shown to inhibit herpes simplex virus replication in keratinocyte models, underscoring its importance in cutaneous antiviral immunity [166,167]. However, direct evidence at the skin level demonstrating Vitamin D–mediated antiviral effects via AMP induction is still lacking. Thus, the causal role of the Vitamin D–AMP axis in cutaneous antiviral defense requires further investigation.

Overall, the roles of AMPs in antibacterial, antifungal, and antiviral defense remain largely supported by mechanistic insights and indirect evidence. Future studies employing skin models and clinical investigations are needed to establish whether Vitamin D regulates AMPs to provide broad protection against diverse pathogens.

Challenges and future directions

Although experimental studies support a role for Vitamin D in regulating AMPs, direct evidence linking this pathway to clinical improvement in human skin diseases remains limited. A key contradiction lies in the inconsistent relationship between AMP induction and disease outcomes: while Vitamin D enhances LL-37 expression in keratinocytes, this does not uniformly translate into clinical benefit. In psoriasis, LL-37 exhibits dual functions, contributing to antimicrobial defense but also amplifying inflammation through nucleic acid complexes; in atopic dermatitis, Th2 cytokines suppress AMP induction, leading to variable responsiveness; whereas in rosacea, elevated LL-37 is largely driven by aberrant protease activity rather than Vitamin D signaling. These discrepancies highlight major limitations in current research. Most studies rely on in vitro systems or indirect clinical readouts, without capturing the dynamic processes of AMP processing, peptide maturation, and spatial distribution in human skin. In addition, clinical trials often assess serum 25(OH)D levels or disease severity scores without directly measuring functional AMP activity or proteolytic profiles, making mechanistic interpretation difficult.

Future research should focus on several key priority areas to clarify the functional role of the Vitamin D–AMP axis in human skin. First, it is essential to delineate the post-translational processing of AMP precursors, particularly the protease-dependent conversion of hCAP18 to functionally distinct LL-37 peptides, as different cleavage products may exert divergent immunological effects. Second, studies should incorporate spatial and single-cell approaches to resolve the cell-type–specific regulation of Vitamin D signaling and AMP expression across the epidermal–dermal interface, which cannot be captured by bulk analyses. Third, the functional coupling between AMP expression and antimicrobial efficacy should be directly assessed in human-based models, rather than inferred from expression levels alone. Fourth, increasing attention should be given to the crosstalk between the skin microbiome and the Vitamin D–AMP axis, including how microbial metabolites modulate VDR signaling and AMP transcription under inflammatory conditions. Finally, future clinical studies should move beyond static measurements of serum 25(OH)D and incorporate dynamic biomarkers, such as active peptide profiles and protease activity, to better define the mechanistic basis of Vitamin D–mediated immunomodulation.

Conclusion

Current evidence suggests that Vitamin D exerts important immunomodulatory effects in the skin, with one proposed mechanism involving the regulation of AMPs, thereby influencing host defense, barrier homeostasis, and inflammatory responses. In psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, and rosacea, AMPs expression or processing is frequently dysregulated and has been associated with disease pathogenesis, suggesting that Vitamin D–mediated AMPs regulation may represent a potential regulatory axis rather than a confirmed therapeutic target. However, evidence establishing a direct causal relationship between Vitamin D, AMPs modulation, and clinical outcomes remains limited, particularly in human studies. Moreover, AMP function appears to be highly context-dependent, with regulatory effects varying according to disease type, protease-mediated processing, and the local microenvironment. While experimental studies provide important mechanistic insights, their translation to clinical settings remains uncertain. Therefore, future research should integrate Vitamin D status, VDR signaling, AMPs expression, and the role of the skin microbiome through systematic investigations at both basic and clinical levels. A clearer understanding of the Vitamin D–AMPs axis may improve our understanding of cutaneous immunology and help inform the development of more targeted and evidence-based therapeutic strategies.
