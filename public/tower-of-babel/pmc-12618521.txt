Nitrogen uptake and water consumption for achieving high yield of winter wheat upon nitrogen addition at different doses
Authors: Bo Qu, Hafeez Noor, Yujie Feng, Jun Di, Majed Alotaibi, Fida Noor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNitrogen nutrition is a critical determinant of agricultural productivity. Field experiments were conducted to investigate the effects of different nitrogen application rates on the growth, development, nitrogen absorption, yield, and protein content of winter wheat, with the aim of determining the optimal nitrogen fertilization regime for high yield. The experiment included seven nitrogen levels: 0 (N0), 90 (N90), 180 (N180), 210 (N210), 240 (N240), 270 (N270), and 300 (N300) kg ha⁻¹. Results demonstrated that nitrogen application significantly improved key growth parameters. The N210 and N240 treatments were particularly effective, promoting water consumption during the growth period, increasing the number of tillers at each growth stage, and enhancing overall nitrogen accumulation, which ultimately led to higher yield. Soil water consumption and water use efficiency (WUE) during the growth period were highest in the N210 and N240 treatments, respectively. Regarding yield components, the number of spikes, grains per ear, 1000–grain weight, and final grain yield were all superior in the N210 and N240 treatments compared to other levels. While the number of spike grains was highest under N240, it was not significantly different from N210. In contrast, the N300 treatment showed a significant reduction in spike grains compared to the N90 and N270 treatments. In conclusion, the application of nitrogen at rates of 210 kg ha−1 and 240 kg ha−1 optimizes yield components by promoting productive water consumption, tillering, and nitrogen accumulation, thereby achieving the highest grain yield in winter wheat.Keywords: Wheat, Nitrogen fertilizer, Precipitation, Water use efficiency, YieldSubject terms: Biochemistry, Plant sciences

Introduction

Winter wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is one of the most important food crops in the world. It is an important food crop in southeast of the Loess Plateau and accounts for about one-fifth the total food production in China1. Most of the wheat grain is used for human consumption. Improvement of wheat grain quality for high quality diet has become more and more noted in daily life. Wheat proteins are imperative to determine the food quality, because they are responsible for dough rheological properties, and influence the processing quality in the production of a range of end products2,3. Precipitation affects crop growth development thus yield, and this process also with affects the quality of winter wheat4. The higher the soil water content, the more obvious the decrease of wheat protein content, especially in the grain nutrition quality to maturity5.

Nitrogen (N) is a fundamental macronutrient governing plant growth, development, and ultimate productivity in cereal cropping systems6. Its optimal management is pivotal for achieving global food security, particularly in the face of a growing population and limited arable land7. In regions like the Loess Plateau of China, where agriculture is predominantly rain-fed, water scarcity is the primary constraint on crop production, making the efficient use of both water and nutrients a cornerstone of sustainable agriculture6,7.

The relationship between nitrogen fertilization and crop yield is well-established, but it is not linear. While nitrogen deficiency severely limits photosynthetic capacity, tillering, and grain formation, excessive application leads to diminished returns, economic losses, and environmental degradation such as groundwater contamination and greenhouse gas emissions6,7. The key challenge, therefore, lies not in maximizing N inputs, but in optimizing them to synchronize nutrient supply with crop demand 10. This optimization is encapsulated in the concept of Nitrogen Use Efficiency (NUE), which reflects the plant’s ability to acquire nitrogen from the soil and convert it into grain yield 6,7.

Excessive rainfall is not conducive to the formation of grain protein, which may be caused by excessive water washing wheat7,8. Before the rainy season in the leisure period (land without crop), the dryland farmland should be cultivated to loosen the soil and expand the soil water storage capacity8,9. Moderate drought promoted grain protein accumulation, while excessive drought and staining water affects grain protein accumulation, resulting in decreased protein yield10. Promote wheat plant growth and then significantly improve wheat yield and water use efficiency. Grain protein content also effected by the soil moisture content and decreased in the year with low precipitation, while increased in the year with high precipitation11. Recent technological advances have focused on the simultaneous and synergistic improvement of several factors including water use, yield, and grain quality12. The too much use of N fertilizers numerous adverse special effects on the environment. Therefore suitable N application should be resolute considering the economic reappearance13,14. Fertilizers constitute an integral part for improved crop production. N essential calcium, phosphorus, potassium, sodium, chloride, magnesium, molybdenum, and manganese crop growth soil fertility16,17. Applying N fertilizer promote wheat, and then significantly improved yield, and water use efficiency (WUE)18. Applying N fertilizer promote wheat root elongation and increase soil water consumption, promote wheat plant growth, and then significantly improve wheat yield and N use efficiency. Grain protein content also effected by the soil moisture content and decreased in the year with low precipitation, while increased in the year with high precipitation15. In China farmers are excessively applying N fertilizers to sustain further increase grain yield, but grain yield does not keep synchronous increase with excessive19. Nitrogen contributes less in enhancing grain yield but can reduce nitrogen-use efficiency and enhances the risk of environmental pollution20–22. Therefore, simultaneous achieving both high yield and high N use efficiency (NUE) is a major challenge23,24. In this study the main objectives were to determine the correlation between yield and soil water content and water consumption at different yield levels, thereby allowing (1) N to increase the yield, and quality (2) comparison of yield components and WUE and (3) an evaluation of the relationship between grain yield or yield components and field water consumption during key plant growth stages.

Experimental design and treatments

The research design was single-factor randomized block cultivar “Liangxing-99” were attained from Taigu Agriculture Bureau, China. Six N set as N0, N90, N180, N210, N240, N270, N300 kg N ha−1. All treatments were replicated 3 times. The area of each plot was 30 m2 (5 m × 6 m). Wheat stubble (about 25 cm high) was left in the field to reduce water evaporation and increase soil organic carbon for the next season. Before seeding, nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium were applied, Nitrogen (N): 150 kg ha−1 [as pure N], Phosphorus (P₂O₅): 150 kg ha−1, and Potassium (K₂O): 75 kg ha⁻1, where the NPK ratio = 2:2:1*, respectively. Basic seedlings 225 × 104 plants ha–1, row spacing 20 cm. During the growing season, artificial weeding is carried out. One sowing methods: wide–space sowing (WS) planting density was 315 × 104 plant ha−1 and each treatment was repeated 3 times. The straw was returned to the field after the previous stubble corn was harvested and planted on the October. Weeds and pests were well controlled by hand (Avoids 20–50% damage in vulnerable phases). At sowing 150 kg ha−1 phosphate fertilizer (16%) and 90 kg ha−1 potash (51%) were applied. The seeding rate was 225 kg ha−1.

PANT = Nitrogen accumulation in kernels (kg/ha or g/plant).

Total N uptake = N in kernels + N in straw/vegetative parts at maturity.

where:

NAAA = Nitrogen accumulation in kernels (e.g., kg/ha or g/plant).

Statistical analysis

The data of winter wheat growth and yield formation were processed and statistically analyzed using SAS-8.6 (SAS Institute Inc., city, NC, USA). ANOVA and least significant difference (LSD) tests, were conducted using SPSS 27 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Figures were generated using Origin 2025b (Origin Lab Inc., USA), and tables were prepared using Excel 2021 (Microsoft, USA). When there was a significant interaction effect between soil water and yield, the least significant difference (LSD) method was used for differential analysis, while the F-test was used to determine independence; the significance level was set to α = 0.05. Differences were considered statistically significant when p ≤ 0.05.

Conclusions

This study confirm that N240 optimally improve water consumption during wintering, jointing, and flowering, while N210 improves water utilization in sowing, wintering, and flowering. Both treatments boosted nitrogen accumulation earlier flowering, supporting grain filling and yield arrangement. In high-yield conditions, fallow cultivation assist balance soil water use, rising yield components. N210 and N240 increased tiller numbers, N uptake, and water use efficiency (WUE), leading to higher productivity. The keypoints are as , N240 maximizes water use in mid-late growth stages, N210 improves early-stage water efficiency, Balanced N management enhances tillering, and N accumulation, and grain yield. Both the N210 and N240 treatments promoted early nitrogen accumulation by the flowering stage, which in turn supported more efficient grain filling and a more favorable yield structure. Under high-yield conditions, these balanced nitrogen rates helped harmonize soil water usage, leading to significant improvements in key yield components. Specifically, these treatments increased tiller numbers, boosted nitrogen uptake, and enhanced overall (WUE), resulting in superior grain productivity.
