Near-Optimal and Tractable Estimation under Shift-Invariance
Dmitrii M. Ostrovskii

Abstract
How hard is it to estimate a discrete-time signal ( x 1 , … , x n ) ∈ ℂ n (x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\mathds{C}^{n} satisfying an unknown linear recurrence relation of order s s and observed in i.i.d. complex Gaussian noise? The class of all such signals is parametric but extremely rich: it contains all exponential polynomials over ℂ \mathds{C} with total degree s s , including harmonic oscillations with s s arbitrary frequencies. Geometrically, this class corresponds to the projection onto ℂ n \mathds{C}^{n} of the union of all shift-invariant subspaces of ℂ ℤ \mathds{C}^{\mathds{Z}} of dimension s s . We show that the statistical complexity of this class, as measured by the squared minimax radius of the ( 1 − δ ) (1-\delta) -confidence ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -ball, is nearly the same as for the class of s s -sparse signals, namely O ⁡ ( s ​ log ⁡ ( e ​ n ) + log ⁡ ( δ − 1 ) ) ⋅ log 2 ⁡ ( e ​ s ) ⋅ log ⁡ ( e ​ n / s ) . O\left(s\log(en)+\log(\delta^{-1})\right)\cdot\log^{2}(es)\cdot\log(en/s). Moreover, the corresponding near-minimax estimator is tractable, and it can be used to build a test statistic with a near-minimax detection threshold in the associated detection problem. These statistical results rely upon a simple analytic observation: the interpretation of the Fourier coefficients of the Christoffel function of any shift-invariant subspace of ℂ ℤ \mathds{C}^{\mathds{Z}} as a reproducing filter with the smallest possible spectrum, in all ℓ p \ell_{p} -norms, p ∈ [ 1 , ∞ ] p\in[1,\infty] , at once.

Introduction

This paper is devoted to answering two questions posed by A. Nemirovski in the 1990s (
[
1
,
2
]
):

What is the statistical complexity of estimating a solution to arbitrary,
unknown
homogeneous linear difference equation of given order?
Is there a tractable estimator?

To state them rigorously we need some notation.
Let
\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})
be the space of two-sided complex sequences, i.e.
x=(x_{t}:t\in\mathds{Z})
with
x_{t}\in\mathds{C}
.
Let
\Delta:\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})\to\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})
be the delay (a.k.a. lag, unit shift) operator, acting as
(\Delta x)_{t}=x_{t-1}
.
A homogeneous linear difference equation of order
s
is

\mathsf{f}(\Delta)x\equiv 0

(1)

where
\mathsf{f}(\cdot)
is a polynomial of degree
s
with
\mathsf{f}(0)=1
; the r.h.s. is the zero sequence.
Assume that a solution
x\in\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})
to such an equation is observed for
t\in\mathds{Z}:|t|\leqslant n
, in Gaussian noise:

y_{t}=x_{t}+\sigma\xi_{t},\quad t\in[n]_{\pm}:=\{-n,...,n\},

(2)

where
\sigma
is the noise level and
\xi_{-n},...,\xi_{n}\sim\mathds{C}{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)
are i.i.d. standard complex Gaussians.
(Changing the observation domain from
[n]_{+}:=\{1,...,n\}
to
[n]_{\pm}
is merely a matter of notation, but this change proves to be convenient for our purposes.)
Our goal is to recover
x
on
[n]_{\pm}
.
As such, while a candidate estimator
{\widehat{x}}={\widehat{x}}(y_{-n},...,y_{n})
is, formally, an element of
\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})
,
its performance is to be measured in terms of the mean-squared error (MSE) on
[n]_{\pm}
, with some marginalization over the noise distribution.
To be more precise, fixing a desired confidence level
1-\delta\in(0,1)
, for any set
X\subseteq\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})
we define the
worst-case
(over
X
)
\delta
-risk
of estimator
{\widehat{x}}
:

\mathsf{Risk}_{n,\delta}({\widehat{x}}|X):=\inf\Bigg\{\varepsilon>0:\;\mathrm{Prob}\Bigg(\frac{1}{2n+1}\sum_{t\in[n]_{\pm}}|{\widehat{x}}_{t}-x_{t}|^{2}>\varepsilon\Bigg)\leqslant\delta\;\;\;\forall x\in X\Bigg\}.

That is,
\mathsf{Risk}_{n,\delta}({\widehat{x}}|X)
is the largest, over
x\in X
, tight upper
(1-\delta)
-confidence bound for the MSE.
Define the
minimax

\delta
-risk over
X
by minimizing
\mathsf{Risk}_{n,\delta}({\widehat{x}}|X)
over all possible estimators:

\mathsf{Risk}_{n,\delta}^{\star}(X)=\inf_{{\widehat{x}}:\mathds{C}^{2n+1}\to X}\mathsf{Risk}_{n,\delta}({\widehat{x}}|X).

(3)

(Hereinafter, we always assume measurability when necessary.)
Finally, define
\mathscr{X}_{s}
as the union of the solution sets of all equations of the form (
1
) and order
s

\mathsf{f}
.
Nemirovski’s first question, in its most challenging version, amounts to computing the minimax risk
\mathsf{Risk}_{n,\delta}^{\star}(\mathscr{X}_{s})
and constructing an estimator
{\widehat{x}}^{\star}
attaining it, that is a
minimax estimator
.
More realistically, one may settle on estimating
\mathsf{Risk}_{n,\delta}^{\star}(\mathscr{X}_{s})
up to a polylogarithmic in
n,s
factor, thus exhibiting a
near-minimax
estimator.
Nemirovski’s second question is whether one can construct a near-minimax estimator which is tractable: ideally, expressed as an optimal solution to a well-structured convex optimization problem, such as a linear or semidefinite program
[
3
]
.

First, let us define the discrete convolution operator on
\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})\times\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})

(u\ast v)_{t}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathds{Z}}u_{\tau}v_{t-\tau}.

\varphi*x

\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})

Definition 1.1
.

We call
\varphi\in\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})

if
\varphi*x=x
for all
x\in X

It is clear that, for any
\varphi\in\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})

X\subseteq\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})
reproduced by
\varphi
is an SIS.
Now, let us define “band-limited” subspaces of
\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})
, which correspond to finitely-supported sequences:

\mathds{C}_{m}(\mathds{Z}):=\{x\in\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z}):\;\;|t|>m\;\Rightarrow\;x_{t}=0\}.

Note that if
\varphi\in\mathds{C}_{m}(\mathds{Z})
, the convolution
\varphi*x
corresponds to
local
linear time-invariant filtering.
Now, if
X
is an
s

\{1,...,s\}
and such that
X
is its maximal reproduced set, namely
I-\mathsf{f}(\Delta)
where

\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})

4

Yet, increasing the support we lose uniqueness of a reproducing filter, and it is natural to select a filter by minimizing certain norm.
It is, furthermore, natural to look for guarantees that are uniform over all SIS of a given dimension

, and the norm used.
As for the choice of norm(-s) to use, it turns out to be dictated by statistical considerations.
Indeed, let

Extensions.

In addition to those just discussed, we prove other results worth to be mentioned.

One-sided recovery.

In the second part of Section
2
, we prove an analogue of Proposition
1.2
where the reproducing filter is constrained to be causal (“one-sided”)—i.e. ordinary, rather than Laurent, polynomial of degree
s
.
As it turns out, in this case the factors
\mathsf{R}_{1}
and
\mathsf{R}_{2}
degrade to
s^{2}
and
s
, respectively; we show this to be optimal in the case of
\mathsf{R}_{2}
, and conjecture the bound on
\mathsf{R}_{1}
to be optimal as well.
The statistical guarantee, i.e. a counterpart of Theorem
1.2
where the estimated filter is constrained to be causal, deteriorates respectively.
This result is rigorously formulated as Theorem
3.2
in Section
3
.

Signal detection.

In Section
3
, after discussing the full version of Theorem
1.2
(cf. Theorem
3.1
) and constructing a near-minimax full-domain estimator based on it (Section
3.3
), we change our focus to the signal detection problem, first considered in
[
17
]
, where the goal is to discover the mere presence of
x\in\mathscr{X}_{s}
in the Gaussian noise
In a nutshell, it turns out that the respective near-minimax detection threshold, measured in
\ell_{2}
-norm, up to logarithmic factors matches the minimax detection threshold for
s
-sparse signals in Gaussian noise
[
18
]
. Simply put, it is almost no harder to
detect
on
\mathscr{X}_{s}
than on
\mathscr{X}({\mathds{T}}_{n}^{(s)})
.

Paper organization.

In Section
2
, we prove and discuss Proposition
1.2
, as well as a counterpart guarantee applicable to the one-sided (“causal”) filtering scenario.
Section
3
is “statistical:” in it, we present statistical guarantees for estimators based on the analytical results presented in Section
2
.
We carry out the proofs of these guarantees in Section
4
.
Some technical results on trigonometric interpolation and minimal-norm reproducing filters are deferred to appendices.
In particular, in Appendix
B
we review the properties of the Dirichlet and Fejér kernels and establish the various bounds for summation of these kernels over equidistant grids;
in Appendix
C
we exhibit the matching lower bound for the
\ell_{2}
-norm of a causal reproducing filter.

In the previous version of the paper, our proof of Proposition
1.2
was erroneous. While the mistake can be corrected (which by itself is a nontrivial exercise in harmonic analysis), we instead chose to provide an alternative, more elegant construction using the Christoffel function.

\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})
, and
\|\cdot\|_{n,p}
be the seminorm on
\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})
as follows:

\|x\|_{n,p}:=\left(\sum_{t\in[n]_{\pm}}|x_{t}|^{p}\right)^{1/p}.

We define the Hermitian form
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{n}
on
\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})
by
\langle u,v\rangle_{n}:=\sum_{|t|\leqslant n}\bar{u}_{t}v_{t}
; thus
\|x\|_{n,2}=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle_{n}}
.

Convolution and
z
-transform.

The discrete convolution
u\ast v
of
u,v\in\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})
is defined by

(u\ast v)_{t}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathds{Z}}u_{\tau}v_{t-\tau},\quad t\in\mathds{Z}.

We use the
z
-transform formalism, associating to
u\in\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})
the formal series
u(z):=\sum_{\tau\in\mathds{Z}}u_{\tau}z^{-\tau}
.
Clearly,
[u*v](z)=u(z)v(z)
, so convolution is a commutative, associative, and bilinear operation.
When viewed as a function on
{\mathds{T}}
, the
z
-transform of
u\in\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})
is a Laurent series, or Laurent polynomial if
u\in\mathds{C}_{n}(\mathds{Z})
, and is also called the discrete-time Fourier transform (DTFT).

Discrete Fourier transform.

For
n\in{\mathds{N}}
, we let
{\mathds{T}}_{n}
be the set of all
(2n+1)^{\textup{st}}
roots of unity:

{\mathds{T}}_{n}:=\left\{\chi_{k,n}:=\exp\left(\frac{i2\pi k}{2n+1}\right),\;\;k\in[n]_{\pm}\right\}.

(18)

We define the unitary
discrete Fourier transform
(DFT) operator
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}:\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})\to\mathds{C}^{2n+1}
as follows:

({\mathcal{F}}_{n}[u])_{k}=(2n+1)^{-1/2}\sum_{\tau\in[n]_{\pm}}\chi_{k,n}^{-\tau}u_{\vphantom{k,n}\tau}^{\vphantom{-\tau}}.

(Here we use our convention of indexing
\mathds{C}^{2n+1}
on
\{0,...,2n\}
; note also that
\chi_{k,n}=\chi_{k-2n-1,n}
.)
For
u\in\mathds{C}_{n}(\mathds{Z})
, this can be expressed succintly via
z
-transform:

({\mathcal{F}}_{n}[u])_{k}=(2n+1)^{-1/2}u(\chi_{k,n}^{-1}).

(19)

The restriction of
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
on
\mathds{C}_{n}(\mathds{Z})
is an isometry; its generalized inverse
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}^{\,-1}:\mathds{C}^{2n+1}\to\mathds{C}_{n}(\mathds{Z})
is

({\mathcal{F}}_{n}^{\,-1}[a])_{\tau}=(2n+1)^{-1/2}\mathds{1}\{\tau\in[n]_{\pm}\}\sum_{k=0}^{2n}\chi_{k,n}^{\tau}a_{k}^{\vphantom{\tau}}.

Furthermore, Parseval’s identity states that
\|u\|_{n,2}=\|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}[u]\|_{2}
for all
u\in\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})
.
More generally,

\left\langle u,v\right\rangle_{n}=\langle{\mathcal{F}}_{n}[u],{\mathcal{F}}_{n}[v]\rangle\quad\forall u,v\in\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z}).

(20)

Shift-invariant subspaces.

For
w_{1},...,w_{s}\in\mathds{C}
, we let
X(w_{1},...,w_{s})
be the solution set of the equation (
1
) whose characteristic polynomial is

\mathsf{f}(z)=\prod_{k\in[s]}\left(1-{w_{k}}{z}\right),

i.e. has
w_{1}^{-1},...,w_{s}^{-1}
as its roots.
Note that if all characteristic roots are distinct,
X(w_{1},...,w_{s})
is the span of
s
complex exponentials
w_{1}^{t},...,w_{s}^{t}
, i.e.
x_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{s}c_{k}^{\vphantom{t}}w_{k}^{t}
for
c_{1},...,c_{s}\in\mathds{C}
.
Repeated roots result in polynomial modulation: if the root
w_{k}^{-1}
of
\mathsf{f}
has multiplicity
m_{k}
,
\sum_{k\in[s]}m_{k}=s
, then
X(w_{1},...,w_{s})
comprises all signals of the form
x_{t}=\sum_{k\in[s]}q_{k}(t)^{\vphantom{t}}w_{k}^{t}
where
q_{k}
is a polynomial of degree
m_{k}-1
.

2
Reproducing filters with near-optimal norms

We begin this section by giving a detailed formulation of Proposition
1.2
with concrete constants.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
n+1\geqslant s
. For arbitrary
w_{1},...,w_{s}\in\mathds{C}
, the shift-invariant subspace
X=X(w_{1},...,w_{s})
admits a reproducing filter
\varphi^{X}\in\mathds{C}_{n}(\mathds{Z})
such that

\varphi^{X}(z)\in[0,1]
on
{\mathds{T}}
, as well as

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\varphi^{X}(e^{i\theta})d\theta=\frac{s}{n+1}.

(21)

As the result, the following inequalities hold simultaneously with the constant
\smash{c_{n}=\frac{2n+1}{n+1}\leqslant 2}
:

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}[\varphi^{X}]\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{(c_{n}s)^{1/p}}{\sqrt{2n+1}}\quad\forall p\in[1,\infty].

(22)

Moreover, the certificate property holds if
n\geqslant s
: one has
\varphi^{X}<1
strictly on
{\mathds{T}}\setminus\{w_{1}^{-1},\dots,w_{s}^{-1}\}
.

Remark 2.1
.

Inequality (
22
) is optimal up to a constant factor, in the sense that even without imposing
\varphi(\cdot)\in[0,1]
on
{\mathds{T}}
, the constant
c_{n}
cannot be decreased below
1
.
Indeed, fix arbitrary SIS of periodic on
[n]_{\pm}
harmonic oscillations, e.g.
X=\{x\in\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z}):x_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{s}c_{k}\chi_{k,n}^{t},\,c_{1},...,c_{s}\in\mathds{C}\}.

For any
x\in X
and
\varphi\in\mathds{C}_{n}(\mathds{Z})
, the ordinary convolution
\varphi*x
is equal to the cyclic convolution modulo
2n+1
, whence

({\mathcal{F}}_{n}[\varphi*x])=\sqrt{2n+1}\,({\mathcal{F}}_{n}[\varphi])_{k}\,({\mathcal{F}}_{n}[x])_{k}

by the DFT diagonalization property.
Since
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
is bijective,
\varphi
reproduces
X
if and only if
({\mathcal{F}}_{n}[\varphi])_{k}\equiv 1
for all
k\in\{1,...,s\}
.
Setting the remaining entries of
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}[\varphi]
to zero minimizes the norms
\|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}[\varphi]\|_{p}
for all
p\in[1,+\infty]
, and the corresponding filter
\varphi^{*}\in\mathds{C}_{n}(\mathds{Z})
satisfies

\|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}[\varphi^{*}]\|_{p}\sqrt{2n+1}\leqslant{s^{1/p}}
granted
2n+1\geqslant s
.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that in the previous example, the reproducing filter
\varphi^{*}
turned out to be nonnegative on the grid
{\mathds{T}}_{n}
, but not on
{\mathds{T}}
. If we add the latter requirement, then the constant
c_{n}
in Proposition
2.1
is sharp; in particular, it is attained by
\psi=(\phi^{*})^{2}
from the previous example.
We leave the proof as an exercise to the reader (it essentially reduces to the Fejér-Riesz theorem).

Remark 2.3
.

By the Fejér-Riesz theorem, there exists a polynomial
p(z)=\sum_{\tau=0}^{n}p_{\tau}z^{\tau}
(that can be identified with a causal filter) with complex coefficients and roots outside the unit disk, such that
\varphi^{X}(z)=|p^{X}(z)|^{2}
over
{\mathds{T}}
.
By Proposition
2.1
, this polynomial satisfies
|p(w_{k}^{-1})|=1
and
|p(z)|\leqslant 1
on
{\mathds{T}}
, where the inequality is strict on
{\mathds{T}}\setminus\{w_{1}^{-1},\dots,w_{s}^{-1}\}
once
n\geqslant s
.
At first glance, this might seem to contradict the well-known results (see e.g.
[
19
]
) on the existence certificate polynomials, namely that such polynomials only exist once the angular separation of
\{w_{1}^{-1},\dots,w_{s}^{-1}\}
—assuming these points are on the circle—exceeds the Abbe limit
\frac{2\pi}{2n+1}
; moreover, this seems to be at odds with our own results in Section
2.2
, giving an
\smash{O(\frac{s}{\sqrt{n+1}})}
sharp bound for the
\ell_{2}
-norm of a causal reproducing filter. The catch here is that the causal factor
p(\cdot)
interpolates
1
in the characteristic roots only up to a phase, whereas for it to be reproducing one would need
p(w_{k}^{-1})=1
.
This corresponds to the fact that the trigonometric polynomial
\varphi^{X}
interpolates
1
in the characteristic roots; meanwhile, the trigonometric polynomial interpolating an arbitrary sign pattern at
\{w_{1}^{-1},\dots,w_{s}^{-1}\}
, while remaining
\in(-1,1)
elsewhere on
{\mathds{T}}
, is only possible under the separation assumption (as immediately follows from the Bernstein theorem).

Finally, for the reference we recall two elementary results on the existence of reproducing filters for shift-invariant subspaces, used in prior work (see e.g.
[
16
]
) to establish Proposition
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
.
While none of these is going to be employed in the proposed construction, understanding them is a good warm-up exercise, so we provide them along with the short proofs.

Proposition 2.2

(cf.
[
16
, Prop. 2]
)
.

Any shift-invariant subspace
X
of
\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})
of dimension
s\geqslant 1
admits a reproducing filter
\phi\in\mathds{C}_{m}(\mathds{Z})
, for arbitrary
m\geqslant s-1
, such that

\|\phi\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant 2s/(2m+1).

Proof.

Let
X_{m}
be the image of
X
under the slicing map
x\in\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})\mapsto(x_{0},...,x_{m})\in\mathds{C}^{m+1}
. Clearly,
X_{m}
is a subspace of
\mathds{C}^{m+1}
with
\dim(X_{m})\leqslant s
.
Now, let
\Pi_{m}\in\mathds{C}^{(m+1)\times(m+1)}
be the coordinate matrix of the projector on
X_{m}
, in the canonical basis.
Its squared Frobenius norm is
\leqslant s
, so
\Pi_{m}
has a row
w\in\mathds{C}^{m+1}
such that
\|w\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\frac{s}{m+1}\leqslant\frac{2s}{2m+1}
.
If the position of
w
in
\Pi_{m}
is
t_{0}\in\{0,...,m\}
, then
x_{t_{0}}=\sum_{\tau=0}^{m}w_{\tau}x_{\tau}
, so by time-reversing, shifting, and zero-padding
w
we get
\phi\in\mathds{C}_{m}(\mathds{Z})
such that
\|\phi\|_{2}=\|w\|_{2}
and
x_{t_{0}}=\sum_{\tau\in[m]_{\pm}}\phi_{\tau}x_{t_{0}-\tau}
.
Finally, the shift-invariance of
X
allows to replace
t_{0}
with arbitrary
t\in\mathds{Z}
.

\square

Proposition 2.3

(
[
15
, Prop. 3]
)
.

If
X\subseteq\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})
is reproduced by
\phi\in\mathds{C}_{m}(\mathds{Z})
:
\|\phi\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant r^{2}/(2m+1)
,
then
X
is reproduced by the filter
\phi^{2}:=\phi*\phi\in\mathds{C}_{2m}(\mathds{Z})
for which

\|{\mathcal{F}}_{2m}[\phi^{2}]\|_{1}\leqslant 2r^{2}/\sqrt{4m+1}.

Proof.

That
\phi^{2}
is reproducing is due to the fact that
1-\phi^{2}(z)=(1-\phi(z))(1+\phi(z))
.
On the other hand, the norm can be estimated via Parseval’s identity and the fact that
\phi\in\mathds{C}_{m}(\mathds{Z})
:

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{F}}_{2m}[\phi^{2}]\|_{1}\sqrt{4m+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{def:DFT}}}{{=}}\sum_{z\in{\mathds{T}}_{2m}}\left|\phi^{2}(z)\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{def:DFT}}}{{=}}(4m+1)\|{\mathcal{F}}_{2m}[\phi]\|_{2}^{2}=(4m+1)\|\phi\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant 2r^{2}.

\square

Remark 2.4
.

Proposition
2.3
can be generalized to repeated autoconvolution
\phi^{k}
.
While such a result is not needed to prove Proposition
2.1
, we give it in Appendix
A.1
for completeness.

We shall now construct the reproducing filter whose existence is claimed in Proposition
2.1
.

2.1
Proof of Proposition
2.1
: construction of the reproducing filter

For
z\in\mathds{C}
, let
v_{n}(z)=[1\;z\;\cdots\;z^{n}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathds{C}^{n+1}
be the associated moment vector.
Moreover, let
\smash{\Pi_{n}\in\mathds{C}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}}
be the projector on
\smash{X_{n}=\textup{span}\{v_{n}(w_{1}),\dots,v_{n}(w_{s})\}}
, i.e. the “slice” of
X
(cf. the matrix
\Pi_{m}
in the proof of Proposition
2.2
).
Consider the following Laurent polynomial:
3
3

3

According to F. Nazarov (private communication), this proof is “from The Book and in the spirit of T. Erdélyi.”

\varphi^{X}(z)=\frac{1}{n+1}v_{n}(z^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}}\Pi_{n}v_{n}(z).

(23)

Clearly,
\varphi^{X}\in\mathds{C}_{n}(\mathds{Z})
as a linear combination of the monomials
1,z,\dots,z^{n}
and their reciprocals.
Furthermore, for
z=w_{k}
with
k\in[s]
, we have that
v_{n}(z)
belongs to
X_{n}
, which guarantees that

\varphi^{X}(w_{k})=\frac{1}{n+1}v_{n}((w_{k})^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}}v_{n}(w_{k})=1.

In other words,
1-\varphi(z)
vanishes at the reciprocal roots
w_{k}=z_{k}^{-1}
of the characteristic polynomial of
X
.
Here, we did
not
use that
\smash{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}v(z)}
is a unit vector (which holds only on
{\mathds{T}}
); instead, we only used the algebraic structure of moment vectors.
Now, for any
z\in{\mathds{T}}
we have
z^{-1}=\bar{z}
, thus

\varphi^{X}(z)=\frac{1}{n+1}v_{n}(z)^{\mathsf{H}}\Pi_{n}v_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{n+1}\|\Pi_{n}v_{n}(z)\|_{2}^{2}\quad\forall z\in{\mathds{T}}.

This implies that
\varphi^{X}(z)\in[0,1]
for any
z\in{\mathds{T}}
. Finally, to establish (
21
) we use the trace trick:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\varphi^{X}(e^{i\theta})d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\textup{tr}\left(\Pi_{n}v_{n}(e^{i\theta})v_{n}(e^{i\theta})^{\mathsf{H}}\right)d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\textup{tr}\left(\Pi_{n}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}v_{n}(e^{i\theta})v_{n}(e^{i\theta})^{\mathsf{H}}d\theta\right)=\frac{1}{n+1}\textup{tr}\left(\Pi_{n}\right)=\frac{s}{n+1}.

We used that
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{ik\theta}d\theta=0
for
k\in\mathds{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. (Note that any distinct
w_{1},\dots,w_{s}
deliver equality.)
Finally, since
\varphi^{X}=|\varphi^{X}|
on
{\mathds{T}}
and due to the cyclic property of the roots of unity, it holds that

\|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}[\varphi^{X}]\|_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n+1}}\sum_{z\in{\mathds{T}}_{n}}\varphi^{X}(z)=\sqrt{2n+1}\varphi^{X}(1)=\frac{\sqrt{2n+1}}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\varphi^{X}(e^{i\theta})d\theta\leqslant\frac{s\sqrt{2n+1}}{n+1}.

This verifies (
22
) for
p=1
, whence the case of any
p\in(1,\infty)
follows by Hölder’s inequality.

\square

Remark 2.5
.

Note that the reproducing filter in (
23
) can be interpreted as the average of those obtained from the rows of
\Pi_{n}
, in contrast to the one row used in Proposition
2.2
.

We note that in the theory of orthogonal polynomials, the construction in (
23
) is known as the Christoffel function. Recently, it found numerous applications in statistical theory and optimization
[
20
]
. Our interpretation and application of the Christoffel function seem to be new.

2.2
One-sided reproducing filters for quasi-stable SIS

We now establish an analogue of Proposition
2.1
under the constraint that the filter is one-sided.

Additional notation.

Let
\mathds{C}_{n}^{+}(\mathds{Z})
be the set of sequences supported within
[n]_{+}:=\{0,...,n\}
, that is
\varphi\in\mathds{C}_{n}^{+}(\mathds{Z})
if and only if
\varphi
is a polynomial with
\deg(\varphi)\leqslant n
.
Note that
u\in\mathds{C}_{2n}^{+}(\mathds{Z})
if and only if
\Delta^{-n}u\in\mathds{C}_{n}(\mathds{Z})
.
We succintly define the one-sided DFT operator
{\mathcal{F}}_{2n}^{+}:\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})\to\mathds{C}^{2n+1}
,

{\mathcal{F}}_{2n}^{+}[u]:={\mathcal{F}}_{n}^{+}[\Delta^{-n}u].

It is well-known that for
X(w_{1},..,w_{s})
to admit a sequence of reproducing filters
\varphi\in\mathds{C}_{n}^{+}(\mathds{Z})
whose norms are vanishing as
n\to\infty
, one must have quasi-stability, i.e.
w_{j}\in{\mathds{D}}

\forall j\in\{1,...,s\}
where
{\mathds{D}}
is the closed unit disk. (This is clear for
s=1
: if
|w|>1
and
\phi=(\phi_{1},...,\phi_{n})\in\mathds{C}^{n}
is such that
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bar{\phi}_{k}w^{-k}=1
, then
\|\phi\|_{2}\geqslant\sqrt{1-|w|^{-2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bar{\phi}_{k}w^{-k}\geqslant\sqrt{1-|w|^{-2}}>0
for all
n
.)
As such, in the context of one-sided reproducing filters, we require that SIS is a quasi-stable one.

Proposition 2.4
.

For some constants
c_{0},c_{1}>0
, the following holds.
For all
n\geqslant c_{0}s^{2}\log(2s)
, any SIS
X(w_{1},...,w_{s})
with
w_{1},...,w_{s}\in{\mathds{D}}
admits a reproducing filter
\varphi^{X}_{+}\in\mathds{C}_{2n}^{+}(\mathds{Z})
such that

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{F}}_{2n}^{+}[\varphi^{X}_{+}]\|_{p}\leqslant\frac{c_{1}(s^{2}\log(en))^{1/p}}{\sqrt{2n+1}}\quad\forall p\in[1,\infty].

(24)

Compared to the situation of Proposition
2.1
, the scaling factor
\mathsf{R}_{p}
for the
\ell_{p}
-norm degrades from
s^{1/p}
to
s^{2/p}
times a logarithmic factor.
This deterioration is due to the fact that we cannot use the filter
\phi
from Proposition
2.2
anymore, since this filter is two-sided by construction. (Which, in turn, is because in the pigeonhole trick employed to prove Proposition
2.2
, one does not know
which
row of the projector
\Pi_{m}
has minimal norm.)
Instead, Proposition
2.4
is immediately implied by the following, more delicate result (proved by complex-analytic methods).

16

)
.

3
Statistical guarantees

Roadmap.

In this section, we carry out the statistical “program” announced in Section
1
. Namely, in Section
3.1
, we formulate and discuss the unabridged version of Theorem
1.2
, i.e. a guarantee on the MSE of estimating the signal “slice”
(x_{t},t\in[n]_{\pm})
with an estimate of the form
{\widehat{x}}={\widehat{\varphi}}*y
, where the data-driven
{\widehat{\varphi}}\in\mathds{C}_{n}(\mathds{Z})
is built from the observations
(y_{t},t\in[2n]_{\pm})
. In Section
3.2
, we make a brief detour to the “one-sided” setting, constructing an estimate
{\widehat{x}}={\widehat{\varphi}}*y
that uses a
causal
data-driven filter
{\widehat{\varphi}}\in\mathds{C}_{2n}^{+}(\mathds{Z})
; in a sense, Section
3.2
stands in the same position towards Section
3.1
as Section
2.2
towards the preceding part of Section
2
. In Section
3.3
, we revisit the “two-sided” guarantee of Section
3.1
and use it, together with a multiscale procedure, to build an estimate of
(x_{t},t\in[2n]_{\pm})
.
Finally, in Section
3.4
, we focus on the associated
signal detection
problem, where the goal is to detect the presence of
x\in\mathscr{X}_{s}
in the noise.
To streamline the exposition, we postpone the proofs of all results to be presented in this section to Section
4
.

3.1
Estimation “on the core”

Recalling Theorem
1.1
, consider the estimate
{\widehat{x}}_{t}=({\widehat{\varphi}}*y)_{t}
with the following data-driven filter:

{\widehat{\varphi}}\in\mathop{\hbox{\rm Argmin}}_{\varphi\in\mathds{C}_{n}(\mathds{Z})}\left\{\|\varphi*y-y\|_{n,2}^{2}:\;\;\max\{\|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}[\varphi]\|_{1},{2s}\|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}[\varphi]\|_{\infty}\}\leqslant\frac{{2s}}{\sqrt{2n+1}}\right\}.

(25)

In plain words, (
25
) corresponds to minimizing the
\ell_{2}
-norm residual of a convolution-type estimate
\varphi*y
under the
\ell_{1}
- and
\ell_{\infty}
-norm constraints on the filter DFT matching the bounds (
22
) for the reproducing filter (cf. Proposition
2.1
).
Same as in the proof of Proposition
2.1
, it suffices to impose only the constraints on the
\ell_{1}
- and
\ell_{\infty}
-norm; any other
\ell_{p}
-norm can then be controlled via Hölder’s inequality, and in our statistical analysis we use the bound on the
\ell_{2}
-norm as well.

Remark 3.1
.

Observe that (
25
) is a well-structured convex program that can be efficiently solved by adapting first-order methods developed in
[
21
]
for the purpose of solving (
11
); in particular, the gradient oracle can be implemented via Fast Fourier Transform.
In order to do so, one has to implement the proximal mapping for the complex version of the polytopal ball
[
22
]
, i.e. the intersection of a complex
\ell_{1}
-ball and complex
\ell_{\infty}
-ball.
This can be done, e.g., by running a variant of the alternating projections method
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
or extending the algorithm of
[
28
]
for efficient projection onto the intersection of norm balls to the case of a complex space.

Next we characterize the mean-squared error of the estimate in (
25
) on the “core” interval.

Theorem 3.1
.

In the assumptions of Proposition
2.1
, an optimal solution
\hat{\varphi}\in\mathds{C}_{n}(\mathds{Z})
to (
25
) satisfies, for arbitrary
\delta\in(0,1)
, the following inequality with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\left\|{\widehat{\varphi}}*y-x\right\|_{n,2}^{2}\leqslant{36}\,\sigma^{2}\left({s}\log(2n+1)+{2s}+\log(\delta^{-1})\right)\log^{2}(e^{4}s).

(26)

Discussion.

The result we have just stated is near-optimal:
as mentioned in the discussion following Theorem
1.1
, neglecting the
\log^{2}(s)
factors, the right-hand side of (
26
) matches the lower bound attained on the class of
periodic
harmonic oscillations, i.e. signals with
sparse
DFT.
This observation can be interpreted as the statistical counterpart of Remark
2.1
: the subclass
\mathscr{X}({\mathds{T}}_{n}^{(s)})
is nearly worst-case in
\mathscr{X}_{s}
, from the statistical viewpoint as well.

Let us now make additional, more technical remarks regarding Theorem
3.1
and its proof.

Firstly, the leading term
s\log(2n+1)
in (
26
) appears as the result of bounding the suprema of two stochastic processes with subexponential tails that arise in our error decomposition, namely
\sup_{\varphi\in\Phi}\langle\xi,\varphi*\xi\rangle_{n}
and
\sup_{\varphi\in\Phi}\|\varphi*\xi\|_{n,2}^{2}
, where
\Phi
is the feasible set in (
25
).
Our control of these suprema, cf. (
56
)–(
60
) and (
66
)–(
72
) respectively, relies on the Krein-Milman theorem and union bound over the extreme points of the set
\{u\in\mathds{C}^{2n+1}:\max\{\|u\|_{1},s\|u\|_{\infty}\}\leqslant s\}
, which are
s
-sparse binary vectors with arbitrarily shifted phases.
Factoring out the phases leaves us with
N_{s}={2n+1\choose s}
extreme points, so the union bound results in
\log{N_{s}}\asymp s\log(2n+1)
overhead.

Secondly, using Proposition
2.1
with Theorem
1.1
also give the guarantee

O(\frac{s\sigma^{2}}{2n+1}\log(en\delta^{-1}))

for the
\ell_{1}
-constrained estimate (
11
).
The bound (
26
) improves the confidence-dependent term from
s\log(\delta^{-1})
to
\log^{2}(s)\log(\delta^{-1})
by incorporating the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm constraint into the estimation process.
One can envision applications where this improvement is crucial, e.g., in the context of generalized linear models
[
29
,
30
]
, where one has to take the union bound over an exponential in
s
number of events; this would render the
s\log(\delta^{-1})
term into
s^{2}
even in the fixed-probability regime, thus breaking the
{\widetilde{O}}(s/n)
parametric rate; see
[
29
, Sec. 4]
for a more technical discussion.
This improvement comes at the very modest price of
\log^{2}(s)
inflation of the
\delta
-independent term.

Finally, it is an interesting question whether the
\log^{2}(s)
factor in (
26
) is an artifact of our analysis.
This factor appears when we bound the measurements of random processes
\langle\xi,\varphi*\xi\rangle_{n}
and
\|\varphi*\xi\|_{n,2}^{2}
at extremal filters
\varphi
, i.e. such that
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}[\varphi]
is an
s
-sparse vector with nonzero entries of the same magnitude. To this end, we use the classical oversampling trick, extracting ordinary convolution
(\xi*\varphi)_{t},

t\in[n]_{\pm}
from the cyclic convolution of the noise, modulo
4n+1
, with the filter
\varphi
padded with zeroes on the sides; see e.g. (
41
). Then,
\log(s)
appears as the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm of an oversampled filter with a sparse DFT, i.e. the sum of the Dirichlet kernel over a subset of
s
nodes of the DFT grid (see Lemma
B.4
). It is unclear how to sidestep the Dirichlet kernel here.

Fortunately, there is a better approach: it turns out that a nearly linear in
s
bound can be ensured by employing instead a multiscale technique that we are about to present next.

3.3
Full-domain estimation and minimax risk on
\mathscr{X}_{s}

Our technique for constructing the full-domain estimate can be summarized as follows.
Starting from the initial interval
[-n,n]
, take the next interval adjacent to the previous one on the right (resp. left) and shorter by a constant factor; fit an estimate on this interval; repeat this process until the interval length shrinks to
O(s)
.
Formally, assuming first that
s
and
n[\geqslant{3s}]
are powers of
3
, we implement the following procedure:

1.

Let
{\widehat{x}}_{t}^{\mathsf{full}}:=({\widehat{\varphi}}^{(0)}*y)_{t}^{\vphantom{\mathsf{full}}}
for
|t|\leqslant n
, where
{\widehat{\varphi}}^{(0)}
is an optimal solution to (
25
).

2.

Letting
n_{k}:=n3^{-k}
and
h_{k}:=2n-2n_{k}=2n(1-3^{-k})
, define two families of intervals:

\begin{aligned} I^{(k,+)}:=\left(2n-n_{k-1},2n-n_{k}\right]&=(h_{k}-n_{k},h_{k}+n_{k}],\\
I^{(k,-)}:=\left[n_{k}-2n,n_{k-1}-2n\right)&=[-h_{k}-n_{k},-h_{k}+n_{k})\end{aligned}\;\;\text{for}\;\;k\in\left\{1,...,K:=\log_{3}\left(\frac{n}{{3s}}\right)\right\}.

Clearly, we have
n_{K}={3s}
and
|I^{(k,+)}\cap\mathds{Z}|=|I^{(k,-)}\cap\mathds{Z}|=2n_{k}\geqslant{6s}
.
Moreover, it holds that
\bigsqcup_{k=1}^{K}I^{(k,+)}=(n,2n-{3s}]
and
\bigsqcup_{k=1}^{K}I^{(k,-)}=[-2n+{3s},-n)
.
In other words,
I^{(k+1,+)}
(resp.
I^{(k+1,-)}
) is appended to the previous interval
I^{(k,+)}
(resp.
I^{(k,-)}
) on the right (resp. left), taking up
2/3
of the remaining gap
[2n-n_{k},2n]
(resp.
[-2n,-2n+n_{k}]
); this process is repeated until the interval length becomes
O(s)
, at which point we terminate.

3.

For
k\in\{1,...,K\}
, we solve the counterparts of (
25
) on
I^{(k,+)}
and
I^{(k,-)}
instead of
[-n,n]
:

\displaystyle\vskip-2.84544pt{\widehat{\varphi}}^{(k,+)}

\displaystyle\in\mathop{\hbox{\rm Argmin}}_{\varphi\in\mathds{C}_{n_{k}}(\mathds{Z})}\left\{\|\Delta^{-h_{k}}[\varphi*y-y]\|_{n_{k},2}:\,\max\{\|{\mathcal{F}}_{n_{k}}[\varphi]\|_{1},{2s}\|{\mathcal{F}}_{n_{k}}[\varphi]\|_{\infty}\}\leqslant\frac{{2s}}{\sqrt{2n_{k}+1}}\right\},

(29)

\displaystyle{\widehat{\varphi}}^{(k,-)}

\displaystyle\in\mathop{\hbox{\rm Argmin}}_{\varphi\in\mathds{C}_{n_{k}}(\mathds{Z})}\left\{\|\Delta^{+h_{k}}[\varphi*y-y]\|_{n_{k},2}:\,\max\{\|{\mathcal{F}}_{n_{k}}[\varphi]\|_{1},{2s}\|{\mathcal{F}}_{n_{k}}[\varphi]\|_{\infty}\}\leqslant\frac{{2s}}{\sqrt{2n_{k}+1}}\right\}.

4.

We let
{\widehat{x}}_{t}^{\mathsf{full}}:=({\widehat{\varphi}}^{(k,\pm)}*y)_{t}^{\vphantom{\mathsf{full}}}
for
t\in I^{(k,\pm)}
respectively, and
{\widehat{x}}_{t}^{\mathsf{full}}:=y_{t}^{\vphantom{\mathsf{full}}}
for
t:|t|>2n-{3s}
.

Note that, the resulting estimate
({\widehat{x}}_{t}^{\mathsf{full}},|t|\leqslant 2n)
uses the observations
(y_{t}^{\vphantom{\mathsf{full}}},|t|\leqslant 2n)
, as desired.
The following result—essentially, a corollary of Theorem
3.1
—shows that our technique leads to essentially the same error on the whole domain as “in the core,” up to an
O(\log(n/s))
overhead.

Corollary 3.1
.

Assume
x\in\mathscr{X}_{s}(\mathds{C})
,
n\geqslant{3s}
, and
n,s
are powers of
3
.
Estimate
{\widehat{x}}_{t}^{\mathsf{full}}
satisfies

\big\|{\widehat{x}}^{\mathsf{full}}-x\big\|_{2n,2}^{2}\leqslant{80}\,\sigma^{2}\left({s}\log(2n+1)+{3s}+\log(\delta^{-1})\right)\log^{2}(e^{4}s)\,\log_{3}(n/s).

Finally, as we discuss next, the assumption that
n,s
are powers of 3 can be easily removed.

Remark 3.3
.

In the general case, the procedure described above can be adjusted as follows.
Let
s_{0}:=3^{\lceil\log_{3}(s)\rceil}\in[s,3s]
,
n_{0}:=3^{\lfloor\log_{3}(n)\rfloor}
, and
K_{0}:=\log_{3}(n_{0}/s_{0})-1
.
Now, cover
[-2n,2n]
with at most
3
overlapping intervals of halfwidth
2n_{0}
; specifically, consider the decomposition

[-2n,2n]\;=\;[-2n,-2n+4n_{0}]\,\cup\,[-2n_{0},2n_{0}]\,\cup\,[2n-4n_{0},2n].

When
n=3^{m}
for
m\in{\mathds{N}}
, one has
n_{0}=n
, and all three intervals
{\widetilde{I}}^{(-1)},{\widetilde{I}}^{(0)},{\widetilde{I}}^{(1)}
in the right-hand side coincide.
When
n=3^{m}-1
, the intervals have almost no overlap (this is “the worst case”).
Now, let us run the procedure separately on each of the three intervals (with
s_{0},n_{0},K_{0}
instead of
s,n,K
) to form the estimates
{\widetilde{x}}_{t}{}^{(-1)},{\widetilde{x}}_{t}{}^{(0)},{\widetilde{x}}_{t}{}^{(1)}
, each defined on the respective interval.
Output
{\widehat{x}}_{t}={\widetilde{x}}_{t}{}^{(0)}
for
t\in{\widetilde{I}}^{(0)}\setminus({\widetilde{I}}^{(-1)}\cup{\widetilde{I}}^{(1)})
,
{\widehat{x}}_{t}={\widetilde{x}}_{t}{}^{(\pm 1)}
for
t\in{\widetilde{I}}^{(\pm 1)}\setminus({\widetilde{I}}^{(0)}\cup{\widetilde{I}}^{(\mp 1)})
, and
in the overlaps simply average the estimates:
{\widetilde{x}}_{t}=\mbox{\small$\frac{1}{2}$}({\widetilde{x}}_{t}{}^{(0)}+{\widetilde{x}}_{t}{}^{(\pm 1)})
for
t\in{\widetilde{I}}^{(0)}\cap{\widetilde{I}}^{(\pm 1)}
.
By the triangle inequality and union bound, the guarantee of Corollary
3.1
is preserved up to a constant factor.

Note that the result we have just established immediately implies the previously announced bound (
17
) for the minimax
\delta
-risk over
\mathscr{X}_{s}
.

3.4
Signal detection

We now focus on the detection counterpart of the estimation problem considered previously.
Namely, we are to test the null hypothesis that the observations
(y_{t},|t|\leqslant 2n)
contain no signal,

\mathscr{H}_{0,n}:=\{x\in\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z}):\|x\|_{2n,2}=0\},

(30)

against the alternative that
x
belongs to some SIS of dimension
s
and is of large enough norm:

\mathscr{H}_{s,n}(\mathsf{r}):=\{x\in\mathscr{X}_{s}(\mathds{C}):\|x\|_{2n,2}\geqslant\mathsf{r}\}.

(31)

Note that this setup naturally extends that of detecting a sparse signal (see e.g.
[
18
]
): the latter one can be recovered if we replace
\mathscr{X}_{s}
in (
31
) with
\smash{\mathscr{X}({\mathds{T}}_{2n}^{(s)})}
.
We do not consider here the superficially more general setup of
[
17
]
, where the null hypothesis allows
x
to come from a known SIS: our methodology can be extended to accommodate for this, but at the cost of obscuring the essential ideas in the proofs.
Our testing procedure compares the statistic

{\widehat{T}}_{n}(y):=\|y\|_{2n,2}^{2}-\|y-{\widehat{x}}^{\mathsf{full}}\|_{2n,2}^{2},

(32)

where
{\widehat{x}}^{\mathsf{full}}
is the full-domain estimate from Section
3.3
, against the threshold value
\mathsf{r}_{0}^{2}
that corresponds to the in-probability bound for
\|{\widehat{x}}^{\mathsf{full}}-x\|_{2n,2}^{2}
established in Corollary
3.1
, and rejects the null whenever the threshold is surpassed.
It admits the following statistical guarantee.

Theorem 3.3
.

Consider testing
\mathscr{H}_{0,n}
, cf. (
30
), against
\mathscr{H}_{s,n}(\mathsf{r})
, cf. (
31
), assuming
n\geqslant{3s}
and

\mathsf{r}^{2}\geqslant\mathsf{r}_{0}^{2}:={300}\,\sigma^{2}\big(\,2s\log(2n+1)+6s+\log(e^{3}s)\log\left(6{\log_{3}(n/s)}{\delta^{-1}}\right)\big)\log(e^{4}s)\,\log_{3}(n/s).

The test rejecting
\mathscr{H}_{0,n}
when
{\widehat{T}}_{n}(y)>{\tfrac{3}{8}}\mathsf{r}_{0}^{2}
makes errors of either type with probability at most
\delta
.

Remark 3.4
.

The proof of Theorem
3.3
, given in the next section, is similar to the combined proof of Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
3.1
, because the various error terms that must be handled in the proof of Theorem
3.1

\langle\xi,{\widehat{\varphi}}*\xi\rangle_{n}

3.1

3.1

Remark 3.5
.

The detection boundary
\mathsf{r}=\mathsf{r}_{0}
established in Theorem
3.3
is known to be near-optimal, up to a logarithmic factor, for sparse regression in the asymptotic highly sparse regime, namely when
s,n\to\infty
with
s\asymp d^{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
, where
d
is the ambient dimension
[
18
]
; this translates to
s\sim n^{\alpha}
, in the denoising version of our problem, i.e. when the condition
x\in\mathscr{X}_{s}
in the definition of
\mathscr{H}_{s,n}(\mathsf{r})
, cf. (
31
), is replaced with
x\in\mathscr{X}({\mathds{T}}_{2n}^{(s)})
.
On the other hand, in the “moderately sparse” regime (
\mbox{\small$\frac{1}{2}$}\leqslant\alpha<1
) the asymptotic detection boundary is known to be
\mathsf{r}^{2}\asymp s^{1/2}(\asymp\mathsf{r}_{0})
, cf.
[
18
]
, i.e. detection is possible at low signal-to-noise ratios. We anticipate that Theorem
3.3
can also be extended in this direction, but we have not attempted to do this.

4
Proofs of statistical results

Preliminaries for proving Theorems
3.1
and
3.3
.

Let
{\widehat{\varphi}}={\widehat{\varphi}}^{(0)}\in\mathds{C}_{n}(\mathds{Z})
be optimal in (
25
), and let
{\widehat{\varphi}}^{(k,\pm)}\in\mathds{C}_{n_{k}}(\mathds{Z})
, for
k\in\{1,..,K\}
and
n_{k}=n3^{-k}
, be as in (
29
); in other words,

{\widehat{x}}_{t}^{\mathsf{full}}=({\widehat{\varphi}}^{(k,\pm)}*y)_{t}^{\vphantom{\mathsf{full}}}\;\;\text{for}\;\;t\in I^{(k,\pm)}.

Finally, let
\varphi^{(0)}\in\mathds{C}_{n}(\mathds{Z})
and
\varphi^{(k,\pm)}\in\mathds{C}_{n_{k}}(\mathds{Z})
, for
k\in\{1,..,K\}
, be feasible solutions to (
25
) and (
29
) respectively, as exhibited in Proposition
2.1
.
That is, letting
X
be the shift-invariant subspace containing
x
with
\dim(X)=s
,
\varphi^{(k,\pm)}
are reproducing for
X
and satisfy

\|{\mathcal{F}}_{n_{k}}[\varphi^{(k,\pm)}]\|_{1}\leqslant\frac{{2s}}{\sqrt{2n_{k}+1}},\quad\|\varphi^{(k,\pm)}\|_{2}\leqslant\frac{{\sqrt{2s}}}{\sqrt{2n_{k}+1}},\quad\|{\mathcal{F}}_{n_{k}}[\varphi^{(k,\pm)}]\|_{\infty}\leqslant\frac{{1}}{\sqrt{2n_{k}+1}};

(33)

similarly for
\varphi^{(0)}=\varphi^{\star}
with
n_{0}=n
.
We find it somewhat more convenient to prove Theorem
3.3
first, and subsequently recycle the estimates of various error terms in the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

4.1
Proof of Theorem
3.3

Type II error.

\bm{{1}^{o}}
.
Consider the following decomposition of the test statistic:

\displaystyle{\widehat{T}}_{n}(y)

\displaystyle=\|y\|_{2n,2}^{2}-\|y-{\widehat{x}}^{\mathsf{full}}\|_{2n-3s,2}^{2}

(34)

\displaystyle=\|y\|_{2n-3s,2}^{2}-\|y-{\widehat{x}}^{\mathsf{full}}\|_{2n-3s,2}^{2}+\|y\|_{2n,2}^{2}-\|y\|_{2n-3s,2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\|y\|_{n,2}^{2}-\big\|y-{\widehat{\varphi}}^{(0)}*y\big\|_{n,2}^{2}}_{{\widehat{T}}_{n}^{(0)}(y)}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\underbrace{\|\Delta^{\mp h_{k}}[y]\|_{n_{k},2}^{2}-\big\|\Delta^{\mp h_{k}}[y-{\widehat{\varphi}}^{(k,\pm)}*y]\big\|_{n_{k},2}^{2}}_{{\widehat{T}}_{n}^{(k,\pm)}(y)}

\displaystyle+\|y\|_{2n,2}^{2}-\|y\|_{2n-3s,2}^{2}.

Here
{\widehat{T}}_{n}^{(k,+)}
or
{\widehat{T}}_{n}^{(k,-)}
examines the respective
I^{(k,+)}
or
I^{(k,-)}
, idem for
{\widehat{T}}_{n}^{(0)}
and
I^{(0)}:=[-n,n]
; the term
\|y\|_{2n,2}^{2}-\|y\|_{2n-3s,2}^{2}
appears as
{\widehat{x}}_{t}^{\mathsf{full}}=y_{t}^{\vphantom{\mathsf{full}}}
for
t:|t|>2n-3s
.
Focusing on
I^{(0)}
w.l.o.g.,

\displaystyle{\widehat{T}}_{n}^{(0)}(y)\geqslant T_{n}^{(0)}(y)

\displaystyle:=\|y\|_{n,2}^{2}-\|y-\varphi^{(0)}*y\|_{n,2}^{2}

\displaystyle\;=\|y\|_{n,2}^{2}-\sigma^{2}\|\xi-\varphi^{(0)}*\xi\|_{n,2}^{2}

\displaystyle\;=\|x\|_{n,2}^{2}-\sigma^{2}\|\varphi^{(0)}*\xi\|_{n,2}^{2}+2\sigma^{2}\operatorname{\mathsf{Re}}\langle\xi,\varphi^{(0)}*\xi\rangle_{n}+2\sigma\operatorname{\mathsf{Re}}\langle\xi,x\rangle_{n}

(35)

where we first used that
\varphi^{(0)}
is feasible and reproducing for
X
, and then expanded the squares.

\bm{{2}^{o}}
.
Define the matrix-valued map
{\mathsf{Rest}}_{p,n}:\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})\to\mathds{C}^{(2p+1)\times(2p+2n+1)}
encoding the convolution,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{Rest}}_{p,n}(\varphi)

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{-n}\;&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\varphi_{n}&0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
0&\varphi_{-n}&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\varphi_{n}&0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\hphantom{\cdots}&\hphantom{\cdots}&\hphantom{\cdots}&\ddots&\ddots&\hphantom{\cdots}&\vdots\\
\vdots&\hphantom{\cdots}&\ddots&\ddots&\hphantom{\cdots}&\hphantom{\cdots}&\hphantom{\cdots}&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0&\varphi_{-n}&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\varphi_{n}&0\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0&\varphi_{-n}&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\varphi_{n}\end{pmatrix},

(36)

and its counterpart
{\mathsf{Circ}}_{p,n}:\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})\to\mathds{C}^{(2p+2n+1)\times(2p+2n+1)}
encoding the circular convolution,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{Circ}}_{p,n}(\varphi)

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}\varphi_{0}&\cdots&\cdots&\varphi_{n}&0&\cdots&0&\varphi_{-n}&\cdots&\varphi_{-1}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\hphantom{\cdots}&\hphantom{\cdots}&\ddots&\ddots&\hphantom{\cdots}&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\varphi_{-n+1}&\cdots&\varphi_{0}&\cdots&\cdots&\varphi_{n}&0&\cdots&0&\varphi_{-n}\\
\end{matrix}\\
\hline\cr\\
{\mathsf{Rest}}_{p,n}(\varphi)\\
\\
\hline\cr\begin{matrix}\quad\varphi_{n}&0&\cdots&0&\varphi_{-n}&\cdots&\cdots&\varphi_{0}&\cdots&\varphi_{n-1}\\
\quad\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\hphantom{\cdots}&\ddots&\ddots&\hphantom{\cdots}&\hphantom{\cdots}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\quad\varphi_{1}&\cdots&\varphi_{n}&0&\cdots&0&\varphi_{-n}&\cdots&\cdots&\varphi_{0}\\
\end{matrix}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(37)

Also, define the “slicing” map
\{\cdot\}_{n}:\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})\to\mathds{C}^{2n+1}
by
\{x\}_{n}:=(x_{-n},\;\cdots\;x_{n})^{\mathsf{T}}
.
For
\varphi\in\mathds{C}_{n}(\mathds{Z})
,

\{\varphi*\xi\}_{n}={\mathsf{Rest}}_{n,n}(\varphi)\,\{\xi\}_{2n},

(38)

whence for the negative term in (
35
) we get

\displaystyle\big\|\varphi^{(0)}*\xi\big\|_{n,2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\{\xi\}_{2n}^{\mathsf{H}}Q^{(0)}\{\xi\}_{2n}^{\vphantom{\mathsf{H}}}\quad\text{with}\quad Q^{(0)}:={\mathsf{Rest}}_{n,n}(\varphi^{(0)})^{\mathsf{H}}{\mathsf{Rest}}_{n,n}(\varphi^{(0)}).

(39)

Here the matrix
Q^{(0)}
is positive-semidefinite, and we can explicitly compute its trace:

\textup{tr}(Q^{(0)})=\|{\mathsf{Rest}}_{n,n}(\varphi^{(0)})\|_{{\mathsf{F}}}^{2}=(2n+1)\|\varphi^{(0)}\|_{2}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:side-filter-norms}}}{{\leqslant}}{2s}.

(40)

By the DFT diagonalization property,
{\mathsf{Circ}}_{p,n}(\varphi)
with
\varphi\in\mathds{C}_{n}(\mathds{Z})
is unitary equivalent to the diagonal matrix
\sqrt{2p+2n+1}\,{\hbox{\rm Diag}}({\mathcal{F}}_{p+n}[\varphi])
.
As
{\mathsf{Rest}}_{n,n}(\varphi)
is a submatrix of
{\mathsf{Circ}}_{n,n}(\varphi)
, we get

\displaystyle\big\|Q^{(0)}\big\|_{\textsf{op}}\leqslant\|{\mathsf{Rest}}_{n,n}(\varphi^{(0)})\|_{\textsf{op}}^{2}\leqslant\|{\mathsf{Circ}}_{n,n}(\varphi^{(0)})\|_{\textsf{op}}^{2}=(4n+1)\|{\mathcal{F}}_{2n}[\varphi^{(0)}]\|_{\infty}^{2}.

(41)

By (
33
),
\sqrt{2n+1}{\mathcal{F}}_{n}[\varphi^{(0)}]
belongs to the set
\mathds{C}_{1,\infty}^{2n+1}({2s})
, see Appendix
A.2
, for which

\max_{w\in\mathds{C}_{1,\infty}^{2n+1}({2s})}\|{\mathcal{F}}_{2n}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}[{\mathcal{F}}_{n}^{\,-1}[w]]\|_{\infty}\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{4n+1}}(\log{s}+3)=\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{4n+1}}\log(e^{3}{s}).

When combined with (
41
) this gives

\displaystyle\big\|Q^{(0)}\big\|_{\textsf{op}}\leqslant\log^{2}(e^{3}{s}),

(42)

whence for the Frobenius norm (cf. (
40
)):

\big\|Q^{(0)}\big\|_{{\mathsf{F}}}\leqslant\sqrt{\big\|Q^{(0)}\big\|_{\textsf{op}}\textup{tr}\big(Q^{(0)}\big)}\leqslant\sqrt{2s}\log(e^{3}{s}).

(43)

Now, recall the deviation bound for an indefinite Hermitian form of a complex Gaussian vector,

\mathrm{Prob}\big\{\mbox{\small$\frac{1}{2}$}\xi^{\mathsf{H}}Q\xi\leqslant\textup{tr}(Q)+\|Q\|_{{\mathsf{F}}}\sqrt{2\log(\delta^{-1})}+\|Q\|_{\textsf{op}}\log(\delta^{-1})\big\}\geqslant 1-\delta.

(44)

(It can be easily verified by combining the right and left tail bounds for
\chi_{2}^{2}
from
[
31
, Lem. 1]
.)

\|\phi^{k}\|_{2}\leqslant\|{\mathcal{F}}_{km}[\phi^{k}]\|_{1}\leqslant\frac{c_{k}\mathsf{R}^{k}}{\sqrt{2km+1}}

where
c_{k}=2^{k-1}
for
k=2^{p}
and
c_{2k+1}\leqslant 3\sqrt{2k+1}c_{k}^{2}
for
k\in{\mathds{N}}
; as such,
\log(c_{k})=O(k\log k)
.

Proof.

The reproducing property follows from
1-\phi^{k}(z)=(1-\phi(z))(1+\phi(z)+...+\phi^{k-1}(z))
.
For
k=2^{p}
, we proceed by induction over
p
: assume that
\|\phi^{k}\|_{2}\leqslant\|{\mathcal{F}}_{km}[\phi^{k}]\|_{1}\leqslant\frac{c_{k}\mathsf{R}^{k}}{\sqrt{2km+1}}
, then

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{F}}_{2km}[\phi^{2k}]\|_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{def:DFT}}}{{=}}\sqrt{4km+1}\|{\mathcal{F}}_{2km}[\phi^{k}]\|_{2}^{2}=\sqrt{4km+1}\|\phi^{k}\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{4km+1}\,c_{k}^{2}\mathsf{R}^{2k}}{2km+1}\leqslant\frac{2c_{k}^{2}\mathsf{R}^{2k}}{\sqrt{4km+1}}.

Whence
c_{2k}^{\vphantom{2}}=2c_{k}^{2}
for all
k\in{\mathds{N}}
.
Unrolling this recursion we arrive at
c_{2^{p}}=2^{\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}2^{j}}=2^{2^{p}-1}
.
Now, to bound
c_{2k+1}
in terms of
c_{k}
observe that

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{F}}_{(2k+1)m}[\phi^{k}]\|_{1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{def:DFT}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(2k+1)m+1}}\sum_{z\in{\mathds{T}}_{(2k+1)m}}\left|\phi^{2k+1}(z)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant(2(2k+1)m+1)\,\|{\mathcal{F}}_{(2k+1)m}[\phi]\|_{\infty}\|{\mathcal{F}}_{(2k+1)m}[\phi^{k}]\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant(2(2k+1)m+1)\,\|\phi\|_{2}^{\vphantom{2}}\,\|\phi^{k}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2(2k+1)m+1}{2km+1}\,\frac{c_{k}^{2}\mathsf{R}^{2k+1}}{\sqrt{2m+1}}\leqslant\frac{2k+1}{k}\,\frac{c_{k}^{2}\mathsf{R}^{2k+1}}{\sqrt{2m+1}}\leqslant\frac{3\sqrt{2k+1}c_{k}^{2}\mathsf{R}^{2k+1}}{\sqrt{2(2k+1)m+1}}.

As such,
c_{2k+1}^{\vphantom{2}}\leqslant 3\sqrt{2k+1}c_{k}^{2}
, indeed.
Finally, to verify that
\log(c_{k})=O(k\log k)
for all
k\geqslant 2
, it suffices to consider the binary representation of
k
, and alternate between the two cases.

\square

A.2
Extreme points of the ball of the norm
\max\{\|\cdot\|_{1},s\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\}
on
\mathds{C}^{n}

In what follows, we let

and define the sets
\mathds{C}_{1,\infty}^{n}(s),\mathds{R}_{1,\infty}^{n}(s),\mathds{R}_{+;1,\infty}^{n}(s)
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathds{C}_{1,\infty}^{n}(s)

\displaystyle:=\{w\in\mathds{C}^{n}:\;\|w\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1,\,\|w\|_{1}\leqslant s\},

(73)

\displaystyle\mathds{R}_{1,\infty}^{n}(s)

\displaystyle:=\{u\in\mathds{R}^{n}:\;\|u\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1,\,\|u\|_{1}\leqslant s\},

\displaystyle\mathds{R}_{+;1,\infty}^{n}(s)

\displaystyle:=\{u\in\mathds{R}_{+}^{n}:\;\|u\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1,\,\|u\|_{1}\leqslant s\}.

We first recall the (known) explicit characterizations of the vertices of

and
\mathds{R}_{1,\infty}^{n}(s)
.

Proposition A.2

(
[
22
, Thm. 2.1 and Eq. (2.1)]
)
.

The polytope
\mathds{R}_{+;1,\infty}^{n}(s)
(resp.
\mathds{R}_{1,\infty}^{n}(s)
) has
{n\choose s}
(resp.
2^{s}{n\choose s}
) vertices, namely all
s
-sparse vectors with entries in
\{0,1\}
(resp.
\{-1,0,1\}
).

As an immediate corollary, we restrict the possible structure of extremal points for
\mathds{C}_{1,\infty}^{n}(s)
.

Proposition A.3
.

Let
e^{0},...,e^{n-1}
be the canonical basis of
\mathds{R}^{n}
.
Any extremal point of
\mathds{C}_{1,\infty}^{n}(s)
is of the form
\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}z_{j}u_{j}e^{j}
for some
z_{0},...,z_{n-1}\in{\mathds{T}}
and
s
-sparse binary vector
u=(u_{0},...,u_{n-1})
.

Proof.

Let

of

\square

Appendix B
Technical results on trigonometric interpolation

B.1
Dirichlet and Fejér kernels

The Dirichlet and Fejér kernels, respectively, are the following Laurent polynomials
[
33
]
:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{Dir}}_{m}(z):=\sum_{|k|\leqslant m}z^{k},\quad{\mathsf{Fej}}_{m}(z)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{|k|\leqslant m}\left(1-\frac{|k|}{m+1}\right)z^{k}.

(74)

That is,
{\mathsf{Dir}}_{m}(z)
is the
z
-transform of the rectangular window signal
u_{t}=\mathds{1}\{|t|\leqslant m\}
;
{\mathsf{Fej}}_{m}(z)
that of the triangular window signal
v_{t}(1-\frac{|t|}{m+1})\mathds{1}\{|t|\leqslant m\}
,
and
{\mathsf{Fej}}_{2m}(z)=\frac{1}{{2}m+1}{\mathsf{Dir}}_{m}^{2}(z)
.
Note that
\frac{1}{2m+1}{\mathsf{Dir}}_{m}(1)=\frac{1}{m+1}{\mathsf{Fej}}_{m}(1)=1
.
These kernels can be recast as trigonometric polynomials:

{\mathsf{dir}}_{m}(\omega):={\mathsf{Dir}}_{m}(e^{i\omega})=\frac{\sin((2m+1)\frac{\omega}{2})}{\sin(\frac{\omega}{2})},\quad{\mathsf{fej}}_{m}(\omega):={\mathsf{Fej}}_{m}(e^{i\omega})=\frac{1}{m+1}\left(\frac{1-\cos((m+1)\omega)}{1-\cos(\omega)}\right).

Finally, we define the causal counterpart of the Fejér kernel by appropriately shifting
{\mathsf{Fej}}_{m}(z)
:

{\mathsf{Fej}}_{2m}^{+}(z):=z^{m}{\mathsf{Fej}}_{m}(z).

(75)

Note that
{\mathsf{Fej}}_{2m}^{+}\in\mathds{C}_{2m}^{+}(\mathds{Z})
. Moreover,
{\mathsf{Fej}}_{2m}^{+}(1)=m+1
, and
|{\mathsf{Fej}}_{2m}^{+}(z)|=|{\mathsf{Fej}}_{m}(z)|
for all
z\in{\mathds{T}}
.

Summation over equidistant grids.

We bound the sums of
|{\mathsf{Dir}}_{n}^{\vphantom{+}}|
,
|{\mathsf{Fej}}_{n}^{\vphantom{+}}|
,
|{\mathsf{Fej}}_{2n}^{+}|
over
{\mathds{T}}_{N}
.

Lemma B.1
.

For any
n,N\in{\mathds{N}}
and
a\in{\mathds{T}}
, letting
H_{N}
be the
N^{\textup{th}}
harmonic number, one has

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2N+1}\sum_{w\in{\mathds{T}}_{N}}\left|{\mathsf{Dir}}_{n}\Big(\frac{w}{a}\Big)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{4n+2}{2N+1}+H_{N},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2N+1}\sum_{w\in{\mathds{T}}_{N}}\left|{\mathsf{Fej}}_{2n}^{+}\Big(\frac{w}{a}\Big)\right|=\frac{1}{2N+1}\sum_{w\in{\mathds{T}}_{N}}\left|{\mathsf{Fej}}_{n}\Big(\frac{w}{a}\Big)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2n+2}{2N+1}+\left(\frac{2N+1}{2n+2}\right)\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}.

Proof.

Since
|{\mathsf{Fej}}_{2n}(z)|=|{\mathsf{Fej}}_{n}^{+}(z)|
for
z\in{\mathds{T}}
, the summation of
|{\mathsf{Fej}}_{2n}^{+}|
reduces to that of
|{\mathsf{Fej}}_{n}|
.
Now, for any
a\in\mathds{C}
, at most 2 points of the grid
\{\frac{w}{a}:w\in{\mathds{T}}_{N}\}
have argument in
(-\frac{2\pi}{2N+1},\frac{2\pi}{2N+1})
.
Each of them contributes at most
{\mathsf{Dir}}_{n}(1)=2n+1
or
{\mathsf{Fej}}_{n}(1)=n+1
to the respective sum.
As for the remaining points, we use the following elementary estimates valid for all
\omega\in[-\pi,\pi]
,

|{\mathsf{dir}}_{n}(\omega)|\leqslant\frac{1}{|\sin(\frac{\omega}{2})|}\leqslant\frac{\pi}{|\omega|}\quad\text{and}\quad 0\leqslant(n+1){\mathsf{fej}}_{n}(\omega)\leqslant\frac{1}{\sin^{2}(\frac{\omega}{2})}\leqslant\frac{\pi^{2}}{\omega^{2}},

whose right-hand sides decrease in
|\omega|
, and summate separately over at most
N
points with argument in
[\frac{2\pi}{2N+1},\pi]
and at most
N
points with argument in
[-\pi,-\frac{2\pi}{2N+1}]
.
Overall, this gives

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in{\mathds{T}}_{N}}\left|{\mathsf{Dir}}_{n}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\frac{w}{a}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\left(2n+1+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\pi}{\arg(\chi_{k,N})}\hskip-2.84544pt\right)={4n+2}+(2N+1)\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{k},

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in{\mathds{T}}_{N}}\left|{\mathsf{Fej}}_{n}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\frac{w}{a}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\left(n+1+\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\pi^{2}}{\arg(\chi_{k,N})^{2}}\hskip-2.84544pt\right)={2n+2}+\frac{(2N+1)^{2}}{2n+2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{k^{2}}.

Here we used that
\arg(\chi_{k,N})=\frac{2\pi k}{2N+1}
for
k\in\{1,...,N\}
; cf. (
18
).

\square

Oversampling bounds.

As it was observed in
[
16
]
, the Dirichlet kernel summation estimate of Lemma
B.1
directly implies a uniform estimate for the inflation of
\ell_{1}
-norm with oversampling, that is the uniform over
\varphi\in\mathds{C}_{n}(\mathds{Z})
upper bound for the ratio
\|{\mathcal{F}}_{N}[\varphi]\|_{1}/\|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}[\varphi]\|_{1}
with
N\geqslant n
.

Lemma B.2

(Oversampling in
\ell_{1}
)
.

For any
n,N\in{\mathds{N}}
such that
N\geqslant n
, and nonzero
\varphi\in\mathds{C}_{n}(\mathds{Z})
,

\displaystyle\frac{\|{\mathcal{F}}_{N}[\varphi]\|_{1}}{\|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}[\varphi]\|_{1}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2N+1)(2n+1)}}\max_{a\in{\mathds{T}}_{n}}\sum_{w\in{\mathds{T}}_{N}}\left|{\mathsf{Dir}}_{n}\left(\frac{w}{a}\right)\right|\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{2N+1}{2n+1}}\log(eN)+2\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{2N+1}}.

Proof.

We first note that the convex function
\|{\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{\vphantom{\,-1}}[{\mathcal{F}}_{n}^{\,-1}[\cdot]]\|_{1}
is maximized at an extreme point of the unit
\ell_{1}
-ball in
\mathds{C}^{2n+1}
, i.e. on a vector of the form

ze^{j},

where
z\in{\mathds{T}}
and
e^{j}
,
0\leqslant j\leqslant 2n
, is the
j^{\textup{th}}
canonical basis vector of
\mathds{R}^{2n+1}
.
As the result,

\displaystyle\max_{\varphi\in\mathds{C}_{n}(\mathds{Z})\setminus 0}\frac{\|{\mathcal{F}}_{N}[\varphi]\|_{1}}{\|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}[\varphi]\|_{1}}

\displaystyle=\max_{0\leqslant j\leqslant 2n}\|{\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{\vphantom{\,-1}}[{\mathcal{F}}_{n}^{\,-1}[e^{j}]]\|_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2N+1)(2n+1)}}\max_{0\leqslant j\leqslant 2n}\sum_{k=0}^{2N}\left|{\mathsf{Dir}}_{n}\left(\frac{\chi_{k,N}}{\chi_{j,n}}\right)\right|.

After that, it only remains to invoke Lemma
B.1
.

\square

Remark B.1
.

For
N\geqslant(1+c)n
, the inequality of Lemma
B.2
is sharp up to a constant factor.

Below we give a counterpart result for
\ell_{\infty}
-norm.
In the context of trigonometric interpolation, the corresponding ratio is known as the Lebesgue constant, and a sharp estimate is known for it (e.g.
[
34
]
,
[
35
, Thm. 2.1]
).
We give a standalone estimate to keep the paper self-contained.

Lemma B.3

(Oversampling in
\ell_{\infty}
)
.

For any

with

, and nonzero

\displaystyle\frac{\|{\mathcal{F}}_{N}[\varphi]\|_{\infty}}{\|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}[\varphi]\|_{\infty}}\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2N+1)(2n+1)}}\max_{a\in{\mathds{T}}_{N}}\sum_{w\in{\mathds{T}}_{n}}\left|{\mathsf{Dir}}_{n}\left(\frac{w}{a}\right)\right|\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{2N+1}}(H_{n}+2).

As a consequence, for any

it holds that
\max_{z\in{\mathds{T}}}|\varphi(z)|\leqslant(H_{n}+2)\,\max_{z\in{\mathds{T}}_{n}}|\varphi(z)|
.

Proof.

The convex function

-ball in

\displaystyle\max_{\varphi\in\mathds{C}_{n}(\mathds{Z})\setminus 0}\frac{\|{\mathcal{F}}_{N}[\varphi]\|_{\infty}}{\|{\mathcal{F}}_{n}[\varphi]\|_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\max_{z_{0},...,z_{2n}\in{\mathds{T}}}\big\|{\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{\vphantom{\,-1}}\big[{\mathcal{F}}_{n}^{\,-1}\big[z_{0}e^{0}+z_{1}e^{1}+...+z_{2n}e^{2n}\big]\big]\big\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2N+1)(2n+1)}}\max_{z_{0},...,z_{2n}\in{\mathds{T}}}\max_{0\leqslant k\leqslant 2N}\left|\sum_{j=0}^{2n}z_{j}{\mathsf{Dir}}_{n}\left(\frac{\chi_{j,n}}{\chi_{k,N}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2N+1)(2n+1)}}\max_{0\leqslant k\leqslant 2N}\sum_{j=0}^{2n}\left|{\mathsf{Dir}}_{n}\left(\frac{\chi_{j,n}}{\chi_{k,N}}\right)\right|.

As previously, we conclude by invoking Lemma
B.1
.

\square

if we only allow for

Lemma B.4

(Oversampling in

.

For

\displaystyle\max_{w\in\mathds{C}_{1,\infty}^{2n+1}(s)}\|{\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{\vphantom{\,-1}}[{\mathcal{F}}_{n}^{\,-1}[w]]\|_{\infty}\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{2N+1}}(\log(\lceil s/2\rceil)+3).

Proof.

We can assume that

Appendix C
Technical results concerning shift-invariant subspaces of
\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})

C.1
Characterization of finite-dimensional shift-invariant subspaces of
\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})

As shown in
[
16
]
,
s
-dimensional shift-invariant subspaces of
\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})
can be characterized in terms of the corresponding characteristic polynomial.
4
4

4

Characterization of the invariant subspaces for the restriction of
\Delta
to
\ell^{2}
is a classical topic
[
36
,
37
,
38
]
. Yet, these classical results seem to be limited to Hilbert spaces, and therefore not allowing to recover Proposition
C.1
.

To state the corresponding result, we start with a brief reminder.
The
lag
operator
\Delta:\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})\to\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})
is defined by
(\Delta x)_{t}=x_{t-1}
.
A linear subspace
X
of
\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})
is called
shift-invariant
if it is an invariant subspace of
\Delta
, that is
\Delta X\subseteq X
.
An easy exercise is to verify that
\Delta
is bijective on any shift-invariant subspace, thus
\Delta X=X
.

Proposition C.1

(
[
16
, Proposition 5]
)
.

Any shift-invariant subspace of
\mathds{C}(\mathds{Z})
of dimension
s
is the set of solutions for some homogeneous linear difference equation
\mathsf{f}(\Delta)x\equiv 0
with a characteristic polynomial
\mathsf{f}(z)=\mathsf{f}_{0}+\mathsf{f}_{1}z+...+\mathsf{f}_{s}z^{s}
of degree
s
, unique if normalized by
\mathsf{f}(0)=1
.

Recall that for
w_{1},...,w_{s}\in\mathds{C}
, we let
X(w_{1},...,w_{s})
be the solution set of the equation (
1
) whose characteristic polynomial is

\mathsf{f}(z)=\prod_{k\in[s]}\left(1-{w_{k}}{z}\right),

i.e. has
w_{1}^{-1},...,w_{s}^{-1}
as its roots. By Proposition
C.1
, any SIS of dimension
s
writes as
X(w_{1},...,w_{s})
for a unique, up to a permutation, selection of
(w_{1},...,w_{s})\in\mathds{C}^{s}
.
In the case of distinct roots,
X(w_{1},...,w_{s})
is the span of
s
complex exponentials
w_{1}^{t},...,w_{s}^{t}
, i.e. comprises signals of the form

x_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{s}c_{k}^{\vphantom{t}}w_{k}^{t}
with
c_{k}\in\mathds{C}.

Generally, we get exponential polynomials: if the root
w_{k}^{-1}
of
\mathsf{f}
has multiplicity
m_{k}
, then
X(w_{1},...,w_{s})
comprises signals of the form
x_{t}=\sum_{k\in[s]}q_{k}(t)^{\vphantom{t}}w_{k}^{t}
, where
\deg(q_{k})=m_{k}-1
and
\sum_{k\in[s]}m_{k}=s
.

C.2
Minimal-norm one-sided reproducing filter for the polynomial subspace

Proposition C.2
.

Let
X
be the space of complex polynomials of degree at most
s-1
.
Any sequence
\{\phi_{+}^{*,m}\in\mathds{C}_{m}^{+}(\mathds{Z})\}_{m\in{\mathds{N}}}
of one-sided reproducing for

-norm satisfies

\lim_{m\to\infty}m\|\phi_{+}^{*,m}\|_{2}^{2}=s^{2}.

(77)

Proof.

Fixing

, we first focus on
\psi\in\mathds{C}_{m}^{+}(\mathds{Z})
such that
\psi(0)=0
, denoted as
\psi\in\mathds{C}_{m}^{++}(\mathds{Z})
,
and associate any such
\psi
with a vector
(\psi_{1},...,\psi_{m})
. We return to the general case in the end.

\bm{{1}^{o}}
.
Observe that
X
is reproduced by
\psi\in\mathds{C}_{m}^{++}(\mathds{Z})
if and only if
\psi
satisfies the equations

\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1&\dots&1\\
0&\frac{1}{m}&\frac{2}{m}&\dots&1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)^{s-1}&\left(\frac{2}{m}\right)^{s-1}&\dots&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-1\\
\psi\end{bmatrix}=0.

(78)

Indeed, each row in (
78
) is the slice
(x_{0},x_{1},...,x_{m})

, so any reproducing

\bm{{2}^{o}}

Letting

\begin{bmatrix}1&1&\dots&1\\

\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)^{s-1}&\left(\frac{2}{m}\right)^{s-1}&\dots&1\end{bmatrix}\psi=e_{0},

that is

The minimal-norm solution

.
But for the entries of
