Pinned Distances and Density Theorems in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d}
Chenjian Wang

Abstract
. We study a pinned variant of Bourgain’s theorem [ 1 ] , concerning the occurrence of affine copies of k k -point patterns in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} . Focusing on the case k = 2 k=2 , which corresponds to pinned distances, we show that the classical conclusion does not extend to the pinned setting: there exist sets of positive upper density in ℝ d , d ≥ 2 \mathbb{R}^{d},~d\geq 2 such that no single pinned point determines all sufficiently large distances. However, we establish a weaker quantitative result: for every point x x in such a set, the pinned distance set at x x has (one-dimensional) positive upper density. We also construct an example demonstrating the sharpness of this bound. These findings highlight a structural distinction between global and pinned configurations.

Introduction

Let us define the
upper density
of a Lebesgue measurable set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

\delta(A):=\limsup_{R\to\infty}\frac{|B(0,R)\bigcap A|}{R^{d}}

where

\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For computational reason, we replace by

the
|B(0,R)|
in the denominator in the standard definition, the difference is not essential.

In 1986, Bourgain proved a Szemerédi-type theorem for simplices. We state its planar case.

Theorem A

(Bourgain
[
1
]
)
.

Suppose set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
has positive upper density.
Then there exists a number
l>0
such that
(l,\infty)\subseteq D(A)
, where
D(A)
is the distance set of
A
, defined as

D(A):=\{|x-y|:x,y\in A\}.

Inspired by the work on pinned distance set (see equation (
1
) for its definition) problems
[
4
,
3
]
, we study a pinned analog of Bourgain’s theorem. Specifically, we consider whether a single point

x
in
A
can serve as the base for observing all large distances. More precisely,

Problem 1
.

Suppose
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d},d\geq 2
has positive upper density. Is there any
x\in A
and a number
l(x)>0
such that for all
l^{\prime}>l(x)
, there exists
y\in A\text{ such that }|x-y|=l^{\prime}
?

This is a natural way to propose the pinned version of Bourgain’s theorem. In the study of the Falconer’s distance set problem in geometric measure theory, people asked the following question.

Problem 2

(Falconer’s distance set problem, pinned version)
.

Assume
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d},d\geq 2
is a Borel set and
\dim_{H}(A)>\frac{d}{2}
. Then is there any
x\in A
such that the pinned distance set

(1)

D_{x}(A):=\{|x-y|:y\in A\}

has positive Lebesgue measure? Here
\dim_{H}(A)
is the Hausdorff dimension of
A
.

Comparing these two problems, in both cases, we have two “original” sets with certain “largeness” conditions: In Problem
1
, the upper density is positive, whereas in Problem
2
, it is
\dim_{H}(A)>\frac{d}{2}
. The conclusions are both about the “largeness” of certain “induced” sets of the original sets, which is pinned distance set in both cases. However, the manners in which we quantify largeness are different. In Problem
1
, we use
(l(x),\infty)\subseteq D_{x}(A)
, whereas in Problem
2
, we use
|D_{x}(A)|>0
.

Returning to Problem
1
, our first result provides a negative answer to it.

Proposition 1
.

For
d\geq 2
, there exists a set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\delta(E)>0
such that for all
x\in E
and all
l>0
, there exists
l^{\prime}>l
such that

|x-y|\neq l^{\prime},~\forall y\in E.

The proof is a construction of such set
E
showing that the pinned analog of Bourgain’s result fails in the strongest form. It will be presented in Example
1
.

Proposition
1
tells us that it is inappropriate to quantify the sizes of the pinned distance sets by “containing
(l(x),\infty)
”. This conclusion turns out to be too strong to be true. Example
1
motivates the search for weaker guarantees on the size of the pinned distance set.
Therefore, our second result answers this quantitatively.

Theorem 1
.

Assume
d\geq 2
and
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\delta(A)>0
. Then for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\delta(D_{x}(A))=\limsup_{R\to\infty}\frac{|D_{x}(A)\bigcap(-R,R)|}{R}\geq\frac{\delta(A)}{2|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}|},

where
D_{x}(A)
is defined in (
1
) and
|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}|
is the area of the
(d-1)
-dimensional unit sphere.

The theorem affirms that although the pinned distance set
D_{x}(A)
does not contain certain interval
(l(x),\infty)
; its upper density, as a subset of
\mathbb{R}
, must be positive.

The proof of Theorem
1
is elementary. However, the result is sharp in the
following sense.

Proposition 2

(sharpness of the Theorem
1
)
.

For
d\geq 2
, there are a constant
C_{d}
and a set
E
with
\delta(E)>0
, such that for all
x\in E,\delta(D_{x}(E))\leq C_{d}\delta(E)
.

This example shows that in Theorem
1
, we cannot expect a better exponent of
\delta(A)
such as
\delta(A)^{t}
for
t<1
. We make this more precise in Example
2
.

In the study of Falconer’s distance set problem, as they are applied to the general distance set, Fourier-analytic techniques work equally well and often imply that large sets yield large pinned distance sets (see
[
4
,
3
]
). Therefore, one may assume that there is a certain “equivalency” between the general distance set and the pinned distance set in such type of problems. However, our construction shows that this implication can fail in the context of Bourgain-type density results. This highlights a subtle geometric divergence between pinned and unpinned frameworks as well as that between Hausdorff dimension and upper density.

The methods used to address these two types of problems are quite different. For example, in the planar Falconer’s problem, the multiscale analysis tools such as induction-on-scales, wave packet decomposition, and decoupling are heavily used
[
3
]
. While in the upper density setting, multilinear analysis tools such as multilinear singular integrals and Gowers’ norm are applied, see
[
1
,
2
,
5
]
. It is interesting to interpret these differences and make the heuristics more precise.

The method we used in the case of two point pattern (distance) can be generalized to study some more complicated patterns in all dimensions by combining other ingredients from combinatorics.

\delta\Big(\bigsqcup_{i\geq M}\tilde{I_{i}}\Big)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|\bigsqcup_{i\geq M}\tilde{I_{i}}\bigcap B(0,\bar{R}_{n})|}{\bar{R}_{n}}.

For each sufficiently large
\bar{R}_{n}\geq R_{2M}
, by pigeonholing, there is
i\geq M
such that
(1+\frac{3\varepsilon_{0}}{2})R_{i}<\bar{R}_{n}\leq(1+\frac{3\varepsilon_{0}}{2})R_{i+1}
. There are two cases for the exact location of
\bar{R}_{n}
:

(1)

\bar{R}_{n}\in[(1-\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{2})R_{i+1},(1+\frac{3\varepsilon_{0}}{2})R_{i+1}]=\tilde{I}_{i+1}
,

(2)

\bar{R}_{n}\not\in\tilde{I}_{i+1}
.

If we are in the first case, since the longest interval dominates, this implies

\frac{|\bigsqcup_{i\geq M}\tilde{I_{i}}\bigcap B(0,\bar{R}_{n})|}{\bar{R}_{n}}\leq C_{d}\frac{|\tilde{I}_{i+1}|}{(1-\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{2})R_{i+1}}\leq C_{d}\frac{2\varepsilon_{0}}{(1-\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{2})}.

If we are in the second case, which means that

(1+\frac{3\varepsilon_{0}}{2})R_{i}<\bar{R}_{n}<(1-\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{2})R_{i+1},

then

\frac{|\bigsqcup_{i\geq M}\tilde{I_{i}}\bigcap B(0,\bar{R}_{n})|}{\bar{R}_{n}}\leq C_{d}\frac{|\tilde{I}_{i}|}{(1+\frac{3\varepsilon_{0}}{2})R_{i}}\leq C_{d}\frac{2\varepsilon_{0}}{(1+\frac{3\varepsilon_{0}}{2})}.

In both cases, the second inequality of equation (
12
) holds if
C_{d}
is appropriately chosen.

Remark 2
.

Intuitively, to make the upper density of the pinned distance set as small as possible, each distance
r
should be very “efficient”, i.e.
r\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
should contain points from
E
as many as possible. The extreme case is the entire
r\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\subseteq E
which is the example. This can also be seen from the proof of Proposition
1
: the last inequality of equation (
10
) is an equality when
\chi_{A}(ry^{\prime})\equiv 1
for
y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
which means
r\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\subseteq A
.
