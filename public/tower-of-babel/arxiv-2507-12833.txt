Analysis of a parabolic-hyperbolic hybrid population model: an integrated semigroup approach
∗

Abstract




Abstract:

This paper is concerned with the global dynamics of a hybrid parabolic-hyperbolic model describing populations with distinct dispersal and sedentary stages. We first establish the global well-posedness of solutions, prove a comparison principle, and demonstrate the asymptotic smoothness of the solution semiflow. Through the spectral analysis of the linearized system, we derive and characterize the net reproductive rate
\mathcal{R}_{0}
. Furthermore, an explicit relationship between
\mathcal{R}_{0}
and the principal eigenvalue of the linearized system is analyzed. Under appropriate monotonicity assumptions, we show that
\mathcal{R}_{0}
serves as a threshold parameter that completely determines the stability of steady states of the system. More precisely, when
\mathcal{R}_{0}<1
, the trivial equilibrium is globally asymptotical stable, while when
\mathcal{R}_{0}>1
, the system is uniformly persistent and there is a positive equilibrium which is unique and globally asymptotical stable.

Keywords:

Hybrid model, integrated semigroup, comparison principle, net reproductive rate, global stability.

92D25, 35K57, 35L50, 35B40

Introduction

Understanding how the interaction between life history traits, vital rates, and dispersal patterns influences population persistence is an essential research question in spatial ecology. Mathematical models, particularly reaction-diffusion equations
[
5
,
32
,
33
,
35
,
39
]
and first-order hyperbolic equations
[
18
,
19
,
25
,
28
,
31
,
47
]
, are essential tools for investigating these dynamics. It is important to note that classical reaction-diffusion models typically assume that growth and dispersal processes occur simultaneously in both time and space. In contrast, classical first-order hyperbolic equations used to model age- or size-structured populations generally assume a homogeneously mixed population, which neglect the spatial dispersal of individuals.
The above assumptions may not accurately reflect the life cycle of many organisms. Actually, species such as plants and certain aquatic organisms, like zebra mussels and corals, have distinct dispersal and stationary stages in their life cycles. For example, plants spread to new habitats through seed dispersal, and once the seeds land, they grow into trees or grasses. The life cycle of zebra mussels includes a dispersive larval stage and sedentary juvenile and adult stages. During breeding seasons, adult mussels release planktonic larvae that are transported by water currents until they settle on the bottom of water bodies (dispersal stage), where they attach to fixed substrates and mature into juveniles and adults (sedentary stage).

To address the limitations mentioned above, researchers have developed hybrid systems to investigate the spatiotemporal dynamics of such species. These hybrid systems typically combine reaction-diffusion (or reaction-diffusion-advection) equations, which model the dispersal phase, with ordinary differential equations
[
27
,
36
,
38
]
(or difference equations
[
17
,
24
]
), which govern the growth phase. Some studies integrate spatial movement and age growth within a single equation of the form
u_{t}+u_{a}-\mathcal{A}u
, where
\mathcal{A}
represents either a diffusion operator
[
12
,
13
,
30
,
45
,
46
]
or an integral operator
[
9
,
10
,
20
]
.

In this work, we study the following parabolic-hyperbolic hybrid system, which models the spatiotemporal dynamics of a population with distinct dispersal and stationary stages:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\partial_{t}u=d\Delta u+B(x,t)-(m(x)+e(x))u-c(x)u^{2},&x\in\Omega,t>0,\\
\partial_{t}w+\partial_{a}w=-\mu(x,a,P(t))w,&x\in\Omega,t>0,a\in(0,a_{\max}),\\
\partial_{\nu}u=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,t>0,\\
w(x,0,t)=\chi(x,P(t))e(x)u(x,t),&x\in\Omega,t>0,\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),&x\in\Omega,\\
w(x,a,0)=w_{0}(x,a),&x\in\Omega,a\in(0,a_{\max}).\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

In this system,
u(x,t)
represents the spatial density of dispersing individuals at location
x\in\Omega
and time
t
. The domain
\Omega
denotes the population habitat, which is a bounded region in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
. The function
w(x,a,t)
represents the density of sedentary individuals with age
a\in[0,a_{\max})
at location
x
and time
t
. The first equation of system (
1.1
) describes the movement, recruitment, mortality, and settlement of dispersing individuals. The term
d\Delta u
models the random movement, where
d
is the diffusion coefficient and
\Delta
denotes the Laplacian operator. The function

B(x,t):=\int_{0}^{a_{\max}}\beta(x,a,P(t))w(x,a,t)da

(1.2)

represents the total contributions of all stationary individuals to recruitment, where
\beta(x,a,P)
is the spatially varying reproduction rate of stationary individuals of age
a
. This rate depends on the total number of stationary individuals
P(t)
, which reflects competition for resources. Specifically,
P(t)
is given by

P(t):=\int_{0}^{a_{\max}}\int_{\Omega}w(x,a,t)dxda,\quad\forall t\geq 0.

The parameter
m(x)
is the natural mortality rate. The term
c(x)u^{2}
denotes mortality due to intra-specific competition, where
c(x)
is the competition coefficient. The parameter
e(x)
is the settlement rate of dispersing individuals. The second equation of system (
1.1
) describes the growth and mortality of stationary individuals, structured by age, where
\mu(x,a,P(t))
is the mortality rate of sedentary individuals. The third equation imposes a homogeneous Neumann boundary condition, indicating that no individuals can enter or exit the habitat through the boundary, where
\nu
is the outward unit normal vector to
\partial\Omega
.
The fourth equation describes the transition of dispersing individuals to stationary individuals once they settle. Here,
\chi(x,P(t))\in(0,1]
represents the proportion of dispersing individuals that successfully transition to the sedentary stage. Finally,
u_{0}(x)
and
w_{0}(x,a)
represent the initial spatial distributions of dispersing individuals and stationary individuals, respectively.

The parabolic-hyperbolic population model (
1.1
) extends the models developed by Deng and Huang
[
7
,
8
]
. In these earlier works, the authors assumed that the transition rate

\chi(x,P(t))\equiv 1
. Additionally, in
[
7
]
, they did not consider the dependence of the reproduction rate
\beta
and the mortality rate
\mu
on the total population abundance
P
. In their works
[
7
,
8
]
, Deng and Huang defined weak solutions (in integral form) for these models by introducing smooth test functions. They then established the existence and uniqueness of these weak solutions using the monotone method based on a comparison principle. Furthermore, they investigated the criteria for population persistence through four interrelated measures. Their study also included numerical simulations to explore the influence of population dispersal, reproduction, settlement, and habitat boundaries on population persistence.

The objective of this work is to analyze the global dynamics of the hybrid system (
1.1
), focusing on the existence, uniqueness, boundedness, and smoothness of solutions, the analysis of the net reproductive rate
R_{0}
, as well as the stability of equilibria. We highlight two major mathematical challenges in analyzing system (
1.1
).
First, proving the compactness or asymptotic smoothness of the model’s solutions is not straightforward, as the first-order hyperbolic equation in the model is spatially dependent.
Second, the dependence of

\beta
,
\mu
, and
\chi
on the total number of sedentary individuals,
P(t)
, results in a strong coupling between the reaction-diffusion and hyperbolic equations. Therefore, this system cannot be transformed into a delay differential equation by solving the hyperbolic equation using the characteristic method. These challenges pose significant obstacles in analyzing the dynamic behavior of the system (
1.1
).

To analyze model (
1.1
), we will adopt the integrated semigroup approach. Since
\chi
depends on
P
, it is not possible to reformulate (
1.1
) as a densely defined abstract Cauchy problem, which is a classical method for studying evolution equations
[
37
]
. Instead, (
1.1
) takes the form of a non-densely defined Cauchy problem, for which integrated semigroup theory provides a suitable analytical framework (see
[
2
,
3
,
6
,
21
,
28
,
34
,
41
,
50
]
and references therein). Alternatively, one could integrate the second equation in (
1.1
) along characteristic lines, reformulating the system into a coupled problem involving a reaction-diffusion equation and an integral equation. Nevertheless, we opt for the integrated semigroup method to leverage its mature theoretical foundation, such as well-established results on the existence, uniqueness, and positivity of solutions.

Importantly, the solution obtained via the integrated semigroup method also satisfies the system derived from integration along characteristic lines (see Proposition
2.3
), a fact we exploit to establish the asymptotic smoothness of solutions in Section 4. It seems that the general theory of eventual compactness for integrated semigroups developed by Magal and Thieme
[
30
]
does not directly apply to our model. Our approach is inspired by ideas from Webb
[
47
]
, particularly the decomposition of solutions as in Lemma
4.1
.

One goal of this work is to demonstrate the effectiveness of monotone methods in analyzing the asymptotic behavior of solutions to first-order parabolic equations. Monotone techniques, based on comparison principles, have long played an essential role in the study of the dynamics of ordinary differential equations and reaction-diffusion systems (see, e.g.,
[
40
]
). More recently, Magal et al.
[
29
]
extended the theory of monotone semiflows and established a comparison principle for abstract semi-linear Cauchy problems with non-dense domains. This principle has been adopted by Ducrot et al.
[
10
]
to construct upper/lower solutions and study the global stability of positive equilibrium.
In Section 2.4, we apply the abstract results of Magal et al.
[
29
]
directly to our model. Our contribution in this direction is to show that the monotone solutions, as constructed in Theorem
2.11
, converge to an equilibrium under the condition that the
\omega
-limit set is compact (see Theorem
4.6
). Additional applications of the comparison principle and Theorem
4.6
are presented in Section 6, where we analyze the global asymptotic behavior of the solutions in terms of the net reproductive rate
\mathcal{R}_{0}
.

The paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we prove the existence and uniqueness of global solutions using the integral semiflow approach. We also establish the positivity of solutions and the comparison principle. In section 3, we investigate the boundedness and global existence properties of the solution. Section 4 provides a proof of the asymptotic smoothness of the solution semiflow. In section 5, through spectral analysis of the generator for the strongly continuous semigroup associated with the linearized system at the zero equilibrium, we introduce a biologically meaningful net reproductive rate

\mathcal{R}_{0}
and characterize its precise relationship with the principal eigenvalue of the linearized system. In section 6, we demonstrate the global stability of steady states, which is determined by the net reproductive rate
\mathcal{R}_{0}
: when
\mathcal{R}_{0}
is below unity, the trivial equilibrium is globally asymptotically stable; when
\mathcal{R}_{0}
exceeds unity, we prove the existence and global stability of a positive steady state. Finally, in section 7, we summarize our findings and propose directions for future research.

Local existence of solutions

In order to apply integral semigroup theory, we need to rewrite system (

Integration along characteristic lines

t\in[0,\infty)
.

It remains to prove (
3.2
). Changing integration variables in (
3.3
) and differentiating with respect to
t
, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{P}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\int_{0}^{t}\chi(x,P(t-a))e(x)u(x,t-a)e^{-\int_{0}^{a}\mu(x,a-s,P(t-s))ds}da

\displaystyle+\partial_{t}\int_{t}^{\infty}w_{0}(x,a-t)e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\mu(x,a-s,P(t-s))ds}da

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\int_{0}^{t}\chi(x,P(a))e(x)u(x,a)e^{-\int_{a}^{t}\mu(x,s-a,P(s))ds}da

\displaystyle+\partial_{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}w_{0}(x,a)e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\mu(x,a+s,P(s))ds}da

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi(x,P(t))e(x)u(x,t)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\mu(x,t-a,P(t))\chi(x,P(a))e(x)u(x,a)e^{-\int_{a}^{t}\mu(x,s-a,P(s))ds}da

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu(x,a+t,P(t))w_{0}(x,a)e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\mu(x,a+s,P(s))ds}da

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi(x,P(t))e(x)u(x,t)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\mu(x,t,P(t))\chi(x,P(t-a))e(x)u(x,t-a)e^{-\int_{0}^{a}\mu(x,a-s,P(t-s))ds}da

\displaystyle-\int_{t}^{\infty}\mu(x,t,P(t))w_{0}(x,a-t)e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\mu(x,a-s,P(t-s))ds}da

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi(x,P(t))e(x)u(x,t)-\int_{0}^{t}\mu(x,a,P(t))w(x,a,t)da-\int_{t}^{\infty}\mu(x,a,P(t))w(x,a,t)da.

Therefore,

\partial_{t}\mathbb{P}(x,t)=\chi(x,P(t))e(x)u(x,t)-\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu(x,a,P(t))w(x,a,t)da.

(3.8)

Note that (
3.8
) can be obtained formally from integrating the second equation of (
1.1
) with respect to variable
a
in
(0,\infty)
. It follows that

\partial_{t}\mathbb{P}(x,t)\leq\bar{\chi}\bar{e}u(x,t)-\underline{\mu}\mathbb{P}(x,t),\quad\forall x\in\bar{\Omega},t\geq 0.

Denote
u_{\infty}=\limsup_{t\to\infty}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{\infty}<\infty
. Let
\epsilon>0
be given. Then there exists
t_{\epsilon}>0
such that
u(x,t)\leq u_{\infty}+\epsilon
for all
x\in\bar{\Omega}
and
t\geq t_{\epsilon}
. Hence,

\partial_{t}\mathbb{P}(x,t)\leq\bar{\chi}\bar{e}(u_{\infty}+\epsilon)-\underline{\mu}\mathbb{P}(x,t),\quad\forall x\in\bar{\Omega},t\geq t_{\epsilon}.

Therefore, we have
\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(x,t)\leq\bar{\chi}\bar{e}(u_{\infty}+\epsilon)/\underline{\mu}
uniformly for
x\in\bar{\Omega}
. Hence, there exists
t_{\epsilon}^{\prime}>0
such that
\mathbb{P}(x,t)\leq\bar{\chi}\bar{e}(u_{\infty}+\epsilon)/\underline{\mu}+\epsilon
for all
t\geq t_{\epsilon}^{\prime}
and
x\in\bar{\Omega}
. By (
2.8
), we have

\partial_{t}u-d\Delta u\leq\bar{\beta}\Big[\bar{\chi}\bar{e}\frac{(u_{\infty}+\epsilon)}{\underline{\mu}}+\epsilon\Big]-\underline{c}u^{2},\quad\forall x\in\bar{\Omega},t\geq t_{\epsilon}^{\prime}.

This means

u_{\infty}=\limsup_{t\to\infty}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\bar{\beta}}{\underline{c}}\Big[\bar{\chi}\bar{e}\frac{(u_{\infty}+\epsilon)}{\underline{\mu}}+\epsilon\Big]}.

Taking
\epsilon\to 0
, we obtain

u_{\infty}\leq\frac{\bar{\beta}\bar{\chi}\bar{e}}{\underline{c}\underline{\mu}}=:N_{1}.

By (
3.7
) and Fatou’s lemma, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\|w(\cdot,\cdot,t)\|_{Z}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\bar{\chi}\bar{e}\int_{0}^{t}\|u(\cdot,t-a)\|_{\infty}e^{-\underline{\mu}a}da+\limsup_{t\to\infty}\int_{t}^{\infty}\|w_{0}(\cdot,a-t)\|_{\infty}e^{-\underline{\mu}t}da

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bar{\chi}\bar{e}\int_{0}^{\infty}\limsup_{t\to\infty}\|u(\cdot,t-a)\|_{\infty}e^{-\underline{\mu}a}da+\limsup_{t\to\infty}e^{-\underline{\mu}t}\|w_{0}\|_{Y}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bar{\chi}\bar{e}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\bar{\beta}\bar{\chi}\bar{e}}{\underline{c}\underline{\mu}}e^{-\underline{\mu}a}da+0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\beta}\bar{\chi}^{2}\bar{e}^{2}}{\underline{c}\underline{\mu}^{2}}=:N_{2}.

Hence, (
3.2
) holds with
N:=\max\{N_{1},N_{2}\}
.

4
Asymptotic smoothness

In this section, we show that the semiflow
\{U(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
induced by the solutions of system (
1.1
) is asymptotically smooth (see
[
16
]
for the definition of asymptotic smoothness of a semiflow).

We will need the following sufficient condition for asymptotic smoothness.

Lemma 4.1
.

(
[
16
, Lemma 3.2.3]
)

Let
X
be a Banach space and
T(t):X\to X
,
t\geq 0
, be a strongly continuous semigroup. The semiflow
\{T(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is asymptotically smooth if there are two maps
T_{1},T_{2}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times X\rightarrow X
such that
T=T_{1}+T_{2}
and the following conditions hold:

(i)

There is a continuous function
k:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
k(t,\xi)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
and
\|T_{1}(t)\bm{\psi}\|_{X}\leq k(t,\xi)
for any
\xi>0
and
\bm{\psi}\in X
with
\|\bm{\psi}\|\leq\xi
;

(ii)

T_{2}
is completely continuous, i.e.,
T(t)B
has compact closure in
X
and
\{T(s)B:\ 0\leq s\leq t\}
is bounded for any bounded set
B\subset X
and
t>0
.

To verify condition (ii) in Lemma
4.1
, we use the following Kolmogorov
′
s compactness criterion in
Z=L^{1}((0,a_{\max}),Y)
.

Lemma 4.2
.

(
[
15
, Theorem A.1]
)

Let
\mathbb{K}\subset Z:=L^{1}((0,a_{\max}),Y)
. The set
\mathbb{K}
is relatively compact in
Z
if and only if:

(i)

\sup_{g\in\mathbb{K}}\|g\|_{Z}<\infty
;

(ii)

\lim_{N\rightarrow a_{\max}}\int_{N}^{a_{\max}}\|g(a)\|_{Y}da=0
uniformly for
g\in\mathbb{K}
;

(iii)

\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{a_{\max}}\|g(a+h)-g(a)\|_{Y}da=0
uniformly for
g\in\mathbb{K}
;

(iv)

For every
\epsilon>0
, there exists a compact set
R_{\epsilon}\subset Y
such that for every
g\in\mathbb{K}
there exists a set
Q_{g,\epsilon}
with Lebesgue measure
\mu(Q_{g,\epsilon})<\epsilon
and

g(a)\in R_{\epsilon}
for any
a\in[0,a_{\max})\backslash Q_{g,\epsilon}
.

We will also need the following result, which follows from
[
14
, Lemma I.5.2]
and
[
14
, Proposition I.5.3]
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\{T(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
be a strongly continuous semigroup on Banach space
B
. Then the map
L\times B_{1}\ni(t,x)\to T(t)x
is uniformly continuous for any compact sets
L\subseteq[0,\infty)
and
B_{1}\subseteq B
.

The main result in this section is the following theorem about the asymptotical smoothness of the semiflow.

Theorem 4.4
.

Suppose
(A1)
-
(A3)
and
(A5)
hold. Let
\{U(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
be the semiflow induced by the solutions of (
1.1
). Then
\{U(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is asymptotically smooth.

Proof.

In order to apply Lemma
4.1
, we decompose the semiflow
\{U(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
as
U(t)=U_{1}(t)+U_{2}(t)
with

U_{1}(t)\bm{x}_{0}=(0_{Y},(0_{Y},w_{1}(\cdot,\cdot,t)))\quad\text{and}\quad U_{2}(t)\bm{x}_{0}=(u(\cdot,t),(0_{Y},w_{2}(\cdot,\cdot,t))),

where
\bm{x}_{0}=(u_{0},(0_{Y},w_{0}))\in\mathbb{X}_{0+}
,
U(t)\bm{x}_{0}=(u(\cdot,t),(0_{Y},w(\cdot,\cdot,t)))
, and
w=w_{1}+w_{2}
. Here,
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are given by

w_{1}(x,a,t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}w_{0}(x,a-t)e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\mu(x,a-s,P(t-s))ds},&\text{if}\ a>t,\\
0_{Y},&\text{if}\ a<t,\end{array}\right.

and

w_{2}(x,a,t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}0_{Y},&\text{if}\ a>t,\\
\chi(x,P(t-a))e(x)u(x,t-a)e^{-\int_{0}^{a}\mu(x,a-s,P(t-s))ds},&\text{if}\ a<t.\end{array}\right.

If
a_{\max}=\infty
, we have that

\displaystyle\|U_{1}(t)\bm{x}_{0}\|_{\mathbb{X}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|w_{1}(\cdot,\cdot,t)\|_{Z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}\|w_{0}(\cdot,a-t)e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\mu(\cdot,a-s,P(t-s))ds}\|_{\infty}da

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}\|w_{0}(\cdot,a-t)\|_{\infty}e^{-\underline{\mu}t}da=e^{-\underline{\mu}t}\|w_{0}\|_{Z}.

So if
\|\bm{x}_{0}\|_{\mathbb{X}}\leq\xi
for some
\xi>0
, then
\|U_{1}(t)\bm{x}_{0}\|_{\mathbb{X}}\leq e^{-\underline{\mu}t}\xi=:k(t,\xi)
. Since
k(t,\xi)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
,
U_{1}(t)
satisfies condition (i) in Lemma
4.1
. If
a_{\max}<\infty
, then
w_{1}(\cdot,\cdot,t)=0_{Z}
for all
t>a_{\max}
and condition (i) in Lemma
4.1
holds trivially.

It remains to prove that
U_{2}(t)
satisfies condition (ii) in Lemma
4.1
. Let
B\subset\mathbb{X}_{0}
be a bounded set and
t>0
be fixed. By Theorem
3.1
,
\{U(s)B:\ 0\leq s\leq t\}
is bounded in
\mathbb{X}
. By the well-known parabolic estimates, embedding theorems and Theorem
3.1
,
\{u(\cdot,t):\ \bm{x}_{0}\in B\}
is precompact in
Y
. Therefore, it suffices to prove that
\{w_{2}(\cdot,\cdot,t):\ \bm{x}_{0}\in B\}
is precompact in
Z
.

We will apply Lemma
4.2
to prove that
\{w_{2}(\cdot,\cdot,t):\ \bm{x}_{0}\in B\}
is precompact in
Z
. We will only consider the case
a_{\max}=\infty
as the case
a_{\max}<\infty
is similar and simpler. By Theorem
3.1
, there is
M>0
such that

\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{\infty}\leq M\quad\text{and}\quad\|w(\cdot,\cdot,t)\|_{Z}\leq M,\quad\forall\bm{x}_{0}\in B,t\geq 0.

(4.1)

Condition (i) of Lemma
4.2
immediately follows from (
4.1
). If
N\geq t
, then

\int_{N}^{\infty}\|w_{2}(\cdot,a,t)\|_{\infty}da=\int_{N}^{\infty}0\,da=0.

Therefore, condition (ii) of Lemma
4.2
holds.

Next, we verify condition (iii) in Lemma
4.2
, i.e., for any
t>0
,

\lim_{h\to 0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\|w_{2}(\cdot,a+h,t)-w_{2}(\cdot,a,t)\|_{\infty}da=0\quad\text{uniformly for}\ \bm{x}_{0}\in B.

(4.2)

We assume
h>0
, and the case
h<0
can be considered similarly. Fix
t>0
. We note that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\|w_{2}(\cdot,a+h,t)-w_{2}(\cdot,a,t)\|_{\infty}da

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t-h}\|w_{2}(\cdot,a+h,t)-w_{2}(\cdot,a,t)\|_{\infty}da

\displaystyle+\int_{t-h}^{t}\|w_{2}(\cdot,a,t)\|_{\infty}da=:I_{1}+I_{2}.

To estimate
I_{1}
, by (
4.1
), we have

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t-h}\Big\|\chi(\cdot,P(t-a-h))eu(\cdot,t-a-h)e^{-\int_{0}^{a+h}\mu(\cdot,a+h-s,P(t-s))ds}

\displaystyle-\chi(\cdot,P(t-a))eu(\cdot,t-a)e^{-\int_{0}^{a}\mu(\cdot,a-s,P(t-s))ds}\Big\|_{\infty}da

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{0}^{t-h}\Big\|\chi(\cdot,P(t-a-h))-\chi(\cdot,P(t-a))\Big\|_{\infty}da+C\int_{0}^{t-h}\|u(\cdot,t-a-h)-u(\cdot,t-a)\|_{\infty}da

\displaystyle+C\int_{0}^{t-h}\Big\|e^{-\int_{0}^{a+h}\mu(\cdot,a+h-s,P(t-s))ds}-e^{-\int_{0}^{a}\mu(\cdot,a-s,P(t-s))ds}\Big\|_{\infty}da

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C(J_{1}+J_{2}+J_{3}).

Here and after,
C
is a constant depending on
M
.

To estimate
J_{1}
, integrating (
3.8
) over
\Omega
, we obtain that

|\partial_{t}P(t)|\leq(\bar{\chi}\bar{e}+\bar{\mu})|\Omega|M,\quad\forall t\geq 0\ \text{and}\ \bm{x}_{0}\in B.

Hence, we have

|P(t+h)-P(t)|\leq h(\bar{\chi}\bar{e}+\bar{\mu})|\Omega|M,\quad\forall t\geq 0\ \text{and}\ \bm{x}_{0}\in B.

(4.3)

It follows that

J_{1}\leq C\int_{0}^{t-h}|P(t-a-h)-P(t-a)|da\leq Cth,\quad\forall\bm{x}_{0}\in B.

(4.4)

To estimate
J_{2}
, we note that, for any
\epsilon>0
, by (
4.1
) and the well-known parabolic estimates and embedding theorems, the following set is precompact in
Y
:

O_{\epsilon,B}:=\{u(\cdot,s):\ \epsilon\leq s\leq t\ \text{and}\ \bm{x}_{0}\in B\}.

Applying variation of constant formula to the first equation of (
1.1
), we obtain

u(\cdot,t-a)=T_{A_{1}}(h)u(\cdot,t-a-h)+\int_{t-a-h}^{t-a}T_{A_{1}}(t-a-s)f(s)ds,\quad\forall a\in[0,t-h],

where
f(s)=B(s)-(m+e)u-cu^{2}
. Since
\{T_{A_{1}}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is strongly continuous, by Lemma
4.3
,
\lim_{h\to 0}T_{A_{1}}(h)v=v
uniformly for
v
in compact subsets of
Y
.
Hence, by the compactness of
O_{\epsilon,B}
and (
4.1
), we have

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t-a)-u(\cdot,t-a-h)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|T_{A_{1}}(h)u(\cdot,t-a-h)-u(\cdot,t-a-h)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle+\Big\|\int_{t-a-h}^{t-a}T_{A_{1}}(t-a-s)f(s)ds\Big\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{\epsilon}h+Ch

for all
0\leq a\leq t-h-\epsilon
and
\bm{x}_{0}\in B
, where
C_{\epsilon}
is depending on
\epsilon
. It follows that

\displaystyle J_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t-h-\epsilon}\|u(\cdot,t-a-h)-u(\cdot,t-a)\|_{\infty}da+\int_{t-h-\epsilon}^{t-h}\|u(\cdot,t-a-h)-u(\cdot,t-a)\|_{\infty}da

(4.5)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle t(C_{\epsilon}+C)h+2M\epsilon

for all
\bm{x}_{0}\in B
.

To estimate
J_{3}
, by the assumptions on
\mu
,

\displaystyle J_{3}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t-h}\Big\|{\int_{0}^{a+h}\mu(\cdot,a+h-s,P(t-s))ds}-{\int_{0}^{a}\mu(\cdot,a-s,P(t-s))ds}\Big\|_{\infty}da

(4.6)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t-h}\Big\|\int_{a}^{a+h}\mu(\cdot,a+h-s,P(t-s))ds\Big\|_{\infty}da

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t-h}\int_{0}^{a}\|\mu(\cdot,a+h-s,P(t-s))-\mu(\cdot,a-s,P(t-s))\|_{\infty}dsda

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}th+\int_{0}^{t-h}\int_{0}^{a}Chdsda\leq C(t+t^{2})h

for all
\bm{x}_{0}\in B
.

Combining (
4.4
), (
4.5
) and (
4.6
), we get

I_{1}\leq C(t+t^{2})h+C_{\epsilon}th+C\epsilon,\quad\forall\bm{x}_{0}\in B.

(4.7)

Now, we estimate
I_{2}
. By (
4.1
),

\|w_{2}(\cdot,a,t)\|_{\infty}\leq\bar{\chi}\bar{e}M.

Hence,

I_{2}=\int_{t-h}^{t}\|w_{2}(\cdot,a,t)\|_{\infty}da\leq\bar{\chi}\bar{e}Mh,\quad\forall\bm{x}_{0}\in B.

(4.8)

Combining (
4.7
) and (
4.8
), since
\epsilon>0
was arbitrary, (
4.2
) holds. This verifies condition (iii) in Lemma
4.2
.

Finally, we verify condition (iv) in Lemma
4.2
. By the definition of
w_{2}
, it suffices to show that, for fixed
t>0
and any
\epsilon>0
, the following set is precompact in
Y
:

W_{\epsilon,B}:=\{w_{2}(\cdot,a,t):\ 0<a<t-\epsilon\ \text{and}\ \bm{x}_{0}\in B\}.

By (
4.1
), there exists
P^{*}>0
such that
0\leq P(t)\leq P^{*}
for all
t\geq 0
and
\bm{x}_{0}\in B
. Since the continuous image of a compact set is compact, the following set

\{e\chi(\cdot,P)e^{-\int_{0}^{a}\mu(\cdot,a-s,P)ds}:\ 0\leq P\leq P^{*}\}

is compact in
Y
. Since
O_{\epsilon,B}
is precompact,
W_{\epsilon,B}
is precompact in
Y
. This completes the proof.

By the well-known dynamical system theory
[
16
,
51
]
, combining Theorems
3.1
and
4.4
, we have the following result:

Corollary 4.5
.

Suppose
(A1)
-
(A3)
and
(A5)
hold. Let
\{U(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
be the semiflow induced by the solutions of (

). Then
\{U(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
has a global attractor in
\mathbb{X}
.

Now, we know that the
\omega
-limit set of
\bm{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{X}_{0}
is compact. So we can improve Theorem
2.11
to obtain the convergence of

to an equilibrium as

.

Theorem 4.6
.

Suppose that
(A1)
-
(A5)
hold. Let
\{U(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
be the semiflow induced by the solutions of (
1.1
) and
\bm{x}_{0}\in D(A)\cap\mathbb{X}_{0+}
. Then the following statements hold:

(i)

If
A\bm{x}_{0}+F(\bm{x}_{0})\leq 0_{\mathbb{X}}
, then the map
t\rightarrow U(t)\bm{x}_{0}
is decreasing on
[0,\infty)
, and
U(t)\bm{x}_{0}\to\bm{x}^{*}
in
\mathbb{X}
as

, where
\bm{x}^{*}\in D(A)\cap\mathbb{X}_{0+}
satisfies
A\bm{x}^{*}+F(\bm{x}^{*})=0_{\mathbb{X}}
;

(ii)

If
A\bm{x}_{0}+F(\bm{x}_{0})\geq 0_{\mathbb{X}}
, then the map
t\rightarrow U(t)\bm{x}_{0}
is increasing on
[0,\infty)
, and
U(t)\bm{x}_{0}\to\bm{x}^{*}
in
\mathbb{X}
as
t\to\infty
, where
\bm{x}^{*}\in D(A)\cap\mathbb{X}_{0+}
satisfies
A\bm{x}^{*}+F(\bm{x}^{*})=0_{\mathbb{X}}
.

Proof.

(i) Suppose that
\bm{x}_{0}\in D(A)\cap\mathbb{X}_{0+}
satisfies
A\bm{x}_{0}+F(\bm{x}_{0})\leq 0_{\mathbb{X}}
. By Theorem
3.1
, the solution
U(t)\bm{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{X}_{0_{+}}
is bounded and exists globally. By Corollary
4.5
, the
\omega-
limit set of
U(t)\bm{x}_{0}
is compact. By Theorem
2.11
,
U(t)\bm{x}_{0}
is decreasing in
[0,\infty)
. Hence,
U(t)\bm{x}_{0}\to\bm{x}^{*}
in
\mathbb{X}
for some
\bm{x}^{*}\in\mathbb{X}_{0+}
as
t\to\infty
.

By Theorem
2.2
,
U(t)\bm{x}_{0}
satisfies
\int_{0}^{t}U(s)\bm{x}_{0}ds\in D(A)
and

\displaystyle U(t)\bm{x}_{0}=\bm{x}_{0}+A\int_{0}^{t}U(s)\bm{x}_{0}ds+\int_{0}^{t}F(U(s)\bm{x}_{0})ds,\quad\forall t>0.

(4.9)

Let
\bm{x}_{n}=\int_{n-1}^{n}U(s)\bm{x}_{0}ds
for any

. Then,

\bm{x}_{n}=\int_{0}^{n}U(s)\bm{x}_{0}ds-\int_{0}^{n-1}U(s)\bm{x}_{0}ds\in D(A),\quad\forall n\geq 1.

Moreover, by (
4.9
),

U(n)\bm{x}_{0}-U(n-1)\bm{x}_{0}=A\bm{x}_{n}+\int_{n-1}^{n}F(U(s)\bm{x}_{0})ds,\quad\forall n\geq 1.

Since

in

as

, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}A\bm{x}_{n}=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{n-1}^{n}F(U(s)\bm{x}_{0})ds=-F(\bm{x}^{*}).

Since the operator
A:D(A)\subset\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{X}
is closed, we must have
\bm{x}^{*}\in D(A)
and
A\bm{x}^{*}=-F(\bm{x}^{*})
.

The proof of (ii) is similar to (i), so it is omitted.

5
The net reproductive rate

In this section, we define the net reproductive rate
\mathcal{R}_{0}
for the system (
1.1
) and study the properties of
\mathcal{R}_{0}
. Biologically,
\mathcal{R}_{0}
can be thought as the average number of offspring produced by a single individual. It is obvious that system (
1.1
) always has trivial steady state
E_{0}=(0_{Y},(0_{Y},0_{Z}))
. To define
\mathcal{R}_{0}
, we consider the linearized system of (
1.1
) at
E_{0}
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\partial_{t}u=d\Delta u+\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a_{\max}}\beta(x,a,0)w(x,a,t)da-(m(x)+e(x))u,&x\in\Omega,t>0,\\
\partial_{t}w+\partial_{a}w=-\mu(x,a,0)w,&x\in\Omega,t>0,a\in(0,a_{\max}),\\
\partial_{\nu}u=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,t>0,\\

Lemma 5.1

The operator

Proof.

Let
(\varpi,(y,\vartheta))\in\mathbb{X}
and

, one can check that

(\lambda I-\mathcal{B})^{-1}\begin{gathered}\begin{pmatrix}\varpi\\
\begin{pmatrix}y\\
\vartheta\end{pmatrix}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}u\\
\begin{pmatrix}0_{Y}\\
w\end{pmatrix}\end{pmatrix}\end{gathered}

holds if and only if

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\ w(x,a)=e^{-\int_{0}^{a}(\mu(x,l,0)+\lambda)dl}(y+\chi(x,0)e(x)u(x))+\int_{0}^{a}e^{-\int_{s}^{a}(\mu(x,l,0)+\lambda)dl}\vartheta(x,s)ds,&\\
&\ u=(\lambda+m+e-d\Delta)^{-1}\varpi.\end{aligned}\right.

(5.3)

),

is invertible and

By our assumption,

Lemma 5.2

\displaystyle\left\|(\lambda I-\mathcal{B})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\varpi\\

5.2

In this subsection, we consider the exponential growth bound of the linearized system (

and the eigenvalues of its generator. We write (

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\ \frac{d\bm{x}}{dt}=\mathcal{L}\bm{x},\quad\forall t\geq 0,&\\
&\ \bm{x}(0)\in\overline{D(\mathcal{L})}=\mathbb{X}_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

(5.7)

where
\mathcal{L}=A+D
and

D:\mathbb{X}_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{X}
denotes the derivative of
F
at
E_{0}
, i.e.,

D\begin{gathered}\begin{pmatrix}u\\
\begin{pmatrix}0_{Y}\\
w\end{pmatrix}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\int_{0}^{a_{\max}}\beta(\cdot,a,0)w(\cdot,a)da-(m+e)u\\

Here,

By Lemma

\mathcal{L}_{0}

on

The

of

is defined as

\omega=\omega(\mathcal{L}_{0}):=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\ln(\|T_{\mathcal{L}_{0}}(t)\|)}{t}.

Then, for any
\omega^{\prime}>\omega

C
such that

for all

.
It is well-known (see

]

where

is the spectral bound of
\mathcal{L}_{0}
and
\omega_{ess}(\mathcal{L}_{0})
is the essential growth bound of

defined by

\omega_{ess}(\mathcal{L}_{0}):=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\ln(\alpha(T_{\mathcal{L}_{0}}(t)))}{t}.

Here

is the measure of non-compactness, that is, for any bounded linear operator
\mathcal{H}
on
\mathbb{X}_{0}

\alpha(\mathcal{H}):=\inf_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\|\mathcal{H}-\mathcal{S}\|,

where
\mathcal{S}
is the subset of compact linear operators on
\mathbb{X}_{0}
.

Our next result shows that
\omega_{ess}(\mathcal{L}_{0})
is negative.

Lemma 5.6
.

If
a_{\max}=\infty
, then

; if

, then

Proof.

We write
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{E}+\mathcal{C}
, where

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{E}\bm{x}=\begin{pmatrix}d\Delta u-(m+e)u+\int_{0}^{a_{\max}}\beta(\cdot,a,0)w(\cdot,a)da\\
-w(\cdot,0)\\
-w_{a}-\mu(\cdot,\cdot,0)w\end{pmatrix},\quad\forall\bm{x}=(u,(0_{Y},w))\in D(\mathcal{E}),\end{gathered}

and

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{C}\bm{x}=\begin{pmatrix}0_{Y}\\
\chi(\cdot,0)eu\\
0_{Z}\end{pmatrix},\quad\forall\bm{x}=(u,(0_{Y},w))\in\mathbb{X}_{0}.\end{gathered}

Then,
\mathcal{E}
is a bounded linear perturbation of
A
, so it is a Hille-Yosida operator. So,
\mathcal{E}_{0}
, the part of
\mathcal{E}
on
\mathbb{X}_{0}

Let
B=\{\bm{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{X}_{0}:\ \|\bm{x}_{0}\|_{\mathbb{X}}\leq r\}
for some

.
Let

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\partial_{t}u=d\Delta u+\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a_{\max}}\beta(x,a,0)w(x,a,t)da-(m(x)+e(x))u,&x\in\Omega,t>0,\\

(5.9)

It is easy to see that the equations of
w

0_{Y},\ &\text{if}\ a<t.\end{cases}

\displaystyle\|w(\cdot,\cdot,t)\|_{Z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\|w(\cdot,a,t)\|_{\infty}da

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}\|w_{0}(\cdot,a-t)e^{-\int_{a-t}^{a}\mu(\cdot,s,0)ds}\|_{\infty}da

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{-\underline{\mu}t}\|w_{0}\|_{Z}\leq re^{-\underline{\mu}t}.

By the well-known parabolic estimates and embedding theorems, the set

is precompact in

\displaystyle\alpha(T_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}(t)B)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\alpha(\{u(\cdot,\cdot,t):\ \bm{x}_{0}\in B\})+\alpha(\{w(\cdot,\cdot,t):\ \bm{x}_{0}\in B\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0+\alpha(\{w(\cdot,\cdot,t):\ \bm{x}_{0}\in B\}

\displaystyle\|\{w(\cdot,t):\ \bm{x}_{0}\in B\}\|_{Z}\leq re^{-\underline{\mu}t}.

This implies that

.

.

=-\infty,&\text{if}\ a_{\max}<\infty.\end{cases}

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\lambda_{0}\phi-d\Delta\phi-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a_{\max}}\beta(x,a,0)\varphi(x,a)da+(m(x)+e(x))\phi=\phi_{0},&x\in\Omega,\\

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\int_{0}^{a}(\mu(x,s,0)+\lambda_{0})ds}\chi(x,0)e\phi+\int_{0}^{a}\varphi_{0}(x,l)e^{-\int_{l}^{a}(\mu(s)+\lambda_{0})ds}dl

7
Discussion

In this paper, we investigated the global dynamics of the parabolic-hyperbolic hybrid system (
1.1
), which models the spatiotemporal behavior of a population with distinct dispersal and sedentary stages. Due to the non-compactness of solution mapping and the presence of nonlocal terms
B(x,t)
and
P(t)
in the system, the global analysis require significant technical effort. Following the non-densely defined operator approach established in
[
28
]
, we reformulated the model (
1.1
) as an abstract Cauchy problem (
2.4
). Using the theory developed in
[
16
]
, we proved the asymptotic smoothness of solution semiflow.

The dependence of the vital rates (
\beta
,
\mu
, and
\chi
) on the nonlocal term
P(t)
presents considerable challenges when applying the Lyapunov method to analyze the global dynamics of model (
1.1
). Therefore, we employ the monotone semiflow theory developed in
[
29
]
to examine the global dynamics of the system. Additionally, we derived a biologically interpretable net reproductive rate,
\mathcal{R}_{0}
, and described its connection to the principal eigenvalue of linearized system (
5.1
) (see Theorem
5.7
). Under suitable monotonicity conditions, we obtained threshold dynamics results:
\mathcal{R}_{0}=1
serves as the boundary separating population persistence from population extinction.

Several avenues for future work are possible. (1) In our analysis, the monotonicity of the vital rates with respect to the total number of stationary individuals,
P
, plays a crucial role. Relaxing these monotonicity assumptions will introduce challenging problems that require further investigation. (2) Model (
1.1
) assumes that the effect of intra-specific competition on the settlement proportion,
\chi
, is spatially homogeneous, i.e.,
\chi=\chi(x,P(t))
. Alternatively, one could consider a spatially heterogeneous nonlocal term by defining
P(x,t)=\int_{0}^{a_{\max}}w(x,a,t)da
and letting
\chi=\chi(x,P(x,t))
in model (
1.1
). Notably, this modification would make proving the asymptotic smoothness of the solution semiflow for system (
1.1
) more challenging. (3) Model (
1.1
) employs the Laplacian operator to describe the local random movement of dispersing individuals. However, some species display nonlocal dispersal behaviors, where individuals select their steps randomly according to a specific distribution, influenced by various eological factors
[
1
,
23
,
26
]
. This observation motivates us to extend model (
1.1
) to a novel nonlocal model by replacing the Laplacian operator with an integral operator, where a dispersal kernel can be introduced to capture the nonlocal dispersal behavior. Exploring this revised model offers an intriguing avenue for future research. (4) The present study assumes that the population habitat is bounded. A fascinating question in spatial ecology is how and at what rate a population spreads through an unbounded domain if it is able to persist. We plan to address this question by analyzing traveling wave solutions and the spreading speeds of system (
1.1
) in an unbounded spatial domain.
