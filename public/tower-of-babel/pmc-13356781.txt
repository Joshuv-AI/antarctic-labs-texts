Impact of solid organ transplant on postoperative outcomes following lumbar spine fusion surgery: A propensity-matched retrospective cohort analysis
Authors: Vu Bui, Kevin Yoon, Theodore Quan, Christopher P Bellaire, Jonathan P Japa, Mark Ehioghae, Addisu Mesfin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Solid organ transplant (SOT) recipients undergoing lumbar spine fusion have higher rates of postoperative complications than non-transplant controls.•At 90 days, SOT recipients demonstrate increased wound dehiscence, renal failure, urinary tract infection, and composite medical complications.•At 1 year, SOT recipients have higher odds of reoperation and pseudoarthrosis.•These findings highlight the need for tailored risk stratification and multidisciplinary perioperative management in this high-risk population.Keywords: Solid organ transplant, Lumbar fusion, Lumbar spine, Pseudoarthrosis, Spine surgery, Immunocompromised

Introduction

Solid organ transplantation (SOT) has become increasingly prevalent in the United States, with more than 42,000 procedures performed annually and over 400,000 Americans living with a functioning transplant [[1], [2], [3]]. The majority of these involve kidney transplants, followed by liver, heart, lung, and pancreas transplants [[1], [2], [3]]. Advancements in both medical and surgical management have shown significant improvement in patient survival, with data suggesting a 5-year survival rates as high as 85% [4,5]. As a result, these patients increasingly undergo treatment for non-transplant-related conditions, including degeneration of the spine [[6], [7], [8]].

Chronic immunosuppression and transplant-related metabolic abnormalities are strongly associated with poor surgical outcomes after spine surgery [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. In addition to immunosuppressive exposure, transplant recipients frequently exhibit reduced bone mineral density, impaired calcium and vitamin D homeostasis, hypoalbuminemia, and chronic kidney disease– all of which may negatively affect wound healing, bone formation, calcium homeostasis, and fusion biology [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Additionally, the catabolic effects of common immunosuppressive therapies, such as calcineurin inhibitors and corticosteroids, directly impede bone healing and formation [13].

Prior studies have shown that SOT recipients undergoing orthopedic surgery are at increased risk of postoperative complications. Amin et al. reported increased medical complications and mortality following lumbar spine fusion in transplant recipients, while Labaran et al. demonstrated increased morbidity and mortality after anterior cervical decompression and fusion in this same population [14,15]. Similar findings have been reported in arthroplasty literature, with systematic review and cohort data showing increased complication burden after hip and knee arthroplasty in transplant recipients [[16], [17], [18]]. These findings support the broader concern that transplant-related comorbidity and chronic immunosuppression may increase postoperative vulnerability across orthopedic procedures [19].

Therefore, this study aims to evaluate short-term (90-day) and long-term (1-year) complications following lumbar spine fusion (LSF), with a particular focus on identifying specific risk factors to inform perioperative risk stratification for this vulnerable population. We hypothesize that SOT recipients will experience significantly higher rates of wound complications, infection, pseudoarthrosis, and mortality at both 90-day and 1-year postoperative intervals following LSF compared to non-transplant controls, primarily due to chronic immunosuppression and impaired physiologic healing mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Study design

The TriNetX United States Collaborative Network (Cambridge, Massachusetts, United States) was used to perform a retrospective cohort study of aggregated and deidentified data collected from more than 120 million patients and 71 Health Care Organizations globally. The platform incorporates biomedical and clinical research data from both insured and uninsured patients from a variety of organizations, including hospitals, primary and specialty care centers. Access to clinical data, including demographics, diagnoses, procedures, medications, laboratory values, and genomic information, facilitates analysis of complex data sets. Institutional Review Board approval was not required for this study, as TriNetX follows the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act and General Data Protection Regulation guidelines for deidentification.

Study population

The TriNetX United States Collaborative Network was queried on August 1, 2025, to identify all patients aged 18 years or older who underwent primary LSF between January 1, 2015 and June 1, 2024. The start date was selected in part to extend prior work that evaluated cases through 2014 [14]. For cases performed earlier in 2015, the corresponding ICD-9 codes were linked to the national transition to ICD-10, whereas cases after the transition were identified using ICD-10 codes. The end date was chosen to allow for a minimum of 1 year postoperative follow-up for all included patients. Eligible procedures were identified using Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes 22612, 22614, 22632, 22633, 22630, and 22634.

Exclusions and comorbidities were determined via ICD-10 codes. Only those with a diagnosis of lumbar spondylosis, lumbar disk disorders, or lumbar spinal stenosis were selected. Patients with a prior history of bone marrow transplant, active infection, malignancy, prior posterior fusion, or fracture were excluded from the analysis. SOT recipients were included regardless of baseline immunosuppressive regimen, and no exclusions were applied based on preoperative immunosuppressive therapy. The final study population was stratified based on transplant status: the SOT cohort consisted of patients with a documented history of kidney, heart, lung, liver, intestine, or pancreas transplant (ICD-10 codes Z94.0, Z94.1, Z94.2, Z94.82, Z94.83, respectively), while the control cohort comprised patients without any recorded SOT history.

Propensity score matching was performed using a 1:1 nearest neighbor algorithm with a caliper of 0.1 pooled standard deviations, matching for age, sex, race, ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), hypertension (HTN, I10), hyperlipidemia (HLD, E78.5), obesity (E66.9), anxiety (F41), nicotine dependence (F17), vitamin D deficiency (E55), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD, J44), heart failure (I50), and depression (F32). Matching variables were based on prior studies on a similar topic [14,15].

Data collection and outcomes

Primary LSF was designated as the index event. Postoperative complications assessed within 90 days of surgery across all propensity-matched cohorts included: surgical site infection (SSI), wound dehiscence, revision surgery, emergency department (ED) utilization, mortality, pseudoarthrosis, spinal cord injury (SCI), deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism (DVT/PE), myocardial infarction (MI), renal failure, sepsis, urinary tract infection (UTI), incision and drainage (I&D), hardware malfunction, composite medical complications (including MI, DVT/PE, pulmonary failure, and pneumonia), reoperation, and composite infection (including sepsis, SSI, and I&D). The same outcomes were evaluated at 1 year following the index fusion procedure. All CPT and ICD-10 codes used to define postoperative complications and procedures are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Data analysis

The TriNetX platform was utilized for all statistical analyses using built-in statistical tools. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all continuous and categorical variables. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas categorical variables were reported as frequencies with percentages. A chi-squared (χ2) test was used to analyze categorical variables, and an independent samples t-test was used to analyze the difference in means for continuous variables.

Propensity score matching was selected as the primary comparative approach to reduce baseline confounding between cohorts while maintaining interpretable between-group outcome comparisons; additional multivariable regression for each complication was not performed given the relatively small transplant cohort and low event counts for several outcomes. Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to assess the association between selected clinical variables and postoperative complications within 1 year of the index LSF. Covariates evaluated included steroid use, hypoalbuminemia, elevated creatinine, and selected immunosuppressive medications, including tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, and cyclosporine. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals were reported. A p-value < .05 was considered statistically significant. Patients with missing data were excluded from the analysis (Table 1).

Results

A total of 108,481 patients were eligible for analysis. Among these, 467 (0.43%) SOT recipients were identified. We report the full eligible cohort to highlight LSF outcomes in SOT recipients. All subsequent analyses were performed using 1:1 nearest neighbor propensity-matched cohorts to optimize comparability and statistical rigor. After matching, there were 437 SOT recipients and 437 nontransplant controls (Table 2). While the relatively small proportion of SOT patients may limit the power to detect rare complications and affect generalizability, it accurately reflects the clinical distribution encountered in practice (Table 3).

At 90 Day outcomes

At 90 days, SOT recipients had significantly higher odds of wound dehiscence (OR 2.33; 95% CI 1.17–4.67), ED utilization (OR 1.86; 95% CI 1.26–2.74), renal failure (OR 3.42; 95% CI 2.07–5.64), UTI (OR 2.97; 95% CI 1.77–5.00), and composite medical complications (OR 1.98; 95% CI 1.18–3.30) compared to matched controls (Fig. 1). The most common medical complications at 90 days included UTI, renal failure, and MI.

One year outcomes

At 1 year postoperatively, SOT recipients continued to experience significantly higher rates of complications compared to matched controls (Table 4). Specifically, SOT recipients had significantly increased odds of wound dehiscence (OR: 2.52, 95% CI 1.27–5.00), ED utilization (OR: 1.89, 95% CI 1.38–2.58), reoperation (OR: 1.75, 95% CI 1.06–4.59) and pseudoarthrosis (OR: 1.76, 95% CI 1.04–3.01) compared to the control group (Fig. 2). There was a non-significant positive trend for hardware failure (OR 1.19, 95% CI 0.53–2.70) and SSI (OR = 1.75, 95% CI 0.96–3.18) for the SOT cohort.

Cox proportional hazards modeling demonstrated that, among covariates, steroid use (HR: 1.32, 95% CI 1.20–1.44, p < .001), hypoalbuminemia (albumin < 3.5; HR: 1.7, 95% CI 1.46–1.98, p < .01), and elevated creatinine (HR: 1.12, 95% CI 1.03–1.21, p = .0048) were each independently associated with an increased risk of wound complications and medical complications at 1 year postoperatively. Use of tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, or cyclosporine was not significantly associated with wound complications in this analysis.

Discussion

In this propensity-matched retrospective cohort study, we found that SOT recipients experienced significantly higher rates of postoperative complications compared to non-transplant controls at both 90 days and 1 year postoperatively. Specifically, SOT status was associated with greater odds of wound dehiscence, pseudoarthrosis, ED utilization, and overall medical complications. Notably, these elevated risks persisted through the first postoperative year, highlighting the vulnerability of this patient population. Our findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating SOT recipients are at high risk for complications following LSF [10,14,[19], [20], [21]].

In our Cox proportional hazards analysis, commonly used immunosuppressive agents such as tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, and cyclosporine were not significantly associated with wound complications. However, the small sample size of SOT patients in our study may underpower the true risk of these medications for wound complications after LSF. Furthermore, the database nature of our study limits our insight into the individualized perioperative management of these medications. As a result, patient-specific interdisciplinary care with medical/surgical transplant services, as well as plastic surgery and infectious disease, is recommended both pre- and postoperatively to optimize wound healing in this high-risk patient population [22].

In SOT recipients, the high rates of pseudoarthrosis at 1 year can be attributed to the inhibitory osteogenic effects of standard immunosuppressive therapy [10,[23], [24], [25], [26]]. Concurrent metabolic dysfunction seen with renal, gastrointestinal, or liver transplant decreases the fusion potential in these patients [[25], [26], [27]]. In our cohort, SOT patients exhibited significantly lower albumin and calcium levels compared to controls. While hypoalbuminemia is frequently used as a surrogate for poor nutritional status, both hypoalbuminemia and hypocalcemia may also reflect underlying organ-specific dysfunction [28,29]. These abnormalities likely contribute to diminished bone healing capacity through multifactorial mechanisms, including impaired substrate availability, altered bone mineral metabolism, and systemic catabolic states [30]. Wada et al. and Yoon et al. reported two case series of renal transplant patients undergoing spine fusion procedures, reporting a 12% and 17% pseudoarthrosis rate, respectively [21,31]. Prior to LSF, computed tomography or Dual-Energy X-ray Absorptiometry scan may be considered to evaluate for bone quality, with preoperative metabolic optimization needed prior to surgery.

The SOT cohort had significantly higher ED utilization, as well as medical complications at all time points, compared to controls, which are expected findings given the morbidity related to organ transplantation and subsequent management [9,13,14,[32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37], [38]]. Higher rates of UTI and acute kidney injury can similarly be attributed to the relative predominance of kidney transplant patients and the renal-toxic effects of immunosuppressive medication [9,[39], [40], [41], [42], [43]].

Current literature suggests that adjustment of immunosuppressive agents, such as temporarily reducing the dose of calcineurin inhibitors or corticosteroids, may lower the risk of wound complications and infection but must be balanced against the risk of graft rejection or disease flare [44]. However, this evidence is not specifically derived from the spine surgery literature, and there are no current guidelines or consensus statements regarding the perioperative administration of these medications. As such, early multidisciplinary coordination between surgical and medical teams is essential to tailor perioperative immunosuppression on an individualized basis, optimize nutritional and metabolic status, and ensure patient safety. Future studies are needed to establish evidence-based guidelines regarding the timing, dosing, and perioperative management of immunosuppressive therapy in this vulnerable population.

Limitations

There were several limitations to our study that warrant consideration. Inherent to retrospective analysis, this work is susceptible to selection bias, potential coding inaccuracies, and unmeasured confounding variables. Although propensity score matching was used to balance key demographic and comorbidity variables and reduce confounding, residual differences between cohorts may persist and influence observed outcomes. Furthermore, the TriNetX database lacks patient-specific clinical and surgical information, such as medication dosing regimens, perioperative management strategies, and intraoperative complications, that could independently impact complication rates. Additionally, granular operative variables such as surgical approach, number of fused levels, and other technical factors were not available in the database and therefore could not be accounted for in the present analysis.

Our study also lacked information on the time from transplant surgery, or the severity or chronicity of organ dysfunction, and organ-specific transplant characteristics, which were unavailable, limiting the temporal impact of our findings. Notably, analysis based on transplanted organs was unavailable due to the limited number of patients available within the SOT cohort. Specific organs can impact bone healing (eg, kidney, liver), while other transplants are tied to inherent increased morbidity and mortality (lung, cardiac). Future studies are needed to better discern how organ type should be considered for individualizing treatment and postoperative management within this patient population.

We are also limited to 1-year LSF outcomes in these patients, with up to 85% of transplant patients surviving more than 5 years from transplantation [4,5]. Small event counts for certain complications among SOT patients may have further limited statistical power to detect rare adverse events; however, our use of a large, multi-institutional data repository strengthens our insights into these rare events, contributing to our growing understanding of the risks associated with LSF in SOT recipients.

Conclusion

SOT recipients undergoing LSF were found to have significantly higher rates of postoperative complications, including wound dehiscence, pseudoarthrosis, renal failure, UTI, and composite medical complications compared to non-transplant controls at 90 days and 1 year postoperatively. Collectively, these findings underscore the heightened perioperative vulnerability of SOT patients and highlight the need for patient-specific risk stratification, multidisciplinary care, and individualized perioperative management strategies to optimize outcomes.
