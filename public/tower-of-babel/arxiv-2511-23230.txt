Language-guided 3D Scene Synthesis for Fine-grained Functionality Understanding


Abstract
Functionality understanding in 3D, which aims to identify the functional element in a 3D scene to complete an action (e.g., the correct handle to “Open the second drawer of the cabinet near the bed"), is hindered by the scarcity of real-world data due to the substantial effort needed for its collection and annotation. To address this, we introduce SynthFun3D, the first method for task-based 3D scene synthesis. Given the action description, SynthFun3D generates a 3D indoor environment using a furniture asset database with part-level annotation, ensuring the action can be accomplished. It reasons about the action to automatically identify and retrieve the 3D mask of the correct functional element, enabling the inexpensive and large-scale generation of high-quality annotated data. We validate SynthFun3D through user studies, which demonstrate improved scene-prompt coherence compared to other approaches. Our quantitative results further show that the generated data can either replace real data with minor performance loss or supplement real data for improved performance, thereby providing an inexpensive and scalable solution for data-hungry 3D applications. 1
Introduction

The development of embodied AI agents capable of navigating and interacting with 3D environments has become a central goal in computer vision and robotics
Yang et al. 2024b
;
Zhou et al. 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2023
;
Song et al. 2023
.
A major driver of progress in this domain is the availability of diverse and realistic 3D simulated environments for training and evaluation
Deitke et al. 2022
;
Puig et al. 2024
;
Szot et al. 2021
.
Recently, Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have been leveraged for scene generation, allowing for the creation of large-scale and varied indoor spaces directly from textual descriptions
Yang et al. 2024b
;
Yang et al. 2024c
;
Huang et al. 2025
;
Halacheva et al. 2025
.
While current methods can produce plausible room layouts, they lack the focus on generating scenes specifically designed to teach agents fine-grained, functional interactions.

A key challenge for embodied AI involves moving beyond coarse object manipulation to understanding and interacting with specific functional parts of articulated objects, such as light switches, cabinet handles, power outlets, and oven knobs
Delitzas et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2025a
;
Rotondi et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025b
.
Developing generative models for such scenarios is complex.
It requires not only producing coherent large-scale scenes but also populating them with objects that feature the correct interactive elements, arranged in layouts that are both meaningful and sufficiently challenging to drive the learning of sophisticated and generalist policies.
Consider training an agent to execute commands like “
open the second top drawer of the cabinet on the left-hand side of the bed
.”
Fulfilling this instruction demands a level of scene understanding that encompasses spatial relationships, object semantics, and the affordances of specific parts
Corsetti et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025a
.
The agent must be trained on a large number of scenes containing diverse arrangements of beds, cabinets, and drawers, possibly in different scenarios and styles.

The effort required to create datasets like SceneFun3D
Delitzas et al. 2024
, which features manually annotated real-world scenes, is significant, and highlights that collecting and annotating such data at scale is practically unfeasible.
This underscores the field’s increasing reliance on synthetic data, yet a critical gap remains: no existing generation method is equipped to create such targeted, functionally-rich environments, presenting a significant bottleneck for advancing embodied AI.
Training perception models, such as those for open-vocabulary segmentation
Takmaz et al. 2024
;
Zhu et al. 2024
;
Nguyen et al. 2024
;
Takmaz et al. 2025
;
Szilagyi et al. 2025
, also requires ground-truth annotations.
To generate training data for this task, a generative method must provide scenes along with their corresponding segmentation masks for all interactive elements.
Such a dataset of synthetic scenes and dense annotations would be key for pre-training perception models, which can then be fine-tuned on smaller, real-world datasets
Delitzas et al. 2024
for specific downstream tasks, bridging the sim-to-real gap and accelerating progress in robotic interaction.

In this paper, we propose
SynthFun3D
, the first 3D scene synthesis method designed to handle prompts describing actions to be carried out in a scene.
SynthFun3D generates a scene based on the implicit and explicit information contained in the prompt, including the arrangement of objects and their characteristics.
For example, a command like
“open the second top drawer of the cabinet on the left-hand side of the bed”
implies that (1) the room is likely a bedroom, (2) there is a cabinet on the left-hand side of the bed, and (3) the cabinet has at least two drawers.
By exploiting datasets of 3D assets with extensive metadata
Deitke et al. 2023
;
Xiang et al. 2020
, SynthFun3D can retrieve the mask of the particular object part that has to be manipulated to carry out the task, thereby providing ground-truth annotations for downstream tasks.
This is accomplished by a novel retrieval strategy that considers both the appearance of the asset and the arrangement of its parts.
SynthFun3D is built on the foundation provided by LLMs in a training-free manner, so that it can be seamlessly extended with new 3D asset datasets.
While most 3D scene synthesis methods rely on user studies to assess the quality of the scenes
Bokhovkin et al. 2025
;
Yang et al. 2024a
;
Sun et al. 2025
, we also test SynthFun3D by using its generated data to train state-of-the-art methods for the functionality segmentation task
Delitzas et al. 2024
.
Our results on SceneFun3D show an increment of 2.5 points in accuracy, compared to only using data from the original training set.

In summary, our contributions are:

•

We develop SynthFun3D, the first 3D scene synthesis method capable of generating scenes based on a functional prompt, and following layouts with strict requirements.

•

We design a robust strategy for the retrieval of parts of 3D assets, based on both object metadata and visual appearance. Our strategy can retrieve object parts with different granularity, and is not limited to functional objects.

•

We introduce the use of LLMs to process metadata of 3D assets to retrieve their segmentation masks based on natural language spatial constraints.

2
Related work

Functionality segmentation in 3D scenes
is a fundamental scene understanding task for embodied AI, requiring the segmentation of the interactive object or element necessary to accomplish an scene interaction (
e.g
.
, segmenting a drawer knob to “
open the drawer
")
Lemke et al. 2024
.
Acquisition and annotation of high-resolution point clouds for this task is challenging and costly, as it demands precise masks for small functional elements.
SceneFun3D
Delitzas et al. 2024
is the only currently available dataset, and even state-of-the-art segmentation models struggle to achieve satisfactory performance
Takmaz et al. 2024
;
Kerr et al. 2023
.
While training-free approaches exist, their performance is insufficient for real-world applications.
With SynthFun3D, we aim to generate large-scale synthetic data for functionality segmentation. Our approach relies on retrieving and placing objects that are pre-annotated with masks and semantic labels. This process allows SynthFun3D to automatically generate a 3D scene from a functional prompt and simultaneously provide perfect ground-truth mask annotations.

3D scene synthesis
methods primarily use either diffusion models
Yang et al. 2024a
;
Bokhovkin et al. 2025
;
Yang et al. 2024b
;
Vogel et al. 2024
or retrieval from large 3D asset datasets
Huang et al. 2025
;
Yang et al. 2024c
.
Most rely on textual priors to infer room type and guide object placement
Yang et al. 2024c
;
Tang et al. 2024
;
Bokhovkin et al. 2025
, though some condition on a provided room layout
Yang et al. 2024a
;
Yang et al. 2024b
.
Prompt granularity varies from high-level functionality descriptions (Holodeck
Yang et al. 2024c
, SceneFactor
Bokhovkin et al. 2025
) to fine-grained object relationships (Diffuscene
Tang et al. 2024
).
While many works are evaluated via user studies or image quality metrics
Yang et al. 2024a
;
Bokhovkin et al. 2025
;
Huang et al. 2025
, embodied AI approaches like PhyScene
Yang et al. 2024b
and Holodeck
Yang et al. 2024c
measure utility by assessing performance on downstream tasks (
e.g
.
, object navigation
Batra et al. 2020
).
For SynthFun3D, we generate data for functionality understanding, for which conditioning on textual prompts is the most natural approach.
We evaluate our generated data by training a VLM for functionality understanding and measuring its performance gain.

3D asset datasets
are essential for retrieval-based
3D
scene synthesis methods, which use textual descriptions
Yang et al. 2024c
;
Huang et al. 2025
to populate environments rather than relying on diffusion models. Early examples like 3D-Future
Fu et al. 2021
focused on furniture, while later databases like Objaverse
Deitke et al. 2023
(with
818\text{k}
objects) broadened categories for greater realism.
However, a critical limitation of both
3D-Future
and
Objaverse
is the lack of fine-grained, part-level annotations within single objects, a strict requirement for generating scenes with both part- and object-level masks. To address this,
SynthFun3D
uses the
PartNet-Mobility
dataset
Xiang et al. 2020
, which provides
2\text{k}
articulated object models across
46
household categories, complete with object part masks and articulation parameters. To retrieve the necessary part mask from a textual object description,
SynthFun3D
employs an
LLM
-powered retrieval strategy that leverages
PartNet-Mobility
’s rich metadata.

3
Our approach

\begin{overpic}[main/figures/_diagram]
\end{overpic}

Figure 2
:

SynthFun3D consists of three main stages, illustrated by the numbered blocks.
Firstly, the input prompt is parsed by an LLM to extract a structured description of the room layout and the objects of interest.
Secondly, the target object is retrieved from a large-scale dataset annotated with masks and semantic labels.
The retrieval pipeline considers both the similarity of the asset with a provided description (1) and the arrangement and quantity of functional elements on it (2-3).
Finally, the retrieved objects are arranged within the generated room according to the spatial relationships explicitly defined in the input prompt, via a Depth-First-Search algorithm.

3.1
Overview

The SynthFun3D pipeline starts with a natural language task description, such as "
open the nightstand drawer next to the bed
".
First, a Large Language Model (LLM) parses the task, identifies the target object (the “
nightstand
”), and then generates a coherent room layout (§
3.3
).
This layout includes object descriptions, placement specifications, and spatial constraints (
e.g
.
, requiring the nightstand to be near the bed).
Next, we retrieve non-target objects from an unannotated database (Objaverse
Deitke et al. 2023
), while the target object comes from a separate database annotated with part masks and semantic labels, PartNet-Mobility
Xiang et al. 2020
(§
3.4
and §
3.5
).
Our retrieval strategy uses vision-language descriptors (like CLIP) and the LLM to reason about necessary object characteristics (
e.g
.
, the target “
nightstand top drawer
” requires a nightstand with drawers arranged vertically).
Using a Depth-First Search algorithm
Yang et al. 2024c
, we generate a valid room layout solution, which is then used to position each object (§
3.6
).

Fig.

2
summarizes our pipeline, with a focus on the retrieval.

3.2
Problem formulation

The goal of our task-based scene synthesis is to generate realistic visual data for embodied AI training, driven by a task description
D
.
We introduce two elements:
F
, the name of the functional object whose mask we aim to recover, and
O
, the name of the contextual object that physically contains the functional object
Corsetti et al. 2025
;
e.g
.
, if
D
is “
open the second drawer of the nightstand
”,
F
is the “
drawer handle
” and
O
is the “
nightstand
”.
SynthFun3D performs retrieval from two 3D asset databases, denoted
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{S}
.
An object
u\in

\mathbf{U}
can be represented only as a mesh.
Instead, an object
s\in

\mathbf{S}
is represented as a mesh and associated with a set of part masks
M_{s}
and a corresponding set of semantic labels
L_{s}
.
These semantic labels are short textual descriptions (appearance and style) of the object part, such as “
handle
” or “
button
”.

3.3
Task description preprocessing

The task description
D
often contains references to spatial relationships between the objects
Corsetti et al. 2025
(
e.g
.
, “
open the drawer of the nightstand to the left of the bed
” implies the presence of a nightstand placed on the left of the bed).
It follows that providing a 3D scene with the correct layout is key to obtaining reliable training data.
Hence, we use an LLM to generate a description of the room layout, defined as
L
, given the task description
D
.
We constrain the LLM to provide a layout that includes all objects mentioned in
D
, but other objects that respect the likely room type can also be added (
e.g
.
, the above example probably defines a bedroom, so it is reasonable to add a dresser to the mentioned objects).
Likewise, we extract the object name
O
with an LLM.

3.4
Object retrieval

Given the layout
L
obtained in the previous step, we query the LLM to generate, for each object, a short description of its appearance and size
Yang et al. 2024c
.
The descriptions are then used to perform vector-based retrieval on
\mathbf{U}
(our asset database), by ensembling text-text similarity with Sequence-Bert
Reimers and Gurevych 2019
and text-image similarity with CLIP
Radford et al. 2021
.
If multiple elements are present for a single description, the one with the highest similarity is selected.

This text-based retrieval strategy allows us to select objects with suitable categories and consistent with specific styles.
However, the task descriptions
D
often imply very specific characteristics of the mentioned objects, which are not recognizable by object descriptions alone.
For example, “
open the left door of the fridge
” implies that the retrieved fridge asset (i.e., the contextual object
O
) should feature two doors arranged horizontally.
Similarly, “
open the top-left drawer of the cabinet
”, implies that the retrieved cabinet should feature drawers arranged in a grid-like arrangement, in which a “top-left” drawer is immediately recognizable.
In order to handle these challenging cases, which are the majority in functionality understanding datasets
Delitzas et al. 2024
, we design a retrieval strategy specifically for the contextual objects, which we describe in the next subsection.

3.5
Metadata-based mask retrieval

The retrieval strategy for contextual objects uses the metadata provided by
\mathbf{S}
, and in particular, the semantic labels associated to each part mask, from which we wish to select the functional element name
F
.

Text-to-asset retrieval.

Given the description of the contextual object
O
generated in §
3.3
, we retrieve objects as detailed in §
3.4
, but we use
\mathbf{S}
as the retrieval database, as it contains assets annotated with masks and semantic labels, and replace CLIP with a PerceptionEncoder
Bolya et al. 2025
for text-image retrieval.
Additionally, we retain all the candidate objects over a threshold instead of selecting a single one.
From this set of candidates, we wish to (i) select an object consistent with the task description
D
, and (ii) retrieve the part mask that corresponds to the functional object used to carry out the action described in
D
.

Requirement-based filtering
.
We consider the labels
L_{s}
of the functional objects present in all the retrieved assets.
We provide the LLM with the task description
D
and the list of candidate functional elements across
\mathbf{S}
, and ask it to provide (i) the name of the functional element
F
consistent with the object type, and (ii) a requirement of the number of
F
a candidate object should have to be considered.
Consider a
D
such as “
open the fridge door
”: this task implies that the fridge has a single door, and therefore a suitable requirement could be
handle = 1
.
Instead, “
open the third drawer of the nightstand
” implies the presence of at least three drawers, so that a requirement could be
handle >= 3
.
With this strategy, SynthFun3D can discard objects that (1) are ambiguous with respect to
D
, and (2) do not provide the correct label names.

Functional elements arrangement.

We select candidate objects based on the fine-grained arrangement of their functional parts.
A prompt like “
open the bottom door of the oven
” requires an asset to have multiple, vertically arranged doors that satisfy the spatial constraint.
For each candidate object retrieved in the previous step, we process all part masks (
\text{M}_{s}
) labeled
F
.
We compute parts’ 3D centroids, discard the depth coordinate (Z), and normalize the X and Y coordinates, yielding a set of 2D centroids for each candidate object.
We then enhance the label names using the
\mathbf{S}
hierarchy metadata.
We concatenate the label of the functional element (
e.g
.
, “
handle
") with its parent object’s label (
e.g
.
, “
door
"), resulting in detailed names like “
door handle
."
This is critical for disambiguating elements in complex furniture (
e.g
.
, distinguishing a door handle from a drawer handle on the same cabinet).
The LLM is then given the object’s 2D centroids, the enriched part labels, the functional prompt (
D
), and a defined frame of reference (
e.g
.
, Y=0 is the bottom, Y=1 is the top).
The LLM is instructed to judge the coherence between the part arrangement and the prompt’s constraints.
This step is designed to discard objects whose functional elements do not match the required arrangement (
e.g
.
, eliminating an object if the prompt demands “
open the left closet door
” but no clear leftmost door exists).
The LLM outputs the corresponding part mask ID for any suitable object.
If multiple objects are selected in this stage, we choose one randomly with equal probability.
This final step selects a contextual object consistent with the provided prompt, while the
\mathbf{S}
metadata provides the relative functional mask
\mathcal{M}
.

3.6
Layout optimization

To translate the layout description (
L
), generated in §
3.4
, into actual object placement, we adapt the approach from Holodeck
Yang et al. 2024c
.
First, we provide an LLM with a fixed set of constraints about object arrangement, and ask it to translate
L
into a set of clauses that use these constraints. Constraints can be absolute when they define the position of an object with respect to the room (
e.g
.
,
table <central>
indicates that the “
table
” asset is positioned in the center of the room), and relative when they define the position of an object relative to another object (
e.g
.
,
nightstand bed <left-of>
means that the “
nightstand
” asset is on the left of the bed).
Holodeck considers these constraints generated based on common-sense knowledge, assuming that the LLM can retrieve information about the common arrangement of furniture in a room.
This is reasonable if the objective is to provide a realistic room layout, but this approach alone fails when a specific layout is requested.
Unlike Holodeck, we instruct the LLM to closely follow the object arrangement described in
L
, while we let the LLM follow common-sense instructions for all the other objects.
This ensures both consistency with the task description and variability in the layout, which is a key characteristic of effective synthetic training data
Zhang et al. 2025b
;
Maini et al. 2025
.
In Holodeck
Yang et al. 2024c
, each constraint is treated as a soft constraint, i.e., it can be dropped to provide an additional solution.
Differently, in SynthFun3D, we require a layout that strictly follows the provided prompt to ensure consistency with
D
, and therefore we treat all constraints as hard constraints.

To obtain the final layout, we use an algorithm based on Depth-First-Search (DFS) to generate the possible solutions and return those that respect all the constraints.
The DFS iteratively chooses one constraint on one object and finds a placement that satisfies it, and continues until a solution is found or a time limit is met.
We run DFS twice: the first pass places all objects strictly required by the layout description, using hard constraints.
The second pass places additional objects with soft constraints to ensure more variability.

3.7
Rendering photorealistic images

Figure 3
:

Left: A synthetic RGB image and its corresponding multi-instance segmentation mask generated with Blender
ble 2024
.
Right: Photorealistic variants produced by our prompt-driven style transfer pipeline based on Cosmos-Transfer 2.5
Ali et al. 2025
. The generated images show spatial and semantic consistency with the input segmentation while exhibiting substantial diversity in style, materials, and illumination.

We render each scene from multiple viewpoints. For every rendered
RGB
frame, we automatically generate two critical annotations: (i) a segmentation mask of the target functional object (for training segmentation and grounding models), and (ii) a multi-instance segmentation mask (for guiding control-based generation models
Zhang et al. 2023
) (
Fig.

3
, left)).
To enhance the photorealism and diversity of this synthetic data, we employ a prompt-driven style transfer pipeline using Cosmos-Transfer 2.5
Ali et al. 2025
.
This pipeline inputs the RGB frames and multi-instance masks, alongside automatically generated textual prompts.
These prompts are created by first using Cosmos-Reason 1
Azzolini et al. 2025
to caption the video content, and then leveraging Llama-Nemotron-Super
Bercovich et al. 2025
for augmentation with varied environmental (
e.g
.
, day, sunset, night) and stylistic contexts (
e.g
.
, office, industrial, mountain, beach).
This process is an effective augmentation strategy to mitigate the synthetic-to-real domain gap (Fig.
3
, right).

4
Experiments

Setup.

We assess the quality of the generated data through qualitative analyses, and user studies in which participants reported their preferences among samples obtained from different sources
Yang et al. 2024c
;
Yang et al. 2024b
.
In these studies, we want to validate the correctness of the objects and of the scene layout, rather than perceived quality.
We compare four sources: our standard model, Holodeck
Yang et al. 2024c
, and two versions of our model without key components in the retrieval: in the first, we remove the functional element filtering, and in the second, we do not use hierarchy metadata.
We finetune Qwen3-VL
Bai et al. 2025
for functionality understanding on our generated data, and compare with the version trained only on real data.

Implementation details.

We use GPT-OSS-20B as our LLM, implemented with Ollama.
As
\mathbf{U}
we use Objaverse
Deitke et al. 2023
, while PartNet-Mobility
Xiang et al. 2020
serves as
\mathbf{S}
.
To perform retrieval, we rely on CLIP
Radford et al. 2021
when retrieving from
\mathbf{U}
, and on PerceptionEncoder
Bolya et al. 2025
for
\mathbf{S}
.

4.2
User studies

\begin{overpic}[main/figures/_charts] \end{overpic}

Figure 5
:

User studies on the agreement between layout description and observed layout (a), and on the correctness of the retrieved masks (b).
(c, left) shows the object retrieval user preference of SynthFun3D vs. the competitors.
(c, right) shows the object retrieval user preference of SynthFun3D compared to Holodeck, with results split by object groups.
We selected four expert annotators for (a) and (b), while 49 voluntary non-expert participants were involved in (c).

Scene-prompt coherence.

We evaluate how faithfully the layout of the generated scenes adheres to the configuration described in the input prompts.
We compare SynthFun3D against Holodeck
Yang et al. 2024c
.
Participants were shown a top-down rendering of a scene generated by one of the two methods, together with the prompt used to generate it.
For each sample, they assessed whether it satisfied two criteria: (1) the presence of the objects mentioned in the prompt, and (2) the correctness of the layout specified in the prompt.
Fig.
5
(a) reports the resulting success rates.
SynthFun3D achieves a success rate of 72.7%, substantially outperforming Holodeck at 40.5%.
Although Holodeck typically places the requested objects, it often fails to produce consistent layouts.
We attribute this to its DFS-based solver: as it applies soft constraints, it may produce solutions that do not satisfy all the spatial relationships specified by the prompt (
e.g
.
, placing a nightstand in front of the bed rather than beside it).
Instead, most failure cases of SynthFun3D arise from incorrect or incomplete spatial constraints produced by the LLM.
A common failure is the retrieval of objects that are similar to the requested ones (
e.g
.
, a dresser instead of a nightstand).

Mask retrieval.

We evaluate the quality of the retrieved mask for the functional element (
F
) through an ablation study, as Holodeck does not perform this task.
Participants were shown the task description (
D
) and the retrieved mask overlaid on the object rendering, and were asked to judge whether the mask correctly segments the object part relevant to the task.

Fig.

5
(b) reports the results.
Our default strategy is highly effective, achieving a success rate of 85.7%.
Most errors are qualitative, typically arising from contextual mismatches (
e.g
.
, retrieving a door when the prompt required a closet door) or inherited from retrieval errors in earlier stages.
The ablation results confirm the critical role of our filtering mechanisms.
Removing hierarchy-based labeling reduces the success rate to 69.2%, demonstrating that less descriptive labels severely hinder the
LLM
’s ability to identify the correct part.
Skipping requirement-based filtering causes an even larger drop, to 60.2%, as providing the LLM with less pertinent objects increases the chance of error.

Object retrieval.

We assess SynthFun3D’s object retrieval by restricting evaluation to the annotated
\mathbf{S}
, where contextual correctness matters for the final mask.
We compare four variants: (i) full SynthFun3D, (ii) Holodeck, (iii) SynthFun3D without requirement-based filtering, and (iv) SynthFun3D without hierarchy metadata.
Participants were shown an object name and two retrieved candidates (from two randomly sampled methods) and asked which better matched the query.

In Fig.
5
(c) (left), the first column shows that our strategy is rated better 38% of the time and worse 36% of the time than Holodeck.
This small difference in SynthFun3D with respect to Holodeck is caused by our retrieval strategy, which is based on the functional assets placed on the objects, rather than solely on the object name.
For a task description like “
open the third drawer of the nightstand
”, Holodeck will retrieve the object that most aligns with
nightstand
without considering fine-grained details (like the three drawers).
Conversely, our strategy might retrieve an object less aligned with
nightstand
(
e.g
.
, a chest of drawers or bedside table), but which has the correct parts to carry out the described action.
The second column shows that when the requirement-based filtering is removed from our retrieval strategy, our standard method is judged better 40% of cases, and worse only 22% of cases.
Thus, filtering helps in removing objects that are visually similar but have a different number of functional elements than the query one (
e.g
.
, a
nightstand
versus a
desk
).
Removing hierarchy metadata (last column) yields largely uniform user preferences across candidates.
While this component does not strongly impact the choice of the object instance, it is crucial for the correctness of the retrieved masks, as it allows the method to distinguish between identical object instances belonging to different parts (
e.g
.
, a

4.3
Data generation quality for downstream tasks

We validate SynthFun3D in generating informative training data, by using to train a model for the functionality segmentation task introduced by SceneFun3D
Delitzas et al. 2024
.
This task involves processing a 3D indoor point cloud, or a set of corresponding videos captured within the scene, to output a 3D mask highlighting the functional parts necessary to execute a specified task (
e.g
.
, highlighting the door handle for the task “
open the door
”).
The original training data of SceneFun3D features high-resolution 3D scans, acquired in more than 200 houses across different European cities, using a high-resolution laser scanner, which enables the reconstruction of fine-grained functional elements, such as knobs, handles, and switches, as well as high-quality RGBD videos captured with cameras equipped with a depth sensor.
The 3D point clouds were manually annotated and then verified using a dedicated annotation interface.
While this procedure ensures high-quality data, it is also expensive and cumbersome, requiring access to numerous different houses or apartments, specialized equipment, and considerable human effort for annotation, which limits the scale of this type of acquisitions.

SynthFun3D offers an inexpensive and scalable solution to generate synthetic training data that can complement or (potentially) replace the original SceneFun3D data.
We use the task descriptions provided in the training set of SceneFun3D to generate 812 synthetic scenes and obtain 3580 video renderings with associated segmentation masks.
We then augment 645 of these scenes to generate 2306 photorealistic videos using Cosmos-Transfer2.5
Ali et al. 2025
.

To validate our data quality, we train a pointing-capable VLM and integrate it with Fun3DU’s pipeline
Corsetti et al. 2025
.
We substitute their pretrained VLM with a Qwen3-VL-4B model
Bai et al. 2025
, and train it with LoRA
Hu et al. 2022
on image-point coordinate pairs coming from the original videos of SceneFun3D (R), from our synthetic videos (S), and from our photorealistic videos (C).
We evaluate on Average Recall (mAR), Average Precision (mAP), and Intersection-Over-Union (mIoU)
Delitzas et al. 2024
;
Corsetti et al. 2025
.
We prompt Qwen3 with the original task description of SceneFun3D,
e.g
.
, “
open the cabinet bottom drawer
”.
Qwen3 outputs a point on the function object that we pass to Fun3DU for further 3D segmentation.

5
Conclusions

We introduced SynthFun3D, the first 3D scene synthesis framework driven by functional action prompts (
e.g
.
, “
open the drawer
").
Our approach is training-free and combines LLM guidance with a robust retrieval stage, including LLM-based metadata parsing.
SynthFun3D satisfies user-specified layout constraints and automatically produces geometrically precise segmentation masks for the relevant functional parts.
SynthFun3D yields a 2.5-point mIoU improvement on the SceneFun3D functionality segmentation benchmark, confirming its effectiveness as a generator of task-specific data for pre-training perception models.
Current limitations arise mainly from the coverage size of the available 3D asset repositories (Objaverse, PartNet-Mobility), reliance on assets’ canonical representations, and the hallucinations caused by photorealistic generation and data augmentation.
Future work will extend SynthFun3D to multi-step action prompts, incorporate physically grounded constraints, and broaden its applicability to embodied AI perception and planning.

2
Training on downstream task

In the following Sections, we detail the process used to obtain the ground-truth points, which are used to train a VLM on the downstream task.

2.1
Generating pointing data from SceneFun3D

To train the pointing-capable VLM used in our studies on the SceneFun3D dataset, we use high-resolution RGB-D videos (
1920\times 1440
), associated camera poses, and annotated 3D point clouds provided for each task description
D
.
We preprocess the video data by sampling every three frames to mitigate data redundancy.

Next, we select only frames where the 3D mask of the functional element is visible and the camera distance is within two meters; this constraint ensures sufficient spatial resolution of the target object.
We project the 3D mask on the point cloud onto the 2D image plane of frames that satisfies these conditions, and compute the mask centroid to serve as the pointing ground truth.
When multiple masks are present (e.g., a drawer with multiple knobs), the centroid for each is selected.
Finally, to prevent truncation artifacts, we discard samples where the calculated centroids fall within 200 pixels of the image boundary.

For each validated training sample, we construct a visual instruction tuning instance in a conversational format.
The input prompt conditions the model on the task description
D
, explicitly requesting a structured JSON output. Specifically, the dialogue is formatted as follows:

User:
Point to the object part I need to interact with in order to “
<task description>
”. Return the points using JSON…

Assistant:

json [{"point_2d": [x, y]}]

where
[x,y]
represents the ground-truth centroid coordinates.
This strict formatting ensures the model outputs machine-readable coordinates suitable for downstream evaluation.

2.2
Generating pointing data with SynthFun3D

We leverage SynthFun3D to synthesize 3D environments populated by assets with part-level annotations, explicitly tailored for task-oriented interactions.
Aligning with the SceneFun3D training distribution, we condition our scene generation on task descriptions
D
to ensure semantic coherence between the scene context and the functional object.
We simulate video sequences by generating random camera trajectories that orbit the target object, maintaining the functional element as the camera’s focal point.
For every trajectory, we simultaneously render RGB frames and their corresponding multi-instance segmentation masks. Consistently with the SceneFun3D preprocessing pipeline, we sample every three frames to avoid using redundant images.
We further filter the data based on object visibility: frames are discarded if the functional element’s mask occupancy is less than 0.01% (insufficient visibility) or greater than 25% (excessive proximity/occlusion) of the total image area.
From the remaining candidates, we select the top-5 frames per video with the highest visible surface area.
Finally, we compute the centroid of each target mask to serve as the pointing ground-truth and construct visual instruction tuning instances following the protocol defined in the previous section.
For the photorealistic variants generated via Cosmos, we replicate this entire procedure utilizing the Cosmos-synthesized RGB frames, instead of the ones produced by the standard renderer.

3.1
Ablation study on scene generation

Holodeck relies on a Depth-First-Search (DFS) algorithm
Yang et al. 2024c
to iteratively solve the constraints by placing the objects in the scene.
With respect to the DFS in Holodeck, our main modification lies in applying this algorithm in two steps.
In the first step, we place only the objects strictly required by the layout prompt
L
.
At this stage, we use hard constraints: if a constraint is not met, the algorithm backtracks to find a more suitable solution.
In the second step, we place additional objects that fit the room type, and, in this case, we use soft constraints, which assign a score to each successful placement.
This allows the algorithm to skip some constraints if no solutions are found.

By default, Holodeck only uses soft constraints, and therefore, it could drop some of the layout requirements requested in the prompt.
In the user studies in the main paper, we observed that this causes a large drop in the scene-prompt coherence judged by users (41% correctness of Holodeck versus 73% of our method).
To provide additional insight about the sources of error, we provide an additional user study, in which we repeat the evaluation described in the scene-prompt coherence study in Sec.
1
, but also add scenes generated using Holodeck with hard constraints.
We report the results in Fig.
2
, which clearly show that the improvement in using hard constraints alone is minimal with respect to soft constraints (42% vs 41%).
This shows that the main improvement is due to our two-step strategy, which applies hard constraints only to the objects that are strictly necessary with respect to the layout prompt
L
.

5.1
Partnet-Mobility annotations

Fig.
5
shows an example of the metadata provided by PartNet-Mobility, which is essential to the design of our retrieval strategy.
Each asset is composed by a set of parts, each associated with a label, and we manually select a set of label names to be used as functional elements for the whole dataset (
e.g
.
, “
handle
”,“
knob
”,“

”).
Fig.
5
shows in red the mask and label of each functional element as an example asset.
Additionally, given an asset, PartNet-Mobility provides a hierarchy in which the parts are organized.
To enrich the representation of each asset provided to the LLM, together with the label of each functional element, we also consider the label of its parent part (i.e., the part of higher level in the hierarchy).
Specifically, for each functional element, we provide to the LLM the label of the functional element concatenated with the label of its parent part.
Fig.
5
shows in green the mask and labels for each parent part of the functional elements.
In this example, all functional elements are labeled “
handles
”, but while the top two handles are on drawers, the bottom two are on cabinet doors.
By concatenating the labels of the parent parts, we obtain two “

” and two “

”.

On the right-hand side of Fig.

As for the object placement, we define left and right from the point of view of a person standing in front of the object.
