Landscape of approaches for streamlining pre-analytical and early analytical variables for solid tumor next-generation sequencing: A Genomics Organization for Academic Laboratories consortium report
Authors: Marjorie P David, Jennifer JD Morrissette, Noah Brown, Georgios Deftereos, Fei Dong, Karin E Finberg, Yulong Fu, Jeffrey Gagan, Christopher D Gocke, Audrey N Jajosky, Eric Konnick, Thomas D Lee, David B Lieberman, Deqin Ma, Laura MacConaill, Christopher Mignogna, Andrew C Nelson, Erik Nohr, Megan Parilla, Adam D Pfefferle, Somak Roy, Pamela Snyder, Eric Vail, Peng Wang, Liqiang Xi, Daniel Jones, Dara L Aisner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe scope of pre-analytical and early analytical methods used in clinical molecular laboratories can have vast effects on the quality and turnaround time for solid tumor next-generation sequencing assays. To learn more about various approaches, a survey of 24 academic laboratories within the Genomics Organization for Academic Laboratories consortium was administered, focusing on pre-analytical and early analytical issues in next-generation sequencing clinical assays that may affect patient care. The survey revealed the landscape of clinical laboratory practices for use of cytopathology smears, block selection, histology practices and protocols, sample transport, slide imaging, sample adequacy assessment, tumor cell enrichment, and deparaffinization methods. Most laboratories reported that they either accepted cytopathology smears for next-generation sequencing testing or had a plan to accept them soon. All laboratories surveyed performed micro- or macro-dissection on unstained formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded slides for tumor cell enrichment. Several laboratories reported sectioning blocks within the molecular laboratory. While most respondents used an organic solvent for deparaffinization, several laboratories used mineral oil as an alternative. The approaches of other laboratories reported here are expected to be an impactful resource to address pre-analytical issues that may improve assay quality and turnaround time.Keywords: Academic laboratory, Clinical laboratory, Molecular pathology, Next-generation sequencing, Pre-analytical, Solid tumor

Introduction

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) is a complex, multistep assay that allows technical customization at many steps, each with its own benefits and drawbacks.1,2 In a clinical laboratory, NGS of tissue from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) neoplasms can yield information essential for patient diagnosis, targeted therapy, or prognosis.3,4 Clinical molecular laboratories face a unique set of pre-analytical and early analytical variables, many of which are not encountered in other nonmolecular clinical laboratories and may have vast impacts on assay quality and turnaround time.5

Molecular pre-analytical variables related to solid tumors include the type of specimens that are accepted for testing, the personnel who select the best material for testing (and their training), and methods for assessing adequacy. The specifics of sample storage, sample preparation and block cutting protocols, transport, laboratory review of material, extraction protocols, and assay setup can all affect results. Regulatory bodies have promulgated best practices in pre-analytic handling and tumor content screening before molecular testing with some assessment of compliance.6

In assessing NGS adequacy, there has been extensive focus on lung cancer samples given the importance of molecular testing for therapy selection, with small core biopsies and fine-needle aspirate (FNA) samples commonly being the only available samples.7, 8, 9 There is also an extensive literature on the accuracy of tumor content assessment by pathologists because it is often critical for correct interpretation of molecular assays, such as expression analysis, copy number variants, and NGS-derived homologous recombination deficiency scores.10, 11, 12 Such tumor cell enumeration challenges have been linked to advances in digital pathology, including automated estimation of tumor content.13, 14, 15

Methods of tumor enrichment and improving NGS outcomes include macro- or micro-dissection, different deparaffinization methods, and postextraction nucleic acid concentration. These various approaches may all be acceptable within the scope of a particular laboratory's cost, staffing levels, assay volume, sample types, cost structure, and complexity of menu offerings. However, only a few studies have more broadly considered the effect of multiple pre-analytical processes on NGS success.5

Genomics Organization for Academic Laboratories (GOAL) is a consortium of academic non-profit laboratories in the United States and Canada with a mission to support, improve, and advance innovative clinical genomic testing for patients at academic and non-profit institutions through collaborative clinical and research projects, assay concordance studies, group purchasing power, aligned informatics opportunities, and clinical practice improvement.16,17 Given the increase in use of NGS testing on various sample types and the shared desire to reduce pre-analytical and early analytical processing time and quality issues, we convened a session of the GOAL membership to consider current practices and the implementation of new technologies. This work intends to inform laboratories of the scope of methods used in clinical molecular laboratories to improve turnaround time and minimize the effect of pre-analytic and early analytic variables on the quality of NGS assays.

Materials and methods

Survey to identify problem areas

The survey presented here arose through a question posed on the GOAL listserv regarding methods laboratories use to decrease pre-analytical and early analytical processing time and quality issues. The 22-question survey was developed and presented to GOAL member sites at a regular monthly GOAL meeting. The survey was administered in real-time via Zoom using the survey feature on April 4, 2023, and to laboratories that could not participate in the Zoom survey through Survey Monkey (open May 12th 2025 through May 23rd 2025). The survey questions and responses are found in Supplementary Material 1. Participants agreed to the publication of the survey results.

A total of 24 laboratories completed the survey. The 16 participating institutions in the first round of the survey included University of Alabama, Birmingham; Brigham & Women's Hospital, Dana Farber Cancer Institute; University of California, Los Angeles; University of California, San Francisco; University of Chicago; Cincinnati Children's; University of Colorado; University of Iowa; University of Minnesota; The Ohio State University; University of Pennsylvania; University of Rochester; University of Texas Health Science Center, San Antonio; University of Texas, Southwestern; and University of Washington. In the second round of the survey, the eight laboratories in the expansion cohort included Alberta Precision Laboratories, Cedars-Sinai Medical Center, Johns Hopkins, University of Michigan, the National Institutes of Health, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, NorthShore University Hospital/Endeavor, and Yale School of Medicine.

Data analysis was conducted using Microsoft Excel for Microsoft 365 MSO (Version 2501 Build 16.0.18429.20132).

Results

Survey responses were received from 24 of the GOAL member laboratories, with not all laboratories responding to every question. The role of the respondents in the laboratory included laboratory director, administrative director, molecular pathologist, and molecular technologist.

Block selection and histology practices

In nearly all responding laboratories (95.5%, 21/22), a pathologist made the initial determination of whether a specimen was suitable to send for molecular testing. Most commonly (59.1% of laboratories, 13/22), a molecular pathologist determined the suitability for sending a sample for molecular testing, while another large portion of laboratories had the sign-out surgical pathologist perform this determination (50.0%, 11/22), and two laboratories noted that a surgical pathologist performs this function, but they may not have signed out the case (9.1%, 2/22). Five laboratories reported two answers to this question, acknowledging this task is shared by multiple roles in some institutions.

To assist in initial block selection, 41.7% (10/22) of laboratories routinely included which block would be preferred for molecular testing within the surgical pathology report as a required component of the report, while 18.2% (4/22) routinely indicated a preferred block as an optional component of the report, 22.7% (5/22) report that some pathologists perform this task, and in 9.1% (2/22) of laboratories, this was not routine. Three sites (13.6%, 3/22) noted that the indication of a preferred block for testing in the pathology report was different for resections versus biopsies.

Most responding laboratories either accepted cytopathology smears for NGS testing (70.0%, 14/20) or had a plan to begin using them in the near future (20.0%, 4/20), while 10% (2/20) of laboratories did not use cytopathology smears. Among the laboratories that tested cytopathology smears, 21.4% (3/14) preferred them as a routine specimen source, 21.4% (3/14) tested them routinely, and 64.3% (9/14) accepted them only when necessary, with one respondent providing two answers (Fig. 1A). For slide review, most laboratories currently used some form of slide imaging (61.9%, 13/21), while 19.0% of laboratories (4/21) had slide imaging planned for future use, and 19.0% (4/21) do not use slide imaging nor plan to implement it soon (Fig. 1B).

In-laboratory pre-analytical processes

Once the specimen was received in the molecular laboratory for testing, nearly all laboratories had a pathologist confirm the specimen was acceptable for molecular testing. This role was performed in the molecular laboratory by a certified molecular pathologist in the majority of laboratories (68.2% or 15/22 with certification, and 13.6% or 3/22 ± certification), by a surgical pathologist in 22.7% (5/22) of laboratories, and by a nonpathologist in 9.1% (2/22), with two laboratories reporting two or three responses.

The assessment of FFPE specimens for molecular testing in the molecular laboratory was performed by individuals also signing out molecular cases at the same time in most surveyed laboratories (63.2%, 12/19), while specimen intake was a dedicated role in 15.8% (3/19). In 21.1% (4/19) of laboratories, FFPE assessment may be shared with molecular sign-out depending on the day of the week, and one laboratory reported that this role was currently in flux (5.3%, 1/19). In 10.5% (2/19) of laboratories, fellows handled this role. There were three laboratories that reported two responses to this item.

Histologic sections were reviewed for adequacy/extraction continuously throughout the day as received in 38.1% (8/21) of laboratories, while 19.0% (4/21) of laboratories performed this task multiple times per day, 28.6% (6/21) of laboratories perform this task once per day, 19.0% (4/21) of laboratories find the frequency varies depending on the day of the week, and one laboratory provided two responses.

When determining adequacy of specimen cellularity for molecular testing, most laboratories (55.0%, 11/20) relied on tumor cell percentage alone. Only 15.0% (3/20) of laboratories relied on the number of tumor cells alone, and no laboratories relied on the tumor surface area alone, while several laboratories (35.0%, 7/20) relied on a combination of those factors to assess adequacy (Fig. 1C). One laboratory provided two responses. Despite these determinations, most surveyed laboratories (65.0%, 13/20) reported they would still attempt to sequence nearly any sample, while 40% (8/20) had distinct cellularity cutoffs, and one laboratory provided two responses.

All laboratories offered either micro- or macrodissection (100%, 20/20), and nearly all laboratories (95.0%, 19/20) performed macrodissection of unstained FFPE slides (manual tissue enrichment without a microscope). Manual microdissection with a dissecting microscope was offered by 25.0% (5/20) of laboratories, laser microdissection was offered by only one laboratory (5.0%, 1/20), and core FFPE tissue punches were offered by 15.0% (3/20) of laboratories. Most laboratories (55%, 11/20) reported two or three methods of tumor cell enrichment. Whole scrolls were accepted by 35.0% (7/20) of laboratories.

After tumor cell enrichment, the next step in nucleic acid extraction from FFPE specimens is usually deparaffinization. The most common deparaffinization method reported was xylene or another organic solvent (55.0%, 11/20). Mineral oil was used for deparaffinization by 20% of laboratories (4/20), while 20% (4/20) of laboratories reported that the deparaffinization method may vary by specimen type. One laboratory (5%, 1/20) reported no deparaffinization method, which may represent the use of a sonication-based method for nucleic acid extraction (Fig. 1D). We found that many laboratories took measures to concentrate low-concentration samples using tools such as a microconcentrator column or a SpeedVac evaporator (45.0%, 9/20).

Turnaround times for molecular testing were provided routinely to clinicians by 20% (4/20) of surveyed laboratories and on request by 30.0% (6/20) of laboratories. Two laboratories (10.0%, 2/20) were planning on creating turnaround time reports for clinicians, while 40% (8/20) of laboratories had no plans to report turnaround time in the near future.

Discussion

Reported here are survey results describing approaches to pre-analytical and early analytical variables from clinical molecular laboratories of 24 institutional members of the GOAL Consortium, the first published survey of its kind, to our knowledge. This exercise compared pre-analytical and early analytical laboratory approaches. This study fits well with the aim of the GOAL Consortium to improve clinical practice and share laboratory technical expertise. A strength of the GOAL Consortium is its ability to easily collect organized data from member academic laboratories to address important questions such as how to improve turnaround time by reducing the time contribution of pre-analytical variables.

There is considerable pathologist effort put into both block selection and post-histology material review at the molecular laboratory, which is largely unreimbursed. There has been progress in inferring tumor content from the results of NGS data,12 which may eventually obviate the need for a precise tumor estimate but not the adequacy review. Given the cost and time involved in complex molecular methods, it will remain essential to screen for adequacy prior to testing. Quickly identifying inadequate samples is essential because this allows pathologists or oncologists to know earlier in the workflow if a new block or new biopsy may be needed.

We report a large proportion of the GOAL laboratories using cytology samples for NGS and other molecular testing, in line with the demonstrated utility of these sample types in other studies.7,18,19 The use of cytology specimens expands the opportunities for successful molecular testing for each patient neoplasm, particularly when an FFPE specimen is not acquired, or when there is insufficient tissue in the FFPE specimen. For the minority of laboratories that use cytopathology smears as a preferred specimen type, this is likely a source of increased efficiency as microdissection and deparaffinization steps are eliminated. Another benefit of using cytology specimens soon after collection may include increased nucleic acid quality due to lack of fragmentation and cross-linking from the formalin fixation process seen in FFPE blocks.20 However, drawbacks may include lower nucleic acid input, particularly with paucicellular specimens, which may lead to an insufficient specimen for molecular testing. For unstained cytopathology smears, pairing with a split-specimen stained smear is needed to estimate neoplastic cellularity. Given the advantages of integrating cytopathology specimens in the NGS workflow, including reducing repeat biopsies, laboratories are encouraged to explore the feasibility of this sample source for their NGS assays.

We found that slide imaging through whole slide scanning or capturing a microscopic image of representative tumor is a common practice among academic molecular laboratories. The benefits of digital pathology are numerous. Tracking a hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained glass slide along with a case for tissue assessment and case sign-out can be cumbersome, while digital images can be viewed remotely and easily accessed later for tumor board or teaching. In cases where macro- or microdissection is performed, the region used for extraction can be documented by scanning the circled H&E slide or annotating digitally. Additionally, with the new research applications of artificial intelligence to digital pathology, a bank of digital images and correlated molecular findings is a promising resource for future studies.

Conclusions

Our results do have the limitation of reporting only on the practices of academic institutions. The practices at community-based laboratories and private for-profit laboratories are not included in this study.

Our survey provides an excellent landscape portrait of the pre-analytical and early analytical phases of testing in academic clinical molecular laboratories. Laboratories of various sizes and specimen volume were included, which may address these variables with a variety of personnel and resources available to them. Insight into the approaches to these variables is expected to be a helpful resource to other laboratories hoping to address clinical molecular assay quality and turnaround time.
