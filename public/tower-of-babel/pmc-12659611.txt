Impact of internal carotid artery thickness on postoperative cognitive function in cervical spondylotic myelopathy patients
Authors: Huijuan Cao, PengXia Wang, Fan Yu, Jianmin Wang, Shanshan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCervical spondylotic myelopathy(CSM) is a prevalent degenerative disorder, with anterior cervical discectomy and decompression as the primary treatment. However, some patients may develop postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD). Carotid artery intima-media thickness (IMT), an early marker of carotid atherosclerosis, could be linked to changes in cerebral blood flow and cognitive function after surgery.MethodThe study included patients with cervical spondylotic myelopathy scheduled for anterior cervical discectomy and decompression. Based on preoperative carotid artery IMT measurements, patients were divided into two groups: high IMT (>0.9 mm) and low IMT (≤0.9 mm). Cognitive function was evaluated using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) before surgery and at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months after surgery. T-tests and multiple regression analyses were used to compare cognitive changes between groups, adjusting for confounding factors.ResultThe study included 100 patients, with 40 in the high IMT group and 60 in the low IMT group. At 1 week post-surgery, the MoCA scores in the high IMT group were significantly lower than those in the low IMT group (P＜0.05). However, no significant differences in cognitive function were observed between the two groups at 1 month and 3 months post-surgery, with both groups improving (P＞0.05).ConclusionElevated carotid IMT is associated with increased POCD risk after Anterior cervical discectomy and decompression (ACDF) in CSM patients. Preoperative IMT assessment may help predict POCD and guide personalized perioperative care.

Introduce

CSM is a prevalent degenerative condition primarily affecting middle-aged and elderly individuals. It arises from intervertebral disc degeneration, ligament hypertrophy, or osteophyte formation, leading to spinal cord compression [1, 2]. As the disease advances, prolonged spinal cord compression gradually impairs sensory, motor, and autonomic functions, manifesting as limb numbness, gait disturbances, balance impairment, and muscle weakness. Given its symptom overlap with other neurological disorders, achieving an early and accurate diagnosis of CSM is particularly challenging [3]. Anterior cervical discectomy and ACDF is widely regarded as the gold-standard surgical intervention for CSM [4]. This procedure effectively alleviates spinal cord compression by removing the affected intervertebral disc, thereby restoring neurological function and improving patient quality of life. However, despite the positive outcomes associated with symptom relief and enhanced functionality, a subset of patients develops POCD, a complication that can significantly hinder recovery and long-term quality of life [5–7]. POCD commonly manifests as memory decline, attentional deficits, language impairment, and cognitive slowing, particularly in elderly surgical patients [8]. Its etiology is multifactorial, encompassing the effects of anesthetic agents, inflammatory responses to surgery, and surgical trauma itself. However, cerebral blood flow dynamics—especially in the context of carotid atherosclerosis—play a pivotal role. Carotid atherosclerosis is frequently accompanied by increased carotid IMT, a recognized marker of vascular health and cerebral perfusion [9]. Elevated IMT signals progressive atherosclerosis and may adversely impact cerebral blood flow and postoperative cognitive outcomes [10, 11]. IMT is a critical metric for assessing carotid atherosclerosis, reflecting vascular wall thickening and stiffening. Studies consistently link increased IMT with heightened risks of cardiovascular events, including stroke, cognitive decline, and dementia. In patients with CSM, elevated IMT may compromise cerebral perfusion, exacerbating the brain’s vulnerability to ischemic injury and contributing to the development of POCD [12–15]. Additionally, individuals with significant IMT thickening often exhibit diminished cerebrovascular reserve, reducing their capacity to tolerate the hemodynamic and metabolic challenges imposed by surgery—another plausible mechanism underlying POCD. Emerging research has underscored the association between elevated IMT and poorer cognitive recovery after surgery, with heightened susceptibility to POCD among patients with increased carotid IMT. These findings suggest that preoperative IMT evaluation could serve as a valuable risk stratification tool for identifying patients at greater risk of cognitive decline [16]. For these high-risk individuals, perioperative management should prioritize tailored strategies, including optimizing anesthetic regimens, employing neuroprotective measures, and implementing vigilant postoperative cognitive monitoring [17]. Despite growing recognition of the interplay between carotid IMT and postoperative cognitive outcomes, the precise mechanisms remain insufficiently elucidated, particularly in the CSM population. This study seeks to explore the impact of carotid IMT variability on cognitive trajectories following ACDF in CSM patients. By delineating the relationship between elevated IMT and POCD, this research aims to establish IMT as a predictive marker for postoperative cognitive risk.

Materials and methods

Study design

This prospective observational study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Affiliated Chenggong Hospital of Xiamen University (approval number: 73JYY2024130527). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. The study aimed to explore the relationship between preoperative carotid IMT and postoperative cognitive function in patients with CSM undergoing ACDF. Patients were stratified based on IMT values to assess the impact of carotid vascular changes on cognitive trajectories following surgery.

Participants

From May to November 2024, this study included 100 cervical CSM patients who underwent ACDF under general anesthesia at Affiliated Chenggong Hospital Of Xiamen University Department of Orthopedics.The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: patients aged 40–65 years scheduled for elective ACDF due to CSM, with an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification of I-II, and without any significant systemic or internal medical conditions. Patients were excluded if they had a history of atrial fibrillation, carotid endarterectomy, uncontrolled hypertension or diabetes, familial hypercholesterolemia, Takayasu arteritis or other large-vessel vasculitis, systemic diseases, liver or kidney dysfunction, coronary artery stenosis < 50%, coronary artery disease (including angina or electrocardiographic evidence of myocardial ischemia), or prior ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke. A total of 135 patients were assessed for eligibility, 35 patients were excluded from the initial screening:25 did not meet the inclusion criteria, 6 had ineffective postoperative follow-up, and 4 refused to participate. Ultimately, 100 patients were included in the study, all of whom signed an informed consent form. A flowchart of the study is shown in Fig. 1. Prior to surgery, all participants underwent cervical vascular ultrasound, and based on their carotid IMT, they were randomly assigned to two groups: the high IMT group (> 0.9 mm) and the low IMT group (≤ 0.9 mm).Fig. 1CONSORT flow diagram for patient enrollment

Cervical vascular ultrasound monitoring

Using a high-resolution color Doppler ultrasound device with a probe frequency of 7–12 MHz, the patient is positioned supine with the neck slightly extended and the head turned to the opposite side to expose the target area of the neck. A properly adjusted pillow is used to provide comfort and stabilize the position.The ultrasound probe is placed at the carotid bifurcation and moved proximally along the common carotid artery (CCA) and distally along the internal carotid artery (ICA) for a thorough scan. Measurements are performed on the posterior wall of the CCA in plaque-free regions, approximately 1–2 cm proximal to the bifurcation, ensuring clear visualization of the intima and media layers. Each side is measured at least three times, with the average value calculated and recorded for both sides.Ultrasound measurements, such as carotid IMT, are inherently subjective and operator-dependent. To minimize potential biases and errors, all ultrasound examinations were performed using a standardized protocol by trained sonographers who were blinded to the clinical data. To ensure consistency, measurements were taken at predefined anatomical landmarks with patients in a standardized supine position and during end-expiration. At least three measurements were obtained from each carotid artery and averaged. Inter- and intra-observer variability was assessed by having a subset of images re-evaluated by two independent sonographers and remeasured by the same sonographer after a one-week interval. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated to evaluate measurement reliability. This rigorous approach ensured high accuracy and reproducibility of ultrasound-derived IMT values.

Anesthesia and surgical management

Standardized general anesthesia protocols were applied across all cases to minimize variability. Anesthesia induction involved intravenous administration of propofol (1.5–2.5 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.2–0.4 µg/kg), and rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg) to facilitate tracheal intubation. Maintenance was achieved using either total intravenous anesthesia with propofol and remifentanil, depending on individual patient factors, while ensuring bispectral index (BIS) monitoring to maintain adequate anesthetic depth (target BIS 40–60). Intraoperative monitoring included electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, end-tidal CO₂, invasive arterial pressure, and nasopharyngeal temperature. Normothermia, normocapnia, and hemodynamic stability were actively maintained. Hypotension (mean arterial pressure < 65 mmHg) was treated promptly with vasoactive agents such as phenylephrine or ephedrine.

The ACDF procedure was performed via a standard transverse anterior cervical approach by the same experienced surgical team. The procedure included discectomy, spinal cord decompression, interbody bone grafting, and internal fixation using titanium plates and screws. Careful hemostasis was achieved, and a closed-suction drain was placed before layered wound closure. The duration of surgery, anesthesia, and intraoperative events were recorded for analysis.

Information recording

Demographic information, gender, age, body mass index(BMI), ASA Grade). vital signs such as heart rate, blood pressure, temperature, pulse oximetry, depth of anesthesia, central venous pressure, intraoperative bleeding and urine output. Key indicators include cognitive function assessed at three points: one week post-surgery(T1), one month post-surgery (T2), and three months post-surgery(T3). The assessments are conducted using the MoCA, a tool with clear scoring criteria that evaluates multiple cognitive domains on a 30-point scale. Detailed scoring criteria are presented in Table 4.

Statistical analysis

This study employed appropriate statistical methods based on data types and distribution characteristics. Baseline characteristics of patients with different carotid IMT were compared using independent sample t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables, and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Group differences in postoperative cognitive function were analyzed using t-tests or non-parametric tests, while pre- and postoperative changes were evaluated with paired tests. Correlation analysis was conducted using Pearson or Spearman methods. Additionally, multivariate regression analysis was performed to adjust for confounding factors, and a significance level (α = 0.05) was set to assess statistical significance.

Result

A total of 100 patients were included, with 40 in the high CIMT group and 60 in the low CIMT group. Baseline demographic and perioperative characteristics are summarised in Table 1. There were no significant differences between groups in gender distribution, age, BMI, or ASA grade (all P > 0.05). Compared with the low CIMT group, patients in the high CIMT group had significantly lower preoperative MoCA scores (25.2 ± 2.1 vs. 26.5 ± 1.8, P = 0.03) and fewer years of education (9.5 ± 3.2 vs. 11.2 ± 2.8, P = 0.01). The prevalence of sleep disturbance, COPD history, and postoperative opioid use did not differ significantly between the two groups (all P > 0.05). These variables, together with other clinically relevant covariates, were subsequently included in multivariable analyses to adjust for potential confounding when evaluating the association between CIMT and POCD.As show in Table 1. After adjusting for confounding factors such as preoperative MoCA scores and education level, multivariate linear regression analysis revealed: the high IMT group had significantly lower MoCA scores at 1 week postoperatively compared to the low IMT group (β = −1.8, 95% CI: −2.5 to −1.1, P = 0.02), each 1-point increase in preoperative MoCA score was associated with a 0.6-point improvement in postoperative MoCA scores at 1 week (95% CI: 0.3 to 0.9, P = 0.01), each additional year of education was associated with a 0.4-point increase in postoperative MoCA scores at 1 week (95% CI: 0.1 to 0.7, P = 0.04).The comparison of basic vital signs between the two groups revealed no significant differences. As show in Table 2. The average heart rate was approximately 70 bpm in both groups, while blood pressure was around 120/65 mmHg. Body temperature remained consistent at approximately 36.1 °C. Both groups had a pulse oximetry value of 100%, and the depth of anesthesia was maintained between 40 and 55, averaging around 50. Intraoperative bleeding was approximately 300 mL, and urine output was around 400 mL, with no statistical differences observed between the two groups (all P > 0.05). At T1, the MoCA scores in the high IMT group were significantly lower than those in the low IMT group (P < 0.05). However, at T2 and T3, no significant differences in MoCA scores were observed between the two groups (P > 0.05). As show in Tables 3 and 4..

Limitation

Future investigations should address several limitations. These include increasing the sample size with balanced group allocation, extending follow-up to capture long-term cognitive outcomes, incorporating biomarker monitoring to elucidate underlying mechanisms, and controlling for additional confounding variables such as educational attainment and intraoperative medication use. Employing a broader battery of cognitive assessment tools would provide a more comprehensive evaluation, while inclusion of obesity-related indices beyond IMT and adoption of a multi-centre study design would enhance the robustness and generalizability of the findings. In addition, the lack of neuroimaging confirmation (e.g., MRI perfusion or diffusion-weighted imaging) constrains the ability to directly link cerebrovascular health to postoperative cognitive outcomes. Future studies should integrate such imaging modalities to strengthen mechanistic insights and improve clinical interpretability.

Discussion

In this study, a carotid IMT threshold of >0.9 mm was selected in accordance with established clinical guidelines from the European Society of Hypertension and the European Society of Cardiology, which recognize this value as indicative of subclinical atherosclerosis and increased vascular risk. This cutoff is further supported by recent high-quality studies demonstrating a significant association between elevated IMT and cognitive decline. The chosen threshold effectively identifies individuals at higher risk of cognitive impairment. Therefore, applying an IMT >0.9 mm cutoff enhances the clinical relevance and reproducibility of our findings [18, 19].

This research sought to assess the cognitive function disparities between patients categorized into high-IMT and low-IMT groups following surgical procedures. Findings revealed that, despite the similarity in baseline characteristics between the two groups, patients in the high-IMT category displayed significantly lower MoCA scores one week after surgery compared to their low-IMT counterparts. This outcome suggests a short-term temporary decline in cognitive abilities post-surgery. Nevertheless, the difference between the groups disappeared and was no longer statistically significant at one and three months after surgery, indicating an improvement in cognitive function over time for both groups. This outcome highlights the possible influence of IMT on cognitive function following surgery, especially during the initial postoperative phase [20, 21]. Elevated levels of IMT are commonly linked to increased abdominal adiposity and systemic inflammation. Visceral fat synthesizes inflammatory agents such as TNF-α, IL-6, and CRP, which could negatively affect cognitive function [22, 23]. These inflammatory substances may affect cerebral blood circulation, compromise the integrity of the blood-brain barrier [24], and trigger neuroinflammation, all contributing to cognitive deterioration. As a result, those categorized in the high IMT group may have undergone a more significant inflammatory reaction post-surgery, leading to short-term cognitive deficits [25]. Surgery induces a stress response in the body, characterized by elevated levels of stress hormones, including cortisol and catecholamines [26, 27]. While these hormones are essential for responding to physiological stress, chronic or excessive activation can negatively impact cognitive functions, particularly memory and learning. Patients with high IMT may exhibit a more pronounced stress response, resulting in a decline in cognitive function during the early postoperative period. However, cognitive function may improve over time as acute stress subsides [28]. Individuals with elevated IMT are at a greater risk of developing sleep disorders, such as obstructive sleep apnea, which can result in diminished sleep quality and increased daytime fatigue. Sleep disturbances can adversely affect cognitive function, particularly during the early postoperative period when patients are already facing the additional stress associated with surgery. While the high-IMT group may have encountered greater sleep disruption, it is probable that cognitive function will improve as their sleep patterns are restored.Obesity, particularly the accumulation of visceral fat, is frequently linked to cardiovascular risks, including hypertension, atherosclerosis, and endothelial dysfunction [29, 30]. These conditions can impair blood flow to the brain, heightening the risk of microvascular damage, which may result in cognitive difficulties. Consequently, the temporary cognitive decline observed in the high IMT group may be associated with reduced cerebral blood flow during the early postoperative period.This study offers a novel perspective on the relationship between IMT and cognitive function. While the results indicate that elevated IMT may be linked to short-term cognitive decline following surgery, the long-term implications remain ambiguous. This finding underscores the necessity of elucidating the mechanisms underlying cognitive dysfunction and suggests that enhancing metabolic factors could potentially improve cognitive outcomes in surgical patients. Future research should incorporate larger sample sizes, extended follow-up periods, and utilize advanced diagnostic methods such as neuroimaging to further investigate the relationship between IMT and cognitive function. Ultimately, these studies may lead to more targeted interventions for patients with elevated IMT, aimed at enhancing their postoperative metabolic and cognitive health [31].

Conclusion

An elevated carotid IMT is associated with an increased risk of POCD in patients with CSM, and its occurrence is influenced by multiple perioperative factors, including pre-existing cognitive impairment, sleep disturbance, intraoperative hyperglycaemia, COPD, lower educational attainment, and the type and frequency of postoperative analgesic use. Preoperative IMT assessment, in conjunction with comprehensive risk factor screening, may facilitate the identification of high-risk patients and inform the development of individualised perioperative management strategies. For such patients, targeted multimodal interventions aimed at preserving cerebral perfusion and cognitive function are recommended to reduce the incidence of POCD and enhance postoperative recovery.
