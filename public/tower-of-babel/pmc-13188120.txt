Reparative “exosome-ark” for mitochondrial transplantation to reprogram macrophages and disrupt pathogenic crosstalk in pulmonary fibrosis
Authors: Xin-Yue Zhang, Yi-Na Liu, Bo Wang, Chen-Guang Zhang, He-Qi Cheng, Xing-Rong Yang, Jin-Huan Wan, Xue-Cheng Sheng, Bin Guo, Hu-Lin Jiang, Xin Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pulmonary fibrosis (PF) progresses through a vicious cycle of crosstalk between injured alveolar epithelial cells II (AECs II) and alveolar macrophages. While mitochondrial transplantation offers a promising cure for macrophage metabolic dysfunction, the efficacy is hampered by poor targeting and rapid loss of mitochondrial integrity in vivo. Herein, we engineered a hierarchical strategy that integrates biomanufacturing, organelle protection and metabolic reprogramming. Initially, we utilized a “hijacking” strategy for manufacturing, where lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) delivering a mitochondrial-targeting sirtuin 3 plasmid (pMTS-SIRT3) rejuvenated injured AECs II, transforming them into factories for reparative exosomes. These harvested vesicles were then engineered into an “Exosome-Ark” by encapsulating healthy mitochondria. The pro-reparative intra-exosomal microenvironment functions as a cytoplasm-like milieu to maintain the biological activity of the isolated mitochondria, while mannose functionalization ensured macrophage-specific targeting. In bleomycin (BLM)-induced PF mice model, “exosomes-ark” restored macrophage mitochondrial homeostasis through enhanced fusion-fission dynamics and metabolic reprogramming, suppressed transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) expression, and attenuated myofibroblast activation. Mechanistically, exosomal reparative signals promoted macrophages for mitochondrial engraftment, revealing a synergistic effect beyond simple organelle replacement. This study presented a biologically inspired platform, offering a translational potential for treating fibrotic diseases driven by AECs II-immune cell crosstalk.

Introduction

Pulmonary fibrosis (PF) is driven by dysregulated crosstalk between injured alveolar epithelial cells II (AECs II) and macrophages [1,2]. The interaction establishes a pathological communication axis that promotes disease progression, which remains unaddressed by current therapies [3,4]. The approved antifibrotic agents target downstream fibrotic mediators but fail to interrupt the upstream intercellular signaling [5,6]. Consequently, these treatments provide only limited clinical benefit and are unable to halt or reverse established fibrosis [7,8]. Developing precision-targeted strategies that disrupt this pathogenic communication network represents an urgent need for achieving the therapeutic reversal of PF.

Injured AECs II occupy a central position in this pathological circuit, functioning as both the initiator and a sustained source of PF-promoting signals [9,10]. Mitochondrial dysfunction in injured AECs II, characterized by excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, impairs epithelial regeneration and drives profibrotic reprogramming [[11], [12], [13]]. Critically, injured AECs II selectively package profibrotic mediators, particularly transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), into secreted exosomes, thereby establishing a pathological communication channel that disseminates fibrotic signals throughout the lung microenvironment [14,15]. Thus, this exosome-mediated communication represents both the mechanism of PF progression and a potential therapeutic opportunity if redirected.

Pathological exosomes from injured AECs II trigger metabolic reprogramming in alveolar macrophages [[16], [17], [18]]. Sirtuin 3 (SIRT3), a mitochondrial NAD+-dependent deacetylase essential for maintaining oxidative phosphorylation and redox homeostasis, acts as a central metabolic central regulator [[19], [20], [21]]. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that expression of SIRT3 is significantly deficient in PF-associated macrophages [22,23]. The resulting metabolic shift promotes lipid accumulation, impairs mitochondrial bioenergetics, and drives polarization toward a profibrotic phenotype [[24], [25], [26]]. Consequently, these dysfunctional macrophages release additional profibrotic mediators, including platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) and connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), which further exacerbate AECs II injury and directly drive fibroblasts to differentiate into activated myofibroblasts, thereby completing and perpetuating the pathological fibrotic circuit [[27], [28], [29]]. Importantly, even if the source of pathological signaling (injured AECs II) is addressed, pre-existing macrophage dysfunction persists and continues to propagate fibrosis, suggesting that effective therapy requires dual intervention targeting both the source (injured AECs II) and the effector (macrophages) of this pathological circuit.

Therapeutic strategies targeting AECs II or macrophage alone have shown limited efficacy [30,31]. Mitochondrial transplantation has emerged as a promising approach for reprogramming dysfunctional macrophages, yet their clinical translation is hindered by functional delivery challenges [[32], [33], [34]]. Conventional mitochondrial delivery systems lack specificity for macrophages and fail to preserve mitochondrial integrity during ex vivo handling and in vivo transit, resulting in rapid degradation and poor functional engraftment [[35], [36], [37]]. Critically, existing approaches overlook the natural exosome-mediated communication between AECs II and macrophages in the diseased lung, a pathway that could provide an inherent solution to both targeting specificity and cargo protection challenges [38,39].

We hypothesized that the pathological exosome-mediated crosstalk between injured AECs II and macrophages provides a biological breakthrough point to resolve the critical limitations of mitochondrial delivery. Specifically, exosomes-derived from injured AECs II possess an intrinsic homing capacity for alveolar macrophages, providing a natural targeting mechanism [[40], [41], [42]]. Building on this, we hypothesized that exosomes derived from restored AECs II would retain the macrophage-targeting capacity while replacing profibrotic signals with a pro-regenerative microenvironment capable of preserving encapsulated mitochondria. In this study, we developed an “Exosome-Ark” platform for therapeutic mitochondrial delivery (Fig. 1). We employed lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) to deliver a plasmid co-encoding a mitochondrial-targeting peptide and SIRT3 (pMTS-SIRT3) to transfect human bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS-2B). The LNPs system was formulated based on our previous works and demonstrated superior transfection efficiency compared to commercial lipofectamine 3000 (Lipo3000) [43,44]. Exosomes harvested from pMTS-SIRT3-transfected BEAS-2B cells, which naturally targeted macrophage through the established AECs II-macrophage communication pathway while providing a pro-regenerative microenvironment that sustained mitochondrial viability. To synthesize the “Exosome-Ark”, the harvested exosomes were surface-functionalized with NHS-PEG2000-mannose, that was the MSE. This modification was specifically selected due to its compatibility with exosomal surface amine chemistry, the well-characterized affinity of mannose for macrophage mannose receptor (CD206), and the PEG2000 spacer that optimizes ligand accessibility. Subsequently, functional mitochondria isolated from healthy macrophages were encapsulated into the MSE using a mild sonication method, generating the final mannose-modified and mitochondria-loaded exosomes (MSE@Mito), named “Exosome-Ark”. In bleomycin (BLM)-induced PF mice, MSE@Mito efficiently targeted pulmonary macrophages, restored mitochondrial homeostasis through normalized fusion-fission dynamics and attenuated fibrotic progression. Our work presented a novel and transformative strategy for achieving therapeutic reversal of PF by harnessing endogenous intercellular communication mechanisms for PF reversal.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of MSE@Mito therapy. In summary, this study presents a hierarchically engineered “Exosome-Ark” platform (MSE@Mito) that simultaneously addresses the two principal drivers of PF, namely injured AECs II and dysfunctional macrophages. Initially, we employed LNPs delivering pMTS-SIRT3 to restore mitochondrial homeostasis in AECs Ⅱ, converting these cells into biofactories that produce reparative exosomes (MSE). The resulting MSE were subsequently loaded with healthy mitochondria and surface-functionalized with mannose to yield MSE@Mito. In BLM-induced PF mice, this platform successfully achieved macrophage-targeted mitochondria delivery and metabolic reprogramming. Our results demonstrate that this dual-targeted strategy produces synergistic antifibrotic efficacy, validating the therapeutic potential of co-targeting intercellular communication and organelle homeostasis in PF.

Cell culture

BEAS-2B cells (ATCC CRL-9609, human bronchial epithelial cell line), RAW264.7 cells (ATCC TIB-71, mouse macrophage cell line) and L929 cells (ATCC CCL-1, mouse fibroblast cell line) were obtained from Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). RAW264.7, L929 and BEAS-2B cells were cultured in DMEM medium containing 10% FBS. Cells were maintained in an incubation chamber at 37 °C with a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. BEAS-2B cells were utilized as an in vitro model for AECs II in this study, as they share key molecular markers with airway epithelium during BLM-induced PF [[45], [46], [47]].

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice aged 6-8 weeks were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., LTD. (Benxi, China). The animals were maintained in the Experimental Animal Center of Jinzhou Medical University under a 12/12 h light/dark cycle. The study involving animals was approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of Jinzhou Medical University, and the assigned approval number for the experimental investigator (Xin-Yue Zhang) was 240197-5.

Assessment of oxidative stress in injured and normal epithelial cells

BEAS-2B cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. The injured group was established by treating epithelial cells with TGF-β (20 ng/mL) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 200 μM) while untreated cells served as normal controls. Both groups were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C under 5% CO2. Subsequently, cells were loaded with the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe for 30 min at 37 °C. Intracellular ROS levels were assessed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) (Nikon AX, Nikon). In parallel, additional injured and normal epithelial cell models under identical conditions were incubated with the JC-1 probe for 30 min to evaluate mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP).

Transwell assays for evaluating cellular crosstalk

Cellular crosstalk between BEAS-2B and RAW264.7 cells was investigated using a transwell system. Specifically, BEAS-2B cells were pre-injured by exposure to TGF-β (20 ng/mL) and H2O2 (200 μM) for 24 h prior to co-culture to establish a stable injured AECs II model. Pre-injured BEAS-2B cells were placed in the upper chamber and co-cultured with RAW264.7 cells in the lower chamber for 24 h to allow sufficient paracrine exosome-mediated crosstalk, followed by a 48 h further incubation in exosome-depleted medium for macrophage phenotype assessment. For exosome-depleted FBS preparation, FBS was ultracentrifuged at 120,000×g for 2 h at 4 °C to pellet bovine exosomes. The supernatant was collected and filtered through 0.22 μm filters before use. After 48 h of culture, macrophages in the lower chamber were subjected to immunofluorescence staining to detect iNOS, CD206 and TGF-β, and were visualized using inverted microscopy. Similarly, crosstalk between RAW264.7 and L929 cells was examined. RAW264.7 cells pretreated with IL-4 (10 ng/mL) were seeded in the upper chamber at 8 × 104 cells per well, and L929 cells were pretreated with 200 μM H2O2 were cultured in the lower chamber at 1 × 105 cells per well. Following 24 h of co-incubation, fibroblasts in the lower compartment were stained for α-SMA and collagen Ⅰ and imaged under an inverted microscope.

In vitro immunofluorescence staining

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and maintained in an incubator with a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. The Cultured cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and subsequently washed three times with phosphate buffered saline (PBS). The cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies including anti-iNOS rabbit pAb (1:1500), anti-CD206 rabbit pAb (1:600), anti-TGF-β rabbit pAb (1:1000), Anti-α-SMA rabbit pAb (1:1000), anti-collagen I rabbit pAb (1:1000), and anti-SIRT3 rabbit pAb (1:300). After incubation, the cells were thoroughly washed three times (5 min per wash). Subsequently, Cy3 conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:400) or FITC conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:200) was applied and incubated with the cells at 37 °C for 2 h. Protein expression levels were finally visualized and documented using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Preparation of LNPs

Lecithin, cholesterol, DSPE-PEG-1K and DOTAP were dissolved at a molar ratio of 4:2:1:14 in a water-immiscible organic solvent to form a homogeneous LNPs. LNPs suspensions were prepared via the ethanol injection method. The resulting mixture was left uncovered under ambient conditions to allow complete evaporation of the ethanol. Subsequently, the product was homogenized into uniform LNPs using an ultrasonicator (VCX105, SONICS).

Construction of plasmid

The plasmid construct of pMTS-SIRT3 labeled GFP was engineered through the precise cloning of the MTS, SIRT3 and GFP inserts into the pcDNA3.1 (+) vector backbone, which is a widely used mammalian expression vector with a high copy number and constitutive expression. The construct utilizes the cytomegalovirus (CMV) as promoter, providing strong and constitutive expression in mammalian cells, including bronchial epithelial cells. The mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS) was ATGCTGTCTCTGAGACAGAGCATCCGGTTCTTCAAGAAGCACAAGCACAAGCACAAACATAAGCACAAGCACAAACACAAGCACAAGCAC. The size of the pMTS-SIRT3 construct was 6.6 kb. Plasmid synthesis service was provided by Public Protein/Plasmid Library (Nanjing, China).

Gene loading capacity of LNPs

The gene encapsulation efficiency and binding capacity of LNPs were evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis. Plasmid pMTS-SIRT3 and LNPs were complexed at a mass ratio of 1:20, followed by incubation at room temperature for 1-2 h to allow complete complexation. Electrophoresis was performed in 1 × TAE buffer (40 mM Tris-acetate, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) at a constant voltage of 100 V for 40 min. The gel was subsequently visualized and imaged using a gel imaging system (Tanon2500R, Shanghai). Naked plasmid DNA was used as a control.

Characterization and transfection efficiency of LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was performed using a Nicolet 6700 instrument in ATR mode over the range 400-4000 cm-1. 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectra were recorded on a Bruker AVANCE III 600 MHz spectrometer in DMSO. BEAS-2B cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 8 × 104 cells per well. LNPs were complexed with the plasmid pMTS-SIRT3-GFP at a mass ratio of 20:1 through incubation at room temperature for 2 h to form LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3-GFP complexes. After 4 h of transfection, the old medium was replaced with fresh medium supplemented with 10% FBS. Transfection efficiency was assessed using inverted fluorescence microscopy at 24 and 48 h, with commercial Lipo3000 employed as a positive control.

Stability evaluation of LNPs

The physicochemical stability of the prepared LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 was assessed over a 7-day period under different storage conditions, including 5% glucose, 0.9% NaCl and PBS. At predetermined time points (days 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7), samples were characterized for particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) with a Zetasizer Nano instrument (Malvern Panalytical, UK). Each measurement was performed in triplicate at 25 °C.

Cell viability of LNPs

BEAS-2B cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and cultured overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2. LNPs at various concentrations (40, 80, 160, 320, 640, 1280, 2560, 5120, 10240 and 20480 nM) were added to the wells and incubated with BEAS-2B cells for 24 h. Subsequently, 20 μL of MTT (5 mg/mL) was added and incubated with the cells for 4 h. Following the careful aspiration of the culture medium, each well was supplemented with 200 μL of DMSO solution. In vivo biodistribution of LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 in pulmonary epithelial cells

LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 complexes were administered to PF mice via intratracheal instillation (1 mg/kg) on day 10 post-BLM. At 8 h and 24 h post-administration, mice were euthanized. Lungs were harvested, embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Cryosections (10 μm thickness) were prepared using a cryostat (CRYOSTAR NX50). Sections were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, washed with PBS, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min, blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h and then immunostained with anti-F4/80 rat mAb and anti-SPC rabbit pAb (both 1:200) to identify macrophages and AECs II respectively. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. GFP signal (from pMTS-SIRT3-GFP plasmid expression), F4/80, SPC, and DAPI were visualized using an upright fluorescence microscope (NIKON ECLIPSE C1).

Extraction and storage of MSE

LNPs were incubated with the plasmid pMTS-SIRT3 at a mass ratio of 20:1 for 1-2 h at room temperature to allow for complex formation, yielding LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3. BEAS-2B cells were transfected with LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3, followed by a 48 h culture in DMEM supplemented with exosome-depleted FBS. The conditioned culture supernatants were subsequently collected for further analysis. The supernatant was subjected to a sequential centrifugation protocol to isolate exosomes. Initially, cellular components were removed by low-speed centrifugation at 400×g for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was further clarified by intermediate-speed centrifugation at 2000×g for 10 min to eliminate dead cells, followed by high-speed centrifugation at 10,000×g for 30 min to eliminate large particulate debris. The supernatant was then filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. Exosomes were pelleted via ultracentrifugation (Optima XPN-100, BECKMAN COULTER) at 120,000×g for 90 min. The supernatant was carefully aspirated and discarded, and the exosomes were resuspended in PBS. A second round of ultracentrifugation was performed under identical conditions (120,000×g, 90 min) to further purify the exosomes. For comparison, exosomes from injured AECs II (treated with TGF-β 20 ng/mL + H2O2 200 μM for 24 h, followed by 48 h culture in exosome-depleted medium) were isolated from the conditioned medium processed by sequential ultracentrifugation following the same protocol. After the second ultracentrifugation, isolated exosomes were incubated with NHS-PEG2000-mannose at a mass ratio of 4:3 (exosomes: mannose, based on protein content) in PBS (pH 8.0) overnight at 4 °C to facilitate NHS-ester conjugation to exosomal surface proteins. Following conjugation, the mixture was purified by ultracentrifugation (120,000×g for 90 min at 4 °C) to remove unconjugated mannose, yielding the MSE. EXO was isolated from untransfected BEAS-2B cells without SIRT3 restoration or mannose surface modification, serving as a control. The final MSE and EXO were resuspended in PBS and stored at −80 °C in an ultra-low temperature freezer (TDE40086FV-ULTS, Thermo Fisher) for long-term preservation.

Mitochondrial isolation

Mitochondria were isolated from RAW264.7 cells using a mitochondrial separation and protein extraction kit according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Preparation of MSE@Mito

MSE obtained via ultracentrifugation was combined with isolated mitochondria. MSE and Mito were combined at a mass ratio of 1:4. The mixture was subjected to mild intermittent sonication in a ice-water bath (5 s pulse on, 5 s pulse off) for a total of 5 min. Following sonication, the mixture was centrifuged at 2000×g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove free mitochondria and large aggregates. The supernatant containing MSE@Mito was then ultracentrifuged at 120,000×g for 90 min at 4 °C to pellet MSE@Mito. The pellet was washed twice with PBS by ultracentrifugation under the same conditions and resuspended in PBS. The final product, MSE@Mito (mannose-functionalized exosomes loaded with mitochondria), was used immediately or stored at −80 °C.

Characterization of MSE@Mito morphology

For MSE, Mito and MSE@Mito samples, freshly isolated samples were diluted to an appropriate concentration in PBS. A 10 μL aliquot of the suspension was deposited onto a carbon-coated copper grid (200 mesh) and allowed to adsorb for 1-2 min at room temperature. Excess liquid was gently removed by blotting with filter paper. The grid was then negatively stained with 2% uranyl acetate (or 2% phosphotungstic acid, pH 7.0) for 1-2 min. After removing excess staining solution by blotting, the grids were air-dried at room temperature. The morphology and size distribution of samples were examined.

Particle size and potential detection

LNPs, LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3, EXO, MSE, Mito and MSE@Mito were diluted in PBS and measured using a Zetasizer Nano instrument at 25 °C. Size distribution and surface charge were recorded. For nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), MSE samples were diluted 1:1000 in PBS and analyzed using a ZetaView instrument (Particle Metrix, Germany). Particle concentration and size distribution were determined from at least three independent measurements.

Western blot analysis

MSE and cell lysates were subjected to western blotting (WB) to determine the expression levels of the indicated proteins. For exosome samples, purified MSE was lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay, and equal amounts of exosomal protein (typically 20-30 μg) were loaded per lane. For cell lysates, cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and lysed in RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected. The following primary antibodies were used including anti-SIRT3 rabbit pAb (1:300), anti-CD81 rabbit pAb (1:800), anti-calnexin rabbit pAb (1:800), recombinant anti-TSG101 Mouse mAb (1:2000), and recombinant anti-HSP70 mouse mAb (1:2000). Anti-β actin rabbit pAb (1:2000) was employed as an internal loading control. Secondary antibody incubation was performed using HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:10000) and HRP-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG (1:10000). Membranes were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and imaged using a Servicebio gel imaging system.

Cellular uptake assay

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in confocal dishes at a density of 7 × 104 cells per dish and cultured for 24 h at 37 °C under 5% CO2 until reaching appropriate confluence. The cells were then labeled with DiO (cell membrane dye, 5 μM, 20 min at 37 °C) according to the manufacturer's instructions, followed by the addition of DiI (red fluorescent lipophilic dye, 5 μM, 30 min at 37 °C)-labeled EXO and MSE (20 μg/mL). Subsequently, free dyes were removed via ultracentrifugation. After incubation for 1 h and 4 h at 37 °C, cells were washed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and imaged using CLSM (Leica TCS SP5II).

Targeting specificity of MSE from different sources

For cell pretreatment, RAW264.7 cells stimulated with IL-4 (10 ng/mL) were seeded in confocal dishes at a density of 7 × 104 cells per dish. BEAS-2B cells were seeded in confocal dishes at a density of 5 × 104 cells per dish and pretreated with TGF-β (20 ng/mL) and H2O2 (200 μM). L929 cells were seeded in confocal dishes at a density of 5 × 104 cells per dish and pretreated with H2O2 (200 μM). For MSE extraction and labeling, MSE were extracted from both injured and normal AECs II following the described above. MSE were labeled with PKH26 according to the manufacturer's instructions and free dye was subsequently removed by ultracentrifugation. Cells were labeled with DiO (cell membrane dye, 5 μM) for 20 min. PKH26-labeled MSE (20 μg/mL) were then added to the cells and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. After incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and imaged.

Shotgun proteomics for MSE analysis

Proteins were affinity-purified and eluted from immuno-precipitation beads using SDT lysis buffer via thermal and ultrasonic treatment. Digestion was performed following a filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) protocol with trypsin overnight at 37 °C. Peptides were desalted using C18 StageTips prior to LC-MS/MS analysis. Chromatographic separation was carried out on a self-packed C18 column with a 60 min acetonitrile gradient under reverse-phase conditions using an Easy nLC 1200 system coupled to a Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). MS data were acquired in a data-dependent top 20 mode with HCD fragmentation. Database searching was performed with MaxQuant (v.1.6.0.16) against the UniProtKB Homo sapiens database, applying carbamidomethylation as a fixed modification, and acetylation and oxidation as variable modifications, under strict 1% false discovery rate (FDR) control at both the peptide-spectrum-matched (PSM) level and protein levels.

Animal model establishment and treatment

The BLM-induced PF mice model was established via intratracheal administration of BLM (5 mg/kg). On day 10 post-instillation, selected models received therapeutic interventions every three days for a total of seven administrations (2 mg/kg) and the dosage of 1 mg/kg was administered via LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 delivery via intratracheal administration. During the intratracheal administration period, animals were anesthetized using tribromoethanol (0.25 mg/g). Body weight was monitored from the first treatment day until sacrifice. On day 31 post-BLM (day 21 post-treatment initiation), mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under anesthesia. Lung, heart, liver, spleen, and kidneys tissues were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological analysis.

H&E and Masson's trichrome staining

Freshly harvested tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by dehydration, embedding and sectioning. Sections were stained with H&E, and subsequently dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. For Masson's trichrome staining, serial sections prepared under identical processing conditions were stained with Masson's composite dye solution, followed by dehydration, clearing, and final mounting. The degree of PF was quantified using the Ashcroft scoring system based on H&E and Masson's trichrome-stained sections. Briefly, five randomly selected fields per section were scored on a scale of 0 to 8. A score of 0 represents normal lung tissue, while a score of 8 indicates total fibrotic obliteration of the field. The final Ashcroft score of each sample was calculated as the mean score of all evaluated fields [48,49].

In vivo immunofluorescence staining

Lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated through graded ethanol series, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene (2 × 10 min), rehydrated through graded ethanol (100%, 95%, 85%, 75%, 5 min each), and rinsed in distilled water. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating sections in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95-100 °C for 20 min, followed by cooling to room temperature. After washing with PBS, sections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min and blocked with 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C including anti-CD86 rabbit pAb (1:2000), anti-NOX4 Rabbit pAb (1:2000), anti-CD206 Rabbit pAb (1:2000), anti-SIRT3 rabbit pAb (1:2000), anti-α-SMA rabbit pAb (1:200), anti-SPC rabbit pAb (1:500), anti-caspase-3 rabbit pAb (1:2000) and anti-p53 mouse mAb (1:400). After washing 3 times with PBS (5 min each), sections were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cy3 or FITC, 1:400) for 2 h at 37 °C. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL, 5 min). Sections were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium and imaged using fluorescence microscopy.

The proliferation of macrophages

Following euthanasia, lung tissues were harvested, minced into 1-2 mm3 pieces, and digested in DMEM containing collagenase I (1 mg/mL) and DNase I (0.1 mg/mL) for 30 min at 37 °C with gentle agitation. The digested tissue was mechanically dissociated by passing through a 70 μm cell strainer using a syringe plunger. The cell suspension was centrifuged at 400×g for 5 min at 4 °C. Red blood cells were lysed with lysis buffer for 3 min on ice, followed by washing with PBS. Single-cell suspensions (1 × 106 cells) were resuspended in 100 μL PBS + 2% FBS. Cells were stained with the following antibody cocktail for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark: PerCP/cyanine5.5 anti-mouse CD45 (2.5 μL), APC anti-mouse/human CD11b (2.5 μL), PE anti-mouse F4/80 (2.5 μL), and brilliant violet 605TM anti-mouse CD68 (2.5 μL). After staining, cells were washed twice with PBS, resuspended in 300 μL PBS, and filtered through 40 μm cell strainer into flow cytometry tubes. Data acquisition was performed using a FACSCelesta flow cytometer (Becton, Dickinson and Company) with at least 10,000 events recorded per sample. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Colocalization analysis of MSE@Mito

To verify the successful assembly of MSE@Mito and evaluate the colocalization efficiency, fluorescent labeling was performed on both components prior to complexation. MSE were labeled with the DiI (5 μM, 30 min) at 37 °C in the dark. Unbound dye was subsequently removed by ultracentrifugation at 120,000×g for 90 min at 4 °C. The DiI-labeled MSE (DiI-MSE) pellet was washed twice with PBS under identical centrifugation conditions and resuspended in fresh PBS for further use. Isolated Mito were labeled with MitoTracker Green (200 nM, 30 min) at 37 °C in the dark. Excess dye was removed by centrifugation at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The labeled Mito were washed twice with mitochondria isolation buffer under the same centrifugation conditions to ensure complete removal of free dye. Subsequently, DiI-labeled MSE and MitoTracker Green-labeled Mito were assembled to form fluorescently dual-labeled MSE@Mito complexes following the aforementioned synthesis protocol. The colocalization of DiI-MSE and MitoTracker Green-labeled Mito within the MSE@Mito complex was examined using CLSM. The dual-labeled MSE@Mito samples were mounted onto glass slides and imaged using a confocal microscope equipped with appropriate filter sets. DiI fluorescence was excited at 549 nm and detected at 565 nm (red channel), while MitoTracker Green was excited at 490 nm and detected at 516 nm (green channel). Colocalization efficiency was further quantified by flow cytometry. The dual-labeled MSE@Mito samples were analyzed using a flow cytometer equipped with 488 nm and 561 nm lasers. MitoTracker Green fluorescence was detected using the FITC channel (530/30 nm bandpass filter), while DiI fluorescence was collected through the PE channel (585/42 nm bandpass filter). Single-labeled DiI-MSE and MitoTracker Green-mitochondria were used as controls to establish compensation parameters and gating strategies. A minimum of 10,000 events were recorded per sample. The percentage of double-positive events (DiI+/MitoTracker Green+) was calculated to determine the colocalization efficiency. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo software.

Mitochondrial targeting and fusion assay

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in confocal dishes at a density of 7 × 104 cells per dish and cultured for 12 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cells were then stimulated with IL-4 (10 ng/mL) for an additional 12 h to induce M2 polarization. Endogenous mitochondria were labeled with PK Mito Orange (200 nM) for 30 min at 37 °C according to the manufacturer's instructions, then washed three times with PBS. Exogenous Mito (within MSE@Mito) were pre-labeled with MitoTracker™ Green FM (200 nM, Thermo Fisher) for 30 min at 37 °C, washed twice by centrifugation (12,000×g, 15 min). MitoTrackerTM Green-labeled MSE@Mito (20 μg/mL) were added to PK Mito Orange-stained macrophages. Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and maintained in live-cell imaging solution. Mitochondrial dynamics and fusion between exogenous (green) and endogenous (red) mitochondria were monitored in real-time using Multi-SIM (Nano Insights).

mRNA expression of mitochondrial fission proteins

Macrophages were divided into three experimental groups. One group remained untreated, while two groups were stimulated with IL-4 (10 ng/mL). Among the IL-4-treated groups, one received additional treatment with MSE@Mito, and was subsequently re-stimulated with IL-4 (denoted as MSE@MitoIL−4) to establish an inflammatory microenvironment. RNA from all three groups was extracted, reverse-transcribed, and subjected to quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis using a CFX Connect system. Glycolytic metabolism analysis

To evaluate the modulation of glycolytic metabolism in polarized macrophages, RAW264.7 cells were first polarized in vitro: M1 polarization was induced by stimulation with LPS (1 μg/mL) for 24 h, whereas M2 polarization was induced by pretreatment with IL-4 (10 ng/mL) for 24 h. The M2-polarized macrophages were then seeded into appropriate culture vessels and treated with Mito, MSE, EXO@Mito, or MSE@Mito at an equivalent dose of 20 μg/mL for subsequent experimental procedures. Untreated M1 and M2-polarized macrophages were used as controls, and blank wells were included in line with the instructions provided by each assay kit. Following treatment, the intracellular expression levels of PDK1 and glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) were quantified by ELISA according to the manufacturer's instructions; intracellular lactate content was measured using a 96-well microplate, and its concentration was calculated according to the formula provided by the Adsbio lactic acid content assay kit; the ECAR and OCR were determined with the corresponding BestBio kits on a fluorescence microplate reader, using cells seeded in clear-bottom, black-walled 96-well plates. In vivo biodistribution

In vivo cellular uptake of MSE@Mito in PF mice

Dysregulated AECs II-macrophage crosstalk drives PF through exosome-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction

To investigate the initiating events and pathologic mechanism of PF, an in vitro injury model was established using BEAS-2B cells stimulated with H2O2 and a minimal concentration of TGF-β to facilitate a profibrotic microenvironment (Fig. 2A). BEAS-2B cells-derived extracellular vesicles had been well-validated as functional mediators of epithelium-macrophage crosstalk in pulmonary pathophysiology, and the fundamental mechanisms of exosome-mediated intercellular communication were highly conserved between human and mouse cells [50,51]. This injury model successfully reproduced major profibrotic features, with quantitative analysis indicating a significant increase of IL-1β and TGF-β under injury conditions via ELISA indicating a significant increase in IL-1β and TGF-β secretion under injury conditions (Fig. S1A and B). As the contribution of injured AECs II to PF pathogenesis is well-established, we focused our investigation on the underlying mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in PF, specifically examining the status of SIRT3, a master regulator of mitochondrial antioxidant defenses. Consistent with our previous hypothesis, we found that SIRT3 expression was substantially compromised in injured AECs II (Fig. 2B). In addition, this SIRT3 reduction was functionally consequential, leading to a concomitant reduction in SOD activity and intracellular GSH levels (Fig. 2C and D). Consequently, the defective antioxidant response resulted in substantial ROS accumulation, as evidenced by elevated DCFH-DA fluorescent intensity (Fig. S1C). Furthermore, assessment of MMP using mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit (JC-1) imaging showed a decreased red/green fluorescence ratio in injured AECs II by CLSM, confirming mitochondrial depolarization and supporting ROS-driven oxidative stress (Fig. S1D). We next investigated whether oxidative stress in injured AECs II could drive dysfunctional intercellular crosstalk. A transwell co-culture system was established with BEAS-2B cells in the upper chamber and RAW264.7 macrophages in the lower chamber (Fig. 2E). The results demonstrated that injured AECs II induced a dominant profibrotic polarization shift in macrophages, significantly upregulating the expression of the M2 marker of mannose receptor (CD206), concurrent with the downregulation of M1 markers such as iNOS, IL-6 and IFN-γ (Fig. 2F–I). To identify the mechanism underlying this crosstalk between injured AECs II and macrophages, we focused on exosomes as critical nanovesicles for intercellular communications. Immunofluorescent staining revealed elevated intracellular TGF-β levels in the co-cultured macrophages, corroborating the transfer of profibrotic signals (Fig. 2J). To conclusively establish that this crosstalk is specifically mediated by EXO rather than soluble factors, EXO was isolated from injured and normal AECs II and applied directly to macrophages. ELISA analysis demonstrated that EXO derived from injured AECs II were significantly enriched with profibrotic and inflammatory mediators compared to those from normal AECs II. Consequently, these injured AECs II-derived EXO alone were sufficient to drive macrophages toward a profibrotic phenotype, downregulating iNOS while promoting fibrotic activation (Fig. S1E–H). In addition, isolation and ELISA analysis of EXO derived from the fibrotic co-culture system revealed the significant enrichment of TGF-β cargo in exosomes from injured AECs II (Fig. S1I). Collectively, these findings established a pathological mechanism in PF wherein injured AECs II characterized by SIRT3 deficiency and mitochondrial dysfunction packaged profibrotic cargo into EXO. Subsequently, these EXO directly reprogrammed macrophages toward a profibrotic M2 phenotype, promoting PF progression. The AECs II-exosome-macrophage axis thus formed a self-amplifying circuit. LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 treats PF via alleviating oxidative stress of AECs II

Having identified the AECs II-exosome-macrophage axis as the central pathological circuit driving PF, we further found that SIRT3 deficiency in AECs II served as the initiating molecular event inducing mitochondrial dysfunction and the release of pathological exosomes. We therefore hypothesized that restoring SIRT3 expression could inhibit the fibrotic cascade at its source. To achieve targeted and efficient gene delivery to AECs II, we engineered lipid nanoparticles loaded with a mitochondria-targeted SIRT3 plasmid (denoted as LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3) (Fig. 3A). Dynamic light scattering analysis demonstrated that both LNPs and LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 exhibited uniform particle sizes, which was critical for achieving efficient pulmonary deposition and cellular internalization (Fig. 3B). The positive zeta potential suggested that LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 possessed favorable capacity for effective interaction with negatively charged cell membranes (Fig. 3C). Agarose gel electrophoresis confirmed nearly 100% encapsulation efficiency of pMTS-SIRT3 within the LNP carriers (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, FTIR spectroscopy and 1H NMR were performed for the LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3. The FTIR spectra of blank LNPs and LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 exhibited the characteristic absorption peaks corresponding to the relevant chemical bonds (Fig. S2A). Additionally, the 1H NMR spectra displayed consistent proton signals associated with the constituent lipid components, verifying the structural consistency of the formulated LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 (Fig. S2B). The transfection efficiency of the LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 was subsequently evaluated, demonstrating high expression of target proteins via immunofluorescent staining and WB (Fig. 3E–S2C and D). To validate the mitochondrial targeting capability of the pMTS sequence, a subcellular colocalization assay was performed. Following transfection, the green fluorescence of the expressed pMTS-SIRT3 highly colocalized with mitochondria labeled by PK Mito Orange, confirming the precise delivery and accumulation of SIRT3 within the mitochondria (Fig. S2E). Furthermore, LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 maintained physicochemical stability under diverse physiological conditions and showed minimal cytotoxicity in MTT assays (Fig. 3F and G). To test whether targeting the “source” of pathological signaling could interrupt the fibrotic cascade, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of AECs II metabolic restoration alone in vivo. LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 was administered via intratracheal instillation to a BLM-induced PF mice model (Fig. 3H). Histopathological analysis demonstrated that LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 treatment effectively mitigated PF progression, as evidenced by suppressed inflammatory cell infiltration in H&E staining and diminished collagen deposition in Masson staining (Fig. 3I). Quantitative Ashcroft scoring revealed that LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 significantly reduced the severity of fibrosis (Fig. S2F). No statistically significant differences in body weight were observed between groups, further indicating the favorable biocompatibility of the treatment (Fig. 3J). A critical consideration for AECs II-targeted gene therapy was the specificity of transgene expression the co-localization of LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3-GFP expression with SPC-positive cells revealed limited plasmid expression at 8 h post administration, but robust localization within AECs II at 24 h, confirming tropism toward injured AECs II (Fig. 3K). Moreover, H&E staining showed no pathological alterations in major organs (heart, liver, spleen, kidneys) of LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3-treated mice, indicating systemic biocompatibility (Fig. S2G).

Mechanistically, LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 treatment upregulated SIRT3 expression in lung tissues, thereby attenuating oxidative stress, restoring mitochondrial function, and ameliorating the pulmonary redox imbalance (Fig. 3L). As a mitochondrial deacetylase, SIRT3 had been extensively documented to regulate cellular metabolism and oxidative stress responses. To identify the therapeutic mechanism of LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3, multiple indicators related to oxidative stress and mitochondrial function were assessed, including GSH, SOD and ATP. The results showed that the levels of GSH and SOD were significantly elevated following LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 treatment, indicating the restoration of redox homeostasis in fibrotic lung tissues (Fig. 3M and N). Additionally, increased ATP content suggested an improvement in mitochondrial bioenergetics (Fig. 3O). The restoration of cellular antioxidant capacity fundamentally contributed to the protection of AECs II damage. Concurrently, the expression of SPC, a key proliferative marker for AECs II, was reduced in the BLM group compared to normal controls, indicating impaired epithelial regeneration during PF pathogenesis. Critically, LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 treatment reversed this deficiency, promoting AECs II survival and regeneration (Fig. 3P).

The pathogenesis of PF involved complex intercellular crosstalk between injured AECs II and macrophages. And macrophages polarization played a crucial role in determining disease progression. Multiplex immunofluorescence analysis revealed that LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 restored the balance of macrophage polarization, as quantified by the populations of M1 macrophage marker CD86 and CD206 subpopulations (Fig. 3Q). The concomitant attenuation of fibrogenesis was confirmed by a reduced density of α-SMA myofibroblast activation (Fig. 3R). Although HYP content decreased post-treatment, the lack of significant intergroup differences suggested that LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 modulated PF primarily through earlier upstream pathways rather than direct collagen degradation (Fig. 3S). Marked downregulation of pro-fibrogenic mediator including VEGF, FGF, TGF-β, and IL-1β further corroborated the inherent antifibrotic activity of LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 (Fig. 3T–W).

Collectively, our data reveal that LNP@pMTS-SIRT3 effectively remediates mitochondrial dysfunction in injured pulmonary epithelium. This metabolic rescue normalizes redox homeostasis, drives epithelial restitution, and endows secreted exosomes with a homeostatic-regulating capacity, thereby reprogramming macrophage polarization and ultimately attenuating fibrogenesis. Importantly, this metabolic rejuvenation transformed the cargo of AECs II-derived exosomes, providing a rationale for potential use as therapeutic delivery vehicles. These results demonstrated that LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 effectively restored SIRT3 expression and mitochondrial function in AECs II, thereby attenuating oxidative stress and reversing PF. Given that injured AECs II exerted the profibrotic effects through exosome-mediated communication with macrophages, we hypothesized that the reparative exosomes derived from SIRT3-restored AECs II could be harvested as natural vehicles carrying functional cargo to directly target and modulate dysfunctional macrophages.

In addition, cellular uptake studies showed that MSE displayed higher mitochondrial-targeting efficiency in macrophages (RAW264.7) compared to EXO, with optimal targeting efficacy observed at 6 h by CLSM (Fig. 4H and S3F). Transfection with LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 significantly enhanced SIRT3 protein expression in BEAS-2B cells which was highly enriched in MSE (Fig. 4I). In addition, mass spectrometry of MSE revealed an enrichment of SIRT3 pathway components, including SIRT3 substrates and mitochondrial homeostasis-related proteins, supporting the antioxidant capacity of MSE (Fig. 4J). Since MMP can be used to monitor mitochondrial biological activity, the change of MMP was monitored by JC-1 staining to assess mitochondrial function. JC-1 monomers exhibited green fluorescence when MMP was low, while JC-1 formed red fluorescent aggregated in healthy mitochondria with high MMP and green fluorescent monomers upon depolarization. In contrast to the IL-4 induced metabolic dysregulation group and the EXO treatment group, MSE treatment effectively enhanced red fluorescence, indicating the recovery of MMP and the restoration of mitochondrial function in macrophages (RAW264.7) (Fig. 4K).

Given the crucial role of SIRT3 in regulating mitochondrial metabolism and cellular homeostasis, we next investigated the therapeutic potential of SIRT3-enriched MSE in reprogramming macrophage homeostasis. In PF, the over-activation of M2 macrophages drove continuous fibrotic progression. ELISA assay demonstrated that MSE treatment rescued the suppressed expression of iNOS, restoring its levels to approximate those of normal macrophages (Fig. 4L). The normalization indicated that rather than triggering excessive inflammation, MSE effectively restored the macrophages to a healthy state. Concurrently, MSE treatment significantly downregulated the expressions of metabolic dysfunction indicators, including IL-13 and Arg-1, in IL-4-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages (Fig. 4M and N). Immunofluorescent staining displayed reduced CD206 expression (indicative of profibrotic M2 phenotype) and recovered iNOS expression, indicating a shift in macrophage polarization toward a M1 phenotype further and supporting these results (Fig. 4O and S3G). To further clarify the functional distinction between LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 and MSE, we performed direct co-culture and transwell experiments on RAW264.7 macrophages, with polarization assessed by immunofluorescence staining of iNOS and CD206 (Fig. S3H and I). These results demonstrated that MSE, rather than LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3, effectively reprogrammed metabolic reprogramming in macrophages (RAW264.7), facilitating the restoration of mitochondrial function and promoting a shift toward a normal balanced state via efficient intercellular cargo delivery.

MSE partially restores macrophage function but shows limited efficacy in mitochondrial rescue

All mice exhibited body weights within the normal range (20 to 30 g) at the endpoint of the study, suggesting no adverse systemic effects (Fig. 5D). We next assessed the ability of MSE to modulate oxidative stress in vivo. The level of antioxidant indicators including SOD and GSH were determined by ELISA, showing a significantly increase in the MSE treatment group compared to the EXO group (Fig. 5E and F). Concurrently, the content of HIF-1α exhibited no significant increasing trend following MSE administration (Fig. 5G). To explore the effect of MSE on the inflammatory response activation, the infiltration of macrophages was analyzed by flow cytometry. The results demonstrated that post-MSE treatment, the level of macrophage accumulation was comparable to that in the normal group (Fig. 5H and S4B). In addition, the expression of NOX4, which played a crucial role in the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate hydrogen (NADPH) oxidase family, was detected by immunofluorescence staining. The result showed a decreasing trend of NOX4 in the MSE group, which was consistent with the restoration of GSH and SOD (Fig. 5I). Additionally, MSE administration significantly decreased the profibrotic cytokine TGF-β (Fig. 5J). In summary, these findings indicated that MSE effectively alleviated PF by simultaneously enhancing antioxidant defenses, suppressing pro-inflammatory and profibrotic pathways, and restoring redox homeostasis.

However, the EXO group showed limited significant improvement in SOD activity, GSH levels, HIF-1α, NOX4 and TGF-β compared to the BLM group, indicating that untargeted EXO alone were insufficient to rescue mitochondrial function under severe oxidative stress conditions. These results suggested that exosome-mediated molecular signaling might be insufficient to fully rescue macrophages with severe mitochondrial damage. Given that SIRT3 functions as a mitochondrial deacetylase governing metabolic homeostasis, we hypothesized that direct mitochondrial supplementation might be required to completely restore metabolic function in dysfunctional macrophages.

Engineering MSE for efficient mitochondrial delivery through development of the “exosome-ark” platform

The therapeutic efficacy of exosomes from SIRT3-restored AECs II validated the concept of reprogramming intercellular communication for antifibrotic therapy. However, the results also revealed a limitation that macrophages with established mitochondrial damage showed incomplete functional recovery following MSE treatment. While SIRT3 restoration and exosome-mediated signaling represented regulatory approaches that modulated cellular function through molecular signals, macrophages with severe mitochondrial depletion might require direct organelle replacement to restore metabolic competence. Building upon this rationale, we developed a biomimetic mitochondrial delivery system by encapsulating healthy mitochondria within MSE (denoted as MSE@Mito), named the “Exosome-Ark”. To develop MSE@Mito (mitochondria was isolated from RAW264.7 cells), we evaluated three encapsulation methods: ultrasonication, extrusion, and combined ultrasonication-extrusion by quantifying ATP levels. Ultrasonication best preserved mitochondrial bioenergetic integrity, maintaining ATP concentrations comparable to untreated mitochondria, and was therefore selected for subsequent MSE@Mito production (Fig. S5A). The particle sizes of both Mito and MSE@Mito were 500-1500 nm, with negative zeta potentials observed for each (Fig. 6A). TEM images confirmed the structural integrity of isolated mitochondria, displaying characteristic double membranes and cristae, and demonstrated their successful encapsulation by MSE (Fig. 6B). Fluorescence co-localization further validated the efficient encapsulation of MSE@Mito by flow cytometry and CLSM (Fig. 6C and D). In addition, we also detected oxidative stress markers to evaluate whether MSE could protect mitochondria from oxidative damage. As depicted in Fig. 6E, MSE@Mito was designed to target and supply cells with exogenous mitochondria, demonstrating excellent uptake efficiency, mitochondrial-targeting capability and underwent fusion with endogenous mitochondria within macrophages (Fig. 6F and S5E, Video S1). Multicolor structured illumination microscopy (Multi-SIM) demonstrated that MSE@Mito delivery to IL-4-polarized macrophages induced significant mitochondrial elongation (Fig. 6G and H). qPCR analysis showed that MSE@Mito treatment modulated the expression of mitochondrial fission regulators, including dynamin-1-like protein (Dnm1l), fission 1 protein (Fis1), and mitochondrial fission factor (Mff), suggesting the reactivation of mitochondrial dynamics and functional recovery (Fig. 6I). Studies have indicated that oxidative stress promoted M2 macrophage polarization and profibrotic responses. To determine the consequences of MSE@Mito fusion, an MMP assay was performed in M2 macrophages to evaluate the oxidative stress level after treatment. The results showed an increased MMP in the mitochondria of M2 macrophages (Fig. 6J). Cell viability assays revealed that the abnormal proliferation of M2 macrophages decreased in response to Mito, MSE and MSE@Mito in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 6K).

Macrophage polarization was closely linked to metabolic reprogramming, where M2 phenotypes typically rely on oxidative phosphorylation. The uptake assay was assessed to verify the MSE@Mito-mediated macrophage polarization balance. Enhanced glucose uptake following MSE@Mito treatment indicated a shift toward glycolytic metabolism, characteristic of M1 macrophages (Fig. 6L). The recovered expression of iNOS further supported this metabolic shift (Fig. 6M). Furthermore, ELISA analysis revealed an obvious upregulation of PDK1 and lactate acid expression, whereas GLUT1 expression remained unchanged after treatment with MSE@Mito, confirming that MSE@Mito induced glycolytic reprogramming via a PDK1-mediated pathway rather than GLUT1 modulation (Fig. S6A–C). To further confirm the metabolic rewiring, we measured the ECAR and OCR to evaluate glycolytic and oxidative metabolism, respectively. MSE@Mito treatment significantly increased the ECAR while decreasing the OCR in IL-4-induced M2 macrophages (Fig. S6D and E). Notably, the ECAR of MSE@Mito-treated macrophages was lower than that of LPS-induced highly inflammatory M1 macrophages, and the OCR remained higher than that of M1 macrophages, demonstrating that MSE@Mito corrected the aberrant metabolic dependency of M2 macrophages and restored metabolic homeostasis without causing excessive inflammation. Moreover, the increased content of SOD and GSH also implied that reprogrammed macrophages treated with MSE@Mito exhibited better redox homeostasis compared to those treated with free Mito, illustrating that MSE had exceptional capabilities in protecting and repairing mitochondria (Fig. 6N).

To further verify the antioxidant capacity of MSE protection, mitochondria was encapsulated in MSE derived from either normal or injured AECs II and the levels of SOD, GSH and ATP were detected in IL-4-induced macrophages. The results showed increased SOD, GSH and ATP in normal MSE@Mito-treated macrophages compared to the injured MSE@Mito group, exhibiting stronger antioxidant capacity (Fig. S6F–H). Furthermore, a transwell co-culture model was employed to evaluate the antifibrotic effect of interrupting the crosstalk between M2 macrophages and myofibroblasts (Fig. 6O). We found that macrophages treated with MSE@Mito produced a conditioned medium that significantly downregulated α-SMA expression and collagen I in co-cultured myofibroblast activation (Fig. 6P and S6I). Additionally, the content of TGF-β in the culture medium was also decreased, indicating the disruption of profibrotic pathways (Fig. 6Q). These results indicated that MSE@Mito restored mitochondrial and redox homeostasis, reprogrammed macrophage polarization toward a healthy balanced state, and disrupted pathogenic crosstalk between macrophages and myofibroblasts, thereby attenuating profibrotic pathways and collagen deposition.

Synergistic therapeutic effects of dual-targeted intervention in PF

To evaluate whether the dual-targeted strategy yields synergistic effects, we compared MSE@Mito with individual interventions in the BLM-induced PF mice model. The therapeutic efficacy of MSE@Mito against PF was evaluated in BLM-induced PF mice model via tracheal administration (Fig. 7A). Subsequently, Mito and MSE@Mito were administered to mice intratracheally. In vivo imaging was detected at different time points, which revealed that DiI-labeled MSE@Mito predominantly accumulated in lung tissue 72 h post-injection (Fig. 7B). In addition, immunofluorescence analysis revealed that DiI-labeled MSE@Mito mainly colocalized with F4/80-positive macrophages, whereas little was observed in AECs II and myofibroblasts. There results indicated that MSE@Mito selectively targeted macrophages in PF lung tissues, likely due to their inherent phagocytic capacity (Fig. 7C). Histopathological assays demonstrated that MSE@Mito treatment significantly attenuated PF progression, manifested by inhibiting inflammatory cell infiltration in H&E staining and reducing collagen deposition in Masson staining, with superior effects compared to Mito or MSE groups (Fig. 7D). The content of HYP was reduced, while there were no significant differences among the treatment groups, suggesting that MSE may primarily ameliorate PF through other pathways (Fig. 7E). No statistically significant differences in body weight changes were observed among treatment groups, indicating excellent biocompatibility (Fig. S7B). Analysis of white blood cells, lymphocytes, monocytes and neutrophils showed that Mito, MSE, and MSE@Mito formulations significantly reduced inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. S7C–F). Molecular analysis of the lung tissues further corroborated the mechanistic pathways observed in vivo. Quantitative post-treatment evaluation revealed significant attenuation of fibrotic severity, as evidenced by elevated expression of the anti-fibrotic cytokine IFN-γ concomitant with reduced expression of profibrotic mediators of TNF-α, IL-6, TGF-β (Fig. 7F–I). Upregulation of SIRT3 implied that MSE@Mito could significantly suppress oxidative stress and improve mitochondrial function to alleviate oxidative stress in lung tissues (Fig. 7J). Mitochondrial morphology in epithelial cells was examined following treatment of MSE@Mito via TEM, further confirming that MSE@Mito could effectively restore mitochondrial to improve mitochondrial function, showing more distinct cristae morphology than BLM group (Fig. 7K). Metabolic analysis demonstrated that MSE@Mito induced a shift toward glycolytic metabolism, a hallmark of M1 macrophage polarization, as evidenced by upregulation of PDK1, Gln and ACA (Fig. 7L–N). Furthermore, the proliferative capacity of AECs II, which represented the regenerative capacity of the injured lung tissue, was assessed via immunofluorescent staining, revealing reduced co-localization of SPC with caspase-3, indicating attenuation of apoptosis and promotion of regenerative proliferation (Fig. 7O). Additionally, reduced co-expression of the fibrotic marker α-SMA and tumor suppressor p53 signified diminished senescent myofibroblasts accumulation, supporting the potent anti-fibrotic and anti-inflammatory effects of MSE@Mito. In summary, MSE@Mito platform enabled efficient mitochondrial delivery and integration in vivo, leading to restored mitochondrial function, modulated oxidative stress, reprogrammed macrophage metabolism, attenuated AECs II apoptosis, and ultimately, significant amelioration of PF. Notably, the therapeutic efficacy of MSE@Mito significantly exceeded the theoretical additive effect of individual interventions, demonstrating true synergy.

Discussion

PF remains a fatal lung disease with limited therapeutic options, largely due to the complex pathogenesis involving dysregulated intercellular crosstalk [52,53]. In this study, we developed a therapeutic platform that repurposes pathological intercellular communication for organelle-based therapy in PF. Our study exploits the intrinsic properties of the AECs II-macrophage exosomal axis to sustain the viability of encapsulated mitochondria. By loading functional mitochondria into these reparative exosomes and enabling specific macrophage targeting via mannose surface modification, we developed an “Exosome-Ark” platform that achieved efficient mitochondrial delivery, overcoming targeting deficiencies and cargo degradation that limited conventional approaches [54,55].

Our data confirmed that MSE@Mito ameliorated PF-associated markers, as evidenced by the inhibition of injured AECs II apoptosis, suppression of myofibroblasts activation, reduced ECM deposition, and restoration of redox homeostasis. The upregulation of antioxidant capacity and modulation of mitochondrial dynamics further validated the involvement of SIRT3-mediated deacetylation pathways and enhanced mitochondrial bioenergetics in the observed therapeutic effects.

MSE derived from SIRT3-restored AECs II exerted significant reparative effects. Compared with exosomes isolated from injured AECs II, MSE carried an enriched cargo of pro-regenerative biomolecules including SIRT3 protein, antioxidant enzymes, and mitochondria-supporting metabolites that collectively attenuated macrophage oxidative stress, suppressed pro-inflammatory polarization, and partially restored MMP. These findings indicated that correcting upstream pathological signal by restoring AECs II health could directly reduce downstream macrophage dysfunction. Therefore, targeting the source of intercellular communication through the LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 intervention could serve as a promising strategy in early-stage PF. Although LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 partially reduced fibrotic markers and Ashcroft scores, its efficacy was moderate compared to MSE@Mito. LNPs@pMTS-SIRT3 acts as upstream of signal correctors in AECs II. This SIRT3-mediated upstream intervention is insufficient to fully rescue downstream macrophages with severe mitochondrial collapse. In contrast, MSE@Mito achieves better antifibrotic outcomes by integrating reparative exosomal signaling with direct mitochondrial transplantation, directly resolving the energy deficit.

MSE monotherapy attenuated oxidative stress and partially normalized macrophage polarization, yet was insufficient to rescue macrophages with severe mitochondrial damage. In contrast, MSE@Mito significantly restored mitochondrial morphology, biogenesis, and metabolic function in these severely damaged macrophages due to the combined action of reparative exosomal contents and direct mitochondria transplantation. Compared with either MSE or Mito alone, the dual-action strategy was more effective in suppressing TGF-β and pro-fibrotic cytokines, inhibiting myofibroblast activation, and reducing collagen deposition. Mechanistically, MSE maintained the survival of transferred mitochondria and enhanced their cellular uptake, while the transferred mitochondria directly repaired the energy deficit that exosomal signals alone could not solve. Together, these two components acted on different stages of the AECs II-macrophage-myofibroblasts pathological axis.

From a translational perspective, MSE@Mito demonstrated significant potential for long-term application in PF. The use of reparative exosomes minimized immunogenic risks, a critical requirement for the chronic dosing needed in progressive fibrotic diseases. Additionally, the intratracheal delivery route aligned with established clinical protocols for inhaled pulmonary therapeutics, enabling localized high dose delivery while avoiding systemic off-target effects. The mannose-functionalized surface enabled macrophage-specific targeting, while the exosomal microenvironment prevented ex vivo mitochondrial deterioration. Future studies would focus on developing standard production protocols to advance MSE@Mito toward clinical application for PF and other fibrotic diseases.

Conclusion

Our work established a transformative strategy that leveraged biomimetic engineering to restore mitochondrial and intercellular homeostasis for PF treatment. To address the limitations of mitochondria delivery in vivo, we developed a generalizable platform which orchestrated a self-sustaining cycle of repair across intercellular communication for treating PF and other diseases characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction and aberrant cellular communication, offering a comprehensive and potentially curative strategy for treating fibrotic diseases.
