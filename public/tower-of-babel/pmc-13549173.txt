Psychometric Evaluation of the Persian Version of a Self-Care Evaluation Instrument to Prevent Diabetic Foot in Iranian Patients Predominantly with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (P-ISPDF)
Authors: Elaheh Ghods, Razieh Bandari, Ali Montazeri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo translate and psychometrically validate the Persian version of the Instrument for Self-care in Preventing Diabetic Foot (P-ISPDF) as a reliable, culturally appropriate tool for assessing foot-specific self-care behaviors among Persian-speaking adults, predominantly those with type 2 diabetes mellitus.MethodsThe ISPDF was translated using a forward–backward procedure, followed by expert review (n=5) and cognitive debriefing with patients (n=10). A split-sample design was used: exploratory factor analysis (EFA; n=289) and a series of confirmatory factor analyses (CFAs), including first-order, second-order, and a hierarchical general-factor model in an independent sample (n=250). Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1–5). Reliability (Cronbach’s alpha, McDonald’s omega, intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC]) and validity (EFA, CFA) were evaluated using SPSS 25, AMOS 24, and JASP.ResultsEFA supported a three-factor structure: self-care maintenance (10 items), monitoring (9 items), and management (7 items). The hierarchical measurement model showed good fit (χ2/df=2.80, CFI=0.974, TLI=0.954, RMSEA=0.060). Cronbach’s alpha was 0.93 for the total scale, with subscale alphas ranging from 0.81 to 0.92. Test–retest reliability was excellent (two-way mixed-effects, absolute agreement ICCs 0.93–0.98 with 95% CIs reported).ConclusionThe P-ISPDF demonstrated strong reliability and preliminary structural validity. However, due to mixed evidence regarding convergent validity and the absence of external validation, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Further psychometric refinement and longitudinal studies are needed to establish its clinical utility.Keywords: self-care, diabetic foot, psychometrics, validation, measurement instrument, inflammation

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a common and fast-growing metabolic disorder.1 It affects approximately 10.5% of adults worldwide.2 The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) estimated that 537 million adults had diabetes mellitus in 2021, costing $966 billion globally. This cost may surpass $1054 billion by 2045.2,3 Type 2 diabetes mellitus involves chronic low-grade inflammation, which drives vascular complications and impairs wound healing. This inflammation also delays healing and complicates diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs).4

This condition is associated with severe micro- and macrovascular complications. These include cardiovascular disease, retinopathy, neuropathy, and nephropathy. Among these, diabetic peripheral neuropathy affects about one-third of patients. It predisposes them to foot deformities, calluses, fissures, and loss of pain sensation. This can ultimately lead to diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs).5

Diabetic Foot Ulcer (DFU) is a serious and costly complication of diabetes mellitus. It places a substantial burden on patients and healthcare systems. About one in four individuals with diabetes mellitus will develop a DFU during their lifetime. Of these, 85% of cases ultimately result in lower limb amputation.6 The five-year mortality rate after DFU development is approximately 40%.7 Despite these alarming statistics, proper self-care practices can greatly reduce the risk of diabetic foot ulcers and amputation. Preventing DFUs is thus a critical public health priority.8

Accurate assessment of self-care behaviors is crucial for preventing diabetic foot ulcers, but available tools are limited. Most self-care assessment tools focus on overall diabetes management, not specifically on foot care.9–25 Some instruments include foot care subdomains, but usually with only a few questions. They generally do not fully assess behaviors related to early detection of foot abnormalities and timely intervention.26

The Self-Care of Diabetes Inventory (SCODI) is commonly used to assess general self-care in diabetes mellitus; however, its foot care component is relatively brief.24 Furthermore, while other foot-specific tools, such as the Nottingham Assessment of Functional Footcare (NAFF),25 focus primarily on the functional and practical aspects of foot care, the Instrument of Self-care for the Prevention of Diabetic Foot (ISPDF) offers a conceptually distinct approach. Developed in 2022 by Herrera Guerra and colleagues in Colombia,27 the ISPDF is grounded in the middle-range theory of self-care of chronic illness and consists of 26 items specifically designed to emphasize the dimensions of maintenance, monitoring, and management. By focusing on these three specific domains, the ISPDF provides a different theoretical lens compared to broader instruments (like SCODI) or other foot-focused measures (like NAFF). However, because direct comparisons of measurement performance across these instruments were beyond the scope of the present study, these distinctions should be interpreted merely as variations in content coverage and theoretical emphasis rather than evidence of psychometric or clinical superiority.

In Iran, culturally validated and comprehensive foot-specific self-care tools are limited. Adapting and validating the Persian version of ISPDF may offer a reliable and culturally appropriate way to assess self-care behaviors related to DFU prevention among Persian-speaking patients. This study aimed to translate and validate the Persian version of the ISPDF.

Methods

Self-Care Evaluation Instrument to Prevent Diabetic Foot (ISPDF)

In 2022, Herrera Guerra et al designed a self-care assessment tool for preventing diabetic foot in people with DM2. This scale is made with 26 items with a five-point Likert format, which is never equal to 1 and always equal to 5. It was determined according to three dimensions for evaluation. The first part is the maintenance of self-care (10 items). The second part is monitoring self-care (8 items without symptoms and item 9 without symptoms). Item 9 has six answer options: I have not had symptoms, score zero, and very quickly is equal to 5. The third part is management self-care (7 items). This study was conducted on 230 people with type 2 diabetes. Construct validity was determined through exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis (RMSEA= 0.071–0.32). Internal consistency was calculated with Cronbach’s alpha coefficient (0.73–0.97).27

Translation Process

This questionnaire translation process was done forward–backward to achieve equivalence between the original and the translated versions.28 After obtaining permission from the instrument developer, two independent translators, both bilingual in English and Persian (one native Persian speaker with medical terminology expertise), translated the English version into Persian. Discrepancies were resolved via research-team consensus focusing on semantic, idiomatic, and cultural equivalence. The reconciled Persian version was then back-translated into English by two other translators (one native English speaker fluent in Persian and one bilingual expert). The final reconciled Persian version preserved conceptual fidelity to the original.

Face Validity

To evaluate qualitative face validity, the instrument was provided to 10 diabetic patients at the Semnan diabetes unit to check clarity of instructions, items, and response format. Patients read items aloud, identified ambiguities, and suggested alternatives if needed. No wording changes were required based on cognitive debriefing; two minor comprehension queries were clarified with examples without altering item text.

Content Validity

For qualitative content validity, five specialists (infectious disease, internal medicine, endocrinology/diabetes/metabolism, orthopedic surgery, and nursing) reviewed the Persian version for writing and content. No modifications were needed as the original phrasing retained conceptual equivalence.29,30

Participants and the Study Setting

This cross-sectional study (October 2023–September 2024) enrolled 539 adults with type 1 or 2 diabetes via convenience sampling at the Semnan diabetes unit and randomly split them into EFA (n=289) and CFA (n=250) subsamples. Inclusion: age ≥18, willingness to participate, Persian fluency, registered at the center. Exclusion: gestational diabetes, untreated active DFU, cognitive problems, or inability to communicate.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using three dedicated software platforms, with specific roles assigned to each to maintain analytical precision:

IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 25.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA): Executed data management, descriptive statistics, baseline demographic comparisons, the random 50/50 sample split procedure (n = 289 for EFA and n = 250 for CFA), and test–retest reliability via Intraclass Correlation Coefficients (ICC; two-way mixed-effects model, absolute agreement).

JASP (Version 0.97.0, JASP Team, Amsterdam, Netherlands): Conducted primary psychometric evaluations suited for ordinal Likert-type data. Specifically, JASP was used to compute polychoric correlation matrices, conduct Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA; Promax rotation, Parallel Analysis), perform primary Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) using the robust Weighted Least Squares Means and Variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimator, and calculate Cronbach’s alpha, McDonald’s omega, AVE, CR, and HTMT matrices.

IBM SPSS AMOS (Version 24.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA): Utilized specifically for specifying, estimating, and comparing complex structural higher-order and hierarchical hybrid models.

Construct Validity

The construct validity of this scale was assessed using two methods: exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). The dataset of 539 Iranian adults was randomly split into two groups using SPSS (version 25). The first group (n = 289) was used for EFA, and the second (n = 250) for CFA. Because the data were Likert-type, ordinal statistical methods were used, and polychoric correlations were calculated for factor analysis. EFA used the WLSMV estimator and Promax rotation, which allows for correlated factors. CFA was also conducted using the WLSMV estimator in JASP 0.97.0. To test whether our results were stable, we compared the WLSMV and maximum-likelihood (ML) estimators; factor structure and fit indices were similar for both, supporting our findings. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were then used to assess the suitability of the data for factor analysis. A significant Bartlett’s test of sphericity (p < 0.01) indicated sufficient correlations among the items for factor analysis. KMO values above 0.90 were considered excellent. The number of factors was determined using Parallel Analysis and inspection of the scree plot (Figure 1). This approach aligns with current recommendations for rigorous psychometric evaluation. It allows the factor structure identified in EFA to be cross-validated in an independent sample through CFA. This process minimizes overfitting and improves generalizability. Such separation is particularly important in cross-cultural adaptation studies because it strengthens confidence in the stability of the identified dimensions.31,32

Scree plots for exploratory factor analysis.

To further validate the factor structure identified through maximum likelihood exploratory factor analysis (MLEFA), Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted using the second randomly selected dataset (n = 250) in AMOS version 27. Model fit was evaluated using multiple fit indices: Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Normed Fit Index (NFI), and Incremental Fit Index (IFI) > 0.90; Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) < 0.08; and Minimum Discrepancy Function divided by degrees of freedom (CMIN/DF) < 3, indicating a good model fit.33

Step 1: First-order CFAs (domain-level). We specified three separate CFAs to examine local fit for each domain: (a) Maintenance, consisting of three correlated first-order factors, F1 (q1–q3), F2 (q4–q7), and F3 (q8–q10); (b) Monitoring, represented as a single first-order factor (q11–q19); and (c) Management, comprising two correlated first-order factors, F1 (q23–q26) and F2 (q20–q22). Cross-loadings were fixed to zero. Residual covariances were freed only within factors and only when strong conceptual or wording overlap existed between items. A limited number of correlated residuals were introduced based on theoretical justification rather than modification indices alone.

Step 2: Second-order CFAs (Maintenance and Management). For Maintenance, a second-order factor was specified to load on F1–F3. After introducing the second-order factor, covariances among F1–F3 were fixed to zero, allowing the shared variance to be captured by the higher-order factor. For Management, a second-order factor was specified to load on F1 and F2. Because only two first-order indicators yield minimal identification at this level, the model was scaled by fixing one higher-order loading to 1.0, and the residual covariance between F1 and F2 was set to zero; this limitation is reported.

Step 3: Hierarchical (third-order/hybrid) model for the full instrument. Finally, we estimated a hierarchical hybrid model with a general factor (ISPDF) loading on the three domains: Maintenance (second-order), Monitoring (first-order), and Management (second-order). After introducing ISPDF, covariances among the three domains were fixed to zero. Because Monitoring is directly measured by items, the final model is a hierarchical hybrid rather than a pure third-order model.

Convergent Validity

Convergent validity assesses whether indicators of the same latent construct share substantial variance. In CFA, it is supported when: (a) standardized factor loadings are ≥ 0.50 (preferably ≥ 0.70) and statistically significant; (b) composite reliability (CR) ≥ 0.70, indicating acceptable internal consistency; and (c) average variance extracted (AVE) ≥ 0.50, meaning the construct explains at least half of the variance of its indicators.34

Convergent and Discriminant Validity

In order to assess convergent and discriminant validity, the framework proposed by Fornell and Larcker (1981) was initially utilized. Specifically, three key metrics were calculated: composite reliability (CR), average variance extracted (AVE), and maximum shared squared variance (MSV). Convergent validity was established based on standard threshold criteria: CR > 0.70, AVE > 0.50, and CR > AVE.35

However, acknowledging that the Fornell–Larcker criterion and cross-loading analyses often fail to reliably detect discriminant validity issues in empirical scenarios (Henseler et al, 2015), discriminant validity was primarily evaluated using the Heterotrait-Monotrait ratio of correlations (HTMT) matrix.35,36 Monte Carlo simulations confirm that the HTMT approach yields superior sensitivity and specificity compared to traditional methods.37 Accordingly, a standard cutoff threshold of 0.85 (and up to 0.90 for conceptually adjacent dimensions) was adopted, where values below this threshold confirm distinct theoretical constructs.38

Reliability

Internal Consistency

The internal consistency of the ISPDF was evaluated by calculating Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega coefficients for the overall questionnaire and its factors. A threshold of 0.70 or higher was used to determine acceptable reliability, consistent with standard criteria.39

Test-Retest Reliability

To examine test-retest reliability, a subset of 30 participants was asked to complete the questionnaire on two separate occasions, with a two-week interval between administrations chosen to minimize recall bias while allowing for stability assessment in chronic conditions like diabetes. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was utilized to assess the consistency of responses over this period, using a two-way mixed-effects model. An ICC value of 0.80 or higher was considered indicative of satisfactory reliability, ensuring the instrument’s stability over time.40

Exploratory Factor Analysis

Maintenance of Self-Care

The scale used consisted of 10 items and three factors: the KMO value (sampling adequacy index) for this dimension was 0.887, and the Bartlett test (with chi-square value =2044.325 and degrees of freedom = 45) was statistically significant (p = 0.001). In exploratory factor analysis (EFA), the eigenvalues for the mentioned factors were 5.05, 2.13, and 2.04, respectively, which explained 70.63% of the total variance related to self-care maintenance (Table 2). High loadings on Factor 3 (0.994 and 0.907) prompted examination of potential redundancy, raising questions about the distinct measurement value of each item. Although the items target related but distinct behaviors (washing and moisturizing), this suggests that future studies should assess whether each item provides unique and necessary information within the measurement model. The reliability of these three factors based on Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.894, 0.879, and 0.833, respectively. Also, McDonald’s Omega Coefficients were calculated for these factors as 0.869, 0.882, and 0.861, respectively. The overall reliability of this factor based on Cronbach’s alpha was 0.92. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for this factor was also 0.98 (with a 95% confidence interval: 0.96 to 0.99; p= 0.001) (Table 3).

Monitoring of Self-Care

This nine-item dimension consisted of one factor. In this regard, the KMO (Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin Measure of Sampling Adequacy) was estimated at 0.787, and Bartlett’s Test (Chi-square =1039.861, degrees of freedom = 36) was statistically significant (p = 0.001). In Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA), the one factor had eigenvalues of 3.09, collectively accounting for 51.98% of the total variance in self-care monitoring (Table 2). The reliability of this one factor, based on Cronbach’s alpha coefficients, was 0.810. Additionally, McDonald’s Omega coefficients for these factors were 0.821. The Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) for this factor was 0.97 (95% CI: 0.93–0.99; p = 0.001) (Table 3).

Management of Self-Care

This seven-item dimension consisted of two factors. In this regard, the KMO (Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin Measure of Sampling Adequacy) was estimated at 0.848, and Bartlett’s Test (Chi-square =719.607, degrees of freedom = 21) was statistically significant (p = 0.001). In Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA), the two factors had eigenvalues of 2.87 and 2.49, collectively accounting for 53.92% of the total variance in self-care management (Table 2). The reliability of these two factors, based on Cronbach’s alpha coefficients, was 0.846 and 0.842, respectively. Additionally, McDonald’s Omega coefficients for these factors were 0.872 and 0.874, respectively. The overall reliability of this dimension was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.840. The Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) for this factor was 0.93 (95% CI: 0.84–0.97; p = 0.001) (Table 3).

Confirmation Factor Analysis (CFA)

First-Order CFAs (Domain-Level)

Maintenance was modeled as three correlated first-order factors. The model demonstrated acceptable fit: χ2 = 12.54, χ2/df = 1.14, CFI = 0.94, TLI = 0.91, IFI = 0.94, NFI = 0.92, RMSEA = 0.06 (Table 3). To account for strong semantic and conceptual overlap, measurement errors were correlated for items 6 and 7 (e4 to e5), as both items specifically reflect protective behaviors against external environmental and physical hazards (footwear and temperature). Similarly, errors for items 9 and 10 (e8 to e9) were correlated due to their nearly identical conceptual focus on general medical adherence and treatment compliance, rather than foot-specific care. The final Monitoring factor retained six items (q11–q14, q17, q18). Based on theoretical justification, measurement errors were correlated for items 11 and 12 (e37 to e38) due to their shared focus on general daily vigilance and observation. Convergent and Discriminant Validity

Composite reliability (CR) was 0.923 for Factor 1, 0.825 for Factor 2, and 0.813 for Factor 3, all exceeding the 0.70 benchmark. Regarding convergent validity, the AVE for Factor 1 was 0.548 (>0.50), supporting convergent validity. In contrast, the AVEs for Factors 2 and 3 were 0.465 and 0.443, respectively, falling below the 0.50 threshold and indicating limited convergent validity for these factors. Furthermore, the HTMT results showed that all pairwise HTMT values were below the chosen threshold (eg, <0.90), indicating adequate discriminant validity (Table 5). These patterns may reflect content overlap across factors and cultural nuances in item interpretation. Overall, while the instrument appears practically useful, the affected subscales warrant item refinement and further validation.

Discussion

This study translated and psychometrically evaluated the Persian version of the instrument for self-care in preventing diabetic foot (P-ISPDF). Using a split-sample design (EFA, n = 289; CFA, n = 250), we confirmed the original three-domain structure: maintenance, monitoring, and management. A hierarchical specification provided a good overall fit (χ2/df = 2.80, CFI = 0.974, TLI = 0.954, RMSEA = 0.060). Internal consistency for the total score and subscales was high (α=0.81–0.92; ω=0.82–0.94).

The Cronbach’s alpha and omega coefficients demonstrated strong internal consistency across all domains, with values approaching or exceeding 0.90 (eg, total scale alpha = 0.93; Maintenance domain alpha = 0.92). While high internal consistency coefficients confirm robust inter-item cohesion, values of this magnitude should not be interpreted solely as evidence of superior reliability; they may also signal item redundancy, conceptual duplication, or overly tight wording similarities within subscales. In this initial adaptation, the full item pool was retained to preserve structural parity with the original instrument. However, our psychometric analyses identified substantial conceptual and statistical overlap between specific pairs of items, such as items 9 and 10 (both assessing general medical adherence) as well as items 23 and 25 (both addressing immediate professional medical consultation). Highlighting these specific redundancies provides a concrete empirical foundation for future psychometric studies utilizing Item Response Theory (IRT) or Rasch analysis to develop a more parsimonious, abbreviated short-form version of the P-ISPDF. Such an abbreviated scale would optimize administrative efficiency and reduce respondent burden without compromising measurement adequacy. Furthermore, test–retest reliability demonstrated excellent stability over time (ICC = 0.93–0.98), supporting the temporal reproducibility and consistency of the instrument scores.

The primary objective of the present study was to evaluate the psychometric properties and factor structure of the Persian ISPDF rather than to assert the superiority of any specific complex measurement model. Therefore, the higher-order and hierarchical analyses should be interpreted strictly as supplementary theoretical exploration regarding score structure, rather than as the principal clinical contribution of the study.

First-order domain-level CFAs demonstrated acceptable to good fit, confirming that the practical three-domain structure (Maintenance, Monitoring, and Management) represents the most direct and interpretable model for clinical and research applications. While second-order and hierarchical hybrid models achieved comparable goodness-of-fit statistics, statistical equivalence between alternative models does not imply superior conceptual clarity or practical utility. These higher-order specifications simply demonstrate that domain covariances can be mathematically modeled in multiple ways. Consequently, the simpler correlated three-factor model remains the most parsimonious and clinically actionable representation, while hierarchical models should be viewed merely as plausible structural alternatives. Future studies should evaluate whether the added complexity of higher-order modeling offers any meaningful practical advantages in real-world application. Our findings align with the original Colombian validation, which proposed three domains with good reliability.27 However, the very high reliability coefficients observed for some domains may also indicate substantial overlap among conceptually related items, a possibility that warrants further investigation in future studies.

Broader self-care instruments devote minimal content to foot care.24 The P-ISPDF includes items addressing several specific aspects of diabetic foot self-care, including inspection, hygiene, drying, moisturizing, nail care, footwear practices, hazard avoidance, and help-seeking behaviors. While these content areas represent a different emphasis compared to other available instruments, such as the Nottingham Assessment of Functional Footcare (NAFF),25 no head-to-head psychometric comparisons were conducted in the present study. Therefore, differences between instruments should be interpreted purely as variations in theoretical focus and content coverage rather than evidence of clinical or psychometric superiority. While the P-ISPDF offers a conceptually distinct approach to measuring self-care behaviors in Iranian clinical settings, future comparative studies are required to directly evaluate its psychometric performance, responsiveness, and clinical utility relative to other tools before any conclusions regarding practical advantages can be established.

Composite reliability exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.70 across all domains, and pairwise HTMT values provided empirical support for acceptable discriminant validity. However, the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values for the Monitoring (0.465) and Management (0.443) domains remained below the 0.50 benchmark. This indicates that observed indicators accounted for less than half of the variance in their respective latent constructs, representing a key psychometric limitation and reflecting limited convergent validity within these subscales.

While this finding may partially stem from the clinical continuum of diabetic foot self-care—where monitoring behaviors directly inform active management actions—it cannot be attributed solely to behavioral overlap or item wording similarities. Methodological and theoretical factors must be critically evaluated. Specifically, sub-threshold AVE values strongly point toward potential underlying weaknesses in construct definition, insufficient theoretical differentiation between domain boundaries, item redundancy, or minor model misspecification. Consequently, interpretations of the Monitoring and Management constructs must be made with appropriate caution. Future psychometric investigations across diverse clinical samples should prioritize refining individual items, removing redundant indicators, and re-specifying domain boundaries to enhance variance explanation and measurement precision.

This finding partially reflects the clinical continuum of diabetic foot self-care, where monitoring behaviors (eg, inspecting the feet) directly inform and transition into active management actions (eg, applying moisturizers or seeking medical care). Beyond this inherent behavioral overlap and similarities in item wording, methodological factors must also be considered. As detailed in the limitations, sub-threshold AVE values may point to partial item redundancy, subtle domain boundary blurring, or minor model misspecification in these specific subscales. Future psychometric investigations across diverse clinical samples should focus on refining and re-evaluating individual items within the Monitoring and Management domains to enhance the instrument’s overall variance explanation and measurement precision.

Clinical and Research Implications

The P-ISPDF provides a standardized and psychometrically robust method for evaluating foot-specific self-care behaviors among Persian-speaking adults with diabetes mellitus in research settings. The availability of domain-specific scores has the potential to assist clinicians and researchers in identifying specific areas of self-care that may warrant targeted educational support.

However, because no implementation research, feasibility testing, or formal usability evaluations were conducted in the present work, clinical capabilities such as risk stratification, guiding targeted therapies, or integrating the tool into routine patient workflows remain strictly potential applications rather than demonstrated effectiveness. Furthermore, the present study did not evaluate predictive validity, diagnostic cut-off scores, or longitudinal responsiveness to change. Therefore, the instrument’s ability to identify high-risk patients, monitor intervention-related progress over time, or directly support clinical decision-making remains to be established. Most importantly, given the sub-threshold Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values observed for the Monitoring (AVE = 0.465) and Management (AVE = 0.443) domains, domain-specific clinical decision-making or individual patient profiling based on these particular subscales should be strictly avoided until further item refinement and construct validation are conducted.

To maximize the practical and clinical utility of the P-ISPDF, future longitudinal and interventional research should prioritize evaluating its responsiveness to clinical change and educational interventions over time. Additionally, establishing multigroup measurement invariance across sex, age brackets, literacy levels, and diabetes types (T1DM vs T2DM) is essential to ensure unbiased score comparisons across diverse patient populations in healthcare settings. Future research should also employ probability-based multicenter designs to enhance broader generalizability, conduct dedicated usability testing to confirm practical feasibility in clinical environments, and determine whether P-ISPDF scores prospectively predict critical diabetic foot outcomes such as ulceration, healing rates, or amputations.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths of this study include a rigorous forward–backward translation process with expert and patient input, a large sample split independently for EFA and CFA, explicit testing of higher-order structures, and comprehensive reliability evidence, including intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC).

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, participants were recruited via convenience sampling from a single specialized diabetes center in Semnan, Iran. This design limits the geographic, cultural, and healthcare diversity of the sample and restricts the generalizability of our findings to broader Persian-speaking populations. Future multicenter studies using probability-based sampling are recommended to evaluate the external validity and stability of the P-ISPDF across culturally and geographically distinct regions.

Second, face validity assessment was conducted with a relatively small sample of 10 patients recruited from a single center, which may not fully capture the complete range of educational, literacy, and cultural diversity within the target population. Although cognitive debriefing revealed no major wording flaws and only required minor clarification via examples, engaging a larger and more socio-demographically diverse sample in future studies could further refine item interpretations across varying literacy levels.

Third, our sample consisted overwhelmingly of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (95.2%). Furthermore, we did not perform multigroup measurement invariance testing across key demographic and clinical subgroups (eg, sex, age, literacy levels, or diabetes type). Consequently, the equivalence of the construct across these groups remains unverified, and mean-level score comparisons between demographic subgroups should be interpreted with caution until measurement invariance is formally established in future studies.

Fourth, our evidence of construct validity remains largely internal. Convergent validity was assessed through internal parameters (AVE, CR, MSV) without incorporating external criterion instruments, such as established diabetes self-care or foot adherence measures, nor did we perform criterion validation. Additionally, while robust estimators appropriate for likert-type ordinal data (eg, WLSMV) were prioritized, any supplementary factor analyses utilizing maximum likelihood estimation represent a methodological limitation and must be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, convergent validity was not supported for the Monitoring and Management domains, as evidenced by AVE values below the recommended 0.50 threshold. While this may partly reflect conceptual overlap among self-care behaviors, it also signals potential psychometric vulnerabilities, such as weak underlying construct definitions, poorly differentiated domain boundaries, item redundancy, or model misspecification. Therefore, interpretations of these specific subscales must remain cautious, and extensive item refinement and construct re-evaluation are required before these subscales can be recommended for individual clinical application.

Fifth, because of the cross-sectional study design, we did not evaluate predictive validity, longitudinal responsiveness to clinical interventions, or associations with objective clinical outcomes such as foot ulceration, healing rates, hospitalizations, or amputations. Therefore, current findings demonstrate the baseline structural and measurement quality of the P-ISPDF rather than its clinical effectiveness or prognostic utility.

Sixth, all data were self-reported and subject to recall or social desirability bias. Specifically, diabetic foot self-care behaviors are highly susceptible to socially desirable responding and over-reporting of adherence, which could have inflated the observed scores and reliability estimates. Furthermore, internal consistency values approaching or exceeding 0.90 suggest that certain subscales contain redundant or conceptually overlapping items. Future studies should apply advanced item-level modeling (such as Item Response Theory or Rasch analysis) to examine individual item functioning and evaluate whether a more parsimonious, short-form version of the scale could achieve equivalent measurement precision while significantly reducing participant response burden.

Seventh, the hierarchical and hybrid models presented here should be viewed strictly as plausible mathematical representations of the covariance structure rather than clinically or practically superior solutions; future research is needed to determine whether their additional statistical complexity provides any tangible practical benefits over simpler, more parsimonious correlated-factor models.

Eighth, the test-retest reliability was evaluated in a relatively small sample of 30 participants. Although an n = 30 sample size is common for preliminary stability evaluations in psychometric studies, it provides less precise Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) estimates with wider confidence intervals and may not fully capture stability across diverse patient subgroups. Future longitudinal studies employing larger and more socio-demographically diverse retest samples are warranted to confirm the stability of the P-ISPDF over extended time intervals.

Conclusion

The Persian adaptation of the Individualized Self-Care Practices for Diabetes Foot (P-ISPDF) addresses an important clinical gap by providing a standardized instrument for evaluating foot self-care behaviors among Persian-speaking adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus in research contexts. Overall, the evaluation supports a practical three-domain factor structure with satisfactory internal consistency and excellent temporal stability. While higher-order and hierarchical modeling demonstrated statistical plausibility, the simpler correlated three-domain structure provides the primary foundation for practical interpretation and clinical utility.

However, current validity evidence for the P-ISPDF must be considered strictly preliminary and largely internal. Sub-threshold Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values in the Monitoring and Management domains reflect limited convergent validity, highlighting potential issues with construct definition, domain differentiation, or item redundancy. Combined with the absence of external criterion validation, unexamined measurement invariance, and a lack of responsiveness assessment, these findings indicate a clear imperative for further psychometric refinement. Consequently, while current evidence supports using the P-ISPDF as a baseline measurement tool in aggregate research settings, individual clinical profiling, risk stratification, or outcome prediction based on subscale scores should be strictly avoided at this stage.

Future multicenter, longitudinal, and interventional studies using probability sampling are needed to test multigroup measurement invariance, evaluate whether higher-order structural representations offer any practical added value over first-order domain scoring, establish diagnostic cut-off scores, and assess responsiveness to change. Furthermore, prospective investigations should determine whether P-ISPDF domain scores predict critical diabetic foot outcomes, such as ulceration or amputation. Independent validation and structural refinement remain crucial before definitive conclusions regarding the instrument’s broader construct validity and clinical effectiveness can be drawn.
