Negligible impact of UV-curable resist shrinkage on NIL pattern fidelity
Authors: Guiyan Yang, Xinhe Cao, Jiabing Wang, Jiahong Chen, Ruiyang Chen, Bo Xu, Xinyu Wang, Xiaofeng Chen, Mengmeng Deng, Zongbin Hao, Yushuang Cui, Haixiong Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Shrinkage of UV-curable resists during nanoimprint lithography (NIL) has long been regarded as a potential source of pattern distortion; however, direct experimental validation of this effect remains scarce. Here, we systematically investigate the influence of resist shrinkage on the imprinted pattern fidelity using two UV-curable resins with distinct volumetric shrinkage ratios (6.8% and 2.6%). Despite this substantial difference, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) reveals no discernible degradation in fidelity of either micro- or nanoscale features across all samples. Notably, certain previously reported distortions may, in fact, originate from electron-beam-induced effects during SEM observation rather than intrinsic material shrinkage. To further quantify macroscopic effects, the profiles of millimeter-scale imprinted lenses were characterized via white-light interferometry. Relative to the mold, the lenses fabricated with resins exhibiting volumetric shrinkage of 6.8% and 2.6% show actual dimensional shrinkage of only 2.5% and 0.36%, respectively. These results indicate that resist shrinkage is largely compensated by the refilling of mold cavities from the residual layer prior to complete curing. Collectively, this work provides compelling experimental evidence that, within the commonly used range, resist shrinkage does not inherently compromise pattern fidelity in NIL.

Introduction

Nearly three decades after its invention, nanoimprint lithography (NIL) has evolved into a powerful and versatile nanofabrication technique capable of producing high-resolution patterns with sub-10-nm features [1–3]. Unlike conventional photolithography, NIL offers a low-cost, high-throughput route by mechanically driving a liquid resist into the relief structures of a mold, followed by solidification and demolding. Among the various NIL modalities, UV-based NIL is particularly attractive due to its low processing temperature, rapid cycle time, broad substrate compatibility, and excellent scalability for large-area patterning [4–7].

In UV-based NIL (UV-NIL), a UV-curable resist is confined between a transparent mold and the substrate, and subsequently solidified upon UV exposure via photoinitiated polymerization or crosslinking [8–10]. During UV curing, volumetric shrinkage typically occurs as intermolecular distances (~ 3–5 Å) between resin monomers contract to shorter intramolecular covalent bond lengths (~ 1.5–2 Å) upon the polymerization of reactive groups, such as (meth)acrylates, epoxides, or vinyl ethers as illustrated in Fig. 1. Volumetric shrinkage of commonly used UV-curable resins is typically reported to range from 5% to 15% for acrylate-based systems and from 1% to 5% for epoxy-based systems [11, 12]. Consequently, shrinkage has long been considered a potential source of pattern distortion and fidelity loss, particularly in high-resolution or high-aspect-ratio structures [13, 14]. Resist developers have therefore often sought to minimize shrinkage through careful material design, sometimes at the expense of other critical properties such as mechanical strength, viscosity, or curing speed.

To mitigate volumetric shrinkage during UV curing, materials exhibiting a negative Poisson’s ratio (NPR) have been explored. These auxetic materials, which laterally expand when stretched, display unconventional deformation behavior that can counteract the contraction associated with polymer network formation [15–17]. By incorporating molecular designs or structural motifs with NPR behavior, researchers aim to suppress inward collapse during crosslinking, thereby improving pattern fidelity in nanoimprint lithography. However, practical implementation of NPR resists remains limited due to complex synthesis routes, reduced mechanical robustness, and potentially higher formulation costs. Furthermore, achieving a balance between NPR behavior and essential properties such as spin-coating performance, viscosity, and curing speed continues to pose significant material design challenges.

However, the actual impact of resist shrinkage on NIL pattern fidelity has not been systematically validated. In many cases, the perceived correlation between shrinkage and pattern distortion is inferred from theoretical expectations or indirect and potentially misleading observations rather than direct experimental comparisons [18, 19].

Similar challenges have long been encountered in conventional UV-curing injection molding and film embossing processes, where polymerization-induced volumetric shrinkage can result in dimensional deviation after curing. In these processes, shrinkage-induced distortion is commonly mitigated through the combination of low-shrinkage resin formulations and constrained curing conditions. The application of external pressure during molding not only promotes complete cavity filling and resin redistribution but also maintains material supply during curing, thereby reducing the dimensional deviation caused by polymerization shrinkage. These mechanisms highlight the critical role of resin confinement and redistribution in achieving high-fidelity replication. Similar shrinkage-compensation mechanisms may also exist in nanoimprint lithography; however, their role in maintaining pattern fidelity has not been fully elucidated. Willson et al. combined simulation and experimental approaches to demonstrate that photopolymerization-induced bulk shrinkage has a limited impact on the critical dimension (CD) and sidewall profile of nanoimprinted structures. Their results revealed that shrinkage-induced deformation occurs primarily along the vertical direction, leading to a slight reduction in feature height and sidewall angle, while the lateral dimensions remain largely unchanged. However, although these studies demonstrated the limited influence of photopolymerization-induced shrinkage on pattern fidelity, the underlying mechanisms responsible for suppressing lateral distortion and maintaining structural accuracy during the curing process remain insufficiently understood [20, 21].

In this study, we systematically investigate the influence of resist shrinkage on the fidelity of imprinted nano-, micro-, and macro-scale structures using a set of UV-curable resins with well-characterized volumetric shrinkage. We quantitatively separate e-beam-induced dimensional shrinkage from real imprint deformation, which is seldom discussed in earlier literature. Comparing imprint outcomes for materials exhibiting 2.6% and 6.8% shrinkage, we find that nano- and microscale structures show virtually no shrinkage-induced distortion, while the dimensional reduction of macroscale features remains substantially lower than the overall volumetric shrinkage of the material. These results provide direct experimental evidence that, within the shrinkage range typical of UV-curable formulations, volumetric shrinkage does not inherently compromise structural fidelity in UV-NIL.

Results and discussion

Shrinkage measurement

To quantify the volumetric shrinkage of the UV-curable resins, we measured the change in thickness of spin-coated resin films on silicon wafers before and after curing, following the method established in our previous study [22]. The film thickness at identical locations before and after UV curing was measured using a spectroscopic ellipsometer. Lateral shrinkage is considered negligible due to the extreme aspect ratio of the films: the lateral dimensions span several centimeters, while the thickness remains in the range of a few hundred nanometers. Furthermore, the adhesion between the resin and the silicon substrate significantly restricts lateral contraction. Consequently, the vertical shrinkage serves as a reliable proxy for the total volumetric change. Imprinted pattern fidelity analysis by SEM

The fidelity of nanoimprinted structures is typically evaluated by comparing their geometric parameters with those of the original mold, necessitating accurate morphological characterization. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) is the most widely adopted tool for this purpose due to its high-resolution imaging capabilities. However, precisely assessing pattern shrinkage in high-resolution structures via SEM remains inherently challenging due to several intrinsic limitations. For instance, the requisite conductive metal coatings (e.g., several nanometers of gold or platinum) used to suppress charging can obscure fine structural details, blur edge transitions, and introduce systematic offsets in linewidth measurements. Furthermore, the incident electron beam may induce localized heating, chain scission, or polymer densification, resulting in an apparent reduction in feature dimensions. These imaging artifacts often make it difficult to decouple true process-induced changes from beam-induced deformation.

Despite these drawbacks, SEM remains the most practical and reliable technique available, as alternatives such as atomic force microscopy (AFM) exhibit even more pronounced limitations. AFM is prone to severe tip–sample convolution due to the finite radius of the probe, which precludes the accurate profiling of narrow trenches, steep sidewalls, and dense arrays. Moreover, tip geometry constraints often prevent the probe from reaching the bottom of high-aspect-ratio features. Given these trade-offs, SEM—when operated under optimized, low-voltage conditions—offers the optimal balance of resolution, throughput, and dimensional accuracy for assessing nanoscale pattern fidelity.

To optimize the SEM measurement conditions, nano- and microstructures were fabricated using acrylic resin via a UV-NIL process. Figure 3 displays SEM images of nano- and micro-scale imprinted structures acquired at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV and a working distance of 3.5 mm. The images represent three successive scan-speed levels (5, 20, and 35), which correspond to pixel dwell times of 0.3 µs, 3 µs, and 30 µs, respectively. The gratings, replicated from a master mold (~ 220 nm linewidth, 340 nm height, 550 nm pitch), exhibit monotonic dimension reduction under increasing electron doses. Linewidth and height decrease from 220 nm to 205 nm and from 340 to 325 nm, yielding lateral and vertical contractions of 6.8% and 4.4%, respectively. The stable period (550 ± 5 nm) suggests inward line shrinkage rather than pitch drift. This deformation, induced by the SEM electron beam, mirrors the magnitude of shrinkage typically observed in UV-curing processes. As a result, SEM imaging can artificially generate shrinkage signatures that may be misinterpreted as photopolymerization-induced deformation, thereby obscuring the true fidelity of the nanoimprint process. It was found that when the scanning time is reduced to around 300 ns, high-clarity images can still be obtained, and the measured feature dimensions remain consistent with those of the mold, indicating that imaging artifacts become negligible under these optimized SEM conditions. Figure 3c shows the nanopillar arrays imprinted with epoxy resin under identical imaging conditions. The pillar height exhibits a reduction from 360 nm to 335 nm (ca. 6.9%). Despite a theoretical curing shrinkage of only ~ 2.6%, the structural contraction observed under SEM is comparable to that of imprints made with acrylic resin.

Subsequently, a hexagonal microcolumn array—originally designed for patterned sapphire substrate (PSS) fabrication—was employed to evaluate the impact of electron-beam-induced shrinkage on micropattern fidelity. As shown in Fig. 4a and b, the micro-scale resist patterns exhibit reduced sensitivity to SEM scan speed variations. While the scan speed increased from level 5 to 35, the structural height decreased only slightly from 2.5 μm to 2.4 μm. The only other observable distortion was a minor deviation from a circular to an elliptical profile in the top view. This close agreement between the imprinted features and the mold dimensions confirms the enhanced structural integrity and stability of the micropatterns under electron-beam exposure.

Figure 4c and d indicate that the initial spin-coated acrylate resin thickness was 1370 nm. Given the PSS mold dimensions (period: 2 μm; height: 2.5 μm; top/bottom diameters: 1.5/1.8 μm), approximately 1170 nm of the resin is required to fill the relief structures. Neglecting volume loss from UV-induced shrinkage, the theoretical residual layer thickness (RLT) would be 200 nm. Considering a volumetric shrinkage of ~ 6.8% for the resin, the calculated shrinkage within the residual layer itself is approximately 14 nm, which would result in an expected thickness of ~ 186 nm. However, the actual measured RLT was roughly 120 nm. The observed reduction in the residual layer is significantly greater than the theoretical calculation; this discrepancy represents approximately 5% of the total volume of the mold structures. This value closely aligns with the UV shrinkage rate of the acrylate resin. Since no significant shrinkage was observed within the imprinted structures themselves, it is proposed that prior to complete solidification, the UV-curable material in the residual layer was further driven into the mold features under the applied imprint pressure. This mass transport effectively compensated for the volumetric shrinkage of the imprinted features, resulting in a thinner-than-expected residual layer. For accurate dimensional calibration, rigid silicon master molds were characterized by SEM as size references. Unlike deformable polymer working molds, silicon templates remain dimensionally stable under imprint pressure, which excludes measurement errors induced by mold compression.

Profile analysis of imprinted macro-patterns via white-light interferometry

We further investigated the influence of resist shrinkage on the fidelity of imprinted macroscopic lens structures (diameter: 2 mm; average height: 55.6 μm). As shown in Fig. 5, white-light interferometry was employed to characterize the morphology of the imprinted lenses. As a non-contact optical technique, white-light interferometry allows for the mapping of three-dimensional surface topography with sub-nanometer vertical resolution. By analyzing interference fringes generated from the reflected light of both the sample surface and a reference mirror, the topography of complex surfaces can be reconstructed over large areas. Here, a Bruker 3D white-light interferometer (vertical resolution < 0.1 nm) was used to capture nanoscale height variations, while large-area stitching was applied to reconstruct the full millimeter-scale profiles.

Compared with the designed geometry (Fig. 5a), measurements reveal that while lateral dimensions remain essentially unchanged, the vertical structural height decreases. For the acrylate-based resist, the average lens height decreased by approximately 1400 nm (~ 2.5% shrinkage) (Fig. 5c), which is considerably smaller than its nominal bulk shrinkage of 6.8%. By contrast, the epoxy-based resist (bulk shrinkage: 2.5%) showed only a ~ 0.36% average height reduction (Fig. 5b). Figure 5d and e display two-dimensional cross-sectional profiles of the imprinted structures using the epoxy and acrylate resists, respectively. In Fig. 5d, the red curve (epoxy-based structure) shows only a slight height deviation from the gray curve (mold geometry). Similarly, in Fig. 5e, the blue curve (epoxy-based structure) demonstrates a minor height discrepancy relative to the mold, consistent with the volumetric data in Fig. 5a and c. These results confirm that for both materials, the observed structural shrinkage is significantly lower than their respective bulk volume shrinkage. This discrepancy in fidelity stems from the interplay between curing kinetics and the compensation mechanism. Owing to the larger volume of the macroscopic structures and the short curing duration, the resins often reach full solidification before volumetric shrinkage can be adequately compensated. The epoxy system exhibits better fidelity due to its slower reaction kinetics, which extends the duration of its fluid state. This broadened processing window allows the surrounding resist to more effectively backfill the volume deficit under imprinting pressure before the polymer network is cross-linked. In contrast, the faster curing kinetics of the acrylate-based resist leads to rapid solidification, which limits this compensatory flow and results in greater vertical height loss. These findings are consistent with the previously proposed compensation mechanism and suggest that extending the fluid window by slowing the curing rate is a viable strategy for enhancing the structural fidelity in large-scale imprinted features.

As depicted in Fig. 6, during UV-induced crosslinking and curing, volume shrinkage of the imprint resist is predominantly accommodated by the thinning of the residual layer, rather than by dimensional distortion of the micro- and nanostructures. Prior to full solidification, the fluidic resist within the residual layer is continuously driven into the mold cavities under the applied imprint pressure. This in-situ viscous flow effectively compensates for the volume loss occurring during polymerization, thereby suppressing structural shrinkage and preserving high-fidelity pattern replication across large areas.

It should be noted that curing rate and intrinsic volumetric shrinkage are two independent physical parameters and are not directly correlated. The intrinsic shrinkage of UV-curable resist is fundamentally determined by the molecular structure of polymerizable groups and the polymerization pathway. Epoxy resin exhibits low shrinkage due to ring-opening polymerization with minimal molecular compaction and network densification, whereas acrylate resin suffers from high shrinkage originating from conventional chain-growth polymerization. In contrast, curing kinetics is governed by the photoreaction rate and primarily affects the dynamic flow behavior of the resist during gelation, rather than changing the final shrinkage magnitude of the material. Slower curing extends the fluidic window of the epoxy resist, allowing continuous residual-layer resist redistribution and cavity refilling to compensate polymerization-induced volume loss. This kinetic fluid compensation effectively reduces pattern distortion and improves replication fidelity. In this study, we did not adjust UV intensity to modulate curing speed, because varied UV dosage inevitably introduces photothermal accumulation and temperature fluctuation. Such thermal interference alters resist rheology and brings extra confounding variables, making it impossible to purely isolate the kinetic effect. Therefore, all comparisons were performed under identical UV conditions to eliminate thermal disturbance. The superior pattern fidelity of epoxy resist is attributed to the synergistic effect of its inherently low chemical shrinkage and favorable slow-curing fluid compensation behavior.

Beyond the kinetic compensation mechanism dominated by curing kinetics, the shrinkage compensation behavior of the residual layer is also restricted by a critical resist-loading threshold, which further defines the effective working condition of the fluid refilling effect. The residual-layer compensation function only takes effect when the initial coated resist volume exceeds the theoretical consumption required to fully fill the mold cavities. Under sufficient resist dosage, the redundant uncured resist acts as a stable material reservoir, continuously supplementing the volume loss induced by polymerization shrinkage during UV curing and thereby maintaining complete and uniform grating morphology. In contrast, when the coated resist amount is insufficient to meet the cavity-filling demand, no spare liquid resist can support the refilling process. For continuous grating nanostructures in particular, inadequate resist supply terminates the compensation mechanism entirely, resulting in discontinuous, broken, and defective imprinted patterns. To visually verify this threshold characteristic and clarify the valid operating range of the residual-layer compensation effect, comparative imprinting tests with both sufficient and insufficient resist loadings are supplemented in Fig. 7.

Conclusion

In summary, a systematic comparison of UV-curable resins with volumetric shrinkage values of 2.6% and 6.8% confirms that NIL pattern fidelity remains uncompromised. We demonstrate that in-situ resist reflow from the residual layer effectively back-fills the mold cavities prior to complete solidification, thereby neutralizing potential height loss or structural sagging. High-resolution SEM analysis reveals no measurable dimensional degradation in micro- or nanoscale imprinted features, even for the 6.8% high-shrinkage formulation. Furthermore, white-light interferometry measurements indicate that the vertical height shrinkage of large-scale imprinted features is significantly lower than the nominal volumetric shrinkage. These findings provide direct evidence that, under standard imprinting conditions, volumetric shrinkage is not a primary limiting factor for pattern fidelity, as the residual layer acts as a material reservoir that maintains structural integrity through compensatory mass transport.

Methods

UV-curable imprinting

The epoxy-based (PL-R-LS001) and acrylate-based (PL-R-PC2000) UV-curable nanoimprint resists, the perfluorinated UV-curable working mold resin (PL-R-UPM), and the Air Cushion Press (ACP) equipment (PL-T) were sourced from Prinano Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). All other reagents were obtained from commercial sources and used as received without further purification, unless otherwise specified.

The UV-curable imprinting process was performed using the Air Cushion Press (ACP) approach. To facilitate the process, disposable polymer working molds were employed. These working molds were fabricated by replicating master silicon molds through a reverse imprinting technique. This involved applying a low-surface-energy, perfluorinated UV-curable resin onto PET sheets using the ACP system.

In this work, we adopted a fixed standardized pressure of 100 kPa for all comparative tests to eliminate pressure-induced variables. Supplementary pressure gradient experiments with multiple pressure levels are not performed here, for two reasons: Capillary force dominates the filling behavior at micro/nano scale; low external pressure barely changes the ultimate compensation capacity of residual layer within a reasonable pressure window; Our core comparison target lies in the intrinsic shrinkage difference between epoxy and acrylate resists, rather than exploring pressure-dependent flow behaviors.

Characterization

All of the SEM images were obtained using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (ZEISS, ULTRA-55). Imprinted replicas and molds of large-scale features were characterized using a Bruker 3D white-light interferometric profiler. Measurement points were spaced approximately 0.9825 μm in X and Y, yielding more than 3 million data points within each unit-cell clear aperture (Bruker, ContourX-1000). Film thickness was measured by spectroscopic ellipsometry, and a hexagonal-point grid was used to map the thickness distribution across the entire substrate (J.A. Woollam, RC2).
