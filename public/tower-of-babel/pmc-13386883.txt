Glycolytic reprogramming in endometriosis: molecular mechanisms, immune modulation, and non-hormonal therapeutic opportunities
Authors: Lijuan Guo, Lanqing Liu, Yu Geng, Bing Zhang, Shaojie Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Endometriosis is a chronic gynecological disorder characterized by ectopic tissue growth and significant morbidity, yet current hormonal and surgical treatments often fail to prevent recurrence or entail severe side effects. Increasing evidence highlights metabolic reprogramming, specifically aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect), as a fundamental driver of endometriotic lesion pathogenesis. This narrative review synthesizes current research on the regulatory mechanisms and therapeutic potential of targeting glycolytic dysregulation in endometriosis. We examine the aberrant upregulation of rate-limiting enzymes, including hexokinase 2 (HK2), 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase 3 (PFKFB3), and pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), driven by signaling axes such as ALKBH5–HK2, PAK5–PKM2, and S1PR4/mTOR. The review further elucidates how enhanced glycolytic flux and lactate accumulation promote cell proliferation, invasion, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) while orchestrating an immunosuppressive microenvironment via M2 macrophage polarization and histone lactylation. Furthermore, we evaluate emerging non-hormonal therapeutic strategies, including specific glycolysis inhibitors and natural compounds, and discuss the utility of immune-metabolic gene signatures for non-invasive diagnosis. Highlighting these metabolic vulnerabilities offers promising avenues for precision medicine to improve clinical management and patient outcomes in endometriosis.

Introduction

Endometriosis is a chronic gynecological condition defined by the ectopic presence of endometrial-like tissue outside the uterine cavity, impacting an estimated 10–15% of women of reproductive age globally. This disorder is frequently linked to debilitating pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea, infertility, and a substantial reduction in the affected individuals’ quality of life [1, 2]. Although classified as a benign disease, endometriosis displays several tumor-like characteristics, including invasive growth, angiogenesis, and resistance to apoptosis, which collectively underpin its complex pathophysiology [3]. This creates a unique “benign-yet-malignant-like” metabolic phenotype: while endometriotic lesions remain histologically benign and non-metastatic, they hijack cancer-like aerobic glycolysis to sustain proliferation, invasion, and immune evasion without crossing into frank malignancy. The etiology of endometriosis is multifactorial, involving a combination of aberrant hormonal regulation, immune dysfunction, inflammatory processes, and metabolic alterations [4, 5]. Hormonal imbalances, particularly estrogen dependence, are pivotal for lesion establishment and maintenance. Concurrently, immune dysregulation creates a permissive microenvironment that facilitates the survival and progression of the ectopic tissue [6, 7]. Furthermore, the involvement of oxidative stress and multi-dimensional metabolic alterations—including extensive cross-talk between aerobic glycolysis, glutaminolysis, and altered lipid metabolism—has been demonstrated in the pathogenesis of endometriosis, further adding to the complexity of the disease landscape.

Current therapeutic strategies for endometriosis are predominantly centered on surgical excision of lesions and hormonal treatments intended to suppress ovarian function and thus lower systemic estrogen levels. While these interventions offer symptom palliation and can enhance fertility outcomes in select patients, their effectiveness is frequently hindered by high disease recurrence rates and considerable side effects, including adverse effects on bone mineral density, increased cardiovascular risk, and compromise to future fertility potential [8–10]. Specifically, surgical management, despite its efficacy in pain relief, inherently involves risks of complications and fails to guarantee permanent resolution given the multifocal and recurrent nature of the disorder [11]. Hormonal therapies—which encompass gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonists, oral contraceptives, and specific progestins such as dienogest—provide effective symptom control but are associated with side effects like hypoestrogenism and are contraindicated in certain patient groups [10, 12]. Crucially, none of the existing treatments are curative and often demand long-term administration, a regimen that is neither feasible nor acceptable for all affected individuals. Consequently, there is an urgent, recognized need to develop novel therapeutic approaches that specifically target the fundamental pathogenic mechanisms of endometriosis, offering superior efficacy and safety profiles [13].

A growing body of recent evidence, which draws distinct parallels with cancer metabolism, has underscored the critical role of metabolic reprogramming in the pathogenesis of endometriosis. Among these metabolic shifts, the aberrant activation of glycolysis—the conversion of glucose to lactate even in the presence of sufficient oxygen, often referred to as the Warburg effect—has emerged as a key characteristic of endometriotic lesions [14, 15]. This metabolic alteration serves a dual purpose: it not only fulfills the elevated energy and biosynthetic requirements of ectopic endometrial cells but also crucially influences their proliferative, invasive, and migratory capabilities, thereby actively facilitating lesion establishment and progression [16, 17]. Key glycolysis-related enzymes, including PFKFB3, PGK1, LDHA, and PKM2, are consistently found to be upregulated in endometriotic tissues. This upregulation drives enhanced glycolytic flux and promotes cellular survival, particularly under the hypoxic and inflammatory conditions that characterize the ectopic microenvironment [18, 19]. Moreover, this metabolic reprogramming extends beyond mere energy production, significantly affecting local immune cell function and the overall immune milieu, a factor vital for the immune evasion and subsequent persistence of endometriotic lesions [20]. The complex interaction between heightened glycolytic metabolism and immune modulation strongly suggests that targeting these specific metabolic pathways offers a promising strategy to disrupt the pathogenic crosstalk that sustains endometriosis.

Given the central role of glycolysis in endometriosis pathophysiology and its profound influence on both cellular vitality and local immune functions, this pathway represents a highly promising therapeutic target. Strategically inhibiting key glycolytic enzymes or modulating upstream metabolic regulators could impair the metabolic adaptability of endometriotic cells, consequently reducing lesion growth and alleviating symptom severity without incurring the systemic adverse effects commonly associated with conventional hormonal therapies [21, 22]. Furthermore, such metabolic interventions could serve to complement existing treatments or offer viable alternatives for patients for whom hormonal therapy is contraindicated. This review aims to systematically summarize the current research progress concerning the role of glycolysis in endometriosis. Our scope encompasses the identification of key glycolytic enzymes, the elucidation of their regulatory mechanisms and associated signaling pathways, and the critical evaluation of potential therapeutic interventions that specifically target glycolytic metabolism. By integrating these molecular insights with clinical implications, we intend to highlight the significant potential of glycolysis-targeted strategies to advance the management of endometriosis and ultimately improve patient outcomes.

Association between glycolytic metabolism and endometriosis

Warburg effect in ectopic endometrial cells

Ectopic endometrial cells in endometriosis adopt a tumor-like metabolic phenotype characterized by aerobic glycolysis, commonly referred to as the Warburg effect. Even in the presence of adequate oxygen, these cells preferentially utilize glycolysis rather than oxidative phosphorylation for energy production. Polarographic measurements of patient-derived endometriotic tissues, including ovarian endometriomas and peritoneal lesions, have consistently shown decreased oxygen consumption and impaired mitochondrial respiration compared with healthy eutopic endometrium [23]. This metabolic reprogramming is accompanied by marked upregulation of key glycolytic enzymes, such as hexokinase 2 (HK2) and pyruvate kinase, which accelerate glycolytic throughput. The resulting overproduction of lactate acidifies the peritoneal microenvironment, creating conditions that enhance ectopic cell survival, motility, immune evasion, extracellular matrix remodeling, and neovascularization—processes that collectively drive lesion implantation and chronic persistence. Studies using immortalized human endometriotic cell lines (e.g., 12Z) have shown that inhibition of lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), a critical enzyme in the final step of anaerobic glycolysis, selectively induces apoptosis in ectopic cells through mitochondrial dysfunction, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, and loss of mitochondrial membrane potential [24]. This glycolytic dependence bears striking resemblance to the metabolic profile of ovarian clear cell carcinoma (CCC), a malignancy that frequently originates from endometriotic cysts. In CCC, hepatocyte nuclear factor-1β (HNF1β) orchestrates sustained anaerobic glycolysis while suppressing oxidative phosphorylation, thereby conferring resilience in adverse microenvironments [25]. The shared metabolic signature between endometriosis and CCC emphasizes the pivotal role of glycolytic reprogramming not only in disease maintenance but also in malignant transformation. Importantly, while aerobic glycolysis is a central metabolic hallmark, it does not operate in isolation. The metabolic reprogramming of ectopic endometrial cells is highly multi-dimensional, featuring significant cross-talk between glycolysis, glutaminolysis, and lipid metabolism. For instance, the excessive diversion of glucose toward lactate production necessitates alternative carbon sources to replenish the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle; consequently, ectopic cells upregulate glutamine anaplerosis to meet these ongoing bioenergetic demands. Furthermore, enhanced glycolytic flux provides crucial intermediates—such as dihydroxyacetone phosphate and acetyl-CoA—that are rapidly shunted into de novo lipogenesis, supplying the lipid bilayers essential for the proliferation of endometriotic lesions. Conversely, altered lipid metabolism, particularly enhanced fatty acid oxidation (FAO), can synergize with glycolysis to confer metabolic flexibility, allowing ectopic cells to survive in nutrient-deprived peritoneal microenvironments. Therefore, understanding the holistic energetic landscape requires acknowledging this interconnected metabolic network.

In summary, the Warburg effect represents a fundamental metabolic adaptation in ectopic endometrial cells that sustains their proliferative and invasive behavior through lactate-mediated microenvironmental alterations. These observations provide a compelling biological foundation for pursuing glycolytic inhibitors as novel therapeutic approaches in the management of endometriosis.

Aberrant expression of key glycolytic enzymes

Ectopic endometrial tissues in endometriosis consistently show elevated expression of key glycolytic enzymes and regulators, including HK2, 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase 3 (PFKFB3), and LDHA. These changes markedly increase glycolytic flux and provide the bioenergetic and biosynthetic support required for enhanced proliferation, migration, and invasion. HK2 is strongly upregulated in endometriotic stromal cells, where its knockdown significantly impairs cell proliferation and migration. This effect is mediated, at least in part, by increased phosphorylation and activation of STAT1, revealing an unexpected non-canonical pathway through which HK2 influences cellular behavior [26]. PFKFB3 is also overexpressed and serves a dual role: it sustains high glycolytic rates by maintaining elevated fructose-2,6-bisphosphate levels and promotes epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) through β-catenin stabilization. Expression of PFKFB3 is driven by heat shock factor 1 (HSF1); inhibition of either HSF1 or PFKFB3 (e.g., with PFK-015) effectively suppresses glycolysis, cell proliferation, and lesion development in preclinical models [27]. LDHA expression is increased in ectopic lesions, particularly under hypoxia, and is essential for cell survival. It preserves mitochondrial function, limits apoptosis, and supports migration. Silencing LDHA disrupts mitochondrial membrane potential, raises ROS levels, and curtails both glycolysis and invasiveness [28]. By contrast, reduced LDHA activity in eutopic endometrium, linked to altered N6-methyladenosine (m6A) methylation, impairs decidualization and contributes to infertility, highlighting the divergent metabolic needs of eutopic versus ectopic tissues [29]. Additional regulators amplify this glycolytic shift. The long non-coding RNA H19, highly expressed in endometriotic lesions, drives aerobic glycolysis and promotes histone lactylation, thereby enhancing proliferation and migration [30]. In ovarian granulosa cells from affected patients, elevated prohibitin 1 (PHB1) boosts expression of glycolytic enzymes, glucose uptake, and lactate production, partially compensating for mitochondrial impairment and potentially affecting oocyte quality [31].

Together, the coordinated upregulation of HK2, PFKFB3, LDHA, and their regulatory networks establishes a robust Warburg-like phenotype in ectopic endometrium that fuels lesion growth and persistence. These metabolic vulnerabilities identify HK2, PFKFB3, and LDHA as attractive therapeutic targets for disrupting the energy supply that sustains endometriosis progression.

Regulation of Cellular Functions by glycolytic metabolites

Lactate, the primary end-product of aerobic glycolysis in ectopic endometrium, exerts far-reaching effects on both immune and cellular behavior in endometriosis. Within the peritoneal microenvironment, elevated lactate levels drive macrophage polarization toward the immunosuppressive M2 phenotype, creating a tolerant niche that supports lesion survival, fibrosis, and immune escape. Although glycolytic metabolism typically sustains proinflammatory M1 macrophages while oxidative phosphorylation favors M2 differentiation, the high lactate concentrations characteristic of endometriotic lesions override this pattern and promote M2 dominance, thereby dampening effective immune clearance [32, 33]. Beyond its immunomodulatory role, lactate serves as a signaling molecule through histone lactylation, a posttranslational modification in which lactyl groups are added to lysine residues on histones, altering chromatin accessibility and gene expression. In endometriotic cells, hypoxia-induced LDHA activity markedly increases lactate production, fueling widespread histone lactylation that upregulates genes involved in proliferation, migration, and invasion. This direct metabolic–epigenetic coupling enables ectopic cells to translate their glycolytic state into sustained pathogenic behavior [28, 34]. Additional layers of regulation involve sirtuin 7 (SIRT7), which suppresses mitochondrial respiration, enhances expression of glycolytic enzymes, and boosts lactate generation. The resulting metabolic shift activates RhoA/ROCK and AKT signaling while triggering EMT in stromal cells, further promoting lesion expansion [35].

Thus, lactate functions as a central hub that simultaneously reshapes the immune microenvironment through M2 polarization and reprograms gene expression via histone lactylation. These dual actions reinforce lesion persistence and invasiveness, positioning lactate production and its downstream pathways as compelling therapeutic targets for restoring immune surveillance and halting disease progression in endometriosis.

Glycolysis-related regulatory factors and signaling pathways

Epigenetic regulation of key glycolytic enzymes in endometriosis

The RNA demethylase AlkB homolog 5 (ALKBH5), an eraser of m6A modifications, drives aerobic glycolysis in endometriosis by stabilizing HK2 mRNA and increasing its translation in endometrial stromal cells. Removal of m6A marks by ALKBH5 enhances HK2 expression, accelerates glycolytic flux, and supplies the energy and biosynthetic precursors required for the heightened proliferation, migration, and invasiveness of ectopic cells. Knockdown of HK2 in ALKBH5-overexpressing stromal cells abolishes these effects, confirming that the ALKBH5–HK2 axis is essential for maintaining the glycolytic phenotype and promoting lesion progression [36]. Similar m6A-dependent control of HK2 has been observed in malignancy. In colorectal cancer, coordinated downregulation of ALKBH5 and the related demethylase fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO) increases m6A on HK2 mRNA, allowing recognition by the reader protein insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 2 (IGF2BP2) and subsequent stabilization, thereby intensifying glycolysis and tumor growth [37]. A comparable pathway involving FTO and glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK3β) regulates HK2 in cervical cancer [38]. Therapeutically, pharmacological interference of ALKBH5 with the small-molecule inhibitor 5-carboxy-8-hydroxyquinoline (IOX1) suppresses HK2 expression, reduces glycolytic activity, and significantly restricts lesion development in mouse models of endometriosis. These findings establish the ALKBH5–HK2 axis as a critical epigenetic driver of aerobic glycolysis in endometriosis and highlight pharmacological inhibition of ALKBH5 as a promising strategy to disrupt HK2-mediated glycolytic reprogramming in ectopic lesions.

In addition to m6A-dependent regulation by ALKBH5, recent studies have identified complementary epigenetic mechanisms that fine-tune the expression of rate-limiting glycolytic enzymes in endometriosis. Specifically, miR-143 directly targets the 3′-UTR of HK2 mRNA, suppressing its translation and thereby attenuating aerobic glycolysis in endometrial stromal cells. Downregulation of miR-143 in ectopic lesions relieves this repression, contributing to HK2 overexpression and enhanced glycolytic flux [39, 40]. Furthermore, histone acetylation at the promoters of HK2 and PKM2, mediated by histone acetyltransferases such as p300/CBP, increases chromatin accessibility and transcriptional activation of these enzymes under hypoxic conditions prevalent in endometriotic tissue [41]. These miRNA- and histone acetylation-mediated layers of regulation work in concert with m6A modification to sustain the Warburg-like metabolic phenotype.

PAK5-mediated phosphorylation of PKM2

P21-activated kinase 5 (PAK5), a serine/threonine kinase of the group II PAK family, is strongly overexpressed in endometriotic lesions and contributes to their growth and invasiveness. PAK5 directly phosphorylates pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) at Ser519, a modification that stabilizes the protein, increases its enzymatic activity, and shifts cellular metabolism toward aerobic glycolysis. This enhancement of PKM2 function accelerates glycolytic flux, providing the energy and biosynthetic intermediates required for rapid proliferation and migration of ectopic endometrial cells. Disruption of this pathway markedly impairs lesion progression. Genetic deletion of PAK5 or treatment with the selective PAK5 inhibitor GNE-2861 abolishes PKM2 Ser519 phosphorylation, reduces glycolytic rate, and suppresses cell proliferation and invasiveness both in vitro and in murine endometriosis models [17]. These results establish the PAK5–PKM2 axis as a central regulator of aerobic glycolysis in endometriosis and identify selective PAK5 inhibition as a promising therapeutic approach to suppress PKM2-driven glycolytic flux and lesion progression.

AURKA–ERβ axis drives glycolysis in ovarian endometriosis

Aurora kinase A (AURKA), a mitotic serine/threonine kinase, is markedly overexpressed in ovarian endometriotic lesions, where it forms a functional complex with estrogen receptor β (ERβ). This interaction stabilizes and transcriptionally activates ERβ, triggering a metabolic shift toward aerobic glycolysis characterized by increased glucose uptake and lactate production. The resulting enhancement of glycolytic flux meets the elevated energetic and biosynthetic requirements of rapidly proliferating and invading ectopic endometrial cells, while supporting their adaptation to the hypoxic, inflammatory peritoneal environment. Pharmacological inhibition of AURKA with alisertib sharply reduces glycolytic activity, suppresses cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, and restricts lesion growth in mouse models of ovarian endometriosis. Rescue experiments demonstrate that these anti-glycolytic and anti-proliferative effects are largely ERβ-dependent, confirming that AURKA acts primarily through ERβ to reprogram metabolism [42]. These findings are consistent with observations in malignant contexts, where AURKA blockade has been reported to reverse the Warburg effect and expose metabolic vulnerabilities [43]. Together, they identify the AURKA–ERβ axis as a key upstream link between estrogen signaling and glycolytic reprogramming in ovarian endometriosis, and support AURKA inhibition as a potential non-hormonal strategy to suppress ERβ-mediated glycolytic activity and lesion growth.

S1PR4/mTOR pathway drives glycolysis in ectopic endometrial cells

Sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor 4 (S1PR4) is markedly upregulated in endometriotic lesions and potently stimulates aerobic glycolysis in ectopic endometrial stromal cells through activation of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway. Elevated S1PR4 expression correlates with increased extracellular acidification and lactate production; its knockdown sharply reduces p-mTOR levels, glycolytic flux, cell viability, and invasiveness, whereas forced S1PR4 overexpression in normal endometrial stromal cells recapitulates these pro-glycolytic and aggressive phenotypes [44]. This mechanism parallels mTOR’s established role as a master regulator of the Warburg effect across malignancies, where it integrates growth signals to sustain high glycolytic rates and biosynthetic capacity [45]. Pharmacological blockade of mTOR with AZD8055 effectively abolishes S1PR4-driven glycolysis, downregulates glycolytic enzymes, and impairs cell survival and invasion in vitro while restricting lesion growth in vivo. Similar benefits of mTOR inhibition have been observed in other glycolytic disorders, including macrophage inflammation and hepatocellular carcinoma [46, 47]. Although S1PR4 also influences immune metabolism, such as promoting fatty acid oxidation in regulatory T cells via Nrf2/PPARα signaling [48], its dominant effect in endometriosis centers on mTOR-dependent glycolytic enhancement. Collectively, the S1PR4–mTOR axis emerges as a critical upstream regulator that drives aerobic glycolysis through enhanced glycolytic enzyme expression in ectopic endometrial cells, positioning mTOR inhibitors as a compelling therapeutic approach to reverse this metabolic reprogramming in endometriosis.

PIM2 phosphorylation stabilizes PFKFB4 to drive glycolysis

PIM2 kinase directly phosphorylates PFKFB4 at Thr140, preventing its ubiquitin-proteasome degradation and markedly increasing its stability and intracellular levels in endometriotic lesions. This modification potently enhances PFKFB4 activity, accelerating glycolytic flux through sustained elevation of fructose-2,6-bisphosphate and promoting the characteristic Warburg-like metabolism of ectopic endometrial cells. The resulting increase in glucose consumption and lactate production fuels the proliferative and invasive behavior that defines lesion persistence and recurrence, with PIM2 and PFKFB4 co-expression confirmed in patient-derived tissues [14, 49]. Inhibition of PIM2 kinase activity effectively disrupts this axis, reducing PFKFB4 phosphorylation and stability, suppressing glycolysis, and impairing cell proliferation both in vitro and in endometriosis animal models. These findings establish the PIM2–PFKFB4 axis as a key stabilizer of glycolytic flux in endometriosis and position PIM2 inhibition as a mechanism-based, non-hormonal therapeutic strategy to destabilize PFKFB4 and suppress aerobic glycolysis in endometriotic lesions.

Glycolysis shapes the immune microenvironment in endometriosis

Glycolysis profoundly influences the immune landscape of endometriotic lesions, primarily through lactate-mediated macrophage polarization toward the immunosuppressive M2 phenotype. Elevated glycolytic flux in ectopic tissues generates high local lactate concentrations that drive M2 dominance, characterized by increased IL-10 and TGF-β secretion, reduced IL-6 and TNF-α production, and enhanced angiogenesis and tissue remodeling—all favoring lesion survival and immune evasion. Hypoxia further amplifies this process via the CD47/AKT axis, which upregulates PDPK1 and LDHA to sustain M2 polarization; silencing PDPK1 or inhibiting CD47/LDHA reverses glycolysis, reduces M2 infiltration, and promotes lesion apoptosis [50]. Importantly, the pathological accumulation of lactate—the primary byproduct of aerobic glycolysis—extends immune suppression beyond M2 macrophage polarization. Within the immunosuppressive niche, elevated local lactate concentrations and extracellular acidification directly impair innate immunity by inhibiting natural killer (NK) cell activation. Specifically, lactate downregulates activating receptors (such as NKp46) and suppresses the release of cytotoxic granules including granzyme B and perforin, thereby weakening NK cell-mediated immunosurveillance [51]. Concurrently, the lactate-rich microenvironment drives metabolic exhaustion of CD8⁺ T cells through activation of the STAT1/PDCD1 signaling axis, which upregulates inhibitory checkpoints and reduces pro-inflammatory cytokine production (e.g., IFN-γ), ultimately facilitating immune escape and ectopic lesion persistence [6]. The immune checkpoint VISTA, modulated by microbiota-derived metabolites tied to glycolytic pathways, further restricts T cell infiltration and activity [52]. Therapeutic targeting of this metabolic-immune axis shows strong promise. Sunitinib matures myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and curbs their suppressive effects, restoring immune competence and reducing lesion burden [53]. Harmine, by inhibiting oxidative phosphorylation and adenosine signaling, shifts macrophages toward the proinflammatory M1 phenotype and suppresses lesion growth [54]. Glycolysis-immune gene signatures, including PDK3, GPC3, and ADH6, correlate with immune infiltration patterns and predict assisted reproductive technology outcomes in endometriosis patients [55, 56].

Thus, glycolysis not only powers ectopic cell proliferation but orchestrates a tolerogenic microenvironment through macrophage and T cell dysfunction. Immunometabolic interventions targeting glycolytic enzymes or lactate signaling offer a novel strategy to reinstate immune surveillance and halt disease progression.

Ubiquitination regulation of key glycolytic enzymes

CHIP targets PFKFB4 for ubiquitination and degradation

The E3 ubiquitin ligase CHIP (STIP1 homology and U-box containing protein 1) suppresses glycolysis in endometriosis by directing PFKFB4 for proteasomal degradation through K305 ubiquitination. Overexpressed PFKFB4 in ectopic endometrial tissues drives excessive glycolytic flux that fuels lesion invasion and persistence; CHIP-mediated ubiquitination destabilizes PFKFB4, sharply reducing its levels and thereby curtailing glycolysis and migratory capacity in endometriotic cells [57]. Pharmacological activation of CHIP with YL-109 (CHIP agonist) enhances this degradative pathway [58], thereby lowering PFKFB4 abundance, glycolytic activity, and lesion progression in animal models of endometriosis. This approach exploits the ubiquitin-proteasome system to reverse metabolic reprogramming, offering a non-hormonal therapeutic strategy with potential to limit disease burden while avoiding endocrine side effects.

CHIP-mediated degradation of hmgb1 suppresses glycolysis

High mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), a chromatin-associated protein that also functions extracellularly as a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP), promotes aerobic glycolysis in multiple pathological settings by engaging receptors such as RAGE and activating downstream pathways that upregulate glycolytic enzymes. In ovarian endometriosis, HMGB1 accumulates in ectopic lesions and sustains the Warburg-like metabolism that drives lesion growth. CHIP counters this process by directly binding HMGB1, catalyzing its ubiquitination, and targeting it for proteasomal degradation. Overexpression of CHIP markedly reduces HMGB1 levels, attenuates glycolytic flux, and impairs endometriotic cell proliferation and invasion; conversely, YL-109 replicates these effects and significantly suppresses lesion development in mouse models [58]. This regulatory axis extends beyond endometriosis. In breast cancer, tumor-derived HMGB1 induces aerobic glycolysis in stromal fibroblasts via RAGE, generating lactate that fuels metastasis [59]. In pulmonary arterial hypertension, HMGB1 drives glycolysis and proliferation of smooth muscle cells through YAP–PFKFB3 signaling [60], while in lung adenocarcinoma it represses tumor suppressors and enhances metastasis via SET/HAT1 complexes [61]. HMGB1 also shapes immune metabolism, such as promoting proinflammatory macrophage polarization in ulcerative colitis [62].

Thus, CHIP-mediated degradation of HMGB1 serves as a critical brake on glycolysis-dependent pathology. By lowering HMGB1 abundance, CHIP disrupts the metabolic reprogramming that sustains inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling across diverse diseases, and its pharmacological activation offers a promising strategy for targeting glycolysis-driven disorders, including endometriosis.

Glycolysis and proliferation, migration, and invasion of endometriosis cells

Glycolysis drives cell proliferation through metabolic and signaling mechanisms

Glycolysis fuels rapid cell proliferation by generating ATP and biosynthetic precursors while simultaneously engaging signaling networks that accelerate cell-cycle progression and suppress apoptosis. Rate-limiting enzymes of the pathway, particularly the PFKFB family (PFKFB2, PFKFB3, and PFKFB4), elevate fructose-2,6-bisphosphate to sustain high glycolytic flux and support biomass accumulation in proliferating cells, as observed in renal cell carcinoma and pancreatic adenocarcinoma [63, 64]. Post-translational modifications further refine this control: citrullination of PKM2 alters its allosteric regulation and enzymatic activity, optimizing glycolysis for proliferative demands [65]. Glycolytic intermediates and enzymes also directly modulate proliferation signaling. HIF1α, stabilized under hypoxia, transcriptionally induces glycolytic genes while paradoxically restraining proliferation when NADH accumulation inhibits GAPDH, illustrating context-dependent regulation [66]. In acute myeloid leukemia, BMAL2 amplifies HIF1α-driven glycolysis to promote leukemic cell expansion [67]. HK2, frequently overexpressed at mitochondrial contact sites, inhibits cytochrome c release and confers apoptosis resistance; its suppression by Rabeprazole in gastric epithelium or by monoamine oxidase A (MAOA) in lung adenocarcinoma triggers cell-cycle arrest [68].

Beyond energy provision, glycolytic metabolites act as signaling effectors. Lactate secreted by ectopic endometrial stromal cells induces M2 macrophage polarization, creating an immunosuppressive niche that sustains lesion proliferation [69]. Enzymes such as phosphoglycerate kinase 1 (PGK1) moonlight as transcriptional co-activators to reinforce pro-proliferative gene expression [19]. Major proliferative pathways—including PI3K/AKT, AMPK/SIRT3, and RhoA/ROCK/AKT—are intimately coupled to glycolytic flux: aspirin activates AMPK/SIRT3 to repress HK2 and curb non-small-cell lung cancer growth [70], P3H4 drives hepatocellular carcinoma proliferation via PI3K/AKT-mediated glycolysis [71], and SIRT7 activates RhoA/ROCK/AKT signaling in endometriosis to link mitochondrial function with glycolytic reprogramming and stromal cell expansion [35].

Thus, glycolysis promotes proliferation not merely as an energy pathway but as an integrated signaling hub that coordinates substrate supply, cell-cycle progression, apoptosis resistance, and microenvironmental remodeling. Disruption of these nodes—through inhibition of key enzymes or regulatory kinases—represents a powerful strategy to restrain aberrant cell growth in endometriosis and malignancy.

Glycolysis drives migration and invasion in endometriosis

Aerobic glycolysis fuels cell migration and invasion by supplying localized ATP for cytoskeletal dynamics, generating biosynthetic intermediates for membrane protrusion, and producing lactate as a potent signaling molecule. Lactate acidifies the peritoneal microenvironment, activating matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2 and MMP-9) that degrade ECM barriers while triggering PI3K/AKT and RhoA/ROCK pathways to promote actin polymerization, focal adhesion turnover, and invadopodia formation [72–74]. Glycolytic enzymes themselves contribute directly: PKM2 stabilizes β-catenin to induce EMT [75], LDHA sustains high lactate output, and HK2 supports energy-intensive membrane ruffling [76–78]. Lactate also drives histone lactylation, epigenetically upregulating migration- and invasion-related genes in ectopic endometrial cells [30]. In endometriosis, upregulation of HK2, PFKFB3, and LDHA markedly enhances stromal cell motility and invasiveness, effects that are reversed by glycolytic inhibitors such as 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) [36, 79, 80]. Secreted lactate further amplifies invasion indirectly by polarizing macrophages toward the M2 phenotype, which secretes pro-angiogenic and tissue-remodeling factors that facilitate lesion implantation and peritoneal attachment [50, 69].

Thus, the glycolysis–lactate axis orchestrates a coordinated program of cytoskeletal remodeling, ECM degradation, EMT induction, and microenvironmental modulation that collectively drives ectopic endometrial cell dissemination. Inhibition of key glycolytic nodes or lactate signaling therefore represents a promising strategy to restrict the migratory and invasive behavior central to endometriosis progression.

Glycolysis promotes EMT in endometriosis

Glycolysis actively drives epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) in endometriosis, with PFKFB3 serving as a central node. Overexpressed PFKFB3 accelerates glycolytic flux by elevating fructose-2,6-bisphosphate levels and directly binds β-catenin, shielding it from proteasomal degradation and promoting its nuclear accumulation. This stabilization activates Wnt target genes that suppress epithelial markers while inducing mesenchymal ones, markedly enhancing stromal cell migration and invasion. Pharmacological inhibition of PFKFB3 with PFK-015 disrupts glycolysis, reduces β-catenin levels, reverses EMT marker expression, and suppresses lesion growth in preclinical models [16]. Broader glycolytic reprogramming reinforces this transition. Upregulation of HK2, PKM2, and glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) sustains high glucose uptake and lactate production, creating a metabolic milieu that favors mesenchymal phenotypes [81–83]. Lactate itself functions as an epigenetic modifier through histone lactylation (e.g., H3K18la), upregulating EMT transcription factors such as SIX1 and KLF5 and perpetuating the mesenchymal state [84, 85]. In parallel, increased histone acetylation at EMT-related gene promoters further reinforces this transition by enhancing the transcriptional activity of β-catenin and HIF-1α, creating a cooperative epigenetic network that links glycolytic flux to sustained mesenchymal reprogramming [86, 87]. Hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), stabilized in the hypoxic peritoneal environment, further amplify both glycolysis and EMT by inducing glycolytic enzymes and mesenchymal regulators, establishing a self-reinforcing loop [81, 82]. Inhibition of glycolysis with 2-DG or targeted silencing of key enzymes reverses EMT markers and impairs invasiveness, confirming the functional dependence of the mesenchymal phenotype on sustained glycolytic activity. Thus, the glycolysis–EMT axis, orchestrated by PFKFB3 and amplified by lactate-mediated epigenetic remodeling, is a major driver of ectopic endometrial cell plasticity and lesion progression. Targeting this metabolic–phenotypic linkage, particularly through PFKFB3 inhibition, offers a compelling non-hormonal strategy to block EMT and limit disease dissemination. Building upon these direct effects on endometriotic cell behavior, enhanced glycolytic flux and lactate accumulation also profoundly reshape the local immune microenvironment, creating a permissive immunosuppressive niche that further sustains lesion survival and progression.

Glycolytic metabolism and immune regulation in endometriosis

Lactate orchestrates macrophage polarization and immune suppression

Lactate, abundantly produced by glycolytic ectopic endometrial cells, acts as both a metabolic fuel and signaling molecule to drive macrophage polarization toward the immunosuppressive M2 phenotype, thereby creating a permissive microenvironment for lesion survival and growth. Mechanistically, lactate is taken up by macrophages via monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1) and aquaporin 9 (AQP9), supporting TCA cycle anaplerosis and ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY)-dependent histone acetylation that licenses M2 gene expression [88–90]. Exogenous lactate rescues defective M2 polarization when mitochondrial pyruvate import or ACLY is blocked, confirming its non-redundant role [88]. Simultaneously, lactate engages surface receptors such as G protein-coupled receptor 81 (GPR81) and GPR132 to suppress TLR4/NF-κB and activate cAMP/PKA/AMPK signaling, dampening proinflammatory M1 responses [91, 92].

Epigenetically, lactate induces pan-histone and non-histone protein lactylation, directly promoting transcription of M2 signature genes and immunosuppressive cytokines in tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) [93, 94]. Notably, lactate also enhances histone acetylation through acetyl-CoA generation, further licensing M2 gene expression programs and reinforcing the immunosuppressive niche in endometriotic lesions [30]. This modification is observed across diverse pathologies: in breast cancer, high lactate levels correlate with M2 dominance and reduced M1 markers [95]; in colitis and pancreatitis, lactate accelerates resolution by shifting macrophages toward repair phenotypes [96, 97]; and in myocardial infarction, sodium lactate enhances STAT3-dependent M2 polarization to improve healing [98]. Therapeutic disruption of lactate signaling reverses immunosuppression. Inhibitors of LDHA, MCTs, or GPR81 reduce M2 polarization and enhance antitumor immunity [99, 100]. Emerging strategies, including lactate-metabolizing photosynthetic bacteria and oxygen-generating nanoparticles that deplete lactate while delivering sonodynamic therapy, reprogram the immune microenvironment and synergize with checkpoint blockade [101, 102].

In endometriosis, the high-lactate milieu generated by glycolytic stromal cells mirrors tumor conditions, sustaining M2 macrophages that foster immune evasion, fibrosis, and lesion persistence. Targeting lactate production, transport, or downstream epigenetic effects therefore offers a powerful approach to restore immune surveillance and limit disease progression.

Glycolysis-related genes shape immune infiltration in endometriosis

Glycolysis-related genes profoundly influence immune cell composition and function in endometriotic lesions, creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment that sustains disease progression. Machine-learning analysis of public datasets has identified five hub genes—CHPF, CITED2, glypican-3 (GPC3), pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 3 (PDK3), and alcohol dehydrogenase 6 (ADH6)—as robust diagnostic markers for endometriosis. Using the CIBERSORT algorithm, expression of these genes strongly correlates with altered infiltration of 22 immune cell types, particularly increased M2 macrophages and reduced cytotoxic lymphocytes compared with healthy endometrium [55]. In the follicular fluid of infertile patients with endometriosis, elevated PDK3 and GPC3 alongside reduced ADH6 levels associate with poorer oocyte yield and lower clinical pregnancy rates, linking glycolytic dysregulation to impaired mitochondrial function and excessive immune activation in the ovarian niche [56]. At the tissue level, hypoxia-driven glycolysis in ectopic stromal cells promotes M2 macrophage polarization through the PDPK1/CD47/AKT axis, which upregulates LDHA, boosts glucose uptake, and increases lactate secretion. This metabolic shift sustains an anti-inflammatory, pro-fibrotic immune milieu that favors lesion survival. Blocking PDPK1, CD47, or LDHA markedly reduces M2 infiltration and lesion burden in preclinical models, confirming the functional importance of this glycolysis–immune crosstalk [50].

Together, these findings reveal that glycolysis-related genes orchestrate immune infiltration patterns in both peritoneal lesions and the ovarian microenvironment, driving immunosuppression and contributing to pain, progression, and infertility. Targeting these metabolic–immune interactions offers a promising avenue for novel diagnostics and therapies aimed at restoring immune homeostasis in endometriosis.

Immune-metabolic diagnostic models for endometriosis

The fusion of glycolysis-related and immune gene signatures has markedly improved non-invasive diagnosis of endometriosis, overcoming the limitations of imaging and laparoscopy. Machine-learning approaches, applied to large transcriptomic datasets, have identified robust biomarker panels that capture the disease’s hallmark metabolic-immune crosstalk. For example, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) combined with supervised algorithms isolated lactate-related differentially expressed genes (LR-DEGs) such as bisphosphoglycerate mutase (BPGM), dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), and solute carrier family 25 member 13 (SLC25A13). Nomograms built on these genes achieve excellent diagnostic accuracy while reflecting immune infiltration status, with high expression linked to M2 macrophage enrichment and T-cell suppression [103]. Similar models integrating glycolysis, lipid metabolism, and ferroptosis pathways have pinpointed hub genes (e.g., HMGCR and CYP27A1) whose expression strongly correlates with immune cell fractions and clinical severity [5, 104]. Cross-disease comparisons further reveal distinct immune-metabolic profiles that separate endometriosis from ovarian cancer, dominated by M2 polarization and oxidative stress signatures [105]. These signatures are detectable in peripheral blood and endometrial biopsies, enabling minimally invasive testing. Clinically, elevated lactate metabolism genes accompany chronic stress-induced immune escape [106] and myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) expansion [107], reinforcing the pathogenic relevance of the identified markers. Incorporating gut microbiota-derived signals—which modulate estrogen and systemic immunity—promises to further refine model performance [108, 109].

Collectively, immune-metabolic diagnostic models offer high sensitivity and specificity for early detection, reduce diagnostic delay, and illuminate targetable pathways. By quantifying the interplay between glycolytic reprogramming and immune dysregulation, these tools not only guide clinical management but also pave the way for precision therapies aimed at restoring immune homeostasis in endometriosis. These intertwined metabolic and immunological alterations provide a strong mechanistic rationale for exploring glycolysis as a novel, non-hormonal therapeutic target. Exploring glycolysis as a novel target for endometriosis intervention

Therapeutic application of glycolysis inhibitors in endometriosis

Glycolysis inhibitors have emerged as effective non-hormonal agents for endometriosis by disrupting the Warburg-like metabolism that sustains ectopic lesion growth. 2-Deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG), a competitive hexokinase inhibitor, potently suppresses proliferation, migration, and invasion of ectopic endometrial stromal cells and markedly reduces lesion size in preclinical models, including those driven by ALKBH5-mediated HK2 upregulation or S1PR4/mTOR signaling [36, 44]. Targeted delivery systems further enhance efficacy: neutrophil-hitchhiking, ROS-responsive liposomes co-loaded with 2-DG and chloroquine (cLipo-DC) exploit the inflammatory peritoneal milieu to selectively release cargo, simultaneously blocking glycolysis and autophagy while inducing substantial lesion regression without systemic toxicity [110]. Enzyme-specific inhibitors also show strong promise. PFK-015, a selective PFKFB3 blocker, destabilizes β-catenin, reverses EMT, and curbs stromal cell invasiveness [16]; the HSF1 inhibitor KRIBB11 suppresses PFKFB3 transcription and lesion progression [27]; and PKM2 antagonists such as compound 3k and cinnamic acid impair viability and glycolytic flux in primary endometriotic cells [111]. Natural compounds further broaden the arsenal: ursolic acid from Prunella vulgaris and resveratrol inhibit LDHA and downregulate glucose transporters (GLUT1/3) and monocarboxylate transporters (MCT1/4), reducing lactate export and neovascularization [24, 112].

These agents consistently attenuate proliferation, invasion, and angiogenesis across cell-based and animal studies, confirming the central dependence of ectopic endometrium on sustained glycolysis. However, it is crucial to note that while these results are highly promising, most of these interventions remain in the preclinical stage, and managing clinical expectations regarding their immediate availability is necessary. Nevertheless, combining direct glycolytic blockade with inflammation-responsive delivery or autophagy inhibition amplifies therapeutic impact, offering a viable path toward effective, well-tolerated, non-hormonal treatments with reduced recurrence risk.

Natural products as regulators of glycolysis

Natural compounds offer a compelling, low-toxicity approach to suppress the enhanced aerobic glycolysis that sustains ectopic endometrial cells in endometriosis. Extracts of Prunella vulgaris and its major triterpenoid ursolic acid inhibit LDHA, reducing lactate production and triggering apoptosis in endometriotic stromal cells by disrupting redox balance and energy supply, mirroring their established anti-glycolytic effects in multiple cancer models [113]. Resveratrol, licochalcone A, brusatol, and cryptolepine similarly target key nodes—HK2, PKM2, LDHA, and HIF-1α—downregulating glycolytic enzyme expression, lowering ATP levels, and shifting metabolism away from the Warburg phenotype [113–115]. Quercetin, by suppressing HIF-1α and downstream glycolytic genes, attenuates lactate-driven fibrosis and inflammation in liver disease, suggesting parallel benefits in the inflammatory peritoneal milieu of endometriosis [116].

These agents frequently act upstream of glycolysis by blocking HIF-1α stabilization, a critical driver of glycolytic reprogramming under the hypoxic conditions prevalent in endometriotic lesions [114]. Their multi-target profiles also dampen inflammatory signaling and oxidative stress, further compromising the survival advantage conferred by aerobic glycolysis [117]. Although direct mechanistic studies in endometriosis remain limited, the consistent suppression of LDHA, HK2, PKM2, and HIF-1α across tumor and inflammatory models strongly supports their translational potential. Natural-product-derived inhibitors thus represent a promising non-hormonal strategy to exploit the metabolic vulnerability of ectopic endometrium, warranting focused preclinical validation in endometriosis-specific systems.

Specific inhibitors targeting PFKFB3, PFKFB4, and PKM2

Highly selective inhibitors of key glycolytic enzymes have shown strong preclinical efficacy in endometriosis by disrupting the metabolic reprogramming that drives lesion growth and invasion. PFKFB3, which sustains elevated fructose-2,6-bisphosphate and activates PFK-1, is overexpressed in ectopic tissues and promotes EMT through β-catenin stabilization; its blockade with PFK-015 sharply reduces proliferation, migration, and lesion burden [16]. PFKFB4, similarly upregulated, is kept in check by CHIP-mediated ubiquitination at K305—pharmacological enhancement of this degradation or direct inhibition with 5-MPN potently suppresses glycolysis and impairs invasiveness in cell and animal models [57]. PKM2, the terminal glycolytic enzyme, is stabilized and activated by PAK5-dependent phosphorylation at Ser519, fueling anaerobic metabolism and stromal cell aggressiveness. Genetic deletion of PAK5 or treatment with the selective inhibitor GNE-2861 abolishes this phosphorylation, curtails glycolytic flux, and halts lesion progression in mice [17]. Complementary strategies targeting HK2 and LDHA—whose downregulation triggers STAT1 phosphorylation or apoptosis via energy crisis—further reinforce the therapeutic value of glycolytic blockade [24, 26]. Lessons from oncology, where PFKFB3, PFKFB4, and PKM2 inhibitors overcome resistance and sensitize cells to photodynamic therapy, strongly support their repurposing for endometriosis [118–120].

These agents act not only by starving cells of ATP and biosynthetic precursors but also by dismantling oncogenic signaling (β-catenin, STAT1 modulation) central to endometriotic behavior. When combined with dual inhibitors of steroid sulfatase and 17β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 1, which curb local estrogen production, glycolytic blockade offers a powerful non-hormonal, multi-pronged approach with potential for durable lesion control [121, 122]. Continued optimization of potency, selectivity, and delivery will be key to translating these inhibitors into clinically viable endometriosis therapies.

Integrated metabolic and signaling pathway interventions

Endometriosis progression is driven by a tightly coupled network of aerobic glycolysis and multiple dysregulated signaling pathways (including mTOR, ERβ, RhoA/ROCK/AKT, and STAT1/PDCD1). These pathways collectively sustain proliferation, invasion, fibrosis, and immune escape. Targeting glycolysis alone (e.g., HK2 knockdown, PKM2 inhibition, or LDHA blockade) impairs energy supply and stromal cell aggressiveness [17, 19, 24, 26], yet maximal efficacy likely requires simultaneous disruption of the signaling circuits that drive and reinforce the glycolytic phenotype.

ERβ overexpression shifts metabolism from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis while conferring progesterone resistance; co-inhibition with glycolytic blockers could restore hormonal sensitivity and metabolic stress [22]. Hyperactive mTOR integrates nutrient sensing with glycolytic enzyme transcription and hypoxic adaptation; combining mTOR inhibitors with 2-DG or PFKFB3 antagonists markedly suppresses lesion growth beyond either strategy alone [15]. SIRT7 exemplifies this crosstalk by boosting glycolytic gene expression while activating RhoA/ROCK/AKT signaling—dual targeting of SIRT7 and downstream metabolic nodes therefore disrupts both arms of the axis [35]. Similarly, PAK5-mediated PKM2 phosphorylation links cytoskeletal dynamics to glycolytic flux, and blocking PAK5 alongside PKM2 inhibition halts proliferation and lesion progression [17]. Natural compounds such as Prunella vulgaris extract and ursolic acid already achieve modest multi-target effects by inhibiting LDHA, reducing mitochondrial ROS, and triggering apoptosis, suggesting that rationally designed phytochemical derivatives could hit both metabolic and signaling vulnerabilities [24]. Restoring CD8+ T cell function through combined reversal of stromal-induced glycolysis and STAT1/PDCD1 exhaustion further amplifies immune-mediated clearance [6].

This integrated metabolic–signaling strategy may exploit therapeutic synergy: glycolytic inhibition starves cells of ATP and precursors, while concurrent pathway blockade prevents compensatory survival signals. Such combination therapies promise greater potency, lower recurrence rates, and reduced reliance on hormonal suppression compared with current standards, warranting accelerated preclinical testing of drug pairs targeting glycolysis together with mTOR, ERβ, PAK5, or SIRT7 axes.

Emerging non-hormonal therapeutic directions

The shortcomings of hormonal therapies—side effects, contraceptive constraints, and high recurrence—have accelerated the search for non-hormonal alternatives that exploit the unique biology of endometriotic lesions without disrupting systemic endocrine balance. Targeting aerobic glycolysis, a hallmark metabolic vulnerability shared with cancer cells, offers one compelling route: selective inhibition of HK2, PFKFB3, PKM2, or LDHA deprives ectopic cells of ATP and biosynthetic precursors while leaving normal endometrium largely unaffected. Beyond pure metabolic blockade, several mechanistically distinct non-hormonal approaches have reached preclinical or early clinical validation. Antioxidants such as resveratrol and polydatin attenuate ROS-driven inflammatory signaling and pain by restoring redox homeostasis, indirectly dampening glycolysis-promoting pathways [123]. Dopamine agonists (e.g., cabergoline) inhibit VEGF-mediated angiogenesis and match hormonal agents in pelvic pain reduction [124]. Selective receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors like pexidartinib (targeting CSF1R and KIT) suppress JNK, STAT3, and AKT signaling, reducing lesion inflammation and viability [125]. Blocking the nociceptor-derived calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP)/receptor activity-modifying protein 1 (RAMP1) axis shrinks lesions and relieves pain by disrupting neuroimmune crosstalk [126], while extracellular ATP triggers pyroptosis in endometriotic epithelial cells and boosts macrophage clearance [127]. Epigenetic and nuclear receptor strategies also show promise. EZH2 inhibition halts vesicle proliferation in rat models [128], dual NR4A1/NR4A2 antagonists curb fibrosis and growth [129], and the natural ERβ-selective ligand oleuropein suppresses lesions while preserving fertility [130]. These agents achieve disease control without broad estrogen suppression, avoiding menopausal symptoms and bone loss. Combining metabolic inhibitors with these non-hormonal modalities—e.g., glycolysis blockade plus RTK, CGRP/RAMP1, or selective ERβ antagonism—could yield synergistic lesion regression and durable symptom relief. Importantly, targeting the metabolic memory established by sustained glycolytic reprogramming may represent a novel strategy to reduce the high postoperative recurrence rate that remains a major clinical challenge in endometriosis. Large, stratified clinical trials and multi-omics patient profiling are now needed to match specific non-hormonal regimens to lesion subtypes and move these targeted, metabolism-aware therapies into routine practice.

Glycolytic reprogramming stands as a central driver of endometriosis pathogenesis, extending far beyond simple energy production to orchestrate immune tolerance, sustained proliferation, invasion, and lesion persistence in the peritoneal microenvironment. Figure 1 provides a schematic overview of the glycolytic reprogramming mechanisms in endometriosis, integrating upstream regulators, key glycolytic enzymes, and downstream effects. Preclinical evidence consistently demonstrates that disrupting this glycolytic circuitry—through specific enzyme inhibitors (2-DG, PFK-015, 5-MPN, PAK5 blockers), natural compounds (ursolic acid, resveratrol), or inflammation-responsive delivery systems—markedly impairs lesion growth, EMT, and M2 macrophage polarization while restoring CD8+ T cell function. Combining metabolic blockade with targeted modulation of intersecting pathways (mTOR, ERβ, RhoA/ROCK/AKT) or immune checkpoints amplifies efficacy and offers a clear path toward synergistic, non-hormonal regimens with lower recurrence risk than current hormonal standards. Emerging immune-metabolic diagnostic models, built on glycolysis- and lactate-related gene signatures, already achieve high accuracy in distinguishing patients from controls and predicting reproductive outcomes, paving the way for precision stratification and early intervention.

Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the regulatory mechanisms and downstream consequences of glycolytic reprogramming in endometriosis. The diagram depicts the multi-layered regulation of the Warburg effect in ectopic endometrial lesions. (Left Panel) Upstream regulators—including ALKBH5, PAK5, HSF1, PIM2, CHIP, AURKA, SIRT7, and S1PR4—modulate metabolic reprogramming through diverse molecular mechanisms. These include N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification, protein phosphorylation, transcriptional upregulation, ubiquitination, and the activation of signaling axes such as ERβ, RhoA/ROCK/AKT, and mTOR. (Middle Panel) These upstream factors converge to regulate the stability and activity of key glycolytic enzymes: Hexokinase 2 (HK2), Pyruvate Kinase M2 (PKM2), 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase 3/4 (PFKFB3/4), and Lactate Dehydrogenase A (LDHA). (Right Panel) The resulting enhancement of glycolytic flux (Glycolysis) and enzyme activity drives critical downstream pathological effects, including Epithelial–Mesenchymal Transition (EMT), remodeling of the immune microenvironment, and the promotion of cellular proliferation, invasion, and migration, collectively sustaining endometriosis progression

Future directions

Building on these advances, future research must prioritize the identification of specific patient subgroups that are most likely to derive clinical benefit from targeted metabolic therapies, thereby advancing precision medicine in endometriosis. Furthermore, glycolytic reprogramming may establish a form of “metabolic memory” in ectopic lesions through persistent histone lactylation and other epigenetic modifications on key glycolytic genes (e.g., HK2, PFKFB3, PKM2), potentially driving postoperative recurrence even after macroscopic lesion removal. Prioritizing clinical translation of these glycolytic inhibitors—alone and in rational combinations—while deepening mechanistic insight into lactate-driven epigenetic and immune effects will be essential. The integration of emerging technologies, such as single-cell spatial transcriptomics and spatial metabolomics, will be pivotal in resolving intra-lesional glycolytic heterogeneity and mapping precise metabolic-immune crosstalk within specific microdomains. Such efforts promise to shift endometriosis management from broad hormonal suppression to targeted metabolic-immune reprogramming, delivering durable lesion control, preserved fertility, and improved quality of life for millions of affected women.
