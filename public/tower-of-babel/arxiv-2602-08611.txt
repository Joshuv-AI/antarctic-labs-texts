Representation theory of inhomogeneous Gaussian unitaries
Jingqi Sun, Joshua Combes, Lucas Hackl

Abstract
Gaussian unitaries, generated by quadratic Hamiltonians, are fundamental in quantum optics and continuous-variable computing. Their structures correspond to symplectic (bosons) and orthogonal (fermions) groups, but physical realizations give rise to their respective double covers, introducing phase and sign ambiguities. The homogeneous (quadratic-only) case has been resolved through a parameterization constructed in a recent work [arXiv:2409.11628]. We extend the previous framework to inhomogeneous Gaussian unitaries parameterized by ( M , z , Ψ ) (M,z,\Psi) . The Baker-Campbel-Hausdorff formula allows us then to factor any Gaussian unitary into a squeezing and a displacement transformation, from which we derive the group multiplication law.

Introduction

Gaussian states are quantum states whose Wigner functions take a Gaussian form in phase space. They play a foundational role in quantum optics

and continuous-variable quantum information

;

;
Aaronson and Arkhipov 2013
. Early works by Holevo and Werner clarified the mathematical structure of Gaussian states and channels

;

, while more recent developments have explored their use in quantum simulation, control, and communication

;

.

Beyond practical applications, recent research has highlighted the geometric and phase properties of Gaussian unitaries, particularly through the lens of the symplectic and metaplectic groups
Dereziński and Gérard 2023
;
Woit 2017a
;
Simon and Mukunda 1993
;
Folland 1989
;

, linked to structures such as the Maslov index and semiclassical analysis

;

. Previous works derived closed-form expressions of the double cover group multiplication rule using phase space formalism for bosons in

Dias and König 2024

More recently, Quesada developed methods for computing vacuum-to-vacuum amplitudes in quadratic bosonic evolutions, offering new analytical techniques for phase characterization in bosonic quantum dynamics

.

A system of
N
bosons can be characterized by their

linear observables

\displaystyle\hat{\xi}^{a}

\displaystyle\equiv(\underbrace{\hat{q}_{1},\cdots,\hat{q}_{N}}_{\text{position}},\underbrace{\hat{p}_{1},\cdots,\hat{p}_{N}}_{\text{momentum}})\,,

(1)

also known as quadrature operators, which we collect in a single vector
\hat{\xi}^{a}
. A general quadratic Hamiltonian (GQH) for bosons takes the form

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}h_{ab}\hat{\xi}^{a}\hat{\xi}^{b}}_{\text{quadratic}}+\underbrace{f_{a}\hat{\xi}^{a}}_{\text{linear}}+\underbrace{c\mathbb{1}}_{\text{scalar}}\,.

Its exponential

;
Tara et al. 1993

;
Sachs et al. 2017

as operators acting on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. The previous
Guaita et al. 2024
that we build on characterized ‘homogeneous’ Gaussian unitaries, namely those generated by purely quadratic Hamiltonians (PQH) where the linear term

in (
9
) vanishes.

In this manuscript, we define the complex phase

of

with respect to a Gaussian state

as
\Psi^{*}=\braket{J|\mathcal{U}|J}/|\braket{J|\mathcal{U}|J}|

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(M_{1},z_{1},\Psi_{1})\,\mathcal{U}(M_{2},z_{2},\Psi_{2})=\mathcal{U}(M_{3},z_{3},\Psi_{3})\,.

(4)

Where
M_{3}=M_{1}M_{2}
and

3

\displaystyle\Psi_{3}=\Psi_{1}\Psi_{2}e^{\mathrm{i}\zeta(M_{1},M_{2},z_{1},z_{2})}

\zeta
is the

which tracks the phases of unitary product. Furthermore, we show how deduce

from
\hat{H}

\det{M}=-1

This manuscript is structured as follows: In Section

II.2
Homogeneous Gaussian unitaries

Here, we summarize the key results of
Guaita et al. 2024
;
Hackl and Bianchi 2021a
concerning
homogeneous
Gaussian unitaries, generated by PHQ. This will be required to generalize the construction to inhomogeneous unitaries, generated by GQH, which also include displacements.

II.2.1
Squeezing operators

In the following, we show that PQHs form a faithful representation of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. However, their exponentials realize only a projective representation of the Lie group
\mathcal{G}
because of phase ambiguities in the group composition. To obtain a true (linear) representation, we must instead consider the double cover group
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
.

We consider the space generated by PQHs in the form

\displaystyle\widehat{K}=-\mathrm{i}\hat{H}=\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_{ac}K^{c}{}_{b}\hat{\xi}^{a}\hat{\xi}^{b}\,,

(11)

where
K^{c}{}_{b}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{i}}\Lambda^{ca}h_{ab}
lies in the respective Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. Using (
6
) and BCH, one finds the relation

\displaystyle[\widehat{K}_{1},\widehat{K}_{2}]

\displaystyle=\widehat{[K_{1},K_{2}]}\,,

(12)

\displaystyle e^{-\widehat{K}}\hat{\xi}^{a}e^{\widehat{K}}

\displaystyle=(e^{K})^{a}{}_{b}\hat{\xi}^{b}\,.

(13)

The first equation demonstrates that
\widehat{K}
forms a Lie algebra
representation, while the second line shows that all
e^{\widehat{K}}
generate a (potentially projective) representation of the Lie group
\mathcal{G}
.

Why we need the double cover:
For a single bosonic or fermionic degree of freedom, we consider

\displaystyle K=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix},\,\,\widehat{K}=\begin{cases}{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}(\hat{q}^{2}+\hat{p}^{2})}&\textbf{(bosons)}\\
{+\hat{q}\hat{p}}&\textbf{(fermions)}\end{cases}

(14)

corresponding to
\widehat{K}=-\mathrm{i}(\hat{n}\pm\frac{1}{2})
, where
\hat{n}
has spectrum
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
for bosons (
+
) and
\{0,1\}
for fermions (
-
). To connect the algebra element
K
with its group action, we exponentiate both generators. This yields the classical symplectic transformation and its quantum counterpart

\displaystyle\hskip-7.11317ptM(t)\!=\!e^{tK}\!=\!\begin{pmatrix}\cos{t}&\sin{t}\\
-\!\sin{t}&\cos{t}\end{pmatrix},\,\mathcal{S}(t)\!=\!e^{t\widehat{K}}\!=\!e^{-\mathrm{i}t(\hat{n}\pm\frac{1}{2})}.

(15)

Here
\mathcal{S}(t)=e^{t\widehat{K}}
denotes the unitary operator generated by the quadratic Hamiltonian
\widehat{K}
, representing the quantum analogue of the classical symplectic transformation
M(t)=e^{tK}
. While
M(t)
is a rotation in the plane and clearly
2\pi
-periodic, we find
\mathcal{S}(2\pi)=-\mathbb{1}
, so this unitary operator is
4\pi
-periodic.

The same argument also works for several degrees of freedom
Guaita et al. 2024
and establishes that for each group element
M\in\mathcal{G}
, there exist two unitaries
\pm\mathcal{S}(M)
in the set generated by all
e^{\widehat{K}}
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}(M)\hat{\xi}^{a}\mathcal{S}(M)=M^{a}{}_{b}\hat{\xi}^{b}\,.

(16)

Note that this equation does not fix the complex phase or sign of
\mathcal{S}(M)
.

II.2.2
Reference complex phase and Gaussian states

We have just seen that
M\in\mathcal{G}
in (
16
) determines a given unitary only up to a complex phase. In order to compare different such unitaries, we somehow need to assign a reference complex phase. We will do this by fixing a reference quantum state
\ket{J}
and then associating to each Gaussian unitary
\mathcal{U}
the complex phase of the expectation value
\braket{J|\mathcal{U}|J}
, provided it does not vanish.

We choose this reference quantum state to be a so-called Gaussian state that is fully determined by a complex structure
J:V\to V
satisfying the following properties: (i)
J^{2}=-\mathbb{1}
, (ii)
J\Lambda J^{\intercal}=\Lambda
and (iii) for bosons we require
-J\Omega
to be positive-definite. These conditions can be encoded in the 2-out-of-3 property via

\displaystyle G\omega=-J\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad J=\Omega g\,,

(17)

where we introduce the missing structure
G
for bosons and
\Omega
for fermions by solving (
17
). The conditions (i)-(iii) for
J
to define a Gaussian state
\ket{J}
is then equivalent to requiring that
G
is a positive-definite metric for bosons and
\Omega
a symplectic form for fermions, respectively. The triple
(\Omega,G,J)
is called Kähler structure and there always exists a basis, such that their matrix forms are

\displaystyle\Omega=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}0&\mathbb{1}\\
\hline\cr-\mathbb{1}&0\end{array}\right)\,,\,G=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathbb{1}&0\\
\hline\cr 0&\mathbb{1}\end{array}\right)\,,\,J=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}0&\mathbb{1}\\
\hline\cr-\mathbb{1}&0\end{array}\right)\,.

Given such
J
, we define
\ket{J}
as any normalized solution to the equation

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}(\delta^{a}{}_{b}+\mathrm{i}J^{a}{}_{b})\hat{\xi}^{b}\ket{J}=0\,,

(24)

which one can show to determine
\ket{J}
uniquely up to a complex phase
Hackl and Bianchi 2021b
. For any operator
\mathcal{A}
we can then introduce the
reference complex phase

\displaystyle\Phi_{J}[\mathcal{A}]=\frac{\braket{J|\mathcal{A}|J}}{|\braket{J|\mathcal{A}|J}|}\,,

(25)

provided
\braket{J|\mathcal{A}|J}\neq 0
. Note that this is invariant under changing the complex phase of
\ket{J}
.

With respect to
J
, we can always decompose a linear map
K:V\to V
into its linear part
C_{M}
and anti-linear part
D_{M}
, as

\displaystyle C_{M}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}(M-JMJ)\,,

(26a)

\displaystyle D_{M}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}(M+JMJ)\,,

(26b)

such that
M=C_{M}+D_{M}
and satisfies
[C_{M},J]=0
and
\{D_{M},J\}=0
. If
C_{M}
is invertible, we can also define

\displaystyle Z_{M}=C_{M}^{-1}D_{M}=\frac{\mathbb{1}-\Delta_{M^{-1}}}{\mathbb{1}+\Delta_{M^{-1}}}\quad\!\!\text{with}\!\!\quad\Delta_{M}=-MJM^{-1}J\,.

(27)

Given
K^{a}{}_{b}
with
[K,J]=0
, we further define

\displaystyle\overline{\operatorname{Tr}}(K)=\operatorname{Tr}(\overline{K})\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\det}(K)=\det(\overline{K})\,,

(28)

where we decompose
K=K_{1}+\mathrm{i}K_{2}
as

\displaystyle K\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}K_{1}&K_{2}\\
\hline\cr-K_{2}&K_{1}\end{array}\right)

in an arbitrary basis, where
J
takes the standard form (
II.2.2
). While
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
depend on the specific basis, (
28
) is basis-independent
Guaita et al. 2024
.

II.2.3
Circle function and cocycles

Result 1 of
Guaita et al. 2024
showed that for a given
\mathcal{S}(M)
, we have

\displaystyle\braket{J|\mathcal{S}(M)|J}^{2}=\overline{\det}\left(\frac{M-JMJ}{2}\right)^{\mathbb{i}^{2}}\,,

(31)

This can be rephrased as
\Phi_{J}[\mathcal{S}(M)]^{2}=\varphi(M)
, where we introduce the circle function
\varphi:\mathcal{G}\to\mathrm{U}(1)
with

\displaystyle\varphi(M)=\frac{\overline{\det}(C_{M})}{|\overline{\det}(C_{M})|}\,.

(32)

We can now compare the phase
\Phi_{J}(\mathcal{S}(M_{1}M_{2}))
with
\Phi_{J}(\mathcal{S}(M_{1}))
and
\Phi_{J}(\mathcal{S}(M_{2}))
by first looking at their respective squares. This motivates the relation

\displaystyle e^{\mathrm{i}\eta(M_{1},M_{2})}=\frac{\Phi^{2}_{J}(\mathcal{S}(M_{1}M_{2}))}{\Phi^{2}_{J}(\mathcal{S}(M_{1}))\Phi^{2}_{J}(\mathcal{S}(M_{2}))}=\frac{\varphi(M_{1}M_{2})}{\varphi(M_{1})\varphi(M_{2})}\,,

(33)

which defines the cocycle function
\eta:\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}\to\mathrm{U}(1)
uniquely by requiring
\eta
to be continuous and satisfy
\eta(\mathbb{1},\mathbb{1})=0
. It can be computed explicitly as

\displaystyle\eta(M_{1},M_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Im}\,\overline{\mathrm{Tr}}\log\big(\mathbb{1}-Z_{M_{1}}Z_{M_{2}^{-1}}\big)\,.

(34)

II.2.4
Parametrization

The above facts imply that the squeezing operators actually form a representation of the double cover
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
of the Lie group
\mathcal{G}
. This representation is either infinite-dimensional (bosons) or
2^{N}
-dimensional (fermions), so it is desirable to describe the multiplication law in
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
using smaller matrices, such as the elements of
\mathcal{G}
.

The idea of
Guaita et al. 2024
building on
Rawnsley 2012
is to construct the double cover as the subset

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}=\{(M,\psi)\mid\varphi(M)=\psi^{2}\}\subset\mathcal{G}\times\mathrm{U}(1)\,,

(35)

with the circle function
\varphi
from (
32
). For each
M\in\mathcal{G}
, there exist thus two elements
(M,\pm\sqrt{\varphi(M)})
in the double cover with the natural projection
\pi:\widetilde{G}\to\mathcal{G}
, such that
\pi(M,\psi)=M
.

It is shown in
Guaita et al. 2024
that the group multiplication can be efficiently described as

\displaystyle(M_{1},\psi_{1})\cdot(M_{2},\psi_{2})=(M_{1}\cdot M_{2},\psi_{1}\psi_{2}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\eta(M_{1},M_{2})})\,.

(36)

The operator
S(M,\psi)
represents the uniquely defined group element
(M,\psi)
in the double cover
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
, implementing the symplectic transformation
M
while fixing its global phase relative to the reference state
\ket{J}
. The squeezing operator
\mathcal{S}(M,\psi)
is uniquely characterized by requiring

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}(M,\psi)\hat{\xi}^{a}\mathcal{S}(M,\psi)

\displaystyle=M^{a}{}_{b}\hat{\xi}^{b}\,,

(37a)

\displaystyle\Phi_{J}[\mathcal{S}(M,\psi)]

\displaystyle=\psi^{*}\,.

(37b)

As a proper representation, we consequently have

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(M_{1},\psi_{1})\cdot\mathcal{S}(M_{2},\psi_{2})=\mathcal{S}(M_{1}\cdot M_{2},\psi_{1}\psi_{2}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\eta(M_{1},M_{2})})\,.

(38)

II.2.5
Cartan decomposition

Tracking the phase effect of composing displacements and squeezings is the prerequisite to deduct the complex phase and multiplication laws of an inhomogeneous Gaussian unitary. Given a Gaussian state
\ket{J}
, the Cartan decomposition allows us to decompose any group element as
M=Tu
, where only
u
contributes to the phase. The components
T=(MM^{\mathsf{T}})^{1/2}
is the squeezing and
u:=T^{-1}M
is a passive rotation (orthogonal-symplectic).

For a Lie group element
M\in\mathcal{G}
and
K\in\mathfrak{g}
, the Cartan decomposition
M=Tu
is given by
Hackl and Bianchi 2021a
;
Guaita et al. 2024

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\Delta_{M}}\quad\text{and}\quad u=T^{-1}M\,,

(39)

where
JT=T^{-1}J
and
Ju=uJ
. One can show [CITE] that
\varphi(T)=1
, so that we can choose

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(M,\psi)=\mathcal{S}(T,1)\mathcal{S}(u,\psi)\,,

(40)

where we used
\eta(\mathbb{1},u)=0
. This shows

\displaystyle\Phi_{J}[\mathcal{S}(M,\psi)]=\Phi_{J}[\mathcal{S}(u,\psi)]\,.

(41)

In physical terms,
\mathcal{S}(u,\psi)
is a passive transformation satisfying
\mathcal{S}(u,\psi)\ket{J}=\psi\ket{J}
, while
\mathcal{S}(T,u)
is a pure squeezing transformation that does not change the phase.

II.2.6
Gaussian unitary from PQH

We have seen that
M\in\mathcal{G}
does not suffice to determine the complex phase of a squeezing operator
\mathcal{S}(M)
, which is why introduced
\mathcal{S}(M,\psi)
. However, given a Lie algebra element
K
, we have the isomorphism (
11
) that uniquely specifies
\widehat{K}
giving rise to a squeezing operator
\exp(\widehat{K})
. It is therefore natural to ask what is the complex phase
\psi=\Psi_{J}[e^{\widehat{K}}]
, such that
\mathcal{S}(e^{K},\psi)=e^{\widehat{K}}
.

This question was answered for bosons and fermions in Result 3a of
Guaita et al. 2024
, which we compactly review in appendix
F
. The strategy was to decompose
\widehat{K}=\widehat{K}_{I}+\widehat{K}^{\prime}
, such that
K_{I}
has purely imaginary eigenvalues and a commuting remainder
K^{\prime}
. By choosing an adapted reference complex structure
\tilde{J}
, the argument (phase) of the expectation value
\braket{J|\exp{\widehat{K}}|J}
is expressed as a sum of three contributions: (i) a simple trace term
\operatorname{Tr}(K_{I}\tilde{J})
determined by the eigenvalues of
K_{I}
, (ii) a cocycle term
\eta
that encodes the change of basis relating
J
to
\tilde{J}
, and (iii) a determinant term involving the remainder
K^{\prime}
. Thus, this closed expression isolates the otherwise ambiguous global phase in terms of classical symplectic data and the cocycle function.

Note that the bosonic case was also considered more recently in
Quesada 2025
, where
\braket{J|e^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{H}}|J}
was referred to as vacuum-to-vacuum amplitude.

The class of Gaussian unitaries generated by linear observables is known as the displacement operators, denoted by

II.3.1
Displacement operators

Detailed derivations of the subsection can be found in appendix
B.1
. Given a phase space vector
z^{a}\in V
or a dual vector
w_{a}\in V^{*}
, we define

\displaystyle\widehat{z}=-z^{a}\lambda_{ab}\hat{\xi}^{b}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{w}=-\mathbb{i}w_{a}\hat{\xi}^{a}\,.

(42)

We define the isomorphism between
V
and
V^{*}
given by
\mathbb{i}\lambda_{ab}
that takes a vector
z^{a}
and maps it to the dual vector
\tilde{z}_{a}=\mathbb{i}\lambda_{ab}z^{b}
with inverse
\mathbb{i}\Lambda^{ab}
that maps a dual vector
w_{a}
to
\tilde{w}^{a}=\mathbb{i}\Lambda^{ab}w_{b}
. We then have
\widehat{z}=\widehat{w}
for
w_{a}=\tilde{z}_{a}
or
\tilde{w}^{a}=z^{a}
. Here, we list their commutators as

\displaystyle[\widehat{z}_{1},\widehat{z}_{2}]

\displaystyle=-z_{1}\lambda z_{2}=z_{2}\lambda z_{1}\,,

(43a)

\displaystyle[\widehat{w}_{1},\widehat{w}_{2}]

\displaystyle=-w_{1}\Lambda w_{2}=w_{2}\Lambda w_{1}\,,

(43b)

\displaystyle[\widehat{Fz},\widehat{z}]

\displaystyle=[\widehat{w},\widehat{wF}]\,,

(43c)

for any linear map
F^{a}{}_{b}
. For the bosonic case, the line (
43a
) gives a symplectic structure
\omega(\cdot,\cdot)
as

\displaystyle[\widehat{z_{1}},\widehat{z_{2}}]=\mathrm{i}z_{1}\omega z_{2}=\mathrm{i}\omega(z_{1},z_{2})\,.

(44)

We further find the relation

\displaystyle[\hat{\xi}^{a},\widehat{z}]

\displaystyle=z^{a}\,,

(45)

\displaystyle e^{-\widehat{z}}\hat{\xi}^{a}e^{\widehat{z}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\xi}^{a}+z^{a}\,,

(46)

which justifies to define the displacement operator as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(z)=e^{\widehat{z}}\quad\text{with}\quad\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}(z)\hat{\xi}^{a}\mathcal{D}(z)=\hat{\xi}^{a}+z^{a}\,,

(47)

satisfying the relation product relation

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(z_{1})\mathcal{D}(z_{2})

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{1}{2}[\widehat{z}_{1},\widehat{z}_{2}]}\mathcal{D}(z_{1}+z_{2})\,,

(48)

which we will be able to relate to the Heisenberg group.

II.3.2
Displacement and Heisenberg group

Using the multiplication law of displacement operators (
48
), we can define the displacement group

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{N}=\mathbb{R}^{2N}\times\mathrm{U}(1)

(49)

with the multiplication law

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69054pt(z_{1},e^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta_{1}})\cdot(z_{2},e^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta_{2}})=(z_{1}+z_{2},e^{\mathrm{i}(\vartheta_{1}+\vartheta_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\omega(z_{1},z_{2}))}).

(50)

By construction the displacement operators
\mathcal{D}(z,e^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta})=e^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta}e^{\widehat{z}}
with additional phase form a faithful representation of the displacement group.

This group is closely related to the real Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}_{N}
, which is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2N+1}
and satisfies the multiplication law

\displaystyle(z_{1},\vartheta_{1})\cdot(z_{2},\vartheta_{2})=(z_{1}+z_{2},\vartheta_{1}+\vartheta_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\omega(z_{1},z_{2}))\,.

(51)

\mathbb{H}_{N}
forms the universal cover of
\mathcal{D}_{N}
via the mapping

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{N}\ni(z,\vartheta)\mapsto(z,e^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta})\in\mathcal{D}_{N}\,,

(52)

which implies that displacement operators can also be understood as (non-faithful) representation of the Heisenberg group. Here,
\mathrm{U}(1)
describes the complex phase factor
e^{\frac{1}{2}[\widehat{z}_{1},\widehat{z}_{2}]}
, thereby yielding a central extension from the Abelian translation group
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, which arises from the non-commutativity of quantum observables.

III
Inhomogeneous Gaussian unitaries

Our aim here is to write down the rule for how squeezing and displacement operations combine for bosons, including the correct overall phase. We do this by working in a lifted space where each operation carries a phase tag
\Psi
, so that composition is associative and matches operator multiplication. This lets us predict,
e.g.
, measurable phase effects in Gaussian circuits and do phase-correct gate compilation for bosonic codes.

From a more mathematical perspective, we want to extend the double cover group
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
to its inhomogeneous version
\mathrm{I}\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
. If we consider a squeezing operator followed by displacement operator while ignoring the sign ambiguity as
(M,z)
schematically,
Hackl and Bianchi 2021a
also provides their multiplication rules

\displaystyle(M_{1},z_{1})\cdot(M_{2},z_{2})

\displaystyle=(M_{1}\cdot M_{2},z_{1}+M_{1}z_{2})\,.

(53)

Hence our main goal is to solve the completed case where we consider
(M,z,\Psi)
for some overall function
\Psi(M,z)
, and come up with their multiplication rules.

Our first result will be an explicit product law for the complex phase. First, we recall the BCH composition rules for displacement and squeezing operators. Next, we apply the Lie-algebra inverse to express any quadratic Hamiltonian as a unitary with an added phase
\theta
. Finally, we compute the extra phase
\zeta(M_{1},M_{2},z_{1},z_{2})
that appears when multiplying two Gaussian unitaries. This pins down the inhomogeneous group for bosonic systems as the double cover

\displaystyle\mathrm{IMp}(2N,\mathbb{R})=(\mathrm{Sp}(2N,\mathbb{R})\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{2N})\,\tilde{\times}\,\mathrm{U}(1)\,.

(54)

with a central
\mathrm{U}(1)
phase.

III.1
Multiplication law

An inhomogeneous operation
\mathcal{U}
is parametrized by
(M,z,\Psi)
where
M^{a}{}_{b}
is a squeezing matrix,
z^{a}
a displacement vector, such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\hat{\xi}^{a}\mathcal{U}=M^{a}{}_{b}\hat{\xi}^{b}+z^{a}\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi_{J}[\mathcal{U}]=\Psi^{*}\,.

(55)

This only becomes a complete parametrization when one specifies a unique multiplication law.

Recall for a Lie group element
M\in\mathcal{G}
, we can always decompose

with

. Then the Cartan decomposition gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(M,\psi)=\mathcal{S}(T,1)\mathcal{S}(u,\psi)\,,

(56)

with

\displaystyle\Phi_{J}[\mathcal{S}(M,\psi)]=\Phi_{J}[\mathcal{S}(u,\psi)]=\psi^{*}\,.

(57)

Lemma 1
.

Expectation value of
\mathcal{D}(z)\mathcal{S}(T,1)
and its phase
\gamma
.

The other decomposed squeezing operator would merge with the displacement operator and have the following expectation value

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\braket{J|\mathcal{D}(z)\mathcal{S}(T,1)|J}\\

where

\displaystyle Y_{M}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{1}-\Delta_{M}}{\mathbb{1}+\Delta_{M}}=\frac{2}{\mathbb{1}+\Delta_{M}}-\mathbb{1}=Z_{M^{-1}}\,,

(59)

\displaystyle\Delta_{M}

\displaystyle=-MJM^{-1}J=MM^{\intercal}\,.

(60)

For convenience, we also denote the phase as

\displaystyle\Phi_{J}[\mathcal{D}(z)\mathcal{S}(T,1)]

\displaystyle=e^{\mathrm{i}\gamma(M,z)}\,,

(61)

where the displaced-squeezing phase is

\displaystyle\gamma(M,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}[\widehat{Y_{M}z},\widehat{z}]=\frac{1}{4}z\omega Y_{M}z=\frac{1}{4}\omega(z,Y_{M}z)\,,

\displaystyle|\braket{J|\mathcal{D}(z)\mathcal{S}(T,1)|J}|=|\braket{e^{\widehat{K}_{+}}}|e^{-\frac{1}{2}zg(\mathbb{1}+MM^{\intercal})^{-1}z}\,,

Proof.

Basically, one can use relevant results from

to make a further derivation to find the explicit expectation value

The next step is to appropriately decompose a general Gaussian unitary into displacements and squeezings.

Lemma 2
.

Decomposition of unitary.

The most general inhomogeneous Gaussian unitary

\displaystyle=e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\mathcal{D}(z)\mathcal{S}(M,\psi)\,,

\displaystyle=\Psi^{*}\psi e^{-\mathrm{i}\gamma(M,z)}\,,

(64b)

where

. This decomposition is unique up to the choice of

, which can also be made unique by fixing a convention, such as

, where the square-root is defined as the principal branch of the complex square root with
\sqrt{-1}=\mathrm{i}
rather than
-\mathrm{i}

First, let us prove that
(M,z,\Psi)
together with the relations (
55
) characterizes the unitary operator
\mathcal{U}(M,z,\Psi)
uniquely. It follows from the Proposition 6 from
Hackl and Bianchi 2021b
, that any complex phase can be absorbed into the phase

, while

is only unique up to a
2\pi
periodicity.

Second, we plug the decomposition (
64a
) into the first equation (
55
) use the properties of the displacement operator (
47
) and the squeezing operator (
37a
), which we find to be saturated, as shown in (
151
).

Finally, we put the decomposition (
64a
) into the second equation of (
55

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Psi^{*}&=\Phi_{J}[e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\mathcal{D}(z)\mathcal{S}(M,\psi)]\\

&=e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\Phi_{J}[\mathcal{D}(z)\mathcal{S}(T,1)]\psi^{*}\\

where we use (

Before we come up with the multiplication rules, we need to appropriately commute the displacement and squeezing operators.

Lemma 3
.

Commutation law.

Given a squeezing operator
\mathcal{S}(M,\psi)
and a displacement operator
\mathcal{D}(z)
, they commute in the way of a semi-direct product

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(M,\psi)\mathcal{D}(z)=\mathcal{D}(Mz)\mathcal{S}(M,\psi)\,.

(66)

Proof.

From the definition (
37a
), we have for both bosons and fermions

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{S}(M,\psi)\mathcal{D}(z)\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}(M,\psi)\\
&=\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}(M^{-1},\psi^{*})e^{-z^{a}\lambda_{ab}\hat{\xi}^{b}}\mathcal{S}(M^{-1},\psi^{*})\\
&=e^{-z^{a}\lambda_{ab}(M^{-1})^{b}{}_{c}\hat{\xi}^{c}}\\
&=e^{-(Mz)^{b}\lambda_{bc}\hat{\xi}^{c}}\\
&=\mathcal{D}(Mz)\,.\end{split}

(67)

Adding after both side a squeezing
\mathcal{S}(M,\psi)
gives the desired commutation rule for the displacement and squeezing.
∎

Our next goal is to figure out the actual multiplication rules for the two unitaries.

Result 1
.

Inhomogeneous Gaussian unitaries from a representation

where we recognized in the final expression again the decomposition from Lemma

with

\displaystyle=M_{1}M_{2}\,,

(72a)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=z_{1}+M_{1}z_{2}\,,

(72b)

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle=\psi_{1}\psi_{2}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\eta(M_{1},M_{2})}\,,

\displaystyle=\Phi_{J}[e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\mathcal{D}(z)\mathcal{S}(M,\psi)]\,,

III.2

.

(79b)

Their commutators are given by

\displaystyle[\widehat{w},\widehat{K}]

\displaystyle=\widehat{wK}\,,

(83)

The linear term just follows from the commutator.
∎

IV
Case studies for one bosonic mode

We provide numerical validations and illustrative examples of our results for quantum systems with a single bosonic degree of freedom, representing the simplest nontrivial case. We further examine how the logarithm of a specific class of Gaussian unitaries can lead to non-quadratic operators.

IV.1
Review: Parametrization and Stability

To facilitate the analysis and provide clear illustrations in the case of a single bosonic mode, we parametrize the symplectic algebra and the corresponding quadratic Hamiltonians.

Lie algebra.
The symplectic Lie algebra
\mathrm{sp}(2,\mathbb{R})
is isomorphic to
\mathrm{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
and generated by the three matrices

\displaystyle X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad Y=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad Z=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(99)

Representation theory.
For a bosonic system with
N=1
, these three generators are represented as anti-Hermitian quadratic operators via relation (
11
), which yields

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{i}\widehat{X}

\displaystyle=\hat{p}^{2}-\hat{q}^{2}=-\hat{a}^{\dagger 2}-\hat{a}^{2}\,,

(100a)

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{i}\widehat{Y}

\displaystyle=\hat{p}\hat{q}+\hat{q}\hat{p}=\mathrm{i}(\hat{a}^{\dagger 2}-\hat{a}^{2})\,,

(100b)

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{i}\widehat{Z}

\displaystyle=\hat{p}^{2}+\hat{q}^{2}=2\hat{n}+1\,,

(100c)

where
\hat{n}=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{p}^{2}+\hat{q}^{2}-1)
is a number operator, whose spectrum consists of non-negative integers.

With respect to this basis, a general element of the Lie algebra,
K=aX+bY+cZ
, is represented by the quadratic operator
\widehat{K}=a\widehat{X}+b\widehat{Y}+c\widehat{Z}
. We define that
r=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}-c^{2}}
. A straightforward calculation shows that
\det(K)=-r^{2}
, and that the eigenvalues of
K
are
\pm r
.

In this representation,
\widehat{X}
and
\widehat{Y}
generate squeezing operations that differ by a
45^{\circ}
rotation in phase space, whereas
\widehat{Z}
generates phase rotations. Taking into account this symmetry, together with the algebraic structure encoded in
r=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}-c^{2}}
, it is natural to reduce the number of independent parameters by setting either
a=0
or
b=0
, since both correspond to squeezing generators with the same algebraic form. Without loss of generality, we restrict our attention to the subspace
K=aX+cZ
.

The
stability
of bosonic systems has been discussed in detail in
Hackl et al. 2018
. In the single-mode setting, stability is determined by the eigenvalues of
K
given by
\pm r
:

•

Stable (imaginary
r
).
The standard example is
\widehat{H}=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}(\hat{p}^{2}+\hat{q}^{2})
, which means the evolution follows a Harmonic oscillator.

•

Unstable (real
r
).
The standard example is
\widehat{H}=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}(\hat{p}^{2}-\hat{q}^{2})
, which means the evolution follows an inverted harmonic oscillator. This is clearly unstable and generates squeezing.

•

Metastable (
r=0
, but
K\neq 0
).
The standard example is
\widehat{H}=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\hat{p}^{2}
describing a free propagating particle. The corresponding generator
K
is non-diagonalizable, meaning there exists a non-trivial Jordan-block in it.

IV.4
Non-quadratic generators of Gaussian unitaries

Gaussian unitaries were introduced via the relation (
55
) and reviewed as being generated by quadratic Hamiltonians. In particular, for a symplectic generator
K\in\mathfrak{sp}(2N,\mathbb{R})
with corresponding group element
M=e^{K}
, the associated Gaussian unitaries
\mathcal{S}=\pm e^{\widehat{K}}
implement the symplectic transformation
M
. However, it is also well-known that the exponential map from the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sp}(2N,\mathbb{R})
to the group
\mathrm{Sp}(2N,\mathbb{R})
is not surjective,
i.e.
, there exist symplectic group elements
M
that
cannot
be written in the form
M=e^{K}
for any
K\in\mathfrak{sp}(2N,\mathbb{R})
.

Despite this, every homogeneous unitary (
i.e.
, symplectic transformation) admits a unitary representation
\mathcal{S}(M)\in\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H}_{\infty})
acting on the bosonic Hilbert space. This guarantees the existence of a (possibly non-quadratic) Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(M)=e^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{H}}\,.

(112)

This naturally raises the question: what types of non-quadratic generators arise when the Gaussian unitary in (
112
) with a quadratic Hamiltonians
\hat{H}
alone?

We primarily concern the
unreachable
region identified in figure
3
, which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\tan^{-1}{\sinh{\rho}}<\tau<\pi-\tan^{-1}{\sinh{\rho}}\,,

(113)

as explained in
(
Guaita et al. 2024
, Eq. (188))
. In this unreachable region, the symplectic matrix (for a single bosonic mode) can be diagonalized to the form

\displaystyle M_{\lambda}=\begin{pmatrix}-\lambda&0\\
0&-\frac{1}{\lambda}\end{pmatrix}=-\mathbb{1}_{2}\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0\\
0&\lambda^{-1}\end{pmatrix}=-\mathbb{1}_{2}e^{K}\,,

(114)

where
K=\log(\lambda)\,Y
. The corresponding unitary representation factorizes as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(M_{\lambda})=\mathcal{S}(-\mathbb{1}_{2})\,\mathcal{S}(e^{K})=e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\pi(\hat{n}+\frac{1}{2})}\,e^{\widehat{K}}\,.

(115)

If we now tried to combine the two generators into a single exponential, we cannot do so immediately, as
\hat{n}
will generally not commute with
\widehat{K}
. This issue can be resolved by introducing the parity operator
\mathcal{P}=e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\pi\hat{n}}
, which satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\ket{n}

\displaystyle=e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\pi\hat{n}}\ket{n}=(-1)^{n}\ket{n}\,,

(116a)

\displaystyle e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal{P}}\ket{n}

\displaystyle=e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi}{2}(-1)^{n}}\ket{n}=\mathrm{i}\mathcal{P}\ket{n}\,,

(116b)

The parity operator decomposes the Hilbert space into even and odd sectors of particle occupation,
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\text{even}}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\text{odd}}
. It clearly commutes with a quadratic generator
\widehat{K}
, which follows from the fact that each term in
\widehat{K}
either leaves the particle number invariant or changes it by
\pm 2
, thereby keeping parity invariant.

Using this operator, we may safely write

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{S}(-\mathbb{1}_{2})&=e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\pi(\hat{n}+\frac{1}{2})}=\pm\mathrm{i}e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\pi\hat{n}}\\
&\overset{\text{choose }+}{=}\mathrm{i}\mathcal{P}=e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal{P}}\,.\end{split}

(117)

Since
\mathcal{P}
commutes with any quadratic generator
\widehat{K}
, the unitary can be written compactly as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(M_{\lambda})

\displaystyle=e^{\widehat{K}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi}{2}\mathcal{P}}\,,

(118)

where the generator explicitly contains the non-quadratic parity operator.

It is natural to ask whether operators of the form
e^{\mathrm{i}\tau\mathcal{P}}
, with
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
, define Gaussian unitaries in the sense of (
55

e^{\mathrm{i}\tau\mathcal{P}}
is Gaussian if and only if
\tau=k\frac{\pi}{2}
for some integer
k
. For generic values of
\tau
, the operator
e^{\mathrm{i}\tau\mathcal{P}}
is non-Gaussian and corresponds to a parity-controlled non-quadratic evolution.

V
Conclusion

We resolve the ambiguity of the global phase in composite Gaussian circuits by constructing a unified phase formalism for inhomogeneous Gaussian unitaries. Building on earlier parametrizations based on the circle function
\varphi(M)
and the cocycle
\eta(M_{1},M_{2})
, we incorporate the displaced-squeezing phase
\gamma(M,z)
to obtain the generalized inhomogeneous cocycle function
\zeta(M_{1},M_{2},z_{1},z_{2})
, which treats symplectic transformations and displacements consistently. These ingredients combine into a single phase function
\Psi(M,z)
that lifts the affine symplectic parameters
(M,z)
to the extended element
(M,z,\Psi)
and yields a unique and associative group product using
\zeta
defined in (
70
). Our framework determines the complex phase of arbitrary Gaussian unitaries and their compositions, enabling phase-correct operator multiplication and measurable interference predictions. Previous works have addressed special cases of Gaussian phases
Paraoanu and Scutaru 2000
;
Yao et al. 2024
, whereas our approach provides a general and systematic solution for the fully inhomogeneous setting focusing on the multiplication laws.

As a second main result, we derive explicit phase formulas for unitaries generated by general quadratic Hamiltonians. Rather than decomposing transformations into elementary gates, the global phase of
e^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{H}}
is obtained directly from its generator, providing compact expressions suited for numerical implementation. This complements recent methods for evaluating Gaussian overlaps
Yao et al. 2024
while supplying the phase information required for exact circuit composition. As a direct application, phase-coherent simulation of optical circuits becomes possible even for long sequences of displaced-squeezing operations. Although we focus on bosonic systems, the same derivations extend to fermionic Gaussian unitaries with minor modifications; in particular, the generator-based phase formula provides the key ingredient needed to lift affine orthogonal transformations to the Pin group.

Beyond the technical contributions, our work connects mathematical and practical treatments of Gaussian quantum mechanics. Physics-oriented approaches often address specific circuits where phases are fixed case by case, while rigorous treatments of the metaplectic and Weyl groups
Woit 2017b
;
Folland 1989
;
Simon and Mukunda 1993
;
Zhang et al. 1990
;
Dereziński and Gérard 2013
are typically less accessible to practitioners. We translate this structure into explicit formulas that can be directly implemented in modeling and simulation. This offers a bridge between abstract representation theory and experimental needs, complementing phase-aware Gaussian computation frameworks developed by
Dias and König 2024
;
Dias and König 2024
;
Paraoanu and Scutaru 2000
;
Bravyi and Gosset 2017b
;
Yao et al. 2024
;
Quesada 2025
.

Our formalism is particularly relevant for non-Gaussian variational methods that rely crucially on phase-sensitive quantities. Superpositions of Gaussian states have been studied extensively as efficient variational representations of many-body states
Bravyi and Gosset 2017b
and as tools for simulation and tomography of quantum systems
Dias and König 2024
;
Dias and König 2024
. In these approaches, overlaps between different Gaussian branches determine norms, energies, and gradients, so a well-defined relative phase between Gaussian unitaries is essential. Similarly, generalized Gaussian states introduced in
Shi et al. 2018
and further developed in
Hackl et al. 2020b
;
Guaita et al. 2021
require the evaluation of expectation values and geometric quantities that depend explicitly on the phases of underlying Gaussian transformations. Without a consistent global-phase convention, interference between Gaussian components becomes ambiguous and variational optimization ill-defined. The inhomogeneous lifting
(M,z,\Psi)
developed here provides precisely this missing structure, enabling unambiguous computation of generalized Wick expectation values, overlaps, and tangent-space objects required for gradient-based optimization on enlarged variational manifolds.

A further application is efficient simulation of
n
-mode Gaussian circuits with correct global phases. Many numerical tools ignore phases or fix them heuristically, leading to inconsistencies when circuits are concatenated or optimized. The closed-form phase function
\Psi
and its composition rule via
\zeta
enable reproducible, phase-aware circuit simulation with improved stability. This directly benefits tasks such as circuit compilation and interferometric phase estimation.

In summary, this work shows that phase information—often treated as a technical nuisance—can be made explicit, computable, and structurally transparent for Gaussian quantum mechanics. We expect these results to facilitate reliable phase-aware simulation, variational optimization, and the integration of Gaussian methods into larger non-Gaussian quantum algorithms.

(134b)

\widehat{w}

\widehat{w}

\widehat{v}
and scalar function

\widehat{w}
and
\widehat{w}^{2}

\displaystyle[[\widehat{K}]^{r},\widehat{w}]

\displaystyle=\widehat{w(-K)^{r}}\propto\widehat{wK^{r}}\,,

(183a)

\displaystyle[[\widehat{K}]^{r-1},[\widehat{w},\widehat{K}]]

\displaystyle=-\widehat{w(-K)^{r}}\propto\widehat{wK^{r}}\,.

(183b)

Thus the linear terms in

\displaystyle\widehat{v}=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{r}\widehat{wK^{r}}=\widehat{w\alpha(K)}\,,

for some power series coefficients

\displaystyle[\widehat{w},[[\widehat{K}]^{s},\widehat{w}]]

\displaystyle\propto[\widehat{w},\widehat{wK^{s}}]\,,

(185a)

\displaystyle[\widehat{w},[[\widehat{K}]^{s-1},[\widehat{w},\widehat{K}]]]

\displaystyle\propto[\widehat{w},\widehat{wK^{s}}]\,.

(185b)

Thus the scalar function takes the form

Where the series (

\tilde{J}^{2}=-\mathbb{1}
, as multiplying with
|K_{I}|^{-1}
changes all eigenvalues of

to be
\pm\mathrm{i}
.

2.

Compute the transformation
T=\sqrt{-\tilde{J}J}
, where
-\tilde{J}J
has purely positive eigenvalues, so that the square root is well-defined.

3.

Evaluate the complex phase as

\displaystyle\arg\Phi_{J}[e^{\widehat{K}}]=\tfrac{1}{4}\operatorname{Tr}(K_{I}\tilde{J})+\tfrac{1}{2}\eta(T^{-1}e^{K},T)\,.

(202)

\tilde{J}

and thus not be a valid complex structure. However, this can be easily fixed by flipping the signs of some of the eigenvalues of
K_{I}
.

General case.
Given a general generator
K
, we can follow the steps explained to write

1.

Follow the steps explained in
Kustura et al. 2019
to find a change of basis
M

MKM^{-1}
takes the form

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}O_{I}&O_{R}\\
O_{L}&-O_{I}^{\intercal}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(203)

where
O_{\chi}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
(
\chi=I,L,R
),
O_{R}=O_{R}^{\intercal}
, and
O_{L}=O_{L}^{\intercal}

O_{\chi}
can each be expressed as a direct sum of blocks over different eigenvalue types

\displaystyle O_{\chi}\equiv O_{\chi}^{(\mathfrak{\mathcal{R}})}\oplus O_{\chi}^{(\mathfrak{\mathcal{C}})}\oplus O_{\chi}^{(0)}\oplus O_{\chi}^{(\mathfrak{\mathcal{I}})}\,,

(204)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}O_{\chi}^{(\mathfrak{\mathcal{R}})}&\equiv\bigoplus_{\mathcal{R}_{i}}\left[{\Moplus_{j=1}^{m_{i}}I_{\chi}^{(1)}(\lambda_{i},D_{ij})}\right],\\
O_{\chi}^{(\mathfrak{\mathcal{C}})}&\equiv\bigoplus_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}\left[{\Moplus_{j=1}^{m_{i}}I_{\chi}^{(2)}(\lambda_{i},D_{ij})}\right],\\

O_{\chi}^{(\mathcal{I})}&\equiv\bigoplus_{\mathcal{I}_{i}}\left[{\Moplus_{j=1}^{l_{i}}I_{\chi}^{(5)}(\lambda_{i},D_{ij})\Moplus_{j=1}^{n_{i}}I_{\chi}^{(6)}(\lambda_{i},D_{ij})}\right]\hskip-28.45274pt\end{split}

(205)

with eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}

D_{ij}

\mathfrak{c}=1,\dots,6
labels six distinct cases.

2.

Define the complex structure
\tilde{J}
, such that
M\tilde{J}M^{-1}

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbb{1}\\

Compute the transformation

In summary, Result 3a from
Guaita et al. 2024
using the standard form from
Kustura et al. 2019
gives a robust and general expression for the phase of the Gaussian vacuum overlap
\arg\braket{J|e^{\widehat{K}}|J}
in the bosonic case. Its careful decomposition and cocycle accounting make the complex phase accessible for analytical calculations. The relevant expressions simplify for many generators
K
encountered in practice, such as those representing physical Hamiltonians. If one is instead mostly interested in numerically evaluating the phase, one can track it efficiently using the formula (
