Endoscopic endonasal depth EEG–guided resection of a temporal encephalocele resulting in seizure freedom: illustrative case
Authors: Keisuke Ohtani, Makoto Satoh, Yohei Ishishita, Kenji Ibayashi, Takashi Yamaguchi, Naoto Kunii, Kensuke Kawai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDTemporal encephaloceles (TEs) are increasingly recognized as epileptogenic lesions in drug-resistant temporal lobe epilepsy. Temporal encephaloceles (TEs) have become increasingly detectable with advances in MRI and are now recognized as important causes of drug-resistant temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE).1–6 Stereo-EEG (SEEG) and intraoperative electrocorticography (ECoG) studies have shown that both the encephalocele and the surrounding temporal cortex can be independently epileptogenic, supporting tailored lesionectomy in selected cases.7–11 Surgical management of TE-related epilepsy has traditionally involved lesionectomy or anterior temporal lobectomy (ATL) using a transcranial approach.7,9,10

In contrast, although the endoscopic endonasal approach (EEA) is well established in skull base tumors, its use with the primary goal of epileptogenic lesion removal has rarely been reported. Prior reports include isolated cases of encephaloceles, olfactory tract hamartoma, and middle cranial fossa arachnoid cysts.12–14 A key limitation of the traditional EEA is its inability to assess epileptogenicity directly, which makes it difficult to determine the optimal extent of resection.

Here, we report a rare case in which epileptogenicity was confirmed intraoperatively using endonasal depth EEG, enabling minimally invasive tailored resection with an excellent seizure outcome.

Illustrative Case

Patient History

The patient was a 41-year-old right-handed man who had experienced focal impaired consciousness seizures since the age of 35. His habitual seizures consisted of behavioral arrest and oral automatisms, followed by dystonic posturing of the left upper limb. He had no notable medical history. Trials of carbamazepine and valproate were ineffective. Subsequent treatment with levetiracetam and lacosamide reduced but did not eliminate seizures, which continued to occur monthly.

Preoperative Evaluation

Brain MRI demonstrated a TE protruding into the lateral recess of the right sphenoid sinus (Fig. 1). No hippocampal sclerosis, cortical dysplasia, or other structural abnormalities were observed. Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) revealed no significant hypometabolism (Fig. 1D). Interictal scalp EEG showed intermittent spikes over the right anterior temporal region. During video-EEG monitoring, habitual seizures were characterized by oral automatisms with impaired consciousness, followed by generalized tonic-clonic seizures. Electrographic seizure onset, characterized by rhythmic delta activity, was identified approximately 5 seconds after the initial clinical manifestations, arising from F8, T4, OR2, and T2 with subsequent propagation, suggesting a right anterior temporal onset (Fig. 2).

Approximately 5 seconds after the clinical seizure onset, electrographic seizure onset became evident, as indicated by the arrow, with the emergence of rhythmic delta activity from F8, T4, OR2, and T2, and subsequently propagating. The delta activity preceding this (over F7, F8, T3, and T4) was interpreted as glossokinetic artifact related to tongue movement.

Based on these findings, we suspected that the encephalocele and adjacent cortex constituted the epileptogenic zone. We therefore planned an EEA with intraoperative depth EEG monitoring to evaluate epileptogenicity and guide tailored resection.

Simulation demonstrated that drilling of the pterygoid base, using the vidian canal as a key landmark, would allow a safe and direct trajectory to the lateral recess of the sphenoid sinus and the encephalocele. These findings supported the selection of a transpterygoid approach. Alternative corridors, including a transorbital endoscopic approach, were considered; however, an endonasal transpterygoid route was selected to allow direct access for depth electrode placement and intraoperative electrophysiological assessment. Prior to surgery, the patient was informed of the standard transcranial surgical option, including lesionectomy with or without ATL, as well as the proposed EEA. The potential advantages and limitations of each strategy were thoroughly discussed. In particular, the patient was informed that while the EEA was less invasive, seizure freedom might not be achieved with a single procedure and a staged transcranial surgery may be required if seizures persisted. After careful consideration, the patient elected to undergo the endoscopic endonasal procedure.

Surgical Procedure

The patient was positioned supine with slight head rotation to the left, and an endoscopic endonasal transpterygoid approach was performed. The sphenoid and ethmoid sinuses were widely opened to expose the sellar floor and ensure adequate visualization of the lateral recess. Drilling of the posterior wall of the maxillary sinus allowed entry into the pterygopalatine fossa, and its contents were mobilized laterally in a subperiosteal plane to expose the pterygoid base. Using the pterygopalatine fossa and vidian canal as anatomical landmarks, we progressively expanded the lateral surgical corridor. After careful drilling of the pterygoid base with a diamond burr, the lateral recess of the sphenoid sinus was fully exposed, revealing a bony defect at the middle cranial fossa floor with herniation of the temporal lobe—consistent with a TE (Fig. 3A). The adjacent temporal dura was also clearly visualized (Fig. 3B).

Intraoperative endoscopic findings (part 1). A: Endoscopic view of the encephalocele (arrow) exposed via a transpterygoid approach. B: Adjacent temporal dura (arrowhead) exposed after removal of the overlying bone. C: Placement of a depth electrode into the lateral temporal cortex through the encephalocele. D: Intraoperative fluoroscopic image obtained during electrode placement (arrow). E: Frequent epileptiform spikes recorded from the depth electrode.

Under fluoroscopic guidance, a depth electrode was inserted through the encephalocele into the lateral temporal cortex (Fig. 3C and D). During intraoperative electrophysiological recording, the concentration of inhaled sevoflurane was increased to 3% to facilitate activation of epileptiform discharges. Frequent epileptiform discharges were recorded from this site (Fig. 3E). The encephalocele was circumferentially dissected and removed (Fig. 4A).

Intraoperative endoscopic findings (part 2). A:The encephalocele was completely removed. B: An additional depth electrode was placed in the adjacent temporal cortex. C: Sparse epileptiform spikes were recorded from the electrode. D: Tailored resection of temporal cortex adjacent to the encephalocele.

A second depth electrode was placed in the temporal cortex adjacent to the encephalocele (Fig. 4B), which showed sparse but definite epileptiform discharges (Fig. 4C), indicating that both the encephalocele and neighboring cortex contributed to the epileptogenic network.

Based on these findings, the temporal cortex adjacent to the encephalocele was resected at a depth of approximately 7 mm (Fig. 4D). The skull base defect was reconstructed in a multilayer fashion using DuraGen (Integra LifeSciences) and a free mucosal graft, reinforced with fibrin glue, achieving a watertight seal of the lateral recess.

Postoperative Course

MRI confirmed complete lesionectomy within the planned boundaries (Fig. 5). Histopathology examination was consistent with encephalocele without cortical dysplasia. The patient remained neurologically intact and seizure free for 48 months.

Postoperative axial (A) and coronal (B) MR images confirming complete resection of the encephalocele and adjacent cortex within the planned margins. No postoperative complications or residual lesion was observed.

Patient Perspective

The patient reported that the minimally invasive nature of the surgery provided reassurance, and that their postoperative recovery was faster than anticipated. They expressed relief that seizures had ceased, describing a marked improvement in daily living functions, particularly the ability to work and live without the fear of sudden seizures.

Discussion

Observations

TEs as Underrecognized but Surgically Remediable Epileptogenic Lesions

TEs were historically regarded as incidental findings; however, advances in contemporary MRI techniques have clarified their considerable contribution to drug-resistant TLE.1–6 Multiple SEEG and intraoperative ECoG studies have demonstrated that both the encephalocele and its adjacent temporal cortex may serve as seizure onset zones,7–11 highlighting the importance of individualized electrophysiological assessment.

Novelty of This Case: Purely Endonasal, Electrophysiology-Guided Epileptogenic Lesionectomy

The EEA, originally used for sellar or parasellar lesions, has undergone significant expansion to encompass a wide spectrum of skull base pathologies.15 Despite this evolution, use of the EEA with the specific intent of resecting an epileptogenic focus remains exceedingly rare. Previous reports include TE resection,12 olfactory tract hamartoma,13 and meningoencephalocele repair in the pterygoid fossa,14 but none have demonstrated seizure freedom following a tailored resection guided directly by intraoperative depth EEG. This case is therefore notable for achieving long-term seizure freedom using a completely transnasal route—without craniotomy—while confirming epileptogenicity in real time. Endoscopic transorbital corridors have been reported as alternative approaches to the lateral recess of the sphenoid sinus for skull base defect repair, potentially avoiding transsphenoidal dissection and vidian nerve manipulation.16,17 However, in the present case, a transnasal transpterygoid approach was considered more appropriate because it provided a more direct and linear surgical trajectory, allowing stable depth electrode placement and real-time electrophysiological assessment.

In this case, depth electrodes placed into both the encephalocele and adjacent cortex revealed frequent epileptiform discharges in both regions, indicating a unified epileptogenic network. This finding, consistent with prior SEEG/ECoG literature,7–9 justified a limited 7-mm tailored cortical resection rather than a more extensive resection such as ATL.

Seizure Freedom Achieved With Minimally Invasive Surgery Alone

Long-term seizure freedom was achieved in the patient exclusively through an endonasal approach, without requiring a craniotomy. This strategy remains compatible with staged escalation—an ATL could still be performed if seizures were to recur. This aligns with contemporary epilepsy surgery paradigms that emphasize minimal invasiveness with conditional augmentation. Similar considerations regarding accurate delineation of the epileptogenic zone apply to other minimally invasive treatment modalities, including laser interstitial thermal therapy. In the present case, the absence of findings suggestive of a widespread mesial temporal epileptogenic network supported an initial limited resection strategy.

Infection Risk Considerations

Although the endonasal corridor is considered clean-contaminated, the reported risk of postoperative meningitis after endoscopic endonasal skull base surgery is low in the absence of postoperative CSF leakage.18,19 In our case, a multilayer skull base reconstruction was achieved and no infectious complications occurred. The depth electrodes used were sterile, single-use devices placed under direct endoscopic visualization and fluoroscopic guidance; therefore, we believe that the incremental infection risk was acceptable when standard perioperative infection prevention measures were applied. As a comparable intracranial electrophysiological technique, SEEG has been widely adopted with a relatively low infection rate reported,20 supporting the notion that carefully performed depth electrode placement itself does not substantially increase infectious risk.

Lessons

The EEA can serve as a safe, effective, and minimally invasive surgical option for selected cases of TE-related epilepsy. Endonasal depth EEG provides high-yield information for confirming epileptogenicity and tailoring the extent of resection. In appropriately selected patients, seizure freedom may be achieved without craniotomy, particularly when electroclinical findings are concordant and multimodal preoperative evaluation shows no evidence of a widespread mesial temporal epileptogenic network. These cases support the role of endonasal surgery within staged epilepsy surgery strategies.
