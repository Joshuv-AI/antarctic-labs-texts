Online Crowdfunding Campaigns for People Living With ALS Demonstrate Financial Burden and Unmet Needs
Authors: Astrid Grouls, Peter A Ubel, Minh Nguyen, Nikhita Pathapati, Steven Doerstling, Caroline E Sloan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsPeople living with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS; pALS) have extensive care needs, from wheelchairs to feeding tubes to caregiving support. The financial impact of these needs on pALS and their caregivers (cALS) has not been fully explored. We aimed to explore the types of expenses for which pALS and cALS crowdfund, and the financial circumstances that lead them to resort to crowdfunding.MethodsWe randomly selected 320 ALS‐related crowdfunding campaigns posted on the GoFundMe platform from 2010–2020. We conducted a summative content analysis to categorize the expenses for which campaigns requested money and descriptions of financial burden.ResultsWe included 266 campaigns. Most campaigns were written by people who were not the pALS or cALS, such as children or coworkers (85.3%). Most campaigns fundraised for medication and treatment costs (21.4%), equipment (25.2%), accessibility needs for home (24.0%), and transportation (19.5%), and in‐home caregiving services (18%). Multiple campaigns (22.9%) requested assistance with nonmedical expenses ranging from rent and utilities to personal travel and hobby goals. Many campaign writers (47.7%) described financial burden related to the pALS' diagnosis, including medical debt (4.5%) and job loss of the pALS (30.5%) and/or cALS (6.8%).DiscussionOur analysis of ALS‐related crowdfunding campaigns highlights the breadth of medical and nonmedical care needs and quality of life goals for which people request additional financial support throughout the disease course. These findings provide important insights for ALS care teams into the expenses of pALS.Keywords: ALS, cost of illness, crowdfunding, financial burden

Introduction

As physical function for people living with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS; pALS) declines and they lose the capacity to work, their needs for medical care, caregiving assistance, and adaptive equipment increase [1]. While many pALS in the US are Medicare‐eligible after applying for Social Security Disability Insurance, out‐of‐pocket expenses can still be substantial, with 35% of caregivers of pALS (cALS) reporting that the financial impact of the disease is devastating or near‐devastating [2]. One 2014 study using a combination of claims data and a mailed survey estimated that total ALS‐related expenses amounted to $63,693/year, with $31,121/year in direct medical costs incurred by patients and insurers (e.g., outpatient visits, hospitalizations), $17,889/year in nonmedical costs incurred by patients (e.g., transportation, home modifications), and $14,682/year in lost family income [3]. These expenses typically increase as the disease progresses [1, 4, 5]. Over 80% of pALS stop working within 20 months of diagnosis, reducing their ability to pay as expenses accelerate [6]. Prior research has generally focused on the costs of large expenses such as outpatient care, hospitalizations [7], home renovations [8], and caregiving assistance [1, 5]; however, they have less completely captured the smaller cumulative expenses that pALS and cALS face.

A clearer picture of all ALS‐related expenses is needed, so that policymakers can learn where gaps in insurance coverage remain and so that clinicians can gain insight into the financial circumstances and self‐reported financial needs of pALS. One way to learn about the ALS‐related expenses that can cause financial strain is to explore online crowdfunding campaigns. In such campaigns, people with medical conditions or their loved ones request money from online contributors, typically posting a narrative of how much money they need and how they plan to use the funds. A recent study found that ALS is the most common neurologic diagnosis for which crowdfunding campaigns are created; however, the content of these campaigns has not been fully described [9]. Prior analysis of GoFundMe crowdfunding campaigns established to support people with diabetes demonstrated that fundraising requests for nonmedical expenses and unproven therapies were as common or more common than requests for direct medical expenses related to approved testing and treatment strategies, such as continuous glucose monitors and insulin pumps [10].

In this study, we conducted a summative content analysis of GoFundMe campaigns to explore the types of expenses that pALS and cALS crowdfund for, and the financial circumstances that lead them to resort to crowdfunding.

Methods

Data Source and Sample Description

We used web scraping to extract a random sample of 89,645 active crowdfunding campaigns posted on GoFundMe from 2010 to August 2020 self‐categorized as “Medical, Illness, and Healing” and posted in the US. The dataset included information on campaign title, campaign text, dates of initial and follow‐up posts, and zip code of the campaign poster.

To identify requests for funding related to ALS, members of the research team read through campaigns that included the following strings: “ALS,” “amyotrophic lateral sclerosis,” “Lou Gehrig,” and “motor neuron disease” (see Table S1 for full list). Campaigns were included in the final analysis if they were in English and requested money for a single person diagnosed with ALS in the US. Campaigns were excluded if the campaign was raising money for an ALS‐related organization or group.

This study was approved by the Duke University Institutional Review Board (approval ID 2020‐0435) and determined not to require IRB approval by the Baylor College of Medicine Institutional Review Board.

Codebook Development

The codebook was developed inductively. Members of the research team independently reviewed 60 randomly selected campaigns and met weekly to reach consensus on categories of medical expenses and types of financial circumstances described by campaign writers. See Table S2 for the codebook and definitions of each category. In brief, we first categorized the campaign writer as the pALS, cALS, or someone else. We then included the following fundraising categories: medications/treatments (stem cell/gene therapy and other), hospital bills, equipment (wheelchair, speech/language devices, ventilatory needs, lift devices, and other), care assistance (in‐home or out‐of‐home), accessibility concerns (home modifications and out‐of‐home transportation aids), healthcare‐related travel (research‐related and non‐research‐related), and other expenses. We also created a category for more general requests for money that did not specify how the money would be used. Finally, the codebook also included the following categories to describe financial circumstances: no medical insurance or inadequate insurance coverage, medical debt, illness‐related job loss for the pALS, and illness‐related job loss for the cALS. The 60 campaigns used to develop the codebook were not included in the final summative content analysis.

Content Analysis

Two members of the research team (MN, NP) independently coded the same 100 campaigns using Quirkos software (version 2.5.3). Intercoder reliability was calculated using the Cohen κ coefficient. Cohen's kappa was ≥ 0.67 for all fundraising request categories and subcategories, as well as all financial circumstance categories. A subsequent 200 campaigns were coded independently by the same two coders, and manual review of an additional 20 overlapping campaigns confirmed ongoing reliability.

Statistical Analysis

We used descriptive statistics to count the frequency of each expense category and each financial circumstance category. Any given code could only be counted once per campaign. Each campaign could include several codes. Quotes included in this text and tables have been minimally modified for clarity, addressing spelling, punctuation, and grammar with care not to alter the meaning of the text. Quotes are taken directly from publicly available GoFundMe campaigns; however, names of individuals have been redacted.

Results

We identified 689 campaigns that met our initial inclusion criteria. We randomly selected 320 of these campaigns for our summative content analysis. We excluded 54 total campaigns, of which 46 campaigns were written for people without ALS and 8 campaigns were fundraising for medical research or ALS‐related nonprofit organizations. Our final analysis included 266 campaigns. Of these, only 14.7% (n = 39) explicitly noted that they were written by the pALS or the cALS. The remaining campaigns were written by other loved ones, including family members and co‐workers, or did not clearly specify who had written the campaign.

Some campaigns focused on raising money for a single major expense. For example, “We are trying to raise money to assist him and his family with a bathroom remodel in his home to accommodate his wheelchair in and out of the bathroom.” Others presented a list of needs: “The funds will help with a mobile power chair to get around in, a modified bed, a power lift chair in the living room for her to sit in, a van conversion so her power chair….” Additional quotes for each fundraising category throughout this manuscript are available in Table S3.

Fundraising Categories

Therapeutics

Many campaigns requested assistance with therapeutics, including treatments and medications (Table 1). Some campaigns specifically mentioned which medications they requested funds for; sometimes these were not FDA‐approved. For example, 13 campaigns requested funds for stem cell or gene therapy. Campaign writers noted that access to these non‐FDA approved treatments was “urgent” and could be “life‐saving”. For example, one campaign writer said of stem cell therapy: “Time is of the essence for her and this needs to happen fast. I want to see this and believe that miracles can happen.” Other campaigns requested money for other therapeutics including ALS‐specific medications such as edaravone and sodium phenylbutyrate/taurursodiol (which has since been withdrawn from the US market), treatments for ALS‐related symptoms, and other experimental treatments. Campaigns requesting funds for experimental treatments often mentioned that the cost was not covered by insurance, with reported costs ranging from $3000 to $475,000.

Hospital Bills

Few campaigns specifically requested assistance with hospital bills. When mentioned, 7 of these 12 campaigns included hospital bills in a longer list of needs: “We would appreciate any help for medical expenses, co‐pays, gas to the ALS clinic in Indianapolis, hospital stays and home equipment.”

Equipment

Over a quarter of campaigns requested funds for equipment, most commonly motorized or electric wheelchairs and lift equipment related to loss of limb function. For example, one campaign described “as [pALS] becomes less able to help lift himself we are having to purchase a powerlift to help get him in and out of bed and the wheelchair.”

Care Assistance

A fifth of campaigns requested assistance with care needs, which typically consisted of in‐home caregiver support (n = 48). Campaigns seeking funds for in‐home support often mentioned a strong desire to avoid facility placement: “My mother promised [pALS] that a nursing home would never be an option.” In‐home support included nurses, “trained and skilled caregivers,” and paid help for simple tasks that “most of us are able to do even without thought,” such as daily self‐care activities.

Campaigns described that caregiving assistance was either not covered by insurance or was covered for an insufficient number of hours per week. As a result, families paid out‐of‐pocket for additional caregiving hours. One writer noted, “this type of care runs several thousand dollars each month.”

Accessibility

Many campaigns mentioned accessibility barriers for pALS once they began requiring wheelchairs. Some requested help with home modifications; for example, “[pALS'] modest home needs to be expanded to accommodate his wheelchair in the bathroom, his bedroom, and the living room.” A fifth of campaigns requested assistance with accessible transportation, typically wheelchair‐accessible vans.

Travel for Specialized Medical Care

Some campaigns requested funds to cover travel to routine medical appointments. One campaign described the distance to an ALS‐focused clinic as prohibitively far: “The family has been making trips to Mayo Clinic to see specialists to try to understand how to live with and deal with this disease. The costs of trips back and forth to Mayo will inevitably put a financial strain on [pALS and spouse].” Other campaigns requested money so that they could travel to academic medical centers that offered clinical trials or experimental therapies.

Nonmedical Requests

Almost a quarter of campaigns made requests for nonmedical expenses, such as rent, utilities, house cleaning services, home maintenance, and “basic necessities of daily living.” Campaigns sometimes mentioned the specific impact the ALS diagnosis had on ability to pay for these services or the need to pay for them due to inability to perform tasks themselves. Multiple campaigns sought funds for relocating or a new home purchase related to the ALS diagnosis. For example: “we will need the financial means to help him with the move and also help him pay for a new place.”

Quality of life goals also featured heavily among nonmedical expenses, including leisure travel, modified recumbent bicycles, video games, and art supplies. Other campaigns anticipated family costs after the person's death, such as educational costs for pALS' children.

Financial Circumstances Surrounding the Request for Funds

Almost half of campaigns described the financial circumstances that prompted the crowdfunding campaign (Table 2). A quarter of campaigns reported that while the pALS was enrolled in an insurance plan, the coverage was insufficient: “Medicare doesn't pay all of the bills for her care.” Some campaign writers described medical debt, such as depletion of savings and taking out loans to pay for their medical care. Multiple campaign writers mentioned having sold their home to make ends meet.

A third of campaigns reported the pALS had lost their job or were no longer working to their full income capacity. cALS also faced strain related to their jobs. Some described losing or quitting their jobs because of the pALS' caregiving needs. Others increased their work hours to compensate for the pALS' loss of income: “Her husband, who works to support them comes home to then support her. However with ALS, even the longest hours worked cannot keep up with costly therapy and treatments.” Other cALS sought help hiring home health aides so that they could remain employed.

Discussion

Our analysis of crowdfunding campaigns developed on behalf of pALS highlights the breadth of unmet care needs and quality of life goals for which people request additional financial support. Campaign descriptions ranged from searches for high‐cost curative therapy to motor deterioration requiring powered wheelchair support and the transportation and home modification needs that arise as pALS require more adaptive equipment. The resulting needs span both medical and nonmedical expenses, from travel for what people believe is curative treatment to planning for post‐mortem expenses. The rate of quality‐of‐life requests for travel and hobbies highlights how families may conceptualize care needs beyond clinical services. Alternatively, families may relegate such expenses to crowdfunding while using private funds for their care needs. The frequent mentions of crowdfunding for family needs beyond the pALS (such as for education for the children) highlight the financial impact that an ALS diagnosis has on the family unit as a whole. It is important for ALS care teams to understand the financial burden associated with ALS in order to help their patients receive holistic and complete care.

These findings align with prior research demonstrating high costs in the US throughout the ALS illness experience, with motor deterioration and resultant equipment, accessibility, and caregiving needs featuring prominently in crowdfunding campaigns [4, 8, 11]. People living with ALS in other high‐ and middle‐income countries similarly have high out‐of‐pocket costs [5, 12]. However, the extent of financial burden experienced by patients outside of the US has not been explored. Most international studies have focused on capturing societal costs [13].

We identified a number of campaigns that sought funds to pay for non‐FDA approved therapies (including 13 for gene and stem cell therapy). Prior work has shown that patients and families crowdfund for unproven therapies to treat a wide range of illnesses, including multiple sclerosis, autism spectrum disorder, cancer, and spinal cord injuries [14, 15]. Spending on unproven therapies could reduce funds available to pay for necessary care, including approved therapies, equipment, and care assistance. People living with ALS and their families may benefit from frank and direct conversations with their medical teams about the risks and limited benefits of investing their resources towards such therapies.

Our findings also suggest that pALS, cALS, and their families may place substantial importance on nonmedical costs, highlighted by the pleas for funds for bucket list vacations, children's education, and more. While these nonmedical costs fall outside insurance jurisdiction, treatment teams should be aware of these expenses to provide guidance when appropriate. They reflect the decisions people make as they balance costs for quality‐of‐life needs and medical needs when faced with a terminal condition. Though these needs are categorized as “nonmedical,” they may be no less meaningful than addressing the traditional medical costs resulting from ALS.

Many campaigns reported the reduced financial circumstances that prompted them to post the campaign, including medical debt and lost income. These reports are consistent with the substantial economic burden of ALS: among pALS, the median time to leaving the workforce is 12 months after onset [6] and families of pALS lose an average of almost $15,000 in income annually [3]. Prior studies have found that lost income is common among crowdfunding campaign writers for other neurologic disorders as well [9]. ALS care teams should consider discussing the potential for job loss early on in the disease process, so that families can anticipate the impact it may have on their finances.

Substantial efforts have been made in recent decades to address the cost of care and improve access to ALS‐specific care. In 2001, the 24‐month waiting period for Medicare coverage was waived for pALS, improving Medicare and Social Security access [16]. In 2008, ALS became a military service‐connected condition, allowing most Veterans to access full VA benefits for their ALS care needs [17]. In 2020, the 5 month entitlement waiting period before receiving disability insurance benefits was waived for pALS [16]. These policies have greatly improved access to treatments and services for pALS. However, many aspects of care remain expensive for many patients, including equipment, home modifications, and caregiving.

Analysis of crowdfunding campaigns for other medical conditions demonstrates that those living with ALS have some unique needs. In a review of campaigns created on behalf of those with diabetes, many campaigns requested funds for items related to medications and disease control, which were not as dominant in ALS given the relative lack of therapeutic options [10]. Among those receiving cancer care, most crowdfunding campaigns sought funds for medical bills and medical travel, and many reported lost wages [18]. A review of campaigns for those seeking organ transplantation highlighted the dominance of costs from relocation and lost wages [19]. And those with Lyme disease most commonly campaigned for funds for unproven treatments and related travel [20]. Similar to cancer, transplantation, and Lyme disease campaigns, we found that many ALS‐related campaigns requested funds for medical bills, travel, lost wages, and unproven treatments. However, the bulk of fundraising requests were for specialized equipment, accessibility modifications, and care assistance, reflecting the progressive nature of the ALS disease course and its associated expenses [4].

This study has several limitations. First, we fully relied on campaign writers' self‐reports and had no means of verifying their claims. The true differences in financial circumstances between campaigns asking for funds for medical needs and those pursuing funds for leisure travel are not known. Second, campaign writers require technologic literacy and access to broadband so our findings may not be generalizable to pALS who do not regularly use or access the internet. Prior studies have shown that lower neighborhood deprivation index is associated with greater crowdfunding success, perhaps because their social networks include a larger proportion of acquaintances with disposable income [21]. Third, the GoFundMe site requires a bank account for fund transfer, further reducing the ability of the lowest income pALS to participate. Ultimately, the campaigns analyzed may represent only a small subsection of people who are unable to afford all their care needs. Finally, we included GoFundMe campaigns posted between 2010 and 2020, so we are unable to assess for any changes that may have occurred in ALS coverage (e.g., Medicare waiting periods), or treatment options in the last 6 years.

Conclusion

This analysis of crowdfunding campaigns highlights the financial requests made on behalf of pALS and cALS, which varied from seeking a potential cure, navigating accessibility needs within and outside the home, and searching for caregiving support. Our findings provide a window into the costs people associate with their ALS diagnosis and how those financial needs are not currently being met by existing systems. These findings highlight the importance of proactive communication by healthcare professionals with pALS about their personal priorities, goals, and financial needs.
