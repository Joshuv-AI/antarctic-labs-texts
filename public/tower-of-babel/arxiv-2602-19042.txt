Quantum Error Correction and Dynamical Decoupling: Better Together or Apart?
Victor Kasatkin, Mario Morford-Oberst, Arian Vezvaee, Daniel A. Lidar

Abstract
Quantum error correction (QEC) and dynamical decoupling (DD) are widely used tools for protecting quantum information, and a natural goal is to combine them to outperform either approach alone. Such a benefit is not automatic: physical DD pulses can conflict with an encoded subspace, and QEC performance is determined by the errors that survive decoding, which need not be those suppressed by DD. We analyze a hybrid memory cycle in which DD is implemented logically (LDD) using elements of the normalizer of an [ [ n , k , d ] ] [[n,k,d]] stabilizer code and is followed by a round of syndrome measurement and recovery (or, in the detection setting, postselection on a trivial syndrome). In an effective Pauli model with physical error probability p p , LDD suppression factor p DD p_{\rm DD} , and recovery imperfection rate p QEC p_{\rm QEC} (or p QED p_{\rm QED} ), we derive closed-form entanglement-fidelity expressions for QEC-only, LDD-only, physical DD (as a baseline), and the hybrid LDD+QEC protocol. The formulas are expressed via a small set of code-dependent weight enumerator polynomials, making the role of the decoder and the LDD group explicit. For ideal recovery we obtain a necessary-and-sufficient criterion for when LDD+QEC outperforms QEC-only: the conditional fraction of uncorrectable Pauli errors is larger in the LDD-suppressed sector than in the unsuppressed sector. In the low-noise regime we give a simple sufficient design rule guaranteeing hybrid advantage, namely that LDD suppresses at least one minimum-weight uncorrectable Pauli error for the chosen recovery map, and we show how stabilizer-equivalent choices of LDD generators can be used to enforce this condition. We supplement our analysis with numerical results for the [ [ 7 , 1 , 3 ] ] [[7,1,3]] Steane code and a [ [ 13 , 1 , 3 ] ] [[13,1,3]] code, mapping regions of hybrid-protocol advantage in parameter space beyond the small- p p regime. Our work illustrates the need for co-design of the code, decoder, and logical decoupling group, and clarifies the conditions under which the hybrid LDD+QEC protocol is advantageous.

Introduction

Protecting quantum information against noise is essential for long-lived quantum memories and for fault-tolerant quantum computation
Gottesman 2010
;
Lidar and Brun 2013
;
Terhal 2015
.
Two widely used approaches are quantum error correction (QEC), which actively diagnoses and corrects errors using redundancy and measurements
Shor 1995
;
Steane 1996
;
Gottesman 1997
;
Knill and Laflamme 1997
;
Calderbank et al. 1998
;
Knill et al. 2000
;
Kribs et al. 2005
, and dynamical decoupling (DD), which uses open-loop control to average out certain error mechanisms
Viola and Lloyd 1998
;
Viola et al. 1999
;
Zanardi 1999
;
Vitali and Tombesi 1999
;
Khodjasteh and Lidar 2005
;
Uhrig 2007
;
Genov et al. 2017
.

It is natural to try to combine QEC and DD in order to improve overall performance.
However, because these techniques have different noise and operating assumptions, it is not
a priori
clear when they are complementary and when they compete.
In particular, given a fixed hardware noise environment and a fixed code, when does adding DD improve the performance of a QEC cycle, and when does it provide little benefit, or even degrade performance once imperfections are included?
This problem was analyzed in terms of resource overhead and the fault-tolerance accuracy threshold in a hybrid DD-QEC setting, where DD pulses are implemented at the physical level
Ng et al. 2011
.
An alternative approach is to apply DD as the logical operations of a code
(encoded DD
Viola 2002
), in particular as logical dynamical decoupling
(LDD), in which the decoupling operations are chosen to preserve the codespace
(e.g., by using elements of the code normalizer) while averaging away errors
that do not commute with a specified logical decoupling
group
Lidar 2008
;
Paz-Silva and Lidar 2013
;
Vezvaee et al. 2025a
.
It is an open question whether a hybrid LDD+QEC protocol is always better than either approach in isolation.

In this work, we analyze a hybrid strategy in which LDD or any other Pauli-group-based DD sequence is implemented on an encoded block
and is followed by stabilizer syndrome measurement and recovery.
More specifically, we focus on a single “encode-wait-decode” cycle for an
[[n,k,d]]
stabilizer code and compare four strategies:
QEC-only, LDD-only, physical DD on
k
unencoded qubits (as a baseline),
and the hybrid protocol LDD+QEC (LDD followed by one round of syndrome measurement and recovery).
The noise during the waiting time is modeled as an effective Pauli channel
parameterized by a physical Pauli error probability
p

and a DD suppression parameter
p_{\rm DD}
that rescales the contribution of errors that do not commute with the chosen decoupling group (followed by renormalization).
Imperfections in syndrome extraction and/or recovery are captured by a phenomenological recovery-failure parameter
p_{\rm QEC}
;
we also treat an error-detection setting with an analogous parameter
p_{\rm QED}
.

We quantify performance using the entanglement fidelity
F
, equivalently the probability of no net logical fault after the protocol.
Our main goal is to extract checkable criteria for when LDD+QEC improves upon QEC-only (and when it does not), as well as to compare both protocols to LDD-only and physical DD.
To do so, we derive the dependence of
F
(and hence the logical failure probability
1-F
) on the code, the decoder, and the LDD group.
Due to the generality of
our approach, many results we initially developed for LDD apply,
in fact, to any
nontrivial Pauli decoupling group
(even if it does not consist of logical operators).

We summarize our main results and takeaways as follows.

1.

Analytical fidelity formulas.

We derive closed-form expressions for the entanglement fidelity of all four strategies
in terms of a small set of weight enumerator polynomials (WEPs)
that count Pauli errors by weight within subsets defined by the stabilizer code,
the recovery map, and the (L)DD group (see
1
and
1
).
These results quantify how performance depends on specific design choices.

2.

An exact criterion for hybrid-protocol advantage under perfect recovery.

In the idealized perfect recovery setting (
p_{\rm QEC}=0
),
we give a necessary and sufficient condition for when LDD+QEC outperforms QEC-only
(
2
):
the hybrid protocol helps precisely when the fraction of uncorrectable errors
is larger among errors suppressed by LDD than among errors it leaves unsuppressed.
Intuitively, LDD must target the errors that survive decoding.

3.

A low-noise criterion for hybrid-protocol advantage.

In the limit
p\to 0
, we prove a simple sufficient condition guaranteeing that
LDD+QEC outperforms QEC-only for all sufficiently small

(for any fixed
p_{\rm DD}<1
):
it suffices that LDD suppress at least one minimum-weight uncorrectable Pauli error
(
3

Moreover, when the sufficient condition fails (

with nontrivial syndrome (a condition that holds, for example, whenever

dressing an LDD generator by a stabilizer can modify the suppressed sector (without changing the logical action)
so as to enforce
\beta^{\prime}=\alpha
and thereby recover the sufficient-condition regime
(
3

4.

Robustness to imperfect recovery

If there is a strict hybrid-protocol advantage at
p_{\rm QEC}=0

then the advantage persists for sufficiently small
p_{\rm QEC}>0
by continuity
(
1
).

5.

Extension to error detection.

We also derive analogous WEP-based expressions and comparison criteria
for an error-detection setting
that trades acceptance probability against conditional fidelity
(
4
and
2
).

6.

Numerical corroboration.

We perform numerical case studies (including the
[[7,1,3]]
and
[[13,1,3]]
codes)
that map regions of advantage in the
(p_{\rm DD},p_{\rm QEC})
plane beyond the low-

theorem regimes,
and illustrate that the hybrid protocol’s advantage
depends on whether the chosen LDD group suppresses the decoder’s dominant uncorrectable errors

Section

V

The main take-home message of this work is the following:
DD and QEC are “better together” when logical decoupling
is chosen to suppress the Pauli errors that result in logical failure after decoding
and the probability of physical errors is low enough for QEC to be effective.
Our WEP formulas and comparison theorems make this criterion explicit and checkable.

II.3
Protocol and Error model

Throughout, we assume the following protocol.

1.

A state is encoded into some code (trivial code or QEC).

2.

We wait for some time during which we do or do not apply (L)DD.

3.

We decode the state, possibly correcting it using QEC.

We assume that the encoding is perfect, so there are only two types of errors: those happening during the waiting time and errors due to imperfect error correction.
More specifically, we assume a simplified Pauli error model in which the state
\rho
transforms as

\rho\ \longmapsto\ \sum_{E^{\prime}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}\Pr(E^{\prime})E^{\prime}\rho(E^{\prime})^{\dagger},

(4)

where
\Pr(\bullet)
is a probability distribution on
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
.
The right hand side
E^{\prime}\rho(E^{\prime})^{\dagger}
is defined as
E\rho E^{\dagger}
for
E\in E^{\prime}
.
This is unambiguous because replacing
E
with
e^{i\phi}E

leaves
E\rho E^{\dagger}
unchanged because the global phase cancels.

We introduce an effective “DD inverse-strength” parameter
p_{\rm DD}\in[0,1]
, which scales the probabilities of DD-suppressed errors to capture the residual error strength after applying DD.
We assume that the DD sequence does not introduce additional error mechanisms beyond those already absorbed into this effective
p_{\rm DD}
; this is best interpreted as either using pulse-robust DD sequences
Quiroz and Lidar 2013
;
Genov et al. 2017
or having independently characterized control imperfections and folded them into the fitted value of
p_{\rm DD}
.
The ideal limit
p_{\rm DD}=0
corresponds to perfect error suppression (of the DD-suppressed sector) and is used only as a formal benchmark; in any realistic implementation one expects
p_{\rm DD}>0
due to finite pulse widths, calibration errors, and finite-bandwidth filtering.

With this in mind, we assume the following error model for the waiting time.
In the absence of DD (equivalently,
p_{\rm DD}=1
, i.e., no suppression via DD), the probability of error
E^{\prime}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
is

\Pr(E^{\prime})=(1-p)^{n-w}\Bigl(\frac{p}{3}\Bigr)^{w},

(5)

where
w=\wt(E^{\prime})
is the number of non-identity single-qubit factors in the canonical representative of
E^{\prime}
.
When DD is applied, the probabilities of errors
E^{\prime}
that anticommute with
\tilde{G}
(i.e.,
E^{\prime}\notin\tilde{G}^{\perp}
) are multiplied by
p_{\rm DD}
, and then all probabilities are renormalized so that they still sum to
1
.

For the QEC case, we assume that with probability
1-p_{\rm QEC}
error correction applies the recovery
D(\sigma)
corresponding to the measured syndrome
\sigma
, and with probability
p_{\rm QEC}
it applies a uniformly random Pauli recovery consistent with that syndrome, but only when a nontrivial syndrome is measured; if the measured syndrome is trivial, the state is left unchanged.
Conditioned on a fixed nonzero syndrome, any two syndrome-consistent recoveries differ by a zero-syndrome operator, i.e., by an element of
\pi(C(\mathcal{S}))
, and hence (modulo
\pi(\mathcal{S})
) by a logical Pauli in
\pi(C(\mathcal{S}))/\pi(\mathcal{S})\cong\pi(\mathcal{L})
.
Therefore, under our assumption that the recovery in the case of decoder failure
is chosen uniformly at random among syndrome-consistent recoveries,
the induced logical Pauli is uniform over the
4^{k}
logical classes
and the probability of the trivial logical class is
4^{-k}
.
Thus, after this step the state lies in the codespace
and the remaining effective errors are logical Paulis.
We call this whole procedure
(regardless of whether
D(\sigma)
or a random recovery was applied) a decoder.
1
1

1

“decoder” often denotes the classical post-processing algorithm

In contrast, in this work “decoder” means the effective single-round

Under this model,

p_{\rm DD}=0
corresponds to perfect suppression of all errors that anticommute with
\tilde{G}
,
and

We treat the case when a QEC code is used but its recovery step is skipped as
p_{\rm QEC}=1
,
i.e., whenever a nontrivial syndrome is detected we apply
a uniformly random syndrome-consistent recovery.
Conditioned on detecting a nontrivial syndrome,
such a random recovery yields the correct logical frame with probability
4^{-k}

(and hence induces a logical error with probability
1-4^{-k}

II.4
Faults
vs
errors

We distinguish between faults, which are stochastic malfunction events in the implementation,
and errors, which denote the resulting effective operators acting on the data.
In the effective-Pauli model used throughout this work,
the cumulative effect of all faults during the memory (waiting) interval
is represented by a single random phase-stripped Pauli error
E^{\prime}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
;
its weight is
\wt(E^{\prime})
, the number of qubits on which it acts nontrivially.
Imperfections in syndrome extraction and/or recovery
are modeled at the event level by a recovery fault:
when a nontrivial syndrome is measured,
the ideal recovery
D(\sigma)
is applied with probability
1-p_{\rm QEC}
,
while with probability
p_{\rm QEC}
a uniformly random syndrome-consistent Pauli recovery is applied
(and if the measured syndrome is trivial, the state is left unchanged).

Given a fixed recovery map
D
, we call a Pauli error
E^{\prime}
correctable if
D(\syn(E^{\prime}))E^{\prime}\in\pi(\mathcal{S})
, i.e., if ideal decoding returns the state to the codespace without inducing a nontrivial logical action.
Otherwise
E^{\prime}
is uncorrectable.
A logical error (or logical fault) refers to the induced action on the encoded subspace
(equivalently, the coset of the residual operator modulo the stabilizer,
an element of
\pi(C(\mathcal{S}))/\pi(\mathcal{S})\simeq\pi(\mathcal{L})
).
A logical failure event occurs when the net operator after the full protocol has a nontrivial logical component; its probability is
1-F
.

III.1

Lemma 1

With no DD (i.e.,

With DD, the probability of no error is

\displaystyle F_{\rm DD}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-p)^{k}}{(1-p)^{k}+p_{\rm DD}\bigl[1-(1-p)^{k}\bigr]}

(9a)

\displaystyle=1-kpp_{\rm DD}+O(p^{2})

(9b)

as
p\to 0
(uniformly in
p_{\rm DD}
).

Proof.

Recall that without DD, we have
Eq.

5
.
The probability of no error (
w=0
) is then
F_{\rm DD}=\Pr[\pi(I)]=(1-p)^{k}
.

In the DD-phys case, all Pauli errors not in
\tilde{G}^{\perp}=\{\pi(I)\}
anticommute with at least one element of
\tilde{G}
and therefore have their probabilities multiplied by
p_{\rm DD}\in[0,1]
.
Thus, the renormalized probability of no error, i.e., the fidelity
F_{\rm DD}
, is

F_{\rm DD}=\frac{\Pr[\pi(I)]}{\Pr[\pi(I)]+p_{\rm DD}\bigl(1-\Pr[\pi(I)]\bigr)},

(10)

which is
Eq.

9a
.

Factoring
(1-p)^{k}
out of the denominator yields
F_{\rm DD}=[1+p_{\rm DD}((1-p)^{-k}-1)]^{-1}
.
For
p\ll 1
we have
(1-p)^{-k}=1+kp+O(p^{2})
.
Hence,

F_{\rm DD}=\bigl[1+kpp_{\rm DD}+O(p^{2})\bigr]^{-1}=1-kpp_{\rm DD}+O(p^{2}),

(11)

which is
Eq.

9b
.
∎

III.2
Weight enumerators

To derive the fidelities in the other three cases,
we need to correctly aggregate probabilities over different classes of physical errors
(e.g., stabilizers vs. logical operators, suppressed vs. unsuppressed, correctable vs. uncorrectable).
We do this using weight enumerator polynomials
Shor and Laflamme 1997
;
Rains 1999
.
For any subset
A\subset\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
, its weight-enumerator polynomial (WEP) is defined as

W(A;z)\coloneqq\sum_{E^{\prime}\in A}z^{\wt(E^{\prime})}=\sum_{w=0}^{n}W_{w}(A)z^{w},

(12)

where
W_{w}(A)
is the number of elements of
A
of weight
w
, i.e.,

W_{w}(A)=\#\bigl\{E^{\prime}\in A\bigl|\wt(E^{\prime})=w\bigr\}
.

For example,
\wt(\pi(I))=0
, hence
W(\{\pi(I)\};z)=1
; for a single qubit,
\wt(X)=\wt(Y)=\wt(Z)=1
, so
W(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1};z)=(1+3z)
; for
n
qubits,

W(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n};z)=\sum_{w=0}^{n}\binom{n}{w}(3z)^{w}=(1+3z)^{n}.

(13)

In our depolarizing waiting-time model [
Eq.

5
],
errors of the same weight have the same probability.
Writing

(1-p)^{n-w}\Bigl(\frac{p}{3}\Bigr)^{w}=(1-p)^{n}\Bigl(\frac{p}{3-3p}\Bigr)^{w},

(14)

we see that for any subset
A\subset\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
,

\sum_{E^{\prime}\in A}\Pr(E^{\prime})=(1-p)^{n}W\left(A;z\right),\quad z\coloneqq\frac{p}{3-3p}.

(15)

Hence, when forming conditional probabilities after DD renormalization,
it is sufficient to evaluate WEPs at
z=p/(3-3p)
; common prefactors cancel.

Similarly,
W\bigl(\tilde{G}^{\perp};z\bigr)
is the WEP of errors unsuppressed by the decoupling group
\tilde{G}
, while
W\bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\setminus\tilde{G}^{\perp};z\bigr)
is the WEP of errors suppressed (rescaled) by
\tilde{G}
.

To handle the protocols that use QEC, we introduce the set
{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c}}
of correctable physical errors, i.e., the errors that are corrected to the trivial logical class by the chosen decoding map
D
:

{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c}}\coloneqq\left\{E^{\prime}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\ \middle|\ D(\syn(E^{\prime}))E^{\prime}\in\pi(\mathcal{S})\right\}.

(16)

This set splits into (trivial) stabilizer errors
\pi(\mathcal{S})
with zero syndrome, and nontrivial correctable errors
{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c}}\setminus\pi(\mathcal{S})
with nonzero syndrome.
Conversely, the set of uncorrectable errors is
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\setminus{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c}}
, which splits into
(i) nontrivial logical errors
\pi(\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L})\setminus\pi(\mathcal{S})
with zero syndrome, and
(ii) detectable but uncorrectable errors
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\setminus{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c}}\setminus\pi(\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L})
with nonzero syndrome.

Considering DD, we obtain additional sets of physical significance.
For example,
({\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c}}\cap\tilde{G}^{\perp})\setminus\pi(\mathcal{S})
is the set of correctable errors that are unsuppressed and have nonzero syndrome, and
(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\setminus\tilde{G}^{\perp})\setminus\pi(\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L})
is the set of suppressed errors that are detectable.

III.3
DD-phys again

Lemma 2
.

In the DD-phys strategy, the fidelity can be written as

F_{\rm DD}=\frac{\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$-{St}}+p_{\rm DD}\textup{S-St}}{\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$}+p_{\rm DD}\textup{S}},

(17)

and for
\tilde{G}=\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{k}
(so that
\tilde{G}^{\perp}=\{\pi(I)\}
) this reduces to
Eq.

9a
.

Proof.

For any
E^{\prime}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
of weight
w=\wt(E^{\prime})
,
Eq.

5
can be rewritten as

\Pr(E^{\prime})=(1-p)^{n}\Bigl(\frac{p}{3-3p}\Bigr)^{w}.

(18)

Thus, evaluating all WEPs at
z=\frac{p}{3-3p}
,
the common prefactor
(1-p)^{n}
cancels between numerator and denominator after DD renormalization.

In our DD model, errors in
\tilde{G}^{\perp}
are left unchanged
(they are unsuppressed, with WEP denoted
\not\!\mathrm{S}
in
Table

1
),
while errors in
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\setminus\tilde{G}^{\perp}
have their probabilities rescaled by a factor
p_{\rm DD}
(suppressed, denoted
S
).
Therefore, the total unnormalized probability mass after rescaling is proportional to

W\bigl(\tilde{G}^{\perp};z\bigr)+p_{\rm DD}W\bigl(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\setminus\tilde{G}^{\perp};z\bigr)=\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$}+p_{\rm DD}\textup{S},

(19)

which gives the fidelity (normalization) denominator.

The “no-logical-error” events are precisely stabilizer errors, i.e., elements of
\pi(\mathcal{S})
.
These split into an unsuppressed subset
\pi(\mathcal{S})\cap\tilde{G}^{\perp}
(unsuppressed stabilizer, denoted
\not\!\mathrm{S}
-St
) and a suppressed subset
\pi(\mathcal{S})\setminus\tilde{G}^{\perp}
(suppressed stabilizer, denoted
S-St
).
Hence the corresponding unnormalized probability mass is proportional to

W\bigl(\pi(\mathcal{S})\cap\tilde{G}^{\perp};z\bigr)+p_{\rm DD}W\bigl(\pi(\mathcal{S})\setminus\tilde{G}^{\perp};z\bigr)=\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$-{St}}+p_{\rm DD}\textup{S-St},

(20)

which gives us the fidelity numerator.
Together, this yields
Eq.

17
.

To recover
Eq.

9a
, note that in the DD-phys case
n=k
,
\pi(\mathcal{S})=\{\pi(I)\}
, and for a
4^{k}
-element DD group we have
\tilde{G}=\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{k}
and thus
\tilde{G}^{\perp}=\{\pi(I)\}
.
Then
W(\{\pi(I)\};z)=1
,
W(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{k}\setminus\{\pi(I)\};z)=(1+3z)^{k}-1
, and
W(\pi(\mathcal{S})\setminus\tilde{G}^{\perp};z)=0
.
Substituting into
Eq.

17
gives

F_{\rm DD}=\frac{1}{1+p_{\rm DD}\bigl[(1+3z)^{k}-1\bigr]},

(21)

which becomes
Eq.

9a
after setting
z=p/(3-3p)
and simplifying.
∎

III.4
LDD+QEC

We are now prepared to state our first main technical result.

Theorem 1

(Hybrid LDD+QEC: infidelity under an effective Pauli noise model with DD suppression and decoder failure)
.

Consider an
[[n,k,d]]
stabilizer code with stabilizer group
\mathcal{S}
and logical Pauli group
\mathcal{L}

(so
\pi(\mathcal{S})\subseteq\pi(\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L})\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
).
Fix a (logical) dynamical-decoupling group
\tilde{G}

and let
\tilde{G}^{\perp}\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
denote the set of Pauli errors
that are not suppressed by the chosen (L)DD procedure
(with suppressed sector
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\setminus\tilde{G}^{\perp}
).

Assume the following effective Pauli noise model.
Writing
z\coloneqq p/(3-3p)
,
each Pauli
E^{\prime}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
of weight
\wt(E^{\prime})
contributes a factor
z^{\wt(E^{\prime})}
.
In addition, if
E^{\prime}
lies in the suppressed sector (
E^{\prime}\notin\tilde{G}^{\perp}
), its contribution is rescaled by a multiplicative
suppression factor
p_{\rm DD}
(with
p_{\rm DD}=1
meaning no DD suppression).

After the (L)DD step we perform one round of QEC:
we measure the stabilizer syndrome
\sigma
and apply a recovery.
If the measured syndrome is trivial, we apply no recovery.
If the measured syndrome is nontrivial, then with probability
1-p_{\rm QEC}
we apply a fixed (ideal) recovery
D(\sigma)
,
while with probability
p_{\rm QEC}
we instead apply a uniformly random Pauli recovery consistent with the measured syndrome.
Under this decoder-failure model, conditioned on any fixed nonzero syndrome, the induced logical Pauli is uniform over the
4^{k}

logical classes, so the probability of the trivial logical class is
4^{-k}
.

Let
{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c}}\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
denote the set of Pauli errors that are corrected to the stabilizer (no logical fault) by the
ideal decoder, i.e., those
E^{\prime}
such that
D(\syn(E^{\prime}))E^{\prime}\in\pi(\mathcal{S})
.

Then the entanglement infidelity of the resulting logical channel under the hybrid LDD+QEC protocol is

\displaystyle 1-F_{\rm Hyb}=\frac{1}{\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$}+p_{\rm DD}\textup{S}}\bigl(

\displaystyle[\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$-\!$\not\!\mathrm{C}$}+p_{\rm QEC}(\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$-C}-4^{-k}\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$-D})]

\displaystyle+p_{\rm DD}

\displaystyle[\textup{S-\!$\not\!\mathrm{C}$}+p_{\rm QEC}(\textup{S-C}-4^{-k}\textup{S-D})]\bigr).

(22)

All WEP tags are as in
Table

1
.

This result includes DD-phys, QEC-only, and LDD-only as special cases.
We give independent derivations of the latter two in
Appendices

B
and
C
;
these derivations go into more detail for each specific case than the unified proof we give here.

Proof.

Just as in the DD-phys case [
Eq.

19
], after DD rescaling, the (unnormalized) total probability weight is proportional to

W(\tilde{G}^{\perp};z)+p_{\rm DD}W(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\setminus\tilde{G}^{\perp};z)=\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$}+p_{\rm DD}\textup{S},

(23)

which yields the denominator in
1
after renormalization.
However, the sets involved are different from the DD-phys case, since now
n>k
.

For the numerator, we count (via WEPs) the total weight of physical Pauli errors
E^{\prime}
that produce a nontrivial residual logical Pauli after the protocol.
This gives the infidelity (rather than the fidelity) in
1
.

Recall that in our model, decoder failure occurs only when the measured syndrome is nontrivial.
Conditioned on any fixed nonzero syndrome, the (failed) decoder applies a uniformly random syndrome-consistent Pauli recovery, so the induced logical Pauli is uniform over the
4^{k}
logical classes.
Hence, under decoder failure, the probability of landing in the trivial logical class is
4^{-k}
.
With this in mind, we now split errors into the two sectors used throughout, i.e., unsuppressed vs. suppressed by LDD.

1.

Unsuppressed sector
(
E^{\prime}\in\tilde{G}^{\perp}
; no factor of
p_{\rm DD}
).

Using the
Table

1
notation,

\not\!\mathrm{S}
-​
\not\!\mathrm{C}
= WEP of unsuppressed uncorrectable errors;

\not\!\mathrm{S}
-C
= WEP of unsuppressed correctable detected errors (nonzero syndrome);

\not\!\mathrm{S}
-D
= WEP of all unsuppressed detected errors (nonzero syndrome),
so in particular
\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$-C}\subset\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$-D}
.
We now do a three-step inclusion-exclusion count:

(i)

Baseline: count all uncorrectable errors as logical faults.
Any error counted by
\not\!\mathrm{S}
-​
\not\!\mathrm{C}
produces a nontrivial logical Pauli under ideal decoding (or is an undetected logical operator, for which no recovery is ever applied), so we start with a baseline contribution
\not\!\mathrm{S}
-​
\not\!\mathrm{C}
.

(ii)

Add: correctable detected errors matter only if the decoder fails.
Errors counted by
\not\!\mathrm{S}
-C
produce no logical fault under ideal decoding, but when the decoder fails (probability
p_{\rm QEC}
) we initially count them as contributing a logical fault.
This gives an additive term
p_{\rm QEC}\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$-C}
.

(iii)

Subtract: when the decoder fails, a detected error is accidentally harmless with probability
4^{-k}
.
Under decoder failure, for any detected error (correctable or uncorrectable) the resulting logical Pauli is uniform, so with probability
4^{-k}
we land in the trivial logical class and should not count a logical fault.
Since
\not\!\mathrm{S}
-D
counts all detected errors in the unsuppressed sector, we subtract the overcount
p_{\rm QEC}4^{-k}\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$-D}
.

Summing these three contributions, the unsuppressed-sector logical-fault weight is

\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$-\!$\not\!\mathrm{C}$}+p_{\rm QEC}\bigl(\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$-C}-4^{-k}\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$-D}\bigr)
.

2.

Suppressed sector
(
E^{\prime}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\setminus\tilde{G}^{\perp}
; overall factor of
p_{\rm DD}
).

The identical bookkeeping applies with the suppressed-sector
Table

1
quantities
\textup{S-\!$\not\!\mathrm{C}$},\textup{S-C},\textup{S-D}
,
and we multiply by
p_{\rm DD}
because these errors are suppressed by LDD.
Hence this sector contributes

p_{\rm DD}\Bigl[\textup{S-\!$\not\!\mathrm{C}$}+p_{\rm QEC}\bigl(\textup{S-C}-4^{-k}\textup{S-D}\bigr)\Bigr]
.

Summing the two sector contributions yields the stated numerator, and dividing by the normalization
\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$}+p_{\rm DD}\textup{S}
yields
1
.
∎

Reductions

We now reduce LDD+QEC to QEC-only, LDD-only, and DD-phys.

\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$-D}+\textup{S-D}=\textup{D}

\displaystyle 1-F_{\rm QEC}

LDD-only reduces to DD-phys when there is no encoding/decoding (

\pi(\mathcal{S})=\{\pi(I)\}

III.6
Comparison of LDD+QEC and QEC-only

We next show that in order to decide which of

is larger when

(ideal recovery), it suffices to compare two sector-wise fractions, and the conclusion is independent of the value of

Theorem 2

Assume
p_{\rm QEC}=0
and
p_{\rm DD}\in[0,1)
.
Then, for any fixed

We prove this using the following lemma:

Lemma 3
.

Let
A,B,C,D
be real numbers with
C,D>0
, and define
f(t)=\frac{A+tB}{C+tD}
for
t\in[0,1]

Then, for any
t\in[0,1)

f(t)>f(1)\iff\frac{A}{C}>\frac{B}{D}.

Proof.

Expanding
(A+tB)(C+D)>(A+B)(C+tD)
and canceling the common terms, we see that
f(t)>f(1)
is equivalent to
(1-t)AD>(1-t)BC
, which is equivalent to
A/C>B/D
(since
1-t>0
).
∎

Proof of
2
.

To interpret

\not\!\mathrm{S}
and

are the total WEP weights of the unsuppressed and suppressed sectors, respectively, while

\not\!\mathrm{S}
-​

Thus, the ratio

and this bias in what becomes suppressed yields the strict advantage

implying
\beta^{\prime}=\alpha
.

For part 3, note that uncorrectable errors
E^{\prime}
satisfying
\syn(E^{\prime})=0
are known
as nontrivial (i.e., non-stabilizer) logical operators,
and recall that the code distance
d
is defined as the minimal
distance of such operators.
Let
L^{\prime}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
be a weight-
d
nontrivial logical operator.
Then
L^{\prime}=E_{1}^{\prime}E_{2}^{\prime}
for
E_{1}^{\prime},E_{2}^{\prime}
of weights
\left\lfloor d/2\right\rfloor
and
\left\lceil d/2\right\rceil
, respectively.
Since
\syn(L^{\prime})=0
, we have
\syn(E_{1}^{\prime})=\syn(E_{2}^{\prime})
.
Let
\sigma\coloneqq\syn(E_{1}^{\prime})=\syn(E_{2}^{\prime})
.
If both
E_{1}^{\prime}
and
E_{2}^{\prime}
were correctable, then
D(\sigma)E_{1}^{\prime},D(\sigma)E_{2}^{\prime}\in\pi(\mathcal{S})
.
Writing
E_{i}^{\prime}=D(\sigma)^{-1}S_{i}
with
S_{i}\in\pi(\mathcal{S})
, we obtain

L^{\prime}\;=\;E_{1}^{\prime}E_{2}^{\prime}\;=\;D(\sigma)^{-2}S_{1}S_{2}\;\in\;\pi(\mathcal{S}),

(71)

(using
D(\sigma)^{2}=I
in the phase-stripped Pauli group),
which contradicts that
L^{\prime}
is a nontrivial logical operator.
Therefore at least one of
E_{1}^{\prime},E_{2}^{\prime}
is uncorrectable.
By definition,
\alpha
is the minimal weight of an uncorrectable error, hence

\alpha\leq\max\bigl(\wt(E_{1}^{\prime}),\wt(E_{2}^{\prime})\bigr)=\left\lceil d/2\right\rceil.

(72)

Since
d\geq 2
, we have
\alpha\leq\left\lceil d/2\right\rceil<d
.
Finally, let
E^{\prime}
be any weight-
\alpha
uncorrectable error.
If
\syn(E^{\prime})=0
, then
E^{\prime}
is a nontrivial logical operator of weight
\alpha<d
,
contradicting the definition of the code distance
d
.
Hence every weight-
\alpha
uncorrectable error satisfies
\syn(E^{\prime})\neq 0
, proving the precondition in part 2.

To prove part 4, assume to the contrary that
\tilde{G}=\pi(\mathcal{L})
,
\beta>\alpha
,
but
\syn(E^{\prime})=0
for some weight-
\alpha
uncorrectable error
E^{\prime}
.
Since
\syn(E^{\prime})=0
,
E^{\prime}
is a logical operator.
Since
\beta>\alpha
, we have
W_{\alpha}(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\setminus\tilde{G}^{\perp}\setminus{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c}})=0
.
Hence every weight-
\alpha
uncorrectable error lies in
\tilde{G}^{\perp}
and therefore commutes with all elements of
\tilde{G}
.
With
\tilde{G}=\pi(\mathcal{L})
, this means that the logical class of
E^{\prime}
commutes with all logical Paulis.
For
k\geq 1
, the center of the logical Pauli group is trivial, so
E^{\prime}
acts as the identity on the codespace.
Together with
\syn(E^{\prime})=0
, this implies
E^{\prime}\in\pi(\mathcal{S})
, contradicting that
E^{\prime}
is uncorrectable.
∎

We can now describe an explicit “stabilizer-dressing” procedure to modify
\tilde{G}
when it does not satisfy the condition of part 1 of
3
, provided there exists a minimal-weight uncorrectable error with nonzero syndrome:
(1) pick any generator list for
\tilde{G}
and let
g
be one generator;
(2) pick any uncorrectable error
E^{\prime}
of minimal weight
\alpha
with
\syn(E^{\prime})\neq 0
;
(3) find a stabilizer generator (or any stabilizer element)
S\in\pi(\mathcal{S})
that anticommutes with
E^{\prime}
;
(4) replace
g
by
gS
and rebuild the decoupling group.
The resulting group
\tilde{G}^{\prime}
has
\beta^{\prime}=\alpha
and therefore satisfies the condition of part 1, implying
F_{\rm Hyb}>F_{\rm QEC}
for sufficiently small
p
(with
p_{\rm QEC}=0
).

Note that if all weight-
\alpha
uncorrectable errors have zero syndrome, i.e., are undetected logical errors, then multiplying DD generators by stabilizers cannot change their commutation relations with those errors; achieving
\beta^{\prime}=\alpha
then requires changing the logical decoupling group itself rather than only its stabilizer dressing.

III.7.4
LDD+QEC vs QEC-only: examples when
\beta>\alpha

A clean sufficient condition for an asymptotic hybrid-protocol advantage is given by part 1 of
3
: if the leading uncorrectable weight in the unsuppressed sector and in the suppressed sector coincide (
\beta=\alpha
), then for sufficiently small
p
the hybrid protocol satisfies
F_{\rm Hyb}>F_{\rm QEC}
(for any fixed
0\leq p_{\rm DD}<1
).
An interesting question is what happens when this condition is violated.
When
\beta>\alpha
,
the theorem is generally inconclusive: the hybrid and QEC-only protocols share the same leading
O(p^{\alpha})
scaling,
and the ordering can depend on subleading terms that are sensitive to how uncorrectable errors are distributed between the suppressed and unsuppressed sectors.

We give two examples below that are chosen to illustrate two qualitatively different mechanisms by which
\beta=\alpha
can fail.
Our first example exhibits a structural mismatch between the chosen LDD group and the recovery map, so that the earliest suppressed uncorrectable events occur at a different weight than the earliest unsuppressed uncorrectable events; this places the hybrid-vs-QEC comparison in the delicate
\beta>\alpha
regime where an asymptotic advantage is not automatic.
Our second example shows that
\beta>\alpha
can also be an artifact of the
recovery/decoder choice: for simplicity, we use canonical syndrome representatives
and a decoder that is convenient to describe analytically, but such a choice can be suboptimal once the effective noise has been modified by LDD. In particular, a decoder
tailored to the original physical noise need not be optimal for the LDD-modified channel, and a suboptimal choice can mask an intrinsic hybrid-protocol advantage.

Part 2 of
3
provides a concrete “way out”
when the sufficient condition
\beta=\alpha
fails: under its hypotheses, one may dress an LDD generator by a stabilizer, which changes the suppressed/unsuppressed partition without changing the logical action of the decoupling cycle,
and can enforce
\beta^{\prime}=\alpha
so that part 1 applies.
In the examples below we keep the recovery map fixed and use the exact criterion of
2
to determine whether
F_{\rm Hyb}
exceeds
F_{\rm QEC}
when
\beta>\alpha
.

Thus, the examples should be read not only as demonstrations that the
\beta>\alpha
regime can arise in natural and analytically tractable settings, but also as illustrations of why decoder choice can matter in this regime.

IV.2
Modified protocol based on partial order

We define a partial order on pairs
(F,P_{A})
by writing
(F_{1},P_{A,1})\succeq(F_{2},P_{A,2})
iff
F_{1}\geq F_{2}
and

P_{A,1}(F_{1}-4^{-k})\geq P_{A,2}(F_{2}-4^{-k})
, where
F-4^{-k}
is the fidelity excess above the maximally mixed
k
-qubit state.
These conditions ensure that scenario 1 is at least as good as scenario 2 in the following operational sense: we can simulate scenario 2 using scenario 1 by optionally discarding accepted runs (to reduce acceptance) and, if
P_{A,1}<P_{A,2}
, by “padding” some of the rejected runs by outputting and accepting a fully mixed
k
-qubit state (whose fidelity is
4^{-k}
).

Concretely, when scenario 1 rejects, we additionally accept the fully mixed state with conditional probability

\frac{P_{A,2}-P_{A,1}}{1-P_{A,1}}
, so that the overall acceptance becomes
P_{A,2}
; this padding increases acceptance while contributing zero fidelity excess, and the inequality

P_{A,1}(F_{1}-4^{-k})\geq P_{A,2}(F_{2}-4^{-k})
ensures the resulting conditional fidelity is still at least
F_{2}
.

Two qualitative features are apparent.
First, in the low-noise regime the encoded strategies (QEC-only and LDD+QEC) eventually outperform the uncorrected strategies (DD-phys and LDD-only) as

This is because with

By contrast, DD-phys and LDD-only do not include an active correction step, so their logical failure probabilities remain dominated by lower-weight errors and scale more like

(up to

The resulting separation in slopes on the log-log plots explains why the encoded strategies dominate as

Second, within the encoded strategies, the hybrid protocol consistently improves upon QEC-only in the plotted range.

In the present Steane-code example (with the specified recovery map and LDD choice), this mechanism manifests visually as the blue curve lying below the red curve throughout the displayed
p

In the small-

Finally, the comparison with DD-phys highlights the role of

At larger physical error rates, DD-phys can yield a lower logical failure probability than the encoded strategies, because it directly suppresses the physical error channel while the finite-distance code and recovery map admit many higher-weight uncorrectable errors whose total probability grows rapidly with

Decreasing

V.2.3
Imperfect recovery

Realistic implementations of QEC are imperfect, and even phenomenologically small recovery failure rates can qualitatively change which error mechanisms dominate the logical failure probability.
When
p_{\rm QEC}>0

Consequently, the logical failure probability can receive contributions of order
p_{\rm QEC}p
from single-qubit errors followed by recovery failure, in addition to the purely uncorrectable-fault contributions that scale as
p^{\alpha}
in the perfect-recovery model.
This motivates exploring hybrid performance outside the idealized
p_{\rm QEC}=0
setting of
2

(p,p_{\rm DD},p_{\rm QEC})

\alpha

Intuitively, the hybrid protocol benefits from both mechanisms: QEC removes errors in the correctable set when recovery succeeds, while LDD reduces the rate at which errors (including low-weight ones that become harmful when recovery fails) occur in the first place.

The boundary observed when comparing LDD+QEC to DD-phys can be understood by inspecting the low-weight structure of the commutant

Recall that in our model, DD/LDD suppresses precisely those Pauli errors that anticommute with the decoupling group (their probabilities are multiplied by

, followed by renormalization), whereas errors in

For the Steane-code choice

Overall, these results reinforce that hybrid benefit is not automatic; it depends on whether the chosen LDD group suppresses the error classes that dominate logical failure for the specific code and recovery model, including how recovery imperfections determine which errors are most consequential.

VI
Summary

This work considers several complementary strategies for protecting quantum information: DD, LDD, QEC, and LDD+QEC.
QEC suppresses errors by encoding information into a stabilizer code and performing syndrome measurement and recovery, while DD uses open-loop control to average away parts of the noise.
Combining these ideas is not automatic: applying dynamical decoupling pulses at the physical level can conflict with the structure of an encoded subspace, whereas logical dynamical decoupling uses operations that preserve the codespace (e.g., elements of the normalizer) and can therefore be interleaved with an encoded memory.

We study a three-step memory cycle:
(i) encode into an
[[n,k,d]]
stabilizer code,
(ii) wait for a fixed duration while optionally applying DD/LDD,
(iii) decode, optionally performing QEC (or, in the detection setting, postselecting on a trivial syndrome).
The physical noise during the waiting time is modeled as an effective Pauli channel with physical Pauli error probability
p
;
DD is captured by a suppression parameter
p_{\rm DD}\in[0,1]
that rescales (down-weights) the probabilities of errors that anticommute with the chosen decoupling group prior to renormalization (
p_{\rm DD}=1
corresponds to no DD suppression, while
p_{\rm DD}=0
corresponds to ideal suppression of the DD-suppressed sector); and imperfections in the QEC (or QED) step are modeled by phenomenological parameters
p_{\rm QEC}
(or
p_{\rm QED}
) that randomize the recovery (or syndrome outcome) with the stated probability.
We quantify performance by the probability of no logical error after the full protocol,
F
in
Eq.

8
, which (as we show in
Appendix

A
) coincides with the standard entanglement fidelity.

Our main technical contribution is a unified, code- and control-dependent description of the logical fidelity for four strategies:
(i) physical DD on
k
qubits (DD-phys), (ii) QEC-only, (iii) LDD-only, and (iv) the hybrid LDD+QEC protocol.
For each strategy, the fidelity can be written in closed form in terms of a small collection of weight-enumerator polynomials (WEPs)
W(A;z)
evaluated at
z=p/(3-3p)
.
The relevant subsets
A
are defined by how Pauli errors relate to (a) the decoupling group (suppressed
vs
unsuppressed), (b) the code structure (stabilizers
vs
nontrivial logical operators), and (c) the chosen decoding map (corrected
vs
uncorrected).
This representation isolates where performance depends on the code, the decoder, and the LDD generating set, and it turns fidelity evaluation into counting problems over well-defined Pauli subsets (see the tag tables in
Tables

1
and
2
).

Beyond providing explicit expressions [e.g., the hybrid protocol’s infidelity in
1
], the WEP formulation yields transparent comparison criteria.
In the idealized limit of perfect QEC (
p_{\rm QEC}=0
),
2
gives an if-and-only-if condition for when LDD+QEC improves upon QEC-only: the hybrid protocol wins precisely when the fraction of uncorrectable errors is larger in the suppressed sector than in the unsuppressed sector.
In other words, LDD is beneficial when it preferentially suppresses the Pauli errors that QEC fails to correct.
The low-
p
analysis in
Section

III.7
gives further insight:
3
shows that if the LDD group suppresses at least one minimum-weight uncorrectable error, then for sufficiently small
p
the hybrid protocol necessarily outperforms QEC-only (for any fixed
p_{\rm DD}<1
).
Moreover, because LDD generators can be modified by stabilizers without changing their logical action,
3
identifies a simple design principle that can be used to ensure suppression of a lowest-weight uncorrectable error.

We also extend the framework from error correction to quantum error detection (QED) in
Section

IV
.
In the detection setting the natural figure of merit is two-dimensional: the logical fidelity conditioned on acceptance and the acceptance probability
P_{A}
(probability of measuring a trivial syndrome).
We derive closed-form expressions for both quantities under QED-only and the hybrid LDD+QED protocol, and we introduce a partial order on pairs
(F,P_{A})
to formalize when one strategy dominates another.
In direct analogy with the QEC case,
4
provides a comparison criterion (and
5
gives a useful sufficient condition) for when LDD improves QED performance.

Finally, in
Section

V
we evaluate the analytic formulas for representative codes and LDD choices to illustrate the theory and to probe regimes beyond the clean asymptotic assumptions.
For the
[[7,1,3]]
Steane code with the decoding map specified in
Appendix

D
, we find that in the
3
regime (
p_{\rm QEC}=0
) the hybrid LDD+QEC strategy outperforms QEC-only, LDD-only, and physical DD as
p\to 0
for the tested suppression values, consistent with the small-
p
ordering results.
When
p_{\rm QEC}
is allowed to vary (including settings that violate
p_{\rm QEC}=o(p^{\alpha-1})
), heat-map comparisons in the
(p_{\rm DD},p_{\rm QEC})
plane show that the region where LDD+QEC has a lower logical failure probability than competing strategies expands as
p
decreases, but also that very strong physical DD can remain competitive when
p_{\rm DD}
is extremely small.
The numerics additionally highlight that the best LDD generating set depends on both the physical noise strength and the QEC imperfection level.
When QEC is imperfect, suppressing low-weight errors can significantly improve performance, while in the near-perfect-QEC regime most gains come from suppressing the higher-weight uncorrectable errors.

The
[[13,1,3]]
code example is deliberately constructed so that the assumptions guaranteeing strict hybrid-protocol advantage are violated (LDD does not suppress some minimum-weight uncorrectable errors), and we observe that then LDD+QEC can indeed lose its clear separation from QEC-only as
p\to 0
.
In the
(p_{\rm DD},p_{\rm QEC})
plane, LDD+QEC remains advantageous over QEC-only and LDD-only across the displayed parameter range, but its advantage relative to physical DD-phys exhibits a boundary rather than expanding to the full plane as
p\to 0
, unlike the Steane code case.
This too is due to the deliberate suboptimal choice of LDD generators we make in the
[[13,1,3]]
case.

These results can be summarized as the statement that DD and QEC are “better together” as long as one carefully co-designs the code, the decoding map, and the LDD group so that suppression is concentrated on the errors that actually result in logical failure.

There are several natural directions to build on this work.
First, it would be valuable to connect microscopic noise models (including biased noise and coherent/non-Pauli components) to the effective parameters
(p,p_{\rm DD},p_{\rm QEC}/p_{\rm QED})
used here, and to incorporate control-induced errors so that
p_{\rm DD}
reflects both suppression and pulse imperfections.
Second, the WEP-based formulas provide a fast evaluation tool that can be used to optimize over LDD generating sets and decoding choices for a given hardware noise profile.
Finally, extending the analysis from a single memory cycle to multi-round fault-tolerant protocols (where syndrome extraction and control must be interleaved repeatedly) would clarify how the advantages identified here translate into end-to-end logical performance and resource tradeoffs in realistic architectures.

Equivalence of our fidelity measure to the entanglement fidelity

Recall the standard definition of the entanglement fidelity
F_{\rm e}
for a CPTP map
\Lambda(\rho)=\sum_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}\rho K_{\alpha}^{\dagger}
acting on a
d
-dimensional system:

\displaystyle F_{\rm e}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bra{\Phi}(\Lambda\otimes\operatorname{id})\bigl(|{\Phi}\rangle\!\langle\Phi|\bigr)\ket{\Phi}

(120a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{\alpha}\Bigl|\Tr\bigl(K_{\alpha}\bigr)\Bigr|^{2},

(120b)

where
\ket{\Phi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\ket{j}\otimes\ket{j}
is a maximally entangled state.
In our setting,
d=2^{k}
.

The set of Kraus operators for the logical Pauli channel
Eq.

6
can be taken as

\{K_{\alpha}\}=\{\sqrt{p_{L}}L\}
(choosing an arbitrary representative
L
of each logical Pauli class acting on the code space).
Thus,

F_{\rm e}(\Lambda)=\sum_{L}p_{L}\frac{|\Tr(L)|^{2}}{d^{2}}.

(121)

For any nontrivial logical Pauli (i.e., any Pauli operator on
k
qubits not proportional to the identity), we have
\Tr(L)=0
, while
\Tr(I)=d
.
Therefore,

F_{\rm e}(\Lambda)=p_{I}=F,

(122)

where
F
is defined in

8

Appendix B
QEC-only

In this appendix, we give an independent derivation of the QEC-only fidelity expression.

The phase-stripped Pauli group
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
splits into the following four disjoint subsets.
For each subset, we state the logical outcome of QEC recovery on an error
E^{\prime}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
, referring to the notation in
Table

1
.

1.

E^{\prime}\in\pi(\mathcal{S})
:
E^{\prime}
is a stabilizer element, so
\syn(E^{\prime})=0
.
This never results in a logical error (independent of
p_{\rm QEC}
).
Because
D(0)=\pi(I)
, every stabilizer is trivially correctable, so
\pi(\mathcal{S})\subset{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c}}
.

2.

E^{\prime}\in{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c}}\setminus\pi(\mathcal{S})
:
E^{\prime}
is a correctable (non-stabilizer) error, so
\syn(E^{\prime})\neq 0
, and its WEP contribution is
C
.
There are two cases:

(a)

With probability
1-p_{\rm QEC}
the decoder applies
D(\syn(E^{\prime}))
and the error is corrected to the trivial logical class.

(b)

With probability
p_{\rm QEC}
the decoder fails and applies a uniformly random syndrome-consistent recovery; this yields a logical error with probability
1-4^{-k}
.

3.

E^{\prime}\in\pi(\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L})\setminus\pi(\mathcal{S})
:
E^{\prime}
is an undetectable nontrivial logical operator, so
\syn(E^{\prime})=0
, and its WEP contribution is
L
.
This always produces a logical error (since decoder failure only affects nontrivial syndromes in our model).

4.

E^{\prime}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\setminus{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c}}\setminus\pi(\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L})
:
E^{\prime}
is an uncorrectable but detectable error, so
\syn(E^{\prime})\neq 0
, and its WEP contribution is
\not\!\mathrm{C}
-D
.
There are two cases:

(a)

With probability
1-p_{\rm QEC}
the decoder applies
D(\syn(E^{\prime}))
and mis-corrects, resulting in a logical error.

(b)

With probability
p_{\rm QEC}
the decoder fails and applies a uniformly random syndrome-consistent recovery, which yields a logical error with probability
1-4^{-k}
.

Lemma 8
.

The infidelity under QEC is

\displaystyle 1-F_{\rm QEC}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\textup{A}}\Big[\textup{L}+\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{C}$-D}+p_{\rm QEC}\bigl[\textup{C}-4^{-k}(\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{C}$-D}+\textup{C})\bigr]\Big]

(123a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\textup{A}}\Big[\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{C}$}+p_{\rm QEC}(\textup{C}-4^{-k}\textup{D})\Big],

(123b)

in agreement with
Eq.

24
.

Proof.

The fidelity
F_{\rm QEC}
is the probability that after one round of the QEC-only strategy, the logical state is unchanged.
Thus,
1-F_{\rm QEC}
is the probability of logical failure.
Of the subsets enumerated above, only
\pi(\mathcal{S})
(subset 1) and properly corrected errors in
{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c}}\setminus\pi(\mathcal{S})
(subset 2a) do not contribute to logical failures.
Let us consider the remaining branches:

3.

Nontrivial logical errors occur whether or not the decoder acts.
Thus, this branch contributes with weight
L
.

4.(a)

The uncorrectable but detectable errors have weight
\not\!\mathrm{C}
-D
and are converted into logical errors with probability
1-p_{\rm QEC}
.

2.(b)

The correctable errors have weight
C
; the decoder fails with probability
p_{\rm QEC}
and then yields a logical error with probability
1-4^{-k}
.

4.(b)

The same happens with the uncorrectable but detectable errors, again with weight
\not\!\mathrm{C}
-D
.

Adding these four branches and dividing by the total phase-stripped Pauli group weight
W(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n};z)=\textup{A}
yields

\begin{split}1-F_{\rm QEC}&=\frac{1}{\textup{A}}\Big[\textup{L}+(1-p_{\rm QEC})\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{C}$-D}\\
&\quad\quad+p_{\rm QEC}(1-4^{-k})\big(\textup{C}+\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{C}$-D}\big)\Big],\end{split}

(124)

which rearranges into
Eq.

123a
.

Eq.

123b
follows since
\textup{L}\dot{\cup}\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{C}$-D}=\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{C}$}
and
\textup{C}\dot{\cup}\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{C}$-D}=\textup{D}
.
∎

Appendix C
LDD-only

In this appendix, we give an independent derivation of the LDD-only fidelity expression.

We now have an
[[n,k,d]]
code and a DD group
\tilde{G}
, which is a subgroup of
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
(in our comparisons we take
|\tilde{G}|=4^{k}
).
The recovery step is skipped, but the syndrome measurement still acts, which means that detected errors (i.e., errors with a nonzero syndrome) have a probability
4^{-k}
of being mapped back to the correct logical class by a random projection.
Thus, relative to pure DD, the contribution of detected errors to logical failure is reduced by a factor
4^{-k}
.

Lemma 9
.

The infidelity under LDD is

\displaystyle 1-F_{\rm LDD}=\frac{1}{\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$}+p_{\rm DD}\textup{S}}\bigl(

\displaystyle(\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$-$\not{\mathrm{St}}$}-4^{-k}\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$-D})

(125a)

\displaystyle+p_{\rm DD}

\displaystyle(\textup{S-$\not{\mathrm{St}}$}-4^{-k}\textup{S-D})\bigr),

(125b)

in agreement with
Eq.

27
.

Proof.

The denominator is unchanged relative to the DD-phys case (
Section

III.1
): the weight of the total number of errors is the sum of the unsuppressed errors (
\not\!\mathrm{S}
), which keep their probability, and the suppressed errors (
S
), which acquire a factor
p_{\rm DD}
.

In the numerator, we count the total weight of logical failures, split into unsuppressed-sector terms (no
p_{\rm DD}
) and suppressed-sector terms (with
p_{\rm DD}
).
The term
\not\!\mathrm{S}
-
\not{\mathrm{St}}
accounts for unsuppressed non-stabilizer errors (all of which would cause a logical failure were it not for the random projection), while
\not\!\mathrm{S}
-D
accounts for unsuppressed detected errors, which are corrected to the trivial logical class with probability
4^{-k}
after a random projection.
Hence the net unsuppressed contribution is
\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$-$\not{\mathrm{St}}$}-4^{-k}\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$-D}
.

The suppressed-sector contribution is identical, but restricted to errors in
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\setminus\tilde{G}^{\perp}
, yielding
\textup{S-$\not{\mathrm{St}}$}-4^{-k}\textup{S-D}
, and multiplied by the DD rescaling factor
p_{\rm DD}
.
Combining these contributions and dividing by the normalization
\textup{$\not\!\mathrm{S}$}+p_{\rm DD}\textup{S}
gives the stated infidelity formula.
∎
