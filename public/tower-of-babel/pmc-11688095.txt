Prevalence and clinical significance of hypouricemia in the emergency department
Authors: Erdinç Şengüldür, Mehmet Cihat Demir, Kudret Selki
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Hypouricemia prevalence has been reported in different clinical settings, but its prevalence and characteristics in the emergency department (ED) are limitedly studied. This study aimed to assess hypouricemia prevalence and clinical significance in the ED of a Turkish tertiary-care hospital. It was a retrospective, single-center observational study. Patients aged 18 years and older who presented to the ED between June 1, 2022 and June 1, 2023 were included. Data including age, gender, comorbid disease, final diagnosis, serum electrolytes, albumin, and serum uric acid (SUA) levels were obtained. SUA <2 mg/dL was considered as hypouricemia. The study included 35,923 patients, 362 of whom had SUA <2 mg/dL. The prevalence of hypouricemia was 1.008% and was higher in women (1.4% vs 0.4%, P < .001). Hypouricemic patients were younger than non-hypouricemic ones (34.5 [26–57] vs 50 [31–68], P < .001). Hypouricemic patients had a higher 6-month mortality rate than non-hypouricemic patients (5.2% vs 2.8%, P = .006). Among hypouricemic patients, men had a higher mortality rate than women (12.9% vs 3.4%, P = .004). A total of 33.7% of hypouricemic patients were pregnant, and the most common final diagnosis in the ED was abortus imminens, with 17.7%. The prevalence of hypouricemia in ED patients differs between genders. Hypouricemia increases 6-month mortality, more so in men. Obstetric pathologies are the most common final diagnosis of ED in hypouricemic patients. SUA can be monitored as a useful biomarker to ensure a healthy pregnancy and has a warning role in predicting mortality risk. Further studies are needed on hypouricemia.

1. Introduction

Uric acid is a molecule produced due to the human body’s metabolism of endogenous and exogenous purine nucleotides. Approximately 70% of the uric acid produced is excreted by the kidneys, while the rest is excreted by the intestinal tract.[1] Uric acid is a potent antioxidant. It plays a vital role in scavenging superoxide, hydroxyl, and free oxygen radicals. A decrease in uric acid level leads to a reduction in antioxidant capacity and, thus, to an increase in oxidative stress on all organs and systems.[2,3]

The adverse effects of hyperuricemia have been acknowledged for a long time, and studies are showing that it is responsible for the development of diseases such as gout, acute coronary syndrome, chronic renal failure, and diabetes mellitus.[4,5] However, the situation with hypouricemic patients remains a mystery. Hypouricemic patients are usually asymptomatic and are diagnosed incidentally with the results of tests performed for different reasons. A few studies performed in recent years on hypouricemia have shown that hypouricemia also plays a role in the development of cardiovascular diseases, renal failure, and neurological diseases such as Alzheimer and Parkinson and that serum uric acid (SUA) level has a U-shaped effect in the development of these diseases.[3,6–8]

SUA level <2 mg/dL is defined as hypouricemia.[9,10] Decreased SUA production or decreased reabsorption in the proximal tubule causes hypouricemia. In previous studies performed with inpatients, healthy patients undergoing checkups, and outpatients, the prevalence of hypouricemia has been reported between 0.2% and 4.14%.[3,10–13]

There is limited research on the prevalence of hypouricemia among patients in the emergency department (ED). This study aimed to reveal the prevalence and clinical significance of hypouricemia in ED patients in Türkiye, as well as their characteristics.

2. Methods

2.1. Study setting and design

This is a retrospective, single-center observational study. It was performed in the ED of a tertiary university hospital in Türkiye, with approximately 110,000 admissions per year. The study was initiated after the approval of the local ethics committee (Düzce University Non-Invasive Health Research Ethics Committee, approval id 2023/200, dated December 18, 2023). Between June 1, 2022 and June 1, 2023, the study was performed on patients aged 18 and older who visited the ED.

During the 1-year period of the study, all adult patients who underwent emergency biochemistry tests and whose results were accessible were identified and included in the study. Patients whose blood test results were not available for various reasons were not included in the study. Serum electrolyte levels, liver and kidney function tests, SUA, and albumin levels are routinely included in the emergency biochemistry tests of the ED where the study was performed. In the hospital where the study was performed, serum electrolyte and albumin measurements were measured on the Cobas c501 autoanalyzer (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany), which uses the spectrophotometric method. Age, gender, laboratory results, comorbid disease, final diagnosis in the ED, hospitalization status, and 6-month mortality data were obtained from the hospital computer system and ED archive records.

2.2. Selection of participants and study protocol

The study included adults over 18 years old with SUA levels obtained during ED evaluation. Patients referred from another hospital or with incomplete data were excluded from the study. The number of patients aged 18 years and over was 72,715 out of 111,842 ED visits during the study period. It was found that the SUA level was analyzed in 35,923 of these visits. The threshold value of hypouricemia was determined as 2 mg/dL by analyzing similar studies in the literature.[6,9,11,12] It was observed that the SUA level was below 2 mg/dL in 362 of the admissions included in the study, and these admissions were grouped as hypouricemic patients. The other admissions were labeled as non-hypouricemic patients in the study.

The prevalence of hypouricemia in ED patients was calculated by dividing the number of hypouricemic patients by the number of patients included in the study. Hypouricemic and non-hypouricemic patient groups were compared with each other regarding the characteristics screened in the study. Descriptive statistics, including comorbidities and final diagnosis of ED in hypouricemic patients, were generated. Hypouricemic patients were divided into male and female gender groups, and the groups were compared in terms of age, serum electrolyte, albumin, SUA levels, and 6-month mortality.

SUA levels were measured on the Cobas c501 autoanalyzer (Roche Diagnostics GmbH). Blood samples were obtained by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 minutes. The SUA measurement was in mg/dL, and the normal reference range at the center where it was studied was 2.4 to 5.7 mg/dL.

2.3. Statistical analysis

Continuous data were summarized as median, 25th, and 75th percentile, and categorical data were summarized as frequency and percentage. Compliance with normal distribution was analyzed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and histogram. Continuous data between the 2 groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. The relationship between 2 categorical variables was analyzed using Pearson Chi-square test or Fisher exact test. Statistical software SPSS version 23 (SPSS Inc., Armonk, NY) was used for these analyses. The significance level was set as P < .05.

3. Results

During the one-year study, the prevalence of hypouricemia among ED visits was 1.008% in the general population, 1.4% in women, and 0.4% in men.

The median age of hypouricemic patients was 34.5 (26–57) years. Compared with the non-hypouricemic group, hypouricemic patients were statistically significantly younger (P < .001). A total of 80.7% (n = 292) of the hypouricemic patients were female. Although this rate was considerably higher than in the non-hypouricemic patients group, there was a statistically significant difference (P < .001). The 6-month mortality rate was 5.2% (n = 19) in the hypouricemic patient group and 2.8% (n = 1009) in the non-hypouricemic patient group, and the nearly 2-fold difference in mortality rates was statistically significant (P = .006). 4. Discussion

This study, which aims to determine the prevalence of hypouricemia in ED patients and the characteristics of hypouricemic patients in ED, is the first study on this subject in Türkiye. In this study, the prevalence of hypouricemia in ED patients was 1.008%.

Almost all of the studies on the prevalence of hypouricemia have been performed with inpatients, healthy people who had blood tests for health screening, or outpatients. The results of these groups are also dissimilar, and the prevalence of hypouricemia in the literature is between 0.2% and 4.14%.[3,10–14] In a study performed in South Korea, the prevalence of hypouricemia was 1.39% in a mixed group consisting of inpatients and outpatients, and the prevalence of hypouricemia in inpatients was found to be 4.14%. The prevalence of hypouricemia in a group of healthy people who came for health screening and outpatients was 0.53%.[14] In a study performed in Japan with approximately 10,000 patients admitted to the ED in 10 years and whose SUA level was examined, hypouricemia prevalence was 1.4% in all patients, 0.9% in males, and 2% in females.[9] The rates in the study performed in Japan with ED patients and in our study are higher than those obtained in previous studies with healthy individuals and outpatients but lower than those obtained with inpatients. This result shows that the prevalence of hypouricemia in ED patients is lower than that in inpatients and higher than that in healthy individuals who underwent health screening and outpatients.

A review of the literature shows that the prevalence of hypouricemia is higher in women. Although the mechanism has not been fully elucidated, several studies have attributed this condition to the hormonal effect of estrogen and progesterone. It is known that SUA level increases towards normal with decreasing estrogen and progesterone levels as menopause approaches in hypouricemic women.[6] It is also known that SUA levels, which are low in the first weeks of pregnancy, increase towards normal in the weeks close to delivery.[15,16] Our study found hypouricemia to be 1.4% in the female population and 0.4% in the male population. Compared to the Japanese study,[9] the incidence of hypouricemia in women was higher in our study than in men. Considering that 80.7% (n = 292) of the hypouricemic patients in our study were women, it can be stated that the effect of female gender and hormones on the prevalence of hypouricemia was higher than in the Japanese ED study.

Hypouricemia occurs due to decreased uric acid production in the body or impaired reabsorption of uric acid in the proximal tubule. In addition to the etiological classification, it is divided into 2 groups: transient and persistent. Persistent hypouricemia is frequently seen due to defects in the gene encoding the xanthine oxidase enzyme involved in uric acid synthesis or defects in the genes encoding UAT1 and GLUT9 transport proteins involved in uric acid reabsorption in the proximal tubule. It is known that hypouricemia may be seen in patients with malignancy, diabetes mellitus, renal failure and in patients who use drugs with hypouricemic effect.[6,10,11,13,14] In our study, 18.2% (n = 66) of the hypouricemic patients were comorbid with diabetes mellitus and 9.7% (n = 35) with malignancy. One-third of the patients in our study were pregnant. Increased estrogen and progesterone effects due to pregnancy, diabetes mellitus, and malignancies stand out as important etiological factors of hypouricemia in our research.

Many studies in the literature have shown that hyperuricemia is a risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, cardiovascular mortality, and all-cause mortality. In recent studies, it has been found that hypouricemia is also a risk factor for cardiovascular diseases and cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, and SUA levels have a U-shaped effect for these conditions.[8,11–13] In a study performed in Japan, it was reported that SUA levels also showed a U-shaped impact on the development of hypertension, and hypouricemia also caused the development of hypertension-like hyperuricemia.[17] Hypouricemia is also known to be a risk factor for the development of renal failure and stroke.[3,18] In our study, 22.1% (n = 80) of the hypouricemic patients were comorbid with hypertension, 13.5% (n = 49) with cardiovascular system diseases, and 3.6% (n = 13) with renal failure. Hypouricemia may have contributed to the development of these diseases, together with other factors.

In our study, 6-month mortality was statistically significantly higher in hypouricemic patients than in non-hypouricemic patients (2.8% vs 5.2%). This finding is consistent with recent studies on the effect of hypouricemia on all-cause mortality.[8,11,17] In terms of gender, the 6-month mortality rate was higher in hypouricemic males than females (12.9% vs 3.4%). The fact that the median age of hypouricemic men (60 [43.25–74.25]) was significantly higher than the median age of hypouricemic women (31 [25–48.75]) may be considered as a reason for the higher mortality rate in men. Because of the high 6-month mortality rate in male patients with hypouricemia, the decision of discharge for these patients must be made after careful evaluation, and post-discharge controls should not be delayed. Just as emergency physicians pay attention to electrolyte disorders such as hypokalemia, hyperkalemia, hyponatremia, or hypernatremia, they should also pay the same attention to hypouricemia, especially in male patients because it is an indicator of high mortality. New studies on this issue will increase the clinical significance of SUA levels in ED patients.

There are many studies in the literature evaluating the effects of hyperuricemia on pregnancy. Hyperuricemia is known to be associated with pathologies such as preeclampsia, eclampsia, intrauterine growth retardation, and low birth weight.[19–23] According to our knowledge, there is no study in which hypouricemia was associated with obstetric pathologies. Interestingly, our study’s important point that attracted attention was the high rate of obstetric pathologies in hypouricemic patients. When the final diagnosis of ED in hypouricemic patients was analyzed, the most common diagnosis was abortus imminens. Abortus imminens is a condition that occurs before the 20th week of pregnancy, which may be accompanied by pelvic pain and bleeding and is a threat of miscarriage.[24] The most common diagnoses made in the ED in hypouricemic patients included preterm labor with 5.8% (n = 21) and hyperemesis gravidarum with 4.7% (n = 17). Obstetric diagnoses accounted for 28.1% of the diagnoses of hypouricemic ED patients. It is noteworthy that approximately 1 in 3 hypouricemic ED patients were diagnosed with pregnancy-related pathologies. We believe that hypouricemia detected in prepregnancy tests may indicate that obstetric pathologies could occur during pregnancy. In addition, we propose that SUA levels tested during pregnancy follow-up may play a warning role regarding the risk of abortus imminens and hyperemesis gravidarum in the first trimester and preterm labor in the following trimesters.

Monitoring SUA level along with other parameters during pregnancy may be helpful in a healthy pregnancy follow-up. We suggest that researchers should be encouraged to work on this subject. In our study, none of the hypouricemic patients diagnosed with obstetric pathology died within 6 months. Unlike preeclampsia and eclampsia, which are associated with hyperuricemia, obstetric pathologies, which we assume to increase with hypouricemia, do not cause maternal death. Further studies on the effect of hypouricemia on pregnancy and the development of pregnancy-related pathologies will contribute to the literature.

The study also compared serum electrolyte and albumin levels between various groups. Statistically significant differences were found between the various groups regarding various electrolyte levels. Still, the differences were generally minimal mathematically, and the values were always within the normal reference ranges, rendering the differences clinically meaningless.

The study had a few limitations that need to be considered. First, the research was based on retrospective data collected from a single center, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Second, the SUA level was measured only once during the study, so it’s unclear whether hypouricemia is transient or persistent. Thirdly, the medications used by the patients included in the study were not recorded, and some of these drugs may affect the SUA level. However, no patients with gout were found among those who had hypouricemia. Fourth, we did not measure the urinary uric acid level of the patients, which means that it is not possible to determine whether the cause of hypouricemia is a decrease in uric acid production or an increase in excretion. As the fifth limitation, the threshold value of hypouricemia was determined as 2 mg/dL for both sexes and all age groups in the study. When the literature is examined, a uric acid level below 2 mg/dL is defined as hypouricemia in most of the studies. However, it is known that uric acid levels increase with age and lower uric acid levels are observed in the female gender due to hormonal reasons. While the research’s use of the same threshold value for all patients is consistent with other studies, it remains a limitation. As the sixth limitation, nonparametric tests were used in the comparative analysis of continuous data in the study because they did not conform to the normal distribution. Finally, the comorbid diseases or the last diagnosis of ED in the non-hypouricemic patients included in the study are unknown, making it impossible to compare hypouricemic patients with non-hypouricemic patients in terms of these parameters. Due to the lack of the mentioned data, regression analyses could not be performed for possible causes of hypouricemia.

5. Conclusion

The prevalence of hypouricemia in ED patients is 1.008%. It is more common in women (1.4%) than in men (0.4%). Hypouricemic patients are typically younger than non-hypouricemic patients, and women with hypouricemia are younger than men with the same condition. The 6-month all-cause mortality rate for hypouricemic patients is approximately twice that of non-hypouricemic patients. In men with hypouricemia, this rate is over 4 times higher. Hypouricemic patients also tend to have a higher rate of abortus imminens diagnosis (17.7%) and obstetric pathology diagnosis (28.1%). SUA level can be a valuable biomarker for both preconception and pregnancy monitoring. Tracking SUA levels during pregnancy can help physicians in ensuring a healthy pregnancy. Further research on this issue is warranted.
