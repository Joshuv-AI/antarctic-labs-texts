Historical Drug Costs Among Elderly Patients Hospitalized with Heart Failure in Canada, 2013-2014 to 2018-2019
Authors: Hena Qureshi, Douglas C Dover, Emily E Ellis, Sean Virani, Nathanial M Hawkins, Robert McKelvie, Anique Ducharme, M Patrice Lindsay, Padma Kaul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeart failure (HF) management guidelines continue to evolve with emerging evidence from landmark trials defining new standards for guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT). However, data on HF drug costs in Canada remain limited. This study examined long-term historical costs of drugs associated with HF management among elderly patients hospitalized with HF between 2013-2014 and 2018-2019.MethodsPrescription claims from the National Prescription Drug Utilization Information System were linked for patients aged > 65 years with a HF hospitalization between April 1, 2013 and March 31, 2019 in Canada, except Quebec, Nova Scotia, and the Territories. HF management drugs included GDMT and diuretics; costs were inflated to 2025 Canadian dollars. Annual total and per-patient drug costs were calculated.ResultsThe total drug cost for managing HF in 163,973 elderly hospitalized patients over the 5-year period was CAD$252.2 million. Although average annual per-patient costs remained relatively stable ($567 in 2013/-2014 to $593 in 2018-2019), total annual costs rose from $31.5 million to $50.8 million, driven largely by an increase in the number of patients hospitalized with HF. GDMT accounted for the majority of drug costs, and its level of use increased from 72.2% to 76.0%. In 2018-2019, costs were higher in male patients ($632) than female patients ($558), were highest among patients aged 65-74 years ($680), and were lowest among those aged ≥ 90 years ($493).ConclusionsThe cost of drugs to manage HF is substantial and is increasing over time, primarily as a result of the increasing number of patients being hospitalized with HF. Modest shifts occurred toward greater use of GDMT, with variations across province, sex, and age groups.Keywords: heart failure, prescription cost analysis, retrospective study, economic analysis, pharmaceutical drugs

The number of patients hospitalized with a primary diagnosis of heart failure (HF), in all Canadian provinces (excluding Quebec) increased from 39,960 in 2010-2011 to 44,567 in 2018-201919, with a corresponding increase in hospitalization costs from CAD$684.3 million to $776.0 million (in 2022 CAD$).1 The burden of HF in Canada is estimated to increase substantially over the next few decades because of an aging population and improvements in survival.2, 3, 4

Advancements in pharmacologic management have transformed the treatment landscape of HF. Innovative pharmacologic drugs and a quadruple therapy approach—compromising renin angiotensin system (RAS) inhibitors, β-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNis) and sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2is)—have been shown consistently to improve survival, reduce hospitalizations, and enhance quality of life for persons living with HF.2,5, 6, 7, 8 Although the cost-effectiveness of these therapies has been established, only limited data are available on the broader economic impact of these therapies at the population level.9 Canada currently lacks population-based data on the real-world costs of cardiovascular drug therapy for HF.

The objective of our study was to address this significant policy and evidence gap. As part of a major initiative by the Heart & Stroke Foundation of Canada, the Canadian Heart Failure Society (CHFS), the HeartLife Foundation, and the Canadian Cardiovascular Society (CCS), along with other partners, we examined the current and future cost burden of HF hospitalizations on the healthcare system at a population level in Canada.1 Building on our earlier work, this analysis provides long-term historical costs of drugs associated with HF management among patients aged ≥ 65 years who were discharged from the hospital with a primary diagnosis of HF between 2013 and 2019 in this cohort.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective population-based cohort study is reported according to the approach outlined in the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.10

Data sources and study population

We used 2 databases that were linked using unique anonymized identifiers at an individual level: (1) The Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI) Discharge Abstract Database (DAD), which records the most responsible and up to 24 other diagnoses for all acute-care hospitalizations for all Canadian provinces and territories, except Quebec; and (2) the CIHI National Prescription Drug Utilization Information System (NPDUIS), which provides cost and utilization information from prescription drug claims-level data collected from publicly financed drug benefit programs. The NPDUIS data for individuals aged > 65 years are available from all Canadian provinces, except Quebec, Nova Scotia, and the Territories (Northwest Territories, Yukon, and Nunavut). Both the CIHI hospitalization data and the NPDUIS prescription claims data were available for the time period of April 1, 2013 to March 31, 2019. Accordingly, the study population consisted of patients aged ≥ 65 years, including residents of all Canadian provinces, except Quebec and Nova Scotia, who were discharged from the hospital with a primary diagnosis of HF between April 1, 2013 and March 31, 2019. The list of International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision-Canada (ICD-10-CA) codes used to identify HF hospitalizations is provided in Supplemental Table S1.

Heart failure drug claims and costs

The CCS/CHFS HF guidelines were used to identify the list of HF drugs to be included in the study. This list was further adjudicated by a panel of experts from the Heart and Stroke Foundation of Canada.7,8 HF drugs were categorized into those used for guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) and those used for HF symptom management (diuretics); they are listed in Supplemental Table S2. GDMT included HF-specific and non-HF-specific management. The drug class in the NPDUIS is defined by the World Health Organization Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification system. Using the system classification code, we identified all HF prescription drug claims for our cohort, from their HF hospitalization date to either their last available prescription claim or the end of the study period. Hydralazine and nitrate (isosorbide dinitrate and isosorbide mononitrate) prescription drug claims were included only if their dispense date and their days supplied overlapped with each other. The prescription drug claim included both public program coverage costs and out-of-pocket patient costs. All costs were inflated to 2025 Canadian dollars using the Statistics Canada Consumer Price Index.11 In a supplemental analysis, we conducted an exploratory scenario analysis comparing observed treatment patterns with a hypothetical scenario reflecting increased uptake of GDMT consistent with contemporary real-world practice (see Supplemental Appendix S1 for additional methods detail).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables and counts and proportions for categorical variables. The Charlson Comorbidity Index score12, 13, 14 and the Hospital Frailty Risk Score15 were determined for each patient based, respectively, on a 5-year and a 2-year look-back period from their hospitalization date. The Charlson Comorbidity Index scores are presented categorically (0-2, 3, 4, or 5+), with a higher score indicating a higher comorbidity burden. The frailty score is presented categorically as frail (> 15), pre-frail (5-15), or not frail (< 5). We determined the annual mean drug cost per patient, the annual total drug cost, and the annual GDMT composition of total HF drug costs stratified by province, age group, and sex. To examine temporal trends in medication costs, we examined the HF drugs accounting for the top 80% of total HF drug costs in fiscal years 2013-2014 and 2018-2019 and stratified it by sex. In supplemental analyses, we examined the HF drugs accounting for the top 80% of total HF drug costs in fiscal years 2013-2014 and 2018-2019, by province.

In compliance with CIHI’s disclosure requirements for the protection of privacy, values representing < 5 patients have been suppressed, as have related values that could result in residual disclosure. When values are based upon denominators < 50, this has been noted, and caution should be exercised in interpreting these values, as they may be variable due to the small sample size. These requirements generally affected the youngest age groups, the smallest provinces, or small groups of drugs. All analyses were carried out using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

The study was approved by the University of Alberta’s Health Research Ethics Board (Pro00100060). Patients’ written informed consent was waived by the University of Alberta Research Ethics Board as the study used data from anonymized electronic health records.

Results

The study population included 163,973 patients, aged 65 years or older, hospitalized with a primary diagnosis of HF between April 1, 2013 and March 31, 2019 (Fig. 1). The median age of the cohort was 82 years (IQR 75-88), and 47.7% were male (Table 1). A majority of patients had multiple comorbidities; 25.0% had Charlson Comorbidity Index score of 0-2; 7.2% had a score of 3; 18.4% had a score of 4; and 49.4% of patients had a score ≥ 5. The number of patients with dispenses for HF drugs increased from 55,611 patients in 2013-2014 to 85,680 patients in 2018-2019.

The total cost of HF drugs from fiscal years 2013-2014 to 2018-2019 was $252.2 million (Central Illustration). The overall annual total HF drug cost in 2013-2014 was $31.5 million, which increased to $50.8 million in 2018-2019 (Central Illustration; Fig. 2A). This increasing trend in total drug costs was observed consistently across all provinces and age groups, and among both male and female patients (Table 2). The overall annual mean HF drug cost per patient was $593 in 2018-2019, which was slightly higher than the cost in 2013-2014 ($567; Central Illustration; Fig. 2B). Mean costs per patient among the provinces varied greatly, with Ontario having the highest mean cost per patient ($636) in 2018-2019 (Table 2). The annual mean HF drug costs for those aged 65-84 years increased from a range of $546-$570 to $604-$680 across provinces, whereas costs for those aged 85-89 years or ≥ 90 years decreased from $584-$543 and from $551-$493, respectively, from 2013-2014 to 2018-2019. In 2018-2019, those aged 65-74 years had one of the highest mean costs ($680), whereas those aged 90+ years had the lowest cost ($493). From 2013-2014 to 2018-2019, the annual mean HF drug cost in female patients decreased (from $588 to $558), whereas the annual mean HF drug costs in male patients increased (from $542 to $632). The annual mean HF drug cost in female patients was higher than that in male patients until 2017-2018.

Drugs classified for use as GDMT composed the highest proportion of the overall total annual HF drug costs, which increased from 72.2% to 76.0% from fiscal year 2013-2014 to 2018-2019, a 5.3% growth rate (Fig. 2A). The corresponding percent composition of drugs classified as diuretics decreased from 27.8% to 24.0%, a 13.3% relative reduction. GDMT accounted for a higher proportion of drug costs in all provinces, except Newfoundland, Prince Edward Island (PEI), and Manitoba (Fig. 3A). In 2018-2019, HF drugs classified for use as GDMT had the highest percent composition in those aged 65-69 years (85.6%) and the lowest in those aged ≥ 90 years (64.6%; Fig. 3B). Additionally, a higher proportion of male patients (79.4%) were on GDMT drugs, compared to female patients (72.5%; Fig. 3C).

Among individual drugs that accounted for the top 80% of the total annual HF drug costs, furosemide made up the highest proportion of patient prescriptions (87% of patients in 2013-2014 and 83% of patients in 2018-2019) and accounted for the highest proportion of the total annual HF drug costs (25.4% of total costs in 2013-2014 and 22.1% in 2018-2019; Table 3). The mean cost of furosemide, and most other HF drugs, remained relatively similar in fiscal years 2013-2014 ($166) and 2018-2019 ($159). The mean HF drug cost per patient in 2013-2014 ranged from a low of $128 (for spironolactone) to $400 (for carvedilol). In 2018-2019, sacubitril/valsartan accounted for the second highest proportion of the total annual HF drug costs (16.4%), despite having the lowest proportion of patients prescribed the drug (4% of patients), resulting in the highest mean cost per patient ($2415). In fact, the mean cost per patient of sacubitril/valsartan was more than 7 times the cost of the second–most expensive drug (carvedilol); sacubitril/valsartan was the only agent for which no generic formulation was available. In addition, sacubitril/valsartan had 4 times the overall mean HF drug cost in 2018-2019. A higher proportion of male patients (6%) were prescribed sacubitril/valsartan, compared to female patients (2.2%). Sacubitril/valsartan accounted for the highest proportion of the total annual HF drug costs for male patients (11.5%), whereas furosemide accounted for the highest proportion of the total annual HF drug costs for female patients (12.6%).

In our supplementary analysis, drugs that accounted for the top 80% of the total annual HF drug costs varied across the provinces (Supplemental Table S3). SGLT2i drugs were not listed as the top 80% of the total annual HF drug costs in fiscal year 2018-2019 in any province except Ontario. Sacubitril/valsartan was listed in the top 80% of the total annual HF drug costs for all provinces except PEI. Information on provincial coverage/inclusion of these drugs during the study period is provided in Supplemental Table S4. In 2018-2019, the combination of sacubitril/valsartan was not listed for PEI. SGLT2is were not listed in British Columbia, Newfoundland, or PEI. All drugs were listed as having special access use on the provincial public programs, except for SGLT2is in Ontario, which listed them as full benefit.

Despite increasing overall drug expenditures, uptake of key guideline-recommended therapies remained low. A substantial care gap in GDMT was observed. In 2018-2019, uptake of key therapies was low, with ARNis and SGLT2is demonstrating the lowest market penetration (4% and 3%, respectively). We conducted an exploratory analysis comparing observed treatment patterns with a hypothetical scenario reflecting increased uptake of GDMT, consistent with contemporary real-world practice. Under a pragmatic scenario of increased GDMT uptake, the weighted mean annual per-patient drug cost increased from $418 to $2282 (more than 5-fold), corresponding to an incremental cost of $1864 per patient (Supplemental Table S5). At the population level, this increase translated to an increase in total drug costs from $50.8 million (2018-2019) to $159.7 million, representing an incremental increase of approximately $108.9 million. The majority of incremental costs were driven by increased uptake of ARNis and SGLT2is. These estimates highlight a substantial gap between current and guideline-recommended care and its associated budget impact.

Discussion

The total cost of drugs to manage HF in patients aged ≥ 65 years, between April 1, 2013 to March 31, 2019 in Canada, not including Quebec, Nova Scotia, or the Territories, was $252.2 million (in 2022 CAD). The annual mean drug costs per patient remained relatively stable over 5 years (from $567 in 2013-2014 to $593 in 2018-2019). The overall total annual HF drug costs in 2018-2019 were $31.5 million, almost twice that in 2013-2014 ($50.8 million). The composition of drug costs shifted slightly over time, with GDMT accounting for an increasing proportion of total drug costs by 2018-2019. Among GDMT agents, sacubitril/valsartan was the most expensive, accounting for 16.4% of total HF drug costs in 2018-2019, despite being prescribed to only 4% of patients. We also observed variation in annual HF drug costs and composition across provinces, and across sex and age groups.

When comparing our findings to international HF-specific studies, mean annual HF drug costs in Canada were higher.16,17 These differences likely reflect variations across studies in patient populations (for example, HF with reduced ejection fraction [HFrEF] or HF with preserved ejection fraction [HFpEF]), definition of HF drugs, prescribing patterns, variation in country market access/drug coverage, or differences in the study time period analyzed. For example, Escobar et al. (2020) reported mean annual HF drug costs of $172 in 2015 and $173 in 2019 (2025 CAD) for adults with HF in Spain—substantially lower than our estimates, despite remaining stable over time.16 However, their classification excluded high-cost drugs such as SGLT2is, as well as nitrates and thiazides. Clinical and demographic factors may also explain the difference in costs between their study and ours— > 50% of the Spanish cohort was aged ≥ 85 years (compared to 40% in our study), which may influence treatment choices and associated drug costs.

The mean annual HF drug cost per patient, which ranged from $567 in 2013-2014 to $593 in 2018-2019, was lower compared to drug costs associated with other chronic conditions. For instance, a recent population-based study in British Columbia estimated annual drug costs for patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease at $1652 per patient-year (2025 CAD).18 Similarly, a population-based study in Alberta estimated annual drug costs for individuals living with chronic pain at $890 per patient (2025 CAD$).19

Although the mean annual drug cost per patient remained relatively stable during our study period, the total HF drug costs in 2018-2019 were almost double relative to those in 2013-2014. This change was driven primarily by the increase in the number of patients receiving treatment, and to some extent the uptake of newer, more expensive GDMT therapies—particularly sacubitril/valsartan. The proportion of total drug costs attributable to GDMT increased in 2018-2019 to 76%, up from 72.2% in 2013-2014, whereas diuretics use decreased to 24% in 2018-2019 from 27.8% in 2013-2014. The potential diuretic effect of sacubitril/valsartan may explain this shift in utilization.7,8 Despite the growing use of newer therapies, a substantial proportion of patients continued to be treated with older HF drugs. Furosemide, a diuretic, remained the most commonly prescribed drug, accounting for 25.4% of total HF drug costs in 2013-2014 and 22.1% in 2018-2019. In 2018-2019, it was used by 83% of patients but remained relatively inexpensive at $159 per patient. In contrast, sacubitril/valsartan was prescribed to only 4% of patients but had the highest per-patient cost at $2415, accounting for 16.4% of total HF drug costs. Although we could not determine the patient eligibility for sacubitril/valsartan based on the drug’s clinical indication, the relatively low uptake of sacubitril/valsartan observed in our analysis aligns with findings in another Canadian study determining the usage of sacubitril/valsartan for the treatment of HF in 2018.20 The study estimated that the eligible HF population for sacubitril/valsartan treatment in those aged ≥ 40 years was only 12% of the 225,562 eligible patients. The same study reported substantial provincial variation in uptake, ranging from 3% to 18% among eligible patients, suggesting underutilization at both the national and provincial levels.

Despite updates in the CCS/CHFS HF treatment management guidelines, the low growth in prescriptions for drugs classified as GDMT (5.3%) would suggest that other factors are potentially interfering with uptake of these newer therapeutics in clinical practice. Sacubitril/valsartan was covered by public drug plans (2018-2019) in all provinces, except PEI. However, most plans had it listed as a special access medication, meaning additional clinical criteria must be met—beyond the disease indication—for the drug to be covered, including completion of a formulary/special authority form.21,22 For example, in some instances, patients are required to initiate standard triple therapy (angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor, beta-blocker, and MRA) at an optimal dose, and if symptoms persist after a certain duration, then only a specialist (cardiologist or internal medicine) may submit a special authorization request to the drug plan. In addition, some drug plans may require the patient to have had a HF hospitalization in the prior 12 months.21 These stricter criteria, placed on higher-cost drugs to help control the cost burden for the provincial drug plans,23 are consistent with other large public drug programs, such as Medicare in the US.24 However, these requirements may represent a significant barrier for prescribing clinicians, as titrating medications to optimal doses can often take 4-6 months and requires closer initial monitoring8,21,25; and primary care physicians and general internists care for most patients with HF.26,27

In addition to provincial differences, we observed variation in HF drug costs and composition, across patient sex, and across age groups. In 2018-2019, sacubitril/valsartan accounted for the highest proportion of the total annual HF drug costs for male patients, whereas furosemide still accounted for the highest proportion of the total annual HF drug costs for female patients. A higher proportion of male patients (6%) were prescribed sacubitril/valsartan, compared to the proportion of female patients (2.2%). The difference in drug prescriptions could be due to the fact that men more often present with HFrEF, whereas women are more likely to suffer from HFpEF, for which sacubitril/valsartan is not indicated.28,29 Variation was also present among mean HF drug costs within age groups. In 2018-2019, those aged 65-74 years had one of the highest mean drug costs ($679.7) and the greatest use of GDMT, and those aged ≥ 90 years had the lowest mean cost ($493.0) and lowest GDMT uptake in 2018-2019. Several explanations are possible for the observed lower rates of GDMT among the very elderly patients. First, this group may have a greater prevalence of HFpEF. Second, as noted by previous studies, a risk-treatment paradox may be occurring, with patients at highest risk (older patients with comorbidities) of death being least likely to receive evidence-based therapies.20,30,31 Third, a higher comorbidity burden could lead to medication intolerance or contraindications.

Although our findings provide an essential baseline for national trends prior to COVID-19, it is important to situate them within the rapidly evolving landscape of HF management in the post-COVID period. Since 2020, substantial changes have occurred in both therapeutic practice and market dynamics. Uptake of ARNIs has increased substantially20 since the end of our observation window, driven by the 2021 CCS/CHFS heart failure guidelines, which repositioned sacubitril/valsartan as a first-line alternative to ACE inhibitors or ARBs for patients with HFrEF.7 Additionally, SGLT2is are recommended for inclusion in HF GDMT following pivotal trials demonstrating significant reductions in mortality and HF hospitalizations, independent of diabetes status.32,33 These developments have redefined contemporary GDMT of HF, and they are likely to increase total HF drug expenditures, as low uptake of key therapies (ARNis 4%, SGLT2 inhibitors 3%) suggests that closing care gaps could substantially increase per-patient cost, by more than 5-fold, and population-level cost, by approximately $109 million. The generic entry of dapagliflozin in 202334 and the anticipated loss of patent exclusivity for sacubitril/valsartan after 202535 may help moderate per-patient costs, but aggregate spending will likely remain high due to the broader uptake of GDMT and the rising HF prevalence in older adults. Beyond the established quadruple GDMT framework, emerging metabolic therapies are anticipated to play an increasing role in HF management over the next 5 years. Interest is growing in glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA) and dual or multi-agonists targeting glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and other metabolic pathways as adjunct options.36,37 As these agents enter clinical practice, they may further broaden HF management options but also introduce new considerations for drug costs. Overall, these advances are expected to increase HF drug costs in the future, though they may yield greater downstream savings through reductions in hospitalizations, readmissions, and resource-intensive end-of-life care.

The study cohort represents an older, hospitalized subset of the HF population, reflecting the constraints of currently available national datasets. However, this focus is clinically and epidemiologically relevant, as approximately 80% of patients with HF in Canada are aged ≥ 65 years.2,38 Beyond the exclusion of those aged < 65 years and of Quebec, Nova Scotia, and the Territories, our findings highlight broader limitations in Canada’s current data infrastructure. Although the linked CIHI DAD-NPDUIS captures hospitalizations and community-dispensed prescriptions, national data on patients managed entirely in the outpatient setting, outpatient care management, in-hospital drug administration, privately paid drug prescriptions, and clinical measures such as ejection fraction or laboratory data are unavailable. This lack of data substantially limits our ability to fully evaluate GDMT uptake, adherence, and effectiveness across the full HF continuum of care and represents a critical gap in Canada’s health data infrastructure. Strengthening the pan-Canadian linked data infrastructure to include outpatient community care, clinical measures, and hospital and pharmacy data across all payer types and age groups would support more comprehensive national pharmacare planning. Although this design excludes younger patients and those managed solely in outpatient settings, it still offers several key strengths. First, this cohort represents the population most affected by HF, accounting for the majority of HF-related hospitalizations and health system costs.1 Second, it provides a nationally harmonized, real-world dataset that enables consistent cross-provincial comparisons of publicly funded HF drug costs. Finally, this study establishes a pre-pandemic, pre-guideline benchmark against which future analyses can be benchmarked.

In addition, this analysis focuses on the direct costs of HF drug therapy but does not capture the countervailing costs of undertreatment or delayed GDMT initiation, which are associated with higher rates of hospitalization, morbidity, and mortality. Future analyses integrating administrative hospitalization and mortality data are warranted to quantify the net health-economic benefit of optimized GDMT uptake.

Another point that should be noted is that the data are based on prescriptions filled rather than prescriptions written, and we are unable to confirm whether a patient actually took the medication as prescribed. The information on drug pricing is based on the total amount paid to pharmacies. This information may not account for actual prices paid by public payers based on confidential listing agreements with manufacturers. The CIHI database cannot differentiate between HFrEF and HFpEF, a distinction that influences the type and number of GDMTs recommended.

Conclusion

This study presents the first comprehensive population-level estimates of publically funded drug costs associated with managing HF in the elderly in Canada. It highlights key trends in total and mean costs, cost composition, and variation across provinces and demographic groups. Although the annual mean HF drug costs per patient remained relatively stable over the study period, the overall HF drug burden increased substantially from 2013-2014 to 2018-2019. This change was driven primarily by an increase in the number of HF patients receiving treatment and the uptake of newer, more expensive GDMTs, particularly use of sacubitril/valsartan. Our findings reveal that the real-world uptake of GDMT is uneven across provinces, sex, and age groups. Variation in provincial drug coverage may partially explain these disparities. Future research should explore both the factors that serve as barriers to the implementation of GDMT in clinical practice and the downstream impact of increased GDMT uptake on healthcare utilization and costs.
