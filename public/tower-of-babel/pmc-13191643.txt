Clinical improvement of inflammatory linear verrucous epidermal nevus (ILVEN) with topical tapinarof: A case series
Authors: Trenton Smith, Tyler Sy, Christine T Pham, Maria Gnarra Buethe
Published: 2026

Introduction

Inflammatory linear verrucous epidermal nevus (ILVEN) is an uncommon mosaic inflammatory disorder characterized by intensely pruritic, erythematous, and hyperkeratotic plaques distributed along the lines of Blaschko.1 Historically considered a subtype of epidermal nevus, ILVEN is now recognized as a heterogeneous group of disorders with diverse genetic etiologies. These mutations result in dysregulated cytokine expression, driving chronic inflammation. The condition frequently demonstrates therapeutic resistance to conventional treatments, including topical and systemic corticosteroids, calcipotriol, 5-fluorouracil, crisaborole, acitretin, cryotherapy, and laser therapies.2 We present 2 pediatric patients with ILVEN who achieved clinical improvement with once-daily tapinarof cream 1%, demonstrating reduction in both lesion morphology and pruritus.

Case 1

A 17-year-old female presented with an 11-year history of a persistent, severely pruritic lesion on the left shin. Previous treatments included oral corticosteroids, topical corticosteroids, intralesional corticosteroids, and oral antihistamines, all without benefit. Physical examination revealed a hyperkeratotic, erythematous plaque on the anterior lower leg following the lines of Blaschko (Fig 1, A). A punch biopsy demonstrated psoriasiform epidermal hyperplasia with alternating orthokeratosis and parakeratosis, with intracorneal neutrophilic aggregates, consistent with ILVEN (Fig 2, A and B).

Histopathology Images (case 1). Hematoxylin and eosin: Left shin, 4× (A) and 10× (B) showing epidermal hyperplasia and intracorneal neutrophilic aggregates but characteristic massive hyperkeratosis with alternating orthokeratosis and parakeratosis consistent with ILVEN.

Initial treatment with compounded topical lovastatin 2%/cholesterol 2% cream applied twice daily was self-discontinued by the patient after 3 months due to a lack of improvement. Tapinarof cream 1% (VTAMA) was initiated once daily. After 2 months of daily application—during which a thin layer was applied to the total lesional surface area without occlusion—the patient reported improvement in both pruritus and lesional morphology, with reduction in erythema and scaling (Fig 1, B). Therapy was then optimized with occlusion. At the 3-month follow-up examination, lesions demonstrated continued improvement. The patient reported that this trend persisted at the 6-month mark. Further follow-up is required to determine the long-term durability of the response.

Case 2

A 6-year-old female presented with hypopigmented thin plaques coalescing into a larger plaque on the left lower extremity, first noted at age 2.5 years (Fig 3, A). Prior treatment included several courses of topical steroids (hydrocortisone and mometasone) without improvement. Biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of ILVEN. Tapinarof cream 1% (VTAMA) was initiated, and the patient was instructed to apply a thin layer over the entire lesional surface area once daily. After 1.5 months of treatment, the lesions demonstrated improvement (Fig 3, B). The response was maintained over the following 6-months. No adverse effects were reported during treatment.

Discussion

ILVEN is an uncommon, intensely pruritic dermatosis characterized by linear, erythematous, and hyperkeratotic plaques distributed along the lines of Blaschko. Once considered a subtype of epidermal nevus, ILVEN is now recognized as a mosaic inflammatory disorder with histologic and molecular overlap with psoriasis. In both cases, differential diagnoses—including linear psoriasis and other mosaic inflammatory keratinization disorders—were considered. However, the early childhood onset, unilateral Blaschkoid distribution, marked pruritus, poor response to topical corticosteroids, and histopathologic findings characteristic of ILVEN collectively supported the diagnosis.

Causative genetic variants including HRAS, KRT10, as well as PMVK, NSDHL, ABCA12, CJA1 and CARD14 variants, have been described, resulting in cytokine dysregulation (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α) and activation of inflammatory pathways.2 Recalcitrance to conventional therapies—including corticosteroids, calcipotriol, 5-fluorouracil, crisaborole, acitretin, cryotherapy, and laser treatments—is common.1

Tapinarof is a first-in-class, nonsteroidal aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) agonist approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for plaque psoriasis in adults and atopic dermatitis in adults and children 2 years of age and older. Through high-affinity AhR binding, tapinarof downregulates proinflammatory cytokines including IL-17, upregulates barrier proteins such as filaggrin and loricrin, activates the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway, and reduces tissue-resident memory T-cell persistence.3 These multimodal immunomodulatory, antioxidant, and barrier-restorative effects may explain its observed efficacy in ILVEN.

Tapinarof cream 1% demonstrated significant efficacy in phase 3 trials for both psoriasis and atopic dermatitis, with a favorable safety profile characterized primarily by mild to moderate folliculitis. A notable feature of tapinarof is its disease-modifying effect, with durable remission averaging 130 days following treatment discontinuation in psoriasis trials. Off-label use has been reported in other inflammatory dermatoses, including granuloma annulare and necrobiosis lipoidica, suggesting broad applicability in granulomatous and immune-mediated skin disorders.4

Both cases demonstrated early clinical improvement within 1.5 to 2 months of initiating tapinarof, with excellent tolerability and no reported adverse effects. These findings suggest that tapinarof may represent a valuable topical therapeutic option for ILVEN, a condition notorious for treatment resistance, particularly introduced earlier in the disease course (as in case 2), prior to the development of significant hyperkeratosis that may limit topical penetration. The mechanism of action—targeting multiple inflammatory pathways while promoting barrier restoration—appears particularly well-suited to the complex pathophysiology of ILVEN.

Limitations of this report include the lack of long-term follow-up data and genetic characterization of the specific mutations in these patients. Future studies should evaluate the durability of response after treatment discontinuation and whether genetic profiling might predict therapeutic response to tapinarof. Nevertheless, these cases expand the therapeutic landscape for ILVEN and support consideration of tapinarof as a promising adjunctive topical therapy for this challenging condition.

Conclusion

We report 2 pediatric patients with treatment-resistant ILVEN who achieved clinical improvement with once-daily 1% tapinarof cream. These cases suggest that tapinarof may be an effective and well-tolerated option for ILVEN, providing meaningful, though partial, clinical benefit and warranting further investigation in larger patient cohorts with long-term follow-up.
