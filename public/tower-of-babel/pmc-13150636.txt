ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionDoes pharmacogenetic-guided prescribing of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) improve treatment response in patients with depression?FindingsIn this randomized clinical trial of 1460 adults and children, analyses were conducted among those with an actionable drug metabolism phenotype. In this group, genotype-guided management using validated, publicly available guidelines for SSRI prescribing did not improve depression symptom scores at 3 months compared with usual care, but remission rates between 3 and 6 months were higher.MeaningThe higher remission rates observed at 6 months in this trial suggest a potential longer-term benefit that warrants further study.

Introduction

Major depression is a substantial health care problem prevalent in adults, adolescents, and school-aged children, with rates rising over the past decade.1,2,3,4 Uncontrolled depression is closely linked to suicide, now the second leading cause of death among adolescents and young adults and a top-10 cause among adults.5,6 Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are the most prescribed pharmacotherapy for depression; however, their effectiveness is often suboptimal. A response may require 6 weeks or longer, and adverse effects may lead to discontinuation.7,8,9

SSRI treatment decisions informed by pharmacogenetic testing may improve medication efficacy and safety by accounting for variation based on cytochrome P450 enzymes CYP2D6 and CYP2C19. Variants in CYP2D6 and CYP2C19 can result in poor, intermediate, rapid, or ultrarapid metabolism, influencing SSRI exposure.10,11 Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) guidelines provide recommendations for SSRI prescribing when genotype information is available.10 However, most psychiatry experts and practice guidelines for treating depression in both adults and children have not yet endorsed pharmacogenetic-informed therapy, citing insufficient evidence.12,13,14,15,16 Concerns have focused on pharmacogenetic trials relying on proprietary commercial algorithms to guide recommendations, small sample sizes, and failure to meet primary end points.

ADOPT PGx (A Depression and Opioid Pragmatic Trial in Pharmacogenetics) is a set of 3 randomized clinical trials designed to test whether routine use of pharmacogenetic testing improves medication response among patients with depression, acute pain, or chronic pain. Here, we report results from the ADOPT PGx Depression trial, which randomized children and adults to genotype-guided SSRI prescribing compared with usual care to evaluate whether pharmacogenetic guidance improves depression over 6 months. The ADOPT PGx Depression trial targeted pharmacogenes with established relevance to SSRI metabolism (CYP2D6 and CYP2C19), incorporated drug interactions known to alter CYP2D6 enzyme activity, and used standardized, nonproprietary, CPIC guideline–based interpretive algorithms.10

Methods

Trial Design and Oversight

The ADOPT PGx Depression randomized clinical trial is part of a Master Protocol Research Program (MPRP) platform trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04445792) that was registered on June 22, 2020, prior to the start of first patient enrollment for all 3 trials under its umbrella. In July 2023, each of the 3 treatment trials under the MPRP was registered under a separate ClinicalTrials.gov identifier, and NCT04445792 was converted to a screening record per recent guidance on MPRPs.31 The ADOPT PGx Depression trial is registered under ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05966155, and the trial design was published previously.17 The ADOPT PGx Depression trial was designed to be pragmatic, which was defined as testing the effectiveness of genotyping prior to antidepressant treatment in clinical practice. The trial was conducted between August 10, 2021, and April 27, 2024. Prior to trial initiation, site teams provided clinicians with education on CYP2C19- and CYP2D6-guided SSRI treatment, trial information, and eligibility criteria.17 A patient advisory board was involved in the trial design. The trial protocol and statistical analysis plan are presented in Supplement 1. The eAppendix in Supplement 2 includes a description of the independent study monitor team that ensured trial fidelity, the trial protocol, protocol changes, and the statistical analysis plan, as well as the trial clinical groups and study team members. The Duke University Institutional Review Board (IRB) served as the single-site IRB and approved the trial. A data and safety monitoring committee independently reviewed safety data and adherence to the protocol twice yearly. All adult patients (aged ≥18 years) provided written informed consent, and children (aged 8 to 17 years) provided assent. This study followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guideline.

Trial Population

Eligible patients were English- or Spanish-speaking individuals aged 8 years or older from primary care, psychiatry, or family medicine clinics at an enrolling site who had a clinical diagnosis of depression. Diagnosis was defined as electronic health record (EHR) documentation or clinician report of a depression diagnosis and evidence of at least 3 months of depressive symptoms, based on patient interview or documentation. Patients self-identified their race and ethnicity consistent with guidance by the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine.18 These guidelines included the following response categories: American Indian, Native American, or Alaska Native (hereinafter, American Indian or Alaska Native); Asian; Black or African American; Hispanic, Latina, or Latino; Middle Eastern, North African, or Mediterranean; Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander; White or European American; unknown or none of these fully describe me; or prefer not to answer. Patients were able to choose more than 1 response. Race and ethnicity were collected to characterize the study population and inform the generalizability of the findings. All participants had a plan to initiate or adjust SSRI treatment and had no psychiatric or neurologic conditions likely to affect study outcomes. Eligibility criteria are available in the eMethods in Supplement 2.

Randomization and Masking

Patients were randomized on the date the DNA specimen was received by the laboratory to either genotype guidance (intervention group) or usual care (control group) for SSRI and dose selection. Randomization occurred within each enrolling site and was not clustered by clinician. Patients in the genotype-guided group were not provided copies of their results but could access them once they were recorded in the EHR. The University of Florida College of Pharmacy study call center conducted the follow-up outcome surveys. At study end, patients were asked if they looked up their test result in the EHR or asked their clinician for a copy of the results. Any instance in which a participant disclosed their study group assignment to the call center staff was documented in the study database.

Procedures

DNA samples were collected at baseline and analyzed at Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments–certified laboratories using a guideline-recommended minimum set of CYP2D6 and CYP2C19 variants (eMethods in Supplement 2).19,20 Patients in the genotype-guided group had their DNA analyzed upon receipt in the laboratory; genotype results were typically reported within 7 to 10 days, with clinical decision support (CDS) uploaded to the EHR within 24 hours of result availability. Follow-up assessments for the genotype-guided group were calculated from the date the CDS results were made available in the EHR. Patients in the usual care group had DNA specimens stored for analysis, and follow-up assessments were calculated from the date of randomization. Patients in the usual care group were followed according to the protocol until study completion at 6 months, after which DNA specimens were analyzed with results returned to the EHR.

Pharmacogenetic phenotype was determined by genetic variants in the SSRI-metabolizing enzymes CYP2C19 and CYP2D6. CYP2C19 phenotypes were determined directly from genotype according to standard allele function.10 For CYP2D6, genotypes were translated into enzyme activity scores by summing the activity score of each allele to provide a diplotype score. CYP2D6 metabolizer phenotype classifications and corresponding activity score thresholds were based on CPIC guidelines in effect at the time of protocol development.32 Although CPIC revised CYP2D6 activity score interpretations in 2023, phenotype definitions were not modified during the trial to preserve consistency of the prespecified protocol and the clinical decision support framework already in use. Activity scores were adjusted for participants with concurrent use of moderate or strong CYP2D6 enzyme inhibitors at baseline (phenoconversion) (eMethods in Supplement 2).11 Phenoconversion status reflects the use of strong and moderate CYP2D6 inhibitors reported at baseline. Reported use of a concomitant strong CYP2D6 inhibitor corresponds to a poor metabolizer phenotype. For moderate inhibitors, the CYP2D6 activity score is reduced by half, and the phenotype is assigned based on the adjusted activity score. Because CYP2D6 activity exists on a continuum, diplotypes with activity scores between standard phenotype categories were classified into the nearest clinically relevant category, favoring the more conservative (actionable) category when ambiguity existed. Thus, phenotypes ranging between intermediate and normal were classified as intermediate metabolizers, and phenotypes between normal and ultrarapid were classified as ultrarapid metabolizers. Actionable phenotypes were defined as those for which clinical guidelines recommend alternative medication selection or dose adjustment (eTable 1 in Supplement 2).10,11

The study intervention included site-specific CDS delivered through the EHR, including computerized alerts, standardized clinical consult notes, pharmacist consultations, or a combination thereof, to guide SSRI and dose selection. The CDS was provided to all patients in the genotype-guided group regardless of phenotype actionability (eTable 1 in Supplement 2).17 Clinicians were not required to follow the recommendations. For patients with nonactionable phenotypes, CDS results were available to confirm no treatment modification was indicated and to inform future prescribing decisions. Recommendations for medication choice and dose followed CPIC guidelines, US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) label information, or both.10,11 There was no CDS intervention for the usual care group. Clinicians were not notified if the patient was randomized to usual care, declined participation, or discontinued prior to randomization; thus, the absence of a CDS report did not explicitly indicate assignment to usual care.

End Points and Assessments

The primary end point was assessed via the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Emotional Distress–Depression 8b survey (for adults) and the PROMIS Pediatric Depressive Symptoms–Short Form 8a survey (for children).21 The primary outcome was defined as the change in PROMIS depression T scores from baseline until 3 months after the return of genetic testing results to the clinician for the genotype-guided group or an equivalent time frame for the usual care group.

Secondary outcomes at 3 months included the change in Patient Health Questionnaire-8 (PHQ-8) score from baseline, the proportion of patients achieving a 50% reduction in PHQ-8 score, severity burden score for study-specific medication adverse effects, and concordance (absence of mismatch) between pharmacogenetic phenotype and self-reported antidepressant use. Additional secondary outcomes at 6 months were 2 measures of depression remission: a PROMIS depression raw score of 16 or less or a PHQ-8 score of 4 or less.21,22

Exploratory analyses included the effect of concordance on the primary end point at 3 months in patients with an actionable phenotype and the time trends in the PROMIS and PHQ-8 depression scores at 1, 3, and 6 months between groups. Additional data on adherence, anxiety, fatigue, sleep disturbance, participation in social roles and activities, pain interference, and pain intensity will be reported elsewhere.17

Statistical Analysis

The intention-to-treat (ITT) population included all participants in the study who were randomized to genotype-guided therapy or usual care. The modified ITT population, prespecified in the statistical analysis plan as the primary analysis population (Supplement 1), was defined as the subset of the ITT population who were successfully genotyped and had an actionable phenotype (hereinafter, the actionable phenotype population). A sample size of 542 patients with an actionable phenotype (n = 271 in each randomized group within the actionable phenotype population) was estimated to provide 90% power to detect a 2.8-point change in PROMIS 8b T scores with a 2-sided type 1 error rate of 0.05.23,24 Randomization was stratified by enrollment site and age group (pediatric compared with adult) utilizing a random block size within each site.

The primary outcome was analyzed using a 2-sample t test. Continuous secondary outcomes were analyzed using a 2-sample t test and baseline covariate-adjusted linear regression. Dichotomous secondary outcomes were analyzed using a test of 2 proportions and baseline covariate-adjusted logistic regression. A prespecified exploratory time-trend analysis utilized a linear mixed-effects model intended to assess the sustained effect of the intervention. Baseline covariates for quantitative end points were age, gender, duration of feeling depressed, and treatment with self-reported counseling and psychotherapy. Baseline covariates for the dichotomous end points were age, duration of feeling depressed, and treatment with self-reported counseling and psychotherapy. Baseline covariates for time-trend analyses were age, gender, race and ethnicity, duration of feeling depressed, current self-reported counseling and psychotherapy, and the baseline value of the end point. The results are reported as the mean difference (t test) or least-square mean difference (linear regression) with 95% CIs. The widths of the CIs were not adjusted for multiplicity and should not be used to make a definitive conclusion. Results were considered statistically significant at P < .05 (2-tailed). Missing data were not imputed because these data were below the prespecified 10% missingness threshold at the 3-month time point. All analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute). Supplementary statistical methods are reported in the eMethods in Supplement 2.

Results

Trial Population

Enrollment for the ADOPT PGx Depression trial began on August 10, 2021, and the final study visit was completed on April 27, 2024. Of the 7243 patients approached for the study, 1460 patients (1096 female [75.1%] and 364 [24.9%] male) were randomized to the intervention or control group and included in the ITT population; 85.0% (n = 1241) were randomized during the COVID-19 pandemic (Figure 1). These patients included 1239 adults (84.9%) (mean [SD] age, 40.6 [16.7] years) and 221 children (15.1%) (mean age, 14.6 [1.8] years) from 9 academic and community health systems. In terms of race and ethnicity, 7 patients (0.5%) were American Indian or Alaska Native; 40 (2.7%) were Asian; 231 (15.8%) were Black or African American; 134 (9.2%) were Hispanic, Latina, or Latino; 6 (0.4%) were Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander; 8 (0.5%) were Middle Eastern, North African, or Mediterranean; 958 (65.6%) were White or European American; 57 (3.9%) were of other race or ethnicity; 11 (0.8%) were of unknown race or ethnicity or could not select a category that fully described them; and 8 (0.5%) preferred not to answer.

The actionable phenotype population included 692 patients (351 [50.7%] in the genotype-guided group and 341 [49.3%] in the usual care group). At 3 months, 22 patients (6.3%) in the genotype-guided group and 21 (6.2%) in the usual care group failed to complete the PROMIS survey or discontinued the study, leaving 329 and 320 patients for the primary end point analysis, respectively.

Demographic and clinical characteristics were balanced between the groups in the ITT (eTable 2 in Supplement 2) and actionable phenotype populations except for greater citalopram use in the genotype-guided group of the actionable phenotype population (Table 1). Of the 692 patients in the actionable phenotype analysis, 463 (66.9%) reported having depressive symptoms for more than 2 years, 603 (87.1%) reported receiving pharmacotherapy, and 354 (51.2%) reported receiving nonpharmacologic treatment (eg, counseling, psychotherapy, cognitive behavior therapy, or other similar therapies). Patients had moderate depressive symptoms at baseline, with a mean (SD) PROMIS depression T score of 61.5 (8.2) and a mean (SD) PHQ-8 score of 12.6 (5.7). In addition, patients had moderate anxiety, with a mean (SD) PROMIS anxiety T score of 62.9 (8.9).

Primary Outcome

At 3 months, the mean (SD) PROMIS T scores were 57.0 (9.3) in the genotype-guided group and 57.5 (9.4) in the usual care group, a decrease of –4.3 (8.4) and –4.0 (8.1) from baseline, respectively (difference, –0.3 [95% CI, –1.5 to 1.0]; P = .68) (Figure 2, Table 3, and eFigure 1 in Supplement 2). Adjustment for prespecified covariates did not alter the findings (difference, –0.3 [95% CI, –1.5 to 1.0]; P = .67).

Secondary Outcomes

No significant differences were observed between the genotype-guided group and the usual care group in the mean (SD) change in PHQ-8 scores from baseline to 3 months (–3.3 [5.2] vs –2.7 [4.8], respectively; between-group difference, –0.6 [95% CI, –1.4 to 0.2]; P = .13) (Table 3). The proportion of patients with a 50% or greater decrease in PHQ-8 score from baseline to 3 months was not significantly different between the genotype-guided and usual care groups (92 of 327 [28.1%] vs 71 of 321 [22.1%]; difference, 6.0 [95% CI, −0.6 to 12.7]; P = .08) (Table 3).

At 6 months, the proportion of patients who experienced remission from depression was significantly higher in the genotype-guided group compared with the usual care group, as measured with both the PROMIS and PHQ-8 surveys (Table 3). Based on PROMIS depression scale scores, 153 of 317 patients (48.3%) in the genotype-guided group achieved symptom remission compared with 122 of 310 (39.4%) in the usual care group, yielding a mean difference of 8.9% (95% CI, 1.2%-16.6%; P = .02). Similarly, remission based on PHQ-8 scores was more frequent in the genotype-guided group than in the usual care group (95 of 318 [29.9%] vs 65 of 309 [21.0%]), with a mean difference of 8.8% (95% CI, 2.1%-15.6%; P = .01).

At 3 months, patients in the genotype-guided group had a higher concordance between actionable CYP2D6 and CYP2C19 phenotypes and SSRI medication or SSRI dose compared with the usual care group (273 of 323 [84.5%] vs 234 of 315 [74.3%]; difference, 10.2% [95% CI, 4.0%-16.5%]; P = .001) (Table 3). A total of 15.5% of patients (n = 50 of 323) with actionable phenotypes in the genotype-guided group were not receiving pharmacogenetically concordant therapy. The most frequently reported moderate or severe adverse effects were sleepiness, anxiety, trouble sleeping, and fatigue, affecting 30% to 50% of patients (eTable 4 in Supplement 2). The severity burden of adverse effects at 3 months was similar between the genotype-guided group and the usual care group (mean [SD], 8.2 [4.3] vs 7.8 [4.5], respectively; difference, 0.3 [95% CI, –0.4 to 1.1]; P = .37) (Table 3). Self-reported emergency department visits and hospitalizations were rare in both groups (eTable 5 in Supplement 2). No adverse events or deaths were reported to be related to the study intervention.

Exploratory Analyses

In the per-protocol subgroup (defined as patients with an actionable pharmacogenetic phenotype and SSRI concordance at 3 months), no significant differences were found between groups in the change in PROMIS T scores from baseline to 3 months (genotype-guided: mean [SD], –4.0 [8.3]; usual care: mean [SD], –4.0 [8.0]; adjusted between-group difference: mean, 0.01 [95% CI, –1.3 to 1.3]; P = .98).

Mean (SD) T scores in the genotype-guided vs usual care groups were 61.6 (8.6) vs 61.5 (7.0) at baseline, 58.2 (8.6) vs 58.4 (8.9) at 1 month, 57.0 (9.3) vs 57.5 (9.4) at 3 months, and 54.7 (9.3) vs 56.6 (9.2) at 6 months (Figure 2). A time-trend analysis adjusted for baseline PROMIS depression T scores showed a significantly greater improvement in T scores at 6 months within the genotype-guided group compared with the usual care group, with a mean difference of –1.9 points (95% CI, –3.1 to –0.8; P = .001) (eTable 6 in Supplement 2). Similar findings were observed in a post hoc time-trend analysis of PHQ-8 scores at 6 months (eFigure 2 in Supplement 2). In a post hoc exploratory analysis, change in depression T scores from baseline was examined separately for participants with actionable and nonactionable phenotypes (eTables 7 and 8 in Supplement 2). Among participants with a nonactionable phenotype, the mean difference between the genotype-guided and usual care groups at 6 months was −0.4 points (95% CI, −1.7 to 1.0 points; P = .61). The treatment-by-actionability interaction was not statistically significant (P = .13).

In the intervention group, 154 of 281 patients (mean [SD], 54.2% [9.1%]) in the actionable phenotype population accessed their pharmacogenetic results during the study via their clinician, EHR, or other means; 132 of 294 patients (mean [SD], 55.1% [8.9%]) in the nonactionable population accessed their test results. Among those with an actionable result, patients who were aware of their results had greater reductions in PROMIS T scores at 6 months compared with those who were unaware of their results (mean difference, –2.3 [95% CI, –4.5 to –0.1]; P = .04). The difference was no longer significant after adjustment for baseline characteristics (mean difference, –2.0 points [95% CI, –4.2 to 0.2]; P = .07). In contrast, among patients randomized to the genotype-guided group with a nonactionable result, the score changes did not differ significantly between those who were aware of their results and those who were unaware (eTable 9 in Supplement 2). No data were collected to determine whether awareness of genotype results led to changes in SSRI treatment.

Discussion

In this large, pragmatic randomized clinical trial, genotype-guided SSRI prescribing did not improve depressive symptoms or adverse effects at 3 months compared with usual care. By 6 months, a significantly larger proportion of patients receiving genotype-guided therapy achieved remission, with consistent effects across both PROMIS and PHQ-8 measures and a significant downward trend in scores among those with an actionable phenotype. These findings suggest that pharmacogenetic testing, including evaluation for CYP2D6 enzyme inhibition (phenoconversion), may offer meaningful benefit with longer follow-up.

The results of this study are consistent with 2 large clinical trials that incorporated pharmacogenetics into antidepressant medication and dosage selection.25,26 The PRIME Care (Precision Medicine in Mental Health Care) trial found that Veterans Affairs patients with major depression receiving usual care had a higher risk of moderate or substantial drug-gene interactions in the first 30 days, whereas patients receiving genotype-guided therapy achieved a higher remission rate at 12 weeks.25 The GUIDED (Genomics Used to Improve Depression Decisions) trial showed higher remission rates within the genotype-guided therapy group after 8 weeks.26 Both the PRIME Care and GUIDED trials used commercial proprietary algorithms across multiple pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic genetic variants, which prevents clinicians and patients from independently evaluating the pharmacogenetic evidence base and reduces comparability across studies. The effect of the combination of these genetic variants on clinical outcomes has not been validated. The ADOPT PGx trial used a well-established approach that focused on 2 genes (CYP2D6 and CPY2C19), supported by a high level of evidence, and incorporated phenotype adjustments for moderate and strong CYP2D6 enzyme inhibition.10,27,28

A 2024 report from the American Psychiatric Association (APA) Council of Research Workgroup on Biomarkers and Novel Treatments by Baum et al15 highlighted several key considerations in evaluating the pharmacogenomic literature and its application in clinician decision-making. The workgroup noted that most major trials evaluating pharmacogenomic-guided treatment have been criticized because treatment groups were unmasked or only partially masked. This is a notable design limitation that remains difficult to overcome. Within the US, the 21st Century Cures Act, enacted in December 2016, requires immediate release of EHR information with limited exceptions, which do not include research participation.29,30 Consequently, in the ADOPT PGx trial, masking to group assignment was feasible for the outcome assessment staff and statistical teams but not for clinicians or patients. Second, Baum et al15 described a potential risk of harm to patients from clinician use of pharmacogenomic tools that are not validated for efficacy or adverse effect prevention, which could potentially delay the clinician’s choice for the next best medication. This may be a valid critique for use of unvalidated commercial algorithms; however, the ADOPT PGx trial incorporated recommendations derived exclusively from peer-reviewed guidelines and FDA sources, thus minimizing the risk of potential harm.

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this trial include the enrollment of a large and diverse population, which included pediatric patients, from 9 distinct health systems. Integration of results with EHR-embedded CDS and clinical pharmacist oversight was designed to create a scalable system of pharmacogenetic guidance that could be implemented in clinical practice, including primary care.

This study also has several limitations. The design precluded formal assessment of a major depression diagnosis; therefore, the study population may have included patients with milder forms of depression. Heterogeneity in implementation was permitted across sites, including differences in the format of the CDS, clinician education, and clinical workflow.17 Patients could access the EHR-integrated results, which may have influenced their subsequent responses to study assessments. At 3 months, 15.5% of patients with actionable phenotypes in the genotype-guided group were not receiving pharmacogenetically concordant therapy. This incomplete concordance may have reduced the between-group difference and underscores that genotype represents only one component of multifactorial clinical decision-making. The trial was designed with the expectation that prescribing changes would occur soon after release of results and an effect, if present, would be observed at 3 months. However, in this pragmatic trial, detailed data on the timing of medication changes were not collected, and delays in follow-up visits or treatment changes may have limited the ability to detect a difference at 3 months. The observation of higher depression remission rates at 6 months suggests that longer follow-up may better capture the clinical impact of genotype-guided prescribing in routine clinical care. Additionally, most patients (85.0%) were enrolled during the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have affected follow-up visit timing. We did not observe differences in adverse event frequency between groups. Genotype-related adverse effects would be expected primarily among poor metabolizers receiving SSRIs for which treatment adjustment would be recommended. Few patients met both criteria, thus limiting power to detect differences in adverse effects between the intervention and usual care groups. The absence of an observed effect should be interpreted in this context. Finally, the pragmatic design did not restrict other types of antidepressant treatment, which may have reduced the power to detect between groups differences study. To address these limitations, future trial designs could consider enrichment of the trial population for actionable pharmacogenetic phenotypes by selecting patients with known adverse events.

Conclusions

In this randomized clinical trial, genotype-guided SSRI prescribing was not associated with improved depression symptom control at 3 months compared with usual care but was associated with higher remission rates at 6 months among patients with actionable phenotypes. Although outcomes were similar early in treatment, differences emerged over time. These findings suggest a possible longer-term clinical benefit and indicate that future studies should focus on evaluating the durability and long-term impact of genotype-guided prescribing in the management of depressive symptoms.
