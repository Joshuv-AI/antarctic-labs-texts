Kidney outcomes and prognostic factors in biopsy-proven kidney sarcoidosis: a nationwide multicenter cohort study

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRenal sarcoidosis is a rare manifestation of systemic sarcoidosis. Evidence guiding management remains limited. We aimed to characterize clinical and histopathological features, renal outcomes, and predictors of recovery and relapse in patients with renal sarcoidosis.MethodsIn this retrospective, multicenter observational study across hospitals affiliated with the Spanish Group for the Study of Glomerular Diseases, we included patients with biopsy-confirmed renal sarcoidosis and ≥6 months of follow-up. Primary outcomes were renal recovery (defined as >50% improvement in estimated glomerular filtration rate) and disease relapse (renal or extrarenal). Secondary outcomes included progression to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) and treatment-related complications.ResultsIn this study, 60 patients with a mean age of 56.8 years were included. Tubulointerstitial involvement predominated (80%), with granulomatous interstitial nephritis in 56.7%, while 23.3% exhibited glomerular disease. Patients with interstitial-only patterns achieved more frequently renal recovery at 12 months compared with those with glomerular involvement (65.9% vs 20%, P = .013). Intravenous corticosteroid pulses were associated with faster renal improvement in our cohort, although it should be interpreted cautiously because of potential indication bias. Higher oral corticosteroid doses, prolonged high-dose exposure, or slower tapering strategies did not improve renal outcomes and were associated with increased complications. Over a median follow-up of 36 months, 38.3% of patients experienced relapse, and 6.7% of patients progressed to ESKD.ConclusionsRenal sarcoidosis exhibits heterogeneous presentations but substantial potential for renal recovery, particularly in patients with granulomatous interstitial nephritis. These findings may support further investigation of pathology-informed treatment strategies aimed at reducing corticosteroid exposure. Prospective studies are needed to define optimal treatment strategies.

BACKGROUND

Sarcoidosis is a systemic inflammatory disease of unclear etiology, characterized by the formation of noncaseating granulomas in various tissues, most commonly the pulmonary parenchyma, lymph nodes, and skin [1]. Its prevalence varies significantly across different regions, ranging from 50 to 160 cases per 100 000 persons [2].

Renal sarcoidosis is an uncommon but clinically important manifestation usually presenting as granulomatous tubulointerstitial nephritis (TIN), though nongranulomatous interstitial nephritis and glomerular involvement such as membranous nephropathy, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, or membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis have also been described [3, 4]. In addition, renal injury may result from hypercalcemia and hypercalciuria secondary to dysregulated vitamin D metabolism. Activated macrophages within granulomas express excessive 1α-hydroxylase, leading to increased calcitriol production and impaired calcium homeostasis [5].

The reported prevalence of renal sarcoidosis varies widely, often based on limited epidemiological data and case series. Some studies estimate an incidence of 0.7%–0.9% among patients with sarcoidosis [3], while other selected cohorts have reported figures as high as 25%–30% [6]. This variability, together with frequent under-recognition, makes the true disease burden uncertain.

Management is not standardized, and evidence remains limited. Although one prospective randomized trial evaluated intravenous methylprednisolone pulses followed by oral prednisone versus oral prednisone alone in biopsy-proven sarcoidosis TIN, no additional benefit of pulse therapy on renal outcomes was observed, and optimal corticosteroid regimens remain uncertain [7]. Corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of therapy, and are typically administered for a minimum duration of 6–12 months, with treatment often extending to 18–24 months [3]. However, relapses after withdrawal of corticosteroids are common, and prolonged steroid exposure carries substantial risks. Although steroid-sparing agents such as azathioprine, mycophenolate, and methotrexate are widely used in other renal inflammatory diseases to reduce corticosteroid burden, evidence supporting their role in renal sarcoidosis is sparse, with only a handful of patients reported in prior series. Notably, in a case series of 47 patients, only three received steroid-sparing agents [8].

Given these uncertainties, we conducted a nationwide, multicenter study of patients with biopsy-confirmed renal sarcoidosis10 in Spain to characterize clinical and histopathological features, evaluate treatment regimens, and identify risk factors associated with relapse and renal outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

We conducted a multicenter, retrospective, observational cohort study across hospitals affiliated with the Spanish Group for the Study of Glomerular Diseases (GLOSEN). Patients with biopsy-confirmed renal sarcoidosis, with or without extrarenal manifestations, were included. Renal sarcoidosis was defined as biopsy-proven renal involvement attributable to sarcoidosis. Eligible patients had a kidney biopsy demonstrating granulomatous or nongranulomatous TIN and/or glomerular lesions considered related to sarcoidosis after exclusion of alternative causes. The diagnosis of systemic sarcoidosis was established before, concurrently with, or following kidney biopsy based on compatible clinical, radiological, and/or histopathological findings. A minimum follow-up of 6 months was required. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Hospital Universitario La Paz in Madrid, Spain (PI-5831). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical, laboratory, and histopathologic data

Clinical data included demographic characteristics, relevant comorbidities, and extrarenal manifestations. Laboratory variables included serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) calculated using the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation, spot urine protein-to-creatinine or albumin-to-creatinine ratio, according to availability, as well as serum calcium and 24-hour urinary calcium excretion. Histopathologic variables at diagnosis included the percentage of globally sclerotic glomeruli, the presence of tubulointerstitial lesions (including granulomas), and the presence and pattern of glomerular involvement.

Treatment regimens

Treatment data included initial therapy with corticosteroids, use of intravenous glucocorticoid pulses, initial oral prednisone dose (stratified as ≥0.8 vs <0.8 mg/kg/day), duration of the initial high-dose steroid period (stratified as ≥30 vs <30 days), and duration of steroid tapering until discontinuation. Use of steroid-sparing agents (azathioprine, mycophenolate, methotrexate, hydroxychloroquine, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) inhibitors, leflunomide, and cyclophosphamide) was recorded, including whether these agents were introduced before or after relapse.

Definitions and objectives

The aim of the study was to evaluate treatment strategies that preserve renal function while minimizing relapse and treatment-related adverse events. The primary outcomes were renal recovery (defined as an eGFR improvement ≥50% from first presentation) assessed at 12 months and at the end of follow-up, and disease relapse (either renal or extrarenal). Renal relapse was defined as a deterioration of kidney function and/or increase in proteinuria attributed to sarcoidosis by the treating physician after exclusion of alternative causes, whereas extrarenal relapse was defined as the recurrence or worsening of sarcoidosis-related symptoms in other organs. Secondary objectives included assessment of risks for kidney disease progression and treatment-related adverse effects. Kidney disease progression was defined as a decline in eGFR ≥40% from baseline or progression to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD).

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), according to data distribution, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Normality was assessed using visual inspection of histograms and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Between-group comparisons were performed using the unpaired Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Univariate logistic regression analyses were first conducted to evaluate factors associated with renal recovery, kidney disease progression, and disease relapse. Variables with P < .10 in univariate analyses or considered clinically relevant were entered into multivariate logistic regression models. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors. Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis. Results are expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-tailed P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant. Given the exploratory nature of the study, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied. Prespecified subgroup and sensitivity analyses according to histopathological patterns and treatment strategies are presented in the Supplementary material. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

Study population

In this study, 60 patients with renal sarcoidosis were included. The mean age was 56.8 ± 15.6 years, and 70% were male. Hypertension and diabetes mellitus were present in 40% and 28%, respectively, while 26.7% had prior chronic kidney disease (CKD). Baseline mean eGFR prior to renal involvement was 74.2 ± 28.7 ml/min/1.73 m2. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Median follow-up was 35.6 months (IQR 17–78 months). Clinical presentation

Extrarenal disease was common (85%), most frequently fatigue (80%), lymphadenopathy (68.3%), and pulmonary involvement (61.7%; stage I in 35%). At presentation of renal sarcoidosis, mean eGFR had declined to 26.9 ± 21 ml/min/1.73 m2. Median proteinuria was 0.41 g/day (IQR 0.20–0.83), and sterile pyuria occurred in 24.6%. Hypercalcemia was present in 60%, hypercalciuria in 34.8%, and nephrocalcinosis in 5.1%. Notably, 80.4% of patients had elevated serum angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) levels, with a mean value of 109 ± 73 U/l.

Patients with hypercalciuria had higher serum calcium at presentation (12.0 ± 1.5 vs 10.5 ± 1.4 mg/dl, P = .03), lower eGFR (18.5 ± 9 vs 31.8 ± 26.4 ml/min/1.73 m2, P = .017), and more advanced histological damage, showing a higher proportion of sclerotic glomeruli and tubular atrophy, yet paradoxically exhibited better recovery, with 50% achieving ≥50% eGFR improvement at 12 months versus 19.2% in nonhypercalciuric patients (P = .036).

Histopathological findings

Table 2 describes the histopathological features of our cohort. TIN predominated as the main histopathologic finding (80%), with granulomas present in 56.7%. Interstitial fibrosis was mild (<25%) in 38.3%, moderate (25%–50%) in 25%, and severe (>50%) in 6.7%. Calcium deposits were detected in 11.7%.

Glomerular involvement was identified in 14 patients (23.3%), including focal segmental glomerulosclerosis in 7 patients, followed by membranous nephropathy, membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis, and non-IgA mesangioproliferative glomerulopathy (each in 2 patients), and IgA nephropathy was found in one patient. Most cases with glomerular lesions also exhibited tubulointerstitial involvement (n = 10, 71.4%). Compared with interstitial-only disease, patients with glomerular involvement were younger (49.4 ± 16.4 vs 58.6 ± 14.8 years, P = .042) and presented with higher eGFR at presentation (40.6 ± 33.33 vs 23.1 ± 14.5 ml/min/1.73 m2, P = .087) and proteinuria (0.9 [0.18–3.32] vs 0.38 [0.2–0.76] g/day, P = .152), though differences were not significant (Supplementary Table 1S). Granulomas were less frequent in glomerular cases (35.7% vs 64.4%, P = .057). Patients with interstitial-only disease were more likely to achieve ≥50% eGFR recovery at 12 months (65.9% vs 20%, P = .013) and at the end of follow-up (73.9% vs 28.6%, P = .002) (Supplementary Table 2S), suggesting a better renal prognosis in this group. There were no significant differences in relapse rates or treatment-related adverse events.

Treatment

Most patients (96.7%) received corticosteroids (initial dose 0.8 ± 0.2 mg/kg); 21.7% used intravenous pulses. Median duration of the initial dosing was 36 days (IQR: 19.5–70). Median time to taper to 5 mg/day was 8.5 months (IQR: 5–15.6), and total duration corticosteroid therapy was 20 months (range: 9–53). Steroid-sparing agents were used in 55% of patients, most often azathioprine or mycophenolate (20% each) followed by methotrexate in 15%, hydroxychloroquine and TNF-α inhibitors in 11.7% each, and cyclophosphamide in 6.7%. Chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cell therapy was utilized in one patient (1.7%) for refractory systemic sarcoidosis. Among patients who received steroid-sparing agents, treatment was initiated at a median of 8 months after the diagnosis of renal involvement (IQR: 3–14). Among those with available data regarding treatment duration (26 of the 33 patients receiving steroid-sparing therapy), 18 patients were still receiving treatment at the time of data collection, while the remaining patients had received steroid-sparing therapy for a median duration of 18 months (IQR: 11–54.25).

Baseline characteristics were further analyzed according to different markers of corticosteroid exposure, including initial corticosteroid dose, duration of the initial dose, time under doses >5 mg/day, and the use of steroid-sparing agents, as shown in Supplementary Tables 3S, 6S, 8S, and 10S.

There were no differences regarding the use of steroid-sparing agents, intravenous steroids, or the initial dose of oral steroids between patients with interstitial-only involvement and those with glomerular disease. However, tapering was slower in interstitial-only sarcoidosis (median 10 vs 5 months, P = .021).

Renal outcomes

Renal recovery (≥50% eGFR increase) occurred in 53.3% at 1 month, 48.3% at 12 months, and 61.7% by last follow-up (Fig. 1). Patients achieving renal recovery had lower kidney function at presentation but reached similar kidney function during follow-up, with mean eGFR values of 49.1 ml/min/1.73 m2 at 12 months and 49.8 ml/min/1.73 m2 at last follow-up, compared with 43.1 and 47.2 ml/min/1.73 m2, respectively, among patients without renal recovery (Table 1). Moreover, 13.3% of patients presented with kidney disease progression, and maintenance dialysis was required in 6.7%. Baseline characteristics were also compared according to the need for maintenance dialysis, without relevant differences between groups, as shown in Supplementary Table 14S.

Patients with interstitial-only sarcoidosis showed more eGFR improvement after 12 months than in those with glomerular involvement (P = .003) (Fig. 2). In a parsimonious multivariable logistic regression model including eGFR at presentation, degree of interstitial fibrosis, granulomatous TIN, and histological pattern, eGFR at presentation remained independently associated with renal recovery at 12 months (OR 1.07, 95% CI 1.02–1.13). Interstitial-only disease showed a strong trend toward improved renal recovery at 12 months (OR 6.19, 95% CI 0.87–43.91, P = .068), although statistical significance was not reached (Table 3). In the expanded multivariable model, included as a sensitivity analysis, both interstitial-only disease (OR 10.62, 95% CI 1.10–102.33, P = .041) and granulomatous TIN (OR 36.95, 95% CI 2.97–459.07, P = .005) were independently associated with renal recovery at 12 months (Supplementary Table 15S).

Patients who received intravenous steroid pulses had greater eGFR improvement at 1 month (91.7% vs 51.2%, P = .017) and at 12 months (90% vs 48.8%, P = .03). At the end of follow-up, patients who had received intravenous steroid pulses showed more frequently a ≥50% improvement in eGFR (91.7% vs 57.8%, P = .041) and had faster recovery rates (median increase of 12.9 [3.2–20.7] vs. 4.7 [1.51–8.41] ml/min/year, P = .035) (Supplementary Fig. 1S). In univariate logistic regression, steroid pulse therapy showed a trend toward improved renal outcomes (OR: 4.07, 95% CI: 0.81–20.5, P = .09); however, in multivariate models that included eGFR at presentation, this benefit was attenuated, suggesting that the effect may depend partly on initial renal function.

Steroid dosing intensity and tapering strategy were not associated with improved renal outcomes. There were no significant differences in renal recovery at 12 months or at the end of follow-up, kidney disease progression, or relapse rates between patients receiving higher versus lower initial oral prednisone doses (≥0.8 vs <0.8 mg/kg/day), longer versus shorter high-dose duration (≥30 vs <30 days), orslower versus faster tapering to 5 mg/day (≥6 vs <6 months) or according to whether patients had received steroid-sparing agents (all P > .05) (Supplementary Tables 4S, 7S, 9S, and 11S). The small group of patients who received an initial prednisone dose of <0.6 mg/kg/day achieved renal recovery less frequently (36.4% vs 74.4%, P = .048) (Supplementary Table 5S).

Relapses

During follow-up of these patients, 38.3% experienced relapse. Most relapses were renal (65.3%), while 21.7% presented with extrarenal relapses and 13% exhibited both renal and extrarenal manifestations. Given the renal focus of the study, subsequent relapse analyses were restricted to patients with renal relapse, either isolated or associated with extrarenal relapse; therefore, the five patients with exclusively extrarenal relapse, representing 8.3% of the cohort, were excluded from these specific analyses. The median time to relapse from diagnosis was 11.8 months (IQR: 7.6–17.5). The median steroid dose at the time of relapse was 7.5 mg/day (IQR: 5–25 mg/day), and eight patients (34.8%) had discontinued steroid therapy prior to relapse, with a median interval of 2.5 months (IQR: 2–8) between steroid discontinuation and relapse onset.

Patients with renal relapse presented with more advanced pulmonary disease compared with those without relapse (stage II: 26.3% vs 16.7%; stage III: 10.5% vs 0%), and a trend toward more relapses in patients with cutaneous involvement (34.7% vs 14.2%, P = .067) and granulomatous TIN (73.9% vs 50%, P = .071), but not with renal function at presentation, histology, initial treatment, or duration of steroid therapy (Supplementary Table 12S). Remarkably, there were no differences in achieving renal recovery or progression to ESKD between patients who presented relapses and those who did not (Supplementary Table 13S).

Complications

Infection-related hospitalizations occurred in 16.7% of patients, including 5% with Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia. Overall mortality during follow-up was 16.7%. Higher initial prednisone doses (≥0.8 mg/kg for ≥30 days) were associated with a trend toward an increased infection risk (31.6% vs 10%, P = .065). Additionally, patients treated with steroid-sparing agents prior to relapse had a significantly higher rate of infection-related hospitalizations compared to those who received these agents afterward or not at all (62.5% vs 7.7%, P = .014), likely reflecting higher disease severity and cumulative immunosuppressive exposure. Notably, no opportunistic infections were reported in patients receiving steroid-sparing therapy or those who received prednisone >5 mg/day for <6 months, compared to 10.7% in those who did not receive steroid-sparing agents (P = .096) and 11.5% in those who received prednisone >5 mg/day for >6 months (P = .211).

DISCUSSION

This study represents one of the largest series of histologically confirmed renal sarcoidosis reported to date, and the largest in Spain. The largest published cohort is a German series comprising 327 patients diagnosed with sarcoidosis over 20 years, of whom only 90 had histological confirmation of renal involvement [9]. Compared with prior cohorts, our patients presented with more advanced renal dysfunction at diagnosis [3, 9], but still achieved substantial recovery with immunosuppressive therapy.

A key observation of our study is the role of calcium metabolism. Hypercalciuria, present in approximately one-third of patients, was associated with better renal recovery despite more advanced disease at presentation. This supports prior suggestions that hypercalciuria may reflect active, treatment-responsive inflammation rather than disease severity alone [10]. Hypercalcemia, in contrast, was strikingly more prevalent in our cohort than in earlier studies [11, 12], a discrepancy that may relate to patient selection or regional variations in disease presentation. Nephrocalcinosis, although observed less frequently in our cohort [13], remains a concern for long-term complications associated with calcium dysregulation. These findings underscore the importance of systematically assessing calcium metabolism in suspected cases, both for diagnostic purposes and prognostic stratification.

Histopathological findings were consistent with prior literature, with granulomatous interstitial nephritis as the predominant lesion [3]. However, glomerular involvement was more frequent than typically reported and was associated with distinct clinical features, including higher proteinuria and relatively preserved eGFR. In contrast, interstitial patterns were associated with worse renal function at presentation but greater recovery during follow-up. In univariate analyses, both granulomatous TIN and interstitial-only disease were associated with higher rates of renal recovery, supporting the concept that active inflammatory lesions may retain greater reversibility [10, 11, 14] . In the parsimonious multivariable model, interstitial-only disease showed a strong trend toward improved renal recovery, although statistical significance was not reached. Similar effect directions were observed in the expanded multivariable model, where both interstitial-only disease and granulomatous TIN were independently associated with renal recovery. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the limited sample size, wide confidence intervals, and potential risk of model overfitting. It should be acknowledged that, in some patients, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis may represent a secondary lesion related to chronic tubulointerstitial inflammation, adaptive changes, or aging, rather than a primary sarcoidosis-related glomerulopathy [4]. Nevertheless, we classified these cases within the glomerular pattern subgroup because glomerular lesions were a prominent histopathological finding with absent or mild interstitial fibrosis, suggesting that these glomerular lesions were not attributable to advanced tubulointerstitial damage and secondary adaptive changes alone. Similar observations have been reported in previous studies describing glomerular disease as a distinct manifestation of renal sarcoidosis [15]. Together, these findings suggest that kidney histopathology in sarcoidosis may provide not only diagnostic information but also clinically relevant prognostic information regarding disease presentation and potential reversibility, although the strength of these associations requires confirmation in larger studies.

Treatment patterns in this cohort mirror international practice, with corticosteroids as first-line therapy in nearly all patients and frequent use of steroid-sparing agents as seen in other previous cohorts [9, 16]. In our cohort, prednisone >5 mg/day was prescribed for a median duration of 8 months, aligning with previous studies [8, 17, 18] but shorter than in the Italian cohort [19]. The total median duration of corticosteroid treatment was 20 months, comparable to Mehta’s findings [3]. Notably, patients who received intravenous corticosteroid pulse therapy experienced faster and more pronounced eGFR recovery compared with standard regimens. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the observational design of the study and the potential for confounding by indication, as patients receiving pulse therapy likely represented a subgroup with more severe disease at presentation. Interestingly, our findings differ from the randomized trial by Mahevas et al., which did not demonstrate additional renal benefit from intravenous methylprednisolone pulses compared with oral prednisone alone [7]. Differences in study design, patient selection, inclusion of glomerular lesions, treatment practices, and the observational nature of our cohort may partly explain these discrepancies. However, the absence of consistent long-term differences and the increased infection risk associated with prolonged immunosuppression highlight the need for individualized strategies that balance efficacy with safety.

An important finding of this study is the apparent lack of benefit from higher initial oral corticosteroid doses, prolonged high-dose exposure, or slower tapering strategies. Patients treated with prednisone doses ≥0.8 mg/kg/day, maintained on high doses for ≥30 days, or tapered more slowly to 5 mg/day did not experience higher rates of renal recovery, lower relapse risk, or reduced kidney disease progression compared with those receiving lower intensity or shorter regimens. In contrast, these more intensive strategies were associated with a higher burden of treatment-related complications, particularly infections.

These findings suggest that treatment intensity beyond a certain threshold may not translate into improved renal outcomes in renal sarcoidosis and instead increases cumulative toxicity. In our cohort, intravenous corticosteroid pulse therapy was associated with faster renal improvement; however, this observation should be interpreted cautiously because patients receiving pulse therapy likely represented a subgroup with more severe disease and greater inflammatory activity at presentation. Given the retrospective design and potential confounding by indication, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and do not establish superiority of any specific corticosteroid regimen.

Sarcoidosis has a reported relapse rate that varies from 13% to 75% depending on the selected population [20]. In our cohort, relapses occurred in 38.3% of patients. Importantly, a substantial proportion of patients relapsed after corticosteroid withdrawal (34.8%), emphasizing the challenge of tapering, thus highlighting the importance of balancing corticosteroid tapering with the use of steroid-sparing agents to mitigate relapse risk while ensuring long-term treatment efficacy. Infections were a notable complication, particularly among patients exposed to prolonged corticosteroids or early steroid-sparing therapy, reflecting the cumulative burden of immunosuppression.

Renal outcomes were encouraging, with 63% of patients achieving renal recovery, only 13% showed kidney disease progression and 7% progressed to ESKD. Lower eGFR at presentation was independently associated with renal recovery in multivariable analyses. Granulomatous TIN and interstitial-only disease were associated with higher rates of renal recovery in univariate analyses and showed similar effect directions in adjusted models. Mortality was comparable to other systemic sarcoidosis cohorts, where renal involvement contributes to increased morbidity [11, 14], underscoring the clinical importance of early recognition and tailored therapeutic intervention.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design and the absence of standardized treatment protocols across centers may have introduced indication bias, particularly regarding the use of intravenous corticosteroid pulses. Treatment decisions were made at the discretion of the treating physicians, reflecting real-world practice but limiting causal inference. Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis, which may have introduced selection bias. Additionally, data regarding P. jirovecii prophylaxis were not systematically collected across participating centers, preventing assessment of prophylaxis practices and their potential influence on infectious outcomes. Because renal recovery was defined as a relative improvement in eGFR, patients with lower kidney function at presentation had greater opportunity to reach the outcome threshold. In addition, the relatively small sample size and limited number of outcome events may have reduced statistical power and contributed to wide confidence intervals in multivariable analyses.

In conclusion, renal sarcoidosis demonstrates a substantial capacity for renal recovery when promptly recognized and appropriately treated, particularly in patients with granulomatous interstitial nephritis. Our findings highlight the prognostic value of kidney histopathology and suggest that active inflammatory lesions may be associated with greater renal reversibility. Intravenous corticosteroid pulse therapy was associated with faster renal improvement in this cohort, although causal interpretation is limited by the retrospective design and potential confounding by indication. Higher oral corticosteroid doses, prolonged high-dose exposure, and slower tapering strategies were not associated with improved renal outcomes and were linked to greater treatment-related morbidity. Prospective studies are needed to confirm these observations and to define evidence-based treatment strategies for renal sarcoidosis.
