Giant primary hydatid cyst of the sigmoid mesocolon: a surgical case report
Authors: Unaiza Erum, Omer Bin Khalid Jamil, Muhammad Imran, Ayesha Noman, Reem Shahid, Muhammad Khalid Siddique, Ali Raza Arshad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Giant primary hydatid cysts of the sigmoid mesocolon are exceptionally rare, and their exact site of origin may be difficult to establish before surgery. We report a 51-year-old woman with an 8-year history of progressive abdominal distension and vague abdominal discomfort. CT showed a multilocular cystic lesion of size 30 × 22 × 28 cm extending from the pelvis to the upper abdomen, with internal septations and multiple daughter cysts. Echinococcus serology was positive, with no radiological evidence of hepatic or pulmonary hydatid disease. The patient had received several previous courses of albendazole without sustained improvement, although the duration and adherence of these courses could not be independently verified. At laparotomy, the cyst was found to arise from the sigmoid mesocolon and was excised by total pericystectomy, with preservation of the sigmoid colon and mesocolic vessels. Recovery was uneventful. Albendazole was continued postoperatively for three months, and no recurrence was seen on clinical and ultrasonographic follow-up at 6 months. This case illustrates a rare giant primary sigmoid mesocolon hydatid cyst in which the diagnosis was suspected radiologically, but the precise mesocolic origin was confirmed only intraoperatively.

Introduction

Hydatid disease, or cystic echinococcosis, is a zoonotic infection caused by the larval form of Echinococcus granulosus, most commonly involving the liver and lungs[1–3]. Peritoneal hydatidosis is uncommon, reported in approximately 2–13% of cases of abdominal hydatid disease, and is usually secondary to hepatic cyst rupture, dissemination, or previous surgery[3,4]. Primary peritoneal and mesenteric hydatid cysts are much rarer, with isolated involvement of the sigmoid mesocolon being exceedingly unusual[4–6]. Case presentation

A 51-year-old woman presented with an 8-year history of progressive abdominal distension and vague abdominal discomfort. She also reported intermittent abdominal pain, early satiety, and bloating. There was no history of fever, jaundice, nausea, vomiting, weight loss, change in bowel habits, or urinary symptoms. She had no known contact with livestock or dogs. Her past surgical history included a hysterectomy, an oophorectomy, and a total thyroidectomy.

Over the preceding 8 years, she had received several intermittent courses of albendazole from local healthcare facilities for presumed hydatid disease. She recalled taking albendazole 400 mg twice daily during these courses and reported compliance; however, the exact duration of each course and written documentation of treatment were not available. She had previously been advised to undergo surgical management but had deferred surgery because of personal and family circumstances.

On examination, she was comfortable and haemodynamically stable. The abdomen was uniformly distended, soft and non-tender, with a palpable, smooth, cystic mass. Routine blood tests were within acceptable limits. Contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen and pelvis revealed a well-defined cystic lesion measuring 30 × 22 × 28 cm, extending from the pelvis to the epigastrium, with internal septations and multiple daughter cysts (Figs 1–3). The lesion was closely related to the urinary bladder, rectum, sigmoid colon, and rectosigmoid junction; however, intervening fat planes were grossly preserved. No hepatic hydatid cyst was identified on abdominal imaging, and chest radiography did not show any pulmonary involvement. Echinococcus antibody testing by ELISA was positive. Preoperatively, the lesion was considered most likely to be a large abdominopelvic hydatid cyst; however, the exact site of origin was not clear on imaging. A mesenteric cyst and an ovarian/pelvic cystic lesion were also considered in the differential diagnosis. Albendazole was started preoperatively after the diagnosis was supported by imaging and serology; however, surgery was not delayed because of the giant size of the cyst, progressive symptoms, and lack of sustained response to previous medical treatment.

Through a midline laparotomy, a large tense cystic mass was encountered and exposed with protection of the surrounding field (Fig. 4). The operative field was isolated with multiple packs and gauze soaked in hypertonic saline to reduce the risk of intraperitoneal contamination. Controlled puncture and aspiration were performed, followed by instillation of 15% hypertonic saline into the cyst cavity. After a 15-minute contact period, the solution was re-aspirated. Approximately 8500 mL of straw-colored fluid and numerous daughter cysts were evacuated. The cyst was then decompressed and dissected from a fibrous attachment to the sigmoid mesocolon (Fig. 5). The sigmoid colon was separate from the cyst wall, and the adjacent mesocolic vessels were preserved. Complete pericystectomy was achieved without gross spillage, bowel injury, or bowel resection. The peritoneal cavity was thoroughly inspected and lavaged, hemostasis was secured, and a drain was placed. The gross specimen demonstrated an intact cyst wall after excision (Fig. 6). The postoperative course was uneventful. She stayed in the intensive care unit for 24 hours for observation and was discharged on the fourth postoperative day. Histopathology confirmed features consistent with a hydatid cyst. Albendazole 400 mg twice daily was continued postoperatively for three months, with liver function monitoring and no treatment-related derangements observed. Follow-up consisted of clinical assessment and abdominal ultrasonography. At 6 months, she remained asymptomatic, with no sonographic evidence of residual or recurrent disease. She was subsequently lost to further follow-up because she lived far from the treating center.

Discussion

Peritoneal echinococcosis is uncommon and usually occurs secondary to rupture, dissemination, or seeding from hepatic hydatid cysts[3,4]. Primary peritoneal or mesenteric hydatid disease is much less frequent, and isolated involvement of the sigmoid mesocolon is particularly rare[4–6]. Hydatid cysts arising within the peritoneal cavity may reach a large size because of the compliant intra-abdominal space and may remain clinically silent for several years[4,5]. In the present case, the long duration of symptoms, giant size of the cyst, absence of hepatic or pulmonary involvement, and sigmoid mesocolon origin made the presentation unusual.

Preoperative diagnosis can be difficult when hydatid disease presents as a solitary giant abdominopelvic cyst. In this patient, the main differential diagnoses included a mesenteric cyst, an ovarian or pelvic cystic lesion, lymphangioma, a duplication cyst, and other benign cystic intra-abdominal lesions. The presence of daughter cysts on imaging and positive Echinococcus serology supported hydatid disease; however, the exact origin from the sigmoid mesocolon was confirmed only during laparotomy[2,5–7].

Similar diagnostic difficulty has been described in other unusual intra-abdominal hydatid cysts. Adelyar et al reported a primary mesenteric hydatid cyst and reviewed the rarity of this presentation[5]. Gandhiraman et al reported a primary peritoneal hydatid cyst presenting as an ovarian cyst torsion, illustrating how pelvic hydatid disease may mimic gynecological pathology[6]. A systematic review of colonic hydatid disease further highlights the rarity of colonic involvement[8]. Compared with these reports, the present case is notable for its giant size, primary sigmoid mesocolon attachment, and successful complete excision without bowel resection.

Imaging is central to diagnosis and operative planning. Ultrasonography is useful as an initial imaging modality, while CT helps define the size and extent of the lesion, its relationship to adjacent organs, and the presence of daughter cysts or internal septations[2,7]. In this case, CT demonstrated a giant multilocular cystic lesion extending from the pelvis to the upper abdomen, with daughter cysts and preserved fat planes around adjacent structures. The lesion was closely related to the bladder, rectum, sigmoid colon, and rectosigmoid junction, but the precise mesocolic origin was not established preoperatively. Serology can support the diagnosis, although negative results do not exclude hydatid disease, particularly in isolated extrahepatic disease[1,4].

Surgery remains the main treatment for giant, symptomatic, or refractory hydatid cysts[9–11]. The main operative concerns are prevention of spillage, avoidance of secondary dissemination or anaphylaxis, and preservation of adjacent bowel and mesocolic vessels. Hypertonic saline was used in this case because it is a commonly used scolicidal agent and is readily available in our setting. Its use was combined with isolation of the operative field using hypertonic saline-soaked packs, controlled aspiration, a 15-minute contact period, re-aspiration, and lavage. Total pericystectomy was possible because the cyst was separate from the sigmoid colon and was attached to the sigmoid mesocolon by fibrous tissue. The adjacent mesocolic vessels were preserved, and no bowel resection was required.

Postoperative albendazole is commonly used after surgical treatment to reduce the risk of recurrence, particularly after large cysts or unusual extrahepatic disease[9–11]. In this patient, albendazole was continued for 3 months after surgery with liver function monitoring. She remained asymptomatic, and ultrasound showed no residual or recurrent disease at 6 months. However, the documented follow-up period remains relatively short for hydatid disease, as late recurrence can occur. In addition, the duration and adherence of her previous albendazole courses could not be independently verified. These limitations restrict assessment of long-term recurrence and the adequacy of prior medical therapy.

This case adds to the limited literature on primary hydatid cysts of the sigmoid mesocolon. To the best of our knowledge, this appears to be the first reported case from Pakistan of a giant primary hydatid cyst arising from the sigmoid mesocolon, although similar cases have been described from other regions. It also illustrates that the absence of classical risk factors or hepatic involvement should not exclude hydatid disease from the differential diagnosis of a giant abdominopelvic cystic lesion. Careful operative isolation and attention to mesocolic vascular preservation can allow complete excision while avoiding bowel resection.

Conclusion

Primary hydatid cyst of the sigmoid mesocolon is extremely rare and may be difficult to localize before surgery. For giant symptomatic cysts, careful operative isolation, controlled use of a scolicidal agent, and attention to mesocolic vascular preservation can allow complete excision without bowel resection. Postoperative albendazole and longer-term surveillance remain important because recurrence may occur late.
