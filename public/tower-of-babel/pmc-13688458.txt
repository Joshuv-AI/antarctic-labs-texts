Clinical Trial of Tolvaptan in Pediatric ADPKD: Two-Year, Open-Label Extension
Authors: Djalila Mekahli, Lisa M Guay-Woodford, Melissa A Cadnapaphornchai, Larry A Greenbaum, Mieczyslaw Litwin, Tomas Seeman, Ann Dandurand, Zhen Zhang, Kimberly Sikes, Susan E Shoaf, Franz Schaefer, Pediatric Trial Investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTolvaptan is indicated for adults with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) at increased risk of rapid progression. Pharmacodynamic activity and acceptable safety and tolerability were demonstrated in a 1-year, randomized, placebo-controlled trial in children and adolescents with ADPKD (Phase A of a 2-part, phase-3b study [NCT02964273]). Here, the 2-year, open-label extension (Phase B) is reported.MethodsPhase A participants could enter Phase B if they had not experienced adverse events requiring discontinuation. All Phase B participants received tolvaptan, titrated based on body weight and tolerability. Outcomes included safety, tolerability, kidney function, height-adjusted total kidney volume (htTKV), and health-related quality of life (HRQoL).ResultsOf 84 Phase A completers, 81 enrolled in Phase B. No liver enzyme elevations observed in either phase met criteria for drug-induced liver injury. The previous placebo group experienced more aquaresis-related (48.7% vs. 14.3%) and dehydration-related (28.2% vs. 14.3%) adverse events than the previous tolvaptan group in Phase B. Tolerability was favorable, with HRQoL outcomes indicating high functioning in both the trial phases. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) remained stable in the tolvaptan arm and decreased in the placebo arm of Phase A (annual slope in ml/min per 1.73 m2 of 2.4 and −2.2, respectively [P = 0.04]), with similar slopes of decline for both arms of Phase B (−1.3 in the prior tolvaptan arm and −2.1 in the prior placebo arm [P = 0.54]). htTKV growth was slowest during the first year of tolvaptan treatment.ConclusionTolvaptan-related safety and overall HRQoL remained favorable, and kidney function was stable in this 2-year extension. The slowest htTKV growth occurred during year 1 of tolvaptan treatment.Keywords: ADPKD, clinical study, open-label extension, pediatrics, tolvaptan

The understanding of ADPKD has shifted in recent years from an “adult” disease to one that, in many cases, already manifests clinical signs and symptoms during childhood.1,2 A small proportion of children with ADPKD have very early-onset or rapidly progressing disease with massive kidney enlargement and decline in GFR.3, 4, 5 However, research has increasingly demonstrated that in young patients with early onset of ADPKD, signs of kidney dysfunction (e.g., urinary concentrating defects, proteinuria, and glomerular hyperfiltration) and comorbidities such as hypertension are often present, highlighting the presence of disease progression before GFR loss is evident.6, 7, 8, 9 Such findings have increased the urgency of questions related to the management of ADPKD in pediatric patients, the optimal frequency of monitoring for individuals at risk due to family history, when to screen or indications for screening, how to assess progression risk, and whether and when to initiate treatment.10,11

The current standard of care for pediatric ADPKD is supportive and includes antihypertensive therapy when needed, maintenance of normal weight, decreased sodium consumption, and attention to adequate hydration.5 Based on the results of 2 large-scale, pivotal clinical trials in adults (TEMPO 3:4 and REPRISE), the vasopressin V2 receptor antagonist tolvaptan received regulatory approval as the first disease-specific pharmacotherapy for ADPKD and is indicated to slow kidney function decline in adults at the risk of rapid progression.12,13 Given the efficacy of tolvaptan in these trials, the possibility may exist for extending the adult treatment paradigm to the pediatric population at risk of rapid progression and represents an opportunity to intervene at an earlier stage and thereby better preserve long-term kidney function.1,2 Leuven Imaging Classification, which stratifies risk for rapid progression based on total kidney volume, was developed recently as a potential method for selecting children with ADPKD who might benefit from early intervention.14,15

The randomized, placebo-controlled Phase A of an ongoing, phase 3b, 2-part study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02964273) provided data on the 1-year safety and tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamic activity of tolvaptan in children and adolescents diagnosed with ADPKD.16 Tolvaptan exerted vasopressin V2 receptor antagonism with acceptable 1-year safety and tolerability, with relatively few discontinuations. Here we present the results of the 2-year, open-label phase of the trial (Phase B), which provided longer-term assessment of tolvaptan in children with ADPKD.

Methods

Study methods for Phase A have been reported in detail.16,17 Key inclusion criteria for Phase A included age 4–17 years, ADPKD diagnosis, and eGFR ≥ 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2. The ADPKD diagnosis had to be based on family history or genetic criteria in addition to the presence of ≥ 10 kidney cysts, each ≥ 0.5 cm, confirmed by magnetic resonance imaging (for participants aged 12–17 years) or the presence of ≥ 4 cysts, each ≥ 1 cm, confirmed by ultrasound (for participants aged < 12 years). Phase B was an open-label extension during which participants who had completed Phase A received treatment with tolvaptan for up to 24 months. The objective of Phase B was to obtain safety and efficacy data on long-term use of tolvaptan. Participants could roll over from Phase A into Phase B if they were willing to continue in the trial and did not have any adverse events that would require treatment discontinuation. As reported, during Phase A, 1 participant in each treatment arm discontinued study medication due to adverse events (pollakiuria [tolvaptan] and dizziness [placebo]).16

Participants who continued into Phase B received open label tolvaptan. To preserve the blind in Phase A, participants in Phase B were started at a dose based on their current body weight and after 1 week were asked to uptitrate once from their starting dose, as previously described for Phase A. Participants who wished to lower their dose in the extension due to tolerability issues had their doses adjusted as necessary.

Postbaseline study visits in Phase B occurred at week 1, month 1, month 6, month 12, month 18, and month 24. Laboratory assessments to monitor safety were performed monthly, with more frequent liver function testing if signs or symptoms of abnormal liver function were observed. htTKV and eGFR were evaluated as described.16,17 Participants aged 12–17 years underwent volumetric measurement using magnetic resonance imaging, whereas those aged < 12 years were evaluated by ultrasound. Per the trial protocol, HRQoL was to be assessed on the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) Generic Core Scale and the PedsQL Multidimensional Fatigue Scale for participants aged ≥ 12 years.18, 19, 20 Each scale ranges from 0–100, with higher scores indicating better quality of life. Detailed description of these questionnaires is provided in Supplementary Methods.

The coprimary trial endpoints (urine osmolality and specific gravity) were evaluated in Phase A. Secondary endpoints evaluated in Phase B included change from baseline in eGFR and htTKV through Phase B at month 24 as well as safety and tolerability. Both eGFR and htTKV were calculated using the mixed model repeated measures method with model terms treatment, visit, treatment by visit, and baseline by visit interaction. The estimated annual slope of eGFR was compared between treatment groups in each trial phase using a mixed model with treatment, year, interaction of treatment and year, and baseline eGFR as covariates, and random intercepts/slopes over year. The model was fitted using an unstructured covariance matrix. Consistent with the previous study report,16 slope estimates were calculated starting at week 1 of each trial phase to exclude the rapid hemodynamic effect after tolvaptan initiation. Annualized change in htTKV during Phase B was estimated using linear mixed modeling.12 All safety and efficacy data were summarized for observed (nonmissing) values only. Missing data were mitigated by encouraging participants who withdrew from treatment to return for their remaining visits.

Results

Participant Disposition

A total of 84 participants (44 in tolvaptan group and 40 in placebo group) completed Phase A, and 81 (42 in prior tolvaptan group and 39 in prior placebo group) participants enrolled in Phase B (Figure 1). Subsequently, 33/42 (78.6%) in the prior tolvaptan group and 30/39 (76.9%) in the prior placebo group completed the Phase B month 24 visit on tolvaptan.

Demographics and Baseline Characteristics

Demographic characteristics in Phase B were well balanced between the treatment groups (Table 1). Of the 81 treated participants, 41 (50.6%) were male. All but 1 of the participants were White. The overall mean age at Phase B baseline was 14.1 years (range: 7–19 years) and the mean body mass index was 20.3 kg/m2 (range: 14.0–35.4 kg/m2). Mean (standard deviation) eGFR at Phase B baseline was 97.0 (17.1) ml/min per 1.73 m2 in the prior tolvaptan group and 98.5 (14.1) ml/min per 1.73 m2 in the prior placebo group.

Tolvaptan Exposure

Treatment compliance as determined by returned study drug was ≥ 90% for most participants in Phase B (32/42 [76.2%] in the prior tolvaptan group and 28/39 [71.8%] in the prior placebo group). Regarding duration of tolvaptan exposure, 64% of the study population had exposure ≥ 90% of Phase B follow-up (i.e., at least 661 days or 22 months), including 27/42 (64.3%) participants in the prior tolvaptan group and 25/39 (64.1%) participants in the prior placebo group. The average daily dose during Phase B was 41.1 mg in the prior tolvaptan group and 38.0 mg in the prior placebo group.

Efficacy

Changes in eGFR and htTKV are displayed through Phase A and Phase B to illustrate the changes over a maximum duration of follow-up. The eGFR data are shown starting at week 1 of Phase A to exclude the early hemodynamic effect of tolvaptan (Figure 2). In Phase A, eGFR remained stable in the tolvaptan group and decreased in the placebo group. In Phase B, the initial drop in eGFR in the prior placebo group shows the hemodynamic effect after tolvaptan initiation, after which eGFR declined slightly to Phase B month 24 in both the treatment groups. The estimated annual slope of eGFR in the mixed model, starting at Phase A week 1, was 2.4 ml/min per 1.73 m2 for the tolvaptan group and −2.2 ml/min per 1.73 m2 for the placebo group in Phase A, with a significant difference of −4.6 ml/min per 1.73 m2 (standard error, 2.2; P = 0.04). The estimated annual slope of eGFR for Phase B, starting at Phase B week 1, was −1.3 ml/min per 1.73 m2 in the prior tolvaptan group and −2.1 ml/min per 1.73 m2 in the prior placebo group, with a nonsignificant difference of −0.8 ml/min per 1.73 m2 (standard error, 1.3; P = 0.54).

Adverse events related to aquaresis were more frequent in the prior placebo group (i.e., the group of participants who newly initiated tolvaptan in Phase B) than in the prior tolvaptan group: thirst (3 [7.7%] in the prior placebo group vs. 1 [2.4%] in the prior tolvaptan group), polydipsia (2 [5.1%] vs. 1 [2.4%]), nocturia (6 [15.4%] vs. 1 [2.4%]), pollakiuria (2 [5.1%] vs. 1 [2.4%]), and polyuria (13 [33.3%] vs. 2 [4.8%]), with aquaretic adverse events of any type occurring in 19 (48.7%) participants in the prior placebo group and 6 (14.3%) participants in the prior tolvaptan group. Similarly, dehydration-related adverse events of any kind occurred in a higher proportion of participants in the prior placebo group (11 [28.2%]) than participants in the prior tolvaptan group (6 [14.3%]).

Serious adverse events were reported for 7 participants in the prior tolvaptan group and 8 participants in the prior placebo group (the events are listed in the Supplementary Results). Among these, an event of increased alanine aminotransferase (ALT) in 1 participant (in the prior tolvaptan group) and events of increased ALT and increased aspartate aminotransferase (AST) in another participant (in the prior placebo group) were considered by the investigators to be related to tolvaptan. In the participant who experienced increased ALT alone, the adverse event resolved without action taken regarding tolvaptan. The individual who experienced elevated ALT and AST recovered after tolvaptan was temporarily interrupted.

Of note, 3 participants, all in the prior placebo group, discontinued tolvaptan due to adverse events, including eating disorder, polyuria, and increased liver function test. The event of polyuria was assessed as related to tolvaptan by the investigator, and the others were not.

Potentially Clinically Significant Laboratory Values

Liver enzyme monitoring indicated that 4 participants in Phase B met prespecified criteria for potentially clinically significant liver enzyme elevations; the criteria are shown in Supplementary Table S1, and the cases are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. Of note, 3 of the elevations were considered serious adverse events (1 participant in the prior tolvaptan group and 2 participants in the prior placebo group). All of these participants recovered after interruption of tolvaptan dosing (2 participants) or no action was taken regarding tolvaptan (1 participant). The other potentially clinically significant elevation was a nonserious adverse event that led to discontinuation (participant in the prior placebo group), with the participant reported as recovering at the time of last follow-up. There were no laboratory abnormalities that met criteria for drug-induced liver injury in either phase of the trial (i.e., ALT or AST > 3 times the upper limit of normal or total bilirubin > 2 times the upper limit of normal; ALT or AST 5–8 times the upper limit of normal for more than 1 week; or ALT or AST > 8 times the upper limit of normal).

In addition, potentially clinically significant changes in other laboratory parameters were assessed (Supplementary Table S1). Creatinine elevations occurred in 3 participants in the prior tolvaptan group and in 3 participants in the prior placebo group. In addition, 3 participants (2 in previously treated with tolvaptan and 1 in previously treated with placebo) had a decrease in neutrophil count, 1 participant in the prior tolvaptan group had an increase in triglycerides, and 3 participants in the prior placebo group had, respectively, an increase in sodium, an increase in potassium, and a decrease in glucose levels.

Growth

There were no notable changes from Phase B baseline in height standard deviation scores in the 2 treatment groups across age and sex subgroups (Supplementary Table S3).

Quality of Life

At the end of Phase A, improvement in PedsQL Generic Core Scale total score in the tolvaptan group reached the specified minimal clinically important difference of 4.4 points,21 whereas participants in the placebo group showed overall improvement but did not reach this threshold. Scores in Phase B continued the trend seen during Phase A, with results staying relatively stable throughout the treatment period, indicating low HRQoL burden and high functioning (Figure 4a). During Phase B, participants in the prior tolvaptan group remained at or above the minimal clinically important difference threshold, whereas participants in the prior placebo group showed improvement to around the threshold. Statistical comparison between treatment arms was not performed.

The PedsQL Multidimensional Fatigue Scale total score indicated a low level of fatigue-related problems for both treatment groups throughout both study phases (Figure 4b). No minimal clinically important difference has been established for this measure. Scores on the sleep and rest fatigue subscale, which specifically measures fatigue related to sleep quality and rest, were assessed to explore potential effects of nocturia. Mean scores indicated moderate sleep and rest-related fatigue during Phase A (ranging from 72–79 points in the tolvaptan arm and 64–79 points in the placebo arm from baseline to month 12), with little numerical difference between treatment arms. Scores indicated mild to moderate fatigue in Phase B (ranging from 79–82 points in the prior tolvaptan arm and 78–82 points in the prior placebo arm from Phase B baseline to month 24).

Discussion

The 2-year extension data presented here enable longer-term evaluation of tolvaptan in a population of children and adolescents with ADPKD previously treated for 1 year in a randomized, controlled trial.16 Consistent with adult data in the TEMPO 3:4 and TEMPO 4:4 trials, children and adolescents who received tolvaptan exhibited more stable eGFR than those who received placebo in the randomized, controlled trial phase. The difference in eGFR decline between treatment groups in Phase A was preserved during the extension, even as eGFR slopes in Phase B were similar for the prior tolvaptan and prior placebo groups, suggesting that earlier intervention to preserve kidney function may be beneficial. These findings in the pediatric population are suggestive rather than definitive, given the relatively small sample size, open-label design of the extension, and post hoc nature of the analysis.

As in adults, total kidney volume growth on magnetic resonance imaging was suppressed in the first year of treatment in each treatment group and was higher after 1 year of treatment. The annualized increase in htTKV during Phase B was lower in the prior placebo group (3.3%) than in the prior tolvaptan group (6.5%), indicating that the effect of tolvaptan on total kidney volume growth is greatest at an early stage after initiation. During the follow-up period encompassing both study phases, there was no evident difference in overall htTKV growth over 3 years between participants who initially received tolvaptan versus those who first received placebo.

Aquaretic adverse events were more frequent in the prior placebo group (i.e., participants who had newly initiated tolvaptan in Phase B), with a similar average daily tolvaptan dose in the prior placebo (38.0 mg) and prior tolvaptan (41.1 mg) groups. This result suggests that patients treated with tolvaptan may become accustomed to aquaretic adverse effects (and hence be less likely to report them) or that they may subside over time. This interpretation is supported by the fact that the frequencies of aquaresis- and dehydration-related adverse events were comparable between the prior placebo group during Phase B and the tolvaptan treatment arm of Phase A.16 Both groups had newly initiated tolvaptan during these respective follow-up periods.

More than 75% of Phase B participants in each treatment group completed Phase B while taking tolvaptan, further supporting the feasibility of pediatric dosing with the option of down-titration for tolerability, as initially established in Phase A.16 It should be qualified that only participants who did not experience adverse events resulting in treatment discontinuation in Phase A were eligible to enter Phase B (1 patient in each of the Phase A treatment arms discontinued due to adverse events). Accordingly, the Phase B participants represent a relatively treatment-tolerant population. Reported compliance with medication was good, with ≥ 90% compliance in 76% of the prior tolvaptan group and 72% of the prior placebo group in Phase B. In each treatment arm, 64% of participants reached a tolvaptan duration of exposure ≥ 90% of the 2-year follow-up. The different tolvaptan compliance and exposure rates may be explained by the different methods used to determine compliance (returned study medication) and exposure time (based on medication start and stop dates recorded by the investigator) and suggest that the results regarding compliance and exposure should be interpreted with caution.

Regular liver function monitoring enabled prompt detection and appropriate management of enzyme elevations, including tolvaptan interruption or discontinuation, to avert drug-induced liver injury. According to the US postmarketing pharmacovigilance data in adults, confirmed events of serious and potentially fatal liver injury were reported for 4 out of 10,879 patients, all of whom discontinued tolvaptan and recovered.22

During both phases of the trial, there were no notable changes in the height standard deviation scores. HRQoL scores for tolvaptan-treated participants in both Phase A and Phase B remained at consistently high levels, with no substantial decreases from baseline, indicating a high level of functioning during follow-up. Participants in both the treatment arms experienced mild to moderate burden on the sleep and rest fatigue subscale of the PedsQL Multidimensional Fatigue Scale across study phases, which might be explained by urinary symptoms of ADPKD.

A limitation of this study is that the statistical comparisons between the treatment groups for eGFR and htTKV were post hoc and conducted in small populations; accordingly, conclusions regarding these outcomes are preliminary and require further statistical evaluation and research. In addition, change in kidney function has limited utility as an outcome measure for pediatric patients with ADPKD, given that eGFR typically remains stable for long periods in individuals who are younger or have early-stage disease, making a treatment effect more difficult to discern. Another complicating factor is that an apparently normal eGFR in early ADPKD may obscure substantial loss of glomerular function in the presence of compensatory hyperfiltration by the remaining glomeruli.23

This trial was conducted before an algorithm was available to estimate progression risk in pediatric ADPKD. Use of risk stratification tools such as Leuven Imaging Classification,14 which is based on htTKV, may facilitate treatment decisions for young patients, who are most likely to benefit from pharmacotherapy. Other early kidney abnormalities, such as hyperfiltration, hypertension, proteinuria, and decreased urinary concentrating capacity, have been proposed as potential indicators of progression risk in pediatric patients.9 Scores on patient-reported HRQoL instruments specific to ADPKD have been shown to predict subsequent clinical and health-economic outcomes in this population, highlighting the importance of quality of life evaluation in clinical trials.24

Future research will better characterize disease progression, potential prognostic indicators, and the optimal monitoring and treatment of pediatric ADPKD. Within the context of an evolving understanding of the disease, the results from Phase B of this trial support additional investigation of tolvaptan as a potential treatment for this population.
