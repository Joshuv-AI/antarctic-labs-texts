Conservative management of placenta accreta spectrum disorders: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Simon Benoit Dubé, Dong Bach Nguyen, Cassandra Della Rocca, Jessica Papillon Smith, Fady W. Mansour, Srinivasan Krishnamurthy, Richard Brown, Andrew Zakhari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlacenta Accreta Spectrum Disorders (PASD) are rare but life-threatening obstetric conditions characterized by abnormal placental adherence into or beyond the myometrium, resultant from previous uterine scarring, often rendering placental separation either impossible or associated with a significant risk of massive hemorrhage. As a result, caesarean hysterectomy is commonly performed following delivery, to manage associated complications. With the rising incidence of PASD paralleling the global increase in cesarean deliveries, there is a growing need to investigate and highlight fertility-preserving surgical alternatives. This study aimed to assess the rate of uterine preservation and maternal and fetal outcomes of conservatively managed placenta accreta spectrum disorders (PASD).MethodsThis retrospective cohort study was performed for conservatively managed patients with PASD at our center between January 2011 and February 2022. Cases were retrieved through operative bookings and codified delivery statistics from the birthing center. Necessary data was then collected through individual chart review.ResultsEleven patients underwent conservative management of PASD, of whom eight were delivered by elective caesarean, two by emergency cesarean, and one was an out-born post-partum transfer. A diagnosis of PASD was suspected antenatally in seven of the patients. Seven patients (63.6%) underwent expectant management, with the placenta left in situ, whereas the other four (36.4%) had extirpative or resective management. A total of 8 patients underwent embolization by interventional radiology immediately following their cesarean, and repeat embolization was performed in two patients. Of the 7 patients managed expectantly, three patients had spontaneous resolution of their residual placenta, three patients underwent interval hysterectomy within a range of 24 to 116 days, while the remaining patient required a curettage. The mean time to resolution of patients who underwent expectant management was 83 days.ConclusionsIn this study, 72.7% of conservatively managed cases of PASD avoided hysterectomy following caesarean delivery. While not without risks, conservative management of PASD can be offered to patients wishing to preserve fertility after individualized counselling.

Background

Placenta Accreta Spectrum disorders (PASD) are a rare but potentially life-threatening group of conditions which have been steadily rising in prevalence in parallel with increased rates of caesarean section worldwide [1]. They are characterized as primary uterine disorders resulting from previous uterine scarring, typically from prior caesarean section, leading to secondary abnormal placental adherence into the myometrium or beyond, making placental delivery either impossible or associated with massive hemorrhage [2–4]. As such, hysterectomy immediately following caesarean delivery is often performed, despite the existence of alternative fertility-sparing surgical options. Abnormal placentation is theorized to occur following cesarean births due to disruption of the endometrial–myometrial interface and subsequent altered physiological cell signaling and regulatory mechanisms [3–5].

With the advent of better imaging and a better understanding of the disease, PASD is increasingly diagnosed in the antepartum period and appropriate counselling and management can be offered well in advance of delivery, taking into consideration patient specific factors such as desire for future pregnancies [6]. However, some cases are still recognized at the time of delivery and rates of maternal morbidity can be extremely high due to the complexity and risk of a caesarean-hysterectomy, whether elective or urgent [7]. For this reason, and with the intention of maintaining fertility, some surgeons have increasingly been advocating for conservative management of PASD [8].

Four main approaches have been described for the conservative management of PASD [9]. Expectant management consists of leaving the adherent placenta in situ, in the hopes of eventual spontaneous involution or detachment and expulsion. In the extirpative method, the surgeon will forcefully detach the entirety or part of the placenta from the myometrium. The resective-constructive method aims at resecting the placenta and the myometrium en bloc before reconstructing the uterus. Finally, the Triple P procedure consists of preoperative placement of internal iliac artery occlusive balloon catheters, full resection of the placenta and affected myometrium following caesarean delivery, followed by uterine reconstruction.

Although these techniques have all shown some degree of success in the literature, studies remain sparse. Fertility after conservative treatment has been demonstrated after successful conservative management [10, 11], but at the potential cost of an increased risk of post-partum hemorrhage, sepsis, and the need for delayed hysterectomy in those cases where this is unsuccessful [12, 13]. Recent meta-analyses have strengthened the evidence supporting conservative management for PASD and its feasibility, however, further research is needed to more definitively evaluate approaches and assess their long-term clinical outcomes [14, 15].

The primary goal of this study is to review all conservatively managed PASD cases at single tertiary-care academic hospital and evaluate the short- and long-term maternal outcomes of retaining the uterus in this high-risk population.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective chart review of all patients who underwent conservative management of PASD at the McGill University Health Centre – Royal Victoria Hospital between January 2011 and February 2022. Patients who delivered outside of our institution but were referred for management of a suspected invasive placenta were also included. All patients who had a hysterectomy at the time of caesarean section were excluded. We conducted a search of our surgical management tool (Centricity Opera 4.5, GE Healthcare, Barrington IL, USA) for caesarean sections that were either done in the main operating room, where most complicated cases are performed, or in the labor and delivery surgical suite with complexity codes including any mention of PASD (i.e. placenta accreta, increta, and percreta), placenta praevia, or internal iliac artery ligation. We also reviewed our obstetrics imaging system for similar keywords or any report mentioning PASD. Demographic data and outcomes of patients with conservatively managed PASD were then collected through chart review of the patient’s electronic medical record and populated in pretested data collection forms. Relevant demographic data included age, parity, gestational age, body mass index (BMI), number of prior caesarean sections, prior gynecological surgery, smoking, and fertility treatments. Imaging reports from ultrasound and MRI were also reviewed, as were operative findings such as estimated blood loss, use of blood products and blood conservation strategies, and surgical approach. Patients’ postoperative course was reviewed for use of additional blood products, ICU admission, need for embolization, use of methotrexate, need for additional procedures (i.e. dilatation curettage, hysterectomy) and resolution of the PASD. Any information on subsequent pregnancies, if available, was also extracted. Missing data predominantly from the out-born patients was marked as unknown.

This study was conducted in accord with the Tri-Council Policy Statement: Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans (2018), and full Research Ethics Board approval was obtained prior to commencement (REB# 2022–8562). Informed consent was not required for this study, given its retrospective nature.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 349 patients were screened through our surgical booking software and 237 through our imaging system. Fifteen patients were identified who had undergone conservative management of PASD during this time period. Among those, four vaginal deliveries were excluded from the study due to the inability to confirm the diagnosis of PASD. Demographic data are presented in Table 1. The median age of patients was 38 years old, and only 5 of the 11 patients had a history of prior caesarean section. However, of the remaining 6 without a prior caesarean section, 4 had undergone prior gynecological surgery or conceived through assisted reproductive technology, both known risk factors for PASD [16].

Most of the patients in this series were treated with expectant management (7/11), meaning that the placenta was left untouched and in place. All patients remained admitted for one week with broad-spectrum antibiotics (Amoxicillin-Clavulanic acid 875 mg twice daily) and underwent serial ultrasounds once a week until resolution. Among these 7, only 3 resolved spontaneously, with an average time to resolution of 93.7 days (range: 47–125). All other patients needed an additional surgery for complete resolution (curettage n = 1/7, hysterectomy n = 3/7). Three patients were treated with the extirpative method, all of whom had low transverse hysterotomies. The placenta was completely extirpated in 1 out of 3. Two required additional hemostatic suturing to the placental bed, and 2 required placement of an intrauterine balloon tamponade and blood transfusions. One patient underwent the resective method, where the placenta is resected with a part of myometrium and the uterus is then reconstructed. Among all 11 patients, 5 had internal iliac artery balloons placed prior to surgery, and 8 underwent immediate postoperative uterine artery embolization (UAE) without any associated procedural complications. The extirpative method seemed to yield a higher estimated blood loss compared to expectant and resective techniques, with unadjusted total blood loss of 1667 mL (n = 3) vs. 857 mL (n = 7) and 700 mL (n = 1), respectively. Six patients received transfusion of packed red blood cells perioperatively (range: 1–5 units). There were no cases of massive transfusion, and no reported uses of cell-saver intraoperatively. When PASD was expected preoperatively, most surgeons opted for internal iliac artery balloons (5/7). All expectantly managed patients, as well as 1 patient who underwent extirpative management, underwent post-operative UAE (n = 8). This latter patient had only 50% of the placenta removed and was therefore managed similarly to the expectant management cohort. Two patients needed a repeat UAE for ongoing bleeding, both of whom ultimately required a hysterectomy for persistent bleeding. A third patient also underwent hysterectomy following expectant management because of infection. A total of 3 of the 11 cases underwent hysterectomy, with a mean interval of 78 days (range: 24–116 days). Of these three patients undergoing hysterectomy, estimated blood loss ranged from 300 ml to 800 ml, there were no peri- or post-operative complications apart from one intraoperative cystotomy (repaired), and no patient received blood transfusions. One patient underwent hysteroscopy remote from surgery, which showed Asherman’s syndrome. In this small cohort, there were no maternal or neonatal deaths reported. From our limited data available, no subsequent pregnancies were reported. Pathologic diagnosis of PASD was either confirmed or suspected for seven of the 10 patients delivered at our center (See Table 4).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort, we identified 11 patients treated conservatively for PASD, of whom two thirds were treated expectantly and the remainder underwent extirpative management. We found that only a small proportion of patients required a hysterectomy – yielding a total uterine preservation rate of 72.7% (8 of 11 cases). Bleeding and infection were the indications for interval hysterectomy.

These findings are in line with two of the largest retrospective cohort studies on the topic showing a uterine preservation rate of 78–80% (n= 193) [13, 17]. When comparing expectant and extirpative management in terms of blood loss, our data demonstrated that expectant management resulted in less blood loss than extirpative management or hysterectomy, consistent with previous literature [18].

The postoperative course of conservative management, especially expectant, can be a long and complicated one. Complete resorption can take months and complications can arise remote from delivery, with studies suggesting interval hysterectomy rates of 14% and need for other interventions like hysteroscopy occurring in 25% of cases [13, 19].

Adjunct therapy has also been studied in the hopes of increasing the rate of uterine preservation. In our series, no patients received methotrexate following delivery, which is in line with recent data showing no clear benefit, but important drug-related side effects [20]. Moreover, all of our patients undergoing expectant management underwent UAE, although the evidence is conflicting on the benefits of this procedure in this context [20]. Surgical ligation of the anterior division of the internal iliac artery at time of delivery has largely replaced the use of internal iliac artery balloons at our center in recent years.

Although uterine preservation is possible in certain cases, fertility may not always be possible. For example, the development of Asherman’s Syndrome, as seen in one of our patients, has been reported following expectant management of PASD and may contribute to subsequent infertility [9]. None of our patients had documented subsequent pregnancies within our center, although adequate follow up was a significant limitation in this review. Prior observational studies have shown that pregnancy after conservative management is possible, with some studies reporting rates ranging from 83% to 89% [10, 11, 20, 21].

The risk of recurrent PASD must also be discussed when considering a fertility-sparing approach, given the up to 20% rate of recurrence [19]. In light of this risk, caesarean-hysterectomy is often favoured when counselling patients with suspected PASD, given the definitive nature of this approach. However, in patients not prepared to lose their childbearing potential and for whom this approach may be distressing, a conservative approach can be considered bearing in mind a hysterectomy may ultimately still be required.

Adequate experience in the management of PASD and complex pelvic surgery is necessary to mitigate the high risk of bleeding, as is a multidisciplinary team of surgeons, interventional radiologists, and intensivists. Centralized referral for cases of PASD can help develop local and regional expertise. Conservative surgical approaches may be able to reduce the morbidity of caesarean-hysterectomy, including such techniques as local resection and the Triple P procedure. In our series, only one patient underwent a local resection, but evidence suggests less blood loss with this alternative technique than concomitant hysterectomy and less risk of emergency hysterectomy compared to expectant management [18, 20].

Choosing the optimal surgical strategy is dependent on numerous considerations: provider experience, local resources, patient preferences, and preoperative evaluation of PASD. In centers with limited support (ex. without surgical expertise, interventional radiology with accessible embolization services, or a robust blood bank), leaving the placenta in situ may represent the safest means of managing complex PASD, with subsequent transfer of the patient to a referral center and definitive management. Patients with strong desire for uterine preservation and future fertility may be counselled on conservative surgery (either extirpative or reconstructive) depending on the exact extent of the abnormal placentation (ex. significant bladder or parametrial involvement). As evidenced by the literature and the heterogeneous spectrum of this condition, individualized counselling and surgical planning are essential in managing PASD.

Strengths of this study include the management of these complex cases by a small, homogeneous group of gynecologic surgeons with experience with these cases. Despite the retrospective nature of this study, reporting of demographic data was fairly complete, including risk factors for PASD as well as nearly complete data on surgical parameters and adjunctive therapies such as UAE and blood transfusion.

The findings of this study must be interpreted within the limitations of the retrospective design. There was significant missing data regarding obstetrical history and prenatal imaging, as well as long-term follow up (including potential subsequent pregnancy) especially for patients returning to their community. Another limitation of this study was the inability to confirm the diagnosis of PASD in most cases of conservative management. Given that expectant management often precludes pathological examination, it can be difficult to confirm the diagnosis beyond clinical judgement, which studies have shown lacks precision [13]. Lastly, this review focused solely on patients with PASD not undergoing caesarean-hysterectomy, effectively excluding patients who may have had attempted extirpative management or other uterine-sparing technique intraoperatively that was immediately unsuccessful (i.e. abandoned due to bleeding or other intraoperative consideration), potentially limiting the generalizability of these findings.

Despite an increasing body of literature on conservative management of PASD, predicting outcomes and planning surgical interventions remains challenging. Future research aimed at identifying suitable candidates for conservative management and optimal surgical approach could aid clinicians and patients in the shared decision-making required for these complex cases.

Conclusions

Conservative management of PASD may be a safe alternative to hysterectomy; however, there remains a significant risk of reintervention, and comprehensive data regarding subsequent fertility outcomes remains sparse. Adequate patient counselling and collaboration with a multidisciplinary team is paramount to optimize outcomes.
