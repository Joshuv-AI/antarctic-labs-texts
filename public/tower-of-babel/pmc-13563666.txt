‘Putting profit over people’: Australian women’s perceptions of the economic and commercial systems shaping women’s health and social inequities
Authors: Simone McCarthy, Hannah Pitt, Samantha Thomas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe commercial determinants of health are important drivers of population health, although their influence on women's health and social inequities remain underexplored. This study explored Australian women’s perceptions of how economic and commercial factors influence women’s health and wellbeing and the actions needed to address these influences and advance gender equity. An online qualitative survey was completed by 506 Australian women aged 18 years and over. Data were analysed using a reflexive approach to thematic analysis. Women described health and social inequities as arising from interconnected economic, commercial, political, and gendered systems. Four themes were constructed from the data. First, women described financial barriers that limited access to essential goods, services, and healthcare. Second, they linked workplace practices, including inflexible work, insecure employment, and discrimination, to poorer health and wellbeing. Third, they identified a range of gendered, social, and environmental harms associated with industries they believed prioritized profit over health. Finally, participants called for stronger government regulation, greater corporate accountability, improved workplace protections, and more equitable access to healthcare and essential services. Findings highlight the need for public health responses that address the broader economic, commercial, and political systems shaping women's health and social inequities. Recognizing women as experts in their own lives can strengthen research, policy, and advocacy and support approaches that prioritize health and wellbeing over commercial interests.

Introduction

Globally, women do not have equal opportunities to achieve and maintain good health. Inequities are produced and sustained by systems of power, which create unequal access to social, economic, and political resources (Brysk 2022, Dahal et al. 2022, Eger et al. 2024, Ojong et al. 2024, Anjum and Aziz 2025, OECD 2025, UN Women 2025). The structural consequences of these systems include ongoing disparities in women’s access to healthcare and education, stable and adequately paid employment, economic resources, and political participation (Sen and Östlin 2008, Matheson et al. 2021, World Health Organization 2024, Simon and Hasan 2025, Fakkel et al. 2026). Inequities are also reflected in the unequal distribution of unpaid care, as well as precarious employment and work, financial insecurity, and exposure to a range of social and economic risks (World Health Organization 2024). Health and social inequities are intensified by intersecting forms of disadvantage and discrimination. These include those associated with cultural and linguistic background, age, disability, and socio-economic position (Rochon et al. 2021, Bull and Dalmer 2026, Ojong et al. 2024, Reinsberg et al. 2024, McCarthy et al. 2026a). Recent crises including COVID-19, geopolitical instability, and austerity measures, have further exposed and intensified how social, political, and economic systems shape women’s health and social outcomes (de Oliveira and Alloatti 2022, Hastings et al. 2023, Nhamo and Maoela 2025). Commercial actors are embedded within these systems. Through their political, economic, and market power, they can influence how resources, risks, and opportunities are distributed. They can also influence how conversations about women’s health are shaped, as well as the conditions in which women live and make decisions about their health. Understanding women’s health inequities therefore requires attention to social and economic conditions as well as the commercial interests, practices, and power relations that help produce and sustain them.

The Commercial Determinants of Health (CDoH) have been defined as ‘the systems, practices, and pathways through which commercial actors drive health and equity’ (Gilmore et al. 2023, p. 1195). As part of the social determinants of health, the influence of commercial actors that are harmful to health operate across multiple levels of influence (Tompson et al. 2026). They involve the ecosystems that support and protect ‘the activities of industries and political systems that prioritize private wealth of a few over public health for all, and neoliberal policies that have favoured decreased regulations’ (Thomas et al. 2024a, p. 1). Researchers increasingly recognize that a range of industries pose significant and disproportionate threats to women’s health and wellbeing (Hill and Friel 2020, McCarthy et al. 2023). Research has largely focused on gendered marketing and public relations tactics, showing how corporations that produce health-harming products, including tobacco, alcohol, and gambling products, use specific strategies to appeal to women (Brown-Johnson et al. 2014, Basch et al. 2016, Swahn et al. 2025, Atkinson et al. 2026) and align themselves with women’s social causes (Murray et al. 2025, McCarthy et al. 2026b).

Commercial actors also generate profit from women’s health needs and life stages, such as menstruation and menopause. They position products and services as necessary solutions to experiences that have often been stigmatized, neglected, or poorly supported (Wood et al. 2025). For example, research has found that commercial practices of menstrual product manufacturers have contributed to menstrual stigma, period poverty, and environmental harms (Brassart Olsen 2024). Similar processes are evident in the rapidly expanding menopause market, where commercial actors increasingly shape how menopause is understood and the products, treatments, and services presented to women as solutions (Wood et al. 2025). Weight-loss pharmaceuticals provide another example, with products increasingly marketed to women through narratives of empowerment, self-care, and freedom from stigma (Warin et al. 2024).

Commercial determinants can also operate beyond ‘products’ through shaping employment practices, income and housing conditions, information environments, and public policy (Anaf et al. 2024, Friel et al. 2024, Purnat et al. 2025, Barham et al. 2026, McCarthy et al. 2026a). Women may be differentially exposed to and affected by these processes, which compound existing gender based health and social inequities (Lee and Freudenberg 2022, McCarthy et al. 2023). McCarthy and colleagues (2023) identified production, consumption, and corporate social responsibility processes as key pathways through which commercial activities can negatively influence women’s working conditions, exposure to health risks, and broader social environments. Underpinning these processes are the broader power relations that shape the distribution of resources, opportunities, and influence within society. Friel and colleagues (2021) argue that health inequities are fundamentally linked to unequal distributions of power, with political, economic, and social structures influencing whose interests are prioritized, what policy responses are pursued, and how resources are allocated.

Longstanding gaps in women’s healthcare and research have also created opportunities for commercial actors to position products and services as solutions to unmet needs (Hawkes 2024, Wood et al. 2025, Nickel et al. 2026). Women are positioned by these actors as responsible consumers who must continually monitor their bodies, assess competing claims, manage risks, and purchase appropriate solutions (Prügl 2015, Corus et al. 2025). While these approaches are often framed through narratives of empowerment, autonomy and choice, they may shift attention away from the structural drivers of health inequities and place greater responsibility on individual women to manage their own health (Onculer and Onculer Yayalar 2025). Greater access to knowledge, products, technologies, and services may claim to support women’s agency and respond to needs that have historically been dismissed. But they can also be appropriated as a commercial narrative that equates autonomy with consumption and presents the market as the primary route to control over one’s health (Copp et al. 2024). However, relatively little is known about how women themselves understand and interpret these processes. Exploring women’s perspectives can provide situated insights into how economic and commercial systems are understood, how women perceive the relationships between commercial activity and health inequities, and where they locate responsibility for addressing harms.

This study addresses this gap by examining Australian women’s perceptions of the economic and commercial determinants of women’s health and wellbeing. The analysis is guided by two research questions:

How do Australian women perceive the influence of corporations, industries, and commercial practices on women’s health and wellbeing?

Materials and methods

Approach

The research involved an online qualitative panel survey with n = 506 Australian women (18+ years). It sought to explore Australian women’s perceptions of the impact of commercial practices on their health and wellbeing. One other paper has been published from this dataset relating to women’s views of corporate social responsibility strategies (Murray et al. 2025). There was no overlap in the qualitative responses analysed between the two papers beyond the reporting of shared demographic characteristics. The present study was informed by an interpretivist epistemological approach which recognizes that people understand health and commercial influence through their own experiences, social positions, and interactions with wider social and cultural environments (Braun and Clarke 2025).

Online surveys are increasingly used as an accessible and convenient way for individuals to participate in qualitative research (Braun et al. 2021, Thomas et al. 2024b). The benefits of this approach to data collection include reaching individuals who may be reluctant to participate in in-depth interviews (Neville et al. 2016), addressing power imbalances between researchers and the participant through the anonymous nature of data collection (Braun et al. 2021, Thomas et al. 2024b), the efficiency and rapid nature of data collection, and less burden in the time required to participate (Thomas et al. 2024b). They have been used in a range of studies investigating women’s experiences of commercial determinants including alcohol, gambling, and menopause (Pitt et al. 2023, McCarthy et al. 2024, 2026b, Thomas et al. 2025, Wood et al. 2025)

Positionality and reflexivity

Our research team has academic expertise in critical approaches to public health, health promotion, gender equity, and the commercial and political determinants of health. These disciplinary perspectives informed our interest in how commercial and political systems shape women’s health and wellbeing. While we are all women with a diverse range of ages, we recognize that we occupy privileged academic and professional positions which may have shaped how we recognized and interpreted women’s experiences of inequity and power. We recognize that the framing of the survey questions, including their focus on corporations and commercial influences, shaped the issues participants were invited to consider and may have foregrounded more critical accounts of commercial actors.

Team discussions were used to challenge our interpretations, consider alternative explanations, and consider participants’ perspectives. For example, early discussions identified that some responses reflected broader concerns about commercial and economic systems affecting the general population. Through reflexive discussion and returning to the data, we refined the analysis to focus on accounts that specifically described impacts on women's health and wellbeing, ensuring the themes remained aligned with the study aims. Some attention was paid to avoiding presenting women’s experiences as a homogeneous group. However, we acknowledge that our capacity to interpret experiences shaped by intersecting forms of marginalization was constrained by the perspectives and experiences of the team.

Sample and recruitment

Participants were recruited through Qualtrics, which facilitated recruitment via its partner panel providers. The study aimed to recruit a sample of approximately n = 500 women (18+ years of age) who lived across Australia, with soft quotas for diversity in age and geographical region. This is because women’s experiences of commercial influence and access to resources may differ across life stages and between metropolitan, regional, rural, and remote settings. The quotas were therefore intended to capture perspectives arising from a range of social and geographic contexts. The sample size was determined from other qualitative online panel studies that have been used previously to gain insight into women’s experiences of the CDoH (McCarthy et al. 2024, 2026a). This sample size was sufficient to provide enough information power to answer the study research questions due to the shorter insights provided by online qualitative surveys (Malterud et al. 2016). Qualtrics sent the study details to individuals who met the study inclusion criteria (identified as a woman, 18+ years of age) and who had signed up to a range of commercial panel databases. Individuals were provided a link to the survey, along with the Plain Language Statement. Participants were compensated in accordance with the policies of the panel provider. Consent was assumed by participants’ decision to begin and submit the survey. This study received ethical approval from Deakin University Human Research Ethics Committee (HEAG-H 101_2024).

Data collection

Data collection took place between July and August 2024. To test the suitability of the survey questions and identify any technical difficulties, the survey was piloted with 22 women. Minimal modifications were made after the pilot period, and pilot responses were removed from the final sample. The full survey was launched, and after reviewing the responses, a final sample of 506 women was included. Participants were asked a range of socio-demographic questions (age, employment, education, household composition). This paper focused on questions related to two survey themes: (i) The impact of commercial determinants on women’s health and equity, in which participants were asked to reflect on the negative impacts of corporations on women’s health and wellbeing, including the ways corporations, products, and marketing practices may influence health and (ii) recommendations for addressing the impact of commercial determinants, which explored the role of governments and corporations in protecting women’s health. The specific questions asked are provided in Supplementary File S1.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were analysed using SPSS software. Open text responses were analysed using a reflexive approach to thematic analysis, following Braun and Clarke’s six-phased iterative approach (Braun and Clarke 2021). Microsoft Excel was used to support the systematic management of the large qualitative dataset and enable comparison across responses and questions. Responses to the open-ended questions were often about one or two sentences in length. Analysis drew on participants’ responses across multiple open-ended questions, enabling patterns to be identified across the dataset. The process of constructing themes from the data was iterative, moving between the different steps. During the familiarization step, responses to the open-ended questions relevant to the research aims were read thoroughly to build an overall understanding of the dataset. Analysis was guided by the study aims and research questions, with attention to how participants described experiences and issues relevant to the economic and commercial determinants of women's health and wellbeing. Coding was then conducted across all relevant responses, with codes used as a mechanism for organizing and categorizing key ideas identified in the data. Generating initial themes involved grouping related codes and examining how patterns were expressed across the dataset. Themes were reviewed by returning to the dataset to check their relevance and fit across responses, and to ensure they reflected the range of participant perspectives. Defining and naming themes involved refining theme boundaries and clearly describing the key ideas represented within each theme. The final phase, reporting, involved selecting illustrative extracts and presenting themes grounded in participants’ accounts. Analysis was reflexive throughout, supported by regular team discussions to strengthen interpretation and analytic rigour.

Results

General characteristics

A comprehensive socio-demographic table of the n = 506 study participants has been reported in detail elsewhere (Murray et al. 2025) and is provided in Supplementary File S2. Participants were aged between 18 and 90 years old (M = 49.09, standard deviation = 17.45), and most resided in Australia’s three most populous states of New South Wales (n = 168, 33.2%), Victoria (n = 131, 25.9%), and Queensland (n = 104, 20.6%). Most participants lived in metropolitan areas (n = 317, 62.6%), and two-thirds (n = 335, 66.2%) had some form of higher education such as a bachelor’s or postgraduate degree, certificate, or diploma. Just under a third (n = 152, 30%) were working full time, and an additional 21.5% (n = 109) were working part time or casually. The most common household compositions were women living as a couple with no dependent children (n = 141, 27.9%), single and living alone (n = 123, 24.3%), and living as a couple with dependent children (n = 99, 19.6%). Other household arrangements included living with parents, single-parent households, and sharing with housemates.

Four themes were constructed from the data.

Theme 1: The impact of corporate practices on unequal access to essential goods and services

When responding to broad questions about corporate practices, participants most commonly referred to the increasing cost of essential goods and services. These accounts positioned affordability as a key pathway through which corporations influenced the health and wellbeing of communities, with particular implications for women’s health and widening existing health inequities. Participants frequently described corporations, including supermarkets, energy providers, and health care services as contributing to rising costs that made essential goods and services increasingly unaffordable. Participants referred to ‘raising costs’, ‘making everything expensive’, ‘increased cost of services’, and the broader ‘cost of living’ as major ways corporations affected community health and wellbeing. Some participants believed that corporations prioritized profits over the affordability and accessibility of essential goods and services. They described companies as contributing to the increased price of healthcare and everyday necessities, placing growing financial pressure on individuals and families.

‘They may put profits before availability, affordability and access.’—35-year-old woman, metropolitan New South Wales.

‘Capitalistic creatures that only exist to make profit, regardless of the consequences.’—24-year-old woman, metropolitan Victoria.

Participants’ accounts also demonstrated that affordability shaped whether people could meet their basic health needs. They perceived that rising costs could lead people to delay or forgo medical appointments, preventive health screens, and medicines, potentially allowing health problems to worsen. For example, some commented that corporations were ‘overpricing critical drugs’ and charging ‘high prices for procedures’. One 71-year-old woman wrote that programmes designed to improve people's health were ‘expensive or not tailored to their target communities’ and that ‘many people cannot access or afford these programs’. Others described hospital systems that left them facing long ‘wait lists’ and limited access to doctors, particularly in regional areas. A few participants explained that the rising costs of healthcare meant people ‘can't afford to go to Drs’ or delayed routine check-ups. Although some responses referred to pressures spanning both public and private health systems, participants commonly connected these experiences to the growing commercialization and perceived profit orientation of healthcare.

These barriers were perceived to be especially significant for women who stated that they had limited income, ongoing chronic healthcare needs, caring responsibilities, or restricted access to services in rural and regional communities. For some participants, the increasing cost of healthcare and essential services was perceived to limit access to basic health needs, particularly among those experiencing financial disadvantage.

‘Some are so expensive that not everyone can afford those expensive costs and therefore miss out on a check up which can prove deadly in some cases.’—68-year-old woman, metropolitan Victoria.

‘I think greedy corporations make certain healthcare almost completely inaccessible.’—20-year-old woman, metropolitan Queensland.

The effects described by participants were often cumulative and contributed to broader financial insecurity. The increasing costs across healthcare, food, housing, and other essential services reduced household capacity to absorb additional expenses and created difficult choices between competing needs. Respondents described families struggling to ‘pay rent and buy groceries’, while others commented that ‘demands for higher prices’ from companies meant households did not have enough money to purchase essential goods. Some participants discussed the price of groceries and compared the affordability of ‘unhealthy’ food with healthier alternatives - ‘health foods are too pricey’. Supermarkets were also frequently identified as contributing to the cost-of-living crisis:

‘Putting profit over people! Supermarkets currently being a predominant factor in the cost of living crisis.’—35-year-old woman, metropolitan Victoria.

‘All corporations care about is profit… Families are struggling to pay rent and buy groceries, let alone eat three meals a day or do things they enjoy in life. Small business have been destroyed due to how far apart everything is nowadays. We have become too reliant on bigger corporations to provide for us.’—18-year-old woman, rural/remote Queensland.

Theme 2: Gendered workplace stress, poor conditions, and lack of support

Participants frequently identified employment and workplace practices as important pathways through which commercial actors affected women’s health and wellbeing. Participants perceived that the pursuit of productivity and profit often came at the expense of safe, equitable, and supportive working conditions. Participants (particularly those with dependent children) frequently described workplace cultures that failed to recognize or accommodate their caring responsibilities. For example, they referred to a ‘lack of workplace flexibility for working parents’ and described dismissive cultures in which women seeking flexible arrangements were viewed as ‘lazy or not career focused’. Others described a lack of flexibility when responding to the ‘inevitable issues’ that arose from caring for their children, while some felt employers failed to recognize women’s health needs by providing inadequate support for menstruation, pregnancy, and parenting. This intensified the pressure experienced by women attempting to combine paid employment with a disproportionate share of unpaid care and domestic work.

‘Women being expected to work full time and still doing all the childcare, housework and cooking.’—68-year-old woman, regional Queensland.

‘The stresses of being in the workforce but still being perfect at keeping the home and motherhood in control.’—79-year-old woman, metro Victoria.

‘Lack of workplace flexibility for working parents.’—59-year-old woman, metro New South Wales.

Participants also described workplace cultures that prioritized productivity, performance and financial outcomes over employee health. Participants referred to excessive workloads, inadequate staffing, long working hours, unrealistic expectations and little or no ‘work life balance’. Some perceived that employers expected workers to take on heavier workloads, work overtime, or continue working despite exhaustion. Some participants linked inflexible working hours to stress, fatigue, difficulties attending medical appointments, and pressures extending into family life.

‘Long hours with little return. This leads to people either not having enough, or working an extra job, leading to stress and tension.’—57-year-old woman, metropolitan Queensland.

Employment insecurity and casualized workforces further shaped women’s experiences of workplace stress. Participants from regional and rural areas in particular discussed insecure employment and limited local employment opportunities in the area.

‘They don’t care about the health of their workers just the bottom line and people stress because they are worried if they don’t perform they will lose their jobs.’—64-year-old woman, regional New South Wales.

Beyond workload and flexibility, a small number of participants described how organizational cultures that tolerated or inadequately addressed bullying, discrimination, sexual harassment and unequal treatment could be impact women’s health and wellbeing. These concerns were most commonly raised by older women who were retired. This may reflect their longer working histories and experiences of employment during periods when workplace protections and expectations around gender equality differed. These participants described workplaces where women were treated as inferior, bullied by colleagues, paid less than men for equivalent work, or discriminated against because of pregnancy or motherhood.

‘Sexual harassment in the workplace.’—76-year-old woman, rural/remote Victoria.

‘Women in the workplace treated inferior. Women being bullied by men. Women being paid less for same job description. Women being discriminated against. Eg—mother, colour, race.’—62-year-old woman, regional New South Wales.

Theme 3: Gendered, social, and environmental consequences of harmful industries

Participants described corporations as shaping harmful gender norms. Some participants highlighted the influence of the media, beauty, wellness, social media, and weight-loss industries in shaping harmful norms and expectations. These industries were described as promoting unrealistic and unattainable appearance standards and creating pressure to conform to narrow ideals of beauty, youth, and femininity. Participants referred to the ‘false beliefs of what a woman should be’ and ‘unattainable perfection’, with some stating that beauty companies implied women ‘need make up to be young and flawless’. Participants perceived that corporations created or intensified women’s insecurities and then marketed products as solutions. These practices were seen as contributing to distorted body image and lower self-esteem among women and girls.

‘The impacts can range from low self-esteem to diseases such as Anorexia and other disorders. Girls can become obsessed about body image.’—51-year-old woman, rural/remote South Australia.

Participants described the promotion of unnecessary, ineffective or costly products, including dietary and weight-loss products, ‘beauty products that don’t work’, and ‘over the counter vitamins and minerals that are costly and don’t always work’. These concerns were commonly raised by women aged under 30 years, potentially reflecting the prominence of beauty, weight-loss, and wellness products marketed to younger women. The harms associated with these industries were described by one participant as being psychological, physical, and financial:

‘Wellness/beauty industries pushing products that we don’t need, harms financially and mentally, sometimes even physically.’—23-year-old woman, metropolitan South Australia.

Social media platforms were perceived to amplify these pressures through the repeated circulation of idealized representations of women. Some participants were concerned that social media facilitated the spread of health misinformation, which was perceived to ‘fear monger’ and generate confusion and distrust. Social media platforms contributed to information environments in which commercial messages, gendered expectations, and misinformation could be rapidly circulated and reinforced.

‘Social media platforms can be detrimental to the wellbeing of communities when they allow misinformation to be posted and spread.’—57-year-old woman, metropolitan Victoria.

A small number of participants identified the environmental impacts of corporations as having implications for the health of women and broader communities. These impacts were more acutely described by women living outside metropolitan areas, including those in regional, rural, and remote communities. These participants described how air pollution, water contamination, and carbon emissions contributed to cancers, reproductive health problems, and adverse pregnancy outcomes.

‘Carbon emissions create less breathable air for people around the world, and seems to be affecting pregnant women the most.’—18-year-old woman, rural/remote Queensland.

When prompted to reflect specifically on industries that may harm women—including examples from media coverage of fossil fuel, alcohol and tobacco industries—participants identified additional pathways through which harmful commodities affected women’s health and wellbeing. Participants described both direct harms arising from the use of these products and indirect or ‘second-hand’ harms experienced through another person’s consumption. These included physical illness and addiction, as well as violence, financial insecurity and household instability. For example, participants perceived alcohol use as escalating existing risks of aggression and violence, with participants referring to alcohol as ‘fuelling men’s anger’ or contributing to a mindset in which some men believed they could control women. Gambling was described as diverting household income and leaving women in financially vulnerable or abusive situations. These accounts demonstrated how the consequences of harmful commodities could be distinctly gendered. Participants perceived that women could experience serious threats to their health, safety, and economic security even when they were not the primary consumers of the product.

‘The gambling industry both impacts women who gamble, but more typically men who gamble family income and leave women in vulnerable financially abusive situations.’—30-year-old woman, metropolitan Queensland.

‘Increased domestic violence due to alcohol abuse.’—18-year-old woman, regional Victoria.

Tobacco and vaping industries were similarly criticized for impacting upon women’s health. Participants pointed to the physical consequences of tobacco use, including lung cancer and broader ‘health danger’, and drew parallels between the historical promotion of smoking and the contemporary growth of vaping. These responses suggested that participants viewed the harms associated with tobacco and vaping as important issues affecting women's health, despite its broader population impacts.

‘Tobacco - many women still smoke and display the effects on their bodies as a result… Smoking was promoted many decades ago causing many to become addicted - nowadays we have the vaping issues growing and causing a new “tobacco” epidemic.’—71-year-old woman, metropolitan Western Australia.

‘Health impacts such as respiratory disease, impacts on fertility or for women of childbearing age if they smoke or vape the health risk to foetus or baby.’—68-year-old woman, regional Western Australia.

Theme 4: Accountability and challenging corporate power

Participants articulated a clear and often sophisticated understanding of the mechanisms required to hold corporations accountable for their broad impact on health and social wellbeing. Many participants believed accountability required corporations to face meaningful consequences for harmful conduct. Participants argued that companies should be ‘heavily fined’, required to compensate communities for harms caused by their products, and subjected to ethical standards that were applied ‘more stringently’. Importantly, participants emphasized that financial penalties needed to be substantial enough to influence corporate behaviour rather than being absorbed as a routine cost of doing business. Some also argued that governments should prevent harmful practices before they occurred, rather than relying solely on penalties or compensation after harm had been established.

‘Laws need to be passed to prevent these corporations from marketing harmful products in the first place, and they should face fines for non-compliance. They should also pay for damages that are proven to be caused by their products.’—51-year-old woman, rural/remote South Australia.

‘Governments must enact legislation to enable them to fine those companies for their health impacts. And this must be a substantial fine.’—73-year-old woman, metropolitan New South Wales.

Transparency and public scrutiny were also seen as important mechanisms for accountability. Participants suggested that corporations engaging in harmful conduct should be ‘called out’ or ‘named and shamed’ and that public pressure could ‘shame them into doing better’. These responses reflected a desire to make harmful corporate practices more visible and to challenge companies’ ability to present themselves as socially responsible while contributing to health, social or environmental harms.

Participants also identified a broad set of expectations for government intervention. Many believed that effective regulation required independent external oversight, with participants calling for ‘an independent body to scrutinise these companies’ rather than relying on corporate self-regulation. Participants argued that governments needed to establish clear standards, monitor corporate conduct, and actively enforce compliance. Regulation was perceived to have limited value when authorities did not ‘actually enforce the regulations’ or allowed corporations to operate without meaningful consequences.

‘Incorporate new requirements in advertising laws, create a body to oversee this to ensure it gets the attention it needs.’—38-year-old woman, rural/remote New South Wales.

Participants called for policies that protected people from harmful products and marketing, improved the affordability of essential goods and services, supported fair and safe working conditions, and established expectations relating to sustainability, inclusivity, and gender equity. These responses positioned government as having a responsibility to prevent harm and protect community wellbeing, particularly where individuals had limited power to challenge large corporations themselves. Participants also recognized, however, that corporate accountability was shaped by substantial imbalances in political and economic power. Some questioned whether governments were sufficiently independent from the industries they were expected to regulate. Participants referred to financial relationships between governments and businesses, with one woman observing that:

‘Governments make money off companies, so it is a two-edge sword.’—69-year-old woman, metropolitan New South Wales.

Others identified lobbying as a barrier to stronger regulation and expressed scepticism about whether governments would prioritize public health when it conflicted with powerful commercial interests.

‘The government is in league with these companies so it’s difficult.’—55-year-old woman, regional New South Wales.

‘More regulation is almost impossible due to lobbying.’—28-year-old woman, metropolitan New South Wales.

Participants frequently argued that governments should place community health and wellbeing above corporate profit. They called for policies that protected populations experiencing vulnerability and required corporations to consider the consequences of their practices for women and communities. This included expectations that governments would set and enforce standards relating to ethical behaviour, affordability, workplace conditions, environmental sustainability, and gender equity.

‘The Government should encourage industries to consider women’s health and needs before worrying about commercial profit gouging.’—72-year-old woman, metropolitan South Australia.

However, not all participants supported stronger government intervention. A small number questioned whether regulation risked creating a ‘nanny state’ or suggested that responsibility ultimately remained ‘up to individuals’. This introduced a tension between collective protections and individual responsibility. While most participants regarded government intervention as necessary to address power imbalances and prevent harm, others expressed concern about the appropriate limits of regulation.

‘Not sure they can, or it becomes too much of a nanny state.’—38-year-old woman, rural/remote New South Wales.

Discussion

This study aimed to explore how Australian women understand the influence of commercial actors and practices on women’s health and wellbeing, and the actions they believe are needed to address these determinants and advance gender equity. Figure 1 provides a model of the constructed themes and key discussion points from the research. The findings raise three points for discussion in relation to the research questions.

Australian women's perceptions of the economic and commercial systems shaping women's health and social inequities.

First, this study provides further evidence that the CDoH should be understood as systems of power rather than as a collection of isolated products, industries or corporate practices. Participants described commercial influence as embedded within the structures and social contexts that shaped their everyday lives. This included their access to affordable goods, essential services, and healthcare, their experiences of inequitable workplaces, and the social expectations and value attached to women’s roles and (unpaid) labour. These findings align with scholarship positioning health inequities as consequences of unequal distributions of political, economic and social power (Brysk 2022, Dahal et al. 2022, Eger et al. 2024, Ojong et al. 2024, Anjum and Aziz 2025, OECD 2025, UN Women 2025). They demonstrate how commercial actors may exercise structural power through their economic importance and control over resources and employment, as well as discursive power through their capacity to shape how women’s needs, social problems, and possible solutions are understood (Nickel et al. 2026). Understanding these processes is particularly important in women’s health given that commercial power interacts with pre-existing gendered inequalities (Phillips 2017, Hill and Friel 2020). Women continue to experience unequal access to secure and adequately paid employment, economic resources, healthcare, and political participation, while carrying a disproportionate share of unpaid care and domestic work (Ervin et al. 2023, World Health Organization 2024). Participants’ accounts suggest that the economic and commercial systems they encounter, especially as they relate to food systems, healthcare, and employment, may deepen these inequities. The commercial practices identified by participants align with several domains described in existing CDoH frameworks, particularly labour and employment practices, marketing practices, and political practices (Gilmore et al. 2023). Participants described how these practices interacted to shape women's access to essential goods and services, workplace experiences, and everyday social environments, rather than experiencing them as separate categories of commercial influence. In contrast, participants rarely referred to scientific practices, financial practices, or reputational management strategies, suggesting that women primarily understood commercial determinants through the tangible ways corporations influenced their daily lives. These findings complement existing conceptual frameworks by demonstrating which aspects of commercial power are most visible and meaningful to women, while highlighting the value of incorporating experiential perspectives alongside analyses of upstream commercial and political processes. This suggests that moving forward, CDoH researchers should pay increased attention to aspects of commercial systems that are often studied separately. Researchers should examine commercial practices that extend beyond factors that encourage the consumption of harmful products, towards the commercial organization of essential goods, services, employment, and information (Lacy-Nichols et al. 2023, McCarthy et al. 2026a).

Second, the findings challenge commercial narratives that equate women’s empowerment with individual responsibility and consumption. Research increasingly shows that commercial actors position women as responsible for monitoring their own health, identifying risks, interpreting complex information and purchasing appropriate products or services (Prügl 2015, Corus et al. 2025). Although these expectations are often presented through the language of empowerment, autonomy, and informed choice (Hill and Friel 2020, Thomas et al. 2025, Nickel et al. 2026), they can obscure the economic and commercial conditions that shape women’s capacity to act. When health is presented primarily as an outcome of personal choice, insufficient attention is given to the systems that shape those choices. Women may be encouraged to become more health literate, resilient, or financially capable without equivalent scrutiny of the corporations that determine prices, organize working conditions, shape digital environments, and market potentially harmful products. Such approaches risk normalizing the conditions that produce harm and implying that women who experience poor outcomes have failed to manage themselves effectively.

Third, participants’ calls for stronger accountability highlighted their perception that existing regulatory and governance arrangements were insufficient to protect women's health and wellbeing from harmful commercial practices. Building on these findings, we argue that the substantial imbalance in resources between corporations and individuals reinforces the need for government action. Governments have a responsibility to establish the conditions under which commercial actors operate and to ensure that private activity does not undermine health, equity, or human rights. A gender based regulatory approach must address the multiple pathways through which commercial systems influence women’s lives. This includes policies relating to the affordability and accessibility of essential goods and services; employment security and flexibility; workplace discrimination and harassment; environmental protection; and access to healthcare. Such an approach requires a Health in All Policies framework (Greer et al. 2022), with coordinated action across a range of areas of government that impact women’s everyday lives. Addressing the CDoH therefore requires more than supporting women to navigate markets or make informed choices. It means confronting the conditions and power structures that shape those choices and ensuring that women’s health and collective wellbeing take precedence over commercial interests.

Finally, our findings highlight the value of meaningfully involving women in CDoH research and policy. Participants’ accounts demonstrated how experiential knowledge can reveal harms overlooked by narrowly focused approaches, including the time required to navigate complex systems, the pressures of combining paid and unpaid work, delayed access to care, the household effects of another person’s product use, and the cumulative burden of multiple commercial pressures. Participants connected experiences across domains that are often researched and governed separately and identified where responsibility for change should lie. These findings reinforce the importance of recognizing women as knowledgeable contributors to debates about health, equity, and corporate power. However, women must not be treated as a homogeneous group. Commercial determinants are experienced through intersecting systems of advantage and disadvantage, with women's socio-economic position, age, disability, cultural and linguistic background, migration status, and geography shaping both their exposure to commercial harms and their capacity to respond to them (Hossain et al. 2025). Women with fewer financial resources, greater caring responsibilities, insecure employment, or limited access to services may have less capacity to avoid harmful commercial environments or absorb their consequences. Future research should therefore examine how economic and commercial systems may widen inequities among different groups of women.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Data were collected using an online qualitative survey, which enabled participation from women across Australia but did not allow researchers to probe responses or explore issues in greater depth. The sample included a relatively high proportion of women with tertiary education. These characteristics may have influenced the perspectives represented in the dataset, including participants’ awareness of, and ability to articulate, structural and commercial influences on women's health. The findings reflect the accounts of those who participated and should not be interpreted as representing the views of all Australian women. The study explored women's perceptions of the economic and CDoH and did not seek to verify the claims participants made about corporations, industries, or commercial practices. Finally, although demographic information was collected, the survey did not include some characteristics that may shape experiences of the CDoH, such as income, sexuality, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander status, or disability. In addition, while some differences between groups of women were identified, the study was not designed to systematically examine differences in experiences across demographic groups. Future research should explore these issues in greater depth and across a broader range of intersecting social and structural positions.

Conclusion

This study examined how Australian women understand the economic and CDoH and the actions they believe are needed to advance women’s health and gender equity. The findings contribute to a growing body of research that conceptualizes the CDoH as systems of power and demonstrate the value of incorporating women’s lived experiences into this field. Recognizing women as experts in the conditions that shape their health can strengthen future research, policy, and advocacy, while supporting more equitable approaches to governing commercial systems in ways that prioritize health and wellbeing over commercial interests.
