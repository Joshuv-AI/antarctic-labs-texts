Sleep quality in Spanish adults: an age- and sex-stratified cross-sectional study
Authors: César Jiménez-Vaquero, Jesús Gonzalez-Sanchez, Rosario Alonso-Dominguez, Irene A. Garcia-Yu, Natalia Sanchez-Aguadero, Andrea Crespo-Sedano, María I. Rihuete-Galve, Jose I. Recio-Rodriguez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract Poor sleep quality is associated with obesity, cardiovascular disease, cognitive impairment, increased hospitalization and mortality. Combining actigraphy with questionnaires effectively evaluates sleep. Age and sex shape both subjective sleep quality and objective actigraphic metrics; however, stratified estimates combining both approaches in Spanish adults are scarce. The aim of this study was to analyse the role of age and sex on sleep quality, in an age–sex stratified random sample of the general Spanish population. For this, it was carried out a cross-sectional descriptive study carried out in Salamanca and Ávila (Spain). The study population included a sample of 500 people aged 25–65 years selected by stratified random sampling by age and sex. Sleep quality was analysed using actigraphy and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index-PSQI. Sociodemographic and clinical variables were also studied. The results were that men obtained a lower mean PSQI score than women. By age group, the poorer subjective sleep quality corresponded to the 25-years group, whereas the 45-year group had the lowest PSQI scores. Actigraphy showed higher efficiency in women. Men had more WASO, more awakenings and higher fragmentation. Midlife groups (35–45 years) showed lower efficiency and higher WASO. Trial registration number: NCT05324267. Introduction

The term ‘healthy sleep’ is a complex concept that encompasses both the quality and quantity of sleep, which can be influenced by the presence of disorders such as alterations in the normal sleep pattern, obstructive sleep apnoea, daytime sleepiness, insomnia or parasomnias, among others1. But beyond the simple absence of sleep disorders, “healthy sleep” is a positive, multidimensional construct encompassing satisfaction/quality, alertness/sleepiness, timing, efficiency and duration, which frames sleep as a health asset at the individual and population level2. Moreover, lifestyle behaviours (including physical activity, diet quality, smoking, alcohol consumption, sedentary time and weight status) are strongly associated with better sleep health in population studies, underscoring the relevance of modifiable factors in defining and promoting healthy sleep3,4.

Among the many existing sleep disorders, insomnia, characterised by a shortening of total sleep time, is the most prevalent5. In the Spanish population, 43.4% of the population has symptoms of insomnia, and 14.0% has an established chronic insomnia disorder, being more prevalent in women (14.6%) than in men (13.4%) and increasing with age6. Nie Q et al., in turn, analysed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey and concluded that the prevalence of sleep disorders was 30.6% in the general adult US population3, rising to 40.5% during the COVID-19 pandemic4.Considering the influence of sleep on physical and mental health, the National Sleep Foundation recommends 7–9 h of sleep per day for the adult population7.

On the other hand, the term ‘sleep quality’ is described in terms of different factors, such as sleep architecture, time taken to fall asleep, nocturnal micro-awakenings, periods of wakefulness during sleep, and total sleep duration8.

Sleep quality encompasses subjective perceptions and objective continuity metrics and is linked to cardiometabolic and neurocognitive outcomes. Current evidence shows that poor sleep quality is associated with a variety of physical health problems, such as obesity9,10, diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular disease11, including cardiac remodelling and impaired ventricular function12. In addition, sleep quality is associated with psychological factors, such as reduced attention13, cognitive impairment14–16, impaired executive functions17, anxiety13,18 and depression16,19. Overall, poor sleep quality can increase the risk of all-cause mortality (relative risk 1.50 [1.02–2.18], p = 0.042)20. In this clinical context, framing sleep quality as both a subjective experience and an objective pattern of continuity clarifies its public-health relevance.

Across adulthood, age is associated with changes in sleep continuity and timing, with midlife often showing increased fragmentation and later life showing shorter duration. With ageing, a gradual decline in sleep quality, characterised by a reduction in sleep time and an increase in nocturnal awakenings and shallow sleep and influenced by individual characteristics and lifestyles, especially physical activity, is observed21,22. It is estimated that 50–70% of older people suffer from any sleep disorder, and up to 40% of adults over 60 years of age report difficulty falling asleep or staying asleep, which can accelerate the development of cognitive impairment and increase the risk of Alzheimer’s disease16.

Poorer sleep quality, together with a greater presence of negative emotions, is associated with poorer subjective perception in older adults with multimorbidity, with perceived social support being a positive influence that can help considerably to compensate for this situation23.

On the other hand, it has been shown that poor sleep quality increases the consumption of health resources, specifically, the presence of one or more symptoms of insomnia increases the probability of hospital admission by 34.1% and home care by 14.3% in older adults24.

When studying the relationship between biological sex and sleep quality, most of the literature consulted suggests that women experience poorer sleep quality25–27, although the results are disparate when analysing this relationship over the years. Thus, Mells J. et al. found that women have poorer sleep quality at any stage of life28, while Onj J. et al. reported that these differences are only significant for the 21–65 age group26. This fact, although not known in depth, could be determined by the role that oestrogens and progesterone play on sleep25, although the influence of exogenous administration of these hormones (in the form of contraceptives, replacement hormones, etc.) on sleep quality in the female population remains unclear29. Some authors suggest that other health-related variables, such as fibromyalgia, chronic pain and overactive bladder, could explain these differences28.

Although in the past it was considered that commercial accelerometers, such as actigraphy, could overestimate or underestimate actual sleep levels22, in 2017 Kang S et al. demonstrated that these portable devices provided comparable results to polysomnography (PSG) in sleep monitoring in healthy populations30. In fact, the use of actigraphy was included in the diagnostic criteria of the International Classification of Sleep Disorders - Third Edition (ICSD-3) for several circadian sleep-wake rhythm disorders, along with sleep logs31,32. The latter, considered subjective methods of sleep quality assessment, are moderately related to objective methods, such as actigraphy and PSG33, which are considered the gold standard in terms of sensitivity and specificity. Specifically, subjective methods have a sensitivity of 73–97.6.6% and a specificity of 50–96%, with sleep diaries being the most accurate, as they are less influenced by the patient’s memory, although sleep questionnaires provide a more global assessment of the subjective perception of sleep. Actigraphy provide sensitivity values of 86–98%, although their specificity is reduced to 20–54% compared to PSG31. Therefore, the concomitant use of actigraphy in combination with subjective methods could be a powerful diagnostic and analytical tool in the study of certain sleep disorders32. Because subjective and objective tools tap partially distinct constructs, using both strengthens inference34.

Regarding sleep duration, various studies have analyzed its association with different sociodemographic and lifestyle variables. However, not many studies have analysed this relationship in terms of sleep quality and not just quantity. To the best of our knowledge, this study is pioneering in analysing multiple variables and their relationship with sleep quality, measured by objective and self-reported methods, which will allow a more accurate understanding of the impact of each variable on sleep independently and will contribute to improving current approaches to sleep disorders.

The aim of this study was to analyse the role of age and sex on sleep quality, both self-perceived and measured by actigraphy in a randomised sample of the general Spanish population aged 25–65 years, stratified by age and sex.

Methods

Design and setting

This was a cross-sectional study carried out in two cities in central Spain: Salamanca and Ávila. The study was conducted between May 2022 and April 2024 and was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT05324267). The study protocol has been previously published35.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated based on the expected score on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), taking as a reference the results obtained by Gómez-García et al. from a sample of Spanish nurses, in which they reported a mean ± standard deviation (SD) of 6.8 ± 3.38 points on the PSQI36. From these parameters, it was determined that a random sample of 500 individuals would be adequate to estimate the population mean with a confidence interval of 95% and a precision of ± 0.32 units, assuming a SD close to 3.38. This reference was additionally supported by the age profile of the Spanish nursing workforce, which is predominantly 25–65 years and thus aligns with the five prespecified age strata in our sampling design (25, 35, 45, 55, and 65 years). Because the SD is the parameter governing precision, and given that a workforce with irregular/rotating schedules may display heterogeneous sleep patterns, the SD reported by Gómez-García et al. provided a conservative estimate of variability for the calculation, minimizing the risk of underestimating dispersion in the target population. A loss to follow-up rate of 15% was also estimated.

Study population and recruitment

Participants were selected using a random stratified sampling strategy by age and sex. A uniform allocation was applied, with an equal number of participants in each stratum, regardless of their actual proportion in the Spanish population. The study population included a sample of 500 people aged 25–65 years from the cities of Salamanca and Ávila (Spain). The inclusion of participants was done by stratified random sampling by age and sex groups, 500 subjects were selected from the health card database of the Castilla y León Health Service (SACYL), comprising 100 individuals (50 women and 50 men) in each of the age strata, which were defined a priori as exact cohorts (25, 35, 45, 55, and 65 years), and only individuals belonging to these exact ages at the time of recruitment were eligible for inclusion. Inclusion criteria

Individuals who at the time of sampling were aged 25, 35, 45, 55 or 65 years old, residents of the cities of Salamanca or Ávila, who agreed to participate in the study and did not meet any of the exclusion criteria.

Exclusion criteria

Subjects with terminal illnesses, those unable to travel to health centres for the corresponding examinations, and pregnant women were excluded.

Study variables

Sleep assessment

Sleep assessment by actigraphy

The accelerometer model used was the ActiGraph® GT3X+ (ActiGraph®, Pensacola, FL, USA). This system has been previously validated and is considered a reliable method for estimating sleep characteristics in both adult and adolescent populations37. The device was placed on the non-dominant wrist of the participants during the visit to the research centre and they were asked to wear it for the next 5 days. Once returned, data were downloaded and subsequently analysed using Actilife 6 software (Version 6.13.15, ActiGraph®, Pensacola, FL, USA). The Cole-Kripke® Sleep Scoring Algorithm38 was used for the analysis of the actigraphy data, while the Tudor-Locke method39 was used for automatic sleep period detection, which automatically detects the onset of a sleep period by requiring 5 consecutive epochs marked as sleep. For this reason, sleep periods that were automatically detected with Tudor-Locke always show a latency of zero, because the sleep onset times and bedtime of sleep periods detected by Tudor-Locke will always coincide40. Therefore, the latency variable has not been included among the actigraphy-derived variables.

Sleep assessment using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)42: It is a Spanish validated and highly reliable instrument specifically designed to measure sleep quality. It is composed of 19 questions, creating 7 main components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication and past daytime dysfunction. Each section is scored from 0 (no difficulty) to 3 (severe difficulty). Subsequently, the scores of the 7 items are summed, resulting in a total value scale from 0 to 21 points. A higher overall score on this questionnaire indicates a poorer quality of sleep. In general, a score > 5 is indicative of poor sleep quality.

Morbidity assessment

The presence of comorbidities was collected using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI)43. This index, developed in 1987, is a common tool in clinical studies to predict long-term mortality risk in patients with multiple comorbidities. The CCI assesses the presence of 19 chronic diseases: myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, peripheral arterial disease, cerebrovascular disease, dementia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, connective tissue disease, gastroduodenal ulcer, mild liver disease, diabetes, hemiplegia, moderate/severe renal failure, diabetes with organ damage, any tumour within the past 5 years, lymphoma, leukaemia, moderate/severe liver disease, metastatic tumour and/or AIDS. Each disease is assigned scores from 1 to 6 points, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 37 points. The index score assigned to each comorbidity was based on the relative risk (RR) associated to it: RR [0–1.2.2), 0 points; RR [1.2–1.5), 1 point; RR [1.5–2.5), 2 points; RR [2.5–3.5), 3 points; RR ≥ 3.5 (metastatic solid tumour and AIDS), 6 points42. In addition, after the age of 50 years, the total score is adjusted by adding one point for each decade of life43. The final score of the questionnaire allows grading comorbidity into 3 groups: 0–1 points, no comorbidity; 2 points, low comorbidity; ≥3 points, high comorbidity44.

Socio-demographic variables

Other variables collected included age, biological sex at birth, marital status, educational level, and employment status. Postpartum status, parity, childcare responsibilities were not collected; postpartum women were eligible.

Weight and height were also collected, and the Body Mass Index quantified in kg/m2 was subsequently calculated.

Structure and planning of visits

Once the sample was selected, the researchers contacted the participants by telephone offering them participation in the study and ensuring compliance with the established inclusion/exclusion criteria. Those who initially agreed to participate were scheduled for a visit with the researcher in charge of conducting the assessments. This visit lasted between 60 and 90 min. During the visit, the development of the study was explained, informed consent was collected and the hetero-administered questionnaires were administered. Finally, the accelerometer was placed and the self-administered questionnaires were handed out. Five days later, each participant was scheduled to return the accelerometer and the questionnaires provided.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was carried out in accordance with the study protocol. For this manuscript, the distribution of the variables was tested using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The quantitative variables analysed have a normal distribution and are expressed as means ± standard deviation, while the categorical variables are expressed by number and frequency distribution.

The results obtained from the variables studied were analysed by age group, sex and overall, in order to identify significant differences between them. The difference in means between quantitative variables was estimated with Student’s t-test and the differences between qualitative variables were estimated with the Chi-square test. To assess the relationship between quantitative variables between more than 2 groups, analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the Bonferroni test were used in the Post-hoc contrasts between age strata. In all cases, a risk α of 0.05 was set as the limit of statistical significance for hypothesis testing. The joint influence of sex and age was examined using two complementary approaches. First, sex- and age-specific prevalence of poor sleep quality (PSQI ≥ 5) were estimated with post hoc contrasts between age strata. Second, a multivariable logistic regression was fitted with PSQI ≥ 5 as the dependent variable and sex, age group (reference: 25 years), Body Mass Index (BMI), and Charlson Comorbidity Index as predictors. Educational level was described and explored for context. Given the equal allocation across strata, overall and subgroup comparisons were interpreted with caution. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Socio-demographic and clinical variables

The participation rate was 25.73% overall (24.08% in men; 27.62% in women). Regarding marital status, 55.2% of participants were married or in a common-law partnership, with a higher proportion in men than in women (60.0% vs. 50.4%; p < 0.05). The main reason for non-participation was an explicit refusal to take part after being informed about the study procedures, followed by being unreachable despite repeated contact attempts. Meeting the prespecified exclusion criteria was comparatively infrequent.

Overall, 48.8% of the sample analysed had higher education (44.4% of men and 54.4% of women, p > 0.05). The 25- and 45-year age groups showed a higher proportion of university education, ranging from 62 to 72% and 42–72% respectively.

Employment status showed differences between the sexes. Overall, 65.6% of participants were employed, with a slightly higher proportion of men (66.4%) than women (64.8%). This gender gap was most evident in the 25-year age group, where 90% of women were employed, compared to 64% of men. In the 55-year age group, 20% of men were unemployed, compared to only 10% of women. It is relevant to note that 1.2% of the sample, dedicated exclusively to home and childcare, was entirely composed of women.

On the other hand, the Charlson comorbidity index increased progressively with age in both sexes, with no significant differences between sexes. The overall mean (± SD) of this index was 0.48 ± 0.96 for men and 0.35 ± 0.79 for women.

The overall mean BMI was 26.9 ± 4.4 kg/m² for men and 25.2 ± 4.7 kg/m² for women (p < 0.05). The highest values were found in the 45-year age group for men (28.4 ± 5.2 kg/m²) and 65-year group for women (26.2 ± 4.4 kg/m²). Table 1.

Efficiency (%)

None of the groups analysed showed sleep efficiency values below 85%, the cut-off point established as good sleep quality. Significant differences were observed in sleep efficiency according to sex and age group. Overall, women showed a higher sleep efficiency with a mean of 91.3 ± 4.3, compared to 89.9 ± 6.8 in men. Differences were also observed between men and women for the 65-year group, in favour of the latter (91.6 ± 3.7 in men, 93.6 ± 2.9 in women), p < 0.05. The highest efficiency was found in the 55-year group (92.9 ± 3.4) and the lowest in the 35-year group (87.0 ± 9.5). Post-hoc tests confirmed significant age-group contrasts for efficiency (25 vs. 35; 35 vs. 45, 55 and 65; 45 vs. 55 and 65; all p < 0.05).

Total minutes in bed (min/day)

Although no significant differences were found between sexes, the total time in bed did show differences between the men and women 55-year groups (352.1 ± 70.2 for men, 381.6 ± 73.8 for women), p < 0.05. Similarly, significant differences were found between age groups, with the 55-year group spending the least time in bed (366.7 ± 73.2) and the 35- and 45-year groups showing the most time in bed (451.2 ± 58.5 and 451.2 ± 63.6, respectively). Post-hoc tests identified differences between 25 vs. 35, 45, 55, 65; 35 vs. 55 and 65; and 45 vs. 55 and 65 (all p < 0.05).

TST- total sleep time (min/day)

Overall, women slept longer than men, although these differences were only significant for the 55-year group (325.5 ± 65.9 for men, 357.4 ± 72.4 for women). Significant differences were also found in the analysis by age, with the 45-year group having the highest TST (403.0 ± 60.7) and 55-year-old the lowest (341.3 ± 70.7). Post-hoc tests showed differences between 25 vs. 45 and 55; 35 vs. 55 and 65; and 45 vs. 55 and 65 (all p < 0.05).

WASO- wake after sleep onset (min)

The WASO showed significant differences both in terms of sex and in the different age groups. Men stayed awake longer after sleep onset (39.4 ± 18.3 min) than women (35.7 ± 18.3 min), this difference also being relevant within the 65-year group (33.0 ± 16.5 for men, 23.8 ± 12.1 for women). The 35-year group showed the highest WASO (52.2 ± 18.3), while the 55-year group showed the lowest (25.4 ± 12.4). Post-hoc analyses revealed significant contrasts for WASO between 25 vs. 35, 45, 55 and 65; 35 vs. 55 and 65; and 45 vs. 55 and 65 (all p < 0.05).

Number of awakenings

Men experienced more nocturnal awakenings (14.5 ± 5.6) than women (13.2 ± 5.5), p < 0.05. The largest differences were observed in the 35-year age group, where men presented 17.0 ± 5.8 awakenings versus 14.3 ± 4.8 for women (p < 0.05). Significant differences were also observed between age groups, with the 25-year age group having the most awakenings (16.0 ± 5.6), progressively decreasing to the 65-year age group, which experienced the fewest (10.9 ± 4.9). Post-hoc tests showed significant contrasts for number of awakenings between 25 vs. 55 and 65; 35 vs. 55 and 65; and 45 vs. 55 and 65 (all p < 0.05).Average awakening length (min)Differences were observed between age groups. The 25-year group had the shortest awakening duration (2.1 ± 0.5), whereas the 35-year group had the longest awakening duration (3.7 ± 1.4), p < 0.05. Sex differences were significant only for the 65-year-old group, where men had longer awakenings (3.0 ± 1.4) compared to women (2.4 ± 0.7). Movement index

The movement index showed significant overall differences depending on biological sex and age groups. In general, men had a higher movement index (14.2 ± 3.6) than women (12.5 ± 3.5), with this difference between men and women observed for all age groups except for the 25-year group. Post-hoc analysis confirmed significant age differences for movement index between 35 vs. 55 and 65 (both p < 0.05).

Fragmentation index

Men presented a higher fragmentation index (11.3 ± 5.0) than women (9.9 ± 5.0), p < 0.05, especially for the 35- and 55-year groups. Post-hoc comparisons indicated no significant differences in the sleep fragmentation index between any age groups.

Sleep fragmentation index

The sleep fragmentation index showed differences between men (25.5 ± 7.5) and women (22.4 ± 7.2), p < 0.05, which were more evident in the 35-year (27.5 ± 6.4 for men vs. 22.8 ± 6.4 for women) and 55-year (23.9 ± 7.4 for men vs. 19.7 ± 6.0 for women) groups, both p < 0.05. Significant differences were also observed based on age, with the 35-year group presenting the highest fragmentation index (25.1 ± 6.8) and the 55-year group the lowest (21.8 ± 7.0). Post-hoc comparisons indicated significant differences in the sleep fragmentation index between 25 vs. 55 and 35 vs. 55 (both p < 0.05).

Discussion

The participation rate in our study, it was below 30%, with the lowest participation observed among 25-year-old men (participation rates in this group: 17.8% men vs. 23.4% women). The proportion of participants with higher education (university level) exceeded official estimates. According to the Spanish National Statistics Institute (INE), in 2022, 26.6% of adults aged 18–69 years in Spain and 25.5% in Castilla y León had university studies, compared with 48.8% in our sample, which is consistent with prior reports on participation patterns in health surveys, whereby individuals with higher educational attainment and stable employment are more likely to volunteer, whereas young men participate less frequently45–48. Beyond education, our 25-year stratum showed an employment imbalance (90% of women employed vs. 64% of men), which reinforces prior evidence that labor-market attachment differentially shapes survey participation by sex and age46.

Regarding self-reported sleep quality, in our sample, the mean score obtained on the PSQI was 7.1 ± 4.1, results comparable to those reported by Khosravi A et al., who analysed sleep quality in the general adult Iranian population, with a mean of 6.9 ± 4.049. Similarly, Gómez-García T et al. obtained a mean score of 6.68 ± 3.39 on the PSQI when assessing sleep quality in a sample of 635 nurses from seven Spanish hospitals36. In contrast, Neumann N et al., after analysing a sample of 1074 individuals aged between 22 and 35 years, based on the Human Connectome Project population, obtained a mean PSQI score of 4.71 ± 2.67, information that contrasts with our results, in which the highest values in this questionnaire corresponded to the 25-year group (8.0 ± 4.1)50. Rodríguez-González-Moro MT et al. obtained a mean score of 5.03 ± 2.9 in a sample of 476 public workers aged between 25 and 66 years in the Region of Murcia (Spain)51. While these comparisons, particularly with Spanish nurses and public workers, provide context for our findings against working-age cohorts with similar socio-demographic profiles, the discrepancy may partly reflect differences in sampling frames and participation patterns across studies. Although we employed age- and sex-stratified random sampling to enhance representativeness, this design does not guarantee it, particularly given the uniform allocation across strata and potential non-response. Therefore, we interpret the higher mean PSQI in our sample with due caution.

Focusing on sex differences, a worse self-reported sleep quality (PSQI global) was identified in women in all age groups, which is consistent with the results reported by other authors25–27. Although, the available literature on the sex gap in subjective sleep quality provides mixed results. Some authors found no significant differences in the overall PSQI score between the sexes51–53. However, they found significant differences in its sleep latency and sleep duration components, both of which were higher in women52. Others reported higher PSQI scores in this group41,50,54, with higher sleep onset latency and lower perceived sleep quality, despite the fact that most sleep disorders are objectively identified in men28. Even though the causes underlying these sex differences remain unclear, they could be related to alterations in sleep architecture due to hormonal factors25,29,55, physical problems such as fibromyalgia, chronic pain or overactive bladder, which are more prevalent in the female population28, or a greater propensity to suffer psychological or psychiatric problems such as anxiety, in which the largest gender gap appears during adolescence and decreases throughout life56. This fact could explain why in our study the highest PSQI score in our sample corresponds to the group of 25-year-old women, with similar results being obtained in the study carried out by Gildner et al.57 who, based on a self-reported questionnaire on quality and duration of 2-night sleep, concluded that in the 6 countries analysed (China, Ghana, India, Mexico, Russia and South Africa), the average assessment of sleep quality was significantly lower in women than in men57.

As for differences across age strata, the lower subjective sleep quality corresponded to the 25-year group, the youngest in the series, while the lowest PSQI scores corresponded to the 45-year group.

Based on prevalence, in our sample, 56.6% met criteria for poor sleep quality (PSQI ≥ 5), a proportion higher than population-based estimates reported for Spanish adults (38.2% with poor sleep quality)58 and for a large German community sample (36%)59. Consistent with both studies, women reported poorer sleep than men. This higher prevalence may reflect a post-pandemic effect, with a persistence of sleep disturbances that is particularly pronounced in Europe and among women, as indicated by the meta-analysis by Linh et al.60

Educational attainment (used as a proxy for socioeconomic status) was not associated with the prevalence of poor sleep quality (PSQI ≥ 5) across educational strata (p = 0.165).

Furthermore, when analysing the PSQI subscales (Fig. 2), statistically significant sex differences were observed in all domains except use of sleep medication, and significant age-group differences were identified for sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, and use of sleep medication. These domain-specific patterns complement the total PSQI findings and reinforce the need to interpret global scores alongside subscale profiles, as they may mask distinct sex- and age-related trajectories.

The actigraphy analysis showed a mean sleep efficiency of 90.6% (± 5.7). There were significant sex differences, with women showing higher values, similar to results found in previous studies26,37. Men, on the other hand, showed more WASO, awakenings and sleep fragmentation than women, which contrasts with the lower scores reported on the PSQI., and could be related to the progressive reduction in testosterone levels65–67 starting at the age of 3028, as well as to an increase in adiposity66. This lack of concordance between the PSQI and actigraphy metrics in our sample is consistent with findings from previous studies, which also showed that subjective sleep quality does not correlate well with objective measures of sleep measured by actigraphy41,61,62 and polysomnography (PSG)63,64. The PSQI provides a one-month, retrospective cognitive–affective appraisal that is sensitive to mood, pain, expectations and daytime consequences, whereas actigraphy in our protocol quantifies 5 consecutive nights of nocturnal continuity. In addition, domain mismatch contributes to divergence: the PSQI includes sleep latency and use of sleep medication, but latency was not estimable in our actigraphy pipeline because the Tudor-Locke procedure automatically sets latency to zero39,40. Methodologically, actigraphy shows high sensitivity but lower specificity compared with PSG, so motionless wakefulness may be scored as sleep, inflating efficiency in subgroups with lower nocturnal movement30,31. Life-stage and caregiving demands may further widen subjective–objective gaps (particularly in younger and midlife adults) by increasing fragmentation and night-to-night variability68,69.Regarding the differences identified by age group, a lower sleep efficiency was observed in the younger age groups, especially in the 35- and 45-year groups, which increased significantly in the 55- and 65-year groups. This contrasts with the TST, since it is the 55- and 65-year groups that obtained the lowest values for this variable, while the highest values corresponded to the 35- and 45-year groups.

As for WASO (in minutes), a mean value of 37.6 ± 18.3 was obtained, significantly higher in men (39.4 ± 18.3 vs. 35.7 ± 18.3 in women) and slightly higher than the 35.6 ± 9.5 reported by Quante M. et al.37, using the same algorithm as in our study, although their sample was composed by only 22 healthy adult volunteers with a mean age of 32.3 ± 11.4 years. In addition, significant differences between age groups were found in the WASO, with the highest values corresponding to the 35- and 45-year groups and the lowest to the 55- and 65-year groups. Regarding the number of awakenings, it is remarkable that the 25-year group presented the highest number of awakenings, reducing its presence progressively until the 65-year group, which presented the lowest value of the series. This dissociation (more frequent but briefer awakenings in younger adults versus fewer but more prolonged awakenings in mid-adulthood) is consistent with evidence that child-rearing and family responsibilities increase parental sleep fragmentation and night-to-night variability, with a greater burden of wake after sleep onset68. In addition, early-postpartum studies indicate that fragmentation (captured by metrics such as WASO or “percent sleep”) has greater functional implications than a mere reduction in total sleep time, reinforcing the interpretation of our finding as an issue of continuity rather than duration69. Taken together, these data suggest that the excess WASO at ages 35–45 reflects nocturnal caregiving demands and work–family conflict characteristic of this life stage, and they support reporting both the number of awakenings and WASO to characterise sleep continuity across adulthood. Our cross-sectional dataset did not include parity, postpartum status, breastfeeding or childcare responsibilities; therefore, we cannot ascertain whether the observed sex differences are independent of parenthood or life stage.

The overall TST was 373.0 ± 76.1 min, a result that coincides with those obtained by Kaplan K et al.70, who found a TST value of 379 ± 81.1 in their study, although the time in bed they obtained was higher. This may be because they analysed a larger population than ours (mean 76.5 years). Quante M et al.37, reported higher mean values in young adults and with the same device used in our study (465.2 ± 56.6 min), although their small sample size may have affected their results. The lowest figures for this parameter were found for the 55-year group, more marked in men than in women, although without reaching the threshold of statistical significance, a finding that is close to that found by Roncero C et al., who identified the group of 48–57 years old as the one with the highest rate of insomnia71. While TST and sleep quality may have a direct impact on executive functions, time spent in different sleep phases and sleep fragmentation may be better related to cognitive function than total sleep time17.

The 65-year stratum coincides with Spain’s statutory retirement window. In our sample, 56.6% met the criteria for poor sleep quality (PSQI ≥ 5), a proportion higher than population-based estimates for Spanish adults (38.2% with poor sleep quality)58 and for a large German community cohort (36%)59. Consistent with both studies, women reported poorer sleep quality than men. However, actigraphic variables reflect high efficiency and low fragmentation despite a shorter total sleep time (TST), which suggests that the freedom from work schedules upon cessation of employment, by aligning with personal preferences, could improve sleep and its continuity even if its duration is limited72,73. It is important to note that the PSQI and actigraphy capture partially distinct constructs in older adults, with weak or inconsistent correlations, which underscores the value of reporting both34.

Due to the fact that sleep disorders and favour the development of a wide range of chronic diseases and increase the probability of hospital admission and the consumption of resources, their identification and treatment could be successful strategies for the control of health expenditure24.

Strengths and limitations

We highlight the following strengths of this study: age–sex stratified random sampling with uniform allocation maximised the internal validity of within-stratum contrasts and enabled balanced comparisons across five exact age cohorts (25, 35, 45, 55, and 65 years); the combination of subjective and objective assessments (PSQI and actigraphy) captures complementary facets of sleep health and reduces single-method bias; the use of standardised procedures and a population-based sample enhances methodological rigour; and the multivariable model quantified the risk of poor sleep quality (PSQI ≥ 5) across sex and age strata, adjusting for BMI and comorbidity burden.

However, several limitations should guide interpretation: uniform allocation across strata means that pooled and sex-aggregated estimates are unweighted and not population-proportional, so absolute prevalences and pooled sex differences are not strictly representative, although stratified comparisons retain internal validity; participation/selection bias cannot be ruled out (e.g., lower participation among 25-year-old men and overrepresentation of university education); geographical scope (two provincial capitals in central Spain) may limit generalisability beyond similar settings; parenthood-related factors were not measured, precluding assessment of whether sex differences are independent of life stage; the Tudor-Locke–based actigraphy pipeline prevented estimation of sleep latency, a clinically relevant variable; and the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference.

Taken together, these considerations call for caution when extrapolating absolute prevalences, while supporting the robustness of the age- and sex-stratified contrasts derived from our design.

Conclusions

The study population reported low self-reported sleep quality (global PSQI Score), especially in women, with the 25-year-old group showing the lower and the 45-year-old group the higher PSQI global scores. However, the actigraphy data did not reflect this perception, as sleep efficiency was above 85% in all groups, with women showing higher efficiency, lower WASO, fewer awakenings and lower rates of movement and fragmentation than men.

These findings indicate that the effect of age and sex differs depending on the measurement method (subjective vs. objective) and underscore the value of reporting both modalities in population studies.
