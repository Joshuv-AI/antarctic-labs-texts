Bi-Virus SIS Epidemic Propagation under Mutation and Game-theoretic Protection Adoption footnoteinfo
Urmee Maitra, Ashish R. Hota, Vaibhav Srivastava

Abstract
We study a bi-virus susceptible-infected-susceptible (SIS) epidemic model in which individuals are either susceptible or infected with one of two virus strains, and consider mutation-driven transitions between strains. The general case of bi-directional mutation is first analyzed, where we characterize the disease-free equilibrium and establish its global asymptotic stability, as well as the existence, uniqueness, and stability of an endemic equilibrium. We then present a game-theoretic framework where susceptible individuals strategically choose whether to adopt protection or remain unprotected, to maximize their instantaneous payoffs. We derive Nash strategies under bi-directional mutation, and subsequently consider the special case of uni-directional mutation. In the latter case, we show that coexistence of both strains is impossible when mutation occurs from the strain with lower reproduction number and transmission rate to the other strain. Furthermore, we fully characterize the stationary Nash equilibrium (SNE) in the setting permitting coexistence, and examine how mutation rates influence protection adoption and infection prevalence at the SNE. Numerical simulations corroborate the analytical results, demonstrating that infection levels decrease monotonically with higher protection adoption, and highlight the impact of mutation rates and protection cost on infection state trajectories.

Containing the spread of infectious diseases is a major challenge for policy-makers. As witnessed during the recent COVID-19 pandemic, the epidemic can spread over waves, potentially driven by the emergence of multiple viral strains through mutations
[

,

or the selfish response of individuals towards protection adoption

In addition to intrinsic factors such as transmission and recovery rates, individual behavior regarding protection adoption plays a crucial role in epidemic dynamics. This motivated researchers to view the problem of epidemic propagation coupled with human behavior through the prism of game theory. Prior research has explored the influence of human decision-making on epidemic spread using game-theoretic frameworks

We consider a susceptible-infected-susceptible (SIS) epidemic model with two virus strains, denoted by
\mathtt{H}
and
\mathtt{L}
, each with distinct transmission and recovery rates, where strain
\mathtt{H}
exhibits higher reproduction number and transmission rate in comparison to strain
\mathtt{L}
. Individuals can only be infected by one strain at any given time. Our game setting models all susceptible individuals as players who choose between adopting protection and remaining unprotected to maximize their individual payoffs.

Our analysis begins with the general case of bi-directional mutation, where individuals infected by one strain can mutate to the other. The mutation from
\mathtt{L}
to
\mathtt{H}
may arise from selective advantages, whereas, mutation from
\mathtt{H}
to
\mathtt{L}
may result from deleterious mutations such as replication errors. We show that the epidemic dynamics with mutations and protection adoption always admits a disease-free equilibrium which is either globally asymptotically stable in a suitable parameter regime or unstable otherwise. We then establish the existence of a unique endemic equilibrium of coexisting strains and prove its global asymptotic stability under suitable assumptions. Furthermore, we characterize the stationary Nash equilibrium (SNE) of the game-theoretic model, and show that infection levels decrease monotonically with increased protection adoption.

We then investigate the case of uni-directional mutation, considering two scenarios: mutation
only
from
\mathtt{L}
to
\mathtt{H}
and mutation
only
from
\mathtt{H}
to
\mathtt{L}
. Our results reveal that in the first scenario, coexistence of both strains is impossible, reducing the system to a single-virus setting. In contrast, in the second scenario, both strains coexist at steady-state. We characterize the SNEs when both viruses coexist. We analytically show that when the mutation rate is below a threshold, both strains persist; however, as the mutation rate increases, the prevalence of
\mathtt{H}
decreases, and beyond the threshold, a single-strain endemic equilibrium dominated by
\mathtt{L}
emerges. We validate our major findings through numerical simulations.

We first establish the invariance of

Lemma 3.1

Given

We now analyze the states governed by (

, then
\dot{\mathtt{I}}_{\mathtt{H}}(t)=q_{\mathtt{LH}}\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}}\geq 0

\dot{\mathtt{S}}=-\dot{\mathtt{I}}_{\mathtt{H}}-\dot{\mathtt{I}}_{\mathtt{L}}

\displaystyle\dot{\mathtt{I}}_{\mathtt{H}}(t)=\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}}(t))\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}(t)(1-\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}(t)-\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}}(t))+q_{\mathtt{LH}}\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}}(t)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-(q_{\mathtt{HL}}+\gamma_{\mathtt{H}})\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}(t),

\displaystyle\dot{\mathtt{I}}_{\mathtt{L}}(t)=\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}}(t))\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}}(t)(1-\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}(t)-\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}}(t))+q_{\mathtt{HL}}\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}(t)

Theorem 3.2

The disease-free equilibrium,

\rho\big(D^{-1}(B+M)\big)\leq 1

Proof.

We begin the proof by computing the Jacobian at DFE as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\textbf{E}_{\mathtt{DFE}})

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}-\gamma_{\mathtt{H}}-q_{\mathtt{HL}}&q_{\mathtt{LH}}\\

(4)

\displaystyle=B-D+M.

Observe that
-D
is a negative diagonal matrix, whereas

is an irreducible nonnegative matrix. Following Proposition

, the necessary and sufficient condition for the DFE to be locally stable is that the stability modulus satisfies
\phi(B-D+M)\leq 0

\rho\big(D^{-1}(B+M)\big)\leq 1
. We denote
\mathcal{R}_{0}:=\rho\big(D^{-1}(B+M)\big)

Note that when the spectral radius
\rho\big(D^{-1}(B+M)\big)>1
, from the computed Jacobian
\mathcal{J}(\textbf{E}_{\mathtt{DFE}})
it can be shown that the disease-free equilibrium is unstable. Thus,
\rho\big(D^{-1}(B+M)\big)\leq 1
is necessary to ensure the stability of the equilibrium.

We now derive the sufficient condition for stability by exploiting a special property of Metzler matrices. Since, the Jacobian matrix
\mathcal{J}(\textbf{E}_{\mathtt{DFE}})=B-D+M
has all non-negative off-diagonal elements, it is a Metzler matrix, and there exists a positive diagonal matrix

such that

(B-D+M)^{\top}P^{\mathtt{DFE}}+P^{\mathtt{DFE}}(B-D+M)=-K^{\mathtt{DFE}},

where

is a positive semi-definite matrix

,
[
20
]

as a candidate Lyapunov function. The time-derivative of

yields

\displaystyle\dot{V}

\displaystyle=\textbf{x}^{\top}P^{\mathtt{DFE}}\dot{\textbf{x}}+\dot{\textbf{x}}^{\top}P^{\mathtt{DFE}}\textbf{x}

\displaystyle=\textbf{x}^{\top}\big(P^{\mathtt{DFE}}(B-D+M)+(B-D+M)^{\top}P^{\mathtt{DFE}}\big)\textbf{x}

\displaystyle\qquad-\textbf{x}^{\top}P^{\mathtt{DFE}}XB\;\mathbb{1}\;\textbf{x}-\textbf{x}^{\top}\;\mathbb{1}\;P^{\mathtt{DFE}}XB\textbf{x}

\displaystyle=-\textbf{x}^{\top}K^{\mathtt{DFE}}\textbf{x}-\textbf{x}^{\top}\underbrace{P^{\mathtt{DFE}}XB}_{\succeq 0}\;\mathbb{1}\;\textbf{x}

\displaystyle\qquad-\textbf{x}^{\top}\;\mathbb{1}\;P^{\mathtt{DFE}}XB\textbf{x}\leq 0.

(5)

Note that
P^{\mathtt{DFE}}XB=BXP^{\mathtt{DFE}}
holds, since
P^{\mathtt{DFE}},X

if and only if
\textbf{x}=\textbf{0}

\displaystyle-\textbf{x}^{\top}P^{\mathtt{DFE}}XB\;\mathbb{1}\;\textbf{x}-\textbf{x}^{\top}\;\mathbb{1}\;P^{\mathtt{DFE}}XB\textbf{x}

\displaystyle=-\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}\big(2p_{1}^{\mathtt{DFE}}\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}^{2}\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}+\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}}(p_{1}^{\mathtt{DFE}}\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}+p_{2}^{\mathtt{DFE}}\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}}\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}})\big)

\displaystyle\quad-\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}}\big(2p_{2}^{\mathtt{DFE}}\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}}^{2}\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}+\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}(p_{1}^{\mathtt{DFE}}\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}+p_{2}^{\mathtt{DFE}}\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}}\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}})\big),

and

Characterization of Endemic Equilibrium

Remark 3.4

Note that when the reproduction number satisfies

Assumption 3.5
.

For a fixed
z_{\mathtt{S}}\in[0,1]
, the mutation rates satisfy the following inequality:

\max(q_{\mathtt{LH}}+\gamma_{\mathtt{L}},q_{\mathtt{HL}}+\gamma_{\mathtt{H}})\leq\min\big(\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}}),\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})\big).

Remark 3.6
.

The above assumption implies that effective transmission rates of both strains are not smaller than the sum of the mutation and recovery rates of both strains. Indeed for most infectious diseases, infection rates are larger compared to the sum of the rates at which they mutate and recover.

Observe that when Assumption
3.5
holds, sum of the eigenvalues of Jacobian
\mathcal{J}(\textbf{E}_{\mathtt{DFE}})
in (
4
) is positive, which implies that the largest eigenvalue is positive. Consequently, when the parameters satisfy Assumption
3.5
at some
z_{\mathtt{S}}\in[0,1]
, we have
\mathcal{R}_{0}(z_{\mathtt{S}})>1
.

Theorem 3.7
.

Suppose Assumption
3.5
holds for a given
z_{\mathtt{S}}\in[0,1]
. Then the system defined by (
2a
) and (
2b
) admits a unique endemic equilibrium.

Proof.

The proof is presented in Appendix
A
.
∎

We now present the main theorem establishing the global asymptotic stability of the endemic equilibrium.

Theorem 3.8
.

Suppose Assumption
3.5
holds for a given
z_{\mathtt{S}}\in[0,1]
. Then, the unique endemic equilibrium
\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}}),\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})\in(0,1)
is (almost) globally asymptotically stable.

Proof.

Recall that when Assumption
3.5
is satisfied,
\mathcal{R}_{0}(z_{\mathtt{S}})>1
holds. Then, disease-free equilibrium is unstable, and a unique locally stable endemic equilibrium exists. We begin the proof of global asymptotic stability of the endemic equilibrium by ruling out the existence of any closed orbits. We omit the dependence on
z_{\mathtt{S}}
is the remainder of the proof.

Our dynamics has two independent states, the dynamics of both are continuously differentiable on the domain of
(0,1)^{2}
. We define the real-valued function
h:(0,1)^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, such that,
h(\textbf{x}):=\frac{1}{\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}}}
where
\textbf{x}=(\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}},\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}})
. Note that domain of the mapping
h(\textbf{x})
excludes the disease-free equilibrium, and it is continuously differentiable on
(0,1)^{2}
. Calculating the product

h(\textbf{x})\;\dot{\textbf{x}}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}}{\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}}}(1-\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}-\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}})+\frac{q_{\mathtt{LH}}}{\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}}-\frac{(q_{\mathtt{HL}}+\gamma_{\mathtt{H}})}{\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}}}\\
\frac{\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}}{\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}}(1-\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}-\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}})+\frac{q_{\mathtt{HL}}}{\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}}}-\frac{(q_{\mathtt{LH}}+\gamma_{\mathtt{L}})}{\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}}\end{bmatrix},

and computing its divergence, we obtain

\nabla\cdot(h\;\dot{\textbf{x}})=-\frac{\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}}{\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}}}-\frac{q_{\mathtt{LH}}}{\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}^{2}}-\frac{\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}}{\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}}-\frac{q_{\mathtt{HL}}}{\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}}^{2}}<0,

throughout the domain of
(0,1)^{2}
, i.e., the sign of
\nabla\cdot(h\;\dot{\textbf{x}})
remains unchanged (i.e., remains negative). Therefore, by leveraging Dulac’s Criterion
[
28
]
, we conclude that closed orbits do not exist in the space of
(0,1)^{2}
.

Consequently, in the absence of any closed orbits, the disease-free equilibrium being unstable, and the unique endemic equilibrium being locally stable in the two-dimensional system defined by (
2a
) and (
2b
), the only possibility left for any trajectory with any initial conditions in the space of
(0,1)^{2}
is to converge to the endemic equilibrium and remain there (which exhibits local stability).
∎

4
Game-Theoretic Protection Adoption under Bi-directional Mutation

We now integrate the mutation-driven epidemic propagation and the game-theoretic strategies of protection adoption. First, we introduce the rewards for susceptible individuals. As mentioned earlier, a susceptible individual has the choice of either adopting protection or remaining unprotected. It incurs a cost of
\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{P}}>0
on adopting protection, whereas on remaining unprotected there is no cost involved. We define the instantaneous reward received by a susceptible agent choosing an action
a\in\{\mathtt{P},\mathtt{U}\}
as

\displaystyle R[\mathtt{P}](\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}},\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}})

\displaystyle=-\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{P}}-\alpha\big(\rho_{\mathtt{H}}\beta_{\mathtt{H}}\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}+\rho_{\mathtt{L}}\beta_{\mathtt{L}}\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}}\big),

\displaystyle R[\mathtt{U}](\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}},\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}})

\displaystyle=-\big(\rho_{\mathtt{H}}\beta_{\mathtt{H}}\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}+\rho_{\mathtt{L}}\beta_{\mathtt{L}}\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}}\big),

(8)

where
\rho_{\mathtt{H}}
(respectively,
\rho_{\mathtt{L}}
) captures the loss a susceptible agent incurs upon infection by strain
\mathtt{H}
(respectively, strain
\mathtt{L}
). We further define

\displaystyle\Delta R(\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}},\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}})

\displaystyle:=R[\mathtt{P}](\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}},\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}})-R[\mathtt{U}](\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}},\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}})

\displaystyle=-\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{P}}+(1-\alpha)\big(\rho_{\mathtt{H}}\beta_{\mathtt{H}}\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}+\rho_{\mathtt{L}}\beta_{\mathtt{L}}\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}}\big).

(9)

We now provide a formal definition of
stationary Nash equilibrium
(SNE) of this game.

Definition 4.1
.

The tuple
(\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}},\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}},z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}})
is a Stationary Nash equilibrium if
(\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}},\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}})
denotes the stable equilibrium point of (
2a
) and (
2b
) at
z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}
, and the proportion that adopts protection
z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}
satisfies the following conditions:

\displaystyle z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}=0\Rightarrow\Delta R(\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}},\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}})\leq 0,\text{ and }\Delta R(\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}},\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}})<0\Rightarrow z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}=0,

\displaystyle z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}=1\Rightarrow\Delta R(\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}},\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}})\geq 0,\text{ and }\Delta R(\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}},\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}})>0\Rightarrow z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}=1,

\displaystyle z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}\in(0,1)\Rightarrow\Delta R(\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}},\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}})=0.

We first state the following lemma which is crucial for the characterization of SNE.

Lemma 4.2
.

The infected proportions at the endemic equilibrium,
\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
and
\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
, are monotonically decreasing in the proportion that adopt protection
z_{\mathtt{S}}
.

Proof.

The proof is included in Appendix
B
.
∎

We now define

\displaystyle\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{\min}}

\displaystyle:=(1-\alpha)\big(\beta_{\mathtt{H}}\rho_{\mathtt{H}}\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}}=1)+\beta_{\mathtt{L}}\rho_{\mathtt{L}}\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}}=1)\big),

\displaystyle\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{\max}}

\displaystyle:=(1-\alpha)\big(\beta_{\mathtt{H}}\rho_{\mathtt{H}}\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}}=0)+\beta_{\mathtt{L}}\rho_{\mathtt{L}}\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}}=0)\big).

(10)

Our main theorem pertaining to the characterization of SNE is stated below.

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose Assumption
3.5
holds for
z_{\mathtt{S}}=1
. The following statements hold for the protection behavior
z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}
at stationary Nash equilibria:

(a)

if
\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{P}}<\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{\min}}\text{, we have }z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}=1
;

(b)

if
\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{P}}>\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{\max}}\text{, we have }z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}=0
;

(c)

if
\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{\min}}\leq\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{P}}\leq\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{\max}}
, then there exists a unique
z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}\in[0,1]
such that
\Delta R(\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}),\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}))=0
.

The infected proportions at the SNE are at their respective unique endemic equilibria given by
\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}),\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}})
.

Proof.

Recall from the proof of Theorem
3.2
that

\mathcal{R}_{0}(z_{\mathtt{S}})=\rho\Bigg(\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}{\gamma_{\mathtt{H}}+q_{\mathtt{HL}}}&q_{\mathtt{LH}}\\
q_{\mathtt{HL}}&\frac{\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}{\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}+q_{\mathtt{LH}}}\end{bmatrix}\Bigg).

Since the above matrix is positive and irreducible, its spectral radius is monotonically increasing in each element of the matrix; a consequence of the Perron-Frobenius Theorem
[
5
, Corollary 8.1.19]
. As a result,
\mathcal{R}_{0}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
is monotonically increasing in
\frac{\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}{\gamma_{\mathtt{H}}+q_{\mathtt{HL}}}
and
\frac{\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}{\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}+q_{\mathtt{LH}}}
. However,
\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
and
\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
are both individually decreasing in
z_{\mathtt{S}}
, which implies that
\mathcal{R}_{0}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
is monotonically decreasing in
z_{\mathtt{S}}
. Consequently,
\mathcal{R}_{0}(1)>1\Rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{0}(z_{\mathtt{S}})>1,\;\forall\;z_{\mathtt{S}}\in[0,1)
, which implies that an endemic equilibrium exists and is GAS for all
z_{\mathtt{S}}
.

We now define the difference in rewards at the endemic equilibrium as

\Delta R[\textbf{E}_{\mathtt{EE}}](z_{\mathtt{S}}):=-\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{P}}+(1-\alpha)\big(\rho_{\mathtt{H}}\beta_{\mathtt{H}}\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})+\rho_{\mathtt{L}}\beta_{\mathtt{L}}\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})\big).

(11)

We now analyze the Nash strategies at the endemic equilibrium. Recall from Lemma
4.2
that both
\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
and
\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
are monotonically decreasing in
z_{\mathtt{S}}
. Thus, from (
11
) we see that
\Delta R[\textbf{E}_{\mathtt{EE}}](z_{\mathtt{S}})
is monotonically decreasing in
z_{\mathtt{S}}
, satisfying

\displaystyle-\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{P}}+\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{\min}}\leq\Delta R[\textbf{E}_{\mathtt{EE}}](z_{\mathtt{S}})\leq-\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{P}}+\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{\max}},

(12)

where
\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{\min}}
and
\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{\max}}
are defined in (
10
). We now derive the conditions under the three sub-cases.

Case (a):

z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}=1

When
\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{P}}<\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{\min}}
then we obtain
\Delta R[\textbf{E}_{\mathtt{EE}}](z_{\mathtt{S}})>0
for all
z_{\mathtt{S}}\in[0,1]
. Therefore, every individual strictly prefers to adopt protection irrespective of the strategies chosen by others. Thus,
z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}=1
is the only strategy that arises at the SNE.

For necessity, let
z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}=1
. Then, we must have

\Delta R[\textbf{E}_{\mathtt{EE}}](1)=-\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{P}}+\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{\min}}\geq 0,

since no individual would prefer to remain unprotected when everyone else adopt protection.

Case (b):

z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}=0

When
\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{P}}>\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{\max}}
then we obtain
\Delta R[\textbf{E}_{\mathtt{EE}}](z_{\mathtt{S}})<0
, which implies that
z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}=0
is the only strategy that arises at the SNE following the reasoning stated above.

For necessity, let
z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}=0
. Then, we must have

\Delta R[\textbf{E}_{\mathtt{EE}}](0)=-\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{P}}+\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{\max}}\leq 0,

since no individual would prefer to adopt protection when no one else is doing so.

Case (c):

z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}\in[0,1]

When the protection cost satisfies
\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{\min}}\leq\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{P}}\leq\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{\max}}
, we observe that

\Delta R[\textbf{E}_{\mathtt{EE}}](1)\leq 0\text{, and }\Delta R[\textbf{E}_{\mathtt{EE}}](0)\geq 0.

Since
\Delta R[\textbf{E}_{\mathtt{EE}}](z_{\mathtt{S}})
in
z_{\mathtt{S}}
is monotonically decreasing in
z_{\mathtt{S}}
, there exists a unique
z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}\in[0,1]
such that
\Delta R[\textbf{E}_{\mathtt{EE}}](z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}})=0
holds which constitutes the SNE. In particular, an individual is indifferent between adopting protection or remaining unprotected, and hence, does not derive a strictly larger utility upon unilaterally changing its action.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

The above theorem characterizes the protection behavior adopted by susceptible agents. When the protection cost exceeds the upper threshold, all susceptible agents remain unprotected, and when the protection cost is below the lower threshold, all agents are incentivized to adopt protection. For an intermediate protection cost, a unique non-zero fraction of the susceptible population adopts protection, while the rest remain unprotected.

Remark 4.5
.

We now briefly discuss the strategy adopted by susceptible agents when Assumption
3.5
is not true for
z_{\mathtt{S}}=1
. From the monotonicity property, we know that if
\mathcal{R}_{0}(0)\leq 1\Rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{0}(1)\leq 1
, and the endemic equilibrium does not exist. At the disease-free equilibrium, both strains are absent, i.e.,
\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}=\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}=0
holds. Following (
9
), we have
\Delta R[\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}},\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}]=-\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{P}}<0
when
\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}=\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}=0
, i.e., for each individual, adopting protection has a smaller reward compared to being unprotected, irrespective of the strategies adopted by others. Thus,
z_{\mathtt{S}}^{\mathtt{NE}}=0
is the only Nash strategy, i.e., at the disease-free equilibrium, there exists a unique Nash strategy of remaining unprotected, which is intuitive as none of the viral strains survive.

The setting where
\mathcal{R}_{0}(0)\geq 1
and
\mathcal{R}_{0}(1)\leq 1
could be analyzed along similar lines as the above theorem. However, it would only lead to more number of case analysis without significantly enhancing the technical contributions, and hence omitted.

With the complete characterization of the SNE in the general case of bi-directional mutation, we now consider the special case of mutation in a single direction, i.e., the mutation is either from strain
\mathtt{H}
to strain
\mathtt{L}
, or from strain
\mathtt{L}
to strain
\mathtt{H}
, but not in both directions.

.

Proof.

Equilibrium
\textbf{E}^{\mathtt{L},\mathtt{H}}_{3}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
has steady-state infection states

to exist, it is necessary that

. We first assume that

, which implies that
\mathcal{N}^{\mathtt{L}}_{2}(z_{\mathtt{S}})>0
must hold for
\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
to be positive. Since,
\beta_{\mathtt{L}}q_{\mathtt{LH}}>0

\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})

to ensure
\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})>0

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathtt{H}}\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}-\beta_{\mathtt{L}}\gamma_{\mathtt{H}}+\beta_{\mathtt{H}}q_{\mathtt{LH}}<0

\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})

does not exist.

7
Conclusion

We investigated the impact of game-theoretic protection adoption on infection prevalence in a susceptible-infected-susceptible (SIS) epidemic model involving two mutant strains. Our analysis encompassed both the general case of bi-directional mutation and the special case of uni-directional mutation. For each setting, we first established the existence and stability of equilibrium points for a fixed level of protection adoption by susceptible agents. When mutation is bi-directional, or when it occurs from the stronger strain to the weaker one, we demonstrated the possibility of coexistence of both strains at equilibrium and characterized the stationary Nash equilibrium (SNE) of the underlying game. Our findings highlighted the influence of mutation on infection prevalence and protection strategies. For example, we showed that higher mutation rates to the dominant strain lead to higher protection adoption among individuals, whereas a lower rate reduces the incentive for protection.

This work opens several avenues for future research. One potential direction is to extend the analysis to networked epidemic models with heterogeneous node degrees. In this work, we have assumed that the mutation rates are constant. A possible direction is to model the mutation rates as functions of the protection adoption behavior of the susceptible agents. Finally, the current study assumes equal effectiveness of protection against both strains; relaxing this assumption to reflect more realistic scenarios presents an interesting area for further investigation.

\frac{d\theta}{d\phi}<0

D_{2}

Appendix B
Proof of Lemma
4.2

Proof.

We begin the proof by rewriting (
6
) at a given
z_{\mathtt{S}}\in[0,1]
as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}{\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{\mathtt{H}}+q_{\mathtt{HL}}-\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}{q_{\mathtt{LH}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{q_{\mathtt{HL}}}{\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}+q_{\mathtt{LH}}-\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}.

(22)

Let
z_{\mathtt{S}_{2}}>z_{\mathtt{S}_{1}}
be two protection strategies adopted by the susceptible agents. For the equality in (
22
) to be preserved with an increase in
z_{\mathtt{S}}
, it is necessary that when protection strategy increases from
z_{\mathtt{S}_{1}}
to
z_{\mathtt{S}_{2}}
, either both quantities
\frac{\gamma_{\mathtt{H}}+q_{\mathtt{HL}}-\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}_{1}})\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}_{1}})}{q_{\mathtt{LH}}}
and
\frac{q_{\mathtt{HL}}}{\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}+q_{\mathtt{LH}}-\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}_{1}})\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}_{1}})}
, simultaneously increase, or both quantities simultaneously decrease, or both remain constant. First, we hypothesize that both quantities increase as
z_{\mathtt{S}}
is increased from
z_{\mathtt{S}_{1}}
to
z_{\mathtt{S}_{2}}
. This is true when
\gamma_{\mathtt{H}}+q_{\mathtt{HL}}-\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
increases with an increased
z_{\mathtt{S}}
, i.e., the relation

\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}_{1}})\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}_{1}})>\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}_{2}})\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}_{2}}),

must hold, which implies

\frac{\alpha z_{\mathtt{S}_{1}}+1-z_{\mathtt{S}_{1}}}{\alpha z_{\mathtt{S}_{2}}+1-z_{\mathtt{S}_{2}}}>\frac{\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}_{2}})}{\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}_{1}})}.

(23)

Similarly, the other quantity increases when
\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}+q_{\mathtt{LH}}-\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
decreases when
z_{\mathtt{S}}
is increased from
z_{\mathtt{S}_{1}}
to
z_{\mathtt{S}_{2}}
, i.e., when

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}_{1}})\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}_{1}})<\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}_{2}})\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}_{2}})

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha z_{\mathtt{S}_{1}}+1-z_{\mathtt{S}_{1}}}{\alpha z_{\mathtt{S}_{2}}+1-z_{\mathtt{S}_{2}}}<\frac{\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}_{2}})}{\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}_{1}})},

holds. Note that the above relation is in contradiction to the inequality obtained in (
23
). This proves that the two quantities of interest can not increase simultaneously. Proceeding along similar lines, it can be shown that it is impossible for the two quantities to decrease simultaneously. Thus, the only possibility is that there exists a constant
C
, such that

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{\mathtt{H}}+q_{\mathtt{HL}}-\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}{q_{\mathtt{LH}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{q_{\mathtt{HL}}}{\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}+q_{\mathtt{LH}}-\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}=C

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}{\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}=C,

holds for all
z_{\mathtt{S}}
.

Note that both
\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
and
\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
decrease monotonically as
z_{\mathtt{S}}
increases from
0
to
1
. Consequently, for the quantities
\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
and
\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
to remain constant for all
z_{\mathtt{S}}
, the state
\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
must be monotonically increasing in
z_{\mathtt{S}}
, unless
\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}})=0
identically holds for all
z_{\mathtt{S}}\in[0,1]
. It is easy to see that substituting
\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}})=0
in (
1
) (suppressing the dependency on
z_{\mathtt{S}}
), implies,

\dot{\mathtt{S}}=-\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}\mathtt{S}^{\star}-\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}\mathtt{S}^{\star}+\gamma_{\mathtt{H}}\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}+\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}=0,

resulting in
\gamma_{\mathtt{H}}\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}=-\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}
, which is not possible. Thus, (
22
) is preserved only when
\mathtt{S}^{\star}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
monotonically increases with
z_{\mathtt{S}}
. This in turn implies that sum of the infection states, i.e.,
\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})+\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
, monotonically decreases with an increase in
z_{\mathtt{S}}
. Exploiting this result, we conclude that
\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
monotonically decreases with an increase in
z_{\mathtt{S}}
, since
\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})+\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})=\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})+C\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
. Similarly,
\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
also monotonically decreases with
z_{\mathtt{S}}
. This concludes our proof.

Appendix C
Proof of Corollary
5.1

Proof.

Note that Assumption
3.5
is not violated in the limiting case of
q_{\mathtt{LH}}=0
. GAS of
\textbf{E}^{\mathtt{H},\mathtt{L}}_{1}
and
\textbf{E}^{\mathtt{H},\mathtt{L}}_{3}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
follows directly from Theorem
3.2
, and Lemma
3.3
and Theorem
3.8
, with the additional condition of
q_{\mathtt{HL}}<\frac{\beta_{\mathtt{H}}\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}}{\beta_{\mathtt{L}}}-\gamma_{\mathtt{H}}
in the case of endemic equilibrium.

For a given
z_{\mathtt{S}}\in[0,1]
, existence of
\textbf{E}^{\mathtt{H},\mathtt{L}}_{2}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
is determined by the proportion
\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}
. The condition for its existence is
0<\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}:=\frac{\mathcal{N}^{\mathtt{L}}_{1}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}{\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}=1-\frac{\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}}{\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}<1
, or equivalently,
\frac{\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}{\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}}>1
.
The Jacobian matrix at endemic equilibrium
\textbf{E}^{\mathtt{H},\mathtt{L}}_{2}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
reduces to

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\textbf{E}^{\mathtt{H},\mathtt{L}}_{2}):=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})\!-\!\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}\!-\!\gamma_{\mathtt{H}}\!-\!q_{\mathtt{HL}}&0\\
-\!\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}\!-\!q_{\mathtt{HL}}&\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})\!-\!2\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}-\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}\end{bmatrix},

whose eigenvalues are
\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})(1-\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}})-\gamma_{\mathtt{H}}-q_{\mathtt{HL}}
and
\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})-2\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})\mathtt{I}^{\star}_{\mathtt{L}}-\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}
. The former eigenvalue, on substitution from (
15
) simplifies to
\frac{\beta_{\mathtt{H}}\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}}{\beta_{\mathtt{L}}}-q_{\mathtt{HL}}-\gamma_{\mathtt{H}}
. The latter reduces to
\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}-\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
. To guarantee local stability, the real parts of both the eigenvalues should be negative, which yields the conditions of
q_{\mathtt{HL}}>\frac{\beta_{\mathtt{H}}\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}}{\beta_{\mathtt{L}}}-\gamma_{\mathtt{H}}
, and
\frac{\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}{\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}}>1
. Since the GAS of
\textbf{E}^{\mathtt{H},\mathtt{L}}_{1}
and
\textbf{E}^{\mathtt{H},\mathtt{L}}_{3}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
are ensured by
\max\bigg(\frac{\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}{\gamma_{\mathtt{H}}+q_{\mathtt{HL}}},\frac{\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}{\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}}\bigg)<1
, and
\frac{\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{H}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}{\gamma_{\mathtt{H}}+q_{\mathtt{HL}}}>1
and
q_{\mathtt{HL}}<\frac{\beta_{\mathtt{H}}\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}}{\beta_{\mathtt{L}}}-\gamma_{\mathtt{H}}
, respectively, the above derived conditions of
\frac{\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}{\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}}>1
and
q_{\mathtt{HL}}>\frac{\beta_{\mathtt{H}}\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}}{\beta_{\mathtt{L}}}-\gamma_{\mathtt{H}}
also guarantee the uniqueness of equilibrium
\textbf{E}^{\mathtt{H},\mathtt{L}}_{2}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
.

We now establish GAS of
\textbf{E}^{\mathtt{H},\mathtt{L}}_{2}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
when it exists. When
q_{\mathtt{LH}}
reduces to zero, Assumption
3.5
and the proof of Theorem
3.8
remain valid. Consequently, by Dulac’s Criterion we rule out the existence of closed orbits lying entirely in the interior of
(0,1)^{2}
. Now, at the boundary of
\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}=0
, we observe
\dot{\mathtt{I}}_{\mathtt{H}}=0
holds, implying that
\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}=0
is invariant. Thus, the dynamics reduces to

\dot{\mathtt{I}}_{\mathtt{L}}=\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(1-\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}})\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}}-\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{L}},

i.e., (
13
) reduces to a one-dimensional system, which in turn rules out the possibility of closed orbits at the boundary
\mathtt{I}_{\mathtt{H}}=0
. Since
\textbf{E}^{\mathtt{H},\mathtt{L}}_{2}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
is the only locally stable equilibrium under
q_{\mathtt{HL}}>\frac{\beta_{\mathtt{H}}\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}}{\beta_{\mathtt{L}}}-\gamma_{\mathtt{H}}
and
\frac{\hat{\beta}_{\mathtt{L}}(z_{\mathtt{S}})}{\gamma_{\mathtt{L}}}>1
, in the absence of any closed orbits, all trajectories with initial conditions in
(0,1)^{2}
of the two-dimensional system converge to
\textbf{E}^{\mathtt{H},\mathtt{L}}_{2}(z_{\mathtt{S}})
, i.e., the equilibrium is GAS. This completes our proof.
∎
