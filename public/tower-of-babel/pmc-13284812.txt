Relationship between cognitive failures, sleepiness and road traffic incidents in patients with untreated obstructive sleep apnoea and controls
Authors: Akshay Dwarakanath, Dipansu Ghosh, Vinod Palissery, Samantha Jamson, Mark William Elliott
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCurrent guidelines emphasise assessing “excessive sleepiness” and severity of sleep disordered breathing (SDB) when advising patients with obstructive sleep apnoea about driving. However, accidents can occur due to lack of concentration and poor reactions. The Cognitive Failures Questionnaire (CFQ) measures self-reported cognitive deficits in the completion of simple everyday tasks. We investigated the relationship between drivers’ cognitive capability and the severity of SDB, sleepiness and self-reported driving incidents.Methods348 patients (n=150, exploratory; n=198, validation) considered for a trial of continuous positive airway pressure and 173 controls (exploratory (n=105), validation (n=68)) matched for age and driving experience completed questionnaires that included the CFQ and a driving questionnaire.ResultsCompared to controls, patients had a higher total mean±sd CFQ score (37±18 versus 28±12, p=<0.0001, 95% CI −13.3– −7.3) and more self-reported driving incidents (accidents and/or near misses in the last year) (38% versus 10%; OR 2.6, 95% CI 2.79–8.34; p=0.0005). Sleepiness, rather than severity of SDB, predicted a higher CFQ score in patients compared to controls, both in the exploratory and validation cohorts. Self-reported driving incidents were significantly more frequent in patients who reported a higher degree of sleepiness (Epworth Sleepiness Score (ESS) >10 40% versus ESS <10 20%, OR 2.6, 95% CI 1.5–4.5; p=0.0005). CFQ score was also a predictor of driving incidents.ConclusionsSleepiness and cognitive dysfunction, rather than severity of SDB, predicts driving incident risk. The CFQ may identify a different aspect of accident risk and therefore may be useful in assessing fitness to drive in patients with obstructive sleep apnoea syndrome.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) is a common disorder with increasing prevalence [1] affecting up to one billion people globally with far-reaching health implications [2]. Untreated OSA is associated with increased road traffic incidents (RTIs) [3–5]. Many patients with OSA drive, some for professional reasons. Advising them about driving is a key issue in management and clinicians find this challenging. There is considerable variation in the advice that a patient is likely to receive in the absence of validated objective tests [6]. Recommendations about fitness to drive for patients with OSA focus primarily on the severity of the sleep-disordered breathing (SDB) and sleepiness [7, 8]. However, driving a vehicle is a skill that involves complex integrated higher cortical function alertness, concentration, physical ability and hand-to-eye coordination.

The causes of RTIs in OSA patients are multifactorial [9, 10], including reduced concentration and poor decision-making. Links have been shown between cognitive failures and road traffic accidents [11–14] and untreated OSA results in cognitive dysfunction [15–18]. Emerging evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses of observational studies show that patients with OSA were 26% more likely to develop cognitive impairment compared to others [19]. Studies have shown deficits in various domains such as executive functions, memory, alertness and attention [20–24]. Impairment in attention plays a pivotal role in all aspects of cognitive deficits and thereby contributes to the poor performance of patients with OSA when compared to that of healthy individuals [21, 25]. Furthermore, in patients with OSA with preserved cognitive function, brain activation is increased compared with that in healthy controls performing the same task, suggesting that in these patients increased cerebral recruitment is required to maintain cognitive performance [26]. Current guidance about whether patients with OSA may drive suggests differences in approach [7, 8]. The European Respiratory Society task force statement [27] recommends the need to identify cost-effective and reliable tests to assess driving ability objectively.

We therefore investigated the impact of cognitive dysfunction upon incidents while driving by using the Cognitive Failure Questionnaire (CFQ) [28], which is a cognitive assessment tool that measures self-reported deficits in the completion of simple everyday tasks. It encompasses failures in attention, memory, names, perception and motor functions. The CFQ has been shown to predict accidents (automobile and work-related) and job performance ratings in electrical workers [29]. Allahyari et al. [30] suggested that the CFQ could be used to identify drivers susceptible to driving errors.

Aims of the study

1) To compare the total CFQ score and self-reported driving incidents between patients with OSA/obstructive sleep apnoea syndrome (OSAS) and controls.

1) To compare the total CFQ score and self-reported driving incidents between patients with OSA/obstructive sleep apnoea syndrome (OSAS) and controls.

2) To explore the relationship between CFQ score versus general sleepiness and severity of SDB.

2) To explore the relationship between CFQ score versus general sleepiness and severity of SDB.

3) To assess if CFQ score can predict accident risk in patients with OSA/OSAS.

3) To assess if CFQ score can predict accident risk in patients with OSA/OSAS.

Methods

Patients attending the sleep clinic at St. James's University Hospital, Leeds, UK with a confirmed diagnosis of OSA (apnoea–hypopnea index (AHI) and/or oxygen desaturation index (ODI) 4% dips in saturation >5 events·h–1) on a respiratory variable overnight sleep study (Embletta, Resmed; Abingdon, Oxford) or overnight oximetry, who drove regularly on motorways, urban roads and rural roads, held a full valid UK driving licence, and who were considered for a continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) trial were approached. A trial with CPAP was considered in patients with mild OSA who were excessively sleepy and in moderate–severe OSA irrespective of sleepiness, as per UK guidance [31]. Patients were divided into two groups on the basis of their Epworth Sleepiness Score (ESS), as follows: 1) OSA and an ESS of <10 (OSA) and 2) OSA with an ESS >10 (OSAS). Healthy volunteers with no symptoms of OSA and with an ESS of <10 were recruited as controls. Patients and volunteers with other causes of cognitive decline, fatigue, excessive sleepiness, shift workers, chronic sleep deprivation and patients using sedatives/hypnotics were excluded. Participants in both groups were provided with an information leaflet and written consent was obtained. Ethics approval for this study was obtained from the NHS Research Ethics Committee (09/H1311/58).

Questionnaire

All subjects completed a questionnaire about driving (see the online supplement). This was developed with the input of patients and healthcare professionals working in the field. Participants were informed that responses were anonymised and would not be used to make fitness-to-drive decisions. The questionnaire included baseline demographics, ESS and driving incidents (near misses in the previous year and driving accidents). All completed the CFQ, which enquired how often they make various common errors on a five-point scale from 0 (never), 1 (very rarely), 2 (occasionally), 3 (quite often) and 4 (very often). The CFQ was scored by adding up the ratings for 25 questions, with the maximum possible total of 100; high scores indicated a higher incidence of cognitive failures.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out using Graph Pad Prism 8 software (San Diego, CA, USA) and SPSS statistics (version 24; IBM, New York, USA). The level of significance was set at p=<0.05. Patients and controls were divided into exploratory and validation cohorts. In the exploratory phase (150 patients and 105 controls) the CFQ scores were explored between controls, OSA and OSAS. One-way ANOVA and a multiple comparison test (Bonferroni) was used to evaluate demographic differences and the CFQ score between the three groups. Spearman's correlation was used to evaluate the relationship between ESS and ODI. Binary logistic regression analysis was used to test the hypothesis that an accident or near miss could be predicted from the CFQ score, thereby exploring the possibility of developing a predictive model. Binary logistic regression analysis was also used to test the hypothesis that driving incidents were more likely related to sleepiness specific to driving rather than sleepiness in general or the severity of SDB. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to calculate the discriminative power of the model and identify optimal cutoffs for a probability score. The sensitivity, specificity and predictive powers of the model were calculated using the cutoff values. The model was then applied to the validation population (198 patients and 68 controls) and an ROC curve was constructed. The area under the two ROC curves was compared using Z statistic [32]. A chi-squared test with Fisher's exact test was performed to evaluate the association between driving incidents, the severity of SDB and general sleepiness.

Comparing total CFQ score and self-reported driving incidents between patients and controls

Patients (OSA/OSAS), as compared to controls, had a higher total mean±sd CFQ score (37±18 versus 28±12, p=<0.0001, 95% CI −13.3– −7.3) and self-reported more driving incidents (accidents and/or near misses in the last year) compared to controls (38% versus 10%; OR 2.6, 95% CI 2.79–8.34; p=0.0005). There was no association between reported driving incidents and the severity of SDB (mild SDB 39% versus moderate-severe SDB 35%, OR 0.82, 95% CI 0.49–1.39; p=0.49). However self-reported driving incidents were significantly more frequent in patients who reported a higher degree of sleepiness (ESS >10 40% versus ESS <10 20%, OR 2.6, 95% CI 1.5–4.5; p=0.0005).

CFQ as a predictor of incidents while driving in patients

Exploratory cohort

150 patients were included in the exploratory model. Binary regression analysis was performed to develop a predictive model for self-reported driving-related incidents (accidents and/or near misses in the last year) based on CFQ score. The positive predictive value for this model was 56.76% (95% CI 42.78–69.74%) and the negative predictive value was 64.60% (95% CI 59.76–69.16%). The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.67 (95% CI 0.58–0.74) (figure 1). There was a significant correlation between total CFQ score and ESS (R=0.45, p=<0.0001) but a weak correlation with ODI (R=0.18, p=0.02).

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) in the exploratory cohort, with the Cognitive Failures Questionnaire (CFQ) as a predictor of driving incidents.

Validation cohort

192 patients were included in the validation model as six patients had to be excluded due to missing driving incidents data. The results for the total CFQ score were similar to those for the exploratory study. There was a significant correlation between total CFQ score and ESS (R=0.40, p=< 0.0001) and a weak and insignificant correlation with ODI (R=0.06, p=0.39). The AUC was 0.65 (figure 2), similar to the exploratory model (Z statistic p=0.74, hence no significant difference), suggesting the model is valid in a different population.

Discussion

Clinical evaluation of driving risk should include multiple parameters such as patient self-reported sleepiness at the wheel including validated questionnaires and occurrence of sleepiness-related accidents. RTIs can occur for various reasons and there has been a growing interest in the understanding of cognitive deficits in SDB and in this observational prospective comparative study we have evaluated this further and its possible impact on driving.

We have confirmed that untreated patients with OSAS are more likely to report a near miss and/or an RTI in the last year compared to controls. Our data are consistent with a retrospective case series observational study by Ward et al. [9], which suggests that the severity of sleepiness rather than of the SDB is associated with increased accident risk. In addition, our data suggests that an assessment of cognitive function is important. In patients with OSAS, a high CFQ score is associated with a near miss and/or an accident in the last year. In both the exploratory and the validation cohorts, we have shown that, rather than the severity of SDB, it is sleepiness that increases both cognitive dysfunction and accident risk. The presence of sleepiness (ESS >10) was associated with a higher CFQ score and this finding was similar in both the exploratory and validation cohorts. Furthermore, there was a significant good correlation between CFQ and ESS but a weak and insignificant correlation with ODI. The lower CFQ score and ESS in the severe SDB group compared to the mild group was not surprising as any patient with severe SDB, irrespective of excessive sleepiness, was offered a CPAP trial, whereas CPAP was only offered to patients with mild SDB and intrusive sleepiness.

The UK Driver Vehicle Licensing Agency (DVLA) and the EU directive both place emphasis on the severity of SDB, based on AHI or equivalent measures [7–8, 27], when determining how patients with OSA should be advised about driving. The study by Ward et al. [9] and data presented here suggest that the severity of the SDB, by itself, is not relevant. In our study, there was no difference in the percentage of participants reporting accidents and/or near misses between those with mild SDB compared to those with moderate or severe SDB. General sleepiness appears to be more relevant; however, even so, a fifth of patients with an ESS of less than 10 reported an accident or near miss in the last year. These figures could be considered unacceptably high, being approximately twice the rate seen in controls, raising the question of whether driving should be advised against in any patient with OSA of sufficient severity to warrant a trial of CPAP. This would be in keeping with the EU Directive regarding the importance of treatment deemed necessary by a physician. It does however have the danger of driving the problem underground, with patients not seeking medical advice or not trying CPAP for fear that they might lose their licence.

The DVLA guidance suggests that, in the absence of significant sleepiness, other symptoms, specifically impaired concentration, should be taken into account when advising patients about driving. Our data suggest that impaired cognitive function and sleepiness are closely linked and that, in the absence of sleepiness, patients are unlikely to have significant problems with cognitive function. However, to confirm this, studies need to be extended to patients with mild SDB but no sleepiness. Patients may underestimate sleepiness, deliberately or inadvertently, and the CFQ may be a useful surrogate marker for ESS; a high CFQ score in a patient with a low ESS raises the possibility that a patient is underestimating their degree of sleepiness.

Other questionnaires have been used to identify cognitive dysfunction in patients with OSA [16–18], but none have investigated the impact upon RTIs. It is possible that others may perform better and this needs further investigations. One of the strengths of this study was that the questionnaires were anonymised, making it more likely participants would tell the truth. It is still possible that they may not have answered the questionnaires honestly or forgotten incidents, but the rate of accidents in our study was similar to that reported in other studies [3–4]. The fact that the CFQ was predictive of accidents and also correlated with sleepiness suggests that it could be used to help identify the at-risk driver. Patients may realise that admitting to sleepiness, particularly while driving, increases the risk that they will be advised to surrender their licence, whereas the CFQ effectively provides similar information, but in a less obvious, and therefore less threatening, manner. However, the CFQ still relies on patient self-report. Objective tests of sleepiness as predictors of driving risk are disappointing and the use of objective, nonquestionnaire-based tests of cognitive function might be more effective and are worthy of further investigation. Driving simulators also hold promise [33] but do not accurately predict accident risk and are only recommended as part of a multi-faceted approach by an experienced clinician [27]. Artificial intelligence may also have a role in identifying the at-risk driver.

Study limitations

Firstly, this was a single-centre cohort study and we only recruited patients with OSA of sufficient severity to warrant a trial of CPAP; this is the group in which a risk assessment needs to be made about an individual's continued driving. It would not be appropriate to say that patient did not have OSA of sufficient severity to warrant treatment, but severe enough that they should not drive. Driving incidents were twice as likely in patients with OSA compared to controls. Further study is needed of the group with mild SDB as we recruited only those with significant sleepiness, because they are currently considered to be those at increased risk of having an accident while driving. Patients with mild SDB and a low ESS may still be at increased risk of cognitive dysfunction and accidents. Secondly, controls did not undergo objective screening for SDB. However, the questionnaire included questions about symptoms of SDB including excessive sleepiness and OSA and controls were only included if they had a very low clinical probability for OSAS. Whilst we cannot discount OSA, though unlikely in absence of snoring, OSAS was discounted as any control with a sleepiness score of 10 or above were excluded and subjected to diagnostic tests. We think it is very unlikely that we included any controls with significant OSAS and the questionnaire data would support this. Patients either underwent an overnight oximetry or a limited channel sleep study. This is keeping in with current national practice. Thirdly, we used a questionnaire to assess cognitive function and this is subject to the same limitations as a questionnaire about driving behaviours. However, it has the advantage that it can be performed easily in patients attending a sleep clinic as it does not involve the same level of supervision as for an objective test. In practical terms, a complementary approach could be considered. If a patient admits to cognitive failures there is no need to follow this up with more time-consuming objective testing, which can be reserved for those who deny problems but in whom there is a high degree of concern. This approach requires further evaluation. We have not evaluated the impact of CPAP treatment on CFQ score; this too would be worthy of further study.

Conclusions

Our study suggests that the severity of SDB is not relevant when assessing untreated patients with OSA for accident risk, but that sleepiness and cognitive failures appear to be a much better predictor in patients referred for a trial of CPAP. Although correlated with sleepiness and a useful surrogate, the CFQ may also identify a different aspect of accident risk to sleepiness and therefore may be complementary to the assessment of sleepiness in assessing fitness to drive in patients with OSA and OSAS. Future research should address whether other questionnaires and nonquestionnaire-based objective tests of cognitive function are more discriminant and have a role in determining which patients with OSA are at increased risk of RTIs.
