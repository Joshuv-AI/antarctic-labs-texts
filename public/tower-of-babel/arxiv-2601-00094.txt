Bounds on Longest Simple Cycles in Weighted Directed Graphs via Optimum Cycle Means Thanks: The core ideas of this work were developed during the author’s graduate research in the late 1990s.
Ali Dasdan

Abstract
The problem of finding the longest simple cycle in a directed graph is NP-hard, with critical applications in computational biology, scheduling, and network analysis. Existing approaches include exact algorithms with exponential runtimes, approximation algorithms limited to specific graph classes, and heuristics with no formal guarantees. In this paper, we exploit optimum cycle means (minimum and maximum cycle means), computable in strongly polynomial time, to derive both strict bounds and heuristic estimates for the weight and length of the longest simple cycle in general graphs. The strict bounds can prune search spaces in exact algorithms while the heuristic estimates (the arithmetic mean λ a ​ v ​ g \lambda_{avg} and geometric mean λ g ​ e ​ o \lambda_{geo} of the optimum cycle means) guarantee bounded approximation error. Crucially, a single computation of optimum cycle means yields both the bounds and the heuristic estimates. Experimental evaluation on ISCAS benchmark circuits demonstrates that, compared to true values, the strict algebraic lower bounds are loose (median 80–99% below) while the heuristic estimates are much tighter: λ a ​ v ​ g \lambda_{avg} and λ g ​ e ​ o \lambda_{geo} have median errors of 6–13% vs. 11–21% for symmetric (uniform) weights and 41–92% vs. 25–35% for skewed (log-normal) weights, favoring λ a ​ v ​ g \lambda_{avg} for symmetric distributions and λ g ​ e ​ o \lambda_{geo} for skewed distributions.



1
Introduction

Finding the longest cycle in a graph (with
n
nodes and
m
edges) is
a fundamental combinatorial optimization problem with applications in
circuit design, scheduling, and network analysis. While the shortest
cycle can be found in polynomial time
(
Orlin and Sedeno-Noda 2017
)
, finding the
longest simple cycle (maximum length or maximum weight) is NP-hard

(
Garey and Johnson 1979
)
. Moreover, the problem resists efficient approximation:
it cannot be approximated within a factor of
n^{1-\epsilon}
for any

\epsilon>0
unless P=NP
(
Björklund et al. 2004
)
. Given this hardness,
practitioners rely on heuristics, branch-and-bound algorithms

(
Morrison et al. 2016
;
Stern et al. 2011
;
Cohen et al. 2020
)
, or exhaustive enumeration on
small graphs using exponential-time algorithms

(
Johnson 1975
;
Hawick and James 2008
;
Bauernöppel and Sack 2025
)
. Polynomial-time computable bounds on the
longest cycle can aid all these approaches by pruning search spaces,
verifying feasibility, or providing quick estimates.

This paper investigates the utility of
Optimum Cycle Means
,
specifically the minimum (
\lambda_{min}
) and maximum
(
\lambda_{max}
) cycle means, as a basis for such bounds.
The
key insight is that the cycle mean of any cycle, including the
longest, must lie between the minimum and maximum cycle
means
. Since these optimum cycle means can be computed efficiently
in theory (strongly polynomial
O(nm)
time) and in practice

(
Dasdan 2004
)
, they offer an attractive trade-off between computational
cost and bounding power. Crucially, a single computation yields both
the strict bounds and the heuristic estimates.

However, effectively leveraging this framework requires distinguishing
between the
strict bounds
derived from critical cycles and
the
heuristic estimates
based on cycle means. To evaluate the
robustness of these estimates, we consider multiple edge weight
distributions. Specifically, in addition to uniform weights, we
evaluate our bounds on graphs with log-normally distributed
weights. This is motivated by applications in digital circuit timing
analysis, where path delays often follow a log-normal distribution due
to the multiplicative nature of process variations
(
Khandelwal and Srivastava 2003
)
.

Our investigation yields two distinct findings:

1.

Strict bounds are guaranteed but loose:

We derive algebraic bounds linking longest cycles to “critical
cycles” (those achieving optimum cycle means). While theoretically
sound, critical cycles tend to be topologically short, making the
resulting lower bounds often loose (median 80–99% below true values).

2.

Heuristic estimates are tighter with bounded error:
We
propose two heuristic estimates: the arithmetic mean

\lambda_{avg}
and the geometric mean
\lambda_{geo}
of the
optimum cycle means. These guarantee bounded approximation error,
with accuracy depending on distribution skewness:
\lambda_{avg}

is preferable for symmetric (uniform) distributions, while

\lambda_{geo}
is preferable for skewed (log-normal) distributions.

Our specific contributions are as follows:

•

We derive formal algebraic bounds on the weight, length, and
mean of max-weight and max-length cycles based on optimum cycle
means (§
2
–§
7
);

•

We establish dual results for shortest cycles
(§
8
);

•

We propose heuristic estimates and bound tightness
analysis (§
9
);

•

We provide an extensive experimental evaluation on ISCAS
benchmark graphs with both uniform and log-normal edge weights,
analyzing how distribution properties affect bound quality
(§
11
);

•

We discuss the limitations and applications of the proposed
approach (§
12
and §
13
).

Related work is discussed in §
14
, and we
summarize our conclusions and avenues for future research in
§
15
.

2
Graph Basics

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a finite directed graph with
|V|=n
nodes
and
|E|=m
directed edges (arcs). The weight function
w:E\to\mathbb{R}
maps edges to real values (negative, zero, or
positive). We denote the weight of an edge
e=(u,v)
as
w(e)
or

w(u,v)
. The minimum and maximum individual edge weights are denoted
by
w_{min}
and
w_{max}
, respectively.

Without loss of generality, we assume
G
is strongly connected and
contains at least one cycle. If
G
is not strongly connected, the
analysis can be applied independently to each Strongly Connected
Component (SCC), which can be identified in
O(n+m)
time

(
Mehlhorn and Sanders 2008
)
.

The weight
w(C)
of a cycle
C
is the sum of its edge weights, and
its length
|C|
is the number of edges it contains. A cycle is termed
positive, zero, or negative based on the sign of
w(C)
. A cycle of
length 1 is a self-loop.

3
Optimum Cycle Means

Definition 3.1
.

The
cycle mean

\lambda(C)
of a cycle
C
is its average
edge weight:

\lambda(C)=\frac{\sum\limits_{e\in C}w(e)}{|C|}=\frac{w(C)}{|C|},

(1)

where
|C|\geq 1
.

Definition 3.2
.

The
maximum cycle mean

\lambda_{max}
and
minimum
cycle mean

\lambda_{min}
of
G
are the optimum values of cycle
means over all cycles in
G
. Formally,

\lambda_{max}=\max\limits_{C\in G}\lambda(C)\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{min}=\min\limits_{C\in G}\lambda(C).

(2)

Both are computable efficiently in
O(mn)
time
(
Dasdan 2004
)
, without
needing to enumerate over all cycles in
G
explicitly.

Optimum cycle means admit a linear programming formulation. For
example,
\lambda_{min}
is the maximum
\lambda
satisfying:

d(v)-d(u)\leq w(u,v)-\lambda,\quad\forall(u,v)\in E,

(3)

where
d(v)
represents a potential function on nodes. At optimality,

\lambda=\lambda_{min}
,
d(v)
becomes the shortest path distance
from an arbitrary source node under the modified weights

w^{\prime}(u,v)=w(u,v)-\lambda_{min}
, and
G
contains no negative cycles
under these modified weights.

4
Criticality

An edge
(u,v)
is termed
min-critical
if it lies on a
critical path in the linear program above, satisfying

d(v)-d(u)=w(u,v)-\lambda_{min}
. A cycle
C
is min-critical if

\lambda(C)=\lambda_{min}
; we denote such a cycle by
C_{min}
. The
subgraph
G_{min}
containing all min-critical edges is the
min-critical subgraph. Analogous definitions apply for

\lambda_{max}
, max-critical cycles (
C_{max}
), and the max-critical
subgraph.

Multiple critical cycles may exist with the same cycle mean but
different lengths. Since our bounds involve
|C_{min}|
and

|C_{max}|
, we can optimize over all critical cycles:

•

For
lower bounds
, take
\max
over all critical
cycles to obtain the tightest (largest) lower bound.

•

For
upper bounds
, take
\min
over all critical
cycles to obtain the tightest (smallest) upper bound.

While finding the longest or shortest critical cycle is also NP-hard,
optimum cycle mean algorithms naturally produce at least one critical
cycle as a witness. Running multiple algorithms or examining the
critical subgraph may yield critical cycles of varying lengths,
enabling tighter bounds.

5
Cycle Mean Inequalities

The definition of optimum cycle means implies the following
fundamental bounds for any cycle
C
:

Lemma 5.1

(Basic cycle mean bounds)
.

For any cycle
C
in
G
:

w_{min}\leq\lambda_{min}\leq\frac{w(C)}{|C|}\leq\lambda_{max}\leq w_{max},

(4)

or equivalently,

|C|w_{min}\leq|C|\lambda_{min}\leq w(C)\leq|C|\lambda_{max}\leq|C|w_{max}.

(5)

Proof.

The inequalities
\lambda_{min}\leq\lambda(C)\leq\lambda_{max}

follow by definition. The outer bounds hold because no cycle mean can
exceed the maximum individual edge weight or be less than the minimum
individual edge weight.
∎

As mentioned in §
2
, we assume
G
is strongly
connected, meaning it has at least one cycle. Hence, it is also
guaranteed that
G
contains at least one min-critical cycle
C_{min}

and one max-critical cycle
C_{max}
, such that
\lambda_{min}=\lambda(C_{min})
and
\lambda_{max}=\lambda(C_{max})
.

6
Longest Cycle Bounds: Basics

We distinguish two notions of “longest” cycle: a
max-weight
cycle
maximizes total weight
w(C)
, while a
max-length cycle

maximizes edge count
|C|
.

Let
L
denote a max-weight or a max-length cycle. Trivial bounds on

w(L)
can be derived from edge weights alone:

|L|w_{min}\leq w(L)\leq|L|w_{max}.

(6)

However, optimum cycle means provide tighter constraints:

Lemma 6.1

(Cycle mean bounds for longest cycles)
.

Let
L
be either a max-weight or max-length cycle in
G
. Then:

\lambda_{min}\leq\lambda(L)\leq\lambda_{max},

(7)

which implies:

|L|\lambda_{min}\leq w(L)\leq|L|\lambda_{max}.

(8)

Proof.

This is a direct application of Lemma
5.1
to the
specific cycle
L
.
∎

While the exact value of
|L|
is unknown,
Lemma
6.1
establishes necessary conditions
for the weight of
L
:

Corollary 6.1

(Sign conditions)
.

1.

\lambda_{min}>0\Rightarrow w(L)>0
.

2.

\lambda_{max}<0\Rightarrow w(L)<0
.

3.

\lambda_{min}=0\Rightarrow w(L)\geq 0
.

4.

\lambda_{max}=0\Rightarrow w(L)=0
.

7
Longest Cycle Bounds: Extensions

We can refine these bounds by analyzing the algebraic properties of
max-weight (
L_{w}
) and max-length (
L_{l}
) cycles separately.

Lemma 7.1
.

If
\lambda_{max}\geq 0
, the cycle mean of the max-weight cycle
dominates that of the max-length cycle:

\lambda(L_{w})\geq\lambda(L_{l}).

(9)

Proof.

If
\lambda_{max}=0
, all cycles have non-positive mean, so
w(L_{w})\leq|L_{w}|\lambda_{max}=0
. But since a zero-mean cycle exists,

w(L_{w})\geq 0
, hence
w(L_{w})=0
and
\lambda(L_{w})=0\geq\lambda(L_{l})
.

If
\lambda_{max}>0
, some cycle has positive weight, so
w(L_{w})>0
and
\lambda(L_{w})>0
. If
w(L_{l})\leq 0
, then
\lambda(L_{l})\leq 0<\lambda(L_{w})
. If
w(L_{l})>0
, then since
w(L_{w})\geq w(L_{l})

and
|L_{w}|\leq|L_{l}|
:

\lambda(L_{w})=\frac{w(L_{w})}{|L_{w}|}\geq\frac{w(L_{l})}{|L_{w}|}\geq\frac{w(L_{l})}{|L_{l}|}=\lambda(L_{l}).

∎

The condition
\lambda_{max}\geq 0
is necessary. When
\lambda_{max}<0
, the relationship between
\lambda(L_{w})
and
\lambda(L_{l})
is
indeterminate; the mean of the max-length cycle may exceed that of the
max-weight cycle depending on the distribution of edge weights.

7.1
Max-weight Cycles

Let
L_{w}
be a max-weight cycle and
\mathcal{C}_{max}
be the set of
all max-critical cycles
C_{max}
in
G
. By definition,
w(L_{w})\geq w(C)
for all cycles
C
in
G
.

Lemma 7.2

(Max-weight key inequality)
.

\lambda_{max}(|L_{w}|-|C_{max}|)\geq 0
.

Proof.

Since
L_{w}
maximizes weight,
w(L_{w})\geq w(C_{max})=|C_{max}|\lambda_{max}
for any
C_{max}\in\mathcal{C}_{max}
. From
Lemma
6.1
, we have
w(L_{w})\leq|L_{w}|\lambda_{max}
. Combining these yields:

|C_{max}|\lambda_{max}\leq w(L_{w})\leq|L_{w}|\lambda_{max}\implies\lambda_{max}(|L_{w}|-|C_{max}|)\geq 0.

∎

Theorem 7.1

(Max-weight cycle bounds)
.

We derive the following three cases from
Lemma
7.2
:

1.

If
\lambda_{max}>0
, then

|L_{w}|\geq\max\limits_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{max}}|C|

and

w(L_{w})\geq(\max\limits_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{max}}|C|)\lambda_{min}
.

2.

If
\lambda_{max}<0
, then

|L_{w}|\leq\min\limits_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{max}}|C|

and

w(L_{w})\leq(\min\limits_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{max}}|C|)\lambda_{max}
.

3.

If
\lambda_{max}=0
, then
w(L_{w})=0
.

Proof.

•

Case 1 (
\lambda_{max}>0
):
Dividing by

\lambda_{max}
preserves the inequality:
|L_{w}|\geq|C_{max}|
. Since this holds for
any

C_{max}
, it must
hold for the maximum length in the set. Thus,
|L_{w}|\geq\max\limits_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{max}}|C|
. This implies
w(L_{w})\geq(\max\limits_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{max}}|C|)\lambda_{min}
since

w(L_{w})\geq|L_{w}|\lambda_{min}
.

•

Case 2 (
\lambda_{max}<0
):
Dividing by negative

\lambda_{max}
reverses the inequality:
|L_{w}|\leq|C_{max}|
. Since this holds for
any

C_{max}
, it must
hold for the minimum length in the set. Thus,
|L_{w}|\leq\min\limits_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{max}}|C|
. This implies
w(L_{w})\leq(\min\limits_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{max}}|C|)\lambda_{max}
since

w(L_{w})\leq|L_{w}|\lambda_{max}
.

•

Case 3 (
\lambda_{max}=0
):
Then
w(C_{max})=0
,
so
w(L)\geq 0
. Since
\lambda_{max}=0
precludes positive
cycles,
w(L)=0
.

∎

7.2
Max-length Case

Let
L_{l}
be a max-length cycle and
\mathcal{C}_{min}
be the set of
all min-critical cycles
C_{min}
in
G
. By definition,
|L_{l}|\geq|C|
for all cycles
C
in
G
.

Lemma 7.3

(Max-length key inequality)
.

w(L_{l})\geq w(C_{min})|L_{l}|/|C_{min}|=|L_{l}|\lambda_{min}
.

Proof.

From
\lambda(L_{l})\geq\lambda_{min}=w(C_{min})/|C_{min}|
, multiply
both sides by
|L_{l}|
.
∎

Theorem 7.2

(Max-length cycle bounds)
.

We derive the following three cases from
Lemma
7.3
:

1.

If
\lambda_{min}>0
, then
w(L_{l})\geq\max\limits_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{min}}w(C)
.

2.

If
\lambda_{min}<0
, then no direct comparison between

w(L_{l})
and
w(C_{min})
is possible without knowing
|L_{l}|
,
so we fall back to the unconditional bounds in
Lemma
6.1
.

3.

If
\lambda_{min}=0
,
w(L_{l})\geq 0
as shown in
Corollary
6.1
.

Proof.

•

Case 1 (
\lambda_{min}>0
):
Since
\lambda_{min}>0
, multiplying
|L_{l}|\geq|C_{min}|
by
\lambda_{min}
yields

|L_{l}|\lambda_{min}\geq|C_{min}|\lambda_{min}=w(C_{min})
. By
Lemma
7.3
and since
C_{min}
is arbitrary,
this implies
w(L_{l})\geq\max\limits_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{min}}w(C)
.

•

Case 2 (
\lambda_{min}<0
):
The inequality
|L_{l}|\geq|C_{min}|
implies
|L_{l}|\lambda_{min}\leq|C_{min}|\lambda_{min}
. The lower bound weakens, so we rely on
the general upper bound
w(L_{l})\leq|L_{l}|\lambda_{max}
.

•

Case 3 (
\lambda_{min}=0
):
Proven above.

∎

Corollary 7.1

(Length bound for max-length cycles)
.

If
\lambda_{min}>0
, then

|L_{l}|\geq\frac{\max\limits_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{min}}w(C)}{\lambda_{max}}.

(10)

Proof.

By Theorem
7.2
,
w(L_{l})\geq w(C_{min})
when

\lambda_{min}>0
. By Lemma
6.1
,
w(L_{l})\leq|L_{l}|\lambda_{max}
. Since
\lambda_{min}>0
implies

\lambda_{max}>0
, dividing by
\lambda_{max}
yields the result.
∎

Remark 1

(Weight shifting)
.

Subtracting
\lambda_{min}
from all edge weights yields a
transformed graph with
\lambda^{\prime}_{min}=0
and
\lambda^{\prime}_{max}=\lambda_{max}-\lambda_{min}
. While this transformation alters the
max-weight cycle, it preserves the max-length cycle. Applying Case 3
of Theorem
7.2
to the transformed graph gives

w^{\prime}(L_{l})\geq 0
, which converts back to
w(L_{l})\geq|L_{l}|\lambda_{min}
, exactly the unconditional bound from
Lemma
6.1
. Similarly, applying
Corollary
7.1
fails since
w^{\prime}(C_{min})=0
yields only the trivial bound
|L_{l}|\geq 0
. This confirms
that when
\lambda_{min}\leq 0
, the weight-shifting technique
cannot improve upon the bounds from
Lemma
6.1
.

Shortest Cycle Bounds

Finding the minimum weight directed cycle is solvable in
O(nm)
time

(
Orlin and Sedeno-Noda 2017

. Notably, this state-of-the-art algorithm explicitly
computes
\lambda_{min}
as a preprocessing step to reweight the graph

min-weight
(
S_{w}
) and
min-length
(

)
cycles analogously to longest cycles. The following results parallel
Section

. We skip the proofs as they are
analogous.

S

(Min-weight key inequality)

9
Heuristic Estimates and Gap Analysis

While optimum cycle means provide strict bounds, they may be
loose. For heuristic search and when admissibility is not required, an
approximation closer to the true value
\lambda(L)
is desirable. We
introduce two estimators:

1.

Arithmetic mean
\lambda_{avg}=(\lambda_{min}+\lambda_{max})/2
.

2.

Geometric mean
\lambda_{geo}=\sqrt{\lambda_{min}\cdot\lambda_{max}}
, defined when
\lambda_{min}>0
.

Both estimators depend only on the optimum cycle means, which are
uniquely defined and efficiently computable.

We introduce both arithmetic and geometric means to handle different
weight distributions. For symmetric distributions (e.g., uniform), the
population mean lies at the center of the range, making

\lambda_{avg}
an appropriate estimator. For right-skewed
distributions (e.g., log-normal), the population mean lies below the
center, making
\lambda_{geo}
more suitable since it is always less
than or equal to
\lambda_{avg}
.

Proposition 9.1

(Arithmetic-geometric mean inequality)
.

When
\lambda_{min}>0
,
\lambda_{geo}\leq\lambda_{avg}
, with
equality if and only if
\lambda_{min}=\lambda_{max}
.

Proof.

This follows directly from the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality
applied to
\lambda_{min}
and
\lambda_{max}
.
∎

Proposition 9.2

(Absolute approximation error for
\lambda_{avg}
)
.

The absolute error of the estimator
\lambda_{avg}
is bounded as

|\lambda(L)-\lambda_{avg}|\leq(\lambda_{max}-\lambda_{min})/2
.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.1
,
\lambda_{min}\leq\lambda(L)\leq\lambda_{max}
. Since
\lambda_{avg}
is the midpoint, the
maximum deviation is half the interval width.
∎

We define the
weight dispersion
, denoted by
\delta
, as the ratio of
the weight range to the sum of the extreme weights:

\delta=\frac{\lambda_{max}-\lambda_{min}}{|\lambda_{max}+\lambda_{min}|},

(12)

assuming
\lambda_{max}+\lambda_{min}\neq 0
. When
\lambda_{max}=-\lambda_{min}
, the denominator vanishes and
\delta
is undefined.

Proposition 9.3

(Relative approximation error for
\lambda_{avg}
)
.

When
\delta
is defined, the relative error of the estimator

\lambda_{avg}
is bounded by
\delta
:

\frac{|\lambda(L)-\lambda_{avg}|}{|\lambda_{avg}|}\leq\delta.

(13)

Proof.

From Proposition
9.2
, the absolute error is
bounded by
(\lambda_{max}-\lambda_{min})/2
. Substituting the
definition
\lambda_{avg}=(\lambda_{min}+\lambda_{max})/2
into
the denominator yields:

\frac{|\lambda(L)-\lambda_{avg}|}{|\lambda_{avg}|}\leq\frac{(\lambda_{max}-\lambda_{min})/2}{|(\lambda_{min}+\lambda_{max})/2|}=\frac{\lambda_{max}-\lambda_{min}}{|\lambda_{max}+\lambda_{min}|}=\delta.

∎

As Propositions
9.2

and
9.3
establish,
\lambda_{avg}
provides
rigorous, instance-dependent error bounds. Similar but looser bounds
hold for
\lambda_{geo}
:

Proposition 9.4

(Absolute approximation error for
\lambda_{geo}
)
.

When
\lambda_{min}>0
, the absolute error of the estimator

\lambda_{geo}
is bounded as
|\lambda(L)-\lambda_{geo}|\leq\lambda_{max}-\lambda_{min}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.1
,
\lambda(L)\in[\lambda_{min},\lambda_{max}]
. By the AM-GM inequality,

\lambda_{geo}\in[\lambda_{min},\lambda_{max}]
. The maximum
distance between any two points in this interval is
\lambda_{max}-\lambda_{min}
.
∎

Proposition 9.5

(Relative approximation error for
\lambda_{geo}
)
.

When
\lambda_{min}>0
, the relative error of the estimator

\lambda_{geo}
is bounded as

\frac{|\lambda(L)-\lambda_{geo}|}{|\lambda_{geo}|}\leq\frac{\lambda_{max}-\lambda_{min}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{min}\cdot\lambda_{max}}}.

(14)

Proof.

\lambda_{max}\neq 0

\lambda_{min}>0

Experimental Results on Benchmark Graphs

11.1
Methodology

To evaluate our bounds on realistic and larger graphs, we utilized the
ISCAS benchmark circuits
(
Brglez and Fujiwara 1985
;
Brglez et al. 1989
;
Hansen et al. 1999
)
, a
standard test suite in electronic design automation. Due to the high
computational cost of the exact cycle enumeration required to
establish ground truth
(
Hawick and James 2008
)
, we selected a subset of smaller
instances from the
iscas-small
directory of the benchmark
repository
(
Dasdan 2014
)
. These graphs, listed in
Table
3
, range from 55 to 2,059 nodes and
contain between 4 and 84,851 cycles.

Since the original ISCAS benchmarks are unweighted logic circuits, we
assigned integer weights to edges using two different distributions to
test the robustness of our bounds:

1.

Uniform Distribution:
Edge weights were generated
uniformly at random in the range
[1,3000]
. This represents a
symmetric distribution with no skew.

2.

Log-Normal Distribution:
Edge weights were
generated using a log-normal distribution such that the range

[1,3000]
corresponds to the
6\sigma
interval (covering

\approx 99.7\%
of values). This models realistic circuit delay
distributions where delays are skewed and strictly positive.

Ground truth for the longest cycles (
L_{w},L_{l}
) was established via
explicit enumeration using the algorithm of Hawick and James

(
Hawick and James 2008
)
, implemented in C based on
(
Schauer 2008
)
. Optimum cycle
means were computed using Howard’s algorithm with the publicly
available implementation from
(
Dasdan 2015
)
, which was also used in

(
Dasdan 2004
)
.

Regarding computational efficiency, the optimum cycle means were
computed in under one second for all benchmarks using Howard’s
algorithm. The cycle enumeration (used only for validation) was the
bottleneck, taking several minutes for graphs with tens of thousands
of cycles; it did not finish after hours for larger ISCAS benchmark
graphs. In practice, the bounds would be used precisely to avoid such
enumeration.

11.2
Evaluation Metrics

To quantify the performance of our theoretical framework, we define two
metrics. First, the
bound relative error
(
\Delta
) measures
the looseness of our strict lower bounds relative to the true ground
truth values. For a true value
V
and a derived lower bound
B
, we
define
\Delta=(V-B)/V
. A value of
\Delta=0\%
indicates a
tight bound, while values approaching
100\%
indicate looseness.
Specifically, for max-weight cycles we evaluate:

\Delta_{w(L_{w})}=\frac{w(L_{w})-|C_{max}|\lambda_{min}}{w(L_{w})},\quad\Delta_{|L_{w}|}=\frac{|L_{w}|-|C_{max}|}{|L_{w}|},

(16)

and for max-length cycles:

\Delta_{w(L_{l})}=\frac{w(L_{l})-w(C_{min})}{w(L_{l})},\quad\Delta_{|L_{l}|}=\frac{|L_{l}|-w(C_{min})/\lambda_{max}}{|L_{l}|}.

(17)

Second, the
heuristic approximation error
(
\epsilon
)
measures the accuracy of our cycle mean estimators (
\lambda_{avg},\lambda_{geo}
) compared to the true mean of the longest cycle

\lambda(L)
. We define this as the standard relative error:

\epsilon=\frac{|\lambda(L)-\lambda_{est}|}{|\lambda(L)|},

(18)

where
\lambda_{est}\in\{\lambda_{avg},\lambda_{geo}\}
.

12
Limitations

While our experimental results demonstrate the efficacy of the
proposed approach, we acknowledge several limitations in the current
study, ranging from empirical constraints to theoretical boundaries.

Sensitivity to Weight Distributions.

As discussed in Section
11
, the performance
of our heuristic estimates exhibits a dependency on the underlying
edge weight distribution. While the method achieves accurate estimates
on symmetric (uniform) distributions, we observed degradation when
applied to graphs with skewed (log-normal) weight distributions. This
suggests that the heuristic is less effective when edge weight
variance is high.

Generalization to Other Domains.

Our evaluation used ISCAS benchmark circuits, real circuit topologies
with synthetically assigned edge weights. We restricted our evaluation
to a subset of instances because exact cycle enumeration did not
terminate on circuits that were either larger or contained too many
cycles. While ISCAS circuits are representative of digital hardware,
the structural properties of graphs in other domains (e.g., biological
pathways, social networks) may differ, potentially affecting the size
of the critical subgraph and hence the tightness of our bounds.

Scalability and Hardware Constraints.

Our experiments were constrained by the runtime of exhaustive cycle
enumeration, which was required to establish ground truth. As a
result, we restricted our analysis to graphs with at most one million
cycles. The optimum cycle means themselves were computed in under one
second for all benchmarks; practical applications of our bounds would
not require enumeration and thus would scale to much larger instances.

Complexity of Optimal Critical Cycles.

Regarding the theoretical application of our bounds, determining the
“best” critical cycles (e.g., the longest possible max-critical
cycle) is itself NP-hard. Nonetheless, since critical cycles lie
within the critical subgraph, restricting search to this subgraph can
substantially reduce the problem size. When the critical subgraph is
small enough, exhaustive cycle enumeration
(
Johnson 1975
;
Hawick and James 2008
;
Bauernöppel and Sack 2025
)

can become feasible.

Triviality on Uniform Weights.

Finally, our bounds are trivial for uniformly weighted or unweighted
graphs: if all edges have weight 1, then
\lambda_{min}=\lambda_{max}=1
.

13
Applications

Despite the limitations regarding sensitivity to weight distributions
discussed in Section
12
, our proposed bounds remain
highly effective for a wide range of practical domains where edge
weights are bounded and the graph topology supports short critical
cycles. In this section, we highlight three specific applications
where these conditions are satisfied.

Application to Branch-and-Bound.

A critical component of Branch-and-Bound algorithms for the longest
simple path or cycle is the
admissible heuristic
: an
optimistic estimate of the remaining path extension. Our bounds
naturally serve as admissible heuristics in such frameworks

(
Morrison et al. 2016
;
Stern et al. 2011
;
Cohen et al. 2020
)
. For example, when searching for
a cycle of length at most
k
, given a partial path of length
p
, at
most
k-p
edges remain to complete the cycle. The product
(k-p)\cdot\lambda_{max}
upper-bounds the weight contribution of these
remaining edges. While this estimate may loosen under heavy-tailed
weight distributions, it guarantees admissibility, enabling the
pruning of branches that cannot exceed the current best solution.

Application to Longest Simple Paths.

Our cycle bounds induce bounds on longest simple paths. Since any
cycle can be converted to a path by removing one edge, the properties
of the longest cycles (
L_{w},L_{l}
) impose strict lower bounds on the
longest paths:

Corollary 13.1

(Path bounds)
.

Let
P_{w}
be a max-weight simple path and
P_{l}
be a max-length simple path. Then:

\displaystyle|P_{l}|

\displaystyle\geq|L_{l}|-1\geq(\max\limits_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{max}}|C|)-1,

(19)

\displaystyle w(P_{w})

\displaystyle\geq w(L_{w})-w_{max}.

(20)

In the case where
\lambda_{max}>0
, we can substitute the bound
from Theorem
7.1
to obtain a computable lower bound
for the path weight:

w(P_{w})\geq(\max\limits_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{max}}|C|)\lambda_{min}-w_{max}.

(21)

This corollary is particularly useful for initialization in
Branch-and-Bound algorithms
(
Morrison et al. 2016
;
Stern et al. 2011
;
Cohen et al. 2020
)
. By
identifying a long or heavy critical cycle in polynomial time, one can
establish a high-quality lower bound (incumbent solution) for the
longest path problem before the search begins.

However, we note that these path bounds inherit the limitations
discussed in Section
12
. Critical cycles are typically
short (length 3–12 in our ISCAS experiments), while longest paths can
approach
n-1
edges, making the length bound loose in sparse
graphs. Furthermore, the weight bound on
w(P_{w})
can become negative
if
w_{max}
is sufficiently large (as in heavy-tailed distributions),
rendering it vacuous. Thus, these bounds are most powerful when the
graph contains long critical cycles and has bounded edge weights.

Application to Graph Structural Bounds.

Literature on cycle length bounds is extensive
(§
14
). Given a structural bound that guarantees
a cycle of length
\ell
for digraphs, our framework automatically
translates it into a weight bound
w(L)\geq\ell\lambda_{min}
. This
observation suggests that any advance in bounding cycle lengths for
specific classes of digraphs can be directly leveraged to bound cycle
weights via optimum cycle means.

14
Related Work

Optimum Cycle Means.

Dasdan 2004
surveys
O(mn)
-time algorithms for computing optimum
cycle means and presents their experimental analysis results. The
efficient implementations of most of those algorithms are available in

(
Dasdan 2015
)
.

Longest Paths and Cycles.

The Longest Path/Cycle problem is a classical NP-hard problem

(
Garey and Johnson 1979
)
and inapproximable within
n^{1-\epsilon}

(
Björklund et al. 2004
)
. Algorithmic approaches include color-coding

(
Alon et al. 1997
)
, which finds paths of length
O(\log n)
in
polynomial time, and improvements by
Björklund and Husfeldt 2003
. For the problem
of finding paths or cycles of a specific length
k
,
Monien 1985
and

Bodlaender 1993
propose fixed-parameter tractable algorithms that run in
linear time for fixed
k
but in exponential time for variable
k

(specifically
O(k!2^{k}n)

(
Bodlaender 1993
)
).

Structural Bounds.

Literature on cycle length guarantees is extensive, e.g.,

Chen and Zhang 1988
;
Ghouila-Houri 1960
;
Thomassen 1981
for directed graphs and

(
Erdős and Gallai 1959
;
Dirac 1952
;
Jansen et al. 2019
;
Anastos 2022
;
Fomin et al. 2024
)
for undirected
graphs. While these works focus strictly on length, our algebraic
framework bridges these structural guarantees to weighted bounds, as
discussed in §
13
.

Cycle Enumeration.

Johnson 1975
gives an output-sensitive algorithm for enumerating all
simple cycles;
Hawick and James 2008
extend it to multigraphs.
Gupta and Suzumura 2021

address bounded-length enumeration.

Heuristic Search.

Morrison et al. 2016
;
Stern et al. 2011
;
Cohen et al. 2020
develop A* and branch-and-bound
methods for longest simple paths using admissible heuristics. Our
bounds provide such heuristics via
\lambda_{max}
.

15
Conclusions

We have presented algebraic bounds for the weight and length of the
longest simple cycle based on optimum cycle means. While these bounds
do not yield constant-factor approximations, they are computable in
strongly polynomial time and provide necessary conditions for cycle
existence. Our experimental analysis on the ISCAS benchmarks yields
two practical conclusions:

First, the strict algebraic lower bounds are often loose (median
errors of 80–99%) regardless of the weight distribution, as critical
cycles (
C_{max}
) are typically short while longest cycles (
L_{w}
)
are long. However, the quality of
heuristic estimates

depends heavily on the distribution of edge weights. For symmetric
uniform distributions, the arithmetic mean
\lambda_{avg}
is an
accurate estimate, achieving median errors of 12.7% (
L_{w}
) and
5.8% (
L_{l}
). For skewed log-normal distributions (typical of circuit
delays),
\lambda_{avg}
degrades significantly, but the geometric
mean
\lambda_{geo}
provides a substantial improvement—reducing
median errors from 91.8% to 24.9% for max-length cycles.

Second, we observed that max-weight and max-length cycles are
structurally similar and often identical, particularly in the uniform
case. This suggests that algorithms designed to find long paths are
likely to find heavy paths in practice, and vice versa. Future work
lies in integrating these bounds into a live Branch-and-Bound solver
to empirically measure the reduction in search nodes and total runtime
for large-scale instances.
