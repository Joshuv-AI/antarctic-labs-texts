The effect of percussion and manual activation massage on explosive strength and balance in young adult males: A crossover pilot study
Authors: Peter Bartík, Martin Pacholek
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecently, the usage of percussive vibration machines in physiotherapy and sports has increased rapidly. Numerous manufacturers claim they can enhance physical performance. However, there is minimal peer-reviewed research on their efficacy. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effect of percussion massage (PM) on muscular performance, particularly explosive strength and balance. Moreover, this is the first study to evaluate the potential PM impact on balance.Materials and methods18 young male participants aged 20.89 ± 3.43 years with a BMI of 25.08 ± 3.95 completed three measurements with two interventions - PM by Theragun device (TG), activation massage (AM), and assessment without activation (WA). The targeted area was m. triceps surae, hamstrings, and m. quadriceps femoris of the dominant leg. Single-leg squat jump (SJ), countermovement jump (CMJ), and Y-balance tests (YBT) were performed. One-Way Repeated Measures ANOVA was utilized to analyze the data. The level significance threshold was set to p ≤ 0.05.ResultsNo statistically significant difference was reported between TG, AM, and WA in the height of the squat jump and countermovement jump (p > 0.05). Furthermore, no significant changes were recorded in the YBT performance score (p > 0.05).ConclusionsNo improvement was reported in the monitored physical parameters when the PM by Theragun or manual AM was applied for 90 s right before the muscular performance. Therefore, we do not recommend the short-term stimulative application of PM and AM before athletic performance when lower limb explosive strength or balance improvements are the objective.Keywords: Percussive massage, Theragun, Sport massage, Jump, Y-balance test, And performance

Background

Percussion massage (PM) has become very popular in recent years for both therapeutic use and sports practice. It is usually applied to increase flexibility and performance and accelerate recovery. Percussion massage guns are highly popular machines utilized for PM. The PM devices manufacturers state that these guns can exert powerful pulsating strokes affecting the deep muscle tissue. They are claimed to help to improve blood circulation, increase flexibility, reduce soft tissue pain, enhance regeneration, and positively stimulate the physical performance [1].

At this point, we need to divide the effect of the percussion and vibration therapy. While vibration therapy is a scientifically proven modality [[2], [3], [4]], PM is a considerably unexplored approach, except for its positive effect on flexibility [1] and has been the subject of intensive academic debate in the last years. The common devices one can find in the market are Theragun, Hypervolt, Turonic, ExoGun, TriggerPoint, and others. Most medical professionals use devices from two manufacturers: Hyperice® and Therabody® [5]. Therefore, the device chosen for our study to evaluate the PM was widely known as Therugun Pro [5].

Several studies have been published regarding the effects of PM devices with various conclusions. The majority is devoted to flexibility and range of motion, although this is unlikely the main target of common PM device users who seek mainly regeneration, pain relief, and performance improvement [5]. This is supported by the study of Cheatham et al., reporting that most participants assumed that PM stimulates myofascial mobility, enhances local blood flow, modulates pain, and decreases myofascial restrictions [5].

Yang et al. confirmed that PM could affect fascial structures [6]. The results revealed that the Hypervolt massage gun reduced the echo intensity within the thoracolumbar fascial area. This phenomenon was probably caused by a decrease in the hyaluronic acid viscosity in the loose connective tissue. PM applied on the paravertebral muscles didn't positively stimulate the lumbar range of motion but enhanced perceived stiffness and boosted temperature in the region [6]. Contrary to this finding, a relatively high number of recently published studies support the claim that PM increases flexibility and joint range of motion [1,[7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. Other research study reports a positive effect on muscular performance during the bench press exercise [13], which does not correspond with the finding of Konrad et al. [1]. The positive impact of PM on pain management is reported as well [7]. Handheld percussion devices have also become popular in warm-up routines aiming for performance improvement but without significant evidence [14].

Considering how popular these machines have become, there is surprisingly minimal peer-reviewed research on their efficacy in improving physical performance. Therefore, the primary focus of the present study was to assess the stimulation effect of the PM tool and compare it with other standard activation techniques used in physiotherapy and sports. It was decided to compare the PM with another widely applied modality - AM. We hypothesize that PM might have a significant positive impact on muscular performance. There are certain similarities between these two modalities. Although there is no evidence that sports massage improves performance directly, it may enhance flexibility and delayed onset muscle soreness [15]. This study aimed to investigate the PM effect on muscular performance, particularly explosive strength of the lower limbs and balance. Moreover, this is the first study to evaluate the potential PM impact on balance.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

The study was a single-blinded group, crossover-controlled with the dependent variables - height of the jump (cm) and distance reached (cm) in the YBT. The independent variables were manual AM, PM by Theragun (TG), and assessment without any activation (WA). The crossover study was controlled, with all participants randomly assigned to different treatment sequences. The washout period was the same for all participants and was established based on the sufficient length of effect of each intervention, thereby minimizing the carryover effect.

Subjects

Twenty-four volunteer male students from Prince Sultan University participated in this study. Medical records were obtained from all participants to exclude individuals with injuries that could impact the measurements. Participants who were unable to complete all three measurements within the specified period or who reported feeling unwell or experiencing muscle or joint discomfort were excluded from the study. Inclusion criteria included participants aged 18–25 years, willingness to comply with the study protocol (including attending sessions and completing assessments), no concurrent participation in any activities that would affect their performance at least two days before and during the testing protocol, and participants were supposed to be recreationally physically active. They all performed physical activity at least two times a week for 30 min. As a result, a total of 18 students who successfully completed all three measurements were included in this research study. The participants had an average age of 20.89 ± 3.43 years, a height of 177.28 ± 6.44 cm, a body mass of 79.06 ± 14.32 kg, and a body mass index of 25.08 ± 3.95. All students were informed about the assessment procedure and aim of the tests and had three tryouts before the first measurement. Each participant signed the written consent and was free to step out of the trial at any time.

The testing time was always the same from 12 to 1 pm and at the same place (The Physical Education Lab). All participants were asked not to exercise 48 h before testing and during the measurements for three consecutive days. The time between measurements for each participant was 24 h.

The percussive and manual activation massage procedures were applied on the dominant leg as follows: 30 s massage per muscle group. There were three muscle groups targeted.

The complete duration for one intervention was 90 s. All participants were lying down prone on the physiotherapeutic table during the procedure. They were instructed to be relaxed. Both PM and activation massage were performed by the same experienced physiotherapist from the proximal to the distal area and back with continuous tolerable pressure [1]. The Theragun Pro model (Therabody, USA) (Fig. 1) was used for PM with a speed of 1750 percussions per minute, with a standard head, as Theragun™ recommends. The Theragun was always in a vertical perpendicular position to the muscle. The activation massage was performed using effleurage and petrissage techniques with a minimum of Herbaflex massage cream.

Assessment methods

Demographic surveys and measurements were used to collect data. The survey was conducted on the first testing day and collected basic demographic information about the students, including their name, age, and health status. In the health status section, participants were asked about their injuries, health issues, current health status, and active participation in sports. After completing the survey, the investigator checked the participants' weight and height, which were measured using the same device: a Seca digital column scale, model 769 (Hamburg, Germany).

The procedure of assessment contained three phases. First, all students underwent a general treadmill warm-up with a velocity of 7:30 min/km for 5 min. Second, the intervention was applied or 2 min rest when participants had testing without activation on that day. Third, the testing of explosives and balance abilities. The SJ and CMJ were selected as reliable (Cronbach's α = 0.97 and 0.98) and valid tests (r = 0.81 and 0.87) for detecting the explosive strength of the lower limbs [16]. The Y-Balance Test Lower Quarter (YBT-LQ) was utilized to evaluate dynamic balance abilities. The intra-rater reliability of YBT-LQ ranges from 0.85 to 0.91 [17]. The same investigator always did the measurements in the same order: 1. Single leg SJ, 2. Single leg CMJ, and 3. YBT-LQ. A valid and reliable Optogate System (Microgate, Bolzano, Italy) was utilized to measure the height of the SJ and CMJ [18].

The SJ test provides information about lower limb muscular strength and power performance [19]. The students started barefoot from a squat position with a knee bent to 90° and a straight torso. They were supposed to remain in this position for 3 s, which were counted by the investigator before executing the jump [20,21]. In the push-off and flight phase, students were asked to perform a maximum high jump (effort) without countermovement, and the knee of the jumping leg had to be fully extended (180°). The landing phase was initiated by the toes of both feet in an upright position with fully extended jumping leg and knee. The hands stayed on the hips during the whole execution of the test [22]. Participants were jumping on their dominant jumping leg. The test was performed three times with a rest of 10–20 s, and the highest jump was recorded.

The CMJ assesses and determines the reactive strength and power of the lower limbs [19]. Students were asked to jump as high as possible with their maximal effort. An erect standing position with a fully extended knee of the jumping leg was a starting position for CMJ [23]. The push-off phase started with the downward countermovement (without an indication of depth) as fast as possible and with swinging arms [24]. During the flying and landing phase, the knee of the jumping leg had to be fully extended, and probands landed on both feet [25]. The jump height was measured, and the best from three attempts was recorded. Students had a rest of 10–20 s between jumps and executed it with the same dominant leg as in SJ.

The YBT-LQ was utilized to evaluate a student's strength, stability, and dynamic neuromuscular control. This standardized testing protocol has been shown to be reliable [17]. The whole procedure was executed barefoot and with lightweight clothing. Probands were instructed to place their dominant leg on the platform center (Y Balance Test Kit, FMS-FS477, USA) with the most distal foot part at the starting mark. Afterward, they were instructed to reach continuously as far as possible with the other leg in the three directions: first anterior, second posterolateral, and lastly posteromedial. Participants were supposed to keep balance, their arms on the hips, and not touch the floor with the reaching foot until they came back to the standing position. If the participant was unable to execute the reach six times, then he was withdrawn from the testing. Distances were measured from the most distal aspect of the toes of the dominant foot to the most distal aspect of the reaching foot in all three directions [26]. All three valid measurements in each direction were recorded together with the subject's length of the dominant leg (from the anterior superior iliac spine to the medial malleolus). Afterward, the YBT performance scores were calculated using the following formula: Absolute reach distance (cm) in one direction = (Reach 1 + Reach 2 + Reach 3)/3, and Absolute reach distance (cm) in all three directions= (maximal anterior reach + maximal posterolateral reach + maximal posterolateral reach 3)/3 and Relative (normalized) reach distance (%) = Absolute reach distance (cm)/limb length (cm) ∗ 100 [27].

Statistical analyses

We analyzed the obtained data using the statistical software IBM SPSS 25. The mean and standard deviation (SD) were reported to present the measurements' results. The Shapiro-Wilk test was utilized to determine the normality of all variables, with the results showing that data were normally distributed (p > 0.05). For reliability of measurements, the two-way random effects intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was performed when values less than 0.5 are indicative of poor reliability, values between 0.5 and 0.75 indicate moderate reliability, values between 0.75 and 0.9 indicate good reliability, and values greater than 0.90 indicate excellent reliability [38]. One-Way Repeated Measures ANOVA test was performed; if there was a significant difference, the Bonferroni Post Hoc was applied to find differences between AM, TG, and WA results in the vertical jump and YBT tests. The significance threshold level was set at p ≤ 0.05. In the case of a significant post hoc test, for interpretation of the practical significance of the study outcomes, the effect size (ES) was reported. Cohen defined effect sizes as small, d = 0.2; medium, d = 0.5; and large, d = 0.8 [28]".

Discussion

The results show no statistically significant difference between TG, AM, and WA in the height of the squat jump and countermovement jump. Furthermore, no significant changes were reported in the YBT performance score after anterior, posterolateral, posteromedial, and maximal reach in all three directions. Based on our findings, we conclude that PM and manual AM applied right before physical activity do not provide additional benefits to muscular performance when assessing strength and balance. Therefore, our hypothesis that PMT might affect muscular activity was not confirmed. We evaluated the muscle strength by the SJ test, followed by the CMJ test, and balance by the YBT. No significant improvement was reported in the monitored physical parameters when the Theragun or manual activation massage was applied. Similarly, there was no improvement when measurement with no intervention was performed. Therefore, we do not recommend the usage of PM before athletic performance when muscular strength or balance enhancement is the objective. A similar PM effect on muscle performance was reported by Konrad et al. [1] when percussive massage treatment did not improve the muscle strength of plantar flexors, while it affected the range of motion positively. The time of PM was 5 min, while in our case, it was 30 s per muscle group. No PM effect on the vertical jump was reported by Byrne et al. [29] with the same 30 s PM duration per one muscle group, while both lower extremities were involved in the intervention and jump. In our case, there was no improvement in CMJ, and SJ reported while only a dominant leg was involved in the assessment. Similarly, the study of Alvarado et al. [12] revealed that athletes who use the PM prior to countermovement jump and drop jump would not have any adverse effects on performance. However, only a positive effect on the range of motion was detected. Moreover, Szymczyk et al. [30] reported that PM might contribute to a slight reduction in the height of subsequent drop jumps when applied before, so authors do not recommend using it before athletic performance. The PM was applied for 60 s on the Achilles tendon. In our study, we did not report any performance drop when the PM was used. The opposite result was presented in the study by García-Sillero et al. [13], reporting a positive effect of PM on deferring the motion loss in the bench press exercise. The PM was performed right after the completion of the final repetition at the end of each set. The authors focused on the bilateral pectoral muscles operating along the fibers from the origin to the insertion, similar to the present study for 15 s. This was paradoxically lower duration than 30 s period applied in our study with no effect on muscular performance. A possible explanation for this fact is that the researchers applied PM at the end of each set, while we applied it once, right before the performance as recommended by the manufacturer.

Bayer and Eken [31] evaluated the effect of various duration sport massages on CMJ and SJ performance. Five, ten, and 15-min massages were compared, with the highest positive effect for the shortest (5 min) one. The area of application was the calf, quadriceps, adductor, hamstring, and hip rotator. In our study, we did not report any improvement in CMJ and SJ with the shorter massage of 90 s duration (30 s per muscle group) applied on the calf, hamstring, and quadriceps. This finding corresponds with the result of the systematic review and meta-analysis [15], reporting no evidence that massage improves jump performance.

We evaluated the effect of PM and activation massage on balance by the YBT with no reported effect from neither of these interventions. The present study was the first one to measure the PM effect on balance. No studies have been published on this topic yet for comparison to our findings. Similarly, we did not report any balance improvement when AM was applied on a dominant leg prior to testing. A partially positive effect on the dynamic balance of clinical massage applied on the lumbar spine was reported by Trampas et al. [32]. Similarly, Vaillant et al. [33] reported improvement in balance after manual treatment applied to the feet and ankles, while half of the allocated time was for massage and the other half for joint mobilization. An explanation for this fact is that activation massage aims to activate and prepare muscles right before a performance. It is considerably shorter in duration. In this study, we applied it for 90 s (30 s per muscle group) with high intensity, while clinical therapeutic massage lasts longer and might enhance muscle proprioception, as reported by Shin and Sung [34]. De Benito et al. [35] measured the effect of the casual and vibration foam roller with the result of an improved Y-balance test in participants in both cases. However, no significant dissimilarities were noticed between casual and vibration rollers. Hemmati, Rojhani-Shirazi and Ebrahimi [36] reported that combined deep massage and stretching resulted in a reduction of postural balance compared with stretching alone. We did not find any significant positive or negative change when AM was applied.

This study has its limitations as well. We measured the effects of interventions on balance and muscle strength in one session. The direct impact of a single intervention should not be compared with the long-term result of a prolonged one. Therefore, further controlled trials with a larger number of participants, longer repetitive treatment duration, and different areas of application are needed. Furthermore, the subjects in this experiment were young adult males, so studies focused on other population groups are needed. Another limitation is the potential difference in the usage of a specific percussive massage device and its frequency or amplitude. While we utilized the Theragun Pro machine as one of the leaders on the market, the other widely used and popular devices, like Hypervolt, might be different in parameters, percussion treatment quality, and overall effect.

We have to mention that PM might cause health problems as well [37]. Although we did not report any harmful impact during the experiment, further investigation is required to explore the indications, contraindications, potential benefits, and especially negative reactions of the human body to PM. Our primary objective was to assess the PM effect by measuring balance and explosive strength of lower limbs, so the other methods of evaluation are recommended for future studies as well. The various lengths of PMT application should be the subject of future studies, as well.

Conclusion

No statistically significant difference between TG, AM, and WA in the height of the squat jump and countermovement jump was reported. Furthermore, no significant changes were reported in the YBT performance score. The PMT by Theragun and AM were applied for 30 s per muscle group and 90 s in total right before the assessment. Therefore, we do not recommend the short-term stimulative application of PM and AM before athletic performance when lower limb explosive strength or balance improvements are the objective.
