Plasma nitrogen fixation for future lunar agriculture
Authors: Toshiro Kaneko, Shota Sasaki, Daiki Suzuki, Hayato Ohkuma, Atsushi Higashitani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHere, we introduce the potential of plasma technology for future lunar agriculture. The use of discharge plasma to convert nitrogen, oxygen, and water into reactive species for nitrogen fixation is discussed. This study demonstrated the effectiveness of dinitrogen pentoxide as a nitrogen fertilizer for rice cultivation in lunar regolith simulants. This technology also shows promise in regulating plant growth and enhancing plant immunity, addressing the challenges of lunar agriculture.Subject terms: Biotechnology, Plant sciences

Introduction

The Artemis mission is a NASA-led international effort, and one of its goals is to return humans to the Moon and develop innovative technologies for Mars exploration. The European Space Agency (ESA) also plans to design a lunar base that will allow continuous manned activity on the Moon. In addition, the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA) has compiled and released a concept for a “lunar farm”1 (Fig. 1A). The development elements necessary to support manned lunar activities include the exploration and recycling of lunar water resources, air generation in pressurized zones, and the development of food production technology through lunar agriculture to support long-term manned activities. Securing food supplies is one of the main challenges of long-term human habitation and exploration missions on the Moon and Mars. Current space missions rely on supplies from Earth; however, this is not sustainable in the long term. In particular, as manned exploration of the lunar surface is promoted as the next step in space exploration, the development of technology for lunar farms to locally produce food is becoming increasingly important. Furthermore, many space-oriented scientific and technological developments, including the efficient use and recycling of resources, contribute to planetary sustainability.

The most popular theory regarding the formation of the Moon is the “Giant Impact Hypothesis,” which posits that the Moon was formed ~4.5 billion years ago when a Mars-sized body, Theia, collided with proto-Earth. This would result in similar rock compositions for both the Earth and Moon. Subsequently, various microorganisms, plants, and life forms have emerged on Earth, transforming dirt and rocks into rich soils and forming diverse ecosystems. In contrast, lunar surface material (regolith) does not contain any organic material. The most important plant nitrogen sources, such as ammonia and nitrate, which are derived from the decomposition of organic matter, are virtually absent in soil.

Plasma technology enables powerful non-equilibrium chemical reactions initiated by energetic electrons, converting nitrogen (N2), oxygen (O2), and water (H2O) molecules into gaseous reactive species HxNyOz [for example, ozone (O3), nitric oxide (NO), and nitrogen dioxide (NO2)]2. Its usefulness has been demonstrated in the fields of environmental science and medicine, as well as in agricultural applications, such as nitrogen fixation for the synthesis of nitrogen oxides (NO and NO2)3,4 and ammonia (NH3)5,6, exclusively from air and water. We have also recently succeeded in selectively producing “dinitrogen pentoxide (N2O5)” from air with low electric power consumption (<100 W)7 as a mediator for on-site nitrogen fertilizer production8,9 (Fig. 1B). N2O5 gas promptly dissolves in water to generate nitrate (NO3−) with a very high dissolution efficiency of almost 100%, which is more than 30 times higher than the dissolution efficiency of the NO and NO2 admixture gases under the same conditions9.

In particular, N2O5 dissolved water can be used as a nitrogen fertilizer for the continuous growth of rice plants, compared to supplying only water to lunar regolith simulants (Space Resource Technologies Ltd: Lunar Mare Simulant: LMS-1; Fig. 2A). As shown in Table 1, the addition of N2O5-dissolved water (equivalent to 10 mM HNO3) neutralized the pH from 9.09 to 6.76 compared to the addition of water alone and significantly enhanced the elution of metal ions from potassium, magnesium, and calcium oxides in regolith, contributing to the mineral supply to plants. The ion most abundantly eluted is Ca2+ at 130 mg/L, which is ~3.2 mM. Following this is Mg2+ at 20 mg/L, equivalent to 0.82 mM. In the MS medium, commonly used for various plant cell cultures, the final concentrations of these ions are 3.0 mM and 1.5 mM, respectively. These levels are suitable for plant cultivation, being neither excessive nor deficient. Interestingly, Al3+ derived from aluminum oxide (Al2O3), which is abundant in lunar regolith simulants, has a negative effect on plant growth, but its elution in N2O5 dissolved water was significantly suppressed from 0.08 mg/L to below the detection limit (0.008 mg/L) of the ECR assay compared to water addition alone. This decrease in Al3+ ions in N2O5-dissolved water may be explained by the neutralization effect and pH-dependent Al3+ solubility10. In contrast, inorganic metal ions, such as potassium, calcium, and magnesium, which are necessary for plant growth, were eluted at higher concentrations with the addition of N2O5 dissolved water than with pure water.

Beyond the impact of nitrogen fertilizer, we have observed that applying N2O5 gas to leaves triggers the plant hormone pathways of jasmonic acid and ethylene, imparts induced systemic resistance11,12, and enhances the production of secondary metabolites, including those involved in flavonoid and lignin biosynthesis13. To study the effects of N2O5-dissolved water and spraying N2O5 gas on rice seedlings, we analyzed the expression of several physiological marker genes in the root tissues. The expression of the nitrate transporter gene NPF6.5/NRT1, which is regulated in response to nitrate14, was significantly increased in the regolith immersed in N2O5 DW (in both the “N2O5 DW” and additional “+N2O5 Gas” exposure) (Fig. 3). In contrast, the expression of the sensitive to aluminum rhizotoxicity 1 STAR1 gene, which responds to aluminum toxicity15, was significantly decreased under the two regolith conditions with N2O5 DW and +N2O5 Gas. This result closely reflects the observations detailed in Table 1, where the release of aluminum ions from the regolith was suppressed under N2O5 DW conditions. Moreover, the expression of the ethylene biosynthesis gene ACC oxidase ACO3 and the CDC48E gene, both integral to defense response, immunity, and growth regulation16–19, was upregulated in the roots of the seedlings exposed to N2O5 gas. Furthermore, stem and internode elongation of seedlings was suppressed by N2O5 gas exposure (Fig. 2B, C; Supplementary Fig. 1). Crop cultivation under low-gravity conditions on the Moon or in a weightless spacecraft requires the suppression of plant legginess with reduced lignin content20, and N2O5 technology may be useful for this purpose.

In conclusion, a recent report indicated that even when all necessary nutrients (including nitrogen fertilizer) are supplied, the growth of Arabidopsis is inhibited by metal ions present in the regolith when cultivated on actual regolith collected from the Moon during the Apollo missions21. Meanwhile, rice, a staple grain, has already been developed into varieties that either resist absorbing heavy metals like Cd or, conversely, absorb them effectively, serving the purposes of safe food production and soil improvement, respectively22–24. In addition to nitrogen fertilizer and the selection of useful varieties, improving fertilization methods for lunar agriculture necessitates the development of efficient techniques to dissolve phosphorus from regolith. When rice was grown using a lunar regolith simulant, a notable difference was observed in the growth of two-week-old seedlings between those provided with a conventional fertilizer containing N-P-K (Hyponex N-P-K-Mg = 6-10-5, diluted 300-fold, Hyponex Japan Corp., Ltd.) and those given only N2O5 dissolved water (Supplementary Fig. 1). Notably, the amount of inorganic phosphate eluted from the lunar regolith simulant was similarly low under both conditions (Table 1). Xia et al.25 recently succeeded in efficiently cultivating tobacco using a similar lunar regolith simulant by dissolving insoluble organic phosphate with phosphate-solubilizing bacteria. This effective dissolution method, utilizing phosphorus-solubilizing bacteria in conjunction with plasma-synthesized N2O5 dissolved water, may benefit not only plants but also the growth and use of this microorganism in lunar agriculture. In addition, for the future implementation of lunar surface farms, as detailed in ref. 1, there are a wide range of issues such as the supply of water, air, and electricity; the utilization of waste from livestock and human residents as fertilizer; and the integration of life support systems in a closed environment. It is essential to steadily address and resolve each of these challenges. Among these challenges, green plasma technology, powered by renewable energy, holds the potential to enhance the sustainability of nitrogen fertilizers used in crop production. This fully electrified nitrogen fixation process, decoupled from conventional fossil fuel dependence, is expected to contribute to lunar nitrogen cycling through plasma-produced N2O5, while simultaneously acting as a plant growth regulator, thereby enhancing plant immunity. The global nitrogen fertilizer market is expanding annually and is projected to reach US$140.51 billion by 2026.

Methods

Rice culture conditions

Three seeds of rice (Oryza sativa L. japonica cv. SASANISHIKI) were surface-sterilized with 0.5% Tween-20 and 5% NaClO for 10 min, and then washed three times with sterile distilled water. Subsequently, 120 mL of lunar regolith simulant (Space Resource Technologies Ltd: Lunar Mare Simulant: LMS-1; Fig. 2A) was placed in a plant growth culture vessel (BC-PB851: Bio Medical Science, Japan), and 80 mL of water or N2O5-dissolved water (N2O5 DW; equivalent to 10 mM HNO3) was added. To enhance the air exchange efficiency, four holes, each with a diameter of 1 cm, were created at the top of the culture vessel and sealed with a DuPontTM Tyvek 2FS 1059B filter seal (Shinkoen Co. Ltd. Japan). The seeds were then sown under conditions in which they were immersed ~1 cm in water. Rice plants were continuously cultivated in a plant growth chamber (MLR-352, PHCbi, Japan) at 30 °C with a 16-h light (150 μmol m-² s-¹)/8-h dark cycle. Water or N2O5-dissolved water was supplied to maintain the water level at ~0.5–1 cm above the surface of the regolith at all times. As the plants grew, intermediate spacers were added to the plant growth culture vessel (BC-PB851S).

To examine the effects of N2O5 gas exposure on the growth of rice seedlings, seedlings ~1 cm in length were exposed to N2O5 gas13 for 3 min on the fifth day after sowing (+N2O5 Gas). Following this exposure, cultivation continued, and shoot length and fresh weight of the aboveground parts were evaluated two weeks after the sowing. The means and statistical significance of plant height and fresh weight data, including outliers, were calculated using Fisher’s one-way ANOVA, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Different letters indicate significant differences between groups.

All plant experiments described in this study were performed in accordance with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation.

Gene expression analysis with quantitative real-time PCR

Five days after sowing in pure water, SASANISHIKI rice seedlings were transplanted into regolith immersed in either distilled water (DW) or N2O5 DW. Three hours after transplantation, half of the N2O5 DW samples were exposed to N2O5 gas for 3 min (+N2O5 Gas). The seedlings were then cultivated for 24 h at 30 °C with a 16-h photoperiod. Roots were collected from each plant (n = 3 per group). Total RNA was extracted, and cDNA was synthesized as previously described26,27. Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted using specific primers for the target genes (Supplementary Table 1).

Ion chromatography analysis

The lunar regolith simulant was immersed for at least one day in pure water or N2O5 dissolved water. Prior to ion analysis, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter and analyzed using an ion chromatography system (Dionex Integrion HPIC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with a conductivity detector. For cation analysis, a Dionex IonPac CS16 column (5.0 mm × 250 mm) with a guard column (5.0 mm i.d., 50 mm length) and a Dionex CDRS 600 suppressor (4 mm) were used. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the injection volume was 25 μL. For anion analysis, a Dionex IonPac AS23 column (4.0 mm × 250 mm) with a guard column (4.0 mm i.d., 50 mm length) and a Dionex ADRS 600 suppressor (4 mm) were employed. The column temperature was maintained at 30 °C, and the injection volume was 25 μL. The mobile phases were 30 mM methanesulfonic acid for cations and a mixture of 4.5 mM sodium carbonate and 0.8 mM sodium bicarbonate for anions. The flow rate was maintained at 1.0 mL/min.

Al3+ concentrations, which were below the reliable detection limit of ion chromatography under the present conditions, were additionally quantified using the Eriochrome Cyanine R (ECR) colorimetric assay (WAK-Al; Kyoritsu Chemical-Check Laboratory, Tokyo, Japan) to achieve higher analytical sensitivity.
