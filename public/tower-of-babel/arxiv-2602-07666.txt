SoK: DARPA’s AI Cyber Challenge (AIxCC): Competition Design, Architectures, and Lessons Learned
Cen Zhang Younggi Park Fabian Fleischer Yu-Fu Fu Jiho Kim Dongkwan Kim Youngjoon Kim, Qingxiao Xu Andrew Chin Ze Sheng Hanqing Zhao Brian J. Lee Joshua Wang, Michael Pelican David J. Musliner Jeff Huang Jon Silliman Mikel Mcdaniel, Jefferson Casavant Isaac Goldthwaite Nicholas Vidovich Matthew Lehman Taesoo Kim

Abstract
DARPA’s AI Cyber Challenge (AIxCC, 2023–2025) is the largest competition to date for building fully autonomous Cyber Reasoning Systems (CRSs) that leverage recent advances in AI—particularly large language models (LLMs)—to discover and remediate vulnerabilities in real-world open-source software. This paper presents the first systematic analysis of AIxCC. Drawing on design documents, source code, execution traces, and discussions with organizers and all finalist teams, we examine the competition’s structure and key design decisions, characterize the architectural approaches of finalist CRSs, and analyze competition results beyond the final scoreboard. Our analysis reveals the factors that truly drove CRS performance, identifies genuine technical advances achieved by teams, and exposes limitations that remain open for future research. We conclude with lessons for organizing future competitions and broader insights toward deploying autonomous CRSs in practice.



I
Introduction

Open-source software (OSS) underpins critical infrastructure,
yet scaling vulnerability discovery and remediation remains challenging.
DARPA’s AI Cyber Challenge (AIxCC, 2023–2025)
addresses this by challenging teams
to build fully autonomous Cyber Reasoning Systems (CRSs)
that leverage large language models (LLMs)
to discover and patch vulnerabilities in real-world C and Java projects.
The final competition in August 2025
represents the largest-scale evaluation of autonomous vulnerability analysis to date:
around 143 hours of fully autonomous operation,
CRSs from seven finalist teams analyzed 53 challenge projects
derived from critical infrastructure software,
each equipped with $85K in cloud compute and $50K in LLM API credits.

Despite the competition’s completion,
no systematic study has examined AIxCC’s design rationale,
the technical approaches employed by participating teams,
or the lessons that emerged from this large-scale competition.
Such an analysis would benefit multiple communities:
competition organizers designing future challenges,
security researchers developing advanced vulnerability detection and patching techniques,
and practitioners seeking AI-driven security solutions.

To fill this gap,
we conducted a systematic study of the final competition (AFC),
drawing on multiple primary sources:
all seven finalist CRS codebases and whitepapers,
the complete competition database
(challenges, results, and execution traces) from organizers,
and discussions with organizers and all finalist teams.
Our analysis examines AIxCC from three perspectives:
the design decisions that shaped the competition,
the architectural and technical choices made by finalist teams,
and competition results and their implications.
Specifically, we address the following research questions:

•

RQ1:
How is AIxCC designed
to guide and evaluate AI-driven vulnerability discovery and patching?

•

RQ2:
What architectural and technical approaches
did finalist teams employ?

•

RQ3:
What insights emerge from the competition results?

•

RQ4:
What are the lessons and future directions?

To address these questions,
our work makes the following contributions:

•

A systematic analysis of AIxCC’s competition design,
covering task design, scoring, challenge construction,
and iterative refinement across rounds.

•

A taxonomy of CRS architectures and techniques
across all seven finalist teams,
spanning PoV generation, patch generation, SARIF validation, and bundling.

•

In-depth result analysis
revealing the gap between technique capability and real-world effectiveness,
grounded in per-CPV evaluation against foundational baselines.

•

Lessons on translating competition outcomes
to industry deployment and research value,
along with directions for future competitions.

To facilitate future research,
all data, experiments, and artifacts
will be made publicly available upon acceptance,
with some materials subject to DARPA’s release timeline
(see
Appendix B
).

II
Background: AIxCC as Competition

Competition Brief.

AIxCC
[
19
]
is a two-year competition (2023–2025)
organized by DARPA and ARPA-H,
in collaboration with major AI providers
(Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, and OpenAI)
and advisors from OpenSSF.
The competition aims to advance fully autonomous
vulnerability discovery and remediation for open-source software.
From 42 teams,
seven advanced via the semifinal (ASC, DEF CON 2024)
[
18
]

to the final (DEF CON 2025).
All finalist CRSs, challenges, and infrastructure
are being open-sourced
[
20
]
.

Comparison with CGC.

AIxCC is a spiritual successor to DARPA’s Cyber Grand Challenge (CGC, 2014–2016)
[
17
]
,
inheriting its core premise of fully autonomous CRSs.
Key differences include:
➀ Scope:
CGC focused on binary security,
i.e., attack-defense of customized binaries on DECREE OS
[
66
]
,
such as crafting exploits and defensive patches against opponents.
In contrast, AIxCC focuses on vulnerability discovery and remediation
for real-world OSS development in C and Java.
➁ AI emphasis:
AIxCC provides LLM infrastructure from AI providers,
highlighting LLM-based technique exploration,
a capability unavailable during CGC.

Final Competition (AFC).

The final competition ran for around 143 hours,
during which seven finalist teams deployed fully autonomous CRSs
to analyze 53 challenge projects (CPs) in C and Java.
CRSs operated without human intervention,
managing their own infrastructure with $85,000 in Azure compute
and $50,000 in LLM API credits per team.

Our Focus on AFC.

This work focuses on the final competition (AFC),
with brief discussion of the semifinal (ASC).

III
Competition Design

III-A

Design Goal: Real-World Relevance

Figure 1
shows the final competition workflow.
The competition is embedded in GitHub to simulate real OSS development,
where development events trigger challenges for CRSs to analyze.
Organizers designed four core CRS capabilities:

•

Full Scan
:
when maintainers tag a new release,
detect and patch vulnerabilities across the full codebase.

•

Delta Scan
:
when new code is merged via pull requests,
conduct targeted analysis on the incremental changes.

•

SARIF Review
:
when developers receive security alerts (in SARIF format
[
45
,
28
]
) from static analyzers,
assess their validity (valid/invalid
\rightarrow
identify true positive/false positive vulnerabilities).

•

Report Synthesis
:
after analysis,
correlate all above findings
into per-vulnerability reports.

CRS Submissions.

Organizers send multiple challenges (Full Scan or Delta Scan) during the competition,
with some challenges including SARIF broadcasts for teams to review.
The total score is the sum of all challenge scores,
where each challenge score is computed from the following submissions:

•

PoV
(
[1,2]
pts):
an input triggering abnormal execution (
e
.
g
., crash) as proof of vulnerability.

•

Patch
(
[3,6]
pts):
a fix that resolves the vulnerability while preserving functionality.

•

SARIF assessment
(
[0.5,1]
pt):
a judgment on whether a SARIF report is valid.

•

Bundle
(
[-7,7]
pts):
a grouping that links related findings for the same vulnerability.

Developer-Centric Scoring.

Each submission type scores within a range due to time decay:
immediate submissions earn full points while last-minute ones receive only half.
The scoring weights reflect how much each submission benefits developers:
➀ Patch scores high as it directly resolves issues;
➁ PoV scores lower as it only demonstrates vulnerabilities;
➂ SARIF scores lowest as report validation is semi-subjective;
➃ Bundle rewards correct linkage but penalizes incorrect ones.
Its actual score ranges based on how many finding types
(PoV, Patch, SARIF) are linked and the score of each finding;
a vulnerability with all three correctly linked can earn at most +7,
while any incorrect linkage flips the reward into a penalty.

For inaccurate submissions,
an accuracy multiplier penalizes the total challenge score.
To encourage technique exploration while discouraging spam,
the penalty is non-linear: low accuracy rates are penalized more severely
(
e
.
g
., 90% accuracy
\rightarrow
negligible penalty;
50%
\rightarrow
6% reduction; 40%
\rightarrow
13% reduction).
See scoring details in
Appendix C
.

Challenge Construction.

All final competition challenges are based on OSS-Fuzz
[
4
]
,
providing build tooling and fuzzing harnesses
as entry points for vulnerability detection.
For project selection,
organizers initially proposed a list of
critical infrastructure and healthcare-critical OSS projects;
then collaborated with the OSS-Fuzz team
to onboard prioritized projects not yet supported.
For vulnerability injection,
most are manually crafted synthetics inspired by historical N-day issues,
avoiding AI training data contamination.
A small number are genuine 0-days
discovered during development
or sourced from unreported OSS-Fuzz findings.

III-B

Iterative Design

To handle the organizational complexity (multi-party collaboration, two-year
timespan, AI evolution speed),
organizers adopted an iterative approach rather than perfect pre-design.

Lessons from Semifinal.

The ASC served as a litmus test,
selecting 7 finalists from 42 competing teams.
With fewer teams to manage,
AFC relaxed infrastructure constraints:
teams deployed their own CRSs on Azure
rather than using a highly constrained sandbox.
AFC also incorporated key lessons from ASC:
➀ ASC required patches to be submitted against self-discovered PoVs,
disadvantaging CRSs that produced quality patches
but struggled with vulnerability discovery;
AFC addressed this via cross-team patch validation
where patches are scored against all PoVs from all teams.
➁ Challenge adoption in ASC required significant engineering effort per repository;
AFC streamlined this by adopting OSS-Fuzz.

Multi-Round Final Competition.

AFC introduced a multi-round structure
to progressively test both organizer infrastructure and team CRSs.
Organizers first conducted multiple internal rounds
from January to March 2025
to validate readiness and identify risks.
Following these,
three unscored exhibition rounds
with increasing challenge counts and budgets
were held before the scored final round.
Each round mirrored the final environment,
prohibiting teams from accessing their CRSs
and requiring fully autonomous operation.
These unscored rounds proved valuable for testing and debugging:
both organizers and competitors identified issues
and made adjustments that prevented failures in the final round.

VI
Taxonomy of CRS Techniques

VI-A

PoV Generation

PoV generation is a typical vulnerability mining scenario:
each project provides one or more fuzzing harnesses,
and CRSs must find inputs that trigger bugs reproducibly through these
harnesses.
Depending on challenge type,
vulnerabilities may exist anywhere in the repository (full-mode)
or within a pull request’s diff (delta-mode).

Two Complementary Pipelines.

Table II
presents finalist teams’ exploration
across all major technical directions
(
Table VIII
in the appendix provides further details).
Two complementary pipelines emerge:

improving the fuzzing pipeline

through traditional or LLM-enhanced techniques,
and
LLM-based PoV generation

that directly leverages LLMs to identify vulnerabilities
and generate exploit inputs.
These pipelines reinforce each other: the
fuzzing pipeline provides coverage information
and valuable inputs to assist LLM-based generation,
while LLM-generated results (even unsuccessful ones)
serve as seeds boosting fuzzers’ effectiveness.

Improving Fuzzing Pipeline.

Most teams (5/7) simultaneously explored both pipelines,
while
42
and
LC
focused exclusively on fuzzing.
Among the five,

AT
and
SP
conducted the most comprehensive exploration;

TB
and
TI
selectively explored techniques directly targeting
fuzzing seeds (reuse, generation, sharing), and

FB
minimized fuzzing exploration (only parallel fuzzing).

•

Pre-competition corpus.

Five teams reused pre-collected corpora to bootstrap fuzzer initial coverage,
typically with two steps:
➀ collecting and grouping seeds from public databases
(ClusterFuzz
[
29
]
, OSS-Fuzz
[
4
]
, GitHub, etc.) before
competition;
➁ matching relevant seeds to each harness via coverage-based ranking
or similarity-based selection using harness names or LLM-inferred formats.

•

LLM-based seed generation.

Teams use LLMs to generate seeds in two scenarios:
early-stage bootstrap (analyzing harness code for input formats)
and troubleshooting (generating inputs for coverage
blockers
[
62
]
).
Teams typically built agents for these scenarios,
incorporating conventional program analysis tools for better performance.
Interestingly, despite the goal being harness input generation,
all teams chose to have LLMs generate Python scripts
that produce inputs upon execution.
Additionally,
AT
,
TI
, and
SP
explored
generating input generators/mutators and explicit
grammars
to further enhance input quality.

•

Advanced fuzzing techniques.

AT
,
SP
, and
42
, primarily
university research teams, invested in advanced fuzzing techniques:
LLM-generated program annotations for semantic feedback,
concolic execution engines
[
10
,
73
,
56
]
for C and Java,
directed
[
9
]
and hybrid fuzzing, dictionary generation, etc.

LLM-Based PoV Generation Pipeline.

Six teams explored this pipeline.
The typical workflow involves two steps:
➀ identifying and filtering bug candidates;
➁ generating PoVs targeting these candidates.

LC
performs bug candidate identification
but uses results for no-PoV patch generation rather than PoV generation.

TB
skips explicit bug identification,
allowing the agent to autonomously judge during PoV generation.

•

Bug candidate identification.

Teams design agent systems that combine LLMs, static analysis tools,
and predefined lists of likely vulnerable functions (sinks)
to identify and filter bug candidates.
Besides standard agentic component construction,
two techniques are explored:

TI
provides logprobs-based classification
[
46
]
as an
agent tool,
enabling agentic classification with token-efficient confidence estimation;

SP
and
LC
employ multi-source voting
[
67
]
,
aggregating weighted scores from multiple tools and LLMs
to rank high-potential candidates.

•

PoV generation.

Similar to seed generation,
teams construct agents leveraging static/dynamic analysis tools,
but targeting crash-triggering inputs rather than coverage extension.
Teams use CWE information
[
41
]
to guide agents;
some decompose PoV generation into reachability and exploitability,
building separate reach and exploit agents.

Pipeline Cooperation.

Most teams explore cooperation between pipelines in both directions.
On one side (LLM PoV Gen
\rightarrow
Fuzz),
successful, failed, and intermediate results from LLM PoV generation
are shared with fuzzers,
expecting fuzzers to extend coverage
or mutate these near-solutions into actual PoVs.
On the other side (Fuzz
\rightarrow
LLM PoV Gen),
fuzzers provide coverage information to guide LLM generation;
for teams with separate exploit agents,
reached-but-not-exploited inputs discovered by fuzzers
are also shared for exploitation attempts.

Challenge-type-specific Adaptations.

Overall, teams adopt a unified technical design,
and most techniques explored are theoretically general.
However, adaptations for different languages and challenge types can be
necessary
for better effectiveness.

Language-specific optimizations address vulnerability pattern differences:
Java’s logical vulnerabilities often stem from unsafe sink function usage,
prompting teams to adopt sink-targeted PoV generation,
directed fuzzing toward sinks, and enhanced sanitizers.

For delta-mode challenges,
teams narrow scope from full codebase to the diff and related code.
For SARIF broadcasts received mid-competition,
teams treat them as pre-specified bug candidates
and incorporate these hints into the pipeline.

PoV Submission.

All teams adopt straightforward strategies:
submit unique PoVs as soon as possible.
This simplicity stems from the scoring rules: correct
but duplicate submissions incur only time-decay penalties,
not accuracy penalties,
making early submission always preferable.
To minimize redundant submissions,
all teams implement deduplication
using crash stack traces, input hashing, sanitizer signatures, etc.

TI
and
FB
further use LLMs
to group semantically equivalent PoVs.

Patching Agent Design.

CRSs’ agent designs are three-fold:

•

Multi-Arch:

Ensembling multiple architectures improves both performance and reliability.

AT
ensembles diverse patcher agent
architectures
[
32
]
,
believing that different agent designs excel in different cases.
The eight agents span diverse designs:
workflow-based pipelines with ReAct-style
[
71
]
tool use,
autonomous agents with iterative context retrieval,
multi-agent systems for handling context limitations,
and off-the-shelf coding agents (Aider
[
26
]
, SWE-Agent
[
70
]
).

SP
takes a different ensembling approach,
combining a fully agentic LLM patcher
with a program-analysis-assisted, one-shot minimal LLM patcher.
When generating,

AT
stops when any agent successfully produces a valid patch,
while
SP
collects all candidates and ranks them by submission
strategy.

•

Multi-Agent:

Multi-agent designs enhance LLM capability through agent specialization.

TB
decomposes patching into sub-problems
with specialized agents for RCA, patch writing, context retrieval, and
reflection.

TI
implements a ReAct-style nested agent loop,
with SourceQuestionsAgent as a tool for code understanding,
separating code exploration from patch generation.

•

Single-Agent:

Three CRSs adopt single-agent designs
with varying degrees of agentic customization.

FB
implements 23 strategies

Beyond agent architecture, CRSs employ various techniques

Three CRSs (

,

,

VII-B

Auxiliary CPV Annotation

Challenges in Per-CPV Analysis.

Figure 3
shows teams’ per-CPV scoring data.
However, understanding why a CRS succeeded or failed
on a specific CPV is usually not feasible.
Competition telemetry—traces, logs, and events
submitted to the infrastructure—is
inherently incomplete and unordered:
teams selectively uploaded logs for performance reasons,
and were not required to guarantee logical ordering
under massive parallelism across their cloud clusters.
Furthermore, even with complete logs,
CRS architectures ensemble multiple techniques
such as fuzzers, symbolic execution, LLM-based analysis, and heuristics,
making attribution to any single technique inherently difficult.

CPV Annotation via Foundational Techniques.

To enable deeper analysis of per-CPV performance,
we evaluate foundational vulnerability discovery and patch generation techniques
on AFC challenges as analytical aids.
The rationale is that
their performance under ideal laboratory conditions
reflects the inherent difficulty characteristics of CPVs.
This allows us to contrast which CPVs the finalist CRSs have solved
against the performance of these foundational techniques.
Note that unlike building end-to-end CRSs as baselines,
we evaluate specific techniques on isolated tasks
under ideal conditions for CPV annotation.
These techniques do not need to handle
the challenges of fully autonomous systems,
such as resource allocation across up to 50 harnesses per CP,
patch generation from self-discovered PoVs,
deduplication, scheduling, etc.

PoV Annotation (PF).

Parallel fuzzing is the de facto standard
for automated vulnerability discovery.
We use
atlantis-multilang-given_fuzzer
from OSS-CRS
[
57
]
,
evaluating only vulnerable harnesses
with 16 cores per harness, shared-memory seed sharing,
and OSS-Fuzz configurations used in AFC.
Fuzzers run up to six hours
or until all expected CPVs are found.
To mitigate randomness,
a CPV is marked fuzz-discoverable (gray cell in
Figure 3
)
if any of the three runs succeeds.
The evaluation takes 8,906 CPU hours.

Patch Annotation (MR and CC).

MultiRetrieval (MR)
[
60
]

is a minimal security-specialized patch agent
from OSS-CRS
[
57
]
,
with a basic loop structure (see
§VI-B
),
no root cause analysis,
relying on AST-based code search
[
64
]

and patch-specialized prompts.
Claude Code (CC)
[
2
]

is a general-purpose coding agent
without domain specialization.
Both receive ground-truth PoVs with sanitizer logs directly
and use
claude-3-7-sonnet-20250219

(CC version
1.0.88
, 2025-08-21).
CC tries at most 3 patch generations per CPV;
MR determines iterations autonomously.
Each agent runs three times per CPV,
and results are reported as the union across runs.
Generated patches are first validated automatically
(build, PoV reproduction, and functional tests),
then manually verified for correctness by two independent reviewers,
as patches passing automated checks can still be semantically incorrect
[
33
]
.
In
Figure 3
,

●

indicates a valid patch is generated;

\varnothing
indicates at least one patch
passed automatic checks but failed manual verification;
and blank indicates no patch passed automatic checks.
Total LLM cost is $119.53 (CC) and $221.69 (MR),
and manual validation takes two person-weeks.

CRS Performance by Annotations.

First, the annotation results reflect that
AFC challenges do not primarily reward
solving exceptionally difficult problems:
around half are solvable by a single foundational technique
(PF: 34/63 PoV; MR: 31/63 patches; CC: 33/63 patches).
Combining these annotations with CRS performance,
two contrasting patterns emerge.
Most CRSs demonstrate genuine improvements
beyond foundational techniques,
finding and patching many CPVs
that PF/MR/CC cannot: particularly
in P1–P2 C and Java challenges
where LLM-based reasoning proves essential.
Yet, CRSs also unexpectedly underperform
on annotated solvable challenges.
The causes are multifaceted:
system-wide failures (
TB
,
TI
ceased after P3–P4),
critical bugs (
42
’s patch submission issue,
SP
’s configuration issue,
AT
’s Java
poi

and the long tail of real-world edge cases
that automated systems cannot generically handle.
This leads to an interesting observation:
a CRS that reliably applies foundational techniques
in real-world conditions would rank among the top three.
Besides, looking back at
Figure 3
,

AT

precisely where other teams unexpectedly underperformed
relative to what the annotations suggest should be achievable.

VIII
Lessons and Future Directions

The AIxCC Trifecta.

AIxCC is fundamentally a test of three intertwined capabilities:

research
,
engineering
, and
strategy
.
Around 143 hours of fully autonomous operation,
CRSs tackled diverse challenges across languages, vulnerability types,
and project scales—with
roughly half of CPVs solvable by foundational techniques,
yet many released in later phases (P3–P7)
where only stable systems could capture them.
Winning required balancing all three,
and the finalist teams illustrate contrasting trade-offs.

TI
built the most agentic architecture
with sophisticated technique designs,
but its aggressive strategy
led to the largest accuracy penalty.

SP
coordinated 53 components
for the broadest technique coverage,
but over-engineered at the expense of reliability,
missing CPVs that foundational techniques could handle.

AT
struck the most effective overall balance,
combining solid technique coverage with robust engineering
and sustaining scoring across all seven phases
while competitors plateaued or crashed after P3–P4.
Despite these stability challenges,
CRSs collectively found and fixed many CPVs
that foundational techniques could not while suffering less from semantic incorrectness issues,
confirming that the real bottleneck is not technique capability
but robust integration into autonomous systems.

From Competition to Industry Deployment.

Reflecting on the competition,
we identify several areas where future efforts
could further ease the path to practical deployment.

In AFC, CRSs operated on self-provisioned Azure clusters
with budgets of hundreds of dollars per challenge,
ensuring resource availability would not limit technical exploration.
This contrasts with individual developers or small teams
who need lightweight, single-machine solutions at minimal cost.
Although finalist CRSs have been open-sourced,
their resource usage models and runtime environments
differ substantially from typical deployment settings,
posing barriers to post-competition adoption.
Future work could develop resource-efficient CRS variants
that remain effective under constrained environments;
future competitions could also introduce resource-limited tracks
that account for the needs of individual developers and small teams.

Beyond resource constraints,
OSS communities need time to adapt their pipelines for CRS integration,
such as provisioning LLM services,
standardizing CRS interfaces for broader OSS applications,
defining end-to-end workflows from AI-assisted bug finding to patch submission,
and ensembling multiple CRSs for combined effectiveness.
Considering these needs at the design stage
would lower adoption barriers for OSS maintainers and practitioners.
On the post-competition side,
initiatives like OSS-CRS
[
57
]
have begun
to address these needs;
future competitions could learn from this
by co-designing deployment pathways with OSS communities
for smoother transition to real-world adoption.

From Competition to Research Advancement.

The competition design and team-built systems
hold significant research value,
yet certain design improvements could be made to further enlarge their value as research assets.

AIxCC is intrinsically a substantial experimental investment,
yet its telemetry primarily serves real-time monitoring and scoring
rather than retrospective analysis of
why
systems behaved as they did.
Designing telemetry with post-hoc analysis as a first-class goal—logging
intermediate outputs, decision traces, and environmental snapshots—would
enable systematic studies of failure modes and technique effectiveness.
Organizer-built baseline CRSs participating alongside teams
would further enrich such analysis
by providing reference points for comparison.

The competition’s exploration of open-source LLMs was minimal:
although two teams fine-tuned open-source models,
only one ultimately deployed them.
The competition structure offered little incentive to invest in open-source models,
as the uncertainty, cost, and data-acquisition difficulty of fine-tuning
made prompt-based techniques on frontier commercial models
a more predictable and cost-effective strategy.
While advancing frontier AI for cybersecurity is a natural focus,
open-source models offer distinct value to the OSS community
through lower cost, customizability, and transparency.
A dedicated sub-track comparing CRS performance
under open-source models would encourage exploration in this direction.

Areas of Expansion.

As the first large-scale competition of its kind,
AIxCC necessarily scoped its focus.
Several directions not covered in this iteration
are worth exploring in future editions:

full autonomy
(generating harnesses and handling arbitrary build systems),

multi-CRS settings
(collaborative analysis or adversarial formats
where CRSs attack competitors’ patches),
and
semantic correctness evaluation
(approaches for patch semantic correctness).

IX
Conclusion

AIxCC represents a milestone in autonomous cybersecurity research,
demonstrating that AI-powered CRSs can discover and patch vulnerabilities
in real-world software at scale.
Through systematic analysis of competition design, CRS architectures, and results,
we summarized technical insights of those systems,
revealed both their genuine performance advances
and the persistent gap between technique capability and system reliability.
We hope this work serves as a foundation
for future competition designs, CRS development, and practical deployment
of autonomous cybersecurity systems.
