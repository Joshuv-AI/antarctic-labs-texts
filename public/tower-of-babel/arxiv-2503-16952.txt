Dimension-free estimates for discrete maximal functions and lattice points in high-dimensional spheres and balls with small radii Note: Both authors were supported by the National Science Centre, Poland, grant Sonata Bis 2022/46/E/ST1/00036. Jakub Niksiński was also supported by the NSF CAREER grant DMS-2236493.
Jakub Niksiński, Błażej Wróbel

Abstract
We prove that the discrete Hardy-Littlewood maximal function associated with Euclidean spheres with small radii has dimension-free estimates on ℓ p ​ ( ℤ d ) \ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}) for p ∈ [ 2 , ∞ ) . p\in[2,\infty). This implies an analogous result for the Euclidean balls, thus making progress on a question of E.M. Stein from the mid 1990s. Our work provides the first dimension-free estimates for full discrete maximal functions related to spheres and balls without relying on comparisons with their continuous counterparts. An important part of our argument is a uniform (dimension-free) count of lattice points in high-dimensional spheres and balls with small radii. We also established a dimension-free estimate for a multi-parameter maximal function of a combinatorial nature, which is a new phenomenon and may be useful for studying similar problems in the future.

Introduction

1.1
Statement of the results

Let
G
be a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For every
t\geq 0
we let
tG=\{tx\colon x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
be the dilation of
G.
Let
\mathbb{J}\subset[0,\infty)
be the set of those
t\geq 0
such that
tG\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is non-empty. For
t\in\mathbb{J}
we consider the discrete Hardy-Littlewood averaging operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{t}^{G}f(x):=\frac{1}{|tG\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|}\sum_{y\in tG\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}}f(x-y),\qquad f\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}),\quad x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

The symbol
|tG\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|
above stands for the number of elements of the set
tG\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}.
For
p\in(1,\infty]
and
\mathbb{I}\subseteq\mathbb{J}
we also let

{\mathcal{C}}(p,\mathbb{I},G)=\sup_{\|f\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}\leq 1}\big\|\sup_{t\in\mathbb{I}}|\mathcal{M}_{t}^{G}f|\big\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}.

(1.1)

Note that trivially,
{\mathcal{C}}(\infty,\mathbb{J},G)\leq 1.

When
G=B=B^{2}(d)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}^{2}\leq 1\}
is the Euclidean ball of radius one then it is well known that in all dimensions
d
we have
{\mathcal{C}}(p,\mathbb{R}_{+},B^{2}(d))<\infty
for
1<p\leq\infty,
where
\mathbb{R}_{+}=(0,\infty).
However, classical arguments rely on interpolating the
\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
bound with a weak-type
(1,1)
estimate, leading to bounds for
{\mathcal{C}}(p,\mathbb{R}_{+},B^{2}(d))
that grow exponentially in
d.
In the mid 1990’s E. M. Stein asked about independent of the dimension estimates for
{\mathcal{C}}(2,\mathbb{R}_{+},B^{2}(d)).
Below and throughout the paper we abbreviate

\mathcal{M}_{t}:=\mathcal{M}_{t}^{B^{2}(d)}.

Stein’s question

(
[
34
]
)
.

Does there exist a universal constant
C>0
such that

\Big\|\sup_{t\geq 0}|\mathcal{M}_{t}f|\Big\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}\leq C\big\|f\big\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}

(SQ)

holds uniformly, independent of the dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
?

Our paper makes progress on this question in the small scales regime
t<d^{1/2-\varepsilon}.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
C_{\varepsilon}>0
depending only on
\varepsilon
and such that

\Big\|\sup_{0\leq t\leq d^{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}}|\mathcal{M}_{t}f|\Big\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}\leq C_{\varepsilon}\big\|f\big\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})},\qquad f\in\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}),

holds uniformly in the dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in[2,\infty]
.

In fact, our method yields a stronger result when balls are replaced by spheres. Namely, let
S^{d-1}
denote the
(d-1)
-dimensional unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d},
i.e.
S^{d-1}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}^{2}=1\}.
For
d\geq 4
and
t\in\sqrt{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
we abbreviate
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{S^{d-1}}
as

\mathcal{S}_{t}:=\mathcal{M}_{t}^{S^{d-1}},

where
\sqrt{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
denotes the set of square roots of non-negative integers
\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Note that for
d\geq 4
the set
tS^{d-1}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is non-empty precisely for
t\in\sqrt{\mathbb{N}_{0}}.
We establish the following result.

Theorem 1.2
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
C_{\varepsilon}>0
depending only on
\varepsilon
and such that

\Big\|\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\colon n\leq d^{1-\varepsilon}}|\mathcal{S}_{\sqrt{n}}f|\Big\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}\leq C_{\varepsilon}\big\|f\big\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})},\qquad f\in\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}),

holds uniformly in dimensions
d\geq 4
and
p\in[2,\infty].

It is easy to see that

\sup_{0\leq t\leq d^{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}}|\mathcal{M}_{t}f(x)|=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\colon n\leq d^{1-2\varepsilon}}|\mathcal{M}_{\sqrt{n}}f(x)|\leq\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\colon n\leq d^{1-2\varepsilon}}|\mathcal{S}_{\sqrt{n}}f(x)|,

where the inequality above follows from the fact that averages over discrete balls are themselves averages of avereges over discrete spheres. This observation shows that Theorem
1.2
implies Theorem
1.1
. However, in the small scales regime
t=O(d^{1/2-\varepsilon})
these two results are in fact closely related. This will become evident at a later stage in the paper.

As a key tool in proving Theorem
1.2
, we establish two types of results that we believe are interesting in their own right; therefore, we explicitly highlight them here.

The first type of result concerns counting lattice points in high-dimensional spheres and balls with small radii. A representative example of this kind is given below. In what follows we write
\mathbb{N}
to denote the set of positive integers and for
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
we let

\alpha=\frac{n}{d}.

For
K\in\mathbb{N}
the symbol
O_{K}
stands for a big
O
notation in which the implicit constant depends only on
K.

We will write
C,C^{\prime},C_{1},C_{2},...,
to denote positive universal constants and we abbreviate
B=B^{2}(d),

S=S^{d-1}.

Theorem 1.3

(Part of Theorem
3.4
)
.

There exist real numbers
b_{k},

k=1,2,\ldots,
and
a universal constant
c\in(0,1)
such that for all
K\in\mathbb{N}
and all
1\leq n\leq cd
we have

\begin{split}|\sqrt{n}S\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|&\leq|\sqrt{n}B\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|\\
&\leq C_{1}2^{n}e^{n}\alpha^{-n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\exp\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{K}b_{k}n\alpha^{k}+O_{K}(n\alpha^{K+1})\Big)\leq C_{2}|\sqrt{n}S\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|.\end{split}

(1.2)

The numbers
b_{k}
are coefficients of a power series with

b_{1}=-\frac{1}{2},

b_{2}=-\frac{1}{6},

b_{3}=\frac{1}{24}
.

The above formula may be thought of as a quantitative uniform (dimension-free) asypmtotic formula in the classical Waring’s problem for the squares within the regime
1\leq n\leq cd.
Such a uniform formula was proved before in the range
n>Cd^{3}
in
[
24
, Theorem 3.1]
. Moreover,
[
29
, Theorem 1]
implies that under mild arithmetic assumptions on
n,d
we have a dimension-free asymptotic formula in Waring’s problem in the broader range
n>Cd^{2}
. The estimate from
[
24
, Theorem 3.1]
combined with
[
4
, Lemma 5.3.]
shows that

C|\sqrt{n}B\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|\leq(n/d)|\sqrt{n}S\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|\leq C^{\prime}|\sqrt{n}B\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|,\qquad\textrm{for }n>Cd^{3}.

The above estimate contrasts with the behavior for
n\leq cd
in which case according to (
1.2
) the quantities
|\sqrt{n}B\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|
and
|\sqrt{n}S\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|
are comparable up to universal constants.

Rewriting Theorem
1.3
in terms of binomial coefficients and taking
K=3
leads to the following corollary.

Corollary 1.4
.

There exists a universal constant
c\in(0,1)
such that for
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
1\leq n\leq cd
we have

|\sqrt{n}S\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|\leq|\sqrt{n}B\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|\leq C_{3}2^{n}\binom{d}{n}\exp\Big(\frac{n\alpha^{3}}{8}+O(n\alpha^{4})\Big)\leq C_{4}|\sqrt{n}S\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|.

In particular, if
n=O(d^{3/4}),
then

|\sqrt{n}S\cap\{-1,0,1\}^{d}|\leq|\sqrt{n}S\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|\leq|\sqrt{n}B\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|\leq C_{5}2^{n}\binom{d}{n}=C_{5}|\sqrt{n}S\cap\{-1,0,1\}^{d}|.

(1.3)

Corollary
1.4
provides an explicit and uniform comparison between the number of lattice points in
\sqrt{n}S
and
\sqrt{n}S\cap\{-1,0,1\}^{d}.
Previous results of this kind were less uniform; for instance
[
35
, Proposition 2.8.2]
implies a variant of (
1.3
) with implicit constants depending on
n
and
d
. The uniformity of these constants in
n
and
d
is crucial for our applications in the proof of Theorem
1.2
. The authors are clueless, whether any results similar to Theorem
1.3
or Corollary
1.4
have been previously established in the literature. However, the case
n=cd
for fixed
c>0
as
d\to\infty
was studied in
[
14
]
and
[
23
]
.

It is worth to remark that Theorem
1.3
and Corollary
1.4
may be thought of as a concentration of measure results, stating that lattice points in the ball
\sqrt{n}B
are concentrated on the sphere
\sqrt{n}S
or even on the set
\sqrt{n}S\cap\{-1,0,1\}^{d}
(when
n=O(n^{3/4})).
This is a much stronger analog of the concentration of measure phenomenon for the ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where most of its mass is concentrated near the sphere.

The second type of results concerns a multi-parameter maximal operator of a combinatorial nature, which may be useful for studying similar problems involving sets other than balls and spheres in the future.
Fix
K\in\mathbb{N}
and take
\overline{n}=(n_{1},n_{2},...,n_{K})\in(\mathbb{N}_{0})^{K}.
Let
D_{\overline{n}}
denote the number of lattice points in
\{-K,\ldots,K\}^{d}
such that exactly
n_{1}
coordinates are equal to
\pm 1,

n_{2}
coordinates are equal to
\pm 2,
and so on; formally

D_{\overline{n}}=\bigcap_{j=1}^{K}\Big\{x\in\{-K,...,K\}^{d}:\#\{i\in[d]:|x_{i}|=j\}=n_{j}\Big\}.

(1.4)

Consider the operator

\mathcal{D}_{\overline{n}}f(x)=\frac{1}{|D_{\overline{n}}|}\sum_{y\in D_{\overline{n}}}f(x-y).

Notice that
\mathcal{D}_{\overline{n}}
is a multi-parameter averaging operator depending on the parameters
d
and
K
. We prove that the corresponding multi-parameter maximal function has a dimension-free bound on
\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}),

p\in[2,\infty].
Below the supremum is taken with respect to non-negative integers
n_{1},\ldots,n_{K}.

Theorem 1.5
.

For all
K\in\mathbb{N}
there is a constant
C_{K}>0
depending only on
K
and such that for all dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
and all exponents
p\in[2,\infty]
we have

\Big\|\sup_{n_{1},n_{2},...,n_{K}\leq\frac{d}{2K}}|\mathcal{D}_{n_{1},...,n_{K}}f|\Big\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}\leq C_{K}\big\|f\big\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})},\qquad f\in\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}).

Later we will see that the lattice point count mentioned in the previous paragraph allows us to reduce Theorem
1.2
to Theorem
1.5
.

It is worth highlighting that Theorem
1.5
gives a multi-parameter and dimension-free estimate. Such an estimate is rare for multi-parameter maximal functions as their estimates are usually dimension dependent.

1.2
State of the art

Dimension-free estimates for Hardy-Littlewood maximal operators were first studied in the continuous
context and thus we briefly describe the current state of the art. For every
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we define the continuous Hardy–Littlewood averaging operator over balls by

\displaystyle M_{t}^{B^{2}(d)}f(x):=\frac{1}{|tB^{2}(d)|}\int_{tB^{2}(d)}f(x-y)dy,\qquad f\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

We denote by

C(p,\mathbb{R}_{+},B^{2}(d))
the smallest constant in
the maximal inequality

\displaystyle\big\|\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}|M_{t}^{B^{2}(d)}f|\big\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C(p,\mathbb{R}_{+},B^{2}(d))\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},\qquad f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(1.5)

As in the discrete setting, standard arguments show that
C(p,\mathbb{R}_{+},B^{2}(d))<\infty
for every
p\in(1,\infty].

In 1982 Stein
[
33
]
(see
also Stein and Strömberg
[
31
]
) proved that there
exists a constant
C_{p}>0
depending only on
p\in(1,\infty]
such
that

\displaystyle\sup_{d\in\mathbb{N}}C(p,\mathbb{R}_{+},B^{2}(d))\leq C_{p}.

(1.6)

Subsequently, Bourgain
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
, Carbery
[
10
]
, and Müller
[
26
]
significantly extended Stein’s result by replacing the Euclidean ball with various symmetric convex bodies
G.
A symmetric convex body
G
is a convex compact non-empty subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d},
which is symmetric and has non-empty interior. These sets are relevant, because any norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is associated with such a set
G
via the Minkowski functional. Let
C(p,\mathbb{R}_{+},G)
be the best constant in (
1.5
) with the ball
B^{2}(d)
replaced by a general symmetric convex body
G
. In
[
6
]
Bourgain proved that
C(2,\mathbb{R}_{+},G)\leq C,
were
C
is a universal constant independent of both the symmetric convex body
G
and the dimension
d.
This result was extended by Borugain
[
7
]
and independently by Carbery
[
10
]
, who showed that
C(p,\mathbb{R}_{+},G)\leq C_{p}
for
p>3/2,
where
C_{p}
depends only on
p.
For general symmetric convex bodies, it remains an open question whether Bourgain and Carbery’s results can be extended beyond the threshold
p>3/2
. The conjecture that
C(p,\mathbb{R}_{+},G)\leq C_{p},
for
p>1
is one of the major unsolved problems in the field.

However, a dimension-free estimate for all
p>1
can be obtained for specific classes of symmetric convex bodies. The case of
\ell^{q}
balls

B^{q}=B^{q}(d)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\sum_{j=1}^{d}|x_{j}|^{q}\leq 1\},\qquad 1\leq q<\infty,

(1.7)

was treated by Müller
[
26
]
who showed that
C(p,\mathbb{R}_{+},B^{q}(d))\leq C_{p,q}
for
p\in(1,\infty].
The picture for these balls was complemented by Bourgain
[
8
]
who proved that also for the cubes
B^{\infty}(d)=[-1,1]^{d}
we have
C(p,\mathbb{R}_{+},B^{\infty}(d))\leq C_{p},

1<p<\infty.
Additionally, Aldaz
[
1
]
showed that the weak-type
(1,1)
constant for the cubes grows to infinity with the dimension. More recently, the second author in collaboration with Bourgain, Mirek, and Stein
[
2
]
proved analogous dimension-free estimates for variational seminorm counterparts of these maximal inequalities. For a broader perspective on dimension-free estimates for continuous maximal functions, we refer the reader to the survey
[
4
]
and the references therein.

The study of dimension-free inequalities for discrete Hardy-Littlewood maximal functions was initiated by the second author in collaboration with Bourgain, Mirek, and Stein. We now mention existing results, highlighting those that are most relevant to our work. Recall that
\mathcal{C}(p,\mathbb{R}_{+},G)
is defined in (
1.1
). In the following, we denote by
\mathbb{D}
the set of dyadic numbers
\mathbb{D}=\{2^{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}.

1.

In
[
3
]
Bourgain, Mirek, Stein, and the second author, constructed a counterexample - a family of ellipsoids
E(d)
- for which there is no dimension-free estimate on any
\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}),

1<p<\infty.
Specifically, they showed that

\mathcal{C}(p,\mathbb{R}_{+},E(d))\geq C_{p}(\log d)^{1/p}.
This implies that a general result as in the continuous setting is impossible. Instead, one must focus on specific symmetric convex bodies in the discrete setting. Arguably the most natural choices are the balls
B^{2}(d)
and the cubes
B^{\infty}(d).

2.

In
[
5
]
the same authors proved that the discrete maximal function over balls and dyadic scales has dimension-free estimates on
\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}),

p\geq 2,
namely,
\mathcal{C}(p,\mathbb{D},B^{2}(d))\leq C,
for
p\geq 2.
Later in
[
24
]
Mirek, Szarek, and the second author, proved an analogous result with spheres replacing balls showing that
\mathcal{C}(p,\mathbb{D},S^{d-1})\leq C,
for
p\geq 2
and
d\geq 5.
However, neither of these papers address dimension-free estimates for the full maximal functions over balls or spheres.

3.

In
[
3
]
the authors obtained a comparison principle between the discrete and continuous settings, valid for all symmetric convex bodies. This comparison principle states that
\mathcal{C}(p,[c(G)d,\infty),G)\leq e^{6}C(p,\mathbb{R}_{+},G),

p\in(1,\infty],
where
c(G)
is a constant depending on the symmetric convex body. Notably for
B^{q}
balls we have
c(B^{q})=d^{1/q}/2.
Moreover, for
q\geq 2
this bound was improved to the estimate
\mathcal{C}(p,[d,\infty),B^{q})\lesssim C(p,\mathbb{R}_{+},B^{q}),
see
[
4
]
(case
q=2
) and
[
16
]
(case
q\in(2,\infty]
). Thus, in view of Stein’s continuous dimension-free estimate (
1.6
) the remaining range of radii to consider in Stein’s Question (
SQ
) is
t=O(d).

4.

It should be noted that the comparison principle from (3) relies on the nestedness of the dilations
t_{1}G\subseteq t_{2}G,

0<t_{1}<t_{2},
which does not hold for all sets
G.
This is the case with Euclidean spheres
G=S^{d-1},
where even the (non-dimension-free)
\ell^{p}
boundedness of the maximal function cannot be deduced from a corresponding continuous result and is a challenging problem by itself. It turns out that the spherical maximal function
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|\mathcal{S}_{\sqrt{n}}f|
is bounded in dimensions
d\geq 5
in the range
p>d/(d-2).
This has been established by Magyar, Stein, and Wainger
[
21
]
, see also Magyar
[
22
]
for a previous result of this kind and Ionescu
[
13
]
for an endpoint estimate.

5.

In
[
16
]
Kosz, Mirek, Plewa, and the second author proved that the norm of the continuous maximal function is always smaller than the norm of the corresponding discrete maximal function, namely,
C(p,\mathbb{R}_{+},G)\leq\mathcal{C}(p,\mathbb{R}_{+},G),

p\in(1,\infty],
for all symmetric convex bodies
G
and
p\in(1,\infty].
This shows in particular that a discrete dimension-free estimate is harder to achieve than its continuous counterpart.

6.

In
[
27
]
and
[
28
]
the first author proved dimension-free estimates for dyadic maximal functions associated with
B^{q}
balls
q\geq 1
in various small scales regimes.

7.

In
[
3
]
it was shown that a dimension-free estimate holds for the full maximal function over the discrete cubes in the range
p>3/2.
Namely, we have
\mathcal{C}(p,\mathbb{R}_{+},B^{\infty}(d))\leq C_{p},
for
p>3/2
together with a dyadic variant
\mathcal{C}(p,\mathbb{D},B^{\infty}(d))\leq C_{p},
which is valid for all
p>1.
This stands in sharp contrast with the analogous question for the balls for which no such results are known.

1.3
Our motivations and methods

When we learned about Stein’s Question (
SQ
) it was natural to conjecture that it had an affirmative answer. However, the counterexample constructed in
[
3
]
cast doubt on this presumption. The ellipsoids considered in that work are given by

E(d)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}^{2}x_{j}^{2}\leq 1\},

where
1\leq\lambda_{1}<\ldots\lambda_{d}<\sqrt{2}.
At first glance, these ellipsoid appear to be only a minor distortion of the ball
B^{2}(d).
Moreover, the failure of the dimension-free estimate occurs on very small scales, specifically for
t\leq\sqrt{2}.
More precisely,
[
3
, proof of Theorem 2, p. 871]
shows that already

\Big\|\sup_{t\in(0,\sqrt{2})}|\mathcal{M}^{E(d)}_{t}f|\Big\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}\geq C(\log d)^{1/2}\big\|f\big\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})},

for a non zero function
f.
This observation is part our motivation for studying a version of Stein’s question restricted to small scales.

On the other hand
[
5
]
and
[
24
]
gave a positive answer to a dyadic variant of Stein’s Question (
SQ
) for both balls and spheres. Moreover, in a recent preprint
[
25
]
Mirek, Szarek, and the second author considered the full maximal function associated with normalized averages over discrete Gasussians defined as
G_{t}f(x)=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}g_{t}(y)f(x-y),
where for
t>0,

g_{t}(n):=\frac{1}{\Theta_{1/t}(0)}\exp\bigg(-\frac{\pi|n|^{2}}{t}\bigg),\quad\Theta_{1/t}(0)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{\pi|j|^{2}}{t}\bigg),\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

Such a maximal function is pointwise dominated by the discrete maximal function corresponding to the Euclidean balls, namely
\sup_{t>0}\mathcal{|}G_{t}f(x)|\leq\sup_{t>0}|\mathcal{M}_{t}^{B^{2}}f(x)|.
It was proved in
[
25
]
that a dimension-free estimate holds for
\|\sup_{t>0}|G_{t}f|\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}
on all
\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
spaces,
1<p<\infty.
This result further supports an affirmative answer to Stein’s question.

Taken together, the above findings suggest that Question (
SQ
) may in fact have a positive answer. Our main results, Theorem
1.1
and
1.2
are steps towards confirming this supposition.

We shall now outline our methods. The maximal operators from Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
and
1.5
are clearly contractions on
\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}).
Thus, by interpolation it is enough to establish these theorems for
p=2
. It is therefore not surprising that one of the most important tools in our paper and also in
[
5
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
24
]
, are Fourier methods. A key component of this approach is obtaining pointwise estimates for the multiplier symbol

m_{t}^{G}(\xi)=\frac{1}{|tG\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|}\sum_{x\in tG\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\exp\big(-2\pi ix\cdot\xi\big),\qquad\xi\in[-1/2,1/2)^{d},

corresponding to the operator
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{G}f
. The general strategy is as follows:

1.

To estimate the dyadic maximal function
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{D}}|\mathcal{M}_{t}^{G}f|
one needs
appropriate (dimension-free) estimates for
|m_{t}^{G}(\xi)-1|
when
|\xi|
is small and for
m_{t}^{G}(\xi)
when
|\xi|
is large. This strategy has been successfully applied in
[
5
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
24
]
among others.

2.

In order to estimate the full maximal function one must control, in an appropriate dimension-free manner, the difference
m_{t_{1}}^{G}(\xi)-m_{t_{2}}^{G}(\xi),
where
t_{1}\neq t_{2}.
This task has only been successfully accomplished for the cubes in
[
3
, Section 3]
.

Unfortunately, it is not clear how to apply strategy (b) for any maximal function corresponding to the discrete averages
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{G}
other than the one for the cubes
G=B^{\infty}(d)
. The primary obstacle is the absence of an explicit and useful formula for
m_{t}^{G}
when
G
is not the cube.

One of the main ideas of our paper is the departure from trying to establish (b) for multipliers corresponding to discrete balls or spheres. Instead, we compare their corresponding maximal functions with the multi-parameter maximal function from Theorem
1.5
. Remarkably, a multi-parameter variant of strategy (b) does apply for the multipliers
\beta_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{K}}
defined by (
2.1
), which correspond to the averages
\mathcal{D}_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{K}}.

Our main source of inspiration is
[
5
, Section 3]
though interpreted in an appropriate way. In this section of
[
5
]
the authors established the bound

\Big\|\sup_{t\leq d^{\frac{1}{2}},t\in\mathbb{D}}|\mathcal{M}_{t}f|\Big\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}\leq C\big\|f\big\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})},\qquad f\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}).

The first step in proving this result is
[
5
, Lemma 3.2]
(restated here as Lemma
4.1
), which states that if
n
is small, then a large proportion of points in
\sqrt{n}B\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
has many coordinates equal to
\pm 1
. Then from the above mentioned lemma one may deduce that

\Big\|\sup_{t\leq d^{\frac{1}{2}},t\in\mathbb{D}}|\mathcal{M}_{t}f|\Big\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}\lesssim\Big\|\sup_{t\leq d/2,t\in\mathbb{D}}|\mathcal{D}_{t}f|\Big\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}+\|f\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}

(1.8)

and the proof is completed by showing that

\Big\|\sup_{t\leq d/2,t\in\mathbb{D}}|\mathcal{D}_{t}f|\Big\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}\lesssim\|f\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}.

(1.9)

The symbol
\mathcal{D}_{t}
above denotes the operator (
1.4
) in the one-parameter case with
K=1
and
t=n_{1}.
Inequalities (
1.8
) and (
1.9
) are not explicitly established in
[
5
]
, but they essentially follow from the methods developed there. Our approach is to extend these three results:
[
5
, Lemma 3.2]
, (
1.8
) and (
1.9
), to remove the restriction
t\in\mathbb{D}
and obtain an estimate for the full maximal function. More precisely, our strategy consists of the following steps:

1.

Our improvement of
[
5
, Lemma 3.2]
is Theorem
3.1
(for balls) and Corollary
3.2
(for spheres). These results aim to capture simultaneously two different phenomena. The first one is a concentration of lattice points in a narrow annulus near the boundary. The second one is the observation that vast majority of points has nearly all their coordinates bounded by
K,
provided the radius is sufficiently small in terms of
K
and
d
. Note that the assumptions in Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
3.2
are stronger then those in
[
5
, Lemma 3.2]
but so is the conclusion.

2.

Our main tool for proving Theorem
3.1
and deducing Corollary
3.2
is the count for lattice points in balls and spheres of small radii

n<cd
established in Theorem
1.3
(see also Theorem
3.4
for a detailed statement). The feasibility of obtaining such estimates for
n\approx d
was noted by Mazo and Odlyzko in
[
23
, Section 3]
. Our approach, based on the saddle-point method, follows an idea briefly mentioned in
[
23
]
but without explicit details.

3.

Our improvement of (
1.8
) is Proposition
4.3
, where we reduce the maximal function corresponding to discrete spheres to the maximal function corresponding to
D_{\overline{n}}.
Here we use Plancherel’s theorem and a square function argument to pass from the multiplier symbol
m_{t}^{S}
to other multipliers. Ultimately, the process leads to two multiplier symbols
\varphi_{\sqrt{n},0}
and
\varphi_{\sqrt{n},1},
whose corresponding maximal function is dominated in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
by
\sup_{n_{1},n_{2},...,n_{K}\leq\frac{d}{2K}}|D_{\overline{n}}f|.
The reduction is accomplished by examining lattice points in
\sqrt{n}S
with coordinates equal to
\pm 1,\pm 2,...,\pm K,
instead of only those with coordinates
\pm 1
as in
[
5
, Section 3]
. The fact that such lattice points constitute a large proportion of
\sqrt{n}S
follows from Corollary
3.2
.

4.

The final component of our argument is a generalization of (
1.9
), which is established in Theorem
1.5
. This result relies on analyzing Fourier multipliers
\beta_{\overline{n}}
corresponding to the multi-parameter averages
D_{{\overline{n}}}
with
\overline{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{K}).
Our approach combines Plancherel’s theorem, a square function argument, a comparison with certain discrete semigroups in Theorem
2.11
, and a multi-parameter Rademacher-Menshov type inequality from Lemma
2.15
. These tools are applicable because, as we already mentioned, a multi-parameter version of strategy b) can be employed for the multipliers
\beta_{\overline{n}}.
Specifically, we obtain appropriate estimates for
\beta_{\overline{n}}-\beta_{\overline{n^{\prime}}},
see Corollary
2.18
. This is because the multipliers
\beta_{\overline{n}}
have combinatorial nature and by using combinatorial arguments one may largely reduce their analysis to properties of Krawtchouk polynomials (see Section
2.1
for their definition and key properties).

It should be noted that in the range
n=O(d^{3/4-\varepsilon})
it suffices to deal with a one-parameter maximal function in Theorem
1.5
, with
K=1
. In this case the difference
\beta_{n}-\beta_{n^{\prime}}
can be essentially expressed in terms of Krawtchouk polynomials. However, going beyond this threshold to
d^{3/4-\varepsilon}<n<d^{1-\varepsilon}
creates significant new difficulties and is the reason for introducing the multi-parameter maximal function. The treatment of this maximal function in Theorem
1.5
is feasible because many desired properties of the multipliers
\beta_{\overline{n}}
can be deduced from a proper understanding of the one-parameter multipliers
\beta_{n}.
This is the case in Lemmas
2.8
and
2.17
. We remark that Lemma
2.8
is an important step for bounding both the dyadic and the full maximal function, while the formula devised in Lemma
2.17
is crucial for controlling the full maximal function. Both these lemmas are instrumental in the proof of Corollary
2.18
.

We conclude this section with a few remarks.

Remark 1
.

Theorem
1.1
, together with
[
4
, Theorem 2]
, implies that the remaining range of radii relevant to Stein’s Question is
t\in(d^{1/2-\varepsilon},Cd).
However, using our methods we do not even see any hope of removing
\varepsilon
from Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
. This is because, as
\varepsilon
goes to
0,
the behavior of lattice points in a ball of radius
d^{1/2-\varepsilon}
becomes increasingly complex, making a reduction to Theorem
1.5
seem no longer possible.

Remark 2
.

By tracing the constants it can be seen that Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
involve implicit constants of the order
(C\varepsilon^{-1/2})^{\varepsilon^{-1/2}}.
This is because the constant in our proof of Theorem
1.5
is of order
(CK)^{K}.

Remark 3
.

Our methods should also extend to maximal functions associated with other
B^{q}
balls (
1.7
), provided the supremum in their definition is restricted to small radii. We plan to study this topic in future work.

Considering functions

Definition 2.3
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and integers
x,k\in[0,n]
we define
k-th
Krawtchouk polynomial

\mathds{kr}_{k}^{(n)}(x)=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j}\binom{x}{j}\binom{n-x}{k-j}.

Here are all the facts about Krawtchouk polynomials that we will need.

Theorem 2.4
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and integers
x,k\in[0,n]
we have

1.

Symmetry:
\mathds{kr}_{k}^{(n)}(x)=\mathds{kr}_{x}^{(n)}(k)
.

2.

Reflection symmetry:
\mathds{kr}^{(n)}_{k}(n-x)=(-1)^{k}\mathds{kr}_{k}^{(n)}(x)
.

3.

Uniform bound: There is
c\in(0,1)
(independent of
k,n,x
) such if
0\leq x,k\leq n/2
, then we have

|\mathds{kr}^{(n)}_{k}(x)|\leq e^{-ckx/n}.

4.

Formula for difference in
k
: If
n\geq 2
, then

\mathds{kr}_{k+2}^{(n)}(x)-\mathds{kr}_{k}^{(n)}(x)=\frac{-4x(n-x)}{n(n-1)}\mathds{kr}_{k}^{(n-2)}(x-1),

where the right-hand side is 0 if
x=0
or
x=n
.

Proofs of the first two points are straightforward, third point is
[
12
, Lemma 2.2]
, last point follows from symmetry combined with equalities (44) and (45) of
[
20
, Corollary 3.1]
(note that their definition of Krawtchouk polynomials is a bit different). We remark that in point (3) one may take
c=-2\log 0.93\approx 0.145
. This is not explicitly stated in
[
12
, Lemma 2.2]
but can be deduced from its proof.

[

.

For any
K\in\mathbb{N}

,

\displaystyle\beta^{J}_{\overline{n}}(\xi)=\underset{{}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1},...,I_{j-1},I_{j+1},...,I_{K}\subseteq J,\\
(\forall k\in[K]\setminus\{j\})|I_{k}|=n_{k},\\

\displaystyle\cdot\sum_{U\subseteq J\setminus(\cup_{k\neq j}I_{k})}\mathds{kr}_{n_{j}}^{(|J|-\sum_{k\neq j}n_{k})}(|U|)\cdot\prod_{i\in J\setminus(\cup_{k\neq j}I_{k})\setminus U}\cos^{2}(j\pi\xi_{i})\cdot\prod_{i\in U}\sin^{2}(j\pi\xi_{i})\Big).

Proof.

Fix
j

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in J\setminus\widetilde{I}}\Big(\frac{1+\cos(2j\pi\xi_{i})}{2}+\epsilon(I_{j})_{i}\frac{1-\cos(2j\pi\xi_{i})}{2}\Big)

. Therefore we see that

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\binom{|J|-m_{j}}{n_{j}}}\sum_{m=0}^{n_{j}}(-1)^{m}\cdot\#\{I_{j}\subseteq J\setminus\widetilde{I}:|I_{j}|=n_{j},|U\cap I_{j}|=m\}

Thus, coming back to (

(2.4)

Finally plugging the above into (

Definition 2.10
.

For

(2.6)

(2.7)

For any

and

we define multiplier operators

It is known that
\{P_{1,t,0}\}_{t>0}
is a symmetric diffusion semigroup in the sense of Stein
[
32
, Chapter III]
. This was observed e.g. in
[
3

and became an important ingredient in
[
5
,
3
,
24
,

]
. It turns out that for each

also

is a symmetric diffusion semigroup. This fact together with other important properties of the objects defined above is described in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.11
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}.
Then
\{P_{k,t,0}\}_{t>0}
is a symmetric diffusion semigroup in the sense of Stein
[
32
, Chapter III]
. In particular it is positivity preserving and there is a universal constant
C>0
such that for all
d,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
f\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
we have

\Big\|\sup_{t>0}|P_{k,t,0}f|\Big\|_{2}\leq C\big\|f\big\|_{2}

(2.8)

Additionally, for each
t>0
the operator
P_{k,t,0}
dominates pointwise
P_{k,t,1}
in the following sense

|P_{k,t,1}f(x)|\leq P_{k,t,0}[|f|](x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

(2.9)

Consequently, with the same constant
C
we also have

\Big\|\sup_{t>0}|P_{k,t,1}f|\Big\|_{2}\leq C\big\|f\big\|_{2}.

(2.10)

Proof.

Throughout the proof we fix
k\in\mathbb{N}.
For each
i\in[d]
we let
e_{i}
be the
i
-th vector of the standard basis, define the operators

T_{i}f(x)=\frac{1}{2}[f(x+ke_{i})+f(x-ke_{i})],\qquad T=\sum_{i=1}^{d}T_{i},

and let
\Delta_{i}=\frac{1}{2}[\Id-T_{i}],

\Delta=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Delta_{i}.
Note that these operators are bounded on
\ell^{p}
spaces and
\Delta=\frac{1}{2}[d\Id-T].
Since
\widehat{(T_{i}f)}(\xi)=\cos(2\pi k\xi_{i})\hat{f}(\xi)
it is easy to see that

\widehat{(\Delta_{i}f)}(\xi)=\sin^{2}(\pi k\xi_{i})\hat{f}(\xi)
so that
\widehat{(\Delta f)}(\xi)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sin^{2}(\pi k\xi_{i})\hat{f}(\xi).
Therefore, the operator
P_{k,t,0}
may be rewritten as
P_{k,t,0}=\exp(-\frac{t}{d}\Delta).
This expression implies that
P_{k,t,0}
is positivity preserving. Indeed, taking

we have

P_{k,t,0}=\exp(-s\Delta)=\exp(-sd/2)\exp(sT/2)=\exp(-t/2)\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{r}T^{r}}{(2d)^{r}r!}.

(2.11)

Since each
T_{i}
is positivity preserving so are
T,

T^{r}
and
P_{k,t,0}.

Expression (
2.11
) also implies that
\{P_{k,t,0}\}_{t>0}
is a symmetric diffusion semigroup in the sense of Stein
[
32
, Chapter III]
. We have just proved that
P_{k,t,0}
is positivity preserving. Obviously, it is also self-adjoint and
P_{k,t,0}(\mathds{1})=\mathds{1}
in view of (

Finally, the contraction property

Thus, we have verified that

\{P_{k,t,0}\}_{t>0}
is a symmetric diffusion semigroup. Hence, applying
[
32
, Chapter
III, Section 3, p.73]

[

Finally combining (

which leads to

Since

Definition 2.14
.

For

,

and a sequence

Slightly abusing the notation we will write

\Delta^{k}_{n_{1},...,n_{K}}(a):=(\Delta^{k}(a))_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{K}}.

Moreover for

\Delta^{U}_{\overline{n}}=\prod_{k\in U}\Delta^{k}_{\overline{n}},

A simple calculation shows that for any

\Delta^{i}_{\overline{n}}\circ\Delta^{j}_{\overline{n}}=\Delta^{j}_{\overline{n}}\circ\Delta^{i}_{\overline{n}},

\Delta^{k}_{\overline{n}}
. Note that for a fixed

\Delta_{\overline{n}}^{U}(b)=\Delta_{\overline{n}+\overline{k}}^{U}(a).

(2.19)

We also introduce the following notation for dyadic intervals

Notice that for

3
Lattice point counting

In this section we will count lattice points in balls
\sqrt{n}B
with small radii. With applications towards maximal operators in mind, our main goals are the following two results.

Theorem 3.1
.

For any
K\in\mathbb{N},\varepsilon>0
there exists
a\in\mathbb{N}
, and
C=C(K,\varepsilon)>0
such that for any
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
, satisfying
n\leq d^{1-\frac{1+\varepsilon}{(K+1)^{2}}}
we have

|\{x\in\sqrt{n}B:\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1,\\
|x_{i}|\leq K\end{subarray}}^{d}|x_{i}|^{2}\leq n-a\}|\leq\frac{C}{d}|\sqrt{n}B|.

(3.1)

Corollary 3.2
.

For any
K\in\mathbb{N},\varepsilon>0
there exists
a\in\mathbb{N}
, and
C=C(K,\varepsilon)>0
such that for any
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
, satisfying
n\leq d^{1-\frac{1+\varepsilon}{(K+1)^{2}}}
we have

|\{x\in\sqrt{n}S:\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1,\\
|x_{i}|\leq K\end{subarray}}^{d}|x_{i}|^{2}\leq n-a\}|\leq\frac{C}{d}|\sqrt{n}S|.

(3.2)

Note that if
n<a
or
d\leq C
, then the statements of Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
3.2
trivially hold.
The point of Theorem
3.1
is to capture two different phenomena simultaneously. The first one is a concentration of lattice points in a small annulus near the boundary of the ball. The second one is the fact that huge proportion of points in the ball has "almost all" coordinates not bigger than
K
(if the radius is sufficiently small in terms of
K
and
d
). Exact powers of
d
on the right-hand sides of
3.1
and
3.2
are not that important, they can be arbitrarily increased by taking bigger
C
and
a
.
We expect the statements of Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
3.2
to be optimal, in the sense that conclusions should fail for
\varepsilon=0
.

Our main tool to prove Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
3.2
will be a formula for the number of lattice points in balls and spheres with small radii from Theorem
3.4
below. This formula will be expressed in terms of an auxiliary function
h
, which is a case of the Jacobi theta function.

Definition 3.3
.

We define
h:\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}\to\mathbb{C}
by the formula

h(z)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}z^{k^{2}}=1+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}z^{k^{2}}.

Notice that for any
d\in\mathbb{N}
we have

h(z)^{d}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|\sqrt{n}S|z^{n}

and

\frac{h(z)^{d}}{1-z}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{n}\big|\sqrt{k}S\big|\Big)z^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\big|\sqrt{n}B\big|z^{n}.

Thus, due to Cauchy’s formula for any
r\in(0,1)
we have

|\sqrt{n}S|=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|z|=r}\frac{h(z)^{d}}{z^{n+1}}dz,\qquad|\sqrt{n}B|=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|z|=r}\frac{h(z)^{d}}{z^{n+1}}\frac{1}{1-z}dz.

(3.3)

Recall that

\alpha=\frac{n}{d}

and note that since we are in the regime
n\lesssim d
we have
\alpha\lesssim 1.
We are now ready to present our most important tool in this section. Theorem
3.4
gives a quantitative and uniform (dimension-free) asypmtotic formula for the number of lattice points in balls and spheres within the regime
1\leq n\leq cd.
The authors are clueless, whether a result of this form was already established in the literature, however the case
n=cd
for fixed
c>0
as
d\to\infty
was studied in
[
14
]
and
[
23
]
.

Theorem 3.4
.

There exists
c\in(0,1)
such that for
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
1\leq n\leq cd
we have

|\sqrt{n}B|\approx\frac{h(r)^{d}}{r^{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\approx|\sqrt{n}S|,

(3.4)

where
r\in(0,1)
is the unique number satisfying
r\frac{h^{\prime}(r)}{h(r)}=\alpha
and the implicit constants do not depend on
n
and
d
. Moreover there are coefficients
b_{1},b_{2},...
such that for any
K\in\mathbb{N}
,
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
1\leq n\leq cd
we have

|\sqrt{n}B|\approx 2^{n}e^{n}\alpha^{-n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\exp\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{K}b_{k}n\alpha^{k}+O_{K}(n\alpha^{K+1})\Big)\approx|\sqrt{n}S|.

(3.5)

Numbers
b_{1},b_{2},...
are coefficients of a certain power series, we have in particular

b_{1}=-\frac{1}{2},\quad b_{2}=-\frac{1}{6},\quad b_{3}=\frac{1}{24}.

Remark 4
.

The error term in (
3.5
) is in the exponent, which might be somewhat disappointing. However, if we fix
K
and restrict our attention to
n,d
such that
n\alpha^{K+1}\leq 1
(that is
n\leq d^{\frac{K+1}{K+2}})
, then we obtain a reasonable approximation for
|\sqrt{n}B|
and
|\sqrt{n}S|
.

We move towards the proof of Theorem
3.4
. It is not elaborate and uses saddle point method, which was highlighted for
n\approx d
at the end of
[
23
]
. We start with a lemma that is essentially contained in
[
23
]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

For any
0<\alpha<1
there exists unique
r\in(0,1)
such that

r\frac{h^{\prime}(r)}{h(r)}=\alpha.

Proof.

We claim that for any
z\in(0,1)
we have

\frac{d}{dz}\Big(z\frac{h^{\prime}(z)}{h(z)}\Big)>0.

This inequality was justified in
[
23
, Lemma 2]
, however, we include the proof for completeness.
Note that

\frac{d}{dz}\Big(z\frac{h^{\prime}(z)}{h(z)}\Big)=\frac{h^{\prime}(z)}{h(z)}+\frac{zh^{\prime\prime}(z)}{h(z)}-\frac{zh^{\prime}(z)^{2}}{h(z)^{2}},

thus is sufficient to prove that

h(z)\big(zh^{\prime}(z)+z^{2}h^{\prime\prime}(z)\big)>z^{2}h^{\prime}(z)^{2},\qquad z\in(0,1).

The above inequality is equivalent to

\Big(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}z^{k^{2}}\Big)\Big(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}k^{4}z^{k^{2}}\Big)>\Big(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}k^{2}z^{k^{2}}\Big)^{2},

and follows from Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Equality holds if and only if there is
C
such that
Cz^{k^{2}}=k^{4}z^{k^{2}}
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, so it never holds.

Using the claim and noticing that
z\frac{h^{\prime}(z)}{h(z)}\Big|_{z=0}=0
and
\lim_{z\to 1^{-}}z\frac{h^{\prime}(z)}{h(z)}=\infty
we complete the proof.
∎

To obtain some understanding of such implicitly defined
r
we use classical Lagrange-Bürmann inversion theorem (see
[
19
]
,
[
9
]
for original articles, or see
[
30
, Theorem 5.4.2]
for various different proofs). We apply this theorem to the equation
H(r)=\alpha,
with
H(r)=rh^{\prime}(r)/h(r)
and this is possible since
H^{\prime}(0)=2\neq 0.
Note that by the implicit function theorem if
\alpha
is sufficiently small, then
r
is given by some convergent power series, hence to compute its coefficients it is sufficient to work on formal power series as in the above cited works.

Proposition 3.6
.

There is
c_{1}>0
such that for all
|\alpha|\leq c_{1}
the equation

z\frac{h^{\prime}(z)}{h(z)}=\alpha

has a unique solution
r
satisfying
r\in(0,1).
The solution is given by the power series

r=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}\alpha^{k},

with radius of convergence at least
2c_{1}
, where

a_{k}=\frac{1}{k!}\Big\{\frac{d^{k-1}}{dz^{k-1}}\Big(\frac{h(z)}{h^{\prime}(z)}\Big)^{k}\Big\}_{z=0},

in particular

a_{1}=a_{2}=a_{3}=\frac{1}{2},\quad a_{4}=\frac{1}{4}.

Before proceeding to the proof of Theorem
3.4
we need to derive some consequences of Proposition
3.6
. Let
\alpha=n/d\leq c_{1}
, where
c_{1}
is from Proposition
3.6
. Then there exists some
r\in(0,1)
such that

r\frac{h^{\prime}(r)}{h(r)}=\alpha

and by Proposition
3.6
for each
K\in N
we have

r=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}\alpha^{k}+O_{K}(\alpha^{K+1}).

Since
a_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\neq 0
for
\alpha
small enough we have

\alpha/4<r<\alpha<1/4.

In what follows we consider the analytic function

f(z):=\log(h(z))-\alpha\log(z)

(3.6)

on a sufficiently small neighborhood of
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=r,|\arg(z)|\leq\delta\},
where
\delta
is a small quantity to be fixed later. Notice that

\exp(df(r))=\frac{h(r)^{d}}{r^{n}}

and

f^{\prime}(r)=\frac{h^{\prime}(r)}{h(r)}-\frac{\alpha}{r}=0.

Denote
f^{(2)}(r)=\beta
, then

\beta=\frac{\alpha}{r^{2}}+\frac{d}{dr}\Big(\frac{h^{\prime}(r)}{h(r)}\Big)=\frac{\alpha}{r^{2}}+O(1),

and since
r\in(\alpha/4,\alpha)
, for small enough
\alpha
we have
\beta\in(\frac{1}{2\alpha},\frac{5}{\alpha})
. By complex Taylor’s theorem for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
|z|=r
,
|\arg(z)|\leq\delta
we have

f(z)=f(r)+\frac{\beta}{2}(z-r)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\wideparen{r,z}}(w-z)^{2}f^{(3)}(w)dw,

where
\wideparen{r,z}
denotes the arc from
r
to
z
along the circle of radius
r
. Notice that

f^{(3)}(w)=-\frac{2\alpha}{w^{3}}+\frac{d^{2}}{dw^{2}}\Big(\frac{h^{\prime}(w)}{h(w)}\Big).

Thus for
|w|=r\approx\alpha
we have

|f^{(3)}(w)|\lesssim\frac{\alpha}{r^{3}}+1\lesssim\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}

and hence

f(z)=f(r)+\frac{\beta}{2}(z-r)^{2}+O\big(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}|z-r|^{3}\big).

(3.7)

We are now ready to prove Theorem
3.4
.

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

Observe first that, if
C>0
is a constant and
n<C
, then

r\approx\alpha\approx_{C}1/d
and thus

\frac{h(r)^{d}}{r^{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\approx_{C}d^{n}.

Furthermore, a simple combinatorial argument shows that

|\sqrt{n}B|\approx_{C}d^{n}\approx_{C}|\sqrt{n}S|.

Indeed, on the one hand we have

|\sqrt{n}B|\geq|\sqrt{n}S|\geq|\{-1,0,1\}^{d}\cap\sqrt{n}S|=2^{n}\binom{d}{n}\approx_{C}d^{n}.

On the other hand using the inclusion
\sqrt{n}B\subseteq nB^{1}(d),
where
B^{1}(d)
denotes the
\ell^{1}
ball (
1.7
), we obtain

|\sqrt{n}B|\leq|nB^{1}(d)|=\sum_{j=0}^{n}2^{j}\binom{d}{j}\binom{n}{j}\lesssim_{C}d^{n}.

The formula for the number of lattice points in
nB^{1}(d)
used above is easy to establish, see e.g.
[
27
, Lemma 2.2]
for a proof. In summary, in the case
n<C
we have

|\sqrt{n}B|\approx|\sqrt{n}S|\approx\frac{h(r)^{d}}{r^{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}.

which establishes both (
3.4
) and (
3.5
).

It remains to consider
n>C.
We start with justifying (
3.4
). For this purpose we will estimate
|\sqrt{n}B|
from above and
|\sqrt{n}S|
from below. In the proof several steps will include taking
\alpha,r,\delta
’small enough’ or
n
’large enough’. Each such steps means that we are taking
\alpha,r,\delta
smaller than a universal constant or
n
larger than a universal constant. The number of these steps will be finite and thus there will be universal constants
c>0
and
C>0
such that all the statements in the proof below hold for
\alpha<c,

r<c,

\delta<c
and
n>C.
Throughout the proof
c_{2},c_{3},c_{4},c_{5}
will denote non-negative universal constants.

1) Estimate from above for
|\sqrt{n}B|
.
Recalling (
3.3
) we have

|\sqrt{n}B|=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|z|=r}\frac{h(z)^{d}}{z^{n+1}}\frac{1}{1-z}dz.

Now take
\delta\in(0,\pi)
(how small we will determine later, it will not depend on
n,d
). We divide the integral

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}|z|=r,\\
|\arg(z)|\leq\delta\end{subarray}}\frac{h(z)^{d}}{z^{n+1}}\frac{1}{1-z}dz+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}|z|=r,\\
|arg(z)|>\delta\end{subarray}}\frac{h(z)^{d}}{z^{n+1}}\frac{1}{1-z}dz=:W_{1}+W_{2}.

(3.8)

Above for any
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
we take
\arg(z)\in(-\pi,\pi]
. We will treat two integrals in (
3.8
) separately.

We start with the first integral
W_{1}
.
Using (
3.7
) we obtain

\displaystyle W_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}|z|=r,\\
|\arg(z)|\leq\delta\end{subarray}}e^{df(z)}\frac{1}{z(1-z)}dz

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}|z|=r,\\
|\arg(z)|\leq\delta\end{subarray}}\exp\Big(df(r)+\frac{d\beta}{2}(z-r)^{2}+O\big(\frac{d}{\alpha^{2}}|z-r|^{3}\big)\Big)\frac{1}{z(1-z)}dz

\displaystyle=\frac{h(r)^{d}}{r^{n}}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}\exp\Big(\frac{d\beta r^{2}}{2}(1-e^{i\theta})^{2}+O\big(\frac{dr^{3}}{\alpha^{2}}|1-e^{i\theta}|^{3}\big)\Big)\frac{1}{(1-re^{i\theta})}d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{h(r)^{d}}{r^{n}}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}\exp\Big(\frac{d\beta r^{2}}{2}(-\theta^{2}+O(|\theta|^{3}))+O\big(d\alpha|\theta|^{3}\big)\Big)\frac{1}{(1-re^{i\theta})}d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{h(r)^{d}}{r^{n}}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}\exp\Big(-\theta^{2}\frac{d\beta r^{2}}{2}\big(1+O(\delta)\big)\Big)\big(1+O(r)\big)d\theta.

We fix
\delta\in(0,1)
and take
\alpha
small enough so that both expressions
O(\delta)
and
O(r)
above have absolute values smaller than
1/2
. Then we have

|W_{1}|\leq\frac{h(r)^{d}}{r^{n}}\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{3}{2}\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}\exp\Big(-\theta^{2}\frac{d\beta r^{2}}{4}\Big)d\theta\lesssim\frac{h(r)^{d}}{r^{n}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{d\beta r^{2}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^{2}/2}dx\approx\frac{h(r)^{d}}{r^{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},

last inequality holds because
d\beta r^{2}\in(\frac{d\alpha}{32},5d\alpha)=(\frac{n}{32},5n)
.

Now we will consider
W_{2}
, the second integral of
(
3.8
)
.
Take any
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
|z|=r
and
|\arg(z)|=\theta\in(\delta,\pi]
. Then, using the inequality
\cos(x)\leq 1-x^{2}/20
,
x\in[0,\pi],
we obtain

\displaystyle|1+2z|^{2}

\displaystyle=(1+2r\cos(\theta))^{2}+(2r\sin\theta)^{2}=1+4r^{2}+4r\cos\theta\leq(1+2r)^{2}-\frac{r\theta^{2}}{5}\leq(1+2r)^{2}-\frac{r\delta^{2}}{5}

\displaystyle=1+2r(2-\delta^{2}/10)+4r^{2}\leq(1+2(1-\delta^{2}/40)r)^{2},

provided that
\alpha
and hence
r
is small enough. Denoting
c_{2}=\delta^{2}/40
we obtain

\frac{|h(z)|}{h(r)}\leq\frac{(1+2(1-c_{2})r+O(r^{4}))}{1+2r}\leq 1-\frac{c_{2}r+O(r^{4})}{1+2r}\leq\exp(-c_{2}r/2)

for small enough

This implies

|W_{2}|\lesssim\frac{h(r)^{d}}{r^{n}}\exp(-drc_{2}/2)\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\frac{h(r)^{d}}{r^{n}},

(3.9)

where the last inequality holds for large enough

since

Recalling (
3.8
) we conclude that

|\sqrt{n}B|\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\frac{h(r)^{d}}{r^{n}}.

(3.10)

2) Estimate from below for
|\sqrt{n}S|.
This time we have

|\sqrt{n}S|=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|z|=r}\frac{h(z)^{d}}{z^{n+1}}dz

and split

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}|z|=r,\\
|\arg(z)|\leq\delta\end{subarray}}\frac{h(z)^{d}}{z^{n+1}}dz+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}|z|=r,\\

For further reference note that repeating the argument used in estimating

cf. (
3.9
), it is easy to see that

|V_{2}|\lesssim\exp(-c_{3}n)\frac{h(r)^{d}}{r^{n}},

(3.11)

where

is a constant that depends only on
\delta
.

Considering
V_{1}
we let

\varphi(\theta)=\Ima(f(re^{i\theta})-f(r))=\Ima f(re^{i\theta}),\qquad\psi(\theta)=\Rea(f(re^{i\theta})-f(r)),

with

being defined in (

).
Then, since
\exp(df(r))=h(r)^{d}r^{-n}
we may write

\Rea(V_{1})\hskip-2.84544pt=\hskip-2.84544pt\Rea\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}|z|=r,\\
|\arg(z)|\leq\delta\end{subarray}}\hskip-8.5359pte^{df(z)}\frac{dz}{z}\right)\hskip-2.84544pt=\hskip-2.84544pt\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{h(r)^{d}}{r^{n}}\hskip-5.69046pt\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}\hskip-5.69046pt\cos(d\varphi(\theta))\exp(d\psi(\theta))\,d\theta.

Recalling (
3.7
) and using the observation that
\Ima\big((r-re^{i\theta})^{2}\big)=O(r^{2}\theta^{3})
we see that

|d\varphi(\theta)|\lesssim d(\beta r^{2}+r^{3}/\alpha^{2})\theta^{3}\lesssim n\theta^{3},\qquad|\theta|\leq\delta.

Taking large enough

we may absorb the implicit constant above and get

|d\varphi(\theta)|\leq n^{-1/4}(\sqrt{n}\theta)^{3},\qquad|\theta|\leq\delta.

Furthermore, (

) also implies that for small enough

we have

-c_{4}n\theta^{2}\leq d\psi(\theta)\leq-c_{5}n\theta^{2},\qquad|\theta|\leq\delta,

where

and

are universal constants. Changing variables

in (

\Rea(V_{1})=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{n}}\frac{h(r)^{d}}{r^{n}}\int_{-\delta\sqrt{n}}^{\delta\sqrt{n}}\cos\Big(d\varphi\Big(\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\Big)\exp\Big(d\psi\Big(\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\Big)\,ds.

it holds

\Big|d\varphi\Big(\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\Big|\leq n^{-1/4}s^{3},\qquad-c_{4}s^{2}\leq d\psi(s/\sqrt{n})\leq-c_{5}s^{2},

we see that for each

and each
n
such that

and
n^{-1/4}C^{3}\leq\pi/3
we have

\displaystyle\int_{-\delta\sqrt{n}}^{\delta\sqrt{n}}\cos(d\varphi(s/\sqrt{n}))\exp(d\psi(s/\sqrt{n}))

\displaystyle\geq\int_{-C}^{C}\cos(d\varphi(s/\sqrt{n}))\exp(d\psi(s/\sqrt{n}))\,ds-\int_{\delta\sqrt{n}>|s|>C}\exp(-c_{5}s^{2})\,ds

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{-C}^{C}\exp(-c_{4}s^{2})\,ds-\int_{|s|>C}\exp(-c_{5}s^{2})\,ds\gtrsim 1,

where the last inequality holds, provided we take large enough

In summary we have justified that

\Rea(V_{1})\gtrsim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\frac{h(r)^{d}}{r^{n}}

and together with (
3.11
) we obtain for large enough

|\sqrt{n}S|\gtrsim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\frac{h(r)^{d}}{r^{n}}.

(3.13)

Collecting (
3.13
) and (

3.4
).

3) Verification of (
3.5
).
It remains to establish (
3.5
). We write

\begin{split}\frac{h(r)^{d}}{r^{n}}&\hskip-2.84544pt=\hskip-2.84544pt\exp\Big(d\log(h(r))\hskip-2.84544pt-\hskip-2.84544ptn\log(r)\Big)\hskip-2.84544pt=\hskip-2.84544pt\exp\Big(d\log\big(1\hskip-2.84544pt+\hskip-2.84544pt2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}r^{k^{2}}\big)\hskip-2.84544pt-\hskip-2.84544ptn\log(r)\Big)\\
&=2^{n}\alpha^{-n}\exp\Big(n\Big(\alpha^{-1}\log\big(1+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}r^{k^{2}}\big)-\log(2r/\alpha)\Big)\Big)\end{split}

(3.14)

and use Proposition
3.6
to express the quantity under the last exponential as a function of
\alpha,
namely

\alpha^{-1}\log\big(1+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}r^{k^{2}}\big)-\log(2r/\alpha)=\alpha^{-1}\log\Big(1+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}\alpha^{j}\Big)^{k^{2}}\Big)-\log\Big(1+2\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}a_{j}\alpha^{j-1}\Big).

Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}\alpha^{j}\Big)^{k^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K+1}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}\alpha^{j}\Big)^{k^{2}}+O_{K}(\alpha^{(K+1)^{2}})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\tilde{a}_{j}\alpha^{j}+O_{K}(\alpha^{K+1}),

\displaystyle\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}a_{j}\alpha^{j-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{K+1}a_{j}\alpha^{j-1}+O_{K}(\alpha^{K+1}),

for some coefficients
\tilde{a}_{j}.
Hence, using the power series expansion of the logarithm we reach

\alpha^{-1}\log\big(1+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}r^{k^{2}}\big)-\log(2r/\alpha)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}b_{k}\alpha^{k}+O_{K}(\alpha^{K+1})

for some coefficients
b_{k}
.

An elementary though tedious calculation shows that
b_{0}=1
,
b_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}
,
b_{2}=-\frac{1}{6}
,
b_{3}=\frac{1}{24}.
Finally, coming back to (
3.14
)
we see that (
3.5
) holds.

Thus we completed the proof of Theorem
3.4
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Fix
K\in\mathbb{N}
,
\delta>0
. Then there is a constant
L(K,\delta):=L>0
, such that for any numbers
n,d,m\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
(1+\delta)m\leq n\leq d^{1-\frac{1}{(K+1)^{2}}}
,
d\geq c^{-(K+1)^{2}}
we have

|\sqrt{n-m}B|\lesssim_{K,\delta}L^{m}\alpha^{m}|\sqrt{n}B|.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.4
we have

|\sqrt{n}B|\approx 2^{n}e^{n}\alpha^{-n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\exp\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{(K+1)^{2}-1}b_{k}n\alpha^{k}+O_{K}(n\alpha^{(K+1)^{2}})\Big)

and our assumptions imply that
n\alpha^{(K+1)^{2}}\leq 1
. Let

\tilde{\alpha}=\frac{n-m}{d}=\alpha-\frac{m}{d}=\alpha(1-\frac{m}{n})
, then we get

\tilde{\alpha}^{-n+m}=\alpha^{-n+m}\Big(1-\frac{m}{n}\Big)^{-n+m}=\alpha^{-n+m}\Big(1+\frac{m}{n-m}\Big)^{n-m}\leq\alpha^{-n+m}e^{m}.

Moreover for any
k\leq(K+1)^{2}-1
we have

\tilde{\alpha}^{k}=\alpha^{k}\Big(1-\frac{m}{n}\Big)^{k}=\alpha^{k}+O_{K}\Big(\frac{m}{n}\Big).

Notice also that
n\geq(1+\delta)m
implies

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-m}}\lesssim_{\delta}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}.

Thus by Theorem
3.4
we obtain

\displaystyle|\sqrt{n-m}B|\approx 2^{n-m}e^{n-m}\tilde{\alpha}^{-n+m}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-m}}

\displaystyle\cdot\exp\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{(K+1)^{2}-1}b_{k}(n-m)\tilde{\alpha}^{k}+O_{K}\Big((n-m)\tilde{\alpha}^{(K+1)^{2}}\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\delta}2^{n-m}e^{n}\alpha^{-n+m}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\exp\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{(K+1)^{2}-1}b_{k}n\alpha^{k}+O_{K}(m)\Big)\lesssim(L\alpha)^{m}|\sqrt{n}B|,

for some constant
L=L(K,\delta)
and the proof is completed.

Corollary 3.8
.

. Then for any

with

\displaystyle\big|\{x\in\sqrt{n}B\hskip-1.42271pt:\hskip-1.42271pt(\exists i\in[d])(|x_{i}|\geq 4K)\}\big|\hskip-1.42271pt\leq\hskip-1.42271pt2d^{3/2}|\sqrt{n^{\prime}}B|\hskip-1.42271pt\lesssim\hskip-1.42271ptL^{16K^{2}}d^{3/2}\alpha^{16K^{2}}|\sqrt{n}B|

In the third inequality above we used the fact that

Definition 3.9
.

For any

|x_{i}|\leq K\end{subarray}}^{d}x_{i}^{2}\leq n-a\}\big|\lesssim_{K,\varepsilon}\frac{1}{d}|\sqrt{n}B|,

Proof of Corollary

for
