Electrochemically tailored ion-trapping nanoarchitectures in COF membranes for selective monovalent/divalent ion sieving
Authors: Jing Wang, Pengrui Jin, Ziwen Dai, Hao Tan, Huying Li, Sha Liang, Jiakuan Yang, Shushan Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The efficient separation of monovalent and divalent ions remains a core challenge in desalination and ion resource recovery. Here, we propose an electrochemical confinement strategy to spatially organize EDTA into aligned arrays within the one-dimensional transport channels of TFP-Tag membrane with a pore size of 1.1 nm. This structure establishes a biomimetic, gradient-functionalized conical nanochannel. The negatively charged upper channel regions capture and block divalent ions, while the lower regions with their larger pore size and an anion-affinitive local environment facilitate monovalent ion transport, thereby achieving highly selective monovalent/divalent ion separation. The membrane exhibits outstanding monovalent/divalent ion separation performance in multi-ion mixed solutions, with Na+ flux reaching 10.6 mmol m−2 hour−1 and the selectivity of Na+/Mg2+ exceeding 500. This breakthrough separation behavior is attributed to an induced transport lag effect arising from the synergy between the COF structure and the chelation-assisted retardation of divalent ions by EDTA. This study establishes a theoretical framework for the rational design of ion-separation membranes exhibiting simultaneous high-selectivity and high-flux performance.

INTRODUCTION

The precise discrimination between monovalent and divalent ions in nanofluid systems represents a critical challenge in membrane science, with notable implications for industrial processes such as lithium extraction (Li+/Mg2+ separation) from saline reservoirs and Li recovery from spent lithium batteries (1–6). This fundamental limitation originates from the intricate interactions among the size of hydrated ions (7, 8), electrostatic interactions (9, 10), and binding kinetics (11). Biological ion channels, in contrast, achieve unparalleled selectivity through precisely arranged coordination geometries and dynamic gating mechanisms (12–14). Synthetic membranes, however, often fail to replicate these complex features due to their static pore structure and uniform surface chemistry (15). Conventional polymer membranes, currently dominating separation technologies, suffer from a trade-off between ion permeability and selectivity; their disordered amorphous pore architectures intrinsically preclude precise ion recognition (16–18).

Emerging covalent organic frameworks (COFs) feature crystal-ordered nanochannels and programmable functional group arrays, thereby offering an unprecedented platform for filling this bionic design gap (19–21). Nevertheless, current COF-based separation systems predominantly rely on size sieving (22–24) or electrostatic exclusion (25–28) effects achieved by pore functionalities and surface charges for sieving. These approaches make it difficult to achieve high-precision ion sieving. Advances in pore functionalization strategies have demonstrated that molecular-level engineering within confined nanospaces can effectively regulate ion transport behaviors (29–33). Emerging methods for incorporating ion-selective ligands (e.g., crown ethers and calixarenes) into nanochannels have improved ion selectivity to a certain extent, with the selectivity ranging from 101 to 102 (34–38). Nevertheless, the current incorporation methods mainly involve two strategies: physical impregnation and solvothermal grafting. The former leads to random distribution of ligands, resulting in inhomogeneous binding sites (39, 40), while the latter causes pore collapse or ligand aggregation. Both scenarios substantially reduce the utilization efficiency of effective pores (41, 42), ultimately leading to a decrease in both selectivity and flux. Thus, there is an urgent need to develop new strategies for the precise anchoring of molecular recognition units within nanochannels while maintaining the structural integrity of COFs.

Electrochemical functionalization has emerged as an effective strategy to address the challenge of precise modification of COF channels, owing to its self-limiting reaction mechanism and the capability of accurate electric field regulation (43–45). This method enables nondestructive anchoring of functional molecules inside COFs while ensuring the structural integrity of the membrane. Different from previously reported EDTA-modified membranes, here the electric field is used to guide EDTA into confined one-dimensional COF channels, leading to a depth-dependent distribution of binding sites along the transport pathway. In this study, we innovatively couple electrochemical functionalization with molecular recognition chemistry and directionally anchor the classic chelating agent EDTA into TFP-Tag COF channels under an electric field, thereby constructing oriented binding sites with a conical channel structure (46). EDTA was selected as a representative multidentate chelator because it combines strong binding affinity toward divalent and transition-metal ions with good compatibility under aqueous processing conditions, while its multiple carboxyl groups also facilitate interactions with the positively charged COF channel environment. Specifically, the hexadentate coordination property of EDTA allows it to preferentially interact with divalent ions (47–50). These confined EDTA sites can provide a selective transport barrier for divalent ions, thereby retarding their transport and forming an“ion-selective trap.” Meanwhile, the larger opening channels at the lower end of the structure and the anion-affinitive local environment facilitate the rapid transport of monovalent ions (Fig. 1). This strategy achieves a high Na+/Mg2+ selectivity (583) and a Na+ flux of 10.6 mmol m−2 hour−1, substantially surpassing the performance of existing membranes.

RESULTS

Fabrication and characterization of COF membranes

To obtain COF membranes with well-ordered pores and appropriate size, we first prepared the positively charged COF membrane (TFP-Tag) by electrochemical deposition. As shown in fig. S1, an acidic aqueous solution containing triamino-guanidine hydrochloride (Tag) and an organic solution containing 1,3,5-benzenetricarboxaldehyde (TFP) were separated by a self-made H-type diffusion cell using polyacrylonitrile (PAN) as the substrate. Following a 24-hour reaction at room temperature, the TFP-Tag membrane formed on the organic phase side of the PAN support (Fig. 1A). To optimize the electrochemical deposition process, different applied voltages were systematically investigated, and 100 V was selected as the optimal condition based on membrane morphology, structural integrity, and ion-transport performance (figs. S2 to S4). Compared with the traditional substrate-supported interfacial polymerization method for membrane preparation, the electrochemical deposition method not only shortens the reaction time from over 3 days to 24 hours but also results in a smoother and denser membrane surface (Fig. 1B and fig. S5). Therefore, the final TFP-Tag membrane has appropriate pore sizes, a dense structure, and a large number of positively charged nitrogen atoms (N+) within its framework. These characteristics facilitate the entry of negatively charged EDTA into the pores under the action of an electric field and its binding to the framework through electrostatic attraction, thereby generating an EDTA-functionalized COF membrane designated TFP-Tag-EDTA (Fig. 1C).

By modulating EDTA concentration, we prepared a series of functional COF membranes (TFP-Tag-EDTA-X, where X denotes EDTA content for modification) with varying EDTA loadings. Their color shifted from light yellow to orange-yellow (fig. S6), indicating EDTA incorporation into the COF membranes. EDTA introduction only altered membrane color without changing morphology. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of top and cross-sectional views showed no obvious pinholes or cracks on TFP-Tag and TFP-Tag-EDTA membranes, confirming continuous, defect-free growth of COF membranes on PAN supports (Fig. 2, A and B). The modified membranes retained similar surface morphology, with an active layer thickness of ∼140 nm (figs. S7 to S10). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy characterized the chemical composition of the synthesized membranes. Disappearance of amine N─H (3208 cm−1) and aldehyde C═O (1696 cm−1) stretching bands, along with the emergence of the C═N stretching at 1627 cm−1 (26), confirmed successful TFP-Tag membrane synthesis (Fig. 2C). For the TFP-Tag-EDTA membrane, the −O═C─OH (1685 cm−1) and broad O─H (3025 cm−1) bands (48) verified EDTA incorporation into COF channels. Similarly, in the C1s spectrum of x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), for TFP-Tag membranes, the binding energy of the C1s fitting peaks is 286.42 eV, corresponding to the formation of imine bonds (fig. S11). For the O1s spectrum of the TFP-Tag-EDTA membrane, the characteristic signal of the C-OH bond appears (Fig. 2D), indicating that EDTA has been successfully introduced into the COF membrane. Meanwhile, we also studied the distribution of element contents in COF membranes. The results demonstrate that EDTA introduction replaces the original Cl of TFP-Tag, reducing its content from 0.48 to 0.23%. Concomitantly, oxygen content on the membrane surface rises from 7.98 to 16.23% with increasing modification, confirming the incorporation of EDTA (fig. S12 and table S1). Furthermore, the hydrophilicity of the TFP-Tag-EDTA membrane increases with the increase in EDTA content. The water contact angle decreases from 69.8° to 57.6°, which is due to the presence of carboxylate groups (COO−) in EDTA (fig. S13). Besides, the solid-state 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectrum of TFP-Tag displayed imine [C═N, 150 parts per million (ppm)] and guanidine [(-NH)2C═NH, 161 ppm] peaks, and TFP-Tag-EDTA showed an additional −COOH peak at 173 ppm (Fig. 2E). Nitrogen adsorption-desorption experiments revealed that TFP-Tag-EDTA had lower specific surface area (30.5 m2/g) and reduced pore size compared to TFP-Tag (11 to 8.0 Å; Fig. 2F). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) images indicated that the membranes remain defect-free and exhibit low surface roughness both before and after modification (fig. S14). Both membranes completely exclude rhodamine B, confirming the high density and integrity of the COF membranes (fig. S15). The cumulative evidence demonstrates that EDTA was successfully incorporated to form the TFP-Tag-EDTA composite membrane without compromising the integrity of the original TFP-Tag membrane.

The internal structure of TFP-tag-EDTA membrane

To further reveal the existence state of EDTA in TFP-Tag membrane, x-ray diffraction (XRD) and structural simulation were used to analyze the crystal structure of the COF membranes. Because of the ultrathin nature of the COF layer, the diffraction data of the active COF layer are often masked by the underlying PAN support. However, the diffraction peak at 10.5° still indicates the high crystallinity of the TFP-Tag membrane (figs. S16 and S17). To further investigate the impact of EDTA incorporation on the COF structure, the PAN layer was dissolved in N,N′-dimethylformamide to isolate a pure COF membrane (51), and its structure was determined (Fig. 3A). The results show that the introduction of EDTA leads to the occupation of COF pores and a reduction in crystallinity. Meanwhile, the introduced EDTA resulted in an increase in the interlayer spacing of the COF from 0.32 to 0.33 nm, which is also confirmed by transmission electron microscopy results (Fig. 3B). In addition, through energy dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping and elemental content distribution of the two membranes (figs. S18 and S19 and table S2), it is evident that the elemental distribution of Cl in TFP-Tag-EDTA membrane (0.15%) is lower than that in TFP-Tag membrane (0.94%). To further illustrate grafting reactions between EDTA and TFP-Tag occurred not only on the surface but also within the pore of the COF membrane, TFP-Tag and TFP-Tag-EDTA membrane were characterized using etching x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy. The results demonstrated that the EDTA was distributed in a gradient concentration profile within the COF pores, with the highest content on the surface of the TFP-Tag membrane, the corresponding oxygen-to-carbon atomic ratio (O/C) ratio is 13%. As the etching depth increased, the EDTA content gradually decreased, When the etching depth reached 80 nm, the O/C ratios of TFP-Tag-EDTA decreased to 3%, a value close to that of the pristine TFP-Tag membrane (Fig. 3C). This distribution suggests a cone-like asymmetric channel structure throughout the membrane channel (Fig. 3D). Specifically, the large amount of negatively charged EDTA in the upper channel regions effectively binds divalent cations, while the lower regions retain the original COF structure with larger pore size and an anion-affinitive local environment (52), which together provide a rapid transport pathway for monovalent cations. This structural design achieves an optimal balance between high selectivity and high permeability. Crucially, the directed incorporation of EDTA via an external electric field was key to achieving this architecture. Compared with the membrane prepared by the impregnation method, the TFP-Tag-EDTA membrane prepared under the electric field driven in a 0.1 M KCl solution exhibits a weak rectification characteristic. Such a rectification characteristic is consistent with an asymmetric channel structure (Fig. 3E), supporting the nonuniform channel architecture generated by the electric field–driven modification. We conducted electrostatic potential (ESP) energy calculations (Fig. 3F) to further explore the relationship between EDTA and the pore structure of COF. In the TFP-Tag framework, the N-containing region has a positive ESP, which is prone to attract electrons to approach, thereby causing the carboxyl functional groups in EDTA to combine with the TFP-Tag framework and fill the COF channels, eventually forming a structure where the long chains of EDTA extend in the pores of the COF membrane. This phenomenon was verified through density functional theory (DFT) calculations. We compared the energy gap between the two structures and found that substantial changes in energy levels occurred. Specifically, TFP-Tag-EDTA exhibits higher highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lower lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) levels, resulting in a reduced HOMO/LUMO gap from 3.89 eV in TFP-Tag to 1.01 eV in TFP-Tag-EDTA (Fig. 3G). The presence of EDTA molecules (fig. S20) leads to more stable LUMO orbitals in TFP-Tag-EDTA, thereby reducing the bandgap. This suggests that EDTA not only engages in strong binding interactions with TFP-Tag but also couples electronically with its molecular orbitals through electron exchange. These theoretical simulations and structural characterizations provide compelling evidence supporting the overall conical channel design of the composite membrane. This characteristic is highly conducive to achieving both high selectivity and high throughput in COF membranes. Furthermore, the TFP-Tag-EDTA membrane exhibits remarkable stability even under strongly acidic conditions (fig. S21).

Ion transport properties in TFP-Tag and TFP-Tag-EDTA membranes under the single-ion system

We systematically investigated ion transport behavior in TFP-Tag and TFP-Tag-EDTA membranes driven by concentration gradients (Fig. 4A and fig. S22). In the single-ion system, 0.1 M aqueous solutions of KCl, NaCl, LiCl, CaCl2, and MgCl2 were used as feed solutions, with deionized (DI) water as the permeate phase. Ion concentrations were recorded using a conductivity meter. Notably, the TFP-Tag membrane exhibited substantially higher ion permeability than that of TFP-Tag-EDTA membranes. Given that the pore size of the TFP-Tag membrane (1.1 nm) is much larger than the hydrated diameters of all tested ions, the order of ion permeability in the membrane is Na+ > K+ > Li+ > Mg2+ > Ca2+ (Fig. 4B and fig. S23). This sequence differs slightly from that predicted based on the hydration diameters of the ions. To evaluate cation transport more accurately, the reversal potential of the TFP-Tag membrane was measured (fig. S24). For KCl and NaCl solutions, the observed reversal potentials were 12.67 and 15.14 mV, indicating that the membrane exhibits higher permeability to Na+ than to K+. This phenomenon may be associated with the structural features of the TFP-Tag membrane. Subsequently, the ionic selectivity calculated based on ion permeability demonstrate the membrane exhibited almost no selectivity between monovalent cations and divalent cations (Fig. 4C). This suggests that TFP-Tag membrane with larger pore size cannot effectively separate monovalent and divalent ions. After the introduction of EDTA, the flux of the TFP-Tag-EDTA membrane decreased (figs. S25 to S29), The selectivity (fig. S30) of monovalent/divalent ions showed a trend of increasing first and then decreasing with the content of EDTA (4, 6, and 8 mg/ml). When the EDTA content in the pores is low (4 mg/ml), the pore size remained larger than that of the hydrated ions, and the number of chelation sites on the pore walls was limited, preventing the achievement of optimal separation performance. As the EDTA concentration increases, the channel size decreases, and the chelation sites for divalent ions multiply. This enhances the chelation-assisted retardation of divalent ions, thereby substantially improving selectivity. Conversely, when the EDTA content is excessively high (8 mg/ml), the accumulation of EDTA leads to pore blockage and spatial steric hindrance, which results in a synchronous decline in both selectivity and flux.

In the optimized TFP-Tag-EDTA membranes (EDTA, 6 mg/ml), the ion permeability sequence in the single-ion system is consistent with that of TFP-Tag membrane, yet monovalent ions permeability vastly exceeds divalent ions permeability (Fig. 4B), thus exhibiting high monovalent/divalent ion selectivity. When the salt solution concentration was 0.1 M (Fig. 4C), the selectivity of the TFP-Tag-EDTA membrane for Na+/Ca2+, Na+/Mg2+, Li+/Ca2+, and Li+/Mg2+ was 179, 150, 112, and 94, respectively. Meanwhile, monovalent cations maintained high permeability, ranging from 17 to 21 mmol m−2 hour−1. Furthermore, to investigate the effect of the concentration gradient on ion diffusion, we used salt solutions with concentrations of 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, and 0.5 M. Ion permeability increased proportionally with the concentration gradients (Fig. 4D). Notably, at the maximum tested gradient (0.5 M), Na+ permeability reached 132 mmol m−2 hour−1. In addition, the selectivity in single salts systems hardly changed under varying concentration gradients (Fig. 4E), substantially surpassing those of previously reported membranes (Fig. 4F and table S3). These results directly confirm that the EDTA in the upper segment of the channel bind to the intrinsic sites of the COF, forming a structurally stable ion-trapping channel. Specifically, the COO− groups of EDTA form more stable coordination with divalent ions, thereby restricting their transport. In contrast, the larger pore size and the positively charged/anion-affinitive local environment of the lower original COF channel facilitate the transport of monovalent ions.

To further elucidate this highly selective characteristic, we conducted molecular dynamics simulations to investigate the transport behavior of ions from the bulk solution phase to the interior of the subnanometer channels. Specifically, we focused on Na+ as the monovalent cation and Mg2+ as the divalent cation (fig. S31). By calculating the average force potential required for the transport of Na+ and Mg2+ ions from the bulk solution to the subnano channels, we analyzed the differences in ion transport energy barriers (Fig.4, G and H). The cations migrate from the bulk solution to the membrane channels driven by a pressure difference. The transport energy barrier for Mg2+ in the bulk solution is 3781 kJ mol−1, which is ∼3.02 times higher than that for Na+ (1252 kJ mol−1). This indicates that monovalent Na+ ions are more readily transported to the membrane channel entrance in the bulk phase solution compared to divalent Mg2+ ions, facilitating the selective transport of monovalent ions. In the ion transport process, the presence of anions, particularly Cl−, can interfere with cation transport. We further analyzed the influence of Cl− on cation transport (figs. S32 and S33). We found that Mg2+ interacts more strongly with water molecules and chloride ions than Na+ in the presence of Cl−. Consequently, Mg2+ faces greater difficulty in being transported from the bulk phase solution to the channel entrance, which aligns with the calculated energy barrier. In addition, we calculated the radial distribution function (RDF) for Na+ and Mg2+. The RDF results indicated that the oxygen density around Na+ was markedly lower than that around Mg2+, suggesting that the hydration energy of Na+ is weaker (fig. S34). Consequently, Na+ is more likely than Mg2+ to enter the narrow subnano channels. Therefore, in the TFP-Tag-EDTA membrane channels, the energy barrier for Na+ (1559 kJ mol−1) is much lower than that for Mg2+ (4527 kJ mol−1), indicating that Na+ is more likely to be selected to enter the membrane channels. When these cations are transported through subnano channels, electrostatic attractions occur between them. The TFP-Tag-EDTA channels contain carboxyl groups from EDTA molecules, which can dissociate to impart a negative charge to the channel. This negative charge attracts alkali metal cations through ionic bonds. The deprotonated carboxyl group (−COO−), along with two electronegative nitrogen atoms, can act as ligands to coordinate with alkaline earth metal and transition metal ions, forming more chemically stable complexes (48). This results in a stronger affinity for Mg2+, increasing the transport energy barrier through the channel. Therefore, when both ions are transported through the membrane channels, the energy barrier required for Mg2+ transport increases to 7013 kJ mol−1, which is ∼3.2 times that of the Na+ transport barrier (2216 kJ mol−1). This result indicates that Na+ transport in the subnano channels is relatively fast, while Mg2+ transport is hindered (Fig. 4G). This further promotes the selective separation of monovalent and multivalent ions. Because Mg2+ has a large energy barrier in all transport links, it is difficult for Mg2+ to be transported across the membrane (Fig. 4H). In addition, Mg2+ requires extremely high energy within the channel, so the selective regulation of the TFP-Tag-EDTA membrane for monovalent and multivalent ions mainly occurs within the channel.

The transport characteristics of ions in complex systems

To further evaluate the ion diffusion behavior in complex mixed systems, we conducted an ion-selective permeation test using a mixed salt solution with a concenttration of 0.1 M (Fig. 5A). The mixed system was analyzed using an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES). In the mixed salt test, the selectivity for monovalent and multivalent ions was markedly enhanced, highlighting the potential of these COF membranes for separating and recovering ions in complex systems. Specifically, the TFP-Tag-EDTA membrane demonstrated remarkably high selectivity ratios for Na+/Ca2+ (309), Na+/Mg2+ (580), Li+/Ca2+ (188), and Li+/Mg2+ (353). These values are substantially higher than those reported for other membrane materials in recent literature (table S3). Meanwhile, the ion flux of the membrane was considerably reduced compared to that in the single-salt system, amounting to approximately half of the flux observed in the single-salt system ranging from 6 to 13 mmol m−2 hour−1 (Fig. 5C). This reduction can be attributed to the lower diffusion rates of divalent ions compared to monovalent ions in the mixed salt system, leading to the accumulation of divalent ions on the membrane surface and the formation of a high-concentration boundary layer (50, 56). In addition, the binding and immobilization of divalent ions within the membrane channels result in a decrease in the effective pore size, further contributing to the reduced membrane flux.

To further elucidate the regulatory mechanism of EDTA introduction on ion selectivity, the dynamic behavior of ions within the TFP-Tag-EDTA membrane was simulated (Fig. 5B). Figure 5E illustrates the number density distribution of ions in a mixed solution under pressure-driven conditions. The order of ion migration in the channel is Na+ > K+ > Li+ > Ca2+ > Mg2+, which aligns with the experimental observations. The simulation results reveal distinct number density distributions within the first 20 Å of the channel, with monovalent ions being substantially more abundant than divalent ions. Specifically, the number density of Na+ is 7.1 ions/nanometer3, which is twice that of Mg2+. This indicates that Na+ is more readily transported through the channel than Mg2+. The maximum number density of Na+ at 38 Å reaches 3.5 ions/nanometer3, with no other ions present, further demonstrating that monovalent ion transport is less restricted in this subnanometer channel. The number density of Li+ and Na+ is higher when the channel is near 60 Å. Divalent ions begin to appear at 65 Å within the channel, but their concentration remains low. This further indicates that in the subnanometer transport channel containing EDTA, the transport of divalent ions is limited, thereby achieving higher separation efficiency for monovalent versus divalent ions.

To demonstrate the practical application potential of the TFP-Tag-EDTA membrane in industrial environments, we evaluated its separation performance using the leaching solution from lithium-ion batteries. The flux of lithium ions (Li+) was 0.19 mol m−2 hour−1, while the flux of Co2+, Ni2+, and Mn2+ were as low as 10−5 mol m−2 hour−1. The separation factor of lithium ions over divalent ions exceeded 12 (Fig. 5D). This characteristic enables the TFP-Tag-EDTA membrane to effectively concentrate lithium ions from lithium-ion battery leaching solutions, produce high-purity solutions containing lithium ions, and promote efficient recovery and reuse of lithium. In addition, the ion sieving performance of TFP-Tag-EDTA membrane is superior to that of most reported membranes (Fig. 5E and table S4). Furthermore, after a long-term test, no changes were observed in the XRD pattern and SEM morphology of the TFP-Tag-EDTA membrane (figs. S37 and S38), confirming its durability and stability in the actual separation process. Based on the above analysis, it can be concluded that in the EDTA-modified subnanochannels of the COF membrane (Fig. 5F), the EDTA-rich upper channel regions provide preferential coordination sites for divalent ions. These interactions retard the transport of divalent ions while exerting a much smaller influence on monovalent ions. Meanwhile, the spacious transport pathway and the anion-affinitive local environment in the lower channel region facilitate the permeation of monovalent ions, thereby enhancing membrane permeability. Consequently, the system achieves ultrahigh monovalent/divalent selectivity even in complex mixtures.

DISCUSSION

In conclusion, a pioneering electrochemical confinement strategy has been proposed for the design of COF nanochannels with biomimetic ion selectivity. This strategy has been shown to solve the long-standing problem of monovalent/divalent ion identification in membrane science. The integration of the electric field–oriented molecular assembly and the multidentate coordination chemistry of EDTA have resulted in the construction of a gradient-functionalized ion trap within the 1.1-nm electric-rich COF pore. The prepared TFP-Tag-EDTA membrane has been demonstrated to be able to capture high-affinity divalent ions at the pore entrance and the first half section, while the larger-opened rear promotes the transport of monovalent ions. This is a notable advance, as it overcomes the trade-off between permeability and selectivity. This work provides guidance for the design and manufacture of efficient ion separation membranes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Characterization

The surface morphology of the samples was examined using SEM (TESCAN Mira Lms). FTIR spectra of membranes were recorded via attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode on a VERTEX 80 Bruker spectrometer. The inner chemistry of the membranes was characterized by XPS (Thermo Scientific K-Alpha). The contact angles of water were measured on a contact angle measuring system (SL200KB, USA KNO Industry Co.) equipped with a charge-coupled device camera in sessile drop mode. The dye concentrations were determined using asn ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectrophotometer (UV-1800). The elemental distribution of the COF membranes was analyzed by field emission SEM coupled with EDS (Phenom XL, China Shanghai). The crystal structure of the COF membranes was investigated via XRD (Shimadzu, λ = 1.5406 Å). The surface morphology of the COF membranes was further analyzed using AFM (Oxford Instruments Asylum Research, USA) in tapping mode. The chemical structure of the COF membranes was characterized by solid-state 13C cross-polarization/magic angle spinning NMR (Bruker Avance Neo 400WB). N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms were measured on a Quantachrome Autosorb IQ3, and the surface areas were calculated using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller method. The pore size distributions were determined from the nitrogen adsorption isotherms using quenched solid DFT.

Synthesis of the TFP-Tag membrane

The COF active layer was formed on the surface of a PAN ultrafiltration membrane with a molecular weight of 50,000 Da through interfacial polymerization. The PAN membrane was vertically placed in the middle of a self-made diffusion cell, each with a volume of 30 cm3. The aqueous solution of Tag (16.5 mg, 0.12 mmol) dissolved in 3 M acetic acid (30 ml) and the organic phase of TFP (30 mg, 0.19 mmol) dissolved in toluene (30 ml) were introduced into both sides of the diffusion cell, respectively. A 100-V voltage was applied using indium tin oxide conductive glass as the electrode, and the reaction mixture was stored at room temperature for 24 hours. Before testing, the obtained membrane was rinsed successively with ethanol, methanol, and water to remove any residual monomers, catalysts, and organic solvents.

Synthesis of the TFP-Tag membrane (traditional liquid-solid-liquid interfacial polymerization)

The COF active layer was formed on the surface of a PAN ultrafiltration membrane with a molecular weight of 50,000 Da through interfacial polymerization. The PAN membrane was vertically placed in the middle of a self-made diffusion cell, each with a volume of 30 cm3. The aqueous solution of Tag (16.5 mg, 0.12 mmol) dissolved in 3 M acetic acid (30 ml) and the organic phase of TFP (30 mg, 0.19 mmol) dissolved in toluene (30 ml) were introduced into both sides of the diffusion cell, and the reaction mixture was stored at room temperature for 72 hours. Before testing, the obtained membrane was rinsed successively with ethanol, methanol, and water to remove any residual monomers, catalysts, and organic solvents.

Ion permeation measurement

Ion permeation experiments were conducted using a homemade H-shaped cell. A membrane was sandwiched between two rubber O-rings and secured in the center of the cell with clamps, resulting in an effective membrane area of 0.2 cm2. In a typical single-salt permeation experiment, a 0.1 M aqueous solution of ions was introduced into one compartment of the H-shaped cell as the feed solution, while the other compartment was filled with an equal volume of DI water as the permeate. During the experiment, magnetic stirring was applied to both the feed and permeate compartments to minimize concentration polarization effects. The conductivity of the permeate was measured using a conductivity meter and recorded as a function of permeation time at room temperature, with data collected every minute. Each permeation measurement was replicated using three COF membranes prepared under identical conditions. Calibration curves were generated using solutions with known ion concentrations. In the ion permeation rate calculation, Ci represents the current ion concentration (mol·liter−1), C0 represents the previous ion concentration (mol·liter−1), V represents the volume of the aqueous solution (liter), A represents the effective membrane area (m2), and Δt represents the interval of test time (hour).

In the multi-ion diffusion experiment, a feed solution containing 0.1 M KCl, NaCl, LiCl, 0.1 M CaCl2, and 0.1 M MgCl2 was used, while an equal volume of DI served as the permeate.

Lithium battery leaching solution treatment measurements

For the transport test of the lithium battery leaching solution, the same apparatus used for ion permeation experiments was used. Subsequently, 50 ml of the leaching solution (containing Li+ 0.3 M and 0.001 M Co2+/Ni2+/Mn2+) was added to the feed side facing the selective layer. The pH of the solution was adjusted to 2 using H2SO4. On the permeate side, facing the support membrane, 50 ml of DI was added. The concentration of different salts in the permeate was analyzed using ICP-OES. The solutions on both sides were stirred at 1500 rpm.

HOMO and LUMO calculation

The HOMO and LUMO of EDTA adsorption in COF were calculated through Dmol3 module in Materials Studio (MS) 2020. First, the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof exchange-correlation functional was used to fully relax EDTA and COF. The double-numeric quality basis sets with polarization functions were used. The iterative tolerances for energy change, force and displacements were 1 × 10−5 Ha, 0.002 Ha/Å, and 0.005 Å, respectively. In the self-consistent field (SCF) procedure, 10−6 atomic unit (a.u.) was used for the convergence standard electron density. After structure optimization, the EDTA was placed into COF void. Then the interaction pair was freely optimized by Dmol3 module. Last, single point energy calculation was executed, and HOMO and LUMO were outputted.

DFT simulation

Quantum chemical calculations were conducted using DFT. All DFT computations were performed using the DMol3 method (57). This method utilizes rapidly convergent three-dimensional numerical integrations to compute matrix elements within the Ritz variational approach. A double numerical basis set with polarization functions on all atoms except hydrogen, known as the DND basis set, was used. For geometry optimizations, the GGA functional BLYP was applied. The convergence criteria for these optimizations included thresholds of 1 × 10−5 Ha for energy, 0.004 Ha/Å for gradient, and 0.005 Å for displacement. In addition, a SCF density convergence threshold of 1 × 10−5 Ha was used. The Pulay direct inversion of iterative subspace technique was used to enhance SCF convergence.

MD simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted using the Forcite module with the COMPASS III force field in Materials Studio (58). Van der Waals and Coulomb interactions were considered, using atom-based and Ewald methods with a 12.5-Å cutoff. The motion equations were integrated at a 1-fs time step. Following energy minimization, each system underwent a 500-ps relaxation period under periodic boundary conditions in the NVT ensemble (pressure = 1 atmosphere, temperature = 298.0 K) using the Nose thermostat and Berendsen barostat for stabilization of temperature, potential, and total energy.
