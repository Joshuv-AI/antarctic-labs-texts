Vaccine Handling Management in Korea, Japan, and Taiwan: A Comparative Study of Storage Standards, Implementation Practices, and Regulatory Approaches
Authors: Cho Ryok Kang, Ji Yun Noh, Won Suk Choi, Young June Choe, Hajime Kamiya, Seiichi Tomotaki, Hiroko Tomotaki, Li-Min Huang, Sumyeong Choe, Young Hwa Lee, Nam-Kyong Choi, Heehyun Won, Suyeon Jeong, Jong-Koo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRobust vaccine cold-chain management safeguards potency and public trust. Korea, Japan, and Taiwan share core practices (2–8°C storage, continuous monitoring, first-expiry-first-out [FEFO] stock rotation) yet differ in implementation and governance. We compared standards and real-world practices across the three systems to identify actionable improvements for Korea.MethodsFrom March–July 2025 we conducted structured interviews and onsite observations at public health centers, hospitals, and clinics in Korea, Taiwan (National Taiwan University Children’s Hospital), and Japan (Kyoto University Hospital, Katsura Hospital, and multiple Osaka/Mie sites). We reviewed the latest national guidance and synthesized findings across four domains: 1) storage facilities/equipment, 2) temperature monitoring and excursion response, 3) inventory management/disposal, and 4) governance/compliance.ResultsAll three countries uphold core cold-chain principles, but system design varies. Taiwan applies centrally coordinated, de facto mandatory standards: government-supported data loggers with alarms, immediate excursion reporting, regular checklist-based inspections, regional backup storage, and a differentiated wastage policy (reimbursement for unavoidable loss; penalties for preventable waste). Japan employs a trust-based, decentralized model, where the widespread use of purpose-built refrigerators and continuous monitoring is common practice rather than a national legal mandate. This system is governed by professional norms, provider-borne financial liability for waste, provider-led excursion decisions (discarding if viability is uncertain), and local-level stock redistribution networks. Korea’s evolving hybrid model provides detailed technical guidance (since 2021), promotes automated monitoring and FEFO, and encourages contingency planning; however, requirements are largely advisory, inspections are ad hoc, and formal redistribution and incident-reporting systems remain under development. Comparative tables highlight stricter equipment mandates and enforcement in Taiwan, Japan’s reliance on professional norms and financial incentives, and Korea’s partial implementation gap.ConclusionTo strengthen reliability and reduce waste, Korea could 1) confer regulatory status on key standards, 2) institute routine audits and incident reporting, 3) formalize regional backup/storage networks, 4) enable systematic stock redistribution, and 5) align incentives with a differentiated compensation scheme. Drawing on Taiwan’s enforcement/support model and Japan’s local-level redistribution networks can help Korea achieve uniformly high performance while maintaining operational feasibility.Keywords: Cold Chain, Vaccines, Drug Storage, Temperature, Quality Control, Korea, Taiwan, Japan

INTRODUCTION

Ensuring that vaccines are stored and transported under proper temperature-controlled conditions is critical for preserving vaccine potency and efficacy.1 Vaccines are biological products that can lose their efficacy if exposed to temperatures outside the recommended range or if frozen when they should not be.2 Failure to store and handle vaccines properly can reduce vaccine potency, leading to inadequate immune responses in recipients and poor protection against disease.3 This not only endangers individual and public health, but also results in significant waste of vaccines and resources, and can undermine public confidence in immunization programs when vaccine recipients must be revaccinated due to mishandled doses. Given the high stakes, cold chain management is regarded as a backbone of immunization programs worldwide, and robust guidelines and practices are needed at every level from national storage facilities down to local clinics.4

Republic of Korea,5 Japan,6 and Taiwan7 each have developed national guidelines and systems for vaccine cold chain management, reflecting differences in their public health infrastructure, climate, and policy environments. All three countries share the common goal of maintaining vaccine efficacy through proper cold chain practices and adhere to basic principles – storing vaccines at 2–8°C, using dedicated refrigeration equipment, continuous temperature monitoring, and strict control of vaccine expiration. However, the implementation approaches and regulatory rigor differ markedly among these countries. Korea’s system has evolved relatively recently with formal guidelines first issued in 2021, largely in response to a cold chain breach incident, and continues to be refined under national oversight but remains guidance rather than legally mandated in all aspects. Japan’s vaccine storage practices have a much longer history grounded in law but often leave many specifics to professional guidelines and the initiative of local authorities or institutions. Taiwan’s approach is characterized by strong central coordination – Taiwan Centers for Disease Control (Taiwan CDC) treats vaccine storage guidelines as de facto mandatory standards, achieving high compliance through strict oversight and support to local facilities. These varying approaches provide a basis for comparison and mutual learning. While individual national policies have been studied, to our knowledge, no systematic, cross-country comparative analysis of vaccine cold chain governance and practice has been conducted among these high-income East Asian nations.

This study provides a comparative analysis of vaccine cold chain management in Korea, Japan, and Taiwan, organized into four key domains: 1) storage facilities and equipment; 2) temperature monitoring and excursion response; 3) vaccine inventory management; and 4) governance and compliance mechanisms. We examine each country’s standards and typical practices under these domains, highlight similarities and differences, and include tables summarizing key comparative data. In the discussion, we consider the policy implications of the differences observed, and we offer recommendations for strengthening Korea’s cold chain management of vaccines by adopting best practices from Japan and Taiwan.

METHODS

From March to July 2025, we conducted fieldwork in Korea, Taiwan and Japan to gather first-hand information on vaccine handling practices. A standard interview guide was used, covering topics included in Supplementary Data 1. This core guide was identical in its thematic structure across all three and minimally adapted to reflect country-specific terminology. Each interview lasted approximately 30–60 minutes. First, we visited 10 public health centers and 43 clinics and hospitals across Korea between March and May 2025. The research team then visited National Taiwan University Children’s Hospital in Taipei, Taiwan, in June 2025. We then visited healthcare facilities in Japan, including Kyoto University Hospital and Katsura Hospital in Kyoto, followed by multiple sites in Osaka and Mie Prefectures in June 2025. The Japanese sites comprised a mix of hospital and clinic settings: Kubota Clinic, Kameyama City Office (public health office), Mie National Hospital, Kinoko Kodomo Clinic, Mie University (Graduate School of Medicine), and Umemoto Kodomo Clinic. These sites were selected to represent a range of healthcare settings involved in vaccination, from large teaching hospitals to private clinics and local public health offices. In order to facilitate the efficient implementation of on-site surveys and interviews, the interview questionnaires were sent to the respective institutions several weeks in advance of the scheduled visits.

At each visited site, we carried out structured interviews and observational assessments. Interviews were conducted with key healthcare personnel responsible for immunization and vaccine management – typically pediatricians, nurses, and pharmacists. A standard interview guide was used, covering topics such as the facility’s vaccine storage equipment and setup, daily temperature monitoring routines, protocols for handling temperature excursions or power outages, vaccine inventory management (stock rotation and dealing with expiring doses), and any challenges faced in maintaining the cold chain. Each interview lasted approximately 30–60 minutes. In parallel, we observed the vaccine storage conditions and practices in each facility. This included examining the type of refrigeration units used (purpose-built vaccine refrigerators vs. domestic refrigerators), the presence and type of temperature monitoring devices (e.g., data loggers with alarms, maximum–minimum thermometers), the organization of vaccines inside the unit (placement, use of thermal stabilizers or cold boxes), and the availability of backup power or contingency plans.

In addition to the field visits, our study involved a review of official guidelines and policy documents on vaccine cold chain management from each country. We obtained the latest national guidelines on vaccine storage and handling published by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA),5 Japan’s Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare,6 and the Taiwan CDC.7 These documents (issued in 2021 for Korea, updated as of 2025 for Japan, and in 2024 for Taiwan) detail the recommended or mandated procedures for maintaining the vaccine cold chain. The content of the guidelines was reviewed to extract key provisions in the same domains covered by our field observations: storage equipment requirements, temperature monitoring practices and excursion response steps, inventory management (stock rotation, expiration handling, redistribution policies), and governance measures (compliance monitoring, reporting requirements, and penalties or incentives).

Finally, we synthesized the information from the interviews, observations, and guideline review. Interview transcripts and observational notes were analyzed using a comparative thematic framework. Data from all three countries were coded and synthesized within the four predefined domains (storage facilities and equipment; temperature monitoring and excursion response; vaccine inventory management and disposal; and governance and compliance mechanisms).

Ethics statement

This study was reviewed by Korea University Anam Hospital Institutional Review Board (No. 2025AN0372). This study did not involve patient data; interviews were conducted with healthcare staff about institutional practices, and verbal informed consent was obtained from all interviewees.

RESULTS

Storage facilities and equipment

All three countries emphasize the use of purpose-built cold storage units for vaccines, but the strictness of requirements varies (Table 1). Korea’s guidelines recommend dedicated vaccine refrigerators or freezers. Use of household combination fridge–freezers is discouraged; if a general-use refrigerator is used, vaccines must be kept in a separate, clearly labeled container to avoid contamination. Japan’s national guidance requires storage in a refrigerator in which temperature can be verified by thermometer; purpose built medical/vaccine refrigerators are widely used and recommended, but not legally mandated.6 Taiwan imposes similarly strict standards: its guidelines strictly ban the use of household refrigerators for vaccines, allowing only equipment proven to reliably maintain the 2°C to 8°C range. In Taiwan, using a non-compliant fridge is not permitted, reflecting a de facto requirement for specialized vaccine cold storage units.

Continuous temperature monitoring is another pillar of equipment standards in all three countries. Korea’s 2021 guidelines call for digital data loggers (DDLs) with alarm functionality to continuously record temperatures, supplemented by manual thermometer checks twice daily. Continuous monitoring and alarms are widely used in Japan, but the national implementation guidelines do not codify them as legal requirements; facilities typically employ max–min thermometers and/or data loggers as standard practice. Alarm systems in Japan are recommended but not yet universal, so some facilities rely on periodic manual checks of the max–min readings. Taiwan has implemented the most proactive approach, equipping virtually every vaccination site with government-provided electronic data loggers that have built-in alarms. Taiwan’s protocols require staff to respond to alarms promptly, and devices are routinely checked and calibrated to ensure accuracy.

Each country’s guidance includes measures to maintain stable temperatures inside refrigerators. Korea instructs providers to avoid overpacking vaccines and to leave space between vaccine packages and refrigerator walls for air circulation. Clinics in Korea are also advised to place thermal stabilizers in any empty space, which helps buffer temperature fluctuations when the door opens. Japan similarly cautions against storing vaccines near the refrigerator’s cold air outlet. Japanese guidelines encourage using units with internal fans or otherwise ensuring air is circulated evenly. Taiwan takes a data-driven approach by performing temperature mapping of each storage unit and organizing vaccine placement accordingly. For example, in Taiwanese clinics, more heat-tolerant vaccines such as live-attenuated vaccines might be placed in slightly warmer areas of the fridge identified by mapping, while more heat-sensitive vaccines occupy cooler spots, ensuring all products remain within safe ranges. In addition to temperature control, all three countries require protecting vaccines from light exposure. Vaccines are kept in their original packaging as a light barrier in Korea and Japan, per each country’s guidelines. Taiwan adds an extra layer of caution by including light-sensitive indicator cards with vaccine shipments. These cards allow Taiwanese providers to monitor if vials have been exposed to excessive light and ensure any over-exposed doses are not used.

Regular maintenance and calibration of equipment are universally encouraged, with some differences in implementation. Korea recommends using auto-defrost vaccine refrigerators to prevent ice buildup that could affect temperature stability. In addition, it is recommended that thermometers and data loggers undergo calibration at least annually, and that calibration certificates be maintained on file. Japan formalizes maintenance responsibilities by designating a specific staff person at each facility to oversee vaccine storage equipment. Japanese guidelines require that temperature monitoring devices meet pharmaceutical calibration standards, and facilities are expected to service their refrigerators on a routine schedule. Taiwan mandates regular servicing and calibration checks for all cold chain equipment. Taiwanese local health bureaus often audit clinics to verify that refrigerators and thermometers/data loggers have been properly maintained and possess up-to-date calibration records, ensuring precision in temperature readings.

Provisions for backup storage and power supply in case of equipment failure or outages show marked differences. Korea only encourages clinics to have contingency plans, such as maintaining a spare refrigerator or an uninterruptible power supply, but these are not strictly required. The Korean guidelines advise keeping refrigerator doors closed during power outages and suggest relocating vaccines to a pre-identified alternative storage if an outage is prolonged, but compliance relies on individual clinic initiative. Japan similarly lacks any formal requirement for backup vaccine storage units or generators. Japanese guidance offers basic advice but stops short of mandating standby equipment. Taiwan, in contrast, has robust backup arrangements built into its system. Taiwan’s health authorities maintain regional vaccine storage hubs with emergency power, and each clinic is integrated into an emergency response network. If a refrigerator fails or a power outage occurs in Taiwan, vaccines can be swiftly moved to a nearby government-run cold storage facility or a backup generator kicks in to maintain refrigeration, preventing losses.

1) Detection and Data: Korea’s protocol calls for immediately recording details of the excursion, including the exact temperature reached, the time and duration of the incident, and the ambient room temperature. Staff are instructed to keep the refrigerator or freezer door closed to preserve the internal temperature and to leave any electronic data loggers running to capture continuous data. Japan instructs providers to promptly check the maximum–minimum thermometer memory to determine how far out of range the temperature went, and to measure the current internal temperature, all while ensuring the unit remains closed. Taiwan requires an immediate response: as soon as an excursion is detected, staff must notify public health authorities right away and document all relevant data, again without opening the storage unit. In all three countries, the first step emphasizes gathering accurate data about the excursion and stabilizing conditions by not exposing the vaccines to further temperature change.

2) Securing vaccines: Upon confirming a temperature excursion, Korean guidelines advise providers to isolate the affected vaccines in place and clearly label them “Do Not Use” until a thorough evaluation can be completed. This prevents potentially compromised doses from being administered. In Japan, providers evaluate affected vaccines against package inserts and stability information; when viability is uncertain, discard is the standard, conservative practice. The decision is largely left to the provider, following product-specific instructions. Taiwan also requires that all vaccines exposed to an excursion be segregated from other stock. Taiwanese guidelines additionally employ chemical indicators–for instance, freeze indicator cards–placed with vaccines; if a vial shows evidence of freezing, that vaccine is definitively discarded. Any vials that were exposed to out-of-range temperatures are kept separated pending further instructions.

3) Notification: Korea does not mandate automatic external reporting of every excursion; instead, providers are instructed to first take internal corrective measures and then report the incident to public health authorities if the situation meets certain criteria or cannot be resolved internally. In practice, many minor excursions in Korea may be handled at the clinic level without formal reports, though documentation is encouraged for internal records. The implementation guidelines do not establish a national, across-the-board mandatory reporting system for storage excursions. Local governments or public health centers may request consultation or reporting as part of their regional program operations, but this is not an explicit national legal requirement. Taiwan, on the other hand, enforces mandatory immediate notification to the health authorities for any cold chain breach. As soon as an excursion is recognized in Taiwan, the clinic must report it through the established reporting system, and they may be asked to submit temperature logs and other data for review.

4) Evaluation and decision: After securing the vaccines, Korean protocol calls for consulting with the vaccine manufacturer or referring to stability data to determine whether the affected doses can still be used. If there is evidence that potency may have been lost, the guidance is to discard those vaccines. Japan largely leaves the decision to the provider’s professional judgment. Japanese guidelines indicate that if there is any doubt about a vaccine’s integrity after an excursion, it should be considered compromised and not used. However, the absence of a strict reporting system means this evaluation is often done internally by the clinic’s staff. Taiwan uses a more systematic approach: the evaluation of questionable vaccines is conducted jointly with local health authorities. The Taiwan CDC will assess the situation, and any doubt about a vaccine’s viability results in mandatory disposal of that vaccine. This ensures a conservative stance where potentially ineffectual doses do not get administered.

Vaccine inventory management and disposal

All three countries enforce a first-expiry-first-out (FEFO) approach to manage vaccine inventories, ensuring that doses with the earliest expiration dates are used first so that none expire on the shelf. Korea’s guidelines mandate regular stock rotation checks and strictly prohibit using any vaccine past its expiration date (Table 3). Japan likewise requires FEFO stock management and maintains detailed ledgers tracking vaccine lots by their expiry, which helps staff identify the next batches to use. Taiwan also adheres to FEFO rigorously, with zero tolerance for keeping expired vaccines in inventory. All three countries instruct that any expired doses be removed from storage immediately. Korean clinics conduct frequent inventory reviews to pull and discard expired vials without delay. In Japan, periodic ledger reviews ensure that any vaccine found past its expiration date is promptly removed. Taiwan implements especially rigorous audits of storage sites, swiftly removing any expired doses during inspections to prevent inadvertent use.

Approaches to redistributing near-expiry or excess vaccine doses differ. Korea currently has no formal system for redistributing soon-to-expire vaccines between providers. If a clinic in Korea finds it has surplus doses or stock nearing expiration, it is generally left to that clinic to find a way to use or transfer those doses. Japan, by contrast, leverages a more organized approach: providers submit periodic inventory reports that the health authorities can use to identify surpluses. This reporting system in Japan allows for reallocation of vaccine stock—vaccines can be transferred from clinics with oversupply to those in need, helping minimize waste. Taiwan also engages in active redistribution, albeit in a centralized manner. Taiwan CDC will occasionally collect nearly expired doses from local facilities and redistribute them for use in mass vaccination campaigns or other needs. This central adjustment mechanism in Taiwan ensures that vaccines are used efficiently at the national level, reducing the likelihood of any doses going unused.

Disposal of unused or expired vaccines is handled as hazardous waste in all three countries. Korea requires that wasted vaccines be treated as medical waste and disposed of by incineration. Japan follows a similar practice, categorizing discarded vaccines as pharmaceutical waste that must be incinerated in compliance with medical waste regulations. Taiwan classifies unused vaccines as infectious medical waste and likewise uses high-temperature incineration for their disposal. While the physical disposal methods are similar, the incentives and penalties related to vaccine wastage vary notably. Korea does not offer any compensation for vaccine wastage; there is no formal reimbursement program, so any loss from expired or spoiled vaccines is typically absorbed by the provider or healthcare facility. Japan also lacks a government compensation scheme for wasted vaccines. This arrangement is operationally enforced by the reimbursement model: vaccines are purchased by providers (clinics or hospitals), and reimbursement from the local municipality is provided only after administration. Consequently, if a dose expires or is mishandled before use, the resulting financial loss is borne out-of-pocket by the provider, creating a strong financial incentive for diligent management and adherence to product labeling.8 In Taiwan, however, a differentiated approach is in place: the government will reimburse clinics for vaccine losses deemed unavoidable. On the other hand, if wastage is deemed preventable and due to provider negligence, the Taiwanese program can hold the facility financially responsible for the cost of those doses. This policy in Taiwan creates both an incentive to follow best practices and a deterrent against carelessness, in contrast to the more laissez-faire approaches of Korea and Japan.

Governance and compliance mechanisms

Korea’s vaccine cold chain guidelines currently have an administrative, non-legally binding status, though efforts are underway to elevate key requirements into formal regulations (Table 4). The KDCA issues detailed guidance, and while compliance is strongly encouraged, not all aspects are yet backed by law. Japan has a legal framework that provides broad mandates for vaccine handling, but the specific standards are laid out in non-binding guidelines by professional bodies. In practice, this means Japanese providers follow published guidelines, but these detailed recommendations are not codified as enforceable law. Taiwan treats its cold chain guidelines as de facto regulations, even if they are formally guidance. Taiwan CDC issues comprehensive vaccine storage and handling guidelines which, while not explicitly legislated, are expected to be followed as if they were legal requirements – non-compliance is not tolerated. This gives Taiwan’s guidelines an authority akin to law in everyday practice.

Oversight and responsibility for cold chain compliance reflect each country’s governance structure. In Korea, oversight is shared between national and local authorities: the KDCA sets standards and provides oversight, and local public health centers also supervise and support clinics in implementing cold chain practices. This cooperative model means that both central guidelines and local monitoring play a role, although the system is still evolving. Japan relies on a more decentralized, self-regulatory approach. There is no dedicated central body routinely inspecting vaccine storage; instead, the onus is on healthcare facilities and providers to adhere to guidelines, with local health authorities intervening only occasionally. Essentially, Japan trusts medical professionals to self-monitor and maintain standards, intervening primarily when issues arise. Taiwan, by contrast, employs a strong centralized oversight model in tandem with local enforcement. Taiwan CDC centrally directs the cold chain program and mandates requirements, while local health bureaus conduct regular enforcement checks on clinics and hospitals. Thus, Taiwan combines top-down governance with on-the-ground oversight to ensure uniform compliance.

The frequency of inspections and audits for vaccine storage differs substantially. Korea to date has conducted only limited or ad hoc inspections focused on cold chain practices. There have been pilot programs and discussions about instituting regular audit schedules in Korea, but systematic, nationwide cold chain inspections are not yet fully in place. Japan rarely carries out dedicated inspections of vaccine storage conditions. Unless triggered by a specific incident, Japanese clinics are not routinely audited solely for cold chain compliance. Taiwan, however, has regular inspections of vaccine storage at clinics and hospitals. Taiwanese inspectors use standardized checklists to evaluate storage units, temperature logs, equipment maintenance records, and compliance with all guidelines. These inspections in Taiwan are scheduled and frequent, serving as a preventative measure to catch and correct any issues early.

Reporting requirements for cold chain compliance and incidents also show differences. Korea currently does not require routine reporting on storage conditions; clinics are generally not obligated to submit regular compliance reports. Vaccine storage problems or excursions in Korea are reported only on an as-needed basis. There is ongoing development of a more systematic incident reporting system, but it is not fully implemented yet. Japan imposes no regular reporting requirements to higher authorities regarding vaccine storage or temperature excursions. In the Japanese system, as long as vaccines are managed properly, there is no mandate to routinely report storage performance data or minor incidents to the government. Taiwan, in contrast, requires that any cold chain excursion or issue be reported immediately as a matter of compliance. In some cases, Taiwanese providers may also be asked to periodically submit temperature logs or compliance reports to the central database, especially if part of a quality assurance program. This formal reporting mechanism in Taiwan is an integral part of its oversight, allowing authorities to track and respond to cold chain incidents in real time.

Enforcement and penalties for non-compliance illustrate a clear gradient in regulatory strictness. Korea so far relies on “soft” enforcement measures. If a clinic repeatedly fails to follow cold chain guidelines, the main recourse is providing corrective guidance or, at most, excluding that provider from participating in certain vaccination programs until issues are resolved. There are currently few if any financial or legal penalties in Korea specifically tied to vaccine storage lapses, reflecting the guidance status of its cold chain standards. Japan also favors a light-touch approach to enforcement. Compliance is largely maintained through professional norms and peer expectations; there are limited explicit penalties for breaking cold chain protocol. A Japanese clinic that negligently handles vaccines might face reputational consequences or internal disciplinary actions, but there is little in the way of formal punitive measures by the government. Taiwan employs a much stricter enforcement regime. Health authorities in Taiwan can issue corrective orders to facilities that violate storage requirements, and non-compliant providers can be temporarily suspended from the national vaccination program. In serious cases, Taiwanese regulations allow for financial penalties imposed on the facility for the wastage. This enforceability gives Taiwan’s guidelines real “teeth” compared to the relatively advisory nature in Korea and Japan.

Finally, the training of personnel in cold chain management differs in formality. Korea’s KDCA offers training workshops and materials on vaccine storage and handling, but there is no mandatory certification or requirement for staff to undergo this training. Participation in cold chain training in Korea is often encouraged as part of continuing professional education, yet healthcare facilities are not obligated to have certified cold chain managers. Japan similarly incorporates vaccine handling topics into general continuing education for healthcare workers, without a dedicated or compulsory certification program. Japanese clinicians and nurses are expected to stay informed of best practices, but there is no formal license or test for vaccine storage competency. Taiwan, however, treats training as a compulsory element of cold chain governance. Taiwan CDC regularly holds mandatory training seminars that all vaccine-providing healthcare facilities must attend. Each clinic in Taiwan is required to designate a staff member as the responsible vaccine storage manager, and this person must undergo the CDC’s training on cold chain practices. Through this certification-like system, Taiwan ensures that at least one person at each site has proven knowledge of proper vaccine storage, adding yet another layer of assurance in its cold chain management.

DISCUSSION

Across Korea, Japan, and Taiwan, the fundamental requirements for vaccine cold chain management—maintaining storage at +2°C to +8°C, using dedicated refrigeration equipment, continuous temperature monitoring, strict adherence to FEFO stock rotation, and proper disposal of compromised or expired doses—are universally recognized. Each country has successfully integrated these core principles into its national immunization programs, and all three have expanded technical guidance in recent years to address emerging challenges, such as ultra-low-temperature requirements for novel vaccine platforms. Yet, the manner in which these principles are implemented and enforced differs significantly, with important implications for consistency, resilience, and cost-effectiveness.

Taiwan’s highly centralized approach ensures that all vaccination sites comply with uniform, government-issued cold chain standards.9 Automated data loggers with alarm features are government-supported, and local health bureaus conduct regular, checklist-based inspections. Any temperature excursion, no matter how minor, is immediately reported and co-managed with authorities, who oversee decisions on vaccine usability. This tight coordination not only minimizes patient risk but also allows real-time system-wide learning from cold chain incidents. Taiwan’s compensation scheme further incentivizes diligent management by reimbursing unavoidable losses while holding clinics financially accountable for preventable waste.

Japan, by contrast, relies on a decentralized, trust-based model that emphasizes professional responsibility.10 While purpose-built refrigeration and continuous temperature monitoring are longstanding requirements, detailed practices are set in non-binding guidelines rather than statutory regulations. Japanese clinics self-manage excursions based on established stability criteria and generally discard any dose that falls outside safe limits. A robust local network for routine stock reporting and redistribution helps prevent unnecessary waste. However, the lack of mandatory reporting for storage incidents can leave national authorities unaware of recurring vulnerabilities and may limit opportunities for targeted improvements.

Korea occupies a middle ground. Since issuing its first comprehensive national guideline in 2021, Korea has promoted automated monitoring, detailed excursion response steps, and recommendations for emergency backup arrangements. Yet, many provisions remain advisory, and compliance verification is uneven across regions. Clinics handle minor temperature deviations internally, reporting only significant incidents to authorities. Inventory redistribution relies on informal coordination rather than a formal system. As a result, cold chain practices in smaller clinics can lag behind those in better-resourced centers. Nevertheless, Korea’s system benefits from clear, up-to-date technical guidance and a growing awareness of the need for stronger oversight and shared learning. Our field investigations also revealed minor, yet notable, discrepancies between official guidelines and routine practices. For instance, while national guidelines in all three countries mandate twice-daily manual temperature checks, our on-site observations found that this was not uniformly practiced. In several clinics, staff primarily relied on continuous DDLs and alarms, with manual checks sometimes performed only once daily. This practical “drift” was often justified by staff as a reasonable adaptation to high workloads, given the reliability of modern DDL technology. This finding suggests that while robust guidelines are essential, future policy revisions should also consider the real-world operational workflows and technological advancements that influence on-the-ground implementation, ensuring that guidelines are perceived as both practical and necessary rather than purely administrative.

By examining these different governance models, it becomes clear that no single approach is without trade-offs. Taiwan’s centralized enforcement delivers uniformity and rapid incident response but demands substantial administrative capacity and ongoing support for providers.11 Japan’s decentralized framework reduces regulatory burden and fosters local innovation but risks inconsistency and limited system-level visibility. Korea’s evolving hybrid model has the advantage of detailed guidance and local autonomy but requires further measures—such as formalized reporting, structured redistribution, and legal reinforcement—to achieve the full reliability seen in Taiwan’s system.

This study has several limitations. First, our analysis is based on policies and field investigations conducted in the first half of 2025; given the dynamic nature of public health, regulations and practices may have evolved since our data collection. Second, as the reviewer insightfully noted, our discussion of certain “ideal” policies, such as Taiwan's reimbursement for unavoidable vaccine loss, does not delve into the significant practical and bureaucratic challenges of implementation. Defining “unavoidable loss” in a way that prevents moral hazard while fairly compensating providers is a complex administrative task that warrants its own in-depth study. Finally, this comparative analysis is focused on three high-income East Asian countries with advanced healthcare systems. Therefore, the findings and policy recommendations may not be directly generalizable to countries with different socioeconomic statuses, health infrastructures, or governance models.

The comparative analysis of cold chain management in Korea, Japan, and Taiwan highlights a spectrum of strategies for translating global best practices into national immunization programs. Taiwan demonstrates the power of centralized standards, proactive oversight, and incentive alignment to achieve consistently high levels of compliance and rapid response to storage breaches.12 Japan’s longstanding reliance on professional norms and facility-level responsibility shows that a trust-based system can sustain strong outcomes, provided there is a culture of diligence and a robust network for stock redistribution.13 Korea, having developed comprehensive guidelines and upgraded technical capabilities, now stands at the threshold of strengthening its governance framework to ensure uniform implementation and resilience.

To build on recent progress, Korea should consider several targeted reforms. Our finding that Japan’s redistribution system successfully minimizes waste directly informs our recommendation to institute periodic inventory reports that enable systematic stock sharing. Similarly, our analysis of Taiwan’s high compliance, which is driven by de facto regulatory status and regular audits, supports our proposal to give Korea’s guidelines formal legal status and institute routine compliance checks. Finally, Taiwan’s differentiated compensation scheme provides a clear model for our recommendation to align financial incentives with best practices—reimbursing unavoidable losses while holding providers accountable for preventable waste. By adopting these specific, proven measures from its neighbors, Korea can forge a balanced, resilient cold chain framework that protects vaccine potency, minimizes waste, and maintains public confidence in immunization programs.
