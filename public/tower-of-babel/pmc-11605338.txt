Investigation of fiber Bragg grating sensor measurability in concrete beams under static load conditions
Authors: Mohammad Hany Yassin, Mohamad Hussein Farhat, Michel Nahas, Ahmad Said Saad
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Recent advances in construction materials and architecture have enhanced the safety of modern structures, which now feature higher, lighter, and more unique designs. Bridges, as critical infrastructure, face increasing load demands due to rising traffic volumes, leading to accelerated structural deterioration. This necessitates more frequent inspections and maintenance, resulting in increased costs and potential operational disruptions. Real-time built-in monitoring systems are thus essential for both new and aging structures. Fiber Optic Sensor (FOS) technology, particularly Fiber Bragg Grating (FBG) sensors, offers a reliable and stable solution for long-term Structural Health Monitoring (SHM). This study evaluates the performance and applicability of FBG sensors (FBGs) in comparison to traditional strain gauges (SGs) for capturing strains, loads, deflections, and temperature in full-scale beams under static loads. Four large-scale reinforced concrete (RC) and CFRP-strengthened beams were tested. Nonlinear finite element analysis (NLFEA) using VecTor2 was employed to simulate the behavior of these beams under static loading. Data from FBGs were compared to those from collocated electrical SGs. The results demonstrated the reliability and flexibility of FBGs in monitoring structural performance. Notably, FBGs continued to provide accurate readings even after the specimens reached their maximum load, showcasing their endurance and resilience compared to conventional SGs.

Introduction and background

Over the past 20 years, significant advancements in SHM have emerged, driven by new sensor technologies, monitoring systems, and non-destructive tests (NDT) methods capable of assessing structural performance [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Preserving the integrity of critical infrastructure, particularly bridges, is essential for economic stability, as neglecting inspection and maintenance can lead to severe financial repercussions.

FBGs have gained increasing attention in SHM for their lightweight design, high sensitivity, and immunity to electromagnetic interference, making them ideal for monitoring structural performance in challenging environments such as aerospace, civil engineering, and underground structures [[6], [7], [8]]. FBGs provide real-time data on strain, temperature, and other parameters, facilitating proactive maintenance and safety assessments that extend the service life of infrastructure [6,9,10].

In civil engineering, FBGs have been effectively used for monitoring bridges and other structures. For example, Shen and Jiang [11] demonstrated the use of FBGs for deformation monitoring in bridge components, while Liu et al. [7] highlighted their effectiveness in underground structures. Their integration into structural components enables continuous assessment of stresses and strains, allowing timely interventions to prevent failures [8,12].

FBGs are also widely used in the aerospace sector to monitor composite materials and structures due to their high strength-to-weight ratios [13,14]. These sensors provide critical real-time data to inform maintenance decisions, enhancing the safety and reliability of aircraft [15,16]. Additionally, integrating FBGs into the manufacturing processes of composite materials improves monitoring capabilities [17].

FBGs also offer valuable applications in monitoring geological stability and landslides, providing high-precision measurements over long distances [10,18]. This capability is particularly beneficial for civil engineering projects where ground stability is critical, as presented in Ref. [18], which demonstrated their use in monitoring deformations during landslide events.

Recent developments in hybrid monitoring systems combine FBGs with other technologies to enhance damage detection. For example, Jinachandran and Rajan [15] explored integrating FBGs with acoustic emission techniques, providing a more comprehensive understanding of structural behavior under various conditions.

In RC bridges, asphalt concrete is used for its waterproofing properties, protecting structures from water-induced corrosion and extending service life [19]. Integrating FBGs into such infrastructure, especially asphalt concrete, offers significant potential for real-time monitoring [20,21]. These sensors help in proactive maintenance strategies, enhancing the longevity of pavement structures [21]. Furthermore, advancements in materials like polymer-modified asphalt, which improve durability, highlight the relevance of FBGs in monitoring enhanced materials [22].

Significance of structural integrity, economic impact, and advanced monitoring for bridges

Maintaining the structural integrity of bridges is crucial for ensuring public safety and economic stability. Neglecting routine inspections and preventive maintenance can lead to deterioration, increased risk of failures, and severe social and economic consequences [[23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]]. Advanced SHM systems, such as those utilizing FBGs, are essential for continuous monitoring, early defect detection, and extending the lifespan of critical infrastructure.

Bridges are key components of national infrastructure, playing a vital role in the economy by facilitating the movement of goods and people. Poor maintenance can result in costly repairs, service disruptions, and reduced economic productivity [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34]]. Implementing SHM systems with FBGs helps optimize maintenance strategies, reduce costs, and ensure the long-term preservation of structural integrity.

Bridge failures can occur due to various factors, including design errors, aging, environmental impacts, and inadequate maintenance [[35], [36], [37], [38], [39]]. Recent incidents have underscored the need for reliable monitoring technologies, such as FBGs, to detect early signs of structural distress and enhance the resilience of bridges against unforeseen failures.

To address these challenges, the use of FRP materials, particularly Carbon Fiber Reinforced Polymers (CFRPs), has gained prominence in retrofitting and strengthening concrete structures. FRPs offer high strength-to-weight ratios, durability, and resistance to corrosion, making them ideal for reinforcing existing bridge components [[40], [41], [42], [43]] as well as other structural and nonstructural components [44,45]. When used in newly constructed structures, FBGs enhance monitoring and maintenance capabilities by providing clearer evaluations of the condition of the structure and its loading history. This approach helps optimize rehabilitation scenarios and assists with the selection and design of FRP strengthening techniques. Additionally, FBGs can detect interfacial strains between FRP laminates and concrete, providing an early warning for potential debonding or other defects in FRP-strengthened structures [46,47].

Modern SHM systems, incorporating FBGs, provide real-time monitoring and early warnings of structural issues [3]. These sensors are particularly effective in environments with temperature fluctuations, offering accurate and reliable data for better management of infrastructure health [9,[48], [49], [50], [51], [52], [53], [54]]. The integration of FBGs with advanced materials like FRPs represents a forward-looking approach to enhancing bridge performance and longevity.

Advantages and application of FBGs in SHM

FBGs based FOS operate differently from traditional electrical SGs. FBG is a microstructure that is a few millimeters in length, created by modifying a standard single-mode telecom fiber which is doped with germanium using a UV laser. The microstructure present in the optical fiber causes a repetitive change in the refractive index [54,55]. As light travels through the fiber, the Bragg grating reflects only a specific range of wavelengths, while allowing all other wavelengths to pass through. The center of this narrow band of reflected wavelengths is known as the Bragg wavelength [56]. When an optical fiber is stretched or compressed under stress, the period of an FBG increases, leading to a shift in the Bragg wavelength, which is detected and recorded by the interrogator (i.e., data acquisition system) [56,57].

FBGs have several advantages over electrical SGs. These devices ensure long-term signal stability and system durability, even when exposed to high levels of vibratory loads, such as those encountered on heavily used highways and bridges [56,57]. Prior research has demonstrated the excellent ability of FBGs to measure strain and temperature concurrently [[58], [59], [60], [61], [62]] or to thermally compensate strain measurements [63,64]. Parameters such as tilt, acceleration, pressure, etc., can also be measured and monitored using FBG-based sensors applied to transducers. The FOS, particularly those based on Bragg grating, to electromagnetic and radio frequency interference (EMI/RFI) is highly advantageous in railway structures, such as bridges and tunnels designed for electrically powered trains. These trains generate intense electromagnetic fields, and the immunity of embedded FOS to EMI/RFI makes them an ideal choice for addressing stress redistribution issues in such environments. Notable study by Bock [65] consistently highlight the benefits of FOS in civil engineering structures, emphasizing their EMI resistance and compatibility with fiber-optic data transmission networks.

However, embedding FBG-based FOS in layered composite materials may lead to stress concentrations around the sensor due to material property discontinuities [[66], [67], [68]]. Encapsulating the FBG within a capillary tube can mitigate the effects of transverse strains on FOS measurements. Research has shown that encapsulating the FBG in this manner preserves a single peak in the reflected optical spectrum after the composite material fabrication process [69]. Other studies utilized capillary encapsulation to increase FBG temperature sensitivity for sensing applications [68].

Additional studies have explored numerous other applications of FBGs in SHM. For example, Braunfelds et al. [52] developed a strain measurement calibration technique for monitoring road pavement, while Qin et al. [70] designed a new variable sensitivity FBG strain sensor for SHM with an accuracy of ±5 %. The fabrication and validation of FBGs using Internet of Things (IoT) and big data, attached to a bridge base plate, were discussed by Mohapatra et al. [71]. Furthermore, Velázquez-Carreón et al. [72] proposed a method to increase the sensitivity of FBG-embedded devices by enhancing the reinforcement in the area where the FBG is embedded. In another study, Luís Pereira et al. [73] reported on the development, characterization, and installation of a simple and low visual impact FBG-based sensing system for long-term crack monitoring in the historical Monastery of Batalha. Given these applications, the use of FBGs in SHM is both timely and critical for advancing sustainable transport infrastructure.

This research presents a novel approach to evaluating the performance of FBGs in real-world conditions by integrating them into full-scale RC and CFRP strengthened beams. Unlike previous studies that have predominantly focused on laboratory-controlled environments, this study uniquely situates the full-scale frame outside the Civil Engineering Laboratory, exposing it to actual weather conditions in Kuwait during the summer months (May to August). This outdoor testing environment introduces variables such as high temperatures and fluctuating humidity, which are often excluded in controlled laboratory tests. The study further distinguishes itself by comparing FBGs with traditional SGs, providing a comprehensive evaluation of FBG sensor performance in terms of their accuracy, reliability, and practical applicability under extreme environmental conditions. Additionally, the use of both destructive and non-destructive testing methods on small cylindrical samples to verify the quality of the concrete material adds another layer of rigor and authenticity to the findings. This research bridges the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical application, offering insights into the real-world effectiveness of FBGs in SHM systems for critical infrastructure.

Methodology

The research evaluated the performance of FBGs in monitoring full-scale RC and CFRP strengthened beams (RC-CFRP) under static loading conditions. The full-scale frame was placed outside the Civil Engineering Laboratory at the Australian University in Kuwait, and the testing was conducted between May and August, reflecting the real weather conditions in Kuwait during the summer, characterized by high temperatures and challenging environmental factors.

The concrete beams were prepared using a mix designed according to ASTM standards, with materials locally sourced. Beams were cast in custom-made wooden molds and cured under conditions replicating the actual environment. A three-point bending test was performed on the beams using a full-scale loading frame to assess their behavior under static loads.

Both destructive and non-destructive tests were conducted on small cylindrical samples to check the quality of the concrete material. Ultrasonic pulse velocity tests and compressive strength tests were performed to evaluate the material properties and ensure consistency. The data obtained from the FBGs were compared to that from traditional SGs to determine the accuracy of the sensors and reliability under harsh environmental conditions.

Destructive and non-destructive testing of concrete

To monitor strength development, both destructive and non-destructive tests were conducted on cylindrical concrete samples at two maturity ages of 7 and 28 days as per the related standards.

The installation of CFRP has followed the ACI 440.2R-17 guidelines [80]. At day 28 of curing, the concrete surface was prepared prior to CFRP application by grinding to remove contaminants and create a slightly rough texture, improving bonding effectiveness. Once cleaned, epoxy resin was applied evenly across the prepared surface, and the CFRP laminate was carefully positioned onto the epoxy-coated area, ensuring full contact with minimal air pockets. To avoid excessive daytime heat, the application was conducted at night when the temperature ranged between 35 and 40 °C. After application, the beams were left outdoors to cure for seven days, allowing the epoxy to set under stable conditions and form a robust bond with the concrete for effective reinforcement. The effectiveness of FBGs in two key aspects was the primary focus of these configurations. Firstly, the capability of FBGs to detect debonding between CFRP and concrete was examined. Secondly, the study aimed to evaluate the capacity of these sensors in measuring large strain values. This investigation was essential for understanding the performance and reliability of FBGs in SHM of concrete beams reinforced with CFRP.

Beams were designed with a flexural failure mechanism to assess the capacity and reliability of FBGs in capturing tensile strains accurately. The test parameters were designed to ensure a controlled and predictable failure mode, primarily due to bending stress rather than shear. This approach simulated conditions that maximally engaged flexural reinforcement, allowing for a comprehensive understanding of the maximum strain limits detected by the sensors. Reinforcement distribution was planned to effectively distribute loads, especially at critical support points, in alignment with the structural requirements outlined in the ACI-318 standard [81]. This specific design and casting process was crucial in evaluating the performance of the FBGs in capturing strains, loads, deflections, and temperature within these beams, thereby confirming their suitability for use in SHM.

Experiment setup

Three-point bending tests were conducted on beams using a full-scale loading frame available at the Australian University - Kuwait, as depicted in Fig. 4, Fig. 5. The beams were anchored from the top side at both ends and subjected to an uplift monotonic load at midspan, inducing a negative moment. This method employed self-strained configuration with the beams inverted to utilize the maximal tensile capacity of the loading frame. As shown in Fig. 6, stiffened steel support beams with drilled webs were utilized to provide the required end supports, allowing high strength threaded rods to secure the beams to the bottom beam of the frame. Temporary scaffolding supported the concrete beams before testing, with the tension side reinforced with CFRP laminates and/or internal steel facing upward. Lateral restraints were provided through the frictional interaction between the supporting steel beams and the concrete. Instruments

The instrumentation plan was designed to capture a comprehensive range of mechanical responses from the tested beam, enabling a detailed analysis of the structural behavior up to the point of failure. It consisted of mechanical instruments for load application, reaction forces, and displacement measurements, in additionto the FBGs and foil SGs, connected to an interrogator and Data Acquisition System (DAQ) system for strain measurements.

Mechanical instruments

The mechanical setup for the experiment consisted of an Enerpac hydraulic jack with a capacity of 550 kN (62 tons), selected for its ability to apply a quasi-static load until failure. Positioned directly below the hydraulic jack was a RAL1-100 KS load cell, capable of measuring up to 445kN, which was used to capture the applied load. The load cell readings were monitored using a DI-1000UHS-10K DAQ with a specified sampling rate, ensuring real-time data capture. Additionally, deflection at midspan was measured using a Linear Variable Displacement Transducer (LVDT) with a 100 mm range, and the LVDT data were recorded using a D1I-1000HS-1K DAQ system.

FBG interrogator and data acquisition and monitoring system

The data acquisition from the FBGs was managed by an FBG X100 Interrogator from FiSens, which featured an embedded light source and onboard processing. It was capable of sampling at rates ranging from 1 to 100 Hz, with a wavelength bandwidth of 808–880 nm, ensuring measurement precision of 0.1 °C and 1 με. The strain gauge data were collected using a Microlink 770 unit, which supported 16 channels, 16-bit data acquisition, and a sampling rate of up to 100 KHz. Applied load data was captured by the DI-1000UHS-10K High-Speed USB Load Cell Interface, operating at sampling rates of up to 50 KHz. Additionally, the DI-1000U-5V was used to interface the displacement sensor, providing 24-bit resolution with a sampling rate of 80 Hz. For comparative accuracy, traditional SGs were installed alongside the FBGs on the steel reinforcement bars and the beam's tension surface, allowing for cross-verification of strain measurements and a comprehensive analysis of strain distribution across the structure. This instrumentation plan was designed to capture a full spectrum of mechanical responses from the tested beam, facilitating a detailed analysis of the structural behavior until failure.

Finite element analysis

A two-dimensional finite element model (FEM) was developed to simulate the behavior of an RC_C beam. The NLFEA program VecTor2 [82] was utilized for this study primarily due to its proficiency in simulating the behaviors of RC. This was achieved through a smeared, rotating-crack approach based on the Modified Compression Field Theory (MCF) [83] and the Disturbed Stress Field Model (DCFM) [84].

The model utilized two continuum materials: steel and concrete. The steel and concrete materials were defined as per the properties in Table 2, Table 3 The beam was modeled using 2D quadratic elements to represent concrete, and 1D truss elements were used for steel reinforcement. These truss elements were constrained to the concrete elements at the common nodes, ensuring accurate load transfer under the assumption of a perfect bond between steel and concrete. Initial element size was set to 100 mm and was subsequently refined to 80 mm, 50 mm, 20 mm, and finally 10 mm. For each trial, the total reaction at the supports was captured and compared to the experimental values. The mesh size was optimized to match the dimensions of the steel and concrete elements, ensuring proper constraints for complete bonding and accurately capturing the resistance of the beam as measured in the experiment. The impact of the mesh size of the results of the FE analysis is presented in Fig. 15. A mesh size of 20 mm was selected, as it provided acceptable results with minimal error and relatively short calculation time. Additionally, mesh refinement was employed to ensure convergence and enhance the accuracy of the FEM results.

The top surface of the beam was restrained at two locations corresponding to the ends of beam to replicate the actual support conditions. Five separate load cases were defined in the analysis: the initial load case represented the dead load of the beam, while the remaining four cases represented the application of upward displacement at the center of the bottom face of the beam, in a monotonic fashion, applied in four stages, to replicate the slow application of the load of the experiment.

A secant stiffness formulation coupled with a total-load iterative procedure was employed for enhanced numerical robustness and convergence. Discussion and conclusion

FBGs demonstrated outstanding reliability, accuracy, and robustness under extreme environmental conditions, such as the intense summer temperatures in Kuwait. Unlike traditional SGs, FBGs maintained accurate strain measurements even under high-load conditions, successfully recording strains up to 16,000 με. Their superior sensitivity enabled the early detection of minor deformations, such as crack initiation and propagation, underscoring high precision of FBGs for SHM applications.

The robustness of FBGs to electrical noise interference, which often affects SG readings, enhanced their reliability in environments with fluctuating temperatures and electromagnetic noise. The lightweight design, chemical compatibility, and multiplexing capabilities of FBGs make them ideal for long-term SHM in harsh conditions, establishing them as the preferred choice for SHM in infrastructure subjected to extreme environments.

In monitoring RC and RC-CFRP beams, FBGs proved highly effective in detecting critical behaviors, including debonding and strain escalation. In CFRP_U beams, sensors captured substantial strain increases, exceeding 15,000 με before stabilization, marking their sensitivity to early failure signs and post crack performance. The U-wrap configuration enhanced confinement and bonding strength, facilitating more effective load transfer than single-sided reinforcement. Notably, staged debonding was observed between side laminates and concrete, indicating improved load transfer capabilities. The precise placement of FBGs allowed for detailed monitoring of these behaviors, reinforcing their essential role in SHM applications, with capabilities for detecting the transition from linear to nonlinear behavior and registering debonding events via sudden load drops and experimental observation (audible snaps).

Integrating FBGs with traditional SGs offered complementary SHM data, providing a more reliable and comprehensive understanding of the structural performance of the tested beams. This combined approach aligns with findings by Zhang et al. [85] and Karatas et al. [86], which suggest that more accurate SHM data enables proactive maintenance and repair, ultimately extending the lifespan of critical infrastructure. The combination of FBG technology with advanced materials such as CFRP offers a robust solution for extending the service life of concrete structures.
