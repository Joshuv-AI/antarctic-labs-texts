Perioperative Metabolomic Profiling in Neonatal Cardiac Surgery Identifies a High-Risk Phenotype for Adverse Outcomes
Authors: Sean J. Cooney, Bhargava K. Chinni, Eric M. Graham, Mark Russell, Brian W. McCrindle, Cedric Manlhiot, Kurt W. Prins, Allen D. Everett, David R. Graham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeonatal cardiac surgery is associated with high morbidity and mortality. Perioperative metabolic stress may result in distinct metabolic phenotypes associated with adverse clinical outcomes.ObjectivesThe purpose of this study was to identify perioperative metabolomic phenotypes associated with adverse clinical outcomes in neonates undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB).MethodsPlasma samples were collected from 230 neonates at 3 institutions at preoperative, post-CPB, and 24-hour time points. Targeted metabolomics was performed via mass spectrometry. Feature selection used the Boruta algorithm, and unsupervised hierarchical clustering (Euclidean distance, Ward’s linkage) identified phenotypes. The primary endpoint was a composite of morbidity, including death, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, cardiac arrest, renal replacement therapy, hepatic injury, elevated lactate, and low cardiac output syndrome.ResultsFifty-one of 76 metabolites passed quality control. Unsupervised clustering identified 3 metabolomic groups with composite outcome rates of 40%, 6.1%, and 58%. For clinical interpretation, these corresponded to intermediate-, low-, and high-risk phenotypes, respectively. The high-risk phenotype was characterized by elevated 24-hour tyrosine, alanine, proline, and methionine, metabolites linked to amino acid catabolism and inflammation. The low-risk phenotype demonstrated higher post-CPB cystine and greater pre/post ratios of galactitol and acetylcarnitine, consistent with preserved redox balance and fatty acid oxidation. Multiple comparison adjustments confirmed associations between these metabolic pathways and clinical outcomes.ConclusionsMetabolomic profiling identified phenotypes associated with risk for adverse outcomes after neonatal cardiac surgery. Amino acid catabolism and inflammatory pathways were linked to morbidity, supporting further evaluation of metabolomic biomarkers for perioperative risk stratification and targeted therapeutic intervention.

Approximately 1% of children born in the United States and Europe are diagnosed with congenital heart disease.1 Of those, 25% have critical congenital heart disease that requires surgery or catheter intervention within the first year of life.1 Unfortunately, neonatal surgical repair morbidity remains high with frequent complications leading to prolonged hospital length of stay (LOS) and long-term health challenges.2 Current risk models, such as the Society of Thoracic Surgeons–European Association for Cardiothoracic Surgery category, combines age, surgical complexity, and preoperative LOS to model mortality risk of surgery.3 Although such static modeling is very important for identifying risk, the clinical variables are not modifiable to decrease risk.

Metabolomics offers promise in deducing mechanisms of disease states, providing risk stratification, and understanding therapeutic responses.4 It does so by analyzing and measuring levels of metabolites, which are small molecules involved in metabolic pathways.5 Prior work suggests that preoperative metabolites predict postoperative outcomes and can improve models when combined with clinical variables,6 though validation across independent cohorts is limited. In order to target therapies to help improve outcomes, it is necessary to understand which metabolic alterations are associated with specific perioperative clinical features.

The current study is larger than prior investigations, multicenter, and focuses on neonates requiring cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) within the first 30 days of life. Our goal was to identify and quantify perioperative metabolites of interest and relate them to clinical outcomes. Understanding which perioperative metabolites associate with postoperative morbidity may inform targeted strategies to improve outcomes.

Methods

Patient population

Plasma samples and clinical data from 230 neonates (aged <30 days) undergoing CPB were collected from patients enrolled in the following 3 parent studies: 1) the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute–funded, Corticosteroid Therapy in Neonates Undergoing Cardiopulmonary Bypass trial, a randomized controlled trial of intraoperative methylprednisolone (MP) or placebo enrolled at the Medical University of South Carolina (MUSC) (NCT01579513) (N = 111)7,8; 2) University of Michigan (N = 83, UMICH); and 3) The Hospital for Sick Children in Toronto (N = 36, HSCT). Society of Thoracic Surgeons–European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (STAT) categories represent empirically derived procedural risk groups based on observed mortality for specific congenital cardiac operations in the Society of Thoracic Surgeons database and reflect predicted mortality for the index procedure, not patient-specific physiologic risk.

Across centers, 60% of patients underwent corrective procedures and 40% underwent palliative procedures, with no significant difference in distribution between sites (chi-square = 0.29, P = 0.87). The most common corrective operations were arterial switch procedures for transposition of the great arteries and arch or ventricular septal defect repairs, while hypoplastic left heart syndrome and other single-ventricle lesions predominated among palliative surgeries. Inclusion criteria were infants aged <1 month undergoing cardiac surgery with CPB. Exclusion criteria included prematurity, defined as <37 weeks postgestational age at the time of surgery and suspected infection. Exclusion included steroid exposure within 48 hours before surgery, including high-dose steroids given as part of bypass protocols because steroid exposure acutely alters amino acid metabolism and inflammatory signaling, potentially confounding perioperative metabolomic profiles.

This secondary study was approved by the Johns Hopkins Institutional Review Board.

Sample collection and handling

Plasma samples were prospectively collected under Institutional Review Board–approved protocols at each site. Samples were obtained at standardized peri-operative time points: preoperatively (prior to skin incision), immediately post-CPB, and 24 hours postoperatively. At all sites, samples were promptly processed into plasma, aliquoted to minimize repeated handling, and stored at −80 °C.

Samples from the MUSC were collected between June 2012 and November 2017 and underwent a single freeze–thaw cycle prior to analysis. Samples from The Hospital for Sick Children (Toronto) were collected between 2012 and 2016, processed immediately, and stored at −80 °C until shipment for analysis in 2021 to 2022; most underwent a single freeze–thaw cycle, with a subset experiencing one additional thaw. UoM samples were collected between 2015 and 2022, stored at −80 °C, and thawed twice prior to shipment.

All samples from all cohorts were analyzed centrally at Johns Hopkins University using the same analytical platform and in a single analytical batch to minimize technical variability. All participants were neonates; sex was included as a covariate in descriptive analyses.

Clinical covariates and definitions

Clinical variables were collected prospectively using standardized definitions across sites. Surgical complexity was characterized using the STAT mortality category, which reflects empirically derived procedural risk based on observed operative mortality for the primary cardiac operation and does not incorporate patient-specific physiologic or comorbidity factors.

Low cardiac output syndrome (LCOS) was defined according to institutional criteria requiring escalation of inotropic or mechanical support for impaired systemic perfusion. Severe hyperlactatemia was defined as a lactate concentration >5 mmol/L measured beyond 12 hours after arrival to the cardiac intensive care unit (ICU), reflecting sustained metabolic derangement rather than transient peri-operative elevations. Acute kidney injury was defined by clinical diagnosis and/or requirement for renal replacement therapy. Hepatic injury was defined as alanine aminotransferase (ALT) or aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels exceeding twice the upper limit of normal beyond 36 hours postoperatively.

Inotropic support was quantified using the inotropic score (IS) and vasoactive inotropic score (VIS), calculated using established formulas.9,10 Additional covariates included sex, gestational age, weight at surgery, CPB duration, cross-clamp time, single-ventricle physiology, and use of prostaglandin E1. Patients receiving perioperative corticosteroids were excluded because steroid exposure acutely alters amino acid metabolism and inflammatory signaling, which could confound perioperative metabolomic profiles.

Clinical covariates were used for descriptive analyses and contextual interpretation of metabolomic risk clusters but were not forced into the unsupervised clustering model, which was driven by metabolomic features.

Metabolite identification and quantification

Plasma samples were collected from neonates at 3 perioperative time points: preoperative (prior to skin incision), immediately following CPB decannulation, and 24 hours postoperatively. Targeted metabolite quantification was performed using a Shimadzu 8060 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization source, operated in positive and negative ion modes, using a 74-plex pentafluorophenylpropyl (PFPP) chromatographic method optimized for polar and semipolar metabolites.

Isotopically labeled internal standards were added at the beginning of sample extraction to control for variability in recovery and ionization efficiency. Data were processed using standardized quality control (QC) procedures, including procedural blanks and pooled QC samples, and normalized to minimize technical variability.

The PFPP assay panel comprised a targeted set of 74 metabolites primarily spanning amino acid, organic acid, nucleotide, polyamine, and related central carbon metabolism pathways. Assay content evolved modestly over time; therefore, only metabolites present on all panel versions were retained for cross-cohort analyses, as detailed in Supplemental Table 2.

Data preprocessing

Metabolite concentrations were determined as the area under the curve for the internal heavy standard. We implemented a site-specific standardization process across the 3 sites in our cohort to enhance the reliability and validity of our cluster analysis, address potential biases, and scale differences inherent in multisite data. The data set was segmented by site. When a metabolite was below the assay’s limit of detection, the value was substituted with half of the lower limit of detection. In addition to the individual metabolite levels, 3 ratio features were computed: the ratio between preop and postop, postop and 24 hours, and preop and 24 hours were calculated for each metabolite. A pairwise test was used to calculate significant changes in individual metabolites at each time point.

Features were normalized within each site by calculating the mean and SD, then calculating a standardized value for each feature by subtracting the site-specific mean and dividing it by the site-specific SD. After standardizing the features for each site, we verified the consistency by examining the distribution of the standardized features, ensuring they followed a standard normal distribution (mean = 0, SD = 1).

Clinical adverse outcome

As mortality as an outcome is low in this population, a binary morbidity/mortality clinical outcome was investigated in this analysis, the composite outcome.6 The composite outcome was met if any of the following occurred after surgery but before hospital discharge: arrest, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), hepatic injury (defined as AST or ALT >2 times normal >36 h postop), rising lactate level (defined as >5 mmol/L), renal replacement therapy, and LCOS.6 The composite outcome was defined a priori to reflect clinically meaningful postoperative morbidity: mortality, need for ECMO, renal replacement therapy, and severe metabolic disturbance (lactate >5 mmol/L beyond 12 hours). To address concerns about overlap, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding lactate from the composite outcome. Importantly, the associations between metabolite clusters and adverse outcomes remained qualitatively unchanged. This limitation has been acknowledged in the Discussion.

Hierarchical clustering analysis

Of the targeted 74-plex PFPP metabolic panel, 51 were consistently quantifiable after QC filtering (≥80% detection across samples; exclusion of metabolites with coefficient of variation (CV) >20%) (Supplemental Table 2). Unsupervised hierarchical clustering was then performed using Euclidean distance and Ward’s linkage. For univariate metabolite–outcome associations, we applied a Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Pathway enrichment analyses were interpreted cautiously given the relatively small number of metabolites per pathway. After standardizing the features, we applied feature preselection using the Boruta algorithm, integrated with a random forest classifier11 to identify key features predicting each outcome. These identified features play a role in predicting the respective outcomes and were then included in the hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA). Hierarchical clustering was performed at the patient level using a feature vector that combined metabolite concentrations measured at preoperative, post-bypass, and 24-hour time points, along with derived ratios. Each participant contributed a single perioperative metabolomic profile to the clustering analysis.

Utilizing the agglomerative clustering algorithm,12,13 we identified features and created a cluster hierarchy through successive grouping of patients based on their similarity. We tested various combinations of hyperparameters, including 3 linkage methods that set the rules for merging clusters, and considered 19 distance metrics. This unsupervised analysis approach was designed to achieve a targeted clustering outcome tailored to the unique characteristics of our multicenter data set.

For every agglomerative clustering combination, we assessed the clustering quality using the confusion matrix, accuracy, and adjusted Rand index (ARI).14 These metrics enabled a direct comparison and analysis of clustering performance across different configurations of the HCA, helping us pinpoint the specific combination that exhibited the optimal performance.

A heatmap together with a dendrogram15 was used to demonstrate the output from HCA, overlaid with the clinical outcomes, to reveal distinct clusters or regions of interest.

Fold changes were then calculated among metabolites involved in HCA and illustrated in a volcano plot. Finally, univariate analysis was used to examine the relationship between individual metabolite levels and specific clinical characteristics in perioperative care. The analysis was implemented using Python version 3.9.12.

Metabolite enrichment disease and pathway-associated analysis

Final metabolites or features from the models were then evaluated using Metaboanalyst 6.0 for disease and metabolic pathways.16 Findings were then compared for relevance to the outcome assessed and prior studies linking to diseases or pathways. Pathway analyses were performed in an exploratory fashion to place observed metabolite associations in biologic context. Given the small number of metabolites, pathways were often represented by only one or 2 compounds; we emphasize these findings are hypothesis-generating. Enrichment ratios were defined as the proportion of detected metabolites mapping to a given pathway that were significantly associated with outcomes, relative to the proportion expected by chance. Significance was estimated with the hypergeometric distribution and corrected for multiple comparisons by FDR. The Discussion has been revised to temper mechanistic speculation and emphasize alignment with prior neonatal cardiac metabolomics studies rather than definitive pathway conclusions.

Statistical analysis

Metabolite concentrations were normalized within site by z-scoring relative to the site-specific mean and SD. Features with <80% detection or CV >20% were excluded. Missing values were imputed at half the lower limit of detection. Missing values refer to metabolite concentrations below the assay’s limit of detection after QC processing and were imputed as half the lower limit of detection.

For temporal changes, paired t-tests compared metabolite concentrations between preoperative, post-bypass, and 24-hour time points, with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction for multiple testing.

Feature selection for hierarchical clustering was performed using the Boruta algorithm with a random forest classifier. Unsupervised agglomerative hierarchical clustering was then applied (Euclidean distance, Ward’s linkage), and clustering quality was assessed using accuracy and ARI.

Associations between individual metabolites (or metabolite ratios) and clinical outcomes were examined using univariate linear regression. Lactate was used only as a component of the composite outcome and was not included as a feature in metabolomic clustering analyses.

Beta coefficients with standard errors and P values are reported. FDR correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons. FDR correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method and applied within individual analyses (eg, within each table of comparisons), rather than across the entire study.

Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted with MetaboAnalyst 6.016 using a hypergeometric test and FDR adjustment. Enrichment ratios were defined as the proportion of detected metabolites mapping to each pathway that were significantly associated with outcomes relative to chance expectation.

All analyses were performed using Python version 3.9.12. Statistical significance was defined as 2-sided P < 0.05 after adjustment for multiple comparisons.

As a sensitivity analysis, we redefined the composite endpoint excluding lactate and repeated feature selection and hierarchical clustering to assess robustness of metabolite–outcome associations.

Results

Demographics

Our study cohort was composed of 230 neonates requiring congenital heart surgery with CPB during the first 30 days of life. As shown in Table 1, study participants were enrolled in the MP (N = 111) trial,7,8 UoM (N = 83), and HSCT (N = 36). Study participants from all 3 cohorts were similar in male predominance, age at repair, and gestational age. The UoM and HSCT cohorts had lower proportions of patients with Black ancestry and HSCT had a higher proportion of patients with Asian ancestry. There was similar use of preoperative prostaglandin E1 and numbers of STAT 3 or greater repairs. There were proportionally small number of STAT 2 cases in the UoM cohort and a higher proportion in the HSCT cohort. The HSCT cohort had the shortest CPB time and MP cohort the longest. The type of congenital heart disease was predominately single ventricle in all 3 cohorts except for the HSCT cohort. Overall, the prevalence of the composite outcome was 31.1%. UoM had the highest prevalence of the composite outcome at 43%. Procedural complexity differed significantly across centers (chi-square = 47.7, P < 0.001), with UoM having the highest proportion of high-complexity (STAT 3-5) repairs (≈98%) compared with MUSC (≈62%) and Toronto (≈44%). This complexity difference parallels higher composite-outcome rates and greater incidence of LCOS and hyperlactatemia at UoM.

Metabolomics analysis

Following targeted metabolite identification, quantification, and processing, 51 metabolites were quantifiable in all cohorts at all 3 time points (Table 2). Because samples were acquired across multiple cohorts, we required metabolites to be consistently detected in all cohorts for inclusion, further reducing analyte number. After stringent QC (≥80% detection, CV ≤ 20%, cross-cohort consistency), 51 of 74 high-quality metabolites remained. This yield is consistent with other neonatal plasma metabolomics data sets where rigorous harmonization is applied. Discussion

The perioperative state in neonatal cardiac surgery represents a complex metabolic and inflammatory milieu further complicated by CPB. We investigated whether perioperative changes in circulating metabolites could identify molecules and pathways associated with postoperative morbidity in a large, multicenter cohort of 230 neonates. We assembled cohorts from 3 major centers with similar age at surgery (5.7-10.1 days), gestational age (∼38 weeks), STAT category distribution, and use of deep hypothermic circulatory arrest, supporting generalizability of results. Following rigorous QC and cross-cohort harmonization, we quantified 51 metabolites. Distinct patterns of metabolite changes clustered patients into high-, intermediate-, and low-risk groups for a composite morbidity/mortality outcome, with specific metabolite profiles associated with distinct postoperative complications including LCOS, hyperlactatemia, hepatic injury, and mortality.

High-risk metabolic phenotype: amino acid dysregulation

Hierarchical clustering analysis identified elevated 24-hour tyrosine, alanine, proline, and methionine as characteristic of the high-risk phenotype. These gluconeogenic amino acids17 were significantly associated with the composite outcome, postoperative lactate elevation, hepatic injury, and mortality. While these metabolites decreased in the overall cohort from preoperative to postoperative time points, persistent elevation or inadequate clearance at 24 hours distinguished high-risk patients.

These findings align with prior pediatric cardiac surgery metabolomics studies. Heibel et al6 similarly found methionine, cystine, and proline associated with adverse outcomes in a subset of the current cohort. Davidson et al18 identified arginine/proline and alanine/aspartate/glutamate metabolism as significantly associated with survival and ICU LOS in neonates and infants undergoing CPB. Recent systematic reviews19 highlight amino acid metabolism as a consistent signal in pediatric cardiac surgery metabolomics, supporting reproducibility across independent cohorts. Emerging work20,21 reinforces that targeted metabolomics can yield clinically relevant risk signals.

The persistent elevation of these amino acids in high-risk patients likely reflects active proteolysis in cardiac and skeletal muscle, indicating heightened metabolic stress and reliance on alternative energy pathways. This suggests that alanine, proline, and tyrosine may serve as biomarkers of inadequate metabolic adaptation to the post-bypass state. Tyrosine elevation is particularly noteworthy as this amino acid serves as a substrate for catecholamine synthesis, and enrichment analysis confirmed that high-risk metabolites mapped to this pathway. Putrescine elevation at 24 hours was similarly associated with higher inotrope requirements.22

Protective metabolic phenotype: preserved oxidative metabolism

The low-risk cluster was characterized by higher post-CPB cystine levels and favorable ratios of acetylcarnitine, galactitol, and acetylputrescine. Enrichment analysis confirmed that low-risk metabolites mapped to fatty acid oxidation pathways, suggesting preserved oxidative metabolism.

Cystine plays a critical role in glutathione metabolism, which has been implicated in neonatal post-bypass outcomes.18 Lower post-CPB cystine was associated with higher inotrope requirements in our cohort, possibly reflecting increased cardiac and skeletal muscle uptake for glutathione synthesis, which modulates sarcoplasmic reticulum-coupled calcium release and contractility.23,24 Acetylcarnitine, a key intermediate in fatty acid oxidation, showed protective associations when maintained at higher preoperative to 24-hour ratios. This aligns with established principles that fatty acids are the primary fuel source in healthy myocardium, while failing hearts shift toward metabolism of ketones, amino acids, and lactate.25 Galactitol elevation in patients requiring higher inotropic support is associated with oxidative stress.26

Clinical implications and risk stratification

Our findings demonstrate that perioperative metabolomic profiling can stratify neonates into distinct risk groups with clinically meaningful outcome differences. Importantly, specific metabolite signatures were associated with different morbidities: tyrosine, alanine, and proline with hyperlactatemia and hepatic injury; cystine, acetylcarnitine, and galactitol with inotrope requirements and LCOS.27 This specificity suggests potential for targeted risk assessment.

The identification of distinct metabolic phenotypes raises the possibility of precision medicine approaches in neonatal cardiac surgery. Patients with high-risk amino acid profiles might benefit from optimized nutritional support or interventions targeting amino acid metabolism.28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33 While such interventions remain investigational, our data provide a foundation for hypothesis-driven clinical trials. The robustness of our findings across 3 independent centers, despite differences in surgical technique, bypass management, and critical care practices, strengthens confidence in the generalizability of these metabolic signatures.

Methodologic considerations

Our feature selection approach, combining the Boruta algorithm with HCA, proved effective for phenotype discovery. The focused metabolite panel enhances clinical translatability, as targeted assays for a small number of metabolites are more feasible for clinical implementation than broad metabolomic screens.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. Cohorts were enrolled at different clinical sites with variability in bypass prime composition, surgical technique, and critical care management, and detailed nutritional data were not uniformly available. There were also differences in the number of freeze–thaw cycles in samples. While we applied z-score normalization to mitigate site effects, unmeasured differences may influence metabolite concentrations and postoperative outcomes. The 3 amino acids in our model (alanine, proline, and tyrosine) belong to a class of metabolites that demonstrate robust stability in plasma across multiple freeze–thaw cycles. Studies evaluating amino acid stability in human serum and plasma using targeted liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry methods have shown that amino acids remain largely stable through up to 3 to 4 freeze–thaw cycles.34,35 This stability profile supports the reliability of these metabolites as biomarkers in multicenter studies where some variation in sample handling may occur.

Future work integrating nutrition and metabolomics, as recently demonstrated in pediatric congenital heart disease populations,36 will be important to advance this field. Hierarchical clustering is inherently sensitive to choice of distance metric and linkage method, and external validation in independent cohorts is needed. Our targeted metabolite panel represents a small fraction of the metabolome, and broader profiling or integration with other -omics platforms may reveal additional pathways and biomarkers. Finally, patients requiring ECMO or renal replacement therapy represented severe postoperative courses and likely fall at the extreme end of the metabolic risk spectrum; their inclusion is consistent with composite morbidity definitions used in prior neonatal CPB trials.

Conclusions

Perioperative metabolomic profiling in neonatal cardiac surgery identifies distinct metabolic phenotypes associated with adverse outcomes. High-risk patients demonstrate persistent elevation of gluconeogenic amino acids, particularly tyrosine, alanine, and proline, suggesting impaired metabolic adaptation and reliance on alternative energy pathways. Low-risk patients show preserved fatty acid oxidation capacity and maintained redox balance. These findings align with and extend prior pediatric cardiac surgery metabolomics studies,6,18,19 highlighting amino acid metabolism and energy substrate utilization as critical determinants of postoperative outcomes. Metabolomic risk stratification may enable precision medicine approaches to improve outcomes in this high-risk population.
