Elevated LncRNA LINC00966 Suppresses PDE2A to Impair Sperm Motility in Asthenozoospermia
Authors: Bingrou Guo, Junyi Ke, Hanran Mai, Ting Zhang, Zhaojin Lu, Zilin Zheng, Jieyi Luo, Yifan Zhang, Mei Yan, Shuo Yang, Yanxia Qu, Fan Jiang, Xiaoping Liu, Hao Chen, Liandong Zuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEmerging evidence indicated that long non‐coding RNAs (lncRNAs) play critical roles in mammalian reproductive physiology, yet their roles in asthenozoospermia (AS), a major contributor to male infertility, remain elusive. Here, we identify LINC00966 as a candidate lncRNA associated with impaired sperm function. LINC00966 expression was significantly elevated in semen from AS patients and negatively correlated with sperm concentration, total motility, and progressive motility. In mouse testes, Linc00966 was detected in spermatogonia, spermatocytes and spermatids with predominant cytoplasmic localization as well as in GC‐2spd cells. Furthermore, Linc00966 overexpression reduced cell viability and migration and increased intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), whereas knockdown enhanced viability and migration and lowered ROS in GC‐2spd cells. Bulk RNA‐seq analysis revealed that the phosphodiesterase 2A (Pde2a), a key cGMP/cAMP‐hydrolyzing enzyme, were upregulated when Linc00966 was knockdown. The GO enrichment and KEGG analysis of differentially expressed genes suggested the cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) process were enriched. Further validation found that Linc00966 suppressed Pde2a. In clinical semen samples, LINC00966 and PDE2A expression levels were inversely correlated, and both were closely associated with semen quality parameters. Collectively, these findings implicated LINC00966 and its downstream target PDE2A as potential pathogenic regulators of sperm motility in AS. By linking lncRNA‐mediated modulation of cGMP signaling and oxidative stress to spermatogenic cell function, our results demonstrated a plausible signaling axis underlying AS and suggests LINC00966/PDE2A as promising candidates for biomarker development and therapeutic investigation in male infertility.

Introduction

Infertility affects approximately 10%–20% of reproductive‐age couples worldwide [1]. Male factors contribute to about half of these cases [2]. According to a global survey, the prevalence of infertility rose gradually from 1990 to 2017, with a 0.291% annual increase in male infertility [3]. In China, where polices such as the Two‐Child and Three‐Child initiatives have heightened focus on reproductive health, infertility consultation have increased. Despite diverse etiologies, 60%–75% of male infertility cases remain idiopathic [2]. Asthenozoospermia (AS), characterized by reduced sperm motility, accounts for over 45% of male infertility cases and is a leading contributor to subfertility [4]. Human spermatogenesis is a tightly regulated process involving multiple genes and epigenetic genes, and aberrant gene or epigenetic regulations is a critical factor for the AS [5]. Although progress has been made, the genetic or epigenetic underpinnings of AS remain elusive, with mechanisms for most implicated genes unclear.

Long non‐coding RNAs (LncRNAs) are non‐protein‐coding transcripts exceeding 200 nucleotides that regulate gene expression and diverse physiological and pathological processes [5, 6, 7, 8], including cancer, diabetes [9], cardiovascular diseases [10] and neurodegenerative diseases [11]. In reproduction, lncRNAs have been uncovered to be involved in the spermatogenesis and sperm functions [12, 13, 14]. Knockdown of lncRNA CR44455/6 results in male sterility in Drosophila [15]. LncRNA AK015322 is highest expression in mouse spermatogonial stem cells (SSCs). Furthermore, knockdown of LncRNA AK015322 reduces SSC proliferation, consistent with a role in supporting their self‐renewal capacity [16]. The X‐inactivating locus‐associated lncRNA, Tsx, was expressed in mouse spermatocytes and interfering Tsx induced spermatocytes apoptosis during the metaphase stage [17]. Inhibition of Rbakdn in mouse testes led to meiotic arrest and apoptosis of spermatocytes, further resulting in seminiferous tubules vacuolization [18]. The elevated expression of lncRNA, much like a coin with two sides, can impair spermatogenesis. Elevated LINC02220 downregulated DNAH5 in terato‐asthenozoospermia and asthenozoospermia [19]. LINC00574, which silences T‐complex associated testis expressed 3 (TCTE3) via RE1 Silencing Transcription Factor (REST), impairing sperm morphology and motility [20]. Apart from sperm, seminal plasma contains stable extracellular mRNAs and non‐coding RNAs that facilitate intercellular communication [21, 22, 23]. A recent study demonstrated that lncRNA Taurine upregulated gene 1 (TUG1) in seminal plasma was downregulated in non‐obstructive azoospermia (NOA) and severe oligozoospermia (SO) patients [24]. These results suggest that either low or high expression of lncRNA contributes to sperm production and function.

In the present study, we observed elevated LINC00966 expression in both the sperm and seminal plasma of AS patients. Linc00966 knockdown in GC‐2spd cells resulted in increased Pde2a expression, with an inverse correlation observed between LINC00966 and PDE2A expression levels in AS patients. Notably, both LINC00966 and PDE2A expression were closely associated with semen quality parameters. These results suggest that LINC00966 contributes to AS by modulating PDE2A expression,supporting the hypothesis that LINC00966 contributes to AS by modulating PDE2A expression.

Method and Materials

Human Samples Collection

Human semen samples and blood samples were collected between 2022 and 2023 from male patients attending the male outpatient clinic of Guangzhou Women and Children Medical Center. Semen samples were obtained from 40 men aged 18‐55 years, comprising 20 normozoospermic controls and 20 patients with asthenozoospermia (AS), and were used for the isolation of seminal plasma and spermatozoa. Peripheral blood samples were additionally collected from 50 participants. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) Adult males with no clear fertility disorder upon examination and no other serious diseases were used as the control group. (ii) Adult men with AS whose results of 2 consecutive semen examinations were sperm progressive motility (PR) < 32% or sperm total motility (PR + NP) < 40%, and whose total number of spermatozoa and sperm concentration were normal. (iii) Informed voluntary participation in this program. The exclusion criteria were as follows: subjects with a history of systemic disease were excluded. Semen samples were collected in sterile conical tubes after 2–5 days of sexual abstinence and allowed to liquefy at 37°C for 30 min.

Gradient Centrifugation for Sperm and Seminal Plasma Isolation

After completing the semen analysis, the remaining semen were collected. Then, 500 μL of 80% density gradient solution was added and followed by 500 μL of 40% density gradient solution to a 15 mL centrifuge tube. Once a stable interface had formed, slowly added 400 μL of liquefied semen to the upper layer of the gradient solution along the tube wall. After centrifugation at 400×g for 20 min, distinct multilayers was visible in the tube: the uppermost layer comprised seminal plasma components, while a pellet of motile spermatozoa formed in the bottom layer. Finally, carefully aspirated and transfered the upper seminal plasma layer, along with 200–400 μL of the sperm pellet suspension, to a new EP tube.

Cell Culture and Transfection

The GC‐2spd mouse spermatocyte cell line (ATCC, Manassas, Virginia, USA) was cultured in low‐glucose DMEM (Manassas, VA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (GIBCO, USA), 100 μg/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin (North China Pharmaceutical Group Corp, China) at 37°C in 5% CO2.

Linc00966 was overexpressed in GC‐2spd cells using lentivirus (HANBIO, China). OE‐NC indicated a negative control of overexpressed Linc00966 in the experiments. OE‐Linc00966 indicated overexpression of Linc00966. Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) was used for the downregulation of Linc00966 by small interfering RNA (siRNA) (KeScience, China) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Si‐NC indicated a negative control of inhibition of Linc00966 expression in the experiments. Si‐Linc00966 indicated inhibition of Linc00966 expression. The following sequences were used:

Linc00966, 5′‐CAAUGAAAGAGGUGUUUAATT‐3′.

Total RNA Extraction and RT‐qPCR Analysis

The total RNA was extracted using an RNAzol RT reagent (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Integrity and yields of RNA were measured by micro/nano spectrophotometer (Biodrop, UK) to determine sample concentration and purity at 260/280 nm. First‐strand cDNA synthesis was performed using the Reverse Transcription System (Roche, Switzerland), following the manufacturer's protocol. The cDNA was then analyzed using a RT‐qPCR assay with an Applied Biosystems 7900 Real‐Time PCR System (Thermo Scientific, USA) and SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Roche, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer's instructions. GAPDH and β‐actin were used as internal controls. The relative expression of RNAs was calculated using the comparative Ct method. The primer sequences used for RT‐qPCR are shown in Table 1.

Nuclear‐Cytoplasmic Fractionation for lncRNA Detection

Cytoplasmic and nuclear fractionation was conducted following the previous protocol [25]. Mouse testes or GC‐2spd cells were ground in liquid nitrogen and then homogenized in lysis buffer. The homogenate was filtered and centrifuged at 1500g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was subjected to an additional centrifugation at 10,000g for 10 min at 4°C to isolate the cytosolic fraction. The pellet was washed with NRBT buffer and resuspended in NRB2. Samples were overlaid on NRB3 and centrifuged at 10,000g for 15 min at 4°C to obtain the nuclear fraction.

FISH Analysis

FISH was used to investigate the expression pattern of Linc00966 in mouse testis. A Cy3‐labeled Linc00966targeting probe was designed and synthesized by Guangzhou Ruibo Biological Technology Co Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). The testes were collected from 8‐week‐old mice and fixed overnight in 4% PFA at 4°C. The tissue was then washed three times for 10 min each in 1 × PBS and dehydrated in a 15% sucrose solution. The tissue was then transferred to 30% sucrose solution at 4°C for dehydration, and finally embedded in OCT. Sections were cut to a thickness of 10 μm. The sections underwent antigen retrieval in a 0.01 M citrate buffer solution (pH 6.0) for 10 min, then, were cooled and treated with 0.3% Triton X‐100 for 20 min. The Linc00966 targeted probe was then diluted to a final concentration of 2 ng/μL in hybridization buffer. After denaturing on a hot plate at 95°C for 5 min, the probe was incubated with the sections overnight at 42°C in a humid chamber. Unbound probes were removed through rigorous washing steps: First, the sections were washed with 2 × SSC at room temperature for 5 min, followed by two washes at 42°C for 20 min each. Secondly, the sections were washed twice with 0.2 × SSC at 42°C for 20 min each time. Finally, wash at room temperature for 10 min. The nuclei were then counterstained with DAPI, the slides were mounted and the fluorescence signals were visualized using an Olympus Spin Scan confocal microscope equipped with a Cy3 filter.

Western Blot Analysis

Cell lysates were prepared with SDS buffer, and HY‐K0010 protease inhibitor Cocktail (MCH, Guangzhou, China) was added to the SDS lysate buffer. Cells were harvested and lysed on ice with the above prepared buffer. The lysates were then centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected as the total protein sample. Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein concentration assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). SDS buffer and 4× Laemmli sample buffer (Bio‐Rad, USA) were then applied, and the protein samples were denatured at 100°C for 30 min. Proteins were then separated by 10% SDS‐PAGE and transferred to an equilibrated PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA). After blocking with a 5% nonfat dry milk solution for 2 h, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against PDE2A (1:1000, ProteinTech, #55306‐1‐AP) and GAPDH (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, #2118S) overnight at 4°C on a shaker. After washing three times with TBST, the membranes were then incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody (1:3000, Cell Signaling Technology, #7074) and visualized with an ECL detection system. GAPDH was used as a loading control. Image J software was used to detect the gray values of the western blot (WB) bands and calculate the relative expression of PDE2A.

Cell Viability Assay

The GC‐2spd cell was seeded in 96‐well plates (2 × 104 cells/well) and incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 0 to 72 h. Their absorbance at 450 nm was examined after culture in a 5% CO2 incubator using a CCK8 kit (DOJINDO, Japan) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Cell Migration Assay

Transwell chambers (Corning, USA) without basement membrane coating (Corning, USA) were used to detect the migration of transfected GC‐2spd cells. A total of 2.5 × 104 cells were seeded into the upper chambers of transwell inserts. Serum‐free medium (Manassas, VA, USA) was added to the upper chamber, and medium supplemented with 15% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After 8 h, nonmigratory cells were wiped and the cells migrating through the membrane were counted under a microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Wound‐Healing Assay

GC‐2spd cells were seeded in six‐well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 24 h at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. When the cells reached approximately 90% confluence, two straight scratches were made through the cell monolayer using a sterile 20‐μL pipette tip. The wells were washed three times with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) to remove detached cells, after which the culture medium was replaced with serum‐free medium (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The cells were then incubated under the same conditions. Images of the scratched areas were acquired using a Leica microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany). Wound widths were measured using ImageJ software. Wound closure was calculated as follows: wound closure (%) = (W 0 − Wₜ)/W₀ × 100%, where W 0 and Wₜ represent the wound widths at the initial and indicated time points, respectively.

ROS Assay

The GC‐2spd cell was seeded in 6‐well plates (5 × 105 cells/well) and incubated at 37°C in the 5% CO2 incubator. When there are 70%–80% cells in the dish, add DCFH‐DA reagent (Solarbio, China) for staining for 30 min according to the instructions. Observation of green fluorescence/red fluorescence using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany).

RNA Sequencing (RNA‐Seq) Analysis

Total RNAs were extracted using TRIzol Reagent. mRNA sequencing library requires 1 μg of total RNA. DNA digestion was carried out after RNA extraction by DNaseI. RNA quality was determined by examining A260/A280 with Nanodrop. RNA Integrity was confirmed by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis (28S/18S ≥ 1.5). Qualified RNAs were finally quantified by Qubit 3.0 with QubitTM RNA Broad Range Assay kit (Life Technologies, Q10210).

2 μg total RNAs were used for stranded RNA sequencing library preparation using KCTM Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Wuhan Seqhealth Co. Ltd. China, DR08402) for Illumina following the manufacturer's instruction. Eukaryotic mRNA was first enriched with magnetic beads with Oligo (dT), followed by randomly interrupting the mRNA into short fragments of about 200 bp. One‐stranded cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription using random primers using mRNA as a template. dTTP in dNTPs was replaced by dUTP when synthesizing two‐stranded cDNA. Double‐stranded cDNA was purified using AMPure XP beads, complemented by End RepairMix ends with A‐tail and sequencing junction. The two‐stranded cDNA was digested using USER enzyme so that the library contained only one‐stranded cDNA. PCR amplification of about 15 cycles was performed to obtain the final cDNA library. Library detection and quantification.

PCR products corresponding to 200–500 bps were enriched, quantified and finally sequenced on a Novaseq 6000 sequencer (Illumina) with a PE150 model. The DESeq2 software was utilized to screen the differentially expressed genes in the two sample groups to satisfy the |log2FC| ≥ 2 and p value ≤ 0.05 differential expression ranges to screen the differentially expressed genes between the two groups.

The TopGO software was used to analyze the GO function of the target gene set. The method of GO function enrichment analysis: all the genes were used as the background list, and the target gene list was used as the candidate list filtered out from the background list. The p‐value representing whether the GO function set was significantly enriched in the target gene list was calculated using the Fisher's exact test, and then the p‐value was corrected by the Benjamini & Hochberg's multiple testing corrected the p‐value to obtain the FDR. And the functional annotation and categorization of Pathways in KEGG database were performed for these target gene sets. The KEGG pathway functional enrichment analysis method is similar to GO functional enrichment analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical calculations were performed using SPSS version 27.0 software (SPSS, Chicago, IL) and presented graphically in GraphPad Prism Software 8.0 (GraphPad, USA). The bioinformatic analysis, presented as a volcano plot, was conducted using R software (version 4.2.0). The heatmap was generated by https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn (last accessed on 10 December 2024), an online platform dedicated to data analysis and visualization. The bioinformatic analysis, in the form of a bubble plot, was performed using the OmicStudio tools available at https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool.

Results

High Expression of LINC00966 in AS Patients Correlates With Impaired Sperm Parameters

To explore the role of LINC00966 in AS, we analyzed blood samples from 25 AS patients and 25 healthy controls using RT‐qPCR. LINC00966 expression was significantly higher in AS patients (Figure 1A). We then examined semen samples from 20 AS patients and 20 controls, confirming LINC00966 overexpression in both sperm and seminal plasma of AS patients (Figure 1B,C). Correlation analyses revealed significant negative correlations between LINC00966 levels and key sperm parameters: sperm count (r = −0.6923, p < 0.0001), total motility (r = −0.6631, p < 0.0001), sperm progressive motility (r = −0.5660, p < 0.0001), and sperm concentration (r = −0.5545, p = 0.0002) (Figure 1D–F, Figure S1A). To assess LINC00966 localization in the testis, RNA in situ hybridization in mouse testis tissue showed LINC00966 was expressed in spermatogonia, spermatocytes, round spermatids, and mature sperm, predominantly in the cytoplasm (Figure 1G). Nuclear‐cytoplasmic fractionation followed by RT‐qPCR in mouse testis tissue and GC‐2spd cells further confirmed its cytoplasmic enrichment (Figure 1H,I). Furthermore, we found that the positively correlation between LINC00966 expression and male age (Figure S1B, r = 0.6716, p < 0.0001).

Linc00966 Inhibits GC‐2spd Cell Viability and Induces Oxidative Stress

To investigate the cellular function of Linc00966, we first overexpressed Linc00966 in GC‐2spd cells by lentiviral transduction (Figure 2A). Linc00966 overexpression reduced cell viability (Figure 2B) and decreased cell migration and invasion in Transwell assays (Figure 2C,D). Wound‐healing assays independently confirmed the reduction in cell migration (Figure S2A,B). Conversely, siRNA‐mediated knockdown of Linc00966 (Figure 2E) increased cell viability (Figure 2F) and enhanced cell migration, as demonstrated by the transwell and wound‐healing assays (Figure 2G,H, Figure S2C,D, respectively).

Given that mitochondria are the primary source of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in germ cells and elevated ROS indicates potential cellular damage [26], we measured intracellular ROS levels. Linc00966 overexpression increased intracellular ROS (Figure 2I,J), whereas knockdown decreased it (Figure 2K,L). These results suggested that Linc00966 promotes mitochondrial oxidative stress, contributing to germ cell dysfunction.

Linc00966 Negatively Regulates Pde2a Expression

To identify downstream targets, we performed RNA‐seq on GC‐2spd cells following Linc00966 knockdown (Figure 3A). The identified 105 differentially expressed genes were found (DEG, 57 upregulated and 48 downregulated, Figure 3B). Gene ontology and pathway analyses highlighted enrichment in cGMP‐stimulated cyclic nucleotide phosphodiesterase activity and cGMP metabolic processes (Figure 3C,D). From these, we selected Pde2a, a gene linked to cGMP metabolism, for validation. Overexpression of Linc00966 in GC‐2spd cells suppressed Pde2a mRNA and protein levels (Figure 4A–C). Conversely, knockdown of Linc00966 upregulated Pde2a expression (Figure 4D–F), aligning with RNA‐seq data. These results indicate that Linc00966 negatively regulates Pde2a.

PDE2A Expression Is Reduced in AS and Correlates With Sperm Quality

In clinical semen samples, PDE2A mRNA levels were significantly lower in AS patients than in healthy controls (Figure 5A). PDE2A levels positively correlated with sperm parameters, including sperm count (r = 0.3520, p = 0.0412), total motility (r = 0.4817, p = 0.0034), sperm progressive motility (r = 0.4473, p = 0.0071), and sperm concentration (r = 0.3739, p = 0.0269) (Figure 5B–F, Figure S1C). PED2A negatively correlated with male age (Figure S1D, r = −0.4919, p = 0.0027). Interestingly, correlation analysis showed significantly negative correlation between PED2A and LINC00966 (r = −0.5867, p < 0.0001, Figure 5E), consistent with our in vitro observations.

Subsequently, we evaluated the predictive performance of LINC00966 and PDE2A as potential triage tool by constructing ROC curves for total motility and sperm progressive motility. The area under curve (AUC) for the LINC00966 in univariate analysis was 0.7929 (95% CI: 0.6060–0.9798) for the prediction of sperm total motility, and the PDE2A was 0.8469 (95% CI: 0.06770–1.000). The combined model of LINC00966 and PDE2A achieved a higher AUC of 0.878 (95% CI: 0.753–1.000) (Figure 5F). In predicting sperm progressive motility, the AUC values for LINC00966, PDE2A and their combination were 0.699 (95% CI: 0.529–0.868, p = 0.0321), 0.713 (95% CI: 0.5511–0.8739, p = 0.0215) and 0.721 (95% CI: 0.538–0.904, p = 0.036) (Figure 5G).

Discussion

The present study uncovered a novel molecular axis in asthenozoospermia (AS), demonstrating that LINC00966 was significantly upregulated in semen and seminal plasma from AS patients, with expression positively correlating with male age but negatively with several sperm parameters (concentration, total count, progressive motility, and total motility). Furthermore, Linc00966 overexpression in GC‐2spd cells suppressed cell viability and migration while elevating reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, suggesting induction of mitochondrial oxidative stress. Mechanistically, Linc00966 negatively regulated Pde2a, a phosphodiesterase linked to cAMP/cGMP signaling and ROS production; PDE2A levels were reduced in AS samples and positively correlated with sperm quality. These findings position LINC00966 as a potential non‐invasive biomarker for AS diagnosis and a regulator of sperm motility via PDE2A modulation, representing the report of its role in male fertility, with implications for age‐related fertility decline.

Spermatogenesis is a tightly orchestrated process involving spermatogonial mitosis, spermatocyte meiosis, and sperm maturation, where germ cell homeostasis is essential for fertility [27, 28]. LncRNAs play pivotal roles in these stages by regulating gene expression, chromatin dynamics, and cellular signaling [29]. It has been reported numerous lncRNAs associated with developmental regulation at specific stages of spermatogenesis [30]. Tsx deletion in mice caused pachytene‐specific spermatocyte apoptosis and testicular atrophy [17], while piR‐18 deficiency led to infertility [31]. In AS, characterized by impaired sperm motility, lncRNAs can disrupt mitochondrial function, ROS balance, and flagellar integrity, leading to reduced progressive motility. Under hypoxic conditions, the lncRNAs MIR210HG and MLLT4‐AS1 exhibited a positive correlation with reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, while their expression was inversely associated with two critical sperm parameters (sperm motility and count) [32]. Additionally, the expression level of lncRNA HOTAIR positively correlated with that of superoxide dismutase (SOD), an enzyme that played a protective role in maintaining sperm motility in patients with asthenozoospermia and oligozoospermia. The downregulation of HOTAIR mediated increased oxidative stress, thereby impairing sperm motility and viability [33]. Our results aligned with these, showing Linc00966's promotion of ROS in spermatocytes, which likely exacerbated AS by compromising energy metabolism and motility.

LncRNA LINC00966, also known as MIR124‐2HG, is the host gene for the microRNA (miRNA) MIR124‐2, and located on chromosome 8q12.3. Despite its established roles in neuronal development, autophagy regulation, and astrocyte suppression [34, 35], function of LINC00966 in male reproduction had not been investigated prior to this study. Our study revealed it as a novel regulator of human sperm motility, particularly in asthenozoospermia (AS). We observed significant upregulation of LINC00966 expression in both semen and seminal plasma from AS patients compared to healthy controls, highlighting its potential as a dysregulated factor in reproductive pathology. Correlation analyses further demonstrated a positive association between LINC00966 levels and male age, alongside significantly negative correlations with critical semen parameters, including concentration, total sperm count, progressive motility, and total motility. These relationships suggested that LINC00966 expression may escalate with advancing age, potentially exacerbating age‐related declines in sperm function through mechanisms such as oxidative stress. Notably, even when semen concentration and total sperm count fell within normal ranges, elevated LINC00966 was linked to reduced motility metrics, indicating its sensitivity as an early indicator of subclinical motility defects.

Another finding in our study was LINC00966's unique cytoplasmic localization in spermatogenic cells and its suppression of PDE2A, differing from neuronal‐focused studies where LINC00966 regulates Dlx5 for development and suppresses astrocyte activation via autophagy [34, 35]. Phosphodiesterase 2A (PDE2A), also called cGMP‐stimulated phosphodiesterase (cGS‐PDE), was a regulator of cAMP/cGMP levels, facilitating regulation of inflammation, immunological responses, oxidative stress through the hydrolysis of cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) and cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) [36]. PDE2A also increased the production of NADPH oxidase‐dependent ROS for the development of new blood vessels [37]. However, its reproductive role was undefined. Comparisons with other PDEs, such as PDE4 in regulating sperm motility, PDE1 in acrosome/head modifications, PDE11 in maintaining pre‐ejaculatory sperm quiescence, and PDE5 in sperm motility and energy acquisition [38, 39], highlighted our novelty. LINC00966‐PDE2A as a specific axis for AS, with biomarker potential absent in prior PDE studies. We found that area under the curve (AUC) values in the combined analysis were higher than those in the univariate analysis, indicating that both LINC00966 and PDE2A are good markers for diagnosing AS. Simultaneously detecting both markers can provide a more accurate prediction of AS occurrence, and their combined diagnosis enhances the diagnostic value of AS. By integrating such molecular markers, future diagnostics could provide more precise evaluations of male fertility, facilitating targeted interventions for AS and related conditions. However, our study has several limitations. First, the molecular mechanisms underlying LINC00966's direct regulation of PDE2A (e.g., via enhancers or transcription factor binding) require further investigation, as current results rely on in vitro knockdown and RNA‐seq without in vivo validation. Second, the clinical sample size is relatively small, necessitating larger cohorts to robustly confirm correlations between LINC00966, PDE2A, and AS parameters for enhanced reliability.

Taken together, our findings uncovered a novel LINC00966‐PDE2A axis in asthenozoospermia (AS), with LINC00966 upregulated in AS patients' semen, correlating positively with age but negatively with sperm motility parameters, while suppressing PDE2A and inducing ROS‐mediated stress in spermatocytes. Our findings also highlighted LINC00966's unique PDE2A regulation, differing from neuronal studies and other PDEs in sperm function, suggesting it as a potential biomarker for age‐related infertility.
