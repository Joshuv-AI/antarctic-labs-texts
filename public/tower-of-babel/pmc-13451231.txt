Aloe vera integrated ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanocomposites for accelerated wound repair and scar reduction
Authors: Mariam H Omran, Mohamed Abd Elkodous, Hossam Tohamy, Nourhan G Naga, Jihad El-Qassas, Samir Nouh, Mahmoud El-Kammar, Howaida Abu-Ahmed, Hoda Elkhenany
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFor centuries, natural extracts such as Aloe vera have been used in wound care due to their soothing, anti-inflammatory, and healing properties. However, their clinical efficacy can be limited by instability, low bioavailability, and restricted antimicrobial potency. Nanotechnology offers promising strategies to enhance and complement the therapeutic benefits of herbal extracts. In this study, we investigated the wound-healing potential of Aloe vera gel combined with ZnO/SWCNT/Ag (zinc oxide-single-walled carbon nanotubes-silver nanoparticles) nanocomposite. Forty full-thickness excisional wounds (15 mm) were created on the dorsum of 20 Sprague Dawley rats and divided into four treatment groups: ZnO/SWCNT/Ag alone (1 mg/mL), Aloe vera gel (30 mg/mL), the combination of Aloe vera gel with the NPs, and control (saline 0.9%). Wound areas were tracked at days 0, 3, 10, 15, and 21 using calibrated imaging, and skin samples were harvested for histopathological analysis. The ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanocomposite alone accelerated wound closure compared to Aloe vera and saline control, while combining Aloe vera with the nanocomposite improved wound healing compared to Aloe vera alone at day 10 (p < 0.05). Although wound closure was similar across all groups by day 21, nanoparticle-treated wounds showed less variability and more uniform healing. Scar area analysis revealed significant reductions for ZnO/SWCNT/Ag based treatment and the combination compared to Aloe vera (p < 0.05), whereas differences versus saline control were not statistically significant. Additionally, the formulations demonstrated strong antibacterial activity against Escherichia coli and enhanced antioxidant properties. These findings suggest that ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanoparticles may serve as a supportive platform to enhance the wound-healing behaviour of Aloe vera.

Introduction

Effective wound healing remains a major clinical challenge due to the complexity of skin architecture and the coordinated interplay of diverse cell types within the extracellular matrix. The skin serves as the body’s primary barrier against the external environment, and its regeneration after injury requires tightly regulated processes of inflammation, angiogenesis, cell migration, proliferation, and matrix remodeling [1, 2]. Despite the availability of conventional wound therapies, limitations such as delayed healing, infection, and scarring continue to drive the development of advanced treatment strategies [3].

Recent research has increasingly focused on combining traditional healing agents with modern biomaterials to create multifunctional wound dressings. Examples include nanofiber scaffolds loaded with silver nanoparticles, Aloe vera–based alginate hydrogels, propolis-incorporated dressing films, and honey-based hydrogel sheets [1]. Nanotechnology, in particular, offers unique opportunities to accelerate wound repair by enabling drug delivery, antimicrobial protection, and extracellular matrix–like scaffolding [4, 5].

Among the broad classes of nanomaterials, carbon nanotubes (CNTs), zinc oxide (ZnO) nanoparticles, and silver (Ag) nanoparticles have received considerable attention. CNTs, including single-walled (SWCNTs) and multi-walled (MWCNTs) forms, consist of cylindrical carbon structures with high aspect ratios and large surface areas [6, 7].

These properties enable CNTs to support cell adhesion, promote scarless repair, and exert antimicrobial effects [8, 9]. Inorganic nanomaterials such as ZnO and Ag-based nanoparticles also show broad-spectrum antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory effects, in addition to stimulating fibroblast proliferation, neovascularization, and collagen deposition [10, 11]. Notably, ZnO nanoparticles enhance angiogenesis through nitric oxide–mediated pathways [12], while Ag nanoparticles reduce bacterial colonization and support tissue repair.

Parallel to these advances, natural products remain indispensable in wound care. More than 80% of the global population continues to rely on herbal and plant-based remedies for primary healthcare [13]. Aloe vera (Aloe barbadensis Miller), often termed the “healing plant,” contains over 75 bioactive compounds, including vitamins, polysaccharides, enzymes, minerals, and amino acids [14, 15]. It is also considered a biopolymer due to its polysaccharide content, which is called glucomannan [b(1,4)-linked acetylated mannan] and constitutes most of the dry matter. This polysaccharide consists of glucose and mannose monomers [16, 17]. These components act on fibroblasts, macrophages, and keratinocytes, stimulating collagen synthesis, epithelialization, and tensile strength development [18–20]. Consequently, Aloe vera gel is widely employed in managing burns, wounds, and skin inflammation, functioning as an anti-inflammatory, antiseptic, antimicrobial, and immunomodulatory agent [21, 22].

Although Aloe vera, CNTs, ZnO, and Ag nanoparticles have each been investigated individually for wound healing applications, their combined use in a nanocomposite framework has not yet been systematically studied. Integrating Aloe vera with ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanocomposites could synergistically enhance healing by coupling the regenerative and immunomodulatory effects of Aloe vera with the antimicrobial, antioxidant, and structural benefits of nanomaterials.

In this study, we aimed to formulate and evaluate an Aloe vera–based ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanocomposite for wound healing. We hypothesized that this hybrid system would accelerate repair by promoting re-epithelialization, collagen remodeling, and neovascularization, while simultaneously reducing infection and inflammation, thereby offering a superior alternative to conventional therapies.

Materials and methods

Preparation of ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanocomposite

Zinc nitrate hexahydrate [Zn (NO3)2·6H2O], potassium hydroxide (KOH), absolute ethanol 99.9% (C2H5OH), ammonium hydroxide 28% (NH4OH), and silver nitrate (AgNO3) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich, Japan. While SWCNTs were purchased from OCSiAl, Japan.

Preparation of ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanocomposite

Firstly, ZnO nanoparticles were prepared according to the method previously reported by Abd Elkodous et al. [23]. A (0.2 M) zinc nitrate hexahydrate solution was prepared in deionized water (D.I.W.) and kept under constant stirring for 30 min at ambient temperature. Then, (0.4 M) of separately prepared potassium hydroxide solution was added drop-by-drop to the first solution under continuous stirring for 2 h. After that, the formed white suspension was collected by centrifugation, washed many times with D.I.W. and absolute ethanol, and dried at 80 °C. Finally, the dried powder was calcined for 3 h at 500 °C.

Secondly, prepared ZnO nanoparticles (from the previous step) were added to SWCNTs and absolute ethanol to form a 15% w/w suspension (15% SWCNTs with respect to ZnO) using an impregnation method via ultrasonication for 30 min as reported earlier [24, 25]. Then, (1 ml) ammonia 28% solution was added drop-wise to the above dispersion, which was kept under vigorous stirring overnight.

Thirdly, using the photodeposition approach assisted by ultraviolet (UV) light from the HgXe lamp (275 W, SP-11, USHIO, Japan), 5 wt.% Ag NPs (with respect to ZnO) were selectively loaded on ZnO nanoparticles as previously described [24, 25]. (200 mM) Silver nitrate solution was carefully added to the ZnO/ SWCNTs dispersion (from the previous step), which was irradiated for 30 min using UV light under N2 bubbling. The employed HgXe lamp was operating at 275 W power and 100% intensity. Finally, the resulting particles were collected (via centrifugation), washed several times with D.I.W. and ethanol, and finally dried at 45 °C under vacuum.

Characterization of the prepared nanocomposite

The formed phase and crystallinity of samples were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis on an Ultima IV X-ray diffractometer applying Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å) from Rigaku (Japan), and the XRD analysis was operated at 40 kV–30 mA. While morphology and purity were revealed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) from (HITACHI high technologies, Japan), connected with an EDX unit (SU8000 Type II).

Preparation and characterization of Aloe vera gel and nanocomposite

Fresh Aloe vera plants were obtained from Alex Cactus Greenhouse, Alexandria Governorate. The preparation method followed Massoud et al. [26] with minor modifications. After thorough washing, the leaves, the base, the tip, and the margins were carefully trimmed, and the inner transparent mucilage was scooped out with a sterile spoon. The collected gel was repeatedly washed to eliminate any residual latex, then transferred into sterilized jars for lyophilization. Lyophilization was performed using a vacuum freeze dryer (Model: FDF 0350, Korea). The dried mucilage was ground into a fine powder with a mortar and stored at −20 °C until further use [27]. Aloe vera gel was prepared at the time of treatment application with a concentration of 30 mg/mL [28] by adding 30 mg of aloe vera powder to 1 mL of saline. For the composite formulation, 30 mg of aloe vera powder was added to 1 mL of a previously sonicated ZnO/SWCNT/Ag solution (1 mg/mL) to obtain a final mixture with a concentration of 30 mg/mL, which was then applied to the wound.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to characterize functional groups and assess molecular interactions between Aloe vera gel and the ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanocomposite. FTIR spectra of Aloe vera gel alone and Aloe vera gel mixed with the nanocomposite were recorded using a Shimadzu FTIR-8400S spectrometer. Zeta potential measurements were performed using a Malvern Zetasizer (software version 2.3) to evaluate surface charge and colloidal stability of Aloe vera gel, ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanoparticles, and the hybrid formulation.

Antioxidant activity assay (DPPH radical scavenging activity)

The antioxidant activity of the extract was evaluated at the Regional Center for Mycology and Biotechnology (RCMB), Al-Azhar University, using the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) free radical scavenging assay. All tests were performed in triplicate, and average values were calculated. A freshly prepared 0.004% (w/v) methanolic solution of DPPH was stored at 10 °C in the dark until use. Methanolic solutions of the test samples were prepared, and 40 μL of each solution was added to 3 mL of the DPPH solution. The decrease in absorbance at 515 nm was monitored continuously using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (Milton Roy, Spectronic 1201). Measurements were recorded after 15 min of incubation. The absorbance of the control (DPPH solution without antioxidant) and the reference compound (ascorbic acid) was also measured. The percentage inhibition (PI) of the DPPH radical was calculated using the following equation: PI(%)=(AC−AT)/AC×100 [29].

Where AC = absorbance of the control at t = 0 min and AT = absorbance of the test sample + DPPH at t = 15 min.

The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀), defined as the concentration required to scavenge 50% of the DPPH radicals, was estimated from the dose–response curve.

In vitro biological characterization of materials under investigation

Antimicrobial activity

The antibacterial activity of ZnO/SWCNT/Ag (1 mg/mL), Aloe vera gel (30 mg/mL), and their combination was evaluated using the agar well-diffusion method as described by [30]. Test organisms included Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923, Bacillus subtilis ATCC 6633, and Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29212) and Gram-negative bacteria (Escherichia coli ATCC 8739, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 15442, Pseudomonas fluorescens ATCC 13525, and Aeromonas hydrophila ATCC 13037). Ciprofloxacin was used as the positive control. Briefly, 100 μL of each suspension was dispensed into 10 mm wells created on Luria-Bertani (LB) agar plates. Following 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the antibacterial activity was assessed by measuring the diameter of inhibition zones. All experiments were performed in triplicate. Suspensions that exhibited antibacterial activity were further analyzed to determine their minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) using the broth microdilution assay in 96-well plates, following CLSI guidelines (CLSI, 2014). Each well was filled with 100 μL of Mueller-Hinton broth and 100 μL of the test suspension. Serial two-fold dilutions were prepared to obtain concentrations ranging from 500 to 4 μg/mL. E. coli ( ~ 3 × 10⁴ CFU/mL) was inoculated into each well. Media-only wells served as the negative control, while untreated bacterial suspensions served as the positive control. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The MIC was recorded as the lowest concentration that completely inhibited visible bacterial growth [31].

Metabolic activity and cytocompatibility on human skin fibroblasts (HSFs)

Human dermal fibroblasts (HSFs) were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 10³ cells/well and allowed to attach overnight. Cells were then exposed to serial concentrations (12.5, 25, 50, and 100 µg/mL) of Aloe vera, ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanoparticles, or their combination for 24 h, with untreated cells serving as the control. Following treatment, 10 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in sterile PBS; Life Technologies, USA) was added to each well, and plates were incubated for 3 h at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere. The resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) with gentle shaking for 10 min, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a FLUOstar Omega microplate reader (BMG Labtech, Cary, NC, USA).

Surgical animal model and treatment protocol

All experiments and procedures were performed at the Institute of Graduate Studies and Research, Alexandria University, following approval by the Institutional Animal Use and Care Committee of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Alexandria University (AU-IACUC; Protocol Number: Au013110920230273). Twenty apparently healthy male Sprague Dawley rats (150 ± 5 g) were obtained from the Institute of Graduate Studies and Research, Alexandria University. Full-thickness circular excisional skin wounds (1.5 cm in diameter), including removal of the panniculus carnosus muscle, were created bilaterally on the dorsal aspect of each rat after aseptic preparation, resulting in a total of 40 wounds. Surgeries were conducted under general anesthesia, with initial sedation using xylazine (5–10 mg/kg, IP) followed by ketamine (100 mg/kg, IP). The wounds were randomly allocated into four groups as follows: ZnO/SWCNT/Ag (1 mg/mL), Aloe vera gel (30 mg/mL) as described by Atiba and Ueno [28], NPs–Aloe vera gel combination, and Saline (negative control). Treatments were applied every other day from day 0 to day 10. The wound healing process was evaluated both macroscopically and microscopically in a blind manner (Fig. 1).

For Microscopic Evaluation, wound biopsies were harvested at day 3 (early inflammatory phase), day 10 (proliferative phase), and day 21 (remodeling phase), with three samples per time point. The collected tissues were processed for histopathological examination to monitor cellular and structural changes across the healing stages in a blinded manner.

Histopathological examination

At the end of the first, second, and third weeks, wound tissues were excised from each group for microscopic evaluation. Samples were immediately fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, routinely processed, and embedded in paraffin blocks. Thin sections of about 5 µm were obtained and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histopathological assessment. The observed changes were interpreted semi-quantitatively and scored as follows: negative (−), very mild (+), mild (++), mild to moderate (+++), moderate (++++), and severe (+++++).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.5). Each experiment was independently performed in triplicate. Data distribution was examined with the Shapiro–Wilk test to verify normality. For wound closure percentages and antimicrobial zone of inhibition (ZOI), the effects of treatment and time were assessed by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test where appropriate. Antioxidant activity (IC₅₀ values) and scar area measurements were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

To account for repeated measurements obtained from the same wounds over time, wound closure data were additionally analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model (LMM) implemented in R (version 4.4.1, 2024-06-14). In this model, treatment, time, and their interaction were included as fixed effects, while animal identity was treated as a random effect. Pairwise comparisons between treatments at each time point were performed using Tukey-adjusted post hoc tests. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05, p < 0.01, p < 0.001, and p < 0.0001.

Results and discussion

Nanoparticles characterization and their combination with Aloe vera

Figure 2 shows the XRD pattern of the prepared samples. Many characteristic peaks were recorded at 2θ = 32.1° (100), 34.3° (002), 36.4° (101), 47.5° (102), 57.2° (110), 62.6° (103), 66.4° (200), 67.8° (112), and 68.3° (201). Those diffraction peaks and their corresponding crystallographic planes were found to be in good agreement with the JCPDS card number (361451) of the hexagonal phase of ZnO with a typical wurtzite structure [1]. While characteristic peaks of SWCNTs disappeared in the XRD pattern due to the sharp and intense peaks of ZnO, and their low-intensity peaks. In addition, one characteristic peak of Ag NPs recorded at 2θ = 38.2° (111) shows the formation of the face-centred cubic crystalline phase of Ag NPs, JCPDS (No. 84–0713). All peaks confirm the presence and conjugation of the composite’s constituent materials. Figure 3(a–i) presents the SEM, EDX, and EDX mapping analyses of the prepared composite.

FTIR spectroscopy was employed to investigate the molecular interactions between Aloe vera gel and the incorporated ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanocomposite (Fig. 4; Table S1). The spectrum of Aloe vera gel exhibited a broad absorption band at 3400–3300 cm⁻¹, corresponding to O–H stretching vibrations of hydroxyl groups from polysaccharides, phenolic compounds, and bound water, consistent with previously reported Aloe-derived biopolymer matrices [32, 33]. Peaks at 2920–2850 cm⁻¹ were attributed to aliphatic C–H stretching vibrations, while bands observed around 1650–1800 cm⁻¹ were assigned to C = O stretching of carbonyl-containing groups. Additional peaks in the region of 1450–1350 cm⁻¹ corresponded to CH₂/CH₃ bending and COO⁻ vibrations, and the strong bands between 1080 and 1020 cm⁻¹ were characteristic of C–O–C and C–O stretching modes of polysaccharides, particularly acemannan [34].

A–C Zeta potential distributions of Aloe vera gel, ZnO/SWCNT/Ag, and Aloe vera gel + ZnO/SWCNT/Ag, indicating surface charge changes after incorporation. D FTIR spectra of Aloe vera gel and Aloe vera gel + ZnO/SWCNT/Ag showing characteristic O–H, C–H, C = O, and C–O–C functional groups, with Zn–O vibration bands confirming successful nanocomposite incorporation

Upon incorporation of the ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanocomposite, the FTIR spectrum retained the main organic features of Aloe vera but exhibited clear intensity attenuation and subtle band shifts, indicating molecular-level interactions rather than simple physical mixing. Notably, automated prominence-based peak analysis revealed that the polysaccharide-associated C–O–H band originally detected at approximately 1261 cm⁻¹ in pure Aloe vera shifted to lower wavenumbers ( ~ 1255 cm⁻¹) with reduced prominence in the composite. Such downshifts and attenuation are commonly associated with coordination or hydrogen bonding between oxygen-containing functional groups and metal or metal oxide surfaces, as reported for Aloe vera-mediated ZnO and Ag nanoparticle systems [35, 36]. The appearance of bands in the 500–450 cm⁻¹ region in the composite further confirmed the presence of Zn–O vibrations, verifying successful ZnO incorporation within the Aloe vera matrix.

The prominence-based FTIR analysis summarized in Supplementary Table S1 quantitatively supports these observations, showing a systematic reduction in peak height and prominence for C–O and C–O–C bands following nanocomposite incorporation. This behaviour strongly suggests that Aloe vera polysaccharides and related functional groups act as surface-binding and stabilizing agents for the ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanostructure, in agreement with earlier reports on biopolymer-assisted stabilization of metal oxide and silver nanoparticles [37]. Zeta potential analysis further corroborated the FTIR findings by providing insight into surface charge modulation and colloidal behavior. Aloe vera gel exhibited a moderately negative surface charge ( − 6.78 ± 4.70 mV), consistent with the presence of deprotonated hydroxyl and carboxyl groups within the biopolymeric network. The ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanocomposite alone showed a near-neutral negative charge ( − 1.56 ± 5.81 mV), reflecting the combined contribution of metal oxide, carbon nanotube, and silver components. Following incorporation into Aloe vera gel, the hybrid system displayed a shifted dominant zeta potential peak at −5.69 ± 6.03 mV, accounting for 97.4% of the total intensity-weighted population. The dominance of a single population indicates effective surface interaction between the nanocomposite and the Aloe vera matrix, resulting in a more homogeneous charge distribution and improved colloidal stability. Minor secondary populations ( < 2%) were attributed to trace aggregates and were considered negligible.

Aloe vera enhances the antioxidant potential of ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanocomposites

The antioxidant results revealed that ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanocomposite exhibited the lowest DPPH radical scavenging percentage among all tested groups (Fig. 5A). The IC₅₀ value of NPs was approximately 45% higher compared to Aloe vera alone, indicating weaker antioxidant capacity. However, incorporating Aloe vera into the nanocomposite showed only a 16% difference compared to Aloe vera gel, which was not statistically significant. In contrast, the difference between ZnO/SWCNT/Ag and the Aloe vera–nanocomposite mixture was 34% and highly significant (p < 0.0001), indicating that the antioxidant activity of the composite is primarily driven by Aloe vera–derived bioactive compounds and is effectively preserved after incorporation into the nanocomposite (Fig. 5B).

A previous study reported that Aloe vera and Cannabis sativa exhibited the strongest antioxidant activity, with IC₅₀ values of 353 μg/mL, compared to 428 μg/mL for Mentha longifolia [38]. In another study, Aloe vera gel was shown to contain significant amounts of phenolic, tannin, alkaloid, saponin, and flavonoid compounds, which were strongly correlated with its antioxidant potential [39].

In our study, incorporation of Aloe vera into the ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanocomposite reduced the IC₅₀ value by nearly 10%, suggesting improved antioxidant activity. This effect may be attributed to the synergistic interaction between Aloe vera flavonoids and the nanomaterials. Notably, it has been demonstrated that biohybrids formed between flavonoids and carbon nanotubes exhibited enhanced antioxidant activity [40]. This enhancement is likely related to hydroxyl (-OH) and carboxyl (-COOH) groups, which act as proton donors and efficiently scavenge free radicals. Such interactions may explain the observed improvement in the antioxidant potential of our nanocomposite when combined with Aloe vera gel.

Selective antibacterial activity of ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanocomposites

The antibacterial activity of ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanocomposite, Aloe vera gel, and their combination was evaluated against Gram-positive (S. aureus, B. subtilis, and E. faecalis) and Gram-negative (E. coli, P. aeruginosa, P. fluorescens, and A. hydrophila) bacterial strains. ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanocomposite suspension demonstrated significant antibacterial activity, producing an inhibition zone of 23 mm against E. coli. However, when combined with Aloe vera gel, an antagonistic effect was observed, reducing the inhibition zone diameter to 16 mm (Fig. 6A). The positive control, ciprofloxacin, produced consistent inhibition zones of approximately 39 mm across all tested strains. None of the other bacterial strains were inhibited by CNTs, Aloe vera gel, or their combination. The MIC assay revealed that the minimum inhibitory concentration of CNTs against E. coli was 250 μg/mL.

The antimicrobial effect of the nanocomposite may be attributed to the conjugation of ZnO and Ag NPs with SWCNTs. ZnO/Ag–SWCNT nanocomposites have been reported to exhibit strong antimicrobial activity, as oxidized SWCNTs are more hydrophilic and disperse well in polar media, allowing better surface contact with bacterial cells. Additionally, short SWCNT fibers can penetrate bacterial membranes, disrupt metabolic pathways, and ultimately cause cell rupture [41]. Ag NPs further contribute to antibacterial activity by accumulating in pits within bacterial cell walls, where the release of free radicals damages and destroys the cells [42]. In line with this, ZnO/SWCNT/Ag hybrids have been shown to enhance bactericidal activity against E. coli through excessive ROS production derived from NOX2 activation [43].

In our study, the nanocomposite exhibited antibacterial activity against E. coli but showed no effect against other tested bacterial strains. This selective activity toward E. coli reflects the strain-dependent antibacterial behavior of ZnO/Ag-based nanocomposites, as Gram-negative bacteria are generally more susceptible due to their negatively charged outer membrane, which enhances nanoparticle interaction, Ag⁺ release, and ROS generation [44]. In contrast, the thick peptidoglycan layer of Gram-positive bacteria and potential diffusion limitations of the nanocomposite in agar media may restrict antibacterial efficacy, resulting in a narrow activity spectrum [42, 45]. Similar findings were reported by Gomaa et al. who suggested that antimicrobial activity could be improved by combining nanocomposites with antibiotics, an approach that warrants further investigation [24]. By contrast, Aloe vera gel alone showed no antimicrobial effect in our study, consistent with earlier reports [46, 47]. The lack of activity has been attributed to poor diffusion of Aloe gel through agar [47] or to the use of crude gel without extraction of its active compounds. Indeed, ethanolic and methanolic extracts of Aloe vera have demonstrated antibacterial activity against various strains due to higher concentrations of bioactive phytochemicals [48, 49].

The metabolic activity of cells treated with the hybrid formulation (Aloe vera + ZnO/SWCNT/Ag) showed a 20% significant increase compared to ZnO/SWCNT/Ag alone at 12.5 µg/mL (p < 0.05, Fig. 6B). In comparison to Aloe vera alone at the same concentration, the hybrid group exhibited a 12% increase in metabolic activity; however, this difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). At higher concentrations (50 and 100 µg/mL), Aloe vera alone resulted in significantly greater metabolic activity than both ZnO/SWCNT/Ag and the hybrid formulation (p < 0.05), suggesting that Aloe vera’s bioactive components may support fibroblast viability independent of nanoparticle effects. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing a concentration-dependent increase in fibroblast metabolic activity with Aloe vera across a broad dose range (5–500 µg/mL) [50].

Importantly, ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanoparticles did not significantly reduce metabolic activity relative to untreated controls at any concentration (p > 0.05), indicating the absence of cytotoxic effects. These observations align with previous reports that plant extracts such as Aloe vera can enhance cell viability and biocompatibility in vitro. For example, incorporation of Aloe vera into polymeric composites or hydrogels has been shown to increase fibroblast viability above baseline, without inducing cytotoxic effects, as measured by MTT assays and ISO cytotoxicity thresholds ( > 70% viability) in murine L929 cells and other models, reinforcing its role as a biocompatible additive that may improve cell response to composite materials [51]. Moreover, studies of green-synthesized ZnO nanoparticles have demonstrated that cytotoxicity can be minimized or absent at moderate concentrations, particularly when synthesized using biological capping agents, whereas chemically synthesized ZnO NPs often exhibit greater toxicity [52].

Accelerated wound closure and scar reduction by nanocomposite treatment

Representative wound images were taken at defined time points (0, 3, 10, 15, and 21 days) with a ruler as reference, and the wound surface area was calculated to track the progression of healing in response to the applied therapies (Fig. 7). Measurement of wound area demonstrated that inclusion of ZnO/SWCNT/Ag in the topical application accelerated wound closure and reduced healing time. NPs alone enhanced wound healing significantly, showing a 1.5-fold ( ~ 35%) improvement compared to Aloe vera (p < 0.001) and a 1.4-fold ( ~ 30%) improvement compared to the saline control group (p < 0.05). Similarly, combining NPs with Aloe vera further improved wound healing, yielding a 1.3-fold ( ~ 25%) enhancement relative to Aloe vera alone (p < 0.05, Fig. 7). At the final time point, analysis of scar tissue at the injury site revealed that NPs (3.7-fold reduction) and NPs + Aloe vera (3.0-fold reduction) significantly decreased residual scar area compared to Aloe vera alone (p < 0.05). Although the difference was not statistically significant due to variability within the control group, ZnO/SWCNT/Ag (2.7-fold reduction) and NPs + Aloe vera (2.2-fold reduction) also showed lower scar formation compared to saline treatment (p > 0.05, Fig. 7).

To further account for repeated measurements within animals, wound closure data were additionally analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model. This analysis yielded results largely consistent with the two-way ANOVA, while also revealing significant treatment-dependent differences at earlier time points (Fig. 8A). Specifically, at day 3, significant differences were observed between Aloe vera and saline (p = 0.0022), Aloe vera + ZnO/SWCNT/Ag and ZnO/SWCNT/Ag (p = 0.0397), and Aloe vera + ZnO/SWCNT/Ag and saline (p < 0.0001). At day 10, significant differences were detected between Aloe vera and ZnO/SWCNT/Ag (p = 0.0006), Aloe vera + NPs and saline (p = 0.0146), and ZnO/SWCNT/Ag and saline (p < 0.0001). Similarly, the difference between Aloe vera + ZnO/SWCNT/Ag and Aloe vera remained significant (p = 0.044). Overall, wound closure was significantly affected by treatment, time, and their interaction. Pairwise comparisons at each time point were performed using Tukey-adjusted post-hoc testing, with full results provided in Supplementary Table S2. Furthermore, the estimated marginal means from the linear mixed-effects model showed faster early wound closure in the NPs and Aloe vera + ZnO/SWCNT/Ag groups compared with Aloe vera and saline, particularly at days 3–10 (Fig. 8B). Our findings are consistent with [53], who showed that Ag NPs promoted keratinocyte proliferation and migration, while also stimulating fibroblast differentiation into myofibroblasts, thereby enhancing wound contraction.

By contrast, Aloe vera alone did not show significant wound healing improvement in our study. This differs from reports by Oryan et al., where Aloe vera treatment modulated inflammation, increased wound contraction and epithelialization, reduced scar tissue size, and improved alignment and organization of regenerated tissue [54]. The discrepancy may be due to differences in Aloe preparation, concentration, or model conditions.

Notably, several recent studies indicate that nanostructuring Aloe vera markedly enhances its therapeutic efficacy compared to free Aloe vera. Altinkaynak et al. demonstrated that Aloe vera–incorporated hybrid nanoflowers exhibited superior antimicrobial, antioxidant, and wound-healing activities relative to free Aloe vera, significantly enhancing fibroblast proliferation, migration, and wound closure at lower concentrations [55]. Similarly, Barbosa et al. reported that Aloe vera–based composite nanofibers supported cell attachment and growth while providing antibacterial activity and favorable physicochemical properties for wound dressing applications [56]. In addition, Aloe vera–loaded nanocomposites incorporating metal or metal oxide nanoparticles have shown enhanced wound healing outcomes. Xanthan gum–based nanocomposites loaded with Aloe vera extract and Ag/MgO nanoparticles demonstrated strong antibacterial activity, low cytotoxicity, and accelerated in vitro wound healing [57]. Likewise, chitosan/Aloe vera/ZnO nanocomposite gels significantly improved wound closure, collagen deposition, and epitheliogenesis in vivo compared to controls [58]. Collectively, these studies support the notion that nanocomposite formulations enable improved bioavailability, sustained activity, and synergistic biological effects, which may explain the limited efficacy of Aloe vera alone observed in our study and highlight the advantage of nanoparticle-based hybrid systems for wound healing applications.

Complete epidermal regeneration and collagen deposition induced by nanocomposite

During the first week, histopathological examination of the control and Aloe vera groups revealed mild to moderate acute interstitial inflammation accompanied by severe hyper-eosinophilic and coagulative necrosis (scab formation). Additionally, mild to moderate eosinophilic albuminous oedema, moderate infiltration of pleomorphic mononuclear cells (neutrophils and macrophages), and severe hyperaemic blood vessels with erythrodiapedesis (leakage of red blood cells into surrounding tissues) were observed. In contrast, ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanocomposite and Aloe vera + nanocomposite groups exhibited severe acute interstitial inflammation, along with moderate coagulative necrosis, oedema, haemorrhage, and pronounced inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 9a, b) (Table 1).

By the second week, the control and Aloe vera groups showed moderate proliferation of mononuclear and fibroblastic cells, along with neovascularization. Similar findings were present in the nanocomposite and Aloe vera + ZnO/SWCNT/Ag groups, but with greater intensity (Fig. 9c). By the third week, the control and Aloe vera groups displayed moderate epidermal regeneration with mature granulation tissue and mild to moderate development of dermal glands and hair follicles. In contrast, the nanocomposite and Aloe vera + nanocomposite groups exhibited complete epidermal regeneration, characterized by well-structured keratinocyte layers, collagen deposition, and granulation tissue, as well as the presence of dermal glands and hair follicles. These findings indicate full restoration of skin structure and function, including regeneration of skin appendages (Fig. 9d).

Our observations align with earlier reports showing that CNT-based treatments influence wound healing by modulating inflammation and tissue regeneration. Wounds treated with chitosan–CNT complexes displayed greater infiltration of inflammatory cells, reflecting enhanced inflammatory activity, while also demonstrating improved epithelialization and collagen deposition compared to chitosan alone [7]. Similarly, CNT- and rGO-based formulations have been associated with macrophage infiltration, neovascularization, fibroblast proliferation, collagen fiber deposition, and epithelial coverage, which parallel the enhanced epidermal regeneration, collagen deposition, and granulation tissue observed in our CNT and Aloe vera + CNT groups [24].

With respect to Aloe vera, earlier work indicated that Aloe vera extracts promoted fibroblast proliferation more strongly than endothelial cells and stimulated fibroblast migration [59]. While this contrasts with our histopathological findings, where Aloe vera alone did not produce marked improvements compared with CNT-containing treatments, our in vitro viability assay provides important context. Specifically, Aloe vera significantly enhanced fibroblast metabolic activity at higher concentrations, confirming its direct cytocompatibility and pro-survival effects. However, increased cellular viability alone may be insufficient to drive the complex tissue-level processes required for accelerated wound repair, such as angiogenesis, matrix remodeling, and coordinated epithelial regeneration, which were more pronounced in nanoparticle-containing formulations. These differences may further reflect variations in extract preparation, concentration, and experimental models across studies.

Conclusion

The ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanocomposite combined with Aloe vera gel demonstrates selective antibacterial activity primarily driven by the nanoparticles and preserves the antioxidant potential of Aloe vera. Incorporation of the nanocomposite enhanced the wound-healing performance of Aloe vera, particularly during early and intermediate healing stages, without altering final wound closure outcomes. Histopathology confirmed enhanced collagen deposition, angiogenesis, and epidermal restoration. These findings suggest that ZnO/SWCNT/Ag nanoparticles can serve as an effective adjunct to Aloe vera, a widely used and biocompatible wound-care agent, warranting further studies to optimize antimicrobial efficacy and assess long-term safety.
