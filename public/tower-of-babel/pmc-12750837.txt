The role of virus in nasal inflammation
Authors: Chen Wang, Yi-Ming Zhang, Min Li, Ke-Jia Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractViral infection is an important pathogenic factor contributing to chronic inflammation of the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses. Rhinovirus, parainfluenza virus, influenza virus, and respiratory syncytial virus are among the most common viral pathogens associated with nasal diseases. These viruses enter host epithelial cells through distinct receptors. In patients with chronic rhinosinusitis or allergic rhinitis, disruption of the physiological balance of the nasal and sinus epithelium increases susceptibility to viral infection. Meanwhile, viral infection can enhance type 2 inflammation, mucus hypersecretion, airway hyper-responsiveness, tissue remodeling, and ultimately contribute to the persistence of chronic inflammation. In addition, viruses can induce nasal epithelial cells, innate immune cells, and tissue-resident memory T cells to develop local immune memory, amplifying secondary inflammatory responses. Viral infection may also predispose patients to secondary bacterial infections, further exacerbating inflammation. The aim of this review is to elucidate the role of respiratory viruses, particularly rhinoviruses, in the pathogenesis of nasal inflammation and to provide new insights into its etiology and potential therapeutic targets.Keywords: Human rhinovirus, Chronic rhinosinusitis, Allergic rhinitis, Airway epithelium

Introduction

Nasal inflammation, including allergic rhinitis (AR), non-allergic rhinitis（NAR）, and chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS), is a common condition characterized by persistent epithelial dysfunction, mucosal edema, and immune dysregulation. AR is caused by immunoglobulin (Ig)E-mediated reactions to inhaled allergens, which is often co-morbid with asthma [1]. CRS can be divided into CRS with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) and CRS without nasal polyps (CRSsNP). CRSwNP is usually associated with type 2 inflammation, characterized by elevated levels of IgE, interleukin (IL)-4, IL-5, and IL-13 and eosinophilic inflammation [2, 3]. Increasing evidence indicates that viral infection plays an important role in the initiation, exacerbation, persistence, and recurrence of CRS and AR [4, 5]. Rhinovirus (RV), influenza virus, human parainfluenza virus (HPIV), and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) are frequently detected in patients with acute and chronic nasal inflammatory diseases, especially RV, which is closely related to the development, aggravation and recurrence of the disease [6].

Different viruses enter host epithelial cells through distinct receptors. In patients with CRS and allergic nasal inflammation, airway epithelial receptor expression is upregulated, antiviral immune responses are impaired, and epithelial barrier integrity is compromised, which increases susceptibility to viral infection and contributes to chronic inflammation or secondary bacterial infections, leading to acute exacerbations of chronic disease [7, 8]. Viral infection also stimulates mucin gene expression and mucus overproduction in the respiratory epithelium, leading to tissue remodeling and contributing to disease recurrence and treatment resistance [9]. Moreover, viral infection can induce the formation of innate immune memory in epithelial cells, macrophages, and type 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s), as well as tissue-resident memory T cells, thereby establishing local airway immune memory that amplifies secondary inflammatory responses upon re-exposure [10–12].

Therefore, in this review, we focus on summarizing the receptors that different viruses use to enter host respiratory epithelial cells, and on how viral infections—particularly RV—affect epithelial barrier function and antiviral immune responses. We also discuss recent advances in understanding the role of viral infections in nasal inflammatory diseases, including AR, NAR and CRS. In addition, we highlight the involvement of trained immunity and adaptive immune memory in the regulation and persistence of nasal inflammation.

Virus and airway epithelium

The epithelium was initially regarded mainly as a physical barrier against allergens, viral infections, airborne pollutants, physical stimuli, and drugs [13]. The upper airway epithelium is not only a mechanical barrier against pathogens through mucociliary clearance and tight junctions (TJs) formation, but also an immune barrier that inhibits viral replication by producing antiviral proteins (such as interferon（IFN）) and regulates innate immune responses through cytokine production [14, 15]. In vitro and animal experiments have revealed that increased susceptibility of airway epithelium to respiratory virus infection primarily occurs through two mechanisms: impaired innate and adaptive immune responses and enhanced expression of viral receptors on the respiratory epithelium [7].

Substantial evidence has shown that nasal inflammation is often accompanied by microbial dysregulation, which may play an important role in causing initial immune damage to the respiratory and sinus mucosa, resulting in chronic inflammation [4]. In children, reduced microbial diversity in the nasal cavity may be associated with subsequent rhinitis, asthma, as well as allergen sensitization [16]. The composition of the nasal microbiota is altered in CRSwNP patients, and treatment with Dupilumab can shift the nasal bacterial community toward that observed in healthy controls [17]. This may be because a diverse array of microbes colonizing the nasal cavity can produce inhibitors and consume nutrients to inhibit pathogen infection [18].

Virus and nasal epithelial receptors

Various types of viruses enter the host epithelium via specific receptors. RV and its subtypes invade host cells through three cell membrane glycoproteins, namely, intercellular cell adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), members of the low density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) family, and cadherin-related family member 3 (CDHR3) [19, 20]. It has been demonstrated that ICAM-1, which serves as the receptor for RV, is upregulated in respiratory epithelial cells of allergic patients [21]. Everman et al. reported an 80% reduction in RV-C infection upon deletion of CDHR3 in human airway epithelium [22].

RSV is mainly composed of two subtypes (A and B) whose external transmembrane glycoproteins (G, F, SH) are critical for RSV attachment and fusion [23]. RSV targets ciliated epithelial cells in the airway [24]. The receptors for RSV entry and pathogenesis are as follows: RSV G protein binds to CX3CR1 and heparan sulfate proteoglycans, while RSV F protein can interact with nucleolin, epidermal growth factor receptor, insulin-like growth factor 1, and ICAM-1 [25]. Influenza A and B viruses have two surface glycoproteins: hemagglutinin and neuraminidase [26]. The hemagglutinin protein on the surface of influenza virus first polyvalently binds to the sialic acid portion (α-2,6-SA or α-2,3-SA) on the surface of host cells [27]. Subsequently, the virus enters the cell through endocytosis and macropinocytosis, and the endocytosed viral particles are transported toward the perinuclear region [28].

HPIV enters cells by fusing directly with the plasma membrane. Virions carry two surface glycoproteins that synergistically mediate viral entry into host cells: the receptor-binding protein hemagglutinin–neuraminidase and the fusion protein [29]. Protein hemagglutinin–neuraminidase binds to the sialic acid-containing receptor, activates and induces conformational changes in the fusion protein, driving virus–host membrane fusion and virus entry into target cells [30].

There are also other factors that can change the level of receptors in epithelial cells. Allergy contributes to the upregulation of influenza virus receptors [7]. The increased replication of RV14 observed in human nasal epithelial cells (hNECs) from allergic subjects may be partly related to enhanced ICAM-1 expression through nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) activation [31].

Viruses and immune responses in nasal epithelial cells

In response to viral infections, such as influenza virus and RV, host cells typically produce two types of IFN: type I (IFN-α/β) and type III (IFN-λ) [32, 33]. Originally recognized for their ability to impede influenza virus replication [34], type I IFNs were subsequently found to have no apparent effect on susceptibility to respiratory viruses in mice lacking type I IFN receptors. Type I IFNs directly inhibit viral replication, stimulating B cells to produce antibodies and T cells to produce adaptive immune responses that recognize and destroy virus-infected cells [35]. Type III IFNs are the predominant IFNs induced during respiratory viral infections [36]. IFN-λ was suggested to act as a first line of defense in the upper respiratory epithelium [32, 37], mediating antiviral response [35]. HNECs induce IFN-λ but not IFN-β upon respiratory viral infection [38, 39]. Furthermore, IFN-λ is the primary IFN produced by epithelial cells and dendritic cells (DCs) in response to influenza A virus infection [34].

During the initial phase of respiratory viral infection, viral nucleic acids in epithelial cells are detected and bound by three pattern recognition receptors, including toll-like receptor (TLR)-3, retinoic acid-induced gene (RIG)-I, and melanoma differentiation-associated protein (MDA)-5 [40–42]. Activation of these receptors enhances the release of antiviral IFNs to combat viral infection, activating NF-κB and interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3), thereby increasing production of IFN-β, IFN-γ, IL-6 and IL-8 [40–42]. Endocytosis is a major cell entry mechanism used by respiratory viruses [43] (Fig. 1).

The state of innate immune preparedness of the respiratory mucosa influences the susceptibility to viral challenge. When the respiratory mucosa exhibits neutrophilic inflammation, it is more susceptible to RSV infection [44]. Type 2 mucosal immune responses can suppress type 1 antiviral immunity, potentially influencing the pathogenesis of RSV in the respiratory mucosa [45]. In asthma, the response of goblet and multiciliated cells is impaired, thereby affecting the signaling pathways involved in the response to RSV [46]. Following exposure to cigarette smoke, the antiviral defense function of airway epithelial cells becomes impaired, resulting in an increased viral load, which may be attributed to the impairment of early IFN gene expression [47]. Wang et al. discovered that cigarette smoke exposure induces oxidative stress and postulated that the cytokine and growth factor, growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) might be upregulated, consequently leading to compromised viral clearance [47]. GDF15 has been shown to enhance RV replication and inflammation by inhibiting the production of IFN-λ1 protein in human airway epithelial cells [48].

Virus disrupts TJs of the nasal epithelium

The upper respiratory epithelium is composed of pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelial cells, which serve as a crucial interface for both adaptive and innate responses through the formation of TJs. These TJs play a vital role in preventing invasion by inhaled environmental agents, such as allergens and pathogens [49, 50]. Key components of TJs include occludin, claudin, junctional adhesion molecules, and the scaffold protein zonula occludens (ZO). The expression of TJ molecules and barrier function of normal hNECs can be influenced by various stimuli, including growth factors, TLR ligands, and cytokines [51]. TJs are intercellular structures that are critical for maintaining epithelial barrier function and play an important role in antimicrobial defense [52].

Evidence has demonstrated that disruption of TJs in the airway epithelium plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of asthma, AR, and CRS. The sinonasal epithelium primarily as a physical barrier, and dysfunction of the epithelial barrier is a common characteristic observed in endotype-specific cytokine-induced CRS, acting both directly and indirectly [53]. In CRSwNP, TJs are impaired, leading to significant reductions in mRNA and protein levels of ZO-1 [8, 54]. RV infection downregulates the expression of TJ molecules, such as ZO-1, E-cadherin, occludin, and claudin-1 in primary hNEC cultures, thereby compromising barrier function [55]. Moreover, RV infection disrupts respiratory epithelial barrier integrity [56], causing destruction of TJ structures in hNECs along with compromised barrier function, which exacerbates mucus production and may contribute to secondary bacterial infections [57].

Rhinovirus

Basic virology of human RV

Human RVs, members of the family Picornaviridae and the genus Enterovirus, were initially discovered in the 1950 s as part of efforts to identify the cause of the common cold [58]. Rather than being classified into consecutively numbered serotypes, RVs are now grouped into two distinct species, RV-A and RV-B, based on phylogenetic sequence criteria [58, 59]. In addition, RV-C was first reported in 2006 and formally recognized as a distinct species in 2009, largely due to its inability to grow in standard cell cultures [6, 60]. RV-A and RV-C, compared to RV-B, are more frequently associated with severe respiratory conditions, including pneumonia, bronchiolitis, influenza-like illnesses, CRS, as well as exacerbations of asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and cystic fibrosis [61].

Different species of RVs exploit distinct receptors to enter epithelial cells, thereby triggering diverse host responses [62, 63]. All RV-B strains and most RV-A strains bind to host epithelial cells through ICAM-1, while a minority of RV-A strains utilize the LDLR for cell entry. RV-C, in contrast, enters cells through CDHR3 [61]. Based on receptor specificity, RV-A and RV-B can be further classified into major and minor groups: the major group utilizes ICAM-1, while the minor group attaches to and enters cells via LDLR [58, 63] (Fig. 2).

Human RV and its impact on respiratory tract infections

RV is one of the most frequent causative agents of acute respiratory tract infections in the world [64, 65]. Nationwide prospective surveillance of all-age patients with acute respiratory infections was conducted in China between 2009 and 2019. Influenza virus, RSV and human RV are the three leading viral pathogens with proportions of 28.5, 16.8 and 16.7% [66]. In immunocompromised hosts, RV infection may progress from the upper to the lower respiratory tract [67]. Infections in the lower respiratory tract are also considered to be an important factor in exacerbation of asthma and COPD. RV triggers an inflammatory response through the release of chemokines and cytokines into the airways and recruit immune cells (such as neutrophils, eosinophils, and T cells), thereby increasing the risk of allergic airway inflammation or exacerbating existing airway inflammation [68]. In hNECs, RV predominantly targets ciliated cells and induces type I and type III IFNs antiviral pathways. Airway inflammatory response was mainly associated with C–X–C motif chemokine ligand (CXCL) 9, IP-10, CXCL11 and regulated on activation, normal T cell expressed and secreted (RANTES) [68].

The role of RV in asthma

Exacerbations of COPD and asthma are frequently associated with respiratory viral infections, with RV being the most prevalent pathogen [69, 70]. In both children and adults with asthma, the detection rate of RV and RSV are elevated [71]. Strong evidence indicates that respiratory viruses, especially human RV and RSV, play a significant role in the development and exacerbation of asthma [72]. The interaction between allergen exposure and RV infection synergistically triggers asthma exacerbations and promotes airway remodeling [73]. RV infected airway epithelial cells robustly produce type 2 cytokines and chemokines, as well as pro-angiogenic and fibroblast activating factors that act in a paracrine manner on neighboring airway cells to stimulate remodeling responses [74]. In asthmatic individuals, defects or delays in the antiviral immune response to RV, together with overactivation of the type 2 immune pathway, may contribute to disease exacerbation [59]. However, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood.

In ovalbumin (OVA)-stimulated asthma mouse models, RV infection increased type 2 cytokines and enhanced eosinophilic inflammation and airway hyper-reactivity [75]. Histamine releasing factor (HRF) regulates mast cell and basophil activation mediated by FcεRI and contributes to allergic diseases [76]. In asthma, HRF has been shown to enhance type 2 inflammation, mucus secretion, and airway hyper-responsiveness [76]. HRF and HRF-reactive IgE may, therefore, play a pathogenic role in severe asthma and RV-induced exacerbations [77]. RV-induced neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) also contribute to acute asthma exacerbations. In a study by Curren et al., mice with chronic experimental asthma were inoculated with RV resulting in an early inflammatory response dominated by neutrophils, neutrophil-related cytokines (IL-1α, IL-1β, and CXCL1), and NETosis. This was followed by a type 2 inflammatory phase characterized by eosinophil infiltration, elevated IL-4 levels, and the hyperplasia of goblet cells [78].

IFN constitutes a critical element of the innate immune response against respiratory viruses and can influence asthma development and exacerbation [79]. Type I IFNs (IFN-α/β) and type III IFNs (IFN-λs) are key mediators of the early antiviral response [80], with type III IFNs being the predominant form elevated following respiratory virus infection and limiting Th2 cell development in asthma [36]. In the innate immune response to RV infection, IFN-λ is likely produced by bronchial epithelial cells (BECs), IFN-α is predominantly generated by peripheral blood mononuclear cells, and IFN-β is produced by both cell types [81]. A study collected upper and lower respiratory tract tissues from patients in the control group, AR group, and AR + asthma group. Polyinosinic–polycytidylic acid [poly(I:C)] induction was employed to perform gene expression analysis, IFN-β1 (IFNB1) and IFN-λ3 (IL28B) were upregulated in control and AR groups but impaired in the AR + asthma group [82]. In children with asthma, BECs exhibit a deficient IFN response to respiratory viruses, leading to increased viral replication [79]. Impaired IFN expression is associated with adverse clinical outcomes of respiratory disease after RV infection [83].

The expression of IL-33 is upregulated in the lungs and airways of asthma patients and correlates with disease severity [84]. Virus-induced release of IL-33, along with IL-33-responsive T cells and group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s), serves as a crucial mechanistic link between viral infection and exacerbation of asthma [85]. In RV infection, ILC responses increase in both asthmatic and healthy individuals, but ILC2s dominate in asthma, driving stronger type 2 cytokine responses, more severe infection, and worse outcomes [86]. In the context of asthma, RSV and RV infections can trigger the release of IL-33, leading to type 2 inflammation [87]. Moreover, IL-33 also dampens type I and III IFN induction, increasing viral load, whereas anti-IL-33 therapies can enhance antiviral immunity, reduce viral load, and mitigate RV-induced exacerbations [88]. In addition, RV infection of BECs activates TLR3, promoting the release of proinflammatory tenascin-C (TN-C), inducing local airway cytokine production, and exacerbating asthma symptoms [89].

The role of RV in AR

The pathogenesis of AR is characterized by IgE-mediated reactions to inhaled allergens, leading to mucosal inflammation driven by type 2 cells [68]. This process includes the release of various Th2 cytokines, which significantly influence eosinophil survival and sIgE synthesis [90]. Currently, our understanding of the role of respiratory viruses in AR remains limited.

In a prospective study, Kim et al. reported a significantly higher detection rate of viral infections among perennial AR patients compared to the control group, with RV being the most frequently identified pathogen [91]. However, there was no statistically significant association between AR symptom scores and the presence of respiratory viruses [91]. In contrast, another search found that RV positivity was correlated with increased nasal symptom severity in children with AR who were not undergoing allergen immunotherapy [92]. For patients with AR, the risk of primary care visits for respiratory tract infection symptoms increases within 1 year following infection with RV-C [64]. Feng et al. reported that patients with AR exhibited more severe cold symptoms and enhanced eosinophilic inflammation after RV infection, accompanied by impaired innate immune responses characterized by reduced interferon production and decreased expression of TLR3, RIG-I, and MDA5, despite comparable viral titers [93]. The interaction between viral infection and allergy is complex and the mechanisms remain unclear. In some patients with allergic asthma, RV infection in the nasal mucosa induces a local increase in IgE, which does not occur in non-allergic individuals [94].

An intricate interplay exists between allergic inflammation and viral infection, as evidenced by the up-regulation of ICAM-1 [31, 95, 96], a receptor for major groups of RV, subsequently increases susceptibility to RV infection [97]. RV infection disrupts the barrier function of respiratory epithelial cells, enhances submucosal allergic responses, and exacerbates allergic reactions [98]. It may also induce a type 2 immune response and local IgE production in AR patients [92]. In AR children with RV infection, nasal IgE, IL-25, IL-4, and CXCL13 levels were elevated [92]. Contoli et al. demonstrated that patients with atopic rhinitis exhibited elevated IL-4 mRNA levels, concomitant reduction in TLR3 expression, and increased replication of RV-16 in hNECs following nasal brushing compared to those with non-atopic rhinitis. Th2 cytokines IL-4 and IL-13 can impair the immune response of respiratory epithelial cells against RV-16 while enhancing vulnerability to viral infection [99]. Głobiońska et al. collected hNECs from AR patients and controls. After PIV3 and RV-1B infection, hNECs from AR patients showed reduced IFN-β mRNA, RANTES protein, and mRNA, suggesting that AR alters viral proliferation rates and the immune response profile in the nasal epithelium [38].

However, some studies have shown the opposite results. Ko et al. obtained the uncinate process mucosa from CRS patients and divided them into groups with or without concomitant AR. They cultured hNECs and then infected them with HRV, showing no significant difference in infection rate between the groups [100]. In AR patients, RV infection can enhance the Th2 environment through the programmed cell death-ligand circuit and is associated with the weakening of the antiviral immune response [100].

The role of RV in non-allergic rhinitis

Apart from AR, rhinitis can also be classified into non-allergic/non-infectious subtypes, such as vasomotor rhinitis, as well as acute or chronic infectious rhinitis, which may involve viral, bacterial, or fungal pathogens [101]. Collectively, these conditions represent a heterogeneous group of diseases [101]. Acute upper respiratory tract infections, or the common cold, are most frequently caused by RVs, with HCoV, SARS, HRSV, and HPIV also common [95, 96]. Although the common cold is generally self-limiting, its extremely high incidence and propensity for recurrent infections contribute to a considerable health, economic, and social burden [102]. In air–liquid interface (ALI) models, infection with HCoV-229E, HCoV-NL63, or RV-16 disrupts epithelial barrier integrity, while epithelial cells alone, rather than resident innate immune cells, were sufficient to clear the viruses through IFN responses, allowing time for the recruitment of myeloid cells for further viral elimination [42].

Although RV infection is not a direct cause of CRS, recurrent infections or acute exacerbations contribute significantly. Repeated viral infections may promote chronicity by disrupting mucosal barrier function [42] and overactivating Th2-type responses [100]. Compared with AR patients, NAR patients develop show similar nasal symptoms, secretions and overall pathophysiological responses after RV infection, but reductions in mucociliary clearance, nasal congestion, eustachian tube dysfunction, and sneezing occur earlier in allergic individuals [103]. Beyond clinical symptoms, immune and inflammatory parameters also differ. In RV-39 infection, both AR and NAR patients showed increased leucocyte histamine release and platelet aggregation, but only AR patients had elevated serum IgE [104]. The prevalence of RV infection is similar in CRS patients with or without allergy; however, in non-allergic CRS patients, the Th2 environment is mainly established via Th2/Th1 modulation, with a higher IL-4/IFN-γ ratio than in allergic CRS [100].Vasomotor rhinitis involves mucosal vascular and secretory responses triggered by non-specific stimuli and is closely related to neural regulation. Mediators such as bradykinin, histamine, and amines act on sensory receptors, including capsaicin receptor transient receptor potential vanilloid-1 (TRPV1) [105]. RV interactions with neurons can lead to early upregulation of TRPA1 and TRPV1 expression [106]. However, no further studies have confirmed the correlation. RV induces airway hyper-responsiveness via IL-11, which may be associated with the symptoms of CRS [107].

The role of RV in CRS

Both acute and chronic sinusitis involve mucosal inflammation that impairs normal mucociliary function in the nose and paranasal sinuses. The spectrum of acute rhinosinusitis (ARS) encompasses various conditions, such as the common cold (acute viral rhinosinusitis), post-viral ARS, and acute bacterial rhinosinusitis, with the latter often being a consequence of the former two [108]. CRS has a multifactorial pathogenesis, including anatomical abnormalities, ciliary dysfunction, allergic reactions, and bacterial, viral, or fungal infections [109, 110]. However, the precise role of viruses in CRS remains unclear [111].

While numerous studies have investigated immune responses triggered by RV infection in the lower respiratory tract, limited attention has been given to the upper respiratory tract [68]. This review, therefore, focuses on the role of RV in CRS, where patients often experience exacerbations following upper respiratory tract infections, with RV as the predominant viral pathogen [112].

Respiratory viral infections are frequently implicated as triggers of CRS flare-ups [6]. Numerous studies have demonstrated RV infection of hNECs in CRS patients [6, 113]. Cho et al. reported a higher prevalence of respiratory virus infection in patients with CRS compared to controls, with RV being the most prevalent among various respiratory viruses [6]. Basharat et al. conducted an extensive Meta-analyses review examining the relationship between RV and CRS, concluding that RV prevalence is elevated in CRS patients and can induce inflammatory responses, potentially exacerbating disease progression [114]. Lal et al. showed elevated antibody levels against Staphylococcus aureus, influenza A virus (H1N1, H3N2) and RV-B14 in CRSwNP and against human herpesvirus type 1 in CRSsNP [111]. Lee et al. discovered that t RV detection rates and serotype diversity were higher in CRS patients. Among the 8 serotypes detected in CRS patients, 5 belonged to RV-A and 3 belonged to RV-B, whereas only RV-A13 and RV-A16 were found in controls; RV-C were not detected in either group [113]. However, there are conflicting findings as Wood et al. did not detect respiratory viruses in the nasal mucosa of both CRS and normal individuals using polymerase chain reaction (PCR), although their sample size was small [115]. Seasonal analysis showed RV detection peaks in autumn and winter, its epidemic season, but its persistence does not appear directly associated with CRS development [116].

Studies have demonstrated the involvement of respiratory viruses in nasal exacerbation and ongoing inflammation. Persistent viral infection may promote CRS development. In CRSwNP patients, RV infection induces differential epigenetic remodeling in the airway epithelium, which may underlie the chronic inflammation characteristic of CRSwNP [117].

The role of virus in CRS inflammation may be related to IFN production disorder in epithelial cells. Kim et al. observed delayed and attenuated IFN-β production and MDA5 mRNA expression in hNECs from CRS patients infected with RV, compared with controls [118]. In inflammatory sinus mucosa from CRSsNP or CRSwNP patients, levels of IFN-β, IFN-λ1, IFN-λ2, and ISG-related proteins (viperin, oligoadenylate synthetase and myxovirus resistance 1) were lower than in normal tissue, which showed the impairment of the antiviral innate response in inflammatory sinonasal epithelial cells [119]. IL-13 serves as a pivotal regulator in type-2 mediated inflammation in CRSwNP. Long-term exposure to IL-13 in the nasal epithelium of patients with CRSwNP disrupts nasal epithelial barrier function and reduces antiviral responses after acute RV infection. When hNECs are exposed to IL-13, the ability of antiviral immune response (IFN-λ1) and chemokine (CXCL10) signaling is impaired compared to untreated epithelium [52]. In asymptomatic patients with RV infection, IFN-λ levels were elevated, while IFN-α/β levels remained low compared with controls, in whom both interferons increased, suggesting that patients with CRSwNP may lack an early innate antiviral response and compensate through a later adaptive induction of IFN-λ production [120]. Lee et al. demonstrated that RV-16 infection or the viral mimic poly(I:C) upregulated type I interferon (IFN-b), type III interferons (IFN-λ1 and IFN-λ2), and interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) [121]. However, another study found no delay or defect in IFNs and ISGs expression in the inflammatory epithelium of CRSwNP compared to normal tissue [40].

RV infection effectively activates the NF-κB signaling pathway in airway epithelial cells, leading to the upregulation of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines. These potent proinflammatory mediators are increased during upper airway injury and play a crucial role in HRV-induced rhinosinusitis [19, 122]. RV-14 stimulation of nasal polyp epithelial cells in CRS patients increased IL-8 significantly, while tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, IL-1β, granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) levels were elevated but not significantly [41]. RV attacks the primary hNECs of CRS patients, significantly inducing the secretion of inflammatory cytokine IL-1b and cell death, and enhancing the secretion of characteristic cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6) of the NF-κB signaling pathway [123]. However, the levels of inflammatory factors INF-γ, macrophage inflammatory protein-2 (MIP-2), and IL-13 in the sinus mucosa of mice stimulated by RV1B were significantly increased in the control group, while INF-γ, TNF-α, MIP-2, IL-6 and IL-13 levels were no significant difference in the OVA-stimulated chronic allergic rhinosinusitis mouse model [124].

Dysregulated mucociliary clearance contributes to CRS and recurrent disease [125], and pendrin is associated with airway mucus production. Seshadri et al. obtained hNECs from CRS patients and found that pendrin was induced by Th2/Th17 cytokines, which was enhanced by RV infection, suggesting that RV infection may promote mucus production in CRS [126]. Liu et al. using hNECs obtained from CRS patients, observed activation of the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome signaling pathway following RV infection, which promotes the production of major airway epithelial mucin and mucus production [123]. Impaired mucociliary clearance and disrupted epithelial integrity, in turn, lead to a significantly higher RV load in patients with CRS compared with healthy individuals, as RV gains easier access to the epithelium [120]. CRS often manifests as tissue remodeling [127]. RV infection induces extracellular matrix protein deposition in airway epithelial cells [128], and upregulates matrix metalloproteinase-9 via NF-κB signaling [129], increased goblet cells and mucin(MUC)5AC expression [9], contributing to tissue remodeling.

Viruses contribute to exacerbations of acute sinusitis and CRS [130]. Respiratory virus infection can induce airway epithelial cells to produce type 2 cytokines through IL-1 and thymic stromal lymphopoietin stimulation, forming a type 2 inflammatory response [131] (Fig. 3).

Co-infection in nasal inflammation

Co-infection is frequently observed in respiratory infections. In patients with influenza-like illness, Staphylococcus aureus was the most frequent pathogen (approximately 30%), followed by SARS-CoV-2 (around 25%), RV (approximately 15%), and HHV-6 (approximately 10%). The most common co-infections were viral and bacterial (about 69%), followed by virus–virus co-infections (about 23%) [132]. Respiratory tract infections are typically caused by multiple pathogens rather than a single one. Therefore, it is important to understand the interactions between bacteria and viruses, as well as among different viruses.

Virus–bacteria co-infection in nasal inflammation

Respiratory viruses can predispose hosts to bacterial infection through various mechanisms such as impairment of the respiratory epithelial barrier, dysregulation of host immune responses, and enhanced adhesion of bacterial pathogens to airway epithelial cells [133, 134]. Damage or destruction of mucociliary function due to viral infection may be the main cause of secondary bacterial infection [135].

RV infection in patients with CRS can predispose individuals to secondary bacterial infections, leading to acute exacerbations of CRS (AECRS) [130, 136]. Co-infection with RV and bacteria in AECRS results in greater disease severity than either single infection or absence of infection in CRS patients [133]. In adults, asymptomatic infections with RSV and RV led to a significant increase in secondary pneumococcal infections [137]. Bacterial small RNAs may contribute to inflammation in RV- and RSV-associated bronchiolitis through different mechanisms [138]. Rs6967330 is the minor allele of CDHR3. Individuals carrying the minor allele rs6967330 (AA/AG) are more prone to CRS and childhood asthma exacerbations [139]. Palumbo et al. found that carrying this minor allele significantly increased the risk of AECRS in RV-positive patients. ALI cultures show higher RV-A and RV-C loads and greater bacterial susceptibility during AECRS due to downregulated TLR signaling [136]. In the first stages on infection, the TLRs play a crucial role in RV recognition [140].

Wang et al. demonstrated that RV-16 infection significantly upregulated the expression of host cell adhesion molecules, including fibronectin, platelet-activating factor receptor, and carcinoembryonic antigen, which serve as receptors for Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Haemophilus influenza, respectively, thereby increasing susceptibility to bacterial upper respiratory tract infections [141]. Respiratory viruses also enhanced Nontypeable Haemophilus influenzae adhesion and expression in hNECs [142]. Moreover, RV-16 and airborne fungi synergistically increased mucin gene MUC4, MUC5AC, and MUC8 expression in hNECs [143]. Influenza virus raised CD47 levels in ciliated cells, potentially providing adhesion sites for Staphylococcus aureus and facilitating secondary infection [144].

Virus–virus co-infection in nasal inflammation

The co-infection of viruses can increase the incidence of asthma, but the mixed infection of viruses does not always show a synergistic effect. In children aged 6–8 years with bronchiolitis, the most frequently identified single viruses were RSV and RV, the most common co-infections involved RSV + RV or RSV + human Bocavirus (HBoV). Patients of bronchiolitis with viral coinfection were 2.5 times more likely to develop asthma and experienced a higher rate of re-hospitalization compared to those with single virus infections [145].

HSIAO et al. discovered that chronic primary RV infection in hNECs resulted in a decrease of secondary influenza A viral load and had no significant impact on reinfection with RV viral load, likely via RIG-I and ISG upregulation, and induced an antiviral state against subsequent influenza infection [146]. Type I/III IFNs from RSV-A or H1N1 infection inhibited later RV infection, while RV did not block subsequent RSV-A or H1N1 [147]. Prior SARS-CoV-2 infection reduced replication of H1N1 and RSV-A2, whereas H1N1 limited later SARS-CoV-2 via IFN production [148].Serine proteinases are associated with Influenza A virus (IAV) entry into host cells and human nasal commensal Staphylococcus epidermidis could potentiate C entry into hNECs by inducing serine proteinase inhibitor Serpine1 levels, and prevent IAV from spreading to the lungs through the downregulation of airway proteases at nasal mucosal levels [149].

Virus-induced immune memory in rhinitis

Trained immunity refers to the metabolic and epigenetic reprogramming of innate immune cells (such as monocytes and macrophages) and structural cells (such as epithelial cells and fibroblasts) after primary infection or vaccination, enabling stronger and faster responses to later pathogens [150]. Risha et al. showed that repeated RV infection of bronchial epithelial cells induced “trained immunity” through epigenetic reprogramming, identifying trained genes (BGN, S100A8) and linking them to asthma pathogenesis [151]. Respiratory viruses may exert antagonistic effects via short-term IFNs or long-term trained immunity, thereby reducing subsequent viral infection and replication [152]. Influenza vaccination has been shown to reduce both the risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection and disease severity [153]. In IAV infection, surviving airway epithelial club cells undergo transcriptional reprogramming and display enhanced type I IFNs antiviral responses and secondary infection, conferring transient protection against subsequent viral respiratory infections [154].

Intranasal adenovirus vaccination in mice generates long-lived memory alveolar macrophages with enhanced MHC-II and glycolysis, which rapidly produce neutrophil chemokines and provide nonspecific protection upon restimulation [155]. Upon viral or allergen exposure, nasal epithelial damage activates macrophages, dendritic cells, and ILC2s. On re-exposure, enhanced TNF-α/IL-6 responses are generated, while trained ILC2s produce more type 2 cytokines, driving allergic reactions and type 2 inflammation [156]. Early life RV-A1B nasal infection in mice expanded ILC2s, establishing trained immunity that enhanced type 2 responses (IL-5, IL-13, mucin genes, and eosinophilia) upon subsequent RV-A2 challenge [10]. Similarly, early enterovirus infection trained macrophages via glycolytic reprogramming, increasing TNF-α/IL-6 secretion and promoting Th2 differentiation, thereby enhancing airway allergic responses [11].

In adaptive immunity, viral infection induces tissue-resident memory T cells in the upper airway, which accelerate cytokine responses and contribute to both inflammation and protection during re-infection or allergen exposure [12, 157]. Circulating memory CD4+T cells can also rapidly target RV capsid proteins upon re-infection [158]. Long-term changes in memory alveolar macrophages also depend on T cells, linking adaptive immunity to trained immunity [155, 159]. Together, virus-induced trained immunity in airway epithelial cells and innate immune cells, along with tissue-resident memory T cells, form an integrated upper airway immune memory. In rhinitis, this lowers the re-exposure threshold, amplifies type 2 inflammation, and disrupts barrier homeostasis, thereby promoting chronicity and refractoriness—providing a theoretical basis for novel intervention strategies. However, the role of virus-mediated immune memory in nasal inflammation still needs further study.

Other viruses and nasal inflammation

RSV primarily infects upper respiratory tract cells, particularly hNECs, which are characterized by TJs and exhibit remarkable resistance to RSV infection. RSV infection upregulates TJ proteins, such as occludin, claudin-4, and ZO-1 [160]. However, BECs obtained from asthmatic patients and healthy people responded basically similar to RSV infection, with lower expression of genes involved in IFN response in goblet cells [161]. A decade-long study has revealed a positive correlation between levels of RSV-specific IgG4 antibodies in children and the incidence of atopic diseases, such as AR, atopic dermatitis, and asthma [162]. Infection of hNECs with RSV induces an IFN-γ-dependent pathway that leads to a significant increase in IL-6, IL-8, and RANTES [163]. Both RSV and RV have been identified as risk factors for asthma [164]. While RSV is the predominant pathogen causing bronchiolitis, RV becomes dominant after 1 year of age [165].

Lewandowska et al. utilized HPIV type 3 to infect hNECs and observed a significant upregulation in IFN-γ mRNA and RANTES protein and mRNA expression [166]. Suptawiwat et al. discovered that the up-regulation of both 2,3- and 2,6-linked sialic acid enhances the susceptibility of nasal polyp tissues to avian and human influenza viruses [167]. Infection with H3N2 IAV can impair the integrity of TJs in hNECs, elevate oncostatin M expression, and exacerbate the inflammatory response in CRSwNP [8]. In the primary hNECs of healthy people infected with H1N1, the IFN-associated proteins IFN-α and IFN-γ and the levels of IL-6 and IL-17A, TGF-β and TNF-α were significantly increased. Then, they co-cultured H1N1 virus-infected hNECs with DCs and found that could significantly promote the migration of DCs to epithelial cells and also promote the maturation of nasal submucosal DCs [168]. Influenza virus can increase the accumulation of type 2 inflammation-related factor IL-25 in upper airway epithelial cells, which may contribute to susceptibility to or perpetuation of type 2 inflammation in the pathogenesis of CRSwNP [169].

The nasal cavity serves as the primary site for both the initial infection and the subsequent immune response of the for SARS-CoV-2 [170]. Significantly elevated levels of IFN-associated proteins (IFN-α2a, IFN-β, and IFN-γ) and TH1 cytokines (IL-2, IL-12, TNF-α, and IL-6) and TH2 cytokines (IL-4) were detected in nasal samples from patients with early COVID-19 infection [171]. CRS does not appear to be a significant risk factor for SARS-CoV-2 [172, 173]. In fact, there may even be a potential protective role against SARS-CoV-2 infection, possibly attributed to the downregulated expression of ACE2 receptor on airway epithelium leading to reduced viral binding [174]. However, current findings present conflicting results [175]. A Korean study conducted by Lee et al. reported an increased susceptibility and severity of SARS-CoV-2 in patients with CRS compared to those without CRS [176], and Sbeih et al. demonstrated a higher likelihood of SARS-CoV-2 hospitalization among patients with CRS [177]. A population-based study found that infections with RSV, SARS-CoV-2, or influenza virus were associated with a significantly increased risk of developing CRS within 1 year, with the risk further elevated after multiple infections. The authors suggested that viral infections may act as initial triggers of chronic rhinosinusitis by promoting sinonasal immune dysregulation, microbiome alterations, and epithelial barrier dysfunction [178].

This review has several limitations that should be considered. Most studies cited are based on specific populations, animal models, or in vitro systems, limiting generalizability to clinical settings. Variability in diagnostic criteria for rhinitis and viral detection methods may also affect the consistency of findings. Due to the “one airway, one disease” concept emphasizes the unified pathophysiology of upper and lower airway inflammation, some references in this review introduce respiratory concepts without fully distinguishing between the two, which may limit interpretability. Moreover, publication bias is possible, as studies with significant results are more likely to be reported. As a narrative review, this article provides a qualitative overview rather than a quantitative synthesis, and future systematic analyses are needed to strengthen the conclusions.

Conclusion

Viruses play a significant role in the initiation and progression of nasal inflammation, with major pathogens, including RV and RSV. HNECs act as a physical barrier through TJs and mount antiviral defenses by producing IFNs and cytokines. RV is detected at significantly higher rates in patients with CRS or AR. Chronic inflammation and allergic conditions impair normal ciliary function and weaken hNECs antiviral responses, increasing susceptibility.

RV infects cells by binding to receptors on the surface of hNECs, activating innate immune signaling pathways, including NF-κB, and induces pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines. These molecules recruit neutrophils, eosinophils, and monocytes to the infection site, triggering vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, mucosal edema, and enhanced secretions—typical inflammatory responses of rhinitis—clinically manifesting as nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, and sneezing. Viral infection activates NF-κB and NLRP3 inflammasome pathways in airway epithelial cells, inducing mucin overproduction and tissue remodeling, which may not only increase the frequency of acute exacerbations but may also contribute to disease chronicity and treatment resistance. Virus-induced immune memory, encompassing both trained innate immunity and tissue-resident adaptive memory, represents a novel mechanism linking repeated viral exposure to persistent nasal inflammation. By enhancing type 2 cytokine responses and disrupting epithelial homeostasis, this long-term reprogramming may underlie the chronicity and refractoriness of rhinitis, offering new insights for preventive and therapeutic strategies.

RV can trigger or exacerbate chronic upper airway diseases, including AR, CRS, NAR, and asthma. In susceptible individuals with impaired epithelial barrier function or pre-existing inflammation, RV infection can sustain mucosal immune activation, promote tissue remodeling, and drive disease chronicity. Viruses can also enhance bacterial colonization, such as Staphylococcus aureus, via upregulating ICAM-1 and altering the local immune environment, leading to viral–bacterial co-infections that prolong disease and worsen symptoms. A deeper understanding of RV and other respiratory viruses in upper airway diseases provides a rationale for targeted antiviral strategies. Antibody-based antivirals such as oseltamivir, zanamivir, and ribavirin block viral entry and replication [179], while inhibitors pro-inflammatory cytokines and inflammatory signaling pathways (such as IL-6, TNF-α, and NF-κB) offer immunomodulatory options [180]. Furthermore, virus-induced trained immunity, which promotes persistent Th2 inflammation, highlights potential therapeutic targets for chronic or refractory nasal inflammation.
