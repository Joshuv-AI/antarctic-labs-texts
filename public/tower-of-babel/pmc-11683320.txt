Efficacy of anal duct ligation and muscle closure: A novel sphincter‐preserving surgical technique for fistula‐in‐ano
Authors: Yoon Hyung Kang, Keehoon Hyun, Dong Ho Cho, Jong‐Kyun Lee, Do‐Yeon Hwang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimAlthough various sphincter‐preserving techniques exist for treating anal fistulas, none have demonstrated clear superiority. Therefore, the aim of this study was to introduce a novel sphincter‐preserving technique for anal duct ligation and muscle closure (ALMC) and analyse its perioperative outcomes.MethodThe data for patients who underwent ALMC for fistula‐in‐ano at Seoul Song Do Hospital between 2009 and 2023 were retrospectively reviewed. Patient demographics, intraoperative information and postoperative outcomes were assessed. The main outcomes were recurrence and wound healing. Recurrence was defined as the presence of a fistula tract or discharge more than 12 weeks after the primary surgery after achieving complete healing. Faecal incontinence was also investigated clinically.ResultsOverall, 556 patients (84.0% male; mean age 41.7 ± 12.3 years) underwent ALMC. Among these, 152 (27.3%) had a history of fistula surgery and 261 (46.9%) had suprasphincteric fistulas. Fistula‐in‐ano recurred in 33 patients (5.9%), wound healing was delayed in 97 (17.4%) and faecal incontinence was observed in 12 (2.2%). The mean follow‐up duration was 10.0 ± 16.0 months, and the average duration until recurrence was 13.8 ± 10.7 months. The proportion of suprasphincteric fistulas was similar in those who experienced recurrence and those who did not (57.6% vs. 46.3%, respectively; p = 0.239). The proportion of suprasphincteric fistulas in the delayed wound healing group was slightly higher, although the differences were not statistically significant (56.7% vs. 44.9%, respectively; p = 0.054).ConclusionALMC appeared to be a safe and feasible option for treating anal fistulas, providing good perioperative outcomes, particularly when sphincter preservation was crucial.Keywords: anal fistula, fistula‐in‐ano, sphincter‐saving procedure, surgical treatment

What does this paper add to the literature?

Numerous sphincter‐preserving techniques are used to treat anal fistulas, but none have definitively proven superiority. This study introduced a novel approach, anal duct ligation and muscle closure (ALMC), and investigated its perioperative outcomes. ALMC proved safe and feasible, with low recurrence and incontinence rates, even in patients with suprasphincteric fistulas.

INTRODUCTION

Fistula‐in‐ano is defined as an abnormal connection between the anorectal canal and perianal skin [1]. While antibiotics can address infective complications, surgical intervention is required to eliminate the fistula itself. Faecal continence relies on the intricate function of the anal sphincter within the anal canal. Surgical procedures can inadvertently cause injury to the anal sphincter muscle, leading to a continence defect. Thus, the main goal of fistula surgery is to prevent recurrence by eliminating the fistula and maintain faecal continence by minimizing damage to the anal sphincter muscle. This goal is more feasible for simple fistulas such as low intersphincteric or low transsphincteric types than for complex fistulas such as high transsphincteric, suprasphincteric or horseshoe types [2].

Various surgical techniques have been developed to prevent recurrence and faecal incontinence in patients with complex fistulas, such as ligation of the intersphincteric fistula tract (LIFT), endorectal advancement flap (ERAF), video‐assisted anal fistula treatment and the use of fistula plugs [3, 4, 5]. LIFT is being considered as a viable sphincter‐preserving surgical option. While favourable outcomes have been reported, it is important to note that LIFT involves ligation of the intersphincteric tract without removal of the anal gland [6, 7]. Thus, the risk of recurrence remains. Moreover, complex fistulas pose a heightened risk of procedural failure during LIFT [8].

Other studies of sphincter‐preserving techniques have reported unsatisfactory results. ERAF has fewer benefits than LIFT [9, 10]. The success of video‐assisted anal fistula treatment diminishes over time, and data on its long‐term outcomes are lacking [11]. Furthermore, although the fistula plug is considered a good option, its success rate varies considerably from 20% to 86% [12].

To overcome the limitations of the existing surgical techniques, the anal duct ligation and muscle closure (ALMC) procedure was developed at our institution and performed in patients with fistula‐in‐ano. ALMC is a surgical approach designed to ensure the complete removal of the anal gland while simultaneously preserving the anal sphincter. According to the cryptoglandular theory, fistulas originate from the anal gland. Therefore, the ALMC procedure is highly compatible with the pathophysiology of fistulas and is expected to reduce recurrence rates and minimize the incidence of faecal incontinence. The purpose of this study was to introduce a novel sphincter‐preserving technique for treatment of anal fistulas and investigate its efficacy by analysing its perioperative outcomes.

METHOD

Patient selection

Patients with fistula‐in‐ano who underwent ALMC at the Seoul Song Do Hospital between January 2009 and May 2023 were enrolled in this retrospective study. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul Songdo Hospital (IRB no. 2023–004).

ALMC was considered in patients with cryptoglandular fistulas when incontinence was expected after surgery, especially in complex fistulas, or when patients required sphincter‐preserving surgery. Patients with the following characteristics were excluded: tuberculosis diagnosed using interferon‐γ releasing assay or acid‐fast bacillus smear test within the fistula; underlying inflammatory bowel disease, including Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis; fistulas of noncryptoglandular origin, such as anovaginal fistula; duplicate data entries; and those with immunosuppression. Patients were also excluded if other procedures had been combined with other coexisting lesions during their treatment including the following: fistulotomy, fistulectomy, seton procedure, modified Hanley's procedure, reroute procedure for other fistula tracts; haemorrhoidectomy, rubber band ligation and injection of sclerosing agent for haemorrhoidal disease; lateral internal sphincterotomy and fissurectomy for fissure; or skin tag incision, rectocele repair, rectovaginal fistula repair, transanal excision for rectal polyp, and anal sphincteroplasty. Furthermore, patients were excluded if the surgeon performing their surgery had fewer than 10 cases of ALMC experience. Finally, five surgeons were included in the study.

Data collection

The following clinicopathological data were prospectively collected and recorded in an electronic medical database: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), comorbidity, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, symptoms of fistula, classification of fistula and location of the fistula tract. The perioperative outcomes included the total duration of surgery, complications (delayed wound healing, postoperative bleeding, itching, faecal incontinence and wound infection), recurrence of fistula‐in‐ano, days until recurrence, length of hospital stay and follow‐up duration.

Definitions

Each surgeon classified fistulas using the Parks classification, the most widely used classification system [13]. Delayed wound healing was defined as the persistence of discharge or the presence of granulation requiring surgery for resolution. Fistula recurrence was defined as the presence of a fistula tract or discharge following complete healing. Considering the regular healing period, a symptom observed 12 weeks or more after the surgery was defined as a recurrence. Faecal incontinence was defined as the inability to hold gas, liquid or solid stool.

Surgical procedure of ALMC

ALMC was performed with the patient in the jack‐knife position under spinal anaesthesia. ALMC consists of two main procedures: ligation of the anal duct and closure of the sphincter muscle.

First, saline mixed with methylene blue is injected through an external opening to identify the internal opening. Saline mixed with epinephrine is injected into the anal verge in the direction of the primary opening to sufficiently lift the submucosa from the internal sphincter. A semi‐circumferential skin incision is made at the site of the saline injection. The anoderm is dissected along the anterior surface of the internal sphincter in the submucosal plane to locate the anal duct, which is the uninflamed internal portion of the fistula tract that extends from the anal gland to the crypt orifice of the anal canal (Figure 1A). This process can be performed more precisely using loupes or a microscope. When the anal duct has been identified, it is fully exposed by further dissection using Mixter forceps. The anal duct is ligated close to the sphincter muscle using PDS 5‐0 (Figure 1B) and carefully excised (Figure 1C). Dissection is performed around the anal duct which penetrates through the internal sphincter, coring out towards the primary focus (Figure 1D). By splitting the internal sphincter muscle along the direction of muscle fibres, this approach minimizes the risk of sphincter muscle injury. The split internal sphincter muscle is intentionally widened to facilitate direct visualization to remove the anal gland completely (Figure 1E). After coring out the anal duct and gland, any remaining epithelialized tissue of the fistula tract is curetted if present. An incision is made around the external opening, which is dissected and cored out. In the case of complex fistulas, including horseshoe fistulas, it is important to accurately assess the tract using preoperative imaging if necessary. Fistulectomy is performed as deeply as possible while minimizing damage to the muscle, and curettage is conducted for select areas that are too deep on the inside. In this case, thorough curettage should be performed to ensure that all tracts are completely removed during surgery. When all the fistula tracts have been removed, saline is injected from the side of the external opening to check for complete passage. The incised sphincter is sutured with PDS 5‐0 using one or two stitches depending on the size (Figure 1F). Saline solution is injected at the site of the secondary opening to ensure closure of the sphincter muscle. Finally, the skin incision is closed using Vicryl 3‐0 sutures.

The procedure for anal duct ligation and muscle closure. (A) After a skin incision, the anoderm is dissected along the anterior surface of the internal sphincter in the submucosal plane and the anal duct is identified. (B), (C) The anal duct is ligated and excised. (D) The sphincter is incised around the anal duct and the muscle fibre is split. Further dissection is performed around the anal gland. (E) After coring out some extent of the anal duct and the primary focus, the fistula tract penetrating the anal sphincter is removed. (F) The sphincter muscle is closed with sutures and the skin incision is closed.

Preoperatively, when an abscess cavity is present, appropriate drainage is ensured through external drainage. Once it has been confirmed that all pus has drained, the external drainage is removed, followed by surgery 1 month later. Aside from this, no other procedures are performed, and ALMC is carried out directly. This principle was uniformly applied to all simple or complex fistulas.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v. 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Nominal data were compared using the chi‐square or Fisher's exact test, and continuous data were examined using Mann–Whitney U‐test. Continuous data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

A total of 912 patients underwent ALMC for fistula‐in‐ano, and 556 were included in this study (Figure 2). The patient demographics are listed in Table 1. The mean age was 41.7 ± 12.3 years and the mean BMI was 25.1 ± 3.5 kg/m2. A total of 467 patients (84.0%) were male, and 538 patients (96.8%) were classified as ASA class 1 or 2.

Preoperatively, 377 (67.8%) complained of discharge from the anus, 100 (18.0%) experienced pain and 91 (16.4%) experienced swelling in the perianal area. A total of 245 patients (44.1%) had transsphincteric fistulas and 261 (46.9%) had suprasphincteric fistulas. Fifty patients (9.0%) were classified as having the intersphincteric type, primarily due to their preference for sphincter‐preserving procedures, driven by concerns over potential sphincter damage.

Perioperative and follow‐up details

Of the 556 patients, 152 (27.3%) had previously experienced surgery for fistula‐in‐ano and 353 (63.5%) for incision and drainage of perianal abscess. Fibrin glue was used in 22 patients (4.0%) and platelet‐rich plasma was used in 13 (2.3%). The mean duration of surgery was 54.1 ± 22.1 min, and the mean follow‐up duration was 10.0 ± 16.0 months.

General surgical complications

The perioperative data were also assessed (Table 2). Twelve patients (2.2%) experienced faecal incontinence; however, in all 12 cases the symptom was mild and temporary. Other complications included postoperative bleeding (4.3%), itching (2.5%) and wound infections (0.5%). Bleeding was controlled by suturing, and antihistamines were prescribed to patients with itching after careful examination of the perianal skin. For cases of wound infection, antibiotics were administered, and the lesion was incised with drainage if there was an abscess cavity.

Recurrence

Anal fistulas recurred in 33 patients (5.9%), and the average time of recurrence was 13.8 ± 10.7 months (Table 3). The fistula type did not differ for patients who did not experience recurrence (p = 0.239). No patients in the intersphincteric fistula group experienced recurrence (0/50, 0%). Among 245 patients with transsphincteric fistulas, 14 (5.7%) experienced recurrence. Among 261 patients with suprasphincteric fistulas, 19 (7.3%) experienced recurrence. The number of patients who had previously undergone fistula surgery was significantly lower for the recurrence group than the nonrecurrence group (9.1% vs. 28.5%, respectively; p = 0.015). Furthermore, the follow‐up duration was significantly longer for the recurrence group (22.3 vs. 9.2 months, respectively; p < 0.001).

Surgical management was attempted for all patients with recurrent fistulas: fistulectomy with seton application for nine patients, modified Hanley procedure for six patients, ALMC for three patients, high seton application for two patients, muscle closure for two patients, fistulectomy with lay open and high seton application for one patient, and reroute procedure for one patient. Seven patients were lost to follow‐up after fistula recurrence.

Delayed wound healing

Ninety‐seven (17.4%) patients experienced delayed wound healing (Table 4). The fistula types of the patients whose wounds healed initially did not differ (p = 0.054). Eleven patients (22.0%) in the intersphincteric fistula group experienced delayed wound healing. Among 245 patients with transsphincteric fistulas, 31 (12.7%) experienced delayed wound healing. Furthermore, among 261 patients with suprasphincteric fistulas, 55 (21.2%) experienced delayed wound healing. The BMI of the delayed wound healing group was significantly higher than that of the nondelayed group (25.9 vs. 24.9 kg/m2, respectively; p = 0.009). However, the delayed wound healing group spent fewer days in the hospital than the nondelayed group (2.4 vs. 2.9 days; respectively, p < 0.001). Furthermore, the follow‐up duration was significantly longer for the delayed wound healing group (13.5 vs. 9.3 months, respectively; p < 0.001).

DISCUSSION

Despite extensive research on sphincter‐preserving techniques, none of the approaches have demonstrated superior efficacy, especially for complex fistulas [3, 4]. In our research, we found that ALMC proved to be a safe and effective approach, with relatively low rates of complications, recurrence and delayed wound healing, even for patients with suprasphincteric fistulas. Additionally, it demonstrated a low recurrence rate when used in patients who had undergone prior fistula surgery.

During ALMC, the internal opening of a cryptoglandular fistula is typically located near the dentate line, making it easier to identify the anal duct via submucosal dissection compared with that during alternative methods. For example, in cases such as posterior fistulas, where the anal gland can reside in a deep posterior space, the LIFT method requires dissection into the deep area of the intersphincteric space, making identification of the anal duct challenging. Conversely, confirming the area for dissection and ligation of the anal duct was more straightforward with ALMC.

The procedure involves delicate dissection of the submucosal layer to ligate the anal duct. The advantage of this technique lies in its ability to minimize injury within the anal canal, contributing to preservation of the anatomical integrity of the anal canal. Moreover, the convenience of transitioning to other surgical techniques renders ALMC less burdensome. Despite complete duct excision, ALMC remains the simplest surgical technique to perform.

Recent studies have explored surgical techniques involving submucosal layer dissection and anal duct ligation, like the ALMC procedure. Ram et al. reported a procedure known as submucosal ligation of the fistula tract, which involves dissection of the submucosal layer, ligation of the anal duct with proximal and distal suture ties, transection of the fistula tract and curettage of the distal component [14]. Sahara et al. introduced a surgical technique called subcutaneous incision of the fistula tract and internal sphincterotomy (SIFT‐IS), which involves approaching the submucosal layer, dissecting the fistula and fibrous tissue, and cutting the internal sphincter to flatten the anal canal [15]. The main difference from ALMC is that submucosal ligation of the fistula tract retains a portion of the septic nidus in the intersphincteric space.

In this study, the recurrence rate was 5.9%, and the rate of delayed wound healing was 17.4% when ALMC was used for patients with fistula‐in‐ano. LIFT is the preferred sphincter‐preserving procedure and, in a recent meta‐analysis, Emile et al. reported a mean success rate of 76.5% for LIFT [16]. However, recent randomized controlled trials of LIFT for complex fistulas have reported rates of recurrence of 9.3%–32.5% [9, 17, 18]. Compared with this, the outcome of ALMC is considered favourable, and this is probably attributable to the thorough removal of the infective focus. Furthermore, the incontinence rate in our study was 2.2%, comparable with the faecal incontinence rates of 0%–1.4% found in previous studies focussing on LIFT [9, 17, 18]. Minimal manipulation of the sphincter muscle in the ALMC technique results in minimal damage to the sphincter muscle, leading to its functional preservation.

The effect of protecting the mucosa in ALMC can be evaluated by comparing it with that of ERAF. In a systematic review and meta‐analysis that included 30 studies, Stellingwerf et al. reported the rate of incontinence as 7.8% for ERAF, which is relatively higher than that of ALMC as found in this study (2.2%), suggesting that ALMC is more effective at reducing this complication [19]. In terms of the healing rate, the overall success rate was reported to be 74.6% for ERAF [10, 19]. Thus, the benefit of protecting the mucosa can be considered meaningful.

Additionally, the recurrence and wound healing rates of patients who had undergone previous fistula surgery were favourable compared with those of the patients who underwent ALMC. Therefore, the ALMC technique can be considered a sphincter‐preserving approach worth attempting for patients who have previously undergone fistula surgery.

While other studies have primarily focused on transsphincteric fistulas, our study included a patient group with a high proportion of suprasphincteric fistulas. In cases of suprasphincteric fistulas where the tract extends upward into the intersphincteric area, the LIFT procedure is not feasible due to the inability to ligate the duct within the intersphincteric space. However, ALMC can be performed in such cases as it allows for the removal of the remaining tract after ligating the anal duct in the submucosal plane. This study has also verified the favourable outcomes associated with this approach, supported by a significantly larger number of participants compared with recent studies.

However, precise manoeuvres are crucial for achieving these outcomes. Therefore, care must be taken to prevent mucosal injuries. Due to the thinness of the duct, it is essential to identify and dissect it carefully, without inadvertently cutting it. The appropriate use of PDS is crucial to ensure secure muscle closure. To help mitigate these risks, our institution employs loupes and a microscope during surgery. In addition, there is a slight possibility of mucosal injury during surgery. Minor injuries can be resolved by suturing. However, for cases with significant damage, a transition to another surgical approach may be necessary. Nevertheless, the ease of transitioning to an alternative procedure does not adversely affect the surgical process. If coverage of the mucosa is needed, ERAF can be performed. In cases where an extensive lesion necessitates aggressive surgery and results in mucosal damage, the fact that an attempt at ALMC was made does not influence the decision to proceed with the modified Hanley procedure.

This study has some limitations. First, it is a retrospective study which has inherent bias. However, to minimize bias, we prospectively collected data from patients and a large number of patients were included. Second, there was a discrepancy in the perioperative care by the operators regarding bowel preparation, period of nil per oral after surgery, length of hospital stay, use of rectal tubes and injection of fibrin glue or platelet‐rich plasma. However, it has not been clearly demonstrated that the aforementioned factors have a significant impact on surgical outcomes. Third, the lack of some information on the preoperative or postoperative Wexner incontinence scores resulted in an overall data inconsistency; therefore, they were not incorporated into the comprehensive analysis for the assessment of faecal incontinence. Thus, the analysis of faecal incontinence is limited from a clinical perspective. Fourth, the mean follow‐up period was shorter than mean time to recurrence; hence, the rate of recurrence might have been underestimated. Moreover, patient factors, such as postoperative pain and quality of life, were not evaluated. Given that patients are beginning to play a more active role in decision‐making these factors should be considered. Further studies using the same perioperative protocol with objective tools in a prospective design to evaluate the precise outcome are required.

In conclusion, considering the low rates of recurrence and incontinence, ALMC is a safe and feasible technique even for patients with suprasphincteric fistulas. It can also be safely applied to patients who have already undergone fistula surgery, with a low rate of recurrence. Further research should be conducted with a prospective design, using standardized surgical methods and perioperative management, to evaluate the precise benefits of this technique.
