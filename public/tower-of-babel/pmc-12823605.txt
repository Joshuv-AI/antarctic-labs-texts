An efficient transformer architecture with depthwise separable convolutions for high-accuracy underwater acoustic target recognition
Authors: Nahid-Al Mahmud, Tao Zhang, Yasir Iqbal, Farhana Bari Sumona, Ikram Azaz, Yanzhang Geng, Wajid Khan, Qasem M Kharma, Oleksandr Rubanenko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderwater Acoustic Target Recognition (UATR) plays a vital role in maritime security and defense, requiring accurate and efficient classification of marine vessels based on their sonar acoustic emissions. Traditional recognition systems rely on handcrafted features and shallow classifiers, which often struggle with complex acoustic patterns and impose substantial computational overhead. Although deep learning methods have improved recognition accuracy, their high computational demands hinder real-time deployment on resource-constrained platforms. We propose the Depthwise Separable Convolutional Multihead Transformer (DCMT), which combines depthwise separable convolutions for localized feature extraction with multi-head self-attention Transformer branches for global contextual modeling. The model has dual Transformer’s parallel branches having 4-head and 8-head structures for additional complementary feature processing, and their outputs are merged using Global Average and Max Pooling to form a more potent feature vector. The model incorporates the following acoustic features: Zero Crossing Rate (ZCR), Root Mean Square-Energy (RMS-Energy), Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs), and Chroma. To enhance generalization of DCMT model, CutMix data augmentation is used to synthetically increase data variability through combining audio segments from different classes. The proposed DCMT model with 0.7 million parameters was evaluated on the public benchmark datasets DeepShip and ShipsEar achieving 0.847 Flops (G) and 0.94 Flops (G), respectively, and it reached a classification accuracy of 97.53% and 98.19%. The model also attained even better classification accuracy on the QiandaoEar22 datasets (SpeedBoat, KaiYuan, and UUV) which are 95.08%, 98.24% and 99.68%, respectively. Moreover, the proposed DCMT model achieves an average inference time of 3.8 ms and 131.6 FPS, which outperform existing models and the baseline UTAR-Transformer (4.3 ms and 230.8 FPS) in both speed and accuracy. This lightweight model exhibits strong potential for real-time deployment in marine environments.Keywords: Acoustic signal processing, Depthwise separable convolution, Feature extraction, Transformer architecture, Underwater acoustic signals, Underwater target recognitionSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Ocean sciences

Introduction

The categorization of marine targets using sonar acoustic data improves maritime security and defense1. Typical approaches depend on manually curated representations of vessel noise and noise feature extraction. In these approaches, learned classifiers of low complexity will refine feature selection and make use of limited non-linear classification abilities2–4. The traditional acoustic representations, such as the waveform5, spectral6, and wavelet7 representations, which are manually designed and expertly designed, are not flexible when it comes to modification in response to new situations. Traditional classifiers, including Support Vector Machines (SVMs)8 and shallow neural networks9 cannot be effectively used to model high-complexity and large-scale data, and cannot generalize well. The extraction of the features and design of the classifier are carried out independently, which may lead to the incompatibility of the feature representations with the classification task. According to the recent literature, attempts to classify audio signals tend to underperform due to a lack of perceptively relevant filter banks10,11. In12 authors developed a speech emotion recognition system which successfully detects the audio signals of different classes. Emotions were recognized in both the Metaverse and the real world using an octonion-based nonlinear echo state network (ONESN) to improve speech emotion recognition (SER) in high-dimensional data scenarios13. Deep neural designs, including CNNs and RNNs are typically used to create a complete end-to-end recognition framework in modern studies14,15. Cao et al.16 designed a single deep learning architecture that combines convolutional neural networks and second-order statistical pooling to derive temporal relationship among radiated noise pattern spectrogram visualization. The technique designed by Hu et al.17 employs depth wise separable and dilated convolutional architectures to deal with raw time-series acoustic data to complete underwater target classification tasks. Zhong et al.18 tested four different convolutional neural network designs to process time frequency spectrograms, and the purpose of this research was to describe acoustic signatures of the beluga whale. The algorithm described in the paper by Luo et al.19 adopts a deep learning framework with ResNet, where several STFT spectrograms are fed through it in order to identify the vessel types.

Zhou et al.20 used deep convolutional neural networks to complete the two tasks simultaneously noise suppression and acoustic features extraction; further classification was done using random-forest algorithms. The UATR field started to use RNN-based approaches extensively, in part, due to their ability to capture temporal patterns in sequential acoustic signals21. In22 Ibrahim et al. study a technique of using deep learning to recognize various species of grouper by analyzing recordings of their sounds. The training of RNNs is particularly difficult because of the unfocused temporal changes of the backpropagated gradients that often times tend to disappear or overtake the computing machine during long sequences23. Most contemporary applications, for enhanced target recognition networks, use advanced RNN architectures such as GRUs24, LSTMs25, and bidirectional LSTMs. The novel design of the Transformer is the first neural network to use attention mechanisms26, and do away entirely with any form of recurrent or convolutional steps27. The Transformer framework is one of the most powerful deep learning architectures and has achieved state-of -the art performance deep learning in the multiple domains of natural language processing28 and visual recognition29 and audio processing30. Exploiting the Transformer neural architecture for the classification of acoustic signals with AST31, Sight and Sound have excelled in Environmental Sound Recognition and Speech Recognition tasks. This work presents the first application of the Transformer methodology to underwater acoustic analysis through the creation of HUATrans, a new target classification model. Swin Transformer32 is an efficient attention mechanism architecture that functions as the basic building block for computation.

Acquiring data for Underwater Acoustic Target Recognition presents difficulties, designing recognition systems for small-scale datasets continues to be an important problem for research. In 2022, Li et al.33 were the first to apply Transformer architecture to UATR, achieving an impressive 13.7% and 50% increase in accuracy against traditional CNN approaches on two popular datasets. Transfer learning involves obtaining transferable representations from a source domain and applying them to a different but related target domain to enhance learning. Incorporating knowledge transfer alleviates problems with data scarcity during the model training process34,35. The current Transformer models for UATR suffer high computational complexity. When dealing with large, high-dimensional acoustic signals, the self-attention mechanism requires quadratic computational resources, which makes real-time implementation on resource-constrained platforms a continual problem36,37. Although Feng et al.38 created a purely attention-based Transformer model that employs progressive token embedding to capture hierarchical features and achieved accuracy that rivals the best convolutional neural networks, their method is still limited in two important ways: it does not eliminate the computational load provided by the standard self-attention and it uses only one transformer pathway which limits representational variation. In response to these obstacles, depthwise separable convolutions have emerged as a viable alternative. By applying depthwise and pointwise convolutions in a sequentially, these convolutions reduce both decreases the number of model parameters and the amount of computational resources used to an impressive level39,40. Nonetheless, the transformer frameworks currently available for UATR have not fully integrated these effective convolution techniques. Such techniques can significantly reduce computational costs while enabling the simultaneous learning of regional and global interdependencies.

In order to overcome this drawback, this paper introduces the Depthwise Separable Convolutional Multi-Head Transformer (DCMT) model for Underwater Acoustic Target Recognition (UATR). This new architecture integrates depthwise separable convolutions with the multihead attention mechanism used in Transformers. This combination improves computational efficiency while preserving the ability to extract both local features and global representations. Completing its feature learning capability, the model employs two parallel branches with varying attention head configurations (4-head and 8-head). This allows the model to extract features from fine-grained to contextual broad scope. To build a comprehensive feature vector for classification, the outputs from the two branches are combined through fusion and it is achieved by integrating Global Average and Max Pooling operations to corresponding transformer branch. In the initial stage, the model separates and extracts relevant acoustic features from the raw signal data. This involves the calculation of the different signal processing parameters, namely the Zero Crossing Rate (ZCR), Root Mean Square-Energy (RMS-Energy), Mel-frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs), and Chroma features. Temporal and spectral characteristics essential for the identifying underwater targets are captured and represented effectively.

Feature extraction

Once the audio samples have been preprocessed, the subsequent task is to extract features that encapsulate the fundamental attributes of the underwater acoustic signals. These characteristics help differentiate among various underwater signals targets. The objective of the features presented is to capture the characteristics of the signal in the temporal and the spectral domains. In proposed method following features are extracted to train the DCMT model:

Zero crossing rate (ZCR)

The Zero Crossing Rate (ZCR) indicates how many times the value of a signal shifts from positive to negative (or negative to positive) over a given time interval. The ZCR is a basic estimator of the main frequency component of a time-domain signal. Identifying instances of zero crossings is necessary for measuring the frequency characteristics of a signal and the stability of a signal, which contributes greatly to the improvement of target discrimination. Root mean square-energy (RMS-energy)

The RMS-Energy represents the average size of a signal. It is computed by squaring each value of the signal, averaging the squared values, and then taking the square root of that average. The resulting value is always positive, reflecting the strength or power of the signal.

These characteristics help assess the fundamental magnitude of a signal, which is essential for distinguishing between different acoustic environments. RMS-Energy allows us to compute 2 additional features including mean of RMS-Energy and standard deviation of RMS-Energy ().

Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCC)

In audio processing, the spectral characteristics of a signal are commonly represented using MFCCs. The computation of these coefficients seeks to mirror the various frequencies to the subjective perception of the human ear. MFCCs are derived by applying a Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) to the log-Mel spectrogram.

This characteristic possesses strong spectral features in a manner that remains unchanged despite variations in the signal. MFCC feature is particularly useful for distinguishing between different target sounds. This is especially important in our approach, as it enables effective separation of different target sounds. It leverages human like auditory perception to detect sublet spectral variations that are essential for underwater acoustic target recognition. From MFCC, we compute 26 features including 13 MFCC coefficients, each with mean and standard deviation and total of 26 features.

Experimental settings

Data collection

In this study, we have used two open-access datasets: DeepShip41, ShipsEar42 and QiandaoEar2243. ShipsEar publicly accessible underwater ship noise data set is a collection of underwater ship noise records captured over a total of about 3 h. This data set has 90 recordings that consists of eleven ship types and single types of environmental noise. For the purpose of analysis, the recordings were broken down into 5 s audio segments. There was no overlap between consecutive segments in the construction of these fragments. Based on this, we produced data with a total of 1440 clips. For the purposes of maintaining equilibrium for our analysis, all of our recordings were reorganized into five categories according to the varying sizes of the vessels. The revised classification can be found in Table 1. A random partitioning of the dataset was performed, allocating 70% for training, while 30% was reserved for validation purposes. DeepShip deploys real-world depth measurement data to provide underwater acoustics classification. The dataset consists of recordings of 265 distinct vessels grouped into four different categories, amounting to 47 h and 4 min of data. As part of this research, the data was divided into segments of 5 s for analysis. This was done without any repeating segments to ensure there was no overlap. As a result, a dataset of 10,000 audio clips was created.

Training setup

The model trained in this paper is the Depthwise Separable Convolution Multihead Transformer (DCMT) for underwater acoustic target recognition. This model is employed in underwater acoustic target recognition operation. The initial learning rate for the Adam optimizer was set to 0.001. The learning rate during training is adjusted via exponential decay. Specifically, decay rates of 0.9 and 0.999 are applied to the first and second moment estimates, respectively. The Adam optimizer was employed for model optimization, with an initial leaning rate set to 0.001. The optimizer’s exponential decay rates were set to 0.9 for the first moment estimate and 0.999 for the second. Training was conducted over 30 epochs using a batch size of 64 and the categorical cross-entropy loss. The network undergoing this optimization contained a total of 0.7 million parameters. The architecture integrates depthwise separable convolution layers to efficiently find features with multihead transformer blocks able to find complicated dependencies in the data. This model fusion enhances the model’s ability to respond more frequently to complex acoustic signals. The model is trained on a high-performance system with two NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 Ti GPUs (CUDA Version 12.2, Driver version 535.171.04). This configuration ensured fast and efficient training.

Using the ablation study shown in Table 5, demonstrates the significance of each component in the DCMT model for underwater acoustic target recognition. This means convolution layers alone are not enough toestimate the target recognition precisely. When a 4-head transformer and Global Average Pooling (GAP) are added to Branch 1, the accuracy increases to 80.61%. This shows the transformer improves feature extraction and GAP retains more global features. To put it simply, the performance will still be limited since the second transformer branch and Global Max Pooling (GMP) have not been put to use yet. Using an 8-head transformer in Branch 2 with GMP (and without the 4-head transformer in Branch 1) improves accuracy to 83.30%. This shows that the 8-head transformer along with GMP assists in focusing discriminative features, but without the complete transformer setup in both branches, it remains inadequate.

Conclusion

In this paper, we present the Depthwise Separable Convolutional Multihead Transformer (DCMT) specifically tailored for Underwater Acoustic Target Recognition (UATR). The model incorporates depthwise separable convolutions with dual parallel multi-head self-attention Transformer branches (configured with 4-head and 8-head branches) to efficiently extract local features and, at the same time, capture fine-grained and broad contextual information. The robust feature vector for classification is built by fusing the outputs of the two transformer branches using Global Average and Max Pooling.

DCMT model using 0.7 million trainable parameters, showed state-of-the-art classification performance with an accuracy of 97.53% on the DeepShip dataset and 98.19% on the ShipsEar dataset. It also exhibited better performance on the QiandaoEar22 datasets, with UTAR classification accuracy of 95.08%, 98.24%, and 99.68% using SpeedBoat, KaiYuan, and UUV, respectively. Computationally, the DCMT model achieves 0.847 Flops (G) on DeepShip and 0.94 Flops (G) on ShipsEar, with an average inference time of 3.8 ms and 131.6 FPS on each dataset, faster and more efficient than the UTAR-Transformer baseline (4.3 ms and 230.8 FPS). Also, the model has benefit of CutMix-based data augmentation, which improves the generalization of the model by artificially augmenting its data variation by combining audio segments across multiple classes. Although the model has achieved significant accuracy and efficiency, it has some limitations with its current design. Although the model has achieved significant accuracy and efficiency, it has some limitations with its current design. The computational overhead of the self-attention mechanism requires further optimizations, especially in real-time embedded systems. Moreover, it is still necessary to test the generalizing capabilities of the model in more complicated and dynamic underwater conditions.

Future directions include the need to improve the self-attention mechanism to enhance efficiency, investigate adaptive or learned feature extraction methods, and apply cross-domain transfer learning to make the model more applicable in a wide range of underwater conditions.
