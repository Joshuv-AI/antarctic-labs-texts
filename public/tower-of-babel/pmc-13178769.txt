Improving the Health of Governance: Identifying and Addressing Burnout Among Public Health Association Board Members
Authors: Caroline Stampfel, Joe Bohn, Russell S. Kirby
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe COVID-19 pandemic caused stress and burnout for governmental public health workers, negatively affecting workers' desire to remain in their professional role. In October 2022, a board for a national association assessed their professional development needs, identified manifestations of burnout, and generated strategies to address burnout. Of 21 board members invited, 16 (76%) responded. Quantitative items were summarized using descriptive analysis. Open-ended responses were summarized with thematic analysis. Board members identified balance between private and professional lives to optimize well-being as a competency growth area. They selected group discussion (56%) as their top learning method. Burnout manifested as frustration, fatigue, disengagement, poor decision making, feeling overwhelmed, and illness. Six categories described strategies to address burnout: positive self-talk, protecting time, modeling, creativity/fun, “get-out-there,” and resources/support. To address burnout and support public health workers, associations can prioritize the development and well-being of board members who might not otherwise prioritize their development.

Introduction and Background

Governmental public health workers experienced the stress of working through a global pandemic as part of their daily life for the past 3 years, and it negatively affected their desire to remain in their professional role. Rapidly deployed surveys in 2021 revealed that public health workers experienced mental health challenges, including depression, anxiety, posttraumatic stress disorder, and suicidal ideation.1 Fifty-two percent of those surveyed reported at least one mental health symptom in the preceding 2 weeks, and 19.6% needed but did not receive mental health services.1 The public health workforce engaged in sustained COVID-19 response activities, but their commitment took a toll; workers unable to take time off were almost twice as likely to report a mental health condition as those who could take time off.1 Furthermore, the Public Health Workforce Interests and Needs Survey (PH WINS) reported 16% of the workforce and 41% of executives felt “bullied, threatened, or harassed by individuals outside the health department.”2 Consequently, nearly a third of the workforce considered leaving their organization within a year.2,3 New 2024 PH WINS data affirm that 71% of public health workers experience at least one burnout symptom.4

With burnout resulting from the COVID-19 response and high rates of turnover, public health workers urgently need support for their professional development.5 A study comparing pre-COVID training needs to those identified during the pandemic found, in addition to existing skill needs, public health workers now needed effective communication skills.5 Providing tailored professional development may address both burnout and turnover, particularly if opportunities offer pathways for advancement.3 Public health associations are poised to address these professional development needs. Associations formed by public health workers represent their interests regionally or nationally, advocate for policy change, and assist the workforce. Boards consist of volunteer members who govern professional associations, serving the organization while maintaining full-time employment. Board service should include professional development to increase board members' individual and collective effectiveness.6 However, prioritizing this was more difficult during the pandemic, when boards could only meet briefly and virtually to accommodate members' roles in pandemic response.7,8

This study describes a project that assessed board members' professional development and well-being after the height of the pandemic. The study aims to identify urgent professional development needs for board members and increase knowledge of how to reduce burnout to improve board member well-being.

Methods

This project was a professional development needs assessment for volunteer board members, with descriptive analysis of the cross-sectional assessment, followed by a training experience. The participants were members of the board of directors of a national public health association, comprising up to 19 members elected by the membership, representing state or territorial governmental public health departments or affiliated family organizations. Using a census approach, we invited all current board members (N = 17) and board members who concluded their service or who joined the board in 2022 (N = 4) to participate through email. Of the 21 invitations, 16 responses were received, for a 76% response rate. One reminder was sent to nonrespondents. Demographics included age, self-reported race/ethnicity, gender identity, and position held.

The assessment used existing frameworks including the Public Health Core Competencies,9,10 the Maternal and Child Health (MCH) Leadership Competencies,11 and a nonprofit board self-assessment.12 Five current or former board members piloted the assessment for cognitive testing. Using pilot tester feedback, we revised wording and question order, eliminated questions that did not elicit useful information or were not perceived as valuable by pilot testers, and added questions pilot testers felt were highly relevant.

The association's leadership team emailed the SurveyMonkey-hosted assessment to eligible participants in October 2022, with the survey open for 4 weeks. The assessment included individual self-reflection, strategic skills ratings for public health leadership, and roles and responsibilities as volunteer board members. Question types included rankings, open-ended short answers, Likert-type, and yes/no questions. The strategic skills scale incorporated priorities and learning needs not now (low priority), need refreshing soon (moderate priority to refresh), need in-depth development (moderate priority to learn), and need help now (high priority). Board members participated in a professional development activity that addressed the most relevant professional development need, burnout, with a free list activity to generate strategies in response to the prompt, “In 5 minutes, write down as many ideas as you have to help your board peers address burnout.” Board members were then asked to asterisk their 2 favorite ideas and share those with the large group, creating the group list.

While no compensation was provided for participation, as an incentive, board members received a learning plan tailored to needs identified in the analysis. No protected health information was collected. The intended use of the assessment was to improve the association's support provided to its board; therefore, institutional review board approval was not pursued.

Quantitative items were summarized using descriptive analysis in Excel to show the percentage of board members who selected each category. Strategic skills and core competencies were sorted and summarized by the top choice for priority and urgency for each competency. Open-ended question responses and free list data were synthesized using thematic analysis in Microsoft Word. The experience was evaluated with a survey assessing knowledge gain, satisfaction, relevance, and applicability rated on a Likert-style scale from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Evaluations were summarized using percents.

Results

Sixty-nine percent of respondents were between 41 and 60 years of age (69%), 63% selected their race/ethnicity as White, non-Hispanic or Latino and 25% selected Black/African American, non-Hispanic or Latino, and 75% of respondents indicated female sex. All board members were employed at time of assessment, with 81% in high-level public health leadership positions and 19% family leaders. Board members identified 5 MCH Leadership Competencies in which they wanted to grow, including a self-reflection competency about striving for balance between private and professional lives to optimize well-being (44%). Their top preferred learning method was engaging in group discussion/sharing/interactivity (56%). The most urgent priorities among the Strategic Skills for Public Health Professionals were in the categories of justice, equity, diversity, inclusion (44% selected “need help now”); change management (31%); systems and strategic thinking (25%); and policy engagement (38%). The core competencies within the strategic skills are displayed by how they were ranked by respondents (See Supplemental Digital Content, Table 2, available at http://links.lww.com/JPHMP/B657).

Burnout manifested for board members as frustration, fatigue, disengagement, poor decision-making, feeling overwhelmed, and illness. Board members indicated needing dedicated time away from work, training and resources for recognizing and supporting staff when burnout happens, and support to focus on what is important. The in-person training activity, which used the group's learning preference for discussion and interactivity, yielded strategies to address burnout in 6 themes (See Table 1). Themes included positive self-talk, focusing on encouragement; protecting time, including setting boundaries for work and personal life; modeling, focusing on demonstrating healthy work-life balance behaviors for staff; creativity and fun, including reconnecting to the human side of public health; get out there, focusing on physical ways to incorporate wellness and connection; and resources and support, including ideas for strategies outside usual workplace supports. All board members who responded to the training experience evaluation agreed or strongly agreed they will be able to apply one or more of the concepts discussed during the experience.

Discussion

This group of public health association board members prioritized burnout as a key professional well-being need distinct from competency-based strategic skills, in line with the broader experience of public health professionals responding to the COVID-19 pandemic since 2020.1,13,14 Burnout manifested in ways that affected board members' health and well-being and their ability to be fully effective in their roles. The strategies brainstormed spanned self-care, supervision, and reconnecting to why they work in public health, all of which can have positive impacts on well-being. The association has prioritized support for implementing these strategies during board meetings and as part of association-provided professional development.

The 28% of state health agency public health workers who intend to leave within the next 5 years represent an upward trend from 22% in 2014.15 Burnout as a reason for leaving is an even starker increasing trend, up from 23% in 2014 to 41% in 2021.15 The literature on public health burnout focuses on quantifying experiences and impacts, including post-traumatic stress disorder symptoms and poor mental health, but has focused less on how to address burnout.1,3,5 Addressing burnout for state health agency workers may help stem the tide of departures, particularly if strategies reinforce the top reasons for staying in state health agency jobs, such as benefits, satisfaction with supervisor, and flexibility.3 Results from this project offer insight into the role associations can play in supporting their board members and their broader membership in exploring peer-generated strategies. Associations can dedicate time during board meetings for professional well-being and incorporate burnout-reduction strategies into national advocacy work.

One limitation of this project was that although this project addressed burnout, it does not address satisfaction with pay, the top reason state health agency workers leave their organization.15 Another limitation is this project assessed board member needs from one association; therefore, these data are not generalizable to other associations. However, the needs identified resonated with board members, indicating the relevance of such activities. Adaptations by other associations are supported through inclusion of the assessment (See Supplemental Digital Content, Table 3, available at http://links.lww.com/JPHMP/B658) as well as suggested demographic stratifiers, such as board tenure and public health position type.

Conclusion

Public health workers are experiencing burnout at higher rates than prepandemic, but little progress has been made toward addressing burnout that also promotes worker retention. This project highlights pathways to address burnout through peer-identified strategies in the context of a public health association board. The assessment (See Supplemental Digital Content, Table 3, available at http://links.lww.com/JPHMP/B658) and professional development activity can be adapted for other associations and can be facilitated virtually. Associations can prioritize professional development and well-being for board volunteers who, as leaders, are typically prioritizing the development of their teams and staff.Implications for Policy & Practice■ Public health workers experienced tremendous stressors in responding to the COVID-19 pandemic leading to professional development needs that public health associations may be instrumental in addressing, including burnout and professional well-being.■ Public health association boards should be supported by their associations to address their most pressing needs, including burnout for themselves and their teams.■ Associations can support all their members by prioritizing support for professional development, implementing activities to improve professional well-being, and incorporating strategies to address burnout into their national advocacy work.
