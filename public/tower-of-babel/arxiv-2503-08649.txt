1.
Introduction

The study of partial differential equations plays a fundamental role in mathematical analysis, with applications ranging from physics to engineering, and has profound implications in understanding the regularity of solutions in various settings.

In this work, we focus on a particular class of Poisson-type problems that involve a degenerate/singular elliptic operator. In many cases, these problems exhibit degeneracy at the boundary, where the behaviour of the solution becomes less regular or singular.

We consider the weighted operator, denoted by
L_{\beta}
, defined as

(1.1)

L_{\beta}(u):=-{\rm div}(d^{\beta}\nabla u).

where the weights
w(x)=d^{\beta}(x)
with
\beta<1
are powers of the distance function

d(x)=dist(x,\partial\Omega),

and

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a bounded domain with
\partial\Omega\in C^{2}
.

We are interested principally in the regularity but mainly in the precise behaviour at the boundary of
classical
solutions of the following Poisson-type problem

(1.2)

\begin{cases}L_{\beta}(u)=f&{\rm in}\ \Omega\\
u=0&{\rm on}\ \partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
f
is a given function that acts as a source term within the domain
\Omega
.

Although the operator is degenerate, if
f\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Omega)
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the classical local Schauder estimates give at least that
u\in C^{2}(\Omega)
, since
L_{\beta}
is uniformly elliptic in every compact set
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\Omega
.

The weight introduces a degeneracy into the operator, which affects the behaviour of the solutions, that in general become less regular.

We aim to refine the understanding of the regularity of solutions to (
1.2
), particularly near the boundary of
\Omega
.

Since the operator is in divergence form, it is natural to also consider the problem from a variational point of view, addressing both the exitence and the possible regularity of the weak solutions of (
1.2
). To this aim it is necessary to introduce the natural (weighted) Sobolev space in which to frame the problem.

More generally, for
w\in L_{loc}^{1}(\Omega)
,
w(x)\geq 0\ a.e.
, we denote with

L^{p}(\Omega,w)
the weighted Lebesgue space of measurable functions
u
on
\Omega
with finite norm

(1.3)

||u||_{L^{p}(\Omega,w)}=\left(\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{p}w(x)\ dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

and with
W^{k,p}(\Omega,w)
the weighted Sobolev space of all measurable functions
u
whose
distributional derivatives belongs to
L^{p}(\Omega,w)
, i.e. for which the norm

(1.4)

||u||_{W^{k,p}(\Omega,w)}=\left(\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}||D^{\alpha}u||^{p}_{L^{p}(\Omega,w)}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

is finite.

It is well-established (see e.g
[
7
]
) that if
w^{-\frac{1}{p}}\in L^{p^{\prime}}_{loc}(\Omega)
, then
W^{k,p}(\Omega,w)
are Banach spaces, and
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)\subset W^{k,p}(\Omega,w)
.
Therefore, one can also define the space

W_{0}^{k,p}(\Omega,w)=\overline{C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{||\cdot||_{W^{k,p}(\Omega,w)}}

as the closure of
C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
with respect to the norm defined in (
1.4
).

An interesting reference for weighted Sobolev spaces and related inequalities is the work by Edmunds & Opic, (
[
4
]
, see also the work of Kufner and Opic
[
7
]
),
which
focuses on the weighted Poincaré inequality
treating in details
the special case of weights
w
which are powers of the distance function
d(x).

It will be exactly this work that we will refer to in order to discuss some preliminary results.

We say that
u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega,d^{\beta})
is a weak solution to (
1.2
), if

(1.5)

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\nabla\phi\ d^{\beta}dx=\int_{\Omega}f\phi\ dx,\quad\forall\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega).

It is standard to prove that, if
f/d^{\beta}\in L^{2}(\Omega,d^{\beta})
, then the problem (
1.2
) admits a weak solution
u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega,d^{\beta})
(see Section 2 for more details).

The present work is partially inspired by the celebrated paper of
Fabes, Koenig and Serapioni (
[
5
]
),
which investigates the local regularity properties of solutions to degenerate elliptic partial differential equations.
Specifically, it focuses on the behaviour of solutions in regions where the ellipticity condition of the equation may fail or degenerate. The authors develop and apply methods to demonstrate that, under certain conditions, solutions to these equations possess local regularity.

Their results involve more general weights that belong to the Muckenhoupt classes
A_{p}
(see e.g.
[
8
]
), i.e. weights
w
defined as follows: for
\ p>1
,
w\in A_{p}
if

\sup_{B\subset\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{|B|}{\int_{B}w\ dx}\right)\left(\frac{1}{|B|}{\int_{B}w^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}}dx\right)^{p-1}<+\infty.

(For example the weight
w=d^{\beta}\in A_{2}
when
\beta\in(-1,1)
, see Section
2
).

In
[
5
]
the authors proved some regularity result about weak solutions, including
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
regularity and Hölder regularity, under specific assumptions on
f
.

Among these results, we highlight the one most relevant to our discussion, and we state it in the case
w=d^{\beta}
, although it holds for more general weights as well:

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem 2.4.8 in
[
5
]
)
.

Let
u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega,d^{\beta})
be a weak solution of

L_{\beta}(u)=-div\overrightarrow{f}
,
where
|\overrightarrow{f}|/d^{\beta}\in L^{p}(\Omega,w)
and
p>2n-\varepsilon
, for some
\epsilon>0
. Then,
u
is Hölder continuous in
\Omega
.

In this paper we do not concern ourselves with the optimal assumptions on
f
to ensure that the solution of (
1.2
) is continuous or bounded. Instead, we simply assume this regularity and then derive further properties from it. We will derive explicit estimates that describe the asymptotic behaviour of the solution as it approaches the boundary.

These estimates provide upper and lower bounds for the solution
u
, highlighting the maximal regularity of the solution in terms of the parameters of the problem, in particular of the exponent
\beta
.

The main result in this direction is stated in our main theorem, which follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that
u\in C^{2}(\Omega)\cap C(\overline{\Omega})
solves
(
1.2
) with

f\geq 0
,
f\not\equiv 0
in
\Omega
,
f\in C(\overline{\Omega})
and
\beta<1
.
Then, for any
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}>0
, there exist
\sigma\in(0,1)
and two positive constants
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
such that

(1.6)

D_{1}d^{1-\beta}(x)(-\log d(x))^{-\eta_{1}}\leq u(x)\leq D_{2}d^{1-\beta}(x)(-\log d(x))^{\eta_{2}},\quad\forall x\in\Gamma_{\sigma}^{\circ}.

where

\Gamma_{\sigma}=\{x\in\overline{\Omega}:\ d(x)<\sigma\}.

Moreover, if
\Omega
is convex, then

(1.7)

D_{1}d^{1-\beta}(x)(-\log d(x))^{-\eta_{1}}\leq u(x)\leq D_{2}d^{1-\beta}(x),\quad\forall x\in\Gamma_{\sigma}.

The estimate (
1.7
) provides a precise control of the solution

u
on the boundary
\partial\Omega
.

In our opinion it is interesting in itself, and in any case, we will use it to deduce some properties of the solution.
A first immediate consequence concerns the maximal Hölder regularity that we may expect from the solution.

As mentioned before,
in
[
5
]
, under some assumptions on
f
, it was proved that the solution
u\in C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
, for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
. We give an upper bound to the value
\alpha
:

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose that
u\in C^{2}(\Omega)\cap C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
solves
(
1.2
) with

f\geq 0
,
f\not\equiv 0
in
\Omega
,
f\in C(\overline{\Omega})
and
\beta<1
. Then
\alpha\leq\min\{1,1-\beta\}.

We point out that the result is sharp, as proved in Remark
4.1
.

Another consequence concerns the regularity of this solution within the framework of Sobolev spaces. The solution to equation (
1.2
) minimizes the functional

F(u):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}d^{\beta}\ dx-\int_{\Omega}fu\ dx

in the space
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega,d^{\beta})
(see Section
2
).
The next result investigates the inclusion of
the solution in the classical Sobolev spaces.

Theorem 1.4
.

Same assumptions as in Theorem
1.2
or Theorem
1.3
. Then
u
belongs to
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
if and only if
\beta<\frac{1}{2}
.

We observe that the results of Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
are influenced by
\beta
, even though the weights
d^{\beta}\in A_{2}

for all
\beta\in(-1,1)
.
This demonstrates that the regularity of the solution to (
1.2
) is not solely dependent on the Muckenhoupt class, but rather on the explicit structure of the weight. Lastly, we note that our results extend to more general weights

w
that behave similarly
near the boundary (see Remark
3.1
).

The paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
we provide some additional properties of the weight
d^{\beta}
and recall other known results; in Section
3
we give the proof of the main estimates of Theorem
1.2
, and in Section
4
we prove Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
.

2.
Notations and preliminary known results

In the introduction we have introduced the so called Muckenhoupt class
A_{2}
, namely the class of locally integrable, nonnegative, real-valued functions
w
that satisfy

(2.1)

\sup_{B\subset\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{|B|}{\int_{B}w\ dx}\right)\left(\frac{1}{|B|}{\int_{B}w^{-1}}dx\right)<+\infty

where the supremum is taken over balls
B\subset\Omega
.

In
[
3
]
it was proved that that, if
d
is the distance function, the weight

(2.2)

w(x):=d^{\beta}(x),\quad{\rm\ \ }

belongs to
A_{2}
for
\beta\in(-1,1)
(see Theorem 3.1 in
[
3
]
).

Before presenting the main result, we would like to highlight some key properties of
d
.

We denote by

(2.3)

\Gamma_{\sigma}=\{x\in\overline{\Omega}:\ d(x)<\sigma\}

the portion in
\overline{\Omega}
of a tubular neighbourhood of
\partial\Omega
. With an abuse of terminology,
from now on we will call
\Gamma_{\sigma}
a neighbourhood of
\partial\Omega
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
a bounded domain with
\partial\Omega\in C^{2}
. Then there exists a small constant
\sigma>0
such that

(2.4)

d\in C^{2}(\Gamma_{\sigma}^{\circ})\cap C^{0}(\overline{\Gamma}_{\sigma}),

(2.5)

|\nabla d(x)|=1\hbox{ for all }x\in\Gamma_{\sigma},

Moreover,
for every measurable nonnegative function
g:(0,\sigma)\to\mathbb{R}

(2.6)

g\circ d\in L^{1}({\Gamma_{\sigma}})\Longleftrightarrow g\in L^{1}(0,\sigma).

Proof.

For (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) see e.g.
[
6
]
Appendix 14.6.

Here’s a brief outline of how to prove (
2.6
):
from the coarea formula and (
2.4
) and (
2.5
), we have, since
|\nabla d(x)|=1

\int_{\Gamma_{\sigma}}g(d(x))\ dx=\int_{0}^{\sigma}g(t)\mathcal{H}^{N-1}(\Gamma_{\sigma}\cap\{d=t\})\ dt,

where
\mathcal{H}^{N-1}
is the Hausdorff measure of
\Gamma_{\sigma}\cap\{d=t\}
. Since the
\partial\Omega
is
C^{2}
, there exist two positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
such that

c_{1}H^{N-1}(\partial\Omega)\leq H^{N-1}(\Gamma_{\sigma}\cap\{d=t\})\leq c_{1}H^{N-1}(\partial\Omega)

(see e.g.
[
9
]
, Appendix 2.12.3). This ends the proof.
∎

Let us end this section with some remarks on the solution to (
1.2
).
If
f/d^{\beta}\in L^{2}(\Omega,d^{\beta})
,
then (
1.2
) admits a weak solution
u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega,d^{\beta})
. This is an immediate consequence of weighted Poincaré inequality

(2.7)

\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{2}d^{\beta}(x)\ dx\leq C\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}d^{\beta}(x)\ dx,

and the compact embedding of
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega,d^{\beta})
in
L^{2}(\Omega,d^{\beta})

(see e.g
[
4
]
, Prop. 5.1 and Ex. 5.2).

Indeed, the functional

F:W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega,d^{\beta})\to\mathbb{R}

(2.8)

F(u):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}d^{\beta}\ dx-\int_{\Omega}fu\ dx

is well defined, coercive and bounded from below. So that
J
attains its infimum on
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega,d^{\beta})
(see also Theorem 2.9 in
[
2
]
).

Regarding the regularity within the weighted Sobolev spaces, we would like to mention this Calderon-Zygmund type result, that was proved, for instance, in
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
:

Theorem 2.2

(Theorem 3.8
[
1
]
and Theorem 3.6 in
[
2
]
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded domain of class
C^{2}
, with
d^{\beta}
-quasiconformal boundary. Let
\frac{f}{d^{\beta}}\in L^{2}(\Omega,d^{\beta})
. Then, if
u
is a weak solution to (
1.2
)
we have
u\in W^{2,2}(\Omega,d^{\beta})
and there exists a constant
C
such that

(2.9)

||u||_{W^{2,2}(\Omega,d^{\beta})}\leq C\left(||u||_{W^{1,2}(\Omega,d^{\beta})}+||{f}/{w}||_{L^{2}(\Omega,d^{\beta})}\right).

Some other interior regularity results have been proven in
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
for the weight
w(x_{1},..,x_{N})=|x_{1}|^{\beta}
with
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\in B_{1}
, the unit ball of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

3.
The main estimate: proof of Theorem
1.2

In this section, we will prove upper and lower bounds for the solutions of the problem (
1.2
) where the datum

f
is a non-negative continuous function.

Let us give the proof of Theorem
1.2
,

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We begin by noting that, since
L_{\beta}
is elliptic in
\Omega
, the weak maximum principle applies (see
[
6
]
). Therefore,
f\geq 0
implies that
u\geq 0
. Furthermore, since
L_{\beta}
is uniformly elliptic in every
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
,
it follows that
u>0
in
\Omega
.
Now, we split the proof into several steps.

Let, for
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\in\Gamma_{\sigma}
,

(3.1)

v(x)=\begin{cases}d^{1-\beta}(x)\left(-\log d(x)\right)^{\eta}\quad&x\in\ \Gamma_{\sigma}\setminus\partial\Omega\\
0&x\in\ \partial\Omega,\end{cases}

Step 1:
we have

(3.2)

\begin{split}L_{\beta}(v)&=-\Delta d\left[(1-\beta)\left(-\log d\right)^{\eta}-\eta\left(-\log d\right)^{\eta-1}\right]\\
&+d^{-1}\left[(1-\beta)\eta\left(-\log d\right)^{\eta-1}-\eta(\eta-1)\left(-\log d\right)^{\eta-2}\right].\end{split}

This is a straightforward computation where we used that
|\nabla d|=1
.

Step 2:
If
\eta<0
we have that here exists
\sigma>0
and
\epsilon_{1}>0
such that

(3.3)

u(x)\geq\epsilon_{1}d^{1-\beta}(x)\left(-\log d(x)\right)^{\eta},\quad\forall x\in\Gamma_{\sigma}.

Indeed, from (
3.2
) we have that, (recall that
\Delta d
is bounded in a neighbourhood
\Gamma_{\sigma}
of
\partial\Omega
)

L_{\beta}(v)=\frac{(1-\beta)\eta}{d\left(-\log d\right)^{1-\eta}}+o\left(\frac{1}{d\left(-\log d\right)^{1-\eta}}\right)\quad{\rm as}\ x\to\partial\Omega.

Since
L_{\beta}(u)=f\geq 0
and
\eta<0
, there exists a (possibly smaller)
\sigma>0
such that

(3.4)

L_{\beta}(v)<0\leq L_{\beta}(u),\quad\forall x\in\Gamma_{\sigma}.

The claim will follow by the maximum principle (see Theorem 3.1 in
[
6
]
). Indeed, for a small
\epsilon_{1}
, it holds

(3.5)

\epsilon_{1}v(x)\leq u(x),\quad\forall x\in\partial\Gamma_{\sigma}.

This is obvious on
\partial\Omega

because both
u
and
v
vanish on
\partial\Omega
and, on the other hand, since
u
is continuous in
\Omega
and strictly positive on
\partial\Gamma_{\sigma}\cap\Omega
, we have that, possibly choosing a small
\epsilon_{1}>0
,

\epsilon_{1}\max\limits_{x\in\partial\Gamma_{\sigma}\cap\Omega}v\leq\min\limits_{x\in\partial\Gamma_{\sigma}\cap\Omega}u.

Resuming, from (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) we derive that

L_{\beta}(u-\epsilon_{1}v)\geq 0{\ \rm on}\ \Gamma_{\sigma},\ {\rm and}\ \ (u-\epsilon_{1}v)\geq 0\ {\ \rm on}\ \ \partial\Gamma_{\sigma}.

Consequently, by the weak maximum principle
(
3.3
) holds,
and the claim of Step 2 is proved.

Step 3:
If
\eta>0
we have that here exists
\Gamma_{\sigma}
and
\epsilon_{2}>0
such that

(3.6)

\epsilon_{2}u(x)\leq d^{1-\beta}(x)\left(-\log d(x)\right)^{\eta},\quad\forall x\in\overline{\Gamma}_{\sigma}.

Again, from (
3.2
) we have that

L_{\beta}(v)=\frac{(1-\beta)\eta}{d\left(-\log d\right)^{1-\eta}}+o\left(\frac{1}{d\left(-\log d\right)^{1-\eta}}\right)\quad{\rm as}\ x\to\partial\Omega.

Therefore, since
\eta>0
, for every
M>0
there exists a neighbourhood
\Gamma_{\sigma}
of
\partial\Omega
such that

(3.7)

L_{\beta}(v)>M\quad\forall x\in\Gamma_{\sigma}.

Now, by the continuity of
f
on
\overline{\Omega}
, we have that

0<\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}f(x)=\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}L_{\beta}(u)<+\infty

We choose
\displaystyle M\geq\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}f(x)
, so that

(3.8)

L_{\beta}(v)\geq L_{\beta}(u).

With an argument similar to the one used in the previous step, by reversing the roles of
u
and
v
, we also have that there exists a constant
{\varepsilon}_{2}>0
such that

(3.9)

\min_{\partial\Gamma_{\sigma}}v\geq\max_{\partial\Gamma_{\sigma}}{\varepsilon}_{2}u,

and the claim follows by the weak maximum principle.

Step 4:
Proof of (
1.6
).

It follows by Step
2
and Step
3
with
D_{1}={\varepsilon}_{1}^{-1}
and
D_{2}={\varepsilon}_{2}^{-1}
.

Step 5:
Proof of (
1.7
).
Here we have only to prove the RHS of (
1.7
). The convexity of
\Omega
will allow to choose
\eta=0
in (
3.2
)
Indeed, if
\eta=0
then (
3.2
) becomes

L_{\beta}(v)=-(1-\beta)\Delta d\geq 0

since
\Omega
is convex (see Lemma 14.17 in
[
6
]
). Next the claim of the RHS of (
1.7
) follows as in the proof of Step
3
.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

The previous proposition can be easily adapted to more general weights. Indeed, the same statement holds if the distance function is replaced by a
C^{2}
-function,
w>0
in a ‘neighborhood’ of
\partial\Omega
and such that
w
has no critical point on
\partial\Omega
. This last claim implies that there exists a positive constant
C
such that

\frac{1}{C}\leq|\nabla w|^{2}\leq C\quad\hbox{in a neighborhood of }\partial\Omega.

and this allows to repeat the proof without any change.

4.
Proof of Theorems
1.3
and
1.4

In this section we apply the estimates of the previous section in order to get regularity result for solutions to (
1.2
). We recall that in
[
5
]
, under some assumptions on
f
, it was proved that if
u
solves (
1.2
) then
u\in C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Note that the case
\beta\leq 0
is trivial, so we assume
\beta\in(0,1)
.

By contradiction suppose that
u\in C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
with
\alpha>1-\beta
, i.e. there exists
C>0
such that

(4.1)

|u(x)-u(y)|\leq C|x-y|^{\alpha}\quad\forall x,y\in\overline{\Omega}.

In particular, let us choose
x\in\Gamma_{\sigma}
and
y\in\partial\Omega
such that
|x-y|=d(x)
. Here
\Gamma_{\sigma}
is the set where (
1.6
) holds. Hence (
4.1
) becomes, for
x\in\Gamma_{\sigma}
,

(4.2)

u(x)\leq Cd^{\alpha}(x),

and by the LHS of (
1.6
), we deduce that

D_{1}d^{1-\beta}(x)(-\log d(x))^{-\eta_{1}}\leq Cd^{\alpha}(x),

which implies, since
\alpha>1-\beta
,

\frac{C}{D_{1}}\leq d^{\alpha+\beta-1}(x)(-\log d(x))^{\eta_{1}}\to 0,\ {\rm as}\ x\to\partial\Omega,

which leads to a contradiction.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

The result of Theorem
1.3
is sharp.

Indeed, if we consider the function

u(r)=\int_{r}^{1}s^{1-N}(1-s)^{-\beta}\left(\int_{0}^{s}t^{N-1}(1-t)^{\beta}dt\right)ds

we have that
u
is a positive radial solution to

(4.3)

\begin{cases}L_{\beta}(u)=d^{\beta}&in\ B_{1}\\
u=0&on\ \partial B_{1},\end{cases}

where
B_{1}
is the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, centered at the origin. Since
d^{\beta}=(1-r)^{\beta}\in C^{0,\beta}(B_{1})
we get that
u\in C^{2}(B_{1})
but an easy computation shows that
u(r)\sim(1-r)^{1-\beta}
near
r=1
. So the maximum regularity near the boundary of
u
is
C^{0,1-\beta}(B_{1})
.

A second intriguing question concerns the regularity of this solution in the context of Sobolev spaces.
We have previously established that solutions to (
1.2
) minimize the functional

F:W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega,d^{\beta})\to\mathbb{R},\quad\quad F(u)=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}d^{\beta}\ dx-\int_{\Omega}fu\ dx.

Now we prove that
u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
if and only if
\beta<\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

The case
\beta=0
is trivial, so we consider only the case
\beta\neq 0
.

We first prove that the condition
\beta<\frac{1}{2}
is necessary.

Indeed, if by contradiction
u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
, and
\beta\geq\frac{1}{2}
, by the Hardy inequality and (
1.6
), we get

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\geq C\int_{\Omega}\frac{u^{2}}{d^{2}}\geq C\int_{\Gamma_{\delta}}\frac{D_{1}^{2}}{d^{2\beta}\left(-\log d\right)^{2\eta_{1}}}=+\infty,

by using (
2.6
) in Section
2
.

Now, let
\beta<\frac{1}{2}
.
Starting from the equation

-{\rm div}(d^{\beta}\nabla u)=f,

let us multiply by
\displaystyle\frac{u}{d^{\beta}}
and integrate. We get

(4.4)

-\int_{\Omega}{\rm div}(d^{\beta}\nabla u)\frac{u}{d^{\beta}}\ dx=\int_{\Omega}\frac{fu}{d^{\beta}}\ dx

For the integral on the left we have, since
|\nabla d|=1
,

(4.5)

\begin{split}-\int_{\Omega}{\rm div}(\nabla ud^{\beta})\frac{u}{d^{\beta}}&=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}-\beta\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u\cdot\nabla d)\frac{u}{d}\\
&\geq\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}-|\beta|\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|\frac{u}{d}\\
&\geq\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}-|\beta|\frac{\epsilon}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}-\frac{|\beta|}{2\epsilon}\int_{\Omega}\frac{u^{2}}{d^{2}},\end{split}

where we used the inequality
\displaystyle ab\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}a^{2}+\frac{1}{2\epsilon}b^{2}
with
a=|\nabla u|
and
b=\frac{u}{d}
.

Now, by choosing

\epsilon=\frac{1}{|\beta|}
in the last inequality, and by (
4.4
), one obtains

(4.6)

\int_{\Omega}\frac{fu}{d^{\beta}}=-\int_{\Omega}{\rm div}(\nabla ud^{\beta})\frac{u}{d^{\beta}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\frac{u^{2}}{d^{2}}.

Therefore,

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\leq{\beta^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\frac{u^{2}}{d^{2}}+2\int_{\Omega}\frac{fu}{d^{\beta}}.

We only need to estimate the first integral on the right side, since the second is bounded by (
2.6
) and by the continuity of
u
and
f
.

In fact, by (
1.7
)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{u^{2}}{d^{2}}\ dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{\sigma}}\frac{u^{2}}{d^{2}}\ dx+\int_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma_{\sigma}}\frac{u^{2}}{d^{2}}\ dx

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\Gamma_{\sigma}}\frac{\left(-\log d\right)^{2\eta}}{d^{2\beta}}\ dx+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\int_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma_{\sigma}}u^{2}\ dx<+\infty,

since
\beta<\frac{1}{2}
using again (
2.6
) in Section
2
. This ends the proof of the sufficient condition.

∎

We note that the threshold
\beta=\frac{1}{2}
holds independently of the regularity of
f
. Thus, even if the datum
f
in (
1.2
) belongs to
C^{\infty}(\Omega)
, the solution does not belong to
W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
for
\beta\geq\frac{1}{2}
. This result highlights that the regularity in Sobolev spaces depends strongly on the operator
L_{\beta}
rather than on the datum
f
.
