Reply: SARS-CoV-2 Infection Control and Burnout Prevention Among Healthcare Workers Require Individual and Public Action
Authors: Ki Tae Kwon
Published: 2023

Dear Editor:

I appreciate to Professor Josef Finsterer for his interest and comments on our article [1,2]. I agree with Finsterer that it's important to maintain high quality of sleep to prevent burnout among coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) healthcare workers (HCWs). I also have published a study showing that burnout among COVID-19 HCWs was correlated with insomnia [3]. The COVID-19 pandemic has led to increased workload, stress, and patient acuity among HCWs, resulting in burnout and sleep disturbances [4]. Studies have shown that HCWs on the front lines during the pandemic experience high chronic stress, which negatively affects their sleep quality and mental health, ultimately leading to burnout [5]. Poor sleep quality has been reported by the majority of HCWs during the pandemic and is particularly associated with emotional exhaustion, a dimension of burnout [6]. These findings highlight the importance of addressing sleep hygiene and promoting good sleep health to mitigate burnout among COVID-19 HCWs. In addition to improving sleep hygiene, individually focused interventions such as mindfulness-based practices (a type of meditation) have benefited HCWs by increasing well-being, engagement and resilience, and reducing burnout [7].

I agree that sufficient staffing is necessary to reduce the pressure on HCWs. In fact, more staff have been mobilized to care for COVID-19 patients than to care for non-COVID-19 patients [8]. However, during the COVID-19 pandemic, it was difficult to ensure adequate staffing or to hire additional staff as the pandemic often deteriorated very rapidly. Consequently, the number of patients increased too quickly for the prepared workforce, often resulting in staff shortages. Further research is needed on how to efficiently mobilize and utilize the healthcare workforce in the context of an emerging infectious disease pandemic.

Finally, we did not intend to advocate for mandatory vaccination of HCWs. We stated that “HCWs must be vaccinated against COVID-19 to avoid infection” in our article [2]. However, I believe that “HCWs are strongly recommended to be vaccinated against COVID-19 to avoid infection” would have been more appropriate. In the evidence-based description of the article, we stated that given the concerns and anxiety regarding hasty development of the COVID-19 vaccines, mandatory vaccination is not desirable. Instead, we stated that vaccination should be encouraged by changing HCWs’ perceptions through transparent disclosure of the adverse reactions and effectiveness and repeated education of HCWs about the safety and effectiveness of the vaccines based on scientific evidence.
