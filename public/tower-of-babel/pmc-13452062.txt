More Than Activity: The Effect of Sustained Engagement in Digital Alcohol Support Interventions for Regional and Remote Communities
Authors: Katinka van de Ven, Getenet Dessie, Kedir Y. Ahmed, Nicole Lee, Hazel Dalton, Julaine Allan, Subash Thapa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeThis study examines differences between major city and regional and remote participants in engagement in a digital alcohol support intervention (Daybreak), risky drinking, and psychological distress and tests the mediating role of psychological distress.MethodsThis was a longitudinal, observational cohort study of Daybreak participants between January 2019 and December 2024 (N = 12,824). Outcomes were risky drinking and psychological distress. Ordered logistic regression examined associations between the outcomes and program engagement, and multilevel generalized structural equation modeling tested mediation by psychological distress.FindingsAt 6 months, risky drinking scores declined by 42% in major city participants and 57% in regional and remote participants. High engagement in reacting to posts reduced risky drinking in both metro (OR = 0.47, 95% CI 0.27–0.80) and regional and remote areas (OR = 0.42, 95% CI 0.18–0.99). High engagement in post sharing was associated with lower odds of high risky drinking only in major city participants (OR = 0.56, 95% CI 0.33–0.98). In regional areas, participants who aimed to quit drinking at registration had lower risky drinking (OR = 0.72, 95% CI 0.53–0.99), while longer program inactivity increased odds of psychological distress (OR = 1.95, 95% CI 1.13–3.38). Psychological distress mediated 56% of the association between post reactions and risky drinking in major city participants and 33% in regional and remote participants.ConclusionsDistinct engagement pathways across regional and remote and major city settings suggest the need for more flexible digital program designs. Psychological distress, as a partial mediator, should be both a treatment target and a mechanism of change.

Introduction

Alcohol use is a major contributor to disease burden globally [1], with regional, rural and remote populations (henceforth, regional and remote) often experiencing elevated risks compared to their major city counterparts. In Australia, individuals living in regional and remote areas are more likely to consume alcohol at harmful levels and experience greater alcohol related harm, including hospitalizations and emergency department presentations [2, 3]. Similar trends are established internationally with regional and remote residents, particularly men, experiencing disproportionate harms from alcohol and other drug use [4], compounded by reduced access to treatment, cultural stigma and geographic isolation [5, 6].

In response to these barriers, digital health interventions, such as Telehealth, mobile apps and wearable devices, have emerged as a scalable and accessible means of delivering care. The COVID‐19 pandemic accelerated the global adoption of digital health [7]. Evidence regarding the effectiveness of digital health interventions do show mixed results in the treatment of mental health conditions and alcohol and other drugs issues depending on the behavior, substance and population targeted and the type of digital intervention used [8, 9, 10]. Some commercially available apps even promote harmful drinking and other drug use [11].

Digital health interventions (non‐commercial) targeting alcohol use however generally show promising results [8, 12, 13, 14]. Although challenges remain (for example, internet reliability and suitability for high‐risk clients), online delivery of alcohol and other drugs treatment and support services have been shown to increase engagement and maintain continuity of care, particularly in regional and remote areas [15, 16]. Online mutual help groups and mobile apps have also shown promise in supporting behavior change and reducing relapse risk [17, 18, 19].

However, evaluations of digital health interventions often lack regional and remote specific analyses [20], limiting our understanding of how geographic context may shape engagement and outcomes. Some research exists on the barriers and enablers of the use of digital interventions for alcohol and other drugs in regional and remote areas [21, 22] and satisfaction levels with digital interventions [15, 23]. Evidence regarding the effectiveness of digital interventions in reducing alcohol use targeting regional and remote population is however limited. In areas such as chronic disease management, mental health, prenatal care and preventive care, there is however some evidence that digital health interventions targeting regional and remote areas improve outcomes and reduce hospital readmissions [20].

In Australia, Hello Sunday Morning Daybreak program is one of the most widely used digital alcohol behavior change tools (with over 175,000 members recorded in 2026) [24], and is particularly popular amongst women [25]. The program provides access to anonymous peer support, self‐monitoring tools and clinical care navigation. Prior research has found that Daybreak and Drink Tracker are effective in reducing risky drinking and psychological distress within a 3‐month period [13, 14, 24, 26]. However, little is known about how members in regional and remote areas engage with and respond to the program compared to those in major city areas. This study aims to compare Daybreak participants, with or without using the Drink Tracker function, from regional and remote areas with the major city population by examining differences in demographic characteristics, psychological distress, risky drinking, and program engagement. By providing a nuanced understanding of regional and remote member experiences, this study contributes to the growing international evidence base on the role of digital health interventions in reducing geographic health disparities.

In addition, emerging evidence, particularly following the COVID‐19 pandemic, suggests that individuals experiencing psychological distress are more likely to engage in higher levels of alcohol consumption [27]. It is plausible that certain factors (for example, goal setting and engagement with the community) may influence both psychological distress and risky drinking, with some effects on risky drinking occurring indirectly through changes in distress. This study therefore also aimed to examine the potential mediating role of psychological distress in the relationship between Daybreak program engagement and risky drinking for people living in major city and regional and remote areas.

Method

Study Design and Ethics

This was a longitudinal, observational cohort study (panel study) using secondary, de‐identified data collected at two time points (baseline and 6‐month follow‑up). The data were collected from members of the Daybreak digital health app (Daybreak) developed and managed by Hello Sunday Morning. Hello Sunday Morning is an Australian digital health organization that offers online support for individuals seeking to change their relationship with alcohol.

The dataset includes self‐reported information collected at program registration and 6 months post registration from Australian members of the Daybreak app who enrolled between January 2019 and December 2024. The research received ethical approval from the Bellberry Human Research Ethics Committee (2024‐08‐1015‐PRE‐1).

Study Setting

Hello Sunday Morning delivers digital early intervention programs that are freely available to all Australians through government funding. The core service, Daybreak, is a smartphone‐based platform offering anonymous peer support and optional features such as a self‐monitoring tool (Drink Tracker) and clinical care coordination (Care Navigator) when required. The program has demonstrated efficacy in reducing risky drinking and psychological distress within the first 3 months of use [13, 14].

Data Collection and Consent

Hello Sunday Morning collects routine administrative data to support service quality monitoring and improvement. Upon joining, members complete a short intake assessment covering demographics, risky drinking and psychological distress. Participants are then invited by email to complete an optional follow‐up survey at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months post registration.

Data are stored and analyzed using Amazon QuickSight. Informed consent is obtained at registration through acceptance of the organization's Terms of Use, which explains that de‐identified demographic and health data may be used for research and evaluation. Further details are provided in Hello Sunday Morning Privacy Policy, outlining the purpose and safeguards for data use in research.

Measures

The outcome variables were psychological distress and risky drinking. Psychological distress was assessed using the 10‐item Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10) [28]. The K10 is a widely validated measure designed to assess psychological distress in population and clinical settings. It includes 10 questions about emotional states experienced over the past four weeks, with responses ranging from 1 (“none of the time”) to 5 (“all of the time”). Total scores range from 10 to 50, with higher scores indicating greater levels of distress.

Risky drinking was measured using the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test—Consumption (AUDIT‐C), a 3 item screening tool with scores ranging from 0 to 12, where higher scores indicate greater risky drinking. Scores were categorized into low (0–4), moderate (5–10), and high (11–12) risk groups (in line with the World Health Organization guidelines). In this study, AUDIT‐C scores are interpreted as a measure of risky drinking rather than direct harm or use.

The explanatory variables were categorized into four groups: sociodemographic factors, risky drinking, initial motivation and engagement. The sociodemographic factors included gender, age, and remoteness of residence. Remoteness was determined using the Australian Bureau of Statistics Remoteness Area classification based on postcode (major cities, inner regional, outer regional, remote, and very remote). Major city participants represent 74.5% of the sample, 18.1% is inner regional, 6.3% is outer regional, 0.8% is remote, and 0.3% is very remote. For analytical purposes (due to the small sample size per category), all non‐major city categories were grouped into a single “regional and remote” category. This approach was used to ensure sufficient statistical power; however, it is acknowledged that regional, rural, and remote populations are heterogeneous.

Initial motivation was captured through participants’ self‐reported focus at registration (for example, to reduce or quit drinking) and their readiness to change their relationship with alcohol. Engagement variables were derived from platform activity data including posting behavior and reactions to other members’ posts. Engagement levels were categorized into quartiles based on the distribution of activity within the sample, representing very low to high engagement.

We included the following covariates as control variables: Drink Tracker use, Care Navigator use and health insurance status. Content engagement was treated as a time varying covariate and modeled with interaction terms with time to account for changing effects across assessments.

Data Analysis

Frequencies and percentages were calculated to describe participant characteristics at baseline and 6‐month follow‐up. Means and standard deviations (SDs) were calculated to summarize changes in risky drinking and psychological distress scores over time, stratified by area of residence and levels of platform engagement.

To investigate the relationship between explanatory variables and ordinal outcomes, we used a mixed effects ordered logistic regression model. This approach accounted for repeated observations within individuals, thereby adjusting for within‐person clustering. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

To test whether the effect of program engagement on risky drinking was mediated by psychological distress, we conducted mediation analyses using a multilevel generalized structural equation modeling (GSEM) framework. This approach was chosen because both the mediator and the outcome were ordinal and the data were hierarchical, with repeated observations nested within individuals. The models simultaneously estimated the effect of program engagement on psychological distress and the effect of psychological distress on risky drinking, while accounting for within‐subject correlations (see Figure 1).

Mediation model showing the hypothesized relationships between distal factors psychological distress (mediator) and the outcome (risky drinking).

Total, direct, and indirect effects were estimated. The Natural Direct Effect (NDE) represents the effect of program engagement on risky drinking independent of psychological distress, the Natural Indirect Effect (NIE) represents the portion of the effect mediated through psychological distress, and the Total Effect (TE) is the sum of the NDE and NIE. The proportion mediated was defined as the ratio of the indirect effect to the total effect. Confidence intervals for regression coefficients and effect estimates were generated using 7000 bootstrap replicates to ensure stable estimation. All analyses were performed using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp, USA).

Change in Risky Drinking Between Baseline and 6‐Month Follow‐Up

Between baseline and 6 months, the mean risky drinking score in major city areas dropped from 8.81 (± 2.30) to 7.44 (± 3.50) among Daybreak members who did not use Drink Tracker. In regional and remote areas, the mean score reduced from 8.79 (± 2.31) to 7.32 (± 3.56). Please note that risky drinking refers to AUDIT‐C categories (low, moderate, high) as defined in the Methods.

Risky drinking dropped from 8.57 (± 2.27) to 7.64 (± 3.14) among major city Daybreak members who also used Drink Tracker. For regional and remote Daybreak members who used this additional function scores reduced from 8.71 (± 2.29) to 7.95 (± 3.06).

In major city areas, both less engaged Daybreak participants (8.83 ± 2.34 to 7.93 ± 3.16) and those highly engaged in post sharing (8.68 ± 2.41 to 6.14 ± 4.14) showed improvements. In regional and remote areas, Daybreak participants highly engaged in post sharing experienced a decline from 8.66 (± 2.33) to 6.17 (± 3.98). In addition, those highly engaged in reacting to posts showed a marked reduction in risky drinking in both major city (8.75 ± 2.35 to 6.15 ± 4.12) and regional and remote areas (8.60 ± 2.38 to 6.09 ± 3.98) (Table 3).

Change in Psychological Distress Scores Between Baseline and 6‐Month Follow‐Up

Among Daybreak members who did not use Drink Tracker, psychological distress reduced in both major city areas (from 24.00 ± 7.84 to 23.40 ± 8.71) and regional and remote areas (from 24.36 ± 7.86 to 23.58 ± 8.86). In regional and remote areas, those with longer periods of inactivity showed higher distress at baseline and follow‐up (medium inactive members, from 24.94 ± 7.84 to 24.53 ± 8.68). In major city areas, long inactive members showed a greater reduction (from 23.67 ± 7.73 to 22.56 ± 8.67) than other groups.

Participants who also used the Drink Tracker function reported a modest decrease in psychological distress in major city areas (from 23.36 ± 7.53 to 22.83 ± 8.13). However, in regional and remote areas, scores increased slightly (from 23.00 ± 7.39 to 23.48 ± 8.71).

In both major city (from 24.53 ± 7.52 to 21.91 ± 9.04) and regional and remote participants (from 25.11 ± 7.42 to 22.65 ± 9.16), higher engagement in post sharing was associated with notable reductions in distress. Similarly, participants with high post reaction engagement had a substantial decrease in both major city and (from 24.13 ± 7.54 to 21.55 ± 8.87) and regional and remote areas (from 24.35 ± 7.64 to 21.93 ± 8.96).

Factors Associated With High Risky Drinking

Our multivariable analysis showed that, compared to baseline, participants at 6 months had a 42% lower likelihood of being in the higher risky drinking category in major city areas (OR: 0.58, 95% CI: 0.41–0.82), and a 57% lower likelihood in regional and remote areas (OR: 0.43, 95% CI: 0.25–0.75). In major city areas, people who had been inactive for a longer time were associated with a 53% lower risky drinking scores compared to those who were inactive for only a short time (OR: 0.47; 95% CI: 0.32–0.67). In major city areas, participants who were enrolled for medium (OR: 1.74; 95% CI: 1.15–2.65) and (OR: 1.95; 95% CI: 1.36–2.79) long periods had higher odds of being in a higher risk category compared to those enrolled for a short period. Participants who were unsure about their focus at registration were less likely to have a high‐risk category compared to those who aimed to reduce drinking (OR: 0.22; 95% CI: 0.14–0.34 in major city vs. OR, 0.19; 95% CI: 0.09–0.38 in regional and remote areas). In regional and remote areas, individuals who focused on quitting drinking had a lower likelihood of being in the highest category for risky drinking compared to those who aimed to reduce their intake (OR: 0.72; 95% CI: 0.53–0.99).

The interaction analysis showed that, as the duration of program engagement increased, participants in major city areas with medium and high engagement had significantly lower odds of being in a higher category of risky drinking, by 43% and 44%, respectively, compared to those with very low engagement. However, this association was not significant among participants in regional and remote areas. Similarly, high engagement in reacting to posts, combined with time, was associated with reduced odds of being in a higher category of risky drinking in both major city areas (OR, 0.47; 95% CI: 0.27–0.80) and regional and remote areas (OR, 0.42; 95% CI: 0.18–0.99) compared to those with very low engagement (Table 4).

Factors Associated With Psychological Distress

There was no significant difference in psychological distress at 6‐month follow‐up compared to baseline in both major city and regional and remote areas. In regional and remote areas, longer inactivity was associated with increased odds of higher distress compared to very short inactivity (OR = 1.95, 95% CI 1.13–3.38). Duration of enrolment showed no significant associations with distress in both settings. Regarding registration focus, participants who reported joining the program to quit drinking were associated with higher odds of distress in both major city (OR = 1.75, 95% CI 1.35–2.28) and regional and remote areas (OR = 1.62, 95% CI 1.15–2.28) compared to those registering for physical health. Similarly, those not sure about their registration focus also had higher odds in both major city (OR = 1.48, 95% CI 1.03–2.12) and regional and remote areas (OR = 1.56, 95% CI 1.05–2.33).

Mediation of Psychological Distress in the Association Between Daybreak Program Engagement and Risky Drinking

After controlling for covariates, reduced psychological distress mediated 33.18% of the total indirect effect of high engagement in post reactions on risky drinking (OR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.57 to 0.97) in regional and remote regions. Similarly, in major city areas, reduced psychological distress mediated 56.27% of the total indirect effect of high engagement in post reactions on risky drinking (OR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.62 to 0.85). In addition, psychological distress mediated the relationship between Drink Tracker usage and risky drinking in regional and remote areas, accounting for 63.47% of this association (OR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.04 to 1.44) (Table 5, Figure 3).

Discussion

This study examined differences in risky drinking, psychological distress and patterns of digital engagement among major city and regional and remote participants using Daybreak. Participants in both groups demonstrated significant reductions in risky drinking scores over the 6‐month period. This confirms earlier evidence that Daybreak supports meaningful reductions in risky drinking over time [13, 14], with our findings illustrating that it is particularly beneficial for people living in regional and remote areas (57% reduction vs. a 42% reduction for major city members). Patterns of engagement and the mediating effect of psychological distress however differed between major city and regional and remote Daybreak members.

Regional and Remote and Major City Differences in Program Engagement and Outcomes

While reductions in risky drinking were observed across both regional and remote and major city participants, the underlying patterns of engagement suggest that members in regional and remote areas may follow distinct pathways to changing their relationship with alcohol.

While major city members appeared to benefit from both sharing and reacting to posts, regional and remote participants only showed a significant reduction in risky drinking when engaging through post reactions. This suggests that lower effort, less visible forms of interaction, such as liking or supporting others’ posts, may be more acceptable or effective among regional and remote members, who may be less inclined to disclose personal experiences publicly. Individuals in regional and remote areas often face heightened stigma and concerns about privacy when seeking help for alcohol related issues [6], which may discourage them from openly contributing to peer forums (even when anonymous). In contrast, reacting to posts allows members to participate in the community and access social reinforcement without the vulnerability associated with personal disclosure.

The benefits observed in major city members from both posting and reacting suggest a wider acceptance of open sharing in these settings, whereas the same mechanisms may not operate similarly in regional and remote contexts. These findings highlight the importance of designing digital programs with multiple modes of engagement, allowing members to benefit from social connection in ways that align with their comfort levels and cultural norms.

In addition, digital access and infrastructure may also play a role. Barriers such as limited internet reliability, mobile data constraints, and lower digital literacy have been previously identified as factors affecting the acceptability and usability of online alcohol and other drugs support [15, 20, 21, 29]. These factors may shape how regional and remote members interact with Daybreak's features, potentially resulting in more passive or private forms of engagement within the app.

Feature Specific Findings: Interpreting Program Engagement Pathways

Different features of the Daybreak program demonstrated varying associations with risky drinking and psychological distress, highlighting that not all forms of engagement contribute equally to behavior change and that their impact may differ by geography. Use of the Drink Tracker was associated with increased odds of high risky drinking at follow‐up in both regional and remote and major city participants. It could be that high risk drinkers are more likely to engage with drink tracking tools who are at elevated risk of relapse despite increased engagement. In addition, mediation analysis indicated that psychological distress partially explained the relationship between Drink Tracker use and risky drinking, supporting that members with higher baseline distress may be relying on the tool more frequently, but without immediate reductions in risky drinking.

Social engagement features (particularly reacting to posts) were associated with reduced risky drinking in both regional and remote and major city Daybreak members. Participants who reacted to others’ posts more frequently had significantly lower odds of being in a high risky drinking category at follow‐up. Sharing posts, while not directly associated with reduced risky drinking, was significantly linked to reductions in psychological distress. These findings are consistent with the broader literature on digital peer communities, which has shown that perceived support, opportunities for emotional expression and identification with peers are key mechanisms of change [15, 17, 19, 30]. While regional and remote members engaged less frequently with community features, the benefits observed for those who did suggest that peer support may be an important resource.

Care Navigator use was associated with higher psychological distress in major city participants, with no significant association observed in the regional and remote group. This finding is best understood as a reflection of service targeting rather than program harm; that is, individuals with higher levels of distress are more likely to be referred to or seek out professional support through care navigation services. The lack of a significant association in regional and remote participants may suggest lower uptake of this feature in those areas, which warrants further investigation.

Implications for Digital Health Interventions

The findings of this study have several implications for the development and delivery of digital alcohol behavior change interventions. The differential patterns of engagement observed between regional and remote and major city members suggest that a one‐size‐fits‐all model of engagement may not be optimal. Regional and remote participants were less likely to engage with features such as posting or the Drink Tracker but still demonstrated reductions in risky drinking. This suggests that digital health interventions should allow for flexible pathways to engagement, including options that accommodate lower visibility, lower interactivity or more private forms of self‐monitoring.

The finding that reacting to posts (a relatively low effort, low exposure activity) was consistently associated with reduced risky drinking in both major city and regional and remote members highlight the importance of designing for accessible and emotionally safe peer interaction. For members who may be reluctant to post or share personal experiences, such features can still facilitate a sense of community and connection. Interventions could explore ways to amplify the benefits of these lower barrier activities by making them more prominent or rewarding within the app interface.

The role of psychological distress as a mediator of risky drinking suggests that digital programs should treat mental health not merely as a co‐occurring issue, but as a dynamic factor that shapes how members interact with intervention content. Embedding more explicit support for emotional wellbeing, such as mood tracking, guided coping strategies or early distress detection, may enhance program effectiveness, particularly for members with elevated baseline distress or limited offline supports.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, data were self‐reported, which may introduce response bias or underreporting of sensitive behaviors. Second, attrition between baseline and follow‐up could affect the representativeness of the 6‐month outcomes. Third, the regional and remote variable grouped all non‐major city areas together, limiting the ability to distinguish between different levels of rurality (for example, inner regional vs. remote). While the geographic distribution of the sample broadly reflects the Australian population by remoteness, outer regional, remote and very remote communities were modestly underrepresented [31], which may reflect known digital access barriers in more geographically isolated settings. The aggregation of diverse non‐major city regions into a single regional and remote category should therefore be interpreted with caution, as rural populations are heterogeneous and may differ substantially in access to services, digital connectivity, and social context.

Finally, although age and sex were included as covariates in the primary analyses, additional subgroup analyses did not identify clear or consistent differences that altered the main findings. However, subgroup sample sizes, particularly within regional and remote participants, limited the stability of more granular analyses. Future research with larger samples should further examine how demographic factors may shape engagement and outcomes.

Conclusion

This study provides new insights into how individuals living in regional and remote and major city areas engage with and respond to a digital alcohol behavior change program. While both groups demonstrated reductions in risky drinking over time, the types of engagement associated with these outcomes differed. Among regional and remote participants, reacting to others’ posts (but not posting themselves) was linked to reduced risky drinking. Psychological distress partially mediated the relationship between program engagement and risky drinking, reinforcing its importance as both a treatment target and a mechanism of change. These findings highlight the need for digital health interventions to offer flexible, culturally sensitive engagement pathways and to integrate mental health support alongside behavior change tools, particularly when seeking to reach populations who face social or structural barriers to care.
