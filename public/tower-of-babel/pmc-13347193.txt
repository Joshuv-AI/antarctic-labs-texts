Cannabis retailers’ marketing practices and compliance with state regulations: a 2025 point-of-sale audit in 5 U.S. cities
Authors: Carla J. Berg, Cassidy R. LoParco, Matthew E. Rossheim, Morgan B. Speer, Elizabeth Platt, Caitlin Davie, Hermela Amdeta, Darby Mihelich, Diane M. Ndisebuye, Yuxian Cui, Patricia A. Cavazos-Rehg, Y. Tony Yang, Scott Burris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cannabis retail surveillance is crucial to inform regulations and enforcement efforts. This point-of-sale audit study examined marketing practices and compliance with age verification rules, required signage, and marketing restrictions among 161 cannabis retailers in 5 US cities in states authorizing nonmedical cannabis retail (Los Angeles [LA], California; Las Vegas [LV], Nevada; Denver, Colorado; Portland, Oregon; Seattle, Washington) in summer 2025. Descriptive and bivariate analyses characterized retailers overall and across cities. Overall, 84.5 % of retailers requested age verification upon entry (range: Seattle = 43.3 % to LV = 100 %); 8.1 % never verified (range: LV/Denver = 0 % to Seattle = 26.7 %). All 5 states required pregnancy-related warning postings/brochures, observed in 64.6 % (less often in LA = 30.8 % and LV = 46.9 %). Nevada and Oregon required signage prohibiting on-site consumption, observed in 51.6 % overall (LV = 81.3 %, Portland = 86.7 %). California and Washington required signage indicating negative impacts on children/youth, posted in 42.2 % (LA = 20.5 %; Seattle = 80.0 %). All 5 states restricted youth-oriented packaging, found in 24.2 % (more often in Denver = 53.3 % and LV = 40.6 %). Other targeted populations (based on advertisements/promotions) included veterans/military (12.4 %), senior citizens (9.3 %), LGBTQ+ (8.7 %), and racial/ethnic minorities (7.5 %). Colorado, Oregon, and Washington prohibited health claims in advertising, found in 65.8 % (Denver = 40.0 %, Portland = 40.0 %, Seattle = 70.0 %). California, Colorado, and Washington restricted outdoor business signage, identified at 36.6 % overall (LA = 18.2 %, Denver = 7.7 %, Seattle = 40.0 %). California, Oregon, and Washington restricted discounting, identified in 93.8 % (LA = 92.3 %, Portland = 96.7 %, Seattle = 86.7 %). Further, 39.8 % promoted online ordering, 33.5 % curbside delivery, pick-up, or drive-through options, and 13.7 % home delivery. Given compliance-related issues with many regulations (e.g., required signage, prohibitions on youth-oriented marketing, health claims), states’ cannabis retail regulations and enforcement must be strengthened.

Introduction

As of February 2026, 40 states and DC have legalized medical cannabis, and 24 states and DC authorized comprehensive nonmedical (‘recreational’) cannabis programs, most of which allow retail/sales (National Conference, 2026; Temple University Center for Public Health Law Research, 2026). Accordingly, over the past 13 years, nonmedical cannabis regulatory frameworks in the US have emerged and evolved to address the expanding cannabis market (Barry & Glantz, 2018; Hudock, 2019; Lancione et al., 2020; Macnamara, 2020; National Academies of Sciences, 2024; Somerset, 2019; Steinberg et al., 2020; Wang & McKinley, 2019). In 2024, the US cannabis market was $38.5 billion, and is predicted to grow substantially, with nonmedical cannabis comprising more than two-thirds of the market (67 %) (Grandview Research, 2025). Past-month adult-use prevalence has also increased from 6.9 % in 2010 to 16.3 % in 2024, with use and increases particularly high in young adults and certain sociodemographic groups (e.g., racial minorities, lower socioeconomic status [SES]) (National Survey on Drug Use and Health, 2024). As access expands and use increases, effective regulation is an increasingly important public health priority (National Academies of Sciences, 2024). Thus, retail surveillance to understand the cannabis market (e.g., product characteristics), retail practices (e.g., advertising and promotional strategies), and regulatory compliance (e.g., with age verification, marketing restrictions, and required signage) is needed to guide regulatory and enforcement efforts.

Marketing (encompassing product design, pricing, promotions, and advertising) is critical for promoting uptake (American Marketing Association, 2007; Chen et al., 2002; Evans et al., 1995; Gilpin et al., 2007; Hanewinkel et al., 2010; Lovato et al., 2011; Paynter & Edwards, 2009; Pucci & Siegel, 1999; Robertson et al., 2016), continued use,(Burton et al., 2012; Choi et al., 2002; Gilpin et al., 2007), brand loyalty (Pucci & Siegel, 1999), and positive perceptions (American Marketing Association, 2007). Notably, the US cannabis market involves a broad range of products, diverse in form (e.g., flower, vapes, concentrates, edibles, beverages), strain (i.e., indica, sativa, hybrid), potency, and purported effects (e.g., energizing, alleviating pain) (Berg et al., 2017; C. J. Berg et al., 2018; Cavazos-Rehg et al., 2018; Krauss et al., 2017; Loflin & Earleywine, 2014; Luc et al., 2020; Schauer et al., 2016). This diversity has implications for advertising, particularly in terms of youth appeal and health benefit claims (Berg et al., 2017; C. J. Berg et al., 2018; Cavazos-Rehg et al., 2018). Accordingly, most states have tried to restrict underage-targeted marketing (Cavazos-Rehg et al., 2018; Duan et al., 2023), as well as health claims and/or false/misleading claims in advertising (Duan et al., 2023; Romm et al., 2024). Yet, US-based studies have repeatedly documented the prevalence of youth-appealing marketing (Berg Romm et al., 2025; Carla J. Berg et al., 2023; Cui et al., 2024; Duan et al., 2023) and health-related claims (e.g., to relieve pain, insomnia, anxiety) at point-of-sale (POS) (Berg et al., 2017; C. J. Berg et al., 2018; Carla J. Berg et al., 2023; Romm et al., 2024), online (Cavazos-Rehg et al., 2018; Cui et al., 2024; Duan et al., 2023; Krauss et al., 2015), and via retail staff (‘budtender’) communication (Romm et al., 2024). Relatedly, industry marketing targeting certain sociodemographic groups is a concern. Prior research shows that cannabis retailers cluster in neighborhoods with more young adults, racial/ethnic minorities, and lower SES (Cooke et al., 2018; Kepple & Freisthler, 2017; Morrison et al., 2014; Novak et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2016; Thomas & Freisthler, 2016; Unger et al., 2020), and that marketing strategies vary by neighborhood demographics (Berg et al., 2024). For example, retailers in lower-income neighborhoods were more likely to post exterior ads (Berg et al., 2024), those who were Black were exposed to more promotions (Berg Romm et al., 2025), and those who were Black and less educated were exposed to more health claims (Berg Romm et al., 2025). Notably, these exposures were related to more frequent and more problematic cannabis use (Berg Romm et al., 2025).

Also important, more than half of the states that allow cannabis retail prohibit cannabis giveaways, and some restrict price discounting (Temple University Center for Public Health Law Research, 2026). Nonetheless, retail surveillance has documented giveaways and high rates of price promotions (>80 %; e.g., membership clubs, buy one/get one) (Berg et al., 2017; Carla J. Berg et al., 2023; Buller et al., 2016; Cavazos-Rehg et al., 2018; Cui et al., 2024; Duan et al., 2023; Romm et al., 2024). Price promotions are particularly influential on price-sensitive groups, like young adults and lower-SES groups (Chaloupka et al., 2011; Cui et al., 2025; Drake et al., 2024; Xu & Chaloupka, 2011).

Another key regulatory strategy is requiring certain warnings including those related to health risks (e.g., use during pregnancy, potentially habit-forming, driving under the influence [DUI]) and regulations (e.g., prohibited public use, no distribution to minors) posted at retailers, in ads, or on product packages (Temple University Center for Public Health Law Research, 2026). However, such required warning signage at retailers is not always posted. For example, one study found that nearly one-third of retailers failed to post required pregnancy-related warnings and compliance varied significantly across jurisdictions (Carla J. Berg et al., 2023). This study also found that, although required warning signage varied across jurisdictions, the proportion posting warning signage in each jurisdiction did not necessarily reflect whether it was required in that jurisdiction (Carla J. Berg et al., 2023). Furthermore, previous cannabis retail audit studies showed that compliance with regulatory signage varied by sociodemographic characteristics of the neighborhood in which cannabis retailers are located (Berg Romm et al., 2025; Berg et al., 2024). For example, cannabis retailers in neighborhoods with more racial/ethnic minority residents were less likely to post pregnancy warnings (Berg et al., 2024), Black adults reported less exposure to minimum-age signs and age verification at retailers (Berg Romm et al., 2025), and less educated young adults reported less warning exposure at retailers (Berg Romm et al., 2025).

Despite some evidence suggesting the effects of certain nonmedical cannabis regulations (e.g., high age verification rates (Carla J. Berg et al., 2023), promising effects of health warnings (Goodman et al., 2021; Leos-Toro et al., 2021), increased warning signs where required (Carla J. Berg et al., 2023)), important gaps remain in understanding how variations in state laws translate into differences in retailer marketing practices and related compliance with state regulations (Carla J Berg et al., 2018; Noar et al., 2016; Steinberg et al., 2020). Given the nuances of cannabis retail and marketing (Berg et al., 2017; C. J. Berg et al., 2018; Carla J. Berg et al., 2023; Romm et al., 2024), cross-state comparisons are needed to illuminate how different policy environments shape on-the-ground implementation. The most recent multi-state cannabis retail audit study was conducted in 2022 (Carla J. Berg et al., 2023; Berg et al., 2024), underscoring the need to update the literature regarding how regulatory variation relates to cannabis retail environments. This study reports data from cannabis retail audits conducted in summer 2025 assessing regulatory compliance and marketing practices among retailers in 5 cities in US states with different nonmedical cannabis regulations (e.g., in restricted health claims, restricted discounting, and required warning signage).

Methods

Study overview

The current study (deemed exempt by the [omitted for blind review] Institutional Review Board) involved POS audits among cannabis retailers in 5 cities with well-established nonmedical cannabis markets: Denver, Colorado (CO; established in January 2014); Seattle, Washington (WA; July 2014); Portland, Oregon (OR; October 2015); Las Vegas (LV), Nevada (NV; July 2017); and Los Angeles (LA), California (CA; January 2018). Supplementary Table 1 summarizes key state policies as of August 1, 2025; the cited source provides detailed information about legal research and coding methods (Temple University Center for Public Health Law Research, 2026). Particularly relevant, all 5 states restricted youth-oriented marketing and prohibited giveaways, California, Oregon, and Washington restricted discounting, and Colorado, Oregon, and Washington restricted health claims. Additionally, required warnings varied across states, but posted pregnancy-related warnings/brochures were required in all 5 states.

The sample for this study was derived from a longitudinal study of cannabis retailers (Cui et al., 2024; Duan et al., 2023; Romm et al., 2024). In April 2022, nonmedical retail licensees in these 5 cities were identified from each state’s cannabis regulatory agency websites (n = 641). Online searches and phone calls verified eligibility, specifically whether the license had a: 1) currently operating brick-and-mortar storefront (n = 561) and 2) retail website, excluding third-party platforms (e.g., social media, Leafly, n = 541, of which 372 were distinct) (Duan et al., 2023). Forty retailers per city were randomly selected for website audits, then used as the sampling frame for subsequent in-person audits (July-September 2022) among a subset of randomly-selected retailers in each city (n = 30/city) (Romm et al., 2024). Annually (2023, 2024, 2025), we revisited these same websites to determine their eligibility (i.e., actively selling cannabis, connected to a storefront), replacing those ineligible with randomly-selected licensees (Cui et al., 2024). The retailers audited in the 2025 website audits served as the sampling frame for the in-person retail audits in July-August 2025. See Supplementary Fig. 1 for retailer sample flowchart.

Data collection

The audit protocol was adapted from previously-published cannabis retail audit protocols (Berg et al., 2017; C. J. Berg et al., 2018; Carla J. Berg et al., 2023). Study staff pairs entered retail stores without identifying as research staff and recorded data (via electronic forms on iPhones) regarding visibly observable characteristics of the store and within the store setting. Prior to data collection (July 2025), 6 Master’s-level research staff (ages 22–30) participated in a 3-day training, involving didactics, mock audits, field practice, group debriefing, and quizzes. Once data collection began in each city (LA, LV, Denver, Portland, and Seattle, respectively), staff met with project leaders (authors CJB, CRL, MER) to address any issues and ensure data quality, and staff pairs discussed and reconciled any discrepancies.

The audit tool (duration 15–45 min) (Berg et al., 2017; C. J. Berg et al., 2018; Carla J. Berg et al., 2023) is provided in Appendix A and summarized below. Supplementary Table 2 provides definitions of specific constructs/measures, and Tables 1–4 and Supplementary Table 3 show additional details regarding all codes for each measure.

Retailer characteristics.

POS auditors coded: 1) building/structure type (e.g., storefront); 2) outside media types (e.g., light-up signs, digital signs) and inside (e.g., whiteboards, screens, functional items like changepads); 3) retail experiences (e.g., able to see/touch products, stand in line to order, attached to consumer lounge); 4) presence of security cameras and guards; and 5) forms of payment accepted (e.g., cash, credit card).

Product availability.

POS auditors coded: 1) cannabis products sold (e.g., bud/flower, pre-rolls, e-liquids, edibles); 2) other substances sold (e.g., CBD, delta-8 THC) and related ads; and 3) paraphernalia, apparel, or other products sold (e.g., glassware).

Regulation-related factors.

POS auditors entered each store (but did not purchase items) and recorded whether they were asked for age verification (asked for ID upon entry, later, never). POS auditors also recorded: regulation-related signage (e.g., no minors, no onsite consumption); and health-related warning signage (e.g., pregnancy, DUI, health risks).

Cannabis product advertisement/promotion.

POS auditors coded for inside and outside of the store: 1) youth-oriented packaging and youth-oriented signs/ads; 2) signs/promotions targeting specific populations (e.g., young adults, LGBTQ + ); 3) ads/signs indicating health benefits/claims (e.g., sleep/insomnia, pain, anxiety) or other benefits (e.g., creativity, concentration); and 4) other advertising/promotional themes in ads (e.g., pop culture references, flavored products, store awards). See Supplementary Table 2 for definitions and examples of each. “Youth-oriented” and “health benefits/claims” were defined referencing common elements among states’ definitions (see Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). For example, youth-oriented was defined as content “using images of minors (i.e., appearing to be under 21), cartoons, toys, candy, flavors (e.g., fruit), characters (e.g., movie, cartoon), or brands commonly used to market to minors, or popular with minors”.

POS auditors also assessed: 1) cannabis price promotions (e.g., daily/weekly/monthly specials, membership/loyalty/rewards programs); 2) signage regarding online marketing/sales and/or delivery; and 3) take-away materials (e.g., product menus, event flyers, coupons).

Data analysis

Analyses were conducted using SPSS v30.0; significance was set at p < 0.05. To determine initial inter-rater reliability, we computed kappas for categorical variables and intra-class correlations (ICCs) for continuous variables. Almost all initial kappa values exceeded 0.70 (representing good agreement), as in prior research using similar tools (Berg et al., 2017; C. J. Berg et al., 2018; Carla J. Berg et al., 2023). Descriptive and bivariate analyses (Chi-square tests, Fisher’s exact tests) were used to characterize the sample and differences across cities.

Results

Retailer characteristics

Table 1 provides an overview of retailer characteristics, showing a broad range of outdoor and indoor promotional media and diverse retail experiences (e.g., 87.0 % did not allow customers to touch/see products up-close, 57.1 % assigned personnel to customers). Nearly half (44.1 %) had security guards onsite, with LA and LV most frequently (92.3 % and 84.4 %) vs. 23.3 % in Seattle and almost never in Denver (0 %) and Portland (3.3 %). Payment forms included cash (99.4 %) and debit (54.7 %), and nearly all (98.8 %) had ATMs onsite.

Products

All retailers sold bud, pre-rolls, vapes, edibles, concentrates, and topicals; there was diversity in other products, such as beverages (94.4 %, n = 152/161) and tinctures (77.0 %, n = 124/161, less often offered in Denver, 43.3 %, n = 26/30 vs. > 76 % in other cities, p < 0.001). Clones and seeds were uncommon (3.7 %, n = 6/161 and 2.5 %, n = 4/161). In terms of other substances, 3 retailers sold tobacco (LA, LV, Portland), 1 sold alcohol (LV), and 2 sold hemp-derived THC products (e.g., delta-8 THC) (Denver, Seattle). Over half sold rolling papers (91.3 %), glassware (91.3 %), and/or apparel (58.4 %).

Regulatory compliance and regulation-related signage

Most retailers (84.5 %) complied with state laws requiring customers to show ID upon entry (range: Seattle: 43.3 % to LV: 100 %). However, 8.1 % did not ask research staff for ID at any time during the audit visit (range: LV/Denver: 0 % to Seattle: 26.7 %, p < 0.001).

Nevada and Oregon required signage indicating no onsite consumption, observed in 51.6 % overall, 81.3 % in LV, and 86.7 % in Portland (Table 2). Nevada was the only state that required posted signs indicating no transport across state lines, possession limits, and no distribution to minors; such signs were present in 84.4 % of LV retailers, respectively.

All 5 states required health warning signage or hand-outs at POS regarding use during pregnancy; compliance was observed in 64.6 % of retailers, least frequently in LA (30.8 %) and LV (46.9 %) and more frequently (>83 %) in the other 3 cities (p < 0.001; Table 2). Across all 5 cities, 42.2 % posted warning signage indicating negative impacts on children/youth, only required in California and Washington where 20.5 % and 80.0 % were compliant (p < 0.001).

Advertising and promotional strategies

Although all states had restrictions related to youth-oriented marketing, 24.2 % of stores audited were observed to sell products in youth-oriented packaging, most frequently in Denver (53.5 %) and LV (40.6 %) vs. ≤ 20 % elsewhere (p < 0.001; Table 3). Also, 8.1 % displayed youth-oriented signage. Other targeted populations (based on advertisements/promotions) included veterans/military (12.4 %), senior citizens (9.3 %), LGBTQ+ (8.7 %), and racial/ethnic minority groups (7.5 %).

Although Colorado, Oregon, and Washington explicitly prohibited health claims in advertising, 40.0 % of retailers in Denver, 40.0 % in Portland, and 70.0 % in Seattle had signage with health claims (Table 3). The most common health benefits promoted included sleep/insomnia (60.9 %), pain (31.1 %), mood/depression/coping (14.9 %), general health/wellness (13.7 %), and anxiety (11.8 %). Other promoted psychological benefits included relaxation (55.3%), energy (29.8 % %), attention/focus (15.5 %), and creativity (15.5 %).

Ads were wide-ranging in content: 87.6 % featured price promotions, 41.0 % flavored products, 24.2% free products/giveaways, 18.6 % pop culture references, 11.2 % known alcohol brands (e.g., PBR, Lagunitas) as brands for cannabis products, 10.6 % celebrity endorsements, 10.6 % holidays or related products/promotions, and 8.1 % references to products being organic/natural.

State laws restricted outdoor business signage in California, Colorado, and Washington, but signs were observed at 36.6 % overall, 18.2 % in LA, 7.7 % in Denver, and 40.0 % in Seattle (p < 0.001). These exterior ads primarily featured price promotions (27.3 %) or the retailer’s home delivery service and/or online presence (e.g., website, social media; 5.6 %; not shown in tables).

All 5 states prohibited giveaway products, and California, Oregon, and Washington also restrict discounting. Despite these regulations, there were a couple of incidents of free samples/gifts (n = 2), and some type of discount/price promotion was available at 93.8 % overall, 92.3 % in LA, 96.7 % in Portland, and 86.7 % in Seattle. The most common discounts/price promotions were daily/weekly/monthly specials (72.0 %), membership/loyalty/rewards programs (52.2 %), happy hour/early bird specials (26.7 %), punch cards (25.5 %), or buy one/get one specials (21.1 %). Several of these were particularly prominent in LV (Table 4).

Other promotional strategies were also observed: 52.2 % promoted their website or social media page, 39.8 % promoted online ordering, 33.5 % provided curbside delivery, pick-up, or drive-through, and 13.7 % home delivery. Additionally, 71.4 % had take-away materials, most often product/company-branded flyers/materials (41.6 %), magazines/newsletters (28.6 %), retail/store-branded flyers/materials (23.0 %), information on use-related warnings/risks (11.8 %, particularly prominent in Denver: 36.7 %), and information about obtaining medical cards (9.9 %).

Discussion

Audits of cannabis retailers in 5 US cities in summer 2025 show, overall, a concerning degree of non-compliance with state laws governing cannabis retail. However, the variation among cities suggests high levels of compliance are feasible for retailers and attainable by regulators.

All the states included in this study had policies limiting marketing with youth appeal and access among those underage. While most retailers (85 %) asked for age verification immediately upon entry, similar to other studies (Berg et al., 2017; C. J. Berg et al., 2018; Buller et al., 2019; Lenk et al., 2020), ~10 % of retailers did not ask research staff for age verification at any time. Compliance was highest in LV and lowest in Seattle, despite no differences in required age verification regulations (Temple University Center for Public Health Law Research, 2026). Age-verification is a straightforward, clear legal requirement. In contrast, limitations on youth-oriented marketing and packaging are difficult to draft and have been subject to free speech challenges, making them potentially more difficult to enforce (Swinburne et al., 2024). Although all states had restrictions related to youth-oriented marketing (Temple University Center for Public Health Law Research, 2026), one-fourth had youth-oriented packaging, which was documented in over half of Denver retailers and 40 % of LV retailers – despite no substantial differences from the other states in youth-related marketing restrictions (Temple University Center for Public Health Law Research, 2026). Furthermore, nearly 10 % displayed youth-oriented signage, with ~20 % of LV retailers posting such signage. Additionally, ~40 % posted signage/ads promoting flavored products, which is a salient feature of cannabis marketing (especially for edibles) (Tan et al., 2022) and may appeal to younger populations in particular (Chaffee et al., 2022; Tan et al., 2022; Villanti et al., 2019).

Over half of retailers promoted their website or social media page (despite restrictions on cannabis advertising on most social media platforms (C. J. Berg et al., 2023)), which is concerning given that social media has high reach to those underage (Ling et al., 2022), can increase positive attitudes towards cannabis and use onset (Cabrera-Nguyen et al., 2016; Cavazos-Rehg et al., 2014; Jackson et al., 2018; Krauss et al., 2017; Paschall & Grube, 2020; Trangenstein et al., 2021), and is typically not age-gated and that even age-gated cannabis retail websites are often ineffective at preventing underage access (Goldfarb, 2018; Madson, 2023). In addition, similar to or higher than rates noted previously (Carla J. Berg et al., 2023; Cavazos-Rehg et al., 2018), ~40 % had signs/promotions indicating online ordering or pickup/delivery options, which could facilitate underage access, due to ease of circumventing age verification and/or gaps in implementing age verification requirements for cannabis deliveries (Ebling et al., 2025; Gaiha et al., 2020).

We observed targeting of other subpopulations, including LGBTQ + and racial/ethnic minority groups, which has been documented previously in cannabis and other substance marketing studies (National Academies of Sciences, 2024; U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2024). However, current findings suggested that the prevalence of marketing targeting these groups was lower than marketing targeting 2 other subgroups – veterans/military and senior citizens. Research has documented increases in cannabis use and use disorder among veteran/military populations (Hasin et al., 2025; Livne et al., 2024). For example, studies using Veterans Health Administration data found that use disorder prevalence increased from ~3.5 % in 2005 to ~6.4 % in 2022 (Hasin et al., 2025), and 10 % past-month use in those 65–84 in 2020–2023, with ~30 % using for medical reasons (e.g., pain, mental health, sleep) (Pravosud et al., 2025). Studies of older populations using other data sources (e.g., National Poll on Healthy Aging (Fernandez et al., 2023), NSDUH (Han et al., 2025)) increased in recent years, with 12 % reporting past-year use in 2021 (of which 34 % used ≥ 4 days/week) (Fernandez et al., 2023). Thus, research is needed to better understand the role of the cannabis marketing/retail in promoting use in these populations.

Despite health claim restrictions in advertising in Colorado, Oregon, and Washington, these were common (40 % in Denver and Portland, 70 % in Seattle), often related to sleep/insomnia and pain, which may be particularly appealing to certain populations (e.g., veterans/military, older populations (Fernandez et al., 2023; Han et al., 2025; Hasin et al., 2025; Pravosud et al., 2025)). These audits observed signs and printed materials, and thus may underestimate health claims conveyed verbally at the time of sale (Dickson et al., 2018; Romm et al., 2024). One mystery shopper study showed that > 94 % of retail staff endorsed use for anxiety, insomnia, and pain, and over half for pregnancy-related nausea (Romm et al., 2024). Interestingly, there were no differences in the prevalence of health claims in states with vs. without such restrictions, suggesting that health claims are seen by retailers as a particularly effective marketing strategy regardless of the state regulations (Carla J. Berg et al., 2023; Cavazos-Rehg et al., 2018). Other benefits were also promoted, such as for relaxation, energy, and attention/focus, which has become increasingly salient (Reboussin et al., 2024) and may appeal to different groups (e.g., younger individuals (Reboussin et al., 2024; Stueber & Cuttler, 2022)).

Price promotions were a particularly prevalent aspect of the retail environment: ~90 % had price promotions (especially daily/weekly/monthly specials and membership/loyalty/rewards programs) and interior signs/ads featuring price promotions (including for free products/giveaways among one-fourth), consistent with prior research (Berg et al., 2017; Carla J. Berg et al., 2023; Buller et al., 2016; Cavazos-Rehg et al., 2018). Furthermore, discounts were the most prominent focus of outdoor advertising. The salience of price promotions within the cannabis industry is concerning, given their impact on use (Choi et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2022), particularly among price-sensitive subpopulations (e.g., young adults, women, lower-SES individuals) (Cui et al., 2025; Liber et al., 2022). Moreover, discounting was restricted in California, Oregon, and Washington (Temple University Center for Public Health Law Research, 2026) but was nonetheless frequent in these locations (ranging from 87 % in Seattle to 97 % in Portland), and outdoor signage was restricted in California, Colorado, and Washington (Temple University Center for Public Health Law Research, 2026), yet compliance ranged from 60 % in Seattle to 92 % in Denver.

Compliance with required regulation-related signage was mixed. For example, despite all 5 states requiring pregnancy-related health warnings (Temple University Center for Public Health Law Research, 2026), over one-third of retailers failed to post such signage, which was particularly problematic in LA and LV where nearly 70 % and 55 %, respectively, were noncompliant. Additionally, Nevada and Oregon required signage forbidding onsite consumption (Temple University Center for Public Health Law Research, 2026), but 15–20 % of retailers in LV and Portland failed to comply. Furthermore, 80 % of LA retailers and 20 % of Seattle retailers were noncompliant with required signage indicating negative impacts on children/youth (Temple University Center for Public Health Law Research, 2026). Compliance with other signage requirements was generally higher (e.g., LV retailers were 85 % compliant with required signs indicating no transport across state lines, possession limits, and no distribution to minors) (Temple University Center for Public Health Law Research, 2026).

Notably, warning signage prevalence was generally higher where it was required. However, there is limited evidence regarding the effectiveness of such signage (Berg Ruchelli et al., 2025), which is especially important to consider given the differences in the types and number of state-required signage (Temple University Center for Public Health Law Research, 2026). Existing research has shown mixed findings (Berg Ruchelli et al., 2025; Britt et al., 2006; Budenz et al., 2022; Coady et al., 2013; Forster et al., 1994; Wolfson & Poole, 2023; Wolfson et al., 1996), and little research has examined how much of this information is noticed, understood, or retained by consumers (Willis, 2015). Thus, future research is needed to examine consumers’ attention to and comprehension of different types and numbers of warning-related information, including at POS, on packaging, etc.

Levels of non-compliance detected in this study were comparable to those found in similar studies of point-of-sale regulations of alcohol and tobacco products across decades (Feltmann et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2014; Wagenaar et al., 1999). In those industries, research on strategies to detect violations and promote greater compliance have provided useful guidance for regulators and improved industry compliance (Wagenaar et al., 1999). Further research and intervention work in cannabis should draw on this literature (Feltmann et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2014).

Limitations

Limitations include retailer representativeness (i.e., 161 retailers in 5 US cities) and, thus, generalizability of findings. Additionally, we did not account for local ordinances, and some state regulations, like those pertaining to youth-oriented ads or packaging, are difficult to operationalize, thus requiring caution when evaluating compliance with such regulations. Further, because audit forms were not tailored to each site, certain factors (e.g., health claims, youth-oriented signage) were coded using harmonized definitions derived from shared elements of regulatory language across states. Nonetheless, this study has key strengths, as it included a relatively large, diverse sample of cannabis retailers across multiple states and links their practices to state laws – providing timely insights on cannabis retail practices across cities with different regulations.

Conclusions

Findings highlight concerns about cannabis retailers’ regulatory compliance and marketing strategies, including gaps in age verification, noncompliance with required signage, and marketing strategies (e.g., health claims, youth-oriented products/ads, price promotions) that may differentially impact certain subpopulations (e.g., underage, veterans/military, older adults, lower-SES). Differences across cities sometimes, but not always, reflected variations in regulations, indicating implementation and enforcement gaps. Thus, as cannabis markets continue to emerge and evolve, ongoing surveillance is necessary to inform regulatory and enforcement efforts.
