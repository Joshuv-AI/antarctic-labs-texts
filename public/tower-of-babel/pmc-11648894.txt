Autonomous phototaxis of hydrogel swimmers
Authors: S. Doruk Cezan, Aaveg Aggarwal, Chuang Li, Hang Yuan, Liam C. Palmer, Monica Olvera de la Cruz, Samuel I. Stupp
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThis study reports on the design of hydrogel materials that mimic living organisms’ abilities to adapt to their environment. Specifically, we developed a hybrid hydrogel combining supramolecular nanofibers and photoresponsive networks with aligned ferromagnetic nanowires that can swim under oscillating magnetic fields. This hydrogel swimmer can autonomously sense and move toward a light source, a phenomenon reminiscent of phototactic behavior found in nature. By combining experimental techniques and theoretical modeling, we analyzed the observed swimming mechanism and the emergence of phototactic properties. This work highlights how the synergistic interplay of covalent and supramolecular networks in materials can be used to emulate the complex behaviors of living systems.

Soft materials that incorporate multiple responsive components to external stimuli have the potential to emulate the properties of living organisms (1–10). Among all the remarkable properties of living organisms, translational motion extending from tropic and nastic behaviors of plants (11–14) to tactic behaviors of bacteria (15, 16) and animals (17–19) has been of great interest since these movements are essential components of life. These behaviors of living systems often emerge from the hierarchical self-assembly of nanoscale building blocks (20–22). Given their similarity to living tissues and ability to exchange fluids in their aqueous environment, soft hydrogels have been an ideal candidate to emulate such behaviors observed in biological systems (9, 23–25). It is well known that functionalized hydrogels can undergo phase transitions, and shape transformations in response to a wide range of external stimuli as their molecular and supramolecular structural changes, like temperature (25, 26), pH (27, 28), chemicals (29, 30), light (9, 31, 32), and magnetic fields (7, 10, 33). Among the wide range of known stimuli-responsive soft materials (34), only a few systems have demonstrated the advantages of the interplay between the dynamic behavior of noncovalent assemblies and functionalized covalent networks to design life-like matter (35, 36). For example, we recently reported on “hybrid bonding polymers” (HBPs), in which covalent and noncovalent bonding among structural units coexist, integrating the dynamic structural properties of supramolecular systems and the robustness of covalent polymer networks (9, 23, 25, 37, 38). We previously demonstrated that supramolecular nanofibers enhance performance in thermoresponsive and photoresponsive HBP actuators facilitated by water diffusion and mechanical anisotropy (9, 25). In a separate study, we introduced high-aspect-ratio ferromagnetic nanowires into a covalent polymer network that responds to light and rotating magnetic fields, enabling fast walking motion under water (10). However, none of these systems integrate dynamic control through structural design to obtain autonomous behaviors resembling those of living organisms. We report here on a hybrid hydrogel swimmer that responds to both light and magnetic fields by incorporating noncovalent assemblies and ferromagnetic nanowires into a light-responsive network. The hydrogel is made of a photoresponsive HBP consisting of peptide amphiphile nanofibers and a covalent polymer containing a photoactive spiropyran monomer. Nickel nanowires are embedded in the hydrogel matrix to enable a fast physical response to magnetic fields. We used feature tracking on video recordings of the hydrogels’ actuation and swimming, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), confocal microscopy, rheology, and nanoindentation to understand the critical functional roles of supramolecular assemblies. We also developed a model using a continuum approach to analyze the interaction of the hybrid hydrogel with external light and magnetic fields. This work highlights how a hybrid bonding hydrogel can utilize the synergistic interplay of covalent and supramolecular networks to generate autonomous functions in materials that emulate living systems.

Results and Discussion

Design of the Hydrogel Swimmers.

To prepare HBP hydrogels containing both a covalent network and a supramolecular polymer, we began by synthesizing a peptide amphiphile containing a polymerizable methacrylamide group (PA1) at the amino terminus (N terminus; C16V3A3E3K-methacrylamide) in order to make covalent connections between the supramolecular nanofibers and the covalent network. PA nanofibers form through the coassembly of PA 1 and non-functionalized PA 2 (C16V3A3E3-NH2) in a 1:1 molar ratio, enabling control over the density of methacrylamide groups on their surfaces (Fig. 1). Next, we introduced ferromagnetic nickel nanowires (Ni NWs), synthesized via electrodeposition (39) in anodic aluminum oxide (AAO) templates with a pore diameter of 200 nm (Fig. 1B), into a prepolymer solution consisting of supramolecular PA nanofibers, N-isopropyl acrylamide (NIPAM) monomer 1), N,N′-methylene bisacrylamide (MBA) crosslinking agent 2), acrylic acid 3), diphenyl(2,4,6-trimethyl benzoyl)phosphine oxide (DPPO) photoinitiator 4), and a merocyanine-containing monomer 5) in a dioxane/water solution (Fig. 1A; see Materials and Methods and SI Appendix, section 4). This prepolymer solution is then transferred into a custom-made glass slide photopolymerization vessel designed to create hydrogel films with ~0.5 mm thickness after photopolymerization. In this photopolymerization container, ferromagnetic Ni nanowires were aligned using a static, uniform magnetic field and subsequently fixed in place via free-radical photopolymerization, embedding the ferromagnetic nanowires within the photoresponsive crosslinked hybrid polymer network (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, section 4). Confocal microscopy of the hybrid hydrogels reveals the incorporation of all components, as shown by regions in which Ni NWs (black) and PA nanofiber (red; labeled through coassembly with a molar ratio of 49.5:49.5:1 of PA 1, PA 2, and PA 3 [C16V3A3E3K-TAMRA]) clusters are aligned and distributed throughout the material (Fig. 1D). SEM images of the HBP network revealed the pore structure (55.7% porosity with ~3.1 μm2 average pore size) of the hybrid hydrogels (Fig. 1E). The as-prepared hydrogel films were cut into the designed sample geometry, whose shape consisted of two slabs (13.5 × 5 × 0.5 mm) joined by a thinner curved bridge (3 × 2 × 0.5 mm) such that the nanowires were aligned along the length of the samples as depicted in Fig. 1H to explore the swimming behavior of the hydrogels under the influence of an oscillating magnetic field. This geometry was chosen to concentrate stress, elastic deformation, and resulting bending in the middle of the samples to facilitate flapping motion. We found that the hydrogels perform oscillatory motion, rather than the desired flapping motion, under oscillating magnetic fields along the z-axis due to the anisotropic magnetization profile formed during photopolymerization. Therefore, the hydrogels were remagnetized by exposure to one pole of a strong magnet (cylindrical NdFeB, 1.48 T) to achieve a radially symmetric magnetization profile (Fig. 1C). These hydrogels with a radially symmetric magnetization profile achieve the flapping motion, as depicted in Fig. 1I. The effect of the magnetization profile on the swimmer’s motion pattern is shown in SI Appendix, section 4 (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 and Movies S9–S11). Lastly, we introduced an approximately 0.5 µL air bubble underwater in the center of the thin bridge using the plastic tip of a positive displacement pipette (Fig. 1A). This bubble, which remains stable in its position, is necessary to increase buoyancy of the samples given the weight of the nickel nanowires. The air bubble effectively reduces the sample’s density from ~1.05 to ~ 1.02 g cm–3. Details of bubble attachment and the effect of potential deviation from the central point are provided in SI Appendix, section 4 and Movies S12 and S13. The photoswitch can adopt two distinct isomeric forms depending on light exposure, one that it is hydrophilic in the dark (merocyanine, MCH+, λmax = 404 nm, t1/2ring-closing = 47 s at 282 mW/cm2 LED λem = 480 nm) and hydrophobic under illumination (spiropyran, SP, t1/2ring-opening = 73 s in dark) (9, 10). The photon-induced conversion of MCH+ to SP in the network creates a gradient in which the portion of the hydrogel closest to the irradiated surface contains primarily hydrophobic SP moieties, while regions further away contain charged MCH+ groups (see SI Appendix, Fig. S1 for UV-vis spectra of ring-opening and ring-closing processes of the photoactive moiety). This results in a lower water concentration within the material where SP units increase, leading in turn to mechanical deformation and bending. Given this photoresponsive behavior of these hydrogels (9, 10), we exposed samples to a light source to obtain a curved shape that would allow us to emulate a swimming jellyfish (Fig. 1J). In the presence of external magnetic fields, the magnetization of the hydrogel causes the sample to deform. When exposed to oscillating magnetic fields along the z-axis, the hydrogel exhibits a flapping motion similar to the swimming behavior of a jellyfish (Movie S1). Our experimental results show that the flapping motion generates propulsion and underwater swimming behavior of the hydrogel robot as shown in Fig. 2.

To quantitatively analyze the deformation of the hydrogel material in response to both light and magnetic fields, we developed a continuum model. The total free energy density of the hydrogel is given by a combination of five terms, that is,[1]Ugel=Uelastic+Umixing+Ulight+Umagnetic+Ureinforcement.

Here, Uelastic accounts for the elastic energy density of the hydrogel’s polymer network and Umixing accounts for the interaction between the polymer and the solvent. Uelastic and Umixing are given by the affine network model and the Flory–Huggins model, respectively (40).[2]Uelastic=12kBTvmNxI1-3,[3]Umixing=kBTvm1-ϕϕln1-ϕ+χ1-ϕ,

where Nx is the average number of monomers between two crosslinking junctions of the polymer network, vm and ϕ are the volume and volume fraction of monomers respectively, I1 is the trace of the Cauchy–Green tensor, and χ is the parameter that describes the interaction between monomers and solvent molecules.

The photoisomerization reaction interacts with hydrogel’s mechanics via the interaction energy, Ulight which is proportional to the concentration of the hydrophobic spiropyran molecules (41, 42), that is,[4]Ulight=kBTvmαrsp1-ϕ,

where, α is used as a fitting parameter and rsp is the normalized concentration of spiropyran molecules (see SI Appendix, section 8 for details on the photochemical reaction). The magnetic response of the hydrogel due to the presence of nickel nanowires is modeled by the magnetic free energy density, Umagnetic (43).[5]Umagnetic=-FM·B

where, B is the magnetic flux density; F is the deformation gradient tensor and M is the magnetization of the material in the initial configuration. The embedded nanowires also reinforce the hydrogel and affect its elastic properties. The anisotropic elastic response of the material is given by Ureinforcement (43), where[6]Ureinforcement=12kBTvmNxγA·FTFA-12

Here, γ is a constant parameter that characterizes the reinforcement and A is a unit vector in the direction of the alignment of the nickel nanowires. The first Piola–Kirchhoff stress tensor is therefore given by P=∂Ugel/∂F, where Ugel defined in Eq. 1. The non-linear elastic model is solved using the finite element method (FEM) (see SI Appendix, section 8 for the numerical implementation). The results of our calculations confirmed that the oscillating magnetic field along the z-axis can cause the slabs of the sample to oscillate along with the field as shown by the schematic in Fig. 1I, allowing the “flapping” motion of the hydrogel, in agreement with experimental observations (SI Appendix, section 8). Periodic flapping motion generates swimming behavior of the hydrogel robot as explained by the fluid–structure interaction (FSI) simulations (Fig. 2). The governing equations for the fluid motion are given by the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations,ρ∂u∂t+u·∇u=-∇p+∇·μ∇u+∇uT+ρg,[7]∇·u=0,

where ρ,u,p,μ are the mass density, the velocity, the pressure, and the dynamic viscosity of the fluid respectively, and ρg is the volumetric force density due to gravity. The computational domain for the fluid changes as the fluid boundary moves along with the swimmer. Therefore, the equation of fluid motion is solved using the moving mesh method defined within the Arbitrary Lagrangian–Eulerian (ALE) framework (44), where all the equations are solved on a freely deformable moving mesh. The advantage of using the ALE method is that it easily handles the changing domain boundaries. The fluid and the swimmer interact via mechanical forces. The swimmer acts like a moving wall for the fluid domain, that is,[8]ub=∂xgel∂t,

where ∂xgel/∂t is the velocity of the hydrogel and ub is the velocity of the fluid at the boundary. Simultaneously, the motion of the fluid produces a force f on the swimmer body given by,[9]f=-n.-pI+μ∇u+∇uT,

where n is the unit normal vector to the hydrogel’s surface and I is the identity matrix. This allows us to resolve the two-way coupling between the fluid and the swimmer’s motion in three dimensions. This interaction between them generates thrust and gives rise to the swimming behavior.

Swimming Mechanism.

To understand the swimming mechanism, we tracked specific features in the videos for two propulsion cycles of the swimmer. We plotted the horizontal (x-axis) and the vertical (z-axis) components of the trajectories of the air bubble in the center (Fig. 2B), and the ends of the Left (Fig. 2C) and the Right (Fig. 2D) arms, demonstrating a vertical displacement of the swimmer. Each cycle of the periodic flapping motion of the swimmer is separated into two phases, the power stroke, and the recovery stroke. During the power stroke the swimmer closes its arms to push against the water and generates an upward thrust due to the drag force, and during the recovery stroke, the arms open and the drag force acts in the opposite direction. Therefore, to generate net upward motion, the power stroke should generate more thrust than the recovery stroke (Fig. 2A and Movie S1). Consequently, the shape of the swimmer becomes critical in determining its motion. For example, a flat initial geometry as shown in Fig. 1H, generates zero net thrust due to the symmetric nature of its power and recovery strokes (Fig. 1I and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 and sections 4 and 8). In contrast, a curved geometry can break this symmetry and undergo locomotion as shown in Fig. 2. We can achieve this geometric curvature using a light source placed at the bottom of the tank, causing the hydrogel to bend toward the light (Fig. 1J) (9, 10). For the sample shown in Fig. 2, the curvature was created by light irradiation with an intensity of 48 mW/cm2 for 30 s which resulted in a ~2 mm net vertical displacement of the swimmer per propulsion cycle and the film can undergo repeated expansion and contraction without rupturing or significant deformation (Movies S3 and S4). It is important to note that both magnetic and light stimuli are necessary for the hydrogel swimmer’s propulsion, as magnetic fields alone cause flapping without upward movement, and light alone induces curvature without flapping (Movies S9 and S14). A significant difference in arm lengths, resulting in high asymmetry, can greatly disturb the swimmer’s propulsion. When the longer arm touches the bottom of the container, it disrupts the smooth flapping motion, causing uneven propulsion along both the vertical and lateral axes. Meanwhile, the shorter arm swings freely but fails to generate sufficient thrust on its own. This imbalance hinders the coordinated contraction and expansion cycles required for effective propulsion, leading to chaotic and inefficient movement (Movie S15). The behavior of the swimmer was reproduced using the FEM simulation shown in Fig. 2I. Based on the density of the material (>1 g/cm3), we would expect the swimmer to sink under the influence of gravity. To study the displacement of the swimmer purely due to generated thrust, we slightly lower the mass of the swimmer in the numerical simulations. We match the force exerted by the air bubble (applied as a point force) to the weight of the swimmer (applied as a body force) such that in the absence of oscillating magnetic fields, the swimmer does not undergo displacement. These experimental and computational results indicate that the swimming mechanism is associated with drag-based propulsion and the geometric curvature created by light is the reason for the upward propulsion and displacement along the z-axis (Fig. 2 E and G).

Phototactic Swimming.

When the swimmer is placed on top of the primary light source under oscillating magnetic fields, it performs upward swimming motion because of consecutive propulsion cycles and the curvature in the swimmer’s geometry created by the primary light source located under the water tank. Fig. 3A shows the upward swimming behavior over 16 propulsion cycles under a magnetic field (16 mT) oscillating along the z-axis with a curvature created by a light source placed underneath the water tank. We applied an intense light source (48 mW/cm2) for 30 s to establish the curvature, then reduced the intensity to 4.7 mW/cm2 for the rest of the experiment. We selected the bubble as the center (gray), and the edges of the left (blue) and right (red) arms as the swimmer’s features. We tracked the trajectories of these features to analyze the swimming over 16 propulsion cycles. We found that the swimmer can perform upward swimming with no significant change in displacement (<0.5 mm) along the x-axis (Fig. 3A). Upon introduction of a second light source, we observed positive phototaxis by recording the swimmer’s response to an additional beam of light positioned on the left side of the water tank. We performed the same feature tracking procedure on a video of 17 propulsion cycles of the swimmer with a secondary light source (Fig. 3B). The swimmer exhibited positive phototactic behavior with a significant change in displacement (up to 25 mm) along the x-axis toward the light source compared to the one with no secondary light source (Fig. 3 B and C). The theoretical model was used to demonstrate that irradiation with a second light source changes the geometry of the swimmer. The background light source at the bottom of the water tank symmetrically irradiates both arms, causing the same change in shape. However, due to the placement of the secondary light source, the swimmer’s arms get irradiated differently causing the left and the right arms to exhibit (slightly) different shapes. To understand the contributions of the geometry, we initially simulated this behavior purely based on the change in geometry. Surprisingly, the swimmer with an asymmetric deformation of the arms, that is, a slight flattening of the arm near the light, exhibited negative phototactic behavior. This indicates that the geometry does not contribute to the positive phototactic swimming, and would, in fact, promote negative phototaxis. We therefore modified the model to introduce a higher stiffness in the arm closer to the light source and indeed found it was consistent with our experimental observation of positive phototactic behavior (Fig. 4 A and B). We investigated whether indeed the light causes stiffness and propose that the physical explanation for the positive phototaxis is a diminished thrust in the stiffer arm near the light source. The ability of this hydrogel hybrid to reversibly change its local stiffness in response to light enables directional control of the swimmer by modulating stiffness in specific regions. This can be effectively described as autonomous behavior rooted in the internal structure of the material.

Light-Induced Stiffening.

Supramolecular nanoscale fibers enhance the mechanical properties of hydrogels due to their persistence length and stiffness (6, 7). In our previous work, we adjusted the monomer-to-crosslinker molar ratio to achieve comparable mechanical properties (viscoelastic behavior) in hydrogels containing only covalent polymeric networks (6). Hydrogels with higher crosslinking densities and without the PA nanofibers have storage and loss moduli comparable to those containing 0.016 mol % PA nanofibers with a lower crosslinker density (SI Appendix, section 5). We hypothesize that the presence of supramolecular nanofibers enhances the hydrogels’ response to light facilitated by proton exchange reactions between the photoactive moiety (MCH+) and charged amino acid residues in the supramolecular assemblies (Fig. 1G). Under irradiation, the hydrophilic MCH+ moieties convert to the hydrophobic SP form, thus releasing protons that can neutralize the ionized glutamate residues in C16V3A3E3 monomers as well as acrylic acid structural units in the covalent network. Neutralizing the negatively charged nanofibers causes them to collapse into bundles as electrostatic repulsion diminishes, thus causing significant stiffening of the hydrogel under illumination, as demonstrated by rheological studies (Fig. 4 and see SI Appendix, section 5). We let the hydrogel swimmer perform flapping cycles of its left arm while fixing its right arm on a flat surface to observe the effect of light-induced stiffening. We tracked the trajectory of the free arm under oscillating magnetic fields (16 mT at 0.5 Hz) in the z-axis under dark conditions and under illumination with a 48 mW/cm2 light source using a video of five flapping cycles at 30 frames per second (Fig. 4 A and B). We found that the arm under light irradiation performs flapping cycles with a shorter range of motion in both the x- and z-components compared to the arm in the dark (Fig. 4 C and D). The average angular range of motion of the arm is ~1.5 times less under the irradiation of light. We inspected the change of velocity over time for both conditions and found that the average velocity of flapping is ~1.6 times less under light irradiation (Fig. 4G). We also performed a FEM simulation to verify this observation and calculated fluid velocity. We found that the range of angular motion and fluid velocity generated by the arm decreases as the material’s stiffness increases (Fig. 4I). Our experimental and theoretical findings suggest that light-induced stiffening indeed leads to a decrease in the range of motion and velocity of the arms, which results in less motion of the fluid around it as confirmed by the numerical results. These findings indicate that the arm under light illumination generates less propulsion than the arm under dark conditions.

Conclusions

In this study, we demonstrated hybrid hydrogel materials composed of supramolecular nanofibers and ferromagnetic nanowires integrated into a photoresponsive covalent network exhibit complex phototactic swimming behavior in response to light and magnetic fields. The propulsion mechanism of the hydrogel swimmer involves drag facilitated by a “flapping” motion induced in oscillating magnetic fields and geometrical curvature induced by light. Also, the interaction between the supramolecular nanofibers and the photoresponsive covalent networks leads to local stiffening of the hybrid material under light irradiation. Therefore, these hydrogels exhibit positive phototactic swimming upon the introduction of a secondary light source. Unlike phototropic behaviors in previously reported synthetic systems, such as simple bending or actuation, the ability of the material to autonomously recognize a light source is rooted in changes of local stiffness induced by structural changes driven by photons. Overall, this research highlights the potential of the synergistic interplay between supramolecular assemblies and covalent networks offering means to the development of sophisticated intelligent and autonomous robotic soft matter.

Materials and Methods

All chemicals used for SP synthesis were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Thermo Fisher Scientific, or other commercial sources. Peptide amphiphiles were synthesized using standard 9-fluorenylmethoxycarbonyl (Fmoc) solid-phase peptide synthesis on the 1 mmol scale using Fmoc–Rink amide 4-methyl benzhydrylamine hydrochloride (MBHA) resin (synthetic details are provided in SI Appendix, section 3). Detailed synthesis and characterization of SP molecules are provided in SI Appendix, section 1. Nickel nanowires were synthesized by adapting a protocol described previously by Bentley et al. (39), and detailed preparation protocols and characterization are given in SI Appendix, section 2.

For self-assembly, peptide amphiphiles were dissolved in water to achieve a final concentration of 1 wt.%, followed by annealing in a water bath (heating at 80 °C for 30 min, then cooling to room temperature overnight). For coassembly, different molar ratios of PA 1 or PA 3 relative to filler PA 2 were dissolved in water to a total concentration of 1 wt.% and annealed following the same protocol.

The photopolymerization setup consisted of a transparent water bath kept at room temperature that held the molds. Samples with Ni nanowires oriented within the plane of the films were placed in the photopolymerization setup and oriented parallel to the direction of the magnetic field. The samples were prepolymerized by irradiation with a pair of 100-W LEDs to increase the solution’s viscosity and reduce aggregation of the Ni nanowires. Details and schematic descriptions of the setups are provided in SI Appendix, section 4.

For upward swimming experiments, samples were irradiated with a light intensity of 48 mW/cm2 from below for 10 s, causing the hydrogel objects to obtain initial curvature. For phototactic swimming experiments, samples were exposed to a light intensity of 48 mW/cm2, similar to upward swimming experiments, but this time, a secondary light source from the side was introduced. A magnetic field ± 16.0 mT oscillating at 0.5 Hz in the z-axis was used for all experiments to cause flapping motion. For all magnetic actuation and locomotion experiments, detailed schematic descriptions of the setups are provided in SI Appendix, section 4.

Eqs. 1–9 was solved using COMSOL Multiphysics’ solid mechanics and computational fluid dynamics modules (see SI Appendix, section 8 for details).
