On the Maximization of Real Sequences
Assalé Adjé

Abstract
In this paper, we study a maximization problem on real sequences. More precisely, for a given sequence, we are interesting in computing the supremum of the sequence and an index for which the associated term is maximal. We propose a general methodology to solve this maximization problem. The method is based on upper approximations constructed from pairs of eventually decreasing sequences of strictly increasing continuous functions on [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] and of scalars in ( 0 , 1 ) (0,1) . Then, we can associate integers with those pairs using inverses on [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] of the functions. We prove that such pairs always exist and one provides the index maximizer. In general, such pairs provide an upper bound of the greatest maximizer of the sequence. Finally, we apply the methodology on concrete examples including famous sequences such as logistic, Fibonacci and Syracuse sequences. We also apply our techniques to norm based peaks computation problems on discrete-time linear systems.

Introduction

In this paper, denoting by
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, the set of real sequences, we consider the following computational problem.

Problem 1
.

For an element
u\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, compute:

•

if it is finite

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}u_{n}

•

and if it exists

k\in\operatorname{Argmax}(u):=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:u_{k}=\sup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}u_{m}\}

The objective of the paper is to propose a theoretical approach explaining how we can compute a supremum and an integer maximizer of a real sequence. The main idea developed in the paper is the construction a formula providing a finite upper bound of the greatest maximizer of the analyzed sequence. This upper bound allows to solve Problem
1
in finite time. Indeed, to compute the supremum of the sequence and to obtain a maximizer, it suffices to compare all the terms from the initial index until the integer part of the upper bound. To limit the number of comparisons, we have to compute the smallest possible upper bound of the greatest maximizer of the sequence.

The study of Problem
1
is motivated by the study of
generalized peaks computation problems
arising in the verification of dynamical systems (see e.g.
[
AGM15
]
in a discrete-time context and for the continuous-time case
[
MHS20
]
). Problem
1
can be viewed as a generalization of
[
Adj21
,
Adj25
]
. A generalized peaks computation problem requires two inputs: a dynamical system and an objective function. Generalized peaks computation problems encompass several verification problems for dynamical systems. The objective function modelizes the quantity of interest for which the evolution has to be analyzed. For example, it can come from
energy function definitions
[
WM07
]
, functional representations of a safe
set
[
AGM15
,
MHS20
]
or norms
[
Shc17
,
APS18
]
. Speaking about norms, we can find in some references, a problem dealing with the computation of an upper bound on the sequences of the norms of the powers of a given matrix (see e.g.
[

]
). The latter work is not directly motivated by dynamical system verification puroposes. Most of the works about norms based generalized peaks computation problems deal with linear systems

In this paper, we aboard maximization problems for sequences and thus the method proposed can be directly applied to generalized peaks computation problems in a discrete-time systems context. The dynamical system input of a generalized peaks computation problem is an autonomous time-invariant discrete-time system characterized by a set of initial conditions

More formally, a generalized peaks computation problem in a discrete-time context is defined from

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{x\in X^{\mathrm{in}}}\varphi(T^{k}(x))

(1)

Then writing

\forall\,k\in\mathbb{N},\ \nu_{k}:=\sup_{x\in X^{\mathrm{in}}}\varphi(T^{k}(x))\text{ and }\nu_{\rm opt}:=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\nu_{k},

2
Preliminaries on the supremum of a real sequence

We provide useful notations concerning the supremum and the limit superior of a sequence.

Notation 1
.

Let
u\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
. We write
\mathfrak{s}(u):=\displaystyle{\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}u_{n}}
and
\boldsymbol{\ell}(u):=\displaystyle{\limsup_{n\to+\infty}u_{n}}
.

In this paper, we are interested in sequences for which the supremum is finite and the set containing such sequences is denoted by
\Lambda
.

Notation 2
.

\Lambda:=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}:\mathfrak{s}(u)<+\infty\}
.

Proposition 1
.

We have the asymptotic characterization of elements of
\Lambda
:

u\in\Lambda\iff\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}.

Proof.

The direct implication follows from
\mathfrak{s}(u)\geq\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)
.
Now assume that
\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
. If
\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)=-\infty
, then for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
u_{n}\leq u_{0}
for all
n\geq N
and
\mathfrak{s}(u)=\max_{0\leq k\leq N-1}u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
. Now suppose that
\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)\in\mathbb{R}
and let
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\sup_{k\geq n}u_{k}\leq\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)+1
. Then,

\mathfrak{s}(u)\leq\displaystyle{\max\{\max_{0\leq j\leq n-1}u_{j},\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)+1\}}\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎

This asymptotic characterization of elements of
\Lambda
leads to consider new sets of integers dealing with the tails of a sequence
u
:

\Delta_{u}:=\left\{k\in\mathbb{N}:\max_{0\leq j\leq k}u_{j}\geq\sup_{j>k}u_{j}\right\}\ \text{ and }\ \Delta^{s}_{u}:=\left\{k\in\mathbb{N}:\max_{0\leq j\leq k}u_{j}>\sup_{j>k}u_{j}\right\}

and their respective infimum:

\mathbf{K}_{u}=\inf\Delta_{u}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}=\inf\Delta^{s}_{u}.

We recall that
\mathbf{K}_{u}<+\infty
(resp.
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}<+\infty
) if and only if
\Delta_{u}\neq\emptyset
(resp.
\Delta^{s}_{u}\neq\emptyset
). Furthermore, if the sets of integers defined above are nonempty, they contain their infimum.

Finally, we will also need to check whether a term of the analyzed sequence is greater than its limit superior and thus we introduce the following sets of integers:

{\mathrm{R}}_{u}:=\left\{k\in\mathbb{N}:u_{k}\geq\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)\right\}\quad\text{ and }\quad{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}:=\left\{k\in\mathbb{N}:u_{k}>\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)\right\}.

Proposition 2
.

If
u\notin\Lambda
then
\Delta_{u}=\emptyset
and
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}=\emptyset
. Obviously, it also implies that
\Delta^{s}_{u}=\emptyset
and
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

If
u\notin\Lambda
then
\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)=+\infty
and as
u\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, we get
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}=\emptyset
. As
\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)=+\infty
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we can find
N>n
such that
\sup_{k>n}u_{k}\geq\sup_{k\geq N}u_{k}\geq 1+\max_{0\leq l\leq n}u_{l}
and
\Delta_{u}=\emptyset
.
∎

Proposition 3
.

Let
u\in\Lambda
. The following assertions hold:

1.

\Delta^{s}_{u}\neq\emptyset\iff{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}\neq\emptyset
;

2.

\Delta_{u}\neq\emptyset\iff{\mathrm{R}}_{u}\neq\emptyset
;

3.

\Delta^{s}_{u}=\emptyset\iff\mathfrak{s}(u)=\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)
.

Proof.

1
. Suppose that
\Delta^{s}_{u}\neq\emptyset
and let
k\in\Delta^{s}_{u}
then we have for some
\ell\leq k
,
u_{\ell}=\max_{0\leq j\leq k}u_{j}>\sup_{j>k}u_{j}\geq\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)
and
\ell\in{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}\neq\emptyset
. Now assume that
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}\neq\emptyset
. If
\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)=-\infty
meaning that
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}=\mathbb{N}
, for some
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}

\sup_{0\leq k\leq N}u_{k}\geq u_{0}>u_{0}-1\geq\sup_{k>N}u_{k}
and
N\in\Delta^{s}_{u}
. Now assume that
\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)\in\mathbb{R}
and suppose that
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}\neq\emptyset
. Let
k\in{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}
and
\varepsilon=u_{k}-\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)>0
. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
\sup_{j>n}u_{j}\leq\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)+\varepsilon/2=\frac{u_{k}+\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)}{2}<u_{k}
. Then
k\leq n
,
\max_{0\leq j\leq n}u_{j}\geq u_{k}>\sup_{j>n}u_{j}
and
n\in\Delta^{s}_{u}\neq\emptyset
.

2
. The implication
\Delta_{u}\neq\emptyset\implies{\mathrm{R}}_{u}\neq\emptyset
can be proved as in
1
. Now, suppose that
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}\neq\emptyset
. If
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}\neq\emptyset
, the result follows from
1
. So, we assume that
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}=\emptyset
. Let
k\in{\mathrm{R}}_{u}
. As
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}=\emptyset
, we have
u_{k}=\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)
and for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
,
u_{j}\leq\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)=u_{k}
. Then
u_{k}=\mathfrak{s}(u)
and

\max_{0\leq j\leq k}u_{j}\geq\sup_{j>k}u_{j}
and
k\in\Delta_{u}\neq\emptyset
.

3
. From
1
. we have
\Delta^{s}_{u}=\emptyset\iff{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}=\emptyset
. As
\Delta^{s}_{u}=\emptyset
iff
\forall\,k\in\mathbb{N},\ u_{k}\leq\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)
iff
\mathfrak{s}(u)\leq\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)
and since
\mathfrak{s}(u)\geq\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)
is always true, we conclude that
\mathfrak{s}(u)=\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)
.
∎

Proposition 4
.

Let
u\in\Lambda
. The following assertions hold:

1.

\operatorname{Argmax}(u)\subseteq\Delta_{u}
;

2.

\operatorname{Argmax}(u)\neq\emptyset\iff\Delta_{u}\neq\emptyset
;

3.

\mathbf{K}_{u}=\inf\operatorname{Argmax}(u)
;

4.

\operatorname{Argmax}(u)=\emptyset
or
\operatorname{Argmax}(u)
unbounded
\implies\mathfrak{s}(u)=\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)
;

5.

If
\Delta^{s}_{u}\neq\emptyset
then
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}=\max\operatorname{Argmax}(u)
.

Proof.

1
. Let
k\in\operatorname{Argmax}(u)
. We have
u_{k}=\mathfrak{s}(u)
; thus
\max_{0\leq j\leq k}u_{j}=u_{k}\geq\sup_{j>k}u_{j}
and
k\in\Delta_{u}
.

2.
From
1
.
\operatorname{Argmax}(u)\neq\emptyset\implies\Delta_{u}\neq\emptyset
. Now as for all
k\in\Delta_{u}
,
\mathfrak{s}(u)=\max_{0\leq j\leq k}u_{j}
, there exists some
j\in\operatorname{Argmax}(u)\cap[0,k]
. We conclude that
\Delta_{u}\neq\emptyset\implies\operatorname{Argmax}(u)\neq\emptyset
.

3
. Assume that
\mathbf{K}_{u}<+\infty
. From
1
.,
\mathbf{K}_{u}\leq k
for all
k\in\operatorname{Argmax}(u)
. Moreover, as
\mathbf{K}_{u}\in\Delta_{u}
,
\mathfrak{s}(u)=\max_{0\leq j\leq\mathbf{K}_{u}}u_{j}
. Then, there exists
j\in\operatorname{Argmax}(u)\cap[0,\mathbf{K}_{u}]
. From
1
., we also have
\mathbf{K}_{u}\leq j
. Finally,
j=\mathbf{K}_{u}
and
\mathbf{K}_{u}\in\operatorname{Argmax}(u)
. If
\mathbf{K}_{u}=+\infty
, then
\Delta_{u}=\emptyset
and, from
2
., this is equivalent to
\operatorname{Argmax}(u)=\emptyset
i.e.
\min\operatorname{Argmax}(u)=+\infty
.

4
. Assume that
\operatorname{Argmax}(u)=\emptyset
. Then
\Delta_{u}=\emptyset
and so
\Delta^{s}_{u}=\emptyset
. We conclude from the third statement of Prop.
3
. Now if
\operatorname{Argmax}(u)
is nonempty but unbounded, then for all
k\in\Delta_{u}
, there exists
j>k
such that
j\in\operatorname{Argmax}(u)
and thus
\max_{0\leq l\leq k}u_{l}=\max_{l>k}u_{l}
and so for all
k\in\Delta_{u}
,
k\notin\Delta^{s}_{u}
and
\Delta^{s}_{u}=\emptyset
. We conclude again using the third statement of Prop.
3
.

5
. Assume that
\Delta^{s}_{u}\neq\emptyset
. Then
\operatorname{Argmax}(u)
is nonempty and let
k\in\operatorname{Argmax}(u)
. We have
k\leq\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
. Otherwise, as
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}\in\Delta^{s}_{u}
,
\mathfrak{s}(u)\geq\max_{0\leq j\leq\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}u_{j}>\sup_{j>\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}u_{j}=u_{k}=\mathfrak{s}(u)
. Suppose that
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}\notin\operatorname{Argmax}(u)
and let
\overline{l}=\max\{0\leq l\leq\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}:u_{l}=\sup_{0\leq j\leq\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}u_{j}\}
. As
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}\in\Delta^{s}_{u}
,
\sup_{l>\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}u_{l}<\max_{0\leq l\leq\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}u_{l}=u_{\overline{l}}
. By definition of
\overline{l}
,
u_{\overline{l}}>\max_{\overline{l}<l\leq\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}u_{l}
.
Finally,
u_{\overline{l}}=\max_{0\leq l\leq\overline{l}}u_{l}>\sup_{l>\overline{l}}u_{l}
and
\overline{l}<\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
. This contradicts the minimality of
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
and
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}\in\operatorname{Argmax}(u)
.
∎

Note that
\operatorname{Argmax}(u)
can be nonempty and bounded when
\Delta^{s}_{u}
is empty. In this case, we do have
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}=+\infty
and thus
\max\operatorname{Argmax}(u)<\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
. Moreover, by the third statement of Prop
3
, we have
\mathfrak{s}(u)=\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)
. However, the sequence finitely touches
\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)
before approaching, up to a subsequence,
\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)
from below (strictly).

3
Resolution of Problem
1
from an upper approximation of the analyzed sequence

In Section
2
, we identify the two integers which characterize the set of maximizer of a real sequence. We propose in this paper, given a real sequence
u
for which
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}\neq\emptyset
, to compute an upper bound of the greatest maximizer
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
. Once an upper bound
K
of
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
computed, we are able to solve Problem
1
by comparing the terms
u_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{K}
. As
K
is an upper bound of
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
, we know that the optimal value
\mathfrak{s}(u)
belongs to this finite truncation of the sequence
u
. To avoid useless computations,
K
must be as close as possible to
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
.

3.1
The set of upper bound sequences and the upper bound functional
\mathfrak{F}_{u}

Given a real sequence
u
,
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
is a index of
u
. Besides, to compute an upper bound of
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
from a closed formula can only be done from the available data i.e. the terms of
u
. Thus, we have to convert terms of
u
into indices. One class of sequences for which the conversion is easy is the class of convergent geometric sequences. However, to keep the highest possible level of generality, we apply bijections to convergent geometric sequences and those particular sequences are used as upper bounds of
u
. To use such sequences as upper bounds forces us to only consider (strictly) increasing surjection and positive convergent geometric sequences. More formally, upper bound candidates of
u
are sequences
\omega:=(\omega_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
h_{k}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
strictly increasing on
[0,1]
and surjective (as a function from
[0,1]
to
[h_{k}(0),h_{k}(1)]
) and
\beta_{k}\in(0,1)
verifying
\omega_{k}=h_{k}(\beta_{k}^{k})
. We can easily convert the terms
\omega_{k}
into indices
k
. With a suitable choice of sequences of functions
(h_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, the obtained indices
k
will be upper bounds of
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
.

Actually, the upper bound candidate
\omega
are completely determined by a sequence
h
of strictly increasing surjections
h_{k}
on
[0,1]
and a sequence
\beta
in
(0,1)
. As a strictly increasing function
g:[a,b]\to[g(a),g(b)]
is surjective if and only if it is continuous on
[a,b]
, we introduce the set of functions:

\Omega([0,1]):=\{h:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}:h\text{ strictly increasing and continuous on }[0,1]\}.

We can define the inverse on
[0,1]
of
h\in\Omega([0,1])
defined from
[h(0),h(1)]
to
[0,1]
. For the sake of simplicity, we will simply denote it by
h^{-1}
.

Then, we use the standard notation
\Omega([0,1])^{\mathbb{N}}
for the sequences of elements of
\Omega([0,1])
. For
u\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, we define the following set of the upper bounds of
u
:

\Gamma(u):=\left\{(h,\beta)\in\Omega([0,1])^{\mathbb{N}}\times(0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}:u_{k}\leq h_{k}(\beta_{k}^{k}),\ \forall\,k\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

We need sometimes to separate functional and scalar parts of an element in
\Gamma(u)
and we introduce the two following sets:

\Gamma_{\rm f}(u):=\left\{h\in\Omega([0,1])^{\mathbb{N}}:\exists\,\beta\in(0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}\text{ s.t. }(h,\beta)\in\Gamma(u)\right\}

and for
h\in\Gamma_{\rm f}(u)
:

\Gamma_{\rm s}(u,h):=\left\{\beta\in(0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}:(h,\beta)\in\Gamma(u)\right\}.

In the sequel, we will consider special sequences in
\Gamma(u)
and
\Gamma_{\rm f}(u)
. First, we define the set
\Gamma_{{\rm ev}}^{\rm cst}(u)
of
eventually constant pairs
in
\Gamma(u)
:

\Gamma_{{\rm ev}}^{\rm cst}(u):=\{(h,\beta)\in\Gamma(u):\exists\,(g,\gamma,N)\in\Omega([0,1])\times(0,1)\times\mathbb{N}\ \forall\,n\geq N,\ (h_{n},\beta_{n})=(g,\gamma)\},

For
(h,\beta)\in\Gamma_{{\rm ev}}^{\rm cst}(u)
, we denote by
\mathfrak{c}(h,\beta)
the smallest index from which the sequence
(h,\beta)
is constant.

We will also consider the set
{\Gamma}_{0}^{\rm cst}(u)
of
constant pairs
in
\Gamma(u)
:

{\Gamma}_{0}^{\rm cst}(u):=\{(h,\beta)\in\Gamma(u):\exists\,(g,\gamma)\in\Omega([0,1])\times(0,1)\ \forall\,n\in\mathbb{N},\ (h_{n},\beta_{n})=(g,\gamma)\}.

For
(h,\beta)\in{\Gamma}_{0}^{\rm cst}(u)
, we have obviously
\mathfrak{c}(h,\beta)=0
.

From elements of
\Gamma(u)
, we can derive a function called
the upper bound functional
. We will see in that Subsection
3.3
, for some selections of elements of
\Gamma(u)
, the function produces upper bounds of
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
.

Definition 1

(Upper bound Functional)
.

Let
u\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
. The upper bound functional is the function
\mathfrak{F}_{u}:\mathbb{N}\times\Omega([0,1])^{\mathbb{N}}\times(0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{+\infty\}
defined as follows. For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
h\in\Omega([0,1])^{\mathbb{N}}
and
\beta\in(0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}
, we have:

\mathfrak{F}_{u}(k,h,\beta):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\dfrac{\ln(h_{k}^{-1}(u_{k}))}{\ln(\beta_{k})}&\text{ if }(h,\beta)\in\Gamma(u)\text{ and }u_{k}>h_{k}(0)\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

(2)

To be complete,
\mathfrak{F}_{u}
is defined and equal to
+\infty
for the elements of
\Omega([0,1])^{\mathbb{N}}\times(0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}
outside
\Gamma(u)
and for elements of
\Gamma(u)
for which
h_{k}^{-1}(u_{k})\leq 0
.
Later, we will prove that, for a pertinent subset of
\Gamma(u)
,
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}
is nonempty (and thus
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}<+\infty
) if and only if we can find an element
(h,\beta)
of this subset for which there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
u_{k}>h_{k}(0)
. Hence, if
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}
is empty,
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
is infinite and
\mathfrak{F}_{u}
is also infinite on this subset.

The existence of an integer
k
such that
u_{k}>h_{k}(0)
plays an important role in the choice of the upper bound candidate. For example, if
u\in\Lambda
, the constant sequence
h
identically equal to
g:x\to\mathfrak{s}(u)+x
belongs to
{\Gamma}_{\rm f,0}^{\rm cst}(u)
but there is no
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
u_{k}>h_{k}(0)
even if
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}
is nonempty. This sequence cannot be used to construct an upper bound of
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
since
\mathfrak{F}_{u}
evaluated at this
h
is infinite. This simple situation leads to introduce a notion of
usefulness
.

Definition 2

(Useful strictly increasing continuous functions)
.

Let
u\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
. A function
h\in\Gamma_{\rm f}(u)
is said to be
useful
for
u
if the set :

\mathcal{S}(u,h):=\{k\in\mathbb{N}:u_{k}>h_{k}(0)\}

(3)

is nonempty.

By extension, a pair
(h,\beta)\in\Gamma(u)
is
useful
for
u
if
h
is useful for
u
.

In the sequel, if there is no ambiguity about the sequence, we will simply say useful instead of useful for
u
.

Unfortunately, as we will see in Example
1
, usefulness is not enough to compute safely an upper bound of
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
from
\mathfrak{F}_{u}
.

Example 1
.

Let
u\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\mathfrak{s}(u)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{+\infty\}
. We define for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
:

h_{k}:x\mapsto\left(\frac{|u_{k}|+1}{|u_{k}|+0.5}\right)^{k}|u_{k}|x\text{ and }\beta_{k}:=\left(\frac{|u_{k}|+0.5}{|u_{k}|+1}\right).

(4)

For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the functions
h_{k}
are clearly in
\Omega([0,1])
and
\beta_{k}\in(0,1)
. Moreover, we have for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
u_{k}\leq h_{k}(\beta_{k}^{k})
and in particular we have
u_{k}=h_{k}(\beta_{k}^{k})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
u_{k}\geq 0
. Let us define
h:=(h_{k})_{k}
and
\beta:=(\beta_{k})_{k}
. We have for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
h_{k}(0)=0
. As
\mathfrak{s}(u)
is supposed to be strictly positive, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
u_{k}>0
and so
\mathcal{S}(u,h)\neq\emptyset
. Hence, for all
k
such that
u_{k}>0
:

\mathfrak{F}_{u}(k,h,\beta)=\dfrac{\ln h_{k}^{-1}(u_{k})}{\ln\beta_{k}}=k

Obviously, we cannot guarantee that
\mathfrak{F}_{u}(k,h,\beta)
is an upper bound of
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
for all
k\geq K
for some
K
independent to
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
.
∎

In Example
1
, we see that we need to restrict the choice of upper bound candidates. Next, we will see that a good choice is to only pick
eventually decreasing
elements of
\Gamma(u)
.

We use the standard functional order on
\Omega([0,1])
i.e. for
f,g\in\Omega([0,1])
,
f\leq g\iff f(x)\leq g(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]
. We also use the standard weak order on
\Omega([0,1])\times(0,1)
i.e. for all
(f,\beta),(g,\gamma)\in\Omega([0,1])\times(0,1)
,
(f,\beta)\leq(g,\gamma)\iff f\leq g
and
\beta\leq\gamma
. Then, we introduce for
u\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, the set
{\Gamma}_{\rm f,{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)
of eventually decreasing of
\Gamma_{\rm f}(u)
i.e.

{\Gamma}_{\rm f,{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u):=\{h\in\Gamma_{\rm f}(u):\exists\,N\in\mathbb{N}\ \forall\,n\geq N,\ h_{n+1}\leq h_{n}\}.

For
h\in{\Gamma}_{\rm f,{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)
, we denote by
\mathfrak{m}(h)
the smallest index from which
h
is decreasing.

The set
\Gamma_{{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)
contains all eventually decreasing elements of
\Gamma(u)
i.e.

\Gamma_{{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u):=\{(h,\beta)\in\Gamma_{\rm f}(u):\exists\,N\in\mathbb{N}\ \forall\,n\geq N,\ (h_{n+1},\beta_{n+1})\leq(h_{n},\beta_{n})\}.

For
(h,\beta)\in\Gamma_{{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)
, we denote by
\mathfrak{m}(h,\beta)
the smallest index from which
h
and
\beta
are decreasing.

Then, we define
{\Gamma}_{{\rm f},0}^{\rm dec}(u)
as the set of decreasing elements of
\Gamma_{\rm f}(u)
, formally:

{\Gamma}_{{\rm f},0}^{\rm dec}(u):=\{h\in\Gamma_{\rm f}(u):\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N},\ h_{n+1}\leq h_{n}\},

and finally, the set
\Gamma_{0}^{\rm dec}(u)
of decreasing elements of
\Gamma(u)
, formally:

\Gamma_{0}^{\rm dec}(u):=\{(h,\beta)\in\Gamma_{\rm f}(u):\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N},\ (h_{n+1},\beta_{n+1})\leq(h_{n},\beta_{n})\}.

It is straightforward to see that
{\Gamma}_{\rm f,{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)
and
\Gamma_{{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)
are respectively subsets of
{\Gamma}_{{\rm f},0}^{\rm dec}(u)
and
\Gamma_{0}^{\rm dec}(u)
.
Obviously, for all
h\in{\Gamma}_{{\rm f},0}^{\rm dec}(u)
and all
(g,\gamma)\in\Gamma_{0}^{\rm dec}(u)
, we have
\mathfrak{m}(h)=\mathfrak{m}(g,\gamma)=0
. Moreover, as constant sequences are decreasing,
\Gamma_{{\rm ev}}^{\rm cst}(u)
and
{\Gamma}_{0}^{\rm cst}(u)
are respectively subsets of
\Gamma_{{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)
and
\Gamma_{0}^{\rm dec}(u)
. Since an eventually constant sequence can be decreasing before being constant, for all
(h,\beta)\in\Gamma_{{\rm ev}}^{\rm cst}(u)
, we have
\mathfrak{m}(h,\beta)\leq\mathfrak{c}(h,\beta)
.

Remark 1
.

We insist on the fact that in the definition of being eventually decreasing both
h
and
\beta
are decreasing after
\mathfrak{m}(h,\beta)
. Let
n
be greater than
\mathfrak{m}(h,\beta)
. As the functional order is used for
h
, we have
h_{n+1}(\beta_{n+1}^{n+1})\leq h_{n}(\beta_{n+1}^{n+1})
. Since
h_{n}
is strictly increasing and
\beta_{n+1}\in(0,1)
, we have
h_{n}(\beta_{n+1}^{n+1})<h_{n}(\beta_{n+1}^{n})
. Finally, as
\beta_{n+1}\leq\beta_{n}
and
h_{n}
is increasing, we get
h_{n}(\beta_{n+1}^{n})\leq h_{n}(\beta_{n}^{n})
. We conclude that
h_{n+1}(\beta_{n+1}^{n+1})<h_{n}(\beta_{n})
.

We could ask if it suffices to only require the weaker condition:
h_{n}(\beta_{n+1}^{n})\leq h_{n}(\beta_{n}^{n})
for all
n\geq\mathfrak{m}(h,\beta)
. However, in the definition of the upper bound functional (Definition
1
),
(h_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is decoupled from
(\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. The weaker condition would not be sufficient to compare
\mathfrak{F}_{u}(k,h,\beta)
,
\mathfrak{F}_{u}(\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u},h,\beta)
and
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
for
k\leq\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
.

We extend usefulness to eventually decreasing elements. Now we require that
\mathcal{S}(u,h)
contains an integer greater than
\mathfrak{m}(h)
.

Definition 3

(Usefully decreasing)
.

Let
u\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
. An element
h\in{\Gamma}_{\rm f,{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)
(resp.
(h,\beta)\in\Gamma_{{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)
) is
usefully decreasing
if and only if
\mathcal{S}(u,h)\cap[\mathfrak{m}(h),+\infty)\neq\emptyset
(resp.
\mathcal{S}(u,h)\cap[\mathfrak{m}(h,\beta),+\infty)\neq\emptyset
).

An element
h\in\Gamma_{0}^{\rm dec}(u)
(or
(h,\beta)\in\Gamma_{0}^{\rm dec}(u)
) is usefully decreasing if and only if it is useful in the sense of Definition
2
.

We can always construct a decreasing pair from an eventually decreasing one. Later, we prove that the procedure may increase the value of
\mathfrak{F}_{u}
.

Proposition 5
.

Let
u\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
. The following equivalence holds :

\Gamma_{{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)\neq\emptyset\iff\Gamma_{0}^{\rm dec}(u)\neq\emptyset.

Moreover, there exists
(h,\beta)\in\Gamma_{{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)
usefully decreasing if and only if there exists
(g,\gamma)\in\Gamma_{0}^{\rm dec}(u)
useful.

Proof.

If
(h,\beta)
is decreasing then it is eventually decreasing. Now suppose there exists
(g,\gamma)\in\Gamma(u)
eventually decreasing. Let us write
N=\mathfrak{m}(g,\gamma)
. Then we can define for all integer
n\leq N
,
h_{n}=\max_{i=0,\ldots,N}g_{i}
and
h_{n}=g_{n}
for all integer
n>N
. Similarly, we define, for all
n\leq N
,
\beta_{n}=\max_{i=0,\ldots,N}\alpha_{i}
and for all
n>N
,
\beta_{n}=\alpha_{n}
. The sequences
\beta
and
h
are clearly decreasing.

Clearly, if there exists
(g,\gamma)\in\Gamma_{0}^{\rm dec}(u)
useful then it is also eventually decreasing and usefully decreasing. Now suppose that there exists
(h,\beta)\in\Gamma_{{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)
usefully decreasing, the construction of
(g,\gamma)
from above is useful as for some
n\geq\mathfrak{m}(h,\beta)
,
u_{n}>h_{n}(0)=g_{n}(0)
.
∎

3.2
Existence of useful upper bound sequences

We have presented the important sets of upper bound sequences and their basic properties. Before developing the main properties of the upper bound functional
\mathfrak{F}_{u}
defined in Definition
1
, we focus on the existence of useful elements in
{\Gamma}_{0}^{\rm cst}(u)
and usefully decreasing elements in
\Gamma_{{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)
when
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}
is nonempty.

First, the set
\Gamma(u)
is still nonempty. Second, it is easy to see that for all
h\in\Gamma_{\rm f}(u)
, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
u_{k}<h_{k}(1)
and
u_{0}\leq h_{0}(1)
. From these two inequalities, we can characterize
\Lambda
from
\Gamma_{\rm f}(u)
and describe a simple situation where
u_{0}
is the maximum of the sequence terms.

Proposition 6
.

Let
u\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
. The following statements hold:

1.

\Gamma(u)\neq\emptyset
.

2.

u\in\Lambda
if and only there exists
h\in\Gamma_{\rm f}(u)
such that the set
\{h_{k}(1):k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is bounded from above.

3.

if there exists
h\in{\Gamma}_{{\rm f},0}^{\rm dec}(u)
and
u_{0}=h_{0}(1)
then
u\in\Lambda
and
u_{k}<u_{0}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

1.
The pair
(h,\beta)
proposed in Equation (
4
) belongs to
\Gamma(u)
even if
u\notin\Lambda
and
\mathfrak{s}(u)
is not strictly positive.

2. Let us take
h\in\Gamma(u)
such that
\{h_{k}(1):k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is bounded from above then
u_{k}\leq h_{k}(\beta_{k}^{k})<h_{k}(1)
which implies that
\mathfrak{s}(u)\leq\sup\{h_{k}(1):k\in\mathbb{N}\}<+\infty
. On the contrary, if
u\in\Lambda
, then the constant sequence
h
defined from
f:x\mapsto x+\mathfrak{s}(u)
belongs to
\Gamma_{\rm f}(u)
and the set
\{h_{k}(1):k\in\mathbb{N}\}=\{\mathfrak{s}(u)+1\}
.

3. Assume that there exists
h\in\Gamma_{\rm f}(u)
decreasing such that
u_{0}=h_{0}(1)
. Then for some
\beta\in(0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}
, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
u_{k}\leq h_{k}(\beta_{k}^{k})<h_{k}(1)\leq h_{0}(1)=u_{0}
.
∎

Remark 2
.

The third statement of Proposition
6
implies, if it holds, that the term
u_{0}
is the supremum of the analyzed sequence
u
. Moreover, the index 0 is the only maximizer of the sequence.

We can go further than the results of Proposition
6
to completely characterize
\Lambda
from eventually decreasing upper bounds.

Proposition 7
.

Let
u\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Then
u\in\Lambda\iff{\Gamma}_{\rm f,{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

Assume that
u\in\Lambda
. The constant sequence function of the proof of the second statement of Proposition
6
belongs to
{\Gamma}_{\rm f,{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)
. Now suppose that
u\notin\Lambda
and
{\Gamma}_{\rm f,{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)\neq\emptyset
. Using the second statement of Proposition
6
, for all
h\in\Gamma_{\rm f}(u)
, the set
\{h_{k}(1):k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is unbounded from above. Taking
g\in{\Gamma}_{\rm f,{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)
, we have for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
g_{k}(1)\leq\max\{g_{i}(1):i=0,\ldots,\mathfrak{m}(g)\}\in\mathbb{R}
. This contradicts the second statement of Proposition

and thus
{\Gamma}_{\rm f,{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)
is empty.
∎

Theorem
1
above is the main result of the section. First, it justifies the finiteness of the upper bound functional
\mathfrak{F}_{u}
for
u\in\Lambda
with
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}\neq\emptyset
. In a second time, it serves to construct constant and non constant elements in
\Gamma(u)
. Finally, the existence of a useful and optimal function is important for the optimality of the proposed function
\mathfrak{F}_{u}
.

Theorem 1

(Existence of a useful optimal affine function)
.

Let
u\in\Lambda
such that
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}\neq\emptyset
. Then, there exist
a>0
,
b\in(0,1)
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that:

1.

\{k\in\mathbb{N}:u_{k}>c\}\neq\emptyset
;

2.

u_{k}\leq ab^{k}+c
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
;

3.

u_{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}=ab^{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}+c
.

Proof.

If
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}
is nonempty then
u_{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}=\mathfrak{s}(u)>\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
and for all
t\in(\boldsymbol{\ell}(u),\mathfrak{s}(u))
there exists

k\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
u_{k}>t
. So let
c
be in
(\boldsymbol{\ell}(u),\mathfrak{s}(u))
. Now as
c>\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)
, there exists
N_{c}\in\mathbb{N}
s.t.
\sup_{k\geq N_{c}}x_{k}\leq c
. This is the same as for all
k\geq N_{c}
,
u_{k}\leq c
. We conclude that for all
k\geq N_{c}
,
u_{k}\leq c+ab^{k}
for all
a>0
and all
b\in(0,1)
. Note that as
c<\mathfrak{s}(u)
and
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
is the greatest element of the argmax of
u
then
N_{c}
is strictly greater than
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
. Now let introduce the set :

{AB}_{c}:=\left\{(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times(0,1):u_{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}=\mathfrak{s}(u)=ab^{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}+c\right\}.

It is easy to see that
S_{c}
is nonempty, indeed, for all
b
in
(0,1)
,
(\frac{u_{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}-c}{b^{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}},b)\in S_{c}
. Moreover, if
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}>0
, for all
(a,b)\in{AB}_{c}
,
for all
0<k<\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
,
u_{k}\leq u_{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}=ab^{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}+c<ab^{k}+c
as
b\in(0,1)
. We also have
u_{0}\leq u_{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}=ab^{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}+c\leq a+c
as
b\in(0,1)
. Hence, the result
u_{k}\leq ab^{k}+c
holds on the sets
[0,\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}]\cap\mathbb{N}
and
[N_{c},+\infty)\cap\mathbb{N}
with for every
(a,b)\in S_{c}
. It suffices to find
(a,b)\in S_{c}
, such that
u_{k}\leq ab^{k}+c
for all
k\in(\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u},N_{c}]\cap\mathbb{N}
. Recall that we have
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}+1\leq N_{c}
.

Let us write
(-\infty,0)\ni\gamma:=\max_{k\in(\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u},N_{c}]}(u_{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}-u_{k})/(\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}-k)
. This cannot be zero since
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
is the maximal integer
k
such that
u_{k}=\max_{n\in\mathbb{N}}u_{n}
. Now, let
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times(0,1)
and define
f_{a,b}:\mathbb{R}\ni y\mapsto ab^{y}+c
. The function
f_{a,b}
is derivable and strictly convex and
f^{\prime}_{a,b}(y)=ab^{y}\ln(b)
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
. If
f^{\prime}_{a,b}(\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u})=\gamma
and
(a,b)\in{AB}_{c}
then, by convexity of
f_{a,b}
, the tangent of
f
at
y=\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
satisfies for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
f(k)\geq f^{\prime}(\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u})(k-\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u})+f(\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u})
which is the same as
ab^{k}+c\geq\gamma(k-\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u})+u_{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}
. By definition of
\gamma
, we have for all
k\in(\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u},N_{c})
,
(\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}-k)\gamma\leq u_{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}-u_{k}
and so
u_{k}\leq u_{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}+(k-\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u})\gamma
. We conclude that if
f^{\prime}_{a,b}(\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u})=\gamma
and
(a,b)\in{AB}_{c}
,
u_{k}\leq ab^{k}+c
for all
k\in(\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u},N_{c}]
. Finally, we are looking for
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times(0,1)
such that :

f_{a,b}(\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u})=u_{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}\text{ i.e. }(a,b)\in{AB}_{c}\text{ and }f^{\prime}_{a,b}(\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u})=\gamma

From
(a,b)\in{AB}_{c}
, we get
ab^{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}=u_{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}-c
. Hence,
f^{\prime}_{a,b}(\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u})=\gamma
if and only if
\ln(b)=\frac{\gamma}{u_{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}-c}<0
. So
b=\exp(\frac{\gamma}{u_{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}-c})\in(0,1)
and
a=(u_{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}-c)\exp(-\frac{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}\gamma}{u_{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}-c})>0
.
∎

In the proof of Theorem
1
, we exhibit the parameters of the affine function. Unfortunately (but naturally) all the parameters depend on
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
and
u_{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}
what is exactly what we want to compute. Theorem

allows to construct an affine function in

which is optimal. Optimal means that at the index
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
, the upper-bound

is equal to

. Hence, we obtain
\mathfrak{F}_{u}(\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u},x\mapsto ax+c,b)=\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}
.

Corollary 1
.

Let
u\in\Lambda
such that
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}\neq\emptyset
. Then, there exists a pair
(h,\beta)\in\Gamma_{{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)
such that
u_{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}=h_{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}}(\beta^{\mathbf{K}^{s}_{u}})
.

Theorem
1
proves that the nonemptiness of
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}
is actually a sufficient condition for the existence of a usefully decreasing element in
\Gamma_{{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)
. Now, we also prove the necessity in Theorem
2
. The necessity requires an auxiliary result formulated in Lemma

.

Lemma 1
.

Let
u\in\Lambda
. Let
(h,\beta)\in\Gamma_{{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)
be usefully decreasing. Then:

•

the sequence
(\beta_{k}^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to 0 strictly decreasingly from
\mathfrak{m}(h,\beta)
;

•

for all
n\geq\mathfrak{m}(h,\beta)
,
\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)\leq h_{n}(0)
.

Proof.

Let us write
N=\mathfrak{m}(h,\beta)
. For all
n\geq N
,
0<\beta_{n+1}\leq\beta_{n}\leq\beta_{N}<1
and thus
0<\beta_{n+1}^{n+1}<\beta_{n+1}^{n}\leq\beta_{n}^{n}\leq\beta_{N}^{n}
. Taking the limit over
n
leads to
\boldsymbol{\ell}(\beta)=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\beta_{n}^{n}=0
.

Now let us write,
\omega:=(h_{k}(\beta_{k}^{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. Let
n\geq N
and define
\zeta:=(h_{n}(\beta_{k}^{k}))_{k}
. For all
k\geq n
, as
(h,\beta)\in\Gamma(u)
and
(h_{m})_{m\geq N}
decreases,
u_{k}\leq\omega_{k}\leq\zeta_{k}
. Taking the
\limsup
over
k
and as
h_{n}
is (upper semi-)continuous, we get
\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)\leq\boldsymbol{\ell}(\omega)\leq\boldsymbol{\ell}(\zeta)\leq h_{n}(\boldsymbol{\ell}(\beta))=h_{n}(0)
.
∎

Theorem 2
.

Let
u\in\Lambda
. Then
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}\neq\emptyset
if and only if there exists
(h,\beta)\in\Gamma_{{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)
usefully decreasing.

Proof.

Theorem
1
asserts that
{\Gamma}_{0}^{\rm cst}(u)
is nonempty when
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}
is nonempty and therefore this implies the sufficiency. The necessity is a consequence of Lemma

. Indeed, if there exists
(h,\beta)\in\Gamma_{{\rm ev}}^{\rm dec}(u)
usefully decreasing, by definition, we can pick

. Therefore,
u_{n}>h_{n}(0)\geq\boldsymbol{\ell}(u)
meaning that

.
∎

Finally, we can ask if we could pick non constant elements in
\Gamma(u)
rather than the affine function furnished by Theorem

. The answer is positive and the construction of the sequence is based on Lemma

.

Lemma 2
.

Let
u\in\Lambda
such that
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}\neq\emptyset
. Let us consider the triplet
(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\times(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
constructed in Theorem.
1
.
Let us consider:

1.

a decreasing sequence
\gamma:=(\gamma_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
(c,\mathfrak{s}(u))
;

3.3.1
Increase properties of the upper bound functional
\mathfrak{F}_{u}

The function
(h,\beta)\to\mathfrak{F}_{u}(\cdot,h,\beta)
relies on the inverse of a function
h\in\Omega([0,1])
. Hence, to study increasing properties of
\mathfrak{F}_{u}(\cdot,h,\beta)
with respect to
h
we give some details on the decreasing property of the application
\Omega([0,1])\ni f\to f^{-1}
. First, we pay attention to the fact that we can only compare the inverses of two elements
f,g\in\Omega([0,1])
on the values in the intersection of their range. Hence, we define, for all sets
J
and all
\{h_{j},j\in J\}\subset\Omega([0,1])
:

I_{[0,1]}\left(\{h_{j},j\in J\}\right)=\bigcap_{j\in J}[h_{j}(0),h_{j}(1)]=\left[\sup_{j\in J}h_{j}(0),\inf_{j\in J}h_{j}(1)\right]

(6)

This property on the inverses is also extended to compute the inverses of minima and maxima of a finite family of elements of
\Omega([0,1])
. For a finite family
\{h_{i}:i\in I\}\subset\Omega([0,1])
, we write:

\underline{h}_{I}:[0,1]\ni x\mapsto\min_{i\in I}h_{i}(x)\quad\text{ and }\quad\overline{h}_{I}:[0,1]\ni x\mapsto\max_{i\in I}h_{i}(x).

(7)

and we define :

\forall\,y\in[\underline{h}_{I}(0),\underline{h}_{I}(1)],\ \underline{I}(y):=\{i\in I:h_{i}(0)\leq y\}

(8)

and

\forall\,y\in[\overline{h}_{I}(0),\overline{h}_{I}(1)],\ \overline{I}(y):=\{i\in I:y\leq h_{i}(1)\}.

(9)

The set
\underline{I}(y)
excludes the functions
h_{i}
for which
y
is strictly less than
h_{i}(0)
and so does not belong to
[h_{i}(0),h_{i}(1)]
. For those functions
h_{i}
, the inverse is not defined for this
y
and we cannot use them to define the inverse of
\underline{h}_{I}
. The set
\overline{I}(y)
has a similar use for
\overline{h}_{I}
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
f,g\in\Omega([0,1])
and
\{h_{i}:i\in I\}
be a finite family in
\Omega([0,1])
. The following statements hold.

1.

If
I_{[0,1]}\left(\{f,g\}\right)=\emptyset
,
f<g\iff f(0)<g(0)
.

2.

If
I_{[0,1]}\left(\{f,g\}\right)\neq\emptyset
,
f\leq g\iff\forall\,y\in I_{[0,1]}\left(\{f,g\}\right),g^{-1}(y)\leq f^{-1}(y)
.

3.

The functions
\underline{h}_{I},\overline{h}_{I}
belong to
\Omega([0,1])
and for all
y\in[\underline{h}_{I}(0),\underline{h}_{I}(1)]
and all
z\in[\overline{h}_{I}(0),\overline{h}_{I}(1)]
:

\underline{h}_{I}^{-1}(y)=\max_{i\in\underline{I}(y)}h_{i}^{-1}(y)\quad\text{ and
}\quad\overline{h}_{I}^{-1}(z)=\min_{i\in\overline{I}(z)}h_{i}^{-1}(z).

Proof.

1
. Suppose that
I_{[0,1]}\left(\{f,g\}\right)=\emptyset
. If
f(x)<g(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]
, it obviously implies that
f(0)<g(0)
. Now suppose that
f(0)<g(0)
. As
I_{[0,1]}\left(\{f,g\}\right)=\emptyset
, we have for all
x\in[0,1]

f(x)\notin[g(0),g(1)]
. Thus either,
f(0)>g(1)
or
f(1)<g(0)
. Then we exclude
f(0)>g(1)
as
f(0)<g(0)
and
g
is strictly increasing on
[0,1]
. We conclude that
f(x)\leq f(1)<g(0)\leq g(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]
as
f
and
g
are strictly increasing on
[0,1]
.

2
. Suppose that
I_{[0,1]}\left(\{f,g\}\right)\neq\emptyset
. Assume that
f\leq g
on
[0,1]
. Let
y\in I_{[0,1]}\left(\{f,g\}\right)
. As
y\in[g(0),g(1)]
,
y=g(x)
for some
x\in[0,1]
. Moreover, by assumption, we have
f(x)\leq g(x)
. As
y\in[f(0),f(1)]
, we can apply
f^{-1}
which is strictly increasing to
g(x)=y
. We get
x\leq f^{-1}(g(x))
. As
x=g^{-1}(y)
, we conclude that
g^{-1}(y)\leq f^{-1}(y)
.

Now assume that for all
y\in I_{[0,1]}\left(\{f,g\}\right),g^{-1}(y)\leq f^{-1}(y)
. Let
x\in[0,1]
. We write
z=f(x)
and thus
z\in[f(0),f(1)]
. Suppose that
z\in I_{[0,1]}\left(\{f,g\}\right)
, then
g^{-1}(z)\leq f^{-1}(z)
. Applying
g
, we get
z\leq g(f^{-1}(z))
which is the same as
f(x)\leq g(x)
. Now suppose that
z\notin I_{[0,1]}\left(\{f,g\}\right)
meaning that
z\notin[g(0),g(1)]
. If
z<g(0)
, then we have
f(x)<g(0)\leq g(x)
as
g
increases on
[0,1]
. Assume now that
z=f(x)>g(1)
. As
I_{[0,1]}\left(\{f,g\}\right)
is nonempty, there exists
u\in[0,x)
such that
f(u)\leq g(1)
. By continuity of
f
, there exists
v\in[u,x)
such that
f(v)=g(1)
. As
f(v)\in I_{[0,1]}\left(\{f,g\}\right)
, we have
g^{-1}(f(v))\leq f^{-1}(f(v))=v
and thus
g^{-1}(g(1))=1\leq v<x\leq 1
. This implies that
z
cannot greater than
g(1)
. This ends the proof.

3
. The minimum and the maximum of a finite family of strictly increasing continuous functions is still strictly increasing and continuous; hence
\underline{h}_{I},\overline{h}_{I}\in\Omega([0,1])
. Let
y\in[\underline{h}_{I}(0),\underline{h}_{I}(1)]
. Then there exists
x\in[0,1]
such that
y=\underline{h}_{I}(x)
. As
I
is finite, there exists
\ell\in I
such that
\underline{h}_{I}(x)=h_{\ell}(x)
. The index
\ell
belongs to
\underline{I}(y)
as for all

,
y<h_{i}(0)\leq h_{i}(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]
. Now let

. Then
y\in[h_{i}(0),\underline{h}_{I}(1)]\subseteq I_{[0,1]}\left(\{h_{i},\underline{h}_{I}\}\right)
. From the second statement,
h_{i}^{-1}(y)\leq\underline{h}_{I}^{-1}(y)
. Finally,
\max_{i\in\underline{I}(y)}h_{i}^{-1}(y)\leq\underline{h}_{I}^{-1}(y)=x=h_{\ell}^{-1}(y)\leq\max_{i\in\underline{I}(y)}h_{i}^{-1}(y)
and we conclude that
\underline{h}_{I}^{-1}(y)=\max_{i\in\underline{I}(y)}h_{i}^{-1}(y)
. The proof for
\overline{h}_{I}^{-1}

is similar.
∎

The set

4.1
A simple case

We start with a very simple example to illustrate our approach. Let us consider, for
a\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, the sequence
x:=(a^{n}(n!)^{-1})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. As presented in Problem
1
, we are interested in the term
x_{n}
which maximizes the sequence
x
and an maximizer index. We apply our techniques to compute them. It is evident that alternative approaches (the study of the variation of the sequence) can solve the problem in a easier way. We can rewrite, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the terms
x_{n}
as follows:

x_{n}=\dfrac{a^{n}}{n!}=\left(\dfrac{a}{a+1}\right)^{n}\dfrac{(a+1)^{n}}{n!}.

Hence, we have for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

x_{n}=h_{n}(\beta^{n})\text{ where }h_{n}:[0,1]\ni t\to t\dfrac{(a+1)^{n}}{n!}\text{ and }\beta=\dfrac{a}{a+1}

It is straightforward to see that
h=(h_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is eventually decreasing and
\mathfrak{m}(h)=a
. Moreover, as for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
h_{n}(0)=0
, we have
\mathcal{S}(x,h)=\mathbb{N}
. Then following Algorithm
1
, we can compute
\mathfrak{F}_{x}(k,h,\{\beta\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}})
from
k=a
. Then we have:

\mathfrak{F}_{x}(a,h,\{\beta\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}})=\dfrac{\ln\left(h_{a}^{-1}(x_{a})\right)}{\ln(\beta)}=\dfrac{\ln\left(\dfrac{a^{a}}{a!}\times\dfrac{a!}{(a+1)^{a}}\right)}{\ln\left(\dfrac{a}{a+1}\right)}=\dfrac{a\ln\left(\dfrac{a}{a+1}\right)}{\ln\left(\dfrac{a}{a+1}\right)}=a.

We conclude that we have to compare the
a+1
first terms of the sequence
x
to find
\mathfrak{s}(x)
and a integer maximizer of
x
. Using alternative approaches, we know that the maximal term of the sequence
x
is achived at
k=a
.

This example shows why we allow sequences of functions in upper bounds of the analyzed sequence whereas Theorem
1
suggests to use a constant sequence determined by an affine upper bound function. For example, defining
g:t\to t(a+1)^{a}
, we have for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x_{n}\leq g(\beta^{n})
. However, as
h_{n}\leq g
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have from Corollary
2

\mathfrak{F}_{u}(a,h,\{\beta\})\leq\mathfrak{F}_{u}(a,\{g\},\{\beta\})
. The main advantage of using a constant sequence is that we can compute a truncation index from
n=0
and
\mathfrak{F}_{u}(n,\{g\},\{\beta\})
is an upper bound of
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{x}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. More precisely, we have for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

\mathfrak{F}_{x}(n,\{g\},\{\beta\})=\dfrac{\ln(g^{-1}(x_{n})}{\ln(\beta)}=\dfrac{\ln\left(\dfrac{a^{n}}{a!(a+1)^{a}}\right)}{\ln\left(\dfrac{a}{a+1}\right)}=\dfrac{\ln\left(\dfrac{a^{n-a}}{a!}\right)}{\ln\left(\dfrac{a}{a+1}\right)}+a

We observe that the fifth statement of Theorem
3
holds that is as
x
is increasing on
[0,a]\cap\mathbb{N}
,
\mathfrak{F}_{x}(n,\{g\},\{\beta\})
is decreasing on
[0,a]\cap\mathbb{N}
. The smallest value of
\mathfrak{F}_{x}(n,\{g\},\{\beta\})
is achieved at
n=a
and

\mathfrak{F}_{x}(a,\{g\},\{\beta\})=-\dfrac{\ln(a!)}{\ln\left(\dfrac{a}{a+1}\right)}+a.

This leads to, for example,
\lfloor\mathfrak{F}_{x}(2,\{g\},\{\beta\})\rfloor=3
,
\lfloor\mathfrak{F}_{x}(5,\{g\},\{\beta\})\rfloor=31
and
\lfloor\mathfrak{F}_{x}(10,\{g\},\{\beta\})\rfloor=168
. The overapproximation gap totally explodes. This justifies to select the tightest possible upper bound of the analyzed sequence even if the upper bound is not a constant sequence.

4.2
Logistic Sequences

Let consider logistic sequences parameterized by
r\in(0,4]
starting at
y_{0}\in(0,1)
. More precisely, we have for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

y_{n+1}:=ry_{n}(1-y_{n})

We can exploit closed forms for
r\in(0,1)
. For
r\in(0,1)
, from
[
CB19
, Lemma A.6]
, we have at hand the upper-bound for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
y_{n}\leq y_{0}(r^{-n}+y_{0}n)^{-1}
. We then have for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
y_{n}\leq h_{n}(\beta^{n})
where
h_{n}:(0,1]\ni t\mapsto y_{0}(t^{-1}+ny_{0})^{-1}
and
h_{n}(0)=0
and
\beta=r
. Then, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
h_{n}
belongs to
\Omega([0,1])
and the sequence
(h_{n})_{n}
is decreasing. Moreover,
y_{0}=h_{0}(1)
and we can then apply the third statement of Proposition
6
to conclude that
\mathfrak{s}(y)=y_{0}
and
\operatorname{Argmax}(y)=\{0\}
.

In the general setting
r\in(0,4]
, we can always find an upper-bound
(h(\beta^{n}))_{n}
for
y
. Indeed, as
t\mapsto rt(1-t)
is bounded by
r/4
on
[0,1]
. Then we have
y_{n}\leq r/4+\beta^{n}
for all
\beta\in[0,1)
. Hence, we have,
y_{n}\leq h(\beta^{n})
where
h:x\mapsto r/4+x
. However, this function
h
is not useful since
y_{n}\leq h(0)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The key difficulty is to find a useful functional upper bound of the sequence.

4.3
The sequence of the ratio of two consecutive terms of a Fibonacci sequence

In this example, we consider the ratio of some Fibonacci sequences starting from
u_{0}\geq 0
and
u_{1}>u_{0}\phi
(for simplicity) where
\phi=(1+\sqrt{5})/2
(the golden number). By definition of Fibonacci sequences, we have for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
u_{n+2}=u_{n+1}+u_{n}
. It is well-known that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
u_{n}=A\phi^{n}+B(-\phi)^{-n}
where
A=\frac{u_{0}+u_{1}\phi}{1+\phi^{2}}
and

B=\frac{(u_{0}\phi-u_{1})\phi}{1+\phi^{2}}
. From, the assumption on
u_{0}
and
u_{1}
, we have
A>0
and
B<0
but
u_{n}>0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. Let us define:

\omega_{0}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}u_{1}/u_{0}&\text{ if }u_{0}>0\\
0&\text{ if }u_{0}=0\end{array}\right.\text{ and for all }n\in\mathbb{N}^{*},\omega_{n}:=\dfrac{u_{n+1}}{u_{n}}\kern 5.0pt.

We have for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
:

\omega_{n}=\phi\left(1+\frac{B\phi^{-2}(-1)^{n+1}-B}{A\phi^{2n}+B(-1)^{n}}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

This proves that
\boldsymbol{\ell}(\omega)=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\omega_{n}=\phi
. This also proves that for all
n
odd,
\omega_{n}<\phi
and for all
n
even and not null,
\omega_{n}>\phi
. Now as
B<0
, we have for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
:

\omega_{n}\leq h(\beta^{n})\text{ where }h:x\to\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\phi\left(1-\dfrac{B\phi^{-2}+B}{Ax^{-1}+B}\right)&\text{ if }x\neq 0\\
\\
\phi&\text{ if }x=0\end{array}\right.\text{ and }\beta=\phi^{-2}

with the equality when
n
is even.

The function
h
is strictly increasing and continuous on
[0,1]
and
\beta\in(0,1)
thus
(\{h\}_{n},\{\beta\}_{n})\in{\Gamma}_{0}^{\rm cst}(\omega)
. When
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
is even and thus the equality holds, we have
h^{-1}(\omega_{n})=(\phi^{-2})^{n}
. For the case where
n=0
, we also have
\omega_{0}\leq h(1)
. If
u_{0}
is null,
0
does not belong to
\mathcal{S}(\omega,h)
. If
u_{0}
is strictly positive, the equality
u_{0}=h(1)
holds and we can conclude from the third statement of Proposition
6
that
\mathfrak{s}(\omega)=\omega_{0}
and
\operatorname{Argmax}(\omega)=\{0\}
.

Now we suppose that
u_{0}=0
. We can evalute
\mathfrak{F}_{\omega}(k,\{h\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\{\beta\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}})
for all
k
such that
\omega_{k}>h(0)=\phi
. Actually,
\mathcal{S}(\omega,h)=\{2k:k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\}
.
For all
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,

\mathfrak{F}_{\omega}(2k,\{h\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\{\beta\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}})=\dfrac{\ln(h^{-1}(\omega_{2k}))}{\ln(\phi^{-2})}=(2k)\dfrac{\ln(\phi^{-2})}{\ln(\phi^{-2})}.

We conclude that
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{\omega}\leq 2
and since
\omega_{0}
and
\omega_{1}
are smaller than
\phi
,
\mathbf{K}^{s}_{\omega}=2
.

4.4
Syracuse sequences

Let consider a (accelerated) Syracuse iteration starting at
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. Formally, we define the following sequence:

y_{0}:=N\text{ and for all }n\in\mathbb{N},\ y_{n+1}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\dfrac{y_{n}}{2}&\text{ if }y_{n}\text{ is even}\\
\\
\dfrac{3y_{n}+1}{2}&\text{ if }y_{n}\text{ is odd}\end{array}\right.

(10)

The Collatz conjecture asserts that for all
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, the sequence
(y_{n})_{n}
defined according to (
10
) admits an index
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
y_{k}=1
. The Collatz conjecture trivially holds when we start with an integer
N\in\{1,2,3,4\}
.

Theorem 6
.

The Collatz conjecture holds for all integers
N\geq 5
if and only if there exists
(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times(0,1)\times(4,5]
such that
y_{n}\leq ab^{n}+c
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

If the Collatz conjecture holds then
\boldsymbol{\ell}(y)=4
as
(1,4,2,1)
is a cycle of the sequence. Since we suppose that
N\geq 5
,
{\mathrm{R}}_{u}^{s}
is nonempty and we can follow the proof of Lemma
1
and choose
c\in(4,5]\subset(\boldsymbol{\ell}(y),\mathfrak{s}(y))
. Again following the proof of Th
1
, we deduce suitable
a
and
b
to obtain the inequality.

Suppose that the inequality holds with
(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times(0,1)\times(4,5]
. As
c\in(4,5]
, then
\boldsymbol{\ell}(y)\leq c\leq 5
. As
y
is a sequence of strictly positive integers, it implies that there exists
M\in\mathbb{N}
such that
y_{n}\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}
for all
n\geq M
. The values 3 and 5 are not possible. Indeed, if
y_{n}=3
,
y_{n+1}=5
and
y_{n+2}=8
which is not less than 5.
∎

4.5
Linear Discrete-time dynamical systems

4.5.1
General case

Let consider a
d\times d
matrix
A
and the trajectories
x_{k+1}=Ax_{k}
starting from
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We suppose that
A
is stable i.e. its spectral radius is strictly less than 1.

Recall that all positive definite
d\times d
matrices
Q
define a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as follows
\left\|x\right\|_{Q}=\sqrt{x^{\intercal}Qx}
. From this, we can define an operator norm for
A
associated to
Q
as follows
\left\|A\right\|_{Q}^{2}=\max_{x\neq 0}\left\|Ax\right\|_{Q}/\left\|x\right\|_{Q}
. This norm is submultiplicative i.e.
\left\|BC\right\|_{Q}\leq\left\|B\right\|_{Q}\left\|C\right\|_{Q}
and thus implies that
\left\|A^{k}\right\|_{Q}\leq\left\|A\right\|_{Q}^{k}
. The fact that
A
is stable is equivalent to that for all matrix norms
\left\|A^{k}\right\|
tends to 0 as
k
goes to
+\infty
.

In
[
Shc17
]
, the authors are interested in the maximum of the Euclidean matrix norm of the powers of
A
i.e.
\max_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\|A^{k}\|_{2}
recalling that
\left\|A\right\|_{2}^{2}=\max_{x\neq 0}\left\|Ax\right\|_{2}/\left\|x\right\|_{2}
. Following
[
Adj25
]
, we can use solutions of the discrete Lyapunov equation i.e. a matrix from
\mathcal{L}_{A}:=\{P\succ 0:P-A^{\intercal}PA\succ 0\}
where the symbol
R\succ 0
means that
R
is positive definite. Now taking
P\in\mathcal{L}_{A}
, we can define the operator norm
\left\|A\right\|_{P}
as
P
is positive definite. The main advantage of using solutions of the discrete Lyapunov equation is to ensure that
\left\|A\right\|_{P}<1
. Moreover, let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
A^{k}x\neq 0
. Then:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\displaystyle{\dfrac{x^{\intercal}{A^{k}}^{\intercal}A^{k}x}{x^{\intercal}x}}&=&\displaystyle{\dfrac{x^{\intercal}{A^{k}}^{\intercal}A^{k}x}{x^{\intercal}{A^{k}}^{\intercal}P{A^{k}}x}\dfrac{x^{\intercal}{A^{k}}^{\intercal}PA^{k}x}{x^{\intercal}Px}\dfrac{x^{\intercal}Px}{x^{\intercal}x}}\\
&\leq&\displaystyle{\max_{y\neq 0}\dfrac{y^{\intercal}y}{y^{\intercal}Py}\left\|A^{k}\right\|_{P}^{2}\lambda_{\rm max}(P)}\\
&=&\displaystyle{\dfrac{1}{\displaystyle{\min_{y\neq 0}\frac{y^{\intercal}Py}{y^{\intercal}y}}}\left\|A^{k}\right\|_{P}\lambda_{\rm max}(P)}\\
&=&\displaystyle{\dfrac{\lambda_{\rm max}(P)}{\lambda_{\rm min}(P)}\left\|A^{k}\right\|_{P}^{2}}\leq\displaystyle{\dfrac{\lambda_{\rm max}(P)}{\lambda_{\rm min}(P)}\left\|A\right\|_{P}^{2k}}\end{array}

If
A^{k}
is not invertible, the inequality also holds for all non-zero
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the kernel of
A^{k}
. Finally taking the maximum over all non-zero
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we get for all
k\in\mathbb{N}

\left\|A^{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq h_{P}(\beta_{P}^{k})\text{ where }h_{P}:t\to\dfrac{\lambda_{\rm max}(P)}{\lambda_{\rm min}(P)}t\text{ and }\beta_{P}=\left\|A\right\|_{P}^{2}\kern 5.0pt.

Finally, let for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
z_{k}:=\left\|A^{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}
and define
z=(z_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
we have
(h_{P},\beta_{P})\in{\Gamma}_{0}^{\rm cst}(z)
. We can apply our method to compute the maximal value of the sequence
z
. As the terms
z_{k}
are strictly positive and for all
P\in\mathcal{L}_{A}
,
h_{P}(0)=0
, we have
\mathcal{S}(z,h_{P})=\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, we have:

\mathfrak{F}_{z}(k,\{h_{P}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\{\beta_{P}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}})=\dfrac{\ln\left(h_{P}^{-1}(z_{k})\right)}{\ln(\beta_{P})}=\dfrac{\ln\left(\dfrac{\lambda_{\rm min}(P)}{\lambda_{\rm max}(P)}\left\|A^{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)}{2\ln(\left\|A\right\|_{P}}.

which provides upper-bounds of the greatest maximizer of
z
.

Going beyond

We could consider as suggested in this paper non constant sequences

N
, if any, such that the sequence
(\left\|A_{\lambda}^{k}\right\|_{2})_{k}
is decreasing ) compared with the condition
P_{k}
minimizes

over
\mathcal{L}_{A_{\lambda}}
.
