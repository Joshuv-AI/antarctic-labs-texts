Factors associated with geriatric syndromes in older adults with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Chia-Ling Lin, Hsueh-Ching Wu, Neng-Chun Yu, Yuan-Ching Liu, Wu-Chien Chien
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThe purpose of this study was to estimate the prevalence of geriatric syndrome (GS) in patients with type 2 diabetes(T2D) and analyse the factors associated with GS in T2D patients.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study of 248 community-dwelling older adults with T2D. GS was assessed using the Activities of Daily Living (ADL), Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Taiwan Geriatric Depression Scale (TGDS), handgrip strength, and 5-m walking speed. Multivariate logistic regression was applied to examine factors associated with each syndrome.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 73.9 ± 5.9 years. The prevalence of functional dependence, depressive symptoms, cognitive impairment, and falls was 17.7%, 18.5%, 12.9%, and 19.4%, respectively. After adjusting for potential confounders, slower walking speed (< 0.7 m/s) was significantly associated with higher risks of functional dependence, depressive symptoms, and falls. In addition, each 1-kg increase in handgrip strength was associated with 13% lower odds of cognitive impairment.ConclusionSlower walking speed was associated with functional dependence, depressive symptoms, and falls in older adults withT2D. Greater handgrip strength was inversely associated with cognitive impairment. Assessing these simple measures may help identify individuals at risk for multidimensional decline at an early stage.Keywords: Cognitive impairment, Geriatric syndromes, Grip strength, Walking speed, Type 2 diabetics

Background

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) has become a major chronic disease in ageing societies. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) Diabetes Atlas 2025, the global prevalence of diabetes among adults aged 20–79 years is 11.1%, affecting approximately 589 million people, and is projected to rise to 853 million by 2050, among adults aged ≥ 65 years, prevalence was 23.7% in 2024 and is expected to increase slightly to 24.3% by 2050 [1].

Geriatric syndromes (GS) are multifactorial clinical conditions in older adults that are not attributable to a single disease entity. They commonly manifest as frailty, depression, malnutrition, cognitive impairment, incontinence, and falls, and are associated with functional and psychological decline [2, 3]. GS complicate clinical care by worsening disease management, impairing quality of life, and increasing the risk of disability and premature mortality. Early detection and prevention are therefore critical to mitigate adverse outcomes [4, 5].

Older adults with T2D are particularly vulnerable to GS, given the contribution of long-term vascular complications, poor glycaemic control, neuropathy, comorbidities, and polypharmacy [6, 7]. Previous research has highlighted the need to integrate the prevention and management of GS into diabetes care [8]. However, evidence on the associated factors of GS in community-dwelling older adults with T2D remains limited, especially regarding the utility of simple physical performance indicators such as walking speed and grip strength.

Therefore, this study aimed to examine the prevalence of GS and their associated factors in older adults with T2D, with a particular focus on walking speed and grip strength as practical markers to support early identification and targeted intervention in community settings.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted in community diabetes clinics. Participants were recruited using systematic random sampling during routine outpatient visits. Specifically, one patient was randomly selected out of every five check-ins. Those who met the inclusion criteria and provided consent were enrolled until the required number for each period was achieved.

The inclusion criteria were age ≥ 65 years, a diagnosis of T2D, clear consciousness, ability to communicate in Mandarin or Taiwanese, and willingness to participate. Exclusion criteria were disability, hearing or communication impairment, acute injury, recent surgery, or conditions affecting mobility, such as fractures or prior knee/hip surgery.

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power software. With α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and a moderate effect size (r = 0.3) referenced from Gobbens et al. [9],, the required sample size was calculated to be 200–300. The final effective sample comprised 248 participants.

Definition of outcomes

GS in this study were defined to include functional dependence, depressive symptoms, cognitive impairment, and falls within the past year.

Assessment tools

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Demographic and clinical characteristics included sex, age, education level, marital status, living status, number of chronic diseases, disease history, and number of falls in the past year. Laboratory and clinical indicators included haemoglobin A1C (A1C), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), body mass index (BMI), and the frequency of hypoglycaemia (< 70 mg/dl) and hyperglycaemia (> 250 mg/dl) during the past year. These data were obtained from medical records.

Functional and psychological assessments

Activities of Daily Living (ADL) was used to evaluate physical function; dependence was defined as ≥ 1 task disability [10]. Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) was used to assess cognitive function, with impairment defined as a score < 20 [11]. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 15-item Taiwan Geriatric Depression Scale (TGDS-15), which evaluates emotional status over the past week; responses are given in a yes/no format, yielding a total score of 0–15, with scores ≥ 5 indicating depression [12], details information has been described in previous research [13].

Physical performance indicators

Handgrip strength was measured three times on the dominant hand using an electronic dynamometer (TKK 5401; Takei, Tokyo, Japan) [14], and the mean value of the three trials was used for analysis to minimize measurement variability and random error [15]. Participants were instructed to stand upright with their arms by their sides and elbows fully extended, without touching any surface for support. The standing posture was adopted based on previous evidence suggesting that this position better reflects overall muscular performance [16].

Walking speed (WS) was assessed over a 5-metre distance (15 feet) at the participant’s usual walking pace. Time was recorded from the first step after a standing start to the last step across the finish line. Participants were instructed to walk at their normal, comfortable speed, as they would in daily life, and to continue walking past the finish line. The use of a cane or walker was permitted when needed. Gait speed was calculated as distance divided by time (m/s) [17].

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Review Board of National Yang-Ming University (IRB No. 2015A013). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrolment, and all data were anonymized to ensure confidentiality.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics (Table 1). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means ± standard deviations. Between-group differences were assessed using the chi-square test for categorical variables and independent t tests for continuous variables. The nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test was applied when the assumption of normality was not met.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 248 participants were included, with a mean age of 73.9 ± 5.9 years; 50.4% were men. The mean duration of T2D was 15.2 ± 9.0 years, with an average of 3.7 ± 1.4 chronic diseases. The mean HbA1c was 7.1 ± 1.1% (Table 1).

Prevalence of GS

The prevalence of functional dependence, depressive symptoms, cognitive impairment, and falls within the past year was 17.7%, 18.5%, 12.9%, and 19.4%, respectively. Stratified analyses showed that these conditions were generally more prevalent in women than in men (Fig. 1) and increased with advancing age (Fig. 2).

Associations of WS and handgrip strength with GS

Slower WS was associated with higher odds of functional dependence (AOR 0.12, 95% CI 0.02–0.78, p < 0.05), depressive symptoms (0.13, 0.02–0.76, p < 0.05), cognitive impairment (0.08, 0.01–0.93, p < 0.05), and falls (0.07, 0.01–0.42, p < 0.01). In addition, each 1-kg increase in handgrip strength was associated with 13% lower odds of cognitive impairment (AOR 0.87, 95% CI 0.78–0.97, p < 0.05) (Table 2).

Association of WS cutoffs with GS

When analysed by categories, participants with WS < 0.7 m/s had significantly higher odds of functional dependence (AOR 3.32, 95% CI 1.15–9.59), depressive symptoms (3.82, 1.33–11.02), and falls (3.59, 1.30–9.87) compared with those walking ≥ 0.9 m/s, but the association with cognitive impairment was not significant (AOR 4.93, 95% CI 0.81–30.16) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, the prevalence of GS, including functional dependence, depressive symptoms, cognitive impairment, and falls among older adults with T2D was 17.7%, 18.5%, 12.9%, and 19.4%, respectively. Notably, a WS < 0.7 m/s was independently associated with functional dependence, depressive symptoms, and falls. These rates are comparable to previous studies reporting that GS is common in patients with T2D [6, 18]. Prior work has suggested that exposure to macrovascular and microvascular complications may increase susceptibility to GS in T2D [7]. In addition, advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), generated through nonenzymatic binding of glucose and protein, accumulate in various tissues, accelerate ageing processes, and may contribute to the development of GS [19]. Diabetes and GS thus share overlapping risk factors and potential bidirectional associations, potentially reinforcing a cycle of vulnerability that adversely affects health outcomes in this population [20].

In our analysis, slower WS was associated with higher odds of functional dependence, depressive symptoms, cognitive impairment, and falls. Walking speed is widely recognized as an indicator of lower-extremity neuromuscular and physical function and has been linked to morbidity, mortality, and overall physical performance in older adults [21, 22]. Consistently, this study also found that older adults with T2D who walked at < 0.7 m/s had markedly higher odds of functional dependence, depressive symptoms, and falls compared with those walking ≥ 0.9 m/s [23]. Beyond reflecting physical performance, slow WS may also capture underlying pathophysiological processes. Diabetes-related vascular complications have been associated with disruptions in frontal–subcortical circuits and white matter integrity, which are thought to contribute to mobility decline [24]. Moreover, reduced WS is widely recognized as a hallmark of frailty [25]. Thus, slow WS not only reflects functional deterioration but may integrate vascular and geriatric pathways that contribute to increased vulnerability in this population [26].

Interestingly, the moderately slow WS group (0.7–<0.9 m/s) showed a significant association with cognitive impairment, whereas the very slow group (< 0.7 m/s) did not. This discrepancy may indicate a nonlinear relationship between WS and cognition. The < 0.7 m/s subgroup was relatively small (n = 56), yielding wide confidence intervals and limited power. Moreover, very slow gait often co-occurs with heterogeneous physical limitations, such as frailty, sarcopenia, or neuropathy, that can dominate gait performance and thereby attenuate the observable association with cognition [27, 28]. In contrast, mild-to-moderate gait slowing may capture early cognitive vulnerability involving executive and attentional control. Similar threshold signals around ~ 0.8 m/s have been reported in previous studies [29, 30]. These findings suggest a potential nonlinear association between gait speed and cognitive function, which should be further examined in larger cohort studies.

In this study, 19.4% of older adults with T2D reported at least one fall in the past year, making falls the most prevalent GS identified. A U.S. study reported that 23% of adults aged ≥ 65 experienced a fall within 12 months [31], which is broadly comparable to our finding. Previous studies have suggested that peripheral neuropathy and impaired gait stability are important contributors to falls in people with diabetes [32, 33].

Another notable finding was that each 1-kg increase in grip strength was associated with approximately 13% lower odds of cognitive impairment. A Korean study reported that higher grip strength was positively correlated with better cognitive function [34], and a longitudinal study in the United States found that every 5-kg increase reduced the likelihood of future cognitive impairment by 3% [35]. Although our study cannot determine directionality, these results, together with previous findings, suggest that grip strength reflects overall muscular and possibly neurocognitive health. Grip strength is a simple and reliable indicator of upper-limb muscle strength and can be easily measured in community or primary-care settings [16]. Routine assessment of grip strength may help identify older adults with T2D who could benefit from further cognitive or functional evaluation.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between WS, grip strength, and GS. Second, participants were recruited from a single community diabetes centre, which may limit the representativeness of the sample, thereby affecting the generalizability of the findings. Third, although gait speed was assessed, other gait parameters such as rhythm, stride length, and variability were not measured, which might provide additional insights into the relationship between mobility and GS. Fourth, both WS and grip strength are dynamic indicators; longitudinal follow-up would better elucidate their trajectories and relationships with health outcomes.

Conclusion

This study found that slower WS and lower handgrip strength were significantly associated with a higher likelihood of functional dependence, depressive symptoms, cognitive impairment, and falls among older adults with T2D. These findings support the potential utility of simple physical performance measures as practical screening indicators for identifying individuals at risk of GS in this population.
