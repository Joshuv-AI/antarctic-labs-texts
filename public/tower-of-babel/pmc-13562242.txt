Comparative efficacy and safety of fractional carbon dioxide and erbium-based fractional lasers for atrophic acne scars: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xin Song, Bing-Wei Zhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFractional laser resurfacing is widely used for atrophic acne scars, but the comparative efficacy and tolerability of fractional carbon dioxide (CO₂) and erbium-based fractional lasers remain uncertain because of heterogeneous outcome definitions and laser platforms. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to compare the clinical response, safety, and recovery profiles of fractional CO₂ and erbium-based fractional lasers for atrophic acne scars. PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library were searched from inception to April 2026. Comparative clinical studies evaluating fractional CO₂ lasers versus erbium: yttrium-aluminum-garnet (Er: YAG) or erbium-glass fractional lasers were included. Clinical response > 50% improvement was harmonized as the primary efficacy outcome. Relative risks (RRs), mean differences (MDs), and odds ratios (ORs) were pooled with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Five studies involving 130 analyzed participants were included. Four studies contributed to the primary efficacy analysis. Erbium-based lasers were associated with a lower probability of > 50% clinical response than fractional CO₂ laser (RR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.49–0.97; I²=45.9%). In the randomized-only sensitivity analysis, the direction was maintained but the CI included the null (RR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.54–1.08). Fractional CO₂ laser was associated with greater treatment-related pain (MD, 1.86; 95% CI, 1.33–2.39) and longer downtime (MD, 3.67 days; 95% CI, 1.90–5.44). No statistically significant differences were observed for post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (OR, 1.75; 95% CI, 0.67–4.57) or acneiform events (OR, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.12–4.25). Available comparative evidence suggests a favorable clinical-response signal for fractional CO₂ laser, accompanied by greater procedural pain and longer recovery. Introduction

Atrophic acne scars are common long-term sequelae of inflammatory acne and remain challenging to treat because of substantial variation in scar morphology, depth, anatomical distribution, and skin phototype [1]. Management therefore extends beyond improvement in scar texture and contour to encompass procedural tolerability, recovery time, and the risk of post-inflammatory pigmentary change [2]. Available treatments include microneedling, subcision, chemical reconstruction of skin scars, medium-to-deep chemical peeling, dermabrasion, punch techniques, injectable fillers, radiofrequency-based procedures, platelet-rich plasma, and combination regimens [3]. Treatment selection is generally individualized according to scar subtype and severity, skin type, expected downtime, and patient preference, and multimodal approaches are frequently considered because different scar morphologies may respond preferentially to different interventions [4]. Energy-based procedures remain an important component of contemporary acne-scar management, particularly for patients with clinically significant textural atrophy [5].

Among energy-based modalities, fractional lasers are widely used because they create microscopic treatment zones while preserving intervening skin, thereby promoting dermal remodeling and re-epithelialization. Fractional carbon dioxide (CO₂) laser and erbium-based fractional lasers, including erbium: yttrium-aluminum-garnet (Er: YAG) and erbium-glass systems, are among the most commonly used platforms [6, 7]. However, these devices differ in wavelength, tissue interaction, ablation profile, and residual thermal injury. Fractional CO₂ laser generally produces greater thermal coagulation and deeper tissue remodeling, whereas Er: YAG and erbium-glass systems may offer reduced thermal injury and shorter recovery [8, 9]. Recent reviews and network meta-analyses have broadened the comparative evidence base, but many combine heterogeneous laser, radiofrequency, platelet-rich plasma, and non-laser interventions, limiting device-specific interpretation. More focused comparisons of CO₂ and erbium-based lasers have also yielded inconsistent findings for efficacy, pain, downtime, and pigmentary adverse events, while relying largely on small trials with variable outcome definitions and follow-up durations [10–12].

Despite the growing comparative literature, an important gap remains in the interpretation of efficacy across fractional laser platforms because outcome definitions vary substantially among studies, while Er: YAG and erbium-glass lasers are often evaluated within different analytical frameworks [13, 14]. Consequently, the comparative efficacy of fractional CO₂ versus erbium-based fractional lasers has not been fully characterized using a uniform and clinically interpretable response threshold across distinct erbium platforms [15]. To address this gap, we performed a systematic review and meta-analysis that harmonized clinical response as > 50% improvement, evaluated Er: YAG and erbium-glass comparators within a unified framework, and examined the robustness of the findings through subgroup and sensitivity analyses.

Methods

Search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [16]. The protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number: CRD420261485358). A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library from database inception to April 2026 to identify comparative clinical studies evaluating CO₂ laser versus erbium-based fractional lasers for the treatment of atrophic acne scars. The eligible erbium-based comparators included ablative Er: YAG lasers and nonablative erbium-glass fractional lasers. No language restrictions were applied. Reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were manually screened to identify additional potentially eligible reports. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms, including Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) in PubMed and Emtree terms in Embase, with free-text terms related to atrophic acne scars, fractional CO₂ laser, erbium-based fractional lasers, laser resurfacing, comparative clinical studies, randomized trials, and split-face studies. The complete database-specific search strategies are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Studies were excluded if they were retrospective studies, prospective single-arm studies, case series, case reports, reviews, conference abstracts, editorials, letters, comments, study protocols, or animal/in vitro studies; did not include patients with atrophic acne scars or did not permit separate extraction of relevant data; did not directly compare fractional CO₂ laser with an eligible erbium-based fractional laser; or did not provide extractable comparative outcome data. Studies involving additional procedures or treatments were excluded when the co-intervention differed between treatment groups or treated sides and could therefore confound the comparison between laser modalities. Co-interventions applied equally to both treatment groups or both sides of a split-face study were permitted. Studies with unavailable full text or duplicated data from the same study population were excluded. For overlapping publications, the report providing the most complete relevant data was retained.

Study selection and screening

All records retrieved from the electronic databases were imported into EndNote, and duplicate records were removed before screening. Two reviewers independently screened the retrieved records in two stages according to the predefined eligibility criteria. First, titles and abstracts were reviewed to exclude clearly irrelevant reports. Second, the full texts of potentially eligible studies were independently assessed for final eligibility. Studies that did not meet the eligibility criteria were excluded, and reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were documented. Disagreements between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer was consulted when consensus could not be reached. The study-selection process was summarized using a PRISMA flow diagram.

Data extraction and outcome definitions

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers using a standardized data-extraction form. Extracted study characteristics included first author, year of publication, country, study design and treatment allocation, number of participants enrolled and analyzed, age, sex distribution, Fitzpatrick skin type, and timing of the final outcome assessment. Treatment-related data included laser device, laser type, ablative or nonablative mode, wavelength, energy or power, treatment density or coverage, pulse or dwell time, number of passes, number of treatment sessions, treatment interval, anesthesia or analgesia, cooling procedures, post-treatment care, and co-interventions when reported. For efficacy outcomes, extracted information included the assessment instrument, original grading scale or response categories, responder and non-responder definitions, assessment timepoint, type of assessor, blinding of outcome assessment, and numbers of responders and non-responders in each treatment group or treated side. Because efficacy instruments differed among studies, binary clinical response was harmonized for quantitative synthesis when compatible categorical data were available. A responder was defined as achieving > 50% improvement, corresponding to the upper two categories of the original study-specific improvement scales (for example, good/excellent improvement or 51%–75% and > 75% improvement). Improvement of ≤ 50% was classified as non-response. Continuous scar-assessment outcomes were not converted into binary outcomes when a verifiable categorical threshold or responder count was unavailable. For safety and recovery outcomes, treatment-related pain, downtime, PIH, and acneiform eruption or acne aggravation were extracted when reported. Continuous outcomes were extracted as means, standard deviations, and sample sizes, whereas dichotomous outcomes were extracted as event counts and corresponding denominators. Data that could not be reliably extracted were recorded as not reported (NR) and were not imputed for quantitative synthesis. Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer. When information was incomplete or unclear, the full text, tables, figures, and supplementary materials of the original study were re-evaluated.

Quality assessment

Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool. The assessment domains included random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other potential sources of bias. Each domain was classified as having low, unclear, or high risk of bias according to the Cochrane Handbook. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

Statistical analyses

Meta-analysis was performed using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). For the primary binary efficacy outcome, clinical response > 50% was defined as the event. Pooled effect estimates were expressed as relative risks (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The RR was calculated as the probability of clinical response with erbium-based fractional laser divided by the probability of clinical response with fractional CO₂ laser. Therefore, an RR < 1 indicated a higher response probability with fractional CO₂ laser. Continuous outcomes were summarized using mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs. Treatment-related pain was analyzed using a 0–10 visual analogue scale, and downtime was analyzed in days. Dichotomous adverse events, including PIH and acneiform eruption or acne aggravation, were summarized using odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic. A fixed-effect model was applied when heterogeneity was low or moderate (I² <50% and P > 0.10 for Cochran’s Q test); otherwise, a random-effects model was used. Exploratory subgroup analyses of the primary efficacy outcome were conducted according to erbium comparator type (erbium-glass vs. Er: YAG), study design (split-face vs. parallel-group), and analyzed sample size (< 30 vs. ≥ 30 participants). Differences between subgroups were evaluated using subgroup interaction tests. Sensitivity analyses were performed by restricting the efficacy analysis to studies with confirmed randomized allocation. Leave-one-out analyses were also performed by sequentially excluding each study contributing to the primary binary efficacy analysis. Studies without verifiable binary responder counts were excluded from the binary efficacy analysis, and continuous outcomes without the variance information required for pooling were excluded from the corresponding analysis. Because only four studies contributed to the primary binary efficacy meta-analysis, formal statistical tests and funnel-plot assessment of publication bias were not performed. All statistical tests were two-sided. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, except for Cochran’s Q test, for which P < 0.10 was considered indicative of statistically detectable heterogeneity.

Results

Search results and study selection

A total of 635 records were identified through the literature search, including 602 records from electronic databases and 33 records from registers. Before title and abstract screening, 259 duplicate records were removed, 108 records were excluded by automation tools, and 86 records were removed for other predefined reasons, leaving 182 records for screening. Of these, 158 were excluded after title and abstract screening. The full texts of 24 reports were sought for retrieval, of which 3 could not be obtained due to unavailable full-text articles. Consequently, 21 full-text reports were assessed for eligibility. Among these, 16 reports were excluded: 7 were reviews, 3 were overlapping publications from the same study populations, 3 did not provide extractable comparative outcome data, and 3 were non-comparative clinical studies without an eligible comparator. Ultimately, 5 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis [17–21] (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

Five comparative studies published between 2010 and 2023 were included, with 134 participants enrolled and 130 included in the final analyses. The studies were conducted in South Korea, Egypt, Thailand, Germany, and Iran. Three studies used randomized split-face designs, one was a parallel-group randomized controlled trial, and one was a prospective fixed-side split-face comparative study. Sample sizes were small, ranging from 8 to 58 participants. Two studies compared fractional CO₂ laser with nonablative erbium-glass lasers at wavelengths of 1,540 or 1,550 nm, whereas three compared fractional CO₂ laser with ablative Er: YAG laser at 2,940 nm. Fractional CO₂ treatment was delivered at 10,600 nm in all studies. Final outcome assessment ranged from 1 to 6 months after treatment or the final treatment session. Participant age, sex distribution, and Fitzpatrick skin type were variably reported across studies (Table 1).

Treatment protocols also varied among studies. The number of treatment sessions ranged from one to four, and repeated treatments were generally administered at intervals of 3 weeks to 2 months. Reported laser settings differed in energy or power, treatment density, pulse duration, and number of passes. Topical EMLA anesthesia was reported in both erbium-glass studies, whereas air cooling was explicitly reported in only one study; anesthesia and cooling procedures were incompletely reported in several Er: YAG studies. Study design and risk-of-bias considerations

The included studies comprised four studies with randomized treatment allocation and one prospective fixed-side split-face study without randomized side assignment (Mokhtari et al.). This distinction was considered in the interpretation of the quantitative findings and addressed through sensitivity analyses restricted to studies with confirmed randomized allocation. The design classification and eligibility of individual studies for major quantitative outcomes are summarized in Supplementary Table S3. All five included studies were assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool. The assessments were performed across seven domains, including random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other potential sources of bias. Comparative clinical response

Four studies provided extractable binary clinical-response data and were included in the primary efficacy synthesis. For quantitative synthesis, clinical response was harmonized as greater than 50% improvement across the four studies with extractable binary data, corresponding to the upper two categories of their respective quartile-based physician assessments. Reinholz et al. reported continuous scar-depth and POSAS-based outcomes but did not provide a verifiable binary responder threshold or responder count and was therefore not included in the binary response meta-analysis. The study-specific instruments, response definitions, assessment timepoints, and original responder counts are detailed in Supplementary Table S4.

For the primary analysis, the event was clinical response greater than 50%, and the RR was calculated as the response probability with the erbium-based laser relative to that with fractional CO₂ laser. The pooled RR was 0.69 (95% CI, 0.49–0.97; I² = 45.9%, P for heterogeneity = 0.136), indicating a lower pooled probability of achieving the predefined clinical-response threshold with erbium-based lasers than with fractional CO₂ laser. Thus, because the numerator of the RR was the erbium-based laser group, an RR below 1 favored fractional CO₂ laser (Fig. 3).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses showed point estimates below 1 across comparator type, study design, and sample-size strata, although the confidence intervals for all individual subgroups included the null value. For erbium-glass comparators, the pooled RR was 0.69 (95% CI, 0.43–1.12; I² = 0.0%), whereas the estimate for Er comparators was 0.41 (95% CI, 0.08–2.14; I² = 80.1%). There was no evidence of a difference between comparator subgroups (P for subgroup difference = 0.552). The corresponding estimates were 0.41 (95% CI, 0.13–1.36) for split-face studies and 0.72 (95% CI, 0.44–1.18) for the single parallel-group trial, with no significant subgroup difference (P = 0.396). Similarly, no subgroup difference was detected according to analyzed sample size (P = 0.360) (Table 2).

When the analysis was restricted to the three studies with both confirmed randomized allocation and extractable binary response data, the pooled RR was 0.76 (95% CI, 0.54–1.08; I² = 0.0%, P for heterogeneity = 0.658). Leave-one-out analyses produced RR estimates ranging from 0.40 to 0.76; however, the 95% CI crossed 1 in each analysis. These analyses therefore showed that the direction of the point estimates remained consistent, whereas the statistical significance of the overall pooled result was sensitive to the inclusion of individual studies (Supplementary Table S5).

Adverse events and recovery-related outcomes

Recovery-related and adverse-event outcomes were reported inconsistently across studies. Two studies provided sufficient mean and variance data for downtime. The pooled mean difference was 3.67 days (95% CI, 1.90–5.44; I² = 54.2%, P for heterogeneity = 0.139), with longer reported downtime after fractional CO₂ treatment. Four studies contributed pain data measured on a 0–10 visual analogue scale. Pain scores were higher with fractional CO₂ laser, with a pooled mean difference of 1.86 points (95% CI, 1.33–2.39; I² = 41.2%, P for heterogeneity = 0.165).

Four studies reported post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation. The pooled OR was 1.75 (95% CI, 0.67–4.57; I² = 0.0%, P for heterogeneity = 0.709), with no statistically significant between-treatment difference. Two studies contributed data on acneiform eruption or acne aggravation, for which the pooled OR was 0.73 (95% CI, 0.12–4.25; I² = 30.7%, P for heterogeneity = 0.230), also without a statistically significant difference (Table 3).

Assessment of small-study effects

Only four studies contributed to the primary binary efficacy meta-analysis. Given the limited number of included studies, formal statistical tests for small-study effects and funnel-plot assessment of publication bias were not performed.

Discussion

Atrophic acne scars remain a clinically challenging condition because treatment selection requires balancing scar remodeling efficacy, tolerability, and recovery characteristics [22]. Fractional CO₂ and erbium-based fractional lasers are among the most widely used energy-based modalities, yet direct comparative evidence between these approaches has remained heterogeneous and difficult to interpret due to differences in laser mechanisms, assessment instruments, and outcome definitions [11]. This systematic review and meta-analysis provides a structured comparison of fractional CO₂ lasers with both erbium-glass and Er: YAG fractional lasers by integrating comparative clinical studies and harmonizing the primary efficacy outcome as clinical response exceeding 50% improvement. In addition to evaluating comparative scar improvement, this study simultaneously examined clinically relevant recovery and safety outcomes, including pain, downtime, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, and acneiform adverse events. By distinguishing erbium-glass from Er: YAG comparators and incorporating sensitivity analyses restricted to studies with confirmed randomized allocation, this analysis provides a more comprehensive interpretation of the comparative evidence landscape. The findings may assist clinicians in individualized treatment selection by highlighting the relative efficacy signals and tolerability profiles of these commonly used fractional laser platforms, while supporting evidence-based discussions with patients regarding expected improvement and recovery characteristics [23].

In this meta-analysis, fractional CO₂ laser showed a more favorable direction of effect for achieving greater than 50% clinical improvement compared with erbium-based fractional lasers, with consistent point estimates across prespecified subgroup analyses. The overall pooled estimate suggests that the comparative efficacy signal remained present across different erbium-based comparator categories and study designs, although subgroup analyses did not demonstrate statistically significant differences between erbium-glass and Er: YAG lasers. This finding indicates that the observed treatment contrast was not clearly attributable to a specific erbium-based platform within the available evidence. From a biological perspective, the potential difference between fractional laser modalities may be related to their distinct patterns of energy delivery and tissue interaction [24]. Fractional CO₂ lasers generate ablative microthermal columns at 10,600 nm, resulting in controlled epidermal disruption and dermal thermal remodeling, which may promote collagen remodeling processes relevant to atrophic scar improvement. Erbium-glass lasers primarily induce nonablative fractional photothermolysis at 1,540–1,550 nm, whereas Er: YAG lasers at 2,940 nm provide ablative resurfacing with different water absorption characteristics and tissue penetration profiles [25]. These differences may contribute to variations in clinical response patterns among studies. However, because individual treatment parameters, including energy settings, density, and treatment schedules, varied across studies, the comparative efficacy findings should be interpreted as an overall evidence pattern rather than evidence of uniform superiority across all clinical scenarios [26, 27].

Safety and recovery outcomes provide important complementary information when comparing fractional laser modalities for atrophic acne scars. In this analysis, fractional CO₂ laser was associated with higher treatment-related pain scores and longer downtime compared with erbium-based fractional lasers among studies with available quantitative data. These findings are consistent with the characteristics of ablative fractional resurfacing, in which deeper thermal injury and greater tissue ablation may increase acute discomfort and prolong epithelial recovery [28]. However, the pooled analyses of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and acneiform eruption did not demonstrate statistically significant differences between treatment modalities, suggesting that these specific adverse outcomes were not clearly differentiated by laser category in the available comparative studies. The clinical interpretation of these findings should incorporate the practical implications of recovery requirements. Fractional CO₂ laser may be considered in treatment strategies where a more intensive resurfacing approach is acceptable, whereas erbium-based fractional lasers may represent alternative options when shorter recovery periods or reduced procedural discomfort are prioritized. In clinical practice, treatment selection should therefore consider not only the magnitude of scar improvement but also patient preferences, expected recovery time, skin characteristics, and individualized tolerance for procedure-related adverse effects [29].

These findings should also be interpreted in the context of recent comparative meta-analyses. Husein-ElAhmed et al. [30] reported no significant overall difference in investigator- or participant-reported scar improvement between erbium and CO₂ lasers, although sensitivity analysis excluding studies at higher risk of bias yielded an estimate favoring CO₂, a pattern broadly consistent with the favorable but not uniformly robust efficacy signal observed in our analysis. Liu et al. [11]subsequently reported higher treatment efficacy with fractional CO₂ than with Er: YAG laser, together with greater treatment-related pain and no significant difference in post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, findings that are largely concordant with our results. However, their analysis suggested shorter downtime with CO₂, whereas our pooled estimate indicated longer recovery, highlighting a potentially important discrepancy that may reflect differences in study eligibility, recovery definitions, laser parameters, and the studies contributing to the respective pooled estimates. More recently, Xue et al. [15] found no significant difference in > 50% improvement between Er: Glass and CO₂ lasers, while Er: Glass was associated with less pain, shorter crusting and erythema, and a comparable risk of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation. This is consistent with our nonsignificant erbium-glass subgroup and the overall tolerability profile observed for erbium-based platforms. Compared with these previous reviews, our analysis integrates both ablative Er: YAG and nonablative erbium-glass comparators within a unified framework, harmonizes efficacy as > 50% clinical improvement, excludes retrospective studies, and incorporates randomized-only and leave-one-out sensitivity analyses, thereby providing a more structured assessment of the comparative efficacy and tolerability of these fractional laser modalities.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although this meta-analysis synthesized comparative clinical studies, the available evidence was limited by the small number of eligible studies, heterogeneous study designs, and reliance on previously published clinical data. Only five comparative studies were included, most of which were split-face trials with relatively small sample sizes and short follow-up durations. Follow-up ranged from 1 to 6 months, which may be insufficient to fully characterize longer-term scar remodeling or delayed pigmentary sequelae. Considerable variation was also present in laser platforms, treatment parameters, session number, adjunctive procedures, and outcome definitions, particularly for recovery-related endpoints. In addition, efficacy assessment was not fully standardized across studies, with differences in physician-rated and patient-reported measures. These methodological inconsistencies may reduce the certainty and comparability of the pooled estimates. Despite these limitations, the present study focused on direct comparative evidence, evaluated both efficacy and safety outcomes, and examined the robustness of the findings through subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Future studies should adopt standardized scar-classification systems, prespecified laser parameters, consistent definitions of clinical response, downtime, and adverse events, and longer follow-up periods. Stratification by scar subtype and Fitzpatrick skin type, together with the use of validated patient-reported outcome measures, may further improve the clinical applicability and comparability of future comparative trials.

Conclusions

Overall, the available comparative evidence suggests a favorable efficacy signal for fractional CO₂ laser in the treatment of atrophic acne scars, while also showing greater treatment-related pain and longer downtime than erbium-based fractional lasers. Given the uncertainty in the available evidence, treatment selection should remain individualized and should consider expected clinical improvement, procedural tolerability, recovery time, and patient-specific risk of pigmentary adverse events.
