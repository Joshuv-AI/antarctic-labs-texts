Distribution of mixed character sums and extremal problems for Littlewood polynomials
Jonathan W. Bober, Oleksiy Klurman, Besfort Shala

Abstract
. We prove distributional results for mixed character sums ∑ n ≤ x χ ⁡ ( n ) ​ e ​ ( n ​ θ ) , \sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)e(n\theta), for fixed θ ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \theta\in[0,1] and random character χ ​ (mod q ) \chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}} , as well as for a fixed character χ \chi and randomly sampled θ ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] . \theta\in[0,1]. We present various applications of our results. For example, we construct Littlewood polynomials with large Mahler measure, thus establishing a new record in the Mahler problem (1963). An important ingredient in our work is a general way of dealing with “log-integrability” problems.



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we develop an approach aiming to understand the mixed
character sums

S(\chi,x,\theta)=\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)e(n\theta),

where
\chi
is a (multiplicative) Dirichlet character mod
q
and

e(x):=e^{2\pi ix}
is the additive character on
\mathbb{R}.
We are motivated by
the following natural questions.

Problem 1
.

For various ranges of parameters
q,x\to\infty
determine the distribution of

S(\chi,x,\theta)
as
\chi
runs over a family of Dirichlet characters.

Problem 2
.

Let
\chi_{q}
be a quadratic character. What is the limiting distribution of

S(\chi_{q},x,\theta)
when
\theta\in[0,1]
is chosen uniformly at random and

q\to\infty
?

In the case
\theta=0
, Problem
1
simply asks for the understanding
of the partial sums
\sum_{n<x}\chi(n)
, which is the most studied aspect
of this question. One of the notable results in this regard is that of Harper
[
17
]
, who showed that in the range when both
x
and
q/x
tend to infinity with
q
, the sums
S(\chi,x,0)
typically exhibit better than square-root cancellation.

Following Montgomery and Vaughan
[
32
]
,
Bober and Goldmakher
[
3
]
and later Bober, Goldmakher, Granville
and Koukoulopoulos
[
2
]
studied the distribution of
\max_{x}S(\chi,x,0)

as
\chi
varies over the characters mod
q
. Hussain
[
19
]
,
inspired by
[
25
]
,
considered the
sum
S(\chi,tq,0)
as a function of
t
and studied its distribution for randomly chosen
\chi
.
Subsequently the more difficult case of quadratic characters was dealt with by
Lamzouri
[
27
]
and Hussain and Lamzouri

[
18
]
, and more recently Lamzouri and Nath

[
26
]
study the distribution of the maximum of partial sums of
cubic characters.

In the case when
\theta\neq 0
, Wang and Xu
[
38
]
show that a smoothed version of
S(\chi,x,\theta)
is typically of size
\sqrt{x}
for all irrational
\theta
satisfying a weak Diophantine condition, contrasting Harper’s result.
Very recently, Dell and Milićević
[
9
]
investigated the distribution
of the incomplete Gauss sums
t\rightarrow S(\chi_{q},tq,1/q)
,
where
\chi_{q}
is the Jacobi symbol mod
q
and
q
is chosen randomly in a dyadic interval, which is
an example of Problem
1
with rational
\theta\neq 0.

In general, the limiting behavior significantly depends on the length
x=x(q)
of the
character sum, and in this paper we confine ourselves to the fixed range

\frac{x}{q}\to\beta\neq 0
when
q\to\infty.
To this end, we consider for a
prime
q
and a primitive (non-principal) Dirichlet character
\chi
mod
q
the shifted-mixed
character sum

S(\chi,\alpha,\beta,\theta)=\sum_{\alpha q<n<(\alpha+\beta)q}\chi(n)e(n\theta).

For some of our applications we will think of this as the polynomial

\sum_{\alpha q<n<(\alpha+\beta)q}\chi(n)x^{n}

evaluated on the unit circle, thus naturally generalizing the well-known
variants of Fekete (
\alpha=0
and
\beta=1
) and Turyn (
\beta=1
)
polynomials.

We will take a different approach to these distributional questions, motivated
by the work of Klurman, Munsch and Lamzouri
[
23
]
on Fekete
polynomials. For each integer
k\in[0,q-1]
and Dirichlet character
\chi,
we
define the function

F_{k,\chi,\alpha,\beta}(t)=\frac{e(-\alpha k)}{q^{1/2}}\sum_{\alpha q<n<(\alpha+\beta)q}\chi(n)e(n(k+t)/q),

a normalised version of the sum
S(\chi,\alpha,\beta,\theta)
, with
\theta=(k+t)/q
and
t\in[0,1).
We regard

F_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}:\hat{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\rightarrow\mathscr{C}[0,1]
as the discrete random
process obtained by choosing
\chi
uniformly at random from the set of
all
characters mod
q.
We will consider the limiting behavior of

F_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}
as
q\rightarrow\infty
over the primes, with
k
either
fixed or tending to infinity as well.

For each integer
k
we define the random process

F_{k,\alpha,\beta}(t)=\mathbb{X}\frac{e(\alpha t)}{2\pi i}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(\alpha l)e(\beta(l+t))-1)}{l+t}\mathbb{W}(k-l),

where
\mathbb{W}(l)
is a completely multiplicative Steinhaus random variable
and
\mathbb{X}
is a random variable uniformly distributed on the unit circle,
independent of
\mathbb{W}
. As we shall see, when
k
tends to infinity the effect of multiplicativity
vanishes, and so we also define the random process

F_{\alpha,\beta}(t)=\frac{e(\alpha t)}{2\pi i}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(\beta(l+t))-1}{l+t}\mathbb{X}(l),

where the
\mathbb{X}(l)
are independent realizations of
\mathbb{X}
.
Both of these processes are almost surely continuous. Our first main result is
the following convergence statement in the space of continuous functions
\mathscr{C}[0,1].

Theorem 1
.

Fix real numbers
\alpha
and
\beta
. Then

(1)

For each integer
k
, the sequence of random processes

F_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}
converges in distribution to
F_{k,\alpha,\beta}

as
q\to\infty
over the primes.

(2)

For each sequence

k_{q}\rightarrow\infty
with
k_{q}=q^{o(1)}
,
the sequence of random processes

F_{k_{q},q,\alpha,\beta}
converges in distribution to
F_{\alpha,\beta}

as
q\to\infty
over the primes.

(3)

For each sequence

k_{q}\rightarrow\infty
with
k_{q}=o(\pi(q))
,
there is a full density subset
\mathcal{P}
of the primes
such that the sequence of random processes

F_{k_{q},q,\alpha,\beta}
converges in distribution to
F_{\alpha,\beta}

as
q\in\mathcal{P}
tends to infinity.

(4)

For each irrational

\theta
, there is a full density subset
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}
of the primes
such that the sequence of random processes
F_{\left\lfloor\theta q\right\rfloor,q,\alpha,\beta}
converges in distribution to
F_{\alpha,\beta}
as
q\in\mathcal{P}_{\theta}
tends to infinity.

We discuss the size restrictions on
k_{q}
and the necessity of removing a zero
density subset of primes in Sections
2
and
6
.

Applied to Problem
1
, we obtain the following corollary, addressing
a question of Wang and Xu
[
38
]
.

Corollary 1
.

For each irrational
\theta
and real number
\beta
there is a full density
subset
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}
of the primes such that for every
t_{0}\in[0,1]
, when

\left\{q\theta\right\}\rightarrow t_{0}
for a sequence of
q\in\mathcal{P}_{\theta}
, the
sequence

\frac{1}{q^{1/2}}\sum_{n<\beta q}\chi(n)e(n\theta)\xrightarrow{\phantom{dd}d\phantom{dd}}F_{0,\beta}(t_{0})

as
\chi
varies over the characters mod
q
.

We now turn to Problem
2
and fix
\chi_{q}
as the quadratic character mod
q
.
We partition the interval
[0,1]
into pieces
[\frac{k}{q},\frac{k+1}{q})
for
k\in[0,q-1]
and our aim is to show that the behavior of the character sum
S(\chi,\alpha,\beta,\theta)
in a randomly chosen subinterval is governed by an explicit random point process.
For each prime
q
, let

G_{q,\alpha,\beta}:\{0,1,\ldots,q-1\}\rightarrow\mathscr{C}[0,1]
be the random process

k\rightarrow\frac{q^{1/2}}{\tau(\chi_{q})}F_{k,\chi_{q},\alpha,\beta}(t)
,
where the set
\{0,1,\ldots,q-1\}
is equipped with the uniform measure.
Finally, we introduce the random process

G_{\alpha,\beta}(t)=\frac{e(\alpha t)}{2\pi i}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(\alpha l)(e(\beta(l+t))-1)}{l+t}\mathbb{Y}(l),

where
\mathbb{Y}(l)
are independent random variables uniformly distributed in
\{-1,1\}
.

Theorem 2
.

For each fixed
\alpha
and
\beta
, the random process

G_{q,\alpha,\beta}
converges in distribution to

G_{\alpha,\beta}
as
q\to\infty
along the primes.

We apply these results in conjunction with some new ideas to make progress on
old questions related to the extremal properties of Littlewood polynomials.

1.1.
Application to the Mahler problem

Our first application concerns a classical quantity of an algebraic
polynomial
P\in\mathbb{C}[x].
Recall the
L_{\lambda}
norm of
P
,
given, for
\lambda>0
, by

\|P\|_{\lambda}=\left(\int_{0}^{1}|P(e(t))|^{\lambda}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{1/\lambda}

and the Mahler measure of
P
, defined as

(1)

M_{0}(P):=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{1}\log|P(e(t))|\mathrm{d}t\right).

In 1963, Mahler
[
29
]
(also mentioned as Problem 10 by Borwein in his
book
[
5
]
) posed the following problem.

Problem 3

(Mahler Problem)
.

What is

b_{m}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{M_{0}(P_{n})}{\sqrt{n}},

where
P_{n}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}x^{k}
with
a_{k}=\pm 1
for all
k
?

Problem
3
is a younger cousin of the infamous Lehmer problem
[
28
]
, asking for integer polynomials with a small Mahler measure.
It easy to see that
\frac{M_{0}(P)}{\|P\|_{2}}<1,
but even the question of whether
b_{m}=1
is wide
open. The conclusion that
b_{m}=1
would immediately follow from the
existence of “ultra-flat” polynomials (Littlewood conjecture) with coefficients
\pm 1.
Indeed, if

P_{n}
is a Littlewood polynomial satisfying the pointwise bound

(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}\leq|P_{n}(z)|\leq(1+\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}

for
|z|=1,
then plugging this estimate into (
1
) and letting

\varepsilon\to 0
yields
b_{m}=1.
In particular, this implies that if one
relaxes the condition allowing complex unimodular coefficients
|a_{k}|=1,
then
we can achieve the upper bound
1.
This follows from the existence of complex
ultra-flat polynomials which were famously constructed by Kahane
[
22
]
using a
probabilistic approach (see also related work of Bombieri and Bourgain

[
4
]
for a deterministic construction). However, Erdős conjectured (Problem 5 in
[
5
]
) that Littlewood
ultra-flat polynomials do not exist.

Interestingly, in
1970
, Fielding
[
14
]
showed directly (without
appealing to the ultra-flatness) that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{M_{0}(P_{n})}{\sqrt{n}}=1,

when the supremum is taken over all complex unimodular polynomials of degree
n.
Beller and Newman
[
1
]
quantitatively refined this by
showing that the expression inside the supremum is
1-O(\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}).

The Mahler problem, which may be regarded as a “flatness” question —
albeit in a somewhat weaker form — has attracted considerable attention over
the last few decades. Choi and Erdélyi
[
7
]
proved that for each
n
,
there exists Littlewood polynomial
f_{n}
satisfying
M_{0}(f_{n})/\sqrt{n}\geq 1/2+o(1)
and consequently
b_{m}\geq 1/2.
In
[
6
]
they established a
stronger result, determining the expected value of the normalized Mahler
measure for random
\pm 1
Littlewood polynomials

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}M_{0}(f_{n}))}{\sqrt{n}}=e^{-\gamma/2}=0.749\ldots,

where
\gamma=0.57721...
denotes Euler’s constant. Building on the work of Rodgers
[
36
]
, Erdélyi
[
13
]
showed
that for the Rudin–Shapiro polynomials
P_{k}(x)
and
Q_{k}(x)
, the value of the
normalized Mahler measure approaches

0.8576\dots
when
k\to\infty.

This provided a record value in the Mahler problem.

Very recently, a new approach to the Mahler question was introduced, relying on
the solution in
[
23
]
of an old problem determining the Mahler measure
of Fekete polynomials
F_{q}(z).
Previously, using subharmonic methods, Erdélyi and
Lubinsky
[
11
]
proved the lower bound
M_{0}(F_{q})\geq(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)\sqrt{q},
which was improved in
[
12
]

to
M_{0}(F_{q})\geq(\frac{1}{2}+c_{1})\sqrt{q},
for some small value of
c_{1}>0.
In

[
23
]
, the authors showed that
M_{0}(F_{q})\sim c\sqrt{q},
where

c=0.748\dots
, by introducing new probabilistic ideas.
Mossinghoff
[
33
]
used these techniques to compute the Mahler measure of Turyn
polynomials (corresponding to the shift
\alpha=\frac{1}{4}
and
\beta=1
)
to significantly improve the record value to
b_{m}\geq 0.951\dots.
We note that
these works crucially relied on the analysis of
complete exponential
sums
in both the probabilistic and “log-integrability” parts of the arguments
(see Section
2
for a more detailed discussion).

We instead apply our distributional results for incomplete exponential sums
(Theorem
2
) in conjunction with a new
general method of dealing with the log-integrability problem to show the
following result, improving the record in the Mahler problem.

Corollary 2
.

There exist Littlewood polynomials of arbitrarily large degree
with normalized Mahler measure
>.954
. Consequently,
b_{m}>.954
.

The last result follows, after some straightforward adjustments, from the following statement.

Theorem 3
.

We have

\exp\left(\int_{0}^{1}\log\left|\frac{1}{(1.1q)^{1/2}}S(\chi_{q},.2,1.1,\theta)\right|\mathrm{d}\theta\right)\longrightarrow c\approx.954\ldots

as
q\rightarrow\infty
over the primes.

These
values come from an approximate numerical
calculation, and we do not wish to give the impression that the Mahler
measure is maximized at these precise values of
\alpha
and
\beta
.
The flexibility of our methods, however, offers a possible way to modify
the construction of the polynomials further, obtaining better bounds. We will explore this in
future work.

In fact, Mossinghoff
[
33
]
already conjectured that a larger normalized Mahler measure might
be achievable by considering
\beta\neq 1
, in analogy with the result of Jedwab,
Katz, and Schmidt
[
21
]
that the merit factor of the generalized Turyn
polynomial is maximized at
\alpha\approx.221
and
\beta=1.058
. Using methods of this paper one can show that for these values

\exp\left(\int_{0}^{1}\log\left|\frac{1}{(1.058q)^{1/2}}S(\chi_{q},.221,1.058,\theta)\right|\mathrm{d}\theta\right)\approx.9535,

which also provides improvement over the bound in
[
33
]
and shows that for the generalized Turyn polynomials, the merit factor and the
normalized Mahler measure are not maximized at the same point.

1.2.
Application to the Günther-Schmidt conjecture

M
In 2017, Günther and Schmidt
[
16
]
determined
the limiting value of the normalized
L_{2k}
norm of the Fekete and Turyn
polynomials when
k\in\mathbb{N}
. More precisely, they proved that there is a function

\phi_{k}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\lim_{q\to\infty}\|\frac{F_{q,\left\lfloor\alpha q\right\rfloor}}{\sqrt{q}}\|_{2k}=\phi_{k}(\alpha)
and described a method
to compute
\phi_{k}(0).
The expressions for
\phi_{k}(\alpha)
are given by a
rather complicated recursive combinatorial identities, which make them hard to
use in practice. However, they showed that
\phi_{k}(\alpha)
attains its minimum
at
\alpha=\frac{1}{4}
for
k=2,3,4
and conjectured that this continues to
hold for every
k\geq 5.
As an application of our results, we confirm this
conjecture.

Theorem 4
.

For every integer
k\geq 2,
we have

\emph{argmin}_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\phi_{k}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{4}.

Theorem
4
implies that the polynomials

F_{q,\left\lfloor\frac{q}{4}\right\rfloor}(\theta):=S(\chi_{q},\frac{1}{4},1,\theta)
tend to
be somewhat flat, and therefore it is natural to expect that the Mahler
measure
M_{0}(F_{q,\left\lfloor\frac{q}{4}\right\rfloor})
is large, explaining the large value
achieved in
[
33
]
.

Finally, we remark that by using the same method as in the proof of Theorem
4
, one would be able to directly recover the main result of
[
21
]
on the largest known merit factor of Littlewood polynomials, offering a different proof.

2.
Roadmap to the proofs

We now briefly describe some key steps in our proofs,
and at the end of this section indicate more precisely how Theorems
1
and
2

are proved.
The starting point in the proof of both Theorem
1
and
Theorem
2
is an application of the quantitative Poisson
summation formula (see Proposition
3
) which transforms
exponential sums into conditionally convergent series

(2)

\frac{e(\alpha t)\tau(\chi)}{2\pi iq^{1/2}}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(\alpha l)(e(\beta(l+t))-1)}{l+t}\overline{\chi}(k-l).

From this formula we begin to see how the random processes that appear in
Theorems
1
and
2

emerge. Indeed, for most characters, the sum (
2
) is well
approximated by a short sum and so we expect to establish convergence to the random process

F_{k,\alpha,\beta}(t)=\mathbb{X}\frac{e(\alpha t)}{2\pi i}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(\alpha l)e((\beta(l+t))-1)}{l+t}\mathbb{W}(k-l).

As we shall see, for large
k=k_{q}\to\infty
,
the multiplicativity generally becomes unimportant at the cost of removing a
zero density subset of primes, so that the limiting process becomes

F_{\alpha,\beta}(t)=\frac{e(\alpha t)}{2\pi i}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(\beta(l+t))-1}{l+t}\mathbb{X}(l).

Here the
\mathbb{X}
corresponding to the Gauss sum and the
e(\alpha l)

terms have gone away, as they do not change the random process.

In Theorem
2
, when we fix the quadratic
character and choose
k
uniformly at random, we follow similar arguments, but
now we exploit randomness coming from the shifts
\{\chi(k-l)\}_{l\leq L(q)}
to arrive at the limiting process

G_{\alpha,\beta}(t)=\frac{e(\alpha t)}{2\pi i}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(\alpha l)(e(\beta(l+t))-1)}{l+t}\mathbb{Y}(l).

An interesting feature of Theorem
1
is the presence of the exceptional set of primes to facilitate convergence of the sequence of random processes
F_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}.

Roughly speaking, when analyzing moments of the distribution

F_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}
, we are led to consider solutions to the polynomial
equations

(3)

\prod_{i=1}^{d}(k+m_{i})\equiv\prod_{j=1}^{d}(k+n_{j}){\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}

where
k=k_{q}
and the variables
m_{i}
and
n_{j}
are
small (depending on
q
). One would like to argue that the mod

q
condition becomes irrelevant as
q
gets large, so that the moments of (
2
) coincide with the random multiplicative model. This is indeed what happens for fixed
k
. The mod
q
condition also becomes irrelevant when
k_{q}=q^{o(1)}
, and in this case the moments of (
2
) coincide with the random model without multiplicativity. However, for certain values of
k=k_{q}\to\infty
, this need not be the case; if, say,
k\equiv(q+1)/3\pmod{q}
(or
k=\left\lfloor q\theta\right\rfloor
is very close to
(q+1)/3
for irrational
\theta
), then this
equation becomes

\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1+3m_{i})\equiv\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1+3n_{j}){\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}

and the mod
q
condition does indeed become superfluous by size
considerations, but this shows that the mod
q
condition was an
essential feature of the first equation. In the case
k_{q}=\left\lfloor q\theta\right\rfloor
,
such linear coincidences happen only finitely often for any irrational

\theta
, however, so do not present a problem.

More insidious are the coincidences of a higher degree.
.
For any fixed choice
of
m_{1},\ldots,m_{d}
and
n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}
, equation
(
3
) becomes a degree
d-1
equation in
k
, so we naturally arrive at
the question of how often is
k=k_{q}
the solution of
polynomial equations with small coefficients mod
q.
In Lemma
6
, we show that when
k_{q}=o(\pi(q))
or
k_{q}=\left\lfloor q\theta\right\rfloor
for irrational
\theta
, this happens for
o(\pi(X))
choices of primes
q\leq X
. We then use iterative density arguments to construct a density one set of suitable primes
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}
to avoid such coincidences for higher moments when
q\to\infty.

We now describe the key ideas to prove Theorem
3
.
We write the logarithmic Mahler measure of
S_{q}:=S(\chi_{q},\alpha,\beta,\theta)
as

\log M_{0}(S_{q})=\int_{0}^{1}\log\left|q^{1/2}\frac{S_{q}(e(t))}{q^{1/2}}\right|dt=\log\sqrt{q}+\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\int_{0}^{1}\log\bigg|F_{q,\alpha,\beta}\bigg(e\bigg(\frac{k+t}{q}\bigg)\bigg)\bigg|\mathrm{d}t.

We will eventually show that

\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\int_{0}^{1}\log\bigg|F_{q,\alpha,\beta}\bigg(e\bigg(\frac{k+t}{q}\bigg)\bigg)\bigg|\mathrm{d}t\longrightarrow\E\left[\int_{0}^{1}\log\left|G_{\alpha,\beta}(t)\right|\mathrm{d}t\right].

However, the functional
\ell(f)=\int_{0}^{1}\log|f(t)|dt
is not
continuous on
\mathscr{C}[0,1]
and so we cannot immediately apply Theorem

2
.
We thus have to deal with the issues of uniform log-integrability, which is
a difficult problem for both random and deterministic series.
To this end, we consider the regularized continuous functional

\ell_{\varepsilon}(f)=\int_{0}^{1}\log(|f(t)|)w_{\varepsilon}(\left|f(t)\right|)\mathrm{d}t

with
w_{\varepsilon}(t)
a smooth minorant of the indicator function
\mathds{1}_{t\geq\varepsilon}
, to which our convergence results of Theorem

2
apply.

Our problem then reduces to estimating the logarithmic integrals

\int_{0}^{1}\log\lvert f(t)\rvert\mathds{1}_{\lvert f(t)\rvert<\varepsilon}\text{d}t

when
\varepsilon

is small and
f
runs over all functions of the form (
2
). We
note that neither the series of
f
nor any of its higher order derivatives
converge absolutely (unlike in the case of Fekete and Turyn polynomials)
and so new ideas are needed to prove uniform bounds. To overcome this significant obstacle, we prove the following soft general result,
possibly of independent interest (which we shall use in future work).

Proposition 1
.

Let
\Delta=\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\partial^{i}
be a linear differential operator of order
k
with constant real coefficients, and suppose that
\Delta f(t)\geq 1
for all
t\in[\alpha,\beta]
for
f\in\mathscr{C}^{k}[\alpha,\beta].
Then for small enough
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\log\lvert f(t)\rvert\mathds{1}_{\lvert f(t)\rvert<\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}t\ll\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2k}},

where the implied constant is uniform in
f
, but may depend on
\Delta,\alpha
and
\beta
.

With this in hand, we cook up a suitable linear differential operator
\Delta

and cover
[0,1]
by a number of
fixed
intervals, such that
either
|\textup{Re }(\Delta f)|\geq 1
or

|\textup{Im }(\Delta f)|\geq 1
across each interval and then apply Proposition

1
to reach the conclusion.

Finally, in order to prove Theorem
4
, we use our Theorem

2
to conveniently rewrite the norms
L_{2k}
and then establish a relevant monotonicity property of the modified Lerch
transcendent to arrive at the result.

2.1.
Proofs of Theorems 1 and 2

We use the approach of Prokhorov to prove relative compactness of our
sequences of random processes. This will allow us to determine the

3.

We begin by recalling the “twisted” Poisson summation formula.

Lemma 1

For a primitive Dirichlet character

Proof.

A version of this is, for example, Theorem 10.5 of

We will also use the following quantitative bound for convergence of Fourier

Lemma 2

Suppose that

Equipped with these lemmas we are in a position to state and prove our general

Proposition 3
.

For a primitive Dirichlet character
\chi
mod
q
and for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}

F_{k,\chi,\alpha,\beta}(t)=e(\alpha t)\frac{\tau(\chi)}{2\pi iq^{1/2}}\sum_{\left|l\right|<K}\frac{e(\alpha l)(e(\beta(l+t))-1)}{l+t}\overline{\chi}(k-l)\\
+O\left(\frac{q^{1/2}\log(q)}{K}+\frac{1}{q^{1/2}}\min\left(1,\frac{1}{K\left\|\alpha q\right\|}\right)+\frac{1}{q^{1/2}}\min\left(1,\frac{1}{K\left\|(\alpha+\beta)q\right\|}\right)+\frac{\log K}{Kq^{1/2}}\right).

In particular, for any
\alpha
and
\beta
such that
\alpha q
and

(\alpha+\beta)q
are not integers,

(5)

F_{k,\chi,\alpha,\beta}(t)=e(\alpha t)\frac{\tau(\chi)}{2\pi iq^{1/2}}\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{\left|l\right|<K}\frac{e(\alpha l)(e(\beta(l+t))-1)}{l+t}\overline{\chi}(k-l).

Note that for

and

the summand becomes
2\pi i\beta
; for

and

it becomes
2\pi i\beta e(-\alpha)

Proof.

We apply the Poisson summation formula (Lemma

)
to the function

f(x)=e(x\theta)\rect((x-\alpha q)/(\beta q)-1/2).

We find that

\sum_{\alpha q\leq n\leq\alpha q+\beta q}\chi(n)e(n\theta)=e(\alpha q\theta)\frac{\tau(\chi)}{2\pi i}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(-\alpha m)(e(\beta(\theta q-m))-1)}{\theta q-m}\overline{\chi}(m).

Now write
\theta=(k+t)/q
, with
k=\left\lfloor q\theta\right\rfloor
and
t=\left\{\theta\right\}
.
Then

\begin{split}e(-\alpha q\theta)\sum_{\alpha q\leq n\leq\alpha q+\beta q}\chi(n)e(n\theta)=&\frac{\tau(\chi)}{2\pi i}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(-\alpha m)(e(\beta(k+t-m))-1)}{k+t-m}\overline{\chi}(m)\\
=&\frac{\tau(\chi)}{2\pi i}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(-\alpha(k-l))(e(\beta(l+t))-1)}{l+t}\overline{\chi}(k-l).\end{split}

Hence

\frac{2\pi ie(-\alpha\left\{q\theta\right\})}{\tau(\chi)}\sum_{\alpha q\leq n\leq\alpha q+\beta q}\chi(n)e(n\theta)=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(\alpha l)(e(\beta(l+t))-1)}{l+t}\overline{\chi}(k-l).

Let
g(\alpha)
denote the left hand side, which, for the moment, we think of
as a function of
\alpha

[0,1]
and the right hand side
is a Fourier series. Then

\int_{0}^{1}\hskip-5.0pt\min\hskip-2.0pt\bigg(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{(2K+1)\pi\sin\pi x}\bigg)|\mathrm{d}g(\alpha+x)|\leq\frac{\left\{q\theta\right\}}{q^{1/2}}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\hskip-5.0pt\min\hskip-2.0pt\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{(2K+1)\pi\sin\pi x}\right)\mathrm{d}x\\
+\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\min\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{(2K+1)\pi\sin\pi x_{j}}\right)+\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\min\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{(2K+1)\pi\sin\pi y_{j}}\right)\Bigg],

where

x_{j}=\frac{j}{q}-\alpha\ \ \ \textrm{ and }\ \ \ y_{j}=\frac{j}{q}-(\alpha+\beta).

We can bound the latter two sums by

O\left(\frac{q\log(q)}{K}+\min\left(1,\frac{1}{\left\|\alpha q\right\|}\right)+\min\left(1,\frac{1}{\left\|(\alpha+\beta)q\right\|}\right)\right)

and the integral by
O((\log K)/K)
, so that Lemma

gives us the quantitative bound claimed.
∎

Remark
.

Variants of this identity, again for complete sums (

[

[

[

We will first prove that we can truncate the infinite sum over
l
when
we vary either the character or the shift. We choose some slowly growing function

and for each

\chi

For each

\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}

which comes from choosing a character
\chi
mod

and the random process
\tilde{G}_{q,\alpha,\beta}

again adjusting

to remove the contribution of the

In most of what follows, the only important properties of

. We may think of

Proposition 4
.

Let
L(q)
be some function such that
L(q)\rightarrow\infty
and

L(q)\ll q^{\varepsilon}
for all
\varepsilon>0
.
For fixed real numbers
\alpha
and
\beta
and
for any bounded Lipschitz function
h:\mathscr{C}[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and
any sequence of integers
k_{q}
, the expected value
\E h(F_{k_{q},q,\alpha,\beta})

approaches
\E h(\tilde{F}_{k_{q},q,\alpha,\beta})
as
q
tends to infinity over the
primes, in the sense that

\left|\E h(F_{k_{q},q,\alpha,\beta})-\E h(\tilde{F}_{k_{q},q,\alpha,\beta})\right|\rightarrow 0.

That is,
F_{k_{q},q,\alpha,\beta}
converges weakly to some
F
(as
q
tends
to infinity over the primes, or over some subsequence of the primes) if and
only if
\tilde{F}_{k_{q},q,\alpha,\beta}
converges weakly to
F
.

Similarly, the expected value
\E h(G_{q,\alpha,\beta})
approaches

\E h(\tilde{G}_{q,\alpha,\beta})
.

Proof.

We will abbreviate
F_{k,\chi}:=F_{k,\chi,\alpha,\beta}
.
We start simply with the triangle inequality.

\left|F_{k,\chi}(t)-\tilde{F}_{k,\chi}(t)\right|^{2}\ll\left|\tilde{F}_{k,\chi}(t)-\frac{\tau(\chi)}{2\pi iq^{1/2}}\sum_{\left|n\right|<q}\frac{e(\alpha n)(e(\beta(n+t)-1)}{t+n}\overline{\chi}(k-n)\right|^{2}\\
+\left|F_{k,\chi}(t)-\frac{\tau(\chi)}{2\pi iq^{1/2}}\sum_{\left|n\right|<q}\frac{e(\alpha n)(e(\beta(n+t)-1)}{t+n}\overline{\chi}(k-n)\right|^{2}.

The second term is
O((\log q)/q^{1/2})
by the quantitative bound of
Proposition
3
for any nontrivial character
\chi
mod
q
,
so

\left|F_{k,\chi}(t)-\tilde{F}_{k,\chi}(t)\right|^{2}\ll\left|\sum_{L(q)<|n|<q}\frac{e(\alpha n)(e(\beta(n+t)-1)}{t+n}\overline{\chi}(k-n)\right|^{2}+\frac{\log q}{q}.

For
t\in[0,1]
and
|n|>L(q)
, we have
1/(n-t)=1/n+O(1/n^{2})
,
so again from the triangle inequality we have

(6)

\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|F_{k,\chi}(t)-\tilde{F}_{k,\chi}(t)\right|^{2}\ll\\
\left|\sum_{L(q)<|n|<q}\frac{e((\alpha+\beta)n)}{n}\overline{\chi}(k-n)\right|^{2}+\left|\sum_{L(q)<|n|<q}\frac{e(\alpha n)}{n}\overline{\chi}(k-n)\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{L(q)^{2}}.

Now taking the average over all nontrivial characters we find that

\frac{1}{q-2}\sum_{\chi\neq\chi_{0}}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|F_{k,\chi}(t)-\tilde{F}_{k,\chi}(t)\right|^{2}\ll\\
\frac{1}{q-2}\sum_{\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}}\left|\sum_{L(q)<|n|<q}\frac{e((\alpha+\beta)n)}{n}\overline{\chi}(k-n)\right|^{2}+\\
\frac{1}{q-2}\sum_{\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}}\left|\sum_{L(q)<|n|<q}\frac{e(\alpha n)}{n}\overline{\chi}(k-n)\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{L(q)^{2}}\\
\ll\frac{q-1}{q-2}\sum_{|n|>L(q)}\frac{1}{n^{2}}+\frac{1}{L(q)^{2}}\ll\frac{1}{L(q)}.

Upon applying Markov’s inequality it follows that the number of primitive
\chi
such that

\|F_{k,\chi,\alpha,\beta}-\tilde{F}_{k,\chi,\alpha,\beta}\|>z
is bounded by

O\left(\frac{q}{z^{2}L(q)}\right)
.

We now suppose that
h:\mathscr{C}[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a bounded Lipschitz function with

\left|h(x_{1})-h(x_{2})\right|\leq K\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|
and
|h(x)|\leq B
for all
x_{1}
and

x_{2}
. Then for any
z
we have

\begin{split}\E h(F_{k,q,\alpha,\beta})-\E h(\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta})&=\E\left[h(F_{k,q,\alpha,\beta})-h(\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta})\right]\\
&\leq Kz+B\prob\left[\left\|F_{k,\chi,\alpha,\beta}-\tilde{F}_{k,\chi,\alpha,\beta}\right\|>z\right]\\
&\leq Kz+O\left(\frac{B}{z^{2}L(q)}+\frac{B}{q}\right)\end{split}

(the term
B/q
arises from the contribution of the trivial character).
Choosing
z=L(q)^{-1/3}
yields desired conclusion.

For the quadratic case, we start with (
6
) and average over

k
.
Notice that

\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\left|\sum_{L(q)<|n|<q}\frac{e(xn)}{n}\overline{\chi}(k-n)\right|^{2}\\
\ll\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\left|\sum_{L(q)<n<q}\frac{e(xn)}{n}\overline{\chi}(k-n)\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\left|\sum_{L(q)<-n<q}\frac{e(xn)}{n}\overline{\chi}(k-n)\right|^{2}

and

\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\left|\sum_{L(q)<\epsilon n<q}\frac{e(xn)}{n}\overline{\chi}(k-n)\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{q}\hskip 3.00003pt\sum\sum_{\hskip-18.0278pt\begin{subarray}{c}L(q)<\epsilon m<q\\
L(q)<\epsilon n<q\end{subarray}}\frac{e(xn-xm)}{nm}\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\overline{\chi}(k-n)\chi(k-m)

for each choice of
\epsilon=\pm 1
.
When
\chi
is the quadratic character, the inner sum is
-1
unless
n=m
,
in which case it is
q-1
. Thus

\begin{split}\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\left|\sum_{L(q)<\epsilon n<q}\frac{e(xn)}{n}\overline{\chi}(k-n)\right|^{2}&=\frac{q-1}{q}\sum_{L(q)<\epsilon n<q}\frac{1}{n^{2}}-\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L(q)<\epsilon n<q\\
L(q)<\epsilon m<q\\
n\neq m\end{subarray}}\frac{e(xn-xm)}{nm}\\
&\ll\frac{1}{L(q)}+\frac{(\log q)^{2}}{q}\ll\frac{1}{L(q)}.\end{split}

We therefore have exactly the same quality bounds when we average over
k
as when we average
over
\chi
and the proof is now complete.
∎

4.
Tightness and continuity

Lemma 3
.

For any fixed
\alpha
and
\beta
,
there is a constant
C
such that for all
k
and
q
and
for all
s,t\in[0,1]
, we have

\E\left|\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}(s)-\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}(t)\right|^{2}\leq C\left|s-t\right|^{2}

and similarly

\E\left|\tilde{G}_{q,\alpha,\beta}(s)-\tilde{G}_{q,\alpha,\beta}(t)\right|^{2}\leq C\left|s-t\right|^{2}.

Proof.

Write
c(n,t)=e(\alpha n)(e(\beta(n+t)-1)
.
From the definition of
\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}
we have

\displaystyle\E\Big|\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}(s)

\displaystyle-\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}(t)\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q-1}\sum_{\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}}\left|\frac{\tau(\chi)}{2\pi q^{1/2}}\sum_{\left|n\right|<L(q)}\frac{c(n,s)\overline{\chi}(k-n)}{s+n}-\frac{c(n,t)\overline{\chi}(k-n)}{t+n}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}(q-1)}\sum_{\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}}\left|\sum_{\left|n\right|<L(q)}\overline{\chi}(k-n)\left(\frac{c(n,s)}{s+n}-\frac{c(n,t)}{t+n}\right)\right|^{2}.

It is now convenient to take just half the sum, so take

\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
which maximizes the sum

\sum_{\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}}\left|\sum_{0\leq\epsilon n<L(q)^{A}}\overline{\chi}(k-n)\left(\frac{c(n,s)}{s+n}-\frac{c(n,t)}{t+n}\right)\right|^{2},

so that

\displaystyle\E\Big|\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}(s)

\displaystyle-\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}(t)\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}}\left|\sum_{0\leq\epsilon n<L(q)}\overline{\chi}(k-n)\left(\frac{c(n,s)}{s+n}-\frac{c(n,t)}{t+n}\right)\right|^{2}.

Then opening up the sum and using orthogonality we have

\E\left|\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}(s)-\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}(t)\right|^{2}\ll\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{0\leq\epsilon n<L(q)}\left|\frac{c(n,s)}{s+n}-\frac{c(n,t)}{t+n}\right|^{2}

as long as
q^{1/2}>L(q)
.

By exactly the same computations and again using the fact that

\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\chi_{q}(k)\chi_{q}(k+a)=q-1
when
a\equiv 0

and
-1
otherwise, we find that

\E\left|\tilde{G}_{q,\alpha,\beta}(s)-\tilde{G}_{q,\alpha,\beta}(t)\right|^{2}\ll\sum_{0\leq\epsilon n<L(q)}\left|\frac{c(n,s)}{s+n}-\frac{c(n,t)}{t+n}\right|^{2}\\
+\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq\epsilon n<L(q)\\
0\leq\epsilon m<L(q)\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{c(n,s)}{s+n}-\frac{c(n,t)}{t+n}\right)\left(\frac{\overline{c(m,s)}}{s+m}-\frac{\overline{c(m,t)}}{t+m}\right).

Now, the derivative of
c(n,t)/(t+n)
is
O(1/(|n|+1))
for
t\in[0,1]
, so

\left|\frac{c(n,s)}{s+n}-\frac{c(n,t)}{t+n}\right|\ll\frac{\left|s-t\right|}{n+1}

by the mean value theorem
and hence

\E\left|A(s)-A(t)\right|^{2}\ll\left|s-t\right|^{2}\sum_{0\leq n<L(q)}\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}}+\frac{\left|s-t\right|^{2}}{q}\left(\sum_{0\leq n<L(q)}\frac{1}{n+1}\right)^{2}\\
\ll\left|s-t\right|^{2},

where
A=\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}
or
\tilde{G}_{q,\alpha,\beta}
.
∎

We will now show that all our processes are almost-surely continuous.

Lemma 4
.

For any real
\alpha
and
\beta
and any integer
k
,
each of the random processes
F_{k,\alpha,\beta}(t)
,

F_{\alpha,\beta}(t)
and
G_{\alpha,\beta}(t)
is almost-surely
continuous.

Proof.

The calculations we give here for
F_{k,\alpha,\beta}
work equally well
for
F_{\alpha,\beta}
and
G_{\alpha,\beta}
with simple modifications (in the latter case, those are simpler as the random variables involved are independent). We make a change of variables and write

F_{k,\alpha,\beta}(t)=\mathbb{X}\frac{e(\alpha t)}{2\pi i}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(\alpha(k-l))e(\beta(k-l+t))-1)}{k-l+t}\mathbb{W}(l).

To prove continuity, we can ignore the multipliers out front and any finite number of terms, so we stay away from the region where the
denominator is
0.
Consider the sum

S=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\in\mathbb{Z}\\
|l|>2k\end{subarray}}\frac{e(\alpha(k-l))e(\beta(k-l+t))-1)}{k-l+t}\mathbb{W}(l).

and note

\frac{1}{k-l+t}=\frac{1}{-l}+\frac{k+t}{l(k-l+t)}.

Consequently

S=e(\alpha k)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\in\mathbb{Z}\\
|l|>2k\end{subarray}}\frac{e(-\alpha l)}{l}\mathbb{W}(l)-e((\alpha+\beta)k+\beta t)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\in\mathbb{Z}\\
|l|>2k\end{subarray}}\frac{e(-\beta l)}{l}\mathbb{W}(l)\\
+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\in\mathbb{Z}\\
|l|>2k\end{subarray}}\frac{e(\alpha(k-l))e(\beta(k-l+t))-1)(k+t)}{l(k-l+t)}\mathbb{W}(l).

The last sum is absolutely convergent, hence continuous for
any realization of
\mathbb{W}(l)
. So the only problems with convergence
may arise from the first two sums. But moment bounds easily show that
they converge with probability 1. More generally, we will shortly compute
moment bounds for all finite dimensional distributions of

F_{k,\alpha,\beta}
which imply almost-sure convergence for every
t
.
∎

5.

We now examine the mixed moments of the finite dimensional distributions
of our various random processes.

5.1.
Varying character
\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}

We use the following bound for the sum of characters weighted by the Gauss sum.

Lemma 5
.

For any
a
mod
q
and
n\geq 1
we have

\frac{1}{q-1}\sum_{\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}}\chi(a)\tau(\chi)^{n}\leq nq^{(n-1)/2}.

Proof.

This is essentially the same as Lemma 8.3 of
[

,
except that we sum over all characters instead of just the odd characters.
Putting in the definition of the Gauss sum and using the orthogonality

Proposition 5
.

For
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{J}\in[0,1]
,
r_{1},\ldots,r_{J},s_{1},\ldots,s_{J}\in\mathbb{N}
,
fixed
\alpha
and
\beta
, and for fixed
k

\E\left[\prod_{j=1}^{J}\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})^{r_{j}}\overline{\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})^{s_{j}}}\right]\longrightarrow\E\left[\prod_{j=1}^{J}F_{k,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})^{r_{j}}\overline{F_{k,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})^{s_{j}}}\right]

as

over the primes. Moreover, this moment sequence of

is determinate, so the finite dimensional distributions of

\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}
tend to those of
F_{k,\alpha,\beta}
as
q
tend to infinity
over the primes.

Proof.

Let
r=\sum r_{j}
and
s=\sum s_{j}
and write

\mathbf{m_{j}}=(m_{j,1},\dots,m_{j,r_{j}},m_{j,r_{j}+1},\dots,m_{j,r_{j}+s_{j}})

and

a(\mathbf{m}_{j};t_{j})=\prod_{i=1}^{r_{j}}c(m_{k,i},t_{j})\prod_{i=r_{j+1}}^{s_{j}+r_{j}}c(-m_{k,i},-t_{j})

where

c(n,t)=\frac{e(\alpha n)(e(\beta(n+t)-1)}{t-n}.

Using the above notation, we have

(7)

\prod_{j=1}^{J}\tilde{F}_{k,\chi,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})^{r_{j}}\overline{\tilde{F}_{k,\chi,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})}^{s_{j}}\\
=e(\alpha T)\frac{\tau(\chi)^{r}\overline{\tau(\chi)}^{s}}{(2\pi)^{r+s}q^{(r+s)/2}}\sum_{\mathbf{m}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{J}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\prod_{\ell=1}^{r_{j}}\overline{\chi}(k+m_{j,\ell})\prod_{\ell=r_{j}+1}^{s_{j}}\chi(k+m_{j,\ell})\prod_{j=1}^{J}a(\mathbf{m}_{j};t_{j}),

where

T=\sum_{j=1}^{J}(r_{j}-s_{j})t_{j}

and the quantity

\mathbf{m}_{j}=(m_{j,1},\dots,m_{j,r_{j}},m_{j,r_{j}+1},\dots,m_{j,r_{j}+s_{j}})

ranges over all
(r_{j}+s_{j})
-tuples of integers
|m_{j,l}|\leq L(q)
.
Note that

\frac{\tau(\chi)^{r}\overline{\tau(\chi)^{s}}}{q^{(r+s)/2}}=\tau(\chi)^{|r-s|}/q^{|r-s|/2},

so bringing the sum over characters inside, we have

\E\left[\prod_{j=1}^{J}\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})^{r_{j}}\overline{\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})}^{s_{j}}\right]=\\
e(\alpha T)\sum_{\mathbf{m}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{J}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}a(\mathbf{m}_{j};t_{j})\frac{1}{q-1}\sum_{\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}}\frac{\tau(\chi)^{|r-s|}}{q^{|r-s|/2}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\prod_{\ell=1}^{r_{j}}\overline{\chi}(k+m_{j,\ell})\prod_{\ell=r_{j}}^{s_{j}}\chi(k+m_{j,\ell}).

For the off diagonal terms (
r\neq s
), we can now insert the bound from Lemma

5
for the inner sum to find that

\E\left[\prod_{j=1}^{J}\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})^{r_{j}}\overline{\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})}^{s_{j}}\right]\leq|r-s|q^{-1/2}\sum_{\mathbf{m}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{J}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\left|a(\mathbf{m}_{j};t_{j})\right|.\ll\frac{(\log L(q))^{r+s}}{q^{1/2}},

where for the final inequality we notice that

\sum_{\mathbf{m}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{J}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\left|a(\mathbf{m}_{j};t_{j})\right|=\prod_{j=1}^{J}\left|\sum_{|m|<L(q)}c(m,t_{j})\right|^{r_{j}+s_{j}}\ll(\log L(q))^{r+s}.

For the limiting process, the off diagonal case is much easier: rotation
invariance tells us that

\E\left[\prod_{j=1}^{J}F_{k,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})^{r_{j}}\overline{F_{k,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})}^{s_{j}}\right]=0

whenever
r\neq s
.

Meanwhile, when
r=s
, the terms
\tau(\chi)^{|r-s|}/q^{|r-s|/2}
go away and

\frac{1}{q-1}\sum_{\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\prod_{\ell=1}^{r_{j}}\overline{\chi}(k+m_{j,\ell})\prod_{\ell=r_{j}+1}^{s_{j}}\chi(k+m_{j,\ell})=\mathcal{S}_{q}(k,\mathbf{m}),

say, where

\mathcal{S}_{q}(k,\mathbf{m})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\textrm{ if }\prod_{j=1}^{J}\prod_{\ell=1}^{r_{j}}(k+m_{j,\ell})\equiv\prod_{j=1}^{J}\prod_{\ell=r_{j}+1}^{s_{j}}(k+m_{j,\ell})\not\equiv 0{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}\\
0&\textrm{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Then

\E\left[\prod_{j=1}^{J}\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})^{r_{j}}\overline{\tilde{F}_{k,q,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})}^{s_{j}}\right]=e(\alpha T)\sum_{\mathbf{m}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{J}}\mathcal{S}_{q}(k,\mathbf{m})\prod_{j=1}^{J}a(\mathbf{m}_{j};t_{j}).

When
r=s
, the same computations yield

\E\left[\prod_{j=1}^{J}F_{k,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})^{r_{j}}\overline{F_{k,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})}^{s_{j}}\right]=\\
e(\alpha T)\sum_{\mathbf{m}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{J}}\mathcal{S}(k,\mathbf{m})\prod_{j=1}^{J}a(\mathbf{m}_{j};t_{j}),

where there is now no restriction on the size of
\mathbf{m}_{j}
and

\mathcal{S}(k,\mathbf{m})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\textrm{ if }\prod_{j=1}^{J}\prod_{\ell=1}^{r_{j}}(k+m_{\ell})=\prod_{j=1}^{J}\prod_{\ell=r_{j}+1}^{s_{j}}(k+m_{\ell})\neq 0\\
0&\textrm{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

As
q\rightarrow\infty
, for any fixed
r,s
and
k
, it is clear that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{m}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{J}\\
\left|m_{j,l}\right|<L(q)\end{subarray}}\mathcal{S}_{q}(k,\mathbf{m})\prod_{j=1}^{J}a(\mathbf{m}_{j};t_{j})\longrightarrow\sum_{\mathbf{m}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{J}}\mathcal{S}(k,\mathbf{m})\prod_{j=1}^{J}a(\mathbf{m}_{j};t_{j}),

provided that the right hand side is absolutely convergent, as the congruence
conditions in the definition of
\mathcal{S}_{q}
play no role for large enough
q
.
Now note that
c(n,t)\ll 1/(\left|n\right|+1)
, so
a(\mathbf{m}_{j},t)\ll\prod_{i=1}^{r_{j}+s_{j}}(\left|m_{j,i}\right|+1)^{-1}
.
To see that the right hand side is absolutely convergent it is convenient to
reorder by the size of
\prod_{j=1}^{J}\prod_{i=1}^{r_{j}}(k+m_{j,i})
. Then
we see that

(8)

\E\left[\prod_{j=1}^{J}F_{k,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})^{r_{j}}\overline{F_{k,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})}^{s_{j}}\right]\ll\sum_{x=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
m_{1},\ldots,m_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
(n_{1}+k)\cdots(n_{r}+k)=x\\
(m_{1}+k)\cdots(m_{r}+k)=x\end{subarray}}1\ll 2^{2r}\sum_{x=1}^{\infty}\frac{d_{r}(x)^{2}}{x^{2}}.

This bound on the moments is also sufficient to prove that the moments
determine the distribution. From
[
3
]
*Proposition 3.2,
for example, we have

\sum_{x=1}^{\infty}\frac{d_{r}(x)^{2}}{x^{2}}\leq C^{2r}(\log 2r)^{2r}

for some constant
C
. So for any collection of
t_{j}
we see that

\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\E\left[\prod_{j=1}^{J}F_{k,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})^{r_{j}}\overline{F_{k,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})}^{s_{j}}\right]^{-1/2r}\gg\\
\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{(2C)^{2r}(\log(2r))^{2r}}\right)^{1/2r}=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2C\log(2r)}=\infty.

Hence the moment sequences satisfy the multivariate complex
Carleman condition (see
[
37
]
*Theorem 15.11) and are determinate.
∎

We now turn to the remaining parts of Theorem
1
, i.e. study what happens when
k
varies with
q
. In our bounds for the
off-diagonal moments, the size of
k
plays no role. On the other hand, the

\mathbf{m}
which contribute to positive values of
\mathcal{S}(k,\mathbf{m})
depend on some
shifted factorizations and
k
visibly affects the moments.

When
k
grows with
q
, it will generally
be the case that
\mathcal{S}_{q}(k,\mathbf{m})
is nonzero exactly
when the second half of
\mathbf{m}
is a permutation of the first half, and
thus the influence of the multiplicativity of
\chi
goes away.
However, if
k
is growing fast enough there will be some primes
where the multiplicativity is important, so we need to remove these primes
to get convergence of the moments. Specifically, we will prove the following.

Proposition 6
.

Fix real numbers
\alpha
and
\beta
. There exist increasing functions
L(q)\to\infty
such that:

(1)

For any sequence of positive integers
k_{q}\to\infty
such that

k_{q}=q^{o(1)}
, the finite dimensional distributions of
\tilde{F}_{k_{q},q,\alpha,\beta}(t)
converge to those of
F_{\alpha,\beta}(t)
as
q\to\infty
over the primes.

(2)

For any sequence of positive integers
k_{q}\to\infty
such that

k_{q}=o(\pi(q))
, there exists a full density subset of the primes
\mathcal{P}

such that the finite dimensional distributions of
\tilde{F}_{k_{q},q,\alpha,\beta}(t)
converge to those of
F_{\alpha,\beta}(t)
as
q\in\mathcal{P}
tends to infinity.

(3)

For any irrational
\theta
, there exists a full density subset
of the primes
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}
such that the finite dimensional distributions
of
\tilde{F}_{\left\lfloor\theta q\right\rfloor,q,\alpha,\beta}(t)
converge to those
of
F_{\alpha,\beta}(t)
as
q\in\mathcal{P}_{\theta}
tends to infinity.

Proof.

We again match the moments of the random processes.
We will abbreviate
F_{\alpha,\beta}=F
and
\tilde{F}_{k_{q},q,\alpha,\beta}=\tilde{F}_{k,q}
, and again write
r=\sum r_{j}
and
s=\sum s_{j}
.
We first examine
F
. Recall that

F(t)=\frac{e(\alpha t)}{2\pi i}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(\beta(l+t))-1}{l+t}\mathbb{X}(n).

F
is clearly unchanged if we multiply by another independent variable

\mathbb{X}
uniformly distributed on the unit circle. So when
r\neq s
the
moments vanish:

\mathbb{E}\prod_{j=1}^{J}F(t_{j})^{r_{j}}\overline{F(t_{j})}^{s_{j}}=\mathbb{E}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\mathbb{X}^{r_{j}}F(t_{j})^{r_{j}}\overline{\mathbb{X}^{s_{j}}F(t_{j})^{s_{j}}}=\mathbb{E}\mathbb{X}^{r-s}\mathbb{E}\prod_{j=1}^{J}F(t_{j})^{r_{j}}\overline{F(t_{j})}^{s_{j}}=0.

When
r=s
we can compute as in the previous proof.
We find that

\E\left[\prod_{j=1}^{J}F(t_{j})^{r_{j}}\overline{F(t_{j})}^{s_{j}}\right]=e(\alpha T)\sum_{\mathbf{m}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{J}}\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{m})\prod_{j=1}^{J}a(\mathbf{m}_{j};t_{j}),

where again there is no restriction on the size of
\mathbf{m}_{j}
and
this time

\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{m})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\textrm{ if }m_{1},\ldots,m_{l}\textrm{ is a permutation of }m_{l+1},\ldots m_{2l}\\
0&\textrm{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

The absolute convergence of this series and upper bounds for its
moments follow as before, again showing that this process is determined
by its moments.
Now we go back and consider the moments of
\tilde{F}_{k,q}
.
When
r\neq s
, the upper bounds from the previous proof work just as well,
as they did not involve
k
.

When
r=s
we are concerned with whether

\mathcal{S}_{q}(k_{q},\mathbf{m})=\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{m})
for all large
q
and small
\mathbf{m}
.
Equivalently, we want to know that the only
solutions to

(k_{q}+n_{1})(k_{q}+n_{2})\cdots(k_{q}+n_{r})\equiv(k_{q}+m_{1})(k_{q}+m_{2})\cdots(k_{q}+m_{r})\pmod{q}

with
n_{l},m_{l}<L(q)
are the “trivial” ones (those where
m_{1},\ldots,m_{r}
is
a permutation of
n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}
). In general, this may not be the case. For
each
n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}
and
m_{1},\ldots,m_{r}
we consider the polynomial

h_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}(x)=(x+n_{1})(x+n_{2})\cdots(x+n_{r})-(x+m_{1})(x+m_{2})\cdots(x+m_{r}).

If
\mathbf{n}
is not a permutation of
\mathbf{m}
, this is not the zero
polynomial. The coefficients are bounded by some power of
L(q)
,
and it is exactly the content of Lemma
7
that
there exist choices of
L(q)
such that: in case
(1), there are only finitely many primes such that

for
any non-zero polynomial with small coefficients; in each of cases (2) and (3),
there is a full
density subset of the primes
\mathcal{P}
such that
h(k_{q})\neq 0\pmod{q}
for any
non-zero polynomial with small coefficients when
q\in\mathcal{P}
is large enough.
∎

5.2.
Quadratic characters

We now turn to the quadratic character case and finish the proof of Theorem
2
. Recall that

G_{q,\alpha,\beta}
is the random process given by
choosing
k
mod
q
uniformly at random and forming the
function

\frac{q^{1/2}}{\tau(\chi)}F_{k,\chi_{q},\alpha,\beta}(t)=\frac{e(-\alpha k)}{\tau(\chi)}\sum_{\alpha q<n<(\alpha+\beta)q}\chi_{q}(n)e(n(k+t)/q),

where
\chi_{q}
is the quadratic character mod
q
.
However, rather than working with this process directly, we work with
the truncated approximation
\tilde{G}_{q,\alpha,\beta}
given by choosing

k
at random and forming the function

\frac{q^{1/2}}{\tau(\chi)}\tilde{F}_{k,\chi_{q},\alpha,\beta}(t)=\frac{e(\alpha t)}{2\pi i}\sum_{\left|l\right|<L(q)}\frac{e(\alpha l)(e(\beta(l+t))-1)}{l+t}\chi_{q}(k-l).

Proposition 7
.

For
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{J}\in[0,1]
,
r_{1},\ldots,r_{J},s_{1},\ldots,s_{J}\in\mathbb{N}
,
fixed
\alpha
and
\beta
, and for fixed
k
, we have

\E\left[\prod_{j=1}^{J}\tilde{G}_{q,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})^{r_{j}}\overline{\tilde{G}_{q,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})^{s_{j}}}\right]\longrightarrow\E\left[\prod_{j=1}^{J}G_{\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})^{r_{j}}\overline{G_{\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})^{s_{j}}}\right]

as
q\rightarrow\infty
over the primes. Moreover, this moment sequence of

G_{\alpha,\beta}
is determinate, so the finite dimensional distributions of

\tilde{G}_{q,\alpha,\beta}
tend to those of
G_{\alpha,\beta}
as
q
tends to infinity
over the primes.

Proof.

We begin with the same notation and combinatorics as in the proof of
Proposition
5
, recalling Equation
(
7
):

\prod_{j=1}^{J}\frac{q^{1/2}}{\tau(\chi)}\tilde{F}_{k,\chi_{q},\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})^{r_{j}}\overline{\tilde{F}_{k,\chi_{q},\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})}^{s_{j}}\\
=e(\alpha T)\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r+s}}\sum_{\mathbf{m}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{J}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\prod_{\ell=1}^{r_{j}}\chi_{q}(k+m_{j,\ell})\prod_{\ell=r_{j}+1}^{s_{j}}\chi_{q}(k+m_{j,\ell})\prod_{j=1}^{J}a(\mathbf{m}_{j};t_{j}).

We take the sum over
k
to compute the expectation.

\E\left[\prod_{j=1}^{J}\tilde{G}_{q,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})^{r_{j}}\overline{\tilde{G}_{q,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})}^{s_{j}}\right]=\\
\frac{e(\alpha T)}{q}\sum_{\mathbf{m}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{J}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}a(\mathbf{m}_{j};t_{j})\sum_{k{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\prod_{\ell=1}^{r_{j}}\chi_{q}(k+m_{j,\ell})\prod_{\ell=r_{j}+1}^{s_{j}}\chi_{q}(k+m_{j,\ell}).

We use the Weil bound for character sums (see
[
20
]
*Corollary 11.24,
for example) in the form

\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\prod_{\ell=1}^{r_{j}}\chi_{q}(k+m_{j,\ell})\prod_{\ell=r_{j}+1}^{s_{j}}\chi_{q}(k+m_{j,\ell})=\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{J}\prod_{\ell=1}^{r_{j}+s_{j}}\mathbb{Y}(m_{j,\ell})\right)+O\left(\frac{r+s}{\sqrt{p}}\right).

Thus, we derive

\displaystyle\ \E\Bigg[\prod_{j=1}^{J}

\displaystyle\tilde{G}_{q,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})^{r_{j}}\overline{\tilde{G}_{q,\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})}^{s_{j}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=e(\alpha T)\sum_{\mathbf{m_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{m_{J}}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}a(\mathbf{m_{j}};t_{j})\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{J}\prod_{\ell=1}^{r_{j}+s_{j}}\mathbb{Y}(m_{j,\ell})\right)+E_{1},

where

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle\ll_{r,s}\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\sum_{\mathbf{m_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{m_{J}}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}|a(\mathbf{m_{j}};t_{j})|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\left(\sum_{|m|\leq L(q)}\frac{|c(m,t_{j})|}{m-t_{j}}\right)^{r_{j}+s_{j}}\hskip-15.00002pt\ll_{r,s}\frac{(\log L(q))^{r+s}}{\sqrt{q}},

since uniformly for
t\in[0,1]
we have
|c(m,t)|/(m-t)\ll 1/|m|
for
|m|\geq 2
and hence

\sum_{|m|\leq L(q)}\frac{|c(m,t)|}{m-t}\ll 1+\sum_{2\leq|m|\leq L(q)}\frac{1}{|m|}\ll\log L(q).

Formally, we get the same expression for the moments of
G_{\alpha,\beta}:

\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{J}{G}_{\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})^{r_{j}}\overline{{G}_{\alpha,\beta}(t_{j})}^{s_{j}}\right)=e(\alpha T)\sum_{\mathbf{m_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{m_{J}}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}a(\mathbf{m_{j}};t_{j})\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{J}\prod_{\ell=1}^{r_{j}+s_{j}}\mathbb{Y}(m_{j,\ell})\right),

where there is now no restriction on the size of
m_{j,l}
. So it is clear
that the moments of
\tilde{G}_{q,\alpha,\beta}
converge to those of

G_{\alpha,\beta}
provided that this sum is absolutely convergent.
The expectation

\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{J}\prod_{\ell=1}^{r_{j}+s_{j}}\mathbb{Y}(m_{j,\ell})\right)

is
0
unless the terms
m_{j,l}
coincide in pairs, in which case it is
1
.
If the
m_{j,l}
coincide in pairs, then in particular their product is a
square. Similar to the bound (
8
) in the proof of
Proposition
5
we have the upper bound

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{m_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{m_{J}}}\left|\prod_{j=1}^{J}a(\mathbf{m_{j}};t_{j})\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{J}\prod_{\ell=1}^{r_{j}+s_{j}}\mathbb{Y}(m_{j,\ell})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\ll 2^{r+s}\sum_{x=1}^{\infty}\frac{d_{r+s}(x^{2})}{x^{2}}

\displaystyle\ll 2^{2r}\sum_{x=1}^{\infty}\frac{d_{r+s}(x)^{2}}{x^{2}}

\displaystyle\ll C^{2(r+s)}(\log 2(r+s))^{2(r+s)}

for some constant
C
. This similarly provides an upper bound for the moments
which proves that the moment sequence is determinate, as before.
∎

Remark
.

We note that the argument in Lemma

such that

for each

by a reasonably explicit quantitative equidistribution statement on the roots modulo prime

7.
log
-integrability

We now turn our attention to proving Theorem
3
, as well as Corollary
2
. As we discussed in Section
2
, it is of key importance to us to understand the size of

\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\log\lvert f(t)\rvert\mathds{1}_{\lvert f(t)\rvert<\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}t

where
f:[\alpha,\beta]\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function, when
\varepsilon
is small. Note that if this quantity goes to
0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, this guarantees the existence of
\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\log\lvert f(t)\rvert\mathrm{d}t.
In this section, we are going to prove the following key result.

Proposition 8
.

Let
\Delta=\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\partial^{i}
be a linear differential operator of order
k
with constant real coefficients, and suppose that
(\Delta f)(t)\geq 1
for all
t\in[\alpha,\beta]
. Then for small enough
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\log\lvert f(t)\rvert\mathds{1}_{\lvert f(t)\rvert<\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}t\ll\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2k}},

where the implied constant is uniform in
f
, but may depend on
\Delta
,
\alpha
and
\beta
.

Proposition
8
will follow by combining the following two lemmas. The first lemma bounds the number of zeros of
f
in terms of the number of zeros of
\Delta f
, whereas the second guarantees that
f
does not spend too much time near
0
.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\Delta=\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\partial^{i}
be a linear differential operator of order
k
with constant real coefficients. Suppose that
\Delta f
has at most
m
zeros. Then
f
has
\leq m+O_{\Delta}(1)
zeros, where the implied constant is uniform in
f
.

Lemma 9
.

Let
\Delta=\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\partial^{i}
be a linear differential operator of order
k
with constant real coefficients, and suppose that
(\Delta f)(t)\geq 1
for all
t\in[\alpha,\beta]
. Then

\mu\left(\{t\in[\alpha,\beta]:\lvert f(t)\rvert<\varepsilon\}\right)\ll\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{k}},

where
\mu
denotes the standard Lebesgue measure.

Proof of Proposition
8
, assuming Lemmas
8
and
9
.

By Lemma
8
and the condition
\Delta f(t)\geq 1
for all
t\in[\alpha,\beta]
,
f
has at most
T
zeros, for some finite number
T
(independent of
f
). For each zero
x
of
f
, let
(x)
denote the interval containing
x
such that
\lvert f(x)\rvert<\varepsilon
.

Now decompose
(0,\varepsilon]
into dyadic intervals and use Proposition
9
in each interval, replacing
\lvert f\rvert
with the lower bound of the interval, namely

\int\log\lvert f\rvert\mathds{1}_{\lvert f\rvert<\varepsilon}\ll\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\int\log\lvert f\rvert\mathds{1}_{\lvert f\rvert\in\left[\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{l}},\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{l-1}}\right)}\ll\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\log\frac{2^{l-1}}{\varepsilon}\cdot(\varepsilon/2^{l})^{1/k}\ll\varepsilon^{1/k}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ll\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2k}}.

This finishes the proof. ∎

Now we prove Lemmas
8
and
9
.

Proof of Lemma
8
.

We factorize the differential operator
\Delta
over
\mathbb{R}
as follows:

\Delta=\prod_{i=1}^{k_{1}}(\partial^{2}-a_{i}\partial+b_{i})\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{k_{2}}(\partial-c_{j}),

where
a_{i}^{2}<4b_{i}
for each
1\leq i\leq k_{1}
. By induction, it suffices to show that both operators
\partial-c
and
\partial^{2}-a\partial+b
do not significantly increase the number of zeros. Indeed,
\partial-c
does not increase the number of zeros by more than one by Rolle’s theorem applied to
e^{-cx}g(x)
, for

(\partial-c)g=g^{\prime}-cg=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(e^{-cx}g(x))e^{cx}.

It remains to show the same holds for the operator
\partial^{2}-a\partial+b
. Note that

(\partial^{2}-a\partial+b)f=u_{2}\partial\left(u_{1}\partial(uf)\right),

where

u_{2}(x)=e^{ax}u(x),u_{1}(x)=e^{-ax}u^{-2}(x),\text{ and }u(x)=\frac{e^{\frac{a}{2}x}}{\cos\left(\frac{\sqrt{4b-a^{2}}}{2}x\right)}.

The claim follows by applying Rolle’s theorem twice in each subinterval of
[\alpha,\beta]
(depending on
a,b
) where
u
has constant sign. In each such subinterval, the number of zeros does not increase by more than two. It is clear that the number of such subintervals only depends on
\Delta
and not on
f
, hence the conclusion follows. ∎

Proof of Lemma
9
.

Let us start with a standard technical claim.
There exists a smooth function
\phi:[0,1]\to[0,1]
, with the following properties:

(i)

\phi(t)=1
for
t\in[\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3}]
;

(ii)

\phi^{(k)}(0)=\phi^{(k)}(1)=0
for all
k=0,1,2,\ldots
;

(iii)

\phi^{(k)}
is bounded for every
k=0,1,2,\ldots
.

Indeed, define

(9)

\beta(t)=\begin{cases}e^{-1/t},&t>0,\\[5.69054pt]
0,&t\leq 0,\end{cases}\qquad\rho(t)=\frac{\beta(t)}{\beta(t)+\beta(1-t)},

and set

\phi(t)=\rho\left(3t\right)\rho\left(3(1-t)\right),\qquad t\in[0,1].

Note that
\phi
is indeed a smooth function, supported on
[0,1]
which is equal to
1
for
x\in[\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3}]
. Moreover, all derivatives of
\phi
vanish at
0
and
1
and are bounded.

By Lemma
8
,
f
has a finite number of zeros (uniformly in
f
). For each zero
x
of
f
, let
(x)=(x^{-},x^{+})
be the interval containing
x
such that
\lvert f(t)\rvert<\varepsilon
for each
t\in(x)
. Let
\varphi
be a smooth function, approximating
\mathds{1}_{(x)}
, with the property that
\varphi(x^{-})=\varphi(x^{+})=\varphi^{\prime}(x^{-})=\varphi^{\prime}(x^{+})=\cdots=\varphi^{(k)}(x^{-})=\varphi^{(k)}(x^{+})=0
(take
\varphi(x)=\phi((x-x^{-})/(x^{+}-x^{-}))
). Note that

\int_{x^{-}}^{x^{+}}f^{(l)}\varphi=(-1)^{l}\int_{x^{-}}^{x^{+}}f\varphi^{(l)}
by integration by parts and vanishing boundary terms and consequently

\int_{x^{-}}^{x^{+}}\varphi(t)(\Delta f)(t)\mathrm{d}t=\int_{x^{-}}^{x^{+}}f(t)(\widetilde{\Delta}\varphi)(t)\mathrm{d}t,

where
\widetilde{\Delta}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}a_{i}\partial^{i}
. By definition of
\varphi
and the condition that
\Delta f\geq 1
, we have

\int_{(x)}\varphi(t)(\Delta f)(t)\mathrm{d}t\gg(x^{+}-x^{-}),

whereas

\int_{(x)}f(t)(\widetilde{\Delta}\varphi)(t)\mathrm{d}t\ll\frac{\varepsilon}{(x^{+}-x^{-})^{k-1}},

since
\lvert f\rvert<\varepsilon
on
(x)
, and
\varphi^{(i)}\ll(x^{+}-x^{-})^{i}
by property (iii) and the chain rule. It follows that
x^{+}-x^{-}\ll\varepsilon^{1/k}
. Given that there are finitely many zeros of
f
(uniformly in
f
), this finishes the proof of the lemma.∎

8.1.
Proof of Theorem
3

The main difficulty in proving Theorem
3
is that the functional on
\mathscr{C}[0,1]
defined by
\ell(f)=\int_{0}^{1}\log(|f(t)|)\text{d}t
is not continuous, so our previous distributional result does not directly apply.
We instead consider

\ell(f)=\ell_{\varepsilon}(f)+\int_{0}^{1}\log(|f(t)|)(1-w_{\varepsilon}(\lvert f(t)\rvert))\mathrm{d}t,

where

\ell_{\varepsilon}(f)=\int_{0}^{1}\log(|f(t)|)w_{\varepsilon}(\lvert f(t)\rvert)\text{d}t

with
w_{\varepsilon}(t)=\rho\left(\frac{t}{\varepsilon}-1\right)
(recall the function
\rho
in (
9
)) a smoothed minorant of
\mathds{1}_{t\geq\varepsilon}
, is a continuous functional on
\mathscr{C}[0,1]
. Roughly speaking, Theorem
3
will follow from applying Theorem
2
to
\ell_{\varepsilon}(f)
, and Proposition
8
to
\ell(f)-\ell_{\varepsilon}(f)
using the fact that
1-w_{\varepsilon}(t)
majorizes
\mathds{1}_{t<\varepsilon}
, where
f=F_{k,\chi_{q},\alpha,\beta}
with
\alpha=.2
and
\beta=1.1
.

We start with the following lemma to handle
\ell(f)-\ell_{\varepsilon}(f)
.

Lemma 10
.

For
\alpha=.2
and
\beta=1.1
, for all large enough primes
q
and all

k\in\{0,1,\ldots,q-1\}
we have

\int_{0}^{1}\log\left|F_{k,\chi_{q},\alpha,\beta}(t)\right|\mathds{1}_{\left|F_{k,\chi_{q},\alpha,\beta}(t)\right|<\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}t\ll\varepsilon^{1/6}

as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, where
\chi_{q}
is the quadratic character mod
q
.

Proof.

Recall, that for a Dirichlet character
\chi
mod
q
and for
\alpha,\beta

such that
\alpha q
and
(\alpha+\beta)q
are not integers,

F_{k,\chi,\alpha,\beta}(t)=e(\alpha t)\frac{\tau(\chi)}{2\pi iq^{1/2}}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(\alpha l)(e(\beta(l+t))-1)}{l+t}\overline{\chi}(k-l).

The only way we know how to show that this integral is well-behaved is via some
explicit numerical calculation that considers all possible values of
\chi_{q}(k-l)
for small
l
. The (ad-hoc) arguments used by
[
23
]
for Fekete polynomials take advantage of two features
that we do not have at our disposal: they were able to deal with real-valued
functions using the intermediate value theorem and most importanly, the derivatives of their series were absolutely convergent,
making the numerical computation (separation from zero value) feasible.

To get around these difficulties, we instead consider a linear combination
of derivatives of our function. Write

A(t)=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(\alpha l)(e(\beta(l+t))-1)}{l+t}\overline{\chi}(k-l).

Then

4\pi^{2}\beta^{2}A^{\prime}(t)+A^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\overline{\chi}(k-l)}{(l+t)^{4}}a(l,t),

where

a(l,t)=\bigg[12\pi\beta(l+t)e(\beta t)e((\alpha+\beta)l)\left(\pi\beta(l+t)+i\right)\\
-e(\alpha l)(e(\beta(l+t)-1)\left(6+4\pi^{2}\beta^{2}(l+t)^{2}\right)\bigg].

(Here
a(l,t)/(l+t)^{4}
becomes
-4\pi^{4}\beta^{4}
when
l=t=0

and
-4\pi^{4}\beta^{4}e(-\alpha)
when
l=-1
and
t=1
.)
This is now something that is given by an absolutely convergent series.
In particular, if we limit the sum to
l\in[-M+1,M]
, say, then the
truncation error will be at most

\left(40\pi^{2}\beta^{2}\right)\left(\zeta(2)-\sum_{m<M}\frac{1}{m^{2}}\right)+24\pi\beta\left(\zeta(3)-\sum_{m<M}\frac{1}{m^{3}}\right)+24\left(\zeta(4)-\sum_{m<M}\frac{1}{m^{4}}\right).

When
k\not\in\{0,q-1\}
, we can take
M=2
so that

\left|4\pi^{2}\beta^{2}A^{\prime}(t)+A^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)-\sum_{l=-2}^{1}\frac{\overline{\chi}(k-l)}{(l+t)^{4}}a(l,t)\right|<326.9.

There are 32 different possible values of the sequence

\chi(k-1),\chi(k),\chi(k+1),\chi(k+2)
(the first or the last term might
be zero, but not the middle two), and we check that for each possible choice
we can cover the interval
[0,1]
into 4 separate intervals such that
on each interval, either
\left|\mathrm{Re}(4\pi^{2}\beta^{2}A^{\prime}(t)+A^{\prime\prime\prime}(t))\right|>1

or
\left|\mathrm{Im}(4\pi^{2}\beta^{2}A^{\prime}(t)+A^{\prime\prime\prime}(t))\right|>1
across the entire
interval.

The terms with
l=-1
and
l=0
are the largest summands, so when one
of them vanishes we have to work a bit harder. Now in order to make the
computation tractable we use the symmetry and multiplicativity of
\chi_{q}
.
In the worst case, when
k=0
and
\chi
is an even character, we take
M=28
, so that the error bound is 17.5. Using multiplicativity, we only need
to check 512 different possibilities for the sequence
\chi(-28),\chi(-27),\ldots,\chi(27)
, rather than the
2^{56}
possibilities we would have to
check naively.

In any case, we find that we can always cover
[0,1]
by 5 intervals such that
on each interval, either
\left|\mathrm{Re}(4\pi^{2}\beta^{2}A^{\prime}(t)+A^{\prime\prime\prime}(t))\right|>1
or

\left|\mathrm{Im}(4\pi^{2}\beta^{2}A^{\prime}(t)+A^{\prime\prime\prime}(t))\right|>1
across the entire interval.

We are now in a position to appeal to Proposition
8
. We have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\log\left|F_{k,\chi_{q},\alpha,\beta}(t)\right|\mathds{1}_{\left|F_{k,\chi_{q},\alpha,\beta}(t)\right|<\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\log\left|A(t)\right|\mathds{1}_{\left|A(t)\right|<2\pi\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\int_{I_{j}}\log\left|A(t)\right|\mathds{1}_{\left|A(t)\right|<2\pi\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}t

for any disjoint collection of intervals
I_{j}
that cover
[0,1]
, and when

\varepsilon
is small enough

\sum_{j}\left|\int_{I_{j}}\log\left|A(t)\right|\mathds{1}_{\left|A(t)\right|<2\pi\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}t\right|\leq\\
\sum_{j}\min\left(\left|\int_{I_{j}}\log\left|\mathrm{Re}(A(t))\right|\mathds{1}_{\left|\mathrm{Re}(A(t))\right|<2\pi\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}t\right|,\left|\int_{I_{j}}\log\left|\mathrm{Im}(A(t))\right|\mathds{1}_{\left|\mathrm{Im}(A(t))\right|<2\pi\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}t\right|\right).

By the computation described above, we can split the interval
[0,1]
into a
finite union of disjoint intervals (independent of
k
and
\chi
) such
that on each interval, either the real part or the imaginary part of
A(t)

satisfies the given linear differential inequality, and hence

\min\left(\left|\int_{I_{j}}\log\left|\mathrm{Re}(A(t))\right|\mathds{1}_{\left|\mathrm{Re}(A(t))\right|<2\pi\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}t\right|,\left|\int_{I_{j}}\log\left|\mathrm{Im}(A(t))\right|\mathds{1}_{\left|\mathrm{Im}(A(t))\right|<2\pi\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}t\right|\right)\ll\varepsilon^{1/6}

as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
by Proposition
8
, and the same bound
holds for the sum over all of the intervals.
∎

Proof of Corollary

We follow exactly the same steps as in the previous subsection. Let

Lemma 11

For
\alpha=.2
and

t
, therefore we have

\lim_{q\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{1}\log|{H}^{\pm}_{q}(t)|\mathds{1}_{\lvert{H}^{\pm}_{q}(t)\rvert\geq\varepsilon}\text{d}t=\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{1}\log|{G_{.2,1.1}}(t)|\mathds{1}_{|{G_{.2,1.1}}(t)|\geq\varepsilon}\text{d}t

by Theorem
2
. We conclude in exactly the same way as in the previous subsection, i.e. we have

(10)

\lim_{q\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{1}\log\left|\frac{1}{1.1^{1/2}}{H}^{\pm}_{q}(t)\right|\text{d}t=\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{1}\log\left|\frac{1}{1.1^{1/2}}{G_{.2,1.1}}(t)\right|\text{d}t.

To finish the proof of Corollary
2
, note that the left hand-side of (
10
) is the limit of the logarithm of normalized Mahler measures of the Littlewood polynomials
\sum_{n=1}^{\lfloor 1.1q\rfloor}\chi(n+\lfloor.2q\rfloor)x^{n}\pm x^{q-\lfloor.2q\rfloor}
(upon noticing the Mahler measure is invariant under multiplication by powers of
x
).

9.
Proof of Theorem
4

In this section, we prove a conjecture of Günther and Schmidt
[
16
]
, on the minimum
L_{2k}
norm of the Turyn polynomial

F_{p,a}(t)=\sum_{n\leq p}\left(\frac{n+a}{p}\right)e(nt).

As discussed in the introduction, in
[
16
]
it was shown that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a function
\phi_{k}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\lim_{p\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\|F_{a,p}(t)\|_{2k}=\phi_{k}(\alpha)

when
a/p\to\alpha
. We will show that
\phi_{k}(\alpha)
achieves its minimum
at
\alpha=1/4
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

We now begin the proof. By Theorem
2
, we have

\phi_{k}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2k}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left\lvert(e(t)-1)\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(m\alpha)}{m+t}\mathbb{Y}(m)\right\rvert^{2k}\text{d}t

where the
\mathbb{Y}(m)
are
\pm 1
-valued uniform random variables.
Expanding the sum, the integral is equal to

\int_{0}^{1}\lvert e(t)-1\rvert^{2k}\sum_{m_{i},n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}}\Bigg(\frac{e(\alpha(m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}-n_{1}-\ldots-n_{k}))}{(m_{1}+t)\cdots(m_{k}+t)(n_{1}+t)\cdots(n_{k}+t)}\times\\
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{Y}(m_{1})\cdots\mathbb{Y}(m_{k})\mathbb{Y}(n_{1})\cdots\mathbb{Y}(n_{k})\big]\Bigg)\text{d}t.

Note that
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{Y}(m_{1})\cdots\mathbb{Y}(m_{k})\mathbb{Y}(n_{1})\cdots\mathbb{Y}(n_{k})\big]
is non-zero if
and only if the
m_{i},n_{i}
are equal in pairs, in which case the expectation is
equal to
1
. If
m_{i}=n_{j}
for some
i,j
, then
m_{i}-n_{j}
will cancel inside
the argument of the exponential. However, if the expectation is non-zero and

m_{i}=m_{j}=m
for some
i,j
, then necessarily we must also have
n_{s}=n_{t}=n

for some
s,t
, meaning that that the terms that do not cancel inside the
argument of the exponential, are all of the form
2(m-n)
. As such, we find
that

\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left\lvert(e(t)-1)\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(m\alpha)}{m+t}\mathbb{Y}(m)\right\rvert^{2k}\text{d}t=\\
\int_{0}^{1}\lvert e(t)-1\rvert^{2k}\sum_{r=0}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{2r+1},\ldots,m_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
n_{2r+1},\ldots,n_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{Y}(m_{2r+1})\cdots\mathbb{Y}(m_{k})\mathbb{Y}(n_{2r+1})\cdots\mathbb{Y}(n_{k})\big]}{(m_{2r+1}+t)\cdots(m_{k}+t)(n_{2r+1}+t)\cdots(n_{k}+t)}M_{2r,t}(\alpha)\text{d}t,

where

M_{2r,t}(\alpha)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\frac{e(2\alpha(m_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}-n_{1}-\cdots-n_{r}))}{(m_{1}+t)^{2}\cdots(m_{r}+t)^{2}(n_{1}+t)^{2}\cdots(n_{r}+t)^{2}}=\left\lvert\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(2m\alpha)}{(m+t)^{2}}\right\rvert^{2r}.

The product
(m_{2r+1}+t)\cdots(m_{k}+t)(n_{2r+1}+t)\cdots(n_{k}+t)

is always a square when the expectation does not vanish, so
each nonzero summand in the sum is positive. We will show that

M_{2r,t}(\alpha)
is minimized at
\alpha=1/4
for every
t\in(0,1)
, from which
the result will follow. To evaluate
M_{2r,t}
, we use the following
integral representation.

Lemma 12
.

For
t\in(0,1)
we have

(11)

\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(m\theta)}{(m+t)^{2}}=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{x^{t-1}}{1-e(\theta)x}+\frac{e(-\theta)x^{-t}}{1-e(-\theta)x}\right)\log\frac{1}{x}\mathrm{d}x.

Proof.

This follows easily from an integral representation of the Lerch transcendent.
From
[
10
]
*Chapter 1.11, Equation (3) (see also
[
34
]
*Formula 25.14.5)
we have

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{m}}{(m+t)^{s}}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{s-1}e^{-tx}}{1-ze^{-x}}\mathrm{d}x.

This holds, for example, with
\mathrm{Re}(s)>1
and
\mathrm{Re}(t)>0
when
\left|z\right|=1
. Thus

\displaystyle\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(m\theta)}{(m+t)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{e(m\theta)}{(m+t)^{2}}+e(-\theta)\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{e(-m\theta)}{(m+1-t)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{ue^{-tu}}{1-e(\theta)e^{-u}}+e(-\theta)\frac{ue^{-(1-t)u}}{1-e(-\theta)e^{-u}}\right)\mathrm{d}u.

We now make the change of variables
u=\log\frac{1}{x}
.
∎

Now writing
\theta=2\alpha
and continuing from Equation
(
11
), we write the square of the absolute value as
the sum of the squares of the real and imaginary parts to get

\left\lvert\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(m\theta)}{(m+t)^{2}}\right\rvert^{2}=\cos(2\pi\theta)^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(x^{-t}-x^{t})+(x^{t-1}-x^{-t+1})}{1-2x\cos(2\pi\theta)+x^{2}}\log\frac{1}{x}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}\\
+\sin(2\pi\theta)^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x^{-t}-x^{t}}{1-2x\cos(2\pi\theta)+x^{2}}\log\frac{1}{x}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}.

We now write the first integral as

\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(x^{-t}-x^{t})+(x^{t-1}-x^{-t+1})}{1-2x\cos(2\pi\theta)+x^{2}}\log\frac{1}{x}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}\\
=\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x^{-t}-x^{t}}{1-2x\cos(2\pi\theta)+x^{2}}\log\frac{1}{x}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x^{t-1}-x^{-t+1}}{1-2x\cos(2\pi\theta)+x^{2}}\log\frac{1}{x}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}

and upon expanding this square and simplifying we arrive at

\left\lvert\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e(m\theta)}{(m+t)^{2}}\right\rvert^{2}=\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x^{-t}-x^{t}}{1-2x\cos(2\pi\theta)+x^{2}}\log\frac{1}{x}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}\\
+2\cos(2\pi\theta)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x^{-t}-x^{t}}{1-2x\cos(2\pi\theta)+x^{2}}\log\frac{1}{x}\mathrm{d}x\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x^{t-1}-x^{-t+1}}{1-2x\cos(2\pi\theta)+x^{2}}\log\frac{1}{x}\mathrm{d}x\\
+\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x^{t-1}-x^{-t+1}}{1-2x\cos(2\pi\theta)+x^{2}}\log\frac{1}{x}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}.

Note that all the integrands are non-negative, as

1-2x\cos(2\pi\theta)+x^{2}\geq(1-x)^{2}.
For every
t
,
the first and third
terms are minimized at
\theta=\frac{1}{2}
, as the denominator
is maximized there. To minimize the middle term, we can assume that

\cos(2\pi\theta)<0
. Now, rewrite it as

-2\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x^{-t}-x^{t}}{\frac{1+x^{2}}{\sqrt{-\cos(2\pi\theta)}}-2x\sqrt{-\cos(2\pi\theta)}}\log\frac{1}{x}\mathrm{d}x\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x^{t-1}-x^{-t+1}}{\frac{1+x^{2}}{\sqrt{-\cos(2\pi\theta)}}-2x\sqrt{-\cos(2\pi\theta)}}\log\frac{1}{x}\mathrm{d}x.

We have to minimize the denominator of the integrals in
\theta
. The
derivative of the expression is equal to

\frac{\pi\tan(2\pi\theta)(x^{2}-2x\cos(2\pi\theta)+1)}{\sqrt{-\cos(2\pi\theta)}},

from which it is clear that
\theta=\frac{1}{2}
is the
minimizer (recall that
\cos(2\pi\theta)<0
, hence clearly
x^{2}-2x\cos(2\pi\theta)+1>0
). We
conclude that
M_{2r,t}(\alpha)
is minimized at
\alpha=\frac{1}{4}
for
every value of
t
, finishing the proof of Theorem
4
.
