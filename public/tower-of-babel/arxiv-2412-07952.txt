On Random Simplex Picking Beyond the Blashke Problem
Dominik Beck

Abstract
New selected values of odd random simplex volumetric moments (moments of the volume of a random simplex picked from a given body) are derived in an exact form in various bodies in dimensions three, four, five, and six. In three dimensions, the well-known Efron’s formula was used by Buchta & Reitzner and Zinani to deduce the mean volume of a random tetrahedron in a tetrahedron and a cube. However, for higher moments and/or in higher dimensions, the method fails. As it turned out, the same problem is also solvable using the Blashke-Petkantschin formula in Cartesian parametrisation in the form of the Canonical Section Integral (Base-height splitting). In our presentation, we show how to derive the older results mentioned above using our base-height splitting method and also touch on the essential steps of how the method translates to higher dimensions and for higher moments.

1.2.2
Odd moments

Extending the work of Crofton, Hostinský
[
8
, p. 22–26]
considered and solved many problems concerning geometric probability. One of them is the ball tetrahedron picking, which was the first metric moment obtained in
d=3
, it reads

v_{3}^{(1)}(\mathbb{B}_{3})=\frac{9}{715}.

(3)

The result was generalised to higher dimensions by Kingman
[
10
]
. For the mean volume of a
d
-simplex picked from a
d
-ball, Kingman got

v_{d}^{(1)}(\mathbb{B}_{d})=\frac{2^{d}\Gamma^{2}\left(\frac{(d+1)^{2}}{2}\right)\Gamma^{d+1}(d+1)}{(d+1)^{d-1}\,\Gamma\left((d+1)^{2}\right)\Gamma^{2(d+1)}\left(\frac{d+1}{2}\right)}.

The result above can be obtained as a special case of even more general formula by Miles
[
15
, p. 363, Eq. (29)]
, the consequence of which is that the metric moments
v_{n}^{(k)}(\mathbb{B}_{d})
are known for any
n
,
k
and
d
. Particularly,

v_{d}^{(k)}(\mathbb{B}_{d})=\left(\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}+1\right)}{\pi^{d/2}d!}\right)^{k}\left(\frac{d}{d+k}\right)^{d+1}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{(d+1)(d+k)}{2}+1\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d(d+k+1)}{2}+1\right)}\left(\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d+k}{2}\right)}\right)^{\!\!d}\,\,\prod_{l=1}^{d-1}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{k+l}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{l}{2}\right)}.

(4)

Less is known about volumetric moments in polytopes. In two dimensions, one of the classical problems of random geometry is to find the mean convex hull area and its moments, that is to express
v_{n}^{(k)}(K_{2})
for various
K_{2}
and with
n\geq 2
. A lot of results were obtained in this direction. For example, Buchta and Reitzner
[
4
]
found a formula expressing
v_{n}^{(1)}(P_{2})
for any convex polygon
P_{2}
.

Apart from a ball, not many exact results were known in three dimensions. Here, we are interested in expressing
v_{n}^{(k)}(K_{3})
with
n\geq 3
, which represents the
k
-th moment of a random volume of a convex hull of
(n+1)
points. When
n=3
, the convex hull is almost surely a tetrahedron, so
v_{3}^{(1)}(K_{3})
represents the
mean tetrahedron volume
and similarly, we have
v_{3}^{(k)}(K_{3})
for higher moments. The famous difficult problem proposed by Klee
[
11
]
and popularised by Blaschke concerns finding
v_{3}^{(1)}(T_{3})
, the mean volume of a tetrahedron formed by four uniformly selected random points from the interior of a fixed unit volume tetrahedron. The first attempt was made by Reed. In
[
18
]
, he uses the Crofton reduction technique
[
19
]
which enables him to express the exact value of
v_{3}^{(1)}(T_{3})=V_{3333}
as a linear combination of mean volumes of four irreducible configurations
(3320)
,
(2222)
,
(3311)
,
(3221)
, in which the points forming the random tetrahedron are chosen from sets of lower dimensions.

•

(3320):
two points inside, one on a face and the fourth being a vertex,

•

(2222):
points on faces only, one on each face,

•

(3311):
two points inside and two on the opposite edges,

•

(3221):
one point inside, two points on adjacent faces and the fourth being a vertex.

Reed was, however, only able to express
V_{3320}=3/64
and
V_{2222}=1/27
in a closed form. The last two configurations in the list were only solved by Mannion
[
13
]
using a clever handling of improper integrals, giving

v_{3}^{(1)}(T_{3})=\frac{13}{720}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{15015}\approx 0.017398.

However, Buchta and Reitzner
[
2
]
obtained this value earlier using Efron’s section formula
[
6
]
, c.f.
[
14
, p. 372]
, which relates
v_{n}^{(1)}(K_{3})
for
n\geq 3
and
K_{3}
with a triple integral over cutting planes. By using Euler’s polyhedral formula, Efron obtained

v_{n}^{(1)}(K_{3})=\frac{n}{n+2}-\frac{n(n+1)}{12}\mathbb{E}\left[\Gamma_{\!3}(\mathbb{X}^{\prime})^{n-1}+(1-\Gamma_{\!3}(\mathbb{X}^{\prime}))^{n-1}\right],

(5)

where
\Gamma_{\!3}(\mathbb{X}^{\prime})=\operatorname{vol}_{3}K_{3}^{+}/\operatorname{vol}_{3}K_{3}
is the volume fraction of one of the two parts
K_{3}^{+}\sqcup K_{3}^{-}
into which
K_{3}
is divided by a plane
\bm{\sigma}=\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{X}^{\prime})
going through the collection
\mathbb{X}^{\prime}=(\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\prime},\mathbf{X}_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{X}_{3}^{\prime})
of random points
\mathbf{X}_{j}^{\prime}
,
j\in\{1,2,3\}
drawn from
K_{3}
uniformly and independently. We can transform the expected value above after some nontrivial algebraic manipulations into a set of calculable double integrals. The same technique enabled Zinani
[
21
]
to deduce

v_{3}^{(1)}(C_{3})=\frac{3977}{21600}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2160}\approx 0.01384277.

The derivation of
v_{3}^{(1)}(C_{3})
itself is straightforward, but at the same time unworldly difficult, containing millions of intermediate integrals necessary to solve (to do so, Zinani used the package Mathematica 4.0). No other values of odd volumetric moments in three dimensions were known.

In higher dimensions, there were no results for polytopes. Efron’s formula completely breaks down because of the existence of cyclic polytopes. At least, for any
K_{d}
, we have the following relation shown by Buchta in
[
3
, p. 96]
by a simple projection argument

v_{d+1}^{(1)}(K_{d})=\frac{d+2}{2}\,v_{d}^{(1)}(K_{d})

(6)

However, Efron’s formula is not the only approach to volumetric moments. The original method by Reed and Mannion to obtain
v_{3}^{(1)}(T_{3})
was the Crofton’s reduction technique. Another derivation of
v_{3}^{(1)}(T_{3})
and
v_{3}^{(1)}(C_{3})
which appeared recently and was not using Efron’s formula (but equally difficult) was due to Philip
[
16
,
17
]
. As we shall see later in this paper, there is yet another way. Had it not been for Philip’s work, the author of this paper would not have been convinced that there might still be another method for obtaining volumetric moments.

1.3
New results

1.3.1
Main theorem (Canonical section integral)

The objective of this work is to extend the number of polytopes for which the volumetric moments are expressed exactly and to present the method to find it effectively. For this purpose, we developed a new technique which can handle these problems. Our method relied crucially on the following theorem:

Theorem 1
.

Let
K_{d}
be a
d
-dimensional convex body,
\mathbbm{x}^{\prime}=(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{d})
a collection of
d
points in
K_{d}
and
\bm{\sigma}=\mathcal{A}(\mathbbm{x}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{A}(d,d-1)
be a hyperplane parametrised by
\bm{\eta}=(\eta_{1}\ldots,\eta_{d})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
\mathbf{x}\in\bm{\sigma}\Leftrightarrow\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=1
, then

v_{d}^{(k)}(K_{d})=\frac{(d-1)!}{d^{k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus K_{d}^{\circ}}v_{d-1}^{(k+1)}(\bm{\sigma}\cap K_{d})\,\zeta_{d}^{d+k+1}(\bm{\sigma})\iota^{(k)}_{d}(\bm{\sigma})\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta})

(7)

for any real
k>-1
, where

\zeta_{d}(\bm{\sigma})=\frac{\operatorname{vol}_{d-1}(\bm{\sigma}\cap K_{d})}{\|\bm{\eta}\|\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d}},\qquad\iota^{(k)}_{d}(\bm{\sigma})=\int_{K_{d}}|\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}-1|^{k}\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}).

(8)

Unlike the approach which relies on Efron’s formula, our own formula enables us to link odd moments with even moments on sections. However, as mentioned earlier, even moments are simple to compute generally. The theorem itself is proven in Section
2.3.2
. Let us look what results can be deduced using this formula…

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Symmetries and genealogic decomposition

2.1.1
Configurations

Let
\mathcal{G}(P_{d})
be the group of all isometries of
P_{d}
(the
symmetric group
of
P_{d}
). That is,
\mathcal{G}(P_{d})
is isomorphic to the group of permutations of vertices of
P_{d}
such that it leaves
P_{d}
unchanged upto rigid transformations (including reflections). Note that in
d=3
,
\mathcal{G}(P_{d})
only consists of
rotations, reflections and improper rotations
. In
Schoenflies notation
, they are denoted
C_{n},\sigma,S_{n}
, respectively (together with inversion
I
and identity
E
). Let us select some subset
S
of vertices
V
of
P_{d}
. We can imagine selecting vertices by colouring them (black/white) to create solid
P_{d}(S)
. We denote
\mathcal{P}_{d}
as the set of all those polytopes with pre-selected (coloured) vertices. We say two
P_{d}(S_{1}),P_{d}(S_{2})\in\mathcal{P}_{d}
are equivalent if there is
g\in\mathcal{G}(P_{d})
such that
gP_{d}(S_{2})=P_{d}(S_{1})
. Moreover we say they are section equivalent if
gP_{d}(S_{2})=P_{d}(S_{1})
or
gP_{d}(S_{2})=P_{d}(V\setminus S_{1})
. We see that the first condition is more strict since in the latter case, we also identify two coloured polytopes with switched colours. We call the representants of all equivalent classes of coloured polytopes as
configurations
. For example, all isometries of a regular octahedron are

\mathcal{G}(O_{3})=\{E,6C_{2},8C_{3},6C_{4},3C_{4}^{2},I,3\sigma_{h},6\sigma_{d},6S_{4},8S_{6}\}

(11)

2.1.2
Weights and orders

The size of an orbit of some configuration
C=P_{d}(S)
with selected representant vertices
S
is by definition
o_{C}=|\mathcal{G}(P_{d})C|
, where
\mathcal{G}(P_{d})C=\{gC\,|\,g\in\mathcal{G}(P_{d})\}
is the
orbit
of
C
. By
orbit-stabilizer lemma
,
o_{C}=|\mathcal{G}(P_{d})|/|\mathcal{G}_{C}(P_{d})|
, where
\mathcal{G}_{C}(P_{d})=\{g\in\mathcal{G}(P_{d})\,|\,gC=C\}
is the
stabilizer
subgroup. The total number of equivalent configurations is given by Burnside’s lemma. We can find those configurations via the help of a computer, see GECRA (Code
9
) in the appendix. The procedure is as follows: First, we represent
\mathcal{G}(P_{d})
as a subgroup of the symmetry group
\mathcal{S}_{|V|}
with
|V|
whose elements (permutations) which act of vertices of
P_{d}
we represent as permutation matrices. This representation is of course an isomorphism. Then, we can represent a selection (colouring) of vertices
S
as a vector
\mathbf{s}
of length
|V|
of ones and zeros. Let us denote the set of all such vectors as
\mathbb{S}
. There are
2^{|V|}
such vectors. The set of all configurations is then simply the classes

\bigcup_{\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{S}}|\mathcal{S}_{|V|}\mathbf{s}|=\bigcup_{\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{S}}\{g\mathbf{s}\,|\,g\in\mathcal{S}_{|V|}\}.

(12)

So far, we have not employed the section equivalence
gC\sim C^{\prime}
, where we write
C^{\prime}=P_{d}(V\setminus S)
. Therefore, for a given configuration
C
with
S
selected (coloured) vertices out of total
n
vertices of
P_{d}
, we define the section weight
w_{C}
as the size of the orbit of
C
with respect to the section equivalence, that is, by symmetry

w_{C}=\begin{cases}o_{C},&|S|<n/2\\
o_{C}/2,&|S|=n/2.\end{cases}

(13)

Since
\bm{\sigma}\cap P_{d}
is also a polytope (more precisely, a
(d-1)
-polytope), we define the
order

n_{C}
of a configuration
C
as the number of vertices of
\bm{\sigma}\cap P_{d}
. We claim this number is well defined for a given configuration.

2.1.5
Decomposition of functionals

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex
d
-polytope. Consider an affinely invariant functional

F(P)=\frac{1}{(\operatorname{vol}_{d}P)^{d}}\int_{P^{d}}f(\mathbbm{x})\lambda_{d}^{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{x}).

(14)

By symmetry, we can decompose this functional as follows

F(P)=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(P)}w_{C}F(P)_{C},

(15)

where

F(K)_{C}=\frac{1}{(\operatorname{vol}_{d}P)^{d}}\int_{P^{d}}\mathbbm{1}\{\mathcal{A}(\mathbbm{x})/C\}f(\mathbbm{x})\lambda_{d}^{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{x}).

(16)

Note that the property
\mathcal{A}(\mathbbm{x})/C
is also affinely invariant, since any affine transformation does not change the set of vertices
S
separated by
\bm{\sigma}
. As a consequence, also
F_{C}(K)
stays invariant under affine trasformations of
K
. By defining
P_{C}=\{\mathbf{x}\in P\,|\,\mathcal{A}(\mathbbm{x})/C\}
, we may also write

F(K)_{C}=\frac{1}{(\operatorname{vol}_{d}P)^{d}}\int_{P_{C}^{d}}f(\mathbbm{x})\lambda_{d}^{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{x}).

(17)

2.2
Integral calculus on real affine subspaces

First, we shall discuss the common techniques of multidimensional integration. The notation used in this section is borrowed from the textbook
Lectures on convex geometry
by Hug and Weil
[
9
]
. Once again, let us recall some basic facts and definitions.

Definition 1

(
\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\omega_{d}
)
.

Let
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
be a unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the usual surface area measure
\sigma_{d}(\cdot)
. That is, for the surface area of
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, we get

\omega_{d}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\sigma_{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u})=\sigma_{d}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})=\frac{2\pi^{\tfrac{d}{2}}}{\Gamma(\tfrac{d}{2})}.

(18)

Also note that we can decompose
\mathbf{x}=r\mathbf{u}
, where
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and
r\in(0,\infty)
, the usual Lebesgue measure
\lambda_{d}
splits into radial and angular part as
\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})=r^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r\sigma_{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u})
.

Definition 2

(
\mathbb{B}_{d},\kappa_{d}
)
.

We write
\mathbb{B}_{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
for the unit ball (with unit radius) and
\kappa_{d}
for its volume. Splitting the Lebesgue measure into radial and angular part,

\kappa_{d}=\operatorname{vol}_{d}\mathbb{B}_{d}=\int_{\mathbb{B}_{d}}\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})=\omega_{d}\int_{0}^{1}r^{d-1}\,\,\mathrm{d}r=\omega_{d}/d.

(19)

Definition 3
.

We denote
\mathbb{G}(d,p)
as the set of all linear
p
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, this set is often called the (linear) Grassmannian. More generally, we denote
\mathbb{A}(d,p)
as the set of all
p
-dimensional affine subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
p
-planes), this set is called the affine Grasmannian.

Remark 1
.

Both spaces
\mathbb{G}(d,p)
and
\mathbb{A}(d,p)
have a finite-dimensional basis. More concretely, we have
\dim\mathbb{G}(d,p)=(d-p)p
and
\dim\mathbb{A}(d,p)=(d-p)(p+1)
.

Definition 4
.

Let
K_{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We define
\mathbb{G}_{K_{d}}(d,p)=\{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathbb{G}(d,p)\,|\,\bm{\gamma}\cap K_{d}\neq\emptyset\}
and analogously,
\mathbb{A}_{K_{d}}(d,p)=\{\bm{\sigma}\in\mathbb{A}(d,p)\,|\,\bm{\sigma}\cap K_{d}\neq\emptyset\}
.

Definition 5
.

Let
\nu_{p}
be the probability Haar measure on
\mathbb{G}(d,p)
. That is,
\nu_{p}
is invariant under action of the group of proper rigid sphere transformations
\mathcal{SO}(n)
and
\nu_{p}(\mathbb{G}(d,p))=1
.

Definition 6
.

We define the standard Haar measure
\mu_{p}
on
\mathbb{A}(d,p)
by

\mu_{p}(\cdot)=\int_{\mathbb{G}(d,p)}\int_{\bm{\gamma}_{\!\perp}}\mathbbm{1}\{\bm{\gamma}+\mathbf{y}\in\cdot\}\lambda_{d-p}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})\nu_{p}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\gamma}),

(20)

where
\bm{\gamma}_{\perp}\in\mathbb{G}(d,d-p)
is the linear space orthogonal to
\bm{\gamma}
. That is,
\bm{\gamma}_{\perp}\oplus\bm{\gamma}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Lemma 2
.

\mu_{p}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{B}_{d}}(d,p))=\kappa_{d-p}=\omega_{d-p}/(d-p)
.

Proof.

By symmetry, we have for any
\gamma_{0}\in\mathbb{G}(d,p)
,

\mu_{p}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{B}_{d}}(d,p))=\int_{\bm{\gamma}_{\!\perp}}\mathbbm{1}\{\bm{\gamma}+\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{B}_{d}}(d,p)\}\lambda_{d-p}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})=\operatorname{vol}_{d-p}(\mathbb{B}_{d}\cap\bm{\gamma}_{\perp})=\kappa_{d-p}.

(21)

∎

2.2.1
Cartesian parametrisation

In the case of
p=d-1
, the affine Grassmannian
\mathbb{A}(d,d-1)
consists of hyperplanes of dimension
d-1
. Note that
\dim\mathbb{A}(d,d-1)=d
so in order to parametrize the space of all affine planes, we need exactly
d
parameters. One choice of those parameters are the coordinates of the closest point to a given hyperplane, we write
\bm{\xi}=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{d})^{\top}
to be the vector from the origin to the closest point on the hyperplane
\bm{\sigma}
. Another choice of parametrisation is by using spherical inversion of
\bm{\xi}
. Namely,

\bm{\eta}=\frac{\bm{\xi}}{\bm{\xi}^{\top}\bm{\xi}}=\frac{\bm{\xi}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|^{2}}.

(22)

so
\|\bm{\xi}\|=1/\|\bm{\eta}\|
. There is a nice interpretation of
\bm{\eta}
. Namely, a plane
\bm{\sigma}
defined uniquely by the vector
\bm{\eta}
has a nonempty intersection with convex body
K_{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
if and only if
\bm{\eta}
does not lie in the
polar body

K_{d}^{\circ}
defined as

K_{d}^{\circ}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,|\,\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{y}\leq 1,\mathbf{y}\in K_{d}\}.

(23)

This follows from the fact that the points
\mathbf{x}
on the hyperplane
\bm{\sigma}\in\mathbb{A}(d,d-1)
satisfy
\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=1
. The following lemma gives us then the Jacobian of transformation between the standard Haar measure on a Grasmannian of hyperplanes and the Lebesgue measure of the closest point intercepts:

Lemma 3
.

Let
\bm{\sigma}\in\mathbb{A}(d,d-1)
and
\bm{\eta}=(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{d})^{\top}
be the plane vector associated to
\bm{\sigma}
such that
\mathbf{x}\in\bm{\sigma}\Leftrightarrow\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=1
, then

\mu_{d-1}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\sigma})=\frac{2}{\omega_{d}}\frac{1}{\|\bm{\eta}\|^{1+d}}\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta}).

(24)

Proof.

First, we show that our measure on the right hand side is invariant with respect to action of the group
\mathcal{G}(d)
of all proper rigid motions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We may view any
g(M,\mathbf{b})\in\mathcal{G}(d)
by its corresponding action on points
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. That is,

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=g(M,\mathbf{b})\circ\mathbf{x}=M\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}

(25)

where
\mathbf{b}
is a translation vector and the matrix
M
corresponds to (proper) rotations, hence
M
satisfies

\det M=1\text{ and }M^{\top}M=MM^{\top}=I_{d},

(26)

where
I_{d}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix. Let us find
\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}=g^{-1}(M,\mathbf{b})\circ(\bm{\sigma})
onto which
\bm{\sigma}
is mapped by applying
g^{-1}(M,\mathbf{b})
. Its associated plane vector
\bm{\eta}^{\prime}
must satisfy
\bm{\eta}^{\prime\top}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=1
. By multiplying Equation (
25
) by
M^{\top}
from the left, we obtain
M^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}+M^{\top}\mathbf{b}
. Further multiplying by
\bm{\eta}^{\top}
from the left, we get
\bm{\eta}^{\top}M^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=1+\bm{\eta}^{\top}M^{\top}\mathbf{b}
, from which we identify

\bm{\eta}^{\prime}=g^{-1}(M,\mathbf{b})\circ\bm{\eta}=\frac{M\bm{\eta}}{1+\mathbf{b}^{\top}M\bm{\eta}}.

(27)

For the norm, we have by using
M^{\top}M=I_{d}
,

\|\bm{\eta}^{\prime}\|=\frac{\|\bm{\eta}\|}{|1+\mathbf{b}^{\top}M\bm{\eta}|}.

(28)

Let us calculate the Jacobian of transformation from
\bm{\eta}^{\prime}
to
\bm{\eta}
. We have

\frac{\partial\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}{\partial\bm{\eta}}=\frac{M(1+\mathbf{b}^{\top}M\bm{\eta})-M\bm{\eta}\mathbf{b}^{\top}M}{(1+\mathbf{b}^{\top}M\bm{\eta})^{2}}=M\frac{I_{d}-\frac{\bm{\eta}\mathbf{b}^{\top}M}{1+\mathbf{b}^{\top}M\bm{\eta}}}{1+\mathbf{b}^{\top}M\bm{\eta}},

(29)

By Matrix Determinant Lemma,

\det\left(\frac{\partial\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}{\partial\bm{\eta}}\right)=\frac{\det M}{(1+\mathbf{b}^{\top}M\bm{\eta})^{d}}\left(1-\frac{\mathbf{b}^{\top}M\bm{\eta}}{1+\mathbf{b}^{\top}M\bm{\eta}}\right)=\frac{\det M}{(1+\mathbf{b}^{\top}M\bm{\eta})^{1+d}}.

(30)

In total,

\frac{1}{\|\bm{\eta}^{\prime}\|^{1+d}}\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta}^{\prime})=\frac{|1+\mathbf{b}^{\top}M\bm{\eta}|^{1+d}}{\|\bm{\eta}\|^{1+d}}\frac{\det M}{|1+\mathbf{b}^{\top}M\bm{\eta}|^{1+d}}\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta})=\frac{1}{\|\bm{\eta}\|^{1+d}}\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta})

(31)

for any
M
and
\mathbf{b}
. Therefore,
\|\bm{\eta}\|^{-1-d}\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta})
is a Haar measure on
\mathbb{A}(d,d-1)
and as such, it must differ from
\mu_{d-1}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\sigma})
by a constant multiple
[
9
, Theorem 5.4]
, say

\mu_{d-1}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\sigma})=\frac{c}{\|\bm{\eta}\|^{1+d}}\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta})

(32)

for some
c
. To check this constant is indeed
c=2/\omega_{d}
, let us calculate the
\mu_{d-1}
measure over planes which pass trough
\mathbb{B}_{d}
(the unit ball with radius one). On one hand, by definition, we already know that
\mu_{d-1}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{B}_{d}}(d,d-1))=\omega_{1}=2
. On the other, let us characterise the condition under which a
(d-1)
hyperplane
\bm{\sigma}
intercepts
\mathbb{B}_{d}
. This happens exactly when the closest point on
\bm{\sigma}
lies inside of
\mathbb{B}_{d}
. That is,
\|\bm{\xi}\|<1
, or equivalently
\|\bm{\eta}\|>1
. Hence, by using spherical coordinates and symmetry,
\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta})=\omega_{d}r^{d-1}\,\,\mathrm{d}r
, where
r=\|\bm{\eta}\|
, and therefore

\mu_{d-1}(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{B}_{d}}(d,d-1))=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{d}}\frac{c}{\|\bm{\eta}\|^{1+d}}\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta})=\omega_{d}\int_{1}^{\infty}c\frac{r^{d-1}}{r^{1+d}}\mathrm{d}r=\omega_{d}c,

(33)

so
c=2/\omega_{d}
indeed.
∎

Remark 2
.

Simple calculation of Jacobian of transformation between
\bm{\eta}
and
\bm{\xi}
(only the radial part is affected) reveals that

\mu_{d-1}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\sigma})=\frac{2}{\omega_{d}}\|\bm{\xi}\|^{1-d}\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}).

(34)

2.2.2
Blaschke-Petkantschin formula

The following formula by Blaschke and Petkantschin enables us to reparametrize an integral over some set of points
\mathbbm{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{p})
as an integral over planes in
\mathbb{A}(d,q)
,
q\geq p
on which these points lie.

Theorem 4
.

Let
f:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{p+1}\to\mathbb{R}
be a Lebesgue integrable function of a collection
\mathbbm{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{p})
of points
\mathbf{x}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
j=0,\ldots,p
. Denote
\mathbb{H}_{p}=\operatorname{conv}(\mathbbm{x})
and
\Delta_{p}=\operatorname{vol}_{p}\mathbb{H}_{p}
, then for any integer
q
such that
0\leq p\leq q\leq d
,

\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{p+1}}f(\mathbbm{x})\lambda_{d}^{p+1}(\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{x})=\beta_{dqp}\int_{\mathbb{A}(d,q)}\int_{\bm{\sigma}^{p+1}}f(\mathbbm{x})\Delta_{p}^{d-q}\lambda_{q}^{p+1}(\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{x})\mu_{q}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\sigma}),

(35)

where

\beta_{dqp}=(p!)^{d-q}\pi^{\frac{1}{2}p(d-q)}\prod_{j=0}^{p-1}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{q-j}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d-j}{2}\right)},

(36)

\lambda_{d}^{p+1}(\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{x})=\prod_{j=0}^{p}\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{j})
and
\lambda_{q}^{p+1}(\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{x})=\prod_{j=0}^{p}\lambda_{q}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{j})
are the Lebesgue measures on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{p+1}
and
\bm{\sigma}^{p+1}
, respectively.

Proof.

See Rubin
[
20
]
for an elementary proof.
∎

Remark 3
.

Denote
\gamma_{d}=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d-1}e^{-r^{2}/2}\,\,\mathrm{d}r=2^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Gamma(\tfrac{d}{2})
as before. We have
\omega_{d}\gamma_{d}=\sqrt{2\pi}^{d}
. We can express
\beta_{dqp}
in terms of
\gamma
’s and
\omega
’s as follows:

\beta_{dqp}=(p!)^{d-q}\sqrt{2\pi}^{p(d-q)}\prod_{j=0}^{p-1}\frac{\gamma_{q-j}}{\gamma_{d-j}}=(p!)^{d-q}\prod_{j=0}^{p-1}\frac{\omega_{d-j}}{\omega_{q-j}}.

(37)

The statement of the Blashke-Petkantschin formula is way too general for our purposes. We will only need its special cases. First, often we assume that the affine plane on which the points lie is exactly their affine hull almost surely. This corresponds to the case
p=q
, for which we define
\beta_{dp}=\beta_{dpp}
. Another special case is got by restricting the domain of integration using the following choice of
f
: Let
K_{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact convex body with
\dim K_{d}=d
and let
f(\mathbbm{x})=\,\,\tilde{\!\!f}(\mathbbm{x})\prod_{0\leq i\leq k}\mathbbm{1}_{K_{d}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
for some
\,\,\tilde{\!\!f}:K_{d}^{p+1}\to\mathbb{R}
suitably integrable, then

\int_{K_{d}^{p+1}}\,\,\tilde{\!\!f}(\mathbbm{x})\lambda_{d}^{p+1}(\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{x})=\beta_{dqp}\int_{\mathbb{A}_{K_{d}}(d,q)}\int_{(K_{d}\cap\,\bm{\sigma})^{p+1}}\,\,\tilde{\!\!f}(\mathbbm{x})\Delta_{p}^{d-q}\lambda_{q}^{p+1}(\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{x})\mu_{q}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\sigma}).

(38)

In this paper, mostly we use the special case with
\,\,\tilde{\!\!f}(\mathbbm{x})=g(\mathcal{A}(\mathbbm{x}))\Delta_{p}^{k}
, where
g(\cdot)
is a function of the cutting plane
\bm{\sigma}=\mathcal{A}(\mathbbm{x})\in\mathbb{A}(d,q)
only. In this case, the Blaschke-Petkantschin formula restricted on
K_{d}
as in Equation (
38
) becomes, using definition of
v_{p}^{(n)}(\cdot)
and denoting
\bm{\sigma}_{\!K_{d}}=K_{d}\cap\bm{\sigma}
(
\dim\bm{\sigma}_{\!K_{d}}=q
almost surely),

\int_{K_{d}^{p+1}}g(\bm{\sigma})\Delta_{p}^{k}\lambda_{d}^{p+1}(\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{x})=\beta_{dqp}\int_{\mathbb{A}_{K_{d}}(d,q)}v_{p}^{(d-q+k)}(\bm{\sigma}_{\!K_{d}})(\operatorname{vol}_{q}\bm{\sigma}_{\!K_{d}})^{1+(d+k)\frac{p}{q}}g(\bm{\sigma})\mu_{q}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\sigma}).

(39)

This still very general relation can be further reformulated in terms of expected values. Let us select the collection
\mathbb{X}=(\mathbf{X}_{0},\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{p})
of
(p+1)
random points
\mathbf{X}_{i}
independently from the same distribution
\mathsf{Unif}(K_{d})
. Then

Corollary 4.1
.

With respect to the uniform probability measure
\mathsf{Unif}(K_{d})
,

\mathbb{E}\left[g(\bm{\sigma})\Delta_{p}^{k}\right]=\frac{\beta_{dqp}}{(\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d})^{p+1}}\int_{\mathbb{A}_{K_{d}}(d,q)}v_{p}^{(d-q+k)}(\bm{\sigma}_{\!K_{d}})(\operatorname{vol}_{q}\bm{\sigma}_{\!K_{d}})^{1+(d+k)\frac{p}{q}}g(\bm{\sigma})\mu_{q}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\sigma}).

(40)

If moreover
q=d-1
, for which, by using Remark
3
,

\beta_{d(d-1)p}=p!\prod_{j=0}^{p-1}\frac{\omega_{d-j}}{\omega_{d-1-j}}=\frac{p!\omega_{d}}{\omega_{d-p}},

(41)

we may use the Cartesian parametrisation
\mathbf{x}\in\bm{\sigma}\Leftrightarrow\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=1
, to get

\mathbb{E}\left[g(\bm{\sigma})\Delta_{p}^{k}\right]=\frac{2\,p!}{\omega_{d-p}(\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d})^{p+1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus K_{d}^{\circ}}v_{p}^{(k+1)}(\bm{\sigma}_{\!K_{d}})(\operatorname{vol}_{d-1}\bm{\sigma}_{\!K_{d}})^{1+\frac{(d+k)p}{d-1}}g(\bm{\sigma})\frac{\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta})}{\|\bm{\eta}\|^{1+d}},

(42)

where
\bm{\sigma}
is now a function of
\bm{\eta}
. If moreover
p=q=d-1
, we get

\mathbb{E}\left[g(\bm{\sigma})\Delta_{d-1}^{k}\right]=\frac{(d-1)!}{(\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d})^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus K_{d}^{\circ}}v_{d-1}^{(k+1)}(\bm{\sigma}_{\!K_{d}})(\operatorname{vol}_{d-1}\bm{\sigma}_{\!K_{d}})^{d+k+1}g(\bm{\sigma})\frac{\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta})}{\|\bm{\eta}\|^{1+d}}.

(43)

We may write this relation in the form of the following corollary

Corollary 4.2
.

With respect to the uniform probability measure
\mathsf{Unif}(K_{d})
,

\mathbb{E}\left[g(\bm{\sigma})\Delta_{d-1}^{k}\right]=(d-1)!(\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d})^{k+1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus K_{d}^{\circ}}v_{d-1}^{(k+1)}(\bm{\sigma}_{\!K_{d}})\zeta_{d}^{d+k+1}(\bm{\sigma})g(\bm{\sigma})\|\bm{\eta}\|^{k}\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta})

where we defined

\zeta_{d}(\bm{\sigma})=\frac{\operatorname{vol}_{d-1}(\bm{\sigma}\cap K_{d})}{\|\bm{\eta}\|\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d}}

(44)

This form of the Blashke-Petkantschin formula is illustrated in Figure

Remark 4
.

We show that
\|\bm{\eta}\|
always cancels out in
\zeta_{d}(\bm{\sigma})
. First, note that
\bm{\sigma}
always separates
K_{d}
into disjoint union
K_{d}^{+}\sqcup K_{d}^{-}
, where

K_{d}^{+}=\{\mathbf{x}\in K_{d}\,|\,\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}<1\},\qquad K_{d}^{-}=\{\mathbf{x}\in K_{d}\,|\,\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}>1\}.

(45)

From homogeneity of
d
-volume,
\frac{\operatorname{vol}_{d}(\bm{\sigma}\cap K_{d})}{\|\bm{\eta}\|}=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j}\frac{\partial\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d}^{+}}{\partial\eta_{j}}
and thus

\zeta_{d}(\bm{\sigma})=-\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j}\frac{\partial\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d}^{+}}{\partial\eta_{j}}=\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j}\frac{\partial\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d}^{-}}{\partial\eta_{j}}.

(46)

2.3
General volumetric moments

In the following subsections, we will investigate the method of section integral. That is, instead of integrating over points, we integrate over section in the spirit of Blashke-Petkantschin formula. In fact, there are two approaches. The first is based on Efron’s section formula. This was the original approach used by Buchta and Reitzner
[
2
]
to deduce
v_{3}^{(1)}(T_{3})
and eventually
[
5
]
also
v_{n}^{(1)}(T_{3})
for any integer
n\geq 3
, as well by Zinani
[
21
]
who obtained
v_{3}^{(1)}(C_{3})
. The second approach, which is the main approach used in this paper, is discussed in the next section and the rest of this chapter.

2.3.1
Efron section integral

Let
K_{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex body and let
\mathbb{X}^{\prime}=(\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{d}^{\prime})
be a collection of points
\mathbf{X}_{j}^{\prime}
drawn uniformly and independently from
K_{d}
. It is convenient to denote

\gamma_{n}(K_{d})=\mathbb{E}\left[\Gamma_{\!d}(\mathbb{X}^{\prime})^{n-d+2}+(1-\Gamma_{\!d}(\mathbb{X}^{\prime}))^{n-d+2}\right],

(47)

where
\Gamma_{\!d}(\mathbb{X}^{\prime})=\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d}^{+}/\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d}
is the
d
-volume fraction of one of the two parts
K_{d}^{+}\sqcup K_{d}^{-}
into which
K_{d}
is divided by a hyperplane
\bm{\sigma}=\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{X}^{\prime})
. Written as an integral, this is equivalent to

\gamma_{n}(K_{d})=\frac{1}{(\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d})^{d}}\int_{K_{d}^{d}}\Gamma_{\!d}(\mathbbm{x}^{\prime})^{n-d+2}+(1-\Gamma_{\!d}(\mathbbm{x}^{\prime}))^{n-d+2}\lambda_{d}^{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{x}^{\prime}),

(48)

where
\mathbbm{x}^{\prime}=(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{d}^{\prime})
is the collection of points
\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\prime}=(x_{1j}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{dj}^{\prime})^{\top}
,
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
and
\lambda_{d}^{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbbm{x}^{\prime})=\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\prime})\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{2}^{\prime})\cdots\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{d}^{\prime})=\prod_{i,j=0}^{d}\mathrm{d}x_{ij}^{\prime}
is the usual Lebesgue measure on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
.

Note that
\gamma_{n}(K_{d})
is an affine functional. If
K_{d}
is some sufficiently symmetric polytope
P_{d}
, we can further use genealogic decomposition

\gamma_{n}(P_{d})=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(P_{d})}w_{C}\,\gamma_{n}(P_{d})_{C}.

(49)

Efron’s section formulae
[
6
]
then can be written in the following compact form

v_{n}^{(1)}(K_{2})=1-\frac{n+1}{2}\gamma_{n}(K_{2}),\qquad v_{n}^{(1)}(K_{3})=\frac{n}{n+2}-\frac{n(n+1)}{12}\gamma_{n}(K_{3}).

(50)

By Blaschke-Petkantschin formula (in the form of Corollary
4.2
) with
k=0
and
g(\bm{\sigma})=\Gamma_{\!d}(\bm{\sigma})^{n-d+2}+(1-\Gamma_{\!d}(\bm{\sigma}))^{n-d+2}
, we get

\gamma_{n}(K_{d})=(d-1)!\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus K_{d}^{\circ}}v_{d-1}^{(1)}(\bm{\sigma}\cap K_{d})\zeta_{d}^{d+1}(\bm{\sigma})g(\bm{\sigma})\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta}),

where
\bm{\eta}
is the Cartesian representation of
\bm{\sigma}
defined by the relation
\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=1
. In this representation, we have
K_{d}^{+}=\{\mathbf{x}\in K_{d}\,|\,\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}<1\}

and (by Remark
4
)

\zeta_{d}(\bm{\sigma})=\frac{\operatorname{vol}_{d-1}(\bm{\sigma}\cap K_{d})}{\|\bm{\eta}\|\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d}}=-\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j}\frac{\partial\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d}^{+}}{\partial\eta_{j}}=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j}\frac{\partial\Gamma_{\!d}(K_{d})}{\partial\eta_{j}}.

(51)

The integral above can be always solved when the integrand is a rational function. This happens when
K_{3}=P_{3}
a convex polygon. Then,
\bm{\sigma}\cap P_{3}
is some convex polytope
P_{2}
. Since
v_{2}^{(1)}(P_{2})
is known for any convex polytope (due to Buchta and Reitzner
[
4
]
), in fact it is a rational function, we can plug this value into the integral and then integrate everything out. We can use this formula to deduce the first volume moment relatively easily regardless of the number of points in the convex hull. This is the method that we originally used to derive
v_{3}^{(1)}(P_{3})
for polyhedra in Table
1.3
.

2.3.2
Canonical section integral

As there is no analog of Efron’s section formula for higher moments and dimensions, we might use the second section integral approach applicable to volumetric moments
v_{d}^{(k)}(K_{d})
for any
k
(picking a
d
-simplex from a
d
-dimensional body
K_{d}
). Let us restate the main Theorem
1
used in this work

Proposition 5
.

Let
K_{d}
be a
d
-dimensional convex body,
\mathbbm{x}^{\prime}=(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{d})
a collection of
d
points in
K_{d}
and
\bm{\sigma}=\mathcal{A}(\mathbbm{x}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{A}(d,d-1)
be a hyperplane parametrised by
\bm{\eta}=(\eta_{1}\ldots,\eta_{d})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
\mathbf{x}\in\bm{\sigma}\Leftrightarrow\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=1
, then

v_{d}^{(k)}(K_{d})=\frac{(d-1)!}{d^{k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus K_{d}^{\circ}}v_{d-1}^{(k+1)}(\bm{\sigma}\cap K_{d})\,\zeta_{d}^{d+k+1}(\bm{\sigma})\iota^{(k)}_{d}(\bm{\sigma})\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta})

(52)

for any real
k>-1
, where

\zeta_{d}(\bm{\sigma})=\frac{\operatorname{vol}_{d-1}(\bm{\sigma}\cap K_{d})}{\|\bm{\eta}\|\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d}},\qquad\iota^{(k)}_{d}(\bm{\sigma})=\int_{K_{d}}|\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}-1|^{k}\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}).

(53)

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Let
\mathbb{X}=(\mathbf{X}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n})
be a collection of random
n+1
i.i.d. points taken uniformly from
K_{d}
, let
\mathbb{H}_{n}=\operatorname{conv}(\mathbb{X})
be their convex hull and
\Delta_{n}=\operatorname{vol}_{d}\mathbb{H}_{n}
, then we have in general (
n\geq d
)

v_{n}^{(k)}(K_{d})=\frac{\mathbb{E}\,[\Delta_{n}^{k}]}{(\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d})^{k}}.

(54)

When
n=d
,
\mathbb{H}_{d}
is almost surely a
d
-simplex. That means that any
d
-tuple of points
\mathbf{X}_{i}
from
\mathbb{X}
form a facet. Let
\mathbb{X}^{\prime}=(\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{d})
,
\bm{\sigma}=\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{X}^{\prime})
as in the statement of the theorem and let
\operatorname{dist}_{\bm{\sigma}}(\mathbf{X}_{0})
be the distance from
\bm{\sigma}
to the point
\mathbf{X}_{0}
, then by base-height splitting,

\Delta_{d}=\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{dist}_{\bm{\sigma}}(\mathbf{X}_{0})\Delta_{d-1},

(55)

where

\Delta_{d-1}=\operatorname{vol}_{d-1}\operatorname{conv}(\mathbb{X}^{\prime})
. See Figure
2.2
below.

Figure 2.2:
Base-height splitting

Fixing
\mathbb{X}^{\prime}
, we get by conditioning,

v_{d}^{(k)}(K_{d})=\frac{\mathbb{E}\,[\mathbb{E}\,[\operatorname{dist}_{\bm{\sigma}}^{k}(\mathbf{X}_{0})\mid\mathbb{X}^{\prime}\,]\Delta_{d-1}^{k}]}{d^{k}(\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d})^{k}},

(56)

where

\mathbb{E}\,[\operatorname{dist}_{\bm{\sigma}}^{k}(\mathbf{X}_{0})\mid\mathbb{X}^{\prime}\,]=\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d}}\int_{K_{d}}\operatorname{dist}_{\bm{\sigma}}^{k}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{0})

(57)

is the
k
-th distance moment from (fixed)
\bm{\sigma}
. If
\bm{\sigma}
is parametrised Cartisianely, that means by
\bm{\eta}=(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{d})^{\top}
such that
\mathbf{x}\in\bm{\sigma}\Leftrightarrow\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=1
, we may write

\operatorname{dist}_{\bm{\sigma}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=|\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{0}-1|/\|\bm{\eta}\|

(58)

and thus

\mathbb{E}\,[\operatorname{dist}_{\bm{\sigma}}^{k}(\mathbf{X}_{0})\mid\mathbb{X}^{\prime}\,]=\frac{1}{\|\bm{\eta}\|^{k}\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d}}\int_{K_{d}}|\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{0}-1|^{k}\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{0}).

(59)

Note that since
\mathbb{E}\,[\operatorname{dist}_{\bm{\sigma}}^{k}(\mathbf{X}_{0})\mid\mathbb{X}^{\prime}\,]
is only a function of
\bm{\sigma}
, we may use Blaschke-Petkantschin formula in Cartesian parametrisation (Corollary
4.2
), that is

\mathbb{E}\left[g(\bm{\sigma})\Delta_{d-1}^{k}\right]=(d-1)!(\operatorname{vol}_{d}K)^{k+1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus K_{d}^{\circ}}v_{d-1}^{(k+1)}(\bm{\sigma}_{\!K_{d}})\zeta_{d}^{d+k+1}(\bm{\sigma})g(\bm{\sigma})\|\bm{\eta}\|^{k}\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta}),

where
\bm{\sigma}_{K_{d}}=\bm{\sigma}\cap K_{d}
. Selecting
g(\bm{\sigma})=\mathbb{E}\,[\operatorname{dist}_{\bm{\sigma}}^{k}(\mathbf{X}_{0})\mid\mathbb{X}^{\prime}\,]
and by definition of
\iota^{(k)}_{d}(\bm{\sigma})
, Equation (
56
) then becomes the desired assertion of the theorem.
∎

Remark 5
.

Note that from the theorem above we can obtain the limiting behaviour of
v_{d}^{(k)}(K_{d})
when
k\to(-1)^{+}
. Let’s take a closer look on
\iota^{(k)}_{d}(\bm{\sigma})
as in is the only singular term in
v_{d}^{(k)}(K_{d})
. Let
y\in\bm{\sigma}
be the closest point on
\bm{\sigma}
to the origin, we have
h=\|\mathbf{y}\|=1/\|\bm{\eta}\|
. Let
\mathbf{e}^{\prime}_{i}
form an orthonormal basis on
\bm{\sigma}
. Then, by Fubini’s theorem with
\mathbf{u}=u_{1}\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\prime}+\ldots+u_{d-1}\mathbf{u}_{d-1}^{\prime}

\iota^{(k)}_{d}(\bm{\sigma})\!=\!\!\!\int_{h-\varepsilon}^{h+\varepsilon}\!\!\!\int_{\bm{\sigma}\cap K_{d}}\!\!\!\!\!\|\bm{\eta}\|^{k}|x\!-\!h|^{k}\lambda_{d-1}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u})\mathrm{d}x\!+\!O(1)\!=\!\frac{2\operatorname{vol}_{d-1}(\bm{\sigma}_{\!K_{d}})}{\|\bm{\eta}\|(k\!+\!1)}\!+\!O(1)\!=\!\frac{2\zeta_{d}(\bm{\sigma})}{(k\!+\!1)\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d}}\!+\!O(1)

(60)

from which immediately

\lim_{k\to(-1)^{+}}(1+k)v_{d}^{(k)}(K_{d})=\frac{2\,d!}{\operatorname{vol}_{d}K_{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus K_{d}^{\circ}}\zeta_{d}^{d+1}(\bm{\sigma})\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta}).

(61)

Remark 6
.

Writing
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}+t\hat{\bm{\eta}}+\mathbf{u}
and integrating over
\mathbf{u}
, we obtain an equivalent expression for
\iota_{d}^{(k)}(\bm{\sigma})
in terms of geometric quantities,

\iota_{d}^{(k)}(\bm{\sigma})=\|\bm{\eta}\|^{k}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\operatorname{vol}_{d-1}((\bm{\sigma}+t\hat{\bm{\eta}})\cap K_{d})|t|^{k}\,\,\mathrm{d}t

(62)

Remark 7
.

By affine invariancy of volumetric moments and when
K_{d}=P_{d}
is a polytope, we may take advantage of its symmetries to obtain

v_{d}^{(k)}(P_{d})=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(P_{d})}w_{C}\,v_{d}^{(k)}(P_{d})_{C},

(63)

where

v_{d}^{(k)}(P_{d})_{C}=\frac{(d-1)!}{d^{k}}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus P_{d}^{\circ})_{C}}v_{d-1}^{(k+1)}(\bm{\sigma}\cap K_{d})\,\zeta_{d}^{k+d+1}(\bm{\sigma})\iota^{(k)}_{d}(\bm{\sigma})\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta}).

(64)

It may seem that finding the precise integration domains
(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus P_{d}^{\circ})_{C}
for various configurations is complicated. In fact, it is relatively easy. Recall that a configuration
C=P_{d}(S)
is defined by the property of
\bm{\sigma}
separating some given vertices from the set
S
out of the set of all vertices
V
of the polytope
P_{d}
. The domain
(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus P_{d}^{\circ})_{C}
in
(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{d})^{\top}
is then the unique solution of the following inequalities

\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{v}<1\text{ for all }\mathbf{v}\in S,\qquad\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{v}>1\text{ for all }\mathbf{v}\in V\setminus S

(65)

or inequalities with
<
,
>
flipped (we then take the union of those two options). Note that
\bm{\sigma}
always separates
P_{d}
into disjoint union
P_{d}^{+}\sqcup P_{d}^{-}
, where

P_{d}^{+}=\{\mathbf{x}\in P_{d}\,|\,\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}<1\},\qquad P_{d}^{-}=\{\mathbf{x}\in P_{d}\,|\,\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}>1\},

(66)

which means that the computation of
\iota_{d}^{(k)}(\bm{\sigma})
is also straightforward as

\iota^{(k)}_{d}(\bm{\sigma})=\int_{P_{d}^{+}}(1-\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x})^{k}\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})+\int_{P_{d}^{-}}(\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}-1)^{k}\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})

(67)

for any real
k>-1
. When
k
is an integer, let us denote

\iota^{(k)}_{d}(\bm{\sigma})_{\mathrm{N}}=\int_{P_{d}}(\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}-1)^{k}\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}),

(68)

then, when
k
is even, we have
\iota^{(k)}_{d}(\bm{\sigma})=\iota^{(k)}_{d}(\bm{\sigma})_{\mathrm{N}}
. For any integer
k
, we get by inclusion/exclusion

\iota^{(k)}_{d}(\bm{\sigma})\!=\!\iota^{(k)}_{d}(\bm{\sigma})_{\mathrm{N}}\!-\!(1\!-\!(-1)^{k})\!\!\int_{P_{d}^{+}}\!\!\!(\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\!-\!1)^{k}\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})\!=\!(-1)^{k}\iota^{(k)}_{d}(\bm{\sigma})_{\mathrm{N}}\!+\!(1-(-1)^{k})\!\!\int_{P_{d}^{-}}\!\!\!(\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\!-\!1)^{k}\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}).

(69)

Lastly, note that
\zeta_{d}(\bm{\sigma})
is a rational function of
\bm{\eta}
. To see this, we know that
\operatorname{vol}_{d}P_{d}^{+}
is a rational function in
(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{d})^{\top}
. From homogeneity (Remark
4
),

\zeta_{d}(\bm{\sigma})=-\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}_{d}P_{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j}\frac{\partial\operatorname{vol}_{d}P_{d}^{+}}{\partial\eta_{j}}=\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}_{d}P_{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j}\frac{\partial\operatorname{vol}_{d}P_{d}^{-}}{\partial\eta_{j}}

(70)

which is also rational since differentiation preserves rationality.

Remark 8
.

Fundamental Lemma of Convex Geometry tells us that a polytope is described equivalently either by linear inequalities or as a convex hull of its vertices (H- and V- representation equivalence). Hence, for example by linear programming techniques, we can deduce the vertices of
P_{d}^{+}
from the inequalities for
P_{d}^{+}
and vice versa. The same applies for the polytope
\bm{\sigma}\cap P_{d}
whose number of vertices is
n_{C}
by definition.

We have seen that the metric moments

can be computed for all odd

for odd
k>1
and

? Or when

? Another obvious question is to deduce the volumetric moments
v_{d}^{(k)}(T_{d})
for
d\geq 6
. When

, there are four section equivalent configurations

in

. Evaluating the section integral
v_{7}^{(1)}(T_{7})_{\mathrm{IV}}
for the fourth configuration is beyond the capabilities of our computer. At least, since
\bm{\sigma}\cap T_{d}
is always a
T_{d-1}
simplex in the first configuration of
T_{d}
, that is
n_{\mathrm{I}}=d
with
w_{\mathrm{I}}=d+1
. By Theorem
1
,

v_{d}^{(k)}(T_{d})_{\mathrm{I}}=v_{d-1}^{(k+1)}(T_{d-1})\frac{(d-1)!}{d^{k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus K_{d}^{\circ}}\zeta_{d}^{k+d+1}(\bm{\sigma})\iota^{(k)}_{d}(\bm{\sigma})\lambda_{d}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta})

(270)

since
v_{d-1}^{(k+1)}(T_{d-1})
are constants. More specifically, for

by using Reed’s formula, we found the following surprising relation

Based on the result we have seen so far for

for some rationals

and

GECRA: Genealogy creation algorithm

The following algorithm generates realisable configurations and their weights for any polytopes by exploiting their symmetries. The code works on iterating over
nos
(the number of selected vertices) and it has the following steps

•

Step 0: initialize empty configuration
\mathrm{N}

•

CYCLE

–

Step I: generate new configurations from old ones

–

Step II: group them into classes, select first configuration from each (the so called representant)

–

Step III: for each representant, determine if it is realisable, discard unrealisable

•

repeat step I until
nos
reaches half the number of vertices,

The algorithm is initialised by inserting vertices of
P_{d}
into
solid
as a list of their coordinates and the symmetry group
\mathcal{G}(P_{d})
into
symgroup
as list of permutations on indexes of these vertices. In the code,
dimen
is the dimension
d
. For example, Code
8
shows the input for
P_{d}=C_{3}
(the three-dimensional unit cube). Note that we only store the generators of
\mathcal{G}(C_{3})
since the whole symmetric group can be obtain by successive composition of the elements with themselves.
