Prospective association between dietary source-determined nitrate/nitrite and the risk of metabolic syndrome among children and adolescents: Tehran lipid and glucose study
Authors: Firoozeh Hosseini-Esfahani, Maryam Rafiei, Zahra Bahadoran, Mitra Hasheminia, Parvin Mirmiran, Fereidoun Azizi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConcerning rare evidence on the relationship of dietary nitrate (NO3)/nitrite (NO2) from plant or animal food groups with metabolic syndrome (MetS) in children and adolescents, this study investigates the longitudinal association of total, plant- or animal-sourced dietary NO2/NO3 intake in children and adolescents with the incidence of MetS and its risk factors.MethodsIn this secondary analysis, 1144 children and adolescents (46.2% male) aged 6–19 years were selected from the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study participants. Dietary data were gathered by a valid and reliable semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire. A validated national database was used to estimate dietary NO2/NO3 intakes. MetS was defined according to Cook’s criteria and the Iranian-modified National Cholesterol Education Program.ResultsThe mean ± SD ages of males and females at baseline were 13.1 ± 3.76 and 14.0 ± 3.77, respectively. The risk of MetS increased with consuming total nitrite and total nitrate exceeding acceptable dietary intake (ADI); however, this association disappears after adjusting for dietary variables. There were positive significant associations between total NO2 intake and the incidence of high fasting blood glucose (FBG) in follow-ups [HR (CI) Q4: 1.42(1.13–1.77), P-trend = 0.001]. Also, the HR of high FBG increased across quartiles of animal-based dietary NO3 and plant-sourced dietary NO2. The risk of high blood pressure (BP) increased about 5% with consuming every 100 mg of total nitrate exceeding ADI. Exposure to nitrate from animal and plant food groups were positively associated with high BP incidence, while higher intake of plant originated nitrite increased the risk of high BP incidence. Participants with the highest animal-sourced nitrate intake had a 19% higher risk of high triglyceride (TG), compared to the lowest intake.ConclusionOur findings provide evidence that dietary NO2/NO3 intake in children and adolescents whether from the plant or animal food sources were not associated with MetS incidence. High dietary intake of total NO2 increased the incidence of some MetS risk factors; while high intake of NO3 in spite of their sources increased the incidence of high BP. Exposure to animal-sourced nitrate intake had increased the risk of high TG.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-025-01239-w.Keywords: Nitrate, Nitrite, Metabolic syndrome, Children, Adolescent, Abdominal obesity, Dyslipidaemia, Dysglycemia

Background

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a set of risk factors, including abdominal obesity, dyslipidemia, dysglycemia, and high blood pressure (BP), that affects all age groups, including children and adolescents [1]. Globally, the prevalence of MetS was estimated to be 3% in children and 5% in adolescents [2]. Among Iranian adolescents, the prevalence of MetS is notably higher than the universal average; the overall prevalence of MetS in adolescents aged 10–18 years was 7.6% (9.4% in boys) based on the International Diabetes Federation definition [3]. MetS in children and adolescents is a powerful predictor for the progression of chronic diseases such as type 2 diabetes (T2D), hypertension, and cardiovascular disease (CVD) later in life [4]. Therefore, the emphasis should be on prevention or early detection of metabolic risk factors [5].

Previous studies reported the protective effects of specific nutrients and food groups, especially plant foods, against T2D and CVD risk factors [6–8]. Inorganic nitrate (NO3)/nitrite (NO2) compounds naturally exist in foods, including plants (mainly green leafy and root vegetables) and animal foods, drinking water, and food additives (mainly processed meats) [9]. There is now a common agreement that dietary NO2/NO3 is converted to nitric oxide (NO) through several different processes in the oral cavity, stomach, and tissues [10]; this free-radical gas and a highly reactive molecule take part in diverse physiologic functions in the cardiovascular system, including balancing glucose homeostasis and insulin signaling pathway [11], vascular regulation, decreasing BP, suppression of platelet aggregation and prevention of ischemia–reperfusion [6]. The cardio-protective attributes of fruits and vegetables and the dietary approach to stop hypertension diet may be related to the NO3-rich food affairs and the role of NO homeostasis as a conditionally essential nutrient [10, 12].

One cross-sectional study demonstrated that dietary NO3 was associated with increased serum triglyceride (TG) in adolescent females, and no associations were found between dietary NO3 intakes and other metabolic markers [13]. No significant association was found between urinary NO levels and obesity in US children and adolescents [14]. Until now, which was searched in the literature, most previous studies have been conducted on the relationship between dietary NO2/NO3 and cardio-metabolic risk factors in adults [11, 15]. Also, NO3 intake in our population was higher than the current acceptable daily intakes (ADIs) [16] which about 97% comes from plant sources. Moreover, the content of NO2/NO3 in frequently consumed food items was measured and validated among Iranians [9, 17]. Also there is rare evidence on this topic in the Middle East region [10],

Methods

Aims, design and setting of study

This study aimed to ascertain whether the dietary total, plant- or animal-sourced of NO3/NO2 intake in children and adolescents could predict the incidence of MetS or its risk factors in later years of life among Iranian population, during about 10 years follow-up.

Participants of this cohort study were chosen from the population-based Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS) [18]; this study was done to evaluate risk factors for non-communicable diseases in a group of residents of District 13 of Tehran, the capital of Iran. The first observation study was done on 15005 individuals aged ≥ 3 years from 1999 to 2001, using the multistage stratified cluster random sampling technique. Every three years, follow-up observations were done to find out developed diseases or risk factors in each period: 2002–2005 (survey 2), 2005–2008 (survey 3), 2008–2011(survey 4) and 2012–2015 (survey 5), 2015–2018 (survey 6) and 2018–2022 (survey 7). Of subjects participating in surveys 3 and 4 (baseline of our study), respectively, 3665 and 7847 individuals were randomly selected to complete the dietary assessment (66% of the total population), of them 1511 subjects aged 6–19 years. Those with MetS diagnoses [4, 19, 20] were excluded (n = 191). Also, Individuals whose energy intake to energy requirement ratio were outside the ± 2SD range excluded (n = 52) as under- or over-reporters [21]. Then a total of 1268 children and adolescents with complete data were tracked until the seventh survey. Subjects without follow-up data or missing data were excluded (n = 124); finally, 1144 subjects were included in this secondary analysis for MetS analysis. Other absolute lines were done for MetS risk factors and analyses were performed in separate files. Of 1511 subjects, 451 individuals excluded due to high WC at baseline; after excluding subjects with under- or over-reporters (n = 39) and loss to follow up or missing data (n = 59), 962 subjects were included in this secondary analysis for high waist circumference (WC) analysis. For high fasting blood glucose (FBG) analysis, 173 individuals excluded at baseline due to high FBG, then 53 and 62 subjects excluded due to under- or over-reporting of energy intake and loss to follow up or missing data, respectively. Also, 158, 433 and 380 individuals had respectively excluded from the study for high BP, low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and high TG at baseline in separate files (Fig. 1).

Outline of study participants

Dietary assessment

Trained nutritionists with extensive experience in interviewing and filling out the dietary questionnaire collected dietary data via a valid and reliable semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) over personal face-to-face interviews [22, 23]. The FFQ includes 147 food items with specific serving sizes. The usual frequency of dietary food items was asked during the last year as portion amounts per day, week, month, or year basis; the portion amounts of taken food items were changed from household measurements to grams per day in such a way that participants' frequency of consumption in each period was converted to days. Then, it was multiplied by the amount of grams per serving. For mixed dishes (e.g. pizza), we used usual restaurant recipes for calculating their food item contents. Due to the imperfection of the Iranian Food Composition Table (FCT), the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) FCT was applied for the nutrient content of cooked food items (e.g., bread, legumes, nuts, white or red meat). The Iranian FCT was used for national foods that could not be incorporated into the USDA FCT. A validated national database was used to estimate dietary NO2/NO3 intakes [9, 17]. The NO3 and NO2 content of 87 usually consumed food items was measured using validated spectrophotometric methods. These food items were categorized including grains, legumes, fruits, vegetables, dairy, meats, processed meats, and nuts. Each food item's NO2/NO3 contents were multiplied by its corresponding daily intake (grams/day) of food items to calculate dietary NO2/NO3 intakes. The validity of measured NO2/NO3 intake was estimated in a subgroup of TLGS participants (n = 251) using their urinary levels, considering individual variations and potential confounding factors. There were good correlations between dietary NO3 and NO2 intakes and their urinary levels (correlation coefficients: r = 0.59 for NO3, r = 0.29 for NO2), which support the validity of dietary assessment [17]. The Acceptable Daily Intakes (ADI) for nitrate and nitrite are respectively 3.7 and 0.07 mg/kg body weight (bw) which is published by the FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives [16]. This value is the same for all age groups, as it is calculated based on body weight.

The average of NO2/NO3 contents of food items (mg/kg bw) were computed through follow-up periods from the baseline until the duration of MetS or its risk factors diagnosis, or the last follow-up visit. Also, the total NO3 and NO2 content of food items were categorized into plant (all kinds of vegetables, fruit and fruit juices, grains, legumes, and nuts) and animal (natural: dairy products, and meats; and Processed meat products) sources.

Physical activity measurements

An expert interviewer completed a Persian-translated modifiable activity questionnaire to estimate physical activity levels. In the previous study, this questionnaire's moderate validity and high reliability were reported [24]. The intensity, frequency, and time of usual daily activities and exercise were noted over the last year. These activity data were changed into metabolic equivalent/minutes/week.

Anthropometric and blood pressure measurements

Weight (kg) was measured with a digital scale (Seca 707, Seca Corporation, Hanover, Maryland; range, 0.1–150) (accuracy 100 g) with no shoes and light clothing. Height (cm) was measured in the standing position with a non-flexible tape measure (model 208 Portable Body Meter Measuring Device; Seca) (accuracy 0.5 cm). We measured WC with the light clothing at the umbilicus after exhaling, without pressure on the body’s surface (accuracy 0.1 cm). The anthropometric assessments were done by one appraiser for women and one for men to avert subjective errors.

A standardized mercury sphygmomanometer was used to determine the systolic and diastolic BP (mmHg) with the cuff placed on the right arm. The participants stayed seated for 15 min, and then a clinician measured BP twice, and mean values were confirmed [18, 25].

Biochemical analysis

The technician collected blood samples between 7:00 and 9:00 a.m. after 12–14 h of overnight fasting. They analyzed blood samples on the day of blood collection using the Selectra 2 auto-analyzer at the TLGS research laboratory. They computed FBG concentration using the enzymatic colorimetric and glucose oxidase techniques (Vital Scientific, Spankeren, the Netherlands). TGs and total cholesterol concentrations were estimated by an enzymatic colorimetric method using glycerol phosphate oxidase, cholesterol esterase, and cholesterol oxidase, respectively (Pars Azmoon Inc, Tehran, Iran). HDL-C concentration was evaluated after precipitation of the apolipoprotein B-containing lipoproteins with phosphotungstic acid. The inter- and intra-assay coefficients of variations of FBG were 2.2%. The inter- and intra-assay coefficients of variations of TG were 1.6 and 0.6%, respectively.

Outcome definition

Participants with three or more of the specified criteria were diagnosed as MetS phenotype according to the Iranian modified National Cholesterol Education Program/Adult aged ≥ 19 years [19, 20]: [1] Abdominal obesity (WC ≥ 95 cm in men and women); [2] BP ≥ 130/85 mmHg or antihypertensive drug treatment; [3] HDL-C < 1.30 mmol/l (< 50 mg/dl) in women, and < 1.04 mmol/l (< 40 mg/dl) in men or receiving drug treatment; [4] FBG ≥ 6.11 mmol/l (≥ 110 mg/dl) or drug treatment for hyperglycemia; [5] TG ≥ 1.70 mmol/l (≥ 150 mg/dl) or drug treatment.

Cook’s criteria were used for the definition of MetS in children and adolescents, determined as having ≥ 3 of the specific criteria [26]: [1] TG ≥ 110 mg/dl; [2] HDL-C < 40 mg/dl; [3] WC ≥ 90th percentile for age and sex, based on the national reference curves; [4] systolic and diastolic BP ≥ 90th percentile for sex, age and height, based on the cut points for the Iranian BP references covering subjects aged 1 month to 18 years, and [5] FPG ≥ 100 mg/dl, based on the recommendations of the American Diabetes Association.

Statistical analysis

The IBM SPSS software, version 20, was applied for data analysis. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was recognized as statistically significant. The normal distribution of variables was tested using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Given that people in all quartiles used above the recommended amount (ADI) and to identify 50% of the population and outliers, we categorize NO2/NO3 intakes (mg/kg bw) into quartiles.

To compare the mean and frequency of participant characteristics, energy and macronutrients intakes across quartiles of the NO2/NO3 intakes (mg/kg bw), χ2 test and one-way ANOVA were used for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. Also the mean ± SD of macronutrients and NO2/NO3 intakes from plant and animal food groups across quartiles of the corresponding total NO2/NO3 intakes (mg/kg bw) were measured using one-way ANOVA. There were no interactions between NO2/NO3 intakes and MetS by sex and baseline weight status.

Multivariable Cox proportional hazard regression analyses assessed the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for MetS and its risk factors incidence across quartiles of the total, plant- or animal-sourced NO2/NO3 intakes. The first quartile was considered as the reference.

The association of MetS and its risk factors with natural- and processed animal food sources were analyzed separately in supplementary Figure.

The confounders were chosen according to the literature and their possible relationship with MetS or its risk factors [7, 27, 28]. Three Cox models were computed: model 1 adjusted for age and sex, model 2 additionally adjusted for education levels (1–8, 9–12 and > 12 years), smoking (non-smoker and smoker in follow-ups), physical activity (MET/min/week), and BMI z-score at baseline (based on sex and age categorization ≤ 10 and 11–19 years); model 3 adjusted for energy intake (kcal/day), saturated fat intake (SFA) (% of energy), fiber intake (gr/1000 kcal/day) plus model 2. Macronutrients and fiber adjusted for energy intake through transferring them to the percentage of energy and gr/1000 kcal/day intake (density method), respectively. Moreover the risk of MetS and its features incidence were estimated per every 2 mg/d NO2 or 100 mg/d NO3 intake exceeding the ADI in three aforementioned models.

Also, we applied each confounder in the univariable Cox regression model; A two-tailed P-value < 0.2 was used for specifying admission in the model. The quantitative value of the NO2/NO3 intake as quartiles was used as a continuous variable to estimate the P for trend in the Cox proportional hazard regression models. The definition of time to event was based on the mid-time between baseline and the event date (for incidence cases) or between baseline and last follow-up (for censored subjects), whichever occurred first. The proportional hazard assumption was confirmed by the Schoenfeld residuals test and plot of log [-log (survival)] vs. log (time) to see if they are parallel.

Results

In our analysis of the association between dietary NO3 and NO2 intakes in children and adolescents and MetS incidence in later years, 1144 people (46.2% male) participated. The mean ± SD ages of males and females were 13.1 ± 3.76 and 14.0 ± 3.77, respectively. The incident cases of MetS included 108 male [incidence rate (95% CI): 23.2 (19.3–28.1)] and 51 female [incidence rate (95% CI): 8.98 (6.82–11.8)] with a median follow-up of 9.74 years. There were 25.2% subjects with high WC [incidence rate (95% CI): 30.2(26.6–34.4)], 25.6% subjects with high TG [incidence rate (95% CI): 31.0 (27.4–35.1)], 36.9% subjects with low HDL-C [incidence rate (95% CI): 47.8 (43.2–53.0)], 18.4% subjects with high FBG [incidence rate (95% CI): 21.1 (18.4–24.1)], and 18.8% subjects with high BP [incidence rate (95% CI): 21.5 (18.9–24.6)].

The mean ± SD of the NO3 and NO2 for the total population were 506 ± 245 (mg/day) and 9.75 ± 4.35 (mg/day), respectively. Plant-based NO3 sources comprised 96.7% of total NO3, and animal-based food groups consumed the rest. Of total NO2 intake, 59% was derived from plant sources, and the rest was consumed from animal food groups; 22% from processed meats and 19% from natural animal foods.

In Table 1, the characteristics of participants beyond quartiles of dietary NO2 and NO3 intakes per body weight (mg/kg bw) were shown. There were statistically significant associations between age, sex, and education at baseline with NO2/NO3 intakes. The percentage of females in the fourth quartile of NO2 and NO3 intakes were 44.4 and 57.7%, respectively. Individuals with higher NO2/NO3 intakes had higher percentage of participants with lower education levels than lower intakes. There were significant associations between BMI, WC, systolic BP, diastolic BP, and HDL-C levels across quartiles of NO2 and NO3 intakes at baseline. Subjects with a higher intake of NO2/NO3 had higher BMI, WC, and TG compared to the lowest intake of NO2/NO3 at baseline. The percentage of subjects with MetS, high WC, and high BP differ across quartiles of NO3/NO2 intakes.

After adjusting for confounding variables, there were positive significant associations between total NO2 intake in children and adolescents and the incidence of high FBG in follow-ups [HR (CI) Q4: 1.42(1.13–1.77), P-trend = 0.001]. Total NO3 intake was not associated with high FBG.

The risk of high TG increased in the fourth quartiles of NO2 and NO3 intakes in model 2, compared to the lowest quartile; however, this association disappears after adjusting for dietary variables.

Low level of NO2 intake (quartile 2) was protective against high WC. Higher levels of NO2 and NO3 intakes were associated with high WC in model 2 and these associations were not significant after adjusting for dietary variables.

Discussion

This prospective study demonstrated that total, animal, and plant-sourced dietary NO2 and NO3 intakes in children and adolescents were not related to MetS incidence in later years of life over a median follow-up of 9.74 years. Dietary intake of total NO2 up to ~ 0.30 mg/kg bw increased the incidence of high FBG, high BP, and high low HDL-C about 42%, 29% and 42%, respectively. Higher intake of NO3 in spite of their sources, increased the incidence of high BP. Higher intake of animal-sourced nitrate increased the incidence of high TG and high FBG and low HDL-C, compared to the lower intakes. The lower level of total NO2 intake up to ~ 0.17 mg/kg bw decreased the incidence of high WC; however this association were disappeared for higher intakes. A positive significant trend in the incidence of high WC, high FBG, and high BP was observed with increasing intake of plant sources of nitrite.

The mean intake of NO3 in our population was higher than the estimated NO3 intake for healthy adult individuals (110 mg/day) in a previous systematic review [29]. Also, our usual NO3/NO2 intakes were more than the current ADI (3.7 and 0.07 mg/kg bw, respectively for NO3 and NO2), which was established by the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives [16]. The high intake of NO3/NO2 in our population may be attributed to the high intake of bread and foods containing NO3/NO2 in the dietary pattern and the high content of NO2/NO3 in Iranian foods [30]. The amount of total dietary NO2 and NO3 intakes and the percentages of their plant and animal sources were similar to the previous study on Iranian adults; only 41.2% of NO2 was derived from animal food groups [31], which may be attributed to low consumption of red meat and haem iron in our population [32].

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the longitudinal associations of dietary plant- or animal-sourced NO3 and NO2 intake in children and adolescents with MetS and its risk factors incidence in later years of life. Previous studies focused on adult population [10, 29]. Earlier cross-sectional study in a group of female Iranian adolescents found a positive relationship between NO3 intake from vegetables and serum TG, and no association was found between dietary NO3 and other lipid markers or BP [13]; this finding was in line to our study to some extent; the estimated total NO3 intake in this study was lower than our investigation due to using different food composition table. Total NO3/NO2 intake in our study was quantified based on the Iranian database of food items [17].

In our study, the association between the highest quartile of total nitrate intake or consuming nitrate exceeding ADI, the highest quartile of plant or animal-sourced nitrate and MetS or some risk factors including low HDL-C, high TG and high WC were not significant after adjusting for dietary factors. Moreover, no significant associations were observed between the highest quartile of nitrite and MetS, low HDL-C, high TG and high FBG outcomes after adjusting of dietary factors. These associations may not be independent of dietary variables; other nutrients including vitamins, minerals and fiber may have cardio-protective properties which is associated with the consumption of nitrate rich foods [12]. A previous study on the Tehranian adult population reported that dietary NO2 intake was not associated with CVD incidence after a median of 6.7 years of follow-up [33]; it appears that the antioxidant capacity of a diet, including vitamin C and flavonoids can modify the pathway of NO3/NO2 metabolism and prohibit the synthesis of phenotypic markers of disease risk [10, 33].

Also, these non-significant associations may be due to non-linearity or insufficient number of outcomes in the fourth quartile and low study power. The significant association between tertiles of total nitrite and MetS outcome was observed in an Ad-Hoc analysis with adjustments according to model 3 [HR (CI): 1, 1.13(0.97–1.32), 1.24(1.03–1.50) P-trend = 0.02]; however no significant associations were found between tertiles of total nitrate and MetS outcome or its risk factors (data not shown).

In our study, the highest quartiles of total nitrite intake, plant dietary sources of nitrite and animal-sourced nitrate were positively associated with high FBG incidence. A previous study on pre-diabetic adults reported that high intakes of total NO2 (≥ 11.5 mg/day) and NO3 (≥ 645 mg/day) were related to the increased risk of T2D incidence by 1.7%; however, this association was only observed in a specific phenotype and cannot be generalized to the entire population [31]. Also, a high intake of NO2 was related to a higher risk of T2D in a meta-analysis of six population-based studies [34]; this may be due to the potential conversion of NO3 to NO2 by oral NO3 reductase bacteria and then the formation of N-nitroso-compounds in the stomach which elevated risk of diseases [6]. It seems that the health effects of dietary NO3 to NO2 should be considered in the origin in which they are consumed (plants, animal foods and drinking water). Animal food sources originate N-nitroso compounds due to the reaction of nitrite and secondary amines in acidic conditions in the stomach. Cured and processed meats contain preservatives and have common preparation methods which lead to higher N-nitroso compounds formation due to its reactions with substrates such as myoglobin and amino acids like cysteine in meat [35, 36]. Previous epidemiological studies reported that the consumption of cured and processed meat associated with specific cancer types [10, 35]. Also, Exposure to these compounds may increase oxidative stress and inflammation and promote T2D [37, 38].

In contrast, plant sources of NO3 and NO2 contain natural antioxidants including vitamin C and phenolic compounds, which prohibit N-nitroso compound formation, increase nitrite reduction in the stomach, and enhance the health effects of nitrate and nitrite on cardiovascular risk factors [12]. Vitamin C enhances the conversion of acidified nitrite to NO in the plasma and gastric lumen, thus, increasing the NO pool and decreasing the oxidative stress [39]. Furthermore, improvements in blood lipids from randomized clinical trial studies found that NO3 supplementation along with vitamin C or other nutraceutical antioxidants can be a beneficial dietary strategy to fight against dyslipidemia, which then improve cardiovascular health [39].

Our findings showed that in children and adolescents, the highest intake of total dietary NO3 and NO2, plant sources of dietary NO2, animal- and plant-based dietary NO3 intakes increased the incidence of high BP, in later years of life. Previous studies reported that higher dietary intakes of NO3 and NO2 from processed meats were associated with an increased risk of hypertension compared with non-consumers in adult participants of the French NutriNet-Sante cohort study [40]. Also, higher consumption of naturally animal-based and additive-permitted meat sources of NO3 and NO2 was associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality compared with lower intakes in adult participants of the Danish Diet, Cancer and Health Study [35]. Considering high prevalence of hypertension in Iran (32% in 2021) [41], recognition and control of modifiable risk factors can improve this condition.

Long-term intake of organic NO3 or chronic intake of pharmaceutical agents, including nitro-glycerine, caused NO3 tolerance due to endothelial nitric oxide synthase (e-NOS) uncoupling via multiple mechanisms. In rodents and humans, excessive intake of NO3 was associated with a hypertensive effect or terminated the antihypertensive effect of NO3 supplementation, suggesting a NO3 tolerance [10, 42]. On the other hand, low dietary NO3 intake decreased NO bioavailability by reducing NOS expression, impairing NOS activity, and, thus, NO oxidation [43]. Thus, optimal dietary NO3/NO2 (neither deficiency nor excess) demands NO homeostasis [10]. Studies reported a decrease in plasma NO2 concentration as a biomarker of NO bioavailability in subjects with CVD risk factors, emphasizing NO homeostasis in different physiological functions [44]. The whole-body NO synthesis rate decreased in patients with hypertension and hypercholesterolemia by 31% and 40%, respectively, compared with their counterparts [45], possibly due to sustained oxidative stress and their e-NOS uncoupling. This change in enzyme function generates different reactive oxygen species instead of NO, thus resulting in NO deficiency. Optimal dietary NO3 supplies an external input for NO production with evidence of CVD health advantages [10].

Our results showed that lower levels of total NO2 intake decreased the incidence of high WC; however this association was disappeared for higher intakes of NO2 and conversely a positive significant trend in the incidence of high WC was observed with increasing intake of plant- sourced nitrite. In animal models, treatment with inorganic NO3 in e-NOS deficient mice reduces MetS through decreasing body weight, amount of visceral fat, a substantial decrease in serum TG levels, and better glucose tolerance [46]. Inorganic NO3 induces anti-obesity effects by increasing thermogenic gene expression in adipose tissue, increasing β-oxidation of fatty acids and oxygen consumption, and developing browning of white adipose tissue [47].

The cohort design of children and adolescents in a Middle Eastern country strengthens this study. The data collection method is not self-reporting. Dietary NO3/NO2 intake was estimated using a national food composition database. Multiple confounders, like smoking and physical activity, were adjusted in the models.

The main limitation of a recent study was the lack of data on the amount of drinking water and its NO2/NO3 concentration; however, the previous study on this population reported that NO2/NO3 content in drinking water had a lower contribution compared to dietary sources [48]. There are possible confounders that we did not measure and control in our models, including, alcohol consumption and dietary supplements. Moreover, puberty as a life stage did not consider in our study as a confounding factor due to missing data; however, previous studies found that overweight/obesity was the most important risk factor for cardio-metabolic risk factors in children and adolescents, while puberty only affect cardio-metabolic risk factors on some stages and the effect may be temporary [49, 50].

The validation of our FFQ was performed against biochemical markers and twelve 24 h dietary recalls [22]; however, the FFQ are prone to recall bias. Energy adjusting of dietary intakes, excluding participants with under- over reporting of energy intake can minimize this bias. Also trained interviewers help participants to recall the frequency and amount of food items in the FFQ.

The result of a recent study can be compared with other studies if high dietary NO2/NO3 intake in our population from plant foods is considered.

Conclusion

Our findings provide evidence that dietary NO2/NO3 intake in children and adolescents whether from their plant or animal food sources were not associated with MetS incidence over a median follow-up of 9.74 years. High dietary intake of total NO2 increased the incidence of some MetS risk factors; while high intake of NO3 in spite of their sources increased the incidence of high BP. The risk of high BP incidence increased about 5% for every 100 mg dietary nitrate exceeding the ADI. Exposure to animal-sourced nitrate intake increased the risk of high TG, high FBG, and high low HDL-C. High plant-sourced nitrite increased the risk of high FBG, high BP, and high WC.
