Imaging blood to periarterial CSF flow coupling using 4D flow MRI and an ultra-high-performance head-only gradient system
Authors: Tomas Vikner, Leonardo A. Rivera-Rivera, Zaynab S. Yardim, Brock Jolicoeur, Steven Kecskemeti, Kevin M. Johnson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeriarterial cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow has been hypothesized to contribute to brain waste clearance but is poorly understood. Animal studies suggest arterial pulsatility drives perivascular CSF in the direction of cerebral arterial blood flow (CBF), but human validation has relied on MRI approaches that do not inform on flow-directionality. Here, we use a high-performance gradient system enabling low velocity encoding (Venc) 4D flow MRI, to characterize cardiac-driven periarterial CSF flow and CBF-to-CSF flow coupling.MethodsHealthy participants (N = 10) underwent high resolution (0.8 mm isotropic) 4D flow MRI of blood (Venc = 120 cm/s) and CSF (Venc = 1.0 cm/s) on a 3.0T head-only system (MAGNUS, GE Healthcare; Gmax = 300mT/m, Smax = 750T/m/s). Images were reconstructed using local low rank (LLR) constrained parallel imaging and background field corrected using iterative, complex domain fitting. Luminal blood and associated periarterial CSF waveforms were extracted along the left and right anterior (ACA A1), middle (MCA M1 and M2), and posterior (PCA P2) cerebral arteries using a centerline approach, and characterized individually by amplitude and stroke volume and jointly by coupling coefficients from maximum cross-correlation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{max}$$\end{document}) and time-lags.ResultsFor low Venc 4D flow MRI, MAGNUS dramatically reduced the echo time and temporal resolution compared to whole-body systems. CBF and CSF measurements were successful in 61/80 locations (up to 8 per participant) with 19 measurements excluded due to velocity aliasing and/or poor local quality of the flow data. Inverse (anti-correlated) CBF-to-CSF coupling (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{max}$$\end{document}) was observed for most segments (56/61), with strong coupling observed for all vessels, including M1 (-0.85 ± 0.06), A1 (-0.80 ± 0.12), P2 (-0.79 ± 0.08), and M2 (-0.78 ± 0.08). Further, CBF preceded CSF for most (43/56) segments, with short CBF-to-CSF lags in A1 (5.30 ± 64 ms) and P2 (4.13 ± 63 ms), higher in M1 (43 ± 39 ms), and highest in M2 (115 ± 39 ms). CBF and CSF flow metrics were also correlated in terms of flow rate amplitude (r = 0.40, p = 0.015) and stroke volume (r = 0.56, p < 0.001).ConclusionsHigh-performance gradient systems facilitate 4D flow imaging of very slow CSF. Joint analysis of CBF and periarterial CSF allowed assessment of CBF-to-CSF dynamics coupling. For most vessels, an inverse coupling and a positive time-lag was found from CBF to periarterial CSF, suggesting that the systolic arterial expansion drives CSF backwards and inwards again during diastolic relaxation. The proposed approach can be used to improve our understanding of CBF and CSF dynamics in aging and dementia.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12987-025-00743-9.

Introduction

The characterization of neurofluid flow dynamics is becoming increasingly important to understand brain physiology, metabolic waste clearance pathways, and the pathophysiology of various neurodegenerative disorders [1, 2]. In particular, discoveries of neurofluids-mediated brain waste clearance systems (e.g., the glymphatic system in 2012 [3]) have created a need for non-invasive methods to study neurofluid flow dynamics in humans [4]. These neurofluids-mediated brain clearance systems rely on a slow influx of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) through perivascular spaces (PVS), that would either mix with or flush through the interstitial fluid (ISF) in the parenchyma to clear waste back to the subarachnoid space (SAS), where CSF would connect with meningeal lymphatic vessels that provide an exit route from brain. A detailed in‑vivo characterization of these dynamics is critical not only for clarifying fundamental physiological mechanisms, but also for identifying how their disruption may contribute to disease and serve as a target for therapeutic intervention. Non‑invasive approaches make it feasible to scan large cohorts, facilitating the collection of large‑scale datasets. Such datasets are essential for developing quantitative biomarkers, enabling early detection, monitoring of disease progression, and to deepen our understanding of neurodegenerative disease mechanisms.

One central knowledge gap in neurofluids-mediated waste clearance systems is the driving forces of CSF and ISF motion facilitating clearance. When intracranial blood volume increases during the systolic phase of the cardiac cycle, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is flushed out due to a near-constant intracranial space [4]. Similarly, as blood volume decreases during diastolic relaxation, spinal CSF flows back to the brain. This creates an oscillatory pattern of CSF motion and an inverse (anti-correlated) coupling between blood and CSF volumes. In humans, an inverse coupling between cervical blood and spinal CSF flow has been repeatedly demonstrated using velocity-sensitive phase contrast (PC) MRI [4–6]. However, in an animal study by Mestre et al. [7] using two-photon microscopy and tracer injections, periarterial CSF motion around penetrating branches of the middle cerebral artery (MCA) showed that CSF, synchronized with the cardiac cycle, followed the direction of the arterial flow – creating a positive rather than an inverse coupling of blood and CSF flow dynamics. Understanding the differences between a global inverse coupling, as required by the Monroe-Kellie doctrine, and the observed local positive coupling observed in animal models has been challenging due to a lack of methods to evaluate slow periarterial CSF flow in-vivo.

In recent years, human studies have used resting-state fMRI and dynamic diffusion-weighted (dDWI) MRI to study coupled regional oscillations of blood and CSF. For instance, Fultz et al. [8] utilized fMRI during sleep to understand interactions between neuronal activity, microvascular blood dynamics, and CSF oscillations captured by the inflow effect in the 4th ventricle. Further, Wen et al. [9] and Wright et al. [10] used fMRI and magnitude images from cardiac-gated dDWI to study blood–CSF flow coupling in proximal segments of the MCA, demonstrating that periarterial CSF pulsates with the cardiac cycle. However, magnitude images from dDWI do not inform on flow-directionality, making it challenging to understand whether this coupling reflects local coupling in the vessel flow direction, or an inverse global coupling as observed in other CSF compartments like the cerebral aqueduct (CA) [11] and spinal canal (SC) [4–6]. Further, inflow-based and diffusion-based methods can have complex relationships between signal change and CSF velocities, making absolute and interpretable quantification challenging.

PC MRI (2D PC [12], 4D flow [13, 14]) has commonly been used to assess cardiac-gated blood flow dynamics in the heart, aorta, and intracranial arteries [6, 15] and veins [16], providing both quantitative flow velocities and information about flow-directionality. 2D PC and 4D flow [17, 18] also enables quantitative imaging of cardiac-related CSF motion, but has typically been limited to study relatively fast-moving CSF in the SC and CA, mainly due to the long echo times (TE) and repetition times (TR) associated with encoding very slow CSF motion [18]. In PC MRI, velocity is encoded as phase (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:-\pi\:$$\end{document} to +\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\pi\:$$\end{document}) where the velocity encoding sensitivity (Venc) corresponds to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\pm\:\pi\:$$\end{document} phase. Lowering the Venc increases the velocity-to-noise ratio (VNR), but leads to phase aliasing for velocities exceeding the Venc. Importantly, the Venc is inversely proportional to the area of the flow-encoding gradients; hence, a low Venc desired for imaging slow CSF flow requires long flow-encoding gradients, leading to long TE/TR times. Recent advances in hardware design have enabled high-performance head-only systems (GE MAGNUS [19] and Siemens Connectome 2.0 [20]) which provide ~4x increase in gradient strength and slew rates compared to typical whole-body systems [19]. These improvements substantially reduce flow-encoding times, TE and TR, thereby enabling the acquisition of higher-SNR images while also allowing 4D flow MRI to capture slower CSF velocities that were previously inaccessible on traditional whole-body systems, without compromising excessively on spatiotemporal resolution and scan time. Despite these advances, potential challenges such as concomitant fields and gradient non-linearities inherent to high-performance head-only systems must be addressed.

Here, we investigate the use of a high-performance head-only MRI gradient system (GE MAGNUS [19]) to characterize cardiac-driven periarterial CSF motion and blood–CSF coupling in humans from low-Venc 4D flow scans. We perform PC MRI simulations to evaluate the sensitivity of the periarterial CSF signal to partial volume effects (PVEs) from mixing with neighboring blood voxels. Further, we show that high-performance gradient systems allow for periarterial CSF flow imaging along segments of the anterior (ACA), middle (MCA), and posterior (PCA) cerebral arteries. Finally, we evaluate blood–CSF flow coupling along the cerebral arteries, demonstrating the potential of our approach to provide new insights into human brain physiology.

Methods

Participants

MRI data were collected in N = 10 healthy participants (age range 24–50 years; mean age 31 ± 8 years; 3 female). The University of Wisconsin Institutional Review Board approved all study procedures and protocols following the policies and guidelines established by the campus Human Resources Protection Program. All participants signed a written informed consent before participation.

MRI acquisition

Participants underwent a single MRI session on a 3.0T high-performance head-only gradient system (MAGNUS, GE Healthcare), with maximum gradient strength 300 mT/m, maximum slew rate 750 T/m/s, and a 32-channel head coil (Nova Medical, Wilmington, MA). To capture cardiac-resolved flow, peripheral pulse gating (PPG) was collected for 4D flow scans. For each participant, 5 scans were acquired in the following order: (1) 3D time-of-flight (ToF) angiography, (2) 4D flow MRI for blood flow dynamics, (3) 4D flow MRI for CSF flow dynamics, (4) T1-weighted magnetization prepared rapid gradient echo (MP-RAGE), and (5) Inversion-prepared T2 CUBE. Total scan time was about 40 to 45 min depending on heart rate. No exogenous contrast agents were used in any of the scans.

Time of flight angiography

A ToF scan was collected immediately after the localizer to facilitate the prescription of the blood and CSF flow scans. The field of view (FoV) of the ToF scan was 24 × 24 × 4.8 cm with an axial slab centered 5 mm above the MCA M1, using an acquired isotropic voxel size of 0.8 mm, a TE/TR 1.7/5.5 ms, a flip angle (FA) of 15°, and a scan time of 2 min.

4D flow MRI

CSF (Venc: 1.0 cm/s) and CBF (Venc: 120 cm/s) 4D flow scans were acquired using a GE Kat-ARC [21] sequence with cartesian kt-undersampling of k-space. Both 4D flow scans were acquired with an isotropic resolution of 0.8 mm and an axial FoV of 24 × 24 × 4.0 cm centered about 5 mm above the MCA M1 (Sup. Figure 1), using the axial ToF images to guide slab prescription. 4D flow imaging parameters included kt-acceleration (center/outer): 1/6 (CSF) and 1/8 (CBF), k-space calibration width (ky/kz): 24/24, acquired cardiac cycle phases: 10 to 11 (depending on heart rate), TE/TR (CSF): 8.0/14.2 ms, TE/TR (CBF): 2.8/6.3 ms, flip angle (FA): 4° (CSF) and 8° (CBF), RBW: 15.6 kHz (CSF) and 31.2 kHz (CBF). The lower RBW (long readout time) for the CSF scan was chosen to better balance the long flow-encoding time with artifacts due to flow, which improved SNR in pilot CSF flow scans. Scan times ranged from 18 to 24 min (CSF) and 7 to 10 min (CBF), depending on heart rate. To assess the TR/TE benefits of the high-performance system for low-Venc 4D flow scans, TE and TR were evaluated vs. Venc for the GE MAGNUS system and a clinical whole-body system (GE Signa Premier, gradient strength = 80 mT/m, slew rate = 200 mT/m).

MP-RAGE

A T1-weighted MP-RAGE was acquired with spatial resolution and FoV matching the 4D flow scans to facilitate co-registrations, with TE/TR: 3.0/6.9 ms, FA: 8°, RBW: 31.2 kHz, acceleration (phase/slice/HyperSense): 2.0/1.0/1.5, and scan time: 2 min.

Inversion-prepared T2 CUBE

A CSF-specific inversion-prepared T2 CUBE scan was acquired to facilitate CSF segmentation, with resolution and FoV matching the 4D flow scans, and TE/TR: 60/7431 ms, FA: 8°, RBW: 31.2 kHz, acceleration (phase/slice/HyperSense): 2.0/1.0/1.5, and scan time 7 min. This scan utilized a magnetization preparation module prior to imaging consisting of a saturation-based magnetization reset, followed by a non-selective adiabatic inversion pulse which the timing of the pulse is optimized to suppress WM/GM signal while maximizing CSF signal, hence creating a contrast to provide CSF isolation. The optimization is solely based on T1 relaxation time. In this way, CSF separation is obtained without the flow sensitivity from T2 preparation modules or imaging with extended echo trains. To reduce blurring and flow artifacts, the flip angles for the refocusing flip angle train were specifically designed using the T1 and T2 of CSF and designed to produce maximal and uniform signal over trains.

MRI reconstruction

4D flow MRI scans were reconstructed offline using a combination of vendor provided tools for data preparation and post-processing (GE Orchestra, MATLAB) and GPU accelerated python libraries for image reconstruction (SigPy [22]). The reconstruction pipeline included coil sensitivity maps estimation by the JSENSE [23, 24] package and iterative SENSE using a local low rank (LLR) constraint to reconstruct the cardiac-resolved data [25]. Due to rapid phase variations in the low-Venc scans, sensitivity maps were derived from CBF scans and utilized to reconstruct both CBF and CSF scans. The reconstruction parameters were for JSENSE: kernel/calibration widths = 6/24 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\lambda\:$$\end{document}= 0.001, and for LLR: block width = 6 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\lambda\:$$\end{document}= 0.001 (CBF) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\lambda\:$$\end{document}= 0.005 (CSF) with adaptive SVD-thresholding [25]. After reconstruction, a Fermi filter was applied to the complex 4D flow data to reduce Gibb’s ringing, and the flow data were interpolated to 0.5 mm isotropic spatial resolution and 20 cardiac frames (x-y-z-t matrix size: 480 × 480 × 80 × 20) in image space, as zero-filling in k-space would substantially increase GPU memory demands and reconstruction time. All other scans were reconstructed at the scanner with the vendor supplied techniques.

4D flow MRI post-processing

Polynomial fitting of the background (BG) field, fitted over a tissue mask obtained from bias-corrected magnitude images after Otsu-thresholding and 0.5 mm spherical erosion, was used to remove phase offsets for both scans. For the blood scan, the complex difference was used to exclude vessels. For the CSF scans, iterative polynomial BG fitting (3rd order) and correction in the complex domain was used to remove concomitant fields effects from the large gradients used for low-Venc imaging (Sup. Figure 2). Laplacian unwrapping was used to correct for phase aliasing in both scans [26]. Finally, the CSF flow images as well as structural images (MP-RAGE, T2 CUBE) were co-registered to the 4D flow CBF scan using Simple-ITK [27]. The T2 CUBE was first co-registered to the MP-RAGE, before co-registration of MP-RAGE (and T2 CUBE) to the 4D CBF scan.

4D flow MRI analysis

To evaluate CBF-CSF flow coupling, the open-source QVT [14] toolbox (https://github.com/uwmri/QVT) designed for 4D flow intracranial blood flow analysis was modified to also sample CSF waveforms in segments surrounding arterial cross-sections along anterior (ACA), middle (MCA), and posterior (PCA) cerebral arteries (Fig. 1, Sup. Figure 3). In the original QVT [14], CBF waveforms are sampled along centerline segments of arterial (or venous) branches through automatic position of 2D-planes orthogonal to the flow direction, applying a local threshold or k-means clustering segmentation to the complex difference (CD) volume to separate vessels from background in the 2D-plane.

In the modified tool here used to analyze CBF–CSF coupling (https://github.com/tomasvikner/QVT-CSF), CSF spaces surrounding the vessels were segmented using a combination of CSF 4D flow scan magnitude and velocity standard deviation (SD), utilizing the fact that in the CSF scans, blood vessels are dark due to dephasing signal loss and tissue surrounding the CSF spaces have low velocity SD compared to periarterial CSF. An adaptive SD threshold was implemented using region growing so that the periarterial CSF space segment was reasonably large compared to the local vessel segment. In supplementary analyses, the effect of varying the periarterial CSF segment area on CSF flow amplitudes and CBF–CSF coupling was evaluated, by allowing the CSF segment to grow relative to the blood vessel segment using CSF/CBF area ratios of 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, and 2.5 (Sup. Figure 6, 7). For the main analyses, an area ratio of 2.0 was chosen. The CUBE was used as visual support to guide the user when selecting a suitable centerline point and used to verify the segmentation. This avoided challenges in achieving exact co-registration between these two scans, while still providing high quality depiction of CSF spaces from the inversion-prepared T2 CUBE.

Using the centerline tool, CBF and CSF waveforms were sampled at up to eight segments (left and right) of the anterior (A1), middle (M1 and M2), and posterior (P2) cerebral arteries for all volunteers. For some measurements, A2 (n = 2) were used instead of A1 and P1 (n = 3) instead of P2, in case the velocity data at the intended segment (A1 and P2) showed aliasing or severe noise. For each segment, waveforms were median-averaged over five neighboring centerline points to improve SNR and reduce operator-related variability in the positioning of the 2D-planes, making the effective slice thickness along the centerline approximately 2.5 mm. The corresponding CBF and CSF waveforms were then analyzed in terms of waveform amplitude (ml/s), stroke volume (ml), CBF–CSF coupling (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{max}$$\end{document}) and time-lag (ms) between CBF and CSF waveforms, where the coupling and time-lag was determined based on the maximum cross correlation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{max}$$\end{document}) between the CBF and CSF waveforms when a maximum lag of one fourth of a cardiac cycle (~250 ms) was allowed. In addition to stroke volumes and amplitude, complementary CSF measures were also considered, including region-averaged velocity amplitudes through the 2.5 mm slice, as well as root-mean-square (RMS) velocity within the slice, to investigate the sensitivity of CSF flow metrics to periarterial CSF segmentation. Further, time-averaged CBF rates and arterial pulsatility index (PI) were also considered for complementary analyses of CBF-to-CSF flow coupling.

Statistics

Summary statistics of the waveform analyses were described in mean (± SD). Linear (Pearson) correlation was used to evaluate links between CBF and CSF flow metrics (e.g., CBF vs. CFS stroke vol) across vessels simultaneously. Due to skewness being slightly outside the expected range for normal distribution (-1 to 1) when evaluating individual vessel pairs, Wilcoxon signed rank test were used to compare CBF and CSF amplitudes across vessel pairs. P-values < 0.05 were considered significant. Extreme outliers (> 3 standard deviations from the mean) were removed before applying linear correlation between CBF and CSF flow metrics (Fig. 6), and before compiling the descriptive statistics for the CBF to CSF coupling coefficients, such that box plots (Fig. 5) only correspond to vessel segments with an inverse CBF to CSF coupling (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{max}$$\end{document}). Adjustments for multiple comparisons (Bonferroni) were made when comparing CBF and CSF flow rate amplitudes and stroke volumes, and in correlation analyses of CBF-to-CSF flow coupling.

Simulations

PC MRI simulations of a 2D plane with a vascular cross section and a periarterial CSF compartment were performed in MATLAB (R2021b) to assess the effect of blood and CSF partial volume errors (PVEs). The CBF and CSF spaces were modeled as concentric cylinders with parabolic flow profiles, with uniform and equal magnitudes over a 24 × 24 mm FoV (241 × 241 voxels). Simulated complex data were obtained for two velocity encodes and converted to k-space data using an FFT. Reconstructed complex images were obtained by downsampling k-space, applying a circular k-space mask, adding k-space noise, and fermi filtering before applying an inverse FFT. The reconstructed phase was calculated as the phase difference of the two complex images and then converted to velocity. The peak CBF velocities (center of the vessel) were set to vary from 1.0 to 50 cm/s, the acquired resolution varied from 0.4 to 1.6 mm, and k-space noise from 0 to 1% of the maximum absolute value of k-space. The CSF peak velocity and the Venc was fixed 1.0 cm/s. More details are available in the supporting material (Sup. Figure 4).

Results

In 2D simulations of blood and periarterial CSF flow dynamics with parameters within expected ranges (acq. res. <= 0.8 mm and peak CBF vel. >10 cm/s), velocity errors in the periarterial CSF space can be expected to be less than 10% with errors approaching zero as the k-space noise approaches zero, and as the acquired resolution approach the smallest simulated voxel size of 0.4 mm (Sup. Figure 4). This arises as the CBF velocities dramatically exceed the Venc for CSF 4D flow scans due to dephasing-related signal loss of the blood signal, reducing its influence on CSF flow. Hence, the effect of blood–CSF partial volume errors on the periarterial CSF flow rates are minimal with respect to other errors. This supports the idea that for PC MRI of CSF, selecting an extremely low Venc far below the blood flow velocity is beneficial for minimizing blood contamination of the CSF velocities.

When comparing 4D flow MRI performance between the high-performance gradient system (GE MAGNUS) and a typical whole-body system with identical gradient amplifiers (GE Signa Premier), the MAGNUS gradient coils enabled dramatically reduced TE and TR, especially at very low Venc (Table 1) suitable for slow ventricular or periarterial CSF flow imaging. For the current protocol using a 4D CSF flow Venc of 1.0 cm/s, this reduced the TE and 34% (8.0 vs. 12.2 ms) and TR by 23% (14.2 vs. 18.4 ms) (Table 1).

Discussion

4D flow MRI of slow periarterial CSF dynamics was feasible using a high-performance head-only system (300 mT/m; 750 T/m/s) with shorter TE/TR times, enabling high spatiotemporal resolution at an extremely low Venc (1.0 cm/s) (Table 1). In combination with a 4D flow CBF scan (120 cm/s), this approach allowed quantitative analysis of CBF and CSF flow amplitudes and coupling along segments of the major cerebral arteries, using a modification of the open-source tool QVT. Along most of the investigated vessel segments, CSF waveforms displayed high anti-correlation to CBF waveforms (i.e., group-averaged \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{max}$$\end{document} ranging from − 0.78 to -0.85), and a positive time delay from CBF to CSF, with longer delays observed further out in the MCAs (e.g., M2) compared to other vessel segments. Hence, our findings suggest that periarterial CSF dynamics along the major intracranial arteries are coupled with the global CBF cardiac pulsatile inflow, aligning with the Monroe-Kellie doctrine.

Previous studies on CBF and CSF flow dynamics have often relied on velocity-sensitive PC MRI. The standard 2D PC has the advantage of being quantitative and straightforward to use for imaging cardiac-related flow through a single imaging slice. Several 2D PC studies have shown an inverse coupling between net blood volume (arterial inflow–venous outflow) and CSF volume at the base of the brain (e.g., ICAs, BA, IJVs, SC) (Tain et al. [4], Baledent et al. [5], Wåhlin et al. [6]). These observations can be explained by the Monroe-Kellie doctrine, suggesting that the systolic increases in intracranial blood volume displaces the CSF volume down to the SC. Similar patterns have also been observed using Multi-VENC 2D PC for simultaneous assessment of arterial, venous, and CSF flow dynamics [30]. Further, similar CBF to CSF coupling dynamics are also clearly observed in smaller high-velocity CSF ROIs throughout the ventricular system, such as the CA (Liu et al. [31, 32]), another CSF space commonly investigated by 2D PC. Collectively, 2D PC studies of CBF to CSF flow coupling suggest that on a global level, the CBF and CSF waveforms are anti-correlated and the CBF inflow peak precedes the CSF outflow peak.

Previous studies on CBF to periarterial CSF dynamics are scarce. Wen et al. [9] and Wright et al. [10] show pronounced coupling between blood and periarterial CSF flow dynamics using cardiac-gated dynamic diffusion-weighted imaging (dDWI) with EPI readouts, and that the CSF waveform shape changes with age. Interestingly, Wright et al. found CBF-to-CSF lags of ~40 and ~69 ms in their groups of younger and older participants when averaging over relatively large periarterial CSF spaces. Hence, our slightly shorter time lags at the MCA M1 (~43 ms) are relatively similar despite large methodological differences, whereas our time lags further out in the M2 are relatively high (~115 ms). While such time-lag differences could potentially reflect propagation delay along CSF spaces of the major cerebral arteries, regional differences in vascular and CSF compliance, coupling with brain motion, as well as technical challenges (e.g., segmentation issues, SNR-differences) could also explain such observations. Importantly, cardiac-induced changes in the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC), explained by dephasing-related signal loss in the magnitude images, does not provide information on flow-directionality, making it difficult to characterize the “direction” or “sign” of the coupling (i.e., correlated vs. anti-correlated). However, a recent study used cardiac-gated diffusion tensor imaging to obtain a direction-dependent ADC [33]. A major advantage of 4D flow MRI compared to magnitude-based approaches (e.g., dDWI and fMRI) is the ability to both inform on flow-directionality and provide quantitative velocities (cm/s). However, 4D flow imaging at low Venc can be limited by extremely long scan times and T2 relaxation effects in clinical scanners requiring high-performance gradients systems to enable periarterial CSF flow low-Venc measures. In previous 4D CSF flow studies, a Venc of 5.0 cm/s or higher has typically been used to image CSF motion in the SC and throughout the ventricular system, making it difficult to assess very slow (e.g., lateral ventricular or periarterial) CSF dynamics. However, a recent 7-T study by Banta et al. using 2D PC MRI at Venc 5.0 cm/s to investigate periarterial CSF dynamics report velocity amplitudes (reported as height) ranging from ~1.0 to ~2.1 mm/s in periarterial CSF spaces of the MCA [34]. In the current study, region-averaged CSF velocity amplitudes ranged from ~1.11 mm/s (PCA-P2) to ~2.27 mm/s (MCA-M2), agreeing with those findings.

Importantly, an animal study by Mestre et al. [7] used two-photon microscopy with very high spatial and temporal resolution, and found that tracers injected into CSF were synchronized to the cardiac cycle and followed the direction of the artery. While they did not report velocity amplitudes, they appear to be ~0.05 mm/s in penetrating MCA branches; however, amplitudes in rodents are difficult to compared to our estimates. In their study, there appears to be a positive coupling between blood in penetrating MCA branches and perivascular CSF [7], contrasting our findings of an inverse coupling. This discrepancy could have several explanations. (1) anesthesia and/or the injection of contrast agents alters CSF pressure and flow dynamics, (2) the 4D flow MRI resolution is not sufficient to capture local CBF to CSF flow coupling and the CSF waveforms are sampled too far from the vessel wall, or (3) CSF dynamics along the major cerebral artery exhibit patterns of global rather than local CBF to CSF coupling, and there is a transition region (e.g., at PVS of penetrating arteries) where local positively-correlated coupling starts to dominate. Regarding (3), it seems likely that periarterial CSF flow dynamics are influenced by a combination of local cerebral arterial pulsations and coupling and a global scale when CSF drains to the SC shortly after the systolic onset. Recently, the existence of a fourth meningeal layer called subarachnoid lymphatic-like membrane (SLYM [29]) was proposed (although this is debated [35]), suggesting that the subarachnoid space (SAS) CSF could be separated into two compartments, creating a separate CSF space around the arteries [29]. Further, a recent study on intrathecal contrast injections suggest contrast spreads faster in what the authors call the perivascular subarachnoid space (PVSAS), suggesting arterial pulsations drives bulk flow in the PVSAS and not just a back-and-forth motion of CSF [28]. If accurate, it also suggests that a local coupling (potentially positively correlated) could dominate within the PVSAS boundary, whereas a global coupling (likely anti-correlated) could dominate outside the boundary. In the 4D CSF flow data, there is no obvious delineation between inner/outer CSF dynamics, although in many cases, there appears to be some degree of flow-compartmentalization, in the sense that CSF velocities are clearly higher near the arterial boundary compared SAS regions further from the vessel (Sup. Figure 8). However, no change in flow-directionality at such boundaries could be observed here. Potentially, an inverse CBF-CSF flow coupling at PVS and parenchymal levels could still create a net flow of periarterial CSF in the vessel flow direction. If the arterial pulse would displace CSF in the opposite direction (Monroe–Kellie), there could be a local pressure drop due to arterial wall recoil. However, whether such oscillatory motion is sufficient to create a backflow powerful enough to sustain CSF-mediated waste clearance in the parenchyma is impossible to investigate with current cardiac-gated MRI sequences. Here, very small arterial branches were not included in the main analyses due to challenges with sampling reliable waveforms; however, examples of M3 and M4 waveforms (Sup. Figure 9) highlight the potential to assess CBF-to-CSF coupling further out in the vascular tree, which could be considered in future studies targeting blood-to-CSF flow coupling at higher resolution.

Other studies have also utilized fMRI to study CSF dynamics. Fultz et al. show that neuronal activity, gray matter blood oxygenation-level dependent (BOLD) signal, and fMRI inflow effect (4th ventricle) are coupled, with higher coupling during sleep than when awake [8]. Roefs et al. combined BOLD fMRI and 2D PC to compare inflow-based CSF dynamics and PC-based CSF dynamics, and to evaluate the blood-CSF flow coupling when relying on both inflow effect and phase [36]. Further, global coupling with BOLD fMRI and CSF oscillations was recently found to be related to Tau pathology in people with elevated AD biomarkers, possibly due to impaired dysfunctional CBF to CSF flow coupling and impaired CSF-related clearance [37]. Furthermore, functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) with a high sampling frequency was used to assess CBF-to-CSF flow coupling in controls and AD patients using cross correlation [38]. They show anti-correlated CBF and CSF waveforms in the subarachnoid CSF space of the forehead [38], as well as positive associations between cross-correlation-based coupling and MMSE score, highlighting the need to further assess 4D CBF to CSF flow coupling in aging and AD cohorts.

The current study is mainly limited by the spatial and temporal resolution needed to image perivascular CSF flow. Quantitative flow analysis in periarterial CSF demands both an extremely low Venc and a high spatial resolution, making it challenging for 4D flow at this point. Importantly, human studies on CBF to CSF coupling need to verify results from animal studies [7] in PVS of penetrating arteries, something that would provide more insights on CBF pulsations as a driving force of periarterial CSF in parenchymal PVS, closer to the ISF compartment. Further, PVEs from blood motion near the vessel boundary could influence the CSF waveform. However, the large bipolar gradients required for a low-Venc CSF scan (1 cm/s) creates a strong dephasing-related signal loss in high-velocity blood voxels. Although our simulations reflect highly simplified perivascular models (concentric cylinders with parabolic flow profiles), they show that the contribution of blood signal to the periarterial CSF signal is small, as long as the acquired voxel size is sufficiently small relatively to the vessel size and the blood velocity is sufficiently high relative to the Venc. However, venous structures accompanying the major cerebral arteries (e.g., middle cerebral vein branches), with relatively slow velocities in the opposite direction of arterial flow, could bias CSF flow estimates. While the inversion-prepared T2 CUBE allowed distinction of arteries, tissue and CSF, the acquired resolution (0.8 mm) is insufficient to capture very small veins potentially present in the periarterial CSF space. Hence, future studies on periarterial CSF flow imaging should consider high-resolution CT or MR venography to minimize the impact of venous contamination on CSF flow. Another limitation is that the background phase induced by the large bipolars might not be sufficiently corrected for. Polynomial background phase correction was employed by fitting the phase to the background field and iteratively correcting the data in the complex domain. Alternatively, the gradient impulse response can be modelled to achieve more accurate correction of background phase errors [39]. Further, previous PC MRI studies targeting tissue motion report cardiac-related velocities up to 2.0 mm/s for the brainstem and 1.5 mm/s for the thalamus [40]. Hence, in low-Venc flow-imaging, tissue motion might also be encoded as phase, which might be difficult to separate from background noise. In the current study, 19/80 segments were excluded due to aliasing or poor data quality, raising concerns about robustness. This was particularly noted in periarterial CSF of smaller vessels (e.g., the PCA), where the spatial resolution was often insufficient to reliably separate CSF from tissue and vessels. Alternatively, spiral or EPI readouts can be used to provide more efficient sampling of k-space, allowing CSF flow imaging at higher resolution. Spirals readouts have been briefly tested on 4D CSF flow MRI on a clinical whole-body system [18]. However, with high-performance systems, applying two gradients at once leads to concomitant gradient terms [41] and trajectory errors, something that could potentially be corrected by measuring the gradient field [42] to correct the k-space trajectories. In addition, the current Venc (1.0 cm/s) might have been too low for some segments (e.g., A1 and M1). Hence, future studies should consider Venc scouts, dual-Venc 4D flow MRI, or slightly higher Venc (e.g., ~2.0 cm/s) when targeting periarterial CSF. Finally, future work should evaluate the accuracy and test-retest reliability and 4D CSF flow MRI at low Venc settings and compare CSF dynamics between whole-body systems and high-performance head-only systems.

In conclusion, the high-performance gradient system facilitated 4D flow imaging of very slow CSF motion at reasonable TE/TR times. Joint analysis of CBF and periarterial CSF motion allowed assessment of CBF to CSF dynamics coupling. An inverse CBF-to-CSF coupling and a positive CBF-to-CSF time-lag was found for most of the investigated vessel segments. These findings suggest that the arterial and subsequent microvascular expansion flushes CSF backwards (in the opposite of the vessel direction) following the systolic onset, and inwards again (along the vessel direction) during diastole. The findings question the effect of a local pumping mechanism as the main driver of periarterial CSF motion where CBF pulsations drives CSF along the vessel direction following the systolic pulse, at least along large segments of the major cerebral arteries. Instead, a more global coupling between blood and CSF motion, potentially mediated by brain motion, seems more likely to explain the inverse coupling associations observed along major cerebral arteries in this study. Potentially, measurements further out in the cerebrovascular tree (e.g., penetrating arteries) would show different coupling dynamics, highlighting the need for further investigation of blood-to-CSF flow coupling along smaller vessels and in larger cohorts.
