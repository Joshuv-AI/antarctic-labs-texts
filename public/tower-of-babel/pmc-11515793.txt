Population genetic differentiation and structure of rare plant Anemone shikokiana based on genotyping-by-sequencing (GBS)
Authors: Kang An, Chunxia Zhou, Boqiang Tong, Dan Liu, Xiaohan Shan, Xin Zhang, Fuhua Bian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnemone shikokiana (Makino) Makino is a perennial herb of the genus Anemone in the family Ranunculaceae. Endemic to the Shandong Peninsula in China and Shikoku Island in Japan, it is a rare and endangered plant with a narrow, disjunct distribution. It is threatened with extinction and is in urgent need of conservation. Evaluating the genetic diversity of species, revealing the population genetic structure and gene flow, and inferring the population history are of great importance for species conservation, especially for rare and endangered plants.ResultsIn our study, 73 samples from eight wild populations in China were sequenced by Super-GBS, yielding a total of 40.59 G clean reads and 52,231 SNPs. Based on the obtained SNP data set, we evaluated the population genetic diversity, genetic structure, and gene flow of A. shikokiana. A low level of genetic diversity was found (He = 0.1925, Ho = 0.1422). The neighbor-joining (NJ) tree, principal component analysis and ADMIXTURE analysis suggested that these 73 A. shikokiana could be considered as two groups. Pairwise genetic differentiation coefficients (Fst) indicated that genetic differentiation was lower between adjacent populations and higher between geographically separated populations. The gene flow between Kunyu Mountain and Lao Mountain was very low. However, neither of the two regions showed evidence of Isolation by Distance.ConclusionsHere, we revealed the population genetic structure and gene flow of A. shikokiana from the Shandong Peninsula, China. This research provides valuable genetic resources for A. shikokiana and contributes to the scientific and effective conservation of the species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05705-1.

Background

On a global scale, Anemone shikokiana is an endemic species distributed in Shandong Peninsula of China and Shikoku Island of Japan. In Japan, it is critically endangered according to IUCN criteria (http://www.biodic.go.jp/rdb/book _list _sy.csv). While it is a rare and narrowly distributed species with an important ornamental and medicinal value in China and exists only in Laoshan and Kunyu mountains above 600 m in altitude, for only a few sites and individuals are known [1]. The Laoshan and Kunyu Mountains are located in the eastern part of the Shandong Peninsula, near the Huanghai Sea, with a distance of over two hundred kilometers between them. The average annual temperature is between 15 and 20 °C above 600 m.

Previous studies have analysed the distribution pattern, physiological and ecological adaptation mechanisms of A. shikokiana in different habitats. In Kunyu Mountain (Hanfengling and Taiboding) and Laoshan Mountain (Hualiukou and Laoding), four populations were discovered. These four restricted patches were located in two distinct typical habitats: the understory of mixed coniferous and broad-leaved forests and in shrubs of mountaintop [2]. A. shikokiana can efficiently use limited resources to adapt to different living environments [3]. It reproduces mainly by outcrossing with the help of insects for pollination, is somewhat capable of self-fertilization, and does not rely on wind pollination or agamospermy [4]. The genetic diversity of A. shikokiana has also been evaluated using traditional molecular marker techniques, such as AFLP [1]. AFLP is a molecular marker technique that amplifies DNA segments through PCR and then cuts them with restriction enzymes, generating a series of DNA fragments of different lengths, forming a fingerprint profile. It evaluates the genetic variation of natural populations by utilizing the differences in DNA fragments, without the need for sequence analysis, primer synthesis, etc., thereby saving time and cost. The results of the previous study [1] indicated that the genetic diversity of this species was related to geographic distance and habitat. Specifically, genetic diversity was higher among populations than within populations, and higher in Kunyu Mountain than in Laoshan Mountain. Additionally, genetic diversity was higher in mixed coniferous and broad-leaved forests than in hilltop shrubs. Understanding the relationship between genetic diversity and the adaptability of species is crucial because genetic diversity provides the raw material for natural selection, allowing populations to adapt to changing environmental conditions. The conservation efforts typically aim to maintain or even increase genetic diversity within species. As an endangered plant species, A. shikokiana is currently in urgent need of conservation attention, and detailed conservation strategies are necessary. Moreover, research on A. shikokiana remains very limited, and its ornamental and medicinal values are yet to be fully explored, which could serve as an effective means to attract widespread attention. Additionally, by studying the intermittent distribution of this herbaceous plant, researchers can also gain insights into the biogeographical connections and distribution patterns between the Shandong Peninsula and the island of Shikoku in Japan, providing valuable references for understanding the formation and evolutionary history of plant populations in these two regions.

Research into the conservation biology of rare and endangered species is essential, and the genetic diversity of populations, genetic structure and gene flow are indispensable research priorities [5]. Threatened species, often rare and narrow distribution, tend to have low levels of genetic diversity, making them more susceptible to local extinction. Therefore, the natural selection or demographic phenomena may be more pronounced in these populations [6], making them more likely to be threatened by global climate changes and anthropogenic pressures [7]. The small effective population size of these species can lead to reduced genetic diversity through allelic loss due to genetic drift and inbreeding [8]. Additionally, significant differences are detected in genetic diversity between geographical distributions [9], and genetic differentiation shows low-to-moderate between close and geographically separated populations [10]. Landscape fragmentation may further increase genetic erosion [11]. Therefore, for endemic and narrowly distributed species, low levels of genetic variation may either cause or result from their rarity. However, such species may still exhibit high genetic diversity due to their evolutionary histories and reproductive strategies [7]. To better understand the genetic characteristics and population dynamics of rare and endangered species with limited geographic ranges, it is important to study their genetic structure and gene flow [10, 12]. Gene flow has a significant impact on shaping genetic structure. Small and isolated populations are vulnerable to genetic drift and reduced gene flow, resulting in significant population structure. Identifying the population structure of these species can help to identify conservation units and develop targeted conservation strategies [13].

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are the most abundant form of genetic variation in plant genomes [14]. They are widely used for genetic mapping, evolutionary studies, and genome-wide association studies, detecting genetic diversity, and as genetic markers in marker assisted selection [15]. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) is a powerful tool for identifying genetic variations, but it can be prohibitively expensive when working with non-model plants without reference genomes, especially when many samples need to be sequenced. Reduced-representation genome sequencing (RRGS) typically sequences only parts of the genome, which is less expensive than WGS, yet still generates a large number of SNPs. Genotyping-by-sequencing (GBS) is a type of RRGS that provides a rapid, cost-effective means of genotyping populations [16]. The GBS technique is a high-throughput sequencing method that involves enzymatic digestion of DNA samples using restriction endonucleases, followed by ligation of adapter sequences and PCR amplification to generate DNA fragments. After sequencing and analysis, a large number of SNPs markers can be obtained and genotyped. This approach is equally effective as whole genome sequencing but is simpler and less costly [17, 18]. And in recent years, GBS has been widely used in the study of genetic diversity, genetic structure, and population evolution of species, such as Tropical Maize [15], Lentil [19], Hunan tea plant [20], Doum Palm [17], and pea [21]. Super-GBS is a simplified genome sequencing technology further developed based on GBS and has been used in several species since the first public publication of the improved GBS technology by Qi et al. [22], such as Tetraena mongolica [12].

In this study, we used an abundance of SNPs generated from Super-GBS to evaluate the genetic diversity, genetic structure, and gene flow of A. shikokiana populations representing the entire distribution range in China. Based on the genetic results, we proposed some possible reasons for limiting genetic diversity and suggested some targeted conservation and management strategies.

Methods

Plant samples

In this study, we first conducted a comprehensive survey of the biology and distribution area of Anemone shikokiana: it is only found in Laoshan (Hualiukou and Laoding) and Kunyu (Hanfengling, Taiboding and Laotieshan) mountains in China. To guarantee comprehensive coverage of A. shikokiana’s distribution area, we devised a sampling strategy comprising 8 sampling sites in the Laoshan and Kunyu mountains. The distance between two sites was not less than 500 m. At each site, we collected ten samples with a pairwise spacing of over 10 m to minimize interference and cross-contamination between samples. However, at one point, A. shikokiana was scarce and only three samples were collected. Finally, we collected fresh and young leaves from 73 individuals of A. shikokiana from 8 sites, in Shandong Peninsula in the fall of 2021. The plant species were identified by Professor Fuhua Bian from Yantai University. Vouchers were stored in the Forest Heritage Museum of Kunyu Mountain (KYC-0032). The details of each population, including collection site name and GPS coordinates, are shown in Fig. 1; Table 1.

DNA extraction and quality testing

DNA extraction was performed using the DNAsecure Plant Kit (TIANGEN, China). DNA integrity was detected by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA concentration and OD260/OD280 ratio were detected by NanoDrop spectrophotometer. The results showed that the quality of 73 DNA samples met the concentration requirements and could be used in the subsequent experiments.

Library construction and high throughput sequencing

DNA extracted from each plant sample was digested with PstI-HF and MspI enzymes and followed by ligation with T4 DNA ligase. The enhanced magnetic bead recovery system was then used to recover 300–700 bp fragments by adjusting the volume ratio of magnetic bead solution to binding product. The PCR was performed using the Expand High Fidelity Plus PCR System, then the concentrations were measured by Qubit (the concentration was adjusted to greater than 5 ng/μL). Finally, the library was sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq PE150.

Raw data filtering and SNP analysis

To obtain raw reads for each sample, the offline data were split by barcode and enzyme cleavage site using Stacks (v2.4) software [23], followed by quality filtering using Fastp (v0.20.0) software [24] to obtain clean reads. The criteria for quality filtering were as follows: (1) splice sequences were removed; (2) reads with 5 or more non-AGCT bases were removed; and (3) a sliding window of 4 bases was used for windows, with excision if the average base quality value was less than 20. As there is no full genome sequence information available for A. shikokiana in public databases, and no closely related species to use as references, we constructed a GBS reference genome for this study and performed mutation detection. The clean reads were clustered by ustacks program, and the cstacks, sstacks, tsv2bam, and gstacks programs were used for genome construction and variant detection. The options were -M 3 for ustacks (the maximum distance (in nucleotides) allowed between stacks was 3) and -n 3 for cstacks (the number of mismatches allowed between sample loci was 3). The Vcftools (v0.1.16) software [25] was used to filter the SNP typing results. The filtering criteria were as follows: (1) non-diploid loci are excluded; (2) the number of supported reads (total depth, DP) must be greater than four; (3) loci with SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) less than 0.01 should be excluded; (4) the deletion rate of SNP typing must be less than 20%. The filtered SNPs were used for subsequent analyses.

Population genetic structure analysis

The Treebest (v1.9.2) [26] software (http://treesoft.sourceforge.net/treebest.shtml) was used to calculate the distance matrix, and thus the neighbor-joining tree was constructed, the reliability of the evolutionary tree was tested by bootstrap method (repeated 1000 times).

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the obtained SNP markers using gcta (v1.26.0) software [27], and the two feature vectors with the highest contribution were obtained.

To compensate for the lack of PCA and phylogenetic tree and gain a better understanding of the population structure of A. shikokiana, we performed population structure analysis from K = 2 to K = 8 using ADMIXTURE (v1.3.0) software [28] with 10 replications for each K value. The optimal K value was determined by the cross-validation error (CV).

Population genetic diversity

The Hardy-Weinberg p-value was calculated using vcftools software. A p-value greater than 0.05 indicates that genetic equilibrium was achieved in the investigated population. Several genetic diversity metrics were calculated using the R package genepop (v1.1.4) [29], including expected heterozygosity (He), observed heterozygosity (Ho), polymorphism information content (PIC), effective number of alleles (Ne), and nucleotide diversity (Pi). Genetic differentiation coefficients (Fst) between populations were calculated using the R package StAMPP (v1.6.3) [30]. Reynolds’ genetic distance (DR) and gene flow (Nm) between populations were estimated by Fst. The formulas are as follows: DR = -ln(1-Fst); Nm = (1-Fst)/4Fst. An Analysis of Molecular Variance (AMOVA) was performed using the R package poppr (v2.9.4) [31] to quantify the genetic variation.

Correlation analysis between genetic distance and geographic distance

The R package vegan (v2.5-7) was used to calculate the haversine distance based on the geographic location information of all populations. The Mantel test was performed using vegan, utilizing the haversine distance and genetic differentiation coefficients. Finally, scatter plots were created, and correlation coefficients and fit equations were included.

Results

Sequencing data statistics and SNP discovery

In this study, 73 A. shikokiana samples (8 populations) were successfully sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq PE150 platform. A total of 7,212,684 restriction tags were obtained, ranging from 61,237 to 138,018 for each sample. The average length of the GBS tags per sample ranged from 142.41 bp to 143.55 bp, and the average depth ranged from 8.11X to 19.68X. A total of 40.59G of high-quality data with 277,746,428 clean reads were obtained after filtering the raw reads. On average, each sample yielded 0.56 Gb of data. The sequencing data volume and data quality statistics for 73 samples are presented in Table S1. The output data showed that the average GC content was 45.40%; the average Q20 (the proportion of bases with a base error rate of less than 1/100) was 95.01%; the average of Q30 (the proportion of bases with a base error rate of less than 1/1000) was 87.85%. The GBS reference genome constructed in this study contained 1,519,935 sequences. A total of 52,231 SNPs were obtained after SNP typing and filtering.

Genetic diversity parameters

Using the SNP data set, we measured several genetic diversity values, including expected heterozygosity (He), observed heterozygosity (Ho), polymorphism information content (PIC), effective number of alleles (Ne), and nucleotide diversity (Pi). For all 73 samples as a whole, the values of He varied from 0.0270 to 0.5000, with an average of 0.1925; the values of Ho varied from 0.0000 to 1.0000, with an average of 0.1422; the values of PIC varied from 0.0267 to 0.3750, with an average of 0.1608; the values of Ne varied from 1.0278 to 2.0000, with an average of 1.3009; the values of Pi varied from 0.0272 to 0.5043, with an average of 0.1941. For each population, the genetic diversity statistics and standard errors are shown in Table 2. Among all the eight populations, the He, PIC, and Ne values in population D were the highest (average 0.1420, 0.1164, and 1.2303, respectively), and population F had the highest Ho and Pi (average 0.1560 and 0.1541, respectively). On the other hand, population C had the lowest He, Ho, PIC, Ne, and Pi with the mean values of 0.0970, 0.1070, 0.0771, 1.1674 and 0.1177, respectively.

Phylogenetic, principal component, and population genetic structure analyses

To evaluate the phylogenetic relationships among all samples, we used the neighbor-joining method to construct a phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2). In our tree, the 73 A. shikokiana samples were distributed on different branches. However, probably due to gene exchange and migration between some populations, they were not strictly clustered by population. First, eight individuals from population F and one individual (AS_54) from population G clustered on one branch. One individual from population G (AS_61) became a separate branch. Twenty-three individuals from populations A, B, and C clustered together as one branch; within this large clade, populations A and B formed a secondary clade and became sister to the branch formed by population C. In terms of geographic distribution, these populations all originated from Mt. Kunyu, with populations A and B in close proximity and population C more distant (Fig. 1). The remaining 40 individuals clustered into a large branch, with D and E becoming sister clades. Unexpectedly, the clade consisting of populations A, B, and C from Mt. Kunyu was sister to the clade formed by populations D, E, G, and H from Mt. Laoshan.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was a non-parametric alternative to investigate genetic structure. To detect the fine genetic structure among the populations, PCA was performed using gcta software based on the whole SNP data set obtained from the 73 samples (Fig. 3). We got the two eigenvectors with the highest impact, PC1 and PC2, with contribution rates of 47.99% and 10.90%, respectively. At PC1, the 73 samples were divided into two groups: one included 8 individuals from population F and one individual from population G, which showed negative values; the other included all the remaining individuals, which showed positive values. At PC2, the total samples were divided into three groups: the first included 23 individuals from populations A, B, and C, showing negative values; the second included 8 individuals from population F and one individual from population G, showing values around 0; and the third included all other individuals, showing positive values. The clustering result was similar to the phylogenetic tree. In the PCA plot, the sample points of the group consisting of 8 individuals from population F and one individual from population G were relatively discrete, indicating greater variability among these individuals.

To complement the phylogenetic and principal component analyses, and estimate the ancestral component of each individual, we used the ADMIXTURE software, assumed in advance that the different cluster numbers (K) were 2 ∼ 8, and repeated each K value for 10 times to find the optimal cluster number (generally, K with the smallest cross-validation error (CV) value is selected). The minimum CV values were 0.43557, 0.43776, 0.45311, 0.47335, 0.51533, 0.53386, 0.55979, respectively, when K was 2–8. The optimal choice for K is 2 as the CV value was at its lowest (0.43557) at this point. In this situation, the 73 A. shikokiana individuals were divided into two groups, one consisting of three populations A, B, and C, and the other consisting of populations D, E, F, G, and H (Fig. 4). It was found that this grouping method was consistent with the geographic origin of the samples, i.e., samples from Mt. Kunyu were grouped in one group and samples from Mt. Laoshan were grouped in another one. Here, only seven individuals showed gene flow: AS_46, AS_57, AS_58, AS_62, AS_67, AS_68, and AS_70. Nevertheless, the case of K = 3 must also be considered, given the phylogenetic tree and the classification results of PCA. When the value of K increased to 3, all samples were divided into three groups (Fig. 4). The first group remained unaltered, comprising all individuals from populations A, B, and C. The second group encompassed all individuals from populations D and E, while the third group comprised the remaining individuals. In addition, in these two cases, all individuals from populations A, B, and C from Mt. Kunyu were grouped together, and the vertical bars corresponding to these 23 individuals were not segmented, indicating that they were likely derived from the same ancestor.

Furthermore, we conducted separate ADMIXTURE analyses for individuals from different regions (Mt. Kunyu and Mt. Laoshan). The CV values for the samples from Mt. Kunyu with K of 1–10 were 0.36155, 0.46559, 0.54465, 0.64952, 0.73596, 0.84235, 0.81169, and 0.92704, 1.02626, and 0.93339. For the samples from Mt. Laoshan, the CVs for K of 1–10 were 0.34458, 0.35547, 0.37664, 0.40659, 0.44399, 0.47861, 0.53255, 0.58415, 0.61035, and 0.70650. The CV values were minimized at K = 1, indicating that the optimal number of clusters is 1.

Genetic differentiation and gene flow

Genetic differentiation coefficients (Fst) between populations and p-values were calculated using R package StAMPP. The output p-values were all reported as 0, indicating that the differentiation between the populations was significant. Reynolds’ genetic distance (DR) and gene flow (Nm) were counted by Fst, and the results are shown in Tables 3 and 4. The Fst value indicates the degree of separation between populations. According to Wright [32], Fst values between 0 and 0.05 indicate gene sharing and no genetic differentiation between populations; Fst values between 0.05 and 0.15 indicate moderate differentiation; Fst values between 0.15 and 0.25 indicate high differentiation, and 1 indicates complete isolation. In this study, pairwise Fst values ranged from a low of 0.0174 (adjacent populations A and B) to a high of 0.4539 between geographically separated populations (C and F). The Fst values presented in Table 3 indicate significant genetic differentiation between populations from distinct regions (Fst > 0.25). Moreover, population differentiation was observed to be lower among populations within the same region compared to those from different regions. In addition, DR values varied from 0.0176 to 0.6049 and Nm values varied from 0.3008 to 14.1178. The results of genetic distance and gene flow level analysis were consistent with the results of genetic differentiation coefficients. Pairwise genetic distances (DR) between individuals in the same area were lower than those in different areas. According to Wright et al. [32], Nm was classified as high (1.0), medium (0.250–0.99) and low (0.0-0.249). A medium Nm value (0.3008–0.3769) indicated limited gene flow between these populations, whereas a high Nm value (2.2179–14.1178) indicated strong gene flow (Table 3). It was found that there was higher gene flow between populations in the same mountain (between A and B, C; D and E, F, G, H). Our data suggested that pollen movement and seed dispersal between Mt. Kunyu and Mt. Laoshan were very limited. In summary, populations in the same region had frequent gene flow, small genetic distances, and low genetic differentiation, whereas populations in different regions had low gene flow, large genetic distances, and significant genetic differentiation. The results showed that the genetic relationships in the A. shikokiana population were strongly influenced by geographic isolation.

To further assess population genetic differentiation, the eight populations were grouped into two based on their geographic regions. Group one included populations A, B, and C, while group two included populations D, E, F, G, and H. The hierarchical AMOVA was performed with three categories: among groups, among populations within groups, and within populations. The results showed that 25.83% of the total variation was found among groups, 31.76% among populations within groups, and 42.41% within populations (Table 5).

Discussion

Understanding genetic indicators between populations has vital importance to species dynamics, evolutionary history, and conservation, especially for rare and endangered species [33]. In this study, we detected genetic diversity, differentiation, and structure of the rare plant A. shikokiana using the Super-GBS method. A total of 52,231 SNPs were obtained from 73 A. shikokiana samples. The average sequencing depth of all samples was 11.87×, the GC content distribution was reasonable at 45.40%, the average Q20 value was greater than 95.01%, and the average Q30 value was greater than 87.85%, indicating that the sequencing quality was high.

Genetic structure of A. shikokiana

Information on genetic structure is necessary for the conservation of natural populations [34]. This research is the first report to identify genome-wide SNPs through the super-GBS approach for A. shikokiana and to determine the population genetic structure of this species in China. The results of the phylogenetic analysis and the PCA were similar, with a division of all samples into three clusters. However, we found that the samples of population G showed a high degree of dispersion and clustered with the branches of other populations, and AS_61 formed a branch alone. Therefore, we suggest that future studies expand the sampling area near population G and include more samples for phylogenetic analysis. Maybe, this will reveal new population structures those were not detected in this study. Many similar studies as the current study on the population genetics of plants by using genome wide SNPs showed that ADMIXTURE, PCA, and phylogenetic analysis were consistent with each other [9, 35]. But in this study, the results of three analyses were still somewhat different. In the ADMIXTURE analysis, the smallest CV value corresponds to K = 2, which grouped all the samples into two genetic clusters: one group consisting of A, B, and C, and the other group consisting of D, E, F, G, and H. The former was from Mt. Kunyu, whereas the latter from Mt. Laoshan. When k = 3, the classification results were unsatisfactory and inconsistent with the PCA and phylogenetic tree. However, no further genetic structure was detected in the following ADMIXTURE analyses of both regions. Combined with the geographic distribution information, populations D, E, F, G, and H were in close proximity and did not show significant segregation. Therefore, we finally determined the number of genetic clusters that best reflected the geographic distribution of the A. shikokiana populations as K = 2. In this case, these 23 individuals of A, B, and C shared the same set of genetic resources and originated from the same ancestor. Furthermore, there was a small amount of genetic material that flowed from Mt. Kunyu to Mt. Laoshan, which may be due to accidental animal pollination.

Moreover, the genetic results obtained from different analyses indicated that the grouping of all samples was not strictly consistent and followed as well as their species lineage [36, 37]. In addition to spontaneous hybridization and mutation among the accessions [37], we think that the most probable reason for this is that both NJ tree and PCA methods focus on using the similarities and differences between samples to explore the relationship and evolution between populations, while ADMIXTURE pays more attention to inferring and interpreting the ancestral components and mixture proportions of each sample in order to help us understand the formation of population genetic structure and the history of gene flow.

In addition, to assess the genetic structure of the entire existing wild population of A. shikokiana, we need to supplement the Japanese samples for further study.

Gene flow and the formation of fragmentation distribution in A. shikokiana

Two important parameters, gene flow (Nm) and genetic differentiation coefficient (Fst), can provide different evidence for understanding population structure, and these two are negatively correlated [38]. In our study, pairwise Fst values showed significant differentiation between populations from different geographic locations (Table 3), with 25.83% of the total genetic variation attributed to differences among groups (Table 5). This suggests that geographical barriers play an important role in shaping genetic structure. For populations within the same region, Fst results showed moderate or no differentiation, while AMOVA appeared to be more sensitive, revealing that 31.76% of the total genetic variation was found among populations within groups. This was higher than the variance between regions, which may be attributed to microhabitat variation and local adaptation. It is significant that genetic variation within populations accounted for the largest share, at 42.41%. This suggests that a large amount of genetic variation exists among individuals, which is crucial for their adaptation and resistance to environmental change.

Strong gene flow between populations can slow or prevent geographic differentiation. In natural plant populations, gene flow is mainly mediated by pollen and seeds [39], therefore, the genetic structure of plants is influenced by seed and pollen dispersal [40]. In our study, the Nm values were smaller at greater distances (e.g., C and F) and larger at shorter distances (e.g., A and B). The observed pattern can be partially explained by the reproductive biology of A. shikokiana. This species produces flat achenes, which are disliked by birds, and its seeds fall randomly as the fruit ripens. This dispersal system reduces dispersal distance and prevents gene flow between regions. It is self-compatible and is mainly outcrossed by insects; the pollinating insects include beetles and hoverflies, which travel short distances [4]. Thus, in the contiguous region, pollen is readily dispersed by insects, resulting in apparent gene flow between closed populations. Geographic range or reproductive biology could partially explain the genetic distance and genetic structure found in A. shikokiana. Although geographic distance may not be the primary factor determining the overall distribution pattern of A. shikokiana, it plays a significant role in the fragmented distribution of its populations.

Low genetic diversity of A. shikokiana

Genetic diversity analysis can facilitate the understanding of genetic structure and provide useful information on the evolutionary history of populations [10]. The level of genetic diversity tends to be related to the range of the species, with narrowly distributed plants usually having lower levels of genetic diversity [41]. A. shikokiana is a typical rare plant species with a narrow range. We provided some new evidence to assess the population genetic diversity using the obtained SNP dataset in this study. Several genetic diversity indices indicate low genetic diversity in A. shikokiana at both the species and population levels. For example, the expected heterozygosity (He) and observed heterozygosity (Ho) of A. shikokiana at the species level were 0.1925 and 0.1422, respectively. At the population level, population D had the highest He at 0.1420, while population F had the highest Ho at 0.1560. Population C had the smallest sample size, with only three individuals, and therefore showed the lowest genetic diversity. This population is located at a low altitude (Table 1), which is likely a contributing factor to the rarity of wild A. shikokiana individuals in the region. Populations D and E were located in Mt. Laoshan National Scenic Spot, but their genetic diversity was still at a high level among the eight populations. To some extent, it can be reflected that the development of local tourism has not destroyed the habitat of the A. shikokiana population on a large scale.

The genetic diversity of A. shikokiana at the species level was explored in the present and previous studies [1, 2] using sample data from the Shandong Peninsula. However, this study still shows a certain novelty in revealing the genetic diversity of A. shikokiana. There are some differences: (1) The sampling strategy differed in the previous studies divided four groups according to different habitats and sample spacing was small (2 and 5 m), whereas in this study sampling was random and sample spacing was all greater than 10 m. Although the number of samples per group was reduced, the number of groups increased and covered a larger area, and the spatial scale effect may have influenced the results on genetic diversity. In addition, natural selection in heterogeneous environments leads to increased fitness of local genotypes, and diverse environments show significant associations with genetic differentiation [42]. (2) AFLP can detect polymorphisms in DNA sequences, while proteomics reflects the expression level of proteins, revealing the actual gene expression. These methods are informative for exploring species’ genetic diversity, but they have limitations in addressing the geographic variation of populations. Sample selection and environmental factors can introduce bias and uncertainty in the results. In contrast, GBS, as a molecular marker method based on high-throughput sequencing technology, uses genome-wide enzyme cleavage sites, providing higher resolution and wider genome coverage, and is able to effectively reveal the genetic structure and variation patterns within populations. In this study, 52,231 high-quality SNPs were obtained through Super-GBS technology. The large number of SNPs provides richer genetic information, enabling a more refined analysis of genetic diversity. Additionally, it improves the reliability and stability of statistical analysis, reducing the chance of incidental results. Having more SNP loci also means covering a wider range of the genome, allowing for a more comprehensive understanding of population genetic structure.

This study revealed that the genetic diversity of A. shikokiana is relatively low, which may be attributed to the following factors: (1) The habitat range of A. shikokiana is narrow, and it has certain requirements in terms of elevation. Although perfect adaptation strategies have been developed in two different habitats (the mountaintop-shrub and the broad-leaved and coniferous mixed forest), it is still difficult to adapt to environments outside the habitat. (2) There are more pollinators in the hilltop scrub and the plants have a higher fruit set rate, but the soil conditions are not favorable for seed germination. The soil under the mixed forest is favorable for seed germination, but there are few pollinators in this environment. This contradiction is not conducive to population development. (3) The breeding system of A. shikokiana is obligate outcrossing and partially self-compatible. It is mainly pollinated by insects, strongly influenced by weather. However, the flowering season coincides with the rainy season, which can negatively affect the flower visiting behavior of insects. This can lead to an increased chance of self-pollination [4]. (4) The pollen and seeds of A. shikokiana have a limited dispersal range, and due to the specific requirements of the habitat, resulting in populations that may not spread widely.

Conservation strategies

Genetic diversity is the product of the long-term evolution of a species or population and is necessary for the survival and sustainability of the population [43]. Rare species will have lower genetic diversity than common species due to their smaller population sizes [44]. Therefore, one of the goals of endangered plant conservation is to maintain the genetic diversity of plant species [45]. In our study, the low level of genetic diversity of A. shikokiana indicate that it has limited adaptation to environmental changes. Maintaining the genetic diversity of natural populations is important for their continued survival, environmental adaptability, and evolutionary potential. Based on the results obtained in this study, we propose some conservation strategies: (1) In situ conservation. (2) Establish a gene bank to collect genetic information of A. shikokiana. (3) Design a seed collection strategy, establish a germplasm resource bank, and collect as many samples as possible from each population with different genetic resources throughout the natural range. (4) In-depth studies on pollinators of A. shikokiana and even Anemone are needed. (5) Conduct artificial hybridization to rapidly improve heterozygosity.

Conclusion

In this study, we used several bioinformatics methods to analyze the population genetic diversity, genetic structure, and gene flow of A. shikokiana. The results showed that the genetic diversity of A. shikokiana was low. Though, the distribution area of the A. shikokiana population was far from the city, and even within the scenic spot, the damage to its habitat by human activities was minimal. Therefore, the low level of genetic diversity observed in this study was most likely due to physiological factors of its own. Gene exchange between closely spaced populations was frequent. In contrast, a high degree of genetic differentiation has developed between populations in Mt. Laoshan and Mt. Kunyu due to the lack of gene flow. In the present study, these samples were divided into two large groups. Geographical distance greatly influenced genetic distance, which may be related to the pollen and seed dispersal. Based on the results of this study, we have made a number of recommendations to prevent further population decline and reduce the risk of extinction. But we also encountered some difficulties in collecting samples from the Japanese population. In the future, further integrative research should include samples from Japan.
