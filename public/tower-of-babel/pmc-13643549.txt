Percutaneous Retrieval of a Hepatic Vein Stent From the Right Ventricle
Authors: Patrick Zamora, Tony Truong, Madjid Boukantar, Romain Gallet, Paul-Matthieu Chiaroni, Anne-Sophie Tuffreau-Martin, Oueies Labidi, Antoine Molho, Emmanuel Teiger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Migration of a venous stent into the right heart is a rare but life-threatening complication of endovascular venous interventions, with a reported overall incidence of 1%-4%.1 Once intracardiac, the device carries risks of ventricular arrhythmia, tricuspid or pulmonary valve injury, right ventricular (RV) outflow tract obstruction, and sudden death, mandating urgent retrieval in virtually all cases. Percutaneous endovascular retrieval is now preferred over open cardiac surgery in hemodynamically stable patients: In a landmark series of 45 patients, Gabelmann et al2 demonstrated a 91.1% success rate for percutaneous removal of lost or misplaced intravascular objects, including endovascular stents, with no late complications over a mean 42-month follow-up. This compares favorably with the morbidity of cardiac surgery and cardiopulmonary bypass.3Take-Home Messages•When standard snaring fails for large self-expanding stents in the inferior vena cava, the Triple-Rail Snare Technique—1 intraluminal Super-Stiff wire plus 2 extraluminal starter wires—restores reliable snare capture using only standard catheterization laboratory inventory.•Planned large-bore femoral venous preclosure must be performed before sheath dilation, not at closure, and cardiac surgery should be available on standby throughout the procedure.

However, published percutaneous techniques have been described predominantly for smaller-caliber stents using conventional loop or dual-snare configurations.4,5 Large self-expanding nitinol stents pose a distinct mechanical challenge: their high radial force causes wall apposition in the inferior vena cava (IVC), preventing reliable loop engagement once the device recovers its nominal diameter. No standardized rescue strategy exists for this scenario.6 We describe the Triple-Rail Snare Technique, a simple, reproducible method requiring no equipment beyond standard catheterization laboratory inventory.

Case Summary

A 71-year-old man with arterial hypertension, dyslipidemia, infrarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm, and hepatocellular carcinoma in a noncirrhotic liver underwent left hepatectomy with curative intent. The postoperative course was complicated by right hepatic vein stenosis producing secondary Budd–Chiari syndrome, treated via a transjugular approach with implantation of a 14 × 60-mm Protégé self-expanding nitinol stent (Medtronic) across the right hepatic vein–IVC junction.

On postoperative day 3, computed tomography imaging was performed to evaluate a new pleural effusion, which incidentally revealed complete migration of the stent into the RV cavity (Figure 1). The patient remained hemodynamically stable without cardiac symptoms. Transthoracic echocardiography confirmed the stent within the RV without tricuspid dysfunction, pericardial effusion, or RV outflow tract obstruction (Figure 2); the electrocardiogram showed normal sinus rhythm. Procedural Steps

Step 1—large-bore access with planned preclosure

Under ultrasound guidance, right femoral venous access was obtained using an 18-gauge micropuncture system. Before fascial dilation, 2 ProStyle suture-mediated closure devices (Abbott Vascular) were preplaced (a mandatory step given the requirement for a 22-F sheath; note: Use of the ProStyle system for closure of a large-bore venous access site represents off-label use of this device). A 45 cm 22 F GORE DrySeal Flex sheath (W. L. Gore & Associates) was then advanced to the IVC under fluoroscopic control, over a 145-cm 0.035-inch Amplatzer Super-Stiff wire (Boston Scientific).

Step 2—stent mapping

A 7 F Judkins Right 4 (JR4) catheter was advanced through the sheath to the right atrium. Hand-injection venography confirmed the stent oriented along the RV long axis, proximal end toward the tricuspid valve, and distal end at the RV apex.

Step 3—initial 3-loop snare: RV to IVC

A 3-loop snare (Merit 15-mm loops) was introduced through the JR4 catheter and maneuvered to engage the proximal end of the stent. Smooth, continuous axial tension was applied, avoiding any rotational or jerky movement to prevent tricuspid leaflet entrapment. The stent was retracted through the tricuspid valve plane into the right atrium and parked in the IVC without resistance.

Step 4—IVC resnaring failure and pivot to Triple-Rail

Standard resnaring within the IVC failed on 2 successive attempts: the 14-mm caliber and high radial force of the Protégé stent caused wall apposition, preventing reliable loop engagement. This is the critical juncture at which the Triple-Rail Snare Technique was applied.

Step 5—Triple-Rail scaffold construction

The JR4 catheter was advanced to the proximal stent end. Three guidewires were deployed in a deliberate hierarchy (Figures 3 and 4): 1) one 0.035-inch Amplatz Super-Stiff wire with a 7-cm floppy tip (Boston Scientific) was threaded through the central stent lumen, exiting distally, providing rigid axial control and preventing buckling during traction; 2) two 0.035-inch J-tip standard starter wires were positioned along the outer stent surface, one on each lateral aspect, acting as snare-guide rails and preventing stent rotation. Step 6—definitive capture and retrieval

An Amplatz GooseNeck single-loop snare (Medtronic, 20 mm) was advanced coaxially over all 3 guidewires as a unit. The loop tracked distally along the wire cluster to the distal stent end, where the 2 extraluminal wires directed it around the strut circumference. The snare was tightened; tactile confirmation of secure engagement was immediate. The stent was retracted into the 22-F sheath under coaxial tension, foreshortening predictably, and retrieved completely without fragmentation.

Step 7—hemostasis

Following sheath removal, the 2 preplaced ProStyle devices were deployed sequentially, achieving complete hemostasis without manual compression or bleeding complications. Postprocedural transthoracic echocardiography confirmed no pericardial effusion, intact valves, and preserved biventricular function. The electrocardiogram was unchanged. The patient was monitored in the cardiac ICU for 24 hours without events.

Potential Pitfalls

•Tricuspid valve entrapment (step 3): apply only smooth, coaxial, slow tension. If resistance is felt at the valve plane, partially release the snare and re-engage before resuming retraction.•Stent fragmentation: self-expanding nitinol stents rarely fragment, but ensure the sheath inner diameter exceeds the stent’s collapsed profile before retraction. Avoid axial compression against the sheath hub.•IVC snare failure: do not persist with standard snaring beyond 2 attempts against a large-caliber, wall-apposed stent. Early pivot to the Triple-Rail scaffold saves time and reduces the risk of vessel injury.•Access site hemorrhage: a 22-F venotomy without preclosure carries a high bleeding risk. Two suture-mediated devices must be preplaced before any dilation—never as an afterthought at closure.•Stent re-embolization during retraction (steps 5 and 6): maintain continuous intraluminal guidewire position throughout. Loss of wire position may permit stent remigration toward the right atrium.

Conclusions

The Triple-Rail Snare Technique provides a simple, reproducible rescue strategy for percutaneous retrieval of large-caliber self-expanding nitinol stents from the right heart when standard IVC snaring fails. Based on the present single-case experience, the technique requires no specialized equipment and can be assembled from standard catheterization laboratory inventory within minutes. It may be considered at any center with large-bore venous access capability, provided cardiac surgery is available on standby throughout the procedure. The core insight—a rigid intraluminal Super-Stiff wire for axial control combined with 2 soft extraluminal starter wires as snare-guide rails—directly addresses the mechanical reasons conventional snaring fails against high-radial-force nitinol devices. Prospective preclosure for 22 F femoral venous access is a nonnegotiable prerequisite. To our knowledge, this is the first percutaneous retrieval of a hepatic vein stent from the RV performed using this technique under local anesthesia without surgical support. Wider applicability will require validation in additional cases.
