Surface-engineered porous MXene-elastomer composites-based ultra-sensitive pressure sensor assembled via electrostatic interaction for human-machine interface
Authors: Dokyung Kim, Dong-Weon Lee, Jaesam Sim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFlexible and wearable devices require high-performance pressure sensors for human motion monitoring, but conventional sensors have limited deformability and complex fabrication. To overcome these issues, a composite structure incorporating conductive nanomaterials and a deformable elastomer has been proposed. However, an inherently weak adhesion between the elastomer and nanomaterials remains a practical challenge. To address this, we show a porous polymer sponge coated with Ti3C2Tx MXene as an ultra-sensitive pressure sensor. The sensor is fabricated by creating a deformable elastomer sponge using a sugar template, followed by a simple dip-coating process to apply MXene nanosheets. The elastomer surface is chemically treated using surfactants to enhance the surface energy and induce electrostatic forces, thereby improving adhesion between the elastomer and MXene while maintaining the intrinsic mechanical properties of the elastomer. This stable MXene-based network ensures high sensitivity across a broad pressure range. The sensor’s low stiffness and porous structure enable rapid response to subtle pressures like breathing and gentle touch, as well as larger forces like arm bending and foot pressure. When integrated into a human-machine interface for the walking assistive device, the pressure sensors enable active control of the exoskeleton, facilitating easier joint motion for rehabilitation. Subject terms: Electronic properties and materials, Nanoparticles, Chemistry

Introduction

The use of flexible materials in pressure sensors has attracted significant interest in areas such as electronic skin (E-skin)1–3, medical monitoring4, soft robotics5, and flexible touch screens6 because of their excellent performance in high-response and wide-range sensing. This interest has propelled research into flexible pressure sensors, making them highly suitable candidates for wearable sensor applications. Flexible pressure sensors can measure body signals with high sensitivity through contact with the skin. Especially, ultra-sensitive performance is essential for flexible pressure sensors to be integrated in assistive technology for disabled people. Conventional input methods based on rigid pressure sensors often require a certain level of force or dexterity, which may not be accessible to users with reduced muscle strength or motor impairments. In contrast, ultra-sensitive interfaces enable precise input detection even with minimal force, allowing disabled people to operate electronic devices more effectively. This technology can be applied to assistive communication devices, adaptive input systems, and prosthetic control interfaces, significantly enhancing accessibility. By detecting subtle pressure variations, these sensors facilitate more natural interactions, thereby improving the usability and inclusivity of human-machine interface (HMI) systems.

Conventional flexible pressure sensors utilize various mechanisms, such as conductive7, capacitive8, triboelectric9, and piezoelectric effects10, to convert external pressure into electrical signals. Among these sensing mechanisms, conductive-type flexible pressure sensors offer several advantages, including simple manufacturing processes, low cost, excellent sensing performance, and easy signal acquisition. Flexible conductive pressure sensors have mainly focused on coating conductive nanomaterials onto polymers such as polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS)11, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA)12, and polyurethane (PU)13,14. The performance of flexible conductive pressure sensors is closely related to the mechanical deformability of polymers. Especially, Eco-flex has emerged as a deformable elastomer with high sensitivity in conductive pressure sensors owing to its inherently low stiffness. It also exhibits excellent biocompatibility, making it suitable for human monitoring applications.

Recently, various microstructure designs, such as pyramid-shaped microcones15, cracks16, and microdomes17,18, have been proposed to induce large deformations under the same pressure, enabling the implementation of flexible pressure sensors with high sensitivity, even to small forces. However, the methods for developing these microstructures involve complex manufacturing processes, leading to mass production difficulties. Another strategy for enhancing the sensitivity of contact-based resistive mechanisms is to use highly elastic sponge-like porous structures as base polymers. When pressure is applied, the porous structure gets compressed, rapidly increasing the internal contact area. This porous structure allows for large deformations under low pressures, resulting in high sensitivity and low detection limits. However, sponge-based pressure sensors exhibit high sensitivity in low-pressure ranges owing to significant deformation but show reduced sensitivity in high-pressure ranges. As an alternative method to improve sensitivity over a wide pressure range, conductive nanomaterials, such as carbon nanotubes (CNT)19, reduced graphene oxide (rGO)20,21, and transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDC)22, have been widely investigated as conductive fillers bonded to elastomers. These nanomaterials exhibit significant electrical conductivity and mechanical strength, making them highly suitable as conductive fillers. However, CNTs and graphene lack surface functional groups, which makes it difficult to adhere to the surface of Eco-flex. Additionally, CNTs have a limited contact area, which results in unstable and poor adhesion with polymers. In the case of two-dimensional nanomaterials such as graphene, they can form an increased contact surface with the polymer. However, because they are typically hydrophobic, it is necessary to first bond the hydrophilic graphene oxide with a polymer and then reduce it again, leading to time-consuming and complex manufacturing processes. MXene, a two-dimensional nanomaterial discovered in 2011, has received extensive attention as a new conductive filler owing to its large surface area, excellent mechanical strength, and high electrical conductivity. Compared to graphene, which only involves C–C bonds, MXene nanosheets have excellent hydrophilicity and numerous surface functional groups, such as –O, –OH, and –F, which allow for a variety of bonds (covalent, metallic, and ionic), facilitating the fabrication of composites with high sensitivity by combining them with other materials23,24. Thus, not only do MXenes show electrical conductivity comparable to that of CNTs and graphene, but MXene nanosheets also contain abundant surface functional groups (–OH, –F, –O) that allow them to enhance the surface adhesion to the Eco-flex compared to CNTs and graphene25.

Various approaches utilizing porous elastomer have been proposed to fabricate pressure sensors with high sensitivity, as these structures exhibit a large compressibility even under very low pressure. Zheng et al. developed an MXene/cotton fabric (MCF) pressure sensor by coating MXene onto cotton fabric, which demonstrated a sensing range of 0–160 kPa and a high sensitivity of 5.30 kPa-1 in the low-pressure region (0–1.30 kPa)26. However, it still exhibited a limited, insufficient sensitivity. Additionally, Wang et al. developed an MXene-sponge pressure sensor using a dip-coating method with PVA nanowires as spacers27. The sensor showed high sensitivity (up to 442 kPa−1), fast response (138 ms), and durability over 10,000 cycles. However, fabricating high-performance wearable pressure sensors that simultaneously achieve high sensitivity and a wide sensing range remains a significant challenge.

In this study, we investigated novel porous sponge-based pressure sensors with MXene coating, advanced from our previous feasibility study28. In contrast to other MXene sponges, we employed Eco-flex with a low elastic modulus as a sponge material to achieve ultra-sensitivity. However, due to the low surface energy of Eco-flex, uniform coating is challenging. To address this, we designed a process that enables electrostatic coating by utilizing cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB). This pressure sensor, developed through a simple and facile fabrication process. First, a three-dimensional (3D) porous sugar template was created by applying moisture and heat to sugar powders to form a scaffold for an effective network. Subsequently, a mixture of Eco-flex (0030) parts A and B in a 1:1 ratio was poured into a mold containing the sugar template to fabricate a porous sponge, which was then functionalized with CTAB solution, producing a cationic terminal group on the surface. This surface treatment allowed us to achieve both a high sensitivity and a wide pressure-sensing range. A highly deformable pressure sensor was fabricated by coating a porous sponge with Ti3C2Tx MXene using a dip-coating method, followed by the attachment of electrodes. The uniform coating of MXene combined with the highly deformable porous structure of Eco-flex induced ultrahigh sensitivity and rapid response. Finally, the surface-engineered porous MXene@Eco-flex based pressure sensor was tested to monitor various human activities, confirming its potential for use as a human–machine interface.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of Ti3C2Tx MXene

Ti3C2Tx MXene nanosheets were synthesized from a Ti3AlC2 MAX phase, where M is a transition metal, A refers to an element from Group 13 or 14, and X is carbon and/or nitrogen. The detailed procedure is as follows. First, to prepare an etching solution for removing Al layers from Ti3C2Tx powder (400 mesh; Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd), 1.6 g of LiF (Tokyo Chemical Industry, Japan) was added to a 9 M HCl solution (37 wt%; Junsei Chemical, China) and completely dissolved using a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) magnetic stirring bar for 30 min. To prevent an explosive exothermic reaction, the Ti3AlC2 MAX phase powder (1 g) was gradually added to the etching solution in five increments of 200 mg each and stirred for 48 h. The mixture was then repeatedly neutralized with deionized (DI) water and centrifuged until the pH reached 5.5–6 (Fig. S1). We conducted a simple pH monitoring procedure during each washing cycle by extracting the solution with a dropper and testing it using litmus paper. In the first cycle, the litmus paper turned red, indicating an acidic environment. As the cycles progressed, a gradual color shift from red to yellow was observed, suggesting a transition toward a neutral pH. Details of the synthesis process are provided in the Supplementary Information. Centrifugation separated the mixture into a supernatant and a black slurry at the bottom. The supernatant was transferred to a clean beaker, ultrasonicated in an ice bath for 1 h, and then dried at 60 °C in a vacuum oven to prevent oxidation.

Fabrication of the porous sponge

A porous polymer sponge was produced using a sugar template. A 3D structured sugar template was obtained by placing sugar powder into a mold and adding moisture as a spray. Then, it was dried in an oven at 80 °C for 30 min, allowing the sugar powder to agglomerate. Next, a 1:1 mixture of Eco-flex (Smooth-on, USA) components A and B was poured onto the 3D porous sugar template and placed in a vacuum chamber for 30 min to remove bubbles. After all the bubbles were removed, the Eco-flex was cured in an oven at 60 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, it was dissolved on a hot plate at 90 °C, producing the porous sponge.

Fabrication of pressure sensors based on MXene-coated porous sponge

To fabricate a pressure sensor by coating MXene nanosheets onto the surface of a porous sponge, surface functionalization of the polymer was performed. As shown in Fig. S2, the porous sponge was immersed in a 0.02 g solution of CTAB (Sigma-Aldrich Chemicals, Germany) diluted in DI water (50 ml). The solution was then subjected to ultrasonic treatment for 1 h to allow CTAB to infiltrate the pores of the sponge. Subsequently, the porous sponge was coated by dipping into a 5 mg/ml MXene solution and dried in a vacuum oven to prevent oxidation. As shown in Fig. S2c, MXene coating is clearly observed on the cross-section of the sponge, and the SEM image confirms that MXene is uniformly coated throughout the internal porous structure (Fig. S2d). Finally, copper foils were attached as electrodes to the top and bottom of the MXene-coated porous sponge and encapsulated with polyimide (PI) passivation.

Characterizations and experimental set-up

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Emcrafts, Cube II) was employed to assess the surface characteristics and morphology of pristine Eco-flex and MXene nanosheet-coated sponges. In addition, the deformation of the sensor cross-section under pressure was examined using SEM. Field-emission SEM (FESEM; FEI, QUANTA 200F) was used to observe the cross-sectional surface morphology of the MXene nanosheets. The materials before and after etching the Al layers of the MAX phase were analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD; Panalytical, XPert Pro) patterns, measured in the range of 5°–80°. Mechanical pressure was applied and recorded using a universal testing machine (UTM; AND, STB-1225L, Japan) to evaluate the performance of the fabricated pressure sensor. When pressure was applied, the change in the electrical conductivity of the sensor was measured using a source meter (Keithley 2634 B). These devices were connected to measure the output of the pressure sensor. The experimental setup is shown in Fig. S3.

Results and discussion

Figure 1a illustrates the synthesis of MXene nanosheets. First, LiF (1.6 g) was added to a 9 M HCl solution and completely dissolved for 30 min. Then, 1 g of Ti3AlC2 MAX powder was etched in the LiF/HCl solution. The mixture was repeatedly neutralized with DI water and centrifuged; in the seventh cycle, MXene was dispersed and separated into a supernatant and a black slurry. Subsequently, ultrasonic exfoliation was performed for 1 h to obtain MXene. Further centrifugation yielded exfoliated MXene nanosheets with a lateral size of 9 µm, an interlayer distance of 1.7 µm, and a monolayer thickness of 400 nm. As shown in the red inset of Fig. 1a, the cross-sectional surface morphology of the MXene nanosheets exhibited an accordion-like structure, indicating that the Al layers were etched29. To fabricate the porous Eco-flex sponge, a sugar template was first obtained by baking sugar and water in a mold (blue inset in Fig. 1b). Next, a 1:1 mixture Eco-flex components A and B were poured and cured to create Eco-flex coated sugar cubes of dimensions 2.5 × 2.5 × 0.8 cm3 (red inset in Fig. 1b). This solution was then dissolved in hot water to obtain a porous sponge (green inset of Fig. 1b). The fabrication process for the MXene@Eco-flex pressure sensor is shown in Fig. 1c. Figure S4 shows the difference in electrical performance of the MXene-sponge with and without CTAB treatment. As MXene cannot form a continuous coating on the surface of the sponge without CTAB, no current path is established, making it difficult to measure resistance. In contrast, the MXene sponge functionalized with CTAB exhibited successful electrical pathways. It is confirmed that the surface treatment of Eco-flex sponge by CTAB plays a key role in stable and strong adhesion between the MXene nanosheets and sponge. To determine the optimal concentration of CTAB solution, we analyzed the coating condition and electrical properties as a function of concentration of CTAB. The resistance gradually decreased as the concentration increased from 2 to 4 mg/ml; however, it increased significantly thereafter (Fig. S5). This is because excessive CTAB concentrations (above 4 mg/ml) led to agglomeration of CTAB and MXene nanosheets into clusters on the sponge surface, resulting in non-uniform and non-continuous coating conditions (Fig. S6).

Schematic diagrams for the fabrication procedures of MXene@Eco-flex(0030) based pressure sensor.

a Synthesis of Ti3C2Tx MXene. b Production of Eco-flex (0030) in a porous sponge form. c Dip-coating of MXene nanosheets on the surface of porous sponge. d Schematic of the device configuration. e Image of pressure sensor

The SEM image in the red inset shows a porous sponge surface with a smooth appearance. The Eco-flex surface lacks hydrophilic functional groups, making it essential to functionalize the surface of the porous sponge. Therefore, CTAB, which has amphiphilic characteristics, was used as the cationic surfactant. Its hydrophobic tail adhered to the surface of Eco-flex through hydrophobic interactions, rendering the surface of the porous sponge positively charged. The hydrophilic head of CTAB, with its positive charge, allowed MXene to bind well to the Eco-flex sponge through electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged groups (–OH, –O, and –F) of MXene. As observed in the SEM image in the blue inset, the increased surface roughness compared to that of the pristine porous sponge indicates that the MXene nanosheets adhered robustly to the surface-functionalized Eco-flex using CTAB solution30, producing a stable conductive network between the MXene nanosheets. As illustrated in Fig. 1d, e, the surface-engineered porous MXene@Eco-flex pressure sensor was composed of five layers: top PI passivation and electrodes, sponge, and bottom PI passivation and electrodes. The fabrication process of the pressure sensor involved attaching a copper foil, cut to the same size as the sponge, onto a PI passivation layer larger than the sensor dimensions for sealing, to serve as an electrode.

The XRD patterns in Fig. 2a show a significant reduction in the prominent peak at 38.2° on the (104) plane after etching with HCl/LiF, indicating that the Al layers were effectively etched, thus confirming the successful synthesis of Ti3C2Tx MXene31. To confirm that MXene was successfully synthesized, we conducted Raman spectroscopy analysis (Fig. 2b). The low-frequency region (100–800 cm−1), which represents lattice vibrations (phonons), is observed in the Raman spectrum of MXene. The modes at 197 and 724 cm−1 correspond to the out-of-plane vibrations of Ti and C, respectively. Meanwhile, the modes at 278, 394, and 567 cm−1 are attributed to the in-plane (shear) vibrations of Ti, C, and surface termination atoms26,32–34. Figure 2c shows the hysteresis characteristics of the MXene@Eco-flex pressure sensor at compression levels of 30%, 50%, and 80%. The mechanical stiffness of the MXene-coated sponge was 1.25 times higher than that of the uncoated pristine sponge at 80% compression. The Young’s modulus of the MXene-coated state was 66.6 kPa under 80% compression, while the uncoated state exhibited a value of 52.6 kPa. This indicates that the mechanical properties of MXene contribute to an increase in the stiffness of the coated sponge. Although the stiffness of the sponge was increased by the mechanical properties of MXene, the overall compressive range remained constant. Figure 2d shows the hysteresis observed under 50% and 80% compressions. Hysteresis refers to the phenomenon in which a sponge, after being deformed in response to an external force, does not fully or immediately return to its original state once the force is removed. In the pristine sponge state, the losses were 15% and 18% under 50% and 80% compressions, respectively. In contrast, the MXene-coated sponge exhibited reduced hysteresis losses of 9.9% and 14.1% at the same compression levels. This lower hysteresis at high compression states can be attributed to the reversible behavior of the porous Eco-flex sponge, which enables the sensor to recover its original state after deformation.

a Comparison of XRD patterns between MXene and MAX. b Raman spectra of the synthesized MXene in the low frequency region (100–800 cm−1). c Stress–strain curves of the MXene-coated sponge and pristine Eco-flex sponge. d Comparison of hysteresis between the MXene-coated sponge and the pristine sponge

The working mechanism of the pressure sensor with the MXene-coated sponge is illustrated in Fig. 3. When pressure was applied to the sponge, the internal pores compressed and deformed into elliptical shapes. This deformation increased the number of contact points between the MXene nanosheets coated on the sponge surface, thereby introducing numerous new current paths. In the region between the initial and low pressures, the contact points between the MXene nanosheets increased as the sponge was compressed, leading to an increase in current flow through the sensor, which detects the pressure. In the low-pressure range, the primary behavior involves the compression of relatively large pores into an elliptical shape and eventually making contact, resulting in a significant current change depending on the applied pressure. In the high-pressure region, while the current paths due to contact in large pores were maintained, smaller pores compressed, creating additional contact points. As shown in the SEM images, when external pressure was applied, the pores gradually compressed into an elliptical shape, providing improved current paths owing to the increased number of contact points. Eventually, under high pressure, significantly more contact points formed compared to those in the low-pressure state. Using ImageJ, the porosity was measured to be 35%, 28%, and 21% of those in the initial state, intermediate pressure, and high pressure, respectively.

Schematic illustration of the sensing mechanism of the MXene@Eco-flex based pressure sensor

Figure 4a shows the current–voltage (I–V) curves of the MXene@Eco-flex pressure sensor at various pressures ranging from 0 to 208 kPa. The developed sensor shows linear characteristics when pressure is applied, indicating ohmic contact between the copper electrodes and MXene-coated sponge. The increase in the slope with pressure indicates that, as the pores within the sponge are compressed, the contact area between the MXene nanosheets increases, allowing more current to flow. The inherently low stiffness of the Eco-flex combined with the sensitive deformation by the pores, induced significant current changes, even under small pressures35. The sensitivity of the sensor was determined by the difference in the relative current corresponding to the changes in the pressure (Fig. 4b). Sensitivity (S) was defined as S = (ΔI/I₀)/P, where I represents the current flowing between the copper electrodes, I₀ is the current measured at initial state (without any applied pressure), and P is the pressure applied to the sensor36. The relative current responses at different pressures were categorized into three distinct regions. The surface-engineered porous MXene@Eco-flex pressure sensor exhibited sensitivities of 353.01, 772.68, and 192.86 kPa−1 for pressures of 0–35, 35–110, and 110–208 kPa, respectively. In addition, three pressure sensors were fabricated under identical conditions, and sensitivity tests were conducted accordingly. The sensors exhibited the largest error of −5.2% at 200 kPa, demonstrating sufficient reproducibility. As explained in the sensing mechanism, the intermediate-pressure range showed greater sensitivity than the low- and high-pressure ranges because it is the primary deformation zone where large pore deformations occur. This highly sensitive response under a wide pressure range of 0–208 kPa was attributed to the stable and robust adhesion between the MXene nanosheets and sponge, achieved through surface functionalization with CTAB, as well as the high deformability resulting from the inherent low stiffness of Eco-flex and porous structure of the sponge. Additionally, the R² values in the first, second, and third pressure regions were 0.96, 0.99, and 0.88, respectively, demonstrating that the current changed linearly with variations in pressure. Consequently, the surface-engineered porous MXene@Eco-flex pressure sensor demonstrated both linear and excellent sensitivity performance, while exhibiting the capability to detect a wide range of pressures up to 208 kPa (Fig. 4c)37–41. To the best of our knowledge, the developed sensor can measure pressure over a wide range with the highest sensitivity, comparable to that of previously reported pressure sensors incorporating porous structures utilizing MXene. The dynamic response of the sensor to repetitive loading and unloading cycles was demonstrated under various pressures, with tests conducted over five cycles at each pressure level in Fig. 4d, e. In the low-pressure range, the porous sponge-based sensor showed relative current changes of 5000, 7000, 11,000, and 14,000 for pressures of 8, 16, 24, and 32 kPa, respectively. In the high-pressure range, the sensor exhibited relative current changes of up to 42,000, 62,000, 78,000, and 89,000 at pressures of 64, 96, 128, and 160 kPa, respectively. Consequently, it was confirmed that the sensor exhibited a consistent and repeatable output response under identical conditions.

a The I–V curves of the MXene@Eco-flex pressure sensor measured at various pressure levels. b The current response to the pressure ranging from 0 to 208 kPa. c Performance comparison of our sensors and recently developed other pressure sensors based on porous structure utilizing MXene. d, e The dynamic response of the pressure sensor with various pressures. f The relative current response under different compression rates at a pressure of 1.2 kPa. g The response and recovery times of the pressure sensor at 0.1 kPa pressure. h Response to the limit of detection (LOD) of the pressure sensor. i Long-term response of the sensor to repeated application of 0.2 kPa for 2000 cycles of loading and unloading

Figure 4f depicts the variation in current of the MXene@Eco-flex sensor under a pressure of 1.2 kPa at different pressing speeds. The tests included dynamic response experiments with five cycles for each pressing speed to demonstrate repeatability. As the compression speed increased, the wavelength of the output signal narrowed. However, when the applied pressure was the same, a consistent response characteristic with a significantly small deviation of 0.52% was observed, regardless of the pressing speed. This constant repetitive response under rapid pressing speeds ensures a stable performance in high-frequency pressure applications. Transient characteristics of the porous-sponge-based pressure sensor were evaluated using loading and unloading tests at a pressure of 0.1 kPa (Fig. 4g). The developed sensor exhibited rapid response and recovery times of 150 and 100 ms, respectively. These sensing performances were attributed to the high deformability due to the synergistic effects of the porous structure and low stiffness of Eco-flex. In addition, we observed the detection limit and resolution of the MXene@Eco-flex pressure sensor by applying 8 Pa of pressure every 5 s using a UTM. Figure 4h presents a graph showing a distinct stepwise change with each incremental addition of 8 Pa, starting at 0 Pa. The long-term durability and reliability of the MXene@Eco-flex pressure sensor were examined, as shown in the real-time response results of repetitive cycle tests under a pressure of 0.2 kPa (Fig. 4i). The developed sensor exhibited consistent sensing signals with an error rate of 10.38% over 2000 cycles. This consistent performance was attributed to the strong interfacial bonding achieved via the special surface functionalization using CTAB, which facilitated the robust surface treatment of the sponge when coated with the MXene nanosheets. To prevent the unintended oxidation of Ti3C2Tx MXene upon exposure to air, we encapsulated a pressure sensor employing PI passivation, thereby minimizing its exposure to the external environment. This sealing technique, in addition to the robust adhesion between the MXene nanosheets and Eco-flex, ensures that the developed pressure sensor exhibits excellent durability and reliability without any drift in the response performance, which is critical for actively applying pressure sensors in human monitoring applications.

We conducted various experiments to demonstrate the superior performance of our MXene@Eco-flex pressure sensor in human motion monitoring applications. To monitor various movements of the human body and obtain signals, a sensor using an MXene nanosheet-coated porous sponge was attached to various parts of the human body. In addition, the fabricated sensor was sealed using polyimide tape and Tegaderm. Hence, it could be fixed to the skin without any interference from bodily factors. As shown in Fig. 5a, we measured the change in current as a finger was flexed at angles of 30°, 60°, and 90°. The results showed that the difference in the bending of the joint was accurately distinguished without delay or drift. The change in the current flowing through the sensor with respect to the degree of elbow flexion is shown in Fig. 5b. At flexion angles of 30°, 60°, and 90°, the response of the sensor increased linearly with the bending angle. Although the finger and arm were at the same angle, the movement of the arm caused a considerable change in the relative current owing to the larger change in surface area. Our MXene@Eco-flex sensor could clearly distinguish a 30° difference in the bending angles at both the elbow and finger joints. Figure 5c shows the feasibility of detecting consecutive activities by touching the sensor with a finger. It also shows an accurate response for more than 10 repetitions, demonstrating stable and reliable sensing. As shown in Fig. 5d, the sensor attached to a mask measured tiny tremors caused by human breathing. Even the slightest breeze from the nose caused a small mechanical deformation in the sensor that could be detected. The movements of the forearm muscles were observed during the motion of clenching a fist (Fig. 5e). The force exerted by the fist caused the forearm muscles to increase slightly, and the sensor accurately detected this movement. Movements of the throat muscles and uvula were monitored while swallowing water. As depicted in Fig. 5f, when drinking water, the throat moved down and then up, causing two quick movements in one cycle that our sensor accurately recognized. The MXene nanosheet-coated porous sponge-based sensor could clearly detect five representative human motions, demonstrating reliable and stable monitoring through multiple repeated experiments.

Figure 6 shows the pressure distribution results of a foot pressure sensor in response to walking motion. The experiment was conducted by attaching an array of nine MXene@Eco-flex pressure sensors to the insole and integrating them with a signal-processing module consisting of a printed circuit board (PCB) and microcontroller unit (Arduino Mega 2560) using an Arduino integrated development environment (IDE) (Fig. S7). A detailed circuit diagram and coding information are provided in Supplementary Information (Fig. S8). The sensor array was optimally designed based on the most widely adopted segmentation scheme of plantar pressure42–44. According to previous studies, plantar pressure during walking is mainly concentrated at the forefoot, midfoot, and heel regions. Based on this, sensors were primarily allocated in each of three locations corresponding to peak pressure points.

Pressure distribution results according to walking motion, measured using 9 sensors attached to the insole and connected to the PCB and Arduino module.

Load distribution on the front (a), entire surface (b), and heel (c) of the foot

The output values obtained from each sensor attached to the insole were monitored in real-time using a serial monitor. In Fig. 6a, which depicts forward movement, the pressure is mainly concentrated at the front of the foot and is distributed across a total of six pressure sensors. Among these, the sensor at the front exhibits a high value, whereas the pressure distribution gradually decreases toward the middle of the foot. As shown in Fig. 6b, when the foot is parallel to the ground, the pressure is evenly distributed across all sensors, reflecting the weight distribution according to the shape of the foot. Figure 6c shows the pressure distribution when the heel first touches the ground. Owing to the confined space, all three sensors in this region exhibited high values.

We developed the walking-assistive device using a pressure sensor, linear actuator, exoskeleton, and Arduino. The device was designed as a wearable system to support walking for users with reduced muscle strength or motor impairments. As shown in Video S1, when the user slightly bends their knee with minimal force, the sensor underwent bending, causing an increase in current. The Arduino detected this increasing current trend and sent a command to the linear actuator, which then folded and assisted in bending the knee. To evaluate the system performance of the walking-assistive device, system responsiveness, control accuracy, and real-time adaptability were examined under actual operating conditions, respectively (Fig. 7). The responsiveness of the system was assessed by simultaneously acquiring signals from both the sensor and the actuator. We included in the code the timestamps for when the pressure sensor data is received by the Arduino and when the actuator command is issued, allowing these events to be monitored in the serial monitor. Real-time adaptability was evaluated under five representative conditions: rapid pressing, rapid release, slow pressing, slow release, and steady state holding (Fig. 7a). In the rapid compression case (case 1), the actuator initiated folding motion within 420 ms. The actuator promptly extended following the sudden removal of pressure (case 2). Under slow pressing and release conditions (cases 3 and 4), the actuator responded appropriately within the designated time window. In the steady-state holding condition (case 5), the system maintained a stable standby state without unintended triggering, demonstrating its robustness against external noise. As shown in Fig. 7b, the MXene sponge pressure sensor began to detect compression, and a control command to drive the actuator was sent by the Arduino after ~181 ± 1 ms. Additionally, to accurately determine the actuator’s operation time, we used an oscilloscope to monitor the current change and timing of the linear actuator. As a result, it was confirmed that the actuator began to operate ~234 ± 5 ms after the command was issued by the Arduino. To investigate control accuracy, the actuator motion was classified into extension and folding modes, and the joint angles for each mode were measured using a digital protractor (Fig. S9). The average angles measured across 10 repeated trials were found to be 40.05 ± 2.9° in the extension mode and 83.1 ± 2.5° in the folding mode. Additionally, the saturated operating current of the actuator was calculated to be a mean value of 23 mA and a standard deviation of ±0.41%, indicating consistent electrical performance.

Conclusion

In summary, we successfully fabricated a highly sensitive pressure sensor with a wide detection range based on MXene@Eco-flex by creating a porous sponge structure using a sugar template and coating it with synthesized MXene nanosheets. The stable and robust adhesion between MXene nanosheets and Eco-flex functionalized by CTAB, combined with the high deformability and inherent low stiffness of Eco-flex’s porous structure, enabled an ultra-high sensitivity of 772.68 kPa−1 within the pressure range of 35–110 kPa. The sensor also exhibited excellent linearity and repeatability over a wide range of 0–208 kPa. Additionally, owing to the high deformability of the MXene nanosheet-coated porous Eco-flex, our sensor could recover its original state even after experiencing a high compression of 208 kPa. The performance of this sensor suggests its suitability for human motion monitoring and accurate detection of finger bending, touch, breathing, and neck movements. Changes in the foot pressure distribution during walking were observed by using a foot pressure detection sensor array integrated with a signal processing module, confirming its potential for applications such as wearable human monitoring. As a result of successfully developing the ultra-sensitive sensor, we created the exoskeleton by integrating it with the pressure sensor, demonstrating a walking assistive device for human-machine interaction.
