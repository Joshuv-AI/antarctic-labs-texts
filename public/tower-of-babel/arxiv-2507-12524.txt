Comments on Class S(YK)
Micha Berkooz, Trivko Kukolj, and Josef Seitz

Abstract
We present a DSSYK-like interpretation of the Schur half-indices of 𝒩 = 2 \mathcal{N}=2 S ​ U ​ ( 2 ) SU(2) gauge theories with matter, in the presence of fundamental Wilson lines. The Schur half-indices of these theories can be understood as transition amplitudes in a non-vacuum sector of ordinary DSSYK. In the language of chord diagrams, the half-indices are obtained by summing over diagrams with special segments, which correspond to coherent states of the q q -oscillator algebra. In addition, we show that the Schur half-index of S ​ U ​ ( 2 ) SU(2) gauge theory with n F = 4 n_{F}=4 fundamental half-hypermultiplets corresponds to the partition function of a particle on the quantum disk.

2.1
The double-scaled SYK model

The SYK model

;
Rosenhaus:2018dtp
is a quantum mechanical model of
N

H=i^{\frac{p}{2}}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<...<i_{p}\leq N}J_{i_{1}...i_{p}}\psi_{i_{1}}...\psi_{i_{p}}.

(1)

The couplings

are independent Gaussian random variables, satisfying:

\langle J_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{p}}\rangle_{J}=0,\qquad\quad\langle J_{i_{1}...i_{p}}J_{j_{1}...j_{p}}\rangle_{J}=\frac{\mathbb{J}^{2}}{\lambda}\binom{N}{p}^{-1}\delta^{i_{1}}_{j_{1}}...\delta^{i_{p}}_{j_{p}},\text{ with }\lambda=\frac{2p^{2}}{N}.

(2)

Here,

denotes the ensemble average. We will normalize

and

, which corresponds to setting
\langle Tr\;H^{2}\rangle_{J}=1
.

This model has been studied extensively in the double-scaled limit (

{\text{with $k$ chords}}\end{subarray}}q^{2\cdot\#\text{intersections}},\qquad\text{ where }q^{2}=e^{-\lambda}.

of the Fermi surface.

3
DSSYK interpretation of Schur half-indices

As observed in
Gaiotto:2024kze
, the Schur half-index of pure
\mathcal{N}=2

SU(2)
gauge theory matches the partition function of DSSYK at infinite temperature, with insertions of fundamental Wilson lines corresponding to DSSYK Hamiltonian moments. This is just the equality of (
9
) and (
17
) for
n_{F}=0
. It is then natural to ask what interpretation in terms of standard chord rules interpretation we can give to
SU(2)
theories with matter. By
standard chord rules
we mean that we propagate the system with the ordinary DSSYK transfer matrix.

The reason to think that this is possible is the following. Recall that matter contributes bosonic particles to the index and the screening picture mentioned above suggests that they can participate in screening/dressing.
In this case, there is no exclusion principle at work, which suggests that the bosons can dress each Kondo impurity independently. Loosely speaking, we expect to have the dynamics of standard chord rules (which corresponds to the impurity and Fermi surface screening) dressed with a diagonal term in the transfer matrix which has to do with the dressing of each impurity by the bosons.

We would like to make this more precise. Even irrespective of the screening/dressing arguments, it will give us of a way of understanding the matter Schur half-indices within the DSSYK framework. The main point is that we can work in two different pictures:

•

In the segment picture, we write the expression for the Schur half-index as an initial/final state in DSSYK, and use the standard DSSYK transfer matrix. Loosely, the DSSYK evolution takes care of the dressing by the Fermi sea and the initial/final states take care of the impurity dressing by the bosons.

•

In the Askey-Wilson picture, we use a more complicated transfer matrix obtained from the recursion relation of orthogonal polynomials in the q-Askey scheme. The orthogonality measure of these polynomials is precisely the density of the Schur half-index with matter. One can relate the transfer matrices of the two pictures to another.

The Askey-Wilson picture

5.3.2
The quantum disk

We now give a very brief review of quantum group actions and the quantum disk as a representation of a quantum group. For details, we refer to Appendix

Consider first the ordinary hyperbolic disk, given by the metric:

It has an action of

(67)

The generators fulfill a reality condition

. The Lie algebra consisting of

gives rise to a universal enveloping algebra
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{su}_{1,1})

To make the disk noncommutative, a convenient choice turns out to be:

(68)

where

is a number between zero and one. We want to find a corresponding deformation of the universal enveloping algebra
\mathcal{U}_{q}(\mathfrak{su}_{1,1})

\begin{split}&K(z)=q^{2}z,\quad E(z)=-q^{\frac{5}{2}}z^{2},\quad F(z)=q^{-\frac{3}{2}}\\

(69)

Consistent with this action are the relations:

KE=q^{2}EK,\quad KF=q^{-2}FK,\quad EF-FE=\frac{K-K^{-1}}{q-q^{-1}},\quad KK^{-1}=K^{-1}K=1.

(70)

We still have to specify how these generators act on tensor products. This is done by the co-product:

\Delta(E)=E\otimes 1+K\otimes E,\quad\Delta(F)=F\otimes K^{-1}+1\otimes F,\quad\Delta(K)=K\otimes K.

(71)

(72)

(73)

which commutes with

Finally, we consider

The segment picture.

The matter contribution to the index can be rewritten as a bilinear sum of q-Hermite polynomials, by using the identity (

\frac{1}{(q;q^{2})_{\infty}(qe^{\pm 2i\theta};q^{2})_{\infty}}=\frac{(q;q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}H_{p}(x|q^{2})H_{p}(x|q^{2})\frac{q^{p}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{p}}.

7
Outlook

The SYK model is usually associated with the study of quantum chaos. As such it has little to do with the BPS sector of
{\cal N}=2
theories, which is as ordered a problem as it gets. Understanding why the two are related is an interesting question. A recent idea in that general direction is the notion of BPS chaos
Lin:2022rzw
;
Lin:2022zxd
;
Chen:2024oqv
when the coupling is taken to be finite. Some other issues worth exploring are the following:

•

Another promising place to look for a connection to chaos is in the near-BPS sector. The same non-commutative algebra also appears in the discussion of stationary solutions of ASEPs which are related to classical probabilistic processes
Derrida:1992fi
;
Sasamoto_2000
;
Golinelli_2006
. If we denote a space of configurations in an ASEP by
x
, and
P(x)
is the probability measure on that space, then the ASEP defines the function
F
in the equation
{\dot{P}}(x)=F(x)
. Stationarity then implies
{\dot{P}}(x)=F(x)=0
. If we think about
F
as a Hamiltonian and
P(x)
as a wave function, then the relation
F(x)=0
is not that different from a BPS condition. To see whether there is such a relation one will need to go to the near BPS sector, which might be doable in this case.

•

With the methods of
Lewis:2025qjq
, one can attempt to write down a chord diagram description of the Schur half-index for
SU(N)
gauge theories. For example, using the generating function for
q
-Whittaker polynomials, one can show that the
SU(N)

\mathcal{N}=4
Schur half-index is a sum over chord diagrams, analogous to the
SU(2)
case discussed in Section
6.3
. Similarly, one can speculate about more complicated representation theory structures. For example, from the Feynman rule description for
n
-point functions in DSSYK
Berkooz:2018jqr
, one can read off that an
SU(2)
bifundamental hypermultiplet corresponds to a matter chord of dimension
\Delta=\frac{1}{2}
. It would be interesting to see whether the Schur half-indices of all class
\mathcal{S}
theories can be formulated as
n
-point functions of matter operators in some (generalized) DSSYK system, and what that might teach us about
\mathcal{N}=2
gauge theories.

•

For
n_{F}=4
, we have seen that we have to supplement our chord diagrams with two reservoir regions. In terms of the chord Hilbert space, we computed an amplitude between two coherent states whose occupation number scales as
n\sim\frac{1}{\lambda}
. This is exactly the limit in which the fake disk algebra of
Lin:2023trc
arises. Insertions of matter operators (in the DSSYK sense) cannot happen in the reservoir region, and in that sense, they resemble insertions in the “physical” region in the fake disk picture. One can therefore speculate whether the two “fake” segments in the fake disk are the reservoir segments. In the semiclassical limit, one can compute the renormalized length of the reservoir segments and finds them to be zero, which is in agreement with the fake disk picture. On the other hand, since the
n_{F}=4
case is related to the quantum disk, one can speculate about a connection between the quantum disk and the fake disk.

The group structure directly is not deformable in such a way, but we can deform two related structures: the algebra of functions on the group, and the universal enveloping algebra of the Lie algebra. The deformations of both of these structures are dual to each other, as they are both Hopf algebras.

Algebra of functions on a group

It is the viewpoint of algebraic geometry that one can study an object by studying properties of the algebra of functions on the object. In view of this idea, we can define a
quantum group
by
q
-deforming the algebra of functions on the group, and working with that algebra instead of the (non-existing) underlying object. Ordinary functions on a group form a so-called Hopf algebra, as we will review now.

Consider the example of
SU(1,1)\cong SL(2,\mathbb{R})
. We can represent it as
2\times 2

SU(1,1)\cong SL(2,\mathbb{R})=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\Bigg|ad-bc=1\right\}.

(112)

are therefore like coordinates on the group manifold. Any (complex) function on a group can be approximated to arbitrary accuracy by a polynomial in the letters
a,b,c,d
, where we identify the polynomials which can be mapped to each other by commutation of the letters and the condition
ad-bc=1
. The algebra of functions on
SU(1,1)
is given by:

\mathcal{O}(SU(1,1))=\mathbb{C}[a,b,c,d]/\sim,

(113)

where
\sim

ab=ba,...,ad=da,ad-bc=1
.
\mathcal{O}(SU(1,1))
is clearly a commutative algebra as we can add and multiply functions together. There is also a neutral element: the function that is one everywhere. We can think of multiplication as a map:

\begin{split}\cdot:&\mathcal{O}(SU(1,1))\otimes\mathcal{O}(SU(1,1))\rightarrow\mathcal{O}(SU(1,1))\\
&(f_{1}\cdot f_{2})(g)=f_{1}(g)f_{2}(g).\end{split}

(114)

Sometimes it is useful to denote the product as
m

(i.e.
m(f_{1},f_{2})=f_{1}\cdot f_{2}
).
We will often drop both the
\cdot

The group structure of

allows us to establish a Hopf algebra structure. We define

16

16

Here we have assumed that

\begin{split}&\eta:\mathcal{O}(SU(1,1))\rightarrow\mathbb{C},\quad\eta(f)=f(e)\\

(115)

\begin{split}&(\Delta\otimes id)\Delta=(id\otimes\Delta)\Delta\quad\text{(co-associativity)}\\

Universal enveloping algebra

Instead of functions on the group itself, we can also consider a dual picture, given by some (linear) action on the algebra of functions. One such action is given by directional derivatives of vector fields acting on the functions. Since such an action should also encode the group structure in some way. As Lie algebra elements are in one to one correspondence with left-invariant vector fields, we can think of the

as acting on the algebra of functions. We define
18
18

18

\sim

\begin{split}&\Delta(H)=1\otimes H+H\otimes 1\\
&\Delta(E)=1\otimes E+E\otimes 1\\
&\Delta(F)=1\otimes F+F\otimes 1\\
&S(H)=-H,\;S(E)=-E,\;S(F)=-F\\
&\eta(H)=\eta(E)=\eta(F)=0.\end{split}

(123)

(124)

\begin{split}&\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{su}_{1,1})\otimes\mathcal{O}(SU(1,1))\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\\
&\langle H,f\rangle\equiv(Hf)(e).\end{split}

(125)

One can show

\text{classical group}\sim\text{universal enveloping algebra}.

(126)

We can

-deform the universal enveloping algebra. This can be done in such a way that there still exists a pairing, i.e. they continue to be dual to each other.

The main modification is that instead of having only algebra-like elements in the generating set of the algebra, we have also group-like elements that are invertible. Concretely, we define the

\begin{split}&\Delta(K)=K\otimes K\\
&\Delta(E)=E\otimes 1+K\otimes E\\
&\Delta(F)=F\otimes K^{-1}+1\otimes F\\
&\eta(K)=1,\;\eta(F)=\eta(E)=0\\
&S(K)=K^{-1},\;S(K^{-1})=K,\;S(E)=-K^{-1}E,\;S(F)=-FK.\end{split}

(129)

We can understand the co-product for

Finally, the reality condition is the involution:

(K^{\pm 1})^{*}=K^{\pm 1},\quad E^{*}=-KF,\quad F^{*}=-EK^{-1}.

(130)

One can find a Casimir operator that commutes with

(131)

&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{3}(t_{i}e^{\pm i\theta_{1}};q^{2})_{\infty}}{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}(t_{i}t_{j};q^{2})_{\infty}(q^{2},e^{\pm 2i\theta_{1}};q^{2})_{\infty}}2\pi\delta(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}).\end{aligned}

(163)

The
n
-orthogonality relation is readily available in the literature
Koekoek2010
;
Ismail2005
;
Gasper2004
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\frac{(e^{\pm 2i\theta};q^{2})_{\infty}}{\prod_{i=1}^{3}(t_{i}e^{\pm i\theta};q^{2})_{\infty}}S_{n}(\cos\theta;t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}|q^{2})S_{m}(\cos\theta;t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}|q^{2})\;=\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\frac{2\pi(q^{2};q^{2})_{n}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}(t_{i}t_{j};q^{2})_{n}}{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}(t_{i}t_{j};q^{2})_{\infty}(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}\delta_{n,m}.\end{aligned}

(164)

Therefore, the two orthogonality relations for the rescaled continuous dual q-Hahn polynomials
\chi_{n}(\cos\theta;t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}|q^{2})
can be written as:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\;\chi_{n}(\cos\theta;t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}|q^{2})\chi_{m}(\cos\theta;t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}|q^{2})=\delta_{n,m},

(165)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\chi_{n}(\cos\theta_{1};t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}|q^{2})\chi_{n}(\cos\theta_{2};t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}|q^{2})=\delta(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}).

(166)

The functions
\chi_{n}(\cos\theta;t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}|q^{2})
obey the following recursion relation:

\displaystyle\frac{2\cos\theta}{\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}\chi_{n}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{[n+1]_{q^{2}}}\sqrt{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}(1-t_{i}t_{j}q^{2n})}\chi_{n+1}

(167)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{[n]_{q^{2}}}\sqrt{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}(1-t_{i}t_{j}q^{2n-2})}\chi_{n-1}

\displaystyle+\bigg(\frac{t_{1}+t_{2}+t_{3}}{\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}+\frac{t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}}{\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}(q^{2n-2}-q^{4n}-q^{4n-2})\bigg)\chi_{n}.

B.5
Askey-Wilson polynomials (
n_{F}=8
).

Askey-Wilson polynomials are generally defined for
|t_{i}|\leq 1
, but for our purposes we restrict this quartet to complex conjugate pairs
(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4})=(t_{1},t_{1}^{*},t_{3},t_{3}^{*})
. This family of polynomials is defined using the hypergeometric
{}_{4}\phi_{3}
series:

\displaystyle P_{n}(\cos\theta;t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}|q^{2})=\frac{(t_{1}t_{2},t_{1}t_{3},t_{1}t_{4};q^{2})_{n}}{t_{1}^{n}}\,_{4}\phi_{3}\bigg({q^{-2n},t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{2n-2},t_{1}e^{\pm i\theta}\atop t_{1}t_{2},t_{1}t_{3},t_{1}t_{4}};q^{2},q^{2}\bigg)

(168)

We define a set of rescaled Askey-Wilson polynomials
\pi_{n}(\cos\theta;t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}|q^{2})
as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\pi_{n}(\cos\theta;t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}|q^{2})\equiv\;\braket{n|\theta}_{n_{F}=8}=\\[5.0pt]
&\sqrt{\frac{(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{4n-2})(t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{-2};q^{2})_{n}}{(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{-2})\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 4}(t_{i}t_{j};q^{2})_{n}}}\\
&\times\sqrt{\frac{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 4}(t_{i}t_{j};q^{2})_{\infty}(q^{2},e^{\pm 2i\theta};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4};q^{2})_{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{4}(t_{i}e^{\pm i\theta};q^{2})_{\infty}}}\frac{P_{n}(\cos\theta;t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}|q^{2})}{\sqrt{2\pi(q^{2};q^{2})_{n}}}.\end{aligned}

(169)

As we now demonstrate,
\pi_{n}
satisfy
\theta
- and
n
-orthogonality conditions with unit measure.

The
\theta
-orthogonality relation can be derived starting from the asymmetric Kernel for Askey-Wilson polynomials:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{4n-2})}{(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{-2})}\frac{(t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{-2};q^{2})_{n}\tau_{1}^{n}t_{1}^{-n}t^{n}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{n}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 4}(t_{i}t_{j};q^{2})_{n}}P_{n}(\cos\theta_{1};t_{i}|q^{2})P_{n}(\cos\theta_{2};\tau_{i}|q^{2})\\[5.0pt]
&=K_{t}^{t_{i},\tau_{i}}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}|q^{2}).\end{aligned}

(170)

The Kernel
K_{t}^{t_{i},\tau_{i}}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}|q^{2})
is expressed in terms of multiple very-well-poised
{}_{8}W_{7}
hypergeometric series
Askey:1996a
. However, in the
t\to 1^{-}
limit, the expression simplifies to:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{4n-2})}{(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{-2})}\frac{(t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{-2};q^{2})_{n}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{n}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 4}(t_{i}t_{j};q^{2})_{n}}P_{n}(\cos\theta_{1};t_{i}|q^{2})P_{n}(\cos\theta_{2};\tau_{i}|q^{2})\;=\\[5.0pt]
&\frac{(t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(\tau_{1}\tau_{2},t_{1}t_{3},t_{1}t_{4},t_{2}t_{3},t_{2}t_{4},t_{3}t_{4};q^{2})_{\infty}}\frac{(\tau_{1}e^{\pm i\theta_{1}},\tau_{2}e^{\pm i\theta_{1}},\tau_{3}e^{\pm i\theta_{2}},\tau_{4}e^{\pm i\theta_{2}},(\tfrac{\tau_{2}}{t_{2}})^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(\tfrac{\tau_{2}}{t_{2}}e^{\pm i\theta_{1}\pm i\theta_{2}};q^{2})_{\infty}}.\end{aligned}

(171)

One can now take the
t_{i}/\tau_{i}\to 1
limit. Using (
162
), we find that the kernel localizes to a
\delta
-function:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{4n-2})}{(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{-2})}\frac{(t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{-2};q^{2})_{n}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{n}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 4}(t_{i}t_{j};q^{2})_{n}}P_{n}(\cos\theta_{1};t_{i}|q^{2})P_{n}(\cos\theta_{2};t_{i}|q^{2})\;=\\[5.0pt]
&\frac{(t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4};q^{2})_{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{4}(t_{i}e^{\pm i\theta_{1}};q^{2})_{\infty}}{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 4}(t_{i}t_{j};q^{2})_{\infty}(q^{2},e^{\pm 2i\theta_{1}};q^{2})_{\infty}}2\pi\delta(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}).\end{aligned}

(172)

The
n
-orthogonality relation is known in literature
Askey:1996a
;
Koekoek2010
;
Ismail2005
and for our choice of parameters
t_{i}
reduces to:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\frac{(e^{\pm 2i\theta};q^{2})_{\infty}}{\prod_{i=1}^{4}(t_{i}e^{\pm i\theta};q^{2})_{\infty}}\frac{1}{(t_{1}t_{2},t_{1}t_{3},t_{1}t_{4};q^{2})_{m}}P_{n}(\cos\theta;t_{i}|q^{2})P_{m}(\cos\theta;t_{i}|q^{2})\;=\\
&\frac{(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{-2})(q^{2},t_{3}t_{4},t_{2}t_{4},t_{2}t_{3};q^{2})_{n}}{(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{4n-2})(t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{-2};q^{2})_{n}}\frac{2\pi(t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4};q^{2})_{\infty}}{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 4}(t_{i}t_{j};q^{2})_{\infty}(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}\delta_{n,m}.\end{aligned}

(173)

Using (
169
), one can now show that the orthogonality relations (
173
), (
172
) become:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\;\pi_{n}(\cos\theta;t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}|q^{2})\pi_{m}(\cos\theta;t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}|q^{2})=\delta_{n,m},

(174)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\pi_{n}(\cos\theta_{1};t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}|q^{2})\pi_{n}(\cos\theta_{2};t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}|q^{2})=\delta(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}).

(175)

\pi_{n}(\cos\theta;t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}|q^{2})
satisfy a tedious three-term recursion relation
Koekoek2010
. We write it out, keeping the
t_{1,2,3,4}
dependence explicit:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{2\cos\theta}{\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}\pi_{n}=\\
&=\bigg(\sqrt{[n+1]_{q^{2}}}\frac{1}{(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{4n})}\sqrt{\frac{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 4}(1-t_{i}t_{j}q^{2n})(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{2n-2})}{(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{4n-2})(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{4n+2})}}\bigg)\pi_{n+1}\;\\[15.0pt]
&+\bigg(\sqrt{[n]_{q^{2}}}\frac{1}{(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{4n-4})}\sqrt{\frac{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 4}(1-t_{i}t_{j}q^{2n-2})(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{2n-4})}{(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{4n-6})(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{4n-2})}}\bigg)\pi_{n-1}\\[15.0pt]
&+\bigg(t_{1}+t_{1}^{-1}-\frac{(1-t_{1}t_{2}q^{2n})(1-t_{1}t_{3}q^{2n})(1-t_{1}t_{4}q^{2n})(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{2n-2})}{t_{1}(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{4n-2})(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{4n})}\\
&\qquad\qquad-\frac{t_{1}(1-q^{2n})(1-t_{2}t_{3}q^{2n-2})(1-t_{2}t_{4}q^{2n-2})(1-t_{3}t_{4}q^{2n-2})}{(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{4n-4})(1-t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}t_{4}q^{4n-2})}\bigg)\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}\pi_{n}.\end{aligned}

(176)

B.6
Continuous q-ultraspherical polynomials (adjoint,
\mathcal{N}=2^{*}
).

Also referred to as Rogers polynomials, this family of polynomials is obtained by setting
(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4})=(\sqrt{\beta},\sqrt{\beta}q,-\sqrt{\beta},-\sqrt{\beta}q)
. One can define them via the
{}_{4}\phi_{3}
hypergeometric series:

\displaystyle C_{n}(\cos\theta;\beta|q^{2})=\frac{(\beta^{2};q^{2})_{n}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{n}}\beta^{-\frac{n}{2}}\,_{4}\phi_{3}\bigg({q^{-2n},\beta^{2}q^{2n},\beta^{1/2}e^{\pm i\theta}\atop\beta q,-\beta,-\beta q};q^{2},q^{2}\bigg).

(177)

The continuous q-ultraspherical polynomials are directly related to Askey-Wilson polynomials (
168
) as:

\displaystyle C_{n}(\cos\theta;\beta|q^{2})=\frac{(\beta^{2};q^{2})_{n}}{(\beta q,-\beta,-\beta q,q^{2};q^{2})_{n}}P_{n}(\cos\theta;\sqrt{\beta},\sqrt{\beta}q,-\sqrt{\beta},-\sqrt{\beta}q|q^{2}).

(178)

They therefore satisfy analogous orthogonality relations to (
172
), (
173
). We find:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1-\beta^{2}q^{4n}}{1-\beta^{2}}\frac{(-\beta,q^{2};q^{2})_{n}}{(-\beta q^{2},\beta^{2};q^{2})_{n}}C_{n}(\cos\theta_{1};\beta|q^{2})C_{n}(\cos\theta_{2};\beta|q^{2})=\\
&\frac{(\beta^{2}q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(-\beta,-\beta q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}(\beta^{2}q^{2};q^{4})_{\infty}^{2}}\frac{(\beta e^{\pm 2i\theta_{1}},\beta q^{2}e^{\pm 2i\theta_{1}};q^{4})_{\infty}}{(q^{2},e^{\pm 2i\theta_{1}})_{\infty}}2\pi\delta(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}),\end{aligned}

(179)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\frac{(e^{\pm 2i\theta};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(\beta e^{\pm 2i\theta},\beta q^{2}e^{\pm 2i\theta_{1}};q^{4})\infty}\frac{(-\beta,q^{2};q^{2})_{m}}{(\beta^{2};q^{2})_{m}}C_{n}(\cos\theta;\beta|q^{2})C_{m}(\cos\theta;\beta|q^{2})=\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\frac{1-\beta^{2}}{1-\beta^{2}q^{4n}}\frac{2\pi(\beta^{2}q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}(\beta^{2}q^{2};q^{4})_{\infty}^{2}(-\beta,-\beta q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}\delta_{n,m}.\end{aligned}

(180)

We now define the rescaled polynomials:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\zeta_{n}(\cos\theta;\beta|q^{2})\equiv\braket{n|\theta}_{n_{F}=2,adj.}=\\[5.0pt]
&\qquad\qquad\sqrt{\frac{1-\beta q^{2n}}{1-\beta}\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{n}}{(\beta^{2};q^{2})_{n}}\frac{(\beta^{2}q^{2};q^{4})^{2}_{\infty}(-\beta,-\beta q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(\beta^{2}q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}}\\
&\qquad\qquad\times\sqrt{\frac{(q^{2}e^{\pm 2i\theta};q^{2})_{\infty}}{2\pi(\beta e^{\pm 2i\theta},\beta q^{2}e^{\pm 2i\theta};q^{4})_{\infty}}}C_{n}(\cos\theta;\beta|q^{2})\end{aligned}

(181)

From (
179
), (
180
), we find that
\zeta_{n}
satisfy following
n
- and
\theta
-orthogonality relations:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\;\zeta_{n}(\cos\theta;\beta|q^{2})\zeta_{m}(\cos\theta;\beta|q^{2})=\delta_{n,m},

(182)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\zeta_{n}(\cos\theta_{1};\beta|q^{2})\zeta_{n}(\cos\theta_{2};\beta|q^{2})=\delta(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}).

(183)

Polynomials
\zeta_{n}(\cos\theta;\beta|q^{2})
satisfy a recursion relation, without a diagonal term
Koekoek2010

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{2\cos\theta}{\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}\zeta_{n}=&\sqrt{[n+1]_{q^{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\beta^{2}q^{2n}}{(1-\beta q^{2n})(1-\beta q^{2n+2})}}\zeta_{n+1}\\
&+\sqrt{[n]_{q^{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\beta^{2}q^{2n-2}}{(1-\beta q^{2n-2})(1-\beta q^{2n})}}\zeta_{n-1}\end{aligned}

(184)
