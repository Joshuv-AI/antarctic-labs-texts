Femoral head fracture management: outcomes of surgical hip dislocation with bioabsorbable screw fixation
Authors: Jianrong Chen, Ying Zhang, Zhoulian Feng, Lixiong Cai, Shijia Huang, Zhenjiang Liu, Weibing Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePipkin fractures are rare, complex hip injuries typically caused by high-energy trauma. Surgical management must balance adequate exposure with preservation of femoral head vascularity. We evaluated outcomes of surgical hip dislocation with absorbable screw fixation for Pipkin fractures.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 10 patients with Pipkin fractures treated by surgical hip dislocation and absorbable screw fixation at Foshan Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital from December 2018 to March 2023. Demographics, Pipkin classification, and injured limb were recorded. All patients had at least 2 years of follow-up. Outcomes included fracture healing time, Modified Harris Hip Score (MHHS), and modified Merle d’Aubigné score. Complications, including avascular necrosis (AVN) and post-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA), were documented.ResultsAt final follow-up, the mean MHHS was 90.3 ± 6.8. Functional results were excellent in 7 patients, good in 2, and fair in 1 (excellent-to-good rate, 90%). Modified Merle d’Aubigné scores paralleled the MHHS. One patient with a Pipkin type III fracture developed AVN; no other major complications were observed. Patients with Pipkin types I and II achieved better functional recovery than those with types III and IV.ConclusionsIn treating Pipkin fractures, surgical hip dislocation provides adequate exposure while preserving femoral head vascularity. Bioabsorbable screws achieve stable fixation through their thread design and axial compression, supporting fracture healing. This technique shows promise as a treatment strategy for complex femoral head fractures.Keywords: The surgical hip dislocation approach, Absorbable screws, Pipkin fractures, Retrospective study

Introduction

Pipkin fractures are a distinct subset of femoral head fractures, most often occurring with complex hip injuries. They typically result from high-energy trauma, such as motor vehicle accidents or falls from a height, in which posterior dislocation of the femoral head causes the posterior acetabular rim to impact the weight-bearing portion of the femoral head, producing the fracture [1–3]. Clinically, patients present with severe hip pain, functional impairment, and a shortened, adducted limb. These injuries are also associated with complications, including disruption of the femoral head blood supply and sciatic nerve injury [2, 4]. In 1957, Professor Pipkin classified these fractures into four types based on their morphological features, a system that remains the most widely used in clinical practice [5, 6]. Epidemiological studies indicate that femoral head fractures associated with hip fracture–dislocation (Pipkin lesions) account for 5%–15% of posterior hip dislocations [7]. This is consistent with reports of approximately 15% in broader hip fracture–dislocation cohorts. In a series of 132 cases, Scolaro et al. [1] reported Type I in 30.3%, Type II in 47.0%, Type III in 5.3%, and Type IV in 17.4%. Similarly, Shakya et al. [8] reviewed 50 cases and found Type I to be the most common (36.0%).

Because Pipkin fractures arise from high-energy trauma, treatment strategies vary by severity and include closed reduction, open reduction with internal fixation, fragment excision, and arthroplasty. Regardless of the method, complications remain common, including avascular necrosis of the femoral head (AVN), post-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA), and heterotopic ossification (HO) [4, 9]. Enocson et al. [7] reviewed 405 patients across 26 studies and reported incidences of 11.8% for AVN, 20% for PTOA, and 16.8% for HO. These findings underscore the importance of both adequate surgical exposure and preservation of femoral head vascularity. In 2001, Ganz [10] described the surgical hip dislocation approach, which provides 360° visualization of the femoral head, complete access to the acetabulum, and preservation of the ligamentum teres. The medial femoral circumflex artery remains protected by the intact obturator externus, and follow-up results showed no cases of AVN. Hosny et al. [11] treated 18 patients with Pipkin fractures using this approach with metal screw fixation, reporting one case of AVN and an overall excellent-to-good rate of 88.9%. Currently, fixation is most commonly achieved with Herbert screws or cannulated screws [8, 11, 12]. While effective, metallic implants present challenges: if displacement occurs, revision surgery is technically demanding and carries higher risk. Absorbable screws, typically composed of polymer materials, offer early compression similar to Herbert screws while gradually transferring stress to the fracture site as they degrade, thereby promoting bone healing. Importantly, they eliminate the need for secondary implant removal [13, 14]. The present study retrospectively analyzed patients with Pipkin fractures treated with the surgical hip dislocation approach combined with absorbable screw fixation. We aimed to evaluate the clinical outcomes of this strategy in terms of hip function, fracture healing, and postoperative complications, thereby providing evidence to optimize treatment strategies for this complex injury.

Materials and methods

This retrospective clinical study included 10 patients with Pipkin fractures who underwent surgical hip dislocation with absorbable screw fixation at Foshan Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine between December 2018 and March 2023. Patients were enrolled according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. All injuries met Pipkin fracture–dislocation criteria; no isolated femoral head fractures were included. The cohort comprised 6 women (mean age, 34.5 years) and 4 men (mean age, 32.0 years). Laterality was evenly distributed (5 left, 5 right). Baseline characteristics—including body mass index (BMI), Pipkin classification, mechanism of injury, and comorbidities—were recorded (Table 1). Preoperative and postoperative imaging, including hip radiographs and computed tomography, was available for all patients (Fig. 1A–D). Functional outcomes were assessed at each follow-up visit using a standardized hip scoring system.

Inclusion criteria

(1) Severe hip pain, a shortened and adducted limb, and functional impairment; (2) posterior hip dislocation with femoral head fracture confirmed by radiographs and/or CT imaging; (3) fracture classification consistent with Pipkin’s system [5, 15]:

Type I: posterior hip dislocation with fracture of the caudal femoral head extending below the fovea centralis;

Type I: posterior hip dislocation with fracture of the caudal femoral head extending below the fovea centralis;

Type II: posterior hip dislocation with fracture of the cephalic femoral head extending above the fovea centralis;

Type II: posterior hip dislocation with fracture of the cephalic femoral head extending above the fovea centralis;

Type III: Type I or II fracture combined with femoral neck fracture;

Type III: Type I or II fracture combined with femoral neck fracture;

Type IV: femoral head fracture accompanied by acetabular rim fracture;

Type IV: femoral head fracture accompanied by acetabular rim fracture;

(4) fracture caused by high-energy trauma (e.g., motor vehicle collision or fall from height); (5) age 15–65 years, with a minimum follow-up of 24 months.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Pathological or non-acute femoral head fractures; (2) history of hip surgery, PTOA, or hip dysplasia at the affected site; (3) injury not consistent with Pipkin’s classification system; (4) follow-up < 2 years, loss to follow-up, or incomplete clinical/imaging data; (5) major systemic disease or poor physical condition precluding surgical intervention.

Surgical method

Preoperative preparation

(1) Comprehensive evaluation to exclude pathological or non-acute femoral head fractures and any prior hip surgery, post-traumatic osteoarthritis, or dysplasia at the affected site; (2) for patients with Pipkin type III or IV fractures, enhanced CT or MRI to assess fragment size, displacement direction, extent of acetabular injury, and femoral neck fracture lines; (3) preoperative assessment to confirm fitness for general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation; (4) completion of relevant laboratory and imaging tests to provide a comprehensive assessment of the patient’s medical condition.

Surgical procedure

All patients underwent general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation. After induction, patients were placed in the lateral position with a folded pad between the legs to prevent adduction of the affected hip. A lateral approach was used. Before the skin incision, the hip was internally rotated to position the greater trochanter laterally. A longitudinal incision approximately 20 cm in length was centered over the greater trochanter. The skin and subcutaneous tissue were incised in layers, and the fascia of the gluteus maximus was split proximally. The knee was flexed and the hip extended to relax the gluteus maximus. An incision was made along its anterior border to expose the posterior edge of the gluteus medius and the vastus lateralis. The trochanteric capsule and attached tissues were mobilized to create a flap and retracted posteriorly to improve exposure. During separation of the greater trochanter and its attached tissues, particular attention was paid to the short external rotators. Dissection proceeded between the inferior gemellus and quadratus femoris, preserving these structures and avoiding excessive separation. The release was limited to the insertion of the short external rotators on the greater trochanter, and approximately 1 cm of soft tissue was preserved at their attachment points. This technique protects vascular and neural structures, minimizes traction-related compromise of the femoral head blood supply, and facilitates postoperative recovery.

A Hohmann retractor was used to elevate the vastus lateralis, exposing the inferior edge of the greater trochanter. An osteotomy was performed along the greater trochanter using a saw, from posterior to anterior, creating a bone fragment approximately 1.5 cm thick. Cutting depth was carefully controlled to ensure that the gluteus medius tendon remained intact and attached to the osteotomized fragment. Care was taken to avoid excessive depth that could injure the piriformis tendon and pull it forward with the gluteus medius and vastus lateralis. The piriformis was released, and the gluteus minimus overlying the joint capsule was dissected to further expose the anterolateral capsule. A Z-shaped capsulotomy was then performed. The medial femoral circumflex artery was identified and protected to safeguard femoral head vascularity. Adequate exposure was obtained while preserving the femoral head blood supply and the integrity of the acetabular labrum.

With continuous traction, the hip was flexed to 110°–120° to expose the anterolateral femoral head along the anterior acetabular rim. The femur was then externally rotated approximately 40°–60° while maintaining flexion. Subluxation was confirmed intraoperatively by palpation or fluoroscopy when the articular surface of the femoral head cleared the acetabular fossa and the posterior femoral neck contacted the posterior acetabular column. External rotation was then increased and the thigh slightly adducted to achieve full surgical dislocation (Fig. 1E), assisted by traction on the gluteus medius and minimus. The ligamentum teres was routinely transected to facilitate exposure; this step does not increase the risk of avascular necrosis [11]. All maneuvers were performed gently to avoid exacerbating fracture displacement or injuring the acetabular cartilage.

A Hohmann retractor was used to expose the anterior and superior acetabulum, as well as the anterior and inferior aspects of the transverse acetabular ligament, with care to protect the sciatic nerve. After adequate exposure of the femoral head and acetabulum, a comminuted fracture was identified in the weight-bearing portion of the femoral head, involving the articular surface adjacent to the ligamentum teres, with articular surface impaction and cartilage injury. All fracture fragments were removed, and the fracture site was debrided (Fig. 1F). The fracture was then reduced and fixed with three 3.5-mm absorbable screws placed in a triangular configuration (Fig. 1G). Cancellous bone harvested from the greater trochanter osteotomy site was implanted into the compressed area of the femoral head. Satisfactory alignment was confirmed under direct visualization. Traction, abduction, and internal rotation were applied to reduce the hip. The joint capsule was sutured with appropriate tension to avoid compromising femoral head blood supply. The greater trochanter osteotomy was repositioned and secured with a cannulated screw. Final C-arm fluoroscopy confirmed proper fracture reduction and correct placement of the internal fixation. The wound was irrigated, and the incision was closed in layers. A drainage tube was placed between the muscle layers and connected to the external drainage system. The surgical site was dressed.

Postoperative management

Postoperative care followed a standard perioperative protocol. Patients received symptomatic treatment, including prophylactic antibiotics for infection prevention, low-molecular-weight heparin for anticoagulation, agents to improve microcirculation, and analgesics for pain control. The drainage tube was typically maintained for approximately 2 days, depending on output. During this period, patients were encouraged to perform active and passive ankle and toe movements. Hip flexion–extension exercises began 1 month after surgery. At 2 months postoperatively, patients were permitted out-of-bed ambulation with crutches without weight bearing. By 3 months, based on follow-up findings, partial weight bearing was introduced, progressing to full weight bearing by 6 months. Follow-up visits were scheduled at 1, 3, and 6 months and at 1 and 2 years postoperatively, with initial assessments relying primarily on radiographs. When further evaluation was needed or when there was uncertainty regarding bone healing or AVN, MRI was obtained. Patients were also monitored for PTOA and HO.

Evaluation metrics

All patients were assessed by the same research team. Baseline demographic and clinical data were collected, including sex, age, body mass index (BMI), Pipkin classification, injured limb, mechanism of injury, and comorbidities. Fracture healing time was recorded for all patients. Functional outcomes were evaluated using the Modified Harris Hip Score (MHHS) [16] and the modified Merle d’Aubigné score [17]at 6 months, 1 year, and at the final follow-up. These scores quantified hip pain, functional mobility, and range of motion. The MHHS categories were defined as < 70, poor; 70–79, fair; 80–89, good; and ≥ 90, excellent [8, 11]. Fracture healing was determined by a combination of radiographic findings (radiographic and CT evidence of callus formation) and clinical signs (absence of local tenderness) [18].

Statistical analysis

In this study, data analysis was performed using SPSS 23.0 statistical software. The fracture healing time and MHHS scores at different postoperative time points were tested for normal distribution. If the data followed a normal distribution, an independent samples t-test was applied. Continuous variables were expressed as “mean ± standard deviation (x̅±s)”, while categorical data were presented as percentages (%). p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Because this was a retrospective case series of a rare injury pattern, the sample comprised all consecutive eligible patients during the study period, and no a priori sample-size calculation was performed. The study was descriptive and noncomparative; therefore, conventional power-based calculations requiring prespecified effect sizes and a comparator cohort were not applicable.

Results

Demographic characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the 10 included patients are summarized in Table 1. All cases met Pipkin criteria (fracture–dislocation); no isolated femoral head fractures were included (0%). The cohort comprised 6 women and 4 men, aged 15–65 years. Body mass index (BMI) ranged from 21.24 to 26.54 kg/m2. Laterality was evenly distributed (5 left, 5 right). By Pipkin type, 5 patients (50%) had Type I fractures, 2 (20%) Type II, 2 (20%) Type III, and 1 (10%) Type IV. Mechanisms of injury were motor vehicle collisions in 7 patients (70%) and falls from height in 3 (30%). Comorbidities and associated injuries included alcoholic hepatitis (n = 1), rib fractures (n = 3), hypertension (n = 1), and diabetes (n = 1).

In small-sample studies, Henle et al. [9] treated 18 patients using surgical hip dislocation with Herbert or cannulated screws and reported 2 cases of AVN. Hosny et al. [11] used surgical hip dislocation with Herbert or cannulated screws in 18 patients, reporting 1 case of AVN. Wang et al. [19] employed the Kocher–Langenbeck approach with bioabsorbable cannulated screws in 12 patients, noting AVN in 33.3% and HO in 8.3%. Massè et al. [20] treated 13 patients with nonabsorbable screws and reported 1 AVN, 2 HO, and 1 case of postoperative stiffness. Park et al. [21] described 5 patients who underwent closed reduction and subsequently developed femoral neck fractures, underscoring the need for accurate imaging to avoid iatrogenic Pipkin type III injury. In medium-sample studies, Shakya et al. [8] treated 50 patients with Herbert screws via the anterior Smith–Petersen or posterior Kocher–Langenbeck approach according to fragment location, reporting AVN in 12.0%, PTOA in 32.0%, HO in 16.0%, and sciatic nerve injury in 12.0%. Yoon et al. [22] treated 34 patients with surgical hip dislocation and cortical or headless compression screws, with AVN in 5.9%. Chen et al. [23] managed 40 Pipkin type IV fractures using a modified Hardinge approach with interfragmentary screws, reporting AVN in 22.5%, PTOA in 12.5%, and HO in 5.0%. In large-sample studies, Scolaro et al. [1] analyzed 147 patients treated with the Smith–Petersen or Kocher–Langenbeck approach and mini-fragment screws, reporting AVN in 4.1% and HO in 19.0%. Tonetti et al. [24] reported on 110 patients—32 treated nonoperatively and 78 surgically (30 with screw fixation, including absorbable screws)—with postoperative AVN in 8 cases, joint stiffness in 25, and limited motion in 2.

Overall, these studies indicate that Herbert screws, cannulated screws, and cortical screws are the primary fixation methods for the surgical management of Pipkin fractures. Using assessments such as the Harris Hip Score and the Merle d’Aubigné score, most studies reported effective relief of hip pain, restoration of mobility, and improvement in walking function. The principal complications were AVN, PTOA, and HO, with incidence influenced by fracture type, sample size, surgical approach, and choice of fixation.

Discussion

Pipkin fractures are a distinct type of femoral head fracture, accounting for approximately 5–15% of hip joint injuries and most commonly associated with high-energy trauma such as motor vehicle collisions and falls from a height [1, 7]. The biomechanical mechanism typically involves the hip flexed to 90° with adduction and internal rotation, allowing the acetabular rim to impart shear stress to the posterolateral weight-bearing zone of the femoral head. This produces a characteristic fracture line below the fovea capitis [6]. Such injuries are frequently accompanied by capsular disruption and contusion of the deep branch of the medial femoral circumflex artery (MFCA), which contributes to the high risk of subsequent AVN [25].

A central controversy in the surgical management of Pipkin fractures lies in balancing adequate exposure of the femoral head for accurate reduction with preservation of the vascular supply to prevent AVN [19, 25]. The femoral head receives its dominant blood supply from the MFCA. Its transverse branch, the inferior retinacular artery, runs beneath the anterior femoral neck within the capsule, while the ascending and deep branches enter posteriorly and posterosuperiorly. Approximately 80% of the deep-branch supply arises posteriorly, underscoring the need to protect this pathway [26]. Several surgical approaches have been described, each with advantages and limitations. The Smith–Petersen approach provides anterior access and avoids direct injury to the MFCA, thereby lowering AVN risk, but it has been associated with higher rates of HO [12, 27]. The Kocher–Langenbeck approach enables direct visualization of posterior acetabular and femoral head fractures; however, detachment of the external rotators may compromise the vascular supply, resulting in higher AVN incidence [9, 27]. The Watson–Jones approach, through the interval between the tensor fasciae latae and sartorius, theoretically preserves the ascending branch of the lateral femoral circumflex artery but offers limited access to posterior pathology and may cause postoperative abductor weakness [28]. The Ludloff approach, typically reserved for basal femoral neck fractures, is technically demanding and carries a significant risk of MFCA injury [28, 29]. In contrast, the surgical hip dislocation approach, using a trochanteric flip osteotomy and Z-shaped capsulotomy, preserves the deep branch of the MFCA, provides 360° visualization of the femoral head and acetabulum, and maintains muscle attachments. This approach reduces the risk of AVN and PTOA and is particularly suitable for complex femoral head fractures [10, 20]. Clinical outcomes in the literature support its effectiveness. Hosny et al. [11] treated 18 patients with this approach combined with Herbert or cannulated screws, reporting one case of AVN (5.6%) and an 88.9% excellent/good rate on the Merle d’Aubigné and Postel score. Similarly, Khalifa et al. [30] treated 31 patients and reported excellent/good rates of 92.6% on the MHHS and 89% on the modified Merle d’Aubigné and Postel score. Complications included AVN in 2 patients (7.6%) and asymptomatic HO in 5 patients (18.5%). Both studies concluded that the surgical hip dislocation approach is a safe and effective option for Pipkin fractures, with acceptable complication rates.

The choice of internal fixation is critical to hip functional recovery and should be guided by fracture anatomy, including fragment size, location, and fracture type [19]. Achieving anatomic reduction is equally essential; inadequate reduction is associated with a higher incidence of late PTOA [19]. Currently, Herbert screws and cannulated screws are the most widely used fixation devices. Their variable-pitch threading and axial compression during insertion provide stable fixation and minimize fragment displacement. However, metallic implants typically require secondary removal, increasing the risk of iatrogenic injury [8, 11, 12, 30–32]. Absorbable screws, most commonly fabricated from high-molecular-weight polymers such as polyglycolic acid (PGA) and polylactic acid (PLLA), offer biological advantages. These materials degrade through hydrolysis into carbon dioxide and water, eliminating the need for secondary surgery [33]. Although absorbable screws have lower initial mechanical strength than metal screws, their properties are generally adequate for most small- to medium-sized fragments. The compression effect of an absorbable screw is influenced by thread design, axial thrust during insertion, and expansion pressure during degradation, together producing initial compression at the fracture site. In addition, the elastic modulus of absorbable screws approximates that of cancellous bone; as the biomaterial degrades, the screw tract is gradually filled with new bone, enabling stress transfer to the bone surface, reducing bone resorption and stress shielding, and providing sufficient early fixation strength [34–36]. Accordingly, we suggest that for elderly patients with hip fractures, weight bearing need not be intentionally delayed or restricted during postoperative rehabilitation. In a retrospective analysis of 131 hip fracture patients, Kamel et al. [37] found that delayed postoperative ambulation was an independent risk factor for new-onset delirium and pneumonia, but not for pressure ulcers or deep vein thrombosis. A prospective study likewise reported that delayed weight bearing could lead to delayed fracture healing and a decline in cognitive function [38]. The development of bioabsorbable metal implants, particularly magnesium alloy screws, has addressed the strength limitations of polymer-based devices. Magnesium screws provide fixation strength comparable to metallic implants while maintaining biodegradability and osteoconductive properties [33, 39]. In experimental research, Waizy et al. [40] demonstrated in a rabbit femoral medullary model that magnesium alloy screws did not cause biochemical abnormalities or histopathological toxicity in major organs within one year, supporting their biocompatibility and bone-healing potential. Clinically, Kose et al. [13] reported on 11 ankle fracture patients treated with magnesium absorbable screws; all achieved radiographic union with good ankle-hindfoot scores and preserved joint mobility. Taken together, the efficacy of Pipkin fracture treatment depends not only on achieving anatomic reduction but also on fixation choice. Metal screws such as Herbert screws provide reliable stability but often require removal; traditional absorbable polymer screws avoid reoperation but have limited mechanical strength; and newer degradable magnesium alloy screws combine adequate fixation strength with favorable biocompatibility, showing promising results in both experimental and clinical studies.

In this study, 10 patients with Pipkin fractures were treated using surgical hip dislocation combined with absorbable screw fixation. Fracture healing, functional outcomes, and complications were systematically evaluated. The mean fracture healing time was 13.8 ± 2.7 weeks, shorter than that reported by Yoon et al. [22]. in patients treated with cortical or headless compression screws. This difference may reflect the small sample size and interpatient variability. Mechanistically, fracture healing with absorbable screws may be facilitated by vascular granulation tissue that forms between screw tunnels. Early in the healing process, absorbable implants promote the development of a thin layer of vascularized fibrous tissue with adjacent trabeculae. As the polymer degrades, multinucleated macrophages and capillaries progressively cover the fracture surface—a biological response not observed with metal screws [34]. At the final follow-up, the mean MHHS was 90.3 ± 6.8; seven patients were rated excellent, two good, and one fair, yielding an excellent-to-good rate of 90%. One patient with a Pipkin Type III fracture developed AVN (10%). This incidence was lower than the 33.3% AVN rate reported by Wang et al. [19], which may be attributable to their exclusive focus on Type III fractures. In comparison, a study using Herbert screws via surgical hip dislocation reported an AVN incidence of 8.3%, closely approximating our findings [41]. A novel aspect of this study was the dynamic evaluation of hip function using serial MHHS at 6 months, 1 year, and the final follow-up, displayed graphically to illustrate recovery trends. To our knowledge, such a longitudinal presentation has not been reported in previous Pipkin fracture studies. The results demonstrated continuous functional improvement, with patients sustaining Type I or II fractures showing superior recovery compared with those with Type III or IV injuries. The poorer outcomes in Type III fractures likely reflect disruption of the femoral head blood supply, which substantially increases AVN risk [1, 19]. For Type IV fractures, combined involvement of the acetabulum and femoral head necessitates restoration of acetabular stability before addressing the femoral head; even with proper management, functional recovery may remain limited [4]. Previous studies further suggest that in Type I or II fractures, when the fragment involves < 1/3 of the non–weight-bearing surface of the femoral head, fragment excision does not adversely affect long-term outcomes, with excellent-to-good rates up to 86.7% [4, 42]. These findings reinforce that fracture type and fragment morphology critically influence both fixation choice and ultimate clinical outcomes.

This study evaluated postoperative fracture healing time and dynamic hip function recovery in 10 patients with Pipkin fractures, demonstrating that surgical hip dislocation combined with absorbable screw fixation provides advantages in both fracture healing and functional recovery. By plotting line graphs of serial MHHS scores, we illustrated functional recovery trajectories and highlighted differences among fracture types, offering a new perspective for clinical evaluation. However, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, it is a single-center, retrospective series with a small sample, which limits statistical power and generalizability and may overestimate treatment effects. Second, there was no comparator cohort (e.g., metal screws or alternative surgical approaches), so the observed outcomes cannot be attributed solely to absorbable screw fixation or surgical hip dislocation. Third, the cohort included multiple Pipkin types, but the sample was insufficient for adequately powered, type-specific analyses, introducing potential confounding by injury severity. Fourth, although all patients were followed for more than two years, AVN and PTOA may manifest later; late complications may therefore be underestimated. Finally, outcome assessment relied on clinical scores and routine radiographs, with MRI obtained selectively, which may reduce sensitivity for detecting early AVN. Future work should use prospective, multicenter designs with predefined sample-size calculations, standardized imaging and rehabilitation protocols, and longer follow-up to validate these findings.

Conclusion

The surgical hip dislocation approach allows complete exposure of femoral head fractures while preserving vascular supply. Although absorbable screws have lower initial mechanical strength than metallic implants, their thread-based design and axial compression during insertion provide sufficient stability for fracture healing. Their elastic modulus, similar to that of cancellous bone, also reduces stress shielding. Treatment of Pipkin fractures with surgical hip dislocation combined with absorbable screw fixation demonstrates effective reduction and preservation of femoral head vascularity. Although early outcomes suggest improved hip function, larger studies with longer follow-up are needed to fully assess the long-term benefits and safety of this approach. This method shows promise as a treatment strategy for complex femoral head fractures.
