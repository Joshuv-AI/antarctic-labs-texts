Selenium-Doped Calcium Hydroxide Nanoparticles Inhibit Osteoclasts and Promote Osteogenesis for Osteoporosis Treatment
Authors: Pengruofeng Liu, Zimo Zhang, Minhan Li, Hairui Li, Guang Chu, Xiaoqian Du, Jun Lin, Weiming Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeOsteoporosis is characterized by an acidic and oxidative stress microenvironment driven by excessive osteoclast activity, which impairs osteogenesis and leads to dysregulated bone remodeling. This study aims to develop selenium-doped calcium hydroxide nanoparticles (SeCaNP) to simultaneously inhibit osteoclastogenesis and promote osteogenic differentiation, thereby providing a multi-targeted microenvironment-modulating strategy for osteoporosis therapy.MethodsSeCaNP was synthesized via a coprecipitation method and characterized using TEM, XRD, and acid-neutralization assays. In vitro, bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMMs) and bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were co-cultured with SeCaNP to assess osteoclast activity and osteogenic differentiation. In vivo, an ovariectomy (OVX)-induced osteoporosis mouse model was established and treated with local injections of SeCaNP. Therapeutic efficacy and biosafety were systematically evaluated.ResultsSeCaNP significantly upregulated GPx1 expression, effectively scavenged reactive oxygen species (ROS), and neutralized the intracellular acidic microenvironment, thereby markedly inhibiting osteoclastogenesis. Concurrently, SeCaNP promoted BMSC cytoskeletal remodeling and significantly enhanced osteogenic differentiation and mineralization, upregulating key markers (RUNX2, ALP, OPN, OCN). RNA-seq analysis indicated that SeCaNP regulated osteogenesis-related membrane signaling platforms and extracellular matrix assembly. In vivo, SeCaNP treatment significantly ameliorated OVX-induced bone loss, notably improving bone mineral density (BMD) and trabecular microarchitecture parameters (BV/TV, Tb.N, Tb.Th). Furthermore, SeCaNP upregulated BMP-2 expression and serum BALP levels without inducing systemic toxicity.ConclusionSeCaNP effectively overcomes the limitations of conventional osteoporosis therapies by integrating acid neutralization, antioxidant properties, and osteogenic support. Through a dual-regulatory mechanism that inhibits bone resorption and promotes bone formation, SeCaNP successfully reconstructs the pathological bone microenvironment and reverses bone loss, highlighting its significant potential as a microenvironment-modulating biomaterial for anti-osteoporotic therapy.Keywords: osteoporosis therapy, nanoparticles, SeCaNP, acid neutralization, antioxidant

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a systemic metabolic skeletal disorder defined by a continuous decline in bone mineral density and the degradation of bone microarchitecture. These pathological changes significantly enhance skeletal fragility, positioning the condition as a major global public health challenge.1,2 Its core pathology involves a severe imbalance in bone homeostasis, where acid and hydrolases secreted by osteoclasts degrade bone minerals and collagen, resulting in substantial bone mineral loss and increased risk of fractures and bone fragility.3–5 Although existing therapeutic drugs (such as bisphosphonates and denosumab) effectively inhibit bone resorption, their capacity to promote bone formation remains limited. Additionally, the occurrence of atypical femoral fractures and osteonecrosis affecting the jaw has been documented as a consequence of prolonged pharmacological intervention.6,7 Given these challenges, it is imperative to establish dual or multi-targeted therapeutic strategies that concurrently modulate osteoclast and osteoblast activities to re-establish bone homeostasis in osteoporotic patients.8,9

Bone remodeling is highly dependent on the precise temporal and spatial coordination between osteoclasts and osteoblasts. Under osteoporotic conditions, the abnormal activation of osteoclasts is one of the key initiating factors driving bone loss.5,10 Mature osteoclasts secrete large quantities of H⁺ into the sealed resorption cavity via the V-ATPase proton pump, creating an extremely acidic microenvironment on the bone surface with a pH as low as approximately 4.5. This acidic microenvironment activates acid hydrolases, such as cathepsin K and acid phosphatase, thereby degrading the organic matrix of bone.11–13 Concurrently, the levels of ROS produced by osteoclast activation and the local inflammatory response are significantly elevated.14 High levels of ROS not only further positively regulate the receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (RANKL) signaling pathway, promoting the differentiation of osteoclast precursors and the function of mature osteoclasts.15 Moreover, the induction of oxidative stress within osteoblasts and their progenitor bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) serves to impair cellular expansion and blunt their osteogenic potential. This suppression of both differentiation and mineral deposition further aggravates the dysregulation between bone synthesis and degradation.16 Following bone injury, the defect site frequently exhibits a malignant microenvironment characterized by intertwined acidification, inflammation, and oxidative stress, severely impeding endogenous bone repair processes.17–19 Consequently, addressing the acidic-high ROS pathological microenvironment characteristic of osteoporotic bone microenvironment necessitates a material capable of simultaneously neutralizing local acidity, alleviating oxidative stress, and intelligently regulating cellular behavior. This approach is essential for resolving the fundamental imbalance within the pathological microenvironment and achieving efficient restoration of bone remodeling homeostasis.

As a fundamental micronutrient for human physiology, Selenium (Se) serves as the cornerstone of the endogenous antioxidant network. It exerts its biological effects primarily through selenoprotein integration, which is indispensable for cellular redox stability.20,21 Selenium deficiency can lead to downregulation of selenoprotein expression, rendering BMSCs and osteoblasts more susceptible to ROS attack and impairing their differentiation capacity.20 In recent years, nano-form selenium has garnered significant attention in bone metabolism regulation due to its excellent bioavailability and safety profile.22,23 Previous studies have shown that, supplementing selenium in nanoform can effectively upregulate the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as GPx1, alleviate oxidative stress in bone tissue, and inhibit osteoclast formation. Ren et al identified that selenium nanoparticles dispersed in phytochemicals have anti-inflammatory activity by regulating the expression of catalase, GPx1 and COX-2 genes.24 Evidence from Zou et al suggested that LNT-Se acts as a potent modulator of GPx1 expression. By enhancing this specific selenoprotein activity, LNT-Se effectively stifles osteoclast differentiation and subsequent bone loss.25 However, the strongly acidic microenvironment in osteoporotic lesions may inactivate selenoproteins, thereby weakening selenium-mediated antioxidant protection and limiting therapeutic efficacy. Consequently, selenium supplementation alone struggles to address this complex pathological microenvironment. However, previous studies have primarily focused on anti-resorptive effects26 or bone repair,27 with limited attention given to the systematic reconstruction of the pathological microenvironment of osteoporosis. This approach aims to effectively inhibit osteoclast differentiation while simultaneously promoting osteoblast proliferation. Consequently, establishing nanomedicine-based interventions that can synergistically modulate bone-forming and bone-resorbing processes is essential. By reshaping the complex pathological landscape, these agents offer a promising avenue for re-establishing bone mass in osteoporotic conditions.

Herein, we engineered a novel selenium-doped calcium hydroxide composite nanoparticle (SeCaNP) to achieve effective treatment targeting the osteoporosis microenvironment. A schematic overview of SeCaNP synthesis and its mechanisms in osteoporotic bone regeneration is shown in Figure 1. Within this system the calcium hydroxide component acts as an acid neutralizer, rapidly responding to and elevating local pH to inhibit osteoclast acid hydrolase activity while providing a stable pH environment for selenoprotein function. Moreover, the incorporated selenium element effectively promotes the expression and activation of intracellular selenoproteins (such as GPx1) within the improved microenvironment. Crucially, the released Ca2⁺ not only directly participates in osteoblast activity and mineral matrix deposition but also regulates cellular metabolism and differentiation through calcium signaling pathways, thereby playing a pivotal role in maintaining skeletal homeostasis. Collectively, these processes systematically reduced ROS levels, thereby inhibiting osteoclast activity and relieving oxidative suppression of BMSC osteogenic differentiation. Through an integrated design combining acid regulation, antioxidant properties, and osteogenic support, we hypothesized that SeCaNP could alleviate the acidic and oxidative-stress microenvironment in osteoporosis. We further evaluated whether SeCaNP suppresses osteoclastogenesis and promotes osteogenic differentiation in vitro and alleviates OVX-induced bone loss in vivo. Overall, this work provides a feasible strategy for microenvironment-oriented regulation of bone remodeling, offering new insights for the development of multifunctional biomaterials in the treatments of osteoporosis.

Materials and Methods

Materials

The CCK-8 assay kit was purchased from Abcam (Beijing, China). Complete cell culture medium, trypsin and foetal bovine serum were purchased from Haixing Bio (Jiangsu, China). Ghost Pencil Peptide (Red) Staining Kit, Reactive Oxygen Species Detection Kit (CM-H2DCFDA), Anti-Tartrate-Resistant Acid Phosphatase (TRAP) Activity Detection Kit, and Osteoblast Mineralized Nodule Staining Kit (Alizarin Red S Method) were purchased from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). PBS, DAPI stain, and DiI (DiIC 18(3)) orange-red fluorescent probe for cell membranes were purchased from White Shark (Anhui, China). BCECF, AM (pH fluorescent probe) purchased from Yisheng (Shanghai, China). GPx1 antibody was purchased from SanYing Bio (Hubei, China). The tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase staining kit was purchased from Solarbio (Beijing, China). Hydroxyapatite-coated plates were provided by Chinxbt. M-CSF and RANKL were supplied by MCE. α-MEM was provided by Prenosai (Hubei, China). The One Step RT-qPCR SYBR Green Kit was supplied by Novazene (Shanghai, China). The RNA extraction kit was provided by Tiangen (Beijing, China).

Synthesis of CaNP

2mL of 0.2 mol/L CaCl2 solution were mixed with 50 mL of 4 mg/mL glucose solution under stirring for 3 min. At 90°C, 4 mL of 0.2 mol/L NaOH solution was added dropwise whilst stirring for 5 min. The mixture was centrifuged, collected, and washed with water.

Synthesis of SeCaNP

Under stirring conditions, 2 mL of a 0.1 mol/L Na2SeO3 solution was mixed with 50 mL of a 4 mg/mL glucose solution and stirred for 1 min. 2 mL of a 0.2 mol/L CaCl2 solution was added and stirred for 3 min. At 90°C, 4 mL of 0.2 mol/L NaOH solution was added dropwise whilst stirring for 5 min. The mixture was centrifuged, collected, and washed with water.

TEM and EDS

Microstructural and chemical analyses were performed using a JEM-2100F microscope operated at 200 kV, which is equipped for high-resolution imaging. EDS point scans and elemental mapping were conducted to determine the local chemical composition and distribution.

SAED Pattern Acquisition

SAED was performed by positioning the aperture over an individual nanoparticle. The diffraction patterns were recorded at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV with a camera length of 80 cm, using a slow-scan CCD camera to ensure sufficient signal-to-noise ratio.

DLS and Zeta Potential

The powder sample were dispersed in deionized water at a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL and sonicated for 30 min to ensure a homogeneous suspension. All measurements were performed at 25°C using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument equipped with a 633 nm laser and a backscatter detector. The zeta potential was derived from the electrophoretic mobility using the Smoluchowski model.

Inductively Coupled Plasma (ICP)

The solid sample (5 mg) was digested in a microwave‑assisted acid system with a 3:1 (v/v) mixture of concentrated HNO3 and HCl to ensure complete dissolution of Ca and Se. The digest was diluted to 50 mL with ultrapure water, and the concentrations of Ca and Se were determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP‑OES).

Acid Neutralization Experiments

A 30 mL volume of PBS solution (pH 4.5 or pH 6.5) was placed under magnetic stirring. The pH was adjusted by stepwise addition of 20 mg/mL CaNP or SeCaNP suspensions at increasing volumes (1, 2, 3, 5, 10, … μL) while continuously monitoring the pH with a pH meter. The titration was stopped when the pH variation was less than 0.01 for three consecutive measurements.

OC Induction

Cells were seeded at a density of 1.5×103 per well in 96-well plates. After overnight culture, the medium was removed, cells were washed once, and then supplemented with α-MEM containing 10% FBS and the corresponding inducer (100 ng/mL RANKL + 50 ng/mL M-CSF). The induction period lasted 3–5 days.

Assessment of Cell Viability

To assess cell viability, cells were seeded into 96-well plates. Cells were treated with Na2SeO3, CaNP, and SeCaNP respectively. Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay kit on day 1 of culture.

F-Actin Staining

Cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well. After 6 hours, cells were treated with Na2SeO3, CaNP, or SeCaNP for 24 hours. Fixed with 4% formaldehyde and washed. Permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 solution. Cover cells with phalloidin working solution and incubate at room temperature in the dark for 20–90 minutes before washing with PBS. Counterstain nuclei with DAPI solution and wash with PBS. Observe and photograph under a fluorescence microscope.

TRAP Staining

The cells were washed and fixed with pre-chilled anti-tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) fixative (2–8°C) for 3 minutes, then excess moisture was removed. Cover cells with TRAP incubation buffer (70–80 μL/well), incubate at 37°C for 60 minutes, then wash. Counterstain with methyl green counterstain solution for 1 minute, wash, photograph, and quantify.

Western Blot Analysis

Culture media was prepared by diluting Na2SeO3, Ca NPs and SeCa NPs in α-MEM+10% FBS. 2 × 104 cells were seed per well, incubate overnight, then the medium was replaced with the corresponding drug-containing medium after 6 hours. The medium was changed every other day. Cells was collected after 7 days of culture, and the proteins were extracted for Western blot analysis. Western blotting was assessed expression levels of the bone core transcription factor RUNX2, OPN, OCN, and ALP.

Measurement of Intracellular ROS Activities

Medium was replaced with PBS, and cells were starved in PBS for 1 hour. H2DCFH-DA solution was added in serum-free medium to each well and incubate for 30 minutes. Fluorescence was observed under a fluorescence microscope and capture images.

Determination of Intracellular Acid Neutralizing Capacity

Each drug group was diluted in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Medium was changed every other day, and induction was performed for 7 days. Upon observation of mature OCs formation, BCECF, AM (pH-sensitive fluorescent probe) was employed for detection. Changes in fluorescence intensity were monitored to assess acidity, with photographs taken. Samples were fixed in 4% formaldehyde and stained with DiI and DAPI. Observations were conducted under fluorescence microscopy, with photographs captured.

ALP Staining

After 6 h of attachment, the medium was replaced with fresh treatment medium, which was changed daily for 7 days. The cells were then fixed with 4% formaldehyde and washed with PBS. The staining solution was added and the cells were incubated in a humidified, dark chamber. After washing to remove unreacted stain, the stop solution was added to terminate the reaction. Finally, the cells were observed and photographed under a microscope.

Alizarin Red Staining

Culture media for each group were prepared by diluting the respective agents in α‑MEM with 10% FBS. At 6 h after plating, we replaced the medium with the corresponding treatment medium and renewed it every other day. For Alizarin Red S staining, the cells were maintained for 14 days, then fixed with 4% formaldehyde and rinsed with PBS. Next, 400 µL of Alizarin Red S solution was added per well and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. After washing away the unbound dye, the stained cells were observed under a microscope.

qRT-PCR

For gene expression analysis, cells were cultured for 7 days prior to total RNA extraction and reverse transcription. RT-qPCR was then employed to evaluate the mRNA expression levels of osteogenic markers, including the key transcription factor RUNX2, as well as OPN, OCN, and ALP.

OVX Mouse Model

Eight-week-old female C57BL/6J mice underwent ovariectomy (OVX). Six weeks post-surgery, the ovariectomized mice received in situ injections of Na2SeO3, CaNP, or SeCaNP, with a saline group serving as the control. Following one month of treatment, the femurs were harvested for micro-CT analysis to evaluate microarchitectural parameters. Finally, bone tissue sections were prepared and subjected to histological analyses, including H&E and TRAP staining.

Statistical Analysis

All quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’ s multiple comparisons test to determine significant differences between groups. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Results and Discussion

Preparation and Characterization of SeCaNP

SeCaNP was synthesized by doping selenium into calcium hydroxide nanoparticles via the coprecipitation method. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of SeCaNP and CaNP are shown in Figure 2A. CaNP appeared as dark particles and aggregates, predominantly forming flocculent clusters of small particles, with some areas exhibiting dispersion. However, SeCaNP exhibited more distinct morphological characteristics: the sample primarily consists of flake-like structures with well-defined outlines and irregular lateral edges, forming layered aggregates from thin two-dimensional sheets or the assembly of polycrystalline domains. The amorphous nature of CaNP was confirmed by its selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern, which displayed a characteristic diffuse halo without any discernible sharp spots or well-defined concentric rings. This indicated that CaNP primarily exhibited low crystallinity or an amorphous-like structure, or is composed of extremely small crystallites. On the contrary, the SAED pattern of SeCaNP exhibited multiple distinct concentric diffraction rings accompanied by a discrete speckled distribution, indicating that SeCaNP possessed pronounced crystallographic order. The diffraction ring characteristics further confirmed its nanocrystalline structure. Figure 2B displayed the elemental distribution mapping results for CaNP and SeCaNP. Both materials exhibited abundant calcium (Ca) and oxygen (O) elemental distributions with uniform spatial distribution, indicating that their primary composition consisted of calcium-containing oxides. Within SeCaNP, the uniform dispersion of Se elements was clearly observable, confirming the successful incorporation of selenium into the material system. Following selenization treatment, the nanoparticle size distribution shifted towards larger dimensions, with SeCaNP exhibiting a significantly larger average size than their precursor CaNP (Figure 2C). This indicated that selenium incorporation altered the equilibrium between crystal nucleation and growth processes, ultimately leading to a coarsening effect. Zeta potential analysis indicated that the surface electronegativity of the nanoparticles significantly increased following selenization treatment (Figure 2D). This change demonstrated that the introduction of selenium raised the proportion of ionized groups, thereby enhancing the surface negative charge density. The quantitative elemental analysis in Figure 2E further revealed that the selenium content in SeCaNP reached 37.24 ± 0.99%. However, CaNP contained almost no Se. This result unequivocally verified the successful incorporation of selenium and its high loading capacity within the material. In the XRD patterns (Figure 2F), SeCaNP exhibited richer and stronger diffraction peaks in the low- to medium-angle region, with overall more pronounced peak shapes. This indicated that the introduction of selenium enhanced the crystallinity of the nanoparticles. CaNP displayed relatively fewer diffraction peaks with weaker intensities, suggesting simpler crystalline phase characteristics and potentially containing a higher proportion of low-crystallinity or amorphous components. To further elucidate the formation mechanism of SeCaNP, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) were employed (Figure 2G and H). The FTIR spectra revealed characteristic Se-O signals within a broad range of approximately 480 to 900 cm−1, which were attributed to the Se=O stretching vibration of selenite (Se4+). Consistently, XPS analysis showed the Se3d core-level spectrum with a peak at 58.7 eV, which, upon deconvolution, was assigned to the Se4+ chemical state. These findings collectively confirm that selenium is primarily incorporated into the Ca(OH)2 lattice as selenite (SeO32-) through chemical bonding, rather than existing as elemental selenium or being physically adsorbed.

The release kinetics of Ca and Se from SeCaNP were then investigated at different pH levels (Figure 2I and J). A markedly higher release was observed under acidic conditions. At 48 h, the cumulative releases of Se and Ca were 74.83% and 24.59%, respectively, which were significantly greater than those at pH 7.4. Figure 2K and L illustrated the acid-neutralizing behavior of the materials under different initial acidity conditions. At pH 6.5 and pH 4.5, both materials could elevate the pH to near physiological levels, demonstrating their capacity for sustained buffering and proton neutralization within acidic microenvironments. CaNP exhibited a more rapid neutralization process (requiring a smaller titration volume to reach near-neutrality), whereas SeCaNP necessitated a larger volume to achieve comparable pH levels. This indicated that acid neutralization in the system was primarily driven by the calcium hydroxide phase. Under acidic conditions, calcium hydroxide gradually dissolved, releasing OH− ions and consuming H⁺ ions, thereby promoting a rise in the system’s pH. Consequently, the calcium hydroxide content in the material was closely correlated with its acid neutralization capacity.

Finally, the storage stability of SeCaNP was assessed by measuring particle size and zeta potential over 7 days of storage in PBS at 4°C (Figure S1). The particle size and zeta potential remained essentially unchanged throughout the period, demonstrating the good colloidal stability of SeCaNP under the tested conditions.

SeCaNP Reduces Oxidative Stress and Suppresses Osteoclastogenesis

To investigate the effect of SeCaNP on osteoclast activity, the biocompatibility of SeCaNP was assessed in vitro. Cell viability changes in bone marrow-derived macrophage (BMMs) cells co-cultured with different calcium ion concentration groups for 24 hours were detected using the CCK-8 assay and quantitatively evaluated. Results indicated that cell activity was optimal at a Se concentration of 1.6 μg/mL, hence this concentration was selected for subsequent experiments (Figure S2). Following co-culture of CaNP, Na2SeO3 and SeCaNP with BMMs, the BMMs exhibited distinct nuclei and cytoskeletal structures (Figure 3A), with these well-preserved cellular morphologies demonstrating favorable biocompatibility. Co-culture experiments with osteoclasts (OCs) demonstrated that, compared to the CaNP group, the number of TRAP-stained OCs was significantly reduced in both the Na2SeO3 and SeCaNP groups (Figure 3B and C). This indicated that the incorporation of Se was a key factor in inhibiting osteoclast proliferation. The presence of selenium components within SeCaNP and their sustained-release properties provided the functional basis for their anti-osteoclast activity. Figure 3D illustrated the expression of the GPx1 protein, with the SeCaNP-treated group exhibiting distinct bands, indicating a significant effect of SeCaNP in enhancing GPx1 protein expression. As a key selenium-dependent antioxidant enzyme, GPx1 could eliminate H2O2 and lipid peroxides through GSH-mediated peroxide reduction reactions, thereby reducing intracellular ROS accumulation and maintaining redox homeostasis. These findings suggested that SeCaNP might alleviate oxidative stress by enhancing the GPx1-associated antioxidant defense system. In ROS fluorescence probe staining experiments (Figure 3E and F), CaNP showed higher mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) and stronger fluorescence signals, indicating elevated ROS levels. In contrast, SeCaNP exhibited lower MFI and weaker fluorescence, demonstrating its superior ROS scavenging ability and effective reduction of oxidative stress during osteoclastogenesis.

To further verify that SeCaNP exerts its ROS‑scavenging effect via GPX4, we pretreated cells with ML210, a GPX4 inhibitor, and assessed ROS levels by flow cytometry (Figure 3G and H). The results showed that ML210 pretreatment markedly attenuated the ROS‑reducing effect of SeCaNP. Consistently, the resorption pit assay on bone slices further demonstrated that GPX4 inhibition significantly restored osteoclast activity compared with SeCaNP treatment alone (Figure 3I). Collectively, these findings indicate that SeCaNP inhibits osteoclast function through a GPX4‑dependent mechanism.

Figure 3J and K displayed the fluorescence probe staining of pH across different treatment groups. Both CaNP and SeCaNP exhibited substantial bright green signals, characterized by dense clusters and extensive distribution, with overall pronounced fluorescence indicating their effective acid-neutralizing capacity. In contrast, the Na2SeO3 group exhibited poorer acid-neutralizing capacity. This aligned with the findings presented in Figure 2K and L, confirming that calcium was the primary contributor to the acid-neutralizing capability within SeCaNP. CaNP effectively mitigated acidic conditions by supplying calcium ions, while SeCaNP demonstrated enhanced acid-neutralizing performance under calcium influence.

SeCaNP Promotes Osteogenic Differentiation of BMSCs

The impact of SeCaNP on BMSC proliferation was further scrutinized through CCK-8 analysis. Specifically, we monitored dose-dependent changes in cell vitality after 24 hours of exposure to various calcium concentrations within the media. A calcium concentration of 1.38 μg/mL was identified as the most favorable for promoting cellular performance; consequently, this dosage was employed in all following investigations (Figure S3). As shown in Figure 4A, BMSCs underwent cytoskeletal fluorescent staining to evaluate the effects of different treatments on cell adhesion and morphology. Compared with the control group, clearer cytoskeletal outlines and more complete cell spreading were observed in the Na2SeO3, CaNP, and SeCaNP groups. This indicated that SeCaNP promoted BMSC adhesion and cytoskeletal remodeling, demonstrated favorable biocompatibility, and established a favorable cellular morphological foundation for subsequent osteogenic differentiation.

Subsequently, we assessed the effects on the differentiation of BMSCs. In alkaline medium, SeCaNP treatment yielded larger positive areas and a higher number of alkaline phosphatase (ALP)-stained cells at day 7 (Figure 4B). Quantitative analysis of ALP staining intensity (SeCaNP reached 61.10±26.11%) and ELISA (Figures 4C and S4) indicated that SeCaNP effectively enhances ALP activity in BMSCs, thereby promoting early osteogenic differentiation. Compared with the control group, on day 14, alizarin red staining revealed more pronounced red calcified nodules and increased staining area in the CaNP group (Figure 4D), indicating its ability to promote mineralized matrix formation. Notably, the SeCaNP group exhibited the strongest ARS-positive signal, with significantly increased numbers and volumes of calcified nodules alongside more extensive staining distribution. This indicated that SeCaNP exerted a more pronounced promotional effect on the late-stage osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs (Figure 4D). Quantitative analysis further corroborated these staining findings (Figure 4E).

Next, we analyzed the expression of a panel of osteogenic-specific genes—RUNX2, ALP, OPN, and OCN. As compared to the control group, Na2SeO3, CaNP, and SeCaNP treatments generally enhanced the expression of early and mid-stage osteogenic markers, with SeCaNP exhibiting the most pronounced effect. This indicated that SeCaNP effectively promoted the differentiation of BMSCs toward the osteogenic lineage. Notably, the late-stage mineralization marker OCN exhibited the most pronounced upregulation, with particularly elevated expression levels in the SeCaNP group. Collectively, the data underscore the capacity of SeCaNP to orchestrate a comprehensive osteogenic response, spanning from early lineage specification to subsequent functional maturation and mineral deposition (Figure 4F). As shown in Figure 4G–J, Western blot analysis was employed to detect the expression levels of osteogenesis-related proteins in BMSCs across different treatment groups, including RUNX2, ALP, OPN, and OCN. SeCaNP and CaNP treatment exhibited an overall upward trend in the aforementioned osteogenic marker proteins, with SeCaNP demonstrating a more pronounced band enhancement. This indicated that SeCaNP more effectively promoted the expression of osteogenesis-related proteins in BMSCs. Overall, these findings demonstrated that SeCaNP could activate the osteogenic differentiation program of BMSCs at the protein level, encompassing multiple stages from early transcriptional regulation (RUNX2) to proteins associated with matrix formation and mineralization (ALP, OPN, OCN).

Aiming to decode the regulatory networks governing bone mineralization, we analyzed the BMSC transcriptome using RNA-seq after 7 days of SeCaNP exposure, alongside a non-treated control group. Prior to differential expression analysis, raw transcriptome data underwent standardization, revealing no batch effects between the two sample groups (Figure 5A). Volcano plots and heatmaps demonstrated that 50 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) - comprising both up- and downregulated genes - were identified through comparison between the control and SeCaNP treated groups (Figure 5B and C). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that DEGs predominantly clustered in pathways associated with angiogenesis regulation and sterol biosynthesis (Figure 5D and E). These data suggest that SeCaNP may influence osteogenic differentiation partly through pathways related to angiogenesis regulation and sterol biosynthesis. In MF, upregulated genes were significantly enriched in key molecules such as glycosaminoglycan binding and heparin binding (Figure 5F). These were closely associated with extracellular matrix (ECM) assembly and growth factor recruitment. Furthermore, the marked enrichment of fibronectin binding and cytoskeletal structural components provided molecular evidence for enhanced cell adhesion capacity and cellular morphologic reorganization during osteogenic differentiation. This multi-pathway synergy ultimately enhanced osteogenic differentiation, bone remodeling, and matrix mineralization processes significantly. GSEA analysis revealed that the metabolic and structural transitions exhibited by the SeCaNP group showed strong positive enrichment in GO terms (Figure 5G–I). Pathways associated with cell surface and structural remodeling showed strong positive enrichment. Notably, significant upregulation was observed in the external side of the plasma membrane, membrane rafts, and membrane microdomains. These specialized membrane compartments served as critical platforms for osteogenesis-related signaling pathways (such as Wnt/β-catenin or BMP signaling). Then, Western blotting (Figure 5J and K) validated the signaling pathway changes identified by RNA-seq. SeCaNP treatment markedly upregulated Wnt-3a, β-catenin, p-GSK-3β (Wnt/β-catenin pathway) and BMP-2, p-Smad1/5/8 (BMP/Smad pathway), while total protein levels remained unchanged. The pathway-specific inhibitors WIP-2 (Figure 5J) and DM-3189 (Figure 5K) partially reversed these effects, confirming that SeCaNP promotes BMSC osteogenic differentiation through synergistic activation of both pathways.

SeCaNP Alleviates OVX-Induced Bone Loss in vivo

To further validate the in vivo effects of SeCaNP on osteoporosis, an OVX model was established in female C57BL/6 mice to simulate postmenopausal osteoporosis (Figure 6A). Ovariectomy was performed on day 1; Starting from postoperative day 42 (three times weekly), SeCaNP was continuously injected at the same site until euthanasia on day 70. Bone tissue and relevant samples were subsequently collected. Bone microstructural alterations were assessed via micro-computed tomography (micro-CT), complemented by H&E and Masson staining, immunofluorescence analysis, and hematological testing to systematically evaluate tissue repair and biological safety. As shown in Figure S5A, micro-CT imaging was employed to evaluate bone tissue in sham-operated and ovariectomized mice. The ovariectomized group exhibited reduced bone structural integrity, characterized by a more sparse trabecular area and enlarged lucent zones within the medullary cavity. This demonstrated typical bone mass loss and microstructural degeneration following ovarian removal. Micro-CT quantitative analysis of trabecular microstructural parameters was conducted in sham-operated and OVX mice. The effective replication of the osteoporosis model was evidenced by the marked deterioration of femoral bone quality in OVX mice. As depicted in Figure S5B–S5G, statistical disparities were identified in bone mineral density alongside trabecular microarchitecture indices, confirming a systemic loss of bone mass.

Subsequently, micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) imaging was employed to evaluate mouse femurs across different treatment groups (Figure 6B). Compared to the control group, both Na2SeO3 and CaNP treatments demonstrated a degree of improvement in bone architecture, manifested as increased trabecular bone retention and reduced areas of bone spongiosis. Notably, the SeCaNP group exhibited a denser trabecular network and higher bone tissue connectivity, with overall bone architecture more closely resembling the control group. This indicated that SeCaNP effectively mitigated OVX-associated bone loss and improved femoral bone microarchitecture. Quantitative analysis of bone-related parameters showed that SeCaNP resulted in improvements to trabecular microarchitecture indices. As depicted in Figure 6C–H, Tb.Sp was markedly reduced compared to untreated OVX mice. Specifically, Tb. BS/TV in the SeCaNP group increased significantly from 2.55±0.79 to 4.22±2.16 mm−1 relative to the untreated OVX group. Moreover, other bone-related parameters such as Tb. BV/TV, Tb.N, and Tb.Th demonstrated recovery comparable to that observed in the sham-operated group. Notably, SeCaNP treatment also significantly increased BMD, with femoral BMD values in treated mice reaching levels similar to those in the sham group. These findings further validate the efficacy of SeCaNP in reversing osteoporosis within this model.

Histomorphometric analysis via hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining confirmed that the SeCaNP group retained a robust bone volume and well-organized skeletal framework, closely resembling pre-OVX conditions (Figure 7A). Masson’s trichrome staining further demonstrated that SeCaNP effectively alleviated bone loss (Figure 7B). As shown in Figure 7C, immunofluorescent staining was performed on bone tissue sections from mice in different treatment groups to assess alterations in osteogenesis-related signaling. The sham group exhibited strong and widespread BMP-2 positive signaling; in contrast, BMP-2 fluorescence was markedly diminished in the OVX group, indicating suppression of osteogenesis-related factor expression under estrogen-deficient conditions. Following treatment with Na2SeO3 and CaNP, BMP-2 signaling exhibited a degree of recovery, with the SeCaNP group demonstrating the most pronounced enhancement of BMP-2 positivity and a more continuous distribution pattern. These findings indicate that SeCaNP effectively upregulates BMP-2 expression in the context of OVX-induced osteoporosis. Additionally, quantitative analysis was performed on the immunofluorescence intensity of BMP-2 in bone tissue across all groups. As depicted in Figure 7D, the relative fluorescence intensity of BMP-2 was markedly reduced in the OVX group compared to the Sham group. Na2SeO3, CaNP, and SeCaNP progressively enhanced BMP-2 fluorescence intensity compared to the OVX group, with SeCaNP showing the most pronounced induction. Among these, SeCaNP achieved the most pronounced increase, reaching 0.99 ± 0.18. These findings indicate that SeCaNP effectively elevates BMP-2 expression levels in an OVX context, demonstrating its potential to improve the osteogenic microenvironment.

As shown in Figure 7E, serum BALP levels were measured in each group of mice via ELISA. Compared with the Sham group, BALP levels were significantly reduced in the OVX group, indicating that the ovarian ablation-induced osteoporotic state was accompanied by decreased bone formation activity. Compared with the OVX group, Na2SeO3 and CaNP treatments significantly elevated BALP levels. Notably, the SeCaNP group exhibited the most pronounced increase in BALP levels, reaching 7.42 ± 0.21, which was significantly higher than that in the OVX group. This demonstrates that SeCaNP effectively ameliorates osteogenic-related metabolic status and enhances osteoblastic activity in OVX mice.

Concurrently, we continuously monitored body weight changes across all mouse groups throughout the experimental period (0–70 days) (Figure S6). Body weights across all groups exhibited a gradual upward trend over time, with broadly similar growth trajectories showing no marked abnormal declines or significant fluctuations. Compared to the Sham group, the OVX group demonstrated a certain degree of weight gain in the later stages; weight changes in the Na2SeO3, CaNP, and SeCaNP treatment groups were generally consistent with those of the Sham groups. Results indicate that under the dosing regimen employed in this study, no significant systemic toxicity or pronounced adverse reactions were induced by the treatments, with animals maintaining stable overall physiological status. Furthermore, biochemical blood analysis revealed no significant differences in liver function markers, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), or renal function markers, blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine (CRE), between the SeCaNP treatment group and the sham-operated group (Figure S7A–S7D). In addition, no obvious histological differences were observed in HE‑stained sections of the major organs (Figure S8). These findings demonstrate the in vivo safety profile of SeCaNP therapy.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated the potential of SeCaNP in treating osteoporosis. Our findings indicate that SeCaNP overcomes limitations in osteoporosis therapy by alleviating local acidification, reducing oxidative stress, inhibiting excessive osteoclast activity, and significantly promoting osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, thereby offering a promising approach to address existing treatment challenges.

In vitro experiments confirmed that SeCaNP promoted GPx1 protein expression, which further inhibited osteoclastogenesis. What’s more, SeCaNP significantly enhanced the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, as evidenced by increased ALP activity, formation of mineralized nodules, and expression of key osteogenic proteins including RUNX2, OPN, and OCN. These findings underscore the pivotal role of SeCaNP in anti-osteoporotic. Regarding in vivo validation, SeCaNP demonstrated clear therapeutic potential in an OVX-induced osteoporosis animal model. SeCaNP effectively mitigated bone loss and promoted bone formation in OVX mice. Micro-CT analysis revealed significant improvements in bone microarchitecture, with histological evidence of bone formation further corroborating these findings. This confirms that SeCaNP attenuated bone loss through a dual-regulatory mechanism that inhibits bone resorption and promotes bone formation.

Collectively, these results indicate that SeCaNP exerts a dual‑regulatory effect by inhibiting bone resorption (via GPX4‑mediated ROS scavenging and acid neutralization) and promoting bone formation (via Wnt/β‑catenin and BMP/Smad pathways), contributing to the amelioration of osteoporotic bone loss. The pH‑responsive release and acid‑buffering properties further endow SeCaNP with a microenvironment‑targeting advantage that distinguishes it from conventional systemic agents.

In summary, our findings support SeCaNP as a promising microenvironment‑modulating biomaterial for preclinical anti‑osteoporotic therapy. However, as a proof‑of‑concept study, several limitations should be acknowledged. The current work lacks pharmacokinetic and biodistribution data, and the long‑term safety and efficacy of SeCaNP remain to be determined. Additionally, while the local injection route was chosen for mechanistic validation, systemic administration or bone‑targeting modifications (eg, via bisphosphonate conjugation) would be more clinically relevant for treating osteoporosis as a systemic disease, and these strategies merit exploration in future work. Future studies extended observation periods, and thorough toxicological evaluation are warranted to assess the translational potential of this nanomaterial. And a full panel of bone turnover markers (CTX‑I, TRACP‑5b, and P1NP) will be included in our future studies to enable a more holistic evaluation of bone remodeling. Despite these limitations, the present study provides a solid foundation for further preclinical development and optimization of SeCaNP‑based therapeutic strategies for osteoporosis.
