Identification of the nsLTP gene family from sugar beet and functional analysis of BvnsLTP10 in drought tolerance
Authors: Yanni Xu, Ronghua Shi, Ziqi Fan, Yingnan Mu, Yaqing Sun, Ningning Li, Shaoying Zhang, Guolong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Non-specific lipid transfer proteins (nsLTPs) participate in lipid-related processes and plant responses to abiotic stress, yet their roles in drought tolerance in sugar beet remain largely unclear. In this study, a genome-wide analysis identified 15 nsLTP genes in sugar beet, distributed across six chromosomes, and promoter analysis revealed multiple cis-acting elements associated with abiotic stress responses. Among these genes, BvnsLTP10 exhibits sustained up-regulation in leaves and peak induction in roots and stems under PEG-induced drought. Subcellular localization analysis showed that BvnsLTP10 is a secreted protein localized to the cell wall/apoplast, suggesting a role in extracellular lipid transport. Functional characterization in transgenic Arabidopsis demonstrated that BvnsLTP10 overexpression enhanced drought tolerance, as evidenced by increased fresh weight (1.85-fold), relative water content (1.41-fold), chlorophyll content (+ 36%), and Fv/Fm (+ 19.6%), along with elevated antioxidant enzyme activities (POD + 65.1%, SOD + 37.5%, CAT + 54.3%) and reduced electrolyte leakage (− 17.63%). SEM observations indicated potential changes in leaf and stem surface structures, and coordinated upregulation of wax biosynthesis and transport genes (KCS1, CER1, CER3, WSD1, FAR3, ABCG11; ~ 20–40-fold) under drought stress, suggesting a possible involvement of wax-related pathways. These findings indicate that BvnsLTP10 enhances drought tolerance by improving plant water status, strengthening antioxidant defense systems, and promoting extracellular structural modifications. Notably, the functional validation was conducted in Arabidopsis thaliana rather than sugar beet, which may limit direct functional extrapolation to the crop species. Nevertheless, BvnsLTP10 represents a promising candidate gene for future studies aimed at enhancing drought resilience in sugar beet.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09095-4.

Introduction

As a biennial herb of the Chenopodiaceae family, Beta vulgaris L. is a major economic crop contributing significantly to sugar production, with extensive cultivation occurring in northern China's arid and semi-arid regions. In addition to serving as a primary raw material for the sugar industry, sugar beet by-products are used in animal feed as well as in the creation of biofuels such as methanol, ethanol, butanol, glycerol, and acetone, highlighting its significant economic value and application potential [1, 2]. Abiotic stress factors, such as drought, salinity, extreme temperatures, and heavy metal toxicity, pose significant challenges to crop growth and productivity [3, 4], among which drought is particularly critical for sugar beet. Limited water availability, low and uneven annual precipitation, and regional water-use restrictions have made drought a major constraint on sugar beet cultivation in China [5, 6]. Therefore, identifying and utilizing drought-responsive genes and developing drought-tolerant cultivars are essential to mitigate drought stress and support the sustainable expansion of sugar beet production.

During evolution, plants have developed diverse drought-responsive mechanisms at morphological, physiological, cellular, and molecular levels. These include structural modifications, scavenging of reactive oxygen species (ROS), regulation of ion and osmotic homeostasis, differential gene expression, and metabolic adjustments [7–13]. Lipids, as essential secondary metabolites, play key roles in stress responses by participating in cellular signaling, preserving membrane integrity, and contributing to cuticle and cell wall formation during plant growth and development [14]. Non-specific lipid transfer proteins (nsLTPs) are small, basic proteins that are rich in cysteine and found extensively in higher plants. They play important roles in lipid transport, cuticular wax deposition, and cutin biosynthesis, and also participate in plant responses to abiotic stresses such as drought and salinity [15–18]. Functional studies in several plant species indicate that nsLTPs contribute to drought tolerance through conserved mechanisms such as enhancing ROS scavenging, maintaining membrane stability, and regulating osmotic balance. In transgenic tobacco, overexpression of NtLTP4 mitigates drought stress by reducing transpiration rates and enhancing ROS scavenging [19]. In transgenic maize, overexpression of ZmLTP3 improves drought resistance by enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity, reducing membrane damage, and increasing chlorophyll content under drought conditions [20]. GmLtpI.3 notably enhances the ability of transgenic soybean hairy roots and Arabidopsis to withstand drought by modulating ROS signaling and preserving intracellular osmotic potential [21]. In colobanthus quitensis, endophytic fungi-mediated expression of CqLTP1 and CqLTP2 contributes to enhanced drought tolerance [22]. Meanwhile, species-specific roles have also been reported, GhLTP4 improves the drought resistance of cotton through the modification of lipid profiles, regulation of abscisic acid (ABA) balance, and enhancement of the tricarboxylic acid cycle [23]. Mutation of OsLTPG22 in rice reduces cuticular wax accumulation and increases leaf permeability, thereby increasing drought sensitivity [24]. Under PEG-induced stress, sugarcane enhances drought tolerance by upregulating ScNsLTP expression [25]. Drought stress induces the differential upregulation of StLTP1 and StLTP7 in potato root, stem, and leaf tissues [26].

nsLTPs play significant roles in plant responses to drought stress; however, their characteristics and specific functions in sugar beet remain largely unexplored. In particular, little is known about which nsLTP genes may contribute to drought tolerance in this crop. This study aimed to systematically characterize the BvnsLTP genes family in sugar beet, including their physicochemical properties, chromosomal locations, gene structures, phylogenetic relationships, and expression profiles. Based on its relatively sustained induction under drought stress, BvnsLTP10 was selected for further functional investigation. These findings provide valuable genetic resources and theoretical insights for developing drought-tolerant sugar beet germplasm and advancing precision molecular breeding.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

All plant materials utilized in this study were cultivated in controlled climate chambers (16/8 h light/dark, 24/20°Cday/night). Materials included the drought-tolerant sugar beet cultivar HI0466(a sugar beet variety developed by Syngenta (Switzerland), was selected for its stable agronomic traits and strong drought tolerance) [27], Arabidopsis thaliana (Columbia-0 wild-type (WT) and overexpression lines OE-1, OE-2, and OE-3). HI0466 seeds were planted in trays filled with a mixture of soil and vermiculite at a 3:1 (v/v) ratio. Treatments began at the two-true-leaf stage. Seedlings in the control group were well-watered, while experimental seedlings were subjected to various abiotic stresses. Drought tolerance was the primary focus of this study, so PEG-induced osmotic stress was used as the main screening condition. For PEG treatment, seedlings were treated with 30% (w/v) PEG-6000 solution, corresponding to an approximate osmotic potential of –0.6 MPa, with 30 mL of solution added per pot without replacement, and stress was maintained for 48 h. Leaf, root, and stem tissues were sampled at 0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, and 48 h after the onset of PEG treatment. Other stress treatments were included for comparative reference: 50 μM abscisic acid (ABA), 600 mM NaCl, and high/low temperature stress at 40 °C or 4 °C, each applied for 48 h. For these treatments, only leaf tissues were sampled at the same time points (0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, and 48 h). All treatments were performed in three independent biological replicates, with untreated seedlings maintained under standard growth conditions as controls.

Identification of BvnsLTP gene family members

To systematically identify the BvnsLTP gene family in sugar beet, the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profile PF00234 was first retrieved from the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org; accessed 21 April 2025). We then searched the sugar beet genomic protein database (https://plants.ensembl.org/index.html) using HMMER 3.3.2 with an E-value cutoff < 1e − 5 to preliminarily identify candidate nsLTP proteins. Domain validation of all candidate proteins was further performed using both the Pfam and SMART databases (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de; accessed 22 April 2025). Physicochemical characteristics of the confirmed proteins were analyzed with ExPASy ProtParam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/), and the sequences were examined in DNAman to confirm the presence of the canonical eight-cysteine motif characteristic of nsLTPs. Signal peptides were predicted using SignalP (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/SignalP), and subcellular localization was assessed with DeepLoc-2.1 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/DeepLoc-2.1/).The Beta vulgaris genome assembly (RefBeet-1.2) and its corresponding annotation file (version 2.61, GFF3 format) were obtained from the Beta vulgaris Database.

Phylogenetic tree construction

To clarify the evolutionary classification and relationships of BvnsLTPs, multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis of nsLTP protein sequences were conducted using MEGA 11 software (Phylogenetic trees were constructed in MEGA 11 using the Neighbor-Joining method with the Poisson model, pairwise deletion for gaps, and bootstrap analysis with 1000 replicates to assess branch reliability). A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor-Joining method and visualized using the iTOL online platform (https://itol.embl.de/).

Sequence analysis of the BvnsLTP family

Conserved motifs in BvnsLTP proteins were analyzed with the MEME suite (max motifs = 10, motif width = 6–50 amino acids, and maximum E-value = 1e-5.) (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme). Cis-acting elements within the 2000 bp promoter regions upstream of BvnsLTP translation start sites were predicted using PlantCARE and visualized in R (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/).

Chromosomal distribution and gene duplication analysis of BvnsLTPs

The physical positions of BvnsLTP genes on chromosomes were mapped using TBtools. To investigate gene duplication events and homologous relationships, orthologous gene pairs across multiple species were identified using BLASTP, and synteny analysis was performed using the MCScanX method implemented in TBtools to identify whole-genome duplication (WGD) or segmental duplication events.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Following cDNA synthesis from extracted RNA (Accurate Biotechnology, China), the product was diluted fourfold with nuclease-free water and used as a template for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis on a Bio-Rad platform. Each sample included three independent biological replicates, and each biological replicate was measured in three technical replicates. BvACTIN and AtACTIN were used as reference genes, with primers listed in Supplementary. Relative expression of BvnsLTP genes was calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method.

Subcellular localization

The recombinant plasmid pBWA(V)HS-BvnsLTP10–GFP and the corresponding Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 cells were transformed with an empty vector by electroporation, cultured on solid YEB medium at 30 °C for two days, and then inoculated into 20 mL of liquid YEB medium for further growth. Cultures were shaken at 170 rpm until the optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) reached approximately 0.6. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation and then resuspended in an infiltration buffer composed of 10 mM MgCl₂ and 120 μM acetosyringone. The abaxial sides of leaves from healthy Nicotiana benthamiana plants were infiltrated with the bacterial suspension. After incubation under low-light conditions for 2 days, leaves exhibiting GFP fluorescence were selected for observation.

For plasmolysis, selected leaves were infiltrated with 20% sucrose solution using a pipette. Leaf segments were then excised and placed on a microscope slide, with a few drops of sucrose solution added to maintain turgor. After incubation for 20 min, a coverslip was gently applied, and cells were observed under a Nikon C2-ER confocal laser scanning microscope. Excitation and emission wavelengths were as follows: GFP (Ex 488 nm/Em 510 nm), NAA60mKate (Ex 561 nm/Em 580 nm).

Identification of transgenic arabidopsis

Arabidopsis thaliana was transformed via the Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated floral dip method. A total of 15 independent T₁ transgenic lines were obtained. Transgenic seedlings were initially selected on MS medium containing 15 μg/mL hygromycin, and PCR analysis of genomic DNA was performed to confirm transgene integration. Among these, three independent T₂ lines exhibiting high transgene expression levels—at least fivefold higher than wild-type plants as determined by qRT-PCR—were selected for subsequent phenotypic analysis under drought stress conditions. T₂ seeds from individual lines were collected for further experiments.

Physiological index measurements

Osmotic stress was simulated using 200 mM mannitol. Twenty seeds each of WT and overexpression lines were sown on MS medium with or without mannitol. Germination rates were recorded after 13 days of growth under light conditions.

Uniformly grown wild-type and BvnsLTP10-overexpressing Arabidopsis thaliana lines were selected for drought experiments to ensure comparable physiological conditions. Control plants were well-watered throughout the experiment. For the experimental group, soil water content was initially maintained at approximately 75% of field capacity, and drought stress was imposed by withholding water for 10 days until soil water content decreased to approximately 32%. Experiments were conducted under standard growth conditions at 22 °C with a relative humidity of approximately 50% and a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod. Following the stress period, phenotypic evaluations were conducted, and leaf samples were harvested for subsequent physiological analyses. Fresh weight was measured gravimetrically. The fresh weight (M1) was immediately measured, after which the leaves were immersed in distilled water for 10 h until fully saturated. Surface moisture was gently removed using filter paper, and the saturated weight (M2) was recorded. Leaves were dried in an oven to measure the dry weight (M3), and RWC was determined according to the equation: RWC (%) = [(M1 − M3)/(M2 − M3)] × 100. Leaf and stem samples from transgenic Arabidopsis plants, collected after drought treatment, were mounted on scanning electron microscopy (SEM) stubs for surface observation and imaging. Lipid coverage was quantified from the SEM images using ImageJ software. Relative electrolyte leakage was measured using a DDSJ318T conductivity meter. Approximately 0.1 g of fresh leaf tissue was placed in a mortar with a small amount of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) and 10 mL of 90% ethanol, then thoroughly ground. The mixture was filtered through filter paper into a brown volumetric flask, and the filtrate was used to measure absorbance at 663 nm and 645 nm for chlorophyll determination. Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters (Fv/Fm) were determined with a FluorPen handheld fluorometer. Peroxidase (POD), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT) activities were measured using commercial assay kits (Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols with adaptations to improve reproducibility. Briefly, approximately 0.1 g of fully expanded Arabidopsis rosette leaves were first homogenized in liquid nitrogen. The powdered tissue was then transferred to 1 mL of ice-cold extraction buffer provided in the kit and thoroughly mixed. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for enzyme assays. POD activity was determined by monitoring the increase in absorbance at 420 nm due to guaiacol oxidation. SOD activity was measured based on the inhibition of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) photoreduction at 560 nm. CAT activity was assessed by monitoring the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide at 405 nm. Enzyme activities were calculated according to the kit instructions and expressed per gram of fresh weight. All assays were performed with three biological replicates, each with three technical replicates. Fresh leaf samples were homogenized and processed according to the kit protocols. All steps, including preparation of extraction buffers, reaction mixtures, incubation times, and temperatures, were carried out strictly as described by the manufacturer to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with three biological replicates (n = 3), each including three technical replicates. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software. For multi-factor experiments, such as physiological measurements under CK and drought conditions, statistical differences between WT and OE lines under the same treatment were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test. For single-factor experiments performed only under drought stress, statistical differences between two groups were assessed using two-tailed Student’s t-test. Statistical significance was considered at P < 0.05. Raw data supporting the findings of this study are available in the Figshare repository under 10.6084/m9.figshare.31566403.

Chromosomal localization of BvnsLTP genes in sugar beet

Sequence analysis of BvnsLTP proteins revealed a length range of 105 to 130 amino acids, with BvnsLTP8 and BvnsLTP2 representing the shortest and longest variants, respectively. The predicted isoelectric points ranged from 5.52 (BvnsLTP7) to 9.64 (BvnsLTP13). Hydrophobicity assessment indicated that all BvnsLTP proteins were hydrophobic, except for BvnsLTP7. Subcellular localization prediction suggested that all BvnsLTP proteins are localized to the extracellular space (Table S1).

Multiple sequence alignment revealed a conserved eight-cysteine motif characteristic of nsLTPs. SignalP analysis indicated that most BvnsLTP proteins possess an N-terminal signal peptide with cleavage sites between residues 24 and 30. In addition, putative calmodulin-binding sites were detected in several members, including BvnsLTP3 and BvnsLTP9–BvnsLTP15 (Figure S1).

Structural characteristics and promoter cis-acting element analysis of BvnsLTP genes

Analysis of conserved motifs, protein domains, and gene structures revealed that all BvnsLTP genes contain at least two highly conserved motifs (Motif 2 and Motif 3), which are strongly conserved across family members (Fig. 2A). Conserved domain analysis indicated that BvnsLTP proteins possess nsLTP1 and nsLTP-like domains (Fig. 2B), supporting their classification as non-specific lipid transfer proteins. Gene structure analysis further showed that, with the exception of BvnsLTP8, the exon–intron organization was highly conserved within the gene family (Fig. 2C).Fig. 2Structural characteristics and promoter cis-acting element analysis of BvnsLTP genes in Beta vulgaris L. To further explore the potential regulatory mechanisms underlying the abiotic stress responses of BvnsLTP genes, the 2,000 bp upstream promoter regions were analyzed for cis-acting regulatory elements. Multiple hormone-responsive and stress-associated motifs were identified, including ABA-responsive elements (ABRE) and drought-related elements such as MBS, as well as MYB and MYC binding sites. In addition, cis-elements associated with other hormonal pathways, including MeJA and gibberellin (GA) responsiveness, were also detected (Fig. 2D). These findings suggest that BvnsLTP genes may be regulated by complex hormonal and environmental signaling networks.

Synteny analysis of nsLTPs among different species

To investigate the evolutionary conservation of nsLTP genes, collinearity analysis was first performed between sugar beet and the model species Arabidopsis and rice (Fig. 3). The results showed that BvnsLTP9, BvnsLTP10, and BvnsLTP11 exhibited syntenic relationships with rice, while BvnsLTP10 also showed synteny with Arabidopsis. To further explore their evolutionary patterns within Chenopodiaceae, synteny analysis was conducted with spinach (Spinacia oleracea) and quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa). In spinach, most identified gene pairs showed a one-to-one collinear relationship with sugar beet. In quinoa, several genes, including BvnsLTP2, BvnsLTP6, and BvnsLTP7, exhibited one-to-two syntenic relationships, consistent with its allotetraploid genome structure. Phylogenetic relationships of BvnsLTPs

To investigate the evolutionary relationships of the BvnsLTP gene family, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using nsLTP protein sequences from sugar beet, rice, maize, Arabidopsis, and tobacco, as well as closely related Chenopodiaceae species including spinach and quinoa (Fig. 4). A total of 282 nsLTP proteins were identified and classified into 25 subgroups. Members from different species were widely distributed across these subgroups, indicating conserved evolutionary relationships among nsLTP genes across plant species. The BvnsLTP proteins were unevenly distributed among subgroups, with enrichment in groups II, VI, XI, XVI, XIX, XXI, XXIV, and XXV, suggesting subgroup-level variation within this gene family. Response of BvnsLTP genes to exogenous ABA and abiotic stress

The expression patterns of BvnsLTP genes in sugar beet leaves under PEG-induced drought treatment were analyzed using RT-qPCR. All 15 genes responded to drought stress, most showing only transient induction at specific time points (Fig. 5). Among them, BvnsLTP10 exhibited a relatively sustained up-regulation, gradually increasing over time and reaching a maximum at 48 h (approximately 14-fold). In contrast, some genes, such as BvnsLTP11 and BvnsLTP12, showed transient early peaks (e.g., 4 h and 8 h) followed by rapid declines. Tissue-specific analysis revealed that BvnsLTP10 expression in roots increased gradually, reaching higher levels at 20 h (2.32), whereas in stems it was induced at 8 h (2.53) and peaked at 12 h (5.26), approximately fivefold higher than the control. Expression under other abiotic treatments, including NaCl, ABA, and temperature stress, was also evaluated. Several family members showed transient induction, but BvnsLTP10 did not display sustained high expression under these conditions (Supplementary S2). Subcellular localization of BvnsLTP10

To clarify the subcellular localization of BvnsLTP10, we performed co-localization analysis with the plasma membrane marker NAA60. In untreated tobacco leaf epidermal cells, BvnsLTP10-GFP signals largely overlapped with NAA60-mKate at the cell periphery (Fig. 6B). Following plasmolysis induced by 20% (w/v) sucrose (Fig. 6C), most BvnsLTP10 signals contracted with the plasma membrane, but substantial enrichment was observed along Hechtian strands connecting the plasma membrane and the cell wall (blue arrows). BvnsLTP10 enhances seed germination as well as seedling development in drought conditions

To assess the response of BvnsLTP10-overexpressing plants during early development, seeds of transgenic Arabidopsis lines and WT plants were germinated on MS medium supplemented with or without 200 mM mannitol to impose osmotic stress. Under normal MS conditions, no significant differences in germination rate or seedling growth were observed between transgenic lines and WT plants (Fig. 7A, C), indicating that overexpression of BvnsLTP10 does not affect early development under non-stress conditions. Under mannitol-induced osmotic stress, seed germination of BvnsLTP10-overexpressing lines was less inhibited compared with WT plants. Three independent transgenic lines exhibited germination rates of 92%, 80%, and 75% at day 13, respectively. However, post-germination seedling development showed no pronounced differences between transgenic and WT plants (Fig. 7B, D). These results indicate that BvnsLTP10 enhances tolerance to mannitol-induced osmotic stress during seed germination. To explore the potential involvement of wax-related pathways in drought tolerance, SEM observations suggested that BvnsLTP10-overexpressing lines may have denser wax crystals on leaves and stems compared with WT plants (Supplementary S4). Discussion

nsLTPs are small, basic proteins crucial for plant growth, development, and environmental stress responses. A genome-wide analysis identified 15 BvnsLTP genes in sugar beet, which are unevenly distributed across six chromosomes—a pattern similar to that reported in cotton [28]. These genes are densely clustered on chromosomes 1, 7, and 9, but are sparsely present on others, suggesting the influence of tandem gene duplication and selective environmental pressures.

Protein characterization revealed that BvnsLTPs are relatively small (7–10 kDa) and possess basic properties. Sequence analysis predicted an N-terminal signal peptide in BvnsLTP10, supporting its classification as a secreted protein. Subsequent bioinformatic predictions suggested that BvnsLTP10 is likely localized to the extracellular space. Consistently, experimental observations indicated that BvnsLTP10 primarily localizes to the cell periphery and becomes enriched along Hechtian strands following plasmolysis, which may reflect a potential role at the interface between the plasma membrane and the cell wall. Such spatial distribution aligns with previous reports showing that nsLTPs mediate the extracellular transport of hydrophobic molecules, including lipids and wax precursors, from the cytoplasm to the plant surface [29]. Collectively, these observations suggest that BvnsLTP10 may contribute to cuticle assembly and plant stress responses, although further experimental validation is required to confirm its precise functional roles.

Motif and gene structure analyses revealed that BvnsLTPs contain highly conserved motifs, with most genes harboring 0–2 introns in conserved positions, consistent with nsLTPs in other plants. The promoter regions of BvnsLTP genes contain several putative cis-acting regulatory elements, including ABRE (abscisic acid-responsive element), MBS (MYB-binding site associated with drought responsiveness), LTR (low-temperature responsive element), and DRE (dehydration-responsive element involved in osmotic stress signaling). These elements suggest that BvnsLTP genes may be regulated by multiple hormonal and environmental cues. Promoter–GUS fusion analysis and MUG enzymatic assays are widely recognized approaches for experimentally characterizing promoter activity, as they enable both spatial visualization and quantitative assessment of transcriptional regulation. However, in the present study, promoter analysis was performed solely based on in silico prediction of cis-elements, and no reporter gene assays were conducted. Therefore, the regulatory implications inferred from cis-element composition should be considered as preliminary and putative. Further experimental validation will be required to clarify their functional roles in transcriptional regulation.

Comparative analysis indicated that the sugar beet nsLTP gene family comprises 15 members, which is smaller than that in Arabidopsis and rice, suggesting differential gene family expansion among plant lineages. This reduced family size in Beta vulgaris may be associated with lineage-specific gene loss or limited whole-genome duplication events during evolution [30]. Tandem duplication events were identified for several BvnsLTP genes, particularly on chromosomes 1, 7, and 9. These local duplication events may have contributed to gene clustering and represent an important mechanism driving gene family maintenance and diversification in sugar beet. To better understand the evolutionary history of nsLTP genes, phylogenetic and synteny analyses were further extended to closely related Chenopodiaceae species, including spinach (Spinacia oleracea) and quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa). Compared with Arabidopsis and rice, the inclusion of these species provides a more relevant evolutionary framework for sugar beet. Phylogenetic analysis showed that nsLTP proteins from different species were widely interspersed across subgroups, indicating likely early diversification prior to species divergence. Within Chenopodiaceae, several BvnsLTP genes clustered closely with homologs from spinach and quinoa, supporting a conserved evolutionary origin within this lineage. Synteny analysis further revealed that most gene pairs in spinach exhibited a one-to-one correspondence with sugar beet, whereas quinoa showed a one-to-two pattern for several genes, consistent with its allotetraploid genome structure. In addition, BvnsLTP9 and BvnsLTP11 were located in close proximity on sugar beet chromosome 7 and corresponded to adjacent homologous loci in quinoa, suggesting partial conservation of local genomic organization. However, BvnsLTP10 lacked detectable syntenic counterparts in both spinach and quinoa, which may reflect lineage-specific divergence or differences in current genome assembly and annotation quality. Overall, these results suggest that the evolution of the nsLTP gene family in Chenopodiaceae has been shaped by a combination of tandem duplication, polyploidization, and lineage-specific gene retention or loss, resulting in both conserved and diversified gene members across species. Notably, plant nsLTPs are often classified under the PR-14 pathogenesis-related protein family and are implicated in biotic and abiotic stress responses through roles in lipid transport and cell wall-associated processes [31]. BvnsLTP10 also showed orthologous relationships with Arabidopsis and rice, suggesting evolutionary conservation despite the absence of detectable synteny in spinach and quinoa. Taken together, these results highlight that nsLTP genes in Chenopodiaceae have undergone both evolutionary conservation and divergence, reflecting complex but traceable patterns of gene retention and functional diversification during plant evolution.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that nsLTPs contribute to abiotic stress tolerance in diverse plant species, often through modulation of antioxidant systems, lipid metabolism, and signaling pathways. For example, cotton GhLTP4 [23] and chestnut CmnsLTP6.9 [18] enhance drought tolerance by elevating antioxidant enzyme activities and regulating lipid metabolism, while wheat TaLTP40 and TaLTP75 improve salt tolerance via enhanced membrane lipid trafficking [32]. In tobacco, NtLTPI.38 confers salt tolerance through a combination of lipid content modulation, Ca2⁺ signaling, ABA pathway regulation, and ion channel gene expression [33]. Similarly, maize ZmLTP3 enhances salt stress tolerance by suppressing ROS accumulation and increasing proline content [34], soybean GsLTP improves drought and salt tolerance via proline biosynthesis [35]. Chrysanthemum DgnsLTP contributes to cold tolerance by increasing peroxidase (POD) activity and reducing ROS levels [36], and wheat TdLTP2 enhances salt stress tolerance via elevated antioxidant enzyme accumulation [37].

In this study, qRT-PCR analysis showed that multiple BvnsLTP genes were transcriptionally responsive to various abiotic treatments, including PEG-induced osmotic stress, salinity, temperature stress, and ABA treatment, suggesting their potential roles in stress adaptation. Similar stress-responsive expression patterns of nsLTPs have been reported in other plant species. For example, nsLTP genes in wheat are strongly induced by salt and drought stress [31], and repeated dehydration leads to nearly sixfold upregulation of nsLTPs in tobacco. Among the BvnsLTP family members, BvnsLTP10 exhibited the strongest and most consistent induction under PEG-induced osmotic stress, indicating that it may play a prominent role in water-deficit–related stress responses.

Functional analyses in transgenic Arabidopsis indicated that overexpression of BvnsLTP10 enhances drought tolerance, accompanied by both structural and physiological changes. Under drought stress, BvnsLTP10-overexpressing plants exhibited improved seed germination and increased cuticular wax deposition, along with elevated expression of several genes involved in wax biosynthesis and transport, including KCS1, CER1, CER3, WSD1, FAR3, and ABCG11. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) provides high-resolution visualization of epidermal surface morphology and cuticular wax crystal structures. However, it is important to note that SEM analysis is limited to surface features and does not allow direct measurement of cuticle thickness or internal ultrastructural organization. Therefore, interpretations in this study are based on alterations in surface wax crystal morphology rather than quantitative changes in cuticle thickness. Further validation using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) or cross-sectional anatomical analysis would be required to accurately assess cuticle thickness. In addition, the transgenic lines showed higher activities of antioxidant enzymes and reduced levels of oxidative damage, suggesting enhanced protection against drought-induced oxidative stress. Although overexpression of BvnsLTP10 is correlated with increased expression of wax-related genes and wax accumulation, direct biochemical evidence demonstrating a primary regulatory role of BvnsLTP10 in wax biosynthesis is currently lacking. Further studies are needed to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms by which BvnsLTP10 may influence wax metabolism.

Furthermore, several ABA-responsive genes and ABA metabolism-related genes showed stronger induction in BvnsLTP10-overexpressing plants under drought stress. Given that the current study did not directly assess ABA levels or ABA signaling activity, these transcriptional changes should be interpreted with caution. It is possible that the altered expression of these ABA-related genes reflects downstream responses associated with improved drought tolerance rather than a direct regulatory role of BvnsLTP10 in the ABA signaling pathway.

Functional analyses in transgenic Arabidopsis indicated that overexpression of BvnsLTP10 under drought stress is associated with increased antioxidant enzyme activities, elevated expression of wax biosynthesis- and transport-related genes, and upregulation of ABA-responsive and ABA biosynthesis-related genes. These observations suggest a potential role of BvnsLTP10 in drought responses, as it is correlated with changes in antioxidant activity, wax-related gene expression, and ABA-responsive pathways, but its precise function remains to be validated. Similar antioxidant and stress-responsive effects have been reported for nsLTPs in other plant species, indicating that some aspects of nsLTP-mediated stress tolerance are conserved. Additional mechanisms, such as effects on membrane stability or modulation of stress signaling pathways, may also contribute to the observed drought tolerance, but these were not directly evaluated in this study. Finally, although functional validation in Arabidopsis may not fully reflect BvnsLTP10 activity in sugar beet due to potential species-specific differences, these findings highlight the potential of BvnsLTP10 as a candidate gene for improving drought tolerance in crops, and future studies in sugar beet will be necessary to evaluate its applicability in breeding programs aimed at enhancing stress resilience.
