Development and Validation of a Clinical Prognostic Risk Score for Stillbirth in Ethiopia: A Cohort Study Using PMA Data
Authors: Chalie Mulugeta, Tadele Emagneneh, Belay Susu, Aynalem Yetwale, Adem Yesuf, Nigus Bililign Yimer, Abebaw Alamrew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundStillbirth remain a major public health concern, particularly in low‐resource settings. Early identification of women at risk can significantly improve neonatal outcomes. The data were exported to R version 4.4.3 for cleaning and analysis. Introduction

Stillbirth remains a major global public health challenge, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries. Recent estimates indicate that approximately 2 million stillbirths occur each year worldwide, with a global stillbirth rate of about 18.4 per 1000 births [1, 2], The World Health Organization's (WHO's) Every Newborn: An Action Plan To End Preventable Deaths aims to reduce the stillbirth rate to 12 or fewer per 1000 births by 2030 in every country, and for countries already meeting this target to reduce equity gaps [3].

The burden of stillbirth is disproportionately high in sub‐Saharan Africa, where the estimated rate is 21.7 per 1000 births, compared with approximately 3.0 per 1000 births in high‐income regions such as North America [4]. In addition, 8 of the 10 countries with the highest global stillbirth burden are located in this region [5]. Although rates vary across countries due to economic, cultural, and health system differences, conflict‐affected settings tend to experience the highest stillbirth rates [6]. Accurate estimation and prevention of stillbirths in the region are hindered by limited health facility infrastructure, shortages of skilled health professionals, and inadequate emergency obstetric care [7]. Furthermore, disparities in healthcare access, particularly among rural and socioeconomically disadvantaged women, along with underreporting of home deliveries, stigma, and cultural perceptions surrounding stillbirth further complicate the true assessment of the burden [1, 8, 9],

Within this context, Ethiopia bears a substantial share of the stillbirth burden in sub‐Saharan Africa. Previous studies in the country have identified a range of maternal, obstetric, and socio‐demographic risk factors associated with stillbirth, including low birth weight [10, 11], preeclampsia [10, 12], preterm birth [10, 13, 14], mode of delivery [15, 16], rural residence [17, 18], lack of antenatal care follow‐up [17, 19], Illiteracy [13, 20], multiple pregnancy [21], premature rupture of membranes [20, 22], obstructed labor [22], advanced maternal age [19], labor length ≥ 24 h [11, 14, 18, 23], and the prevalence of infectious diseases [20]. These risk factors are often interrelated and more prevalent in rural areas, where delays in seeking and receiving care remain common [24]. Beyond the clinical burden, stillbirth also has profound psychological and social consequences for affected women, including grief, depression, stigma, and reduced quality of life [25].

Despite the identification of these risk factors, translating this knowledge into practical tools for early risk identification remains limited. Existing studies in Ethiopia and similar settings have largely focused on individual risk factors rather than developing and validating comprehensive prediction models. Therefore, there is a critical need for context‐specific prognostic models that can support early identification of high‐risk pregnancies and guide targeted interventions. The availability of nationally representative datasets, the Performance Monitoring for Action (PMA) Ethiopia survey, provides a valuable opportunity to address this gap. Hence, this study aimed to develop and internally validate a prognostic risk score for stillbirth among women in Ethiopia using PMA 2021 data.

Methods

Setting

Ethiopia is the tenth most populous countries in the world and the second most populous African countries next to Nigeria with an estimated population of 120 million [26], which comprises 12 national regional states, and two chartered administrative cities (Addis Ababa, Dire Dawa). The number of health facilities in Ethiopia increases over time. Currently, there are about 17,534 health posts, 3587 health centers, 3643 hospitals, 3867 private clinics, and 43 private hospitals in the country [27].

Data Sources and Data Collection Procedure

This study used secondary data from the Performance Monitoring for Action (PMA) Ethiopia cohort 2 longitudinal survey, conducted between 2019 and 2021 by Addis Ababa University, the Ethiopian Federal Ministry of Health, and Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health. PMA Ethiopia is a nationally representative survey of women aged 15–49 that collects information on reproductive, maternal, and newborn health indicators [28]. The survey employed multistage stratified cluster sampling across five predominantly agrarian regions (Oromia, Amhara, Tigray, Afar, and SNNP) and one urban region (Addis Ababa). Eligible women provided verbal consent before enrollment, and experienced female enumerators collected data using a smartphone application. The data tool is found in this link: https://doi.org/10.34976/8r5s-dx31.

Study Design

This study utilized a secondary data analysis approach to develop and evaluate a predictive model for identifying risk factors associated with stillbirth in Ethiopia. The analysis was based on data from the Performance Monitoring for Action (PMA) survey (2019–2021), a nationally representative dataset that provides insights into maternal health, and healthcare access. The cohort followed women from pregnancy through the postpartum period, enabling the assessment of maternal characteristics and delivery‐related factors prior to the occurrence of birth outcomes.

The conceptual framework assumes that the occurrence of stillbirth is influenced by multiple maternal, behavioral, and intrapartum factors measured before or during the labor process. In this study, predictors were obtained from different stages of pregnancy and childbirth, including antenatal and intrapartum. The model is intended to support risk assessment during late pregnancy and labor, rather than early antenatal screening.

The theoretical design [29] of the study assumes that the occurrence of stillbirth (SB) at a future time “t” is a function of multiple maternal, demographic, and healthcare‐related factors measured at an earlier time point “t0”.

SB (t0 + 1) = f(X1(t0) + X2(t0) + X3(t0) +…). Where: SB(t0 + 1) = fpreterm PROM before 9 month (t0) + membrane rupture within 24 h during delivery (t0) + partner encouragement at ANC (t0) + prolonged labor (t0)).

The probability of stillbirth occurrence is estimated as: P(SB = 1) = f(β0 + β1X1 + β2X2 + β3X3 + β4X4). Where β0 is the intercept and β1–β4 are the estimated regression coefficients.

P(SB = 1) = f(β0 + β1 preterm PROM before 9 month + β2 membrane rupture within 24 h during delivery + β3 partner encouragement at ANC + β4 prolonged labor). A predictive modeling approach was used to analyze these risk factors LASSO model was applied.

Study Population and Sampling Technique

The study population included women aged 15–49 years who participated in the PMA Ethiopia Panel Cohort 1 six‐week follow‐up survey. Eligible participants were women 5–9 weeks postpartum at baseline who consented to the survey, women 0–4 weeks postpartum at baseline who consented to baseline and follow‐up, and women pregnant at baseline who consented to both baseline and follow‐up. A total of 2582 women completed the 6‐week follow‐up survey. After excluding records with missing outcome or predictor data (n = 195), miscarriages (n = 98), and abortions (n = 3), a total of 2286 women were included in the final analysis, which was used for model development and validation.

Variables of the Study

Outcome Variable

The outcome variable of this study was stillbirths. Stillbirths are defined as the number of fetal deaths in pregnancies after 28 weeks of pregnancy [30]. The stillbirth rate was calculated as the number of stillbirths (numerator) divided by the total number of pregnancies meeting the criteria (denominator), multiplied by 1000. Then, the stillbirth rate was categorized as Yes = 1, and live birth = 0.

Independent Variables

The candidate predictor variables included maternal, obstetric, and healthcare‐related factors. These factors were chosen because previous studies have identified them as important maternal or intrapartum determinants of adverse birth outcomes. Predictors were coded as binary variables for analysis (1 = presence of the condition, 0 = absence of the condition). These were: antenatal care (ANC) visits fewer than four, late ANC initiation, ANC HIV testing, pregnancy edema, discussion of danger signs during pregnancy, partner encouragement during ANC, skilled delivery attendance, prolonged rupture of membranes (> 24 h) during delivery, preterm rupture of membranes (< 9 months), prolonged labor, labor duration greater than 2 h at home, cesarean section delivery, home‐based antenatal care, multiple pregnancy, and rural residence.

Operational Definitions

Stillbirth: The stillbirth rate is defined as the number of babies born with no signs of life at 28 weeks or more of gestation [31].

Discrimination: the ability of a predictive model to accurately distinguish between pregnancies that will result in a stillbirth and those that will result in a live birth [32].

Calibration: it measures how well the model's predicted risk for stillbirth reflects the true outcomes in the population being studied. The best calibration occurs when the predicted probabilities of stillbirth closely match the observed rates, as shown by a calibration curve in which the points align with the 45‐degree line [33].

Risk score: is tools that combine multiple predictors by assigning relative weights to each predictor to obtain a risk or probability of a condition happening. It is simple to calculate, easily interpretable, and actionable [34].

Data Processing and Analysis

This study utilized secondary data extracted from the Performance Monitoring for Action (PMA) dataset collected between 2019 and 2021. The dataset was originally obtained in Stata 14 format and subsequently exported for further processing. During data cleaning, cases with missing outcome data, miscarriages, and abortions were excluded to ensure completeness and relevance for stillbirth prediction.

Missing data were assessed for all candidate predictors before model development. Seven variables showed missing values ranging from 23.4% to 38.7%, including cesarean delivery (38.7%), duration of labor at home (36.9%), number of antenatal care (ANC) visits (33.9%), timing of ANC initiation (33.9%), ANC services received at home (33.9%), ANC discussion on danger signs (23.4%), and HIV testing and counseling during ANC (23.4%).

Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE). Multiple imputed datasets were generated, incorporating all candidate predictors and the outcome variable to preserve associations among variables.

Continuous variables with missing data were imputed using median imputation, while categorical variables were imputed using mode imputation. Variables without missing data were analyzed using complete case values. Following imputation, predictor variables were appropriately encoded for analysis. The cleaned dataset was imported into R version 4.2.2 for statistical analysis. The final processed dataset was used for predictive model development and internal validation.

Statistical Analysis and Model Development

The Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guideline was followed for developing and reporting the prediction model for stillbirth [35] (Supporting Information S1). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro‐Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are summarized as means and standard deviations (SDs), whereas non‐normally distributed variables are summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. To prevent overfitting and improve model generalizability, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) logistic regression was applied using the glmnet package in R (version 4.4.3). LASSO applies an L1 penalty to shrink regression coefficients toward zero, allowing automatic variable selection [36, 37]. Ten‐fold cross‐validation was used to determine the optimal penalty parameter (λ). The value of λ that minimized the cross‐validated deviance (λ_min) was selected for the final model. Predictors with non‐zero coefficients at λ_min were retained in the final prediction model. Logistic regression analyses were two‐sided, with a significance threshold set a priori at p < 0.05. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported to provide estimates of effect size and precision.

The theoretical framework of the study assumed that the incidence of stillbirth at a future time “t” is influenced by maternal socio‐demographic characteristics, obstetric history, and pregnancy‐related factors measured at “t0”, the point of prediction:

SB (t0 + 1) = f (preterm PROM before 9 month (t0) + membrane rupture within 24 h during delivery (t0) + partner encouragement at ANC (t0) + prolonged labor (t0)).

The risk score was generated by assigning predictors based on their estimated coefficients. The resulting scores were rounded to the nearest integer. A total risk score was calculated for each participant by summing the assigned points, and the risk categories were classified as high‐risk or low‐risk for stillbirth based on an optimal cut‐off point determined using the Youden Index method [37].

Model Performance and Validation

The predictive model was evaluated using discrimination, calibration, and overall performance measures: the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was used to assess the individual predictors' discriminative power and to develop the prediction model. AUROC is < 0.5 have no information, between 0.5 and 0.7 poor, between 0.7 and 0.9 good, and > 0.9 excellent discrimination ability, and 1 is the ideal one which is perfect discrimination.

To evaluate the model's performance for the rare stillbirth outcome, we also plotted the precision–recall (PR) curve and calculated the area under the PR curve (AUC‐PR). Calibration of predicted probabilities was assessed using calibration plots, Brier score, and the Spiegelhalter Z‐test. Points above the diagonal in the calibration plot indicate underestimation of risk, and points below indicate overestimation.

Classification Metrics

Predicted probabilities were initially dichotomized using a threshold of 0.50 to generate a confusion matrix and evaluate classification performance. Accuracy, sensitivity (true positive rate), specificity (true negative rate), positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated. PPV was defined as the proportion of true stillbirth cases among all pregnancies classified as positive by the model (PPV = true positives/[true positives + false positives]), while NPV was defined as the proportion of live births among all pregnancies classified as negative (NPV = true negatives/[true negatives + false negatives]).

Because stillbirth is a rare outcome, threshold‐dependent measures at the 0.50 cutoff were interpreted cautiously and complemented with threshold‐independent measures, including the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), precision–recall curve analysis, and evaluation of alternative cutoffs using sensitivity, specificity, likelihood ratios, and the Youden index.

Subgroup Analyses

To evaluate robustness across different clinical and demographic contexts, discrimination metrics were also calculated for subgroups stratified by geographic setting (urban vs. rural) and pregnancy plurality (singleton vs. twin).

Internal Validation and Clinical Utility

Internal validation was conducted using bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations to estimate model optimism and overfitting. The optimism‐corrected AUC was calculated by subtracting the mean optimism from the apparent AUC. Finally, the clinical utility of the model was assessed using decision curve analysis (DCA) to determine its benefit in guiding clinical decision‐making.

Ethics Consideration

The analysis conducted in this study is based on publicly available data from the Performance Monitoring for Action (PMA) project in Ethiopia. Ethical approval for the PMA study was granted by the Addis Ababa University, College of Health Sciences (AAU/CHS) (Ref: AAUMF 01‐008) and the Johns Hopkins University Bloomberg School of Public Health (JHSPH) Institutional Review Board (IRB) (FWA00000287). All data collection procedures followed ethical guidelines, and informed consent was obtained from all participants involved in the original study.

Model Development and Prediction Model Performance Assessment

A total of 15 potential predictors were initially included based on prior evidence, clinical relevance, and data availability. These predictors comprised: number of antenatal care (ANC) visits, timing of ANC initiation, place of residence, pregnancy type (single or multiple), mode of delivery (specifically cesarean section), duration of labor at home, preterm premature rupture of membranes (occurring before 9 months of gestation), prolonged labor, leakage of membranes within 24 h before delivery, partner encouragement to attend ANC, type of delivery assistant, ANC discussion on danger signs, experience of edema during pregnancy, HIV testing and counseling during ANC, ANC services received at home, and whether a primary health care provider provided ANC.

To develop a prediction model for stillbirth, all candidate predictors were initially entered into a Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) logistic regression with 10‐fold cross‐validation. The optimal penalty parameter minimizing cross‐validated deviance was λ = 0.00349. LASSO automatically shrank the coefficients of less informative predictors to zero, retaining only the most relevant variables. Four predictors prolonged labor, leakage of membranes before 9 months, leakage of membranes ≥ 24 h, and partner encouragement for antenatal care (ANC) were retained and included in the final multivariable logistic regression model. All four predictors were independently associated with stillbirth. Leakage of membranes before 9 months had the strongest effect, increasing the odds of stillbirth more than 45‐fold (AOR = 45.69, 95% CI: 17.71–127.72, p < 0.001). Leakage of membranes lasting ≥ 24 h increased the odds 4.53‐fold (95% CI: 1.65–12.90, p = 0.004), while prolonged labor and lack of partner encouragement for ANC were associated with 3.42‐fold (95% CI: 1.21–9.77, p = 0.019) and 2.46‐fold (95% CI: 1.44–4.12, p = 0.001) increased odds of stillbirth, respectively (Table 2).

Calibration of the Model

The calibration curve assesses agreement between predicted probabilities (x‐axis) and observed outcomes (y‐axis) for stillbirth. The diagonal dashed line represents perfect calibration, where predicted risk equals observed risk. Points above the diagonal indicate underestimation (observed risk higher than predicted), while points below indicate overestimation (observed risk lower than predicted).

The calibration plot shows that at very low predicted risk (approximately 1%), the observed stillbirth rate was 3%, indicating some underestimation. Across higher risk strata, observed outcomes slightly exceeded predicted probabilities (3% predicted vs. 5% observed; 6% predicted vs. 7% observed; 8% predicted vs. 9% observed) (Figures 2 and 3).

Calibration plot comparing predicted versus observed risk of stillbirth.

Calibration plot of the predictive model.

Quantitative metrics confirm the excellent calibration of the model: the optimism‐corrected calibration slope was 1.005, and the intercept was 0.041, indicating near‐perfect agreement between predicted and observed risks. The optimism‐corrected area under the curve (AUC) was 0.905, demonstrating excellent discrimination. While the mean absolute error was low (0.005), this metric is influenced by the large number of correctly classified low‐risk patients, and small deviations in higher‐risk strata are visible in the plot.

Brier Score

The Brier score is 0.0072, which measures the mean squared difference between predicted probabilities and actual outcomes. Scores range from 0 to 1, with 0 indicating perfect prediction accuracy. The low Brier score reflects good overall performance but is influenced by the low prevalence of stillbirth and high proportion of non‐events. This value reflects that, on average, predictions deviate from observed outcomes by less than one percentage point. However, this metric must be interpreted in the context of the rare event the low score is predominantly driven by the large volume of correctly classified non‐events and may mask poor performance in identifying the positive class.

Spiegelhalter Test

The Spiegelhalter test assesses calibration by evaluating whether the mean squared error of predictions is consistent with a well‐calibrated model. The test yields a Z‐statistic of −6.31 × 10−8 with a p‐value of 1.00. The non‐significant p‐value (p = 1.00) indicates no evidence of miscalibration. The Z‐statistic approaching zero demonstrates that the model's predictions align closely with observed outcomes when assessed at the individual level. No statistical evidence of miscalibration was observed, although the low event rate limits the power of calibration tests.

Model Performance and Discrimination Plot

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (Figure 4) demonstrates the discriminatory ability of the predictive model in distinguishing between cases with and without birth complications. The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.907 (95% CI: 0.83–0.979), indicating excellent discrimination.

An AUC value close to 1.0 suggests that the model has a high ability to correctly classify individuals with and without the outcome. In this case, the model accurately distinguishes between women who experienced adverse birth outcomes and those who did not, with minimal misclassification. The Precision–Recall (PR) curve plots the trade‐off between precision (positive predictive value) and recall (sensitivity) across different classification thresholds. Confusion Matrix and Threshold‐Based Metrics

Confusion Matrix and Threshold‐Based Metrics at a probability threshold of 0.5, the model's predictions were compared with the observed outcomes to assess classification performance. The model achieved an accuracy of 0.991, indicating that the vast majority of cases were correctly classified. The specificity (true negative rate) was 0.999, and the negative predictive value (NPV) was 0.992, demonstrating excellent performance in correctly identifying live births. In contrast, the sensitivity (true positive rate) was 0.472, reflecting moderate ability to identify stillbirths, which is consistent with the low event rate (1.6%) in the study population. The positive predictive value (PPV) was 0.895, indicating that among cases predicted as stillbirths, nearly 90% were true positives. Because a fixed probability threshold of 0.50 may not be optimal for rare outcomes, these threshold‐dependent measures were interpreted alongside threshold‐independent performance measures, including the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), precision–recall curve analysis, and the optimized risk‐score cutoff analysis. The results demonstrate that although the model showed excellent discrimination and strong ability to identify low‐risk pregnancies, sensitivity at the conventional 0.50 threshold should be interpreted cautiously due to the rarity of stillbirth events.

Subgroup Analysis: Model Discrimination Across Clinical Subgroups

We evaluated model performance stratified by geographic setting (urban vs. rural) and plurality (singleton vs. twin) (Figure 6).

Presentation of ROC curves with AUC values and 95% confidence intervals for each subgroup.

Urban Verses Rural Residence

The model demonstrated excellent discrimination in both populations. For urban residents, the AUC was 0.889 (95% CI: 0.687–1.000). For rural residents, the AUC was 0.908 (95% CI: 0.828–0.987). The overlapping confidence intervals indicate no statistically significant difference in performance between settings (ΔAUC = 0.019). The wider confidence interval for the urban subgroup reflects greater estimate uncertainty, likely due to a smaller sample size or fewer events.

Singleton Verses Twin Gestations

Substantial performance variation was observed by plurality. For singleton pregnancies, the AUC was 0.890 (95% CI: 0.805–0.974). For twin pregnancies, the AUC was 0.993 (95% CI: 0.977–1.000), representing outstanding near‐perfect discrimination. The non‐overlapping confidence intervals indicate this difference (ΔAUC = 0.103) is statistically significant. The superior performance in twins likely reflects concentrated risk factors and reduced clinical heterogeneity in multiple gestations.

Optimism‐Corrected Model Performance

To account for potential overfitting, we conducted a bootstrap validation with 1000 resamples to estimate optimism and compute an optimism‐corrected AUC. For each bootstrap sample, the model was fitted on the resampled data, the AUC was calculated on both the bootstrap sample and the original dataset, and optimism was defined as the difference between these two AUCs. The mean optimism across all bootstrap samples was then subtracted from the apparent AUC to obtain the optimism‐corrected AUC.

In our results, the apparent AUC of the LASSO model on the full dataset was 0.907. Across bootstrap samples, the mean bootstrap AUC was 0.908 (SD = 0.036; 95% CI: 0.834–0.969), yielding a mean optimism of 0.002. The resulting optimism‐corrected AUC was 0.905, indicating minimal overfitting. Decision Curve Analysis

Decision curve analysis commonly used in medical research to evaluate the usefulness of a risk prediction model. The Y‐axis measures “Standard Net Benefit,” which is the benefit of identifying true cases of a disease minus the harm of unnecessary interventions. The X‐axis shows the “High Risk Threshold,” ranging from very low odds (1:100) to very high odds (100:1), representing the probability at which a doctor would decide to treat a patient. The graph compares three strategies: using the “Simplified Risk Score,” treating “All” patients, and treating “None.” The purpose is to determine which threshold values the risk score provides a higher net benefit than simply treating everyone or no one. However, given the low event rate, potential overfitting, and limited external validation, these results should be interpreted cautiously, and further validation is recommended before widespread clinical use (Figure 10).

Development and Performance of the Stillbirth Risk Nomogram

This nomogram predicts the probability of stillbirth using key risk factors: prolonged labor, membrane rupture at 9 months, membrane rupture within 24 h, and partner encouragement of antenatal care. Each factor is assigned points, which are summed into a total points score. This score is converted via a Linear Predictor to estimate the patient's probability of stillbirth, ranging from 10% to 90%. Clinicians use the nomogram by assigning points for each factor, summing them, and mapping the total to the predicted risk, enabling individualized risk assessment and targeted interventions (Figure 11).

Discussion

The prevalence of stillbirth in this study was 1.6%, indicating that 98.4% of births were live births. This finding is consistent with previous estimates. A global systematic review and meta‐analysis reported a stillbirth prevalence of 1.4%, which closely aligns with our finding [38], Similarly, global reports on stillbirth and preterm birth have documented a stillbirth rate of approximately 2% [39], while a nationwide survey in China found a rate of 1.2% [40]. A multilevel analysis conducted across sub‐Saharan Africa revealed a stillbirth prevalence of 1.54% [41].

The stillbirth prevalence observed in this study is higher than that reported in a recent study conducted in East Africa, which found a stillbirth rate of 0.86% [42]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in study design, population characteristics, data sources, and healthcare system performance across countries in the region. Our study utilized a nationally representative dataset with a larger sample size and included rural areas where access to timely and quality maternal healthcare services may be more limited.

However, the stillbirth prevalence found in this study is lower than estimates reported in several other studies conducted both within Ethiopia and in other low‐ and middle‐income countries. Higher rates were documented in the Tigray region, 68% [43], in the Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples' Region 8.7% [16], in Oromia, 12.5% [44], Bahir Dar (ranging from 3.8% to 8.7%) [45, 46], Additionally, studies from broader contexts such as sub‐Saharan Africa have reported substantially higher stillbirth prevalence 6.18% in a multilevel analysis 6.18% [47], and 6.4% in a systematic review and meta‐analysis [48]. Internationally, higher rates have also been observed, such as 15.8% in Kenya [49] and 2.97% in India [50]. These discrepancies may be attributed to variations in sample size, health system infrastructure, quality and coverage of maternal healthcare services, as well as regional differences in socioeconomic status and cultural practices surrounding pregnancy and childbirth.

In this study, we developed and validated a predictive model for stillbirth. The final model incorporated four key predictors selected by the LASSO to optimize predictive performance: prolonged labor, membrane rupture before 9 months, membrane rupture during delivery lasting 24 h, and partner encouragement for ANC.

Prolonged labor was a significant predictor of stillbirth, reflecting the well‐established relationship between extended labor duration and fetal compromise. Prolonged labor increases the risk of fetal hypoxia, as uteroplacental perfusion may be impaired, leading to intrapartum fetal distress and asphyxia [11, 51, 52]. Additionally, Preterm premature rupture of membranes significantly increased the likelihood of stillbirth. Premature rupture exposes the fetus to risks including intrauterine infection, cord compression, and preterm birth, all of which are strong contributors to perinatal mortality [52]. PPROM disrupts the protective barrier of the amniotic sac, increasing susceptibility to ascending infections and compromising fetal oxygenation, highlighting the need for early detection, prophylactic antibiotics, and close monitoring in such cases. Membrane rupture during labor lasting more than 24 h similarly predicted stillbirth. Prolonged rupture of membranes during labor increases the risk of cord prolapse, meconium aspiration, and fetal distress [13, 53].

Moreover Partner encouragement for ANC emerged as a protective factor for stillbirth. Male involvement in maternal care has been shown to improve maternal adherence to recommended antenatal visits, nutritional supplementation, and early recognition of complications [54]. The predictive value of this variable highlights that social support and family engagement are not merely psychosocial factors but can directly influence perinatal outcomes through improved utilization of preventive and curative services.

The calibration assessment showed good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities of stillbirth. The calibration curve indicated slight underestimation of risk at very low predicted probabilities; however, across most risk strata, predicted risks were close to observed outcomes. The optimism‐corrected calibration slope (1.005) and intercept (0.041) further confirmed near‐perfect calibration. When compared to previous studies in Nigeria [55], where calibration issues were reported due to model overfitting or under fitting, our model demonstrates a more consistent calibration across risk levels.

The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was approximately 0.90, indicating that the model has a high capacity to distinguish between pregnancies that result in stillbirth and those that result in live birth. An AUC value approaching 1.0 indicates a strong ability of the model to accurately distinguish between individuals with and without the outcome of interest. This finding aligns with previous studies that have reported high AUC values in predictive models for birth complications. Earlier study revealed that an AUC of 0.95 in their model for predicting adverse birth outcomes, suggesting good discriminatory ability, similar to our findings [56]. These results highlight better predictive model discrimination comparing to in Midwestern United States quaternary referral center 1999‐2009 (0.66) [57], in Sweden 0.69 [58], in Austria 0.72 [59] and in Nigeria 0.8 [55].

At a probability threshold of 0.5, the model achieved very high overall accuracy and specificity, indicating excellent ability to correctly identify live births. The specificity (true negative rate) was 0.999, and the negative predictive value (NPV) was 0.992, demonstrating excellent performance in correctly identifying live births. In contrast, the sensitivity (true positive rate) was 0.472, reflecting moderate ability to identify stillbirths, which is consistent with the low event rate (1.6%) in the study population. The positive predictive value (PPV) was 0.895, indicating that among cases predicted as stillbirths, nearly 90%. Compared with previous studies reporting lower accuracy (0.66) [60], and specificity (0.82) [59], as well as lower PPV (0.58) and NPV (0.97) in the UK [61], our model demonstrated improved predictive ability. Internal validation using bootstrap resampling indicated minimal model optimism. The optimism‐corrected AUC remained very close to the apparent AUC, suggesting that the model is stable and not substantially over fitted to the development dataset [32, 62].

A simplified risk score was developed from the final model to enhance practical use in clinical settings. The score ranges from 0 to 10 and includes four predictors: prolonged labour, premature rupture of membranes before 9 months, membrane rupture within 24 h of delivery, and lack of partner encouragement for antenatal care. Higher scores were associated with an increased probability of stillbirth, indicating good ability to distinguish between low‐ and high‐risk pregnancies. Decision curve analysis further suggested that the simplified risk score provides greater clinical benefit than strategies of treating all or no patients across a range of threshold probabilities. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously due to the low event rate and the lack of external validation. A previous study demonstrated that their predictive model offered clinical benefit when the threshold probability was greater than 0.01 [63].

Subgroup analyses demonstrated consistently high discrimination across geographic settings. The model performed similarly among urban and rural populations, indicating that it may be broadly applicable across different contexts within Ethiopia. Interestingly, the model showed exceptionally high discrimination among twin pregnancies compared with singleton pregnancies. This difference may reflect the higher concentration of clinical risk factors and greater biological vulnerability associated with multiple gestations.

Clinical Implication

The simplified stillbirth risk score offers a practical tool for clinicians to identify pregnancies at elevated risk using readily available maternal and intrapartum factors. It enables targeted monitoring and timely interventions for high‐risk pregnancies, particularly in low‐resource settings where comprehensive screening may be limited. By stratifying risk, healthcare providers can prioritize care, allocate resources efficiently, and potentially reduce stillbirth rates. With further external validation, this tool could be integrated into routine antenatal and intrapartum care to support evidence‐based decision‐making.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, the predictive model was developed and internally validated using a single dataset, which may limit its generalizability to other populations or settings. Second, external validation in independent datasets was not performed, so model performance in other populations remains uncertain. Third, the relatively small number of stillbirth events may reduce the precision of risk estimates and affect sensitivity and calibration in certain risk strata. Some potentially important predictors, such as medical disorders, were not available in the dataset and could not be included. Although LASSO regression was applied to reduce overfitting, sparse data in some exposure categories resulted in wide confidence intervals for certain predictors. Future studies should perform external validation in diverse populations, consider additional predictors, and explore temporal or geographic split‐sample validation to enhance model reliability.

Conclusion

We developed and internally validated a predictive model and a simplified risk score for stillbirth using nationally representative data from the PMA Ethiopia cohort. The model demonstrated excellent discrimination, good calibration, and stable performance on internal validation, indicating reliability and potential generalizability. The simplified risk score, based on four easily obtainable clinical and maternal factors, effectively identifies pregnancies at low and high risk of stillbirth, providing a practical tool for early risk stratification. With further external validation, this tool could support targeted monitoring and timely interventions, particularly in resource‐limited settings, to reduce the burden of stillbirth.
