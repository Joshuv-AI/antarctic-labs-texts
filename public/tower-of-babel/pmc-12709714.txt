Acute neuroinflammation induces prolonged transcriptional reprogramming in microglia
Authors: Sehoon Moon, Cheol Gyun Kim, Young-Kwang Kim, Cheol-Heui Yun, Min-Kyoo Shin, Hyungseok Seo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute neuroinflammation rapidly activates brain immune responses, but its lasting effects on microglia are unclear. Using systemic LPS administration and LCMV-Armstrong infection, we found that blood–brain barrier disruption and cytokine shifts resolved within 30 days, yet microglial recovery was incomplete—marked by persistent numerical loss and an IFN-γ–low phenotype in the LPS model and reduced relative abundance in the LCMV model. Single-cell RNA sequencing revealed sustained transcriptional alterations, including disease-associated microglia (DAM) features and a distinct recovery-biased population. These acute signatures overlapped with profiles from Alzheimer’s model mice and were enriched in human microglia from multiple sclerosis, Alzheimer’s disease, and other neuroinflammatory conditions. Although our observation period was shorter than the chronic course of these diseases, the persistence of disease-like microglial states suggests that transient inflammation can prime the brain for long-term vulnerability. Introduction

Microglia are the primary innate immune cells of the central nervous system (CNS), responsible for maintaining homeostasis and responding to injury, infection, or neurodegenerative processes [1, 2]. Upon sensing danger signals, microglia rapidly transition from a surveillant to an activated state, producing cytokines, clearing debris, and modulating the activity of other CNS cells [2, 3]. While their role in chronic neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and multiple sclerosis (MS) has been extensively studied, the consequences of acute neuroinflammatory episodes on long-term microglial function remain incompletely understood [4, 5].

Acute neuroinflammation—induced by systemic infections, trauma, or viral invasion—typically involves transient disruption of the blood–brain barrier (BBB), rapid immune cell infiltration, and robust cytokine release [3, 5–8]. Although such responses are often considered reversible, emerging evidence suggests that acute immune challenges may leave persistent molecular or functional imprints on CNS-resident immune cells, particularly microglia [9–12]. However, whether and how such transient insults reshape the transcriptional identity or long-term reactivity of microglia remains an open question.

Previous studies have shown that chronic neuroinflammation in AD and other diseases is associated with a distinct microglial activation state termed disease-associated microglia (DAM), characterized by upregulation of genes such as Apoe, Trem2, and Il1b [9, 13, 14]. Interestingly, similar gene signatures have been observed in microglia following acute inflammatory stimuli, raising the possibility of convergent immune responses between acute and chronic settings [10–12, 15]. Yet, whether such convergence is transient or results in lasting reprogramming of microglia is unclear.

In this study, we used two independent models of acute neuroinflammation—lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced systemic inflammation and lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV) infection—to investigate the temporal dynamics of microglial responses. We combined flow cytometry, single-cell RNA sequencing, and transcriptomic comparisons with chronic neurodegenerative disease models and human datasets. We hypothesized that acute inflammation leaves persistent transcriptional imprints in microglia, which may phenocopy changes observed in chronic neurodegenerative settings.

Our findings reveal that acute inflammation induces sustained transcriptional and phenotypic reprogramming of microglia, resembling chronic disease states and implicating transient immune events as potential priming factors for long-term neuroimmune dysregulation.

Result

Acute systemic inflammation induces dynamic changes in brain-resident microglia and infiltrating immune cells

We examined how acute systemic inflammation affects the brain immune landscape by administering lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 2 mg/kg) intraperitoneally to C57BL/6J mice and analyzed immune cell composition in whole-brain suspensions by flow cytometry at multiple time points post-injection (1, 3, 5, and 7 days) (Fig. 1A). Immune cell populations were defined using a standardized gating strategy (Supplementary Fig. 1), with microglia identified as CD45intCD11b+ and infiltrating leukocytes classified into distinct subsets including T cells, B cells, NK cells, neutrophils, dendritic cells (DCs), Ly6Chigh and Ly6Clow macrophages. Following LPS challenge, we observed a subsequent decline below baseline levels in microglial proportion at 1–7 dpi (day post injection) (Fig. 1B). Concurrently, the proportion and partial absolute number of Ly6Chigh inflammatory macrophages markedly increased and peaked at 7 dpi, while Ly6Clow macrophages exhibited a transient increase that peaked earlier at 1 dpi. These results suggest that microglial reduction is accompanied by progressive infiltration of monocyte-derived macrophages into the brain parenchyma. Among non-microglial immune populations, a delayed increase in T cells, NK cells, neutrophils, and DCs was detected from 5 dpi onward, whereas B cell frequencies remained relatively unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 2A–B). Notably, both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells expanded to a similar extent, indicating a broad T cell activation profile (Supplementary Fig. 2C).

We next analyzed key inflammatory cytokines in microglia. Flow cytometry revealed a significant decrease in IL-10 expression at 1–3 dpi, followed by recovery at 5–7 dpi. In contrast, IL-1β expression was sharply elevated at 1 dpi and gradually normalized thereafter (Fig. 1C). These opposing dynamics of IL-10 and IL-1β are consistent with their roles as anti- and pro-inflammatory cytokines, respectively. In macrophages, IL-1β followed a similar trajectory, whereas IL-10 expression increased early and peaked at 3 dpi (Supplementary Fig. 3A-B), suggesting distinct regulatory kinetics across cell types.

To further assess phenotype remodeling in microglia, we examined IFN-γ and IL-10 expression by dual gating analysis. Over time, microglia exhibited a progressive increase in IFN-γ expression accompanied by a concomitant decrease in IL-10 expression (Fig. 1D). Although both cytokines showed a partial recovery toward later time points, the extent of normalization did not reach the levels observed in PBS-treated controls, indicating sustained alterations in the microglial functional profile after acute systemic inflammation.

Together, these data demonstrate that acute systemic inflammation drives marked yet time-dependent remodeling of the brain’s immune landscape, encompassing both shifts in cellular composition and persistent changes in microglial phenotype.

Microglia exhibit long-term phenotypic alterations after resolution of acute neuroinflammation

To determine whether brain immune alterations persist over time following acute systemic inflammation, we extended our analysis to a long-term time frame. C57BL/6J mice were administered LPS (2 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) and immune profiles in whole-brain suspensions were analyzed by flow cytometry at 3, 7, 20, and 30 days post-injection (dpi) (Fig. 2A). Compared with PBS-treated controls, the proportion and absolute number of microglia remained markedly reduced even at 30 dpi (Fig. 2B). In contrast, Ly6Chigh and Ly6Clow macrophages, as well as most other infiltrating immune cell types, returned to baseline numbers by 30 dpi (Supplementary Fig. 4A-C), indicating that while peripheral immune populations recover, brain-resident microglia fail to fully reconstitute within one month of the inflammatory insult. This suggests that the microglial compartment remains incompletely restored long after resolution of the acute inflammatory phase.

In contrast to cell population dynamics, cytokine expression profiles exhibited complete recovery by 30 dpi. In microglia, both IL-10 and IL-1β returned to PBS levels, and macrophages showed full restoration of IL-10 alongside a decline and normalization of IL-1β expression (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Fig. 5A), suggesting that cytokine-mediated regulatory functions are re-established earlier than cellular homeostasis.

To further assess phenotypic changes, we examined the expression of M1-associated markers (CD80, CD86) and the M2-associated marker CD163. Although all three markers showed recovery trends by 30 dpi, their trajectories differed between microglia and macrophages. In microglia, CD86 and CD163 followed the expected inflammatory pattern (CD86 upregulation, CD163 downregulation) but CD80 unexpectedly decreased. In macrophages, all three markers, including CD163, were increased (Supplementary Fig. 5B), indicating cell type–specific regulatory programs.

We next applied UMAP projection to microglia to assess global phenotypic remodeling (Fig. 2D–E). PBS-treated microglia were predominantly localized to clusters 2 and 3, whereas microglia at 30 dpi shifted toward clusters 1 and 2. Notably, clusters were defined by IFN-γ/IL-10 co-expression states: the proportion of IL-10+ IFN-γ– cells (cluster 1) increased at 20–30 dpi, while IL-10+ IFN-γ+ cells (cluster 3) declined (Fig. 2F). This shift was not observed for other cytokines (Supplementary Fig. 5C), suggesting that sustained reduction in microglial IFN-γ may be linked to the persistent deficit in microglial numbers.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that although cytokine expression and most infiltrating immune populations return to baseline within a month after LPS challenge, microglial numbers and IFN-γ–associated phenotypes remain altered, indicating incomplete restoration of the brain’s resident immune network.

Single-cell transcriptomic profiling reveals distinct microglial trajectories following acute systemic inflammation

Persistent phenotypic alterations in microglia at 30 dpi prompted transcriptomic profiling to identify underlying regulatory programs. We therefore performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on immune cells isolated from the brains of LPS-treated mice at 3 dpi and 30 dpi. Immune cells from five to seven mice per time point were pooled and enriched for CD45+ cells by flow cytometric sorting prior to sequencing.

Unsupervised clustering identified eight major immune cell types, including two distinct microglial clusters (designated microglia 1 and microglia 2) (Fig. 3A–B, Supplementary Fig. 6A). Analysis of cell composition revealed that at 3 dpi, disease-associated microglia (DAM) accounted for 38.6% of all microglia, representing more than half of the total microglial compartment, whereas at 30 dpi, the proportion of microglia 2 increased to 19.6% (Fig. 3C). Notably, microglia 2 exhibited elevated expression of genes such as Elmo1 and Jmjd1c, consistent with enhanced phagocytic activity compared with microglia 1 (Fig. 3D).

Differential gene expression analysis of all microglia compared with PBS controls identified 190 upregulated and 76 downregulated genes at 3 dpi, and 124 upregulated and 154 downregulated genes at 30 dpi. Of these, 48 upregulated and 32 downregulated genes remained persistently altered across both time points (Fig. 3E). Persistent changes included sustained high expression of inflammatory genes (Cybb, Cd44, Cd69) and reduced expression of homeostatic microglial markers (P2ry12, Tmem119) (Supplementary Fig. 6B).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that at 3 dpi, microglia were enriched for biological processes related to inflammatory activation, whereas at 30 dpi, enriched pathways were more consistent with recovery and restoration of homeostasis (Fig. 3F). Interestingly, genes involved in the response to amyloid beta displayed opposite regulation patterns between 3 dpi and 30 dpi (Supplementary Fig. 6C), suggesting that acute systemic inflammation induces temporally dynamic transcriptional programs with potential implications for neurodegenerative disease susceptibility.

To investigate whether microglia transition into DAM and whether microglia at 30 dpi revert to a phenotype resembling PBS controls, we performed pseudotime trajectory analysis on the microglial populations identified in our scRNA-seq dataset (Fig. 4A–C). This analysis revealed three major terminal branches leading to cluster 1 (DAM), cluster 4 (microglia 2), and cluster 6 (microglia 1) (Fig. 4D, left). Analysis of highly expressed genes revealed that cluster 1 exhibits DAM-like characteristics, cluster 4 shows phagocytic features, and cluster 6 displays homeostatic properties (Supplementary Fig. 6D). Partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA) further indicated that DAM arises likely from microglia, whereas microglia 2 did not appear to originate from DAM (Fig. 4D, right).

(right) Partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA) plot of microglia based on the clusters shown in (C). E Principal component analysis (PCA) of microglia and DAM populations from each sample

Principal component analysis (PCA) supported these findings, showing that DAM formed a distinct transcriptional cluster separated from both microglia 1 and microglia 2, with microglia 2 positioned closer to PBS microglia (Fig. 4E). These results suggest that while DAM emerge from activated microglia during acute inflammation, a subset of microglia (microglia 2) represents a transcriptionally distinct population that does not derive from DAM and may instead follow an alternative activation or recovery.

Single-cell RNA sequencing of brain immune cells at 3 and 30 days post-LPS challenge revealed persistent transcriptional alterations in microglia, characterized by the emergence of disease-associated microglia (DAM) during the acute phase and distinct microglia 2 population enriched at later stages. The pseudotime and PCA analyses indicated that DAMs are potentially induced from activated microglia; however, microglia 2 followed an alternative trajectory, remaining transcriptionally distinct from DAM and more closely aligned with homeostatic microglia. These findings suggest that acute inflammation drives heterogeneous microglial responses, with some cells adopting a disease-associated phenotype and others progressing toward partial recovery.

Chronic neuroinflammation in alzheimer’s disease mirrors acute inflammation–induced microglial States

Given that neuroinflammation is typically considered a chronic condition driven by persistent pathological stimuli, such as in neurodegenerative diseases, it was initially expected that LPS-induced acute inflammation would elicit distinct microglial responses. However, our earlier analysis revealed that the transcriptional shift in microglia during LPS-induced acute inflammation aligns with the direction of change observed in the response to amyloid beta (Supplementary Fig. 6C). This finding prompted a direct comparison between acute and chronic neuroinflammation.

To this end, we analyzed a publicly available single-cell RNA-seq dataset (GSE207701) from 8-month-old Alzheimer’s disease model mice (5xFAD) and non-transgenic (NonTg) controls. In this dataset, immune cells from brain parenchyma were isolated by flow cytometric sorting.

Unsupervised clustering, consistent with the analysis by W. Su et al., 2023, identified eight major immune cell types, with comparable overall composition between groups (Fig. 5A–C, Supplementary Fig. 7A) [16]. Microglia were resolved into two clusters corresponding to those defined in our LPS dataset (Fig. 3D), namely microglia 1 and microglia 2 (Fig. 5D). Pseudotime trajectory analysis revealed three major branches, mirroring the differentiation patterns observed under acute inflammation (Fig. 5E, Supplementary Fig. 7B).

Differential gene expression analysis identified 82 upregulated genes in 5xFAD microglia compared with NonTg controls, with 39 overlapping with LPS_day3 DEGs and 9 overlapping with LPS_day30 DEGs (Fig. 5F). Among 25 downregulated genes, only 3 overlapped with LPS_day3 DEGs.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) demonstrated that 5xFAD microglia exhibited inflammatory response–associated transcriptional programs highly like those induced by LPS, including enrichment of pathways related to the cellular response to LPS (Fig. 5G, Supplementary Fig. 7C). These results indicate that acute and chronic neuroinflammation share convergent microglial activation signatures, despite arising from distinct pathological contexts.

Acute viral infection recapitulates microglial alterations observed in LPS-induced inflammation but exhibits pathogen-specific resolution dynamics

To assess whether the microglial alterations observed in the LPS model were generalizable to other forms of systemic immune activation, we turned to the LCMV-Armstrong model (Fig. 6A). Using the LCMV-Armstrong strain, which triggers a transient yet robust antiviral response, we found by flow cytometry that at 7 dpi—corresponding to peak immune activation—multiple immune cell populations were markedly increased, with most returning to baseline by 30 dpi (Fig. 6B, Supplementary Fig. 8A–B). Similar to the LPS model, the relative proportion of microglia remained reduced at 30 dpi, despite numerical recovery, and CD8+ T cells persisted within the brain from 14 dpi onward, whereas CD4+ T cells declined more rapidly (Supplementary Fig. 8C). Cytokine profiling showed full recovery of IL-10 and IL-1β expression in both microglia and macrophages by 30 dpi, following temporal patterns nearly identical to those in the LPS model (Fig. 6C, Supplementary Fig. 9A–B). Both cell types exhibited increased CD86 and decreased CD163 at 7 dpi, indicative of M1-like polarization (Fig. 6D, Supplementary Fig. 9C–D). UMAP visualization revealed transient emergence of an IL-10+ IFN-γ+ microglial cluster and a granzyme B+ cluster at 7 dpi, both of which resolved by 30 dpi (Fig. 6E–G, Supplementary Fig. 9E).

Building on these findings, single-cell RNA sequencing was performed on LCMV-infected brains at 7 and 30 dpi to resolve microglial transcriptional states in greater detail. Unsupervised clustering identified seven major immune cell types, with microglia partitioning into two clusters as in the LPS model (Fig. 7A–B, Supplementary Fig. 10A). Consistent with the flow cytometry results, LCMV infection resulted in T cell enrichment (Supplementary Fig. 8C) and a transient expansion of DAM at 7 dpi, followed by contraction at 30 dpi (Fig. 7C). DEG analysis detected 93 upregulated and 38 downregulated genes at 7 dpi, and 10 upregulated and 28 downregulated genes at 30 dpi, with 6 and 19 genes, respectively, remaining persistently altered across time points (Fig. 7D). Cross-comparison with LPS datasets showed substantial overlap in early-stage gene regulation (47 shared DEGs at 7 dpi) but minimal overlap at 30 dpi (Fig. 7E–F), suggesting pathogen-specific recovery trajectories. GSEA confirmed enrichment of immune activation pathways at 7 dpi and recovery-associated pathways at 30 dpi (Fig. 7G). Consistent with the LPS model, LPS–responsive genes showed opposite regulation between early and late stages (Supplementary Fig. 10B), indicating a conserved transcriptional polarity in microglial adaptation across distinct inflammatory triggers.

Across both flow cytometric and single-cell transcriptomic analyses, the LCMV-Armstrong acute infection model recapitulated many of the microglial alterations observed in LPS-induced inflammation, including sustained microglial depletion, transient emergence of DAM, M1-like polarization, and complete cytokine recovery by 30 dpi. Early-phase responses in both models shared substantial transcriptional overlap, particularly in immune activation and inflammatory pathways, while the resolution phase displayed pathogen-specific recovery trajectories.

Acute inflammation-related microglial gene signatures are enriched in human neuroinflammatory diseases

Building on these experimental findings, we investigated whether acute inflammation–associated DEGs are also dysregulated in human neuroinflammatory diseases. Microglial transcriptomic datasets from Alzheimer’s disease (AD; GSE125050) and multiple sclerosis (MS; GSE124335) patients were analyzed. From the LPS-induced model, we defined LPS3-UP and LPS30-UP gene set comprising genes upregulated at 3 dpi and 30 dpi, respectively; analogous upregulated gene set were generated for the LCMV model. Gene set scoring revealed significant elevation of all UP signatures in MS microglia, whereas AD microglia showed no consistent change (Fig. 8A–B).

Focusing on selected inflammation-associated genes from the LPS3-UP set, we found APOE expression elevated only in AD, CD44 increased in both AD and MS, and IL1B decreased in MS, highlighting disease- and gene-specific relationships (Fig. 8C). We then defined an LPS-UP signature consisting of genes upregulated at both 3 and 30 dpi in the LPS model. This composite signature was significantly elevated in AD as well as MS microglia (Fig. 8D), suggesting that acute inflammation–induced transcriptional programs can persist or be reactivated in chronic disease contexts. In contrast, downregulated gene sets from the acute models showed no significant association with either disease cohort (Supplementary Fig. 11A-B).

To investigate whether LPS-UP genes are specific to DAM, we compared LPS-UP gene set with previously defined DAM signature genes [17]. When compared with the LPS3-UP dataset, we observed a partial overlap (16 shared DEGs), suggesting that DAM-specific gene expression increases during the early acute phase. However, when compared with the LPS-UP set, only the Cybb gene appeared as a uniquely shared gene, indicating that DAM-specific genes are not upregulated for a long time, and the expression of inflammatory genes with distinct characteristics becomes dominant, which can also be associated with AD and MS (Fig. 8E).

To assess the broader relevance of this finding, we analyzed an additional dataset (Gosselin et al., 2017) containing microglial transcriptomes from patients with acute ischemia, epilepsy, or brain tumors [18]. Heatmap visualization revealed consistently elevated LPS-UP signature scores across all three conditions (Fig. 8F), indicating that acute inflammation–associated transcriptional programs are a common feature of diverse human neuropathological states.

Collectively, these data suggest that acute immune insults may induce transcriptional states in microglia that resemble those observed in chronic human neuroinflammatory conditions, with the potential to persist or be reactivated.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that acute neuroinflammation, despite its transient nature, can induce long-lasting transcriptional reprogramming in microglia [10]. Using both LPS-induced systemic inflammation and LCMV infection as models of acute neuroimmune challenge, we observed convergent early-phase microglial responses but divergent recovery trajectories, indicating that while acute activation programs are shared, the resolution phase is pathogen-dependent [19]. We selected the Armstrong strain of lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV), a well-established model of acute CD8⁺ T cell–mediated neuroinflammation in C57BL/6J mice. It induces robust peripheral cytokine production, BBB disruption, and direct activation of brain-resident glia, enabling pathogen-specific comparisons with LPS-induced inflammation [20, 21]. These findings challenge the conventional notion that the central nervous system (CNS) rapidly returns to homeostasis following acute immune insults and suggest that such events may leave a lasting molecular “imprint” on brain-resident immune cells [22].

One of the key observations from our work is that microglia undergo a dynamic yet incomplete recovery process following acute inflammation. While cytokine levels such as IL-10 and IL-1β normalized within 30 days post-injection, microglial numbers remained reduced and IFN-γ expression decreased, suggesting a hyporesponsive or potentially dysfunctional phenotype. This shift, observed in the LPS model but not in LCMV, was specific to microglia and not mirrored in other CNS-infiltrating immune populations [22, 23]. UMAP and cluster analyses further revealed a sustained transition from IL-10⁺ IFN-γ⁺ (Cluster 3) toward IL-10⁺ IFN-γ⁻ (Cluster 1) microglia, indicating that altered IFN-γ regulation may be linked to persistent numerical deficits.

Single-cell transcriptomic analyses revealed that microglia exposed to acute inflammation acquired transcriptional signatures sharing a subset of features with those of disease-associated microglia (DAM), which are typically observed in chronic neurodegenerative models such as 5xFAD [17]. Shared upregulated genes, including Apoe, Il1b, and Saa3, underscore a convergent pro-inflammatory program activated in both acute and chronic disease contexts [24]. The origin of these DAM remains unclear, while pseudotime and PCA analyses indicated that DAM is likely derived from activated microglia, we cannot definitively rule out the contribution of infiltrating macrophages. Along with DAM, distinct subset of microglia (“microglia 2”) appeared to follow an alternative, recovery-biased trajectory. The emergence of DAM-like microglia within 3 days and their persistence in altered form at 30 days suggest that acute immune challenges may prime microglia, lowering the threshold for future neuroinflammatory or neurodegenerative responses [25].

Whether these transcriptional imprints are universally detrimental or contextually beneficial remains unclear. Notably, pre-conditioning 5xFAD mice with a single low dose of LPS early in life reduced Aβ plaque burden and improved cognition months later, suggesting that acute inflammation–induced reprogramming could, under certain conditions, confer resilience. Thus, the functional consequences of acute inflammation-induced microglial reprogramming—whether it predisposes the CNS to damage or imparts resilience—remain to be fully defined and may depend on the timing, context, and nature of secondary insults [26].

Comparative analyses further supported the relevance of these findings to human disease. Gene signatures from acutely inflamed mouse microglia were enriched in transcriptomic datasets from patients with Alzheimer’s disease (AD), multiple sclerosis (MS), and non-chronic conditions such as stroke and epilepsy [27]. Interestingly, while MS microglia exhibited consistent elevation of all acute inflammation–associated UP signatures, AD microglia showed selective enrichment, with strong association for the persistent LPS-UP signature but variable changes in early-phase gene sets, suggesting disease-specific engagement of acute activation programs. This may retain that microglia possess an epigenetically or transcriptionally plastic state capable of encoding prior inflammatory experiences [28]. The consistent inversion of amyloid beta–responsive gene expression between early and late stages in both models further suggests that transient immune events can modulate microglial interactions with neurodegenerative pathology over time. Such “immune memory” in the CNS may help explain the link between early-life infections, systemic inflammation, or trauma and increased susceptibility to neurodegenerative diseases later in life [29]. While our findings raise the possibility that these altered states could persist or be reactivated, further longitudinal and functional studies, including epigenetic analyses, will be necessary to directly confirm their long-term stability and plasticity.

The clinical implications of these findings are considerable. Current therapeutic strategies often focus on chronic inflammation, but our findings suggest that even transient episodes may initiate maladaptive microglial reprogramming long before symptoms arise. Identifying molecular mediators—such as persistent DAM-associated gene expression or sustained suppression of IFN-γ—may provide opportunities for early intervention [30, 31]. An additional consideration is whether pharmacological interventions, such as glucocorticoids, administered after an acute inflammatory insult could modulate microglial activation dynamics and promote faster phenotypic reversion. While glucocorticoids like dexamethasone have been reported to suppress microglial inflammatory responses to LPS or S. aureus, high doses have also been shown to reduce M2-like microglia after traumatic brain injury, potentially exerting neurotoxic effects [32–34]. Other studies found that while 5-androsten-3β,17β-diol (ADIOL) attenuated microglial inflammation (Saijo et al., 2011), corticosterone worsened LPS-induced microglial activation (Sorrells et al., 2013; Munhoz et al., 2010), and chronic glucocorticoid exposure could prime microglia toward a pro-inflammatory state (Frank et al., 2014) [35–38]. These divergent findings underscore that steroid effects on microglia are context-dependent and may differ by dose, timing, and inflammatory milieu. Future experiments in our model will be needed to directly determine whether post-insult steroid administration shortens the duration of microglial activation and facilitates recovery toward homeostasis.

Despite its strengths, our study has several limitations. First, the latest time point in our model (30 dpi) represents a much shorter timescale than the years- to decades-long progression of human neurodegenerative diseases. Our intent was not to imply direct temporal equivalence, but rather to highlight shared transcriptional and phenotypic features that, if sustained, could contribute to chronic pathology. Longer-term in vivo studies and chronic inflammation models will be essential to bridge this temporal gap. Second, the systemic inflammatory stimuli used may not fully capture the complexity of human neuroinflammatory triggers [39]. Third, the mechanisms underlying persistent microglial depletion remain unclear and may involve altered survival, proliferation, or migration [40]. Finally, while we identified cross-species transcriptional similarities, functional consequences in the human brain remain to be confirmed in patient-derived models or postmortem tissue [18]. In addition, future studies should also examine other CNS cell types to gain a broader view of acute inflammation’s long-term impact on brain homeostasis [41–43].

In conclusion, our work establishes that acute neuroinflammation can leave enduring molecular imprints on microglia [44]. These primed states include both chronic disease–like DAM and partial recovery phenotypes, each with the potential to shape long-term CNS vulnerability or resilience. Targeting this primed microglial state may offer novel opportunities for preventing or delaying neurodegenerative diseases.

Materials and methods

Mice and treatments

C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from the JA Bio. Male mice were used throughout all experiments. All animal procedures were approved by and were carried out according to the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Seoul National University (SNU-240213-4-2). All mice were housed under specific-pathogen free conditions at the Seoul National University Animal Center for Pharmaceutical Research. Mice were maintained on 12 h light/12hr dark cycles, and the housing facility was maintained at 18–26 °C and 30–70% humidity.

C57BL/6J mice were intraperitoneally (i.p.) injected with 2 mg/kg of LPS, derived from Escherichia coli O111:B4 (Sigma, L2630) or 2 × 105 PFU of LCMV Armstrong and analyzed after a specific time. LCMV Armstrong was a kind gift from Dr. Cheol-Heui Yun.

Preparation of single-cell suspensions

Single cell suspensions were prepared from a whole brain of mice. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and underwent cardiovascular perfusion with PBS, followed by isolation of the brain. The brain was homogenized by mechanical force on 100-µm strainer. The suspension was pelleted by centrifugation at 300 x g for 5 min and resuspended in digestion buffer [1.5 mg/ml Collagenase D (Sigma, 11088866001), 10U/ml DNase I (Sigma, D5025) in RPMI (Gibco, 22400105)], subsequently transferred to a gentleMACS C tube (Miltenyi). Enzymatic digestion was performed under stirring at 160 rpm, 37 °C for 30 min. Cells were resuspended in 5 ml of 30% Percoll (Sigma, P1644) diluted in RPMI and 2 ml of PBS was carefully added on top of the layer. Immune cells were purified by density gradient centrifugation at 500 x g (with lowest acceleration and low brake) for 20 min at 18 °C. After the myelin layer and the supernatant were removed, pelleted immune cells were washed and resuspended in DPBS (Gibco, 14190250).

Flow cytometry

Before staining, cells were incubated with anti-CD16/CD32 antibody to block Fc receptor (1:1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 14–0161-85) for 15 min at 4 °C. For surface staining, cells were labelled with following antibodies for 15 min at 4 °C: Live/Dead Fixable blue (1:1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, L34961), anti-CD45 BUV737 (1:200, BD, 568344), anti-CD3 Alexa Fluor 647 (1:400, Biolegend, 100209), anti-CD4 Alexa Fluor 700 (1:400, Biolegend, 100536), anti-CD8 BUV496 (1:400, BD, 569181), anti-CD19 PE-Cy5.5 (1:400, eBioscience, 35–0193-82), anti-Ly6G BB700 (1:200, BD, 566453), anti-NK1.1 APC-Cy7 (1:400, Biolegend, 108724), anti-F4/80 eFluor450 (1:200, eBioscience, 48–4801-82), anti-CD11b PerCP-Cy5.5 (1:400, Biolegend, 101228), anti-CD11c PE-Cy7 (1:400, BD, 558079), anti-MHCII BUV805 (1:200, BD, 748844), anti-Ly6C PerCP (1:400, Biolegend, 128028), anti-CD80 BV750 (1:200, BD, 741272), anti-CD86 RB780 (1:400, BD, 747439), anti-CD163 BUV615 (1:200, eBioscience, 366–1631-80), anti-CD206 PE-Cy5 (1:200, Biolegend, 141739). Cells were fixed with Foxp3 fixation and permeabilization buffer kits (eBioscience, 00–5521-00) for 20 min at 4 °C before cytokine staining. For cytokine staining, cells were labelled with following antibodies for 1 h at 4 °C: anti-IFNγ RY586 (1:400, BD, 568550), anti-TNFα BV421 (1:400, Biolegend, 506328), anti-Granzyme B RB744 (1:100, BD, 570498), anti-Perforin PE (1:200, eBioscience, 12–9392-80), anti-pro-IL-1β APC (1:200, eBioscience, 17–7114-80), anti-IL-4 BV480 (1:200, eBioscience, 414–7041-80), anti-IL-6 Alexa Fluor 488 (1:100, BD, 561363), anti-IL-10 BV510 (1:200, BD, 563277). Flow cytometry data were required using Cytek® Aurora (Cytek Biosciences) equipped with 5 lasers (355, 405, 488, 561, 640 nm) and 64 detectors, and all cytometric data were analyzed with FlowJo software v10.10 (FlowJo).

UMAP projection

All microglial cells were concatenated and analyzed using Flowjo UMAP v4.1.1 plugins after the adjustment of compensation. UMAP projection was performed with default settings of nearest neighbors of 15 and minimum distance of 0.5 using the Euclidean distance function with selected parameters: BUV737-A (CD45), eFluor450-A (F4/80), PerCP-Cy5.5-A (CD11b), RY586-A (IFNγ), BV421-A (TNFα), RB744-A (Granzyme B), PE-A (Perforin), APC-A (Pro-IL-1β), BV480-A (IL-4), Alexa Fluor 488-A (IL-6), BV510-A (IL-10).

Single cell RNA sequencing

Immune cells (CD45+ and CD45int) from the whole brain of LPS- or LCMV- or PBS-treated mice were collected and sorted on a FACS Aria III (BD) or SH800S (Sony) after staining with viability dye APC-eFluor780 (1:1000, eBioscience, 65–0865-14) and anti-CD45 PE (1:200, Tonbo, 50–0451-U100). Cells from 5 ~ 7 mice were pooled for one sample. The collected cells were suspended in DPBS and loaded onto a Chromium IX (10X Genomics) to generate 10,000 single-cell gel beads in emulsion. Libraries were constructed using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5’ Kit v2 (dual index) (10X Genomics) following manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA and final GEX library were qualified using high sensitivity D5000 ScreenTape with a Tapestation (Agilent Technologies). The final libraries were sent to Macrogen and sequenced using the NovaSeq6000 platform (Illumina).

Data processing and analysis

The Cell Ranger v8.0.1 (10X Genomics) pipeline was used to process scRNA-seq data. FASTQ files were generated using ‘cellranger mkfastq’ command and aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm38). Raw gene count matrices were generated using ‘cellranger count’ command.

Downstream analysis was carried out using the python package, ScanPy (v1.10.2). Samples were concatenated into a single Anndata object for consistent filtering. Low quality cells expressing less than 100 genes or abnormally high genes (> 7,000) were removed. Cells with high mitochondrial gene percentages (> 3%), and genes expressed by fewer than three cells (rare genes) were removed. Cell doublets were also removed using the scanpy.pp.scrublet. Libraries were then log-normalized with a scaling factor of 10,000 and gene expression was scaled to unit variance of 10 and mean of zero. For batch-effect correction and data integration, scvi-tools (v1.3.3) was used and the nearest neighbors’ distance matrix was computed by scanpy.pp.neighbors using the latent representation of the data. Cells were clustered based on Leiden algorithm using scanpy.tl.leiden with resolution of 1.0. UMAP dimensionality reduction was used for visualization. Cell types were determined using the following marker genes: T cells (Cd3e+), B cells (Cd79a+Cd19+), NK cells (Ncr1+), Macrophages (Cd14+Mrc1+), Dendritic cells (Clec9a+, Klk1+), Neutrophils (Hdc+), Microglia 1 (P2ry12+Tmem119+), Microglia 2 (P2ry12+Tmem119+Elmo1+Jmjd1c+), DAM (P2ry12+Tmem119+ Apoe+). Clusters not representing the above markers were excluded from the subsequent analysis. Differentially expressed genes for each cluster listed by scanpy.tl.rank_genes_groups were used to confirm cluster annotation.

Differential gene expression analysis

Differential gene expression analysis was performed between PBS- and LPS-/LCMV-treated mice or between 5xFAD and NonTg mice using a pseudobulk approach with PyDESeq2 (v0.5.0). For Microglia or DAM, three pseudo replicates with the same number of cells were randomly generated and raw read counts were merged across samples to create a pseudobulk count matrix.

Anndata object was created based on pseudobulk count matrix using pydeseq2.dds.DeseqDataSet function and genes expressed by fewer than one cells were removed. Dispersions were computed using pydeseq2.dds.DeseqDataSet.deseq2() functions. For statistical tests for differential expressions, pydeseq2.ds.DeseqStats function was utilized with default parameters. Genes with absolute value of log2FC > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.01 were considered to differentially expressed.

Gene set enrichment analysis

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was carried out using GSEApy (v1.1.5) against the gene set of Biological Process from the Gene ontology databases. A normalized enrichment score (NES) and corresponding false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted q-values were calculated using gseapy.gsea() function under the following conditions: permutation_num = 1000, min_size = 15, method = signal_to_noise. Only FDR-adjusted p-values below 0.01 were considered significant.

Public dataset analysis

For analysis of 5xFAD and NonTg mice, filtered feature-barcode matrix files from single-cell RNA sequencing of CD45int/+ immune cells from brain parenchyma were downloaded (GSE207702). The data were analyzed using the Scanpy pipeline, as described above, resulting in 10,304 cells from 5xFAD group and 7,447 cells from NonTg group. Cell types were defined using the marker genes as previously described [16]. Clusters not representing the marker genes were excluded from the subsequent analysis.

For analysis of K. Srinivasan et al. (2020), sorted cell RNA-seq dataset of myeloid cells from Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and healthy individuals were downloaded (GSE125050) [42]. The gene ID and corresponding raw counts were obtained, and the IDs were converted to gene symbols using Human gene annotation table (GRCh38) from NCBI. Raw counts were normalized by Trimmed Mean of M-values (TMM) method using the edgeR package (v4.6.0) in R (v4.4.2) and nRPKM values were calculated based on the normalized count values.

For analysis of T. Masuda et al. (2019), count matrix files from single-cell RNA sequencing of CD45+ cells isolated from Multiple sclerosis (MS)-affected and healthy human brains were downloaded (GSE124335) [15]. The cells expressing less than 100 genes, and the genes expressed by fewer than three cells were removed. Raw gene counts from cells of each individual sample were combined into one pseudobulk dataset. Count normalization followed by nRPKM calculation was performed as described above.

For analysis of D. Gosselin et al. (2017), RNA-seq dataset of microglia from human brain tissues were downloaded and author-provided TPM values were used [18].

Gene set score calculation

nRPKM values were calculated as described above and the gene set scores were computed as previously described by K. Srinivasan [42]. In brief, gene expression values were transformed as log2(nRPKM + 1), then the mean log-scale expression values of the controls were subtracted out from each gene in the dataset to obtain control-centered gene expression values. Gene set scores for each individual were then calculated by averaging the control-centered expression values of all genes within the corresponding gene set.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (v8.0.1) software by two-tailed unpaired t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Error bars represent ± Standard Error of the mean (SEM) calculated by Prism. Differences were considered statistically significant when p-values < 0.05. Data collection and analysis were not performed blind to experimental conditions.
