Abstract
. We study the space of conjugacy classes of subgroups of a compact Lie group G G whose identity component is a torus, and consider how various invariants of subgroups behave as sheaves over this space. This feeds in to the author’s programme to give algebraic models of rational G G -equivariant cohomology theories.



1.
Introduction

1.A.
Contents

The paper is part of a general programme to understand equivariant
cohomology theories. We explain this briefly for the interested reader
in Subsection
1.B
below, but the mathematical content
of the present paper is purely group theoretical.

This paper is about the space
\mathfrak{X}_{G}=\mathrm{Sub}(G)/G
of conjugacy classes
of (closed) subgroups of a compact Lie group
G
. More specifically it
considers the special case that
G
is a finite extension
of a torus:

1\longrightarrow\mathbb{T}\longrightarrow G\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi}}{{\longrightarrow}}W\longrightarrow 1.

It gives a method for identifying the set
\mathfrak{X}_{G}
as well as certain
additional structure, including the Hausdorff
metric topology and the normalizers of subgroups.

The group
G
is determined by an action of
W
on
\mathbb{T}
and a cohomology
class determining the extension. Since

\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{T})=\mathrm{Aut}(H_{1}(\mathbb{T};\mathbb{Z}))
, the action is
specified by the
\mathbb{Z}W
-module
\Lambda_{0}=H_{1}(\mathbb{T};\mathbb{Z})
,
which we call the
toral latice
.
We then write
L\mathbb{T}=\Lambda_{0}\otimes\mathbb{R}
for
the associated real representation; by virtue of the exponential map

\mathbb{T}=L\mathbb{T}/\Lambda_{0}
. The extension class
\epsilon(G)

lies in
H^{2}(W;\mathbb{T})\cong H^{3}(W;\Lambda_{0})
.

In effect, the identification of
\mathfrak{X}_{G}
is just an extended exercise exploiting Pontrjagin
duality, which states that subgroups of the compact Lie group

\mathbb{T}
correspond to subgroups of the discrete abelian group

\mathbb{T}^{*}=\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{T},T)
, where
T
is the circle group. The point
is to do it in such a way that we keep track of the dependence of
invariants on subgroups.

We partition the subgroups
K
according to the conjugacy class of the
image of
K
in
W
, and for the most part, we
consider only ‘full’ subgroups (those mapping onto
W
). To any full
subgroup
K
we associate the subgroup
S=K\cap\mathbb{T}
, which is
invariant under the conjugation action of
W
. This gives a map

c:\mathfrak{X}^{\mathrm{full}}_{G}\longrightarrow\mbox{$W$-$\mathrm{Sub}(\mathbb{T})$}\cong\mbox{$W$-$\mathrm{Sub}(\mathbb{T}^{*})$},

and we show (Corollary
3.5
) that
c
is an isomorphism up to finite indeterminacy: it
is almost surjective, and has finite fibres. There are finitely many
different fibres, and these are cohomologically determined. We can also understand how
other invariants of subgroups (such as the Weyl groups
W_{G}(K)
)
vary with
S
.

When working out
\mathfrak{X}_{G}
it is helpful to have
already worked out
\mathfrak{X}_{H}
for subgroups
H
of
G
, but some
modifications are necessary (i) several
H
-conjugacy classes may fuse to form a

G
-conjugacy class, so the space
\mathfrak{X}_{G}|H
is a quotient of
\mathfrak{X}_{H}
and (ii)
the normalizer of a subgroup
K
in
H
is a subgroup of the
normalizer in
G
and hence the
H
-Weyl group
W_{H}(K)
is a subgroup
of the
G
-Weyl group
W_{G}(K)
.

We show that the method is effective by giving explicit descriptions
for a number of rank 2 groups
G
. The reader wondering if this
apparatus is necessary might wish to try a direct approach on a simple
example. The case of the normalizer of the maximal torus in
U(2)
(which is
nothing more than the semidirect product
T^{2}\rtimes C_{2}
where

C_{2}
exchanges the factors) is instructive. The direct approach is
certainly possible, but the apparatus helps organize and
understand the answer.

1.B.
Motivation

We briefly explain the larger project of which this is a part.

For a compact Lie group
G
, it has been conjectured
[
6
]
that the category of
rational
G
-spectra is Quillen equivalent to differential graded
objects of an abelian category
\mathcal{A}(G)
. The abelian model

\mathcal{A}(G)
is a category of sheaves over the space
\mathfrak{X}_{G}=\mathrm{Sub}(G)/G
of conjugacy
classes of subgroups of
G
. The basic data associated to a subgroup

H^{*}(BW_{G}^{e}(K))
with an
action of the finite group
W_{G}^{d}(K)
where
W_{G}(K)=N_{G}(K)/K
is the Weyl group with
identity component
W_{G}^{e}(K)
and component group
W_{G}^{d}(K)
. As the
subgroup varies,
H^{*}(BW_{G}^{e}(K))
gives a ‘sheaf’ of rings, and

W_{G}^{d}(K)
gives a ‘component structure’.
The basic input data is therefore the space
\mathrm{Sub}(G)/G
together with
the Weyl group
W_{G}(K)
for each subgroup
K
.

The relevant structure on
\mathfrak{X}_{G}
comes from it being the
Balmer spectrum of finite rational
G
-spectra. The Zariski topology is determined by the Hausdorff metric
topology and the cotoral inclusion order
1
1

1

(K)\leq_{ct}(H)
if
K
is conjugate
to a subgroup
K^{\prime}
normal in
H
with
H/K^{\prime}
a torus
.

Part I
Subgroups of toral groups (generalities)

The purpose of Part 1 is to describe a method for the classification of conjugacy
classes of subgroups of a toral group
G
, which we will illustrate
in Part 2 by applying it to all rank 2 toral groups occurring in
connected compact Lie groups.

2.
Pontrjagin duality for subgroups of toral groups

We set the scene by partitioning subgroups by their image in the
component group, and introducing Pontrjagin duality.

2.A.
Crude partition

First we observe that for toral groups we can always partition
subgroups by their image in the component group. As before we consider
a toral group

1\longrightarrow\mathbb{T}\longrightarrow G\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi}}{{\longrightarrow}}W\longrightarrow 1.

Lemma 2.1
.

For a toral group
G
as above, the space

\mathfrak{X}_{G}=\mathrm{Sub}(G)/G
of conjugacy classes of subgroups of the toral group

G
is partitioned into pieces,
\mathcal{V}^{G}_{\overline{H}}
one for each conjugacy
class of subgroups
\overline{H}
of
W
.

If
\overline{H}\subseteq W
, the set

\mathcal{V}_{\overline{H}}^{G}=\{(K)\;|\;\pi(K)=\overline{H}\}

is clopen in the Hausdorff metric topology and
closed under passage to cotoral subgroups. Furthermore,

\mathcal{V}^{G}_{\overline{H}}
is dominated by
\pi^{-1}(\overline{H})
in the sense that it
consists of all subgroups cotoral in
\pi^{-1}(\overline{H})
.

Accordingly, the Balmer spectrum with its Zariski topology is a coproduct

\mathfrak{X}_{G}=\coprod_{(\overline{H})}\mathcal{V}_{\overline{H}}^{G}.

Proof :
The map
\pi
induces
\pi_{*}:\mathrm{Sub}(G)\longrightarrow\mathrm{Sub}(W)
, which is
continuous in the h-topology and compatible with conjugation.

Since a torus is connected, we see that if
K
is cotoral
in
H
then
\pi_{*}(K)=\pi_{*}(H)
. Conversely all subgroups
H
in

\mathcal{V}^{G}_{\overline{H}}
are cotoral in
\pi^{-1}(\overline{H})
. It is clear that

H
of
G
lies inside
\pi^{-1}(\overline{H})
where
\overline{H}=\pi(H)
. If
we pick a (set theoretic) splitting
\sigma
of
H\longrightarrow\overline{H}
then
H
is generated
by
S=H\cap\mathbb{T}
and
\sigma(\overline{H})
so
H
is normal in

\pi^{-1}(\overline{H})
and
\pi^{-1}(\overline{H})/H
is a quotient of
\mathbb{T}
and
hence a torus. Thus
H\in\mathcal{V}^{G}_{\overline{H}}
.

Thus
\pi_{*}
is continuous in the Zariski topology. The space

\mathrm{Sub}(W)/W
is discrete, and so the singletons give a partition into
clopen sets. Since
\pi_{*}
is continuous, their inverse images give
a partion of
\mathrm{Sub}(G)/G
.
∎

2.B.
Subgroups of a torus

Before considering subtleties, we consider
\mathcal{V}^{G}_{1}
, the subgroups
with image the trivial subgroup. More concretely, this is the space of
subgroups
S\leq\mathbb{T}
.
The group theory is very simple in this case, and the associated part
of the Balmer spectrum is always indecomposable. On the other hand, one
can argue that the complexity of the space
\mathcal{V}^{G}_{\overline{H}}
is governed by the rank
of the
\overline{H}
-fixed part of the torus
\mathbb{T}^{\overline{H}}
; since
\mathbb{T}
is of rank
r
, the part of the model
over
\mathcal{V}^{G}_{1}
is often the one of maximal complexity.

It is worth a little extra generality, so we consider an abelian
compact Lie group
A
and apply Pontrjagin duality
B^{*}=\mathrm{Hom}(B,T)
.
Associated to a subgroup
B\subseteq A
we have
the subgroup
B^{\dagger}=\ker(A^{*}\longrightarrow B^{*})
(since we are considering
subgroups of a fixed group
A
, the
dependence on
A
is not recorded in the notation).

Lemma 2.2
.

The dagger construction is an order reversing isomorphism
\mathrm{Sub}(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathrm{Sub}(A^{*})
. The cotoral ordering
on
\mathrm{Sub}(A)
(
C
is cotoral in
B
if
B/C
is a
torus) corresponds to the
cofree ordering
on
\mathrm{Sub}(A^{*})
(
M
is cofree in
N
if
N/M
is free).

Proof :
Pontrjagin duality is an exact functor so the two short exact
sequences

1\longrightarrow B\longrightarrow A\longrightarrow A/B\longrightarrow 1\mbox{ and }1\longleftarrow B^{*}\longleftarrow A^{*}\longleftarrow(A/B)^{*}\longleftarrow 1

are dual to each other. The group
A/B
is a torus if and only if

(A/B)^{*}
is a free abelian group.
∎

Our general strategy is to use Pontrjagin duality and work in terms of
sublattices of the dual toral lattice

\Lambda^{0}:=\mathbb{T}^{*}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{r}
. Because
W
acts on

\mathbb{T}
, it acts on
\Lambda^{0}
, and a
W
-invariant subgroup
S
of

\mathbb{T}
corresponds to a
W
-invariant sublattice
\Lambda^{S}:=S^{\dagger}
of

\Lambda^{0}
. If
S
is of dimension
d
then
\Lambda^{S}
is of
corank
d
. We note explicitly that
\Lambda^{S}
depends on
\mathbb{T}
as
well as
S
, and that
\Lambda^{0}
is the special case when
S
is
the trivial group.

Remark 2.3
.

As we use naturality, it is very important to be clear about variance.
We have

\Lambda^{0}=H^{1}(\mathbb{T};\mathbb{Z})=\mathrm{Hom}(H_{1}(\mathbb{T};\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{Z})=\mathrm{Hom}(\Lambda_{0},\mathbb{Z}),

so that the lattices
\Lambda^{0}
and
\Lambda_{0}
are dual.

3.
Classifying subgroups of toral groups

Considering even the case of
O(2)
we see that knowing the subgroups
is only the first step. For the applications to equivariant cohomology
theories, we need a calculational understanding of conjugacy.
We give cohomological methods for this, and the examples in Part 2
will provide convincing evidence of their effectiveness.

3.A.
Splittings

Let
H
be a full subgroup of
G
, and note that
S=H\cap\mathbb{T}
is
normal in
H
, so
S
is a
W
-invariant subgroup of
G
.
In particular, we have an extension

1\longrightarrow S\longrightarrow H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi}}{{\longrightarrow}}W\longrightarrow 1.

We choose a function
\sigma\colon W\longrightarrow H
splitting
\pi
; we will
say it is
normalised
if
\sigma(e)=e
. The subgroup
S
and
the section
\sigma
together specify
H
.
Given
G
and
S
, the condition that a
section
\sigma\colon W\to G
of
\pi\colon G\to W
determines a
subgroup
H
is that the so called
factor set

f_{\sigma}(v,w):=\sigma(vw)^{-1}\sigma(v)\sigma(w)
takes values in
S
. Indeed,
f_{\sigma}
is a cycle representing
the extension class
\epsilon(H)\in H^{2}(W;S)
.

Conversely, given a subgroup
S
of
T
invariant under
W
and a
section
\sigma
of
\pi
with factor set taking values in
S
,
we write
H(S,\sigma)
for the associated subgroup of
G
.

Example 3.1
.

If
G=O(2)
then
\mathbb{T}
is the circle and
W
is of order 2, and we
consider the case that
S
is cyclic of order
n
. The splittings
\sigma

correspond to a choice of reflection. We write

\sigma_{\theta}
for the splitting defined by taking

\sigma_{\theta}(w)
to be reflection in a line at an angle
\theta

to the
x

The splittings only depend on
\theta
mod
2\pi
, but
\sigma_{\theta}

and
\sigma_{\theta+\pi/n}
specify the same subgroup. Thus the space
of subgroups
H
with
H\cap\mathbb{T}=S
is the circle specified by
\theta

mod
\pi/n
. Finally, we observe that if we conjugate

\sigma_{\theta}
by a rotation by
\phi
we obtain

\sigma_{\theta+\phi}
and the circle of subgroups
H
with
H\cap\mathbb{T}

of order
n
constitute a single conjugacy class.

3.B.
Subgroups up to conjugacy

We have described the basic invariants of full subgroups of a toral group
in terms of the standard form
H(S,\sigma)
, and we have noted that

\sigma
determines a factor set representing the extension class

\epsilon(H)\in H^{2}(W;S)
. We describe the
G
-conjugacy classes of
such subgroups.

We can calculate cohomology with the bar resolution, with cochains

C^{i}(W;M)=\{f:W^{i}\longrightarrow M\}
. In particular,
C^{0}(W;M)=M
and low dimensional
coboundaries are as follows, with
m\in M,g:W\longrightarrow M,f:W^{2}\longrightarrow M
.

\delta(m)(v)=m^{v}-m,(\delta g)(v,w)=g(v)^{w}-g(vw)+g(w),\\
(\delta f)(u,v,w)=f(u,v)^{w}-f(u,vw)+f(uv,w)-f(v,w).

Now
\sigma:W\longrightarrow G
determines

f_{\sigma}(v,w)=\sigma(vw)^{-1}\sigma(v)\sigma(w)
, and since
G
is
a group
\delta f_{\sigma}=0
. We write

\mathrm{Split}(G|S)=\{\sigma:W\longrightarrow G\;|\;f_{\sigma}(v,w)\in S\}

for splittings which give subgroups of the form
H(S,\sigma)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

(i) If
\sigma:W\longrightarrow G
is a splitting with
f_{\sigma}(v,w)\in S

then
H(S,\sigma)
is a subgroup intersecting
\mathbb{T}
in
S
.

(ii)
H(S,\sigma)=H(S,\sigma\tau)
if
\tau\in C^{1}(W;S)
.

(iii) If

then
f_{\sigma g}=f_{\sigma}+\delta g
.

(iv)
tH(S,\sigma)t^{-1}=H(S,\sigma\delta t)
for
t\in\mathbb{T}=C^{0}(W;\mathbb{T})
.

Proof :
Parts (i) and (ii) are clear.

For Part (iii) we calculate

f_{\sigma g}(v,w)=[\sigma(vw)g(vw)]^{-1}\sigma(v)g(v)\sigma(w)g(w)=\\
g(vw)^{-1}\sigma(vw)\sigma(v)\sigma(w)g(v)^{w}g(w)=f_{\sigma}(v,w)g(vw)^{-1}g(v)^{w}g(w).

For Part (iv) we calculate

t\sigma(v)t^{-1}=\sigma(v)\sigma(v)^{-1}t\sigma(v)t^{-1}=\sigma(v)t^{v}t^{-1}.

∎

Lemma 3.3
.

If

, with

, giving

in

, and since we are assuming
\epsilon(G)

, we may assume that

takes values in
S
,
so that
\sigma_{0}
gives a subgroup

and

in
H^{2}(W;S)
.

Any other splitting
\sigma
takes the form

for

. Since

by

\lambda=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{r})
and
a_{1}>0
is the entry of the
largest modulus. Then if the point
P_{b}:=(x_{1}+b,x_{2},\ldots,x_{r})
lies in
\ker(\theta)
for
b=0
, then the point
P_{n/a_{1}}
lies in
\ker(\theta)
for every integer
n
. This shows that the distance from a
point to an element of
\ker(\theta)
is
\leq 1/(2a_{1})
. A little
trigonometry shows the maximum distance is
\geq 1/(2\sqrt{r}a_{1})
.
∎

This is sufficient to give an estimate for arbitrary subgroups, and we may now prove the proposition.

Proof :
First of all, if
\theta\in\Lambda^{S}
then
S\subseteq\ker(\theta)
so that

d(S,\mathbb{T})\geq d(\ker(\theta),\mathbb{T})>a/\mu(\theta)\geq a/\mu(\Lambda^{S}),

so that any convergent sequence must have
\mu(\Lambda^{S_{n}})\longrightarrow\infty
.

On the other hand, we may express
S
as an intersection of
codimension 1 subgroups so that

S=S^{c}\subset S^{c-1}\subset\cdots\subset S^{0}=\mathbb{T}

and

d(S,\mathbb{T})\leq\sum_{i=0}^{c-1}d(S^{i},S^{i+1}).

If
S^{i}=S^{i-1}\cap\ker(\theta_{i})
then

d(S^{i},S^{i-1})<d(\ker(\theta_{i}),\mathbb{T})<b/\mu(\theta_{i})
, so that if

\mu(\Lambda^{S_{n}})\longrightarrow\infty
then
d(S_{n},\mathbb{T})\longrightarrow 0
.
∎

Corollary 5.6
.

For any subgroup
S\subseteq\mathbb{T}
there is a base of neighbourhoods
\mathcal{N}_{t}(S)

of
S
so that the corresponding collection of lattices is

\mathcal{N}_{t}(\Lambda^{S})=\{\Lambda^{\prime}\supseteq\Lambda^{S}\;|\;\mu(\Lambda^{\prime})>1/t\}.

Remark 5.7
.

Since the number of vectors with all terms
<N
is finite for any
N
,
this base of neighbourhoods generates the same topology as the cofinite base of
neighbourhoods so the topology is entirely determined by containment of subgroups.

This lets us describe the space
\mathrm{Sub}(\mathbb{T})
as a compactification of
the discrete space
\mathfrak{X}_{0}
of finite subgroups. In fact we let

\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{r}=\mathrm{Sub}(\mathbb{T})

denote the dimension filtration, with

\mathcal{F}_{i}=\{S\subseteq\mathbb{T}\;|\;\dim(S)\leq i\}.

Now we define
\mathfrak{X}_{i}
to be the set
\mathcal{F}_{i}
with a suitable
topology. Indeed, we take
\mathfrak{X}_{0}:=\mathcal{F}_{0}
to be discrete and we define

\mathfrak{X}_{i}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{X}_{i+1}
to be a partial compactification. Indeed,

\mathfrak{X}_{i+1}
is obtained from
\mathfrak{X}_{i}
by adding the subgroups of
dimension
i+1
, and we give it a locally cofinite topology:
the neighbourhoods of the
(i+1)
-dimensional
subgroup
H
correspond to finite sets
\tau
of subgroups of
H

U_{\tau}:=\{K\;|\;H\supseteq K\not\in\tau\}.

One sees immediately that any set of subgroups with finitely many
maximal elements is compact, so the space
\mathfrak{X}=\mathfrak{X}_{r}
is compact.

We will discuss small examples of two types. First we consider the

7.B.
Rank 1

We first suppose that
