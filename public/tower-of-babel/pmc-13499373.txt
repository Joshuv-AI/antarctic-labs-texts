Enhancing the silage quality of Kangar (Gundelia tournefortii L.) as a potential forage for ruminants: The role of molasses, urea, and microbial inoculants
Authors: Mohsen Kazemi, Hassan Saleh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study was conducted to evaluate whether Kangar (Gundelia tournefortii L.) forage can be successfully preserved as silage despite its low water-soluble carbohydrate content and high buffering capacity, and to assess the individual and combined effects of molasses (Mol, 5% dry matter: DM), Lactobacillus plantarum inoculant (LAB, 1 × 10⁶ CFU/g fresh forage), and urea (1% DM) on fermentation quality, microbial populations, aerobic stability, and in vitro digestibility. In a completely randomized design with six replicates per treatment, silages were evaluated after 60 days of ensiling. The Mol+LAB treatment resulted in the lowest pH (3.98), highest lactic acid content (8.08% DM), highest Flieg score (113), highest aerobic stability (96.1 h), and lowest DM loss (58.8 g/kg DM) among all treatments (p < 0.001). This treatment also significantly reduced butyric acid, ammonia–N, and populations of mould, yeast, and Clostridium, while increasing LAB counts. In terms of nutritive value, Mol+LAB and Mol+Urea showed the highest in vitro digestibility of DM, organic matter, crude protein, and NDF, as well as the highest total volatile fatty acids and metabolizable energy (p < 0.001). Mol alone or combined with LAB effectively improved fermentation quality and digestibility, whereas urea alone increased crude protein but negatively affected fermentation (higher pH, ammonia–N, and butyric acid). Overall, the combination of Mol and LAB (Mol+LAB) was the most effective additive for producing high-quality Kangar silage with excellent fermentation characteristics, aerobic stability, and rumen fermentability, offering a viable strategy to enhance alternative forage utilization in arid and semi-arid regions.

Introduction

In recent decades, the growing global demand for livestock products, coupled with the increasing scarcity of high‑quality forage resources in arid and semi‑arid regions, has intensified the search for locally available alternative feedstuffs that can sustain or improve ruminant production (Kazemi and Ariapour, 2025; Kazemi and Kafi, 2025). Iran, with its vast rangeland areas covering approximately 52% of the country’s territory, supports a significant sheep industry; however, climate change, compounded by overgrazing, recurrent droughts, and limited water resources, has severely reduced the availability of conventional forages, forcing farmers to rely on low‑quality crop residues such as wheat straw (Kazemi and Ariapour, 2025; Savari, 2023). These residues, particularly wheat straw, are characterized by high lignocellulosic and lignin contents, low crude protein (CP) concentrations, and poor nutrient digestibility, which limit feed intake, ruminal fermentation efficiency, and ultimately animal productivity (Esbati et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022). Consequently, identifying and evaluating indigenous drought‑tolerant plants with adequate nutritional value has become a priority for sustainable livestock production in Iran and other parts of the Middle East.

One plant that shows considerable promise is Gundelia tournefortii L., commonly referred to as Kangar (also known as Akkoub or Tumble Thistle). This perennial herb belongs to the Asteraceae family and is native to the rangelands of Iran, Turkey, Iraq, Jordan, and other parts of the Eastern Mediterranean region (Hani et al., 2024). Historically, Kangar has served multiple purposes: it has been utilized as a seasonal vegetable, employed for its medicinal properties, and used as animal feed for sheep, goats, and camels when other forage sources were limited (Baydoun et al., 2015; Hani et al., 2024; Mehrzadeh et al., 2024). Furthermore, its ability to thrive under challenging environmental conditions such as extreme cold, drought, and nutrient-poor soils positions it as a robust option for cultivating forage in less productive, marginal areas (Kazemi and Ariapour, 2025).

From a nutritional standpoint, several studies have evaluated the chemical composition and digestibility of Kangar. Karabulut et al. (2006) reported that Kangar hay harvested at the beginning of flowering contained 11.2 % CP and 31.3 % crude fiber, with a metabolizable energy (ME) value of 10.5 MJ/kg DM, which was comparable to or even higher than that of alfalfa hay. Kamalak et al. (2005) demonstrated that the nutritive value of Kangar is strongly influenced by maturity stage, with early vegetative growth providing the highest CP (14.5 %) and ME (10.5 MJ/kg DM). In a more recent study conducted in northeastern Iran, Esbati et al. (2021) found that CP decreased from 14.9 % at the vegetative stage to 9.5 % at the seeding stage, while ME ranged from 11.77 to 10.46 MJ/kg DM. These authors concluded that the optimal harvest time for Kangar for livestock feeding is the vegetative period (March–April). Moreover, the plant contains a range of bioactive compounds, including phenolic acids, flavonoids, saponins, tocopherols, and phytosterols, which have demonstrated antioxidant, anti‑inflammatory, and hypolipidemic properties in various experimental models (Bati et al., 2021; Gholami‑Ahangaran et al., 2022; Hachem et al., 2025; Hani et al., 2024).

Despite its promising nutritional and nutraceutical potential, the utilization of fresh Kangar forage is constrained by its high moisture content and seasonal availability. Ensiling offers a practical solution to preserve the forage for year‑round feeding. However, fresh Kangar forage, like many leguminous and herbaceous species, has a moderate to low water‑soluble carbohydrates (WSC) content and a high buffering capacity, which can hinder proper lactic acid fermentation and lead to poor silage quality, characterized by elevated pH, excessive proteolysis, and the proliferation of spoilage microorganisms such as clostridia, yeasts, and molds (Kung et al., 2018; Okoye et al., 2023).

To overcome these limitations, additives such as molasses (Mol, a sugar source providing substrates for lactic acid bacteria: LAB), Lactobacillus plantarum inoculants (to accelerate acidification and inhibit undesirable microbes), and urea (to enhance CP, though potentially increasing buffering capacity) are widely employed (Chen et al., 2021; Gao et al., 2021; Muck et al., 2018). While these additives have been extensively studied in conventional crops (Olorunlowu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025), research on Gundelia tournefortii ensiling remains scarce, with only limited reports examining basic additives (Ayoubi Far et al., 2021; Zarrin et al., 2018). To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has systematically evaluated the preservation dynamics and multidimensional responses of this non-conventional forage using individual and combined additives. To address these knowledge gaps, this study provides the first comprehensive evaluation of Kangar (Gundelia tournefortii L.) silage using a broad, multidimensional approach that integrates chemical composition, microbiological profiling, fermentation characteristics, and in vitro ruminal fermentation assessments.

Specifically, this study was designed with three specific objectives: (1) to determine whether Kangar forage can be successfully preserved as silage despite its low WSC content and high buffering capacity; (2) to evaluate the individual effects of Mol (5% DM), a Lactobacillus plantarum inoculant (LAB, 1 × 10⁶ CFU/g fresh forage), and urea (1% DM) on chemical composition, fermentation quality, microbial populations, aerobic stability, and in vitro digestibility; and (3) to assess whether additive combinations (Mol+LAB and Mol+Urea) produce superior improvements in silage preservation quality and feeding value compared with individual additive treatments in a controlled trial with six replicates per treatment (n = 6). By evaluating these additive strategies, this research aims to provide a practical and immediately applicable solution, particularly through the Mol+LAB combination, for livestock farmers in arid and semi-arid environments seeking to optimize alternative forage preservation.

Materials and methods

Forage preparation and experimental design

Fresh Gundelia tournefortii L. (Kangar) forage was harvested at the early flowering stage from natural rangelands in Kashmar County, Razavi Khorasan Province, Iran (approximately 35°14′ N, 58°27′ E). The plants were collected manually, chopped into 2–3 cm pieces using a forage chopper, and thoroughly mixed to ensure homogeneity. The moisture content of the fresh forage was determined to be approximately 75%, which was then wilted to about 70% moisture by spreading in a shaded, well-ventilated area for 12–24 h.

Six silage treatments were prepared with six replicates each (total 36 experimental units). The treatments were as follows:•Control: no additive.•Mol: addition of molasses at 5% of dry matter (DM).•LAB: inoculation with a commercial Lactobacillus plantarum strain (Probioway Company, Iran) at 1 × 10⁶ CFU/g fresh forage.•Urea: addition of urea at 1% of DM.•Mol+LAB: combination of molasses (5% DM) and LAB (1 × 10⁶ CFU/g fresh forage).•Mol + Urea: combination of molasses (5% DM) and urea (1% DM).

Treatment application and ensiling

For additive preparation, a minimal volume of distilled water was used to dissolve the additives without excessively increasing the moisture content of the forage. The final application volume was standardized to 10 mL of solution per kg of fresh forage for all treatments, including the control, to ensure uniform distribution of the additives while minimizing any effect on forage moisture content. Accordingly, Mol, urea, and the commercial LAB inoculant were each prepared in distilled water to obtain a final application volume of 10 mL/kg fresh forage. For the combination treatments (Mol+LAB and Mol + Urea), both additives were dissolved together in the same 10 mL volume of distilled water immediately before application. The control received 10 mL of distilled water per kg of fresh forage without additives. All prepared solutions were sprayed uniformly onto the chopped forage, followed by thorough manual mixing for 5 min to ensure homogeneous distribution. Finally, exactly 500 ± 3.5 g of each treated forage was packed into 25 × 35 cm polyethylene vacuum bags (six bags per treatment; the initial packed weight was identical across all treatments), sealed using a vacuum sealer (DZQ-390, Anshengke, Fujian, China), and stored at ambient temperature (22 ± 2°C) for 60 days to allow fermentation.

Chemical composition of silage

At the end of the ensiling period (60 d), all bags were opened and subsamples were taken for analysis. For DM determination, approximately 100 g of fresh silage was dried in a forced-air oven at 65°C for 48 h to constant weight (AOAC, 2005; method 934.01). Dried samples were ground to pass a 1-mm sieve using a laboratory mill (SM300, Chile Ltd., Shanghai, China) and stored in airtight containers for further analysis.

The CP was determined by the Kjeldahl method (N × 6.25) according to AOAC method 976.05 (AOAC, 2005). Ether extract (EE) was determined using a Soxhlet apparatus (Bakhshi, Iran) with petroleum ether (AOAC method 973.18). Ash content was measured by incineration in a muffle furnace at 550°C for 4 h (AOAC method 942.05). Neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and acid detergent fiber (ADF) were analyzed sequentially using an Ankom 200 Fiber Analyzer (Ankom Technology, Macedon, NY, USA) following the procedure of Van Soest et al. (1991). Sodium sulfite and heat-stable α-amylase were used for NDF analysis, and results were expressed inclusive of residual ash. The WSC were determined by the anthrone-sulfuric acid colorimetric method (Nelson, 1944) using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Azura, Germany). Calcium (Ca) and phosphorus (P) concentrations were determined by atomic absorption spectrophotometry (SavantAA, GBC, Australia) and the molybdovanadate method, respectively.

Fermentation characteristics and microbial analysis

For fermentation analysis, 20 g of fresh silage were mixed with 180 mL of distilled water and homogenized for 2 min using a stomacher (MIX2, AES Laboratoire, Combourg, France). The mixture was filtered through four layers of cheesecloth and then through Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The filtrate was used immediately for pH measurement (pH meter, Model HI 2210‐01, Hanna Instruments, USA) and then stored at −20°C for subsequent analyses (Kazemi et al., 2024).

Lactic acid, acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid concentrations were determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using a Waters 2489 UV/Vis detector set at 210 nm (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA). Separation was performed on an Aminex HPX-87H column (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) with 0.005 M H₂SO₄ as mobile phase at a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min and column temperature of 35°C. Ammonia–N (NH3–N) was determined by the phenol-hypochlorite method (Broderick and Kang, 1980) and expressed as percentage of total nitrogen (TN = CP / 6.25). The DM loss (g/kg DM) was calculated as the difference between the DM weight before and after ensiling divided by the initial DM weight and multiplied by 1000 (Kohler et al., 2013).

For microbial enumeration, 10 g of fresh silage were homogenized with 90 mL of sterile 0.85% NaCl solution in a stomacher for 2 min, and serial decimal dilutions (10−110−7) were prepared. An aliquot of 0.1 mL from appropriate serial dilutions was spread-plated in duplicate onto selective agar media. The LAB were counted on de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) agar (Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK) after anaerobic incubation at 30°C for 72 h (ISO 15214:1998). Total bacteria were enumerated on plate count agar (PCA) after aerobic incubation at 30°C for 72 h (ISO 4833-1:2013). Moulds and yeasts were counted on potato dextrose agar (PDA) supplemented with chloramphenicol after aerobic incubation at 25°C for 5 days (moulds) and 3 days (yeasts) (ISO 21527-1:2008). Clostridium spp. were enumerated using tryptose sulfite cycloserine (TSC) agar under anaerobic conditions at 37°C for 48 h (ISO 7937:2004). Microbial counts obtained from plate enumeration were adjusted according to the DM content of each silage sample and expressed as log₁₀ CFU/g DM. Aerobic stability was assessed by exposing approximately 500 g of silage from each silo to air at room temperature (22–25°C). The silage was loosely placed in open plastic containers and monitored for temperature changes during aerobic exposure. Ambient temperature was recorded simultaneously throughout the aerobic exposure period. Aerobic stability was defined as the number of hours required for silage temperature to rise more than 2°C above ambient temperature after exposure to air, following the method of Kung et al. (2000). The Flieg score was calculated using the formula: Flieg score = 220 + [2 × (DM% − 15)] − (40 × pH) (Kilic, 1986), where scores ≥101 indicate very good, 81–100 good, 61–80 satisfactory, 41–60 medium, and ≤ 20 poor quality.

In vitro digestibility and gas production

Rumen fluid was collected from three ruminally fistulated Moghani male lambs (body weight 30 ± 3.5 kg) fed a maintenance diet of alfalfa hay (60%) and concentrate (40%) twice daily. The fluid was strained through four layers of cheesecloth and maintained at 39°C under continuous CO₂ flushing. The artificial rumen buffer solution was prepared according to Menke and Steingass (1988). In vitro gas production was performed following the procedure of Theodorou et al. (1994). Dried silage samples (200 mg on a DM basis, ground to 1 mm) were weighed into 120 mL fermentation bottles. Rumen fluid and artificial saliva were mixed in a 1:2 ratio (rumen fluid:saliva), and 30 mL of the mixture was added to each bottle under CO₂. In each incubation run, six blank bottles containing only 30 mL of the buffered rumen fluid mixture (without forage substrate) were included to measure baseline gas production from the inoculum. Net gas production values were calculated by subtracting the average gas volume produced by the blank bottles from the total gas produced by substrate-containing bottles. Six replicates were used per treatment. To increase the reliability and reproducibility of the measurements, the in vitro incubation was performed in two independent runs under identical experimental conditions. Bottles were sealed and incubated in a water bath at 39°C for 96 h. Gas pressure was measured at 3, 6, 9, 12, 24, 48, 72 and 96 h of incubation using pressure transducers, and pressure values were converted to gas volume (mL) according to Theodorou et al. (1994). Cumulative gas production data were fitted to the exponential model: GP = b × (1 – e⁻ᶜᵗ), where b is the potential gas production (mL/200 mg DM), c is the fractional rate constant (h⁻¹), and t is incubation time (h) (Ørskov and McDonald, 1979).

After 24 h of in vitro incubation (corresponding to the standard endpoint for gas production measurement according to Menke and Steingass, 1988), the pH of the fermentation fluid was measured immediately using a pH meter (Model HI 2210-01, Hanna Instruments, USA). Subsequently, a 5-mL aliquot of the fermentation fluid was collected from each bottle and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatant was then stored at −20°C for subsequent determination of total volatile fatty acids (TVFA) and ammonia–N. The TVFA concentration was measured using the steam distillation method described by John et al. (1957) with a Markham distillation apparatus. The residual contents from the gas production bottles were filtered through pre-weighed sintered glass crucibles (porosity 1), dried at 105°C to constant weight, and ashed at 550°C to calculate in vitro dry matter digestibility (IVDMD), in vitro organic matter digestibility (IVOMD), in vitro crude protein digestibility (IVCPD), and in vitro neutral detergent fiber digestibility (IVNDFD) according to the following equations (Kazemi and Ghasemi Bezdi, 2021):•IVDMD (%) = [(DM input – DM residue) / DM input] × 100•IVOMD (%) = [(OM input – OM residue) / OM input] × 100•IVCPD (%) = [(CP input – CP residue) / CP input] × 100•IVNDFD (%) = [(NDF input – NDF residue) / NDF input] × 100

The ME (MJ/kg DM) was estimated using the formula: ME = 2.20 + 0.1357 × GP₂₄ₕ + 0.0057 × CP + 0.0002859 × EE² (Menke and Steingass, 1988), where GP₂₄ₕ is the net gas production (mL) after 24 h of incubation per 200 mg DM, CP is crude protein (g/kg DM), and EE is ether extract (g/kg DM). Net energy for lactation (NEL, MJ/kg DM) was calculated as NEL = 0.54 + 0.0959 × GP₂₄ₕ + 0.0038 × CP + 0.0001733 × EE² (Menke and Steingass, 1988), where GP₂₄ₕ is net gas production after 24 h incubation (mL/200 mg DM), CP is crude protein (g/kg DM), and EE is ether extract (g/kg DM).

Ruminal fermentation parameters were determined after 24 h incubation because the gas production system of Menke and Steingass (1988) and the associated prediction equations (ME and NEL) for energy evaluation are based on 24-h fermentation measurements. Therefore, pH, NH3–N and VFA measurements were performed at the same incubation endpoint.

Statistical analysis

The experimental unit was the laboratory silo. Six independent silos were prepared for each treatment, resulting in a total of 36 experimental units (6 treatments × 6 replicates). All chemical, microbiological, fermentation, and in vitro measurements were conducted using samples obtained from these independent silos. For the in vitro gas production assay, incubations were performed in two independent runs under identical experimental conditions to improve measurement reliability and reproducibility. The average values obtained from the two runs for each experimental unit were used for statistical analysis.

The experiment was conducted as a completely randomized design. All data are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Normality of residuals was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was verified using Levene’s test. Data were analyzed using the GLM procedure of SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). The statistical model used was: Yij = μ + Ti + εij, where Yij is the observed response variable, μ is the overall mean, Ti is the fixed effect of treatment (i = 1–6), and εij is the random residual error.

Gas production values at each incubation time were analyzed separately to evaluate discrete treatment differences at biologically key incubation intervals (e.g., 24 h for energy estimation and 96 h for total gas potential), a procedure commonly adopted in in vitro gas production studies focusing on specific fermentation thresholds. Treatment means were compared using Tukey’s honest significant difference test, and differences were considered significant at p < 0.05. Fermentation characteristics, dry matter loss, microbial populations, and aerobic stability

Fermentation parameters are summarized in Table 3. All treatments differed significantly in pH (p < 0.001). The lowest pH was recorded in Mol+LAB (3.98), followed by LAB (4.15) and Mol (4.21). Control (4.82) and urea (4.65) had the highest pH values. Discussion

The present study demonstrated that Kangar forage can be successfully preserved through ensiling, although silage quality was strongly influenced by additive type. The results demonstrated that the combination of Mol and LAB (Mol+LAB) produced the highest quality silage, as evidenced by the lowest pH, highest lactic acid content, highest Flieg score, greatest aerobic stability, and lowest DM loss. In contrast, urea alone, while increasing CP content, negatively affected fermentation quality by elevating pH, ammonia–N, and butyric acid.

Chemical composition

Compared to the initial chemical composition of fresh Kangar forage (Table 1), ensiling expectedly altered the nutrient profile and fermentation matrix across treatments. The significant increase in CP content in urea‑containing silages (urea and Mol+Urea) was expected, as urea is a non‑protein nitrogen source that adds nitrogen to the silage mass (Méndez-Llorente et al., 2025). However, the higher CP in these treatments did not translate into better fermentation quality, as discussed below.

The reduction in NDF and ADF in Mol and LAB treatments is consistent with previous reports that adding fermentable carbohydrates and LAB may contribute to partial degradation of structural carbohydrates and changes in fiber concentration during ensiling (Lunsin et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2020). According to recent literature, successful silage fermentation requires an adequate concentration of WSC, typically ranging from approximately 3% to 10% of DM depending on forage species, DM content, buffering capacity, and the use of silage additives. Insufficient WSC may restrict lactic acid production and delay pH decline during ensiling (Ali and Tahir, 2021; Ertekin and Yilmaz, 2026).

The elevated WSC content in Mol‑treated silages reflects the direct contribution of Mol sugars and indicates that sufficient substrate was available for LAB fermentation (Gao et al., 2021). The increased DM content in Mol+LAB compared to control may reflect reduced nutrient losses during ensiling, although the direct contribution of Mol solids to DM content cannot be excluded.

Fermentation quality and microbial populations

Before ensiling, the application of additives modified the initial fermentation substrate of Kangar forage. The Mol increased the readily available carbohydrate supply, providing additional fermentable sugars for LAB, whereas urea increased the nitrogen availability of the ensiled material. Therefore, the subsequent differences observed among treatments should be interpreted as a combined effect of the initial additive-induced changes and the fermentation processes occurring during ensiling.

The pH of silage is a critical indicator of fermentation success. The optimal pH for well‑fermented silage is below 4.2 (Kung et al., 2018; Yi et al., 2023). In this study, only treatments containing Mol or LAB (alone or in combination) achieved pH values below 4.2, with Mol+LAB showing the lowest pH (3.98). This lower pH is likely associated with the substantial production of lactic acid by LAB (8.08% DM in Mol+LAB), which serves as a primary driver of silage acidification (Okoye et al., 2023; Soundharrajan et al., 2021). Conversely, urea‑treated silages had elevated pH (4.65–4.29) because urea hydrolyzes to ammonia, which has a strong buffering capacity and neutralizes organic acids. The high ammonia‑N levels in urea‑containing silages further reflect extensive proteolysis and deamination, which are undesirable in silage (Kazemi et al., 2024; Khan et al., 2023; Kung et al., 2018).

The fermentation profile observed in the Mol+LAB treatment (lactic acid: 8.08% DM, acetic acid: 1.21% DM, propionic acid: 0.25% DM, and butyric acid: 0.11% DM) aligns closely with established benchmarks for high-quality silages (Kung et al., 2018; Muck et al., 2018). High lactic acid coupled with low acetic and trace butyric acid concentrations is consistent with a predominantly homolactic fermentation pathway. Butyric acid is produced by Clostridium spp., which thrive at high pH and low sugar conditions (Huffman et al., 2023; Kung et al., 2018). The marked reduction in Clostridium spp. counts in Mol+LAB (2.64 log₁₀ CFU/g DM) compared to control (4.25) suggests that rapid acidification helped inhibit clostridial growth. Similarly, the low yeast and mould counts in Mol+LAB contributed to its superior aerobic stability, as yeasts are the primary initiators of aerobic deterioration (Liang et al., 2026; Okoye et al., 2023). The high Flieg score (113) obtained for Mol+LAB classified the silage as “very good” quality, indicating favorable fermentation characteristics, including a low pH and adequate DM content, which are generally associated with an efficient and well-controlled ensiling process (Segato et al., 2022).

Aerobic stability and dry matter loss

Aerobic stability, measured as the time taken for silage temperature to rise 2°C above ambient after air exposure, was significantly improved by Mol and LAB addition. The Mol+LAB treatment exhibited the highest aerobic stability (96.1 h), which is more than 30 h longer than the control (64.1 h). The improved aerobic stability was likely associated with the lower pH and reduced yeast and mould populations (Kung et al., 2018; Olorunlowu et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2024). The reduced yeast counts in Mol+LAB (3.77 log₁₀ CFU/g DM) compared to control (4.80) support this interpretation. DM loss followed the opposite trend, with the highest loss in control (92.5 g/kg DM) and the lowest in Mol+LAB (58.8 g/kg DM). This reduction reflects the more efficient preservation of nutrients in well‑fermented silage, where less organic matter is lost as CO₂, heat, or effluent (Borreani et al., 2018; Muck et al., 2020).

In vitro digestibility and rumen fermentation parameters

The in vitro digestibility parameters (IVDMD, IVOMD, IVCPD, IVNDFD) were all significantly higher in Mol+LAB compared to control and most other treatments. The improved digestibility may be associated with the lower NDF and ADF content in Mol+LAB silage, which likely made the fiber fraction more accessible to rumen microorganisms, alongside improved preservation of structural carbohydrates during ensiling (Li et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2020). The higher TVFA concentration in Mol+LAB (95.4 mmol/L) suggests greater availability of fermentable substrates for rumen microbes, resulting in increased fermentation activity (Bai et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2023). The lower ammonia–N in the Mol+LAB incubation medium (14.9 mg/dL) compared to urea (24.1 mg/dL) suggests reduced protein degradation and improved nitrogen retention during rumen fermentation, which is consistent with the lower ammonia–N concentration observed in the silage itself (Ho et al., 2025; Hong et al., 2025).

The higher estimated ME and NEL values in Mol+LAB (9.66 and 5.91 MJ/kg DM, respectively) compared to control (8.56 and 5.12) indicate that the combination of Mol and LAB resulted in higher calculated energy values, reflecting improved OM fermentability. The higher gas production observed in Mol+LAB (Fig. 2) indicates greater availability of fermentable substrates and enhanced microbial fermentation activity in vitro. Gas production is closely associated with volatile fatty acid (VFA) production, and together they provide complementary information on ruminal fermentation patterns. However, gas production measurements primarily reflect fermentation kinetics under controlled laboratory conditions and should not be interpreted as a direct predictor of feed intake or animal performance. Therefore, the higher gas production potential observed in Mol+LAB suggests improved fermentability of the silage, which requires further validation through in vivo feeding studies.

Comparison of additive combinations

Among the evaluated treatments, the two additive combinations (Mol+LAB and Mol+Urea) showed better fermentation characteristics than the individual additives. However, Mol+LAB generally produced a more favorable fermentation profile than Mol+Urea, as indicated by lower pH, higher lactic acid concentration, lower ammonia–N and butyric acid concentrations, reduced undesirable microbial populations, improved aerobic stability, and greater in vitro digestibility.

These differences reflect treatment effects under the experimental conditions based on one-way ANOVA results rather than predictive outcomes for animal performance. The superior fermentation profile of Mol+LAB may be attributed to the concurrent application of Mol and the LAB inoculant. The Mol provides readily fermentable substrates, while LAB promotes rapid lactic acid production and suppression of undesirable microorganisms (Gao et al., 2021; Muck et al., 2018).

In contrast, Mol+Urea combines a fermentable carbohydrate source with a non-protein nitrogen source. Although urea inclusion successfully enhanced the CP content of the silage mass, this CP enrichment involved a clear physiological trade-off with fermentation quality. Urea rapidly hydrolyzes into ammonia during the early stages of ensiling, which substantially increases the buffering capacity of the ensiled mass and neutralizes organic acids. Consequently, this buffering effect retarded the rapid decline of pH, thereby creating a favorable environment for secondary fermentation, increased proteolysis, and elevated butyric acid and ammonia–N production compared with Mol+LAB (Chen et al., 2021). Thus, while Mol+Urea offers nutritional nitrogen enrichment, Mol+LAB proved to be the superior combination for rapidly establishing dominant homolactic fermentation, minimizing nutrient degradation, and ensuring long-term microbial stability under the present experimental conditions.

Study limitations and future perspectives

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. First, the experiment was conducted under laboratory-scale ensiling conditions using a single harvest of Kangar forage from one region and fixed inclusion levels of Mol, LAB inoculant, and urea. Variation in plant ecotype, soil type, climate, and harvest season may influence forage composition and ensiling characteristics; therefore, the results should be interpreted within the scope of the experimental conditions employed.

Second, the evaluation of silage quality was based on fermentation characteristics and in vitro ruminal fermentation responses, whereas animal performance, feed intake, nutrient utilization, and long-term production responses were not assessed. Additionally, the replication number (n = 6) was chosen based on standard laboratory practice in similar silage evaluation studies rather than a formal pre-experiment statistical power analysis.

Third, cumulative in vitro gas production data were evaluated separately at each incubation interval rather than using a repeated-measures ANOVA design; while this approach allows direct treatment comparisons at critical time points, it does not fully account for temporal correlations between successive measurements.

Fourth, the study utilized a completely randomized single-factor design rather than a full factorial arrangement; consequently, main factorial effects and statistical interaction terms (synergy or antagonism) between additives could not be explicitly tested.

Future studies should address these limitations by evaluating Kangar silage under practical farm conditions through in vivo feeding trials to assess animal performance, feed intake, and nutrient utilization; investigating multiple inclusion rates of Mol, LAB, and urea to determine their optimal application levels for maximizing silage quality and feeding value; applying repeated-measures statistical models for time-course data, where appropriate; employing full factorial experimental designs to quantify the main effects of additives and their interactions; and examining the dynamics of bioactive compounds (e.g., phenolics and flavonoids) in Kangar forage during ensiling and their potential biological effects in ruminants.

Conclusion

As the first comprehensive evaluation of Kangar silage incorporating a multidimensional assessment framework (integrating chemical, microbiological, fermentative, and in vitro analyses), the findings of this study indicate that the combination of 5% Mol with LAB (Mol+LAB) resulted in the most favorable overall fermentation characteristics among the evaluated Kangar silages. This treatment was associated with improved silage quality, likely by facilitating rapid acidification, as indicated by the lowest pH (3.98), highest lactic acid concentration (8.08% DM), lowest butyric acid content (0.11% DM), highest Flieg score as an integrated quality index, and greatest aerobic stability (96.1 h), while reducing DM losses and undesirable microbial populations. Moreover, Mol+LAB enhanced in vitro digestibility, ruminal fermentation characteristics, and estimated ME, suggesting greater potential fermentability of the resulting silage under laboratory conditions. Although urea supplementation increased CP content, it negatively affected fermentation quality by increasing pH, ammonia–N, and butyric acid concentrations. The addition of Mol partially improved the fermentation characteristics of urea-treated silage, but it did not achieve the overall performance observed with Mol+LAB. Under the conditions of the present study, the concurrent application of Mol and LAB inoculation provides an immediate, highly practical, and accessible strategy for livestock producers and farmers in arid and semi-arid regions to improve the preservation quality and nutritive value of non-conventional forages like Kangar. However, further in vivo studies are needed to confirm its effects on animal performance, nutrient utilization, and the economic feasibility of applying these additives under practical feeding conditions.
