Relationship Quality Among Married Indian Couples: A Narrative Review
Authors: Akanksha Mendiratta, Pankaj Singh, Navin Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndian marriages have traditionally been understood as fulfilling religious commitments, forming alliances between families and fostering companionship between spouses. However, relationship quality in such marriages is shaped by structural and cultural factors that Western models of marriage, which consider only the dyadic relationship, do not account for. This narrative review examines relationship quality among married Indian couples across three themes: caste and joint-family structure; communication and conflict; and intimacy, identity and sexual satisfaction, to understand how relationship quality is shaped beyond the couple.SummaryThe review draws on attachment theory, social exchange theory and family systems theory, as well as on indigenous Indian thought and the feminist bargaining-power framework of Sen and Agarwal, alongside broader cultural and philosophical perspectives. It considers how structure and individual agency combine in the process of Indian marriage, drawing on narrative, empirical, theoretical, economic and philosophical sources, as well as Indian legal decisions. Caste and kinship structure a wife’s bargaining power and household authority before dyadic interactions occur; communication and conflict are shaped by joint-family privacy rather than the dyad alone; and emotional intimacy and identity shape relationship quality, while sexual satisfaction remains comparatively unexplored in the Indian context. The review proposes two original theoretical constructs, active endurance, which extends Das’s notion of endurance, and the aspiration–structure gap, to capture patterns evident in the literature but not yet formally theorised. It also notes neurobiological evidence, namely hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis dysregulation in chronic marital distress and oxytocin’s modulation of couple conflict, which warrants investigation in Indian samples.Key MessageTherapy and policy addressing relationship quality in Indian marriages need to account for structural barriers, such as caste and household bargaining power, rather than focusing only on communication skills and attitudes, if they are to meaningfully improve relationship quality in this context.

Introduction

In India, marriage has conventionally organised families and forged alliances between kinships. This function is now diversifying as individual qualifications, compatibility, urbanisation, education and women’s increasing involvement in the workforce reshape partner selection. 1 Concurrently, the global dissemination of ideas about companionate relationships is altering people’s expectations concerning the kinds of emotional support that marriage should provide. 2 With increased opportunities and greater possibilities for failure, relationship quality within the Indian marital context thus becomes an important sociological issue. This review argues that the quality of marital relationships in the Indian context cannot be dependent on the couple as the sole unit of analysis. Rather, it emerges due to the interface between structure (including the caste system and kinship) and psychological factors (communication, conflict, attachment, identity and sexual satisfaction), making the study of purely dyadic models insufficient on their own.

The importance of such framing stems from the realisation that much of our knowledge about relationship quality comes from Western social psychology, which frequently views the couple as a self-contained unit and determines relationship quality by individual satisfaction, balance of power between partners and openness of communication. 3 In India, marriage is not an institution separate from family and operates within a kinship system that shapes partner choice through caste 4 and that determines who holds power, who deserves respect and the quantity as well as openness of communication acceptable in joint-family structures.5, 6 Applying the concept of relationship quality developed in the West without modification could lead to a misunderstanding of normal behaviour, such as communicative discretion and respect for elders in a joint family as personal shortcomings within the relationship. These structural features of caste and kinship therefore form the starting point of this review’s first research question (RQ).

If caste and joint family represent the external parameters of marriage, then communication can be considered to be one of the clearest channels for achieving relationship quality within such parameters. Another well-studied phenomenon is conflict, which has a specific and well-documented shape: spouses tend to experience conflicts over money, chores, children and relatives, with more wives than husbands reporting relatives as a source of tension at all, though daily argument frequency on this topic was nearly identical between genders. 7 One of these reasons arises from the inherent contradiction that lies at the heart of the kinship role, where the wife is supposed to be on good terms with her mother-in-law, but in reality, both may not even know each other for quite some time. 8 Moreover, the extended family also limits the independence of decision-making and expression that can be had by the wife.5, 6

Apart from the issues related to structure and communication, there is yet another aspect of marriage that must be considered, involving the level of emotional intimacy and sexual satisfaction of couples. Ghosh and Chaurasiya 9 studied 98 married individuals in India, and it was concluded that couples who had quality time together and a high level of ‘us-ness’ in their relationships perceived their marriages as successful. Sexual satisfaction has been relatively neglected in the literature on couples, not because of lack of importance, but due to its lack of openness. Literature dealing with the theme of married couples is based on a mostly Western understanding that marriage consists of two separate autonomous individuals deciding to live together, which is associated with a cultural context where people consider themselves as separate beings. 10 However, within joint families, the formation of personal identity occurs in connection with family relations prior to marriage. Thus, the main question changes from the way in which an individual identity separate from family can be built to the way in which it is built within a family setting.9, 11

Theoretical Frameworks

Marriages in India operate on three levels, matching RQ1–RQ3: structural and societal contexts, day-to-day communication and the intimate experience of the relationship. No single theoretical construct built for Western dyads can account for all three levels. This review therefore draws on several frameworks, organised from the wider context to the individual, 12 matching the RQ1–RQ3 sequence above, as depicted in Table 1.

Caste and joint families operate at the structural level, prior to any interactive or psychological analysis, through the three indigenous frameworks in Table 1: kinship system, 5 fallback position13, 14 and caste boundaries on marriageability. 15 Relationship quality is therefore not homogeneous across Indian marriages; the structural variation is precisely what RQ1 is designed to capture.

Communication and conflict persistence in Indian marriages emerge from this same structural context through three interactional frameworks in Table 1. The investment model 16 relates commitment to past investments and not to present satisfaction, an aspect that is evident in studies conducted by Indians to study the sustainability of relationships, in which the bonding between the couple and the well-being of the partners determine marital satisfaction. 22 Social exchange theory 18 considers the benefits in relation to the cost, while family systems theory 17 sees the relevant unit not as the couple but the family. Together, these frameworks treat communication and conflict as relational processes embedded in the family structure, not the couple alone, which RQ2 presupposes.

Intimacy, sexual satisfaction and identity are constructs that develop during the life-course of marriage, hence making the internal analysis layer indispensable in this review. Attachment theory 19 assumes a primary attachment bond, predicting that emotionally distant, avoidant individuals will be less satisfied. However, a study among couples of Northeast India showed that the satisfaction of husbands increased with moderate avoidant attachment similarity between spouses. 23 Self-construal theory 10 changes the discussion from satisfaction to identity, as in this case identity becomes a product of family. Building on Das’s 20 concept of endurance as active sense-making in the aftermath of violence, this review extends the idea to how a spouse makes sense of and endures a failing marriage, in contrast to merely tolerating it. This differs from resilience, which treats coping as a trait applied to an external stressor; here, endurance itself is the meaning-making act, not a response to one. In practical terms, this suggests that a woman who justifies enduring an unpleasant marriage as her way of keeping the family together or safeguarding her children is engaged in an act of meaning-making, not merely enduring because she has no alternative. 20 Nussbaum’s 21 capabilities approach raises an entirely different question: not the satisfaction level of the spouse, but the effect of marriage on the capabilities of that individual. This distinction, deemed pivotal in this review, forms the basis of RQ3.

Methods

This review employs a narrative approach for three reasons. First, there is not enough homogeneity within the existing literature concerning the quality of relationships within Indian marriages to support formal synthesis. Narrative approaches are well suited to a broad, heterogeneous body of evidence and to combining different kinds of research. 24 A related second reason is that some sources considered key in this review (ethnographic research, economic reasoning and philosophical argumentation) do not meet the standards required for a systematic review, though the argument could hardly exist without them. The third reason is more conceptual than practical: the question this review poses about the relationship between structural constraint and individual autonomy needs to be interpreted across multiple pieces of evidence rather than expressed as a single aggregated estimate. 25

Database searches, with no date restriction applied, included PsycINFO, Sociological Abstracts, EconLit and Google Scholar; empirical sources on Indian married couples ranged from 2008 to 2026. Search terms combined variants of ‘marriage’, ‘relationship’, ‘marital quality’, ‘relationship quality’, ‘marital relationships’ or ‘marital satisfaction’ with ‘India’ or ‘Indian’, alongside construct-specific terms such as ‘caste’, ‘joint family’, ‘dyadic coping’, ‘attachment’ or ‘sexual satisfaction’. Screening was conducted by the first author based on titles and abstracts; borderline inclusion decisions were discussed with the other two authors to reach consensus. The exclusion criteria included any study failing to meet all the following requirements: (a) concerns an Indian married population, (b) is either peer-reviewed or an openly documented institutional research report (e.g., a research-institute working paper) and (c) investigates marital satisfaction, dyadic adjustment, couple identity, marital sustainability or some other similar concept. The mentioned concepts are considered to be related but not interchangeable according to the warnings issued by Fincham and Bradbury. 3 As a result, a total of 48 references were used, including empirical, theoretical, economic and philosophical sources connected to Table 1, along with three Indian legal decisions. The restriction of the search to the English language means that articles written in Hindi, Tamil, Bengali, Marathi, Gujarati and other Indian languages were not taken into account.

Results

Caste and Joint Family Structure

Caste has a significant role in partner choice, as it continues to be one of the most reliable factors when it comes to forming alliances through marriage in India. This trend exists irrespective of any improvement in income and educational status. 4 Endogamy is still common, as inter-caste marriages account for only 5% of marriages. 26 As per Chakravarti, 15 endogamy keeps the caste pure through controlling the sexual and marital behaviour of women. Honour killing has been allowed by Khaps (a caste council in North India) in many instances of inter-caste and inter-religious marriages. Eventually, the Supreme Court banned Khap intervention in the marriage of consenting adults. 27

Household authority does not rest with the couple but rests with the in-laws, as it is the husband’s parents who make most of the decisions about spending and the distribution of household labour, even if the wife earns and contributes to the household income. 6 Sen 13 and Agarwal’s 14 bargaining framework ties a woman’s power within marriage to her fallback position: her ability to survive outside it, supported by her household contribution and access to her natal family. That support network, however, is structurally harder to reach: Karve 5 shows that patrilineal, patrilocal kinship systems, common in northern India though not uniform across the country, keep a woman closer to her husband’s family than her own. Together, these three accounts reveal an active mechanism, not mere correlation: patrilocal living undermines a wife’s fallback position, and a weaker fallback position is exactly what Sen 13 and Agarwal’s 14 model would predict to reduce household authority, a pattern for which Rizvi 6 provides preliminary qualitative support.

Caste and kinship together position a wife along two axes: her marriage options, defined by caste, and her household authority, defined by kinship and fallback position. Neither axis is included in the constructs that measure relationship quality, such as the Dyadic Adjustment Scale, which only takes into account the dyad. Nor do the axes remain constant. According to Damodaran et al., 28 the second axis showed early signs of becoming flexible within the nuclear family setting, which is more egalitarian in nature.

Communication and Conflict

Literature on Indian couples usually considers communication and conflicts in a larger context of dyadic coping. An example is the analysis of 340 married couples, where the ability to cope with common problems predicted dyadic adjustment, and the effect size was the highest for husbands. 29 Synthesising the structural evidence above, communication among spouses in the Indian context appears indirect, constrained and situation-specific, reflecting hierarchy awareness and lack of privacy in joint families rather than Western-style avoidance. Goffman’s 30 front-stage/back-stage distinction applies: couples in extended families have limited backstage privacy, since family members are often present, so the private self-disclosure many Western measures assume may be less common. When couples do create private disclosure space, that itself may signal higher relationship quality, consistent with a Maharashtra communication-program intervention showing gains within 5 weeks. 31

Conflicts in joint families emanate from problems related to money, in-laws, reproduction and household chores. Often, the in-laws tend to support either of the two partners, leading to the emergence of what is termed triangulation in the therapeutic framework of family systems. 17 However, this triangulation pattern has not been studied empirically among couples in India. In this context, conflicts between spouses do not emanate only from the friction that exists in marriage, and the available self-report measures of marital quality fail to capture such complexities. 20

While most of the literature reviewed here tends to examine the experiences of wives, the few studies on husbands show a similar trend. For instance, in a study involving 340 couples, dyadic coping was more strongly associated with adjustment in husbands than in wives, 29 while a cluster randomised trial showed improvement in egalitarian attitudes and marital satisfaction among men in the experimental group. 32 Nonetheless, neither finding captures men’s experience of marriage directly, and this review did not identify a study examining men’s bargaining power, active endurance or aspiration–structure gap from that perspective.

Intimacy, Sexuality and Identity

Urbanised and educated people prefer companionate marriages and intimacy. According to Uberoi, 33 as much credit goes to Bollywood movies and romantic literature for this phenomenon as to reality itself. This builds on the earlier claim that the identity of an Indian is constructed in tandem with a pre-existing family identity, which exists even prior to the person and persists after them. 10 The findings support this perspective. Couple identity and problem-solving have positive correlations with flourishing of the couple, while conflict engagement, withdrawal and compliance have variable negative correlations with flourishing. 9 Results from dyadic studies show that there is a positive relationship between the attachment security of one partner and the identity of the other, which is mediated by the partner’s responsiveness. 11

A widening gap has emerged between couples’ expectations and household restrictions, uncaptured by current relationship-quality measures. The household restrictions discussed above, that is, financial control concentrated in the hands of in-law members within the household and lack of private spaces for disclosure, are contrary to the joint decision-making process in marriage as proposed by Uberoi. 33 The empirical data about the phenomenon in question were obtained by Sandhya, 34 who carried out research among 182 urban Hindu couples. The unfulfilled expectations with regard to intimacy were found to result in conflicts rather than discussions, while this avoidance behaviour was related to low marital satisfaction. This discrepancy is termed the aspiration–structure gap in this review. It is offered as a hypothesis derived from the literature discussed above, not as an established fact; a woman may experience the gap without perceiving it as dissatisfaction. This would manifest, for instance, in a woman who is committed to the ideology of companionate love yet does not describe her marriage as being unhappy since there has been no opportunity to experience conflict within the power structure of her family.

Defined more precisely, the aspiration–structure gap is the distance between what a wife has come to expect from marriage, largely companionate norms absorbed from education, media and urban peer networks, and what the household’s authority structure actually permits her to pursue, regardless of whether she names that distance as dissatisfaction. This distinguishes it from two similar constructs. An unmet-expectations account would locate the issue within the relationship itself. Cognitive dissonance expects the person’s perceptions to change over time in order to rectify the conflict. The aspiration–structure gap is different because the discrepancy occurs between a wife and the structure that constrains her; therefore, the change needed is in the structure and not in her perception of the world, which is why she is more likely to endure or quietly negotiate her needs. The theories identified in this review ignore the gap because of their level of analysis. The investment model 16 and social exchange theory 18 consider marriage as a whole in their analyses. The constraint in this case exists at the higher level, in the structure in which the marriage takes place.

Sexual satisfaction appears to be the least researched dimension of the three outlined in this section, not because it matters less, but because it remains rarely discussed, even in academic literature. 35 In a longitudinal study of poor married women in Mumbai, roughly half of the participants were found to have negative attitudes towards sexual relations with their partners. 36 In the second, exploratory study of Indian couples seeking clinical intervention for sexual problems, the researchers found significant marital distress accompanied by notably elevated intimacy problems, alongside separate measures of affection, commitment and openness. 37 Since the latter information was obtained in a sample seeking clinical help for their problem, the data cannot be generalised to other married people. Intimacy and identity affect the quality of relationships and are backed by a substantial body of evidence; however, sexual satisfaction’s effect on relationship quality remains less certain, since the available evidence is limited to two narrow samples, one low-income and one clinical, neither representative of Indian married women as a whole.

Discussion

This review contributes two original constructs, active endurance and the aspiration–structure gap, to describe patterns already present in the literature that have not yet been named or theorised. A single pattern recurs across all three RQs: relationship quality is an emergent feature of structural and interpersonal mechanisms, not a dyadic one alone. On a structural level, Karve’s 5 kinship system and the household-bargaining approach of Sen 13 and Agarwal 14 converge on the same mechanism found explicitly in the results section: caste scrutiny, in-laws’ control over expenses and household chores, and a wife’s fallback position determine relationship quality even before the couple’s dynamic becomes a part of it. Interpersonally, the same structural context remains relevant: the lack of backstage privacy in extended families 30 and the presence of credible, if empirically untested, in-law triangulation 17 suggest that marital communication and conflict in India cannot be considered a two-person affair alone. Inside the marriage, the very concept introduced in this review, the aspiration–structure gap, captures what happens when a wife’s expectations exceed what the household’s authority structure allows her to pursue.

A second pattern, separate from the previous one, is that endurance and quiet negotiation can occur simultaneously rather than being at odds with each other. According to Das, 20 endurance is considered to be a constructive activity rather than an activity of accepting things without any resistance; this review treats that distinction as a significant contribution, along with the aspiration–structure gap.

Theoretical Implications

If relationship quality is indeed an emergent characteristic of structural and interactional factors, not simply of dyadic dynamics, then none of the theories described above can explain it alone, but all of them offer some limited explanation in their own right. In addition to the theoretical perspectives shown in Table 1, Uberoi’s 33 cultural perspective fills an important gap left by the others: it explains not just companionate marriage aspirations but their cultural origin in Bollywood cinema and romantic fiction as much as lived experience. Whether this cultural account extends to rural, non-Hindi-language or lower-income media markets remains untested.

This review contributes by addressing a related but distinct gap. Active endurance and the aspiration–structure gap were developed because no single theory could cover the empirical data: a wife’s apparent tolerance of an unpleasant marriage can be explained by neither satisfaction approaches nor bargaining approaches alone. According to Agarwal’s 14 bargaining theory, joint-family structure and limited outside opportunities should leave a wife with lower bargaining power and lower household authority; what the theory does not explain is why she would appear to endure rather than leave or contest the situation, and whether that endurance is adaptive or forced. The capabilities approach 21 raises the same question without answering it either. This is precisely what active endurance and the aspiration–structure gap address.

Nevertheless, each construct leaves open a question of boundaries that this review is unable to resolve. Active endurance is not, by definition, a sign of a healthy relationship because the difference between endurance understood as an adaptive process of meaning-making and endurance as a reaction to limited options is conceptual rather than descriptive, as it relies on knowledge about the wife’s options, such as income, family support and legal possibilities, that cannot be deduced from the narrative of her meaning-making alone. In turn, the aspiration–structure gap raises a similar but more tractable question, since it implies a testable divergence between endorsement of companionate ideals and reported decision-making power within the household, rather than a deficiency on either measure separately, which no existing single measure of relationship quality can detect. Operationalising this distinction, for example by comparing narratives of endurance or aspiration with independent measurement of fallback position,13, 14 is a challenge for future empirical research rather than one this review can resolve.

Beyond these psychological mechanisms, structural and interactional patterns likely have neurobiological correlates as well. There is evidence of hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation among individuals experiencing chronic marital distress, which is believed to mediate the relationship between marital quality and physical health outcomes.38, 39

Clinical and Policy Implications

Distinguishing adaptive tolerance from forced tolerance matters for couple therapy. Indigenous approaches offer a tentative solution: a single case study found that the Indian model for Attunement To Tackle Active Conflict And Helplessness (I-ATTACH) framework, which helped resolve conflicts in one couple, though as a single case this is preliminary, hypothesis-generating evidence rather than proof of efficacy. 40 Alongside indigenous psychotherapeutic models, neuroendocrine approaches offer a further promising line of inquiry: intranasal oxytocin administration has been shown to modulate dyadic conflict directly, lowering cortisol reactivity and increasing positive communication during couple-conflict tasks, though with sex-specific effects. 41 However, caution is warranted in applying either the psychoneuroendocrine model described above or this specific approach to the Indian setting, given how differently conflict, privacy and disclosure are handled within the joint-family context. Both directions nonetheless warrant empirical investigations in India.

The policy evidence is more concerning, coming from findings of a randomised trial. A cluster randomised controlled trial revealed more egalitarian gender role attitudes and higher marital satisfaction for men in the experimental group after 18 months; however, this did not reduce the violence suffered by women. 32 This result is in keeping with the expectations based on the capabilities approach of Nussbaum, 21 where bodily safety is seen as an intrinsic capability, not as a marginal one. When combined with the bargaining framework of Sen 13 and Agarwal, 14 as mentioned above, this implies that change in attitudes alone is not enough. As such, combining communication skills with women gaining actual bargaining power and income appears a feasible option worth investigating.

Limitations and Future Research Directions

This review is mostly focused on educated urban upper-caste individuals, consistent with a broader tendency in psychological research towards literate urban samples. 42 It is also limited to heterosexual marriages and does not address lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer/questioning, intersex, asexual and other sexual and gender minorities (LGBTQIA+) couples. Future research could use more extensive, diverse samples, including Dalit and Adivasi communities, rural couples, inter-caste and inter-faith couples, and LGBTQIA+ couples,43, 44 and should remain culturally sensitive45, 46 following the guidelines of the National Family Health Survey (NFHS). 47 Sexual satisfaction, violence and financial management will continue to be the most sensitive topics to explore, as they reflect gender biases. An equivalent gap can be found on the men’s side. Men’s experiences are touched upon only marginally in the reviewed literature, appearing in effect-size comparisons or as secondary outcomes within trial-based research,29, 32 rather than as a primary focus. This likely reflects a broader research emphasis on women’s bargaining position in Indian marriages, rather than a deliberate exclusion by this review. Future studies should examine men’s experience of these mechanisms directly, not only through wife-reported data.

The screening of the studies involved only the first author; thus, selection bias may have occurred, although marginal cases were discussed with the other two authors to help mitigate this issue. This English-language restriction (noted in section ‘Methods’) is a real limitation, given how much family life in India takes place in other Indian languages. The existing literature relies mostly on cross-sectional designs with inconsistent measurement instruments 46 and rarely disaggregates by generation. As a result, little is known about how caste-linked bargaining power, communication patterns or the aspiration–structure gap change over the course of a single marriage, as distinct from differences between generational cohorts. Longitudinal dyadic designs, specifically the Actor–Partner Interdependence Model (APIM), 48 are needed to establish both directionality and generational variation. Finally, pre-registered randomised trials are needed to move I-ATTACH 40 from single case reports towards practice.

Conclusion

This review reflects that, among urban, educated, upper-caste couples, relationship quality is not only determined by dyadic psychology but also by structural elements such as kinship, household power, caste and bargaining power. The two key contributions of this review are active endurance and the aspiration–structure gap, which capture patterns already present in the literature but not yet theorised. Additionally, the review identifies potential limitations underlying standard measures of relationship quality. The central message of this review is that relationship quality in Indian marriages does not derive from the two individuals alone, but from the institutions that govern the freedom and space available to them.
